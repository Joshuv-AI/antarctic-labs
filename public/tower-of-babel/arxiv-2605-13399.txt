The Diffusion Encoder
Akhil Premkumar, Sarah Lucioni

Abstract
We construct a new kind of encoder, leveraging the expressive power of diffusion models. In a traditional variational autoencoder, the encoder and decoder jointly negotiate a latent representation of the input. This is made possible by the reparameterization trick, which simplifies training at the cost of restricting the encoder to a simple family of distributions. Replacing this encoder with a diffusion model requires rethinking how the decoder pressure can be transmitted back to the encoder, given that they tend to update their internal estimates of the latent in opposing directions. We solve this problem with an alternating training scheme, inspired by the expectation-maximization algorithm. Our method enables more reliable synchronization between encoder and decoder, while preserving the simple and efficient training objective of standard diffusion models.



1
Introduction

Can a diffusion model be used as an encoder? After all, diffusion models are highly expressive probabilistic models, which makes them a natural candidate for parameterizing complex distributions over latent variables. The challenge is that these models are typically applied to cases where the target distribution is fixed, whereas a latent distribution must necessarily evolve during training as the encoder and decoder negotiate an optimal representation of the input signal.

In variational autoencoders (VAEs)
[
19
]
, the encoder is a Gaussian distribution with a diagonal covariance. Although this choice limits representational flexibility, it enables joint training of the encoder and decoder through the reparameterization trick. That is, it is straightforward to express the latents in terms of the encoder parameters, which allows the latter to be directly coupled to the decoder pressure. The same is not true for a diffusion model, since the latents are generated through many more iterations over the underlying score network, which makes their functional dependence on the network parameters more complicated
[
3
]
. Hence, we need to resort to a training algorithm that alternates between encoder and decoder updates, similar to techniques commonly used in maximum entropy reinforcement learning
[
12
,
25
]
.

Sacrificing the convenience of joint training may still be worthwhile if it yields a more flexible latent representation. The limited expressiveness of a rigid encoder family can hinder the autoencoder’s effectiveness in the following ways: (1) the optimal encoder may not lie within the chosen family, (2) an overly smooth encoder may map semantically distinct inputs to overlapping neighborhoods in latent space, and (3) an inexpressive encoder can throttle the reconstruction fidelity of the decoder
[
5
]
. These effects are discussed in greater detail in
section

2
.

Part of the expressive power of a diffusion model comes from its iterative generative process, which allows the model to gradually build up complex internal correlations in the generated sample (see
section

3
). Normalizing flows
[
34
]
and neural ODEs
[
4
]
also share this feature, but diffusion models benefit from a considerably simpler and more robust training objective (see
section

5
). Flow matching models
[
27
]
also inherit this property. However, diffusion models remain differentiated by their firm grounding in information theory, specifically, their ability to efficiently estimate quantities like mutual information and neural entropy
[
11
,
21
,
22
,
32
]
, which are otherwise difficult to compute in high-dimensions. This is particularly relevant to autoencoders, which are, at their core, realizations of an information bottleneck
[
39
,
35
,
1
]
.

In this paper, we develop a framework to leverage a diffusion model as an encoder. The key innovation is a training scheme that updates the encoder and decoder in alternation, while keeping them synchronized with one another (see
section

4
). This procedure is directly inspired by the expectation-maximization algorithm
[
6
]
, where the E-step updates a diffusion model with samples from the posterior, and the M-step updates the decoder. The posterior samples are generated from a short Langevin chain that explores the decoder landscape, while an affine drift term keeps the samples regularized. Lifting the regularization pressure from the encoder side subdues the tendency for the encoder and decoder to fall out of alignment with one another during their independent updates. The diffusion encoder takes on a more passive role in this arrangement, which means it can be trained with the standard, well-established denoising objective.

Finally, it is important to be clear about the scope of this work. Our goal is not to surpass the state-of-the-art in encoder design; over a decade of refinement has made the Gaussian encoder a highly competitive baseline. Rather, we seek to motivate a new application of diffusion models that draws on their representational capacity and training simplicity
[
15
,
8
,
37
]
. Therefore, the experiments in
section

6
serve to demonstrate proof of concept—showing that the proposed setup trains stably and produces coherent reconstructions—rather than optimizing reconstruction quality at scale. We will be transparent about the limitations of our framework as they arise, and suggest potential avenues for improvement throughout. We hope these ideas spur further research on the diffusion encoder.

Information Bottlenecks and Variational Autoencoders

We begin with an information-theoretic view of unsupervised representation learning, starting from the information bottleneck principle
[
39
,
35
]
. Consider two random variables
{\bm{X}}
and
{\bm{Y}}
that are correlated with one another (see
appendix

F
for notation). We want to predict
{\bm{Y}}
given some value of
{\bm{X}}
. This can be done by extracting from
{\bm{X}}
only the information that is relevant to
{\bm{Y}}
. Such information can be encoded in a third random variable
{\bm{Z}}
, called the
latent
, which compresses from
{\bm{X}}
the details pertinent to
{\bm{Y}}
. The optimal assignment from
{\bm{X}}
to
{\bm{Z}}
is obtained by finding the encoder
q({\bm{z}}|{\bm{x}})
that solves (with
\gamma>0
)

\min_{q({\bm{z}}|{\bm{x}})}I({\bm{X}};{\bm{Z}})-\gamma^{-1}I({\bm{Z}};{\bm{Y}}).

(1)

The mutual information (MI) between two random variables quantifies how much we learn about the value of one of them, given a measurement of the other (see App. A of
[
33
]
for a review). Minimizing
I({\bm{X}};{\bm{Z}})
while maximizing
I({\bm{Z}};{\bm{Y}})
squeezes into
{\bm{Z}}
only the
{\bm{Y}}
-specific content of
{\bm{X}}
.

Implementing
eq.

1
in practice requires estimating the MI terms, which is challenging when the random variables are high-dimensional. One workaround is to choose a simple form of the encoder,

q_{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{z}}|{\bm{x}})={\mathcal{N}}({\bm{z}};\mu_{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{x}}),\textrm{diag}(\sigma_{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{x}})^{2})),

(2)

and the objective

\min_{{\bm{\phi}},{\bm{\psi}}}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{X}},{\bm{Y}}}\mathbb{E}_{q_{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{z}}|{\bm{x}})}[-\log{p}_{\bm{\psi}}({\bm{y}}|{\bm{z}})]+\gamma\mathbb{E}_{\bm{X}}{D_{\textrm{KL}}}(q_{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{z}}|{\bm{x}})\,\|\,p({\bm{z}})){\,=:\,}\min_{{\bm{\phi}},{\bm{\psi}}}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{X}},{\bm{Y}}}{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm VI}.

(3)

The first term is the
reconstruction
term, which also trains the decoder
{p}_{\bm{\psi}}({\bm{x}}|{\bm{z}})
. It follows from

\min_{{\bm{\phi}},{\bm{\psi}}}-I({\bm{Z}};{\bm{Y}})=-S({\bm{Y}})+\min_{{\bm{\phi}},{\bm{\psi}}}S({\bm{Y}}|{\bm{Z}})\equiv\min_{{\bm{\phi}},{\bm{\psi}}}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{Y}},{\bm{Z}}}[-\log{p}_{\bm{\psi}}({\bm{y}}|{\bm{z}})],{\,=:\,}\min_{{\bm{\phi}},{\bm{\psi}}}{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm rec}

(4)

since the entropy of
{\bm{Y}}
is fixed by the dataset, and is independent of the parameters
({\bm{\phi}},{\bm{\psi}})
.
If we choose
{\bm{Y}}={\bm{X}}
, we recognize
eq.

3
as the negative of the evidence lower bound (ELBO) of a variational
auto
encoder
[
19
]
(see
appendix

C
). The second term in
eq.

3
, called the
regularization
term, incentivizes the encoder to be as uninformative as possible, by pushing
q_{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{z}}|{\bm{x}})
to be as close to the prior
p({\bm{z}})\equiv{\mathcal{N}}(0,I)
. It is straightforward to show that

{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm reg}{\,:=\,}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{X}}{D_{\textrm{KL}}}(q_{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{z}}|{\bm{x}})\,\|\,p({\bm{z}}))=I({\bm{Z}};{\bm{X}})+{D_{\textrm{KL}}}(q_{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{z}})\|p({\bm{z}})).

(5)

Intuitively, the aggregate
q_{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{z}}){\,:=\,}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{X}}[q_{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{z}}|{\bm{x}})]
is the distribution of all available ‘latent codes,’ and
q_{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{z}}|{\bm{x}})
is concentrated on the subset that encodes a given
{\bm{x}}
. Minimizing
{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm reg}
is equivalent to (1)
\min I({\bm{Z}};{\bm{X}})
, which forces
{\bm{Z}}
to retain as little information about
{\bm{X}}
as possible, and (2)
\min{D_{\textrm{KL}}}(q_{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{z}})\|p({\bm{z}}))
, which makes
q_{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{z}})
as close as possible to
p({\bm{z}})
, constraining ‘total channel capacity.’ Up to this additional KL term,
eq.

3
realizes the bottleneck from
eq.

1
.

The regularization term,
{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm reg}
, forces
q_{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{z}}|{\bm{x}})\to p({\bm{z}})
for every
{\bm{x}}
. In the limit, the encoder ignores the input
{\bm{x}}
entirely, leading to posterior collapse
[
29
,
13
]
. This can happen, for instance, when the decoder is very powerful; if the decoder can model
{\bm{X}}
without any help from
{\bm{Z}}
, there is little incentive for the encoder to produce informative latents, and the regularization pressure wins.

In a conventional VAE, the KL between
q_{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{z}}|{\bm{x}})
and
p({\bm{z}})
is easy to compute. The form of
q_{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{z}}|{\bm{x}})
in
eq.

2
produces a simple expression for this term,

{D_{\textrm{KL}}}(q_{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{z}}|{\bm{x}})\,\|\,p({\bm{z}}))=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{{D_{{\bm{Z}}}}}((\mu_{\bm{\phi}}^{j})^{2}+(\sigma_{\bm{\phi}}^{j})^{2}-\log(\sigma_{\bm{\phi}}^{j})^{2}-1),

(6)

where
\mu_{\bm{\phi}}^{j}
and
\sigma_{\bm{\phi}}^{j}
are the
j^{\rm th}
components of
\mu_{\bm{\phi}}
and
\sigma_{\bm{\phi}}
. During training,
{\bm{z}}
’s are generated from the data using
{\bm{z}}=\mu_{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{x}})+\sigma_{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{x}})\odot\bm{\epsilon}
, where
\bm{\epsilon}\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,I)
. This is called the reparameterization trick, and it allows the gradients to flow through the sampling step, since each
{\bm{z}}
is a function of
{\bm{\phi}}
. Despite these advantages, the Gaussian encoder from
eq.

2
has some major drawbacks. First, it is not, in general, the optimum of
eq.

3
. Varying
{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm VI}
w.r.t.
q_{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{z}}|{\bm{x}})
, with
{\bm{Y}}={\bm{X}}
, we find

0=\frac{\delta{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm VI}}{\delta q_{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{z}}|{\bm{x}})}\implies q^{\star}_{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{z}}|{\bm{x}})\propto p({\bm{z}})e^{\frac{1}{\gamma}\log p_{\bm{\psi}}({\bm{x}}|{\bm{z}})}.

(7)

So the optimal encoder is generically non-Gaussian. For
\gamma=1
, we recover the standard result that
q^{\star}_{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{z}}|{\bm{x}})=p_{\bm{\psi}}({\bm{z}}|{\bm{x}})
, the true posterior. An ideal family of variational distributions
q_{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{z}}|{\bm{x}})
should be flexible enough to contain
eq.

7
as one solution. Second, the encoder parameters
{\bm{\phi}}
are shared across all inputs, so the encoder cannot optimize independently for each data point
{\bm{x}}
. Therefore, even if there exists some
\hat{q}_{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{z}}|{\bm{x}})
within the chosen encoder family that is as close as possible to the ideal in
eq.

7
, the learned encoder
q_{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{z}}|{\bm{x}})
may be different from
\hat{q}_{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{z}}|{\bm{x}})
for each individual
{\bm{x}}
. The total inference gap can therefore be decomposed into an
approximation gap
, and the
amortization gap
, the latter quantifying the inability of the encoder to attain per-instance optimum due to the shared parameter mapping
{\bm{x}}\to{\bm{\phi}}
.
It is shown in
[
5
,
18
]
that the amortization gap is often the dominant component.
Therefore, the encoder architecture must also support a mapping from
{\bm{x}}\to q_{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{z}}|{\bm{x}})
that is expressive enough to specialize to each input, while generalizing reliably to unseen data.

A third, subtler limitation arises from the coupling between the encoder and decoder during training. Since the optimal encoder in
eq.

7
depends on
p_{\bm{\psi}}({\bm{x}}|{\bm{z}})
, the decoder can adjust itself to make
q^{\star}_{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{z}}|{\bm{x}})
easier to represent within the chosen encoder family. Thus, the decoder effectively absorbs the encoder’s limitations rather than driving the encoder toward a better posterior approximation—co-adapting to a limited encoder ultimately stifles the decoder
[
5
]
.

).

A toy example

It is instructive to consider a simple example that illustrates the above concerns. We consider sample generated by a process
z\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,1),x|z\sim{\mathcal{N}}(z^{2},\sigma^{2})
, where
Z
and
X
are one-dimensional random variables. The posterior is

p(z|x)\propto\exp\left(-\frac{(z^{2}-x)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}-\frac{z^{2}}{2}\right),

(8)

which is
not
a Gaussian in
z
. For large
x
, the posterior is bimodal, with symmetric peaks at
z=\pm\sqrt{x}
. For small
x
, the posterior is concentrated near zero but is non-Gaussian (see
fig.

2
). A Gaussian encoder cannot capture the true posterior at any
x
, which creates an approximation gap.

Next, we consider a simple polynomial decoder
d_{\bm{\psi}}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{3}\psi_{k}z^{k}
, with learnable coefficients
{\bm{\psi}}\equiv\{\psi_{k}\}
, and likelihood
p_{\bm{\psi}}(x|z)={\mathcal{N}}(x;d_{\bm{\psi}}(z),\sigma^{2})
(cf.
section

C.2
). The true posterior corresponds to
\psi_{2}=1
and
\psi_{k}=0
for all
\ k\neq 2
. However, if we try to approximate the posterior with a Gaussian family, the decoder coefficients
\psi_{0}
and
\psi_{1}
will be strongest since a linear decoder,
d_{\bm{\psi}}(z)=\psi_{0}+\psi_{1}z
, gives a Gaussian posterior. This is an example of the co-adaptation problem: even though the decoder can express the true solution, the limitations of the encoder prevent it from doing so.

3
Towards a Stochastic Encoder

A simple way of creating a more expressive encoder is to construct a chain of Gaussian encoders, each with its own mean and variance. That is, consider a chain of latent variables
\{{\bm{z}}_{0},{\bm{z}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{z}}_{n}\}
and the product
q({\bm{z}}_{n}|{\bm{z}}_{n-1},{\bm{x}})\cdots q({\bm{z}}_{1}|{\bm{z}}_{0},{\bm{x}})q({\bm{z}}_{0})
where

q({\bm{z}}_{i+1}|{\bm{z}}_{i},{\bm{x}})={\mathcal{N}}({\bm{z}}_{i+1};\mu_{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{z}}_{i},{\bm{x}}),{\sigma}_{i}^{2}),

(9)

and
q({\bm{z}}_{0})={\mathcal{N}}(0,I)
. We have kept the variance
{\sigma}_{i}^{2}
of each kernel independent of
{\bm{z}}_{i}
or
{\bm{x}}
for simplicity. To see how this setup creates correlations within
{\bm{z}}_{n}
, assume for a moment that
\mu_{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{z}}_{i},{\bm{x}})^{a}\propto{\bm{z}}^{a}_{i}+\beta{\bm{z}}^{b}_{i}
where
a\neq b
. That is, in the course of producing a sample
{\bm{z}}_{i+1}
, the kernel mixes into the
a^{\rm th}
component of
{\bm{z}}_{i}
a term proportional to its
b^{\rm th}
component. This establishes a correlation between different components, which can grow richer as we travel deeper along the chain. The resulting
{\bm{z}}_{n}
has richer statistics than those afforded by a single Gaussian encoder.

We can stack together a large number of such kernels, provided that the transformation induced by each kernel is scaled appropriately,

q({\bm{z}}_{i+1}|{\bm{z}}_{i},{\bm{x}})={\mathcal{N}}({\bm{z}}_{i+1};{\bm{z}}_{i}+f_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{z}}_{i},{\bm{x}},{t}_{i})\Delta{t},{\sigma}(t_{i})^{2}\Delta{t}).

(10)

Here
{t}_{i}{\,:=\,}i\Delta{t}
, and
n\Delta{t}{\,=:\,}{T}
is a finite number. The noise variance scales as
\Delta t
, so that the accumulated standard deviation of
{\bm{z}}_{n}
remains
{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{T})
and stays finite for large
n
. Thus
f_{\bm{\theta}}
and
{\sigma}
need not vanish in the continuum limit
n\to\infty
.
The samples from the above kernel are

{\bm{z}}_{i+1}={\bm{z}}_{i}+f_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{z}}_{i},{\bm{x}},{t}_{i})\Delta{t}+{\sigma}(t_{i})\sqrt{\Delta t}\bm{\epsilon}_{i},

(11)

where
\bm{\epsilon}_{i}\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,I)
. In the continuum limit, this becomes the stochastic differential equation (SDE)

{\textrm{d}}{\bm{Z}}_{t}=f_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{Z}}_{t},{\bm{x}},{t}){\textrm{d}}{t}+{\sigma}({t}){\textrm{d}}{\bm{B}}_{t}.

(12)

where
{\bm{B}}_{t}
is the Wiener process and
{\bm{Z}}_{t}
is a random variable, a sample of which is
{\bm{z}}_{t}
. Starting from a trivially distributed
{\bm{Z}}_{0}
, we can transform to a highly-structured distribution
q_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{z}}_{T}|{\bm{x}})
by applying
eq.

12
over time
{T}
. The requirement that
q_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{z}}_{T}|{\bm{x}})
remains close to the standard normal
p({\bm{z}})
is enforced by penalizing deviations of
eq.

12
from the SDE

{\textrm{d}}{\bm{Z}}_{t}=f_{\rm eq}({\bm{Z}}_{t},{t}){\textrm{d}}{t}+{\sigma}({t}){\textrm{d}}{\bm{B}}_{t}.

(13)

where
f_{\rm eq}({\bm{Z}}_{t},{t})=-\frac{{\sigma}({t})^{2}}{2}{\bm{Z}}_{t}
, so
p({\bm{z}})
is the equilibrium distribution of
eq.

13
. The KL between the path measures
\mathbb{Q}
and
\mathbb{P}
generated by
eqs.

13
and
12
upper bounds the KL between the terminal distributions at
{T}
, which follows from the data processing inequality (cf. Theorem 1 in
[
36
]
):

{D_{\textrm{KL}}}(\mathbb{Q}\|\mathbb{P})\geq{D_{\textrm{KL}}}(q_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{z}}_{T}|{\bm{x}})\|p({\bm{z}}_{T})).

(14)

The KL on the left is

{D_{\textrm{KL}}}(\mathbb{Q}\|\mathbb{P})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\left[\int_{0}^{{T}}{\textrm{d}}{t}\frac{1}{2{\sigma}(t)^{2}}\|f_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{z}}_{t},{\bm{x}},{t})-f_{\rm eq}({\bm{z}}_{t},{t})\|^{2}\right]

(15)

This KL would replace the regularization term in the autoencoder objective in
eq.

3
. During training the encoder generates a sample by, say, an Euler-Maruyama integration of
eq.

12
:

{\bm{z}}_{n}={\bm{z}}_{0}+\Delta{t}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}f_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{z}}_{i},{\bm{x}},t_{i})+\sqrt{\Delta t}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}{\sigma}(t_{i})\bm{\epsilon}_{i}.

(16)

In principle, we can feed this into the decoder, which then applies pressure on
f_{\bm{\theta}}
to shape
{\bm{z}}_{n}
to be maximally informative about
{\bm{x}}
, while
eq.

15
tries to do the opposite. This is the SDE version of the reparameterization trick, and it is used in
[
3
]
. But this approach has a few drawbacks: (1)
f_{\bm{\theta}}
determines
{\bm{z}}_{t}
via
eq.

16
, and we evaluate the
f_{\bm{\theta}}
in the loss
eq.

15
at those
{\bm{z}}_{t}
, so changing
f_{\bm{\theta}}
changes where we evaluate
f_{\bm{\theta}}
, making the loss landscape non-stationary, and (2) we must store
n
intermediate states
{\bm{z}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{z}}_{n}
and the activations in
f_{\bm{\theta}}
at each step, as well as the noise samples
\bm{\epsilon}_{i}
, so the memory scales linearly in
n
.

The adjoint method
[
4
,
26
]
is a principled solution to these issues. Instead of backpropagating through
eq.

16
, they solve an adjoint equation backwards in time, which can be used to directly compute the loss gradient w.r.t.
{\bm{\theta}}
. This approach is not without its own difficulties, however, as gradient-based optimization on the resulting non-convex problem has been reported to be empirically unstable
[
10
]
. Adjoint
matching

[
9
]
resolves this issue by recasting the training objective into a convex form, while also simplifying the backward evolution of the adjoint state. This method is also particularly well-suited to modeling tilted distributions like the one in
eq.

7
.

Fitting a moving target

A fundamental feature of the autoencoder setup is that
q_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{z}}_{T}\|{\bm{x}})
evolves during training. In a classic VAE, the reparameterization trick allows the latent to be expressed as a function of the encoder parameters, so the encoder and decoder can be trained jointly—the decoder influence backpropagates to the encoder parameters via

\nabla_{\bm{\phi}}{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm rec}=\nabla_{\bm{z}}{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm rec}\frac{{\partial}{\bm{z}}}{{\partial}{\bm{\phi}}},\qquad{\bm{z}}=\mu_{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{x}})+\sigma_{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{x}})\odot\bm{\epsilon}.

(17)

The analogue for a diffusion encoder would be to let the decoder influence the encoder parameters through
eq.

16
, but this is computationally expensive and potentially unstable.

On the other hand, adjoint methods are built on the assumption that the target distribution is fixed. During training, samples
{\bm{z}}_{T}
are generated with
eq.

16
, which are then used to compute the initial values of the adjoint states at
{t}={T}
.
Notably, only the value of
{\bm{z}}_{T}
is used in this approach, and
f_{\bm{\theta}}
is never differentiated through the generative process itself. The parameters
{\bm{\theta}}
receive gradient information exclusively via the adjoint equation, which propagates corrections backward in time independent of how
{\bm{z}}_{T}
was generated.

In the autoencoder context, the adjoint method can be adapted to train the stochastic encoder on a transient target
q_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{z}}_{T}|{\bm{x}})
, provided the distribution evolves slowly over the course of training. First, the encoder generates latents
{\bm{z}}_{T}
with the current
{\bm{\theta}}
. These
{\bm{z}}_{T}
are then used to update the encoder and decoder
separately
. This creates a synchronization problem: the encoder and decoder have opposing preferences for what the latent should be, as manifest from the competing terms in
eq.

1
, and updating them in isolation allows this disagreement to compound. A more detailed account of this issue is given in
section

A.1
. The setup can still be made to work, albeit with delicate tuning of the training parameters, but our goal is a more robust approach that does not depend on such sensitivity.

4
Equilibrating to the Posterior

Alternating optimization schemes have a storied history in machine learning, with the expectation-maximization (EM) algorithm being one of the oldest and most well-established. We draw inspiration from this method to develop a stable training routine with the stochastic encoder from
section

3
.
Briefly, the EM algorithm
[
6
]
is a general method for maximum likelihood estimation in latent variable models. The goal is to maximize the marginal log likelihood
p_{\bm{\psi}}({\bm{x}})
, which is exactly

\log p_{\bm{\psi}}({\bm{x}})=\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q({\bm{z}}|{\bm{x}})}[\log{p}_{\bm{\psi}}({\bm{x}}|{\bm{z}})]-{D_{\textrm{KL}}}(q({\bm{z}}|{\bm{x}})\,\|\,p({\bm{z}}))}_{{\mathcal{L}}(q;{\bm{\psi}})}+{D_{\textrm{KL}}}(q({\bm{z}}|{\bm{x}})\|p_{\bm{\psi}}({\bm{z}}|{\bm{x}})).

(18)

Since the KL term is non-negative,
{\mathcal{L}}(q;{\bm{\psi}})
is a lower bound on
\log p_{\bm{\psi}}({\bm{x}})
—it is the ELBO (cf.
eq.

38
). EM finds a solution to the maximum likelihood problem by alternating between two updates, called the E-step and M-step. The expectation step, or E-step, sets
q({\bm{z}}|{\bm{x}})=p_{\bm{\psi}}({\bm{z}}|{\bm{x}})
at the
current
values of the parameter
{\bm{\psi}}
. Then, the M-steps maximizes
{\mathcal{L}}(q;{\bm{\psi}})
over
{\bm{\psi}}
with
q
fixed. Since the KL term in
{\mathcal{L}}(q;{\bm{\psi}})
is independent of
{\bm{\psi}}
, this reduces to

\max_{\bm{\psi}}{\mathcal{L}}(q;{\bm{\psi}})=\max\mathbb{E}_{q({\bm{z}}|{\bm{x}})}[\log{p}_{\bm{\psi}}({\bm{x}}|{\bm{z}})].

(19)

The bound is tight at the start of each M-step, since setting
q
to the true posterior makes
\log{p}_{\bm{\psi}}({\bm{x}})={\mathcal{L}}(q;{\bm{\psi}})
. Therefore, any increase in
{\mathcal{L}}(q;{\bm{\psi}})
guarantees a corresponding increase in
\log p_{\bm{\psi}}({\bm{x}})
, ensuring monotone convergence. We can adopt the same strategy for the stochastic encoder.

We propose a novel scheme, where the encoder directly models the optimal solution from
eq.

7
,

q^{\star}({\bm{z}}|{\bm{x}})\propto p({\bm{z}})e^{\frac{1}{\gamma}\log p_{\bm{\psi}}({\bm{x}}|{\bm{z}})}\propto\exp\left(-\frac{{\bm{z}}^{2}}{2}+\frac{1}{\gamma}\log p_{\bm{\psi}}({\bm{x}}|{\bm{z}})\right).

(20)

Notice that this is the equilibrium distribution of an SDE (with
{\bm{\psi}}
fixed),

5
The Diffusion Encoder

The final component in our autoencoder setup is a flexible probabilistic model capable of capturing
q^{\star}({\bm{z}}|{\bm{x}})
from its samples. We use a diffusion model for the purpose—the titular
diffusion encoder
. Diffusion models convert noise to data by learning to reverse a diffusion process that transforms data to noise. To use such a model in the paradigm described above, the transformation
{\bm{Z}}_{0}\to{\bm{Z}}_{T}|{\bm{x}}
under
eq.

12
would be replaced by the generative stage of the diffusion model. This is the reversal of a forward process that converts
{\bm{Z}}_{T}|{\bm{x}}\to{\bm{Z}}_{0}
via

{\textrm{d}}{\tilde{{\bm{Z}}}}_{s}=f_{\rm eq}({\tilde{{\bm{Z}}}}_{s},{T}-{s}){\textrm{d}}{s}+{\sigma}({T}-{s}){\textrm{d}}\hat{{\bm{B}}}_{s},

(22)

where we have introduced the forward time variable
{s}{\,:=\,}{T}-{t}
, so
{\tilde{{\bm{Z}}}}_{0}\equiv{\bm{Z}}_{T}
and
{\tilde{{\bm{Z}}}}_{T}\equiv{\bm{Z}}_{0}
(see
fig.

10
). Over a large
{T}
,
eq.

22
reaches a stationary distribution, which we will also choose as the prior,
q({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}_{T})\equiv p({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}_{T})
. The ideal reverse process is

{\textrm{d}}{\bm{Z}}_{t}=-\left[f_{\rm eq}({\bm{Z}}_{t},{t})-{\sigma}({t})^{2}\nabla\log q({\bm{Z}}_{t},{t}|{\bm{x}})\right]{\textrm{d}}{t}+{\sigma}({t}){\textrm{d}}{\bm{B}}_{t}.

(23)

Here
q({\bm{Z}}_{t},{t}|{\bm{x}})\equiv q({\tilde{{\bm{Z}}}}_{s},{s}|{\bm{x}})
is obtained by applying
eq.

22
to
{\tilde{{\bm{Z}}}}_{0}|{\bm{x}}
for time
{s}
. To learn the reverse process we take
f_{\bm{\theta}}\equiv f_{\rm eq}+{\sigma}^{2}{\bm{e}}_{\bm{\theta}}
to beget the neural-network parameterized SDE

{\textrm{d}}{\bm{Z}}_{t}=\left[f_{\rm eq}({\bm{Z}}_{t},{t})+{\sigma}({t})^{2}{\bm{e}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{Z}}_{t},{T}-{t};{\bm{x}})\right]{\textrm{d}}{t}+{\sigma}({t}){\textrm{d}}{\bm{B}}_{t}.

(24)

This is the
entropy-matching
parameterization, introduced in
[
32
]
. It makes the calculation of certain information-theoretic quantities more transparent, as we will see in a moment. For now, we note that we can switch to the score-matching parameterization by simply setting
\bm{s}_{\bm{\theta}}=2f_{\rm eq}/{\sigma}^{2}+{\bm{e}}_{\bm{\theta}}
. We train
eq.

24
to match
eq.

23
by minimizing the KL between their path measures
[
36
]
,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}^{{\bm{Z}}|{\bm{X}}}_{\rm EM}{\,:=\,}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{X}}}\left[\int_{0}^{{T}}{\textrm{d}}{s}\frac{{\sigma}^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}_{{\tilde{{\bm{Z}}}}_{s},{\tilde{{\bm{Z}}}}_{0}|{\bm{x}}}\left[\big\|\nabla\log p({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}_{s})-\nabla\log q({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}_{s},{s}|{\bm{x}})+{\bm{e}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}_{s},{s};{\bm{x}})\big\|^{2}\right]\right]

(25)

\displaystyle\text{where, }\nabla\log p({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}})=-{\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}\overset{(\ref{eq:EqSDE})}{=}\frac{2f_{\rm eq}}{{\sigma}^{2}}.

(26)

The expectation is taken over the paths generated by
eq.

13
, which are the same as those produced by
eq.

22
as it transforms
{\tilde{{\bm{Z}}}}_{0}|{\bm{x}}\to{\tilde{{\bm{Z}}}}_{T}|{\bm{x}}
. In practice, we only have access to
q({\bm{z}}_{T}|{\bm{x}})\equiv q({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}_{0}|{\bm{x}})
through its samples
\{{\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}_{0}\}
. However, given a sufficient number of such samples, we can train a diffusion model without access to the true score
\nabla\log q(\cdot|{\bm{x}})
. This is possible because minimizing
eq.

25
is equivalent to minimizing the
denoising
objective
[
41
]
,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}^{{\bm{Z}}|{\bm{X}}}_{\rm DEM}{\,:=\,}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{X}}}\left[\int_{0}^{{T}}{\textrm{d}}{s}\frac{{\sigma}^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}_{{\tilde{{\bm{Z}}}}_{s},{\tilde{{\bm{Z}}}}_{0}|{\bm{x}}}\big\|\nabla\log p({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}_{s})-\nabla\log q({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}_{s},{s}|{\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}_{0})+{\bm{e}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}_{s},{s};{\bm{x}})\big\|^{2}\right]

(27)

\displaystyle\quad\approx{T}\,\mathbb{E}_{{s}\sim\mathcal{U}(\varepsilon,{T}]}\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{l}{\bm{x}}\sim{{p}_{{\textrm{d}}}}({\bm{x}})\\
{\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}_{0}\sim q({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}_{0}|{\bm{x}})\\
{\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}_{s}\sim q({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}_{s},{s}|{\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}_{0})\end{subarray}}\left[\lambda({s})\frac{{\sigma}({s})^{2}}{2}\big\|\nabla\log p({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}_{s})-\nabla\log q({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}_{s},{s}|{\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}_{0})+{\bm{e}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}_{s},{s};{\bm{x}})\big\|^{2}\right].

(28)

In the second step, we have approximated the integral and expectations with a Monte Carlo average. More importantly, the unknown density
q({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}_{s},{s}|{\bm{x}})
has been replaced by the transition kernel, which is the Gaussian
[
17
]

q({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}_{s},{s}|{\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}_{0})={\mathcal{N}}\left({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}_{s};\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}({s})}{\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}_{0},(1-\bar{\alpha}({s}))I\right),\qquad\bar{\alpha}({s})=\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{s}{\textrm{d}}\bar{{s}}\,\beta(\bar{{s}})\right),

(29)

for the VP SDE with
f_{\rm eq}({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}},{s})=-\frac{\beta({s})}{2}{\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}
and
{\sigma}({T}-{s})=\sqrt{\beta({s})}
. Thus, the unknown score in
eq.

25
is replaced with

\nabla\log q({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}_{s},{s}|{\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}_{0})=-\frac{\bm{\epsilon}}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}({s})}},\qquad\bm{\epsilon}\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,I).

(30)

Notice that the denominator vanishes at
{s}=0
, which is why the Monte Carlo estimate starts at
{s}=\varepsilon
, a small positive number. We have also introduced a factor
\lambda({s})
in going from
eq.

27
to
eq.

28
, which re-weights the objective in a time-dependent way. The choice
\lambda({s})=2(1-\bar{\alpha}({s}))/\beta({s})
yields the “variance dropped” training objective, which gives better empirical results
[
15
,
36
]
.

The Gaussian kernel in
eq.

29
also enables
simulation-free
sampling. That is, we can compute averages of the form
\int_{s}\mathbb{E}_{\scriptstyle{\tilde{{\bm{Z}}}}_{s}}[\cdots]
without solving
eq.

22
iteratively over each time step. Instead, we can use the kernel to sample any point in a full trajectory in a single jump,

{\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}_{s}=\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}({s})}{\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}_{0}+\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}({s})}\bm{\epsilon}.

(31)

This is one of the key features that make diffusion model training so efficient. For comparison, the classical adjoint methods, as well as adjoint matching, lack this property (see
appendix

D
). Faster training of the encoder helps offset a portion of the latency due to the Langevin iterations in
algorithm

1
.

Finally, setting
f_{\bm{\theta}}\equiv f_{\rm eq}+{\sigma}^{2}{\bm{e}}_{\bm{\theta}}
in
eq.

15
, we see that the KL between the diffusion encoder output and the prior is upper bounded by the conditional
neural entropy

[
32
,
33
]
,

S^{{\bm{Z}}|{\bm{X}}}_{\rm NN}{\,:=\,}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{X}}\left[\int_{0}^{{T}}{\textrm{d}}{s}\,\frac{{\sigma}^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}_{{\tilde{{\bm{Z}}}}_{s},{\tilde{{\bm{Z}}}}_{0}|{\bm{x}}}\left[\left\|{\bm{e}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}_{s},{s};{\bm{x}})\right\rVert^{2}\right]\right]\geq{D_{\textrm{KL}}}(q_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{z}}_{T}|{\bm{x}})\|p({\bm{z}}_{T})).

(32)

Briefly, the neural entropy
S{{}^{{\bm{Z}}|{\bm{X}}}_{\rm NN}}
measures the amount of information injected by the diffusion model to drive
q({\bm{z}}_{0})
to
q_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{z}}_{T}|{\bm{x}})
. It is the information that was captured and stored in the neural network during training. If
S{{}^{{\bm{Z}}|{\bm{X}}}_{\rm NN}}
is small the encoder produces a
{\bm{Z}}_{T}
that is uninformative about
{\bm{X}}
. This is a satisfying physical picture:
{\bm{Z}}_{T}
can carry no more information about
{\bm{X}}
than the network has stored.

7
Conclusion

Can a diffusion model be used as an encoder? Yes, absolutely. Is there more work to be done before it is ready for primetime? Also yes.

The main contribution of our paper is
algorithm

1
, which enables (1) synchronized training of a decoupled encoder and decoder pair, and (2) training of the diffusion encoder with the standard denoising objective. A key enabler of our diffusion encoder is the architecture described in
appendix

E
, which progressively injects information from the input into the latent over the course of the denoising process. We do not claim this to be an optimal design, and expect that more careful architectural choices would yield further improvements.

The primary weakness of
algorithm

1
is the sequential overhead introduced by the Langevin chains—patience is the price we pay for stability. Here again, there is room for improvement, by adaptive adjustment of
n_{\rm steps}
as training settles, and/or the use of critically damped dynamics to reach the stationary distribution more rapidly. We elaborate on these ideas in
section

A.2
. Fortunately, for lower-dimensional
{\bm{X}}
training times remain reasonable; this makes our setup a strong candidate for certain maximum entropy reinforcement learning problems
[
12
,
25
]
.

In closing, we note that our training scheme exhibits a certain resilience to posterior collapse, as noted in
section

6
. This makes sense, since
algorithm

1
is explicitly designed to accommodate a certain amount of lag from the encoder, allowing the encoder to catch up with the decoder if it sprints ahead. Whether this structural robustness has broader applications beyond our current setting remains an intriguing open question.

Appendix A
Outtakes

We present some approaches we tried in the course of this work, explaining the rationale behind those ideas, and why they were ultimately scrapped in favor of the method in the main text. This section is intended to save future researchers the effort of rediscovering these detours, and perhaps spark inspiration for more elegant solutions.

A.1
Regularization on the Encoder side

A notable feature of
algorithm

1
is that regularization happens through Langevin dynamics under
eq.

21
. Equilibrating this SDE is also the highest latency step in the whole process. Another approach would be to use a regularization term analogous to
eq.

6
, that throttles the information content of
{\bm{Z}}
by directly constraining the encoder’s network parameters. For the diffusion encoder, this is accomplished through the conditional neural entropy (cf.
eq.

32
),

S^{{\bm{Z}}|{\bm{X}}}_{\rm NN}{\,:=\,}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{X}}\left[\int_{0}^{{T}}{\textrm{d}}{s}\,\frac{{\sigma}^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}_{{\tilde{{\bm{Z}}}}_{s},{\tilde{{\bm{Z}}}}_{0}|{\bm{x}}}\left[\left\|{\bm{e}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}_{s},{s};{\bm{x}})\right\rVert^{2}\right]\right]\geq{D_{\textrm{KL}}}(q_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{z}}_{T}|{\bm{x}})\|p({\bm{z}}_{T})).

(33)

Combining
S{{}^{{\bm{Z}}|{\bm{X}}}_{\rm NN}}
with the denoising objective (cf.
eq.

28
), we obtain

{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm DEnc}{\,:=\,}{\mathcal{L}}^{{\bm{Z}}|{\bm{X}}}_{\rm DEM}+\gamma S^{{\bm{Z}}|{\bm{X}}}_{\rm NN}.

(34)

By adjusting
\gamma
we can throttle the amount of information stored in the encoder network, which bounds the information compressed into
{\bm{Z}}_{T}
from
{\bm{X}}
. Thus, the regularization responsibility shifts back to the encoder. Conveniently, the neural entropy can also be estimated by simulation-free sampling, just like the denoising loss.

The encoder and decoder are still decoupled, so we need to train them with an alternating scheme. This is
algorithm

2
. In each cycle, fresh latents are generated by the encoder. We update these to the current decoder parameters, by taking a step (or steps) in the direction of
\nabla_{\bm{z}}L_{\rm rec}
, where
L_{\rm rec}({\bm{\psi}},{\hat{{\bm{z}}}}){\,:=\,}-\log p_{\bm{\psi}}({\bm{x}}|{\hat{{\bm{z}}}})
. Here again, we found that generating
B_{\bm{z}}
latent samples per input
{\bm{x}}
provided a better training signal to the encoder later, provided we average the updated latents over
B_{\bm{z}}
before training the encoder on them (see step 7).

(35b)

where we have taken

for simplicity, and
\eta_{\bm{\psi}}

A.2
Latency

The biggest drawback with
algorithm

1
is the latency induced by sequential iteration of the posterior SDE,
eq.

21
, each step of which involves taking a derivative of the decoder network. As mentioned there,
n_{\rm steps}
does not have to be large for the Langevin chain to equilibrate, since the encoder places the initial latents close to their final destination. We typically set
n_{\rm steps}=10
in our experiments (see
table

1
). This parameter is what allows the setup to tolerate some slack between the encoder and decoder, as the latter races ahead in
11
. Even though there is no regularization pressure on the encoder side, the encoder lags because it was last updated with a
{\bm{z}}_{\star}
that may no longer reflect the decoder’s preference at the end of each cycle.

The lag is largest early in training, when the decoder loss landscape is changing most rapidly. As the losses stabilize, the encoder catches up and tracks the decoder more closely. It also means we no longer need as many
n_{\rm steps}
for the latents to settle to
{\bm{z}}_{\star}
. In fact, it is possible to adaptively lower
n_{\rm steps}
as training progresses with little to no effect on synchronization. We did this experiment, but there was a catch: we used JAX as our ML framework
[
2
]
, and it requires a separate JIT-compilation of the model for each
n_{\rm steps}
setting. For smaller datasets, this offsets the latency improvements from adaptive
n_{\rm steps}
, but cleverer ML engineers than us should be able to resolve this.

Another possibility is to introduce a velocity variable into the Langevin dynamics, imbuing
{\bm{z}}_{\tau}
with inertia. This smoothens the evolution of
{\bm{z}}_{\tau}
, which (1) regulates it from making large jumps when it encounters regions with large
\nabla_{{\bm{z}}_{\tau}}\log p_{\bm{\psi}}({\bm{x}}|{\bm{z}}_{\tau})
, and (2) leads to faster equilibration if the dynamics is tuned correctly. We did not try this ourselves, but we state the idea here for future development.

Appendix C
Variational Autoencoders

We review the variational autoencoder (VAE) framework here, for completeness.

C.1
ELBO

We derive the training objective for a VAE, following
[
20
]
. Given some data
\{{\bm{x}}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, we would like to model the underlying probability distribution that produced these samples. We assume each observation
{\bm{x}}
arises from an unobserved latent variable
{\bm{z}}
via the generative process
{\bm{z}}\sim p({\bm{z}})
, and
{\bm{x}}\sim p_{\bm{\psi}}({\bm{x}}|{\bm{z}})
. The marginal likelihood of
{\bm{x}}
under this model is obtained by integrating over all possible values of
{\bm{z}}
,

\log p_{\bm{\psi}}({\bm{x}})=\log\int{\textrm{d}}{\bm{z}}\,p_{\bm{\psi}}({\bm{x}}|{\bm{z}})p({\bm{z}})=\log\int{\textrm{d}}{\bm{z}}\,p_{\bm{\psi}}({\bm{x}},{\bm{z}}).

However, this integral is computationally infeasible. We instead derive a tractable lower bound by introducing a variational distribution
q_{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{z}}|{\bm{x}})
. Multiplying and dividing the integrand with this object, we get

\log p_{\bm{\psi}}({\bm{x}})=\log\int\frac{q_{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{z}}|{\bm{x}})}{q_{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{z}}|{\bm{x}})}\,p_{\bm{\psi}}({\bm{x}},{\bm{z}})\,{\textrm{d}}{\bm{z}}=\log\mathbb{E}_{q_{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{z}}|{\bm{x}})}\left[\frac{p_{\bm{\psi}}({\bm{x}},{\bm{z}})}{q_{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{z}}|{\bm{x}})}\right],

to which we can apply
Jensen’s inequality
and obtain

\log\mathbb{E}_{q_{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{z}}|{\bm{x}})}\left[\frac{p_{\bm{\psi}}({\bm{x}},{\bm{z}})}{q_{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{z}}|{\bm{x}})}\right]\geq\mathbb{E}_{q_{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{z}}|{\bm{x}})}\left[\log\frac{p_{\bm{\psi}}({\bm{x}},{\bm{z}})}{q_{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{z}}|{\bm{x}})}\right].

This gives the Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO),

\displaystyle\log p_{\bm{\psi}}({\bm{x}})

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}_{q_{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{z}}|{\bm{x}})}\left[\log p_{\bm{\psi}}({\bm{x}},{\bm{z}})-\log q_{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{z}}|{\bm{x}})\right]

(36)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q_{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{z}}|{\bm{x}})}\left[\log p_{\bm{\psi}}({\bm{x}}|{\bm{z}})+\log p({\bm{z}})-\log q_{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{z}}|{\bm{x}})\right]

(37)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q_{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{z}}|{\bm{x}})}[\log p_{\bm{\psi}}({\bm{x}}|{\bm{z}})]}_{\text{Reconstruction term}}\;-\;\underbrace{{D_{\textrm{KL}}}(q_{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{z}}|{\bm{x}})\,\|\,p({\bm{z}}))}_{\text{Regularization term}}

(38)

Maximum likelihood estimation of the parameters
{\bm{\phi}}
and
{\bm{\psi}}
follows from maximizing the r.h.s. Equivalently, we can minimize its negative,

({\bm{\phi}},{\bm{\psi}})=\operatorname{argmin}_{{\bm{\phi}},{\bm{\psi}}}\left\{\mathbb{E}_{q_{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{z}}|{\bm{x}})}[-\log p_{\bm{\psi}}({\bm{x}}|{\bm{z}})]\;+\;\gamma{D_{\textrm{KL}}}(q_{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{z}}|{\bm{x}})\,\|\,p({\bm{z}}))\right\}.

(39)

Note that we have introduced a
\gamma>0
to adjust the regularization term
[
14
]
; if
\gamma
is large, the KL term forces all
q_{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{z}}|{\bm{x}})
to collapse to the same distribution
p({\bm{z}})
, which is usually a Gaussian, and the VAE becomes unable to capture sufficient information from
{\bm{x}}
for reconstruction. A smaller
\gamma
allows
q_{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{z}}|{\bm{x}})
to deviate farther from
p({\bm{z}})
, trading off compression for reconstruction fidelity. Therefore, the parameter
\gamma
plays the role of a temperature—higher
\gamma
scrambles the encoder distribution, and lower values allow it to be more structured.

C.2
Model components

To implement the ELBO as a training loss, we choose the latent prior
p({\bm{z}})
, the encoder
q_{\phi}({\bm{z}}|{\bm{x}})
, and the decoder
p_{\bm{\psi}}({\bm{x}}|{\bm{z}})
to be Gaussians.

Encoder

The encoder is a network that maps an input, say
{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{{D_{{\bm{X}}}}}
, to
\mu_{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{x}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{D_{{\bm{Z}}}}
and
\log\sigma_{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{x}})^{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{D_{{\bm{Z}}}}
, which parameterize a Gaussian

q_{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{z}}|{\bm{x}})={\mathcal{N}}({\bm{z}};\mu_{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{x}}),\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{x}})^{2})).

The latent prior is
p({\bm{z}})=\mathcal{N}(0,I)
. The KL divergence between two diagonal Gaussians,
p_{1}({\bm{x}})=\mathcal{N}({\bm{x}};\mu_{1},\Sigma_{1})
and
p_{2}({\bm{x}})=\mathcal{N}({\bm{x}};\mu_{2},\Sigma_{2})
, has a closed-form expression

{D_{\textrm{KL}}}(p_{1}\|p_{2})=\frac{1}{2}\left[\log\frac{|\Sigma_{2}|}{|\Sigma_{1}|}-d+\text{tr}\{\Sigma_{2}^{-1}\Sigma_{1}\}+(\mu_{2}-\mu_{1})^{\top}\Sigma_{2}^{-1}(\mu_{2}-\mu_{1})\right].

With
\mu_{1}=\mu_{\bm{\phi}}
,
\mu_{2}=0
,
\Sigma_{1}=\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_{\bm{\phi}}^{2})
, and
\Sigma_{2}=I
, we get

\displaystyle\text{tr}\{\Sigma_{2}^{-1}\Sigma_{1}\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sigma_{j}^{2}

\displaystyle(\mu_{2}-\mu_{1})^{\top}\Sigma_{2}^{-1}(\mu_{2}-\mu_{1})

\displaystyle=\mu_{\bm{\phi}}^{\top}I\mu_{\bm{\phi}}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mu_{j}^{2}

\displaystyle\log\frac{|\Sigma_{2}|}{|\Sigma_{1}|}

\displaystyle=-\log\prod_{i=1}^{D}\sigma_{j}^{2}.

Thus, the regularization term in the ELBO is

{D_{\textrm{KL}}}(q_{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{z}}|{\bm{x}})\,\|\,p({\bm{z}}))=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{{D_{{\bm{Z}}}}}((\mu_{\bm{\phi}}^{j})^{2}+(\sigma_{\bm{\phi}}^{j})^{2}-\log(\sigma_{\bm{\phi}}^{j})^{2}-1),

(40)

where
\mu_{\bm{\phi}}^{j}
and
\sigma_{\bm{\phi}}^{j}
are the
j^{\rm th}
components of
\mu_{\bm{\phi}}
and
\sigma_{\bm{\phi}}
. In practice, the encoder outputs log-variance
\log\sigma_{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{x}})^{2}
, which we exponentiate to get
\sigma_{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{x}})^{2}
.

Decoder

Assume the decoder defines a Gaussian distribution with fixed variance:

p_{\bm{\psi}}({\bm{x}}|{\bm{z}})={\mathcal{N}}({\bm{x}};d_{\bm{\psi}}({\bm{z}}),\sigma^{2}I)

Taking the log of this likelihood:

\log p_{\bm{\psi}}({\bm{x}}|{\bm{z}})=-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|{\bm{x}}-d_{\bm{\psi}}({\bm{z}})\|^{2}+\text{const}

(41)

Since the constant and
\sigma^{2}
can be absorbed into optimization, the reconstruction term becomes:

\mathbb{E}_{q_{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{z}}|{\bm{x}})}[-\log p_{\bm{\psi}}({\bm{x}}|{\bm{z}})]\approx\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{z}}\sim q_{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{z}}|{\bm{x}})}[\|{\bm{x}}-d_{\bm{\psi}}({\bm{z}})\|^{2}].

(42)

So the reconstruction loss is mean squared error (MSE). In each training cycle the
{\bm{z}}
values are generated from the training data
{\bm{x}}
using
{\bm{z}}=\mu_{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{x}})+\sigma_{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{x}})\odot\bm{\epsilon}
where
\bm{\epsilon}\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,I)
. This is called the
reparameterization trick
, and it allows the gradients to flow through the sampling step, since each
{\bm{z}}
is a function of
{\bm{\phi}}
.

Appendix D
Adjoint Matching

We briefly review the
adjoint matching
method, introduced in
[
9
]
, which improves on classic adjoint methods for training neural SDEs. We begin with a base generative process

{\textrm{d}}{\bm{Z}}_{t}=f_{\rm b}({\bm{Z}}_{t},{t}){\textrm{d}}{t}+{\sigma}({t}){\textrm{d}}{\bm{B}}_{t},\qquad{\bm{Z}}_{0}\sim q_{0}={\mathcal{N}}(0,I).

(43)

The goal is to fine-tune it by adding a learned control
{\mathfrak{u}}_{\bm{\theta}}(z,t)
such that the controlled process,

{\textrm{d}}{\bm{Z}}_{t}=\left[f_{\rm b}({\bm{Z}}_{t},{t})+{\sigma}({t}){\mathfrak{u}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{Z}}_{t},{t})\right]{\textrm{d}}{t}+{\sigma}({t}){\textrm{d}}{\bm{B}}_{t},

(44)

generates samples from a
tilted
distribution at time
{T}
,

q({\bm{z}})\propto q_{\rm base}({\bm{z}})e^{r({\bm{z}})}

(45)

where
r
is some reward. This is accomplished by minimizing the adjoint matching objective

\begin{gathered}{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm AM}({\mathfrak{u}}_{\bm{\theta}};\mathfrak{Z}):=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{{T}}{\textrm{d}}{t}\,\|{\mathfrak{u}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{z}}_{t},{t})+{\sigma}({t})\tilde{{\bm{a}}}({t};\mathfrak{Z})\|^{2},\quad\mathfrak{Z}\sim q_{\bar{{\mathfrak{u}}}},\ \bar{{\mathfrak{u}}}=\texttt{stopgrad}({\mathfrak{u}}_{\bm{\theta}}),\\
\text{where}\quad\frac{{\textrm{d}}}{{\textrm{d}}{t}}\tilde{{\bm{a}}}({t};\mathfrak{Z})=-\tilde{{\bm{a}}}({t};\mathfrak{Z})^{\top}\nabla f_{\rm b}({\bm{Z}}_{t},{t}),\\
\tilde{{\bm{a}}}_{T}=-\nabla r({\bm{Z}}_{T}).\end{gathered}

(46)

Here
\mathfrak{Z}
denotes an entire path
\mathfrak{Z}=({\bm{Z}}_{t})_{t\in[0,{T}]}
generated by the controlled process
eq.

44
, with
\bar{{\mathfrak{u}}}
.
{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm AM}({\mathfrak{u}}_{\bm{\theta}};{\bm{Z}})
is optimized with a gradient-based method, but
{\bm{Z}}_{t}
and
\tilde{{\bm{a}}}_{t}
should be computed without gradients. Two key features in
eq.

46
are worth highlighting. First, the functional landscape of
{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm AM}
is convex, which leads to more stable training compared to the highly non-convex standard adjoint objective
[
26
]
. Second, the adjoint ODE does
not
involve
\nabla{\mathfrak{u}}_{\bm{\theta}}
, unlike the standard adjoint method. This makes the ODE cheaper to compute, which is why
\tilde{{\bm{a}}}
is called the
lean adjoint
.

The relevance of this method becomes apparent when we note that the optimal encoder from
eq.

7
has precisely the same form as
eq.

45
. Indeed, setting
f_{\rm b}=f_{\rm eq}
,
f_{\bm{\theta}}=f_{\rm eq}+{\sigma}{\mathfrak{u}}_{\bm{\theta}}
, and
r=\frac{1}{\gamma}\log{p}_{\bm{\psi}}({\bm{x}}|{\bm{z}}_{T})
(cf.
eq.

15
),

\min\mathbb{E}\left[{D_{\textrm{KL}}}(\mathbb{Q}\|\mathbb{P})-\frac{1}{\gamma}\log p_{\bm{\psi}}({\bm{x}}|{\bm{z}}_{T})\right]\rightarrow\min\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{{T}}{\textrm{d}}t\|{\mathfrak{u}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{z}}_{t},{t};{\bm{x}})\|^{2}-r({\bm{z}}_{T};{\bm{x}})\right].

(47)

The latter is precisely the starting point from which
eq.

46
is derived in
[
10
]
. Note that we have introduced
{\bm{x}}
-dependence into the control in accordance with the discussion in
section

3
. Once again, we see an encoder training objective that includes regularization pressure, just like
eq.

34
. For this reason, adjoint-matching is poised to suffer from synchronization issues similar to those described in
section

A.1
—the encoder and decoder can fall out of alignment since they are pulling the latent in opposing directions in each decoupled update.

Both adjoint matching and our diffusion encoder require the target
{\bm{Z}}_{T}
to be frozen. A key difference, compared to our diffusion encoder, is that we must store the entirety of each path
\mathfrak{Z}
to integrate the adjoint ODE, and optimize
{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm AM}
. In practice, the paths are generated by an Euler-Maruyama solver (cf.
eq.

16
), with a finite number of steps. On the other hand, the diffusion loss is trained with forward noised samples rather than the adjoint, and these can be produced without solving the forward SDE (cf.
eq.

31
); this is simulation-free training. We also do not need to store the trajectories from the encoding step.

Appendix E
Encoder Architecture

At the heart of our diffusion encoder is a neural network
{\bm{e}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}_{s},{s};{\bm{x}})\equiv{\bm{e}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{z}}_{t},{T}-{t};{\bm{x}})
. It accepts three inputs: the noised latent
{\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}_{s}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{{D_{{\bm{Z}}}}}
, a conditioning image
{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{{\mathsfit{H}}\times{\mathsfit{W}}\times{\mathsfit{C}}}
, and the forward time
{s}\in(0,{T}]
. Its output is an estimate of the entropy-matching vector, and has the same shape as
{\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}_{s}
.

The network
{\bm{e}}_{\bm{\theta}}
controls the evolution of the initial distribution
q({\bm{z}}_{0})
to the posterior
q({\bm{z}}_{T}|{\bm{x}})
(cf.
eq.

24
). The value of
{\bm{e}}_{\bm{\theta}}
at each time slice parameterizes an
{\bm{x}}
-dependent Gaussian kernel (see
section

3
). Thus, the network effects a continuum of minuscule transformations that gradually encode information from
{\bm{x}}
into
{\bm{z}}_{T}
. The encoder architecture must, therefore, include an effective mechanism to compress
{\bm{x}}
into
{\bm{z}}_{t}
in a
{t}
-dependent way. This can be accomplished with attention
[
40
]
.

E.1
Attention

To review, cross-attention is the transformation

{\mathbf{Y}}({\mathbf{Z}})={\textrm{Attention}}({\mathbf{Q}},{\mathbf{K}},{\mathbf{V}})\equiv\mathrm{softmax}\left[\frac{{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{K}}^{\top}}{\sqrt{{D_{\rm k}}}}\right]{\mathbf{V}},

(48)

where
{\mathbf{Z}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N_{Q}\times{D_{{\bm{Z}}}}}
and
{\mathbf{X}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N_{K}\times{D_{{\bm{X}}}}}
, with

\displaystyle{\mathbf{Q}}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{Z}}{\mathbf{W}}_{Q}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N_{Q}\times{D_{\rm k}}},\quad{\mathbf{W}}_{Q}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{{D_{{\bm{Z}}}}\times{D_{\rm k}}}

(49)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{K}}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{X}}{\mathbf{W}}_{K}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N_{K}\times{D_{\rm k}}},\quad{\mathbf{W}}_{K}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{{D_{{\bm{X}}}}\times{D_{\rm k}}}

\displaystyle{\mathbf{V}}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{X}}{\mathbf{W}}_{V}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N_{K}\times{D_{\rm v}}},\quad{\mathbf{W}}_{V}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{{D_{{\bm{X}}}}\times{D_{\rm v}}}

Therefore,
{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{K}}^{\top}
has dimension
N_{Q}\times N_{K}
, and the softmax is taken over the key (column) axis. Each row can be viewed as the conditional probability of the keys, given the query. Each of the
N_{Q}
queries is assigned
N_{K}
probabilities this way. These probabilities are then used to linearly combine the
N_{K}
value vectors in
{\mathbf{V}}
, each of
{D_{\rm v}}
dimensions, to produce the final output of shape
N_{Q}\times{D_{\rm v}}
. The weights
{\mathbf{W}}_{\bullet}
are learnable parameters. In self-attention
{\mathbf{Z}}={\mathbf{X}}
.

In multi-head attention, there are
H
attention heads, which are combined as

{\mathbf{Y}}({\mathbf{Z}})=\textrm{Concat}[{\mathbf{H}}_{1},\dots,{\mathbf{H}}_{H}]{\mathbf{W}}_{O},\quad\textrm{where}\ {\mathbf{H}}_{h}={\textrm{Attention}}({\mathbf{Q}}_{h},{\mathbf{K}}_{h},{\mathbf{V}}_{h}).

(50)

Each
{\mathbf{H}}_{h}
has dimension
N_{Q}\times{D_{\rm v}}
, so the concatenated matrix has dimension
N_{Q}\times H{D_{\rm v}}
. This is transformed by the linear matrix
{\mathbf{W}}_{O}
of dimension
H{D_{\rm v}}\times{D_{{\bm{Z}}}}
to produce
{\mathbf{Y}}
of dimension
N_{Q}\times{D_{{\bm{Z}}}}
, which is the same as that of the original input matrix
{\mathbf{Z}}
. Each position in
{\mathbf{X}}
looks at all positions in
{\mathbf{Z}}
, computes relevant probabilities, and pulls out a weighted combination of information from
{\mathbf{Z}}
. In short, “
{\mathbf{Z}}
attends to
{\mathbf{X}}
.”

For our purposes, the key point is that attention is an input-dependent transformation. For example, by using
{\bm{z}}_{t}
to attend to
{\bm{x}}
, we can extract different levels of information about
{\bm{x}}
depending on where we are along the transformation
{\bm{z}}_{0}\to{\bm{z}}_{T}
. To make this precise, consider the problem of encoding an image of shape
[{\mathsfit{H}},{\mathsfit{W}},{\mathsfit{C}}]
into a 1D feature vector with ‘feat’ number of components. First, we flatten the image so that
[{\mathsfit{H}},{\mathsfit{W}},{\mathsfit{C}}]\to[{\mathsfit{H}}{\mathsfit{W}},{\mathsfit{C}}]
. This will be
{\mathbf{X}}
, so
N_{K}={\mathsfit{H}}{\mathsfit{W}},{D_{{\bm{X}}}}={\mathsfit{C}}
. Next, recall that the final output
{\mathbf{Y}}({\mathbf{Z}})
has the same shape as
{\mathbf{Z}}
, so we choose the query
{\mathbf{Z}}
to have the same dimensions as the desired feature vector. Therefore,
N_{Q}=1,{D_{{\bm{Z}}}}={\textrm{feat}}
. If we choose
H=4
heads in
eq.

50
, then
4{D_{\rm v}}={D_{{\bm{Z}}}}\implies{D_{\rm v}}={\textrm{feat}}/4
. With these choices,
{\mathbf{Z}}\in{R}^{1\times{\textrm{feat}}}
and
{\mathbf{X}}\in{R}^{{\mathsfit{H}}{\mathsfit{W}}\times{\mathsfit{C}}}
, and
eq.

49
is

\displaystyle{\mathbf{Q}}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{X}}{\mathbf{W}}_{Q}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{1\times{D_{\rm k}}},\quad{\mathbf{W}}_{Q}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{{\textrm{feat}}\times{D_{\rm k}}}

(51)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{K}}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{Z}}{\mathbf{W}}_{K}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{{\mathsfit{H}}{\mathsfit{W}}\times{D_{\rm k}}},\quad{\mathbf{W}}_{K}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{{\mathsfit{C}}\times{D_{\rm k}}}

\displaystyle{\mathbf{V}}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{Z}}{\mathbf{W}}_{V}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{{\mathsfit{H}}{\mathsfit{W}}\times{\textrm{feat}}/4},\quad{\mathbf{W}}_{V}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{{\mathsfit{C}}\times{\textrm{feat}}/4}

We will choose
{D_{\rm k}}
shortly. For now, notice that
{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{K}}^{\top}\in{R}^{1\times{\mathsfit{H}}{\mathsfit{W}}}
is a row vector. Then,
\mathrm{softmax}[{\mathbf{Q}}{\mathbf{K}}^{\top}/\sqrt{{D_{\rm v}}}]
can be interpreted as probabilities over
{\mathsfit{H}}{\mathsfit{W}}
pixels—it weighs how much each pixel influences (
1/4
of) the final feature vector. The rows of
{\mathbf{V}}
are
{\mathsfit{H}}{\mathsfit{W}}
‘basis’ vectors built up from the image
{\mathbf{X}}
itself—each of the
{\mathsfit{H}}{\mathsfit{W}}
pixels in the image is converted to a basis vector of length
{\textrm{feat}}/4
by
{\mathbf{W}}_{V}
, and then mixed according to the
{\mathsfit{H}}{\mathsfit{W}}
probabilities from the softmax.

Having multiple attention heads allows the same pixels to be mapped to different feature basis vectors. These basis vectors can also get mixed in different proportions since the softmax in a different head can produce a new set of probabilities for the same
{\mathbf{X}}
and
{\mathbf{Z}}
. So each
{\textrm{feat}}/4
segment in
{\mathbf{Y}}
can encode different information about the image.
Finally, we turn to the
{\mathbf{W}}_{Q}
and
{\mathbf{W}}_{K}
matrices. In
eq.

48
,
{\mathbf{W}}_{Q}{\mathbf{W}}_{K}^{\top}
induces a rank-
{D_{\rm k}}
attention bottleneck on the logits. A similar bottleneck also exists in the value pathway,
{\mathbf{W}}^{h}_{V}{\mathbf{W}}^{h}_{O}
, where
{\mathbf{X}}
is projected down to
{D_{\rm v}}
and then back up to
{D_{{\bm{Z}}}}
.

The calculation above is meant as an illustration. In practice, it is wasteful to attend to the input image
{\bm{x}}
at its native resolution. Instead, we run the image through a shallow convolutional network to scale it from
{\mathsfit{H}}\times{\mathsfit{W}}\times{\mathsfit{C}}\to({\mathsfit{H}}/4)\times({\mathsfit{W}}/4)\times{\mathsfit{C}}^{\prime}
(see
section

E.3
). The query vector can be
{\bm{z}}_{t}
or an embedding of the time variable itself.

E.2
Input Embeddings

The time variable is lifted into a higher-dimensional feature space using random Fourier features, which are fixed at initialization and receive no gradient updates. This is particularly beneficial for low-dimensional inputs, which would otherwise be spectrally underrepresented in the network’s feature space
[
38
]
. Concretely, for a signal
u
and a frozen random matrix
{\mathbf{B}}
drawn from
\mathcal{N}(0,I)
and scaled by
\gamma
, the embedding is

\phi(u;{\mathbf{B}})=\bigl[\sin(2\pi u{\mathbf{B}}^{\top}),\,\cos(2\pi u{\mathbf{B}}^{\top})\bigr]\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2M},

(52)

where
M
is the
mapping size
. For the time coordinate
{s}
, we use a dedicated matrix
{\mathbf{B}}_{s}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{M\times 1}
with scale
\gamma_{s}=10
, giving a
2M
-dimensional time embedding. Optionally, in cases where
{D_{{\bm{Z}}}}
is low, we also found it useful to embed the latent
{\bm{z}}_{t}
, with its own
{\mathbf{B}}_{\bm{z}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{M\times{D_{{\bm{Z}}}}}
with scale
\gamma_{\bm{z}}=10
. Both embeddings are then projected to width
M
via a learned linear layer followed by a SiLU activation.

E.3
Weak Image Encoder

The input image
{\bm{x}}
is downsized with a shallow convolutional feature extractor, which we call the

weak encoder
, before it is sent to the attention block. This stage serves to reduce the computational overhead of attending to the whole image
{\bm{x}}
, without compressing away too much information from it. It consists of two strided convolutional layers, each with a
4\times 4
kernel, stride 2, and
same
padding, activated by SiLU:

{\bm{x}}_{\rm enc}=\mathrm{WeakEncoder}({\bm{x}})\in\mathbb{R}^{{\mathsfit{H}}/4\times{\mathsfit{W}}/4\times{\mathsfit{C}}^{\prime}}.

(53)

The spatial grid is then flattened to produce a sequence of
N_{K}={\mathsfit{H}}/4\times{\mathsfit{W}}/4
‘patch’ tokens of dimension
{\mathsfit{C}}^{\prime}
,

{\bm{x}}_{\rm flat}=\mathrm{reshape}({\bm{x}}_{\rm enc})\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{K}\times C^{\prime}}.

(54)

This is the input
{\mathbf{X}}
to the attention block in
eq.

48
. By scaling down the spatial dimensions by a factor of
4
in each direction, we reduce the sequence length
N_{K}\to N_{K}/16
, shrinking the cost of the cross-attention operation by the same factor since it is linear in
N_{K}
(cf.
eq.

51
).

Positional encodings and multi-scale tokens

The raw sequence in
eq.

54
is permutation-invariant—patches at opposite corners of the image are distinguishable only by content, not by location. We therefore inject 2-D sinusoidal positional encodings into the fine-scale grid
{\mathsfit{H}}/4\times{\mathsfit{W}}/4
and concatenate a coarse-scale token sequence obtained by average pooling to
{\mathsfit{H}}/8\times{\mathsfit{W}}/8
before the same positional encoding step. Both token sequences are passed through a shared linear projection before being concatenated,

{\bm{x}}_{\rm tokens}=\bigl[{\bm{x}}_{\rm fine}+\mathrm{PE_{fine}}\,;\;{\bm{x}}_{\rm coarse}+\mathrm{PE_{coarse}}\bigr]\in{\mathbb{R}}^{(N_{K}+N_{K}/4)\times D_{\rm tok}},

(55)

where
N_{K}=({\mathsfit{H}}/4)^{2}
and
N_{K}/4=({\mathsfit{H}}/8)^{2}
for square inputs. The dual-scale design provides both fine-grained texture detail and low-frequency layout information at a 25% overhead in token count.

E.4
Cross-Attention Image Conditioning

The projected time embedding
{\mathbf{e}}_{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
from
section

E.2
serves as the single query vector for multi-head cross-attention against the image token sequence:

{\bm{x}}_{\rm attended}=\mathrm{MultiHeadAttn}\!\bigl({\mathbf{Q}}={\mathbf{e}}_{s}[{:},\,\mathrm{None},{:}],\;{\mathbf{K}}={\bm{x}}_{\rm flat},\;{\mathbf{V}}={\bm{x}}_{\rm flat}\bigr)\in\mathbb{R}^{M},

(56)

with
H=4
heads and key/value dimension
M
. Setting the key–value dimension equal to
M
ensures no information bottleneck is introduced through the projection matrices
{\mathbf{W}}_{K}
and
{\mathbf{W}}_{V}
. The attended vector summarizes the patches that the current time state
{\mathbf{e}}_{s}
finds relevant, conditioning the subsequent score network on that selection. The global conditioning vector is then assembled from the time embedding and the attended image summary

{\bm{c}}=\mathrm{Dense}\bigl(\mathrm{SiLU}([{\mathbf{e}}_{s};\,{\bm{x}}_{\rm attended}])\bigr)\in\mathbb{R}^{M},

(57)

where
[\,\cdot\,;\,\cdot\,]
denotes concatenation across the feature axis. In the simplest design, this vector
{\bm{c}}
is computed once and reused at every residual block in the backbone described below. A more expressive alternative—performing cross-attention at every block using the current hidden state as the query—is described at the end of the next section.

E.5
MLP Backbone with Adaptive Conditioning

The backbone is a depth-
L
residual MLP with hidden widths given by the sequence
\texttt{features}=[d_{1},\ldots,d_{L}]
. Each block
i=1,\ldots,L
applies the following operations to the current hidden state
{\bm{h}}
:

1.

Projection.

{\bm{h}}\leftarrow\mathrm{Dense}(d_{i})({\bm{h}})
.

2.

Layer normalization.

{\bm{h}}\leftarrow\mathrm{LayerNorm}({\bm{h}})
, standardizing each sample to zero mean and unit variance.

3.

Adaptive modulation (FiLM/AdaLN).
The conditioning vector
{\bm{c}}^{(i)}
is projected to a scale–shift pair
(\rho_{i},\beta_{i})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{i}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}
via a single linear layer, and applied as

{\bm{h}}\leftarrow{\bm{h}}\odot(1+\rho_{i})+\beta_{i}.

(58)

Multiplying by
(1+\rho_{i})
rather than
\rho_{i}
alone initializes the modulation near the identity, which improves the stability of the training. This is the Adaptive Layer Normalization (AdaLN) design used in DiT
[
31
]
.

4.

Activation.

{\bm{h}}\leftarrow\mathrm{SiLU}({\bm{h}})
.

5.

Residual connection.
If
{\bm{h}}_{\rm in}
and
{\bm{h}}
share the same width,
{\bm{h}}\leftarrow{\bm{h}}+{\bm{h}}_{\rm in}
; otherwise, a learned linear projection aligns the dimensions before addition.

After the final block, a linear read-out projects
{\bm{h}}
to
{\mathbb{R}}^{{D_{{\bm{Z}}}}}
, producing the score network output
{\bm{e}}_{\bm{\theta}}
.

Per-block cross-attention

When
{\bm{c}}
is fixed across all blocks as in
eq.

57
, the image context is computed from the initial latent embedding and reused at every depth. As the hidden state
{\bm{h}}
evolves through successive nonlinear transformations, this fixed summary grows increasingly misaligned with the current representation. To address this, the improved architecture performs cross-attention at every residual
block, using the current hidden state as the query:

{\bm{x}}_{\rm attended}^{(i)}=\mathrm{MultiHeadAttn}^{(i)}\!\bigl({\bm{h}}^{(i)},\;{\bm{x}}_{\rm tokens}\bigr),\qquad{\bm{c}}^{(i)}=\mathrm{Dense}^{(i)}\!\bigl([{\mathbf{e}}_{s};\,{\bm{x}}_{\rm attended}^{(i)}]\bigr),

(59)

so that the scale and shift parameters of every FiLM modulation are derived from an image context that is semantically coherent with
{\bm{h}}^{(i)}
. Each block maintains its own Q/K/V/O projection weights.

E.6
Decoder Architecture

The decoder maps a latent vector
{\bm{z}}
back to image space through a sequence of convolutional upsampling stages. The latent is first projected by a dense layer to a small spatial grid, which is then progressively upsampled using transposed convolutions, each followed by a residual refinement block. Optionally, self-attention blocks with layer normalization can be inserted at low-resolution feature maps to capture long-range spatial dependencies during upsampling. A final transposed convolutional layer maps the feature map to the output image dimensions, with a sigmoid activation to constrain pixel values to
[0,1]
.
