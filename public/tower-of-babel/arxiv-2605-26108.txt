Reinforcing Few-step Generators via Reward-Tilted Distribution Matching
Yushi Huang, Xiangxin Zhou, Ruoyu Wang, Chi Zhang, Jun Zhang, Tianyu Pang

Abstract
Recent advances in few-step diffusion distillation have enabled efficient image generation, yet aligning these models with human preferences remains challenging. We propose Reward-Tilted Distribution Matching Distillation (RTDMD), a two-stage framework that unifies distribution matching distillation with reward-guided reinforcement learning for few-step flow generators. We show that minimizing the KL divergence to a reward-tilted teacher distribution naturally decomposes into a distribution matching term and a reward maximization term. In the first stage, we introduce Ambient-Consistent Distribution Matching Distillation (AC-DMD), which performs subinterval-wise distribution matching and augments the fake score objective with a consistency regularizer to help the fake score model track the shifting generator distribution under limited updates. In the second stage, we jointly optimize both terms: for the reward maximization term, we derive a hybrid policy gradient that combines a GRPO-style estimator for the stochastic intermediate transitions with direct reward backpropagation through the deterministic final step, and further introduce step-subset GRPO (SubGRPO) to reduce variance. Experiments on SD3, SD3.5, and FLUX.2 demonstrate that RTDMD establishes new state-of-the-art results across preference, aesthetic, and compositional metrics with only 4 inference steps, outperforming previous few-step text-to-image generation methods. Code and models are available at https://github.com/Harahan/RTDMD .

Preliminaries

2.1
Diffusion and Flow Models

Diffusion and flow-based generative models
[
65
,
25
]
define a continuous probability path
\{p_{t}\}_{t\in[0,1]}
that connects the data distribution
p_{0}
to a simple prior
p_{1}
(typically a standard Gaussian). A common Gaussian interpolation is
q_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t}\mid{\bm{x}}_{0})=\mathcal{N}(\alpha_{t}{\bm{x}}_{0},\sigma_{t}^{2}\mathbf{I})
,
where
{\bm{x}}_{0}\sim p_{0}
and
{\bm{\epsilon}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})
, so that
{\bm{x}}_{t}=\alpha_{t}{\bm{x}}_{0}+\sigma_{t}{\bm{\epsilon}}
and
p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})=\int q_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t}\mid{\bm{x}}_{0})p_{0}({\bm{x}}_{0})\,d{\bm{x}}_{0}
. Here
\alpha_{t}
and
\sigma_{t}
specify the noise schedule.

Sampling is described by the probability-flow Ordinary Differential Equation (PF-ODE)
[
38
]

\frac{\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}={\bm{v}}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)
,
where
{\bm{v}}({\bm{x}},t)
is the
marginal velocity field
transporting the density
p_{t}
. Its relation
[
48
]
to the
score function

s({\bm{x}},t)=\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\log p_{t}({\bm{x}})
is

{\bm{v}}({\bm{x}},t)=\frac{\dot{\alpha}_{t}}{\alpha_{t}}{\bm{x}}+\left(\frac{\dot{\alpha}_{t}}{\alpha_{t}}\sigma_{t}^{2}-\dot{\sigma}_{t}\sigma_{t}\right)s({\bm{x}},t).

(1)

Thus, the score function indicates how the density changes locally, while the marginal velocity determines how samples move along the probability path.

In this work, we adopt flow matching with the rectified schedule
[
38
,
16
]
, namely
\alpha_{t}=1-t
and
\sigma_{t}=t
, which gives the linear path
{\bm{x}}_{t}=(1-t){\bm{x}}_{0}+t{\bm{\epsilon}}
. For a fixed pair
({\bm{x}}_{0},{\bm{\epsilon}})
, the conditional velocity is simply
{\bm{v}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t}\mid{\bm{x}}_{0},{\bm{\epsilon}})={\bm{\epsilon}}-{\bm{x}}_{0}
, and the marginal velocity satisfies

{\bm{v}}({\bm{x}},t)=\mathbb{E}[{\bm{\epsilon}}-{\bm{x}}_{0}\mid{\bm{x}}_{t}={\bm{x}}]=-\frac{1}{1-t}{\bm{x}}-\frac{t}{1-t}s({\bm{x}},t).

(2)

Flow matching trains a neural velocity field
{\bm{v}}_{\theta}
by regressing it to the conditional target velocity
[
38
]
:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CFM}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{t,{\bm{x}}_{0},{\bm{\epsilon}}}\bigl[w(t)\|{\bm{v}}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)-({\bm{\epsilon}}-{\bm{x}}_{0})\|_{2}^{2}\bigr],\quad{\bm{x}}_{t}=(1-t){\bm{x}}_{0}+t{\bm{\epsilon}},

(3)

where
w(t)
is an optional weighting function. This objective is commonly referred to as the conditional flow matching (CFM) loss.

2.2
Distribution Matching Distillation

Sampling from a pretrained flow model typically requires many function evaluations, motivating distillation into a few-step generator.
Let
{\bm{v}}_{\psi}
denote the pretrained
teacher
velocity field, and let
p_{\psi}
be its induced distribution.

Distribution Matching Distillation (DMD)
[
84
,
83
]
trains a few-step
student
generator
G_{\theta}
with
{\bm{x}}_{0}=G_{\theta}({\bm{\epsilon}})
, where
{\bm{\epsilon}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})
, so that its induced distribution
p_{\theta}
matches
p_{\psi}
.
A natural objective is the reverse Kullback–Leibler (KL)
\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{\theta}\,\|\,p_{\psi})
.
Because this divergence can be difficult to optimize directly in data space when the two distributions have limited overlap, DMD instead compares their noised marginals in an ambient space.
Specifically, for
t\sim\mathrm{Unif}[0,1]
and an independent
{\bm{\epsilon}}^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})
, it defines

{\bm{x}}_{t}=(1-t){\bm{x}}_{0}+t{\bm{\epsilon}}^{\prime}
,
which induces marginals
p_{\theta,t}
and
p_{r,t}
for the student and teacher, respectively.
The resulting time-averaged reverse KL yields a generator gradient proportional to the difference between the student and teacher scores at
{\bm{x}}_{t}
.

Using Eq. (
2
) to convert between velocities and scores, DMD writes the generator update as

\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\text{DMD}}=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\epsilon}},t,{\bm{\epsilon}}^{\prime}}\big[w_{t}\alpha_{t}\bigl(s_{\phi}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)-s_{\psi}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)\bigr)\nabla_{\theta}G_{\theta}({\bm{\epsilon}})\big],

(4)

where
w_{t}>0
is a time-dependent weight and
\alpha_{t}=1-t
comes from
\partial{\bm{x}}_{t}/\partial{\bm{x}}_{0}
.

Since the student score is not available in closed form, DMD introduces an auxiliary
fake
velocity field
{\bm{v}}_{\phi}
to track the current student distribution
p_{\theta}
.
Via Eq. (
2
),
{\bm{v}}_{\phi}
defines the fake score
s_{\phi}
, which serves as a surrogate for the student score in Eq. (
4
).
The fake velocity is trained with the conditional flow-matching objective

\mathcal{L}_{f}(\phi)=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\epsilon}},t,{\bm{\epsilon}}^{\prime}}\big[\|{\bm{v}}_{\phi}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)-({\bm{\epsilon}}^{\prime}-{\bm{x}}_{0})\|^{2}\big].

(5)

At optimum,
{\bm{v}}_{\phi}
recovers the marginal velocity field of the current student distribution, providing the score estimate required by the DMD update.
DMD therefore alternates between updating the student generator
G_{\theta}
and training the fake model
{\bm{v}}_{\phi}
to track it.

3
Method

We present
Reward-Tilted Distribution Matching Distillation
(RTDMD), a principled framework for training high-quality few-step generators (an overall algorithm can be found in App.
I
).
Let
p_{\psi}
denote the distribution induced by the pretrained teacher model.
While DMD
[
84
,
83
]
aims to replicate
p_{\psi}
, the teacher distribution itself is not necessarily aligned with human preferences, which means it can assign equal probability to both high-reward and low-reward samples. A natural remedy is to up-weight high-reward regions of
p_{\psi}
while down-weighting low-reward ones. Therefore, we define the
reward-tilted teacher distribution
as

\tilde{p}_{\psi}({\bm{x}})=\frac{p_{\psi}({\bm{x}})\exp(\beta r({\bm{x}}))}{Z},

(6)

where
r({\bm{x}})
is a scalar reward function,
\beta\geq 0
controls the reward strength, and
Z
is the normalizing constant.
We optimize the few-step generator
G_{\theta}
by minimizing

\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{\theta}\|\tilde{p}_{\psi})
.
Since
\log\tilde{p}_{\psi}({\bm{x}})=\log p_{\psi}({\bm{x}})+\beta r({\bm{x}})-\log Z
, and
Z
is independent of
\theta
, we have
\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{\theta}\|\tilde{p}_{\psi})=\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{\theta}\|{p}_{\psi})-\beta\mathbb{E}_{\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{0}\sim p_{\theta}}[r(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{0})]+\log Z
and

\nabla_{\theta}\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{\theta}\|\tilde{p}_{\psi})=\underbrace{\nabla_{\theta}\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{\theta}\|{p}_{\psi})}_{\text{distribution matching}}-\beta\underbrace{\nabla_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{0}\sim p_{\theta}}[r(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{0})]}_{\text{reward maximization}}.

(7)

This decomposition shows that minimizing the KL to the reward-tilted distribution naturally separates into a
distribution matching
term and a
reward maximization
term. This motivates our two-stage framework: we first perform distribution matching as a cold start via
Ambient-Consistent DMD
(AC-DMD, Sec.
3.1
), and then jointly optimize both terms using a
hybrid policy gradient
with
step-subset GRPO
for the reward term (Sec.
3.2
).

).

, and

3.1
Ambient-Consistent Distribution Matching Distillation

Existing DMD methods adopt either the deterministic Euler ODE sampler
[
19
]
or the consistency model (CM) sampler
[
84
,
83
]
for few-step generation. To unify these choices under a single framework and facilitate the subsequent policy-gradient derivation (Sec.
3.2
), we first employ coefficient-preserving sampling (CPS)
[
71
]
, which encompasses both as special cases by a predefined hyperparameter
\eta
(see App.
C
for the full formula).
Under CPS, each generation step consists of a denoising prediction followed by noise injection, and it also ensures that the noise level of the latent variable remains consistent with the predefined scheduler at every timestep.

To be more specific, we use a
K
-step generator with
K=4
and a decreasing timestep schedule
0=t_{K}<\cdots<t_{1}<t_{0}=1
.
Starting from
\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{0}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})
, step
k
takes the current latent
\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k-1}}
and outputs an
x
-prediction,
\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{\mathrm{pred}}^{(k)}=G_{\theta}(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k-1}},t_{k-1}),\,k=1,\dots,K,

which is the sampler output under the
x
-parameterization, rather than a clean sample itself. The next latent
\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}}
is a linear combination of the
x
-prediction
\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{\mathrm{pred}}^{(k)}
, the current latent
\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k-1}}
, and a freshly sampled Gaussian noise
{\bm{\epsilon}}_{k}
. Here,
\eta\in[0,1]
controls the sampling stochasticity:
\eta=0
recovers the deterministic Euler sampler, while
\eta>0
injects noise at each step.

Ambient distribution matching distillation.
Since CPS (
\eta>0
) injects noise at intermediate steps, the generator output
\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}}
at
t_{k}>0
could no longer be a clean sample but a noisy latent at noise level
t_{k}
. The standard
DMD
[
84
,
83
]
, which assumes clean samples and performs score matching over the full interval
[0,1]
, is therefore no longer directly applicable. We re-derive the distribution
matching objective on the subinterval
[t_{k},1]
conditioned on the noisy intermediate, and term this
Ambient Distribution Matching Distillation
(A-DMD).

Concretely, let
p_{\theta}^{(k)}
denote the distribution of
\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}}
after
k
steps. To train step
k
, we match the teacher distribution on the subinterval
[t_{k},1]
. Under the rectified schedule
[
38
,
16
]
, we re-noise
\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}}
to any level
t\in[t_{k},1]
via
{\bm{x}}_{t}^{(k)}=\alpha_{k}(t)\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}}+\sigma_{k}(t){\bm{\epsilon}}
, where
\alpha_{k}(t)=\frac{1-t}{1-t_{k}}
,
\sigma_{k}(t)=\frac{t-t_{k}}{1-t_{k}}
, and
{\bm{\epsilon}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})
. Let
p_{\theta,t}^{(k)}
denote the resulting student marginal at noise level
t
. We minimize the reverse KL:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{gen}}^{(k)}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{t\sim\mathcal{U}[t_{k},1]}\bigl[\lambda_{k}(t)\,\mathrm{KL}(p_{\theta,t}^{(k)}\,\|\,p_{\psi,t})\bigr],

(8)

where
p_{\psi,t}
is the teacher marginal and
\lambda_{k}(t)
is a timestep-dependent weight.

Since the student score
\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\log p_{\theta,t}^{(k)}({\bm{x}})
is intractable, following DMD
[
84
,
83
]
, we introduce a fake score model
s_{\phi}({\bm{x}},t)
to approximate it, yielding the practical generator gradient

\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{gen}}^{(k)}\approx-\mathbb{E}_{t,{\bm{\epsilon}},\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}}}\left[\lambda_{k}(t)\alpha_{k}(t)\,\bigl(s_{\psi}({\bm{x}}_{t}^{(k)},t)-s_{\phi}({\bm{x}}_{t}^{(k)},t)\bigr)^{\top}\frac{\partial\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}}}{\partial\theta}\right].

(9)

This form (see App.
B
for the detailed derivation) makes the training signal entirely local to the subinterval
[t_{k},1]
: the teacher score
s_{\psi}({\bm{x}}_{t}^{(k)},t)
provides the target direction, while the fake score
s_{\phi}({\bm{x}}_{t}^{(k)},t)
compensates for the intractable student marginal score.

To train
s_{\phi}
, we fit it on the same interval
[t_{k},1]
using denoising score matching (DSM):

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fake}}^{(k)}(\phi)=\mathbb{E}_{t\sim\mathcal{U}[t_{k},1],\,\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}},\,{\bm{\epsilon}}}\left[\omega_{k}(t)\,\left\|s_{\phi}({\bm{x}}_{t}^{(k)},t)-\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\log q_{t}^{(k)}({\bm{x}}\mid\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}})\right\|_{2}^{2}\right],

(10)

where

\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\log q_{t}^{(k)}({\bm{x}}\mid\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}})=\left(\alpha_{k}(t)\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}}-{\bm{x}}\right)/\sigma_{k}(t)^{2}
is the conditional score of the Gaussian corruption kernel, and
\omega_{k}(t)\!>\!0
is a timestep-dependent weight (a design choice independent of
\lambda_{k}(t)
in Eq. (
8
)).

Stabilizing fake score training via consistency regularization.
However, when
t_{k}>0
, the fake score model
s_{\phi}
is trained on corrupted intermediate latents
\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}}
rather than clean samples. Although the DSM objective in Eq. (
10
) is theoretically unbiased (its optimal solution is the true student marginal score
\nabla_{\bm{x}}\log p_{\theta,t}^{(k)}
as proved in App.
D
), a practical challenge arises: the generator
G_{\theta}
is updated concurrently, so the target distribution
p_{\theta}^{(k)}
shifts at every training iteration. With only a limited number of fake score updates per generator step,
s_{\phi}
must track this moving distribution under a tight sample and compute budget, making accurate estimation difficult.

To stabilize fake score training, we introduce a consistency regularizer
[
11
,
12
]
. The key insight is that the optimal fake score model
satisfies a self-consistency property (see App.
E
for a detailed proof): for any
t^{\prime\prime}<t^{\prime}
, its
x
-prediction at
t^{\prime}
must equal the expected
x
-prediction after one reverse-diffusion step to
t^{\prime\prime}
,

i.e.
,
\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{\phi}({\bm{x}}_{t^{\prime}},t^{\prime})=\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{{\bm{x}}}_{t^{\prime\prime}}\sim p_{\phi}({\bm{x}}_{t^{\prime\prime}}\mid{\bm{x}}_{t^{\prime}})}[\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{\phi}(\tilde{{\bm{x}}}_{t^{\prime\prime}},t^{\prime\prime})]
, where
\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{\phi}({\bm{x}},t)
denotes the fake score model’s
x
-prediction. This couples the fake score predictions across different timesteps, reducing the effective degrees of freedom and lowering the overall estimation variance.

Concretely, writing the fake score model in its
x
-prediction form, we penalize violations of this property:

\!\!\!\!\!\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cons}}^{(k)}(\phi)=\mathbb{E}_{t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime},\,\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}}\!\sim p_{\theta}^{(k)},\,{\bm{x}}_{t^{\prime}}\!\sim q_{t^{\prime}}^{(k)}(\cdot|\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}})}\!\left[\left\|\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{\phi}({\bm{x}}_{t^{\prime}},t^{\prime})-\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{{\bm{x}}}_{t^{\prime\prime}}\!\sim p_{\phi}({\bm{x}}_{t^{\prime\prime}}\mid{\bm{x}}_{t^{\prime}})}\!\bigl[\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{\phi}(\tilde{{\bm{x}}}_{t^{\prime\prime}},t^{\prime\prime})\bigr]\right\|_{2}^{2}\right],

(11)

where
p_{\phi}({\bm{x}}_{t^{\prime\prime}}\mid{\bm{x}}_{t^{\prime}})
is the fake score model’s reverse transition kernel from
t^{\prime}
to
t^{\prime\prime}
. In practice, we use an approximated estimator following
Daras et al. 2024
(see App.
F
). We choose
t^{\prime}
and
t^{\prime\prime}
to be close so that the consistency term remains local and can be estimated efficiently with a single transition step. The final fake-score objective is

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fake\mbox{-}total}}^{(k)}(\phi)=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fake}}^{(k)}(\phi)+\gamma\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cons}}^{(k)}(\phi).

(12)

Intuitively, Eq. (
10
) provides pointwise score supervision at each noise level, while the consistency term couples nearby timesteps by requiring them to predict the same underlying clean sample, thereby reducing the variance of ambient fake-score training.

Overall, we refer to our method as
Ambient-Consistent Distribution Matching Distillation
(AC-DMD), reflecting that the fake score model is trained on noisy intermediate latents (the “ambient” setting) and regularized by a consistency loss to improve estimation quality.

3.2
Reinforcing the Few-step Generator

After the cold start with AC-DMD, we proceed to the second stage: jointly optimizing both terms in Eq. (
7
). The distribution matching term is handled by AC-DMD as before; we now focus on deriving efficient gradient estimators for the reward maximization term.

Few-step generator as a policy.

The few-step generator induces a
K
-step policy over the latent trajectory

\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{0}}\!\to\!\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{1}}\!\to\!\cdots\!\to\!\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{K}}
.
At each step, the CPS update combines the generator’s
x
-prediction with the current latent and injected Gaussian noise
1
1

1

We set
\eta>0
throughout this work, as it naturally introduces stochasticity into the sampling trajectory, which is essential for exploration in reinforcement learning.
.
As a result, for the first
K-1
steps, the transition defines a Gaussian policy

\pi^{(k)}_{\theta}(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}}\mid\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k-1}})=\mathcal{N}\!\bigl(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}};\,{\bm{\mu}}^{(k)}_{\theta}(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k-1}}),\,\sigma_{k}^{2}{\mathbf{I}}\bigr),\qquad k=1,\dots,K-1,

(13)

where
{\bm{\mu}}^{(k)}_{\theta}
is determined by the CPS update (App.
C
) and
\sigma_{k}=t_{k}\sin(\eta\pi/2)
.
The final step is deterministic: since
t_{K}=0
, the noise term vanishes and
\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{0}=G_{\theta}(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{K-1}},t_{K-1})
.
Therefore, the few-step generative process is a hybrid policy consisting of
K-1
stochastic Gaussian steps followed by one deterministic step.

Hybrid policy gradient.
As a result, the reward gradient (
i.e.
,
\nabla_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{0}\sim p_{\theta}}[r(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{0})]
in Eq. (
7
)) naturally decomposes into a contribution from the stochastic intermediate transitions and a contribution from the deterministic final mapping.

Specifically, let

\tau=(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{0}},\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{1}},\dots,\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{K-1}},\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{0})

denote a generated trajectory. Then

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{0}\sim p_{\theta}}[r(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{0})]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K-1}\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim p_{\theta}}\!\left[r(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{0})\,\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}^{(k)}(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}}\mid\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k-1}})\right]

(14)

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim p_{\theta}}\!\left[\bigl(\nabla_{\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{0}}r(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{0})\bigr)^{\top}\partial_{\theta}G_{\theta}(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{K-1}},t_{K-1})\right].

The first term is a REINFORCE-style estimator and accounts for how the parameters affect the distribution of the stochastic intermediate states, while the second term differentiates the deterministic final denoising step (a formal derivation is provided in App.
G
; see Prop.
G.1
). Since the reward is typically differentiable, we estimate the second term by directly backpropagating through
G_{\theta}
:

\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{det}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bigl(\nabla_{\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{0}}r(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{0}^{(i)})\bigr)^{\top}\partial_{\theta}G_{\theta}(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{K-1}}^{(i)},t_{K-1}).

(15)

For the first term, directly using the REINFORCE-style term leads to high variance. Following GRPO
[
64
]
, we reduce variance of it by sampling a group of
N
trajectories
\{\tau_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
per prompt and replacing the raw reward
r
with a group-normalized advantage
A_{i}=(r_{i}-\bar{r})/\operatorname{std}(\{r_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
, where
r_{i}=r(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{0}^{(i)})
is the reward of the
i
-th trajectory and
\bar{r}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}r_{j}
.

Step-subset GRPO with shared noise.
However, naive GRPO (see App.
H
for more details) uses independent noise at every step, so reward differences across trajectories conflate contributions from all steps. Inspired by MixGRPO
[
36
]
, we propose
step-subset GRPO with shared noise
(SubGRPO) to further reduce variance by isolating the effect of selected steps. For each prompt, we uniformly sample a subset of stochastic steps

\mathcal{S}\subset\{1,\dots,K-1\}

with
|\mathcal{S}|=M<K-1
.
The full
K
-step trajectory is still rolled out, but only the steps in
\mathcal{S}
use independent noise across trajectories; the remaining steps share noise within the group:

\tilde{{\bm{\epsilon}}}_{k}^{(i)}=\begin{cases}{\bm{\epsilon}}_{k}^{(i)},&k\in\mathcal{S},\\
{\bm{\epsilon}}_{k}^{\mathrm{grp}},&k\notin\mathcal{S},\end{cases}\qquad{\bm{\epsilon}}_{k}^{(i)},\,{\bm{\epsilon}}_{k}^{\mathrm{grp}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}),

(16)

where
\{{\bm{\epsilon}}_{k}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N}
are independent across trajectories, while
{\bm{\epsilon}}_{k}^{\mathrm{grp}}
is shared by all trajectories in the same group at step
k
.
Only the selected steps
k\in\mathcal{S}
contribute the gradients, yielding

\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{stoc}}^{\mathrm{SubGRPO}}=\frac{K-1}{M}\cdot\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{S}}A_{i}\,\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}^{(k)}\!(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}}^{(i)}\mid\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k-1}}^{(i)}).

(17)

Under the same gradient sample budget, SubGRPO can be viewed as a Rao–Blackwellized variant of the corresponding independent-noise estimator under mild assumptions
[
4
]
. Therefore, its gradient estimator typically has a smaller variance.

Total objective.

Combining Eqs. (
15
), (
17
), and (
9
), the generator in the second stage is updated by descending along:

\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{total}}=\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{AC\text{-}DMD}}-\beta\bigl(\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{stoc}}^{\mathrm{SubGRPO}}+\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{det}}\bigr).

(18)

Effect of AC-DMD.
In A-DMD, the fake score model is trained on noisy intermediate latents rather than clean samples, making denoising score matching less accurate under limited updates. As shown in
Tab.
4
, the consistency loss

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cons}}^{(k)}
improves fake score estimation by enforcing coherent predictions across neighboring timesteps. Notably, the improvement is consistent across all tested values of
\gamma
, confirming the robustness of the regularizer. The best result at
\gamma\!=\!0.01
yields an additional +1.58 PickScore
and +0.027 HPSv2 over A-DMD.

Effect of SubGRPO.

As shown in Tab.
4
and Fig.
4
, replacing naive GRPO with SubGRPO consistently improves all metrics. Naive GRPO samples independent noise at every stochastic step, so reward differences across trajectories conflate
contributions from all steps, leading to a noisy gradient signal. SubGRPO instead shares the injected noise at non-selected steps (
k\notin\mathcal{S}
) across the group (Eq. (
16
)), attributing reward variation primarily to
the selected subset
\mathcal{S}
. This shared-noise design reduces gradient variance by isolating the effect of selected steps. Increasing the subset size from
M\!=\!1
to
M\!=\!2
brings further gains, with PickScore improving from 23.33 to 23.45 and HPSv2 from 0.3332 to 0.3459. This reflects a trade-off between per-step variance reduction (fewer selected steps yield tighter control) and optimization coverage (more selected steps provide gradient signal to a larger portion of the trajectory); in our setting,
M\!=\!2
strikes a favorable balance.

Effect of hybrid policy gradient.

Existing GRPO-based methods for diffusion models
[
40
,
82
]
only optimize the stochastic denoising steps via the GRPO-estimator, neglecting the final deterministic transition entirely. However, our hybrid policy gradient (Eq. (
14
)) combines SubGRPO for
stochastic intermediate steps with a pathwise gradient through this deterministic final step. Tab.
4
shows that adding this term consistently improves performance: under
M\!=\!2
, PickScore increases from 23.45 to 23.57 and CLIPScore from 0.2839 to 0.2843, confirming that the deterministic final mapping carries a meaningful optimization signal that is otherwise left unexploited.

5
Conclusions and Limitations

We present RTDMD, a two-stage framework for few-step image generation that unifies distribution matching distillation with reward-guided reinforcement learning. The first stage,
AC-DMD employs ambient distribution matching and augments fake score training with a consistency regularizer to stabilize estimation. The second stage minimizes the KL divergence to a reward-tilted teacher distribution, which naturally decomposes into a distribution matching term and a reward maximization term. For the latter, we derive a hybrid policy gradient that combines SubGRPO for stochastic intermediate steps with direct reward backpropagation through the deterministic final step. Comprehensive experiments demonstrate the SOTA performance of our method across baselines. In terms of limitations, this work focuses on text-to-image generation; extending the framework to video synthesis and image editing, where reward design and temporal consistency pose additional challenges, is left for future work.

Appendix B
Derivation of the Step-
k
Training Objective

Learning the generator after
k
sampling steps.

Let
p_{\theta}^{(k)}
denote the distribution of the generated state
\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}}
after the first
k
sampling steps.
For training step
k
, we define the student marginal at time
t\in[t_{k},1]
by diffusing
\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}}
forward within the remaining interval.

Under the rectified schedule, the diffusion path is

{\bm{x}}_{t}=(1-t){\bm{x}}_{0}+t{\bm{\epsilon}},\qquad{\bm{\epsilon}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}).

By the Markov property of the Gaussian path, conditioning on an intermediate state
{\bm{x}}_{t_{k}}
yields

{\bm{x}}_{t}=\frac{1-t}{1-t_{k}}{\bm{x}}_{t_{k}}+\frac{t-t_{k}}{1-t_{k}}{\bm{\epsilon}},\qquad t\in[t_{k},1].

Therefore, for generated samples
\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}}\sim p_{\theta}^{(k)}
, we define

{\bm{x}}_{t}^{(k)}=\alpha_{k}(t)\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}}+\sigma_{k}(t){\bm{\epsilon}},\qquad\alpha_{k}(t)=\frac{1-t}{1-t_{k}},\qquad\sigma_{k}(t)=\frac{t-t_{k}}{1-t_{k}},

with conditional density

q_{k}({\bm{x}}_{t}\mid\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}},t)=\mathcal{N}\!\left({\bm{x}}_{t};\,\alpha_{k}(t)\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}},\,\sigma_{k}(t)^{2}\mathbf{I}\right).

The induced student marginal is

p_{\theta,t}^{(k)}({\bm{x}})=\int q_{k}({\bm{x}}\mid\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}},t)\,p_{\theta}^{(k)}(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}})\,d\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}}.

Following the reverse-KL formulation used in distribution matching distillation, we optimize

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{gen}}^{(k)}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{t\sim\mathcal{U}[t_{k},1]}\left[\lambda_{k}(t)\,\mathrm{KL}\!\left(p_{\theta,t}^{(k)}\,\|\,p_{\psi,t}\right)\right].

Using the pathwise derivative of the reverse KL gives

\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{gen}}^{(k)}=\mathbb{E}_{t,{\bm{\epsilon}},\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}}}\left[\lambda_{k}(t)\,\left(\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\log p_{\theta,t}^{(k)}({\bm{x}}_{t}^{(k)})-s_{\psi}({\bm{x}}_{t}^{(k)},t)\right)^{\top}\frac{\partial{\bm{x}}_{t}^{(k)}}{\partial\theta}\right].

Since

{\bm{x}}_{t}^{(k)}=\alpha_{k}(t)\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}}+\sigma_{k}(t){\bm{\epsilon}},

we have

\frac{\partial{\bm{x}}_{t}^{(k)}}{\partial\theta}=\alpha_{k}(t)\frac{\partial\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}}}{\partial\theta},

and hence

\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{gen}}^{(k)}=\mathbb{E}_{t,{\bm{\epsilon}},\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}}}\left[\lambda_{k}(t)\alpha_{k}(t)\,\left(\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\log p_{\theta,t}^{(k)}({\bm{x}}_{t}^{(k)})-s_{\psi}({\bm{x}}_{t}^{(k)},t)\right)^{\top}\frac{\partial\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}}}{\partial\theta}\right].

Because the score of
p_{\theta,t}^{(k)}
is intractable, we approximate it with a learned fake score

\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\log p_{\theta,t}^{(k)}({\bm{x}})\approx s_{\phi}({\bm{x}},t),

which yields the practical score-difference gradient

\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{gen}}^{(k)}\approx-\mathbb{E}_{t,{\bm{\epsilon}},\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}}}\left[\lambda_{k}(t)\alpha_{k}(t)\,\left(s_{\psi}({\bm{x}}_{t}^{(k)},t)-s_{\phi}({\bm{x}}_{t}^{(k)},t)\right)^{\top}\frac{\partial\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}}}{\partial\theta}\right].

Learning the fake score within
[t_{k},1]
.

Since clean samples
{\bm{x}}_{0}
are unavailable when
t_{k}>0
, the fake score must be trained using the generated endpoint
\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}}
rather than a global clean target. Restating Eq. (
10
), the fake-score model
s_{\phi}
is fit on the subinterval
[t_{k},1]
via the DSM objective

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fake}}^{(k)}(\phi)=\mathbb{E}_{t\sim\mathcal{U}[t_{k},1],\,\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}},\,{\bm{\epsilon}}}\left[\omega_{k}(t)\,\left\|s_{\phi}({\bm{x}}_{t}^{(k)},t)-\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\log q_{t}^{(k)}({\bm{x}}_{t}^{(k)}\mid\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}})\right\|_{2}^{2}\right],

which is unbiased for the marginal score
\nabla_{\bm{x}}\log p_{\theta,t}^{(k)}
(App.
D
); reusing the full-interval objective with a mismatched conditioning would in general yield a biased estimator.

In practice, we parametrize
s_{\phi}
via an
x
-prediction model
f_{\phi}
that predicts
\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}}
. Under the Gaussian subinterval kernel

q_{t}^{(k)}({\bm{x}}\mid\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}})=\mathcal{N}\!\left({\bm{x}};\,\alpha_{k}(t)\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}},\,\sigma_{k}(t)^{2}\mathbf{I}\right),

the score estimator follows from Tweedie’s formula:

s_{\phi}({\bm{x}}_{t}^{(k)},t)=-\frac{{\bm{x}}_{t}^{(k)}-\alpha_{k}(t)\,f_{\phi}({\bm{x}}_{t}^{(k)},t)}{\sigma_{k}(t)^{2}}.

Appendix C
Coefficient-Preserving Sampling Formula

Under the rectified-flow modeling,
{\bm{x}}_{t}=(1-t){\bm{x}}_{0}+t{\bm{\epsilon}}
with
{\bm{\epsilon}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})
, so
\alpha_{t}=1-t
and
\sigma_{t}=t
.
Given the current latent
\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k-1}}
at noise level
t_{k-1}
, the generator produces an
x
-prediction
\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{\mathrm{pred}}^{(k)}=G_{\theta}(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k-1}},t_{k-1})
and a corresponding noise estimate

\hat{{\bm{\epsilon}}}^{(k)}=\frac{\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k-1}}-(1-t_{k-1})\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{\mathrm{pred}}^{(k)}}{t_{k-1}}.

(19)

coefficient-preserving sampling (CPS)
[
71
]
constructs the next latent
\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}}
by enforcing that the signal and noise coefficients exactly match the scheduler at time
t_{k}
:

\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}}=(1-t_{k})\,\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{\mathrm{pred}}^{(k)}+t_{k}\cos\!\Bigl(\frac{\eta\pi}{2}\Bigr)\hat{{\bm{\epsilon}}}^{(k)}+t_{k}\sin\!\Bigl(\frac{\eta\pi}{2}\Bigr){\bm{\epsilon}}_{k},

(20)

where
{\bm{\epsilon}}_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})
is fresh noise and
\eta\in[0,1]
controls stochasticity. Setting
\eta=0
recovers the deterministic Euler ODE scheduler
\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}}=(1-t_{k})\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{\mathrm{pred}}^{(k)}+t_{k}\hat{{\bm{\epsilon}}}^{(k)}
, while
\eta=1
fully replaces the old noise with fresh samples, recovering the consistency model (CM) scheduler
[
67
]
. CPS thus provides a unified formulation that subsumes both common DMD
[
83
,
84
]
sampling (
i.e.
, Euler and CM) strategies as special cases.

Substituting
\hat{{\bm{\epsilon}}}^{(k)}
from Eq. (
19
) into Eq. (
20
) and collecting terms gives

\displaystyle\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}}

\displaystyle=(1-t_{k})\,\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{\mathrm{pred}}^{(k)}+\frac{t_{k}}{t_{k-1}}\cos\!\Bigl(\frac{\eta\pi}{2}\Bigr)\bigl[\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k-1}}-(1-t_{k-1})\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{\mathrm{pred}}^{(k)}\bigr]+t_{k}\sin\!\Bigl(\frac{\eta\pi}{2}\Bigr){\bm{\epsilon}}_{k}

\displaystyle=\Bigl[1-t_{k}+t_{k}\cos\!\Bigl(\frac{\eta\pi}{2}\Bigr)\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{t_{k-1}}\Bigr)\Bigr]\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{\mathrm{pred}}^{(k)}+\frac{t_{k}}{t_{k-1}}\cos\!\Bigl(\frac{\eta\pi}{2}\Bigr)\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k-1}}+t_{k}\sin\!\Bigl(\frac{\eta\pi}{2}\Bigr){\bm{\epsilon}}_{k}.

(21)

The transition defines a Gaussian policy with mean
{\bm{\mu}}_{\theta}^{(k)}=\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}}\big|_{{\bm{\epsilon}}_{k}=\mathbf{0}}
and standard deviation
\sigma_{k}=t_{k}\sin(\eta\pi/2)
.

Appendix D
Optimality of Ambient Denoising Score Matching

We show that the denoising score matching (DSM) objective in Eq. (
10
) has the true student marginal score
\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\log p_{\theta,t}^{(k)}({\bm{x}})
as its unique optimal solution.

Proposition D.1
.

Let
p_{\theta,t}^{(k)}({\bm{x}})=\int q_{t}^{(k)}({\bm{x}}\mid\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}})\,p_{\theta}^{(k)}(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}})\,d\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}}
denote the student marginal. Then, over functions
s:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[t_{k},1]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\argmin_{s}\;\mathbb{E}_{t\sim\mathcal{U}[t_{k},1]}\,\mathbb{E}_{\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}}\sim p_{\theta}^{(k)},\,{\bm{x}}\sim q_{t}^{(k)}(\cdot\mid\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}})}\left[\omega_{k}(t)\,\left\|s({\bm{x}},t)-\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\log q_{t}^{(k)}({\bm{x}}\mid\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}})\right\|^{2}\right]

is achieved at
s^{*}({\bm{x}},t)=\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\log p_{\theta,t}^{(k)}({\bm{x}})
for almost every
t\in[t_{k},1]
.

Proof.

Since
s
is a function of both
({\bm{x}},t)
, the outer expectation over
t
decouples and the minimization can be carried out independently for each
t
. Indeed, the full objective can be written as

\frac{1}{1-t_{k}}\int_{t_{k}}^{1}\omega_{k}(t)\,\mathcal{J}_{t}\bigl(s(\cdot,t)\bigr)\,dt,\qquad\mathcal{J}_{t}(f):=\mathbb{E}_{\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}},\,{\bm{x}}}\left[\|f({\bm{x}})-\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\log q_{t}^{(k)}({\bm{x}}\mid\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}})\|^{2}\right].

For each fixed
t\in[t_{k},1]
,
\omega_{k}(t)>0
is a strictly positive constant and does not affect the inner argmin, so it suffices to minimize
\mathcal{J}_{t}(f)
over
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Since the joint density of
({\bm{x}},\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}})
is
p_{\theta}^{(k)}(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}})\,q_{t}^{(k)}({\bm{x}}\mid\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}})
, we can write

\mathcal{J}_{t}(f)=\int p_{\theta,t}^{(k)}({\bm{x}})\,\mathbb{E}_{\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}}\sim p(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}}\mid{\bm{x}})}\left[\|f({\bm{x}})-\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\log q_{t}^{(k)}({\bm{x}}\mid\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}})\|^{2}\right]d{\bm{x}},

where
p(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}}\mid{\bm{x}})=\frac{q_{t}^{(k)}({\bm{x}}\mid\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}})\,p_{\theta}^{(k)}(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}})}{p_{\theta,t}^{(k)}({\bm{x}})}
is the posterior. For each
{\bm{x}}
, the inner
expectation is minimized pointwise by choosing
f({\bm{x}})
equal to the conditional mean:

f^{*}({\bm{x}})=\mathbb{E}_{\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}}\sim p(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}}\mid{\bm{x}})}\left[\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\log q_{t}^{(k)}({\bm{x}}\mid\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}})\right].

It remains to show that this equals the marginal score. By Bayes’ rule,

\displaystyle\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\log p_{\theta,t}^{(k)}({\bm{x}})

\displaystyle=\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\log\int q_{t}^{(k)}({\bm{x}}\mid\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}})\,p_{\theta}^{(k)}(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}})\,d\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\int\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\,q_{t}^{(k)}({\bm{x}}\mid\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}})\,p_{\theta}^{(k)}(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}})\,d\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}}}{p_{\theta,t}^{(k)}({\bm{x}})}

\displaystyle=\int\frac{q_{t}^{(k)}({\bm{x}}\mid\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}})\,p_{\theta}^{(k)}(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}})}{p_{\theta,t}^{(k)}({\bm{x}})}\,\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\log q_{t}^{(k)}({\bm{x}}\mid\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}})\,d\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}}\sim p(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}}\mid{\bm{x}})}\left[\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\log q_{t}^{(k)}({\bm{x}}\mid\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}})\right]=f^{*}({\bm{x}}).

Hence the pointwise optimum
s^{*}({\bm{x}},t)=\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\log p_{\theta,t}^{(k)}({\bm{x}})
holds for almost every
t\in[t_{k},1]
.
∎

Appendix E
Self-Consistency of the Optimal Fake Score

We prove that the optimal fake score model satisfies the self-consistency property invoked in Sec.
3.1
.

Proposition E.1

(Self-consistency of optimal denoiser)
.

Let
\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{\phi}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)
denote a score model in
x
-prediction form trained under the flow matching framework. If
\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{\phi}
is optimal, i.e.,

\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{\phi}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)=\mathbb{E}[{\bm{x}}_{0}\mid{\bm{x}}_{t}],\qquad\forall\,t,

then for any
t^{\prime\prime}<t^{\prime}
:

\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{\phi}({\bm{x}}_{t^{\prime}},t^{\prime})=\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{{\bm{x}}}_{t^{\prime\prime}}\sim p({\bm{x}}_{t^{\prime\prime}}\mid{\bm{x}}_{t^{\prime}})}\bigl[\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{\phi}(\tilde{{\bm{x}}}_{t^{\prime\prime}},t^{\prime\prime})\bigr].

Proof.

Under the forward process,
{\bm{x}}_{t}=\alpha_{t}{\bm{x}}_{0}+\sigma_{t}{\bm{\epsilon}}
with
{\bm{x}}_{0}\sim p_{0}
,
{\bm{\epsilon}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})
, and
(\alpha_{t},\sigma_{t})
specifying the noise schedule (
e.g.
,
\alpha_{t}=1-t
,
\sigma_{t}=t
for the rectified-flow schedule). For
t^{\prime\prime}<t^{\prime}
, the Gaussian interpolation implies that
({\bm{x}}_{0},\,{\bm{x}}_{t^{\prime\prime}},\,{\bm{x}}_{t^{\prime}})
forms a Markov chain in the increasing-noise direction, yielding

\mathbb{E}[{\bm{x}}_{0}\mid{\bm{x}}_{t^{\prime\prime}},\,{\bm{x}}_{t^{\prime}}]=\mathbb{E}[{\bm{x}}_{0}\mid{\bm{x}}_{t^{\prime\prime}}].

Starting from the right-hand side and applying optimality at
t^{\prime\prime}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{{\bm{x}}}_{t^{\prime\prime}}\sim p({\bm{x}}_{t^{\prime\prime}}\mid{\bm{x}}_{t^{\prime}})}\bigl[\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{\phi}(\tilde{{\bm{x}}}_{t^{\prime\prime}},t^{\prime\prime})\bigr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathbb{E}[{\bm{x}}_{0}\mid{\bm{x}}_{t^{\prime\prime}}]\;\big|\;{\bm{x}}_{t^{\prime}}\bigr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathbb{E}[{\bm{x}}_{0}\mid{\bm{x}}_{t^{\prime\prime}},\,{\bm{x}}_{t^{\prime}}]\;\big|\;{\bm{x}}_{t^{\prime}}\bigr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[{\bm{x}}_{0}\mid{\bm{x}}_{t^{\prime}}]=\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{\phi}({\bm{x}}_{t^{\prime}},t^{\prime}).

The second equality uses the Markov property; the third is the tower rule.
∎

The consistency regularizer
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cons}}^{(k)}
(Eq. (
11
)) penalizes deviations from this property, constraining the fake score model toward the
optimal solution even as the generator distribution shifts during training.

Practical Consistency Loss Estimator

Computing
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cons}}^{(k)}
(Eq. (
11
)) requires evaluating
\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{{\bm{x}}}_{t^{\prime\prime}}}[\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{\phi}(\tilde{{\bm{x}}}_{t^{\prime\prime}},t^{\prime\prime})]

to obtain the unbiased estimator:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cons}}^{(k)}(\phi)\approx\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}_{t^{\prime}},\,\tilde{{\bm{x}}}_{t^{\prime\prime}}^{1},\tilde{{\bm{x}}}_{t^{\prime\prime}}^{2}}\!\left[\left(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{\phi}(\tilde{{\bm{x}}}_{t^{\prime\prime}}^{1},t^{\prime\prime})-\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{\phi}({\bm{x}}_{t^{\prime}},t^{\prime})\right)\!^{\top}\!\left(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{\phi}(\tilde{{\bm{x}}}_{t^{\prime\prime}}^{2},t^{\prime\prime})-\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{\phi}({\bm{x}}_{t^{\prime}},t^{\prime})\right)\right].

(22)

Appendix G
Hybrid Policy Gradient

G.1
Derivation

We provide a formal derivation of the gradient estimator used for the reward term

\nabla_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{0}\sim p_{\theta}}[r(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{0})].

Since the few-step generator consists of
K-1
stochastic transitions followed by one deterministic final step, the resulting gradient decomposes naturally into a contribution from the stochastic transitions and a contribution from the deterministic terminal mapping.

Proposition G.1

(Hybrid policy gradient)
.

Consider the few-step generative process

\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{0}}\to\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{1}}\to\cdots\to\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{K-1}}\to\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{0},

where for
k=1,\dots,K-1
,

\pi_{\theta}^{(k)}(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}}\mid\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k-1}})=\mathcal{N}\!\bigl(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}};{\bm{\mu}}_{\theta}^{(k)}(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k-1}}),\sigma_{k}^{2}\mathbf{I}\bigr),

and the final output is deterministic:

\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{0}=G_{\theta}(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{K-1}},t_{K-1}).

Define

J(\theta):=\mathbb{E}_{\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{0}\sim p_{\theta}}[r(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{0})].

Then

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}J(\theta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K-1}\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim p_{\theta}}\!\left[r(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{0})\,\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}^{(k)}(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}}\mid\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k-1}})\right]

(23)

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim p_{\theta}}\!\left[\bigl(\nabla_{\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{0}}r(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{0})\bigr)^{\top}\partial_{\theta}G_{\theta}(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{K-1}},t_{K-1})\right].

Proof.

Because the last step is deterministic, it is convenient to separate the stochastic and deterministic parts of the generation process. Let

\zeta:=(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{1}},\dots,\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{K-1}})

denote the intermediate random states. Conditioned on
\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{0}}
, their density is

p_{\theta}(\zeta)=\prod_{k=1}^{K-1}\pi_{\theta}^{(k)}(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}}\mid\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k-1}}).

(24)

The terminal sample is then determined by

\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{0}=G_{\theta}(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{K-1}},t_{K-1}),

and the objective can be written as

J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\zeta\sim p_{\theta}}\!\left[r\bigl(G_{\theta}(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{K-1}},t_{K-1})\bigr)\right].

(25)

Differentiating Eq. (
25
) under the integral sign gives

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}J(\theta)

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\theta}\int p_{\theta}(\zeta)\,r\bigl(G_{\theta}(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{K-1}},t_{K-1})\bigr)\,d\zeta

(26)

\displaystyle=\int\nabla_{\theta}p_{\theta}(\zeta)\,r(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{0})\,d\zeta+\int p_{\theta}(\zeta)\,\nabla_{\theta}r(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{0})\,d\zeta.

Using

\nabla_{\theta}p_{\theta}(\zeta)=p_{\theta}(\zeta)\nabla_{\theta}\log p_{\theta}(\zeta),

we obtain

\nabla_{\theta}J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\zeta\sim p_{\theta}}\!\left[r(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{0})\nabla_{\theta}\log p_{\theta}(\zeta)\right]+\mathbb{E}_{\zeta\sim p_{\theta}}\!\left[\nabla_{\theta}r(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{0})\right].

(27)

By Eq. (
24
),

\log p_{\theta}(\zeta)=\sum_{k=1}^{K-1}\log\pi_{\theta}^{(k)}(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}}\mid\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k-1}}),

and therefore

\nabla_{\theta}\log p_{\theta}(\zeta)=\sum_{k=1}^{K-1}\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}^{(k)}(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}}\mid\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k-1}}).

(28)

Substituting Eq. (
28
) into Eq. (
27
) yields

\nabla_{\theta}J(\theta)=\sum_{k=1}^{K-1}\mathbb{E}_{\zeta\sim p_{\theta}}\!\left[r(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{0})\,\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}^{(k)}(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}}\mid\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k-1}})\right]+\mathbb{E}_{\zeta\sim p_{\theta}}\!\left[\nabla_{\theta}r(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{0})\right].

(29)

For the second term,
r(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{0})
depends on
\theta
through the final deterministic mapping
G_{\theta}
. Hence, by the chain rule,

\nabla_{\theta}r(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{0})=\bigl(\nabla_{\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{0}}r(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{0})\bigr)^{\top}\partial_{\theta}G_{\theta}(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{K-1}},t_{K-1}).

(30)

Combining Eqs. (
29
) and (
30
) proves Eq. (
23
).
∎

G.2
Discussion

Prop.
G.1
separates the gradient into two parts. The first part comes from how the parameters affect the distribution of the stochastic intermediate states, and therefore involves the log-derivatives of the transition densities. The second part comes from the explicit dependence of the final deterministic output on the parameters, and is obtained by directly differentiating the terminal mapping.

A subtle point is that the above derivation applies the log-derivative identity only to the stochastic part of the trajectory. One could formally write the full trajectory distribution as

p_{\theta}(\tau)=p(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{0}})\prod_{k=1}^{K-1}\pi_{\theta}^{(k)}(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}}\mid\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k-1}})\cdot\delta\!\left(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{0}-G_{\theta}(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{K-1}},t_{K-1})\right),

but this representation contains a Dirac delta corresponding to the deterministic last step. For this reason, directly applying the log-derivative form to the entire trajectory is unnecessary and less clean. By isolating the random intermediate states
\zeta
, the derivation remains fully standard and avoids manipulating the logarithm of a Dirac delta.

Another potential concern is whether the second term in Eq. (
23
) ignores the dependence of
\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{K-1}}
on
\theta
. It does not. In Eq. (
27
), the dependence of the random trajectory
\zeta
on
\theta
is already accounted for by the term

\mathbb{E}_{\zeta\sim p_{\theta}}\!\left[r(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{0})\nabla_{\theta}\log p_{\theta}(\zeta)\right]
.
Therefore, in the remaining term

\mathbb{E}_{\zeta\sim p_{\theta}}\!\left[\nabla_{\theta}r(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{0})\right]
,
the sampled
\zeta
is treated as fixed, and only the explicit parameter dependence of the terminal mapping
G_{\theta}
is differentiated. Re-introducing the dependence of
\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{K-1}}
on
\theta
in this term would count the same contribution twice.

Appendix H
Background on GRPO and Its Application to Few-step Generators

Group relative policy optimization.

GRPO
[
64
]
is a variance-reduced policy gradient method originally developed for language model alignment. Given a prompt
c
, GRPO samples a group of
N
responses,
computes a group-normalized advantage for each, and updates the policy with a clipped objective. The key idea is to use the group mean reward as a baseline, eliminating the need for a
separate value network.

Naive GRPO for few-step generators.

In our setting, the few-step generator defines a
K
-step policy
\pi_{\theta}
with Gaussian transitions (Sec.
3.2
). A direct (naive) application of GRPO proceeds
as follows: for each prompt
c
, sample
N
independent trajectories
\{\tau_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
by drawing independent noise
{\bm{\epsilon}}_{k}^{(i)}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})
at
every stochastic step
k=1,\dots,K-1
of each trajectory
i
. Compute rewards
r_{i}=r(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{0}^{(i)},c)
and group-normalized advantages

A_{i}=\frac{r_{i}-\bar{r}}{\operatorname{std}(\{r_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N})},\qquad\bar{r}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}r_{j}.

The naive GRPO gradient estimator for the stochastic part is then

\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{stoc}}^{\mathrm{GRPO}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{K-1}A_{i}\,\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}^{(k)}(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}}^{(i)}\mid\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k-1}}^{(i)}),

(31)

where the Gaussian log-likelihood is

\log\pi_{\theta}^{(k)}(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}}\mid\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k-1}})=-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{k}^{2}}\|\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}}-{\bm{\mu}}_{\theta}^{(k)}(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k-1}})\|^{2}+\mathrm{const},

with
{\bm{\mu}}_{\theta}^{(k)}
and
\sigma_{k}=t_{k}\sin(\eta\pi/2)
determined by the CPS update (App.
C
).

Limitation.

Since all
K-1
steps use independent noise across trajectories, the reward difference between any two trajectories reflects the combined effect of noise injected at
all
steps
simultaneously. The advantage
A_{i}
is therefore a noisy credit assignment signal, which means it cannot attribute reward variation to any specific step, leading to high gradient variance. This
motivates our SubGRPO (Sec.
3.2
), which shares noise at non-selected steps to isolate contributions from a chosen subset.

Appendix K
Discussion on the Sampling Schedule and Connection to DMD2

Choice of
\eta
.

As shown in Fig.
5
, we find that performance is relatively insensitive to the specific value of
\eta
as long as sufficient stochasticity is introduced (
i.e.
,
\eta\!\geq\!0.8
), with all such
configurations yielding comparable results. We attribute this to the fact that the subsequent GRPO optimization relies on adequate stochasticity in the intermediate transitions to produce diverse trajectories for effective exploration. We therefore set
\eta\!=\!0.9
throughout our experiments.

Connection and comparison to DMD2.

We further find that the only difference between the original DMD2
[
83
]
(which originally uses the CM scheduler) and our A-DMD with
\eta\!=\!1.0
(where CPS reduces to the CM scheduler) lies in the noise-level interval used during training:
t
in Eq. (
8
) ranges over
[0,1]
for DMD2 but only over
[t_{k},1]
for A-DMD. This stems from a different choice of conditioning data for score/denoising matching: A-DMD re-noises from the actually generated intermediate state
\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}}
and uses it directly as the training data, whereas DMD2 instead takes the generator’s
x
-prediction
\hat{{\bm{x}}}^{(k)}_{\mathrm{pred}}
as a clean sample
{\bm{x}}_{0}
and re-noises it across the entire interval
[0,1]
.

Our design is more general across different schedulers (
e.g.
, CM and Euler ODE), because
\hat{{\bm{x}}}^{(k)}_{\mathrm{pred}}
can be safely treated as a clean sample only under the CM parameterization. In a CM-trained model, the consistency property forces the network to map any noisy input back to the same underlying clean
{\bm{x}}_{0}
along the PF-ODE trajectory, so
\hat{{\bm{x}}}^{(k)}_{\mathrm{pred}}
behaves as a sample from the student’s clean-data distribution. Under non-CM schedulers (
e.g.
, the Euler ODE schedule with
\eta\!=\!0
), the model does not satisfy this consistency property and is not trained to directly map noisy inputs to clean samples, so
\hat{{\bm{x}}}^{(k)}_{\mathrm{pred}}
cannot be regarded as a sample from
p_{0}
. Treating it as if it were a clean
{\bm{x}}_{0}
and re-noising it over
[0,1]
would therefore introduce a scheduler-dependent bias in the score-matching objective
[
19
]
. By instead conditioning on the realized noisy state
\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t_{k}}
, which is by construction a sample from the student marginal
p_{\theta}^{(k)}
(defined in Sec.
3.1
), and matching the score only on the subinterval
[t_{k},1]
, A-DMD remains a valid re-derivation of distribution matching for any
\eta\in[0,1]
and any scheduler choice.

Empirically, in the special case
\eta\!=\!1.0
A-DMD becomes structurally equivalent to DMD2 up to this training-interval choice, and as shown in Fig.
5
the corresponding curve converges to nearly identical final performance as the standard DMD2 setting. This confirms that A-DMD does not sacrifice quality in the CM regime; rather, the re-derivation is introduced primarily to provide a unified, scheduler-agnostic framework.
