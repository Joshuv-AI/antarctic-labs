Clavicular Augmentation With Poly-d,l-lactic Acid (Juvelook GLAM) using Cannula
Authors: Kyu-Ho Yi, Jovian Wan, Isabella Rosellini, Jong Keun Song, Han Earl Lee, Kian Hong Lau, Jin-Hyun Kim, Juhyuk Park, Hongseok Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Prominent clavicles are an increasingly requested aesthetic goal in East Asian patients. Poly‑d,l‑lactic acid (PDLLA) is a collagen‑stimulating injectable, but clinical data for clavicular augmentation remain limited.Methods:In this prospective, single‑arm case series, 44 East Asian women underwent bilateral clavicular augmentation with PDLLA (Juvelook GLAM, VAIM Inc., Korea) delivered via blunt cannula in the subcutaneous plane. Outcomes included adverse events, Global Aesthetic Improvement Scale ratings from standardized photographs, a 5‑point satisfaction scale, and objective projection change measured with 3‑dimensional imaging at 3 and 6 months.Results:No major complications were observed through 6 months. Transient bruising (19.4%) and swelling (25.8%) resolved spontaneously. Mean clavicular projection increased by +1.8 ± 0.6 mm at 3 months and +1.6 ± 0.5 mm at 6 months (P < 0.05). At 6 months, 90.3% of patients reported being satisfied or very satisfied.Conclusions:In this uncontrolled early‑outcomes series, PDLLA clavicular augmentation was feasible and was associated with high short‑term satisfaction and a modest but measurable increase in projection. Larger controlled studies with longer follow‑up and validated patient‑reported outcome measures are needed to confirm clinical meaningfulness, durability, and rare or late‑onset adverse events.

Takeaways

Question: How effective and safe is poly-d,l-lactic acid (PDLLA) for clavicular augmentation in East Asian women?

Findings: In this prospective case series involving 44 women, PDLLA resulted in significant aesthetic improvements with minimal complications. At the 6-month follow-up, 90.3% of participants reported satisfaction. Transient swelling and bruising were the only notable side effects.

Meaning: PDLLA offers a safe, nonsurgical alternative for enhancing clavicular contours, achieving high satisfaction rates and minimal downtime.

INTRODUCTION

The clavicle constitutes a pivotal aesthetic landmark of the upper torso, significantly influencing perceptions of skeletal elegance and the feminine silhouette. Increasingly, prominent clavicular contours represent a sought-after aesthetic ideal within East Asian populations, symbolizing refinement and delicate bone structure.1 This cultural preference manifests clinically through rising requests for clavicular enhancement, amplified by social media showcasing décolletage aesthetics.2

Although clavicular prominence is primarily genetically determined, factors including aging, weight fluctuation, and inherent anatomy may diminish visibility. Injectable biostimulatory fillers provide a minimally invasive solution, contrasting with surgical alternatives such as autologous bone grafting, which, while used in reconstructive orthopedic contexts, entail significant downtime and unpredictable resorption.3 Poly-d,l-lactic acid (PDLLA), a stereoisomer blend, stimulates neocollagenesis through controlled inflammatory cascades.4,5 Juvelook GLAM (VAIM Inc., Seoul, South Korea) is a PDLLA-based injectable approved for tissue augmentation, though its application beyond facial volumization remains insufficiently documented.

This prospective case series describes PDLLA clavicular augmentation in 44 young East Asian women using a blunt cannula technique with local anesthetic admixture and without additional infiltrated anesthesia, and reports early (3‑ and 6‑month) objective and patient‑reported outcomes. Because this is an uncontrolled case series with 6‑month follow‑up, statements regarding comparative efficacy and long‑term safety are necessarily preliminary.

METHODS

Study Design and Participants

A prospective case series was conducted between January 1, 2025, and June 30, 2025, at an aesthetic clinic in Seoul, South Korea. Forty-four consecutive female patients seeking clavicular enhancement were enrolled. The mean patient age was 29.4 ± 4.7 years (range 22–38 y). All patients prioritized enhanced clavicular visibility as their primary objective. Exclusion criteria included active infection at the injection site, history of keloid scarring, coagulopathies, immunosuppression, pregnancy or lactation, hypersensitivity to PDLLA, and previous clavicular procedures. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Each participant received a detailed explanation of the procedure’s purpose, potential risks, benefits, and available alternatives before treatment.

Clinical Assessment

Standardized digital photography was performed preprocedurally and at 3 and 6 months posttreatment using consistent lighting and patient positioning. Clinical outcomes were evaluated using the Global Aesthetic Improvement Scale (GAIS), with treating physicians rating improvements as exceptional (6 patients, 12.9%), very much improved (21 patients, 48.4%), much improved (13 patients, 29.0%), improved (4 patients, 9.7%), and no change (0 patients, 0%). The mean GAIS score was 3.6 ± 0.8, indicating significant clinical improvement across the cohort. To minimize evaluator bias, 2 independent physicians, blinded to the treating clinician, also reviewed and confirmed GAIS ratings based on standardized photographs. Complementing the GAIS findings, patient-reported satisfaction was recorded using a 5-point Likert scale (1 = very dissatisfied to 5 = very satisfied), with scores 4 or 5 classified as “satisfied.” A 5‑point Likert scale was used as a pragmatic satisfaction measure because a validated patient‑reported outcome measure (PROM) specific to clavicular augmentation is not currently available; future studies will incorporate validated body-aesthetics PROMs where applicable.

In addition, objective evaluations of clavicular projection were conducted using 3‑dimensional stereophotogrammetry and linear anthropometry, allowing quantitative analysis of contour enhancement. Measurements were obtained at baseline and at 3 and 6 months under standardized imaging conditions.

STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

Descriptive statistics are reported as mean ± SD or frequency (%). Changes in clavicular projection from baseline to follow‑up were evaluated using paired comparisons (2‑sided α=0.05). Correlation analyses between objective projection change and GAIS/satisfaction were not performed in this exploratory dataset and are planned for future analyses.

Procedure

Juvelook GLAM (VAIM Inc.) vials were reconstituted with 15 mL of distilled water according to manufacturer guidelines and equilibrated to room temperature prior to injection. The reconstituted solution was allowed to hydrate for appropriate duration to ensure optimal particle suspension and injection characteristics.

Patients remained seated upright during anatomical marking. Using a surgical skin marker, the target augmentation zone was outlined along the superior clavicular ridge and medial curvature, following each patient’s individual anatomy. No supplemental topical anesthesia was required due to the anesthetic properties of the prepared suspension.

Under aseptic conditions, bilateral entry points were established medial to each curved area of the clavicle (Fig. 1) using a 18G needle. A 21G, 70-mm blunt-tipped cannula was then advanced subcutaneously along the predetermined trajectory (4-8 cc were used per side). Injections were performed in multiple retrograde passes across the clavicular width, from deep to superficial subcutaneous planes, to ensure uniform distribution and a natural linear contour. Using a retrograde linear threading technique. Immediate gentle manual molding was performed to achieve even product distribution over the bony contour, followed by brief compression at entry sites to minimize product leakage. Injection depth was intentionally limited to the subcutaneous plane immediately superficial to the clavicle; the operator maintained continuous palpation of the cannula tip and used skin tenting/rolling to confirm a superficial track. If firm bony contact or marked resistance was encountered, the cannula was withdrawn and redirected more superficially. Injections were confined to the superior clavicular ridge and avoided the supraclavicular fossa and medial neurovascular region. Ultrasound guidance was considered but not used in this series because the intended plane was superficial subcutaneous and the technique used a blunt cannula; we acknowledge that ultrasound could further standardize depth and will be incorporated in future studies where feasible.

Summary of 6-month Clinical and Objective Outcomes

Minor complications included transient swelling in 11 (25.8%) patients, resolving spontaneously within 3–7 days, and bruising in 9 (19.4%) patients, resolving within 7–14 days. No interventions were required. No major complications occurred, including infection, vascular compromise, nodule formation, or persistent asymmetry.

Objective measurement revealed mean increases in clavicular projection of +1.8 ± 0.6 mm at 3 months and +1.6 ± 0.5 mm at 6 months (P < 0.05), supporting the observed contour enhancement.

Patient satisfaction at 6 months remained high: 27 (61.3%) patients reported being “very satisfied,” 13 (29.0%) “satisfied,” and 4 (9.7%) “neutral.” No patients reported dissatisfaction. The mean satisfaction score was 4.3 ± 0.7. Patients consistently noted enhanced clavicular definition, creating a sculpted décolletage. Neutral responders desired greater prominence but declined additional treatment.

DISCUSSION

This prospective case series suggests that PDLLA can produce a measurable increase in clavicular projection with high patient‑reported satisfaction and no major complications over 6 months in young East Asian women. Because the study lacks a control group and blinding at the patient level, the findings should be interpreted as preliminary early‑outcomes data rather than definitive evidence of comparative efficacy or long‑term safety.

The mean projection increase at 6 months (+1.6 ± 0.5 mm) was statistically significant, but a minimal clinically important or perceptible difference for clavicular projection has not been established. In practice, even small changes along a long, high‑contrast bony landmark may alter light reflex and contour visibility; however, this interpretation remains hypothesis based. We did not perform correlation analysis between objective projection change and GAIS or satisfaction scores, so the relationship between millimetric change and subjective benefit cannot be quantified from this dataset.

Patient satisfaction was collected using a 5‑point Likert scale as a pragmatic tool for an early feasibility series, acknowledging the current lack of a validated PROM specific to clavicular augmentation. Expectation effects and placebo responses are possible in elective aesthetic procedures; to reduce observer bias, we used standardized photography and independent GAIS review, and we paired subjective outcomes with objective 3‑dimensional measurements. Future studies should incorporate validated body-aesthetics PROMs where applicable and use controlled designs to better isolate treatment effects.

Although no major complications were observed, a cohort of 44 patients with 6‑month follow‑up has limited ability to detect rare or late‑onset adverse events (eg, delayed nodules, granulomas, calcification). Accordingly, we describe the procedure as having favorable short‑term tolerability rather than concluding definitive safety. Because PDLLA is biostimulatory and tissue response may continue to evolve, durability beyond 6 months cannot be determined from this report; 12‑month follow‑up of this cohort is ongoing and will be reported separately.

Technique considerations are important when interpreting safety. In this series, injections were performed in the subcutaneous plane immediately superficial to the clavicle using a blunt cannula, with continuous palpation and skin tenting to avoid overly deep placement near the periosteum and to minimize risk to adjacent neurovascular structures. Ultrasound was not used, which is a methodological limitation; future work should evaluate ultrasound‑guided clavicular augmentation to standardize depth and document regional vascular anatomy.

External validity is limited because the cohort consisted exclusively of young East Asian women. Outcomes may differ in men, older individuals, or other ethnic populations with different skin thickness, subcutaneous fat distribution, and tissue laxity. These factors could influence both the volume required for visible contour change and the biologic response to PDLLA, and they should be examined in more diverse cohorts.

Finally, the discussion of PDLLA relative to hyaluronic acid or calcium hydroxyapatite is based on indirect comparisons from the literature rather than head‑to‑head data from this study. Future research should consider comparative or split‑body designs to directly evaluate materials, dosing strategies, patient‑reported outcomes, and durability for clavicular augmentation.

CONCLUSIONS

PDLLA clavicular augmentation was associated with high patient satisfaction and a modest, objectively measurable increase in clavicular projection over 6 months in this cohort of young East Asian women, with only transient minor adverse events observed. Given the uncontrolled design, limited follow‑up, and limited power to detect rare complications, these results should be considered preliminary and hypothesis generating. Controlled studies with longer follow‑up and validated PROMs are required before definitive statements regarding efficacy, durability, and long‑term safety can be made.
