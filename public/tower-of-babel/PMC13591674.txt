Economic burden of COVID-19 on hospitalized patients: a cross-sectional study from Semnan, Iran
Authors: Esmaeil Moshiri, Zahra Gheinali, Masoumeh Ebrahimi Tavani, Farid Gharibi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOne of the responsibilities of health systems is to protect citizens from the financial burdens caused by disease. This study aimed to estimate the economic burden of the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) on hospitalized patients and identifying its determinants.MethodsThe current cross-sectional study was conducted during the sixth peak of COVID-19 in Iran (summer and fall 2021) with the participation of 400 hospitalized patients at Kowsar Hospital in Semnan from the patients’ perspective. The data was collected using the patients’ medical records and through interviewing with the patients. We used a researcher-made questionnaire, whose content validity was confirmed by scores of 0.91 and 0.93, respectively, for content validity ratio (CVR) and content validity index (CVI). Depending on the nature of the independent and dependent variables, student’s t-test, analysis of variance (ANOVA), Chi-square, linear regression, and Tukey’s post-hoc tests were used.ResultsThe economic estimations revealed that hospitalized COVID-19 patients and their families paid 2773.43 USD (116484080 IRR) monthly, of which 1833.43 USD (77004080 IRR) (66%) was related to direct medical costs, 357.97 USD (15035000 IRR) (13%) to direct non-medical costs, and 582.02 USD (24445000 IRR) (21%) to indirect costs. In addition to the factors relating to the severity of the disease and its side effects, a wide range of demographic and contextual factors, including age, gender, residence status, residence region (urban or rural), type of basic and supplementary insurance, location of care, presence of an underlying disease, and receiving medication for the underlying disease had an impact on the total cost.ConclusionsAccording to our results, COVID-19 hospitalization had a significant financial impact on hospitalized patients and their families, just like complicated procedures or chronic severe illnesses. We also identified significant geographic, economic, and employment-related health inequities.

Background

The corona virus has caused significant outbreaks in East and Central Asia over the past two decades, including the severe acute respiratory syndrome (SARS) epidemic in 2002 and the Middle East Respiratory Syndrome (MERS) epidemic in 2012 [1]. COVID-19, which is currently regarded as the deadliest pandemic of the 20th century, has a lesser pathogenicity compared to its older strains, but its speed and ease of transmission are higher, causing it to spread over the globe considerably more rapidly [2]. As of October 25, 2021 (Persian calendar: Aban 3, 1400), this disease has caused 243,260,214 verified cases and 49,410,399 confirmed fatalities worldwide, including 5,860,844 cases and 125,223 deaths in Iran [3].

Patients, insurance companies, and the healthcare system will suffer significant diagnostic and treatment costs, to which the price of mass vaccination will be added. The high cost of reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) as a standard diagnostic test for the disease, the high variety and extent of treatment methods, as well as high cost of maintenance in the intensive care unit (ICU) result in considerable costs for patients, the health system, and insurance companies when providing a type of cure that has not been proven effective yet. During this epidemic, numerous health, treatment, and prevention commodities experienced a doubling or tripling in price in several nations due to a combination of high demand and limited supply. Clearly, this situation will be even more challenging for developing nations with less and low-quality health resources, as all their equipment and raw materials are imported [4–6].

The COVID-19 pandemic can be viewed as the cause of the largest and most severe economic crisis since the Great Depression of the 1930s [4], for which health systems and international organizations in charge of health, despite advancements in medical sciences and related research, have faced significant challenges in managing health consequences, as well as its destructive economic and social effects [7]. As a result of the outbreak of the COVID-19 epidemic and the subsequent direct medical costs in Turkey, the yearly health system cost has risen to over USD1.2 billion, representing 2% of the entire annual health system budget and 0.8% of the nation’s gross domestic product (GDP) [8].

According to surveys, the economic impact of this epidemic has been catastrophic for cities and their inhabitants, resulting in severe financial challenges. Except for sectors such as the environment, health and pharmaceutical production factories, online trading companies, and supermarkets, which have benefited from the occurrence of this pandemic [4], other sectors are experiencing unprecedented severe economic and social problems [9]. The decline in oil prices, weakening of the tourism industry, decline in industrial production, recession of the aviation and transportation sectors, stagnation of service companies, and the weakening of educational organizations, among other factors, have contributed to the increase in the unemployment rate, decrease in production hours, and the double-digit unemployment rate in developed countries. This has also led to a decline in the countries’ GDP and increased unemployment [4]. In China, the epidemic has caused a 13.5% decline in industrial production, a 21% decline in trades, a 92% decline in automobile sales, and a 95% decline in restaurant revenue [4]. Therefore, the economic costs incurred by families and communities must be added to the expenditures associated with vaccine, diagnosis, and treatment.

Health care is an expensive service that frequently necessitates skilled personnel, substantial physical space, and expensive raw materials and equipment. These expenditures take a substantial portion of the financial resources and the residents’ income in various nations. The provision of health care in developing nations, which frequently face severe financial constraints and allocate a small proportion of their inadequate revenue to the health sector, will generally result in severe shortcomings and issues. Another factor to consider is the importation of equipment and raw materials, particularly medication, which due to the low value of the common currency in importing nations relative to exporting countries (exchange rate), will significantly amplify the magnitude of the problem [10, 11].

It is essential to provide financial help to society’s citizens, particularly those with lower incomes. It has been observed that if patients and their families cannot afford health care (prohibitive health) costs, they will not receive it on time or ignore it, opting instead for low-quality and sometimes risky care, which seems to be more economical; and this results in negative health outcomes. Patients and their families are also forced to sell their assets or take out loans from individuals and banks to cover the enormous costs imposed, which results in lower living standards for the patients and their family members, especially when it comes to necessities like healthcare, food, and education [12, 13].

As administrators of community health, health systems are obligated to provide effective prepayment mechanisms, such as insurance systems that give financial protection to residents via risk pooling, risk transfer, and cross-subsidization. Moreover, those who are susceptible to incurring exorbitant medical charges or who have little financial resources will not be required to pay exceptional and unaffordable costs. Thus, the precise evaluation of the financial consequences of a disease and the identification of patients who have a greater likelihood of incurring prohibitive health care costs is crucial to implement promotional treatments and adopt financial protection measures [11].

The Iranian health insurance system is a public assistance model with a mixed structure. While individuals employed in the public and private sectors are covered by occupational insurance, others benefit from government-sponsored public insurance. Although nearly the entire population of Iran has access to health insurance, the extent of service coverage and the proportion of costs covered vary significantly across different insurance schemes. In addition, many organizations and financially capable individuals purchase supplementary insurance to cover more comprehensive health expenses [14].

Out-of-pocket (OOP) payments refer to health care costs paid directly by patients to providers at the time of receiving services [13]. National policies in Iran have aimed to reduce OOP payments to below 30% and limit household exposure to health care costs to less than 1%. However, these targets have never been achieved, even partially. For instance, per capita health care spending in Iran more than doubled between 2004 and 2008, with the majority of expenses paid out-of-pocket. In 2006, OOP payments accounted for more than 50% of total health expenditures [12].

Previous studies have shown that most COVID-19–related services provided in public hospitals are covered by various health insurance schemes, although coverage in private centers depends on the type of insurance. However, costly procedures such as hemoperfusion are not covered by any insurance [14]. One study reported that 61% of hospitalized COVID-19 patients faced catastrophic health care costs [14], yet no published research has examined the overall economic burden imposed on these patients. Accordingly, the present study seeks to estimate the economic burden of COVID-19 from the patient’s perspective and to identify its determinants in Semnan, Iran.

Methods

Type of the study and participants

The present cross-sectional study included 400 COVID-19 patients hospitalized in Kowsar Hospital of Semnan city, Iran with at least one month since their infection. Those who died from COVID-19 or any other cause were eliminated from the research. Where n is the sample size used to determine the average community exposure to prohibitive health costs, z is the z-score value at a specified confidence level, s is the expected changes in responses, and d is the margin of error. Considering a confidence level of 95%, 0.5 forecast changes (due to the lack of primary information regarding the occurrence of prohibitive health costs in patients), and a margin error of 0.05, the sample size was calculated to be 384 individuals. Finally, considering 5% attrition rate, the sample size was determined as 400 individuals. This study is single-center and includes only patients hospitalized for at least one month—a criterion that selects the most severe cases and likely produces an upper-bound estimate of the cost burden rather than an average. Data were collected using systematic random sampling. Given the large sample size and the use of this method, the findings of the study demonstrate good generalizability; however, the generalizability is limited to the specific time and location in which the study was conducted.

Instruments and data collection

We collected the data using a researcher-made questionnaire, the validity of which was confirmed. In this manner, following a review of the literature and interviews with qualified experts, a preliminary questionnaire containing demographic and background variables, as well as questions to assess the types of costs imposed on patients was developed; the content validity and face validity of the questionnaire were reviewed and approved by ten experts. In this regard, all questions were examined by experts based on the four indices of necessity, relevance, transparency, and simplicity, and the final judgment on the content validity of the questionnaire was made by calculating the CVR and CVI indices using the following formula [15]:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{C}\mathrm{V}\mathrm{R}/\mathrm{C}\mathrm{V}\mathrm{I}=\frac{\mathrm{n}\mathrm{E}-\:\frac{N}{2}}{\frac{N}{2}}$$\end{document}

Where nE denotes the number of experts who chose two positive spectrum options and N denotes the total number of experts. Since ten experts participated in this stage, the acceptance score of 0.62 served as the basis for approving or disapproving the questionnaire’s items [15]. The final questionnaire included 75 questions, 23 items of which were related to demographic and background components, 42 items to information on disease costs, four items to the patients’ and their income status, and six items to obtain patients’ perspectives on the negative consequences of disease costs imposed on them. The validity of the designed tool was confirmed by scores 0.91 and 0.93 for the CVR and CVI indicators, respectively. In addition, the face validity of the questionnaire was determined by qualitative opinions of experts on the literature and the way the questions embedded in them were written [14]. Patients’ medical records were the primary source of information regarding direct medical costs. In addition to the questionnaire, telephone interviews were conducted with patients to collect data on demographic and background factors as well as incurred costs.

Data analysis

While direct medical costs are diagnostic and therapeutic care costs during treatment, direct non-medical costs are the costs related to non-clinical support and personal services, provision of mobility aids, securing the home environment, travel to and from care centers, as well as lodging and food during treatment. Also, indirect costs are the loss of economic opportunities by the patients and their families. In this study, we examine the statistical relationship between demographic and background characteristics and several forms of imposed costs. Depending on the nature of the independent and dependent variables, a combination of statistical tests, including the student’s t-test, ANOVA, Chi-square, linear regression, and Tukey’s post-hoc tests were employed. All the analyses were conducted using SPSS 19, and P < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant. After calculating the incurred direct medical costs in Iranian Rials (IRR), the calculated costs were changed to US dollars (1 USD = 42,000 IRR) in September and October 2021.

Ethical considerations

The participants were free to accept or decline participation in the study. All participants signed an informed consent prior to the study. During the study, the participants’ privacy and identities were protected, and they were assured that the results would be utilized exclusively in accordance with the research objectives. In addition, the ethics council of the Semnan University of Medical Sciences, Iran approved the study protocol (code: IR.SEMUMS.REC.1400.204).

Results

The mean age of participants was 56 years, and the proportion of males to females was nearly the same. About half of the participants (46%) had a university degree, and the most common types of job included housewife (35%), self-employed (21%), employee (20%), and retired (18%). More than 90% of the patients were residing in urban regions and lived in the provincial capital. While more than 97% of patients had basic health insurance and over two-thirds of them were coved by social security insurance, only 30% had supplementary insurance and 85% solely received care from public clinics. More than 86% of patients had underlying diseases, including high blood pressure (41%), heart problems (40%), and diabetes (30%); and more than 95% of patients believed their condition had been cured or was under control. Although only 37% of the patients reported they exercised before the disease, 58% reported engaging in physical exercise due to the nature of their jobs. The patients in the study were admitted to the ward and ICU for an average of six days and about 7% experienced coma. About 98% experienced severe adverse effects from COVID-19, including 96% with lung failure and 68% with cardiac issues. A small fraction of patients and their family members lost their jobs due to the disease, and more than one-third of them had a substantial drop in income due to absence or diminished capacity to conduct tasks [14]. The assessment of direct medical costs revealed that patients paid an average of 209.48 USD (8798470 IRR) for the necessary diagnostic treatment, of which 78% was related to tests related to the side effects. Also, the patients spent an average of 1610.45 USD (67639230 IRR) on therapeutic care, where Hemoperfusion and medications accounted for the largest portions [14]. (Table 1)

Investigating the statistical relationship between demographic and background factors and direct medical costs revealed that being a local resident, basic health insurance, type of basic health insurance, supplementary health insurance, place of receiving care, prior underlying diseases (especially hypertension and lung diseases), concurrent hospitalization in the ward and ICU, history of coma, lingering heart problems after COVID-19, and the need for exchange were associated with higher direct medical costs (P < 0.05). Patients residing in the provincial capital had less direct medical costs than patients residing in other parts of the province and those from other provinces. In addition, patients with basic health insurance, especially those covered by the military forces and banks, as well as those with supplementary insurance, spent less than patients having other types of basic insurance or people without basic and supplementary health insurance. In addition, individuals who received care exclusively from public facilities paid far less than those who received care from both public and private hospitals (note: expensive treatment and unmanaged induced demand). Patients with underlying conditions, particularly those with a history of heart and lung issues, paid less than those without these diseases. Patients requiring concurrent admission in the ward and ICU, with a history of coma, requiring a Hemoperfusion, and those experiencing heart problems due to COVID-19 infection had much higher costs compared to other patients (Table 4).

Examining the statistical relationship between demographic and background factors with direct non-medical costs showed that gender, being a local resident, having supplementary health insurance, having an underlying health problem, concurrent admission to the ward and ICU, experiencing coma, suffering from heart and kidney disorders due to COVID-19 infection, as well as the need for Hemoperfusion had a significant effect on the amount of direct non-medical costs imposed on patients and their families (P < 0.05). Male patients, those living in the provincial capital, and patients with supplementary insurance incurred significantly less indirect non-medical costs compared to females, those referred from other parts of the province or neighboring provinces, and people without supplementary insurance. In addition, concurrent hospitalization in the ward and ICU, as well as experiencing coma while receiving care incurred additional direct non-medical costs. Moreover, the development of complications and heart and kidney failure as side effects of COVID-19, as well as the patient’s need for Hemoperfusion significantly increased direct non-medical costs (Table 5).

Examining the statistical relationship between demographic and background factors and indirect costs revealed that variables such as age, gender, employment status, residence type, place of receiving care, underlying heart diseases and hypertension, concurrent hospitalization in the ward and ICU, experiencing a coma, incidence of cardiac disorder due to COVID-19, and the need for Hemoperfusion had a significant effect on indirect costs (P < 0.05). Also, patients aged 41–60 years compared to the elderly population (over 60 years), males, and workers and self-employed individuals had more indirect costs compared to housewives, retirees, students, and unemployed individuals. In addition, people living in the provincial capital compared to people referred from other parts of the province and neighboring provinces, as well as people receiving services from public centers compared to people receiving services from both public and private centers (note: the role of the referral system and its efficiency and effectiveness) had less indirect costs. In the meantime, underlying heart disease and hypertension caused lower indirect costs (note: early and previous adjustment of activity hours and its severity due to the occurrence of these two diseases). In addition, concurrent hospitalization in the ward and ICU, experiencing a state of coma, complications of cardiac discomfort due to the infection of COVID-19, and the need for Hemoperfusion (note: all these items are examples of the severity of the disease) led to higher indirect costs on patients and their families (Table 6).

Discussion

Economic estimations in this study revealed that hospitalized COVID-19 patients and their families incurred monthly expenses of 2,773.43 USD (116,484,080 IRR). Of this amount, 1,833.43 USD (77,004,080 IRR) (66%) was attributed to direct medical costs, 357.97 USD (15,035,000 IRR) (13%) to direct non-medical costs, and 582.02 USD (24,445,000 IRR) (21%) to indirect costs. The most salient findings highlight the substantial financial burden, particularly in relation to direct medical costs. The magnitude of these expenses—even over a short period—can be compared to the costs associated with chronic debilitating conditions or major medical procedures. Another critical factor is the high price of imported drugs and the considerable expense of hemoperfusion, a treatment not recommended in any official health protocol.

Barasa et al. (2021) in Kenya determined the unit cost, i.e., the cost of providing services to COVID-19 patients at different stages of the disease. In this study, which was carried out with the approach of costs imposed on the health system, the costs imposed per patient in one day for asymptomatic patients and patients with mild to moderate disease who were under care at home were calculated as 18.89 USD and 18.99 USD, respectively. The cost of care for patients under care in the hospital was 63.68 USD and for asymptomatic patients and patients with mild to moderate disease was 63.70 USD. Also, the cost of care for patients with severe and critical states of the disease was estimated to be around 124.53 USD and 599.51 USD for each day of hospitalization, respectively [16].

Maltezou et al. (2010) in Greece estimated the costs imposed on the health system due to the infection of health workers with COVID-19 in the first epidemic. The results indicated that a total of 1,735,830 Euros was imposed on the Greek health system in the first epidemic, more than 80% of which was due to the absence of employees from work (indirect costs). Diagnostic and hospitalization costs were 10.2 USD and 6.5 USD, respectively [17].

Oksuz et al. (2021) in Turkey investigated the direct medical costs of COVID-19 epidemic on the society. The results of the study showed that the average hospitalization time for patients in the hospital was 9.1 days, which was estimated 8 and 14.8 days for the inpatient and ICU departments, respectively. The study also revealed that the annual cost imposed on the health system due to the outbreak of the COVID-19 epidemic and the resulting direct medical costs was more than 1.2 billion dollars, which is equivalent to 2% of the total annual budget of health system and 0.08 of the nation’s GDP [8].

Another study in the United States calculated the characteristics of the elderly people with COVID-19 and the related costs of medical care. The cost imposed on Medicare insurance in the form of merit pay (payment per care) for the patients under investigation was estimated to be more than 6.3 billion dollars, of which 92.6% was allocated to inpatients. The average duration of hospitalization for patients was 9.2 days and the hospitalization cost per patient was calculated to be 21,752 USD. Also, the cost of hospitalization was significantly higher in patients who needed a ventilator and patients who died. Males and colored individuals were more likely to be hospitalized and had higher medical care costs. Although the elderly people aged over 75 years had a higher chance of hospitalization, they imposed a lower cost on the health system [18].

The statistical correlation between demographic and background variables and direct medical costs revealed that factors such as residing in the provincial capital, having basic health insurance (particularly being covered by the armed forces and banks), having supplemental health insurance, and only receiving care from public facilities led to a decrease in the direct costs. The patient referral system is quick, efficient, and effective, allowing for timely access to the necessary specialized and sub-specialized care and eliminating the need for repeated diagnostic and therapeutic procedures, which is why living in the provincial capital had a positive impact on direct medical costs. Basic health insurance to cover the costs of patients based on insurance tariffs and the effectiveness of certain types of basic insurance or supplementary insurance can be attributed to their wider and more effective care coverage. Also, the effect of receiving care from public health centers on the direct medical costs with a higher probability of induced demand from the service provider, higher tariffs of private centers than public ones, the small requirement of private centers to comply with the approved tariffs, and even the lack of correct location of the private centers in the referral system, and therefore the less efficiency and effectiveness of the care provided by them can be justified. Also, the effect of simultaneous hospitalization in the ward and ICU, experiencing a coma, and the need for Hemoperfusion (which are components of the severity of the disease) on the incidence of direct medical costs can be attributed to the longer and more severe need of patients for care, and subsequently the more specialized and expensive care. The issue to consider is how underlying diseases such as heart disease can negatively affect the amount of direct medical costs. This is likely because patients previously took the initiative to obtain basic and supplemental health insurance for their underlying and occasionally chronic problems and used these insurances to obtain COVID-19-related care.

The study also revealed that having supplementary health insurance, being male, and residing in the provincial capital were associated with significantly lower direct non-medical costs. Men’s higher physical strength, improved ability to handle illness, and quicker recovery times are among the factors that contribute to the effect of gender on the lower costs imposed. It should also be noted that the presence of their spouses to care for them resulted in reduced indirect costs. Given that women managed domestic affairs and were frequently housewives in this study, it is obvious that if a woman becomes unwell and is unable to do household duties, there would be a much greater demand for financial resources to care for her and other family members. Another point is that the risk of disease among females is lower and they receive more medical attention compared to males. The lower travel costs of patients and their family members to care facilities, as well as the lack of a need to pay for lodging and food might justify the lower direct non-medical expenditures of patients who reside in the provincial capital. The effect of supplementary insurance on direct non-medical costs can be attributed to all or most of the costs related to the provision of medical equipment (as the main direct non-medical cost in this study). The severity of the condition is also indicated by factors like being admitted to the ward and ICU simultaneously, experiencing a coma, requiring Hemoperfusion, as well as heart and kidney disease, which prolongs the treatment process and raises such costs as transportation, accommodation, and food; this can also increase the possibility of longer recovery period and the need for support services, medical equipment, and adaptation of the home and work environment. Direct non-medical costs including medical equipment, modifying the living environment, and even providing support services to deal with previous underlying conditions prior to COVID-19 are the cause of the inverse relationship between previous underlying diseases and direct non-medical costs.

Our statistical analysis indicated that patients aged 41–60 years, males compared to females, and employees compared to most other occupational groups had a greater share of indirect costs compared to elderly patients or those with freelancing jobs. The higher indirect costs imposed on patients aged 41–60 years can be attributed to their higher employment rate and earning power compared to the elderly, because elderly people are often retired and may not lose their income if they are hospitalized. Also, the higher rate of employment in males than females in the study is the main reason for the higher indirect costs in males. Another point is that workers and employed people experience higher indirect costs than other occupational groups due to their daily wage situation (corporate employment) and the fact that their salaries and benefits are reduced if they lose their jobs. This is because workers are more likely to be employed than other occupational groups. Also, the higher indirect costs in self-employed people is also related to their income being dependent on economic productive activities and not receiving a fixed monthly income (like employees), as well as the little protection of the labor law. The lower indirect costs imposed on the patients living in the provincial capital can be attributed to the fewer days of absence from work due to being closer to the care centers, and subsequently less income reduction. The lower indirect costs of people who refer to public health centers is due to the role of referral system and the timely referral, which saves time for the patient and the family members, as well as the possibility of induced demand in referring patients. The negative role of patients suffering from heart disease and high blood pressure on the imposition of indirect costs can be justified by considering the early and previous adjustment of hours and intensity of activity due to the occurrence of these two diseases and before contracting the virus. So, simultaneous hospitalization in the ward and ICU, experiencing a coma, developing cardiac complications due to COVID-19, and the need for Hemoperfusion can be regarded as the examples for the severity of the disease, which lead to more indirect costs on the patients and their families.

The analysis of the statistical relationship between the demographic and contextual variables and the total costs imposed on the patients revealed that patients living in the provincial capital had a lower total cost compared to the patients referred from other parts of the province and neighboring provinces. Quick access to high-quality treatment facilities (in this case, Kowsar Hospital) results in the elimination of transportation, hotel, and food costs, which explains the drop in direct medical and non-medical costs, as well as the less indirect charges. Given the close connection between public health centers and the referral system and the absence of this relationship in the private sector, as well as the higher likelihood of induced demand in the private sector, it is reasonable to expect that patients receiving services from the public sector would pay a lower overall cost. Since most patients with an underlying problem, especially high blood pressure, have basic and supplemental health insurance to manage the costs of the underlying disease, provide auxiliary equipment, and modify the home and work environment before treatment, the total cost imposed on them is lower. Other factors that have a statistically significant relationship with the overall costs incurred by patients include more severe illness, the need for longer and more specialized (and more expensive) services, the need for additional care during recovery, and late return of the patients and their family members to their jobs.

According to the results of some similar studies, patients with basic health insurance [19–21], supplemental health insurance [10, 22], or insurances that cover additional care services and expenditures [23] have less medical costs. In another study, factors including the patient’s residence region [10, 20] and residence place (rural or urban) [21, 24, 25] had an impact on incurred costs. Additionally, parameters associated with disease severity are effective in imposing various costs on patients [10, 26]. There is a strong agreement between the results of prior studies and those reported in current study.

Some useful recommendations to better understand the issues and resolve them include the production of essential medicines by indigenous knowledge-based companies, removing health sanctions through international legal proceedings, monitoring the implementation of health protocols and preventing the provision of unsupported services, completion and continuity of full coverage of the population by insurance organizations, emphasis on expanding the coverage of care and treatment costs by basic health insurance, expanding supplementary insurances especially for high-risk and poor people, providing essential treatment facilities in all cities and emphasis on attracting local patients, and requiring public health and treatment facilities to operate within the confines of the nation’s referral system while also requiring them to refuse to create induced demand. Additionally, health insurance providers should pay extra attention to individuals with significant side effects and more advanced illness stages.

One of the limitations of this study is that it was conducted at the peak of the COVID-19 (delta type). However, since other strains of the disease may cost differently due to their unique characteristics and symptoms, conducting similar studies on those strains can be beneficial. Another limitation is that we failed to assess the costs of deceased hospitalized patients, who possibly had longer and more specialized care due to the severity of the illness, leading to higher costs. This study was conducted at Kowsar Hospital, the largest hospital in Semnan Province, which admitted the highest number of COVID-19 patients. Although the province has four other hospitals in its subordinate counties that also admitted COVID-19 patients, it is possible that some critically ill patients were referred to Kowsar Hospital. Consequently, selection bias may have occurred. Efforts were made to maximize the accuracy of patient-reported direct medical costs by reviewing available medical records. However, there remains a possibility of recall bias in the calculation of direct non-medical costs as well as indirect costs.

Conclusion

According to the results of this study, contrary to the common belief that high costs and complex surgeries are exclusive to chronic and debilitating diseases, COVID-19 imposes significant costs on hospitalized patients and their families. This can be attributed to the existence of significant geographic, economic, and occupational disparities because residents of the provincial capital, those capable to purchase supplementary insurances, and those with the organization’s basic exclusive insurances incur the lowest costs. Other determining factors that impose health costs on patients and their families are frequently related to the severity of the disease, among which concurrent hospitalization in the ward and ICU, experiencing a coma, side effects (especially cardiovascular complications), and the need for Hemoperfusion play the biggest role in the occurrence of all kinds of costs imposed on patients. The findings of this study can help managers and policy makers of the health system by highlighting the dimensions and severity of the issue, as well as the factors affecting it. This can result in better and more targeted protection for patients, especially in terms of appropriate insurance coverage.
