Clinical Significance of Lymph Node Count in Colon Cancer Surgery: A Tumour Location-stratified Prognostic Analysis Based on an Integrated Phase-III Randomized Controlled Trial (JCOG2310AS3)
Authors: Kenta Iguchi, Kozo Kataoka, Junki Mizusawa, Akira Ouchi, Yusuke Sano, Fumihiko Fujita, Satoshi Maruyama, Kohei Murata, Yusuke Suwa, Kazuma Ito, Takeshi Suto, Shin Fujita, Ryo Inada, Yasuhiro Shimada, Masafumi Inomata, Tetsuya Hamaguchi, Yasumasa Takii, Atsuo Takashima, Shunsuke Tsukamoto, Yukihide Kanemitsu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectives: The prognostic significance of lymph node (LN) count in colon cancer (CC) remains controversial. Current guidelines recommend retrieval of ≥12 LNs for accurate staging; however, tumour sidedness may influence its prognostic relevance. This study evaluated the impact of LN count on survival according to tumour sidedness and pathological stage (pStage). Methods: This integrated analysis used data from randomised trials conducted by the Japan Clinical Oncology Group. Data from patients with pStage II/III CC who underwent curative resection were analysed. The prognostic significance of LN count was evaluated using Cox proportional hazards models for each subgroup consisting of a combination of tumour sidedness (right-sided CC [RSCC] vs. left-sided CC [LSCC]) and pStage. Results: A total of 2,849 patients (1,034 RSCC, 1,815 LSCC) were included. LN counts were higher in RSCC than in LSCC (median: 30 vs. 21). LN count was not independently associated with overall survival (OS) in pStage II. In contrast, in pStage III, lower LN counts were associated with worse OS on both tumour sides. In RSCC, thresholds of 9–14 (hazard ratio range: 0.17–0.50) and 26–41 (0.50–0.65) were associated with improved OS, whereas in LSCC, thresholds of 8–12 (0.34–0.61) were significant. Conclusion: Tumour sidedness and stage were associated with the prognostic impact of LN count in patients with pStage II/III CC. LN count showed limited prognostic impact in pStage II but was associated with survival in pStage III. Keywords: colon cancer, lymph node count, surgery, survival, tumour sidedness

1. Introduction

The occurrence of metastatic lymph nodes (LNs) is a critical factor in defining the stage of colorectal cancer, as outlined in the national guidelines issued by organisations such as the European Society for Medical Oncology, American Joint Committee on Cancer, and National Comprehensive Cancer Network[1-3]. Although the number of metastatic LNs is considered a prognostic factor, no conclusive evidence exists regarding the association between the total dissected LN count and the prognosis of colorectal cancer.

Numerous studies have investigated the relationship between the dissected LN count and prognosis, proposing that LN count may serve as a quality indicator for lymphadenectomy[4-7]. National guidelines acknowledge that a low LN count (≤12 nodes) is a poor prognostic factor for stage-II colon cancer (CC)[1-3,8]. In the 1980s, Scott et al. reported that retrieving a minimum of 12 LNs resulted in a 90% probability of detecting metastatic nodes[9]. This finding led to the establishment of “12 nodes” as a cut-off value for adequate staging—a benchmark that has persisted for nearly half a century.

Surgical techniques for CC have evolved considerably. With advances in surgical quality, standardized lymphadenectomy (including complete mesocolic excision and D3 dissection), and more meticulous pathological evaluation, both the distribution and clinical implications of retrieved LN counts have changed. These developments necessitate a re-evaluation of conventional benchmarks such as the 12-node threshold. Furthermore, recent reports have suggested that LN counts differ between right- and left-sided colon surgeries[10-13]. Such differences may stem from anatomical, embryological, or technical variations, potentially affecting oncological outcomes. Thus, their clinical significance warrants further investigation. The studies reported to date on LN count in CC have been limited by the use of real-world data, a wide range in the median number of LNs, and the lack of a population with controlled surgical quality in the target cohort[14-17]. In addition, although LN count has been recognised as a high-risk factor in stage-II CC, its prognostic significance in stage-III disease remains unclear owing to a lack of definitive evidence.

Therefore, the present study aimed to examine the stage- and sidedness-specific association pattern between LN count and survival outcomes in stages II and III CC using an integrated analysis of the data from large randomised controlled trials (RCTs) conducted by the Colorectal Cancer Study Group (CCSG) of the Japan Clinical Oncology Group (JCOG)[18].

2. Methods

2.1. Primary Analysis Population

As part of a collaborative initiative within the JCOG–CCSG, we developed a protocol for pooled analysis, designated as JCOG2310A[18], to integrate data from four phase-III RCTs—JCOG0205[19], JCOG0910[20], JCOG0404[21], and JCOG1006[22]—each of which focused on optimising treatment strategies for colorectal cancer. This protocol was designed to enable high-quality standardised analyses across trials with consistent surgical procedures and data-collection methods. The JCOG0205 evaluated the efficacy of 5-fluorouracil/levofolinate vs. uracil and tegafur plus leucovorin as a postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy in patients with pathological stage (pStage) -III colorectal cancer. The JCOG0910 trial examined the non-inferiority of S-1 over capecitabine as an adjuvant therapy for pStage-III disease. The JCOG0404 trial investigated the comparative outcomes of open vs. laparoscopic surgeries in patients with clinical stage II or III CC. The JCOG1006 trial assessed the oncological impact of conventional surgery vs. the non-touch isolation technique in patients with clinical stage II or III CC. All trials included patients of 20–80 years of age (JCOG0404 limited to those ≤75 years old) who underwent curative surgery for colorectal cancer without prior chemotherapy. All patients with pStage-III received fluoropyrimidine-based adjuvant chemotherapy. The median follow-up period ranged from 72.8 to 78.2 months.

In the present study, we analysed data from three of the four trials. Patients with pStage II or III CC who underwent radical resection and were enrolled in the JCOG0404, JCOG0910, or JCOG1006 study were included in the analysis. Data from the JCOG0205 trial were excluded because this study did not record dissected LN count. Additionally, patients with pStage IV disease, appendiceal cancer, and those who did not undergo surgery were excluded. In cases where patients were enrolled in both JCOG0910 and JCOG1006, overlapping cases were identified through dataset-level verification and were counted only once, being assigned to JCOG0910 to avoid duplication. Each participant was included as a single case in the final analytic cohort.

2.2. Evaluation of the Optimal LN Count by Tumour Sidedness

Tumour location was classified according to the Japanese Society for Cancer of the Colon and Rectum (JSCCR) guidelines as follows:

•Right-sided CC (RSCC): tumours arising from the caecum to the transverse colon.

•Left-sided CC (LSCC): tumours arising from the descending to the rectosigmoid colon.

Because the JSCCR guidelines do not explicitly define tumours at the hepatic or splenic flexure, they were categorised based on their anatomical location as either ascending or transverse CC for hepatic flexure tumours, and transverse or descending CC for splenic flexure tumours. The Tumour, Node, Metastasis stage was determined using the Union for International Cancer Control 8th edition staging system.

This study analysed the impact of retrieved LN counts on survival outcomes in patients who underwent surgery for stages II and III CC, stratified by tumour location into RSCC and LSCC. The primary analysis focuses on the association between LN count and overall survival (OS) stratified by tumour sidedness. Secondary analyses include stage-specific evaluations, and exploratory analyses include assessment of potential cut-off values. A key feature of the patients included in this integrated analysis was the standardised approach to lymphadenectomy, with D2 or D3 LN dissection strictly mandated according to the JSCCR guidelines. LNs were divided into three distinct categories: main LNs (MLNs), intermediate LNs (ILNs), and pericolic LNs (PLNs). MLNs are LNs located at the origin of the feeding artery, whereas ILNs are those positioned along the course of the feeding artery. PLNs included LNs along the marginal artery and adjacent to the bowel wall. During D3 lymphadenectomy, MLNs, ILNs, and PLNs were carefully dissected. For the D2 lymphadenectomy, ILNs and PLNs were dissected. Surgical procedures were uniformly defined by the JCOG0404 and JCOG1006 study protocols, and adjuvant chemotherapy was uniformly defined by each study protocol, ensuring a homogeneous patient population compared with real-world data.

2.3. Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were summarised using median and range, and categorical variables were analysed using Fisher's exact test or chi-squared test, as appropriate. The LN count was categorised based on cut-off values, and multivariable analysis was performed to assess prognostic significance. Multivariable analysis was performed using OS as the outcome measure. A Cox proportional hazards model was used to evaluate the effect of LN count on survival for each subgroup consisting of a combination of pStage (II or III) and tumour location (right- or left-sided). Covariates were selected a priori based on their established clinical relevance and previously reported prognostic significance in CC. These variables were considered potential confounders, as they may be associated with both LN count and survival outcomes. Adjustment for these factors was performed in the multivariable analysis to account for potential confounding. The model included the following covariates: age (<70 vs. ≥70 years), sex (male vs. female), histological type (differentiated [well-differentiated and moderately differentiated tubular adenocarcinoma] vs. poorly differentiated [poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma, mucinous adenocarcinoma, and signet-ring cell carcinoma]), extent of lymphadenectomy (D2 vs. D3), LN yield (above vs. below the cut-off value), and receipt of adjuvant chemotherapy (yes vs. no). For stage II, the pT category (T3 vs. T4) was included, whereas for stage III, both pT (T1–T4) and pN (N1 vs. N2) categories were incorporated. In the multivariable analysis, for each cut-off value of the LN count, patients with counts below the cut-off were used as the reference group (hazard ratio [HR] = 1), and the HRs for those with counts equal to or above the cut-off were evaluated accordingly. Given the limited number of events for OS in some subgroups, additional exploratory analyses were performed using recurrence-free survival (RFS). Spline-based univariable analyses were conducted to evaluate the overall trend in the relationship between LN count and survival, with 12 nodes used as the reference value. P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS software version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC).

2.4. Ethical Approval

The independent ethics committees of the participating centres approved the secondary use of anonymised data. The ethics committees of the National Cancer Center and Kanagawa Cancer Center, the main research institutes for this analysis, approved this integrated study protocol. Individual patient consent was not required.

Stage II

In pStage II disease, LN count was not consistently associated with OS across the tested cut-off values in either RSCC or LSCC group. In the RSCC group, no cut-off demonstrated a statistically significant association with OS. In the LSCC group, statistical significance was observed only within a narrow cut-off range of 25–26 nodes (hazard ratio [HR] range: 0.37–0.42); however, other LN cut-off values were not independently associated with prognosis.

Stage III

In pStage III, LN count tended to be associated with improved OS in both RSCC and LSCC; however, the magnitude and statistical significance of HRs varied according to the selected threshold. In the RSCC group, statistically significant HRs were observed when lower thresholds (9–14 nodes) were applied (HR range 0.17–0.50), as well as when higher thresholds (26–41 nodes) were examined (HR range 0.50–0.65). In contrast, intermediate thresholds showed attenuated associations and were not consistently statistically significant. In the LSCC group, an LN count of 8–12 was an independent prognostic factor (HR range 0.34–0.61).

4. Discussion

The present study suggests that the prognostic relevance of LN count differs according to stage and tumour sidedness. LN count was higher in RSCC than in LSCC, indicating that the distribution of retrieved LNs varied by tumour location. In pStage II, LN count was not consistently associated with OS in either RSCC or LSCC, indicating limited independent prognostic value in this stage. In contrast, in pStage III, very low LN counts were consistently associated with worse OS on both tumour sides.

Current guidelines recommend a minimum LN removal of ≥12 in number for accurate staging in colorectal cancer[2,3,23]. However, emerging evidence suggests that this threshold may not be universally applicable across tumour subtypes and stages. Previous studies have demonstrated that RSCC and LSCC exhibit distinct biological behaviours[24-26]. RSCC is associated with a higher prevalence of microsatellite instability (MSI-H), BRAF mutations, and mucinous histology, whereas LSCC is more frequently characterised by chromosomal instability and TP53 mutations[25]. These molecular differences may influence the patterns of lymphatic spread, immune response, and tumour aggressiveness, ultimately affecting the prognostic value of LN dissection[27-29]. These biological differences may partly explain the distinct patterns observed in the present study. For example, the higher prevalence of MSI-H and enhanced immune response in RSCC may be associated with increased LN retrieval and a different relationship between LN count and prognosis. In contrast, the molecular and anatomical characteristics of LSCC may result in a more localized pattern of lymphatic spread, potentially contributing to the differing prognostic implications of LN between tumour sides.

In the present study, the total LN counts differed significantly between stage II RSCC and LSCC. However, the LN count had limited impact on prognosis on both sides. Meanwhile, in stage II LSCC, although 25–26 LNs were considered an independent prognostic factor, considering that this result was derived from multivariable analysis with varying cut-off values, the significance observed at 25–26 LNs may be incidental. Considering that no other cut-off values demonstrated independent prognostic significance, we could conclude that, overall, the LN count is not an independent prognostic factor in either RSCC or LSCC among patients who underwent oncologic resection. Although accurate staging and prevention of stage migration are crucial for appropriate postoperative management and treatment of stage II CC, regardless of tumour sidedness, the widely used cut-off of 12 LNs may not function as a reliable prognostic discriminator. Importantly, low LN count should not be interpreted as a direct causal factor for poor survival. Rather, it may represent a surrogate marker reflecting multiple underlying factors, including the quality of surgical resection, the extent and thoroughness of pathological examination, and inherent tumour biology. The clinical significance of a low LN count may differ between stage II and stage III disease. In stage II, a low LN count may lead to understaging due to occult nodal metastases, potentially resulting in the omission of adjuvant chemotherapy and adversely affecting outcomes. In contrast, in stage III disease, the negative prognostic impact of a low LN count is less likely to be explained by stage migration and may instead reflect other factors beyond staging accuracy.

In stage III disease, lower LN counts were consistently associated with worse survival outcomes in both RSCC and LSCC. Although statistically significant associations were observed across several examined thresholds, the magnitude and significance of HRs varied depending on the selected cut-off, and no single optimal LN count could be identified. The apparent bimodal pattern observed in RSCC should be interpreted with caution. Because multiple thresholds were sequentially examined, the composition of comparison groups changed according to the selected cut-off. Lower thresholds primarily identified patients with markedly low LN yields, whereas higher thresholds isolated patients with relatively high LN yields. Intermediate thresholds compared groups with more overlapping LN distributions, potentially attenuating detectable differences. Therefore, the observed bimodal distribution of significant thresholds is likely attributable to the analytical approach and underlying LN distribution rather than a true biological threshold effect. Given these considerations, our findings support the conclusion that insufficient LN yield is associated with worse prognosis in stage III disease, regardless of tumour sidedness.

This study utilised data from phase-III RCTs on CC, which offer several advantages. The high proportion of D3 dissections and standardised surgical quality in this dataset distinguish it from previous reports, ensuring greater uniformity and robustness. Therefore, the likelihood of stage migration or incomplete LN resection is likely to be low. In contrast to most prior studies, which have relied on real-world datasets with inherent variability, the findings presented here were derived from a more controlled and rigorous dataset, enhancing the reliability of the conclusions. Furthermore, in the present study, the median LN count was relatively high, with 21 and 30 nodes on the left and right sides, respectively. In Japan, meticulous LN retrieval is routinely performed postoperatively by surgeons or pathologists. When evaluating the LN count, considering the extent to which LN examination is conducted in the background population is essential. This methodological rigor, which is based on data from RCTs, represents a key strength of the present study.

The present study has few limitations. First, as an ad hoc analysis with a limited sample size, the study may have been subject to residual confounding factors, potentially limiting the statistical power of the findings. In particular, the lack of information on key biomarkers, such as RAS, BRAF, and MSI status, restricts the ability to fully assess the molecular heterogeneity of tumours. However, differences in biomarker profiles are strongly associated with tumour localisation. By stratifying tumours based on right- and left-sided locations, this study highlights the potential clinical relevance of tumour sidedness beyond current guideline frameworks. Second, differences in adjuvant chemotherapy regimens and patient selection due to historical treatment practices may result in OS outcomes that differ from those observed in current clinical settings. Therefore, further studies are necessary to determine whether the findings of this study are valid for contemporary treatment strategies. In addition, further validation through prospective and molecular studies is warranted to refine CC surgical strategies and improve patient outcomes.

The present study suggests that LN count has limited prognostic impact in stage II but appears to be more relevant in stage III disease. Rather than indicating distinct optimal LN counts for right- and left-sided CCs, our findings suggest that very low LN yield is associated with worse survival outcomes in both tumour sides.
