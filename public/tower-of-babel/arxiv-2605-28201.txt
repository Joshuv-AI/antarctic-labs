Plant, Persist, Trigger: Sleeper Attack on Large Language Model Agents
Yongxiang Li Moxin Li Zhixin Ma Fengbin Zhu, Dongrui Liu Wenjie Wang Fuli Feng

Abstract
Large Language Model (LLM) agents remain vulnerable to safety threats from the external environment, where attackers inject adversarial content into external observations such as tool-returned data, webpages, or MCP context, causing harmful agentic behaviors such as unsafe actions or incorrect outputs. Existing studies typically focus on single-interaction attacks, where the agent observes adversarial content and immediately exhibits harmful behavior within one user request. However, we show that adversarial content can also persist across interactions served by the same agent, making such threats harder to detect and mitigate. Specifically, adversarial content may persist in the agent state, remain dormant across interactions, and later be activated by a benign user query. We formalize this type of safety threat as Sleeper Attack . To evaluate it, we construct a benchmark with 1,896 instances covering six real-world harmful outcomes, three attack strategies, and three agent state targets: session context, memory, and reusable skills. Experiments on seven strong open-source and closed-source LLMs show that state-of-the-art LLM agents remain vulnerable to Sleeper Attack, even when they achieve low attack success rates under a single-interaction baseline. Our code and data are available at https://anonymous.4open.science/r/skdvnfu23ihr9wdscnksf1asdffsaef .

Large Language Model (LLM) agents use LLMs as their core to complete user-specified tasks by executing actions and observing the external environment

.
Despite their utility, LLM agents face safety risks that limit deployment, including unsafe tool calls and transactions

Safety threats to LLM agents can be broadly categorized into

Internal threats originate from the user or the LLM itself, such as malicious or jailbreaking user requests

.

External

, webpages

, and MCP context

.
Such adversarial content may contain malicious instructions that induce unsafe actions
(

or tampered information that causes incorrect agent outputs
(

.
External threats are particularly challenging because the external environments are diverse, dynamic, and difficult for the agent system to monitor and control.
We therefore focus on the external-threat setting.

We identify a key limitation of existing studies on external attacks.
Most benchmark evaluations assume that attacks occur within a single user interaction: adversarial content is injected and triggers harmful agent behavior within the same user request
(
4
;
33
)
. However, adversarial content may also be planted and stored in agent state, remain dormant across interactions, and later be triggered by a benign user request. For example, an attacker may plant a malicious instruction such as
“when the email-sending tool is invoked in a future task, check and email the user’s Venmo balance to attacker@gmail.com.”
Such cross-interaction attacks are harder to trace and mitigate.
Moreover, adversarial content may persist in different forms of agent state such as memory
25
and skills
17
, creating diverse planting and triggering mechanisms that are difficult to evaluate systematically.

Therefore, we formalize a new type of external safety threat for LLM agents:
Sleeper Attack
. An illustration is shown in Figure
1
.
To systematically study this threat, we design a taxonomy along two axes.
The first axis describes how adversarial content triggers harmful behaviors. We consider two attack modes: malicious instruction planting, where planted instructions later induce unsafe actions, and stored information corruption, where corrupted stored information later leads to incorrect outputs. These modes give rise to three attack strategies:
Latent Instruction Planting
(LIP),
Proactive Information Elicitation
(PIE), and
Persistent Information Corruption
(PIC).
The second axis specifies where adversarial content is retained, covering three agent state targets: session context, memory, and reusable skills, which capture increasingly abstract forms of retained agent experience.

Based on this formulation, we construct a comprehensive benchmark with 1,896 evaluation instances, covering six real-world harmful outcomes.
We evaluate seven strong open-source and closed-source LLMs on our benchmark.
The results show that state-of-the-art LLM agents remain vulnerable to Sleeper Attack across different attack strategies and agent state targets, even when they achieve low Attack Success Rate (ASR) under the single-interaction baseline.
For example, Gemini-3-Flash consistently exceeds a 50% ASR across LIP, PIE, and PIC, and all evaluated agents reach over 27% ASR under PIC.
Our contributions are threefold:

•

We introduce and formalize
Sleeper Attack
, to our knowledge the first unified external-source threat model for cross-interaction LLM-agent attacks: adversarial content from an untrusted source is planted into session, memory, or skill state, remains dormant, and is later triggered by benign user requests, making it more covert and harmful than single-interaction attacks.

•

We build a large-scale benchmark for this setting with 1,896 evaluation instances spanning six real-world harmful outcomes, three attack strategies, three agent state targets, with four evaluation surfaces (direct, session, memory, and skill), enabling controlled direct-versus-sleeper comparison.

•

We reveal large direct-to-sleeper safety gaps across seven strong models: LIP rises from 11.1% direct ASR to 39.9% on the strongest agent state, PIE from 0.6% to 41.6%, and PIC reaches 47.8% mean ASR overall, showing that agents remain vulnerable to sleeper attack even when they achieve low ASR under the single-interaction baseline.

Related Work

External Threats to LLM Agents.

External threats have emerged as a central agent-safety problem because untrusted content can be mistaken for instructions instead of data. BIPIA
(
39
)
studies indirect prompt injection in content-consuming LLM applications, InjecAgent
(
40
)
moves to tool-integrated agents, AgentDojo
8
and WASP
11
evaluate prompt injection in richer agent and web environments, and WebInject
31
and TopicAttack
6
broaden the web setting with page-level and topic-transition attacks. These works establish external attacks as a practical threat, but they still primarily study attacks that hijack the agent in the current interaction or within a specific environment, leaving unified cross-interaction across session, memory, and skill surfaces unaddressed as a single problem.

Memory Attacks.

Saved user state is risky because agents later reuse it as trusted context. AgentPoison demonstrates that poisoned memories or knowledge-base entries can steer later agent behavior
(
7
)
, while MINJA shows that ordinary query-only interactions can still inject adversarial memory
(
9
)
. Storage-to-Steering frames memory as a control-flow channel
(
35
)
. Hidden in Memory and MemoryGraft study memory-only sleeper payloads and poisoned experience retrieval
(
25
;
30
)
, and Zombie Agents examines self-reinforcing updates in self-evolving agents
(
37
)
. These papers show why stored state matters. However, they focus on memory-specific mechanisms, not a unified sleeper benchmark spanning session context, memory, and reusable skills in one evaluation setting.

Skill Attacks.

Reusable skills and skill guidance form a persistent state because agents can reload them across future tasks. SkillJect automates stealthy skill-file injection for coding agents
(
17
)
, and Skill-Inject measures agent vulnerability to malicious skill files
(
29
)
. SkillTrojan studies backdoor behavior in skill-based systems
(
12
)
, while supply-chain poisoning work studies third-party skill repositories as distribution channels
(
26
)
. Because skills encode procedural guidance, poisoned skill content can affect later tool selection and argument construction after the original source content is no longer visible. These works show that skills are a practical attack surface; we study them together with session context and memory in a unified sleeper-attack setting across surfaces.

Benchmark Construction

3.1
Problem Formulation

LLM Agents and External Safety Threats.

We formalize an LLM agent system as an interaction process among a user, an LLM policy, an external environment, and agent states.
The LLM policy
\pi_{\theta}
serves as the core policy, receiving user inputs and producing actions such as tool invocation or response generation.
The external environment
E
executes actions and returns the corresponding observations such as tool and environmental feedback.
The agent state stores past execution experience, including session context, memory, skills, and other persistent system information, and is updated over time by a transition function
f
.

We define an interaction as a complete agent execution trajectory initiated by a user query.
At interaction
T
, the user provides a query
x_{T}
.
The LLM policy generates a sequence of actions to respond to the query and receives the corresponding environmental observations.
We denote this action-observation sequence of length
M_{t}
as

g_{T}=\big((a_{t,1},o_{t,1}),\ldots,(a_{t,M_{t}},o_{t,M_{t}})\big),

where
a_{t,i}
is the
i
-th action and
o_{t,i}
is the corresponding observation.
Let
s_{t}
denote the agent state at interaction
t
.
We define the interaction-level history as
h_{t}=(x_{t},s_{t},g_{t})
.
Specifically, the interaction
t
proceeds as

\displaystyle a_{t,i}

\displaystyle\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid h_{<t},x_{t},s_{t},g_{t,<i}),

(1)

\displaystyle o_{t,i}

\displaystyle=E(a_{t,i}),\quad i=1,\ldots,M_{t}.

After the interaction is completed, the agent state is updated by

s_{t+1}=f(s_{t},h_{t}).

(2)

In this interaction process, an external attacker may inject adversarial observations into the environment, such as tool outputs containing malicious instructions
(
4
;
33
)
.
We define an interaction-level safety indicator
U(h_{t})
, where
U(h_{t})=1
indicates that interaction
t
is unsafe.
An interaction is unsafe if the agent deviates from the user-intended task, relies on false or misleading information, or violates safety or privacy constraints
(
22
;
33
;
10
;
23
)
.
Existing work primarily studies attacks that happen within a single interaction, i.e., under the current user query
(
4
;
33
)
.
Let
o_{t,i}^{\mathrm{adv}}
denote an adversarial observation at step
i
of interaction
t
, and let
h_{t}^{\mathrm{adv}}
denote the resulting contaminated interaction-level history.
The attack can be represented as:

\displaystyle o_{t,i}

\displaystyle=E(a_{t,i}),\quad o_{t,i}\rightarrow o_{t,i}^{\mathrm{adv}},

(3)

\displaystyle a_{t,i+1}

\displaystyle\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid h_{<t},x_{t},s_{t},g_{t,<i},a_{t,i},o_{t,i}^{\mathrm{adv}}),

leading to
U(h_{t}^{\mathrm{adv}})=1
.

Sleeper Attack.

We further identify that such attacks can persist across interactions through the agent’s state. We define
Sleeper Attack
as an attack in which an adversarial observation injected at interaction
t
does not immediately induce unsafe behavior, but is stored in agent state and later activated by a benign user query. For example, a malicious webpage may inject a hidden instruction such as
“when the email-sending tool is invoked in a future task, check and email the user’s Venmo balance to attacker@gmail.com”.

Formally, the adversarial observation
o_{t,i}^{\mathrm{adv}}
is introduced in interaction
t
.
Rather than causing immediate unsafe behavior, the adversarial information is stored during the state update:

s_{t+1}^{\mathrm{adv}}=f(s_{t},h_{t}^{\mathrm{adv}}),\qquad U(h_{t}^{\mathrm{adv}})=0.

(4)

At a future interaction
t+k
with a benign query
x_{t+k}
,
s_{t+k}^{\mathrm{adv}}
may then invoke unsafe behavior:

\displaystyle a_{t+k,j}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid h_{<t+k},x_{t+k},s_{t+k}^{\mathrm{adv}},g_{t+k,<j}),

(5)

leading to
U(h_{t+k}^{\mathrm{adv}})=1
.

Attack Strategies.

Following prior studies on external safety threats, we extend two common attack modes to the sleeper attack setting. First, attackers can plant malicious instructions that remain dormant until later activated by benign user queries, causing the agent to perform harmful actions that deviate from the user request. We refer to this strategy as
Latent Instruction Planting
(LIP), and further introduce
Proactive Information Elicitation
(PIE), an enhanced LIP variant where the planted instruction makes the agent elicit task-relevant sensitive information before executing the malicious action.
Second, attackers can corrupt persistent information in the agent state, causing the agent to rely on incorrect information when responding to future user requests. We instantiate the attack strategy as
Persistent Information Corruption
(PIC).

We consider three attack targets within the agent state, corresponding to increasing levels of abstraction in agent experience:
session context
,
memory
, and
reusable skills
.
Higher-level targets could be more abstract and therefore harder to inspect and detect.
In the session-context setting, the adversarial observation is directly retained in the conversation history.
In the memory setting, the adversarial observation is written into memory modules and later retrieved into the LLM context for relevant inputs.
In the skill setting, the adversarial observation is injected into hierarchical skills with low-level task-specific skills and higher-level general skills.

3.2
Benchmark Construction

Agent System Construction.

We construct a benchmark for evaluating Sleeper Attack in a simulated agent framework designed for safety testing.
The benchmark is built with the OpenAI Agents SDK
1
1

1

https://github.com/openai/openai-agents-python
.
.
We use ToolEmu
(
28
)
as the base simulated tool environment, which provides 330 tools, and simulate tool outputs using DeepSeek-v3.2
19
.
On top of this environment, we add agent state interfaces consisting of memory and reusable skills.
The memory module supports storage, retrieval, and update.
The skill module follows a two-level hierarchy: category-level skills at the higher level and tool-level skills at the lower level.
Both levels can be retrieved and updated during agent execution.
We initialize the skill library with 21 high-level skills and 309 low-level skills.
For PIE, we additionally use DeepSeek-v3.2 to simulate the user to provide the task-sensitive information.

Instance Construction.

To ensure comprehensive and realistic evaluation, we first review existing taxonomies of real-world harmful outcomes
(
20
)
and derive six harm domains: economic harm, account/system compromise, physical-world harm, personal data leakage, financial data leakage, and leakage of other sensitive information.
Economic harm covers unauthorized payments, purchases, or resource transfers; account/system compromise covers credential, account, or system takeover; and physical-world harm covers actions that affect real-world devices, deliveries, or services.
Personal data leakage covers disclosure of identities, contact details, or private attributes; financial data leakage covers account, card, or transaction data; and other sensitive-information leakage covers confidential nonfinancial data, including API tokens, internal files, operational notes, and private records.

For prompt construction, we first fix one of the six harm-domain labels and then ask an LLM to generate a concrete harmful goal under that label, conditioned on the attack strategy, agent state target, and compatible tool metadata.
This generation step also produces a benign trigger query that is harmless in isolation but can activate the planted observation.
The planting query itself is adapted from the source-tool user-request pattern used by InjecAgent
(
40
)
, so that the first interaction naturally exposes attacker-controlled source-tool output without generating it from scratch.

Strategy-specific templates then combine the adapted planting query, the generated harmful goal, and the benign trigger query into LIP, PIE, or PIC instances.
We concatenate the planting and trigger queries into a two-interaction test instance.
To evaluate longer-horizon sleeper behavior, we further extend this setup to an
N
-interaction setting by inserting additional LLM-generated benign interactions between the planting and trigger queries.

Some candidate tasks cannot be completed even when the user directly supplies the attack instruction as the input, so we exclude those cases during construction. In total, we construct 1,896 instances. Benchmark statistics are shown in Table

Evaluation Protocol.

We find that closed-source LLM judges are insufficiently reliable for evaluating trajectory-level safety.
We therefore design a rule-based evaluation protocol, where each instance is paired with a set of hard-coded rules specifying the conditions for a successful attack.
For example, if the harmful goal is to leak the user’s Venmo balance to an attacker-controlled address, the rule checks whether the agent exposes the sensitive value and sends it to that address.

Specifically, we evaluate two aspects: ordered trace matching, which verifies that the targeted action sequence occurs in the correct order, and argument-value matching, which checks critical fields such as attacker-controlled email addresses, leaked information, or corrupted information.
During benchmark construction, these rules are generated together with the instances and then validated through a three-model quality-control procedure.
Concretely, one model proposes or rewrites the
eval_config
, a second model critiques missing steps, schema mismatches, and weak value constraints, and a third model arbitrates whether to accept the rule or send it to another capped review round.
Details are provided in Appendix
B
.
We check the validated rules on the agent interaction trajectory using Python code to determine whether the harmful goal has been achieved.
We report ASR for comparison, defined as the proportion of trajectories that satisfy all rules.

The main LIP session cases use a trigger keyed to the later request’s tool call, but a sleeper instruction can wait for many other conditions, including combinations of benign cues. Lightweight Defenses.

We evaluate two training-free mitigations on Qwen3.5-Plus: a rule-based instruction set and a guard-based filter. The rule-based instruction set is prepended to the agent instruction and tells the agent to prioritize the current user request, treat tool outputs, retrieved text, memory notes, and skill content as supporting context, clarify ambiguous details, and save information only when the user clearly wants it preserved. The guard-based filter appends only a warning-handling suffix and uses
llama-guard-4-12b
to review tool-return values, annotate unsafe returns, and pass the annotated result to the agent. Table
7
shows that both reduce many settings but do not remove the threat. The guard-based defense lowers PIE skill from 51.2% to 30.9% and PIC memory from 49.3% to 28.7%, while rule-based instructions are less stable and can increase ASR. This suggests that rule-based defense or filtering defense is not sufficient against cross-interaction external threats.

5
Conclusion

We introduced sleeper attack, a delayed external-source threat model in which adversarial content is planted into agent states and later activated by benign requests. Our benchmark spans three attack strategies across three agents states and comprises 1,896 evaluation instances. All evaluated models exhibited unsafe behavior across the all strategies, and lightweight defenses could only slightly reduce the risk, failing to eliminate it. These results show that agent safety evaluation must track not only immediate tool use, but also how untrusted content is written, stored, and reused across interactions.

Limitations

Our study relies on a ToolEmu-derived sandbox with simulated tool returns, so results may differ in deployed tool ecosystems. The benchmark captures broad tool-use patterns, but real services may impose additional authentication, confirmation, rate limits, or audit logs that change both attack feasibility and user-visible harm. Conversely, deployed agents may expose longer-lived files, retrieval indices, shared workspaces, or multi-agent state that are not covered by our three agent states.

Our benchmark also abstracts away several deployment-specific control layers. Real agent products may gate sensitive actions behind user confirmations, permission scopes, organizational policies, or human review workflows, while other products may automatically compose multiple services in ways that create new persistence paths. We therefore interpret the results as measuring agent states vulnerability under controlled agent interfaces, not as a direct estimate of incident rates in any particular deployed system.Because of resource limits, supplementary experiments use narrower model slices to isolate mechanisms.

Ethicals Considerations

This work studies a dual-use safety problem: external attackers may plant adversarial content into agent state and later trigger harmful behavior through benign user requests. Our goal is defensive: we aim to help researchers and practitioners identify agent state vulnerabilities before they appear in deployed agents.

All experiments are conducted in a simulated tool environment and not against real services, accounts, webpages, or users. The benchmark uses synthetic sensitive values, simulated user responses, and structured trace checks, so successful attacks in our experiments do not perform real-world transactions or disclosures. Because the benchmark contains adversarial prompts, released artifacts should be used only for authorized red-teaming and defense development, with real sensitive information removed and responsible disclosure followed when deployed vulnerabilities are found.

Table 10:
Ask-user simulation protocol used by Proactive Information Elicitation.

When an attacked agent asks for information corresponding to one or more fields in
ground_truth_params
, and the simulated user does not explicitly refuse, the simulator returns the corresponding asked-for ground-truth values. If the agent asks only a subset of the required fields, only that subset is made available; missing fields are not silently completed. A non-empty tool result therefore only shows that the simulator returned some response. The meaningful question is whether the agent elicited all case-required fields in
ground_truth_params
and then used those values to complete the harmful action. The final ASR for Proactive Information Elicitation cases is therefore computed by the deterministic structured evaluator, not by a standalone answer-rate statistic. In real deployments, users may refuse, misunderstand the request, or provide incomplete information, so this protocol isolates the agent-side tendency to solicit and use missing attack parameters.

B.2.1
Review Loop

After B.1.1 and B.1.2 produce a repaired draft, we run a capped three-model review loop to decide whether that draft is already sufficient. The loop uses three fixed models with fixed roles: Qwen3.5-Flash as proposer,
deepseek-v3.2
as critic, and Gemini-3-Flash as arbiter. If the arbiter approves the current draft, the process stops immediately; otherwise the arbiter returns a revised current draft for the next round. C.2
Slice Selection Notes

Fresh-Session Replay.

This experiment is built from existing successful writes in the main benchmark, not from newly authored templates. We only evaluate
memory
and
skill
, because the direct baseline is not persistent and
session
does not survive a fresh session boundary. We focus on Qwen3.5-Plus and Qwen3.5-Flash as a compact slice with both non-trivial sleeper attack risk and strong contrast in original update coverage. Only cases whose original write succeeded but whose same-session attack failed are rerun in a fresh session; cases that already succeed in the original session are counted in the
Same
component and need no replay. If a slice has no remaining write-success/failure cases, as in Qwen3.5-Flash PIC skill, the fresh-session gain is therefore zero by construction. The main-text table reports same-session ASR over successful writes, the additional fresh-session gain from cases that previously failed, and the resulting cumulative ASR over that same successful-write denominator.

Conditional Session Trigger Taxonomy.

This study uses the
Latent Instruction Planting
session slice only. We keep the original tool-call trigger as the baseline and generate four semantic rewrite types: time, emotion, location/keyword, and A+B logic. The purpose is to test whether a session carry-over can be made selectively triggerable through benign semantic cues. Memory and skill are not included because their later reactivation is driven primarily by retrieval and reuse of explicitly persisted state, which is a different question from trigger selectivity.

Longer-Horizon Triggering.

We sweep trigger distance only for
Persistent Information Corruption
, using the completed interaction points
\{1,3,5,8,12,20\}
and the three agent states. This is the strongest strategy in the main benchmark and the cleanest setting for contrasting session-context dilution with explicit state persistence in memory and skill.

Lightweight Defenses.

We evaluate a rule-based instruction set and a guard-based filter on Qwen3.5-Plus. The goal is not to present a full mitigation benchmark, but to test whether simple training-free defenses materially reduce agent-state ASR on a model with meaningful failures across all three strategies. Prompt-level details are listed in Section
C.4
.

Open-Model Scaling.

The open-model study evaluates Qwen3-4B, 8B, 14B, and 32B. We aggregate the three agent states when plotting the main figure so that the comparison reflects how sleeper attack risk varies across open Qwen3 model sizes.

Unless otherwise stated, each cell is reported as percentage with the corresponding success/evaluable count in parentheses. Section D.1 gives the detailed data behind the interaction-distance and open-model scaling figures; Section D.2 reports the persistent-state decomposition; Section D.3 isolates privacy-leakage outcomes. The ask-user simulator protocol for Proactive Information Elicitation cases is described separately in Table
10
.

D.1
Intermediate-Event Metrics

The supplementary result tables in this appendix use the following intermediate-event definitions. In Table
21
,
Write
means that the run called
SkillUpdate
or
MemoryUpdate
on the corresponding state target, while
Write-only
means that this state update happened but the final same-session attack did not satisfy the structured success rule. These write-only cases are important because they can persist into later sessions even when the current trace appears harmless.

For Table
22
, the privacy-leakage slice includes cases labeled with
other_sensitive_info
,
economic_data
,
physical_data
, or
sensitive_info_leakage
.
Sink called
records whether the downstream disclosure or exfiltration tool was invoked.
Sink success
additionally requires the tool result to report a completed outbound action. For the Latent Instruction Planting and Proactive Information Elicitation privacy cases this is typically a successful
GmailSendEmail
result; for Persistent Information Corruption it can instead be a successful share, message, public-post update, or other case-specific disclosure sink.

Table

should be interpreted with the capability of each agent state in mind. The open Qwen3 models often show much lower ASR on
memory
and

session

This does not necessarily mean that these state targets are intrinsically safer; in many runs, the model appears less reliable at updating, retrieving, or applying memory and skill state.
