Safety analysis of water collection troughs in cooling towers based on wind tunnel tests
Authors: Qing Zhu, Lujun Li, Xiaojun Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo evaluate the structural safety of Water Collection Troughs (WCTs) in high-level water collection cooling towers under extreme wind conditions, wind tunnel tests were conducted on a 1:363 geometrically scaled WCT model. The internal and bottom pressure distributions were measured under various wind directions, and the upward and horizontal wind-induced pressures of the full-scale prototype were determined based on the measured pressure difference coefficients. Results indicate that without windbreak partition walls, ambient wind exhibits negligible influence on the internal pressure difference coefficients but strongly modulates the bottom pressure distribution, yielding a maximum pressure difference coefficients of 0.45. In contrast, substantial alterations in pressure characteristics were observed following the installation of windbreak partition walls, with the peak coefficient rising to 0.68 (a 51% increase). Under the extreme design wind speed of 46 m/s, the maximum upward and horizontal wind-induced pressures reach 2100 Pa and 850 Pa, respectively. For applications in extreme wind regions, the structural integrity of the WCTs and their connections should be thoroughly verified during the design phase. Results from scaled model tests, at lower Reynolds numbers than the prototype but within the drag square-law region with Reynolds-independent aerodynamics, primarily apply to similar tower configurations; field validation is recommended for distinct geometries or extreme wind assessments.Keywords: Water collection troughs (WCTs), High-level water collection cooling towers, Wind tunnel tests, Ambient wind, Structural safetySubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering

Introduction

Cooling towers are core thermal-hydraulic equipment that enables efficient heat exchange between ambient air and circulating water, and thus play a critical role in the reliable and efficient operation of power generation sector1,2. Within this category, high-level water collection cooling towers, whose structural comparison with traditional cooling towers is shown in Fig. 1, have been widely deployed in modern thermal power plants owing to their inherent advantages of superior energy efficiency, low noise emission, and reduced environmental footprint.

Structural comparison with high-level water collection cooling towers and traditional cooling towers.

To quantify the performance benefits of this tower design, Guo Y. et al.3 performed a systematic comparative analysis of flow resistance coefficients, thermal-hydraulic performance, and full-life-cycle economic viability between high-level water collection cooling towers and conventional counterflow cooling towers. Their findings confirmed that high-level water collection cooling towers outperform conventional designs across multiple key metrics: they deliver a higher ventilation rate, enhanced cooling efficiency, reduced shaft power of matched circulating water pumps, and improved comprehensive economic benefits over the full service life, alongside better environmental performance.

The most defining structural difference between high-level water collection cooling towers and their conventional counterparts is the complete elimination of the rain zone. After thermal exchange in the tower packing, circulating water is directly intercepted by a Water Collection Trough (WCT) suspended immediately beneath the tower packing. This structural modification fundamentally alters the thermal-hydraulic resistance characteristics of the entire tower4,5, and has driven extensive research into the optimization of the water collection system – the most distinctive component of this tower type6.

Existing research on high-level water collection systems has focused primarily on three key directions: flow resistance optimization, thermal performance tuning, and splash prevention. For flow resistance characteristics, Long G. et al.7 conducted wind tunnel tests on typical U-shaped WCTs, identifying the flow channel inlets between adjacent collectors and the upper space of WCTs as the dominant sources of air resistance. Lv D. et al.8 further investigated the internal flow field of WCTs, and quantified the influence of key design parameters on flow resistance. For thermal performance optimization, Wang X9. found that the tower outlet water temperature exhibits a non-monotonic trend with inclined water collection plate angles between 30° and 60°, while Wu Y10. determined the optimal inclined plate angle to be 50° via comparative tests of 40°, 45°, and 50° designs. For splash prevention, Tian S. et al.11 developed 20 optimized baffle schemes and validated their performance via numerical simulation, while Gong X. et al.12 screened novel splash-proof materials and recommended designs with a splashing rate below 6 per 100,000.

Beyond the above investigations on the hydraulic and thermal performance of WCTs, extensive wind tunnel tests have been conducted by researchers worldwide to characterize the wind-resistant performance of hyperbolic cooling towers, with typical engineering prototypes including the cooling tower of Ferrybridge Thermal Power Station in the UK13 and the cooling tower of Hancheng Power Plant in Shaanxi, China. These studies have obtained the distribution laws of mean and fluctuating wind pressure on the inner and outer surfaces of cooling towers along the height and circumferential (latitudinal) directions, and clarified the action mechanism of key influencing factors including terrain roughness, structural geometry, Reynolds number, and surface roughness14–17.

As detailed above, the wind-resistant performance of the main shell of hyperbolic cooling towers has been extensively studied and a mature research system has been established. However, the wind-induced structural safety of WCTs, the core functional component of high-level water collection cooling towers, has not received corresponding attention in existing literature. In engineering practice, WCTs are installed via hoisting, which makes them prone to operational failures such as water leakage at plate-support connections and structural defects of the trough body. Such failures can be effectively prevented and controlled through strict construction quality control18,19. Nevertheless, for power plants located in regions with frequent high-wind events, the hoisted WCTs face significant structural safety risks under extreme ambient wind loads. Once wind-induced damage occurs to WCTs, the repair work is not only complicated and time-consuming, but also directly threatens the safe and stable operation of the power plant. Critically, there is still a notable research gap in the existing literature regarding the targeted research on the wind-induced structural safety of WCTs.

To address this research gap, the present study has two primary objectives: (1) to investigate the wind pressure distribution on Water Collection Troughs (WCTs) at extreme wind speeds and varying wind directions, and (2) to quantify the wind-induced loads and assess the effects of windbreak partition walls on the structural loading state of WCTs. To achieve these goals, we developed a geometrically scaled model of a high-level water collection cooling tower and conducted systematic wind tunnel tests to measure the internal and bottom pressure distributions of the WCTs. We calculated pressure difference coefficients across test conditions, and used these results to analyse the stress state of WCTs in the full-scale prototype tower. The findings of this study provide a critical technical reference for the safety design of WCTs under extreme high-wind conditions.

Methods

Model design and fabrication

All wind tunnel tests in this study were designed and conducted in strict compliance with international wind engineering conventions, to ensure the reliability and repeatability of test results. To meet the mandatory requirement of wind tunnel testing that the model blockage ratio must not exceed 5%20, a geometrically scaled model of the high-level water collection cooling tower was designed and fabricated at a scale of 1:363 (blockage ratio = 5%). The 1:363 scaled experimental model was fully fabricated from 304 stainless steel via high-precision machining, to ensure geometric consistency with the full-scale prototype and minimize measurement errors.

The WCT system, the core component of the model, comprises 148 groups of U-shaped WCTs and matched inclined water collection plates. The top of the high-level WCTs is designed to be flush with that of the main WCT. All WCTs are installed with a consistent design inclination along the water flow direction, and their upper sections are connected to the tower’s upper crossbeam via steel tension rods. Given that the inclination of the WCTs and the tension rods have a negligible influence on the tower’s internal air flow field, their structural details were appropriately simplified in the scaled model to avoid unnecessary interference with flow field measurements.

To enable controlled single-variable tests, the windbreak partition walls were designed as two separate plates that can be assembled onto or disassembled from the central shaft via a slot-and-groove connection, as shown in Fig. 2. This design allows for a direct comparative analysis of the wind load characteristics and structural safety of WCTs with and without windbreak partition walls, which aligns with the core research objective of this study.

Layout of measuring points and wind tunnel measurement system

To systematically quantify the influence of ambient wind speed and incoming wind direction on the internal and bottom pressure distributions of the WCTs, we conducted synchronous measurements of ambient meteorological parameters, free-stream wind speed, and pressure at key structural locations of the WCTs, in strict compliance with the wind tunnel test specifications stipulated20.

Two meteorological monitoring points were installed upstream of the wind tunnel test section (near the air inlet) to capture steady ambient meteorological parameters. The dry-bulb and wet-bulb air temperatures were measured using a mechanical ventilation psychrometer, and the local atmospheric pressure was recorded with a high-precision digital barometer. The free-stream wind speed in the test section was monitored and controlled in real time via the wind tunnel’s built-in closed-loop test control and data acquisition system.

Pressure signals on the target surfaces of the WCTs were acquired via flush-mounted pressure taps with an inner diameter of 1.2 mm, which were connected to a 9816/98RK-1 multi-channel pressure scanning system for synchronous data acquisition. The layout of the pressure measuring points and the adopted wind direction definition convention are illustrated in Fig. 3. All pressure measuring points were uniformly arranged along the radial line at an azimuth angle of 45°, among which points P1 to P6 were arranged to measure the internal pressure of the WCTs, and points P7 to P12 were set to monitor the bottom pressure of the WCTs.

Layout of pressure measuring points on the WCTs and adopted wind direction definition convention.

All wind tunnel tests were conducted in a closed-circuit low-speed environmental wind tunnel, which comprises sequentially arranged key sections: an air inlet section, power fan section, wide-angle diffuser, flow settling chamber with honeycomb and screens, contraction cone, closed test section, and outlet diffuser. The test section has a rectangular cross-section of 2 m × 2 m and an effective working length of 11 m. The aerodynamic performance of the test section meets the technical requirements of the cooling tower wind tunnel test, with the following key indicators: flow stability ≤ 1%, streamwise velocity uniformity ≤ 1%, axial static pressure gradient ≤ 0.01 m⁻¹, free-stream turbulence intensity ≤ 1%, and flow deflection angle < 1°.

Uncertainty propagation

Experimental errors are generally divided into three groups: calibration, data reduction and data acquisition errors, with various sub-types included in each group. The measuring instruments used in the tests have factory-specified performance indicators, namely resolution, accuracy, linearity, repeatability and drift, which lead to uncertainties during calibration and data collection. In this study, uncertainty evaluation was carried out via the root-sum-square (RSS) method. Following the method described in Ref.22, this technique combines systematic bias (B) and random precision error (P) to determine the total uncertainty of each measured parameter, and then conducts uncertainty propagation for the pressure difference coefficient (ζi).

The primary measured parameters in the test include pressure, ambient air temperature, and wind speed in the test section. The corresponding measurement uncertainties are ± 1 Pa for pressure, ± 0.2 K for ambient temperature, and ± 0.1 m/s for wind speed. The main purpose of the test is to determine the pressure difference coefficient at each measuring point. where: Δpi is the measurement uncertainty of pressure at the i-th measuring point, Pa; Δpref is the measurement uncertainty of pressure at reference point, Pa; Δvw is the measurement uncertainty of velocity, m·s−1; Δpa is the measurement uncertainty of atmospheric pressure, Pa; ΔTa is the measurement uncertainty of ambient temperature, K.

It can be seen from Eq. (3) that the experimental error of the pressure difference coefficient mainly stems from four sources: pressure measurement error, wind speed measurement error at the test section, ambient air temperature measurement error, and ambient atmospheric pressure measurement error. The first two items are the dominant error sources.

The measurement error is not only related to the inherent error of the measuring instrument, but also closely associated with the magnitude of the pressure difference. The smaller the measured pressure difference, the larger the relative experimental error of the pressure difference coefficient. Therefore, to reduce the error of experimental results, the test data obtained at a wind speed of 30 m/s in the wind tunnel test section are selected. Results

Pressure distribution characteristics of WCTs in the scaled test model

This section presents the measured pressure distribution characteristics of the WCTs under the working condition without windbreak partition walls, with the pressure difference coefficient adopted as the core evaluation index.

As shown in Fig. 5a, the pressure difference coefficients at the internal WCT measuring points (Points P1 to P6) exhibited essentially identical variation patterns with the change of incoming ambient wind direction. The circumferential distribution of the internal pressure difference coefficients was highly uniform, with a stable value of approximately − 0.46 across all wind directions. This result suggests that ambient wind directions exerts no obvious influence on the internal pressure field of the WCTs when windbreak partition walls are not installed. Figure 5b shows the variation of pressure difference coefficients at the WCT bottom measuring points (Points P7 to P12) with ambient wind direction. The coefficients at all bottom measuring points also followed a consistent variation trend with wind direction, and were affected by ambient wind direction over a wide azimuth range of approximately 195°. Two distinct distribution patterns were observed: (1) When the incoming ambient wind direction and the target measuring points were located on the opposite sides of the main WCT, the pressure difference coefficients were relatively small, ranging from − 0.36 to −0.66; (2) When the incoming ambient wind direction and the target measuring points were located on the same side of the main WCT, the pressure difference coefficients changed noticeably, reaching a maximum value of approximately 0.45.

Distribution of pressure difference coefficients at an incoming wind speed of 30 m/s: working condition without windbreak partition walls.

To clarify the influence of windbreak partition walls on the wind pressure distribution of the WCTs, comparative tests were performed at a fixed incoming wind speed of 30 m/s, with the pressure difference coefficient still adopted as the core evaluation index. The test results are presented in Fig. 6, and the observed distribution characteristics are described in detail below.

Distribution of pressure difference coefficients at an incoming wind speed of 30 m/s: working condition with windbreak partition walls.

As shown in Fig. 6a, the pressure difference coefficients at the internal WCT measuring points (P1 to P6) followed an essentially identical variation trend with the change of incoming ambient wind direction. Two main distribution features were observed: (1) When the incoming wind direction was directly opposite the quadrant where the target measuring points were located, the pressure difference coefficients reached the peak, with a maximum value of approximately 0.53; (2) Under all other wind direction conditions, the pressure difference coefficients remained at a low level, ranging from − 0.37 to −0.51.

Spatial differences in the internal pressure distribution were also identified: the pressure difference coefficients at the middle-area measuring points (P3, P4) were higher than those at the outer-area (P1, P2) and inner-area (P5, P6) measuring points. This finding suggests that the airflow velocity in the middle area of the WCTs was clearly enhanced under the action of ambient wind and windbreak partition walls, while the airflow intensity in the inner and outer areas was relatively weak.

Figure 6b illustrates the pressure difference coefficient distribution at the WCT bottom measuring points (P7 to P12). Consistent with the internal pressure distribution, the bottom pressure difference coefficients were dominated by the incoming wind direction relative to the measuring point quadrant. When the incoming wind direction was directly opposite the measuring point quadrant, the pressure difference coefficients in the outer area were relatively low, while those in the middle and central areas increased markedly, reaching a maximum value of approximately 0.68. Under all other wind directions, the pressure difference coefficients of all bottom measuring points were highly uniform, with a stable value of approximately − 0.43.

Compared with the working condition without windbreak partition walls, the installation of windbreak partition walls led to a 51% increase in the maximum pressure difference coefficient of the WCTs (from 0.45 to 0.68). This suggests that the WCTs are subjected to substantially elevated wind-induced pressure under extreme wind conditions, which may reduce the structural safety of the WCTs and their connecting components – a core finding consistent with the research hypothesis of this study.

Windbreak partition walls constrain the lateral cross-flow path of ambient wind, forcing the flow to converge and compress along fixed quadrants, which increases the airflow velocity, forms a pressure enrichment effect in the area, and drives the rise of the peak pressure difference coefficient.

Figure 7 compares the pressure difference coefficients between the internal and bottom WCT measuring points under the working condition with windbreak partition walls, with an incoming wind speed of 30 m/s.

Pressure difference coefficient comparison of WCT internal and bottom measuring points (with windbreak partition walls).

Two main pressure distribution characteristics were observed, depending on the relative position between the incoming ambient wind direction and the target measuring points: when the incoming wind direction and the measuring points were located in different quadrants, the pressure difference coefficients between the internal and bottom of the WCTs showed a similar pattern, with no appreciable radial spatial difference; when the incoming wind direction and the measuring points were located in the same quadrant, clear radial gradient differences in pressure difference coefficients were identified: (1) For the outer-area measuring points, the pressure difference coefficients of the internal measuring points (P1, P2) were higher than those of the corresponding bottom measuring points (P7, P8); (2) For the middle-area measuring points, the pressure difference coefficients of the internal measuring points (P3, P4) were slightly lower than those of the corresponding bottom measuring points (P9, P10); (3) For the central-area measuring points, the pressure difference coefficients of the internal measuring points (P5, P6) were lower than those of the corresponding bottom measuring points (P11, P12).

Based on the characteristics of pressure difference coefficients, the observed radial pressure gradient may imply that a closed longitudinal vortex structure could potentially form in the quadrant facing the incoming ambient wind. The differential pressure distribution is regarded as the main driving factor for the development of this vortex. It is speculated that the general flow path of this vortex is as follows: airflow may rise from the bottom of the middle-area WCTs into their interior, converge toward the main inner space of the cooling tower, then flow radially outward into the interior of the outer-area WCTs, and eventually descend to the bottom of the outer-area WCTs. This pattern presumably forms a complete longitudinal vortex circulation. The above inferences are basically consistent with the numerical simulation results reported in Reference23.

Pressure difference analysis and wind load calculation for WCTs in the full-scale prototype

Based on the pressure distribution characteristics of the scaled test model obtained from wind tunnel tests, with the extrapolation to the full-scale prototype based on the assumption that the effect of Reynolds number difference is negligible within the drag square-law region (detailed in Sect. 2.1), this section extrapolates the test results to the full-scale prototype WCTs, analyzes the radial pressure difference distribution between adjacent WCTs, and calculates the wind-induced pressure on the prototype WCTs under extreme wind speeds. It should be noted that the maximum pressure values presented below refer to the single-point extreme values extracted from the measured data, to provide a quantitative basis for the structural safety design of WCTs in engineering applications.

The test results show that the maximum difference in pressure difference coefficients between the internal and bottom surfaces of the WCTs reaches approximately 0.50 (between the central internal measuring point P5 and the corresponding bottom measuring point P11), and the maximum radial difference in pressure difference coefficients between two adjacent internal measuring points is about 0.66 (between the outer-area measuring point P1 and the adjacent middle-area measuring point P2). There are approximately 4 WCTs arranged along the radial direction between two adjacent measuring points, so the pressure difference coefficient between adjacent WCTs is calculated to be no more than 0.17, which provides a control index for the differential wind load design between adjacent WCTs.

In accordance with the definition of pressure difference coefficient in Sect. 2.3, the ambient reference pressure for the full-scale prototype is set to 0 Pa in the wind load calculation. The calculation results show that the maximum values of the upward wind-induced pressure and horizontal wind-induced pressure acting on the prototype WCTs increase monotonically with the increase of incoming ambient wind speed. Specifically, at an ambient wind speed of 35 m/s, the maximum upward pressure reaches 1200 Pa and the maximum horizontal pressure reaches 500 Pa; at an extreme design wind speed of 46 m/s, the maximum upward pressure rises to 2100 Pa and the maximum horizontal pressure reaches 850 Pa.

The actual structural response of the WCTs under the above wind-induced pressure loads is determined by the specific structural form of the WCTs and the mechanical properties of their connecting components. The test results and quantitative wind load calculation data obtained in this study can provide a basis for the subsequent structural safety assessment, connection design, and anti-wind optimization of the WCTs in high-level water collection cooling towers.

Limitations of scaling effect in full-scale wind load extrapolation

Although the flow around the cooling tower model in this test has entered the quadratic drag region (self-similar region), fully satisfying the Reynolds number self-similarity requirements for wind tunnel tests and effectively minimizing the influence of Reynolds number variations on the overall flow characteristics, certain minor inherent factors associated with scale effects remain when extrapolating the wind pressure distribution results of the scale model measured in the wind tunnel test to the full-scale prototype WCTs. The relevant details are described below:

Incomplete equivalence in geometric similarity may introduce minor deviations in the local flow field and corresponding extrapolation uncertainties. Restricted by the processing technology and dimensional accuracy of the scale model, the detailed structural features of the prototype (such as tower surface roughness) cannot be fully replicated via strict isometric scaling. Such differences in detailed structures may slightly modify the local flow field distribution around the WCTs, which may in turn lead to minor deviations between the measured local wind pressure results and the full-scale flow characteristics.

Deviations in atmospheric boundary layer simulation may introduce minor inherent systematic deviations in the full-scale extrapolation process. The mean wind profile, turbulence intensity and turbulence integral scale simulated in the wind tunnel are simplified approximations of the actual atmospheric boundary layer, and cannot fully reproduce the unsteady complex turbulence characteristics of the natural wind field at the actual project site. This may lead to systematic differences in wind load excitation and structural response of the WCTs between the scale model and the full-scale prototype, which is a common and inherently unavoidable feature of wind tunnel tests for building structures, with a limited impact on the overall results for preliminary engineering analysis.

In consideration of the above minor inherent factors, the full-scale wind load results obtained by extrapolation in this study are fully valid and reliable for the preliminary design, safety check, and wind-resistant optimization of WCTs in engineering practice, providing a solid technical basis for preliminary engineering analysis. For the refined structural design of full-scale WCTs and specialized safety assessment under extreme working conditions, it is recommended that subsequent studies further optimize and validate the extrapolation results in conjunction with field measurement data from full-scale cooling towers.

Conclusion

To address the critical research gap of wind-induced structural safety of WCTs in high-level water collection cooling towers under extreme gale conditions, this study conducted systematic environmental wind tunnel tests on a 1:363 geometrically scaled cooling tower model, and investigated the pressure distribution characteristics inside and at the bottom of the WCTs across a range of incoming wind directions. Based on the test results, we calculated the pressure difference coefficients, revealed the underlying flow field mechanism, and extrapolated the wind-induced pressures on the full-scale prototype WCTs. The main conclusions are as follows:

For the working condition without windbreak partition walls, the incoming ambient wind direction exerts a negligible influence on the pressure difference coefficients at the WCT bottom, but exhibits minimal impact on the internal pressure difference coefficients of the WCTs. The pressure difference coefficients at the windward measuring points reach a maximum value of approximately 0.45.

For the working condition without windbreak partition walls, the incoming ambient wind direction exerts a negligible influence on the pressure difference coefficients at the WCT bottom, but exhibits minimal impact on the internal pressure difference coefficients of the WCTs. The pressure difference coefficients at the windward measuring points reach a maximum value of approximately 0.45.

For the working condition with windbreak partition walls, both the internal and bottom pressure difference coefficients of the WCTs are dependent on the incoming wind direction. The maximum pressure difference coefficient at the windward measuring points rises to approximately 0.68 (a 51% increase compared with the no-partition condition), which undermine the structural integrity of the WCTs and their connections.

For the working condition with windbreak partition walls, both the internal and bottom pressure difference coefficients of the WCTs are dependent on the incoming wind direction. The maximum pressure difference coefficient at the windward measuring points rises to approximately 0.68 (a 51% increase compared with the no-partition condition), which undermine the structural integrity of the WCTs and their connections.

Under the action of ambient wind with windbreak partition walls installed, a closed longitudinal vortex structure may exist in the quadrant directly facing the incoming wind direction. It is inferred that the complete flow path of this vortex is: the flow rises from the bottom of the middle-area WCTs into the interior of the middle-area WCTs, converges into the main internal space of the cooling tower, flows radially outward into the interior of the outer-area WCTs, and finally descends to the bottom of the outer-area WCTs.

The maximum upward and horizontal wind-induced pressures acting on the full-scale prototype WCTs increase steadily with the rise of ambient wind speed. At an ambient wind speed of 35 m/s, the maximum upward pressure reaches 1200 Pa and the maximum horizontal pressure reaches 500 Pa; at the extreme design wind speed of 46 m/s, the maximum upward and horizontal pressures rise to 2100 Pa and 850 Pa, respectively.

The installation of windbreak partition walls increases the wind pressure on water collection troughs. Therefore, the use of windbreak partition walls is not recommended in typhoon-prone or strong wind regions. Therefore, the structural integrity of WCTs, tie rods, and connections should be rigorously verified during the design stage to prevent potential failure under extreme winds.
