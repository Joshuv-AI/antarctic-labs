An Age‐Friendly Approach to Caring for Chronic Lung Disease in the Ambulatory Setting
Authors: Angela O Suen, Lauren R Pollack, C Adrian Austin, Leah J Witt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMany common chronic lung diseases, such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and interstitial lung disease (ILD), have much greater prevalence among older adults and as a result, pose a substantial burden of morbidity, disability, and mortality. The care of older adults with chronic lung disease necessitates an age‐friendly approach due to marked differences in physiology, clinical presentation, disease trajectories, and healthcare priorities as compared to younger adults. Pulmonary care models have historically prioritised disease‐focused approaches over person‐centered care. Disease‐focused care fails to adequately address geriatric syndromes that complicate the management of chronic disease, such as multicomplexity, functional impairment, cognitive dysfunction, and psychosocial complexity. In this narrative review, we propose a deliberate, geriatrics‐informed approach to ambulatory pulmonary care using the Age‐Friendly 5Ms framework—What Matters, Medications, Mentation, Mobility, and Multicomplexity. This framework is an easy‐to‐use reference for chronic disease management and offers a practical roadmap to support clinical decision‐making, inform health system improvement, and guide future research focused on outcomes that matter most to older adults living with chronic lung disease. We review how the 5 M framework helps to align care with patients' goals and values, minimize medication‐related harms, address cognitive, psychological, and caregiving needs, preserve mobility and functional independence, and manage the intersecting medical and social challenges common in later life.Keywords: chronic lung disease, COPD, geriatrics, IPF, multicomplexity, older adult, oxygen

Case Vignette

Ms. Jones is an 81 year‐old woman with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) presenting with her daughter to pulmonary clinic for an initial visit after a recent hospitalisation for COPD exacerbation. She also has a history of insulin‐dependent diabetes, coronary artery disease, osteoarthritis, and glaucoma. Over the past year, she has had two hospitalisations for COPD exacerbations. Pulmonary function testing demonstrates an FEV1 of 0.9 L (51% predicted) with an FEV1/FVC ratio of 0.6. At rest, her oxygen saturation is 90% on room air, and she was recently prescribed long‐term supplemental oxygen for use with exertion, after desaturating to 86% on room air while walking. She finds it challenging to walk around the house with the oxygen tubing and has been leaving her home less since hospital discharge. She reports severe dyspnea, with a modified Medical Research Council (mMRC) dyspnea score of 4. Ms. Jones also reports an unintentional 10 kg weight loss over the last year as well as frequent, loose stools. Her medication regimen includes a twice daily inhaled corticosteroid (ICS) combined with a long‐acting beta agonist (LABA) Diskus inhaler, a once daily long acting muscarinic agent (LAMA) Handihaler. She is additionally prescribed an albuterol hydrofluoroalkane (HFA) inhaler to be used four times daily as needed, ipratropium/albuterol nebulizer solution to be used four times daily as needed, and daily oral roflumilast. She lives in a third‐floor walk‐up apartment, ambulates with a cane, and has a 40–pack‐year smoking history, having quit 1 year ago. Her daughter shared concerns that her mother does not remember to take all her medications and has been avoiding social activities for the past few months.

The Case for a Deliberate Approach to Caring for Older Adults With Chronic Lung Disease

Older adults with chronic lung disease face distinct challenges that are inadequately addressed by disease‐centred pulmonary care. Compared with younger adults, older adults differ substantially in physiology, clinical presentation, disease trajectories, and treatment priorities [1, 2]. Among adults aged 65 years and older, respiratory diseases, including chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), are a leading cause of disability and the third most common cause of death worldwide [3]. As the population ages and age‐associated respiratory diseases such as COPD and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) become increasingly prevalent, pulmonary clinicians must develop geriatric expertise to deliver high‐quality care.

There is no universally accepted definition of ‘older adult’, and chronologic age alone is an imprecise marker of health status, vulnerability, or care needs. For the purposes of this manuscript, we do not use a specific age to define older adults because chronologic age does not necessarily reflect physiologic age. Contributors to advanced physiologic age include an individual's functional status, comorbidity burden, cognitive changes, and physiologic reserve. As a result, physiologic age may differ substantially from chronologic age. The natural history of lung aging is characterised by gradual reductions in elastic recoil, chest wall compliance, gas exchange, and immune defence [4, 5, 6]. However, many pulmonary diseases appear to represent an acceleration of these aging processes, driven by factors such as cellular senescence [7], systemic inflammation, cumulative exposures, and recurrent exacerbations that hasten loss of lung reserve. As a result, some younger or middle‐aged adults with advanced lung disease may exhibit vulnerabilities typically associated with older age. Individuals with reduced physiologic reserve, frailty, functional impairment, cognitive vulnerability, or complex caregiving needs may therefore benefit from geriatrics‐informed care regardless of chronologic age. This approach prioritizes vulnerability and complexity rather than age thresholds and supports broader application of geriatrics principles in pulmonary medicine.

While geriatrics‐focused care frameworks are endorsed in subspecialties such as oncology [8, 9], cardiology [10], and emergency medicine [11], no such guidance currently exists in pulmonary medicine. Traditional care models fail to account for how common geriatric conditions like cognitive dysfunction and frailty may affect diagnostic accuracy, treatment tolerance, and patient‐centred outcomes prioritised by older adults. For example, older adults with cognitive impairment may be unable to follow the detailed and complex instructions required to ensure adequate spirometry quality during pulmonary function testing. Moreover, treatment plans often neglect to evaluate whether older adults can effectively use devices like inhalers or adhere to complex treatment regimens [12, 13].

Geriatrics frameworks are also critical to safeguard against the risks of under‐ or over‐diagnosis. Traditional cut points for spirometry impairment fail to account for natural lung function decline with age and can lead to overdiagnosis of COPD (e.g., use of percentage predicted (% pred)) values to grade severity of lung function impairment or use of absolute ratio cut‐offs (i.e., FEV1/FVC ratio ≤ 0.7 for COPD) [4]. Underdiagnosis is also a risk for older adults, as clinicians may approach them with implicit biases of ageism, assuming that they are ‘too old’ for appropriate diagnostic or screening testing. Clinical tools (e.g., the COPD Assessment Test and pulmonary function tests) validated in younger, healthier populations may lead to under‐ or over‐diagnosis of chronic lung disease, with resultant inappropriate medication use and potential harm. Psychological and social dimensions of chronic illness, such as increased rates of depression, anxiety, isolation, and caregiver dependency, are also frequently overlooked [14, 15, 16].

To address these needs, pulmonary clinicians must develop an approach to integrate geriatric principles into practice, recognize the limits of diagnostic testing in the older adult, examine their own implicit biases, and identify tactics to work in the necessary multidisciplinary teams that support excellent comprehensive care for older adults. Ultimately, given the unique needs of older adults, a novel care model tailored to the needs of this population is necessary. A geriatrics‐informed pulmonary care model would provide a systematic approach for both clinicians and health systems to deliver high‐quality care that aligns with patient goals and preferences and reduce harm and safety risks in this growing patient population.

Age‐Friendly Healthcare Systems and the 5Ms

In the early 2000s, the Institute for Healthcare Improvement (IHI) and the John A. Hartford Foundation created the 4Ms Framework as part of the Age‐Friendly Health System to integrate elements of high‐quality care specifically for older adults. The Age‐Friendly Health System and the 4Ms Framework have been broadly adopted in hundreds of healthcare systems across the United States and internationally. The evidence‐based 4Ms Framework—What Matters, Medications, Mentation/Mood, and Mobility/Frailty—is associated with improved rates of follow up in ambulatory settings, higher satisfaction and quality of life metrics, and improved mobility [17]. What Matters reflects the importance of aligning care with the goals and care preferences of older adults across settings of care. Medications considers how pharmacologic therapies may impact the other components of this framework, such as mobility, mood, or care values (what matters). Mentation and Mood assess for cognitive impairment, mood disorders such as anxiety and depression, and tailoring diagnostic and treatment plans accordingly. In Mobility/Frailty, clinicians evaluate how older adults move in their home and community, and identify tactics to match their abilities to their environment [18]. A ‘5th M’, Multicomplexity, was subsequently proposed by the geriatrician Mary Tinnetti, MD, and represents the compounding effect of multiple comorbidities and psychosocial challenges in the care of chronic diseases and health outcomes.

Multiple medical specialties have embraced the 4 or 5 M approach to age‐friendly care, and many have demonstrated improved outcomes, showing that specialties can tailor and modify the 5Ms framework to meet the unique needs of their patient population. For example, many orthopaedic surgery services around the country have adopted a geriatric co‐management model for older adults hospitalised for a hip fracture [19]. Geriatric consultants help to determine physiologic reserve to determine if patients should receive surgical or medical management, help prevent delirium, address polypharmacy issues, and optimize mobility after surgical intervention. In cardiology, emerging models have focused on aligning polypharmacy of guideline directed management with functional goals and preventing delirium [10]. Given the success in other medical specialties, the 5Ms framework could be applied to pulmonary medicine to guide care of older adults.

The aim of this narrative review is to demonstrate how the 5Ms framework can be applied to the care of an older adult with chronic lung disease, highlighting relevant literature that examines how these domains intersect with the care of older adults with chronic lung disease. For each ‘M’, we synthesize evidence from pulmonary, geriatric, and palliative care literature to illustrate common gaps in current practice and opportunities for more person‐centred, age‐friendly care. We conclude with a practical case‐based example demonstrating how the 5Ms framework can be applied in routine pulmonary care to support clinical decision‐making, align treatments with patient priorities, and address the complex needs of older adults with advanced respiratory disease (Figure 1). What Matters

What Matters informs clinical decision‐making across all the other 5 M domains. Understanding what matters most entails eliciting patients' values, preferences, and healthcare goals, as well as how these priorities are weighed when healthcare decisions involve trade‐offs [49]. Outcomes of high importance to many older adults, such as preservation of social activities and independence, may differ from traditional disease‐centred endpoints (e.g., FEV1 or 6‐min walk distance) that are the focus of clinical practice guidelines [50]. These outcomes should be explicitly incorporated into care planning [49]. Clinicians should anticipate the implications of diagnostic and therapeutic decisions for outcomes commonly prioritised by this population, such as preservation of cognition and functional independence. Pulmonary clinicians should therefore develop an approach to eliciting what matters most to their older patients.

Fortunately, evidence‐based strategies exist to support eliciting “What Matters”. One of the most widely adopted is the Patient Priorities Care (PPC) framework. This framework was developed through a multistakeholder initiative (patients, care partners, clinicians, health system leaders, payers) focused on supporting clinical decision‐making for older adults with multiple chronic conditions [51]. The approach entails identifying patients' core values, translating those values into actionable and realistic health goals, recognizing symptoms or barriers that interfere with goal attainment, and clarifying which treatments or interventions patients are willing or unwilling to pursue [52]. The PPC Framework has overlapping features but is distinct from Serious Illness Conversations [53, 54]. The distinction is that the PPC Framework is also intended for use with older adults who would not typically be categorised as having serious illness or approaching the end of life. Examples of such decisions include whether or not to pursue invasive diagnostic testing for older adults with pulmonary nodules or asymptomatic pulmonary fibrosis, frequency of routine monitoring in patients with bronchiectasis, and whether supplemental oxygen is appropriate for patients with well‐controlled COPD and exertional hypoxemia.

Importantly, eliciting what matters most is an iterative process that evolves over time and clinical context. Patients' family members or care partners often play an important role, as they may help articulate patients' values, provide insight into daily functioning, and share in the consequences of treatment decisions. Additional tools, guides, and implementation resources are available through the Institute for Healthcare Improvement's Age‐Friendly Health Systems initiative to support clinicians in integrating these conversations into routine subspecialty care [55].

What Matters is especially important for older adults who have life‐limiting chronic pulmonary diseases. Such patients may favor a palliative approach to care, especially near the end of life. Pulmonologists should, therefore, have the basic skills and resources to deliver primary palliative care. Such necessary skills include assessing patients' illness understanding, delivering information about prognoses, exploring physical and psychosocial suffering, assessing caregiver needs, coordinating care within an interdisciplinary team, prescribing comfort‐oriented treatments such as opioids for breathlessness, creating advance care planning, and discussing hospice [56].

Numerous studies have shown that palliative care is under‐utilised in chronic lung disease management despite it being associated with important patient‐centred outcomes [56]. In particular, a small body of literature supports that older adults with COPD or IPF and their families prefer palliative care integration before end‐stage disease develops, and concurrent with disease‐directed therapies [21, 57, 58]. Suggested triggers for consideration of referral to specialty palliative care were reviewed in a 2022 ATS/AAHPM/HPNA/SWHPN policy statement and include FEV1 or FVC < 30%, refractory breathlessness, extreme cachexia, and intensive care unit admission [56]. Although commonly used disease‐specific prognostic models (e.g., BODE, ADO, and GAP indices) [46, 59, 60] can inform patient risk stratification, prognostication in chronic lung disease remains challenging. Chronic lung diseases, in particular COPD and bronchiectasis, often have unpredictable trajectories and lack a distinct terminal phase [61, 62]. Existing models focus primarily on mortality and do not predict functional trajectory or quality of life, outcomes that may matter most to some older adults [63]. In addition, many disease‐specific indices, such as the BODE index for COPD survival, were derived from studies that excluded patients with multimorbidity or did not assess frailty, despite evidence that frailty is a stronger predictor of poor outcomes in older adults than chronological age [64, 65, 66]. Further research is needed to develop prognostic approaches that account for multimorbidity and frailty and predict outcomes of highest importance to older adults to inform healthcare decision‐making.

While there are no large randomised controlled trials of palliative care interventions for patients with COPD or IPF, several care models have been studied and were associated with improved outcomes. Specialist palliative care involvement for patients with COPD has been shown to be associated with fewer hospital deaths and less frequent use of high‐intensity care near end‐of‐life [46, 59, 60]. For patients with IPF, one promising model is the Multidisciplinary Collaborative care model, which entails a focus on early symptom management, advance care planning, and referrals to community‐based care to manage patients in their home and support caregivers [25]. The Multidisciplinary Collaborative care model was shown to be associated with reduced health care use in the last year of life and more home (relative to in‐hospital) deaths [25].

Medications

Older adults have unique physiologic and logistical considerations when formulating treatment plans, such as medication side effects, polypharmacy, and proper medication techniques, particularly with inhaler therapies. Older adults may also be especially impacted by burdens of complex treatments, such as supplemental oxygen and nebulizers [67]. Therefore, any treatments should be considered within a larger framework of treatment goals and patient preferences. Older adults may have different priorities when starting treatment, beyond extension of life. They may prioritise quality of life, minimisation of treatment side effects, symptom reduction, and independence as highly as mortality [68]. Therefore, conversations to start or withhold treatment should be individualised with careful consideration given to the benefits and potential downsides of a treatment regimen. This is discussed in greater detail in the What Matters section.

Changes in drug metabolism occur with older age and with common comorbidities such as chronic kidney or liver disease. For these reasons, older adults are at higher risk for significant side effects and drug–drug interactions than younger adults. For example, older adults may experience more side effects from oral steroids, including hyperglycemia, elevated blood pressure, edema, and cognitive/mood disturbances. These effects can worsen underlying diabetes, hypertension, and heart failure as well as increase risk for delirium [69]. Antifibrotics (i.e., pirfenidone and nintedanib) used to slow progression of IPF or progressive pulmonary fibrosis have higher rates of adverse effects and resultant dose reduction or discontinuation in older adults and those with multiple comorbidities [30, 70]. When initiating a medication with potential adverse effects, caution should be paid to prescribe the lowest possible effective dose and escalate the dose with caution and monitoring. Conversely, it is equally important that older adults achieve an effective dose of treatment, because this population is also at risk for ageism causing inappropriate undertreatment.

Nearly half of all older adults experience polypharmacy, defined variably but most commonly as taking five or more medications [71]. Older adults with COPD and IPF may be at higher risk for experiencing polypharmacy [72]. A Canadian study found that 62% of all older adults with COPD were prescribed 10 or more medications [73]. Nearly all older U.S. veterans with IPF who receive care in the Veterans Affairs experience polypharmacy [74]. The downstream consequences of polypharmacy in chronic lung disease remain unclear; although broader studies point to possible increases in mortality and healthcare utilisation, these findings are substantially limited by confounding [71, 75]. From the patient perspective, polypharmacy is associated with substantial out‐of‐pocket costs, logistical burden, and clinically significant drug–drug interactions, collectively contributing to potential iatrogenic harm [27, 76].

Proper inhaler technique in moderate or severe lung disease poses unique challenges for many older adults, particularly those with cognitive impairment, arthritis, or severe respiratory disease. Different inhaler devices require unique inhalational techniques. This is often overlooked during prescribing education. For example, dry powder inhalers (DPI) rely on the patient's inspiratory effort to disperse the powder into respirable particles and require rapid, forceful inspiration. Conversely, Respimat inhalers deliver the medication through built‐in mechanical energy that requires less patient effort but need to be inhaled with a slow, deep inspiration. Improper technique leads to ineffective medication delivery and efficacy. Those with moderate or severe lung disease may not have the expiratory or inspiratory force to effectively deliver the medications to the lungs [12, 13]. Nebulised therapies may be easier to use as they require little inspiratory effort, although they are logistically complex and may be more costly [29, 77]. Older adults with cognitive impairment may not be able to remember the different inhaler techniques required for 2–3 inhalers. In the United States, combination inhaler branding and always evolving insurance coverage have created a potpourri of inhaler names that confuse prescribers and patients alike. Pulmonary clinicians should regularly review medication lists, particularly inhaler names, with education on proper technique [28] and adherence as part of routine pulmonary care.

Supplemental oxygen can be especially burdensome for older adults and warrants careful assessment of the patient's goals, their physical capacities, availability of caregiver help, and the home environment [67]. Patients report difficulty lugging heavy oxygen tanks outside of the home, and portable oxygen concentrators can incur significant out of pocket costs. Patients fear tripping over the oxygen cannula tubing. Oxygen equipment can reduce mobility, independence, and worsen social isolation and perceptions of health [14, 78, 79, 80]. Clinicians should take an individualised approach to prescribing supplemental oxygen in instances where evidence for mortality (i.e., supplemental oxygen for severe exertional hypoxia or moderate resting hypoxia) or symptomatic benefit is limited [81, 82, 83]. Future studies are needed to determine which patients are most likely to benefit or experience harm from oxygen therapy. Furthermore, existing evidence, largely derived from COPD populations, may not be directly applicable to those with IPF, warranting disease‐specific investigation. Careful assessment of the home environment, potential trip hazards, and the patient's ability to use the therapy are needed to minimize implications on quality of life, mobility, and independence; health outcomes that many older adults prioritize highly [67].

Mentation/Mood/Caregiving

The fourth M—Mentation/Mood—addresses cognition, mental health, and the need for social support or caregiving, all of which become increasingly relevant with aging. In chronic lung diseases, these concerns are often amplified. Patients with chronic lung disease are at higher risk for cognitive impairment, anxiety, depression, and growing dependence in activities of daily living [1, 84]. Recognising and assessing these conditions is essential for pulmonary clinicians, as impairments in cognition or mood or caregiving needs directly influence treatment adherence and the feasibility of self‐management strategies.

Psychological distress, such as anxiety and depression, and poor social well‐being are highly prevalent in older adults with chronic lung disease, especially in advanced stages of disease. Anxiety and/or depression can heighten physical symptoms such as dyspnea in COPD, which is strongly linked with anxiety and/or depression. Dyspnea is also strongly associated with increasing mortality, acute exacerbations, and healthcare use [15, 85, 86]. The bi‐directional relationship between symptoms and anxiety/depression highlights the importance of addressing psychological distress as part of routine symptom management. Currently, treatment strategies are largely focused on non‐pharmacological management, including nursing‐led cognitive behavioural therapy [36], mindfulness and relaxation based interventions, mind–body exercise (such as tai chi or yoga), and pulmonary rehabilitation where patients can participate in support groups or learn self‐management strategies [87]. The role of pharmacologic treatment for psychological distress remains poorly defined.

Social isolation and loneliness are highly prevalent and important in this population as well. They are related but distinct entities. Social isolation refers to an individual's lack of social connections, whereas loneliness describes the psychological distress experienced from a perceived lack of social connection. Social connections with friends, family, and acquaintances in volunteering or religious activities can naturally decrease with age, but play an important role in caregiving and social support. Suen and colleagues found that older adults with COPD were twice as likely to experience social isolation and loneliness compared to those without COPD, and social isolation in COPD is associated with higher mortality [14, 88]. The clinical implication of social isolation may mean older adults may not have the social or caregiving support to implement complex medication regimens or attend healthcare visits. Clinicians should identify social isolation and identify available additional community and family resources, such as transportation options or identifying backup care plans during sudden illness.

Cognitive and functional impairment, or inability to perform activities of daily living, can impair a patient's ability to implement complex management plans. COPD is a risk factor for cognitive impairment, which can range from mild cognitive impairment to dementia. Witt and colleagues found that nearly 1 in 3 older adults with COPD had at least one impairment in activity in daily living (ADL, i.e., eating, dressing, walking, and toileting) [1]. Those with cognitive and/or functional impairment may have increased caregiver needs in order to complete daily routines and adhere to medical treatment plans. It is therefore critically important that clinicians collaborate with caregivers who will have to implement care plans. In addition to identifying and tailoring medical management around cognitive and functional impairments, clinicians should be aware that caregivers of seriously ill older adults suffer as well [89]. Caregiving affects employment, relationships, and the health and well‐being of caregivers. Clinicians should be aware and respectful of these challenges, offering caregiver resources such as social work referrals and resources such as copdfoundation.org and caregiver.org.

Mobility and Frailty

Mobility impairment and loss of independence (defined by impairments in activities of daily living) is common with increasing age and morbidity [90]. Older adults consistently rank maintaining mobility and function as critical for their wellbeing [90]. It is therefore critical to identify older adults with lung disease who have existing mobility impairments, or are at high risk of developing them, to inform clinical decision‐making such as use of home oxygen equipment, hospital versus home‐based management of exacerbations, and risks of corticosteroid use which contributes to sarcopenia.

Frailty is an important mobility‐adjacent geriatric syndrome and is defined as physiological vulnerability with reduced resilience to stressors across multiple organ systems [91]. Multicomplexity is considered an etiologic risk factor for and disability an outcome of frailty [91]. There are multiple validated assessment tools for frailty, and no single tool has been demonstrated to be superior when used in clinical practice. Common tools include the Clinical Frailty Scale, the FRAIL scale, the Deficit Accumulation Index, and the Physical Frailty Phenotype [91, 92, 93, 94]. Studies have shown that frailty is common in older adults with COPD as well as those with IPF, and is associated with restrictions in recreational activities, greater symptom burden, and higher short‐term mortality [39, 40, 43, 44]. Since 2023, the GOLD COPD Guidelines have explicitly addressed frailty, noting that it is associated with greater risk of poor outcomes and that patients with COPD identified as frail may benefit from physical rehabilitation, nutritional counselling, and psychological therapies [95].

Pulmonary rehabilitation is an effective and underutilised treatment for older adults with COPD that can dramatically benefit those older adults with COPD and concurrent frailty [41, 42, 96]. In one study, older adults with COPD and frailty participating in pulmonary rehabilitation showed improvements in mMRC dyspnea scores, exercise performance, physical activity levels, and overall health status [42]. Remarkably, just over half of previously frail participants no longer met criteria for frailty after completing the program [42]. Pulmonary rehabilitation has also been shown to be beneficial for patients with chronic lung diseases other than COPD, including IPF, bronchiectasis, and asthma [97, 98, 99]. Emerging evidence supports a similar benefit of home‐based and virtual pulmonary rehabilitation programs compared to centre‐based programs, which may benefit older adults who are homebound or who face other access barriers [100, 101]. Lastly, while physical rehabilitation programs are traditionally delivered as disease‐specific services, older adults with both pulmonary and cardiac comorbidities may benefit from personalised rehabilitation that meets their needs irrespective of their cardiovascular or pulmonary index diagnosis [102]. Relative to disease‐specific rehabilitation programs, there is little existing research focused on models of personalised ‘multimorbidity’ rehabilitation, and this is an important direction for future work.

Multicomplexity

The term ‘multicomplexity’ describes the reality that many older adults with chronic lung disease live with multiple coexisting medical conditions, geriatric syndromes, mood disorders, and social challenges that influence their health outcomes and the feasibility of implementing guideline‐concordant care [103]. Older adults with COPD have a greater burden of multimorbidity relative to those without COPD, and in particular are more likely to be diagnosed with comorbid heart failure, myocardial infarction, cerebral vascular disease, and arthritis [1]. The clinical complexity resulting from multiple chronic conditions, geriatric syndromes, and psychosocial challenges can lead to competing risks and conflicts among disease‐specific best practices. For example, comorbid cognitive impairment may limit a patient's ability to use inhalers with proper technique. Systemic corticosteroids prescribed for COPD exacerbations can lead to fluid retention, hypertension, and worsened glycemic control, potentially precipitating heart failure decompensation or diabetic ketoacidosis. The comorbid presence of cardiovascular disease among those with COPD is associated with a worse prognosis in the aftermath of an acute COPD exacerbation, with increased short‐term mortality even following moderate exacerbations [104]. Addressing multicomplexity requires specialists to move beyond organ‐based care toward an integrated, person‐centered approach, supported by interdisciplinary collaboration with case managers, pharmacists, social workers, and other team members to align treatment plans with patients' individual needs, resources, and circumstances. We did not identify any studies in the primary literature describing models of care to address multicomplexity specifically among older adults with chronic lung disease. This is an area in which further research is urgently needed.

Gaps in the Evidence and Priorities for Future Research

Although there is emerging literature to support integration of geriatric principles into pulmonary medicine and in the management of chronic lung disease, we have identified several evidence gaps which need to be addressed. First, intervention trials should include older adults with multi complexity, polypharmacy, and frailty. Trials should identify and study clinical outcomes that matter most to older adults beyond mortality and spirometry measures, such as functional independence, symptom burden, and time at home (or hospital‐ or nursing home‐free days). Second, many of the diagnostic, prediction, and assessment models and tools created often exclude older adults or those with multicomplexity. Even when older adults are included, prediction scores using age cut‐offs likely miss the nuances of physiologic age or frailty. Use of frailty or multimorbidity may better indicate risk for poor outcomes than numeric age alone. Therefore, new or refined models are needed that include these complexities. Third, we advocate for more research into care transitions to reduce harm during vulnerable timepoints, such as the transition home after a COPD exacerbation. Research should expand beyond interventions for patients, but also for caregivers as well. Finally, development and testing of practical tools to aid practitioners in implementing individualised 5Ms assessments in pulmonary medicine remains an important direction for future research and quality improvement.

A 5Ms‐Informed Approach to Caring for Ms. Jones

Using the Geriatrics 5Ms framework, we began by clarifying and prioritising what matters most to Ms. Jones. She prioritised independence and the ability to leave her home. She identified her symptoms of shortness of breath and diarrhoea as barriers to these goals, in addition to the challenge of mobilising with oxygen equipment. Her priorities guided decisions about medications, oxygen use, pulmonary rehabilitation, and future care planning.

We assessed mentation and mood. Her daughter reported difficulties with medication adherence and recall, raising concern for cognitive impairment or depression contributing to poor self‐management and recurrent exacerbations. Ms. Jones described mobility as a major challenge due to breathlessness, balance impairment, and difficulty managing oxygen tubing. These factors were limiting her ability to leave her home and possibly contributing to social isolation and loneliness. We carefully reviewed her medications and her inhaler technique was observed by a respiratory therapist, who raised concern that she was not generating enough inspiratory force for optimal drug delivery when using the Diskus inhaler. We discussed how her overall multi‐complexity implied the need for an interdisciplinary approach to her care.

Based on these assessments and identified priorities, our management plan included the following: (1) discussion of the risks and benefits of home oxygen, presenting discontinuation of home oxygen therapy as an option, (2) referrals for home‐based or virtual pulmonary rehabilitation, fall risk assessment and mitigation, and mental health assessment, (3) discontinuation of her Diskus inhaler in favour of a soft mist inhaler, (4) discontinuation of roflumilast given concern that it was contributing to her weight loss and diarrhoea, (5) review and discussion of palliative treatment options, including opioids, for management of breathlessness, and finally (6) discussion of her preferences surrounding life‐sustaining treatments such as mechanical ventilation in the event of future more severe exacerbations.

Conclusion

The 5Ms framework provides a structured approach to addressing the unique and often complex needs of older adults with lung disease. Pulmonary clinicians should be educated in this framework to provide goal‐aligned care through deliberate consideration of the impact of treatment decisions on what matters most. Important directions for future research include developing and testing care models that operationalize the 5Ms in ambulatory pulmonary care of older adults.
