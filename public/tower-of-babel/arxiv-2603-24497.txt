Propagation of singularities and inverse problems for the viscoacoustic wave equation
Giovanni Covi, Maarten V. de Hoop, Mikko Salo

Abstract
. We study an inverse problem for the viscoacoustic wave equation, an integro-differential model describing wave propagation in viscoacoustic media with memory in the leading order term. The medium is characterized by a spatially varying sound speed and a space-time dependent memory kernel. Assuming that waves are generated by sources supported outside the region of interest, we consider exterior measurements encoded by the source-to-solution map. To study this inverse problem, we construct solutions concentrating near fixed geodesics and establish a corresponding propagation of singularities result for the semiclassical wave front set. These results are valid without any restriction on the underlying sound speed. Then, under certain geometric conditions, we prove that the exterior data uniquely determine not just the sound speed inside the domain but also all time derivatives at zero of the memory kernel. This involves a reduction to the lens rigidity and geodesic ray transform inverse problems. As an application, we establish uniqueness for the recovery of variable parameters in the extended Maxwell model. Nous étudions un problème inverse pour l’équation des ondes viscoacoustiques, un modèle intégro-différentiel décrivant la propagation des ondes dans des milieux viscoacoustiques avec mémoire apparaissant dans le terme d’ordre principal. Le milieu est caractérisé par une vitesse du son dépendant de la variable spatiale et par un noyau de mémoire dépendant de l’espace et du temps. En supposant que les ondes sont engendrées par des sources supportées à l’extérieur de la région d’intérêt, nous considérons des mesures extérieures encodées par l’opérateur source-solution. Pour l’étude de ce problème inverse, nous construisons des solutions se concentrant au voisinage de géodésiques fixées et établissons des résultat de propagation des singularités pour le front d’onde semi-classique associé. Ces résultats sont obtenus sans hypothèse restrictive sur la vitesse du son sous-jacente. Sous certaines conditions géométriques, nous démontrons ensuite que les données extérieures déterminent de manière unique non seulement la vitesse du son à l’intérieur du domaine, mais également toutes les dérivées temporelles en zéro du noyau de mémoire. La démonstration repose sur une réduction aux problèmes inverses de rigidité des lentilles et de la transformée des rayons géodésiques. En application, nous établissons un résultat d’unicité pour la reconstruction de paramètres variables dans le modèle de Maxwell étendu.



1.
Introduction

In this article we study an inverse problem in viscoacoustics. This can be viewed as a scalar model for viscoelastic media. Our methods would likely work also for the viscoelastic equation, but for simplicity we focus our attention to the scalar case in this article.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

T>0
. The set
\Omega
represents a viscoacoustic medium within which acoustic waves originating from
\Omega_{e}:=\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega
propagate during the time interval
[0,T]
. The medium is characterized by two physical quantities: the wave speed
c
, which is independent of time, and the memory kernel
G
, which is a function of both space and time. We make the following geometric assumptions:

(A1)

The manifold

(A2)

If

(\overline{\Omega},c(x)^{-2}g_{0})
is a simple manifold;

(A3)

If

(\overline{\Omega},c(x)^{-2}g_{0})
is foliated by strictly convex hypersurfaces, in the sense of
[
29

The pressure

(1.1)

Pu:=-\frac{1}{2}\left(\partial_{t}^{2}u-\nabla\cdot(c(x)\nabla u)+\int_{0}^{t}\nabla\cdot(G(x,t-s)\nabla u(x,s))\,ds\right)=0,

whose derivation is recalled in Appendix

We want to consider waves originating from sources

is well-posed in

for all sources

. This allows us to define the source-to-solution map

\mathcal{S}:f\mapsto u|_{M^{\prime}\setminus M},

associating exterior sources to the waves they generate, as observed in the exterior of
\Omega

Are the wave speed

and the memory kernel
G

Several results for this inverse problem were proved by Romanov and collaborators, see
[
26

27
]
and references therein. The result in
[
26
]
states roughly that if the manifold
(\overline{\Omega},c(x)^{-2}g_{0})
is simple, then
c
and all derivatives
\partial_{t}^{j}G(x,0)
for
j\geq 0
are determined by the source-to-solution map
\mathcal{S}
when the sources are delta functions at points
y\in\partial\Omega
at time
t=0
. The proof is based on writing a singularity expansion for solutions generated by a delta source. From the boundary measurements of solutions one can read off the travel times of waves between boundary points (i.e. the boundary distance function) and certain integrals over geodesics involving the quantities
\partial_{t}^{j}G(x,0)
. By invoking the solution of the boundary rigidity problem and invertibility of geodesic X-ray transforms on simple manifolds, one can determine
c
and
\partial_{t}^{j}G(x,0)
in
\Omega
.

We will consider the above inverse problem in cases where the wave speed may not be simple. Then there may be caustics, which are generically present in the seismic applications that we have in mind. In the presence of caustics, the conormal structure of the solution corresponding to a delta source exploited in
[
26
]
breaks down, and in general it is not possible to read off the required information from such boundary data. We will take a more microlocal approach and employ oscillatory sources that generate Gaussian beam type solutions concentrating along a fixed null bicharacteristic. This requires a propagation result for wave-type singularities of the viscoacoustic equation. We establish such a result for the semiclassical wave front set, which is the appropriate setting for oscillatory solutions. It is interesting that the memory term, even though it contains second order derivatives, turns out not to affect the propagation of wave-type singularities. The effect of the memory term is visible only in the amplitudes, obtained via solving both transport and Volterra equations, of the Gaussian beam solutions.

Solutions of the viscoacoustic equation can also have stationary singularities which do not move (see Remark
2.3
for a discussion). However, we only use wave-type singularities in the solution of the inverse problem, since these are the singularities that travel through the unknown part of medium and collect information on the coefficients there.

1.1.
Main results

We begin with the construction of special approximate solutions to the viscoacoustic equation, called
Gaussian beam quasimodes
given as superpositions of wave packets as in
[
22
]
. We have obtained the following result:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\Omega\Subset\Omega^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\geq 2
, be open bounded sets, and assume
T>0
. Define the spacetime cylinders
M:=[0,T]\times\overline{\Omega}
and
Z:=[0,T]\times\overline{\Omega}^{\prime}
. Let
c\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega}^{\prime})
be a positive function and consider a bicharacteristic
\gamma(\sigma)=(z(\sigma),\zeta(\sigma))
,
\sigma\in[0,T]
, of the wave operator
Q
, where
z(\sigma)=(x(\sigma),\sigma)
,
x(\sigma)
is a geodesic such that
x([0,T])\subset\overline{\Omega}^{\prime}
and
x(0),x(T)\in\Omega^{\prime}\setminus\overline{\Omega}
, and
\zeta(\sigma)=(\xi(\sigma),1)
.

Then for any
N\in\mathbb{N}
there exists a quasimode
u\in C^{\infty}_{c}(Z)
of the form

u(x,t)=\sum_{l=0}^{N-1}(-ik)^{-l}\int_{0}^{T}\bigg(e^{ik\varphi(x,t,\sigma)}a^{\prime}_{l}(x,t,\sigma)+(-ik)^{-1}e^{ik\varphi(x,0,\sigma)}a^{\prime\prime}_{l}(x,t,\sigma)\bigg)d\sigma

such that

(1.2)

Pu=(-ik)(e^{ik\varphi(z,0)}a^{\prime}(z,0)-e^{ik\varphi(z,T)}a^{\prime}(z,T))+r_{N}

in
Z
, and
r_{N}\in L^{2}((0,T),L^{2}(\Omega^{\prime}))
satisfies

\lVert r_{N}\rVert_{L^{2}((0,T),L^{2}(\Omega^{\prime}))}=O(k^{-N})\text{ as $k\to\infty$}.

The functions
\varphi,a^{\prime}_{l},a^{\prime\prime}_{l}\in C^{\infty}(Z\times[0,T])
are smooth and independent of
k
, and respectively solve suitable eikonal, transport, and Volterra equations. In particular, the complex phase function
\varphi
satisfies

\varphi(z(\sigma),\sigma)=0,\qquad\nabla_{z}\varphi(z(\sigma),\sigma)=\zeta(\sigma),\qquad\partial_{z_{j}z_{k}}\varphi(z(\sigma),\sigma)=H_{jk}(\sigma)

where
H(\sigma)
solves the matrix Riccati equation (
2.11
) with
\mathrm{Im}(H(\sigma))
positive definite, and the principal amplitude
a_{0}^{\prime}
solves the transport equation

-\partial_{\sigma}(a_{0}^{\prime}(z(\sigma),\sigma))+b(z(\sigma),\sigma)a_{0}^{\prime}(z(\sigma),\sigma)=0

where
b(z,\sigma):=2Q\varphi(z,\sigma)+\frac{G(x,0)|\nabla\varphi(z,\sigma)|^{2}}{\partial_{t}\varphi(z,\sigma)}
.

The equation (
1.2
) states that
Pu
is
O(k^{-N})
away from the endpoints
z(0)
and
z(T)
. In particular
Pu
is an approximate solutions (with error
O(k^{-N})
) near the smaller cylinder
[0,T]\times\overline{\Omega}
. If there is no memory term, the
a^{\prime\prime}
terms in Theorem
1.1
are zero and
u
resembles a standard Gaussian beam quasimode for the wave equation (see e.g.
[
16
]
). However, the presence of a memory term requires us to include the
a^{\prime\prime}
amplitudes, and these are indeed obtained via solving Volterra equations. Moreover, as shown in Proposition
2.1
, if the sound speed is known to be simple, an easier
geometrical optics
construction with real phase functions is sufficient.

With the approximate solutions at hand, we can prove a propagation of singularities result for the semiclassical wave front set of solutions. This will make use of some concepts from semiclassical analysis, which we will rigorously define in Section 2 before the proof of Theorem
1.2
(see also
[
32
,
22
]
). In our case, the small semiclassical parameter
h
will be
h:=k^{-1}
. The following propagation of singularities result will be used when solving the viscoacoustic inverse problem.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
u
be an
L^{2}
-tempered solution of
Pu=0
in
Z
. Consider the Hamiltonian flow
(x(\sigma),t_{0}+\sigma,\xi(\sigma),1)
of the principal symbol of
P
through
(x_{0},t_{0},\xi_{0},1)
, where
x(\sigma)
is a geodesic and
\xi(\sigma)
is the parallel transport of
\xi_{0}
along the geodesic. Then
(x(\sigma),t_{0}+\sigma,\xi(\sigma),1)\in WF_{scl}(u)
if and only if
(x_{0},t_{0},\xi_{0},1)\in WF_{scl}(u)
.

Note that the above theorem describes the singularities at points
(x,t,\xi,\tau)
when
\tau\neq 0
. Theorem
1.2
shows that the memory term does not affect the propagation of such singularities, and they behave exactly like those for the standard wave equation. This is due to the fact that the memory term
R
is of lower order (with respect to the parameter
k
) than the wave term
Q
. Due to an integration by parts in time, we obtain
Ru=O(k)
, while of course
Qu=O(k^{2})
. As mentioned above, solutions of the viscoacoustic equation can also have stationary singularities, which correspond to the case where
\tau=0
(see Remark
2.3
). We will not study these singularities since they are not needed in the inverse problem.

Using propagating singularities as for the wave equation, we can recover the scattering relation and the travel time (the so called
lens data
) from the source-to-solution data. This is the content of Proposition
4.1
. The sound speed can then itself be recovered from the lens data under suitable geometric assumptions. In dimension
n=2
, it is a classical result by Muhometov
[
20
]
, see also
[
9
,
24
]
, that a simple metric can be recovered within a given conformal class from the lens data. In dimension
n\geq 3
, Stefanov, Uhlmann and Vasy have obtained that the lens data suffices in order to recover a metric within a given conformal class, assuming that the manifold is foliated by strictly convex hypersurfaces
[
29
]
. Both the
n=2
and the
n\geq 3
cases further assume that the manifold
(\Omega,c(x)^{-2}g_{0})
is strictly convex, where
g_{0}
represents the Euclidean metric. The use of the cited lens rigidity results, and related invertibility results for the geodesic X-ray transform, is the reason for the geometric assumptions (A1)-(A3) appearing in our main theorems.

Finally, we are able to achieve unique recovery for the time derivatives of the memory kernel at time
t=0
by means of Proposition
4.2
, which relates the values of the quasimode along a fixed null bicharacteristic with the geodesic X-ray transform of a quantity depending on the memory kernel. The derivatives of
G
of all orders can be obtained by repeating this argument recursively. These results are contained in the following theorem:

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\Omega\Subset\Omega^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\geq 2
, be open bounded sets with smooth boundary, and assume
-S^{\prime}<0<T<T^{\prime}
. Define the spacetime cylinders
M:=[0,T]\times\overline{\Omega}
and
M^{\prime}:=[-S^{\prime},T^{\prime}]\times\overline{\Omega}^{\prime}
. Let
c_{1},c_{2}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
be positive functions verifying
c_{1}=c_{2}
in
\overline{\Omega}^{\prime}\setminus\Omega
and the geometric conditions (A1)-(A3). Moreover, let
G_{1},G_{2}\in C^{\infty}(M)
be memory kernels verifying
G_{1}=G_{2}
in
\overline{\Omega}^{\prime}\setminus\Omega
, and consider the source-to-solution data
S_{1},S_{2}
corresponding to
c_{1},G_{1}
and
c_{2},G_{2}
respectively. Assume that the source-to-solution data coincide, i.e.

\mathcal{S}_{1}(f)=\mathcal{S}_{2}(f),\qquad\mbox{for all }f\in C^{\infty}_{c}(M^{\prime}\setminus M).

Then
c_{1}=c_{2}
and
\partial^{j}_{t}G_{1}|_{t=0}=\partial^{j}_{t}G_{2}|_{t=0}
for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
.

Indeed we prove that the time derivatives of all orders of the memory kernel at time
t=0
and the sound speed are uniquely determined by the source-to-solution map. If
n=2
this is analogous to the result in
[
26
]
(which considered the viscoelastic case), but for
n\geq 3
there are many non-simple geometries satisfying (A3)
[
23
]
.

As an application of our results, in the last Section
5
we present the so called extended Maxwell model for viscoacoustics, for which we show uniqueness in the recovery of the associated viscous and elastic parameters
\alpha,\beta
:

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
\widetilde{G}_{1}(x,t)
and
\widetilde{G}_{2}(x,t)
have the form given in Section

\widetilde{G}_{1}(x,t)

1.2.
Motivation and connection to the literature

The viscoacoustic wave equation has been an object of study since at least the work of Dafermos
[
10
]
, who has considered the direct problem and analyzed the behavior of its solutions for times
t\rightarrow\infty
by means of the theory of abstract Volterra equations. Carcione, Kosloff and Kosloff
[
7
]
have applied the viscoacoustic wave equation in order to model the anelastic properties of real materials in seismology, using the theory of viscoelasticity based on Boltzmann’s superposition principle. A related inverse problem was studied in
[
18
]
by Leander, who has analyzed the transient linear acoustic wave propagation in fluids with Maxwell relaxation processes, in particular in the case of a single process, and has obtained estimates of the governing parameters of the Maxwell fluid by means of numerical approximation methods. The fundamental work of Christensen
[
8
]
on the theory of viscoelasticity has put the bases for the results obtained in more recent years. The numerical methods of Bai, Yingst, Bloor and Leveille
[
1
]
have shown that viscoacoustic inversion is a more realistic approach than acoustic inversion in the study of elasticity of real earth materials. Hao and Alkhalifah
[
13
]
have developed a quite general
representation of the viscoacoustic wave
equation in the cases of (quasi-elastic) orthorhombic anisotropy and transverse isotropy, and have studied the related direct problem. In relatively recent work
[
14
]
, Hao and Greenhalgh have succeeded in incorporating the quality factor
Q
, which is a common phenomenological description of seismic wave attenuation in the interior of the Earth, into a viscoacoustic wave equation. In seismic applications the relevant case is
n=3
, and caustic formation is essentially unavoidable when considering the case of
P
waves.

The wave equation, which corresponds to the viscoacoustic wave equation in the case of vanishing memory kernel, plays a central role in seismic modeling, processing, imaging and inversion.
Inverse problems for the wave equation have been studied in several formulations and through a variety of methods. Rakesh and Symes
[
25
]
have used geometrical optics solutions for recovering a potential in the wave equation with fixed sound speed equal to
1
. The method of geometrical optics has been used also in several works by Stefanov and Uhlmann, such as
[
30
]
for the case of simple metrics, and in collaboration with Oksanen and Salo for the more general case of principal type operators
[
22
]
. The boundary control method has also been very successful in the study of inverse problems for the wave equation: for this we refer to the book by Katchalov, Kurylev and Lassas
[
16
]
, as well as to the references therein. Finally, we report the technique based on distorted plane waves that appeared in a recent work by Oksanen, Rakesh and Salo
[
21
]
.

The memory term appearing in the viscoacoustic operator
P
defined in (
1.1
) has the same order as the wave operator. Related inverse problems with lower order memory terms have been investigated in works of Bukhgeim, Dyatlov, Isakov and Uhlmann, e.g.
[
4
,
6
,
5
]
where the authors considered the recovery of the memory term from Dirichlet-to-Neumann data for the operator

u_{tt}-\Delta u+\int_{0}^{t}k(x,t-s)u(x,s)ds

and for its version with first and zero-th order perturbations. For the actual viscoelastic case where the memory term involves second order derivatives in space,
[
26
,
27
]
and references therein state several results on inverse problems for integro-differential equations with convolutional memory terms for both electrodynamics and viscoelasticity.

The viscoacoustic wave equation and related inverse problems constitute a very active field of study. To our knowledge, propagation of singularities in the case of
C^{1,1}
coefficients is currently being researched by Mazzuccato and Qi, who are employing a Hart Smith type parametrix
[
28
]
and microlocal factorization techniques. However, both the methods used and the results obtained, while related to our study, do not substantially overlap with it.

1.3.
Organization of the article

The rest of the article is organized as follows. In Section
2
we prove Theorem
1.1
and show how to construct approximate solutions, both in the form of geometrical optics in the case of simple metrics, and more generally as Gaussian beam quasimodes when the metric is not known to be simple a priori. Our propagation of singularities result of Theorem
1.2
is also proved in the same section. In Section
3
we briefly study the well-posedness of the direct problem for the viscoacoustic wave equation, and are thus able to define the source-to-solution map, which encodes the data. The solution of the inverse problem itself is presented in Section
4
, which includes the proof of our main result, Theorem
1.3
. Section
5
is dedicated to the reconstruction of the viscosity and elasticity coefficients in the extended Maxwell model, using our Theorem
1.3
as the main tool. Finally, we have added a short Appendix in which we present the derivation of the viscoacoustic operator from the classical theory of viscoelasticity discussed in
[
8
]
.

2.
Existence of approximate solutions and propagation of singularities

In this section we construct special approximate solutions to the viscoacoustic equation. We start from the case of simple sound speed
c
, for which we show how to construct approximate geometrical optics solutions.

Proposition 2.1
.

Suppose that the sound speed
c
is simple in
\overline{\Omega}
, and assume that
\varphi(x,t):=t-\psi(x)
, where
\psi\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
solves the eikonal equation

|\nabla\psi|=\frac{1}{\sqrt{c(x)}}\quad\text{ in $\Omega$}.

Further, assume that
a^{\prime}_{0}\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega}\times[0,T])
solves the transport equation

2(\partial_{t}+c(x)\nabla\psi\cdot\nabla)a^{\prime}_{0}+b(x)a^{\prime}_{0}=0\quad\text{ in $\Omega\times[0,T]$}

where
b(x):=(c^{-1}G)(x,0)-Q\psi(x)
.

Then for any
N\in\mathbb{N}
there exists a function
u\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega}\times[0,T])
of the form

u(x,t)=e^{ik\varphi(x,t)}\left(a^{\prime}_{0}(x,t)+\sum_{l=1}^{N-1}(-ik)^{-l}\bigg(a^{\prime}_{l}(x,t)+e^{-ikt}a^{\prime\prime}_{l-1}(x,t)\bigg)\right)

such that

\lVert Pu\rVert_{L^{2}((0,T),L^{2}(\Omega))}=O(k^{-N}).

The functions
a^{\prime}_{j},a^{\prime\prime}_{j}\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega}\times[0,T])
are independent of
k
, and respectively solve transport and Volterra equations.

Proof.

Step 1 (Set-up).

Let
u(x,t):=e^{ik\varphi(x,t)}a(x,t)
, where
\varphi(x,t):=t-\psi(x)
for some real valued function
\psi
to be determined, and

a(x,t):=\sum_{l=0}^{N-1}(-ik)^{-l}a_{l}(x,t),\qquad\mbox{with}\qquad a_{l}(x,t):=a^{\prime}_{l}(x,t)+(-ik)^{-1}e^{-ikt}a^{\prime\prime}_{l}(x,t).

The wave operator part satisfies

Qu=e^{ik\varphi}(k^{2}q(x,t,\nabla_{x,t}\varphi)a+ikLa+Qa),

where
q(x,t,(\xi,\tau)):=\frac{1}{2}(\tau^{2}-c(x)|\xi|^{2})
and
L:=-(\partial_{t}\varphi\partial_{t}-c\nabla\varphi\cdot\nabla)+Q\varphi
. For the memory term we compute

\displaystyle Ru

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}e^{ik\varphi(s)}(\nabla+ik\nabla\varphi(s))\cdot[G(t-s)(\nabla+ik\nabla\varphi(s))a(s)]\,ds

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\left(-k^{2}\int_{0}^{t}e^{ik\varphi(s)}G(t-s)|\nabla\varphi(s)|^{2}a(s)\,ds\right.

\displaystyle\qquad+ik\int_{0}^{t}e^{ik\varphi(s)}\left[2\nabla\varphi(s)\cdot G(t-s)\nabla a(s)+\nabla\cdot(G(t-s)\nabla\varphi(s)a(s))\right]\,ds

\displaystyle\qquad\left.+\int_{0}^{t}e^{ik\varphi(s)}\nabla\cdot(G(t-s)\nabla a(s))\,ds\right)

\displaystyle=:-k^{2}\int_{0}^{t}e^{ik\varphi(s)}A(t,s)\,ds+ik\int_{0}^{t}e^{ik\varphi(s)}B(t,s)\,ds+\int_{0}^{t}e^{ik\varphi(s)}C(t,s)\,ds.

In order to study the behaviour in
k
of the memory term, we can integrate by parts using the formula

(2.1)

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{t}e^{ik\varphi(s)}h(s)\,ds&=\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{s}(e^{ik\varphi(s)})\frac{h(s)}{ik\partial_{t}\varphi(s)}\,ds\\
&=\frac{1}{ik}\left[\frac{e^{ik\varphi(s)}h(s)}{\partial_{t}\varphi(s)}\right]_{s=0}^{t}-\frac{1}{ik}\int_{0}^{t}e^{ik\varphi(s)}\partial_{s}\left(\frac{h(s)}{\partial_{t}\varphi(s)}\right)\,ds.\end{split}

We define by recursion
h^{(j+1)}:=\partial_{s}\left(\frac{h^{(j)}(s)}{\partial_{t}\varphi(s)}\right)
for all non-negative integers
j
, and
h^{(0)}:=h
. Then we can iterate the previous formula to obtain, for all non-negative integers
N
,

(2.2)

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{t}e^{ik\varphi(s)}h^{(0)}(s)\,ds=-\sum_{j=1}^{N}(-ik)^{-j}\left[\frac{e^{ik\varphi(s)}h^{(j-1)}(s)}{\partial_{t}\varphi(s)}\right]_{s=0}^{t}+(-ik)^{-N}\int_{0}^{t}e^{ik\varphi(s)}h^{(N)}(s)\,ds.\end{split}

Step 2 (Eikonal equation).
Observe that we have

\partial_{t}\varphi\equiv 1,\qquad\nabla\varphi(x,t)=-\nabla\psi(x),\qquad\varphi(x,s)-\varphi(x,t)=s-t,\qquad L=-(\partial_{t}+c\nabla\psi\cdot\nabla)-\frac{1}{2}\nabla\cdot(c\nabla\psi).

Moreover, because
\psi
is a real function we have

|e^{ik\varphi(s)}|=|e^{iks}|=1,

and so integrating by parts
N\in\mathbb{N}
times we get

(2.3)

\int_{0}^{t}e^{iks}h(t,s)\,ds=-\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}(-ik)^{-j}\left[e^{iks}\partial^{j-1}_{s}h(t,s)\right]_{s=0}^{t}+O(k^{-N}).

Applying this formula to the computation of
Ru
, it becomes apparent that the memory term
e^{-ik\varphi(t)}Ru
has order at most
O(k)
. Therefore, it holds that

e^{-ik\varphi(t)}Pu=k^{2}q(x,t,\nabla_{x,t}\varphi)a+O(k).

Since the sound speed is simple, we can solve the eikonal equation
(\partial_{t}\varphi)^{2}-c|\nabla\varphi|^{2}=0
by letting
\varphi(x,t)=t-\psi(x)
where
\psi
was a solution of
|\nabla\psi(x)|^{2}=\frac{1}{c(x)}
.

Step 3 (Lower order terms).
Given that
A(x,t,s)=-\frac{1}{2}(c^{-1}G)(x,t-s)a(x,s)
, we will also write

\displaystyle A_{l}(x,t,s)

\displaystyle:=-\frac{1}{2}(c^{-1}G)(x,t-s)a_{l}(x,s)=-\frac{1}{2}(c^{-1}G)(x,t-s)\bigg(a^{\prime}_{l}(x,s)+(-ik)^{-1}e^{-iks}a^{\prime\prime}_{l}(x,s)\bigg)

\displaystyle=:A^{\prime}_{l}(x,t,s)+(-ik)^{-1}e^{-iks}A^{\prime\prime}_{l}(x,t,s),

and similarly for
B(x,t,s)
and
C(x,t,s)
, for all
l\in\{0,...,N-1\}
. Then we compute

\displaystyle La

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=0}^{N-1}(-ik)^{-l}\bigg(La^{\prime}_{l}+e^{-ikt}\bigg[2a^{\prime\prime}_{l}+(-ik)^{-1}La^{\prime\prime}_{l}\bigg]\bigg),

\displaystyle Qa

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=0}^{N-1}(-ik)^{-l}\bigg(Qa^{\prime}_{l}+e^{-ikt}\bigg[(-ik)a^{\prime\prime}_{l}+2\partial_{t}a^{\prime\prime}_{l}+(-ik)^{-1}Qa^{\prime\prime}_{l}\bigg]\bigg),

and by the eikonal equation the operator satisfies

\displaystyle e^{-ik\varphi(t)}Pu

\displaystyle=e^{-ik\varphi(t)}(Qu+Ru)

\displaystyle=ikLa+Qa+\int_{0}^{t}e^{ik(s-t)}\bigg(-k^{2}A+ikB+C\bigg)(t,s)\,ds

\displaystyle=ikLa+Qa+\int_{0}^{t}e^{ik(s-t)}\sum_{l=0}^{N-1}\bigg((-ik)^{2-l}A^{\prime}_{l}+(-ik)^{-l+1}e^{-iks}A^{\prime\prime}_{l}\bigg)(t,s)\,ds

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{0}^{t}e^{ik(s-t)}\sum_{l=0}^{N-1}\bigg(-(-ik)^{1-l}B^{\prime}_{l}-(-ik)^{-l}e^{-iks}B^{\prime\prime}_{l}\bigg)(t,s)\,ds

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{0}^{t}e^{ik(s-t)}\sum_{l=0}^{N-1}\bigg((-ik)^{-l}C^{\prime}_{l}+(-ik)^{-l-1}e^{-iks}C^{\prime\prime}_{l}\bigg)(t,s)\,ds

\displaystyle=ikLa+Qa+\sum_{l=0}^{N-1}(-ik)^{-l}\int_{0}^{t}e^{ik(s-t)}\bigg(-k^{2}A^{\prime}_{l}+ikB^{\prime}_{l}+C^{\prime}_{l}\bigg)(t,s)\,ds

\displaystyle\qquad+e^{-ikt}\sum_{l=0}^{N-1}(-ik)^{-l-1}\int_{0}^{t}\bigg(-k^{2}A^{\prime\prime}_{l}+ikB^{\prime\prime}_{l}+C^{\prime\prime}_{l}\bigg)(t,s)\,ds

For the first integral we use the integration by parts formula (
2.3
), which gives

\displaystyle\sum_{l=0}^{N-1}(-ik)^{-l}\int_{0}^{t}e^{ik(s-t)}\bigg(-k^{2}A^{\prime}_{l}(t,s)+ikB^{\prime}_{l}(t,s)+C^{\prime}_{l}(t,s)\bigg)\,ds=

\displaystyle=-\sum_{l=0}^{N-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}(-ik)^{-(l+j)}\left[e^{ik(s-t)}\partial^{j-1}_{s}\bigg(-k^{2}A^{\prime}_{l}+ikB^{\prime}_{l}+C^{\prime}_{l}\bigg)(t,s)\right]_{s=0}^{t}+O(k^{-N})

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\sigma=1}^{N-1}(-ik)^{-\sigma}\sum_{l=0}^{\sigma-1}\left[e^{ik(s-t)}\partial^{\sigma-l-1}_{s}\bigg(-k^{2}A^{\prime}_{l}+ikB^{\prime}_{l}+C^{\prime}_{l}\bigg)(t,s)\right]_{s=0}^{t}+O(k^{-N})

Then we can write

\displaystyle e^{-ik\varphi(t)}Pu=-\sum_{l=0}^{N-1}(-ik)^{1-l}La^{\prime}_{l}-e^{-ikt}\sum_{l=0}^{N-1}(-ik)^{-l}\bigg(2(-ik)a^{\prime\prime}_{l}+La^{\prime\prime}_{l}\bigg)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{l=0}^{N-1}(-ik)^{-l}Qa^{\prime}_{l}+e^{-ikt}\sum_{l=0}^{N-1}(-ik)^{-l-1}\bigg((-ik)^{2}a^{\prime\prime}_{l}+2(-ik)\partial_{t}a^{\prime\prime}_{l}+Qa^{\prime\prime}_{l}\bigg)

\displaystyle\quad-\sum_{l=1}^{{N}-1}(-ik)^{-l}\sum_{j=0}^{l-1}\partial^{l-j-1}_{s}\bigg((-ik)^{2}A^{\prime}_{j}-(-ik)B^{\prime}_{j}+C^{\prime}_{j}\bigg)(t,t)

\displaystyle\quad+e^{-ikt}\sum_{l=1}^{{N}-1}(-ik)^{-l}\sum_{j=0}^{l-1}\partial^{l-j-1}_{s}\bigg((-ik)^{2}A^{\prime}_{j}-(-ik)B^{\prime}_{j}+C^{\prime}_{j}\bigg)(t,0)

\displaystyle\quad+e^{-ikt}\sum_{l=0}^{N-1}(-ik)^{-l-1}\int_{0}^{t}\bigg((-ik)^{2}A^{\prime\prime}_{l}-(-ik)B^{\prime\prime}_{l}+C^{\prime\prime}_{l}\bigg)(t,s)\,ds+O(k^{-N})

\displaystyle=-(-ik)\big\{La^{\prime}_{0}\big\}-\big\{La^{\prime}_{1}-Qa^{\prime}_{0}\big\}

\displaystyle\quad-\sum_{l=1}^{N-1}(-ik)^{-l}\bigg\{La^{\prime}_{l+1}-Qa^{\prime}_{l}+\sum_{j=0}^{l-1}\partial^{l-j-1}_{s}\bigg((-ik)^{2}A^{\prime}_{j}-(-ik)B^{\prime}_{j}+C^{\prime}_{j}\bigg)(t,t)\bigg\}

\displaystyle\quad-e^{-ikt}\sum_{l=0}^{N-1}(-ik)^{-l+1}\bigg(a^{\prime\prime}_{l}-\int_{0}^{t}A^{\prime\prime}_{l}(t,s)\,ds\bigg)

\displaystyle\quad-e^{-ikt}\sum_{l=0}^{N-1}(-ik)^{-l}\bigg(La^{\prime\prime}_{l}-2\partial_{t}a^{\prime\prime}_{l}+\int_{0}^{t}B^{\prime\prime}_{l}(t,s)\,ds\bigg)

\displaystyle\quad+e^{-ikt}\sum_{l=0}^{N-1}(-ik)^{-l-1}\bigg(Qa^{\prime\prime}_{l}+\int_{0}^{t}C^{\prime\prime}_{l}(t,s)\,ds\bigg)

\displaystyle\quad+e^{-ikt}\sum_{l=1}^{N-1}(-ik)^{2-l}\sum_{j=0}^{l-1}\big(\partial^{l-j-1}_{s}A^{\prime}_{j}\big)(t,0)-e^{-ikt}\sum_{l=1}^{N-1}(-ik)^{1-l}\sum_{j=0}^{l-1}\big(\partial^{l-j-1}_{s}B^{\prime}_{j}\big)(t,0)

\displaystyle\quad+e^{-ikt}\sum_{l=1}^{N-1}(-ik)^{-l}\sum_{j=0}^{l-1}\big(\partial^{l-j-1}_{s}C^{\prime}_{j}\big)(t,0)+O(k^{-N}),

and after some reordering of the terms

\displaystyle e^{-ik\varphi(t)}Pu=-(-ik)\big\{La^{\prime}_{0}\big\}-\big\{La^{\prime}_{1}-Qa^{\prime}_{0}\big\}

\displaystyle\quad-\sum_{l=1}^{N-1}(-ik)^{-l}\bigg\{La^{\prime}_{l+1}-Qa^{\prime}_{l}+\sum_{j=0}^{l-1}\partial^{l-j-1}_{s}\bigg((-ik)^{2}A^{\prime}_{j}-(-ik)B^{\prime}_{j}+C^{\prime}_{j}\bigg)(t,t)\bigg\}

\displaystyle\quad-e^{-ikt}(-ik)\bigg\{a^{\prime\prime}_{0}-\int_{0}^{t}A^{\prime\prime}_{0}(t,s)\,ds\bigg\}

\displaystyle\quad-e^{-ikt}\bigg\{a^{\prime\prime}_{1}-\int_{0}^{t}A^{\prime\prime}_{1}(t,s)\,ds+La^{\prime\prime}_{0}-2\partial_{t}a^{\prime\prime}_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}B^{\prime\prime}_{0}(t,s)\,ds\bigg\}

\displaystyle\quad-e^{-ikt}\sum_{l=0}^{N-1}(-ik)^{-l-1}\bigg\{a^{\prime\prime}_{l+2}-\int_{0}^{t}A^{\prime\prime}_{l+2}(t,s)\,ds+La^{\prime\prime}_{l+1}-2\partial_{t}a^{\prime\prime}_{l+1}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad+\int_{0}^{t}B^{\prime\prime}_{l+1}(t,s)\,ds-Qa^{\prime\prime}_{l}-\int_{0}^{t}C^{\prime\prime}_{l}(t,s)\,ds\bigg\}

\displaystyle\quad+e^{-ikt}(-ik)A^{\prime}_{0}(t,0)+e^{-ikt}\bigg(\sum_{j=0}^{1}\partial^{1-j}_{s}A^{\prime}_{j}-B^{\prime}_{j}\bigg)(t,0)

\displaystyle\quad+e^{-ikt}\sum_{l=1}^{N-1}(-ik)^{-l}\bigg(\sum_{j=0}^{l+1}\partial^{l+1-j}_{s}A^{\prime}_{j}-\sum_{j=0}^{l}\partial^{l-j}_{s}B^{\prime}_{j}+\sum_{j=0}^{l-1}\partial^{l-j-1}_{s}C^{\prime}_{j}\bigg)(t,0)

\displaystyle\quad+O(k^{-N}).

If we define the operator
V
such that

Vu(x,t):=u(x,t)+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}(c^{-1}G)(x,t-s)u(x,s)ds,

then we obtain

\displaystyle e^{-ik\varphi(t)}Pu=-(-ik)\big\{La^{\prime}_{0}+A^{\prime}_{0}(t,t)\big\}-\bigg\{La^{\prime}_{1}+A^{\prime}_{1}(t,t)-Qa^{\prime}_{0}+\bigg(\partial_{s}A^{\prime}_{0}-B^{\prime}_{0}\bigg)(t,t)\bigg\}

\displaystyle\quad-\sum_{l=1}^{N-1}(-ik)^{-l}\bigg\{La^{\prime}_{l+1}+A^{\prime}_{l+1}(t,t)-Qa^{\prime}_{l}+\bigg(\sum_{j=0}^{l}\partial^{l+1-j}_{s}A^{\prime}_{j}-\sum_{j=0}^{l}\partial^{l-j}_{s}B^{\prime}_{j}+\sum_{j=0}^{l-1}\partial^{l-j-1}_{s}C^{\prime}_{j}\bigg)(t,t)\bigg\}

\displaystyle\quad-e^{-ikt}(-ik)\big\{Va^{\prime\prime}_{0}-A^{\prime}_{0}(t,0)\big\}

\displaystyle\quad-e^{-ikt}\bigg\{Va^{\prime\prime}_{1}+La^{\prime\prime}_{0}-2\partial_{t}a^{\prime\prime}_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}B^{\prime\prime}_{0}(t,s)\,ds-\bigg(\sum_{j=0}^{1}\partial^{1-j}_{s}A^{\prime}_{j}-B^{\prime}_{j}\bigg)(t,0)\bigg\}

\displaystyle\quad-e^{-ikt}\sum_{l=1}^{N-1}(-ik)^{-l}\bigg\{Va^{\prime\prime}_{l+1}+La^{\prime\prime}_{l}-2\partial_{t}a^{\prime\prime}_{l}+\int_{0}^{t}B^{\prime\prime}_{l}(t,s)\,ds-Qa^{\prime\prime}_{l-1}-\int_{0}^{t}C^{\prime\prime}_{l-1}(t,s)\,ds

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad-\bigg(\sum_{j=0}^{l+1}\partial^{l+1-j}_{s}A^{\prime}_{j}-\sum_{j=0}^{l}\partial^{l-j}_{s}B^{\prime}_{j}+\sum_{j=0}^{l-1}\partial^{l-j-1}_{s}C^{\prime}_{j}\bigg)(t,0)\bigg\}

\displaystyle\quad+O(k^{-N}).

This implies that the equations solved by the functions
a^{\prime}_{l},a^{\prime\prime}_{l}
are of the form

(2.4)

La^{\prime}_{l}+A^{\prime}_{l}(t,t)=\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{l}(x,t),\qquad Va^{\prime\prime}_{l}=\mathcal{R}^{\prime\prime}_{l}(x,t),

for all
l\in\{0,...,N-1\}
, where the right-hand side terms
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{l}
depend only on
\{a^{\prime}_{j}\}_{j<l}
, and the right-hand side terms
\mathcal{R}^{\prime\prime}_{l}
depend only on
\{a^{\prime\prime}_{l}\}_{j<l}
and
\{a^{\prime}_{j}\}_{j\leq l}
. This means that we can solve the equations recursively, starting from
a^{\prime}_{0}
and then alternating
a^{\prime\prime}_{0},a^{\prime}_{1},a^{\prime\prime}_{1},a^{\prime}_{2},...
.

Step 4 (Volterra equations).
For the second equation in (
2.4
), which is a
Volterra integral equation of the second kind
, we refer to
[
3
, Theorems 1.2.3 and 2.1.1]
. The equation takes the form

u(t)=f(t)+\int_{0}^{t}K(t-s)u(s)ds,

with
K:=c^{-1}G
. Define
K_{1}:=K
,

K_{n}(t):=\int_{0}^{t}K_{1}(t-s)K_{n-1}(s)ds,\qquad n\geq 2.

and finally the resolvent kernel
R
as the (absolutely and uniformly) convergent Neumann series

R(t):=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}K_{n}(t).

The unique solution
u(t)
of the Volterra integral equation then takes the form

u(t)=f(t)+\int_{0}^{t}R(t-s)f(s)ds,

which in particular implies that for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
we have the estimate

\|u\|_{C^{N}}\lesssim\|f\|_{C^{N}}(1+\|R\|_{C^{N}})
.

Step 5 (Transport equations).
We now solve the first equation in (
2.4
). Define the operators
T,Z
and the function
g
as

g(x):=-\frac{1}{2}(c^{-1}G)(x,0),\qquad Z:=-\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{t}+c(x)\nabla\psi(x)\cdot\nabla),\qquad T:=-Z+\frac{1}{2}(Q\psi(x)-g(x)).

Then for every
l\in\{0,...,N-1\}
we have

\displaystyle Ta^{\prime}_{l}(x,t)=La^{\prime}_{l}(x,t)+A^{\prime}_{l}(x,t,t)=\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{l}(x,t).

To solve the transport equation, assume that
\mu(x,t)=\mu_{G}(x,t)
is an integrating factor solving

\mu^{-1}Z\mu=\frac{g-Q\psi}{2},

so that

\begin{split}Z(\mu a^{\prime}_{l})=\mu\left(Z+(\mu^{-1}Z\mu)\right)a^{\prime}_{l}=\frac{\mu\,Ta^{\prime}_{l}}{2}=\frac{\mu\,\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{l}}{2}.\end{split}

The integral curve
\eta_{x}(t)
of
Z
with
\eta_{x}(0)=(x,0)
is given by
\eta_{x}(t)=(\alpha_{x}(t),t)
, where
\alpha_{x}(t)
satisfies
\dot{\alpha}_{x}(t)=(c\nabla\psi)(\alpha_{x}(t))
and
\alpha_{x}(0)=x
. One can check that
\alpha_{x}(t)=\gamma_{x,(c\nabla\psi)(x)}(t)
where
\gamma_{x,v}
is the geodesic of the metric
\frac{1}{c(x)}\delta_{jk}
with
\gamma_{x,v}(0)=x
and
\dot{\gamma}_{x,v}(0)=v
. We can choose
\mu
as

\mu(\alpha_{x}(t),t)=\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\big(g-Q\psi\big)(\alpha_{x}(s))\,ds\right),

which can be rewritten as

\mu(x,t)=\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\big(g-Q\psi\big)(\alpha_{x}(-s))\,ds\right).

We now fix an initial value
a^{\prime}_{l}(x,0)=f^{\prime}_{l}(x)
, and want to find
a^{\prime}_{l}(x,t)
solving

Z(\mu a^{\prime}_{l})=\frac{\mu(x,t)\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{l}(x,t)}{2},\qquad a^{\prime}_{l}(x,0)=f^{\prime}_{l}(x).

We need that

(\mu a^{\prime}_{l})(\alpha_{x}(t),t)=(\mu a^{\prime}_{l})(x,0)+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\mu(\alpha_{x}(s),s)\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{l}(\alpha_{x}(s),s)\,ds.

Since
\mu(x,0)=1
, the solution of the transport equation is

\displaystyle a^{\prime}_{l}(\alpha_{x}(t),t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mu(\alpha_{x}(t),t)}\left(f^{\prime}_{l}(x)+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\mu(\alpha_{x}(s),s)\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{l}(\alpha_{x}(s),s)\,ds\right),

which can be rewritten as

\displaystyle a^{\prime}_{l}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{f^{\prime}_{l}(\alpha_{x}(-t))}{\mu(x,t)}+\frac{1}{2\mu(x,t)}\int_{0}^{t}\big(\mu\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{l}\big)(\alpha_{x}(-s),t-s)\,ds.

Assuming that
f_{l+1}\equiv 0
for all
l\geq 0
, we get

(2.5)

\displaystyle a^{\prime}_{0}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{f^{\prime}_{0}(\alpha_{x}(-t))}{\mu(x,t)},\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad a^{\prime}_{l+1}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\mu(x,t)}\int_{0}^{t}\big(\mu\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{l+1}\big)(\alpha_{x}(-s),t-s)\,ds,\quad l\geq 0,

where for all
l\geq 0
it holds

(2.6)

\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{l+1}(x,t)=-Qa^{\prime}_{l}+\partial_{s}A^{\prime}_{l}(t,t)-B^{\prime}_{l}(t,t)+\sum_{j=0}^{l-1}\partial^{l-j-1}_{s}\bigg(\partial^{2}_{s}A^{\prime}_{j}-\partial_{s}B^{\prime}_{j}+C^{\prime}_{j}\bigg)(t,t).

Observe that by the definition of the amplitude
a(x,t)
it holds

(2.7)

a(x,t)=a^{\prime}_{0}(x,t)+\sum_{l=0}^{N-1}(-ik)^{-l-1}\bigg(a^{\prime}_{l+1}(x,t)+e^{-ikt}a^{\prime\prime}_{l}(x,t)\bigg)=\frac{f^{\prime}_{0}(\alpha_{x}(-t))}{\mu(x,t)}+O(k^{-1}).

If the initial value
f^{\prime}_{0}
is supported near
x_{0}
, then by the above formula we see that
a
is itself supported near the curve
(\alpha_{x_{0}}(t),t)
modulo an
O(k^{-1})
term. This proves that there exist approximate geometrical optics solutions concentrating near geodesics of the metric
\frac{1}{c(x)}\delta_{jk}
, just like in the case of the standard wave equation.

Step 6 (Final estimate).
We have proved that
|Pu|=|e^{-ik\varphi(t)}Pu|=O(k^{-N})
. Therefore, the
L^{2}
estimate
\lVert Pu\rVert_{L^{2}((0,T),L^{2}(\Omega))}=O(k^{-N})

follows immediately by the boundedness of
\Omega\times(0,T)
.
∎

Next, we turn to the case of general (thus, not necessarily simple) sound speed, for which we construct approximate solutions in the form of quasimodes.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Step 1 (Set-up).
In this case we look for a Gaussian beam approximate solution of the form

u(x,t)=\int_{0}^{T}e^{ik\varphi(x,t,\sigma)}a^{\prime}(x,t,\sigma)d\sigma+\int_{0}^{T}e^{ik\varphi(x,0,\sigma)}a^{\prime\prime}(x,t,\sigma)d\sigma,

where
a^{\prime},a^{\prime\prime},\varphi
are allowed to be complex valued. Here we use the ansatz in
[
22
]
written in terms of superpositions of wave packets, which will be useful later when we prove propagation of singularities results. We also assume that

a^{\prime}(z,\sigma)=\sum_{l=0}^{N-1}(-ik)^{-l}a^{\prime}_{l}(z,\sigma),\qquad a^{\prime\prime}(z,\sigma)=(-ik)^{-1}\sum_{l=0}^{N-1}(-ik)^{-l}a^{\prime\prime}_{l}(z,\sigma),

which gives

u(x,t)=\sum_{l=0}^{N-1}(-ik)^{-l}\int_{0}^{T}\bigg(e^{ik\varphi(x,t,\sigma)}a^{\prime}_{l}(x,t,\sigma)+(-ik)^{-1}e^{ik\varphi(x,0,\sigma)}a^{\prime\prime}_{l}(x,t,\sigma)\bigg)d\sigma.

In what follows we allow
z=(x,t)
and
\zeta=(\xi,\tau)
, while
\sigma\in[0,T]
will be the parameter of the bicharacteristic
\gamma(\sigma)=(z(\sigma),\zeta(\sigma))
. We make this choice of symbols in order to avoid the confusion arising from the fact that the coordinates defining
P
already include time
t
, and thus the space projection
z
of
\gamma
is actually in spacetime.

Step 2 (Matrix Riccati equation).
We shall look for a phase function
\varphi(z,\sigma)
satisfying

(2.8)

q(\cdot,\nabla_{z}\varphi(\cdot,\sigma))+\partial_{\sigma}\varphi(\cdot,\sigma)=0\quad\mbox{ to infinite order at }z(\sigma)\mbox{ for }\sigma\in[0,T],

(2.9)

\varphi(z(\sigma),\sigma)=0,\qquad\nabla_{z}\varphi(z(\sigma),\sigma)=\zeta(\sigma),

where we recall that

q(z,\zeta):=\frac{1}{2}(\tau^{2}-c(x)|\xi|^{2}).

For the construction of
\varphi
we propose

(2.10)

\varphi(z,\sigma)=\zeta(\sigma)\cdot(z-z(\sigma))+\frac{1}{2}H(\sigma)(z-z(\sigma))\cdot(z-z(\sigma))+\varphi_{3}(z,\sigma),

with
H
complex and symmetric, and
\varphi_{3}
vanishing of order
3
at
z(\sigma)
. It is immediately seen that the above
\varphi
satisfies (
2.9
). As in
[
22
]
, using these and the Hamilton equations, one proves that (
2.8
) holds to first order. In order to show that (
2.8
) holds to second order, one has to solve the matrix Riccati equation

(2.11)

\partial_{t}H+D+(BH+HB^{T})+HCH=0,

where
B,C
and
D
are matrices with components given by the second derivatives of the Hamiltonian
q(z,\zeta)
:

B_{jl}:=\frac{\partial^{2}q}{\partial z_{l}\partial\zeta_{j}},\qquad C_{jl}:=\frac{\partial^{2}q}{\partial\zeta_{j}\partial\zeta_{l}},\qquad D_{jl}:=\frac{\partial^{2}q}{\partial z_{j}\partial z_{l}}.

By
[
16
, Lemma 2.56]
we can find a solution with the additional property that
\Im H(\sigma)
is positive definite for all
\sigma\in[0,T]
. The construction of
\varphi_{3}(z,\sigma)
is completed by successively solving linear systems of ODEs, see
[
19
, Lemma 6.3]
.

We observe that we can ensure that
\Im\varphi(z,\sigma)>0
when
a^{\prime}
and
a^{\prime\prime}
are chosen to be supported sufficiently close to
\gamma
. In fact, because the first order term of
\varphi
is real valued, we have

\Im\varphi(z,\sigma)=\frac{1}{2}\Im H(\sigma)(z-z(\sigma))\cdot(z-z(\sigma))+O(|z-z(\sigma)|^{3})>0

close to
\gamma
by the positive definitedness of
\Im H(\sigma)
. In particular, this implies that for all
s\in[0,t]
and all
k\in\mathbb{N}
it holds

\displaystyle|e^{ik\varphi(x,s,\sigma)}|=|e^{ik\Re\varphi(x,s,\sigma)}e^{-k\Im\varphi(x,s,\sigma)}|=e^{-k\Im\varphi(x,s,\sigma)}\leq 1,

which by the integration by parts formula (
2.2
) ensures that the memory terms of
P
contribute at most to order
O(k)
.

Step 3 (Lower order terms).
By linearity, in order to compute
Pu
it suffices to find

P(e^{ik\varphi(x,t)}a^{\prime}_{l}(x,t))\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad P(e^{ik\varphi(x,0)}a^{\prime\prime}_{l}(x,t)),

where for the sake of simplicity we have suppressed the dependence on
\sigma
. By the computations in the proof of Proposition
2.1
, we find

\displaystyle P(e^{ik\varphi(x,t)}a^{\prime}_{l}(x,t))

\displaystyle=-e^{ik\varphi}((-ik)^{2}q(z,\nabla_{z}\varphi)+(-ik)L-Q)a^{\prime}_{l}

\displaystyle\quad+(-ik)^{2}\int_{0}^{t}e^{ik\varphi(s)}A^{\prime}_{l}(t,s)\,ds-(-ik)\int_{0}^{t}e^{ik\varphi(s)}B^{\prime}_{l}(t,s)\,ds+\int_{0}^{t}e^{ik\varphi(s)}C^{\prime}_{l}(t,s)\,ds

with clear meaning of the symbols
A^{\prime}_{l},B^{\prime}_{l},C^{\prime}_{l}
. The integration by parts formula (
2.2
) then gives

\displaystyle P(e^{ik\varphi(x,t)}a^{\prime}_{l}(x,t))=

\displaystyle=-\frac{e^{ik\varphi}}{\partial_{t}\varphi}\bigg((-ik)^{2}\bigg\{\partial_{t}\varphi q(z,\nabla_{z}\varphi)a^{\prime}_{l}\bigg\}+(-ik)\bigg\{\partial_{t}\varphi La^{\prime}_{l}+A_{l}^{{}^{\prime}(0)}\bigg\}+\bigg\{A_{l}^{{}^{\prime}(1)}-B_{l}^{{}^{\prime}(0)}-\partial_{t}\varphi Qa^{\prime}_{l}\bigg\}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}(-ik)^{-j}\bigg\{A_{l}^{{}^{\prime}(j+1)}-B_{l}^{{}^{\prime}(j)}+C_{l}^{{}^{\prime}(j-1)}\bigg\}\bigg)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{e^{ik\varphi(0)}}{\partial_{t}\varphi(0)}\bigg((-ik)\bigg\{A_{l}^{{}^{\prime}(0)}(0)\bigg\}+\bigg\{\big(A_{l}^{{}^{\prime}(1)}-B_{l}^{{}^{\prime}(0)}\big)(0)\bigg\}+\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}(-ik)^{-j}\bigg\{\big(A_{l}^{{}^{\prime}(j+1)}-B_{l}^{{}^{\prime}(j)}+C_{l}^{{}^{\prime}(j-1)}\big)(0)\bigg\}\bigg)

\displaystyle\quad+O(k^{-N}).

Summing over the index
l

\displaystyle\sum_{l=0}^{N-1}(-ik)^{-l}P(e^{ik\varphi(x,t)}a^{\prime}_{l}(x,t))=

Next, we shall use the above results to prove that propagation of singularities holds for our operator. We begin with a short reminder of some semiclassical concepts, following
[
32
]
. Let
h
be a small parameter (in our case, we will assume
h:=k^{-1}
), and assume that
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is open. A family
\{u_{h}\}_{h\in(0,1]}\subset L^{2}_{loc}(X)
is said to be
L^{2}
-tempered
if for any
\chi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(X)
there exists
N\geq 0
such that
\|\chi u_{h}\|_{L^{2}}=O(h^{-N})
as
h\rightarrow 0
. Let
\mathcal{F}_{h}
denote the
semiclassical Fourier transform
given by

\mathcal{F}_{h}f(\xi):=(2\pi h)^{-n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{-ih^{-1}x\cdot\xi}f(x)dx.

We say that
u_{h}
is
semiclassically smooth
at
(x_{0},\xi_{0})\in T^{*}X
if in some local coordinates near
x_{0}
(the definition is independent of the choice of local coordinates) there exist
\phi,\psi\in C^{\infty}_{c}
such that

\phi=1\mbox{ near }x_{0},\quad\psi=1\mbox{ near }\xi_{0},\quad\psi\mathcal{F}_{h}(\phi u_{h})=O_{L^{2}}(h^{\infty}).

The
semiclassical wave front set

\WF_{scl}(u_{h})
is then defined as the complement of the set of those points
(x_{0},\xi_{0})\in T^{*}X
where
u_{h}
is semiclassically smooth. We will make use of the following characterization of the semiclassical wavefront set:

Lemma 2.2
.

[
22
, Proposition 2.3]

Let

be
L^{2}
-tempered in
X
. If

, one has
(z_{0},\zeta_{0})\notin WF_{scl}(u)
if and only if

(u,e^{ik\Psi(\cdot;z,\zeta)}b)_{L^{2}(Z)}=O(k^{-\infty})\text{ as $k\to\infty$}

uniformly over
(z,\zeta)
near
(z_{0},\zeta_{0})
, where
\Psi,b
are smooth functions such that in local coordinates near

it holds

\Psi(y;z,\zeta)=\zeta\cdot(y-z)+\frac{i}{2}|y-z|^{2},\qquad\mbox{supp}(b)\mbox{ close to }z_{0},\qquad b=1\mbox{ near }z_{0}.

1.2
.

Let

3.
Well-posedness of the direct problem and source-to-solution map

We study the well-posedness of the following problem:

(3.1)

\begin{cases}Pu=f,&\mbox{ in }\Omega\times[0,T]=:M,\\
u(0)=u_{0},\quad\dot{u}(0)=\dot{u}_{0},&\mbox{ in }\Omega.\end{cases}

For this we follow
[
12
, Appendix B]
and
[
10
, Theorem 2.2]
. Observe that, as in the first reference, we shall define

\mathbf{C}u(\cdot,t):=\nabla\cdot(c(\cdot)\nabla u(\cdot,t)),\qquad\mathbf{G}u(\cdot,t,s):=\nabla\cdot(G(\cdot,t-s)\nabla u(\cdot,s)),

so that our equation
Pu=f
can be rewritten in the form

\partial^{2}_{t}u(x,t)=\mathbf{C}u(x,t)-\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{G}u(x,t,s)ds+f(x,t),

as in the second reference. We make the following assumptions on the coefficients
\mathbf{C},\mathbf{G}
(see beginning of Section 2 in
[
10
]
):

•

\mathbf{C}(t),\mathbf{C}_{t}(t)\in L^{\infty}([0,T],\mathcal{L}(H^{1}_{0}(\Omega),H^{-1}(\Omega)))
,

•

\langle\mathbf{C}(t)v,w\rangle=\langle\mathbf{C}(t)w,v\rangle,\quad\mbox{ for all }v,w\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega),

•

there exists a constant
K>0
such that

-\langle\mathbf{C}(t)v,v\rangle\geq K\|v\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2},\quad\mbox{ for all }v\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega),

•

\mathbf{G}(t,s),\mathbf{G}_{t}(t,s)\in L^{\infty}([0,T]\times[0,T],\mathcal{L}(H^{1}_{0}(\Omega),H^{-1}(\Omega)))
.

Under these assumptions, we have

Proposition 3.1

(Well-posedness, Theorems 2.1 and 2.2 in
[
10
]
)
.

Let
f\in L^{2}([0,T],{H^{-1}(\Omega)})
, and
u_{0}=\dot{u}_{0}=0
. There exists a unique generalized solution
u\in L^{2}([0,T],H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))\cap H^{1}([0,T],L^{2}(\Omega))
to (
3.1
). Moreover, the following estimate holds:

\|u\|_{L^{2}([0,T],H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))}\lesssim\|f\|_{L^{2}([0,T],{H^{-1}(\Omega)})}.

The proof of existence and uniqueness is obtained by applying the Lax-Milgram theorem on small time intervals. Given
t_{0}\in(0,T)
, one defines the bilinear form

B_{t_{0}}(u,v):=\int_{0}^{t_{0}}(t-t_{0})\left(\langle\dot{u},\ddot{v}\rangle+\langle\nabla\cdot(c\nabla u),v\rangle-\int_{0}^{t}\langle\nabla\cdot(G(t-s)\nabla u(s)),\dot{v}\rangle ds\right)dt+\int_{0}^{t_{0}}\langle\dot{u},\dot{v}\rangle dt,

where for the sake of simplicity we suppressed all dependencies on the variables
x,t
. Using the assumptions for
\mathbf{C},\mathbf{G}
, one sees that
B_{t_{0}}
is both coercive for small values of
t_{0}
, and bounded. Using the Riesz representation theorem, this guarantees the existence of a unique generalized solution in the time interval
[0,t_{0}]
, with the well-posedness estimate

\|u\|_{L^{2}([0,t_{0}],H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))}\lesssim\|f\|_{L^{2}([0,t_{0}],{H^{-1}(\Omega)})}.

The final result and estimate are obtained by repeating this argument a finite amount of times.

Consider now two spacetime cylinders
M\Subset M^{\prime}
with
M:=[-S,T]\times\Omega
,
M^{\prime}:=[-S^{\prime},T^{\prime}]\times\Omega^{\prime}
. The source-to-solution map associated to the operator
P
and cylinders

is the map

\mathcal{S}:C^{\infty}_{c}(M^{\prime}\setminus M)\times H^{1}_{0}(\Omega^{\prime})\times L^{2}(\Omega^{\prime})\rightarrow L^{2}([-S^{\prime},T^{\prime}],H^{1}(\Omega^{\prime}\setminus\Omega))

such that

\mathcal{S}(f,u_{0},\dot{u}_{0}):=u|_{M^{\prime}\setminus M},

where
u\in L^{2}([-S^{\prime},T^{\prime}],H^{1}_{0}(\Omega^{\prime}))\cap H^{1}([-S^{\prime},T^{\prime}],L^{2}(\Omega^{\prime}))
is the unique solution to the problem

(3.2)

\begin{cases}Pu=f,&\mbox{ in }M^{\prime},\\
u(-S^{\prime})=u_{0},\quad\dot{u}(-S^{\prime})=\dot{u}_{0},&\mbox{ in }\Omega^{\prime},\end{cases}

whose existence is granted by Proposition
3.1
.

5.
Applications

As an application of our results, we consider a scalar version of the extended Maxwell model (EMM) for viscoacoustic systems described in
[
11
,
12
]
. This is a microscopic model for viscoacoustic behaviour in polymeric materials, in which the natural energy collecting and dissipating properties of the medium are modeled by many elementary spring-dashpot units connected in parallel. Each spring-dashpot element represents the behaviour of a given chemical species, which collects (elasticity) and dissipates (viscosity) energy according to its specific properties. The integro-differential equation is

\partial^{2}_{t}u(x,t)-\nabla\cdot\left(\widetilde{G}(x,0)\nabla u(x,t)+\int_{0}^{t}(\partial_{t}\widetilde{G})(x,t-s)\nabla u(x,s)ds\right)=0,

which corresponds to our viscoacoustic operator
P
. The function
\tilde{G}
is named
relaxation function
, and experimental evidence suggests that for each elementary spring-dashpot unit it takes the form

\tilde{G}(x,t)=\tilde{G}(x,0)e^{-t/\tau(x)},

where
\tau(x):=\frac{\eta(x)}{\tilde{G}(x,0)}
is the
relaxation time
,
\tilde{G}(x,0)
is the
spring constant
, and
\eta(x)
is the
viscous constant
. The assumption that the relaxation function depends on time as an inverse exponential is aptly named
fading memory
: the most recent deformations have a much heavier effect than the least recent ones. For the
extended
Maxwell model one assumes

\widetilde{G}(x,t)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}e^{t\alpha_{j}(x)}\beta_{j}(x),\qquad\alpha_{j}(x):=-\tau_{j}^{-1}(x),\quad\beta_{j}(x):=\tilde{G}_{j}(x,0)

for some
N\in\mathbb{N}
and coefficients
\alpha,\beta:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N}
. In order to avoid repetitions, we assume that the coefficients
\alpha_{j}
are all distinct.

\widetilde{G}_{0}(0)
\widetilde{G}_{1}(0)
\eta_{1}
\widetilde{G}_{2}(0)
\eta_{2}
\widetilde{G}_{N}(0)
\eta_{N}
\vdots

In this framework, we want to prove the uniqueness result of Theorem
1.4
. We start with the proofs of the following Lemmas.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
N\in\mathbb{N}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, and assume that
\{\alpha_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N},\{\beta_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}\subset\mathbb{R}
. Then it holds

(5.1)

\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{j}^{N+k}\beta_{j}=-\sum_{l=0}^{N-1}\frac{1}{l!}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{j}^{l+k}\beta_{j}\right)\partial_{y}^{l}\left(\prod_{\nu=1}^{N}(y-\alpha_{\nu})\right)_{y=0}.

Proof.

Let
p(y):=\prod_{\nu=1}^{N}(y-\alpha_{\nu})
. We immediately see that
p(\alpha_{j})=0
for all
j\in\{1,...,N\}
, and thus by Taylor’s formula

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{j}^{N+k}\beta_{j}=-\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{j}^{k}\beta_{j}\left(p(\alpha_{j})-\alpha_{j}^{N}\right)=-\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{j}^{k}\beta_{j}\left(\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\frac{\partial^{l}p(0)}{l!}\alpha_{j}^{l}-\alpha_{j}^{N}\right).

However, we also see that
\partial^{l}p\equiv 0
for all
l>N
, and
\partial^{N}p\equiv N!
. Therefore

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{j}^{N+k}\beta_{j}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{j}^{k}\beta_{j}\left(\sum_{l=0}^{N-1}\frac{\partial^{l}p(0)}{l!}\alpha_{j}^{l}\right)=-\sum_{l=0}^{N-1}\frac{1}{l!}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{j}^{l+k}\beta_{j}\right)\partial^{l}p(0).

∎

Lemma 5.2
.

Assume that the set
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
defined by

S:=S_{\beta}\cup S_{\alpha}:=\bigcup_{l=1}^{N}\beta_{l}^{-1}(0)\cup\bigcup_{j<k,\;j,k=1}^{N}(\alpha_{j}-\alpha_{k})^{-1}(0)

has vanishing measure. Then the matrix
M
defined by
M_{ij}:=\sum_{l=1}^{N}\alpha_{l}^{i+j-2}\beta_{l}
is invertible almost everywhere.

Proof.

It suffices to show that the determinant of
M
is

\det M=\prod_{l=1}^{N}\beta_{l}\prod_{j<k,\;j,k=1}^{N}(\alpha_{j}-\alpha_{k})^{2}.

In order to see this, observe that
\det M
is a polynomial in the variables
\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{N},\beta_{1},...,\beta_{N}
such that each summand has exactly
N
factors coming from
\beta
. If any of the
\beta
factors is repeated, that is the summand is divisible by e.g.
\beta_{l}^{2}
, then in the determinant the same summand also appears with opposite sign. This is due to the existence of two permutations of opposite signs interchanging the two rows of
M
(say, the
k
-th and the
j
-th) from which the
\beta_{l}
factors come. Observe that this permutation leaves unchanged the associated power of
\alpha_{l}
, which is
j+k
in both cases. Thus all summands with repeated
\beta
factors vanish, and the determinant can be written as

(5.2)

\det M=\pi(\alpha)\prod_{l=1}^{N}\beta_{l}

for some fixed polynomial
\pi
in the variables
\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{N}
. Observe that
\pi
does not depend on
\beta
, and thus it must be a homogeneous and symmetric polynomial with respect to
\alpha
. Moreover, observe that the two sequences of coefficients
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{1},\alpha_{3},...,\alpha_{N},\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\beta_{3},...,\beta_{N}
and
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{1},\alpha_{3},...,\alpha_{N},0,(\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}),\beta_{3},...,\beta_{N}
give rise to the same matrix, and hence the same determinant, which clearly vanishes in the second case because of formula (
5.2
). Together with symmetry, this proves that
(\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2})
divides
\pi(\alpha)
an even number of times, and the same can be said for all polynomials of the kind
(\alpha_{j}-\alpha_{k})
, with
j,k\in\{1,...,N\}
. Thus we must have

\pi(\alpha)=\tilde{\pi}(\alpha)\prod_{j<k,\;j,k=1}^{N}(\alpha_{j}-\alpha_{k})^{2}

for some fixed polynomial
\tilde{\pi}
. In order to find the final polynomial
\tilde{\pi}
, we will compute the degrees of
\pi
and of the product on the right-hand side of the above formula. The power with which
\alpha
appears in the element
M_{ij}
is
i+j-2
; because each summand of the homogeneous polynomial
\pi
is produced by multiplying
\alpha
factors coming from
N
distinct rows and columns of
M
, we must have

\deg\pi=\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}(i+j-2)=2\sum_{i=1}^{N}i-2N=N(N+1)-2N=N(N-1).

As for the product on the right-hand side, we see that it consists of
\frac{N(N-1)}{2}
quadratic factors, and so its degree is also
N(N-1)
. This proves that
\tilde{\pi}
is a constant. Next, we will show one example in which we are able to compute the determinant easily, which will establish that
\tilde{\pi}\equiv 1
and complete the proof.

Let
V
be the Vandermonde matrix given by
V_{ij}:=\alpha_{j}^{i-1}
, and consider the linear system

V\beta=e_{N},

where
e_{N}
is the
N
-th standard coordinate vector. Because
V
is invertible in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus S_{\alpha}
, by the properties of the Vandermonde matrix we can compute
\beta
as the unique solution given by

\beta=V^{-1}e_{N}=\left(\begin{matrix}L_{0,0}&...&L_{N-1,0}\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
L_{0,N-1}&...&L_{N-1,N-1}\end{matrix}\right)e_{N}=(L_{N-1,0},...,L_{N-1,N-1}),

where for all
j\in\{0,...,N-1\}
the function
L_{j}
is the Lagrange polynomial

L_{j}(y):=\prod_{i\neq j,\;i=0}^{N-1}\frac{y-\alpha_{i+1}}{\alpha_{j+1}-\alpha_{i+1}}=:\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}L_{i,j}y^{i}.

In particular, we have obtained that for all
l\in\{1,...,N\}
it holds

\beta_{l}=\prod_{i\neq l,\;i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{\alpha_{l}-\alpha_{i}}
. Since this
\beta
solves the linear system, we see that the first row of the matrix
M
is
e_{N}
, and thus because of its a priori structure we have that
M
is lower anti-triangular with
1
along the non-principal diagonal. Therefore,
\det M=(-1)^{\frac{N(N-1)}{2}}
. Computing
\det M
with formula
\det M=\tilde{\pi}\prod_{l=1}^{N}\beta_{l}\prod_{j<k,\;j,k=1}^{N}(\alpha_{j}-\alpha_{k})^{2}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\det M

\displaystyle=\tilde{\pi}\prod_{i\neq l,\;i,l=1}^{N}\frac{1}{\alpha_{l}-\alpha_{i}}\prod_{j<k,\;j,k=1}^{N}(\alpha_{j}-\alpha_{k})^{2}=\tilde{\pi}\prod_{i<l,\;i,l=1}^{N}\prod_{j<k,\;j,k=1}^{N}\frac{\alpha_{j}-\alpha_{k}}{\alpha_{l}-\alpha_{i}}=(-1)^{\frac{N(N-1)}{2}}\tilde{\pi}

Thus,
\tilde{\pi}\equiv 1
.
∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem
1.4
.

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

Observe that for all
k\in\{0,...,2N-1\}
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
it holds

(5.3)

\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{1,j}^{k}\beta_{1,j}=(\partial_{t}^{k}\widetilde{G}_{1})(\cdot,0)=(\partial_{t}^{k}\widetilde{G}_{2})(\cdot,0)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{2,j}^{k}\beta_{2,j}.

Using formula (
5.1
), we obtain that for all
k\in\{1,...,N\}
it holds

\sum_{l=1}^{N}\frac{1}{(l-1)!}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{1,j}^{l+k-2}\beta_{1,j}\right)\partial^{l-1}p_{1}(0)=\sum_{l=1}^{N}\frac{1}{(l-1)!}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{2,j}^{l+k-2}\beta_{2,j}\right)\partial^{l-1}p_{2}(0),

where
p_{\mu}(y):=\prod_{\nu=1}^{N}(y-\alpha_{\mu,\nu})
for
\mu=1,2
. Because the sums in parentheses coincide by assumption, we can define a matrix
M
as

M_{lk}:=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{1,j}^{l+k-2}\beta_{1,j}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{2,j}^{l+k-2}\beta_{2,j}.

Observe that
M
is invertible almost everywhere by Lemma
5.2
. Thus, if we define the vectors
c_{\mu}
by
c_{\mu,l}:=\frac{\partial^{l-1}p_{\mu}(0)}{(l-1)!}
for
\mu=1,2
, we see that it must hold

c_{1}=M^{-1}Mc_{2}=c_{2}.

Because
c_{\mu,l}
is by definition the coefficient of the monic polynomial
p_{\mu}
corresponding to the
(l-1)
-th power, we deduce that it must be
p_{1}=p_{2}
. Thus
\alpha_{1,j}=\alpha_{2,j}
for all
j\in\{1,...,N\}
, as these are the roots of the identical polynomials
p_{1},p_{2}
.

We are left with the problem of showing the equality of the
\beta
coefficients. To this end, we define the Vandermonde matrix
V
as

V_{ij}:=\alpha_{1,j}^{i-1}=\alpha_{2,j}^{i-1},

and observe that by rewriting the first
N
equations from (
5.3
) in matrix form we obtain

V\beta_{1}=V\beta_{2}.

Because
V
is invertible for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus S_{\alpha}
, we deduce that indeed
\beta_{1,j}=\beta_{2,j}
for all
j\in\{1,...,N\}
.
∎

Appendix A
Derivation of the viscoacoustic equation

In this short appendix, for the convenience of the reader, we show the derivation of the viscoacoustic operator from the classical theory of viscoacousticity developed in
[
8
]
. Let
\sigma
and
u
respectively be a vector function and a scalar function of space and time, respectively indicating the time derivative of the particle velocity and pressure. We assume that there exists a linear relation between
\sigma
and
\nabla u
, and that
\sigma(t)
,
t\in[0,T]
depends on all the history of
\nabla u
up to time
t
. Then, by the Riesz representation theorem there exists a function
\widetilde{G}(x,t)
such that
\sigma
can be written as a Stieltjes integral:

\sigma(x,t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\nabla u(x,t-s)\,d\widetilde{G}(x,s).

Here
\widetilde{G}(x,t)
vanishes for all
t<0
. Assume that
\nabla u(x,t)
also vanishes for all negative times. Then we can write

\sigma(x,t)=\int_{0}^{t}\nabla u(x,t-s)\,d\widetilde{G}(x,s),

and using the relation between Stieltjes and Riemann integrals we have

\displaystyle\sigma(x,t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\nabla u(x,t-s)\widetilde{G}^{\prime}(x,s)ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\nabla u(x,t-s)\bigg(\frac{d}{ds}\widetilde{G}(x,s)+\widetilde{G}(x,s)\delta(s)\bigg)ds

\displaystyle=\widetilde{G}(x,0)\nabla u(x,t)+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{d\widetilde{G}}{ds}(x,s)\nabla u(x,t-s)ds

\displaystyle=\widetilde{G}(x,0)\nabla u(x,t)-\int_{0}^{t}\frac{d\widetilde{G}}{ds}(x,t-s)\nabla u(x,s)ds.

Thus using
\partial^{2}_{t}u=\nabla\cdot\sigma
we obtain the viscoacoustic equation

\partial^{2}_{t}u-\nabla\cdot(\widetilde{G}(x,0)\nabla u(x,t))+\int_{0}^{t}\nabla\cdot\bigg(\frac{d\widetilde{G}}{ds}(x,t-s)\nabla u(x,s)\bigg)ds=\partial^{2}_{t}u-\nabla\cdot\sigma=0.

In what follows we will let

c(x):=\widetilde{G}(x,0),\qquad G(x,t):=\frac{d\widetilde{G}}{dt}(x,t),

and we will write the equation as
Pu=0
, where
P
is the (scalar) viscoacoustic operator given by

Pu:=-\frac{1}{2}\left(\partial_{t}^{2}u-\nabla\cdot(c(x)\nabla u)+\int_{0}^{t}\nabla\cdot(G(x,t-s)\nabla u(x,s))\,ds\right).
