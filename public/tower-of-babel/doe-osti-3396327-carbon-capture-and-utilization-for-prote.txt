 Executive Summary
This project advanced an integrated open raceway algae cultivation and processing system to
engineering scale for carbon capture and utilization (CCU) from the flue gas of a naphtha-fired
power plant. This project is an important step in moving algae CCU from R&D to
commercialization through the engineering-scale integrated demonstration that includes Global
Algae’s radical innovations in every process step. The order-of-magnitude improvements from
these innovations are required make the algae products economically competitive in
commodities markets that are commensurate with utility and industrial scale CCU. This
implementation is for a utility flue gas containing 3.5% CO2 that is similar to flue gas from natural
gas power, so results will be directly usable for natural gas power and co-gen plants as well
petroleum-fired plants. As a result, companies have expressed strong interest in building algae
farms alongside datacenters to capture and utilize the CO2 from natural gas-fired power
generation to support the datacenter operations.
All the project objectives were achieved, and many were exceeded;
 1. Completed installation and field testing that demonstrates parameters listed in the state
 table for carbon capture and utilization efficiency; cultivation and processing; and
 product suitability.
 2. Economic and environmental benefits of the target products were quantified through
 techno-economic analyses (TEA) and life cycle analyses (LCA) informed by the field testing
 results that:
 a. Validated the net decrease in CO2 emissions; and
 b. Validated required selling price for the products with a carbon price of $0/ton.
 3. Achieved a 10% increase in peak or average algae productivity over the baseline in the
 state table (15% and 11% increases were achieved, respectively).
The engineering scale equipment was installed and successfully tested at the Kauai Algae Farm,
Fig. 1. A simplified block flow diagram for the integrated system is illustrated in Fig. 2. The
achieved carbon capture efficiency of 94% and utilization efficiency of 107% were much higher
than the Projected/Targeted values of 60% and 90%, respectively. The Projected/Targeted values
in the state table were achieved or exceeded for algae characteristics, algae cultivation, and CO 2
utilization, Table 1. The algae characteristics were consistent with the target products: (i) protein
concentrate as a food ingredient; (ii) monounsaturated fatty acids for polyurethane consumer
products; (iii) saturated fatty acids for use in soaps, detergents, cosmetics, lubricants, and food
processing; (iv) omega-3 fatty acids as a food and feed ingredient; (v) technical grade glycerin for
use in personal care, food, and manufacturing; and (vi) animal feed. Diatomaceous earth was
dropped because silica in diatom shells can be recycled to reduce overall process cost.
The life cycle analysis and techno-economic analysis and confirmed net decrease in CO 2
emissions of about 79%; and the required selling price for the products is less than the current
commodity prices, so the carbon price is $0/ton, i.e. no subsidies are required to make the
process economical.

 Engineering-scale integrated system
This project included integrated test operations from CO2 capture through product separation.
The Kauai Algae Farm is located adjacent to a naphtha-fired power plant, lower left of Fig. 3, on
page 4. A pipeline containing a slip stream from the power plant stack provides flue gas for use
at the farm. Four unit operations were upgraded in this project for the integrated engineering-
scale testing: the carbon dioxide absorber, the cultivation raceways, oil hydrolysis to fatty acids,
and oil winterization. The first two upgrades were made for higher fidelity to the planned scale-
up design so that key performance data could be generated for scale-up calculations and a more
accurate techno-economic analysis. The last two upgrades were made because this capability
was not available at the farm and a suitable vendor could not be found to perform the separation
at engineering scale. Design packages, procurement, installation, shakedown and testing were
completed for each of the unit operations; and all were used in the integrated testing.
Process Description
The CO2 is captured from flue gas in a counter-current packed bed CO2 absorber by converting
Na2CO3 to NaHCO3. The NaHCO3 solution is stored in a carbonated media tank until it is used in
algae cultivation. During algae cultivation, carbonated media and fertilizer are added to the
raceways to support the algae growth. In the raceways, algae fix carbon via photosynthesis and
convert NaHCO3 to Na2CO3. The algae are harvested from the cultivation media via the Zobi
harvester® and the crystal-clear recycled media is stored in a recycled media tank until it is
pumped to the packed bed absorber to capture CO2.
After cultivation in the raceways, the algae is harvested with a Zobi harvester®. The Zobi® system
achieves 100% capture efficiency and up to 20% solids slurry with very low energy input and
lower capital investment compared to conventional technology1. This enables full recycling of
the cultivation media without returning any of the bacteria, predators, or weed algae. The Zobi
harvester uses a hollow fiber membrane with a 40 nm pore size that has been used in wastewater
for a throughput of up to 300 million gallons per day.
After harvesting, the algae is fractionated into products. The baseline approaches did not work
well in the integrated testing. Resources were not allocated in this project to develop an
alternative, so the high lipid biomass produced in the integrated testing was provided to other
projects that were developing extraction and processing unit operations. In the end,
approximately 10 liters of oil was extracted and processed using engineering-scale conventional
technology and additional oil was extracted using novel approaches, including the engineering-
scale combined algal processing system at the National Laboratory of the Rockies. Based on the
results of these tests, a new baseline process was developed as described below.

A simplified block flow diagram of the system is illustrated Fig. 3. The system includes packed
bed absorber columns with demisters and intercolumn connections; media storage, pumping
and flow measurements; media sampling and analysis; KIUC flue gas supply and flow
measurements; and gas sampling and analysis. Fig. 4 provides a photograph of the installed
absorber system. Fig. 5 provides a simplified process flow diagram indicating the measurement
points and sample ports. The 6-inch diameter columns each contain 33 feet of packing, so the
total packed bed height is 66 feet. The media is fed to the top of the columns with metering
pumps. The flue gas is drawn through the columns from the power plant stack with a fan that
also returns the carbon dioxide lean gas back to the stack. The carbon dioxide concentration in
the power plant flue gas was approximately 3.5% by volume. The total gas phase pressure drop
for the system is approximately 2.5 inches of water.
A series of parametric tests were conducted to validate the mass transfer model and
demonstrate at least 70% carbon capture efficiency. These tests confirmed that the absorber
system performance matches carbonation mass transfer model predictions developed from prior
data with a smaller absorber system. Inlet flue gas CO2 concentration varied between 3.4 and 4.6
% by volume depending on the operating conditions at the power plant. Flue gas flow rate varied
between 3.2 and 11.9 scfm. Inlet media pH varied between 9.8 and 10.2. Media flow rate varied
between 5 and 10 gpm/ft2. The following absorber system charts, Fig. 6-10, show the predicted
carbonation performance for the specific media and flue gas inlet flows/conditions along with
measured results.

The mass transfer model was used to generate over 200 cases for packing height as function of
pH, temperature, molarity, liquid loading, gas loading, flue gas CO 2 concentration. These cases
were then used to generate 2nd degree polynomial least squares fit for all variables. The resulting
empirical equation is used in the TEA to size the absorber system including packing height,
column diameter, and gas pressure drop as a function of the input parameters. This greatly
improved the fidelity of the scale-up design and cost estimation in the TEA.
The engineering-scale absorber system is fully integrated into the farm operations as illustrated
in Fig. 11. Photographs of the recycled media and carbonated media bladder tanks are presented
in Fig. 12. The absorber system was used to supply all carbonated media from power plant flue
for the engineering-scale integrated tests. The average capture efficiency exceeded 90% for all
the engineering-scale integrated tests.
A two-week preliminary test was conducted to verify that using carbonated media from the flue
gas and the absorber system does not impact productivity. One raceway was operated on
carbonated media from the absorber system, Batch 1214, and one raceway was operated on
carbonated media prepared using purchase sodium bicarbonate, Batch 1216. The productivity
results are illustrated in Fig. 13. The average productivity of the two batches over the two-week

Oil Extraction
The plan for this project was to utilize an oil extraction process developed on other projects, so
no resources were allocated to develop oil extraction. Unfortunately, planned extraction process
did not work well, so alternatives had to be utilized. To obtain oil for fractionation tests, algae oil
was extracted at a vender site using conventional solvent extraction with hexane and ethanol.
Approximately 10 liters of algae oil were obtained using conventional solvent extraction - 5 liters
with hexane and 5 liters with ethanol. This oil was used in the fractionation tests described in the
next section.
Additionally, high lipid biomass produced on the project was sent to the National Laboratory of
the Rockies for testing in their engineering-scale combined algal processing extraction system.
These tests were successful. Also, high lipid biomass was used in two other projects at Global
Algae Innovations developing novel extraction processes. Multiple new unit operations were
developed on the other projects. A new baseline for oil extraction process was developed. This
process was further evaluated and used in the TEA and LCA for this project. Two invention
disclosures were submitted covering two portions of the new process.

Oil fractionation
The objective of the oil fractionation is to split the oil into an omega-3 rich stream, a C16:1 mono-
unsaturated rich fraction, and saturated rich fraction. The Nitzschia inconspicua oil fatty acid
profile is presented in Table 2. The di-and triglycerides that make up the oil contain a mixture of
fatty acid chains, so they must be hydrolyzed or transesterified before the individual fatty acids
can be separated. The baseline approach was to use subcritical water hydrolysis to hydrolyze the
oil into free fatty acids. At commercial scale this is typically performed in a continuous reactor
with glycerin and fatty acid outlet streams at discharged at opposite ends of a counter-current
hydrolysis reactor. For the engineering-scale tests at 2-gallon Parr reactor was installed to
perform the hydrolysis in batch mode to simulate the commercial continuous flow reactors, Fig.
22. The process conditions for hydrolysis were determined in shakedown tests with mixture of
palm oil and fish oil to simulate the Nitzschia inconspicua oil composition. Then approximately
2.5 liters of hexane extracted algae oil was hydrolyzed as well as 2.5 liters of ethanol extracted
algae oil.

Then the fatty acid mixture is vacuum distilled into an omega-3 rich fraction (C20 and above) and
a lower molecular weight fraction (C14 to C18). The difference in boiling point between C18 and

 20

C20 is about 25C, Fig. 23, so the separation is straight forward in a fractional distillation column.
The fatty acid mixtures from the subcritical hydrolysis were distilled by Pope Scientific, Fig. 24.

 Fig. 23. Boiling point curves for fatty acids based on number of carbon atoms

After fractional distillation, the low molecular weight portion is separated into saturated and
unsaturated fractions by winterization. Because the C16:0 and C14:0 are solid above 50C and the
C16:1 unsaturated fatty acid is liquid above about 30C, Fig. 25, they can be separated by cooling
to about 40C and letting the saturated fatty acids precipitate as a solid. Then the saturated fatty
acids are removed via filtration. An engineering-scale winterization system was designed and
installed to perform this separation, Fig. 26. The system shakedown was performed with palm
oil since it has large fractions of palmitic (C16:0) and palmitoleic (C16:1) fatty acids. The saturated
and unsaturated fatty acids were readily separated, Fig. 27.
Two liters of the remaining algae oil was converted to fatty acids via the saponification and
acidification route. In this process, the triglycerides are first hydrolyzed to a soap using a strong
base. Then the soap is neutralized to pH 7 forming a salt and fatty acids, which precipitate from
the aqueous solution. Approximately 1 liter of fatty acids were prepared using this method. The
fatty acid mixture is solid at room temperature, but melts at about 25-30C, Fig. 28. These fatty
acids were sent to Pope Scientific for fractional distillation, but the project ended before results
could be obtained.

 Techno-economic and life cycle analyses
Global Algae has a detailed process-wide Excel-based TEA and LCA model that was used to
guide all R&D so that the resultant integrated algal system is scalable and leads to economic
viability for algae products. The model was updated with results from the engineering-scale
integrated tests and modified to incorporate the changes in the extraction and fractionation

 24

processes. The LCA model estimated a 79% reduction in greenhouse gas for the process. The
TEA results validated that the process is projected to economical without subsidies for CO 2
capture or greenhouse gas reductions. Fig. 29 illustrates the estimated minimum selling price of
$1045/mt of algae products based on an 8% internal rate of return. Table 3 illustrates the
estimated selling price of the algae products of $1328/ mt based on parity with other
commodities. These results indicate there is still a good profit margin on the product selling
price.
