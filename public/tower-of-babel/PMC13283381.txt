Gas Vesicles and Acoustic Protein Nanostructures in Molecular Ultrasound Nanomedicine: Translational Archetypes, Biomaterial Design, and Barriers to Clinical Realization
Authors: Ailin Cui, Yaoqiang Du, Litao Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUltrasound is clinically established, portable, and repeatable, but its dominant contrast logic remains anatomical and perfusion-based. Gas vesicles (GVs), acoustic reporter genes (ARGs), targeted GV derivatives, and GV-containing release materials have introduced a protein-based route toward molecular ultrasound nanomedicine. Their translation, however, cannot be inferred from acoustic detectability alone. This Review examines GVs and related acoustic protein nanostructures as biomaterial and nanomedicine systems. We distinguish clinical microbubbles, purified GVs, exogenous GV-labeled cells, intracellular ARG systems, GV-based release materials, and prospective computationally designed acoustic protein nanostructures. These formats differ in biological setting, route of administration, pharmacologic burden, and evidentiary maturity. We define computational molecular sonography as a bounded organizing framework that links reporter design, acoustic signal representation, biological fate, exposure estimation, and clinician-governed safety. The strongest current evidence comes from natural and engineered GV systems, including pressure-dependent collapse, nonlinear detection, gas vesicle protein C (GvpC)-mediated surface engineering, blood-component interactions, and reporter-gene imaging. By contrast, fully de novo acoustic protein nanostructures remain prospective rather than in vivo-ready. The major translational barriers include shell mechanics and stability, surface corona and immune clearance, repeat-dose safety, toxicology, manufacturing consistency, critical quality attributes, and Sim-to-Real uncertainty in tissue and acoustic fields. Future clinical value will require standardized biomaterial characterization, direct acoustic phenotyping, reporter-sensitive acquisition, and regulatory-grade control of product and exposure. Ultrasound visibility is therefore necessary but insufficient; translation will depend on aligning material design, biological fate, signal interpretation, and safety as a coupled problem.

Introduction

Ultrasound is one of the most widely used imaging modalities in clinical medicine because it is real-time, portable, relatively inexpensive, and broadly accessible. In most clinical settings, however, it remains a method for depicting anatomy, perfusion, and gross tissue behavior. It usually does not resolve molecular state directly. A lesion can be localized and its vascularity mapped, but the same examination often cannot determine which molecular program is active, whether an engineered cell process is present, or whether a therapeutic payload has reached its intended site.

This limitation has become more important as nanoscale imaging and therapeutic systems have advanced. Nanomedicine could, in principle, help ultrasound move beyond anatomical depiction toward molecular sensing, targeted readout, and localized actuation. In practice, that transition is constrained by material fate, signal representation, acoustic-field uncertainty, and safety. A nanoscale reporter that is detectable in a phantom is not automatically a clinically viable imaging agent.

Clinical microbubble contrast agents narrowed part of the historical gap by enabling vascular contrast imaging, targeted preclinical imaging, and ultrasound-assisted delivery strategies.1–3 Their clinical maturity is real, but so is their compartmental constraint. Conventional microbubbles are generally micron-scale and remain largely intravascular. They therefore provide limited access to extravascular molecular targets and intracellular reporter biology.

Gas vesicles (GVs) are different. GVs are gas-filled protein nanostructures naturally produced by microorganisms. They exclude liquid water while allowing gas diffusion, and they can scatter ultrasound despite nanoscale dimensions.4–7 Subsequent acoustic reporter gene (ARG) systems showed that GV-family gene clusters can be used as genetically encoded ultrasound reporters in microorganisms, mammalian cells, and more recently stem-cell-compatible systems.8–13 These advances introduced a protein-based acoustic reporter platform that is materially distinct from conventional microbubbles.

The field should not be described as a simple, continuous maturation pathway. Clinical microbubbles, purified GVs, exogenous GV-labeled cells, intracellular ARG systems, GV-based composite release materials, and computational design workflows are often grouped under “molecular ultrasound”. That grouping is convenient but imprecise. These formats differ in size, route of administration, biological setting, pharmacologic burden, and evidence base. Evidence generated in one archetype cannot be transferred wholesale to another.

The same problem becomes sharper as computational design enters the field. ProteinMPNN (a protein-sequence design tool based on message-passing neural networks) and RFdiffusion (RoseTTAFold Diffusion, a diffusion-based framework for de novo protein-backbone generation) now support sequence design and backbone generation at scales that were previously impractical.14,15 This does not mean that in vivo-capable acoustic protein nanostructures can now be designed on demand. Structural plausibility, biological expression, shell assembly, acoustic nonlinearity, pharmacokinetics, and safety remain separable requirements. Current benchmark evidence in de novo protein design supports computational filtering, but not reliable prediction of experimental success.16,17

This Review addresses a specific problem: how to evaluate acoustic protein nanostructures as translational nanomedicine systems without conflating materially different platforms or overextending computational and systems-engineering concepts. We use the term computational molecular sonography as an organizing framework, not as an established field label. In this Review, it means the coupled design, acquisition, and interpretation of engineered acoustic reporters when molecular information depends on pressure-dependent acoustic phenotype, reporter biodistribution, and local acoustic exposure.

This scope is narrower than “molecular ultrasound”, which broadly includes molecularly targeted ultrasound imaging. It is also narrower than “ultrasound theranostics”, which includes many image-guided therapeutic approaches not based on protein reporters. It differs from ARG imaging, which is one reporter modality within the framework. It also differs from digital-twin medicine, which is a wider patient-modeling paradigm. Foundation models, federated learning, digital twins, and clinician-in-the-loop control are discussed here only where they affect reporter-sensitive inference, exposure estimation, or bounded decision support. They are not treated as independent topics.

The central argument is therefore practical rather than speculative. Acoustic protein nanostructures are promising, but their clinical value will depend on whether biomaterial design, biological fate, acoustic signal representation, manufacturing control, and safety can be aligned. The following sections separate the major translational archetypes, review structural and surface-engineering evidence, define representation limits, assess generative design and acoustic phenotyping, and outline the biomaterial, chemistry, manufacturing, and controls (CMC), regulatory, and Sim-to-Real barriers that must be solved before clinical realization. Distinct translational archetypes in molecular ultrasound nanomedicine.

The most mature acoustic contrast platform remains the clinical microbubble. Microbubbles have an established role in contrast-enhanced vascular imaging and have supported targeted imaging and ultrasound-assisted delivery.1,2 Their limitation is not weak acoustic performance. It is restricted biological access. Because conventional microbubbles are larger than most endothelial gaps, they are largely confined to the vascular compartment. Molecularly targeted microbubbles can interrogate vascular markers, but they do not by themselves solve extravascular delivery or intracellular reporting.

Purified GVs represent a different translational object. Relative to microbubbles, they offer nanoscale dimensions, genetically encoded composition, tunable collapse behavior, and a shell architecture that can be engineered at the protein level.4–7 Their relevance lies not only in size, but also in acoustic mechanics. Pressure-dependent collapse, harmonic scattering, and shell-structure dependence allow GV signals to be used as reporter phenotypes rather than only passive contrast.

Bicone particles extend the purified-GV class toward an ultrasmall format.18 These sub-80 nm acoustic biomolecules were reported to be detectable in vitro and in vivo, to accumulate in tumor models, and to support ultrasound-induced inertial cavitation under experimental conditions. They remain preclinical, but they sharpen an important distinction: smaller size may improve transport and tissue access, while also creating new requirements for dose, biodistribution, acoustic potency, and clearance.

A related but distinct direction uses purified GVs as exogenous labels for therapeutic cells. In this format, the relevant question is not whether a circulating nanoscale particle can reach a lesion. The question is whether GV loading preserves cell viability, phenotype, trafficking, immune compatibility, and therapeutic function. GV-assisted ultrasound imaging of chimeric antigen receptor T cell (CAR-T) antitumor efficacy in mice illustrates this archetype.19 It should not be interpreted as equivalent to systemic free-particle use.

ARG systems occupy another translational axis. Here the object is not an injected particle, but a genetically encoded intracellular reporter. Once the reporter is produced inside the cell, the dominant constraints change. Instead of circulation time and mononuclear phagocyte system (MPS) clearance, the relevant issues are gene delivery, copy number, expression burden, shell assembly, host compatibility, retention, immune consequences of heterologous proteins, and reporter-sensitive ultrasound acquisition.8–13

GV-containing release materials, including targeted acoustic percolation switches (TAPS), define another category again.20 These systems are neither vascular microbubble agents nor intracellular reporters. They are composite biomaterials in which intact GVs both confer ultrasound visibility and restrict diffusional release. Ultrasound-triggered GV collapse can then open percolation channels and release payloads. Their burden of proof is therefore material-specific: imaging, release kinetics, local compatibility, payload stability, and organ-specific delivery must be validated together.

Computationally designed acoustic protein nanostructures remain more prospective. ProteinMPNN and RFdiffusion broaden the upstream design space,14,15 and acoustic plate-reader screening provides an experimental route for selecting pressure-responsive variants.11 What remains unresolved is whether fully de novo designs can reliably express, assemble, remain stable, generate useful nonlinear acoustic signals, and survive in vivo. At present, this class should be treated as a design frontier, not as a mature translational format.

These distinctions are not semantic. They determine the relevant assays, claims, and failure modes. Clinical maturity currently belongs to microbubbles. Strong preclinical experimental evidence belongs to natural and engineered GV-family systems. Prospective design logic belongs to computational workflows. A rigorous review must keep these evidentiary levels separate.

Biomaterial Architecture, Mechanical Stability, and Surface Engineering

Shell Architecture and Mechanical Acoustic Phenotype

GVs are not simply small gas bubbles. Their acoustic behavior is controlled by a protein shell whose architecture sets the relationship among size, collapse pressure, nonlinear response, and stability. Early molecular ultrasound work reported GV widths of approximately 45–250 nm and lengths of approximately 100–600 nm, with a protein shell that excludes water while permitting gas exchange.4 Molecular engineering studies further described GVs as protein-shelled nanostructures whose dimensions and collapse thresholds vary by genetic origin.5

In the Anabaena flos-aquae (Ana) system, gas vesicle protein A (GvpA) is the principal shell protein and forms ribs around the gas compartment, while gas vesicle protein C (GvpC) binds externally and reinforces the shell.5–7 Cryogenic electron tomography (cryo-ET) of Anabaena GVs resolved a corrugated shell with GvpA ribs and GvpC density, while cryogenic electron microscopy (cryo-EM) and structural modeling in related systems clarified the helical wall assembly.6,7 These studies provide direct structural support for the view that shell mechanics, rather than gas content alone, controls acoustic phenotype.

GvpC provides an experimentally tractable link between structure and mechanics. Removal or truncation of GvpC reduces the pressure required to collapse the shell. In engineered Anabaena GV variants, the collapse midpoint was reported as approximately 195.3 kPa for DeltaGvpC, 374.3 kPa for a truncated DeltaN&C GvpC variant, and 569.9 kPa for a GvpCWT-like variant.5 These values demonstrate that collapse pressure can be tuned over hundreds of kilopascals by modifying shell reinforcement. Earlier species-comparison data also showed distinct collapse ranges, including lower thresholds for Halobacterium (Halo) GVs and higher thresholds for Ana GVs.4

Cryo-ET/cryo-EM data refine this interpretation. Structural studies reported that the GV shell is only a few nanometers thick, with a corrugated wall and gas-permeable pores.6,7 Binding of GvpC can increase effective wall thickness from roughly 3 nm to roughly 4 nm in the structural model, and thin-shell mechanics predicts that buckling pressure is strongly sensitive to wall thickness.7 Finite-element shell models further support the idea that GvpC density and shell geometry influence buckling behavior.6

A limitation should be stated explicitly. The GV literature usually reports operational collapse or buckling pressure rather than direct material constants such as Young’s modulus, bending modulus, or fracture energy. These pressure thresholds are valuable because they are directly tied to ultrasound function, but they are not a complete mechanical characterization. Translation would benefit from standardized mechanical testing that connects cryo-EM/cryo-ET geometry, shell thickness, GvpC occupancy, collapse-pressure curves, and acoustic signal decay under repeated insonation.

Chemical, Enzymatic, and Storage Stability

Physical stability is one of the main reasons GVs are attractive as nanoscale acoustic reporters. Unlike conventional bubbles, GVs do not rely on trapping preloaded gas under an unstable Laplace-pressure condition. Their protein shells permit gas exchange while excluding water, which allows stable nanoscale gas compartments.4,5 Structural work has reported that purified GVs can remain stable for years at cool or ambient temperature.6

The shell also tolerates some harsh processing conditions. In the Anabaena engineering platform, native GvpC was removed with 6 M urea while leaving the GvpA-based shell intact, after which engineered GvpC variants were re-added.5 This result supports modular surface and mechanical engineering, but it should not be overinterpreted as a complete chemical-stability package. Urea tolerance in a purification workflow does not establish stability across all clinically relevant pH, temperature, enzymatic, serum, freeze-thaw, agitation, lyophilization, and long-term storage conditions.

For clinical translation, stability should be treated as a critical quality attribute rather than a descriptive property. Minimum characterization should include size distribution, optical density or gas-volume proxy, zeta potential, aggregation state, intact-shell fraction, collapse-pressure midpoint, acoustic potency, repeated-insonation durability, residual host-cell proteins, endotoxin or pyrogen burden, and sterility before and after storage. For intracellular ARG systems, the analogous stability problem shifts to genetic stability, expression control, host viability, and persistence of acoustic signal after cell division or differentiation.8–13,21

Direct pH-, temperature-, and enzyme-tolerance data remain sparse compared with the acoustic literature. This gap matters because clinical manufacturing and distribution require more than short-term buffer stability. Future studies should report storage buffer, concentration, temperature, container, freeze-thaw exposure, agitation, time, sterility strategy, and post-storage acoustic performance. Without these variables, comparisons across GV sources and engineered variants remain difficult.

Surface Engineering, Targeting, and Protein Corona

Surface engineering is a dominant determinant of nanomaterial fate. For GVs, the surface is not an inert coating. It is a protein shell that can bind plasma proteins, cells, antibodies, and engineered ligands. GvpC is especially important because it can function as a modular engineering handle. Fusion of a lysine-rich protein to GvpC increased zeta potential by approximately 28 ± 4 mV relative to GvpCWT, demonstrating that shell charge can be tuned genetically.5

GvpC engineering has also been used for targeting and cellular interaction control. RGD-displaying GVs showed increased binding to integrin-overexpressing U87 glioblastoma cells compared with controls, while GvpC fusions with a CD47-derived “self” peptide reduced uptake by RAW 264.7 macrophages and polyarginine R8 increased uptake.5 SpyTag-SpyCatcher chemistry further enabled modular covalent functionalization, with approximately 1,000 SpyTag functionalities reported per modified GV.5 These studies show that the GV surface can be engineered, but they do not establish a single optimal stealth or targeting strategy.

Recent targeted-GV work extends this concept. Alpha(v)beta(3)-targeted arginine-glycine-aspartic acid-modified Halobacterium gas vesicles (RGD-hGVs) were reported to have particle sizes near 240 nm, less negative zeta potential than native Halobacterium GVs, targeted tumor-cell binding, and prolonged tumor ultrasound contrast compared with non-targeted controls in osteosarcoma and melanoma models.22 These data support the feasibility of targeted acoustic nanoprobes, but they remain preclinical. They also show that targeting changes physicochemical attributes and should therefore be characterized together with acoustic potency and biological fate.

Blood interactions are not a peripheral issue. Ling et al showed that erythrocytes and serum proteins bind to GVs and shape acoustic response, circulation time, and immunogenicity.23 In their study, unmodified GVs produced a dual-peak hemodynamic ultrasound signal after intravenous injection. Surface polyethylene glycol (PEG) modification reduced erythrocyte binding, neutralized zeta potential, increased apparent circulation half-life from approximately 560 ± 51s to 840 ± 56s, and altered the protein corona.23 These findings indicate that the protein corona can change both biological fate and acoustic performance.

PEGylation is useful but not immunologically neutral. In one Halobacterium NRC-1 gas vesicle formulation, 5 kDa methoxy-poly(ethylene glycol)-amine (mPEG-amine) coupled by 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)carbodiimide/N-hydroxysuccinimide (EDC/NHS) amidation reduced macrophage uptake and immunogenicity and prolonged in vivo tumor contrast relative to unmodified GVs.24 This evidence should not be conflated with the Ana-PEG repeat-dose results, because the latter used Anabaena GVs modified with 10 kDa mPEG-azide through copper-catalyzed azide-alkyne cycloaddition (CuAAC) chemistry.23 However, anti-PEG immunoglobulin M (IgM) and immunoglobulin G (IgG), accelerated blood clearance, and hypersensitivity reactions are recognized problems in the broader PEGylated nanomedicine literature.25,26 In the GV-blood interaction study, the second Ana-PEG dose in a repeat-dose experiment was followed by acute hypotension within minutes of injection in some mice, and several Ana-PEG-dosed mice failed to recover after the second injection, whereas repeated administration of unmodified Ana GVs was well tolerated in the tested animals.23 PEG should therefore be treated as a context-dependent surface strategy rather than a universal solution.

Other surface strategies require careful framing. Zwitterionic polymers, polysarcosine, glycosylation, antibody or peptide conjugation, and cell-membrane coating are common or emerging approaches in nanomedicine.27,28 For GVs, direct comparative evidence across these strategies is still limited. The safest conclusion is that such approaches are plausible design options whose effects on shell integrity, collapse pressure, protein corona, macrophage uptake, complement activation, acoustic signal, and manufacturability must be measured empirically.

Representation Limits and Reporter-Sensitive Inference

The previous sections address the material. A separate problem concerns representation: what part of the ultrasound signal is preserved for inference. This Review discusses ultrasound artificial intelligence (AI) only in this restricted sense. The issue is not whether foundation models are useful for clinical ultrasound. They are. The issue is whether conventional image-space representations preserve weak, pressure-dependent, and transient reporter signals from acoustic protein nanostructures.

Ultrasound foundation models (USFMs) have improved performance across conventional clinical tasks, including classification, segmentation, enhancement, and workflow applications across organs and acquisition settings.29,30 These studies establish broad image-space generalization for standard ultrasound products. They do not by themselves establish molecular decoding for GV or ARG signals. A model trained mainly on B-mode and Doppler semantic structure may become robust to the very acquisition variability that contains reporter-specific acoustic information.

Delay-and-sum (DAS) beamforming remains dominant because it is simple, efficient, and compatible with real-time imaging. Its performance, however, depends on assumptions about sound speed, aperture, f-number, aberration, and tissue homogeneity.31,32 These parameters affect point-spread function, contrast, phase coherence, speckle statistics, and apparent signal amplitude. For acoustic reporters whose function depends on pressure-dependent nonlinearity or collapse, upstream reconstruction is not neutral.

Radiofrequency (RF)-, in-phase/quadrature (I/Q)-, and channel-domain approaches support this point. Deep learning beamformers operating on raw channel or RF data can improve image reconstruction under limited or variable acquisition conditions.33–36 These studies were not designed as molecular ultrasound decoders, but they show that informative signal structure can exist before final display-image formation. For GV systems, reporter-sensitive inference may require RF-native, I/Q-native, pressure-sweep, or beamforming-aware features rather than only postprocessed B-mode pixels.

GV-specific imaging methods make this dependence explicit. Harmonic imaging and cross-amplitude modulation (xAM) improve nonlinear detection of acoustic biomolecules by isolating GV-derived signals from tissue background.37 Amplitude-modulated singular value decomposition (AM-SVD) was developed for ultrafast GV imaging and demonstrates that temporal and modulation-domain structure can help separate reporter signal from background.38 These methods support the view that molecular ultrasound may require signal-processing pipelines matched to reporter physics.

Federated learning should be interpreted with the same caution. Privacy-preserving and federated frameworks are valuable for multi-institutional ultrasound AI, and recent work suggests that collaborative pretraining can improve generalization while respecting data governance.30,39,40 For molecular ultrasound, however, harmonization across institutions could either preserve or attenuate weak reporter signatures. This is an empirical question. Federated scale is not a substitute for reporter-specific validation.

Uncertainty quantification also needs reporter-specific meaning. Bayesian approximations, Monte Carlo dropout, and ensemble-based methods are increasingly used in medical image analysis, and recent ultrasound segmentation work shows that uncertainty-aware models can improve interpretability and trustworthiness.41,42 For acoustic protein nanostructures, uncertainty should not only refer to segmentation confidence. It should also cover local pressure estimation, acoustic exposure, signal-to-background separation, collapse-state inference, biological localization, and out-of-distribution acquisition conditions.

The conclusion is not that current ultrasound AI is unsuitable It is that image-space success for conventional ultrasound should not be treated as proof of molecular-readout validity. Reporter-sensitive inference will require explicit access to the signals on which GV and ARG function depends, together with uncertainty estimates that are tied to acoustic field, material state, and biological deployment.

Generative Design and Acoustic Phenotyping

The strongest experimental evidence in molecular ultrasound still comes from natural or evolution-derived GV systems and engineered variants built on that substrate. Fully de novo acoustic protein nanostructures remain a prospective frontier. This distinction is essential. Natural GV-family systems have already shown pressure-dependent collapse, harmonic detection, multiplexing, ARG expression, and surface engineering.4–13,18,23 De novo design tools have not yet produced a general class of in vivo-validated acoustic protein reporters.

Computational design is still relevant. ProteinMPNN supports robust sequence design for protein backbones, and RFdiffusion enables de novo backbone generation and functional protein design.14,15 These tools expand the search space for future acoustic nanostructures. They may help generate shell motifs, scaffolds, or protein assemblies that can be screened for acoustic function. But their outputs are structural proposals, not finished reporters.

Benchmark data justify caution. In a dataset of 614 experimentally characterized de novo designed monomers, only 269 designs, or 43%, met all experimental success criteria, including expression, solubility, monomeric state, and correct secondary structure.16 Predictive metrics from AlphaFold-class predictors, ESMFold, and ProteinMPNN showed only moderate ability to discriminate success from failure.16 This matters for molecular ultrasound because acoustic function adds further requirements beyond expression and folding.

For acoustic protein nanostructures, function depends on shell geometry, gas exclusion, gas permeability, mechanical stability, collapse threshold, nonlinear response, aggregation behavior, and biological compatibility. A design with a plausible backbone could still fail because it does not assemble into a gas-filled shell, cannot exclude water, collapses outside the usable pressure window, aggregates in serum, or loses signal after expression in a host cell. Structural confidence is therefore an incomplete surrogate.

Reporter engineering provides the clearest experimental path at present. GvpC modification tuned collapse pressure and harmonic response.5 Directed evolution of ARGs used high-throughput acoustic screening to select variants with improved or shifted acoustic behavior.11 Multiplexed ultrasound imaging of gene expression used pressure-coded reporters to separate signals from engineered variants.12 These studies show that acoustic phenotype can be engineered and selected, but they do so by measuring acoustic behavior directly.

Acoustic phenotyping is therefore not a downstream convenience. It is the experimental bridge between protein design and ultrasound function. Minimum phenotyping should include pressure-dependent signal curves, collapse midpoint and slope, nonlinear or harmonic response, repeated-insonation durability, concentration-response behavior, temperature and serum conditions, host-cell context for ARGs, and post-acoustic structural verification. Where possible, acoustic readouts should be linked to cryo-EM/cryo-ET, transmission electron microscopy (TEM), dynamic light scattering (DLS), zeta potential, and protein composition.

TAPS illustrates a useful boundary case. It shows that GV-containing materials can support ultrasound-visible, mechanically triggered release in a composite hydrogel system.20 This is important evidence for actuation-coupled function. It is not evidence that generative protein design has already produced in vivo-ready acoustic nanostructures. It is a material system that uses existing GV properties for local release.

The practical conclusion is straightforward. Generative methods have expanded candidate generation. Structural biology has clarified GV mechanics. Reporter engineering has demonstrated programmable acoustic response. What remains unresolved is yield: how often a plausible protein design becomes a stable, biologically compatible, acoustically useful reporter. Until that yield is quantified, de novo acoustic protein nanostructures should be described as a research frontier rather than a near-term clinical platform. These barriers do not apply uniformly across acoustic protein systems. Purified injected GVs face systemic pharmacokinetic and immunological barriers. Intracellular ARGs face gene-delivery, host-expression, and cell-engineering barriers. GV-containing release materials face local biomaterial and payload-release barriers. Computationally designed systems face all of these plus functional-yield uncertainty. Biodistribution, Protein Corona, and MPS Clearance

The first barrier is biological fate. Ling et al provided systematic evidence that erythrocytes and serum proteins bind to purified GVs and influence acoustic response, circulation behavior, and immunogenicity.23 This finding is central because it shows that the same biological interactions that govern nanoparticle distribution can also alter the ultrasound signal itself.

The broader nanoparticle literature explains why this matters. Biodistribution is shaped by nominal size and composition, but also by plasma-protein adsorption, opsonization, uptake by the MPS, endothelial transport, tumor interstitial fluid pressure (IFP), and extracellular-matrix density.27,45–47 Protein corona formation can mask targeting ligands, create new biological identities, alter macrophage recognition, and change organ accumulation. For GVs, these mechanisms are not theoretical; direct evidence shows serum-protein binding and altered acoustic output.23

PEGylation remains a common strategy to extend circulation and reduce macrophage recognition, but it should be interpreted as formulation-specific rather than intrinsically protective. In Halobacterium NRC-1 GVs, 5 kDa mPEG-amine coupled through EDC/NHS amidation reduced macrophage uptake, attenuated immune readouts, and improved tumor imaging.24 In Anabaena GVs, 10 kDa mPEG-azide coupled by CuAAC reduced erythrocyte binding and increased apparent circulation half-life.23 These beneficial effects must be balanced against anti-PEG antibodies, accelerated blood clearance, and hypersensitivity reactions documented for PEGylated nanobiopharmaceuticals.25,26 Repeat dosing should therefore be evaluated explicitly for each GV scaffold, PEG architecture, linker chemistry, and administration schedule rather than assumed safe.

Alternatives to PEG deserve study but should not be oversold. Polysarcosine has emerged as a potential PEG alternative in biomedical applications.28 Zwitterionic surfaces and cell-membrane camouflage can reduce protein adsorption in some nanoparticle systems.27 Antibody, peptide, glycan, and protein-corona engineering may improve targeting or immune modulation. For GV systems, however, each surface strategy must be evaluated for its effects on shell mechanics, collapse pressure, acoustic potency, serum aggregation, and manufacturing consistency.

Safety Evaluation: Immunogenicity, Complement, Repeat Dosing, Toxicity, and Clearance

Safety evaluation for GVs should be systematic. GVs are composed of prokaryotic proteins, and this matters biologically. Biosynthetic GVs from Halobacterium NRC-1 induced stronger immune activation than PEG-modified GVs in reported in vitro and in vivo assays, while PEG modification reduced macrophage uptake and immunogenic readouts.24 These results support surface modification as a safety strategy, but they also confirm that immune recognition is a core issue.

Repeat-dose safety is especially important. The GV-blood interaction study reported that repeated injections of unmodified Ana GVs were well tolerated in the tested animals despite antibody-associated acceleration of clearance. By contrast, the second Ana-PEG dose in repeat-dose experiments was followed by acute hypotension within minutes of injection in some mice, and several Ana-PEG-dosed mice failed to recover after the second injection.23 This pattern argues against relying on single-dose tolerability or assuming that PEGylation is universally protective. A translational program should include anti-GV antibodies, anti-PEG or anti-surface-polymer antibodies, complement activation, cytokines, organ histology, hematology, coagulation, and clinical chemistry after repeat dosing.

Acute and chronic toxicity need to be separated. Several preclinical studies report limited acute cytotoxicity, hemolysis, histological injury, or liver and kidney marker abnormalities for specific GV preparations.22,24 These data are useful but incomplete. They do not define long-term degradation, tissue persistence, delayed immune effects, reproductive or developmental risk, or safety after repeated exposure. Purified injected particles, ex vivo cell labels, intracellular ARGs, and local release materials require different toxicology packages.

Genotoxicity is also route-dependent. Purified GVs are protein nanoparticles and do not inherently imply genome integration. Intracellular ARG systems, however, require genetic delivery or stable cell engineering. Their risk assessment should include vector design, insertional mutagenesis where relevant, transgene stability, off-target editing if genome editing is used, promoter leakiness, expression burden, and consequences of long-term heterologous protein production.8–13,21 For therapeutic cell applications, these questions should be integrated with standard cell-product release testing.

Clearance and degradation remain insufficiently defined. Liver and spleen accumulation, macrophage uptake, biliary or intestinal elimination, renal exclusion by size, proteolytic degradation, and persistence of collapsed shell fragments should be quantified. For local GV-containing hydrogels, the degradation of the matrix, payload, and GV debris must be evaluated together.20 For ARGs, the relevant clearance question may instead be loss of engineered cells, silencing of the reporter, or immune elimination of reporter-expressing cells.

Manufacturing, CMC, Batch Consistency, and Regulatory-Grade Standardization

Manufacturing is not a downstream administrative issue. It is part of the translational problem. Current GV production depends on biological expression systems, either native producers or heterologous hosts. Yield, host viability, shell assembly, residual impurities, and acoustic potency can all depend on expression conditions. Temporal control of GV gene expression improved host viability in bacterial systems, underscoring that production conditions and biological burden are tightly coupled.21

A practical CMC framework should begin with a quality target product profile and a set of critical quality attributes (CQAs). For injected purified GVs, likely critical attributes include identity of the GV source and engineered sequence, particle morphology, hydrodynamic size distribution, intact-shell fraction, zeta potential, aggregation, gas content or optical-density proxy, collapse-pressure midpoint, acoustic potency, residual host-cell proteins and deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA), endotoxin or pyrogen burden, sterility, serum stability, and storage stability. For ARG cell products, additional attributes include transgene copy number, integration site or vector persistence, expression leakiness, cell viability, differentiation state, and acoustic signal per viable cell.

United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) guidance for nanomaterial-containing human drug and biological products emphasizes that nanomaterials may require particular examination of quality, nonclinical, and clinical attributes because their properties may differ from non-nanomaterial products.43 International Council for Harmonisation of Technical Requirements for Pharmaceuticals for Human Use (ICH) quality frameworks likewise emphasize development of predefined quality criteria, critical quality attributes, design space, manufacturing process, control strategy, and lifecycle quality risk management.44 These principles are directly relevant to GVs even though no GV product has yet reached routine clinical use.

Standardization should include acoustic assays, not only chemical assays. A GV batch that has the correct protein composition but altered collapse pressure or nonlinear output is not functionally equivalent. Release testing should therefore include calibrated pressure-sweep acoustic phenotyping, concentration-response curves, and post-storage signal retention. Where a product is intended for triggered release, release kinetics and residual payload after insonation should be specified. Where it is intended for cell tracking, signal per cell, durability over time, and effects on cell function should be specified.

Route-specific regulatory pathways will differ. An intravenously injected purified GV probe resembles a biologic nanomaterial or imaging agent. An ex vivo GV-labeled CAR-T product resembles a modified cell therapy with an imaging label. An intracellular ARG platform resembles a genetically engineered cell or microbial reporter. A GV-containing hydrogel for local release resembles a combination product. These formats should not share a single generic translational narrative. Each requires a separate risk analysis, manufacturing control strategy, and clinical value proposition.

The Sim-to-Real Barrier

The principal difficulty in this field is no longer the absence of candidate materials. It is the difficulty of carrying a reporter concept across biological realization, tissue mechanics, and propagation physics without losing the function that made it attractive. In molecular ultrasound nanomedicine, Sim-to-Real mismatch has three coupled layers. These coupled mismatches are summarized in Figure 4. Intracellular Realization

The first mismatch appears when a proposed reporter must become a biological object. For intracellular systems, the relevant problem is not only whether a design is structurally plausible, but whether it can be expressed, assembled, retained, and detected without damaging the host. ARG studies have demonstrated noninvasive imaging of microorganisms, mammalian cells, tumors, tumor-homing bacteria, and stem-cell-compatible systems.8–13 These achievements show feasibility, but they also reveal that expression context matters.

Host burden is not trivial. Gas-vesicle gene clusters encode hydrophobic shell proteins and accessory factors that must be coordinated during assembly. Temporal gene regulation improved controlled expression and bacterial viability in recent work.21 That result does not determine how every mammalian host will respond, but it demonstrates that expression burden and host compatibility are core translational variables. For therapeutic cells, any reporter should be tested for proliferation, differentiation, exhaustion, migration, cytokine release, and therapeutic potency.

Local Tissue Mechanics and Microenvironment

The second mismatch appears after the reporter enters tissue. Acoustic protein nanostructures are often characterized in buffer, agarose, or simplified phantoms. Tissues are not neutral media. Tumors and other diseased tissues are shaped by solid stress, extracellular-matrix architecture, IFP, vascular leakiness, heterogeneous perfusion, and immune-cell distribution.45,48,49 These factors can alter distribution, local concentration, acoustic attenuation, and microenvironmental clearance.

The evidence does not yet show that GV reporter function generally fails in such settings. The stronger conclusion is more modest: reporter behavior and actuation thresholds should not be assumed to transfer unchanged from phantoms to tissue. If a system depends on a specific collapse pressure, nonlinear onset threshold, or triggered-release condition, then local tissue mechanics and transport must be measured or bounded rather than treated as background variables.

Brain delivery studies make this point concrete. Focused ultrasound and microbubble-mediated delivery are influenced by tissue architecture and vascular organization. Gray matter, white matter, and tumors can differ in baseline vascular permeability and response to ultrasound-mediated blood-brain barrier or blood-tumor barrier modulation.50,51 Molecular ultrasound nanomedicine will face analogous variability when it attempts to combine reporter localization, exposure, and effect.

Field Uncertainty and Thermal Constraints

The third mismatch lies in the acoustic field itself. The exposure prescribed at the transducer is not the field experienced at the target. Aberration, attenuation, focal distortion, tissue heterogeneity, device-specific beamforming, and local perfusion alter both image formation and delivered energy.31,32,52–54 For GVs, the relevant variable is not nominal mechanical index alone. It is the in situ pressure and temporal exposure at the reporter.

Propagation modeling is therefore part of dose validity. Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) for transcranial ultrasound wave propagation provide one example of computational field prediction under complex conditions.52 Thermal-intervention literature makes the same problem visible from another angle: tissue composition, perfusion, and vascular heat-sink effects alter the relationship between commanded exposure and effective heating.53,54 These issues become safety-critical when a reporter or material is designed to collapse, release, cavitate, or actuate within a defined pressure window.

The implication for molecular ultrasound is direct. A reporter may be designed for a collapse threshold, pressure-coded nonlinear transition, or triggered-release behavior. If the in situ field is uncertain, then decoding and actuation are uncertain. Claims about multiplexed molecular readout, exposure-aware control, or bounded nanotheranostics must therefore include field estimation, calibration, and uncertainty bounds.

Summary of the Sim-to-Real Problem

These three layers define the Sim-to-Real problem in this field. Biological realization can fail before a reporter reaches tissue. Tissue mechanics can alter distribution and acoustic context even after successful deployment. Propagation and thermal physics can shift the relationship between nominal and experienced exposure. None of these layers invalidates progress in molecular ultrasound. They explain why component-level success has not yet become a general clinical platform.

Toward Bounded Clinician-in-the-Loop Nanotheranostics

The long-term significance of acoustic protein nanostructures may lie in connecting reporter-linked sensing with localized acoustic intervention. This possibility should be framed as a systems hypothesis, not as an emerging clinical capability. A closed-loop molecular-ultrasound workflow would require reporter-linked readout, signal representation, state estimation, exposure estimation, safety gating, intervention planning, and feedback verification.10,12,20,55–61 A bounded systems architecture is summarized in Figure 5. Any such workflow must separate measurement, interpretation, and action. Reporter-linked signals should be converted into reviewable state variables without collapsing localization, molecular decoding, dose estimation, and intervention choice into one opaque controller.55–57 Clinical trust depends on traceability, uncertainty visibility, and the ability to stop or override an action. This is especially important for systems that may increase acoustic pressure or trigger payload release.

The near-term model is therefore not full autonomy. It is a clinician-in-the-loop system in which low-risk operations, such as diagnostic sweeps, calibration updates, or additional sub-threshold probing, may become increasingly assisted. Transitions toward higher-pressure interrogation, triggered release, cavitation, or other actuation should remain constrained by estimated in situ pressure, uncertainty bounds, exposure limits, and human approval.

This caution is consistent with more mature biomedical closed-loop systems. Automated insulin delivery remains constrained by sensing lag, delayed pharmacodynamics, calibration uncertainty, and intrapatient variability, and modern controllers incorporate explicit safety layers rather than relying on nominal model performance alone.58–60 Molecular ultrasound would face additional failure modes, including uncertain acoustic propagation, local tissue heterogeneity, reporter collapse, and immune or inflammatory responses.

A useful clinical stress test is resistant triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC). TNBC exhibits intratumoral heterogeneity, spatially variable immune states, and clinically relevant local differences in treatment response.62–69 A single biopsy may not capture the states that matter most for treatment selection. At the same time, the clinical standard is already demanding; sacituzumab govitecan improved major outcomes in metastatic TNBC in the ASCENT trial.70 Any acoustic-protein-nanostructure strategy would therefore need to provide actionable information or local control beyond molecular targeting in the abstract.

The most plausible near-term contribution in such a setting would not be a universal cure platform. It would be spatially repeated molecular assessment or local delivery verification under strict safety constraints. Even this narrower aim would require validated targeting, biodistribution, acoustic decoding, repeat-dose safety, and clinical decision rules. TNBC therefore illustrates the translational bar rather than proving readiness.

Portability and repeatability remain genuine strengths of ultrasound. Stretchable ultrasonic phased arrays and related monitoring technologies suggest that repeated or continuous deep-tissue assessment may become more feasible outside conventional imaging suites.61 That development could support future molecular-ultrasound workflows, but it does not remove the need for standardized biomaterials, exposure estimation, and clinician governance.

Conclusion and Outlook

GVs and related acoustic protein nanostructures have expanded what ultrasound can plausibly become. They introduce nanoscale, genetically encodable, and mechanically engineerable reporters that can support molecular reporting, targeted imaging, cell tracking, and, in some material systems, actuation-coupled release.4–13,18–20,22 The central issue is no longer whether these systems are technically interesting. It is whether they can be made reproducible, safe, interpretable, and clinically useful.

The evidence supports a differentiated view of the field. Clinical microbubbles remain the most mature platform, but they are largely constrained to the vascular compartment.1–3 Purified GVs, bicones, targeted GV derivatives, intracellular ARGs, exogenous cell labels, and GV-based composite materials extend ultrasound in ways that microbubbles cannot.4–13,18–20,22 They do not represent the same translational object. Each has its own route of administration, safety burden, and product-control requirements.

The strongest experimental base still comes from natural and engineered GV-family systems. GvpC-mediated engineering, directed evolution, pressure-coded multiplexing, blood-interaction studies, and TAPS-like release materials provide direct functional evidence.5,11,12,20,23 Computational protein design has expanded the candidate space,14,15 but current benchmark data do not support treating de novo design as a reliable route to in vivo-ready acoustic reporters.16,17 Acoustic phenotyping remains mandatory.

Translational barriers lie beyond acoustic detectability. Once acoustic protein nanostructures are considered as nanomedicine candidates, they inherit concerns about biodistribution, protein corona formation, MPS clearance, immunogenicity, complement activation, repeat dosing, toxicology, degradation, and manufacturing consistency.21,23–28,43–46,71 Direct GV studies already show that blood interactions and surface modification can alter acoustic signals and biological fate.23,24

The Sim-to-Real barrier sharpens this conclusion. A reporter may be plausible in design space, functional in purified form, and still fail when expression burden, tissue mechanics, acoustic-field uncertainty, or safety constraints are introduced. Reporter-sensitive inference will require RF-, I/Q-, pressure-sweep-, or modulation-aware representations rather than uncritical reliance on standard display images.31–38 Safety will require exposure estimation and uncertainty-aware governance, not only good average imaging performance.

Acoustic protein nanostructures may eventually support platforms in which molecular sensing informs localized intervention. Current evidence does not justify treating such platforms as clinically emergent. What can be defended now is a bounded translational framework: distinguish archetypes, characterize biomaterials quantitatively, measure acoustic phenotype directly, control biological fate and manufacturing attributes, estimate local exposure, and preserve clinician oversight. Ultrasound visibility is the beginning of translation, not its endpoint.
