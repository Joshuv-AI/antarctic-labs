1.
Introduction

Let
\Gamma
be an abelian locally compact group. Parrott
[
29
]
and Strichartz
[
32
]
showed that, for
p\neq 2
, a Fourier multiplier
T
on
L^{p}(\Gamma)
is an isometry if and only if there exist
c\in\mathbb{T}
, the unit circle, and
\gamma\in\Gamma
such that
T=c\,\tau_{\gamma}
, where
\tau_{\gamma}(f)=f(\cdot-\gamma)
denotes translation by
\gamma
. Equivalently, the symbol of
T
is a continuous character of the dual group
G=\widehat{\Gamma}
multiplied by an element of
\mathbb{T}
. This characterization reveals a strong rigidity phenomenon for isometric Fourier multipliers in the commutative setting.

For non-abelian groups, harmonic analysis and Fourier multiplier theory naturally lead to the framework of left group von Neumann algebras and their associated non-commutative
L^{p}
-spaces. Let
G
be a locally compact group equipped with a left Haar measure, and let
\mathcal{L}G\subset B(L^{2}(G))
denote its left group von Neumann algebra. This algebra carries a canonical normal semifinite faithful weight, the left Plancherel weight. When
G
is abelian,
\mathcal{L}G
identifies with the
L^{\infty}
-space over the dual group, and the Plancherel weight coincides with integration against the Haar measure on the dual group. The Plancherel weight is a trace if and only if
G
is unimodular, in which case the
L^{p}
theory simplifies considerably.

The study of Fourier multipliers on non-commutative
L^{p}
-spaces has attracted considerable attention in recent years, largely within the unimodular setting; see, for instance,
[
7
,
12
,
27
]
, as well as
[
26
]
and the references therein. In joint work with C. Arhancet
[
2
]
, we contributed to this line of research by extending the Parrott–Strichartz characterization of isometric Fourier multipliers to
L^{p}(\mathcal{L}G)
in the unimodular/tracial case. The aim of the present paper is to push this analysis beyond the unimodular framework. In the non-unimodular setting, the modular function introduces an intrinsic asymmetry between left and right structures, and the resulting absence of a trace becomes a principal source of new difficulties. Consequently, several arguments from
[
2
]
no longer apply and must be replaced by new techniques tailored to the setting of non-tracial weights.

Non-unimodular locally compact groups arise naturally in many fundamental examples, including affine groups of the form
\mathbb{R}\rtimes\mathbb{R}^{+}
and, more generally, groups of
ax+b
type. Only recently has a systematic study of
L^{p}
-Fourier multipliers in this setting begun to emerge; see
[
5
,
6
,
7
,
9
,
28
,
38
,
39
,
40
]
.

There are several equivalent constructions of non-commutative
L^{p}
-spaces in the non-tracial setting, most notably those due to Haagerup, Connes–Hilsum, and Kosaki (see
[
14
,
18
,
17
,
23
,
36
,
37
]
). These frameworks provide natural extensions of classical
L^{p}
-spaces to the non-commutative context. In this paper, we investigate Fourier multipliers on
L^{p}(\mathcal{L}G)
using the Connes–Hilsum construction.

In Section
3
, we recall the definition of bounded Fourier multipliers on
L^{p}(\mathcal{L}G)
for
1\leq p<\infty
, following the approach of
[
40
]
, and show that the various natural formulations of this notion are equivalent. Particular attention is devoted to the range
p<2
, which is typically addressed only briefly in the existing literature on Fourier multipliers for non-unimodular groups. Our aim is to fill this gap.

Let
1\leq p<\infty
and
\phi\in L^{\infty}(G)
. We say that
\phi
is a bounded Fourier multiplier on
L^{p}(\mathcal{L}G)
if there exists
C\geq 0
such that for all
f\in\mathcal{H}\ast\mathcal{H}
,

\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}}\lambda(\phi f)\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}}\in L^{p}(\mathcal{L}G)\quad\text{and}\quad\bigl\|\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}}\lambda(\phi f)\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}}\bigr\|_{p}\leq C\,\bigl\|\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}}\lambda(f)\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}}\bigr\|_{p}.

Here,
\mathcal{H}=\{f\in L^{2}(G):\operatorname{Supp}(f)\text{ is compact}\}
and
\Delta
denotes the operator of multiplication by the modular function of
G
. In this case, the map

\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}}\lambda(f)\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}}\longmapsto\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}}\lambda(\phi f)\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}},\qquad f\in\mathcal{H}\ast\mathcal{H},

extends uniquely to a bounded operator
M_{\phi,p}\colon L^{p}(\mathcal{L}G)\to L^{p}(\mathcal{L}G)
.

In Section
4
, we show that
\phi
is a bounded Fourier multiplier on
L^{p}(\mathcal{L}G)
if and only if it is a bounded Fourier multiplier on
L^{p^{\prime}}(\mathcal{L}G)
, where
p^{-1}+{p^{\prime}}^{-1}=1
. We also prove that continuous characters of
G
give rise to positive onto isometric Fourier multipliers on
L^{p}(\mathcal{L}G)
for all
p
. Our main result, Theorem
5.3
, establishes a converse for
p\neq 2
: if
M_{\phi,p}
is a positive Fourier multiplier that is an onto isometry, then
\phi
coincides locally almost everywhere with a continuous character of
G
.

Section
6
is devoted to the endpoint cases
p=1
and
p=2
. We show that the rigidity phenomenon established for
1<p\neq 2<\infty
continues to hold at
p=1
without any positivity assumption, and at
p=2
under a slightly stronger condition.

2.
Group von Neumann algebras and their non-commutative
L^{p}
-spaces

2.1.
The weighted von Neumann algebras
({\mathcal{L}}G,\varphi_{0})
and
({\mathcal{R}}G,\psi_{0})

We briefly recall the definition of the left and right group von Neumann algebras, starting with some required background on locally compact groups; for details, see
[
11
]
,
[
13
, Section 2]
,
[
34
, Chapter 18]
and
[
35
, Section VII.3]
.

Let G be a locally compact group equipped with a fixed left Haar measure dt. For
1\leq p<\infty
, we write
\|\cdot\|_{p}
for the norm on the associated
L^{p}
-space
L^{p}(G)
.

Given any two locally measurable functions
\phi_{1},\phi_{2}\colon G\to\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}
, we say that
\phi_{1}=\phi_{2}
locally almost everywhere if the set
\{\phi_{1}\not=\phi_{2}\}
is locally null. Let
L^{\infty}(G)
denote the space of locally measurable functions
\phi\colon G\to\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}
that are bounded except on a locally null set, modulo functions that are zero locally almost everywhere. Equipped with

\|\phi\|_{\infty}=\inf\{c\,:\,|\phi(t)|\leq c\text{ locally a.e.}\},

this is a Banach space. For any
\phi\in L^{\infty}(G)
and
f\in L^{1}(G)
, the product
\phi f\in L^{1}(G)
is well-defined. Moreover, the duality pairing
\langle\phi,f\rangle=\int_{G}\phi f\,
yields an isometric identification
L^{\infty}(G)=L^{1}(G)^{*}
. When
G
is
\sigma
-compact,
L^{\infty}(G)
coincides with the usual Lebesgue
L^{\infty}
-space associated with the Haar measure, although the two spaces may differ in general. See
[
11
, Section 2.3]
for details, and also
[
2
, Lemma 3.2]
and the subsequent discussion. Within
L^{\infty}(G)
we distinguish two important subspaces. The subspace
C_{b}(G)
consists of all bounded continuous functions on
G
, and the subspace
C_{c}(G)
consists of compactly supported continuous functions.

Finally, we recall the modular function
\Delta\colon G\to(0,\infty)
.
This is a continuous group homomorphism defined by the way the left Haar measure transforms under right translation and inversion. More precisely, we have

\int_{G}f(t)\,dt=\int_{G}\Delta(t)^{-1}f(t^{-1})\,dt,\qquad f\in L^{1}(G),

and

\int_{G}f(ts)\,dt=\Delta(s)^{-1}\int_{G}f(t)\,dt,\qquad f\in L^{1}(G).

These two properties will be used freely in the sequel.

Next, we recall the definition of the regular representations of a locally compact group. The left and right representations

\lambda,\rho\colon G\to B(L^{2}(G))
are defined by

[\lambda(t)h](s)=h(t^{-1}s),\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad[\rho(t)h](s)=\Delta(t)^{\frac{1}{2}}h(st),

for any
t,s\in G
and
h\in L^{2}(G)
.

Let
{\mathcal{L}}G\subset B(L^{2}(G))
and
{\mathcal{R}}G\subset B(L^{2}(G))
denote the von Neumann algebras generated by
\lambda(G)
and
\rho(G)
, respectively.
These are called the left and right group von Neumann algebras of
G
and they are mutual commutants, that is,

(2.1)

\mathcal{R}G=\mathcal{L}G^{\prime}.

The algebra
\mathcal{L}G
admits a concrete description via convolution operators. Indeed, given
f\in L^{1}(G)
, we define

\lambda(f)=\int_{G}f(t)\lambda(t)\,dt,

where the integral is understood in the strong sense.
The resulting operator acts on
L^{2}(G)
by left convolution, that is,
[\lambda(f)](h)(s)=(f\star h)(s)=\int_{G}f(t)h(t^{-1}s)\,dt
for all
h\in L^{2}(G)
.
Moreover,
\lambda(f)
belongs to
{\mathcal{L}}G
, and satisfies

\|\lambda(f)\|\leq\|f\|_{1}
.
The image
\lambda(L^{1}(G))
is
w^{*}
-dense in
{\mathcal{L}}G

and since
C_{c}(G)\subset L^{1}(G)
is a
dense subspace, the same is true for
\lambda(C_{c}(G))
.

The map
f\mapsto\lambda(f)
respects both the algebraic and involutive structures. Indeed,
\lambda(f_{1}\star f_{2})=\lambda(f_{1})\lambda(f_{2})
for all
f_{1},f_{2}\in L^{1}(G)
. If, for
any
f\in L^{1}(G)
,
f^{*}
is defined by

f^{*}(t)=\Delta(t)^{-1}\overline{f(t^{-1})},

then
f^{*}\in L^{1}(G)
and
\lambda(f)^{*}=\lambda(f^{*})
. Thus,
\lambda(L^{1}(G))
is a
*
-subalgebra of
{\mathcal{L}}G
.

Associated with
\mathcal{L}G
is the left Plancherel weight

\varphi_{0}\colon{\mathcal{L}}G^{+}\longrightarrow[0,\infty].

This weight is normal, semifinite and faithful, and
is characterized by the identity

(2.2)

\varphi_{0}\bigl(\lambda(f)^{*}\lambda(f)\bigr)=\|f\|_{2}^{2},\qquad f\in L^{1}(G)\cap L^{2}(G).

The modular nature on the group is reflected in tracial properties of
\varphi_{0}
:
\varphi_{0}
is a trace if and only if
G
is unimodular (that is,
\Delta\equiv 1
).

A completely analogous construction applies to the right von Neumann algebra
\mathcal{R}G
. The right Plancherel weight
\psi_{0}
is normal, semifinite, faithful on
\mathcal{R}G
satisfying

\psi_{0}\bigl(\rho(f)^{*}\rho(f)\bigr)=\|f\|_{2}^{2},\qquad f\in L^{1}(G)\cap L^{2}(G).

The two Plancherel weights are related by the standard form of the group von Neumann algebra (see e.g.
[
17
, Chapter 3]
). Indeed,
let
{\mathfrak{J}}\colon L^{2}(G)\to L^{2}(G)
be the
anti-unitary operator on
L^{2}(G)
defined by

[{\mathfrak{J}}(h)](s)=\Delta(s)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\overline{h(s^{-1})}
. This operator intertwines the left and right regular representations in the sense that
{\mathfrak{J}}\lambda(t){\mathfrak{J}}=\rho(t)
for all
t\in G
. As a consequence, the weights
\varphi_{0}
and
\psi_{0}
are related by conjugation with
{\mathfrak{J}}
as follows

(2.3)

\psi_{0}(a)=\varphi_{0}({\mathfrak{J}}a{\mathfrak{J}}),\qquad a\in{\mathcal{R}}G^{+}.

2.2.
The spaces
L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)

We briefly recall the Connes–Hilsum construction of non-commu-tative
L^{p}
-spaces in a form adapted to group von Neumann algebras. For the general construction and its basic properties, as well as its relation to the Haagerup non-commutative
L^{p}
-spaces
defined in
[
14
]
, we refer to
[
17
, Section 11.3]
or to the original paper
[
18
]
. Additional background and related results can be found in
[
36
]
and
[
15
, Section 1]
.

For
1\leq p\leq\infty
, we denote by
L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)
the Connes–Hilsum non-commutative
L^{p}
-space associated with the triple

({\mathcal{L}}G,L^{2}(G),\psi_{0}).

These spaces are studied in detail in
[
38
]
. In what follows, we only recall the properties that will be used later and refer to
[
38
]
for proofs and further details (see also
[
17
, Chapter 11]
and
[
40
]
).

Throughout the sequel, we will identify
\Delta
with the multiplication operator

h\mapsto\Delta h

on
L^{2}(G)
. This is a closed and densely defined operator with domain

\{h\in L^{2}(G)\,:\,\Delta h\in L^{2}(G)\}.

The same convention applies to any measurable function
G\to\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}
in place of
\Delta
, and in particular to the functions
\Delta^{z}
for any
z\in\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}
.

Given a normal semifinite weight
\varphi
on
\mathcal{L}G
, we denote by

\frac{d\varphi}{d\psi_{0}}

its spatial derivative with respect to
\psi_{0}
(see, e.g.,
[
17
, Definition 11.14]
). This is a positive operator on
L^{2}(G)
. As shown in
[
38
, p. 551]
, the spatial derivative of the left Plancherel weight with respect to the right Plancherel weight is given by

(2.4)

\frac{d\varphi_{0}}{d\psi_{0}}=\Delta.

This identity may be also deduced from either
[
8
, Lemma 10]
or
[
17
, Proposition 11.6]
, using (
2.3
).

Let
A_{0}
be the space of closed and densely defined operators on
L^{2}(G)
that are affiliated with
{\mathcal{L}}G
.
For a real number
\alpha\neq 0
, let
A_{\alpha}
be the set of all closed and densely defined operators
a
on
L^{2}(G)
such that, if
a=u|a|
is the polar decomposition of
a
, then
u\in{\mathcal{L}}G
and there exists a normal semifinite weight
\varphi
on
{\mathcal{L}}G
satisfying

(2.5)

|a|=\Bigl(\frac{d\varphi}{d\psi_{0}}\Bigr)^{\alpha}.

In this case, the weight
\varphi
is uniquely determined by
a
.

Each
A_{\alpha}
is a complex vector space under the strong sum of operators and the usual scalar multiplication.
It coincides with the so-called
(-\alpha)
-homogeneous operators (see
[
38
, Section 2]
or
[
17
, pp. 202–203]
) and is closed under taking adjoints. Moreover, the family
(A_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}}
is compatible with multiplication: if
a\in A_{\alpha}
,
b\in A_{\beta}
, the operator
ab
, with domain

\{h\in\mathrm{dom}(b)\,:\,b(h)\in\mathrm{dom}(a)\},

is closable, and its closure belongs to
A_{\alpha+\beta}
(see
[
38
, Remark 2.2]
or
[
40
, Lemma 2.4.2]
) and
moreover, this operation is associative. The closure of
ab
is called the strong product of
a
and
b
, and by convention it will simply be denoted by
ab
in the sequel.

By (
2.4
),
\Delta
belongs to
A_{1}
.
Hence, for any
z\in\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}
, we have
\Delta^{z}\in A_{\mathrm{Re}(z)}
.
Therefore, for any
f\in L^{1}(G)
and
z_{1},z_{2}\in\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}
, the operator

(2.6)

\Delta^{z_{1}}\lambda(f)\Delta^{z_{2}}\in A_{\mathrm{Re}(z_{1})+\mathrm{Re}(z_{2})}

is well-defined.

Let
{\mathcal{L}}G_{*}
denote the predual of
{\mathcal{L}}G
, and let
1\leq p<\infty
.
By definition, the non-commutative
L^{p}
-space
L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)
is the set of all
a\in A_{\frac{1}{p}}
such that (
2.5
) holds for some
\varphi\in{\mathcal{L}}G_{*}^{+}
.
In this case, we set

\|a\|_{p}=[\varphi(1)]^{\frac{1}{p}}.

Equipped with this norm,
L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)
becomes a Banach space and is naturally contained in
A_{p}
.
Moreover,
a^{*}\in L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)
for all
a\in L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)
and satisfies

\|a^{*}\|_{p}=\|a\|_{p}.

By convention,
L^{\infty}({\mathcal{L}}G)={\mathcal{L}}G
.

The positive cone
L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)^{+}
consists by definition of those
a\in L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)
that are positive in the sense of (closed and densely defined) operators over a Hilbert space, and a bounded operator
S\colon L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)\to L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)
is called
positive
if it maps
L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)^{+}
into
L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)^{+}
.

For any
1\leq p,q,r\leq\infty
with
p^{-1}+q^{-1}=r^{-1}
, the product mapping

A_{\frac{1}{p}}\times A_{\frac{1}{q}}\longrightarrow A_{\frac{1}{r}}

arising from the above discussion restricts to a contractive bilinear map

L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)\times L^{q}({\mathcal{L}}G)\longrightarrow L^{r}({\mathcal{L}}G).

In particular, taking
q=\infty
shows that, for every
p\geq 1
, the space

L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)
is a contractive
{\mathcal{L}}G
-bimodule.

The correspondence

\varphi\in{\mathcal{L}}G_{*}^{+}\longmapsto\frac{d\varphi}{d\psi_{0}}\in L^{1}({\mathcal{L}}G)^{+}

extends to an isometric
{\mathcal{L}}G
-bimodule isomorphism
\tau\colon{\mathcal{L}}G_{*}\to L^{1}({\mathcal{L}}G)
. This identification allows one to define a contractive linear functional

\mathrm{Tr}\colon L^{1}({\mathcal{L}}G)\longrightarrow\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}

by setting
\mathrm{Tr}(\tau(\varphi))=\varphi(1)
for every
\varphi\in{\mathcal{L}}G_{*}
. This functional is usually called the integral with respect to
\psi_{0}
(see
[
17
, p. 203]
or
[
38
, p. 554]
) and provides the appropriate replacement for a trace in the non-unimodular setting.

Let
p^{\prime}=p/(p-1)
denote the conjugate exponent of
p
. Using this functional, one obtains a natural duality pairing between
L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)
and
L^{p^{\prime}}({\mathcal{L}}G)
given by

\langle a,b\rangle=\mathrm{Tr}(ab),\qquad a\in L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G),\ b\in L^{p^{\prime}}({\mathcal{L}}G).

This pairing identifies
L^{p^{\prime}}({\mathcal{L}}G)
isometrically with the dual space of
L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)
.

Finally, the space
L^{2}({\mathcal{L}}G)
carries a Hilbert space structure with inner product

(a\mid b)=\mathrm{Tr}(b^{*}a),\qquad a,b\in L^{2}({\mathcal{L}}G).

2.3.
Some dense subspaces

In this section, we introduce certain dense subspaces of
L^{p}(\mathcal{L}G)
which form the foundation for defining bounded Fourier multipliers. We first recall the subspaces of
\mathcal{L}G
commonly used in connection with the left Plancherel weight
\varphi_{0}
. Let

(2.7)

\mbox{${\mathcal{N}}$}=\{x\in{\mathcal{L}}G\,:\,\varphi_{0}(x^{*}x)<\infty\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mbox{${\mathcal{M}}$}=\mathrm{Span}\{y^{*}x\,:\,x,y\in\mbox{${\mathcal{N}}$}\}.

It is well-known that
{\mathcal{N}}
is a
w^{*}
-dense left ideal of
{\mathcal{L}}G
, while

{\mathcal{M}}
is a
w^{*}
-dense
*
-subalgebra of
{\mathcal{L}}G
; see, for example,

[
35
, Section VII.1]
. Applying
[
37
, Theorem 26]
, which holds for general Connes-Hilsum
L^{p}
-spaces, we obtain the following result.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
1\leq p<\infty
.

(1)

If
p\geq 2
, then
x\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}}\in L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)
for all
x\in\mbox{${\mathcal{N}}$}
.

(2)

For any
p
, the product
\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}}x\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}}
belongs to

L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)
for all
x\in\mbox{${\mathcal{M}}$}
.

In the special case
p=2
, we further have

\lambda(L^{1}(G)\cap L^{2}(G))\subset\mbox{${\mathcal{N}}$}
and

(2.8)

\|\lambda(f)\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}}\|_{2}=\|f\|_{2},\qquad f\in L^{1}(G)\cap L^{2}(G),

see
[
38
, Theorem 3.2]
.

Let
C_{c}(G)\star C_{c}(G)
denote the linear span of all convolutions
f\star g
with
f,g\in C_{c}(G)
.
It follows from Lemma
2.1
that for all
1\leq p<\infty
,

\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}}\,\lambda(C_{c}(G)\star C_{c}(G))\,\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}}\subset L^{p}(\mathcal{L}G),

and for all
2\leq p<\infty
,

\lambda(C_{c}(G))\,\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}}\subset L^{p}(\mathcal{L}G).

The next lemma establishes that these subspaces are in fact dense.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
1\leq p<\infty
.

(1)

If
p\geq 2
, then
\lambda(C_{c}(G))\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}}
is a dense subspace of

L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)
.

(2)

For any
p
, the space

\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}}\lambda(C_{c}(G)\star C_{c}(G))\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}}

is dense in
L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)
.

Proof.

Assertion (1) follows from the proof of
[
38
, Theorem 4.5, (2)]
.
Assertion (2) is obtained by applying (1) with the index
2p
, and then using the contractive
product

L^{2p}({\mathcal{L}}G)\times L^{2p}({\mathcal{L}}G)\longrightarrow L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G).\qed

3.
Bounded Fourier multipliers on
L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)

In this section, we introduce bounded Fourier multipliers on
L^{p}(\mathcal{L}G)
and show that all natural definitions of these multipliers are equivalent.
Our approach relies on the modular structure associated with the left Plancherel weight
\varphi_{0}
and on the identification of a convenient dense subspace of
L^{p}(\mathcal{L}G)
on which Fourier multipliers can be defined in a canonical way.
This subspace consists of operators arising from compactly supported functions and enjoys good stability and analyticity properties with respect to the modular action.

Let
(\sigma_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}
be the modular
automorphism group of the weight
\varphi_{0}
.
Then

\sigma_{t}(x)=\Delta^{it}x\Delta^{-it}

for all
x\in{\mathcal{L}}G
and all
t\in\mbox{$\mathbb{R}$}
, see
[
13
, Section 2]
.
This implies that

\sigma_{t}(\lambda(f))=\lambda(\Delta^{it}f),\qquad f\in L^{1}(G),\,t\in\mbox{$\mathbb{R}$}.

We denote by
{\mathcal{L}}G_{a}
the set of all
x\in{\mathcal{L}}G
such that
t\mapsto\sigma_{t}(x)

extends to an entire function on
\mathbb{C}
.
Such elements are called analytic, see e.g.
[
35
, Section VIII.2]
.

Next, we introduce the space

\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}=\bigl\{f\in L^{2}(G)\,:\,{\rm Supp}(f)\ \hbox{is compact}\bigr\}.

Clearly,
\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}\subset L^{1}(G)\cap L^{2}(G)
, so
\lambda(f)
is well-defined
for
f\in\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}
.
Moreover, for any

z\in\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}
we have
\Delta^{z}f\in\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}
. Hence
\lambda(f)

belongs to
{\mathcal{L}}G_{a}
and we have

\sigma_{-iz}(\lambda(f))=\lambda(\Delta^{z}f),\qquad f\in\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$},\,z\in\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}.

It therefore follows from the above that

(3.1)

\lambda(\Delta^{z}f)=\Delta^{z}\lambda(f)\Delta^{-z},\qquad f\in\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$},\,z\in\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}.

We also observe that

(3.2)

\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}\star\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}\subset\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad L^{\infty}(G)\,\cdotp\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}=\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}.

For any
1\leq p<\infty
, we introduce

{\mathcal{B}}_{p}=\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}}\lambda(\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}\star\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$})\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}}.

By Lemmas
2.1
and
2.2
, this is a dense subspace of
L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\phi\in L^{\infty}(G)
and let
1\leq p<\infty
. We say that
\phi
is a bounded Fourier multiplier
on
L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)
if there exists a constant
C\geq 0
such that
for all
f\in\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}\star\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}
,

\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}}\lambda(\phi f)\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}}\in L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad\bigl\|\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}}\lambda(\phi f)\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}}\bigr\|_{p}\leq C\bigl\|\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}}\lambda(f)\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}}\bigr\|_{p}.

Note that by (
2.6
), we know that

\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}}\lambda(\phi f)\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}}
belongs
to
A_{p}
for any
f\in\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}\star\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}
. However, there is no a priori reason for it to belong to

L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)
.

If
\phi
is a bounded Fourier multiplier
on
L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)
, the mapping

\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}}\lambda(f)\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}}\mapsto\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}}\lambda(\phi f)\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}},\qquad f\in\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$},

uniquely extends to a bounded operator

M_{\phi,p}\colon L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)\longrightarrow L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G).

We now collect a series of remarks showing that all reasonable formulations of bounded Fourier multipliers on
L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)
are equivalent.

Remark 3.2
.

Consider
\phi\in L^{\infty}(G)
,
1\leq p<\infty
, and
\theta\in[0,1]
.
We show that
\phi
is a bounded Fourier multiplier on
L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)

if and only if there exists a constant
C\geq 0
such that, for all

f\in\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}\star\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}
,

(3.3)

\Delta^{\frac{\theta}{p}}\lambda(\phi f)\Delta^{\frac{1-\theta}{p}}\in L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)\quad\text{and}\quad\bigl\|\Delta^{\frac{\theta}{p}}\lambda(\phi f)\Delta^{\frac{1-\theta}{p}}\bigr\|_{p}\leq C\bigl\|\Delta^{\frac{\theta}{p}}\lambda(f)\Delta^{\frac{1-\theta}{p}}\bigr\|_{p}.

Assume that
\theta\in\bigl[\frac{1}{2},1\bigr]
and let

c\geq 0
satisfy
\frac{\theta}{p}=\frac{1}{2p}+c
.
For any
f\in\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}\star\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}
, using (
3.1
) we obtain

\Delta^{\frac{\theta}{p}}\lambda(f)\Delta^{\frac{1-\theta}{p}}=\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}}\lambda(\Delta^{c}f)\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}}.

Since
\Delta
is multiplicative,
multiplication by
\Delta^{c}
maps

\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}\star\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}
onto itself, and therefore

{\mathcal{B}}_{p}=\Delta^{\frac{\theta}{p}}\lambda(\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}\star\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$})\Delta^{\frac{1-\theta}{p}}.

Similarly, by (
3.2
) and (
3.1
), for all
f\in\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}\star\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}
we have

\Delta^{\frac{\theta}{p}}\lambda(\phi f)\Delta^{\frac{1-\theta}{p}}=\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}}\lambda(\phi\Delta^{c}f)\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}}.

It follows that the inequality in (
3.3
)
is equivalent to the boundedness of the map

\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}}\lambda(f)\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}}\longmapsto\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}}\lambda(\phi f)\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}}

on the dense subspace
\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}}\lambda(\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}\star\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$})\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}}

of
L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)
, which is precisely the definition of
\phi
being
a bounded Fourier multiplier on
L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)
. In this case,

M_{\phi,p}\Bigl(\Delta^{\frac{\theta}{p}}\lambda(f)\Delta^{\frac{1-\theta}{p}}\Bigr)=\Delta^{\frac{\theta}{p}}\lambda(\phi f)\Delta^{\frac{1-\theta}{p}},\qquad f\in\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}\star\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}.

An analogous argument applies when
\theta\in\bigl[0,\frac{1}{2}\bigr]
,
writing
\frac{1-\theta}{p}=\frac{1}{2p}+c
.

In the case
p=2
, the situation simplifies considerably, and we have the following.

Corollary 3.3
.

Let
\phi\in L^{\infty}(G)
. Then
\phi
is a bounded Fourier multiplier on
L^{2}({\mathcal{L}}G)
and moreover,

\|M_{\phi,2}\|=\|\phi\|_{\infty}.

Proof.

Let
f\in\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}\star\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}
. Using (
2.8
), we have

\|\lambda(f)\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}}\|_{2}=\|f\|_{2}
and

\|\lambda(\phi f)\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}}\|_{2}=\|\phi f\|_{2}
.
The result then follows from Remark
3.2
applied with
p=2
and
\theta=0
.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

Let
\phi\in L^{\infty}(G)
and let
2\leq p<\infty
.

(1)

We set

{\mathcal{C}}_{p}=\lambda(\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$})\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}}.

It follows from Lemmas
2.1
and
2.2
that this is a dense subspace of

L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)
. Moreover by the second half of (
3.2
),

\lambda(\phi f)\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}}
belongs to

L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)
for any
f\in\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}
. We claim that

\phi
is a bounded Fourier multiplier
on
L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)
if and only if there exists a constant
C\geq 0
such that

(3.4)

\bigl\|\lambda(\phi f)\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}}\bigr\|_{p}\leq C\bigl\|\lambda(f)\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}}\bigr\|_{p},\qquad f\in\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}.

The ‘if part’ follows from the first half of Definition
3.1
and Remark

3.2
applied with
\theta=0
.

To prove the ‘only if part’, assume that

\phi
is a bounded Fourier multiplier
on
L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)
and consider the resulting map

M_{\phi,p}\colon L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)\rightarrow L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)
.
We need
[
38
, Theorem 4.5, (1)]
which asserts that

(3.5)

\bigl\|\lambda(f)\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}}\bigr\|_{p}\leq\|f\|_{p^{\prime}},\qquad f\in L^{1}(G)\cap L^{2}(G).

Here
p^{\prime}
is the conjugate number of
p
.

Let
f\in\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}
.
There exists a compact subset
K\subset G
and a sequence
(g_{n})_{n\geq 1}
in

\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}\star\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}
such that
{\rm Supp}(g_{n})\subset K
for all
n\geq 1
and

\|g_{n}-f\|_{p^{\prime}}\to 0
.
This implies that
\|\phi g_{n}-\phi f\|_{p^{\prime}}\to 0
. Then, applying (
3.5
)
twice, we see that

\lambda(\phi f)\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}}=\lim_{n}\lambda(\phi g_{n})\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}}=\lim_{n}M_{\phi,p}\bigl(\lambda(g_{n})\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}}\bigr)=M_{\phi,p}\bigl(\lambda(f)\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}}\bigr).

This yields (
3.4
), with
C=\|M_{\phi,p}\|
.

(2)
Arguing as in Remark
3.2
, we obtain that for any

\theta\in[0,1]
,

{\mathcal{C}}_{p}=\Delta^{\frac{\theta}{p}}\lambda(\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$})\Delta^{\frac{(1-\theta)}{p}},

and that
\phi
is a bounded Fourier multiplier
on
L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)
if and only if there exists a constant
C\geq 0
such that

\bigl\|\Delta^{\frac{\theta}{p}}\lambda(\phi f)\Delta^{\frac{(1-\theta)}{p}}\bigr\|_{p}\leq C\bigl\|\Delta^{\frac{\theta}{p}}\lambda(f)\Delta^{\frac{(1-\theta)}{p}}\bigr\|_{p},\qquad f\in\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}.

Further in this case,

M_{\phi,p}\Bigl(\Delta^{\frac{\theta}{p}}\lambda(f)\Delta^{\frac{(1-\theta)}{p}}\Bigr)=\Delta^{\frac{\theta}{p}}\lambda(\phi f)\Delta^{\frac{(1-\theta)}{p}},\qquad f\in\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}.

We conclude this section with a duality result that extends
[
1
, Lemma 6.4]
.
We set
\check{g}(t)=g(t^{-1})
for any
g\in L^{1}(G)+L^{\infty}(G)
. With this notation,

g^{*}=\Delta^{-1}\overline{\check{g}}
for all
g\in L^{1}(G)
.
It is plain that for any
g\in\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}
,
\Delta^{-\frac{1}{2}}\overline{\check{g}}\in\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}
.
Consequently,
g^{*}\in\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}
and hence by part (1) of Lemma
2.1
,

\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}}\lambda(g)=\bigl(\lambda(g^{*})\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}}\bigr)^{*}\in L^{2}({\mathcal{L}}G)
. We now claim that

(3.6)

{\rm Tr}\bigl(\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}}\lambda(g)\lambda(f)\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}}\bigr)=\int_{G}\Delta(t)^{-1}g(t^{-1})f(t)\,dt,\qquad f,g\in\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}.

By the above discussion, both sides of this equality are well defined.
Then by polarization, it suffices to prove it when
g=f^{*}
. In this case,
both sides of (
3.6
) are equal to
\|f\|_{2}^{2}
, by (
2.8
). Hence
the desired equality holds.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
1<p<\infty
, let
p^{\prime}
denote its conjugate number and let
\phi\in L^{\infty}(G)
. If
\phi

is a bounded Fourier multiplier on
L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)
,
then
\check{\phi}
is a bounded Fourier multiplier on
L^{p^{\prime}}({\mathcal{L}}G)
,
and

M_{\phi,p}^{*}=M_{\check{\phi},p^{\prime}}.

Proof.

Assume first that
p\leq 2
and let
T=M_{\phi,p}
. Let
g\in\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}

and
f\in\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}\star\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}
.
By Remark

3.2
and (
3.1
), we have

\displaystyle\Bigl\langle T^{*}\bigl(\Delta^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}\lambda(g)\bigr),\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{2}}\lambda(f)\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}}\Bigr\rangle

\displaystyle=\Bigl\langle\Delta^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}\lambda(g),T\bigl(\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{2}}\lambda(f)\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}}\bigr)\Bigr\rangle

\displaystyle=\Bigl\langle\Delta^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}\lambda(g),\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{2}}\lambda(\phi f)\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}}\Bigr\rangle

\displaystyle={\rm Tr}\Bigl(\Delta^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}\lambda(g)\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{2}}\lambda(\phi f)\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}}\Bigr)

\displaystyle={\rm Tr}\Bigl(\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}}\lambda\bigl(\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p}}g\bigr)\lambda(\phi f)\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}}\Bigr).

Hence by (
3.6
),

\Bigl\langle T^{*}\bigl(\Delta^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}\lambda(g)\bigr),\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{2}}\lambda(f)\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}}\Bigr\rangle=\int_{G}\Delta(t)^{-\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}-\frac{1}{2}}g(t^{-1})\phi(t)f(t)\,dt.

Changing
g
into
\check{\phi}g
and
T
into
I_{L^{p}}
, this identity implies that

\Bigl\langle T^{*}\bigl(\Delta^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}\lambda(g)\bigr),\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{2}}\lambda(f)\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}}\Bigr\rangle=\Bigl\langle\Delta^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}\lambda(\check{\phi}g),\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{2}}\lambda(f)\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}}\Bigr\rangle.

The result follows.

We now assume that
p>2
. This case is a bit more delicate. Again, we let
T=M_{\phi,p}
.
This time, we fix
g\in\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}\star\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}

and consider an arbitrary
f\in\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}
.
By the discussion before (
3.6
),

\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}}\lambda\bigl(\check{\phi}g)

belongs to
L^{2}({\mathcal{L}}G)
and hence

\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}}\lambda\bigl(\check{\phi}g)\Delta^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}-\frac{1}{2}}\lambda(f)\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}}\,\in L^{1}({\mathcal{L}}G).

Then arguing as in the first case, we obtain that

\Bigl\langle T^{*}\bigl(\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}}\lambda(g)\Delta^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}-\frac{1}{2}}\bigr),\lambda(f)\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}}\Bigr\rangle={\rm Tr}\Bigl(\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}}\lambda\bigl(\check{\phi}g)\Delta^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}-\frac{1}{2}}\lambda(f)\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}}\Bigr).

If we can prove that

(3.7)

\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}}\lambda\bigl(\check{\phi}g)\Delta^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}-\frac{1}{2}}\in L^{p^{\prime}}({\mathcal{L}}G),

then we obtain the result as in the first case. At this stage, we have an estimate

\Bigl|{\rm Tr}\Bigl(\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}}\lambda\bigl(\check{\phi}g)\Delta^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}-\frac{1}{2}}\lambda(f)\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}}\Bigr)\Bigr|\lesssim\bigl\|\lambda(f)\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}}\bigr\|_{p}.

Then by duality, there exists
\Gamma\in L^{p^{\prime}}({\mathcal{L}}G)
such that

{\rm Tr}\Bigl(\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}}\lambda\bigl(\check{\phi}g)\Delta^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}-\frac{1}{2}}\lambda(f)\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}}\Bigr)={\rm Tr}\Bigl(\Gamma\lambda(f)\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}}\Bigr),\qquad f\in\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}.

Using (
3.2
), we may change
f
into
f\star\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}}h
for any
h\in\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}
in the above identity.
Then using (
3.1
), we write

\lambda(f\star\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}}h)\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}}=\lambda(f)\lambda\bigl(\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}}h\bigr)\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}}=\lambda(f)\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}}\lambda(h)

and we obtain

{\rm Tr}\Bigl(\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}}\lambda\bigl(\check{\phi}g)\Delta^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}-\frac{1}{2}}\lambda(f)\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}}\lambda(h)\Bigr)={\rm Tr}\Bigl(\Gamma\lambda(f)\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}}\lambda(h)\Bigr),\qquad f,h\in\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}.

Since
C_{c}(G)\subset\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}
and
\lambda(C_{c}(G))
is
w^{*}
-dense in
{\mathcal{L}}G
,
this implies

\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}}\lambda\bigl(\check{\phi}g)\Delta^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}-\frac{1}{2}}\lambda(f)\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}}=\Gamma\lambda(f)\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}},\qquad f\in\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}.

We deduce that
\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}}\lambda\bigl(\check{\phi}g)\Delta^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}-\frac{1}{2}}=\Gamma
, and (
3.7
) follows.
∎

4.
Extension properties

In this section, we leverage the Haagerup–Junge–Xu extension theorem from
[
15
]
to transfer positivity and contractivity of Fourier multipliers from the von Neumann algebra level to non-commutative
L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)
-spaces. In particular, we show that continuous characters
\phi
on
G
give rise to positive onto isometric Fourier multipliers on all
L^{p}(\mathcal{L}G)
(Corollary
4.3
).

For any
1\leq p\leq\infty
, we let
L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G;\varphi_{0})
denote
the Haagerup non-commutative
L^{p}
-space associated with
\varphi_{0}
,
see
[
14
,
36
,
15
]
or
[
17
, Chapter 9]
.
We recall that
L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G;\varphi_{0})
consists of closed, densely defined
operators acting on
L^{2}(\mbox{$\mathbb{R}$};L^{2}(G))
. We let
\pi\colon{\mathcal{L}}G\to B(L^{2}(\mbox{$\mathbb{R}$};L^{2}(G)))
be the
*
-representation
such that
\pi({\mathcal{L}}G)=L^{\infty}({\mathcal{L}}G;\varphi_{0})
.

Let
D
be the so-called
density operator of
\varphi_{0}
and recall
{\mathcal{M}}
from (
2.7
).
Then as noticed in
[
15
, Remark 5.6]
,

{\mathcal{L}}:=D^{\frac{1}{2}}\pi(\mbox{${\mathcal{M}}$})D^{\frac{1}{2}}\subset L^{1}({\mathcal{L}}G;\varphi_{0}),

and
{\mathcal{L}}
is a dense subspace. This is an analogue of Lemma
2.2
, part (2).

Let
T\colon{\mathcal{L}}G\to{\mathcal{L}}G
be a positive map such that

(4.1)

\varphi_{0}(T(x))\leq\varphi_{0}(x),\qquad x\in{\mathcal{L}}G^{+}.

Then
T
maps
{\mathcal{M}}
into
{\mathcal{M}}
. One may therefore define

T^{1}\colon{\mathcal{L}}\to{\mathcal{L}}
by
setting

T^{1}\bigl(D^{\frac{1}{2}}\pi(x)D^{\frac{1}{2}})=D^{\frac{1}{2}}\pi\bigl(T(x)\bigr)D^{\frac{1}{2}},\qquad x\in\mbox{${\mathcal{M}}$}.

The Haagerup-Junge-Xu extension theorem
from
[
15
, Theorem 5.1 and Remark 5.6]
asserts that

T^{1}
uniquely extends to a
bounded operator

T^{1}\colon L^{1}({\mathcal{L}}G;\varphi_{0})\longrightarrow L^{1}({\mathcal{L}}G;\varphi_{0}),

with
\|T^{1}\|\leq 1
.
This result can be translated into the framework considered in Section
3
, as follows.
In the next statement, we rely on Lemma
2.1
, part (2).

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
T\colon{\mathcal{L}}G\to{\mathcal{L}}G
be a positive map satisfying (
4.1
).
Then the mapping

\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}}\mbox{${\mathcal{M}}$}\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}}\longrightarrow\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}}\mbox{${\mathcal{M}}$}\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}},\qquad\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}}x\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}}\,\mapsto\,\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}}T(x)\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}},

uniquely extends to a contractive map
L^{1}({\mathcal{L}}G)\to L^{1}({\mathcal{L}}G)
.

Proof.

We use the natural unitary identification between
L^{2}(\mbox{$\mathbb{R}$};L^{2}(G))

and the Hilbertian tensor product
L^{2}(\mbox{$\mathbb{R}$})\overset{2}{\otimes}L^{2}(G)
.
We will use tensor products of unbounded operators acting on

L^{2}(\mbox{$\mathbb{R}$})
and
L^{2}(G)
, respectively, for which we refer to
[
17
, Remark 11.33]
.
In this context, we have
D=d\otimes 1
, where
d
is the analytic generator of the unitary group of
translation operators on
L^{2}(\mbox{$\mathbb{R}$})
. Apply
[
17
, pp. 204-206]
to the left Plancherel weight
\varphi_{0}
on
M={\mathcal{L}}G
and to

\psi=\psi_{0}
, the right Plancherel weight on
M^{\prime}={\mathcal{R}}G
.
By (
2.4
), the operator

d_{0}
defined on the first line of
[
17
, p. 204]

coincides with
\Delta
.
Let
U_{0}\colon L^{2}(\mbox{$\mathbb{R}$};L^{2}(G))\to L^{2}(\mbox{$\mathbb{R}$};L^{2}(G))
be the unitary operator
considered in
[
17
, Lemma 11.30]
. Then the aforementioned lemma yields

(4.2)

U_{0}^{*}(1\otimes x)U_{0}=\pi(x),\qquad x\in{\mathcal{L}}G,

and

(4.3)

U_{0}^{*}(d\otimes\Delta)U_{0}=D.

By
[
17
, Lemma 11.32]
, there exists an isometric isomorphism

W\colon L^{1}({\mathcal{L}}G)\longrightarrow L^{1}({\mathcal{L}}G;\varphi_{0}),\qquad W(a)=U_{0}^{*}(d\otimes a)U_{0}.

For any
x\in\mbox{${\mathcal{M}}$}
, we have

\displaystyle U_{0}^{*}\bigl(d\otimes\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}}x\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}}\bigr)U_{0}

\displaystyle=U_{0}^{*}\Bigl(\bigl(d^{\frac{1}{2}}\otimes\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}}\bigr)(1\otimes x)\bigl(d^{\frac{1}{2}}\otimes\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}}\bigr)\Bigr)U_{0}

\displaystyle=U_{0}^{*}\bigl(d^{\frac{1}{2}}\otimes\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}}\bigr)U_{0}\cdotp U_{0}^{*}(1\otimes x)U_{0}\cdotp U_{0}^{*}\bigl(d^{\frac{1}{2}}\otimes\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}}\bigr)U_{0}

\displaystyle=\bigl(U_{0}^{*}(d\otimes\Delta)U_{0}\bigr)^{\frac{1}{2}}\cdotp U_{0}^{*}(1\otimes x)U_{0}\cdotp\bigl(U_{0}^{*}(d\otimes\Delta)U_{0}\bigr)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Using (
4.2
) and (
4.3
), we deduce that

W\bigl(\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}}x\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}}\bigr)=D^{\frac{1}{2}}xD^{\frac{1}{2}},\qquad x\in\mbox{${\mathcal{M}}$}.

Therefore, the contraction
W^{-1}T^{1}W
maps
\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}}x\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}}

to
\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}}T(x)\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}}
for all
x\in\mbox{${\mathcal{M}}$}
.
This proves the result.
∎

We refer to
[
20
,
25
]
for more information on the Haagerup-Junge-Xu extension theorem.

For any
\phi\in C_{b}(G)
, let

M_{\phi}\colon\lambda(G)\to\lambda(G)
be the linear map taking

\lambda(t)
to
\phi(t)\lambda(t)
for all
t\in G
. Following
[
4
]
, we
say that
\phi
is a bounded Fourier multiplier on
{\mathcal{L}}G
if
there exists a (necessarily unique)
w^{*}
-continuous map
{\mathcal{L}}G\to{\mathcal{L}}G

extending
M_{\phi}
. In this case, we still denote this extension by

M_{\phi}\colon{\mathcal{L}}G\to{\mathcal{L}}G
.

In the sequel, we let
e
denote the unit of
G
.

Corollary 4.2
.

Let
\phi\in C_{b}(G)
such that
\phi
is a bounded Fourier multiplier on
{\mathcal{L}}G

and
M_{\phi}
is a positive map.
Then for any
1\leq p<\infty
,
\phi
is a bounded Fourier multiplier on
L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)
, and we have

(4.4)

\|M_{\phi,p}\colon L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)\longrightarrow L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)\|\leq\phi(e).

Proof.

The positivity assumption ensures that
\phi(e)\geq 0
and

\bigl\|M_{\phi}\colon{\mathcal{L}}G\longrightarrow{\mathcal{L}}G\bigr\|=\|M_{\phi}(1)\|=\phi(e).

For any
f\in C_{c}(G)
such that
\lambda(f)\geq 0
, we have

\varphi_{0}(\lambda(f))=f(e)
, see e.g.
[
34
, §18.17]
.
Hence

\varphi_{0}\bigl(M_{\phi}(\lambda(f))\bigr)=\phi(e)f(e)
for any such
f
.
According to the Pedersen-Takesaki theorem
[
34
, Theorem 6.2]
, this implies that

\varphi_{0}\circ M_{\phi}=\phi(e)\varphi_{0}.

Hence by Theorem
4.1
,
\phi
is a bounded Fourier multiplier on
L^{1}({\mathcal{L}}G)
, with

\bigl\|M_{\phi,1}\colon L^{1}({\mathcal{L}}G)\longrightarrow L^{1}({\mathcal{L}}G)\bigr\|\leq\phi(e).

Once this result is settled, the boundedness on
L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)
and the estimate (
4.4
)
follow from Terp’s interpolation theorem
[
37
, Section 2]
.
∎

Corollary 4.3
.

Let
\phi
be a continuous character on
G
.
For any
1\leq p<\infty
,
\phi
is a bounded Fourier multiplier on
L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)

and
M_{\phi,p}
is a positive onto isometry on
L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)
.

Proof.

Let
\phi\colon G\to\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}
be a continuous character. Then
\phi

is positive definite and
\phi(e)=1
. Hence according to
[
4
, Proposition 4.2]
,

\phi
is a bounded Fourier multiplier on
{\mathcal{L}}G

and
M_{\phi}
is a positive, contractive map.
Hence by Corollary
4.2
,
\phi
is a bounded Fourier multiplier on
L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)

and
M_{\phi,p}
is a contraction.

Since
\phi^{-1}=\overline{\phi}
is also a continuous character,

M_{\phi,p}
is invertible and
M_{\phi,p}^{-1}=M_{\overline{\phi},p}
is a contraction. Consequently,

M_{\phi,p}
is an onto isometry.

The positivity of
M_{\phi,p}
directly follows from the positivity of
M_{\phi}

and from the symmetric nature of the definition of
M_{\phi,p}
.
∎

Remark 4.4
.

Let
L^{p}(M):=L^{p}(M,H,\psi)
be any Connes-Hilsum non-commutative

L^{p}
-space associated with
a von Neumann algebra
M\subset B(H)
and a normal semifinite
faithful weight
\psi
on
M^{\prime}
. For any integer
n\geq 1
, let

M_{n}
be the
C^{*}
-algebra of all
n\times n
matrices and
consider
M_{n}(M)\subset B(\ell^{2}_{n}\overset{2}{\otimes}H)
in the usual way.
Then the commutant of
M_{n}(M)
is
I_{\ell^{2}_{n}}\otimes M^{\prime}
.
We may therefore consider
\psi
as a weight on
M_{n}(M)^{\prime}
.
This allows to define the Connes-Hilsum space

L^{p}(M_{n}(M)):=L^{p}\bigl(M_{n}(M),\ell^{2}_{n}\overset{2}{\otimes}H,\psi\bigr).

It is well known that
L^{p}(M_{n}(M))
can be regarded as a space of
n\times n
matrices
with entries in
L^{p}(M)
, which allows to write an algebraic identification

L^{p}(M_{n}(M))=M_{n}\otimes L^{p}(M).

For any
S\colon L^{p}(M)\to L^{p}(M)
, we set
S_{n}=I_{M_{n}}\otimes S
and we say
that
S
is
n
-positive if the map
S_{n}\colon L^{p}(M_{n}(M))\to L^{p}(M_{n}(M))
is positive. Next, we say that

S
is completely positive if
S
is
n
-positive for all
n\geq 1
.

It is not hard to check (left to the reader) that in
Corollary
4.2
, if
M_{\phi}\colon{\mathcal{L}}G\to{\mathcal{L}}G
is completely positive,
then for any
1\leq p<\infty
,
M_{\phi,p}
is completely positive on
L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)
.

Now coming back to Corollary
4.3
, we know from
[
4
, Proposition 4.2]

that if
\phi
is a continuous character on
G
, then
M_{\phi}\colon{\mathcal{L}}G\to{\mathcal{L}}G
is completely positive.
Therefore,
M_{\phi,p}
is completely positive for all
1\leq p<\infty
.

We now turn to another application of the Haagerup-Junge-Xu theorem to Fourier multipliers.

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
\phi\in L^{\infty}(G)
and let
1\leq p<\infty
. If
\phi
is a bounded Fourier multiplier on

L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)
, then
\check{\phi}
is also a bounded Fourier multiplier, with

\|M_{\check{\phi},p}\|=\|M_{\phi,p}\|
.

Proof.

We consider the anti-
*
-automorphism
\kappa\colon{\mathcal{L}}G\to{\mathcal{L}}G
such
that
\kappa(\lambda(t))=\lambda(t^{-1})
for all
t\in G

(see
[
10
, Theorem 3.3.6]
).
This is a positive, involutive isometry.
For any
f\in L^{1}(G)
, we have

\kappa(\lambda(f))=\int_{G}f(t)\lambda(t^{-1})\,dt\,=\int_{G}\Delta(t)^{-1}f(t^{-1})\lambda(t)\,dt,

and hence

(4.5)

\kappa(\lambda(f))=\lambda\bigl(\overline{f}^{*}\bigr),\qquad f\in L^{1}(G).

Consequently,

\varphi_{0}\circ\kappa\bigl(\lambda(f)^{*}\lambda(f)\bigr)=\varphi_{0}\bigl(\kappa(\lambda(f))\kappa(\lambda(f^{*}))\bigr)=\varphi_{0}\bigl(\lambda(\overline{f})^{*}\lambda(\overline{f})\bigr),\qquad f\in L^{1}(G)\cap L^{2}(G).

Applying (
2.8
), we deduce that
\varphi_{0}\circ\kappa
and
\varphi_{0}
coincide
on
\lambda(f)^{*}\lambda(f)
for any
f\in L^{1}(G)\cap L^{2}(G)
.
Hence
\varphi_{0}\circ\kappa=\varphi_{0}
by the
Pedersen-Takesaki theorem
[
34
, Theorem 6.2]
.

Therefore, we can apply Theorem
4.1
to
\kappa
.
Combining this extension result with Terp’s interpolation theorem as in the proof of
Corollary
4.2
, and applying (
4.5
), we deduce the existence of an involutive isometry

\kappa_{p}\colon L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)\to L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)
such that

\kappa_{p}\bigl(\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}}\lambda(f)\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}}\bigr)=\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}}\lambda\bigl(\overline{f}^{*}\bigr)\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}},\qquad f\in\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}\star\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}.

Assume that
\phi
is a bounded Fourier multiplier on

L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)
. A simple calculation shows that

\bigl(\kappa_{p}\circ M_{\phi,p}\circ\kappa_{p}\bigr)\bigl(\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}}\lambda(f)\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}}\bigr)=\bigl(\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}}\lambda(\check{\phi}f)\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}}\bigr)\qquad f\in\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}\star\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}.

This implies that
\check{\phi}
is a bounded Fourier multiplier and that

M_{\check{\phi},p}=\kappa_{p}\circ M_{\phi,p}\circ\kappa_{p}.

∎

Corollary 4.6
.

Let
\phi\in L^{\infty}(G)
, let
1<p<\infty
and let
p^{\prime}
be its conjugate number.
Then
\phi
is a bounded Fourier multiplier on

L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)
if and only if
\phi
is a bounded Fourier multiplier on

L^{p^{\prime}}({\mathcal{L}}G)
. In this case, we
have
\|M_{\phi,p^{\prime}}\|=\|M_{\phi,p}\|
and
\|\phi\|_{\infty}\leq\|M_{\phi,p}\|
.

Proof.

Assume that
\phi\in L^{\infty}(G)

is a bounded Fourier multiplier on

L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)
.
Combining Proposition
4.5
and Proposition
3.5
, we obtain
that
\phi
is a bounded Fourier multiplier on

L^{p^{\prime}}({\mathcal{L}}G)
, with
\|M_{\phi,p^{\prime}}\|=\|M_{\phi,p}\|
.
Then by interpolation,

\|M_{\phi,2}\|\leq\|M_{\phi,p}\|
and hence

\|\phi\|_{\infty}\leq\|M_{\phi,p}\|

by Corollary
3.3
.
∎

Corollary 4.7
.

Let
\phi\in L^{\infty}(G)
and let
1<p<\infty
.
If
\phi
is a bounded Fourier multiplier on

L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)
, then
\overline{\phi}
is also
a bounded Fourier multiplier, with

\|M_{\overline{\phi},p}\|=\|M_{\phi,p}\|
.

Proof.

According to Corollary
4.6
, we may assume that

p\geq 2
. This allows us to apply part (2) of Remark

3.4
. Let
C=\|M_{\phi,p}\|
. Then

\bigl\|\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}}\lambda(\phi f)\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}}\bigr\|_{p}\leq C\bigl\|\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}}\lambda(f)\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}}\bigr\|_{p},\qquad f\in\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}.

Passing to adjoints in
L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)
,
this yields

(4.6)

\bigl\|\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}}\lambda(\phi f)^{*}\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}}\bigr\|_{p}\leq C\bigl\|\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}}\lambda(f)^{*}\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}}\bigr\|_{p},\qquad f\in\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}.

We note that

(\phi f)^{*}(t)=\Delta(t)^{-1}\overline{\phi(t^{-1})}\overline{f(t^{-1})}=\overline{\phi(t^{-1})}f^{*}(t).

Thus, we have
\lambda(\phi f)^{*}=\lambda\bigl(\check{\overline{\phi}}f^{*}\bigr)
, whereas

\lambda(f)^{*}=\lambda(f^{*})
. Since
{\mathcal{H}}
is closed under taking adjoints,
(
4.6
) actually shows that

\check{\overline{\phi}}
is a bounded
Fourier multiplier on
L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)
, with norm
less than
C
. Combining with Proposition
4.5
,
we deduce that
\overline{\phi}
is a bounded
Fourier multiplier on
L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)
, with norm
less than
C
. The result follows.
∎

5.
A characterization of positive isometric Fourier multipliers

The main objective of this section is Theorem
5.3
which provides a converse
to Corollary
4.3
for
1<p\not=2<\infty
. The special cases
p=1
and
p=2
are treated in the
next section.

We recall that for any locally measurable functions
\phi_{1},\phi_{2}\colon G\to\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}
, saying that

\phi_{1}=\phi_{2}
locally almost everywhere means that the set

\{\phi_{1}\not=\phi_{2}\}
is a locally null set. If
\phi_{1},\phi_{2}
are
bounded except on a locally null set, the above is equivalent to
the equality
\phi_{1}=\phi_{2}
in
L^{\infty}(G)
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
1\leq p<\infty
and let
\phi\in L^{\infty}(G)
be a bounded Fourier
multiplier on
L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)
. If the resulting operator

M_{\phi,p}\colon L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)\to L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)
is positive, then
for every compact set
K\subset G
, the restriction
\phi_{|K}
is almost everywhere equal
to a continuous function
K\to\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}
.

Proof.

We adapt the proof of
[
1
, Lemma 6.10]
.
For any arbitrary

g\in C_{c}(G)
, the product
\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}}\lambda(g^{*}\star g)\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}}=\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}}\lambda(g)^{*}\lambda(g)\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}}

is a positive element of
L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)
.
Hence by the positivity assumption on
M_{\phi,p}
,

\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}}\lambda\bigl(\phi(g^{*}\star g)\bigr)\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}}\,\in L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)^{+}.

Take any
\xi\in C_{c}(G)
.
Then
\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}}\xi\in C_{c}(G)
. Since

\lambda(\phi(g^{*}\star g))\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}}\xi=\phi(g^{*}\star g)\star\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}}\xi
,
this implies that
\lambda(\phi(g^{*}\star g))\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}}\xi
belongs
to
C_{c}(G)
. Hence
\xi
belongs to the domain
of the operator
\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}}\lambda\bigl(\phi(g^{*}\star g)\bigr)\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}}
.
Let
(\,\cdotp|\,\cdotp)
denote the inner product on
L^{2}(G)
.
Then it follows from the above that

\bigl(\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}}\lambda\bigl(\phi(g^{*}\star g)\bigr)\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}}\xi\,|\,\xi\bigr)\geq 0.

Therefore,

\bigl(\lambda(\phi(g^{*}\star g))\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}}\xi\,\big|\,\Delta^{\frac{1}{2p}}\xi\bigr)\geq 0.

Changing
\xi
into
\Delta^{-\frac{1}{2p}}\xi
,
we deduce that

(5.1)

\bigl(\lambda(\phi(g^{*}\star g))\xi\,\big|\,\xi\bigr)\geq 0,

for all
\xi\in C_{c}(G)
. By density, this implies that (
5.1
)
holds for all
\xi\in L^{2}(G)
.

For any
g\in C_{c}(G)
, we write

(g^{*}\star g)(v)=\int_{G}\Delta(u^{-1})\overline{g(u^{-1})}g(u^{-1}v)\,du,\qquad v\in G.

Then, we have

\lambda(\phi(g^{*}\star g))=\int_{G^{2}}\Delta(u^{-1})\overline{g(u^{-1})}g(u^{-1}v)\phi(v)\lambda(v)\,dudv.

Changing
u
into
t^{-1}
and then
v
into
t^{-1}s
, we deduce that

\lambda(\phi(g^{*}\star g))=\int_{G^{2}}\overline{g(t)}g(s)\phi(t^{-1}s)\lambda(t^{-1}s)\,dtds.

Therefore,

\bigl(\lambda(\phi(g^{*}\star g))\xi\,\big|\,\xi\bigr)=\int_{G^{2}}\overline{g(t)}g(s)\phi(t^{-1}s)\bigl(\lambda(t^{-1}s)\xi\,|\,\xi\bigr)\,dtds,

for all
\xi\in L^{2}(G).

This integral is non-negative, by (
5.1
).
We deduce that for all
\xi\in L^{2}(G)
, the function

t\mapsto\phi(t)(\lambda(t)\xi|\xi)
is positive definite
in the sense of
[
35
, Definition VII.3.20]
. According to

[
35
, Proposition VII.3.21]
and its proof, this implies that
this function belongs to the Fourier-Stieltjes algebra
B(G)
, in the sense that
it is locally almost everywhere equal to an element of
B(G)
.
By polarization, for all
\xi,\xi^{\prime}\in L^{2}(G)
, we deduce the same property for the function

t\mapsto\phi(t)(\lambda(t)\xi|\xi^{\prime})
. Thus, for all
\gamma
in the Fourier algebra
A(G)=\{\,t\mapsto(\lambda(t)\,\xi|\xi^{\prime})\mid\xi,\xi^{\prime}\in L^{2}(G)\,\}
,
the product
\phi\gamma
is locally almost everywhere
equal to a continuous function.
Since for any compact set
K\subset G
, there exists
\gamma\in A(G)
that does not vanish on

K
, the result follows.
∎

The next lemma is a consequence of Sherman’s work
concerning isometries
on non-commutative
L^{p}
-spaces
[
30
]
(see also
[
31
]
).

Recall that a Jordan homomorphism
J\colon M\to N
between two
von Neumann algebras
M
and
N
is a bounded linear map which satisfies

(5.2)

J(xy+yx)=J(x)J(y)+J(y)J(x)\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad J(x^{*})=J(x)^{*},

for all
x,y\in M
. If further
J
is a bijection, we say that

J
is a Jordan isomorphism.

It follows from
[
33
, Theorem 3.3]
that
J
is a Jordan isomorphism if and only if there exist
von Neumann algebra decompositions

(5.3)

M=M_{1}\oplus M_{2}\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad N=N_{1}\oplus N_{2},

as well as a

*
-isomorphism
\pi\colon M_{1}\to N_{1}
and an anti-
*
-isomorphism

\rho\colon M_{2}\to N_{2}
, such that

(5.4)

J=\pi\oplus\rho.

This implies that
J
is
w^{*}
-continuous. We refer to
[
16
]
and
[
22
, Exercices 10.5.21-10.5.31]

for more information on Jordan homomorphisms.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
1<p\not=2<\infty
and let
M,N
be two von Neumann algebras. Let

L^{p}(M)
and
L^{p}(N)
be any Connes-Hilsum spaces built upon
M
and
N
, respectively.
Let
S\colon L^{p}(M)\to L^{p}(N)
be an onto positive isometry.
Then there exists a Jordan isomorphism
J\colon M\to N

such that

(5.5)

S(ax+xa)=S(a)J(x)+J(x)S(a),\qquad a\in L^{p}(M),\ x\in M.

Proof.

Let
S\colon L^{p}(M)\to L^{p}(N)
be an onto positive isometry.
Sherman’s theorem
[
30
, Theorem 1.2]
asserts that there exists
a Jordan isomorphism
J\colon M\to N

such that

(5.6)

S(a)=\bigl(J^{-1}_{*}(a^{p})\bigr)^{\frac{1}{p}},\qquad a\in L^{p}(M)^{+}.

In this statement,
J_{*}\colon L^{1}(N)\to L^{1}(M)
is the pre-adjoint of
J
,
taking into account the natural isometric isomorphisms

M_{*}\simeq L^{1}(M)
and
N_{*}\simeq L^{1}(N)
, and

J^{-1}_{*}\colon L^{1}(M)\longrightarrow L^{1}(N)

is its inverse. In
[
30
,
31
]
, this statement is given within the framework
of Haagerup non-commutative
L^{p}
-spaces. However using the isomorphism
between the Haagerup spaces and the Connes-Hilsum spaces (see

[
36
, Chapter IV]
or
[
17
, Section 11.3]
), we see that
Sherman’s proof works as well in the Connes-Hilsum framework.

We are going to show that the mapping
J
from (
5.6
) provides
the identity (
5.5
).
It follows from the proof of
[
30
, Theorem 1.2]
that
M,N
and
J

have decompositions (
5.3
) and (
5.4
) such that

(5.7)

S(ax)=S(a)\pi(x),\qquad a\in L^{p}(M),\ x\in M_{1},

and

(5.8)

S(xa)=\rho(x)S(a),\qquad a\in L^{p}(M),\ x\in M_{2}.

Since
S
is positive, we have
S(b)=S(b^{*})^{*}
for all

b\in L^{p}(M)
. Hence for all
a\in L^{p}(M)
and
all
x\in M_{1}
, we have

S(xa)=S(a^{*}x^{*})^{*}=\bigl(S(a^{*})J(x^{*})\bigr)^{*}=J(x^{*})^{*}S(a^{*})^{*}=J(x)S(a),

by (
5.7
). Applying once more the latter identity, we obtain (
5.5
) in
the case when
x\in M_{1}
. Now using (
5.8
) instead of (
5.7
),
we obtain (
5.5
) in
the case when
x\in M_{2}
. This establishes the identity (
5.5
) for all
x\in M
.
∎

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
\phi\in L^{\infty}(G)
and let

1<p\not=2<\infty
. Assume that
\phi
is a bounded Fourier multiplier on
L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)

and that the resulting map

M_{\phi,p}\colon L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)\to L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)
is a positive, onto isometry.
Then
\phi
is locally almost everywhere equal to a continuous
character.

Proof.

We assume for simplicity that
G
is
\sigma
-compact and refer to the last lines
of this proof for the general case.
Thus, “locally almost everywhere” just means “almost everywhere” and by
Lemma
5.1
,
\phi
is almost everywhere equal to a continuous function.
We may therefore assume that
\phi\in C_{b}(G)
.

In summary, to establish the multiplicativity of
\phi
, we apply the identity (
5.5
) to carefully chosen approximate units in
{\mathcal{L}}G
and pass to a limit, obtaining a pointwise relation that yields the desired conclusion, while also proving some auxiliary identities that simplify the computations.

For any
t\in G
and for any
f\in C_{c}(G)
, we define

\lambda^{\prime}(t)f\in C_{c}(G)
by

[\lambda^{\prime}(t)f](s)=f(st^{-1}),\qquad s\in G.

We observe that

(5.9)

\Delta(t)\lambda(f)\lambda(t)=\lambda\bigl(\lambda^{\prime}(t)f\bigr),\qquad f\in C_{c}(G),\ t\in G.

Indeed,

\lambda(\lambda^{\prime}(t)f)=\int_{G}f(st^{-1})\lambda(s)\,ds=\Delta(t)\int_{G}f(s)\lambda(st)\,ds=\Delta(t)\Bigl(\int_{G}f(s)\lambda(s)\,ds\Bigr)\lambda(t).

Consider a net
(f_{i})_{i\in I}
of
C_{c}(G)\star C_{c}(G)
such that
f_{i}\geq 0
and
\int_{G}f_{i}(t)\,dt=1

for all
i\in I
, the support of every

f_{i}
is contained in some compact neighborhood

V_{i}
of
e
, the net
(V_{i})_{i\in I}
is decreasing, and the intersection of the
V_{i}
is equal to
\{e\}
.
Then, we let
e_{i}=\lambda(f_{i})
for every
i\in I
.

Let

C(G)
be the space of continuous functions on
G
. We first observe
that

(5.10)

\hbox{SOT-}\lim_{i}\lambda(hf_{i})=h(e),\qquad h\in C(G).

Indeed, given any
h\in C(G)
and
\xi\in L^{2}(G)
, we may write

\displaystyle\lambda(hf_{i})\xi-h(e)\xi

\displaystyle=\int_{G}f_{i}(t)\bigl(h(t)\lambda(t)\xi-h(e)\xi\bigr)\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{G}f_{i}(t)\bigl(h(t)-h(e)\bigr)\lambda(t)\xi\,dt\,+h(e)\int_{G}f_{i}(t)\bigl(\lambda(t)\xi-\xi\bigr)\,dt.

This implies

\bigl\|\lambda(hf_{i})\xi-h(e)\xi\bigr\|_{2}\leq\|\xi\|_{2}\int_{G}f_{i}(t)\bigl|h(t)-h(e)\bigr|\,dt\,+|h(e)|\int_{G}f_{i}(t)\|\lambda(t)\xi-\xi\|_{2}\,dt.

These two integrals tend to
0
when
i\to\infty
, which proves (
5.10
).
It is plain that property (
5.10
) holds with
f_{i}^{*}
instead of
f_{i}
.
Applying this with
h\equiv 1
, we obtain in particular that

(5.11)

\hbox{SOT-}\lim_{i}e_{i}=1\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad\hbox{SOT-}\lim_{i}e_{i}^{*}=1.

Let
S=M_{\phi,p}\colon L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)\to L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)
and let

J
be the Jordan isomorphism provided by Lemma
5.2
.
Let
s\in G
. We are going to apply the identity
(
5.5
) with

(5.12)

x=e_{i}\lambda(s)\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad a=e_{i}\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}}.

The latter belongs to
L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)
, by Remark
3.2
.
We note that by (
3.1
) and (
5.9
), we
can write alternatively

(5.13)

a=\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}}\lambda(\Delta^{-\frac{1}{p}}f_{i})\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad x=\Delta(s)^{-1}\lambda(\lambda^{\prime}(s)f_{i}).

In the following, we use either
(
5.12
) or (
5.13
)
to express
a
and
x
.

First, we write

ax=\Delta(s)^{-1}\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}}\lambda(\Delta^{-\frac{1}{p}}f_{i})\lambda(\lambda^{\prime}(s)f_{i})=\Delta(s)^{-1}\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}}\lambda\Bigl((\Delta^{-\frac{1}{p}}f_{i})\star(\lambda^{\prime}(s)f_{i})\Bigr).

Then by Remark
3.2
again, we have

S(ax)=\Delta(s)^{-1}\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}}\lambda\Bigl(\phi\bigl((\Delta^{-\frac{1}{p}}f_{i})\star(\lambda^{\prime}(s)f_{i})\bigr)\Bigr).

Likewise,

xa=\Delta(s)^{-1}\lambda\Bigl((\lambda^{\prime}(s)f_{i})\star f_{i}\Bigr)\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}},

and hence

S(xa)=\Delta(s)^{-1}\lambda\Bigl(\phi\bigl((\lambda^{\prime}(s)f_{i})\star f_{i}\bigr)\Bigr)\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}}

We also have
S(a)=\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}}\lambda(\phi\Delta^{-\frac{1}{p}}f_{i})
, and then

S(a)J(x)=\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}}\lambda(\phi\Delta^{-\frac{1}{p}}f_{i})J(e_{i}\lambda(s)).

Likewise,
S(a)=\lambda(\phi f_{i})\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}}
and we have

J(x)S(a)=J(e_{i}\lambda(s))\lambda(\phi f_{i})\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}}.

For any integer
n\geq 1
, we let

E_{n}=\{t\in G\,:\,n^{-1}\leq\Delta(t)\leq n\}
. We write
(
5.5
) using the above computations,
and we multiply the resulting identity on the left and on the right
by the indicator function
\chi_{E_{n}}
. We obtain the identity

(5.14)

\displaystyle\Delta(s)^{-1}\Bigl[\chi_{E_{n}}\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}}

\displaystyle\lambda\Bigl(\phi\bigl((\Delta^{-\frac{1}{p}}f_{i})\star(\lambda^{\prime}(s)f_{i})\bigr)\Bigr)\chi_{E_{n}}+\chi_{E_{n}}\lambda\Bigl(\phi\bigl((\lambda^{\prime}(s)f_{i})\star f_{i}\bigr)\Bigr)\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}}\chi_{E_{n}}\Bigr]

\displaystyle=\chi_{E_{n}}\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}}\lambda(\phi\Delta^{-\frac{1}{p}}f_{i})J(e_{i}\lambda(s))\chi_{E_{n}}+\chi_{E_{n}}J(e_{i}\lambda(s))\lambda(\phi f_{i})\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}}\chi_{E_{n}},

valid for all
i\in I
and all
n\geq 1
.
The function
\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}}\chi_{E_{n}}
is bounded, and therefore
the above identity holds in
B(L^{2}(G))
.

We momentarily fix
n\geq 1
. We let
i\to\infty
and unless otherwise stated,
we use SOT-convergence in the following limiting processes. We note that by
(
5.11
), we both have
e_{i}\lambda(s)\to\lambda(s)
and
(e_{i}\lambda(s))^{*}=\lambda(s)^{*}e_{i}^{*}\to\lambda(s)^{*}
.
Since
J
is
w^{*}
-continuous, it therefore follows from
[
2
, Lemma 2.2]
that

J(e_{i}\lambda(s))\longrightarrow J(\lambda(s)).

Next, using the continuity
of
\phi
and the fact that
\Delta(e)=1
,
we derive from (
5.10
) that

\lambda(\phi f_{i})\longrightarrow\phi(e)\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad\lambda(\phi\Delta^{-\frac{1}{p}}f_{i})\longrightarrow\phi(e).

Thus, we know the SOT-limit of the right hand side of (
5.14
).

Passing to the left hand side, we write

\bigl(\lambda^{\prime}(s)f_{i})\star f_{i}\bigr)(t)=\int_{G}[\lambda^{\prime}(s)f_{i}](u)f_{i}(u^{-1}t)\,du\ =\int_{G}f_{i}(us^{-1})f_{i}(u^{-1}t)\,du,

for any
t\in G
. We deduce

\displaystyle\lambda\Bigl(\phi\bigl((\lambda^{\prime}(s)f_{i})\star f_{i}\bigr)\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\int_{G^{2}}\phi(t)f_{i}(us^{-1})f_{i}(u^{-1}t)\lambda(t)\,dudt

\displaystyle=\int_{G^{2}}\phi(ut)f_{i}(us^{-1})f_{i}(t)\lambda(ut)\,dtdu

\displaystyle=\Delta(s)\int_{G^{2}}\phi(ust)f_{i}(u)f_{i}(t)\lambda(ust)\,dtdu.

Likewise,

\phi(s)\lambda\bigl((\lambda^{\prime}(s)f_{i})\star f_{i}\bigr)=\Delta(s)\int_{G^{2}}\phi(s)f_{i}(u)f_{i}(t)\lambda(ust)\,dtdu,

hence

\displaystyle\Bigl\|\lambda\Bigl(\phi\bigl((\lambda^{\prime}(s)f_{i})\star f_{i}\bigr)\Bigr)-\phi(s)

\displaystyle\lambda\bigl((\lambda^{\prime}(s)f_{i})\star f_{i}\bigr)\Bigr\|_{B(L^{2}(G))}

\displaystyle\leq\Delta(s)\int_{G^{2}}|\phi(ust)-\phi(s)|f_{i}(u)f_{i}(t)\,dtdu.

We deduce, by the continuity of
\phi
and the properties of the net
(f_{i})_{i\in I}
, that

(5.15)

\Bigl\|\lambda\Bigl(\phi\bigl((\lambda^{\prime}(s)f_{i})\star f_{i}\bigr)\Bigr)-\phi(s)\lambda\bigl((\lambda^{\prime}(s)f_{i})\star f_{i}\bigr)\Bigr\|_{B(L^{2}(G))}\longrightarrow 0,

when
i\to\infty
.
By (
5.9
), we have

\lambda\bigl((\lambda^{\prime}(s)f_{i})\star f_{i}\bigr)=\lambda(\lambda^{\prime}(s)f_{i})\lambda(f_{i})=\Delta(s)\lambda(f_{i})\lambda(s)\lambda(f_{i})=\Delta(s)e_{i}\lambda(s)e_{i}.

Since
(e_{i})_{i\in I}
is bounded, we deduce from (
5.11
) that

\lambda\bigl((\lambda^{\prime}(s)f_{i})\star f_{i}\bigr)\longrightarrow\Delta(s)\lambda(s).

Combining with (
5.15
), we obtain that

\lambda\Bigl(\phi\bigl((\lambda^{\prime}(s)f_{i})\star f_{i}\bigr)\Bigr)\longrightarrow\Delta(s)\phi(s)\lambda(s).

A similar calculation yields

\lambda\Bigl(\phi\bigl((\Delta^{-\frac{1}{p}}f_{i})\star(\lambda^{\prime}(s)f_{i})\bigr)\Bigr)\longrightarrow\Delta(s)\phi(s)\lambda(s).

Passing to the limit in (
5.14
), we deduce from these calculations that

\displaystyle\chi_{E_{n}}\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}}\phi(s)\lambda(s)\chi_{E_{n}}

\displaystyle+\chi_{E_{n}}\phi(s)\lambda(s)\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}}\chi_{E_{n}}

\displaystyle=\chi_{E_{n}}\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}}\phi(e)J(\lambda(s))\chi_{E_{n}}+\chi_{E_{n}}\phi(e)J(\lambda(s))\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}}\chi_{E_{n}}.

Let
A,X\in B(L^{2}(G))
be defined by

A=\chi_{E_{n}}\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}}\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad X=\chi_{E_{n}}\bigl(\phi(s)\lambda(s)-\phi(e)J(\lambda(s))\bigr)\chi_{E_{n}}.

Since

\chi_{E_{n}}^{2}=\chi_{E_{n}}\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad\chi_{E_{n}}\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}}=\chi_{E_{n}}\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}}\chi_{E_{n}}=\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}}\chi_{E_{n}},

the above identity reads

AX+XA=0.

According to the definition of
E_{n}
,
\sigma(A)\subset[n^{-\frac{1}{p}},n^{\frac{1}{p}}]
,
hence

\sigma(A)\cap\sigma(-A)=\emptyset
. It therefore follows from
[
3
, Theorem VII.2.1]

that
X=0
.
Thus,

\chi_{E_{n}}\bigl(\phi(s)\lambda(s)-\phi(e)J(\lambda(s))\bigr)\chi_{E_{n}}=0,\qquad n\geq 1.

Since
(E_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is non-decreasing and the union of the
E_{n}
is equal to
G
,
\chi_{E_{n}}\to 1
in the SOT.
Hence we deduce from above that

\phi(e)J(\lambda(s))=\phi(s)\lambda(s).

With this identity in hands, the last part of the proof of
[
2
, Theorem 3.7]

shows that
\overline{\phi(e)}\phi
is a character. Let
\widetilde{\phi}=\overline{\phi(e)}\phi
, then

M_{\widetilde{\phi},p}
is a positive isometry, by Corollary
4.3
. Since

M_{\widetilde{\phi},p}=\overline{\phi(e)}M_{\phi,p}
and

M_{\phi,p}
is a positive isometry, we have

\phi(e)=1
and therefore,

\phi=\widetilde{\phi}
is a character. This completes the proof in the
\sigma
-compact case.

If
G
is not
\sigma
-compact it suffices to apply the proof of
[
2
, Corollary 3.13]

to deduce the result from the above arguments. Details are left to the reader.
∎

We end this section with a result showing that in the case when

p\geq 2
, the onto assumption in Theorem
5.3
is superfluous. We do not know
if the same holds for
p<2
.

Proposition 5.4
.

Let
\phi\in L^{\infty}(G)
and let
2\leq p<\infty
. Assume that
\phi
is a bounded Fourier multiplier on
L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)
, and that the resulting operator
M_{\phi,p}\colon L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)\to L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)
is an isometry.
Then
M_{\phi,p}
is onto.

Proof.

We adapt the proof of
[
2
, Lemma 3.9]
. We assume that

M_{\phi,p}\colon L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)\to L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)
is an isometry.
In particular,
M_{\phi,p}
is injective.

Let
K\subset G
be an arbitrary compact
set and let
N_{K}=\{t\in K\,:\,\phi(t)=0\}
.
The indicator function

h=\chi_{N_{K}}
belongs to
L^{1}(G)\cap L^{2}(G)
, hence
\lambda(h)
to
{\mathcal{N}}
.
By Lemma
2.1
, (1) and Remark
3.4
,
\lambda(h)\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}}
belongs to
L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)

and
M_{\phi,p}\bigl(\lambda(h)\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}}\bigr)=\lambda(\phi h)\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}}
.
Since
\phi h=0
, this means that
M_{\phi,p}\bigl(\lambda(h)\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}}\bigr)=0
.
This implies
\lambda(h)=0
, and hence
h=0
. Thus,

(5.16)

|N_{K}|=0.

Let
f\in C_{c}(G)
and let

K={\rm Supp}(f)
.
For any
\delta>0
, set

N_{\delta}=\{t\in K\,:\,|\phi(t)|<\delta\}
.
Then it follows from (
5.16
) that

(5.17)

\lim_{\delta\to 0}|N_{\delta}|\,=0.

We may define

\phi^{-1}f\chi_{N_{\delta}^{c}}
, which belongs to
L^{1}(G)\cap L^{\infty}(G)
. Hence arguing as above,

\lambda\bigl(\phi^{-1}f\chi_{N_{\delta}^{c}}\bigr)\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}}\in L^{p}({\mathcal{L}}G)\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad M_{\phi}\Bigl(\lambda\bigl(\phi^{-1}f\chi_{N_{\delta}^{c}}\bigr)\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}}\Bigr)=\lambda\bigl(f\chi_{N_{\delta}^{c}}\bigr)\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}}.

Consequently,

M_{\phi,p}\Bigl(\lambda\bigl(\phi^{-1}f\chi_{N_{\delta}^{c}}\bigr)\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}}\Bigr)-\lambda(f)\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}}=-\lambda\bigl(f\chi_{N_{\delta}}\bigr)\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}}.

By (
3.5
), this implies that

\Bigl\|M_{\phi,p}\Bigl(\lambda\bigl(\phi^{-1}f\chi_{N_{\delta}^{c}}\bigr)\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}}\Bigr)-\lambda(f)\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}}\Bigr\|_{p}\leq\|f\chi_{N_{\delta}}\|_{p^{\prime}}\leq\|f\|_{\infty}|N_{\delta}|^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}.

According to (
5.17
), this implies that

\lambda(f)\Delta^{\frac{1}{p}}\in\overline{{\rm Ran}(M_{\phi,p})}.
By the density Lemma
2.2
, (1), this implies that the range of
M_{\phi,p}
is dense.
Since
M_{\phi,p}
is an isometry, this means that
M_{\phi,p}
is onto.
∎

6.
The cases
p=1
and
p=2

In this section we consider the endpoint cases
p=1
and
p=2
. We first show that Theorem
5.3
extends to the case
p=1
without assuming positivity, as follows.

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
\phi\in L^{\infty}(G)
be a bounded Fourier multiplier on
L^{1}({\mathcal{L}}G)
.
If the operator

M_{\phi,1}\colon L^{1}({\mathcal{L}}G)\to L^{1}({\mathcal{L}}G)

is an onto isometry, then there exists a complex number
\delta
with
|\delta|=1

such that
\delta\phi
is locally almost everywhere equal to a continuous
character.

Proof.

As in the proof of Theorem
5.3
, we may assume
for simplicity that
G
is
\sigma
-compact.
Let
T=M_{\phi,1}^{*}\colon{\mathcal{L}}G\to{\mathcal{L}}G
. The argument in the proof of
Proposition
3.5
shows that

T(\lambda(g))=\lambda(\check{\phi}g)
for all
g\in\mbox{${\mathcal{H}}$}
.
This implies that

\|\lambda(\check{\phi}g)\|\leq\|\lambda(g)\|,\qquad g\in L^{1}(G).

Then,
[
4
, Proposition 1.2, (3)
\Rightarrow
(2)]
and its
proof show that
the function
\phi
belongs to
C_{b}(G)
, that
\check{\phi}
is a bounded
Fourier multiplier on
{\mathcal{L}}G
and that
T=M_{\check{\phi}}
.

Since
M_{\phi,1}
is an onto isometry, its adjoint

T
is an onto isometry as well. Hence by Kadison’s non-commutative
Banach-Stone theorem from
[
21
]
,
there exist a Jordan
isomorphism
J\colon{\mathcal{L}}G\to{\mathcal{L}}G

and a unitary
U\in{\mathcal{L}}G
such that

T(x)=UJ(x),\qquad x\in{\mathcal{L}}G.

Consider an arbitrary
s\in G
.
Let
(e_{i})_{i\in I}
be the net introduced in the proof of Theorem

5.3
.
It follows from
[
2
, Lemma 3.11]
(which does not use
any unimodularity assumption) that the following SOT-convergences hold:

(6.1)

T(\lambda(s)e_{i})\to\check{\phi}(s)\lambda(s),\quad T(\lambda(s)e_{i}^{2})\to\check{\phi}(s)\lambda(s)\quad\hbox{and}\quad T\bigl(e_{i}\lambda(s)e_{i}\bigr)\to\check{\phi}(s)\lambda(s).

Moreover it follows from an observation in the proof of Theorem

5.3
that

(6.2)

J(\lambda(s)e_{i})\longrightarrow J(\lambda(s)).

By the first half of (
5.2
), we have

J\bigl(e_{i}\lambda(s)e_{i}+\lambda(s)e_{i}^{2}\bigr)=J(e_{i})J\bigl(\lambda(s)e_{i}\bigr)+J\bigl(\lambda(s)e_{i}\bigr)J(e_{i}).

After left multiplication by
U
, this means that

T\bigl(e_{i}\lambda(s)e_{i}\bigr)+T\bigl(\lambda(s)e_{i}^{2}\bigr)=T(e_{i})J\bigl(\lambda(s)e_{i}\bigr)+T\bigl(\lambda(s)e_{i}\bigr)J(e_{i}).

Passing to the SOT-limit when
i\to\infty
and applying (
6.1
) and (
6.2
), we deduce that

\check{\phi}(s)\lambda(s)+\check{\phi}(s)\lambda(s)=\check{\phi}(e)J(\lambda(s))+\check{\phi}(s)\lambda(s)
, that is,

\check{\phi}(e)J(\lambda(s))=\check{\phi}(s)\lambda(s),\qquad s\in G.

We can now conclude as in the proof of Theorem
5.3
that

\check{\phi}(e)^{-1}\check{\phi}
is a character and the result follows at once.
∎

Remark 6.2
.

Let
\phi\in C_{b}(G)
such that
\phi

is a bounded Fourier multiplier on

{\mathcal{L}}G
and assume that
M_{\phi}\colon{\mathcal{L}}G\to{\mathcal{L}}G
is an isometry.
(See the paragraph preceding Corollary
4.4

for definitions.) Then
|\phi(t)|=\|M_{\phi}(\lambda(t))\|=1

for all
t\in G
, hence
\lambda(G)\subset{\rm Ran}(M_{\phi})
. Since
{\rm Ran}(M_{\phi})
is

w^{*}
-closed, this implies that
M_{\phi}
is onto.
Then, the proof of Proposition
6.1
shows as well
the existence of a complex number
\delta

with
|\delta|=1
such that
\delta\phi
is a character.

We now turn to the case
p=2
.
Until Lemma
6.4
, we consider any Connes-Hilsum
non-commutative
L^{2}
-space
L^{2}(M)
built upon
an arbitrary von Neumann algebra
M
. In

[
19
]
, Junge introduced
\ell^{1}
-valued
non-commutative spaces
L^{p}(M;\ell^{1})
. Here, we consider

L^{2}(M;\ell^{1}_{2})
obtained by restricting to the case

p=2
and to the situation when
\ell^{1}
is replaced by the
2-dimensional space
\ell^{1}_{2}
. Thus,
L^{2}(M;\ell^{1}_{2})
is the
direct sum

L^{2}(M)\oplus L^{2}(M)
equipped with
a specific norm. We refer e.g. to
[
24
]
for information on this norm
(that we will not explicitly use here).

We say that two
elements
a,b\in L^{2}(M)
are disjoint if
a^{*}b=0
and
ab^{*}=0
.
Then, we say that a bounded map
S\colon L^{2}(M)\to L^{2}(M)
is separating
if, for all disjoint
a
and
b
in
L^{2}(M)
,
the images
S(a)
and
S(b)
are disjoint.
This terminology extends the one in
[
24
, Section 3]

to the possibly non tracial case.

Lemma 6.3
.

(1)

Let
a,b\in L^{2}(M)
. Then,
a
and
b
are disjoint if and only if

\bigl\|(a,b)\bigr\|_{L^{2}(M;\ell^{1}_{2})}\leq\bigl(\|a\|_{2}^{2}+\|b\|_{2}^{2}\bigr)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(2)

Let
S\colon L^{2}(M)\to L^{2}(M)
be a 2-positive isometry. Then
S
is separating.

Proof.

The assertion (1) is proved as
[
24
, Lemma 4.1]
in the case when
L^{2}(M)
is a tracial
non-commutative
L^{2}
-space. The proof extends verbatim to the general case.

Now let
S\colon L^{2}(M)\to L^{2}(M)
be a 2-positive isometry. Then by

[
24
, Proposition 5.1]
, we have

(6.3)

\|(S(a),S(b))\|_{L^{2}(M;\ell^{1}_{2})}\leq\bigl\|(a,b)\bigr\|_{L^{2}(M;\ell^{1}_{2})}.

Again this result is proved for tracial spaces only but it extends to the general case.

Let
a,b\in L^{2}(M)
be disjoint elements. Then combining part (1) and (
6.3
), we obtain

\|(S(a),S(b))\|_{L^{2}(M;\ell^{1}_{2})}\leq\bigl(\|a\|_{2}^{2}+\|b\|_{2}^{2}\bigr)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Since
\|S(a)\|_{2}=\|a\|_{2}
and
\|S(b)\|_{2}=\|b\|_{2}
, this shows, by part (1) again, that

S(a)
and
S(b)
are disjoint.
∎

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
S\colon L^{2}(M)\to L^{2}(M)
be a positive onto isometry. Then

S^{-1}
is positive.

Proof.

An element
a\in L^{2}(M)
is positive if and only if

(a|b)\geq 0
for all
b\in L^{2}(M)^{+}
. Using this criterion,
we see that
S^{*}
is positive if
S\colon L^{2}(M)\to L^{2}(M)
is positive.
If further
S
is an onto isometry, then

S^{-1}=S^{*}
, hence
S^{-1}
is positive.
∎

Recall from Remark
4.4
that for any

S\colon L^{2}(M)\to L^{2}(M)
, we let

S_{2}\colon L^{2}(M_{2}(M))\longrightarrow L^{2}(M_{2}(M))

be its natural tensor extension.
The following is an analogue of Theorem
5.3
for
p=2
.
This is a converse to the fact that if
\phi
is a continuous
character on
G
, then
M_{\phi,2}
is a completely isometric onto isometry
(see Corollary
4.3
and Remark
4.4
).

Theorem 6.5
.

Let
\phi\in L^{\infty}(G)
. If the operator

S:=M_{\phi,2}\colon L^{2}({\mathcal{L}}G)\to L^{2}({\mathcal{L}}G)

is such that
S_{2}
is a positive isometry, then
\phi
is locally
almost everywhere equal to a continuous character.

Proof.

Assume that for
S=M_{\phi,2}
, the operator

S_{2}
is a positive isometry. Then
S
is a
2
-positive
isometry hence by Lemma
6.3
,

S
is separating. By Proposition
5.4
,
S
is onto, which implies that
S_{2}

is onto. Then, applying Lemma
6.4
with
S_{2}
, we obtain that

S_{2}^{-1}
is positive. Thus,
S^{-1}
is a 2-positive isometry
hence the above reasoning shows that
S^{-1}
is
separating as well. Now a thorough look at Sherman’s proof
reveals that
[
30
, Theorem 1.2]

holds for any onto isometry between non-commutative

L^{2}
-spaces that is separating, as well as its inverse.
Then the proofs of Lemma
5.2
and Theorem
5.3
apply
to
S=M_{\phi,2}

if
S_{2}
is a positive isometry.
∎

We do not know if Theorem
5.3
fully extends to
p=2
.
