Microneedles for allergy skin testing: Comprehensive analysis of agreement, accuracy, and pain
Authors: Manaporn Sirichuwong, Kantima Kanchanapoomi, Surapon Piboonpocanun, Witchaya Srisuwatchari, Punchama Pacharn, Yossawat Rayanasukha, Nattaporn Paetanom, Kittipong Tantisantisom, Sattra Thongma, Supone Manakasettharn, Orathai Jirapongsananuruk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe skin prick test (SPT) is a standard method for identifying allergic sensitization. However, pain and needle phobia often compromise pediatric compliance. Microneedle devices offer a minimally invasive alternative to conventional lancets.ObjectiveWe evaluated the agreement, diagnostic accuracy, and patient tolerability of a novel microneedle compared to a lancet for SPT.MethodsSubjects with allergic rhinitis underwent SPT using both devices with Dermatophagoides pteronyssinus (Dp) extract, histamine, and saline controls. Net mean wheal diameters (MWD) were calculated by subtracting the saline MWD from the Dp and histamine MWDs. Agreement was assessed by Bland-Altman analysis and Cohen kappa (k), diagnostic performance by receiver operating characteristic analysis, and pain by a visual analog scale.ResultsOf 150 subjects (aged 8-60 years), 53.3% were sensitized to Dp. Both devices produced significantly larger Dp and histamine MWDs than saline MWD (P < .001). Median [interquartile range] Dp MWD was smaller with microneedle (2 [0-8.13] mm) than lancet (4 [0-12.5 mm], P < .001). For net Dp MWD, Bland-Altman analysis indicated a mean bias of −2.1 mm for microneedle, with 93.3% within the limits of agreement. With lancet used as the reference, the microneedle had an area under the curve of 0.904. At a 3 mm cutoff, sensitivity was 82.5%, specificity 94.3%, and accuracy 86%. Interdevice concordance was good (k = 0.76). Median pain scores were significantly lower for microneedle (2.5 [0-9]) compared to lancet (8.0 [1-26], P < .001).ConclusionThe microneedle demonstrates high diagnostic performance and good concordance with the standard lancet while significantly reducing pain, thus supporting its utility in pediatric and needle-averse populations.Key words: Accuracy, concordance, diagnostic test, lancet, microneedle, pain, sensitivity, skin prick test, specificity, visual analog scale

The skin prick test (SPT) is the standard procedure for identifying allergen sensitization in patients with IgE-mediated allergic diseases, including allergic rhinitis, asthma, atopic dermatitis, and food, drug, and insect sting allergies.1 Since its inception in 1873, the SPT has remained a widely adopted diagnostic tool in clinical allergy practice.2

Mechanistically, the SPT introduces allergens into the dermis, where they encounter mast cells.3 Allergen-specific IgE bound to mast cell membranes binds and cross-links these allergens, leading to mast cell degranulation and release of mediators such as histamine. These mediators induce localized skin inflammation, resulting in a characteristic wheal-and-flare reaction. In clinical practice, the interpretation of SPT results may be influenced by variables such as device types, allergen extract quality, anatomic test site, and concomitant medications.4, 5, 6, 7

Although the SPT is simple to perform and provides rapid results, its clinical utility may be compromised by pain-related distress in pediatric and needle-phobic populations.8 Consequently, there is a clinical need for novel diagnostic devices that minimize discomfort without compromising diagnostic accuracy.

Microneedles are micron-scale projections (50-2000 μm) designed to penetrate the stratum corneum and target the dermis while minimizing the stimulation of dermal nociceptors, thereby reducing pain.9, 10, 11, 12, 13 They are fabricated in various forms and used for the transdermal delivery of small molecules, biologics, and vaccines.9, 10, 11,13,14 This technology is rapidly evolving, with expanding diagnostic and therapeutic applications in the medical field. While microneedle technology offers a theoretically painless alternative for percutaneous allergen delivery, there is a paucity of clinical data thoroughly comparing the diagnostic concordance, sensitivity, and specificity of multiwell microneedle systems against the established lancet reference standard. This lack of validation prevents the adoption of more patient-centric diagnostic modalities in clinical practice.

The present study aimed to evaluate the clinical validation and diagnostic performance of a novel microneedle device for allergy skin testing. We assessed the agreement, diagnostic accuracy, and pain tolerability of a microneedle-based device compared to the conventional lancet reference standard.

Methods

Subjects

The study was conducted at the Division of Allergy and Immunology, Department of Pediatrics, Siriraj Hospital, Mahidol University, and was approved by the local institutional review board. Written informed consent was obtained before study participation. A total of 150 subjects aged 8 to 60 years with allergic rhinitis were enrolled. Exclusion criteria included skin infections or lesions on the back, drug or food allergies, history of anaphylaxis, chronic urticaria lasting more than 6 weeks, pregnancy, dermographism, current use of topical steroids on the upper back, or receipt of oral antihistamines within 7 days before the SPT.

Microneedle device

The novel microneedle device, illustrated in Fig 1, measures 3 × 12 cm. The device consists of two layers (Fig 1, A). The top layer is a needle holder (NH) pad, a nonwoven pad commonly used in bandages. The bottom layer beneath the NH pad consists of a waterproof transparent adhesive film (3M, Maplewood, Minn), for attachment to the skin. The NH pad is divided into 3 wells spaced 3 cm apart. Three flaps of the NH pad can be flipped open. An array of microneedles (1000 μm in length) is mounted at the center of each NH flap. The microneedles are made of a biocompatible acrylate-based polymer and manufactured under sterile conditions. They were tested for in vitro cytotoxicity in accordance with ISO 10993-5:2009 and the ISO 13485 quality management standard for medical devices. This device is approved by the Thai food and drug administration (67-1-2-2-0000541) and protected by utility and design patents.

SPT procedure

This study used Dermatophagoides pteronyssinus (Dp) extract (ALK-Abelló, Hørsholm, Denmark), histamine (10 mg/mL) as a positive control, and saline as a negative control. A standard metal lancet (Stallergenes, Sydney, Australia) served as the reference device. To minimize bias, the sequence of SPT using the lancet or microneedle device was randomly assigned. Testing was performed on the back, with subjects placed prone so they could not see the device during the procedure.

All procedures were performed by a single physician (K.K.). Before SPT, the skin on the back was examined for suitability and subsequently cleansed with an alcohol pad. The microneedle procedure was performed as follows. The bottom adhesive layer was removed and the device applied to the skin (Fig 1, B). Each NH pad flap was lifted, and a drop of Dp extract, histamine, or saline was spotted in the designated well. The flap was then closed and pressed onto the skin. At completion, the microneedle device was gently removed, and excess solution was patted dry. For the lancet method, a drop of each solution was spotted on the skin at 3 cm apart, followed by skin pricking according to standard procedures. At completion, the excess solution was gently patted dry.

Pain was assessed by visual analog scale scores.15,16 Subjects rated pain immediately after testing with each device. After 15 minutes, skin reactions were measured as mean wheal diameter (MWD), calculated as the average of the longest wheal diameter and its perpendicular diameter, by a single technician. In general, the SPT results from the lancet are reported as the difference between the MWD of the allergen extract and the negative control. Results were considered positive if the MWD from the antigen extract was ≥3 mm larger than the negative control.17 Therefore, the MWD data for histamine or Dp extract used in the Bland-Altman, Cohen kappa, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses were derived from the subtraction of MWD from saline and were designated as “net histamine MWD” and “net Dp MWD.”

Sample size

The primary objective was to evaluate the performance of microneedle-based SPT compared to the standard lancet in patients with allergic rhinitis. A 95% confidence level and a 2-sided type I error rate of 0.025 were applied. Sample size estimation was conducted by nQuery Advisor software on the basis of pilot data from 30 subjects (data not shown). The calculation determined that there were 75 subjects with dust mite allergy and 75 without dust mite allergy, as determined by lancet-based SPT.

Statistical analysis

Demographic characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics. Comparative analyses of MWD and pain scores between devices were performed by Wilcoxon signed-rank test, while the time required for each device was compared by paired t test. Agreement between the microneedle device and the lancet was evaluated by Bland-Altman analysis for continuous outcomes and Cohen kappa (k) for categorical outcomes.18,19 With the lancet used as the reference standard, a ROC curve analysis was conducted to determine the optimal cutoff value and diagnostic performance of the microneedle device. A positive test result for the lancet was defined as net Dp MWD ≥ 3 mm, whereas the cutoff for the microneedle device was determined on the basis of the optimal threshold identified by ROC analysis.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 150 subjects aged 8-60 years were enrolled, including 67 male subjects (44.7%). Of these, 58 (38.7%) were aged 8-12 years, 39 (26%) were aged >12-18 years, and 53 (35.3%) were aged 18-60 years. All subjects had a diagnosis of allergic rhinitis, and 8% had concomitant asthma. Sensitization to Dp was observed in 80 subjects (53.3%).

SPT results using lancet and microneedle device

The MWDs of the SPT with saline, histamine, and Dp extract using the lancet and microneedle device are shown in Fig 2. For both devices, MWDs induced by histamine and Dp extract were significantly larger than those induced by saline (P < .001). The median (interquartile range [IQR]) MWD for saline was 0 (0-0) for both the lancet and the microneedle device (P = .28). The median (IQR) MWD for histamine was 6.0 (5.0-7.5) for the lancet and 5.5 (4.0-7.5) for the microneedle device (P = .001). The median (IQR) MWD for Dp extract was 4.0 (0-12.5) for the lancet and 2.0 (0-8.13) for the microneedle device (P < .001). Subgroup analysis stratified by Dp sensitization status was performed. Among Dp-sensitized participants, the lancet produced significantly larger wheal diameters than the microneedle device (median [IQR], 11.8 [6.6-16.0] mm vs 6.5 [4.0-12.4] mm, P < .001). In contrast, among nonsensitized participants, both devices produced minimal wheal responses with no significant difference between the lancet and microneedle device (median [IQR], 0 [0-2] mm vs 0 [0-0] mm, P = .74).

Agreement between lancet and microneedle device and diagnostic performance of microneedle device

When histamine was used for SPT, Bland-Altman analysis showed that the microneedle device produced a net histamine MWD that was, on average, 0.8 mm smaller than that produced by the lancet (Fig 3, A). The 95% (mean ± 1.96 standard deviation) limit of agreement ranged from −6.7 to 5.2 mm, with 94.7% of data points falling within these limits. The upward trend of the regression line indicated that for MWDs <6 mm, the microneedle device tended to underestimate the lancet, whereas for MWDs >6 mm, it tended to overestimate the lancet.

Bland-Altman plots showing difference versus average of MWDs between microneedle device and lancet. A, Net histamine response (histamine minus saline). B, Net Dp extract response (Dp extract minus saline).

When Dp extract was used for SPT, Bland-Altman analysis demonstrated that the microneedle device produced a net Dp MWD that was, on average, 2.1 mm smaller than that of the lancet (Fig 3, B). The limit of agreement ranged from −11.9 to 7.7 mm, with 93.3% of data points falling within these limits. A downward shift of the regression line suggested that for MWDs <6 mm, the microneedle tended to overestimate the lancet, whereas for MWDs >6 mm, it tended to underestimate the lancet.

To determine the optimal cutoff value for net Dp MWD obtained using the microneedle device, ROC curve analysis was performed with the lancet as the reference standard. For all participants, the area under the curve was 0.904 (Fig 4). The optimal cutoff value, defined by the highest Youden index, was 3 mm, yielding a sensitivity of 82.5%, specificity of 94.3%, positive predictive value (PPV) of 94.3%, negative predictive value (NPV) of 82.5%, overall accuracy of 86%, positive likelihood ratio (LR+) of 14.4, and negative likelihood ratio (LR−) of 0.2.

Diagnostic performance was further evaluated by age group. The areas under the curve for children (8-12 years), adolescents (>12-18 years), and adults (>18-60 years) were 0.968, 0.853, and 0.861, respectively, with 3 mm as the optimal cutoff in all groups. For children aged 8-12 years, sensitivity was 96.9%, specificity 92.3%, PPV 93.9%, NPV 96.0%, accuracy 94.8%, LR+ 12.6, and LR− 0.03. For adolescents, sensitivity was 76.0%, specificity 92.9%, PPV 95.0%, NPV 68.4%, accuracy 82.1%, LR+ 10.7, and LR− 0.26. For adults, the sensitivity was 69.6%, specificity 96.7%, PPV 94.1%, NPV 80.6%, accuracy 84.9%, LR+ 21.1, and LR− 0.31.

Agreement between the microneedle device and the lancet for positive or negative SPT results based on net Dp MWD was further assessed by Cohen kappa. At the 3.0 mm cutoff, the k value was 0.76, indicating good agreement between the two devices.19

Discussion

Although the lancet is widely used for SPT, several alternative devices have been developed. The performance and validation of these devices have been investigated. Comparisons among different SPT devices revealed significant differences in the histamine-induced wheal sizes. Nelson et al compared several SPT devices, including Multi-Test II, Duo Tip-Test, Quintest, the smallpox needle, and DermaPik, using histamine and glycerol-saline.20 They reported significant interdevice differences in MWD for histamine (4.28 to 8.59 mm) and glycerol-saline (0 to 2.48 mm). Werther et al performed histamine SPT using Greer Pick, Quintip, Stallergenes Lancet, and Feather Lancet, with MWDs ranging from 3.86 to 6.05 mm.21 They found that the Stallergenes Lancet exhibited the least variability. Overall, devices using the puncture method demonstrated less variability than those using the prick method. These interdevice differences have been corroborated by other studies.22,23 Notably, these previous studies relied solely on histamine as a positive control and did not assess antigen-induced responses. This is a critical distinction: Allergen-induced histamine release in the skin may not correlate completely with wheal size induced by histamine alone.24

In general, more traumatic devices tend to produce larger MWDs.17 Consequently, developing an SPT device that produces reactions identical to conventional methods is challenging. Therefore, device-specific cutoff wheal sizes should be established to optimize SPT performance (eg, ALK lancet, 3.0 mm; bifurcated needle prick, 4.0 mm; bifurcated needle puncture, 4.5 mm; Multi-Test device, 5.0 mm; and DermaPik device, 5.5 mm).17

To understand the mechanism of our device, it is essential to consider that the skin consists of the epidermis (50-200 μm thick) and the dermis (1.5-3 mm thick). The dermis contains an extensive nervous system and a dense network of immune cells, including mast cells. Microneedles for skin testing are designed to penetrate the stratum corneum and viable epidermis into the superficial dermis while avoiding injury to nerve fibers and blood vessels located in the deep dermal layer.11,25, 26, 27, 28 With this principle, Hackethal applied 750 μm stainless steel microneedle arrays and administered histamine to both microneedle-treated and untreated skin.29 Histamine penetrated the skin more rapidly after microneedle pretreatment than in untreated skin, as evidenced by wheal and flare responses. This result demonstrated that microneedles create microchannels in the epidermis, enabling transcutaneous substance delivery and inducing a microvascular response.

Novel microneedles for SPT should reach the upper dermis, where mast cells reside, while avoiding neural injury to minimize pain.9, 10, 11 Accordingly, microneedle length is critical for eliciting a specific skin reaction without nonspecific irritation. Spina et al compared adhesive-tape stripping, single-prick lancets, and microneedle arrays for epicutaneous allergen delivery using graded concentrations of birch pollen extract.30 The microneedle patch comprised 352 microneedles, each <100 μm in length. All subjects exhibited immediate reactions with both lancet and microneedle testing. In contrast, only 10% showed reactions with adhesive-tape stripping, and only at the highest allergen concentration (10 HEP/mL). Most lancet reactions (95%) occurred at ≤0.1 HEP/mL, whereas 50% of microneedle reactions required ≥1 HEP/mL. Skin test responses differed significantly among the 3 devices (P < .001). These findings suggest that microneedles shorter than 100 μm may be insufficient to penetrate the dermis for effective mast cell activation. Another study by Gong et al reported that microneedles measuring 0.8-1.0 mm in length on SPT tape with allergen chambers demonstrated good concordance with lancet-based SPT using house dust mite extract.31 Together, these findings highlight needle length as a key determinant of optimum skin reactivity.

Our study demonstrated that SPT performed with both the lancet and the microneedle device provided MWDs that significantly differentiated histamine and Dp extract from saline (Fig 2). The median MWD difference between the two devices was 0.5 mm for histamine and 2.0 mm for Dp extract. Both differences fall within the 2 mm equivalent threshold and below the 3 mm clinically significant threshold.22,32 This systematic difference is likely attributable to the depth of penetration and the volume of allergen delivered. The lancet typically penetrates deeper and causes more mechanical trauma to the skin, potentially eliciting a larger inflammatory response. In contrast, the microneedle device is engineered to target the upper dermis, minimizing mechanical irritation while successfully reaching cutaneous mast cells required for the IgE-mediated response.

Bland-Altman analysis for the net Dp response indicated that the microneedle device produced an average 2.1 mm smaller MWD than the lancet’s (Fig 3, B). The regression trend suggested overestimation by the microneedle at average MWDs of <6 mm, whereas differences diminished at higher values, with relative underestimation. This attenuation may contribute to reducing patient discomfort from large Dp-induced reactions, as maximum MWDs were smaller with the microneedle device than with the lancet (23.5 vs 35 mm; Fig 2). Furthermore, smaller wheals may prevent wheal coalescence when testing multiple allergens in close proximity.

In clinical practice, physicians prioritize diagnostic reliability (positive vs negative results) to determine sensitization status. Previous studies comparing multiple histamine SPT devices reported false-negative rates ranging 0-45%, defined by wheal response <3 mm and/or <2 mm greater than the diluent control.20, 21, 22, 23 Our study observed good agreement (k = 0.76) between the two devices.19 At the 3.0 mm cutoff, the microneedle device achieved 94.3% specificity and 82.5% sensitivity, along with high PPV and LR+ (Fig 4). The false-negative rate was 17.5%, consistent with previous reports.20, 21, 22, 23 These results suggested that the smaller absolute MWD did not compromise diagnostic performance. Overall, diagnostic performance was highest in children and decreased with increasing age, primarily due to lower sensitivity in adolescents and adults while maintaining high specificity, PPV, and LR+ across all groups.

Pain scores were significantly lower with the microneedle device than with the lancet across all age groups, with the greatest reduction observed in younger participants. This marked reduction in discomfort may improve acceptance among patients who exhibit fear or refusal when undergoing SPT.

Although the time required for the microneedle device was longer than for the lancet, this is likely due to its novel design utilizing multiple flaps. Further optimization of the patch design is warranted to improve clinical usability.

This study has several limitations. First, the microneedle device exhibited a systematic negative bias of 2.1 mm in MWD compared to the lancet. Although an optimal 3.0 mm cutoff was identified via ROC analysis, traditional diagnostic criteria may require specific calibration for microneedle-based devices. Second, this study focused primarily on house dust mite (Dp) sensitization. Reactivity may vary across other extracts, such as pollen or food proteins, as a result of differing viscosities and protein concentrations; thus, future research should confirm if the 2.1 mm bias persists across broader allergen panels. Third, the study was conducted by a single investigator at a single clinical center. While this ensured consistency, it may limit the generalizability of the findings across different clinical settings and providers. Fourth, the cost of the microneedle device is likely higher than that of the conventional lancet. Further studies evaluating cost-effectiveness and willingness to pay are warranted. Finally, the microneedle application time was longer than that of the conventional lancet. Future patch designs should simplify the sequence to shorten procedural time in high-volume clinics.

In conclusion, the microneedle device demonstrated good agreement and high diagnostic performance relative to the conventional lancet while significantly reducing procedural pain. Although application time was slightly longer, improved tolerability may enhance patient compliance and overall testing experience, particularly in pediatric and needle-averse populations. With its simplified, self-contained design and integrated allergen wells, microneedle-based SPT represents a promising and practical alternative for allergy diagnostics, especially in settings where conventional methods are less acceptable.

Clinical implication

The microneedle device for SPT revealed good agreement with the conventional lancet, with 94.3% specificity, 82.5% sensitivity, and 86% accuracy, while significantly reducing procedural pain.
