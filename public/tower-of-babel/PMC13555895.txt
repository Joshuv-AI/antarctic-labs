Lameness-associated changes in milk yield, composition, and inflammatory quality indicators in dairy cows under tropical conditions in Bangladesh
Authors: Solama Akter Shanta, Mst Tahomina Akter, Muhammad Aktaruzzaman, Md Siam Ahmed, A K M Anisur Rahman, M Ariful Islam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLameness is a major welfare and economic challenge in dairy herds and is associated with reduced milk yield and compromised milk quality; however, evidence under tropical production systems such as Bangladesh remains limited. This observational study was conducted at a commercial dairy farm in Mymensingh, Bangladesh (July 2024-June 2025) to evaluate the effects of lameness on milk yield and inflammatory milk quality indicators. Sixteen Holstein-Friesian lactating cows were enrolled, including 12 clinically lame cows and 4 healthy controls. Lame cows were observed at three predefined stages: 7 days before clinical diagnosis, on the diagnostic day, and 7 days after diagnosis; healthy cows were sampled once as baseline references. Lameness was assessed using a standardized 1–5 locomotion scoring system. Milk yield was obtained from farm records, and milk quality traits, including pH, electrical conductivity, somatic cell count (SCC), fat, protein, lactose, solid-not-fat, and total solids, were analyzed using Ekomilk Horizon Unlimited. Multivariable linear mixed-effects models revealed a significant reduction in milk yield during lameness (15.0 +/- 0.6 L/day) compared with pre-diagnosis values (19.0 +/- 0.5 L/day; p < 0.001), representing a 21% decline. SCC increased more than two-fold (812.8 +/- 45.6 × 10^3 cells/mL; p < 0.001), accompanied by significant increases in electrical conductivity, pH, milk fat, lactose, and total solids. Diagnostic-day lameness showed strong positive correlations with SCC (r = 0.84) and electrical conductivity (r = 0.89), and a strong negative correlation with milk yield (r = -0.76). These findings indicate that lameness is associated with measurable changes in milk quality and reduced milk production under tropical Bangladeshi field conditions.Keywords: Lameness, Dairy cow, Milk yield, Milk quality, Somatic cell count, Electrical conductivity, Bangladesh

Introduction

Lameness is widely recognized as one of the most prevalent health and welfare disorders in dairy cattle and represents a substantial economic burden to the global dairy industry. Meta-analyses indicate that the average prevalence of clinical lameness in dairy herds ranges from 20 to 30%, with even higher rates reported in intensively managed systems [1, 2]. Economically, lameness contributes to losses through reduced milk yield, treatment costs, impaired reproductive performance, premature culling, and labor inefficiencies such as additional time required for moving, treating, monitoring, and segregating affected cows. These losses arise primarily from decreased milk production, prolonged calving intervals, and increased replacement rates [3–5].

Beyond locomotor impairment, lameness is increasingly recognized as a systemic inflammatory condition rather than solely a mechanical disorder. Pain-induced stress responses alter feeding behavior, reduce dry matter intake, and trigger metabolic and endocrine changes that compromise milk synthesis [5–7]. Studies report that lame cows produce 1.5–4.0 L less milk per day compared with healthy cows, depending on severity and chronicity [4, 5, 8]. In developed dairy systems, the estimated economic loss per lame cow ranges from $200–500 US per lactation depending on severity and duration [3, 9]. Inflammatory mediators may influence mammary epithelial permeability, leading to increased somatic cell count (SCC), altered milk pH, and elevated electrical conductivity, recognized indicators of mammary inflammation and compromised milk quality [10, 11]. Elevated SCC also adversely affects milk processing properties, cheese yield, and shelf life, further amplifying economic losses [10, 12].

Although the epidemiology and economic consequences of lameness have been extensively studied in high-income dairy systems, evidence from tropical production environments remains comparatively scarce. Climatic heat stress, prolonged standing on hard concrete flooring, nutritional imbalances, inadequate manure management, and limited access to preventive hoof-care services in developing countries may compound both the incidence and severity of lameness [13, 14]. Emerging evidence from tropical Asia—including Malaysia, India, Thailand, and Pakistan—indicates that hot-humid conditions, intensive housing, and breed-specific susceptibility may amplify lameness prevalence and its associated production penalties [15–18]. Importantly, SCC thresholds for raw milk acceptability (≤ 400,000 cells/mL per IDF guidelines) are more readily exceeded in herds with high lameness prevalence, with direct implications for market access and farmgate income in developing dairy markets [12].

In Bangladesh, the dairy sector has expanded rapidly over the past decade, driven by increasing demand for animal protein and by livestock development initiatives, including expansion of crossbred cattle, artificial insemination services, farmer training, and market-oriented dairy support programs. According to the Bangladesh Bureau of Statistics, the national cattle population exceeds 24 million, with a growing proportion of crossbred and high-yielding cows managed under semi-intensive and commercial systems [19]. Recent field investigations report lameness prevalence ranging from 15 to 25% in selected dairy-producing regions, particularly in tie-stall systems with wet or uneven concrete floors, limited hoof care, and inadequate manure removal [20, 21]. However, the impact of lameness on milk yield and inflammatory milk quality indicators under Bangladeshi conditions remains insufficiently quantified. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the effects of lameness on milk yield, composition, and inflammatory milk quality indicators in dairy cows under tropical field conditions in Bangladesh.

Materials and methods

Study area and herd management

The study was conducted at a commercial dairy farm located in Mymensingh Sadar, Bangladesh, from July 2024 to June 2025. The herd comprised approximately 110 Holstein-Friesian dairy cows managed under semi-intensive production conditions. Cows were housed in concrete-floored barns and milked twice daily. The feeding regimen consisted of green fodder, a farm-formulated concentrate mixture, and mineral supplementation according to lactation stage and farm management practice. Routine veterinary supervision and herd health monitoring were maintained throughout the study period. The selected animals received FMD and LSD vaccines and underwent routine deworming against internal parasites. Farm-level temperature and humidity records, as well as detailed concentrate composition, were not available in the farm record book.

Study design and animal selection

A longitudinal, observational study design was implemented. Sixteen lactating cows were deliberately selected based on locomotor health status (lame vs. non-lame) and broadly comparable lactation stage. The study population comprised 12 clinically lame cows and 4 clinically healthy, non-lame cows serving as controls. The unequal group size reflected the field-based objective of following naturally occurring clinical lameness cases while retaining a small healthy reference group. The body weight of enrolled animals ranged between 450 and 600 kg; individual body weight data by health group were not available for inclusion as a model covariate. Lame cows were identified by farm identification numbers and monitored at three predefined time points: (i) 7 days before clinical diagnosis (pre-diagnostic stage), (ii) on the day of confirmed clinical lameness (diagnostic stage), and (iii) 7 days after diagnosis (post-diagnostic stage). Healthy cows were evaluated once during the same period to provide baseline reference values.

Sample size justification

Sample size estimation was based on previously reported reductions in milk yield associated with clinical lameness, typically 15%-25% [3–5]. Assuming a conservative 20% reduction in milk yield (approximately 3–4 L/day), a standard deviation of 2.5–3.0 L, a two-sided significance level (α = 0.05), and statistical power of 80%, a minimum of 10–12 animals was required for repeated-measures analysis. Inclusion of 12 lame cows ensured sufficient power to detect stage-related differences. The repeated-measures design enhanced statistical efficiency by reducing within-animal variability.

Lameness assessment

Lameness was assessed using the standardized 1–5 locomotion scoring system described by Sprecher et al. [22]. In this scoring system, a score of 1 indicates normal locomotion, whereas scores 2–5 represent progressively increasing degrees of gait abnormality and lameness severity. Cows with a locomotion score ≥ 2 were classified as clinically lame for the purpose of this study. This cut-off was selected to capture early and clinically relevant locomotor abnormalities under field conditions, where early identification is important for minimizing welfare impairment and production losses.

Clinical lameness diagnosis was further confirmed by veterinary examination. During examination, cows were observed for abnormal gait, uneven weight-bearing, shortened stride, reluctance to move, arched back posture, and difficulty standing or walking. Veterinary assessment was also used to exclude cows with concurrent systemic, metabolic, infectious, or non-locomotor disorders that could independently affect milk yield or milk composition and thereby confound the study outcomes. Lame cows were identified at the time of clinical diagnosis and followed at predefined time points: 7 days before diagnosis, on the diagnostic day, and 7 days after diagnosis. However, individual body weight records, repeated locomotion scores at diagnosis and follow-up, scorer reliability data, specific hoof or limb lesion diagnoses, and detailed treatment records were not available in the farm dataset. Therefore, these variables could not be incorporated into the statistical models and are acknowledged as limitations of the study.

Milk yield recording and sampling procedure

Daily milk yield (L/cow/day) was obtained from official farm production records. Milk samples were collected aseptically during routine milking sessions. Samples were transported immediately to the Animal Welfare & Behavior Laboratory, Department of Medicine, Bangladesh Agricultural University, and analyzed within four hours of collection to minimize compositional alteration.

Milk composition and quality analysis

Milk quality parameters were determined using the Ekomilk Horizon Unlimited analyzer (Bultech, Bulgaria) according to manufacturer guidelines. Raw milk samples were gently homogenized prior to analysis to ensure uniform distribution of components. The analyzer simultaneously quantified milk yield, milk pH, electrical conductivity (mS/cm), somatic cell count (SCC; ×10³ cells/mL), milk fat (%), milk protein (%), lactose (%), solid-not-fat (SNF; %), and total solids (%). Calibration was routinely verified using standard reference solutions to ensure analytical accuracy and repeatability. All measurements were performed in duplicate, and the mean value was used for statistical analysis.

Electrical conductivity and SCC were considered primary indicators of mammary gland inflammation and udder health, as elevated values reflect increased epithelial permeability and leukocyte infiltration during inflammatory processes [10, 11].

Statistical analysis

Data were entered into Microsoft Excel and analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R software version 4.3.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Descriptive statistics were calculated and presented as mean +/- standard error (SE). Normality of residuals was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test and inspection of Q-Q plots. Where residual distributions were skewed, sensitivity analyses using transformed variables were considered; untransformed means are presented to retain biological interpretability.

To evaluate the longitudinal effect of lameness stage (pre-diagnostic, diagnostic, post-diagnostic) on milk yield and quality parameters, linear mixed-effects models were fitted using the lme4 and lmerTest packages in R. Lameness stage was included as a fixed effect, and cow identification number was included as a random intercept to account for repeated measurements within lame cows. Body weight was not included as a model covariate because individual body weight records were not available in the dataset. Model assumptions were verified through residual diagnostics. When significant main effects were detected, Tukey-adjusted pairwise comparisons were conducted using the emmeans package. Effect estimates with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported where appropriate.

Associations between health status (0 = healthy baseline; 1 = diagnostic-day lame) and milk traits were assessed using point-biserial correlations. 95% confidence intervals were calculated using Fisher’s z-transformation. Because the number of healthy control cows was small, logistic regression results were interpreted cautiously and were not emphasized as primary evidence. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and statistical significance was declared at p < 0.05.

Results

Effects of lameness stage on milk yield and quality traits

Linear mixed-effects modeling demonstrated a significant effect of lameness stage on milk yield (p < 0.001), SCC (p < 0.001), electrical conductivity (p < 0.001), milk pH (p < 0.001), milk fat (p < 0.001), lactose (p < 0.001), and total solids (p < 0.001). Milk protein and SNF were not significantly influenced by stage (p > 0.05). As shown in Table 1, the diagnostic stage was characterized by lower milk yield and higher inflammatory milk quality indicators than the pre-diagnostic and post-diagnostic stages.

Milk yield

Milk yield differed significantly across lameness stages (Table 1). The lowest yield was recorded on the diagnostic day, and yield partially recovered by 7 days after diagnosis but remained below the healthy baseline mean.

Somatic cell count (SCC)

SCC increased more than two-fold during clinical lameness (812.8 ± 45.6 × 10³ cells/mL) compared with the pre-diagnosis stage (335.4 ± 22.1 × 10³ cells/mL; p < 0.001). Post-diagnosis SCC decreased to 468.1 ± 31.4 × 10³ cells/mL but remained significantly higher than both pre-diagnosis and healthy cows (342.6 ± 18.3 × 10³ cells/mL; p < 0.01), suggesting persistent inflammatory activity beyond clinical locomotor improvement.

Electrical conductivity and pH

Electrical conductivity differed significantly across stages, with the highest value on the diagnostic day (Table 1). Conductivity declined after diagnosis but remained numerically above the pre-diagnostic and healthy baseline values. Milk pH, milk fat, lactose, and total solids also showed positive correlations with diagnostic-day lameness, whereas milk protein and SNF were not significantly associated with lameness status.

Milk composition

Milk fat, lactose, and total solids differed significantly across lameness stages (Table 1), with the highest mean values observed on the diagnostic day. Milk protein and SNF did not differ significantly across stages.

Correlation between health status and milk traits

Point-biserial correlation analysis comparing diagnostic-day lame cows with healthy baseline cows (Table 2) demonstrated strong associations between lameness status and inflammatory milk quality indicators. Discussion

The present study provides field-level evidence that clinical lameness in Holstein–Friesian dairy cows under tropical Bangladeshi conditions is associated with a significant reduction in milk yield and concurrent deterioration of inflammatory milk quality indicators across the peri-diagnostic period. The 21% decline in daily milk yield observed on the diagnostic day is both biologically and commercially significant, consistent with previously reported reductions of 10–25% across diverse production systems [3–5]. Meta-analytic evidence confirms that lameness is one of the most consistent herd-level drivers of depressed milk production globally [1], and the present findings extend this evidence to the under-represented context of tropical South Asian field conditions.

Under semi-intensive tropical conditions in Bangladesh, production losses associated with lameness may be amplified by environmental and management stressors. Heat stress, hard concrete flooring, hygiene challenges, and variation in feed quality can each independently affect udder health, SCC, and milk yield, and may also intensify the consequences of lameness [13, 14]. Local epidemiological studies from Bangladeshi dairy-producing regions have reported lameness prevalence of 15%-25%, particularly in tie-stall systems with wet or uneven flooring and limited hoof-care infrastructure [20, 21]. Comparable findings from Malaysia, Thailand, India, and Pakistan indicate that hot-humid climates, housing surface characteristics, parity, and hoof lesions can exacerbate lameness-associated reductions in milk yield in tropical Asia [15–18].

In the present study, the reduction in milk yield may be explained by pain-induced behavioral and physiological mechanisms. Lame cows commonly show reduced feed intake, may consume lower-quality feed because of impaired mobility or altered access, spend more time standing or lying in suboptimal conditions, and have altered rumination patterns [13, 23]. These changes can reduce metabolizable energy available for lactation. At the physiological level, lameness may activate stress pathways and inflammatory responses, redirecting metabolic resources toward immune defense and tissue repair rather than milk synthesis [3, 7, 9]. This metabolic reallocation may explain the observed decline in productivity during the diagnostic stage.

In the present study, SCC and electrical conductivity peaked on the diagnostic day, confirming that systemic inflammatory responses triggered by lameness extend to the mammary compartment. The mean diagnostic-day SCC of 812.8 × 10³ cells/mL substantially exceeded the IDF raw milk acceptability threshold of 400,000 cells/mL [12], indicating not only udder health impairment but also a direct commercial quality risk for farm profitability. Elevated SCC reflects leukocyte infiltration into mammary tissue, and may be further compounded by increased teat-end contamination risk under the hygienic conditions of field housing [10, 11]. Increased electrical conductivity is consistent with disruption of mammary epithelial tight junction integrity and increased permeability to sodium and chloride ions [10, 11]. Importantly, post-diagnostic SCC (468.1 × 10³ cells/mL) remained significantly above pre-diagnostic levels at 7 days, suggesting that inflammatory resolution in the mammary gland lags behind apparent locomotor recovery—a pattern consistent with reported persistence of subclinical mastitis indicators following transient stress events [5]. This reinforces the practical need for continued milk quality monitoring beyond the point of clinical locomotor improvement.

The transient increase in milk fat during lameness in the present study likely reflects a concentration effect secondary to reduced milk volume rather than enhanced lipogenesis. In addition, a negative energy balance associated with reduced intake may stimulate the mobilization of adipose reserves, thereby influencing milk fat concentration [6, 9]. The observed lactose increase may reflect altered osmotic regulation related to epithelial permeability, given that lactose contributes to milk osmolarity and secretion volume [11]. In contrast, milk protein and SNF showed weaker associations with lameness status, suggesting that cellular and ionic milk quality indicators were more responsive to short-term lameness-associated stress than structural milk solids.

Correlation analyses supported these interpretations. Strong positive associations between diagnostic-day lameness and SCC and conductivity, combined with an inverse association between lameness and milk yield, suggest that cows with poorer locomotor health also had poorer inflammatory milk quality profiles. These associations do not establish causality, but they are consistent with a systemic inflammatory framework of lameness rather than a purely mechanical disorder.

The economic implications are directly relevant for commercial dairy farm management in Bangladesh. Lameness is globally recognized as one of the most costly dairy health disorders, owing to its combined effects on milk loss, treatment costs, reproductive impairment, and premature culling [9]. The 4 L/cow/day yield reduction observed on the diagnostic day, if sustained across a typical 4–6-week lameness episode [4], corresponds to an estimated gross milk loss of 112–168 L per affected cow. At a conservative farmgate price of 60 BDT/L, this represents a direct revenue loss of approximately 6,720–10,080 BDT per affected cow per episode, before accounting for treatment costs, fertility penalties, or price deductions from SCC-related milk quality failures. In larger commercial herds where multiple cows may be affected simultaneously, the cumulative financial burden is substantial and provides a compelling economic rationale for investment in proactive lameness prevention, early-detection infrastructure, and welfare-based management protocols.

Collectively, these findings reinforce and extend the growing body of evidence from both tropical and temperate dairy systems demonstrating that lameness is a systemic, inflammatory disorder whose consequences reach far beyond locomotor impairment. From a practical management standpoint, integrating standardized locomotion scoring [22] with routine surveillance of SCC and electrical conductivity provides a low-cost, evidence-based framework for early identification of cows at risk of concurrent production decline and milk quality deterioration. In the Bangladeshi dairy context, where herd sizes are increasing, market quality standards are tightening, and profit margins remain narrow, welfare-oriented monitoring protocols represent a priority intervention for sustaining productivity, farm profitability, and animal welfare outcomes.

This study should be interpreted in light of several limitations. The sample size was small and the control group was unbalanced relative to the lame group, reflecting the field-based design and naturally occurring clinical cases. Healthy cows were sampled once as baseline references rather than repeatedly across equivalent time points. Individual body weight, farm-level temperature and humidity, detailed concentrate composition, lameness lesion diagnosis, treatment records, lameness-score reliability, and post-treatment locomotion scores were not available in the attached dataset; therefore, these factors could not be incorporated into the mixed-effects models. Future studies should include larger balanced cohorts, repeated sampling of healthy controls, detailed lesion and treatment records, and environmental monitoring to clarify the causal pathways linking lameness, inflammation, milk yield, and milk quality.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that clinical lameness significantly compromises both milk yield and milk quality in dairy cows under tropical Bangladeshi field conditions. The 21% decline in milk yield on the diagnostic day, combined with a more than two-fold increase in SCC exceeding the IDF commercial acceptability threshold of 400,000 cells/mL, and concurrent elevations in electrical conductivity and milk pH, confirm that lameness triggers a systemic inflammatory response with direct consequences for mammary gland function and farm revenue. These findings highlight the dual nature of lameness as both an animal welfare and an economic challenge, and underscore the particular vulnerability of intensifying South Asian dairy systems where infrastructure for early detection and intervention remains limited. The integration of routine locomotion scoring with SCC and electrical conductivity monitoring represents a practical, evidence-based, cost-effective strategy for early intervention. Future large-scale, longitudinal studies incorporating detailed lesion classification, treatment records, environmental measurements, and balanced study designs are needed to elucidate causal pathways and to develop regionally tailored lameness management guidelines for Bangladesh and comparable tropical dairy systems.
