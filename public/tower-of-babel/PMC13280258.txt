Optimization of Thulium Fiber Laser Lithotripsy Efficacy Based on Multidimensional Parameter Combination Strategies
Authors: Zhilong Li, Xiaoyu Tang, Shaoxi Liu, Du Wang, Tongzu Liu, Sheng Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Take Home MessageThis study systematically investigated multiparameter combinations (peak power [Ppeak], average power [AP], and pulse energy [Ep]) in thulium fiber laser lithotripsy using a decoupling platform and BegoStone phantom models to evaluate fragmentation efficiency, dusting efficiency, thermal damage, and retropulsion. Higher Ppeak with lower AP/Ep enhanced fragmentation and dusting efficiency while minimizing thermal damage, whereas lower Ppeak with higher AP/Ep reduced retropulsion without compromising fragmentation and dusting efficiency. This multilevel optimization strategy establishes a foundation for personalized urinary stone treatment across anatomical locations.Keywords: Thulium fiber laser, Lithotripsy effect, Parameter optimization

What does this study add?

In this report, we investigated the effects of multiple sets of parameters (peak power [Ppeak], average power [AP], pulse energy [Ep]) and their multilevel combinations on the comprehensive lithotripsy effect. We found that higher Ppeak with lower AP and Ep can improve fragmentation and dusting efficiency while minimizing thermal damage. Conversely, lower Ppeak with higher AP and Ep can reduce stone retropulsion while maintaining fragmentation and dusting efficiency. This multilevel parameter optimization strategy provides a critical foundation for the personalized treatment of patients with urinary tract stones in different anatomical locations.

Clinical Relevance

This in vitro study systematically evaluates the effects of different thulium fiber laser parameter combinations on stone fragmentation, dusting efficiency, thermal effects, and stone retropulsion using a decoupled multi-parameter experimental platform. The authors analyzed the interaction between peak power, average power, and pulse energy across multiple settings and demonstrated that higher peak power combined with lower average power and pulse energy improves fragmentation and dusting efficiency without significantly increasing thermal effects. Conversely, lower peak power at higher average power and energy settings reduced stone retropulsion while maintaining lithotripsy efficacy. The study aims to provide experimental evidence to support future optimization and personalization of TFL lithotripsy settings in different clinical scenarios. Associate Editor: Silvia Proietti.

Patient Summary

This study represents the first comprehensive, systematic, and detailed evaluation of the integrated lithotripsy effects of thulium fiber laser (TFL) across multiple parameter levels and their combinations. Our study primarily focused on assisting clinical practitioners in selecting the most suitable parameter combinations for lithotripsy procedures in different anatomical regions of the human body to mitigate postprocedural complications. It also aimed to provide valuable guidance to manufacturers of TFL treatment devices, aiding in the establishment of essential parameter presets prior to product distribution.

Introduction

The recently introduced thulium fiber laser (TFL) has gained traction in endoscopic urological surgery as a safe and effective alternative to the “gold standard” holmium:yttrium-aluminum-garnet (Ho: YAG) laser [1], [2], [3]. Leveraging its unique wavelength characteristics [4], TFL demonstrates notable advantages, including higher fragmentation efficiency [5], broader operational parameter ranges [6], reduced stone retropulsion [4], [7], and improved visual clarity [8]. However, the development and clinical application of TFL for lithotripsy remain in their infancy. Systematic optimization of key performance parameters and standardized configurations requires further investigation.

In vitro studies have confirmed the effect of a single parameter of TFL on lithotripsy efficiency. However, the clinical efficacy of TFL lithotripsy is influenced by multiple factors, including the “Snowstorm effect” or impaired surgical visibility due to bleeding [9], [10], and stone retropulsion, which prolongs procedure time by necessitating repeated fiber repositioning. Furthermore, the assessment of comprehensive lithotripsy should extend beyond fragmentation efficiency alone. Critical factors such as thermal effects and tissue injury must be holistically evaluated. While increasing pulse energy (Ep) may enhance stone fragmentation rates, it also carries elevated stone retropulsion, and exacerbated tissue damage from unintended laser activation [11]. Similarly, higher average power (AP) improves fragmentation rates but intensifies thermal effects [12]. Elevated peak power (Ppeak) boosts fragmentation efficacy yet further contributes to stone retropulsion. These parameters interact synergistically in TFL lithotripsy [13], [14]. Thus, optimizing outcomes requires a holistic approach—adjusting individual parameters alone is insufficient; their interdependencies and trade-offs must be carefully balanced.

Previous research on TFL lithotripsy parameters has mostly used the whole-machine equipment provided by manufacturers [15], [16], [17]. The coupling relationship between different parameters of the TFL machine has been fixed, limiting the multilevel combination of parameter checks on the user interface. In addition, the main focus of the current study is to compare existing TFLs with traditional Ho: YAG [17], [18]. There is currently no research report comprehensively investigating the effect of combinations of different levels of Ppeak, AP, and Ep on the comprehensive effect of TFL lithotripsy. Against this backdrop, our study employs a domestic TFL prototype and a decoupled multiparameter experimental platform to systematically evaluate the effects of Ppeak, AP, and Ep across multilevel combinations on fragmentation efficiency, dusting efficiency, thermal effect, and stone retropulsion. The purpose is to provide evidence-based guidance for personalized TFL parameter optimization in treating patients with stones at different anatomical locations, while also informing manufacturers in establishing optimal preset parameters for commercial devices.

Materials and methods

Prototype TFL system and BegoStones

Since the Ppeak corresponding to specific AP and Ep in the TFL therapeutic device is preset during manufacturing and cannot be adjusted via the user interface in lithotripsy mode, a multilevel combination of different parameters was unfeasible. We compared the integrated TFL system with a prototype TFL of the same class (1940 nm wavelength, identical waveform). By modifying the control logic of the prototype TFL via software, parameters such as frequency, pulse width, and Ppeak were adjusted to construct a decoupled multiparameter experimental platform. This allowed matching the Ep and AP of the integrated TFL system while varying Ppeak. The actual output power of the prototype TFL was measured using a power meter, while pulse width and repetition rate were recorded via a digital oscilloscope and photodetector. Ppeak was derived from the oscilloscope-measured pulse width and Ep.

The production method of BegoStones used in this experiment was the same as in previous articles [19]. BegoStones were prepared with reference to previous literature, with a powder-to-water ratio of 1:1, and dried at 37 °C for 72 h. BegoStone with strictly screened mass (±0.05 g) and dimensions (diameter ±0.1 mm) were randomly allocated to each experimental group. Each parameter experiment was repeated three times. According to the experimental purpose, different specifications were selected: 6 mm spherical for thermal effect experiment, 10 mm spherical for stone fragmentation and dusting efficiency evaluation, and 7 × 7 × 7 mm cubic for stone retropulsion tests. All models were soaked in normal saline for ≥1 h before laser irradiation. After each test, the stone model was replaced, residual fragments were cleared, and a fresh fiber end was prepared using a high-precision fiber cutter. All experiments were conducted by a single operator under strictly controlled environmental conditions. The implementation of this standardized method aimed to minimize potential variations in individual experimental assessments caused by differences in operational techniques or environmental factors.

Experimental setup and testing procedures of fragmentation efficiency, dusting efficiency, and stone retropulsion

The in vitro setup and testing methods for evaluating fragmentation efficiency, dusting efficiency, and stone displacement under multilevel TFL parameter combinations align with those described in previous literature [19]. The experiment was conducted three times for each parameter group.

Experimental setup and testing procedures for thermal effect

The experiment is shown in Fig. 1. The in vitro testing system utilized a 50 ml centrifuge tube as the primary container. An electrosurgical probe sheath was inserted into the centrifuge tube to serve as a dual-purpose working channel to provide access for the laser fiber delivery system and continuous irrigation flow. The stone holder was fixed on the outer wall of the centrifuge tube for the placement of BegoStones. An opening was made on the side wall of the centrifuge tube, which facilitated replacement of the BegoStones after each set of parameters and the exchange of liquid in the centrifuge tube. The K-type thermocouple was placed in the centrifuge tube 2 mm from the end plane of the fiber and was connected to a data acquisition system (PICO TC-08) to monitor temperature changes. The K-type thermocouple and the laser fiber tip were both located below the notch on the wall of the centrifuge tube. The equipment was placed in the water tank, and the lower edge of the notched wall of the centrifuge tube was above the water surface in the water tank.

Thermal effect testing device during TFL lithotripsy.

During the thermal effect test of each set of parameters, the 20 s resting temperature was first observed. Subsequently, the intelligent peristaltic pump was started at a flow rate of 40 ml/min for 20 s, followed by laser activation for 60 s. After the laser stopped excitation, continuous flushing was performed for 20 s. The highest and lowest temperatures recorded during laser activation were collected for each experiment. The difference between the highest temperature and the lowest temperature was the thermal effect index. Every parameter group underwent three experimental repetitions.

Statistical methods

All experiments were performed with at least three independent replicates. SPSS 26.0 and GraphPad Prism 8.0 were used for statistical analysis and data visualization. Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. This study primarily evaluates four outcome measures: fragmentation efficiency, dusting efficiency, thermal effect, and stone retropulsion. It systematically analyzes the effects of different combinations of Ppeak, AP, and Ep, employing the following analytical strategy: (1) Between-group comparison of Ppeak with fixed Ep and AP parameters: Under the condition of fixed AP and Ep parameters, the independent-samples t test was used to compare the effects of different peak levels on the experimental outcomes. Welch’s correction was applied when the assumption of homogeneity of variances was violated. This design aims to avoid confounding interference from surgical field clarity caused by AP changes, as this factor is difficult to quantify and correct in variance models. Given that this study is a highly controlled in vitro experiment with good reproducibility, the t test is appropriate for this specific scenario. (2) Analysis of the main effect and interaction between Ep and AP under fixed Ppeak: Under fixed Ppeak conditions, two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to evaluate the main effect and interaction between Ep and AP (Ep × AP). Effect sizes were assessed as η2 values. Post hoc multiple comparisons were performed by stratifying into subgroups based on energy levels (0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.5, and 0.8 J). Within each energy subgroup, the effects of differences among the three AP levels (20, 30, and 40 W) on the experimental outcomes were compared. For each independent multiple comparison within each Ep subgroup, the Bonferroni method was used to set the corrected significance level set to α/k, where k is the total number of comparisons made within that particular group. All p values presented in the figures and text were corrected accordingly. A p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Differences in fragmentation efficiency across a multilevel combination of TFL parameters

To investigate the effects of multilevel parameter combinations on TFL fragmentation efficiency, tests were conducted under the following conditions: different Ppeak (133.3 W, 200 W, 266.7 W vs 500 W) combinations at identical Ep and AP; different AP (20 W, 30 W, 40 W) combinations at identical Ep and Ppeak levels. In vitro stone fragmentation efficiency differed significantly across various TFL parameter combinations of AP and Ppeak.

At AP values of 20 W and 30 W, the differences in fragmentation efficiency between high– and low–Ppeak groups were statistically significant across all energy levels (all p ≤ 0.0001). However, when AP reached a higher level of 40 W, the effect of Ppeak on fragmentation efficiency gradually decreased with the increase in Ep, and there was no statistical difference in fragmentation efficiency (2.90 ± 0.22 mm3/s vs 2.50 ± 0.13 mm3/s, 0.396 95% CI [−0.00764, 0.800], t = 2.72, p = 0.053) when the Ep exceeds 0.5 J (Fig. 2A).

When the Ppeak was fixed, two-way ANOVA results revealed that both Ep and AP significantly affected fragmentation efficiency (Ep: F(4,30) = 28.7, η2 = 0.793, p < 0.0001; AP: F(2,30) = 306.8, η2 = 0.953, p < 0.0001), while their interaction showed no statistical significance (F(8,30) = 1.15, η2 = 0.235, p = 0.4). Further post hoc pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni correction demonstrated that increasing AP significantly improved fragmentation efficiency at a fixed Ppeak. This enhancement was statistically significant across groups in the low-energy range (0.1–0.5 J, all p ≤ 0.04), whereas the between-group differences gradually diminished and plateaued in the high-energy range (≥0.8 J), with no significant difference at some energy points (ns). When the Ep exceeds 0.8 J, there was no significant difference in lithotripsy efficiency between 30 W and 40 W (2.64 ± 0.10 mm3/s vs 2.87 ± 0.19 mm3/s, −0.231 95% CI [−0.485, 0.022], t = 2.32, p = 0.08, Fig. 2B). During fragmentation testing with parameters set to 0.8 J Ep, 40 W AP, and 500 W Ppeak, significant dust accumulation was observed floating in the water during the procedure (Fig. 2C,D).

Differences in dusting efficiency across a multilevel combination of TFL parameters

Stone fragments greater than 0.5 mm were collected after each test for calculation of dusting efficiency. In vitro stone dusting efficiency differed significantly across various TFL parameter combinations of AP and Ppeak. The results showed that when the AP was fixed, dusting efficiency increased with Ep (0.1–0.8 J), and the improvement in the high–Ppeak group (500 W) was significantly greater than that in the low–Ppeak groups (133.3 W, 200 W, 266.7 W) (Fig. 3A). At AP values of 20 W and 30 W, differences in dusting efficiency between high– and low–Ppeak groups were statistically significant across all Ep levels (all p ≤ 0.001). However, when AP reached 40 W, the effect of Ppeak on dusting efficiency gradually decreased with the increase in Ep, and there was no statistical difference in Ppeak’s dusting efficiency between 500 W and 266.7 W (1.72 ± 0.23 mg/s vs 1.48 ± 0.06 mg/s, 0.243 95% CI [−0.137, 0.624], t = 1.78, p = 0.15) when the Ep exceed 0.5 J (Fig. 3A).

Differences in thermal effects across a multilevel combination of TFL parameters

Thermal effects in the in vitro model, indicated by temperature increase (ΔT) at 2 mm from the laser fiber tip, differed significantly across various TFL parameter combinations. In the experiments of thermal effects testing, we observed that when the AP was held constant, thermal effects showed an initial increase followed by a plateau with increasing Ep (0.1–0.8 J). The thermal effects in the high-Ppeak group (500 W) were slightly higher than those in the low-Ppeak groups at the same Ep, but the between-group differences were not statistically significant (all p ≥ 0.18) (Fig. 4A).

Differences in stone displacement across a multilevel combination of TFL parameters

Stone retropulsion in the in vitro model differed significantly across various TFL parameter combinations. When AP was fixed, stone displacement increased progressively with Ep from 0.1 to 0.8 J. When AP was set at 30 W or 40 W, retropulsion in the high–Ppeak group (500 W) was significantly greater than that in the low–Ppeak groups (133.3 W, 200 W, 266.7 W, all p ≤ 0.04). When the AP was at a lower level of 20 W and the Ep was ≤ 0.2 J, there was no statistical difference in stone displacement between 500 W and 133.3 W (1.76 ± 0.23 mm/s vs 1.31 ± 0.17 mm/s, 0.453 95% CI [−0.00213, 0.908], t = 2.76, p = 0.051) (Fig. 5A). When Ppeak was fixed, the two-way ANOVA results indicated that both Ep (F(4,30) = 356.6, η2 = 0.979, p < 0.0001) and AP (F(2,30) = 294, η2 = 0.951, p < 0.0001) significantly affected stone displacement, while their interaction showed no statistical significance (F(8,30) = 1.33, η2 = 0.262, p = 0.3). Post hoc comparisons further demonstrated that stone displacement increased with higher AP at the same Ep, and this trend was highly statistically significant across all Ep levels (0.1–0.8 J, all p ≤ 0.0003) (Fig. 5B). Discussion

In this study, the impact of various combinations on fragmentation efficiency, dusting efficiency, thermal effect, and stone retropulsion in the lithotripsy process was explored to evaluate the comprehensive lithotripsy effect by multilevel combinations of TFL Ppeak, AP, and Ep.

The results of this study showed that increasing Ppeak from 133.3 W to 500 W significantly improved fragmentation efficiency and dusting efficiency at lower levels of AP (20 W) and Ep (≤ 0.2 J), while the thermal effect and stone retropulsion did not significantly increase. This finding has significant clinical implications for patients with ureteral stones. Due to the unique anatomical structure of the ureter, urologists using semirigid ureteroscopic lithotripsy often employ lower AP to avoid excessive thermal effects that may lead to ureteral strictures [20], while simultaneously using lower Ep to prevent stone retropulsion into the renal pelvis. However, these conservative parameter settings of low AP and Ep inevitably reduce lithotripsy efficiency and significantly prolong surgical duration [21], [22]. Our research results showed that in this case, the Ppeak of the laser could be appropriately increased, reducing excessive thermal effects, while improving lithotripsy efficiency and optimizing surgical results. In the case of higher AP of 40 W and Ep of ≥0.5 J, reducing Ppeak has no significant impact on fragmentation efficiency, dusting efficiency, or thermal effects, but could significantly reduce stone retropulsion. This finding provides an important reference for patients with bladder or dilated renal pelvis stones, where urologists could optimize outcomes by combining higher power/energy (40 W, >0.5 J) with moderated Ppeak, preserving fragmentation efficiency and dusting efficiency while reducing stone retropulsion. This helps reduce the time to track stones during surgery, reduce surgical duration, and improve surgical efficiency.

Our study showed that increasing TFL Ppeak significantly increases fragmentation efficiency, which was consistent with the findings of Soto-Palou et al. [23]. However, Soto-Palou et al immobilize the stone model, which may not reflect the real situation in clinical practice. In our experimental process, the stones remained mobile, while the irrigation flow rate remained constant. This method takes into account the effect of stone retropulsion during the lithotripsy process and the floating of stone powder in water on the efficiency of lithotripsy, which is closer to clinical practice. Similarly, it explains why our experiments showed no statistically significant differences in either fragmentation or dusting efficiency between the two parameter combinations (Ppeak 500 W vs 266.7 W) when operating at higher power settings (AP 40 W, Ep > 0.5 J). Furthermore, we found that increasing Ppeak from 266.7 W to 500 W under constant power conditions (AP 40 W, Ep >0.5 J) induced significant stone retropulsion during lithotripsy, necessitating repeated laser fiber repositioning for larger fragments while generating excessive powder that both compromised the operative visual field and substantially reduced stone targeting accuracy. The results of this study are different from those of Ventimiglia et al, who believe that reducing Ppeak does not reduce lithotripsy efficiency [24]. This is because they conducted a comparative study of TFL and Ho: YAG lasers, which have essential differences.

Regarding thermal effect, similar to previous studies, our study also confirms the effect of AP on thermal effects [25], [26]. However, their main limitation was that the stone model was not included during the test and they interpreted that the stone did not absorb energy during the fragmentation process. The study conducted by Aldoukhi et al did not add the stone model and explained that the stone absorbs too little energy and could be ignored or eventually reradiated into the water in the form of heat [27]. However, they all overlooked the important fact that the presence of stones may change the direction of the flushing fluid flow, causing a change in the distribution of the end temperature of the fiber. To date, few studies have investigated the potential effects of Ppeak on thermal effects in TFL lithotripsy. Our experimental device incorporates a stone model, and the K-type thermocouple is precisely positioned 2 mm from the junction of the fiber and stone [15]. It was observed for the first time that the thermal effect in laser lithotripsy was not affected by the changes in Ppeak.

Regarding stone retropulsion, this study observed that increasing Ppeak or AP would increase it. Our results are consistent with those of Ventimiglia et al. [24]. Petzold et al evaluated the reverse thrust of the stone by measuring the force exerted by the fiber tip when laser excitation was used by a fixed piezoelectric sensor [28]. However, they ignored the potential effects of laser-induced cavitation bubble oscillation and shock wave-induced liquid ejection, which might have an impact on stone retropulsion [29], [30]. Our research method is more similar to that of Andreeva et al. [31], who observed the effect of single Ep on stone shift. Surgeons usually respond to stone movements in the range of 0.1 to 1 s in clinical practice [24], [32]. Therefore, the observed stone shift is the cumulative effect of stone retrograde after multiple laser pulse emissions. We evaluated stone retropulsion by observing laser excitation with a duration of 1 s, a research method that is closer to clinical practice.

However, our research also has some limitations. First, in evaluating stone retropulsion, there is a friction between the stone and the track. However, each experiment process was performed using stone models with almost the same quality to keep the friction force consistent. This does not affect the conclusion of the stone retropulsion trend, and the repeated experimental results also confirm this. Secondly, in the thermal effect testing device, the saline in the tank was not heated to 36.5 °C to mimic the core temperature of the human body. Although consistency in testing was ensured by monitoring baseline temperature, calculating temperature differences, and storing irrigation fluid under thermal insulation, thereby minimizing the impact of temperature on parameter comparison, this setup still differs from the in vivo thermal environment encountered clinically. Third, a flush rate of 40 ml/min was used in this experiment. Although all experiments maintained the same flow rate and did not affect the study conclusions [33], in clinical surgery, irrigation flow rate and pressure vary dynamically depending on the surgical scenario and individual patient differences, which prevents the in vitro results from fully replicating real clinical conditions. Finally, the in vitro model lacks simulation of human urinary system anatomy, uses standardized stone samples that do not account for clinical heterogeneity, and does not incorporate surrounding normal tissues or consider laser-tissue interactions. These factors collectively contribute to discrepancies between the in vitro results and actual clinical scenarios. Given that this study involved multiple comparisons between groups, the cumulative risk of potential type 1 errors should be noted when interpreting p values. The analysis of this study focuses on observing effect size and change trends, rather than relying solely on statistical significance. Caution is recommended regarding relevant conclusions, and excessive statistical inferences should be avoided. This study was strictly confined to in vitro experimental conditions. The observed parameter effects do not constitute clinical practice outcomes. Future research should incorporate animal models and progressively integrate anatomical simulations, variable irrigation conditions, and heterogeneous stone samples for validation. Ultimately, prospective clinical trials are required to demonstrate that its safety and efficacy are superior to those of standard protocols.

Conclusion

This study indicated that appropriately matching lower Ep and AP with higher Ppeak can significantly enhance both stone fragmentation and dusting efficiency without increasing laser-induced thermal effects. Conversely, reducing Ppeak while maintaining higher Ep and AP can decrease stone displacement while preserving stone fragmentation and dusting efficiencies. These findings provide experimental evidence for TFL parameter selection in different anatomical regions. In addition, these results are intended solely to guide the design of subsequent clinical research and the direction of parameter optimization, offering insights for the refinement of clinical lithotripsy protocols and the development of preset device parameters.
