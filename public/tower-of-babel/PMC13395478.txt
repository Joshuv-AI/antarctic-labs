Misperception of Extreme Weather Event Mortality Risk in the United States
Authors: Mitchell Manware, Lingzhi Chu, Ying Hu, Jennifer Marlon, Anthony Leiserowitz, Kai Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCommunicating the risks posed by extreme weather events remains a challenge for researchers and policy makers. This study evaluates the gap between public perceptions on climate risk and scientific estimates of mortality burden from climate‐related extreme weather events. Results show each mortality risk perception gap follows a similar spatial pattern to the observed attributable mortality rate. The perception that “global warming is happening” was significantly associated with smaller risk perception gaps for all extreme weather events. Improving population‐level understanding of climate change is critical to reducing health risks associated with extreme weather events and to motivate climate‐oriented actions.

Introduction

Recent epidemiological studies have quantified mortality rates associated with various extreme weather events in the US. Specifically, county‐level excess mortality rates have been estimated for wildfire smoke (Ma et al., 2024), temperature (heat and cold) (Chu, Dubrow et al., 2025), floods (Chu, Warren et al., 2025), and droughts (Hu et al., 2025) in the contiguous US, improving the evidence available for public health and policy decisions. Despite these scientific advances, effectively conveying the health risks posed by extreme weather remains a challenge for researchers and policy makers (Howe et al., 2015). Insufficient or ineffective communication on the risk of morbidity or mortality arising from climate‐related hazards can lead to low risk perceptions among the public, driving inappropriate adaptation measures and potentially increasing vulnerability (Howe et al., 2015). According to the Yale Program on Climate Change Communication's (YPCCC) 2024 Climate Opinion Maps, only 47% of American adults believe that “global warming will harm them personally,” which varies across counties (Howe et al., 2015). This study aims to evaluate the gap between public perceptions on climate risk and scientific estimates of mortality burden from climate‐related extreme weather events.

Methods

We obtained risk perception data from the YPCCC 2024 Climate Opinion Maps (Howe et al., 2015; Marlon et al., 2025). YPCCC methodology uses multilevel regression and poststratification to estimate the percentage of adults who hold a certain climate‐related opinion in each US county (Howe et al., 2015; Marlon et al., 2025). The models are based on 31 nationally representative surveys, collectively including 35,000 respondents, and demographic and political covariates, and thus are not direct observations (Howe et al., 2015; Marlon et al., 2025). The county‐specifc percentage of adults who think that “global warming will harm them personally a moderate amount or a great deal” variable was used to quantify climate risk perception (hereafter referred “climate risk perception”) (Howe et al., 2015).

Attributable mortality rates from wildfire smoke (Ma et al., 2024), heat (Chu, Dubrow et al., 2025), flood (Chu, Warren et al., 2025), and drought (Hu et al., 2025) were obtained from previous studies. The total extreme weather event‐attributable mortality rate was calculated by summing these individual rate estimates.

To compare climate risk perception, a percentage, to actual climate‐related burden, a rate, the county‐level data were rank transformed. For risk perception, 1 represented the highest percentage and 3,094 represented the lowest. Similarly for extreme weather event attributable mortality rates, 1 represented the highest rate per 100,000 people and 3,094 represented the lowest. For heat‐related burden rank, counties with missing data (n = 2,035) were not counted in the ranking (Table 1). We quantified county‐specific gap between climate risk perception and actual climate‐related burden (hereafter “mortality risk perception gap”) by subtracting the attributable mortality rank from the risk perception rank for each extreme weather event.

To assess the relationship between the mortality risk perception gap and public understanding that climate change is happening, we then fitted generalized linear regression of the mortality risk perception gap on county‐level percentages of understanding that global warming is happening (“happening”) (Howe et al., 2015). Models adjusted for median age (“age”) (U.S. Census Bureau, 2022c), median household income in the past 12 months (“income”) (U.S. Census Bureau, 2022d), and the percentage of non‐non‐Hispanic White residents within the county (“demographic”) (U.S. Census Bureau, 2022b), with a 12‐year average calculated for 2009 to 2020 using annual estimates, and location using a thin plate spline for population‐weighted county centroid. The percentage of population with less than a completed bachelor's degree (“education”) (U.S. Census Bureau, 2022a) was also included as a covariate, but a 9‐year average for 2012 to 2020 was used due to unavailable data prior to 2012. Covariate data were accessed using the tidycensus package for R (Walker & Herman, 2025). Some demographic covariates included in our regression models overlap with the YPCCC predictors used to generate the opinion estimates, introducing potential non‐independence.

Results

The county‐level percentages of adults who think that global warming will harm them personally in 2024 ranged from 28.5% to 60.5% with a mean of 37.7% (standard deviation = 5.1%). Mortality risk perception gaps for each of the extreme weather events vary, with each spanning positive and negative values and small and large magnitudes (Table 1). Positive values indicated an under‐awareness of health burden related to each extreme weather event. Notably, each risk perception gap follows a similar spatial pattern to the observed attributable mortality rate (Figure 1). We observed larger under‐awareness of climate risk relative to health burden from wildfire smoke and flood in the midwestern and northern US, and for drought in the southern and western regions.

Diagnostic tests for models with all covariates showed multicollinearity. Individual terms were dropped until model performance was maximized without multicollinearity between covariate terms, with final models retaining terms for spatial smooth, “happening”, and “income.” The all extreme weather events model explained 66.6% of total deviance (R 2 = 0.64), indicating covariate terms are strong fit to the mortality risk perception gap. Event‐specific models also reported similar deviance explained and R2. The percentage of adults who think that global warming is happening was significantly associated with mortality risk perception gaps (p < 0.001) and appeared nonlinear in all models (Figure 2; Figure 3).

Discussion

We found that mortality risks from extreme weather events, in total and in isolation, are not well understood in most counties in the contiguous US. The percentage of a county's population who think “global warming is happening” was significantly associated with smaller risk perception gaps, indicating a better understanding of personal mortality risk for all climate‐related extreme weather events. However, the risk perception data used in this analysis are modeled. population‐level, and describe general climate opinions, which contain unpropagated uncertainty and potential non‐independence. We also note that ranking both opinion estimates and mortality burdens may magnify small differences and increase sensitivity to model uncertainty. Because we did not propagate the full posterior uncertainty of the YCOM estimates, effect sizes and significance values should be interpreted with appropriate caution. Our findings should thus be viewed as describing broad spatial and conceptual patterns rather than providing precise county‐level estimates. Finally, risk perception gaps were calculated using excess mortality rates, which do not consider morbidity, economic, or social losses associated with extreme weather events. With these considerations in mind, the results indicate that improving population‐level understanding of climate change is critical to reducing health risks associated with extreme weather events and to motivate climate‐oriented actions.
