Edaravone-loaded ROS-responsive nanosystem for targeted ischemic stroke therapy by crossing the gut-brain barrier
Authors: Wujing Cao, Xinxin Liu, Wensheng Wang, Jianbo Li, Jianjun Chen, Lei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Oral administration is highly desirable for sustained ischemic stroke management. However, clinical efficacy is often limited by poor intestinal absorption, restricted blood-brain barrier (BBB) penetration, and insufficient brain retention. To overcome these challenges, we engineered an equilibrative nucleoside transporter 2 (ENT2)-mediated, reactive oxygen species (ROS)-responsive, and size-transformable nanoplatform (LAG-PA/PMP NPs). The system is self-assembled from an amphiphilic lipoic acid-guanine (LAG) conjugate and phenylboronic acid (PA), encapsulating the neuroprotectant edaravone (3-methyl-1-phenyl-2-pyrazolin-5-one, PMP). Guanine serves as a targeting ligand for ENT2, which is highly expressed in intestinal epithelial cells and brain microvascular endothelial cells, thereby facilitating dual crossing of the intestinal epithelial barrier and BBB. Cross-linked disulfide bonds in alpha-lipoic acid enhance the stability of the system, while allowing cleavage under ROS-rich microenvironments to trigger controlled drug release. ROS cleaves the B–O bonds in phenylboronic acid, transforming LAG-PA/PMP NPs into larger particle structures, enhancing brain retention, and further accelerating disulfide bond cleavage to trigger PMP release. The oral bioavailability of this nanosystem is high (86.1%) with no significant toxicity. In both short- and long-term treatment models of MCAO rats, LAG-PA/PMP NPs reduced cerebral infarction volume, lowered inflammation and oxidative stress levels, and exhibited superior neuroprotective effects compared with oral free PMP. Altogether, this dual-targeting, morphology-adaptable nanoplatform provides a promising strategy for highly efficient oral therapy of ischemic stroke.

Introduction

Ischemic stroke (IS) represents a major global public health burden owing to its high mortality and disability rates [[1], [2], [3]]. Current clinical therapy mainly relies on intravenous thrombolysis and neuroprotective agents [4,5]. However, thrombolysis is restricted by a narrow therapeutic window (within 4.5 h of onset) and strict eligibility criteria [[6], [7], [8]]. Additionally, thrombolytic agents exhibit uneven release and distribution during treatment [9,10]. Small-molecule neuroprotective agents, such as edaravone (3-methyl-1-phenyl-2-pyrazolin-5-one, PMP) and butylphthalide, are predominantly administered intravenously and exhibit limited ability to cross the blood–brain barrier (BBB), thereby restricting their therapeutic efficacy in brain diseases [11]. Moreover, intravenous administration depends heavily on medical resources and infusion conditions and requires prolonged treatment cycles, leading to poor patient compliance. Therefore, developing a safe and convenient delivery system capable of overcoming the BBB is of considerable importance for ischemic stroke therapy [[12], [13], [14]].

Oral administration offers advantages in safety, convenience, patient adherence, and long-term management, which is regarded as an important direction for sustained neuroprotection after ischemic stroke. Nevertheless, oral delivery of neuroprotective agents is constrained by physiological barrier defenses: drugs must first cross the intestinal epithelial barrier (IEB) to enter systemic circulation and subsequently penetrate the BBB to reach the brain [15,16]. This “serial dual-barrier” effect thus reduces effective brain exposure following oral administration, even for agents with established pharmacological activity.

Nanodrug delivery systems provide a potential strategy to regulate the sequential processes of absorption, systemic distribution, and brain entry [17,18]. Previous approaches have targeted BBB transport via transferrin or lactoferrin receptors; however, these strategies often suffer from insufficient or mismatched intestinal receptor expression and unstable barrier-crossing efficiency in oral applications [[19], [20], [21]]. In contrast, endogenous nutrient transport pathways offer high-capacity and sustained transport functions. Intestinal epithelial cells transport nucleobases into the bloodstream primarily via equilibrative nucleoside transporter 2 (ENT2) [[22], [23], [24]]. Similarly, ENT2 is widely expressed on brain microvascular endothelial cells. Importantly, our previous work indicates that ENTs in cerebral microvascular endothelial cells may be compensatorily upregulated under hypoxic ischemic conditions, with enhanced functional activity toward nucleosides and their analogues. Therefore, this positions guanine as a potential dual ligand for cascaded targeting across both IEB and BBB. This strategy enables “gut-to-brain” cascade targeting, extending from oral absorption to intracerebral delivery rather than optimizing a single barrier in isolation [25]. Leveraging the pathophysiological features of IS to overcome both the intestinal and blood–brain barriers is critical for the development of an advanced drug delivery system [26,27].

Lipoic acid (LA), with its disulfide bond–mediated crosslinking capability, is a promising material for enhancing drug stability. As a natural amphiphilic molecule, LA contains hydrophilic carboxyl groups that enable the coupling of targeted ligands. The disulfide bonds within LA play two key roles in the construction of the drug delivery system. On the one hand, they can form cross-linked self-assembled networks via thiol-disulfide exchange reactions to improve the structural stability of the nanoscale system within the gastrointestinal environment. On the other hand, these disulfide bonds possess reactive oxygen species (ROS) responsiveness, providing the structural foundation for controlled drug release at the infarct region, where high levels of ROS are generated [28,29].

Effective IS therapy requires sufficient local drug concentration and sustained exposure. Nanodelivery systems inherently show the nature of “penetration–retention paradox”: smaller particles can easily cross biological barriers but are rapidly cleared from brain tissue, whereas larger particles exhibit greater retention yet have limited BBB permeability. An ideal oral brain-targeting system must therefore not only penetrate but also remain at the lesion site and release the drug in a controlled manner. Phenylboronic acid (PA), conferring amphiphilicity and ROS responsiveness, offers an effective means for controlled drug release at sites of oxidative stress [30]. ROS-mediated cleavage of the B–O bond induces nanoparticle structural expansion. Concurrently, ROS exposure accelerates disulfide bond reactions, promoting responsive depolymerization and facilitating drug release. This environmentally induced structural reorganization enables a dynamic transition from the penetrative state to the retained state after BBB crossing, enhancing local retention and prolonging effective drug exposure [31].

Herein, we synthesized a novel biocompatible amphiphile, LAG, by conjugating LA and guanine via an amide bond. And then, LAG and PA were used to construct a multifunctional oral self-assembled nanosystem (LAG-PA/PMP NPs) for safe and effective ischemic stroke therapy, employing PMP as a model drug (Scheme 1) [32,33]. Firstly, the formulation crosses the IEB/BBB through the ENT transport receptor with the help of guanine. After reaching the brain, LA acts as a structural scaffold to enhance ROS-triggered drug release, while PA acts as a ROS reactive component to promote brain accumulation and further accelerate disulfide bond cleavage. The sustained and abundant release of PMP at the lesion site can exert a synergistic effect, effectively scavenging ROS, attenuating inflammation, and improving therapeutic outcomes. Colloidal stability was verified by DLS of LAG-PA/PMP NPs and LA-P/PA NPs suspended in PBS over 30 days, where the particle size revealed negligible changes. After storing LA-P/PA NPs and LAG-PA/PMP NPs at room temperature for 30 days, no significant changes in particle size were observed for either nanoparticle (Fig. 1F), and both exhibited favorable PDI dispersion coefficients (Fig. S4). Both nanoparticles demonstrated excellent stability in bodily fluids and the gastrointestinal environment when diluted with phosphate-buffered solutions (PBS) at pH 1.2, pH 6.8, and pH 7.4 (Fig. S4–5), and when incubated in artificial gastric and intestinal fluids (Fig. 1G). When LAG-PA/PMP NPs were placed in pH 6.0 PBS containing 10 mM H2O2 for 8 h, a rapid increase in particle size and PDI was observed (Fig. 1H).

In vitro release studies of LA-P/PA NPs and LAG-PA/PMP NPs were investigated using membrane dialysis (MWCO 3500 Da) under different pathological microenvironment conditions, including pH 7.4, pH 6.0, pH 6.5, pH 6.0 + 10 mM H2O2, and pH 6.5 + 10 mM H2O2. At pH 7.4, pH 6.0, and pH 6.5, the cumulative release rates of both LA-P/PA NPs and LAG-PA/PMP NPs after 24 h remained approximately 20%, indicating good structural stability under physiological and mildly acidic conditions. In contrast, under oxidative stress conditions containing 10 mM H2O2, the release rates increased markedly. At pH 6.0 + 10 mM H2O2, the cumulative release rates of LA-P/PA NPs and LAG-PA/PMP NPs reached approximately 80% after 24 h, while similar release trends were also observed under pH 6.5 + 10 mM H2O2 conditions (Fig. 1I). These results indicate that both nanosystems exhibit high stability in systemic circulation following oral administration, yet show rapid and responsive drug release behavior under ischemia-relevant oxidative stress conditions.

IEB-crossing efficiency and mechanism analyzed in Caco-2 cells

Competitive inhibition further supported ENT-mediated uptake: pre-treatment with guanine (an ENTs substrate) or dipyridamole (an ENT inhibitor) reduced the uptake of LAG-PA/PMP NPs to 53.39% and 48.90%, respectively, while the uptake of LA-P/PA NPs remained largely unchanged (Fig. 2C). Mechanistic studies with endocytosis inhibitors indicated that LAG-PA/PMP NPs primarily enter via clathrin- and caveolin-mediated pathways, whereas LA-P/PA NPs showed greater dependence on macropinocytosis (Fig. 2D). Exocytosis-pathway analysis showed that brefeldin A, monensin, and nocodazole significantly reduced LAG-PA/PMP NP efflux, suggesting a stronger involvement of the endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi apparatus, and microtubules; in contrast, LA-P/PA NP efflux was less affected (Fig. 2E). Bafilomycin A1 did not increase intracellular fluorescence for either formulation, indicating that lysosomal pathways contribute minimally to nanoparticle exocytosis under these conditions.

Transcellular transport was evaluated using a Transwell system. After 21 days of culture to establish fully differentiated Caco-2 monolayers, TEER was monitored before and after nanoparticle treatment. TEER values remained >500 Ω cm2 for both LA-P/PA and LAG-PA/PMP NPs, indicating that the nanoparticles did not compromise the integrity of the intestinal epithelial barrier during the permeability assay (Fig. S9A). The apparent permeability coefficient (Papp) of LAG-PA/PMP NPs was 1.75 times greater than that of LA-P/PA NPs (Fig. 2F). Three-dimensional CLSM imaging further showed stronger basolateral fluorescence for LAG-PA/PMP NPs (Fig. 2G), indicating more efficient barrier traversal, consistent with transporter-mediated transcytosis.

BBB-crossing efficiency and mechanism analyzed in bEnd.3 cell and antioxidant activity in PC12 cells

To evaluate the BBB transport efficiency, we established a bEnd.3 Transwell monolayer over 14 days to model the BBB, following the same protocol as for Caco-2 cells [35]. LA-P/PA NPs and LAG-PA/PMP NPs showed no significant cytotoxicity in bEnd.3 cells (Fig. S7). CLSM and flow cytometry revealed significantly stronger intracellular fluorescence for LAG-PA/PMP NPs versus LA-P/PA NPs (Fig. 3A and Fig. S8). To investigate the role of ENTs (highly expressed on bEnd.3 cells), uptake was measured after treatment with competitive inhibitors dipyridamole or NBMPR. Pre-treatment with guanine or dipyridamole reduced LAG-PA/PMP NP uptake to 63.54% and 56.72% (Fig. 3B), supporting ENT-mediated internalisation. LAG-PA/PMP NPs exhibited a higher Papp value compared with LA-P/PA NPs (Fig. 3C). LAG-PA/PMP NPs revealed stronger basolateral fluorescence than LA-P/PA NPs in bEnd.3 Transwell monolayers (Fig. 3D), indicating enhanced BBB traversal driven by ENT-mediated transport. PC12 cells were used as a neuronal cell model. LA-P/PA NPs and LAG-PA/PMP NPs showed no significant cytotoxicity in PC12 cells (Fig. S10). The antioxidant protective effect of LAG-PA/PMP NPs against OGD/R-induced PC12 cells damage was assessed [36]. An in vitro neuronal OGD/R model was established to simulate ischemic brain injury conditions. The antioxidant capacity of the nanoparticle formulation was evaluated by observing morphological changes in PC12 cells 12 h post-treatment. ROS levels under H2O2 stimulation were also quantified, showing that LAG-PA/PMP NPs reduced intracellular ROS by 22.24-fold compared with Control and by 6.39-fold compared with LA-P/PA NPs, as shown in Fig. 3E and F. After 12 h of treatment with LAG-PA/PMP NPs, PC12 cells exhibited the most complete morphological recovery (Fig. 3G). A significant majority of the cells had regained a polygonal shape with shortened processes, suggesting that LAG-PA/PMP NPs substantially mitigate oxidative stress-induced cellular damage.

Oral absorption mechanism and targeting ability analyzed using rats

Rats were orally administered with free DiR, DiR-labelled LA-P/PA NPs and DiR-labelled LAG-PA/PMP NPs. Gastrointestinal (GI) tracts were imaged by IVIS [37]. At 1 h post-administration, LAG-PA/PMP NPs were detected in the stomach, duodenum and jejunum. At 6 h, strong fluorescence persisted in the ileum and colon for LAG-PA/PMP NPs, whereas signal from LA-P/PA NPs declined markedly (Fig. 4A). Next, we assessed the absorption of nanoparticle formulations within intestinal villi through intestinal section analysis. As shown by 2D and 3D scanning results from CLSM (Fig. S11A–B), both LA-P/PA NPs and LAG-PA/PMP NPs were able to penetrate the mucus layer. Notably, the red fluorescence of LAG-PA/PMP NPs remained clearly visible even at greater mucus depths, indicating superior mucus penetration capability. We further examined the absorption of DiD-labelled LA-P/PA NPs and LAG-PA/PMP NPs in the jejunum (Fig. 4B). The fluorescence of LAG-PA/PMP NPs was uniformly distributed around the small intestinal villi, extending from the apical to the basal sides. Their absorption capacity in this intestinal segment was significantly superior to that of LA-P/PA NPs, indicating more efficient polar transmembrane transport mediated by ENTs in intestinal epithelial cells.

Brain targeting is key to the design of the oral nanodelivery system. Biodistribution analysis was conducted to evaluate the gut-to-brain delivery efficiency using ex vivo IVIS. Rats subjected to middle cerebral artery occlusion (MCAO) via thread occlusion were administered oral DiR or DiR-labelled nanoparticles after reperfusion. The brains were collected at predefined time points to assess biodistribution and quantification (Fig. 4C). LAG-PA/PMP NPs exhibited strong fluorescence from 1 to 12 h post-administration, achieved significant brain enrichment peaking at 6 h, suggesting superior brain-targeting capability and prolonged intracerebral retention. Confocal imaging of frozen brain sections further confirmed the retention and colocalization of LAG-PA/PMP NPs in the ischemic brain region, and revealed localized aggregation of nanoparticles under oxidative and mildly acidic conditions, consistent with their ROS/pH-responsive structural transformation (Fig. S12). HPLC quantification further confirmed these findings (Fig. S13), showing average brain entry rates of 0.14% for free PMP (p.o.), 3.12% for LA-P/PA NPs, and 5.81% for LAG-PA/PMP NPs. LAG-PA/PMP NPs achieved ∼41-fold higher brain delivery than free PMP and ∼1.9-fold higher than LA-P/PA NPs, demonstrating effective oral gut-to-brain transport.

Short-term therapeutic efficacy in vivo

First, the short-term therapeutic efficacy of LAG-PA/PMP NPs was evaluated 24 h post-administration through histological and functional analyses (Fig. 6A). Clinical treatment of ischemic stroke with neuroprotective agents typically requires a prolonged intervention period. Accordingly, a long-term treatment model was established to evaluate whether LAG PA/PMP NPs provide enhanced therapeutic efficacy. Rats were randomly assigned to the Sham, MCAO, PMP (p.o.), PMP (i.v.), LA-P/PA and LAG-PA/PMP groups, with treatment administered immediately following cerebral ischemia-reperfusion. A 24 h treatment model was first established to evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of LAG-PA/PMP. TTC staining showed the greatest reduction in infarct volume (9.74%) in the LAG-PA/PMP group (Fig. 6B and C). In the MCAO group, malondialdehyde (MDA) levels were elevated, while antioxidant enzymes such as superoxide dismutase (SOD), glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px) and catalase (CAT) were reduced, indicating severe oxidative stress. Among treatments, LAG-PA/PMP produced the largest increases in SOD, GSH-Px and CAT, consistent with the strongest antioxidant effect with oral dose (Fig. 6H). Inflammatory cytokines were also quantified using ELISA. The LAG-PA/PMP group significantly reduced IL-6, IL-1β and TNF-α levels while elevating SOD, GSH-Px and CAT levels expression relative to other treatments, approaching levels seen in the PMP (i.v.) group (Fig. 6G), To visualise short-term therapeutic effects, we performed H&E staining, Nissl staining, TUNEL staining and ROS fluorescence staining on brain sections collected 24 h after treatment. H&E staining (Fig. S14) showed preserved cellular morphology in the LAG-PA/PMP group, with plump, round nuclei. Nissl staining (Fig. 6D) indicated restoration of Nissl bodies, with an increased number and deeper staining intensity. TUNEL staining (Fig. 6E) showed minimal apoptotic signal in the LAG-PA/PMP group. Long-term therapeutic efficacy in vivo

Next, we evaluated the long-term therapeutic effects via daily administration for 7 consecutive days (Fig. 7A). The LAG-PA/PMP group exhibited markedly enhanced survival rates and gradual recovery of body weight (Fig. 7C and S15). TTC staining showed the greatest reduction in infarct volume (8.72%) in the LAG-PA/PMP group (Fig. 7B). Neurological scores and infarct volume were measured in both short-term and long-term (Fig. S16 and 17) assessments. The results demonstrate that oral administration of LAG-PA/PMP NPs significantly improved neurological outcomes compared with free PMP (oral and intravenous) and LA-P/PA NPs, indicating effective functional recovery, and levels of SOD, GSH-Px and CAT were further increased in the LAG-PA/PMP group (Fig. 7H). In vivo safety evaluation

After confirming the therapeutic efficacy of LAG-PA/PMP NPs, we assessed their biocompatibility. H&E staining revealed no evidence of inflammation or tissue injury in major organs from LAG-PA/PMP NPs-treated rats (Fig. 8A and B). Blood analysis of rat samples showed no significant differences in red blood cell (RBC), hemoglobin (HGB), or platelet (PLT) levels across groups (Fig. 8C). The MCAO group showed significantly higher levels of alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), creatinine (CREA), and urea (UREA) compared to the Sham group, suggesting that ischemic stroke may cause hepatic and renal dysfunction. In contrast, these indicators in the other treatment groups showed no significant differences (Fig. 8D), while behavioral assessments further demonstrated improved neurological function, including recovery of motor coordination, balance ability, and spontaneous locomotor activity, in the LAG-PA/PMP-treated group (Fig. S16B), indicating that LAG-PA/PMP NPs did not cause toxic damage to hepatic or renal functions. Conclusion

This study developed a novel oral self-assembling drug delivery system, LAG-PA/PMP NPs, for treating IS. The nanoparticles utilize guanine as a ligand to actively target ENTs overexpressed on intestinal epithelial cells and cerebrovascular endothelial cells, efficiently crossing the physiological “gut-brain” barrier. ENT2-mediated transport across the BBB may contribute to the efficient brain delivery of LAG-PA/PMP NPs, facilitating transcellular uptake into cerebrovascular endothelial cells. Additionally, under ischemic conditions, BBB integrity is often compromised, which can further promote nanoparticle extravasation into the brain parenchyma. These dual mechanisms—ENT2-mediated transcytosis and passive extravasation from a disrupted BBB—have been reported previously, supporting a more comprehensive understanding of nanoparticle accumulation in the ischemic brain [38,39]. Simultaneously, LAG-PA/PMP NPs enhanced stability in the gastrointestinal tract through disulfide cross-linking in LA. Under high ROS conditions at IS lesions, the system undergoes responsive cleavage, enabling controlled release of PMP. Furthermore, the ROS-responsive conformational change of PA induced the nanoparticles to adopt a “retention state” at the lesion site, accelerating disulfide bond depolymerization and promoting drug release for synergistic IS treatment. Subsequent experiments confirmed significantly enhanced uptake and transcellular transport of LAG-PA/PMP NPs in Caco-2 and bEnd.3 cells. These nanoparticles exhibited prolonged retention time in the intestine, enhanced mucosal penetration, and strong targeting toward brain tissue. They also demonstrated high oral bioavailability with no significant drug toxicity, and in MCAO rats, they exhibited potent antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and neuroprotective effects. In summary, this research offers a simplified and effective oral combination therapy strategy for acute cerebral infarction.
