Data mining revealed no other trinucleotide repeat expansion associated with the Huntington's disease biomarker (CAG)
Authors: Omar Alaqeeli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The CAG trinucleotide repeat expansion is believed to be the only expansion associated with Huntington's Disease and other phenotypically similar genetic diseases. Here, we mine a large set of data that contains samples from patients of Huntington's disease as well as control to find associated trinucleotide repeat expansions of CAG. The total number of pathological and control datasets is 83 and 74, respectively. The total number of DNA base pairs in these samples is 210 and 162 Gigabases (1 × 109 bases). Predicated on association rules, most of the trinucleotide repeats found in these datasets have no significant expansions, although some trinucleotides, such as AAA, GAA, and TGC, showed higher presence in pathological samples than controls, but not to the point of being viewed as significant. Evidently, the expansions of CAG trinucleotide are the only expansions that can act as a biomarker for Huntington's disease and other related genetic diseases.

Introduction

Trinucleotide (codon) repeat disorders [19], [24], [25] are a type of genetic disor- ders [14], [29], [31], [35] that are caused by abnormal trinucleotide repeat expan-sions [8], [10], [14], [15], [26], [41]. The expansions disrupt the synthesis of proteins [2], [4], [7], [33] normally produced by coding genes [3], [6], [20], [39], [51], leading to the production of chemically different components and therefore causing ab- normality of phenotype. Normally, coding genes contain a certain number of trinucleotide expansions. When these expansions reach a certain threshold (due to mutational change in amino acids glutamine structure), an individual will likely develop the related genetic diseases. These types of diseases mostly have symptoms of cognitive, movement, and psychiatric disorders [1], [22], [23], [38]. The most well-known cases of these genetic disorders are Huntington's disease [13], [16], [43], Fragile X syndrome [18], [44], Myotonic dystrophy [27], [42], [48], and Spinocerebellar ataxias (SCAs) [21], [32].

Huntington's disease (a Poly-glutamine (polyQ) disease [12], [28], [36], [37], [45]) is a neurodegenerative disease that is cause by the CAG expansions (Cytosine, Adenine, and Guanine) trinucleotide repeats in the HTT gene [11], [40] (the Huntington's gene) that is located in chromosome 4. The normal number of repeats in the CAG expansion is less than 27. If the repeats are between 27 and 39, the possibility of developing the disorder increases. When the num- ber of CAG repeats is 40 or more, an individual or their offspring will develop Huntington's disease. The disease causes movement impairment, cognitive, and psychiatric symptoms. The CAG expansion is also associated with the existence of some types of Spinocerebellar ataxias (SCAs), though patients have differ- ent symptoms, such as coordination, balance, and walking impairment. On the other hand, Parkinson's [5], [47] and Alzheimer's [30], [34], [46] diseases have sim- ilar phenotypical symptoms as Huntington's disease except they are not fully considered genetic disorders.

In this research, we mine a large number of datasets to find another alter- native genetic biomarker associated with the Huntington's disease CAG trinu- cleotide expansions, hence Spinocerebellar ataxias. We use a modified version of the Apriori algorithm (elaborately described in a separate paper) to find any expansion of the other possible 63, 43 −1, formations of trinucleotides associated with the existence of the CAG poly-glutamine expansions.

The Apriori algorithm [9], [17], [29], [49], [50] is a data mining algorithm predom- inantly used in business transactions to detect associations between purchased items, such that if item A is purchased, then there is a certain probability of item B also being purchased. In the modified version of the Apriori algorithm we use, the transaction repeat expansions represent itemsets and DNA sequences (Ex- ons, Exomes, Introns, or even complete genomes) represent transactions. Thus an existence of an expansion of a specific trinucleotide repeat is associated with the existence of another expansion of a different trinucleotide repeat. Both of those expansions can be biomarkers for the same genetic disease. The algorithm calculates support count (the number of appearance of the trinucleotide in the samples) and support percentages.

In this paper, we focus on finding possible trinucleotide expansions that could be associated with the CAG trinucleotide expansions and their related disorders (Huntington's disease and Spinocerebellar ataxias).

Methodology

Through the SRA Run Selector of each study, Fasta format files of each dataset are downloaded. Initially, and due to the nature of these studies, the DNAs in the datasets are in the form of fragments, so we have to reconstruct them before we run our code. We use the R programming language for algorithm implementation.

Based on different literature, the lowest number of consecutive repeats in a known poly-glutamine disease (that is, Spinocerebellar ataxia type 6 (SCA6)) is 21 where at this point the expansion become pathological [12]. Since the CAG trinucleotide is a poly-glutamine, then we will set our threshold to 21, thus at this number of repeats the expansion will be considered ‘significant’.

There are 63 trinucleotides, other than the CAG, that were potentially as- sociated with the existence of the CAG trinucleotide. For every dataset, the algorithm iterates over its samples of DNAs to calculate the support count and support percentages of ‘significant’ expansions of every individual trinucleotide. We then calculate the association of every trinucleotide with CAG in both pathological and control samples separately by calculating the confidence. LetT be a trinucleotide, the confidence is defined as:ConfidenceCAG→T=Support ofCAG&TSupport ofCAG

Let us assume that the support count of CAG in pathological samples is 80 and the support count of, say, TGC, is 35 in the same group of pathological samples, then the confidence that whenever CAG exists the TGC will also exist isConfidenceCAG→TGC=Support ofCAG&TGCSupport ofCAG=3580=43.8%

That is, if CAG exists, then there is a 43.8% chance that TGC will also exist.

Discussion

In both pathological and control samples, GAA, AGA, and AAG have shown nearly the same support percentages; between 64 and 61%. AAA in pathological samples is higher, 51%, while it is 46% in the control samples. This trend is the same for TGC and GCT, where in both samples the support percentages defer from 61 to 50% for the pathological samples. TCT and CTT have nearly the exact support percentages. The rest of the trinucleotides have no significance at all. The AAA, TGC, and GCT are the ones that could be potentially important since the different in pathological samples is higher than in control samples.

Thus, we will calculate their confidence with CAG existence in the pathological samples as follows:ConfidenceCAG→AAA=Support ofCAG&AAASupport ofCAG=4588=51.13%ConfidenceCAG→TGC=Support ofCAG&TGCSupport ofCAG=5388=60.22%ConfidenceCAG→GCT=Support ofCAG&GCTSupport ofCAG=5388=60.22%

While in the control samples, their confidence in the existence of CAG isConfidenceCAG→AAA=83177=46.89%ConfidenceCAG→TGC=93177=52.54%ConfidenceCAG→GCT=90177=50.85%

The differences in confidence in the pathological and control samples are no more than 9.37%.

Because the degree of significance of the coexistence between trinucleotides is crucial to our analysis, we apply the Chi-Square test (χ2) on both pathological and healthy counts. The degree of freedom here is 63 (CAG is excluded). The χ2 is 15.82, and the p-value is approximately 1.0. Since the p-value is high, there is no statistically significant difference between trinucleotide repeat counts in pathological and healthy groups. The repeats are nearly identical.

In addition to the global Chi-square test, which confirmed the insignificance, we also ran a trinucleotide-specific Chi-Square test to test the significance of each trinucleotide repeat within our samples. Conclusion

There are two trinucleotides that could potentially be associated with the ex- istence of CAG: AAA and TGC/GCT (an expansion with TGCTGC repeat will yield either TGC or GCT). The association rule showed that if CAG ex- ists, then there is a 51.13% chance that AAA will also exist. Similarly, if CAG exists, then there is a 60.22% chance that TGC/GCT will exist. The matter here is that these percentages are no more than 9.37%, at their best, when compared with the same percentages for the same trinucleotides in the control samples. Therefore, the association of AAA and TGC/GCT with CAG is in- significant, and consequently, CAG repeat expansions are the only biomarkers for Huntington's, Spinocerebellar Ataxias, Huntington's, and others, diseases.
