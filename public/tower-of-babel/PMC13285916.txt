Real-Time Stream Learning System for Monitoring Activities of Daily Living in Older Adults
Authors: Paula Sofía Muñoz Ordoñez, Ana Sofía Orozco Orozco, Ricardo Salazar-Cabrera, Diego M López
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeMonitoring activities of daily living (ADLs), such as walking, sitting, and stair climbing, is an important indicator of functional status, autonomy, and overall well-being in older adults. Traditional assessment approaches, such as questionnaires or offline machine learning models, struggle to adapt to dynamic environments and to the natural variability of human movement. This study aims to design, implement, and evaluate a real-time data collection and processing system that supports the training and assessment of Stream Learning (SL) models for ADL classification, and to compare their performance with conventional offline models. This study aims to design, implement, and evaluate a real-time data collection and processing system for ADL classification using Stream Learning (SL) models. Additionally, the study analyzes whether SL-based monitoring can provide more reliable and responsive metrics than traditional offline approaches under real-world conditions.Materials and MethodsThe study involved nine adult participants during offline and online experimental phases aimed at evaluating real-time ADL monitoring under controlled and uncontrolled conditions. A mobile application captured accelerometer and gyroscope signals from smartphones and transmitted the data streams to a cloud server for window segmentation, label alignment, storage, and incremental model updates. Both offline models and SL algorithms were evaluated using precision, adaptability, and prediction stability metrics. The data captured were obtained through experiments approved by an Ethical Committee, and those involved signed an informed consent.ResultsThe system successfully enabled continuous real-time data acquisition and incremental model training. Offline models achieved competitive accuracy but limited adaptability to variations in movement patterns or acquisition conditions. SL models demonstrated more robust and stable predictions, adapting better to the natural variability of daily activities in real-world scenarios.ConclusionThe proposed system demonstrated the feasibility of integrating mobile sensing and SL for continuous ADL monitoring under real-world conditions. Compared with traditional offline models, SL approaches achieved superior adaptability, stability, and responsiveness during continuous operation, with Hoeffding Tree showing the best overall balance between accuracy and latency. These findings support the potential of SL-based systems for scalable and low-cost functional monitoring applications. Further validation using larger and clinically characterized populations is still required.

Introduction

Activities of Daily Living (ADLs) refer to fundamental self-care and mobility activities routinely performed by individuals in everyday life, such as walking, sitting, climbing stairs, and maintaining postural transitions. Assessment of ADLs is essential for evaluating functional status and independence, as it enables objective identification of the activities an individual can perform autonomously.1 ADL assessment is widely used in the diagnosis of chronic diseases, the early detection of cognitive impairment,2 and risk prevention in older adults, generally defined as individuals aged 60 years and older according to the World Health Organization (WHO),3 contributing to improved quality of life and more effective clinical interventions.4

Traditionally, ADL assessment has relied on clinical instruments such as the Barthel5 or Katz6 Indices and self-reported questionnaires, such as the Activities of Daily Living Questionnaire (ADLQ),7 which depend on the professional’s interpretation and the information provided by the patient. Therefore, these instruments have limitations in terms of subjectivity,8 low sensitivity to gradual changes, and a lack of continuous data on real-world activity performance.9

Advances in wearable sensors and artificial intelligence have spurred the development of automatic ADL recognition systems that integrate inertial data from smartphones and wearables with machine learning algorithms.10,11 However, most current ADL recognition approaches rely on offline machine learning models trained using previously collected static datasets under controlled conditions where no new information is incorporated after deployment. These models remain fixed during deployment and therefore exhibit limited adaptability to new users, behavioral variability, or changes in acquisition conditions over time.12 Recent reviews on wearable-sensor-based HAR systems have also highlighted the challenges of maintaining robust performance under dynamic real-world conditions and the need for more adaptive and continuous learning approaches.13

Real-time processing of sensor data poses challenges related to the volume, speed, and variability of incoming information. In this study, real-time refers to the continuous acquisition, transmission, processing, and classification of sensor data while activities are being performed, enabling immediate model response and incremental adaptation during operation.14 Several conventional machine learning approaches, including Naive Bayes (NB), Support Vector Machines (SVM), and Hidden Markov Models (HMM), have demonstrated promising results in controlled ADL recognition tasks. However, these approaches are typically trained offline, often require extensive manual feature engineering, and lack mechanisms for continuous adaptation during deployment.15 On the other hand, deep learning models have achieved significant advances in locomotion and gesture recognition tasks, although they still present limitations in capturing the temporal and contextual complexity of ADLs under dynamic real-world conditions.16

Within this landscape, Stream Learning (SL) has emerged as a promising alternative. SL is a machine learning paradigm in which models continuously process and incrementally adapt to incoming data streams over time without requiring complete retraining from scratch.17 Recent studies on continual learning for sensor-based HAR have further highlighted the importance of adaptive mechanisms capable of handling behavioral variability and evolving acquisition conditions in real-world environments.18 This capability enables faster adaptation to user-specific patterns, behavioral variability, and changing acquisition conditions during deployment.

This study presents the design and implementation of a data-collection and processing system for training SL models for ADL classification. The experimental evaluation involved a total of nine adult participants distributed across the offline and online phases of the study, including controlled laboratory-like tests and uncontrolled real-world monitoring sessions. The proposal encompasses three components: (i) the design and selection of an inertial-sensor-based mobile data capture system optimized for low resource consumption and real-time use; (ii) the implementation of a streaming data collection and recording scheme that integrates sensor samples, user-provided labels, and model predictions; and (iii) the development and evaluation of machine learning models capable of classifying human activities and analyzing their adaptive behavior under real-world operating conditions. Together, these components aim to explore whether continuous SL-based monitoring can provide more precise and contextually relevant insights into functional performance than traditional offline approaches.

The experiments in the different phases of development were conducted within the framework of an internal project at the University of Cauca with ID 6524, titled “TeleCUS: Telehealth Services at the Alfonso López University Health Center”. The human subjects study (collection of datasets) was reviewed and approved by the Ethical Committee of the University of Cauca, Colombia (https://portalantiguo.unicauca.edu.co/versionP/documentos/acuerdos/acuerdo-no-063-de-2002). Project ID 6524 was approved by the Committee on August 25, 2025 (the document has no particular number), and the subjects provided informed consent.

All procedures involving human participants were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional research committee and with the Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments.

A methodology based on the CRISP-DM (Cross Industry Standard Process for Data Mining) guidelines,19 adapted to the SL paradigm, was adopted for this work. The project was organized into coherent phases covering the design, implementation, and evaluation of the system. Unlike traditional machine learning approaches that operate on static datasets, SL requires continuous processes of data capture, preprocessing, and model updating.

The following sections describe the methods and procedures used to build the system, present the experimental results for each phase, and finally discuss and conclude how effective SL models are compared to traditional ones for continuous ADL monitoring in contexts that could involve older adults or those with physical limitations.

Materials and Methods

This section describes the methods and procedures applied to develop the proposed data collection and classification system. The section addresses the processes of systematic literature review, understanding the problem, identifying the ADLs to be evaluated and their respective relevant variables, selecting the capture device, designing the architecture, and implementing and assessing offline and online learning models. Each methodological stage was designed to ensure the experimental validity of the system and its ability to adapt to continuous-data-flow environments.

State of the Art

This section reviews recent advances in the detection and classification of Activities of Daily Living (ADL) using machine learning approaches, with particular emphasis on continuous learning and Stream Learning (SL) paradigms. A structured literature review was conducted to identify current trends, limitations, and methodological gaps that informed the design decisions of the proposed system.

The review followed a systematic mapping methodology comprising five stages: scope definition, inclusion and exclusion criteria, database search, document classification, and study mapping. Searches were performed in Scopus, Web of Science, and an institutional database using the keywords “Stream Learning”, “Machine Learning”, “Activity Recognition”, and “ADL”, yielding 129 publications from 2017 onward. Studies unrelated to machine-learning–based activity recognition were excluded.

The selected works20–27 were analyzed according to their primary focus (ADL recognition or other domains), learning paradigm (offline, online, or hybrid), sensor usage, data management strategy, and computational requirements. The analysis revealed that most existing approaches rely on offline or hybrid learning schemes and predominantly use publicly available datasets. While these datasets facilitate benchmarking, they limit adaptability to real-time scenarios and do not address concept drift or continuous personalization.

Only a small subset of studies explored online or incremental learning approaches for ADL recognition, and even fewer incorporated custom data-collection strategies for continuous model updating. Moreover, many proposals rely on computationally expensive models or resource-intensive processing pipelines, which limit their deployment on mobile or low-power platforms.

The comparative analysis of the reviewed literature highlights a clear gap in systems that jointly address real-time data acquisition, adaptive learning, and efficient resource utilization. In contrast to existing work, the proposed approach integrates a client–server architecture with a dedicated mobile data collection system and stream learning models that adapt dynamically to new data in real-world conditions.

An additional complementary study on IoT-based in-home monitoring14 emphasized the relevance of combining adaptive learning mechanisms with user feedback and contextual information to improve ADL detection. These findings further support the need for flexible, user-aware systems that can operate continuously outside controlled laboratory environments.

Overall, the state-of-the-art analysis demonstrates that while significant progress has been made in ADL recognition, there remains a lack of solutions that effectively combine continuous data collection, real-time learning, and scalability for deployment in everyday healthcare and telemonitoring scenarios. The proposed system directly addresses these limitations by providing a technically efficient and adaptable framework for continuous ADL monitoring.

Identification of Key ADLs for Incremental Learning

Monitoring ADLs is an essential component in clinical and rehabilitation settings, as limitations in basic tasks such as personal hygiene, eating, or mobility (walking, sitting, or standing) reflect the patient’s level of autonomy and the evolution of their functional status.10 Continuous observation enables early detection of physical or cognitive deterioration, prevention of complications arising from immobility, and adjustment of treatment strategies for patients with chronic diseases or in recovery.20,28,29 Especially in older adults who live independently, monitoring ADLs has preventive value, eg, detecting falls is particularly critical, as they are one of the main causes of hospitalization and loss of autonomy in older people.30 Similarly, the frequency or difficulty of lying down or standing up may reflect fatigue, weakness, or mobility problems.

Traditionally, patients’ functional capacity is assessed using standardized clinical instruments such as the Katz and Barthel indices, which, although useful for measuring dependence, rely on self-report and do not provide a continuous or objective record of patient behavior. This lack of continuity and objectivity also carries over to ubiquitous sensor-based systems, where not all activities can be reliably monitored. For example, activities such as personal hygiene, continence, or eating are difficult to capture with continuous learning algorithms due to the lack of dedicated sensors, the ambiguity of the collected signals, and their low frequency of occurrence. These limitations limit the generation of homogeneous data and reduce models’ ability to generalize. Therefore, it is necessary to focus attention on those activities that, in addition to being clinically relevant, are frequent and clearly distinguishable.

According to the literature focused on the classification of ADLs with SL models, activities must meet conditions such as the following:31 Defined patterns, ie, they must have distinguishable kinematic characteristics that are relatively constant over time, which facilitates their identification by the model.Adequate frequency within the target population, so that there are enough examples to train and validate the system.Low ambiguity with other activities, avoiding confusion between similar movements or postures. For example, “standing” and “walking” generate very similar signals, while “going up” and “going downstairs” are usually grouped into a single category called “stairs”.

Identification of Key Aspects and Relevant Variables When Using SL Algorithms

In the context of applying SL algorithms for ADL detection, the key aspects of their use were initially identified, including model processing, storage, and updating. These aspects are essential for the correct application of an SL algorithm, as they determine real-time data management. Additionally, measurement variables were considered as features extracted from data processing, such as mean, variance, entropy, or spectral coefficients, which serve as input for the learning models.

These measurements allowed to identify relevant patterns and optimize activity detection within the SL framework, where algorithms must continuously adapt to new samples without needing to store large volumes of historical data.

Key Considerations for SL

The implementation of flow learning required several methodological decisions to ensure real-time operation, temporal stability, and efficient model adaptation. Instead of transmitting raw sensor samples, the system operated with statistical windows and fixed data segments that summarized a compact feature vector. This approach minimized processing load and provided the model with a structured, memory-based representation of recent activity.

Since real-world activity data is non-stationary, the system was designed to handle conceptual drift, allowing the model to adjust to user-specific movement patterns, changes in device orientation, and variability in environmental conditions. Model performance during the transmission phase followed a prequential evaluation scheme, in which each incoming window is first used for prediction and then incorporated into the incremental model update. This methodology facilitates continuous learning, early drift detection, and adaptive behavior without requiring batch retraining.

Sensor Variables and Feature Set

To capture the movements and postural transitions involved in ADL classification, the system relied on inertial variables such as linear acceleration (ax, ay, az), angular velocity (gx, gy, gz), and inclination angles derived from the sensor orientation. Temporal variables were used to segment the continuous signal into fixed windows, while frequency-related descriptors characterized the regularity and periodicity of each activity. In addition, a supervision variable, the user-provided label, was incorporated during the data collection phase to support the learning and evaluation processes required by Stream Learning models.

Identification of Sensors for Activity Detection

Inertial sensors were selected as the most suitable option for ADL detection due to their ability to capture body motion and postural transitions in real time. The accelerometer and gyroscope provide complementary information on linear acceleration and angular velocity, enabling reliable discrimination of activities such as walking, sitting, standing, stair climbing, and falls. Their combined use has been widely validated in the literature for activity recognition tasks and is supported by their low power consumption and widespread availability in portable devices. Other sensors, such as magnetometers and GPS, were excluded due to their susceptibility to environmental interference, limited contribution to indoor ADL differentiation, and higher energy consumption.

Device Selection

Smartphones were selected as the primary data collection devices because they offered the best balance among sensor accuracy, processing capability, energy efficiency, connectivity, and portability. Their integrated inertial sensors enable stable data acquisition at configurable sampling rates. At the same time, their computational resources and network interfaces support real-time transmission and interaction with the stream learning architecture, making them well-suited for continuous and adaptive ADL monitoring.

Understanding and Data Collection

The main objective of this phase was to ensure that the data obtained were representative, consistent, and suitable for feeding both the initial models (called offline) and the models implemented later (called online). To do this, an acquisition system comprising two complementary phases, each using a different architecture.

Offline Collection System Design

In the offline phase, the objective was to evaluate the optimal conditions for data capture, such as sampling frequency and device placement on the body, before implementing the final architecture. The data obtained would be used to train an offline baseline model, which would not be part of the streaming learning system but would serve as a comparative baseline and an initial predictor. The offline model would be used while the incremental models adapted to new data, reducing the impact of the SL “warm-up” stage. The “warm-up” is a characteristic initial period in which the model has not yet reviewed enough instances to generalize correctly,32 causing a temporary decrease in its accuracy. Participants and Ethical Considerations

It is important to clarify the population scope of the experimental evaluation conducted in this study. Although the proposed system is motivated by applications oriented toward older adults and individuals with functional limitations, the data collected in both the offline and online phases were obtained from adult participants aged between 20 and 60 years, without diagnosed mobility or neurological impairments affecting gait or balance.

The decision not to conduct experimental trials directly with older adults was intentional and based on safety and ethical considerations. Several of the evaluated activities, particularly those related to fall detection and dynamic movements, may be physically demanding or potentially risky for older individuals during an initial system validation stage. Therefore, experimentation was conducted with healthy adult participants of both sexes who could perform all required activities independently and without external assistance.

No clinical assessment, functional scoring, or stratification by health condition was performed, as the objective of the study was to validate the technical feasibility, stability, and robustness of the proposed data collection and streaming learning architecture rather than to assess clinical outcomes.

Offline Application

An important component of the architecture presented in Figure 1 is the offline application, referred to as the Flutter App in the diagram.

A custom mobile application was developed in Flutter to collect controlled inertial data during the offline phase.33,34 The app continuously captured tri-axial accelerometer and gyroscope signals at nominal frequencies of 20 Hz and 50 Hz, storing the recordings locally in CSV format. Participants were instructed to perform each activity while carrying the smartphone in one of two predefined positions, wrist or back pocket. The application’s interface enabled starting and ending recording sessions for each activity configuration pair, ensuring standardized acquisition across participants.

The structure of the generated files follows a uniform format that includes a timestamp, sensor type, values for the three axes (X, Y, Z), and the actual sampling frequency. However, the nominal frequency cannot be strictly enforced at the hardware level due to operating system restrictions.

Construction of the Dataset for the Offline Phase

The offline dataset was constructed from recordings in which each participant performed the four selected ADLs under the four acquisition configurations, combining sampling frequency (20 Hz, 50 Hz) and device location (wrist, pocket). This protocol produced 16 recordings per participant and 80 recordings in total, each stored as a CSV file containing raw accelerometer and gyroscope data with timestamps and real sampling frequency estimates.

Because each recording corresponded to a single continuous activity, activity labels were assigned at the session level, and all samples within a file inherited the same class label. The 80 files were then merged into a unified dataset organized by participant and configuration.

Each sample includes a timestamp, linear acceleration (ax, ay, az) and angular velocity (gx, gy, gz), the real sampling frequency, and metadata such as participant ID, device location, and nominal frequency. The dataset contains four activity classes: walking, sitting/standing, stairs, and falling, and it is publicly available on the github platform.35

Data Preparation

This section presents the analysis and preparation of the dataset obtained in the offline phase, aimed at ensuring its quality and suitability for training SL models. The distribution of samples by activity, participant, and sampling frequency was evaluated, the absence of null values was verified, and smoothing filters and outlier removal techniques were applied to improve signal stability. Subsequently, transformations including pitch and roll calculation and fixed-window segmentation with statistical feature extraction were performed to structure the data consistently with the continuous-flow requirements of the online classification phase.

The final dataset contained 79,574 samples across the four ADLs and five participants. Two participants exhibited unusually high deviations at 50 Hz.

Modeling

Offline Phase

The offline phase evaluated the influence of sampling frequency (20/50 Hz) and device location (wrist/pocket) on activity classification. Two baseline models Random Forest (RF) and Support Vector Machine (SVM) were trained using the feature windows generated during preprocessing. RF was configured with 100 trees and class-balanced weighting, while SVM used an RBF kernel with standardized features and hyperparameter tuning via GridSearchCV (C and γ). Models were trained with a 70/30% train–test split to obtain a stable reference prior to deploying the online system.

The RF configuration was selected because 100 trees provide a balance between predictive robustness and computational cost, reducing variance without excessively increasing model size or inference time. Class-balanced weighting was used to mitigate the effect of class imbalance during training. For SVM, the RBF kernel was selected because inertial-sensor features can present nonlinear decision boundaries between activities. Standardization was applied because SVM performance is sensitive to feature scale, and GridSearchCV was used to identify suitable C and γ values while maintaining a reproducible parameter-selection procedure.

To address the dataset’s moderate class imbalance, particularly affecting minority activities, two resampling strategies were evaluated: Random Oversampling and Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE).36 Both techniques were applied to each frequency location combination, and RF and SVM were retrained under identical settings to assess their effect on class-level discrimination.

Across all configurations, SVM with SMOTE consistently outperformed the remaining models, achieving the most stable results for minority classes. The wrist–50 Hz configuration provided the best overall balance between accuracy and computational efficiency, yielding 96% accuracy, macro F1 = 0.95, and a compact model size suitable for deployment. This model was therefore selected as the final offline classifier and serialized in.pkl format for initialization of the online learning phase.

Online Phase

During the online phase, a mobile application developed in Flutter was used as the real-time client of the system. The application continuously captured inertial signals from the accelerometer and gyroscope, segmented the data into fixed-length windows, and transmitted extracted features to the server through a secure WebSocket (WSS) communication channel.37 Communication between the processing server and the classification server was performed using HTTPS protocols to ensure secure data exchange across system components. Additionally, user-related information and authentication credentials were managed through encrypted storage and secure authentication procedures, including login and password management mechanisms. Incoming data structures were also validated before processing to reduce the risk of corrupted or malformed transmissions during continuous operation. In addition to data transmission, the application interacted with the user only when required, requesting activity labels in cases of low confidence or detected activity transitions. This design enabled continuous data flow and adaptive supervision while minimizing user intervention during monitoring. If the user does not provide the requested label assignment, the system continues operating normally using the current state of the classification model. In such cases, the corresponding data segment is excluded from the supervised incremental update process, preventing the incorporation of potentially uncertain labels into the Stream Learning model. This strategy was adopted to avoid propagating incorrect pseudo-labels that could negatively affect model adaptation during continuous learning. Consequently, the classifier maintains real-time inference capabilities, although prolonged absence of user feedback may reduce the system’s ability to adapt efficiently to new behavioral patterns or activity transitions over time.

Processing and Classification Servers (Online)

The online environment consisted of two cloud-based services: a processing server, responsible for receiving data windows and managing session-related operations, and a classification server, which executed prediction, label request decisions, and incremental model updates. Both services were implemented in Python and deployed on Render,38 with persistent storage in a PostgreSQL database.

When the model detected a consistent change in activity or when confidence was insufficient to validate the prediction, the smartphone issued a standard notification asking the participant to report the activity they had been performing during the previous moments. The participant selected the appropriate option, and this information was used to assign a label to the corresponding windows through temporal alignment and overlay.

This procedure ensured that labeling was based on meaningful events rather than fixed intervals, while providing a practical balance between user oversight and automated inference. Although the system did not include post-hoc manual review or inter-annotator comparison, the rule-based confirmation strategy allowed for the generation of temporally consistent labels that reflected both the user’s self-report and the dynamic behavior of the SL model.

When the policy engine requests a label, the system creates a pending interval in the database, representing the temporal range associated with uncertain or transitional predictions awaiting user confirmation. This interval temporarily stores the affected windows and the reason for the query until a valid response is received from the participant. Once the activity is confirmed, the aligner associates the confirmed label with the corresponding windows through temporal alignment and incorporates them into the incremental learning process. Testing

Once the optimal set of parameters was identified during the offline phase, the system was validated under real-world conditions through the online testing stage. This stage aimed to evaluate the SL model’s performance, adaptability, and robustness under natural variability in users, devices, and usage environments.

The online evaluation consisted of two stages: Initial online tests focused on verifying client-server communication and calibrating the hyperparameters of the online models.Final online tests aimed at measuring the real performance of the system in uncontrolled environments with diverse users.

Final online tests aimed at measuring the real performance of the system in uncontrolled environments with diverse users.

Final online tests aimed at measuring the real performance of the system in uncontrolled environments with diverse users.

During the initial online tests, four participants performed everyday activities in sessions lasting approximately 15 minutes. This stage allowed the verification of correct data transmission between the client and the processing server, as well as the adjustment of internal parameters of the HT and ARF models. Various combinations of hyperparameters, such as grace_period, delta, leaf_prediction, n_models, max_features, and lambda_value, were evaluated until those that yielded greater stability in prequential metrics were identified. The resulting configurations were adopted for the final tests.

The final online tests were conducted with eight participants, each completing a 20-minute session in real-world uncontrolled environments, including participant homes, indoor and outdoor university areas, and public outdoor spaces. Participants used their own smartphones with the application installed and configured at 50 Hz. This evaluation strategy was intentionally adopted to assess the adaptability and robustness of the Stream Learning models under realistic operating conditions beyond controlled laboratory settings.

During these sessions, participants freely performed the ADL previously monitored during the offline phase, without a predefined sequence. Each time the system detected a significant variation in accelerometer or gyroscope signals (using a 3.0 threshold), the application prompted the user to confirm or manually label the activity. This mechanism enabled continuous validation of predictions and real-time feedback to the Stream Learning model.

The dataset generated during the online phase was used to analyze the model’s accuracy, stability, and adaptability under uncontrolled conditions, verifying its suitability for real applications in continuous monitoring.

Online Phase

During the online testing phase, a total of 8601 data windows were generated, of which 6912 had user-confirmed labels and were used to evaluate the classification models. No predictions were recorded in 167 windows, all from the first session. This is explained by the warm-up period characteristic of flow learning models. During this period, which lasts approximately 100 windows, the classifiers accumulate examples before producing reliable outputs, a behavior inherent to this type of model.

The results in Table 6 indicate that both RF and SVM models achieved improved performance when balancing techniques were applied. For RF, Random Oversampling yielded higher performance than SMOTE. Higher sampling frequency (50 Hz) generally led to better metrics, particularly in SMOTE-based configurations. Among all evaluated models, SVM-SMOTE showed the most consistent performance, achieving a macro F1-score of up to 0.95 and an accuracy of 0.96, especially in the wrist–50 Hz configuration. Less represented classes, such as sitting, reached F1-scores of up to 0.99 without degrading the performance of other activities.

Reaction time (lag) was defined as the time difference between the moment a true activity transition occurred and the moment the model produced its first correct and stable prediction of the new class. In contrast, the offline SVM presented the highest lag values and the widest spread, including several extreme outliers, revealing difficulties in adapting to changes under real-world conditions. These results indicate that incremental learning models, particularly HT, are better suited for continuous activity recognition scenarios where rapid adaptation is required. Chattering

Chattering, or prediction-switch rate, was defined as the number of output class changes made by the model per minute. A prediction switch was counted whenever the output transitioned from one class to another. Figure 13 compares this metric across models. The offline SVM exhibited the highest switching rate, indicating unstable predictions and frequent oscillations between classes. HT substantially reduced the number of prediction switches while maintaining strong predictive performance. ARF achieved the lowest switching rate and the narrowest variability, demonstrating the most stable prediction behavior among the evaluated models. However, this increased stability was accompanied by lower classification performance, as previously observed in the accuracy and recall results. Overall, the results highlight a trade-off between stability and predictive effectiveness, with HT providing the best balance between both aspects. The offline SVM achieved low accuracy (~23%) and exhibited long reaction times (~11 s), making it unsuitable for real-time monitoring. These results highlight the trade-offs between adaptability, latency, and stability in continuous activity recognition systems.

Discussion

This section discusses the results obtained following the proposed methodological workflow. In the offline phase, a controlled dataset was collected, and a traditional SVM model was trained as a baseline. In the online phase, the client–server architecture was validated and evaluated under uncontrolled conditions using heterogeneous devices and participants. During online testing, 8601 windows were generated, of which 6912 were labeled by users and used for evaluation. An initial warm-up period (~100 windows) accounted for 167 windows without predictions, a behavior inherent to incremental learning models.

A clear contrast was observed between the offline and online approaches. The offline SVM exhibited poor generalization in real-world conditions, achieving ~23% accuracy and a macro-F1 of ~0.24, indicating strong domain mismatch. In contrast, Stream Learning models showed substantial adaptability. The HT model achieved the best overall performance, with ~82% accuracy, balanced precision and recall (~0.61), and stable behavior across sessions. ARF showed intermediate performance (~58% accuracy) but suffered from pronounced oscillations in response to noise and abrupt activity transitions.

Class imbalance strongly influenced performance: sitting represented 59% of windows, followed by walking (29.6%), stairs (7.8%), and falling (3.5%). While majority classes achieved higher scores, HT improved performance on minority activities, particularly for the safety-critical falling class (F1 ≈ 0.44), highlighting the importance of class-level evaluation beyond global accuracy.

Temporal and confusion-matrix analyses confirmed these trends. The SVM showed erratic behavior, while HT rapidly stabilized above 0.8 accuracy. Reaction-time analysis further differentiated the models: HT achieved the fastest response (median ~1.5 s), compared to ARF (~50–60 s) and SVM (>40 s). In terms of stability, ARF produced fewer prediction switches, but at the cost of accuracy and responsiveness, positioning HT as the most practical trade-off.

The slower response of the ARF model to abrupt activity transitions may be associated with the ensemble-based structure of the algorithm, where adaptation depends on the collective behavior of multiple incremental trees and drift-detection mechanisms. Although this architecture improves stability under noisy conditions, it may also introduce adaptation latency during rapid changes in activity patterns. Future improvements could explore lightweight ensemble configurations, optimized drift-detection strategies, adaptive windowing mechanisms, or dynamic tree management approaches to reduce response time while preserving robustness during continuous operation.

Session-level results revealed variability across users and devices, indicating strong adaptability but also motivating future personalization strategies. Importantly, the study was conducted on a limited, non-clinical adult cohort, which constrains generalizability. Movement patterns in older adults and vulnerable populations may differ significantly, particularly for events such as falls and gait transitions.

Overall, the results demonstrated that offline models were insufficient for real-world ADL recognition, while Stream Learning approaches, particularly HT, provided an effective balance of adaptability, accuracy, and low latency. From a healthcare perspective, such systems could support routine monitoring in primary care, rehabilitation, and nursing follow-up, acting as decision-support tools that complement, rather than replace, clinical judgment.

The obtained findings also demonstrate the practical feasibility of deploying adaptive ADL monitoring systems beyond controlled laboratory environments. Unlike traditional static approaches, the proposed Stream Learning framework was capable of maintaining stable performance under heterogeneous real-world conditions, including variations in users, devices, movement patterns, and environmental contexts. The combination of continuous data acquisition, event-driven supervision, incremental model updating, and low-latency inference enabled the system to adapt dynamically to changes occurring during everyday activities without requiring full model retraining.

These characteristics are particularly relevant for long-term healthcare monitoring applications, where patient behavior, mobility patterns, and sensor conditions may evolve over time. Furthermore, the lightweight nature of the employed models and the distributed client–server architecture support continuous operation on commonly available mobile devices, increasing the feasibility of scalable deployment in telemonitoring, rehabilitation, and assisted-living scenarios outside laboratory settings.

Limitations of the Study

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the results of this study. First, the online evaluation involved a limited cohort of healthy adult participants, which restricts the generalizability of the findings to older adults or populations with mobility impairments. In addition, the activity distribution was notably imbalanced, with sitting representing most of the collected windows, potentially influencing the performance of minority classes such as falls and stair-related activities. Another limitation was the restricted storage and computational capacity available in the cloud database infrastructure during online testing, which constrained the duration and scale of continuous experiments. Consequently, most sessions were relatively short, limiting the observation of long-term adaptation behavior in the Stream Learning models. Furthermore, although the proposed architecture demonstrated robust adaptability under heterogeneous real-world conditions, the system currently relies exclusively on smartphone-based inertial sensing, without incorporating contextual or multimodal physiological information that could improve activity discrimination in complex environments.

Future Directions and Recommendations

Future work should focus on evaluating the proposed system in larger and more diverse populations, particularly among older adults and individuals with mobility-related conditions. Long-term deployments are also necessary to further assess the robustness and stability of continuous adaptation under prolonged real-world operation. From a technical perspective, future implementations could explore personalization mechanisms and multimodal sensing approaches to improve contextual understanding and user-specific adaptation. Additionally, although the current implementation performs classification and incremental learning on a remote server, the low computational requirements of the employed Stream Learning algorithms suggest the feasibility of future fully local or edge-based deployments on mobile devices. Such an approach could reduce communication overhead and improve system autonomy in environments with limited connectivity.

Conclusion

This work validated the technical feasibility of a real-time ADL recognition system based on mobile inertial sensors and stream learning. The offline analysis highlighted the importance of device placement and sampling frequency, while the data preparation process ensured a consistent, reproducible pipeline across the offline and online phases.

The experimental results confirmed that stream learning approaches outperform traditional offline models in real-world conditions. Among the evaluated models, HT provided the best balance between adaptability, accuracy, and responsiveness, while ARF exhibited greater stability but slower reaction to activity changes. The offline SVM, although efficient in controlled settings, lacked the flexibility required for continuous learning scenarios.

The proposed client–server architecture proved effective for continuous data acquisition, incremental learning, and low-latency operation. By separating processing and classification tasks, the system optimized resource usage and maintained a stable real-time data flow.

Although the offline SVM model demonstrated limited adaptability under continuous real-world conditions, future implementations could explore hybrid or incremental SVM variants capable of online updating and adaptive retraining strategies. Such approaches may help preserve the strong discriminative performance of SVM models while improving their ability to respond to evolving activity patterns and concept drift in continuous monitoring scenarios.

Regarding ARF, future improvements could focus on reducing adaptation latency during abrupt activity transitions. Potential strategies include optimizing drift-detection mechanisms, reducing ensemble complexity, dynamically adjusting the number of trees, or incorporating adaptive windowing techniques to improve responsiveness while maintaining stability during continuous operation.

This study should be interpreted as a proof-of-concept and feasibility evaluation. Although the system demonstrated robust performance under real conditions, validation on larger and clinically characterized cohorts, particularly involving older adults and individuals with functional limitations, is required to assess clinical effectiveness, safety, and long-term reliability. Future work will focus on controlled clinical deployments and integration into routine healthcare workflows.
