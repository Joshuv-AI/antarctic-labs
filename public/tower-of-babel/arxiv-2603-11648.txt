Abstract
As an alternative to visibly pushdown automata, we introduce visibly recursive automata (VRAs), composed of a set of classical automata that can call each other. VRAs are a strict extension of so-called systems of procedural automata, a model proposed by Frohme and Steffen. We study the complexity of standard language-theoretic operations and classical decision problems for VRAs. Since the class of deterministic VRAs forms a strict subclass in terms of expressiveness, we propose a (weaker) notion that does not restrict expressive power and which we call codeterminism. Codeterminism comes with many desirable algorithmic properties that we demonstrate by using it, e.g., as a stepping stone towards implementing complementation of VRAs.

The submodules of an SPA are classical finite automata (FAs) that are not interconnected, and

In

Interestingly, SPAs are strictly less expressive than VPAs.

Visibly Recursive Automata

A

Each of these FAs is identified by a unique

A

2

Our concept of codeterminism is inspired by the concept of
codeterministic grammars
introduced in

7

Definition 5
(Codeterministic VRA)

A VRA
\mathcal{A}
is
codeterministic
if all automata linked to the same call/return symbols have pairwise disjoint languages:

\forall c\in\Sigma_{\mathit{call}},\forall r\in\Sigma_{\mathit{ret}},\forall J,J^{\prime}\in\Sigma_{\mathit{proc}}^{\langle c,r\rangle}:J\neq J^{\prime}\Rightarrow\widetilde{L}(\mathcal{A}^{J})\cap\widetilde{L}(\mathcal{A}^{J^{\prime}})=\varnothing.

The notion of complete VRA requires two conditions.
The first asks all the FAs of the VRA to be complete. The second is a universality condition on the recursive languages.
These conditions guarantee that there always exists a recursive run on any well-matched word, regardless of the starting configuration.

Definition 6
(Complete VRA)

A VRA
\mathcal{A}
is
complete
if

•

for all
q\in Q_{\mathcal{A}}
and
a\in\Sigma_{\mathit{int}}\cup\Sigma_{\mathit{proc}}
, there exists
(q,a,p)\in\delta_{\mathcal{A}}
;

•

for all
c\in\Sigma_{\mathit{call}}
and
r\in\Sigma_{\mathit{ret}}
:
\bigcup_{J\in\Sigma_{\mathit{proc}}^{\langle c,r\rangle}}\widetilde{L}(\mathcal{A}^{J})=\mathit{WM}(\widetilde{\Sigma})
.

The conditions on recursive languages for a VRA to be codeterministic and complete can be replaced by a condition at the level of regular languages:

We here study the complexity of the emptiness, universality, inclusion, and equivalence decision problems. They belong to the same complexity class as for VPAs

,
24
]
, since

states that VRAs and VPAs are equivalent under a logspace reduction.
However, using direct algorithms without translations into equivalent VPAs yields lower upper-bounds, as summarized in

Theorem 4.2
(Decision problems for VRAs)

Let
\mathcal{A}_{1},\mathcal{A}_{2}
be two VRAs. The emptiness decision problem is PTIME-complete, with an upper-bound time complexity in
\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{A}_{1}|^{2})
. The universality, inclusion, and equivalence decision problems are EXPTIME-complete, with an upper-bound time complexity respectively in
2^{\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{A}_{1}|)}
,

, and
2^{\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{A}_{1}|+|\mathcal{A}_{2}|)}
.

Proof
(Sketch)

The complexity class was discussed above. We here propose an algorithm that solves the emptiness decision problem for VRAs. The other decision problems are solved with classical methods (see
Appendix 0.I
).

The main idea to solve the emptiness problem is to progressively compute the set of automata in
\Lambda\cup\{\mathcal{A}^{S}\}
whose languages are not empty, using a reachability algorithm. Initially, the algorithm starts from all initial states and is limited to the internal transitions. When a final state of an automaton
\mathcal{A}^{J}\in\Lambda
is reached, this automaton is marked as having a nonempty recursive language, and the algorithm is then allowed to take procedural transitions reading the symbol
J
.

The algorithm updates a set
\mathit{Reach}_{i}\subseteq Q_{\mathcal{A}}
, for
i\in\mathbb{N}
, which contains the states marked as reachable from an initial state. It also uses a set
\mathcal{J}_{i}\subseteq\Sigma_{\mathit{proc}}
which contains symbols
J\in\Sigma_{\mathit{proc}}
such that
\mathit{Reach}_{i}
contains a final state of
\mathcal{A}^{J}
:

•

Initialization:

\mathit{Reach}_{0}=\bigcup_{J\in\Sigma_{\mathit{proc}}\cup\{S\}}I^{J}
;

•

Main loop:
Let
\mathcal{J}_{i}=\{J\in\Sigma_{\mathit{proc}}\mid F^{J}\cap\mathit{Reach}_{i}\neq\varnothing\}
, then
\mathit{Reach}_{i+1}=\mathit{Reach}_{i}\cup\{p\in Q_{\mathcal{A}}\mid\exists q\in\mathit{Reach}_{i},a\in\Sigma_{\mathit{int}}\cup\mathcal{J}_{i}:q\xrightarrow{{\raisebox{-0.60275pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle a$}}}p\in\delta_{\mathcal{A}}\}
;

•

Output:
When
\textit{Reach}_{i+1}=\textit{Reach}_{i}
,
\widetilde{L}(\mathcal{A})=\varnothing
iff
F^{S}\cap\textit{Reach}_{i}=\varnothing
.

The correctness and complexity of the algorithm are detailed in
Appendix 0.I
.

0.D.1
VPA Equivalent to a Given VRA

Taking inspiration from
Figure 2
, we can easily transform any VRA
\mathcal{A}
into an equivalent VPA
\mathcal{B}
. We keep the same set of states; the initial and final states are those of the starting automaton of
\mathcal{A}
; the stack alphabet is equal to the set of states; we keep the same internal transitions as in
\mathcal{A}
, and the call and return transitions are defined to match the semantics of VRAs. Formally, the construction works as follows.

Construction 1

Let
\mathcal{A}=\langle\widetilde{\Sigma},\Sigma_{\mathit{proc}},\Lambda,\mathcal{A}^{S}\rangle
be a VRA. We construct the equivalent VPA
\mathcal{B}=\langle\widetilde{\Sigma},\Gamma,Q_{\mathcal{B}},I,F,\delta_{\mathcal{B}}\rangle
, where:
\Gamma=Q_{\mathcal{A}}
,
Q_{\mathcal{B}}=Q_{\mathcal{A}}
,
I=I^{S}
,
F=F^{S}
, and
\delta_{\mathcal{B}}=\delta_{\mathit{int}}\cup\delta_{\mathit{call}}\cup\delta_{\mathit{ret}}
is defined as follows:

d1
.

q\xrightarrow{{\raisebox{-0.60275pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle a$}}}q^{\prime}\in\delta_{\mathit{int}}\Leftrightarrow q\xrightarrow{{\raisebox{-0.60275pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle a$}}}q^{\prime}\in\delta_{\mathcal{A}}
;

d2
.

q\xrightarrow{{\raisebox{-0.60275pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle c/p$}}}q^{\prime}\in\delta_{\mathit{call}}\Leftrightarrow\exists J\in\Sigma_{\mathit{proc}}:f_{\mathit{call}}(J)=c,q^{\prime}\in I^{J},q\xrightarrow{{\raisebox{-0.60275pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle J$}}}p\in\delta_{\mathcal{A}}
;

d3
.

q\xrightarrow{{\raisebox{-0.60275pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle r[p]$}}}p\in\delta_{\mathit{ret}}\Leftrightarrow\exists J\in\Sigma_{\mathit{proc}}:f_{\mathit{ret}}(J)=r,q\in F^{J}
.

Let us discuss the size of
\mathcal{B}
. It is clear that it has as many states as
\mathcal{A}
and as many internal transitions as
\mathcal{A}
. For the call transitions, we have one call transition per procedural transition and initial state, i.e.,
|\delta_{\mathit{call}}|=\mathcal{O}(|\delta_{\mathcal{A}}|\cdot|Q_{\mathcal{A}}|)
. Finally, we have one return transition per final state and stack symbol, i.e.,
|\delta_{\mathit{ret}}|=\mathcal{O}(|Q_{\mathcal{A}}|\cdot|\Gamma|)=\mathcal{O}(|Q_{\mathcal{A}}|^{2})
. We conclude that
\mathcal{B}
has a size of
|\mathcal{B}|=\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{A}|^{2})
.

Since this construction only translates at worst a constant number of components from the input into a component of the output, it can be computed in a logarithmic space
[
29
]
. Let us explain why this construction is correct and sound.

A word

Construction 2

•

It remains to explain how we compute

with a time complexity in
\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{A}|^{2})
. We suppose that, given

and

, we can access to all transitions of the form
(q,a,p)\in\delta_{\mathcal{A}}
in
\mathcal{O}(1)
, for example with a matrix

giving the list of those states
p
.
Algorithm 1
works as follows.
We process each state
q\in\mathit{Reach}
at most once. When we process
q
,
we add to
\mathit{Reach}
all states
p
such that
p
has not been processed yet and there exists a transition
(q,a,p)\in\delta_{\mathcal{A}}
with

.
Then, if
q
is a final state of some
\mathcal{A}^{J}
with

, we add
J
to
\mathcal{J}
and we process all transitions

for all

already processed. When
\mathit{Reach}
becomes empty, the algorithm has converged, and we know that

iff no final state of

has been processed.

\mathit{Reach}
is first processed once and later reprocessed once for each

. As

0.I.2
Universality, Inclusion, and Equivalence Decision Problems

The algorithms for the universality, inclusion, and equivalence decision problems for VRAs are classical. We present them one after the other.

Without transforming the VRA
\mathcal{A}
into a VPA, we describe a simple way to decide whether
\widetilde{L}(\mathcal{A})=\mathit{WM}(\widetilde{\Sigma})
. We first construct a VRA
\mathcal{B}
such that
\widetilde{L}(\mathcal{B})=\overline{\widetilde{L}(\mathcal{A})}
(see
Theorem 4.1
). We then decide whether
\widetilde{L}(\mathcal{B})=\varnothing
using the emptiness decision problem (see
Theorem 4.2
), with a total time complexity in
2^{\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{A}|)}
.

Deciding whether
\widetilde{L}(\mathcal{A}_{1})\subseteq\widetilde{L}(\mathcal{A}_{2})
amounts to deciding whether
\widetilde{L}(\mathcal{A}_{1})\setminus\widetilde{L}(\mathcal{A}_{2})=\widetilde{L}(\mathcal{A}_{1})\cap\overline{\widetilde{L}(\mathcal{A}_{2})}=\varnothing
. Using the intersection and complementation constructions (see
Theorem 4.1
), and the emptiness decision algorithm (see
Theorem 4.2
), we obtain a time complexity in
\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{A}_{1}|^{2})\cdot 2^{\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{A}_{2}|)}
.

Solving the equivalence decision problem is equivalent to solving two inclusion decision problems, that is, whether
\widetilde{L}(\mathcal{A}_{1})\subseteq\widetilde{L}(\mathcal{A}_{2})
and
\widetilde{L}(\mathcal{A}_{2})\subseteq\widetilde{L}(\mathcal{A}_{1})
. By
Theorem 4.2
, we get a total complexity in
2^{\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{A}_{1}|+|\mathcal{A}_{2}|)}
.
