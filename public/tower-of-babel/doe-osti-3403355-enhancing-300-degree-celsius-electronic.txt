ABSTRACT
A major goal in geothermal energy development is to drill deeper into hotter formations to access
higher-temperature resources, thereby increasing energy output and improving the economic viability
of geothermal power plants. Achieving this requires robust downhole instrumentation capable of
reliable operation in extreme temperature environments often exceeding 200 °C and reaching up to
320 °C.

Measurement While Drilling (MWD) tools play a critical role in this effort by providing real-time
navigation, positioning, and basic measurements such as temperature during drilling operations.
Unlike traditional logging tools, MWD tools cannot rely on vacuum flask enclosures for thermal
protection and must function directly in the harsh downhole environment. Their ability to operate
reliably at elevated temperatures encountered at greater depths is essential for enabling deeper drilling
and accessing these high-temperature geothermal resources.

Meanwhile, downhole logging tools equipped with various sensors are deployed to characterize well
properties such as fracture detection and well integrity. These tools often use low-temperature
electronic components housed inside specialized vacuum flasks that reduce heat transfer, allowing
operation at elevated temperatures for limited durations. While these flasked tools can operate at
temperatures up to approximately 350 °C, their operational duration is limited to about four hours,
making them unsuitable for long-term deployment in harsh geothermal conditions.

To address these challenges, all components in geothermal downhole tools must be rated for sustained
high-temperature operation. Sandia National Laboratories is developing methods to improve the
reliability of embedded electronic systems capable of functioning at temperatures up to 300 °C. This
involves using commercially available high-temperature integrated circuits (ICs) attached to ceramic
printed circuit boards (PCBs) via gold-tin soldering or wire bonding, depending on the device format.
Ceramic PCBs are preferred because they maintain stability and do not degrade at 300 °C, unlike
conventional PCB materials. Additionally, Sandia is exploring epoxies with compatible thermal
expansion properties to conformally coat the ICs, enhancing their durability against vibration and
mechanical shock. These combined techniques aim to enable long-term, reliable operation of
geothermal subsurface instrumentation, including both MWD and logging tools—in extreme
temperature environments up to 300 °C.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
The drive to access higher-temperature geothermal resources by drilling deeper into hotter formations
is critical for increasing energy output and improving the economic viability of geothermal power
plants. However, current Measurement While Drilling (MWD) tools are limited to operating
temperatures around 210 °C, restricting their deployment in these extreme environments. Similarly,
conventional geothermal logging tools rely on low-temperature electronics housed within vacuum
flasks, which can operate at temperatures up to approximately 350 °C but only for short durations of
about four hours, making them unsuitable for long-term monitoring in harsh geothermal conditions.
To overcome these limitations, Sandia National Laboratories has developed and demonstrated
advanced high-temperature electronic packaging techniques enabling geothermal tools to operate
reliably at sustained temperatures up to 300 °C. Central to this effort is the use of ceramic printed
circuit boards (PCBs) that maintain structural and electrical integrity at elevated temperatures where
traditional PCB materials degrade. High-temperature commercial off-the-shelf integrated circuits
(ICs), including the RelChip RC10001 microcontroller and other critical components, were integrated
onto ceramic PCBs using gold-tin soldering and wire bonding techniques optimized for thermal and
mechanical robustness. Additionally, conformal coatings with specially formulated epoxies were
applied to enhance mechanical stability and protect against vibration and thermal cycling
A proof-of-concept prototype data link board was designed and tested, demonstrating continuous
operation at 295 °C for over 1000 hours. The system successfully maintained stable analog-to-digital
conversion and signal modulation under these extreme conditions, validating the effectiveness of the
packaging approach. However, testing revealed solder joint degradation manifested as Au-Sn solder
wicking away from capacitor pads, leading to mechanical bond weakening and occasional component
detachment. These findings highlight critical reliability challenges under combined high-temperature
and mechanical stress, particularly relevant for MWD applications subject to vibration.
To address these issues, the study proposes mitigation strategies including the use of sintered silver
die attach, application of ceramic epoxy for mechanical reinforcement, and design modifications such
as extended relief traces to slow solder migration. Future work will focus on completing PCB assembly
with die bonding and wire bonding, applying conformal coatings, and conducting vibration and
thermal cycling tests to ensure long-term durability. Additionally, development of a fully functional
high-temperature data link and exploration of silicon carbide (SiC) integrated circuits are planned to
further advance geothermal electronics.
This research represents a significant milestone toward enabling reliable, long-duration electronic
instrumentation for enhanced geothermal systems (EGS) and closed-loop geothermal technologies,
supporting improved well characterization, fracture detection, navigation, and well integrity
assessment in increasingly demanding subsurface environments.

1. INTRODUCTION
The global pursuit of sustainable and reliable energy sources has intensified interest in geothermal
energy, particularly through enhanced geothermal systems (EGS) and closed-loop geothermal
technologies. These advanced approaches aim to unlock geothermal resources in deeper, hotter, and
more challenging subsurface environments than conventional geothermal wells. EGS and closed-
loop systems offer the potential for increased energy extraction efficiency, reduced environmental
impact, and expanded geographic applicability. However, realizing these benefits requires robust
subsurface instrumentation capable of long-term operation under extreme temperature, pressure,
and chemical conditions to monitor reservoir behavior, well integrity, and fracture dynamics
effectively.
In addition to these monitoring needs, Measurement While Drilling (MWD) instrumentation plays a
critical role during the drilling process itself. MWD tools provide real-time navigation and
positioning data, as well as basic environmental measurements such as temperature and pressure
near the drill bit. These measurements are essential for steering the drill accurately, optimizing
drilling parameters, and ensuring safe and efficient well construction. Unlike logging tools, MWD
electronics must operate continuously and reliably in the harsh downhole environment without the
benefit of protective enclosures like vacuum flasks, further increasing the technical challenges.
Evaluating geothermal wells using electronic systems presents significant challenges due to the
extreme environmental conditions encountered deep underground. Temperatures in geothermal
wells often exceed 200 °C and can reach up to 320 °C, far beyond the operating limits of most
commercially available electronics. In addition to high temperatures, downhole electronics must
withstand high pressures, corrosive fluids, and mechanical vibrations, all of which severely impact
the performance and reliability of electronic components and packaging methods.
Subsurface instrumentation is critical for characterizing geothermal reservoirs, monitoring well
integrity, detecting fractures, and optimizing energy extraction. These instruments must be compact
to fit within the limited volume of downhole tools, yet robust enough to operate reliably over
extended periods in harsh environments. Achieving this requires the use of small form factor
electronic components, such as surface-mount devices (SMDs) and unpackaged dies, which offer
high integration density and functionality within constrained spaces. Previous research has
demonstrated long-term high-temperature subsurface instrumentation [1]; however, these efforts
were limited to temperatures below 260 °C and below and relied on through-hole components,
which are relatively large and technologically outdated.
A major technical hurdle lies in the limited availability of electronic components that can reliably
operate above 200 °C. While some high-temperature integrated circuits exist, the primary challenge
is not only the components themselves but also their packaging. Standard packaging techniques and
materials used for SMD and die components are typically designed for much lower temperatures and
fail to maintain mechanical and electrical integrity in geothermal well conditions approaching 300
°C.
Packaging these small form factor components for high-temperature operation involves overcoming
several critical challenges. Conventional printed circuit board (PCB) materials degrade or delaminate
at elevated temperatures, solder joints can weaken or fail due to diffusion and thermal expansion
mismatches, and protective coatings must withstand thermal cycling and mechanical stress without
compromising electrical performance. Furthermore, the harsh geothermal environment demands
that the entire electronic assembly—including interconnects, die attach materials, and conformal

 11

coatings—be engineered to resist oxidation, corrosion, and mechanical fatigue over long deployment
durations.
Addressing these packaging challenges is essential to enable the deployment of embedded electronic
systems (EES) capable of digitizing sensor signals near the source, thus mitigating signal degradation
over long cable runs to the surface. This capability is fundamental for real-time data acquisition and
reliable well evaluation.
Sandia National Laboratories is actively tackling these challenges by developing and evaluating
advanced packaging techniques tailored for high-temperature geothermal instrumentation. Building
on prior work that identified critical failure modes in standard surface-mount device packaging,
recent studies have demonstrated promising results using ceramic printed circuit boards (PCBs),
gold-tin soldering, wire bonding, and high-temperature conformal coatings. These approaches
individually show robust performance at elevated temperatures, mitigating material degradation and
mechanical failures.
This work advances prior research by integrating these packaging methods into a functional
prototype electronics board designed for reliable operation at 300 °C. Serving as a proof-of-concept
platform, the prototype validates the combined mechanical and electrical integrity of the packaging
approaches under realistic geothermal conditions. While initial software development has been
limited to basic analog-to-digital (ADC) and digital-to-analog (DAC) functionalities, the hardware
integration marks a critical step toward realizing a fully operational high-temperature data link
capable of supporting advanced downhole sensing, telemetry, and well characterization.
This paper details the design, fabrication, and preliminary testing of the prototype board,
highlighting the challenges encountered during integration and the strategies employed to overcome
them. The successful demonstration of this prototype will inform future efforts to develop
comprehensive high-temperature electronic solutions, ultimately enabling enhanced geothermal
system (EGS) monitoring, navigation and positioning, fracture detection, and well integrity
assessment over extended deployment periods. While focused on geothermal applications, the
packaging approaches developed here have potential relevance for other industries requiring
electronics capable of withstanding extreme environments.

2. HIGH TEMPERATURE CHALLENGES WITH STANDARD PCBS
The deployment of electronic systems in geothermal wells at temperatures exceeding 200 °C presents
significant challenges that standard printed circuit board (PCB) materials and surface-mount device
(SMD) packaging techniques are ill-equipped to handle. Conventional PCB substrates commonly used
in electronics, such as Roger’s materials (e.g., RO3003, RO4003) and polyimides (e.g., Arlon85N),
have decomposition temperatures typically around 260 °C. When exposed to sustained temperatures
near or above this threshold, these materials undergo thermal degradation, including dielectric
breakdown, delamination, and mechanical deformation. For example, Rogers 4003 PCBs have been
observed to develop internal bubbles and delamination after prolonged exposure to 300 °C,
compromising the electrical integrity and mechanical stability of the board, as seen in Figure 1. Such
degradation not only threatens the reliability of the PCB itself but also induces strain on solder joints
and component interfaces, leading to premature failure of the embedded electronic system. Much of
this research and these findings were detailed in our 2023 SAND report [2].

Figure 1: Cross-sectional image showing bubbling and delamination within a multilayer Rogers
4003 PCB after baking at 300 °C. The formation of internal voids compromises mechanical
integrity and electrical performance.
Surface-mount device packaging, while advantageous for its compact footprint and high component
density, introduces additional complexities at elevated temperatures. SMD components rely on solder
joints to establish both mechanical and electrical connections to the PCB. Standard solder alloys, such
as 60/40 tin-lead, have melting points well below the operational temperatures encountered in
geothermal wells, resulting in solder softening or melting that compromises joint integrity. To address
this, high-melting-point (HMP) solders, typically composed of approximately 93.5% lead, 5% tin, and
1.5% silver, with a liquidus temperature near 301 °C, are employed. However, these HMP solders
interact adversely with the gold plating commonly used on component leads and PCB pads. Gold
tends to diffuse into the solder matrix at elevated temperatures, a process known as gold
embrittlement, which degrades the mechanical strength and electrical conductivity of the solder joint
over time, as seen in Figure 2. This interaction accelerates solder joint failure, especially under the
combined stresses of thermal cycling and mechanical vibration typical in geothermal environments.

 13

Figure 2: Micrograph of a high-melting-point (HMP) solder joint illustrating gold diffusion into the
solder and degradation of the gold plating on both the PCB pad and the SMD component pins
after prolonged exposure to elevated temperatures.
Another critical issue arises from the mismatch in coefficients of thermal expansion (CTE) between
the PCB substrate and the SMD components. Materials expand and contract at different rates when
subjected to temperature changes, and this mismatch induces mechanical strain at the solder joints
and interfaces. Over repeated thermal cycles, these stresses can cause solder fatigue, cracking, and
eventual debonding of components from the PCB. In geothermal applications, where tools must
endure prolonged exposure to high temperatures combined with mechanical vibrations and shocks,
such failures are particularly problematic.
Furthermore, the standard PCB fabrication processes and metallization stacks used for conventional
electronics are often inadequate for high-temperature operation. For instance, copper layers capped
with thin gold coatings can suffer from copper migration and oxidation at elevated temperatures,
leading to increased electrical resistance and reduced solderability. The presence of nickel as a diffusion
barrier and the use of palladium layers in electroless-nickel electroless-palladium immersion-gold
(ENEPIG) stacks have been explored to mitigate these effects. However, even these advanced
metallization schemes require careful optimization to prevent degradation during extended high-
temperature exposure.
Collectively, these challenges highlight the limitations of standard PCB materials and SMD packaging
for geothermal electronics operating at temperatures approaching or exceeding 300 °C. Addressing
these issues requires the development of specialized materials, packaging techniques, and assembly
processes tailored to the harsh geothermal environment. The subsequent sections detail the
approaches investigated by Sandia National Laboratories, including the use of ceramic PCBs, gold-tin
soldering, wire bonding, and high-temperature conformal coatings, which collectively aim to
overcome the inherent limitations of conventional packaging methods.

3. TECHNIQUES TO INCREASE OPERATING TEMPERATURE OF
 PRINTED CIRCUIT BOARD AND INTERCONNECTS
To enable reliable operation of off-the-shelf integrated circuits (ICs) on printed circuit boards (PCBs)
at temperatures up to 300 °C, our research focuses on three critical aspects: the choice of PCB
substrate material, the method of electrical interconnection between the IC and PCB, and the
application of conformal coatings to protect and mechanically reinforce the assembly. Sandia National
Laboratories specifically investigated the use of ceramic PCBs, gold-tin (Au/Sn) solder, gold wire
bonding, and high-temperature epoxy conformal coatings to address the challenges posed by harsh
geothermal environments. Similar approaches have been studied by GE in collaboration with other
research groups, as documented in several of their joint publications [3,4].

3.1. Ceramic Printed Circuit Boards
Ceramic substrates, particularly alumina (Al₂O₃), are highly suitable for high-temperature applications
and are commonly employed in power electronics where effective heat dissipation and thermal stability
are critical. Alumina offers excellent electrical insulation, robust mechanical strength, and is readily
available for industrial manufacturing, making it an ideal choice for geothermal tool electronics.
For metallization, the industry-standard electroless-nickel electroless-palladium immersion-gold
(ENEPIG) plating was initially selected, with targeted layer thicknesses of titanium (Ti), copper (Cu),
nickel (Ni), palladium (Pd), and gold (Au) at approximately 150/750/250/250/250 nm, respectively.
Each layer serves a specific function to ensure electrical performance and durability in harsh
environments. The titanium layer acts as an adhesion promoter, bonding the metal stack securely to
the ceramic substrate. The thick copper layer provides the primary electrical conductivity and thermal
management for the PCB traces. Nickel functions as a critical diffusion barrier, preventing copper
atoms from migrating upward into the palladium and gold layers, which could otherwise lead to
oxidation and degradation. Palladium serves as an additional diffusion barrier and oxidation protector,
while also reducing the amount of costly gold required. Finally, the thin gold surface finish offers
excellent corrosion resistance, oxidation protection, and a highly solderable and wire bondable surface,
essential for reliable assembly and long-term operation.
Thin gold coatings have long been used to prevent corrosion and oxidation of underlying metal layers.
However, at elevated temperatures, diffusion processes can degrade this protection: gold atoms may
migrate into the base layers, while copper can diffuse upward through the gold, leading to oxidation
and loss of solderability. This degradation can occur rapidly at high temperatures, severely impacting
electrical performance.
To mitigate these degradation effects, diffusion barrier layers such as nickel and palladium are
incorporated between the copper and gold layers. However, testing revealed that the standard
ENEPIG stack oxidized significantly after a few weeks at 300 °C due to copper diffusion through the
gold layer. Interestingly, a fabrication error resulting in the absence of copper in one sample led to
markedly improved oxidation resistance, indicating that eliminating copper from the metallization
stack enhances long-term stability at elevated temperatures. To compensate for the loss of copper’s
electrical conductivity, the thickness of the nickel diffusion barrier was increased.
These findings are illustrated in Figure 3. The left image shows the initial substrate with a clean gold
surface immediately after metallization of the ceramic. The middle image depicts the conventional
ENEPIG stack (Ti/Cu/Ni/Pd/Au) after baking at 300 °C for over 100 hours, revealing pronounced
oxidation on the gold surface, although the metal layers maintained strong adhesion to the ceramic
substrate. The right image shows a copper-free stack (Ti/Ni/Pd/Au) subjected to the same thermal

 15

exposure. While the gold surface exhibits discoloration, no oxidation is evident, suggesting that metal
migration from the underlying layers to the surface occurred without compromising the protective
gold layer. Importantly, the metallization remained firmly adhered to the ceramic substrate,
demonstrating improved thermal stability.

Figure 3: (Left) ENEPIG stack up on ceramic, (middle) ENEPIG stack up exposed to 300 C, (Right)
Ti/Ni/Pb/Au stack up exposed to 300C.
Further studies have progressed to remove the nickel layer as well, resulting in a simplified
Ti/Pd/Au metallization stack. This configuration, discussed in detail later in the paper,
demonstrated superior performance at 300 °C by eliminating diffusion pathways and reducing
oxidation. The titanium layer continues to provide adhesion, palladium acts as a diffusion barrier,
and gold remains the protective surface finish.
Following metallization, the circuit patterns on the ceramic PCBs were defined using laser
machining with an LPKF Protolaser R4 picosecond laser (515 nm wavelength). This laser system,
designed for rapid prototyping, effectively processes ceramic substrates with minimal heat-affected
zones due to its short pulse duration, preserving the integrity of both the ceramic and metallization
layers.
The laser machining process involved three key routines:
 • Isolation: Removal of material between traces to electrically isolate them, using 3.65 W
 power, 150 kHz frequency, 600 mm/s speed, and 20 repetition passes. This step may be
 omitted in future to reduce unnecessary material removal on non-laminated substrates.
 • Hatching: Complete ablation of residual thin films between traces, using 7.3 W power, 300
 kHz frequency, 1900 mm/s speed, and 2 repetition passes.
 • Cutting: Defining the board outline and drilling vias. Due to size differences, vias required
 higher laser interaction (8 W power, 200 kHz frequency, 300 mm/s speed, 750 passes)
 compared to the board outline (same power and frequency, 500 passes).
Combining these steps, the resulting sample is shown in Figure 4. This PCB features a
Ti/Cu/Ni/Pd/Au stack, as the removal of copper and nickel was discovered later. The pattern was
etched to evaluate Cissoid’s CHT-RIGEL Voltage Regulator.

3.2. Gold-Tin Solder Interconnect
Building on the stable ceramic PCB metallization, we evaluated the electrical bonding of integrated
circuits (ICs) using gold-tin (Au/Sn) solder. Mitsubishi Materials USA Au78/Sn22 solder paste was
used, characterized by particle sizes of 5–16 µm, flux type AS1, viscosity of 90 Pa·s, and
solidus/liquidus temperatures of 278 °C and 301 °C, respectively. This solder alloy is well-suited for
high-temperature applications approaching 300 °C.
Soldering was performed in a four-stage Sikama oven, which precisely controls the temperature
profile to ensure uniform solder reflow while minimizing thermal exposure to the IC and PCB. The
temperature profile is shown in Figure 5, with a maximum temperature of 300 °C and occasional
excursions up to 320 °C.

 17

Figure 5: Four-stage Sikama oven temperature profile for the Au78/Sn22 solder. In this profile, the
temperature max was 300 °C, but sometimes the temperature may reach up to 320 °C.
The use of a multi-stage oven with a controlled nitrogen atmosphere offers several advantages over
traditional hand soldering. The nitrogen environment significantly reduces oxidation during
soldering, preserving the integrity of gold plating and preventing oxide formation that can degrade
solder joints. Precise temperature control minimizes thermal stress and improves reproducibility and
consistency of solder joints, which is critical for high-reliability geothermal electronics.
A Cissoid CHT-RIGEL voltage regulator was bonded to the ceramic PCB using this gold-tin solder

the device at 300 °C. Importantly, no delamination of the surface-mount device (SMD) IC from the
PCB was observed during testing.
However, the gold traces on the PCB blackened due to copper presence in the ENEPIG

strong mechanical and electrical integrity. The gold-tin solder also helped mitigate oxidation near the

Future work will include vibration testing of the packaged PCB at 300 °C to assess whether the

fusion or degradation of the deposited metal layers contributes to solder joint failure. These tests are
critical to validate the mechanical robustness of the solder joints under realistic geothermal
downhole conditions, where vibration and thermal cycling are prevalent.

3.3. Gold Wire Bonding Interconnect
In addition to solder bonding, wire bonding of unpackaged integrated circuit (IC) dies directly onto
ceramic printed circuit boards (PCBs) offers important mechanical benefits. This method uses
conductive curable paste to attach the die to the ceramic substrate, followed by gold wire ball
bonding to establish electrical connections. One key advantage of wire bonding is its ability to act as
strain relief, accommodating the mismatch in coefficients of thermal expansion (CTE) between the
silicon die and the ceramic PCB. This flexibility helps maintain reliable electrical connections despite
the thermal cycling and mechanical stress common in geothermal environments.
However, this approach has limitations. Only a limited number of high-temperature (HT) ICs are
available in die form, and the bare die is considerably more fragile than surface-mount device (SMD)
packaged ICs, increasing susceptibility to damage during handling and packaging.
The die attach material must satisfy stringent processing and operational requirements, including
strong adhesion to the alumina substrate, survivability at temperatures up to 300 °C, electrical
conductivity, and corrosion resistance. Additionally, it must have very low levels of unbound ionic
contaminants—typically less than 25 parts per million (ppm)—since ions such as sulfur, sodium, and
lithium can promote corrosion and degrade device reliability.
Conventional die attach epoxies are generally unsuitable for 300 °C environments due to polymer
degradation at such temperatures. While eutectic solders are commonly used for die attachment in
ceramic packages, their processing temperatures are below the required operating temperature.
Brazing materials, although capable of withstanding higher temperatures, require processing
conditions that are too harsh for silicon dies.
To overcome these challenges, sintered silver die attach was selected. This material consists of fine
silver particles suspended in a polymer and solvent matrix, which sinter during curing at
approximately 200 °C. The sintering process forms a dense metallurgical bond between the die
backside and the gold-plated alumina substrate. Solvent evaporation during curing leaves minimal
polymer residue, resulting in a mechanically robust and electrically conductive bond.
For this study, Abelstik ICP 9000 hybrid sintered silver paste was used to attach a Cissoid CHT-
RIGEL voltage regulator die to the ceramic PCB. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was
performed to assess mass loss and thermal stability at elevated temperatures, shown in the results in
Figure 7.

3.4. Ceramic Based Conformal Coating
We hypothesized that applying a conformal coating over the electrical connections could enhance
the mechanical bond between the integrated circuit (IC) and the printed circuit board (PCB), thereby
improving durability under high-temperature and mechanical stress conditions. To evaluate this,
several commercially available high-temperature (HT) epoxies from multiple manufacturers were
tested.
Three key criteria guided the selection of a suitable epoxy:
 • Low viscosity, to enable application of a thin, uniform layer over delicate structures such as
 wire bonds and solder joints.
 • Thermal stability and suitability for continuous operation at elevated temperatures.
 • A coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE) closely matched to that of the ceramic PCB
 substrate, typically in the range of 6–9 ppm/°C, to minimize thermomechanical stresses
 during temperature cycling.
Based on these criteria, Micro Measurements’ 800 °C line of ceramic epoxies was selected for
testing. The specific formulations included WC-16 (CTE 7.2 ppm/°C), HG-1 (10.8 ppm/°C), and
NCC-3 (12.6 ppm/°C). Due to its closest CTE match to alumina, WC-16 was anticipated to provide
the best mechanical compatibility and reliability.
The WC-16 epoxy was applied to the wire-bonded device described in Section 3.3. The coated
device was then subjected to an additional 300 hours of testing at 300 °C. During this period, the
device exhibited a drop in output voltage and an increase in input current. Visual inspection of the
electrical connections was not possible due to the opaque nature of the ceramic epoxy coating, as
seen in Figure 9. It is believed that the increased current draw resulted from further degradation of
the IC itself, rather than any electrical conduction through the epoxy, which remained dielectric and
did not compromise electrical isolation.
To further assess the epoxy’s protective capability, additional experiments were conducted on WC-
16 coated wire bonds bridging two PCB traces. After 500 hours at 300 °C, electrical continuity was
maintained through the wire bonds, indicating that the epoxy effectively preserved the mechanical
and electrical integrity of the connections under prolonged thermal stress.

3.5. 800 Degree Celsius Evaluation
Building upon the initial success of the 300 °C packaging techniques presented in our previous work
[5], a preliminary study was conducted to evaluate the survivability of the packaging materials and
assembly at an elevated temperature of 800 °C for a duration of 24 hours. This accelerated thermal
exposure was designed to assess the robustness and identify potential failure modes of critical
components, including the sintered silver die attach, gold wire bonds, and ceramic PCB
metallization, under extreme conditions far exceeding typical geothermal well temperatures.
The results demonstrated that the integrated circuit (IC) die remained firmly attached to the ceramic
substrate via the sintered silver die attach, confirming the metallurgical bond’s resilience at these
elevated temperatures. This is illustrated in Figure 10, which shows images focused on both the PCB
surface and the die itself. The gold wire bonds largely retained their mechanical integrity; however, a
few wire bonds exhibited noticeable thinning and appeared partially absorbed into the underlying
metal stack on the ceramic board, as shown in Figure 11. This observation suggests possible
interdiffusion or morphological changes occurring at the wire bond interface during prolonged high-
temperature exposure.

4. 300 DEGREE CELSIUS EMBEDDED ELECTRONIC SYSTEM
Building upon the advanced high-temperature packaging techniques previously described, we
designed and evaluated a proof-of-concept electronics board intended to operate reliably at 300 °C.
The primary goal of this system is to establish a robust data link capable of transmitting sensor data
from deep geothermal wells to the surface, while simultaneously delivering power to the downhole
electronics.

4.1. Datalink Block Diagram
Initially, the design aimed to utilize a single-conductor wireline—specifically a coaxial cable—to
carry both sensor data and power. This approach extended our earlier work on a 210 °C data link
[9], leveraging a 12-bit resistor digital-to-analog converter (DAC), a microcontroller, and an amplifier
to generate a modulated low-impedance signal suitable for transmission over long distances. The
concept included transmitting a DC bias current along the single conductor, which would be
separated from the modulated data signal at the surface using a bias-tee.
However, implementing this design required an inductor with sufficiently high inductance to
effectively isolate the DC and AC components within the bandwidth constraints of geothermal
wirelines. Although a commercial inductor rated for 300 °C was identified, its inductance was
insufficient for the application. To overcome this, a custom high-inductance inductor was fabricated
using ceramic-coated wire wound around an iron core, demonstrating operation at and above 300
°C. Despite this success, the inductor’s large physical size and significant temperature-dependent
variation in inductance rendered the single-conductor approach impractical for compact downhole
tools.
As a result, the system architecture was revised to employ a four-conductor wireline configuration:
two conductors dedicated to supplying DC power and two conductors reserved for transmitting the
modulated data signal. This separation simplifies signal integrity and power delivery, facilitating more
reliable operation under geothermal conditions.
The electronics board incorporates an onboard 5 V voltage regulator to supply stable power to all
integrated circuits. Key components include a single-channel analog-to-digital converter (ADC) for
digitizing sensor inputs, a 4 MHz crystal oscillator to provide a stable clock signal for the
microcontroller, and a memory IC for optional data storage. This fundamental circuit architecture
supports the acquisition of essential subsurface parameters such as temperature, pressure, and flow
rate, enabling comprehensive well characterization.

Figure 12: Data link block diagram.
The data link system was designed to collect sensor data, process it locally, and transmit it, closely
mimicking the hardware used in geothermal subsurface instrumentation. It is engineered to receive
power over a wireline extending thousands of feet, accommodating variations in power delivery and

 23

signal degradation inherent in long-distance transmission. The block diagram of the system is shown
in Figure 12, and the key components selected are reviewed below.

4.2. Component Selection
Several components were selected for the data link based on their demonstrated ability to operate
reliably at 300 °C or through successful high-temperature testing. It is important to note that many
of these integrated circuits are no longer in production due to the closure of X-Fab’s high-
temperature silicon-on-insulator (SOI) process; the components used in this work were acquired
prior to that announcement.
The selection includes devices from several specialized manufacturers known for their expertise in
high-temperature electronics. Honeywell has a long history of producing robust analog and power
components, such as the HTOP01 operational amplifier and HTNFET transistors, which have been
proven reliable in geothermal and deep gas drilling applications [6,7]. Cissoid contributes high-
temperature analog and mixed-signal ICs, including voltage regulators and analog-to-digital
converters (ADCs), essential for sensor signal conditioning and power management.
RelChip provides advanced high-temperature microcontrollers and memory devices fabricated using
SOI technology. Their RC10001 microcontroller, built on a 0.15 µm SOI process, supports
operation up to 300 °C and offers modern processing capabilities for embedded geothermal systems
[8]. Frequency Management supplies high-temperature crystal oscillators that maintain stable clock
signals under extreme thermal conditions, ensuring reliable microcontroller timing.
Together, these components form a comprehensive platform enabling robust sensor data
acquisition, processing, and transmission in the demanding environment of geothermal wells.

4.2.1. Honeywell Opamp
The Honeywell HTOP01 operational amplifier was selected as the amplifier and low-impedance
driver for the resistor digital-to-analog converter (DAC) due to its proven high-temperature
performance and robust design tailored for harsh environments. This opamp features a wide
operating temperature range, low input offset voltage, and stable gain characteristics, making it
suitable for precision analog signal conditioning in geothermal instrumentation.
For packaging, the HTOP01 die was directly attached to a ceramic printed circuit board (PCB) using
a high-temperature compatible die attach material, ensuring strong mechanical bonding and thermal
conduction. Electrical connections were established via gold wire bonding, which provides flexibility
to accommodate thermal expansion mismatches and maintains reliable contact at elevated
temperatures. The packaged device is shown in Figure 13, where it is wire bonded to the ceramic
PCB and connected to test equipment for evaluation.
The evaluation setup involved a basic voltage follower circuit external to the PCB, powered and
monitored using a function generator and oscilloscope. This configuration allowed precise
measurement of the opamp’s output stability, gain, and response under controlled temperature
ramping.
Testing demonstrated that the HTOP01 maintained stable operation up to 290 °C, with minimal
drift in output voltage and consistent gain performance. However, upon increasing the temperature
to 295 °C, the device exhibited failure after several hours, characterized by loss of output signal and
increased noise. This failure threshold provides critical insight into the thermal limits of the
component and informs the design margin for the overall system.

 24

Challenges observed during testing included thermal-induced drift and potential degradation of
internal transistor junctions at temperatures approaching the failure point. Future work will explore
improved thermal management, alternative die attach materials, and potential device screening to
enhance reliability.
The performance of the HTOP01 directly impacts the fidelity of the DAC output signal and the
integrity of the modulated data transmitted to the surface, underscoring its critical role in the high-
temperature data link architecture. However, since the component did not reliably operate at the
target temperature of 300 °C, we decided not to use the HTOP01 as the amplifier for the data link
and shifted our focus to evaluating the Honeywell HTNFET line driver as a more suitable
alternative for high-temperature operation.

4.2.2. Honeywell NFET Line Driver Circuit
After determining that the Honeywell HTOP01 operational amplifier did not reliably operate at the
target temperature of 300 °C, our focus shifted to evaluating the Honeywell HTNFET line driver as
a more suitable alternative for high-temperature operation.
The Honeywell HTNFET transistor, available in die form, provides reliable switching performance
at elevated temperatures, making it well-suited for driving low-impedance geothermal wireline
cables. Its high current handling capability, low on-resistance, and thermal stability are essential for
maintaining signal integrity and efficient power delivery in harsh downhole environments.
For testing, two Honeywell HTNFETs were used in a dual in-line package (DIP) format. The
devices were mounted on a ceramic breadboard, chosen for its thermal stability and electrical
insulation properties. To establish electrical connections during testing, an older but reliable
technique called wire wrapping was employed. This method involves tightly wrapping thin wire
around the pins and corresponding board contacts, providing secure and low-resistance connections
suitable for prototyping and high-temperature evaluation.
The line driver circuit, whose schematic is shown in Figure 14, was designed using these two
HTNFET transistors to provide sufficient drive strength and impedance matching for the wireline.

switching delay, and power dissipation under load conditions representative of geothermal wireline

4.2.3. Honeywell Voltage Regulator
The components on the data link rely on a stable 5 V power supply for proper operation. The
Cissoid CHT-RIGEL voltage regulator, initially considered for this role, was found to be non-
functional at 300 °C. Consequently, the packaged Honeywell HTPLREG voltage regulator was
selected and evaluated extensively for high-temperature reliability. The HTPLREG operated
continuously at 300 °C for 1,848 hours without failure. During testing, the regulator was supplied
with a 12 V input voltage and drew approximately 100 mA of current. Throughout this period, it
maintained a consistent 5 V output, demonstrating excellent voltage regulation under extreme
thermal conditions. Figure 16 shows the packaged Honeywell HTPLREG voltage regulator under
testing.
For the data link, we will use the HTPLREG in a die format. To understand how to use the die IC,
we carefully opened the standard 4-pin packaged HTPLREG and discovered a second IC die inside,
which we interpreted as a high-temperature field-effect transistor (HTNFET), as seen in Figure 16.
Leveraging this insight, our approach involves replicating the packaged HTPLREG’s functionality
by mounting and wire bonding the HTPLREG die and the HTNFET die separately onto the
ceramic PCB. This method offers greater flexibility in packaging and the potential for improved
performance under high-temperature conditions. The HTNFET die will be used to drive the 5 V
power rail supplying the microcontroller, memory, ADC, DAC, and line driver circuits, ensuring
stable power delivery throughout the system.

4.2.4. RelChip Microcontroller and Memory
At the core of the data link system is the RelChip RC10001 microcontroller [8], housed in a 144-pin
quad flat package (QFP). This microcontroller is one of the few commercially available integrated
circuits qualified for reliable operation at temperatures up to 300 °C. Its robust architecture and
extensive pin count provide the computational power and input/output flexibility necessary for
sensor data acquisition, signal modulation, and communication in harsh geothermal environments.

 27

The microcontroller operates at a clock frequency of 4 MHz under elevated temperature conditions,
balancing performance with thermal constraints.
Complementing the microcontroller is the RelChip RC2110836 static random-access memory
(SRAM), which features an 8K x 36-bit memory array housed in a 68-pin J-lead ceramic QFP
package. This volatile memory device offers stable data retention and mechanical reliability at high
temperatures, ensuring dependable storage and retrieval of sensor data during downhole operations.
Together, these components form a critical processing and memory backbone for the high-
temperature data link.
Extensive research on the performance and reliability of these microcontroller and memory
components under high-temperature conditions, including testing up to 300 °C, has been conducted
and documented in detail in a previous Sandia National Laboratories report [10]. Given the 300 °C
rating of these components and the prior successful testing, additional 300 °C testing was not
performed as part of this study.

4.2.7. Rhopoint Resistors
The Rhopoint Components SRT CHR 0805-HT resistor series was selected for use in the data link

4.2.8. Presidio NHT Capacitors
Presidio’s NHT series capacitors were selected based on supplier specifications indicating reliable
capacitance and low equivalent series resistance (ESR) under high thermal stress. These capacitors
are rated for operation at 300 °C and higher, making them suitable for use in high-temperature
geothermal electronics where effective filtering and signal integrity are critical. According to the
manufacturer’s data, the capacitance values of these capacitors are expected to remain largely
unaffected by elevated temperatures, ensuring stable performance in harsh environments.
For the 5 V power rail, the HT2225NHT503K3H1 capacitor, with a capacitance of 50 nF, was
chosen as it represents the largest available capacitance within the NHT series suitable for this
application. To achieve the required total capacitance on the 5 V rail, multiple
HT2225NHT503K3H1 capacitors will be used in parallel. Additionally, the HT0402NHT270J3H1
capacitor, with a capacitance of 27 pF, was selected as the load capacitor for the 4 MHz crystal
oscillator, providing the necessary load capacitance to ensure stable and accurate clock operation at
elevated temperatures.
Due to significant delivery delays and limited project timelines, these capacitors were not tested
independently prior to the design and fabrication of the data link circuit and PCB. Instead, we relied
on the manufacturer’s specifications and the capacitors’ rated capability to operate at 300 °C and
above. Since the capacitors on the power rail primarily serve filtering functions, minor changes in
capacitance were not considered critical. However, capacitance stability was a greater concern for the
crystal load capacitor, as variations in capacitance can alter the crystal’s resonant frequency,
potentially detuning the microcontroller’s clock and affecting system timing accuracy. That said,
capacitance drift was less of a concern overall due to the manufacturer’s specification indicating
minimal capacitance change at 300 °C.

4.2.9.     Bias-Tee
As discussed at the beginning of Section 4, the initial design concept for the data link aimed to
operate over a single-conductor wireline, where power would be transmitted down to the electronics
and the modulated signal sent back to the surface along the same conductor. However, this
approach was ultimately abandoned due to the requirement for a large inductor, which posed

significant integration challenges. This section reviews the research conducted on designing a bias-
tee and the associated inductors for such a system.
For the bias tee to function effectively within the frequency bandwidth of the single-conductor
wireline [9], simulations using LTspice® indicated the need for a 1000 µH inductor and a 600 nF
capacitor, as shown in Figure 19. Achieving the 600 nF capacitance would require incorporating
twelve HT2225NHT503K3H1 capacitors in parallel on the PCB. While this is feasible, it is not ideal
due to space constraints.

4.2.10. High Temperature Connector
A high-temperature edge-connect hermetic header capable of operating up to 400 °C has been
developed by colleagues in Sandia’s 7616 division and was considered as a potential connector
solution for the data link. Given the dimensional constraints of geothermal instrumentation, we
aimed to limit the width of the electronics board to 2 inches or less to facilitate integration within
the tool.
However, the fabrication limitations of ceramic PCBs, which are currently restricted to a maximum
size of approximately 4.5” x 4.5”, presented a challenge. To accommodate all necessary components
for the data link within this footprint, additional vertical stacking of PCBs would be required,
making a reliable connector essential for interconnecting multiple boards.
Upon reviewing the custom hermetic header, it became apparent that modifying it into an edge
connector suitable for PCB mating would be complex and costly. Developing a reliable edge-mating
connector set tailored for the data link would demand a significant portion of the project’s funding
and resources.
Consequently, to maintain simplicity and control costs, we decided against incorporating this
connector. Instead, the design was constrained to fit all components within a single 4.5” x 4.5”
ceramic PCB, avoiding the need for additional inter-board connectors. Future research would
explore integrating this technology for high temperature PCBs.

oscillator was not available and subsequently removed from the design, leaving only the crystal. The
four-layer board was slated for fabrication by AdTech Ceramics, a leader in high-temperature
ceramic PCB manufacturing. Unfortunately, the complexity of the four-layer design combined with

 36

the small via sizes resulted in prohibitively high fabrication costs. Consequently, to reduce cost and
fabrication complexity, the design was revised to a simpler two-layer board with increased via
diameters. To accommodate this reduction in layers, the second SRAM was removed to simplify the
circuit layout.
The final PCB design consists of two metal layers, with minimum trace widths and spacing of 8 mils,
and via drill diameters of 28 mils. The board measures 4.2” × 3.5”. Although a narrower board
would be preferable for integration within geothermal tools, limitations in processing equipment—
including metal plating, etching, and soldering—as well as the challenges of interconnecting multiple
high-temperature PCBs, led to the decision to fabricate a single, larger board as a proof-of-concept.

Figure 28: PCB layout of the data link with two SRAM devices and the Frequency Management
oscillator (left) and without the second SRAM and oscillator (right).

Figure 29: R4 substrate with copper cladding and laser-etched PCB layout used for laser etching
testing.
Due to budget and time constraints, the PCB was fabricated in-house. To validate the laser etching
process, an FR4 substrate with copper cladding was first tested to ensure the laser etching steps were
effective before proceeding to the ceramic substrate, as seen in Figure 29. The ceramic substrate
used is CoorsTek ADS-996 DSP, composed of 99.6% Al₂O₃, with a flatness of 0.0005” and a
surface roughness (Ra max) of 1 μm. The substrate thickness is 0.025”. The fabrication process

 37

began with laser etching to define the board outline and via holes. After cutting and drilling, the
ceramic substrate was metallized with a titanium/palladium/gold (Ti/Pd/Au) stack to ensure full
metal coverage of the vias. Based on prior observations that copper and nickel can migrate through
palladium and gold layers—leading to degradation of the gold finish at 300 °C—the metallization
stack was modified to exclude copper and nickel, using only Ti/Pd/Au layers.
To ensure proper metallization within the vias during sputtering, the ratio of substrate thickness to
via hole diameter was maintained at approximately 1:1. Given the ceramic thickness of 25 mils, via
diameters were set to 25 mils or larger to guarantee adequate metal plating and electrical continuity.
The metallization process began with substrate cleaning, involving soaking in a 1 vol% Liquinox
solution (Alconox, LLC), followed by rinsing with deionized water (18 MΩ). The substrates were
then rinsed sequentially with semiconductor-grade acetone and isopropanol, followed by vapor
degreasing in Kyzen SLV901 for 10 minutes. After drying with nitrogen, the substrates were fired in
an electric air furnace at 860 °C for 2 hours.
Thin films were deposited using a custom-built sputter deposition system (Innotech). The substrates
and a witness sample—with a masked step for thickness measurement—were loaded into the
vacuum chamber, which was pumped down to a base pressure below 5 × 10⁻⁷ torr. Deposition rates
and thicknesses were calibrated from prior runs.
Titanium films were sputtered at 4.5 mtorr argon pressure using 520 W DC power from an 8-inch
target in a sputter-down configuration. Without breaking vacuum, palladium was deposited at 5.0
mtorr argon pressure with the same power and target size. Finally, gold was sputtered under
identical pressure and power conditions. Deposition times were 22.5 minutes for Ti, 11.88 minutes
for Pd, and 5.5 minutes for Au, resulting in a total stack thickness of approximately 1500 Å Ti /
2500 Å Pd / 5000 Å Au.

Figure 30: Final fabrication of the two-layer ceramic PCB with Ti/Pb/Au stack for the data link.
The gold layer thickness was increased compared to previous samples to ensure sufficient electrical
conductivity and to mitigate concerns about titanium and palladium migration through the gold.

 38

Titanium and palladium layers were deposited with planetary rotation for uniformity, while gold
deposition was performed with samples stationary under the sputter source. After deposition, the
system was cooled under vacuum, then vented with nitrogen, and the parts were removed and
stored in clean-room-grade nylon bags.
Profilometry measurements using a Bruker DektakXT confirmed total deposited thicknesses of
approximately 9217 Å on side 1 and 9330 Å on side 2.
Following metallization, a second laser etching step was performed to pattern the traces and pads.
The final board, shown in Figure 30, underwent resistive probing to verify via conductivity and
overall electrical integrity. No continuity issues were observed during testing, confirming the success
of the fabrication process.

4.5. PCB Assembly
Due to time and budget constraints, the PCB assembly process was minimized. Die-attached
components were not populated on the board; consequently, neither the voltage regulator nor the
line driver circuits were installed. Consistent with previous efforts, 78/22 Au/Sn solder was used to
attach the surface-mount devices (SMDs) to the ceramic PCB.
To address concerns that mechanical strain from wiring could dislodge connections, sintered silver
was employed to electrically bond Teflon-insulated, nickel-plated copper wires to the circuit board.
However, during assembly, two wires were inadvertently omitted and were subsequently added using
Au/Sn solder.
A Sikama four-stage oven was essential for soldering all components onto the board. The process
involved carefully applying Au/Sn solder paste to each pad and precisely positioning the ICs in their
designated locations. Since the PCB lacked a solder mask, meticulous solder application was critical
to prevent shorts between adjacent pads. The Sikama temperature profile used to successfully solder
the components is shown in Figure 31.
To ensure all Au/Sn solder joints were properly formed, the PCB was exposed to temperatures up
to 350 °C for approximately 30 seconds. This elevated temperature exposure was necessary due to
the large size of the board and to guarantee that the entire board reached temperatures above 301
°C, the liquidus point of the 78/22 Au/Sn solder.

Figure 31: Sikama temperature profile for Au-Sn solder.
The final assembled PCB is shown in Figure 32, immediately prior to high-temperature oven testing.
Before subjecting the board to elevated temperatures, basic software was loaded to verify
functionality. Initially, the software was intended to collect data from a sensor and transmit it with a
“high speed” modulated signal through the DAC to demonstrate the data link functionality, as well

 39

as store the sensor data on the memory. However, due to budget restrictions, the software was
simplified to continuously output a sine wave through the resistor DAC, with the frequency
modulated by the voltage input to the Cissoid ADC. Software can be found in Appendix A.2. The
software did not interact with the memory component. Since the ADC received signal simply
changed the frequency output of the DAC, the communication speed was slower compared to more
complex modulation schemes such as OFDM and/or QAM. This simplified software can
technically transmit sensor data through the four-conductor wireline. For this experiment, a wireline
was not used since the line driver was not installed on the PCB.
After resolving initial issues—such as missing serial connections to the ADC and a non-functioning
crystal oscillator (temporarily replaced by a function generator)—the board operated as expected at
room temperature. The ADC wiring corrections included connecting Dout to GPIO2_10 and
SCLK to GPIO2_11. The crystal oscillator was observed not to generate any signal for the
microcontroller. Due to limited funding and time, the crystal oscillator issue was not resolved, and
elevated temperature testing proceeded using the function generator. It is possible the crystal was
damaged during assembly or the capacitors selected for the crystal were not appropriate for proper
operation.
It is also noteworthy that during ADC assembly, the lid of the CSOIC28 package detached, exposing
the die to the environment. While the ADC functioned at room temperature, its operation was
intermittent.

4.6. Results
The test setup for the data link is illustrated in Figure 33. A vacuum oven was used to heat the board
to temperatures approaching 300 °C. A function generator supplied the 4 MHz clock signal, while a
power supply energized the electronics. An oscilloscope monitored the modulated sine wave output,
enabling real-time verification of proper electronic function at elevated temperatures. To minimize
oxidation and extend the lifetime of the electronics during high-temperature testing, the oven
atmosphere was purged with argon gas. However, due to multiple leaks around the wire
feedthroughs at the oven door seal, argon gradually escaped over time, allowing some air ingress.
Following initial verification of board functionality with the basic software generating the modulated
output, the oven temperature was ramped to 100 °C and held for 24 hours to remove residual
moisture. The temperature then incrementally increased over several days to 295 °C. The
temperature was capped below the nominal 300 °C target to avoid surpassing the Au/Sn solder’s
liquidus temperature of 301 °C and to accommodate oven temperature variability.
During testing, the power supply was set to 4.75 V, with the current draw rising from approximately
40 mA at room temperature to about 50 mA at 295 °C. Throughout the test, the modulated output
signal remained stable, with no significant drift in amplitude or frequency observed. Surprisingly, the
ADC remained stable at the elevated temperature, suggesting a loose connection to the IC. Because
the microcontroller relies on volatile memory, any power interruption necessitated reprogramming
the board. The electronics operated continuously at 295 °C for over 1000 hours without failure,
demonstrating the capability of the packaging techniques and achieving the primary objective of this
study.

Figure 33: Data link circuit board testing at 295 C showing the alternating sine wave on the
oscilloscope at a voltage of 4.75 V drawing ~50mA.
After testing was completed, the board was removed from the oven, as shown in Figure 34. The
metal surface retained its gold finish but was slightly tarnished. However, inspection revealed that
the Au-Sn solder had wicked away from some capacitor pads, as seen in Figure 35 Figure 36.
Wicking was also observed around the memory pins. Notably, this effect was not seen around the
resistors, microcontroller pins, and 27 pF capacitors. During removal, one capacitor popped off the
board, indicating solder fatigue likely caused by the wicking phenomenon or coefficient of thermal
expansion (CTE) mismatch strain.

 41

Figure 34: Image of the data link after it has been tested at 295 °C for more than 1,000 hours.
To mitigate this issue, several approaches are proposed: replacing the Au-Sn solder with sintered
silver (which has slightly higher resistance), applying WC-16 ceramic epoxy for mechanical
reinforcement, or designing zones around capacitors with reduced metal fill to limit solder
migration. Notably, the relief traces around the capacitors acted as barriers, reducing solder wicking
across the metal surface. Extending these relief traces may slow the wicking rate and prolong solder
joint life, although this is considered a temporary solution.

Figure 35: Image of the Au-Sn solder wicking away from the capacitor through the relief trace and
across the metal fill.

 42

Figure 36: Image of minimal Au-Sn solder wicking away from the capacitor.
The successful continuous operation of the data link board at 295 °C for over 1000 hours validates
the effectiveness of the high-temperature packaging techniques employed. However, the observed
solder wicking and capacitor detachment underscore critical reliability challenges that must be
addressed to ensure long-term durability, especially under mechanical stresses such as vibration.
These findings emphasize the need for further refinement in assembly methods and protective
measures, such as conformal coatings and optimized solder joint designs, to enhance mechanical
robustness. Addressing these issues will be essential for advancing toward fully integrated, reliable
high-temperature electronics suitable for geothermal well deployment.

5. CONCLUSION
This work demonstrates the successful development and testing of a high-temperature embedded
electronic system capable of sustained operation at temperatures approaching 300 °C, marking a
major milestone for geothermal well instrumentation. By integrating advanced packaging techniques,
including ceramic PCBs, gold-tin soldering, wire bonding, and high-temperature conformal
coatings—this effort addresses key barriers to deploying commercially available high-temperature
integrated circuits in harsh geothermal environments.
While the prototype board was not fully populated with all components nor equipped with software
to demonstrate full data link functionality, it successfully operated with basic software for sensor
data acquisition and signal modulation, validating the feasibility of the packaging approach under
elevated temperatures.
During extended testing, solder wicking away from capacitor pads revealed critical reliability
challenges, particularly under vibration and thermal stress typical of downhole conditions. These
findings underscore the need for further refinement in assembly methods and protective measures
such as conformal coatings, sintered silver die attach, and optimized PCB trace designs to enhance
mechanical robustness and long-term durability.
An additional challenge impacting the development of high-temperature geothermal electronics is
the loss of the X-Fab high-temperature silicon-on-insulator (SOI) process, which has limited the
availability of multiple high-temperature ICs. This constraint highlights the critical need for future
research and development of new high-temperature integrated circuits. In particular, silicon carbide
(SiC) technology [11,12], with its inherently higher operating temperature range and robustness, is a
promising candidate to support not only geothermal applications but also a broader range of high-
temperature electronics. Advancements in SiC ICs could significantly expand the market and
capabilities of high-temperature electronics technology.
Overall, this work represents a significant advancement toward reliable, long-duration electronics for
enhanced geothermal systems and closed-loop geothermal technologies, supporting improved well
characterization, fracture detection, and operational efficiency in increasingly demanding subsurface
environments. Moreover, the packaging approaches and lessons learned have potential relevance for
other industries requiring electronics capable of withstanding extreme environments.

5.1. Future Efforts
Future work will focus on completing the PCB assembly by incorporating die bonding and wire
bonding, followed by the application of conformal coatings to improve mechanical robustness.
Comprehensive vibration testing at elevated temperatures will evaluate the mechanical integrity of
gold-tin solder joints and the effectiveness of conformal coatings in mitigating mechanical stresses.
These tests will simulate the combined thermal and mechanical stress encountered in geothermal
downhole environments, providing critical data on long-term reliability.
Thermal cycling experiments will be conducted to assess the effects of repeated heating and cooling
cycles on strain accumulation, solder joint fatigue, and overall system durability. Understanding these
effects is essential to ensure electronics can withstand the rigors of real-world geothermal
deployment over extended periods.
Software development will be prioritized to enable full data link functionality, including
implementation of advanced modulation schemes such as orthogonal frequency-division
multiplexing (OFDM) and quadrature amplitude modulation (QAM). Integration of all components

 44

into a cohesive system will be pursued to demonstrate reliable, high-speed data transmission under
harsh conditions.
Additionally, ongoing research will explore alternative materials and packaging methods to further
enhance thermal and mechanical resilience. This includes investigating novel die attach materials,
advanced solder alloys, and improved metallization stacks.
Finally, recognizing the limitations imposed by current IC availability, future efforts will emphasize
collaboration with semiconductor manufacturers and research institutions to accelerate the
development of silicon carbide (SiC) based integrated circuits. SiC technology promises higher
temperature operation, improved radiation hardness, and greater robustness, which are critical for
expanding the capabilities and applications of high-temperature electronics beyond geothermal
systems.

/* masks for the 12 DAC bits on GPIO1 */
#define DAC_MASK 0x0FFFu // bits 0..11 on GPIO1

void DAC_Init(void)
{
  /* enable AHB clock for GPIO1 (bit15) only */
  RC_SYSCON->AHBCLKCTRL |= (1u << 15);

 /* configure P1[11:0] as outputs */
    RC_GPIO1->DIR |= DAC_MASK;
}

void DAC_Write(uint16_t value)
{
  uint32_t tmp;

    /* port 1: update bits 0..11 */
 tmp = RC_GPIO1->DATA;
    tmp &= ~DAC_MASK;               // clear P1[11:0]
    tmp |= (value & DAC_MASK); // set them to value bits 0..11
 RC_GPIO1->DATA = tmp;
}

#define ADC_CS_MASK (1u << 5) // GPIO3_5 - ADC chip select (active low)
#define ADC_RnC_MASK (1u << 4) // GPIO3_4 - R_nc (output)
#define ADC_SCLK_MASK (1u << 11) // GPIO2_11 - SCLK (output)
#define ADC_DOUT_MASK (1u << 10) // GPIO2_10 - DOUT (input)
#define ADC_nEOC_MASK (1u << 10) // GPIO0_10 - nEOC (input)

/*
 * tiny delay loop – calibrate 'loops' until
 * delay_short(2) ~ 1 µs if CPU_FREQ ~ 3.6864 MHz
 */
static void delay_short(int loops)
{
 while (loops--) {
 __NOP();
 }
}

  RC_SYSCON->AHBCLKCTRL |= (1u << 14) | (1u << 16) | (1u << 17);

  RC_GPIO2->DIR |= ADC_SCLK_MASK;       // SCLK output (GPIO2_11)

  RC_GPIO0->DIR &= ~ADC_nEOC_MASK; // nEOC input (GPIO0_10)

uint16_t CHT_ADC_Read(void)
{
 uint16_t result = 0;
 int i;

 // idle settle
 delay_short(2);

 // trigger conversion: R_nc low->high->low (GPIO3_4)
 RC_GPIO3->MASKED_ACCESS[ADC_RnC_MASK] = ADC_RnC_MASK;
 delay_short(2);
 RC_GPIO3->MASKED_ACCESS[ADC_RnC_MASK] = 0;

 // wait for nEOC to go LOW (GPIO0_10)
 while (RC_GPIO0->MASKED_ACCESS[ADC_nEOC_MASK] != 0) {
 ; // spin
 }
 delay_short(2);

 // clock out 10 bits (MSB first), sample on falling edge (GPIO2.11, GPIO2.10)
    for (i = 0; i < 10; ++i) {
 RC_GPIO2->MASKED_ACCESS[ADC_SCLK_MASK] = ADC_SCLK_MASK;
 delay_short(4);

 RC_GPIO2->MASKED_ACCESS[ADC_SCLK_MASK] = 0;
 delay_short(2);

        result = (uint16_t)((result << 1) |
               ((RC_GPIO2->DATA & ADC_DOUT_MASK) ? 1u : 0u));
 }
