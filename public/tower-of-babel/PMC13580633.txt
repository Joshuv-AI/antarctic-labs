An inexplicable benefit—musical self-expression in a hospital based Day Treatment Program
Authors: Shreya Jha, Julie Birrell, Karen Leslie
Published: 2026

A degree in music composition is perhaps not the most common undergraduate concentration undertaken before medical school. Yet after my music composition specialist (alongside a neuroscience major) at the University of Toronto, I pursued medicine. I wanted to specialize in paediatrics and was particularly drawn to brain, behaviour, and mental health.

My composition degree has proven useful for medicine in many ways, as I recite to people that (understandably) react to my educational path with shock. Coordinating an orchestra to premiere a new musical is not unlike collaborating within an interprofessional team. The flow state and sheer determination it takes to finish a composition with a pressing deadline reflect the mental demands of a busy clinical teaching unit or rigorous academic paper. Yet I also had a feeling that the composition degree would be meaningful to my medical career in other, more concrete ways; musical self-expression has an ability to modulate mental health, well-being, and perhaps even biological and neural responses in ways we have not yet fully discovered.

I saw the positive impact of musical self-expression in my own life, noticing how pouring my emotions into the piano, and later my compositions, provided a unique comfort. I then saw this impact in young people when I taught at Sistema Toronto, an organization that gives free music lessons to underprivileged youth. Providing children the tools to express creativity through music allowed them to blossom in the rest of their life. Hence, when the opportunity came to implement a clinical project during my fourth year of medical school, I jumped at the possibility of bringing compositional teaching to the Hospital for Sick Children (SickKids).

The Substance Use Day Treatment program at SickKids provides concurrent treatment, support, and education for young people engaged in problematic substance use. Youth attend programming Monday to Friday from 09:00 to 15:00 and receive the opportunity to work toward secondary school credits with a Toronto District School Board teacher while undergoing treatment. With a team made up of myself (at the time, a final year medical student), an adolescent medicine physician, a classroom teacher, music therapists, and child and youth counselors, we brought a songwriting curriculum previously used in an afterschool classroom at Sistema Toronto to the substance use classroom at SickKids.

Bringing an educational curriculum from a larger class of school-aged children at Sistema to a smaller class of adolescents allowed us to design it to be maximally flexible, person-centered, and open to meeting the students where they were—the very values of the Day Treatment program. Students began by looking to their favorite songs for inspiration, then chose topics for their own compositions. They completed numerous exercises to generate lyrics and melodies. With my compositional guidance, we created instrumental tracks and vocal guides that the music therapists used to record the final songs for the students to keep. Students were given agency over their learning at every stage—when one expressed the desire to explore an audio interface to edit their own song's instrumental before creating lyrics, their interest was accommodated.

The youth in the Day Treatment classroom, like any youth engaged in longitudinal treatment, have the unique experience of being patients while progressing through adolescent milestones and emotions. We observed key elements of the youth's clinical and non-clinical experiences through their self-expression—they discussed friendships, relationship challenges, and the challenges of growing up in and out of the context of their treatment. Through the medium of music, they tackled challenging, vulnerable topics with maturity and nuance. One student stated “The theme of my song focused on a deep friendship—one filled with love and connection—but also the struggles that can arise over time.”

The program was positively received—in our end-of-curriculum debrief, the child and youth counselors reported high levels of student engagement in overall treatment and ownership and initiative over their songs. The project also attracted attention from other groups at SickKids interested in similar intersections, including the psychiatry team at the Centre for Community Mental Health and the PeriOp Culture Change Committee.

We determined that optimal success and positive impact was brought about by students having a meaningful connection with all staff members, as well as the entire interdisciplinary team being involved by participating in the creative process. Our classroom teacher wrote a song alongside the students—directly modeling and encouraging self-expression. We discussed that a future iteration should implement more robust scales to measure clinical benefits, aiming to more specifically and definitively describe the impact of musical expression on youth. Possibilities include assessment of mental health, emotional regulation, or measures of engagement in treatment.

While each of these future avenues are interesting, valid, and thought-provoking research paths, the two most memorable components of the project were not related to the scholarly goals or outcomes.

We recorded the songs in SickKids' Marnie's Studio on a sunny March morning immediately after March break. There was a weariness in the air with the persistent cold weather and with the days of hard work and logistics leading up to recording. Yet when the music therapist sang one of our students' songs for the first time, that weariness gave way to a palpable excitement—and seeing the look on our young composer's face was not to be forgotten.

The other standout memory is a student who at first glance the curriculum perhaps did not serve as well. They deal with perfectionism and found the creation of a final product overwhelming. Ultimately, the student did not attend the recording session. I wondered if the program had failed them—or if I had. Yet, in their end-of-year meeting with our teacher, they described the program as “confidence-building” and “a way to express themselves” and appreciated the creative freedom of the curriculum. One other student developed a deep interest in the interplay of lyricism and musicianship—some of the more technical aspects of creating a song. They ultimately stated that they “expressed what they set out to convey, which in itself is an accomplishment.”

Their reflections served as a reminder that the process of music-making is as important a focus for this project as the product of improved clinical or treatment outcomes, or even the product of completed songs. Music educators often discuss process vs product frameworks, suggesting that an emphasis on the learning and creative process is more beneficial for a student's development than an emphasis on generating a product.

Similarly, the substance use day treatment program goes beyond medical management of addiction and mental health to reframing these youth's self-concept and build healthier habits. Hence, the opportunity to build confidence and creativity as a composer transcends beyond building a novel skill to building a new self-identity with interests and habits that may mitigate the need to use substances. In this way, creative self-expression can be one of the small building blocks that help maintain the path to harm reduction.

As excited as we were about the songs themselves and measurable, specific clinical outcomes, perhaps some of the true benefits lie in the connection and safety fostered through the process of curriculum implementation and teaching self-expression. And perhaps ultimately, some of the impact of music-making cannot be fully explained, just like the magic in the room on our recording day.

Through our program, we observed students having an outlet for creative self-expression, confidence building, interest in the arts, and meaningful connections and trust with their program staff. I look forward to building on this program with validated scales and clinical tools that uncover the health benefits of self-expression for young people. I look forward to putting statistics and evidence behind the phenomenon that has driven my artistry throughout my life. Yet I will equally remember the pure excitement in my own heart as I eagerly edited students' tracks prior to recording, swept up in the experience of music-making myself beyond my role as the medical student facilitator.

The phrase “art of medicine” is used to describe the unique ways we connect with patients, sometimes having to think outside the box. Using art itself to connect to patients could be considered one such method, but when we think about the universality of music—it does not seem so unorthodox at all.

Regardless of what concrete outcomes we can achieve with music as treatment in the future, the true art of paediatrics as it pertains to our curriculum lies in the shared moments and learning between the students and staff in the day treatment program—showcasing the very heart of where they have been, where they are going, and ultimately who they are.
