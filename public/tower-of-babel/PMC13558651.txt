Cross-sectional and prospective associations between multidimensional psychological distress and urogenital disorders: findings from the UK biobank
Authors: Hao Liu, Lanbing Liu, Zhiyi Zhao, Xue Liu, Yin Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPsychological distress is a major public health issue with multisystemic effects, yet its multidimensional nature and specific links to urogenital disorders remain inadequately characterized due to methodological limitations of prior studies. This large-scale study utilized data from the UK Biobank (N = 501,988) to investigate both cross-sectional and prospective associations between six validated psychometric instruments including emotional distress (MHS-20), neuroticism (N-12), somatic symptoms (RDS-4), depression (PHQ-9), and anxiety (GAD-7) and eight non-infectious urogenital conditions. Multivariable-adjusted logistic and Cox proportional hazards models were employed, controlling for demographic, socioeconomic, lifestyle, and clinical confounders. Multiplicative interaction analyses were introduced to evaluate effect modification by age, sex, and menopausal status. Higher scores across all psychological scales were associated with increased incidence of urogenital diseases, with the strongest associations observed for RDS-4 with urgency urinary incontinence (HR = 1.48, 95% CI: 1.39–1.58), IC/BPS (HR = 1.49, 95% CI: 1.25–1.78), and pelvic pain (HR = 1.49, 95% CI: 1.35–1.64). Interaction analyses indicated significant effect modifications by age, sex, and menopausal status for specific bladder and pelvic floor disorders. For sex-specific patterns, females exhibited stronger associations for IC/BPS and other bladder disorders, whereas pelvic floor dysfunction showed stronger associations in males. Associations for pelvic myalgia remained consistent across sexes, indicating no significant effect modification. Chronic prostatitis showed limited to no prospective associations with psychological distress. Multidimensional psychological distress represents a risk factor for urogenital disorders, with notable demographic variations. Introduction

Mental health disorders have emerged as a leading cause of global disability, with their burden extending far beyond emotional distress to exert profound multisystemic physiological effects. Epidemiological evidence indicates a substantial and continuing increase in the burden of depression and anxiety worldwide, highlighting their growing relevance for chronic disease research1. According to the Global Burden of Disease Study, mental and substance use disorders collectively represent the foremost contributor to disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) worldwide, with their relative impact growing as the burden of communicable diseases declines2–4. Increasing attention has therefore been directed toward understanding how psychological distress interacts with somatic health through integrated biological pathways.

Emerging evidence underscores the existence of interconnected “mind–body” axes, wherein psychological states influence cardiovascular, metabolic, and inflammatory processes through shared pathophysiological mechanisms. For instance, dysregulation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis and chronic low-grade inflammation have been linked to metabolic dysfunction, vascular disease, and altered immune responses5,6. These interconnected pathways suggest that psychological distress may co-occur with, or contribute to, a range of chronic somatic conditions, although the directionality and underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood.

In the field of urology, growing evidence highlights associations between psychological factors and conditions such as bladder pain syndrome, chronic prostatitis7, urgency urinary incontinence8, and urinary tract infections9. Cohort studies have further reinforced these associations, demonstrating that psychological distress is linked not only to symptom severity and quality of life impairment, but also to disease persistence and healthcare utilization patterns, with consistent patterns observed even in younger populations, including primary school–aged children10. These disorders are characterized by chronicity, symptom heterogeneity, high recurrence rates, and substantial impairment in quality of life11. Clinically, management strategies have increasingly incorporated psychosocial interventions. For example, cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) has been applied in patients with chronic pelvic pain and interstitial cystitis/bladder pain syndrome (IC/BPS), demonstrating improvements in pain perception, coping strategies, and health-related quality of life12. However, compared with conventional medical treatments such as pharmacotherapy and pelvic floor therapy, the effectiveness of these approaches remains variable, and patient selection criteria are not well defined13. This variability underscores the need for a more refined understanding of which dimensions of psychological distress are most relevant to specific urogenital conditions.

Despite these advances, several important limitations persist in the current literature. First, prior studies have often relied on single-domain measures such as depression and anxiety alone or ICD diagnostic codes or brief screening tools, which do not adequately capture the multidimensional structure of psychological distress. Although emerging research has emphasized that distinct psychological constructs such as neuroticism14, somatic stress symptoms, and emotional dysregulation may exhibit differential associations with physical health outcomes15, these dimensions are rarely examined simultaneously within a unified analytical framework, limiting the ability to disentangle their independent and overlapping associations with physical health outcomes. Furthermore, much of the existing evidence is derived from cross-sectional or small-scale clinical studies, which constrains generalizability and limits the ability to assess temporal relationships between psychological distress and urogenital disorders. As a result, it remains unclear whether these associations reflect antecedent risk markers, concurrent correlates, or downstream consequences of disease processes. Third, substantial selection bias may be present, with clinical studies predominantly focusing on severe cases, thereby underrepresenting individuals with milder or subclinical psychological distress. This gap restricts our understanding of how psychological factors operate across the full spectrum of symptom severity in community-based populations.

Accordingly, the primary objective of this study was to systematically examine the associations between multiple dimensions of psychological distress (MHS-20, N-12, RDS-4, PHQ-4, PHQ-9, GAD-7) and a spectrum of non-infectious urogenital disorders in a large population-based cohort. We further aimed to evaluate whether these associations vary by age and sex. We hypothesized that higher levels of psychological distress across multiple domains would be associated with an increased risk of urogenital disorders, and different psychometric scales capturing distinct dimensions of psychological distress would demonstrate varying effect sizes and association patterns across urological outcomes.

Methods

Study design and population

The entire analysis was conducted using data from the UK Biobank (UKBB), a large-scale prospective cohort study that recruited over 500,000 participants aged 37–73 years between 2006 and 2010 across the United Kingdom16. Participants were identified through National Health Service (NHS) registers and invited via postal mail. The study protocol was approved by the North West Multi-Centre Research Ethics Committee (reference: 21/NW/0157), and written informed consent was provided by each participant prior to enrollment15. This cohort is ongoing, with longitudinal follow-up through linkage to national health records.

Baseline assessments were conducted at 22 assessment centers and included touchscreen questionnaires, nurse-led interviews, physical measurements, and biological sample collection. Follow-up data were obtained through linkage to hospital inpatient records, death registries, and other health databases. In the present study, follow-up was available until December 31, 2022. Both cross-sectional and prospective cohort analyses were performed. For prospective analyses, participants with pre-existing urogenital diseases at baseline (diagnosed prior to recruitment) were excluded, with outcome-specific exclusion counts detailed in Supplementary Table 1.

Psychological distress assessment and cross-scale comparison

Psychological distress was assessed using six validated instruments to capture its multidimensional nature, including the Mental Health Scale-20 (MHS-20), which is a comprehensive 20-item tool designed to evaluate global mental health across emotional, cognitive, and social functional domains17; the Eysenck Neuroticism-12 (N-12) scale, which measures stable traits of neuroticism18; the Rare Distress Symptom-4 (RDS-4), a 4-item instrument focusing on somatic symptoms associated with psychological stress19; the Patient Health Questionnaire-4 (PHQ-4), an ultra-brief screener for core symptoms of depression and anxiety20; the 9-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9), which quantifies the severity of depressive symptoms21; and the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) scale, assessing the severity of anxiety-related symptoms22. All scale scores were analyzed as continuous variables in regression models to preserve statistical power and capture the full spectrum of symptom severity, consistent with the analytical approach implemented in the code.

These instruments capture psychological constructs over different temporal frameworks. Specifically, PHQ-9, GAD-7, PHQ-4, and RDS-4 assess symptoms experienced over the preceding two weeks, with RDS-4 further operationalized using frequency-based responses reflecting recent distress. In contrast, N-12 measures a stable neuroticism trait without a defined recall period, while MHS-20 reflects a broader multidimensional mental health construct not restricted to a specific short-term timeframe.

Each scale employs distinct scoring frameworks based on Likert-type responses. The N-12 items are scored binarily (yes = 1, no = 0), with “do not know” and “prefer not to answer” treated as missing. The RDS-4 uses a 4-point frequency scale scored 0, 0.33, 0.67, and 1, reflecting symptom persistence over the previous two weeks. Both the PHQ-4 and PHQ-9 utilize a 4-point Likert system (0–3) for each item, with total scores categorizing severity. Similarly, the GAD-7 employs a 4-point frequency scale (0–3 per item) with a total score range of 0–21, where higher scores indicate greater anxiety severity. The MHS-20 incorporates a combination of binary and multi-level responses, with certain items reverse-scored as appropriate to compute a global mental health composite. All instruments were administered according to UKBB protocol with detailed scoring algorithms and corresponding field IDs summarized in Supplementary Table 2.

Urogenital disorders

The primary outcomes of this study were incident diagnoses of eight non-infectious, non-malignant urogenital disorders, defined using ICD-10 codes from hospital inpatient records (UKBB field ID 41270). Specifically, these outcomes included IC/BPS, pelvic floor dysfunction (PFD), pelvic myalgia, hypoactive sexual desire disorder (F52.0, F52.4, F52.8–F52.9), benign prostatic hyperplasia (N40), other bladder disorders (N32.8), chronic prostatitis (N41.1), and organic erectile dysfunction (N48.4). For each participant, the earliest recorded diagnosis date was identified and used to define incident events. Individuals with diagnoses occurring prior to baseline were classified as prevalent cases and excluded from prospective analyses according to the predefined survival classification procedure.

Covariates

We incorporated covariates including fundamental demographic characteristics like age, sex, ethnicity (categorized as White, Asian, Black, Mixed, Chinese, or Other). Socioeconomic position was operationalized through two principal indicators: highest attained educational qualification and total annual household income. Lifestyle factors comprised smoking status, alcohol consumption, sleep duration, and insomnia. Clinical covariates incorporated waist circumference, body mass index (BMI) modeled both as a continuous variable and as a categorical measure (underweight, normal weight, overweight, obesity), in addition to medically documented histories of diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and malignant neoplasms. In addition, medication use variables, including diuretics and alpha-blockers, were incorporated based on the Field ID 20,003. For sensitivity analyses restricted to female participants, additional reproductive factors were further adjusted for, including menopause status, number of births, age at first birth, and age at menarche. All covariates were measured at baseline. The covariates and corresponding Field ids are shown in Supplementary Table 3.

Cross-scale psychological assessment comparison

To further characterize the relationships among psychological distress measures and to evaluate their potential overlap and incremental predictive value, a series of analyses were conducted. First, pairwise correlations between all six psychological scales were assessed using Spearman correlation coefficients. Correlation matrices were visualized using the corrplot package (v 0.95). In addition, principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on standardized scale scores. The proportion of variance explained by each principal component was evaluated using scree plots, and variable contributions were visualized to assess the relative influence of each scale on principal components.

To further quantify the independent and incremental predictive value of each psychological scale, logistic regression models were constructed for each urogenital outcome. A baseline model including covariates only was first established, followed by models sequentially adding each psychological scale individually, as well as a full model including all six scales simultaneously. Model discrimination was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), with 95% confidence intervals estimated. Differences in AUC between models were evaluated using DeLong’s test. Additionally, likelihood ratio tests (LRTs) were conducted to compare nested models and assess the incremental contribution of each scale beyond baseline covariates.

Specifically, to investigate the unique and incremental prognostic contribution of the RDS-4, as a measure of non-specific general psychological distress, to determine whether it captures a fundamental core of distress that predicts urological risk independently of syndrome-specific depression (PHQ-9) and anxiety (GAD-7) assessments, we constructed joint Cox proportional hazards models. In these joint models, the RDS-4 was included simultaneously with the PHQ-9 and GAD-7 scales, while adjusting for the aforementioned baseline covariates. Subsequent LRTs were conducted to quantitatively confirm whether the inclusion of the RDS-4 scale significantly improved the overall model fit compared to baseline models containing only the depression and anxiety scales alongside covariates. All analyses were performed using complete-case datasets, consistent with the primary analytical pipeline. The detailed missingness rates for all core exposures and covariates is summarized in Supplementary Table 4.

Stratified and interaction analyses

To investigate potential effect modification by key demographic and biological factors, stratified analyses were conducted within the Cox proportional hazards framework for the associations between psychological distress measures and urogenital outcomes. Stratification was performed by age (< 65 vs. ≥65 years) and sex, and, in female-specific analyses, additionally by menopausal status.

To comprehensively assess interaction effects, multiplicative interaction terms between psychological distress scores and stratification variables were introduced into fully adjusted Cox models. Statistical significance of interactions was evaluated using likelihood ratio tests comparing models with and without interaction terms.

Statistical analysis

Cross-sectional associations were evaluated using multivariable logistic regression models, with odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) reported. Prospective associations were examined using Cox proportional hazards models, with follow-up time calculated from the recruitment date to the date of outcome occurrence, death, or the end of follow-up (December 31, 2022), whichever came first.

All analyses were fully adjusted for age, sex, ethnicity, BMI, waist circumference, educational attainment, household income, smoking status, alcohol consumption, sleep duration, insomnia, hypertension, diabetes, cancer history, and medication use including diuretics and alpha-blockers. Female-specific sensitivity analyses were further conducted with additional adjustment for reproductive factors, including menopause status, parity, and age at first birth. To account for multiple testing, false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.4.2, with a two-tailed alpha level of 0.05 denoting statistical significance.

Multiple imputation for missing data

To assess the robustness of our prospective findings to missing data, we performed outcome-specific multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) as a sensitivity analysis. The imputation model included all six psychological scales, all covariates from the fully adjusted Cox models. Continuous variables were imputed using predictive mean matching; binary, categorical, and ordinal variables were imputed using logistic regression, multinomial logistic regression, and proportional odds models, respectively. We generated 50 imputed datasets with 5 iterations each. Cox proportional hazards models were fitted on each imputed dataset, and estimates were pooled using Rubin’s rules. Complete-case estimates were compared with pooled MICE estimates to evaluate the potential impact of missing data on effect estimates.

Results

Baseline population characteristics

The cohort comprised 501,988 participants, including 66,905 (15.0%) individuals diagnosed with at least one urogenital disorder (Table 1). Compared with participants without urogenital diseases, those with urogenital conditions were older (59.67 ± 7.23 vs. 56.05 ± 8.11 years, p < 0.001) and more likely to be male (52.3% vs. 44.6%, p < 0.001). Across all six psychological scales, median scores were modestly but consistently higher in the urogenital disease group. Participants with urogenital diseases also exhibited a higher prevalence of metabolic and clinical comorbidities, including diabetes (7.5% vs. 4.9%, p < 0.001), cancer (9.9% vs. 7.4%, p < 0.001), and hypertension (49.1% vs. 45.0%, p < 0.001). They were more likely to be overweight or obese (73.0% vs. 66.1%, p < 0.001), current or former smokers (49.4% vs. 44.6%, p < 0.001), and users of diuretics (6.2% vs. 4.2%, p < 0.001) and alpha-blockers (7.0% vs. 1.7%, p < 0.001).

Cross-scale correlation and principal component analysis

Pairwise Spearman correlation analysis revealed significant and moderate-to-strong positive associations among all six psychological distress scales (Fig. 1A). The most striking correlation was observed between the MHS_20 and N_12 scales (r = 0.96). PHQ_9 and GAD_7 also exhibited a robust correlation (r = 0.68), while other inter-scale coefficients ranged from 0.40 to 0.66. To further consolidate these findings and address potential multicollinearity, we performed PCA on the standardized scores. The scree plot demonstrated that the first principal component (Dim1) alone accounted for 60.3% of the total variance, with a sharp decline in explanatory power for subsequent dimensions (Dim2: 16.1%; Dim3: 9.6%) (Fig. 1B). Visualization of variable contributions further clarified the relationships between scales (Fig. 1C). All six scales loaded heavily and point in a similar direction along the Dim1 axis, confirming that they capture a largely unified underlying construct of psychological distress. Notably, MHS_20 and N_12 clustered closely together, as did PHQ_9, GAD_7, and PHQ_4.

(A) Pairwise Spearman correlation of six psychological distress scales; (B) Scree plot of explained variance by principal components; (C) Loading plot of scale contributions to the first two principal components.

Psychological distress scores and the prevalence of urogenital disorders: the cross-sectional study

The distributions of all six psychological distress scores were illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 1. Multivariable logistic regression analyses with p-values adjusted for multiple comparisons using the FDR method, revealed significant positive associations between higher scores on most psychological scales and increased odds of various urogenital disorders (Fig. 2, Supplementary Fig. 2A). Among the psychological measures, the RDS-4 score exhibited the highest odds ratios across urogenital outcomes. Specifically, RDS-4 showed significant associations with pelvic myalgia (OR = 1.37, 95% CI: 1.31–1.44, FDR p < 0.001) and IC/BPS (OR = 1.30, 95% CI: 1.25–1.36, FDR p < 0.001). Elevated scores on the PHQ-9, GAD-7, and MHS-20 were also consistently associated with multiple urogenital disorders. For example, hypoactive sexual desire showed significant positive associations with GAD-7 (OR = 1.07, 95% CI: 1.03–1.11, FDR p < 0.01), MHS-20 (OR = 1.03, 95% CI: 1.01–1.06, FDR p < 0.01), and PHQ-9 (OR = 1.09, 95% CI: 1.06–1.13, FDR p < 0.001). Organic erectile dysfunction was associated with GAD-7 (OR = 1.11, 95% CI: 1.07–1.16, FDR p < 0.001), MHS-20 (OR = 1.04, 95% CI: 1.02–1.07, FDR p < 0.01), and PHQ-9 (OR = 1.09, 95% CI: 1.05–1.13, FDR p < 0.001). Pelvic floor dysfunction and benign prostatic hyperplasia similarly demonstrated significant positive associations with PHQ-9, GAD-7, and MHS-20 (all FDR p < 0.001). Conversely, chronic prostatitis showed non-significant associations with most psychological scores. A significant association was observed only for MHS-20 (OR = 1.02, 95% CI: 1.00-1.03, FDR p < 0.05). All other psychological scales, including N_12, PHQ-4, PHQ-9, GAD-7, and RDS-4, showed non-significant associations with chronic prostatitis (all FDR p > 0.05).

To further assess the independent and incremental predictive value of the psychological scales, we evaluated the change in model discrimination upon their addition to baseline clinical models (Supplementary Fig. 3). The baseline models, comprising only traditional clinical covariates, demonstrated AUC values ranging from approximately 0.60 for IC/BPS to 0.89 for benign prostatic hyperplasia. Sequentially adding individual psychological scales (MHS-20, N-12, RDS-4, PHQ-4, PHQ-9, and GAD-7) yielded modest but consistent improvements in the absolute predictive performance across most urogenital outcomes. Additionally, the full model simultaneously incorporating all six psychological scales generally maximized the discriminative ability. For instance, in predicting pelvic floor dysfunction, the addition of all mental scales increased the baseline AUC from 0.774 to 0.779 (∆AUC = 0.0049; DeLong’s test p < 0.001). While the 95% confidence intervals of the absolute AUCs frequently overlapped between nested models, LRTs confirmed that the inclusion of these psychological variables provided a statistically significant improvement in overall model fit (LRT p < 0.001 for the full models).

Psychological distress scores and the prevalence of urogenital disorders: the cohort study

Cox proportional hazards regression models were employed to investigate longitudinal associations between baseline psychological distress scores and incident urogenital disorders. After adjusting for age, sex, socioeconomic, lifestyle, and clinical confounders (Table 2; Fig. 3, Supplementary Fig. 2B), the associations remained statistically significant for the majority of outcomes. Consistent with the cross-sectional findings, the RDS-4 scale exhibited the highest hazard ratios across multiple incident urogenital conditions. Notably, elevated RDS-4 scores were strongly associated with an increased risk of pelvic myalgia (HR = 1.33, 95% CI: 1.23–1.44, FDR p < 0.001), IC/BPS (HR = 1.25, 95% CI: 1.19–1.32, FDR p < 0.001), and other bladder disorders (HR = 1.22, 95% CI: 1.18–1.27, FDR p < 0.001). Similarly, significant risks were observed for organic erectile dysfunction (HR = 1.24, 95% CI: 1.04–1.48, FDR p = 0.022) and pelvic floor dysfunction (HR = 1.17, 95% CI: 1.14–1.21, FDR p < 0.001). Elevated scores on the PHQ-9, GAD-7, and MHS-20 were consistently associated with incident urogenital disorders in the fully adjusted models. For example, the incidence of benign prostatic hyperplasia was significantly associated with PHQ-9 (HR = 1.03, 95% CI: 1.02–1.04, FDR p < 0.001), GAD-7 (HR = 1.02, 95% CI: 1.02–1.03, FDR p < 0.001), and MHS-20 (HR = 1.03, 95% CI: 1.02–1.03, FDR p < 0.001). Similarly, significant positive associations were observed for hypoactive sexual desire with PHQ-9 (HR = 1.09, 95% CI: 1.05–1.13, FDR p < 0.001) and GAD-7 (HR = 1.07, 95% CI: 1.02–1.12, FDR p = 0.006). Notably, chronic prostatitis showed minimal to no associations with psychological distress. All psychological scales, including MHS-20, N-12, RDS-4, PHQ-4, PHQ-9, and GAD-7, showed non-significant associations with incident chronic prostatitis (all FDR p > 0.05).

Independent predictive value of general psychological distress

In joint model analyses incorporating general psychological distress (RDS-4) alongside specific symptoms of depression (PHQ-9) and anxiety (GAD-7), RDS-4 maintained an independent association with the risk of other bladder disorders (HR = 1.09, 95% CI: 1.01–1.18, p = 0.024), pelvic myalgia (HR = 1.21, 95% CI: 1.02–1.42, p = 0.024), and benign prostatic hyperplasia (HR = 1.07, 95% CI: 1.01–1.13, p = 0.017) (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Furthermore, LRT confirmed that the addition of the RDS-4 scale to baseline models already containing depression and anxiety scales provided a statistically significant improvement in overall model fit across other bladder disorders (LRT p = 0.026), pelvic myalgia (LRT p = 0.028), and benign prostatic hyperplasia (LRT p = 0.018) (Supplementary Fig. 4). However, this independent predictive value of RDS-4 was not observed across other five diseases.

Sensitivity analysis in the female cohort

Female-specific sensitivity analyses were further conducted with additional adjustment for reproductive factors, including menopause status, parity, and age at first birth. In the cross-sectional analyses using multivariable logistic regression, elevated scores across all six psychological scales remained significantly associated with higher odds of urogenital conditions (Supplementary Fig. 5A, Supplementary Table 5). For instance, the RDS-4 score demonstrated particularly strong associations with hypoactive sexual desire (OR = 2.03, 95% CI: 1.14–3.59, FDR p = 0.018) and IC/BPS (OR = 1.35, 95% CI: 1.28–1.44, FDR p < 0.001). In the longitudinal Cox analysis, these psychological distress measures consistently predicted an increased risk of incident urogenital disorders (Supplementary Fig. 5B, Supplementary Table 6). Elevated RDS-4 scores were highly predictive of incident IC/BPS (HR = 1.32, 95% CI: 1.22–1.43, FDR p < 0.001) and other bladder disorders (HR = 1.31, 95% CI: 1.23–1.40, FDR p < 0.001).

Age, sex, and menopause-stratified analyses

Age-stratified analyses (< 65 vs. > 65 years) with formal interaction tests were performed to investigate potential effect modification by age (Fig. 4A). For other bladder disorders, age-stratified analyses revealed that the associations with psychological distress were more pronounced in the younger age group. Formal interaction tests indicated evidence of age significantly modified these associations across multiple psychological scales, specifically for MHS-20 (P_interaction < 0.001), N-12 (P_interaction = 0.0014), RDS-4 (P_interaction = 0.0166), and GAD-7 (P_interaction = 0.0063). Similarly, for IC/BPS, the stronger associations were also observed in the younger group (< 65 years), with age significantly modifying the effects for PHQ-4 (P_interaction = 0.0072) and GAD-7 (P_interaction = 0.0248).

To explore the potential modifying effect of reproductive aging in the female cohort, we conducted menopause-stratified analyses (pre/peri-menopausal vs. post-menopausal) for female-relevant urogenital disorders (Supplementary Fig. 6). For the robust associations identified between RDS-4 and conditions such as IC/BPS and pelvic myalgia, the effect sizes remained highly consistent across menopausal strata, with no significant interaction observed (all P_interaction > 0.05). Interestingly, significant interactions were observed for pelvic floor dysfunction, specifically for MHS-20 (P_interaction < 0.001), N-12 (P_interaction = 0.0014), N-12 (P_interaction = 0.0044), and RDS-4 (P_interaction = 0.0031), with the deleterious effects of these psychological factors were more pronounced among pre/peri-menopausal women compared to their post-menopausal counterparts.

Sex-stratified analyses were performed to investigate potential effect modification by sex (Fig. 4B). For other bladder disorders, sex significantly modified the associations across all six psychological scales (all P_interaction < 0.001), with females exhibiting consistently stronger associations compared to males (e.g., for RDS-4: females HR = 1.28, 95% CI: 1.21–1.35, p < 0.001; males HR = 1.17, 95% CI: 1.11–1.23, p < 0.001). Furthermore, IC/BPS showed evidence of sex-related effect modification for RDS-4, N-12, and MHS-20 (all P_interaction < 0.001), with more pronounced risk in females. Conversely, for pelvic floor dysfunction, these risks were markedly stronger in males, as evidenced by the interaction tests for MHS-20 (P_interaction = 0.0193), RDS-4 (P_interaction = 0.0070), PHQ-4 (P_interaction = 0.0102), and PHQ-9 (P_interaction = 0.0073). However, the formal interaction tests did not reveal any significant sex differences across all psychological scales for pelvic myalgia.

Multiple imputation

We compared baseline characteristics between participants with complete data on all six psychological scales and fully adjusted covariates (n = 90,084) and those with at least one missing value (n = 411,904; Supplementary Table 7). Participants in the complete-case group were younger (55.10 ± 7.53 vs. 56.85 ± 8.18 years), had lower BMI (26.51 ± 4.43 vs. 27.64 ± 4.86 kg/m²), and were more likely to have higher income and higher educational attainment (71.4% vs. 46.1%,). The complete-case group also had a lower proportion of hypertension (39.7% vs. 41.8%) and diabetes (2.7% vs. 5.8%), as well as a substantially lower death rate during follow-up (0.0% vs. 11.1%).

Given this observed selection bias, we further performed outcome-specific multiple imputation by MICE, generating 50 imputed datasets with 5 iterations each. Across all 48 exposure-outcome pairs, the pooled MICE HRs demonstrated strong concordance with the complete-case estimates with Pearson’s r = 0.97 (Supplementary Fig. 7A). Specifically, the association between RDS-4 and pelvic myalgia yielded a complete-case HR of 1.33 (95% CI: 1.23–1.44) and a MICE-pooled HR of 1.29 (95% CI: 1.22–1.37). Similarly, for RDS-4 with other bladder disorders, the HRs were 1.22 (1.18–1.27) and 1.24 (1.21–1.27), respectively. Notably, for hypoactive sexual desire associated with RDS-4, the complete-case analysis yielded a non-significant HR of 1.09 (0.95–1.25), whereas the MICE analysis produced a statistically significant HR of 1.24 (1.12–1.36) (Supplementary Fig. 7B, Supplementary Table 8), suggesting that missing data may have slightly attenuated this particular association in the complete-case analysis.

Discussion

The present study provides population-scale evidence that multidimensional psychological distress is associated with a broad spectrum of non-infectious urogenital disorders in the UKBB. By integrating both cross-sectional and prospective analyses, we demonstrated that various dimensions of distress consistently influenced the incidence of these conditions over time. These findings suggest that psychological burden is not merely a concurrent correlate but represents an independent risk factor for urological morbidity.

A key finding of our analyses was the robust association between the RDS-4 scale and several functional urological outcomes, including pelvic myalgia, urgency urinary incontinence, IC/BPS, and other bladder disorders. The RDS-4 focuses on acute, somatic manifestations of stress such as tension and fatigue, which distinguished it from instruments measuring stable personality traits like the N-1219. However, the interpretation of these findings requires caution, as the direct validation of RDS-4 in large cohorts outside the UKBB remains relatively limited. Furthermore, our correlation and PCA analyses indicated significant latent overlap between RDS-4 and traditional depression or anxiety measures (e.g., PHQ-9: r = 0.49; GAD-7: r = 0.41). Crucially, RDS-4 likely captures a distress-somatic construct, and many of the diseases examined in this study such as pelvic pain, IC/BPS, and urgency incontinence are intrinsically somatic, symptom-driven, and highly stress-sensitive23–25. Therefore, the strong associations may reflect shared symptom reporting, reporting bias, or greater healthcare-seeking behavior among distressed individuals, rather than a distinct mechanistic link8.

Mechanistically, the associations observed between psychological factors and urogenital disorders may reflect complex pathways of stress-induced physiological dysregulation. It is hypothesized that chronic stress may activate the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, potentially provoking neurogenic inflammation and functional alterations in brain regions linked to urogenital function26,27. Previous studies utilizing functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) have identified alterations in neural connectivity during the provocation of lower urinary tract symptoms27. Research in healthy females has further elucidated the brain activation centers associated with micturition initiation, revealing a coordinated network involving regions for motor control, executive function, and emotion processing28. Such evidence offers a speculative basis for how psychological distress might correlate with urological function in clinical populations.

Moreover, perceived psychological stress may be associated with elevated urinary oxidative damage markers, such as urinary 8-hydroxy-2’-deoxyguanosine (8-OHdG)29,30, pointing toward oxidative stress as a potential mediator. By capturing the somatic manifestations of autonomic and HPA axis hyperactivity, psychometric scales likely identify individuals with stress-mediated end-organ dysfunction, such as pelvic floor instability31, bladder hypersensitivity32, or chronic inflammation. Although these mechanisms should be framed as hypothesis-generating, these results substantiated the “psychoneuroimmunology”33 and “brain-bladder axis” frameworks34, positioning chronic stress as a key modifiable risk factor for functional urogenital disorders.

Regarding the specific findings for chronic prostatitis, our analysis only revealed a significant association for the MHS-20 scale in the cross-sectional logistic regression, whereas all six psychological scales demonstrated non-significant associations with incident chronic prostatitis in the Cox regression models. These findings suggest that while concurrent psychological distress may be present, it does not consistently predict the long-term risk of developing the condition. This pattern contrasts with conditions such as IC/BPS35 or urgency urinary incontinence36, where central psychoneuroimmune mechanisms appear to a pivotal role and psychological stress may trigger neuroendocrine responses that can exacerbate peripheral neurogenic inflammation and facilitate central sensitization37, thereby amplifying symptom perception. In contrast, chronic prostatitis appeared to be driven primarily by localized pathological processes. Animal models found prostatic inflammation with distinct histological features such as monocyte infiltration and elevated mast cell tryptase activity, as well as cytokine profiles with upregulation of IL-10 and NGF, which persisting weeks after induced inflammation34. This suggested a self-perpetuating peripheral inflammatory cascade. Besides, chronic prostatitis/chronic pelvic pain syndrome (CP/CPPS) is clinically heterogeneous where diagnostic coding limitations probably failed to distinguish inflammatory (Category II) from non-inflammatory subtypes (Category III). Since the latter is often hypothesized to exhibit stronger psychosomatic linkages38, the heterogeneous grouping of these distinct pathologies under a single code (N41.1) likely introduced outcome misclassification and diluted the observed effect sizes.

Our stratified analyses revealed distinct age-, sex-, and menopause-specific patterns in the associations between psychological distress and urogenital disorders, suggesting that the impact of psychological burden is modulated by physiological and psychosocial contexts across the life course. In our age-stratified analyses, we observed that the associations between psychological distress and the risk of both other bladder disorders and IC/BPS were significantly more pronounced in younger individuals (< 65 years). While direct epidemiological evidence for this specific age group remains limited, this pattern may be explained by heightened neuroplasticity and central sensitization early in life39,40. Preclinical and neuroimaging studies suggest that neural plasticity is most active in younger populations, where early stress exposure may induce lasting alterations in central pain processing pathways41. Such changes could predispose younger individuals to central sensitization, which is a state of amplified neural responsiveness to bladder afferent signals, manifesting as the hypersensitivity characteristic of IC/BPS. Furthermore, chronic psychological distress in younger demographics frequently triggers HPA axis hyperreactivity and autonomic nervous system dysregulation42, driving somatic manifestations such as pelvic floor muscle tension and voiding dysfunction common in other functional bladder disorders. Conversely, in older adults (≥ 65 years), urological morbidities are typically dominated by cumulative organic and structural alterations43, such as physiological detrusor decline, benign prostatic enlargement, and systemic comorbidities. The overwhelming presence of these age-related organic factors may proportionally dilute or “mask” the specific prognostic impact of psychological distress in the elderly cohort.

Notably, our interaction analyses indicated evidence of effect modification by menopausal status in the association between psychological distress and pelvic floor dysfunction, with the deleterious impact of MHS-20, N-12, and RDS-4 scores being substantially more pronounced in pre- and peri-menopausal women compared to their post-menopausal counterparts. This heightened susceptibility suggests that the hormonal volatility and neuroendocrine flux characteristic of the menopausal transition44,45 may synergistically interact with psychological burden to exacerbate pelvic floor instability. Consequently, these findings identify the perimenopausal stage as a critical clinical window for integrated biopsychosocial monitoring to mitigate stress-mediated pelvic impairment.

Sex differences were equally pronounced, particularly for bladder disorders, IC/BPS, where females exhibited consistently stronger and more robust associations with psychological distress. This disparity may partly reflect anatomical and hormonal vulnerabilities in women, including shorter urethral length and pelvic floor structural susceptibility, which may be exacerbated by psychological stress through hypertonia or estrogen-mediated pathways46. Additionally, the female-specific association for IC/BPS aligns with its higher diagnosed prevalence in women47,48, though this may also reflect a diagnostic bias where similar symptoms in men are often misclassified as chronic prostatitis. Conversely, the observed association between psychological distress and pelvic floor dysfunction was significantly stronger among male participants. In females, pelvic floor dysfunction is heavily driven by profound biomechanical and obstetric events such as pregnancy, childbirth, and menopausal tissue atrophy49 which represent overwhelming organic risk factors that may dilute the specific psychosomatic signal. In contrast, males lack these major structural and reproductive confounders. Consequently, psychoneuromuscular mechanisms might emerge as a primary, unmasked driver of the disease. For pelvic myalgia, the associations with psychological distress did not significantly differ by sex, suggesting that while certain functional bladder disorders may exhibit sex-related patterns, the impact of psychological distress on pelvic muscular pain may operate through more universal mechanisms across both genders. However, these interpretations should be made with caution, as the observed differences may also reflect non-biological factors, including variations in diagnostic practices, symptom perception and reporting, or healthcare-seeking behavior, rather than true underlying biological heterogeneity.

This study has several strengths, most notably the implementation of a multidimensional psychological assessment that overcomes the constraints of prior research relying on single-domain measures or narrow diagnostic codes. By leveraging the large-scale, population-based UKBB cohort, we minimize the selection biases typically found in clinical samples, thereby ensuring greater generalizability to broader community populations. Besides, the dual analytical approach combining cross-sectional and prospective cohort designs enhances our ability to characterize the temporal relationships between psychological distress and urogenital health. These associations will provide empirical support for a more integrated perspective in urological practice, suggesting a transition from a traditionally localized, organ-centric view of disease toward a systemic, biopsychosocial framework. Specifically, for pre- and peri-menopausal women, practitioners can better implement integrated monitoring to mitigate the compounding effects of psychological burden on pelvic stability. Similarly, the inverse age-dependent trend observed for IC/BPS underscores the importance of early intervention in younger populations. Furthermore, for older adults with diminishing physiological reserves, managing psychological distress may become an essential component of comprehensive geriatric urological care to prevent the further amplification of bladder instability.

Despite these strengths, several limitations warrant a cautious interpretation of our results. The reliance on ICD-10 diagnostic codes for outcome definition remains a primary constraint. These codes often lack the clinical granularity required to distinguish between heterogeneous subtypes, such as inflammatory (NIH Category II) versus non-inflammatory (NIH Category III) chronic prostatitis, which likely introduced outcome misclassification and may have diluted the observed associations for subtypes with stronger psychosomatic linkages. Furthermore, as ICD-coded diagnoses in hospital records tend to under-capture mild or subclinical urological conditions, our findings may primarily reflect more severe clinical phenotypes. There is also a risk of overlapping classifications among symptom-driven conditions like pelvic pain and interstitial cystitis, which complicates the differentiation of distinct mechanistic links. Secondly, the generalizability of our findings may be constrained by a known selection bias, as UKBB participants tend to be healthier and more socioeconomically advantaged than the wider population. Moreover, non-participation at baseline and potential attrition bias during the longitudinal follow-up period may have further influenced our estimates. Additionally, missing data represent a methodological consideration. We cannot definitively exclude Missing Not At Random (MNAR) mechanisms under which the severity of psychological distress itself may have influenced questionnaire completion. However, given the strong concordance between complete-case and MICE estimates, any such MNAR bias would likely operate toward the null, making our estimates conservative. Thus, the true associations may be equal to or stronger than those reported. Thirdly, because psychological measures were self-reported, the potential for measurement error or reporting bias cannot be excluded. Finally, it is crucial to recognize the bidirectional and context-dependent nature of these relationships: while psychological distress may contribute to physiological dysregulation, the persistent burden of urogenital pain and inflammation can also exacerbate psychological symptoms. In addition, individuals with higher psychological distress may engage more frequently with healthcare services or receive increased clinical attention, potentially leading to higher likelihood of diagnosis for symptom-driven and stress-sensitive conditions such as pelvic pain and IC/BPS, which could partly inflate observed associations. Thus, our findings should be viewed as identifying risk associations within a complex, reciprocal disease process rather than a linear causal chain. Future studies employing approaches such as Mendelian randomization or other robust causal inference frameworks will be essential to disentangle the directionality and underlying mechanisms of these relationships.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that multidimensional psychological distress serves as a risk factor for incident urogenital disorders, characterized by distinct age-, sex-, and menopause-specific patterns. These findings support the transition toward an integrated biopsychosocial framework in urological practice, considering psychological profiling as a tool. Integrating these assessments into current practice adds diagnostic value by identifying patients whose urological symptoms are predominantly driven by central stress-mediated pathways or somatic hyper-responsiveness, particularly those who may otherwise remain refractive to traditional, organ-centric therapies. While the efficacy of psychobehavioral interventions like cognitive-behavioral therapy is established for symptom reduction, our work highlights a critical need for prospective research to evaluate whether early, targeted interventions can proactively mitigate the long-term risk of incident urological disease by modulating shared neuroendocrine and inflammatory pathways. Ultimately, these results highlight the potential utility of incorporating biopsychosocial perspectives into urological practice to better address the complex, multifaceted needs of patients with urogenital disorders.
