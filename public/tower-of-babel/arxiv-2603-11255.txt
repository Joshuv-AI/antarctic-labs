Abstract
. This paper is part of a series of papers in which we have investigated the differential smoothness of families of noncommutative algebras. Here, we consider this topic for the family 3-dimensional skew polynomial rings characterized by Bell and Smith [ 2 ] .

1.
Introduction

Brzeziński and Sitarz
[
11
]
defined a notion of smoothness of algebras, termed
differential smoothness
due to the use of differential graded algebras of a specified dimension that admits a noncommutative version of the Hodge star isomorphism, which considers the existence of a top form in a differential calculus over an algebra together with a string version of the Poincaré duality realized as an
isomorphism between complexes of differential and integral forms. “The idea behind the
differential smoothness
of algebras is rooted in the observation that a classical smooth orientable manifold, in addition to de Rham complex of differential forms, admits also the complex of
integral forms
isomorphic to the de Rham complex
[
31
, Section 4.5]
. The de Rham differential can be understood as a special left connection, while the boundary operator in the complex of integral forms is an example of a
right connection
”
[
11
, p. 413]
. This notion of smoothness is different and more constructive than other homological smoothnesses considered in the literature (for more details on the subject, see
[
4
,
6
,
14
,
22
,
29
,
47
,
49
,
50
]
).

Since its introduction, Brzeziński and several authors (e.g.
[
7
]
-
[
11
]
,
[
15
,
16
,
27
,
28
,
38
]
) have characterized the differential smoothness of different families of noncommutative algebras such as the following: quantum spheres, noncommutative torus, the coordinate algebras of the quantum group
SU_{q}(2)
, the noncommutative pillow algebra, the quantum polynomial algebras, Hopf algebra domains, families of Ore extensions, some 3-dimensional skew polynomial algebras, diffusion algebras in three generators, and noncommutative coordinate algebras of deformations of examples of classical orbifolds. Recently, in a series of papers
[
41
]
-
[
46
]
, the authors considered the question on the differential smoothness of different types of algebra appearing in noncommutative algebraic geometry. In this paper, the objects of interest are the
3-dimensional skew polynomial rings
introduced by Bell and Smith
[
2
]
, which have been studied from the point of view of ring theory and noncommutative algebraic geometry (e.g.
[
12
,
18
,
33
,
35
,
36
,
37
,
39
,
40
]
, and references therein).

The article is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we consider the preliminaries on differential smoothness of algebras and 3-dimensional skew polynomial rings, in order to set up notation and render this paper self-contained. Section
3
contains the original results of the paper: Theorem
3.1
formulates sufficient conditions to guarantee that a 3-dimensional skew polynomial ring is differentially smooth, and Theorem
3.2
establishes sufficient conditions to assert that there are no one-dimensional connected integrable calculi over these rings. Section
4
presents some ideas for possible future work.

Throughout the paper,
\mathbb{N}
denotes the set of natural numbers including zero. The word ring means an associative ring with identity not necessarily commutative. All vector spaces and algebras (always associative and with unit) are over a fixed field
\Bbbk

\Bbbk^{\times}
denotes the non-zero elements of
\Bbbk
. As usual, the symbols
\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbb{C}
denote the fields of real and complex numbers, respectively. As usual,

means the number of elements of the set

2.
Definitions and Preliminaries

2.1.
Differential smoothness of algebras

We follow Brzeziński and Sitarz’s ideas on differential smoothness carried out in
[
11
, Section 2]
(see also
[
4
,
6
,
9
]
).

Definition 2.1

(
[
11
, Section 2.1]
)
.

(i)

A
differential graded algebra
is a non-negatively graded algebra
\Omega
with the product denoted by
\wedge
together with a degree-one linear map
d:\Omega^{\bullet}\to\Omega^{\bullet+1}
that satisfies the graded Leibniz’s rule and is such that
d\circ d=0
.

(ii)

A differential graded algebra
(\Omega,d)
is a
calculus over an algebra

A
if

\Omega^{0}A=A\quad{\rm and}\quad\Omega^{n}A=A\ dA\wedge dA\wedge\dotsb\wedge dA\quad(dA\,\,{\rm appears}\,n-{\rm times}),

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
(this last is called the
density condition
). We write
(\Omega A,d)
with
\Omega A=\bigoplus_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\Omega^{n}A
. By using the Leibniz’s rule, it follows that

\Omega^{n}A=dA\wedge dA\wedge\dotsb\wedge dA\ A.

A differential calculus
\Omega A
is called
connected
if
{\rm ker}(d\mid_{\Omega^{0}A})=\Bbbk
.

(iii)

A calculus
(\Omega A,d)
is said to have
dimension

n
if
\Omega^{n}A\neq 0
and
\Omega^{m}A=0
for all
m>n
. An
n
-dimensional calculus
\Omega A

admits a volume form
if
\Omega^{n}A
is isomorphic to
A
as a left and right
A
-module.

The existence of a right
A
-module isomorphism means that there is a free generator, say
\omega
, of
\Omega^{n}A
(as a right
A
-module), i.e.
\omega\in\Omega^{n}A
, such that all elements of
\Omega^{n}A
can be uniquely expressed as
\omega a
with
a\in A
. If
\omega
is also a free generator of
\Omega^{n}A
as a left
A
-module, it is said to be a
volume form
on
\Omega A
.

The right
A
-module isomorphism
\Omega^{n}A\to A
corresponding to a volume form
\omega
is denoted by
\pi_{\omega}
, i.e.

(2.1)

\pi_{\omega}(\omega a)=a,\quad{\rm for\ all}\ a\in A.

By using that
\Omega^{n}A
is also isomorphic to
A
as a left
A
-module, any free generator
\omega
induces an algebra endomorphism
\nu_{\omega}
of
A
by the formula

(2.2)

a\omega=\omega\nu_{\omega}(a).

Note that if
\omega
is a volume form, then
\nu_{\omega}
is an algebra automorphism.

Next, we recall the key ingredients of the
integral calculus
on
A
as dual to its differential calculus.

Let
(\Omega A,d)
be a differential calculus on
A
. The space of
n
-forms
\Omega^{n}A
is an
A
-bimodule. Consider
\mathcal{I}_{n}A
the right dual of
\Omega^{n}A
, the space of all right
A
-linear maps
\Omega^{n}A\rightarrow A
, that is,
\mathcal{I}_{n}A:={\rm Hom}_{A}(\Omega^{n}(A),A)
. Notice that each of the
\mathcal{I}_{n}A
is an
A
-bimodule with the actions given by

\displaystyle(a\cdot\phi\cdot b)(\omega)=a\phi(b\omega),\quad{\rm for\ all}\ \phi\in\mathcal{I}_{n}A,\ \omega\in\Omega^{n}A\ {\rm and}\ a,b\in A.

The direct sum of all the
\mathcal{I}_{n}A
, that is,
\mathcal{I}A=\bigoplus_{n}\mathcal{I}_{n}A
, is a right
\Omega A
-module with action given by

(2.3)

\displaystyle(\phi\cdot\omega)(\omega^{\prime})=\phi(\omega\wedge\omega^{\prime}),\quad{\rm for\ all}\ \phi\in\mathcal{I}_{n+m}A,\ \omega\in\Omega^{n}A\ {\rm and}\ \omega^{\prime}\in\Omega^{m}A.

Definition 2.2

(
[
4
, Definition 2.1]
)
.

A
divergence
(also called
hom-connection
) on
A
is a linear map
\nabla:\mathcal{I}_{1}A\to A
such that

(2.4)

\nabla(\phi\cdot a)=\nabla(\phi)a+\phi(da),\quad{\rm for\ all}\ \phi\in\mathcal{I}_{1}A\ {\rm and}\ a\in A.

Note that a divergence can be extended to the whole of
\mathcal{I}A
,

\nabla_{n}:\mathcal{I}_{n+1}A\to\mathcal{I}_{n}A,

by considering

(2.5)

\nabla_{n}(\phi)(\omega)=\nabla(\phi\cdot\omega)+(-1)^{n+1}\phi(d\omega),\quad{\rm for\ all}\ \phi\in\mathcal{I}_{n+1}(A)\ {\rm and}\ \omega\in\Omega^{n}A.

By putting together (
2.4
) and (
2.5
), we get the Leibniz’s rule

(2.6)

\nabla_{n}(\phi\cdot\omega)=\nabla_{m+n}(\phi)\cdot\omega+(-1)^{m+n}\phi\cdot d\omega,

for all elements
\phi\in\mathcal{I}_{m+n+1}A
and
\omega\in\Omega^{m}A

[
4
, Lemma 3.2]
. In the case
n=0
, if
{\rm Hom}_{A}(A,M)
is canonically identified with
M
, then
\nabla_{0}
reduces to the classical Leibniz’s rule.

Definition 2.3

(
[
4
, Definition 3.4]
)
.

The right
A
-module map

F=\nabla_{0}\circ\nabla_{1}:{\rm Hom}_{A}(\Omega^{2}A,M)\to M

is called a
curvature
of a hom-connection
(M,\nabla_{0})
.
(M,\nabla_{0})
is said to be
flat
if its curvature is the zero map, that is, if
\nabla\circ\nabla_{1}=0
. This condition implies that
\nabla_{n}\circ\nabla_{n+1}=0
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

\mathcal{I}A
together with the
\nabla_{n}
form a chain complex called the
complex of integral forms
over
A
. The cokernel map of
\nabla
, that is,
\Lambda:A\to{\rm Coker}\nabla=A/{\rm Im}\nabla
is said to be the
integral on
A
associated to

\mathcal{I}A
.

Given a left
A
-module
X
with action
a\cdot x
for all
a\in A,\ x\in X
, and an algebra automorphism
\nu
of
A
, the notation
{}^{\nu}X
stands for
X
with the
A
-module structure twisted by
\nu
, i.e. with the
A
-action
a\otimes x\mapsto\nu(a)\cdot x
.

The following definition of an
integrable differential calculus
seeks to portray a version of Hodge star isomorphisms between the complex of differential forms of a differentiable manifold and a complex of dual modules of it

(

An

The

\omega:=\Theta_{n}^{-1}(1)\in\Omega^{n}A
is called an

.

The algebra of complex matrices
M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
with the
n
-dimensional calculus generated by derivations presented by Dubois-Violette et al.
[
15
,
16
]
, the quantum group
SU_{q}(2)
with the three-dimensional left covariant calculus developed by Woronowicz
[
51
]
and the quantum standard sphere with the restriction of the above calculus, are examples of algebras admitting integrable calculi.

The following proposition shows that the integrability of a differential calculus can be defined without explicit reference to integral forms. This allows us to guarantee the integrability by considering the existence of finitely generator elements that allow to determine left and right components of any homogeneous element of
\Omega(A)
.

Proposition 2.5

(
[
11
, Theorem 2.2]
)
.

Let
(\Omega A,d)
be an
n
-dimensional differential calculus over an algebra
A
. The following assertions are equivalent:

(1)

(\Omega A,d)
is an integrable differential calculus.

(2)

There exists an algebra automorphism
\nu
of
A
and
A
-bimodule isomorphisms

\Theta_{k}:\Omega^{k}A\rightarrow\ ^{\nu}\mathcal{I}_{n-k}A,\quad k=0,\ldots,n,

such that for all
\omega^{\prime}\in\Omega^{k}A
and
\omega^{\prime\prime}\in\Omega^{m}A
, we have that

\displaystyle\Theta_{k+m}(\omega^{\prime}\wedge\omega^{\prime\prime})=(-1)^{(n-1)m}\Theta_{k}(\omega^{\prime})\cdot\omega^{\prime\prime}.

(3)

There exists an algebra automorphism
\nu
of
A
and an
A
-bimodule map
\vartheta:\Omega^{n}A\rightarrow\ ^{\nu}A
such that all left multiplication maps

\displaystyle\ell_{\vartheta}^{k}:\Omega^{k}A

\displaystyle\ \rightarrow\mathcal{I}_{n-k}A,

\displaystyle\omega^{\prime}

\displaystyle\ \mapsto\vartheta\cdot\omega^{\prime},\quad k=0,1,\dotsc,n,

where the actions
\cdot
are defined by
(
2.3
)
, are bijective.

(4)

(\Omega A,d)
has a volume form
\omega
such that all left multiplication maps

\displaystyle\ell_{\pi_{\omega}}^{k}:\Omega^{k}A

\displaystyle\ \rightarrow\mathcal{I}_{n-k}A,

\displaystyle\omega^{\prime}

\displaystyle\ \mapsto\pi_{\omega}\cdot\omega^{\prime},\quad k=0,1,\dotsc,n-1,

where
\pi_{\omega}
is defined by
(
2.1
)
, are bijective.

A volume form
\omega\in\Omega^{n}A
is an
integrating form
if and only if it satisfies condition
(4)
of Proposition
2.5

[
11
, Remark 2.3]
.

The most interesting cases of differential calculi are those where
\Omega^{k}A
are finitely generated and projective right or left (or both)
A
-modules (for more details, see
[
5
]
).

Proposition 2.6
.

(1)

[
11
, Lemma 2.6]
Consider
(\Omega A,d)
an integrable and
n
-dimensional calculus over
A
with integrating form
\omega
. Then
\Omega^{k}A
is a finitely generated projective right
A
-module if there exist a finite number of forms
\omega_{i}\in\Omega^{k}A
and
\overline{\omega}_{i}\in\Omega^{n-k}A
such that for all
\omega^{\prime}\in\Omega^{k}A
, we have that

\omega^{\prime}=\sum_{i}\omega_{i}\pi_{\omega}(\overline{\omega}_{i}\wedge\omega^{\prime}).

(2)

[
11
, Lemma 2.7]
Let
(\Omega A,d)
be an
n
-dimensional calculus over
A
admitting a volume form
\omega
. Assume that for all
k=1,\ldots,n-1
, there exists a finite number of forms
\omega_{i}^{k},\overline{\omega}_{i}^{k}\in\Omega^{k}(A)
such that for all
\omega^{\prime}\in\Omega^{k}A
, we have that

\omega^{\prime}=\displaystyle\sum_{i}\omega_{i}^{k}\pi_{\omega}(\overline{\omega}_{i}^{n-k}\wedge\omega^{\prime})=\displaystyle\sum_{i}\nu_{\omega}^{-1}(\pi_{\omega}(\omega^{\prime}\wedge\omega_{i}^{n-k}))\overline{\omega}_{i}^{k},

where
\pi_{\omega}
and
\nu_{\omega}
are defined by
(
2.1
)
and
(
2.2
)
, respectively. Then
\omega
is an integral form and all the
\Omega^{k}A
are finitely generated and projective as left and right
A
-modules.

Brzeziński and Sitarz
[
11
, p. 421]
point out that, in order to relate the dimension of an integrable calculus
(\Omega A,d)
with the “size” of the underlying affine algebra
A
, a suitable notion of dimension is needed: its Gelfand-Kirillov dimension
\text{GKdim}(A)
introduced by Gelfand and Kirillov
[
19
,
20
]
(in some sense, this dimensions measures the deviation of the algebra
A
from finite dimensionality). For more details about this dimension, see the excellent treatment by Krause and Lenagan
[
30
]
.

We arrive to the key notion of the paper.

Definition 2.7

(
[
11
, Definition 2.4]
)
.

An affine algebra
A
with integer Gelfand-Kirillov dimension
n
is said to be
differentially smooth
if it admits an
n
-dimensional connected integrable differential calculus
(\Omega A,d)
.

From Definition
2.7
it follows that a differentially smooth algebra comes equipped with a well-behaved differential structure and with the precise concept of integration
[
10
, p. 2414]
.

As we said in the Introduction, several examples of noncommutative algebras have been proved to be differentially smooth (e.g.
[
7
,
9
,
10
,
11
,
27
,
28
,
38
]
). Of course, there are examples of algebras that are not differentially smooth. Consider the commutative algebra
A=\mathbb{C}[x,y]/\langle xy\rangle
. A proof by contradiction shows that for this algebra there are no one-dimensional connected integrable calculi over
A
, so it cannot be differentially smooth
[
11
, Example 2.5]
.

2.2.
3-dimensional skew polynomial algebras

Bell and Smith
[
2
]
(see also
[
40
, Definition C4.3]
) defined the
3-dimensional skew polynomial rings
as those
\Bbbk
-algebras generated by the indeterminates
x,y,z
restricted to relations

yz-\alpha zy=\lambda,\quad zx-\beta xz=\mu\quad{\rm and}\ xy-\gamma yx=\nu,

such that

(i)

\lambda,\mu,\nu\in\Bbbk+\Bbbk x+\Bbbk y+\Bbbk z
, and
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in\Bbbk\ \backslash\ \{0\}
, and

(ii)

standard monomials
\left\{x^{i}y^{j}z^{l}\mid i,j,l\geq 0\right\}
are a
\Bbbk
-basis of the algebra
A
.

The classification of these skew polynomial rings is presented in the following proposition.

Proposition 2.8

(
[
40
, Theorem C.4.3.1]
)
.

If
A
is a 3-dimensional skew polynomial ring, then
A
is one of the following algebras:

(1)

if
|\{\alpha,\beta,\gamma\}|=3
, then
A
is given by the relations

yz-\alpha zy=0,\ zx-\beta xz=0\ {\rm and}\ xy-\gamma yx=0.

(2)

if
|\{\alpha,\beta,\gamma\}|=2
and
\beta\neq\alpha=\gamma=1
, then
A
is one of the following algebras:

(i)

yz-zy=z,\ zx-\beta xz=y
and
xy-yx=x

(
if
\beta=-1
, then we get the Dispin algebra
)
.

(ii)

yz-zy=z,\ zx-\beta xz=b
and
xy-yx=x
;

(iii)

yz-zy=0,\ zx-\beta xz=y
and
xy-yx=0
;

(iv)

yz-zy=0,\ zx-\beta xz=b
and
xy-yx=0
;

(v)

yz-zy=az,\ zx-\beta xz=0
and
xy-yx=x
;

(vi)

yz-zy=z,\ zx-\beta xz=0
and
xy-yx=0
,

where
a,b
are any elements of
\Bbbk
. All non-zero values of
b

give isomorphic algebras.

(3)

If
|\{\alpha,\beta,\gamma\}|=2
and
\beta\neq\alpha=\gamma\neq 1
, then
A
is one of the following algebras:

(i)

yz-\alpha zy=0,\ zx-\beta xz=y+b
and
xy-\alpha yx=0
;

(ii)

yz-\alpha zy=0,\ zx-\beta xz=b
and
xy-\alpha yx=0
.

In this case,
b
is an arbitrary element of
\Bbbk
. Again, any
non-zero values of
b
give isomorphic algebras.

(4)

If
\alpha=\beta=\gamma\neq 1
, then
A
is the algebra defined by the relations

\displaystyle yz-\alpha zy=

\displaystyle\ a_{1}x+b_{1},

\displaystyle zx-\alpha xz=

\displaystyle\ a_{2}y+b_{2},\ {\rm and}

\displaystyle xy-\alpha yx=

\displaystyle\ a_{3}z+b_{3}.

If
a_{i}=0
for all
i
, then all non-zero values of
b_{i}
give isomorphic
algebras.

(5)

If
\alpha=\beta=\gamma=1
, then
A
is isomorphic to one of the following algebras:

(i)

yz-zy=x,\ zx-xz=y
and
xy-yx=z
;

(ii)

yz-zy=0,\ zx-xz=0
and
xy-yx=z
;

(iii)

yz-zy=0,\ zx-xz=0
and
xy-yx=b
;

(iv)

yz-zy=-y,\ zx-xz=x+y
and
xy-yx=0
;

(v)

yz-zy=az,\ zx-xz=z
and
xy-yx=0
;

Parameters
a,b\in\Bbbk
are arbitrary, and all non-zero values of

b
generate isomorphic algebras.

Remark 2.9
.

As wee saw above, Rosenberg
[
40
, Theorem C4.3.1, case (5)(v), p. 101]
presented the relations given by

yz-zy=az,\ zx-xz=x\quad{\rm and}\quad xy-yx=0.

However, there is a typo in the second relation as one can check using Bergman’s diamond lemma
[
3
]
to guarantee the PBW basis of the algebra (see Remark
2.11
for more details). As a matter of fact, he presented this algebra as the Ore extension
[
34
]
given by

A=\Bbbk[x,y][z;\varphi],

where
\varphi\in\mathrm{Aut}(\Bbbk[x,y])
is given by
\varphi(x)=x+1
and
\varphi(y)=y-a

[
40
, p. 108]
. By the defining rule
za=\varphi(a)z
for all
a\in\Bbbk[x,y]
, we obtain that

\displaystyle zx=\varphi(x)z=(x+1)z

\displaystyle\quad{\rm whence}\quad zx-xz=z,

\displaystyle zy=\varphi(y)z=(y-a)z

\displaystyle\quad{\rm i.e.}\quad yz-zy=az.

Therefore, the correct defining relations of case (5)(v) are given by

yz-zy=az,\ zx-xz=z,\quad{\rm and}\quad xy-yx=0.

Let us see some remarkable examples of 3-dimensional skew polynomial rings appearing in Bavula

Finally,

(3.20)

\displaystyle\nu_{z}\circ\nu_{y}(x)=

\displaystyle\ \gamma\beta^{-1}x-\gamma\beta^{-1}c_{\mu}+b_{\nu},

(3.21)

\displaystyle\nu_{y}\circ\nu_{z}(x)=

\displaystyle\ \beta^{-1}(\gamma x+b_{\nu}-c_{\mu}),

(3.22)

\displaystyle\nu_{z}\circ\nu_{y}(y)=

\displaystyle\ \alpha y+c_{\lambda},

(3.23)

\displaystyle\nu_{y}\circ\nu_{z}(y)=

\displaystyle\ \alpha y+c_{\lambda},

(3.24)

\displaystyle\nu_{z}\circ\nu_{y}(z)=

\displaystyle\ \alpha^{-1}(\beta z-b_{\lambda})\quad\text{and}

(3.25)

\displaystyle\nu_{y}\circ\nu_{z}(z)=

\displaystyle\ \beta\alpha^{-1}(z-b_{\lambda}).

Thus, (
3.20
) and (
3.21
), as well as (
3.24
) and (
3.25
) coincide whenever

b_{\nu}(1-\beta)=c_{\mu}(1-\gamma)\quad{\rm and}\quad b_{\lambda}(\beta-1)=0.

Let
\Omega^{1}A
be a free right
A
-module of rank three with generators
dx
,
dy
, and
dz
. For each
p\in A
, define a left
A
-module structure by

\displaystyle p\,dx=

\displaystyle\ dx\nu_{x}(p),

\displaystyle p\,dy=

\displaystyle\ dy\nu_{y}(p)\quad\text{and}

(3.26)

\displaystyle p\,dz=

\displaystyle\ dz\nu_{z}(p).

Then the bimodule relations in
\Omega^{1}A
are

\displaystyle x\,dx=

\displaystyle\ dx\beta^{-1}x,

\displaystyle x\,dy=

\displaystyle\ dy(\gamma x+b_{\nu}),

(3.27)

\displaystyle x\,dz=

\displaystyle\ dz\beta^{-1}(x-c_{\mu}),

\displaystyle y\,dx=

\displaystyle\ dx\gamma^{-1}(y-a_{\nu}),

\displaystyle y\,dy=

\displaystyle\ dy\,y,

(3.28)

\displaystyle y\,dz=

\displaystyle\ dz(\alpha y+c_{\lambda}),

\displaystyle z\,dx=

\displaystyle\ dx(\beta z+a_{\mu}),

\displaystyle z\,dy=

\displaystyle\ dy\alpha^{-1}(z-b_{\lambda})\quad\text{and}

(3.29)

\displaystyle z\,dz=

\displaystyle\ dz\beta z.

Our aim is to extend the assignment

x\mapsto dx,\qquad y\mapsto dy\quad{\rm and}\quad z\mapsto dz

to a
\Bbbk
-linear map
d:A\to\Omega^{1}A
satisfying the Leibniz rule. This is possible precisely when
d
is compatible with the nontrivial relations (
2.7
), i.e. when

\displaystyle d(yz)-\alpha\,d(zy)

\displaystyle=b_{\lambda}dy+c_{\lambda}dz+d(d_{\lambda}),

\displaystyle d(zx)-\beta\,d(xz)

\displaystyle=a_{\mu}dx+c_{\mu}dz+d(d_{\mu})\ \text{and}

\displaystyle d(xy)-\gamma\,d(yx)

\displaystyle=a_{\nu}dx+b_{\nu}dy+d(d_{\nu})

hold.

Define
\Bbbk
-linear maps

\partial_{x},\partial_{y},\partial_{z}:A\rightarrow A

by requiring that

d(a)=dx\,\partial_{x}(a)+dy\,\partial_{y}(a)+dz\,\partial_{z}(a),\quad\text{for all }a\in A.

Since
dx
,
dy
and
dz
form a free basis of the right
A
-module
\Omega^{1}A
, the maps
\partial_{x},\partial_{y},\partial_{z}
are well-defined. Moreover,

d(a)=0\quad{\rm if\,and\,only\,if}\quad\partial_{x}(a)=\partial_{y}(a)=\partial_{z}(a)=0.

Using the relations in (
3.26
) together with the definitions of
\nu_{x}
,
\nu_{y}
, and
\nu_{z}
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\partial_{x}(x^{k}y^{l}z^{s})=

\displaystyle\ \sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\beta^{-i}x^{k-1}y^{l}z^{s},

(3.30)

\displaystyle\partial_{y}(x^{k}y^{l}z^{s})=

\displaystyle\ l(\gamma x+b_{\nu})^{k}y^{l-1}z^{s}\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle\partial_{z}(x^{k}y^{l}z^{s})=

\displaystyle\ \sum_{i=0}^{s-1}\beta^{i-k}(x-c_{\mu})^{k}(\alpha y+c_{\lambda})^{l}z^{s-1}.

Therefore
d(a)=0
if and only if
a
is a scalar multiple of the identity, showing that
(\Omega A,d)
is connected, where

\Omega A=\Omega^{0}A\oplus\Omega^{1}A\oplus\Omega^{2}A.

The universal extension of
d
to higher forms, compatible with (
3.27
), (
3.28
) and (
3.29
), yields the following relations in
\Omega^{2}A
:

(3.31)

\displaystyle dy\wedge dx=

\displaystyle\ -\gamma^{-1}dx\wedge dy,

(3.32)

\displaystyle dz\wedge dx=

\displaystyle\ -\beta\,dx\wedge dz\quad\text{and}

(3.33)

\displaystyle dz\wedge dy=

\displaystyle\ -\alpha^{-1}dy\wedge dz.

Since
\nu_{x}
,
\nu_{y}
, and
\nu_{z}
commute, no additional relations arise, and hence

\Omega^{2}A=dx\wedge dy\,A\oplus dx\wedge dz\,A\oplus dy\wedge dz\,A.

Finally, since
\Omega^{3}A=\omega A\cong A
as a right and left
A
-module with
\omega=dx\wedge dy\wedge dz
and
\nu_{\omega}=\nu_{x}\circ\nu_{y}\circ\nu_{z}
, the form
\omega
is a volume form for
A
. By Proposition
2.6
(2),
\omega
is an integral form upon taking

\displaystyle\omega_{1}^{1}=

\displaystyle\ \bar{\omega}_{1}^{1}=dx,

\displaystyle\omega_{2}^{1}=

\displaystyle\ \bar{\omega}_{2}^{1}=dy,

\displaystyle\omega_{3}^{1}=

\displaystyle\ \bar{\omega}_{3}^{1}=dz,

\displaystyle\omega_{1}^{2}=

\displaystyle\ dy\wedge dz,

\displaystyle\omega_{2}^{2}=

\displaystyle\ -\gamma dx\wedge dz,

\displaystyle\omega_{3}^{2}=

\displaystyle\ \alpha\beta^{-1}dx\wedge dz,

\displaystyle\bar{\omega}_{1}^{2}=

\displaystyle\ \gamma\beta^{-1}dy\wedge dz,

\displaystyle\bar{\omega}_{2}^{2}=

\displaystyle\ -\alpha dx\wedge dy\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle\bar{\omega}_{3}^{2}=

\displaystyle\ dx\wedge dy.

Indeed, for
\omega^{\prime}:=dx\,a+dy\,b+dz\,c
with
a,b,c\in\Bbbk
, Proposition
2.6
(2) gives

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{3}\omega_{i}^{1}\pi_{\omega}(\bar{\omega}_{i}^{2}\wedge\omega^{\prime})=

\displaystyle\ dx\,\pi_{\omega}(\gamma\beta^{-1}a\,dy\wedge dz\wedge dx)

\displaystyle+dy\,\pi_{\omega}(-\alpha b\,dx\wedge dz\wedge dy)

\displaystyle+dz\,\pi_{\omega}(c\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ dx\,a+dy\,b+dz\,c=\omega^{\prime}.

On the other hand, if

\omega^{\prime\prime}:=dx\wedge dy\,a+dx\wedge dz\,b+dy\wedge dz\,c\quad{\rm with}\,\,a,b,c\in\Bbbk,

then

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{3}\omega_{i}^{2}\pi_{\omega}(\bar{\omega}_{i}^{1}\wedge\omega^{\prime\prime})=

\displaystyle\ dy\wedge dz\,\pi_{\omega}(c\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz)

\displaystyle\ -\gamma dx\wedge dz\,\pi_{\omega}(b\,dy\wedge dx\wedge dz)

\displaystyle\ +\alpha\beta^{-1}dx\wedge dy\,\pi_{\omega}(a\,dz\wedge dx\wedge dy)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ dx\wedge dy\,a+dx\wedge dz\,b+dy\wedge dz\,c=\omega^{\prime\prime}.

As was shown above, all forms of degrees
1
and
2
are generated by
\omega_{i}^{j}
and
\bar{\omega}_{i}^{3-j}
for
j=1,2
and
i=1,2,3
. Therefore, Proposition
2.6
(2) ensures that
\omega
is an integral form. Finally, Proposition
2.5
implies that
(\Omega A,d)
is an integrable differential calculus of degree
3
. Since
{\rm GKdim}(A)=3

[
18
, Section 8.7]
, it follows that
A
is differentially smooth.
∎

Theorem 3.2
.

If one of the conditions
a_{\lambda}\not=0
,
b_{\mu}\not=0
or
c_{\nu}\not=0
holds, then there are no one-dimensional connected integrable calculi over
A
.

Proof.

By contradiction. Suppose that
A
has a first order differential calculus
(\Omega A,d)
. Without loss of generality, we consider the case
a_{\lambda}\not=0
. Since
d
must be compatible with the relations (
2.7
), then we get that

dyz-\alpha ydz=a_{\lambda}dx+b_{\lambda}dy+c_{\lambda}dz,

whence
dx
is generated by
dy
and
dz
. This means that
\Omega^{1}A
is generated by two elements and
\Omega^{3}A=\Omega^{1}A\wedge\Omega^{1}A\wedge\Omega^{1}A=0
, i.e. there is no third-order calculus. Since
{\rm GKdim}(A)=3
, the assertion follows.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

In
[
38
]
, the differential smoothness of the algebra of type (5)(v) cannot be settled by the method developed therein. This arises from the fact that the defining relations for case (5)(v) are taken from Rosenberg’s statement in
[
40
, Theorem C4.3.1, case (e)(v), p. 101]
. As explained in Remark
2.9
, that statement contains a typo: the relation
zx-xz=x
must be replaced with
zx-xz=z
. Consequently, the arguments in
[
38
]
that rely on the misprinted presentation do not apply to the genuine algebra of type (5)(v); using the corrected relations, its differential smoothness is settled in the present work.

4.
Future work

Jordan
[
24
]
introduced
certain iterated skew polynomial rings

K[x;\sigma][y;\sigma^{-1},\delta]
in two indeterminates over a commutative ring
K
which include the universal enveloping algebra of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{sl}(2)
and several quantum groups. Two years later, in
[
25
]
he extended this class of rings by considering an extra parameter
\rho\in\Bbbk\ \backslash\ \{0\}
and include the quantized Weyl algebra, the universal enveloping algebra of the Dispin Lie superalgebra, and an algebra introduced by Woronowicz
[
51
]
. Let us see the details.

Definition 4.1

(
[
25
, Section 1.1]
)
.

Let
K
be a finitely generated commutative algebra over an algebraically closed field
\Bbbk
, let
\sigma
be a
\Bbbk
-automorphism of
K
, and consider
u\in K
and
\rho\in\Bbbk\ \backslash\ \{0\}
. Form the skew polynomial ring of automorphism type
K[x;\sigma]
and extend
\sigma
to
K[x;\sigma]
by setting

\sigma(x)=\rho^{-1}x\quad{\rm whence}\quad\sigma^{-1}(x)=\rho x.

There is an
\sigma^{-1}
-derivation
\delta
of
K[x;\sigma]
such that
\delta(K)=0
and
\delta(x)=u-\rho\sigma(u)

[
13
, p. 41]
(see also
[
21
, Section 2.8]
). Consider the skew polynomial ring

\displaystyle K[x;\sigma][y;\sigma^{-1},\delta],

with defining relations given by

(4.1)

\displaystyle xk=

\displaystyle\,\sigma(k)x,

(4.2)

\displaystyle yk=

\displaystyle\,\sigma^{-1}(k)y,\quad{\rm for}\ k\in K,\,{\rm and}

(4.3)

\displaystyle yx=

\displaystyle\,\sigma^{-1}(x)y+\delta(x)=\rho xy+u-\rho\sigma(u).

He stated
[
25
, Section 1]
that not all noncommutative polynomial
\Bbbk
-algebras in three indeterminates considered by Bell and Smith
[
2
]
are iterated skew polynomial rings over
\Bbbk
, but these can be obtained from the construction in Definition
4.1
with
K=\Bbbk[t]
. Also, he mentioned that their objects are related with the class of iterated skew polynomial rings in two indeterminates defined by Goodearl and Letzter
[
21
]
, called
q
-
skew polynomial rings
, so that a natural task is to investigate the differential smoothness of both families of skew polynomial rings.
