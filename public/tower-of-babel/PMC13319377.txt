Kaempferol from Moringa oleifera L. increases longevity and ameliorates efavirenz-induced functional senescence in Drosophila melanogaster
Authors: Walter Mdekera Iorjiim, Luther Luka Makwin, Selumun Solomon Ipav, Simeon Omale
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Efavirenz acts against human immunodeficiency virus (HIV-1), induces functional senescence, and increases mortality in Drosophila melanogaster (Fruit fly). Kaempferol is a plant-derived flavonoid with neuroprotective and antioxidant properties. This study evaluated the protective effect of Kaempferol on efavirenz-induced functional senescence in D. melanogaster. Kaempferol was isolated from Moringa oleifera leaves. Adult flies (24–72 h old) were exposed to graded concentrations (1–1000 mg/10 g diet) to determine the median lethal concentration (LC₅₀). Sublethal doses (5–50 mg/10 g diet) were used to assess longevity, acute toxicity, fecundity, locomotor performance, and biochemical parameters. For protective evaluation, flies were co-treated with efavirenz (5 mg/10 g diet) and kaempferol (10 or 20 mg/10 g diet) for 7 days. Survival, climbing ability, fecundity, antioxidant biomarkers, and acetylcholinesterase activity were subsequently measured. Kaempferol showed an LC50 of 338.60 mg/10 g diet. Additionally, it significantly improved survival, climbing ability, and reproductive capacity compared to control flies (P < 0.05). It also enhanced the antioxidant status in flies, as evidenced by a significant (P < 0.05) increase in total thiol levels, as well as glutathione S-transferase (GST), catalase (CAT), and superoxide dismutase (SOD) activities, alongside a decrease in malondialdehyde (MDA) concentration. Furthermore, Kaempferol mitigated efavirenz-induced mortality, climbing weakness, reproductive decline, and deficits in acetylcholinesterase (AChE) activity (P < 0.05). Overall, this study demonstrated the chemoprotective potential of Kaempferol against efavirenz-induced mortality and functional senescence in D. melanogaster through antioxidative mechanisms. This, to the best of our knowledge, has not been reported before.

Introduction

Highly active antiretroviral drugs have extended the lifespan of people with HIV−1 infection. Paradoxically, the toxicities induced by these drugs remain a public health challenge [1], [2], [3]. Recently, our findings showed that efavirenz induces senescence in the fruit fly [4]. Functional senescence is characterized by a gradual loss in the normal operational integrity of cells, organs, systems, or the entire organism due to physiological aging, diseases, exposure to drugs, or other environmental toxins [5], [6]. Efavirenz is a potent non-nucleoside inhibitor of the HIV−1 reverse transcriptase enzyme [7]. Efavirenz-induced toxicity partly results from its interference with mitochondrial function and subsequent induction of oxidative stress, manifesting as deleterious changes in cellular, organ, and system functions at therapeutic doses [8]. Generally, efavirenz, administered orally, is absorbed into the systemic circulation within 6 h [9]. In the plasma, it is extensively metabolized by the cytochrome P450 enzyme (CYP2B6) to a more toxic byproduct, 8-hydroxy-EFV, which induces mitochondrial dysfunction, with concomitant initiation and propagation of oxidative stress signals [9], [10].

Oxidative stress involves an overproduction of reactive species, which can induce functional senescence, ultimately causing an organism’s death [11]. Reactive species, also called free radicals, are associated with the pathogenesis of many human diseases, such as diabetes, cancer, and physiological aging [12], [13], [14]. Additionally, oxidative stress induces reproductive impairment and developmental delay in animal models [14], [15]. While efavirenz is known to induce oxidative stress, pharmacological interventions to counteract these effects are scarcely available. In silico modeling of efavirenz’s interaction with Drosophila glutathione S-transferase suggested that it may compete with the enzyme, thereby hindering its antioxidant function [16].

The fruit fly (Drosophila melanogaster) is a popular model in toxicological studies because of the strong similarity that exists between its genes and vertebrates [17], [18]. For example, several key oxidative stress markers (like SOD and CAT), and certain genes implicated in disease processes in humans, including cancers and diabetes, are conserved in the fruit fly [19]. Moreover, D. melanogaster is an excellent model organism in the understanding of behavioral ecotoxicology, functional senescence, and the testing of new drug candidates [6], [20]. Furthermore, the Harwich strain of D. melanogaster shows high susceptibility to toxicants at all stages of its life cycle, making it an ideal model organism in the current study [21].

Recently, plant-based antioxidants have been studied as preventive and protective measures in experimental and epidemiological research to mitigate, inhibit, or manage the development of diseases associated with oxidative stress [22], [23]. M. oleifera, from the family Moringaceae, is well known for its antioxidant and nutritional properties [23], [24]. Furthermore, the potential of M. oleifera to counteract antiretroviral drug-induced oxidative stress through activation of nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor−2 (Nrf2), a key transcription factor that promotes the antioxidant response element (ARE), has been documented [25], [26]. However, the impact of Kaempferol (isolated from M. oleifera leaf) on efavirenz-induced functional senescence in D. melanogaster is scarcely available. Thus, this study aimed to investigate the protective role of Kaempferol, an isolated compound from M. oleifera leaf, against efavirenz-induced oxidative damage in the fruit fly.

Materials and methods

Chemicals, drug, and experimental model

Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), glutathione (GSH) 5,5-dithiobis 2-ni-trobenzoic acid (DTNB), 1-chloro−2, 4-dinitrobenzene (CDNB), dimethyl sulfoxide and acetylthiocholine iodide, were sourced from Sigma USA through the ACEPRD University of Jos, Jos. 30% Trichloroacetic acid (30%), 0.75%, Thiobarbituric acid (0,75%), carbonate buffer (0.05 M, pH 10.2), Tris- KCl buffer (0.15 M, pH 7.4), 0.3 M Adrenaline, and 0.1 M HCl, and were purchased from the Department of Biochemistry, National Veterinary Research Institute (NVRI), Vom, Jos Nigeria.

Efavirenz (600 mg/tablet, Cipla Ltd) was donated by the General Hospital, Gboko, Benue State, Nigeria. Ten (10) tablets were counted and weighed using an analytical balance (Meltlar–MT200B). The average weight was then calculated, and the tablets were crushed and smoothly powdered in a porcelain mortar. The required quantity of active efavirenz was determined relative to the total excipients and weighed out for experimental use. A 40 mg/mL stock of test drugs was prepared using 0.3% DMSO (dimethyl sulfoxide) to improve solubility. A concentration of 10 mg/10 g diet was selected for the current investigation based on our previous observed mortality [4]

The Harwich strain of D. melanogaster was obtained from the Africa Center of Excellence in Phytomedicine Research and Development (ACEPRD), University of Jos. The fruit flies were reared on a standard diet and environmental settings as established by Abolaji et al. [27]. Each cluster of experimental flies consisted of 50 young flies (not older than 3 days) in five independent replicates.

Isolation of Kaempferol from Moringa oleifera leaf

Moringa oleifera leaves were harvested from the wild at Dogo village, Buruku, Benue State, Nigeria. The leaves were washed with clean water, air-dried under a shed, and ground using a mortar and pestle. Isolation of Kaempferol was carried out following a modified method described by Farombi et al. [28]. First, the powdered Moringa leaves were defatted with petroleum ether, then extracted in 80% methanol. The extract was dried at room temperature (23–25 °C), diluted with distilled water to twice its original volume, and then isolated by ethyl acetate extraction. The ethyl acetate fraction was dried at room temperature to yield a yellow crystalline solid. The purity of the yellow powder was verified by thin-layer chromatography using silica gel GF 254-coated plates and a mobile phase of ethyl acetate:chloroform (9:1, v/v). Subsequently, the powder underwent Proton Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (HNMR) analysis to determine the identity of the isolate.

Determination of median lethal concentration (LC50) of Kaempferol in D. melanogaster

The median lethal concentration (LC50), defined as the concentration of a test substance in a standard fly diet that could trigger a 50% lethality in 7 days [29], was determined as previously described [16] with minor modifications. The LC50 determination was essential to guide the choice of experimental concentrations of Kaempferol, since some compounds exhibit hermetic efficacy in D. melanogaster [30]. Fruit flies (mixed sexes) were fed with food pre-mixed with ten varying concentrations of Kaempferol (1–1000 mg per 10 g diet) for seven (7) days. Mortality was recorded every 24 h, and the data obtained were imported into GraphPad Prism (version 8.0.2) to simulate a dose-response relationship and calculate an LC50. Similarly, the dose of efavirenz was chosen below 25% LC50 previously calculated [4].

Efavirenz exposure and Kaempferol treatment for climbing ability, fecundity, longevity and biochemical tests

The chosen concentrations of the active drug (efavirenz) and the fly diet were adjusted to account for excipients in the finished pharmaceutical product. A 7-day exposure plan was adopted for the current study based on our previous observations [4]. After a 7-day oral exposure to efavirenz or kaempferol, the climbing ability (negative geotaxis) of the exposed flies was determined using the method described by Adedara et al. [31]. Negative geotaxis is a standard behavioral assessment used to investigate neuromuscular senescence in D. melanogaster [5], [6]. Fecundity of the adult flies after the 7-day exposure to the test drugs and Kaempferol was investigated using a published protocol [32]. The number of offspring hatched during the 14-day experimental period was counted every 24 h, and total eclosion reflected the reproductive ability of the exposed flies. Decreased fecundity during oxidative stress conditions suggests reproductive senescence in D. melanogaster [33], which we previously observed in efavirenz-exposed flies [4]. The 7-day exposure plan was extended indefinitely until the last fly died, as described by Farombi et al. [28] to investigate the chronic effects of kaempferol on longevity.

Biochemicals assays

Biochemical parameters were evaluated in the current study because several metabolic and subcellular processes undergo age-like functional senescence under stress conditions in D. melanogaster [6]. For ex vivo biochemical tests, 50 clusters of fruit flies per control or treated group in plastic vials (12 cm diameter, 15 cm height) were anesthetized on ice. Then, weighed and homogenized in a 0.1 M potassium phosphate buffer solution (pH 7.4, flies: volume = 1 g:10 μL), and centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 10 min at −4 °C. The supernatants were separated from debris into Eppendorf tubes and stored at −20 °C for evaluation of biochemical parameters, including total protein, total thiol, and malondialdehyde levels, as well as the enzymatic activities of acetylcholinesterase (AChE), glutathione S-transferase (GST), superoxide dismutase, and catalase. All the test parameters investigated in the current study were previously susceptible to Efavirenz toxicity [4].

Total protein levels in fly homogenates were measured and used to normalize other biochemical parameters. The protein content of fly homogenates was measured using Bradford’s technique with the Randox protein kit, as described by Omale et al. [34]. Briefly, the Randox reagents were thoroughly mixed and then incubated at room temperature (23–25 °C) for 30 mins. The absorbances was measured at 546 nm using a spectrophotometer (Jenway Model No. 7315). To establish a standard calibration curve, absorbance values were plotted against concentrations to generate a linear equation (y = mx+c) for the quantification of the total protein per sample. Subsequently, the test samples were also mixed with the Randox reagents, and the same procedure followed for the calibration curve was repeated. Finally, the total proteins were quantified using the formula x = y−cm

The total thiol level was assessed using Ellman’s method as reported by Abolaji et al. [27]

with some modifications. The reaction mixture consisted of 25 µl of fly homogenate, 510 µl of potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.4), 30 µl of DTNB, and 30 µl of GSH, bringing the total volume to 600 µl. The mixture was kept at room temperature (23–25 °C) for 30 min. Finally, the absorbance was measured at 412 nm. A standard curve was prepared using glutathione (GSH) and used to determine the total thiol content, expressed per mg of protein.

The activity of GST was determined by the method of Habig and Jakoby [27] utilizing 1-chloro−2,4-dinitrobenzene (CDNB) as substrate. The total reaction mixture was 270 μL, consisting of 20 μL of 0.25 M potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0), 2.5 mM EDTA, 10.5 μL of distilled water, and 500 μL of 0.1 M GSH at room temperature (23–25 °C). Then, 10 μL of 25 mM CDNB and 20 μL of sample (diluted at 1:5) were added. The reaction mixture was observed every 10 s for 5 min in a spectrophotometer at 340 nm. The calculated GST activity is expressed as µmol/min/mg protein.

The SOD activity of fly homogenate was determined following Misra and Fridovich’s method described by Iorjiim et al. [35]. The reaction mixture and absorbance readings used to calculate SOD activity were prepared and measured as described by Iorjiim et al. [35]. SOD activity is presented in µmol/min/mg protein.

The CAT activity was evaluated as described by Omale et al. [34] with some modification. A fresh reaction mixture (labeled solution A) consisted of 100 µL of potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) and 194 µL of 300 mM H2O2. Exactly 10 µL of the sample was pre-mixed in solution A (590 µL). The disappearance of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) was quickly measured at 240 nm (10 s interval for 2 min) using a spectrophotometer (Jenway Model No. 7315), and expressed in µmol of H2O2 consumed/min/mg of protein.

The activity of AChE was evaluated using Ellman’s method described by Farombi et al. [28] with a few modifications. The reaction medium consisted of freshly prepared distilled water (285 µƖ), potassium phosphate buffer (180 µƖ, pH 7.4), DTNB (60 µƖ), fly sample (15 µƖ), and acetylcholine (60 µƖ). The absorbance was monitored for 2 min (at intervals of 15 s) at 412 nm. The activity of AChE was calculated and expressed in μmol/min/mg protein.

The level of malondialdehyde (MDA) was evaluated using the methods of Varshney & Kale [35] and utilized as a measure of free radical generation in D. melanogaster. It was useful in forecasting the overall oxidant/antioxidant status in the model organism. The composition of the reaction mixture and the wavelength for absorbance measurement to calculate the MDA level in fly homogenate were as previously reported [35], using a molar extinction coefficient of 1.56 × 105 M−1 cm−1. The MDA level is expressed as µmol MDA/mg protein.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism (version 8.0.2 for Windows) and presented as Mean ± SEM. The statistical analysis employed a one-way ANOVA (analysis of variance) with Tukey’s post hoc test to identify significant differences at P < 0.05. The Log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test was used to analyze survival curves. P-values in this case were adjusted using a Bonferroni-adjusted threshold: P−valueK. K was taken as 4 comparisons with statistical significance at P < 0.01.

Discussion

Recent decades have seen a rise in concerns over the toxicities of antiretrovirals at clinical doses [38], [39]. Single drug substitution or regimen switching are the main clinical approaches that have not fully addressed the menace of antiretroviral drug-induced toxicities [40]. There is, therefore, a need to develop pharmacologic interventions against antiretrovirals using animal models. Currently, D. melanogaster is widely used to obtain mechanistic insights into the pathophysiology of diseases induced by environmental toxicants. The fruit fly is also used as a model for phytochemical screening [28]. Kaempferol, isolated from M. oleifera leaf in the current study (Fig. 1), is a flavonoid that is likewise abundant in other vegetables and fruits [24]. Reports have shown that Kempferol from M. oleifera exhibited strong anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties [24], [41]. Moreover, it has been used to protect the fruit fly against the motor impairment induced by exposure to the insecticide, fipronil [20]. In this study, we evaluated the ameliorative role of Kaempferol (from M. oleifera leaf) on Efavirenz-induced toxicity in D. melanogaster. To our knowledge, this is the first report demonstrating that kaempferol prolongs lifespan and attenuates efavirenz-induced behavioral dysfunction and oxidative stress–mediated toxicity in D. melanogaster.

Several compounds are beneficial as lifespan-extending agents at low doses, but lethal at high doses [30]. Thus, we first administered a range of Kaempferol concentrations (1– 1000 mg/10 g diet) to determine its LC50 in the fruit fly. The result (Fig. 2) showed the hermetic efficacy of Kaempferol, as it prevented mortality at low doses but exhibited lethality at concentrations above 350 mg/10 g diet in D. melanogaster. Sub-toxic concentrations of Kaempferol are suggested to induce a transient, mild disturbance in the cellular redox state, resulting in the accumulation of Nrf2 in the nucleus. Under oxidative stress, Nrf2 separates from its cytoplasmic repressor Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1(Keap1) and moves into the nucleus, where it attaches to antioxidant response elements (ARE) to upregulate antioxidant and detoxifying genes like SOD, CAT, and GST [25], [26]. These findings are consistent with the report by Chattopadhyay et al. [30], where an antioxidant phytocompound, Rutin, exhibited lethality at concentrations above 400 µM, but extended Drosophila lifespan at 200 µM. The current results suggest that M. oleifera leaves should be used with caution in traditional medical practice. Oral exposure to Kaempferol extended fly lifespan in the current study (Fig. 3), in agreement with reports that consumption of antioxidant-rich diets could extend lifespan in organisms [27], [28]. Therefore, the lifespan-extending role of Kaempferol might be attributed to its antioxidant effects. The 20 mg/10 g diet of Kaempferol resulted in the longest lifespan in flies compared with the other doses (5, 10, and 15 mg/10 g). Put together, the results of the LC50 (Fig. 2), longevity (Fig. 3), and acute mortality (Fig. 4A) assays suggest that Kaempferol exhibits hermetic efficacy, evidenced by its safety and life-extending ability at low concentrations, but lethality at doses greater than 350 mg/10 g diet in a Harwich strain D. melanogaster.

Flies treated with Kaempferol alone (Fig. 4B) significantly (P < 0.05) improved climbing abilities at all concentrations with no effect on AChE (Fig. 5A), while Efavirenz-treated flies exhibited a climbing deficit, through the impairment of negative geotaxis (Fig. 6B) with a concomitant reduction in AChE activity (Fig. 7A). AChE is the enzyme that metabolises acetylcholine, a neurotransmitter that regulates motor activity and movement [28]. Studies have shown that a reduction in Drosophila AChE activity correlates positively with climbing deficits [42], [43]. Thus, a significant (P < 0.05) reduction in AChE activity, as observed in the current study, might result in the accumulation of acetylcholine, leading to a prolonged firing of electrical signals and eventual movement impairment due to paralysis of fly muscles. In this context, the Efavirenz-induced deficits in AChE activity could explain the corresponding climbing failures in fruit flies. In the same vein, the rescuing role of Kaempferol against Efavirenz-induced climbing deficits (Fig. 6B) could be attributed to its stabilizing effect on AChE activity (Figs. 5A and 7A).

Efavirenz significantly (P < 0.05) reduced offspring emergence, a reduction modified by co-treatment with Kaempferol (Fig. 6C). Studies linking reductions in AChE activity and the induction of oxidative stress to declines in fertility rates have been reported [44], [45], [46]. For example, AChE inhibitors used as insecticides were linked to increased oxidative stress, diminished spermatogenesis, and sperm quality in exposed flies [44]. Beyond the cholinergic axis, the elevated MDA observed in efavirenz-exposed flies indicates lipid peroxidation, which may have affected reproductive tissues, including germline and follicle cells, whose membrane integrity is critical for oocyte maturation and embryonic development [45], [46].

These findings have triggered a scientific hypothesis that antioxidants could mitigate oxidative stress-induced infertility [46]. Notably, Kaempferol in the current study rescued fruit flies from efavirenz-induced reduction in AChE activity and reproductive deficits via its antioxidant activity.

Efavirenz is metabolized by CYP2B6 to its primary hydroxylated metabolite, 8-hydroxy-efavirenz (8-OH-EFV), which is more cytotoxic than the parent drug and drives mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress [8], [10]. In the mitochondria, impaired electron transport chain function leads to uncoupled superoxide generation, which overwhelms enzymatic antioxidant defenses. Since Efavirenz is a known inducer of oxidative stress [4], oxidative stress markers and antioxidant status in Efavirenz-exposed fruit flies and control groups were evaluated to assess the rescue role of Kaempferol. The results showed that Efavirenz significantly (P < 0.05) depleted T-SH levels (Fig. 7B) and reduced the activities of GST (Fig. 7C), SOD (Fig. 7D), and CAT (Fig. 7E) compared to the control flies. Efavirenz’s mechanism of antioxidant suppression involves a dual impairment of the thiol–GST detoxification axis. As previously demonstrated by in silico modeling, efavirenz competes with the physiological substrate for the active site of Drosophila GST [16], effectively blocking the enzyme. Simultaneously, reactive metabolites of efavirenz consume cellular thiols as conjugation substrates, thereby depleting the total thiol pool. Total thiols contain at least a sulfhydryl (-SH) group in their chemical structures, which, with the help of the glutathione S-transferase enzyme, conjugates free radicals as a first-line defense against oxidative stress in living systems [47]. Similarly, GSTs play important roles in regulating several processes necessary for the survival of organisms under stress [28]. Furthermore, superoxide dismutase facilitates the conversion of two superoxide ions (2 O2•−) to hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) and free oxygen (O2). Catalase, on the other hand, catalyzes the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide into water and molecular oxygen, thereby reducing the risk of hydroxyl radical formation via the Fenton reaction [48]. The inhibitory effect of Efavirenz observed in this study on T-SH levels (Fig. 7B), as well as GST (Fig. 7C), SOD (Fig. 7D), and CAT (Fig. 7E) activities in D. melanogaster strongly suggests increased susceptibility to oxidative stress and a compromised antioxidative status in the fruit flies. Interestingly, all the observed antioxidant deficits were ameliorated in flies co-treated with Kaempferol and Efavirenz, thereby confirming Kaempferol’s antioxidative efficacy in flies.

The ability of Kaempferol to prevent free radical generation was investigated by measuring MDA levels in D. melanogaster exposed to efavirenz, compared with the control. MDA is a genotoxic product of free radical-induced lipid peroxidation, which is used as an indirect measure of free radical generation in vivo [49], [50]. In the current results (Fig. 7F), efavirenz increased MDA levels concomitantly with reductions in antioxidant markers: T-SH (Fig. 7B), GST (Fig. 7C), SOD (Fig. 7D), and CAT (Fig. 7E). These findings agree with a previous report that HAART drugs induced MDA elevation in rodents [51]. Taken together, the current results suggest severe oxidative stress-induced toxicity in fruit flies. The result also showed that Kaempferol rescued the efavirenz-induced MDA elevation dose dependently (Fig. 7F). The antioxidant properties and protective activity of Kaempferol against other environmental toxins, similar to its current evidence in efavirenz-induced oxidative stress toxicity, have been reported in D. melanogaster [20], [52].

We therefore proposed a rescue mechanism of kaempferol against efavirenz-induced oxidative injury as follows: kaempferol co-treatment reduces MDA levels (Fig. 7F), indicating protection of mitochondrial and cellular membrane lipids from peroxidative damage. Elevated MDA levels indicate free radical-induced membrane disruption and serve as a marker of HAART-induced oxidative stress in animal models [51]. The dose-dependent reduction in MDA by kaempferol suggests that its antioxidant activity inhibits lipid peroxidation through direct radical scavenging. This further restores AChE stability and activity, normalizing cholinergic neurotransmission and improving climbing. Similar findings in D. melanogaster showed that antioxidants restored AChE activity and locomotion [42], [43]. In another report, Kaempferol improved fipronil-induced climbing deficits in fruit flies at doses below 30 mg [20].

Furthermore, our current findings suggest that kaempferol protects efavirenz-exposed D. melanogaster by activating the Nrf2–ARE pathway, thereby boosting the biosynthesis of thiols and the enzymatic antioxidant upregulation of GST, SOD, and CAT. This proposed pathway aligns with evidence that kaempferol activates Nrf2 to reduce oxidative injury in macrophages [52] and that M. oleifera stimulates Nrf2 and attenuates antiretroviral toxicity in human liver cells [26]. Thus, Kaempferol could be a vital therapeutic candidate for mitigating the risks of antiretroviral drug-induced oxidative stress toxicities. Undeniably, this report may spark researchers’ curiosity about other phytonutrients with antioxidant properties as potential therapeutic agents against oxidative stress toxicities induced by antiretroviral drugs.

Conclusion

Kaempferol from the ethyl acetate fraction of M. oleifera leaves increased longevity in D. melanogaster and ameliorated efavirenz-induced functional senescence by attenuating oxidative stress (Scheme 1). The protective effects of Kaempferol are partly associated with its antioxidant and free-radical-scavenging properties. Therefore, Kaempferol may serve as a therapeutic agent for efavirenz-induced oxidative stress toxicities.
