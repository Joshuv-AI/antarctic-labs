Replicable Composition
Kiarash Banihashem, MohammadHossein Bateni, Hossein Esfandiari, Samira Goudarzi, MohammadTaghi Hajiaghayi

Abstract
Replicability, the requirement that algorithmic conclusions remain consistent when run on independently drawn stochastic data, is a cornerstone of reliable data analysis and has become a central topic in theoretical computer science. A key structural question in this context is composition . Specifically, given k k problems each admitting a ρ \rho -replicable algorithm with sample complexity n n , how many samples are needed to solve all of them jointly while maintaining replicability? The naive composition analysis yields O ~ ​ ( n ​ k 2 ) \widetilde{O}(nk^{2}) samples, and Bun et al. (STOC’23) further observed that reductions through differential privacy imply an alternative O ~ ​ ( n 2 ​ k ) \widetilde{O}(n^{2}k) bound, leaving open whether the optimal O ~ ​ ( n ​ k ) \widetilde{O}(nk) scaling could be achieved. In this work, we resolve this open problem and, more generally, show that problems with sample complexities n 1 , … , n k n_{1},\ldots,n_{k} can be jointly solved with O ~ ​ ( ∑ i n i ) \widetilde{O}(\sum_{i}n_{i}) samples while preserving constant replicability. Our approach converts each replicable algorithm into a perfectly generalizing one, composes them in that space using a privacy-style analysis, and then maps the result back to a replicable algorithm via correlated sampling. The choice of perfectly generalizing algorithms and suitable parameters is crucial for obtaining a tight result. This yields the first advanced composition theorem for replicability , achieving a nearly linear dependence on n n and k k , which is approximately tight. As a direct application, we show that existing ad hoc multi-problem analyses from prior work can be unified under a single general framework. En route, we obtain new bounds for the composition of perfectly generalizing algorithms: the composition of k k such algorithms with parameters ( ε i , δ i ) (\varepsilon_{i},\delta_{i}) is perfectly generalizing with parameters ( ε ∗ , δ ∗ ) (\varepsilon^{*},\delta^{*}) , where ε ∗ ≈ ∑ i ε i 2 \varepsilon^{*}\approx\sqrt{\sum_{i}\varepsilon_{i}^{2}} and δ ∗ \delta^{*} can be made arbitrarily small at only a polylogarithmic cost in sample complexity. As part of our study, we provide a new boosting theorem for increasing the success probability of a replicable algorithm which may be of independent interest. For a broad class of problems, we show that a low failure probability can be achieved by splitting the dataset into a replicable and a non-replicable part, with only the latter requiring low failure probability. This makes the failure probability appear in a separate additive term independent of ρ \rho , immediately yielding improved bounds for several problems studied in the literature, including statistical query estimation, various PAC learning settings (such as finite classes, thresholds, and bounded Littlestone dimension), and the heavy-hitters problem. Finally, we ask whether the linear scaling of our composition theorem extends to adaptive adversaries who can base the problems they pose, as well as the distribution of those problems, on previous answers. We show that it does not: any algorithm solving k k problems in the adaptive setting requires Ω ⁡ ( n ​ k 2 ) \Omega(nk^{2}) samples, establishing a quadratic separation between the adaptive and non-adaptive settings. We use the key technique behind our lower bound, which we refer to as the phantom run , to show structural results for replicable algorithms which are of independent interest

Introduction

Replicability
aims to ensure that algorithmic conclusions are stable under dataset resampling, which is central to the credibility of empirical science. Informally, an algorithm is replicable if, when rerun on a fresh i.i.d. sample from the same distribution but with the same internal random bits, it produces the same output with high probability (see
Definition

2
).
The notion was introduced by Impagliazzo, Lei, Pitassi, and Sorrell
[
27
]

and has since motivated a growing line of research on stability in statistical estimation, learning theory, and data analysis.
Subsequent papers have explored its relationships with other notions of stability and applied the notion to different statistical problems
[
8
,
11
,
10
,
25
,
4
,
1
,
32
,
6
]
.
Together, these results position replicability as a unifying stability concept that connects statistical reliability with algorithmic design.

Differential Privacy (DP) is a framework for protecting individual privacy, but it also implies a strong form of algorithmic stability. In DP, stability arises as a consequence of its indistinguishability guarantee—outputs remain nearly unchanged under small perturbations of the input dataset. In replicability, by contrast, stability is captured through invariance of outputs under resampling from the same distribution. This parallel motivates an analogous question for replicability that mirrors a central theme in DP:
composition
. Suppose there are
k
statistical problems, each solvable by a
\rho
-replicable algorithm using
n
samples. What is the sample complexity required to solve all
k
problems jointly while preserving replicability of the combined output?

Open Question (Bun et
al., STOC’23
[
8
]
).

Given
k
problems, each solvable with
n
samples by a
\rho
-replicable algorithm, can one design a
\rho
-replicable algorithm that solves all
k
problems simultaneously with sample complexity
\widetilde{O}(nk)
?

The authors asked this question for both adaptive and non-adaptive composition.
We answer this open question in the affirmative for non-adaptive composition, and show that the picture changes fundamentally when adaptivity is allowed.
Specifically, for heterogeneous sample complexities
n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}
, we give a non-adaptive composition scheme that is
\rho
-replicable and uses
\widetilde{O}\big(\sum_{i=1}^{k}n_{i}\big)
samples, matching the natural linear scaling in
k
.
We believe this result is of fundamental importance: composition is a core structural question for any stability notion, and our theorem provides a simple, general principle that can be applied across problems.
Indeed, we show that existing problem-specific analyses from prior work can be replaced by our general framework, turning ad hoc multi-problem arguments into a standard, unified approach.

For adaptive composition, the situation is fundamentally different: we prove an
\Omega(nk^{2})
lower bound, showing that one cannot improve on naive composition.
The key distinction is how replicability errors — the events where two runs on fresh samples diverge — interact across algorithms.
In the adaptive setting, each algorithm runs without knowledge of future algorithms and must commit to its output before subsequent ones run, forcing the replicability errors of individual algorithms to behave independently; independent errors compound.
In the non-adaptive setting, algorithms run in coordination, allowing them to correlate their replicability errors rather than accumulate them independently — which is precisely what enables the
\widetilde{O}(\sum_{i}n_{i})
bound.

Connection to differential privacy.

In differential privacy, composition quantifies how privacy loss accumulates
when multiple analyses are performed using private algorithms. The naive
composition bound scales linearly in the number of analyses, but the

advanced composition theorem

[
17
]
(FOCS’10) shows a
sublinear
\sqrt{k}
dependence—a property that is widely used in DP. This
shift serves as the guiding analogy for our work: we seek the replicability
counterpart of advanced composition. In replicability, if one simply runs the

k
algorithms together, the replicability parameter degrades linearly as

O(\rho k)
, and boosting this parameter to maintain constant replicability
leads to a total sample complexity of
\widetilde{O}(nk^{2})
(see

Section

8.1
). Bun et al.
[
8
]
further pointed
out that through reductions to DP, one can obtain an alternative bound of

\widetilde{O}(n^{2}k)
, and left open whether the optimal
\widetilde{O}(nk)
scaling
could be achieved. Our non-adaptive composition theorem resolves this question.
In this sense, our theorem can be viewed
as an
advanced composition theorem for replicability
that also uncovers
a fundamental structural distinction from differential privacy.
Notably, however, the adaptive lower bound reveals a separation with no
counterpart in DP: while advanced composition in DP applies regardless of
adaptivity, in replicability the
\widetilde{O}(nk)
bound is provably
unachievable in the adaptive setting.

Structural properties of replicable algorithms.

The proof of our adaptive lower bound relies on a technique we call the
phantom run
: a third independent execution of the algorithm used as an intermediate between the two standard runs.
Beyond the lower bound, this technique has broader applicability: any replicable algorithm can be converted into one whose output depends on the input only through a sufficient statistic of the distributional family (
Section

6.1
), with replicability preserved for distributions in
\mathcal{F}
; this implies in particular that any replicable algorithm can be made
order-invariant
(its output is invariant to the ordering of the input sample).
Extending our techniques, we further show that for symmetric problems, any replicable algorithm can be made
label-invariant
, meaning its output distribution is invariant to relabelings of the domain.

These properties first became of interest to the community through the lower
bound of
[
35
]
, who studied uniformity testing, proved lower bounds for label-invariant
testers, and asked whether the restriction to this class was
necessary.

[
1
]
established that order-invariance and label invariance are without loss of generality in more restricted settings, confined to binary hypothesis testing; they additionally showed that for hypothesis testing, any replicable algorithm can be converted to one that depends on a sufficient statistic, with replicability guaranteed for distributions within the parametric family.
Our approach establishes label invariance for any symmetric problem; our sufficient statistic result holds likewise, for any problem.

Boosting success probability.

To complement our composition theorem, we further develop a framework for
boosting the success probability of replicable algorithms, which may be of
independent interest (see the discussion surrounding
Theorem

1
for the
motivation behind studying this question).
For a broad class of algorithms, we show that the success probability can be boosted at a very small cost: in most cases the dependence on the failure probability is no worse than the corresponding non-replicable version of the problem and appears as a separate additive term independent of
\rho
. Our framework immediately improves the sample complexity of several replicable algorithms obtained in prior work.

Our Results

2.1
Replicable Composition

Our main result is the following theorem which bounds the sample complexity of the composition of
k
statistical problems; i.e., solving all problems simultaneously (see
Section

4
for a formal definition of statistical problem and composition).

Theorem 1

(Main Theorem)
.

Suppose algorithms
\mathcal{A}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{A}_{k}
are each
0.0001
-replicable and solve statistical problems
\mathcal{T}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{T}_{k}
, respectively, where
\mathcal{A}_{i}
has sample complexity
n_{i}
, failure probability at most
\beta_{0}
, and its output is supported over a finite space. Then, for any
\rho>0
, there exists a
\rho
-replicable algorithm that solves the composed problem
\mathcal{T}^{*}=(\mathcal{T}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{T}_{k})
with failure probability at most
\beta
and sample complexity
n
, where

\displaystyle n=O\left(\frac{\sum_{j}n_{j}}{\rho^{2}}\operatorname{polylog}\left(\frac{k}{\rho\beta_{0}}\right)\right),\qquad\beta=O\left(k\sqrt{\beta_{0}}\log\frac{k}{\rho\beta_{0}}\right).

(1)

This theorem matches the natural linear target in
k
and resolves the open question of Bun et al.
[
8
]
.
We note that linear dependence on
n
and
k
is essentially necessary and one cannot, e.g., obtain
O(n^{0.99}k)
or
O(nk^{0.99})
dependence; see
Section

8.2
for a full proof.
We note that the assumption that the output of
\mathcal{A}_{i}
is supported over a finite space is mild as, in most cases, one can discretize the space and round the output to the closest answer to ensure the guarantee (see
Section

4
for a more detailed discussion).

The increase in failure probability is relatively mild and broadly consistent with what occurs in the context of differential privacy: when composing
k
differentially private algorithms—each with failure probability
\beta_{0}
—the overall failure probability increases to
O(k\beta_{0})
under independence. In our setting, the bound in
Theorem

1
is slightly worse, scaling as
O(k\sqrt{\beta_{0}}\log(\cdot))
, but this is still a gentle growth in most regimes.
The increase motivates a natural question however:
how does the sample complexity scale with the success probability.
We study this question in
Section

2.3
, providing a general framework for boosting the success probability that applies for a broad class of problems. In particular, for many problems we show a logarithmic dependence on
1/\beta_{0}
.
This in turn means that boosting
\beta_{0}
by replacing it with
(\beta_{0}/k)^{2}
causes only a logarithmic increase in sample complexity.

Why naive composition fails.

A natural starting point is to simply run all
k
algorithms in parallel with shared randomness.
Since each component is
\rho
-replicable, a naive union bound implies a joint replicability parameter grows linearly as
O(\rho k)
.
In general, this bound is tight up to constants.
Intuitively, if the algorithms are solving
“independent”
problems, then their replicability errors are also independent and as such, the probability that at least one algorithm changes its output under resampling scales with
\Omega(k\rho)
(see
Section

8.1
for a formal proof).
This behavior is in sharp contrast to differential privacy, where advanced composition follows from a refined
analysis
of the same combined algorithm.
For replicability, no such analysis improvement is possible—the naive construction is inherently limited—so we must instead design a new composition scheme.

Our approach.

Our construction proceeds in three steps.
First, we convert each replicable algorithm
\mathcal{A}_{i}
into a
perfectly generalizing
(PG) algorithm
\mathcal{B}_{i}
with carefully chosen parameters
(\varepsilon_{i},\delta_{i})
.
Second, we compose the PG algorithms.
Finally, we transform the result back into a replicable algorithm using correlated sampling.
An algorithm
\mathcal{A}
is
(\gamma,\varepsilon,\delta)
-PG if for every distribution
D
there exists a
“simulated”
distribution
\texttt{sim}_{D}
such that,
with probability
1-\gamma

over the sample
S\sim D^{n}
,
the distribution of
\mathcal{A}(S)
satisfies
the following
for all measurable
O
:

\displaystyle e^{-\varepsilon}(\textnormal{Pr}_{\texttt{sim}_{D}}\left[O\right]-\delta)\leq{\textnormal{Pr}\left[\mathcal{A}(S)\in O\right]}\leq e^{\varepsilon}\textnormal{Pr}_{\texttt{sim}_{D}}\left[O\right]+\delta.

This notion parallels differential privacy: both constrain multiplicative deviations in output probabilities with a possibility for a small additive error.
However, while differential privacy guarantees stability under pointwise perturbations of the dataset, perfect generalization ensures stability under resampling from the underlying distribution.

Why perfect generalization is the right bridge.

While the idea of transforming to an intermediate stability notion is crucial, the choice of this notion is even more so
as other natural alternatives are not suitable.
Differential privacy, though powerful, is too strong: converting between DP and replicability introduces a quadratic blow-up in sample complexity (
n\mapsto n^{2}
).
Intuitively, DP constrains log-probability ratios for
neighboring datasets
, while replicability concerns resampling under shared randomness—bridging the two loses a quadratic factor.
Moreover, in general there is no reverse improvement from
n
to
\sqrt{n}
when going from replicability to DP, so DP cannot yield an optimal composition result.

A seemingly closer notion is
TV-indistinguishability
.
In a sense, it captures replicability without requiring the outputs to be synchronized: it demands that for two independent samples
S^{(1)},S^{(2)}
, the expected total-variation distance between
\mathcal{A}(S^{(1)})
and
\mathcal{A}(S^{(2)})
be at most
\rho
.
This notion can be thought of as a special case of perfect generalization with
\varepsilon=0
and is very close to replicability; indeed, one can
“sync”
its outputs through correlated sampling.
However, it is unclear how to bound the accumulation of errors of composed TV-indistinguishable algorithms beyond the naive analysis and as such we need to use a different approach.

Perfect generalization occupies a well-defined position precisely between these two extremes.
Like differential privacy, it constrains the
log-probability ratio
of
output events, thereby permitting a concentration-based analysis akin to that
used in the advanced composition theorem for DP: instead of a standard linear
scaling of
\sum_{i}\varepsilon_{i}
we obtain an improved scaling of
\sqrt{\sum_{i}\varepsilon_{i}^{2}}
for the multiplicative factor (see
Theorem

2
). Importantly, the existing
transformation from replicability to perfect generalization allows us to choose
the parameter
\delta_{i}
to be very small at only a polylogarithmic cost in the
sample complexity.
Since the parameters
\delta_{i}
add up, this choice ensures that even cumulatively, they do not have a large effect.
Concretely, setting
\varepsilon_{i}\approx\rho\sqrt{n_{i}/\sum_{j}n_{j}}
(see
Equation

5
) ensures
\sum_{i}\varepsilon_{i}^{2}\approx\rho^{2}
, and by
Lemma

1
, the cost of converting
\mathcal{A}_{i}
to a PG algorithm scales as
\widetilde{O}(n_{i}/\varepsilon_{i}^{2})\approx\widetilde{O}(\sum_{j}n_{j}/\rho^{2})
, yielding the linear dependence in
Theorem

1
.

Composition of perfectly generalizing algorithms.

As part of our approach, we generalize existing results for the composition of perfectly generalizing algorithms.
We state an informal version of our result below and refer to
Theorem

6
for more details. On a high level, ignoring
\delta_{i}
, our result shows that the composition of
k
algorithms with parameters
\varepsilon_{i}
is also perfectly generalizing with parameter
\sqrt{\sum_{i}\varepsilon_{i}^{2}}
.

Theorem 2

(Informal composition theorem)
.

Assume each algorithm
\mathcal{A}_{i}
is
(\delta_{i},\varepsilon_{i},\delta_{i})
-PG with small enough
\varepsilon_{i}
and
\delta_{i}\ll\varepsilon_{i}
.
Then for any
\delta^{\prime}\in(0,1/2)
, their composition is
(\delta^{*},\varepsilon^{*},\delta^{*})
-PG, where

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{*}=O\Big(\sqrt{\log(1/\delta^{\prime})\sum_{i}\varepsilon_{i}^{2}}+\sum_{i}\varepsilon_{i}^{2}\Big),\quad\delta^{*}=O\Big(\sqrt{\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{*}}\Big(k\delta^{\prime}+\sum_{i}\frac{\delta_{i}}{\varepsilon_{i}}\Big)}\Big).

The above theorem also holds in the adaptive setting; see
Section

5.3
for a formal definition of the setting and
Lemma

2
for the precise statement.
Our proof builds on the existing result of
[
5
]
who study the more general concept of typical stability but focus on the special case of homogeneous parameters.

1

The proof of
[
5
]
has a subtle issue in the adaptive setting—specifically, certain objects are named in a way that is inconsistent with what the proof requires, making the argument difficult to verify as written.
We identify and correct this issue in

and prove our extension to heterogeneous parameters in

.
We note that the existence of an adaptive result is perhaps surprising: the closely related concept of max-information under product distributions does not compose adaptively (see Theorem 4.1 in
[

), and conditioning on the output of a previous algorithm means the input distribution is no longer i.i.d., making it a priori unclear whether PG composition can handle adaptivity.
A high-level sketch of how this conditioning issue is sidestepped appears after
Lemma

2
.
We emphasize that the non-adaptive version of
Theorem

2

already suffices for
Theorem

1
; the adaptive result is included
because it is a natural question one may ask about PG composition, and—as
the above discussion suggests—the answer is not obvious.
While we do not pursue this here, one may obtain a simpler proof for the non-adaptive case.
We also note that the adaptive PG composition theorem does not make
Theorem

1
adaptive in any way: even though the PG algorithms themselves can be adaptively composed, the transformation back to a replicable algorithm requires knowledge of all algorithms up-front.

On a high level, the main intuition behind the proof is similar to that of advanced composition in DP.
Specifically, when composing algorithms with parameters
(\varepsilon_{i},\delta_{i})
, we look at the logarithm of the probability ratio of a value under the algorithm compared to the simulator.
The expected contribution of each algorithm to the overall instability scales as
\varepsilon_{i}^{2}
, rather than linearly in
\varepsilon_{i}
. While the
“worst-case”
contribution is still
\varepsilon_{i}
, using concentration one can show that
the cumulative
\varepsilon
parameter grows as
\sqrt{\sum_{i}\varepsilon_{i}^{2}}
—exactly paralleling the behavior established by advanced composition in differential privacy.
Two additional complications arise. First, the PG condition only guarantees closeness with probability
1-\gamma_{i}
, so the bad events where the bound fails must be tracked carefully as they accumulate across rounds. Second, in the adaptive case, conditioning on previous outputs means the distribution of samples is no longer i.i.d., introducing dependencies that require a more involved inductive argument.
Concretely, we first show that (with high probability) the values generated by the simulator satisfy the desired probability ratio bounds; since the simulator is independent of the input, it does not suffer from the same i.i.d. issue.
We then use the fact that the simulator and the algorithm are close to each
other—a fact that follows inductively from the argument applied to the first

i-1
rounds—to show that the values of the algorithm satisfy the probability ratio bounds as well.
We refer to the discussion following
Lemma

2
for a more detailed overview with the key definitions and notation.

2.2
Lower Bound for Adaptive Composition

While
Theorem

1
shows that non-adaptive composition can be achieved with nearly linear sample complexity,
a natural follow-up question is whether this guarantee extends to the
adaptive
setting,
where the problems posed in later rounds may depend on the outputs of earlier ones and may be based on new distributions.
We show that the answer is no: adaptive composition necessarily incurs a quadratic cost in
k
.
Formally, we consider an adaptive game (
Definition

4
)
similar to that of
[
17
]
(see Section III.A in their paper)
in which a
random adversary poses
k
statistical problems sequentially, each potentially
depending on previous outputs and potentially on a new distribution.
The algorithm must solve each problem replicably using fresh samples at every round.

Theorem 3

(Informal; see
Theorem

10
)
.

Any algorithm that is
\rho
-replicable in the adaptive
k
-round game and answers all rounds correctly with constant probability requires sample complexity
m=\Omega(nk^{2})
, where
n
is the single-round sample complexity.

This establishes a quadratic separation between adaptive and non-adaptive composition:
the upper bound of
Theorem

1
achieves
\widetilde{O}(nk)
, while the lower bound shows
\Omega(nk^{2})
is necessary for adaptive adversaries.
Note that the lower bound is tight: boosting the replicability parameter of each round from
\rho
to
\rho/k
and applying naive composition yields an
O(nk^{2})
algorithm.

Proof sketch.

Our starting point is the hard instance of replicability posed by
[
27
]

(see Lemma 7.2 in their paper):
any algorithm that takes
m
samples from
\operatorname{Bernoulli}(\theta)
and must decide whether
\theta
is above or below
1/2
(outputting
+
when
\theta=1/2+\tau
, outputting
-
when
\theta=1/2-\tau
, and either when
|\theta-1/2|<\tau
) has replicability parameter
\geq\Omega(1/(\tau\sqrt{m}))
.
On a high level, the goal is to repeatedly present instances of this problem to the algorithm and
show that the replicability errors are
independent
and therefore compound:
two different runs with different sample sets should diverge at
some point
with probability at least

\Omega(k/\tau\sqrt{m})
, implying the desired bound.
It is not a priori clear how to show this though given that the algorithm’s randomness can cause the errors to correlate;
indeed, correlating errors is precisely how our non-adaptive algorithm is able to obtain an improved sample complexity.

To overcome this issue, we modify the hard instance of
[
27
]
slightly
and show the following. There exists a single hard
distribution
over threshold problem instances
such that for any algorithm and
any fixed random bits
r
, one of the following should hold:
either the probability (over the randomness of the problem instance and the samples) of the algorithm outputting a wrong solution
is large or the probability (again over the randomness of the problem instance and the samples)
of two runs with independent samples disagreeing is high (see
Lemma

18
and its preceding discussion for more details).
Our hard instance for the
k
-round lower bound simply draws
k
independent problem instances from this distribution, one per round.
In expectation over
k
rounds, this means that at least
k/2
rounds have either high error or high replicability parameter.
Since the random bits of the algorithm
r
is fixed,
the algorithm’s output is a deterministic function of the current samples and history.
This ensures that as long as the adversary chooses independent problem instances,
the errors (either correctness or replicability) are independent and therefore compound, implying the desired
\Omega(nk^{2})
sample complexity (see
4
).

We note a subtlety arises when making the compounding argument rigorous;
when we compare two independent runs with different samples on round
i
they do not
share the same
history
, even if they have the same output up to round
i-1
and (by assumption) use the same random bits.
Concretely, the information from the samples of the previous rounds means that in principle, the algorithms
used in round
i
may be different in the two runs and therefore we cannot apply the hardness of
Lemma

18
directly.
We resolve this by introducing a third
phantom
run that shares run
2
’s history but uses fresh independent samples.

Lemma

18
ensures run
2
and
3
must disagree with lower bounded probability
which implies at least one of them must disagree with run
1
. Since the two runs have the same distribution,
this implies a lower bound on the probability of run
1
and
2
disagreeing (see the proof of
4
for more details).

We refer to this technique—introducing the third independent run—as the
phantom run
.
As we discuss next, this technique has applications beyond the lower bound itself.

2.2.1
Structural Properties of Replicable Algorithms

The phantom run used in the lower bound above has a broader conceptual message.
In that argument, a fresh resample from the conditional distribution given the sufficient statistic
(here, the empirical distribution) was used to decouple the history from the current round.
The same resampling idea yields a general structural principle: a replicable algorithm never
needs to look at information in the data that is statistically irrelevant to its task.

Precisely, recall that a
sufficient statistic
for a parametric family
\mathcal{F}=\{D_{\theta}:\theta\in\Theta\}

is a function
f(S)
of the sample that captures all information about
\theta
.
We show that any replicable algorithm can be converted to one that operates
only on
f(S)
rather than the full sample, with replicability preserved for distributions in
\mathcal{F}
:

Theorem 4
.

[Informal; see
Theorem

11
]
Let
\mathcal{A}
be a
\rho
-replicable algorithm for a statistical problem over a family
\mathcal{F}
,
and let
f
be a sufficient statistic for
\mathcal{F}
.
Then there exists an algorithm
\mathcal{B}
with the same accuracy and sample complexity
such that
\mathcal{B}(S)
depends on
S
only through
f(S)
,
and
\mathcal{B}
is
2\rho
-replicable for any distribution
D\in\mathcal{F}
.

In general, the factor-of-
2
loss in the replicability parameter is a mild drawback: one can start with a
(\rho/2)
-replicable algorithm and apply
Theorem

4
to obtain a
\rho
-replicable one with no asymptotic cost.
Concretely, given the replicability parameter boosting result of

[
27
]
, the dependence of sample complexity on

\rho
is always
1/\rho^{2}
in the worst case, so the loss is fairly mild.

A natural special case of the above result is
order invariance
, obtained by taking
f(S)
to
be the unordered multiset
\{S_{1},\ldots,S_{n}\}
: the algorithm sees only the set
of samples, not their order.

Theorem

4
then implies that any
\rho
-replicable algorithm can be converted into a
2\rho
-replicable, order-invariant one with no change in accuracy or sample complexity (see
Corollary

2
).

Label invariance for symmetric problems.

The same resampling idea also yields
label invariance
when the problem is
symmetric
—that is,
its correctness criterion is invariant under relabeling of the domain elements
(uniformity testing, closeness testing, and entropy estimation are canonical examples).
Here, instead of resampling the datapoints, one resamples the domain labels:
apply a uniformly random permutation
\pi
of the domain
\mathcal{X}

(drawn from
\mathfrak{S}_{|\mathcal{X}|}
, the symmetric group on
|\mathcal{X}|
elements, using shared randomness
r^{\prime}
)
to the input before running
\mathcal{A}
.

Corollary 1

(Informal; see
Corollary

3
)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a
\rho
-replicable algorithm for a symmetric statistical problem over a finite domain
\mathcal{X}
.
Then there exists a
\rho
-replicable, label-invariant algorithm
\mathcal{B}
with the same accuracy and sample complexity.

The construction is
\mathcal{B}(S;r,r^{\prime})=\mathcal{A}(\pi(S);r)
, where
\pi\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(\mathfrak{S}_{|\mathcal{X}|})
is drawn from
r^{\prime}
.
Since
\pi
is shared, the relabeled samples
\pi(S_{1})
and
\pi(S_{2})
are i.i.d. from
\pi_{*}(D)
,
and replicability applies directly.
The label invariance guaranteed here is
distributional
: for any fixed sample
S
,
the output distribution of
\mathcal{B}(S)
is the same as that of
\mathcal{B}(\sigma(S))
for every domain permutation
\sigma
.
A stronger
pointwise
guarantee—where
\mathcal{B}(S;r)=\mathcal{B}(\sigma(S);r)
holds for every fixed seed
r
—follows
for any finite output space by a further step: compute the output distribution
of
\mathcal{B}(S)
and resample from it using correlated sampling, at the cost of a
factor of
2
in

(see
Corollary

4
).

Relation to prior work.

Whether replicable algorithms can always be assumed to have canonical structural properties
has been an active question in the literature.

[
35
]
studied replicable uniformity testing and proved lower bounds only for the
restricted class of
label-invariant
algorithms (their Definition 1.5), explicitly leaving open
whether this restriction is without loss of generality.
Their notion of label invariance is
pointwise
: for every fixed seed
r
, the output is unchanged
under domain relabeling.

[
1
]
resolved this open question for binary hypothesis testing of symmetric properties
(their Theorem 1.2): any
\rho
-replicable binary tester can be converted, with no loss in
\rho
,
to one that is simultaneously order-invariant and label-invariant.
Interestingly, their
definition
of label invariance (Definition 4.2) is distributional,
but their construction—which averages the test statistic
f
over all domain permutations to form a
deterministic symmetric function
h
—achieves the stronger pointwise notion as a byproduct.
Their proof represents
\mathcal{A}
via a scalar acceptance probability
f(S)\in[0,1]
and
constructs the symmetrized algorithm by considering all possible permutations of
f
.
This is fundamentally specific to binary output: only for binary algorithms does a single scalar
completely characterize the output distribution, so the approach has no counterpart for general output spaces.
Our
Corollary

2
and
Corollary

3
together give the same two structural properties
for
any
output space and
any
symmetric statistical problem, via simple black-box resampling.

Regarding the sufficient statistic result specifically:
[
1
]
prove a related reduction in their lower bound proof for Gaussian mean testing, using the fact that the empirical mean is a sufficient statistic for the Gaussian family.
Their reduction, like
Theorem

4
, guarantees replicability when both datasets are drawn from the same member of the parametric family.

Theorem

4
is stated as a general standalone transformation that applies to any statistical problem and any sufficient statistic, rather than being specific to hypothesis testing.
We note that their approach preserves the replicability parameter exactly, whereas
Theorem

4
incurs a factor-of-
2
loss; as discussed above, this loss is negligible.

2.3
Boosting Success Probability

Following
Theorem

1
, a natural question is whether the dependence on the failure probability
\beta
can be improved.
Our next result shows that, for a broad class of problems, this is indeed possible, providing a black-box boosting of the success probability.
The question of when boosting is possible is a central theme studied in learning theory, with the celebrated AdaBoost result of
[
22
]
being perhaps the most famous example.
3
3

3

Indeed, the primary motivation of the work of
[
17
]
who introduce advanced composition is boosting as indicated by the title.
Previous works in replicability have studied boosting the replicability parameter and accuracy (see
Section

3
for more details). Our work provides the first boosting results for success probability, directly improving several existing bounds from the literature.

To illustrate our approach we begin with a very simple idea. Suppose that we are given a
\rho
-replicable algorithm with failure probability
\rho
, and we want to reduce the failure probability to some value
\beta\ll\rho
.
Assume that we can efficiently test whether or not a given solution is valid for a statistical problem.
In this case, to boost the success probability, we can first run a replicable algorithm to find a candidate solution and then, if the solution is invalid, fall back on a non-replicable algorithm and use that instead.
It is easy to see that this approach (approximately) preserves replicability: if we take two separate runs of the algorithm, with probability
1-\rho
they agree and with probability
1-\rho
, the output is correct. Assuming the tester performs correctly in both cases, with probability at least
1-2\rho
the algorithms will both have the same output; indeed, they will both have the same output as the original algorithm. It is also clear that, since the original replicable algorithm’s output is always checked, the failure probability is determined by the non-replicable algorithm which, in most cases, is much more efficient.

The problem with the above idea is that sometimes it can be very difficult to test whether or not a proposed value is a valid solution. As an example, take the problem of estimating the mean of a Bernoulli random variable with additive error
\epsilon
.
Let
\mu
and
x
denote the true mean and proposed value respectively.
A natural approach is to estimate the mean empirically and compare its distance from
x
with
\epsilon
. If
\left|\mu-x\right|
is exactly
\epsilon
however, this distance can be either higher or lower than
\epsilon
with roughly equal probability.

To address the issue, we slightly adjust our approach by employing a stronger algorithm to obtain the initial solution, which in turn allows us to use an approximate tester later on.
To formalize this,
we consider problems parameterized by an
accuracy parameter

\alpha
, where smaller values of
\alpha
correspond to more accurate solutions,
and require the following two properties for the problem:

1.

The replicable sample complexity depends polynomially on
(\rho^{-1},\alpha^{-1},\beta^{-1})
.
More precisely, changing the parameters by constant factors should not dramatically change the sample complexity.

2.

There is a small-sample
tester
that, given a candidate solution, accepts whenever it is valid for
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
and rejects whenever it is invalid for
\mathcal{T}_{2\alpha}
.

Below, we briefly mention how a tester can be implemented for two example problems.

•

Mean estimation.

Consider the problem of estimating the mean with additive error
\alpha
. To implement a tester, estimate the mean non-replicably with additive error
\alpha/4
;
a standard analysis shows that this can be achieved using at most
O(\log(1/\beta)/\alpha^{2})
samples.
We then test whether a proposed value
x
is within error
3\alpha/2
of the estimated mean. By the triangle inequality, if the proposed value is at most
\alpha
away from the actual mean, then it is at most
3\alpha/2
away from the estimated mean and, conversely, if it is at least
2\alpha
away from the actual mean then it is at least
3\alpha/2
away from the estimated mean. Therefore, comparing the distance from the estimated mean with the threshold
3\alpha/2
gives us a tester.

•

PAC learning.

Consider the problem of finding some hypothesis
h
from a class of
\mathcal{H}
with error at most
\alpha
.
To test a hypothesis, non-replicably estimate its error using fresh samples to test whether it is below
\alpha
or above
2\alpha
. Using standard multiplicative Chernoff bounds, it can be shown that this can be achieved with sample complexity
O(\log(1/\beta)/\alpha)
.

We briefly state our result here and refer to
Section

7
for more details (see
Theorem

12
and
Remark

5
).

Theorem 5

(Informal)
.

Let
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
be a statistical problem parameterized by an accuracy parameter
\alpha
satisfying the two properties mentioned above.
Let
n_{\textnormal{replic}}(\rho,\alpha,\beta)
denote the sample complexity of a
\rho
-replicable algorithm solving the problem with failure probability
\beta
.
Let
n_{\textnormal{tester}}(\alpha,\beta)
denote the sample complexity of an approximate tester and

n_{\textnormal{non-replic}}(\alpha,\beta)
denote the sample complexity of a non-replicable algorithm for the problem.

For sufficiently small
\alpha,\rho,\beta>0
, there exists a replicable algorithm for the problem with sample complexity

\displaystyle n_{\textnormal{new}}(\rho,\alpha,\beta)=O\left(n_{\textnormal{replic}}(\rho,\alpha,\rho)+n_{\textnormal{tester}}(\alpha,\beta)+n_{\textnormal{non-replic}}(\alpha,\beta)\right)

The above result shows that the cost of driving the failure probability of a replicable algorithm to a very small value
\beta
appears as a separate
additive
term independent of
\rho
.
One can view the input to the algorithm as consisting of two parts: a
“replicable part”
, which produces a stable candidate, and a small
“non-replicable part”
, which certifies or corrects its validity.

Related Work

Replication.

The formal study of replicability was initiated by Impagliazzo, Lei, Pitassi, and Sorrell
[
27
]
, who examined algorithmic guarantees ensuring that outputs remain consistent under independent resampling. The work introduced replicability as a rigorous framework for reasoning about stability in statistical learning and provided initial examples of replicable algorithms for basic estimation and learning tasks. Subsequent research explored its conceptual links to other stability notions. Bun, Gaboardi, Hopkins, Impagliazzo, Lei, Pitassi, Sivakumar, and Sorrell
[
8
]
investigated the relationships among replicability, approximate differential privacy, and generalization, providing black-box reductions with polynomial overhead in the sample complexity. Kalavasis, Karbasi, Moran, and Velegkas
[
29
]
studied similar connections in the PAC learning setting, introducing the related notion of TV-indistinguishability and studying its relation with privacy and replicability. Together, these works clarified how replicability fits within the broader landscape of stability concepts in learning theory.

Replicability has been examined in a broad range of algorithmic settings. This includes
statistical query estimation
[
23
,
27
]
,
clustering
[
21
]
, bandits and online learning
[
20
,
2
]
, reinforcement learning
[
30
,
19
,
26
,
18
]
, convex optimization
[
39
]
, uniformity testing
[
35
]
,
prophet inequalities
[
3
]
,
median estimation
[
4
]
,
high-dimensional estimation and hypothesis testing
[
25
,
1
,
13
]
, and learning half-spaces
[
28
]
. In addition, several papers have used topological and geometric techniques to identify fundamental limitations of replicability, establishing lower bounds and separation results for a variety of statistical learning problems
[
11
,
14
,
10
,
38
]
. These developments have collectively expanded the theoretical understanding of replicable algorithms and their role in statistical and learning theory.

Composition.

Given
k
differentially private algorithms, each with parameter
(\varepsilon,\delta)
, it is easy to see that their composition is
(\varepsilon^{*},\delta^{*})
-DP where
(\varepsilon^{*},\delta^{*})=(k\varepsilon,k\delta)
. As shown by the advanced composition theorem however, this bound can be improved significantly; in particular, one can bound
\varepsilon^{*}
with approximately
\varepsilon\sqrt{k}

[
17
]
. The theorem has numerous applications in privacy, and the underlying ideas of its proof are frequently used in different problems
[
5
,
9
,
15
,
16
,
8
]
. In particular, the transformation from differential privacy to replicability obtained by
[
8
]
relies on the fact that differentially private algorithms are also perfectly generalizing; the proof of this fact draws heavily on techniques from the proof of advanced composition.

In the context of replicability, a similar naive analysis shows that the composition of
k

\rho
-replicable algorithms is
(k\rho)
-replicable. In this case however, the analysis is essentially tight, as such one cannot hope for an improved analysis (see
Section

8.1
).
As we show in this paper however, if we change our perspective and look at the sample complexity of statistical problems, one can obtain a nearly linear scaling, as opposed to the nearly quadratic scaling implied by the naive composition, and this is essentially tight (see
Section

8.2
).
Furthermore, we show in
Theorem

10
that once the adversary is allowed to choose problems adaptively based on previous outputs, no algorithm can achieve better than
\Omega(nk^{2})
sample complexity, establishing a sharp separation between the adaptive and non-adaptive settings.

Beyond naive composition, prior work has achieved better sample-complexity bounds under the relaxed paradigm of
coordinate sampling
.
In this model, we are given
k
statistical problems
\mathcal{T}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{T}_{k}
, possibly over different domains.
As a simple example, consider estimating the means of
k
distinct coins, which we also study in our work.
In the coordinate-sampling model, the algorithm may allocate samples adaptively across coordinates—deciding, based on previous observations, how many samples to take from each coin—so as to minimize the
total number
of samples.
In particular, for the
k
-coin problem, the number of samples may vary across different coins.

[
25
]
analyze this model and show that it yields non-trivial improvements, effectively shaving a factor of
k
from the sample complexity implied by naive composition (see Section 1.1.3 of their paper where they introduce this relaxation).
The key insight is that while a
\rho
-replicable algorithm requires
O(1/\rho^{2})
samples in the worst case, it needs only
O(1/\rho)
samples in expectation.
Hence, one can boost the replicability parameter of each subroutine to
\rho/k
without incurring a quadratic blowup in total sample complexity.
Notably, this improvement does not extend to the standard
vector-sampling
model, where each coin must be sampled the same number of times.

Parameter boosting.

As part of our results, we present a general reduction that amplifies the success probability of any replicable algorithm.
Parameter boosting is a well-studied theme in learning theory—appearing both in the contexts of replicability and differential privacy, and more broadly in PAC learning (see, e.g., the AdaBoost algorithm
[
22
]
).

In the context of replicability, prior works have studied boosting the replicability parameter of the algorithm and, for replicable PAC learning, the accuracy of the underlying learner.
For the replicability parameter, this was studied by the original work of
[
27
]
, who showed that one can boost replicability from a constant value
0.0001
to a much smaller value
\rho
at (nearly) a
\rho^{-2}
cost in sample complexity, which is essentially tight (see the lower bound for the coin problem in the same paper).
For boosting accuracy, the problem was also studied originally by
[
27
]
, and the recent work of
[
33
]
improves on their bounds, showing that one can obtain an
\alpha

\alpha^{-1}
in sample complexity (see Theorem 1.2 in their paper for full results).

In contrast to these works, our focus is on boosting the correctness probability.
For differential privacy,
prior work has proposed approaches for this using multiple runs of the base algorithm
[
24
,
34
,
37
]
.
Most closely to our work,
[
37
]
use multiple parallel
runs to show that one can boost the correctness of a PAC learning algorithm at
the cost of a
\log(1/\beta)
multiplicative factor in the sample complexity.
We study boosting the correctness parameter for replicability and obtain a general result that applies to a broad class of problems (see
Theorem

12
and the surrounding discussion for details).
To our knowledge, no such result was previously known in the context of replicability, and as we discuss in
Section

2.3
, our boosting result directly yields improved bounds for a large class of problems studied in the literature. Interestingly, for the special case of PAC learning, our framework shows that failure probability can be boosted to
\beta
at the cost of an
additive

\alpha^{-1}\log(\beta^{-1})
term.

Preliminaries

Statistical problem.

We study randomized algorithms that solve statistical problems under replicability constraints and analyze how such guarantees compose across multiple problems. Formally, we define the concept of a statistical problem as follows.

Definition 1

(Statistical Problem)
.

A
statistical problem
over a domain
\mathcal{X}
and output space
\mathcal{Y}
is a set of pairs
\mathcal{T}=\{(D,G_{D})\}
, where
D
is a distribution over
\mathcal{X}
and
G_{D}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}
specifies the set of acceptable outputs for
D
.
A randomized algorithm
\mathcal{A}
solves the problem
\mathcal{T}
with failure probability
\beta
and sample complexity
n
if, for all
(D,G_{D})\in\mathcal{T}
, the algorithm
\mathcal{A}
, given
n
i.i.d. samples from
D
, outputs a value in
G_{D}
with probability at least
1-\beta
.

For example, in the problem of mean estimation up to additive error
\varepsilon
, the output set is
G_{D}=[\mu_{D}-\varepsilon,\mu_{D}+\varepsilon]
where
\mu_{D}
is the mean of
D
. In a binary hypothesis testing problem distinguishing between distributions with mean above
1
versus below
0
, the output space is
\{0,1\}
and
G_{D}
depends on which case
D
falls into. Note that
\mathcal{Y}
may differ from
\mathcal{X}
.

We assume throughout the paper that
\mathcal{T}
contains a pair
(D,G_{D})
for every distribution

over
X
. This assumption is without loss of generality, as any missing pair can be added by defining
G_{D}=\mathcal{Y}
. From an algorithmic standpoint, this can be achieved by terminating

when a predefined time limit is exceeded and returning an arbitrary output. Under this assumption, we will often write
\mathcal{T}(D)
to refer to the set
G_{D}
.
We further note that this does not affect replicability; the standard definition of replicability considered in the literature requires
Equation

to hold for
all
distributions over
\mathcal{X}
.

In this work, we focus on algorithms
\mathcal{A}
for which the output space
\mathcal{Y}
is finite. This assumption is needed because the existing transformation between replicability and perfect generalization, which we use in our paper, requires finite
\mathcal{Y}
(see
Theorem

7
).
In most cases, this assumption is not restrictive; when
\mathcal{Y}
is continuous one can generally discretize the space and round the output to the nearest point in the discretization scheme.
For instance, for mean estimation up to additive error
\epsilon
, we can first run a replicable algorithm with additive error
\epsilon/2
, then round the output to the nearest integer multiple of
(\varepsilon/2)
. Note that the rounding procedure is deterministic and as such does not affect the replicability guarantee.
An alternative approach is to assume that the input domain
\mathcal{X}
is countable and, in order to transform a perfectly generalizing algorithm to a replicable one, apply Corollary 1.4 from
[
8
]
instead of Theorem 3.17.

Replicability and Perfect Generalization.

Our main focus in this paper is designing
replicable
algorithms for statistical problems, which we formally define below.

Definition 2

(Replicability
[
27
]
)
.

A randomized algorithm
\mathcal{A}:\mathcal{X}^{n}\to\mathcal{Y}
is said to be
\rho
-replicable if for every distribution
D
over
\mathcal{X}
,

\displaystyle\textnormal{Pr}_{S_{1},S_{2},r}\left[\mathcal{A}(S_{1};r)=\mathcal{A}(S_{2};r)\right]\geq 1-\rho,

(2)

where
S_{1},S_{2}
are independent samples of size
n
drawn from
D
and
r
is the internal randomness of
\mathcal{A}
.

As part of our analysis, we will use the concept of perfect generalization as introduced by
[
12
]
.

Definition 3

(Perfect Generalization (PG)
[
12
]
)
.

An algorithm
\mathcal{A}:\mathcal{X}^{n}\to\mathcal{Y}
is said to be
(\gamma,\varepsilon,\delta)
-
perfectly generalizing
(abbreviated as
PG
) if, for every distribution
D
over
\mathcal{X}
, there exists a distribution
\texttt{sim}_{D}
over
\mathcal{Y}
such that, with probability at least
1-\gamma
over the sample
S\sim D^{n}
, the output distribution of
\mathcal{A}(S)
satisfies, for every set of outcomes
O\subseteq\mathcal{Y}
,

\displaystyle e^{-\varepsilon}\left({\textnormal{Pr}\left[\texttt{sim}_{D}\in O\right]}-\delta\right)\leq{\textnormal{Pr}\left[\mathcal{A}(S)\in O\right]}\leq e^{\varepsilon}{\textnormal{Pr}\left[\texttt{sim}_{D}\in O\right]}+\delta.

Intuitively, perfect generalization requires that if we independently sample two datasets, the output distribution of the algorithm is similar on these two datasets. In contrast, replicability requires that their output is almost always equal when using the same random bits.
The two concepts are closely related and, as formally shown by
[
8
]
, replicable algorithms can be transformed into perfectly generalizable algorithms and vice versa.

Composition of statistical problems.

Let
\mathcal{T}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{T}_{k}
be
k
statistical problems defined over the same input space
\mathcal{X}
, with possibly different output spaces
\mathcal{Y}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{Y}_{k}
. For each
i\in[k]
, let
\mathcal{A}_{i}
be a replicable algorithm for
\mathcal{T}_{i}
. Given access to these algorithms, we are interested in designing a new algorithm that solves all of these problems simultaneously and remains replicable.
Formally, we define the
composed problem

\mathcal{T}^{*}
over the input space
\mathcal{X}
and output space
\mathcal{Y}^{*}=\mathcal{Y}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathcal{Y}_{k}
as

\mathcal{T}^{*}(D)=\mathcal{T}_{1}(D)\times\dots\times\mathcal{T}_{k}(D)\subseteq\mathcal{Y}^{*}.

Our goal is to construct a replicable algorithm
\mathcal{A}^{*}
that solves
\mathcal{T}^{*}
while minimizing sample complexity.

Comparison with composition in privacy.

In differential privacy, the composition is generally defined over algorithms
\mathcal{A}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{A}_{k}

directly, rather than the statistical problems. Specifically, the main question is what the privacy parameters
of the algorithm
\mathcal{A}
defined as
\mathcal{A}(S)=(\mathcal{A}_{1}(S),\dots,\mathcal{A}_{k}(S))
are.
For replicability, we focus on the composition of statistical problems because, perhaps surprisingly, it allows
us to obtain a better sample complexity.
Indeed, if we have
k
algorithms

\mathcal{A}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{A}_{k}
where
\mathcal{A}_{i}
is
\rho
-replicable,
their composed algorithm
\mathcal{A}
is
(k\rho)
-replicable and this is essentially tight (see
Section

8.1
).
It follows that boosting the replicability parameters of each
\mathcal{A}_{i}
to
\rho/k

would incur a quadratic dependence on
k
.
In contrast,
Theorem

1
shows that
one can obtain a nearly linear dependence which is essentially tight.

5.1
Application: Multiple Statistical Queries

For our first application, we study the sample complexity of estimating multiple statistical queries on the same distribution.
Formally, we are given
k

\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{k}
where
each
\phi_{i}
maps an input
x\in\mathcal{X}
from the ground set to some value in the range
[0,1]
. Defining
\mu_{i}=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim D}\left[\phi_{i}(X)\right]
, the goal is to estimate all values
\mu_{i}
at the same time with additive error
\alpha
.
The problem has numerous applications in statistics; in its simplest form, it captures the problem of estimating the mean of
k
Bernoulli random variables with
\ell_{\infty}
error
\alpha
, also known as the
k
-coin
problem.

When

, the problem can be solved with sample complexity
\widetilde{O}(\frac{\log(1/\beta)}{\rho^{2}\alpha^{2}})
as shown by
[
27
]
, where
\beta
denotes the failure probability.
A naive generalization to
k

\mu_{i}
with replicability parameter
\rho/k
, but this leads to sample complexity quadratic in
k
which is sub-optimal.
Previously, this problem was studied by
[
30
]
who also noted the sub-optimality of the naive approach and proposed a new algorithm with (nearly) linear dependence on
k
through connections to TV-indistinguishability.
Concretely, they first (non-replicably) estimate all values
\mu_{i}
with error
\varepsilon\rho/\sqrt{k}
. Next, inspired by the Gaussian mechanism
from DP, they add Gaussian noise to the estimate in order to ensure that the output distribution has the TV-indistinguishability property.
Finally, they use correlated sampling to transform this back to a replicable algorithm.

Subsequent work of
[
25
]
also studied the
k
-coin problem. They showed that this problem is part of a broader class for which replicable estimation is equivalent to isoperimetric tiling. They then used the existence of such a tiling to obtain an algorithm with nearly linear scaling in
k
(see Theorem 1.9 in their paper).

Theorem

1
provides an alternative, and in our view much simpler, approach for the problem.
Specifically, we set
\mathcal{T}_{i}
to be the problem of estimating
\phi_{i}
with additive error
\alpha
. The sample complexity of this problem is bounded by the aforementioned work of
[
27
]
.

\alpha
(i.e., finding some
v_{i}
satisfying
\left|\mu_{i}-v_{i}\right|\leq\alpha
).
Let
n_{0}
denote the sample complexity of this problem for a
\rho^{\prime}
-replicable algorithm with
failure probability
\beta_{0}
where
\rho^{\prime}=0.0001
.
Since each
\mathcal{T}_{i}
corresponds to an instance of the problem with
d=1
and
\rho^{\prime}
is constant,
we have

n_{0}\leq\widetilde{O}(\frac{\log(1/\beta_{0})}{\alpha^{2}})
.
It is clear that finding a vector
\nu
satisfying

\left\lVert\nu-\mu_{D}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\alpha

corresponds to the composition of all
\mathcal{T}_{i}
.

Theorem

1
gives an algorithm for solving this problem.
The failure probability of the algorithm is bounded with

\displaystyle\Theta\left(k\sqrt{\beta_{0}}\log(k/\rho\beta_{0})\right)

\displaystyle=\Theta\left(k\sqrt{\beta_{0}}\left(\log(k/\rho)+\log(1/\beta_{0})\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\Theta\left(k\sqrt{\beta_{0}}\left(\log(k/\rho)+\log(1/\beta)+\log(1/c)\right)\right)

(
Equation

12
)

\displaystyle\leq\Theta\left(k\sqrt{\beta_{0}}\log(k/\rho)\log(1/\beta)\log(1/c)\right)

(Since
\log(k/\rho),\log(1/\beta),\log(1/c)\geq\Omega(1)
)

\displaystyle=\Theta\left(k\frac{c\beta}{k\log(k/\rho)\log(1/\beta)}\log(k/\rho)\log(1/\beta)\log(1/c)\right)

(Definition of
\beta_{0}
)

\displaystyle=\Theta\left(c\beta\log(1/c)\right)

(Simplifying)

Letting
c
be a small enough absolute constant, the above quantity is at most
\beta
as required.
It follows that

\displaystyle n_{0}\leq\widetilde{O}\left(\frac{\log(1/\beta_{0})}{\alpha^{2}}\right)\leq\widetilde{O}\left(\frac{\log(k/\rho\beta)}{\alpha^{2}}\right),

which means
the sample complexity of the final algorithm is at most

\displaystyle\frac{n_{0}k}{\rho^{2}}\operatorname{polylog}(k\rho/\beta_{0})=\widetilde{O}\left(\frac{k\operatorname{polylog}(1/\beta)}{\rho^{2}\alpha^{2}}\right),

as claimed.
∎

5.2
Application: Parallel Best Arm

The recent work of
[
26
]
studies
replicable reinforcement learning. As part of their analysis, they focus on solving parallel instances of the best arm problem. In the best arm problem, we are given i.i.d samples from
|A|
reward distributions (i.e., arms), and the goal is to (replicably) output an arm with (approximately) maximum reward.
Formally, each
a\in A
has a corresponding distribution
r_{a}
over
[0,1]
. Denoting
\mu_{a}={\mathbb{E}\left[r_{a}\right]}
, the goal is to choose some
a
satisfying

\mu_{a}\geq\max_{a^{\prime}\in A}\mu_{a^{\prime}}-\alpha
.
They show that this problem can be solved with
O(\frac{1}{\rho^{2}\varepsilon^{2}}\log^{3}(|A|/\beta))
samples from each arm where
\beta
denotes the failure probability.
Their approach relies on using correlated sampling together with the exponential mechanism from differential privacy.
They then discuss the generalization of this to solving
|S|
parallel instances of the same problem.
They specifically note that the naive composition approach leads to a scaling of
|S|^{2}
and to avoid this, they use a more nuanced approach by directly sampling from the joint exponential mechanism on vectors of actions
(a_{1},\dots,a_{S})\in[A]^{S}
.

In this section, we show that the generalization can actually be done in a black-box manner using
Theorem

1
.

Theorem 9
.

For any parameters
\beta\leq\rho\leq 1/2
and
\alpha>0
, there exists a
\rho
-replicable algorithm that solves
k
parallel instances of the best-arm problem over a set
A
with error at most
\alpha
and failure probability at most
\beta
, using
\widetilde{O}(k\alpha^{-2}\rho^{-2}\operatorname{polylog}(|A|/\beta))
samples per instance-arm pair.

Proof.

The same proof as
Theorem

8
holds.
Formally, set
\beta_{0}
as

\displaystyle\left(\frac{c\beta}{k\log(k/\rho)\log(1/\beta)}\right)^{2}.

Let
n_{0}
be the sample complexity of solving a single instance of the problem with replicability parameter
0.0001
and failure probability
\beta_{0}
.
Define
a=|A|
.
We have

n_{0}\leq O(\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}\log^{3}(a/\beta_{0}))
.
As before we have

\log(1/\beta_{0})\leq\Theta(\log(k/\rho\beta)+\log(1/c))
.
It follows that the failure probability is bounded with

\Theta(c\beta\log(1/c))
as before.
Setting
c
to be small enough, this ensures that
the failure probability is at most
\beta
.
Therefore,

n_{0}\leq O(\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}\log^{3}(a/\beta_{0}))
, which is at most
O(\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}\log^{3}(ka/\rho\beta))
.
It follows that the total sample complexity is bounded as

\displaystyle\frac{n_{0}k}{\rho^{2}}\operatorname{polylog}(k\rho/\beta_{0})\leq\widetilde{O}\left(\frac{k}{\alpha^{2}\rho^{2}}\operatorname{polylog}(a/\beta)\right).

∎

5.3
Heterogeneous Composition for Perfect Generalization

In this section, we analyze the
adaptive
composition of
k
perfectly generalizing algorithms with heterogeneous parameters
(\gamma_{i},\varepsilon_{i},\delta_{i})
. Our main result is
Lemma

2
provided below.
In
Section

5.3.1
, we use this to prove
Theorem

6
which is easier to work with and is used in
Section

5
.

Throughout, for any two random variables
U,V
, we use the shorthand

U\approx_{\varepsilon,\delta}V
to mean
that for all measurable
O
:

\displaystyle e^{-\varepsilon}\left({\textnormal{Pr}\left[V\in O\right]}-\delta\right)\leq{\textnormal{Pr}\left[U\in O\right]}\leq e^{\varepsilon}{\textnormal{Pr}\left[V\in O\right]}+\delta.

Adaptive composition model.

We consider a sequence of
k
adaptively chosen algorithms

\mathcal{A}_{i}:\mathcal{X}^{n}\times\mathcal{Y}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathcal{Y}_{i-1}\to\mathcal{Y}_{i}
,

.
Given a dataset
X\sim D^{n}
, the composition proceeds iteratively:
at round

Z_{i}=\mathcal{A}_{i}(X,Z^{i-1})
where
Z^{i-1}=(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{i-1})
is the transcript of previous outputs (with
Z^{0}=\perp
).
We say that each
\mathcal{A}_{i}
is
(\gamma_{i},\varepsilon_{i},\delta_{i})
-PG in the adaptive sense if,
for every distribution
D
over
\mathcal{X}
and every fixed prefix
z^{i-1}\in\mathcal{Y}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathcal{Y}_{i-1}

there exists a distribution
\mathcal{W}_{i}(z^{i-1})
over
\mathcal{Y}_{i}
(the
oracle
for prefix

) such that,
with probability at least
1-\gamma_{i}
over
S\sim D^{n}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{i}(S,z^{i-1})\approx_{\varepsilon_{i},\delta_{i}}\mathcal{W}_{i}(z^{i-1}).

Define the
oracle transcript

\widetilde{Z}^{k}=(\widetilde{Z}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{Z}_{k})

by sampling
\widetilde{Z}_{i}\sim\mathcal{W}_{i}(\widetilde{Z}^{i-1})
iteratively,
using the oracle prefix
\widetilde{Z}^{i-1}
(not the real prefix
Z^{i-1}
).
Since
\mathcal{W}_{i}(z^{i-1})
depends only on
D
and
z^{i-1}
, the oracle transcript
\widetilde{Z}^{k}
is independent of
X
.
We say that the adaptive composition
\mathcal{A}^{*}
is
(\gamma^{*},\varepsilon^{*},\delta^{*})
-PG if, with probability at least
1-\gamma^{*}
over
X\sim D^{n}
, the real transcript satisfies
\mathcal{A}^{*}(X)\approx_{\varepsilon^{*},\delta^{*}}\widetilde{Z}^{k}
, where
\widetilde{Z}^{k}
serves as the oracle for the composed algorithm.

Lemma 2
.

Let
\mathcal{A}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{A}_{k}
be
k
adaptively chosen algorithms such that

\mathcal{A}_{i}(\cdot,z^{i-1})
is

(\gamma_{i},\varepsilon_{i},\delta_{i})
-PG for every prefix
z^{i-1}
.
Assume that

\varepsilon_{i}\in(0,1]
,

\delta_{i}\in(0,\varepsilon_{i}/50]
and
\gamma_{i}\in(0,1)
.
For any
\delta^{\prime}\in(0,1/2)
, the adaptive composition

\mathcal{A}^{*}=(\mathcal{A}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{A}_{k})
is
(\gamma^{*},\varepsilon^{*},\delta^{*})
-PG with

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{*}=3\varepsilon^{(k)},\quad\delta^{*}=\gamma^{*}=5\sqrt{\frac{\delta^{(k)}}{\min\left\{\varepsilon^{(k)},1\right\}}}

where

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{(j)}=2\sqrt{2\ln(1/\delta^{\prime})\left(\sum_{\ell\leq j}\varepsilon_{\ell}^{2}\right)}+\sum_{\ell\leq j}\left(\psi_{\ell}(\delta_{\ell})+2\varepsilon_{\ell}\left(\frac{e^{2\varepsilon_{\ell}}}{1-\hat{\delta}_{\ell}}-1\right)\right),

\displaystyle\delta^{(j)}=j\delta^{\prime}+\sum_{\ell\leq j}\widehat{\delta}_{\ell}+\sum_{\ell\leq j}e^{\varepsilon^{(\ell-1)}}\gamma_{\ell},

\displaystyle\hat{\delta}_{\ell}=\frac{2\delta_{\ell}}{1-e^{-\varepsilon_{\ell}}},\qquad\psi_{\ell}(\delta_{\ell})=\delta_{\ell}\left(2e^{\varepsilon_{\ell}}+1\right)+2\delta_{\ell}^{2}\left(\frac{2e^{2\varepsilon_{\ell}}}{e^{\varepsilon_{\ell}}-1}+1\right)^{2},

with
\varepsilon^{(0)}=0
.

We define the key quantities used in the proof.
For any
i
and

z^{i}\in\mathcal{Y}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathcal{Y}_{i}
,
define

\displaystyle f_{i}(x,z^{i})=\ln\left(\frac{{\textnormal{Pr}\left[\mathcal{A}_{i}(x,z^{i-1})=z_{i}\right]}}{{\textnormal{Pr}\left[\mathcal{W}_{i}(z^{i-1})=z_{i}\right]}}\right).

Define
the sets

\mathcal{C}_{i}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{i}=\left\{(x,z^{i-1}):\mathcal{A}_{i}(x,z^{i-1})\approx_{\varepsilon_{i},\delta_{i}}\mathcal{W}_{i}(z^{i-1})\right\}.

Next, we define

\mathcal{E}_{i}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{i}=\left\{(x,z^{i}):\left|f_{i}(x,z^{i})\right|\leq 2\varepsilon_{i}\right\}.

Define the
sets
\mathcal{H}_{i},\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{i}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{i}=\left\{(x,z^{i}):\forall\ell\leq i:(x,z^{\ell-1})\in\mathcal{C}_{\ell}\text{ and }(x,z^{\ell})\in\mathcal{E}_{\ell}\right\},

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{i}=\left\{(x,z^{i-1}):\forall\ell\leq i:(x,z^{\ell-1})\in\mathcal{C}_{\ell}\text{ and }(x,z^{\ell-1})\in\mathcal{E}_{\ell-1}\right\},

where

.
Note that
(x,z^{i})\in\mathcal{H}_{i}
if and only if

(x,z^{i-1})\in\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{i}
and
(x,z^{i})\in\mathcal{E}_{i}
.
Define

F_{j}(x,z^{j})=\sum_{\ell\leq j}f_{\ell}(x,z^{\ell})

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{j}=\left\{(x,z^{j}):F_{j}(x,z^{j})\leq\varepsilon^{(j)}\right\},\qquad\mathcal{G}^{\prime}_{j}=\left\{(x,z^{j}):F_{j}(x,z^{j})\geq-\varepsilon^{(j)}\right\}.

High level sketch.

We next give the high level sketch of the proof.
The main goal will be to show that

\displaystyle(X,Z^{k})\approx_{\varepsilon^{(k)},\delta^{(k)}}(X,\widetilde{Z}^{k})

(13)

This would imply the lemma using a standard conditioning argument (see

4
).
To prove the above, we need to show that

(X,Z^{k})\in\mathcal{G}_{k}
and

(X,\widetilde{Z}^{k})\in\mathcal{G}^{\prime}_{k}
each hold with probability at least
1-\delta^{(k)}
;
this implies
Equation

13
by standard techniques (
Lemma

3
).

The main idea is to first show that, with high probability
4
4

4

For the purposes of the sketch, we use

informally to denote

\varepsilon_{i},\delta_{i}

, we have

and

\widetilde{Z}^{i-1}

over the randomness of

Additionally, if
(X,\widetilde{Z}^{i-1})\in\mathcal{C}_{i}

(X,\widetilde{Z}^{i-1})\in\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{i}

Taking a union bound, allows us to obtain that

To show that

The former condition ensures that probabilities under

are upper bounded by probabilities under

(X,\widetilde{Z}^{i-1})

(X,Z^{i-1})\in\mathcal{G}_{i-1}
with high probability (

and

Formally, the informal induction steps are as follows.

1.

If
(X,Z^{i-1})\in\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{i}

(X,Z^{i})\in\mathcal{H}_{i}

2.

If
(X,Z^{i})\in\mathcal{H}_{i}

Lemma

10
).

3.

If
(X,Z^{i})\in\mathcal{G}_{i}

4.

If

.

The above induction allows us to show that

Lemma 3

\approx_{\varepsilon,\delta}
is symmetric (the upper bound of one direction gives the lower bound of the other), so
\mathcal{W}_{i}(z^{i-1})\approx_{\varepsilon_{i},\delta_{i}}\mathcal{A}_{i}(x,z^{i-1})
.
Moreover,
\mathcal{E}_{i}
is unchanged under swapping since
\left|\ln(a/b)\right|=\left|\ln(b/a)\right|
.
Applying
Lemma

6
with
X=\mathcal{W}_{i}(z^{i-1})
and
Y=\mathcal{A}_{i}(x,z^{i-1})
gives
{\textnormal{Pr}\left[(x,(z^{i-1},Z_{i}))\in\mathcal{E}_{i}\right]}\geq 1-\hat{\delta}_{i}
for
Z_{i}\sim\mathcal{W}_{i}(z^{i-1})
.
The “in particular” statement follows since
(x,z^{i-1},Z_{i})\notin\mathcal{H}_{i}

implies
(x,(z^{i-1},Z_{i}))\notin\mathcal{E}_{i}
.
∎

Lemma 8
.

Assume
\varepsilon_{i}\in(0,1]
and
\delta_{i}\in(0,\varepsilon_{i}/50]
.
For any
(x,z^{i-1})\in\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{i}
, define

\rho_{i}=2\varepsilon_{i}\left(e^{2\varepsilon_{i}}/(1-\hat{\delta}_{i})-1\right)+\psi_{i}(\delta_{i})
.

1.

(Real process.) If
Z_{i}\sim\mathcal{A}_{i}(x,z^{i-1})
, then

{\mathbb{E}\left[f_{i}(x,(z^{i-1},Z_{i}))\mid(x,(z^{i-1},Z_{i}))\in\mathcal{E}_{i}\right]}\leq\rho_{i}
.

2.

(Oracle process.) If
Z_{i}\sim\mathcal{W}_{i}(z^{i-1})
, then

{\mathbb{E}\left[-f_{i}(x,(z^{i-1},Z_{i}))\mid(x,(z^{i-1},Z_{i}))\in\mathcal{E}_{i}\right]}\leq\rho_{i}
.

Proof.

We only prove Part (1).
Part (2) follows from the same proof as part (1) with the roles of
\mathcal{A}_{i}(x,z^{i-1})
and
\mathcal{W}_{i}(z^{i-1})
swapped.
Fix
(x,z^{i-1})\in\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{i}
.
Write
P=\mathcal{A}_{i}(x,z^{i-1})
and
Q=\mathcal{W}_{i}(z^{i-1})
for brevity, so that
P\approx_{\varepsilon_{i},\delta_{i}}Q
.
We abuse notation and write
\mathcal{E}_{i}
for the
set
\left\{z_{i}:(x,(z^{i-1},z_{i}))\in\mathcal{E}_{i}\right\}
.

Claim 2
.

\sum_{z_{i}\in\mathcal{E}_{i}}{\textnormal{Pr}\left[Q=z_{i}\right]}\ln\left(\frac{{\textnormal{Pr}\left[P=z_{i}\right]}}{{\textnormal{Pr}\left[Q=z_{i}\right]}}\right)\leq\psi_{i}(\delta_{i})
.

Proof of
2
.

Observe that

\displaystyle\sum_{z_{i}\in\mathcal{E}_{i}}{\textnormal{Pr}\left[Q=z_{i}\right]}\ln\left(\frac{{\textnormal{Pr}\left[P=z_{i}\right]}}{{\textnormal{Pr}\left[Q=z_{i}\right]}}\right)

\displaystyle={\textnormal{Pr}\left[Q\in\mathcal{E}_{i}\right]}\sum_{z_{i}\in\mathcal{E}_{i}}\frac{{\textnormal{Pr}\left[Q=z_{i}\right]}}{{\textnormal{Pr}\left[Q\in\mathcal{E}_{i}\right]}}\ln\left(\frac{{\textnormal{Pr}\left[P=z_{i}\right]}}{{\textnormal{Pr}\left[Q=z_{i}\right]}}\right)

(Cancelling product)

\displaystyle\leq{\textnormal{Pr}\left[Q\in\mathcal{E}_{i}\right]}\ln(\sum_{z_{i}\in\mathcal{E}_{i}}\frac{{\textnormal{Pr}\left[Q=z_{i}\right]}}{{\textnormal{Pr}\left[Q\in\mathcal{E}_{i}\right]}}\frac{{\textnormal{Pr}\left[P=z_{i}\right]}}{{\textnormal{Pr}\left[Q=z_{i}\right]}})

(Jensen’s inequality)

\displaystyle\leq\ln\left(\sum_{z_{i}\in\mathcal{E}_{i}}\frac{{\textnormal{Pr}\left[P=z_{i}\right]}}{{\textnormal{Pr}\left[Q\in\mathcal{E}_{i}\right]}}\right)

(Since
{\textnormal{Pr}\left[Q\in\mathcal{E}_{i}\right]}\leq 1
; see below)

\displaystyle=\ln\left(\frac{{\textnormal{Pr}\left[P\in\mathcal{E}_{i}\right]}}{{\textnormal{Pr}\left[Q\in\mathcal{E}_{i}\right]}}\right)

(Total probability)

\displaystyle=\ln\left(\frac{1-{\textnormal{Pr}\left[P\notin\mathcal{E}_{i}\right]}}{1-{\textnormal{Pr}\left[Q\notin\mathcal{E}_{i}\right]}}\right).

(The “see below” step uses
{\textnormal{Pr}\left[Q\in\mathcal{E}_{i}\right]}\leq 1
: if
{\textnormal{Pr}\left[P\in\mathcal{E}_{i}\right]}\geq{\textnormal{Pr}\left[Q\in\mathcal{E}_{i}\right]}
then the log is non-negative and the inequality holds; otherwise the post-Jensen expression is already negative, so the claim
\leq\psi_{i}(\delta_{i})
holds trivially.)
Observe however that,
since
P\approx_{\varepsilon_{i},\delta_{i}}Q
, we have

{\textnormal{Pr}\left[Q\notin\mathcal{E}_{i}\right]}\leq e^{\varepsilon_{i}}{\textnormal{Pr}\left[P\notin\mathcal{E}_{i}\right]}+\delta_{i}
.
Define

q:={\textnormal{Pr}\left[P\notin\mathcal{E}_{i}\right]}
.
We claim that
e^{\varepsilon_{i}}q+\delta_{i}\leq 1/2
.
This is because

\displaystyle q

\displaystyle\leq\hat{\delta}_{i}

(By
Lemma

7
)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\delta_{i}}{1-e^{-\varepsilon_{i}}}

(Definition of
\hat{\delta}_{i}
)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\varepsilon_{i}}{50\left(1-e^{-\varepsilon_{i}}\right)}

(Since
\delta_{i}\leq\varepsilon_{i}/50
)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4}{50}

\displaystyle\text{\emph{(Since $1-e^{-x}\geq x/2$ for $x\leq 1$ and $\varepsilon_{i}\leq 1$)}}.

Which further implies

\displaystyle e^{\varepsilon_{i}}q+\delta_{i}\leq\frac{4e}{50}+\frac{1}{50}<\frac{1}{4}.

It follows that

\displaystyle\ln\left(\frac{1-{\textnormal{Pr}\left[P\notin\mathcal{E}_{i}\right]}}{1-{\textnormal{Pr}\left[Q\notin\mathcal{E}_{i}\right]}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\ln(\frac{1-q}{1-e^{\varepsilon_{i}}q-\delta_{i}})

\displaystyle=\ln(1-q)-\ln(1-(e^{\varepsilon_{i}}q+\delta_{i}))

\displaystyle\leq-q+e^{\varepsilon_{i}}q+\delta_{i}+2(e^{\varepsilon_{i}}q+\delta_{i})^{2}

\displaystyle=\delta_{i}+(e^{\varepsilon_{i}}-1)q+2(e^{\varepsilon_{i}}q+\delta_{i})^{2},

where for the second inequality we have used

\ln(1-x)\leq-x
which holds for all
x
and

\ln(1-x)\geq-x-2x^{2}
which holds for all
x\leq 1/2
.
Recall however that

q\leq\hat{\delta_{i}}=\frac{2\delta_{i}}{1-e^{-\varepsilon_{i}}}
.
Since the above expression is increasing in
q
, it is at most

\displaystyle\delta_{i}+(e^{\varepsilon_{i}}-1)\frac{2\delta_{i}}{1-e^{-\varepsilon_{i}}}+2(\frac{2\delta_{i}}{1-e^{-\varepsilon_{i}}}e^{\varepsilon_{i}}+\delta_{i})^{2}

\displaystyle=\delta_{i}\left(1+\frac{2(e^{\varepsilon_{i}}-1)}{1-e^{-\varepsilon_{i}}}\right)+2\delta_{i}^{2}\left(\frac{2e^{2\varepsilon_{i}}}{e^{\varepsilon_{i}}-1}+1\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\delta_{i}\left(2e^{\varepsilon_{i}}+1\right)+2\delta_{i}^{2}\left(\frac{2e^{2\varepsilon_{i}}}{e^{\varepsilon_{i}}-1}+1\right)^{2}=\psi_{i}(\delta_{i}),

where for the final equality we have substituted the definition of
\psi_{i}

from the lemma statement.
Combining the above derivations we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{z_{i}\in\mathcal{E}_{i}}{\textnormal{Pr}\left[Q=z_{i}\right]}\ln\left(\frac{{\textnormal{Pr}\left[P=z_{i}\right]}}{{\textnormal{Pr}\left[Q=z_{i}\right]}}\right)\leq\psi_{i}(\delta_{i})

(16)

∎

Claim 3
.

\sum_{z_{i}\in\mathcal{E}_{i}}{\textnormal{Pr}\left[P=z_{i}\mid P\in\mathcal{E}_{i}\right]}\ln\left(\frac{{\textnormal{Pr}\left[P=z_{i}\right]}}{{\textnormal{Pr}\left[Q=z_{i}\right]}}\right)\leq\sum_{z_{i}\in\mathcal{E}_{i}}{\textnormal{Pr}\left[Q=z_{i}\right]}\ln\left(\frac{{\textnormal{Pr}\left[P=z_{i}\right]}}{{\textnormal{Pr}\left[Q=z_{i}\right]}}\right)+2\varepsilon_{i}\left(\frac{e^{2\varepsilon_{i}}}{1-\hat{\delta_{i}}}-1\right)
.

Proof of
3
.

We note that,

\displaystyle\sum_{z_{i}\in\mathcal{E}_{i}}{\textnormal{Pr}\left[P=z_{i}\mid P\in\mathcal{E}_{i}\right]}\ln\left(\frac{{\textnormal{Pr}\left[P=z_{i}\right]}}{{\textnormal{Pr}\left[Q=z_{i}\right]}}\right)-\sum_{z_{i}\in\mathcal{E}_{i}}{\textnormal{Pr}\left[Q=z_{i}\right]}\ln\left(\frac{{\textnormal{Pr}\left[P=z_{i}\right]}}{{\textnormal{Pr}\left[Q=z_{i}\right]}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{z_{i}\in\mathcal{E}_{i}}\left|{\textnormal{Pr}\left[P=z_{i}\mid P\in\mathcal{E}_{i}\right]}-{\textnormal{Pr}\left[Q=z_{i}\right]}\right|\cdot\left|\ln\left(\frac{{\textnormal{Pr}\left[P=z_{i}\right]}}{{\textnormal{Pr}\left[Q=z_{i}\right]}}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2\varepsilon_{i}\sum_{z_{i}\in\mathcal{E}_{i}}\left|{\textnormal{Pr}\left[P=z_{i}\mid P\in\mathcal{E}_{i}\right]}-{\textnormal{Pr}\left[Q=z_{i}\right]}\right|

\displaystyle=2\varepsilon_{i}\sum_{z_{i}\in\mathcal{E}_{i}}{\textnormal{Pr}\left[Q=z_{i}\right]}\left|\frac{{\textnormal{Pr}\left[P=z_{i}\mid P\in\mathcal{E}_{i}\right]}}{{\textnormal{Pr}\left[Q=z_{i}\right]}}-1\right|.

where for the second inequality we have used definition of
\mathcal{E}_{i}
.
Substituting

{\textnormal{Pr}\left[P=z_{i}\mid P\in\mathcal{E}_{i}\right]}={\textnormal{Pr}\left[P=z_{i}\right]}/{\textnormal{Pr}\left[P\in\mathcal{E}_{i}\right]}

and using the fact that

\left|\ln\left(\frac{{\textnormal{Pr}\left[P=z_{i}\right]}}{{\textnormal{Pr}\left[Q=z_{i}\right]}}\right)\right|\leq 2\varepsilon_{i}
for
z_{i}\in\mathcal{E}_{i}

we can write this as

\displaystyle 2\varepsilon_{i}\sum_{z_{i}\in\mathcal{E}_{i}}{\textnormal{Pr}\left[Q=z_{i}\right]}\max\left\{\frac{e^{2\varepsilon_{i}}}{{\textnormal{Pr}\left[P\in\mathcal{E}_{i}\right]}}-1,1-\frac{e^{-2\varepsilon_{i}}}{{\textnormal{Pr}\left[P\in\mathcal{E}_{i}\right]}}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq 2\varepsilon_{i}\left(\frac{e^{2\varepsilon_{i}}}{1-\hat{\delta_{i}}}-1\right),

where for the final inequality we have used the facts that

{\textnormal{Pr}\left[Q\in\mathcal{E}_{i}\right]}\leq 1
,

{\textnormal{Pr}\left[P\in\mathcal{E}_{i}\right]}\geq 1-\hat{\delta}_{i}
and

\frac{e^{2\varepsilon_{i}}}{{\textnormal{Pr}\left[P\in\mathcal{E}_{i}\right]}}-1\geq 1-\frac{e^{-2\varepsilon_{i}}}{{\textnormal{Pr}\left[P\in\mathcal{E}_{i}\right]}}
.
The final inequality is equivalent to

\displaystyle\frac{e^{-2\varepsilon_{i}}}{{\textnormal{Pr}\left[P\in\mathcal{E}_{i}\right]}}+\frac{e^{2\varepsilon_{i}}}{{\textnormal{Pr}\left[P\in\mathcal{E}_{i}\right]}}\geq 2,

which holds because of the inequality
x+1/x\geq 2
and
the fact that

{\textnormal{Pr}\left[P\in\mathcal{E}_{i}\right]}\leq 1
.
It follows that

\displaystyle\sum_{z_{i}\in\mathcal{E}_{i}}{\textnormal{Pr}\left[P=z_{i}\mid P\in\mathcal{E}_{i}\right]}\ln\left(\frac{{\textnormal{Pr}\left[P=z_{i}\right]}}{{\textnormal{Pr}\left[Q=z_{i}\right]}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{z_{i}\in\mathcal{E}_{i}}{\textnormal{Pr}\left[Q=z_{i}\right]}\ln\left(\frac{{\textnormal{Pr}\left[P=z_{i}\right]}}{{\textnormal{Pr}\left[Q=z_{i}\right]}}\right)+2\varepsilon_{i}\left(\frac{e^{2\varepsilon_{i}}}{1-\hat{\delta_{i}}}-1\right)

(17)

∎

Combining the two claims: since
Z_{i}\sim P
conditioned on
(X,Z^{i-1})=(x,z^{i-1})
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}\left[f_{i}(X,Z^{i})\mid(X,Z^{i-1})=(x,z^{i-1}),(X,Z^{i})\in\mathcal{H}_{i}\right]}

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}\left[f_{i}(x,(z^{i-1},Z_{i}))\mid(x,(z^{i-1},Z_{i}))\in\mathcal{E}_{i}\right]}

(
Z_{i}\sim P
;
(x,z^{i-1})\in\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{i}
)

\displaystyle=\sum_{z_{i}\in\mathcal{E}_{i}}{\textnormal{Pr}\left[P=z_{i}\mid P\in\mathcal{E}_{i}\right]}\ln\left(\frac{{\textnormal{Pr}\left[P=z_{i}\right]}}{{\textnormal{Pr}\left[Q=z_{i}\right]}}\right)

(Expanding
{\mathbb{E}\left[.\right]}
)

\displaystyle\leq 2\varepsilon_{i}\left(\frac{e^{2\varepsilon_{i}}}{1-\hat{\delta_{i}}}-1\right)+\sum_{z_{i}\in\mathcal{E}_{i}}{\textnormal{Pr}\left[Q=z_{i}\right]}\ln\left(\frac{{\textnormal{Pr}\left[P=z_{i}\right]}}{{\textnormal{Pr}\left[Q=z_{i}\right]}}\right)

(
3
)

\displaystyle\leq 2\varepsilon_{i}\left(\frac{e^{2\varepsilon_{i}}}{1-\hat{\delta_{i}}}-1\right)+\psi_{i}(\delta_{i})

\displaystyle\text{\emph{(\lx@cref{creftypecap\lx@tilde refnum}{cl:Q_weighted_bound})}}.

∎

We next state the Azuma’s inequality which we will use in our proof.

Lemma 9
.

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
be random variables such
that for any
i
we have

{\textnormal{Pr}\left[|X_{i}|\leq c_{i}\right]}=1
and
for any sequence of values
x^{i-1}
we have

{\mathbb{E}\left[X_{i}\mid X^{i-1}=x^{i-1}\right]}\leq\mu_{i}
. Then
for any
t>0
,

\displaystyle{\textnormal{Pr}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}+t\right]}\leq e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}^{2}}}.

Note that the standard statement for Azuma’s inequality considers a supermartingale sequence
(Y_{i})
such that
{\textnormal{Pr}\left[\left|Y_{i}-Y_{i-1}\right|\leq c_{i}\right]}=1
and
{\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{i}\mid Y^{i-1}\right]}\leq Y_{i-1}
.
Applying the change of variable
Y_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{i}(X_{j}-\mu_{j})
, this directly yields the above lemma.

Lemma 10
.

For any
i\in[k]
,

\displaystyle{\textnormal{Pr}\left[(X,Z^{i})\notin\mathcal{G}_{i}\text{ and }(X,Z^{i})\in\mathcal{H}_{i}\right]}\leq\delta^{\prime}

Proof.

Define

g_{i}(x,z^{i})
as

\displaystyle g_{i}(x,z^{i}):=\begin{cases}f_{i}(x,z^{i})&\quad\text{if}\quad(x,z^{i})\in\mathcal{H}_{i}\\
0&\quad\text{otherwise}\end{cases}.

We claim that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}\left[g_{i}(X,Z^{i})\mid X=x,Z^{i-1}=z^{i-1}\right]}\leq 2\varepsilon_{i}\left(\frac{e^{2\varepsilon_{i}}}{1-\hat{\delta_{i}}}-1\right)+\psi_{i}(\delta_{i})

(18)

To see why, observe first that by total expectation,

{\mathbb{E}\left[g_{i}(X,Z^{i})\mid X=x,Z^{i-1}=z^{i-1}\right]}

is a weighted average of

{\mathbb{E}\left[g_{i}(X,Z^{i})\mid X=x,Z^{i-1}=z^{i-1},(X,Z^{i})\in\mathcal{H}_{i}\right]}

and

{\mathbb{E}\left[g_{i}(X,Z^{i})\mid X=x,Z^{i-1}=z^{i-1},(X,Z^{i})\notin\mathcal{H}_{i}\right]}.

The latter term equals
0
by definition of
g_{i}
.
It therefore suffices to show that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}\left[g_{i}(X,Z^{i})\mid X=x,Z^{i-1}=z^{i-1},(X,Z^{i})\in\mathcal{H}_{i}\right]}\leq 2\varepsilon_{i}\left(\frac{e^{2\varepsilon_{i}}}{1-\hat{\delta_{i}}}-1\right)+\psi_{i}(\delta_{i})

for all
(x,z^{i-1})
such that

{\textnormal{Pr}\left[(X,Z^{i})\in\mathcal{H}_{i}\mid X=x,Z^{i-1}=z^{i-1}\right]}>0
.
To prove this, observe that
we can assume

(x,z^{i-1})\in\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{i}
since otherwise,
the mentioned probability is
0
.
Since
(x,z^{i-1})\in\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{i}
, invoking
Lemma

8
finishes the proof.

Define

\displaystyle\rho_{i}=2\varepsilon_{i}\left(\frac{e^{2\varepsilon_{i}}}{1-\hat{\delta_{i}}}-1\right)+\psi_{i}(\delta_{i}).

By definition of
\mathcal{H}_{i}
, we have

\left|g_{i}(x,z^{i})\right|\leq 2\varepsilon_{i}
.
Invoking Azuma’s inequality and substituting the definition of
\varepsilon^{(i)}
,

\displaystyle{\textnormal{Pr}\left[\sum_{j\leq i}g_{j}(X,Z^{j})>\varepsilon^{(i)}\right]}

\displaystyle\leq{\textnormal{Pr}\left[\sum_{j\leq i}g_{j}(X,Z^{j})>\sum_{j\leq i}\rho_{j}+2\sqrt{2\sum_{j}\varepsilon_{j}^{2}\ln(1/\delta^{\prime})}\right]}

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\frac{8\sum_{j\leq i}\varepsilon_{j}^{2}\ln(1/\delta^{\prime})}{8\sum_{j\leq i}\varepsilon_{j}^{2}}}

\displaystyle=\delta^{\prime}

(19)

Observe however that,

\displaystyle{\textnormal{Pr}\left[(X,Z^{i})\notin\mathcal{G}_{i}\text{ and }(X,Z^{i})\in\mathcal{H}_{i}\right]}

\displaystyle={\textnormal{Pr}\left[\sum_{j\leq i}f_{j}(X,Z^{j})>\varepsilon^{(i)}\text{ and }(X,Z^{i})\in\mathcal{H}_{i}\right]}

\displaystyle={\textnormal{Pr}\left[\sum_{j\leq i}g_{j}(X,Z^{j})>\varepsilon^{(i)}\text{ and }(X,Z^{i})\in\mathcal{H}_{i}\right]}

\displaystyle\leq{\textnormal{Pr}\left[\sum_{j\leq i}g_{j}(X,Z^{j})>\varepsilon^{(i)}\right]}

where the first equality follows from the definition of
\mathcal{G}_{i}
and the second equality follows from
the definition of
g_{i}
.
Plugging
Equation

19
finishes the proof.
∎

The same result holds for the oracle transcript
\widetilde{Z}
with
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}_{i}
in place of
\mathcal{G}_{i}
.

Lemma 11
.

For any
i\in[k]
,

\displaystyle{\textnormal{Pr}\left[(X,\widetilde{Z}^{i})\notin\mathcal{G}^{\prime}_{i}\text{ and }(X,\widetilde{Z}^{i})\in\mathcal{H}_{i}\right]}\leq\delta^{\prime}

Proof.

The proof is identical to that of
Lemma

10
,
replacing
Z
with
\widetilde{Z}
and
f_{i}
with
-f_{i}
throughout.
In particular, define
\tilde{g}_{i}(x,z^{i})=-f_{i}(x,z^{i})
if
(x,z^{i})\in\mathcal{H}_{i}

and
0
otherwise. By
Lemma

8
(Part (2)),

{\mathbb{E}\left[\tilde{g}_{i}(X,\widetilde{Z}^{i})\mid X=x,\widetilde{Z}^{i-1}=z^{i-1}\right]}\leq\rho_{i}

for any
(x,z^{i-1})\in\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{i}
.
Since
\left|\tilde{g}_{i}\right|\leq 2\varepsilon_{i}
, Azuma’s inequality gives

{\textnormal{Pr}\left[\sum_{j\leq i}\tilde{g}_{j}(X,\widetilde{Z}^{j})>\varepsilon^{(i)}\right]}\leq\delta^{\prime}
.
The event
\{(X,\widetilde{Z}^{i})\notin\mathcal{G}^{\prime}_{i}\text{ and }(X,\widetilde{Z}^{i})\in\mathcal{H}_{i}\}

\sum_{j\leq i}(-f_{j}(X,\widetilde{Z}^{j}))>\varepsilon^{(i)}

(by definition of

.
∎

We next claim the following.

Lemma 12
.

For any
i\in[k]
,

{\textnormal{Pr}\left[(X,\widetilde{Z}^{i-1})\notin\mathcal{C}_{i}\right]}\leq\gamma_{i}
.

Proof.

By the PG assumption on
\mathcal{A}_{i}
, for any fixed prefix

Lemma 13
.

For any
i\in[k]
,

{\textnormal{Pr}\left[(X,\widetilde{Z}^{i-1})\notin\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{i}\right]}\leq\sum_{\ell\leq i}\gamma_{\ell}+\sum_{\ell<i}\hat{\delta}_{\ell}
.

Proof.

The event
(X,\widetilde{Z}^{i-1})\notin\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{i}
implies either

(X,\widetilde{Z}^{\ell-1})\notin\mathcal{C}_{\ell}
for some
\ell\leq i
,
or
(X,\widetilde{Z}^{\ell})\notin\mathcal{E}_{\ell}
with
(X,\widetilde{Z}^{\ell-1})\in\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{\ell}
for some
\ell<i
.
The former has probability
\leq\gamma_{\ell}
by
Lemma

12
;
the latter has probability
\leq\hat{\delta}_{\ell}
by
Lemma

7
.
A union bound gives the result.
∎

As outlined in the sketch, using induction, this allows us to obtain the following.

Lemma 14
.

For any
i\in[k]
,

\displaystyle{\textnormal{Pr}\left[(X,Z^{i-1})\notin\mathcal{C}_{i},\;(X,Z^{i-1})\in\mathcal{G}_{i-1}\right]}\leq e^{\varepsilon^{(i-1)}}\gamma_{i},

where
\mathcal{G}_{0}
denotes the entire space (so the condition is vacuous for
i=1
).

Proof.

For fixed
x
, on the event
(x,Z^{i-1})\in\mathcal{G}_{i-1}
we have

F_{i-1}(x,Z^{i-1})\leq\varepsilon^{(i-1)}
, i.e., the log-likelihood ratio of

Z^{i-1}\mid X\!=\!x
versus
\widetilde{Z}^{i-1}
is bounded pointwise. Therefore,
for any event
E
over prefixes:

\displaystyle{\textnormal{Pr}\left[Z^{i-1}\in E,\;(x,Z^{i-1})\in\mathcal{G}_{i-1}\mid X=x\right]}\leq e^{\varepsilon^{(i-1)}}{\textnormal{Pr}\left[\widetilde{Z}^{i-1}\in E\right]}.

Applying this with
E=\left\{z^{i-1}:(x,z^{i-1})\notin\mathcal{C}_{i}\right\}
:

\displaystyle{\textnormal{Pr}\left[(x,Z^{i-1})\notin\mathcal{C}_{i},\;(x,Z^{i-1})\in\mathcal{G}_{i-1}\mid X=x\right]}\leq e^{\varepsilon^{(i-1)}}{\textnormal{Pr}\left[(x,\widetilde{Z}^{i-1})\notin\mathcal{C}_{i}\right]}.

Taking expectation over
X
and using
X\perp\widetilde{Z}^{i-1}
:

\displaystyle{\textnormal{Pr}\left[(X,Z^{i-1})\notin\mathcal{C}_{i},\;(X,Z^{i-1})\in\mathcal{G}_{i-1}\right]}\leq e^{\varepsilon^{(i-1)}}{\textnormal{Pr}\left[(X,\widetilde{Z}^{i-1})\notin\mathcal{C}_{i}\right]}\leq e^{\varepsilon^{(i-1)}}\gamma_{i},

where the last step uses
Lemma

12
.
∎

Lemma 15
.

For any
j\in[k]
,

\displaystyle{\textnormal{Pr}\left[(X,Z^{j})\notin\mathcal{H}_{j}\right]}\leq\sum_{\ell\leq j}{\textnormal{Pr}\left[(X,Z^{\ell-1})\notin\mathcal{C}_{\ell}\right]}+\sum_{\ell\leq j}\hat{\delta}_{\ell}.

Proof.

Consider the sequential process generating
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{j}
.
At step
\ell
: first check
(X,Z^{\ell-1})\in\mathcal{C}_{\ell}
; if not, we call this
a
type-1 failure
.
Otherwise, if no prior failure occurred,
(X,Z^{\ell-1})\in\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{\ell}
,
so by
Lemma

7
,

{\textnormal{Pr}\left[(X,Z^{\ell})\notin\mathcal{E}_{\ell},\;(X,Z^{\ell-1})\in\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{\ell}\right]}\leq\hat{\delta}_{\ell}
;
we call this a
type-2 failure
.
The event
(X,Z^{j})\notin\mathcal{H}_{j}
implies a type-1 or type-2 failure at some step

\ell\leq j
; a union bound gives the result.
∎

Combined with
Lemmas

10
,
11
,
14
,
12
and
7
, this gives us the following.

6
Lower Bound for Adaptive Composition

We demonstrate that while nonadaptive composition of replicable algorithms can be achieved with nearly linear sample complexity, extending this guarantee to an adaptive adversary necessarily incurs a quadratic cost in the number of rounds.

Definition 4

(Adaptive Composition against a Random Adversary)
.

Let
\mathcal{T}
be a domain of statistical problems.
An adaptive game of
k
rounds between a random adversary and an algorithm
\mathcal{A}
proceeds as follows.
The adversary possesses internal random bits
\widetilde{r}
.
For each round
i\in[k]
:

1.

The adversary announces a statistical problem
\mathcal{T}_{i}=\operatorname{Adv}(y_{1},\dots,y_{i-1};\widetilde{r})
,
potentially depending on previous outputs.
The adversary also selects a distribution
\mathcal{D}_{i}
with
(\mathcal{D}_{i},G_{\mathcal{D}_{i}})\in\mathcal{T}_{i}
;
this choice is not revealed to
\mathcal{A}
.

2.

The algorithm
\mathcal{A}
, using fixed internal coins
r
, observes
\mathcal{T}_{i}
,
draws
m
independent samples
S_{i}\sim\mathcal{D}_{i}^{m}
,
and produces an output
y_{i}=\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{T}_{i},S_{1},\dots,S_{i},y_{1},\dots,y_{i-1};r)
.
A valid output satisfies
y_{i}\in G_{\mathcal{D}_{i}}
.

The algorithm
\mathcal{A}
is
\rho
-replicable in the adaptive setting if, upon resampling all datasets
S=(S_{1},\dots,S_{k})
and
S^{\prime}=(S^{\prime}_{1},\dots,S^{\prime}_{k})
while keeping the coins
r
and
\widetilde{r}
fixed, the sequence of outputs matches with high probability:

\displaystyle\textnormal{Pr}_{r,\widetilde{r},S,S^{\prime}}\left[(y_{1},\dots,y_{k})=(y^{\prime}_{1},\dots,y^{\prime}_{k})\right]\geq 1-\rho.

To establish the lower bound, we rely on the following lemma concerning threshold estimation, demonstrating that forcing high accuracy over a sharp decision boundary induces an unavoidable replicability error dictated by the sample noise floor.

The lemma is analogous to Lemma 7.2 in
[
27
]
, but differs in two important respects tailored to our adaptive setting.
First, the lemma is stated for a
fixed
choice of internal coins
r
: for any fixed
r
, either the algorithm’s correctness or its replicability (with that same
r
) must take a hit.
Second, the hard instance
\theta
is drawn from a
fixed known distribution

\mathcal{P}
rather than being an arbitrary worst-case parameter.

These two features work together in the proof of
Theorem

10
.
Because
r
is fixed, the algorithm’s behavior on each round is a deterministic function of the samples alone, which ensures that the errors across rounds are not correlated with each other—without fixing
r
, the algorithm could in principle correlate its randomness across rounds to avoid simultaneous failures.
Because the hard instance is drawn fresh from the known distribution
\mathcal{P}
at each round (independently of history), the algorithm cannot “learn” anything useful from earlier rounds: the second problem instance is statistically no easier than the first, even after observing the samples and outcome of round one.
Together, these properties allow us to re-use the single-round hardness argument across all
k
rounds of the adaptive game.

Lemma 18

(Replicability Lower Bound for Threshold Estimation)
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
\tau\in(0,1/4)
.
Let
\Theta=[1/2-\tau,1/2+\tau]
and define an adversary distribution
\mathcal{P}
over
\Theta
such that
\theta=1/2-\tau
with probability
1/3
,
\theta=1/2+\tau
with probability
1/3
, and
\theta
is drawn uniformly from
(1/2-\tau,1/2+\tau)
with probability
1/3
.

Define the set of valid outputs for each
\theta\in\Theta
as

\displaystyle G_{\theta}=\begin{cases}\{-\}&\theta=1/2-\tau,\\
\{+\}&\theta=1/2+\tau,\\
\{+,-\}&\theta\in(1/2-\tau,1/2+\tau).\end{cases}

Let
\mathcal{A}
be any randomized algorithm mapping
m
samples
S\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(\theta)^{m}
and internal coins
r
to a sign in
\{+,-\}
.
For fixed internal coins
r
, if
\mathcal{A}
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\mathcal{P}}\left[\textnormal{Pr}_{S\sim\theta^{m}}\left[\mathcal{A}(S;r)\in G_{\theta}\right]\right]\geq 1-\frac{1}{16},

then, with
r
still fixed,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\mathcal{P}}\left[\textnormal{Pr}_{S,S^{\prime}\sim\theta^{m}}\left[\mathcal{A}(S;r)\neq\mathcal{A}(S^{\prime};r)\right]\right]\geq\Omega\left(\frac{1}{\tau\sqrt{m}}\right).

(25)

Proof.

Fix the internal coins
r
.
Let
p_{+}(\theta)=\textnormal{Pr}_{S\sim\theta^{m}}\left[\mathcal{A}(S;r)=+\right]
denote the probability that
\mathcal{A}
outputs
+
, with
r
held fixed.

Since
G_{\theta}=\{+,-\}
for interior
\theta
, the hypothesis gives expected failure probability at most
1/16
:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{3}p_{+}(1/2-\tau)+\frac{1}{3}\left(1-p_{+}(1/2+\tau)\right)\leq\frac{1}{16}.

Since both terms are non-negative, each is individually at most
1/16
, yielding

p_{+}(1/2-\tau)\leq 3/16
and
p_{+}(1/2+\tau)\geq 13/16
.

For a fixed target
\theta
, given that exactly
j
out of
m
samples are ones, the sequence is uniformly distributed among all Boolean vectors of Hamming weight
j
.
Let
a_{j}\in[0,1]
denote the fraction of sample sequences of weight
j
for which
\mathcal{A}(\cdot;r)
outputs
+
.
We express the probability of outputting
+
as a polynomial:

\displaystyle p_{+}(\theta)=\sum_{j=0}^{m}a_{j}\binom{m}{j}\theta^{j}(1-\theta)^{m-j}.

By the Intermediate Value Theorem, since
p_{+}(1/2-\tau)<1/2
and
p_{+}(1/2+\tau)>1/2
, there is a point
q\in(1/2-\tau,1/2+\tau)
where
p_{+}(q)=1/2
.
Taking the derivative yields:

\displaystyle p_{+}^{\prime}(\theta)=\sum_{j=0}^{m}a_{j}\binom{m}{j}\theta^{j}(1-\theta)^{m-j}\frac{j-m\theta}{\theta(1-\theta)}.

For
\tau<1/4
, we have
\theta\in(1/4,3/4)
, meaning the denominator
\theta(1-\theta)>3/16
.
Since
0\leq a_{j}\leq 1
, the absolute derivative is bounded by
6\,{\mathbb{E}\left[\left|X-{\mathbb{E}\left[X\right]}\right|\right]}
where
X\sim\operatorname{Bin}(m,\theta)
.
As the expected absolute deviation is bounded by the standard deviation
\sqrt{m\theta(1-\theta)}
, we have
\left|p_{+}^{\prime}(\theta)\right|=O(\sqrt{m})
.

Because
\left|p_{+}^{\prime}(\theta)\right|=O(\sqrt{m})
and
p_{+}(q)=1/2
, there exists an interval
I
around
q
of length
\Omega(1/\sqrt{m})
wherein
1/3<p_{+}(\theta)<2/3
.
The adversary distribution
\mathcal{P}
places
1/3
uniform probability mass on
(1/2-\tau,1/2+\tau)
.
The probability that a randomly drawn
\theta
lands in
I
is at least
\frac{1}{3}\cdot\frac{\left|I\right|}{2\tau}=\Omega\left(\frac{1}{\tau\sqrt{m}}\right)
.
For any
\theta\in I
, the disagreement probability
\textnormal{Pr}_{S,S^{\prime}\sim\theta^{m}}\left[\mathcal{A}(S;r)\neq\mathcal{A}(S^{\prime};r)\right]=2p_{+}(\theta)(1-p_{+}(\theta))>4/9
(with
r
fixed), so

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\mathcal{P}}\left[\textnormal{Pr}_{S,S^{\prime}\sim\theta^{m}}\left[\mathcal{A}(S;r)\neq\mathcal{A}(S^{\prime};r)\right]\right]\geq\frac{4}{9}\cdot\textnormal{Pr}_{\theta\sim\mathcal{P}}\left[\theta\in I\right]=\Omega\left(\frac{1}{\tau\sqrt{m}}\right),

as required.
∎

Assuming

•

By the triangle inequality:
{\textnormal{Pr}\left[y^{\prime}_{i}\neq y^{\prime\prime}_{i}\mid\mathcal{F}_{i-1},r,\theta_{i}\right]}\leq 2{\textnormal{Pr}\left[y_{i}\neq y^{\prime}_{i}\mid\mathcal{F}_{i-1},r,\theta_{i}\right]}
.

•

Lemma

18
applied to
\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{T},\cdot,H^{\prime}_{i-1};r)
gives
\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{i}\sim\mathcal{P}}\left[\textnormal{Pr}_{S^{\prime}_{i},S^{\prime\prime}_{i}\sim\theta_{i}^{m}}\left[y^{\prime}_{i}\neq y^{\prime\prime}_{i}\mid\mathcal{F}_{i-1},r,\theta_{i}\right]\right]\geq pX^{\prime}_{i}
.

Hence
\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{i}\sim\mathcal{P}}\left[{\textnormal{Pr}\left[y_{i}\neq y^{\prime}_{i}\mid\mathcal{F}_{i-1},r,\theta_{i}\right]}\right]\geq\frac{p}{2}X^{\prime}_{i}
,
so
{\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathds{1}\left\{y_{i}=y^{\prime}_{i}\right\}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{i-1},r\right]}\leq 1-\frac{p}{2}X^{\prime}_{i}
.
Therefore
{\mathbb{E}\left[M_{i}\mid\mathcal{F}_{i-1},r\right]}\leq M_{i-1}(1-\frac{p}{2}X^{\prime}_{i})
always
(trivially on
\left\{M_{i-1}=0\right\}
).
Since
M
is non-increasing (
M_{k}\leq M_{i-1}
pointwise for all
i\leq k
), we proceed via additive telescoping.
Taking expectations of
{\mathbb{E}\left[M_{i}\mid\mathcal{F}_{i-1},r\right]}\leq M_{i-1}(1-\frac{p}{2}X^{\prime}_{i})
and using
M_{i-1}\geq M_{k}
:

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}\left[M_{i-1}-M_{i}\mid r\right]}\geq\tfrac{p}{2}{\mathbb{E}\left[M_{i-1}X^{\prime}_{i}\mid r\right]}\geq\tfrac{p}{2}{\mathbb{E}\left[M_{k}X^{\prime}_{i}\mid r\right]}.

Summing from
i=1
to
k
and using

:

\displaystyle 1-\mathbb{E}_{\widetilde{r},S,S^{\prime}}\left[M_{k}\mid r\right]\geq\tfrac{p}{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{\widetilde{r},S,S^{\prime}}\left[M_{k}Y^{\prime}\mid r\right].

Since
Y^{\prime}
is identically distributed to
Y
, we have
{\mathbb{E}\left[Y^{\prime}\mid r\right]}\geq k/2
.
Using
Y^{\prime}\leq k
and
1-M_{k}\geq 0
:

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}\left[M_{k}Y^{\prime}\mid r\right]}={\mathbb{E}\left[Y^{\prime}\mid r\right]}-{\mathbb{E}\left[(1-M_{k})Y^{\prime}\mid r\right]}\geq\tfrac{k}{2}-k\left(1-{\mathbb{E}\left[M_{k}\mid r\right]}\right).

Substituting the bound on
{\mathbb{E}\left[M_{k}Y^{\prime}\mid r\right]}
into

:

\displaystyle 1-{\mathbb{E}\left[M_{k}\mid r\right]}\geq\tfrac{p}{2}\Bigl(\tfrac{k}{2}-k\left(1-{\mathbb{E}\left[M_{k}\mid r\right]}\right)\Bigr)=\tfrac{pk}{4}-\tfrac{pk}{2}\left(1-{\mathbb{E}\left[M_{k}\mid r\right]}\right).

Collecting the
\left(1-{\mathbb{E}\left[M_{k}\mid r\right]}\right)
terms:

\displaystyle\left(1-{\mathbb{E}\left[M_{k}\mid r\right]}\right)\left(1+\tfrac{pk}{2}\right)\geq\tfrac{pk}{4},\quad\text{so}\quad\rho(r)=1-\mathbb{E}_{\widetilde{r},S,S^{\prime}}\left[M_{k}\mid r\right]\geq\tfrac{pk/4}{1+pk/2}.

Case 2: Many inaccurate rounds
(
{\mathbb{E}\left[Y\right]}<k/2
).
Here
{\mathbb{E}\left[Z\right]}\geq k/2
, where
Z=\sum_{i=1}^{k}(1-X_{i})
counts the inaccurate rounds.
Let
N_{i}=\prod_{j=1}^{i}{\mathds{1}\left\{y_{j}\in G_{\theta_{j}}\right\}}
be the indicator that all rounds

are answered correctly, with
N_{0}=1
.
Since
X_{i}
and
N_{i-1}
are both
\mathcal{F}_{i-1}
-measurable, and an inaccurate round satisfies

\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{i}\sim\mathcal{P}}\left[\textnormal{Pr}_{S_{i}\sim\theta_{i}^{m}}\left[\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{T},S_{i},H_{i-1};r)\in G_{\theta_{i}}\right]\right]<15/16
,
we have
{\mathbb{E}\left[N_{i}\mid\mathcal{F}_{i-1},r\right]}\leq N_{i-1}\left(1-\tfrac{1}{16}(1-X_{i})\right)
, and hence:

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}\left[N_{i-1}-N_{i}\mid\mathcal{F}_{i-1},r\right]}\geq\tfrac{1}{16}N_{i-1}(1-X_{i})\geq\tfrac{1}{16}N_{k}(1-X_{i}).

Taking expectations and summing from
i=1
to
k
:

\displaystyle 1-{\mathbb{E}\left[N_{k}\mid r\right]}\geq\tfrac{1}{16}\,{\mathbb{E}\left[N_{k}Z\mid r\right]}.

Using
Z\leq k
and
1-N_{k}\geq 0

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}\left[N_{k}Z\mid r\right]}={\mathbb{E}\left[Z\mid r\right]}-{\mathbb{E}\left[(1-N_{k})Z\mid r\right]}\geq\tfrac{k}{2}-k\left(1-{\mathbb{E}\left[N_{k}\mid r\right]}\right).

Substituting and rearranging:

\displaystyle\left(1-{\mathbb{E}\left[N_{k}\mid r\right]}\right)\left(1+\tfrac{k}{16}\right)\geq\tfrac{k}{32},\quad\text{so}\quad\beta(r)=1-\textnormal{Pr}_{\widetilde{r},S}\left[\text{all correct}\mid r\right]\geq\tfrac{k/32}{1+k/16}.

∎

We now derive the theorem from
4
.
Let
R_{1}=\left\{r:{\mathbb{E}\left[Y\right]}\geq k/2\right\}
and
R_{2}=\left\{r:{\mathbb{E}\left[Y\right]}<k/2\right\}
be a partition of the support of

.
Since
\textnormal{Pr}_{r}\left[R_{1}\right]+\textnormal{Pr}_{r}\left[R_{2}\right]=1
(where
{\textnormal{Pr}\left[\cdot\right]}
is over the randomness of

),

or

must hold.

If
{\textnormal{Pr}\left[R_{2}\right]}\geq 1/2
: averaging

over

gives

,
which exceeds

for large enough

—a contradiction.
Hence
{\textnormal{Pr}\left[R_{1}\right]}\geq 1/2
, and averaging

over

:

\displaystyle\rho\geq\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{pk/4}{1+pk/2}=\frac{pk/8}{1+pk/2}.

Since

is increasing and positive, keeping

Combined with

∎

6.1
Replicable Algorithms and Sufficient Statistics

A classical principle in statistics is that a sufficient statistic captures all the information a sample carries about the underlying distribution.
In this section, we show that this principle extends to replicability:
any replicable algorithm for a problem over
\mathcal{F}
can be converted into an equally accurate algorithm that operates only on a sufficient statistic of its input, with replicability preserved for distributions in
\mathcal{F}
.

We first recall the definition of a sufficient statistic.

Definition 5

(Sufficient Statistic)
.

Let
\mathcal{F}=\{D_{\theta}:\theta\in\Theta\}
be a parametric family of distributions over
\mathcal{X}
.
A function
f:\mathcal{X}^{n}\to\mathcal{Z}
is a
sufficient statistic
for
\mathcal{F}
if the conditional distribution of
S\sim D_{\theta}^{n}
given
f(S)=t
does not depend on
\theta
, for every
t
in the range of
f
.

The key algorithmic consequence of sufficiency is that one can resample a fresh dataset
S^{\prime}
with the same marginal distribution as
S
, using only
f(S)
and fresh randomness, without knowing the underlying parameter
\theta
.
Concretely, for any
t
in the range of
f
, let
\mu_{t}
denote the conditional distribution of
S
given
f(S)=t
;
this is well-defined and
\theta
-free by sufficiency.
Given
f(S)=t
and fresh randomness
r^{\prime}
, one can draw
S^{\prime}\sim\mu_{t}
using
r^{\prime}
; we write
S^{\prime}=\mathrm{Resample}(f(S),r^{\prime})
.

Lemma 19
.

Let
f
be a sufficient statistic for
\mathcal{F}
, and let
S\sim D_{\theta}^{n}
for some
\theta\in\Theta
.
Let
r^{\prime}
be independent of
S
and let
S^{\prime}=\mathrm{Resample}(f(S),r^{\prime})
.
Then
(f(S),S^{\prime})\overset{d}{=}(f(S),S)
,
and in particular
S^{\prime}\sim D_{\theta}^{n}
.

Proof.

We give the argument for the case when
\mathcal{X}
is discrete; the continuous case is analogous
with probability mass functions replaced by densities.
For any
t
and
s
,

\displaystyle{\textnormal{Pr}\left[f(S)=t,\;S^{\prime}=s\right]}

\displaystyle={\textnormal{Pr}\left[f(S)=t\right]}\cdot{\textnormal{Pr}\left[S^{\prime}=s\mid f(S)=t\right]}

\displaystyle={\textnormal{Pr}\left[f(S)=t\right]}\cdot\mu_{t}(s)

\displaystyle={\textnormal{Pr}\left[f(S)=t\right]}\cdot{\textnormal{Pr}\left[S=s\mid f(S)=t\right]}

\displaystyle={\textnormal{Pr}\left[f(S)=t,\;S=s\right]},

where the second equality uses that
S^{\prime}\sim\mu_{t}
by definition of
\mathrm{Resample}
,
and the third uses the definition of
\mu_{t}
.
Hence
(f(S),S^{\prime})\overset{d}{=}(f(S),S)
.
Marginalizing over
t
gives
S^{\prime}\overset{d}{=}S\sim D_{\theta}^{n}
.
∎

We can now state and prove the main result of this section.

Theorem 11
.

Let
\mathcal{A}:\mathcal{X}^{n}\to\mathcal{Y}
be a
\rho
-replicable algorithm for a statistical problem
\mathcal{T}
over the family
\mathcal{F}=\{D_{\theta}:\theta\in\Theta\}
, with failure probability
\beta
.
Let
f:\mathcal{X}^{n}\to\mathcal{Z}
be a sufficient statistic for
\mathcal{F}
.
Then there exists an algorithm
\mathcal{B}:\mathcal{X}^{n}\to\mathcal{Y}
for
\mathcal{T}
with the same sample complexity and failure probability
\beta
, such that
\mathcal{B}(S;r,r^{\prime})=\mathcal{A}(\mathrm{Resample}(f(S),r^{\prime});r)
for all
S
, and for every
\theta\in\Theta
and independent
S_{1},S_{2}\sim D_{\theta}^{n}
:

\displaystyle\textnormal{Pr}_{r,r^{\prime}}\left[\mathcal{B}(S_{1};r,r^{\prime})=\mathcal{B}(S_{2};r,r^{\prime})\right]\geq 1-2\rho.

In particular, the output of
\mathcal{B}
depends on
S
only through
f(S)
.

Proof.

Define
\mathcal{B}(S;r,r^{\prime})=\mathcal{A}(S^{\prime};r)
, where
S^{\prime}=\mathrm{Resample}(f(S),r^{\prime})
and
(r,r^{\prime})
are the shared random bits.

Correctness.

By
Lemma

19
,
S^{\prime}\overset{d}{=}S\sim D_{\theta}^{n}
.
Therefore the output distribution of
\mathcal{B}
is identical to that of
\mathcal{A}
run on a fresh sample from
D_{\theta}^{n}
, and
\mathcal{B}
has failure probability
\beta
.

Replicability.

Let
S_{1},S_{2}\overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}D_{\theta}^{n}
be two independent samples and let
(r,r^{\prime})
be shared random bits drawn independently of
S_{1},S_{2}
, with
r
and
r^{\prime}
independent of each other.
Set
S_{i}^{\prime}=\mathrm{Resample}(f(S_{i}),r^{\prime})
for
i=1,2
.
We want to show
{\textnormal{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}(S_{1};r,r^{\prime})=\mathcal{B}(S_{2};r,r^{\prime})\right]}\geq 1-2\rho
, i.e.,
{\textnormal{Pr}\left[\mathcal{A}(S_{1}^{\prime};r)=\mathcal{A}(S_{2}^{\prime};r)\right]}\geq 1-2\rho
.

Introduce an auxiliary sample
S^{*}\sim D_{\theta}^{n}
drawn independently of
(S_{1},S_{2},r,r^{\prime})
.
By a union bound,

\displaystyle{\textnormal{Pr}\left[\mathcal{A}(S_{1}^{\prime};r)\neq\mathcal{A}(S_{2}^{\prime};r)\right]}\leq{\textnormal{Pr}\left[\mathcal{A}(S_{1}^{\prime};r)\neq\mathcal{A}(S^{*};r)\right]}+{\textnormal{Pr}\left[\mathcal{A}(S^{*};r)\neq\mathcal{A}(S_{2}^{\prime};r)\right]}.

We bound each term using the
\rho
-replicability of
\mathcal{A}
.

•

S_{1}^{\prime}=\mathrm{Resample}(f(S_{1}),r^{\prime})
depends only on
(S_{1},r^{\prime})
, both of which are independent of
S^{*}
.
By
Lemma

19
,
S_{1}^{\prime}\sim D_{\theta}^{n}
.
Hence
(S_{1}^{\prime},S^{*})
are i.i.d. draws from
D_{\theta}^{n}
, independent of each other and of
r
,
so
{\textnormal{Pr}\left[\mathcal{A}(S_{1}^{\prime};r)\neq\mathcal{A}(S^{*};r)\right]}\leq\rho
.

•

Symmetrically,
S_{2}^{\prime}
depends only on
(S_{2},r^{\prime})
, independent of
S^{*}
, and
S_{2}^{\prime}\sim D_{\theta}^{n}
,
so
{\textnormal{Pr}\left[\mathcal{A}(S^{*};r)\neq\mathcal{A}(S_{2}^{\prime};r)\right]}\leq\rho
.

Therefore
{\textnormal{Pr}\left[\mathcal{A}(S_{1}^{\prime};r)=\mathcal{A}(S_{2}^{\prime};r)\right]}\geq 1-2\rho
, establishing the replicability guarantee for
D_{\theta}\in\mathcal{F}
.

Dependence on
f(S)
only.

By construction,
\mathcal{B}(S;r,r^{\prime})=\mathcal{A}(\mathrm{Resample}(f(S),r^{\prime});r)
, which depends on
S
only through
f(S)
.
∎

Remark 1
.

The factor-of-
2
loss in the replicability parameter is unavoidable in the above argument
because it relies on an intermediate auxiliary sample
S^{*}
to decouple
S_{1}^{\prime}
and
S_{2}^{\prime}
,
which share the random bits
r^{\prime}
.
In many applications this loss is negligible, since one can simply start
with a
(\rho/2)
-replicable algorithm and apply
Theorem

11
to
obtain a
\rho
-replicable algorithm at no asymptotic cost.
Concretely, given the replicability parameter boosting result of
[
27
]
, the dependence of sample complexity on
\rho
is always
1/\rho^{2}
in the worst case and as such the drawback is fairly mild.

Remark 2
.

Theorem

11
gives a useful design principle: when designing a replicable algorithm for a problem over a parametric family
\mathcal{F}
, it is enough to design one that operates on
f(S)
rather than the full sample
S
, since replicability (for distributions in
\mathcal{F}
) is preserved under the transformation.
For example, for problems over Bernoulli distributions, the sufficient statistic is
\sum_{i}S_{i}
, and it suffices to design replicable algorithms based on the empirical sum rather than the full sample.

Comparison with prior work.

A closely related argument result is proved by

[
1
]
in their
lower bound proof for replicable Gaussian mean testing.
Using the fact that the empirical mean
\bar{X}
is a sufficient statistic for
\mu
in

\mathcal{N}(\mu,I)
, they reduce, without loss of generality, to algorithms that depend only on

\bar{X}
.
Their reduction guarantees stability only when both datasets are drawn from the same member of the
parametric family
\mathcal{F}
; similarly,
Theorem

11
guarantees replicability for distributions
within
\mathcal{F}
.

Theorem

11
is stated as a general standalone transformation that applies to any problem and any sufficient statistic, rather than being specific to hypothesis testing.
It should be noted however that the approach of

[
1
]
preserves the replicability parameter exactly (no factor-of-
2
loss).

Theorem

11
incurs a factor-of-
2
loss due to the union bound over the phantom sample
but this is a negligible loss given that one can generally replace
\rho
with
\rho/2
with no asymptotic cost (see
Remark

1
)

6.2
Special Case: Order-Invariant Algorithms

We instantiate
Theorem

11
explicitly for the case of order invariance,
where the sufficient statistic is the unordered multiset of samples.
This serves both as a sanity check for the general proof and as a self-contained result
that is easy to state and verify directly.

Definition 6

(Order-Invariant Algorithm)
.

An algorithm
\mathcal{A}
is
(pointwise) order-invariant
if
for every sample
S=(S_{1},\ldots,S_{n})
, every permutation
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
,
and every fixed randomness
r
:

\mathcal{A}(S;r)=\mathcal{A}(\sigma(S);r).

Corollary 2
.

Let
\mathcal{A}:\mathcal{X}^{n}\to\mathcal{Y}
be a
\rho
-replicable algorithm for a statistical problem
\mathcal{T}

over an i.i.d. family with failure probability
\beta
.
Assume
\mathcal{X}
carries a total order.
Define
\mathcal{B}(S;\,r,r^{\prime})=\mathcal{A}(S^{\prime};\,r)
, where
S^{\prime}=\sigma(\mathrm{sort}(S))
,

\mathrm{sort}(S)
is the sample sorted in non-decreasing order,
and
\sigma\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(\mathfrak{S}_{n})
is drawn using
r^{\prime}
.
Then
\mathcal{B}
is
2\rho
-replicable, (pointwise) order-invariant, solves
\mathcal{T}
with failure probability
\beta
,
and uses the same number of samples as
\mathcal{A}
.

Proof.

This is
Theorem

11
instantiated with
f(S)=\{S_{1},\ldots,S_{n}\}
, the unordered multiset.
We verify the three conditions.

Sufficient statistic.

For any i.i.d. family, the conditional distribution of
(S_{1},\ldots,S_{n})
given the multiset

\{S_{1},\ldots,S_{n}\}=M
is the uniform distribution over all
n!
orderings of
M
,
independently of
D
.
Hence the multiset is a sufficient statistic.

Correctness.

Since
\sigma
is a uniformly random permutation independent of
S
,

S^{\prime}=\sigma(\mathrm{sort}(S))
is a uniformly random ordering of the multiset
\{S_{1},\ldots,S_{n}\}
,
i.e.,
S^{\prime}=\mathrm{Resample}(f(S),r^{\prime})
with
f(S)=\{S_{1},\ldots,S_{n}\}
.
By
Lemma

19
,
S^{\prime}\overset{d}{=}S\sim D^{n}
,
so
\mathcal{B}
inherits failure probability
\beta
.

Replicability.

Let
S_{1},S_{2}\overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}D^{n}
and
(r,r^{\prime})
shared, with
\sigma
determined by
r^{\prime}
.
Set
S_{i}^{\prime}=\sigma(\mathrm{sort}(S_{i}))
for
i=1,2
.
Introduce
S^{*}\sim D^{n}
independent of
(S_{1},S_{2},r,r^{\prime})
.
Each
S_{i}^{\prime}
is a function of
(S_{i},r^{\prime})
and hence independent of
S^{*}
;
by
Lemma

19
,
S_{i}^{\prime}\sim D^{n}
.
Hence
(S_{1}^{\prime},S^{*})
and
(S_{2}^{\prime},S^{*})
are each i.i.d. pairs from
D^{n}
, independent of
r
,
giving
{\textnormal{Pr}\left[\mathcal{A}(S_{1}^{\prime};r)\neq\mathcal{A}(S^{*};r)\right]}\leq\rho
and
{\textnormal{Pr}\left[\mathcal{A}(S^{*};r)\neq\mathcal{A}(S_{2}^{\prime};r)\right]}\leq\rho
.
By a union bound,
{\textnormal{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}(S_{1};r,r^{\prime})\neq\mathcal{B}(S_{2};r,r^{\prime})\right]}\leq 2\rho
.

Order invariance.

For any permutation
\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
and any fixed
(r,r^{\prime})
:

\mathrm{sort}(\tau(S))=\mathrm{sort}(S)
, so
S^{\prime}=\sigma(\mathrm{sort}(\tau(S)))=\sigma(\mathrm{sort}(S))
.
Hence
\mathcal{B}(\tau(S);\,r,r^{\prime})=\mathcal{A}(S^{\prime};\,r)=\mathcal{B}(S;\,r,r^{\prime})
as values, not merely in distribution.
∎

Comparison with prior work.

Theorem 1.2 of
[
1
]
establishes an analogous order-invariance result:
any
\rho
-replicable algorithm for testing a symmetric property of discrete distributions
can be converted to a
\rho
-replicable, order-invariant algorithm of the same sample complexity.
The two results differ in a number of ways.

Corollary

2
applies to any statistical problem and any (possibly non-binary) output space.

[
1
]
restrict to binary hypothesis testers.
In addition, we do not require the underlying problem to be symmetric

5
5

5

If the problem
is
symemtric,
[
1
]
further show that their algorithm is label order invariant,
whereas
Corollary

2
does not have any such implications.
We discuss this notion and how our techniques yield such a result in the general case in
Section

6.3

It should be noted however that the approach of

[
1
]
preserves the replicability parameter exactly (no factor-of-
2
loss).

Corollary

2
incurs a factor-of-
2
loss due to the union bound over the phantom sample
but this loss is negligible as one can generally replace
\rho
with
\rho/2
with no asymptotic cost (see
Remark

1
).

6.3
Label Invariance for Symmetric Problems

We show that for symmetric problems, any replicable algorithm can be converted to a label-invariant
one with no loss in the replicability parameter. We first define the notion of a label-invariant algorithm.

Definition 7

(Label-Invariant Algorithm)
.

An algorithm
\mathcal{A}:\mathcal{X}^{n}\to\mathcal{Y}
is
distributionally label-invariant
if for every sample

S=(S_{1},\ldots,S_{n})\in\mathcal{X}^{n}
and every permutation
\pi:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}
,

\mathcal{A}(S)
and
\mathcal{A}(\pi(S))
have the same output distribution (over all internal randomness of
\mathcal{A}
),
where
\pi(S)=(\pi(S_{1}),\ldots,\pi(S_{n}))
.
We say
\mathcal{A}
is
pointwise label-invariant
if

\mathcal{A}(S;r)=\mathcal{A}(\pi(S);r)
for shared randomness
r
.

Throughout this section,
\pi
denotes a permutation of the
domain

\mathcal{X}

(an element of
\mathfrak{S}_{|\mathcal{X}|}
), in contrast to the permutations of sample indices
in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
used in
Section

6.2
.

Definition 8

(Symmetric Problem)
.

A statistical problem
\mathcal{T}
over a discrete domain
\mathcal{X}
is
symmetric
if for every
distribution
D
over
\mathcal{X}
, every valid output
y\in G_{D}
, and every permutation
\pi:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}
,
the output
y
is also valid for the pushforward distribution
\pi_{*}(D)
,
where
\pi_{*}(D)(x)=D(\pi^{-1}(x))
.

Informally, a problem is symmetric if the correct answer depends only on the shape of the distribution,
not on which domain element has which probability.
Uniformity testing is the canonical example: whether a distribution is
\varepsilon
-close to uniform
is preserved under any relabeling of
\mathcal{X}
.

Corollary 3
.

Let
\mathcal{A}:\mathcal{X}^{n}\to\mathcal{Y}
be a
\rho
-replicable algorithm for a symmetric statistical problem
\mathcal{T}

with failure probability
\beta
where
\mathcal{X}
is finite.
Define
\mathcal{B}(S;\,r,r^{\prime})=\mathcal{A}(\pi(S);\,r)
, where
\pi\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(\mathfrak{S}_{|\mathcal{X}|})

is drawn using
r^{\prime}
.
Then
\mathcal{B}
is
\rho
-replicable, distributionally label-invariant, solves
\mathcal{T}
with failure probability
\beta
,
and uses the same number of samples as
\mathcal{A}
.

Proof.

Correctness.

For any distribution
D
and fixed
\pi
, the sample
\pi(S)
is i.i.d. from
\pi_{*}(D)
.
Since
\mathcal{T}
is symmetric, any output valid for
\pi_{*}(D)
is also valid for
D
.
Since
\mathcal{A}
has failure probability
\beta
on every distribution including
\pi_{*}(D)
,
so does
\mathcal{B}
on
D
.

Replicability.

Let
S_{1},S_{2}\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}D^{n}
and let
(r,r^{\prime})
be shared random bits,
with
r
and
r^{\prime}
independent of each other and of
S_{1},S_{2}
.
Fix
\pi
determined by
r^{\prime}
.
Since
S_{1},S_{2}
are i.i.d. and
\pi
is shared,

\pi(S_{1})
and
\pi(S_{2})
are i.i.d. from
\pi_{*}(D)
.
By
\rho
-replicability of
\mathcal{A}
applied to
\pi_{*}(D)
:

\displaystyle{\textnormal{Pr}\left[\mathcal{A}(\pi(S_{1});r)=\mathcal{A}(\pi(S_{2});r)\right]}\geq 1-\rho.

This holds for every fixed
\pi
, hence after averaging over
r^{\prime}
:

{\textnormal{Pr}\left[\mathcal{B}(S_{1};r,r^{\prime})=\mathcal{B}(S_{2};r,r^{\prime})\right]}\geq 1-\rho
.

Distributional label invariance.

Fix any sample
S
and any domain permutation
\sigma
.
For any fixed
r
, we have
\mathcal{B}(S;r,r^{\prime})=\mathcal{A}(\pi(S);r)
and

\mathcal{B}(\sigma(S);r,r^{\prime})=\mathcal{A}((\pi\circ\sigma)(S);r)
, where
\pi
is determined by
r^{\prime}
.
As
r^{\prime}
varies,
\pi
is uniform over
\mathfrak{S}_{|\mathcal{X}|}
;
since left-composition by the fixed
\sigma
is a bijection on
\mathfrak{S}_{|\mathcal{X}|}
,

\pi\circ\sigma
has the same distribution over
r^{\prime}
.
Hence for every fixed
r
,
\mathcal{B}(S;r,r^{\prime})
and
\mathcal{B}(\sigma(S);r,r^{\prime})
have the same distribution over
r^{\prime}
.
Since this holds for every
r
, the two have the same distribution over
(r,r^{\prime})
.
∎

Remark 3
.

Unlike
Theorem

11
,
Corollary

3
incurs no loss in the replicability parameter.
The reason is structural: the permutation
\pi
is shared across both runs, so
\pi(S_{1})
and
\pi(S_{2})

are i.i.d. from the same distribution
\pi_{*}(D)
, and replicability of
\mathcal{A}
applies in one step
without a phantom sample argument.

6.4
Pointwise Label Invariance via Correlated Sampling

Corollary

3
produces an algorithm that is distributionally label-invariant
(
Definition

7
): for any fixed sample
S
, the output distribution is unchanged under domain relabeling.
We now show this can be strengthened to
pointwise
label invariance—the notion used by

[
35
]
(their Definition 1.5)—where for every fixed seed
r
, the output
is identical under any relabeling.

The key tool is
correlated sampling
, formalized below following
[
8
]
.

Definition 9

(Correlated Sampling
[
7
]
)
.

A
correlated sampling strategy
for a finite set
\mathcal{Y}
with error function

\varepsilon:[0,1]\to[0,1]
is a deterministic function
\mathrm{CS}

that takes a distribution
P\in\Delta(\mathcal{Y})
and a random string
r
and outputs an element of
\mathcal{Y}
,
satisfying:

•

(Marginal correctness)
For all
P\in\Delta(\mathcal{Y})
and
y\in\mathcal{Y}
:

{\textnormal{Pr}\left[\mathrm{CS}(P,r)=y\right]}=P(y)
.

•

(Error guarantee)
For all
P,Q\in\Delta(\mathcal{Y})
:

{\textnormal{Pr}\left[\mathrm{CS}(P,r)\neq\mathrm{CS}(Q,r)\right]}\leq\varepsilon\!\left(\mathrm{TV}(P,Q)\right)
,
where
r
is the same random string in both calls.

In particular,
\varepsilon(0)=0
, so
\mathrm{CS}(P,r)=\mathrm{CS}(Q,r)
with probability
1
whenever
P=Q
.

[
7
]
construct a correlated sampling strategy achieving

\varepsilon(\delta)\leq\frac{2\delta}{1+\delta}
.

Corollary 4
.

Let
\mathcal{A}:\mathcal{X}^{n}\to\mathcal{Y}
be a
\rho
-replicable algorithm for a symmetric statistical
problem
\mathcal{T}
with failure probability
\beta
, where
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
are finite.
Let
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
be the algorithm from
Corollary

3
, and let
P_{S}\in\Delta(\mathcal{Y})
denote
the output distribution of
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(S)
over its internal randomness.
Define
\mathcal{B}(S;r)=\mathrm{CS}(P_{S},r)

[
7
]
,
which satisfies
\varepsilon(\delta)\leq\frac{2\delta}{1+\delta}

Then
\mathcal{B}
is
2\rho
-replicable, satisfies
\mathcal{B}(S;r)=\mathcal{B}(\sigma(S);r)

\sigma
and all fixed

, solves
\mathcal{T}
with failure probability
\beta
,
and uses the same number of samples as
\mathcal{A}
.

Proof.

Pointwise label invariance.

By
Corollary

3
,
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
is distributionally label-invariant, so
P_{S}=P_{\sigma(S)}

for every
\sigma
and every
S
.
Since
\mathrm{CS}
is a deterministic function of the distribution and the seed,

\mathcal{B}(S;r)=\mathrm{CS}(P_{S},r)=\mathrm{CS}(P_{\sigma(S)},r)=\mathcal{B}(\sigma(S);r)

for every fixed
r
, where the middle equality uses
P_{S}=P_{\sigma(S)}
and the fact that

\mathrm{CS}
is a deterministic function (so equal inputs give equal outputs).

Replicability.

Let
S_{1},S_{2}\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}D^{n}
and let
r
be the shared CS seed, independent of
S_{1},S_{2}
.
Since
P_{S_{1}}
and
P_{S_{2}}
are fixed distributions once
S_{1},S_{2}
are fixed,
the error guarantee of
\mathrm{CS}
gives, for any fixed
S_{1},S_{2}

\displaystyle\textnormal{Pr}_{r}\left[\mathcal{B}(S_{1};r)\neq\mathcal{B}(S_{2};r)\right]\leq\varepsilon\!\left(\mathrm{TV}(P_{S_{1}},P_{S_{2}})\right)\leq\frac{2\,\mathrm{TV}(P_{S_{1}},P_{S_{2}})}{1+\mathrm{TV}(P_{S_{1}},P_{S_{2}})}\leq 2\,\mathrm{TV}(P_{S_{1}},P_{S_{2}}).

It remains to bound
\mathbb{E}_{S_{1},S_{2}}\left[\mathrm{TV}(P_{S_{1}},P_{S_{2}})\right]
.
Let
\tilde{r}
be an independent copy of the shared randomness of
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}

r
and
S_{1},S_{2}

Running
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(S_{1};\tilde{r})
and
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(S_{2};\tilde{r})
with the same
\tilde{r}
induces a coupling
of
P_{S_{1}}
and
P_{S_{2}}
, so by the definition of TV distance:

\displaystyle\mathrm{TV}(P_{S_{1}},P_{S_{2}})\leq\textnormal{Pr}_{\tilde{r}}\left[\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(S_{1};\tilde{r})\neq\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(S_{2};\tilde{r})\right].

Taking expectation over
S_{1},S_{2}
and combining:

\displaystyle\textnormal{Pr}_{S_{1},S_{2},r}\left[\mathcal{B}(S_{1};r)\neq\mathcal{B}(S_{2};r)\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2\,\mathbb{E}_{S_{1},S_{2}}\left[\mathrm{TV}(P_{S_{1}},P_{S_{2}})\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2\,\textnormal{Pr}_{S_{1},S_{2},\tilde{r}}\left[\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(S_{1};\tilde{r})\neq\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(S_{2};\tilde{r})\right]\leq 2\rho,

where the last inequality is
\rho
-replicability of
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}

Correctness.

By marginal correctness of
\mathrm{CS}
,
\mathcal{B}(S;r)\sim P_{S}
marginally over
r
.
Since
P_{S}
is the output distribution of
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(S)

\mathcal{A}^{\prime}

\mathcal{B}

∎

Remark 4
.

Corollary

4
is a structural result: computing
P_{S}
exactly
requires marginalizing over all internal randomness of
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
, which may be computationally
expensive. The result shows existence of a pointwise label-invariant algorithm, not
efficient constructibility.

Comparison with
[
1
]
.

Theorem 1.2 of
[
1
]
establishes label invariance for
\rho
-replicable
binary hypothesis testers of symmetric properties, with no loss in
\rho
.

Corollary

3
gives the same guarantee for
any

any

Their construction represents
\mathcal{A}
via a scalar acceptance probability
f(S)\in[0,1]

and symmetrizes by averaging over all

domain permutations; this is specific to binary output,
where a single scalar fully characterizes the output distribution.
Our construction applies a single random domain permutation from the shared randomness,
giving a black-box transformation that requires no structural access to
\mathcal{A}

Regarding the
strength
of the guarantee: their definition of label invariance

In general this loss is negligible as one can replace
\rho

1
).

7
Boosting Success Probability

In this section, we show that for a broad class of statistical problems, a replicable algorithm’s success probability can be boosted at relatively small cost in sample complexity.
Our results hold for problems parameterized by an
“accuracy”
parameter, which we will denote with
\alpha
, with smaller values corresponding to more accurate solutions.
The two properties we require from the problem are as follows:

1.

The dependence of the replicable sample complexity on the accuracy parameter is relatively mild (e.g., polynomial). More concretely, doubling the accuracy should not cause a dramatic increase in sample complexity.

2.

The accuracy should be, with high probability, approximately testable with a relatively small sample complexity. Note the test does not need to be replicable.

For these problems, we show that the dependence of the sample complexity on the failure probability is no worse than the dependence of either the tester (which need not be replicable) or a non-replicable algorithm for the original problem.
For most problems, designing a tester is no more difficult than designing a non-replicable algorithm as such, the dependence on
\beta
is the same as the non-replicable counter-part.
Formally, we prove the following theorem.

.

be a class of statistical problems

Remark 5
.

In many cases, such as the applications we discuss below, the sample complexity depends polynomially on the

7.1
Application: Statistical Queries

As a concrete example, consider the statistical query problem: given a function
\phi:\mathcal{X}\to[0,1]
, we aim to estimate
\mathbb{E}_{X\sim D}\left[\phi(X)\right]
with additive error
\alpha
.
The problem has both of the aforementioned properties.
The first property holds because the dependence on
\alpha
is polynomial; specifically the sample complexity scales with
\alpha^{-2}
. Consequently, replacing
\alpha
with
\alpha/2
causes the sample complexity to stay the same up to constant factors.
The second property holds because testing whether or not a value
\hat{\mu}
is at most
\alpha
away from the mean or it is at least
2\alpha
away from the mean requires at most
O(\frac{\log(1/\beta)}{\alpha^{2}})
samples where
\beta
denotes the failure probability. Concretely, we can first (non-replicably) estimate the mean with additive error
\alpha/4
. If the value
\hat{\mu}
is at most
\alpha
away from the mean, then, by the triangle inequality, it will be at most
5\alpha/4
away from our estimate.
Conversely, if it is at least
2\alpha
away from the mean then it will be at least
7\alpha/4
away from our estimate.
As such, we can invoke
Theorem

12
to obtain an improved sample complexity bound.
Formally, the following result holds.

Theorem 13
.

For any
\alpha,\rho,\beta>0
, there exists a
\rho
-replicable statistical oracle with error
\alpha
and sample complexity

\displaystyle n(\alpha,\beta,\rho)\leq O\left(\frac{\log(1/\rho)}{\alpha^{2}\rho^{2}}+\frac{\log(1/\beta)}{\alpha^{2}}\right)

Proof.

We assume without loss of generality that
\beta<\rho/4
.
If
\beta
is larger, we can simply invoke the theorem with
\beta=\rho/4
to get the same sample complexity bound.

As shown by
[
27
]
(see Theorem 2.3 in their paper), for
\beta<\rho/2
, there exists a replicable algorithm with sample complexity

=O(\frac{\log(1/\beta)}{\alpha^{2}(\rho-2\beta)^{2}}).

Since
2\beta<\rho/2
, this can be written as

O(\frac{\log(1/\beta)}{\alpha^{2}\rho^{2}})
.
While this bound is for
\beta<\rho/4
, it is clear that
an algorithm with failure probability
\beta
is also an algorithm with failure probability
\beta^{\prime}
for any
\beta^{\prime}>\beta
. Therefore,

\displaystyle n_{\textnormal{replic}}(\rho,\alpha,\beta)=O(\frac{\log(1/\min\{\rho,\beta\})}{\alpha^{2}\rho^{2}}).

A tester for the problem can be implemented as mentioned above.
Formally, to distinguish values that are at most
\alpha
far from the true mean from those that are at least
2\alpha
far, we can estimate the mean up to additive error
\alpha/4
and failure probability
\beta

with sample complexity

\displaystyle n_{\textnormal{tester}}(\alpha,\beta)=O(\frac{\log(1/\beta)}{\alpha^{2}}).

This follows from Hoeffding’s inequality which ensures that, using
n
samples, the probability that the empirical mean is more than
\alpha
away from the true mean is at most

2e^{-2n\alpha^{2}}
.
Letting
\hat{\mu}
denote the estimate,
we can then accept values that are closer than
3\alpha/2
to
\hat{\mu}
and reject those that are not.

Finally, by Hoeffding’s inequality, the sample complexity of the non-replicable version of the problem is bounded by

\displaystyle n_{\textnormal{non-replic}}(\alpha,\beta)=O(\frac{\log(1/\beta)}{\alpha^{2}}).

Plugging in
Theorem

12
, It follows that the total sample complexity is at most

\displaystyle n(\alpha,\beta,\rho)\leq O\left(\frac{\log(1/\rho)}{\alpha^{2}\rho^{2}}+\frac{\log(1/\beta)}{\alpha^{2}}\right).

as claimed.
∎

7.2
Application: PAC Learning

We now discuss the implementation of a tester for a PAC learner.
For simplicity, we focus only on realizable PAC learning as there are already multiple results from prior work for which our framework applies.

We start by implementing a tester as required by
Theorem

12
.
While the implementation is fairly standard, for completeness we provide a proof.

Lemma 20
.

Let
X
be a Bernoulli random variable with a unknown mean
\mu\in[0,1]
.
Let
\alpha\in(0,1)
be an arbitrary parameter.
There exists a tester with sample complexity
O(\log(1/\beta)/\alpha)

with the following property:

•

If
\mu\geq 2\alpha
, the algorithm outputs accept

•

If
\mu\leq\alpha
the algorithm outputs reject.

Proof.

Draw
n
i.i.d. samples
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\sim\mathrm{Bern}\left(\mu\right)
and
let
\hat{\mu}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
.
Set the threshold
\tau=\frac{3}{2}\alpha
and define the tester to

accept
if
\hat{\mu}\geq\tau
and
reject
otherwise.

We use the following variant of the Chernoff bound: for any
\delta>0
,

\displaystyle{\textnormal{Pr}\left[\left|\hat{\mu}-\mu\right|\geq\delta\mu\right]}\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\delta^{2}n\mu}{3}\right).

We first consider the case
\mu\geq 2\alpha
.
We bound
{\textnormal{Pr}\left[\hat{\mu}<\tau\right]}
, a lower-tail deviation.
It is clear that the probability is maximized for
\mu=2\alpha
so we assume, without loss of generality, that

\mu=2\alpha.

It follows that
\tau=(1-\delta)\mu
, where

\delta=1/4
.
By Chernoff,

\displaystyle{\textnormal{Pr}\left[\hat{\mu}<\tau\right]}={\textnormal{Pr}\left[\hat{\mu}\leq(1-\delta)\mu\right]}\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\delta^{2}n\mu}{3}\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{n\alpha}{24}\right).

Hence if
n\geq 24\log(1/\beta)/\alpha
, we have
{\textnormal{Pr}\left[\text{reject}\right]}\leq\beta
.

We next consider the case
\mu\leq\alpha
.
We now bound
{\textnormal{Pr}\left[\hat{\mu}\geq\tau\right]}
, an upper-tail deviation.
Similar to before, the probability is maximized for

\mu=\alpha
so we assume without loss of generality that this is the case.
Write
\tau=(1+\delta)\mu
, where

\delta=1/2
.
Then,

\displaystyle{\textnormal{Pr}\left[\hat{\mu}\geq\tau\right]}={\textnormal{Pr}\left[\hat{\mu}\geq(1+\delta)\mu\right]}\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\delta^{2}n\mu}{3}\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{n\alpha}{12}\right).

Thus, if
n\geq 12\log(1/\beta)/\alpha
, we have
{\textnormal{Pr}\left[\text{accept}\right]}\leq\beta
.

Thus, taking
n\geq 24\log(1/\beta)/\alpha
ensures both guarantees:

•

If
\mu\geq 2\alpha
, the tester accepts with probability at least
1-\beta
.

•

If
\mu\leq\alpha
, the tester rejects with probability at least
1-\beta
.

Hence the desired tester exists with sample complexity
O\left(\log(1/\beta)/\alpha\right)
.
∎

The above lemma provides a tester which we can use for PAC learning. Concretely, for any hypothesis
h\in\mathcal{H}
, we can use
O(\log(1/\beta)/\alpha)
samples to test whether it is
\alpha
-accurate.
Since a non-replicable PAC learning algorithm exists with sample complexity nearly linear in the VC dimension, we obtain the following result.

Lemma 21
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a binary concept class with VC dimension
d
.
Assume that for any
\rho,\alpha,\beta\in(0,1/2)
, there exists a
\rho
-replicable algorithm with sample complexity
n_{\textnormal{replic}}(\rho,\alpha,\beta)
.
For
\rho,\alpha,\beta\in(0,1/2)
, there exists a
\rho
-replicable algorithm for solving the problem with sample complexity

\displaystyle n_{\textnormal{replic}}(\rho/4,\alpha/2,\rho/4)+O\left(\frac{d\log(1/\alpha)+\log(1/\beta)}{\alpha}\right).

Proof.

The theorem follows directly from
Theorem

12
by using

Lemma

20
for
\mathcal{A}_{\textnormal{tester}}
and
using a standard non-replicable learner for

\mathcal{A}_{\textnormal{non-replic}}
.
Specifically, it is well-known that
we have

n_{\textnormal{non-replic}}(\alpha,\beta)\leq O\left(\frac{d\log(1/\alpha)+\log(1/\beta)}{\alpha}\right).

By
Lemma

20
, we have

n_{\textnormal{tester}}(\alpha,\beta)\leq O\left(\frac{\log(1/\beta)}{\alpha}\right).

Plugging these bounds in
Theorem

12
we obtain the desired bound.
∎

Corollary 5
.

Any class
\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle n(\rho,\alpha,\beta)\leq\widetilde{O}\left(\frac{d^{12}}{\alpha^{2}\rho^{2}}+\frac{\log(1/\beta)}{\alpha}\right).

Proof.

As shown by

\displaystyle n_{\textnormal{replic}}(\rho,\alpha,\beta)\leq\widetilde{O}(\frac{d^{12}\log^{3}(1/\beta)}{\alpha^{2}\rho^{2}}),

which in turn implies

n_{\textnormal{replic}}(\rho/4,\alpha/2,\rho/4)\leq\widetilde{O}(\frac{d^{12}}{\alpha^{2}\rho^{2}}).

It is well-known that the Littlestone dimension is larger or equal to the VC dimension.
Therefore, the VC dimension is at most
d
.
Applying
Lemma

21
finishes the proof.
∎

We additionally obtain the following result for learning finite classes.

Corollary 6
.

For any finite class
\mathcal{H}
, there exists
a
\rho
-replicable algorithm for outputting an
\alpha
-accurate learner with failure probability
\beta
that has
sample complexity

\displaystyle O\left(\frac{\log^{2}|\mathcal{H}|+\log(1/\rho)}{\alpha\rho^{2}}\log^{3}(1/\rho)+\frac{\log(1/\beta)}{\alpha}\right).

Proof.

As shown by
[
8
]
we have

\displaystyle n_{\textnormal{replic}}(\rho,\alpha,\rho)\leq O\left(\frac{\log^{2}|\mathcal{H}|+\log(1/\rho)}{\alpha\rho^{2}}\log^{3}(1/\rho).\right)

Additionally, the VC dimension of a finite class is at most
\log|\mathcal{H}|
.
Furthermore, for finite
H
, we can apply the sharper
bound

\displaystyle n_{\textnormal{non-replic}}(\rho,\alpha,\beta)\leq\frac{\log|\mathcal{H}|+\log(1/\beta)}{\alpha},

removing a
\log(1/\alpha)
factor.
Other than the
\log(1/\beta)/\alpha
, the rest of the bound is absorbed by the larger terms already present in
n_{\textnormal{replic}}
and the result follows.
∎

Corollary 7
.

For any
\rho,\alpha,\beta>0
, there exists a
\rho
-replicable algorithm for PAC learning threshold functions over the finite domain
{0,1,\dots,d}
that achieves error at most
\alpha
and failure probability at most
\beta
, with sample complexity

\displaystyle n(\rho,\alpha,\beta)\leq\widetilde{O}\left(\frac{(\log^{*}d)^{3}}{\alpha^{2}\rho^{2}}+\frac{\log(1/\beta)}{\alpha}\right).

Proof.

As shown by

,

Since the VC dimension of threshold functions is at most

7.3
Application: Heavy-hitters

We next discuss the approximate heavy-hitters problem. In this problem, we are given a distribution
\mathcal{D}
and two parameters
\nu,\epsilon
. The goal is to output a list
L
such that:

•

For any
x
such that
\textnormal{Pr}_{X\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[X=x\right]\geq\nu
we have
x\in L
, and

•

For any
x
such that
\textnormal{Pr}_{X\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[X=x\right]\leq\nu-\epsilon
we have
x\notin L
.

For this problem, we prove the following theorem.

Theorem 14
.

For any
\rho,\nu\in(0,1)
,
\beta<\rho
and
\epsilon\in(0,\nu)
, there exists a
\rho
-replicable algorithm for solving the heavy-hitters problem with failure probability
\beta
and sample complexity

\displaystyle\widetilde{O}(\frac{1}{\nu\epsilon^{2}\rho^{2}}+\frac{\log(1/\beta)}{\epsilon^{2}}).

We assume throughout that
\epsilon\leq\nu/2
. If not, we can solve the problem with
\epsilon=\nu/2
. While this is clearly a harder problem, and any solution is a valid solution for the original problem, the claimed sample complexity bound is the same.

Given parameters
\nu,\epsilon
, define

\nu^{\prime},\epsilon^{\prime}
as

\nu^{\prime}=\nu-\epsilon/4
and
\epsilon^{\prime}=\epsilon/2
which implies
\nu^{\prime}-\epsilon^{\prime}=\nu-\epsilon+\epsilon/4
.

We begin by implementing a tester for this problem.

Lemma 22
.

There exists an algorithm with sample complexity
at most

O\left(\epsilon^{-2}\log(\nu^{-1}\beta^{-1})\right)

that accepts any valid solutions to
\mathcal{T}_{\nu^{\prime},\epsilon^{\prime}}
and rejects any invalid solutions for
\mathcal{T}_{\nu,\epsilon}
with failure probability at least
1-\beta
.

Proof.

We will show a tester that accepts all valid solutions to
\mathcal{T}_{\nu^{\prime},\epsilon^{\prime}}
and rejects all invalid solutions for
\mathcal{T}_{\nu,\epsilon}
with high probability.

To implement this, we start by checking the size of
L
. If the size of
L

exceeds
4/\nu
, then we reject. Note that any valid solution to
\mathcal{T}_{\nu^{\prime},\epsilon^{\prime}}
cannot have any elements with probability less than
\nu/4
. As
such, its size cannot exceed
4/\nu
.

Next, for all the elements in
L
, we estimate their probability up to error
\epsilon/32
and failure probability
\frac{\beta}{8|L|}
.
This can be done with sample complexity

O(\log(4|L|/\beta)/\epsilon^{2})
.
Note that the same sample set can be used to estimate all probabilities.
By union bound,
it follows that with probability at least
1-\beta/4
, all of the probability estimates are correct up to the desired error.
Now, if any of the probability estimates are below
\nu^{\prime}-\epsilon^{\prime}-\epsilon/16
, we reject. We observe that a valid solution to
\mathcal{T}_{\nu^{\prime},\epsilon^{\prime}}
cannot be rejected at this stage because all probability estimates are going to be at least
\nu^{\prime}-\epsilon^{\prime}-\epsilon/32
.

We then take a new sample set
A
consisting of

n_{1}=\Theta(\nu^{-1}\log(\nu^{-1}\beta^{-1}))
samples.
With probability
1-\beta/16
, the set contains all
x
such that

\textnormal{Pr}_{D}\left[x\right]\geq\nu^{\prime}
.
We then estimate the probability of all elements in
A
up to additive error
\epsilon/32
and failure probability
\frac{\beta}{8|A|}
. This takes

O(\log(4n_{1}/\beta)/\epsilon^{2})
samples in total. By union bound, all estimates are correct with probability
1-\beta/8
. If there is some
x\in A
for which the estimate is at least
\nu-\epsilon/16
but
x\notin L
, then we reject.
We again note that a valid solution to
\mathcal{T}_{\nu^{\prime},\epsilon^{\prime}}
cannot be rejected; if it’s rejected because of some
x\in A\backslash L
, then that
x
must have probability at least
\nu-\epsilon/16-\epsilon/32>\nu^{\prime}
, which means
L
was not a valid solution after all.

We next claim that any invalid solution to
\mathcal{T}_{\nu,\epsilon}
will be
rejected assuming the failure events mentioned above do not occur. Consider
some list
L
. If
L
contains some
x
with probability less than
\nu-\epsilon
, then the estimate for the probability will be at most
\nu-\epsilon+\epsilon/32
which means it will get rejected. Let us assume
that there is some element
x
with probability more than
\nu
that does
not appear in
L
. The probability will appear in the set
A
if the
failure events do not occur. Its probability estimate will be at least
\nu-\epsilon/32
which is larger than
\nu-\epsilon/16
. Therefore, since

x\notin L
, the set
A
will get rejected.

Since we can assume without loss of generality that
|L|\leq\Theta(\nu^{-1})
, the total sample complexity of the algorithm is

\displaystyle O\left(\log(4|L|/\beta)/\epsilon^{2}+\nu^{-1}\log(\nu^{-1}\beta^{-1})+\log(\nu^{-1}\beta^{-1})/\epsilon^{2}\right)=O\left((\log(\nu^{-1})+\log(\beta^{-1}))(\epsilon^{-2}+\nu^{-1})\right).

This implies the bound in the lemma statement since
\epsilon\leq\nu
.
The failure probability is also at most
\beta
by union bound.
∎

We next consider a non-replicable algorithm for the problem. We start by
sampling a set
A
consisting of
n_{1}=\Theta(\nu^{-1}\log(\nu^{-1}\beta^{-1}))
elements. We claim that, with probability at least
1-\beta/8
, the
set contains all items with probability at least
\nu
. This is because for any
such
x
, the probability that it does not appear in
A
is at most

\displaystyle(1-\textnormal{Pr}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[x\right])^{n_{1}}\leq e^{-n_{1}\nu}\leq\nu\beta/16.

Since the number of such elements is a most
\nu^{-1}
, a union bound implies the claim.
Next, for all elements in
A
, we estimate the probability of each value with additive error
\epsilon/32
.
To achieve this, we take
n_{2}
samples where

n_{2}=O(\log(n_{1}/\beta)/\epsilon^{2})
. This implies that each estimate is correct with probability at least
1-\frac{\beta}{8n_{1}}
. By union bound, all estimates are correct with probability at least
1-\frac{\beta}{8}
. For any element, if the estimate is above
\nu-\epsilon/16
, we put in the output list
L
and if the estimate is below this threshold we do not.
The total sample complexity of the algorithm is at most

\displaystyle n_{1}+n_{2}\leq O\left(\frac{\log(\nu^{-1}\beta^{-1})}{\nu}+\frac{\log(\nu^{-1}\beta^{-1})}{\epsilon^{2}}\right)\leq O\left(\frac{\log(\nu^{-1}\beta^{-1})}{\epsilon^{2}}\right)

As shown by
[
21
]
however, we have

\displaystyle n_{\textnormal{replic}}(\rho,\nu,\epsilon,\beta)\leq\widetilde{O}(\frac{\log(1/\beta)}{\nu\epsilon^{2}\rho^{2}}).

Applying
Theorem

12
, the theorem follows.

7.4
Application: Best Arm Problem

We next consider the
best arm problem
.
Here, we are given a set of arms
A
. Each arm
a\in A
has a corresponding distribution over
[0,1]
with mean
\mu_{a}
. The goal is to find an arm
a
satisfying
\mu_{a}\geq\max_{a^{\prime}}\mu_{a^{\prime}}-\alpha
.
As shown by
[
26
]
, the problem can be solved with

n_{\textnormal{replic}}(\rho,\alpha,\beta)=O(\frac{1}{\rho^{2}\alpha^{2}}\log^{3}(|A|/\beta))

samples per arm (see Theorem 3.3 in their paper).

We next implement a tester for this problem.
To achieve this, we first estimate the mean of each arm with additive error
\alpha/16
and failure probability
\frac{\beta}{16|A|}
. This can be done with
O(\log(|A|/\beta)/\alpha^{2})
samples per arm by Hoeffding’s inequality.
Let
\hat{\mu}_{a}
denote the obtained estimates.
A union bound implies that, with probability at least
\beta/8
we have

\left|\mu_{a}-\hat{\mu}_{a}\right|\leq\alpha/16
for all
a
.
To check if an arm
a
is indeed a correct solution, we check if it satisfies

\hat{\mu}_{a}\geq\max_{a^{\prime}}\hat{\mu}_{a^{\prime}}-\frac{3}{2}\alpha
.
If the solution is indeed valid for
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
, then we have

\mu_{a}\geq\max_{a^{\prime}}\mu_{a^{\prime}}-\alpha
. Given the bound on
\left|\mu_{a}-\hat{\mu}_{a}\right|
, this implies that the tester will accept. Conversely, if a solution
a
is invalid for
\mathcal{T}_{2\alpha}
, then for some arm
a^{\prime}
we have

\mu_{a^{\prime}}\geq\mu_{a}+2\alpha
. Given the same bound on differences, we conclude that

\hat{\mu}_{a^{\prime}}\geq\hat{\mu}_{a}+\frac{3}{2}\alpha
and as such the tester will reject.

The sample complexity of the non-replicable version of the problem can be similarly bounded. We estimate each
\mu_{a}
with additive error
\alpha/16
and failure probability
\frac{\beta}{16|A|}
using

O(\log(|A|/\beta)/\alpha^{2})
samples per arm.
We then output the arm with maximum estimated mean.
A union bound implies that with probability at least
\beta/8
, all estimates are correct up to the desired error correct. Therefore, the chosen arm is a solution to
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
as required.

Combining the above arguments and invoking
Theorem

12
, we obtain the following result.

Theorem 15
.

Let
\beta\leq\rho\leq 1/2
. There exists a
\rho
-replicable algorithm for solving the best-arm problem over a set
A
with additive error
\alpha
and failure probability
\beta
, using

\displaystyle n_{\textnormal{new}}(\rho,\alpha,\beta)=O\left(\frac{1}{\rho^{2}\alpha^{2}}\log^{3}(\frac{|A|}{\rho})+\frac{\log(\frac{|A|}{\beta})}{\alpha^{2}}\right)

samples per arm.

8.1
Analysis of Naive Composition

In this section we demonstrate that the standard union bound analysis for naive composition is, up to constants, tight.
Consider an algorithm
\mathcal{A}
for some problem
\mathcal{T}
.
The choice of problem is arbitrary and all we require is that
\mathcal{A}
is not
\rho
-replicable with
\rho=0
; e.g., one can consider the mean estimation problem.
Let
\rho
be the
“optimal”
replicability parameter for
\mathcal{A}
; i.e.,

\displaystyle\rho=\inf\left\{\rho^{\prime}:\text{$\mathcal{A}$ is $\rho^{\prime}$-replicable}\right\}.

Assume that
\rho>0
.
By definition of the infimum, the algorithm
\mathcal{A}
is
(2\rho)
-replicable.
However, there exist some distribution
D
such that for two independent samples
S^{(1)},S^{(2)}
from
D
we have

\displaystyle{\textnormal{Pr}\left[\mathcal{A}(S^{(1)};r)\neq\mathcal{A}(S^{(2)};r)\right]}\geq\rho/2.

Let
\mathcal{D}^{*}
denote the product distribution of
k
independent copies from
D
.
Let
\mathcal{T}_{i}
denote the problem
\mathcal{T}
over the
i
-th coordinate of the input and define

\mathcal{T}^{*}=(\mathcal{T}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{T}_{k})
. Let
\mathcal{A}_{i}
denote the algorithm that runs
\mathcal{A}
on the
i
-th coordinate of the input samples. Define
\mathcal{A}^{*}=(\mathcal{A}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{A}_{k})
to be the composition of
\mathcal{A}_{i}
.
Given the guarantees on
\mathcal{A}
, it is clear that
\mathcal{A}_{i}
is
(2\rho)
-replicable.
We will show however that the replicability parameter of
\mathcal{A}^{*}
is at least
\Omega(\min\{\rho k,1\})
.

Define the event
\texttt{err}_{i}
as
\texttt{err}_{i}=\left\{\mathcal{A}_{i}(S^{(1)};r)\neq\mathcal{A}_{i}(S^{(2)};r)\right\}

Given the guarantees on
\mathcal{A}
, for any two samples
S^{(1)},S^{(2)}
from
\mathcal{D}^{*}
we have

\displaystyle{\textnormal{Pr}\left[\texttt{err}_{i}\right]}\geq\rho/2.

Observe that
\texttt{err}_{i}
depends only on the coordinate
i
in the samples and the random bits used by
\mathcal{A}_{i}
. Since
\mathcal{D}^{*}
was assumed to be a product distributions, this implies that
the events
\left\{\texttt{err}_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{k}
are independent.
It is easy to show however that given
k
independent events with probability at least
p
, the probability of their union is at least
\min\{kp,1\}/2
:

\displaystyle{\textnormal{Pr}\left[\text{Union event}\right]}\geq 1-(1-p)^{k}\geq 1-e^{-kp}\geq 1-\max\{1/2,1-kp/2\},

where the last inequality follows from

e^{-x}\leq\max\{1/2,1-x/2\}
which holds for all
x>0
.
It follows that

\displaystyle{\textnormal{Pr}\left[\mathcal{A}^{*}(S^{(1)};r)\neq\mathcal{A}^{*}(S^{(2)};r)\right]}

\displaystyle={\textnormal{Pr}\left[\cup_{i=1}^{k}\texttt{err}_{i}\right]}\geq\frac{1}{2}\min(1,k\rho/2).

8.2
Tightness of Composition Bounds

A natural question is whether the
\widetilde{O}(nk)
bound obtained above is tight or one can improve it in terms of the dependence on either
n
or
k
.
The following theorem shows that the result is indeed tight; in particular, the theorem implies that if we fix either of the parameters
n
or
k
, we cannot obtain a composition that is truly sublinear in the other parameter.

Theorem 16
.

Suppose there exists a constant
c>0

and a mechanism with the following property.
For any
\beta>0
and any collection of
k
algorithms

\mathcal{A}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{A}_{k}
that are each
c
-replicable with sample
complexity
n
and failure probability
\beta
, the mechanism produces a

0.0001
-replicable algorithm
\mathcal{A}^{*}
solving the composed problem with
failure probability as high as
O(\operatorname{poly}(k)\operatorname{poly}(\beta))
and sample complexity

\widetilde{O}(n^{\chi}k^{\psi}\operatorname{polylog}(1/\beta))
for some constants

\chi,\psi
. Then
\chi\geq 1
and
\psi\geq 1
.

In the above statement, since
\beta<1
, the term
\operatorname{poly}(\beta)
refers to any function of the form
\beta^{r}
for some
r>0
(e.g.,
\sqrt{\beta}
). The theorem shows that one cannot, for instance, obtain a better than linear dependence on
n
and
k
by allowing the failure probability of the composition to be
k^{10}\beta^{1/10}
.

The proof of the above theorem follows from the hardness of the
one-way
marginals problem, which is a special case of the multi-dimensional mean estimation problem considered in
Theorem

8
.

Definition 10

(One-way marginals)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}=\{0,1\}^{d}
and set
D

to be the product of Bernoulli distributions with parameters
p=(p_{1},\dots,p_{d})
on
\mathcal{X}
;
i.e.,

\textnormal{Pr}_{X\sim D}\left[X=x\right]=\prod_{i=1}^{d}p_{i}^{x_{i}}(1-p_{i})^{1-x_{i}}
.
We say
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is an
\alpha
-accurate solution for the one-way marginal problem if

\left\lVert p-v\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\alpha
.

The following lemma lower bounds the sample complexity of this problem.

Lemma 23

(Theorem 5.3 in
[
8
]
)
.

There exist constants
C>0
with the following property. For any
d>C
any
0.0001
-replicable algorithm that outputs
an
0.01
-accurate solution to the one-way marginals problem with failure
probability at most
0.001
must have sample complexity at least

\widetilde{\Omega}(d)
.

Combining the above lemma with the positive result from
Theorem

8
, we obtain the following lemma which we will then use to prove
Theorem

16
.

Lemma 24
.

For any
n,k>0
, there exists
statistical problems
\mathcal{T}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{T}_{k}
such that
the following holds.

•

For any
\rho,\beta>0
, each
\mathcal{T}_{i}
can be solved with a
\rho
-replicable algorithm with failure probability
\beta
and complexity
\widetilde{O}(n\rho^{-2}\operatorname{polylog}(1/\beta))
.

•

Solving the composed problem
\mathcal{T}^{*}=(\mathcal{T}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{T}_{k})

with a
0.0001
-replicable algorithm and failure probability
0.001

requires sample complexity at least
\widetilde{\Omega}(nk)
.

Proof.

Set
d=n
,
d^{\prime}=nk
, and
\alpha=0.01
.
Define
\mathcal{X}=\{0,1\}^{d^{\prime}}
and
let
D
be a product of Bernoulli distributions on
\mathcal{X}
with parameters
p=(p_{1},\dots,p_{d^{\prime}})
.
For any
i\in[k]
, define

p^{(i)}=(p_{(i-1)d+1},\dots,p_{id})
and let
\mathcal{T}_{i}
be the problem of finding a vector
v\in[0,1]^{d}
satisfying
\left\lVert v-p^{(i)}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\alpha
.
It is clear that each
\mathcal{T}_{i}
is the problem of obtaining an
\alpha
-accurate solution to the one-way marginal problem which has sample complexity
\widetilde{O}(d\rho^{-2}\log(\beta^{-1}))
by
Theorem

8
.

The composition
\mathcal{T}^{*}
is
the problem of finding a set of vectors
v^{(1)},\dots,v^{(i)}
such that
\left\lVert v^{(i)}-p^{(i)}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\alpha
for all
i
.
If we glue these vectors
v^{(i)}
into a single vector however, it is clear that this problem is equivalent to finding a vector
v
satisfying

\left\lVert v-p\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\alpha
. This is again an instance of the one-way marginal problem with
d^{\prime}
instead of
d
and, by
Lemma

23
, requires
sample complexity
\widetilde{\Omega}(d^{\prime})
.
The lemma now follows from the definition of
d
and
d^{\prime}
.
∎

We now use the above lemma to prove
Theorem

16
.

Proof of
Theorem

16
.

Assume that such a mechanism exists and that
if
\mathcal{A}_{i}
have failure probability
\beta
, then
the output algorithm has failure probability
\Theta(k^{r}\beta^{\ell})
for some constants
r,\ell>0
.
Let
n,k>0
be arbitrary values
and consider the instances
\mathcal{T}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{T}_{k}

from
Lemma

24
.

Let
\beta=\Theta(\frac{1}{k^{r/\ell}})
for small enough constant hidden
under
\Theta(.)
.
By assumption, the mechanism produces an algorithm for
\mathcal{T}_{i}
with failure probability

\Theta(k^{r}\beta^{\ell})=\Theta(1)
which, picking the constant for
\beta
small enough, is at most
0.001
.
Since
r,\ell
are constants, we have
\log(1/\beta)=O(\log k)
.
Therefore, the sample complexity of the composed problem is bounded by

\displaystyle\widetilde{O}(n^{\chi}k^{\psi}\operatorname{polylog}(1/\beta))=\widetilde{O}(n^{\chi}k^{\psi}\operatorname{polylog}(k)).

Since the algorithm
\mathcal{A}^{*}
is
0.0001
-replicable and has failure probability
0.01
however,
by the assumption in
Lemma

24
, its sample complexity is at least

\widetilde{\Omega}(nk)
.
Therefore,

\widetilde{O}(n^{\chi}k^{\psi}\operatorname{polylog}(k))\geq\widetilde{\Omega}(nk)
.
Since this holds for all

we must have
\chi\geq 1
and
\psi\geq 1
as claimed.
∎

Appendix A
Challenge of adaptivity in perfect generalization

A.1
Issues in the proof of adaptive composition for PG

The fact that one can obtain an adaptive composition theorem for perfect generalization
(Theorem 4.8 in
[
5
]
and our extension in
Lemma

2
to the heterogeneous case)
is at first surprising given that the related notion
of Max-information under product distributions does not compose as shown by
[
36
]
.

We briefly recall the relevant definitions.
Given jointly distributed random variables
X
and
Z
,
we write
X\otimes Z
for an independent copy, i.e., a pair drawn from the product of the marginals of
X
and
Z
.
The
\beta
-approximate max-information
between
X
and
Z
is defined as

I_{\infty}^{\beta}(X;Z)=\log\sup_{\mathcal{O}}\frac{{\textnormal{Pr}\left[(X,Z)\in\mathcal{O}\right]}-\beta}{{\textnormal{Pr}\left[X\otimes Z\in\mathcal{O}\right]}}

where the supremum is over all events
\mathcal{O}
with
{\textnormal{Pr}\left[(X,Z)\in\mathcal{O}\right]}>\beta
.
An algorithm
\mathcal{A}:\mathcal{X}^{n}\to\mathcal{Y}
has
\beta
-approximate max-information of
k
under product distributions

if
I_{\infty}^{\beta}(X;\mathcal{A}(X))\leq k
for every product distribution
\mathcal{P}
over
\mathcal{X}
when
X\sim\mathcal{P}^{n}
.

Why max-information fails to compose.

Theorem 4.1 in
[
36
]
shows that max-information under product distributions does not compose.
They construct algorithms

\mathcal{A}:\mathcal{X}^{n}\to\{0,1\}^{r}
with
r\in\Theta(\log(n))
and
\mathcal{B}:\mathcal{X}^{n}\times\{0,1\}^{r}\to(\mathcal{X}\cup\{\perp\})
,
where
\mathcal{X}=\{0,1\}
and
\perp
is a special error token, such that the following holds.
Let
X
be sampled uniformly from
\mathcal{X}^{n}
.
The algorithm
\mathcal{A}
has small max-information under product distributions and,
for any fixed
z\in\{0,1\}^{r}
,

\mathcal{B}(\cdot,z)
also has small max-information.
However,
\mathcal{B}(X,\mathcal{A}(X))
recovers
X
with high probability,
meaning the composition has very high max-information.
The key reason max-information under product distribution does not compose is that once we condition on the output of

\mathcal{A}
, the distribution of
X
is no longer a product distribution.
As such, even though
\mathcal{B}(\cdot,z)
has low max-information under product distributions for any fixed

z
,
this does not yield a bound for the max-information of
\mathcal{B}(X,\mathcal{A}(X))
for random
X
.

How perfect generalization sidesteps this issue.

Intuitively, the same issue should arise for perfect generalization:
conditioned on
\mathcal{A}(X)
, the distribution of
X
is no longer i.i.d.
The key idea that makes composition work for PG is to define the oracle transcript

\widetilde{Z}

independently
of
X
, using the oracle’s own prefix rather than the algorithm’s.
To see why this helps, consider
the above example of the two algorithms
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
.
Define
Z=\mathcal{A}(X)
and let
\widetilde{Z}
denote a sample from the oracle corresponding to

\mathcal{A}(\cdot)
.
The key problem is that we cannot immediately claim
\mathcal{B}(X,Z)\approx W(Z)
because,
conditioned on
Z
, the distribution of
X
is no longer i.i.d.
However we can still show
\mathcal{B}(X,Z)\approx W(Z)
using a more involved argument.
Concretely, observe that
X
and
\widetilde{Z}
are independent.
As such,
\mathcal{B}(X,\widetilde{Z})
has the desired perfect generalization guarantees:
with high probability over the randomness of
X
, the distribution of
\mathcal{B}(X,\widetilde{Z})
is close
to some fixed
W(\widetilde{Z})
.
Letting
\hat{C}
denote the set of all pairs
(x,z)
such that

\mathcal{B}(x,z)\approx W(z)
, this means that

(X,\widetilde{Z})\in\hat{C}
with high probability.
Since
\mathcal{A}(\cdot)
is PG, this implies that

(X,Z)\in\hat{C}
with high probability as well.
Therefore,

\mathcal{B}(X,Z)\approx W(Z)
with high probability.
The proof of
Lemma

2
follows this approach; we refer to the high-level sketch
after the statement of
Lemma

2
for a more detailed explanation.

An issue in the existing proof and its fix.

The proof of
[
5
]
as written has a small issue related to the above discussion: it implicitly assumes
that
\mathcal{A}(X)
and
X
are independent.
For simplicity, we discuss the proof of Theorem 4.2 in their paper, which handles
the composition of pure typically stable algorithms (i.e.,
\delta=0
); the same issue exists for the main result.
The key issue is in the definition of
W_{i}(P)
, which is defined
as the oracle corresponding to
\mathcal{A}_{i}(\cdot,Z^{i-1})
(see the discussion after Theorem 4.2 on page 10).
The random variables
\widetilde{Z}_{i}
are then sampled from the distribution
W_{i}(P)
.
Since
W_{i}(P)
is defined in terms of
Z^{i-1}
, which itself depends on the input
X
,
the variables
\widetilde{Z}_{i}
depend on
X
as well. This is problematic because at multiple points (e.g., right
after Equation 14, as well as right before the last equation on page 13) the paper assumes that
X
and
\widetilde{Z}^{i}
are independent.

The fix is simple: define the oracle transcript recursively using its own prefix rather than the algorithm’s.
Concretely, it suffices to define
W_{i}(P)
as the oracle corresponding to

\mathcal{A}_{i}(\cdot,\widetilde{Z}^{i-1})
where
\widetilde{Z}_{i}\sim W_{i}(P)
. In other words,
we first define
W_{1}(P)
as the oracle corresponding to
\mathcal{A}_{1}(\cdot)
. Note that,
by definition, we have

\mathcal{A}_{1}(X)\approx_{\varepsilon}W_{1}(P)
with probability at least
1-\nu
,
where we write
U\approx_{\varepsilon}V
to denote

\left|\ln\left(\frac{{\textnormal{Pr}\left[U=x\right]}}{{\textnormal{Pr}\left[V=x\right]}}\right)\right|\leq\varepsilon
for all
x
.
We then sample
\widetilde{Z}_{1}
from
W_{1}(P)
and define

W_{2}(P)
as
the oracle corresponding to
\mathcal{A}_{2}(\cdot,\widetilde{Z}^{1})
, and repeat.
Since each
\widetilde{Z}_{i}
is defined only in terms of
\widetilde{Z}^{i-1}
(and not
X
), the variables
\widetilde{Z}^{k}
are independent of
X
.
We now verify that the remaining steps of their proof go through unchanged.

Lemma 4.3 (in their paper) is unaffected since it does not involve
\widetilde{Z}
.
Equation 15 holds as well: while the equation follows from applying Lemma 4.3 to

W^{j}(P)
, the lemma does not at any point use the fact that
\mathcal{A}_{j}(\cdot,\widetilde{Z}^{j-1})\approx W_{j}(P)
.
Indeed, the way in which closeness of the distributions is leveraged is by
bounding
{\textnormal{Pr}\left[(X,\widetilde{Z}^{j})\notin\widetilde{G}_{j}\right]}
under the assumption that
(X,\widetilde{Z}^{j-1})\in\widehat{C}_{j}
;
the latter assumption is what ensures the closeness of distributions.
This assumption holds for
(X,\widetilde{Z}^{j-1})
because of the following.
For any fixed
z^{j-1}
, we have

\mathcal{A}_{j}(X,z^{j-1})\approx_{\varepsilon}W_{j}(P)
with probability at least

1-\nu
.
While
\widetilde{Z}^{j-1}
is random, since
X
is independent of
\widetilde{Z}^{j-1}
, we have

\mathcal{A}_{j}(X,\widetilde{Z}^{j-1})\approx_{\varepsilon}W_{j}(P)
with probability at least
1-\nu
as well.
It follows that, for any fixed
j
,
{\textnormal{Pr}\left[(X,\widetilde{Z}^{j-1})\notin C_{j}\right]}\leq\nu
, which by a union bound
implies
{\textnormal{Pr}\left[(X,\widetilde{Z}^{j-1})\notin\widehat{C}_{j}\right]}\leq j\nu
.
