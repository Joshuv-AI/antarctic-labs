ABSTRACT
 The ZX Initiative is a lean effort to upgrade the capabilities of Sandia’s Z Machine pulsed power
facility to ensure the nation’s continued technical superiority in the key strategic areas of Radiation
and High Energy Density Physics, Dynamic Material Properties, and Fusion. The initiative aims to
deliver a doubling of stored energy and delivered power to relevant loads. While the effort does not
supplant the need for a Next Generation High Energy Density Facility, it provides a technical bridge
which maintains the strategic hedge while aligning to the needs of the DoE Strategic Investment
Plan. The effort focuses on maximizing utilization of existing assets, such as the existing Z Facility
and infrastructure to realize a substantial improvement in capabilities with limited burden.

 This will be the third major in-place upgrade (PBFA II to Z, Z to ZR, ZR to ZX) which delivers
significantly increased energy within the same physical footprint of the machine. This report
documents a 70-capacitor Marx design necessary to deliver the prime pulse required to meet the
needs of the ZX initiative and provides the technical rationale for required Z-facility modifications.
It identifies the facility and hardware changes needed to support assembly and maintenance of the
Marx, describes the modifications required for Marx Test-Bed integration, and outlines
considerations for final installation and operation in the Z facility. Our approach evolves the existing
60-capacitor folded Marx generator fielded on Z. Alternative architectures such as Impedance-
Matched Marx Generators, and Linear Transformer Drivers would require significant, costly
modifications to the existing facilities and have yet to demonstrate sufficient technical readiness.

 Experiments of interest are optimized for existing Z pulse widths. This necessitates that a doubling
of delivered power is attained by a doubling of delivered energy. The 70-capacitor ZX Marx realizes
these design requirements by doubling the stored energy from the existing 20 MJ (ZR) to 40 MJ
(ZX). This increase will be achieved by three primary strategies: increasing the number of capacitors
(from 60 to 70), increasing capacitance of individual capacitors substantially (from 2.6 µF to greater
than 3.2 µF), and raising charge voltage by up to 18%.

  Integration plans have evolved in response to the growing commitment to delivering ZX
expediently. The current approach phases the work to manage risk. Phase I will integrate the
70-capacitor Marx into the existing Marx Test Bed facility using existing capacitors (2.6 µF). Phase II
will integrate the 70-capacitor Marx with higher energy-density capacitors into a new ZX-specific
test bed named, “Phoenix”. This facility has recently existed the design phase and entered into
procurement.

 The 70-capacitor design effort encompassed requirements development, verification and validation
planning, mechanical and electrical analyses, failure-modes and effects analysis (FMEA),
concept-of-operations development, and multiple iterative design reviews—each activity
documented to produce a mature design package ready for integration and facility planning. Key
outcomes include development of a new spreader for maintenance; mechanical and electrical
modifications to the baseline Marx hardware to ensure compatibility with the larger, higher-energy
design; upgrades to the Marx Test Bed to support the increased weight; mezzanine modifications in
Facility 983 to accommodate the increased height, and an overview of items to be addressed during
integration into the Z facility.

1. 70-CAPACITOR MARX DESIGN
The 70-capacitor Marx is a subsystem of the ZX Technology Maturation Project. The design
objective is to double stored energy—from 20 MJ on ZR to 40 MJ on ZX—by upgrading all 36
Marxes (38 including spares). Table 1 summarizes the key strategies and compares them to the ZR
baseline. The 70-capacitor Marx design overview is shown in Figure 1 Total stored energy closely
follows the capacitor energy relation: E = ½ C V2, small increases in voltage can yield significant
gains in energy, but often at the expense of even more substantial reductions in longevity. Optimal
component selection will follow the conclusion of ongoing reliability assessment efforts being
performed independently of this effort.

The extra row of capacitors in ZX reduces the distance between the bottom of the Marx and floor

2. COLUMN-TO-COLUMN
Column-to-column breakdown can initiate from capacitor edges, corners, and from capacitor
faceplates. The overall distance between columns will remain 8” for ZX, but the peak voltage
between columns will increase by 17%, from 2.4 MV to 2.8 MV on ZX due to the additional row of
capacitors.
Electrostatic analysis of two capacitor columns on Z shows the peak electric fields on a capacitor
edge are 425 kV/cm. Z does not use any additional field shaping except for the small radius of the
capacitor. Breakdowns on Z often occur in areas where the capacitors or capacitor faceplates are
misaligned.
Field shapers were designed for ZX to reduce electric fields at the capacitor edges and hide field
enhancements where small misalignments may occur. With the field shapers, the peak electric field
on the ZX capacitor field shapers is reduced to 375 kV/cm. Approximately 12% lower than Z
today despite 17% higher voltage.

3. MARX HANGING STRAP
The Marx hanging strap is required to remain 48” long while holding off a higher voltage (7.2 MV
on ZX vs 6.0 MV on Z).
To reduce the peak electric fields along the surface of the hanging strap modifications were made to
increase the radii on the top corona shield at the bottom of the hanging strap and to the tube field
shaper near the top of the hanging strap.
The modifications reduced the peak electric fields along the hanging strap from 170 kV/cm on Z to
161 kV/cm on ZX. The simulation of the ZX modifications is shown in Figure 6.

The 70-capacitor Marx model was developed with conservative weight margins. A worst case per
capacitor mass of 270 lbs was assumed based on design candidates from a credible vendor which are
oversized relative to the final design. When fully loaded with these capacitors, as well as the
supporting structure and ancillary hardware, the Marx mass is estimated at ≈28,000 lbs. For analysis
and design purposes, we applied additional margin and used a round design weight of 30,000 lbs.

4. 70-CAPACITOR MARX SPREADER
ZR has 36 Marxes installed on the machine and two spare Marxes (38 total). Because ZR could not
release a spare for technology maturation, fabricating an additional 70-capacitor Marx would raise
the inventory to 39 and require dedicated handling and storage. Although a storage rack could have
met immediate needs, converting the existing spreader and mover to handle the larger Marx would
have entailed substantial cost, schedule risk, and operational disruption. Given those tradeoffs and
the long-term benefits of a purpose-built fixture, we chose to build a new spreader in the 983 high
bay rather than simply add a storage rack; the new spreader enables handling of the larger Marx
without extensive changes to ZR support equipment or reliance on the Marx mover. Figures 7 and 8
show the ZR mover and spreader.

Figure 9 shows the new spreader design, which is more robust than the ZR version and incorporates
upgraded columns, larger bolts, and strengthened structural members. Figure 10 depicts the
spreader’s nominal location beside the MITL refurb tent.

Construction and assembly will be coordinated between Sandia Z Facility operations support and
contractor personnel. Contractors will install the columns — including concrete anchors, leveling
nuts, and grout pads — and complete electrical work to support the trolleys.

Table 6 lists supporting documents. Table 7 presents a compact comparison of spreader
performance across configurations and use cases; it reports only static results because the spreader
does not experience the peak accelerations seen in the MTB or Z.

5. MARX TESTBED
Integrating the 70-capacitor Marx into the Marx Testbed (MTB) is an early technology maturation
milestone (see Figure 11). Testing in the MTB provides early reassurance and an opportunity to
identify and implement design or procedural changes ahead of Phoenix commissioning. The initial
build will use ZR spare capacitors; final evaluation of the higher energy density capacitors will take
place in Phoenix (see Figure 12).

 Figure 11: Marx Test Bed

 Figure 12: Phoenix Tank (963)

Before installing a ZX Marx in the MTB, we evaluated the tank, saddles, support beams columns,
and the slab. The ZX Marx’s design weight is capped at 30,000 lbs.—about a 40% increase over the
ZR Marx—so verifying the testbed’s capacity to support the additional load was required.

 20

Figure 13 is the MTB in building 983. The MTB has two bays, A and B; only Bay A can be used for
firing a Marx (ZR or ZX) because Bay B is closer to the wall and is limited to storage. Table 8 and 9
summarize the analysis and key findings.

 At the time of the FDR, an accelerometer had not yet been installed on the MTB to measure
dynamic loads. Using ZR accelerometer data, we expected the MTB’s dynamic response to be lower
than the Z system’s because the MTB is mechanically decoupled from Z. After collecting data from
several shots, we measured the MTB’s response during Z shots to be approximately 2 g.

For clarity, we present two tables: one showing the static analysis and a second quasi-static analysis
that incorporates potential dynamic loading. Table 8 and 9 represent the MTB “baseline” — the
FOS that would apply if the 70-capacitor Marx were integrated with no structural modifications. The
saddles are already problematic under static loading. Table 9 shows FOS scenarios when a 2 g
dynamic load is applied. Many major MTB structural components do not meet the target FOS of 3,
which has driven the need for hardware modifications.

The SNL structural engineer’s slab analysis concludes that the ZX/ZR configuration is acceptable

Marxes, detailed design solutions were outside the scope of this report; nevertheless, it is a

mitigated. The slab was evaluated using a performance threshold rather than an FOS metric (see
Figure 14). The tank’s hydrostatic loading yields an FOS of approximately 2, which matches current

the ZX Marxes are transmitted through the cross beams into the internal columns and do not
appear to reduce the tank’s FOS.

6. ZR
At present there are no plans to integrate a 70-capacitor Marx into ZR for technology maturation
purposes. Initial analyses identified technical and integration challenges that must be resolved before
pursuing this option. Because MTB and ZR share similar Marx support architectures, any
modifications required for one system would likely apply to the other; Table 12 summarizes the
changes needed for the technology maturation scenario. If the facility is later upgraded to
accommodate a full complement of 70-capacitor Marx modules with higher acceleration loadings,
the affected hardware will require additional structural and system-level evaluation.

Recommendation: replace all saddles with an upgraded design sized and certified to carry the weight
of the 70-capacitor Marx and the higher peak accelerations expected during operation.
Simultaneously, re-evaluate the Marx support beams: although their cross section matches the
testbed beams, the thicker wall does not substitute for a full structural qualification under the
relevant bending, torsional, and dynamic load cases.

7. CONCLUSION
In conclusion, the modifications needed before technology maturation activities (i.e., testing a
70-capacitor Marx in the MTB) are modest and appear reasonable. Integrating a single 70-capacitor
Marx into ZR also looks feasible with the anticipated hardware changes. By contrast, a full ZX
upgrade—converting the machine to all 70-capacitor Marx modules—will require a thorough
reevaluation of support structures and energy scaling analyses to predict and accommodate
substantially higher peak accelerations. The final designs for the 70-capacitor Marx, the Spreader,
and the Marx Test Bed modifications, along with the final drawings to support each effort, have
been completed. Most hardware has been delivered, and we have begun assembly of the Spreader.
