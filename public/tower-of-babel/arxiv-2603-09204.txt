Semiclassical WKB Problem for the non-self-adjoint Dirac operator
Setsuro Fujiié ∗, Nicholas Hatzizisis †, Spyridon Kamvissis ‡

Abstract
. We review some recent rigorous results on the semiclassical behavior ( ϵ ↓ 0 \epsilon\downarrow 0 ) of the scattering data of a non-self-adjoint Dirac operator with potential A ​ exp ⁡ { i ​ S / ϵ } A\exp\{iS/\epsilon\} where both A A and S S are differentiable functions tending to constants as x → ± ∞ x\to\pm\infty . We have either employed the so-called exact WKB method, or the older WKB theory of Olver. Our analysis is motivated by the need to understand the semiclassical behaviour of the focusing cubic NLS equation with initial data A ​ exp ⁡ { i ​ S / ϵ } A\exp\{iS/\epsilon\} , in view of the well-known fact discovered by Zakharov and Shabat that the spectral analysis of the Dirac operator enables us to obtain the solution of the NLS equation via inverse scattering theory.

1.
Introduction

Consider the
semiclassical limit
(
\epsilon\downarrow 0
) of the solution to
the initial value problem of the one-dimensional
nonlinear Schrödinger equation

with cubic nonlinearity:

(1.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}i\epsilon\partial_{t}\psi+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2}\partial_{x}^{2}\psi+|\psi|^{2}\psi=0,\quad(x,t)\in{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}\\
\psi(x,0)=A(x)\exp\{iS(x)/\epsilon\},\quad x\in{\mathbb{R}}\end{array}\right.

where
A
and
S
are real valued functions defined on the real line. According to the
seminal discovery in
[
20
]
, this initial value problem can be studied via the
so-called
inverse scattering method
for any fixed
\epsilon>0
.
The limit
\epsilon\downarrow 0
requires a careful analysis at two levels.
A necessary first component of the semiclassical analysis is the
direct
spectral
and scattering analysis of the associated
Dirac

(or Zakharov-Shabat) operator
\mathfrak{D}_{\epsilon}
given by

\mathfrak{D}_{\epsilon}=\begin{bmatrix}-\frac{\epsilon}{i}\frac{d}{dx}&-iA(x)\exp\{iS(x)/\epsilon\}\\
-iA(x)\exp\{-iS(x)/\epsilon\}&\frac{\epsilon}{i}\frac{d}{dx}\end{bmatrix}.

The second component is of course the analysis of the inverse problem, which paradoxically
was the first to be studied rigorously. This was the object of a previous review dedicated
to Percy Deift 20 years ago (
[
10
]
,
[
12
]
,
[
13
]
,
[
11
]
). We have belatedly
returned to the direct problem!

Our main goal in this review is to present some recent rigorous results on the semiclassical
behavior of the
scattering data
(reflection coefficient, eigenvalues and their
associated norming constants) for the operator
\mathfrak{D}_{\epsilon}
.

2.
The case of zero initial phase

We begin with the case where the initial phase
S(x)=0
. Our first result assumes the
existence of an analytic extension of the initial data
A(x)
. We have studied the
semiclassical asymptotics of the reflection coefficient and the eigenvalue distribution
of the Dirac operator

L=\begin{bmatrix}-\frac{\epsilon}{i}\frac{d}{dx}&-iA(x)\\[8.0pt]
-iA(x)&\frac{\epsilon}{i}\frac{d}{dx}\end{bmatrix}.

Here,
\epsilon>0
is the semiclassical small parameter and
A(x)
is a function
satisfying the following.

(A1):

A(x)
is a real positive smooth function on
{\mathbb{R}}
, and extends analytically to the
complex domain

D_{0}=D(\rho_{0},\theta_{0}):=\{x\in{\mathbb{C}};|\Im\,x|<\max(\rho_{0},(\tan\theta_{0})|\Re\,x|)\}

for some positive
\rho_{0}
and
\theta_{0}
.
Moreover, there exists a positive
\tau
such that, as
x\to\infty
in
D_{0}
,

A(x)={\mathcal{O}}(|x|^{-1-\tau}).

Under this assumption, it is known that the spectrum of the non-self-adjoint operator

L
consists of the continuous spectrum
{\mathbb{R}}
and a finite number of eigenvalues coming
in complex conjugate pairs close to
i[-A_{0},A_{0}]
, where
A_{0}:=\max_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}}A(x)>0

when
\epsilon
is small.

Before describing our results let’s remind the reader of the definition of the
spectral/scattering data. For
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}\setminus\{0\}
, there exist a pair
of solutions
{\bf f}^{r}_{+}(x,\epsilon)
,
{\bf f}^{r}_{-}(x,\epsilon)
to the equation

L{\bf f}=\lambda{\bf f}
which behave, as the real part of

x,~\Re\,x\to\infty
in
D_{0}
, like

{\bf f}^{r}_{+}\sim\begin{bmatrix}0\\
e^{i\lambda x/\epsilon}\end{bmatrix},\quad{\bf f}^{r}_{-}\sim\begin{bmatrix}e^{-i\lambda x/\epsilon}\\
0\end{bmatrix},

as well as a pair of solutions
{\bf f}^{l}_{+}(x,\epsilon)
,
{\bf f}^{l}_{-}(x,\epsilon)

which behave, as
\Re x\to-\infty
in
D_{0}
, like

{\bf f}^{l}_{+}\sim\begin{bmatrix}0\\
e^{i\lambda x/\epsilon}\end{bmatrix},\quad{\bf f}^{l}_{-}\sim\begin{bmatrix}e^{-i\lambda x/\epsilon}\\
0\end{bmatrix}.

These solutions are called
Jost solutions
.

Each of these pairs is uniquely determined and forms a basis of solutions.
Let
T(\lambda,\epsilon)
be the 2
\times 2
constant matrix depending on
\lambda

and
\epsilon
expressing the change of basis of these two pairs:

(2.1)

({\bf f}^{l}_{+},{\bf f}^{l}_{-})=({\bf f}^{r}_{+},{\bf f}^{r}_{-})T.

Then
T
is of the form

(2.2)

T(\lambda,\epsilon)=\begin{bmatrix}a(\lambda,\epsilon)&b^{*}(\lambda,\epsilon)\\
b(\lambda,\epsilon)&a^{*}(\lambda,\epsilon)\end{bmatrix}

where
a^{*}
,
b^{*}
denote the complex conjugates of
a,b
. The reflection coefficient

R(\lambda,\epsilon)
is by definition

(2.3)

R(\lambda,\epsilon)=\frac{b(\lambda,\epsilon)}{a(\lambda,\epsilon)}.

It is easy to see that it can be expressed by wronskians of Jost solutions:

(2.4)

R(\lambda,\epsilon)=\frac{\mathcal{W}({\bf f}^{r}_{+},{\bf f}^{l}_{+})}{\mathcal{W}({\bf f}^{l}_{+},{\bf f}^{r}_{-})}.

We have the following results
[
6
]
. For the reflection coefficient,
we first have the following result for
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}
satisfying
|\lambda|\geq\delta

with
\epsilon
-independent positive

.

Theorem 2.1
.

Assume (A1). Then, for any
\delta>0
, there exists
\sigma>0
independent of
\epsilon

such that

|R(\lambda,\epsilon)|={\mathcal{O}}(e^{-\sigma/\epsilon}),

as
\epsilon\to 0
uniformly for

For an estimate of the eigenvalues, we assume moreover (for simplicity) that
A(x)
is a

A(x)=A(-x)

Let

Then the so-called Bohr-Sommerfeld quantization rule is written as

The meaning of this rule is that as

To make this more precise we will show that

Theorem 2.2
.

Assume (A1), (A2) and (A3).

if and only if

Next we consider the case where

The exact WKB method goes back to the works of Jean Ecalle
[

and André

3.
The case of non-analytic data

The previous results employ the exact WKB theory and thus make heavy use of
analyticity. The following results use the alternative method of Olver

[
17
]
which only assumes some smoothness. Rather than resumming,
one proves rigorously that the approximation of the semiclassical Dirac problem
by a parabolic cylinder function is valid in a very precise sense.

We have the following results
[
8
]
. We assume that the function
A

satisfies the following

4.
The case of non-trivial initial phase

We finally consider the case where
S
is not identically zero. We definitely
need some analyticity or at least meromorphicity properties, since both existing
WKB methods (exact WKB and Olver’s method) rely on some kind of analyticity for
the phase. In the examples we have considered
A,S
admiting a meromorphic
extension in the complex plane.

In
[
7
]
and
[
5
]
we have studied the special case

A(x)=S(x)=\sech(2x)
using a combination of the two WKB methods; our work
was inspired by an older paper by Peter Miller (
[
16
]
) and also some
older numerical observations by Jared Bronski
[
1
]
. Understanding fully
this particular example gives an insight to the most general generic
meromorphic case. Semiclassically the spectrum accumulates along a countable
union of analytic arcs.

4.1.
The general method

This section presents a modification of the procedure proposed in
[
16
]

for finding the semiclassical eigenvalues of the Dirac (or Zakharov-Shabat)
operator

(4.1)

-\omega^{*}(x,\epsilon)&\frac{\epsilon}{i}\frac{d}{dx}\end{bmatrix}

where
\omega
is the complex potential function

\omega(x,\epsilon)=-iA(x)\exp\Big\{i\frac{S(x)}{\epsilon}\Big\}

for some real-valued, analytic functions
A(x)
and
S(x)

defined on the real line
[of course
\omega^{*}(x,\epsilon)
represents the complex conjugate of

\omega(x,\epsilon)
] such that

are integrable.
We are interested in the spectral problem

(4.2)

\mathfrak{D}_{\epsilon}\textbf{u}=\lambda\textbf{u}

where
\textbf{u}=[u_{1}\hskip 3.0ptu_{2}]^{T}
is a function from

\frac{\epsilon}{i}\frac{d}{dx}\textbf{v}(x,\lambda,\epsilon)=M(x,\lambda)\textbf{v}(x,\lambda,\epsilon)

The key function to study is

\displaystyle=g_{-}(x,\lambda)g_{+}(x,\lambda)

The zeros of this function play an important role.

Definition 4.1

The potential function

. With the above intuition

Definition 4.2

Consider a pair

4.2.
The exact WKB method

In this and next subsection

Let us fix

\displaystyle z(\cdot,\lambda,\alpha):\Omega

(4.13)

\displaystyle z(x,\lambda,\alpha)=i\int_{\gamma(\alpha\rightsquigarrow x)}

\displaystyle\sqrt{-V_{0}(t,\lambda)}dt

Observe that

We look for solutions of (

Substituting this in (

\frac{\epsilon}{i}\frac{d}{dz}\tilde{\textbf{w}}^{\pm}(z)=\begin{bmatrix}\pm i&H(z)^{-2}\\
-H(z)^{2}&\pm i\end{bmatrix}\tilde{\textbf{w}}^{\pm}(z)

Finally, we set

iH(z)&-iH(z)\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix}^{\frac{1\pm 1}{2}}

and apply the transformation

Then the system in (

\frac{\epsilon}{i}\frac{d}{dz}\textbf{w}^{\pm}(z)=\begin{bmatrix}0&\mathcal{H}(z)\\

\mathcal{H}(z)=\frac{\dot{H}(z)}{H(z)}

and where the dot denotes differentiation with respect to

In the usual WKB theory, the vector valued
symbols

\textbf{w}^{\pm}

are constructed as a power series in the parameter
\epsilon
, which is in general
divergent. The exact WKB method consists in the resummation of this divergent series
in the following way. By taking a point

, setting

z_{0}\equiv z_{0}(\lambda)=z(x_{0},\lambda,\alpha)

and postulating the

\textbf{w}^{\pm}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\textbf{w}^{\pm}_{n}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\begin{bmatrix}w^{\pm}_{2n}\\
w^{\pm}_{2n-1}\end{bmatrix}\equiv\begin{bmatrix}w^{\pm}_{\rm even}\\
w^{\pm}_{\rm odd}\end{bmatrix}

can be defined inductively by

w_{-1}^{\pm}\equiv 0,\quad w_{0}^{\pm}\equiv 1

and for

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\frac{d}{dz}w_{2n}^{\pm}(z)&=\mathcal{H}(z)w_{2n-1}^{\pm}(z)\\
(\frac{d}{dz}\pm\frac{2}{\epsilon})w_{2n-1}^{\pm}(z)&=\mathcal{H}(z)w_{2n-2}^{\pm}(z)\\
w_{n}^{\pm}(z_{0})&=0.\end{array}\right.

These recurrence equations uniquely determine (at least in a neighborhood
of
x_{0}
) the sequence of scalar functions
\{w_{n}^{\pm}(z,\epsilon,z_{0})\}_{n=-1}^{\infty}

and hence the sequence of vector-valued functions

Then (

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}w_{2n}^{\pm}&=\mathcal{J}[w_{2n-1}^{\pm}]\\
w_{2n-1}^{\pm}&=\mathcal{I}_{\pm}[w_{2n-2}^{\pm}].\end{array}\right.

Going back to the initial system (

),
we have constructed (formal) WKB solutions of the form

\textbf{u}^{\pm}(x,\lambda,\epsilon,\alpha,x_{0})=\\

1&0\end{bmatrix}^{\frac{1\pm 1}{2}}\textbf{w}^{\pm}[z(x,\lambda,\alpha),\epsilon,z_{0}]

-1&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}H(z)^{-1}&H(z)^{-1}\\
iH(z)&-iH(z)\end{bmatrix}.

Here, we added the superscript
\pm

By the definition of a progressive path,

Thus, with this point
