A Century of Overabundant Ungulates in Yellowstone’s Lamar Valley: First Elk and Now Bison
Authors: Robert L. Beschta, William J. Ripple, J. Boone Kauffman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
After decades of intensive herbivory by Rocky Mountain elk (Cervus canadensis), many deciduous plant communities across Yellowstone National Park’s Northern Range began to recover following the mid-1990s reintroduction of gray wolves (Canis lupus), a trophic cascade. However, since the early 2000s, foraging pressure by the Park’s North American bison (Bison bison) population has greatly increased. During this period, we evaluated the effects of an increased bison population on riparian plant communities in the Lamar Valley, an area of high bison use in the eastern portion of the Park’s Northern Range. Using measurements of seedlings, saplings and small trees, and overstory trees, as well as chronosequence photographs, we studied the valley’s cottonwoods (Populus spp.) and quaking aspen (P. tremuloides), both long-lived keystone species. Results indicated that cottonwood seedling heights were being suppressed by bison herbivory, preventing the establishment and growth of new cottonwood forests. The horning and rubbing effects of bison-accessible saplings and small trees of cottonwood and aspen were found to cause high levels of bark damage and mortality. The proportion of mature Lamar Valley cottonwood trees with bark damage from bison horning and rubbing increased from 5-6% to nearly 50% between 2001 and 2023, a period during which nearly one-third of these overstory trees had died. Our findings highlight how a century of unnaturally high levels of ungulate use, initially elk and now bison, has dramatically transformed the valley’s riverine ecosystem. A recent management decision by the Park Service indicates the agency will maintain large numbers of bison into the future, thus continuing the disruption of riparian plant communities and ecosystem functions within the Lamar Valley, as well as other locations of high bison use in the Northern Range.

Over the last century, the two most prevalent large ungulates in Yellowstone National Park’s northern ungulate winter range, or “Northern Range,” have been Rocky Mountain elk (Cervus canadensis) (Houston 1982) and North American bison (Bison bison) (White et al. 2015; Yellowstone National Park 2024b). While the historical effects of the Park’s elk population on riparian and upland plant communities have been well documented (e.g., Skinner 1928; Jonas 1955; Kay 1990; Ripple and Larsen 2000; National Research Council 2002; Barmore 2003; Beschta 2005), the effects of a recently increased bison population on the composition, structure, and function of these communities have only begun to be acknowledged (Painter and Ripple 2012; Geremia and Hamilton 2019; Beschta et al. 2020; Kauffman et al. 2023; Painter et al. 2023).

Riparian areas typically occupy ≤2% of western montane landscapes, yet they normally provide food resources and physical habitats for more plant, amphibian, mammal, and avian species than surrounding uplands. For example, as much as 53% of wildlife species, including up to 80% of the local avifauna, breed in riparian habitats (Baril et al. 2011; Walker et al. 2023; Kauffman et al. 2001). Furthermore, functional riparian plant communities can significantly influence the physical and biological attributes of riverine systems by moderating microclimates and water temperatures, stabilizing riverbanks, cycling nutrients, and producing organic carbon, an essential component of food webs for both terrestrial and aquatic organisms (Gregory et al. 1991; Allan et al. 2021). Elevated levels of ungulate use in such areas can thus have major effects on the habitats of numerous terrestrial and aquatic wildlife species (Kauffman et al. 2001; Naiman et al. 2005).

We utilized several lines of inquiry to quantify ecosystem effects of a historically high population of bison in the Park’s Lamar Valley (Yellowstone National Park 2024b). Our primary objectives were to evaluate changes in riparian plant communities during the last two decades, including:height growth of narrowleaf cottonwood (Populus angustifolia) and black cottonwood (Populus trichocarpa) seedlings,ungulate bark damage to saplings and small trees of cottonwood and quaking aspen (Populus tremuloides), andungulate bark damage to overstory cottonwood trees and rates of tree mortality.

Management History

Yellowstone National Park was established in 1872 as the nation’s first National Park. However, market hunting of elk and other ungulates persisted after Park establishment, a situation that ultimately prompted the United States Cavalry to assume wildlife protection responsibilities in 1886. Ungulates and North American beavers (Castor canadensis) were generally protected, however, predators such as gray wolves (Canis lupus), cougar (Puma concolor), bears (Ursus spp.), and coyotes (Canis latrans) were persecuted—a practice that generally continued after the Park Service assumed administrative control in 1926 (Yellowstone National Park 1997; Stahler et al. 2020).

By the mid-1920s, wolves had been extirpated and cougar populations significantly diminished (Yellowstone National Park 1997; Ripple et al. 2022), marking a potential ecological tipping point. Shortly thereafter, Park biologists and administrators observed increased elk browsing in the Park’s Northern Range (Skinner 1928; Rush 1932). The Park Service initiated a program to reduce elk numbers in the late 1920s to mitigate the ecological impact of these large herbivores, but the program ended in 1968 due to public opposition (Allin 2000). Thereafter, the Park adopted a policy of “natural regulation” which, with low cougar numbers and an absence of wolves, allowed elk numbers to rapidly grow, attaining a population of nearly 20,000 elk in the late 1980s (Fig. 1). Throughout most of the seven decades when wolves were absent, high levels of elk browsing limited the growth of young cottonwoods, willows (Salix spp.), aspen, and other deciduous woody species across the Northern Range (Kay 1990; Barmore 2003; Peterson et al. 2020) (Fig. 2), effects that ultimately contributed to the loss of beavers (Jonas 1955).Fig. 1Annual elk and bison counts, Northern Range of Yellowstone National Park (Data source: Yellowstone National Park)Fig. 2Heights (cm) of browsing-suppressed young aspen and willow from several areas of the Northern Range between 1935 and 1991, a period when wolves were absent; dashed horizontal lines represent average heights of 32 cm for aspen (n = 54) and 40 cm for willows (n = 25). Cougar populations began to rebound in the 1980s (Ripple et al. 2022) and 31 wolves were reintroduced into Yellowstone National Park in 1995-96. This reintroduction was followed by a pronounced reduction in elk numbers due to various factors (e.g., severe winters and a degraded winter range, hunting of elk that wintered outside of the Park, increased large carnivore predation) (Vucetich et al. 2005). In 2003, when wolf numbers in the Northern Range peaked at nearly 100 animals, the elk population was approximately half that present in the late 1980s and early 1990s. Since 2010, annual counts have indicated 34–79 wolves and 3900–7600 elk utilizing the Northern Range, with an annual predator-prey ratio averaging nearly 9 wolves/1000 elk (CV = 24%).

Gray wolves, cougars, grizzly bears (U. arctos), and black bears (U. americanus) currently constitute the Northern Range’s large carnivore guild (Smith et al. 2003). A recovered wolf population, along with cougars and grizzly bears, currently provides significant predation pressure on elk wintering inside the Park (Barber-Meyer et al. 2008; Metz et al. 2020). As a result, reduced elk herbivory over the last couple of decades, particularly outside of areas of high bison use, has initiated recovery of riparian vegetation and aspen (i.e., a trophic cascade) in various portions of the Northern Range (Beschta and Ripple 2016; Painter et al. 2018; Ripple et al. 2025).

The capability of the Park’s large predators to exert predation pressure on elk and bison varies. For example, elk are the primary prey for wolves year-round in the Park and these apex predators are particularly effective at making elk kills in winter (Smith et al. 2003). However, due to their larger size and other factors, it is more difficult for wolves and Yellowstone’s other large predators to kill bison (MacNulty et al. 2020; Stahler et al. 2020). In the absence of significant predation pressure on bison, herd reductions (culling) by the Park Service and other agencies are necessary to control population size. Yet, even with a program of nearly annual management removal and harvest of bison that migrate outside the park (Yellowstone National Park 2024b), their numbers have greatly increased over the last two decades (Fig. 1).

Study Area

The Lamar Valley in Yellowstone National Park is located in the eastern portion of the 1500 km2 Northern Range (Fig. 3). Covering approximately 20 km2 of valley-bottom terrain upstream of the Lamar River Canyon, our study area contains extensive floodplain and alluvial surfaces bordering the main river. Historically, structurally diverse gallery forests of cottonwoods, tall willows, quaking aspen, thinleaf alder (Alnus incana subsp. tenuifolia), and other deciduous species would have occurred along the river, along with scattered stands of lodgepole pine (Pinus contorta) and Engelmann spruce (Picea engelmannii) (Houston 1982; Despain 1990; Kay 1990; Yellowstone National Park 1997; Beschta et al. 2020). Big sagebrush (Artemisia tridentata)-Idaho fescue (Festuca idahoensis) represents the dominant plant community on hillslopes adjacent to the valley bottom, transitioning to coniferous forests at higher elevations. Other native bunchgrasses are also present, along with an increasing component of exotic grasses and forbs in some areas (Kauffman et al. 2023). Scattered stands of aspen commonly occur on hillslopes adjoining the valley bottom.Fig. 3Lamar Valley study area in northern Yellowstone National Park; shaded area encompasses the Northern Range

The Northern Range provides important wintering habitat for elk and a small moose (Alces alces) population. Bison utilize the area year-round, along with small populations of mule deer (Odocoileus hemionus) and pronghorn (Antilocapra americana) (Yellowstone National Park 1997; National Research Council 2002). While vast herds of bison historically utilized lower elevation valleys and plains outside the Park (Bailey 2013), various lines of evidence suggest that significant numbers of bison did not occupy the Northern Range prior to the Park’s establishment (Haines 1955; Mosley and Mundinger 2018; Beschta and Ripple 2019b; Keigley 2019).

Methods

Bison can have multiple effects on ecosystems and plant communities (Knapp et al. 1999; White et al. 2015) and our field measurements evaluated some of these effects, particularly regarding the growth and mortality of cottonwood, a relatively long-lived deciduous species. Vegetation measurements occurred primarily between the Lamar Canyon (downstream end) and the river’s confluence with Soda Butte Creek (upstream end), an approximately 8-km long portion of the Lamar Valley study area, identified herein as the “Lower Lamar.” A subset of measurements also occurred along a portion of the Lamar River that extends nearly 2 km upstream from the Soda Butte Creek confluence, identified as the “Upper Lamar.”

Ungulate Use

We recorded the number of fecal piles by ungulate species in two parallel belt transects, 2 m wide x 50 m long (100 m2) and 10 m apart, to index general levels of ungulate use. In August 2012, 35 pairs of belt transects (each pair separated by 250 m) were sampled along the Lower Lamar and eight pairs (200 m apart) along the Upper Lamar (Beschta and Ripple 2015). In September 2018, we sampled 10 pairs of belt transects (400 m apart), all along the Lower Lamar.

Seedlings

Cottonwood seedling heights along the Lower Lamar had been previously measured in September 2002 (Beschta 2005) and August 2012 (Beschta and Ripple 2015); we again measured them in September 2023. The sampling approach for these surveys was to establish a long transect generally parallel to the river’s main channel, a transect that ran from the Lamar River Canyon to the Soda Butte Creek confluence. Within each 50-m segment of this transect, we searched between the river and its floodplain for the three tallest seedlings ≥0.2 m in height and recorded their heights (m). We considered a height of ≥0.2 m to indicate they had successfully survived two or more years of growth since germination, a period when mortality is often high. By focusing on the tallest young cottonwoods within each segment, we aimed at identifying the leading edge (i.e., earliest occurrence) of any height increases that might lead to recruitment success (i.e., growth of plants above the general upper level of ungulate herbivory).

Saplings and Small Trees

Within approximately a decade after wolf reintroduction, young cottonwoods and aspen, as well as other deciduous species, began to grow taller in various portions of the Northern Range, thus providing a new cohort of saplings [≥2 m tall and <5 cm in diameter at breast height (dbh)] and small trees (5-10 cm dbh) (Beschta and Ripple 2007, 2015; Ripple and Beschta 2007; Painter et al. 2015). To investigate the process by which contemporary bison horning/rubbing might affect the growth and survival of these size classes, in September 2023, we measured the proportion (%) of stems with bark damage in two bison-accessible stands of saplings and small trees, one containing cottonwoods and the other aspen. The aspen stand had been previously evaluated in September 2018 (Beschta et al. 2020). We defined “bark damage” as the removal of sufficient bark due to the horning/rubbing actions of bison such that a plant’s underlying sapwood becomes exposed. Both stands had grown well above the general upper browse level of bison, a height of ~1.5 m.

Mature Trees

A September 2001 inventory of all cottonwoods ≥5 cm dbh on the floodplains and riverbanks in the study area found scattered groves of overstory cottonwood trees, ranging in size from 26 to 135 trees per grove (Beschta 2003). These trees were re-inventoried in August 2012 (Beschta and Ripple 2015) and again in September 2023 to determine rates of mortality (%) over the intervening periods. During these inventories, we also inspected the lower 2 m of each tree’s bole to determine the presence of bark damage from bison horning and rubbing (Beschta et al. 2020) and for each sampling date calculated the proportion (%) of trees with bark damage.

Chronosequence Photographs

We searched for historical photographs showing deciduous plant communities at various sites along the valley bottom and adjacent hillslopes. We rephotographed these sites to illustrate general patterns of vegetation change over time, such as changes to the status of riparian cottonwood and upland aspen communities. Our earliest photographs occurred between 1929 and 1991, all before the reintroduction of wolves. Repeat photographs occurred between 2016 and 2023.

Results

Ungulate Use

Belt transects indicated an average of 15.8 and 16.3 bison fecal piles/100 m2 in 2012 within the Lower and Upper Lamar study reaches, respectively, and an average of 14.2 bison fecal piles/100 m2 in 2018 within the Lower Lamar study reach (Supp. Info. A). Approximately 98% of all fecal piles were from bison with the remainer from elk, deer, or pronghorn.

Seedlings

The number of 50-m segments sampled along the Lower Lamar was 198 in 2002, 193 in 2012, and 180 in 2023 (Supp. Info. B); these numbers varied due to changes in the location and sinuosity of the main river channel over the period of this study. Nevertheless, 81% (n = 160) of the segments had at least some seedlings ≥0.2 m in height in 2002, declining to only 8% (n = 15) of the transects by 2023 (Fig. 5). Saplings and Small Trees

The potential effects of bison on aspen saplings and small trees were illustrated in a stand (n = 126 stems) that had become established within the last two decades (Fig. 6). In 2018, 69% of the aspen had bark damage from bison and nearly all were “high-lined” (i.e., branches and leaves in the lower 1.5 m had been removed by bison). When this stand was revisited five years later in 2023, only 17% of the plants were alive and all exhibited bark damage (Supp. Info. C). Similarly, our assessment of a small stand of cottonwood saplings/small trees (n = 29 stems) found 11 of them were dead. Mature Trees

In 2001, nearly 700 overstory cottonwood trees existed within the study area (Supp. Info. D). All trees were ≥20 cm dbh (Beschta 2003), confirming an absence of intermediate tree diameters (i.e., 5–20 cm dbh) and clearly identifying that a major recruitment gap had occurred over a period of decades (Beschta 2003, 2005). Subsequent inventories in 2012 and 2023 chronicled a continuing decline in the number of trees (Fig. 6). Over a 22-yr period, 2001–2023, some 24% and 35% of cottonwood trees in the Lower Lamar and Upper Lamar study reaches, respectively, have been lost.

In 2001, bark damage from bison was observed on 6% and 5% of cottonwood trees in the Lower and Upper Lamar portions of the study area, respectively (Fig. 7). Discussion

Recent increases in the Park’s Northern Range bison population were found to have several major effects on riparian cottonwoods in the Lamar Valley. At current densities, these large herbivores have effectively prevented any cottonwood recruitment within the Lower Lamar study reach during the last two decades. While extensive areas of alluvium normally provide sites for cottonwood seedlings as well those of willow, over the last two decades such seedlings in the Lower Lamar were unable to grow taller due to high levels of bison herbivory. Existing overstory cottonwood trees across the study area also experienced increased levels of bark damage from bison horning and rubbing in recent years, potentially contributing to the high rates of ongoing mortality. Chronosequence photographs indicated a long-term loss of functional riparian plant communities along the Lamar River, a change that has likely contributed to accelerated bank erosion. Overall, the composition, structure, and functions of the valley’s deciduous plant communities have been greatly simplified, effects initiated by elk in the mid- to late-1900s and now continued by an enlarged bison population. Such communities would normally be highly productive, contain a diversity of native plant species, and capable of stabilizing riverbanks (Rosgen 1993; Kauffman et al. 2025).

Ungulate Use

Our fecal counts indicated bison are currently by far the most abundant and impactful large ungulate in the Lamar Valley. Other studies in and around the Lamar Valley have obtained similar results, with bison representing 94–98% of all fecal counts (Painter and Ripple 2012; Ripple et al. 2013; Kauffman et al. 2023). In a study of aspen stands distributed across the Northern Range, Painter et al. (2015) found that bison and elk accounted for 42% and 57% of all fecal piles, respectively, with relatively high fecal counts of bison in the eastern and central portions of the Northern Range. Since about 2007, the overall foraging pressure of Northern Range bison has increasingly exceeded that of elk (Beschta et al. 2020), representing a profound shift in ungulate use within a relatively short period.

Riparian Vegetation

As pioneer species, cottonwoods are prolific producers of seed (Stettler et al. 1996). There are also large areas of bare substrate within the Lamar Valley representing suitable sites for the germination and establishment of cottonwood seedlings. Although many hundreds of thousands of seedlings in the Lamar Valley have previously been documented (Beschta 2005; Rose 2012), our 2002–2023 surveys in the Lower Lamar study reach indicate that established cottonwood seedlings are unable to survive and grow into taller individuals. High rates of herbivory by an abundant bison population are currently preventing recruitment of this key riverine tree species, an effect that also extends to other deciduous species. For example, there is a lack of recent aspen and willow recruitment outside of long-term ungulate exclosures, such as the Lamar West (Fig. 11) and Lamar East exclosures (Kauffman et al. 2025). Similar effects are found outside of the Junction Butte ungulate exclosure, located several kilometers down-valley from our study area and also an area of high bison use (Kauffman et al. 2025).

Even though we found a continuous decline in the presence of cottonwood seedlings ≥0.2 m in height between 2002 and 2023 along the Lower Lamar, our measurements in 2012 indicated a temporary increase in the occurrence of cottonwood saplings, plants >2 m in height (Fig. 4). This increase likely resulted from reduced rates of ungulate herbivory in the early 2000s, when elk numbers in the eastern sector had become low and before bison numbers had dramatically increased (Beschta et al. 2020). During this period, small groups of young aspen and willows were able to grow above the upper browse level of elk and bison (Painter and Ripple 2012; Painter and Tercek 2020; Painter et al. 2023), as well as cottonwoods in portions of the Upper Lamar study reach (Beschta and Ripple 2015). However, the horning and rubbing of saplings and small trees accessible to bison are easily capable of removing bark and cambium, killing them directly by girdling or indirectly by creating increased opportunities for disease entry (Debyle and Winokur 1985; Newcombe 1996; Stettler et al. 1996; Beschta and Ripple 2020). The breakage of young cottonwood, willow, and aspen stems by bison also has been documented (Painter and Ripple 2012; Painter and Tercek 2020; Painter et al. 2023).

In a 2010 field study of young willows and cottonwoods in the Lamar Valley, Painter and Ripple (2012) evaluated browsing rates and plant growth. At that time, the Northern Range bison population comprised ~2000 animals, slightly more than half the size of the 2023 population. Even so, summertime utilization rates associated with bison were 54% for cottonwood and 84% for willow. This pattern of relatively high use of woody plants during the growing season contrasts markedly with that of elk, whose use normally occurs in late fall or winter. Trampling and grazing damage during the growing season likely affects plant growth, stature, and vigor to a greater extent than when plants are dormant (Elmore and Kauffman 1994).

Although bison most commonly forage along valley bottoms and other low-elevation areas of northern Yellowstone (Geremia and Hamilton 2019; Beschta et al. 2020; Yellowstone National Park 2024b), increased bison herbivory, as well as the associated effects of trampling, horning, rubbing, and stem breakage, currently represent important mechanisms capable of adversely affecting deciduous plant communities in other portions of the Northern Range (Kauffman et al. 2023; Painter et al. 2023; Hobbs et al. 2024; Kauffman et al. 2025). In some cases, these large herbivore effects may be reversing aspen and willow recovery—a recovery that generally began in the early 2000s following a decline in elk browsing (Beschta and Ripple 2016).

The rate of increase in bark-damaged trees from the effects of bison horning and rubbing differed between our two study reaches. Nevertheless, both areas illustrate increased tree damage between 2001 and 2023, with more than a 12-fold increase in damaged trees in the Lower Lamar study reach and a 6-fold increase in the Upper Lamar (Fig. 7). The trend for the Lower Lamar suggests that nearly 100% of its cottonwood trees will likely have measurable bark damage within a few more years. Since the plants within the genus Populus are often susceptible to various foliar and stem diseases (Newcombe 1996), these results suggest that bark damage to the valley’s overstory cottonwoods could be contributing to the ongoing loss of these iconic trees.

Lodgepole pine is noted for having particularly thin bark (Parker 1950) that is easily affected by physical abrasion. Although lodgepole pines normally grow on upland sites, in 2018 Beschta et al. (2020) evaluated a stand of 91 mature lodgepole pines (dbh range = 32–49 cm) growing immediately adjacent to the river in the Lower Lamar study reach. Ninety-seven percent of them were dead, with cambium absent from 60–80% of their circumferences due to bison horning and rubbing. Earlier, Olenicki and Irby (2004) measured over 1300 conifers ≥5 cm dbh bordering sagebrush-grassland in the Park’s Hayden Valley. More than 20% of the circumference of 56% of their sampled trees had been damaged by bison horning and rubbing, and an additional 28% of the trees were dead, all showing visual signs of bark damage from bison. These findings were consistent with earlier observations by Meagher (1973) indicating that bison had damaged the bark of nearly every pine tree around Hayden Valley.

The physical effects of bison horning and rubbing appear to represent a relatively “new” disturbance process within the Park, capable of affecting the composition and structural diversity of cottonwoods and willows in riparian areas, aspen in uplands and riparian areas, and conifers along rivers and grassland edges (White et al. 2015; Beschta et al. 2020; Painter et al. 2012, 2023). This mechanism was likely an important factor affecting woody plant communities on the Great Plains, where bison evolved and vast numbers of them once existed (Bailey 2013), but not in the topographically constrained valleys of northern Yellowstone National Park where bison were historically scarce.

Although many of valley’s cottonwoods grow on floodplains and terraces some distance from the river, the general lack of riverbank vegetation allows accelerated bank erosion and channel avulsions to remove large numbers of cottonwoods within a single flooding event. Such was the case in the Upper Lamar, where a channel avulsion in 2002 removed 90 trees (average dbh = 82 cm) (Beschta and Ripple 2015). Prior to the major flooding of June 2022, another avulsion occurred in the Lower Lamar study reach. It began ~1.8 km downstream of the Lamar River’s confluence with Soda Butte Creek and created more than two kilometers of new river channel. Although no cottonwoods were lost, the river nevertheless removed large volumes of floodplain soil.

An analysis of cottonwood tree stand structure in the Lamar Valley (Beschta 2003, 2005) confirmed that recruitment was not occurring throughout the late 1900s, a period during which our chronosequence photographs (Figs. 8–10 and 12) confirm that significant loss of overstory cottonwoods was ongoing. Our cottonwood inventories for 2001, 2012, and 2023 indicate a high rate of contemporary overstory mortality has continued (Fig. 7).

During periods of high flow, the hydraulic roughness imparted by above-ground stems and branches of riparian vegetation reduce water velocities, thereby retarding riverbank erosion and enhancing the deposition of fine sediment and organic matter on floodplains (Darby 1999; Gyssels and Poesen 2003; Smith 2004). Additionally, the extensive root systems of riparian vegetation help to stabilize riverbanks, thus facilitating narrow river channels and reduced rates of lateral channel migration (Hickin 1984; Micheli and Kirchner 2002; Murray and Paola 2003). The general absence of woody plants along the Lamar River by the early 1990s led Rosgen (1993) to conclude that riparian communities were no longer stabilizing riverbanks, thereby leading to high rates of riverbank erosion and increased rates of channel migration. That conclusion was supported by a subsequent analysis of river sinuosity for the Lower Lamar, where it was found to have decreased by ~30% over the relatively short period of only six decades (i.e., 1954–2015) (Beschta et al. 2020). This general straightening of the river’s channel represents a trend toward greater stream power which, in turn, increases a river’s capability to erode riverbanks, migrate laterally, and increase sediment loads (Gordon et al. 1992). Accelerated riverbank erosion, increased frequency of avulsions, and less sinuous and wider active channels have become increasingly common features associated with the Lower Lamar study reach (Figs. 9, 10, 13, 14), effects that may have been exacerbated during the June 2022 flood.

Field observations of riparian areas along the Lower Lamar indicate that willow recruitment during the last two decades, except for a few small locations, has been rare. An evaluation of riparian vegetation along small streams in the Northern Range by Kauffman et al. (2023) similarly found a near-total absence of willows and other woody species along three Lamar River tributaries. In that study, declines in willow cover, species diversity, and wetland species were significantly associated with areas of high levels of bison use. Riparian plant communities along these streams are now dominated by exotic perennial grasses, principally Kentucky bluegrass (Poa pratensis) and meadow timothy (Phleum pratense), and by noxious weeds. When streamside vegetation is suppressed or eliminated, stream incision and over-widened active channels are likely to occur, thus contributing to reduced frequencies of overbank flow and the lowering of riparian water tables. Such effects can shift plant assemblages toward species adapted to warmer and drier conditions and exacerbate the effects of climate change.

Importantly, contemporary vegetation responses across the Northern Range are spatially heterogeneous. In areas of high bison use such as the Lamar Valley, intensive herbivory, trampling, and bark damage are suppressing recruitment and degrading many riparian plant communities (Beschta et al. 2020; Kauffman et al. 2023; Painter et al. 2023). These areas are continuing to experience significant loss of ecosystem services normally provided by diverse riparian plant communities, effects initiated by elk nearly a century ago, and are trending toward a state of severe biotic impoverishment (Kauffman et al. 2023). However, in other areas of the Northern Range with relatively low bison densities, the large carnivore–elk–plant trophic cascade has reduced elk browsing and initiated the recovery of riparian woody plants (Beschta and Ripple 2016; Painter et al. 2018; Ripple et al. 2025). The system thus reflects a mosaic, with trophic cascade-driven recovery in some locations but strong bison impacts overriding those effects in others.

Management Implications

Riparian plant communities and aspen stands across western North America are normally recognized for their exceptionally high biodiversity and productivity (Mueggler 1985; Berger et al. 2001; Kauffman et al. 2001). Maintaining these hotspots of biodiversity would seemingly align with the National Park Service mission statement, which is “to conserve the scenery and the natural and historic objects and the wildlife therein and to provide for the enjoyment of the same in such manner and by such means as will leave them unimpaired for the enjoyment of future generations” (Ross 2013).

From 2014 to 2023, the Park’s managed Northern Range bison herd had an average population of ~3,600 animals (Yellowstone National Park 2024b), well above previous levels. This recent increase in bison numbers appears to mark the beginning of a new phase of ungulate dominance, not only within the Lamar Valley study area but increasingly across other portions of the Northern Range. A recent Record of Decision (Yellowstone National Park 2024a) indicates Park Service managers intend to maintain a relatively large population of bison into the future.
