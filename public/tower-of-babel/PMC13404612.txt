Introduction

The publish-or-perish paradigm puts pressure on academics worldwide to produce internationally visible research, with journal reputation, citation rates and open-access publications, which all together shape academic promotion, funding and institutional evaluation.1

However, the conditions under which research is carried out and published remain unequal across countries. In high-income countries (HICs), stable national research funding, multiple competitive grant cycles and well-developed research infrastructure support continuous scientific production.2 Researchers in many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) conduct research with minimal funding, limited infrastructure and few opportunities for sustained collaboration.3 Yet their work is often assessed against the same international benchmarks as that produced in much better-resourced systems. In response, publishers, funders and international organisations have introduced APC waivers, targeted funding schemes and capacity-building initiatives. While these measures have expanded access for some, they are largely tied to World Bank income classifications, which assume a country’s income status reflects its research capacity. In this commentary, we question this assumption and argue that the metric itself, not only how it is applied, needs to be reconsidered.

Open access and financial barriers

Open access (OA) publishing is central to global health research and is promoted on grounds of transparency, equity and access to publicly funded knowledge. The prevailing APC-funded model has restructured rather than eliminated financial barriers, shifting them from library subscriptions to author-facing fees. Many HICs have national Read-and-Publish agreements that centrally cover APCs (eg, Germany, the UK, the Netherlands, Sweden and Israel) as part of broader open-access strategies.4 In countries with low research and development (R&D) spending, by contrast, researchers often pay out of pocket, in amounts consuming a substantial share of their monthly salary. In Iraq, an early-career researcher with a Ph.D. earns approximately 429 000 Iraqi dinars (IQD; about US$327) per month, with total gross monthly income averaging around 1 056 700 IQD (US$807).5 APCs for some high-impact medical journals range between US$7,000 and US$9,000; in such cases, publication may consume roughly 1 year of gross salary, and even with a 25–50% discount for upper-middle-income countries (UMICs), the cost remains unaffordable for many researchers.

When researchers in under-resourced settings cannot afford OA fees, the published record skews toward WEIRD societies (Western, Educated, Industrialised, Rich and Democratic).6 This particularly affects fields such as psychology and public health, where context shapes both what is studied and how it is measured. Evidence from trauma intervention studies among US military veterans, for example, does not automatically show how such interventions would work for survivors of the Anfal or Rwandan genocides; these findings need adaptation and validation before they can be applied elsewhere. Yet the local researchers best placed to conduct this work and share their findings globally are often the ones excluded from publishing by APC barriers

Income classification is the wrong metric for research capacity

The World Bank Group classifies countries into four income groups (low, lower-middle, upper-middle and high) based on gross national income (GNI) per capita through the Atlas method.7 GNI per capita was developed for comparative economic monitoring and concessional lending, not to measure the conditions under which research is produced.

The metric has well-known limitations even as a measure of development. As a single indicator, it does not capture income distribution within countries (the Gini coefficient), nor non-income dimensions such as the Human Development Index, the Multidimensional Poverty Index or the Fragile States Index. It is also not sensitive to the composition of national income; countries whose GNI is concentrated in extractive sectors may be ranked as UMICs or HICs while having limited investment in education, research infrastructure and public services.8 Research capacity, in particular, depends on R&D spending, access to grants, infrastructure, administrative support and political stability, none of which are captured by GNI per capita.

According to the UNESCO Science Report, eight out of ten countries spend less than 1% of their gross domestic product (GDP) on R&D; this is not restricted to LMICs but includes many HICs and UMICs.9 Such investment levels are insufficient for maintaining strong research ecosystems, especially where academic institutions are destabilised by ongoing conflict and political instability.10

Most Middle Eastern countries exemplify this discrepancy. Iraq is classified as a UMIC and allocates approximately 0.04% of its GDP to R&D, while Kuwait and Bahrain, both HICs, invest approximately 0.08% and 0.1% of their GDP, respectively.11 As illustrated in figure 1, these levels are comparable to or below the average in certain LMICs.

Despite Iraq’s UMIC classification, 36.8% of the population lives in multidimensional poverty,12 and a substantial share of national income is based on oil and gas revenues, while investment in education, health and research remains low due to prolonged conflict and political instability. UMIC status here reflects extractive-sector revenues rather than the conditions under which research is conducted.

Existing reform proposals and their limits

Previous commentaries, mostly focused on sub-Saharan Africa, have highlighted shortcomings of income-based APC waivers, including poor alignment with researcher salaries, grant access and collaboration and proposed refined or tiered models.13–15 However, these proposals retain income classification as the underlying criterion. We argue the criterion itself should be replaced, as GNI per capita was developed for concessional lending eligibility, not for measuring research conditions. What is needed is therefore a different basis for eligibility, grounded in research-system capacity and structural vulnerability. We also extend the critique beyond sub-Saharan Africa to UMICs and HICs with low research investment or fragile institutions and introduce two considerations: payment infrastructure and perverse authorship incentives that the existing reform debate has not addressed.

Structural problems with discount and waiver programmes

Income-based APC waivers face two limitations that further refinement of eligibility rules cannot remedy.

Payment infrastructure is one such barrier. In HICs with Read-and-Publish agreements, the institution pays the APC, and the researcher never sees the transaction. In low-resource and conflict-affected settings, the researcher handles the invoice personally, often issued in their own name, and frequently without financial infrastructure to make payment. Universities in such settings have no institutional accounts for international transfers, salaries are paid in cash and personal access to international banking is limited or, in sanctioned countries, blocked entirely. Under these conditions, even a reduced fee can remain impossible to use if there is no way to transfer the money.

A second limitation is the perverse authorship incentives that income-based waiver systems can create. Researchers in low-resource settings may be tempted to add HIC-affiliated colleagues as co-authors without substantive contribution to access institutional APC funds otherwise unavailable to them. Conversely, HIC-led research teams may submit through LMIC corresponding authors to obtain country-based waivers, reproducing the same inequity in mirror form. Both practices distort authorship norms, dilute intellectual ownership of the research and shift credit away from those who did the work.

From individual waivers to institutional support

A reform addressing both the metric problem and the structural problems above requires two complementary moves, namely, a multi-criterion eligibility framework for waivers and discounts and the extension of institutional Read-and-Publish agreements to under-resourced settings. Under multi-criterion eligibility, the framework should combine several indicators that together reflect research conditions more accurately than country income alone. National R&D expenditure relative to the global median, drawn from existing World Bank data, would distinguish HICs and UMICs that invest substantially in research from those that do not.

Structural vulnerability can be captured through the World Bank’s Harmonized List of Fragile and Conflict-Affected Situations, complemented by political-instability indices that identify conflict-affected sub-national regions not reflected in country-level lists. Eligibility should also turn on where the work is led, based on whether the principal investigator holds a primary appointment in an under-resourced research system, regardless of the income classification of any co-authors. To discourage proxy and honorary authorship in either direction, authorship contributions should be verified, for example, through the CRediT taxonomy. Local ethics approval should accompany these criteria, signalling genuine local engagement and strengthening ethics governance where the research is conducted.

A more structural response would move beyond individual waivers. Read-and-Publish agreements could be extended to under-resourced settings through regional bodies such as the African Union, the League of Arab States and the Association of Southeast Asian Nations, in partnership with their university networks. This would allow institutional contributions to reflect actual research capacity, payment infrastructure and operating conditions, rather than relying only on national income categories. A pilot agreement between a major publisher and a regional consortium, such as universities affiliated with the Association of Arab Universities, could test this, with contributions tiered by national R&D expenditure and institutional capacity.

Conclusion

The World Bank income classification was developed for economic policy, for monitoring global development and concessional lending eligibility, rather than guiding research funding or OA publication support. As OA publication becomes central to academic recognition, eligibility criteria must reflect research-system structure rather than the aggregate income of the countries in which those systems operate. We propose two complementary steps. First, journals and publishers should adopt multi-criterion eligibility for waivers and discounts, based on research-system characteristics and structural vulnerability rather than income classification. Second, regional bodies should take the lead in negotiating read-and-Publish agreements covering their member institutions, and major publishers should be willing to propose such initiatives in turn so that researchers worldwide have a fairer chance to publish.
