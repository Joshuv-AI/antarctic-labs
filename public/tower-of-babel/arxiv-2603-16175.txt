Classification of unmixed parity binomial edge ideals of cactus and chordal graphs
Deblina Dey, A. V. Jayanthan, Sarang Sane

Abstract
. In this article, we characterize all unmixed and Cohen-Macaulay parity binomial edge ideals of cactus and chordal graphs in terms of the structural properties of the graph.

Introduction

Let
G
be a finite simple graph on the vertex set
V(G)=[n]=\{1,\ldots,n\}
and the edge set
E(G)
. Kahle, Sarmiento and Windisch in 2016,
[
9

parity binomial edge ideals
:

\mathcal{I}_{G}:=(x_{i}x_{j}-y_{i}y_{j}:\{i,j\}\in E(G))\subset R=K[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}],

where

is a field. This ideal is a subtle variation of another class of binomial ideals, called
binomial edge ideal
, defined by

[
8
]

J_{G}:=(x_{i}y_{j}-x_{j}y_{i}:\{i,j\}\in E(G))\subset R=K[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}],

A close relation has been observed between several algebraic properties and/or invariants of these edge ideals and combinatorial properties and/or invariants of the graph
G

If
G
is a bipartite graph, then its parity binomial edge ideal is obtained from its binomial edge ideal by a simple change of variables. As noted in the paper of Kahle et al., parity binomial edge ideals share a number of properties with binomial edge ideals, but the combinatorics is subtler. In
[
9
]
, the authors described a Gröbner basis for
\mathcal{I}_{G}
.
If char
(K)\neq 2
, they show that
\mathcal{I}_{G}
is a radical ideal and explicitly obtain the structure of the minimal primes in terms of the disconnector sets (see Definition
2.3
) of the graph. Our study of the unmixed property of
\mathcal{I}_{G}
crucially uses this description of the minimal primes and hence in this article, we assume that char
(K)\neq 2
.

In
[
13
]
, Kumar studied some of the structural properties of
\mathcal{I}_{G}
. He proved that for a bipartite graph
G
,
\mathcal{I}_{G}
is a complete intersection if and only if
G
is a disjoint union of paths, and, for a non-bipartite graph,
\mathcal{I}_{G}
is a complete intersection if and only if
G
is a cycle on an odd number of vertices.
He also classifies graphs with almost complete intersection parity binomial edge ideals and establishes when their associated Rees algebra
\mathcal{R}(I_{G})
is Cohen-Macaulay.

Among the homological invariants, Betti numbers, Castelnuovo-Mumford regularity and depth are well studied for many classes of homogeneous ideals of polynomial rings. While computation of depth is in general challenging, one particular instance, of maximal depth, i.e., being Cohen-Macaulay, has received a lot of attention.

An ideal
I
in a Noetherian ring
R
is said to be Cohen-Macaulay if
R/I
is Cohen-Macaulay. Understanding the Cohen-Macaulayness of an ideal is a classical topic. When it comes to ideals associated with certain geometric/topological/combinatorial structures, the attempt is to characterize the Cohen-Macaulayness in terms of the structure associated with the ideal. When
G
is a bipartite graph, Bolognini, Macchia and Strazzanti completely characterized when the binomial edge ideal
J_{G}
(and hence the parity binomial edge ideal
\mathcal{I}_{G}
) is Cohen-Macaulay in terms of the structure of
G
,
[
1
]
. For an arbitrary graph
G
, Bolognini et al. conjectured that
J_{G}
is Cohen-Macaulay if and only if
G
is accessible (see
[
2
]
for the details). They proved the ‘only if’ part of this conjecture and proved the ‘if’ part for chordal graphs and graphs containing a Hamiltonian path. The general case is still open. For the case of parity binomial edge ideals, no such results, and not even a conjecture is available in this direction.

Any Cohen-Macaulay ideal must be unmixed. Hence, to understand the Cohen-Macaulayness, one should understand the unmixed property of the ideal as well. In this article, we first investigate the unmixedness of parity binomial edge ideals of non-bipartite chordal graphs. As a consequence of our investigation, we are also able to characterize their Cohen-Macaulayness as well.

The unmixed property of the parity binomial edge ideal is much more complex than that of the binomial edge ideals. In the case of binomial edge ideals, the combinatorial object that determines the minimality of an associated prime is called a cut set and there is a unique prime ideal associated to each cut set. In the case of parity binomial edge ideals, the corresponding object is called a disconnector set and there are several prime ideals associated with one disconnector set. Our investigation of the unmixedness property crucially depends on the construction of suitable disconnector sets.

Definition 2.2

Disconnector sets and unmixedness

While a disconnector set may not always satisfy the sign-split property, some restriction on the set allows it to be sign-split as discussed below.

Remark 2.6

[

Let

Proposition 2.7

Let

Hence, the sign-split property is preserved.
∎

G
is a cactus graph, any path from
C_{3}
to
C_{2}
passes through
C_{1}
. So, any path from
v_{3}
to
v_{2}
passes through
v_{1}^{\prime}
and
v_{1}
. Since
C_{3}
intersects any path from
v_{3}
to
v_{2}
and it does not intersect any path from
v_{1}
to
v_{2}
, we get
\delta(v_{2},v_{3})\geq\delta(v_{2},v_{1})+1>\delta(v_{2},v_{1})
. This is a contradiction to the selection of
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
. Hence
(C_{1},v_{1})
is a pendant odd cycle.
∎

Notation 3.4
.

Let
G
be a cactus graph and
(C,v)
be a pendant odd cycle of
G
. Note that
v
is a cut vertex and
\{v\}\in\mathcal{D}(G)
. It follows that
C\setminus\{v\}
must be contained in a bipartite connected component of
G\setminus\{v\}
, say
G_{0}
. Write
G\setminus\{v\}=G_{0}\sqcup G^{\prime}
. Here
G^{\prime}
is the union of connected components of
G\setminus\{v\}
except
G_{0}
.

Our aim is to show that unmixedness descends from
\mathcal{I}_{G}
to
\mathcal{I}_{G^{\prime}}
. For this purpose, we first prove a couple of technical lemmas.

Lemma 3.5
.

With the notation as in
3.4
, let
S\subseteq V(G^{\prime})
. Then
c_{G}(S\cup\{v\})=c_{G^{\prime}}(S)+1
and
b_{G}(S\cup\{v\})=b_{G^{\prime}}(S)+1
.

Proof.

Since
G\setminus\{v\}=G_{0}\sqcup G^{\prime}
and
S\subseteq V(G^{\prime})
, we have

\mathcal{C}_{G}(S\cup\{v\})=\mathcal{C}_{G\setminus\{v\}}(S)=\mathcal{C}_{G_{0}\sqcup G^{\prime}}(S)=\mathcal{C}_{G^{\prime}}(S)\sqcup\{G_{0}\}.

Similarly,
B_{G}(S\cup\{v\})=B_{G^{\prime}}(S)\sqcup\{G_{0}\}
. Hence the assertion follows.
∎

Lemma 3.6
.

With the notation as in
3.4
, if
S\in\mathcal{D}(G^{\prime})
, then
S\cup\{v\}\in\mathcal{D}(G)
.

Proof.

Let
S\in\mathcal{D}(G^{\prime})
. Therefore
S
is a sign-split disconnector set of
G^{\prime}
and for every
s\in S
,
c_{G^{\prime}}(S)+b_{G^{\prime}}(S)>c_{G^{\prime}}(S\setminus\{s\})+b_{G^{\prime}}(S\setminus\{s\})
.
Then it follows from
Lemma

3.5
that for every
s\in S,

c_{G}(S\cup\{v\})+b_{G}(S\cup\{v\})>c_{G}((S\cup\{v\})\setminus\{s\})+b_{G}((S\cup\{v\})\setminus\{s\}).

Since
G_{0}
is bipartite and
G
is non-bipartite,

c_{G}(S\cup\{v\})+b_{G}(S\cup\{v\})>c_{G}(S)+b_{G}(S)
. We now prove that
S\cup\{v\}
satisfies the sign-split property. Note that
S
has a sign-split property in
G^{\prime}
. Let
s\in S\cup\{v\}
. If
s\neq v
, then
\mathcal{C}_{G\setminus(S\cup\{v\})}(s)=\mathcal{C}_{G^{\prime}\setminus S}(s)
. If
s=v
, then
\mathcal{C}_{G\setminus(S\cup\{v\})}(s)=\mathcal{C}_{G^{\prime}\setminus S}(s)\cup\{G_{0}\}
, and
G_{0}
is bipartite. Hence in both cases
S\cup\{v\}
satisfies sign-split property in
G
. Therefore
S\cup\{v\}\in\mathcal{D}(G)
.
∎

We now prove that the unmixedness property descends.

Proposition 3.7
.

With the notation as in
3.4
, if
\mathcal{I}_{G}
is unmixed, then so is
\mathcal{I}_{G^{\prime}}
.

Proof.

Since
\{v\}\in\mathcal{D}(G)
and
\mathcal{I}_{G}
is unmixed,
b_{G}(\{v\})=1
. Hence the connected components of
G\setminus\{v\}
other than
G_{0}
must be non-bipartite.
In particular,
G^{\prime}
must be non-bipartite. It follows from
Lemma

3.6
that for all
S\in\mathcal{D}(G^{\prime})
,
S\cup\{v\}\in\mathcal{D}(G)
. As
\mathcal{I}_{G}
is unmixed,
b_{G}(S\cup\{v\})=|S\cup\{v\}|
. This implies
b_{G^{\prime}}(S)=|S|
, by
Lemma

3.5
. Thus
\mathcal{I}_{G^{\prime}}
is unmixed.
∎

We now proceed to characterize the cactus graphs having unmixed parity binomial edge ideals.

Proposition 3.8
.

Let
G
be a non-bipartite cactus graph with only one odd cycle. Then
\mathcal{I}_{G}
is unmixed if and only if
G
is an odd cycle.

Proof.

The ‘if part’ follows from
[
13
, Theorem 3.5]
. Now assume that
\mathcal{I}_{G}
is unmixed. Let
C
be the odd cycle in
G
. If there exists a
v\in V(C)
such that
\deg_{G}(v)>2
, then
v
is a cut vertex. Thus
\{v\}\in\mathcal{D}(G)
. Moreover,
G\setminus\{v\}
is bipartite and has at least two components, i.e.,
b_{G}(\{v\})\geq 2
. This contradicts the unmixedness of
\mathcal{I}_{G}
. Hence for every
v\in V(C)
,
\deg_{G}(v)=2
. Therefore,
G=C
.
∎

We now show that if a cactus graph contains more than one odd cycle, then the parity binomial edge ideal is not unmixed.

Theorem 3.9
.

Let
G
be a cactus graph having at least two odd cycles as subgraphs, then
\mathcal{I}_{G}
is not unmixed.

Proof.

We prove the statement by induction on
r(G)
, the number of odd cycles in
G
. First assume that
r(G)=2
and
\mathcal{I}_{G}
is unmixed. Since
G
is cactus with only two odd cycles, both must be pendant odd cycles, say
(C_{1},v_{1})
and
(C_{2},v_{2})
. If
v_{1}=v_{2}
, then
\{v_{1}\}\in\mathcal{D}(G)
and
b_{G}(\{v_{1}\})=2
which is a contradiction to the unmixedness of
\mathcal{I}_{G}
. Suppose
v_{1}\neq v_{2}
. First, we take the disconnector set
\{v_{1}\}
. Suppose
G\setminus\{v_{1}\}=G_{0}\sqcup G^{\prime}
, where
G_{0}
is the bipartite component containing
C_{1}\setminus\{v_{1}\}
and
G^{\prime}
is the non-bipartite graph containing
C_{2}
. Since
b_{G}(\{v_{1}\})=1
and since there are only two odd cycles in
G
,
G^{\prime}
is also connected.
Moreover,

Proposition

3.7
implies that
\mathcal{I}_{G^{\prime}}
is unmixed. But
G^{\prime}
is a connected cactus graph with only one odd cycle. So, from
Proposition

3.8
, it follows that
G^{\prime}
is an odd cycle, i.e.,
G^{\prime}=C_{2}
. Using the same argument for
\{v_{2}\}
, we get
G\setminus\{v_{2}\}=(C_{2}\setminus\{v_{2}\})\sqcup C_{1}
. So,
V(G)=V(C_{1})\cup V(C_{2})
and
E(G)=E(C_{1})\cup E(C_{2})\cup\{e\}
, where
e=\{v_{1},v_{2}\}
is the only edge between
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
.
Choose
x_{1}\in V(C_{1})\setminus\{v_{1}\}
and
x_{2}\in V(C_{2})\setminus\{v_{2}\}
. Then
\{x_{1},x_{2}\}\in\mathcal{D}(G)
, but

, a contradiction to the unmixedness of
\mathcal{I}_{G}
. Therefore
\mathcal{I}_{G}
is not unmixed.

Now let
r(G)\geq 3
and assume by induction that if
H
is a connected non-bipartite cactus graph with
2\leq r(H)<r(G)
, then
\mathcal{I}_{H}
is not unmixed. Suppose
\mathcal{I}_{G}
is unmixed. Let
(C,v)
be a pendant odd cycle of
G
. Then
\{v\}\in\mathcal{D}(G).
If
v
is contained in more than one pendant odd cycles, then
b_{G}(\{v\})>1
which contradicts the unmixedness of
\mathcal{I}_{G}
. Thus we can write
G\setminus\{v\}=G_{0}\sqcup G^{\prime}
, where
G_{0}
is the bipartite component containing
C\setminus\{v\}
and
G^{\prime}
is a non-bipartite graph with each component a non-bipartite cactus graph. Observe that
G^{\prime}
will not have any bipartite component, since otherwise
b_{G}(\{v\})>1
contradicting the unmixedness of
\mathcal{I}_{G}
. Note that for any non-bipartite connected component
H
of
G^{\prime}
,
r(H)<r(G)
. We know from
Proposition

3.7
that
\mathcal{I}_{G^{\prime}}
is unmixed. Now we have two cases.

Case 1:
If any of the connected components of
G^{\prime}
has at least two odd cycles, then by induction hypothesis
\mathcal{I}_{G^{\prime}}
is not unmixed, which contradicts
Proposition

3.7
.

Case 2:
Suppose every non-bipartite connected component of
G^{\prime}
has only one odd cycle. Since
\mathcal{I}_{G}
is unmixed, so is
\mathcal{I}_{G^{\prime}}
, by
Proposition

3.7
. Therefore, by
Proposition

3.8
, we get that all these connected components of
G^{\prime}
are cycles. Write
G^{\prime}=C_{1}\sqcup C_{2}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup C_{k}
, where
C_{i}
are odd cycles. Let
v_{i}\in V(C_{i})
be such that
\{v,v_{i}\}\in E(G)
. Since
G
is a cactus graph, these
v_{i}
are the unique vertices of
C_{i}
that are adjacent to
v
. Hence
(C_{i},v_{i})
are all pendant odd cycles of
G
, for
1\leq i\leq k
. Now consider
G\setminus\{v_{1}\}=G_{0}^{\prime}\sqcup G^{\prime\prime}
, where
G_{0}^{\prime}
is the bipartite component containing
C_{1}\setminus\{v_{1}\}
and
G^{\prime\prime}
is the non-bipartite connected component containing

. Since
r(G)\geq 3
,

. Hence by induction
\mathcal{I}_{G^{\prime\prime}}
is not unmixed. But this contradicts the conclusion by
Proposition

3.7
that
\mathcal{I}_{G^{\prime\prime}}
is unmixed. Hence
\mathcal{I}_{G}
is not unmixed.
∎

Summarizing all the above results, we get:

Corollary 3.10
.

Let
G
be a non-bipartite cactus graph. Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

\mathcal{I}_{G}
is unmixed;

(2)

\mathcal{I}_{G}
is Cohen-Macaulay;

(3)

\mathcal{I}_{G}
is Gorenstein;

(4)

\mathcal{I}_{G}
is a complete intersection;

(5)

G
is an odd cycle.

Proof.

The implications

are always true. By
[
13
]
,
(4)
and
(5)
are equivalent. The implication
(1)\Rightarrow(5)
follows from
Proposition

3.8
and
Theorem

3.9
.
∎

4.
The algorithm

Unlike cactus graphs, it is not clear how to study the unmixed property of

using hereditary behavior for chordal graphs.

The above theorem gives us some classes of unmixed parity binomial edge ideals. Later, we show that those are the only possible classes
among chordal graphs
. The unmixed property of
\mathcal{I}_{G}
depends on the sign-split disconnector sets of
G
. Unlike binomial edge ideals, for parity binomial edge ideals of chordal graphs, the disconnector sets does not behave well under the graph operations as studied in
[
2
]
. Also, we do not have a short exact sequence as given in the papers
[
5
]
and
[
11
]
which enables one to use induction on certain related graphs. In short, what we see is that the standard techniques that work for the binomial edge ideals are not really useful for parity binomial edge ideals. So, to understand the behavior of disconnector sets, we construct an algorithm based on the structure of the graph and this algorithm leads us to some specific disconnector sets. These disconnector sets satisfy certain combinatorial properties which play an important role in characterizing
G
when
\mathcal{I}_{G}
is unmixed.

To simplify further exposition, we define the following function
m
.

m:\mathcal{P}([t])\rightarrow\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\},\quad\qquad\text{where}\qquad m(\Gamma)=|V(\cap_{i\in\Gamma}K_{n_{i}})|.

Here
\mathcal{P}([t])
represents the power set of
[t]=\{1,2,\ldots,t\}
.

4.1.
Algorithm:

Now we describe the algorithm below. Recall that
G
is a clique sum of
K_{n_{1}},\ldots,K_{n_{t}}.
At each step and each iteration of the algorithm, we choose a maximal subset of
\{1,\ldots,t\}
such that it takes a nonzero value under the function
m
. Then we choose a vertex from the intersection of all complete graphs corresponding to that set. The construction of the algorithm ensures that all these chosen vertices form a connected subgraph of
G
. Later on we show that this connected graph is an induced tree and it is a maximal tree of
G
. To get a better understanding of the algorithm, we recommend the reader to follow Example
4.8
alongside the algorithm:

Global Input:
The function
m
and the graph
G
.

Step
0
:

\displaystyle\textit{Iteration $1$}:\text{Define}

\displaystyle T_{1}^{0}=[t],

\displaystyle\Gamma_{1}^{0}=\{L\subseteq[t]:m(L)>0\},

\displaystyle M(\Gamma_{1}^{0})=\{\text{maximal elements of}~\Gamma_{1}^{0}~\text{with respect to the order relation $\subseteq$}\}.

\displaystyle\text{Fix }L_{1}^{0}\in M(\Gamma^{0}_{1}).

\displaystyle\textit{Outputs of iteration $1$:}\quad L_{1}^{0}.

Proceed to Iteration
2
in Step
0
.

\displaystyle\textit{Iteration $q\geq 2$}:\quad\text{Inputs:}\quad L_{i}^{0},~i<q.

Define

\displaystyle T_{q}^{0}=T_{q-1}^{0}\setminus L_{q-1}^{0}=[t]\setminus\big(\bigcup_{j=1}^{q-1}L_{j}^{0}\big),

\displaystyle\Gamma_{q}^{0}=\{L\subseteq[t]:L\cap T_{q}^{0}\neq\emptyset,~m(L)>0\},

\displaystyle M(\Gamma_{q}^{0})=\{\text{maximal elements of}~\Gamma_{q}^{0}~\text{with respect to the order relation $\subseteq$}\},

\displaystyle M_{1}(\Gamma_{q}^{0})=\{L\in M(\Gamma_{q}^{0}):L\cap L_{i}^{0}\neq\emptyset~\text{for exactly one $i<q$}\},

\displaystyle\text{If }M_{1}(\Gamma_{q}^{0})=\emptyset,\text{then the }0^{th}\text{ step terminates}.

\displaystyle\text{Else }M_{2}(\Gamma_{q}^{0})=\{L\in M_{1}(\Gamma_{q}^{0}):|L\cap L_{i}^{0}|~\text{is minimum}\}.

Choose
L_{q}^{0}\in M_{2}(\Gamma_{q}^{0})
and
\displaystyle{x_{q}^{0}\in\bigcap_{i\in L_{q}^{0}}V(K_{n_{i}})}
.

\displaystyle\textit{Outputs of Iteration $q$:}\quad L_{q}^{0}.

Proceed to Iteration
q+1
in Step
0
.

Once the
0^{th}
step terminates, define

\displaystyle D_{0}

|V(H_{\mathrm{n}}\setminus\{x_{q}^{p}\})\cap N_{G}(\alpha)|=1

If

is unmixed, then

is a path graph for all

.

Note that the algorithm provides us
with
two special disconnector sets

and

, and using them we proved two important results

and

in this section. We use all these data to characterize the unmixed chordal graphs in the next section.

5.
Conditions when non-bipartite chordal graphs are not unmixed

In this section we consider those chordal graphs
G
for which
\cap_{i\in[t]}K_{n_{i}}=\emptyset
, i.e.,
m([t])=0
.
We show that for a large subclass of chordal graph for which
\mathcal{I}_{G}
, the algorithm produces a tree which is not a path and hence all such graphs are not unmixed.

The following remark will be implicitly used in many of the subsequent hypotheses.

Remark 5.1
.

Note that
m([t])=0
if and only if
L_{1}^{0}
is a proper subset of
[t]
if and only if
L_{2}^{0}
exists in any run of the algorithm. Therefore
m([t])=0
if and only if there exist two different sets
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
in
M(\Gamma_{1}^{0})
so that
L_{1}\cap L_{2}\neq\emptyset
.

We now prove a lemma that plays a crucial role in showing that
\mathcal{I}_{G}
is not unmixed, for a large subclass of chordal graphs.
If there exists a subset
S
of
V(G)
so that
G\setminus S
has at least three components, then the following lemma gives a sufficient condition for
\mathcal{I}_{G}
to not be unmixed. Recall that by
\mathcal{L}
we denote one run of the algorithm.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
S\subset V(G)
and
l_{0},l_{0}^{\prime},l_{0}^{\prime\prime}\in[t]
be such that
K_{n_{l_{0}}}\setminus S
,
K_{n_{l_{0}^{\prime}}}\setminus S
and
K_{n_{l_{0}^{\prime\prime}}}\setminus S
are in different connected components of
G\setminus S
. Further suppose that there exist
L_{1},L_{2}\in M(\Gamma_{1}^{0})
such that
l_{0},l_{0}^{\prime},l_{0}^{\prime\prime}\in L_{1}
and
l_{0}\in L_{2}
. Then
\mathcal{I}_{G}
is not unmixed if any one of the following holds:

(1)

Each run
\mathcal{L}
of the algorithm with
L_{1}^{0}(\mathcal{L})=L_{2}
and
L_{2}^{0}(\mathcal{L})=L_{1}
satisfies
V(H_{n(\mathcal{L})})\cap S=\{x_{2}^{0}\}
.

(2)

Each run
\mathcal{L}
of the algorithm with
L_{1}^{0}(\mathcal{L})=L_{1}
and
L_{2}^{0}(\mathcal{L})=L_{2}
satisfies
V(H_{n(\mathcal{L})})\cap S=\{x_{1}^{0}\}
.

Proof.

Note first that runs as in (1) and (2) exist since
L_{1}\neq L_{2}
and
L_{1}\cap L_{2}\neq\emptyset
. Let us assume the statement (1) in the hypothesis and that
\mathcal{I}_{G}
is unmixed. Let
\mathcal{L}
be a run of the algorithm such that
L_{1}^{0}=L_{2}
and
L_{2}^{0}=L_{1}
. We construct a run
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
of the algorithm which contradicts
Corollary

4.35
. First take
L_{q}^{0}(\mathcal{L^{\prime}})=L_{q}^{0}(\mathcal{L})
and
x_{q}^{0}(\mathcal{L^{\prime}})=x_{q}^{0}(\mathcal{L})
for
q\geq 1
, i.e.,
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{L^{\prime}}
have same choices at the
0
th step.
Note that no vertices
x_{q}^{0}(\mathcal{L})
other than
x_{2}^{0}(\mathcal{L})
are in
S
. From now on we write
x_{q}^{0}
instead of
x_{q}^{0}(\mathcal{L^{\prime}})
.

Let
C_{1}
,
C_{2}
and
C_{3}
be the connected components of
G\setminus S
containing
K_{n_{l_{0}}}\setminus S
,
K_{n_{l_{0}^{\prime}}}\setminus S
and
K_{n_{l_{0}^{\prime\prime}}}\setminus S
respectively. Suppose there exists
l\in A_{2}^{0}(\mathcal{L^{\prime}})
such that
V(K_{n_{l}})\cap V(K_{n_{l_{0}^{\prime}}}\setminus S)\neq\emptyset
. Then all vertices in
K_{n_{l}}\setminus S
are in the connected component
C_{2}
. Since
K_{n_{l_{0}}}\setminus S
is in the component
C_{1}
,
V(K_{n_{l}})\cap V(K_{n_{l_{0}}})\subseteq S
. Since
x_{1}^{0}\notin S
by the hypothesis of
(1)
, it follows that
l\notin L_{1}^{0}(\mathcal{L^{\prime}})
. Since
l\in A_{2}^{0}(\mathcal{L^{\prime}})
, there exists
q\in D_{0}(\mathcal{L^{\prime}})
such that
l\in L_{q}^{0}(\mathcal{L^{\prime}})
, where
q\notin\{1,2\}
. So,
x_{q}^{0}\in V(K_{n_{l}}\setminus S)\subseteq V(C_{2})
. Since
\mathcal{I}_{G}
is unmixed,
H_{0}(\mathcal{L^{\prime}})
is a path graph (
Theorem

4.34
). Therefore there exists a unique path from
x_{1}^{0}
to
x_{q}^{0}
in
H_{0}(\mathcal{L^{\prime}})
. Since
x_{1}^{0}
is in
C_{1}
and
x_{q}^{0}
is in
C_{2}
, the path must pass through
S
, and hence through
x_{2}^{0}
.

Similarly, if there exists
l^{\prime}\in A_{2}^{0}(\mathcal{L^{\prime}})
such that
V(K_{n_{l}^{\prime}})\cap V(K_{n_{l_{0}^{\prime\prime}}}\setminus S)\neq\emptyset
, then there exists
x_{q^{\prime}}^{0}\in C_{3}
such that the path from
x_{1}^{0}
to
x_{q^{\prime}}^{0}
passes through
x_{2}^{0}
. Thus we have the following cases based on whether or not such
l
and
l^{\prime}
exist.

Case 1:
V(K_{n_{l_{0}^{\prime}}}\setminus S)\cap\left(\bigcup_{l\in A_{2}^{0}}V(K_{n_{l}})\right)\neq\emptyset
,
V(K_{n_{l_{0}^{\prime\prime}}}\setminus S)\cap\left(\bigcup_{l\in A_{2}^{0}}V(K_{n_{l}})\right)\neq\emptyset
:

As discussed above, there exist paths from
x_{1}^{0}
to
x_{q}^{0}
and
x_{q^{\prime}}^{0}
passing through
x_{2}^{0}
. Note that these paths are distinct because

x_{q}^{0}
and
x_{q^{\prime}}^{0}
are in distinct components. Hence
\deg_{H_{0}(\mathcal{L^{\prime}})}(x_{2}^{0})\geq 3
, a contradiction to
Corollary

4.35
.

Case 2:
V(K_{n_{l_{0}^{\prime}}}\setminus S)\cap\left(\bigcup_{l\in A_{2}^{0}}V(K_{n_{l}})\right)\neq\emptyset
,
V(K_{n_{l_{0}^{\prime\prime}}}\setminus S)\cap\left(\bigcup_{l\in A_{2}^{0}}V(K_{n_{l}})\right)=\emptyset
or vice versa:

We consider only the first situation since the other one has the same proof with the roles of
l_{0}^{\prime}
and
l_{0}^{\prime\prime}
interchanged. In the first situation,
since
(K_{n_{l_{0}^{\prime}}}\setminus S)\cap\left(\bigcup_{l\in A_{2}^{0}(\mathcal{L^{\prime}})}K_{n_{l}}\right)\neq\emptyset
, from the above discussion there exists a path from
x_{1}^{0}
to
x_{q}^{0}
that contains
x_{2}^{0}
. Hence
\deg_{H_{0}(\mathcal{L^{\prime}})}x_{2}^{0}\geq 2
. Now
(K_{n_{l_{0}^{\prime\prime}}}\setminus S)\cap\bigcup_{l\in A_{2}^{0}}K_{n_{l}}=\emptyset
implies
K_{n_{l_{0}^{\prime\prime}}}\nsubseteq\bigcup_{l\in A_{2}^{0}}K_{n_{l}}
. So,
\{l_{0}^{\prime\prime}\}\in\Gamma_{1}^{1}(\mathcal{L^{\prime}})
. We choose
L_{1}^{1}(\mathcal{L^{\prime}})\in M(\Gamma_{1}^{1}(\mathcal{L^{\prime}}))
such that
l_{0}^{\prime\prime}\in L_{1}^{1}(\mathcal{L^{\prime}})
. Since
L_{2}^{0}(\mathcal{L^{\prime}})\cap L_{1}^{1}(\mathcal{L^{\prime}})\neq\emptyset
,
x_{2}^{0}
and
x_{1}^{1}
are adjacent. Hence
\deg_{H_{1}(\mathcal{L^{\prime}})}x_{2}^{0}\geq 3
, a contrad
iction to
Corollary

4.35
.

Case 3:
(K_{n_{l_{0}^{\prime}}}\setminus S)\cap\bigcup_{l\in A_{2}^{0}}K_{n_{l}}=\emptyset
,
(K_{n_{l_{0}^{\prime\prime}}}\setminus S)\cap\bigcup_{l\in A_{2}^{0}}K_{n_{l}}=\emptyset
:

As in Case 2,
K_{n_{l_{0}^{\prime}}}\nsubseteq\bigcup_{l\in A_{2}^{0}}K_{n_{l}}
and we choose
L_{1}^{1}(\mathcal{L^{\prime}})\in M(\Gamma_{1}^{1}(\mathcal{L^{\prime}}))
such that
l_{0}^{\prime}\in L_{1}^{1}(\mathcal{L^{\prime}})
. Since
l_{0}^{\prime}\in L_{1}^{1}(\mathcal{L^{\prime}})\cap L_{2}^{0}(\mathcal{L^{\prime}})
, we see that
x_{2}^{0}
and
x_{1}^{1}
are adjacent. Note that for any
l\in L_{1}^{1}(\mathcal{L^{\prime}})
,
V(K_{n_{l}})\setminus S
and
V(K_{n_{l_{0}^{\prime\prime}}})\setminus S
are in distinct components
C_{2}
and
C_{3}
respectively. This implies
K_{n_{l_{0}^{\prime\prime}}}\nsubseteq\bigcup_{l\in A_{2}^{0}(\mathcal{L^{\prime}})\cup L_{1}^{1}(\mathcal{L^{\prime}})}K_{n_{l}}
. Hence
\{l_{0}^{\prime\prime}\}\in\Gamma_{2}^{1}(\mathcal{L^{\prime}})
. Choose
L_{2}^{0}\in M(\Gamma_{2}^{1}(\mathcal{L^{\prime}}))
such that
l_{0}^{\prime\prime}\in L_{2}^{1}(\mathcal{L^{\prime}})
. So,
l^{\prime\prime}_{0}\in L_{2}^{1}(\mathcal{L^{\prime}})\cap L_{2}^{0}(\mathcal{L^{\prime}})
and hence
x_{2}^{0}
and
x_{2}^{1}
are adjacent. Therefore
\deg_{H_{1}(\mathcal{L^{\prime}})}x_{2}^{0}\geq 3
, a contradiction.

This completes the proof assuming hypothesis (1). Since hypothesis (2) is obtained by interchanging the roles of
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
, the same proof as above with the roles of
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
interchanged, and
x_{1}^{0}
and
x_{2}^{0}
interchanged, works.
∎

The following corollary gives us a combinatorial condition on the graph so that
\mathcal{I}_{G}
is not unmixed and this condition does not depend on the choices in the algorithm.

Corollary 5.3
.

Let
L\subseteq[t]
be such that
m(L)>0
. Further, let
l_{0}\in L
and
S\subseteq V(K_{n_{l_{0}}})
be a disconnector set such that the following conditions hold:

(i)

\cap_{l\in L}V(K_{n_{l}})\cap S=\emptyset
.

(ii)

there exist
l_{0}^{\prime},l_{0}^{\prime\prime}\in[t]
such that
K_{n_{l_{0}}}\setminus S
,
K_{n_{l_{0}^{\prime}}}\setminus S
and
K_{n_{l_{0}^{\prime\prime}}}\setminus S
are in different connected components of
G\setminus S
.

(iii)

V(K_{n_{l_{0}}})\cap V(K_{n_{l_{0}^{\prime}}})\cap V(K_{n_{l_{0}^{\prime\prime}}})\neq\emptyset
.

Then
\mathcal{I}_{G}
is not unmixed.

Proof.

Since
m(L)>0
,
L\in\Gamma_{1}^{0}
. Choose
L_{2}\in M(\Gamma_{1}^{0})
so that it contains
L
. Now
\{l_{0},l_{0}^{\prime},l_{0}^{\prime\prime}\}\in M(\Gamma_{1}^{0})
due to condition (iii). We take
L_{1}\in\Gamma_{1}^{0}
so that it contains
\{l_{0},l_{0}^{\prime},l_{0}^{\prime\prime}\}
. Now we run the algorithm with
L_{1}^{0}=L_{2}
and
L_{2}^{0}=L_{1}
. Then
x_{1}^{0}\notin S
(by (i)). Since
S\subset V(K_{n_{l_{0}}})
and
l_{0}\in L_{1}^{0}\cap L_{2}^{0}
, for every vertex
v\in S
,
\{v,x_{1}^{0},x_{2}^{0}\}
gives a
3
-cycle. Hence no vertices of
H_{n}
other than
\{x_{2}^{0}\}
belong to
S
, i.e.,
V(H_{n})\cap S=\{x_{2}^{0}\}
. Thus from
Lemma

5.2
(1),
\mathcal{I}_{G}
is not unmixed.
∎

Now we consider all those non-bipartite chordal graphs which has a clique
K_{r_{i}}
,

, such that

has at least three components. We show that for such a graph

Notation 5.4

Let

i_{2}=\min\{j\in\{2,\ldots,t\}:c_{G}(V(K_{r_{j}}))\geq 3\}

and
i_{1}\in\lambda(i_{2})
. Let

be a connected component of

such that
C\cap(K_{n_{i_{1}}}\cup K_{n_{i_{2}}})=\emptyset
. Since
c_{G}(V(K_{r_{i_{2}}}))\geq 3
, such a component

exists. Let

i_{3}=\min\{j\in[t]:K_{n_{j}}\cap C\neq\emptyset~\text{ and }~K_{n_{j}}\cap K_{r_{i_{2}}}\neq\emptyset\}.

Remark 5.5
.

Since
i_{1}\in\lambda(i_{2})
,
K_{n_{i_{1}}}\cap K_{n_{i_{2}}}=K_{r_{i_{2}}}
. From the choice of
i_{3}
,
K_{n_{i_{3}}}\cap K_{r_{i_{2}}}\neq\emptyset

and

The next proposition, along with

Proposition 5.6
.

Let

and

Let

and

Recall that

Recall that the chordal graph

Theorem 6.1
.

Cohen-Macaulay parity binomial edge ideals

In the previous section, we characterized unmixed parity binomial edge ideals of non-bipartite chordal graphs. We now characterize the Cohen-Macaulayness. Since any Cohen-Macaulay ideal is unmixed, we need to understand which are the Cohen-Macaulay ones among the graphs given in

Proposition 7.1
.

Let
G
be a graph on
[n]
having a pendant vertex

G^{\prime}

0pt(R/\mathcal{I}_{G^{\prime}})=0pt(R/\mathcal{I}_{G})-1

Proof.

Let
\{u,n+1\}
be the new edge in
G^{\prime}
and
g=x_{u}x_{n+1}-y_{u}y_{n+1}
. Then
\mathcal{I}_{G^{\prime}}=\mathcal{I}_{G}+(g)

u

G

u\notin T

g\notin\mathfrak{p}
, for every
\mathfrak{p}\in\text{Min}(\mathcal{I}_{G})
. Therefore
g
is regular on
\frac{R}{\mathcal{I}_{G}}
which yields
0pt(R/\mathcal{I}_{G^{\prime}})=0pt(R/\mathcal{I}_{G})-1
.
∎
