Poster 240. PASS or Fail: Predictors of Poor Outcome After Arthroscopic Posterior Labral Repair in a Multi-Surgeon Prospective Cohort Study
Authors: Jacob M Modest, Edward J Testa, Chao Zhang, Mark S Schickendantz, Michael J Scarcella
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjective:Posterior shoulder instability (PSI) is an increasingly recognized cause of shoulder dysfunction, representing 2-10% of instability cases. Arthroscopic posterior labral repair (aPLR) remains the primary surgical treatment, with generally favorable outcomes. Despite its increasing recognition, there is limited data on predictors of patient-reported outcomes measures (PROMs) following this procedure. The primary objective of this study was to identify preoperative, intraoperative, and demographic predictors of failing to achieve the Patient Acceptable Symptom State (PASS) after aPLR. Secondary objectives included identifying predictors of 1-year ASES and SANE scores and evaluating their correlation with PASS, as well as analysis of overall outcome data and patient improvement. We hypothesized that male sex, abnormal articular cartilage, presence of bone loss, lower mental health status, lower educational status, and a positive smoking status were predictors of poor PROMs following aPLR.Methods:A multi-surgeon multi-site prospective cohort study was conducted with 177 patients aged >=13 years (mean age 19 years, 86.4% male) who underwent aPLR between 2015 and 2023 across 9 surgical sites. Patients with isolated posterior or posterior-inferior labral tears were included. Demographic data, baseline mental and physical scores (VR-12 MCS/PCS), and intraoperative findings (cartilage status, capsule integrity, and glenoid bone loss) were collected. Prospectively collected responses to PASS, ASES, and SANE scores were analyzed to identify preoperative, intraoperative, and demographic factors associated with poor PROMs at 1 year postoperatively. Multivariable logistic regression and univariate linear regression identified predictors of poor outcomes.Results:A total of 133 patients (75.1%) completed one year outcome surveys. At 1 year, 81.2% of patients achieved PASS. Multivariable analysis identified abnormal articular cartilage status as a significant predictor of failing to achieve PASS (OR: 0.16, 95% CI: 0.04-0.63, p=0.010) and patients with normal articular cartilage status were more likely to achieve PASS (94.4% vs. 76.0%, p=0.010). PASS was significant correlated with other PROMs, with patients achieving PASS having significantly higher 1-year SANE (95 vs. 70, p<0.001) and ASES total (98.2 vs. 65.0, p<0.001) compared to PASS 'no' responders. Median SANE scores increased preoperatively 50 [40; 65] to 90 [80; 98] (p<0.001) with 84% achieving minimally clinically important difference (MCID) and 53.4% achieving substantial clinical benefit (SCB). Median ASES total increased from 65 [51.67; 80.0] to 95 [80.8;100.0] (p<0.001) with 76.7% achieving MCID and 32.6% achieving SCB. Lower VR-12 MCS (p=0.012) was also associated with failure to achieve PASS in the linear regression model. In linear regression models, lower BMI (p=0.038), nonwhite race (p=0.040), and lower VR-12 MCS (p=0.029) were associated with lower 1-year SANE scores. ASES outcomes were influenced by age, BMI, and baseline ASES scores. Capsular status, glenoid bone loss, smoking, and education level were not significant predictors of outcome.Conclusions:Abnormal articular cartilage status is the most significant predictors of failure to achieve PASS following aPLR. While most patients experienced significant improvement and high satisfaction, those with cartilage damage were over six times more likely to fail achieving an acceptable symptom state. PASS was significantly correlated with more commonly used ASES and SANE scores. aPLR results in favorable 1-year PROMs, with over 80% of patients achieving PASS and 91.6% achieve excellent or good ASES outcome. However, those who failed to achieve PASS had the same median ASES score as the baseline preoperative score, 65, illustrating that those who fail to achieve PASS didn't just not do well, but demonstrated a significant failure to meet any improvement with operative management. These data support the use of PASS as a reliable, simplified surrogate for other PROMs and highlights aPLR as an effective treatment for PSI in appropriately selected patients.
