Impact of repressing and thermomechanical fatigue on color stability and translucency of IPS Emax and Celtra press: an in vitro study
Authors: Sally N El Adawy, Dina M Aboulftouh, Shahenda T AbuAmu, Shaimaa A Alrafee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo evaluate the stain resistance of repressed IPS Emax and Celtra Press after thermo-mechanical fatigue. Two pressable glass ceramics, IPS e.max press (Ivoclar, Vivadent) and Celtra press (Dentsply, Sirona), were used in this study. Thirty discs (2 × 10 mm) were fabricated: (n = 15) e.max press (Group E) and Celtra press (Group C). The discs were fabricated using a repressing technique where remnant buttons from the initial press were reshaped as new ingots by removing all sprues. Each group was subdivided into three equal subgroups after thermo-mechanical fatigue according to the immersion solution: subgroup (AS): Artificial saliva (control immersion medium), subgroup(T): Tea solution and subgroup (C): Cola solution, (n = 5). Color and translucency measurements were recorded for all specimens. Data was collected and statistically analyzed using a two-way ANOVA test to study the interaction of study variables. Statistical analysis revealed that IPS e.max Press displayed significantly greater color deviation (ΔE) compared to Celtra Press in artificial saliva and tea groups, whereas no significant difference was observed between groups immersed in cola (p = 0.064).TP increased after staining, particularly in cola subgroups, with Celtra Press/cola showing the highest values. Two-way ANOVA revealed significant effects of material, staining medium, and their interaction on both ΔE and TP (p < 0.001). Both repressed IPS e.max Press and Celtra Press ceramics are susceptible to changes in color stability and translucency following exposure to common staining agents and thermomechanical aging.Keywords: Celtra press, Color stability, Lithium disilicate, Repressing, Staining media, Translucency parameter

Introduction

The utilization of dental ceramics has gained significant traction owing to their remarkable mechanical qualities, biocompatibility, and aesthetic appeal, which allow them to replicate the appearance of natural teeth. Owing to their superior mechanical properties and biocompatibility, dental ceramics are extensively utilized in various restorative applications. Examples of such restorations include crowns, partial coverage restorations, full arch bridges, three-unit bridges, and denture teeth (Amin and Alassar 2023). The clinical requirement for superior aesthetics and mechanical longevity has catalyzed the extensive adoption of all-ceramic systems. Among these, lithium disilicate ceramics (e.g., IPS e.max Press) and zirconia-reinforced lithium silicate ceramics (e.g., Celtra Press) are renowned for their favorable mechanical properties and translucency, making them suitable for anterior and posterior restorations.

One of the most popular methods for creating glass-ceramic dental restorations is heat pressing. Apart from its ease of use, the heat pressing method enhances the crystalline dispersion in the glass matrix, reduces porosity, and improves marginal accuracy when compared to alternative methods (Kotb Salem and Shalaby 2020) and also allows for the recycling of leftover materials through a process known as repressing, where the leftover ceramic buttons were reshaped as new ingots by removing all sprues, which is both cost-effective and environmentally friendly. However, concerns have been raised regarding the potential consequences of repressing on the mechanical and optical properties of these ceramics, including color stability and translucency.

Repressing of lithium disilicate ceramics has gained attention as a cost-saving and sustainable alternative in restorative dentistry, especially for maximizing material use in laboratory workflows. However, recent evidence indicates that repressing may compromise the mechanical integrity of ceramic restorations. Studies have shown that repeated pressing cycles can reduce flexural strength and increase surface roughness, which in turn may affect esthetic properties such as color stability and translucency (El-Etreby et al. 2021, Li et al. 2024).

Color stability is a censorious factor in longevity and esthetic success of ceramic restorations. Discoloration can result from various factors, including exposure to staining agents like coffee and tea, surface roughness, and the material’s inherent properties. Research has shown that repeated firing rounds and aging can affect the color and translucency of repressed lithium disilicate ceramics, with increased firing cycles leading to higher color differences (ΔE values) (Camacho et al. 2022).

Dental materials in the oral cavity are exhibited to a range of conditions, such as eating patterns, food and beverage coloring agents, oral hygiene, and mechanical, chemical, and thermal activities that might eventually alter the material’s qualities. The collision of aging on the mechanical properties of repressed ceramics was examined in several in vitro investigations (Elnaggar et al. 2021). Prior research has documented color changes and surface texture of CAD/CAM resin composites following immersion in carrot juice, curry, cigarette smoke, red wine, and energy drink which have different effects on ΔE (Reymus et al. 2021).

So, the purpose of the present study is to assess the influence of repressing on the color stability of pressable glass ceramics after thermomechanical fatigue to mimic serving on oral cavity and after immersing in different immersion solutions. The null hypothesis postulated that neither repressing nor thermomechanical fatigue would significantly affect the color stability of the tested ceramics.

Materials and methods

This invitro study was conducted at the lab of faculty of dentistry Al Azhar university girls branch-Cairo.

Sample size calculation

Based on Mohamed S, et al. (Mohamed et al. 2024) ∆E varied between 2.7 ± 0.31 and 2.17 ± 0.44. Using G Power statistical software (version 3.1.9.4) for sample size calculation, a total of 30 specimens (divided into two groups of 15 each, with 5 in each subgroup) will provide adequate power. This sample size can detect a large effect size (d = 1.39) with 80% statistical power (1-β) at a 5% significance level (α) for a two-tailed hypothesis test.

Study design and specimen preparation

Two industrially glass ceramic materials were used after two pressing cycles; Repressed IPS e.max press (Ivoclar, vivadent) and Repressed Celtra press (Dentsply, Sirona). A total of thirty discs, with dimensions 2 × 10 mm, were fabricated following the manufacturer’s recommendations, representing two groups (15 specimens each) according to the type of repressed glass ceramic material:

Group (E)

Repressed IPS e.max Press discs.

Group (C)

Repressed Celtra press discs.

The specimens were then put through a 6-month-long thermomechanical fatigue test utilizing a chewing simulator. Three subgroups were created from each group (5 specimens each), according to the immersion solutions:

Subgroup(A)

Artificial Saliva (Control immersion medium) (AS).

Subgroup (B)

Tea.

Subgroup(C)

Cola.

Color changes and translucency were evaluated before and after immersion of specimens in the immersing solutions for twelve days.

Discs fabrication

Fabrication of disc wax patterns A split custom-made Teflon mold with a diameter of 10 mm and a thickness of 2 mm was created so as to manufacture a standardized disc-shaped wax pattern.

Repressed ceramic discs fabrication

Following the initial pressing cycle, the leftover material of pressed IPS e.max Press or Celta Press were collected. To ensure the purity of the material, the outer reaction layer was meticulously removed using 50 μm aluminum oxide (Al2O3) sandblasting at a pressure of 2 bar until all investment traces were eliminated. The cleaned ceramic remnants were then ultrasonically cleaned in distilled water for 10 min and dried (Mohamed et al. 2024). An abrasive disc set on a straight-angle handpiece was used to separate the discs from the sprues and buttons. The buttons were retained for second cycle, while the sprues were thrown away. Using diamond discs and stones, the buttons were finished and reshaped to resemble a fresh ingot then subjected to a secondary heat-pressing cycle following the same manufacturer-recommended temperature and vacuum parameters as the initial press.

Following the manufacturer’s directions, all wax pattern discs (BEGO Bellavest, Germany) were sprued and invested. Before beginning the wax removal process, the investment was allowed to settle for one hour. As advised by the manufacturer, wax removal was carried out using a wax-burnout furnace (Ney, US Dental Depot, USA).

Using the buttons that resemble fresh ingots, the discs were pressed in accordance with the manufacturer’s specifications in an EP3000 press furnace (Ivoclar, Liechtenstein, Germany). The investment ring was taken out of the furnace as soon as the program was finished and allowed to chill on a wide-spaced grid under room conditions for around an hour, ensuring rapid and consistent cooling.

Following the cooling of the investment, the discs were made visible by rough divesting using polishing jet medium with polishing alumina beads (100 microns) for IPS e.max ingot at 4 bar (60 psi) pressure, following the manufacturer’s directions. Each disk was then finished and polished in accordance with the manufacturer’s directions. Figure (1: A-D). All of the investment and pressing steps for the IPS e.max discs were duplicated for Celtra Press, with the pressing parameters being adjusted following the manufacturer’s directions.

After divesting, the ceramic discs were finished using fine diamond burs (Komet, Germany) under constant water cooling to remove any surface irregularities while preventing thermal stress. All specimens were then cleaned in an ultrasonic bath with distilled water for 10 minutes to ensure a contaminant-free surface. For the glazing procedure, IPS e.max Ceram Glaze Paste (Ivoclar Vivadent) was applied in a thin, uniform layer to the Group E specimens and fired at a peak temperature of 770°C. For Group C, Celtra Universal Glaze (Dentsply Sirona) was applied and fired at 750°C using a calibrated vacuum furnace (e.g., Programat P3000, Ivoclar). The firing cycles followed the specific ‘Stain and Glaze’ programs recommended by each manufacturer, including a slow cooling phase to room temperature to maintain the structural integrity of the repressed ceramics (Rasha 2019).

Thermo-mechanical fatigue

The tested group discs underwent thermo-mechanical fatigue using a programmable logic-controlled chewing simulator (ROBOTA, AD-TECH Technology CO., Germany) combined with a thermocycling method driven by a servomotor. ROBOTA chewing simulator has four chambers, each chamber consists of an upper Jacob’s chuck holding a 6 mm diameter hardened steel stylus (antagonist) (Rasha 2019). To clinically recreate six months of chewing conditions, the simulator applied a sliding load 1 mm vertical and 3 mm horizontal movements with a weight of 5 kg, or 49 N of chewing power, which was applied in the center of the discs at a frequency of 1.6 Hz for 75,000 cycles (Steiner et al. 2009, Stewardson et al. 2010). Discs underwent 500 thermal cycles in water baths. Each cycle consisted of a 30 s / time at temperatures ranging from 5 to 55 °C (Ziębowicz et al. 2023). To be ready for baseline measurement, the finished and polished specimens were stored in dry, tight-sealed plastic containers.

Baseline color measurements

The color values for each Specimen were assessed using a reflective spectrophotometer (X-Rite, model RM200QC, Neu-Isenburg, Germany). These values were represented in the CIELAB (Commission International de l’Eclairage L*a*b*) color space, where color was documented through three parameters: a, b, and L. The parameter ‘a’ signifies greenness when negative and redness when positive.b: indicates a value of plus for yellowness, zero for neutrality, and minus for blueness. L: It characterizes the achromatic nature of color by quantifying its lightness; one hundred was denoted as white and zero was as black.

A white Teflon holder was used to center the specimens in the spectrophotometer’s measuring head. The attachment allowed for consistent measurements of every specimen from the same location and ensured no outside light could interfere with the system. After setting the aperture to 4 mm, the specimens were carefully positioned in alignment with the instrument.

Measurements were conducted using a white background based on the CIE L*a*b* color space, a standardized system established by the International Commission on Illumination (CIE). The CIE L*a*b* model is a near-uniform color space that defines color attributes through three coordinates: lightness (L), ranging from white to black, the red-green spectrum (a), and the yellow-blue spectrum (b). Translucency parameters

The specimens were assessed using a Reflective spectrophotometer (Model RM200QC, X-Rite, Neu-Isenburg, Germany), with the aperture set to 4 mm. Each specimen was precisely aligned with the device. Measurements were taken at the center of each specimen against both a white and a black backing, in accord with the CIE standard illuminant D65. The specimens were positioned centrally in the measuring port and maintained in the same orientation for both backings.

The translucency parameter (TP) was calculated by assessing the color variation of the specimens when placed against black and white backgrounds. The formula used was: TP= [(Lb* - Lw*)2 + (ab* - aw*)2 + (bb* - bw*)2 ]1/2, where “b” and “w” denote the color coordinates over a black background. In the L* scale, 0 corresponds to black, while 100 signifies a reference white (Gaafer et al. 2023).

This coordinate measures the material’s lightness (L) and darkness, with higher L values indicating a lighter specimen. The a* value indicates redness (positive) or greenness (negative), while the b* value represents yellowness (positive) or blueness (negative). Together, a* and b* define the color’s position on the red-green and yellow-blue chromatic scales (Stewardson et al. 2010). In this study, ∆L*, ∆a* and ∆b* represent the variations in the CIE color space parameters between two colors. Specifically, these values were calculated as the differences in L*, a* and b* measurements of the specimens when placed against black and white backgrounds.

After the baseline color and translucency measurements were obtained, the specimens were exposed to the following staining protocol.

Staining procedure

Preparation of immersion solutions

The 1st control immersing medium, the artificial saliva (AS) was prepared by dissolving the following ingredients to obtain Xanthan gum (0.92), KCl (1.2), NaCl (0.85), MgCl2 (0.05), NaH2PO4 (0.13), C8H8O3 (0.13), and CaCl2 (0.13) in 1 L of deionized water (Mohamed et al. 2024).

The 2nd immersing solution, the tea solution was prepared by doubling the concentration of a standard beverage to simulate a strong staining challenge. Twenty grams of tea (Yellow Tea, Lipton, Egypt) were immersed in 1 L of boiling distilled water. The solution was stirred every 5 min for 10 s until it cooled down to room temperature and then filtered through a filter paper.

The 3rd immersing solution, Cola, was 1 L of cola (Coca-Cola, Coca-Cola Co., Egypt) stored at room temperature. To standardize the exposure, the beverage was used immediately after opening to maintain its initial pH (approximately 2.5) and carbonation level. The solution was replaced daily to ensure a constant acidic challenge throughout the 12-day immersion period.

Immersion protocol

Specimens were immersed in labelled containers containing 20 ml of each immersion solution as in Fig. 2 and kept in the incubator (CBM.TORRE PICENARDI(CR), Model 431/V, Italy) at 37°c for (12 successive days) simulating 1 year of clinical service (Elraggal et al. 2023). Solutions were renewed every 24 h, and each disc was rinsed with running water before immersion in the new freshly prepared solution. Twice daily, the solutions were agitated throughout the whole experimental time (12 days). At the end of the immersion time, the discs were rinsed with running water then ultrasonically cleaned in distilled water and dried.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using a commercially available software program (SPSS 20-Statistical Package for Scientific Studies, SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) for Windows.

Data were analyzed by calculating the mean, standard deviation, confidence intervals, and range. To evaluate the data’s normality, the distribution was inspected, and both the Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Shapiro-Wilk tests were performed. Since the data followed a normal distribution, intergroup comparisons were conducted using an independent t-test. For subgroup analyses, a One-Way ANOVA was applied, with subsequent pairwise comparisons carried out using Bonferroni’s post hoc test.

The interaction effects between the variables were assessed using a two-way ANOVA test. Two-sided p-values were used, with statistical significance set at p ≤ 0.05.

Discussion

The present in vitro study was conducted to evaluate the influence of common immersing solutions, artificial saliva, tea, and cola on the colour stability(ΔE) and translucency parameter (TP) of IPS e.max Press and Celtra Press ceramics following a second pressing cycle and thermomechanical aging. Evaluating the long-term performance of reprocessed materials in environments that closely resemble the oral environment is becoming essential as the clinical demand for aesthetic restorations keeps growing.

The methodology implemented was designed to closely replicate clinical aging conditions, including 75,000 chewing cycles and thermocycling, followed by 12 days of soaking in immersing solution. This approach aligns with previously established protocols in the literature (Hassan et al. 2024). Tea has been the most frequently used immersion solution in color studies, followed by cola (Camacho et al. 2022, Dawood and El-Farag 2021). The evaluation of color and translucency was performed using standardised CIE Lab* coordinates, ensuring objective comparison of the ceramic materials before and after immersion.

Based on the results of this study, the null hypothesis stating that there would be no change in the color and translucency of repressed IPS e.max Press and Celtra Press ceramics following thermomechanical fatigue and immersion in immersion media was rejected. Significant differences in color stability (∆E) and the translucency parameter (TP) were observed between materials and among immersion subgroups. These findings indicate that both the ceramic type and the surrounding conditions play a crucial role in maintaining the aesthetic stability of the repressed ceramics, particularly following thermomechanical aging.

The results of the present study demonstrated that the IPS e.max Press exhibited significantly greater color change values compared to Celtra Press in both artificial saliva and tea subgroups. This difference can be primarily attributed to the variation in material composition, where the lithium disilicate glass matrix of IPS e.max Press is more susceptible to hydrolytic degradation and staining compared to zirconia reinforced structure of Celtra Press.

This finding is consistent with the results of Makkeyah et al. (Makkeyah et al. 2025), who explained these changes as a consequence of silicate glass matrix degradation in acidic conditions. Similarly, Elzoughary et al. (Elzoughary et al. 2024) demonstrated that hydrothermal aging resulted in significant color changes in lithium disilicate ceramics. The observed (ΔE) values in artificial saliva can be attributed to hydrolytic degradation and surface fatigue induced by thermomechanical aging, which increase susceptibility of ceramics to discoloration (Hamam et al. 2023).

Despite standardized cleaning and repressing procedures, IPS e.max Press group exhibited significant optical changes. This suggests that the observed degradation is related to intrinsic material alterations induced by repeated thermal exposure during the repressing cycle rather than external contamination during the reshaping process.

Material Composition, specifically zirconia reinforcement, appears to be a critical factor affecting the observed outcomes. Zirconia reinforced glass ceramics have been reported to exhibit resistance to acidic degradation and altered color stability compared with conventional lithium disilicate glass ceramics due to the chemical stability and resistance to degradation of zirconia (Makkeyah et al. 2025). This may explain the superior color stability of Celtra Press observed in artificial saliva and tea immersion.

Celtra Press, on the other hand, showed the highest color difference in the cola subgroup. This is likely due to the low PH and erosive nature of phosphoric acid in cola, which can deteriorate the ceramic surface and increase pigment retention. These findings are in accordance with other studies (Binici Aygün et al. 2025, Omara et al. 2024), which confirmed the possibility of glass matrix to acidic medium and its impact on colour stability, suggesting that zirconia reinforcement doesn’t make the material immune to the aggressive erosive effects of highly acidic solutions like cola.

Additionally, the effect of surface condition on stainability has been well demonstrated. Lorenzetti et al. (Lorenzetti et al. 2024) demonstrated that different polishing protocols significantly affect the surface roughness and, consequently, the color stability of both conventional and CAD/CAM restorative materials. Although this study didn’t specifically assess polishing effects, such evidence highlights the significance of surface finishing in improving color resistance.

Recent evidence supports that the acidic beverages significantly affect the surface integrity of lithium disilicate ceramics, leading to increased surface roughness and altered bonding characteristics. In a recent study, Futian et al. (Futian et al. 2024) demonstrated that Coca-Cola exposure produced the highest surface roughness values among the tested erosive agents. This surface degradation is primarily related to the chemical dissolution of the ceramic glass matrix, which disrupts the microstructure and increases susceptibility to mechanical wear and discolouration.

These findings are consistent with the results of the present study, where immersion in cola and tea led to significant changes in both color stability (ΔE) and translucency (TP), highlighting the adverse effects of low pH media on the aesthetic and structural properties of ceramic restorations.

Furthermore, it was reported by El-Farag (Dawood and El-Farag 2021) that surface finishing plays a key role in the color stability of all ceramic restorations, with glazed surfaces showing less discolouration and surface roughness than polished ones after immersion in tea and cola. These findings are relevant to the current study, as immersion media, particularly cola and tea, were found to significantly affect both (ΔE) and (TP) values. Although surface finishing was not assessed in this investigation, their results underline the influence of surface texture on stain resistance and support the use of glazing to improve the aesthetic durability of repressed ceramics.

In agreement with the present findings, Hassan et al. (Hassan et al. 2024) reported that the type of ceramic and the immersion medium significantly influence both color stability and the translucency parameters, regardless of the percentage of repressing. These results align with our observations, where acidic and pigmented media such as tea and cola induced significant changes in both (ΔE) and (TP) across both materials. These results emphasize the importance of choosing the right ceramic end and indicate that while repressing may be viable within certain limits, exposure to dietary immersion agents remains a critical factor in the long-term aesthetic performance of ceramic restorations.

Regarding the repressing and thermocycling fatigue of lithium disilicate ceramics, a previous study (Saber et al. 2023) concluded that repeated pressing did not significantly affect the color stability (ΔE) or translucency (TP) of IPS e.max Press crowns. These findings support that repressing, even when combined with simulated clinical ageing can preserve acceptable optical properties. The inconsistency between the two studies may be due to differences in methodology particularly in the specimen design, material thickness, and immersion in pigmented or acidic solutions which may have caused the observed optical changes.

Optical properties of glass ceramics are highly influenced by material thickness (Makkeyah et al. 2025) and surface conditions after acidic exposure (Omara et al. 2024). In the current study, standardised disc-shaped specimens with uniform thickness were used, allowing precise assessment of color stability and translucency changes. In contrast, other studies (Anazi and Sultan 2023, Saber et al. 2023) evaluating full contour crowns have demonstrated different optical behaviour.

Additionally, the number of pressing cycles affects ceramic microstructure and optical properties (Elnaggar et al. 2021). The present investigation focused on a single repressing cycle followed by thermomechanical fatigue and immersion in acidic and pigmented solutions, while Saber et al. (Saber et al. 2023) assessed the effect of repeated heat pressing without exposure to immersion media.

All tested subgroups showed an increase in TP values following immersion in terms of translucency, with the Celtra Press specimens exposed to cola showing the biggest change. This increase is probably caused by increased porosity and surface etching brought on by acid, which improves light scattering. These findings are in line with earlier findings from other research (Elzoughary et al. 2024, Bal and Öztürk 2025), which showed that different agent procedures, such as hydrothermal cycling and UV exposure, have an impact on the optical characteristics of glass ceramics.

Remarkably, following immersion, the Cola subgroup in both material groups displayed an unanticipated rise in (TP) values. This unexpected outcome is consistent with the research by Lee et al., who found that ceramic specimens immersed in Cola had higher (TP) values than those exposed to tea or coffee. The authors attribute this phenomenon to the acidic erosion brought on by Cola, which may have increased microstructural porosity and surface roughness, changing the material’s ability to transmit light and leading to increased translucency (Lee et al. 2018).

Simultaneously, Hamam et al. (Hamam et al. 2023) noted notable alterations in both and (TP) in resin composites exposed to various aging procedures, highlighting how sensitive aesthetic dental materials are to the environmental factors. According to Lorenzetti et al. (Lorenzetti et al. 2024), surface finishing techniques may also be able to modify the impact of immersion and surface degradation on (TP) values, which emphasises the significance of surface finishing procedures.

Clinical implications

Clinically, (ΔE) values above the perceptibility threshold (3.3) are regarded as noticeable and aesthetically unacceptable. This limit was exceeded by most of the tested groups in this study, suggesting that the second pressing of ceramics, particularly IPS e.max Press, may show reduced color stability after being exposed to common dietary immersion agents.

Although Celtra Press demonstrated better color stability, it also showed more (TP) alterations, especially in acidic environments. These results highlight the importance of carefully choosing materials according to the patient’s dietary preferences and the location of the restoration within the dental arch. Furthermore, applying suitable surface treatment like glazing or polishing could help to improve long-term performance.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of this in vitro study, it can be concluded that both repressed IPS e.max Press and Celtra Press ceramics are susceptible to changes in color stability and translucency following exposure to common immersion agents and thermomechanical aging.

These results imply that:

The aesthetic performance of repressed ceramics is influenced by the combination of material composition, immersion medium and aging conditions.

Limitations and future directions

This in vitro study, while controlling variables, does not replicate intraoral conditions such as dynamic salivary flow and biofilm formation. Limitations include a small sample size and the lack of surface characterization techniques, such as scanning electron microscopy, limiting the ability to correlate microstructural alterations with optical changes. Future research should focus on longer immersion durations, surface analysis, dynamic aging, and multiple repressing cycles to better understand material behaviour. While CIELAB was used for color difference calculation to maintain consistency with previous literature, future studies should consider employing the CIEDE2000 formula to provide a more refined correlation with human visual perception thresholds.”
