Mapping definitions and measures of police violence in health literature: a scoping review
Authors: Latesha K. Harris, Jamie L. Conklin, Valentina Marginean, Cheryl Woods Giscombé, Yamnia I. Cortés
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPolice violence continues to be a significant public health problem, especially in the United States. A limited but growing body of research has explored the relationship between police violence and health outcomes. Emerging evidence suggests that exposure to police violence may be a contributing factor to health inequities, particularly among Black Americans. Yet, there remains a lack of consensus on a conceptual and operational definition of police violence within health disciplines. This scoping review aims to address this gap by mapping the definitions and measures for assessing police violence in health literature.MethodsWe comprehensively searched PubMed, Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL), and APA PsycInfo databases. We included studies published as peer-reviewed primary research in health journals and in the English language. Eligible studies measured police violence.ResultsOf the 1692 references identified, 204 references were retained for full-text review, and 114 references reporting on 98 studies met the inclusion criteria. We identified over 100 terms for police violence, which we clustered into 22 categories including abuse, assault, harassment, killing, and violence. The most common terms were “police violence,” “police abuse,” and “police brutality.” Only two-thirds of studies (n = 76) included a definition of police violence. Police violence was most frequently defined as “police killings” (n = 23) and excessive use-of-force (n = 10). Self-reports of police violence were the most common measure (n = 48). Other measures included counts of fatal police violence from open-source and governmental databases, as well as counts of deaths/injuries classified as “legal intervention” in medical records.ConclusionsThe health literature lacks a clear and consistent definition of police violence. While multiple measures show some potential for surveillance of police violence, few studies have conducted in-depth psychometric analyses. Recommendations for future research include developing an explicit definition of police violence and reporting psychometric data for measures. Additionally, there is a need for systematic data collection of police violence in health settings and the establishment of a federally supported and accessible public database of police violence to address underreporting and undermeasurement of police violence.

Background

Police violence, a significant public health issue in the United States (U.S.) [1–3], remains challenging to define and quantify [4]. Ambiguities about what constitutes police violence, particularly whether it refers only to excessive police use of force or also to other harmful or discriminatory policing practices, contribute to divergent definitions, measurement challenges, and inconsistent prevalence estimates in the U.S. Although the U.S. makes up only 4% of the world’s population, it accounts for nearly 13% of global fatal police-related deaths [5]. While police violence is inconsistently measured and underreported [4], available data demonstrate a pronounced racial difference in the risk of death after a police encounter. For example, the risk of death after a police encounter in the U.S. is 2.5 times higher for African American/Black men compared to White men [6] and 1.4 times higher for African American/Black women compared to White women [7]. Public health and human rights concerns regarding police violence have been intensified by the police killings of George Floyd, Breonna Taylor, Ta’Kiya Young, Tyre Nichols, Michael Brown, Philando Castille, and many others [8–11]. Similar concerns have been documented globally including high-profile cases in Brazil, Nigeria, France, and South Africa [12–15].

The American Public Health Association has identified police violence as a public health priority [16, 17]. However, there is little consensus on measuring or defining police violence. In fact, few studies even include a definition of police violence and use various methods to measure it [18–22]. The World Health Organization (WHO) broadly defines violence as the “intentional use of physical force or power, threatened or actual, against oneself, another person, or against a group or community, that either results in or has a high likelihood of resulting in injury, death, psychological harm, maldevelopment, or deprivation” [21]. While the WHO definition provides a broad framework encompassing both the use of physical force and the exercise of power that results in psychological harm, we recognize that not all police encounters involving authority constitute “violence.” In this scoping review, we focus specifically on encounters that constitute police misconduct (e.g., racial profiling, unlawful searches, false arrest), or coercive harmful uses of authority that may legally be permissible but are experienced as threatening or discriminatory (e.g., excessive traffic stops, frisking), whether physical violence was present or not. This approach may capture a wider spectrum of experiences than studies using narrower, force-only definitions. This is consistent with the purpose of scoping reviews, which is to map heterogeneity rather than adjudicate a single operational definition [23, 24].

A limited but growing body of research has examined the link between police interactions and health outcomes. Two recent systematic literature reviews [25, 26] have found that police exposure (e.g., indirect experiences with police such as witnessing or hearing stories of police) and police interactions (e.g., direct contact with police officers) may negatively impact the mental health and health behaviors of African Americans. Findings suggest that negative police encounters may be associated with suicidal ideation, psychological distress, and post-traumatic stress disorder [27–29]. Police brutality has also been linked to non-fatal injuries, death, stress, and financial strain [30]. Beyond physical and psychological harm, recent evidence suggests that negative encounters with the police may be associated with a greater medical mistrust, and in turn higher reported unmet health needs [31]. These findings suggest that police violence functions as a social determinant of health, influencing access to care and health behaviors. These findings highlight the importance of further exploring how police violence may affect health outcomes. Although there is a pressing need for deeper exploration of police violence, a standardized definition of the term is lacking. Additionally, current health literature does not present a standardized approach for measuring police violence.

Several academic disciplines such as sociology [32], criminology [33–35], and more recently, public health, have proposed definitions and frameworks for understanding police violence [32, 36, 37]. However, little is known about how police violence has been defined and measured within the health literature itself. Unlike conceptual or theoretical papers, the present scoping review does not aim to evaluate existing frameworks or propose a new one. Instead, its purpose is to identify and map the definitions and measurement approaches in health research to inform future studies examining associations between police violence and health. Although a unified conceptual definition of police violence may ultimately support more consistent measurement, developing such a definition lies outside the scope of this review. To our knowledge, no published synthesis has examined how police violence is operationalized in health studies. Therefore, the objective of this scoping review is to describe how police violence has been defined and measured in health literature.

Methods

This scoping review was conducted following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extensions for Scoping Reviews [38]. Scoping methodology was best suited for this review because it allows researchers to describe the breadth, range, and nature of existing evidence rather than evaluating or developing conceptual frameworks. Aligned with this purpose, our review aims to identify and map the wide range of terms used to describe police violence (e.g., police brutality, excessive use of force, fatal police encounters), and to catalogue the diverse methods used to measure police violence within the health literature. This descriptive approach is consistent with established scoping review guidance, which focuses on summarizing existing evidence [23, 24]. The study protocol has been previously published elsewhere [39].

Search strategy and study selection

A health sciences librarian (JLC) was consulted to create a search strategy [39]. A systemic search was conducted using three health sciences databases: PubMed, Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL) Plus with Full Text (EBSCOhost), and APA PsycInfo (EBSCOhost). Databases were searched from their dates of inception through the last search date of January 3, 2023. The following is the full search used in PubMed: "police violence"[tiab] OR "police brutality"[tiab] OR "police use of force"[tiab] OR "law enforcement violence"[tiab] OR "law enforcement brutality"[tiab] OR "law enforcement use of force"[tiab] OR “police abuse”[tiab] OR “law enforcement abuse”[tiab] OR (("legal intervention"[tiab] OR "legal interventions"[tiab] OR “excessive force”[tiab: ~ 5]) AND (police[tiab] OR "law enforcement"[tiab])). A hand search of the reference lists of included articles was conducted to retrieve any additional pertinent references missed by the electronic search. All references were imported into EndNote to remove duplicates. The remaining references were uploaded into the Covidence systematic review software (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia) for screening and data charting.

Studies were included in the scoping review if they 1) were published in English, 2) were published as primary research and peer-reviewed, 3) were published in health-related journals, and 4) measured police violence. Studies that measured police violence were included whether or not they had a definition of police violence. Qualitative studies that identified police violence as a key theme were included in the review. Studies that evaluated predictors or perceptions of police violence, violence against police officers, or violence in communities but did not specifically define or measure police violence were excluded. Since the focus of the review was how police violence was defined and measured in original research articles, we excluded commentaries and literature reviews.

Following a pilot screening of a subset (n = 30) of references, the titles and abstracts of potentially relevant studies were screened by three researchers (LKH, JLC, YIC) using the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Each title and abstract were independently screened by two of the reviewers for inclusion. Two independent reviewers further reviewed the full-text (LKH, JLC), and those that did not meet the inclusion criteria were excluded (reasons provided in Fig. 1). Disagreements between reviewers were resolved through discussion or with consensus from a third reviewer. Covidence provided the Cohen’s Kappa coefficient for full text review, which was 0.68, indicating substantial agreement (0.61 −0.80) between reviewers [40].Fig. 1Preferred reporting items for scoping reviews (PRISMA ScR) flow diagram of literature screening and inclusion

Charting

Two independent reviewers used a pre-defined charting form to extract relevant data from the included studies [41]. The extracted data included year of publication, study design, study location, population, setting, key terms and concepts of police violence, measures of police violence, and type of health literature or academic discipline where the study was published. Any disagreements between the reviewers on data extraction were resolved through discussion or with a third reviewer. The selected studies were then organized according to the desired outcomes – (1) language encapsulating police violence, (‘definitions’), and (2) measures of police violence.

Results

Figure 1 provides an overview of the selection process for the scoping review. The literature search yielded 1316 references after duplicates were removed. During the title and abstract screening, 1112 references were excluded. Of the 204 identified references, 36 were excluded for not measuring police violence. An additional 20 were excluded because they focused on violence against police officers, general violence non-specific to police, and use of force that was not excessive. A total of 27 commentaries, literature reviews, and editorials were excluded. Next, three references were not available in English and four were retracted. Thus, we included 114 references reporting on 98 unique studies in the review.

While reviews typically combine multiple reports (such as two articles reporting findings on a single study), we have opted to provide report-level findings for publication dates, health focus areas, and police violence terms. This allowed us to provide a more complete analysis as reports provided different police violence terms and were published in different health focus areas. Although the health focus area and terminology for police violence vary across reports, the measurement approach remains consistent because it was defined at the study level. Therefore, we present study-level findings for police violence measurement. In the following results, we clarified the unit of analysis by using the terms reports and studies.

Datasets

Forty-five of the 98 included studies used datasets to measure police violence. Two such datasets are Mapping Police Violence [46] and The Counted [47], which use crowdsourced materials from regional news outlets, research groups, and other open-source reporting projects to collect information on police-related fatal encounters. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) maintains the National Violent Death Report System (NVDRS), a comprehensive governmental database of all deaths in participating states that result from homicide, suicide, legal intervention (excluding legal execution), unintentional shooting, and injuries of undetermined intent [48]. NVDRS uses information obtained from death certificates, medical examiner/coroner reports, and law enforcement reports where the abstractors at the state level code the cause of death as a "legal intervention." Only one study used the Gun Violence Archive [49] (GVA), a near-real-time online archive of firearm violence incidents (both fatal and nonfatal) in all 50 states and the District of Columbia. Of the publicly available data sets used, The Washington Post had the most restrictive case definition, "only including shootings in which a police officer, in the line of duty, fatally shoots a civilian. It does not track deaths of people in police custody, fatal shootings by off-duty officers, or non-firearm deaths" [50]. Some studies combined multiple datasets (n = 7). One such study [51] cross-linked individual-level mortality data from the 2015 NVDRS and five other open-source datasets: Fatal Encounters, Mapping Police Violence, The Guardian's "The Counted," Gun Violence Archive, and The Washington's Post's "Fatal Force Database.”

Self-report

Forty-eight studies measured police violence by self-report (Table 1). In quantitative studies, common items included “have you ever had negative encounters with the police?”; and “has a police officer slapped, punched, shoved, kicked, shaken or otherwise physically hurt you?” One study [52] used a modified version of the Classes of Racism Frequency of Racial Experiences Measure (M-CRFRE) [53] to assess perceived racism-based police violence across three domains (victim, witness in person, seen in media). Qualitative interview guides assessed experiences or perceptions of police violence among at-risk and historically marginalized populations such as people who inject drugs, people living with mental illnesses, African American and Black men, women and children, and the intersectionality of race, gender, or sexual identity with police violence experiences. Participants described their experiences in detail as " ‘we were hanging out on the street when police approached us swearing. They hit me, threw me against the ground, put a gun in my face, threw me against the wall…’” [54]. Another participant described their experience as “‘I've been propositioned by police, I've traded my freedom and drugs from police for sex.’” [55].

Three studies in this review used the Police Practices Inventory (PPI) [28, 56, 57]. The PPI is a 27-item measure developed based on the four WHO domains of violence (i.e., physical, sexual, psychological, and neglect) [48]. The PPI consists of 5 dichotomous items: 1) “has a police officer ever used a gun, baton, taser, or other weapon on you?”; 2) “has a police officer ever hit, punched, kicked, dragged, beat, or otherwise used physical force against you?”; 3) “has a police officer ever engaged in nonphysical aggression toward you, including threatening, intimidating, stopping you without probable cause, or using slurs?”; 4) “has a police officer ever forced inappropriate sexual contact on you, including while conducting a body search in a public place?”; and 5) "have you ever called or summoned the police for assistance and the police either did not respond, responded too late, or responded inappropriately?” Respondents were then asked to report how many times in the past 12 months they were exposed to each experience. Notably, the PPI was the only self-report measure identified in this scoping review with psychometric data. The PPI was found to have moderate to strong test–retest reliability for each WHO domain of police violence: physical (K = 0.58), sexual (K = 0.40), psychological (K = 0.60), and neglectful victimization (K = 0.64) [58].

Medical records and administrative records

Six studies in this review used electronic medical records, death certificates, and medical examiner/coroner reports to assess police violence with International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes [42, 59–63]. Two studies evaluated injuries in the emergency department as a result of legal interventions [62, 63]. ICD-9 and ICD-10 codes (i.e., Y35.0-Y35.9) were used to identify any injury sustained because of an encounter with any law enforcement official, serving in any capacity at the time of the encounter. Death certificates and medical examiner reports were reviewed to monitor deaths while in police custody. Four studies reviewed complaints of excessive force by police documented in police department records, emergency room documentation [64–67], and one study used the incidence reports from the California Ursus Program Dataset [68].

Discussion

This scoping review aimed to map existing definitions and measurement approaches to police violence in the health literature. Police violence has received relatively new attention in public health and health services research, as evidenced by the growing number of publications identified in this review after 2020 (Fig. 2). This coincides with the murder of George Floyd by Minneapolis police officer Derek Chauvin and the COVID-19 pandemic which highlighted social inequities and health disparities [69–71]. In the health literature, most of this work is being conducted in public health, epidemiology, and social medicine. While emerging research suggests that police violence is associated with premature death [72], increased cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk [60], depression, anxiety [73], and posttraumatic stress disorder [74], there is a lack of clarity on how to define and measure the phenomenon. We identified more than 100 terms for police violence, varying from "police harassment” to “police assault,” and “death by legal intervention.” One inconsistency in the definitions noted in this review was whether physical violence (fatal, non-fatal, sexual, or non-sexual) was the defining characteristic of police violence. While few studies, considered any act by police that dehumanizes people (e.g., intimidation, neglect, verbal abuse) a form of police violence [22], other studies [43, 75, 76] focused on deaths during interactions with the police. In addition, several studies [52, 77–79] included the intention of the police in their definition of police violence. However, Alang et al. (2021), and Smith (2019), do not include police intent when determining if the use of force was excessive. These discrepancies make it difficult to rigorously assess the impact of police violence. The WHO definition of violence provides a useful reference point because it incorporates multiple domains of harm (e.g., physical, psychological, sexual, and neglectful) that are relevant to understanding police encounters. Not accounting for multiple domains of harm can lead to the underreporting of non-physical forms of police violence. Narrow conceptualizations may overlook documented structural and discriminatory policing practices such as racial profiling that disproportionately affect minority communities and can produce psychological harm.

Furthermore, available data suggest that current measures do not fully capture the pervasiveness of police violence. Open-source, governmental, and publicly available datasets are a common measure used by researchers to study the risk factors of police violence [33, 80, 81], ways to reduce incidents of police violence [45, 54], and the prevalence of police violence [34, 35, 82, 83]. However, a recent study found that more than half of police-related deaths between 1980 and 2019 were misclassified or underreported in the US National Vital Statistics System (NVSS) [84], and evidence suggests that more than half of police violence incidents were not counted using combined non-governmental and open-source databases [85]. Since definitions of police violence may differ across databases, pertinent interactions and subsequent documentation may vary. This review highlights that these databases are inconsistently applied in the health literature, which may perpetuate inaccurate estimates. While nine studies used Mapping Police Violence and six studies used The Counted, others combined multiple sources, resulting in varying estimates of police violence.

This review found that self-reporting is a common method for measuring non-fatal police violence within the health literature. This approach was widely utilized both in the U.S. and internationally [86–89]. Self-report was common across all research designs, but the questions used to elicit the information varied widely. Single-item measures, such as “Have you ever been beaten by police?”, may not fully reflect experiences of police violence. Multiple-item scales assess police violence more broadly, possibly including the varying domains of violence. To limit the inconsistency of measures in the literature, several novel and validated self-report measures have been developed including the PPI [48]. Consistent definitions and standardized measures are essential to improve comparability across studies and advance understanding of health impacts.

While our study did not look at perceptions of police violence, future studies should examine how anticipatory stress related to police violence affects health, particularly among disadvantaged and vulnerable populations. Anticipatory stress related to police violence has been associated with psychological distress and suicide attempts among adults aged 18 and older from diverse U.S. community samples [28, 29] and with depressed mood among women [90]. Police involved in use-of-force incidents also face significant health risks, including acute stress responses and adrenaline dumps that lead to sleep disruption and fatigue [91]. Repeated exposure to high-intensity encounters increases vulnerability to PTSD and physical injury, with these cumulative health challenges extending beyond individual well-being to affect organizational stability through decreased morale and lower retention rates among officers [92, 93]. Similar to efforts to generate consistent measures of racism in the health literature [94], there is a need for shared understanding and language to advance equity and justice.

The definitions and measures described in this review highlight important concerns and challenges for the state of investigating police violence and its health impacts. The absence of consistent terminology, a reliable operationalization, and an explicitly stated definition of police violence that captures the various forms of police violence underpins the fragmentation in measuring this detrimental phenomenon. It is important to note that this issue has been raised previously by multiple reviews concerning the state of police violence exposure measurement [7, 34, 35, 51]. However, this is the first review to focus on terms, definitions, and measures in the health literature.

Limitations and strengths

There are several limitations and strengths to the current scoping review. We used a scoping review methodology and considered broad definitions of police violence, which provides a comprehensive mapping of the literature but also introduces limitations. Broader conceptualizations may reduce comparability across studies that use narrower, force-only definitions. We limited our search to English language publications and only included journal articles within our chosen health databases to align with our research aims, which may exclude additional reports consisting of a term and/or measure of police violence. Applying WHO’s definition to police violence requires careful consideration of how ‘power’ is exercised beyond physical force. While this inclusive approach captures structural and psychological harms, it also raises challenges for distinguishing misconduct from routine policing. Future research should aim to develop operational definitions that balance conceptual breadth with practical clarity, ideally through cross-disciplinary consensus. Since this is a scoping review, we did not include risk of bias or assess methodological quality of the evidence. A limitation in the databases we searched is the lack of a controlled vocabulary for police violence; for example, there is no Medical Subject Heading (MeSH) for police violence.

Although police violence is ill-defined in the health literature, a key strength of this review is our comprehensive search strategy to ensure a broad capture of both MeSH and non-MeSH reports [39]. An additional strength of this review is the use of a scoping review methodology, which allows for a comprehensive mapping of existing literature and accommodates diverse study designs and sources. Finally, this is the first scoping review to describe and synthesize definitions and measures of police violence in the health literature, which is necessary to identify gaps in the literature and opportunities for future areas of scholarship.

Implications for practice, policy, and research

Practice

By incorporating terms and measures for police violence, healthcare providers can better identify this social determinant of health during assessments, document it consistently, and connect patients to appropriate support and resources [95]. This review found that medical records offer an innovative approach to measuring police use of force, but they often underrepresent the full spectrum of police-related injuries (especially less severe or fatal cases), and lack contextual details, as noted by prior research [96]. Our scoping review highlights inconsistent use of terms related to police violence in the literature, which contributes to these gaps. One way to address this is by training health care professionals to screen for a history of police-related trauma (e.g., abrasions from too tight handcuffs, physical injuries from excessive use of force, inappropriate sexual contact during police encounters) during clinical visits. Clinicians must assess, document, and report police related injuries and trauma to better identify patients’ health and social needs [97]. A systematic approach to data collection of police violence in healthcare settings is needed.

Policy

Police violence is a complex issue influenced by various factors, including policy, environmental conditions, and systemic elements [3, 98, 99]. Our scoping review highlights how standardized language is necessary for comprehensive data collection, which in turn can influence policy aimed to mitigate excessive use of force, defined as more force than warranted in a particular situation [100] leading to fatal and nonfatal police violence. Our review found wide variation in terminology and measurement tools across studies, which limits comparability and obscures the true prevalence of fatal and nonfatal police violence. Addressing these gaps through shared definitions and validated measures can strengthen accountability frameworks and inform legislation such as the George Floyd Justice in Policing Act of 2021 [101], which seeks to improve data transparency, restrict qualified immunity, and establish national use-of-force standards. Although this bill faces significant legislative challenges, several states—including New York, Colorado, New Mexico, New Jersey, California, and Arizona—have amended qualified immunity [102–105]. Future research should examine whether improved definitional clarity and standardized measurement contribute to more effective policy reforms and reductions in police violence.

Research

Individuals and communities who suffer the most (e.g., Black Americans, LGBTQ +, people living with mental illnesses) from the multifaceted impacts of police violence are underrepresented in research, and similar patterns are observed among marginalized groups globally [106]. Although disparities in police violence are well-documented, only a handful of studies have examined the meaning and lived experiences of police violence among these communities [107–111]. One such study included in this review is the U.S Transgender Survey, the largest survey examining the experiences of transgender people in the U.S., which reported high levels of mistreatment and harassment by the police [112]. Beyond the U.S., research such as Miles-Johnson’s study in Australia demonstrates that transgender individuals globally experience police mistreatment and harassment, reinforcing the need for cross-national strategies to address these systemic issues [113]. As noted in Fig. 3, our review suggests there is a growing body of research examining the associations between police use of violence and mental health outcomes, yet studies on police use of violence among people living with mental illness is lacking. In addition, there is scant literature exploring the mechanism by which police violence impacts health and contributes to health inequities. Further examination of systems of power, social and built environments, individual responses to police violence, and physiological pathways linking police violence to health outcomes are needed. Notably, this review identified several public databases and validated questionnaires that can be used in future studies.

Conclusions

The present review sought to map the definitions and measures of police violence in health literature. There is an increasing demand for research that investigates the impact of police violence on a range of health outcomes. However, a limited number of studies have explored how to conceptualize and operationalize this complex phenomenon. Our study makes a significant contribution to the existing literature on police violence and health by clarifying definitions and measures of police violence while also identifying critical gaps in current research. In this review, we identified three primary measures of police violence: self-reports, incident counts from existing databases, and medical records. To advance the understanding of police violence exposure, it is crucial for researchers to consistently refine their measurement approaches. This can be achieved by carefully operationalizing terms, utilizing psychometrically validated measures, and employing methodologies that shed light on the impact of police violence on health, especially among historically marginalized communities. Given that these communities face compounded effects from police violence and additional stressors related to systemic discrimination, it is vital to investigate how these factors influence health outcomes.
