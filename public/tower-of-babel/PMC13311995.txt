Syntrophic interactions driving anaerobic methane oxidation in deep-sea cold seeps revealed by DNA-SIP
Authors: Qiuyun Jiang, Hao Liu, Hongmei Jing, Yue Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryAnaerobic oxidation of methane is a key process reducing methane emissions in oxygen-depleted marine environments such as deep-sea cold seeps. However, the stability and adaptability of microbial interactions driving this process remain poorly understood. Here, we combined stable isotope probing, tracer incubations, and bacterial inhibition experiments to investigate methane-oxidizing communities in sediments from the South China Sea. We found that methane oxidation was sustained by interactions between distinct archaeal and bacterial groups under different electron acceptor conditions. Inhibition experiments revealed tightly coupled microbial partnerships, while community shifts toward alternative metabolic pathways maintained overall methane oxidation rates. These results demonstrate functional redundancy and metabolic flexibility within microbial consortia. Our findings highlighted how dynamic environmental conditions supported multiple overlapping pathways, ensuring stable methane consumption. This work provided insights into the resilience of biogeochemical processes and informs future strategies for methane mitigation.

Introduction

Cold seeps, globally situated along continental margins, are formed by the expulsion of the deep subsurface into the sediment-seawater interface, and are often rich in hydrogen sulfide, methane, and other hydrocarbons.1,2,3,4 These unique environments are distinguished by their chemosynthetic microorganisms that utilize hydrocarbons—such as methane—as sources of carbon and energy to support a diverse array of microorganisms.3,5,6 Anaerobic oxidation of methane (AOM), which often occurs in conjunction with sulfate reduction of the deep-sea cold seeps, is an important microbial process of methane cycling to control the emission of methane from anoxic sediments, and plays an important role in regulating methane content in the ocean.7,8 AOM processes coupled to iron, manganese,9 sulfate,10 nitrite, and nitrate11 reduction performed by anaerobic methanotrophic (ANME) archaea (ANME-1, ANME-2a/b/c, ANME-2d, and ANME-3), sulfate-reducing bacteria (SRB), and the bacterium Ca. Methylomirabilis (NC10) has been demonstrated in marine methane seeps and hydrate layers.

Sulfate-dependent anaerobic methane oxidation process (SAMO) was the most studied AOM process in the deep-sea methane seeps by far,12,13 and was performed by a consortium of ANME-1/2/3 and their syntrophic SRBl partners—SRB of the genera Desulfosarcina/Desulfococcus or Desulfobulbus.14,15 The bacterium Ca. Methylomirabilis can couple AOM to nitrite reduction through an intra-aerobic methane oxidation pathway (known as “nitrite-dependent AOM” or N-DAMO),16 and an ANME lineage named Candidatus Methanoperedens nitroreducens (ANME-2d) can perform nitrate-driven AOM pathway (known as “nitrate-dependent AOM” or Nr-DAMO)17,18 as an important methane sink was revealed in the deep-sea cold seeps.11,19 Moreover, molecular analyses and 13CH4 tracer experiments have revealed that these denitrifying methanotrophs might have a competitive advantage over sulfate-dependent methanotrophs in consuming CH4 in hydrate-bearing deep-sea cold seeps.11 This is likely because nitrite and nitrate are thermodynamically more favorable electron acceptors than sulfate, making NO2−/NO3−-dependent AOM energetically more favorable.20

Denitrifying anaerobic methane oxidation (DAMO), which can be classified into N-DAMO and Nr-DAMO based on the electron acceptors—nitrite (NO2−) and nitrate (NO3−), respectively, is a crucial microbial process in the global biogeochemical cycles, serving as an important link between the nitrogen and carbon cycles.21 Traditionally, methane-dependent nitrate and nitrite removal has been attributed to syntrophic cooperation between ANME-2d and Ca. Methylomirabilis. In the DAMO pathway, ANME-2d archaea oxidize methane and reduce nitrate to nitrite, while Ca. Methylomirabilis bacteria utilize nitrite, converting it to dinitrogen gas through nitric oxide dismutation and intracellular oxygen production.16,17 This kind of cooperation has long been considered essential for the complete DAMO pathway. However, this paradigm is being challenged by recent discoveries. The isolation of Ca. Methylomirabilis sinica, capable of independent methane-dependent complete nitrate reduction to N2, challenged the traditional syntrophic DAMO paradigm.22 These findings not only revised the traditional model of DAMO as a strictly syntrophic process but also suggest that single-organism methane-dependent denitrification may be more widespread and ecologically significant than previously assumed.22 However, whether ANME-2d archaea are capable of performing the complete DAMO process independently remains unresolved, especially under nitrite-limited conditions of the deep-sea cold seeps. Yet, the possibility that ANME-2d archaea possess intrinsic pathways for complete nitrate or nitrite reduction cannot be ruled out. Clarifying this issue is essential for understanding the metabolic versatility and ecological roles of ANME-2d in natural and engineered environments. Although SAMO was traditionally regarded as the predominant AOM pathway in marine habitats,23,24 more recent studies have shown that DAMO can contribute substantially to methane removal and may rival sulfate-dependent AOM under nitrate-rich conditions.11,25,26 These findings highlight that the relative importance of SAMO and DAMO varies across different marine habitats, showing that both pathways are critical in regulating methane cycling in marine environments.

DNA-stable-isotope-probing (DNA-SIP) is an established and targeted cultivation-independent technique used to evaluate metabolic responses and offer opportunities to connect the function and identity of active microorganisms in their natural environments. Such methods can be used to identify microorganisms involved in the AOM by introducing substrates labeled with stable isotope (13CH4) to monitor their assimilation into microbial DNA.27 Here, we conducted an incubation experiment using the sediments collected from the two typical cold seeps of the South China Sea (Figure 1). The geochemical characteristics of the sediment samples are summarized in Table 1. Microcosms were established with SO42−, NO2−, and NO3− addition as electron acceptors. Meanwhile, antibiotics were used to inhibit the growth of cultured bacteria during the enrichment process. 13CH4 was added during the incubation to estimate the transformation of methane and carbon dioxide, coupled with DNA-SIP to identify organisms that are responsible for the negative methane flux. The combined approach provided experimental evidence to assess the degree of metabolic dependence between ANME-2d and NC10 bacteria in sediment communities. We performed 16S rRNA genes (bacteria and archaea) and functional genes (mcrA: ANME-2d subcluster, pmoA: NC10 bacteria, dsrB: SRB) amplicon sequencing of the 13C-labeled extracted DNA from the sediments to identify microbiota involved in AOM during incubation. And the potential AOM activity was estimated with stable isotope tracing methods, to elucidate the dynamics of AOM processes in the deep-sea hydrate-bearing sediments. Although ANME-2d archaea and NC10 bacteria were widely recognized as key DAMO microorganisms, and their ecological relevance had been increasingly documented, direct experimental evidence testing whether their interaction was tightly metabolically coupled in sediment communities remained limited.25,26 Therefore, we combined bacterial inhibition with DNA-SIP to identify the microorganisms actively assimilating methane-derived carbon in South China Sea cold seep sediments, and to test whether ANME-2d/NC10 associations were tightly metabolically coupled or whether the disruption of this coupling would shift methane oxidation to alternative AOM pathways.

Results and discussion

DNA-SIP revealed shifts in active microbial populations with bacterial inhibition

The DNA fractions with buoyant density (BD) of 1.7065–1.7262 g/mL, 1.6984–1.7181 g/mL, 1.6984–1.7193 g/mL and 1.6937–1.7204 g/mL were selected as “heavy” fractions, while those of 1.6808–1.7019 g/mL, 1.6761–1.6949 g/mL, 1.6761–1.6949 g/mL and 1.6773–1.6914 g/mL were selected as “light” fractions (Data S1), from 13C-CH4 amended microcosms at stations (Stns.) SQ_58, SQ_81, SQ_261, and SQ_264, respectively, which were supported by the quantitative distribution of density-resolved DNA concentration obtained from 12C-CH4 and 13C-CH4 treatments (Figure S1). Microbes actively assimilating 13C-derived carbon were detected by comparing their presence between light and heavy DNA fractions in the 12C/13C-CH4 treatments.

For the prokaryotic communities, the predominant bacterial phylum in site F (SQ261, SQ264) was Campylobacteria, while that of Haima (SQ58, SQ81) was Proteobacteria, mainly comprised of α- and γ-Proteobacteria (Figure 2A, Data S2). For the bacterial phylum, the proportion of Proteobacteria, Chloroflexi and Desulfobacterota decreased and the proportion of Campylobacterota increased at Stn. SQ261, while the opposite trend was observed at Stn. SQ264 (Figure 2A). The proportion of Proteobacteria decreased at Stn. SQ58, while that of Campylobacterota increased at Stns. SQ58 and SQ81 (Figure 2A). Sulfate-reducing bacterial groups (e.g., Desulfobacterales, Desulfobulbales, Syntrophobacterales) were largely unaffected, maintaining relative stability (Figure S2). For the archaeal phylum, the proportion of Methanosarcinia (Halobacterota) decreased, while that of Nitrososphaeria (Crenarchaeota) increased in all samples (Figure 2B). Notably, the ANME-1 (Halobacterota) increased at Stns. SQ261 and SQ81, whereas ANME-2d declined across all stations; other ANME-2 clades (ANME-2a/2b/2c) showed little change (Figures 2B and S2). Halobacterota was the most abundant archaeal phylum in all samples, and was dominated by Methanosarcinia (Figure 2B and Data S3). Nitrososphaeria and Bathyarchaeia, belonging to Crenarchaeota, together with Thermoplasmata (Thermoplasmatota) occupied a higher proportion (Figure 2B). However, ANME-1 belonging to Halobacterota only occupied a higher proportion at station (Stn.) SQ81 (Figure 2B). These DNA-SIP-based findings provided evidence that inhibiting bacterial activity triggered a structural shift in the active methane-oxidizing community from nitrate/nitrite-dependent groups toward sulfate-dependent groups. The addition of a bacterial inhibitor significantly reduced the abundance of Ca. Methylomirabilis, which led to a concomitant decrease in ANME-2d archaeal populations. This supported that ANME-2d archaea were metabolically dependent on their bacterial partners to maintain electron acceptor turnover, especially under nitrate-rich but nitrite-scarce seeps.18,30 Interestingly, we observed a compensatory increase in the abundance of ANME-1 archaea, a group typically associated with SAMO.9,24 This suggested a microbial community shifted in response to the inhibition of DAMO, in which ANME-1 archaea and their sulfate-reducing partners sustained methane oxidation through the SAMO pathway. Consistent with this, the sulfate-reducing community, particularly Group IV, remained functionally stable, contributing to AOM in our incubations. The relative stability of ANME-2c under antibiotic treatment suggested that the ANME-2c/SRB-mediated sulfate-dependent AOM pathway was not substantially disrupted under the tested conditions. This pattern might be explained by the fact that ANME-2c is archaeal and therefore is not directly targeted by antibiotics against bacteria, while many SRB are Gram-negative and may be less susceptible to penicillin-type antibiotics because of the permeability barrier imposed by the outer membrane, with biofilm formation potentially providing additional protection.31,32 Consistent with the established association of ANME-2c with sulfate-dependent AOM and ANME-2d with nitrate/nitrite-dependent AOM coupled to NC10 bacteria or other denitrifying partners,16,17,24 these results suggested that the antibiotic treatment primarily perturbed the DAMO-related ANME-2d/NC10 consortium, thereby promoting a shift among alternative AOM pathways rather than suppressing overall methane oxidation. The coexistence of DAMO- and SAMO-related consortia likely enhanced the resilience of the methane sink in cold seep sediments, because pathway-level compensation could preserve methane oxidation when the availability of nitrate, nitrite, sulfate, or syntrophic partners changed.4,6 Antibiotics were applied here as a perturbation treatment to suppress bacterial partners during enrichment rather than to completely sterilize the sediment community, because penicillin primarily targets peptidoglycan synthesis and archaea generally lack peptidoglycan, although some off-target effects of streptomycin on archaeal protein synthesis could not be fully excluded.33,34

Phylogeny of functional genes

To further elucidate changes in key functional groups, we analyzed the mcrA, pmoA, and dsrB genes. Functional gene analyses further revealed distinct shifts of functional groups and their responses to bacterial inhibition. The mcrA gene sequences phylogenetically fell into six clades: ANME-1, ANME-2a, ANME-2b, ANME-2c, ANME-2d, and ANME-3 (Figure 3). Among them, ANME-2c was the dominant clade across all samples, highlighting its central role in AOM under the experimental conditions. Notably, the relative abundance of ANME-2d decreased significantly after the addition of bacterial inhibitors (Figure S3A), suggesting that its activity might depend on syntrophic interactions with bacterial partners. In contrast, analysis of the pmoA gene revealed three major clades: group A, group B, and group C (Figure 4). Group A was the most abundant under control conditions, indicating its dominance among methane-oxidizing bacteria (Figure S3B). However, bacterial inhibition led to a marked reduction in the relative abundance of Group A, accompanied by an increase in Group C, suggesting niche shifted or differences in sensitivity to bacterial suppression (Figure S3B). For sulfate-reducing microorganisms, dsrB gene sequences were assigned to four clades: group II, group IV, Syntrophobacteraceae, and Desulfobulbaceae (Figure 5). Group IV was the predominant clade, and its relative abundance exhibited only minor increases following bacterial inhibitor treatment (Figure S3C). This result implied that the sulfate-reducing community, particularly Group IV, was largely unaffected by bacterial inhibition and remained functionally stable during AOM (Figure S4C). Notably, the antibiotic treatment likely resulted in incomplete and differential inhibition across bacterial groups, with sulfate-reducing populations appearing less affected than NC10 bacteria under the incubation conditions. This differential response might explain the persistence of SRB and their continued contribution to sulfate-dependent AOM. The mutualistic relationship between ANME-2d archaea and Ca. Methylomirabilis bacteria appeared to be obligate under anoxic, nitrate-rich settings. The disruption of either partner destabilized the DAMO process. Our previous studies from the SCS showed that the abundance of ANME-2d correlated strongly with nitrite availability and pmoA transcripts from NC10 bacteria, particularly at Site F, where methane and nitrate co-occur in high concentrations.11 It also showed that the transcript abundance of mcrA and dsrB genes was significantly higher in cold seeps than in the trough,11 which indicated that the microbial genetic potential for both DAMO (via ANME-2d) and SAMO (via SRB) was concurrently present and actively transcribed. The high dsrB transcript levels, in particular, provided a ready molecular basis for the SAMO pathway to compensate when DAMO was inhibited. ANME-2c likely mediated SAMO and formed relatively stable syntrophic partnerships with SRB, which might confer resistance to short-term perturbations.24,35 In contrast, ANME-2d-dependent DAMO relied on tightly coupled interactions with NC10 bacteria,16,17 making this pathway more sensitive to bacterial inhibition.Figure 3Maximum-likelihood phylogenetic tree shows the affiliations of the 16S rRNA gene sequences of M. nitroreducens-like archaea recovered from the cold seeps in each treatmentBootstrap values >50% (out of 1000 replicates) are shown in front of respective nodes.Figure 4Maximum-likelihood phylogenetic tree shows the affiliations of Methylomirabilis-like bacterial 16S rRNA gene sequences recovered from the cold seeps in each treatmentBootstrap values >50% (out of 1000 replicates) are shown in front of respective nodes.Figure 5Maximum-likelihood phylogenetic tree shows the affiliations of the TOP 30 Desulfosarcina-like sulfate-reducing bacterial 16S rRNA gene sequences recovered from the cold seeps in each treatmentBootstrap values >50% (out of 1000 replicates) are shown in front of respective nodes.

Potential rates of anaerobic methane oxidation

The 13CH4 tracing experiments showed that the production of 13CO2 significantly increased in both the control group (without inhibitor) and the group amended with bacterial inhibitors after 7 days of incubation (p < 0.01; two-tailed t test) (Figure 6). The production of 13CO2 in the control group reached 1.87 nmol g−1 [wet sediment], while that in the inhibitor-treated group was 1.82 nmol g−1 [wet sediment], with no significant difference between the two treatments (two-tailed t test, p = 0.67). These results indicated that the inhibition of bacterial activity did not markedly suppress methane oxidation under the experimental conditions. However, subsequent community analyses revealed that archaeal abundance decreased significantly upon bacterial inhibition, suggesting that while bacteria were not simply auxiliary members of the consortium, but played key roles in electron-acceptor turnover, interspecies coupling, and maintenance of DAMO-associated activity. This finding aligned with our interpretation that archaea were the primary executors of AOM, whereas bacteria acted as indispensable partners facilitating the overall DAMO process. In addition, the production rate of 13CO2 was generally higher on day 3 compared to day 7 in both treatments, indicating that AOM activity was more pronounced during the early stage of incubation (Figure S4). The addition of bacterial inhibitors also resulted in only a slight reduction in CH4 consumption compared to the uninhibited control (two-tailed t test, p > 0.05; Figure S4), further supporting the notion that bacterial partners were not the main methane oxidizers but were metabolically required to maintain archaeal activity in the consortium. The addition of bacterial inhibitors resulted in significantly (two-tailed t test, p < 0.01) lower nitrite consumption at Site F, which demonstrated that the consumption of nitrite decreased, converting DAMO to the other pathway. Previous study showed that at these cold seeps, high nitrate concentrations might enhance nitrate reduction and nitrite delivery, further promoting DAMO activity.11 Although a clear shift in active AOM populations, the net effect was generally unchanged based on the overall methane oxidation rate at the end of the incubation, despite the suppression of DAMO, highlighting the functional redundancy and metabolic flexibility of AOM communities. While DAMO appeared to be the dominant methane oxidation pathway under nitrate-rich conditions,11 the microbial ecosystem could shift toward SAMO35 or other electron-acceptor-dependent AOM pathways under various perturbations—e.g., selective bacterial inhibition, increased sulfate availability or salinization, and broader geochemical changes (e.g., temperature or redox shifts) that favor sulfate-, iron-, or manganese-dependent AOM,36,37,38 indicating ecological plasticity and functional redundancy. Deep-sea cold seeps were characterized by spatially and temporally dynamic redox conditions, with multiple electron acceptors such as sulfate, nitrate, and nitrite coexisting or fluctuating in availability.39,40,41 This geochemical heterogeneity supported substantial metabolic flexibility among methane-oxidizing microorganisms and allowed distinct AOM pathways to operate under different conditions. As a result, the coexistence of multiple functionally overlapping microbial groups created functional redundancy, which buffered environmental perturbations and maintained methane oxidation even when specific partners or electron acceptors became limiting. Such redundancy and flexibility likely enhanced ecosystem resilience and helped ensure that cold seep sediments persisted as a stable methane sink and carbon reservoir. Notably, geochemical characteristics such as organic carbon, sulfur, and nitrate contents appeared to influence both the structure and activity of AOM communities.6,11 Sediments richer in organic matter and reduced sulfur generally favored SAMO, whereas those with higher nitrate concentrations supported more active nitrate-driven DAMO. These geochemical gradients likely regulate the balance between ANME-SRB and ANME 2d-Methylomirabilis associations, reflecting the adaptive metabolic strategies of microbial consortia under varying redox conditions.11

Furthermore, rate measurements and gene transcription analyses suggested that DAMO might contribute substantially to methane oxidation, even surpassing SAMO under nitrate-rich conditions.11 Previous study showed that the addition of inhibitors could disrupt soil microbial communities, but the production would be increased, which could support our results.42 The DAMO rate exceeded that of SAMO during 13CH4 tracer incubations, with earlier CO2 production peaks, indicating a faster response of nitrate-dependent methanotrophs. Given the limited availability of nitrite in natural environments, ANME-2d likely depends on the rapid nitrite turnover by Methylomirabilis.16,17,43

By integrating DNA-SIP, 13CH4 tracer incubations, and bacterial inhibition treatments, our findings highlight the critical role of microbial syntrophy in DAMO and reveal the potential for functional compensation by alternative AOM pathways, such as SAMO, under selective inhibition in cold-seep sediments. These results offered insights into microbial resilience, and community-level responses to environmental stressors in the extreme conditions of the deep-sea cold seeps, with broader implications for biogeochemical cycling and methane mitigation strategies. Moreover, the spatial heterogeneity of methane availability and redox conditions in regions such as the SCS strongly shaped AOM community structure, highlighting the necessity of site-specific considerations in ecological modeling and microbial resource development.

Limitations of the study

This study provided insights into methane-oxidizing microbial interactions in cold seep sediments; however, several limitations should be considered. First, the incubation experiments were conducted under controlled laboratory conditions, which might not have fully captured the complexity and spatial heterogeneity of in situ deep-sea environments. Second, the use of inhibitor treatments might have introduced non-specific effects, potentially influencing microbial groups beyond the targeted populations. Third, DNA-based stable isotope probing identified active populations incorporating labeled carbon but did not directly resolve metabolic rates or quantify pathway-specific contributions at high resolution. In addition, the temporal scale of the incubations was limited, which constrained our ability to assess long-term community dynamics and stability. Finally, while this study focused on nitrate- and sulfate-dependent pathways, other potential electron acceptors and interactions were not explored. Future studies integrating in situ measurements, multi-omics approaches, and longer-term experiments would help address these limitations.

Experimental model and study participant details

Environmental sediment samples

This study did not involve human participants, animals, plants, cell lines, primary cell cultures, or isolated microbial strains. Environmental sediment samples were collected from deep-sea cold seep sediments in the South China Sea, including the Haima cold seep and Site F. Sediments were collected using push-core samplers during research cruises in June 2018 and June 2021. The 4–8 cm sediment interval was used for anoxic microcosm incubations because this layer represents a near-surface transition zone with active methane flux and redox gradients where AOM activity is typically pronounced in cold seep sediments. Samples were maintained under anoxic conditions by flushing with N2 and were stored at 4°C before incubation. No institutional approval for human or animal research was required.

Method details

Sample collection and chemical analysis

Sediment samples were collected from the Haima cold seeps (16°43′N, 110°28′E) and Site F cold seeps (22°6′N, 119°17′E) on the continent shelf of the South China Sea (SCS; Figure 1; Table 1) using a push-core sampler during expeditions in June 2018 and June 2021, respectively, following previously described protocols.11,49 Two sampling stations were investigated at each region: SQ_58 and SQ_81 at Haima, and SQ_261 and SQ_264 at Site F. By use of a sterile spatula, the uppermost 3 cm of the freshly exposed sediment surface were removed. Then, the top layer (4–8 cm) which representing a near-surface transition zone with active methane flux and redox gradients where AOM activity was typically pronounced in cold seep sediments, was sliced and flushed with N2 and immediately stored at 4 °C for anoxic incubation experiments.6,11,50 And the physicochemical characterization was conducted as described previously.11,49

SIP microcosms incubations for identification of microbes involved in the AOM

SIP incubations were carried out in slurry microcosms. The SIP microcosms were prepared in 20-mL serum bottles filled with 3 g of sediments and 9 mL of N2-purged deionized water amended with NaCl (34.57‰), and then preincubated for 2 days (4°C, dark) on orbital shakers to remove background NOx− (NO2− + NO3−) and SO42−. Subsequently, the vials were injected with 50 μL of N2-degassed stock solutions of either NaNO2 (0.8 M), NaNO3 (0.16 M), and 100 μL of Na2SO4 (2.4 M) for final concentration of 5 mM NaNO2, 1 mM NaNO3, and 30 mM Na2SO4 in the slurry. The slurries were subsequently split into different treatment groups: (a) 13CH4 (13C at 99%, Sigma-Aldrich), (b) 13CH4 + antibiotics and (c) 12CH4. After that, 4 mL 13CH4 or 12CH4 was immediately injected into these vials in the headspace. Antibiotics were used to inhibit the growth of cultured bacteria during the enrichment process by adding a 50 mg/L Penicillin-Streptomycin solution.51 All microcosms were incubated at in situ temperature (4°C) in the dark with gentle shaking at 120 rpm. On day 3 and 7 after incubation, three samples from each treatment were taken for total CO2 and CH4 concentration and production of 13CO2 using a gas chromatography (GC-2014, Shimadzu, Japan) and isotope ratio mass spectrometer (Delta V Advantage, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Germany), respectively. Potential AOM rate in different samples were quantified according to the production of 13CO2 and consumption of CH4 over the incubation period, normalized to wet sediment weight and incubation time (nmol g−1 d−1 for 13CO2 production; ppm g−1 d−1 for CH4 consumption). After 7 days incubation, sediment samples from each treatment were taken for DNA extraction.

DNA extraction, ultracentrifugation and high-throughput sequencing

Total DNA in the sediment was extracted according to the instruction manual of PowerSoil DNA Isolation Kit (MO BIO Laboratories, Inc., Carlsbad, USA) and quantified with a Qubit 2.0 fluorometer (Thermo Scientific, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Corp.). The extracted DNA samples from the 12C-CH4 and 13C-CH4 microcosms were subjected to density gradient ultracentrifugation. Separation of the CsCl gradient followed the method from Martineau et al.52 A concentration of 1.72 g mL-1 CsCl was used to create a gradient during ultracentrifugation at which separation of the heavy and light band could be achieved. The separation of “light” and “heavy” DNA fractions was achieved using cesium chloride (CsCl) density gradient ultracentrifugation. Briefly, DNA was mixed with CsCl solution to form a density gradient during ultracentrifugation. Under high centrifugal force, DNA molecules distribute along the gradient according to their buoyant density (BD). Incorporation of heavy isotopes (e.g., 13C) increases the BD of DNA, resulting in a measurable shift toward heavier fractions. After centrifugation, the gradient was fractionated, and BD was determined for each fraction (typically using refractive index measurements). Fractions with higher BD than the corresponding peaks in the 12C control were considered “heavy” DNA, indicating isotope incorporation. The DNA samples were further purified to remove residual salt using the QIAEX II Gel Extraction Kit (Qiagen, Germany). After fractionation and purification, each fraction was carried out by sequencing as described previously.53 The V3-V4 hypervariable region of bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified using the universal primer pair 338F/806R,54 whereas the archaeal 16S rRNA gene was amplified with primers 340F/806R.55 Nested PCR assays were employed to amplify key functional genes, including the mcrA gene of Methanoperedens-like archaea (ANME-2d),56,57 the pmoA gene of M. oxyfera-like bacteria (Methylomirabilis; NC10 bacteria),58,59 and the dsrB gene of Desulfosarcina-like sulfate-reducing bacteria (DSRB).60 Detailed information on all primer sets used for 16S rRNA and functional gene amplification is provided in Table S1. PCR products were verified by electrophoresis on 1.2% agarose gels stained with SYBR Safe and subsequently sequenced using paired-end (PE250) technology on an Illumina HiSeq platform (Novogene Co., Ltd., www.novogene.com).

Quantification and statistical analysis

The workflow for sequence analyses is described in detail (e.g., software and parameters) in our previous study.53 Briefly, raw sequencing reads were quality filtered, denoised, and merged using the DADA2 plugin in QIIME2 (2023.5)44 to generate high-quality sequences and representative amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). The resulting ASV table was further filtered using the q2-filter-feature plugin to remove low-abundance ASVs with fewer than 10 total reads.

For bacterial and archaeal 16S rRNA gene datasets, taxonomic assignment and compositional analyses were performed using the q2-classify-sklearn algorithm with the SILVA (V.138) database. Potential contaminants were removed using the q2-feature-table plugin and visualized with the q2-taxa-barplot plugin. ASVs annotated as mitochondria, chloroplasts, or eukaryotes were subsequently excluded from downstream analyses. ANOSIM (analysis of similarities) was used to analyze the similarities of the microbial community compositions among different regions.

For the functional genes (mcrA, pmoA and dsrB), resulting representative ASV sequences were used to calculate phylogenetic trees for subsequent analyses. The phylogenetic trees were constructed against reference sequences retrieved from GenBank database at NCBI (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov). Sequences were aligned using MUSCLE (v3.8.1551)45 and trimmed with TrimAL (v1.4.1)46 with default settings. Maximum-likelihood trees were constructed with IQ-TREE (v2.2.0.3)47 with the “-m MFP -B 1000” options. The produced tree was visualized and beautified using Interactive tree of life (iTOL; v6).48 Community composition plots and rate figures were generated using R (v4.2.3).
