Cross-cultural adaptation and psychometric evaluation of the dengue preventive attitudes questionnaire for Indonesian public health nurses
Authors: Nuzul Sri Hertanti, Yeu-Hui Chuang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPublic health nurses serve as the frontline for dengue prevention and control at the community level. Assessing their attitudes is essential, as these influence the effectiveness of nursing practices in disease prevention. However, no validated Indonesian questionnaire exists for this purpose. This study aimed to translate, culturally adapt, and evaluate the reliability and validity of the Dengue Preventive Attitudes Questionnaire (DPAQ) for use among public health nurses in Indonesia.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted with 458 public health nurses working in primary healthcare (PHC) units across Indonesia. The study involved two phases: a cross-cultural adaptation process and psychometric evaluation. A six-step cross-cultural adaptation process, including forward translation, synthesis, backward translation, expert committee review, pretesting, and instrument appraisal, was employed to generate the Indonesian version of the DPAQ (DPAQ-I). Psychometric evaluations, including the face validity, content validity, item analysis, construct validity, test-retest reliability, and internal consistency, were assessed.ResultsThe DPAQ-I demonstrated excellent content validity (scale-CVI = 1.00, evaluated by eight experts). Exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses confirmed a single-factor structure, explaining 42.82% of the variance, with good model fit (CMIN/df = 2.51, CFI = 0.95, TLI = 0.92, RMSEA = 0.07, SRMR = 0.05). Reliability testing showed acceptable internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.75 and McDonald’s ω = 0.74) and acceptable test–retest reliability (ICC = 0.75 over a two-week interval).ConclusionsThe DPAQ-I showed acceptable validity and reliability for measuring public health nurses’ attitudes toward dengue prevention. Its concise format enhances feasibility and ease of use in busy clinical settings. The findings support its potential as a brief instrument for evaluating nurses’ dengue preventive attitudes in both research and educational contexts, although further validation using independent samples from more diverse geographic settings is recommended before broader application.

Background

Dengue, a mosquito-borne febrile illness, is a major public health problem in tropical and subtropical regions globally [1]. In 2024, over 7.6 million cases were reported, including 3.4 million confirmed cases, more than 16,000 severe cases, and over 3,000 deaths [1]. This global trend is mirrored in Indonesia, where the incidence rate increased from 41.36 per 100,000 in 2023 to 66.58 per 100,000 in 2024 [2]. The case fatality rate was 0.60% in 2024 [2], with an economic burden rising from USD 381.15 million in 2015 to USD 681.26 million in 2017 [3, 4]. These data emphasize that dengue remains a serious public health issue, resulting in substantial health and economic burdens that require sustained prevention initiatives at both clinical and community levels.

In Indonesia’s primary healthcare system, public health nurses work in community-based clinics called primary healthcare (PHC) units (Pusat Kesehatan Masyarakat / Puskesmas). These nurses provide first-line healthcare service that emphasize disease prevention, health promotion, and early detection rather than hospital-based curative care [5]. They are responsible for identifying early cases, providing care, and referring severe cases to hospitals [6]. In addition, they lead health promotion and prevention programs within their communities [6]. Considering these responsibilities, nurses require not only adequate knowledge of dengue but also positive attitudes toward its prevention [7]. Moreover, greater knowledge and more positive attitudes have been found to significantly predict nurses’ confidence in dengue-related practice [8, 9]. However, findings from a previous study indicated that good knowledge alone may not be sufficient to ensure better preventive practice [10]. This suggests that nurses’ motivation and willingness, reflected in their attitudes, are crucial for translating knowledge into meaningful action in dengue prevention and management. Although attitude is not the only factor influencing dengue-related practice, it represents a modifiable psychological component that can bridge the gap between knowledge and actual behavior.

According to a recent systematic review, the Knowledge-Attitudes-Practice (KAP) framework provides a useful conceptual basis for understanding the relationship among knowledge, attitudes, and practices [11]. The framework explains that knowledge shapes attitudes, and attitudes subsequently influence behaviors [11]. Within this framework, attitudes function as a mediating factor that determines whether knowledge is effectively translated into action. In the context of dengue, even when nurses have adequate knowledge about dengue, their attitudes largely determine their motivation to engage in preventive activities and to sustain these behaviors in practice. While external factors, such as education, media exposure, and socioeconomic context, may also influence behaviors [11], strengthening positive attitudes among nurses remains central to promoting and maintaining dengue prevention practices within PHC settings.

Although several questionnaires exist to assess dengue prevention attitudes [6, 12–16], most were developed for community residents or students, rather than nurses working in PHC units. Among the available tools, two questionnaires have potential relevance for public health nurses [6, 16]. The first questionnaire, developed by Suwanbamrung et al. [6], includes 14 knowledge items, 10 attitudes items, and 47 practice items. However, it provides limited information on the validity and reliability of its individual sections. In addition, its attitudes section uses “yes,” “no,” and “no opinion” response options, which are close to knowledge-based questions and may potentially limit their effectiveness in capturing attitudes. The second questionnaire, developed by Yusuf and Ibrahim [16], provides better documentation of the validity and reliability of its knowledge, attitudes, and practice sections. Its attitudes section consists of 10 items measured on a five-point Likert scale [16]. The content of the attitudes items addresses perceptions that are conceptually relevant to dengue prevention in the PHC context, such as beliefs about dengue preventability, perceived personal responsibility in prevention, awareness of environmental risks, and recognition of dengue as a public health concern. Based on both conceptual relevance and existing psychometric evidence, the attitudes section of the Yusuf and Ibrahim [16] questionnaire was selected for adaptation and validation in this study, as no Indonesian version currently exists for use among public health nurses. The absence of a culturally adapted and psychometrically tested instrument limits the ability to accurately assess nurses’ attitudes and to guide training or policy interventions. Therefore, this study aimed to translate, culturally adapt, and evaluate the reliability and validity of the Dengue Preventive Attitudes Questionnaire (DPAQ) for use among public health nurses in Indonesia.

Methods

This study used a cross-sectional research design with two phases: (1) cross-cultural adaptation and (2) psychometric testing of the Indonesian version of DPAQ (DPAQ-I).

Phase 1: Cross-cultural adaptation

Permission was obtained from the original questionnaire’s author to adapt the instrument for use in Indonesia. The adaptation followed Beaton et al.’s [17] six-step guidelines, including forward translation, synthesis, backward translation, expert review, pretesting, and instrument appraisal.

Forward translation, synthesis, and backward translation

In this study, two independent translators, fluent in English and Indonesian, completed the forward translation of the Yusuf and Ibrahim [16] dengue preventive attitudes questionnaire into Indonesian. One translator was a dengue researcher, and the other was a Ministry of Health (MOH) officer. Their translations (T1 and T2) were synthesized into a single version (T12) by the researchers. Subsequently, two other translators, both fluent in Indonesian and English, performed backward translation without access to the original questionnaire. The back-translated versions (BT1 and BT2) were compared with the original English version by the researchers to ensure that the T12 version accurately captured the intended meaning of the original items.

Expert review and pretesting

In this study, an expert panel (n = 8) reviewed the original questionnaire and its translations (T1, T2, T12, BT1, and BT2). The experts included two dengue clinicians, two nurses with expertise in dengue, two public health experts, one dengue officer from a district health office (DHO), and one dengue officer from Indonesia’s MOH. All panel members held at least a Master’s degree and had over 10 years of dengue-related experience. Using a structured review form, they independently evaluated the translations for semantic, idiomatic, experiential, and conceptual equivalence and provided qualitative suggestions for wording improvement. After receiving feedback from all experts, the researchers synthesized their suggestions to generate the prefinal version of the DPAQ-I.

The prefinal version was tested among 30 nurses working in PHC units to evaluate clarity and comprehensibility. Participants rated each item on a four-point Likert scale (1 = very difficult to understand; 4 = very easy to understand). Two types of face validity indices (FVIs) were calculated: the item-level FVI (I-FVI) and the scale-level FVI (S-FVI/Ave). A value of ≥ 0.80 was considered acceptable for both the I-FVI and S-FVI/Ave [18]. In this study, the I-FVI was calculated as the proportion of raters who rated an item as clear and comprehensible (with a score of 3 or 4). The S-FVI/Ave was calculated by averaging the I-FVI scores across all items. The same 30 nurses were also invited to complete the questionnaire again two weeks later for test-retest reliability assessment. They had been informed in the study information that a second administration would be conducted after two weeks. The retest link was distributed via WhatsApp and no attrition occurred.

Instrument appraisal

In the finalization step, the research team and expert panel reviewed the pretesting results, evaluated the questionnaire for the content validity, and finalized the instrument. The content validity of the DPAQ-I was then evaluated by the same panel of eight experts. Each expert rated the relevance of every item using a four-point scale (1 = not relevant, 2 = somewhat relevant/requires some revision, 3 = quite relevant/requires minor revision, and 4 = highly relevant). We calculated the item-level content validity index (I-CVI) and scale-level content validity index (S-CVI/Ave). Thresholds of I-CVI ≥ 0.78 and S-CVI/Ave ≥ 0.90 were considered indicative of acceptable content validity [19]. In this study, the I-CVI was calculated by dividing the number of experts who rated an item as 3 or 4 by eight (eight experts), representing the proportion of experts who judged the item to be relevant. The S-CVI/Ave was then calculated by averaging all I-CVI values across the items to obtain an overall measure of the scale’s content validity. Because ratings of 3 or 4 were both considered relevant, an item could achieve an I-CVI of 1.00 even when some experts assigned a rating of 3 rather than 4.

Phase 2: Psychometric testing

Phase 2 focused on the statistical evaluation of the Indonesian questionnaire, including item analysis, exploratory factor analysis (EFA), confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), internal consistency reliability, and test-retest reliability. Face validity and content validity were conducted earlier during the cross-cultural adaptation process in Phase 1.

Study participants

Nurses working in PHC units in Indonesia were recruited using convenience sampling. This approach was considered appropriate because nurses were distributed across multiple provinces and had limited availability for research participation. The inclusion criterion was nurses currently working in PHC units in Indonesia, referred to in this study as public health nurses. Nurses who were on leave or had moved to other departments outside the PHC unit during the study period were excluded.

A sample size of at least 10 subjects per item is recommended for psychometric testing using EFA [20]. Because the questionnaire consisted of 10 items, a minimum of 100 participants was considered sufficient for EFA. Moreover, to ensure a robust sample size for CFA, at least 300 participants were required [20, 21]. Using independent samples for EFA and CFA is recommended to confirm the stability of the factor structure and to reduce the risk of overfitting [22–24]. Because no specific rule exists for determining the proportion of data assigned to EFA versus CFA [25], we allocated approximately 30% of the sample to EFA and 70% to CFA. This allocation ensured that the EFA sample exceeded the minimum requirement (> 100 participants) and that the CFA sample met the recommended threshold of at least 300 participants. Finally, 458 participants completed the questionnaire and were randomly split into two subsets using the Random Case Selection function in SPSS, resulting in 137 participants for the EFA and 321 participants for the CFA.

Instruments

There are two sections in the questionnaire: demographic characteristics questionnaire and dengue preventive attitudes questionnaire.

Demographic characteristics questionnaire

This questionnaire included age (in years), gender (male or female), level of education (diploma, defined as a three-year education program from an academy of nursing, or bachelor’s degree and above), years of experience working in PHC units, experience assessing patients suspected of having dengue (yes or no), experience caring for dengue patients (yes or no), prior information on dengue received during formal education (yes or no), and participation in dengue training (yes or no).

Dengue preventive attitudes questionnaire

The 10-item questionnaire assessing attitudes toward dengue prevention was originally developed by Yusuf and Ibrahim [16]. The original instrument included three sections: knowledge, attitudes, and practices. In the present study, only the attitudes section was used because the research objective was to translate and validate a tool specifically for measuring nurses’ attitudes toward dengue prevention, rather than their knowledge or practices. Including the other sections would introduce additional constructs beyond the intended scope of this study. The attitudes section uses a five-point Likert scale, where 1 indicates “strongly disagree” and 5 indicates “strongly agree.” The total score ranges from 10 to 50, with a higher score reflecting more favorable attitudes [16]. The original questionnaire demonstrated validity and reliability in previous studies with health professionals in Ethiopia, achieving excellent content validity (CVI = 1.00) and acceptable reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.73) [16]. In the present study, this attitude section was translated, culturally adapted, and psychometrically evaluated for use among Indonesian public health nurses.

Data collection

After obtaining ethical approval and permission from the authorized institutions, data were collected from May to August 2024 using Google Forms. Participants were approached through both online and offline recruitment strategies. The recruitment process occurred in two phases. First, during the cross-cultural adaptation phase, 30 participants were recruited online for pretesting of the prefinal version of the questionnaire. These participants were not included in the psychometric testing sample.

Second, after the instrument was finalized, psychometric testing was conducted using both online and offline recruitment strategies. For the online recruitment strategy, the study invitation was distributed through professional nursing networks and social media platforms, such as WhatsApp and Instagram, to reach nurses working in PHC units in provinces beyond those accessible to the research team. The eligibility criteria were stated in the study invitation and information. Before accessing the informed consent form, respondents completed a screening question to confirm their eligibility as PHC nurses, and only eligible respondents could proceed to the next step. Two online forms were used: the first contained the study information and informed consent form, and the second included demographic questions and the DPAQ-I. Nurses who agreed to participate completed the first form by providing electronic informed consent. Participants who completed this step then received the link to the second form via WhatsApp.

For the offline recruitment strategy, research assistants visited PHC units in an accessible province and coordinated with the head nurse at each unit to facilitate recruitment. Eligible nurses received an explanation of the study, provided written informed consent, and then completed the same questionnaire using Google Forms. To ensure data quality, each email address was limited to one submission to minimize duplicate entries. In addition, Google Forms required all items to be completion before submission, which prevented missing data. The final psychometric testing sample included 458 participants, comprising 80 participants recruited online and 378 recruited offline.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS, version 27.0, Armonk, NY, USA) and AMOS (version 26.0) software for Windows. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic characteristics of participants. Categorical data were reported as frequencies and percentages, while continuous data were presented as means and standard deviations (SDs). The item analysis of the 10-item DPAQ-I was conducted using corrected item-total correlations to evaluate each item’s contribution to the overall scale. Items with correlation coefficient of ≤ 0.30 or ≥ 0.80 were reviewed for potential removal, as coefficients of ≤ 0.30 indicate insufficient contribution to the construct, while coefficients of ≥ 0.80 suggest redundancy or potential multicollinearity [26, 27]. Final decisions regarding item retention or removal were based on both statistical performance and conceptual alignment with dengue preventive attitudes.

In this study, both EFA and CFA were conducted because they serve different purposes in instrument validation [23]. Since the original questionnaire did not report a predefined factor structure and item removal was anticipated during the adaptation process, EFA was initially used to explore the underlying factor structure of the Indonesian version of the questionnaire. After the factor structure was identified through EFA, CFA was then performed using a separate sample to confirm the structure and assess model fit.

Before conducting the EFA, the suitability of the data for factor analysis was examined. The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure and Bartlett’s test of sphericity were used to assess sampling adequacy and factorability, with KMO ≥ 0.6 and p < .05 considered acceptable [28, 29]. The EFA was conducted using principal axis factoring (PAF), which is appropriate for identifying latent constructs based on the shared variance among items [30, 31]. Because the attitude items represented related aspects of dengue prevention, the factors were expected to be correlated. Therefore, Promax rotation was applied when more than one factor was explored [30, 31]. Since no predefined factor structure was available, factors were extracted based on eigenvalues of > 1.00, inspection of the scree plots, and factor loadings ≥ 0.32 [27, 30]. Items that loaded onto multiple factors were reviewed in relation to their conceptual meaning and their alignment with other items within each factor to determine the most suitable placement. A minimum of three items per factor was required to establish robust and interpretable factors [30, 31]. After the initial rotated solution was examined, a one-factor solution was also evaluated to assess whether the retained items could be represented by a single broader construct. For the one-factor solution, no rotation was applied. These criteria were applied in the present study to determine the underlying structure of the Indonesian version of the questionnaire.

The CFA was performed to evaluate the model fit of the DPAQ-I. Model fit indices included Chi-square divided by the degrees of freedom (CMIN/df < 3.0), comparative fit index (CFI > 0.90), Tucker-Lewis index (TLI > 0.90), root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA < 0.08), and standardized root mean square residual (SRMR < 0.08) [32]. These indices and their recommended cutoff values were used as the criteria for assessing the goodness of fit of the DPAQ-I model in this study.

The internal consistency reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s α, with coefficients of 0.70–0.95 considered acceptable [33]. McDonald’s omega (ω) was additionally calculated using the OMEGA macro for SPSS developed by Hayes and Coutts [34] as another indicator of internal consistency reliability. McDonald’s ω of 0.70 or higher was considered acceptable [34]. To assess the stability of the scale over time, test–retest reliability was evaluated over a two-week interval using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) derived from a two-way mixed-effects model with absolute agreement and a single-measure ICC [33, 35]. ICC values > 0.70 were interpreted as indicating acceptable reliability [33, 35]. These reliability assessments provided evidence of the questionnaire’s consistency and stability.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Medical and Health Research Ethics Committee, Universitas Gadjah Mada-Dr. Sardjito General Hospital, Indonesia (KE/FK/0455/EC/2024), and the Taipei Medical University Joint Institutional Review Board, Taiwan (N202405011). All procedures followed the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant ethical guidelines. Participants were informed about the study purpose, procedures, potential risks and benefits, confidentiality, voluntary participation, and their right to withdraw at any time. Electronic informed consent was obtained from participants recruited online, whereas written informed consent was obtained from those recruited offline before participation. Confidentiality was maintained by using coded identifiers and secure data handling.

Results

Phase 1: Cross-cultural adaptation

Terminology modifications

Expert review identified several terms requiring modification for cultural relevance (Table 1). The term “dengue fever” (Items 1 and 4) and “dengue” (Item 6) were changed to “dengue infection” to align with Indonesia’s national dengue management guideline, which uses “dengue infection” in the context of prevention and control, while “dengue fever” or “dengue” are typically used for clinical diagnosis and treatment [36]. For Item 2, “dengue tanpa tanda-tanda peringatan” (dengue without warning signs) was revised to “infeksi dengue tanpa warning signs” (dengue infection without warning signs). The term “infeksi dengue” (dengue infection) was added for consistency with Indonesia’s national dengue guideline, which uses “infeksi dengue tanpa warning signs” [36]. This terminology follows the 2009 WHO classification, which classifies dengue as dengue without warning signs, dengue with warning signs, severe dengue [37], but was adjusted for consistency with the Indonesian guideline. Previously, dengue was classified under the 1997 WHO guideline as dengue fever, dengue hemorrhagic fever, and dengue shock syndrome. Since some healthcare facilities in Indonesia still use the older classification, keeping the English phrase “warning signs” in the translated questionnaire helps nurses recognize the terminology. Other modifications enhanced clarity for public health nurses: “major problem” (Item 5) became “significant public health problem,” “morbidity” (Item 8) was replaced with “incidence,” and “alarming diagnosis” (Item 9) was changed to “alarming disease.” These refinements ensured consistency with commonly used terms in Indonesian dengue prevention programs.

Summary of item modifications

Face validity and content validity

Face validity was confirmed, with I-FVI values ranging from 0.93 to 1.00 and an S-FVI/Ave of 0.98, indicating that most items were considered clear and easy to understand by the 30 nurses in the pretesting phase. Although a small number of participants gave ratings below 3 on several items, no additional wording changes were required after pretesting.

Item relevancy, assessed by eight experts, showed I-CVI values of 1.00 for all items and an S-CVI/Ave of 1.00, indicating acceptable content validity. These perfect CVI values should be interpreted in light of the scoring method, in which ratings of both 3 and 4 were considered relevant. Thus, some experts rated several items as 3 rather than 4, but all items were still classified as relevant. In addition, several wording revisions had already been made during the expert review stage to improve clarity and cultural appropriateness. After reviewing the validity and reliability results, the experts suggested finalizing the 10-item DPAQ-I and proceeding with psychometric testing.

Phase 2: Psychometric testing

Demographic characteristics of participants

A total of 460 nurses provided their consent to participate in the study. However, two participants did not complete the questionnaires, resulting in a final sample of 458 respondents. Of these, 137 participants were included in the EFA, while 321 participants were included in the CFA (Table 2). The mean age (SD) of participants was 40.14 (9.42) years in the EFA group and 39.92 (9.49) years in the CFA group. Over 70% of participants in each group were female and held a diploma-level education. Most reported experience conducting dengue assessments and caring for dengue patients. More than 80% of participants in both groups had received dengue-related education during their formal education. However, fewer than 20% of participants had participated in dengue-specific in-service training.

Item analysis

The corrected item-total correlation values of the 10-item DPAQ-I ranged from 0.13 to 0.63. In this study, items with corrected item-total correlations of ≤ 0.30 were reviewed for potential removal. Three items met this criterion: Item 2 (r = .30), Item 3 (r = .13), and Item 6 (r = .15). These items were removed after considering both their statistical performance and conceptual alignment with the construct of dengue preventive attitudes. Therefore, subsequent factor analyses were conducted using the remaining seven items (Table 3).

Exploratory factor analysis (EFA)

EFA was conducted using the PAF and Promax rotation on the seven items DPAQ-I (Model 1). Results showed a KMO value of 0.80 and Bartlett’s test of sphericity (p < .001), confirming data suitability for factor analysis. Based on eigenvalues > 1.00, the scree plot, and factor loadings ≥ 0.32, the EFA identified two factors, explaining 52.53% of the total variance. Model 1 was identified: Factor 1 included Items 1, 4, 7, and 9, while Factor 2 included Items 5 and 8. Item 10 showed only a weak loading on Factor 1. Cronbach’s α for Model 1 was 0.80 for Factor 1 and 0.69 for Factor 2 (Table 4). While Factor 1 showed acceptable reliability, the reliability of Factor 2 was slightly below the recommended cutoff of 0.70. In addition, Factor 2 comprised only two items, which did not meet the minimum threshold of three items per factor required for a stable and interpretable factor structure [30, 31].

Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA)

Based on the two models generated during the EFA, CFA was performed on each model using data from 321 participants to determine the best-fitting structure. As shown in Table 5, the initial two-factor model (Model 1) demonstrated acceptable fit, whereas the initial one-factor model (Model 2) showed borderline fit. To improve the fit of Model 2, one residual covariance was added between Item 5 and Item 8 because this item pair showed the highest modification index and both items reflected closely related perceptions of dengue as a public health burden. The modified Model 2 then demonstrated acceptable fit. Although the initial Model 1 and the modified Model 2 showed the same fit indices, the modified Model 2 was selected as the final structure of the DPAQ-I (Fig. 1). This decision was based on the limited psychometric stability of Model 1, in which the second factor comprised only two items, and on the conceptual coherence of all seven retained items as a single broader construct of dengue preventive attitudes. The final model fit indices were χ² = 32.67, df = 13, p = .002, CMIN/df = 2.51, CFI = 0.95, TLI = 0.92, RMSEA = 0.07, and SRMR = 0.05 (Table 5).

Reliability

The final seven-item DPAQ-I demonstrated acceptable internal consistency reliability, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.75 and McDonald’s ω of 0.74 in the full psychometric testing sample (n = 458). In addition, the two-week test-retest reliability showed acceptable stability of the instrument over time, with an ICC of 0.75 (95% confidence interval = 0.47–0.88, p < .001) among the 30 participants in the pretesting phase.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to cross-culturally adapt the DPAQ questionnaire and examine its psychometric properties among nurses working in PHC units in Indonesia. The findings demonstrated that the seven-item DPAQ-I showed acceptable validity and reliability for evaluating nurses’ attitudes toward dengue prevention. While the cross-cultural adaptation finalized a 10-item DPAQ-I, psychometric testing reduced the questionnaire to seven items. The seven-item DPAQ-I measures attitudes as a single factor that reflects nurses’ perspectives of dengue prevention in PHC units.

The high face validity and content validity findings in the present study should be interpreted in the context of the review process. Although the final I-CVI and S-CVI/Ave values were perfect, this did not indicate an absence of feedback from the expert panel. Instead, several wording revisions were made to improve clarity and cultural appropriateness, as described in the Results section, particularly in relation to terminology used in Indonesian dengue prevention practice. In addition, because ratings of both 3 and 4 were considered indicative of item relevance in the CVI calculation, an item could achieve an I-CVI of 1.00 even when minor revisions were suggested. This indicates that expert feedback primarily focused on refining item wording rather than questioning the relevance of the items. Overall, the findings reflect strong agreement on content relevance following iterative refinement, rather than an absence of critical evaluation.

The original questionnaire was developed and validated among healthcare professionals working in Ethiopian PHC units [16]. However, it did not provide the construct validity, so the factor structure was not clear. Regarding the validity and reliability, the original version reported the content validity and internal consistency reliability, with Cronbach’s α of 0.73 for its 10 items [16]. In our study, removing three items resulted in a slightly higher Cronbach’s α of 0.75 among 458 participants, suggesting that these items did not significantly contribute to the scale. The items we removed were Item 2, “Do you think you are fully trained to manage a patient with an infection of dengue without warning signs?”; Item 3, “Do you think that eliminating the breeding site of the dengue-causing mosquito is complicated and time-consuming?”; and Item 6, “Do you think that dengue infection is difficult to detect?” Despite their relevance to dengue-related practice, these three items showed low correlation coefficients (r ≤ .30) during the item analysis and were conceptually less aligned with the core construct of preventive attitudes.

Item 2 focuses on attitudes toward nurses’ training adequacy for managing dengue cases without warning signs. This item demonstrated a low corrected item-total correlation, which suggests that it did not meaningfully contribute to the underlying construct and met the statistical criterion for item removal [26, 27]. One possible explanation is that most participants in this study had never attended dengue-specific training. With limited training exposure, participants were more likely to select neutral responses (3 on a 5-point scale), reducing response variability and weakening the item’s correlation with the overall attitude scale. Moreover, perceived clinical training reflects self-efficacy rather than a preventive attitude, making it less conceptually aligned with the construct measured by the scale. These statistical and conceptual considerations support the removal of Item 2 from the scale.

Item 3 reflects an important attitude regarding the dengue prevention program, specifically about eliminating mosquito breeding sites. In our study, most participants disagreed with the statement that eliminating breeding sites is complicated and time-consuming, indicating a positive attitude. In contrast, the Ethiopian study reported that most participants agreed with this statement, suggesting it was seen as a challenge [16]. This difference might be attributed to dengue control programs in Indonesia, which have actively involved community participation since 2000. Programs such as the mosquito eradication program (Pemberantasan Sarang Nyamuk / PSN) and “Juru Pemantau Jentik / Jumantik” (mosquito larva observers) encourage routine cleaning of homes and neighborhoods to eliminate mosquito breeding sites [38]. A study in Yogyakarta, Indonesia, found that about 90% of individuals practiced dengue prevention measures like cleaning water containers, covering them, and recycling discarded materials [39]. These common practices likely led to similar responses, with most participants selecting “disagree.” Consequently, Item 3 did not differentiate well among participants and did not meaningfully contribute to the overall measurement of attitudes, which justified the decision to remove it. Although eliminating mosquito breeding sites is part of dengue prevention, its low correlation suggests that nurses’ views on this operational challenge were not strongly aligned with the broader preventive attitude construct measured by the scale.

Item 6 evaluates whether dengue infection is difficult to detect. Most participants disagreed with the statement, indicating that dengue is considered easy to detect. This finding differs from the Ethiopian study, where participants viewed detection as more challenging [16]. In Indonesia, the availability of rapid diagnostic tests, such as the non-structural protein 1 (NS1) antigen test, in PHC units likely influenced this perception. A diagnostic evaluation study in Indonesia revealed that the NS1 had sensitivity ranging from 73% to 80% and specificity of 100% [40]. This rapid test is easy to administer and has higher sensitivity for detecting dengue during the first four days of fever [40]. If a rapid test is unavailable, nurses may use the tourniquet test, which has a low sensitivity of 58% and specificity of 71% but is still helpful in resource-limited settings [41]. Because this item relates more to diagnostic knowledge than to preventive attitudes, the limited conceptual fit combined with its weak statistical performance supported its removal.

Removing three items was a challenging decision, as the original questionnaire was already short with only 10 items. However, when both EFA and CFA were conducted using the complete 10-item version, the results consistently showed low factor loadings and weak correlation coefficients for these three items (Item 2, 3, and 6). These items showed corrected item-total correlations of ≤ 0.30 and were therefore reviewed for potential removal. Item 2 was at the threshold (r = .30), but it was removed based on both statistical performance and conceptual considerations. Specifically, it appeared to reflect perceived competence in dengue case management, which is conceptually closer to self-efficacy than to preventive attitude. Items 3 and 6 also showed weak statistical performance and appeared to reflect operational challenge and clinical knowledge, respectively, rather than attitudes toward dengue prevention. According to psychometric guidelines, items that demonstrate weak statistical performance and limited conceptually aligned with the underlying construct should be removed to strengthen construct validity and reduce measurement error [26, 27, 42, 43]. Removing these items improved the model fit and overall performance of the instrument. In addition, fewer items make the questionnaire more practical for nurses in clinical settings to complete. The resulting seven-item DPAQ-I provides a concise measure of nurses’ attitudes toward dengue prevention, which is relevant to public health practice.

In addition to the removed items, the seven retained items represent a coherent set of constructs that align well with the dengue preventive attitudes. These items capture nurses’ perceptions about the preventability of dengue (Item 1), perceived personal responsibility in prevention (Item 4), perceptions of disease severity and population impact (Items 5, 8, and 9), environmental conditions that support transmission (Item 7), and the broader societal consequences of dengue (Item 10). Although the retained items address different aspects of dengue prevention, they appear to reflect one broader attitude toward dengue prevention. These domains reflect key evaluative components that meaningfully contribute to the underlying latent construct of preventive attitudes. The EFA findings supported this interpretation. Although a two-factor solution initially emerged, the second factor was defined by only two items (Items 5 and 8), which did not meet the minimum threshold of three items per factor required for a stable and interpretable factor structure [30, 31], while Item 10 showed only a weak loading on Factor 1. This pattern suggested that the two-factor solution (Model 1) was not sufficiently stable or distinct to support a clear multidimensional structure. In contrast, the one-factor solution (Model 2) showed acceptable internal consistency and retained all seven items within a single broader construct. These findings suggest that the finalized seven-item scale provides a useful measure of nurses’ attitudes toward dengue prevention.

The CFA findings further supported the choice of a unidimensional structure. Results of the model fit were better for the initial two-factor model than for the initial one-factor model. However, the second factor in the two-factor model (Model 1) was represented by only two items, which limited its psychometric stability and interpretability. To improve the fit of the one-factor model (Model 2), one residual covariance was added between Items 5 and 8 because this item pair showed the highest modification index and both items reflected closely related perceptions of dengue as a public health burden [22, 32]. After this modification, the one-factor model demonstrated the same fit indices as the initial two-factor model. When the content of the retained items was considered, all seven items appeared to reflect related beliefs and perceptions concerning dengue prevention rather than clearly separate latent dimensions. Considering this, we treated the instrument as a single-factor model, with Cronbach’s α of 0.75, slightly higher than the original version. Therefore, the one-factor model was favored not only because of the weaker reliability of the second factor, but also because it provided a more coherent and interpretable representation of dengue preventive attitudes among public health nurses. Overall, the psychometric results indicate that the single-factor model provides the most reliable and conceptually appropriate structure for the DPAQ-I.

Limitations

Our study was conducted among nurses working in PHC units, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other healthcare professionals, such as physicians, midwives, and public health workers. In addition, convenience sampling was used, and participants were recruited through both online and offline approaches. Although participants were recruited from multiple provinces in Indonesia, the number of participants from each province was not proportional, and offline recruitment was limited to one province accessible to the research team. Therefore, the sample may not be fully representative of all public health nurses working in PHC units in Indonesia, and the generalizability of the findings should be interpreted with caution. Future studies are encouraged to use probability-based sampling methods and more geographically balanced recruitment strategies to enhance the sample representativeness. Moreover, although the final one-factor structure demonstrated acceptable model fit and reliability, the psychometric testing process involved item deletion, model selection, and the addition of one residual covariance during model refinement. Therefore, further validation using independent samples from more diverse geographic settings is recommended to confirm the stability and generalizability of the refined structure before broader application. In addition, although this study evaluated several important psychometric properties, other aspects of construct validity, such as convergent validity, discriminant validity, known-groups validity, and measurement invariance, were not examined. Therefore, further studies are needed to provide additional evidence for the scale’s measurement properties.

Conclusions

The seven-item DPAQ-I demonstrated acceptable validity and reliability for measuring attitudes toward dengue prevention among public health nurses in Indonesia. Total score ranges from 7 to 35, with a higher score indicating more positive attitudes. The instrument showed satisfactory psychometric properties, including the face validity, content validity, item analysis, construct validity, test-retest reliability, and internal consistency. Its concise format enhances feasibility and ease of use in busy clinical settings. The DPAQ-I may be useful in future cross-sectional and experimental studies, particularly for examining nurses’ attitudes toward dengue prevention. However, because the final structure was established through item reduction, model selection, and model refinement, further validation using independent samples from more diverse geographic settings is recommended before broader application.
