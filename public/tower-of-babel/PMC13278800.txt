Rescue transcatheter tricuspid valve replacement with a 56-mm expandable prosthesis following edge-to-edge repair: a case report
Authors: Rui Campante Teles, Márcia José Oliveira Presume, Regina Ribeiras, Pedro Araújo Gonçalves, Manuel de Sousa Almeida, Annagrazia Cecere, Mohammed Shahbaaz Khan, Giulia Bruno, Deepti Ranganathan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTricuspid regurgitation (TR) is a challenging condition, particularly in advanced stages. Transcatheter therapies are emerging as viable alternatives for selected patients.Case summaryWe present an 85-year-old woman with longstanding right heart failure symptoms and torrential TR who underwent edge-to-edge repair (T-TEER), complicated by single leaflet partial device attachment (SLDA). Her condition deteriorated, with readmission for decompensated heart failure. Given persistent torrential TR, clinical deterioration, and unsuitable anatomy for further leaflet-based repair due to septal leaflet plastering, she underwent successful transcatheter tricuspid valve replacement (TTVR) with a newly available 56-mm prosthesis. The patient experienced symptomatic and haemodynamic improvement.DiscussionThis case highlights the limitations of leaflet-based repair in anatomically complex TR and supports TTVR as an effective alternative even in SLDA cases. Introduction

Tricuspid regurgitation (TR) has been underrecognized and undertreated, despite its association with progressive right heart failure and poor long-term outcomes.1–3 In advanced stages, significant TR represents a therapeutic challenge, mainly due to patient comorbidities and right ventricular (RV) dysfunction.4–6 Transcatheter therapies have emerged as promising alternatives for patients previously managed conservatively.7–9 We present a case in which transcatheter tricuspid valve replacement (TTVR) offered a successful rescue strategy for an elderly patient with torrential TR following failed T-TEER.

Case presentation

An 85-year-old functionally independent woman presented with longstanding right-sided heart failure, secondary to torrential TR. She had a medical history of hypertension, dyslipidaemia, type 2 diabetes mellitus, permanent atrial fibrillation, and prior ischaemic stroke without residual deficits.

Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) in October 2021 showed severe dilation of the right atrium (143 mL/m2) and right ventricle with preserved RV longitudinal function, and torrential TR with a broad central jet mainly due to annular dilation. Left ventricular function was preserved. In early 2023, the patient reported progressive fatigue, exertional dyspnoea, orthopnoea, and peripheral oedema consistent with the New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional class III heart failure. Transoesophageal echocardiography (TOE) confirmed torrential secondary TR from a large central coaptation gap (14 mm), plastering of the septal leaflet, and severe annular dilation (54 mm in diastole). Mechanistically, it was consistent with atrial functional aetiology (Figure 1). Mild mitral regurgitation (MR) due to mild A2 scallop prolapse was also noted. Right heart catheterization revealed combined pre- and post-capillary pulmonary hypertension (mean pulmonary artery pressure 31 mmHg, pulmonary capillary wedge pressure 16 mmHg, pulmonary vascular resistance 4 Wood units). The TAPSE/sPAP ratio was 0.39 mm/mmHg. Obstructive coronary artery disease was excluded by invasive catheterization.

Echocardiographic evaluation before T-TEER. TOE shows severe annular dilation with a large central coaptation gap (panel A), central torrential TR (panel B), leaflet tethering, and septal leaflet plastering (panel C).

Given the patient’s high surgical risk (TRI-SCORE 4) and persistent symptoms despite optimal medical therapy, T-TEER was planned in an attempt to reduce TR, despite the unfavourable anatomy. At that time, no appropriately sized transcatheter tricuspid prosthesis was commercially available. T-TEER was performed in July 2024 with a TriClip® GT4 XTW at the antero-septal commissure. During the attempt to deploy a second clip, the initially implanted device partially detached from the septal leaflet, remaining attached only to the anterior leaflet (SLDA). Despite multiple attempts, placement of a second clip was not feasible due to the plastering of the septal leaflet. Post-TriClip TTE showed persistent torrential TR, with a large central jet and significant mobility of the partially detached clip. No complications were observed.

Over the following weeks, right-sided heart failure symptoms progressively worsened. She was admitted in late November 2024 with acute decompensated heart failure, presenting with NYHA class IV symptoms, global respiratory insufficiency, bilateral pleural effusions, and marked peripheral oedema. Laboratory tests revealed elevated NT-proBNP levels (3308 pg/mL), preserved renal function, and no significant inflammatory response. Chest radiography showed cardiomegaly and bilateral pleural effusions. TTE and TOE confirmed persistent torrential TR with partial detachment of the previously implanted clip (Figure 2; Supplementary material online, Videos S1 and S1). A significant increase in RV dimensions was observed (3D VTD 198 mL/m2; VTS 113 mL/m2), although RV systolic function remained relatively preserved (3D EF 43%). There was no MR worsening.

Echocardiographic evaluation after T-TEER. TOE in the transgastric view shows partial detachment of the implanted clip from the septal leaflet (panels A and B). TTE in the right ventricular inflow view demonstrates torrential TR (panels C and D).

Initial intravenous diuretics and oxygen therapy led to gradual improvement, but the patient remained NYHA class III.

The case was discussed by the multidisciplinary heart valve team. Following failed T-TEER, with persistent torrential TR and ongoing clinical deterioration, repeat leaflet-based repair was considered unsuitable due to severe annular dilatation, a large coaptation gap, and septal leaflet plastering. Given the patient’s high surgical risk, with a recalculated TRI-SCORE of 7, and the subsequent availability of a larger prosthesis size, rescue TTVR was considered the most appropriate treatment strategy. Suitability for TTVR was confirmed by multislice cardiac computed tomography (MSCT) assessment (Supplementary material online, Figure S1.1–S1.3).

In January 2025, the patient underwent successful TTVR with a 56-mm EVOQUE® prosthesis (Edwards Lifesciences). The device was delivered via a transfemoral approach under general anaesthesia, with transient vasoactive support. The safari wire was deployed into the RV apex, requiring a manual bend of the safari wire to achieve a correct nosecone positioning. Careful, step-by-step manipulation was necessary to ensure a proper trajectory, centralization, and depth of the delivering catheter, guided by combined fluoroscopic and TOE imaging, allowing a progressive exposure, engagement, and expansion of the nine anchors (Supplementary material online, Videos S2 and S2–S3). The prosthesis was secured to the annulus and leaflets, with no interaction with the native chordae. The SLDA TriClip was also engaged and immobilized inside the prosthesis anchors, ensuring stable implantation, without procedural complications (Figures 3 and 4). Immediate post-implant TOE confirmed a well-positioned prosthesis with a mean transvalvular gradient of 2–3 mmHg and a mild posterior paravalvular leak, opposite to SLDA (Supplementary material online, Video S3).

TOE in the mid-oesophageal view with X-plane, showing the 56 mm-prosthesis well seated after TTVR. Note a mild paravalvular regurgitant jet (star) well depicted in the septal-lateral view.

Intraprocedural fluoroscopic imaging (panel B) during intraprocedural EVOQUE valve implantation: progressive evertion of the prosthetic anchors until full leaflet engagement and deploy. Note the SLD-TriClip (star) grabbed inside the anchors (panel C).

Post-procedure, the patient remained haemodynamically stable and was transferred from the intensive care unit after 48 h. Before discharge, TTE demonstrated a well-seated prosthesis with mild posterior paravalvular leak, opposite to the detached clip, quantified by an effective regurgitant orifice of less than 20 mm2 (Supplementary material online, Video S4). Haemodynamic improvement was evident, with normalization of cardiac output and near-complete resolution of the left ventricular D-shape. The RV remained dilated with moderate systolic dysfunction (3D VTD 189 mL/m2, VTS 117 mL/m2, EF 38%, FAC 32%, and TAPSE 12 mm) (Supplementary material online, Videos S5 and S6).

During hospitalization, the patient showed clear improvement in symptoms, with no signs of recurrent congestion, and was subsequently discharged.

At the 6-month follow-up, no adverse events occurred, and the patient reported increased functional capacity, without signs of clinical congestion with furosemide 80 mg per day (NYHA I).

Discussion

In this case, the patient’s clinical deterioration following failed T-TEER illustrates the limitations of leaflet edge-to-edge techniques in TR.1,4 A large coaptation gap, severe annular dilation, and septal leaflet plastering reduce procedural success and are associated with poor outcomes.2,4 SLDA is a recognized complication in this setting8,9 and was likely related to the anatomical substrate rather than procedural factors, potentially further contributing to persistent severe TR.

With persistent torrential TR, ongoing clinical decline, and anatomy unsuitable for repeat leaflet-directed repair because of severe annular dilatation, a large coaptation gap, and septal leaflet plastering, valve replacement emerged as the most appropriate rescue strategy.5,7,8 The recent availability of the larger EVOQUE valve enabled TTVR in this case.8 Although a mild paravalvular leak was observed after implantation, it did not appear to affect early recovery, in keeping with emerging clinical data suggesting clinical benefit when TR burden is substantially reduced after TTVR.3,8

Furthermore, despite persistent RV dilatation and moderate dysfunction, the patient experienced symptomatic and haemodynamic improvement after TTVR. RV function assessed by echocardiography remained unchanged in the early post-procedural period. This likely reflects advanced myocardial remodelling, as well as the increased afterload following correction of severe TR, which may delay early RV recovery.8,10 The patient presented with combined pre- and post-capillary pulmonary hypertension, a haemodynamic profile associated with increased RV afterload, and limited RV reserve, which may attenuate reverse remodelling after TTVR.8 Importantly, the new-sized EVOQUE valve was successfully implanted despite the presence of a partially detached clip in situ, which was incorporated into the prosthetic frame.8

Overall, this case illustrates that TTVR may offer a safe and effective rescue solution in anatomically complex TR, even after failed leaflet-based repair.5,7,8
