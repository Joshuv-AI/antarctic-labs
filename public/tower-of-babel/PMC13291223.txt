The metabolic vulnerability index predicts outcomes in patients with metabolic dysfunction associated steatotic liver disease
Authors: Mohammad S Siddiqui, Mark L Van Natta, Margery A Connelly, Jeanne Clark, Brent A Neuschwander-Tetri, Anna Mae Diehl, Srinivasan Dasarathy, Rohit Loomba, Naga Chalasani, Kris V Kowdley, Bilal Hameed, Laura A Wilson, Katherine P Yates, Patricia Belt, David E Kleiner, Cynthia Behling, James Tonascia, Arun J Sanyal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe evaluated the prognostic performance of the metabolic vulnerability index (MVX), reflective of inflammation and amino acid dysmetabolism, in a cohort (n = 1613) with the full histological spectrum of MASLD. Over a median follow up of 4 years, MVX predicts all-cause mortality (H.R. 2.7 (95% CI = 2.1-3.5) for every 10-point increment; p < 0.001), liver-related mortality (H.R. 5.1 (95% CI = 2.8-9.1); p < 0.001), hepatic decompensation (H.R. 2.5 (95% CI = 1.8-3.4); p < 0.001), a rise in model for end-stage liver disease (MELD) score to ≥ 15 (H.R. 1.8 (1.4-2.2); p < 0.001) and a decline in eGFR ≥ 40% (H.R. 1.5 (95% CI = 1.2-1.8); p < 0.001). A combination of fibrosis stage and MVX is superior to fibrosis stage alone for prediction of all-cause mortality (AUROC 0.79 vs 0.72, p = 0.01), liver-related mortality (0.95 vs 0.84, p = 0.002) hepatic decompensation (0.88 vs 0.86, p = 0.01) and hepatocellular cancer (0.84 vs 0.78, p = 0.001). These data support further development of MVX as a prognostic biomarker in MASLD.Subject terms: Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, Prognostic markers

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is a growing cause of liver-related morbidity and mortality1,2. It is estimated that about 20–25% of adults have MASLD, previously called nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), but only a minority experience liver-related adverse health outcomes3. Identification of those most at risk of significant clinical outcomes is a cornerstone of current paradigms for evaluation and management of MASLD3.

Histological assessment of liver tissue sections is the reference standard for assessment of prognosis in those with MASLD, but has many limitations, which has spurred research to develop non-invasive tests (NITs) for this purpose1,2. The severity of fibrosis burden and the fibrosis stage, defined histologically, are critical determinants of prognosis3,4. The NITs that have been developed for prognostic purposes in MASLD have therefore largely attempted to reflect fibrosis burden in the liver. The fibrosis-4 (FIB-4) and liver stiffness measurement (LSM) by transient elastography are two of the most studied prognostic biomarkers for MASLD and are part of all major practice guidelines5,6. While both provide prognostic information, they also have specific limitations. LSM measurement requires access to either vibration-controlled transient elastography or MRI, which are not universally available even in resource-enriched regions such as North America and Western Europe. The FIB-4 is influenced by age and its performance is impacted by the presence of type 2 diabetes7,8. These provide a rationale to continue to explore additional biomarkers for various contexts of use within the MASLD population.

Prior histology-based studies have failed to establish a relationship between markers of disease activity, such as steatosis, hepatocellular ballooning, and lobular inflammation with outcomes9,10. Disease activity, however, drives the fibrogenic response and is an upstream driver of disease progression. Indeed, changes in histological activity are mirrored in changes in fibrosis over time, especially for those with early-stage disease11. Histological severity of activity tends to decrease with progression in to advanced fibrosis even though underlying metabo-inflammation remains active12,13. These observations provide a rationale to study non-hepatic markers of metabo-inflammation as prognostic biomarkers in patients with MASLD, especially those that can be deployed in any clinical setting.

The metabolic vulnerability index (MVX) is a proprietary biomarker panel based on two sub-panels; the inflammation vulnerability index (IVX), calculated from circulating levels of small-high density lipoprotein particles (S-HDL-P) and GlycA, and the metabolic malnutrition index (MMX), which is derived from circulating branched chain amino acids and citrate levels10. The IVX has been associated with cardiovascular outcomes and MMX has been related to sarcopenia both which commonly occur in those with MASLD10–12. In this study, we evaluated the prognostic performance of the MVX in a well-characterized cohort with the full histological spectrum of MASLD and long-term outcomes data available. These data provide foundational information to support future studies to establish its clinical utility both as a stand-alone test and as part of a hierarchical testing system.

Results

A total of 1773 patients were considered for this analysis (Fig. 1). Of these, 1633 individuals had a baseline plasma sample available for analysis. In twenty instances, the laboratory could not obtain a value for one or more analytes. A total of 1613 patients with MVX data and at least 48 weeks of follow-up were included for the current analysis.

Selection criteria for study population.

Patients with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MAFLD) were enrolled into the Database 2 noninterventional registry (DB2) conducted through the Nonalcoholic Steatohepatitis (NASH) Clinical Research Network (CRN).

The baseline demographics, clinical, laboratory, and liver histological data stratified by MVX quartiles are provided in Table 1. The overall mean ± S.D age was 52 ± 12 years and the majority were female (64%) and Caucasian (85%). The overall mean ± SD body mass index (BMI) was 34 ± 7 kg/m2. The proportion of those with more advanced fibrosis stage was higher in the higher quartiles, which also had higher proportion of white participants and smokers, and higher mean BMI, mean AST, mean GGT, and mean alkaline phosphatase and longer mean time from diagnosis to entry in to the study. However, the mean age of those in the highest quartile (50 years) was lower than in the lowest quartile (54 years).

For every 10-point increment in MVX, the adjusted hazard ratio for any hepatic decompensation event increased by 2.5 (1.8–3.4) while the adjusted hazard ratio of liver-related mortality increased by 5.1 (2.8–9.1). The likelihood of both outcomes increased with increasing values of both subcomponents of the MVX. Increasing MVX was associated with increased risk of rise of MELD score from ≤ 12 to ≥ 15 (HR = 1.8 (1.4, 2.2) and a 40% decline in eGFR (HR = 1.5 (1.2, 1.8)). MVX was, however, not related to the risk of hepatocellular or non-hepatic cancer or cardio-metabolic outcomes.

The overall mortality was 2.9 and 2.3% experienced hepatic decompensation during the study period. The risk of all-cause mortality, liver-related mortality, hepatic decompensation, and rise of MELD score to ≥ 15 was greatest in those with the highest quartile of MVX (Fig. 2).

Prognostic performance of MVXFor all-cause mortality and liver-related mortality, the overall area under the ROC (AUROC) were 0.76 (0.68–0.84) and 0.91 (0.86–0.96), respectively (Supplemental Table 1). For liver-related mortality, diagnostic statistics include a sensitivity of 0.92, specificity of 0.77, negative predictive value of 1.00, but a low positive predictive value of 0.03 at the Youden index cut-point of 49.9. The performance of MVX was qualitatively similar for hepatic decompensation (AUROC 0.71 (0.62–0.8)) and rise of MELD score to ≥ 15 (0.64 (0.57–0.72)). While the MVX did not predict future HCC (Table 2), those in the top two quartiles had a shorter time to development of HCC (Fig. 2) with an overall AUROC of 0.73 (0.63–0.83). The overall performance was qualitatively similar for Cox versus logistic regression (Supplemental Table 2). An increase in MVX was associated with 40% decline in eGFR in whites (HR = 1.50) but not in non-whites (HR = 0.95) (interaction p = 0.0001), but no other interactions between MVX with race, age, sex, BMI, T2DM, or use of statins were present (Supplemental Table 3). In sub-group analysis of patients with and without cirrhosis, MVX remained significantly associated with future risk of liver-related mortality (cirrhosis HR 3.12, 1.30–7.48, p = 0.01 and fibrosis stages 0–3 HR 8.76, 3.25–23.56, p < 0.0001).

The AUROC of MVX for all-cause mortality, liver-related mortality, rise in MELD scores to 15 or higher and development of hepatocellular cancer was comparable to fibrosis stage. Comparative prognostic performance of MVX to FIB-4

The MVX was comparable to the FIB-4 index in its ability to predict all-cause mortality, liver-related mortality, and an increase in MELD score to ≥ 15, but was inferior to FIB-4 for prediction of future hepatic decompensation and hypertension (Table 4 and supplemental Table 5). Further, a combination of MVX and FIB-4 was not statistically superior to FIB-4 alone although the AUROC for prediction of all-cause mortality and liver-related mortality were numerically higher.

Discussion

The qualification of a biomarker is a specific process used by federal regulatory agencies that includes the initial demonstration of its clinical performance, followed by assessment of its clinical utility, which includes assessment of how it would be used in a given setting, e.g., as first-line or as a secondary testing tool, the clinical decisions that would be made based on the read-out and the related benefits and risks14. In this long journey, the demonstration of clinical performance for its “fit for purpose” context of use is a critical first step.

The current study represents this “first-step” for the use of the MVX as a prognostic biomarker for MASLD. It demonstrates that the MVX predicts all-cause mortality, liver-related deaths, hepatic decompensation, a decline in eGFR, and rise in MELD score to 15 or higher in MASLD. Its prognostic utility is similar to histological assessment of fibrosis stage with the exception of predicting hepatic decompensation. The prognostic utility of the MVX was independent of underlying fibrosis stage and the combination of histological fibrosis staging and MVX was superior to histological assessment alone for mortality, decompensation, and particularly for identifying those that developed hepatocellular cancer. These findings have several important implications.

First, this demonstrates the ability of the MVX to predict the future development of key clinical events of interest and could provide clinicians with a validated tool to assist decision-making. Next, it can be used to define enrichment strategies for outcome risk in clinical trials within clinical trial populations. As expected, despite excellent sensitivity (92%) and specificity (77%) for liver-related mortality, given the low rates of events, the negative predictive values of MVX are very high, while the positive predictive values are low.

The current regulatory path for drug development in MASLD requires demonstration of histological improvement followed by demonstration of clinical benefit15. Different clinical trials have focused on patients with different stages of disease or only cirrhosis for outcomes studies. The current study raises the possibility that MVX could be used to either stratify the population or even define the population if it is identified solely by NITs. The ability to predict outcomes of interest by MVX with the same accuracy as histological stage provides additional rationale to begin to move away from the use of liver biopsy-based risk stratification purposes or to further enhance risk prediction from histological assessment.

A key question is how MVX may be used or integrated in to management algorithms for MASLD. The FIB-4, which is well established, had similar prognostic ability to identify risk of all-cause and liver-related mortality16,17. While FIB-4 is a key starting point in risk stratification, its performance is limited in certain populations, e.g., younger patients or those with type 2 diabetes7,8. Also, while vibration-controlled transient elastography is recommended as a second-line test1, it is not universally available. The prognostic performance of MVX was not superior to FIB-4 overall and is therefore unlikely to replace FIB-4 as a starting point for risk stratification of those with MASLD. However, given that the mean age of those with the highest quartile of scores for MVX picked up younger individuals, it raises the potential for providing valuable information in such populations where FIB-4 may not be optimal. The robust prognostic performance of MVX provides a rationale for further evaluation in larger, well-defined populations of young individuals with MASLD and in those with T2DM. Importantly, the prognostic performance of MVX also indicated its ability to risk-stratify those with MASLD without the need for a liver biopsy.

The current study also shifts the central conceptual paradigm that only fibrosis stage predicts outcomes in MASLD. Several prior studies focused on histological assessment of disease activity and fibrosis found only fibrosis to be relevant for this purpose6. However, conventional histological assessment does not capture the metabolic and inflammatory components completely and are likely to under-estimate the metabo-inflammatory nature of MASLD. The components of MVX capture changes in metabolism and inflammation and provides proof of concept that metabo-inflammatory markers affect outcomes10. They further predicted outcomes after correcting for their collinear relationship to fibrosis. This is expected to promote additional studies on the role of metabo-inflammation as a trigger of decompensation and further progression to liver-related death. These findings may be consistent with the observation that many of the pharmacological approaches to treat NASH with encouraging preliminary data, such as FGF-21 analogues and PPAR agonists, target global dysmetabolism.

It is noteworthy that while the MVX was developed to identify cardiovascular outcomes10–12, it did not predict such outcomes in the current study. A potential explanation is that the MVX was developed for populations with very high risk for cardiovascular events and the overall risk profile of this cohort was more mixed, translating into significant lower cardiovascular events in the NASH Clinical Research Network (CRN) cohort compared to prior published literature that focused on patients who already had heart failure or clinically significant CAD10,12. Moreover, in our prior publication from this cohort, cardiovascular events did not increase with increasing fibrosis stage4.

There are several strengths of this study that support the validity of the findings. This cohort has been well characterized and the liver histology assessed by a validated protocol-driven manner18,19. The clinical outcomes of interest were pre-defined and adjudicated centrally using pre-specified definitions, which has been previously published4. The studies were well within assay stability parameters and all samples were run simultaneously, avoiding batch-effects. The MVX and component scores were generated by a group without access to any clinical data and data analyses were performed by the NASH CRN data coordinating center without any involvement of the manufacturer of the MVX (Labcorp), supporting the rigor of the study.

The principal limitation of this study is that the study population was largely white and caution is needed to extrapolate the findings to other races. The robustness of the models were established by both bootstrap and the use of AIC for the models. Also, the model performance at specific cutoffs and sub-populations (i.e., diabetes, ethnicity) will require validation in independent cohorts in the future. No association between MVX and non-liver clinical outcomes (e.g., cardiac, malignancy, renal) were noted; however, this is likely due to significantly lower incidence of these clinical events in the study cohort, for example, non-hepatic malignancy and CVD occurred in less than 5% of the study cohort.

In conclusion, the current study provides novel data to support the prognostic utility of the MVX in MASLD. Its performance rivals that of histological assessment for this purpose for all endpoints other than hepatic decompensation and is also similar to that of the FIB-4 index. The MVX retains its prognostic value even after accounting for the collinearity between MVX scores and fibrosis severity. Together, these provide foundational data to support further evaluation of the MVX as a prognostic tool and studies on the role of metabo-inflammation in driving decompensation and liver-related mortality in MASLD.

Materials and methods

The current study is an ancillary study of the NAFLD Database 2 (DB2) (NCT # 01030484) of the NIDDK NASH CRN. The analysis of MVX was performed in samples and data collected prospectively in the course of the DB2 registry study. All patients provided informed consent for such analyses at the time of enrollment and the DB2 study was approved by individual site institutional review boards and NIDDK. This study was conceived, implemented, analyzed, and reported entirely by the investigators who are responsible for the contents of this manuscript. The studies are reported in compliance with TRIPOD guidance for rigor and transparency in reporting biomarker studies13.

Context of use

The context of use was to define the prognostic performance of MVX in individuals with MASLD. This is expected to allow risk-stratification using MVX in those with MASLD to inform risk-based clinical management strategies.

Patient population

The DB2 study included individuals with biopsy-proven MASLD within the previous 90 days of enrollment and with at least one follow-up visit after 48 weeks of follow-up. Based on AUDIT questionnaires, all participants were considered to be consuming less than 20 gm of alcohol daily for women and 30 gm per day for men14. Patients with other causes of chronic liver diseases, prior hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), liver transplantation, or bariatric were excluded. Patients were provided uniform standard of care recommendations across sites.

Histological assessment

Baseline liver histology was evaluated by consensus of the pathology committee of the NASH CRN using a validated protocol-driven approach that has been previously published15,16. The presence of steatotic liver disease, fatty liver versus steatohepatitis, its activity, and fibrosis stage were identified and scored using the NASH CRN scoring system15.

Clinical meta-data and outcomes assessment

Patients were seen for research visits every 48 weeks and clinical outcomes were captured during these visits or when reported at interim time points. All data were captured in case report forms and outcomes adjudicated centrally as previously described14. The principal outcomes were all cause and liver-related mortality, hepatic decompensation events (overt encephalopathy, ascites, or variceal hemorrhage), an increase in the model for end-stage liver disease (MELD) score from <12 to ≥ 15, hepatic and non-hepatic cancers, coronary artery disease, cerebrovascular events, hypertension, and decline in eGFR by 40%. The operational definitions for these have been previously described by us14. The association between histology and clinical outcomes have been previously reported14.

Measurement of the metabolic vulnerability index

Plasma samples in heparin obtained in fasted state during the enrollment visit were analyzed by Labcorp, who provided this service free of cost to the NASH CRN and without access to any clinical meta-data under a research agreement with Virginia Commonwealth University. None of the study investigators had received funding from Labcorp. The MVX, IVX, and MMX were calculated by Labcorp from concentrations of S-HDL-P, GlycA, leucine, isoleucine, valine, and citrate using proprietary algorithms. The indices were scaled from 0 to 100 and stratified by sex with higher values indicating worse disease. The scores for the indices were provided to the investigators by Labcorp, who remain masked to individual patient level data.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were measured and compared across MVX quartiles using Cochran’s chi-square test for trend for binary covariates and linearly regressing a covariate on MVX quartile modelled ordinally for continuous covariates. Event rates were computed as number of events per 100 person-years of follow-up in those who had not experienced the event prior to enrollment. Hazard ratios per 10-unit increase with 95% confidence limits and p-values were obtained from Cox regression time to event (death, liver-related mortality, hepatic decompensation, MELD score increase to ≥ 15, coronary artery disease, cerebrovascular event, hepatocellular cancer, extrahepatic cancer, hypertension, and 40% decline in estimated GFR), adjusting for age, race, sex, and ethnicity. Separate regression analysis was performed, accounting for the distribution of fibrosis stages across various MVX strata to determine if any prognostic information was independent of fibrosis. Cross-validated (jackknife leave one out procedure) area under receiving operating characteristic (AUROC) curves with 95% confidence limits and sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value and negative predictive value were obtained from 3 separate logistic regressions regressing death or clinical events on (1) MVX, (2) histological fibrosis stage, and (3) MVX and fibrosis stage comparing models (1) vs (2) and (2) vs (3). Logistic regression was adjusted for age and gender. AUROCs using Cox regression were qualitatively similar to those using logistic regression. The cutoff used were calculated using Youden index. Additional AUROC analyses were performed with (1) MVX, (2) FIB-4 (a commonly used laboratory aid based on age, AST, ALT and platelet counts that is a surrogate measure for fibrosis stage and also related to mortality and liver outcomes17,18 and (3) MVX and FIB-4 together as predictor variables comparing models (1) vs (2) and (2) vs (3). The relationship of mortality and clinical events on MVX was further examined by subgrouping on age, race, sex, BMI, Type 2 diabetes status, and statin use using p < 0.01 as indicator of significant interaction. Given the exploratory nature of the analysis, p-values are nominal and not adjusted for multiplicity. The data analysis for this paper was generated using both SAS (SAS version 9.4, SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC) and Stata software (StataCorp. 2017. Stata Statistical Software: Release 15.1 College Station, TX: StataCorp LLC).
