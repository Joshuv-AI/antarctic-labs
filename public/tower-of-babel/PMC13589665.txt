User‐Generated Google Maps Ratings, State Staffing and Training Regulations, and the Quality of Hospice Care in Assisted Living Communities
Authors: Wenhan Guo, Shubing Cai, Yue Li, Brian E. McGarry, Thomas V. Caprio, Helena Temkin‐Greener
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundHospice services are growing among Medicare beneficiaries, yet concerns remain about the hospice quality for residents in assisted living (AL) communities. Little is known about how AL characteristics and state regulations are associated with hospice care in these settings.ObjectivesTo examine (1) whether AL user‐generated google maps ratings (AL google maps ratings) and (2) state‐level regulations on AL staffing are associated with hospice ratings:MethodsWe conducted a cross‐sectional analysis of 15,947 AL communities. We included data on AL‐specific direct care worker (DCW) state staffing and staff training regulations. Logistic regression models were used to assess associations, adjusting for AL, hospice, and market‐level characteristics.ResultsA one‐star increase in an AL google maps rating was associated with 6% higher odds of being served by hospices with higher HIS scores (OR = 1.06, p = 0.032), but not with CAHPS scores. AL communities in states with higher regulatory specificity for DCW staffing had higher odds of being served by hospices with higher CAHPS (OR = 1.46, p < 0.001) and HIS scores (OR = 1.35, p < 0.001). ALs in states mandating more than 21 h of DCW training, AL communities were more likely to be served by hospices with higher CAHPS scores (OR = 1.33, p < 0.001).ConclusionHigher AL google maps ratings were associated with higher hospice HIS performance. DCW staffing regulations were associated with both CAHPS and HIS, and DCW training regulations were associated with CAHPS only. These findings underscore the role of organizational quality and state regulatory policies in guiding end‐of‐life care in AL communities.

Background

Hospice use has grown substantially, with over 1.7 million Medicare beneficiaries and $23.7 billion in spending in 2022, up from 530,000 beneficiaries and $2.9 billion in 2000 [1, 2, 3]. Hospice use is especially prevalent among beneficiaries in assisted living (AL) communities, where more than 65% of decedents elect hospice before death [4, 5, 6]. However, AL residents often report lower‐quality hospice care than those in other settings such as the community or nursing home [7, 8, 9, 10], and tend to have longer hospice stays [10]. Given this widespread use and the documented variation in quality, evidence on the AL‐related factors associated with higher‐quality hospice care is needed.

One important determinant of hospice quality may be the staffing characteristics of the AL. In addition to any care provided by hospice providers, AL hospice recipients receive daily care from AL, including symptom monitoring, administration of medications, and relaying changes in residents' conditions to hospice providers and family members [6]. Around 80% of staff working in AL communities are direct care workers [11]. Because hospice providers are not continuously on‐site, AL staff may substitute by performing monitoring and care tasks that hospice staff would otherwise handle. While actual data on AL staffing levels are rarely available, prior studies have shown that more stringent staffing regulations were associated with improved EOL outcomes for AL residents [6, 12, 13, 14]. ALs operating under stronger regulatory environments may therefore be better positioned to fulfill these on‐site care responsibilities, serving as a more effective partner for hospice providers.

A separate factor may relate to AL‐hospice partnerships. ALs with better quality of care may be more selective in choosing hospice partners, preferring higher‐quality providers. Although direct empirical evidence supporting this argument remains limited, the public availability of hospice quality ratings may make such selection easier, and previous discussions have suggested that hospice referrals may increase when both AL facilities and hospices recognize the importance of high‐quality EOL care [6, 8].

Prior literature has established associations between state AL staffing regulations and the quality of AL care or EOL care. Existing literature already showed that state staffing regulations are associated with EOL transitions and higher hospice enrollment [12, 13]. A recent study suggests that staffing regulations in AL settings may also be associated with both the frequency of hospice visits and the quality of care delivered by hospice providers [14]. The regulatory environment may also shape how AL communities and hospice providers form partnerships, as higher‐quality hospices may be more likely to align with better‐resourced AL settings, consistent with the mechanisms described above. But this relationship has received very little research attention. Google Maps ratings and state DCW regulations capture different but complementary aspects of the AL care environment, and examining both offers a fuller view of what shapes hospice care for AL residents.

Unlike nursing homes, AL communities are mainly regulated at the state‐level and lack standardized federal‐level reporting of quality metrics [15]. User‐generated google maps ratings represent a scalable proxy for AL quality, with prior research demonstrating their association with quality of care in long‐term care settings [16, 17]. Using this measure, along with the well‐established quality metrics from consumer assessment of healthcare providers and systems (CAHPS) and hospice item set (HIS) surveys [7, 18, 19], and motivated by these gaps in the literature, we examined: (1) whether AL ratings, as captured by Google Maps data, are related to the ratings of hospice providers serving these communities; and (2) whether variations in state regulations on direct care workers' staffing and training were associated with differences in hospice quality ratings. Given the limited availability of data on hospice and end‐of‐life care in AL settings, this is an exploratory study without a specific directional hypothesis.

Methods

Data

Building on previous work [20], we identified a national cohort of AL residents using CY2019 master beneficiary summary files and aggregated AL‐level characteristics from this cohort, consistent with established methods in the literature [7, 21, 22].

AL google maps ratings was derived from google maps 5‐star reviews, ranging from 1 to 5 [17, 23]. Prior research has linked these ratings to nursing home compare ratings in long‐term care settings [16]. They may also signal quality in AL communities, with review patterns varying alongside community‐ and state‐level measures associated with AL quality [17, 23]. AL communities in our sample had a median of 7 reviews, with substantial variation across communities (range: 1–376). We identified reviews for approximately 90% of ALs nationwide (16,244 of 18,197).

Hospice characteristics and quality were derived from CMS's CareCompare website, which publishes two quality ratings as follows: the CAHPS Hospice Survey, collecting caregiver‐reported ratings [24]; and the HIS, a claims‐based measure of standardized items collected at each admission [25]. We included HIS measures from Q3 2018 to Q2 2019 and CAHPS Hospice Survey data from Q3 2017 to Q2 2019.

State regulatory data were obtained from the 2019 National Center for Assisted Living (NCAL) Regulatory Review and from previously published research [26, 27]. Information on county‐level poverty rates was derived from CY2019 Area Health Resources Files.

Analytical Sample

We began with a sample of 18,197 AL communities in the United States. We excluded those with no residents receiving hospice care (identified through Medicare hospice claims), reducing the sample to 16,596, then removed communities with missing data on key variables (N = 416).

At the hospice level, we excluded providers that did not meet CMS public reporting thresholds: fewer than 50 completed CAHPS surveys per reporting period, or fewer than 30 HIS‐eligible admissions. AL communities served exclusively by such hospices were dropped from the analytic sample (N = 233). The presence of non‐reporting providers was limited. Only 1.9% of decedents in the AL during CY2018–2019 received care from these hospices. Furthermore, these providers served at least a quarter of decedents in only 2.2% of the AL sample and at least half in just 1.5%.

The final analytic sample included 15,947 AL communities.

Outcome Variables

The outcomes of interest were the aggregated HIS composite scores and aggregated average CAHPS scores of all hospice providers serving each AL. The HIS composite score reflects the proportion of patients for whom a provider met all CMS‐defined process indicators at admission (e.g., pain and dyspnea screening and treatment, treatment preferences, bowel regimen for opioid recipients) [25]. The average CAHPS score was the mean of “top box” scores (percentage selecting the most positive response) across eight measures including hospice rating, willingness to recommend, emotional and spiritual support, symptom management, communication, and timely help [24].

Because the number of residents each hospice served varied across hospice providers within an AL, we constructed an AL‐level resident‐weighted mean of hospice ratings by averaging the HIS and CAHPS scores of all hospice providers that delivered services to that AL community in calendar year 2019, weighting each provider's score by the number of residents it served in that community. This weighting approach was designed to account for the varying levels of involvement hospice providers have within each community [7].

We dichotomized the outcomes at their national medians (CAHPS = 81.2; HIS = 95.3) to identify ALs served by hospices exceeding national benchmarks, which also accommodates the upward skew that limits variation at the upper end of the scale.

Key Independent Variables

AL google maps ratings, described in Section 2.1, were modeled as a continuous measure from 1.0 to 5.0.

Guided by previous literature [13, 14, 26], state AL regulations on direct care worker staffing and training were also included. Regulations on direct care worker staffing were categorized by whether a state specified a required staff‐to‐resident ratio for direct care workers (yes vs. no). Direct care worker training regulations were categorized based on whether a state required more than 21 h of training versus fewer hours [26].

These definitions and categorization of state regulations are shown in Table S1.

Other Covariates

We controlled for the AL‐level median resident age and percentage of residents who were dually eligible for Medicare and Medicaid, consistent with previous studies on end‐of‐life care in AL [13, 14]. AL bed size was also included as a proxy for AL resource availability [13, 14]. Lastly, we controlled for percentage of residents with Alzheimer's disease, schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, or depression/anxiety as individuals with these conditions may experience lower quality of care [14, 28].

Hospice‐level covariates, including percentage of for‐profit hospices, average daily census, and average proportion of AL residents served [7, 29], were aggregated to the AL level using the same resident‐weighted approach described for the outcomes. Market‐level covariates included the Herfindahl–Hirschman Index (HHI) for hospice providers (expressed as 1‐HHI), capturing local hospice market competition, and county‐level poverty rates, adjusting for socioeconomic conditions that may shape access to hospice care [30, 31].

Statistical Analysis

All analysis was conducted at the AL level. Descriptive comparisons were made between AL communities with a google maps rating below 4.0 and those with a rating of 4.0 or higher using t‐tests and Pearson χ 2 tests. The cut‐off point of 4.0 was selected as individual google maps ratings can only be whole numbers, and it is also close to the median rating in our sample of 4.2.

Logistic regression models were used to examine associations between each outcome and AL google maps ratings, as well as state regulations. All models included county‐level random effects to account for clustering of hospice providers within counties.

As a sensitivity check, we repeated the regression analysis using linear models treating the outcomes as continuous variables (as originally reported by CMS) to assess the robustness of our findings. Results from these alternative models are presented in Table S2.

All analyses were performed using statistical software SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC). This study was reviewed and approved by the University of Rochester Institutional Review Board.

Results

Descriptive Analysis

The results of descriptive analysis, stratified by google maps ratings, are reported in Table 1. ALs with higher google maps ratings (≥ 4.0 stars) were more likely to be served by hospices with above‐median CAHPS scores (52.1% vs. 48.8%, p < 0.001) and above‐median HIS composite scores (54.3% vs. 51.5%, p < 0.001). They also had fewer dual‐eligible residents and smaller bed size and were served by hospices with lower average AL volume (all p < 0.001).

State regulations requiring ALs to have DCW staff in proportion to residents were associated with a 46% higher odds of being served by hospices with higher average CAHPS scores (≥ 81.2; OR = 1.46, p < 0.001) and a 35% increase in the odds of having higher average HIS scores (OR = 1.35, p < 0.001). AL communities located in states with regulations requiring more training for DCW staff had 33% higher odds of being served by hospices with higher average CAHPS scores (OR = 1.33, p < 0.001), but the association with HIS scores was not statistically significant.

We also found ALs with a higher share of dual‐eligible residents were more likely to be served by hospices that had lower average CAHPS scores (OR = 0.948, p < 0.001) and lower average HIS (OR = 0.978, p = 0.038). ALs in more competitive hospice markets (measured by 1‐HHI) were more likely to be served by hospices with higher average CAHPS (OR = 1.54, p < 0.001) and higher average HIS (OR = 1.63, p < 0.001). A 10‐point increase in county poverty rate was associated with lower odds of being served by hospices with higher average CAHPS (OR = 0.664, p < 0.001) and lower odds of higher average HIS (OR = 0.820, p < 0.001).

Sensitivity Analysis

Sensitivity analyses with continuous outcomes are reported in Table S2. Findings were largely consistent with the logistic models. The google maps and HIS association remained significant, while the dual‐eligible and county‐poverty associations with HIS weakened and became non‐significant.

Average marginal effects from population‐averaged generalized estimating equations models, presented in Table S3, and were consistent in direction and statistical significance with the odds ratios reported in Table 2.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine associations between AL google maps ratings, state AL regulations, and the quality of hospice providers serving AL residents. Prior work has linked state staffing and training regulations to end‐of‐life outcomes in AL [4, 7, 32]. Building on this, we found that ALs with higher google maps ratings and those in states with more specific DCW staffing and training regulations were more likely to be served by hospices performing better on at least one process measure (CAHPS or HIS).

For AL residents, hospice care is delivered where they reside, so its effectiveness may depend on routine coordination with AL staff, such as communication, visit scheduling, and resolving interruptions in multi‐site workflows [33]. Therefore, hospice providers may also prefer to partner with AL communities with higher staffing and training levels, which could enhance their ability to oversee and support their hospice patients. The relationship between state regulatory requirements for staffing and training and the performance on CAHPS and HIS process measures supports this interpretation.

Our findings also highlight the role DCW training and staffing may play in perceived quality among AL hospice recipients. For staffing regulations, requiring more AL staff on site may support the workflow of hospices, symptoms monitoring, and communication [6]. The association between staffing regulations and HIS may operate through a different pathway: ALs with greater on‐site staffing capacity are likely better positioned to coordinate hospice admissions, support timely completion of intake assessments, and partner with hospices that more reliably document HIS process measures at admission. Training regulations, however, were associated with CAHPS but not HIS. CAHPS captures caregiver‐perceived experiences such as communication, emotional support, and family communication, dimensions that are more sensitive to day‐to‐day interactions between AL staff and hospice teams. Better‐trained staff may therefore be more effective partners in these interpersonal aspects of EOL care, even with little involvement in the hospice‐led intake processes captured by HIS.

A separate mechanism may also be at play through the matching of AL communities and hospice providers. Stricter regulatory environments may attract or retain higher‐quality hospice providers through a sorting process, as higher‐resourced ALs operating under stronger regulatory oversight may be better positioned to evaluate and select hospice partners, potentially creating a spillover effect that extends the reach of AL regulations to external providers.

A few implications can be drawn from our study. First, the AL regulatory environment may be associated with hospice quality in ways that go beyond what individual ALs directly control. Hospice care delivery in AL settings may be partly a system‐level outcome, shaped by a combination of hospice characteristics, AL quality, and state policy, not just hospice performance alone. Second, current hospice quality metrics (CAHPS and HIS) are designed to evaluate hospice providers in isolation, but our findings suggest that performance in AL settings is also a product of the care environment in which hospice is delivered. This raises questions about whether existing quality metrics adequately account for the settings in which hospices operate, and whether site‐of‐care specific benchmarks may be warranted. Third, while this study did not examine referral processes directly, the association between AL google maps Ratings and hospice quality ratings suggests that referrals are unlikely to be random, and may reflect AL communities' organizational capacity and partner‐selection patterns. Lastly, our study adds to a growing body of evidence on the broader implications of AL regulations for external care partners. EOL care in AL settings involves multiple providers, and policymakers may benefit from a more coordinated approach across the AL‐hospice interface rather than treating these as separate regulatory domains.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this study is observational and does not assess causal relationships. Second, our analyses rely on data from CY2019. Practice patterns may have shifted since then. Also, Google Maps ratings reflect cumulative experiences over time, while CAHPS and HIS measures cover defined reporting windows. This temporal mismatch is a known limitation of using online reviews alongside time‐bounded quality metrics, and findings should be interpreted accordingly. Third, the potential for omitted variable bias remains, given the limited scope of publicly available data on key AL community characteristics, such as staffing patterns, ownership structures, or service models. Our state regulation variables also only measured the presence of policies at the state level but do not capture whether or how consistently these policies are implemented at the facility level. Fourth, we acknowledge that hospice providers may provide services in multiple counties, and including random effects in our model may not fully adjust for differences in practice patterns. Furthermore, the CAHPS and HIS data used in this study primarily reflect responses from larger and more established hospices. As such, smaller hospices or those newer to the market—some of which may operate in the AL setting—may not be fully represented in our analysis. These hospices only accounted for a small proportion of decedents and AL communities in the sample. Therefore, their exclusion is unlikely to introduce significant bias into our conclusions. However, as these providers may differ in unmeasured claims‐based quality, the possibility of some residual bias cannot be entirely excluded. Lastly, both the HIS composite and CAHPS scores exhibited ceiling effects, with national medians of 95.3 and 81.2, respectively, reflecting limited variation across hospice providers, particularly for HIS. This compression at the upper end may have reduced statistical power to detect associations, and dichotomizing outcomes at national medians only partially addressed this constraint.

Conclusion

AL communities with higher Google Maps Ratings were more likely to be served by hospices with higher performance on at least one process measure. State staffing regulations were associated with both HIS and CAHPS, while training regulations were associated with CAHPS only. These findings underscore the potential relationships between AL and hospice providers and the role of state AL regulations in end‐of‐life care.
