Residual Burden in Adalimumab-Treated Patients with Hidradenitis Suppurativa: An Observational Cohort Study Based on Insurance Claims in the USA
Authors: Maria A Aleshin, Tanja Tran, Ingrid Pansar, Aijing Shang, David Friesen, Alexa B Kimball
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionHidradenitis suppurativa (HS), a chronic, inflammatory skin disease, causes significant morbidity. Until 2023, adalimumab was the only US-approved biologic for patients with moderate to severe HS.AimThis study describes the clinical profile, treatment utilization, and residual disease burden in patients with HS initiating adalimumab in the USA.MethodsThis observational cohort study used data from the Merative MarketScan® Commercial, Medicare, and Multi-State Medicaid claims databases. Adult patients who initiated adalimumab treatment between 2015 and 2018 were included (main cohort). Patients required continuous insurance enrolment during the year prior to adalimumab initiation (baseline period). Patients were followed until adalimumab discontinuation or study end (2022) to examine treatment patterns, discontinuation rates, predictors of discontinuation, and healthcare resource utilization (HCRU). Study objectives were repeated from the onset of the coronavirus 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic (COVID-19 cohort; 2020–2022). Statistical methods included Kaplan–Meier estimates and a multivariable proportional hazard regression model.ResultsThe main cohort included 2367 adult patients (70.4% Commercial/Medicare-insured and 29.6% Medicaid-insured). Key Points

This study showed that while adalimumab helps patients manage their HS, there is still a large need for accessible treatments with a durable effect.Around six in ten patients stopped using adalimumab within a year of starting it.Women, younger patients, and those with Medicaid insurance were more likely to stop using adalimumab.Despite showing a reduction in the use of healthcare services, patients often still needed additional treatments such as antibiotics and surgeries. Introduction

Hidradenitis suppurativa (HS) is a chronic, relapsing, disabling skin disease characterized by painful inflammatory nodules, abscesses, and draining tunnels; HS affects ~0.4–1.0% of the population globally [1–5]. In addition to negatively impacting patients’ quality of life, HS is associated with significant co-morbidities, including cardiovascular disease, arthritis and other inflammatory conditions, and depression/anxiety [2–4]. Clinical management of HS is challenging and often involves a combined approach including both medical and surgical modalities [6].

Over the last decade, therapeutic approaches have evolved rapidly and include the use of topical therapies, systemic antibiotics, hormonal therapies, and a wide range of immunomodulating medications [6]. Despite therapeutic advancements, some patients may not respond to biologic treatment, or lose treatment response over time, requiring additional care and/or treatments for their HS [7, 8]. Drug discontinuation can be a proxy measurement of patient response to treatment and access to healthcare resources and medications [9]

From 2015 until 2023, adalimumab, an immunoglobulin (Ig)G1 monoclonal antibody targeting tumor necrosis factor-alpha, was the only approved biologic for patients with moderate to severe HS in the USA [7, 10]. Despite the effectiveness of adalimumab, patients with HS treated with adalimumab can require additional interventions including antibiotics, antihormonal agents, surgeries, pain medications, and other biologics [11].

This study was designed to describe the clinical profile of patients with HS initiating adalimumab treatment in routine clinical practice in the USA from 2015 to 2018 (main cohort) and from 2020 to 2021 (coronavirus 2019 [COVID-19] cohort), their treatment utilization, and their remaining disease burden during adalimumab treatment based on concomitant medications and healthcare resource utilization.

Methods

Study Design and Patients

This observational cohort study used Merative® MarketScan® Commercial, Medicare, and Multi-State Medicaid claims databases, which provide de-identified, closed claims databases aggregated from over 450 unique suppliers including patients from all 50 US states. The primary analysis set (main cohort) included patients who were new adult users of adalimumab and who started treatment between September 2015 and December 2018. The index date was defined as the treatment start date (first adalimumab claim).

Patients had at least one diagnostic code for HS (International Classification of Disease [ICD]-9: 705.83 or ICD-10: L73.2) and continuous insurance enrollment in the 1-year period before the treatment start date (baseline period before adalimumab treatment; 365 days before the date of first claim; continuous defined as gaps ≤ 60 days). Patients were followed until the first occurrence of adalimumab discontinuation (the end date of the first prescription that is not repeatedly claimed within 90 days), end of enrollment in the claims databases, or end of data availability in the databases (31 March 2022). Patients were excluded if they had an adalimumab claim at any point before the index date.

Patients with HS with Commercial/Medicare insurance and patients with Medicaid insurance were analyzed separately. Since the COVID-19 pandemic may have affected treatment pattens, in a secondary analysis (COVID-19 cohort), the study objectives were repeated from the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic onwards. Patient selection for the COVID-19 cohort took place from 1 March 2020 to 30 September 2021; this end date was chosen to allow patients to be followed for at least 6 months before the database end date. Adalimumab was the only treatment approved in the USA during both periods.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the results and to increase the likelihood of adalimumab being used as a treatment for HS by excluding patients with other potential indications identified in the baseline period or index date, such as ankylosing spondylitis, axial spondyloarthritis, inflammatory bowel disease (recorded as either inflammatory bowel disease, Crohn’s disease, and ulcerative colitis), juvenile idiopathic arthritis, noninfectious uveitis, psoriasis, psoriatic arthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, or sarcoidosis.

Patient Characteristics and Study Outcomes

Baseline Characteristics and Medication Use

Demographics, patient characteristics, and medication use at baseline were assessed for all new users of adalimumab.

Adalimumab Treatment Patterns

The first adalimumab treatment episode was defined as the time from the index date until the end of adalimumab treatment. A proxy measure was used for the starting dose of adalimumab treatment, quantified as the total amount of adalimumab in the first prescription, divided by the number of days’ supply, expressed as mg/week. Data on doses of adalimumab after the starting dose or the frequency of dosing were not assessed.

Drug Discontinuation

Discontinuation was defined as ceasing treatment of adalimumab, when there was a gap of at least 90 days since the last recorded claim of adalimumab. Baseline characteristics used as predictor candidates for adalimumab discontinuation were age, sex, insurance type (Medicaid versus Commercial/Medicare), specialty of the adalimumab prescriber, comorbidities (including hypertension, obesity, depression, and other inflammatory diseases), HS-related medication (including biologics, advanced nonbiologics/small molecules [apremilast, baricitinib, tofacitinib], conventional systemic disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs [DMARDs; azathioprine, cyclosporine, methotrexate, mycophenolate], hormones [including spironolactone], corticosteroids, and antibiotics), and wound care/surgery. Follow-up termination owing to a change in insurance coverage was treated as a censoring event rather than as a discontinuation event.

Switching from Adalimumab to Other Biologics

A switch to another biologic was defined as the initiation of another biologic (anakinra, brodalumab, canakinumab, certolizumab pegol, etanercept, golimumab, guselkumab, ixekizumab, natalizumab, secukinumab, tildrakizumab, ustekinumab, or vedolizumab) within the period of the last adalimumab claims’ days of supply + 30 days. The date of switch was defined as the day of the first claim of the new biologic. In case a switch to another biologic occurred within the period of the last claim's days of supply, the end of the exposure period was set to the day before the switch. The proportions of patients who switched to another biologic, and the average time to discontinuation in those who switched versus those who did not switch, were assessed.

Healthcare Resource Utilization

During the baseline period and during adalimumab treatment, the proportions of patients with healthcare resource utilization events and the healthcare resource utilization rate were evaluated. The rate of healthcare resource utilization was estimated for each patient as the number of different encounters per person-year of adalimumab exposure, and the average rate per group was assessed.

HS Providers

HS provider was defined as the provider type category on the closest HS claim with a non-missing provider type code, on or before the index date. The provider who initiated adalimumab was defined as the provider type who likely prescribed the first adalimumab, based on the most recent claim (not necessarily with a claim for HS) on or before the index date.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics of patient baseline characteristics and adalimumab treatment patterns were conducted for the primary (main cohort) and secondary (COVID-19 cohort) analyses.

Drug discontinuation was quantified using Kaplan–Meier estimates (presented as the percentage of patients using adalimumab at 3, 6, 9, and 12 months). Predictors of adalimumab discontinuation were identified with a multivariable proportional hazard regression model, using a forward stepwise selection approach. Predictors were estimated using hazard ratios (HR).

Missing Data

Missing data were not imputed. The proportion of missing data was reported for each variable and classified as “missing” data.

Results

Baseline Characteristics and Medication Use

Baseline Characteristics

The main cohort included 2367 adult patients (70.4% Commercial/Medicare-insured and 29.6% Medicaid-insured) and 1736 adults (57.6% Commercial/Medicare-insured and 42.4% Medicaid-insured) in the COVID-19 cohort (Table 1). Mean (standard deviation [SD; min–max]) age was 36.7 (11.7; 18.0–78.0) years, and 76.5% were female in the main cohort. In the COVID-19 cohort, mean (SD; min–max) age was 35.3 (11.2; 18.0–84.0) years, and 80.5% were female.

Approximately half of the patients in the main cohort had a dermatologist as their HS provider, and approximately 35% had a dermatologist as the provider for initiating adalimumab. There were considerable differences in the distribution of the providers between Commercial/Medicare-insured and Medicaid-insured patients. Overall, in the main cohort, higher variability was seen in Medicaid-insured patients, where only 25% had a dermatologist as their HS provider, and only 15% had a dermatologist as the adalimumab provider. In Commercial/Medicare-insured patients, 63% had a dermatologist as their HS provider, and 42% had a dermatologist as the adalimumab provider.

In the main cohort, women were younger, more often overweight/obese, and more often had anxiety and depression than males. These differences between the sexes were not observed in the COVID-19 cohort. Patients in the main cohort with inflammatory bowel disease (6.0%) were more likely to have other specialists and acute care providers as their HS/adalimumab providers. Similarly, patients in the main cohort with psoriatic arthritis (3.0%) were more likely to have a rheumatologist as their adalimumab provider and less likely to have a dermatologist in these roles.

Baseline Medication Use

Analgesic, anxiolytic, and antidepressant use was higher in patients with Medicaid insurance compared with Commercial/Medicare-insured patients. In the main cohort, the use of opioid analgesics was higher in Medicaid-insured patients than in Commercial/Medicare-insured patients: 20.4% versus 7.6% (Fig. S1A). Anxiolytics, nonopioid analgesic use, and antidepressants use was 11.6% versus 6.1%, 8.0% and 22.6%, and 32.0% versus 23.0% in the same groups, respectively.

Compared with adult men, women had a higher use of analgesics, anti-fungals, systemic corticosteroids, spironolactone, metformin, supplements, anxiolytics, and antidepressants, but a lower use of retinoids. Adult patients with any biologic use in the baseline period had higher use of conventional systemic DMARDs, advanced nonbiologics/small molecular inhibitors, topical analgesics, systemic antibiotics, and corticosteroids, and a lower use of spironolactone and retinoids, compared with those without biologic use.

The medication-use patterns in the COVID-19 cohort were similar to those in the main cohort (Fig. S1B). However, analgesic and opioid use were lower compared with the main cohort. For example, the overall proportion of patients with concomitant use of opioid analgesics was 11.4% in the main cohort (20.4% in Medicaid-insured patients and 7.6% in Commercial/Medicare-insured patients) and 6.3% in the COVID-19 cohort (9.4% in Medicaid-insured patients and 4.1% in Commercial/Medicare-insured patients).

Adalimumab Treatment Patterns

Most patients (around 70%) initiated a regimen of adalimumab plus another HS-related pharmacotherapy (including other biologics, corticosteroids, antibiotics, analgesics, and antidepressants, among others) and only around 30% of patients initiated adalimumab without other HS-related medication, which was more pronounced in Commercial/Medicare-insured patients.

There were no relevant differences in the average starting dose of adalimumab between the two insurance-type populations. The starting dose was relevantly lower in three subgroups of patients: those with comorbid psoriasis or comorbid psoriatic arthritis and patients with prior use of biologic medication.

Adalimumab Discontinuation

In the main cohort, 2429.79 patient-years of follow-up data were available, and in the COVID-19 cohort, 1085.33 patient-years. During follow-up, 1427 (60.3%) patients in the overall main cohort discontinued adalimumab. The median (IQR) drug survival was 0.96 (0.38–2.60) years. Drug survival at 6 and 12 months was 68.4% and 48.0%, respectively (Fig. 1). Drug survival rates were similar in the COVID-19 cohort (72.6% and 55.1% at 6 and 12 months, respectively) (Fig. S2).

Drug survival was higher in Commercial/Medicare-insured patients (1-year survival: 53%) compared with Medicaid-insured patients (1-year survival: 37%) in the main cohort, but this was not observed in the COVID-19 cohort (1-year survival: 54% [Commercial/Medicare] and 58% [Medicaid]).

Of the 525 patients who re-initiated treatment, 390 patients (74.3%) re-initiated adalimumab treatment within 1 year and 476 patients (90.7%) re-initiated adalimumab treatment within 2 years.

Switching from Adalimumab to Other Biologics

Of the 1427 adults in the main cohort who discontinued adalimumab, 93 (6.5%) switched to another biologic. Infliximab was the biologic most patients switched to, followed by ustekinumab, etanercept, and secukinumab (Table 2). The average time until adalimumab discontinuation was higher in patients who switched to another biologic (441 days) than in those who did not switch (285 days).

The average time until adalimumab discontinuation in the COVID-19 cohort was 149 days. Of the 630 adults in the COVID-19 cohort who discontinued adalimumab, 50 (7.9%) switched to another biologic. Healthcare Resource Utilization (HRCU)

During the baseline period (before adalimumab treatment; 365 days before the date of first claim), the proportion of patients in the main cohort who had at least one HCRU event was generally higher than during adalimumab treatment. Definitions for HCRU events are provided in Table S1.

Despite a decrease in the proportion of patients with ≥ 1 outpatient dermatology visit during adalimumab treatment, the HCRU rate for outpatient dermatology visits increased by 404 per 1000 person-years (/kPY). Increases in HCRU rates were also seen for antidepressant use (165/kPY) and neuropathic pain agents (71/kPY) (Fig. 4). In addition, decreases in HCRU rates were seen for systemic antibiotics (−978/kPY), and both opioid and nonopioid analgesics (−258/kPY and −208/kPY).

For some HCRU events, rates differed between insurance types (Fig. S3). ER/acute/urgent events decreased in Medicaid-insured (−115/kPY) versus Commercial/Medicare-insured (38/kPY) patients. Neuropathic pain agent use increased in Medicaid- (237/kPY) versus Commercial/Medicare- (1/kPY) insured patients.

Longer duration of adalimumab use resulted in larger HCRU rate changes, notably for systemic antibiotics, nonopioid analgesics, and inpatient visits. With ≥ 1 year of treatment (n = 784, 33.1%) outpatient claim rates decreased by −529/kPY and systemic antibiotic claim rates decreased by −1679/kPY. With ≥ 2 years of treatment (n = 353, 14.9%), outpatient claim rates decreased by −851/kPY and systemic antibiotic claim rates decreased by −1909/kPY.

Overall changes in HCRU and changes in HCRU by insurance type were similar in the COVID-19 cohort (Fig. S4), with differences in opioid and nonopioid analgesic use (decreased in main cohort; increased in COVID-19 cohort) and inpatient days (increased in main cohort; decreased in COVID-19 cohort) between the two cohorts.

Sensitivity Analyses

Sensitivity analyses, which excluded patients with other potential indications identified in the baseline period or index date, were also performed. When excluding patients with competing conditions, there was a relevant decrease in drug claim rates of hydroxychloroquine, topical and systemic corticosteroids, and supplements during the adalimumab use period.

When excluding the 317 patients (13.4%) with other conditions for which adalimumab can be prescribed, the starting dose of adalimumab was higher. This finding indicates some patients were likely treated with adalimumab for other conditions. However, sensitivity analyses showed that excluding patients with potential competing diagnoses for adalimumab use did not result in relevant changes in the observed drug survival (Fig. S5). Notably, the exclusion of patients with competing conditions resulted in a lower percentage of patients switching from adalimumab to another biologic (Fig. S5).

Discussion

This population-based study describes the remaining disease burden in patients with HS treated with adalimumab. Overall, these analyses showed that treatment with adalimumab, the only approved biologic available at the time of this study, resulted in improvements in patients with HS. These improvements included decreased use of opioid pain medication use and an overall decrease in HCRU. Some increases were seen (neuropathic pain use, antidepressant use), however these increases are potentially indicative of a patient receiving access to appropriate care, rather than an effect of adalimumab treatment. The clinical and economic impacts of these changes in HCRU would therefore be a useful area for future study. Although relevant improvements were seen following adalimumab treatment, not all patients experienced these benefits. Our analyses showed that there are high discontinuation rates of adalimumab in HS, highlighting the residual disease burden and the need for new effective therapies with long-term drug survival.

While drug survival was low, it is worth noting that around one-fifth of patients re-initiated treatment, with three quarters of these patients re-initiating within 1 year. Most predictors of adalimumab discontinuation (such as female sex, younger age, and being Medicaid-insured) were likely proxies for disease severity, healthcare access, healthcare-seeking behaviour, new contraindications, and/or social determinants of health. The predictive value of excision surgery (including wide excisions and de-roofings) was also likely due to its association with disease severity. The link between treatment with spironolactone and improved drug survival is unclear, but could be related to improved efficacy for patients treated with both medications or a marker for less severe disease that clinicians think would be responsive to hormonal therapy [12]. In addition, studies have shown that simultaneous treatment of adalimumab with antibiotic courses resulted in better treatment outcomes compared with adalimumab treatment alone [13].

The findings from this study show that, while adalimumab treatment was beneficial to patients with HS, there was a need for additional effective treatments with a longer drug survival and a better understanding of why patients pause or discontinue treatment in a disease that has potentially irreversible consequences. Disruptions in care are likely increasing as patients in the USA are increasingly being switched to biosimilars or requiring additional prior authorizations from insurance companies, sometimes with serious consequences [14–16]. Although new therapies have since been approved and become available, it is likely that there are still residual burdens that patients experience. Further research in this area could be very helpful in establishing more consistent care for these patients.

Of note, there were meaningful differences in outcomes between patients with Medicaid insurance, of whom only 25% had a dermatologist as their primary care provider for HS, versus those with Commercial/Medicare insurance (63% had a dermatologist as their primary care provider, respectively). The residual burden in this cohort of patients with HS during adalimumab use is considerable and is affected by the shorter duration of adalimumab use, given the increase in discontinuation over time. Many patients may not be receiving adequate treatment, raising the need for both access and longitudinal follow up.

HS typically presents during puberty and into early adulthood, with the incidence of disease highest in young adults [17, 18]. Owing to the physical and mental impact of HS, young patients may struggle to maintain study or employment due to absenteeism [19]. This could explain why such a high proportion of young patients in this study are on Medicaid. Furthermore, patients with HS in general are known to deal with lower income growth and higher rates of disability and workplace absenteeism, resulting in higher burden of disease [19, 20]. Patients also experience cumulative life course impairment, exacerbating the situation over time [21].

Our findings also show generally better treatment survival in Commercial/Medicare-insured patients compared with Medicaid-insured patients. Previous studies in other therapy areas have shown that patients from socioeconomically disadvantaged communities are more likely to receive Medicaid insurance; our results suggest these patients may deal with worse treatment outcomes [22]. There may indeed be a vicious cycle in which patients with more severe disease are unable to work, and therefore on Medicaid, and while on Medicaid, have reduced access to care and medications, leading to worse treatment outcomes. In the COVID-19 cohort, treatment survival was similar in Commercial/Medicare-insured patients compared with Medicaid-insured patients, and correspondingly, discontinuation rates were similar between the groups. This difference suggests that the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic, including reduced healthcare access and delivery, may have mitigated the influence of insurance type on treatment survival.

The more recent data from the COVID-19 cohort showed similar drug survival and healthcare utilization patterns as those in the main cohort. Outpatient visits increased during adalimumab treatment in both cohorts, but this could be due to recommended therapeutic monitoring of biologics in HS [23]. Given increasing awareness of HS, increased outpatient visits could also be due to the patient receiving appropriate regular contact with a physician having found the right care provider, as with the neuropathic pain agent and antidepressant use, as noted previously. Nonetheless, these findings show that despite the pandemic, the need for treatment in patients with HS remained high.

Overall, the findings from this analysis showed that treatment with adalimumab provides benefits to patients with HS but is also associated with limitations. These findings demonstrate the need for new effective therapies with long-term drug survival. Since this study was conducted, other biologics have been approved for the treatment of HS and additional treatment options are currently in development [7, 10]; the lack of alternatives at the time may therefore have been responsible for the low number of patients who switched to another biologic within this study.

Strengths and Limitations

This study presents data from a large population of patients with HS using adalimumab in a real-world setting over a long period of time (5 years). Using MarketScan data provides a comprehensive overview of claimed in- and outpatient healthcare encounters, including pharmacy claims, therefore enabling a comprehensive analysis of healthcare over time. The large sample size within MarketScan allows for a reliable assessment of changes in care over time, as well as the current standard of care for patients with HS in the USA. Evaluation of claims data from MarketScan may also enable identification of areas of improvement, ensuring patients with HS have access to appropriate treatments.

The results of this study may not be generalizable to US patients with other types of insurance or without insurance, since patients included in the study were required to have private or public health insurance coverage.

The sensitivity analyses in which patients with other conditions for which adalimumab could be prescribed suggested the study population included patients that used adalimumab for an indication other than HS (at most 14% of the population in the main cohort). However, the impact of the misclassification of adalimumab indication on this study's findings appeared to be limited.

The definition of drug discontinuation in this study was based on fills of pharmacy claims data, therefore the reasons for discontinuation are not known and could be for any reason including nonresponse.

As a claims database study, this study was also limited by the potential for misclassification of variables and covariates, including inaccurate diagnoses, and missing or incomplete data. It should be noted that the reported provider percentages may be underestimated, as provider type information could be missing in the claims data. This limitation may contribute to the low percentage of dermatologists identified as adalimumab prescribers within this analysis.

During the reported period, from 2015 to 2022, adalimumab was the only approved treatment for HS; there are now other treatment options. Since the start of this study, the efficacy and safety of several other biologics for the treatment of HS have been reported. This includes the publication of longer-term data on secukinumab [24, 25] and bimekizumab [26, 27]. The treatment environment for HS is actively changing, and future publications should address the residual disease burden and drug survival of newer agents.

Conclusions

Overall, treatment with adalimumab was associated with HS-related healthcare resource utilization improvements. The predictors of adalimumab discontinuation (based on baseline characteristics) were likely proxies for disease severity, healthcare access, healthcare-seeking behavior, new contraindications (e.g., pregnancy), and/or social determinants of health. In addition, patients discontinued treatment before 1 year, though some re-initiated. These analyses highlight the need for other effective treatment alternatives with long-term drug survival.
