 and secondary system integration

by
Yinbin Miao, Yan Cao, Yiqi Yu, Soon Kyu Lee, Nicolas Stauff, and Kun Mo
Argonne National Laboratory

SEPTEMBER 30, 2026

 Executive Abstract

 The Nuclear Energy Advanced Modeling and Simulation (NEAMS) Multiphysics Applications
team continues to assess code usability and functionality for microreactor design and safety analyses,
while demonstrating that NEAMS tools capture both steady-state and transient behavior across
distinct microreactor concepts. In FY2026, the team enhanced full-core, high-fidelity multiphysics
models of a heat pipe microreactor (HPMR), a gas-cooled microreactor (GCMR), and the Kilopower
Reactor Using Stirling TechnologY (KRUSTY) experiment by incorporating advanced heat pipe
modeling, graphite and tristructural isotropic (TRISO) performance models, and secondary sys-
tem integration. These models employ the Multiphysics Object-Oriented Simulation Environment
(MOOSE) MultiApp and Transfers architecture with Griffin for neutronics, BISON for heat con-
duction and thermomechanics, Sockeye for heat pipes, SAM and the Thermal Hydraulics Module
(THM) for coolant channels and balance of plant, SWIFT for hydride behavior, and Grizzly for
graphite structural models, with meshes generated via the MOOSE Reactor Module.

 For the HPMR, the full-core model closed the loop with its balance of plant for the first time,
coupling Griffin, BISON, and Sockeye to a THM Brayton loop that sets its own boundary conditions.
The 2 MW-class core converged to steady state at about 20% cycle efficiency, delivering roughly
400 kW of output, with every heat pipe inside its operating limits. A decade of graphite dose
was shown to change peak fuel temperature by only a few kelvin, too small to be life-limiting.
Sockeye’s non-condensable-gas treatments were demonstrated across all 192 individually resolved
heat pipes without altering the coupling scheme. Separately, the converged full-core fields drove 672
particle-level TRISO simulations, giving a negligible SiC failure probability.

 For the GCMR, Griffin’s online multigroup cross-section generation through SSAPI was demon-
strated for the first time on a two-dimensional full-core model, standalone neutronics rather than
coupled multiphysics. Without building an offline Serpent model, Griffin can calculate the eigenval-
ues, control drums worth and power distributions accurately, although the offline still resolves drum
states slightly better. Separately, a coupled Griffin/BISON/THM balance-of-plant hierarchy carried
the plant from rest to a converged, full-power steady state, and a motor-torque reduction produced
a Doppler-feedback power dip with partial recovery.

 For KRUSTY, a coupled Griffin/BISON/Sockeye model of the fully assembled reactor, with all
sodium heat pipes installed and operating, converged to a steady state at 750 W fission power, just
below delayed criticality. This is the first integration of Sockeye into the KRUSTY model: three
of the eight heat pipes, one full-pipe and two half-pipe symmetry models, were resolved through
a mixed-dimension 3D-2D-1D decomposition capturing the azimuthally non-uniform heat load at
the fuel groove. Predicted fuel temperature was consistent with test observations, and thermal
expansion produced modest axial displacement with even smaller radial displacement. The success
in steady-state modeling of KRUSTY paves the way for future transient analyses scheduled in
FY2027.

 The team’s Na-HPMR model became the basis for an Organisation for Economic Co-operation
and Development (OECD)/Nuclear Energy Agency (NEA) international microreactor multiphysics
benchmark, drawing more than twenty organizations. Models continued onto the Virtual Test
Bed (VTB), where external users including Westinghouse and the DOE-NE DNCSH program

 i

adopted them for fission-matrix and criticality-safety analyses. Valuable experience was gained in
the application of the MOOSE-based code suite for multiphysics microreactor analyses with related
feedback provided to the codes developers.

1 Introduction

 Nuclear energy plays an indispensable role in a secure and resilient U.S. energy portfolio, and its

role is set to grow as electricity demand rises. Meeting that demand depends on bringing advanced

reactor technologies to deployment quickly, central to the mission of the U.S. Department of Energy

(DOE) Office of Nuclear Energy (NE). Among the concepts being pursued, nuclear microreactors

stand out for their compactness, transportability, and autonomy: factory-built units shipped sealed

for on-site installation, capable of flexible load following, and suited to applications large plants

cannot serve, such as remote communities and defense installations. Realizing those attributes

requires design features that depart from light water reactor practice: liquid-metal heat pipes

or high-temperature gas cooling, metal hydride moderators, TRISO fuel, movable control drums,

and direct Brayton power conversion, often combined within a single small core. The resulting

systems couple neutronic, thermal, mechanical, and chemical behavior tightly, so licensing depends

on predicting that coupled behavior across normal and off-normal conditions with little integral

experimental data. These demands strain the single-physics, loosely coupled tools traditionally

used in reactor analysis, creating the need for the advanced capabilities being developed under the

NEAMS program.

 The NEAMS program answers this need with a suite of tools built on the MOOSE [2] —

including Griffin [3] for neutronics, BISON [4] for heat conduction and thermomechanics, Sockeye [5]

for heat pipes, SAM and the THM [6, 7] for thermal hydraulics and balance of plant, and SWIFT [8]

and Grizzly [9] for moderator and structural behavior — coupled in memory to resolve strongly

interacting phenomena at high fidelity. Demonstrating that capability on realistic reference problems,

and feeding lessons back to the code teams, is the role of the Multiphysics Applications technical

area and of the Microreactor Application Driver activity documented in this report.

 This project builds on several years of accumulated expertise [10, 11, 12, 13]. In FY2025 [14],

a sodium working-fluid variant of the HPMR (Na-HPMR) was built to exercise Sockeye’s liquid-

conduction vapor-flow (LCVF) model, and solid mechanics was implemented in the full-core HPMR

 1

BISON model for the first time. The GCMR model was extended from steady state to a suite of

transients and coupled to a Brayton-cycle balance of plant (BOP) model, and KRUSTY warm

critical validation was completed and published [15].

 FY2026 advances these lines further. Validation against KRUSTY moves from the warm

critical tests to the nuclear system tests, performed on the fully assembled reactor with heat pipes

and Stirling converters installed, which requires heat-pipe modeling in the KRUSTY multiphysics

model for the first time. For the HPMR, the graphite monolith’s structural response is analyzed

systematically under thermal and irradiation loading, advanced heat pipe features are exercised, and

a secondary loop and balance of plant are added so the heat sink is modeled rather than prescribed;

TRISO fuel performance evaluation moves from the assembly level to the full-core level. For the

GCMR, online cross-section generation is demonstrated in a standalone two-dimensional neutronics

calculation, and the balance-of-plant model is extended to be driven by the coupled neutronics

solution rather than a prescribed power history. The team continues to engage the microreactor

community through the VTB and the OECD/NEA microreactor multiphysics benchmark.

 The remainder of this report is organized as follows. Section 2 summarizes the NEAMS

capabilities applied in this project, with a focus on the features newly assessed in FY2026 and the

feedback conveyed to the respective code teams. Section 3 covers the validation effort based on the

KRUSTY nuclear system tests. Section 4 and Section 5 present the FY2026 enhancements to the

full-core HPMR and GCMR models. Section 6 describes model dissemination through the VTB

and the OECD/NEA benchmark activity. Section 7 summarizes the capabilities demonstrated, the

feedback provided to developers, and recommended follow-up work.

2 Methodology and Assessment

 This section summarizes the NEAMS capabilities applied in this project, with a focus on the

features newly exercised or assessed in FY2026 (see Table 2.1). The microreactor multiphysics

toolchain comprises Griffin [3] for neutronics; BISON [4] for solid heat conduction and solid

mechanics; Grizzly [9] for graphite structural models; Sockeye [5] for heat pipe modeling; SAM [6]

and the THM [7] for one-dimensional coolant channel flow and balance-of-plant components; and

SWIFT [8] for hydrogen redistribution in hydride moderators. All of these codes are built within

the MOOSE framework [2], which enables in-memory multiphysics coupling through the MultiApp

system. Meshes are generated using the MOOSE Reactor Module [16] and related MeshGenerator

objects.

 One of the main outcomes of this project is the user experience gained by the team while exer-

cising new capabilities, and the feedback that experience generates for code developers. Assessment

highlights are noted throughout this section; the corresponding development requests and feedback

are collected in Section 7.

2.1 Mesh

2.1.1 MOOSE Reactor Module

 The MOOSE Reactor Module [16] is a specialized module of MOOSE designed to streamline the

creation of finite element meshes for nuclear reactor core geometries. It allows analysts to construct

detailed meshes for reactor pins, assemblies, and full cores using built-in mesh generation tools

tailored to both Cartesian and hexagonal configurations. Automatic subdomain volume preservation,

control drum modeling, and component tagging for post-processing eliminate the need for external

meshing software and integrate directly with MOOSE-based physics applications. The module has

been applied to fast reactors, microreactors, and high-temperature gas-cooled reactors.

2.2 Neutronics

2.2.1    Griffin

 Griffin is a reactor physics application jointly developed by Idaho National Laboratory and

Argonne National Laboratory [3]. Built on the MOOSE framework, it is well suited to multiphysics

applications for advanced reactor designs. Griffin solves the neutron transport equation using finite

element methods for spatial discretization and multigroup approximations for energy discretization,

and it offers a range of angular treatments including the discrete ordinates method, spherical

harmonics expansion, and diffusion. It also supports point kinetics for rapid transient scoping

analysis and space-time kinetics for higher-fidelity transient simulation. More broadly, Griffin offers

reactor physics capabilities spanning cross-section preparation, multi-scheme and multi-radiation

radiation transport, reactor core fuel management and radionuclide depletion.

 Within the Microreactor Application Driver, Griffin is the primary neutronics solver and

often serves as the parent application that orchestrates coupling with the other physics. Most

simulations use the discontinuous finite element method (DFEM)-SN approach, accelerated by

CMFD. Time-dependent core behavior is solved through the MOOSE TimeIntegrator System using

either explicit or implicit time discretization scheme. Some applications, such as fission product

poison tracking, previously relied on a reduced-order treatment using Griffin point kinetics equations

(PKE) solver, was extended in FY2025 and FY2026 to use high-fidelity transport solvers for modeling

long-duration transients in which Xe-135 and Sm-149 evolution is important. This same high-

fidelity space-time kinetics approach underpinned the FY2025 KRUSTY warm critical reactivity

insertion validation [15]. FY2026 KRUSTY work is confined to the steady-state nuclear system test

configuration, with the comprehensive transient analyses planned for FY2027 (Section 3.4).

 A new focus in FY2026 is Griffin’s recent development of online cross-section generation

capability for TRISO-fueled advanced reactors, including both prismatic and pebble-bed designs. In

 5

the current HPMR and GCMR multiphysics workflow, neutronic calculations followed a traditional

two-step approach in which multigroup cross sections prepared offline as a tabulated library,

parameterized by state variables such as fuel temperature, moderator temperature, and hydrogen

concentration using Serpent-2 [17]. During Griffin simulations, cross sections were then interpolated

from this library on the fly. The recent development of an online cross section generation procedure

in ISOXML, called Self-Shielding Application Programming Interface (SSAPI) [18], provides an

alternative approach that eliminates the need to set up a separate reactor model in another reactor

physics code such as Serpent.

 SSAPI in Griffin combines a unified fuel self-shielding treatment with a fast and accurate

non-fuel resonance treatment, along with on-the-fly group condensation, so that cross sections can be

generated directly for the actual reactor state and geometry without interpolation. In FY2026, the

two-dimensional full-core GCMR model was used to test this new capability in standalone neutronic

calculations; the demonstration of moving from the traditional two-step approach to a one-step

approach is described in Section 5.2, with extension to three-dimensional and multiphysics-coupled

applications planned for FY2027.

2.2.2   Serpent

 Serpent is a Monte Carlo code that solves neutron and photon transport in three-dimensional,

continuous-energy and angular space [17]. Developed at the VTT Technical Research Centre of

Finland, it is widely used to model a variety of nuclear reactors. Within the Microreactor Application

Driver, Serpent models currently generate the multigroup cross sections used in the multiphysics

calculations for HPMR and GCMR. In addition, Serpent provides reference solutions for critical

eigenvalues, fluxes and power distributions, which are used to validate Griffin steady-state results.

For KRUSTY, Serpent was also used to generate multigroup cross sections for the different material

regions except the reflectors.

 6

2.2.3    MC2 -3

 It was found and reported in FY2024 [13] that Monte Carlo–generated cross sections can

introduce significant biases in core criticality calculations for small, leaky, fast-spectrum cores such

as KRUSTY, particularly where anisotropic scattering is prominent. The Argonne lattice physics

code MC2 -3 [19] produces improved high-order anisotropic cross sections in the reflector region but

lacks accurate fuel Doppler reactivity feedback. A hybrid cross-section set — MC2 -3 for the reflector

and Serpent-2 for all other regions — was therefore adopted for KRUSTY and retained through

FY2025. In FY2026, the hybrid cross section sets were updated from using the ENDF/B-VII.0

library to a more recent ENDF/B-VII.1 version. Cross sections for heat pipe regions were also

generated for modeling the nuclear system tests. The hybrid approach is retained for the nuclear

system test configuration, and the library was regenerated over an extended fuel and component

temperature grid to cover the wider operating envelope (Section 3.2).

2.3 Heat Conduction and Thermomechanics

2.3.1    BISON

 BISON [4] is a nuclear fuel performance code developed within the MOOSE framework. It is

capable of modeling a wide variety of fuel forms, including light water reactor fuel rods, TRISO

particle fuel, and metallic rod and plate fuel, and it is used in this project for full-core macroscopic

heat conduction and solid mechanics as well as for fuel performance evaluation.

 In FY2025, BISON was extended from a heat-conduction-only role to a fully coupled heat

conduction and solid mechanics solve in the full-core HPMR model, enabling evaluation of thermal

expansion effects on steady-state and transient behavior and providing the stress fields needed for

structural assessment. For simplicity, TRISO compacts in both the HPMR and GCMR models

were treated as a homogenized medium; because homogenization can underpredict peak kernel

temperatures under non-uniform power deposition, a heat source decomposition (HSD) treatment

 7

was implemented in FY2025 to recover heterogeneity effects at modest cost.

 FY2026 work builds on both of these developments. The solid mechanics capability is used for

the systematic graphite monolith stress analyses of Section 4.2, and the coupled full-core solution

provides the operating conditions for the full-core TRISO fuel performance evaluation of Section 4.5.

2.3.2   Grizzly

 Grizzly [9] is a structural materials simulation code built on the MOOSE framework, designed

to model aging and degradation mechanisms in nuclear reactor components. Originally tailored to

light water reactor pressure vessels and reinforced concrete structures, its capabilities have expanded

to support advanced reactor designs.

 Grizzly was not exercised as part of the multiphysics model in FY2025. Instead, a systematic

comparison of the graphite models available in Grizzly and BISON was performed to inform code

selection for graphite analyses. That comparison found that BISON provides a comprehensive suite

of thermophysical and mechanical models for grade H-451 — sufficient for baseline analyses of a core

built from that grade — while Grizzly offers broader mechanical coverage across additional grades

(IG-110, JAEA, AGC, AGC3) and is therefore required for grade-specific studies. Grizzly does not

currently provide graphite thermal conductivity or specific heat models, so analyses requiring both

heat conduction and grade-specific solid mechanics in a single application need both codes to be

available, either through dynamic library linking or a prebuilt combined application.

 In FY2026 both the BISON and Grizzly graphite model sets were evaluated, and the graphite

results reported in Section 4.2 were produced with the H-451 models in BISON as the representative

case.

 As Grizzly is not a submodule of those MOOSE-based super applications such as DireWolf

and BlueCRAB, it must be either dynamically linked with a DireWolf or BlueCRAB executable or

compiled as part of a combined application by manually modifying the makefiles and some source

files to include Grizzly’s components. In this report, the Grizzly was running with other MOOSE

 8

application through dynamic linking, which allowed for seamless integration without modifying the

existing build system of the super applications.

2.4 Heat Removal and Power Conversion

2.4.1    Sockeye

 Sockeye [5] is a heat pipe modeling code built on the MOOSE framework, focused on high-

temperature, liquid-metal heat pipes with annular screen or porous wick structures. It offers an

effective conduction model, the LCVF model [20], and a two-phase-flow model, enabling prediction of

operational limits such as the capillary and dryout thresholds under both steady-state and transient

conditions. Sockeye has been validated against the SPHERE facility experiments [21], and a heat

pipe Phenomena Identification and Ranking Table (PIRT) has been developed to guide further

model development [22].

 In FY2025 the full-core HPMR heat pipe model was upgraded from the effective conductance

model to the LCVF model, alongside the introduction of the sodium working fluid variant of the

HPMR concept. That upgrade made it possible to model a startup transient, which the effective

conductance approach could not represent.

 Two Sockeye capabilities are central to FY2026. First, KRUSTY employed heat pipes with

a wick-free condenser region, forming a hybrid heat pipe/thermosyphon configuration, and these

heat pipes were vertically oriented so that gravitational effects on fluid return are non-negligible.

Support for gravity and partial-wick configurations was requested of the Sockeye development team

in FY2025 and scheduled for FY2026. Gravity became available during the year and is represented

in the LCVF model as an evaporator pool, which is carried in the steady-state KRUSTY model

described in Section 3.2.4. The partial-wick feature remains with the Sockeye developers and is

expected to be assessed in FY2027 (Section 7.3). Second, recent Sockeye development on startup

modeling and noncondensable gases [23] offers additional fidelity for microreactor startup analyses.

Heat pipe fidelity was selected per scenario rather than uniformly across the FY2026 demonstrations:

 9

the noncondensable gas study of Section 4.3 ran on the full-core Na-HPMR model, whereas the

HPMR secondary loop coupling of Section 4.4 reverted to the effective conduction model to avoid

the numerical difficulty posed by a frozen heat pipe during Brayton cycle startup.

2.4.2    SAM/THM

 The System Analysis Module (SAM) [6] is a modern, MOOSE-based system analysis tool

developed under the NEAMS program for advanced non-LWR safety analysis. As a MOOSE

application, SAM couples flexibly with other MOOSE-based codes and has direct access to framework

capabilities and physics modules, including the Navier–Stokes Module and the THM [7]. Its core

capability is assembling a network of coupled components such as pipes, junctions, and valves; the

current THM component library is built primarily on a one-dimensional, single-phase, variable-area,

compressible flow model together with heat conduction. The FY2026 secondary loop and BOP

models reported here are built from that THM component library, accessed through SAM.

 The GCMR coolant channel model follows the approach established in FY2024, and the

Brayton-cycle BOP model developed in FY2025 was adapted from a similar model available in the

VTB [24]. FY2026 extends BOP modeling in two directions: the GCMR BOP is coupled into the

Griffin-governed multiphysics model rather than driven by a prescribed power history (Section 5.3),

and a secondary loop model is introduced for the HPMR, where the heat pipe condensers rather

than a coolant channel form the interface to the power conversion system (Section 4.4).

2.5 Moderator Performance

2.5.1    SWIFT

 SWIFT [8] is a MOOSE application dedicated to simulating the performance of hydride-based

moderators. The performance of a hydride moderator depends closely on hydrogen density: more

hydrogen results in better neutron moderation and potentially higher reactivity. At the same time,

 10

the local hydrogen-to-metal ratio changes during operation because of redistribution driven by the

thermochemistry of the system — set by the local temperature and the hydrogen partial pressure in

the cover gas — as well as by the temperature gradient and hydrogen loss through encapsulation

permeation.

 SWIFT was first assessed in this project in FY2023 and FY2024, with hydride performance

analyses performed for both the full-core HPMR and GCMR models. FY2025 work focused on the

impact of hydrogen redistribution on GCMR behavior during normal operation and power transients,

where it was found to shift the effective multiplication factor enough to make a considerable difference

to the behavior of the coupled system.

 No new SWIFT assessment was undertaken in FY2026. The code remains an integral component

of the established GCMR and HPMR coupled model sets, in which it supplies the moderator hydrogen

distribution that Griffin consumes as a cross-section state parameter. The online cross-section

demonstration of Section 5.2 was run as standalone neutronics and so did not exercise SWIFT.

2.6 Multiphysics Coupling

 The NEAMS codes described above are coupled through the MOOSE MultiApp and Transfers

systems, with Griffin as the parent application governing neutronics. This architecture enables in-

memory data exchange between Griffin, its child application (BISON), and its grandchild applications

(SAM/THM, Sockeye, and SWIFT). Although SWIFT can intrinsically solve diffusion-based heat

conduction for the moderator temperature, the temperature profile is transferred from BISON

instead; SWIFT then computes the hydrogen distribution in the moderator modules and returns it

to BISON, which aggregates the distributions from all hydride moderator modules and passes the

full-core distribution to Griffin for use as a grid variable in cross-section evaluation.

 Three hierarchies were exercised in FY2026, each instantiating this architecture differently:

3 Nuclear System Tests Modeling of KRUSTY

 Validation is essential to demonstrate code applicability and to build user confidence. For

the single-physics NEAMS codes, validation has drawn on experimental data produced by other

programs: BISON’s TRISO models were validated against the DOE-NE Advanced Gas Reactor

irradiation campaigns, and Sockeye’s sodium heat pipe models against the SPHERE experiments

sponsored by the DOE-NE Microreactor Program [21]. For coupled multiphysics microreactor models,

however, experimental data — particularly data involving neutronics — are scarce. KRUSTY is

a unique microreactor whose critical experiments produced exactly this kind of data, and it will

remain a primary source of operational microreactor validation data until more microreactors are

built and tested.

 FY2025 completed the validation of the KRUSTY warm critical reactivity insertion tests,

with the 15 ¢ and 30 ¢ cases reproduced in close agreement with the measurements and the work

published in Nuclear Science and Engineering [15]. FY2026 shifts the focus to the KRUSTY nuclear

system tests, which exercise a substantially different reactor configuration and a correspondingly

different set of physics.

 That shift is a two-year effort, and this chapter reports the first year of it. FY2026 builds the

steady-state model of the nuclear system test configuration and brings heat pipe modeling into

the KRUSTY multiphysics model for the first time, establishing a model ready to be extended to

transient operation. The validation itself, drawing chiefly on the transient data recorded during the

tests, is FY2027 work. What follows should therefore be read as the foundation for that comparison

rather than as the comparison.

3.1 KRUSTY Tests Introduction

3.1.1 KRUSTY Overview

 KRUSTY was conducted at the National Criticality Experiments Research Center as part of

the Kilopower Reactor Project, jointly led by NASA and the NNSA [25]. The project targeted space

reactors capable of continuously producing 1 to 10 kW(electric) to support a human habitat on a

planetary surface or deep-space exploration, and the KRUSTY test was the key milestone — the

most significant step toward deploying a space fission reactor since the end of the SNAP program.

 KRUSTY was a fast-spectrum system fueled with three stacked blocks of highly enriched

uranium (HEU) (93%-enriched U-8Mo alloy) sized to produce about 1 kW(electric) [26]. The core

was very small, made possible by beryllium oxide (BeO) neutron reflectors and a stainless steel

shield. Fission heat was removed by sodium heat pipes attached to axial grooves machined into the

outer surface of the fuel block, and converted to electricity by Stirling converters.

 Experiments were conducted incrementally: first with the core at room temperature (component

and cold critical experiments); then gradually warmed by fission power following an external

reactivity insertion (warm critical experiments) [27]; and finally at high temperature and high power

with the complete system installed (nuclear system tests) [28]. Five critical configurations were

subsequently evaluated and included in the International Criticality Safety Benchmark Evaluation

Project (ICSBEP) handbook as HEU-MET-FAST-101, and the multiphysics models developed in

this project were built on the corresponding MCNP6 benchmark geometry.

3.1.2 Main Differences from Warm Critical Tests

 The nuclear system tests differ from the warm critical tests in ways that bear directly on how

the multiphysics model should change. In the warm critical tests the core was insulated and had

no operating heat removal mechanism, so the fuel simply heated up while out-of-core components

stayed near ambient, and the reactivity feedback that was measured came primarily from core

 15

temperature rise and the consequent thermal expansion. The nuclear system tests were instead

run on the fully assembled reactor, with every heat pipe installed and operating and the power

conversion system in place, so the heat pipes now remove a substantial fraction of the generated

power and open a reactivity feedback path of their own. Sockeye therefore had to be brought into

the KRUSTY multiphysics model alongside the Griffin and BISON applications adopted before.

 That coupling is not entirely straightforward, because the KRUSTY heat pipes are unconven-

tional: the condenser region is wick-free and fluid return relies on gravity, so the device behaves more

like a hybrid of a thermosyphon and a capillary-driven heat pipe; the vertical orientation makes

gravitational effects non-negligible; and mounting on the outer surface of the fuel block leaves the

boundary conditions azimuthally heterogeneous. Support for gravity-driven return and partial-wick

configurations was requested of the Sockeye team in FY2025 and scheduled incrementally across

FY2026 and FY2027. The gravity capability arrived in time for the model described in Section 3.2.4,

while the partial-wick treatment is expected in FY2027.

 The operating envelope is wider as well. The nuclear system tests reached far higher temperatures

and ran far longer than the warm critical tests, which peaked near 300 ◦ C over tens of minutes.

That stretches the range over which material property and cross-section models must remain

valid, and it increases both the accumulated displacement seen by the neutronics solver and the

overall computational cost. The test series itself is also richer — startup, steady-state operation,

load-following transients, heat removal mechanism failure, and shutdown were all exercised on the

full system — giving a considerably broader validation basis than the reactivity insertion tests alone,

and one that maps directly onto the transient scenarios run for the HPMR and GCMR concepts

elsewhere in this report. Consistent with the FY2026 scope, the work reported here concentrates on

3.2 Modeling Approach

3.2.1 Mesh Update

 The warm critical model was built on the ICSBEP HEU-MET-FAST-101 case 1 geometry,

which places a neutron source holder at the center and four supporting steel rods in the east, west,

north, and south heat pipe locations. A few approximations were introduced to make a fully coupled

multiphysics model tractable: structural materials in the radial shield region were homogenized, the

horizontal wand plug was removed, and some air gaps and plug holes below the lower reflectors

were eliminated. These simplifications changed keff by only about 39 pcm relative to the detailed

ICSBEP model — considerably less than the effect of the nuclear data library choice — and had

negligible effect on the neutron energy spectrum in the core.

 Rather than solving the neutron transport problem on MOOSE’s displaced mesh, Griffin

relocates the transport-mesh nodes directly using the MoveNodesByParsedExpressionModifier

mesh modifier to apply the BISON displacement field. The modifier consumes the x disp, y disp,

and z disp fields transferred from BISON with notify mesh changed set so the transport sweeper

is rebuilt against the moved geometry before the next sweep. Because relocating nodes alone

would locally dilute or concentrate material without changing its mass, density factor variable

= griffin density rescales each cell’s density in proportion to its volume change, conserving mass

under the deformation.

3.2.4 Heat Pipe Models

 Bringing Sockeye into the KRUSTY model is the central modeling task of this fiscal year,

and it is not a matter of reusing the HPMR configuration. Three features of the KRUSTY heat

pipes fall outside the configurations Sockeye has been exercised on in this project: the wick-free

condenser region, the reliance on gravity for fluid return in a vertically oriented device, and the

circumferentially anisotropic boundary condition that results from brazing the heat pipe into a

groove on the outside of the fuel block rather than surrounding it with fuel. Of these, gravity became

available in Sockeye during FY2026 and is represented in the LCVF model as an evaporator pool,

carried in the steady-state model described here. Its fine-tuning belongs with the transient model

and is deferred to FY2027. The graded, or partial, wick treatment is still to be implemented by the

Sockeye developers; the model was built so that it can be adopted without rework once released.

 Figure 3.4: The mounting configuration of the heat pipe in the fuel block groove [1]

 In KRUSTY the heat pipes are not embedded in an axisymmetric annulus of fuel. Each pipe is

laid in a groove machined into the outer surface of the fuel block and is closed in by the external

structure that surrounds the block (see Figure 3.4). Only the fraction of the pipe circumference

that faces the groove wall is in contact with the fuel, which is the heat source, and only that sector

accepts a significant heat flux; the remainder of the perimeter faces the external structure and

 21

carries comparatively little load. The heat input to the evaporator is therefore concentrated over an

azimuthal sector rather than distributed uniformly around the pipe.

 This is not a detail that can be averaged away. Forcing the entire perimeter to absorb a load

that physically enters over a single sector both misstates the local wall temperature and degrades

the nonlinear solve — a purely axisymmetric setup runs into convergence difficulties in the transient.

Retaining the azimuthal coordinate in the cladding removes the problem at its source.

 On the other hand, resolving the whole pipe in 3D is not a practical alternative either. The

vapor core requires a compressible two-phase treatment that is only supported in 1D form, and the

thin wick and annulus region is responsible for the transition between 1D core and 3D external

model.

 The pipe is therefore decomposed by dimensionality matched to the physics, following the

3D–2D–1D strategy suggested by the Sockeye developer (see Figure 3.5 and Table 3.1):

3.2.5 Multiphysics Coupling Scheme

 Back in FY2025, a comprehensive Griffin-BISON multiphysics simulation workflow has been

successfully established for the KRUSTY warm critical experiments, which do not involve heat

pipes. In that setup, Griffin takes temperature and displacement information from the BISON’s

thermomechanical computation to inform its neutronics simulation, the power density calculated by

Griffin is provided to BISON as heat source. The two applications are coupled using fixed point

iteration.

 For the KRUSTY nuclear system tests, the presence of heat pipes as the heat removal mechanism

does not change the nature of the Griffin-BISON coupling. Meanwhile, the heat pipe models

governed by three Sockeye sub-applications, as described above, were coupled with the BISON

thermomechanical application so that heat generated in fuel can be transferred to the heat pipes and

removed. Thus, a three-level coupling hierarchy, as illustrated in Figure 3.8, was used to simulate

the KRUSTY system during nuclear system tests.

 The evaporator interface between BISON and each Sockeye sub-application uses a Robin–Robin,

or optimized Schwarz, coupling rather than a temperature or flux hand-off. Both applications

impose a Robin condition on the shared surface with the same coefficient, and each exchanges the

cancels identically at convergence, so no value of it inserts an artificial contact resistance between

the cladding and the fuel block, unlike a penalty heat transfer coefficient; it is free to be chosen

3.4 Perspectives for Transient Modeling

 Two issues carried over from FY2025 bear on transient modeling of the nuclear system tests.

The first is a Griffin convergence problem encountered when large displacement fields are applied to

the neutronics mesh. It was first observed in the 30 ¢ case after the power dropped below 100 W and

was worked around by defining the base mesh at the 15 ¢ insertion state, but that workaround does

not extend to the 60 ¢ case, where displacement continues to grow past the threshold. A standalone

Griffin calculation confirmed that the divergence originates in Griffin rather than in the coupled

 29

solve, and that a mesh with the displacement baked into the node positions converges normally.

With help from the Griffin developers the issue was addressed by constructing Griffin’s sweeper

on the displaced mesh, which fixed the standalone case; the subsequent multiphysics run, however,

stalled with Griffin looping in the sweeper at an earlier time step. Because the nuclear system tests

run hotter and longer than any warm critical test, they will accumulate larger displacements, and

resolving this issue is a prerequisite for the comprehensive transient work planned for FY2027. The

mesh modifier route demonstrated in FY2026 provided an alternative way to solve this issue by

averting use of displaced mesh, which proved to work, making the transient work possible even

without the Griffin-side fix. In the meantime, Griffin team has been working on a solution within

the displaced mesh scheme, which will also be assessed in FY2027.

 The second is the maturity of the Sockeye features described in Section 3.2.4. Gravity arrived

in time to be carried in the steady-state model as an evaporator pool, but tuning that pool requires

the transient model to exercise it, and the partial-wick treatment has yet to be implemented. The

transient nuclear system tests provide experimental data of a kind that is rarely available for

multiphysics code validation. A selection of those scenarios, chosen for how well they exercise the

coupled models, will be investigated in FY2027.

4 Enhancement of Heat-Pipe Microreactor Models

4.1 Model Status and FY2026 Scope

 The HPMR used in this project is a conceptual design assembled to gather the technologies of

interest to microreactor developers — control drums, liquid-metal heat pipes, hydride moderators,

and TRISO fuel in a graphite monolith — rather than to reproduce any single vendor concept [29].

It has served as the primary vehicle for demonstrating NEAMS multiphysics capability since

FY2021 [30], and the resulting full-core model, the Micro-Reactor Application Driver (MRAD)

modeling problem [31], has been published on the VTB [24], where it has been picked up by external

users including Westinghouse for fission matrix method evaluation [32, 33] and the DOE-NE DNCSH

program for criticality safety analyses [34].

 Two variants are currently maintained. The original potassium working fluid variant (K-HPMR)

is the model into which full-core solid mechanics was implemented in FY2025. The sodium working

fluid variant (Na-HPMR) was constructed in FY2025 to align with the recently validated sodium

heat pipe experiments and with Sockeye’s LCVF capability, and it enabled mechanistic heat pipe

transients and the first HPMR startup simulation.

 FY2026 enhances the HPMR model along four directions, addressed in turn below: a systematic

structural assessment of the graphite monolith under combined thermal and irradiation loading

(Section 4.2); adoption of advanced heat pipe modeling features (Section 4.3); addition of a secondary

loop and balance of plant so that the heat sink is modeled mechanistically rather than prescribed

(Section 4.4); and elevation of TRISO fuel performance evaluation from the assembly level to the

full-core level (Section 4.5).

4.2 Advanced Thermomechanics of the Graphite Monolith

 In the HPMR and GCMR concepts examined in this project, graphite is the primary structural

and matrix material. A monolithic graphite block accommodates the key core components — TRISO

 32

fuel compacts, hydride moderator modules, burnable poisons, and heat pipes (or coolant channels

in the GCMR) — and graphite also forms the matrix of the TRISO compacts themselves. It is

therefore critical both as the thermal medium for heat conduction and as the structural member

governing the mechanical response of the core.

 Graphite is also a demanding material to model. Its thermal conductivity, specific heat, thermal

expansion, elastic moduli, creep rate, and dimensional stability all depend on temperature and

on fast neutron fluence, and that fluence dependence is non-monotonic: irradiation first drives

volumetric shrinkage and then, past a turnaround fluence, swelling. In a monolith that is constrained

by its own geometry and subject to steep radial and axial temperature gradients, these effects

generate internal stresses that are not present at beginning of life and that cannot be inferred from

a thermal analysis alone. Assessing whether those stresses remain within acceptable bounds over

the intended core lifetime is the motivation for the work in this subsection.

 Through FY2024, HPMR model development focused on neutronics, heat transfer, and hydride

moderator behavior; solid mechanics had not been integrated into the full-core model set. Because

the reactor operates in the thermal spectrum, solid mechanics effects — principally fuel thermal

expansion — are less dominant than in fast-spectrum microreactors such as KRUSTY, and FY2025

confirmed this directly: adding solid mechanics to the full-core K-HPMR model changed keff by

roughly 127 pcm and left the fuel temperature distribution essentially unchanged, with maximum

fuel temperatures differing by less than 0.01 K. The value of the implementation lies elsewhere. It

provides stress fields throughout the reactor, which makes structural assessment possible and which

supplies the mechanical boundary conditions needed for full-core, multiphysics-informed TRISO

fuel performance evaluation.

4.2.1 Full Implementation of Thermomechanics

 The FY2025 implementation was deliberately partial. Solid mechanics was solved on the

fuel compact and inner core region only, excluding the outer reflectors and control drums, with

diffusion kernels used to disperse displacements smoothly across the excluded components so that the

 33

displacement field remained continuous at the interface. Convective heat flux boundary conditions

were applied to all external surfaces at 800 K. The mesh also had to be refined by roughly 106%

over the VTB model, which converges neutronics and heat transfer but not thermomechanics, to

obtain convergence in both steady-state and transient scenarios.

 This year’s effort completes that implementation and extends it to both heat-pipe microreactor

variants, K-HPMR and NA-HPMR. The solid mechanics solution covers the full set of structurally

active core regions – fuel compacts, graphite monolith, moderator and its cladding, and the axial

reflectors. The simplified symmetry-plane treatment from FY2025 is replaced with boundary

conditions that correctly enforce mirror (reflective) pressure conditions on the 1/6-core model,

resolving the convergence limitations. The neutronics and heat pipe components of each model

are unchanged and inherited directly from the existing K-HPMR and NA-HPMR Multiphysics

models; the work described here is confined to the BISON solid mechanics extension, implemented

identically across both models.

 One enhancement includes applying well-defined mechanical boundary conditions to the dis-

placement degrees of freedom to reflect the geometric symmetry of the reduced core model and to

eliminate rigid-body motion. On the flat, axis-aligned symmetry planes, normal displacement is

constrained to zero using simple Dirichlet boundary conditions: the displacement component normal

to each flat face is fixed on the face, and the two components normal to the intersecting centerline

boundary are also fixed, together pinning the in-plane rigid-body modes. Axial displacement is fixed

to zero on the core base to prevent rigid-body translation in the vertical direction while leaving

the top surface free to expand upward under thermal loading. The 60◦ angled symmetry plane

cannot be handled the same way, as no single displacement component is normal to the inclined face.

An InclinedNoDisplacementBC is applied to all three displacement components on the inclined

boundary, using a penalty parameter of 1010 to enforce that the displacement component normal to

the inclined plane vanishes. This replaces the approximate symmetry treatment used previously.

 Material-specific thermal expansion eigenstrain and elasticity models are assigned to each

structurally active block in both models: a graphite-matrix eigenstrain model for the fuel compacts,

 34

a grade-specific (H-451) eigenstrain model for the graphite monolith, a constant isotropic coefficient

for the moderator, and temperature-dependent eigenstrain and elasticity models for the stainless-

steel cladding and BeO reflectors. In the outer reflector, poison, gas-gap, shield, and heat-pipe

placeholder blocks, a diffusion kernel with isotropic diffusivity propagates the displacement solution

smoothly from the mechanically active interior, preserving mesh continuity without introducing

artificial stiffness for neutronics convergence.

 Computed displacements are transferred from BISON to Griffin at each timestep, with a

geometric correction applied on the inclined symmetry boundary to keep the transferred field

consistent with the inclined boundary constraint. Griffin’s DFEM-SN transport solver is configured

with use displaced mesh = true, so the neutron transport equation is evaluated on the resulting

displaced mesh, so thermally induced deformation of the fuel, moderator, monolith, and reflector

regions is reflected directly in the keff and flux solution for both models.

 Transient capability, however, remains specific to K-HPMR: the coupled neutronics-thermomechanics-

heat pipe model has been extended to fully transient scenarios, using the steady-state solution as

the initial condition. NA-HPMR thermomechanics is currently limited to steady-state as the sodium

heat pipe Sockeye model has not yet been extended to transient simulation.

 These extensions were demonstrated on both heat-pipe microreactor variants, with results

presented separately for K-HPMR and NA-HPMR (Section 4.2.2). For K-HPMR, both the steady-

state and load-following transient thermomechanics demonstrations were carried out, and the fully

coupled neutronics-thermomechanics-heat pipe model will be made available in the VTB. For

NA-HPMR, only the steady-state thermomechanics model has been demonstrated to date, and its

results are used to support the TRISO fuel performance analysis presented in Section 4.5.

 Core deformation tracks this thermal evolution directly (Figure 4.3). Displacement magnitude

increases immediately during the temperature excursion, peaking at ∼1.4 mm, with the largest dis-

relaxes to a new nominal profile as temperatures stabilize. The deformation is dominated by axial

expansion (disp z), and the resulting field remains continuous throughout the transient, consistent

with smooth, thermally driven expansion (Figure 4.4). Because contact between components is

not yet modeled, differential expansion between core materials with differing thermal expansion

coefficients does not produce the localized interface stress concentrations that would be expected in

practice, and these are not captured here.

4.2.3 Graphite Property Models and Grade Selection

 FY2025 compared the graphite models available in BISON and Grizzly to inform code selection.

BISON provides thermal conductivity and specific heat models covering a broad set of grades (A3-3,

A3-27, H-451, IG-110, PCEA, NG2020), applicable to both TRISO-loaded and TRISO-free graphite,

whereas Grizzly provides none. For mechanical behavior the balance reverses: both codes provide

thermal expansion, elasticity, creep, and irradiation eigenstrain models depending on temperature

and fast neutron fluence, but Grizzly covers more grades — notably creep for IG-110, JAEA, and

AGC grades in addition to H-451, and irradiation eigenstrain for AGC3. BISON’s coverage is

complete for H-451, so a core built from that grade can be analyzed in BISON alone; any other

grade requires Grizzly, and analyses needing both heat conduction and grade-specific mechanics in

one application require both codes to be loaded together.

4.2.4 Dose Effects on Graphite Models

 Different grades of graphite are used in HPMR. In previous full-core models, for simplicity, A3-3

grade graphite models are used for all the graphite components of the HPMR. In reality, A3-3 is

only used as the matrix graphite of TRISO fuel compacts, while the main monolith structure should

be made of by graphite such as H-451. As discussed in previous report, both BISON and Grizzly

are equipped with thermophysical and mechanical models of a series of grades of graphite with

dependence on temperature and radiation dose. Both BISON and Grizzly models were investigated

and evaluated this year. In this report, the results based on the H-451 grade graphite models in

BISON are discussed as a representative case.

 Graphite in a HPMR accumulates fast neutron damage over the core life. Two effects are

involved in these irradiated graphite: thermal conductivity degradation, and irradiation-induced

dimensional change. Both are represented in the BISON material models we adopted, so their

coupled consequence for core temperature and reactivity can be evaluated directly.

 • Uniform sweep (seven cases, 0 to 1 × 1025 n m−2 ). A single fluence is applied to all graphite

4.3 Advanced Heat Pipe Model Assessment: Non-Condensable Gas Effects

 Sockeye offers three levels of heat pipe fidelity: an effective conduction model, the LCVF

model [20], and a two-phase flow model. The full-core HPMR model used the effective conductance

model through FY2024. In FY2025 it was upgraded to LCVF in the Na-HPMR variant, and the

upgrade was verified to couple consistently within the multiphysics framework at steady state and

in transients. The practical payoff was a startup simulation, which captured vapor front progression

along the heat pipe and the corresponding activation of heat removal — behavior the effective

conductance approach cannot represent at all, since it presumes an operating heat pipe from the

outset.

 In FY2026, the NCG model in Sockeye’s LCVF model was assessed within the full-core HPMR

multiphysics model to study the impact of this advanced option on heat pipe performance and

overall reactor behavior.

 Sodium heat pipes accumulate NCG over life, the vapor flow sweeps it to the closed end of the

condenser, where it collects and blocks part of the condensing surface. Sockeye offers two ways to

model this [23], sketched in Figure 4.10. The sharp-front model treats the inventory as a single plug

of uniform composition pinned against the closed end, and merely tracks its length. On the other

hand, the mixture model carries NCG (argon in this study) as a second species, transported with

the vapor and free to diffuse, so the composition becomes a continuous axial field with a graded

front. The former approach is essentially free and needs only the inventory as input; the latter adds

a species transport equation, a binary diffusion closure, and an interfacial heat transfer coefficient

at the liquid–vapor boundary. Both approaches have their merits and limitations, and the choice

depends on the desired balance between computational cost and physical fidelity.

 A property-model constraint complicates the comparison. The mixture formulation requires

the vapor to be governed by the ideal-gas equation of state (EOS) to facilitate second gas mixture.

4.4 Secondary Loop Model and Balance of Plant

 Transient analyses of the HPMR to date have imposed the heat sink as a boundary condition:

the condenser-side heat removal is prescribed, and a load-following transient is initiated by simply

reducing it. This is adequate for demonstrating the core’s inherent feedback, but it sidesteps the

question of what the secondary side can actually deliver, and it cannot represent scenarios in which

the power conversion system itself is the initiating event or the limiting component. Adding a

secondary loop closes that gap and makes the modeled load-following behavior attributable to a

physical demand rather than an assumed one.

4.4.1 Standalone Loop Model

 The HPMR secondary side differs from the GCMR case addressed in FY2025 in an important

respect. In the GCMR, the primary coolant is helium flowing through channels in the core, so the

interface between core and BOP is a fluid boundary that THM handles naturally. In the HPMR,

there is no primary coolant loop: heat leaves the core through the condenser sections of several

hundred individual heat pipes, and the secondary working fluid takes it up across those condensers.

The coupling between Sockeye and SAM/THM at the condenser is therefore the central modeling

problem for this task; it is addressed in Section 4.4.2.

 Unlike the coupled model, which prescribes fixed core inlet conditions (inlet temperature, outlet

pressure, and inlet velocity) for the detailed per-channel coolant analysis, the BOP model computes

these boundary conditions dynamically from the plant response. This closes the loop between the

core and the power conversion system and enables plant-level transient analyses, such as the startup

sequence and load-following maneuvers, that a standalone core model cannot capture.

 The reactor concept, fuel form, and core layout are described in Section 4.1; only the parameters

the loop model consumes directly are given here. The core operates at 2 MW thermal. Each heat

pipe is an annular-wick, potassium-charged device with an SS316 envelope, sized to remove a

nominal 1800 W; this value follows from dividing the 2 MW core power among heat pipes with a

 53

180 cm evaporator at a 1.05 cm outer radius. The condenser section is 90 cm long, and this is the

length over which the secondary loop model picks up heat from each heat pipe, as described in

Section 4.4.2.

 The power conversion system was selected from several candidate technologies — steam Rankine,

closed and open Brayton, supercritical carbon dioxide Brayton, Stirling, and static conversion. A

recuperated open-air Brayton cycle was chosen as the reference system. It needs no cooling-water

source, which matters for a microreactor sited in a remote or arid location, and its compressors,

turbines, and controls draw on decades of aeroderivative and industrial gas-turbine experience. It

also reaches acceptable efficiency at the moderate turbine inlet temperatures, 500–850°C, that a

heat-pipe microreactor can supply. Appendix A gives the full technology comparison, the candidate

table, and the turbomachinery constraints that shaped the final selection.

 The BOP loop is modeled with THM. On the primary side, the core geometry is simplified

to a one-dimensional/two-dimensional (1D–2D) representation. The whole model is built in y-z

plane and the original point is set at the bottom of the lower reflector to make future coupling

seamless. This heat structure represents the combined fuel, graphite and moderator regions using

concentric hollow cylinders with geometric parameters chosen to preserve the total volume of each

material region. On the secondary side, the model represents the complete Brayton cycle loop:

ambient-temperature air enters a compressor, is preheated by exhaust gases in a recuperator, and is

then further heated in a primary-to-secondary heat exchanger where the full 2 MW is transferred

from the heat pipe (Fig. 4.13). The heat pipe is represented by an effective-conduction model

rather than a two-phase model, the vapor core conductivity is calibrated to 1.0 × 106 W/m.K. The

THM BOP model also incorporates the mechanical dynamics of the turbomachinery shaft system,

including the coupled rotational inertia of the compressor, turbine, and generator. A motor provides

the initial torque to spin up the shaft from rest during reactor startup. Motor torque is governed by

a proportional-integral-derivative (PID) controller, which rapidly increases the shaft speed during

the first few seconds of the startup transient and then gradually reduces the motor torque to zero

once the turbine generates sufficient torque to sustain operation independently.

4.4.2 The Loop Mode Coupled with Full-Core HPMR

 The standalone loop model of Section 4.4.1 exercises the THM Brayton cycle, its startup and

trip logic, in isolation, with the primary side reduced to a simplified core heat structure and the

 62

Figure 4.20: Air temperatures at the hot leg and cold leg of the recuperator during the start-up
transient

heat-pipe condenser represented as a prescribed boundary. This subsection couples that same loop

model to the full-core HPMR neutronics, conduction, and heat-pipe model of Sections 4.1 and 4.3,

so that the secondary side computes its own boundary conditions from the core state rather than

receiving them as an assumption.

 The coupled model extends the Griffin→BISON→Sockeye MultiApp hierarchy already used for

the full-core HPMR analyses of Section 4.2.2 with a second sub-application for BISON: the THM

loop model of Section 4.4.1. Griffin, the top-level parent, solves the DFEM-SN eigenvalue problem

on the one-sixth core sector and transfers the fission power density to BISON, which returns fuel

and monolith temperature feedback. BISON in turn drives two sub-applications of its own: the 192

Sockeye heat pipes of the sector, each carried as an individually resolved sub-application positioned

at its true in-core location, receiving the local wall heat flux from BISON’s conduction solution

and returning the heat-pipe wall temperature; and the THM loop, which receives the aggregated

heat-pipe condenser duty (to be described later) and returns the primary-heat-exchanger (PHX)

outlet pressure and inlet velocity and temperature that close the boundary condition on all 192

 63

pipes. Every exchange is executed each outer fixed-point iteration, so the neutronics, conduction,

heat-pipe, and loop states are driven to a single mutually consistent solution rather than coupled

once and left fixed. As heat pipe frozen condition is involved during the Brayton cycle startup, the

effective conduction model is used in this case for Sockeye instead of the LCVF model to avoid

numerical difficulties in reaching steady state.

 Where the standalone loop model of Section 4.4.1 represents the heat pipe as a uniform

effective-conduction path, the coupled model instead resolves each of the 192 pipes explicitly and lets

their combined condenser duty load the loop’s PHX directly. Each pipe reports its own condenser

heat-rejection rate together with its axial position along the condenser; these 192 (position, rate)

pairs are collected at the BISON parent and passed to the loop sub-application, where they define, by

nearest-neighbor interpolation in the axial coordinate, the heating profile imposed on the PHX flow

channel. Aggregation therefore preserves the axial distribution of heat rejection along the condenser

length while collapsing the sector’s 192 individually resolved pipes onto the single PHX channel

that represents the loop’s working fluid; the loop, in turn, returns one common secondary-side state

to every pipe’s condenser boundary. No lumped or symmetry-reduced heat-pipe representation is

used anywhere in this path.

 At convergence, the coupled model reaches keff = 1.0472597, a fuel temperature of 1021.17 K

(maximum), and a monolith temperature of 1020.05 K (maximum). 1.977 MW out of 2.074 MW full

power is picked up across the PHX, and the generator delivers 400.0 kW at a cycle efficiency of 20.2%.

The core operates at an average fuel temperature of 965.75 K and an average heat-pipe surface

temperature of 958.76 K. All 192 heat pipes operate within all five of the Sockeye operating limits

(i.e., sonic, capillary, entrainment, viscous, and boiling). A cycle efficiency near 20% is consistent

with what a system at this temperature level can be expected to deliver, and reaching higher values

4.5 Full-Core TRISO Fuel Performance Analysis

 This work is the first application within the NA-HPMR modeling framework to couple full-core

multiphysics predictions, including the fuel compact hydrostatic stress obtained from the newly

implemented solid-mechanics model, to detail representative TRISO fuel performance calculations.

 TRISO fuel offers functional containment of fission products and low failure rates during

accident scenarios, which is why it is common to microreactor concepts. Evaluating its performance

in a full core is difficult because of the length-scale disparity: a compact contains thousands of

particles whose individual layers are tens of micrometers thick, embedded in a core meters across.

Explicit heterogeneous modeling of every particle in a full core remains out of reach, so full-core

heat conduction models represent compacts as a homogenized medium with an effective thermal

conductivity, and fuel performance is evaluated separately on representative particles driven by

conditions taken from the core model.

 Previous work in this project has established both ends of that chain. Assembly-level TRISO

performance analyses were completed and published on the VTB, and FY2025 addressed the accuracy

of the homogenization itself: a comparison of homogenization strategies against a fully heterogeneous

reference model of a GCMR compact showed that the conventional treatment underpredicts peak

kernel temperatures and temperature reactivity effects, particularly under the non-uniform power

deposition characteristic of transients, and a fast-running multiscale HSD treatment was developed to

recover that accuracy at acceptable cost. Separately, the implementation of full-core solid mechanics

in the HPMR model made the stress state of the core available for the first time.

 FY2026 connects these two developments by driving TRISO fuel performance evaluation

directly from the full-core multiphysics solution, so that the conditions applied to the particles —

temperature, temperature gradient, fast fluence, burnup, and the mechanical state of the surrounding

compact — come from the coupled model rather than from assumed or assembly-level boundary

conditions.

4.5.1 Coupling of Full-Core Conditions to TRISO Models

 Three parameters - fuel temperature, power density, and hydrostatic stress - are extracted from

the fuel compacts of the full-core NA-HPMR steady-state model (Section 4.2.2) to serve as spatial

boundary conditions for TRISO particle fuel performance analysis. This extraction is performed in

post-processing using a Python script that reads the MOOSE simulation output directly from the

Exodus (.e) file format.

 The full-core 1/6 model is executed using the MOOSE multiphysics framework, producing an

Exodus output file containing spatial field data across all mesh blocks. Within the fuel compact

blocks (fuel quad and fuel tri), the variables of interest are stored in two distinct forms depending

on their underlying finite element representation: fuel temperature is stored as an elemental variable

using a constant monomial basis, so a single representative value is assigned to each element, while

power density and hydrostatic stress are stored as nodal variables using a first-order Lagrange basis,

so values are defined at the mesh nodes and vary continuously across each element.

 The Exodus file is read using the netCDF4 Python library, which provides direct access to the

underlying netCDF data structure of the Exodus format. Fuel compact elements are identified

by targeting the fuel quad and fuel tri mesh blocks, and for each element in these blocks the

centroid coordinates are computed by averaging the coordinates of the element’s constituent nodes

obtained from the mesh connectivity table. Elemental variables are read directly as a single value

per element, while nodal variables are mapped to element-level values by averaging the nodal values

at all nodes belonging to each element, yielding a consistent element-centered representation across

all three parameters.

 To facilitate systematic analysis of the spatial distribution of these fuel performance parameters,

the extracted element-level data are organized into a two-dimensional binning scheme defined by

radial distance from the reactor axis and axial position along the fuel compact height. The radial
 p
coordinate is computed for each element centroid as r = x2 + y 2 , assuming the reactor axis passes

through the origin. Axial binning is applied at uniform intervals of 0.1 m spanning the full axial

 68

extent of the fuel compact region (0.2 m to 1.8 m), corresponding to the axial element layers in the

mesh. Radial bins are distributed uniformly from the reactor center to the outermost radial extent

of the fuel compact blocks, with the number of radial bins set equal to the number of distinct radial

element centroid positions present in the mesh, determined by examining the raw extracted data

prior to binning. This ensures that each radial bin corresponds to a physically meaningful radial

layer in the mesh without introducing empty bins between real data points; increasing the number

of bins beyond this natural mesh resolution would not yield additional spatial information, since

the underlying elemental data does not exist at finer radial intervals than those defined by the mesh

geometry.

 Within each spatial bin defined by a unique (rbin , zbin ) pair, the mean, minimum, and maximum

values of each parameter are computed across all fuel elements assigned to that bin. Each bin

is assigned a unique location identifier (loc id), numbered sequentially from 1 to n in order of

increasing radial distance and then increasing axial position, allowing consistent cross-referencing of

spatial location with parameter values across all output files. The extraction method produces four

CSV output files sharing a common loc id column, enabling direct comparison of values at each

spatial location: one file containing raw parameter values at every individual fuel element centroid,

including the exact x, y, z, and r coordinates, and three files containing the spatially averaged mean,

minimum, and maximum parameter values, respectively, for each (rbin , zbin ) bin. These binned

profiles serve as the spatial input distributions for the TRISO particle fuel performance analysis

described in Section 4.5.2. With the computed binned profiles, a Python script was used to generate

BISON input files for each populated (rbin , zbin ) location. The script was run separately using the

maximum, average, and minimum values within each bin to generate three corresponding sets of

TRISO particle input files. For each spatial location, the local hydrostatic stress, power density, and

fuel temperature were extracted from the binned profiles and applied as the boundary conditions for

the corresponding steady-state BISON TRISO particle simulation. For the NA-HPMR steady-state

model, this resulted in 672 individual TRISO particle input files for the full grid of 16 axial bins

and 42 radial bins, as shown in Figure 4.23. The resulting cases were independently dispatched as

an HPC job array, allowing the TRISO particle simulations to be performed in parallel.

 A mechanistic Pd penetration model developed by INL was implemented this year to complement

 71

the existing empirical Pd penetration correlation in BISON. Where the empirical correlation is a

temperature-dependent curve fit to historical particle-fuel penetration data, the new mechanistic

model builds the penetration depth mechanistically [35]. The molecular dynamics simulations

provide Pd diffusivities in SiC grain bulk and grain boundaries, which inform a mesoscale phase-field

diffusion model, which in turn yields a reduced-order model (ROM) for how SiC microstructure

governs the Pd penetration rate, evaluated within BISON at the engineering scale. The two models

predict different penetration depths at NA-HPMR conditions. The empirical model predicts a mean

penetration depth of 0.703 µm, while the mechanistic model predicts a mean of only 0.0026 µm

(Figure 4.24) and is more spatially sensitive, with a relative uncertainty of 86.9% (CV ≈ 0.41)

compared to 14.0% for the empirical correlation. Both models peak in spatial sensitivity at the

similar core location (r ≈ 0.62–0.65, z ≈ 0.95–1.05), distinct from the stress hot spots discussed

below, indicating that Pd penetration risk is governed by a different local driver than mechanical

failure risk.

 A gap of this magnitude between empirical and mechanistic Pd penetration depth predictions

is not unique to this study; it reflects an active, recognized area of TRISO fuel performance model

development. Existing empirical Pd correlations are fit to experimental penetration data that

implicitly folds grain-boundary transport enhancement, stress effects, and silicide reaction kinetics

into a single Arrhenius term, and that data shows significant scatter outside the specific conditions

under which it was measured [35]. Recent Bayesian uncertainty-quantification work extending this

same mechanistic ROM, calibrated and validated against a broad suite of in-pile and out-of-pile

experiments spanning different particle compositions, geometries, and operating conditions, found

that the mechanistic approach retained credible, well-characterized accuracy across that full range,

whereas the empirical correlation did not generalize as well outside the specific conditions it was fit

to [36]. A comparable pattern has emerged for other fission-product species transported through SiC,

where independent multiscale mechanistic models built the same way have been developed for silver

diffusion [37], and a more recent silver transport model built from first-principles grain-boundary

diffusivities was found to overpredict measured diffusivity until a multiplicative correction for

reversible silver trapping at nanoscale pores was introduced [38]. This suggests that mechanistic,

 72

physics-derived transport models for fission products in SiC require an explicit calibration step

against experiment and that the gap seen here is best treated as a model-induced uncertainty

to be resolved through continued validation rather than as an indication that either model is

incorrect. In either case, both predicted penetration depths remain a small fraction of the SiC layer

thickness under nominal steady-state conditions, therefore neither result indicates near-term risk to

fission-product retention. The empirical correlation defines failure as Pd penetration reaching half

the SiC layer thickness; no analogous failure criterion yet exists for the mechanistic model, so its

predictions can currently be compared only in terms of absolute penetration depth, not against a

defined threshold.

 These stress states translate into varying Weibull failure probabilities for the two protective

layers. The IPyC layer shows a mean failure probability of 49.9%, ranging from 47.6% to 55.0%

across the core (11.2% relative uncertainty; Figure 4.26 (a)). The apparent high failure probability

is not indicative of impending particle failure; rather, it is an expected outcome of the modeling

approach and a well-documented characteristic of TRISO fuel performance analysis [39]. IPyC

cracking is treated in BISON as a statistical, stress-driven phenomenon, wherein a Weibull fracture

criterion is applied to the local tensile stress state within the layer. Accordingly, the predicted

failure rate represents the probability that the stress in a given layer exceeds its statistical strength,

rather than the outcome of a deterministic failure criterion. This probabilistic treatment is standard

practice for coupling IPyC cracking behavior to subsequent SiC layer loading in BISON TRISO

failure analyses [39].

 Under fast-neutron irradiation, the PyC layers undergo anisotropic irradiation-induced defor-

mation, while the porous buffer layer undergoes densification and yields to kernel swelling and

fission-gas-driven deformation. The resulting differential deformation, internal gas buildup, and

IPyC—SiC bonding govern the IPyC hoop stress. Although IPyC shrinkage can generate tensile

stress, irradiation creep partially relaxes it, such that the net stress reflects a balance among

 74

dimensional change, creep, internal pressure, and inter-layer constraint. The particle-average IPyC

stress is nearly uniform across the core, and the model returns a failure probability of approximately

50% for particles across the full core, rather than at an isolated defect location. As IPyC cracking

functions as a loading condition transferred to the SiC layer, rather than as a fuel failure criterion, its

predicted rate does not indicate a performance concern. BISON-based studies of coupled IPyC—SiC

failure similarly find that the stress concentration following a predicted IPyC crack can raise the

local SiC failure probability by orders of magnitude relative to the intact-particle case, which is

why the two layers are tracked as separate, complementary failure metrics [39]. Consistent with

this framework, the SiC layer, which provides the primary fission-product retention barrier, shows

a mean failure probability of only 2.13 × 10−6 %, effectively negligible for NA-HPMR steady-state

operation. Notably, the IPyC and SiC failure probabilities both peak in spatial variability at the

same location, r ≈ 0.84, z ≈ 1.75, coinciding with the IPyC peak-stress hot spot identified above.

The relatively lower temperature near the edge of the active fuel region increases particle material

stiffness and reduces stress relaxation mechanisms, and therefore acts as the primary driver for

elevated IPyC stress and failure probabilities.

4.6 Summary of HPMR Work

 FY2026 extended the full-core HPMR multiphysics model along four fronts — structural

mechanics, heat-pipe fidelity, plant-level coupling, and particle-scale fuel performance — and closed

the loop between the reactor and the balance of plant for the first time:

 • Full solid mechanics was implemented in the HPMR model, and the resulting stress and

 displacement fields were used to assess graphite dose effects over the design life. At a nominal

 ten-year irradiation the total effect on the core is 4.5 K on peak fuel temperature, 3.2 K on

 peak monolith temperature, and 4.3 pcm of reactivity — negligible against the quantities they

 compete with, so graphite irradiation is not a life-limiting phenomenon for this core.

 • Both non-condensable-gas treatments available in Sockeye, a sharp-front model and a mixture

 model, were demonstrated in the coupled full-core Na-HPMR model with 192 individually

 resolved heat pipes, and their effects were decomposed from a co-varying vapor equation-

 of-state change; neither model required any change to the multiphysics coupling scheme or

 fixed-point tolerances. Non-condensable-gas modeling therefore integrates smoothly into the

 76

  full-core HPMR framework.

• The standalone THM Brayton loop model of the secondary side was coupled to the full-core

  neutronics, conduction, and heat-pipe model, so that the loop computes its own boundary

  conditions from the reactor state rather than receiving them as an assumption. In the resulting

  integrated demonstration, all 192 heat pipes of the sector are resolved explicitly and load the

  loop’s primary heat exchanger through their individual condenser duties; the coupled system

  converges to keff = 1.0472597, with a sector fission power of 345.6 kW (2.074 MW full-core)

  and a sector heat-pipe duty of 339.6 kW (2.037 MW full-core) delivered into the loop, and a

  generator output of 400.0 kW at a cycle efficiency of 20.2%.

• Full-core steady-state fuel temperature, power density, and hydrostatic stress fields were

  extracted from the NA-HPMR model and used to drive 672 spatially resolved TRISO particle

  fuel-performance simulations across the core. A new mechanistic palladium-penetration model

  was implemented alongside the existing empirical correlation, and IPyC/SiC layer stresses and

  Weibull failure probabilities were computed at every location; the IPyC layer shows a mean

  failure probability of 49.9%, an expected outcome of its stress-driven statistical treatment

  rather than a sign of impending particle failure (Section 4.5), while the SiC layer, which

  provides the primary fission-product retention barrier, shows a mean failure probability of

  only 2.13 × 10−6 %, and both Pd-penetration models predict ingress depths that remain a

  small fraction of the SiC layer thickness. Taken together, these metrics are consistent with the

  range expected for sound TRISO particle behavior, though the high IPyC cracking probability

  warrants continued tracking as an input to SiC layer loading.

5 Enhancement of Gas-Cooled Microreactor Models

5.1 Model Status and FY2026 Scope

 The GCMR examined in this project is a 20 MWth conceptual design developed at Argonne as

a modeling benchmark. The core is 2.42 m in diameter and 2.40 m tall with a 200 cm active height,

divided into three radial fuel zones, surrounded by BeO axial and radial reflectors, and controlled

by twelve drums located in the reflector. Fuel is TRISO particles at 40% packing fraction with

19.75% low-enriched uranium (LEU) enrichment, embedded in graphite blocks. Moderator pins of

YH1.8 are chromium-coated and enclosed in FeCrAl envelopes, and burnable poison rods of Gd2 O3

are distributed axially through the assemblies. Helium at 7 MPa flows upward through coolant

channels, entering near 650 ◦ C and leaving near 850 ◦ C. The concept is designed for a 9.5-year cycle

length and a Brayton power conversion cycle.

 Analysis of GCMR technology began in FY2022 at the assembly level, followed by initial

full-core modeling and assembly-level TRISO performance analyses. The core was redesigned in

FY2024 to enable 1/6 reflective symmetry, and a steady-state multiphysics model incorporating

hydride performance was developed. FY2025 extended the model to time-dependent simulation

with a suite of four transients — coolant depressurization, loss of coolant velocity, coolant inlet

temperature drop, and single coolant channel blockage — each of which the reactor shut down

passively through its inherent negative temperature reactivity coefficient. That year also produced

a Brayton-cycle BOP model, an assessment of fission product poisoning during load following using

Griffin’s poison tracking capability, and the HSD treatment for TRISO compact heterogeneity.

Hydrogen redistribution in the yttrium hydride moderator was found to shift keff by roughly 600 pcm,

large enough that SWIFT cannot be omitted from the coupled system.

 FY2026 work on the GCMR concentrates on two capability demonstrations: online cross-section

generation, demonstrated in standalone neutronic calculations on the two-dimensional full-core

model, and extension of the BOP model so that it is driven by the coupled neutronics solution

rather than by a prescribed power history.

5.2 Online Cross-Section Generation

5.2.1 Motivation and Approach

 Multiphysics models in this project have relied on tabulated multigroup cross-section libraries

generated offline. A lattice physics or Monte Carlo calculation — Serpent for most of the models

here — is run over a grid of state parameters such as fuel temperature, moderator temperature,

and moderator hydrogen concentration, and Griffin interpolates within that grid during the coupled

solve. The approach works, but it carries two costs. The library must be generated before the

coupled model can run at all, and the cost of populating it grows combinatorially with the number of

state parameters, which is precisely the direction microreactor models have been moving as hydride

behavior, poison tracking, and thermal expansion are added. Interpolation between grid points also

introduces an error that is difficult to bound, particularly near the edges of the tabulated range or

when a transient drives the local state outside it.

 Online cross-section generation removes the need of preparing a tabulated library by generating

cross sections within the multiphysics coupling steps at the local state conditions. For a steady-

state model, where the state converges to a fixed distribution, this is a natural first application:

the cross-section generation cost is incurred a bounded number of times, and the result can be

compared directly against the tabulated-library solution to quantify the interpolation error that the

conventional workflow incurs.

 Griffin’s online multigroup cross-section generation capability was implemented through the Self-

Shielding Application Programming Interface (SSAPI) in ISOXML. This capability was developed

primarily for thermal reactors, with particular emphasis on advanced TRISO-fueled reactors concepts,

including prismatic high temperature gas-cooled reactors (HTGR) and Pebble-bed reactors [18].

 For TRISO-fueled reactors, Griffin solves the model-dependent, double-heterogeneity problem

by identifying resonant fuel and non-fuel isotopes. It constructs a representative unit-cell models that

capture both the micro-scale TRISO particle structure and the macro-scale fuel region environment.

 79

Ultra-fine group slowing-down calculations are first performed to evaluate the self-shielding effects

within the TRISO-bearing fuel. Ultra-fine slowing-down calculations are also carried out for a

macro-cell representing the compact fuel region surrounded by the moderators and structural

materials. In both stages, Dancoff factors are calculated and are used to represent lattice shadowing

effects. The resulting ultra-fine energy spectrum from the macro-cell calculation has been used to

condense self-shielded data into broader 70-g cross-sections.

 Griffin also treats the non-fuel resonant materials separately using the Tone method or the

Dancoff Category-based Equivalence Theory (DCET) method. Through this process, self-shielded

and homogenized cross sections are generated for the different material regions in each pin cell

and at each mesh plane. These online-generated cross sections are then passed directly to Griffin’s

transport solver for calculating the critical eigenvalues, full-core fluxes and power distributions.

 From a user perspective, the online cross-section generation workflow begins with defining the

problem-dependent geometry using the mesh block and specifying material compositions in the

composition block. Cross section regions are defined directly in the geometry setup by assigning a

unique region id. Griffin then automatically constructs the self-shielding region IDs by combining

the region id with the material id, radial id (if present), pin geom setup id, pin id and axial plane id.

At present, the Griffin implementation only supports extruded geometries. For cases in which

multiple axial layers share the same radial compositions, grouping is possible through an additional

element ID, such as axial region setup id.

 Once the geometry being setup and cross section regions are defined, the online cross-section

calculation is initiated through the SelfShielding UserObject. Temperature distributions within the

core can be supplied through an AuxVariable. Griffin does not operate directly on continuous-energy

nuclear data, instead, it uses a default 7178 light water reactor (LWR)-based energy spectrum

for selfshielding calculations. Users may replace it with a problem-dependent library for further

improving the accuracy of the calculated cross sections for advanced reactor applicationsI. For

GCMR, the improved geometry was tallied from a Serpent assembly model and processed through

NJOY.

 80

 The work described here represents the beginning of testing this new Griffin capability using

the 2-D GCMR full core model, both for capability demonstration and for accuracy evaluation. The

default LWR spectrum and a detailed 7178 group neutron spectrum tallied from a representative

GCMR fuel assembly model using Serpent were both used as the library basis. The simulations

were performed assuming the core is under isothermal core conditions at 725 K. The ENDF/B-

VII.1 nuclear library was used throughout the testing. All calculations are standalone neutronic

calculations. Future work will extend this testing to a more detailed 3-D GCMR model and to

applications within the multiphysics simulation framework.

5.3 Extension of the Balance-of-Plant Model

 The FY2025 GCMR BOP model represents an open-air recuperated Brayton cycle built in

THM. Air enters through a compressor, is heated first by the exhaust gases in a recuperator and

then in a heat exchanger that transfers 20 MWth from the primary to the secondary loop, expands

through a turbine that drives both the generator and the compressor on a common shaft, and gives

up residual heat in the recuperator before being released. A motor, controlled by a PID loop, brings

the shaft up from rest and withdraws once the turbine produces matching torque. The standalone

model reproduced a stable startup, with core inlet and outlet temperatures reaching their design

values and the shaft settling near 130,000 RPM within 20,000 s.

 That model was then coupled to the full-core BISON model for a startup transient, with the

core geometry simplified in THM to a single representative coolant channel inside a concentric

heat structure preserving total volume. The coupling was deliberately one-directional in scope:

because the startup assumed a prompt initial power increase, Griffin was not run as the parent

application, and the power history computed separately by the full-core multiphysics model was

applied as a volumetric heat source. The demonstration established that the coupling works and

that long-duration transients are tractable, but it left the neutronic feedback out of the loop.

 The FY2026 BOP extension adds the coupled neutronics-to-plant feedback path that the

FY2025 one-directional demonstration lacked: Griffin now drives the outer iteration, BISON’s

full-core fuel and structure thermal solution sits beneath it, and the THM plant model returns actual

coolant conditions to the core rather than receiving a prescribed power history. Two scenarios are

analyzed with this hierarchy. The first is a plant startup transient that brings the plant from rest to a

converged, full-power steady state and includes the motor-driven shaft spin-up and motor-to-turbine

torque handoff. The second is a primary motor torque reduction transient, beginning from the

startup transient’s converged end state, in which a deliberate reduction in primary circulator motor

torque exercises the coupled plant’s response to a reduced-flow operating point. The two scenarios

are reported in sequence because of this shared starting point, but each is evaluated only against

itself; no comparison is drawn to any other run, prior-year result, or external documentation of the

 87

model. Additionally, the loop parameters of the original FY2025 model is tuned so that the inlet

coolant temperature is more close to the design level of 873 K.

5.3.1 Balance-of-Plant Model

 The BOP loop is modeled in THM as an open-air recuperated Brayton cycle: air enters through

a compressor, is heated first by turbine exhaust in a recuperator and then by the primary heat

exchanger carrying the reactor’s 20 MWth design duty from the primary loop, expands through a

turbine that drives both the generator and the compressor on a common shaft, and gives up its

residual heat in the recuperator before being released. The primary side of this loop is represented

at two different levels of spatial resolution rather than one. Within the loop model itself the core is

a single lumped 1D coolant channel that receives the total heating density computed by BISON

and returns bulk core inlet/outlet conditions to close the loop; separately, BISON drives its own

per-channel coolant sub-application, a set of 1D channels resolved at the discrete 2D positions of

the coolant channels within the modeled fuel sector, each of which exchanges wall temperature for

local fluid temperature and heat transfer coefficient with BISON directly. This 1D loop-scale / 2D

channel-scale split lets the plant dynamics be resolved at plant time and length scales while the

core retains channel-resolved thermal detail. Two power scales therefore coexist in this hierarchy

and are kept distinct throughout this section: BISON solves a one-sixth core sector, while the THM

loop is modeled at full-core scale, carrying the reactor’s full 20 MWth design duty.

 The secondary Brayton cycle – compressor, turbine, generator, and a startup motor – shares

a common shaft. A PID-controlled trip governs the motor-to-turbine handoff: the motor is held

at a commanded torque profile until its torque crosses a shutdown threshold of 150 N m and the

elapsed time since the start of that profile exceeds 100 s, at which point the trip status latches and

the motor torque is switched to an exponential shutdown with an 8000 s time constant, decaying

the motor’s contribution toward zero while the turbine carries the shaft going forward.

 88

5.3.2 Multiphysics Coupling

 The coupling hierarchy has three levels. Griffin is the parent application; it solves the DFEM-

SN neutronics problem with CMFD acceleration – an eigenvalue solve for the startup scenario, a

transient solve for the primary motor degradation scenario (Sections 5.3.3 and 5.3.4) – and drives a

full-solve BISON sub-application with the resulting power density. BISON, solving fuel and core

heat conduction, in turn drives two of its own THM sub-applications as siblings, each returning a

different pair of quantities:

 • A single THM application governing the full BOP loop as described in Section 5.3.1. It returns

 core inlet temperature, core outlet pressure, and core inlet velocity to BISON.

 • A series of of per-channel coolant model. It returns local fluid temperature and heat transfer

 coefficient to BISON.

 Demonstrating a capability internally is only part of the value this project delivers. The

6.1 OECD/NEA Microreactor Multiphysics Benchmark

6.1.1 Background and Status

 Multiphysics modeling capabilities for microreactors are being developed at institutions world-

wide. However, validation of these models is constrained by the scarcity of experimental data —

the same limitation that motivates the KRUSTY work described in Section 3. In the absence of

sufficient experimental data, cross-comparison among independently developed codes provides a

practical alternative. Several participants in the OECD/NEA Working Party on Scientific Issues of

Reactor Systems (WPRS) therefore requested the development of an international benchmark.

 In FY2025, the team prepared a benchmark proposal based on the Na-HPMR multiphysics

model published by this project on the VTB. The proposal received substantial support from the

international community and was subsequently accepted by the OECD/NEA. The benchmark is

organized into three phases over a three-year period. The final year will culminate in a milestone

report summarizing the results obtained throughout the activity:

    • 2026 — Phase 1

 – Phase 1A: Neutronics-only comparison. The comparison will evaluate keff , power distri-

 butions, control-drum worth, and temperature coefficients.

 – Phase 1B: Heat-pipe analysis. The comparison will evaluate heat-transfer performance

 under steady-state and transient conditions.

 • 2027 — Phase 2: Steady-state multiphysics comparison, including evaluations of keff , power

 distributions, and temperature distributions.

 • 2028 — Phase 3: Transient comparison, including load-following behavior and heat-pipe

 Three WPRS expert groups are involved: Physics of Reactor Systems (EGPRS), Reactor Core

7 Summary and Conclusion

 FY2026 extended all three microreactor models toward more complete coupled behavior. The

KRUSTY model took on heat pipes and a third code, which moved the validation basis from

the warm critical tests to the nuclear system test configuration; the HPMR model added solid

mechanics, non-condensable gas effects, a coupled secondary loop, and full-core TRISO evaluation;

and the GCMR model added online cross-section generation and closed the neutronic feedback path

to its balance of plant. Where a reference solution or a prior-year baseline existed, results were

checked against it, and the problems encountered in building these models were returned to the code

development teams as specific requests. Mature models continue to reach external users through

the VTB and the OECD/NEA benchmark.

7.1 Capability Demonstration

 FY2026 shifted the KRUSTY validation effort from the FY2025 warm critical reactivity-

insertion tests to the fully assembled nuclear system test configuration, with all heat pipes installed

and power conversion in place. Sockeye was integrated into the KRUSTY model for the first time,

with three heat pipes — one full-pipe model and two half-pipe symmetry models — coupled to

BISON’s clad wall through a Robin-type thermal boundary condition. A node-moving strategy built

on the MoveNodesByParsedExpressionModifier mesh modifier carried BISON’s displacement field

into Griffin’s transport mesh, with density rescaling conserving mass. The resulting three-code

Griffin/BISON/Sockeye model converged to a steady state at 750 W fission power, keff = 0.9959632

(405 pcm below delayed criticality), establishing the foundation for FY2027 transient work.

 FY2026 extended the full-core HPMR model in structural mechanics, heat-pipe fidelity, plant

coupling, and fuel performance. Full solid mechanics was implemented, and graphite dose over a

nominal ten-year irradiation changed peak fuel temperature by 4.5 K, peak monolith temperature

by 3.2 K, and reactivity by 4.3 pcm — negligible, so graphite irradiation is not life-limiting for this

core. Both of Sockeye’s non-condensable-gas treatments were demonstrated in the coupled full-core

 101

Na-HPMR model with 192 resolved heat pipes without changing the coupling scheme or fixed-point

tolerances. The THM Brayton loop was coupled to the full-core neutronics and heat-pipe model,

computing its own boundary conditions and converging to keff = 1.0472597, with a full-core fission

power of 2.074 MW and a generator output of 400.0 kW at 20.2% cycle efficiency. The resulting

fields drove 672 TRISO simulations, giving a mean SiC failure probability of 2.13 × 10−6 % and an

IPyC value of 49.9%, flagged for continued tracking.

 Griffin’s online multigroup cross-section generation capability (SSAPI) was demonstrated for the

first time on the 2-D GCMR full-core model across three control-drum configurations, benchmarked

against a Serpent reference and the project’s offline tabulated-library workflow. Supplying a

problem-specific GCMR neutron spectrum, rather than the default light-water-reactor spectrum,

substantially improved agreement, making online generation a viable alternative to offline tabulation

for this class of problem. The coupled balance-of-plant hierarchy was exercised on a plant startup

carrying the plant from rest to a converged full-power steady state over 55.6 h, and, from that state,

a primary motor torque reduction transient applying a 25% torque reduction over 5000 s, producing

a Doppler-feedback power dip to 93.75 % with partial recovery to 98.89 %.

7.2 User Experiences and Code Application Feedback

 For Griffin online cross section generation, setting up the online SSAPI workflow required

noticeably less upfront effort than the conventional offline approach. The online treatment still

requires some pre-processing to obtain a fine-group spectrum, which for the GCMR model was tallied

with Serpent. That tally, however, only requires modeling a single fuel assembly with significantly

less effort to build and run a full-core 3-D Serpent model needed to generate an offline library

spanning a grid of state parameters. Beyond that one-time spectrum tally, only small input changes

are needed to trigger cross-section generation in Griffin. The trade-off surfaced downstream rather

than upfront: memory use were substantially higher in the current Griffin implementation than for

the offline libraries (Table 5.3). Diagnosing discrepancies was less straightforward and a 3-D full

core Serpent model is typically still used to provide reference solutions.

 102

 The Griffin convergence issue with large applied displacement fields, identified in FY2025 during

the KRUSTY 60-cent case, carried into this year’s work: building the sweeper on the displaced mesh

fixed the standalone case, but the coupled run continued to stall with Griffin looping in the sweeper.

FY2026 sidestepped the issue by moving transport-mesh nodes directly with a mesh modifier and

rescaling densities, rather than solving on a displaced mesh at all. That workaround holds for

KRUSTY’s current displacements but remains an open request, since the nuclear system tests will

accumulate larger displacements than any warm critical test.

 This year’s models also pushed the MultiApp hierarchy deeper and wider: a 192-pipe Sockeye

ensemble and a plant loop now sit beneath the same parent application, and the GCMR startup

transient spans 55.6 h of plant time while the neutronics resolves far shorter time scales. Timestep

strategy and the cost of coexisting scales remain the practical constraint on these coupled models.

On a more positive note, both of Sockeye’s NCG treatments integrated into the full-core HPMR

model without requiring any change to the coupling scheme or fixed-point tolerances, a sign that

Sockeye’s physics options are maturing into robust building blocks for full-core work.

 When Griffin serves as the main application, the fixed-point iteration between Griffin and its

sub-applications is handled inside Griffin’s Richardson iteration scheme. Beyond the displaced-mesh

case noted above, that Richardson iteration can itself converge slowly for reasons internal to the

neutronics solver. Where it does, the fixed-point iteration slows with it, and the sub-applications are

executed more times than the coupling itself requires. Griffin’s fixed point solve outer option

relieves this by moving the fixed-point iteration outside the Richardson iteration. In practice it

reduces the number of sub-application executions and lowers the overall computational cost.

7.3 Recommended Follow-Up Work

 A first recommended follow-up item is extending the SSAPI’s double-heterogeneity treatment

to the burnable absorber. The current Griffin SSAPI does not resolve the TRISO-form burnable

absorber particles packed within the absorber pins; instead, the particle material is smeared uniformly

 103

over the pin volume during the online cross-section generation. Removing the double heterogeneity

in this way exposes the absorber to the full pin-averaged flux rather than the self-shielded flux

each particle actually sees, which significantly increases the effective absorption in these pins and

depresses the calculated criticality. The comparisons reported here remain internally consistent, since

the Serpent reference was constructed with the same homogenization, but the resulting eigenvalue

is not representative of the as-designed GCMR core. Extending the SSAPI to treat these absorber

particles explicitly is therefore a prerequisite for applying the online workflow to design-basis GCMR

calculations, and is recommended for future development and testing.

 A second recommended follow-up item is testing Griffin’s on-the-fly group condensation ca-

pability. The version of the SSAPI used here only supports generating a 70-group cross-section

set at acceptable accuracy, which is what ties the online treatment’s runtime to the cost of a

70-group evaluation ultimately used in transport. A recent development adds group condensation

on-the-fly during the self-shielding calculation, which appears capable of producing much coarser

cross-section sets (8-group) at the same accuracy level. This new capability would substantially

reduce both the runtime and the memory footprint reported in Table 5.3 while preserving the local

power-distribution advantage observed for online cross section generation. This new capability once

available in the Griffin main branch will be exercised and tested on the GCMR model. FY2027, the

online cross section capability will be further tested with its application subsequently extended to

the GCMR 3-D model and to coupled multiphysics simulations.

 The FY2027 work plan continues this effort along several complementary lines. On the

experimental validation side, the KRUSTY nuclear system test model will be extended beyond the

steady-state and null-transient cases already demonstrated, to load-following, restart, shutdown,

and other transient cases, with the load-following analysis carried through to publication so that

the benchmark can potentially be shared with industry. A parallel validation effort will build a

multiphysics model of the existing DEIMOS experiments and exercise it against both the cold and

heated experiment data.

 Several Sockeye items follow directly from this year’s KRUSTY work. The evaporator pool

 104

that now represents gravity in the LCVF model will be tuned once the transient KRUSTY model is

available to exercise it. Should the pool treatment prove insufficient, Sockeye’s two-phase model with

by the Sockeye developers and will be assessed once released. On the HPMR side, the preliminary

coupled transients tested this year (Section 4.4.2) will be extended to a full-scope transient simulation,

 On the capability side, the GCMR multiphysics model will be extended to include the secondary

In parallel, participant contributions to the OECD/NEA benchmark will be gathered and compared,

 This appendix documents the survey of power conversion options behind the technology choice

A.1 Candidate technologies

 The choice of power conversion system affects plant efficiency, balance-of-plant complexity,

heat-rejection requirements, operational flexibility, and deployment constraints for a heat-pipe-cooled

microreactor. Candidate technologies include steam Rankine cycles, open- and closed-Brayton cycles,

supercritical carbon dioxide cycles, Stirling engines, and direct or static conversion technologies.

Their suitability depends on reactor operating temperature, power rating, site conditions, technology

maturity, and the performance of the interface between the heat-pipe condensers and the conversion-

system working fluid [41], [42], [43]. A qualitative comparison of the principal candidates is given in

Table A.1.

 Steam Rankine cycles are commercially mature and benefit from extensive operating experience.

A subcritical steam system can typically reach approximately 33–35% conversion efficiency in

nuclear applications, while supercritical steam systems may reach approximately 38–42% under

suitable conditions. Rankine cycles have a relatively low compression-work requirement because

pressurization happens while the working fluid is still liquid. Steam systems do, however, require

boilers or steam generators, turbines, condensers, feedwater systems, water treatment, and asso-

ciated piping and controls, and their dependence on a substantial heat sink is a disadvantage for

microreactors sited in remote, arid, or infrastructure-limited locations. At the microreactor scale,

system complexity, maintenance requirements, freeze protection, and condenser size can outweigh

the efficiency advantage.

 111

 Closed Brayton cycles can use helium, nitrogen, carbon dioxide, or a gas mixture as the

working fluid. These systems can reach high efficiency and avoid atmospheric contamination of the

working fluid, and a closed cycle also permits selection of the operating pressure and working-fluid

composition to optimize turbomachinery size and performance. A closed Brayton system does,

however, need a gas cooler, pressure-retaining piping, working-fluid inventory control, and a sealed

high-integrity loop, and it has less commercial operating experience than open-air gas turbines at

the power levels relevant to terrestrial microreactors.

 Supercritical carbon dioxide Brayton cycles are attractive for their high power density, compact

turbomachinery, and projected efficiency. Cycle efficiencies approaching 48% at a turbine inlet

temperature near 650°C have been projected, with higher values possible for reheated configurations.

Printed-circuit heat exchangers suit supercritical carbon dioxide well because they can handle

high operating pressures while providing a compact heat-transfer surface. The technology has not,

however, reached the commercial maturity and supply-chain readiness of air or steam systems, and

high pressures, shaft sealing, bearings, transient control, materials compatibility, and small-scale

turbomachinery efficiency remain open development issues.

 Stirling engines are externally heated and can be coupled directly or indirectly to heat-pipe

condensers. Free-piston Stirling systems are modular and have space-power experience behind them,

which makes them attractive for kilowatt-scale reactors, but no mature industrial supply chain exists

yet for multi-megawatt terrestrial systems. Scaling to megawatt power would require either large

machines or many parallel conversion modules, which adds system complexity and maintenance.

 Static conversion technologies, including thermoelectric, thermionic, thermophotovoltaic, and

alkali-metal thermal-to-electric conversion systems, eliminate rotating equipment and can offer high

reliability. Their low demonstrated conversion efficiency, high-temperature requirements, and limited

availability at megawatt scale make them better suited to specialized or lower-power applications

than to near-term terrestrial microreactor deployment.

 Combined cycles pair an air-Brayton topping cycle with a steam or organic-Rankine bottoming

 112

cycle to recover additional exhaust energy. This arrangement can reach high efficiency in large

conventional power plants, but the extra equipment, water requirements, startup complexity, and

maintenance burden are difficult to justify for a transportable plant in the approximately 1–10 MW

electric range.

A.2 The recuperated open-air Brayton cycle

 Among the candidates, the recuperated open-air Brayton cycle is a strong candidate for near-

term heat-pipe microreactor deployment. The system draws in ambient air, compresses it, preheats

it in a recuperator, heats it further in a primary heat exchanger connected to the reactor heat

 113

pipes, and expands it through gas and power turbines. The turbine exhaust passes through the

recuperator before it is discharged to the atmosphere. Air does not flow through the reactor core,

so the reactor and power cycle stay separated by the primary heat exchanger, which avoids direct

activation of atmospheric air within the core.

 Recuperation uses heat remaining in the turbine exhaust to preheat the compressed air before

it enters the primary heat exchanger, which reduces the reactor heat needed to reach a given turbine

inlet temperature. This matters for heat-pipe microreactors because their turbine inlet temperatures,

typically 500–850°C depending on reactor design, are well below those of modern combustion

turbines. At these moderate temperatures a simple open-air Brayton cycle has limited efficiency:

optimized simple-cycle efficiencies of approximately 23.6%, 26.4%, and 31.0% were calculated for

turbine inlet temperatures of 500°C, 650°C, and 850°C, respectively. Recuperation improves on this

without adding a water-dependent bottoming cycle.

 The recuperated open-air configuration has several practical advantages for deployment. Because

exhaust heat is discharged directly to the atmosphere, the cycle needs no cooling-water source or

steam condenser, so it can be used at remote or arid sites. Its compressors, turbines, generators,

bearings, recuperators, and control systems draw on decades of aeroderivative and industrial gas-

turbine experience, and the balance of plant is simpler than a steam system because it avoids

steam generators, condensers, feedwater pumps, water treatment, and high-pressure steam piping.

Recuperated cycles also tend to optimize at lower compressor pressure ratios than simple Brayton

cycles, which eases pressure-boundary requirements and simplifies integration with the primary heat

exchanger.

 Gas-turbine machinery has a high power-to-volume ratio and can be packaged in modular or

skid-mounted units, and it starts up and responds to load changes quickly, which suits microgrids

and sites with variable renewable generation. The power conversion equipment can also be located

separately from the reactor module, so it can be maintained or replaced without disturbing the

reactor core. Finally, an open-air cycle uses ambient air as its working fluid and so needs no storage,

makeup, purification, or inventory control of the kind that helium or carbon dioxide systems require.

A.3 Turbomachinery selection

 Commercial aeroderivative engines are a useful starting point but not a direct fit. Conventional

gas turbines are designed for combustion temperatures well above the outlet temperatures of most

heat-pipe microreactors, so their compressor pressure ratios are often too high for nuclear-heated

operation. At a turbine inlet temperature of 500°C, some commercial aeroderivative configurations

were calculated to produce negative net power because turbine output could not drive the compressor.

The reference design instead uses purpose-matched, low-to-moderate-pressure-ratio turbomachinery

together with a high-effectiveness recuperator and an optimized primary heat exchanger. Based on

technology maturity, siting flexibility, system simplicity, and heat-exchanger analyses supported by

experiment, a single-heater recuperated open-air Brayton cycle is the recommended power conversion

system for a near-term heat-pipe microreactor and is the configuration modeled in this work.
