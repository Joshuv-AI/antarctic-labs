Gap-free telomere-to-telomere genome assembly of stone flounder (Kareius bicoloratus)
Authors: Weiwei Zheng, Kaiqiang Liu, Tao Yang, Dong Xu, Zhihong Liu, Wei Yuan, Yan Liu, Tengteng Wang, Huizong Han, Xueyan Fang, Wenteng Xu, Songlin Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Stone flounder (Kareius bicoloratus) is an emerging commercially important flatfish species in China, Japan, Korea, and Taiwan, because of its significant industrial value. However, no stone flounder genome sequence was reported to date, which greatly hinders the research on genetics and evolutionary genomics. In this study, we successfully assembled the first gap-free telomere-to-telomere (T2T) genome of stone flounder combining Illumina short-reads sequencing, PacBio HiFi sequencing, Hi-C sequencing, and ONT ultra-long sequencing. The genome spans 587.80 Mb with a contig N50 of 25.64 Mb. 98.58% of the genome sequences were assembled onto 24 chromosomes without gaps. Telomeres were identified at both ends of 8 chromosomes and at one end of 15 chromosomes. We identified 128.10 Mb repetitive sequences and 22,312 protein-coding genes, among which 98.03% of the coding genes were functionally annotated. BUSCO assessment, mapping ratio, and quality value (QV) evaluation collectively indicated the high quality and completeness of the genome assembly. This work advances the progress of evolutionary genomics, genetics and molecular genetic breeding research on stone flounder.

Background & Summary

Stone flounder (Kareius bicoloratus, FishBase ID: 8557), a member of the family Pleuronectidae of the order Pleuronectiformes, is naturally distributed in the East China Sea and the coastal waters of southern Japan1,2. In recent years, it has become a commercially important species in China, Japan, Korea, and Taiwan, because of its industrial values, such as low temperature tolerance3, adaptation to euryhaline conditions4, high market demand2, and excellent meat quality and flavor5.

At present, a large number of studies on stone flounder mainly focus on early life history6, biology7, feeding habits8–11, nutritional composition12,13, growth4,11, salinity tolerance14,15, population structure16, sperm cryopreservation2, hybridization3,17, and molecular marker18. However, to date, no high-quality reference genome of stone flounder has been reported, which has greatly hindered the comprehensive research in genetics, evolutionary genomics and functional genomics. Thus, there is an urgent need to assemble a high-quality genome of stone flounder.

The rapid development of sequencing technologies and assembly algorithms, especially long-read sequencing technologies like PacBio (Single Molecule Real Time) SMRT sequencing, have made it possible to obtain high-quality reference genome. It is worth mentioning that several high-quality reference genomes of flatfish have been reported, including Verasper variegatus19, Japanese flounder (Paralichthys olivaceus)20, turbot (Scophthalmus maximus)21, and starry flounder (Platichthys stellatus)22. Furthermore, recently, the application of Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT) ultra-long sequencing has successfully assisted in assembling multiple T2T reference genomes of fish, such as marbled flounder (Pseudopleuronectes yokohamae)23, Chinese sea bass (Lateolabrax maculatus)24, the giant grouper (Epinephelus lanceolatus)25, the tomato hind (Cephalopholis sonnerati)26, Asian icefish (Protosalanx chinensis)27, humphead wrasse (Cheilinus undulatus)28, Amur minnow (Phoxinus lagowskii)29, rock carp (Procypris rabaudi)30, African catfish (Clarias gariepinus)31, Yadong trout (Salmo trutta)32, and zig-zag eel (Mastacembelus armatus)33, blackspot tuskfish (Choerodon schoenleinii)34, blackhead seabream (Acanthopagrus schlegelii)35, glass catfish (Kryptopterus vitreolus)36 and X-ray tetra (Pristella maxillaris)37.

In this study, we reported a gap-free T2T genome of stone flounder combing Illumina short-read sequencing, PacBio high-fidelity (HiFi) sequencing, and high-throughput chromosome conformation capture (Hi-C) sequencing, and ONT ultralong read sequencing technologies (Fig. 1). To our knowledge, this is the first reported genome sequence of stone flounder so far. This high-quality genome assembly will not only advance the evolutionary genomics and genetic research of flatfish, but also provide a robust foundation for the development of molecular genetic breeding in stone flounder.Fig. 1The genome snail plot of K. bicoloratus.

Methods

Sample collection and genome sequencing

For genome sequencing, a two-year-old artificially farmed female stone flounder weighing 452.08 g with a total length of 28.36 cm was sampled from Laizhou Mingbo Aquatic Co., Ltd., Yantai, Shandong, China. Muscle tissues were collected for Genomic DNA (gDNA) extraction. Eight different tissues such as gill, gonad, heart, kidney, liver, muscle, skin, and spleen were sampled for RNA extraction. All samples collected in this study were approved by the the Animal Care and Use Committee of Yellow Sea Fisheries Research Institute, Chinese Academy of Fishery Sciences (CAFS). gDNA was extracted using the TIANamp Marine Animals DNA Kit (Tiangen, China) for Illumina short-read sequencing and Hi-C sequencing. For PacBio HiFi sequencing and ONT ultra-long sequencing, gDNA was extracted using the standard sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) extraction method. For RNA-seq used for coding gene annotation, RNA was extracted using the RNA Easy Fast Tissue/Cell Kit (Tiangen, China). All gDNA was quantified and qualified using agarose gel electrophoresis, NanoDrop One Spectrophotometers (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and Qubit 3.0 Fluorometer (Life Technologies, USA) to meet the requirements of library construction. RNA was quantified and qualified by agarose gel electrophoresis, NanoDrop One Spectrophotometers, Qubit 3.0 Fluorometer, and Agilent 2100 (Agilent Technologies, Germany).

For Illumina short-read sequencing, a 200–400 bp short-read library was constructed using Illumina DNA PCR-Free Prep kit (Illumina, USA) following the standard protocol, and the qualified library was sequenced on Illumina Novaseq 6000 platform with 150 bp pair-end (PE) mode. For PacBio HiFi sequencing, qualified genomic DNA was used to construct a SMRTbell library using the SMRTbell Prep Kit 3.0 (Pacbio Biosciences, CA, USA) according to the manufacturing protocols, and the qualified library was then performed on PacBio Sequel II platform for sequencing with the Circular Consensus Sequencing (CCS) mode. Finally, 18.38 Gb (31.27×) PacBio HiFi sequencing data were obtained, with an average read length of 17.34 kb and N50 read length of 17.29 kb (Table 1).

The Hi-C library construction process includes formaldehyde crosslinking, cell lysis, enzymatic digestion, end repair and biotin labeling, blunt-end ligation, crosslinking reversal, and DNA purification38. Then, the qualified Hi-C library was sequenced on Illumina Novaseq 6000 platform with 150 bp PE mode. As a result, 177.54 Gb (302.04×) Hi-C sequencing data was generated (Table 1).

For ONT ultra-long sequencing, the library was prepared using the Oxford Nanopore SQK-ULK001 ligation kit, and the purified library was loaded onto primed flow cell and sequenced on PromethION sequencer (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, Oxford, UK). Finally, 23.41 Gb (39.83×) of ONT ultra-long sequencing data were obtained (Table 1). The average read length and read N50 length are 36.56 kb and 52.72 kb, respectively.

For assist in coding genes annotation, RNA from eight different tissues, including spleen, gill, gonad, liver, muscle, skin, heart, and kidney, was extracted. Eight pair-ended sequencing library was constructed and sequenced on Illumina Novaseq 6000 platform. As a result, a total of 59.41 Gb RNA-seq data were generated (Table 1). In addition, RNA isolated from the above eight different tissues was equally mixed into a total RNA for Isoform-sequencing (Iso-seq). Qualified RNA was used for library construction with SMRTbell prep kit 3.0. Then, the library was sequenced on the PacBio Sequel IIe platform, resulting in 93.43 Gb of Iso-seq data (Table 1).

Genome assembly and telomere identification

The primary contigs were initially assembled using Hifiasm39 (v0.19.9) with the PacBio HiFi, Hi-C, and ONT ultralong sequencing data described above. Subsequently, the draft genome was anchor to 24 chromosomes using Yahs pipeline40 (v.1.2a.1). Then, ONT data was used to fill gaps with TGS-GapCloser41 (v1.2.1) to lengthen the contigs. Thereafter, Pilon42 (v1.23) was employed to polish the genome assembly with Illumina short-read data. Finally, a gap-free genome assembly of stone flounder with a length of 587.80 Mb was generated, and its contig N50 was 25.64 Mb (Fig. 1, Table 2). We further identified the telomeric repeat motifs (CCCTAA/TTAGGG) at both ends of each chromosome in the stone flounder genome sequence using quarTeT43 (v1.2.5). Repeat annotation

A combined strategy involving homologous prediction and de novo prediction were employed to identify the whole genome repeats. The homology prediction was conducted using RepeatMasker software44 (v4.1.2) and its in-house scripts (RepeatProteinMask) (v4.1.2) with default parameters based on Repbase database45 (http://www.girinst.org/repbase) (v21.01) to extracted repeat regions. de novo repetitive elements database was built by RepeatModeler46 (v2.0.3) with default parameters. Subsequently, integrating the Repbase database with the de novo repetitive elements database library to generate a non-redundant library through uclust processing. Then, the custom library was supplied to RepeatMasker for DNA-level repeat identification. Ultimately, a total of 128.10 Mb of non-redundant repetitive sequences were predicted, constituting approximately 21.79% of the stone flounder genome (Table 4). Among these repeats, short interspersed nuclear elements (SINEs), long interspersed elements (LINEs), long terminal repeats (LTRs), and DNA transposons accounted for 0.26%, 3.05%, 2.14%, and 7.82% of the genome, respectively (Table 4).Table 4Classification statistics of repeated elements in K. bicoloratus.TypeLength(bp)Percentage(%)SINE1,507,1150.26LINE17,647,7013.05LTR12,392,4852.14DNA45,326,3987.82Unknown51,228,8238.52Total128,102,52221.79

Protein-coding gene prediction and functional annotation

De novo prediction, homology-based prediction, transcriptomic-assisted prediction, and evidence-based prediction approaches were employed to protein-coding gene annotation. For de novo prediction, Augustus47 (v3.5) and SNAP48 (v2013.11.29) with default parameters were used for the gene structure prediction. For homology-based prediction, homologous protein sequences of Verasper variegatus, Pleuronectes platessa, Hippoglossus hippoglossus, H. stenolepis, Scophthalmus maximus, Paralichthys olivaceus, Cynoglossus semilaevis, and Danio rerio were downloaded from NCBI or Ensembl. Protein sequences were aligned to the stone flounder genome using TblastN (v2.2.26; E-value ≤ 1e–5), and then the matching proteins were aligned to the homologous genome sequences for accurate spliced alignments with GeneWise49 (v2.4.1) to predict gene structure contained in each protein region. For transcriptomic-assisted prediction, RNA-seq reads from Illumina and Pacbio Sequel IIe sequencing were aligned to genome using Hisat50 (v2.2.1) with default parameters to identify exons region and splice positions. The alignment results were then used as input for genome-based transcript assembly by Stringtie51 (v2.2.1) with default parameters and validated by PASA (v2.5.2) (https://github.com/PASApipeline/PASApipeline). Additionally, to ensure high-quality structural annotation of the genome, we also employed an evidence-based approach using EGAPx52 (prediction of gene structures for eukaryotic genomes, v0.5.0; https://github.com/ncbi/egapx). RNA-seq data generated in this study were utilized as transcriptomic evidence to support gene modeling. The EGAPx pipeline integrated short reads alignments and assembled transcripts to define gene structures, including exon-intron boundaries and coding sequences (CDS). The EvidenceModeler (EVM)53 pipeline (v1.1.1) was employed to integrate all the protein-coding genes predicted by the above four methods and the result was corrected using PASA. Finally, a non-redundant reference gene set including 22,312 coding genes was obtained with an average gene length of 12,246.75 bp (Table 5). Data Records

The Illumina sequencing data, PacBio HiFi sequencing data, Hi-C sequencing data, and ONT ultra-long sequencing data have been uploaded to NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) database with accession number SRP63603464. The RNA-seq and Iso-seq data used for coding genes annotation have also been uploaded to NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) database with accession number SRP63859165. The genome assembly has been submitted to NCBI under accession number GCA_054131325.166. The genome annotation file, gene CDS, and protein data have been deposited at Figshare67.

Technical Validation

Completeness and quality assessment of genome assembly

The completeness of the stone flounder genome assembly was evaluated using BUSCO (v5.3.2)68 with the actinopterygii_odb10 database including 3,640 BUSCOs. Of these, 3,512 (96.5%) complete BUSCOs including 3,482 (95.7%) single-copy BUSCOs and 30 (0.8%) duplicated BUSCOs were identified. Only 18 (0.5%) fragmented BUSCOs and 110 (3.0%) missing BUSCOs were detected. The genome quality value (QV) was accessed by Merqury69, and the QV score was 48.41, highlighting a high-quality assembly. Furthermore, the accuracy rate was evaluated by mapping the sequencing data to the genome assembly using bwa70 (v0.7.17) and minimap271 (v2.17). The mapping rates were 95.63% and 99.24% for Illumina sequencing data and PacBio HiFi sequencing data, respectively.

Evaluation of the gene annotation

The accuracy of gene annotation was evaluated using BUSCO (v5.3.2) on the basis of actinopterygii_odb10 database containing 3,640 BUSCOs. The results showed that 3,432 (94.3%) complete BUSCOs, containing 3,403 (93.5%) single-copy and 29 (0.8%) duplicated BUSCOs, were detected, 22 (0.6%) fragmented BUSCOs and 186 (5.1%) missing BUSCOs were identified.
