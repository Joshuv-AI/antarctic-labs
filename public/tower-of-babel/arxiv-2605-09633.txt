Minimizing Worst-Case Weighted Latency for Multi-Robot Persistent Monitoring: Theory and RL-Based Solutions
Weizhen Wang \affilnum 1,2, Ziheng Wang \affilnum 1, Jianping He \affilnum 1,2, Xinping Guan \affilnum 1,2, and Xiaoming Duan \affilnum 1,2

Abstract
We study multi-robot persistent monitoring on weighted graphs, where node weights encode monitoring priorities and edge weights encode travel distances. The goal is to design joint robot trajectories that minimize the worst-case weighted latency across all nodes over an infinite time horizon. The widely adopted worst-case latency objective evaluates team performance over the entire time horizon and therefore may fail to distinguish strategies with poor transient behavior but strong asymptotic performance. To address this limitation, we propose a family of tail-performance objectives that generalize the standard objective and study the resulting functional optimization problems. We establish several key theoretical properties, including the existence of optimal strategies, relationships among the proposed objectives and their corresponding optimization problems, approximation by periodic solutions to arbitrary accuracy, and reductions to event-driven decision models with discretized waiting times. Building on these results, we construct an equivalent event-driven Markov decision process (MDP), called the Tail Worst-case Latency-Optimizing Markov Decision Process (TWLO-MDP), which reformulates the tail-performance objective as a standard average-reward criterion. We then develop reinforcement-learning-based solution methods for the TWLO-MDP and introduce the multi-robot monitoring benchmark (M2Bench), a unified platform that supports the evaluation and comparison of heuristic and learning-based monitoring algorithms. Experiments on synthetic and realistic monitoring scenarios show that our methods effectively reduce the worst-case weighted latency and outperform representative baselines.

Introduction

Persistent monitoring is a key application area for many real-world robotic systems. Typical examples include urban crime surveillance
(
73
)
, post-disaster area monitoring
(
59
)
, anomaly detection
(
110
)
, ocean monitoring
(
91
)
and forest fire detection
(
68
)
. In these scenarios, a team of robots repeatedly visits a set of important locations so that abnormal events can be detected in a timely manner. A widely used performance measure is the latency, which measures the amount of time a location remains unvisited. At its core, this task concerns the design of long-term motion strategies for multiple robots moving on a graph.
Robots traverse edges representing physical paths, subject to limited maximum speeds. Since the robots’ decisions affect one another, the resulting joint strategy must coordinate their movements over an infinite time horizon. The objective is to minimize the worst-case latency throughout the mission.
This problem has attracted significant attention over the past two decades
(
62
;
15
;
83
;
49
;
11
)
. Earlier studies formalized the worst-latency objective and showed that computing an optimal monitoring strategy is both NP-hard and APX-hard. A variety of algorithmic approaches have since been proposed, including cycle-based patrol strategies, graph-partition methods, and so on. More recently, learning-based approaches have also been explored to address complex environments. Although these efforts have substantially advanced our understanding of multi-robot persistent monitoring, several fundamental questions remain open.

In this work, we investigate the persistent monitoring problem and introduce a family of tail-performance objectives that capture long-run system behavior beyond the initial transient phase, while also generalizing the standard worst-latency objective.
We then carry out a theoretical analysis of the problem and establish several key results, including the existence and structural properties of optimal solutions, the validity of periodic approximations, and the soundness of discretizing waiting times at locations.
A central consequence of this analysis is that the original infinite-horizon functional optimization problem can be reformulated as an equivalent event-driven Markov decision process, termed TWLO-MDP.
Building on this MDP formulation, we apply reinforcement learning algorithms to compute monitoring strategies. We further develop M2Bench, a modular platform that integrates heuristic and RL-based monitoring methods for unified training, evaluation, and comparison.

However, despite their empirical success, most existing approaches rely on heuristic reward designs that are only loosely connected to the original monitoring objectives. In particular, for minimizing worst-case latency, commonly used reward functions, such as instantaneous latency or its normalized variants, do not faithfully represent the true objective and may therefore lead to suboptimal policies with respect to the actual performance metric.
This limitation largely stems from a fundamental mismatch between the problem objective and the standard MDP formulations. Specifically, classical RL objectives, including discounted cumulative reward and average reward, are based on additive criteria over time, whereas worst-case latency is inherently a non-additive and highly nonlinear functional of the trajectory.
To address this gap, we aim to construct an MDP formulation that is fully equivalent to the original persistent monitoring problem under the worst-case latency criterion. In this way, solving the resulting MDP yields an optimal policy for the original problem directly, rather than relying on surrogate reward approximations.
Moreover, to the best of our knowledge, there is currently no unified platform that integrates both RL-based and heuristic monitoring methods within a common framework for training, evaluation, and fair performance comparison.

Contributions:

Our core contributions are as follows:

1.

We introduce a family of tail-performance objectives for multi-robot persistent monitoring on weighted graphs.
This formulation generalizes the conventional worst-case weighted latency criterion by excluding an initial transient phase, thereby capturing the long-run monitoring performance of robot teams under fixed initial deployments.

2.

We establish theoretical properties of the proposed objective family and the associated strategy classes.
In particular, we prove the existence of optimal strategies, invariance of the optimal value with respect to the transient horizon, monotonicity of optimal strategy sets, reachability of optimal long-run performance from arbitrary initial configurations, approximation by periodic solutions, and bounded performance loss under discretized waiting times.

3.

We reformulate the original continuous-time trajectory optimization problem as an equivalent event-driven Markov decision process, termed the TWLO-MDP.
By augmenting the state with a historical worst-latency tracker and an elapsed-time counter, the TWLO-MDP transforms the non-additive tail objective into a standard average-reward criterion while preserving optimality.

4.

We develop reinforcement-learning-based solution methods for the TWLO-MDP.
The proposed learning pipeline incorporates several problem-specific training techniques, including time-normalized training, transparent advantage estimation for asynchronous event transitions, counterfactual credit assignment, parameter sharing with role information, and imitation-based initialization.

5.

We develop M2Bench, a modular platform that integrates heuristic and reinforcement-learning-based monitoring algorithms within a unified framework.
Experiments on synthetic and realistic monitoring scenarios, including a San Francisco crime-map benchmark, demonstrate that the proposed TWLO-MDP-based learning methods effectively reduce worst-case weighted latency and outperform representative baselines.

Problem formulation

2.1
Monitoring environment and performance metrics

The monitoring environment is modeled by a finite weighted graph
{\mathcal{G}}=({\mathcal{V}},{\mathcal{E}},{A},{\phi})
,
where
{\mathcal{V}}
is the set of nodes representing critical locations,
{\mathcal{E}}
is the set of edges connecting these locations,
{A}:{\mathcal{E}}\to{\mathbb{R}_{>0}}
assigns each edge a positive length corresponding to the physical distance between its endpoints, and
{\phi}:{\mathcal{V}}\to{\mathbb{R}_{>0}}
assigns each node a positive weight reflecting its monitoring priority. Let
{\phi_{\max}}:=\max_{v\in{\mathcal{V}}}{\phi}(v)
and
{\phi_{\min}}:=\min_{v\in{\mathcal{V}}}{\phi}(v)
denote the maximum and minimum priority, respectively. The finite nature of
{\mathcal{V}}
and
{\mathcal{E}}
is consistent with real monitoring scenarios, where only a limited number of significant sites and physical passages are present in the environment. We identify the graph with its geometric realization
{|{\mathcal{G}}|}
, in which each edge
e\in{\mathcal{E}}
is represented as a closed interval of length
{A}({e})
attached to its incident nodes. Equipped with the shortest-path distance
{d_{{|{\mathcal{G}}|}}}(\cdot,\cdot)
, the pair
({|{\mathcal{G}}|},{d_{{|{\mathcal{G}}|}}})
forms a path-metric space, so robots can be located not only at nodes but also at any point along edges. We let
{D_{{\mathcal{G}}}}
denote the diameter of
{\mathcal{G}}
.

A team of
{k}
robots, indexed by
{\mathcal{R}}=\{r_{1},\dots,r_{k}\}
, is deployed to carry out persistent monitoring on the environment
{\mathcal{G}}
. Each robot moves on the metric graph
{|{\mathcal{G}}|}
with maximum speed one, and it may stay at any point for an arbitrary duration. The behavior of robot
r\in{\mathcal{R}}
is described by a schedule
\pi_{r}:{\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}\to{|{\mathcal{G}}|}
, which specifies its position on the graph over time. Formally, the joint strategy (team behavior) of the team is represented by a trajectory

\boldsymbol{\pi}(t)=(\pi_{r_{1}}(t),\dots,\pi_{r_{k}}(t))\in{|{\mathcal{G}}|}^{k},\quad t\geq 0.

Since
{\mathcal{V}}
and
{\mathcal{E}}
are finite sets and all edges have finite length,
{|{\mathcal{G}}|}
is compact
(
57
, Theorem 3.8)
. Besides, due to the fact that finite Cartesian products of compact sets are compact,
{|{\mathcal{G}}|^{k}}
is also compact.
Let
\boldsymbol{p}=(p_{1},\dots,p_{k})\in{|{\mathcal{G}}|^{k}}
and
\boldsymbol{p}^{\prime}=(p^{\prime}_{1},\dots,p^{\prime}_{k})\in{|{\mathcal{G}}|}^{k}
, then we can endow
{|{\mathcal{G}}|^{k}}
with the sup metric:

\displaystyle{d_{|{\mathcal{G}}|^{{k}}}}(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{p}^{\prime}):=\max_{i\in\{1,\dots,{k}\}}{d_{{|{\mathcal{G}}|}}}(p_{i},p^{\prime}_{i}).

Consequently,
({|{\mathcal{G}}|}^{k},{d_{|{\mathcal{G}}|^{{k}}}})
is a compact metric space.
We define the set of feasible strategies by

\Pi:=\\
\Big\{\boldsymbol{\pi}\in C({\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}},{|{\mathcal{G}}|}^{k}):{d_{|{\mathcal{G}}|^{{k}}}}(\boldsymbol{\pi}(t),\boldsymbol{\pi}(t^{\prime}))\leq|t-t^{\prime}|,\forall t,t^{\prime}\Big\}.

(1)

In (
1
), the Lipschitz condition reflects the unit-speed constraint, and is equivalent to
{d_{{|{\mathcal{G}}|}}}(\pi_{r}(t),\pi_{r}(t^{\prime}))\leq|t-t^{\prime}|
for all
r\in{\mathcal{R}}
. A joint strategy
\boldsymbol{\pi}
is called periodic if there exist
t^{*}\geq 0
and
0<W<\infty
such that
\pi_{r}(t^{*}+t+W)=\pi_{r}(t^{*}+t)
for all
t\in{\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}
and all
r\in{\mathcal{R}}
.

A node
v\in{\mathcal{V}}
is said to be visited at time
t
if
\pi_{r}(t)=v
for some
r\in{\mathcal{R}}
.
To quantify how efficiently each node is monitored,
we will next introduce the notion of
latency
as the key metric:
For each node
v\in{\mathcal{V}}
and
r\in{\mathcal{R}}
, the most recent visit time to
v
by robot
r
up to time
t
is defined as

h_{r}^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(v,t):=\begin{cases}\sup\{t^{\prime}\leq t:\pi_{r}(t^{\prime})=v\},&\text{if such $t^{\prime}$ exists},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(2)

The overall last visit time to node
v
by the whole robot team
{\mathcal{R}}
is then

{h}^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(v,t):=\max_{r\in{\mathcal{R}}}h_{r}^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(v,t).

(3)

The instantaneous latency at time
t
is then given by

{L_{v}^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}}(t):=t-{h}^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(v,t),

(4)

which measures the duration a node remains unvisited. Without loss of generality, we assume the initial latency
{L_{v}^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}}(0)=0
for all nodes
v
. To evaluate the instantaneous monitoring performance of the team with a joint strategy
\boldsymbol{\pi}
at time
t
, we account for the relative importance of different nodes and adopt a worst-case perspective over the whole graph. Specifically, we introduce the instantaneous worst weighted latency, defined by
{L}^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(t):=\max_{v\in{\mathcal{V}}}\,{\phi}(v)\,{L_{v}^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}}(t)
.

2.2
Tail objective formulation

We have introduced the monitoring environment and the instantaneous latency metric above. Furthermore, to evaluate the overall quality of
\boldsymbol{\pi}
, one should consider its performance over time. A natural approach, adopted in existing studies (e.g.,
(
5
;
3
;
2
)
), is to take the supremum of
{L}^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(t)
over all
t\in{\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}
, i.e.,
\sup_{t\geq 0}{L}^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(t)
, which corresponds to the worst weighted latency attained over all time. However, this definition is sensitive to transient effects at the beginning of the surveillance mission: for instance, if robots start from unfavorable initial positions, some nodes may experience large latency values in the early stage, even though the system eventually settles into a regular monitoring pattern and should be regarded as “good”. We here provide an example to illustrate this point.

Example 1

(Good strategies with bad transient behaviors)
.

Consider the graph in
Fig.

1
: nodes
\{1,2,3\}
form a triangle with unit edge lengths, node
4
is connected to node
1
by an edge of length
5
, and all node weights are one.

\boldsymbol{\pi}_{1}
.

. The robot at node

Strategy
\boldsymbol{\pi}_{2}
.

(typical single-base deployment). One robot moves at full speed to node

increases up to

for

thereafter. Consequently, we have

\sup_{t\geq 0}{L}^{\boldsymbol{\pi}_{2}}(t)=5,\quad\text{while}\quad\sup_{t\geq 5}{L}^{\boldsymbol{\pi}_{2}}(t)=\tfrac{3}{2}.

\boldsymbol{\pi}_{3}
.
Again all robots start at node

. One moves to node

and then shuttles between nodes

and

. Then, we have

From a practical standpoint, evaluating
\boldsymbol{\pi}_{2}
and
\boldsymbol{\pi}_{3}
using
\sup_{t\geq 0}{L}^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(t)
is inappropriate, as both yield the same value under this metric, yet their long-term behaviors differ substantially.
For
\boldsymbol{\pi}_{2}
, after an initial transient phase, it converges to the same steady-state monitoring pattern as the optimal
\boldsymbol{\pi}_{1}
, achieving the same long-run latency performance.
Therefore, a better assessment approach is to exclude the transient period and consider
\sup_{t\geq 5}{L}^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(t)
instead.
Under this new criterion, both
\boldsymbol{\pi}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{\pi}_{2}
can be regarded as optimal, exhibiting superior steady-state efficiency compared with

.

Example

1
illustrates that evaluating strategies solely based on the global supremum
\sup_{t\geq 0}{L}^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(t)
may lead to misleading conclusions, as transient effects may dominate the metric while contributing little to the long-term monitoring quality.
Consequently, we define
\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}:=\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\cup\{+\infty\}
and introduce a family of tail-performance metrics.

Definition 1

(Tail monitoring performance)
.

For any
T\in\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
, the worst weighted latency of strategy
\boldsymbol{\pi}
after time
T
is defined by

J_{T}(\boldsymbol{\pi})=\sup_{t\geq T}{L}^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(t).

(5)

Note that the family
\{J_{T}(\boldsymbol{\pi})\}_{T\in\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}}
strictly generalizes the performance metrics commonly adopted in the literature. Besides, when we take
T=+\infty
, the objective function
J_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\pi})
is equivalent to the limit superior of
{L}^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(t)
, i.e.,

J_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\pi})=\lim_{T\to+\infty}\sup_{t\geq T}{L}^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(t)=\limsup_{t\to\infty}{L}^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(t)

(
1
)
.
Since the limit superior is always well-defined and unique in the extended real line
\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}

(
86
)
,
J_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\pi})
is also well-defined.

With these definitions in place, the multi-robot monitoring problem can now be formally stated:

Problem 1

(Multi-robot monitoring problem)
.

Given a finite graph
{\mathcal{G}}=({\mathcal{V}},{\mathcal{E}},{A},{\phi})
, a robot team
{\mathcal{R}}=\{r_{1},\dots,r_{{k}}\}
and fixed
T\in\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
, the multi-robot monitoring problem is to find an optimal joint strategy
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}\in\Pi
that minimizes the worst weighted latency defined in (
5
):

\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}\in\underset{{\boldsymbol{\pi}\in\Pi}}{\arg\min}J_{T}(\boldsymbol{\pi})
.

2.3
Rationale of the tail performance family

The family of objective functions
\{J_{T}(\boldsymbol{\pi})\}_{T\in\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}}
introduced in (
5
)
serves as the foundation for our later analysis.
Before presenting the formal theoretical results, we first summarize several key properties that justify the use of this family as a reasonable and physically meaningful performance formulation.
The summary below clarifies the motivation and structure of the subsequent development, while the detailed proofs will be given in
Section

3
.

1) Existence of optimal solutions.

For every
T\in\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
, there exists at least one optimal strategy
\boldsymbol{\pi}_{T}^{*}\in\Pi
that attains the minimum of
J_{T}(\cdot)
.
This guarantees that each problem instance defined by
J_{T}(\cdot)
is well-posed.

2) Consistency of optimal values.

Let
J^{*}_{T}:=\inf_{\boldsymbol{\pi}\in\Pi}J_{T}(\boldsymbol{\pi})
.
It will be shown that
J^{*}_{T}=J^{*}
for all
T\in\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
, meaning that all members of this objective family lead to the same optimal monitoring efficiency.
Physically, this implies that although
T
changes the evaluation window, the achievable long-run performance remains identical.

3) Monotonicity of optimal sets.

Denote by
\Pi_{T}^{*}
the set of optimal strategies for

4) Reachability under fixed initial conditions.

3.3
Event-driven decision and discretized waiting time

In the following study, we focus on a specific strategy set: A joint strategy
\boldsymbol{\pi}
is called rational if the robots following it always move along edges at maximum speed and may only wait at nodes. Let the set of rational strategies be
\bar{\Pi}\subset\Pi
. It is straightforward to verify that we do not lose optimality when restricting to this subset of strategies: any feasible strategy can be transformed into this canonical form without degrading the monitoring performance by accelerating all edge traversals to unit speed and reallocating any residual waiting time to the subsequent node.

For rational strategies, robot motion evolves deterministically between two consecutive
events
, which occur when a robot either arrives at a node or completes a waiting period.
We thus discretize the continuous time horizon into a sequence of event times
\{t_{j}\}_{j\geq 0}
, with each
t_{j}
marking one such transition.
It is sufficient to record robot positions and the global latency state at these discrete instants, because between two events the system evolution is fully determined since robots either move at unit speed or remain stationary, and node latencies increase linearly and reset only at events.
Therefore, the maximum weighted latency over an interval
[t_{j},t_{j+1})
is always attained at its right limit
t_{j+1}^{-}
, leading to

J_{T}(\boldsymbol{\pi})=\sup_{t\geq T}{L}^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(t)=\sup_{t_{j}\geq T}{L}^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(t_{j}^{-}).

Hence, focusing solely on these discrete event times is
sufficient
to capture both the decision process and the performance criterion of the original continuous-time problem.

At each event time
t_{j}
, every robot that becomes available i) either selects an adjacent node from the neighbors of its current location to move to; ii) or chooses a waiting time from
(0,\tau_{\max}]
before the next event (a prescribed upper bound
\tau_{\max}
can always be imposed without loss of generality since multiple consecutive waiting actions are allowed).
Although the waiting time can, in principle, take any continuous value, we discretize it into a finite set
\{\Delta,2\Delta,\dots,\kappa_{\max}\Delta\}
where
\Delta>0
is the temporal resolution and
\kappa_{\max}\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
is a prescribed upper bound, in order to obtain a finite action space, which simplifies the policy representation and facilitates the training and optimization of reinforcement-learning algorithms in the subsequent study.

To further align the formulation with practical deployment scenarios, we additionally fix a given initial configuration
\boldsymbol{p}_{0}
for the robot team, where all robots are initially placed on nodes and the latency is zero everywhere. This setup reflects common applications in which robots begin operation from predetermined deployment sites (e.g., charging docks or task-specific starting posts).
Let
\bar{\Pi}^{\Delta}_{\boldsymbol{p}_{0}}
denote the set of rational strategies with the discretized waiting time and the fixed initial configuration
\boldsymbol{p}_{0}
.

4
MDP reformulation and reinforcement learning

4.1
MDP reformulation

Based on the preceding analysis, we then reformulate the monitoring problem,
1
, as a standard Markov decision process
\mathcal{M}=(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},{P},R,T)
whose optimal solution is the same as that of
1
. This formulation enables the use of standard reinforcement learning algorithms:

State space
\mathcal{S}
: The MDP is
event-driven
, where each decision step corresponds to the occurrence of an event (when one or more robots complete a traveling or waiting segment). Each state
s_{n}=(\boldsymbol{p}_{n},\boldsymbol{L}_{n},z_{n},\eta_{n})\in\mathcal{S}
is defined at the event time
t_{n}
, where
\boldsymbol{p}_{n}=(p_{n,1},\dots,p_{n,{k}})\in{|{\mathcal{G}}|^{k}}
denotes the joint positions of all robots, with
p_{n,r}=(v^{\mathrm{from}}_{n,r},v^{\mathrm{to}}_{n,r},\delta_{n,r})
containing the starting node
v^{\mathrm{from}}_{n,r}
, target node
v^{\mathrm{to}}_{n,r}
and remaining action duration
\delta_{n,r}
of robot
r
;
\boldsymbol{L}_{n}=(L_{n,1},\dots,L_{n,{|{\mathcal{V}}|}})
denotes the latency of all nodes;
z_{n}=\sup_{T\leq t\leq t_{n}}L(t)
tracks the worst weighted latency observed from
T
to
t_{n}
; and
\eta_{n}
is the cumulative elapsed time since the start of monitoring, used to determine whether the evaluation phase (
t\geq T)
has begun.

Action space
\mathcal{A}
: The joint action
\boldsymbol{a}_{n}=(a_{n,r})_{r\in{\mathcal{R}}}\in\mathcal{A}_{1}\times\cdots\times\mathcal{A}_{k}
specifies decisions of all robots that have just completed an action and are located at
v^{\mathrm{cur}}_{n,r}
, i.e., its state
p_{n,r}
satisfies that
\delta_{n,r}=0
and we take
v^{\textrm{cur}}_{n}=v^{\textrm{to}}_{n,r}
. Each component
a_{n,r}=(v^{\mathrm{tgr}}_{n,r},\tau_{n,r})
determines the next node and the action duration,
where
v^{\mathrm{tgr}}_{n,r}\in\{v^{\mathrm{cur}}_{n,r}\}\cup\mathcal{N}(v^{\mathrm{cur}}_{n,r})
. If the target is a neighboring node
v^{\mathrm{tgr}}_{n,r}\in\mathcal{N}(v^{\mathrm{cur}}_{n,r})
, the duration is fixed and equals the edge length
A(v^{\mathrm{cur}}_{n,r},v^{\mathrm{tgr}}_{n,r})
; if
v^{\mathrm{tgr}}_{n,r}=v^{\mathrm{cur}}_{n,r}
, the robot decides to wait, and the waiting duration
\tau_{n,r}
is chosen from the discrete set
(0,\tau_{\max}]
. On the other hand, robots still traveling continue their current motion and take a
no-op
action.

Transition kernel
P
: The transition is event-driven and deterministic. Given the current state
s_{n}
and action
\boldsymbol{a}_{n}
, first, for every robot with zero remaining duration
\delta_{n,r}=0
, apply the assigned action
a_{n,r}=(v^{\mathrm{tgr}}_{n,r},\tau_{n,r})
and set
p_{n,r}=(v^{\mathrm{cur}}_{n,r},\,v^{\mathrm{tgr}}_{n,r},\,\tau_{n,r})
; robots with
\delta_{n,r}>0
keep their current segment (no-op). Then we proceed to the next event. Let
\Delta t_{\mathrm{evt}}=\min_{r\in{\mathcal{R}}}\delta_{n,r}
be the smallest remaining duration among all robots and consider an additional event triggered when the cumulative elapsed time reaches the evaluation horizon
T
(the same
T
used in the original monitoring objective
J_{T}(\boldsymbol{\pi})
, representing the time after which transient effects are ignored):

\Delta t=\begin{cases}\min\left(\Delta t_{\mathrm{evt}},T-\eta_{n}\right),&\text{if }\eta_{n}<T\\
\Delta t_{\mathrm{evt}},&\text{if }\eta_{n}=T.\end{cases}

Then update remaining durations
\delta_{n+1,r}=\delta_{n,r}-\Delta t
for all robots
r\in{\mathcal{R}}
. The components
v^{\textrm{from}}_{n,r}
and
v^{\textrm{to}}_{n,r}
are inherited from the previous state:
v^{\textrm{from}}_{n+1,r}=v^{\textrm{from}}_{n,r}
and
v^{\textrm{to}}_{n+1,r}=v^{\textrm{to}}_{n,r}
. For each node
v

L_{n+1,v}=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if }v\text{ is visited during }(t_{n},t_{n+1}],\\
L_{n,v}+\Delta t,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Besides, update the time counter and the performance tracker as follows. Before the evaluation phase begins (
\eta_{n+1}<T
), the tracker
z
remains inactive, while once the horizon
T
is reached, it records the worst weighted latency observed so far:

\displaystyle\eta_{n+1}=\min\{\eta_{n}+\Delta t,T\},

\displaystyle z_{n+1}=\begin{cases}\max\{z_{n},\underset{{v\in V}}{\max}{\phi}(v)[L_{n,v}+\Delta t]\},&\text{if }\eta_{n+1}=T,\\
0,&\text{if }\eta_{n+1}<T.\end{cases}

The next state is
s_{n+1}=(\boldsymbol{p}_{n+1},\boldsymbol{L}_{n+1},z_{n+1},\eta_{n+1})
and the transition is deterministic

Reward function
R

R_{n}=R(s_{n},\boldsymbol{a}_{n})=z_{n}.

Objective function

J^{\mathrm{ave}}_{s_{0}}(\mu)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[R_{n}\,|\,s_{0}],

Time-Normalized Objective.

A practical issue arises when training the event-driven MDP with rollouts truncated by the number of decision steps. Since different event steps may correspond to vastly different amounts of physical time, a fixed-step rollout does not in general correspond to a fixed elapsed monitoring time. In particular, a policy may repeatedly choose short-duration actions, such as minimal waiting, so as to slow down the progression of physical time within each rollout. As a result, the growth of latency at unvisited nodes can be artificially suppressed over the truncated horizon, even though such behavior is undesirable from the viewpoint of the original monitoring problem. This reveals a mismatch between fixed-step truncation and the physical-time semantics of persistent monitoring.
A natural remedy is to truncate each rollout according to elapsed physical time rather than the number of event steps. However, modifying only the stopping rule is still insufficient. If one continues to use the original step-wise reward
z_{n}
, then the resulting objective may still be distorted, since a policy can influence the number of events occurring within the same physical-time horizon. Therefore, to make the training objective fully consistent with the physical-time semantics of the monitoring problem, one must jointly modify both the rollout truncation rule and the contribution of each event to the objective.
This motivates the following time-normalized reformulation, which is equivalent to (
8
).

Lemma 2

(Equivalent time-normalized objective)
.

For any stationary policy
\mu
and initial state
s_{0}
, let
\Delta t_{n}
denote the physical duration of the
n
-th event interval, and let
z_{n}
be the worst weighted latency tracker defined in
Section

4.1
. Then the step-average objective is equivalent to the following time-normalized objective:

J^{\mathrm{ave}}_{s_{0}}(\mu)=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[\left.\frac{\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}z_{n}\Delta t_{n}}{\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\Delta t_{n}}\right\rvert\,s_{0}\right].

(9)

Proof.

The proof is postponed to Appendix
E2
.
∎

Based on
Lemma

2
, we make a slight modification to the MDP used in practical RL training. Specifically, instead of using the step-wise reward
z_{n}
, we assign to each event step a duration-aware reward
z_{n}\Delta t_{n}
. Accordingly, rather than truncating each rollout after a fixed number of steps, we terminate trajectories using a fixed physical-time horizon. In this way, the contribution of each event to the training objective is weighted by the amount of elapsed monitoring time, thereby aligning the learning process with the time-normalized objective in (
9
).

To illustrate the practical difference between (
8
) and (
9
), consider a two-node graph connected by an edge of length
A
, with
T=0
and minimum waiting duration
\Delta
. We compare two strategies: (i) repeatedly waiting for duration
\Delta
, and (ii) moving back and forth between the two nodes. The latter achieves a worst-case latency of
2A
. Under a fixed-step rollout of length
N
, the waiting strategy yields the truncated step-average objective
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}n\Delta=\Delta\frac{N+1}{2}
. Therefore, the waiting policy ceases to appear preferable only when
\Delta\frac{N+1}{2}>2A
, i.e., when
N>\frac{4A}{\Delta}-1
. For example, when
A=20
and
\Delta=0.1
, one needs roughly
400
steps before fixed-step truncation can eliminate the spurious preference for repeated waiting. By contrast, under physical-time truncation with horizon
H
, the same waiting strategy yields the time-normalized objective
\frac{\Delta(\Delta+2\Delta+...+H)}{H}=\frac{H+\Delta}{2}
. Hence, under physical-time truncation, the pathological waiting strategy ceases to appear preferable once
H>4A-\Delta
. This threshold is expressed directly in physical time and therefore admits a clear interpretation in terms of the underlying monitoring task.
In principle, fixed-step truncation can also suppress the pathological waiting strategy, but only if the rollout length is chosen sufficiently large to cover the relevant physical-time scale. In our setting, however, the required number of decision steps can become very large when short-duration actions are allowed, leading to reduced training stability and efficiency. By contrast, physical-time truncation specifies the rollout horizon directly on the appropriate elapsed-time scale. In practice, this modification results in more stable training and helps avoid degenerate local optima, such as repeatedly choosing short waiting actions.

Transparent GAE.

Because topological graphs have heterogeneous edge lengths and robots may execute waiting actions, state transitions naturally occur over variable time intervals. This makes the multi-robot monitoring problem well suited to an event-driven Semi-Markov Decision Process (SMDP) formulation
(
95
)
. Existing asynchronous multi-agent reinforcement learning methods
(
111
)
often model variable execution times using hierarchical macro-actions. However, such mechanisms introduce substantial computational overhead and are not necessary for the atomic navigation actions considered in graph-based monitoring environments. To retain the efficiency of standard parallel training paradigms without sacrificing asynchronous semantics, we align the multi-agent system through globally synchronized event steps. Specifically, we augment the action space with a
no-op
action. At each event step, agents that are still traversing edges or executing waiting actions are constrained by an action mask to select
no-op
. This mechanism converts asynchronous agent-level events into dense joint transitions, thereby preserving a fully tensorized rollout structure for efficient batching while avoiding the engineering complexity of ragged, agent-specific event logs.

However, naively incorporating these masked
no-op
transitions into a conventional optimization pipeline can distort value estimation, because standard GAE
(
89
)
discounts and accumulates temporal difference (TD) errors across inactive steps. To address this issue, we propose
Transparent Generalized Advantage Estimation (Trans-GAE)
, which folds inactive intermediate steps and bridges the temporal gap between consecutive decision points. Formally, let
\mathbb{I}_{n}^{i}\in\{0,1\}
be an indicator that equals
1
if agent
i
is at a valid decision step
n
, such as when it arrives at a node or completes a waiting action. We define the set of active decision steps for agent
i
as
\mathcal{K}^{i}=\{n\mid\mathbb{I}_{n}^{i}=1\}
. For any
n\in\mathcal{K}^{i}
, let
n^{+}=\min\{n^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K}^{i}\mid n^{\prime}>n\}
denote the next active decision step, and let
\ell=n^{+}-n-1
be the number of intermediate inactive steps during which the agent is forced to take a
no-op
. Instead of using the standard one-step TD error, Trans-GAE constructs a folded multi-step TD error
\tilde{\delta}_{n}
that accumulates the rewards collected between two consecutive decision points:

\tilde{\delta}_{n}=\sum_{j=0}^{\ell}\gamma^{j}r_{n+j}+\gamma^{\ell+1}V(s_{n^{+}})-V(s_{n})

where
\gamma
is the discount factor and
V(s)
is the value function. The generalized advantage
\hat{A}_{n}
is then computed recursively over active decision steps, thereby transparently bypassing the inactive steps:

\hat{A}_{n}=\begin{cases}\tilde{\delta}_{n}+(\gamma\lambda)^{\ell+1}\hat{A}_{n^{+}},&\text{if }n\in\mathcal{K}^{i},\\
0,&\text{if }n\notin\mathcal{K}^{i},\end{cases}

where
\lambda
is the trace decay parameter. By using the accumulated decay factor of
(\gamma\lambda)^{\ell+1}
, Trans-GAE accounts for the true temporal distance between consecutive decision points. As a result, rewards are attributed to the actions that initiate the corresponding movements or waiting periods, while value gradients are masked out for non-decision transitions. This stabilizes policy optimization in asynchronous parallel environments.

Customized Observation Preprocessing.
Standard observation preprocessing methods typically normalize all observation dimensions using running means and standard deviations. However, the observation space in TWLO contains discrete dimensions that explicitly represent vertex indices. Applying continuous normalization to these categorical variables can distort their underlying semantics and degrade learning performance. To address this issue, we introduce a heterogeneous preprocessing module that applies different transformations to different classes of observation dimensions before they are fed into the neural networks.

For vertex-index dimensions, including each agent’s departure node, destination node, and current position when available, we replace standard normalization with a graph-structural embedding. Specifically, we compute a Graph Positional Encoding (GPE)
(
12
)
for each vertex by eigendecomposing the symmetric normalized Laplacian
\tilde{L}=I-D^{-1/2}WD^{-1/2}
, where
W\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{V}|\times|\mathcal{V}|}
denotes the symmetrized adjacency matrix of the patrol graph with directed edges treated as bidirectional,
and
D
is the corresponding diagonal degree matrix with
D_{ii}=\sum_{j}W_{ij}
. We retain the
d_{\text{gpe}}
eigenvectors associated with the smallest non-trivial eigenvalues, excluding the constant eigenvector. This produces a fixed
(|{\mathcal{V}}|\times d_{\text{gpe}})
embedding table that encodes the topological position of each vertex in the patrol graph. The table is kept frozen throughout training. At runtime, each integer vertex index is mapped to its corresponding
d_{\text{gpe}}
-dimensional positional descriptor, which is then passed through a trainable linear projection to obtain a
d_{\text{proj}}
-dimensional feature vector. This representation preserves graph-structural proximity and avoids dependence on arbitrary integer labels.

For scalar continuous features, we apply transformations according to their numerical properties. The historical worst weighted latency is a monotonically nondecreasing scalar that can grow without a predictable upper bound within an episode. We therefore first apply a
\log(1+\cdot)
transformation to reduce distributional skew, and then normalize the result using running mean and standard deviation statistics estimated online from the training stream. Instantaneous weighted latency values and per-agent remaining action times are normalized similarly using online running statistics. In contrast, the elapsed-time counter
\eta
has a known upper bound: it increases from zero up to the evaluation horizon
T
and remains constant thereafter. We therefore normalize it by dividing by
T
, mapping it to the interval
[0,1]
without requiring online statistics. Binary readiness flags and categorical one-hot identity encodings are already bounded in
\{0,1\}
and do not encode magnitude information, so they are passed through without modification.

Credit assignment.
Another practical challenge arises from the use of a common reward. Since all agents receive the same global signal
z_{n}
, and since
z_{n}
often remains unchanged for many event steps before increasing, the resulting feedback is only weakly informative at the individual-agent level. This makes explicit credit assignment important for stable multi-agent learning. To address this issue, we adopt a counterfactual credit assignment scheme
(
99
;
32
)
, which estimates each agent’s contribution by comparing the realized joint outcome with a counterfactual one in which the agent instead takes a baseline action. Motivated by the idea of encouraging agents to influence external states in cooperative MARL
(
61
)
, we distinguish two environment-level quantities in our monitoring MDP: the current latency profile
\boldsymbol{L}_{n}
and the historical worst-latency tracker
z_{n}
. Based on these two quantities, we design two counterfactual reward signals to quantify each agent’s contribution to the evolution of these external states. Because the environment in our MDP is deterministic, the effect of an individual agent’s action can be identified explicitly. For each agent
r\in{\mathcal{R}}
, we construct two one-step counterfactual rewards by comparing the realized next state with the counterfactual next state obtained by replacing the agent’s action with a baseline action. The first reward measures the agent’s contribution to the instantaneous latency,

R_{n,r}^{L}=\sum_{v\in V}{\phi}(v)\left(L_{n+1,v}^{(-r)}-L_{n+1,v}\right),

where
L_{n+1,v}
is the realized next-step latency, and
L_{n+1,v}^{(-r)}
is the corresponding counterfactual latency. The second reward captures the agent’s contribution to the historical worst-latency tracker,

R_{n,r}^{z}=z_{n+1}^{(-r)}-z_{n+1},

where
z_{n+1}
and
z_{n+1}^{(-r)}
denote the realized and counterfactual next-step tracker values, respectively. The final credit signal is then defined as a weighted combination of these two terms:

R_{n,r}^{\mathrm{cf}}=\lambda_{L}R_{n,r}^{L}+\lambda_{z}R_{n,r}^{z},

where
\lambda_{L}
and
\lambda_{z}
are tunable hyperparameters that control the relative contributions of the two components.

Parameter sharing and role information.
To improve scalability and sample efficiency, we adopt parameter sharing across all agents, which is common in MARL
(
31
;
39
;
113
)
. This choice is also well aligned with our monitoring problem, where the robots are homogeneous and are expected to follow a common decision rule. However, parameter sharing introduces an inherent symmetry issue. To provide sufficient decision information, each agent is allowed to access the global state. Yet, when all agents receive exactly the same global input under a shared network, the policy cannot distinguish which agent is being controlled. A natural remedy is to additionally provide each agent’s own position. Nevertheless, this remains insufficient in our setting: if two agents arrive at the same location and observe the same global state, then under a shared network and deterministic environment dynamics, a deterministic policy may drive them to take identical actions thereafter, leading to severe redundancy and inefficient use of monitoring resources. A common way to break this symmetry is to augment the input with an agent-specific index, as is standard in parameter-sharing MARL
(
32
;
85
;
112
)
. Although such a design can improve coordination without fundamentally reducing policy expressiveness, it also breaks the permutation symmetry inherent in the original monitoring problem. To address this issue, we introduce a lightweight role encoding that distinguishes agents while preserving symmetry as much as possible. Specifically, instead of assigning each agent a fixed identity index, we augment its positional information with a local decision-order label, defined as the order in which the agent arrives at the current node among all agents that are ready to act there.

Imitation learning.
Pure reinforcement learning can be inefficient for our monitoring problem, especially in large-scale environments with multiple agents, since effective policies typically require nontrivial coordination patterns and often exhibit strong structural regularity. In such complex multi-agent settings, discovering these strategies purely through exploration is challenging, particularly when the reward is sparse, delayed, and coupled across agents. Motivated by the widespread use of imitation learning as a warm-start mechanism for difficult RL problems
(
84
;
87
;
47
;
101
)
, we combine imitation learning with subsequent RL. As outlined in
Algorithm

1
, this approach provides a strong initial policy prior from heuristic demonstrations, while still allowing RL to further improve the policy beyond the original heuristic. Specifically, we first run heuristic policies to collect demonstration trajectories and compute normalized Monte Carlo returns from Line
5
to
8
. These tuples are subsequently stored in an offline dataset
\mathcal{D}
at Line
9
. Based on this dataset, we pretrain both the actor and the critic before RL optimization. As detailed in Line
17
, the actor is pretrained via behavior cloning on the demonstrated actions using a cross-entropy loss, while enforcing the corresponding action masks
m_{n}^{i}
to ensure only valid actions are considered. To remain consistent with the value normalization
(
112
)
used in the subsequent RL stage, Line
16
shows that the critic is trained to fit the normalized returns
\hat{G}_{n}^{i}
via mean squared error. The corresponding mean
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{G}]
and standard deviation
\mathrm{std}(\mathbf{G})
are then utilized to initialize the running return statistics during the RL training phase in Line
23
. In this way, imitation learning provides a strong warm start for the policy and aligns the critic initialization with the normalized value learning used later in MAPPO. Following the same paradigm, we extended this imitation learning pretraining mechanism to other MARL algorithms, with empirical evaluations demonstrating that MAPPO yields the best performance.

Platform

Having established the theoretical foundations and developed the RL-based solution methods for the multi-robot persistent monitoring problem, we now present a modular and extensible simulation and evaluation platform that integrates representative heuristics and RL-based algorithms within a unified framework. The platform is designed to support direct and fair comparison across algorithms under shared benchmark settings, while allowing easy incorporation of newly developed methods. Such a platform is needed because, although a wide range of methods has been proposed for multi-robot monitoring, systematic comparison among these methods remains limited. As discussed in the related work section, several existing platforms integrate representative heuristic algorithms, such as
patrolling_sim

(
79
)
. However, to the best of our knowledge, no unified platform currently supports both heuristic and RL-based monitoring methods in a manner that enables direct comparison under unified environments, interfaces, and evaluation criteria.
To address this gap, we develop
Multi-Robot Monitoring Benchmark (M2Bench)
, a modular and extensible simulation platform for persistent monitoring.

As illustrated in
Fig.

2
, M2Bench structures the experimental workflow into several modular layers. These include a YAML-driven configuration layer, an environment layer for shared monitoring dynamics and task-specific MDP wrappers, a data layer for rollout collection and replay storage, an algorithm layer that integrates heuristic and learning-based methods, and a utility layer for evaluation, visualization, logging, checkpointing, and hyperparameter sweeps. The remainder of this section provides a detailed description of the main modules and features.

Modular Design and Configuration Workflow.

Building on this layered architecture, M2Bench represents each part of an experiment as an interchangeable module with a clearly defined interface. Users can modify or replace individual components, including the graph instance, monitoring formulation, policy class, algorithm, and neural network architecture, without rewriting the remaining experimental pipeline. The platform builds on widely used libraries, including PyTorch
(
76
)
, Gymnasium
(
98
)
, and PettingZoo
(
97
)
, while avoiding dependence on a heavy monolithic training framework.
From the users’ perspective, these modules are assembled through a unified configuration-driven workflow. A single YAML file specifies the graph, monitoring or MDP formulation, robot settings, algorithm type, network options, and training hyperparameters. Given this configuration, M2Bench automatically instantiates the corresponding environment, policy, collector, buffer, trainer, and evaluation routines, and executes the associated experimental pipeline, including training, checkpointing, testing, quantitative evaluation, logging with tools such as W&B and TensorBoard, visualization, animation rendering, and hyperparameter sweeps. This declarative interface facilitates switching between heuristic and RL-based methods, comparing different monitoring formulations under shared settings, and reproducing experiments without repeated modifications to low-level code.

Monitoring-Oriented Simulation.
The core environment component of M2Bench is
MonitorWorld
, which simulates persistent monitoring dynamics on finite weighted graphs. Following
Section

2.1
, it models robot motion, waiting, travel, visit events, physical-time evolution, and latency updates. MonitorWorld is independent of specific MDP formulations. Observations, action encodings, rewards, and termination conditions are instead defined by external wrappers, allowing multiple learning formulations and non-RL heuristic policies to share the same simulator. It supports both fixed-step synchronous simulation and event-driven asynchronous simulation, enabling discrete-time and event-driven MDPs to be built on a common environment. This shared design also facilitates imitation pretraining in
Section

4.2
, since heuristic trajectories can be directly reused by learning-based methods.

Dataflow across the pipelines.
To support the integrated algorithms, M2Bench organizes the experimental workflow into four main dataflow pipelines: heuristic execution, on-policy MARL, off-policy MARL, and imitation-based pretraining. The first three pipelines largely follow standard control and reinforcement learning paradigms. In the
heuristic
pipeline, the standalone evaluator directly interacts with
MonitorWorld
by reading the global state and executing joint actions. The
RL-based
pipelines instead use tensorized data from vectorized parallel environments, including per-agent observations and auxiliary tensors such as
active_mask
, which indicates whether each agent is at a valid decision step. The
imitation-learning
pipeline is detailed in
Section

4.2
, with additional implementation details to be released in our GitHub repository.

Policy Mapping.
Inspired by Tianshou
(

Unified Evaluation Metrics.
To enable fair comparison across methods, M2Bench evaluates all approaches under a unified set of monitoring-quality metrics. Following the classical patrolling literature
(
88
)
, we consider four standard criteria: instantaneous graph idleness (IGI), average graph idleness (AGI), instantaneous worst idleness (IWI), and worst idleness (WI). The metrics are defined consistently with their classical counterparts, but are extended to incorporate the node-priority weights introduced in
Section

2.1
. Specifically, for a policy
\boldsymbol{\pi}
and time
t\geq 0
,

\mathrm{IGI}(\boldsymbol{\pi},t):=\frac{1}{|{\mathcal{V}}|}\sum_{v\in{\mathcal{V}}}{\phi}(v)\,{L_{v}^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}}(t),

which measures the mean weighted latency over all nodes. Its time-averaged version over a horizon
H>0
is

\mathrm{AGI}(\boldsymbol{\pi},H):=\frac{1}{H}\int_{0}^{H}\mathrm{IGI}(\boldsymbol{\pi},t)\,dt.

The instantaneous worst idleness is defined by

\mathrm{IWI}(\boldsymbol{\pi},t):=\max_{v\in{\mathcal{V}}}{\phi}(v)\,{L_{v}^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}}(t),

which is exactly the weighted worst latency
{L}^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(t)
introduced in
Section

2.1
. Finally, the worst idleness over horizon
H
is defined by

\mathrm{WI}(\boldsymbol{\pi},H):=\sup_{0\leq t\leq H}\mathrm{IWI}(\boldsymbol{\pi},t).

(10)

When all node weights are identical, these definitions recover the standard unweighted idleness metrics (up to a constant scaling factor).
This unified metric suite allows heuristic, imitation-based, and reinforcement-learning-based methods to be evaluated under a common protocol.

Extensibility.

M2Bench is designed for extensibility through a centralized registry-and-configuration mechanism. Core components such as MDP wrappers, algorithms, network modules, and trainers are instantiated from the registry rather than hard-coded into the training loop. Thus, adding a new module typically requires only implementing a standardized interface and registering it with the framework, without modifying the overall execution pipeline.

7
Conclusion

This paper studied multi-robot persistent monitoring on weighted graphs under a tail worst-case weighted-latency objective. We introduced a family of tail-performance objectives to capture long-run monitoring quality beyond the initial transient phase, and established several theoretical results, including optimal-solution existence, invariance of the optimal value, periodic approximation, and bounded error under discretized waiting
times. We then reformulated the original continuous-time problem as an equivalent event-driven Markov decision process, termed the TWLO-MDP. This reformulation converts the non-additive tail objective into a standard average-reward criterion while preserving optimality, thereby enabling reinforcement-learning-based solution methods. We further developed M2Bench, a unified platform for training, evaluating, and comparing heuristic and learning-based monitoring algorithms. Experiments on synthetic and realistic scenarios demonstrated the effectiveness of the proposed methods.

Future work will consider more complicated monitoring settings, including dynamic graphs, uncertain travel times, changing node priorities, and additional robot constraints, as well as improved scalability to larger teams and environments.

A
Auxiliary definitions and lemmas

We first introduce several concepts and preliminary results that will be repeatedly used in the subsequent proofs in the Appendices.

Canonical motion.

We fix a canonical motion rule to uniquely determine how the system evolves
between position configurations.
Whenever the system moves from
\boldsymbol{p}
to
\boldsymbol{p}^{\prime}
in
{|{\mathcal{G}}|^{k}}
,
each robot travels at unit speed along a fixed shortest path and waits at the
destination until all others arrive.
This induces a unique canonical transition between any two configurations within
the same connected component, and the duration of such a transition never exceeds
the graph diameter
{D_{{\mathcal{G}}}}
.

Joint state.
In order to reason about the system’s evolution and design optimal decision rules, the information required at any time
t
consists of two parts: the current positions of all robots
\boldsymbol{p}=(p_{r_{1}},\dots,p_{r_{k}})\in{|{\mathcal{G}}|^{k}}
and the current latency values
\boldsymbol{L}=(L_{1},\cdots,L_{|{\mathcal{V}}|})\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{{|{\mathcal{V}}|}}
at all nodes. Therefore, we define the joint state of the system as

{\Xi}:=\left(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{L}\right).

(11)

The joint state space is then

{\mathcal{X}}:={|{\mathcal{G}}|}^{{k}}\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{{|{\mathcal{V}}|}}.

(12)

To compare different joint states, we equip the state space
{\mathcal{X}}
with the following distance metric. For two joint states
{\Xi}=\left(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{L}\right)
and
{\Xi}^{\prime}=\left(\boldsymbol{p}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{L}^{\prime}\right)
, define

{d_{{\mathcal{X}}}}\big(\Xi,{\Xi}^{\prime}):=\max\Big\{\,{d_{|{\mathcal{G}}|^{{k}}}}(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{p}^{\prime}),\;\max_{v\in{\mathcal{V}}}|L_{v}-L^{\prime}_{v}|\,\Big\}.

This metric measures the maximum discrepancy between robot positions (under the shortest path metric
{d_{|{\mathcal{G}}|^{{k}}}}
on
{|{\mathcal{G}}|^{k}}
) and node latencies (under the absolute difference on
{\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}
). It is straightforward to verify that
{d_{{\mathcal{X}}}}
satisfies non-negativity, identity of indiscernibles, symmetry, and the triangle inequality. Therefore,
({\mathcal{X}},{d_{{\mathcal{X}}}})
is a valid metric space.

We present two straightforward lemmas that are directly related to the intrinsic properties of the monitoring problem.

Lemma 3

(Coverage in sufficiently long intervals)
.

Let
[\alpha,\beta]
be an interval of length
\tau=\beta-\alpha
and the worst weighted latency of a strategy
\boldsymbol{\pi}
over
[\alpha,\beta]
is
L
, i.e.,
\sup_{t\in[\alpha,\beta]}{L}^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(t)=L
. Define

\tau_{\mathrm{cov}}(L):=\frac{L}{{\phi_{\min}}}.

Lemma 11

(Bound of the discretization error)
.

For any rational strategy
\boldsymbol{\pi}\in\bar{\Pi}
, there exists a strategy with discretized waiting time

We will prove this theorem by constructing a satisfying discretized strategy via rounding each waiting duration up or down while tracking the accumulated rounding error. Specifically, for each robot

, we divide its time axis into alternating segments of

and

, which is denoted as

\displaystyle 0=t_{r,0}<t_{r,1}<t_{r,2}<\cdots,

\displaystyle\tau_{r,j}=t_{r,j}-t_{r,j-1}>0,\quad j\geq 1.

We use two index sets to distinguish the two types of events:
\mathcal{W}_{r}:=
{indices of waiting events},
\mathcal{M}_{r}:=
{indices of traveling events}. According,

represents all waiting durations and
\{\tau_{r,j}\}_{j\in\mathcal{M}_{r}}
all traveling durations, which are fixed and decided by the edge length.

Given a granularity
\Delta>

\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\Delta}\in\bar{\Pi}_{\Delta}
of each robot’s event sequence. For every robot

, we deal with the discretization in the order of increasing index

•

For all traveling indices

, we keep
\hat{\tau}_{r,j}=\tau_{r,j}
, since traveling times are fixed by the edge lengths.

•

For all waiting indices

is quantized among the two nearest integer multiple of

\hat{\tau}_{r,j}\in\bigl\{\lfloor\frac{\tau_{r,j}}{\Delta}\rfloor\Delta,\ \lceil\frac{\tau_{r,j}}{\Delta}\rceil\Delta\bigr\}

During quantization, if

\hat{\tau}_{r,j}=\lfloor\frac{\tau_{r,j}}{\Delta}\rfloor\Delta

,

.

By recursively defining the new event times as

\hat{t}_{r,0}=0,\quad\hat{t}_{r,j}=\hat{t}_{r,j-1}+\hat{\tau}_{r,j},\quad j\geq 1,

. Note that if a waiting segment happens to be rounded to zero, the associated event simply disappears without affecting the sort of event sequence.

By the above construction, for any robot
r\in{\mathcal{R}}
and any index

, the event time of
\boldsymbol{\pi}
and
\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\Delta}
satisfies

\displaystyle\hat{t}_{r,j}-t_{r,j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\leq j}(\hat{\tau}_{r,i}-\tau_{r,i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in W_{r},\,i\leq j}(\hat{\tau}_{r,i}-\tau_{r,i})

\displaystyle=E_{r,j+1}.

The quantization rule guarantees

for all

\left|\hat{t}_{r,j}-t_{r,j}\right|\leq\Delta,\quad\forall r\in{\mathcal{R}},\forall j\geq 0.

(28)

Now we consider a fixed node

under
\boldsymbol{\pi}

\displaystyle\geq t_{r,j^{*}}(v)-\Delta

The node

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}R_{n}=J_{T}(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\xi}).

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}R_{n}=\infty=J_{T}(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\xi}).

1

, we obtain

\lim_{N\to\infty}B_{N}=z_{\infty}=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}z_{n}.

We then show that
B_{N}
is nonnegative and nondecreasing: since
B_{N}\leq z_{N-1}\leq z_{N}
, we have

B_{N+1}-B_{N}=\frac{\Delta t_{N}\,(z_{N}-B_{N})}{\sum_{n=0}^{N}\Delta t_{n}}\geq 0.

Hence, by the conditional monotone convergence theorem, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[B_{N}\mid s_{0}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[\lim_{N\to\infty}B_{N}\,\big|\,s_{0}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[z_{\infty}\,\big|\,s_{0}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}z_{n}\,\big|\,s_{0}\right]=J^{\mathrm{ave}}_{s_{0}}(\mu),

where the last equality holds as we show in (
30
).
This completes the proof of
Lemma

2
.

∎
