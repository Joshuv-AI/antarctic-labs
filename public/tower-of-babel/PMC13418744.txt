Twist-encoded magnetic plasmon interferometry for label-free, orientation-resolved single-molecule imaging
Authors: Yi Sun, Wen-Li Lv, Qi-Bing Xiao, Gang Wu, Xian-Wei Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Molecular twist-related rotational dynamics at solid-liquid interfaces governs recognition, dissipation, and barrier crossing, yet direct label-free visualization of single-molecule torsion remains challenging. Here, we present twist-encoded magnetic plasmon interferometry (TEMPi), which converts nanorod twist near a metal film into a phase-encoded, orientation-resolved optical signal. Near-field coupling between a gold nanorod and its mirror image in the film excites a localized magnetic plasmon, enabling quantitative mapping of probe twist to interferometric phase. Wide-field, label-free operation in native buffers enables real-time tracking of rotational dynamics and interfacial conformational changes. TEMPi resolves three confinement-dependent rotational regimes, distinguishes effective dual- and single-contact protein anchoring, and localizes single binding sites on the probe through twist-dependent centroid shifts. Applied to DNA hybridization, it quantifies an effective ninefold increase in torsional stiffness and an order-of-magnitude faster twisting relaxation upon duplex formation. These results establish TEMPi as a general twist-resolved metrology for probing effective interfacial molecular mechanics and designing torsion-responsive interfaces in chemistry, biology, and nanotechnology.

INTRODUCTION

The ability to image and mechanistically resolve single-molecule events at solid-liquid interfaces underpins advances in biophysics, catalysis, and surface chemistry (1–5). Many interfacial processes are governed not only by position and binding state but also by torsional motion (twist), the in-plane and out-of-plane reorientations that reshape local energy landscapes, modulate dissipation pathways, and gate barrier crossing (6, 7). In nucleic acids, local twist and supercoiling shape replication and transcription (8); in proteins, ligand binding often induces torsional changes that trigger allosteric switching (9); and biological motors transduce energy through twist (10). Direct characterization and quantification of twisting behavior—including amplitude, stiffness, relaxation time, and the associated energy landscape—would therefore enable predictive structure-function relationships and mechanism-aware sensing at interfaces.

At solid-liquid interfaces, nanoparticle-tether-substrate (“sandwich”) architectures are versatile platforms for label-free studies in catalysis, biosensing, single-molecule biophysics (11–13). Plasmonic films and nanogaps amplify light-matter interactions under native conditions, enabling high sensitivity (14). Yet, most implementations prioritize scalar signals or center-of-mass orientation, leaving the twisting degree of freedom largely unmeasured (15). A twist-resolved channel can decouple binding geometry from environmental heterogeneity media, reveal hidden energy barriers, and enhance molecular specificity. Among colloidal probes, gold nanorods (GNRs), are natural interfacial twist sensors because their longitudinal and transverse plasmon modes and strong anisotropy produce angle-dependent phase responses that isotropic particles cannot provide (16–18).

Existing approaches leave the torsional dimension underresolved for different reasons. Label-free spectral or polarization-based imaging can detect single particles and track position (19, 20), but angles are often inferred from intensity and are vulnerable to illumination instability, focus drift, and background scattering in heterogeneous interfacial environments. Force-based single-molecule instruments (e.g., magnetic tweezers and optical torque) provide exquisite torsional control but rely on external fields and molecular handles (21, 22), limiting throughput and complicating measurements at fragile interfaces. These constraints motivate an interface compatible, label-free, and force-free modality that converts nanoscale twisting into quantitative optical observables.

Here, we report twist-encoded magnetic plasmon interferometry (TEMPi), a label-free, force-free interferometric approach that reads out torsionally constrained interfacial twist rotation as a phase-encoded signal. Unlike traditional intensity-based readouts, TEMPi converts rotational motion of an anisotropic plasmonic probe near a metal film into a robust phase contrast via magnetic-plasmon coupling, with the measured signal reflecting the rotational degree of freedom of the nanorod probe constrained by the attached molecular assembly. A simple phase-mapping algorithm enables wide-field, high-throughput measurements under native buffers without external fields or labels. With this capability, we resolve effective twisting states and transitions, localize functional binding sites on the probe, and quantify torsional free-energy landscapes, effective stiffness, and relaxation times from thermal fluctuations. By adding a twist-resolved information channel to label-free imaging, TEMPi empowers mechanism-centric studies and, more specific, quantitative interfacial sensing.

RESULTS

Principle of TEMPi

To resolve in real time the subdiffusive twisting rotational dynamics of tethered molecular assemblies at surface-confined interfaces, we built a wide-field plasmonic interferometric microscope that uses a single nanorod as the optical probe (Fig. 1) (23). This platform is based on an inverted optical microscope equipped with a high numerical aperture oil-immersion objective [60×, numerical aperture (N.A.) = 1.49]. A linearly polarized 670-nm beam is directed to the gold film (47 nm)/glass interface through the objective to launch an evanescent surface-plasmon polariton (SPP) field. The reflected interfacial signal is efficiently collected by the same objective and directed through the reflection pathway to a high-sensitivity charge-coupled device (CCD) camera, allowing real-time acquisition of wide-field, high-throughput interferometric scattering image sequences. From these images, we simultaneously extract phase, intensity, and two-dimensional (2D) position of individual nanoparticles, providing a robust foundation for systematic analysis of orientation- and trajectory-resolved analysis.

Owing to dipolar anisotropy, GNRs support longitudinal and transverse localized surface plasmon resonances that, in proximity to a gold film, strongly couple to SPPs. During rotation, near-field coupling between the rod and its mirror charges in the film breaks symmetry and drives a circulating displacement current, creating a localized magnetic plasmon [a rod-film inductor–capacitor (LC) circuit analog] (Fig. 1, top left). The strength and phase of this hybrid mode depend sensitively on nanorod orientation with respect to SPP propagation, providing an optical transduction pathway from twist to phase.

In the far field, a single GNR appears as ripple-like interferometric pattern with parabolic tails. As the rod reorients, the centerline of the pattern along the vertical axis exhibits characteristic bright-dark reversals and fringe shifts that arise from phase evolution of the interferometric signal, although intensity can covary with orientation in rotational regimes. We analyze each frame by mapping two features, phase inversion and pattern asymmetry, into orientation, thereby yielding the nanorod’s in-plane orientation and uniquely determining its azimuthal angle (Fig. 1, top right). TEMPi also discerns out-of-plane tilt; we calibrate its signature below and use it to track 3D rotational dynamics. Through this phase-encoded readout, we can continuously track the rotational motion of individual probes and map their interference patterns to orientation-related parameters (Fig. 1, bottom left). The directly measured observable is the rotational motion of the nanorod probe, from which torsionally constrained molecular behavior is inferred in the context of probe-molecule coupling. Leveraging these parameters, we extract detailed information on effective twisting dynamics and its effects, including multicontact and single-contact anchoring properties, conformational changes, binding-site localization on the nanorod, the effective free-energy landscapes, and torque-related parameters. In addition, the SPP excitation is evanescent, confined to ∼100 nm above the gold film, which effectively suppresses the bulk-solution background. Moreover, since the orientation is derived from phase rather than raw intensity, the readout is less sensitive to global illumination drift and other amplitude-based artifacts. Under the present imaging conditions, TEMPi achieves an angular detection limit of ∼0.3° to 0.4° and a lateral localization precision of ∼2.1 to 2.2 nm (Supplementary Text, section 1.9). These factors allow TEMPi to operate in a label-free and force-free manner, supporting wide-field, high-throughput measurements in native buffers while remaining compatible with fragile interfaces.

Magnetic plasmon induced by nanorod rotation

To quantify how nanorod orientation influences local field distributions and phase response, we performed finite-difference time-domain (FDTD) simulations for a GNR (20 nm by 80 nm) above a gold film under SPP excitation. When SPPs propagate along the surface and couple with a GNR (fig. S1), the near field concentrates at the ends of the nanorod and forms vortex-like magnetic patterns, characterized by a typical oppositely symmetric circular flow, indicative of the formation of local magnetic plasmon (24) (Fig. 2A and fig. S2). This circulation originates from the resonant response of conduction electrons within the metallic nanorod under SPP excitation, essentially manifesting as a localized magnetic hybridized mode (25). The vortex symmetry and strength are strongly angle-dependent: As the nanorod orientation shifts from perpendicular (90°) to parallel (0°) with respect to the SPP propagation direction, the vortices are progressively reshaped and suppressed, underscoring the high sensitivity of the magnetic response to structural anisotropy.

A cross section analysis at the nanorod–gold film interface reveals that the induced current loop is primarily localized near the lower region of the nanorod (Fig. 2B). Physically, the nanorod’s dipolar mode induces mirror charges in the film (26, 27). The resulting circulating displacement current (current density J) produces an oscillating magnetic dipole along the surface normal, yielding strong subwavelength-scale magnetic field enhancement. The system is described by an equivalent LC circuit model (28), in which the rod and film act as inductors and the dielectric spacer provides the gap capacitanceλMM=2πc(Lnr+Lfilm)Cshell/2(1)where λMM denotes the resonance wavelength of the dipolar-mirrored mode, Lnr and Lfilm are the rod/film inductances, and Cshell is the gap capacitance. Because each term depends on geometry and orientation, rotation continuously tunes the magnetic-mode amplitude and spatial profile, effectively modulating the local effective medium (Fig. 2C). In the experiments, the effective nanocavity medium can vary with biomolecular tethers, nanorod surface modification layers, and hydrated polymer confinement layers, thereby changing the dielectric filling of the rod-film gap. A first-order sensitivity analysis at fixed nanorod/film geometry (fig. S3) shows that isolated biomolecular tethers [immunoglobulin G (IgG)/DNA] induce only a very small resonance shift of 0.08 to 0.17% and phase perturbation of 0.15 to 0.30% at 670 nm. The surface modification layer on the nanorods, represented by poly(ethylene glycol) (PEG), introduces a baseline dielectric offset, with a resonance shift of 0.3 to 0.8% and phase perturbation of 0.5 to 1.4%. Even for a hydrated polymer confinement layer, represented by poly(acrylic acid) (PAA), the resulting shift remains relatively limited and still minor, with a resonance shift of 2.9 to 4.0% and phase perturbation of 5.1 to 7.0%. Thus, dielectric gap filling may slightly shift the operating point and rescale phase sensitivity but preserves the monotonic phase-angle relation used for orientation determination.

Using S-parameter retrieval (29), we extracted the phase ψ from effective permittivity εeff of the rod-film system versus wavelength and rotation angle (Fig. 2, D to F). From εeff, we computed the complex polarizability, whose phase ψ sets the interferometric phase in TEMPi. The calculations predict a maximal phase excursion near ∼670 nm, guiding our excitation choice. At 670 nm, ψ spans 0-π and is symmetric about 90° (Fig. 2E), producing a 0° to 180° azimuthal ambiguity if phase is used alone. We therefore combine phase with a pattern-asymmetry metric, originating from weak mirror-like reflection along the nanorod long axis and a convolution along that axis (fig. S4), to determine orientation (Fig. 2E). The plasmonic interferometric scattering signal follows (30, 31)I∼∣α∣eiψEsp(r)Es(r′)e−iκ∣r−r′∣(2)where Esp represents the planar plasmonic wave at position r; Es is the scattered wave by the nanorod at position r′; ∣α∣ is the amplitude of the GNR’s polarizability; ψ denotes its phase, which also sets the phase of the interferometric pattern; and κ is the plasmonic wave number. Both FDTD and this analytical model predict a monotonic increase of phase with angle, in excellent agreement with measured fringe inversions and line profile shifts extracted from a cross section located 2 μm above the scattering center. The analysis of simulated near-field scattering patterns at different rotational angles reproduces the observed fringe phase shifts and envelope changes (Fig. 2G), linking local magnetic response and effective-medium modulation to the far-field interferometric signature. There results establish the physical basis for angle determination and rationalize the high orientation sensitivity of the phase-encoded TEMPi readout. The present analysis is established for a dilute-particle regime with well-separated single-nanorod interferometric patterns. Denser nanorod configurations would introduce overlap and cross-talk that are not captured by the current single-particle analysis (fig. S5). Beyond interparticle spacing, nanorod dimensions also define the single-particle phase-angle transfer relationship. Changes in rod size can alter both the monotonicity and angular sensitivity of this mapping, as illustrated by simulations of different nanorod sizes (Supplementary Text and fig. S6).

Plasmonic imaging of nanorods under surface confinement

We established a controlled surface confinement assay to interrogate multidimensional rotation of nanorods at interfaces and their interferometric signature. The gold substrate was sequentially modified with a positively charged poly-L-lysine (PLL) layer and a sparse, weakly negatively charged PAA network, yielding a loosely crosslinked scaffold capable of adsorbing positively charged GNRs. Electrochemical and atomic force microscopy characterization indicate a compact PLL base layer (∼1 to 3 nm) and a hydrated PAA overlayer with an effective thickness of ∼15 to 38 nm (Supplementary Text and fig. S7). This architecture suppresses 3D translational diffusion, while its flexible, sparse mesh preserves substantial rotational freedom locally, providing an optimal platform for single-particle rotational measurements (Fig. 3A). On the basis of the confinement physics, three discrete states are anticipated according to the degree of nanorod engagement with the polymer network and the associated rotational behavior: weak confinement with large-amplitude angular fluctuations, moderate confinement with partially restricted fluctuations, and strong confinement with near-immobilized orientation. These states produce pronounced differences in rotational dynamics that TEMPi resolves directly in the interferometric images.

Using the high-phase sensitivity of TEMPi and its high stability during long-term observations, we tracked the spatiotemporal evolution of interferometric fringe profiles to extract the nanorod’s dynamic phase (Fig. 3B, Supplementary Text, and figs. S8 and S9). The zeroth-order fringe reports the instantaneous particle phase, whereas broadening or narrowing of higher-order fringes reflects dynamic changes in orientation and rotational angle. Comparative measurements under the three confinement conditions and two nanorod preparations corroborate this correspondence (Fig. 3, C and D): In weak confinement, phase traces show pronounced fluctuations indicative of persistent large-scale rotation (SDψ = 0.286 π); in moderate confinement, phase excursions are reduced (SDψ = 0.067 π) (movie S1); and in strong confinement, fringes are nearly stationary, indicating a stable orientation (SDψ = 0.017 π; movie S2). The distinct phase and fringe evolution across states highlights the TEMPi’s ability to resolve subwavelength rotational dynamics.

To push sensitivity under weak-to-moderate confinement, we used a dedicated phase recognition routine and interferometric signal analysis, enabling quantitative, real-time tracking of single-nanorod rotation. The observed two-state switching events show that local viscosity heterogeneity in complex environments markedly shapes time-dependent rotational kinetics (Fig. 3D). The phase recognition framework developed here also facilitates more detailed investigations of subsequent surface-twist processes. These measurements provide direct experimental validation of the link between interferometric phase and rotational dynamics and furnish a general strategy for interrogating and modulating multidimensional probe motion at confined interfaces.

Decoding molecular features from twist-related dynamics

Replacing spherical particles with anisotropic nanorods introduces a directly imageable and quantifiable rotational degree of freedom that is shaped by molecular constraints. On this basis, TEMPi resolves diverse molecule-particle binding modes and geometries at interfaces, thereby elevating the sandwich architecture from a mere signal amplifier to a decoupled, quantitative and predictive biointerfacial metrology. Guided by a geometric-constraint model, TEMPi distinguishes strongly constrained multicontact states from more weakly constrained single-contact states. These assignments are made operationally from the combined phase/intensity signatures, z-fluctuation amplitudes, and inferred free-energy profiles; in the latter, in-plane and out-of-plane twisting signatures are further resolved, and transient events such as hopping, sliding, and dissociation are captured. For modeling and comparison, these interfacial behaviors are organized into five staged states (BS.I to BS.V) with seven finer subtypes (Supplementary Text and figs. S10 to S12). These states arise sequentially as the nanorod-interface interaction weakens, corresponding to a progression from strong to weak binding. Neighboring states can undergo repeated interconversion, while the overall trend remains toward weaker constraint (Supplementary Text and fig. S13). The discussion below highlights two representative recognition modes, BS.III and BS.IV.

We first examine a C18-protein interface to probe nanorod desorption under milder conditions (Supplementary Text), providing a platform aligned with liquid chromatography separations and a canonical model for probing molecule-interface mechanisms (32). TEMPi provides quantitative readout of orientation, phase, and tether mechanics as interfacial hydrophobicity is reduced, with bare-GNR controls confirming negligible contribution from other interfacial adsorption (fig. S13), clarifying how binding geometry and chain compliance govern release. As interfacial hydrophobic interactions weaken, the protein-mediated anchoring configuration evolves. Geometric analysis indicates that multiprotein cotethering and a single dominant tethering constraint would produce distinct rotational freedoms, providing a basis for differentiating these anchoring configurations (fig. S13). Initially, two effective contact points constrain the nanorod at the C18 surface, yielding predominantly in-plane twist with limited out-of-plane fluctuations (BS.IIIα; Fig. 4A). With further attenuation, one effective contact is released, increasing z-axis fluctuations; the system approaches a more compliant, single-contact tether (BS.IIIβ; Fig. 4B). Because IgG conjugation to the nanorod and binding to the C18 surface are nonspecific, these states are interpreted operationally as dual-contact and single-contact constraints rather than as assignments to specific antibody domains.

TEMPi traces support this assignment: raw intensity and phase covary as expected for in-plane rotation (Isca∝cos2θt; fig. S14) (33). Since the raw intensity (purple) contains contributions from both axial displacement and rotation-dependent scattering, phase-based correction was applied to remove the rotational component and better reveal the axial state–dependent intensity fluctuation (Fig. 4C, top). Following phase-based correction, the blue intensity trace stays near its mean (Fig. 4C, top), while the yellow phase trace maintains large, persistent excursions (Fig. 4C, bottom); a marked rise in intensity fluctuation denotes the transition point from the dual-contact state to the single-contact state (∼5.3 s). Free-energy profiles ΔG-Δz (Fig. 4D and Supplementary Text) reveal distinct mechanical properties: BS.IIIα displays higher rigidity (Kf = 5.07 × 10−4 N/m) with a deep potential well, whereas BS.IIIβ is more compliant (Kf = 1.94 × 10−4 N/m) with a broader well. Phase statistics indicate substantial in-plane freedom persists across the transition (Fig. 4C, bottom), implying that the release of one effective contact mainly changes the out-of-plane/axial compliance while preserving in-plane rotational freedom.

A simple geometric consistency check using representative IgG-sized dimensions indicates that release of one effective contact in an IgG-sized tether can increase the accessible extension by a few nanometers (34, 35), in agreement with the observed ∼3.2-nm increase in z-axis fluctuation amplitude after the transition. This result links the anchoring change directly to the mechanical twisting response of the probe without assigning the contact to a specific antibody domain. Critically, TEMPi provides the observables required for nanometer-scale localization on the probe itself. Because nanorod anisotropy induces twist-dependent centroid shifts (absent for spheres or cubes), cross-correlating the nanorod-centroid distribution with a tethering model pinpoints the effective binding site at 33.4 nm from the rod tip for a representative case (Fig. 4E, Supplementary Text, figs. S15 and S16, and movie S3). A modeling framework that incorporates the protein anchoring position and the statistical distribution of its flexible conformations yields the centroid probability density for different in-plane binding positions (fig. S17). The statistical analysis of the binding sites for 23 nanorods reveals a centrosymmetric pattern under twist and clusters near the central region (>27 or <57 nm from one end), reflecting the cooperative geometric constraint on in-plane orientation and out-of-plane flexibility (Fig. 4F).

Beyond in-plane twist, TEMPi resolves out-of-plane behavior. Unlike in-plane twist, out-of-plane behavior exhibits a distinctly different intensity-phase relationship and single-particle localization signature, reflecting its additional pitch-related degree of freedom. In BS.IVα, a single effective tethering constraint near one end of the nanorod preserves substantial pitch and out-of-plane twisting freedom (Fig. 5A). Correspondingly, both imaging intensity and plasmonic phase exhibited large, nonsynchronous fluctuations (Fig. 5B, top), consistent with sustained 3D reorientation. Using the established localization framework (Supplementary Text), the trajectory-derived spatial distribution pinpoints a site 2.1 nm from the rod tip (Fig. 5B, middle; figs. S18 and S19, and movie S4). Across 28 nanorods, binding is enriched near the long-axis terminus (Fig. 5B, bottom), consistent with a mechanical-lever model, where tip anchoring maximizes attainable pitch angle and 3D twisting freedom.

In BS.IVβ, hydrophobic interactions between the protein and the interface are further weakened, placing the system near a binding threshold and producing cycles of binding, dissociation, and rebinding with a persistent twisting contribution. Here, the assignment is made from the correlated changes in displacement, instantaneous velocity, phase, and intensity, rather than from the lateral trajectory alone. Because TEMPi operates in an evanescent field, the phase-corrected intensity reflects the nanorod-interface separation, while abrupt changes in displacement and velocity indicate transient switching between confined binding and released searching states; the phase continues to report the twisting contribution. A representative trajectory exhibits hopping-sliding-hopping dynamics (Fig. 5C): At 0.56 s, the rod shifts 90 nm along x, and this event occurs with a ∼30% drop in intensity. This coupled signature indicates transient dissociation from the interface and out-of-plane hopping, rather than simple in-plane Brownian wandering. The binding-velocity probability density P (0 to 8 s) shows a bimodal distribution (Fig. 5D), peaking near ∼3.7 and ∼19.3 μm/s, consistent with confined motion versus rapid hopping. At 11.11 s, a second step exhibits sliding without a large intensity change (Fig. 5D); the velocity distribution becomes predominantly unimodal (with brief minor peak at ∼21.8 μm/s) (Fig. 5C), indicating 2D diffusion and rapid reanchoring. At 13.62 s, a further hopping event (2.2 s) involves free searching above the interface before recapture (Fig. 5D).

Together, these measurements delineate the multiscale evolution of dynamics during nanorod-protein binding and release. Across regimes, large and rapid rotational excursions accompany motion, implicating twist kinetics coupling in which torsional constraint helps gate binding-dissociation transitions by effectively modulating local energy barriers. In the present protein system, these in-plane and out-of-plane responses evolve together with changes in binding geometry, effective contact disengagement, hinge flexibility, bending of the protein linkage, and subunit reorientation within the protein-nanorod assembly, collectively shaping the observed angular freedom and transition behavior. Spatial localization and free-energy analyses reveal site enrichment near nanorod tips, corroborating a lever-amplified mechanism for enhanced pitch freedom. Using TEMPi as an intrinsic reporter yields maps of orientation, phase and site occupancy, and resolves sequentially confined, hopping, reconfined, dissociated states; transition rates and sliding characteristics emerge from bimodal velocity statistics. The systematic measurement of twisting amplitudes, rates, and phase diffusion thus provides mechanistic markers for barrier crossing and rebinding, deepening our understanding of single-molecule interfacial dynamics and establishing an emerging experimental paradigm for precision control of nanoscale behavior at interfaces.

Quantifying twisting parameters arising from conformational change

To quantify torsional parameters associated with conformational change, we operated TEMPi in a passive microrheology mode and used DNA hybridization as a model system. This provides a label-free, force-free route to extract effective torsional stiffness, relaxation time, and free-energy profiles from thermal fluctuations. We established a single-stranded DNA (ssDNA)/complementary ssDNA (csDNA) hybridization model (λ-phage DNA, 74 nt) on a gold film (Fig. 6A). Under the present buffer conditions, the ssDNA state is treated as a more flexible single-stranded conformational ensemble than double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) while potentially including transient intramolecular secondary structures. Briefly, ssDNA was immobilized, GNRs were flowed across the surface, weakly adsorbed particles were removed with buffer, and z-axis thermal fluctuations and twisting readouts were recorded in buffer. The complementary strand was then introduced to form dsDNA, followed by a rinse that retained only GNRs tethered by a single rigid duplex (Supplementary Text).

Time-domain angle traces reveal a pronounced contraction of accessible angular space upon hybridization: For ssDNA, the twisting amplitude was 2.6° to 179.8°, whereas dsDNA narrowed to 25.2° to 137.4° (Fig. 6B and movie S5). After mean subtraction, the SD decreases from ∼43.0° (ssDNA) to ∼19.8° (dsDNA), a fivefold reduction in angular variance indicative of a steeper post-hybridization energy landscape. Angle probability densities P(θt) were approximately Gaussian near equilibrium (Fig. 6C), and fitting the twisting free-energy profile U(θt) yields effective torsional spring constants of 1.970 pN·nm·rad−1 (ssDNA) and 18.372 pN·nm·rad−1 (dsDNA) (Fig. 6D), an ∼9.3-fold stiffness increases consistent with conformational rigidification and entropic compaction on forming the duplex. Dynamics accelerate in parallel with stiffening. The autocorrelation C(τ) decays more rapidly for dsDNA, with twisting relaxation times tτ ≈ 0.1349 s (ssDNA) and tτ ≈ 0.0126 s (dsDNA), an ∼10.7-fold shortening (Fig. 6E). These quantities should therefore be interpreted as effective mechanical parameters of the nanorod-DNA construct under the present sequence and buffer conditions. The nanorods used in the DNA hybridization experiments had a narrow size distribution, so the resulting size-dependent variation in rotational drag is expected to be small and to contribute only modestly to particle-to-particle differences in the extracted relaxation behavior (Supplementary Text). In the frequency domain, Lorentzian fits to the power spectral density indicate a right shift of the cutoff frequency from ∼2.7 Hz (ssDNA) to ∼15.3 Hz (dsDNA) (Fig. 6F), an ∼5.7-fold increase. These trends are mutually consistent under an overdamped twisting Langevin framework, where tτ∝ζ/kτ and fc≈(2πtτ)−1; a higher kτ with approximately constant damping ζ naturally yields a shorter tτ and a larger fc. Population statistics across n = 85 particles demonstrate robust separability of kτ, tτ, and fc between the ssDNA and dsDNA states (Fig. 6G), establishing that TEMPi, operated as a passive microrheology tool, enables label-free, force-free, quantification of torsional conformational changes associated with DNA hybridization (fig. S20). In the DNA hybridization model, this description is more directly connected to torsional constraint because the nanorod is retained by a single ssDNA or dsDNA tether, and hybridization primarily changes the rigidity of that tether. The measured angular fluctuations therefore provide a readout of the effective rotational constraint imposed by the DNA linkage, allowing effective stiffness and relaxation parameters to be extracted from thermal motion. At the same time, we do not imply strict equivalence to the periodic torsional coordinate and conjugate torque defined in torque-based techniques such as magnetic tweezers; rather, the quantities reported here should be understood as effective parameters derived from thermally driven rotational fluctuations of the nanorod-DNA construct.

DISCUSSION

In summary, this work establishes TEMPi as an interface-compatible, label-free and force-free method for visualizing torsionally constrained rotational dynamics at the single-molecule level. By mapping nanoscale twist to interferometric phase via a localized magnetic-plasmon mode in a nanorod-film cavity, TEMPi converts the rotational motion of GNRs into phase-encoded optical signals that are informative of molecular mechanics at interfaces. More generally, TEMPi directly measures nanorod rotational motion, from which effective molecular torsional constraints are inferred within the framework of probe-molecule coupling and, depending on the system, may include contributions from binding-geometry changes, linker flexibility, or subunit reorientation. Accordingly, TEMPi should be viewed as reporting the rotational free-energy landscape and effective restoring dynamics of the coupled assembly, rather than the canonical periodic torsional coordinate measured in externally torque-imposed methods. Unlike traditional methods, TEMPi operates without the need for external forces or molecular labels, offering high spatial and temporal resolution to track rotational dynamics in real time. Our findings demonstrate TEMPi’s ability to resolve distinct twisting regimes, quantify effective torsional stiffness, and track relaxation dynamics in biomolecular systems, including protein anchoring and DNA hybridization. The technique also uncovers critical insights into the effects of binding geometry, molecular conformation, and energy landscapes, opening up promising opportunities for studying complex biological and catalytic processes with improved precision and specificity.

More broadly, TEMPi provides a general framework for addressing biological and chemical questions in which nanoscale rotational fluctuations at the single-molecule level encode otherwise inaccessible information about interfacial interactions, configurational transitions, and local mechanical constraints. These capabilities are expected to be particularly valuable for interrogating multivalent receptor-ligand recognition at immunological synapse-like interfaces, weak and transient interactions at membrane protein or lipid membrane surfaces, conformational switching of nucleic acid aptamers during molecular recognition, enzyme-driven structural rearrangements at interfaces, and the spatiotemporal evolution of nanoscale mechanical heterogeneity in soft materials. It would also be interesting in future work to explore chiral anisotropic probes, which may introduce additional chirality-dependent and polarization-sensitive plasmonic responses in the TEMPi nanocavity. By enabling high-throughput, wide-field, and label-free access to torsionally constrained dynamics at the single-molecule level, TEMPi extends the experimental toolbox for probing interfacial molecular behavior. In this sense, TEMPi not only provides a versatile platform for studying dynamic molecular processes at interfaces but also opens a distinct experimental window for mechanism-resolved investigations across biophysics, catalysis, and materials science.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Detailed materials and methods are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Materials and chemicals

Cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB)–stabilized GNRs purchased from Nanoseedz were used as received. HS-PEG-COOH [molecular weight (MW) of 5 kDa], methoxy-PEG-thiol (mPEG-SH, MW ≈ 2 kDa), 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl) carbodiimide hydrochloride (EDC), N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS), bovine serum albumin (BSA), Tween 20, tris·HCl, MgCl2, NaCl, phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), ethanol, and acetone were purchased from Aladdin (Shanghai Aladdin Bio-Chem Technology Co. Ltd., China). PAA [40% (w/w) stock] and PLL (aqueous stock) were obtained from Beyotime (Shanghai Beyotime Biotechnology Co. Ltd., China) and diluted as indicated before use. 1-Octadecanethiol (C18, ≥98%) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Merck, USA). Deionized water (18.2 MΩ·cm) was used throughout. Polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS; Sylgard 184, Dow Corning) was used to fabricate the observation wells. Rabbit IgG (H + L) was obtained from Adamas life (catalog no. P9138, lot no. P3215876), while recombinant streptavidin (SA) was purchased from Cellwaylab (catalog no. C040206, lot no. 20220519). Both proteins were used without further purification. Biotin-modified ssDNA (biotin-ssDNA-SH) and complementary DNA (csDNA) were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co. Ltd. The experiments employed deionized water, which was furnished by a Milli-Q deionized water system (Milli-Q, Millipore).

ssDNA oligonucleotides

A 74-nt segment from bacteriophage λ DNA (GenBank J02459.1, bases 12,001–12,074), selected from a widely used and reliable model system for DNA hybridization studies, was chosen to design two ssDNA oligonucleotides:

1) P1 (74 nt; 5′ → 3′):

AGCATGCCGGAGCAAATGAGAAAATCAGCCAGCAGCGCCGGGATTTGTGGAAGGCGGAGAGTCAGTTCGCGGTA.

End-modifications: 5′ biotin–TEG; 3′ thiol (–SH, C3 linker).

2) P2 (74 nt; 5′ → 3′):

TACCGCGAACTGACTCTCCGCCTTCCACAAATCCCGGCGCTGCTGGCTGATTTTCTCATTTGCTCCGGCATGCT.

Oligonucleotides were synthesized and purified by standard methods (high-performance liquid chromatography grade) and used as received. P1 provides biotin for SA-mediated anchoring and a 3′ thiol handle for covalent coupling; P2 serves as the complementary strand in hybridization assays. Sequences are reported exactly as used in the experiments.

Surface modification of GNRs for protein conjugation

CTAB-stabilized GNRs (∼0.1 nM in ∼0.1 M CTAB) were mixed at room temperature with HS-PEG-COOH (molecular weight 5 kDa; final concentration of 1 mM) and gently agitated overnight (12 hours) to displace CTAB on the gold surface and introduce terminal carboxyl groups. Unreacted species were removed by centrifugation (12,000 rpm, 10 min) or dialysis, yielding carboxyl-terminated PEG-GNRs. Carboxyl groups were activated in PBS (pH 7.4) using EDC/NHS chemistry by adding EDC (final concentration of 0.2 M) and NHS (final concentration of 0.05 M) and incubating for 30 min. The activated suspension was then supplemented with the target protein solution (IgG or SA, 1 mg ml−1; 50 μl), mixed thoroughly, and incubated for 3 hours at room temperature to form covalent amide bonds between protein amines and surface NHS esters. Last, samples were washed several times with PBS to remove nonbound protein, affording protein-conjugated GNRs (IgG-GNRs or SA-GNRs). All steps were performed under light-protected conditions to minimize protein denaturation and photothermal effects on the nanorods.

Experimental setup and image processing for single particle optical imaging

Measurements were performed on an inverted microscope (Nikon Ti-E, Japan) equipped with a Perfect Focus System (MEP59390) and a 60× oil-immersion objective (N.A. = 1.49) for excitation of surface plasmons and collection of reflected and scattered light. Glass coverslips (22 mm by 22 mm) were coated with 47-nm gold by electron-beam evaporation with a 3-nm chromium adhesion layer. Illumination from a 670-nm superluminescent diode was converted to p-polarized light using a polarizer and focused at the back focal plane of the objective. This p-polarized illumination was maintained throughout the measurements because efficient excitation of the SPP field is required for the TEMPi interferometric readout. Surface plasmon resonance at the gold-solution interface was tuned by offsetting the focal spot from the optical axis. The incident angle was set to the resonance mode (θ ≈ 70.5°). Interference between reflected and scattered fields was recorded on a CCD camera (Pike-032B, Allied Vision Technologies, Germany). Experiments were conducted in a transparent PDMS well bonded to the substrate. All imaging measurements were performed at room temperature (25° ± 1°C) under the same experimental conditions.

Image sequences were recorded with a CCD camera at ∼100 frames s−1. Raw plasmonic images were converted into 16-bit tiff format files with the MATLAB program. After long-duration acquisition, the data were processed with home-built MATLAB routines: A suitable blank-field reference frame was first selected and used for frame-wise background subtraction to obtain a clean, time-resolved series of interferometric images. Subsequent analysis used frequency-domain filtering and Gaussian localization (2D Gaussian fitting) to extract further quantitative information.

Surface functionalization of ssDNA and hybridization assay

Gold-film chips were cleaned by sonication in ethanol for 5 min, rinsed with deionized water, and immediately stored in deionized water until use. For mixed monolayer formation, a thiolated PEG/thiolated ssDNA solution was prepared at a molar ratio of ∼104:1 [example: 1 mM mPEG-SH (MW ≈ 2 kDa), 0.1 μM biotin–ssDNA-SH, 10 mM PBS with 150 mM NaCl, pH 7.4], freshly prepared and gently vortexed for 1 min in the dark. An aliquot of 100 μL was introduced into the flow cell and incubated at room temperature for 2 hours, or overnight at 4°C, to achieve sparse landing of PEG and ssDNA. The surface was then backfilled with 1 mM mPEG-SH at 37°C for 30 min to passivate exposed gold and enhance antifouling performance. The chip was subsequently blocked with PBS (200 μL, 30 min) containing 0.1% BSA and 0.01% Tween 20 (surface blocking and suppression of weak nonspecific adsorption), followed by a rinse with 3 mL PBS-0.01% Tween 20 to minimize nonspecific adsorption.

For specific particle binding, a SA-GNR working solution (fig. S21) was prepared in PBS with 0.005% Tween 20 and 0.05 mg mL−1 BSA (final particle concentration 0.2 nM). Prior to use, particles were pelleted at 8000 g for 10 min and washed twice to remove storage buffer. Then 100 μL of the SA-GNR working solution was added to the chip and incubated for 30 min to allow specific binding to sparse biotin-DNA sites. Unbound particles were removed by rinsing with PBS-0.01% Tween-20, and the chip was transferred to the imaging system for acquisition of time-series dynamics of GNRs, including nanorod twisting dynamics. For extended observations, 0.002% Tween 20 was included in the imaging buffer to suppress gradual nonspecific adsorption during long-term imaging while minimizing perturbation to nanorod dynamics and interfacial molecular behavior.

For hybridization experiments, the chamber buffer was first exchanged with freshly prepared hybridization buffer [10 mM MgCl2, 100 mM NaCl, 10 mM tris·HCl (pH 7.4)] to maintain ionic strength and pH stability. The csDNA (100 nM; typical volume, 50 to 150 μl) was then introduced and incubated at room temperature for 1 hour under the defined hybridization conditions. After hybridization, the chamber was rinsed with three to five chamber volumes of the same hybridization buffer to remove unbound or nonspecifically adsorbed DNA, followed by a brief rinse with a low-salt buffer [10 mM tris·HCl (pH 7.4) and 0.01% Tween 20] to reduce background from weakly paired duplexes. Last, the chip was immediately transferred into imaging buffer for collection of nanorod twisting dynamics.

C18 functionalization of gold film substrates

Gold-film chips were sequentially sonicated in acetone, ethanol and deionized water for 5 min each to remove organic residues and particulates. As an optional step, the gold surface was treated by oxygen plasma for 5 min to further improve cleanliness. Chips were dried under nitrogen and set aside. 1-Octadecanethiol (C18) was dissolved in anhydrous ethanol to 5 mM. Cleaned substrates were fully immersed in the C18 solution in a clean glass vessel and incubated overnight (12 to 18 hours) at room temperature in the dark to form a stable self-assembled monolayer. After modification, substrates were removed, rinsed thoroughly with anhydrous ethanol to eliminate unbound thiols, dried under nitrogen, and then stored in deionized water until use.

Constructing a loosely crosslinked PAA/PLL net structure of the polymer layer

Gold-coated substrates were briefly rinsed with deionized water and ethanol and kept wet. Commercial PLL stock was diluted 10× in deionized water and applied to fully cover the surface, incubated at room temperature for 5 min and then removed by multiple short rinses with deionized water to eliminate unbound polymer. A 40% (w/w) PAA stock was diluted 1000× in deionized water [≈0.04% (w/w)], dispensed to cover the substrate, and incubated at room temperature for 1 to 2 hours, followed by thorough water rinsing to exchange the solution in chambers and wells while avoiding surface drying. Positively charged, ligand-modified GNRs were then introduced; 1× PBS was included to raise ionic strength and promote rapid electrostatic adsorption onto the loosely crosslinked, polyanionic PAA layer. After a brief quiescent period to allow binding, the surface was gently rinsed to remove unbound particles and imaged under liquid on the microscope stage.
