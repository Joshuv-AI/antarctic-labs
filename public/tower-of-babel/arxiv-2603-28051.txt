Abstract
. This paper investigates domain hemivariational inequality problems arising from the non-stationary two- and three-dimensional convective Brinkman-Forchheimer extended Darcy (CBFeD) equations, which describe the flow of viscous incompressible fluids through saturated porous media in bounded domains. These equations may be regarded as generalized Navier-Stokes systems incorporating both damping and pumping mechanisms. For all admissible absorption exponents r ≥ 1 r\geq 1 and effective viscosity μ > 0 \mu>0 , the existence of weak solutions to the non-stationary 2D and 3D CBFeD equations with hemivariational inequalities is established via a regularized Galerkin approximation scheme, based on a suitable regularization of the Clarke subdifferential. A noteworthy aspect of the analysis is that the existence results extend to the three-dimensional non-stationary Navier-Stokes equations. Moreover, under appropriate conditions on the absorption exponent, specifically, r ≥ 1 r\geq 1 in two dimensions and r ≥ 3 r\geq 3 in three dimensions, it is shown that weak solutions satisfy the energy equality. In addition, uniqueness of solutions is proved for r ≥ 1 r\geq 1 in 2D and r ≥ 3 r\geq 3 in 3D, with the additional requirement 2 ​ β ​ μ > 1 2\beta\mu>1 in the critical case r = 3 r=3 .



1.
Introduction

1.1.
The model

In this manuscript, we study the 2D and 3D CBFeD equations subject to a domain hemivariational inequality. We consider the following initial-boundary value problem for the 2D and 3D CBFeD system on bounded domain
\mathfrak{D}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
for
d\in\{2,3\}
, which describes the steady flow of an incompressible viscous fluid through a porous medium (see
[
32
]
):

(1.1)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial\boldsymbol{y}}{\partial t}-\mu\Delta\boldsymbol{y}+(\boldsymbol{y}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{y}+\alpha|\boldsymbol{y}|^{q-1}\boldsymbol{y}+\beta|\boldsymbol{y}|^{r-1}\boldsymbol{y}+\nabla\pi&=\boldsymbol{f}+\boldsymbol{g},\ \text{ in }\ \mathfrak{D}\times(0,T),\\
\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{y}&=0,\ \text{ in }\ \mathfrak{D}\times[0,T),\\
\boldsymbol{y}&=\mathbf{0}\ \text{ on }\ \partial\mathfrak{D}\times[0,T],\\
\boldsymbol{y}(0)&=\boldsymbol{y}^{0}\ \text{ in }\ \mathfrak{D},\end{aligned}}}\right.

where
\boldsymbol{y}(\cdot,\cdot):\mathfrak{D}\times[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
denotes the flow velocity field,
\pi(\cdot,\cdot):\mathfrak{D}\times[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
is the pressure and
\boldsymbol{f}(\cdot,\cdot):\mathfrak{D}\times[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is given forcing term. In addition, we are assuming
that the forcing term
\boldsymbol{g}(\cdot,\cdot):\mathfrak{D}\times[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfies

(1.2)

\displaystyle-\boldsymbol{g}(x,t)\in\partial_{\mathpzc{C}}j(x,t,\boldsymbol{y}(x,t))\ \text{ in }\ \mathfrak{D}\times(0,T),

where
\partial_{\mathpzc{C}}j
is the subdifferential of the superpotential

j:\mathfrak{D}\times(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
which is defined in the sense of Clarke (see Definition
2.5
).
In the above model (
1.1
), the constants
\mu,\beta>0
denotes the
Brinkman coefficient
(effective viscosity) and
Forchheimer coefficient
(proportional to the porosity of the material), respectively. In addition, the term
\beta|\boldsymbol{y}|^{r-1}\boldsymbol{y}
is known as the absorption or damping term with absorption exponent
r\geq 1
(cf.
[
1
]
). In particular,
r=3
is known as the
critical exponent
. The cases
r<3
and
r>3
are known as
subcritical
and
supercritical
, respectively.

As mentioned in
[
32
]
, the additional nonlinear term
\alpha|\boldsymbol{y}|^{q-1}\boldsymbol{y}
introduced by the authors in the system (
1.1
) which represents a potential pumping mechanism that counteracts the damping effect when
\alpha<0
, as assumed throughout this work. The parameter
q\in[1,r)
governs the strength of the pumping effect. For
\alpha=\beta=0
, the system (
1.1
) reduces to the classical Navier-Stokes equations (abbreviated as NSE), and if
\alpha,\beta>0
, then it can be considered as damped NSE. The above model (
1.1
) is accurate when the flow velocity is too large for Darcy’s law to be valid as long as the porosity is not too small (see
[
32
]
). For physical significance of the model, the interested readers are referred to see
[
15
,
32
]
.

1.2.
Literature survey

In the literature, convective Brinkman-Forchheimer equations (abbreviated as CBF) are also known as tamed NSE or NSE modified with an absorption term, cf.
[
1
,
38
]
etc., and references therein. The damping
\beta|\boldsymbol{y}|^{r-1}\boldsymbol{y}
arises from the resistance to the motion of the flow, which describes several physical phenomena such as drag or friction effects, porous media flow, some dissipative mechanisms, cf.
[
6
,
17
,
32
,
45
]
etc., and references therein. The global solvability results for the CBF model involving fast growing nonlinearities in both two- and three-dimensions can be found in
[
1
,
14
,
17
,
24
,
32
]
.

We are naturally led to a mathematical model that involves the Clarke subdifferential of a locally Lipschitz superpotential. This formulation, known as a hemivariational inequality, was first introduced and studied in the early 1980s by Panagiotopoulos
[
35
,
36
]
. Hemivariational inequalities serve as natural extensions of variational inequality problems and originate from non-convex and non-smooth mechanics and one can see
[
19
]
for more information regarding variational-hemivariational inequalities. In
[
21
]
, the authors analyzed a time-dependent mixed hemivariational inequality arising from incompressible fluid flow governed by the Stokes system. The model incorporates a nonsmooth friction-type boundary condition formulated using the Clarke subdifferential. The authors in
[
10
,
25
]
studied the existence of solutions of the hemivariational inequalities for the NSE, through a unified framework of an abstract problem and explores the
uniqueness, and continuous dependence on the data. In addition, they applied the results to the nonstationary
hemivariational inequalities for the NSE that are of the boundary type, corresponding to nonlinear slip boundary conditions, and of the domain type, corresponding to hydraulic flow controls. As compared to boundary type inequalities, a domain hemivariational inequality is a mathematical problem in which the non-smooth and non-convex superpotential influences the system throughout the interior of the spatial region

\mathfrak{D}
, instead of being restricted only to the boundary.
The authors in
[
10
,
25
]
studied the existence of solution through constructing a temporally semi-discrete approximation whose solutions converge to a solution of the Navier-Stokes hemivariational inequalities (NSHVIs). This approach has been successfully applied, for instance, in the analysis of the time-dependent hemivariational inequalities with CBF equations that are of the boundary type, where the authors of
[
23
]
proved both the existence and uniqueness of weak solutions.

1.3.
Comparison with the existing literature

Most studies on hemivariational inequalities (HVIs) establish the existence of solutions either through fixed-point techniques or by employing Rothe’s method. An alternative and widely used approach is the Galerkin method combined with a suitable regularization procedure, as applied in
[
30
]
to study boundary type HVIs arising in the NSE. In the Galerkin discretization scheme, the infinite-dimensional operator equation is replaced with a finite-dimensional one, whose well-posedness can be shown via classical existence and uniqueness results or fixed-point arguments. After obtaining uniform a-priori estimates for the approximate solutions, the Banach-Alaoglu theorem is used to extract a weakly convergent subsequence. By applying some compactness results like Aubin-Lions, one can extract a further subsequence which converges strongly, so that the weak limit serves as a solution to the original problem (cf.
[
42
]
). For further details on the application of the Galerkin method to parabolic and elliptic problems in the framework of variational inequalities, we refer the reader to
[
41
]
. The authors in
[
16
, Section 7.3, Chapter 7]
examined the existence of weak solutions for domain-type hemivariational inequalities associated with parabolic systems in bounded domains. Furthermore, Migórski-Ochal in
[
29
]
obtained an existence result for weak solutions of systems governed by a parabolic hemivariational inequality of boundary type via Galerkin method combined with a regularization procedure (see also
[
30
]
).

In 2016, Migórski et al.
[
10
]
studied a new class of hemivariational inequalities for time-dependent NSE, addressing both domain and boundary HVIs by employing Rothe’s method for semi-discretization in time, and surjectivity result for multivalued pseudomonotone operators. Furthermore, using this methodology, Jindal et al.
[
23
]
analyzed the well-posedness of boundary hemivariational inequalities for non-stationary two and three-dimensional CBF equations. Recently, the authors in
[
20
]
investigated the well-posedness of the Stokes hemivariational inequality for incompressible fluid flows with damping in three dimensions, under the restriction
1\leq r\leq 5
, by reformulating the problem in a minimization framework. Moreover, using similar ideas to
[
20
]
, the author of
[
34
]
established the well-posedness of stationary two- and three-dimensional CBFeD hemivariational inequalities. Also, the authors in
[
43
]
investigated the well-posedness of the hemivariational inequality associated with the stationary NSE including a nonlinear damping term via finite element method. Further, the authors in
[
2
]
studied the hemivariational inequality of the stationary CBFeD model via mixed finite element method and also discussed optimal control of stationary CBFeD with hemivariational inequalities in
[
3
]
.

Despite these contributions, the theoretical analysis of CBF equations coupled with domain hemivariational inequalities for viscous incompressible flows remains largely undeveloped. To address this gap, the present work introduces and investigates the existence and uniqueness of weak solutions for NSHVIs with damping and pumping effects, thereby providing new insights into the mathematical theory of such fluid flow models. To the best of our knowledge, domain-type hemivariational inequalities in this context have not been investigated so far. Motivated by this observation, our work extends the existing theory by employing the Galerkin method to analyze time-dependent hemivariational inequalities for the CBFeD equations (
1.1
), which are of domain type.

1.4.
Difficulties, approaches and novelties

•

The authors in
[
10
]
established the existence of a weak solution by using Rothe’s method for the 2D NSE with domain-type hemivariational inequalities under the additional condition

is the

\mathfrak{D}

. In contrast, the approach adopted in the present work avoids imposing such a restriction on the viscosity parameter
\mu
. We establish the existence of a weak solution by employing analytical techniques similar to those developed in

,
29
]
without getting any restriction on
\mu
. The main difficulty in our analysis lies in handling the term

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{\mathfrak{D}}\theta_{in}(s,\boldsymbol{y}_{n,i}(s))\boldsymbol{y}_{n,i}(s)\/\mathrm{d}\/x\/\mathrm{d}\/s

appearing in the inequality (

). In the earlier works such as
[
10
]
, the a-priori estimates are obtained by using the bound (

), which leads to the additional restriction on the viscosity coefficient

. In the present work, however, we overcome this difficulty by applying Lemma

to derive the necessary a-priori estimates, as demonstrated in

of Theorem

.

•

In
[
1
]
, the authors studied the NSE modified by the absorption term
|\boldsymbol{y}|^{r-2}\boldsymbol{y}
, for
r>2
in bounded domains with compact boundary. They proved the existence of Leray-Hopf weak solutions for dimension

and its uniqueness for

. However, in three dimension, they were unable to establish the energy equality satisfied by the weak solutions.

Recently, the authors
in
[
14
]
established the existence and uniqueness of global weak solutions (without non-linear damping) satisfying the energy equality to the system (

) for all

, when

and for

, when

. For more information, one can see

) for

in 3D without any additional assumption

, when

.

•

Leray and Hopf
[
28
]
established the existence of at least one weak solution for the three-dimensional NSE. However, the global existence and uniqueness of classical solutions to the 3D NSE (see
[
37
]
) remains one of the most challenging open problems for the mathematical community. Consequently, several modified versions of the 3D NSE have been proposed and studied (cf.
[
1
,
45
]
). In
[
32
]
, the authors considered the classical 3D NSE with nonlinear damping

and pumping

(without linear damping) and established the existence of weak solutions for

and uniqueness for

.

In
[
10
]
, the authors established the uniqueness of weak solutions under the additional restriction
\mu>m\|l\|^{2}
, where

is the constant appearing in the monotonicity condition (see
[
10
, Lemma 6.2]
). The novelty of the present work lies in establishing the uniqueness of weak solutions to problem (
1.1
) together with condition (
3.63
), for

in the two-dimensional case and for

in the three-dimensional case. In particular, when

, the uniqueness result is obtained under the additional assumption

. Unlike
[

]
, our analysis does not impose any restriction on the viscosity coefficient

1.5.
Organization of the paper

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In the next section, we present the essential mathematical preliminaries and set up the functional framework. Since the framework used here for the CBF equations differs from that employed for NSE (as detailed in
[
42
]
), we provide an in-depth discussion of the function spaces involved. We then proceed with a thorough analysis of the linear, bilinear, and nonlinear operators that appear in our formulation and dedicated to exploring key properties of these operators.

Section
3
focuses on proving the existence and uniqueness of a weak solution to the domain hemivariational inequality associated with 2D and 3D CBFeD equations. Using Galerkin discretization scheme, we first establish the existence of a solution to an abstract hemivariational inequality, as shown in Theorem

. Then, we prove the energy equality stated in Proposition

for
r\geq 1
in 2D and
r\geq 3
in 3D and the corresponding uniqueness results are presented in Theorem

2.5.
Clarke subdifferential

We address Problem
3.1
by employing a Galerkin approximation scheme applied to a suitably regularized version of the problem. The main analytical challenge in the variational formulation stems from the presence of the term involving the Clarke subdifferential, which is generally non-smooth and multivalued. To handle this difficulty and to make the Galerkin method applicable, we first introduce a regularization of the Clarke subdifferential. This regularization yields a family of well-posed finite-dimensional problems, enables the derivation of uniform energy estimates, and facilitates the passage to the limit in the approximation process.

3.1.
Regularization of Clarke subdifferential

For
i=1,\ldots,d
, let

\theta_{i}:\mathfrak{D}\times(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R},\ (x,t,\xi)\mapsto\theta_{i}(x,t,\xi),

be a function which is possibly non-monotone and non-smooth with respect to the variable
\xi
. The following hypothesis on
\theta_{i}
is adopted from the framework developed in
[
29
]
and
[
16
, Section 7.3, Chapter 7, pp. 108]
, with minor modifications:

Hypothesis 3.4
.

We assume that the function
\theta_{i}
satisfies the following hypotheses:

(i)
Local boundedness:
For every
r>0,
there exists a constant
c=c(r)>0
such that

\displaystyle|\theta_{i}(x,t,\xi)|\leq c(r),\ \text{ for all }\ (x,t)\in\mathfrak{D}\times(0,T)\text{ and a.e. }|\xi|\leq r;

(ii)
Continuity in
(x,t)
:
For a.e.
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
, the mapping

(x,t)\mapsto\theta_{i}(x,t,\xi)

is continuous on
\mathfrak{D}\times(0,T);

(iii)
Growth condition:

\theta_{i}\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R})
is such that the left and right limits
\theta_{i}(x,t,\xi\pm 0)
exist for every
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
and it verifies the growth condition

(3.6)

\displaystyle|\theta_{i}(x,t,\xi)|\leq\bar{\alpha}(x,t)+C_{1,i}+C_{2,i}|\xi|,\ \text{ for all }\ (x,t,\xi)\in\mathfrak{D}\times(0,T)\times\mathbb{R},

with a non-negative function
\bar{\alpha}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathfrak{D}\times(0,T))\text{ and positive constants }C_{1,i},C_{2,i};

(iv)
Ultimate monotonicity in
\xi
:
For each
i=1,\ldots,d
, the graph
(\xi,\theta_{i}(x,t,\xi))
is ultimate increasingly with respect to
\xi
, that is, there exists
\phi_{i}\geq 0
such that

(3.7)

\displaystyle\esssup\limits_{\xi\in(-\infty,-\phi_{i})}\theta_{i}(x,t,\xi)\leq 0\leq\essinf\limits_{\xi\in(\phi_{i},\infty)}\theta_{i}(x,t,\xi).

Since
\theta_{i}(x,t,\cdot):\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
may be discontinuous with respect to
\xi
, we introduce its regularization by ‘filling in the jumps’. For
\varepsilon>0
,
(x,t)\in\mathfrak{D}\times(0,T)
and
\xi\in\mathbb{R},
we define the lower and upper envelopes

\displaystyle\underline{\theta_{i}}^{\varepsilon}(x,t,\xi):=\essinf\limits_{|\tau-\xi|\leq\varepsilon}\theta_{i}(x,t,\tau),

\displaystyle\overline{\theta_{i}}^{\varepsilon}(x,t,\xi):=\esssup\limits_{|\tau-\xi|\leq\varepsilon}\theta_{i}(x,t,\tau).

For
(x,t)\in\mathfrak{D}\times(0,T)
and
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
fixed, the functions
\underline{\theta_{i}}^{\varepsilon}
and
\overline{\theta_{i}}^{\varepsilon}
are monotone with respect to
\varepsilon
, being increasing and decreasing, respectively. Consequently, the following limits are well-defined:

\displaystyle\underline{\theta_{i}}(x,t,\xi):=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\underline{\theta_{i}}^{\varepsilon}(x,t,\xi),

\displaystyle\overline{\theta_{i}}(x,t,\xi):=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\overline{\theta_{i}}^{\varepsilon}(x,t,\xi).

For
\varepsilon>0,
and
t\in\mathbb{R},
we define the multivalued map
\widehat{\theta_{i}}\colon\mathfrak{D}\times(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}\multimap\mathbb{R}
by

(3.8)

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta_{i}}(x,t,\xi)=[\underline{\theta_{i}}(x,t,\xi),\overline{\theta_{i}}(x,t,\xi)],

that is,

is the closed interval with end points given by
\underline{\theta_{i}}(x,t,\xi)
and

\overline{\theta_{i}}(x,t,\xi)
, respectively.

From
[
5

exist for every

, then one can determine the Clarke subdifferential for a locally Lipschitz function

such that

\displaystyle\partial_{C}j_{i}(x,t,\xi)=\widehat{\theta_{i}}(x,t,\xi)\ \text{ for }\ \xi\in\mathbb{R},

and

is obtained from

by the following relation

\displaystyle j_{i}(x,t,\xi)=\int_{0}^{\xi}\theta_{i}(x,t,s)\/\mathrm{d}\/s.

Proposition 3.8
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
3.7
, the weak solution
\boldsymbol{y}
of the Problem
3.1
satisfies the energy equality for
r\geq 1
in dimension two and
r\geq 3
in dimension three.

Proof.

To prove the energy equality, we follow the approximation technique given in
[
12
]
. In
[
12
]
, the authors constructed approximations of
\boldsymbol{y}(\cdot)
in bounded domains such that the approximating sequence remains bounded and converges simultaneously in both Sobolev and Lebesgue spaces. Following this approach, we approximate
\boldsymbol{y}(t)
for each
t\in[0,T]
by employing the finite-dimensional space spanned by the first
n
eigen-functions of the Stokes operator (see
[
12
, Theorem 4.3]
), which is written as following:

(3.38)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{y}_{n}(t):=\mathcal{P}_{1/n}\boldsymbol{y}(t)=\sum_{\lambda_{j}<n^{2}}e^{-\lambda_{j}/n}\langle\boldsymbol{y}(t),\boldsymbol{w}_{j}\rangle\boldsymbol{w}_{j}.

We point out that
\mathcal{P}_{1/n}
is a self-adjoint operator but not a projection. Now, firstly note that

(3.39)

\|\boldsymbol{y}_{n}\|_{\mathscr{H}}^{2}=\|\mathcal{P}_{1/n}\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\mathscr{H}}^{2}=\sum_{\lambda_{j}<n^{2}}e^{-2\lambda_{j}/n}|\langle\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{w}_{j}\rangle|^{2}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}|\langle\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{w}_{j}\rangle|^{2}=\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\mathscr{H}}^{2}<+\infty,

for all
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathscr{H}
. Moreover, it follows that

\displaystyle\|(\mathrm{I}_{d}-\mathcal{P}_{1/n})\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\mathscr{H}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\mathscr{H}}^{2}-2\langle\boldsymbol{y},\mathcal{P}_{1/n}\boldsymbol{y}\rangle+\|\mathcal{P}_{1/n}\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\mathscr{H}}^{2}

(3.40)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda_{j}<n^{2}}\big(1-e^{-\lambda_{j}/n}\big)^{2}|\langle\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{w}_{j}\rangle|^{2}+\sum_{\lambda_{j}\geq n^{2}}|\langle\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{w}_{j}\rangle|^{2},

for all
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathscr{H}
. Since the series
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}|\langle\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{w}_{j}\rangle|^{2}
is convergent, it implies that the final term on the right-hand side of the equality (
3.1
), that is

(3.41)

\displaystyle\sum_{\lambda_{j}\geq n^{2}}|\langle\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{w}_{j}\rangle|^{2}\to 0\text{ as }n\to\infty.

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\big(1-e^{-\lambda_{j}/n}\big)^{2}|\langle\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{w}_{j}\rangle|^{2}\leq 4\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}|\langle\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{w}_{j}\rangle|^{2}=4\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\mathscr{H}}^{2}<+\infty.

(3.42)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\big(1-e^{-\lambda_{j}/n}\big)^{2}|\langle\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{w}_{j}\rangle|^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\lim_{n\to\infty}\big(1-e^{-\lambda_{j}/n}\big)^{2}|\langle\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{w}_{j}\rangle|^{2}=0.

3.41
) and (
3.42
) in (

\displaystyle\|(\mathrm{I}_{d}-\mathcal{P}_{1/n})\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\mathscr{H}}\to 0\text{ as }n\to\infty.

&\text{ for a.e. }t\in[0,T].\end{aligned}}}\right.

.

Note that for
r\in[1,3)
in
d=3
, the inequality (
3.1
) no longer holds. Consequently, it follows from (
3.52
) that
\mathscr{F}^{1}(\boldsymbol{y})\notin\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathscr{V}^{\prime})
and therefore the relation (
3.1
) fails. As a result, the energy equality cannot be justified for
r\in[1,3)
in three dimensions.

We now turn to the question of the uniqueness of solutions. Unlike existing results for evolution boundary type hemivariational NSE problems (for instance see
[
30
]
), which only address existence, the presence of an absorption term
\beta|\boldsymbol{y}|^{r-1}\boldsymbol{y}
in our model (
1.1
) enables us to establish uniqueness. In particular, for
r>3,
the additional dissipative effect of the absorption term plays a crucial role in controlling the nonlinearities and leads to the uniqueness of weak solutions.

Theorem 3.10
.

Let
\theta_{i}:\mathfrak{D}\times(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfy the Hypothesis
3.4
and assume that the following condition holds:

(3.63)

\displaystyle\essinf\limits_{\xi_{1}\neq\xi_{2}}\frac{\theta_{i}(x,t,\xi_{1})-\theta_{i}(x,t,\xi_{2})}{\xi_{1}-\xi_{2}}\geq-\mathcal{K}\ \text{ for all }\ (x,t)\in\mathfrak{D}\times(0,T)

with
\mathcal{K}>0
. Then, for
r\geq 1
in dimension
d=2
, for
r>3
in dimension
d=3
, and in the case
d=r=3
provided that
2\beta\mu>1
, the Problem
3.1
admits a unique solution.

Proof.

Let
(\boldsymbol{y}^{1},\boldsymbol{\chi}^{1})
and
(\boldsymbol{y}^{2},\boldsymbol{\chi}^{2}),
where
\boldsymbol{\chi}^{k}=(\chi^{k}_{1},\ldots,\chi^{k}_{d})
, for
k=1,2
, be two weak solutions of (
3.33
), with
\boldsymbol{y}^{1},\boldsymbol{y}^{2}\in\mathcal{W}
and

\boldsymbol{\chi}^{k}\in\partial_{\mathpzc{C}}j(\cdot,\boldsymbol{y}^{k})
for
k=1,2
satisfying (
3.3
), corresponding to the external forcing
\boldsymbol{f}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathscr{V}^{\prime})
and the initial data
\boldsymbol{y}^{0}\in\mathscr{H}
, respectively. Then,
(\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{\chi})=(\boldsymbol{y}^{1}-\boldsymbol{y}^{2},\boldsymbol{\chi}^{1}-\boldsymbol{\chi}^{2})
satisfies the following:

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{\/\mathrm{d}\/}{\/\mathrm{d}\/t}\langle\boldsymbol{w},\mathfrak{v}\rangle+\langle\mu\mathscr{A}\boldsymbol{w},\mathfrak{v}\rangle+\langle\mathscr{B}(\boldsymbol{y}^{1})-\mathscr{B}(\boldsymbol{y}^{2}),\mathfrak{v}\rangle+\alpha\langle\widetilde{\mathscr{C}}(\boldsymbol{y}^{1})-\widetilde{\mathscr{C}}(\boldsymbol{y}^{2}),\mathfrak{v}\rangle\\
&\quad+\beta\langle\mathscr{C}(\boldsymbol{y}^{1})-\mathscr{C}(\boldsymbol{y}^{2}),\mathfrak{v}\rangle+(\boldsymbol{\chi},\mathfrak{v})=0,\ \text{ for a.e. }\ t\in[0,T],\end{aligned}}}\right.

for all
\mathfrak{v}\in\mathscr{V}\cap\mathbb{L}^{r+1}
. Then, from Proposition
3.8
,
\boldsymbol{w}
satisfies the following energy equality:

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{w}(t)\|_{\mathscr{H}}^{2}+2\mu\int_{0}^{t}\|\boldsymbol{w}(s)\|_{\mathscr{V}}^{2}\/\mathrm{d}\/s+2\beta\int_{0}^{t}\langle\mathscr{C}(\boldsymbol{y}^{1}(s))-\mathscr{C}(\boldsymbol{y}^{2}(s)),\boldsymbol{w}(s)\rangle\/\mathrm{d}\/s

\displaystyle=\|\boldsymbol{w}(0)\|_{\mathscr{H}}^{2}-2\int_{0}^{t}\langle\mathscr{B}(\boldsymbol{y}^{1}(s))-\mathscr{B}(\boldsymbol{y}^{2}(s)),\boldsymbol{w}(s)\rangle\/\mathrm{d}\/s-2\alpha\int_{0}^{t}\langle\widetilde{\mathscr{C}}(\boldsymbol{y}^{1}(s))-\widetilde{\mathscr{C}}(\boldsymbol{y}^{2}(s)),\boldsymbol{w}(s)\rangle\/\mathrm{d}\/s

(3.64)

\displaystyle\quad-2\int_{0}^{t}({\boldsymbol{\chi}}(s),\boldsymbol{w}(s))\/\mathrm{d}\/s,

for all
t\in[0,T]
. Now, from the assumption (
3.63
), we get

(3.65)

\displaystyle\inf_{\xi_{1}>\xi_{2}}\frac{\underline{\theta}(x,t,\xi_{1})-\overline{\theta}(x,t,\xi_{2})}{\xi_{1}-\xi_{2}}\geq-\mathcal{K}\quad\text{for all }(x,t)\in\mathfrak{D}\times(0,T).

Let
\Omega_{1}(s)=\{x\in\mathfrak{D}:\boldsymbol{y}^{1}(x,s)>\boldsymbol{y}^{2}(x,s)\}

and
\Omega_{2}(s)=\{x\in\mathfrak{D}:\boldsymbol{y}^{2}(x,s)>\boldsymbol{y}^{1}(x,s)\}
for all
s\in[0,T]
.
Using (
3.9
), the definition of
\widehat{\theta}
in (
3.8
) and (
3.65
), we have

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{\chi}^{1}(s)-\boldsymbol{\chi}^{2}(s),\boldsymbol{y}^{1}(s)-\boldsymbol{y}^{2}(s))

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega_{1}(s)}(\boldsymbol{\chi}^{1}(x,s)-\boldsymbol{\chi}^{2}(x,s))\cdot(\boldsymbol{y}^{1}(x,s)-\boldsymbol{y}^{2}(x,s))\/\mathrm{d}\/x

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\Omega_{2}(s)}(\boldsymbol{\chi}^{1}(x,s)-\boldsymbol{\chi}^{2}(x,s))\cdot(\boldsymbol{y}^{1}(x,s)-\boldsymbol{y}^{2}(x,s))\/\mathrm{d}\/x

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\Omega_{1}(s)}\Big[\underline{\theta}(x,s,\boldsymbol{y}^{1}(x,s))-\overline{\theta}(x,s,\boldsymbol{y}^{2}(x,s))\Big]\cdot(\boldsymbol{y}^{1}(x,s)-\boldsymbol{y}^{2}(x,s))\/\mathrm{d}\/x

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\Omega_{2}(s)}\Big[\underline{\theta}(x,s,\boldsymbol{y}^{2}(x,s))-\overline{\theta}(x,s,\boldsymbol{y}^{1}(x,s))\Big]\cdot(\boldsymbol{y}^{2}(x,s)-\boldsymbol{y}^{1}(x,s))\/\mathrm{d}\/x

\displaystyle\geq-\mathcal{K}\int_{\Omega_{1}(s)}|\boldsymbol{y}^{1}(x,s)-\boldsymbol{y}^{2}(x,s)|^{2}\/\mathrm{d}\/x-\mathcal{K}\int_{\Omega_{2}(s)}|\boldsymbol{y}^{2}(x,s)-\boldsymbol{y}^{1}(x,s)|^{2}\/\mathrm{d}\/x

(3.66)

\displaystyle=-\mathcal{K}\|\boldsymbol{y}^{1}(s)-\boldsymbol{y}^{2}(s)\|_{\mathscr{H}}^{2}.

Now, in view of Lemma
2.3
, and by an application of Taylor’s formula and Hölder’s inequality, we have the following estimates:

(3.67)

\displaystyle\langle\mathscr{C}(\boldsymbol{y}^{1})-\mathscr{C}(\boldsymbol{y}^{2}),\boldsymbol{w}\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\beta}{2}\||\boldsymbol{y}^{1}|^{\frac{r-1}{2}}\boldsymbol{w}\|^{2}_{\mathscr{H}}+\frac{\beta}{2}\||\boldsymbol{y}^{2}|^{\frac{r-1}{2}}\boldsymbol{w}\|^{2}_{\mathscr{H}},

(3.68)

\displaystyle|\alpha||\langle\widetilde{\mathscr{C}}(\boldsymbol{y}^{1})-\widetilde{\mathscr{C}}(\boldsymbol{y}^{2}),\boldsymbol{w}\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\beta}{4}\||\boldsymbol{y}^{1}|^{\frac{r-1}{2}}\boldsymbol{w}\|^{2}_{\mathscr{H}}+\frac{\beta}{4}\||\boldsymbol{y}^{2}|^{\frac{r-1}{2}}\boldsymbol{w}\|^{2}_{\mathscr{H}}+(\varrho_{1}+\varrho_{2})\|\boldsymbol{w}\|^{2}_{\mathscr{H}},

where
\varrho_{1}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{r-q}{r-1}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{2^{q+1}q|\alpha|(q-1)}{\beta(r-1)}}}\right)^{\frac{q-1}{r-q}}\text{ and }\varrho_{2}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{r-q}{r-1}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{2^{q+1}q|\alpha|(q-1)}{\beta(r-1)}}}\right)^{\frac{q-1}{r-q}}
.
On substituting (
3.1
)-(
3.68
) into (
3.1
), we obtain

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{w}(t)\|_{\mathscr{H}}^{2}+2\mu\int_{0}^{t}\|\boldsymbol{w}(s)\|_{\mathscr{V}}^{2}\/\mathrm{d}\/s+\frac{\beta}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\||\boldsymbol{y}^{1}(s)|^{\frac{r-1}{2}}\boldsymbol{w}(s)\|^{2}_{\mathscr{H}}\/\mathrm{d}\/s+\frac{\beta}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\||\boldsymbol{y}^{2}(s)|^{\frac{r-1}{2}}\boldsymbol{w}(s)\|^{2}_{\mathscr{H}}\/\mathrm{d}\/s

(3.69)

\displaystyle\leq\|\boldsymbol{w}(0)\|_{\mathscr{H}}^{2}+2(\varrho_{1}+\varrho_{2}+\mathcal{K})\int_{0}^{t}\|\boldsymbol{w}(s)\|_{\mathscr{H}}^{2}-2\int_{0}^{t}\langle\mathscr{B}(\boldsymbol{y}^{1}(s))-\mathscr{B}(\boldsymbol{y}^{2}(s)),\boldsymbol{w}(s)\rangle\/\mathrm{d}\/s,

for all
t\in[0,T]
.
Now, we discuss the cases for different values of
r
written as follows:

Case I:

For
r\geq 1
in
d=2
. By using Hölder’s, Ladyzhenskaya’s and Young’s inequalities, we compute

\displaystyle|\langle\mathscr{B}(\boldsymbol{y}^{1})-\mathscr{B}(\boldsymbol{y}^{2}),\boldsymbol{w}\rangle|

\displaystyle=|\langle\mathscr{B}(\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{y}^{2}),\boldsymbol{w}\rangle|=|b(\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{y}^{2},\boldsymbol{w})|

\displaystyle\leq\|\boldsymbol{w}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{4}}\|\boldsymbol{w}\|_{\mathscr{V}}\|\boldsymbol{y}^{2}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{4}}\leq\|\boldsymbol{w}\|_{\mathscr{H}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\boldsymbol{w}\|_{\mathscr{V}}^{\frac{3}{2}}\|\boldsymbol{y}^{2}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{4}}

(3.70)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mu}{2}\|\boldsymbol{w}\|_{\mathscr{V}}^{2}+\frac{27}{32\mu^{3}}\|\boldsymbol{w}\|_{\mathscr{H}}^{2}\|\boldsymbol{y}^{2}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{4}}^{4}.

Then (
3.1
) along with (
3.1
) yields

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{w}(t)\|_{\mathscr{H}}^{2}+\mu\int_{0}^{t}\|\boldsymbol{w}(s)\|_{\mathscr{V}}^{2}\/\mathrm{d}\/s+\frac{\beta}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\||\boldsymbol{y}^{1}(s)|^{\frac{r-1}{2}}\boldsymbol{w}(s)\|^{2}_{\mathscr{H}}\/\mathrm{d}\/s+\frac{\beta}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\||\boldsymbol{y}^{2}(s)|^{\frac{r-1}{2}}\boldsymbol{w}(s)\|^{2}_{\mathscr{H}}\/\mathrm{d}\/s

(3.71)

\displaystyle\leq\|\boldsymbol{w}(0)\|_{\mathscr{H}}^{2}+2(\varrho_{1}+\varrho_{2}+\mathcal{K})\int_{0}^{t}\|\boldsymbol{w}(s)\|_{\mathscr{H}}^{2}+\frac{27}{16\mu^{3}}\int_{0}^{t}\|\boldsymbol{w}(s)\|_{\mathscr{H}}^{2}\|\boldsymbol{y}^{2}(s)\|_{\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{4}}^{4}\/\mathrm{d}\/s,

for all
t\in[0,T]
. On employing Grönwall’s Lemma into (
3.1
), one obtains

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{w}(t)\|_{\mathscr{H}}^{2}+\mu\int_{0}^{t}\|\boldsymbol{w}(s)\|_{\mathscr{V}}^{2}\/\mathrm{d}\/s+\frac{\beta}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\||\boldsymbol{y}^{1}(s)|^{\frac{r-1}{2}}\boldsymbol{w}(s)\|^{2}_{\mathscr{H}}\/\mathrm{d}\/s+\frac{\beta}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\||\boldsymbol{y}^{2}(s)|^{\frac{r-1}{2}}\boldsymbol{w}(s)\|^{2}_{\mathscr{H}}\/\mathrm{d}\/s

(3.72)

\displaystyle\leq\|\boldsymbol{w}(0)\|_{\mathscr{H}}^{2}e^{2(\varrho_{1}+\varrho_{2}+\mathcal{K})t}\exp\bigg(\frac{27}{16\mu^{3}}\int_{0}^{t}\|\boldsymbol{y}^{2}(s)\|_{\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{4}}^{4}\/\mathrm{d}\/s\bigg),

for all
t\in[0,T]
. Since,
\boldsymbol{w}(0)=\boldsymbol{0}
and
\boldsymbol{y}^{2}\in\mathcal{W}
, therefore (
3.1
) immediately yields
\boldsymbol{y}^{1}(t)=\boldsymbol{y}^{2}(t)
for all
t\in[0,T]
.

Case II:

For
r>3
in
d=3
. By using Hölder’s and Young’s inequalities, we find

(3.73)

\displaystyle|\langle\mathscr{B}(\boldsymbol{y}^{1})-\mathscr{B}(\boldsymbol{y}^{2}),\boldsymbol{w}\rangle|=|\langle\mathscr{B}(\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{y}^{2}),\boldsymbol{w}\rangle|\leq\mu\|\boldsymbol{w}\|_{\mathscr{V}}^{2}+\frac{1}{4\mu}\|\boldsymbol{y}^{2}\boldsymbol{w}\|_{\mathscr{H}}^{2}.

We estimate the last term
\|\boldsymbol{y}^{2}\boldsymbol{w}\|_{\mathscr{H}}^{2}
by using Hölder’s and Young’s inequalities with exponent
\frac{r-1}{2}
and
\frac{r-1}{r-3}

(3.74)

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{y}^{2}\boldsymbol{w}\|_{\mathscr{H}}^{2}\leq\frac{\mu\beta}{2}\||\boldsymbol{y}^{2}|^{\frac{r-1}{2}}\boldsymbol{w}\|_{\mathscr{H}}^{2}+4\mu\varrho_{3}\|\boldsymbol{w}\|_{\mathscr{H}}^{2},

where
\varrho_{3}=\frac{1}{4\mu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{r-3}{r-1}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{4}{\mu\beta(r-1)}}}\right)^{\frac{2}{r-3}}
. On combining (
3.73
)-(
3.74
) into (
3.1
), we get

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{w}(t)\|_{\mathscr{H}}^{2}+\frac{\beta}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\||\boldsymbol{y}^{1}(s)|^{\frac{r-1}{2}}\boldsymbol{w}(s)\|^{2}_{\mathscr{H}}\/\mathrm{d}\/s+\frac{\beta}{4}\int_{0}^{t}\||\boldsymbol{y}^{2}(s)|^{\frac{r-1}{2}}\boldsymbol{w}(s)\|^{2}_{\mathscr{H}}\/\mathrm{d}\/s

\displaystyle\leq\|\boldsymbol{w}(0)\|_{\mathscr{H}}^{2}+2(\varrho_{1}+\varrho_{2}+\varrho_{3}+\mathcal{K})\int_{0}^{t}\|\boldsymbol{w}(s)\|_{\mathscr{H}}^{2}\/\mathrm{d}\/s,\text{ for all }\ t\in[0,T].

An application of Grönwall’s Lemma immediately implies that
\boldsymbol{y}^{1}(t)=\boldsymbol{y}^{2}(t)
for all
t\in[0,T]
.

Case III:

For
d=r=3
with
2\beta\mu>1
.
Modifying the calculations (
3.68
), we compute

\displaystyle|\alpha||\langle\widetilde{\mathscr{C}}(\boldsymbol{y}^{1})-\widetilde{\mathscr{C}}(\boldsymbol{y}^{2}),\boldsymbol{w}\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq 2^{q-2}q|\alpha|\int_{\mathfrak{D}}(|\boldsymbol{y}^{1}(x)|^{q-1}+|\boldsymbol{y}^{2}(x)|^{q-1})|\boldsymbol{w}(x)|^{2}\/\mathrm{d}\/x

(3.75)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4\mu}\||\boldsymbol{y}^{1}|\boldsymbol{w}\|_{\mathscr{H}}^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\beta-\frac{1}{2\mu}}}\right)\||\boldsymbol{y}^{2}|\boldsymbol{w}\|_{\mathscr{H}}^{2}+(\varrho_{4}+\varrho_{5})\|\boldsymbol{w}\|_{\mathscr{H}}^{2},

where
\varrho_{4}:=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{3-q}{2}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2^{q-1}q|\alpha|\mu(q-1)}}\right)^{\frac{q-1}{3-q}}
and

\varrho_{5}:=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{3-q}{2}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{2^{q-1}q|\alpha|(q-1)}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\beta-\frac{1}{2\mu}}}\right)}}}\right)^{\frac{q-1}{3-q}}
. On substituting (
3.1
) along with (
3.67
) (for
r=3
), (
3.73
) and (
3.1
) into (
3.1
), and simplifying, we further obtain

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{w}(t)\|_{\mathscr{H}}^{2}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\beta-\frac{1}{2\mu}}}\right)\int_{0}^{t}\||\boldsymbol{y}^{1}(s)|\boldsymbol{w}(s)\|^{2}_{\mathscr{H}}\/\mathrm{d}\/s+\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\beta-\frac{1}{2\mu}}}\right)\int_{0}^{t}\||\boldsymbol{y}^{2}(s)|\boldsymbol{w}(s)\|^{2}_{\mathscr{H}}\/\mathrm{d}\/s

\displaystyle\leq\|\boldsymbol{w}(0)\|_{\mathscr{H}}^{2}+2(\varrho_{4}+\varrho_{5}+\mathcal{K})\int_{0}^{t}\|\boldsymbol{w}(s)\|_{\mathscr{H}}^{2},

for all
t\in[0,T]
. Finally, for
2\beta\mu>1
, Grönwall’s Lemma yields uniqueness and this completes the proof.
∎

Now, let us give the example of a zig–zag function which are commonly used for adhesive or contact laws and satisfies Hypothesis
3.4
and the condition (
3.63
). For more information, one can see
[
16
, Application 8.2.6]
.

Example 3.11
.

Consider the function
\theta_{i}:\mathfrak{D}\times(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

\theta_{i}(x,t,\xi):=\beta_{i}(\xi),

where
\beta_{i}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is given by

\beta_{i}(\xi)=\begin{cases}-3,&\xi<-2\\[4.0pt]
2\xi+1,&-2\leq\xi<-1,\\[4.0pt]
-\xi,&-1\leq\xi<0,\\[4.0pt]
3\xi,&0\leq\xi<1,\\[4.0pt]
-\xi+4,&1\leq\xi<2,\\[4.0pt]
2,&\xi\geq 2.\end{cases}

Now, let us verify that the function
\theta_{i}
satisfies Hypothesis
3.4
and the condition (
3.63
).

(i) Local boundedness.

Since
\beta_{i}
is piecewise linear and constant outside the interval
[-2,2]
, it is bounded on every bounded interval of
\mathbb{R}
. Hence, for every
r>0,
there exists a constant
c(r)>0
such that

|\theta_{i}(x,t,\xi)|=|\beta_{i}(\xi)|\leq c(r)

for all
(x,t)\in\mathfrak{D}\times(0,T)
and
|\xi|\leq r
.

(ii) Continuity in
(x,t)
.

Moreover, the function
\theta_{i}(x,t,\xi)=\beta_{i}(\xi)
is independent of
(x,t)
. Therefore, for every
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
, the mapping

(x,t)\mapsto\theta_{i}(x,t,\xi)

is continuous on
\mathfrak{D}\times(0,T)
.

(iii) Growth condition.

Since
\beta_{i}
is piecewise linear, there exist constants
C_{1,i},C_{2,i}>0
such that

|\theta_{i}(x,t,\xi)|=|\beta_{i}(\xi)|\leq C_{1,i}+C_{2,i}|\xi|,

for all
(x,t,\xi)\in\mathfrak{D}\times(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}
. Hence the growth condition

|\theta_{i}(x,t,\xi)|\leq\bar{\alpha}(x,t)+C_{1,i}+C_{2,i}|\xi|

holds with
\bar{\alpha}(x,t)=0
.

(iv) Ultimate monotonicity in
\xi
.

From the definition of
\beta_{i}
, we observe that

\beta_{i}(\xi)=-3\leq 0\text{ for }\xi<-2,

and

\beta_{i}(\xi)=2\geq 0\text{ for }\xi\geq 2.

Thus there exists
\phi_{i}=2
such that

\esssup\limits_{\xi\in(-\infty,-\phi_{i}]}\theta_{i}(x,t,\xi)\leq 0\quad\text{and}\quad 0\leq\essinf\limits_{\xi\in[\phi_{i},\infty)}\theta_{i}(x,t,\xi).

Also, the function satisfies the condition (
3.63
) with
\mathcal{K}=1
.
