Genome-wide identification of MADS-box genes and functional analysis of EgMADS3 in oil palm flower development
Authors: Qiufei Wu, Muhammad Imran, Guanming Chen, Xianhai Zeng, Lixia Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOil palm (Elaeis guineensis) is a high-yielding crop of global economic importance, contributing significantly to the food, biofuel, and pharmaceutical industries. The role of flower development in fruit formation and overall productivity is well established; however, the specific regulatory networks governing floral transition remain poorly understood.ResultsThis study aimed to identify members of the MADS-box gene family and investigate the functional roles of EgMADS3 and EgMADS45 in the reproductive growth and development of oil palm. A total of 61 MADS-box genes were identified in the oil palm genome and classified into two major types and 15 subfamilies based on phylogenetic analysis. Conserved motif structures, gene architectures, and cis-regulatory elements supported the functional conservation of these genes. All identified EgMADS-box genes were mapped onto 14 chromosomes. Collinearity and gene duplication analyses identified 17 pairs of segmentally duplicated paralogous EgMADS-box genes, contributing to the expansion of this gene family. RNA-seq data indicated that type II EgMADS genes are likely involved in floral development, with qRT-PCR confirming the tissue-specific expression patterns of 6 EgMADS genes across various floral organs. Functional validation in transgenic Arabidopsis thaliana showed that overexpression of EgMADS3 and EgMADS45 induced early flowering and abnormal floral morphology, suggesting their regulatory role in floral development.ConclusionThese findings offer new insights into the molecular functions of EgMADS genes and provide promising targets for molecular breeding strategies aimed at improving oil palm yield and productivity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09279-y.

Background

MADS-box gene-encoded proteins belong to a group of transcription factors (TFs) found across eukaryotic organisms, playing a crucial role in regulating growth and developmental processes [1, 2]. During the evolution of plants, MADS-box genes have undergone numerous duplication events, forming a large multigene family [3, 4]. The TFs encoded by this gene family contribute to various developmental processes and signaling pathways in plants, with a significant role in flower and fruit initiation and maturation [5–7]. Based on gene structure and phylogenic relationships, MADS-box genes are classified into two categories, namely, Type-I and Type-II [8]. Type-I genes are typically characterized by 1–2 exons and a highly conserved MADS domain, although their functional characterization is still limited. In contrast, Type-II genes exhibit a more complex structure with multiple exons and contain MADS I, K, and C domains, which show varying degree of conservation [9]. Among these, the MADS domain at the N-terminus is the most conserved, followed by the relatively conserved K domain, while the I and C domains are more divergent [10]. Based on differences in protein structure, Type-II MADS-box genes are further divided into MIKC* and MIKCC. Compared to MIKCC, MIKC* has a longer I domain and a shorter, less conserved K domain [11]. According to phylogenetic relationships, MIKCC can be further subdivided into several subfamilies, including AG, AP1/FUL, AP3/PI, AGL6, AGL12, AGL15, AGL20, AG/STK, BS, SVP and SEP [12]. Type II genes, particularly those of the MIKCC class, have been extensively studied in relation to floral structure and fruit development [13]. Floral organ initiation and development are regulated by various TFs, especially those belonging to the A (AP1/FUL), B (AP3/PI), C/D (AG/STK), and E (SEP) functional classes of MADS-box genes [14, 15].

Oil palm (Elaeis guineensis Jacq.), a perennial tropical species belonging to the family Arecaceae, is widely recognized as the most efficient oil-producing crop worldwide based on yield per unit area [16, 17]. It is commercially cultivated on various scales in more than 40 countries, particularly across Southeast Asia, West Africa, and South America [17–19]. Despite occupying only 5% of the global oil crop cultivation area, oil palm contributes significantly to the global oil supply, producing 33% of the world’s vegetable oil and 45% of its edible oil [20]. As a monoecious species bearing separate male and female flowers, oil palm is considered an ideal candidate for studying the determinants of male and female flower development [21, 22]. However, research on the molecular mechanisms underlying sex differentiation in oil palm flowers remains relatively limited [23, 24]. Only a few oil palm MADS-box transcription factors have been cloned and characterized to date. For example, a study reported EgSQUA1 (SQUA subfamily), which is expressed throughout male and female floral development, while EgGLO2 (GLO subfamily) is primarily expressed in sepals and petals. In contrast, EgDEF1 (DEF subfamily) was not observed in the floral meristem of either male or female flower inflorescences, or in leaves and roots. EgAG2 (AG subfamily) was highly expressed during pistillate flower development and weakly detected in leaves and roots. EgAGL2-1 (AGL2 subfamily) is hypothesized to play a specific role in the expansion of petals and the primordia of stamens [25]. An E-type MADS-box gene, EgMADS8 has been studied in the oil palm fruit mesocarp, though its specific function is yet to be determined [26]. Moreover, the expression levels of SEP, AG, and GLO/PI sub-family genes in the fruit mesocarp of oil palm have been found to be closely linked to the content of oil and carotenoids [27]. AG genes exhibited stronger expression in oil palm fruit compared to flowers, while a GLO gene, classified as B-type, showed notable expression during both flowering and fruit ripening. A MADS TF involved in the regulation of unsaturated fatty acid metabolism in oil palm has been successfully identified [28]. Emerging research has focused on oil palm MADS-box genes in the fruit mesocarp, with RNA-seq analysis used to determine their expression levels during fruit development [5]. Due to their importance in floral development and physiology, it is essential to examine the evolutionary patterns and functional characterization of MADS-box genes in oil palm [5, 29, 30]. Therefore, this study was planned to explain the genome-wide identification, composition and functional adaptation, conserved topology, and transcriptomic profiling of MADS-box genes in oil palm. First, we assessed the physicochemical characterization, evolutionary relationships, conserved protein motifs, chromosomal locations, cis-elements, and functional annotations of the identified MADS-box genes. Subsequently, expression analysis of these MADS-box genes was executed at different stages of floral development. Additionally, we constructed an overexpression vector by cloning the coding sequences of several EgMADS genes and introduced them into Arabidopsis for functional analysis. These findings enhance our understanding of EgMADS gene functions and identify potential targets for molecular breeding to improve oil palm yield and productivity.

Results

Identification and physicochemical properties of EgMADS-box genes

A total of 61 MADS-box genes were identified in the oil palm (Elaeis guineensis) genome after excluding sequences lacking complete MADS domains, and were subsequently renamed accordingly (Table 1, Table S4). The evaluation of the basic physicochemical properties of this gene family showed that the predicted molecular weights of these proteins ranged from 10.11 to 60.90 kDa, and their isoelectric points ranged from 4.99 to 10.33. Among the 61 proteins, 14 were classified as acidic (pI < 7), while the remaining proteins were alkaline (pI > 7). Subcellular localization prediction indicated that, all EgMADS-box proteins are primarily localized in the nucleus (Table 1).

Comparative phylogenetic analysis of EgMADS-box genes

To further investigate the functional evolution of EgMADS-box genes, a phylogenetic tree of MADS-box proteins from oil palm, A. thaliana and O. sativa was assembled using the neighbor-joining (NJ) method. The results indicated that 17 (27.87%) of the EgMADS-box genes were categorized as type I, whereas 44 (72.13%) were classified as type II. Type I genes were further subdivided into three subgroups, including Mα (11 genes), Mβ (2 genes), and Mγ (4 genes), whereas the 44 type II genes were subdivided into MIKC* (4 genes) and MIKCC (40 genes). The MIKCC group was further subdivided into 10 distinct subfamilies, including AG (3), AGL6 (5), AGL12 (4), AGL16 (3), AP3/PI (4), SOC1-like (4), SEP (7), SVP (2), TM3 (6), and FLC (2) (Fig. 1). Evolutionary analysis suggested that EgMADS-box genes clustered within the same group may have similar functions, however, this hypothesis requires further validation and clarification through experimental approaches.

Gene structure and conserved domain analysis of EgMADS-box family members

Gene structure and conserved domains are critical for understanding gene function and the molecular mechanisms underlying protein activity. To explore the structural diversity of EgMADS-box genes, all 61 genes members were categorized into type I and type II groups (Fig. 2a). The number of introns ranged from 2 to 18 across the family. Genes belonging to type I contained simpler structures with 2 introns, whereas type II genes exhibited a more complex structure, with the number of introns varying from 2 to 18 (Fig. 2d). In addition, conserved domain analysis was performed at the protein level using InterPro, the NCBI Conserved Domain Database (NCBI-CDD), and SMART tools. The results indicated that most EgMADS-box proteins possessed both MADS and K-box domains, which are essential for DNA binding and protein–protein interactions [31] (Fig. 2b & c). A subset of MIKC-type genes including EgMADS18, EgMADS19, EgMADS24, EgMADS33, EgMADS51, and EgMADS52, contained only the MADS domain, whereas no proteins were found to possess only the K-box domain. Importantly, conserved domains are encoded by exonic sequences at the protein level; therefore, intronic sequences were not used for domain prediction. Instead, introns were analyzed separately to investigate gene structural variation.

Variation in intron–exon organization among EgMADS genes suggests functional diversification and may contribute to differential regulation during oil palm development. A total of 31 genes (EgMADS3, EgMADS10, EgMADS14, EgMADS15, EgMADS18, EgMADS21, EgMADS23, EgMADS24, EgMADS26, EgMADS27, EgMADS28, EgMADS29, EgMADS31, EgMADS32, EgMADS33, EgMADS34, EgMADS36, EgMADS37, EgMADS38, EgMADS39, EgMADS40, EgMADS41, EgMADS45, EgMADS46, EgMADS47, EgMADS49, EgMADS51, EgMADS52, EgMADS53, EgMADS54, EgMADS56) showed relatively conserved exon–intron organization typical of MADS-box transcription factor genes (Table S6).

These genes were identified as members of the MADS-box transcription factor family based on the presence of conserved MADS and K-box domains confirmed through protein-level domain analysis (Fig. 2b, & c). No protein domains are encoded within introns, and therefore intronic regions do not contain MADS or K-box domains. This confirms that all 61 EgMADS genes belong to the MADS-box transcription factor family, which is highly conserved in plants. Intronic regions may instead harbor regulatory elements influencing gene expression and alternative splicing, contributing to functional diversity.

Analysis of Cis-acting elements in promoter region of EgMADS-box genes

Cis-acting elements in promoter regions are important regulatory components that control gene expression. To determine the regulatory roles of EgMADS-box genes, cis-elements within the 2,000 bp upstream region of the translation initiation site were predicted using the PlantCARE database (Fig. 3a; Table S5). The identified cis-acting elements were classified into four functional groups: stress responsiveness, light responsiveness, hormone responsiveness, and plant growth and development. Our analysis revealed that EgMADS-box promoters contain multiple light-responsive elements (which were the most abundant cis-elements identified), as well as hormone-responsive elements, including TGA motifs (auxin-responsive), ABRE (abscisic acid-responsive element), TCA elements (salicylic acid-responsive), and CGTCA/TGACG motifs (MeJA-responsive elements). Several stress-responsive elements were also identified, including the G-box (which also functions in light signaling), LTR (low temperature response), MBS (drought-responsive MYB binding site), the WUN motif (wound response), STRE (general stress response), and TC-rich repeats (defense and stress response elements). Additionally, regulatory elements associated with plant growth and development, such as the CAT-box and O2-site, were identified. Notably, light-responsive elements were the most abundant cis-elements across EgMADS promoters (Fig. 3b). These findings indicate that EgMADS-box genes are under complex transcriptional regulation and may participate in multiple biological processes, with light-mediated regulation being particularly prominent given the abundance of light-responsive elements, in addition to hormone signaling, stress responses, and developmental regulation (Fig. 3c).

Chromosomal distribution and duplication events of EgMADS-box genes

The EgMADS-box genes were unevenly distributed across 14 chromosomes in the oil palm genome. Chromosomal localization analysis revealed that 49 out of 61 EgMADS-box genes were mapped to 14 chromosomes, while the remaining 12 MADS-box genes could not be assigned to any chromosome (Fig. 4). These genes were primarily located at the proximal or distal ends of chromosomes with densities ranging from 1 to 13 genes per chromosome. Chr-2 contained 13 EgMADS-box genes, followed by Chr-3 with 6 genes. Chr-13 contained 5 EgMADS-box genes, whereas Chr-1, 7, and 15 each contained 4 genes. Additionally, Chr-4, 8, 11, 12, and 16 contained 2 genes each, whereas the remaining chromosomes had only one gene (Fig. 4). Furthermore, gene duplication analysis indicated that gene family expansion occurred via genome-wide duplication events.

Gene duplication and collinear correlation analyses showed that 17of the 61 genes were involved in the duplication process (Fig. 5a). Two tandemly duplicated gene pairs including EgMADS3 and EgMADS15 (Chr-2), EgMADS6 and EgMADS20 (Chr-2) were identified. The duplicated genes were classified into AG, AGL12, SEP, AP3/PI, SOC1-like, and Mα subfamilies, indicating that the MADS-box gene family has undergone extensive duplication events during evolution, particularly in key subfamilies regulating floral organ development (e.g., AG, AP3/PI, SEP) and fruit development (e.g., AGL12, SOC1-like). Duplications in AG and AP3/PI may contribute to floral morphological diversity, while those in SEP and AGL12 could enable plants to adapt to different developmental or environmental demands. The duplication of Mα may confer species-specific functions, potentially associated with unique traits such as specialized fruit structures or reproductive strategies. These duplication events likely promoted functional diversification of MADS-box genes, thereby influencing floral development, fruit formation, and adaptive evolution in plants.

The remaining three gene pairs (EgMADS42 (Chr-13) and EgMADS55 (scaffold p5_sc00101), EgMADS43 (Chr-13) and EgMADS55 (scaffold p5_sc00101), EgMADS12 (scaffold p5_sc00044) and EgMADS13 (Chr-2)) were segmentally duplicated on the unanchored regions of different chromosomes. The most abundant group of segmentally duplicated genes was present on chromosome Chr-2 with 6 EgMADS-box genes, followed by Chr-3, Chr-7 and Chr-15 with 2 duplicated genes pairs each. However, only a single duplication was observed on Chr-6, Chr-8, Chr-9, and Chr-13 respectively. Interestingly two tandem duplication events were present on Chr-2 in the oil palm genome (Fig. 5a, Table S7).

The values of Ka, Ks, and Ka/Ks are important for evaluating the selective pressure acting on genes. Typically, a Ka/Ks ratio > 1 indicate positive selection, a Ka/Ks ratio < 1 indicates purifying selection, and Ka/Ks ratio = 1 indicates neutral selection. In this study the Ka/Ks ratios for all EgMADS-box gene pairs ranged from 0.01 to 0.36, all of which were less than 1 (Table S8). This indicates that purifying selection has been acting, preserving the structural conservation of the EgMADS-box genes throughout the domestication or evolutionary process. To explore the origins of these gene family members, we conducted synteny analysis between the oil palm, rice and Arabidopsis genome, focusing on the EgMADS-box genes (Fig. 5b). Synteny analysis offers valuable insight into the evolutionary relationships and origins among the gene families by comparing conserved chromosomal segments across different species. Our findings showed that 20 EgMADS genes were positioned in 38 collinear blocks with O. sativa, whereas 8 EgMADS genes were located in 17 collinear blocks within the Arabidopsis genome (Fig. 5b).

Expression profiling of EgMADS-box genes in various tissues

The gene expression patterns of the EgMADS-box genes were investigated across various tissues of oil palm, including flowers collected at 0, 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 24, 48, and 72 h after pollination, along with root and leaf samples, and mesocarp samples collected at 15, 17, 21, and 23 weeks after germination. RNA-seq analysis showed distinct tissue-specific expression patterns of these genes across various tissues. For example, a large number of genes demonstrated higher expression levels in mesocarp and flowers compared with other tissues. Specifically, EgMADS26, EgMADS21, EgMADS45, and EgMADS29 were predominantly expressed in flowers, suggesting their potential involvement in flower-specific transcriptional regulation and floral organ-associated biological processes. In contrast, EgMADS41 was mainly expressed in roots and leaves. Additionally, EgMADS10, EgMADS40, EgMADS39, and EgMADS29 exhibited high expression levels across almost all tissues, while EgMADS26 was highly expressed in most tissues except in roots and leaves (Fig. 6). This pattern of temporal and spatial expression implies that these EgMADS-box genes likely play diverse and tissue-specific regulatory roles in the growth and development of oil palm, although their precise biological functions require further experimental validation.

Over-expression of EgMADS genes in transgenic A. thaliana

To investigate the functional roles of EgMADS genes, six candidates (EgMADS3, EgMADS13, EgMADS21, EgMADS26, EgMADS29, and EgMADS45) were selected based on their relatively higher and/or flower-associated expression patterns in oil palm. These genes were cloned into overexpression vectors under the control of the constitutive CaMV 35 S promoter, introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101, introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101, and transformed into wild-type A. thaliana via the floral dip method. Positive transformants were initially confirmed through hygromycin selection and genomic PCR verification (Fig. S1). To confirm the successful generation of these transgenic lines prior to phenotypic evaluation, we examined target transgene expression levels using qRT-PCR. All six newly generated lines (35 S::EgMADS03, 35 S::EgMADS13, 35 S::EgMADS21, 35 S::EgMADS26, 35 S::EgMADS29, and 35 S::EgMADS45) exhibited significantly higher transcript accumulation compared with wild-type (WT) controls (Fig. S3). This validated that each individual line robustly overexpresses its respective EgMADS transgene.

Following this confirmation, phenotypic evaluation was performed under long-day conditions (Table S9 & Fig. 8). Phenotypic analysis revealed that only EgMADS03 and EgMADS45 overexpression lines exhibited clear alterations in flowering time compared with the wild type, whereas 35 S::EgMADS13, 35 S::EgMADS21, 35 S::EgMADS26, and 35 S::EgMADS29 lines showed no obvious phenotypic differences (Fig. S2). This indicates that only a specific subset of EgMADS genes functionally influence flowering pathways within the heterologous Arabidopsis system. Although both EgMADS03 and EgMADS45 influenced flowering time, EgMADS03 was ultimately selected for detailed downstream functional characterization due to its highly consistent phenotype across independent transgenic lines and its stronger molecular response in downstream target gene expression analyses.

Functional analysis of EgMADS3 gene

To investigate the biological function of EgMADS3, a recombinant overexpression vector (pC1301-EgMADS3) was constructed and transformed into wild-type A. thaliana via Agrobacterium-mediated transformation. Putative transformants were screened on hygromycin-containing medium and subsequently verified by genomic PCR in the T1 generation, resulting in 10 independent stable transgenic lines. Phenotypic characterization of T3 plants grown under long-day conditions demonstrated that ectopic expression of EgMADS3 markedly accelerated flowering and reduced vegetative biomass at bolting compared with wild-type plants (Fig. 9a, b). Quantitative qRT-PCR analysis further confirmed significantly elevated EgMADS3 transcript accumulation in transgenic lines #2 and #5 compared with the wild type (Fig. 9c). In addition to the early-flowering phenotype, overexpression of EgMADS3 induced pronounced abnormalities in floral organ development and inflorescence architecture (Fig. 9d–o). GUS staining analysis

Based on the 2 kb upstream sequence of the EgMADS3 gene retrieved from the oil palm genome database, primers were designed and utilized to amplify the target fragment using DNA extracted from oil palm seedling leaves as the template. PCR amplified product was cloned into a vector, and following sequence validation, bioinformatics analysis was conducted on the EgMADS3 promoter sequence. The analysis results, presented in Table S10, indicated that light responsive motifs, i.e., the I-box and G-box, were the most predominant regulatory elements. Several hormone-responsive elements were identified, including the MeJA-responsive CGTCA / TGACG motifs, the TCA element involved in salicylic acid signaling, and the ABRE element linked to abscisic acid regulation.

To validate the transcriptional activity of the EgMADS3 promoter, the amplified fragment was fused to the GUS reporter gene to construct a promoter-GUS expression vector. This recombinant plasmid was transformed into Arabidopsis via Agrobacterium-mediated transformation. After antibiotic screening and PCR verification, three independents homozygous T3 lines were obtained for GUS staining assays. The expression pattern of the EgMADS3 promoter in Arabidopsis was observed under a stereo microscope (Fig. 10). The results showed that wild-type Arabidopsis (control) exhibited no blue staining (Fig. 10a, c, e), whereas the cloned EgMADS3 promoter successfully drove GUS expression in transgenic plants, resulting in blue staining. High GUS expression was detected in the petals and sepals of floral organs (Fig. 10f), while lower but clearly detectable expression was observed in leaves (Fig. 10b) and stems (Fig. 10f), with almost no expression detected in mature siliques (Fig. 10d). These findings suggest that this promoter region primarily functions in the development of sepals and petals, with additional weaker activity in vegetative tissues.

Subcellular localization of EgMADS3 and EgMADS45

To determine the subcellular localization of EgMADS3 and EgMADS45 proteins, their coding sequences (CDSs), devoid of the native stop codon, were individually cloned into the plant expression vector PVBG2307 in frame with the Green Fluorescent Protein (GFP) gene, under the control of the CaMV 35 S promoter. The resulting constructs (35 S::EgMADS3-GFP and 35 S::EgMADS45-GFP) were transiently expressed in tobacco protoplasts. Nuclear counterstaining with DAPI revealed that the green GFP signals from both fusion proteins completely overlapped with the blue DAPI nuclear signals, whereas the free GFP control (35 S::GFP) displayed fluorescence throughout the entire cytoplasm and nucleus. These observations confirm that both EgMADS3 and EgMADS45 are nuclear-localized proteins (Fig. S5), which closely aligns with their predicted downstream biological function as transcription factors.

Discussion

MADS-box genes are essential for floral development in flowering plants, contributing to reproductive development, regulation of flowering time and monitoring of vegetative growth [32, 33]. Their significance has drawn the attention of evolutionary biologists, particularly regarding their important role in floral parts regulation and growth. The latest completion of multiple whole-genome sequencing projects has greatly accelerated research in this field [34]. A wide range of MADS-box genes has been identified across different plant species, offering valuable insights into their evolutionary dynamics. However, their regulatory functions often exhibit species-specific variations. Therefore, comprehensive screening and functional analysis of MADS-box genes across diverse species are essential for a deeper understanding of their roles and mechanisms. In the current study, multiple MADS-box genes were screened and identified in oil palm, encompassing all major classes found in plants. Gene nomenclature was assigned using a homology-based approach, considering their predicted orthologous relationships with previously characterized MADS-box regulatory genes in oil palm. The declining costs of next generation sequencing (NGS) technologies have significantly facilitated the prompt expansion of plant genome sequencing efforts by research groups worldwide [16, 35, 36]. The accessibility of these genomic resources has facilitated large-scale gene and gene family identification studies in plants, enhancing our perspective on evolutionary mechanisms, functional genomics, and plant responses to various environmental conditions [37, 38]. The naming conventions for genes within a gene family have varied across different studies, lacking a universally accepted standard [39]. For instance, in Zizania latifolia, MADS-box genes were designated in accordance with their chromosomal positions [40]. In contrast, studies on Malus domestica [41] and Glycine max [42] did not follow a defined naming strategy, highlighting the need for a more consistent approach in gene nomenclature. In Sesamum indicum, MADS-box genes were identified and named sequentially in accordance with their gene IDs [43]. Similarly, in the orchid species Phalaenopsis equestris and Dendrobium officinale, genes were matched to their A. thaliana orthologs, the specific naming approach was not explicitly described [44]. These variations might pose obstacles not only in gene identification but also in assigning functional annotations, as genes with the same name may have different roles across various species [45]. Implementing a more consistent approach, such as naming genes according to their confirmed or hypothesized biological roles based on sequence homology, would contribute to the standardization of gene nomenclature and improve the comparability of genes across different species [46]. Steps have been taken to create a consistent nomenclature system for plant gene families. In this study, we utilized an orthology-based naming system for the oil palm MADS-box genes. Gene names are structured with the first two letters for genus and species, followed by ‘MADS’ and a sequential number. Patterns of gene expression, along with overexpression and mutagenic studies can offer the most compelling evidence for orthology.

Although a comprehensive report on oil palm MADS-box genes has not yet been published, previous research has recorded different numbers of MADS-box genes in other species. For instance, A. thaliana is known to have 107 MADS-box genes [47], Populus trichocarpa has 105 [6], Pyrus bretschneideri harbors 95 [48], Malus × domestica accommodates 147 [49], and rice possesses 75 [13]. In contrast, pineapple and bamboo have relatively fewer MADS-box genes, with 48 and 42 members respectively [50, 51]. In this study, we identified a total of 61 MADS-box genes in the oil palm genome (Table 1). The differences in the number of MADS-box genes among various species are likely due to genome duplication events [52]. For example, pineapple has experienced two ancient whole-genome duplications [53], while rice has undergone a recent whole-genome duplication, leading to a greater number of MADS-box genes in rice than in pineapple [54]. This variation in gene number suggests that the regulatory mechanisms governing flowering and flower development have diverged significantly among species [55]. However, considerable research has been conducted on the classification and functional roles of MADS-box genes [33, 56–58]. They are divided into Type-I and Type-II, with type-II further divided in to MIKC* and MIKCC subgroups [59, 60]. MIKCC-type genes are particularly important for floral organ identity and development [39, 61]. The expansion and diversification of MADS-box genes, especially the MIKCC subgroup, have been influenced by whole-genome duplications and tandem duplications, which may lead to novel functions through neofunctionalization or sub-functionalization [6, 62]. These evolutionary dynamics of MADS-box genes highlight their essential roles in plant adaptation and development [63].

The present gene structure analysis revealed that the number of introns in MADS-box genes varied from 2 to 18. Type-I genes had a less complex structure, typically containing 2 introns, whereas type-II genes displayed a more intricate structure, with introns varying from 2 to 18 (Fig. 2D). The genetic structure of EgMADS-box genes was found to be similar to that of Arabidopsis, apple, and Zizania latifolia, demonstrating the conserved nature of MIKC-type MADS-box gene structure across these species [40, 64, 65]. Zhao et al. (2020) analyzed the conserved sequences of 36 MIKC-type MADS-box genes, revealing that genes within the same subfamily shared an identical number of motifs and exhibited sequence similarity. In contrast, variations in motif numbers were observed across different subfamilies [65]. Furthermore, genes belonging to the same subfamily displayed structural similarities. Interestingly, MIKC*-type genes exhibit features of both M-type and MIKCC family members, highlighting their distinctive evolutionary and functional role within the MADS-box gene family. Phylogenomic studies indicate that gene and genome duplication events have played a fundamental role in expanding the diversity of MADS-box transcription factors and influencing regulatory networks responsible for floral organ development and flowering time regulation. In this investigation, tandem-duplicated gene pairs from the MIKCC subfamily were identified. Additionally, several cis-acting elements related to the regulation of stress related genes were observed in the upstream regions of the MADS-box genes. These elements include hormone responsive and stress responsive motifs (Fig. 3). Consistent observations have been made in previous studies on rice MADS-box genes [57, 66–70]. Studying gene expression in plants provides valuable insights for enhancing crop yield, resilience to stress, and nutritional value. Gene expression profiling helps uncover new gene functions and interactions, providing insights into cellular mechanisms [71]. For instance, studying MADS-box genes associated with floral development can contribute to breeding strategies aimed at enhancing desirable traits [72]. For this purpose, six selected MADS-box genes were analyzed for expression patterns in oil palm floral tissues. The chromosomal mapping provides valuable context for understanding their genetic regulation of development. The upward expression trends observed for EgMADS3, EgMADS13, and EgMADS21 in calyx, stamen, and pistil tissues are consistent with previous reports demonstrating that specific MADS-box genes exhibit tissue-preferential expression patterns related to floral organ development. For example, Adam et al. (2007) showed that oil palm MADS-box genes belonging to key subfamilies (e.g., SQUA, AGL2, GLO, DEF, AG) have distinct expression zones in male and female inflorescences, reflecting conserved floral homeotic functions similar to model dicot species like Arabidopsis [25]. The differential expression, including low expression of some genes in petals, suggests functional specialization within the gene family. These findings are consistent with findings in other monocots and dicots, where type-II MADS-box genes predominantly regulate reproductive organ identity, although some members may have broader or distinct roles [25, 73]. Genes such as EgMADS26 and EgMADS29, which showed expression across multiple tissues, might have general regulatory roles beyond floral morphogenesis or could participate in earlier developmental stages, paralleling observations in bamboo and other plants where MADS-box genes show diverse temporal and spatial expression patterns [73]. The relatively low expression of EgMADS45 in stamen tissues suggests functional divergence within the oil palm MADS-box gene family, consistent with evolutionary evidence that gene duplication and sub-functionalization drive diversification of gene expression and function among family members [73]. Notably, EgMADS45 exhibited comparatively higher expression in pistil tissues than in stamens, indicating a potential role in reproductive organ differentiation. This expression pattern highlights EgMADS45 as a promising candidate for future functional studies, particularly in relation to reproductive development and its agronomic importance in oil palm. Taken together, these findings support the conserved yet functionally diversified roles of MADS-box genes in floral development across plant species, in line with the classical ABC model and its extensions.

APETALA1 (AP1) is critical for determining floral meristem identity regulation and the formation of petals and sepals [71, 74]. Research has indicated that MADS-box genes within the Type-I clade are mainly implicated in the development of the female gametophyte, embryo, and seeds, while Type-II MADS-box genes are primarily engaged in floral organ development [75, 76]. In various plant species, different subfamilies within the members of the MADS-box gene family, exhibit distinct expression patterns [50, 77]. Genes in the type-I clade showed low transcript levels during flower development, while those in the type-II clade displayed a broader range of expression patterns, with most being expressed in reproductive organs. This suggests that type-II MADS-box genes are potentially involved in the floral development of safflower [78]. To gain deeper insights into the role of MADS-box genes in the development of oil palm flowers, we analyzed the expression patterns of several genes in various floral organs. The results revealed that multiple MADS-box genes exhibited differential expression levels throughout flower development, highlighting their distinct roles in the formation of floral organs in oil palm (Fig. 6). Such observations agree with global genome-wide analyses revealing that MADS-box gene families have expanded and diversified to acquire specialized roles during floral development in many plant species, with distinct regulatory modules active at different developmental stages [33]. In Cyclocarya paliurus, the expression profile showed that most MIKC-type MADS-box genes were differentially expressed in six tissues (with particularly high expression in floral buds), and the expression patterns were also visibly varied in the same subfamily [79]. These findings indicate that MIKC-type MADS-box genes display functional diversity in plant development, with distinct floral bud-specific expression patterns implicating their crucial regulatory roles in floral organogenesis [79]. Functional validation using six overexpression vectors confirmed that EgMADS3 and EgMADS45 promote early flowering and cause abnormal floral morphology, underscoring their conserved roles in flowering regulation. Our findings revealed that the overexpression of EgMADS3 and EgMADS45 significantly accelerated flowering under long-day conditions (Fig. 11). In contrast, the remaining 35 S::EgMADS transgenic lines did not exhibit notable differences in flowering time, compared to the wild-type. These results align with previous studies conducted on different plant species, including Dianthus caryophyllus [51] and orchid [28]. In Juglans mandshurica, 30 MADS-box genes showed predominant expression in female flowers, while 26 were primarily associated with male flowers. Notably, 19 genes displayed high expression during the later stages of female flower development (FS3), whereas 16 genes were upregulated during the final stages of male flower maturation [80]. The overexpression of EgMADS3 in Arabidopsis significantly accelerated stem elongation and induced flowering 11–15 days earlier than the wild type, consistent with known roles of MADS-box genes in promoting floral transition and shoot growth [81]. The sepal-to-carpel transitions and curled leaf phenotypes observed reflect altered floral organ identity typical of MADS-box floral homeotic gene functions, as established in prior studies. Furthermore, silencing of MdMADS15/22, which causes leaf-like carpels and increased petal numbers aligns with reports of floral organ identity disruption in Rosaceae species [82]. These findings confirm the key role of EgMADS3 in coordinating flowering time, floral organ morphogenesis, and vegetative growth regulation, consistent with the multifunctional nature of MADS-box transcription factors [82, 83]. Complementing these molecular and phenotypic analyses, GUS staining provided valuable spatial insights into gene expression patterns [84]. The β-glucuronidase (GUS) assays revealed intense color localization predominantly in petals and sepals of transgenic Arabidopsis plants, emphasizing the potent involvement of these genes in floral organ development. These observations concur with prior research demonstrating strong GUS activity in petals and young flower buds across species, including cotton and Arabidopsis, with comparatively weaker expression in sepals [84]. Chen et al. (2011) investigated the expression of the GUS: FYF (AGL42) gene under the 35 S promoter in different floral tissues of Arabidopsis [85]. These results are consistent with the mRNA expression profile of AGL42 in various tissues, with the highest expression observed in petals, followed by leaves, immature fiber (8 DPA), and the abscission zone of floral structures [86, 87]. This spatial expression pattern underscores the significant contribution of MADS-box genes in controlling flower organ identity and developmental dynamics. In addition, the identification of multiple light-, hormone-, and stress-responsive cis-acting elements in the EgMADS3 promoter suggests its potential responsiveness to environmental and hormonal signals. However, this regulatory potential has not been experimentally validated in the present study. Future investigations using proEgMADS3 transgenic lines under controlled treatments (e.g., light, temperature, ABA, and MeJA) combined with quantitative assays such as GUS activity measurement and qRT-PCR will be necessary to confirm these predicted regulatory responses. Together, these integrated expression, functional, and histochemical data provide a comprehensive understanding of the multifaceted roles of MADS-box genes in floral development. They lay a strong foundation for future studies aiming to manipulate flowering traits for crop improvement and deepen our grasp of plant reproductive biology.

Conclusion

This study offers a comprehensive genome-wide examination of 61 MADS-box genes in oil palm, highlighting their structural conservation and potential regulatory roles in development of floral parts. The functional identification and characterization of 61 MADS-box genes, along with expression profiling and functional characterization of EgMADS3 and EgMADS45, reveal their significant roles in reproductive growth. The over-expression of these genes in Arabidopsis induced early flowering and floral abnormalities, further supporting their involvement in flower development. These findings enhance our understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying oil palm reproduction and offer promising targets for molecular breeding strategies to optimize flowering time and increase oil yield.

Materials and methods

Identification of EgMADS-box gene family members

MADS-box protein sequences of A. thaliana were retrieved from The Arabidopsis Information Resources (TAIR) online database (http://www.arabidopsis.org). The obtained sequences were subsequently used as queries to perform BLAST searches against the oil palm genome database using local BLAST. The Hidden Markov Model (HMM) for the MADS domain (PF00319) was downloaded from InterPro (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro/) and employed for domain-based searches using the HMMER tool against the oil palm genome [88]. The results obtained from both BLASTP and HMMER approaches were merged, and the presence of conserved domains in the identified gene candidates was validated using InterPro, the NCBI Conserved Domain Database (NCBI-CDD), and SMART online resources. Sequences with redundant entries or incomplete domain architectures were manually excluded. The physicochemical properties of the final set of MADS-box proteins, including the isoelectric point (pI) and molecular weight (Mw) were analyzed using the ExPASy ProtParam tool (https://web.expasy.org/protparm/). Furthermore, the subcellular localization of the predicted EgMADS-box proteins was predicted using the CELLO v.2.5 prediction server.

Phylogenetic analysis and structural characterization, chromosomal localization, and duplication events

Multiple sequence alignment of MADS-box proteins was performed using the MUSCLE algorithm implemented in MEGA 11.0 software [89]. The alignment results were filtered and visualized using Jalview software (http://www.Jalview.org). Protein sequences of Oryza sativa were retrieved from the Planttfdb database (http://planttfdb.gao-lab.org) and aligned with the MADS-box protein sequences from Arabidopsis and oil palm [90]. These aligned sequences were subsequently used to construct a phylogenetic tree of the MADS-box gene family using the Neighbor-Joining (NJ) method with the p-distance substitution model in MEGA 11.0 with a bootstrap value of 1,000 and default parameters for other settings [89]. Gene structure and conserved motif analysis of the EgMADS-box genes was conducted using GSDS (http://gsds.cbi.pku.edu.cn) and the MEME suite (http://meme-suite.org/tools/meme). For motif discovery, the parameters were set to identify 10 motifs with widths ranging from 6 to 50 amino acids, while other settings remained at default values. The resulting gene structure and motifs were visualized using TBtools software. Genomic data of A. thaliana and O. sativa were retrieved from NCBI GenBank (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). The One-step MCScanX module in TBtools was used to analyze both the intra- and interspecies collinearity of the EgMADS-box gene family. The Advanced Circos function (implemented in TBtools) and the Multiple Synteny Plot function in TBtools were applied to visualize collinearity relationships. Gene pairs involved in segmental and tandem duplications were identified based on collinearity analysis. Homologous gene pairs within the EgMADS-box family were identified based on a sequence similarity threshold of > 80%. Tandem duplications were defined as gene pairs located within a 100 kb genomic interval. The Simple Ka/Ks Calculator function was used to estimate the nonsynonymous substitution rate (Ka), synonymous substitution rate (Ks), and the Ka/Ks ratio to infer selection pressure on duplicated gene pairs.

Prediction and analysis of promoter Cis-acting elements

The 2,000 bp sequence upstream of the start codon (ATG) of the target EgMADS-box genes was extracted using TBtools. These promoter sequences were then analyzed for the presence of cis-regulatory elements associated with phytohormone responses using the PlantCARE database (http://bioinformatic.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/) [91]. The intron sequences of the EgMADS genes were obtained from the oil palm genome database, and the FIMO method in the MEME Suite (https://meme-suite.org/meme) was used to identify the number of motifs and their binding sites.

Expression profiling of EgMADS-box genes using available transcriptome datasets

Transcriptomic data from various oil palm tissues including mesocarp at various developmental stages i.e. (15 weeks: SRR190698, 17 weeks: SRR190699, 21 weeks: SRR190701, and 23 weeks: SRR190702), for flowers (SRR851108), for fruits (SRR851067), for leaves (SRR851096), for shoots (SRR851103) and for roots (SRR851071) were retrieved from the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) database. Plant materials

Eight-year-old oil palm (E. guineensis Jacq.) plants cultivated at the Germplasm Nursery of the Coconut Research Institute, Chinese Academy of Tropical Agricultural Sciences (Wenchang), were used in this study. The floral samples were collected on March 13, 2025, at five distinct developmental stages: S1 (young bud), S2 (white bud), S3 (red petal), S4 (bloom initiation), and S5 (full bloom). Bolting time was defined as the number of days from sowing to the stage when the primary inflorescence stem reached 1 cm in height. Flowers were carefully dissected into calyxs, petals, stamens, and pistils. Immediately after collection, all samples were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 ℃ for further analysis. Three biological and three technical replicates were performed for each sample.

Tissue-specific expression analysis of EgMADS-box genes

Total RNA was extracted from oil palm floral tissues using an RNA isolation kit (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai), and RNA integrity was assessed via 1.25% agarose gel electrophoresis. RNA concentration and purity were determined using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. First-strand cDNA was synthesized from 1 µg of total RNA using the MightyScript First-Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit with gDNA digester, following the manufacturer’s guidelines. Six EgMADS-box genes (EgMADS3, EgMADS13, EgMADS21, EgMADS26, EgMADS29, and EgMADS45) were selected for qRT-PCR analysis based on their differential expression patterns across floral tissues and to represent diverse members of the EgMADS-box gene family. These genes were chosen to capture variation in expression levels and potential regulatory diversity within the family. Quantitative real-time qRT-PCR was performed using the Mastercycler ep realplex 4 system, with reaction sets in 384-well optical plates using the 2× SYBR Green qPCR ProMix (low ROX). Gene-specific primers for EgMADS-box genes were designed using the QuantPrime qPCR primer design tool (Table S1). Each reaction was conducted in a final volume of 10 µl under the following amplification conditions: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 s, annealing at 55 °C for 15 s, and extension at 68 °C for 20 s. A melting curve was generated by gradually increasing the temperature from 60 °C to 95 °C over 20 min. Each reaction was repeated three times with three biological replicates and technical replicates. Gene expression levels were calculated using the 2-ΔΔCt method with the ELF gene serving as the internal reference [93]. Statistical analysis was performed using one-way ANOVA in SPSS software, with significance levels set at p < 0.05 and p < 0.01.

Generation and flowering analysis of transgenic A. thaliana

Six EgMADS genes with high expression levels in oil palm flowers were selected to construct pCXSN-EgMADS overexpression vectors. These constructs were introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101 via electroporation. Transgenic Arabidopsis thaliana plants were subsequently generated using the Agrobacterium-mediated floral dip method. DNA was extracted from the leaves of putative transgenic Arabidopsis lines and screened using hygromycin primers and gene-specific primers for the corresponding EgMADS gene (Table S2).

Assessment of EgMADS3 expression patterns in transgenic Arabidopsis

The recombinant expression vector was constructed using the pCAMBIA1301 vector and the ClonExpress® II One Step Cloning Kit, with the primer sequences being EgMADS3(1301)_F: GAGCTCGGTACCCGGGGATCCACTGGGCCGGCACGGGCGT, EgMADS3(1301)_R: ACGACGGAACCTGCCAAGCTTCTAAGAAGCAACACAAGGCA. T3 transgenic Arabidopsis was obtained, and the flowering time and morphological analysis of transgenic plants and wild-type Arabidopsis were statistically analyzed. The expression profiles of EgMADS3 and the flowering regulatory genes in Arabidopsis were analyzed using qRT-PCR, with the primers listed in Table S3.

Promoter amplification and GUS staining

The transcriptional start sites, cis-acting elements and structural domains of the EgMADS3 promoter were predicted using the PlantCARE database (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/). A ~ 2 kb fragment upstream of the EgMADS3 coding sequence (ProEgMADS3) was amplified and cloned into the GUS expression vector pCXGUS-P. The EgMADS3 promoter was cloned with the primers: EgMADS3-Pro-F: GTCCACAGCACATAGTCCCAA, EgMADS3-Pro-R: GCGCGGTAAAGTGAAAACGA. The freeze-thaw method was used to transfer the GUS expression vectors into the A. tumefaciens GV3101 strain. Agrobacterium harboring the GUS expression vectors was introduced into the leaves of 3-week-old Arabidopsis plants through the whole-leaf injection method. Transgenic seedlings were cultivated on half-strength MS nutrient medium supplemented with 50 mg/L kanamycin. Vigorous seedlings were subsequently transferred to soil for GUS staining analysis.

Subcellular localization of EgMADS3 and EgMADS45

The full-length coding sequences of EgMADS3 and EgMADS45 were amplified and individually fused into the PVBG2307-GFP vector, generating the constructs PVBG2307-CaMV35S-EgMADS3-GFP and PVBG2307-CaMV35S-EgMADS45-GFP. The empty PVBG2307-CaMV35S-GFP vector served as a negative control. These constructs were subsequently delivered into tobacco protoplasts. Following a 24-hour dark incubation at 25 °C, GFP signals were examined. Fluorescence images and chloroplast autofluorescence were captured using confocal laser scanning microscopy.
