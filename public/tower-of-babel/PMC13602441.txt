Integration of Clinical Risk Factors With Radiological Metrics Associated With PSH: The Impact of Stoma Site Features, Obesity and Muscle Atrophy
Authors: Moea Nimmo, Kari A Clifford, John C Woodfield
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTo determine our incidence of parastomal hernia (PSH) after end colostomy formation and to integrate clinical and novel computed tomography (CT)‐derived stoma‐site risk factors.MethodsWe performed a retrospective study of adults undergoing operations resulting in permanent end colostomy at Dunedin Hospital, New Zealand (2009–2022), who had at least one pre‐ or post‐operative abdominal CT scan. PSH and risk factors were identified clinically from electronic medical records or radiologically using the Moreno‐Matias classification. Radiological variables included abdominal wall width, rectus muscle‐to‐subcutaneous fat ratio, rectus density, intraperitoneal area, aperture size and stoma location within the rectus muscle; univariable and multivariable logistic regression identified independent predictors of PSH.ResultsAmong 217 patients, 64 (29%) were diagnosed with PSH, with approximately half symptomatic. Univariable analysis identified six risk factors: body mass index, diabetes, abdominal wall width, abdominal wall muscle‐to‐fat ratio, fascial defect diameter and stoma position within the rectus. In adjusted analyses, larger defect diameter (OR: 3.24 per 1 cm increase, 95% CI; 1.96–5.91), abdominal wall muscle‐to‐fat ratio (OR: 0.68 per 10% increase, 95% CI: 0.49–0.88) and lateral stoma position (OR: 2.79, 95% CI: 1.03–7.86) were independently associated with PSH.ConclusionCombining clinical information with CT‐based morphological assessment identified aperture size and abdominal wall muscle‐to‐fat ratio as key risk factors, and precise central transrectus stoma placement as a risk factor of interest. These findings may inform preoperative risk stratification, technical optimisation of stoma construction and selection of patients for prophylactic interventions.Keywords: colorectal surgery, complications, hernia, risk assessment, risk factors

Introduction

Parastomal hernia (PSH) is defined as the herniation of abdominal contents through the abdominal wall defect adjacent to the stoma [1]. Rates of PSH are reported between 20%–60% [2, 3] and vary because of the historical lack of an accepted gold standard definition for PSH [1, 4], and differences in follow‐up time, methods used for detection and type of stoma [5]. Although most PSH occur within 2 years, they can develop many years later, with the highest incidence occurring with an end colostomy [6].

PSH significantly reduces quality of life (QoL) through bulging, pain, difficulty with stoma appliance application and leakage [7, 8, 9]. More serious complications, including subacute and acute obstruction, occur in approximately 15% of cases [10]. Symptoms necessitate surgery in 10%–30% of patients, yet recurrence remains high, with one third requiring re‐operation within 5 years [11]. PSH also increases healthcare and community costs due to greater stoma appliance use and reduction in work productivity [5].

Common risk factors for PSH include female sex, age ≥ 60 years, obesity and history of abdominal hernia [12, 13, 14, 15, 16]. Less frequently reported risk factors include smoking [17], diabetes [18], peristomal infection [17] and long term corticosteroids [18]. Technical risk factors include lack of preoperative stoma site marking [19] and the size of the stomal aperture [20]. Bringing the stoma through the rectus abdominus muscle, and forming an extraperitoneal tunnel, may minimise PSH [2].

Radiological studies allow evaluation of the abdominal wall (diameter, area and location in rectus) and patient specific characteristics (waist circumference, distribution of fat, psoas attenuation) [5, 21, 22]. To our knowledge, measurement of the ratio of rectus abdominis to subcutaneous fat in the abdominal wall and intra‐peritoneal area has not been investigated. Radiological measurements may also allow preoperative assessment of risk factors at the planned site of a stoma. We therefore assessed a range of radiological features, their association with PSH, and their comparison with established clinical risk factors. Understanding these risk factors may inform surgical planning, patient counselling, and preventive strategies in the context of permanent stoma formation.

Method

Study Design and Setting

We performed a retrospective review of patients undergoing surgery with formation of an end colostomy at Dunedin Hospital, New Zealand, between January 2009 and December 2022. The objectives were to determine the cumulative incidence of PSH and to explore the association between pre‐specified clinical and radiological risk factors and the development of PSH in this cohort.

Study Population

Inclusion criteria included adults (age > 18) undergoing formation of an end colostomy for any pathology. Exclusion criteria included no CT scan. Patients were identified using the Otago Clinical Audit (OCA) database which prospectively documented all operations and complications [23]. Procedures included in the study were Hartmann's procedure (with sigmoid or rectal resection), abdominoperineal resection and end colostomy formation. Additional patient data were obtained in electronic medical records (EMR) up to 2025.

Measurement of PSH

Patients with PSH were identified clinically or radiologically. A clinical PSH was identified using EMR or from diagnosis and procedural information in the OCA. Patients were not clinically reassessed as part of this study. Radiologically, PSH was diagnosed on CT scan and included Type Ib to Type III hernias according to the Moreno‐Matias classification [4]. Hernia incidence was calculated as the proportion of patients undergoing index surgery between January 2009 and December 2022 developing a hernia. Duration of follow‐up was up to the time of the last clinical follow‐up, time of stoma reversal, time of PSH repair or death.

Measurement of Risk Factors

Preoperative CT scans were used for all radiological measures except for area and size of the aperture. CT scans were reviewed using Picture Archiving and Communication System (PACS) software (Sectra PACS, Sectra AB, Linköping, Sweden). The most recent post‐operative CT scans were primarily used to measure aperture area and site and also to document other measurements in patients without a preoperative CT scan.

Preoperative measurements at the height of the umbilicus included: Abdominal wall width (mm) which was measured antero‐posteriorly from peritoneum to skin (Figure 1a); Rectus abdominis muscle‐to‐fat ratio (muscle:fat) where the width of rectus was measured antero‐posterior (mm) from anterior rectus sheath to posterior rectus sheath and compared in a ratio to the overlying subcutaneous fat measured from anterior rectus sheath to skin (mm) (Figure 1b); Rectus muscle density was measured in Hounsfield's units (HU) and the volume of the intra‐peritoneal area in mm2 (Figure 1d). The diameter of the stoma aperture was measured at the site of the fascial defect in mm2 (Figure 1c). The location of the stoma was defined by its relation to the rectus abdominis muscle as medial, central or lateral based on division of the muscle into thirds.

Outcomes of interest included characteristics (age, sex, body mass index [BMI], smoker status), comorbidities (COPD, diabetes, obstructive sleep apnoea, congestive heart failure) and operative details (date of surgery, indication, type of stoma and whether emergency or elective).

Ethics

The study was conducted in full conformity with the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was granted by the University of Otago Human Ethics Committee (Health) (S‐166/23).

Statistical Analysis

Clinical and demographic characteristics were reported as medians (IQR) for continuous variables and counts (percentages) for categorical variables. Independent variables were compared using univariable logistic regression, with occurrence of a hernia as the dependent variable. Significant predictors for hernia were compared in a multivariable logistic regression. Missing data were not imputed. Model adequacy was evaluated with standard diagnostics, including assessment of multicollinearity, goodness of fit (Hosmer–Lemeshow) and discrimination. Residuals were examined to assess model assumptions. p‐values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using R [24]. Code and dataset are available on request.

Results

We identified 217 patients with an end colostomy. No patients had prophylactic mesh placement. Two hundred and three patients had at least one preoperative CT scan and 155 had at least one post‐operative CT scan. CT scans were not routinely performed using a Valsalva manoeuvre or in a prone position. Median (IQR) follow‐up was 6.1 (3.2, 11.9) years overall and 8.5 (4.8, 12.5) years for patients diagnosed with PSH. The median time to first post‐operative CT scan was 10 (3, 61) months.

The cumulative incidence of PSH was 29% over a 16‐year period. Of the 64 patients with PSH, 29 patients were diagnosed both clinically (i.e., PSH documented in electronic medical records or had an operation for PSH repair) and on post‐operative CT. Five patients had a clinical diagnosis when no post‐operative CT scan was performed. Thirty patients had a radiological diagnosis of PSH with no documented clinical diagnosis. Using the Moreno‐Mattias classification system 44% of CT confirmed PSH patients had Type Ib (hernia sac > 5 cm containing the bowel forming the stoma), 10% Type II (hernia sac containing omentum) and 46% Type III (hernial sac containing a loop of bowel different to that forming the stoma) hernia. Thirty percent of Type Ib and Type II hernias and 66% of Type III hernias had clinical documentation of PSH.

Of the six radiological factors investigated, only two (density of rectus and intra‐abdominal area) were not significantly associated with the occurrence of a PSH (Table 1). The positive association between abdominal wall width and PSH was greater in females than males and the association between muscle‐to‐fat ratio was greater in males than females. The significant factors were then examined using multivariable regression models (Table 2). In the adjusted model, defect diameter, muscle‐to‐fat ratio and site of stoma were the most important predictors. Every 1 cm increase in defect diameter was associated with 3.24 times higher odds of developing a PSH (OR: 3.24, 95% CI [1.96, 5.91], p < 0.001). For each 10% increase in abdominal wall muscle‐to‐fat ratio, the odds of developing a PSH were approximately 32% lower (OR: 0.68, 95% CI [0.49, 0.88], p = 0.01). Additionally, patients with laterally sited stomas had approximately 2.8‐fold higher odds of a PSH compared with those with centrally sited stomas. While this did not meet conventional thresholds for statistical significance there was no overlap in the 95% CI (OR: 2.79, 95% CI [1.03, 7.86], p = 0.09). A subanalysis of patients with a completed post‐operative CT scan showed the same unadjusted and adjusted significant predictors for PSH. When we include only those who had a preoperative CT scan, the associations between the occurrence of a hernia and muscle‐to‐fat ratio became stronger, with every 10% increase in this ratio associated with a 98% lower odds of hernia (OR: 0.02, 95% CI [0.001, 0.24], p = 0.008). Other factors of interest were similar to those of the full cohort.

Discussion and Conclusions

The incidence of PSH in this study was 29%, in the lower literature range [2, 3]. This reflects our retrospective medical record review rather than inviting patients for clinical assessment and 62 patients lacking a post‐operative CT.

Main Risk Factors

The multifactorial aetiology of PSH involves patient and surgical factors. Some previously documented risk factors (sex, age, cancer, COPD) were not associated with PSH. The six associated variables included diabetes, BMI and four radiological measurements. Multivariable adjustment resulted in stoma site, the abdominal wall muscle to fat ratio, and initial defect size being independently associated with PSH. A similar study found aperture > 34 mm, COPD and longer operating time predictive [5].

Aperture Size

Aperture size predicted PSH (median 2.65 cm without vs. 3.59 cm with PSH), matching other studies [5, 20]. In one study, the respective median aperture diameters were 2.2 and 3.5 cm, with no PSH in patients with an aperture below 2.5 cm [20]. In another, the median diameter with PSH was 3.4 cm and all patients developing a PSH with a diameter > 5 cm [5]. Studies recommend aperture diameters between 2.5 and 3.4 cm [5, 25]. The common technique of developing a trephine accommodating two fingers introduces substantial variability, with a glove size of 7.5 equating with an aperture diameter of at least 3.5 cm, which is associated with PSH formation. Expert opinion recommends the smallest possible trephine diameter that allows for passage of the afferent stomal limb and associated mesentery without causing clinical obstruction or ischaemia [26]. While a larger aperture size contributes to a PSH, this will also be further increased by the PSH itself (Table 1, Last Postop CT). A further study will identify the natural history of changes in the size of the abdominal wall aperture with and without a PSH over time.

Stoma Site

Although not reaching the cut‐off for statistical significance (p = 0.09), the 2.8‐fold higher odds with a confidence interval above 1 is compatible with a clinically important effect. Central transrectus stoma position showed benefit over lateral, perhaps due to greater muscular support or alternatively the increase in PSH in the lateral position may be contributed to by damage to the neurovascular bundle in the dorsolateral rectus sheath. A reduction in PSH with a transrectus route has been noted [27] but not confirmed by systematic reviews [3, 28]. Further research is required to confirm our observation.

Obesity and the Muscle to Fat Risk Factor

Obesity increases PSH. This may be recorded via weight, BMI, waist circumference, abdominal width [5, 13] and is also associated with diabetes. The difference in BMI between patients with and without a PSH is usually small; at approximately three units [13] as it does not distinguish between muscle, bone and adipose tissue. Diabetes may also increase PSH by influencing wound healing. The strongest and only independent obesity measurement in our multivariable analysis was the rectus muscle‐to‐abdominal wall fat ratio. This uniquely combines a measurement of the quality of the abdominal wall muscle, essential for countering the increased load around the stoma, with a direct measurement of obesity. The ability of CT‐measured ratio to predict PSH better than BMI supports the preoperative use of CT to identify patients with an increased risk of PSH and may also be useful in preoperatively assessing the best site for a stoma. As no prior studies have measured the abdominal wall muscle:fat ratio, further research is required to confirm these findings.

Limitations

Limitations related to the retrospective nature of this study include the inability to control for unidentified confounders, the ability to show association but not establish causation, the presence of incomplete data such as missing preoperative or post‐operative CT scans and the inability to document some reported risk factors such as operative time. Second, we relied on medical records and OCA data for clinical diagnosis of PSH without clinical reassessment, contributing to under‐reporting of PSH. Third, as two of the three independent risk factors associated with a change in PSH on multivariable analysis were CT endpoints reported for the first time, these require further assessment and validation.

Conclusion

This study showed the potential benefit of combining clinical and CT‐measured morphological measurements to identify risk factors for PSH. The main findings were that a higher muscle:fat ratio in the abdominal wall, a smaller aperture size in the rectus sheath/rectus abdominus and the passage of the stoma through the central portion of the rectus abdominus were associated with fewer PSHs. This information may be used to optimise patients before surgery, perform the technical steps of surgery in a way that may reduce PSH rates and to identify patients that may benefit from prophylactic interventions, such as the 3D funnel shaped intra‐abdominal mesh [29]. Further work in validating these newly identified risk factors associated with PSHs is planned.
