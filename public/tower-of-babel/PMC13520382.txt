Cross-cultural adaptation of the Spanish version of the Swimming with Independent Measure (SWIM)
Authors: María Isabel Gaviña-Barroso, Soraya Pacheco-da-Costa, Isabel Rodríguez-Costa, Guillermo García-Pérez-de-Sevilla, Leticia Martínez-Caro, Beatriz Sánchez-Sánchez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAssessing aquatic functional abilities is essential for designing effective interventions for children and adolescents who undergo aquatic activities and/or therapy. However, there is a lack of validated instruments in Spanish addressing this need. The Swimming with Independent Measure (SWIM) assesses functional performance in aquatic environments across typical and atypical development. This study aimed to cross-culturally adapt and to evaluate content equivalence and face validity of the SWIM for use with Spanish-speaking professionals working in aquatic environments with children and adolescents.MethodA methodological study was conducted to perform the cross-cultural adaptation of the SWIM and evaluate its content equivalence and face validity. Cross-cultural adaptation followed Beaton’s six-phase methodology: translation, synthesis, back-translation, expert committee review, and pilot testing. Two translators and two back-translators performed the linguistic adaptation. A multidisciplinary expert committee (n = 5) evaluated content equivalence, and 20 professionals meeting predefined eligibility criteria participated in pilot testing. Content equivalence was assessed during the expert committee review, while face validity was evaluated during pilot testing.ResultsAfter two expert committee rounds, 83.6% of items were classified as conceptually equivalent and 16.4% as similar. Content equivalence indices improved from Ave-CVI = 0.93 to 1.00 and UA-CVI = 0.73 to 1.00. Pilot testing with 20 professionals confirmed adequate comprehensibility, requiring minor revisions to a few items. The final Spanish version showed adequate content equivalence and satisfactory face validity.ConclusionsThe Spanish version of the SWIM was cross-culturally adapted, demonstrating semantic, conceptual, idiomatic, and content equivalence with the original, and showed adequate face validity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-026-07054-4.

Background

Aquatic activities and aquatic therapy have gained increasing relevance in paediatric rehabilitation for children and adolescents, both with typical and atypical development. The unique properties of water, such as, buoyancy, viscosity, hydrostatic pressure, and temperature, make it an ideal medium for promoting motor development [1]. Consequently, aquatic interventions have been associated with improvements in trunk control, balance, flexibility, pain management, muscle strength and respiratory improvements [2–11]. In addition to these physical benefits, participation in aquatic activities has also been linked to enhanced emotional well-being, mental health, self-esteem, cognitive abilities, academic performance, and contributes to an overall improvement in quality of life in children and adolescents [12–15].

For children and adolescents with atypical development, aquatic environments may offer unique opportunities to develop motor skills within a motivating and accessible context. Several studies have reported improvements in gross motor function and aquatic skills following aquatic interventions in populations such as children with cerebral palsy and autism spectrum disorder [2–10, 13]. Furthermore, the enjoyable and socially engaging nature of aquatic activities may promote participation, social interaction, and psychological well-being [12–15].

Within aquatic therapy, the Halliwick Concept represents one of the most widely used therapeutic approaches for promoting independence and functional control in water. The Halliwick Concept is based on a structured 10-point programme that focuses on mental adjustment to water, rotational control, balance, and the development of independent movement in aquatic environments [16, 17]. From a theoretical perspective, this approach conceptualises aquatic motor function as a multidimensional construct that integrates postural control, orientation in water, breathing regulation, and coordinated movement, all of which are progressively acquired to achieve functional independence in an aquatic environment.

Motor function in water refers to the progressive acquisition of skills that enable independence and participation in aquatic environments [16–18]. It encompasses a wide range of activities accessible to diverse populations, including aquatic therapy, rehabilitation, and sport [17]. In line with the Halliwick framework, this construct can be operationalised through observable behaviours related to water adaptation, postural and rotational control, balance, breathing regulation, and propulsion, which together reflect the individual’s level of functional autonomy in water. These domains represent progressively acquired competencies that enable individuals to interact effectively with the aquatic environment, maintain orientation, and perform goal-directed movements. Given that these skills underpin participation and functional performance in aquatic environments, their accurate and systematic assessment is essential for understanding individual abilities, identifying limitations, and guiding intervention planning and progression. Individual factors, such as age, cognitive, communicative, motor, and sensory abilities, may influence both performance and assessment outcomes [19], making accurate evaluation critical for planning and measuring intervention outcomes. In addition, the interaction between these individual characteristics and the physical properties of water may alter motor strategies and performance, further supporting the need for context-specific assessment. Because these aquatic skills are influenced by the unique physical properties of water, their evaluation should ideally be conducted within the aquatic environment itself [20]. This need becomes particularly relevant in heterogeneous populations, where variability in developmental profiles may affect both performance and interpretation of assessment outcomes, requiring instruments that are sensitive to a wide range of functional abilities and capable of capturing different levels of independence.

Assessing motor function in aquatic environments is essential for understanding the abilities of children and adolescents within a specialised therapeutic context, particularly in aquatic therapy (AT). Several instruments, such as the Inventario del Desarrollo Evolutivo en el Agua (IDEA) [21] and the Escalas de Competencia Motriz Acuática (ECMA) [22], have been developed and validated in Spain to assess aquatic motor skills in children with typical development. However, to the best of the authors’ knowledge, no assessment tools have yet been adapted or validated for assessing aquatic abilities in children with atypical development within the Spanish population. This distinction is particularly relevant, as children and adolescents with atypical development may present a wide range of conditions, including neurological, developmental, and neurodevelopmental disorders, characterised by heterogeneous motor, sensory, cognitive, and behavioural profiles. These differences may significantly influence how individuals perceive, respond to, and interact with the aquatic environment, as well as how they acquire and perform aquatic skills. For instance, variations in postural control, muscle tone, coordination, sensory processing, or communication abilities may affect both task execution and the consistency of performance during assessment. In addition, cognitive and behavioural factors, such as attention, motivation, or understanding of instructions, may further impact the evaluation process. Consequently, this heterogeneity may not only influence performance outcomes but also affect the interpretation of results, highlighting the need for assessment tools that are sufficiently flexible, sensitive, and adaptable to capture a broad spectrum of functional abilities across different developmental conditions.

The Swimming With Independent Measure (SWIM) is an assessment tool derived from the Halliwick Concept and was developed to address the need for a structured and standardised evaluation of aquatic functional abilities. It was designed to evaluate functional abilities that promote independence in aquatic environments among children and adolescents with both typical and atypical development [23]. The SWIM provides a structured observational framework that enables clinicians to assess aquatic functional performance and to guide the development of individualised intervention programmes [23, 24]. Specifically, the instrument captures key domains aligned with the Halliwick 10-point programme (e.g., mental adjustment, rotational control, balance, and propulsion), thereby operationalising the theoretical construct of aquatic motor function into measurable components. Previous studies have demonstrated high reliability, validity, and sensitivity to change, supporting its use in both clinical and research contexts [23–26]. In addition, cross-cultural adaptations in other contexts have reported consistent psychometric robustness, reinforcing its applicability across different populations. Importantly, to the best of the authors’ knowledge, the SWIM has not previously been translated and culturally adapted for use in the Spanish population. Although the original version is intended for individuals with both typical and atypical development, its direct use in a different linguistic and cultural context may lead to misinterpretations of item meaning, particularly in populations with developmental disorders, where comprehension, communication, and behavioural responses may differ substantially. Therefore, cultural and linguistic adaptation is necessary to ensure accurate assessment rather than relying solely on interpretation adjustments.

The cross-cultural adaptation of instruments originally developed in other languages and countries is essential to ensure their suitability for use within the Spanish population. The translation, cultural adaptation, and linguistic validation constitute an essential preliminary step to confirm the psychometric properties of the instrument. One of the first aspects to be examined during cross-cultural adaptation is content equivalence, that is, the extent to which the adapted items preserve the conceptual meaning and intended function of the original instrument within the target context [27]. Although content equivalence is typically established in the original version, cross-cultural adaptation requires careful examination of semantic, idiomatic, experiential, and conceptual equivalence to maintain its integrity [28]. Additionally, assessing face validity is particularly relevant during translation and adaptation, as the perceived relevance and clarity of items may vary across cultural contexts [29]. Furthermore, from an international perspective, the cross-cultural adaptation of assessment instruments contributes to improving comparability across studies and supports the development of evidence-based aquatic therapy practices. This is particularly important given the growing international use of aquatic therapy approaches, as culturally adapted and methodologically robust instruments facilitate the comparison of outcomes across countries and support the advancement of global evidence in paediatric aquatic rehabilitation.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to cross-culturally adapt the SWIM and to evaluate its content equivalence and face validity for use by Spanish-speaking professionals working in paediatric aquatic environments with children and adolescents with typical and atypical development in Spain.

Methods

Study design

A methodological study was conducted in Spain to perform the cross-cultural adaptation of the SWIM and to evaluate its content equivalence and face validity.

Sample

Participants were recruited from the target population for which the instrument is intended. The sample consisted of professionals, including physiotherapists (PT) and physical activity and sport science professionals (PASSP), who are native Spanish speakers and work in aquatic environments with children aged 1–5 years with typical development and 1–17 years with atypical development.

Participants were recruited from paediatric rehabilitation and aquatic therapy centres in Madrid, Spain, using convenience sampling among professionals experienced in aquatic therapy and sport science.

A sample of 24 participants was included in the pilot testing, in accordance with methodological recommendations for cross-cultural adaptation studies [30–33].

The pilot testing sample consisted exclusively of professionals, who were the participants in this phase of the study. Children were involved only insofar as the professionals administered the SWIM during routine aquatic sessions in order to evaluate the comprehensibility and practical applicability of the instrument under real clinical conditions.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Hospital Clínico San Carlos, Madrid (reference: CI:19/495-E), as the aquatic therapy centres and facilities where the study was conducted belong to its healthcare area. It was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the World Medical Association’s Declaration of Helsinki for research involving human participants [34]. Written informed consent was obtained from all participating professionals (PT and PASSP). Parents or legal guardians of the children involved in the pilot testing were informed about the study and provided written informed consent for the administration of the SWIM during routine aquatic therapy sessions; children also provided assent when appropriate. Additionally, authorization to use the original instrument was obtained from the Halliwick Association of Swimming Therapy (Halliwick AST).

Instrument: the Swimming with Independent Measure (SWIM)

The SWIM evaluates functional abilities that contribute to independent swimming and participation in aquatic environments among children and adolescents with both typical and atypical development [23].

The instrument is composed of eleven constructs: water entry, mental adjustment, breathing control, balance, back transverse rotation, front transverse rotation, sagittal rotation, longitudinal rotation, combined rotations, stroke, and water exit. Each construct is scored using a 7-point Likert scale, where 1 represents total dependence and 7 complete independence. This scoring system allows for the assessment of both the acquisition and progression of aquatic skills over time [23].

The SWIM is administered individually, preferably by an evaluator trained in aquatic therapy and familiar with the Halliwick Concept. The assessment is based on direct observation of the participant’s performance during aquatic sessions, with evaluators using standardized behavioural descriptors for each score level. Administration of the full test generally requires 20–30 min, depending on the child’s motor and cognitive abilities and the level of cooperation during the session [23].

The instrument does not record any personal or sensitive information beyond anonymized demographic data (age, sex, developmental condition). Its design facilitates the systematic and objective assessment of motor abilities in water, providing data that support clinical decision-making, individualized goal setting, and progress monitoring within therapeutic and educational aquatic programs [23].

The original English version of the SWIM has demonstrated excellent psychometric properties, including high internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.95–0.98), high inter-rater reliability (ICC = 0.96), and robust content and construct validity across multiple populations [23, 24]. Moreover, previous cross-cultural adaptations into Greek and Brazilian Portuguese have confirmed its methodological stability and applicability in diverse cultural contexts [25, 26].

Procedure

This study involved the cross-cultural adaptation of the SWIM and the evaluation of its content equivalence and face validity for use in Spain, in order to ensure conceptual, linguistic, and cultural equivalence of the Spanish version.

The cross-cultural adaptation process, including translation and both, linguistic and cultural adaptation, was carried out in accordance with established international guidelines for cross-cultural adaptations [30, 31]. The procedure followed the methodology proposed by Beaton et al. [31], which includes a six-phase approach (Fig. 1). Content equivalence was examined by an expert committee, and face validity was evaluated during pilot testing with professionals from the target population [30–33].

Phase i: translation

Two independent translators participated in this phase. Translator 1 (T1) was a physiotherapist with extensive experience administering the instrument, possessing advanced proficiency in both English and Spanish, and a deep understanding of the relevant cultural contexts. Translator 2 (T2) held an academic degree in English Philology, contributing linguistic precision and specialized expertise to the translation process.

T1 and T2 independently translated the SWIM from English into Spanish, with the objective of preserving the meaning of the original version [35, 36]. Throughout this process, particular attention was given to ensuring semantic, idiomatic, experiential, and conceptual equivalence [30–33]. Both translators submitted written reports identifying and explaining any discrepancies or conflicting translation choices.

Phase ii: synthesis of the translation

T1 and T2 met to review both translations, identify discrepancies, and discuss conflicting terms and expressions. Through this collaborative process, a consensus version, designated as T1-2, was developed. The complementary expertise of both translators ensured a rigorous, accurate, and culturally appropriate translation [37, 38].

Phase iii: back-translation

For the back-translation process, two new translators were recruited, both native English speakers. Back-translator 1 (RT1) was a scholar specializing in the English language, while Back-translator 2 (RT2) was a physiotherapist originally from Canada and residing in Spain. Their linguistic and professional backgrounds contributed to ensuring the accuracy and cultural appropriateness of the back-translated version.

Both translators were native English speakers, were not involved in the earlier phases of the study, and were blinded to the original version of the instrument, with no prior knowledge of its content. This approach aimed to ensure an independent back-translation process and to minimise potential bias [37–39]. This process yielded two independent English back-translations (RT1 and RT2). Subsequently, all four translators met with the study coordinator to review the translations, clarify ambiguous terms, and agree upon a consolidated version (RT1-2). The RT1-2 version was then submitted to the Halliwick AST for review and for recommendations regarding potential modifications. Finally, a detailed report was prepared, documenting all discrepancies, translator suggestions, and proposed revisions.

Phase iv: expert committee review

A multidisciplinary committee of experts, integrated by 5 professionals specialized in Neurology and/or Paediatrics, with a PhD Degree and proficient in English, evaluated content equivalence using the European Research Group on Health Outcomes (ERGHO) methodology [40, 41]. This expert profile was considered particularly suitable because the questionnaire addresses developmental and functional aspects typically managed within these disciplines, ensuring that all items were assessed for clinical relevance and developmental appropriateness [40–42]. They were provided with the Spanish adaptation (T1-2), the joint back-translation (RT1-2), and the original SWIM. ERGHO guidelines, items were classified as Type A (conceptually equivalent), Type B (similar meaning), or Type C (loss of meaning) [41]. Consensus was reached when at least 80% of experts rated items as Type A or B [42].

Items rated as Type A or B were considered relevant; however, Type B items were further reviewed to improve their cross-cultural adaptation based on the experts’ suggestions. All items rated as Type C were modified for a second round of evaluation.

Content equivalence was evaluated qualitatively during the expert committee’s review of each item in terms of conceptual relevance, clarity, and cultural adequacy. Through an iterative consensus process, the expert panel developed a preliminary Spanish version of the SWIM [43]. Although CVI indices are traditionally used in content validity studies, in this study they were applied as quantitative indicators of expert agreement to support the assessment of content equivalence.

Phase v: pilot testing

After establishing cross-cultural equivalence, face validity was assessed through a qualitative evaluation of the test items. To this end, a comprehensibility survey was conducted to examine the clarity of the instructions, test items, and scoring system as perceived by professionals administering the SWIM [43, 44].

Following the methodological guidelines proposed by Guillemin et al. [31], two complementary strategies were implemented: (1) a comprehensibility survey and (2) an instrument administration and comprehension assessment.

For the comprehensibility survey, the preliminary Spanish version of the SWIM, together with a structured feedback form, was distributed by e-mail to PT and PASSP professionals working in aquatic environments with children aged 1–5 years with typical development and 1–17 years with atypical development.In the instrument administration and comprehension assessment, the participating professionals acted as evaluators by administering the Spanish version of the SWIM during routine aquatic sessions to children who met the inclusion criteria, and subsequently completed the comprehensibility survey [42, 43]. The children included in this phase were aged 1 to 5 years with typical development or 1 to 17 years with atypical development. This step was intended to confirm the practical comprehensibility and applicability of the instrument under real clinical conditions.

Phase vi: review of the process

All reports from the different phases were systematically reviewed to compile the comprehensive data collected throughout the cross-cultural adaptation process. This final evaluation aimed to ensure the highest possible level of equivalence between the original and the adapted versions, thereby contributing to the development of a robust and culturally valid instrument of high methodological quality.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the characteristics of the participating professionals and to analyse the responses obtained from the comprehensibility survey. Frequencies and percentages were calculated for categorical variables, while means and standard deviations were reported for continuous variables.

To analyse content equivalence, the Item-level Content Validity Index (I-CVI) and the Universal Agreement Content Validity Index (UA-CVI) were calculated as indicators of agreement among experts. Inter-rater agreement was further assessed using Cohen’s kappa coefficient.

Face validity was assessed through the analysis of professionals’ feedback obtained from the comprehensibility survey and the instrument administration and comprehension assessment. The participants’ comments and suggestions were reviewed descriptively to identify potential ambiguities or items requiring modification.

For the pilot testing phase, the analysis focused on the frequency and percentage of professionals reporting comprehension difficulties or suggesting modifications for each item. Items with discrepancies reported by more than 15% of evaluators were reviewed for potential revision.

All data were organized and analysed descriptively using IBM SPSS Statistics (version XX, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

In the first round, 63.6% of the Spanish items were classified as equivalent (Type A), increasing to 83.6% after the second round. Items classified as partially equivalent (Type B) represented 27.3% in the first round and decreased to 16.4% in the second. Only two items were consistently rated as Type B by all committee members. The remaining 9.1% of items were initially classified as not equivalent (Type C); however, no items received a Type C classification in the second round.

The main issues identified after the first round concerned items classified as Type C equivalence.

Item C (Points 1–7) was considered inadequate in both form and substance by Expert 3 (E3). The purpose of this item was clarified to determine whether modifications were required. As the majority of experts had classified the item as Type A according to the ERGHO system, it was provisionally retained, pending further discussion in the second round. Within the same item, the term humming—initially translated into Spanish as zumbar—was perceived by another expert as lacking experiential relevance in the aquatic environment. It was clarified that the intent of this item was to assess the ability to produce vocal sounds underwater without experiencing respiratory difficulty. Consequently, the term tatarear (“to hum,” in a more contextually appropriate sense) was proposed for the second round.

In Item D (Point 7), an error occurred in translating roll as balanceo (“swaying”), which led to an inaccurate back-translation as sway. A semantic equivalence error was identified by comparing the original and Spanish versions. This was corrected in the subsequent back-translation, and vaivén was replaced by giro (“roll”) in the Spanish text.

Finally, in Item K (Points 3–6), the verb botar hacia arriba (“to bounce up”) was revised to empujarse hacia arriba (“to push upwards”), and contonearse was replaced by serpentear (“to slither”), as these alternatives offered greater semantic and experiential equivalence in the target language.

After two rounds of evaluation using the equivalence criteria established by the ERGHO group [41], 16.4% of the items were classified as Type B, indicating no substantial loss of meaning, while the remaining 83.6% were classified as Type A. The expert committee subsequently reviewed the equivalence results and the accompanying test manual guidelines and instructions to finalize the preliminary Spanish version of the SWIM.

Regarding content equivalence, in the first round of evaluation the instrument showed a high level of agreement among experts (Table 3). I-CVI values ranged from 0.60 to 1.00, with an Ave-CVI of 0.93 (95% CI: 0.75–1.00) and a UA-CVI of 0.73 (95% CI: 0.42–0.98). These results indicate that most items were considered conceptually appropriate and equivalent to the original version. However, the low agreement observed for Item C (Kappa = 0.00) suggested that this item required revision to improve its clarity and conceptual equivalence.

Phase v: pilot test

The preliminary Spanish version of SWIM was sent along with a form for submitting suggestions via email to twenty-four professionals (PT and PASSP). For face validity, the comprehensibility assessment, designed to determine whether the text of the T1-2 post-ERGHO version was clearly understood, identified discrepancies in comprehension among 20% of evaluators for Item B, 35% for Item E, and 30% for Item G. These items were subsequently reviewed, and the necessary adaptations were implemented through consensus during a meeting involving all translators.

Specifically, Item B was reported as confusing by 20% of participants. Consequently, the descriptions corresponding to scores 6 and 7 were revised based on evaluator feedback. Item E was found difficult to understand by seven participants (35% of the sample), which prompted a review of the item and a meeting with the expert committee and translators (T1 and T2) to refine the terminology. Based on these discussions, a revised version was incorporated into the final instrument.

For Item G, scores 6 and 7 were marked as unclear by 30% of participants. After reviewing the evaluator comments, a recurring suggestion—raised by three participants—was selected, presented to the expert committee, and included in the final version to improve clarity. Two participants identified a grammatical error in the skill description of Item C, which was subsequently corrected, and a clarification was made to Item D to improve the text, although this did not constitute a modification based on comprehension issues.

Finally, four participants (20% of evaluators) suggested including a definition for the term chair position. Although this term is commonly used in Halliwick Concept training, an explanatory note was added under Skill B, where the term first appears in the instrument. This addition, which is not part of the original version, was based on the definition provided by the International Halliwick Association (IHA) [45].

Discussion

Most health outcome measures are developed in English-speaking countries; however, their use in non–English-speaking contexts has increased considerably [31, 35]. This trend highlights the need to either develop new instruments or adapt existing ones for different populations through translation and cross-cultural adaptation [36]. In the present study, the SWIM served as the foundation for the cross-cultural adaptation into Spanish for use with children and adolescents with both typical and atypical development, following internationally recognized guidelines by Guillemin et al. [30], Beaton et al. [31] and Sousa et al. [33]. The process aimed to achieve conceptual, semantic, and idiomatic equivalence with the original version while ensuring cultural and linguistic appropriateness for Spanish-speaking professionals. The adapted version was then evaluated for content equivalence and face validity through expert committee review and pilot testing to confirm its clarity, relevance, and applicability in aquatic rehabilitation and educational settings. Overall, the findings showed that the Spanish version of the SWIM achieved a high level of conceptual, semantic, and idiomatic equivalence with the original version, while pilot testing supported its comprehensibility and practical applicability in Spanish-speaking aquatic settings.

During the synthesis phase, the collaborative integration of the distinct professional profiles of translators T1 and T2 proved particularly effective in enhancing the semantic fidelity of the scale and ensuring a more precise transmission of the intended conceptual meaning. Although the usefulness of back-translation remains debated in the literature [35, 46–50], it was instrumental in this linguistic adaptation process. This phase helped identify translation errors—such as around instead of near, method instead of form, stay instead of stand, and objectives instead of goals—as well as conceptual discrepancies when comparing the back-translations with the original version. These findings prompted corrections in the T1-2 synthesis, ensuring the overall accuracy of the translation. While some authors argue that back-translation is not essential [51], previous validations of this scale in other languages followed the same procedure and found it beneficial for verifying equivalence between the adapted and original versions [25, 26].

The expert committee evaluated the Spanish version of the SWIM using the criteria established by the ERGHO group. Other health outcomes instruments often used for measuring outcomes related to physiotherapy interventions in children and adolescents with atypical development previously validated in Spanish also used it as references [51–55]. To achieve consensus, two anonymous rounds and one in-person meeting were conducted. Notably, Expert 5 (E5) initially disagreed with several items, classifying many as Type C in the first round. After receiving additional clarifications, E5 subsequently rated nearly all items as Type A. These initial discrepancies appeared to stem from limited familiarity with terminology related to aquatic therapy and the Halliwick Concept, underscoring the importance of domain-specific knowledge for accurate interpretation and application of the instrument [56, 57].

As recommended for cross-cultural adaptation studies, we explicitly examined whether cultural adaptations were required and implemented targeted modifications where necessary to preserve conceptual equivalence. The adaptation of a questionnaire to another culture can present significant challenges, as certain items may carry very different meanings or even no meaning at all within a specific cultural context. In fact, in some cases, cultural differences can be so pronounced that the underlying ways of thinking and interpreting concepts differ substantially. Therefore, a key challenge in adapting the SWIM involved achieving cultural equivalence. In our process, linguistic and cultural issues were identified and addressed as follows:

For instance, in Item A.1, the term plinth lacked a direct equivalent in Spanish, as it refers to a specific structural feature of swimming pools. The Brazilian version also encountered this issue [26], suggesting that this may represent a broader challenge across Romance languages, potentially exacerbated by the concise wording of the original instrument. Similarly, in Item C.5, the term activity eggs, referring to a specific Halliwick material, was not understood when translated literally as huevos de actividades and was instead adapted to bolitas que flotan (“floating balls”) in the Spanish version. This choice aligns with terminology commonly used by Spanish-speaking clinicians and maintains the intended functional meaning of the item. Other items required revision due to experiential misunderstandings regarding the intended action (e.g., Items C.7, G.4–G.5, and K.3–K.6). Additionally, the expression chair position—well known within the Halliwick framework—was clarified to ensure that evaluators unfamiliar with the method could recognise the target posture and administer the item consistently.

After the second round of review, consensus was reached, and all items were classified as either Type A or Type B. A final meeting was then held to review the test instructions and administration guidelines before finalizing the preliminary Spanish version of the instrument. Importantly, these cultural adaptations were minor in scope and did not alter the constructs assessed by the SWIM; rather, they ensured that items communicated the same conceptual meaning, functional intent, and expected performance across languages and contexts.

In addition, the expert review supported the content equivalence of the Spanish version of the SWIM, as most items were rated as conceptually equivalent in the first round and all items achieved acceptable equivalence after revision. These findings suggest that the adapted version preserves the intended meaning and functional scope of the original instrument, supporting its use in future clinical and research studies in Spanish-speaking contexts [24].

Although face validity alone does not ensure that an instrument is valid, it remains a fundamental property to examine in the context of cross-cultural adaptation [48]. In the present study, face validity was assessed through the participation of professionals with extensive experience in aquatic settings, who regularly work with children and adolescents with both typical and atypical development. These professionals acted as evaluators, administering the Spanish version of the SWIM and assessing the clarity, relevance, and cultural adequacy of each item. Their expertise in aquatic therapy and instruction provided a realistic perspective on how the instrument functions in everyday professional contexts.

The qualitative analyses of comprehensibility revealed that the instrument was clearly understood by the evaluators and appropriately captured the aquatic skills it intends to measure. The involvement of experienced practitioners ensured that the assessment of face validity reflected authentic clinical and educational conditions, confirming that the Spanish version of the SWIM can be confidently applied by professionals working in aquatic environments to evaluate motor performance and progression in children and adolescents.

The SWIM was evaluated by a group of professionals with experience in aquatic environments [58], and although all feedback was carefully considered, particular attention was given to items with a disagreement rate exceeding 15%, as these indicated comprehension difficulties and were subsequently modified [28]. Items D and E, which had presented recurring issues during earlier phases, were re-evaluated with the input of the translators and expert panel to achieve a more precise and contextually appropriate Spanish version.

Some comments from the evaluators referred to terminology specific to the Halliwick Concept. The SWIM manual recommends that the test be administered by individuals with appropriate training in this approach [23], and this recommendation remains valid, as the comprehension challenges identified were mainly attributed to limited familiarity with Halliwick principles among some participants. Consequently, the explanation of “chair position” was expanded in Item B, despite its absence in the original version, because this concept, though fundamental within Halliwick methodology, lacks a clear symbolic representation for those not trained in the method. Its inclusion was therefore necessary to ensure accurate understanding and application of the instrument.

Additional modifications were made to Items E and G, both of which had posed challenges since the initial translation phase. Although the conceptual meaning of these items was retained, achieving precise and contextually meaningful terminology required the addition of brief explanatory notes. These refinements ensured that professionals working in aquatic settings could interpret and apply the instrument consistently and accurately in their clinical and educational practice, even though the final version became slightly longer.

Limitations, strengths, and clinical implications

Throughout the study, several public health restrictions associated with the COVID-19 pandemic caused delays in data collection and required the adoption of alternative strategies to ensure the successful completion of each phase. These circumstances represented a significant limitation, affecting both the initial timeline and the logistical planning of the project.

Another limitation is the relatively small pilot testing sample, which may restrict the transferability of the findings. In addition, the use of convenience sampling may have introduced selection bias, as the participating professionals were experienced in aquatic environments and may not represent the full range of potential SWIM users. A further limitation is that parents or legal guardians and children were not directly involved in the evaluation of the wording or structure of the instrument beyond their participation in routine aquatic sessions, which may have limited the breadth of feedback obtained during the face validity phase.

Although back-translation is not considered essential by many authors in cross-cultural adaptation processes, it proved to be a valuable procedure in this study, enabling the identification and correction of subtle linguistic and conceptual inconsistencies. Likewise, the use of the ERGHO methodology was highly effective for systematically identifying and classifying equivalences, ensuring semantic, idiomatic, and conceptual alignment between the original and adapted versions. While the original instrument had already undergone validation, the present linguistic and cultural adaptation strengthened its accuracy and contextual relevance for Spanish-speaking professionals.

A further limitation is that this study focused exclusively on the translation and cross-cultural adaptation processes, together with the evaluation of content equivalence and face validity. Psychometric properties such as construct validity, test–retest reliability, and internal consistency should be examined in future studies to confirm the measurement stability and reproducibility of the Spanish version.

Despite these limitations, the adaptation process yielded a Spanish version of the SWIM that demonstrates high linguistic and cultural equivalence, as well as excellent comprehensibility among professionals who work in aquatic environments with children and adolescents with both typical and atypical development. The availability of this version provides a valuable, standardised tool for evaluating aquatic motor skills, supporting clinical decision-making, and promoting consistent assessment practices within aquatic therapy. Furthermore, it establishes a shared conceptual framework that fosters collaboration among professionals and researchers, facilitating the advancement of evidence-based therapeutic and educational interventions in aquatic settings.

Future research should therefore evaluate the full psychometric properties of the Spanish version of the SWIM, including internal consistency, test–retest reliability, construct validity, and responsiveness. Additionally, studies in larger and more diverse populations are recommended to confirm its clinical utility and to explore its applicability across different aquatic rehabilitation and educational contexts. Such efforts will help ensure that the Spanish version of the SWIM can be confidently utilised by qualified evaluators and integrated into broader evidence-based practice.

Conclusions

The Spanish version of the SWIM was successfully cross-culturally adapted, demonstrating semantic, conceptual, idiomatic, and content equivalence with the original instrument. The adapted version also showed adequate face validity, supporting its clarity, comprehensibility, and applicability for Spanish-speaking professionals working in aquatic environments with children and adolescents with typical and atypical development.

These findings provide initial evidence supporting the linguistic and cultural suitability of the Spanish version of the SWIM. However, this study represents an initial step in the overall validation process of the instrument. Further research is needed to evaluate its psychometric properties, including reliability, construct validity, and sensitivity to change, in order to support its use in both clinical practice and research settings.
