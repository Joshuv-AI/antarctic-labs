1 Introduction
Nuclear safety standards serve an essential function in establishing accepted good practices and in
capturing lessons learned from operational experience, experimental research, and safety analysis.
Their continued maintenance and modernization, however, become increasingly difficult as new
technologies emerge, experience in the community mature, industry needs shift, and regulatory
frameworks adapt to new applications. This challenge is especially pronounced for hazards
involving complex physical behavior and facility-specific consequences. Sodium and sodium–
potassium (NaK) 1 hazards, particularly leaks and fires, involve unique underlying phenomena and
protection considerations. These hazards are only partially addressed in broader fire protection
standards and therefore require more specialized technical guidance [1, 2]. Moreover, recognized
limitations in sodium fire modeling tools [3], combined with historically deterministic and
prescriptive approaches, can lead to excessively conservative assumptions and, in some cases,
impractical or unnecessarily costly design outcomes [4]. In the context of advanced reactor
deployment, these factors underscore the importance of modernizing sodium fire protection
guidance to provide a more robust, flexible, and defensible basis for safety assessments and
regulatory decision-making.

ANS-54.8, originally issued as the “Liquid Metal Fire Protection in Liquid Metal Reactor (LMR)
Plants” standard, is the American Nuclear Society (ANS) consensus standard addressing liquid
metal fire protection, specifically focusing on sodium leaks and fires [5]. The standard was
originally issued in 1988 and withdrawn in 2000. The purpose of this standard is to establish
guidelines and requirements to ensure that the functional performance of sodium leak and fire
detection, alarm, suppression, control, and structural protection systems are adequate to protect
public health and safety and protect facility personnel. As early as 2022, revival of this standard
was recommended by several Sodium Fast Reactor (SFR) vendors and consensus standards
committees, such as the Advanced Reactors Codes and Standards Collaborative (ARCSC) [6]. It
was deemed a critical need that would benefit stakeholders during both the design process and
associated licensing interactions.

The ANS-54.8 Working Group held its kickoff meeting on August 20, 2024, and work at Argonne
National Laboratory (Argonne) began shortly thereafter to support engagement with industry
stakeholders for the reissuance and revision of the standard. As part of the reissuance, the title was
revised to “Liquid Metal Fire Protection” to better reflect the current scope of the standard, which
extends its applicability to a broader range of facilities beyond liquid metal reactor plants [7]. Figure
1-1 illustrates the structured consensus-based communication, coordination, and review pathway
that supports both the near-term reissuance and longer-term modernization of ANS-54.8. The ANS-
54.8 Working Group sits at the base of the process, bringing together the subject matter experts
who draft and revise the standard, including industry representatives such as vendors and current
designers, a representative from the Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC), and independent
consultants (see section 3.2 for more details). The Working Group operates under the leadership of
Argonne as Working Group Chair, with this role supported by the U.S. Department of Energy,
Office of Nuclear Energy (DOE NE).

Once the Working Group produces a draft or revision, it moves to the ANS Review Process for

returned to the Working Group for disposition, creating an iterative loop until consensus is reached.
From there, the standard advances to American National Standards Institute (ANSI) for final

The ANS-54.8 effort brings together stakeholders with different but complementary motivations.
Industry seeks practical and modern guidance that supports efficient design and avoids unnecessary
conservatism. Regulators seek a strong technical basis that can inform a more structured and
streamlined endorsement process. The broader technical community seeks to strengthen the
underlying methods, address limitations in legacy approaches, and advance the state of practice.
Accordingly, the effort is intended not only to produce a revised standard, but also to provide a
shared framework for safety, design, licensing, and implementation across sodium-related
facilities. Building on these needs and motivations, the objectives of this work are as follows:

 • Restore the near-term usability of ANS-54.8 through a timely reissue with only minor
 editorial and clarifying revisions;

 • Modernize the standard's structure, technical basis, scope, terminology, and references to
 better reflect current technologies, facility types, and design needs;

 • Implement a graded, risk-informed, performance-based (RIPB) framework that can be
 applied flexibly and proportionally across a range of facilities, addressing limitations in
 legacy modeling tools and overall deterministic, prescriptive guidance;

2 Background
This chapter provides the technical and regulatory background for the ANS-54.8 modernization
effort. It summarizes the nature of sodium fire hazards and the analytical challenges they present;
and traces the development of the ANS-54.8 standard and how it is related to other applicable codes
and standards.

2.1 Sodium Fire Protection
Sodium and NaK present a distinctive fire protection challenge because their behavior differs
fundamentally from that of ordinary combustible materials and from the scenarios typically
addressed in conventional fire protection standards. When released from system boundaries, these
alkali metals can react rapidly with air and moisture, producing intense local heat release,
chemically reactive and corrosive aerosols, and combustion products that may threaten personnel,
equipment, ventilation systems, and structural materials [1, 2]. Depending on the release conditions
and geometry, the event may develop as a pool fire, a spray fire, a combination of pool and spray
fires, localized burning, or reactions with surrounding materials. Additionally, because sodium-
water interactions can intensify the event through additional heat generation and hydrogen
production, water-based suppression is generally unsuitable [1, 2].

The technical basis for sodium fire protection is therefore centered on limiting the likelihood and
consequences of sodium-air or sodium-reactant interaction. In practice, this leads to a defense-in-
depth approach that emphasizes prevention of leakage, limitation of spill spreading, reduction of
oxygen availability where appropriate, prompt detection and isolation, use of compatible
suppression methods, and protection of structures, systems, and components from thermal and
aerosol effects [8, 9]. Key protective features commonly associated with this approach include
robust sodium boundaries, guard piping or secondary containment, catch pans and collection
features, inerted or oxygen-controlled spaces where warranted, leak and fire detection systems, dry-
powder or inert-gas-based suppression approaches, and measures to protect steel and concrete
surfaces exposed to sodium fire conditions [4].

Protection of sodium facilities must also account for the fact that fire consequences are strongly
scenario dependent. Important factors include leak size, sodium temperature, release mode, room
geometry, oxygen availability, ventilation conditions, and the performance of detection, isolation,
and mitigation systems. These factors influence not only whether ignition occurs, but also the
resulting fire type, thermal loads, pressure effects, aerosol production, and the extent of damage to
nearby equipment or barriers [3]. As with conventional fire protection, these considerations affect
both physical protection features and associated operational programs; however, unlike typical
combustible fire hazards, the available database for sodium fires is limited and the analytical basis
for simulating these phenomena remains comparatively in need of further development. As a result,
the design and evaluation of sodium fire protection systems typically relies on a combination of
established engineering principles, conservative assumptions, and facility-specific assessment of
plausible hazard progression.

Recent Argonne work for advanced reactor applications reinforces this need for facility-specific
assessment. Development of sodium fire analysis capabilities for the Versatile Test Reactor (VTR)

           Introduction | Background | Modernization Strategy | Major Updates | Summary            5

supported evaluation of postulated leak scenarios, including associated temperatures, pressure
response, and aerosol generation, while related methodology work has framed protection system
design within an integrated defense-in-depth strategy [3, 4]. These efforts do not replace the
established engineering principles of sodium fire protection, but they strengthen the technical basis
for applying those principles in modern facilities by linking them more directly to mechanistic
analysis and design-specific consequences.

At the same time, uncertainties remain in important areas such as leak source characterization,
sodium aerosol behavior, fire progression, and interactions between combustion products and
confinement or ventilation features [3, 4]. Accordingly, the technical basis for sodium fire
protection must recognize both established good practice and the limits of current modeling and
data. This is one of the main reasons that modernization of ANS-54.8 is needed. The standard must
provide practical guidance while also allowing graded, flexible application that reflects facility-
specific hazards, uncertainties, the varying significance of different fire scenarios, and future
improvements in sodium fire modeling and simulation.

2.2 ANS-54.8 Relationship to Other Standards
ANS-54.8 is intended to complement, rather than replace, the broader set of fire protection codes,
standards, and regulatory requirements applicable to nuclear and industrial facilities. As reflected
in its scope and limits of application, the standard focuses on those aspects of fire prevention and
protection that are unique to sodium leak or fire events and are not addressed in sufficient detail
elsewhere. It, by itself, is not sufficient to serve as a comprehensive facility-wide fire protection
standard for all hazards, but rather as a sodium-specific standard that works in conjunction with
other applicable requirements.

ANS-54.8 interfaces with a broader set of fire protection, structural, and regulatory documents that
provide the general framework within which its sodium-specific provisions are applied. As
summarized in Table 2-1, National Fire Protection Association (NFPA) standards such as NFPA
10, NFPA 70, and NFPA 72 provide the baseline requirements for extinguishers, electrical systems,
and fire alarm and signaling systems, while ANS-54.8 adds the additional provisions needed for
reactive metal hazards. Similarly, 10 CFR Parts 20 and 100, ACI 349, and guidance such as
NUREG-1934 provide relevant regulatory, structural, and risk-informed context for the application
of the standard.

Accordingly, ANS-54.8 functions as a specialized overlay within the broader fire protection and
regulatory framework. General fire protection requirements remain governed by other applicable
codes, standards, and regulations, while ANS-54.8 provides the additional sodium-specific
requirements and guidance needed to address the distinctive hazards posed by these systems. Where
conflicts arise, the requirements of ANS-54.8 are intended to take precedence only for those
portions of fire protection systems or features specifically related to sodium. At the same time, the
standard does not preclude the use of new methods or devices, provided that sufficient technical
basis or experience is available to demonstrate an equivalent level of safety and reliability.

3 Modernization Strategy
The previously published and withdrawn ANS-54.8-1988 standard [5] reflected the sodium
technology base, facility arrangements, and fire protection practices available at the time it was
developed. It was organized around a conventional prescriptive framework emphasizing general
design objectives, fire protection features, detection and alarm systems, and inspection, testing, and
maintenance provisions. Figure 3-1 illustrates the overall logic of this earlier framework through
its main sections. In general, the structure was straightforward and deterministic, with each section
specifying the applicable fire protection provisions and expectations, but with limited
accommodation for graded applications or alternative technical bases. This approach was consistent
with the state of sodium fire protection knowledge and practice at the time and was well suited to
the LMR concepts and hazard understanding that informed the original standard.

 Figure 3-1: Logical structure of the previously published ANSI/ANS-54.8-1988 standard.

Building on that foundation, the modernization of ANS-54.8 has been structured as a staged effort
intended to restore the near-term usability of the standard while also establishing a path for broader
technical and methodological revision. This approach reflects both the immediate need for an active
sodium fire protection consensus standard and the recognition that the earlier framework does not
fully accommodate the diversity of modern sodium facilities, current analytical capabilities, or
contemporary expectations for graded/streamlined and RIPB approaches [7]. The staged strategy
therefore preserves the useful aspects of the earlier standard while creating a path for more
substantial updates in structure, scope, and implementation philosophy.

3.1 Strategic Approach
To balance urgency with technical depth, the ANS-54.8 update has been pursued through two
stages, as described below. This approach was developed in response to stakeholder
recommendations that ANS-54.8 be made available for current use as soon as possible, since no

             Introduction | Background | Modernization Strategy | Major Updates | Summary              8

equivalent consensus standard existed and the withdrawn document continued to be referenced in
practice [7].

 • Stage 1 focused on the rapid reissuance of the withdrawn ANSI/ANS-54.8-1988 standard
 with only minor targeted edits. The purpose of this stage was to restore an active consensus
 standard as quickly as practical while preserving the technical basis and overall structure of
 the earlier version. Accordingly, the changes made in this stage were intentionally limited
 to editorial cleanup, essential clarifications, and narrow scope-related refinements needed
 to support current application of the document. These changes included the following:

 o Updating the document to the modern ANS standards template and formatting
 conventions;

 o Revising the front matter and publisher information;

 o Correcting typographical, grammatical, and other minor editorial issues;

 o Reviewing and updating definitions and terminology for consistency with current
 ANS usage;

 o Clarifying the scope to focus more explicitly on sodium fire hazards; and

 o Making limited reference-related updates needed to support near-term use,
 including clarification of the reference section language.

 In late 2025, the reissued version of the standard, ANSI/ANS-54.8-2025, was officially
 released [10].

 • Stage 2 builds on the reissued baseline established in ANSI/ANS-54.8-2025 [10] and
 undertakes a broader modernization of the standard. This stage includes substantial
 modifications and updates to the structure and technical basis of the standard. Further details
 are provided in Chapter 4.

3.2 Regulatory and Stakeholder Engagement
A notable feature of the ANS-54.8 modernization effort has been the continued involvement of
both regulators and other stakeholders throughout the consensus standards process. In particular,
participation by the NRC has been important not only for technical feedback during development,
but also for supporting future regulatory usability and possible endorsement efforts of the standard.
Early NRC engagement helps identify issues of terminology, scope, structure, technical basis,
requirements, and implementation approach while the document is still being developed, rather
than after consensus approval has already been completed. For a specialized standard such as ANS-
54.8, this is especially valuable because the document is intended to inform future licensing-related
evaluations in an area where regulatory precedent remains limited.

Early NRC engagement is important because it can improve the efficiency of possible regulatory
endorsement or use of the standard in regulatory guidance, while maintaining a clear distinction

           Introduction | Background | Modernization Strategy | Major Updates | Summary           9

between participation and approval. Early regulatory engagement during standards development
can provide several benefits, including:

 • Improved familiarity and understanding of the standard’s structure, terminology,
 assumptions, and implementation concepts as they evolve;

 • Earlier identification of areas needing clarification, stronger traceability, or additional
 technical support from the regulatory perspective;

 • More effective consideration of evolving concepts such as graded application,
 implementation logic, and RIPB approaches;

 • Better preparedness for future regulatory review, endorsement, or use of the standard;

 • Improved understanding of the institutional and procedural considerations associated with
 NRC participation in external consensus standards working groups; and

 • Practical lessons on how such participation can support future endorsement activities for
 other standards.

Within the ANS-54.8 effort, NRC participation has therefore functioned as an avenue for early
technical exchange during development. This has been particularly important because it can support
a more efficient endorsement process at a later stage.

Industry and technical stakeholder participation has been similarly important and is an expected
feature of the consensus standards development process. Vendors, designers, consultants,
laboratory staff, and other subject matter experts have contributed through the Working Group and
related review activities. Their involvement has helped ensure that the revised standard remains not
only technically grounded, but also practical and usable across a range of sodium facilities and
applications. More broadly, the ANS-54.8 effort illustrates the value of continuous engagement
during standards development, particularly in specialized technical areas where legacy knowledge,
modern design needs, and evolving regulatory expectations must be integrated.

As an illustration of this ongoing engagement, Figure 3-2 summarizes the comment review process

4 Major Revision and Restructuring (Stage 2)
Stage 2 constitutes a substantive revision of and major update to ANS-54.8. The major updates can
be grouped into four broad areas:

 • Structural reorganization of the standard to improve internal alignment, readability, and
 implementation;

 • Expansion and clarification of the technical basis and scope, including broader applicability
 beyond the historical LMR framing;

 • Introduction of a streamlined, graded RIPB implementation framework to support
 proportional treatment of sodium fire hazards; and

 • Modernization of supporting content, including definitions, appendices, references, and
 explanatory material.

These changes collectively reposition the standard from a largely prescriptive legacy document to
a more structured and adaptable framework for modern and forward-looking sodium fire protection.

4.1 Revised Structure of the Standard

One of the major Stage 2 changes is the restructuring of the document itself. The revised draft
adopts a more streamlined structure built around the overall implementation process (summaries in
Section 4.2). Following the introductory material, the document includes:

 • An explicit implementation process, to guide application of the standard through a
 structured and graded sequence of evaluation steps;

 • A section on general, high-level fire protection attributes, to establish the foundational
 safety objectives and design basis for sodium fire protection;

 • A structured fire hazard evaluation process, to identify analysis units 2, screen hazards, and
 support detailed quantitative evaluation;

 • Updated requirements and recommendations for fire protection features, to reflect current
 design needs and available protective measures;

 • Updated provisions for detection and alarm systems, to support timely identification of
 leaks and fire events and enable appropriate response;

 • Updated requirements for testing, inspection, maintenance, and quality assurance, to ensure

 • An optional section on asset risk management, to allow consideration of financial and

 • A section on documentation, to ensure traceability of assumptions, analyses, credited

This revised organization aims to make use of the standard more straightforward. It more clearly
connects hazard identification and evaluation to the selection of protection features and the
supporting programs needed to ensure their effectiveness.

4.2 Summary of the Implementation Process
A central feature of Stage 2 is the implementation process introduced in the revised draft standard.
As shown in Figure 4-1, this process provides a structured and streamlined approach for applying
the standard across different facility types and sodium fire scenarios. It is intended to support either
deterministic or RIPB approaches, depending on the significance and complexity of the analysis
unit being considered.

At a high level, the implementation process consists of the following steps:

 1. Establish general fire protection attributes, which define the overall fire protection basis and
 objectives for the facility.

 2. Identify analysis units and fire scenarios, which establish the set of facility areas or hazard
 progression scenarios to be evaluated.

 3. Perform qualitative screening, which identifies analysis units that may require further
 evaluation without initially crediting specific fire protection features.

 4. Perform quantitative screening, which applies preliminary quantitative criteria to the
 retained analysis units to determine whether more detailed evaluation is warranted.

 5. Identify appropriate fire protection features, based on the hazards associated with each
 analysis unit and the results of the screening process.

 6. Conduct quantitative evaluation, using either a deterministic or RIPB approach with
 credited fire protection features.

 7. Evaluate detection and alarm systems, to ensure that they support the selected fire protection
 strategy.

 8. Evaluate testing, inspection, maintenance, and quality assurance programs, to ensure
 continued reliability and effectiveness of credited features.

4.3 Fire Hazard Screening and Evaluation
The fire hazard assessment introduced in Stage 2 is one of the major methodological changes in the
revised standard. Rather than relying only on fixed prescriptive requirements, the revised draft
establishes a structured process for identifying and screening analysis units, selecting appropriate
performance metrics, and evaluating whether credited fire protection features are sufficient for the
hazards present.

A key aspect of this update is the use of screening to make the process both more streamlined and
more graded. Analysis units that can be screened out early through qualitative or preliminary
quantitative evaluation may require little or no further safety-focused analysis, allowing attention
to be concentrated on those with greater potential safety significance. In this way, the level of
evaluation and the extent of credited fire protection are made more proportional to the hazard being
addressed.

The revised framework also distinguishes between analysis units that are initially identified as risk-
significant and those that can be shown to be low risk, either inherently or through credited fire
protection features. Higher-significance analysis units proceed to more detailed quantitative
evaluation, whereas lower-risk units may be addressed through a more limited application of the
standard when supported by an adequate technical basis.

The detailed quantitative evaluation can be performed using either a deterministic approach or an
RIPB approach.

 • Deterministic approach: This approach relies on conservative assumptions and bounding
 conditions to demonstrate that the credited fire protection features are adequate. It typically
 uses worst-case or otherwise limiting scenarios to show that fire protection objectives can
 be met without explicitly considering scenario likelihoods.

 • RIPB approach: This approach allows the evaluation to incorporate both performance-
 based analysis and, where appropriate, risk-informed insights. A performance-based
 approach focuses on whether the credited features can achieve specified safety outcomes
 under defined conditions and may use percentile-based or best-estimate analyses to evaluate
 performance. A risk-informed approach supplements this by considering the relative
 likelihood and significance of scenarios, together with explicit treatment of uncertainty, to
 support a more realistic and proportionate application of fire protection measures.

Overall, this streamlined, graded structure is intended to improve proportionality and reduce
unnecessary conservatism, while still ensuring that all relevant sodium fire hazards are addressed
appropriately.

4.4 Updates to Fire Protection Features and Supporting Provisions
Stage 2 updates and improves the requirements on fire protection features. The revised draft
includes provisions for a broader and more explicit range of features. The following features are
newly introduced as standalone provisions with explicit requirements, whereas the earlier standard
either addressed them only indirectly or embedded them within broader discussions:

 • Dry-powder suppression systems;

 • Fire suppression decks;

 • Guard pipe or double-walled piping;

 • Insulation and leak jackets; and

 • Collection tanks and vaults.

The following features were largely retained from the earlier standard but revised, clarified, and
reorganized to improve usability, technical consistency, and alignment with the updated
implementation framework:

 • Inert atmospheres;

 • Inert gas flooding systems;

 • Protection of concrete and steel structures;

 • Concrete structures;

 • Steel liners;

 • Catch pans;

 • Sodium/NaK dumping;

 • Materials for protective barriers;

 • Portable extinguishers and personnel safety;

 • Smoke control; and

 • Controls on water and other reactive materials.

In addition, the revised draft more clearly connects these features to the hazard evaluation process
and to the general fire protection attributes established earlier in the document. Detection and alarm
systems, as well as testing, inspection, maintenance, and quality assurance provisions, are similarly
treated as integral parts of the overall protection strategy.

4.5 Other Stage 2 Updates
In addition to the major structural and methodological changes, Stage 2 includes several supporting
updates intended to improve clarity and usability. These include:

 • Editorial cleanup and formatting improvements to align the document with current ANS
 standards template and conventions;

 • Updated definitions and acronyms to reflect current terminology and newly introduced
 concepts, such as the addition of “analysis unit” and refinement of “fire compartment” and
 “cell,” among others;

 • Revised Appendix A that consists of supplementary and explanatory material and
 demonstrative examples to reflect the expanded scope, updated technical content, and
 suggested implementation process;

 • Addition of Appendix B that provides a quick-reference guide for users navigating the
 revised structure which connects the suggested implementation process steps to the
 corresponding sections of the standard; and

 • Updated references to replace withdrawn or outdated standards with their current editions,
          such as replacing ACI 349-85 with ACI 349-23, NFPA 72D-1986 and NFPA 72E-1987
          with NFPA 72-2025, and NFPA 70-1987 with NFPA 70-2026.

These changes help align the document with current ANS formatting and terminology practices
and make the revised standard easier to navigate and apply.

5 Summary and Key Takeaways
ANS-54.8 has been successfully reissued as ANSI/ANS-54.8-2025 with minor, targeted edits to
restore near-term usability, achieving this milestone within approximately 13 months of the
Working Group kickoff meeting. Stage 2 is actively modernizing the standard through substantive
revisions to its structure, technical basis, scope, and implementation approach.

Key outcomes and observations from this effort include the following:

 • The staged approach effectively balanced the urgency of restoring an active consensus
 standard with the depth needed for meaningful modernization.

 • The introduction of a streamlined, graded RIPB framework that addresses recognized
 limitations in legacy prescriptive approaches and supports flexible, forward-looking, and
 proportional application across a range of sodium facilities and analyses.

 • Continuous NRC engagement throughout the development process, rather than only at the
 endorsement stage, has strengthened the technical quality of the draft and may support more
 efficient future regulatory review and use.

 • The structured comment review process has maintained transparency and momentum
 during revision efforts.

 • Several fire protection features have been newly introduced as standalone provisions, while
 others have been revised and reorganized to improve clarity and alignment with the updated
 framework.

Stage 2 review activities remain ongoing, with additional Working Group and NRC review rounds
expected before the draft advances to the formal ANS subcommittee and consensus committee
ballot process. Publication of the fully revised standard is anticipated in late 2027.

Looking ahead, a few areas merit continued attention as the standard progresses toward finalization
and future revisions:

 • Ensuring that the scope of the standard adequately supports both domestic and international
 applications, given the growing global interest in sodium technologies and sodium test
 facilities. For example, this may include engaging international stakeholders and standards
 organizations to promote harmonization and broader adoption of the modernized standard.

 • Ensuring that the standard can accommodate a broad spectrum of sodium leak and fire
 modeling and simulation capabilities, including both established tools and emerging
 methods.

 • Monitoring early applications of the revised standard to identify practical implementation
 challenges and inform future maintenance cycles.
