Sleep quality and anxiety among final-year medical interns during a family medicine clerkship: a cross-sectional study with implications for primary care training
Authors: Elif Keskin Çolak, Erhan Şimşek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSleep problems and anxiety are common but often under-recognised concerns in primary care. Final-year medical interns, who are medical students undertaking their final year of supervised clinical training, represent an important group for sleep health research because they face clinical responsibilities and concerns related to night shifts, emergency department work, direct patient care, and career-related stressors. This study aimed to evaluate sleep quality and its association with anxiety among medical interns during a Family Medicine clerkship.MethodsThis single-centre cross-sectional study was conducted at Ankara Yıldırım Beyazıt University Faculty of Medicine between June 2023 and September 2023. Data were collected from medical interns during their Family Medicine clerkship. Participants with previously diagnosed psychiatric disorders or sleep disorders were excluded. Face-to-face questionnaires were used, including a sociodemographic and lifestyle characteristics form, the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index, and the Beck Anxiety Inventory. Descriptive statistics, comparative analyses, correlation analysis, and multivariable logistic regression analysis were performed to evaluate factors associated with poor sleep quality.ResultsA total of 219 medical interns were included. Poor sleep quality was identified in 59.4% of participants. The mean Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index score was 6.59 ± 3.36, the mean sleep duration was 6.73 h, and the mean Beck Anxiety Inventory score was 9.63 ± 8.45. Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index and Beck Anxiety Inventory scores were positively correlated (r = 0.320, p < 0.001). In the multivariable logistic regression model, higher BAI scores, sex, and self-reported concern about taking night shifts were associated with poor sleep quality. Each one-point increase in the BAI score was associated with higher odds of poor sleep quality (odds ratio [OR] = 1.075; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.021–1.131; p = 0.006), while self-reported concern about taking night shifts was associated with 2.48-fold higher odds (OR = 2.483; 95% CI: 1.160–5.317; p = 0.019). Female sex was associated with lower odds of poor sleep quality compared with male sex (OR = 0.513; 95% CI: 0.265–0.994; p = 0.048); however, this finding was less robust in sensitivity analyses and should be considered exploratory.ConclusionPoor sleep quality was common among medical interns and was associated with higher anxiety symptoms and self-reported concern about taking night shifts. These findings support consideration of sleep health and anxiety-related concerns within Family Medicine training. However, the cross-sectional design does not permit causal inference, and intervention studies are needed to determine whether educational, screening, or supportive approaches improve interns’ well-being or future clinical practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12875-026-03500-5.

Background

Sleep quality is a multidimensional construct that includes subjective satisfaction, sleep duration, sleep latency, sleep maintenance, and sleep efficiency [1]. Poor sleep quality has important consequences for daytime functioning and is associated with a range of physical and mental health outcomes. Although objective methods such as polysomnography are available, they are costly and impractical for routine clinical use and large-scale epidemiological studies [2]. Therefore, the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) is widely used as a valid and reliable instrument for assessing subjective sleep quality [3].

Anxiety is closely related to the anticipation of potential future threats and uncertainty, and may involve psychological, physiological, and behavioural responses [4]. Although adaptive anxiety can support preparation for challenging situations, excessive or persistent anxiety is associated with impaired functioning and represents a major mental health concern [5]. Sleep disturbances and psychosocial stressors are closely interconnected, and their relationship may be bidirectional [6].

Medical students are considered a high-risk group for poor sleep quality and anxiety because of academic demands, clinical responsibilities, and chronic psychosocial stress. This risk may be particularly pronounced among final-year medical students undertaking supervised clinical internship training, hereafter referred to as medical interns, who are exposed to clinical responsibilities, emergency department work, night duties, preparation for the national Medical Speciality Examination (TUS), and concerns about mandatory state service and future career plans [7, 8]. Although young adults are generally advised to sleep 7–9 h per night, many medical students do not meet this recommendation, especially during clinical training [9, 10].

Previous studies have reported high rates of poor sleep quality and anxiety among medical students. In a BMC Research Notes study from Saudi Arabia, poor sleep quality was common among medical students and was associated with poorer mental health and academic performance [11]. Similarly, a BMC Medical Education study reported that sleep deprivation and daytime sleepiness among medical students were associated with poorer quality of life, poorer perception of the educational environment, and depression and anxiety symptoms [12]. In Türkiye, previous research has shown that intern physicians may experience sleep disturbances that impair daily cognitive and physical functioning [13].

Primary care has an important role in the early recognition and longitudinal management of both sleep problems and anxiety. However, these conditions are often underreported by patients and insufficiently addressed during routine primary care consultations [14, 15]. In this context, the Family Medicine clerkship offers a relevant educational setting in which future physicians can both experience and learn to recognise sleep- and anxiety-related problems within a primary care framework.

Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate subjective sleep quality, examine its association with anxiety levels, and identify sociodemographic, lifestyle, and clinical training-related factors associated with poor sleep quality among medical interns during a Family Medicine clerkship.

Methods

Study design and setting

This descriptive, single-centre, cross-sectional study was conducted at Ankara Yıldırım Beyazıt University Faculty of Medicine between June 2023 and September 2023. Data were collected from medical interns during their Family Medicine clerkship, a clinical training period involving supervised patient care, emergency department work, night duties, and primary care-oriented clinical learning.

Participants and sampling

The target population consisted of approximately 300 medical interns enrolled in the final year of the 2023–2024 medical education programme at the study institution. The minimum required sample size was calculated to be 169 participants, assuming a 95% confidence level, a 5% margin of error, and a 50% response rate.

A convenience sampling approach was used, and participation was voluntary; no probability-based sampling method was applied. A total of 235 medical interns were reached and assessed for eligibility. Fourteen interns with a known psychiatric disorder and two interns with a previously diagnosed sleep disorder were excluded. No participant was excluded because of incomplete questionnaire data. The final analytic sample therefore included 219 participants, corresponding to 73.0% of the approximately 300 medical interns in the target population.

The inclusion criteria were being a medical intern enrolled in the final year at the study institution and providing written informed consent. The exclusion criteria were refusal to participate, incomplete questionnaire data, and any pre-existing physician-diagnosed psychiatric or sleep disorder.

Data collection

Data were collected through face-to-face administration of a structured questionnaire package during the Family Medicine clerkship. Before participation, all medical interns received written and verbal information about the purpose of the study, voluntary participation, confidentiality of data, and their right to withdraw. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The questionnaire package consisted of three sections: an investigator-designed form for sociodemographic and lifestyle characteristics, the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), and the Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI). The investigator-designed form was developed specifically for this study and has not previously been published; its English-language version is provided as Supplementary File 1. The PSQI and BAI were previously published and validated instruments, and their original and Turkish validation references are cited below. The average completion time was approximately 15 min.

Measures

Sociodemographic and lifestyle characteristics form

Sociodemographic, lifestyle, and clerkship-related data were collected using an investigator-designed form developed specifically for this study. The form included items on age, sex, body mass index, perceived income level, family history of psychiatric illness, household sleep problems, eating before bedtime, electronic device use before sleep, regular physical exercise, smoking, smoking before bedtime, caffeinated beverage consumption, self-rated sleep pattern, feeling rested upon waking, and nightmares. Participants were also asked, in a multiple-response item, which situations caused them the greatest concern; response options included working in the emergency department and taking night shifts. These variables therefore represented self-reported concerns and did not measure actual shift frequency, duration, workload, or exposure. A separate multiple-response item asked participants which factors they believed most impaired their sleep quality. This form has not previously been published. The English-language version of the investigator-designed form is provided as Supplementary File 1.

Sleep quality

Subjective sleep quality over the previous month was assessed using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), developed by Buysse et al. [16]. The PSQI includes 19 self-rated items grouped into seven components: subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, habitual sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, use of sleep medication, and daytime dysfunction. Each component is scored from 0 to 3, generating a global score ranging from 0 to 21. Higher scores indicate poorer sleep quality, and a global PSQI score greater than 5 was used to define poor sleep quality. The Turkish validity and reliability study of the PSQI was conducted by Ağargün et al. [17].

Anxiety symptoms

Anxiety symptoms were assessed using the Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI), developed by Beck et al. [18]. The BAI is a 21-item self-report scale that evaluates the severity of anxiety symptoms. Each item is rated on a 4-point Likert scale from 0 to 3, producing a total score ranging from 0 to 63, with higher scores indicating greater anxiety severity. The Turkish validity and reliability study of the BAI was conducted by Ulusoy et al. [19].

Statistical analysis

Primary statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26.0. Descriptive statistics were presented as mean ± standard deviation, median, minimum–maximum values, frequencies, and percentages, as appropriate. The distribution of continuous variables was assessed using visual methods and normality tests. Since the main continuous variables were not normally distributed, non-parametric tests were used for group comparisons. The Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare two independent groups, and the Kruskal–Wallis test was used for comparisons involving more than two groups. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test. Correlations between continuous variables were assessed using Spearman correlation analysis. All 219 participants had complete data for the variables included in the reported analyses; therefore, no imputation was required.

Poor sleep quality, defined as a global PSQI score greater than 5, was used as the dependent variable in the multivariable logistic regression analysis. BAI score was included as the principal explanatory variable because the primary objective of the study was to examine the association between anxiety symptoms and sleep quality. Self-reported concern about taking night shifts, self-reported concern about working in the emergency department, electronic device use before sleep, and perceived income level were included based on their univariable associations with PSQI scores and their relevance to the study objectives. Sex, smoking status, caffeinated beverage consumption, and regular physical exercise were additionally included as clinically relevant covariates. Variables directly reflecting subjective sleep experience or participants’ perceptions of factors impairing their sleep were not entered because of their conceptual overlap with the PSQI outcome. Variable selection was therefore based on the principal research question, clinical relevance, univariable findings, and model parsimony rather than on statistical significance alone. Results were presented as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors for single-degree-of-freedom predictors and generalised variance inflation factors adjusted for degrees of freedom for multicategory predictors. Model stability was explored using a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis in which the multivariable model was refitted after excluding one participant at a time.

Ethical considerations

The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ankara Yıldırım Beyazıt University Ethics Committee on 17 May 2023 with decision number 115/05. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before inclusion in the study.

Poor sleep quality, defined as a PSQI score greater than 5, was identified in 59.4% of participants. The mean PSQI score was 6.59 ± 3.36, the mean sleep duration was 6.73 h, and the mean BAI score was 9.63 ± 8.45. PSQI and BAI scores were positively correlated (r = 0.320, p < 0.001), indicating that higher anxiety symptom scores were associated with poorer sleep quality.

In univariable analyses, higher PSQI scores were associated with lower perceived income, eating before bedtime, electronic device use before sleep, not feeling rested upon waking, poorer self-rated sleep pattern, self-reported concern about working in the emergency department, self-reported concern about taking night shifts, nightmares, and anxiety related to negative daily events. Higher BAI scores were associated with female sex, family history of psychiatric illness, smoking, smoking before bedtime, frequent caffeinated beverage consumption, electronic device use before sleep, lack of regular physical exercise, not feeling rested upon waking, poorer self-rated sleep pattern, and concerns about post-graduation placement, economic issues, and family-related problems. No significant correlations were found between PSQI scores and age, BMI, smoking amount, or time between smoking and bedtime. Similarly, BAI scores were not significantly correlated with age, BMI, smoking amount, or time between smoking and bedtime.

In the multivariable logistic regression model, BAI score, sex, and self-reported concern about taking night shifts were associated with poor sleep quality. Each one-point increase in the BAI score was associated with a 7.5% increase in the odds of poor sleep quality (OR = 1.075; 95% CI: 1.021–1.131; p = 0.006). Participants who selected taking night shifts as one of their main concerns in the multiple-response questionnaire item had higher odds of poor sleep quality than participants who did not select this response (OR = 2.483; 95% CI: 1.160–5.317; p = 0.019). This variable represented self-reported concern rather than actual night-shift exposure. Female sex was associated with lower odds of poor sleep quality compared with male sex in the primary multivariable model (OR = 0.513; 95% CI: 0.265–0.994; p = 0.048). However, the confidence interval was close to the null value, and the association was less stable in the leave-one-out sensitivity analysis; this finding was therefore considered exploratory. Self-reported concern about working in the emergency department, electronic device use before sleep, smoking status, caffeinated beverage consumption, regular exercise, and income level were not associated with poor sleep quality in the multivariable model (Table 3).

No evidence of problematic multicollinearity was identified; the maximum variance inflation factor was 1.43, and the adjusted generalised variance inflation factors for multicategory predictors were approximately 1.03. In the leave-one-out sensitivity analysis, 218 of 219 refitted models converged successfully. The associations involving BAI score and self-reported concern about taking night shifts remained statistically significant in all successfully refitted models, whereas the sex-related association remained statistically significant in 117 of 218 models.

Discussion

This study found that poor sleep quality was common among medical interns during a Family Medicine clerkship, affecting more than half of the participants. PSQI scores were positively correlated with BAI scores. In the multivariable model, higher BAI scores and self-reported concern about taking night shifts were associated with poor sleep quality, while the sex-related association was less robust and was considered exploratory. These findings describe concurrent associations and do not establish causal or temporal relationships.

The prevalence of poor sleep quality in this study was 59.4%, which is consistent with previous research showing that sleep problems are highly prevalent among medical students. A global review reported that sleep disturbances are common among medical students across different countries and educational systems [20]. Similarly, studies published in BMC journals have shown that poor sleep quality, sleep deprivation, and daytime sleepiness are associated with poorer mental health, lower quality of life, impaired academic functioning, and negative perceptions of the educational environment among medical students [11, 12]. Previous research from Türkiye has also shown that intern physicians may experience sleep disturbances that impair daily cognitive and physical functioning [13]. The present study adds to this literature by focusing specifically on final-year interns during a Family Medicine clerkship, a period in which clinical responsibilities, career-related uncertainty, and primary care-oriented learning intersect.

A key finding of this study was the association between anxiety symptoms and poor sleep quality. The positive correlation between PSQI and BAI scores is consistent with previous literature describing a potentially bidirectional relationship between sleep disturbances and psychosocial stressors [6], but the present cross-sectional study cannot determine the direction of this association. In the multivariable model, each one-point increase in the BAI score was associated with higher odds of poor sleep quality. Medical interns may experience anxiety related to clinical responsibilities, preparation for the national Medical Speciality Examination, mandatory state service, and future career plans [7, 8]; however, the present findings indicate co-occurrence rather than a causal effect of anxiety symptoms on sleep quality.

Self-reported concern about taking night shifts was associated with poor sleep quality. This variable was derived from a multiple-response item asking participants which situations caused them the greatest concern. It should therefore not be interpreted as a measure of actual night-shift frequency, duration, workload, or exposure. The observed association indicates that interns who identified taking night shifts as a concern were more likely to have poor sleep quality; however, the cross-sectional design does not establish whether this concern preceded, resulted from, or shared common determinants with poor sleep.

Female sex was associated with lower odds of poor sleep quality in the primary multivariable model, whereas female participants had higher BAI scores in the univariable analysis. The confidence interval for the multivariable estimate was close to the null value, and the association was less robust in the leave-one-out sensitivity analysis. This apparently divergent pattern may reflect residual confounding, differences in the relationships between sex, anxiety symptoms, and sleep quality, or sampling variability. Given the cross-sectional design and the limited robustness of the estimate, the finding should be considered exploratory and should not be interpreted as evidence that female sex has a protective causal effect on sleep quality. Larger multicentre and longitudinal studies are required to clarify possible sex-related differences.

Several lifestyle and psychosocial characteristics were associated with PSQI or BAI scores in univariable analyses, including electronic device use before sleep, eating before bedtime, perceived income, smoking-related variables, caffeinated beverage consumption, physical exercise, and family- or economic-related concerns. These findings should be interpreted descriptively because several associations did not remain statistically significant in the multivariable model and the cross-sectional design does not establish directionality. They nevertheless illustrate the range of behavioural and psychosocial characteristics that may accompany sleep and anxiety symptoms in medical interns.

The findings are relevant to Family Medicine training because sleep complaints and anxiety symptoms are encountered in primary care and may coexist with behavioural, psychological, and occupational concerns [14, 15, 21, 22]. The observed association between PSQI and BAI scores may provide a basis for discussing the relationship between sleep and mental health within a primary care educational framework. However, the present study did not assess interns’ clinical knowledge, counselling skills, patient-care performance, or the effect of any educational intervention.

The PSQI and BAI were used in this study as validated research instruments to characterise sleep quality and anxiety symptoms in the study sample [3, 16–19]. The findings support consideration of sleep health and anxiety-related concerns as possible topics within Family Medicine training, but they do not establish that routine screening, counselling, or supportive programmes would be effective. The feasibility, acceptability, confidentiality, referral requirements, and potential benefits and harms of such approaches should be evaluated in prospective intervention studies.

The study has several strengths. First, it focused on medical interns, a group exposed to multiple academic, clinical, and career-related stressors. Second, the study was conducted during the Family Medicine clerkship, allowing the findings to be interpreted within a primary care training framework. Third, validated Turkish versions of the PSQI and BAI were used to assess sleep quality and anxiety symptoms. Finally, multivariable logistic regression allowed adjusted associations with poor sleep quality to be examined.

However, several limitations should be considered. First, the cross-sectional design prevents causal inference and does not establish the temporal direction of the associations between anxiety symptoms, self-reported concerns, and poor sleep quality. Second, convenience sampling and voluntary participation may have introduced self-selection bias. Medical interns who were more concerned about their sleep or anxiety symptoms may have been more willing to participate, which could have affected the estimated prevalence of poor sleep quality and the observed associations. Although the final sample represented 73.0% of the target population, this participation rate does not eliminate the possibility of selection bias. Third, the study was conducted at a single medical faculty in Türkiye; differences in curricula, duty schedules, institutional support, workload, and healthcare systems may limit the generalisability of the findings to other institutions or countries. Sleep quality and anxiety symptoms were assessed using self-report instruments, which may be affected by recall or social desirability bias. Self-reported concern about taking night shifts and working in the emergency department was derived from a multiple-response questionnaire item and did not measure actual frequency, duration, workload, or exposure. Several categorical levels included small numbers of participants, particularly no caffeinated beverage consumption, caffeinated beverage consumption several times per month, no electronic device use before sleep, and inadequate perceived income. Estimates for these categories had wide confidence intervals and should be considered imprecise. Although no problematic multicollinearity was identified, sensitivity analyses indicated that the sex-related estimate was less robust than the associations involving BAI score and self-reported concern about taking night shifts. Accordingly, the sex-related finding and estimates derived from sparse categorical levels should be interpreted as exploratory. Finally, residual confounding from unmeasured factors such as detailed duty schedules, academic workload, chronotype, social support, and other institutional characteristics may remain.

Conclusions

Poor sleep quality was common among medical interns and was associated with higher anxiety symptoms and self-reported concern about taking night shifts. These findings support consideration of sleep health and anxiety-related concerns within Family Medicine training. However, the cross-sectional design does not permit causal inference, and intervention studies are needed to determine whether educational, screening, or supportive approaches improve interns’ well-being or future clinical practice.
