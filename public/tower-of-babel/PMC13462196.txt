Patient perception of robotic-assisted arthroplasty: how confidence in expected outcomes influences recovery
Authors: Nick D. Clement
Published: 2026

The recent study by Cozowicz et al. (1) highlighted the increased costs associated with hip and knee arthroplasty from perioperative complications and reducing these would be paramount to improving patient outcomes and reducing expenditures in arthroplasty surgery. Current orthopaedic practice is influenced not only by evidence and national guidance, but also by surgeons’ beliefs and attitudes towards innovation (2,3). Including the perception that newer technologies may offer superior patient outcomes, sometimes in advance of robust supporting evidence (2,3). Few technologies have embodied this more recently than robotic-assisted arthroplasty, with increasing adoption year on year (4). The data-driven robotic systems with enhanced precision in implant placement are a prospect that is particularly appealing to orthopaedic surgeons and promises improved outcomes for patients (5-7). However, when subjected to the methodological rigour of randomised controlled trials (RCTs), the evidence becomes more ambiguous (8-10). Radiographic accuracy improves, but early clinical outcomes often do not match this (5,6). The gap between what patients feel and what trials prove invites an uncomfortable but necessary question: are we witnessing a potential placebo effect, and if so, should we care?

Robotic-assisted arthroplasty improves surgical precision. Alignment, component positioning, and variability are reliably better (5-7). For example, meta-analyses of RCTs demonstrate superior anatomical and mechanical alignment with robotic total knee arthroplasty (5,6). Similarly, studies in hip arthroplasty show reduced variability in leg length and component positioning (7). These improvements do not translate directly into clinically meaningful improvements in outcomes that matter most to patients. Pain scores, functional scores, complication rates, and satisfaction are often not statistically different between robotic and conventional techniques (5-7). Even where differences exist, they are often small, inconsistent, or below the threshold of minimal clinically important difference (11). This creates a paradox where the surgeon is demonstrably better at doing the operation, but not clearly better at improving the functional outcome of the patient in a clinically meaningful way (12). This is according to the currently defined clinically meaningful differences in patient-reported outcome measures, which may not be the most ideal measure (13).

Outside RCTs, however, a different picture emerges. Observational studies and registry analyses report improved early recovery, better patient-reported outcomes, and even psychological benefits associated with robotic surgery (14,15). Numerous unblinded studies have found improvements in early ambulation and mental health domains, such as vitality and perceived well-being (16,17). Therefore, the potential benefit of robotic assistance may lie not only in the improved precision but in the patient’s perception of it. Unlike pharmacological interventions, surgical trials are inherently challenging to blind, as masking of surgeons is impossible, and patient blinding is often difficult to achieve (18). Patients frequently believe they know whether robotic technology was involved in their surgery, with high levels of awareness and expectation reported in arthroplasty cohorts, despite often limited or inaccurate understanding of the technology (19). The surgeon obviously knows. The “intervention”, therefore, includes not just the operation, but the narrative surrounding it (20). This is where the RCTs attempt to strip away this narrative, isolating the effect of the intervention of interest (21). But in doing so, this may also remove a meaningful component of the patient’s experience and confidence in their potential outcome (10,11,22).

The placebo effect is not imaginary; it is neurobiologically real (23). Patient expectations are known to influence clinical outcomes (24), with evidence demonstrating that positive expectations can reduce perceived pain, enhance reported treatment efficacy, and improve satisfaction (25). Highlighting the important role of placebo mechanisms in both symptom perception and recovery, and in surgery, this effect can be particularly potent. The more technologically advanced, expensive, or novel an intervention appears, the greater the expectation of benefit (26). Robotic surgery, with its promise of precision engineering and cutting-edge innovation, is perfectly designed to amplify patient and probably surgeon expectations. Patients may believe they are receiving a “better” operation, being more accurate, more personalised, and more modern. That belief could translate into reduced perceived pain, greater engagement with rehabilitation, higher satisfaction scores and more positive reporting of outcomes. In unblinded settings, these effects may result in improved patient-reported outcome measures (15,27). In blinded or tightly controlled RCTs, where expectation is neutralised, the advantage may not be observed (11,28). This is not evidence that robotic surgery does not work, but that part of its beneficial effect may be psychological rather than mechanical.

The instinctive reaction in evidence-based medicine may be to discount placebo effects as bias, being noise to be eliminated (18). But this view may be too simplistic, especially in elective procedures like arthroplasty, where the primary goals are pain relief and improved quality of life. If a patient feels better, functions better, and reports higher satisfaction, does it matter whether the mechanism is biomechanical precision or due to the psychological positive expectation? Is a placebo effect necessarily a flaw of robotic-assisted surgery, or could it be an additional beneficial feature?

Medicine has long grappled with the ethics of the placebo (29). Deception is generally considered unacceptable (29). Patients must give informed consent, and clinicians should not mislead them during this process. However, robotic surgery does not require deception. The technology is real; the precision gains in implant placement are real. The psychological benefit arises not from a falsehood, but from the patient’s interpretation. Patients are told that the robot will improve the accuracy of their surgery, and it does. If they infer that this means better outcomes, the resulting expectation may enhance their recovery. This is arguably an ethically permissible placebo, given the evidence-based truth. This controversial point was overlooked in a recent review assessing the ethical landscape of robot-assisted surgery (30).

From the perspective of the National Health Service (NHS), the key issue is whether robotic-assisted surgery offers value (31). Robotic systems are expensive, with capital costs, maintenance, training, and longer operative times all adding up to greater financial cost (32). If clinical outcomes are equivalent, such technology may be difficult to justify. But this calculation may be incomplete. If the psychological effect of robotic surgery leads to fewer postoperative complaints, reduced follow-up visits, lower demand for postoperative imaging or investigation(s) for those who may not be “happy” with their arthroplasty, better adherence to physiotherapy, and fewer revision surgeries driven by dissatisfaction (33). This may translate into downstream savings that could offset the upfront costs of robotic-assisted surgery (34). These potential benefits may be difficult to capture in traditional RCT endpoints. They lie in patterns of healthcare utilisation, patient behaviour, and subjective well-being domains often under-measured in surgical trials. Additionally, certain groups may benefit more from the placebo effect, with individual traits such as higher optimism being more likely to lead to stronger positive placebo responses (35). Patients who value high-tech interventions, or those with higher anxiety about surgical accuracy, may particularly respond to this effect. Understanding which patients are more influenced by the perception of cutting-edge technology could help tailor preoperative counselling and set realistic expectations. Given the strain on healthcare systems to lower outpatient and increasing revision burdens after arthroplasty (36), interventions that patients believe improve outcomes may offer practical value, even when objective physiological improvements are modest.

The deeper issue is conceptual. Evidence-based medicine prioritises objective endpoints: implant survival, complication rates, and patient-reported outcome measures (12). These are essential, but they do not encompass the whole story. Arthroplasty is, fundamentally, an intervention to improve an individual’s quality-of-life (37,38). Pain, mobility, and satisfaction are subjective experiences shaped by both biology and psychology (23). If a technology reliably enhances those experiences, even partly through expectation, then it is exerting a real therapeutic effect. The dichotomy between ‘real’ and ‘placebo’ effects may therefore be misleading, as treatment outcomes arise from complex, interacting factors that cannot be cleanly separated (39).

None of this is to suggest or to support the uncritical adoption of robotic surgery in orthopaedics (31). There are legitimate concerns: increased operative time in some settings, capital and maintenance costs (40), learning curves (41), workflow disruption and the major possibility that commercial marketing outpaces evidence. There is also a danger that belief in the technology could compensate for deficiencies in surgical skill or decision-making. Precision is valuable, but only if it is directed toward the correct target (42-44). A point noted in clinical discussions where improved accuracy does not guarantee improved patient-reported outcomes despite faster return of symmetrical gait and improved range of motion (45). The most reasonable position may be neither wholesale embrace nor outright dismissal. Instead, robotic-assisted arthroplasty should be understood as a complex intervention that combines technical precision, data-driven planning, surgeon ergonomics, and patient perception and expectation. Certain patient subgroups may derive disproportionate benefit from robotic-assisted arthroplasty (46), but these effects can be diluted or lost in RCTs that include all patients presenting for arthroplasty. For example, patients with high preoperative anxiety, those particularly concerned about surgical accuracy, or individuals who value advanced technology may experience enhanced confidence, reduced perceived pain, or higher satisfaction due to the perceived sophistication of the technology (23,25). Similarly, patients with complex anatomy or alignment issues may achieve clinically meaningful improvements that are not apparent when averaged across a heterogeneous trial population (47,48). Recognising these subgroups is important, as it highlights the potential value of robotic-assisted surgery, which may extend beyond standard outcome measures, and tailored patient selection could maximise both objective and subjective benefits (49). The design of an RCT is well-suited to isolating the effect of the individual intervention but may be less capable of capturing the full system effect. Future research could therefore expand beyond traditional endpoints. Pragmatic trials, registry data, and health economic analyses may better capture the real-world impact, including psychological and behavioural dimensions.

The possibility that robotic-assisted arthroplasty derives part of its benefit from a placebo effect may not be a weakness of the technology but a reflection of the human context in which medicine operates. Patients are not passive recipients of surgical interventions, such as arthroplasty, and are active participants whose beliefs, expectations, and perceptions shape outcomes. A robot, by symbolising precision and technological progress, may influence those beliefs in ways that improve an individual’s recovery. If that translates into better patient experiences and potentially lower long-term costs, healthcare systems should not dismiss these benefits. The challenge is not to eliminate the possible placebo effect, but to understand and harness it, ethically, transparently, and in conjunction with genuine technical excellence. Unlike traditional placebo-controlled surgical trials, which compare an inactive sham procedure to an active intervention (50), here both arms receive an effective therapeutic procedure. The addition of a more precise intervention may confer objective benefits, but it may also influence outcomes through psychological mechanisms such as patient expectations or confidence. In the end, the success of arthroplasty may depend as much on the mind as on the machine.
