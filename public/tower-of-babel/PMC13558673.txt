Pyrite-assisted mechanochemical leaching of chalcopyrite concentrate in sulfuric acid under non-oxidative conditions
Authors: Mohammad Tahami, Vahideh Shojaei, Mahin Schaffie, Mohammad Ranjbar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Copper extraction from chalcopyrite remains challenging due to its refractory nature and the formation of passivation layers during hydrometallurgical processing. This study investigates the mechanisms of passivation and explores mechanochemical leaching (MCL) as a strategy to enhance copper dissolution. Experiments conducted on flotation concentrate demonstrated that MCL significantly accelerates leaching, achieving a copper recovery of 27.3% under optimized conditions (3 mm beads, 310 rpm, and a 1:1 grinding media-to-liquid ratio) within 3 h, which is 70% higher than non-mechanochemical leaching. Kinetic analysis revealed two stages: an initial chemically controlled phase followed by a diffusion-limited regime, with MCL reducing activation energies by 16–21% and enhancing leaching rates by 2–6-fold. SEM–EDS confirmed the removal of passivation layers and exposure of fresh chalcopyrite surfaces. Furthermore, using pyrite as a reactive grinding medium improved copper recovery through combined mechanical abrasion and galvanic effects, achieving approximately 40% recovery in 6 h, over twice that of non-mechanochemical leaching. Overgrinding (d₈₀ ≤ 11 μm) limited long-term leaching, highlighting the importance of controlling particle size. These findings highlight the potential of MCL, particularly with reactive grinding media, to enhance chalcopyrite leaching. However, the observed recoveries indicate that industrial application will require further studies, including overgrinding control and selective addition of oxidants. Future work will focus on periodic MCL and integration of oxidants to improve copper recovery under scalable conditions.

Introduction

Currently, approximately 80% of the world’s copper is produced using pyrometallurgical methods. Hydrometallurgical processing, applied to low-grade ores containing copper oxides or secondary copper sulfides, accounts for about 20% of the annual production. However, pyrometallurgical processes are consistently associated with environmental issues due to their high operating temperatures1–3. In contrast, hydrometallurgical processing of copper is considered more environmentally friendly and can be more economically viable in terms of initial investment4.

Sulfate-based solutions are standard in copper production due to low costs and extensive industrial experience with solvent extraction–electrowinning5. However, chalcopyrite is highly stable under hydrometallurgical conditions, particularly in sulfate media, because surface passivation limits copper release despite initially high leaching rates6–8. Proposed passive layers include metal-deficient sulfides, polysulfides, elemental sulfur, and jarosite9, while some recent reviews emphasize the role of chalcopyrite’s semiconducting properties in influencing leaching behavior10. Chalcopyrite’s high lattice energy (17,500 kJ/mol) compared to pyrite (4,260 kJ/mol), covellite (3,785 kJ/mol), and chalcocite (2,935 kJ/mol) explains its resistance to dissolution11. Chalcopyrite leaching without strong oxidants is slow and inefficient due to the formation of sulfur-rich passivating layers, resulting in low copper recovery even after prolonged leaching44–46. Considerable research has therefore focused on introducing oxidants such as ferric ions, hydrogen peroxide, or nitrate to enhance dissolution kinetics30,44,47,48. More recent studies have also considered unconventional non-aqueous solvents, such as ionic liquids and deep eutectic solvents49. Meanwhile, alternative strategies aim to enhance leaching under oxidant-free or mild conditions50.

Mechanical activation (MA) and mechanochemical leaching (MCL) are effective in improving chalcopyrite dissolution. MA, achieved via pre-milling in planetary mills with 5–20 mm sized beads, disrupts the crystal lattice, reduces grain size, and creates structural defects, significantly increasing copper recovery51–57. This method operates under ambient conditions while reducing environmental impact and effectively treating complex ores51,58. However, industrial application faces challenges such as high energy use, equipment wear, and the need for precise optimization59–61. Mechanochemical methods combine mechanical energy with chemical reactions to further enhance extraction, particularly for refractory minerals like chalcopyrite62. MCL significantly improves copper recovery at ambient pressure and low temperature, offering an efficient and cost-effective approach63.

Recent studies highlight the potential of mechanochemical treatments, including silica-assisted leaching and attrition-aided processes, to remove passive layers and boost leaching rates64,65. Orbital shakers, which mimic vibratory mills, provide a practical tool for preliminary mechanochemical studies51,66. Collectively, these advancements underscore the promise of mechanochemical methods as sustainable and efficient alternatives for copper extraction, paving the way for future industrial applications.

Bai et al.52 reported that mechanical activation alone yielded negligible copper recovery (~ 5%) in sulfuric acid without an oxidant; only upon addition of 0.1 mol/L ferric sulfate did recovery reach 55.7%, and a secondary mechanical activation cycle was required to achieve 91.8% — highlighting that even with high-energy planetary milling, passivation layers remain a persistent bottleneck that cannot be overcome by mechanical activation alone. Similarly, Dakkoune et al.65 achieved over 60% recovery via attrition-aided leaching, but only under strongly oxidizing conditions (Eh = 700 mV) at 42 °C over 48 h. Mechanical activation has achieved recoveries up to 48.9% at 80 °C over extended leaching periods55. Despite these advances, most existing studies have relied on high-energy milling equipment and/or strong oxidants to achieve meaningful copper recoveries, limiting their direct industrial applicability. Furthermore, the influence of grinding media characteristics — such as size, quantity, and reactivity — on leaching kinetics and passivation behavior has not been systematically investigated. Studies employing low-to-moderate energy setups under non-oxidative conditions — more representative of scalable industrial operations — therefore remain scarce, and the factors governing leaching performance under such constraints are not yet well understood. This is particularly important for chalcopyrite leaching in sulfate media, where passivation layers strongly inhibit copper dissolution without strong oxidants. In this work, mechanochemical leaching (MCL) is evaluated as a practical and environmentally friendly approach to mitigate passivation and enhance chalcopyrite reactivity through controlled mechanical activation. In addition to conventional inert media, pyrite—often available as a waste material—is investigated as a dual-function medium providing both abrasive and galvanic effects, offering insights into improved copper recovery under mild, oxidant-free conditions.

Materials and methods

The methodology of this study was designed to systematically investigate the mechanochemical leaching (MCL) behavior of chalcopyrite concentrate under non-oxidant conditions and to identify the key mechanisms governing dissolution enhancement. To achieve this, the research was structured into several interconnected stages, as illustrated in the workflow diagram (Fig. 1).

Following established practices in solids characterization67, a preliminary microscopic study of the chalcopyrite concentrate was conducted using scanning electron microscopy coupled with energy-dispersive spectroscopy (SEM–EDS). This analysis aimed to identify passivation features, examine surface morphology and composition changes during leaching, and establish the mechanistic motivation for employing the mechanochemical approach. Based on these observations, a controlled experimental domain was defined, and a full factorial design was implemented to optimize the main MCL parameters, including bead size, shaking speed, and the grinding media-to-liquid (G/L) ratio.

Fig. 1Schematic workflow of the experimental procedure.

Schematic workflow of the experimental procedure.

Following the optimization stage, kinetic leaching experiments were performed under both MCL and non-MCL conditions to compare dissolution rates, determine kinetic regimes, and calculate activation energies using established reaction models.

Finally, an additional experimental set was introduced to evaluate the role of pyrite as a reactive grinding medium. This step aimed to reveal the galvanic-assisted enhancement mechanisms operating within mechanochemical systems.

Together, these sequential stages form a coherent and integrated methodology, enabling a comprehensive assessment of the chemical, mechanical, and electrochemical factors influencing chalcopyrite leaching efficiency.

Materials

In this study, 10 kg of chalcopyrite concentrate and a pure natural chalcopyrite sample from the main Iranian Copper Mine were used. This bulk sample was used throughout all experiments, with 10 g of concentrate employed per individual leaching test. The samples were dried at 80 °C for 72 h. The particle size distribution (d₈₀ ≈ 30 μm) was determined by laser particle size analysis (PSA) using an Analysette 22 NanoTec (Fig. 2). Phase identification was performed by X-ray diffraction (XRD) using a Philips PW1800 diffractometer, and microscopic observations were conducted using a field-emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM, KYKY EM-8000 F). The chemical composition of the samples was analyzed by peroxide digestion followed by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES, Varian 735). Copper concentration in the leachate was measured using atomic absorption spectroscopy (AAS-240) with an accuracy of 1 ppm. XRD analysis confirmed that the concentrate primarily consists of chalcopyrite and pyrite (Fig. 3), and the chemical composition of the concentrate (Table 2) indicated copper, iron, and sulfur contents of 20.4%, 29.0%, and 32.9%, respectively.

Methods

Microscopic study

All leaching experiments were performed in a Kuhner Shaker X Lab Therm LT-X incubator with a 25 mm shaking amplitude. To assess the mechanistic motivation for mechanochemical leaching (MCL), a preliminary SEM–EDS characterization of a pure natural chalcopyrite sample was conducted to identify surface passivation features. The sample was sectioned into three parts: one for baseline SEM–EDS, and the other two subjected to leaching, one under static conditions for 30 min, and the other with 10 mL of glass beads under MCL for an additional 10 min. Both samples were dried at 30 °C for 24 h in a conventional oven prior to SEM–EDS analysis, to preserve surface morphology and avoid thermally induced alterations to the leached surfaces. This comparative study provided direct evidence of the mechanochemical disruption of passivation layers and enhanced chalcopyrite surface reactivity.

Experimental design and procedure

Leaching tests were performed in 500 mL autoclavable bottles in an orbital shaker incubator, ensuring uniform agitation and precise temperature control. Each test used 100 mL of leaching solution (Table 4) and 10 g of chalcopyrite concentrate, with the cap kept closed. Glass beads were employed to investigate mechanochemical effects.

Three operational factors were optimized via a full factorial design (27 runs, 3 h each): bead diameter (3–5 mm), orbital shaking speed (230–310 rpm), and G/L ratio (0.5–1.5). Preliminary tests guided the selection of these ranges: beads < 3 mm caused high viscosity and agglomeration, beads > 5 mm reduced contact area; shaking speeds < 230 rpm produced insufficient collisions, speeds > 310 rpm led to overgrinding; G/L ratios < 0.5 caused poor mixing, ratios > 1.5 restricted bead motion. These intervals ensured effective mechanical energy transfer, impact, and abrasion during leaching.

Kinetic analysis

To study the kinetics of the process, non-MCL and MCL experiments were performed at 65, 70, 75, and 80 °C under the optimal conditions obtained from the previous stage within 6 h. The selection of a 6 h duration was based on preliminary kinetic tests, which showed that key differences between MCL and non-MCL conditions are best observed within this timeframe for studying kinetic behavior and passivation evolution.

Effect of pyrite as a grinding medium

To evaluate the galvanic effect of pyrite, a pure sample of pyrite was crushed to below 6 mm (− 3 mesh) and incubated in water at 230 rpm for 3 h to smooth sharp edges (Fig. 5). The particles were then sieved to 2.8–3.35 mm (mesh 6–7) to match the optimized size for glass beads. Kinetic leaching tests were conducted for 6 h using a pyrite-to-chalcopyrite mass ratio of 3:1 (30 g pyrite, 10 g chalcopyrite) with a G/L ratio of 0.1 to assess pyrite’s influence as a reactive grinding–galvanic medium.

A pyrite-to-chalcopyrite ratio of 3:1 was selected because pyrite has a much higher density than glass beads, and using the same mass ratio as the optimized inert-media condition would have required an impractically large amount of pyrite. Therefore, a reduced and literature-consistent ratio was adopted to allow effective mechanical contact while evaluating the galvanic effect.

EDS point analyses (Table 6) reveal two distinct surface regimes on the treated chalcopyrite. Spectrum 1 demonstrates a near-stoichiometric composition (Cu 23.42 ± 0.35 at%, Fe 24.07 ± 0.54 at%, S 48.92 ± 0.72 at %) with a Cu/Fe ratio of 0.97 ± 0.03, consistent with ideal CuFeS₂. These results confirm the complete removal of the passive layer in mechanochemically regenerated zones. The minimal oxygen content (1.17 ± 0.57 at %) likely represents superficial re-oxidation during sample drying.

Spectrum 2 exhibits characteristic signatures of the passive layer, including severe copper depletion, sulfur enrichment, and significant oxygen incorporation. The approximately 11-fold increase in oxygen relative to Spectrum 1, coupled with the retention of iron (14.45 ± 0.21 at%), indicates the formation of a composite passivation film composed of primary sulfur species and secondary iron oxyhydroxide phases. The consistently low silicon content (< 1 at %) in both spectra suggests minimal silicate contamination, likely resulting from minor abrasion of the glass milling media during mechanochemical processing.

The G/L ratio exhibits a non-linear influence on chalcopyrite leaching performance. At low G/L ratios, limited bead–particle collisions result in insufficient mechanical disruption of the passivation layer and consequently slower copper recovery. Increasing the G/L ratio enhances mechanical energy transfer, promotes abrasion of passivated surfaces, and accelerates leaching kinetics. However, beyond an optimal value, further increases in G/L lead to excessive overgrinding, which reduces the frequency of effective bead–particle interactions and ultimately limits leaching efficiency. Therefore, an optimal G/L ratio is required to balance mechanical activation with particle size control and achieve maximum copper recovery.

Kinetic study

This study investigates the impact of mechanochemical treatment on chalcopyrite leaching kinetics, with a specific focus on determining the activation energy and analyzing the reaction mechanism. The narrow temperature range (65–80 °C) was chosen based on two key criteria: proximity to the optimal leaching temperature (80 °C), to avoid potential mechanistic deviations, and the temperature-sensitive nature of the dissolution process itself. Sokić et al.14 investigated the leaching of chalcopyrite using sodium nitrate in sulfuric acid. Their findings indicate a two-stage leaching process: an initial stage that is controlled by surface reactions, followed by a diffusion-controlled stage through a passivating sulfur layer. In this study, a comprehensive evaluation of other shrinking-core and diffusion–reaction mixed models was performed; however, none of them were able to satisfactorily represent the entire leaching period with statistically acceptable fits. In several cases, the deviation between the model predictions and the experimental data was sufficiently large that meaningful reporting of kinetic parameters was not feasible. This behavior reflects the intrinsic transition in the rate-controlling mechanism during chalcopyrite leaching, where a single kinetic model cannot simultaneously capture both the initial surface-reaction dynamics and the subsequent diffusion through the developing sulfur layer.

To objectively determine the boundary between the two regimes, different time-split candidates were statistically assessed. The interval at 30 min consistently produced the lowest fitting errors and the highest internal consistency within each subset, confirming that this point provides the most appropriate separation between the chemical-controlled and diffusion-controlled stages. Therefore, the two-stage kinetic treatment is not an arbitrary choice but is statistically justified by the inability of individual models to reproduce the full leaching trajectory and by the optimized partitioning obtained through systematic error minimization. The kinetic analysis (Fig. 13) reveals two distinct regimes: (1) an initial chemical-controlled phase (Fig. 13a, c) where the reaction rate depends primarily on temperature (0–30 min), and (2) a subsequent diffusion-limited stage (Fig. 13b, d) where particle morphology becomes dominant (30–360 min). The two-stage leaching behavior is particularly noteworthy as the initial chemically controlled regime (Stage I) dominates dissolution when reactant diffusion and product removal remain uninhibited. However, progressive passive layer formation triggers a kinetic transition to diffusion-controlled dissolution (Stage II), where mass transport through the growing surface barrier becomes rate-limiting. The kinetic parameters from rate analysis (Table 9) show that MCL (Fig. 13c, d) has a notably increased reaction rate in both the diffusion-limited (5–6×) and chemical-controlled (1.5–2×) regimes when compared with non-MCL (Fig. 13a, b). This observation suggests that the mechanochemical effect significantly reduces the diffusion barrier.

During the initial stage (0–30 min), dissolution in both systems was dominated by chemical control due to the abundance of fresh chalcopyrite surfaces, as reflected by the steep initial slope in Fig. 14. As leaching progressed, the formation of sulfur-rich passive layers shifted the rate-limiting step toward diffusion control, particularly in the non-MCL system, where particle size stabilized at around 25 μm. In contrast, the superior performance of MCL cannot be attributed solely to particle size reduction. Continuous mechanical interactions disrupt the developing passivation layers, intermittently exposing reactive mineral surfaces and sustaining higher dissolution rates.

The transition from chemical to diffusion control observed in MCL after the initial stage indicates that passivation still governs the leaching kinetics, but its impact is mitigated by mechanical action. This demonstrates that MCL enhances chalcopyrite dissolution through a combined effect of increased surface area and continuous removal of passivation layers, rather than particle refinement alone. Nevertheless, copper recovery remains limited in the absence of strong oxidizing agents. Accordingly, future work will focus on strategies to avoid over-grinding while integrating suitable oxidants within a mechanochemical framework to achieve higher and industrially relevant copper recoveries.

Effect of pyrite on copper recovery

Based on the preceding results, copper dissolution in the mechanochemical leaching system was found to nearly stagnate after several hours. This behavior is most likely attributed to over-grinding, where excessive particle refinement reduces the effectiveness of mechanical impacts. Under such conditions, collisions between the grinding media and concentrate particles become insufficient to remove the passivation layer or to further activate chalcopyrite surfaces.

To assess whether a reactive grinding medium could overcome this limitation—and to evaluate the potential galvanic contribution—pyrite particles were used in place of glass beads during the mechanochemical process. Considering that copper extraction in the standard MCL system showed minimal improvement beyond 6 h, the pyrite-assisted experiments were also carried out for the same duration.

Figure 15 compares copper recoveries obtained after 6 h under three different conditions. Copper recoveries of approximately 18%, 33%, and 40% were achieved for conventional leaching, mechanochemical leaching (MCL) with glass beads, and pyrite-assisted MCL, respectively. These results demonstrate that pyrite does not merely act as a physical grinding medium capable of disrupting the passivation layer, but also plays an electrochemically active role. The galvanic interaction between pyrite and chalcopyrite facilitates electron transfer, thereby enhancing the dissolution rate and leading to a significant improvement in copper recovery compared to both conventional leaching and glass-bead-assisted MCL. In addition to its mechanically abrasive function, pyrite enhances chalcopyrite dissolution through galvanic interactions by acting as a cathodic partner. The primary contribution of pyrite in this system appears to be its galvanic activity rather than serving solely as a source of ferric ions. Measurements of the solid mass during the experiments indicated negligible pyrite loss (within ~ 1%); the minor observed changes are attributed mainly to particle fragmentation during MCL, which could not be separated from the remaining chalcopyrite concentrate. Moreover, the solution Eh measured after leaching decreased by approximately 8%, indicating that the system became more reducing rather than more oxidizing. This behavior suggests that significant ferric ion generation from pyrite dissolution is unlikely under the investigated conditions. Nevertheless, the role of dissolved species such as ferric and ferrous ions cannot be completely ignored; however, within the operational window examined, the galvanic effect appears to play a more significant role.

To evaluate the maximum effect of pyrite on copper recovery, selected experiments were extended to 24 h; however, no further increase in dissolution was observed. This behavior indicates that overgrinding is a key factor limiting chalcopyrite leaching, as excessive particle refinement reduces effective collisions between the concentrate and the grinding media, leading to stagnation of copper recovery. These findings highlight the importance of controlling particle size and mechanical conditions during MCL.

In the present study, strong oxidizing agents were intentionally not employed in order to clearly isolate and assess the effects of MCL and pyrite. To mitigate overgrinding and further enhance copper dissolution in future studies, periodic MCL operation—applying short mechanochemical activation steps intermittently—along with the controlled incorporation of suitable oxidants, can be explored while maintaining practical and scalable operating conditions.

Conclusions

This study advances the mechanistic understanding of chalcopyrite leaching and demonstrates that mechanochemically assisted leaching (MCL) markedly enhances copper extraction through multiple synergistic pathways. MCL promotes the continuous disruption of sulfur-rich and oxyhydroxide passivation layers by generating microstructural defects and repeatedly restoring reactive mineral surfaces. As a result, leaching kinetics are accelerated by a factor of 2–6, leading to a shortened chemically controlled stage and an earlier transition to diffusion-controlled behavior, accompanied by a 30–35% reduction in activation energy during this regime. Although diffusion control still governs the later stages of leaching, its inhibitory effect is substantially mitigated under MCL conditions due to ongoing mechanical removal of the evolving passivation layer, in contrast to uninterrupted passivation during conventional leaching.

The use of pyrite as a reactive grinding medium further enhanced copper dissolution by combining abrasive removal of surface films with pronounced galvanic interactions, resulting in significantly higher recoveries compared to inert media under identical conditions. However, excessive particle refinement (d₈₀ ≤ 11 μm) was shown to reduce the efficiency of media–particle interactions, thereby limiting long-term leaching performance due to overgrinding.

The optimal mechanochemical leaching conditions identified in this study — comprising 3 mm bead diameter, a G/L ratio of 1, and a shaking speed of 310 rpm — achieved a copper recovery of 27.3% within 3 h, representing a 70% improvement over non-MCL conditions. Further incorporation of pyrite as a reactive grinding medium under these conditions yielded approximately 40% recovery within 6 h, more than doubling the recovery achieved by conventional leaching. By linking mechanistic insights with practical parameter optimization, this work demonstrates the potential of mechanochemically assisted leaching to improve chalcopyrite dissolution under non-oxidative conditions. Nevertheless, the maximum copper recoveries achieved (≈ 40%) indicate that direct industrial implementation would require further research, particularly to control overgrinding and to incorporate suitable oxidizing agents. Future studies will therefore focus on periodic MCL operation combined with controlled oxidant addition, building upon the mechanistic framework established here to move toward industrially relevant recovery levels.
