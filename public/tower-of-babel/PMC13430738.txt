How resilience and age shape the link between negative life events and suicidal ideation in clinically depressed adolescents: a multicenter study
Authors: Kexin Xu, Shimeng Ma, Xin Zhang, Hongbo He, Jingchu Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMood disorders and related suicidal ideation among adolescents are pressing global health concerns. Although negative life events (NLEs) are recognized risk factors, the psychological mechanisms underlying the transition from adversity to suicidal ideation (SI), particularly in clinically depressed adolescents, remain unclear. This study is grounded in the vulnerability‒stress framework and investigates the developmental and psychological pathways linking NLEs and suicidal ideation in a clinical sample of depressed adolescents.MethodsA total of 341 adolescents (aged 10–19) with clinically diagnosed depression were recruited from five psychiatric hospitals in China via a multicenter design. The participants completed self-report questionnaires assessing NLEs, resilience, and suicidal ideation. A moderated chain mediation model was used to test whether resilience and depressive symptoms sequentially mediate the relationship between NLEs and SI and whether age moderates this pathway.ResultsNLEs were associated with lower resilience, which predicted greater depressive symptoms and ultimately higher SI. Furthermore, age significantly moderated the relationship between depressive symptoms and SI. Simple slope analysis confirmed that depressive symptoms had a stronger predictive effect on SI in younger adolescents.ConclusionOur findings reveal a critical chain of vulnerability: negative life events were associated with lower resilience and higher depressive symptoms, which in turn predicted suicidal ideation. The significant moderating role of age suggests that this pathway is sensitive to development and is especially potent in younger adolescents. This multicenter study provides compelling evidence of the importance of fostering resilience and treating depressive symptoms as key targets in suicide prevention efforts, especially through interventions tailored to different age groups.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-026-08140-8.

Introduction

Suicide is one of the leading causes of death worldwide [1]. Suicidal ideation (SI) refers to a broad range of contemplations, wishes, and plans regarding self-inflicted death [2]. It is generally regarded as the initial stage in the development of suicidal behavior and serves as a critical psychological indicator for predicting subsequent suicidal plans and attempts [3]. Crucially, the ideation-to-action framework [2] proposes that the development of suicidal ideation and the progression to suicidal attempts are distinct processes governed by different risk factors. According to this framework, factors like psychological pain and hopelessness—which are closely linked to the negative life events and resilience depletion examined in this study—primarily drive the development of ideation. This issue is particularly severe and complex among adolescents, as this developmental stage is characterized by intensive identity formation, still-maturing neurobiological systems for emotion regulation, and heightened sensitivity to social evaluation and peer rejection [4, 5]. Within the framework of developmental psychopathology, these evolving processes can amplify the impact of environmental stressors, making adolescents more prone to experiencing overwhelming psychological distress and perceiving suicide as a potential escape from such instability. The WHO has identified suicide as one of the three leading causes of death among young people [1]. In China, a recent meta-analysis reported pooled prevalence rates of suicidal ideation, suicide planning, and suicide attempts among adolescents of 15.4%, 6.4%, and 3.5%, respectively [6]. Notably, the reported prevalence in China is higher than the global 12-month prevalence of suicidal ideation among adolescents, which is estimated at approximately 14.2% [7]. This disparity suggests that Chinese adolescents may experience a particularly heightened vulnerability to suicidality, further emphasizing the critical need to identify the specific psychological pathways, such as the interplay between life events and resilience, that contribute to this risk within this cultural and clinical context.

Extensive research has shown that suicide ideation (SI) in adolescents is a complex, multifactorial process resulting from the interaction of various risk and protective factors. Regarding risk factors, multiple levels of influence have been identified. For example, depression [8], low self-esteem, neuroticism, and impulsivity significantly increase the risk of suicide ideation [9–11]. Adverse life events, such as family violence, low social support, negative life events (NLEs), and high academic pressure, play a prominent role at the social-environmental level and are closely linked to the occurrence of SI [9, 10, 12]. In contrast, protective factors, such as high resilience and self-esteem, as well as strong social support systems, like healthy family functioning and positive peer relationships, can effectively buffer the risk of suicide ideation (SI) [9, 13–15].

Among these influencing factors, three may be important in Chinese adolescents: depression as a strong internal risk factor, negative life events as key environmental triggers, and resilience as a core individual protective mechanism [16–18]. This salience is rooted in the unique developmental environment of Chinese adolescents. Cross-cultural research suggests that resilience may function as a more critical internal resource in collectivist societies, where its negative association with depression is often more pronounced than in Western samples [19]. Furthermore, the specific nature of negative life events in China—characterized by intensive academic competition within an exam-oriented system and interpersonal stressors tied to the cultural emphasis on social harmony—creates a pervasive stress environment. These cultural imperatives not only increase the prevalence of depressive symptoms but also necessitate a higher degree of psychological resilience to maintain mental health, making these three factors particularly vital for understanding suicidal ideation in this population [20]. Although previous studies have confirmed the independent associations of these factors with adolescent SI, the complex interplay among them remains insufficiently understood. This gap regarding the underlying mechanisms represents an important research void and provides the central motivation for the present study.

NLEs are a significant risk factor in adolescent development and exert considerable influence on psychosocial outcomes [21]. In this study, NLEs are defined as the cumulative burden of discrete, recent stressors—such as academic pressure and interpersonal conflicts—experienced within the past year, rather than chronic or early-life traumatic events. Extensive research has demonstrated a positive association between exposure to these recent NLEs and adverse mental health outcomes, including heightened negative emotions and the onset of depressive symptoms [22, 23]. Importantly, longitudinal and large-scale studies in Chinese adolescents support a mediational pathway from NLEs to suicidal ideation via depressive or emotional symptoms [16, 24]. Once depressive symptoms develop, they are recognized as one of the most potent risk factors for suicidal behaviors in adolescents [25, 26], with approximately 38% of depressed adolescents experiencing suicidal ideation [18]. This association is often understood through the lens of experiential avoidance, in which SI represents an attempt to escape unbearable internal distress [27]. These findings suggest a pathway linking negative life events (NLEs), depressive symptoms, and suicidal ideation (SI), indicating that NLEs may increase the likelihood of SI by impacting depressive symptoms. This highlights a potential mediating pathway. However, while the indirect effect of depressive symptoms is well-documented, few studies have integrated resilience as a dynamic mediator that may precede depression in this cascade or examined how these pathways are conditionally altered by developmental stages (i.e., age). By incorporating these factors, our study extends the current understanding from a simple mediation to a more nuanced, developmentally sensitive framework.

Drawing on prior findings that suggest a connection between NLEs, depressive symptoms, and SI, it is important to identify protective factors that may intervene in this sequence. Among all these protective factors, resilience could be a critical protective factor for adolescence, a developmental period characterized by high vulnerability to stress, and has been empirically shown to effectively buffer depressive symptoms and suicidal risk in this age group [28, 29]. Although resilience has been shown to act as a protective factor that reduces the adverse effects of NLEs on various negative outcomes [30], its mechanistic role as a mediator preceding depression within this hypothesized pathway remains under investigation.

Resilience, defined as the ability to adapt to and recover from adversity [31], is widely recognized as a key psychological resource that enables individuals to cope with stressors and buffer the impact of negative emotions [32]. Empirical evidence consistently demonstrates that greater resilience is associated with a lower probability and severity of suicidal ideation and reduced depressive symptoms [28, 33]. Although the negative association between resilience and depression is well-established [34], recent evidence further indicates that resilience mediates the effects of early adversity on depressive symptoms and promotes posttraumatic growth [35, 36]. However, these links have rarely been examined in clinically depressed adolescents, and the specific role of resilience in the pathway from NLEs to suicidal ideation remains under investigated.

Age plays a critical role in the transition of depressive symptoms into suicidal ideation, but the empirical findings remain mixed. Several studies report that suicide risk increases with age, with older adolescents exhibiting higher rates of suicidal ideation and behavior than their younger peers do [37–39]. This trend may reflect developmental processes that amplify the impact of depressive symptoms, including increases in comorbid psychopathology, greater engagement in risk-taking behaviors, and more advanced cognitive abilities that facilitate rumination [40, 41]. Conversely, some evidence suggests that younger adolescents may be particularly vulnerable. Following the lifting of COVID-19 restrictions in China, younger middle- and high-school students reported higher rates of suicidal ideation and suicide attempts than did emerging adults [42]. Similarly, a longitudinal study of depression treatment revealed that younger patients exhibited persistently elevated suicidal ideation relative to their older counterparts [43]. Collectively, these findings indicate that the age‒suicidal ideation link is not strictly linear, highlighting the importance of examining age as a moderator of the link between depressive symptoms and suicidal ideation in adolescents. We propose that these mixed findings may stem from distinct, age-dependent developmental mechanisms. While older adolescents often demonstrate greater behavioral capability, younger adolescents may be uniquely susceptible due to a ‘developmental mismatch’ (or maturity gap). In this stage, the rapid intensification of limbic-driven emotional reactivity often outpaces the structural and functional maturation of prefrontal cognitive control systems [44, 45]. This neurodevelopmental imbalance may render younger adolescents less equipped to regulate the intense psychological pain associated with depression. Consequently, we hypothesize that the association between depressive symptoms and suicidal ideation will be more pronounced in younger adolescents, as their relatively immature self-regulatory buffers may fail to contain the progression from emotional distress to suicidal thoughts.

Recent research applying the vulnerability‒stress framework has increasingly utilized sequential mediation models to understand complex risk pathways [46]. The vulnerability‒stress framework posits that psychological outcomes emerge from the interaction between individual vulnerabilities and environmental stressors [47]. For example, resilience has been identified as a key initial mediator that channels the effects of adverse experiences (such as childhood maltreatment or low life satisfaction) on subsequent outcomes such as coping styles and depressive symptoms [48, 49]. Furthermore, a similar sequential pathway has been validated in which depressive symptoms mediate the link between trauma (channeled through emotion regulation difficulties) and SI [50]. Collectively, these studies establish a strong precedent for a model where resilience acts as an initial protective factor and depressive symptoms act as a subsequent vulnerability.

Guided by the vulnerability-stress model [46], the present study proposes an integrated model in which NLEs are hypothesized to influence SI indirectly through depressive symptoms, with resilience potentially serving both as a mediator and as a moderator. In addition, we examine whether age moderates the indirect pathway such that the effects of depressive symptoms on SI may differ across developmental stages.

While extensive research has established the individual links between NLEs, resilience, depression, and SI, there is a notable gap in understanding the precise sequential, developmentally sensitive pathway through which these factors operate in concert. Few studies have simultaneously examined the full-chain mediation from NLEs through resilience and depression to SI. Crucially, this comprehensive mechanism has not been investigated in a large, multicenter clinical sample of depressed adolescents in China. Therefore, the present study aims to investigate (i) the associations between NLEs, resilience, depressive symptoms, age, and SI; (ii) the roles of resilience on the relationship between depressive symptoms, NLEs, and SI among clinically depressed adolescents; and (iii) the moderating role of age on the indirect effect of depressive symptoms on SI within this pathway. By addressing these gaps, the present study offers novel insights that can inform the development of more precise, developmentally sensitive intervention strategies aimed at mitigating suicide risk.

Method

Participants

This study was conducted between January 2022 and April 2024 at five psychiatric hospitals in Guangdong Province, China: Shenzhen Kangning Hospital, Guangzhou Medical University Affiliated Brain Hospital, Guangdong Mental Health Centre, The Fifth Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University, Guangdong Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine. The study protocol was approved by the institutional ethics committee (Approval Number: 2022 No. 031) and followed the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants and their legal guardians prior to participation.

Participants were recruited through consecutive sampling from both outpatient and inpatient psychiatric units. Eligible participants were adolescents aged 10–19 years, an age range falling within the World Health Organization’s (WHO) definition of adolescence. The participants also met the diagnostic criteria for unipolar depressive disorder according to the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10). Additional inclusion criteria included (1) willingness to participate in the study and (2) no major physical illnesses. The exclusion criteria included (1) a diagnosis of bipolar disorder or other major psychiatric disorders (e.g., schizophrenia) and (2) refusal to participate or incomplete assessment.

A total of 1,631 questionnaires were distributed. By April 7, 2024, 749 responses had been collected from adolescents aged 10–19 years. After data screening, 114 individuals diagnosed with bipolar disorder or other severe psychiatric disorders were excluded, along with 294 responses containing extensive missing data. The final sample comprised 341 adolescents with clinically confirmed depression. Comparing included (n = 341) and excluded (n = 294) participants showed no significant difference in gender (see supplementary Table 1). Although the excluded group was statistically older (p < 0.001), the mean difference was negligible (0.71 years), this minor age discrepancy was considered clinically and developmentally negligible in the context of our study’s age range (10–19 years), suggesting that the final analytic sample remains largely representative of the initial pool.

All participants completed a series of self-report questionnaires and demographic assessments via the “South China Adolescent Depression Data Platform” under the guidance of trained mental health professionals. Demographic and clinical data were collected by certified evaluators who followed standardized procedures to ensure the consistency and validity of the assessments. Only complete and logically coherent data were included in the final analysis.

Measures

Demographic variables

Basic demographic information was collected through self-report questionnaires. This information included age, sex, and family history of psychiatric disorders.

Negative life events

NLEs experienced in the past year were assessed via the Adolescent Self-Rating Life Events Checklist [51]. The scale consists of 27 items (e.g., “failing an exam or having unsatisfactory grades” and “being scolded or beaten by parents”) covering interpersonal conflicts, academic stress, health issues, and other stressors. Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (no impact) to 5 (extreme impact) on the basis of the perceived psychological effect. The total impact score was calculated by summing these ratings across all 27 items, with higher scores indicating a greater cumulative psychological burden from negative life events during the past year. This approach prioritizes the subjective severity of stressors over simple occurrence counts, aligning with the study’s focus on psychological vulnerability. The ASLEC demonstrated high internal consistency in this study, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.85.

Resilience

Resilience was measured via the self-report 10-item Mental Toughness Questionnaire (MTQ-10) [52], which assesses individuals’ ability to cope with stress and adversity (e.g., “Even under considerable pressure, I usually stay calm”) and has been shown to capture resilience across cultures effectively [53]. The MTQ-10 was selected as a resilience proxy due to its brevity, which reduced respondent burden in this clinical sample. It is a reliable measure of an adolescent’s capacity to withstand stress while maintaining adaptive functioning, which aligns with the functional definition of resilience employed in this study. The items are rated on a 5-point scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree), with higher scores indicating greater resilience. The MTQ-10 has shown good reliability and validity in Chinese adolescents. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.83.

Depressive symptoms

Depressive symptoms were assessed via the clinician-administered 17-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD) [54]. Each item is rated from 0 to 4, with higher total scores indicating greater severity of depression. All the interviews were conducted by trained mental health professionals. The Cronbach’s alpha for the HAMD in this study was 0.88. While 313 of the 341 participants (91.8%) scored at or above the standard clinical cutoff of ≥ 8, our primary inclusion criterion was a formal psychiatric diagnosis. Therefore, all participants were retained, and the HAMD-17 was used to provide a continuous measure of current symptom severity rather than for sample selection.

Suicidal ideation

Self-reported suicide ideation (SI) was measured via the self-report Beck Scale for Suicide Ideation (BSI) [55]. The instrument consists of 19 items. Following previous research emphasizing the first five items [55], the current study utilized the mean score of the first five screening items to assess SI intensity. These items were rated on a scale of 1 to 3, with higher scores reflecting more severe ideation. The Cronbach’s alpha for the BSI in this study was 0.86.

Data analysis

All the statistical analyses were conducted via IBM SPSS version 27.0. Apart from sex and family history of mental illness, all the study variables were continuous. Spearman correlation analysis was first performed to examine the relationships among negative life events, resilience, depressive symptoms, and SI. Prior to hypothesis testing, we systematically verified the assumptions required for regression-based modeling, including residual independence, multicollinearity, homoscedasticity, and multivariate normality. Specifically, the Durbin-Watson statistic was used to test independence, and Variance Inflation Factors (VIF) were examined to rule out multicollinearity. Scatterplots of residuals were inspected for homoscedasticity. To address potential deviations from normality and ensure robust estimation of mediation effects, we employed bias-corrected bootstrapping (5000 resamples) using the PROCESS macro version 4.2 [56]. We applied PROCESS Model 6 to test the sequential chain mediation and Model 58 to explore the potential moderating role of resilience. Finally, Model 87 was used to examine the moderating effect of age on the mediation pathway. Indirect and moderated effects were considered statistically significant if the 95% bias-corrected confidence interval (CI) did not include zero. For other analyses, significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Assumption Checks Prior to testing the mediation models, assumption checks confirmed the suitability of the data for multivariate analysis. The Durbin-Watson statistic was 2.13, falling within the acceptable range (1.5–2.5) and indicating residual independence. Multicollinearity was not a concern, with Variance Inflation Factors (VIF) for all predictors ranging from 1.02 to 1.31, well below the threshold of 5. Visual inspection of scatterplots confirmed homoscedasticity. Although the residuals approached a normal distribution, the use of bootstrapping provided robust confidence intervals for the subsequent analyses.

Harman’s single-factor test

To assess potential common method bias, Harman’s single-factor test was performed via unrotated principal component analysis. The results indicated that the first factor accounted for 17.71% of the total variance, which is well below the 40% threshold recommended by Podsakoff et al. [57]. This indicates that common method bias was not a major threat to the validity of the current findings.

The chain mediation model

Before examining the chain mediation effects, we tested whether resilience function as a moderator of the pathways from negative life events to depressive symptoms and suicidal ideation. The results in Fig. 1 indicate that none of the moderation effects of resilience were significant. While both moderation and mediation were initially considered, the lack of interaction suggests that in this clinical sample, resilience may not function as a stable buffer. Consequently, we proceeded to test the chain mediation model to examine whether resilience instead operated as a mediating mechanism within this specific context.

Table 3; Fig. 2 present the results of the chain mediation analysis examining the indirect effects of NLEs on SI. After controlling for gender and family history of mental illness, Table 3 shows that negative life events significantly predicted lower resilience (B = − 0.095, β = − 0.212, t = − 5.870, p < 0.001) and that resilience was negatively associated with depressive symptoms (B = − 0.354, β = − 0.198, t = − 5.730, p < 0.001). Negative life events also directly increased depressive symptoms (B = 0.121, β = 0.227, t = 6.250, p < 0.001). In the final model, both resilience (B = − 0.038, β = − 0.272, t = − 7.890, p < 0.001) and depressive symptoms (B = 0.026, β = 0.236, t = 6.340, p < 0.001) significantly predicted suicidal ideation, whereas the direct effect of life events was nonsignificant (B = 0.000, β = 0.004, t = 0.190, p = 0.847).

Table 4 further indicates that the total indirect effect of life events on suicidal ideation was significant (Effect = 0.008, BootSE = 0.001, 95% CI [0.006, 0.010]). Specifically, three indirect pathways emerged: through resilience (Effect = 0.004, BootSE = 0.001, 95% CI [0.002, 0.006]), through depressive symptoms (Effect = 0.003, BootSE = 0.001, 95% CI [0.002, 0.005]), and through the sequential pathway of resilience and depressive symptoms (Effect = 0.001, BootSE = 0.000, 95% CI [0.000, 0.001]). To evaluate whether the specific types of negative life events influenced the mediation model differently, we conducted supplemental analyses for each of the six ASLEC factors. The results (see Supplementary Table 2) demonstrated that the direct and indirect pathways were largely consistent across stress domains, particularly for interpersonal and academic-related stressors, confirming the robustness of the chain mediation framework.

Moderated chain mediating effect analysis

To address our third research aim, we controlled for gender and family history of mental illness and examined whether age would moderate the sequential mediation pathway. The moderated mediation analysis was conducted, and the results are presented in Table 5; Fig. 3. The results showed that age significantly moderated the pathway from depressive symptoms to SI (interaction β= −0., p = 0.03, 95% CI [− 0.205, − 0.009]). Additionally, we explored whether age moderated the upstream pathways involving resilience (i.e., NLEs → Resilience and Resilience → Depressive symptoms). However, supplemental analyses revealed no significant moderating effects for these pathways (ps > 0.05), suggesting that the structural relationships in the initial stages of the model were stable across the age range of our sample. Additional sensitivity analysis further confirmed the robustness of our results; even after removing the suicide item from the HAMD total score, the interaction effect between depressive symptoms and age on suicidal ideation remained statistically significant (p = 0.028, β = -0.112), ruling out the possibility of tautological inflation. A simple slope analysis was conducted to investigate the significant moderating effect of age on the relationship between depressive symptoms and suicidal ideation (SI). As shown in Fig. 4, depressive symptoms had a significantly stronger predictive effect on SI in the younger age group than in the older age group.

Discussion

This multicenter study examined how resilience and age influence the psychological process linking negative life events to suicidal ideation among adolescents with depression. The findings provide important insights into the mechanisms underlying suicidality in this population. Overall, our findings provided strong support for the proposed moderated chain mediation model. The results revealed that the influence of negative life events on suicidal ideation was fully mediated by resilience and depressive symptoms. This finding offer partial support for the vulnerability-stress framework, revealing a distinct mechanism for resilience in this clinical context, and offering specific empirical support for the Interpersonal Theory of Suicide (IPTS) [58]. Supplemental analyses revealed that among the various negative life events, interpersonal stressors (e.g., social isolation, peer conflict) emerged as the primary drivers of the mediation pathway. This aligns with the IPTS construct of ‘Thwarted Belongingness,’ where a perceived lack of social integration serves as a potent trigger for psychological distress. In the context of our model, these interpersonal failures appear to initiate a ‘loss spiral’—first eroding the individual’s resilience, which in turn predicts depressive symptoms, and ultimately associating with suicidal ideation. This integration underscores that for clinically depressed adolescents, the pathway to suicide may be paved by the breakdown of interpersonal protective resources. Furthermore, our finding that age moderates the pathway from depressive symptoms to suicidal ideation supplements existing research on the influencing factors of adolescent suicidal ideation. For example, a significant body of research has identified a wide array of risk and protective factors for adolescent suicidal ideation across individual, social, and environmental domains [9, 12]. While identifying such factors is crucial, our study provides a more nuanced understanding of the underlying process, demonstrating that the impact of depressive symptoms on suicidal ideation is developmentally sensitive and significantly more potent among younger adolescents. This interpretation is highly consistent with the ideation-to-action framework [2], which posits that factors such as depression, psychological pain, and life stress serve as the foundational drivers for the emergence of suicidal ideation. Our findings demonstrate that negative life events and the subsequent erosion of resilience create a state of distress that strongly predicts suicidal thoughts in clinical adolescents. By identifying these specific pathways, we provide empirical support for the framework’s distinction between the development of suicidal desire and the transition to suicidal behavior, affirming that our study addresses the critical first stage of this process.

A key finding of our study was the significant role of resilience as the first mediator in the chain linking negative life events to suicidal ideation. Consistent with longitudinal evidence showing that baseline resilience levels prospectively predict the trajectory of depressive symptoms in Chinese adolescents [59], our results reinforce the view of resilience as a fundamental self-righting mechanism. In this sequence, resilience acts as the ‘first-line defense’ that is initially challenged by negative life events; its subsequent depletion or mobilization then dictates the intensity of depressive symptoms. This conceptualization aligns with the Resilience Framework [60], which views resilience as the operation of basic human adaptive systems that precede and mitigate the onset of psychopathology. These systems prevent the escalation of stress into depression through distinct pathways: First, greater resilience is associated with better emotional regulation, enabling adaptive strategies to alleviate emotional fluctuations [61]. Second, resilience is positively correlated with self-efficacy: resilient individuals have greater confidence in their ability to cope with life challenges [62]. Third, resilience is linked to inhibitory control and cognitive flexibility, allowing individuals to suppress impulsive responses and break free from rigid negative thinking [63]. Furthermore, it is worth noting that resilience was found to be malleable and susceptible to intervention [64]. Research has shown that cognitive behavioral therapy and social support training can significantly improve resilience in adolescents [65, 66].

Notably, we did not observe a moderating effect of resilience in our sample, which contrasts with some community-based studies but aligns with the ‘resource depletion’ hypothesis. According to Conservation of Resources (COR) theory [68], individuals possess a finite pool of psychological resources that can be exhausted under chronic or high-intensity stress. In a clinical context where adolescents are already experiencing severe depressive episodes, the sheer magnitude of negative life events may systematically erode their adaptive capacity. Thus, rather than acting as a stable buffer that interacts with stress (moderation), resilience functions as a primary mediator—a vulnerable internal resource that, once depleted by adversity, opens the pathway to intensified depressive symptoms and suicidal ideation. This emphasizes the dynamic nature of resilience as described by Luthar and Cicchetti [67], where it represents a process that can be overwhelmed by clinical-level pathology.

Our findings underscore the central role of depressive symptoms in the development of suicidal ideation among adolescents, which is consistent with prior evidence [18, 49]. Specifically, depression functions as a key intermediary through which life events and resilience affect suicidal ideation, underscoring the importance of timely detection and management.

These findings must be interpreted in the context of contemporary Chinese society. Firstly, due to the influence of collectivist values, Chinese adolescents often view academic success as a moral obligation to their family rather than a personal goal [69]. This cultural imperative is reinforced at family level by high parental expectations and the traditional concept of filial piety, whereby academic failure is equated with not fulfilling family roles [70]. Consequently, at an individual level, this dynamic can increase stress levels, as setbacks are seen not just as failures in performance, but also as significant sources of interpersonal shame and ‘perceived burdensomeness’—a key driver of suicidal thoughts [71]. Within this high-pressure environment, resilience acts as a vital yet fragile resource that can quickly become depleted under the weight of meeting systemic cultural expectations. Once depleted, depression and suicidal thoughts may become more likely.

Our moderation results revealed that age plays a critical role, with depressive symptoms having a stronger predictive effect on suicidal ideation in younger adolescents, with this influence gradually diminishing with age. These results imply that adolescents in different age groups cope with psychological distress in distinct ways, highlighting the importance of considering developmental factors in suicide risk. In particular, these findings could potentially be interpreted in light of neurodevelopmental maturation. Prior research suggests that the prefrontal cortex—a region critical for cognitive control and inhibitory capacity—continues to undergo significant structural and functional changes throughout adolescence [44]. It is plausible that the relative immaturity of these regulatory systems in younger adolescents may limit their capacity for top-down emotion regulation, thereby increasing their susceptibility to extreme cognitive patterns such as suicidal ideation when experiencing depressive symptoms [45]. Additionally, the limited emotional regulation strategies and immature coping mechanisms of younger adolescents can exacerbate the risk of depression escalating into suicidal thoughts [72]. From a psychosocial standpoint, the nature of the adolescent identity crisis differs critically by age [73]. The younger adolescents in our study are more likely to be in a state of identity diffusion, a known risk factor for psychopathology, whereas older adolescents tend to be in the more structured stage of identity moratorium (active self-exploration) [74]. This distinction is critical, as recent research has shown that a high degree of identity confusion is a significant predictor of increases in depressive symptoms over time [75]. Therefore, the fusion of a depressive episode with the profound instability of identity diffusion likely explains the more potent link to suicidal ideation observed in younger group.

Although this study has made initial progress in revealing the relationships among life events, suicidal ideation, and their psychological mechanisms among adolescents, several limitations remain. First, the reliance on self-report measures and the specific nature of our clinical sample limits generalizability, as resilience may function differently in nonclinical populations, potentially maintaining well-being rather than mitigating existing disorders [76]. Future studies should examine whether these pathways hold in broader, community-based samples. Second, the reliance on self-reported data is a limitation; for example, mood-congruent recall bias in depressed individuals can artificially inflate the association between current mood and the recollection of past negative life events [77]. The incorporation of multiple assessment methods, such as parent or teacher reports, interviews, and physiological indicators, could improve validity and reduce bias. Furthermore, the cross-sectional design of this study precludes definitive causal inferences and may generate biased estimates of longitudinal mediation parameters, as noted by Maxwell and Cole [78]. Consequently, the observed mediation and moderation effects should be interpreted as stable concurrent associations within a clinical state rather than a chronological sequence. Despite this limitation, our findings provide a necessary empirical foundation for understanding the complex interplay of psychological resources and symptoms in suicidal adolescents, serving as a precursor to more intensive, longitudinal research into these mechanisms. Finally, the current study did not account for prior depressive symptom levels or the duration of the current episode. Consequently, we cannot definitively distinguish whether NLEs and resilience predict prospective changes in symptom severity or primarily reflect the stability of symptoms within a clinical population. This limitation underscores the need for future longitudinal studies to track the dynamic evolution of these psychological factors from the onset of a depressive episode.

The findings of this study have important implications. This is one of the first studies to test a comprehensive moderated chain mediation model of suicidal ideation in a multicenter clinical sample of depressed adolescents in China. This enhances the robustness and generalizability of our findings across diverse clinical settings and regional variations in China. Theoretically, our results show that the pathway from negative life events to suicidal ideation is mediated by the sequential internal processes of resilience and depressive symptoms. Our model specifically positions resilience as a crucial upstream protective factor that alleviates the development of depressive symptoms, thereby disrupting the cascade toward suicidality. Given that resilience is a malleable trait susceptible to intervention [64, 57, 79], these findings strongly advocate for a clinical shift toward preventive interventions that focus on fostering resilience through skills-based training. Crucially, the moderating role of age underscores the need for such strategies to be developmentally sensitive. Since our study identified younger adolescents as being significantly more vulnerable to the transition from depressive symptoms to suicidal ideation, interventions that bolster resilience and emotion regulation skills should be specifically prioritized for this high-risk age group. These findings provide an evidence-based roadmap for developing more precise, developmentally timely interventions to prevent suicide in high-risk adolescents.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that negative life events influence suicidal ideation among depressed adolescents primarily through reduced resilience and heightened depressive symptoms rather than through direct effects. Importantly, age moderated the pathway from depressive symptoms to suicidal ideation, suggesting that younger adolescents may be more vulnerable to the impact of depression on suicidal thoughts. Conducted across multiple clinical centers, this study offers robust evidence regarding the generalizability of these mechanisms among a high-risk Chinese adolescent population. These findings provide a clear evidence-based roadmap for suicide prevention, emphasizing the need for age-sensitive interventions focused on fostering resilience and enhancing emotional regulation skills, particularly in vulnerable younger adolescents.
