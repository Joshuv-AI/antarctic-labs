Health system support and strengthening for cross-border HIV services: key informants’ perspectives on testing and linkage for Lesotho-South Africa migrant workers
Authors: Joanne E Mantell, Tsitsi B Masvawure, Noelle Anna Thomas, Gavin George, Andrea A Howard, Michael Strauss, Felix Ndagije, Molibeli Lethoko, Andrea Low, Yael Hirsch-Moverman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionCross-border migrants, especially those in destination countries with high HIV burden and high population mobility, often face structural challenges in accessing HIV testing and linkage-to-care. There is limited research on the most effective HIV testing and linkage-to-care strategies tailored to cross-border migrants. We conducted a qualitative study to explore barriers and facilitators to HIV testing and linkage-to-care among Lesotho-South Africa cross-border migrant workers.MethodsWe conducted in-depth interviews with 15 purposively selected stakeholders from Lesotho who interfaced with migrants, namely administrative and programme staff from the Ministry of Health, non-governmental organisations, community counsellors and testers and migrant advocates. We captured diverse perspectives on barriers and facilitators to HIV services among migrant workers. Template analysis, a rapid form of thematic analysis, was used to synthesise the data. We applied Chee et al’s health systems framework differentiating health system support and health system strengthening to analyse our findings and guide actionable programme and policy strategies.ResultsParticipants recommended strategies that both support the health system by improving service provision and strengthen the health system by implementing structural and policy-level changes. Support strategies included expanding migrant-centred access to HIV testing and prevention services, reducing structural and economic barriers to service utilisation and strengthening community-based services. Strengthening strategies included cross-border collaboration and harmonised referral systems for continuity of care, expanding differentiated HIV care models for mobile populations, integrating HIV services into general healthcare, establishing workplace HIV policies for migrant workers and flexible cross-border funding for migrant health.ConclusionsChee et al’s framework allows policymakers and programme implementers to more intentionally distinguish and plan for solutions that merely support versus actually strengthen health systems. Strengthening approaches, in contrast to supporting ones, have the potential to transform HIV services for migrants by ensuring continuity of services in both home and destination venues butrequire greater commitment and action from politicians, policymakers, funders and advocacy groups to implement.

Introduction

In 2024, there were an estimated 304 million international migrants, nearly double the number since 1990.1 This increase has exacerbated the global challenge of providing healthcare services to internationally mobile populations,2 particularly in the context of HIV. Migrant populations living with or at risk for HIV face challenges in accessing HIV testing, treatment and prevention services when they cross international borders due to the confluence of discriminatory policies and practices and stigma, xenophobia, restricted laws and policies, poor navigation of unfamiliar health systems, language issues, unstable economic conditions and lost work time when seeking services.3 Progress in accessing HIV services for migrants has been exceedingly slow, with limited programmatic and policy improvements, despite increasing migration trends globally.

Migrant health is an urgent issue in countries like Lesotho and South Africa, which have substantial cross-border population movement4 and bear some of the world’s highest HIV burdens. Migrant workers are considered a priority population in Lesotho’s HIV Strategic Plan 2023–2028.5 Lesotho’s economy is highly dependent on labour migration to South Africa for formal and informal employment.6 7 Transient lifestyles, combined with social and economic marginalisation, broader sociopolitical factors and the failure of HIV programmes to address migrant workers’ needs, underscore the necessity for tailored service delivery approaches. Effective interventions must address both the structural drivers of vulnerability and health system barriers limiting access to HIV testing and linkage services.8

Lesotho adopted a Test and Treat policy in 2016, followed by differentiated HIV testing services in 2018, and subsequent optimisation of antiretroviral therapy (ART).9 Under this policy, same-day ART initiation is recommended after a confirmed HIV diagnosis and contingent on client readiness. While facility-based initiation remains the standard of care, community-based ART initiation may be implemented when appropriate. Recognising the mobility and access challenges migrant workers face, differentiated service delivery guidelines recommend multimonth dispensing at initiation along with phone check-ins at 7, 14, 30 and 60 days to review baseline lab results, monitor side effects, support adherence, reinforce U=U messaging and confirm the ongoing plan. The guidelines also encourage exploring HIV care options at migrants’ destinations to support continuity of care and timely PrEP initiation for those who test negative.

The 2020 Lesotho Population-Based HIV Impact Assessment survey shows progress in the national HIV response between 2016 and 2020. After adjusting for demographic factors, adults living with HIV in 2020 were significantly more likely to know their HIV status, initiate ART if aware of their status and be virally suppressed if on ART than in 2016.10 However, 8.3% of participants had lived outside Lesotho in the previous year, and HIV prevalence was significantly higher among recent cross-border migrants (29.2%) than in the overall sample (23.5%). Only 72.4% of these migrants were virally suppressed compared with 82.4% of non-migrants.11

Research on HIV testing and linkage-to-prevention and care models for cross-border migrants in sub-Saharan Africa remains limited. A 2018 study found that many migrants living with HIV in Lesotho preferred receiving HIV services in Lesotho and often interrupted treatment due to difficulties accessing healthcare in South Africa and perceptions that Lesotho’s healthcare was superior.12 Various HIV testing and linkage models have been implemented, including facility-based testing, SMS follow-up after HIV self-testing, referrals from border testing points, mobile clinic outreach and peer-supported follow-up.13 However, consensus on the most effective models to support HIV testing and post-test linkage services among cross-border migrant populations is lacking.12 14 To address this gap, this paper draws on qualitative data with key informants from the Promoting HIV Testing and Linkage to Care in Cross-Border Migrants in Lesotho study, which explored perceived enablers and impediments to accessing the HIV testing and linkage to prevention and care services among Lesotho-South Africa cross-border migrants. While individual-level and interpersonal-level factors affect migrants’ access to HIV-related services, these interviews focused specifically on potential programmatic and policy-level strategies.

Materials and methods

Overview of the linkage-to-care for cross-border migrants in Lesotho study

We used an exploratory sequential mixed-methods design for a study focused on HIV testing and linkage-to-care services for Lesotho-South Africa cross-border migrant workers. The initial qualitative phase included focus group discussions with migrant workers and in-depth interviews (IDIs) with policymakers, community workers, and migrant advocates in Maseru, Berea, and Leribe districts of Lesotho. In addition, a discrete-choice experiment embedded in a quantitative survey was conducted with Basotho migrant workers. This paper is limited to findings from key informant IDIs.

Conceptual framework

We use Chee et al’s framework which differentiates between health system support and health system strengthening. This framework responds to critiques of the WHO’s 2007 health systems strengthening framework15 by clarifying the type and durability of interventions needed to improve outcomes. Health system support refers to activities that enhance service delivery by increasing inputs (eg, distributing supplies, hiring staff, upgrading facilities), which are often short-term and narrowly targeted, addressing immediate gaps but with limited influence on system performance. In contrast, health system strengthening entails long-term institutionalised changes that reshape how the system functions (eg, policies, organisational structures, and relationships across WHO’s six building blocks) to influence behaviour, improve coordination and optimise resource use. While both approaches are necessary, Chee et al argue that awareness of this distinction is essential for priority setting and improving programme design and evaluation.

Study sample and recruitment

We conducted IDIs with 15 purposively selected key informants highly experienced in working with cross-border Basotho migrants. Key informants were selected due to their ability to elucidate understanding of context-specific narratives that are not accessible or implicit in survey data. Participants included staff from TEBA (n=3), the Lesotho Ministry of Health (n=3), Lesotho Migrant Workers Association (n=3), International Organization for Migration (n=3) and community counsellors (n=3). No invited stakeholders refused to participate in the study. Our goal was to achieve thematic saturation by ensuring that our sample was sufficiently broad to capture a wide range of perspectives about health system challenges.

Data collection and analysis

The research team conducted sensitisation meetings prior to study launch to orient potential participants to the study goals. IDIs were conducted in-person by experienced male and female research assistants trained on the interview guide. Participants were interviewed in their workplaces. The interview guide was translated from English to Sesotho and back-translated to ensure accuracy and was evaluated by the local team. Interviews were conducted in Sesotho or English and lasted between 32 and 84 min and included questions about policy-level and programme-level barriers, stigma and linkage to HIV services (table 1). Interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, anonymised, translated into English if needed and cross-checked for accuracy by research assistants. Field notes were taken to capture additional observations.

We applied template analysis, a rapid but flexible form of thematic analysis, to systematically synthesise the data.1619 Both deductive and inductive approaches informed the identification and categorisation of themes. Research assistants summarised the transcripts and created template summary tables in Excel. Chee et al’s framework was used to classify proposed interventions by whether they improve existing service delivery processes (health system support) or involve broader policy and structural changes to improve system performance and sustainability (health system strengthening).

Patient and public involvement

The study was reviewed initially at Stakeholders Advisory Group meetings, where community stakeholders advised on study design, implementation, and challenges. Stakeholders included implementing partners, representatives from non-governmental organisations, migrant advocates and community representatives; no patients were represented in these meetings.

Results

Participants’ demographic characteristics

Of the 15 participants, 12 were women and three were men. Their median age was 38 years and 13 had a bachelor’s degree or higher. The median number of years participants had worked with migrants was 6 years (IQR: 3, 7).

Health system support approaches

Participants identified several health system support strategies for Basotho migrant workers, namely, expanding HIV service access points, reducing economic and structural barriers to service utilisation and increasing community-based educational activities.

Expanding migrant-centred access to HIV testing and prevention services

Bring services to migrant-dense areas: Participants highlighted the importance of expanding HIV testing and prevention services beyond traditional health facilities by bringing services to known migrant-dense areas, such as taxi ranks, and during peak travel periods, such as Easter and Christmas, as strategic sites for reaching migrant workers who may have limited opportunities to access health services.

… tents that are for Basotho migrants should be placed mainly in main taxi [ranks] in South Africa.

-01-KS-01, Local NGO

We know that in December they are coming in huge numbers and even the Easter Holiday… that’s how the migrants usually work. It can be done in the Lesotho site, the border.

-03-KS-02, International NGO

Bring services to border areas: Participants proposed establishing clinics near border crossings that could provide testing, medication refills and referrals, serving as central coordination points for linkage to facilities in home districts. They also suggested expanding existing border health infrastructure, like Port Health, to include HIV testing and prevention services. Self-testing was viewed as convenient for migrants who have limited time to visit clinics, face the risk of lost income when attending clinic visits, or prefer more private testing options.

If there could be a clinic at the border… that clinic should be a bridge between other clinics in the country and the people that cross the border.

-01-KS-02, Local NGO

Why not avail those services within the portfolio of work of Port Health?… we are talking about the saliva test… you don’t even need to prick someone.

-03-KS-03, International NGO

Expand distribution of HIV self-test kits and pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP): Dispensing self-test kits and PrEP at border crossings, through community-based delivery, pharmacies and secondary distribution by partners or family members, was suggested as a practical strategy for migrants to easily learn their HIV status, initiate treatment quickly if positive, or continue to prevent infection if negative.

We can provide self-testing packages at border gate facilities because that’s where most of us pass.…if we give out ten self-test kits, there is a possibility that out of the ten, seven will go for confirmatory testing.

-01-KS-01, Local NGO

However, participants emphasised the need for counselling, clear and accessible information about these prevention services, including where to access them. Several noted that marketing of PrEP and monitoring of confirmatory tests to migrants was currently limited.

…confirmations is where we get a little bit challenged. Some of them will be here for a short while, and sometimes the results are reported when the person has already gone back to work.

-05-KS-03, Community Counsellor

We can link them to PrEP in the communities. It can also be at the health facilities…. If he is close to the clinic, he can go to the clinic, if he is close to the facilities in the community that offer PrEP services…. he can also go to the border. If there are people who provide PrEP services outside the country, he can also go there.

-05-KS-02, Community Counsellor

Participants noted stigma as a barrier but suggested community-based pharmacies as possible solutions.

…when I go there to get PrEP… you indeed know that the issue of sexual intercourse to Basotho is still one of those things that you do not want it should be known that a person is [sexually] active. So, at the community I think they will be too afraid, but… when it’s there at the facility or if it’s given at the pharmacies, it will be easier…

-04-KS-03, MOH

Improve advertising of existing services: Some participants were concerned that health services provided at border health facilities were not widely known and stressed the need to increase awareness of these.

Although a health center operates at the Maseru border…no one is telling people about the services that are being provided there… if there can be more awareness about services at the border, people can know and take such services.

-02-KS-03, Local NGO

Reducing structural and economic barriers to service utilisation

Incentives for using HIV services: Participants noted structural barriers that discourage migrant workers from accessing services, including transportation costs, long waiting times at health facilities and the risk of lost income when attending clinic visits. Some participants thought incentives, such as transportation or food, could encourage service utilisation; others thought it would create dependency or be perceived as coercive.

There should be a car available to escort a person because transport fare is expensive. That car should reach all communities. It will be easier that way.

-05-KS-02, Community Counsellor

It can help, and it cannot help…it will seem like we are being bought to access such a service. Often when people are given incentives, it doesn’t last long and when there are no funds, people will not go to the facilities.

-02-KS-02, Local NGO

Hire more health staff to make services efficient: Participants noted that shortages of healthcare workers contribute to delays in service delivery, limiting the ability of facilities to provide timely care. It deterred migrants who typically work long hours, have limited flexibility to leave their jobs and often prioritise work over health services, especially when accessing care involves significant time costs.

They get there and want service, but if we don’t have enough human resources, how is that possible?

-03-KS-01, International NGO

Strengthening community-based services

Engaging community leaders and structures: Participants highlighted the value of engaging trusted community leaders and peer networks to deliver health communications, as they are perceived as more credible than outsiders. They suggested partnering with migrant-serving organisations to train peer educators, and recruiting community members, such as street vendors, to provide HIV testing and referrals to nearby clinics.

You need to explain to the community leaders to make the people understand the benefits if HIV testing…not just testing but a whole sector of comprehensive care.

-03-KS-02, International NGO

Participants also suggested community-based support groups, including home-based HIV services in migrant-dense areas as essential spaces where individuals can share experiences, receive encouragement, and learn strategies for remaining in treatment.

If they have support groups, those who were demotivated will feel motivated… they will know they are not alone and can share how they manage their medication.

-02-KS-02, Local NGO

If we have home-based care where the nurses are having the right attitude and … understand your situations, then I think it will improve not only migrants, but everybody.

-02-KS-02, Local NGO

Involving sexual partners and family members in HIV education and secondary distribution of HIV self-test kits was another strategy proposed by participants.

Our best shot…is getting in contact with their sexual partners, or their families right here at home. We are distributing self-tests for them, and then they will have access through that secondary distribution.

-05-KS-03, Community Counselor

Sustained and targeted HIV education and communication. Participants stressed the importance of sustained community education efforts to raise awareness of services as migrants often miss national awareness campaigns due to extended periods outside the country. Targeted messaging for specific groups (eg, women vs men migrants, different professions and documented vs undocumented migrants) and other outreach campaigns that are timed to coincide with periods when migrants return home were suggested.

Miners mostly come home every month so it can be possible to provide such services for them at the border. But for the farm workers and domestic workers, we need to go to them after their working hours.

-02-KS-02, Local NGO

…for males, we said they hate waiting. So, for the men we can extend the service hours and weekends because it’s hard to find them. But for the women obviously we can find them…because they come for family planning and every other thing.

-04-KS-01 MOH

Digital communication platforms, such as WhatsApp and automated reminder systems and follow-up mechanisms, were suggested as tools that could facilitate peer support and service utilisation in light of migrants’ geographical dispersion. However, some worried that these messages could be easily ignored and also that migrants often changed their numbers when they arrived in South Africa.

Many of them have a phone and WhatsApp…we can send migrant-friendly information and people can ask questions privately.

-03-KS-02, International NGO

If the message goes straight to the intended receiver… sometimes a person may forget, other times one may just decide not to go but if they are reminded, his conscience might not allow him to miss the appointment.

-01-KS-03. Local NGO

Participants were realistic in acknowledging that information alone is insufficient and must be aligned with the lived experiences of individuals.

Health system strengthening approaches

Proposed health system strengthening strategies proposed by participants included cross-border institutional coordination, expanding differentiated HIV care delivery models, integrated health services, workplace HIV policies and flexible financing mechanisms to better accommodate the realities of cross-border mobility.

Cross-border collaboration and harmonised referral systems for continuity of care

Cross-border collaboration: Participants highlighted the need for stronger institutional collaboration between Lesotho and South Africa to support continuity of HIV care for migrants. They noted that once migrants leave Lesotho for employment in South Africa healthcare providers often lose track of them because there are no integrated systems linking patient records or monitoring treatment continuation across borders. Electronic linkage of health records to national identification systems and establishing formal agreements between the two countries was viewed as a strategy to improve patient tracking and facilitate continuity of treatment.

…we are lacking a proper system in the country that can track those people wherever they are…. we are using this libukana [booklet] that is not connected with the national bureau…. if we were to use the national IDs in the public health system…even the passport itself can tell ‘Oh! No, this guy is no longer here…there is a lack of communication between these two countries.

-02-KS-01, Local NGO

….We need to talk to South Africa counterparts as well…. then have a clear memorandum of understanding [on] how they are going to work with these people.

-02-KS-01, Local NGO

Policy-level inconsistencies were also noted as barriers to care. Although South African health policies allow migrants to receive services regardless of legal status, contradictions between immigration and health policies can discourage migrants from seeking services.

Home Affairs says when someone is a foreigner and doesn’t have the papers or proper documents they should be deported while the health policy says everybody should be assisted at health services regardless of their status… if there can be harmonizing between those two policies…migrant workers can be able to access services.

-02-KS-03, Local NGO

Harmonisation of referral and documentation systems: Participants noted the need to harmonise referral and documentation systems between Lesotho and South Africa. Participants suggested that standardised documentation, such as regional health passports or harmonised referral procedures across the Southern African Development Community (SADC) countries could improve continuity of care for migrants accessing services in different locations.

…harmonization of referral mechanisms between Lesotho and South Africa should be discussed at higher levels to reach an agreement of how we want that to be done…. something like health passports…means that somebody wherever they are can access services.

-03-KS-01, International NGO

In the absence of harmonised systems, migrants are sometimes required to obtain transfer letters from clinics in Lesotho before receiving medication in South Africa, which is difficult for migrants working on short-term contracts or living far from home.

In terms of getting HIV medication on the other side, they are asked to produce transfer letters… they cannot come back home for the transfer letters.

-02-KS-02, Local NGO

Formal communication and coordination between facilities: Participants suggested developing shared information resources, such as databases or directories of facilities serving migrant populations, to strengthen referral coordination across borders.

We would need to know where all those facilities are… perhaps when you give them the saliva test share with them a brochure of facilities they can go to on the South African side. -03-KS-03, International NGO

If we have a database of the facilities across the border… we will be able to know where we can find our people.

-04-KS-01, MOH

Participants noted that communication between facilities in the two countries often depends on informal provider networks and personal contacts to verify treatment histories when migrants arrive without documentation than on established referral systems.

…people from facility A should call facility B to confirm if the patient is indeed one [rather] than physically going back to the facility to get the referral letter.

-02-KS-02, Local NGO

Miners come with referral letters…. some don’t. Such a miner has to go back to the mine to get his referral letter. But now it’s easy for the miner to come to [migrant NGO] and say, ‘I was taking my medication at Harmony Mine’. I will talk to those on the other side… and then they send me an email with the miner’s referral.

-01-KS-02, Local NGO

Despite these informal efforts, participants noted that migrants’ continuity of care was often disrupted for months as providers in Lesotho are unable to direct them to migrant-friendly facilities in South Africa.

When we test them here at home, we do not know the facilities to link them to in South Africa… and at times …they will tell you that they spent months before they could find a facility that they could get services from.

-04-KS-01, MOH

Expanding differentiated HIV care models for mobile populations

Participants recommended expanding differentiated HIV service delivery models to offer medication for 1 year to better accommodate the mobility patterns of Basotho migrant workers. Current models often assume relatively stable populations than is the case in reality.

It should not be uniform…. We already have multi-month dispensary, but the efficiency of that for this mobile population of migrants is still considerable… if we say in-country that the guidelines do not support one-year dispensary, then, what do we say for the migrant who only comes back once a year?

-03-KS-01, International NGO

Integrating HIV services into general healthcare

Participants emphasised that providing HIV services alongside other health services could encourage migrant workers to seek care without fear of being identified as a person living with HIV. Integrated services were seen as a way to identify individuals who might otherwise remain undiagnosed as someone seeking care for a minor illness may also agree to HIV testing and learn their status. Furthermore, integrating services allows individuals to receive multiple screenings during a single visit and may facilitate earlier detection of other health conditions.

We need to provide inclusive health-friendly services whereby, a one-stop shop… if you make it in such a way that there is more services, then no one will know… [what you are] going to that place for….

-02-KS-01, Local NGO

I strongly recommend it because if one does not know their status, opportunistic diseases will take advantage so if we integrate health services a person will be screened for all diseases. For example, do I have TB, do I have sugar diabetes?

-02-KS-02, Local NGO

Integrating services in organisations frequented by migrant workers would ensure seamless access to HIV services.

I do feel like there could be some use of already existing institutions that have other things to do with them that are not related to HIV…. Something should be done to incorporate those services in those institutions that have a more frequent association with them to access these services more smoothly.

-05-KS-03, Local NGO

Strengthening workplace policies for migrant health

Participants discussed the potential role of workplace HIV policies in facilitating access to HIV services for migrant workers directly at worksites and via partnerships with other organisations to help reach migrant workers who typically spend most of their time at work.

Look at the factories… they are constantly there. … companies should ensure they have HIV workplace policies and allow NGOs or government to have an office somewhere where people can go for services.

-03-KS-03, International NGO

…it is not a must to go to the clinic for HTS services. So we can take the services to their workplace, provide services there, then they will take the full package maybe when we initiate ART or PrEP after testing.

-04-KS-01, MOH

Others suggested that workplace policies should consider time spent accessing health services as part of the workday as a way to remove the financial disincentive for utilising health facilities for migrant workers.

People are not motivated to go for testing because they lose work time. If workers are reimbursed or considered as still being at work while attending health services, that could encourage more to get tested.

-02-KS-03, Local NGO

Most of these employers, once they pay for a service for an employee, they are going to deduct it from their salaries… if services are available anywhere then there is no need for payment. People just need to know where they can get services.

-02-KS-01, Local NGO

However, some participants expressed doubt that broader systemic change would occur without a real shift in attitudes. Concerns about confidentiality and stigma if testing occurs in the workplace were also expressed.

If I am working in the office, am I going to do the HIV testing there? No! I would go somewhere else so that nobody would know… people don’t want to be known by their employer because they fear losing their job.

-03-KS-02, International NGO

Funding for migrant health

Participants suggested flexible funding frameworks that account for the needs of migrants who receive services across national borders. Donor funding structures often limit programmes from providing services to Basotho migrants outside Lesotho.

We need to have that policy that is very intentional about cross border health management. If you look at some donors like Global Fund, they will tell you, ‘We are going to give services for only Basotho who are in Lesotho.’ But I can assure you… there are so many who are in South Africa…. so we need to unlock that bottle neck to see to it that grants should allow anybody to operate as long as you can prove that there is Basotho who are staying in that particular location.

-02-KS-01, Local NGO

Discussion

We found strong consensus among individuals who work closely with Basotho migrants that a health system strengthening approach focused on improving collaboration, coordination and harmonisation of HIV services between the Lesotho and South African governments is critical for ensuring the possibility of any real, meaningful and sustainable change. Several studies support this view, arguing that migrant health and well-being require political commitment12 21 from governments in both the home and host countries and can facilitate collaboration and record sharing between cross-border facilities. The use of health passports was suggested as a strategy to harmonise referral and documentation systems between Lesotho and South Africa. Although we are unaware of any country that has fully implemented this strategy, a systematic review of health records for migrants and refugees across 33 programmes found that both electronic and non-electronic health records show promise for improving access to and uptake of health services.22 However, these systems are in early pilot stages in several European Union countries. Efforts to scale them up are constrained by limited funding and technical support, as well as insufficient evidence regarding their cost-effectiveness and acceptability among migrants. Partnerships between governments and local communities and community engagement interventions are also key to successful implementation of community-based health services and improving health outcomes and quality of care for migrants and other disadvantaged populations.132326

Participants also suggested changes to funding models so that implementing organisations could continue to provide services to Basotho migrants in South Africa. Most funding models currently restrict funding to the country in which the recipient organisation is based, unless the latter is regional. As countries in SADC push for more open borders, it is important that migrants have easy access to comprehensive health services, including for HIV, in host countries. The recent termination of funding from the US Agency for International Development has significantly disrupted HIV services resulting in 71% of USAID HIV awards terminated,27 and affecting programmes across sub-Saharan Africa.28 29 These cuts risk worsening the existing barriers that are faced by migrant workers. However, this crisis also presents an opportunity to restructure and strengthen local health systems by investing in sustainable, locally-driven solutions that improve access to HIV prevention and care services for migrant workers.30

Key informants recognised that health system strengthening interventions are difficult to implement, and several described them as ‘aspirational’. Consequently, many of their proposed strategies focused on more practical solutions designed to support the health system so that it can continue to provide services to migrants. Expanding service delivery points in migrant-dense areas and communities and at strategic times of the year were offered as real-world approaches that could be implemented with less bureaucracy and quickly. In terms of the actual package of services that should be prioritised, key informants noted that PrEP, HIV self-tests, workplace-based and home-based services and year-long ART dispensing could greatly improve migrants’ access to services. However, participants suggested that such services should be provided in a way that does not inadvertently expose migrants to stigma and discrimination.

While we agree with many of the proposed strategies, we must be cognisant of the undercurrent of ethical issues that stream through implementation of programmes and policies for cross-border migrants. Access to healthcare has been recognised as a fundamental human right, regardless of migration status, in numerous policy and framework documents across the SADC region. Both South Africa and Lesotho have policy commitments aligned with Sustainable Development Goal 3, which promotes universal health coverage and the principle of ‘leaving no one behind’.31 Yet, a disconnect remains between policy and practice, and healthcare providers in South Africa do not consistently implement migrant-inclusive guidelines.8 Even when migrant health rights are clearly articulated in regional and national frameworks, they are not always operationalised,32 and despite South Africa’s strong rights-based frameworks, the country does not fully support migrants’ rights to healthcare.32 33 In Lesotho, the right to health is not constitutionally recognised and the recent 2022 Tenth Amendment to its constitution, which states that “…Lesotho shall adopt policies aimed at ensuring the highest attainable standard of physical and mental health for its citizens”, is not enforceable by court.34 35

Study strengths and weaknesses

Key strengths of this study were the focus on a high HIV burden setting with extensive cross-border migration and the incorporation of perspectives from multiple key stakeholders with substantial expertise working with Basotho migrant workers via in-depth exploration of programmatic and policy challenges associated with HIV testing and linkage-to-care. However, IDIs with key informants from South Africa and the lived experiences of migrant workers themselves were not included. Instead, Basotho community counsellors, testers and migrant programme advocates provided meaningful insights about their experiences with the target population. We recognise that cross-border migrant workers are not an entirely homogenous group and may inhabit different geographical, social, economic and behavioural spaces, which may not be fully captured in this paper as the perspectives of stakeholders are rooted in a broader understanding of healthcare structure and policy. While Chee et al’s framework of health system support and strengthening clarifies the role and relevance of different healthcare interventions mentioned by participants, some strategies were applicable to both the ‘support’ and ‘strengthening’ categories and are thus not mutually exclusive as the framework might imply.

Conclusions

Access to HIV prevention and care services remains inadequate for cross-border migrant workers in South Africa and Lesotho. Addressing this gap requires a clearer understanding of the structural barriers migrants face and their preferences for service delivery. Reliance on short-term or stop-gap measures is insufficient to overcome the systemic challenges that limit equitable access to HIV testing and linkage-to-care services for cross-border migrants at risk for, or already living with HIV. Sustainable progress will require health system strengthening and implementation science research to ensure that policies and programmes translate into accessible migrant-responsive HIV services.
