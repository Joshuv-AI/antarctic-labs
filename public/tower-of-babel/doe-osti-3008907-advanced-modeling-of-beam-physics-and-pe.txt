1 Background and Motivation
Luminosity is the primary figure of merit for collider performance. The sPHENIX detector [1], installed at
IR8 of RHIC and commissioned in 2024, targets precision measurements of quark–gluon plasma properties.
Meeting the physics program requirements motivates increased luminosity and, specifically, improved control
of the longitudinal distribution of collisions.
 For sPHENIX, collisions within the vertex window (±10 cm) are reconstructable by the vertex detector,
while collisions outside this region contribute disproportionately to backgrounds. A key operational lever is
precise control of the local optics at the interaction point (IP), in particular ensuring that the longitudinal
waist location of β ∗ , denoted s∗ , is well aligned with the collision point sIP . Achieving this alignment
requires robust, local optics control within the IR, together with repeatable optics measurements suitable
for closed-loop tuning and subsequent Bayesian Optimization.

3 Methodology
3.1 Optimization Study
The project utilized GPTune [2](Gaussian Processes Tuning), a Bayesian Optimization (BO) auto-tuning
framework developed at Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory. BO is well-suited for accelerator tuning
because it treats the machine as a black-box objective function, builds a surrogate model (typically a Gaussian
Process), and identifies improved operating points with a limited number of measurements, thereby reducing
required machine time.
 For general luminosity optimization, the objective is to maximize total luminosity. For sPHENIX, the
ideal formulation is multi-objective:
 • maximize luminosity within the detector vertex window;

 • minimize luminosity outside the vertex window (background reduction).
 Figure 1 illustrates the simulated Pareto front: along the front, one objective cannot be improved without
sacrificing the other.

3.2 Optimization Procedures
The IR contains 17 quadrupoles with sufficient local leverage to modify the optics at the IP. For safe and
operationally compatible tuning, the procedure must satisfy:
 • Locality: the optics perturbation should remain confined to the IR, minimizing impact on the re-
 mainder of the ring;

 • Aperture/collimation safety: the beta function near the IR8 collimator should not exceed its
 baseline value.
 Directly optimizing all 17 quadrupole strengths using BO was deemed unsafe and inefficient due to narrow
operating tolerances and the risk of unintended global optics perturbations. Instead, we employed dimension
reduction by constructing physics-motivated pseudo knobs that parameterize meaningful IR optics changes
(e.g., ∆s∗x , ∆s∗y ). GPTune operated on these pseudo-knobs, while an underlying optics model computed the
corresponding magnet current changes subject to boundary conditions (preserving optics outside the IR and
maintaining global tunes) and additional operational constraints. Figure 2 summarizes the experimental
workflow.

3.3 Improved Optics Tuning (Sensitivity Matrix)
Initial experimental use of MAD-X matching to translate desired s∗ movements into magnet strengths
proved unreliable, as the resulting optics measurements showed inconsistent s∗ values compared to intended
movements.
 To address this, the MAD-X scheme was replaced with an optics response matrix B [3], or sensitivity
matrix. This matrix defines a linear relationship between the change in optics (∆O) and the change in power

 2

supply currents (∆I):
 ∆O = B ∆I.
The inverse equation,
                                          ∆I = B −1 ∆O − null(B) C,
was used to calculate the required current changes. The null space term null(B) C allowed the optimization
of specific constraints—such as power supply current limits, hysteresis effects (by imposing monotonicity
constraints on magnet strengths), and ensuring the beta function at the IR8 collimator remained below its
original value—without disturbing the overall optics requirements.

3.4 Enhanced Optics Measurement
In order to achieve accurate measurements of linear optics parameters (s∗ , β ∗ ), especially given high variance
in existing RHIC measurements, two major improvements were implemented:

 1. Dynamic Mode Decomposition (DMD): DMD [4] was introduced to process turn-by-turn (TBT)
 data from BPMs to reduce noise and improve consistency. DMD extracts spatial and temporal modes
 from the dataset, creating a reconstructed, cleaner signal.
 2. Total Least Squares (TLS): The TLS algorithm was used to solve the one-turn map equation based
 on BPM data collected in drift spaces, minimizing error in both dependent (Y ) and independent (X)
 variables. Error analysis showed that TLS provided better β measurements in high-β regions, such as
 IP8, compared to Ordinary Least Squares (OLS).

 Figure 3: Comparison of data processing procedures for IR optics measurement

 Figure 3 compares the enhanced optics measurement routine using DMD followed by the curve fitting
(CF), harmonic analysis(HA), and total least squares (TLS) method with the original method used in
RHIC (R-CF). The uncertainty using the new methods is reduced at least 3 times using the enhanced data
processing methods. This development set the foundation for luminosity optimization.

4.2 First Optimization Experiment
In the experimental setup for optimization, we have to modify the planned optimization target. The original
plan was to optimize the vertex luminosity while lowering the luminosity outside the vertex region. However,
due to the heavy delay of the vertex signal (several minutes), we are unable to get real-time vertex data for
optimization. Therefore, we decided to optimize the total luminosity, which can be measured in real-time
by the ZDC(zero-degree calorimeter) rate. Also, the RHIC beam’s bunch length is longer than planned due
to the lack of the 56 MHz cavity in operation, the 0.1-0.2m s∗ will only contribute less than 5% luminosity
loss, which is less than the noise level of the ZDC rate.
 Therefore, we first test the optimization process starting from a suboptimal s∗ at approximately 0.5m
for both transverse directions. Figure 5 illustrates that the ZDC rate can be quickly recovered (less than 10
min or 5 iterations) from a suboptimal state.
 However, due to dynamical effects and/or beam losses, there is a quick decay of luminosity, the ZDC rate
also naturally drop in our 40-min optimization process. Such decay is difficult to model and correct. For
example, figure 5 compares 2 different correction algorithms that consider the contribution of the increasing
effect of the length of the bunch, which is believed to be the dominant effect during the experimental time.
The difference between the two algorithms comes from the length of the bunch evaluated by two different
measurement devices. Therefore, it is difficult to evaluate the effect of optimization over a long period.

4.3 Second Optimization Experiment
To overcome this limit, a follow-up experiment was implemented to test the optimization again. In order
not to rely on a specific model of the luminosity decay, we frequently pause the optimization process and
manually revert the setting of s∗ to its initial value. These reset steps are not included in the optimization
process. This helps us to remove the natural decay of the signal so that we can compare the optimization in
a longer time range. It is obvious that the luminosity can repeatedly come back to its optimum value during
the optimization process. However, because we expect the real value of s∗ is only about 0.1-0.2m away from
its ideal position, which only results in up to 5% of the luminosity difference. The ZDC rate is too noisy to
observe the small luminosity gain during the process. However, if we record all requested settings s∗ and the
corresponding ZDC rate, as shown in Figure 4, we will find that the ’equally good’ region of the settings s∗
is shifted to the negative side in both directions. This observation agrees with the measurement s∗ in Figure
4.

6 Conclusion and Future Directions
The project successfully addressed the main technical hurdle for luminosity optimization: achieving reliable
and safe control over local optics, particularly the s∗ collision point parameter, by leveraging a model-based
approach that uses the sensitivity matrix and rigorous optic measurement validation. The integration of the
physics model and AI algorithm (GPTune) proved to be encouraging.
 The techniques developed in this project are highly relevant and expected to be crucial for the upcoming
EIC commissioning and operation, particularly given the anticipated 10× reduction in vertical beta function
required for both beams, which requires a much tighter requirement of s∗ to prevent significant luminosity
loss.
