Abstract
We report on a gravitational wave search for compact binary coalescences involving at least one component with mass between 0.2 ​ M ⊙ 0.2\,M_{\odot} to 1 ​ M ⊙ 1\,M_{\odot} , and ratio of component masses between 0.1 and 1. The analysis uses data collected by the LIGO detectors between May 24 2023 15:00 UTC and January 16 2024 16:00 UTC. No statistically significant sub-solar mass candidates were identified by the participating search algorithms. We report the detection sensitivity of the current searches to the target sub-solar mass black hole population, while also reporting the sensitivity of the search to low-mass neutron star binaries for the first time. With the absence of detections, we place upper limits on the merger rate of sub-solar mass black holes, ranging from 110 ​ Gpc − 3 ​ yr − 1 110\,{\rm Gpc^{-3}\,yr^{-1}} to 10000 ​ Gpc − 3 ​ yr − 1 10000\,{\rm Gpc^{-3}\,yr^{-1}} at 90% confidence. We use the merger rate limits to constrain two illustrative dark matter scenarios that can form sub-solar mass compact objects: primordial black holes, and dark black holes forming in a dissipative dark matter model. For late-forming primordial black hole binaries, our search excludes the fraction of dark matter in primordial black holes to be 1 for masses above 0.9 ​ M ⊙ {0.9\,M_{\odot}} . In the early-formation scenario, we limit this fraction to be ≤ 7 % \leq{7\%} at 1 ​ M ⊙ {1\,M_{\odot}} , and ≤ 40 % \leq{40\%} at 0.35 ​ M ⊙ {0.35\,M_{\odot}} . For the dissipative model, the excluded region in the parameter space of dark matter fraction in dark black holes and their minimum possible mass extends down to ( 1.2 ​ to ​ 1.3 ) × 10 − 5 {(1.2\,\mathrm{to}\,1.3)\times 10^{-5}} when the minimum mass is 1 ​ M ⊙ 1\,M_{\odot} . For binary neutron stars that include sub-solar mass components, we estimate the sensitive space-time hypervolume to be ∼ ​ 10 − 3 Gpc 3 yr \sim{10}^{-3}\text{\,}{\mathrm{Gpc}}^{3}\text{\,}\mathrm{yr} , and report the upper limit on their merger rate for a simple, fixed population as ∼ \sim 86 86 Gpc − 3 ​ yr − 1 {\rm Gpc^{-3}\,yr^{-1}} .

(1+z)\mathcal{M}=(1+z)(m_{1}m_{2})^{3/5}/(m_{1}+m_{2})^{1/5}
: the TaylorF2 approximant
(
43
;
114
;
39
)
is used for
(1+z)\mathcal{M}\leq 1.73\,M_{\odot}
, and the SEOBNRv4ROM approximant
(
43
;
37
;
59
)
for
(1+z)\mathcal{M}>1.73\,M_{\odot}
.
After matched-filtering, triggers—potential
GW
signals in data—are ranked using a likelihood function
(
138
;
45
)
.
The likelihood term for multi-detector signal consistency
(
77
)
is evaluated over a frequency range of
45\,\mathrm{Hz}
to
512\,\mathrm{Hz}
for this search.
Although this high-frequency cutoff is lower than
1024\,\mathrm{Hz}
used in template whitening, we find negligible difference in signal weighting between cutoffs of
512\,\mathrm{Hz}
and
1024\,\mathrm{Hz}
for this likelihood term.
Ultimately, all triggers are assigned a
FAR
(
FAR
) which quantifies the significance of a candidate signal by comparing it to the distribution of likelihoods for non-astrophysical events.

II.2
MBTA

The Multi-Band Template Analysis (
MBTA
;
9
;
27
;
31
;
30
) is a matched-filtering-based pipeline that searches for
GW
from
CBC
in data from the LIGO–Virgo–KAGRA detectors.

MBTA
stands out by its approach of splitting the matched-filtering process across multiple frequency bands to optimize computational efficiency
(
4
)
.
This approach significantly reduces the computational cost associated with processing a large template bank.

The template bank used by
MBTA
is the same as the one employed in the low-latency analysis described in
30
.
It comprises more than
2.5
million templates, covering the parameter space described above, and was generated using a geometric placement algorithm
(
41
)
.
The waveforms used to build the template bank are computed using the second-order post-Newtonian TaylorF2
(
43
;
114
;
39
)
, while the SpinTaylorT4 approximant
(
88
)
is used for the analysis.
A low-frequency cutoff of
45\,\mathrm{Hz}
and high-frequency cutoff of
1000\,\mathrm{Hz}
are applied to limit the duration of the templates, ensuring that the filtering remains computationally manageable while preserving the search sensitivity
(
30
)
.

As an improvement over
O3
,
MBTA
now includes a single-detector trigger search in its archival analysis
(
30
)
, enhancing sensitivity and enabling the use of periods when only one of the two
LIGO
detectors is operational.
The post-processing procedure has also been refined with the implementation of a new internal data quality assessment tool called the SNR-Excess technique which is detailed in
30
.

II.3
PyCBC

The
PyCBC
pipeline
(
29
;
61
;
140
;
110
;
63
;
108
)
performs a matched-filtering analysis to detect
GW
signals from
CBC
.
The
PyCBC

SSM
search employs the latest version of the pipeline, described in detail in
4
.
However, specific choices were made here to tailor the analysis to the search for
SSM
compact objects and improve its sensitivity.
These changes are summarized below.

The ranking statistic is the same as in the
O3

SSM
search
(
21
)
, with the only difference being that corrections for short term variations of the
PSD
(
PSD
)
(
104
)
are not included.
The
PyCBC

SSM
template bank contains approximately
2.0
million templates, and was generated using a geometric placement algorithm
(
41
)
.
The TaylorF2 post-Newtonian approximant
(
43
;
114
;
39
)
, a low-frequency cutoff of
45\,\mathrm{Hz}
, and a high-frequency cutoff of
1000\,\mathrm{Hz}
were adopted to compute the matches between templates.

The pipeline starts filtering the data at
30\,\mathrm{Hz}
, with a maximum allowed template duration of
1008\,\mathrm{s}
imposed to keep computational resources usage under control.
This limit affects only about 6
\%
of the templates, those with the lowest masses, increasing their starting frequency to approximately 33 Hz at most.
This choice was found to increase the sensitivity compared to the more straightforward approach of starting from
45\,\mathrm{Hz}
.
By contrast, using
30\,\mathrm{Hz}
as the low-frequency cutoff for bank generation would have required a substantially larger number of templates, making the search computationally impractical.
The SEOBNRv4ROM approximant
(
43
;
37
;
59
)
is used instead of TaylorF2 for waveform generation when the redshifted total mass
(1+z)(m_{1}+m_{2})>{1.0\,M_{\odot}}
.

Analogously to MBTA, starting in
O4
,
PyCBC
includes single-detector triggers in this archival analysis following
62
. This increases overall sensitivity, and allows for detection of events occurring when only one detector is observing.

III
Search Sensitivity

We quantify the sensitivity of our searches with the
O4a
detector network to
GW
signals from
SSM

CBC
to set an upper limit on their merger rates.
Due to the changing sensitivity across the observing run and the complex nature of the detection process, we determine this sensitivity empirically via the procedure laid out in
4
;
5
;
69
.
Following
69
, we simulate and inject signals into detector strain data, and all pipelines perform the search described in Section
II
on this augmented data.
Any statement of our sensitivity to
SSM
sources depends on the assumed astrophysical population that describes their distribution of masses and spins.
Therefore, we state the sensitivity to
SSM
sources
as a function of an assumed population
.
This allows us to use the results from a single injection-recovery analysis to determine the sensitivity to multiple population models.

The sensitive space-time hypervolume
\langle VT\rangle
, and its corresponding uncertainty
\sigma_{\langle VT\rangle}
for an assumed population denoted by
\mathbf{\Lambda}
is given by

\langle VT\rangle(\mathbf{\Lambda})=\mathcal{\xi}(\mathbf{\Lambda})VT_{\rm total}

(1)

\sigma_{\langle VT\rangle}=VT_{\rm total}{\sigma_{\mathcal{\xi}}}

(2)

VT_{\rm total}=T_{\rm obs}\int^{z_{\rm max}}_{0}\mathrm{d}z\frac{1}{1+z}\frac{\mathrm{d}V_{c}}{\mathrm{d}z}

(3)

where
\xi(\mathbf{\Lambda})
is the fraction of detectable mergers which depends on a chosen detection threshold,
VT_{\rm total}
is the hypervolume spanned by the injected population assuming a constant number of events per unit time per unit comoving volume within a sphere of redshift
z_{\rm max}
,
\sigma_{\mathcal{\xi}}
is the square root of the variance in the estimate of
\mathcal{\xi}
, and
T_{\rm obs}
denotes the time spanned by the augmented data containing simulated signals, which in this case is the duration of
O4a
.

We consider two fiducial populations from which simulated sources are drawn to assess the sensitivity of the search: a
BBH
population with at least one
SSM
component, and a
BNS
population, which includes tidal interaction effects, extending into the sub-solar regime.
The
BBH
population is largely similar to previously considered fiducial populations
(
23
)
, but an
SSM

BNS
population has not been considered for such studies previously.
If
SSM

BNS
systems exist, their
GW
signals will exhibit effects due to tidal interactions.
These effects are significantly more pronounced for low-mass
NS
, so the sensitivity of the
SSM
search will depend on the composition of the assumed population.
We model the
BNS
signals using the
IMRPhenomXP_NRTidalv3
waveform model
(
118
;
8
)
with tidal effects given by the
GPPVA+DD2
equation of state
(
129
)
as implemented in the
LALSuite
library for
GW
data analysis
(
96
)
.
Tidal effects for systems including low-mass
NS
are extrapolated from those found in numerical simulations of systems with components above
1\,M_{\odot}
.
However, this equation of state produces large-radius stars with tides near the upper limit allowed by previous
GW
observations
(
15
;
119
)
, so should give a reasonably conservative estimate for our sensitivity to
SSM

BNS
using template waveforms that do not include tides.

Table
2
summarizes the properties of the
BBH
and
BNS
fiducial populations (
\mathbf{\Lambda}_{0}
).
The lowest component mass for the
BBH
population is chosen in accordance with the search parameter space which extends to 0.2
M_{\odot}
.
For
BNS
, we restrict component masses to be greater than
0.5\,M_{\odot}
.
At
0.5\,M_{\odot}
, tidal effects extrapolated for the
GPPVA+DD2
equation of state are on the order of
10^{5}
and would be even higher at lower
NS
masses, thereby exacerbating systematic uncertainties from waveform validity.
The secondary component mass is restricted such that every source has at least one sub-solar mass component.
For both populations, we choose an equally weighted mixture model of a half normal distribution and a Beta distribution such that the dimensionless spin magnitude peaks at
\chi_{1,2}

=

0
with small support extending to high spins.
This choice is motivated by expected low spins for primordial black holes
(
74
;
55
;
64
;
65
)
, and observed low spins for Galactic BNS
(
136
)
without excluding support for highly-spinning systems entirely.
The component spins are isotropically distributed, i.e. the cosine of the angles between the orbital angular momentum vector and the component spin vectors are distributed uniformly.
The sources are distributed uniformly in comoving volume up to a maximum redshift
z_{\rm max}=

0.2
which results in a total hypervolume
VT_{\rm total}=

1.65\,\mathrm{Gpc^{3}\,yr}
.

Details on the calculation of the fraction of detectable mergers,
\mathcal{\xi}(\mathbf{\Lambda})
using searches performed on the fiducial injection population (
\mathbf{\Lambda}_{0}
) are provided in Appendix
A
.
The choice of
FAR
thresholds for estimating
\mathcal{\xi}(\mathbf{\Lambda})
are described in relevant sections.
We use the software package
GWPopulation

(
137
)
to compute the fraction of detectable mergers
\xi(\mathbf{\Lambda})
through importance sampling (Equation
A2
), and finally compute the sensitive hypervolume
\langle VT\rangle
(Equation
1
).
The following subsections detail the
\langle VT\rangle
for selected
BBH
and
BNS
models.
Additionally, we provide the complete injection datasets and search results in a supplemental data release
(
97
)
, allowing for the reweighting of these fiducial results to alternative populations.

V
Constraining Dark Matter

The upper limits on merger rates derived in Section
IV
can be cast into constraints on any model that predicts an observable number of binary mergers within the considered sub-solar mass search parameter space.
In this work we consider two possible sources of
BBH
mergers,
PBH
in Section
V.1
and
DBH
in Section
V.2
.
For each model, we present the constraints in terms of the maximum fraction of dark matter allowed by our observations that can be composed of
BH
below a solar mass.

V.1
Primordial Black Holes

In the literature, numerous
PBH
models have been proposed
(
48
;
32
)
, each predicting a different
PBH
abundance characterized by the fraction of the dark matter cosmological density composed of
PBH
, defined as
f_{\mathrm{PBH}}=\rho_{\mathrm{PBH}}/\rho_{\mathrm{DM}}
,
where
\rho_{\mathrm{PBH}}
and
\rho_{\mathrm{DM}}
are the present-day mass densities of
PBH
and dark matter, respectively.
Constraining
f_{\mathrm{PBH}}
robustly using
GW
observations is challenging because, for a given

These variations primarily arise from differences in the initial mass distributions and clustering properties of
PBH
.
Moreover, the merger rate predictions remain highly uncertain as they are strongly influenced by complex phenomena such as many-body interactions and non-linear gravitational effects, which are difficult to model accurately.

PBH
binaries are typically considered to form in two scenarios, early binaries (EB) and late binaries (LB).
Early binaries form shortly after
PBH

PBH
decouple from the Universe’s expansion and form eccentric binaries through tidal interactions with nearby
PBH

(
105
)
.
Consequently, merger rates of early binaries are strongly influenced by initial
PBH
clustering and mass distribution.
In contrast, late binaries form in dense halos through dynamical interactions, with merger rates shaped by local density and halo properties
(
131
)
.

V.2
Dissipative Dark Matter

Dissipative dark matter is a class of models which posit that dark matter may have complex particle interactions similar to those of the Standard Model, including dissipative processes.
Whenever there is a fermion in the model, dark matter has a Chandrasekhar limit analogous to that of the Standard Model, but with a value that depends on the dark matter particle mass(es)
(
103
;
66
;
130
;
56
;
51
;
40
)
.
In dissipative scenarios, particle interactions allow energy to be transported away from gravitationally bound systems such as dark matter halos.
These dissipative interactions become more efficient in denser regions, and some dense regions may dissipate energy efficiently enough for gravity to dominate, leading to the formation of ultra-compact objects made of dark matter.
When those are sufficiently massive, above the dark Chandrasekhar limit, they are
DBH
.
Therefore,
GW
signals from
DBH
binaries provide a way to probe the microphysics of dissipative dark matter even if it is not coupled to the Standard Model beyond gravity, or if the coupling is too weak to be detectable in the near future.

Although the fragmentation scale of the dark gas depends on the chemistry of the dark sector, the dynamical processes that lead to the formation of compact objects are analogous to those in star formation.
While simulation of such small-scale phenomena in novel dark matter scenarios remains challenging, it is reasonable to expect that the initial mass function and binary fraction of
DBH
are qualitatively similar to that of baryonic objects.
For the particular worked example of atomic dark matter
(
26
;
93
;
60
)
, where cooling and compact object formation has been extensively studied
(
123
;
124
;
76
;
125
)
, there is a close parallel between
DBH
formation and Population III star formation that further supports this statement.
Thus, we use a truncated power-law mass model characterized by a minimum mass
M_{\text{min}}
, power
-b
, and ratio of maximum mass to minimum mass
r=M_{\text{max}}/M_{\text{min}}
, and assume a binary fraction of
f_{\text{binary}}=0.26

(
130
)
in our analysis.
The total number of
DBH
in this population is set by
f_{\mathrm{DBH}}\equiv\rho_{\text{DBH}}/\rho_{\text{DM}}
where
\rho_{\text{DBH}}
and
\rho_{\text{DM}}
are the densities of
DBH
and dark matter, respectively.
The rate of
DBH
binaries merging in this population,
\mathcal{R}
, is computed as
(
132
)

\begin{split}\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{M}\mid f_{\mathrm{DBH}},\theta)}{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{M}}&=\Bigl[p(\mathcal{M}\mid t_{m},\theta)\times p(t_{m}\mid\theta)\Bigr]_{t_{m}=10\,\mathrm{Gyr}}\\
&\;\;\;\;\times\left(\frac{\rho_{\mathrm{DM}}\times f_{\mathrm{DBH}}\times f_{\mathrm{binary}}}{\langle M\rangle}\right),\end{split}

(9)

where
p(\mathcal{M}\mid t_{m},\theta)
is the distribution of
\mathcal{M}
with a time of merging
t_{m}
,
p(t_{m}\mid\theta)
is the probability distribution of times-to-merger (these two factors evaluated at
t_{m}=10\,\mathrm{Gyr}
),
\rho_{\text{DM}}=3.3\times 10^{19}\,M_{\odot}~\text{Gpc}^{-3}
, and
\langle M\rangle
is the average component mass of the
DBH
binaries.
The merger times are calculated using Peters formula for coalescing binaries
(
116
)
.

We use a Bayesian analysis to constrain this population of
DBH
by creating
\mathcal{M}
bins and modeling the number of detected binaries in each bin using a Poisson distribution.
With the non-detection of
SSM
events, the likelihood in the
i
–th chirp-mass bin is
P(0|\mathcal{R}_{i},\langle VT\rangle_{i},\mathcal{M})=e^{-\mathcal{R}_{i}\langle VT\rangle_{i}}
, where the rate
\mathcal{R}_{i}
is determined by integrating Equation (
9
) over the width of the
i
–th
\mathcal{M}
bin, and the sensitive hypervolume
VT_{i}
is informed by this
SSM
search.
To maintain a consistent null-detection result in every
\mathcal{M}
bin, we adopt a more stringent
FAR
threshold of
0.01~\mathrm{yr}^{-1}
when estimating the pipeline-specific
\langle VT\rangle_{\rm O4a}
for this analysis.
We use priors consistent with previous analyses
(
21
;
23
)
: uniform priors on
M_{\text{min}}
over the interval
[10^{-3}\,M_{\odot},3.1\,M_{\odot}]
,
b
over the interval
[-1,2]
, and
f_{\text{DBH}}
over the interval
[10^{-10},1]
, and a log-uniform prior on
r
over the interval
[2,1000]
.
Bayes’ law then states the log-posterior is

\ln p(f_{\text{DBH}},M_{\text{min}},r,b)=-\sum_{i}\mathcal{R}_{i}(f_{\text{DBH}},M_{\text{min}},r,b)\langle VT\rangle_{i}-\ln r+C,

(10)

where
C
is the normalization constant.

Previous analyses evaluated the posterior distribution using a grid-based method
(
21
;
23
)
, while we use the MCMC sampler
emcee

(
72

to explore the parameter space for faster convergence in this analysis.
We marginalize over

and

and

VI
Conclusion

We present results from
SSM
searches for compact objects in data from the first part of the fourth LIGO–Virgo–KAGRA observing run
O4a
.
No significant candidate, other than the previously published candidate GW230529_181500
(
1
)
, is reported by the three participating searches.
Although all searches detect GW230529_181500, it is excluded as a possible
SSM
detection because the component masses of the source binary are reliably known to be greater than a solar mass.
Given the null detection result, we report upper limits on the merger rates for
SSM

BBH
with masses greater than
0.2\,M_{\odot}
using the estimated search sensitivity.

Using the inferred sensitive hypervolume and limits on the merger rate of
SSM

BBH
, we update the constraints on two dark matter models:
PBH
, and
DBH
that form in a dissipative dark matter model.
While both models can populate the sub-solar and super-solar regime, we turn to the
SSM
parameter space for clear evidence of such objects because no known astrophysical channel produces
SSM
black holes.

O4a
data alone provides only marginal improvement over previous limits
(
23
)
.

The sensitive hypervolume is subject to both statistical and systematic uncertainties that directly influence the resulting merger rate upper limits and constraints on dark matter models.
Statistical uncertainty from the finite injection sampling is compounded by effects like calibration errors, and waveform systematics.
We adopt a more conservative, binned approach to report the sensitivity of searches ensuring the limits remain robust against sampling fluctuations.

In the
PBH
scenario, we set informative constraints on the fraction of dark matter in
PBH

f_{\mathrm{PBH}}
in late-forming binaries for the first time, with
f_{\mathrm{PBH}}<1
at the 90% C.L. for
m_{\mathrm{PBH}}\gtrsim{0.9\,M_{\odot}}
using the
\langle VT\rangle
from
O4a
.
For early-forming
PBH
binaries, we obtain the strongest constraints from gravitational wave observations to date, with
f_{\mathrm{PBH}}
upper limits between
{0.4}
and
{0.07}
over the range of
PBH
masses probed by this search.
For the dissipative dark matter scenario, we find that
SSM

DBH
can only form
{(1.2\,\mathrm{to}\,1.3)\times 10^{-5}}
of the total dark matter density in the Universe.

For the first time, we report the sensitivity of
SSM
searches to binaries containing low-mass neutron stars.
We find that the typical sensitive hypervolume is
\langle VT\rangle

\sim{10}^{-3}\text{\,}{\mathrm{Gpc}}^{3}\text{\,}\mathrm{yr}
for populations of
BNS
with at least one
SSM
neutron star.
These searches can also provide insight into matter at high densities given the overlap of the neutron star and
SSM
parameter space, serving as motivation for low-latency
SSM
searches
(
30
;
79
)
which started to release alerts in early 2025.

Recently,
92
reported results from an
SSM
search that includes tidal effects for low mass neutron stars covering binaries with
0.1\,M_{\odot}\leq m_{1}(m_{2})\leq 2\,M_{\odot}(1\,M_{\odot})
, and aligned spins
-0.05\leq\chi_{1,2}\leq 0.05
.
They report no significant candidate from this search, and find consistent limits on the merger rate for overlapping regions of the parameter space.

The LIGO detectors resumed observation with improved sensitivity, and were joined by the Virgo detector in the
O4b
(
O4b
), and by the KAGRA detector in the
O4c
(
O4c
).
With increased observing time as we include data from the later parts of the fourth observing run, we expect the search sensitivity to increase by a factor of 2–3 leading to improved constraints on the merger rate of
SSM

BBH
.
These advances will provide more stringent limits on the fraction of dark matter in
SSM
compact objects, or perhaps even detect a gravitational wave signal originating from the merger of
SSM
compact objects.
Such a detection would have deep implications for fundamental physics.

Appendix A
Methods to determine the Search Sensitivity

To quantify the sensitivity towards a population with model parameters
\mathbf{\Lambda}
that determine the population distribution of masses and spins, we determine the fraction of detectable mergers
\xi({\Lambda})
as follows:

\mathcal{\xi}(\mathbf{\Lambda})=\int p(d|\boldsymbol{\theta})p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{\Lambda})p_{\rm det}(d)\mathrm{d}d\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta},

(A1)

where
p(d|\boldsymbol{\theta})
is the likelihood of the data
d
given a
GW
source with parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
(the component mass and spins for the individual source),
p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{\Lambda})
is the likelihood of this source given the population-model hyperparameters
\mathbf{\Lambda}
, and
p_{\rm det}(d)
is the probability that the data
d
produces a trigger above the detection threshold in a search.
In practice,
p_{\rm det}(d)
is determined by the detection statistic assigned by the search.

The importance sampling approach from
67
is adopted to calculate
\xi(\mathbf{\Lambda})
for various choices of
\mathbf{\Lambda}
.
We draw
N_{\rm inj}
source properties from a fiducial population model described by
\mathbf{\Lambda}_{0}
, and inject simulated signals drawn from the fiducial population into data from
O4a
.
Subsequently, the searches analyze this data to determine the injection recovery.
Since the injections are randomly sampled from
\mathbf{\Lambda}_{0}
,
\xi(\mathbf{\Lambda})
can be computed through a Monte Carlo sum over the subset of found detections denoted by the set of
GW
source parameters
\{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\}_{\rm found}
, which pass the chosen detection threshold as follows:

\mathcal{\xi}(\mathbf{\Lambda})\approx\frac{1}{N_{\rm inj}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\}_{\rm found}}\frac{p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{\Lambda})}{p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{\Lambda}_{0})},

(A2)

where
p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{\Lambda}_{0})
denotes the probability of the detected source drawn from the fiducial population (
\mathbf{\Lambda}_{0}
), while
p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{\Lambda})
denotes the probability of the detected source given the population distribution of interest (
\mathbf{\Lambda}
).

2
summarizes the fiducial population describing the source parameters for simulated signals.

32
s apart.

PyCBC
and

filtered the
BBH
