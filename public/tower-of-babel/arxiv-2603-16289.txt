VisBrowse-Bench: Benchmarking Visual-Native Search for Multimodal Browsing Agents
Zhengbo Zhang 1,2,∗,† , Jinbo Su 3,2,∗,† , Zhaowen Zhou 2,∗ , Changtao Miao 2,∗ , Yuhan Hong 4 , Qimeng Wu 5 , Yumeng Liu 6 , Feier Wu 5 , Yihe Tian 5 , Yuhao Liang 7 , Zitong Shan 2 , Wanke Xia 5 , Yi-Fan Zhang 1 , Bo Zhang 2 , Zhe Li 2 , Shiming Xiang 1,‡ , Ying Yan 2,‡ 1 CASIA, 2 Ant Digital Technologies, Ant Group, 3 RUC, 4 FZU, 5 THU, 6 USTB, 7 PKU

Abstract
The rapid advancement of Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) has enabled browsing agents to acquire and reason over multimodal information in the real world. But existing benchmarks suffer from two limitations: insufficient evaluation of visual reasoning ability and the neglect of native visual information of web pages in the reasoning chains. To address these challenges, we introduce a new benchmark for visual-native search, VisBrowse-Bench. It contains 169 VQA instances covering multiple domains and evaluates the models’ visual reasoning capabilities during the search process through multimodal evidence cross-validation via text-image retrieval and joint reasoning. These data were constructed by human experts using a multi-stage pipeline and underwent rigorous manual verification. We additionally propose an agent workflow that can effectively drive the browsing agent to actively collect and reason over visual information during the search process. We comprehensively evaluated both open-source and closed-source models in this workflow. Experimental results show that even the best-performing model, Claude-4.6-Opus only achieves an accuracy of 47.6%, while the proprietary Deep Research model, o3-deep-research only achieves an accuracy of 41.1%. The code and data can be accessed at: https://github.com/ZhengboZhang/VisBrowse-Bench

Specifically, most current benchmarks (e.g., MMSearch

Such tasks do not require fine-grained understanding of multimodal information and thus fail to sufficiently challenge models’ multimodal comprehension capabilities in deep research scenarios, instead primarily emphasizing tool-calling abilities.

Moreover, even though some benchmarks (e.g., MMSearch-Plus
[
15
]
and VDR-Bench
[
25
]
) require initial visual perception of text-image queries, the subsequent information gathering process degenerates into single-modal text traversal. Existing benchmarks structure their search space such that once the query image yields an entity name or caption, all downstream reasoning can be completed through textual document retrieval and synthesis. The search trajectory never necessitates grounding, parsing, or reasoning over additional visual information discovered during the search process. The task thus degenerates into text-only browsing, failing to assess whether models can dynamically acquire and integrate visual information when it is not provided upfront but must be actively sought across web pages containing multimodal information. This ability to conduct visual search and understanding throughout the task is vital in real-world retrieval, but existing benchmarks fail to evaluate this capability, limiting further development.

To address these challenges, we introduce
VisBrowse-Bench
, a challenging benchmark to comprehensively evaluate the reasoning and search capability of multimodal browsing agents. VisBrowse-Bench relies on two core design principles: the integration of multimodal information within the reasoning chain, and an inherent dependence on visual capabilities. We constructed our benchmark through a multi-stage, expert-guided pipeline, resulting in 169 VQA instances that cover seven distinct domains. Based on a seed entity with inherent visual ambiguity, domain experts recursively construct multi-hop reasoning chains, which require locating and reasoning novel visual evidence that cannot be paraphrased textually, alongside textual sources that provide complementary but insufficient information. Furthermore, we propose an efficient agentic workflow for visual reasoning and visual information retrieval during the search process. In this workflow, the agent is driven to actively perform visual reasoning and visual information retrieval using a rich set of tools. The preview performance of the MLLMs using our workflow on VisBrowse-Bench is shown in Figure
2
. The best performance in VisBrowse-Bench is achieved by Claude-4.6-Opus, with an accuracy of 47.6%, while most models achieve an accuracy of around 30%.

Our key contributions can be summarized as follows:

•

We formalize the task of multimodal browsing, identifying two critical challenges in existing benchmarks: insufficient evaluation of visual reasoning ability and the neglect of visual-native information in the reasoning chains.

•

We propose VisBrowse-Bench, a new benchmark comprising 169 rigorously validated instances constructed by human experts. This benchmark aims to jointly evaluate the search and visual reasoning capabilities of multimodal browsing agent systems.

•

We introduce a multi-turn visual information retrieval-reasoning agentic workflow to solve multimodal browsing problems in the real world. Compared to direct answer, the models’ performance are significantly improved using our workflow, but performance limitations still exist. The results demonstrate that the agentic workflow is effective and our benchmark is challenging.

Multimodal browsing benchmarks are crucial for evaluating the performance of multimodal browsing agents, but existing benchmarks still have limitations. MMSearch

VisBrowse-Bench

Our benchmark focuses on seven high-interest categories: Media, Life, Art, Geography, Technology, Sport and Finance, and each category has several subcategories .
Examples of each category in VisBrowse-Bench are shown in Figure
3
.
To ensure high-quality and data diversity, the question and answer pairs in each category were obtained through collaborative work by two experts in the field. Each question is an image–text bundle: a textual query paired with a small set of images containing initial entity information.

In this section, we will outline the details of VisBrowse-Bench. The entire pipeline includes the development of data design criteria, data collection, and a strict validation process.

3.1
Data Design Principles

Our goal is to jointly evaluate the agent’s capabilities in deep search and thinking-with-image, requiring the agent to search for specific visual information during the process and reason with the visual information to obtain evidence. This emphasizes that two key principles must be included in the data design process:

Multimodal Information Integration:

To evaluate genuine multi-modal deep search capabilities, we enforce that both the query formulation and evidence acquisition phases require active processing of visual and textual modalities. Each question is paired with a set of reference images containing task-critical cues, ensuring that query understanding necessitates visual perception. And the search space comprises interleaved text documents and visual assets. Successful evidence gathering requires the model to issue multi-modal queries and rank mixed-modality results.

Visual Competency Enforcement:

All visual information is designed to be structurally indispensable, and it cannot be substituted by textual captions or semantic descriptions, compelling the model to invoke core visual competencies, including spatial grounding, attribute perception, and relational parsing. The problem uses spatial information to describe visual elements, requiring the model to perform precise visual localization. Furthermore, the visual information involves perceptual attributes missing from the text, prompting the model to perform pixel-level analysis. The final information integration for the answer requires cross-image reasoning, which necessitates visual alignment rather than text matching.

3.2
Data Collection

Two experts in each predefined category are independently responsible for selecting initial entities and managing related search keywords. These entities undergo rigorous selection to ensure a sufficient number of publicly available multimodal documents. For each initial entity, the experts initiate a systematic search process from publicly accessible data sources to obtain images that meet the above principles, especially those containing visually ambiguous entities, overlapping visual regions, or multiple co-existing entities, which require precise spatial localization and pixel-level analysis for proper interpretation.

The collection workflow proceeds through an iterative entity-event-visual information traversal. The experts first retrieve real-world events associated with the identified entity, then locate complementary visual information through these events. And the visual information must implicate a distinct related entity, distinct from the previous hop. This process of visual retrieval, event bridging, and cross-modal entity transition repeats recursively to construct multi-hop evidence chains with no fewer than two visual evidences, ensuring that both the query formulation and evidence acquisition phases require active processing of visual and textual modalities. The accumulated evidence chains undergo expert synthesis into unified questions through careful annotation, with each question designed to require integration of all retrieved evidence and explicit grounding of intermediate reasoning steps in specific visual and textual blocks, thereby guaranteeing complete instantiation of multimodal integration and visual competency enforcement principles across the entire deep search trajectory.

3.3
Quality Validation

Our quality validation ensures strict adherence to both design principles through a multi-layer verification framework. At the instance level, each collected question undergoes the review of two experts to confirm that visual information is structurally indispensable. We additionally enforce that the solution of each question must span a minimum of three hops and at least two visual evidence blocks, with traversal checks ensuring no single-hop shortcut can yield correct answers.

For corpus-level quality assurance, we implement solvability and answer uniqueness verification procedures. Each question is independently solved by two domain experts with full reasoning traces documented to ensure that sufficient information exists for deterministic resolution. Instances where experts fail to reach consistent answers or identify critical evidence gaps are flagged for reconstruction or elimination.

3.5
Agentic Framework

To instantiate the multimodal deep search paradigm, we design a comprehensive agentic workflow equipped with five core tools: text search, image search, reverse image search, image crop and webpage visit. This agentic workflow enables the MLLMs to interact with real-world web environments through iterative tool invocation, dynamically acquiring and integrating heterogeneous evidence across modalities. The detail of agentic workflow is shown in Figure
2
. The workflow operates as a closed loop: the MLLM parses the multimodal queries to formulate initial search strategies, issues appropriate tool calls based on current evidence gaps, processes returned results to update its internal state, and refines subsequent queries until sufficient evidence is gathered for answer synthesis. This design ensures that visual and textual evidence acquisition are interleaved throughout the reasoning trajectory, preventing premature collapse into unimodal search patterns. 4.2
Results and Analysis

The main results on VisBrowse-Bench are shown in Table
2
, and we make the analysis as follow:

Inherent challenge.

Under the direct answer method, all models perform poorly, demonstrating the challenging nature of our benchmark in complex real-world search tasks, where the model’s parameterized knowledge is insufficient to handle complex queries requiring dynamically acquired evidence.

Insufficiency of textual evidence.

Introducing text search (+ TS) improves the performance of all models to varying degrees, indicating that the models can correctly interact with the tool and effectively acquire information. However, the improvement is limited for most models, demonstrating that the visual evidence needed to answer questions in our benchmark cannot be easily obtained through text retrieval alone.

Necessity of visual evidence.

The integration of image search (+ IS) further improves the performance of models, demonstrating the effectiveness of our framework in driving the model to actively discover the visual evidence needed to solve the problem. However, the Claude-4.6-Sonnet cannot use image search tools correctly, which causes the framework to fail and results in lower accuracy.

On our benchmark, the best-performing model achieves only 47.6% accuracy, while the top open-source model reaches merely 14.2%, reflecting the inherent difficulty of our dataset. Additionally, we find that these models with rich prior knowledge and strong reasoning capabilities tend to rely solely on text-based reasoning. Case studies are included in the Appendix
5.3
.

Conclusion

We identify two key gaps in existing multimodal browsing agent benchmarks: they inadequately test visual reasoning and overlook multimodal information integration in the reasoning process. To address this, we introduce VisBrowse-Bench, a benchmark constructed by human experts with 169 questions covering seven categories. It is designed so that multimodal information fusion is required during search and visual understanding is essential for task completion. We implement an agentic workflow with tools including text search, image search, reverse image search, image cropping, and webpage browsing to encourage proactive collection and reasoning over visual content.

5.1
Prompts of agentic framework

System Prompt

You are a Web Information Seeking Master. Your task is to thoroughly seek the internet for information and provide accurate answers to visual questions.

As you proceed, adhere to the following principles:

1. Decompose the original visual question into sub-questions and solve them step by step. Summarize the knowledge obtained from the previous round of dialogue, then think about what is next sub-question.

2. Whether you can answer the question or not, you should describe the image in detail. if the image includes multiple sub-image, you should describe each one separately.

3. Before calling any tools, you must provide a brief explanation of why you are calling the tool and what you expect to achieve.

4. You should provide the final answer within 15 turns, regardless of whether all valid information has been collected.

Input Prompt

You are an intelligent agent engaged in a conversation with a user. The user poses a question and provides a corresponding image for context. As an agent, you approach the problem with care and methodical precision, following a multi-step process to arrive at a solution. You utilize a variety of tools, ensuring that the information gathered from each one is cross-validated before you reach a final answer. Rather than relying on any single tool for accuracy, you employ multiple tools iteratively to prioritize the comprehensiveness and reliability of your responses.

To be successful, it is very important to follow the following rules:

1. The assistant starts with one or more cycles of (thinking about which tool to use -> performing tool call -> waiting for tool response), and ends with (thinking about the answer -> answer of the question).

2. If additional visual information is needed during the searching process, ’image_search’ tool can be used to search for images.

3. You can use ’crop_image’ tool to zoom in on a specific region of the image and search for it.

4. You can only make one tool call per round and wait for the tool’s response.

5. Your answer should be inside ’<answer></answer>’ tags, and the answer must be the most concise output.
