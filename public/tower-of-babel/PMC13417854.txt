Efficacy of phage vB_EcoP_ZCEC16 in controlling Escherichia coli contamination in fresh poultry meat
Authors: Azza G Kamel, Zainab K Hammouda, Sohaila Tarek, Yousef Nassar, Eman E Elawsya, Ahmed Osama, Bishoy Maher Zaki, Mohammad Gharaibeh, Rashad Al-Hindi, Ihab Habib, Ayman El-Shibiny
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAvian pathogenic Escherichia coli (APEC) poses a substantial economic and health challenges for poultry and meat industries. One of the promising alternative approaches to overcome bacterial contamination in food products is bacteriophage, due to its high specificity, safety, and effectiveness. We aimed to isolate a bacteriophage against E. coli causing poultry diseases and investigate its potential as biocontrol/therapeutic agent. A novel E. coli phage vB_EcoP_ZCEC16 was isolated from poultry samples. vB_EcoP_ZCEC16 is a podophage as visualized by transmission electron microscope, and it exhibited a high thermal and pH stability, and high lytic activity at MOIs 0.1 and 1 demonstrated by rapid reduction in bacterial optical density. Whole-genome analysis clustered the phage within Kuravirus genus and confirmed its virulence and the absence of antimicrobial resistance genes. The phage was also safe at high concentration (109 PFU/mL) on human fibroblast cells (HSF) and human colon carcinoma (Caco-2) cell lines as no cytotoxic effect was observed after 24 h. In addition, the lowest tested phage MOI (0.1) was the most protective to HSF cells against bacterial infection. Finally, phage vB_EcoP_ZCEC16 was evaluated for its biocontrol efficacy, where it significantly reduced the count of E. coli contaminating fresh poultry meat after 2 h of treatment. Introduction

Foodborne diseases remain a major global public health concern, causing an estimated 600 million illnesses and 420,000 deaths annually [1]. Poultry meat is among the most frequently implicated sources of foodborne outbreaks, largely due to contamination by pathogenic bacteria such as Escherichia coli, Salmonella, and Campylobacter [2, 3]. While most E. coli strains are commensal inhabitants of the avian gut, certain lineages, known as avian pathogenic E. coli (APEC), are responsible for colibacillosis—an extraintestinal infection associated with high morbidity, mortality, and substantial economic losses in the poultry industry [4–6]. APEC strains fall within the Extraintestinal Pathogenic E. coli (ExPEC) group and share virulence and resistance determinants with human uropathogenic and septicemic E. coli strains [7, 8]. These shared genetic features, including adhesins, iron acquisition systems, and serum resistance factors—raise concerns regarding zoonotic transmission and horizontal transfer of antimicrobial resistance genes from poultry to humans [5, 9–11]. Therefore, multidrug-resistant APEC are considered a One Health challenge, emphasizing the need for sustainable antimicrobial alternatives [12].

In addition to planktonic contamination, APEC strains are capable of forming biofilms on poultry carcasses and food-contact surfaces, enhancing their survival during processing, storage, and distribution [12, 13]. The gastrointestinal tract of poultry represents a primary reservoir of E. coli, and contamination of carcasses frequently occurs during slaughter, especially at the evisceration stage, when intestinal contents probably may get in contact with the carcass surface [14, 15]. Therefore, the intestinal E. coli may be transferred to poultry meat and subsequently enter the food chain process. Because biofilm is more resistant to disinfectants, environmental stressors, and antimicrobial agents; they pose a significant challenge conventional sanitation strategies in poultry processing facilities [16]. Consequently, alternative control strategies that can effectively target both planktonic and biofilm-associated bacteria are urgently needed.

During food processing, several advanced thermal and nonthermal technologies, such as high pressure, ultraviolet light, ozone, ultrasound, and cold plasma, have been developed for microbial control; however, their antimicrobial efficacy remains restricted by limited effectiveness against bacterial spores, induction of sublethal injury, and microbial stress adaptation [17, 18]. Additionally, combining these technologies with mild heat can enhance inactivation but may still Adversely affect food quality and fail to prevent microbial recovery [19, 20]. Moreover, antibiotic use in poultry production contributes to selective pressure that drives antimicrobial-resistance dissemination across the food chain [21]. These drawbacks highlight the necessity of targeted, harmless antimicrobial approaches that can effectively control foodborne pathogens without inducing cross-protection or antimicrobial resistance.

Bacteriophage-based biocontrol provides a promising approach for pathogen reduction in food systems. The effectiveness and safety of bacteriophage (phage) because they exhibit high host specificity, can multiply at the site of infection, and are generally recognized as safe for humans, animals, and the environment [22–24]. These characteristics make phages suitable for both upstream and downstream stages of food production and handling. Lytic phages are generally preferred for food biocontrol due to their lower potential for horizontal gene transfer compared to temperate phages, which can establish lysogeny and facilitate the dissemination of virulence determinants and antibiotic resistance genes [25, 26]

In poultry production, phage preparations have been successfully applied through feed supplementation, carcass-surface treatment, and environmental sanitation to control Salmonella and E. coli contamination [27–30]. This study aimed at isolating and characterizing a novel virulent E. coli phage with biocontrol efficacy against E. coli contamination in fresh poultry meat.

Materials and method

Sample collection

Twenty-three intestinal samples (~ 10 g each) were aseptically collected from broiler chickens slaughtered for commercial food production at poultry shops, between January and February 2025 across Cairo and Giza governorates. Samples were transported to the lab on ice and processed within 6 h of collection. These samples were used to isolate poultry-associated E. coli and their phages.

Note: Bacterial isolates were obtained from the gastrointestinal tract of poultry, which serves as a primary reservoir of E. coli. These bacteria can contaminate carcasses during slaughter and evisceration; therefore, intestinal isolates were selected as they represent strains potentially involved in poultry meat contamination.

Isolation of E. coli from poultry

Each poultry sample was inoculated into 10 ml of MacConkey broth (Oxoid, UK) and incubated for 18—24 h at 37 °C without agitation. A loopful of the enriched cultures were then plated onto MacConkey agar (Oxoid, UK) and incubated for another 18—24 h at 37°C. Colonies with typical E. coli morphology (pink, lactose-fermenting) were further subcultured for purification. Lactose-fermenting isolates were identified using Vitek MS automated system (bioMérieux, France). A reference strain of E. coli (ATCC 25922) was used as a control for the validation of both the growth conditions and identification. The E. coli identified isolates were stocked in 20% (v/v) glycerol at −80°C. Prior to each experiment, a bacterial day culture was prepared by inoculating a colony from MacConkey agar into one ml of tryptic soy broth (TSB; Oxoid, UK) at 37 °C for 24 h.

Screening, isolation, and amplification of bacteriophage

Bacteriophages were isolated from the same poultry samples using enrichment method [31]. To facilitate phage propagation, each one ml sample filtrate was mixed (1:1 v/v) with an exponentially growing culture of E. coli in TSB and incubated for 18—24 h at 37°C. The mixture was centrifuged twice at 8000 rpm for 10 min, and the supernatant of the phage lysate was spotted onto E. coli lawns prepared by double-layer agar method [31]. Individual plaques were picked and purified through five successive picking of single plaque. High titer lysate (10⁹ PFU/mL) was obtained by propagation in isolation host and stored in SM buffer (100 mM NaCl, 10 mM MgSO₄, 50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 0.01% gelatin) at 4°C.

Host range determination

The host lysis range of the isolated phages was studied against 23 bacterial isolates of E. coli using the double-layer agar method [32]. Briefly, 10 µl of each phage lysate were spotted onto bacterial lawns and then incubated for 18—24 h at 37°C. The lysis patterns were categorized as positive (clear zone or turbid zone) or negative including the resistant isolates (no lysis).

Transmission Electron Microscope (TEM)

The phage morphology was examined using transmission electron microscopy (TEM), at the National Research Center (CRC) Cairo, Egypt, following previous protocol [33]. In brief, 10 µl of high titer phage lysate (1010 PFU/mL) was sonicated for 20 min on an ultrasonicator (Crest Ultrasonics Corp., New Jersey, USA). Then few drops were loaded on carbon-coated copper grid and left to dry. Few drops of 1% (w/v) phosphotungstic acid (pH 7.2) were loaded on the grid and left to dry. The grid was then loaded with the sample and examined for phage by HR-TEM (JEOL, JEM-2100, Tokyo, Japan) operated at accelerating voltage 200 kV. Measurements of the capsid and tail length were obtained from micrographs using Gatan Digital Micrograph software (Gatan Inc., USA). Morphotype classification followed ICTV guidelines.

Phage stability (pH and temperature)

To assess the temperature stability of vB_EcoP_ZCEC16, the phage stock (109 PFU/mL) was incubated at various temperatures (−80, −20, 4, 37, 50, 60 and 70 °C). Likewise, the phage pH stability was evaluated by incubating the phage suspension in SM buffer adjusted to different pH 2, 3, 4, 6, 7, 10, 12, and 14. After one hour of treatment, aliquots were collected and spotted using the double layer agar method. Results were expressed as the mean ± SD from three independent replicates. The enumerated phage titers at each condition of 37 °C and pH 7.0 were considered control reference, to which the phage titers from other conditions were compared statistically [34]

Time killing curve

The lytic activity of the phage was quantified using time kill curve assay in 96-well microplates, as previously described [35]. Each well contained 180 μl bacterial culture (106 CFU/mL) and 20 μl of phage suspension at different multiplicities of infection (MOI) 0.1, 1, 10, 100. Control wells contained bacteria without phage, and blanks contained sterile TSB media. The microtiter plate was incubated at 37 °C for 12 h, and the optical density (OD600) was recorded every 30 min using FLUOstar® Omega plate reader, (BMG LABTECH, Germany).

Phage DNA extraction, genome sequencing, and characterization

Bacteriophage genomic DNA extraction and genome sequencing

The phage DNA was extracted utilizing phenol–chloroform-isoamyl alcohol method [36]. Briefly, high-titer phage lysate (1010 PFU/mL) was filter-sterilized through a 0.22 μm membrane, mixed with lysis buffer, and incubated at 56 °C for 1h. An equal volume of phenol–chloroform–isoamyl alcohol was added, and the mixture was centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min. The aqueous phase was collected, supplemented with sodium acetate and ice-cold isopropanol, and incubated at − 80 °C to precipitate the DNA. After centrifugation, the pellet was washed with 90% and then 70% ethanol, air-dried, and resuspended in nuclease-free water. DNA concentration and purity were assessed using the FLUOstar Omega Microplate reader (BMG LABTECH, Germany).

Indexed sequencing libraries were prepared using the Illumina DNA Prep Kit (Illumina, 20,018,705) and IDT for Illumina DNA/RNA UD Indexes Set B (Illumina, 20,042,666). Library amplification was performed for five PCR cycles. Quantification was done using the Qubit 4 Fluorometer with the Qubit 1X dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Q33231). Library fragment size distribution was assessed on the Agilent TapeStation 4200 using the High Sensitivity D1000 ScreenTape Assay (Agilent, 5067–5584 and 5067–5585). Libraries were pooled in equal volumes and quantified using the KAPA Library Quantification Kit for Illumina platforms (Roche, KK4824). The pooled libraries were sequenced on an Illumina MiSeq using the MiSeq Reagent Nano Kit v2 (500 cycles, Illumina, MS-103—1003) at the University of Nottingham, generating 250 bp paired-end reads.

Genome assembly and processing

The quality of the sequence reads was checked using Fastq Utilities and de novo assembled using the Unicycler v.0.4.8 on BVBRC platform [36]. The nucleotide sequence of the assembled contig was searched by BLASTn against the NCBI nucleotide database to identify the closest related phage genomes [37]. Using progressiveMauve, the phage genome and its closest relatives (hits showing ≥ 70% query coverage and ≥ 70% nucleotide identity) were visualized and aligned [38]. Based on this alignment, the orientation and arrangement of the phage genome sequence was modified by using UGENE software v.52.1 to match Escherichia phage vB_EcoP_SU10 (NC_027395.1- closest RefSeq) [39].

Genome annotation and safety evaluation

Automated annotation was performed by using a customized RASTtk pipeline. Open reading frames (ORFs) were predicted in the following order: glimmer, geneMark, and prodigal. The annotation workflow was adjusted to run the protein phage option before kmer-v2. Also, functional annotation was performed using PHOLD, which relies on prodigal for ORF calling [37, 40]. The ORFs that differed in start codons, or whose presence varied between the two calling tools were evaluated based on presence of Shine-Dalgarno sequences (SDS), amino acid length, homology with related phage proteins, synteny, and absence of overlap with neighboring genes. ShineFind (Galaxy Version 21.1.0.0) were used to identify shine-dalgarno sequences for genes with different start codons [41]. A second functional annotation round was manually conducted based on the phage proteins homologs by using BLASTp and HHpred. The protein sequences were also analyzed by InterProScan to identify functional domains and protein features. The lysis genetic cassette encoding proteins (endolysin, holin, and spanins) were further analyzed for protein topology by using SignalP 6.0 and DeepTMHMM [42, 43]. The tRNA genes were while tRNA genes were identified on ARAGORN [44]. Transmembrane helices were predicted with DeepTMHMM, and potential polysaccharide depolymerases (DPOs) were identified using PhageDPO on Galaxy Docker platform [45]. Lastly, the curated genome annotation was visualized in a circular map on Proksee server [46]. The genome was screened for antimicrobial resistance genes by using the Resistance Gene Identifier (RGI) within the CARD server (RGI v5.2.1, CARD v3.2.3) [47]. Lysogeny-related markers were analyzed on PhageLeads server [45].

Phylogenetic and comparative genomic analysis

Phylogenetic classification at the family level was performed on ViPTree server, based on whole genome proteomic similarity [46]. The reference genomes with the highest tBLASTx similarity scores (SG), together with the top BLASTn hits and an outgroup genome having low SGs scores, were selected for intergenomic comparison using the Virus Intergenomic Distance Calculator (VIRIDIC) [48]. Genus-level classification in VIRIDIC was based on three metrics: (i) intergenomic similarity percentage, (ii) aligned genome fraction, and (iii) alignment length. Orthologue gene clusters were determined by using OrthoMCL on KBase to identify conserved signature genes [49]. The amino acid sequence of highly conserved signature genes including, terminase large subunit, DNA polymerase, and major capsid protein were used to construct a protein-based phylogenetic tree. To construct this tree, the amino acid sequences of the isolated phage and its closest relatives and a group of outliers from different families and morphotypes, were aligned by using ClustalW on MEGA v.11. The best-fit substitution model was identified using MEGA’s “Find Best DNA/Protein Models” function. The reliability of the inferred phylogenies was assessed by 500 bootstrap replicates, and tree branch lengths were measured as the number of substitutions per site. The final trees were visualized and annotated using MEGA v.11 [50].

Cytotoxicity of phage

Phage cytotoxicity was evaluated using Human Skin Fibroblast (HSF) and Human colon carcinoma (Caco-2) cell lines. Cells were cultured in 96-well plate with seeding density 104 cells/well, in DMEM complete media supplemented with10% fetal bovine serum, 100 I.U./ml penicillin and 100 (µg/ml) streptomycin), incubated at 37°C/5% CO2 for 24 h. Following, medium was discarded and replaced with serial concentrations of phage (109, 108, 107 and 106 PFU/mL), then incubated at 37°C/5% CO2 for 24 h. The cell imaging was performed using inverted microscope (Optika IM-3, Italy). After incubation, the medium was discarded followed by addition of MTT (stock concertation 5 mg/ml) and incubated for 4 h at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Lastly, the medium was discarded, and solubilizing agent (DMSO) was added and the O.D was measured at 570 nm.

Phage-bacterial dynamic interaction on HSF cells

To evaluate the efficacy of the phage against adherent bacteria, the bacteria was cultured with HSF cells and exposed to phage at MOIs (0.1, 1, 10 and 100). Throughout the experiment, the bacteria and phage titer were measured at different time points, and the HSF cell viability was measured at the end of the experiment (24 h), as described previously [34]. After confluency was checked, the cells were trypsinzed and cultured in 96-well plates with seeding density 5 × 104 cells/well, at 37°C/5% CO2 for 24 h.

An overnight culture of E17 was centrifuged at 8000 × g for 1 min, the pellet was resuspended with phosphate buffer saline (PBS), and OD was measured to reach a final bacterial concentration of 106 CFU/mL. Then prepared bacterial concentration was centrifuged and resuspend with complete DMEM without antibiotics.

Following the examination of cell adhesion on a 96-well plate, the old medium was discarded, and the cells were washed twice with PBS. Bacterial suspension in DMEM was added at 100 µl per well and incubated for one h at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Then, the planktonic bacteria were discarded, and 100 µl of phage was added at different MOIs (0.1, 1, 10 and 100). At time point zero, 10 µl from each well was aspired, bacterial and phage enumeration were assessed. The same procedure was repeated at time points of 0, 1, 2, 6 and 24 h. After 24 h of incubation, the media was discarded, the cells were washed twice with PBS, and 40 µl of trypsin was added and incubated for 15 min to facilitate cell detachment. Then, 60 µl of complete DMEM was added, and cells was counted using hemocytometer under inverted light microscope (Optika IM-3, Italy).

Biocontrol of E. coli infection on poultry meat

The phage was assessed for its biocontrol efficacy on poultry meat model, as stated previously [51]. Chicken breasts were purchased from a local market in Cairo, Egypt. The chicken breast was aseptically cut into 5 g cube-shaped pieces, which were swabbed with alcohol (70%) and sterile water, and then each cube side was exposed to UV for 10 min. The chicken meat cubes were transferred into sterile stomacher bags (Westlab, Australia) and divided into the following groups, of which each has 3 replicates:

Group 1: Bacterial control (treated with bacteria only).

Group 1: Bacterial control (treated with bacteria only).

Group 2: Treatment (treated with bacteria and phage).

Group 2: Treatment (treated with bacteria and phage).

Group 3: Phage control (treated with phage only).

Group 3: Phage control (treated with phage only).

Group 4: Broth control (treated with Maximum Recovery Diluent (MRD)).

Group 4: Broth control (treated with Maximum Recovery Diluent (MRD)).

Bacterial inoculum was added onto the cubes of chicken breast at 103 CFU/mL and left for 20 min at 4°C. Following, phage was added at concentration 108 PFU/mL to the pieces of chicken breast cubes of treatment and phage control groups. All chicken pieces were stored at 4 °C for 72 h, during which random cubes were selected at 1, 2, 4, 6, 12, 24, 48 and 72 h to enumerate viable bacteria and phage counts on Tryptone Bile Glucuronic Agar (TBX) agar plates.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 8.0.2).

Genome sequence accession number

The complete genome sequence of phage vB_EcoP_ZCEC16 has been deposited in the NCBI GenBank database under the accession number PX645697.

Results

E. coli isolation and vitek confirmation

Twenty-three E. coli isolates (ZCE1 to ZCE23) were identified by using Vitek MS system.

Phage isolation and host range

Six phages were isolated from the poultry intestines. All these phages had lytic activity on the E. coli isolates, yet they had different host range. Notably, phage coded as EZC10 had the highest number (11) of susceptible bacterial isolates (Fig. 1). Accordingly, phage EZC10, namely vB_EcoP_ZCEC16, was selected for characterization and biocontrol efficacy evaluation.

Phage stability

Physiochemical stability of vB_EcoP_ZCEC16 was evaluated at various temperatures and pH. The vB_EcoP_ZCEC16 phage demonstrated stability at broad temperatures (−20, −80, 4, and 37 °C), with a titer 1010 PFU/mL (Fig. 3A). However, the phage titer showed a log reduction at 50 °C and showed a significant drop at 60 °C and 70 °C, with approximately 4 log reductions. Similarly, the phage demonstrated a strong stability at a wide range of pH values from 3 to 7, with a titer 1010 PFU/mL (Fig. 3B). A log reduction was observed with pH values from 10 and 11. However, with high acidity (pH 3), the phage significantly reduced by 3 log reduction. Additionally, at high alkalinity, the phage was significantly dropped with about 6.5 log reduction at pH 13.

Genome annotation and safety evaluation

A total of 130 open reading frames (ORFs) were predicted. Among these, 44 were functionally annotated, 28 were hypothetical proteins, and 58 were phage-related proteins of unknown function. The coding sequences were classified based on their product function, which included DNA packaging (three genes), host lysis (four genes), regulation of DNA metabolism and transcription function (five genes), 10 genes encoding for the virion structures, and 21 genes for replication and metabolism (Fig. 5A). One tRNA gene with anticodon for arginine (tRNA-Arg-TCT) was identified (Table S1). The lysis cassette is composed of a gene encodes for SAR endolysin and overlapping spanins encoding genes. The predicted amino acid sequence of endolysin, encoded by ORF16, had a signal peptide at the N-termini and muramidase domain (Fig. S1). The adjacent ORF15 encoded a protein with high sequence similarity to holins of related phages; however, no transmembrane domain (TMD) was detected, and only a signal peptide was observed at the first 30 amino acid residues. ORFs 35 and 36 encode two spanin system components, representing an inner-membrane spanin (i-spanin) containing an N-terminal TMD and an outer-membrane spanin (o-spanin) with N-terminal lipobox motif. Both spanins encoding genes were located adjacent to the endolysin encoding gene (Fig. S2). PhageDPO identified putative depolymerase-related domains in tail fiber proteins encoded by ORFs 13, 14, and 18 with confidence above 90%. The Resistance Gene Identifier (RGI) did not identify antimicrobial resistance markers, and PhageLeads analysis indicated no lysogeny-related markers, supporting a strictly lytic lifestyle.

Phylogenetic and comparative genomic analysis

The ViPTree proteomic comparison clustered the phage within the Pseudomonadota host group (Fig. 6A and B). Intergenomic similarity analysis using VIRIDIC grouped the isolated phage within the Kuravirus genus, alongside 14 related members, confirming its classification as a distinct species within the genus (Fig. 6C). OrthoMCL analysis of Kuravirus genomes identified 308 shared orthologous genes, including the terminase large subunit, DNA polymerase, and major capsid protein as conserved signature genes. The maximum likelihood method was applied for tree construction using the following models: LG for DNA polymerase, WAG + G for the major capsid protein, and WAG for the terminase large subunit. The phylogenetic trees of the conserved signature genes further supported the previous classification. The phage vB_EcoP_ZCEC16 was positioned within the Kuravirus lineage, but formed an independent branch separated from all reference genomes. The phage consistently appeared closest to Escherichia phages WF1101126, ES17, and SU10, yet without forming a distinct subclade, supported by moderate bootstrap values (55–70). The long branch lengths observed across the three trees indicate clear amino acid divergence from related Kuravirus members (Fig. 7).

Cytotoxicity assay

Phage cytotoxicity was determined using HSF and Caco-2 cell lines. vB_EcoP_ZCEC16 phage did not exhibit cytotoxicity with all administrative phage concentrations (109 to 106). The cell viability was ~ 100%, for both cell lines, compared to the control (Fig. 8A). The cell viability was confirmed with MTT assay images, which demonstrate the formazan formation in the live cell with all phage concentrations, compared to the control (Fig. 8B).

Dynamic of vB_EcoP_ZCEC16 phage- bacteria interaction on HSF cell line model

To evaluate the phage dynamic in vitro, the HSF cell line was used for mimicking the cellular environment. Following 1 h of phage infection to the adherence bacteria, the phage induced a decrease in bacterial count with an approximate decrease of 1.5 log units at all MOIs (Fig. 9A). After 2 h of infection, the bacterial concentration decreased by approximately 2 log reductions at MOIs (1 and 100), and by 3 log reductions at MOI of 10, while MOI 0.1 maintained a steady reduction of 1.5 log units. This indicated the different bactericidal activity among the tested MOIs, reflecting variations in the interaction efficiency between the phage and bacteria within the cellular model.

Bacterial-phage dynamic on HSF cell line model. A The bacterial measurement through different time points. B The phage measurement. C The cell viability following 24 h of bacterial-phage interaction. D Cell images (control cells, control bacterial, MOI 0.1, 1, 10 and 100)

After 6 h of phage infection, the bacterial began to show resistance at higher MOIs (100 and 10), while the culture infected with MOI 1 continued inducing bacterial reduction with 3.5 log decrease compared to the control. In contrast, infection with MOI 0.1 maintained a steady reduction of 1.5 log units. After 24 h of infection, all MOIs showed bacterial regrowth, and their bacterial concentration returned to levels comparable to the control, except with MOI 0.1, which have a log reduction with approximately 4 log reduction.

In addition to the bacterial reduction, the titer of phage vB_EcoP_ZCEC16 increased within the time points, suggesting effective phage generation (Fig. 9B). For the HSF cell line, the cell viability was evaluated after 24 h, and it showed that the cell viability with all MOIs was highly affected (40%), except with MOI 0.1 that maintained high viability comparable with the control (99.2%) (Fig. 9C). This was reflected in the microscopic examination of cell images, where the cells treated with MOI 0.1 retained normal morphology with adherent, elongated monolayer with boundaries, like the control (Fig. 9D). However, with MOIs (1, 10 and 100) the cells lost their fibroblast-like shape, exhibiting membrane damage and detachment. These results demonstrated the dose-dependent cytotoxic response to phage-bacterial interaction.

Bacteriophage as a biocontrol of E. coli infection in fresh poultry meat

The phage efficiency for treatment of contaminated poultry meat was evaluated using vB_EcoP_ZCEC16 phage. Breast poultry meat samples were inoculated with E. coli E17 isolate at ~ 103 CFU/mL and treated with vB_EcoP_ZCEC16 at ~ 108 PFU/mL (MOI 100000). The samples were stored at (4°C for 3 days).

With the control samples (without phage administration), E. coli showed increase throughout the incubation period with approximately 3.2 log (p < 0.05) (Fig. 10A). In contrast, with the application of vB_EcoP_ZCEC16 phage, the viable cell counts of E. coli decreased to values below the detection limit (10 CFU = 1 log), following 2 h of phage infection on inoculated poultry meat with 103 CFU/mL. Over the subsequent time points, till 48 h of incubation, the bacterial count in the phage-treated group remained relatively stable, below the detection limit, (~ 1 log), followed by bacterial regrowth observed at 72 h (4.2 log).

The Eco_ZCEC16 phage titer was measured, it showed increase following 1 h of infection (109 PFU/mL) and demonstrated consistency throughout time points till 24 h (Fig. 10B). However, following the phage showed a log reduction (108 CFU/mL) at 48 h and another log reduction at 72 h. This could be attributed to the depletion of susceptible bacterial host following the initial rapid lytic phase. Additionally, the phage storage and inactivation for long time, results in the adsorption to tissue components, which results in the phage titer decline following 72 h of incubation.

Discussion

Foodborne disease is a major threat to human health, and APEC causing colibacillosis infections is considered one of the leading causes to substantial economic losses [52, 53]. These losses are attributed to decreased egg and meat production, high mortality and culling rates, reduced hatching rates, growth retardation, and increased veterinary costs [54, 55]. Efforts are directed towards improving the safety of raw poultry products; however, several commonly employed decontamination approaches, including ultraviolet irradiation and ionizing radiation, show limited efficacy or negatively affect meat quality characteristics such as texture, odor, and color [56].Intestinal E. coli isolates represent a relevant source of E. coli stains that can be transferred to poultry meat and subsequently enter the food chain process. Therefore, targeting these isolates is critical for developing effective biocontrol strategies which aimed at reducing microbial contamination.

In the current study, a novel Escherichia coli phage vB_EcoP_ZCEC16 was characterized for its potential in controlling E. coli in poultry meat. The phage exhibited strong lytic activity and a moderately broad host range, which enhances its practical value for food related applications. As bacteriophage’s physicochemical stability is a critical factor influencing therapeutic suitability, formulation, and storage [57]. The phage showed robust stability across a wide pH range (3–12) and temperatures (−20 to 60 °C), consistent with previous reports of thermally and chemically resilient phages targeting E. coli and Salmonella [58–60].

Genomic analysis showed that vB_EcoP_ZCEC16 has the typical organization of a virulent podovirus, including DNA packaging, structural, replication, regulatory, and lysis modules [61–63], and lacks genes associated with lysogeny, antimicrobial resistance, or toxins, supporting its biosafety [64–67]. This is a critical safety feature, as lytic phages are less likely to mediate horizontal gene transfer compared to temperate phages. Tail fiber proteins with depolymerase-related domains suggest a role in host specificity and polysaccharide degradation, consistent with Kuravirus phages [68]. Morphological features observed by TEM and genome organization confirmed its classification as a podovirus within the Kuravirus genus, closely related to phages SU10, WF1101126, and EK5, yet showing species-level divergence, indicating it as a novel strain [46, 48].

The consistent pattern across all trees and the observed branch length differences support classification of vB_EcoP_ZCEC16 as a novel strain in Kuravirus species.

Safety evaluation demonstrated that vB_EcoP_ZCEC16 exhibited no cytotoxic effects on HSF cells, which indicate its compatibility with mammalian cells. These results are consistent with several studies showing the safety of phages when applied to various human cell lines including HT-29, HSF, A375 and Hela cells [59, 69].

In the phage dynamic assay on HSF cells, vB_EcoP_ZCEC16 demonstrated strong lytic activity at MOI 0.1, consistent with the time-kill curve, while maintaining cell viability comparable to the control. In contrast, higher MOIs (1, 10, and 100) reduced cell viability due to rapid bacterial lysis and the release of toxic components such as endotoxins and cell debris [70, 71]. Additionally, bacterial resistance emerged at all MOIs, a common outcome in vitro co-culture experiments result in the development of phage-resistant bacterial mutants, which led to the regional elimination of the phage population [72]

For effective bacteriophage application on raw chicken meat, a high dose of phage was recommended because the growth rate of the E. coli reduces during refrigerated storage (≈4°C), which limits the phage replication. Additionally, the possibility of phage to encounter the bacteria is reduced due to their limited distribution within the food matrix and potential inactivation by food ingredients [51].

The vB_EcoP_ZCEC16 phage demonstrated effectiveness in controlling E. coli contamination on poultry meat. The phage could induce a rapid reduction in bacterial counts to below the detection limit (10 CFU) after 2 h of phage treatment. This reduction level was maintained at the undetectable level throughout storage at 4 °C for 48 h (Fig. 10). These results indicated on the effectiveness of phage in maintaining prolonged inhibition of bacterial growth in freezer storage. These results are consistent with several previous studies demonstrating the phage treatment effectiveness was achieved when a high phage dose is applied, and low initial inoculum [73–75]. In a previous study evaluating the phage effectiveness at two storage temperatures, it was found that the phage (SE-phages) could reduce the Salmonella count in the chicken breast and skin to an undetectable level, whether storage at 4 or 25 °C [51]. Another study reported a reduction in S. typhimurium and S. enteritidis with 2.2 and 0.9 log reduction, respectively through the application of phage cocktail for 7 days at 4 °C [76]. Another investigation also demonstrated Salmonella reduction in chicken breast with about 1.1 log, when applying commercial phage for 6 h at 4 °C [77]. Another research showed the effectiveness of phage application in raw chicken with a significant decrease of Salmonella by 5.4 log CFU/g following 3 h of treatment at room temperature [76]. Beyond poultry, phages showed successful results when applied in various food matrices including milk, rice gruel, and sausage contaminated with E. coli strains, supporting their biocontrol potential role in food products [58, 78].

Several limitations were recognized in this study. Firstly, we did not assess the vB_EcoP_ZCEC16 phage efficacy on E. coli biofilm, which may reduce the phage effectiveness and may require the use of phage cocktail for enhanced antimicrobial performance. Additionally, the potential impact of phage on the poultry meat quality such as taste, odor and texture were not studied. While phages are frequently considered harmless and have no effect on food quality, it is essential to verify that vB_EcoP_ZCEC16 has no adverse effect on the final product. Moreover, while UV treatment was employed to lessen background microbiota and ensure controlled inoculation conditions, this method reflects a laboratory model and may not completely mimic industrial poultry processing environments.

Future work intends widening the sample set of bacterial strains to include an additional variety of pathogenic serotypes and mutant variations, facilitating a more comprehensive assessment of the phage's host range and therapeutic efficacy. Additionally, several studies should assess additional quality parameters, such as pH, water activity, lipid oxidation (TBARS), texture, and sensory characteristics, to further evaluate the impact of bacteriophage treatments on the quality of poultry meat during storage. Moreover, conducting in vivo trials in broiler chickens is highly recommended to confirm the phage effectiveness for controlling E. coli spread in the poultry and food industries. Further, functional assays such as transduction experiments and detailed genomic analyses should be studied to fully assess the potential for unintended gene transfer and to confirm the biosafety of the phage for food biocontrol applications.

Our results concluded that bacteriophage is a highly recommended alternative to various antimicrobial intervention techniques, such as antibiotics, heat treatment and high pressure for food products.
