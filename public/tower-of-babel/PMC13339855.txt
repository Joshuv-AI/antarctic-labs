Design and usability-testing of an asynchronous online course for concussion management: Pedagogical framework and early impact on clinician confidence
Authors: Joshua Plener, Gaelan P. Connell, Carol Cancelliere, Scott Howitt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe objectives of this study were to (1) describe the pedagogical framework and design of an asynchronous online concussion management course for clinicians and (2) report usability testing results.MethodsA multidisciplinary team developed an interactive, evidence-based course in Articulate Rise 360 software. Modern instructional strategies were incorporated, including chunking, quizzes, gaming elements, video demonstrations, and clinical vignettes. Content drew on current guidelines for adult and pediatric concussion and the 6th International Consensus on Concussion in Sport. Usability testing was conducted with a convenience sample of clinicians using an adapted System Usability Scale, Likert ratings for relevance and impact, and open-ended questions. The course was refined based on participant feedback.ResultsEight clinicians (3 physiotherapists, 3 chiropractors, 2 occupational therapists) from 3 Canadian regions completed usability testing. All participants (8/8) felt confident using and navigating the course, with 7/8 (87.5%) agreeing that its functions were well integrated. All participants reported improved confidence in adult concussion assessment and management, and 7/8 (87.5%) in adolescent concussion management. Although 7/8 (87.5%) felt confident assessing persistent symptoms, only 3/8 (37.5%) felt confident managing them. Qualitative feedback emphasized interactivity and convenience as strengths and suggested improvements to navigation and content scope.ConclusionAn asynchronous online concussion management course engaged clinicians and enhanced confidence in concussion assessment and management. Future research should evaluate its long-term impact on clinical practice.

INTRODUCTION

Concussion is an acute neurophysiological event resulting from mechanical energy applied to the head, neck or body.1 Although it can cause neuropathological changes, it more often presents as a functional injury, similar to musculoskeletal injury.1 Concussions can arise in diverse contexts, including sports and motor vehicle accidents.1 Sport-related concussions are among the most complex sport injuries to diagnose, assess, and manage, as clinical signs and symptoms can fluctuate rapidly, differ between individuals, and be influenced by psychosocial factors.1–4 Contact sports such as hockey, football, and rugby carry the highest incidence, though concussions occur across all sports and competitive levels, from amateur to professional.1

Managing concussions requires clinicians to educate patients about signs and symptoms, recovery expectations, and return-to-school/work/activity or sport.1,5 Furthermore, clinicians must select appropriate interventions based on the individual’s presenting symptoms. In adults, symptoms typically resolve within 2 weeks, whereas recovery often takes longer in children and adolescents.3,6–9 Persistent postconcussive symptoms may be nonspecific and include emotional (eg, depression), physical (eg, headache), or cognitive (eg, fatigue) difficulties.3 Preinjury factors such as mental health conditions or migraine, and postinjury factors such as symptom severity, can predict poorer recovery.3 Clinicians must recognize these risk factors and either manage them or co-manage/refer patients as needed.

Although several high-quality resources exist, such as clinical guidelines, consensus statements, and systematic reviews, few present this information in a user-friendly format that applies pedagogical principles to support learning. Accessible, well-designed resources can strengthen continuing education for chiropractors and other first-contact health care providers, ultimately promoting safe and effective care. Prior work emphasizes that a framework-based knowledge translation approach is critical when developing concussion resources to ensure relevance and uptake of evidence-based recommendations.10

This project aimed to develop and disseminate a freely accessible, asynchronous, evidence-based online learning course on concussion assessment, diagnosis, and management in adults and adolescents, targeting first-contact health care providers with a particular focus on rehabilitation professionals.

METHODS

Course Design and Development

We developed the course in 2 phases. First, content was created based on current clinical practice guidelines and peer-reviewed evidence on concussion management across pediatric and adult populations. A multidisciplinary expert panel reviewed the draft content and provided feedback. Second, chiropractors, physiotherapists, and occupational therapists, completed the course to assess usability and ensure clinical applicability. We revised the course according to their feedback.

Course Content

Content was drawn from leading evidence-based guidelines, including the Ontario Ministry of Health living concussion guidelines in adults1 and children,11 the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention guideline on pediatric mTBI,5 and the 6th International Consensus Conference on Concussion in Sport.12

We organized the course into the following sections: (1) Concussion definition, pathophysiology, and biopsychosocial symptomatology, including prognosis and long-term effects; (2) Initial assessment, including pertinent health history questions, recognition of red/yellow/orange flags, and imaging criteria for suspected moderate to severe traumatic brain injury; (3) Physical examination emphasizing the SCOAT-6; (4) Initial management including expected recovery trajectory, activity modifications, and dispelling common myths; (5) Sport-related concussion, including preseason education and on-field assessment; (6) Return to activity, work, or school, and strategies to facilitate these transitions; and (7) Assessment and management of persistent symptoms, covering posttraumatic headaches, fatigue, sleep-wake disturbances, mental health disorders, cognitive difficulties, and vestibular and vision dysfunction.

Platform and User Interface

We built the course in Articulate Rise 360, selected for its cross-device compatibility, interactive capabilities, and user-friendly interface. The design emphasized clear navigation, consistent layout, and easy access to resources to accommodate varying levels of technological experience.

Pedagogical Framework

We integrated the following evidence-informed instructional strategies to enhance engagement, comprehension, and retention: (1) Chunking: Divided complex material into smaller, digestible segments to facilitate learning and review; (2) Gaming elements: Embedded interactive scenarios to promote active problem-solving in realistic clinical contexts; (3) Frequent quizzes: Used short formative assessments throughout to provide immediate feedback and reinforce learning; (4) Video demonstrations: Presented clinical techniques and patient interactions through high-quality video; (5) Clinical vignettes: Incorporated realistic patient cases to integrate knowledge and skills for effective decision-making.

Expert Panel

Through our professional networks, we recruited a 6-member panel from various relevant disciplines (sports chiropractic, sports medicine, physiatry, neuropsychology, pediatric neurology, occupational therapy). We selected members based on professional background, experience in concussion management, and familiarity with concussion literature and digital resources. They reviewed content for accuracy, completeness, structure, and usability.

Usability Testing

Participants

Using our professional networks, we recruited a convenience sample of 8 clinicians (3 chiropractors, 3 physiotherapists, 2 occupational therapists) from Western, Central, and Eastern Canada to facilitate geographic and experiential diversity. Eligibility criteria included English proficiency, basic technological literacy, and willingness to provide detailed feedback. Ontario Tech University’s Research Ethics Board approved the study (REB# 17885). All participants provided written informed consent before participation.

Procedure

Participants accessed the course through a secure link and completed it over approximately 3 hours. We anonymized responses using unique participant IDs. After completion, participants filled out an online survey assessing the course. First, usability was evaluated using an adapted System Usability Scale (SUS) was used to assess the usability of the online course.13 The SUS is a 10-item questionnaire with a mix of positive and negative questions scored on a 5-point Likert scale anchored by strongly disagree to strongly agree.14 The SUS has shown to have excellent reliability and concurrent validity.14 We adapted the questions to reflect “module” instead of “system” and included items relevant to our objective. Second, self-rated knowledge of pediatric and adult concussion assessment and management was evaluated postcourse using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from strongly disagree to strongly agree.15 Third, overall feedback was collected through open-ended questions addressing perceived strengths, weaknesses, and suggestions for improvement. We refined the course based on feedback from this usability testing.

Ratings of Usability Participants on Questions Related to the Module Relevance and Impact

Regarding persistent concussion symptoms, 7 participants (7/8; 87.5%) agreed or strongly agreed that they felt confident assessing these symptoms after completing the course. However, only 3 (3/8; 37.5%) agreed or strongly agreed that they felt confident managing persistent symptoms following course completion, while 5 (5/8; 62.5%) rated their confidence as neutral.

Overall, all participants (8/8; 100%) agreed or strongly agreed that they felt confident in the assessment and management of concussions in adults following course completion, and 7 (7/8; 87.5%) agreed or strongly agreed that they felt confident in assessing and managing concussions in adolescents. All participants (8/8; 100%) agreed or strongly agreed that the course met their expectations and that they would recommend it to others.

Qualitative Feedback

Participants identified several strengths of the course, including its interactivity, multimedia variety, and flexibility. They also provided feedback on how the course could be improved to enhance interdisciplinary content, streamline course length and worksheet flow, and expand coverage of cognitive and emotional issues. All suggested refinements were incorporated into the final version prior to dissemination.

Strengths

Participants reported that interactivity, including gaming elements and clinical vignettes, maintained their engagement and encouraged critical thinking.“It was really easy to use. I liked that it varied methods of text, videos, clickable cards—it kept me engaged instead of just a wall of text you glaze over.” (Participant 3)

Participants appreciated the inclusion of diverse media formats, which reinforced key concepts and maintained attention.“Generally easy to use, lots of great resources, engaging with the opportunity to interact.” (Participant 4)

Participants valued the flexibility to complete the course at their own pace and revisit sections as needed.“I liked that it was available online and I could easily self-pace. Given my clinic and family schedule it made it very easy to pick up and navigate the material when time permitted.” (Participant 7)

Areas for Improvement

Participants recommended including additional content on interdisciplinary management and referral processes. In response, further information was added, particularly in the section on persistent concussion symptoms.“We don't want to overstep or be redundant in assessment, or take on assessment/treatment outside our lane so putting some specific ideas around how to approach assessments in the various settings would be wonderful.” (Participant 2)

“We don't want to overstep or be redundant in assessment, or take on assessment/treatment outside our lane so putting some specific ideas around how to approach assessments in the various settings would be wonderful.” (Participant 2)

Some participants felt content in some sections was too long. In response, we condensed content, replaced dense text with bullet points where appropriate, and streamlined explanations.“If one was to dive into all the additional resources and material this would equate for 3–5 hours, especially in individuals who do not have an extensive background with concussion or a history of working on the living concussion guidelines.” (Participant 7)

“If one was to dive into all the additional resources and material this would equate for 3–5 hours, especially in individuals who do not have an extensive background with concussion or a history of working on the living concussion guidelines.” (Participant 7)

Participants suggested better aligning the learner worksheet with the course sequence to enhance the flow. In response, the worksheet was restructured to reflect this feedback.“I would ensure that the worksheet flow is matched in order with all the presentation material. There were a few times where I had to skip back/forth.” (Participant 7)

“I would ensure that the worksheet flow is matched in order with all the presentation material. There were a few times where I had to skip back/forth.” (Participant 7)

One participant recommended expanding content on cognitive and emotional challenges. In response, we added relevant material and referral guidance, balancing this with the need to maintain concise course length.“Depends on target market—lots of good information here regarding physical aspects, but less so on cognitive and emotional issues. The cognitive and emotional issues would be very relevant to OTs whom you have identified as a target for this course.” (Participant 4)

DISCUSSION

We developed an evidence-based online course on concussion assessment and management for first-contact health care providers, with a focus on rehabilitation professionals. To our knowledge, this is the first published description of an asynchronous concussion course tailored to this audience. Usability testing confirmed its clinical relevance and identified specific areas for refinement, which were addressed prior to dissemination.

Our findings demonstrate that an asynchronous online format can effectively deliver in-depth concussion management training. This aligns with previous research showing that flexible, learner-centered continuing education improves learners’ knowledge, skills, and confidence.16–19 Participants rated the course highly for usability, relevance, and clinical impact, and most reported increased confidence in assessing and managing concussions in both adults and adolescents. However, confidence remained lower for managing persistent concussion symptoms, suggesting an opportunity to develop a future course dedicated to this topic.

Comparisons with existing educational tools, such as the Concussion Awareness Training Tool (CATT), indicate that our course provides a comparable user experience while addressing a different audience.19 The CATT is a 40-minute e-learning program primarily designed for emergency and family physicians.19 Similar to our course, it covers concussion definition, assessment, and management of both acute and persistent symptoms, and health care professionals who have completed the CATT have reported improved evidence-based knowledge and management skills.19 When exploring implementation factors, users emphasized the tool’s accessibility, clarity, and practical resources, feedback consistent with our participants’ experiences.20 Our course builds on this evidence by incorporating content specifically tailored to rehabilitation professionals, aiming to enhance their knowledge and attitudes towards the assessment and management of both acute and persistent concussion symptoms.

Future Studies

Future studies will build upon these findings by evaluating learner uptake and perceived usefulness of the module. In addition, future research will examine both the short- and long-term impact on clinical practice, including changes in clinician knowledge, confidence, interprofessional collaboration and application of skills in the assessment and management of patients with acute and persistent concussion.

Strengths

Strengths of this study include a multi-phase development process grounded in current clinical guidelines, expert panel review, and iterative usability testing, which ensured both content accuracy and positive learner outcomes. We employed a pedagogical framework designed to enhance engagement, comprehension, and retention by integrating interactive and multimedia elements, an approach that accommodates diverse learning preferences among users.

Limitations

There are limitations of our study. The usability testing sample was small and consisted primarily of clinicians with prior experience in concussion management, which may limit generalizability to the broader target audience. In addition, we did not assess long-term knowledge retention or changes in clinical practice behavior, which represent important directions for future research.

CONCLUSION

We developed an asynchronous, interactive online course to deliver concussion management education to first-contact clinicians. Usability testing demonstrated that the course is accessible, engaging, and clinically relevant, while also identifying areas for refinement. By improving clinician confidence in concussion assessment and management, the course has the potential to enhance patient care. Future work will evaluate learner uptake and inform subsequent studies examining the short- and long-term impact on clinical practice.
