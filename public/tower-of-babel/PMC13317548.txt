Noninvasive Prediction of Significant Hepatic Injury in Treatment-Naïve Children with Chronic HBV Infection
Authors: Jiaying Wu, Xiaorong Peng, Yunan Chang, Hongmei Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSignificant hepatic injury (SHI) in children with chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection requires timely identification, yet liver biopsy is invasive and not routinely feasible. This study aimed to develop a non-invasive predictive model for SHI.MethodsThis retrospective analysis included treatment-naïve children with chronic HBV infection undergoing liver biopsy. Participants were randomly split into 70% training and 30% independent testing cohorts prior to preprocessing. Missing data imputation and least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO)-based feature selection were performed, and nine machine learning (ML) models were developed and optimized using nested cross-validation (CV). The optimal logistic regression (LR) model was used to construct and validate a predictive nomogram.ResultsA total of 246 eligible children were included, of whom 147 (59.8%) had SHI. LASSO regression identified five predictors for model development. Among the evaluated ML models, LR showed stable performance, achieving an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.799 (95% CI: 0.740–0.858) in the training folds, 0.781 (95% CI: 0.585–0.972) in the validation folds, and 0.707 (95% CI: 0.562–0.851) in the independent testing cohort. The nomogram showed moderate calibration and better clinical utility compared with alanine aminotransferase (ALT) alone across a broad range of threshold probabilities.ConclusionWe developed a non-invasive predictive model based on routine clinical data to detect SHI in treatment-naïve children with chronic HBV infection, which exhibited moderate predictive power and marginal improvement over ALT alone.

Background

Chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection remains a major global public health concern.1 Children are particularly susceptible to chronic infection due to their immature immune systems.2,3 Early identification of significant hepatic injury (SHI) is essential for timely initiation of antiviral therapy and prevention of disease progression.4,5 Liver biopsy is currently the gold standard for assessing liver histology and guiding treatment decisions. However, due to its invasive nature and potential complications, liver biopsy is limited in its routine clinical use in children. Therefore, accurate non-invasive methods are urgently needed to assess SHI in children with chronic HBV infection.

Conventional non-invasive approaches, such as serum biomarkers and clinical scoring systems, have been developed for this purpose.6,7 However, these approaches are typically based on predefined rules or a limited number of variables, which may not fully capture the complex relationships among clinical factors. As a result, their diagnostic performance remains suboptimal, particularly in children with heterogeneous disease characteristics.8

Machine learning (ML), a crucial branch of artificial intelligence (AI), offers a data-driven approach for constructing predictive models by automatically identifying patterns within complex datasets.9 Unlike traditional statistical methods, which often rely on predefined assumptions and linear relationships, ML algorithms can capture nonlinear interactions and handle high-dimensional clinical data. This makes ML particularly suitable for integrating multiple demographic and laboratory variables to improve diagnostic accuracy. Recent studies have demonstrated the value of ML in disease prediction, risk stratification, and clinical decision support,10–12 suggesting its potential utility in assessing liver injury in chronic HBV infection.

Therefore, we aimed to develop a non-invasive model by utilizing demographic characteristics and laboratory data to identify SHI in treatment-naïve children with chronic HBV infection, providing valuable guidance for clinical diagnosis and treatment.

Materials and Methods

Participants

This retrospective study included children with chronic HBV infection who underwent ultrasound-guided percutaneous liver biopsy at Children’s Hospital of Chongqing Medical University between January 2017 and October 2024. Eligible participants were younger than 18 years, had been positive for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) for at least 6 months, and had not received prior antiviral therapy. Patients were excluded if they had coinfection with other hepatitis viruses or human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), other chronic liver diseases, cirrhosis or liver failure, severe systemic diseases, or incomplete clinical data with a missing rate exceeding 20%.

The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of our hospital (Approval No. 20241030420), and written informed consent was obtained from the guardians of all participants. Data Collection and Definitions

Clinical and laboratory data were extracted from the electronic medical record system within one week before liver biopsy to ensure that all data reflected the baseline status of the participants. The extracted variables included demographic characteristics, routine blood parameters, liver function tests, coagulation indices, and HBV virological markers.

Ultrasound-guided percutaneous liver biopsy was performed using an 18G biopsy needle. Adequate biopsy specimens were defined as samples with a length of ≥1.5 cm and containing at least six portal tracts.13 Histological inflammation and fibrosis were assessed according to the Scheuer scoring system.14 SHI was defined as inflammation grade ≥G2 and/or fibrosis stage ≥S2.15 All biopsy specimens were independently evaluated by two experienced liver pathologists who were blinded to clinical information. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus, and interobserver agreement was assessed using Cohen’s kappa coefficient.16

Data Preprocessing and Feature Selection

To minimize the risk of information leakage, the complete dataset was randomly divided into a training cohort (70%) and an independent testing cohort (30%) before any data preprocessing procedures.17 The testing cohort remained completely isolated throughout model development and was reserved for independent validation.

Missing values in the training cohort were addressed using multiple imputation (MI). Missing values in the testing cohort were subsequently imputed using parameters derived exclusively from the training cohort. Following imputation, feature selection was performed in the training cohort using least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression with 10-fold cross-validation (CV). Variables with non-zero coefficients were retained for subsequent model development.

Four non-invasive fibrosis indexes were calculated: aspartate aminotransferase to platelet ratio index (APRI), fibrosis-4 index (FIB-4), γ-glutamyl transpeptidase to platelet ratio (GPR), and prothrombin activity (PTA) using previously published formulas.18–21

Model Development and Selection

Based on the selected predictors, nine ML models were developed, including logistic regression (LR), extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), light gradient boosting machine (LightGBM), random forest (RF), adaptive boosting (AdaBoost), decision tree (DT), gradient boosting decision tree (GBDT), k-nearest neighbors (KNN), and support vector machine (SVM).

Model development and hyperparameter optimization were performed using a nested CV framework within the training cohort. In the outer loop, a 10-fold CV was applied, partitioning the full training cohort into ten non-overlapping subsets. For each outer iteration, nine subsets were used as training folds for inner tuning, while the remaining subset served as the validation fold. Predictive performance was assessed on the validation folds in the outer loop. Meanwhile, within each training fold, an inner 5-fold CV combined with a grid search was nested to optimize the hyperparameters. After the nested CV procedure was completed, all models were refitted on the entire training cohort using the finalized optimal hyperparameters.22

Model performance was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), F1 score, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and Cohen’s kappa coefficient. The optimal model was identified based on its overall discriminative ability and classification performance across these metrics.

The performance of all nine developed ML models was further evaluated in the independent testing cohort to assess their predictive performance and generalizability. The testing results were compared with the CV results obtained in the training cohort to verify the stability and reliability of the optimal model identified during model development.

Nomogram Construction and Evaluation

Based on the selected LR model, a nomogram was constructed to facilitate individualized risk assessment. Bootstrap resampling (1,000 iterations) was performed to estimate the optimism-corrected AUC, calibration slope, calibration intercept, and maximum calibration error (Emax), thereby assessing model discrimination, stability, and calibration. Model calibration was further evaluated using calibration curves. Net reclassification improvement (NRI), integrated discrimination improvement (IDI), and decision curve analysis (DCA) were calculated to quantify the added predictive value beyond ALT alone.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were compared using Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U-test; categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test. All analyses were performed using R (version 3.6.3) and Python (version 3.7), with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Several ensemble-learning models, particularly RF and GBDT, demonstrated excellent discriminative ability in the training folds, achieving AUCs of 0.988 (95% CI: 0.973–1.000) and 0.989 (95% CI: 0.975–1.000), respectively. However, their performance declined markedly in the validation folds, with corresponding AUCs of 0.736 (95% CI: 0.520–0.951) and 0.681 (95% CI: 0.457–0.903), suggesting potential overfitting and limited model robustness (Tables 3–4 and Figure 3A and B).

In contrast, LR exhibited more stable performance across the training and validation folds, yielding AUCs of 0.799 (95% CI: 0.740–0.858) and 0.781 (95% CI: 0.585–0.972), respectively. Consistent findings were observed for other evaluation metrics, including the F1 score, sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, and Cohen’s kappa coefficient (Tables 3–4 and Figure 3A and B). Owing to its balanced discriminative ability and stable CV performance, LR was selected as the optimal model.

To further assess model generalizability and confirm the reliability of the selected model, all nine ML models were subsequently evaluated in the independent testing cohort. Although most models exhibited varying degrees of performance deterioration compared with their CV results, LR maintained moderate discrimination, achieving an AUC of 0.707 (95% CI: 0.562–0.851) (Table 5, Figure 3C and D). Notably, the modest reduction in AUC from the validation folds to the independent testing cohort suggested relatively stable performance. Taken together, these findings supported the selection of LR as the final predictive model.

The multivariable model demonstrated significantly improved risk classification compared with ALT alone. The NRI was 0.433 (95% CI: 0.078–0.751), and the IDI was 0.166 (95% CI: 0.083–0.239). DCA further confirmed that the nomogram provided greater net clinical benefit across a broad range of threshold probabilities (Figure 5B). Discussion

Accurate identification of SHI is essential for optimizing the management of children with chronic HBV infection. In our cohort, although the prevalence of SHI increased progressively with rising ALT levels, a considerable proportion of children with normal or only mildly elevated ALT nevertheless exhibited SHI, consistent with previous studies.23–26 This observation suggests that ALT-based assessment may underestimate SHI in a subset of pediatric patients and highlights the need for more comprehensive risk stratification strategies.

In this study, we developed a non-invasive model for individualized prediction of SHI in treatment-naïve children with chronic HBV infection using routine clinical data. LASSO regression identified five predictors (GLB, ALT, HBeAb, TT, and PTA) from 33 candidate variables. Nine ML models were evaluated using nested CV and an independent testing cohort. Several ensemble-learning models demonstrated higher apparent performance but poorer stability, whereas LR showed more consistent performance across datasets. The nomogram demonstrated acceptable discrimination, moderate calibration, and greater clinical utility than ALT alone, supporting its potential for individualized SHI risk prediction.

The predictors retained in the final model may collectively reflect multiple pathophysiological processes underlying SHI in chronic HBV infection. ALT is a well-established marker of hepatocellular injury, whereas GLB may be associated with chronic immune activation and inflammatory responses. PTA and TT provide complementary information on hepatic synthetic function and coagulation status, both of which may be altered in the setting of ongoing liver injury.27 Notably, HBeAb emerged as an important contributor to model performance. Although HBeAb generally reflects ongoing or previous HBeAg seroconversion, HBeAg loss does not necessarily indicate disease remission, as a subset of patients subsequently develop chronic HBeAg-negative hepatitis characterized by persistent viral activity and ongoing liver injury.28–32 Further studies are needed to clarify the biological mechanisms underlying this relationship.29,33,34

Relatively few studies have explored non-invasive models for predicting SHI in children with chronic HBV infection. Although the multivariable model achieved a modest improvement in discrimination compared with ALT alone, its overall predictive performance remained moderate. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as preliminary evidence that routinely available clinical variables may provide complementary information for the non-invasive prediction of SHI rather than serve as a clinically definitive diagnostic tool.

Admittedly, this study has several limitations. First, this was a retrospective single-center study and may therefore be subject to selection bias. Second, although internal validation was performed, the overall sample size was modest, and the independent testing cohort was relatively small for robust ML evaluation. Third, the study population consisted predominantly of Asian HBeAg-positive children infected with HBV genotype B or C, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other populations. Fourth, external validation was not performed, so the generalizability and transportability of the model to independent populations remain uncertain. Finally, although missing data were handled using multiple imputation, sensitivity analyses employing alternative missing-data approaches were not conducted. Future multicenter studies with larger sample sizes and more diverse patient populations are warranted to externally validate the model and confirm its clinical utility.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we developed a non-invasive prediction model for SHI in children with chronic HBV infection. The model showed moderate discriminative performance and good stability after internal validation. These findings are preliminary and exploratory. Further multicenter external validation is required before any potential clinical application.
