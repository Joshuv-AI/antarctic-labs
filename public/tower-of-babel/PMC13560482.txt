State Choices, Unequal Access: Policies Shaping Reproductive Health Care Across the United States
Authors: ALINA SALGANICOFF, IVETTE GOMEZ, USHA RANJI
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract Policy PointsState policies and programs play an outsized role in shaping availability and access to sexual and reproductive health services across the nation. Reproductive health care access in the united states is defined by state borders. The person living in rural Alabama has considerably different options for their reproductive care than a person who lives in Arizona or Washington State. Ultimately, whether a resident's access is expanded or constrained depends not only on federal law but also on the specific political choices made within their state capital. This is because state policies and programs play an outsized role in shaping availability and access to sexual and reproductive health services across the nation. These differences reflect the politics and disparate approaches that policymakers, both elected officials and executive agencies, take in operating state and federal programs, like Medicaid and other grant programs, and the policies that they enact. For example, by controlling Medicaid eligibility levels, provider reimbursement rates, and federal grant implementation, state officials act as the primary gatekeepers to reproductive health care services. In addition, state decisions about health care credentialing, reproductive rights, and regulatory policy can affect whether access is broadened or constrained. State policies and programs that define abortion access for all people, establish coverage rules for state‐regulated insurance programs, and determine the role of pharmacists in providing contraceptive care and services outside of traditional settings all affect the availability of care.

This commentary focuses on three interrelated reproductive health areas: contraception, abortion, and maternity care. It examines how state choices can affect the extent to which developed and implemented policies can support or limit access to care. Although these policies shape access for all who seek reproductive health care, people with low incomes and communities marginalized by racism and systemic barriers are disproportionately affected by state‐level policies and programs. In addition, the social determinants of health, the conditions in which people are born, grow, live, work, and age, shape a person's access to care and reproductive health outcomes. 1 State‐level policies that affect education, climate, housing, employment, health coverage, and personal safety can also impact access to and availability of reproductive health care services. This paper does not specifically address these broader societal influences, but they have both direct and indirect effects on care and outcomes.

Contraception

At some point in their lives, preventing undesired pregnancy is a priority for most reproductive‐age women. Eight in ten (82%) women of reproductive age say they used some form of contraception in the past 12 months, and seven in ten (69%) women in the same age group say it is very important for them to avoid becoming pregnant in the next month. 2 Despite its use among nearly all women, access to the full range of contraceptive care and methods is not always a reality for many. Affordability, availability, and coverage are still barriers in many parts of the nation, particularly, but not exclusively, among women who lack insurance coverage and those who seek more effective, costlier methods, including intrauterine devices and contraceptive implants. Over the past decade, many states have prioritized access to contraceptive care for their residents. Examples of recent state actions to promote access to contraception range from sweeping policies such as those that guarantee and enshrine the right to contraceptive access, to those that strengthen contraceptive coverage through commercial insurance plans and Medicaid, as well as those that have broadened the availability of hormonal methods through pharmacist access.

The Right to Contraception

Despite the broad use of contraception, there is concern in some sectors that women's right to contraception affirmed by the Griswold v Connecticut case in 1973 is legally vulnerable. 3 Fourteen states and District of Columbia, currently have constitutional amendments that recognize the right to contraception for state residents (Figure 1). 4 Since the 2022 Dobbs ruling, six states and District of Columbia have enacted protections for contraceptive access. These new protections specifically emerged as a legislative and citizen response to the fall of Roe v Wade. 5 They are meant to protect people's ability to obtain contraceptive services in the face of a shifting federal landscape, a conservative Supreme Court bench, and Justice Thomas's concurring opinion in Dobbs that explicitly calls for a reconsideration of the Griswold decision. 6

Contraceptive Coverage

Assuring the affordability of contraceptive care has long been viewed as foundational to assuring access. In 1998, Maryland became the first state to enact a law requiring insurance plans regulated by the state to cover contraceptives. While coverage for prescription contraceptives was generally widespread in the private and public sectors before the passage of the Affordable Care Act (ACA), state laws did not require plans to cover all methods, and services were typically subject to cost sharing. The ACA changed that by requiring most private plans to cover the full cost of all contraceptive services and supplies approved, granted, and cleared by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA), which exceeds the scope and reach of most state mandates. 8 Some states have enacted laws post‐ACA that mandate coverage of over‐the‐counter methods or vasectomies for men, which is broader than the ACA's preventive services requirement. Despite state efforts to fill gaps, state laws are limited in their reach as they only apply to state‐regulated plans, not self‐funded plans under which 67% of covered workers are insured. 9 If the ACA preventive services requirement were to be restricted, invalidated, or repealed in the future, only individuals enrolled in state‐regulated plans living in states with contraceptive coverage mandates would be assured some sort of coverage. 10

Medicaid

In the United States, Medicaid is the primary coverage program for people with low incomes. While Medicaid covers one in five people living in the United States, it is a particularly significant source of coverage for certain populations. Nearly half of the program's enrollees are children under age 19 years, and the largest group of adults on the program are reproductive‐age women. 11 , 12 Since 1972, coverage of family planning has been a federally mandatory benefit under Medicaid. Enrollees are protected against cost sharing for a broad range of contraceptive methods, states enjoy a federal matching rate of 90% for the provision of family planning services to enrollees, and until recently, enrollees have been allowed to see any willing participating Medicaid provider for family planning care. In addition to providing coverage for full‐scope Medicaid enrollees, many states have opted to extend family planning services through State Plan Amendments or 1115 waivers for people who do not qualify for full‐scope Medicaid coverage, usually because their incomes exceed the state income eligibility thresholds. Thirty states provided family planning coverage to individuals who did not qualify for full Medicaid benefits in 2025. 13 In most states, eligibility for the program is based solely on meeting income requirements and is available to men as well as women, but coverage of teenagers is generally more limited. In recent years, enrollment in these programs has dropped in states that have adopted the ACA Medicaid expansion that extends full Medicaid to eligible individuals.

However, these programs may again garner the attention of state officials seeking to provide a family planning safety net for people who lose their full Medicaid coverage as a result of the implementation of the Medicaid work requirements that are part of the 2025 One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA). 14 , 16 In the 40 states and District of Columbia that have expanded Medicaid eligibility under the ACA, OBBBA will require many Medicaid enrollees who qualify through the ACA expansion to work or look for work starting in 2027. KFF research shows that most adult women covered by Medicaid meet work requirements or would qualify for an exemption (parents with children under age 14 are exempt), but many would be at risk of losing coverage because of the administrative burdens related to reporting requirements. 16 , 17 , 15 Additionally, all states are predicted to experience reductions in Medicaid enrollment due to additional administrative hurdles related to eligibility determination, verification, and the frequency of renewals required by the new law.

This erosion in coverage will be compounded by the expiration of the enhanced premium tax credits for ACA Marketplace enrollees at the end of 2025. On average, enrollees who previously received a tax credit face a doubling of their premium payments, which is unaffordable for some individuals, who have no choice other than to go without coverage or choose a high‐deductible bronze plan. 18 Additionally, the Congressional Budget Office estimates that the law's restrictions on eligibility for federally funded health coverage for lawfully present immigrants will result in about 1.4 million lawfully present immigrants becoming uninsured. 19

The 2025 OBBBA specifically targets Medicaid family planning access in Section 71113 by imposing a one‐year ban on Medicaid payments to certain reproductive health care entities that provide both contraception and abortion care. 20 While not specifically called out in the law, this provision was developed by abortion opponents to restrict federal funds to Planned Parenthood, a major provider of family planning services to Medicaid enrollees, but also affects two other family planning provider organizations in Maine and Massachusetts. 21 The law bars Medicaid payments for any service, including contraceptives and preventive care, not only abortion. Federal dollars are already barred from paying for abortion care because of the decades‐old Hyde Amendment, which restricts federal funding for abortions to those pregnancies that threaten the pregnant person's life or those that are resulting from rape or incest. Some states have come through with additional funds for the Planned Parenthood clinics and other providers impacted by the law to help offset Medicaid cuts. 22 Even if this ban is not renewed by a future Congress, the 2025 Supreme Court ruling in the case Medina v Planned Parenthood South Atlantic has given states the ability to disqualify Planned Parenthood from their Medicaid program, which at least a dozen states have attempted to do. 23

Access to Hormonal Methods and Over‐the‐Counter Products

Over the past 15 years, there have been efforts to broaden contraceptive availability outside of traditional clinical settings. Given the nationwide shortage of primary care providers, many have looked to pharmacists to help fill the gap in providing access to primary and preventive services, including hormonal contraceptives. As of February 2026, 36 states and District of Columbia have passed laws enabling pharmacists to prescribe self‐administered hormonal contraception, such as oral contraceptive pills, the depot medroxyprogesterone acetate injection, the patch, and the ring. 24 All these states allow pharmacists to prescribe oral contraceptives, at a minimum, but vary in other details, such as how the prescriptive authority is structured, minimum age requirements for the patient, allowed methods supply duration, and the need for a prior prescription from a physician. Policy pathways states use to expand pharmacists’ scope of practice adopt varying degrees of authority, ranging from collaborative practice agreements with physicians being the most restrictive to broad prescriptive authority that offers pharmacists the greatest autonomy.

However, the implementation of these policies has been challenging on various levels. Pharmacists have reported that while private insurance and Medicaid cover the contraceptives, they often do not pay for the time that pharmacists spend counseling patients because they do not have a pathway to billing for “medical services.” To address this gap, pharmacists typically charge patients consultation fees, which insurers are not required to cover and that can be as high as $50. Pharmacists have reported several additional challenges to the successful implementation of their provision of contraceptives, including the need for additional training, pharmacist capacity to provide services in the face of increased demands, patient awareness of these services, and better integration of contraceptive services in a pharmacy setting. 25

Abortion

State‐level abortion policies have long dictated the ease of access to abortion services within state boundaries, even prior to the 2022 Dobbs decision that overturned Roe in June 2022. Abortion is banned or restricted in large swaths of the United States. Despite the abortion bans, abortion volume in the United States has increased since the Dobbs decision, even in states where abortion is banned. 26 This paradoxical effect is likely due to a variety of factors, including growth in the use of medication abortion, the expansion of telehealth for abortion care, and the enactment of legal protections for reproductive health care providers in states that support abortion rights.

State Efforts to Protect/Restrict Abortion

In the aftermath of the Dobbs decision, states have taken steps to either restrict or protect abortion access. As of April 27, 2026, 13 states have effectively banned all abortions, and six states have imposed early gestational limits between 6 and 12 weeks (Figure 2). 27 While all states with bans and early gestational limits have exemptions in place to prevent the death of the pregnant person, there have been several documented cases of women dying in states where abortion is banned because they were denied abortion care during pregnancy‐related emergencies. 28 , 29 Fewer states with abortion bans have exemptions to protect the health of the pregnant person, cases of rape/incest, or lethal fetal anomaly. In practice, even in states that have so‐called health exceptions, they have often proven to be unworkable, except in the most extreme circumstances.

States continue to propose and enact new laws aimed at restricting abortion in their state. Notable examples include polices that allow private citizens to sue anyone who helps people get abortions, reclassifying medication abortion drugs as controlled substances, and criminalizing interstate travel as abortion trafficking.

Ballot initiatives have emerged as a pathway that some states have used to address abortion access. Since June 2022, voters in 16 states have voted on abortion‐related ballot measures, some aimed at protecting abortion access while others aimed to restrict abortion in the state. 4 In all but three states, efforts to protect abortion access prevailed with ten states enacting measures amending their state constitutions to protect the right to abortion. However, not all states allow citizen‐initiated ballot initiatives, so their reach is limited. Among abortion ban states that allow for citizen‐initiated ballot initiatives, only Arkansas and Oklahoma have yet to vote on an abortion‐related ballot measure.

Telehealth for Medication Abortion and Shield Laws

Over the past five years, there has been an increase in the use of telehealth to expand medication abortion access enabled by FDA changes in the dispensing requirements for mifepristone. Since mifepristone's FDA approval in 2000, the use of medication abortion has steadily increased and now accounts for 63% of all abortions in the United States. 30 In 2021, the FDA loosened the Risk Evaluation and Mitigation Strategy restrictions for dispensing mifepristone by eliminating the in‐person dispensing requirement for the medication, which enabled the use of telehealth for abortions. State laws that ban or restrict abortion apply to both medication and procedural abortions. Some states where abortion is not fully banned have adopted other policies to restrict the use of telehealth abortion such as explicitly prohibiting the use of telehealth, banning the mailing of medication abortion drugs. In addition, other state regulations, including ultrasound and counseling requirements, waiting periods, and specific in‐person dispensing mandates, also limit the feasibility of using telehealth for medication abortion in states without complete abortion bans.

The remaining 27 states and District of Columbia do not have barriers for the use of telehealth for medication abortion. Since the Dobbs decision, telehealth has been instrumental in expanding access to abortion services across the country, including a slight increase in the total number of abortions despite the state bans and early gestational limits. 31 Over one in four (28%) abortions in the United States are now provided via telehealth. 26

As a related state policy activity to uphold abortion access, some states have enacted “shield laws” that aim to protect physicians from prosecution for offering telehealth abortion care to patients in states where it is banned. As of December 2025, eight states have shield laws in place that explicitly protect providers regardless of where the patient lives. 32 By the end of 2024, over one in seven (15%) abortions in the country were medication abortions for which the pills were mailed by providers in states with shield laws to patients living in states where abortion is banned or heavily restricted. 33 There are cases that are specifically testing the shield laws that are anticipated to ultimately reach the Supreme Court as well as ongoing litigation seeking to reinstate the FDA's policy requiring in‐person dispensing for mifepristone, which effectively would result in a national ban on the use of telehealth for medication abortion if successful. 34

Abortion Coverage

Federal and state laws, as well as insurers’ coverage policies, shape the extent to which women can have coverage for abortion services under both publicly funded programs like Medicaid and Medicare as well as private health plans. Since 1977, the Hyde Amendment has banned the use of any federal funds for abortion for Medicaid enrollees, only allowing exceptions for pregnancies that endanger the life of the pregnant person or that result from rape or incest. 35 In 17 states and the District of Columbia where abortion is not banned, Medicaid programs do not pay for any abortions beyond the Hyde exceptions, and low‐income enrollees who seek abortion must pay for that care or rely on abortion funds to assist with the costs. 36 Twenty states, however, use state funds to pay for abortions for Medicaid enrollees who seek abortion outside of the limitations imposed by the Hyde Amendment.

However, even in states that cover abortions for their Medicaid enrollees, the state‐established reimbursement rates have historically been lower than those paid by Medicare and are even lower relative to private insurance rates. A 2020 analysis found that the Medicaid reimbursement rates for first‐ and second‐trimester abortions would only cover 37% and 41%, respectively, of what a self‐pay patient would be charged for the procedure. 37 Like other services, Medicaid reimbursement rates for abortion can be a deciding factor for abortion clinics on whether they accept Medicaid in their practice, and many providers do not due to these low payment rates. Notably, a court ruling requires Nevada to cover abortions deemed to be “medically necessary” for Medicaid enrollees, but no clinics in the state currently accept Medicaid because the reimbursement rate is so low relative to other payers. While the policy has changed, the low payment rates cancel out the impact of the new coverage requirement.

Maternal Health

The crisis of preventable maternal mortality and morbidity in the United States, characterized by profound racial and ethnic inequities, has reached an inflection point. As of 2024, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention estimates that over 80% of pregnancy‐related deaths are preventable, yet the national maternal mortality rate remains significantly higher than that of other high‐income nations. 38 , 39 This crisis has mobilized a broad coalition of stakeholders—from clinicians and public health practitioners to advocates and policymakers.

At the state level, policymakers have focused much of their efforts to improve maternal and birth outcomes on expanding and bolstering Medicaid coverage, a significant lever given that the program covers four in ten births nationally. 40 However, coverage represents only one element of a broader strategy needed to strengthen and improve maternity care and outcomes. While state efforts to address maternal health have largely focused on addressing health care delivery, there has been greater recognition that maternal deaths and poor outcomes are symptomatic of societal and structural conditions that systematically disadvantage many women of color and women with lower incomes. State‐level reforms that focus exclusively on health care financing and delivery system reforms, while greatly needed, will be limited in their impact without addressing the inequities that drive these disparate health outcomes.

Medicaid Coverage Expansions

Recognizing the value of prenatal care for both maternal and infant health outcomes, states have embraced the opportunity to cover pregnant people and get them into care as early as possible. The federal minimum Medicaid eligibility level for pregnant women is 138% of the federal poverty level (FPL), and many states go higher. Today, all but two states (Idaho, South Dakota) extend Medicaid eligibility to pregnant people beyond the federal floor. Forty states and District of Columbia have taken advantage of the ACA Medicaid expansion to extend Medicaid coverage to all qualifying individuals with incomes up to 138% FPL, which ensures access to coverage before and after pregnancy. Medicaid expansion is associated with a number of positive outcomes in sexual and reproductive health, including increased coverage rates before, during, and after pregnancy. 41 Medicaid expansion is also associated with increased access to and utilization of prenatal and postpartum care. Some studies have tied expansion to lower rates of maternal and infant mortality. 42 , 43

More recently, federal and state efforts aim to address and improve maternal health in the postpartum period, the first year after the end of pregnancy, to address ongoing health needs, including conditions that may have been identified during pregnancy but require ongoing care and treatment. Historically, Medicaid coverage for pregnant people ended after 60 days, but now all but one state (Arkansas) have taken actions to extend Medicaid postpartum coverage to 12 months via a state plan amendment available because of the COVID‐19 era American Rescue Plan Act. 44

After the postpartum year, however, some new parents no longer qualify for Medicaid. In states that have adopted the Medicaid expansion, new parents with income below 138% FPL remain eligible for the program, and those with higher incomes may qualify for subsidized coverage in the Marketplace. However, in the ten states that have not adopted Medicaid expansion, median eligibility for parents is 33% FPL or approximately $8,800 annually for a family of three.

Doulas and Medicaid

Doulas are individuals who support pregnant and postpartum people by providing a variety of services including visits during the prenatal period, labor coaching and support at the time of delivery, and postpartum care and assistance. Doula services are linked to more positive delivery outcomes, including reductions in C‐sections, premature deliveries, and length of labor. 45 Doulas can also help advocate for their pregnant patients, which is particularly important to Black women and others who experience discrimination and racism from the health system. Given that Medicaid pays for about two‐thirds of births to Black women, many states have looked to doulas as an approach to address the high rates of poor maternal and infant birth outcomes experienced by Black women. As of January 2026, more than half of states have included or are implementing doula benefits in their Medicaid programs. 46 While this growth represents a long‐sought advance in state efforts to improve outcomes, many states, such as Minnesota and Oregon, struggled to build a doula network because Medicaid payment rates have generally been extremely low. 47 California was set to begin doula coverage for Medicaid enrollees in 2022, but after hearing from doulas that the initial payment rate of $450 per pregnancy was too low and not enough to provide a living wage, the state delayed implementation of the benefit for a year and increased reimbursement to $1,154. 48

Workforce Supply and Availability of Care

The maternity care field struggles with many of the same provider supply pressures of the broader health care system. The March of Dimes characterizes more than a third (35%) of US counties, where 2.3 million reproductive‐age women live, as maternity care deserts without a single birthing facility or obstetric clinician. 49 This problem has been worsening, particularly in rural communities, and has been attributed to low Medicaid reimbursement rates, difficulty maintaining around‐the‐clock maternity ward staffing, and declining patient volume in some communities. 50 Nationally, Medicaid reimbursement for obstetric care is 88% of Medicare rates, ranging from a low of 40% in Rhode Island to a high of 138% in Arizona, and is considerably lower than employer‐sponsored insurance rates (Figure 3). 51 Some states, such as Texas, have increased payment for maternity units in an effort to improve quality and access. 52

While the vast majority of US births are in hospitals, some states have made efforts to expand access to midwifery and birth center care as they are associated with lower costs and high‐quality health outcomes for low‐risk pregnancies and can help ease some of the provider shortages. For example, California eased regulatory requirements that made it financially onerous to operate birth centers, such as those that required a location near a hospital or following building requirements designed for surgical centers that must offer care that is more complex. All states are required to reimburse certified nurse midwives, but some argue that support for a wider array of midwives, including those who do not have nursing degrees like Certified Professional Midwives, would further expand access.

Quality of Care

In the wake of many preventable pregnancy‐related deaths and near misses, the quality of maternity care has been in the spotlight over the past decade. Annually, approximately 700 to 800 people die within one year of pregnancy, with rates three to four times as high among Black and Native American women compared to White women. State maternal mortality review committees (MMRCs) have emerged as a research‐based avenue to help understand the causes of the untimely deaths of women, but they vary in scope, composition, and influence. For example, some states allocate funds for MMRCs to carry out their work and commit Medicaid agency staff to participate in MMRCs. California has gone beyond the MMRC by investing in quality improvement efforts on multiple fronts. In addition to leading the state's annual Pregnancy‐Associated Mortality Review, the California Maternal Quality Care Collaborative operates a Maternal Data Center that collects data from most of the state's hospital maternity wards and publishes toolkits for providers on how to better manage causes of mortality and morbidity, such as hypertension, hemorrhage, and preeclampsia. California has seen a decline in its mortality rate, although large racial and ethnic inequities persist. Some other states, such as Washington and Oregon, have adopted elements of California's efforts. 53 , 54 Most states are also addressing maternal patient safety by implementing safety bundles as part of the Alliance for Innovation on Maternal Health patient safety initiative. 55 For example, in Ohio, research found that hospitals in the initiative improved across a number of process measures, including documentation of blood loss during delivery and completion of risk assessment for hemorrhage prior to delivery. 56

Conversely, over the last several years, some states have enacted policies that have scaled back work on maternal health. For example, Texas will not publish pregnancy mortality data for 2022 and 2023, blocking the state's ability to assess the impact of the Dobbs ruling on maternal health for Texas residents. 57 The state of Georgia replaced all of their committee members in 2024 after news reporting highlighted the impact of the state's abortion ban on maternal deaths. 58

Final Considerations

As state policymakers develop and adopt approaches to address reproductive health services for residents, key policy efforts cut across all the services women need for sexual and reproductive health care. States have made numerous efforts to broaden access to health coverage for sexual and reproductive health care as well as expand the scope of coverage. While coverage does not guarantee access, women who are uninsured face greater obstacles to care and affordability challenges than those with insurance. Today, about one in ten reproductive‐age women are uninsured, and this number is likely to climb over the next decade with implementation of the 2025 OBBBA.

Medicaid is typically the second largest component of states’ budgets and the strongest policy lever they have to address access for people with low incomes. State decisions about eligibility requirements, scope of benefits, and reimbursement rates have all shaped the wide variation in health care services that Medicaid enrollees can obtain across the country, and reproductive health care is no exception. Some states are trying to be more expansive about workforce reforms, increasing support for pharmacists, doulas, as well as greater use of telehealth. 59 These strategies can help fill some gaps in reproductive health care access, but clinicians in women's health are subject to the same pressures and burnout facing the larger health workforce and seek to be compensated fairly to provide care, especially when it comes to serving those who face the greatest health challenges with the fewest resources.

Finally, the policy silos separating contraception, abortion, and maternity care ignore the reality of the reproductive health care continuum and women's lives. While financing and regulatory policies treat these issues as distinct domains, they are inextricably linked in practice. For example, the management of a nonviable or life‐threatening pregnancy requires access to abortion services, which are banned or severely restricted in large areas of the United States. Contraceptive access is fundamental to optimizing pregnancy intervals and maternal outcomes. Patients often engage with the same clinicians and health systems for their reproductive health care, but fragmented state policies and programs disrupt this continuity, and ultimately negatively impact their health outcomes and well‐being. States that take a coordinated, system‐level approach to health care—one that recognizes the full continuum of care across the reproductive years and reflects people's diverse circumstance and choices—can strengthen access, improve care delivery, and better meet patients’ needs.
