Social Risk Factors and Peritoneal Dialysis Use Among Maintenance Dialysis Patients: A National Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Seda Babroudi, Monica Shieu, Nien-Chen Li, Hiba Hamdan, Antonia Harford, Karen Majchrzak, Doug Johnson, Eduardo K. Lacson, Daniel E. Weiner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Rationale & ObjectivePeritoneal dialysis (PD) utilization remains lower in the United States compared with other countries. Data regarding the presence and association of patient-level social risk factors (SRFs) with PD use are lacking.Study DesignMulticenter cross-sectional study.Setting & PopulationMaintenance dialysis patients receiving in-center hemodialysis or peritoneal dialysis with dialysis vintage <2 years who dialyzed between 2023 and 2025 at a medium-size, national dialysis provider.ExposuresPatient-level SRFs (housing, food, transportation, utility insecurity, and interpersonal safety) ascertained using the Accountable Health Communities Health-Related Social Needs Screening Tool.OutcomeNonreceipt of PD at the time of social risk screening.Analytical ApproachTotal SRF burden was tallied and categorized (0 (referent), 1, or >1). Adjusted logistic regression models accounted for demographics, comorbid conditions, dialysis vintage, and facility-level clustering. Sensitivity analysis were employed to explore the effect of an alternate definition of SRF burden.ResultsAmong 7,237 patients treated at 244 dialysis facilities, mean age was 64 ± 15 years, 43% were female, 26% were non-Hispanic Black, and 1,305 (18%) individuals received PD. One or more than 1 patient-level SRF was present in 9% and 3% of patients, respectively. Food insecurity (7%) and housing instability (4%) were most common. After adjustment, presence of 1 and more than 1 SRF was associated with higher odds of nonreceipt of PD as compared with no SRFs [adjusted odds ratio = 1.46 (95% confidence interval 1.10-1.92) and 2.27 (1.35-3.82), respectively]. These findings should be explored in longitudinal analyses to identify potential patient- and policy-level interventions to improve PD utilization.

Use of home dialysis modalities is increasingly a health policy priority in the United States.1 Specific to peritoneal dialysis (PD), temporal trends suggest that utilization has increased over the last decade across all demographic and socioeconomic subgroups.2 Despite this trend, less than 15% of incident or prevalent dialysis patients used PD in 2023.3 Patient- and environment-specific barriers to PD use have previously been well-characterized and include high comorbid disease burden, poor functional status, lack of predialysis nephrology care, lack of caregiver support, and lack of physical space to store supplies.4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 In prior studies, data associating social risk factors (SRFs) with PD nonutilization, however, are generally limited to race, insurance status, employment status, educational status, and area-level indices of socioeconomic deprivation. In this context, previous studies have shown that Black and Hispanic individuals are less likely to initiate PD than their White counterparts.9,10 Similarly, being uninsured or insured with Medicaid, being unemployed, having lower levels of educational attainment, and residing in a neighborhood with greater social deprivation have all negatively associated with PD use.2,6,9,11 Notably, in a Canadian study, when the association of socioeconomic status, defined by income level and educational attainment, with PD eligibility and, separately, PD modality choice, was evaluated among a cohort of incident dialysis patients who all underwent modality education, socioeconomic factors were not associated with differential PD eligibility or modality choice.12 These discrepant findings, paired with the fact that much of the previous work has focused on community- and/or neighborhood-level measures of social risk, warrant further investigation to better characterize the association of individual-level social risk factors with nonreceipt of PD, which may then inform future social interventions and health policy initiatives to promote PD use.

Accordingly, this study evaluated the association of patient-level SRFs, including housing instability, transportation insecurity, food insecurity, utility insecurity, and interpersonal safety concerns, with nonreceipt of PD in a national cohort of dialysis patients treated at a medium-sized national dialysis provider. We hypothesized that the presence of one or more SRFs would be associated with a higher likelihood of nonreceipt of PD as compared with the absence of any reported SRFs.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Data Sources

Using a cross-sectional study design, patient-level demographic, clinical, and social risk data were obtained from the electronic health record of Dialysis Clinic, Inc (DCI), the largest national not-for-profit dialysis provider in the United States.

Study Population

Maintenance dialysis patients 18 years or older receiving dialysis for ≥90 days who completed a health-related social needs survey between May 2023 and March 2025 at a DCI facility were included in this study.13 Excluded were patients with a current dialysis vintage of greater than two years, patients with history of prior PD use, patients with recent kidney transplantation with delayed graft function, and patients receiving home hemodialysis. Patients with a dialysis vintage of greater than 2 years were excluded because most patients who initiate home dialysis do so within 2 years of dialysis onset, after which time additional confounding variables such as clinical appropriateness and patients’ preferences for home modalities may contribute to nonreferral and initiation of home dialysis.3 Patients with recent kidney transplantation who resumed dialysis for delayed graft function were excluded because these patients were anticipated to transition off dialysis. Lastly, patients receiving home hemodialysis were excluded because these patients already utilize a home dialysis modality, comprise fewer than 10% of home dialysis patients at DCI, and, more broadly, fewer than 1% of all adult incident dialysis patients in the United States.3

Outcome

The outcome of interest was nonreceipt of PD at the time of social risk screening.

Exposures

The exposure of interest was presence of one or more patient-level SRFs identified by the questions derived from the Accountable Health Communities Health-Related Social Needs (AHC HRSN) screening tool.13 The AHC HRSN screening tool was developed by a panel of experts using previously studied and validated screening questions in the following 5 domains of social risk: housing instability, food insecurity, transportation insecurity, utility insecurity, and interpersonal safety concerns.14 DCI began administering these questions to all maintenance dialysis patients on an annual basis in May 2023 as a part of the social work assessment. Screening was performed more frequently among patients with unstable care plans. Questions could be asked of patients or their proxies. If an individual patient had more than 1 screening response on record, the most recent response was utilized for analyses. Each patient’s response to screening questions was tallied and categorized as presence of none (referent), 1, or more than 1 patient-level social risk factor (Table S1).

Covariates

Covariates were selected based on clinical relevance and their potential to confound the associations under study (Fig S1). Covariates were ascertained from the time of the AHC HRSN screening and include age, sex, race and ethnicity (American Indian, Asian, Black, Hispanic, non-Hispanic Black, non-Hispanic White, and Other/Unknown), comorbid conditions (diabetes mellitus, congestive heart failure, and chronic pulmonary disease), and dialysis vintage at the time of AHC HRSN survey administration. Although insurance type (Medicare/Veteran Affairs, Commercial, Medicare Advantage, Medicaid, or Dual Eligible (patients with Medicare who are eligible for concurrent Medicaid based on low income and low wealth), educational attainment (did not complete high school, completed high school, or completed some college or greater), and financial discomfort (an additional DCI-specific screening question) are reported, they were considered antecedent variables on the causal pathway associating SRFs with nonreceipt of PD and were not included as potential covariates in primary analyses (Fig S1). All covariates were obtained from the DCI electronic health record. Comorbid conditions were identified from the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) 2728 medical evidence form and updated with diagnoses by International Classification of Diseases (ICD)-10 codes from the electronic health record (Table S2). Rurality of dialysis clinic location was dichotomized as rural or urban using the rural-urban commuting area primary score.15

Statistical Methods

Summary statistics were provided for covariates, and baseline characteristics were compared by the number of SRFs collectively (0, 1, or >1), and individually, using contingency table χ2 - χ2 test for categorical variables and analysis of variance F-test for continuous variables. We summarized the co-occurrence of SRFs in the cohort with a heatmap. We then summarized the presence of specific SRFs by dialysis modality (ie, by patients receiving in-center HD versus PD). We examined the association of SRFs (overall burden and individual SRFs separately) with nonreceipt of PD and reported the odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) using mixed-effect logistic regression models, both unadjusted and adjusted for covariates. The fixed-effect part of the mixed-effect model included covariates: age, sex, race/ethnicity, dialysis vintage, presence of diabetes mellitus, congestive heart failure, and chronic pulmonary disease. The random-effect part of the mixed-effect model included dialysis facilities. Variance estimate, standard error of the variance estimate, median OR (MOR) and intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) were calculated and reported for the random effect.16 Variance estimate and MOR are measures of interdialysis facility heterogeneity on the probability of nonreceipt of PD. ICC indicates how much of the total variation in the odds of nonreceipt of PD is accounted for dialysis facility level factors (the random effect). When an individual social risk factor was evaluated as the exposure of interest, other social risk factors were also included as a covariate in the adjusted model. No collinearity of variables was observed (variance inflation factor < 5) among fixed-effect covariates. We additionally evaluated for an interaction between the exposure (SRF burden) and race/ethnicity. Given that the interaction term was not significant (P > 0.05), we did not perform a post-hoc subgroup analysis evaluating the association of SRFs with nonreceipt of PD stratified by race/ethnicity category. There was no missingness of the exposure or outcome variables. The absence of comorbid conditions from the CMS 2728 medical evidence forms or from ICD-10 codes was assumed to signify true absence of the comorbid conditions and not because of missingness.

Sensitivity Analysis

We performed a sensitivity analysis in which we expanded the definition of SRFs to include being primarily insured with Medicaid or being dual eligible (insured with Medicare and eligible for Medicaid), self-reported financial discomfort, and completing less than a high school education.

This study is reported in accordance with the “Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology” guidelines.17 This study was reviewed and deemed exempt by the WIRB-Copernicus Group (formerly Western Institutional Review Board) Institutional Review Board Work Order 1-1456342-1 as it did not qualify as human subjects research. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS software, version 9.4. Results

Among 18,455 adults receiving in-center maintenance hemodialysis who completed an AHC HRSN screen between 2023 and 2025 across 244 DCI facilities, 10,188 were excluded because of dialysis vintage greater than 2 years, 882 were excluded for prior PD use, 17 were excluded because of recent kidney transplantation, and 131 were excluded because of receiving home HD. This yielded a final cohort of 7,237 patients. The mean (SD) age of the study population was 64 ± 15 years, 43% were female, 47% were non-Hispanic White, and 26% were Black (Table 1). The median (interquartile) vintage of patients was 0.4 (25th to 75th percentile, 0.3-1.3) years. Comorbid conditions with the highest prevalence were diabetes mellitus and congestive heart failure (43% and 18%, respectively). Thirty-three percent of patients were insured with Medicaid or were dual eligible, whereas 8% of patients reported that their highest level of educational attainment was less than high school. Eighteen percent of patients reported financial discomfort, and 24% of patients were receiving dialysis at a rural dialysis clinic. A total of 656 (9%) and 211 (3%) patients reported 1 and more than 1 social risk factor, respectively (Table 1). Patients who reported presence of one or more SRFs were younger and more likely to be female, to be insured with Medicaid or dual eligible, and to have diabetes mellitus, but were less likely to have an active malignancy. Non-Hispanic Black, American Indian, and Hispanic patients reported more SRFs than non-Hispanic White patients. The presence of one or more SRFs was associated with both self-reported financial discomfort and high school or grade school as the highest level of educational attainment (P value < 0.001 for both). Baseline characteristics associated with individual SRFs were similar across the 5 SRF domains (Table S3). Food insecurity was the most common social risk factor (7%), followed by housing instability (4%), transportation insecurity (4%), and utility insecurity (2%) (Fig 1, Table S4). Very few patients reported interpersonal safety concerns. Of patients who reported at least two SRFs, the most commonly co-occurring SRFs in descending order were food insecurity and housing instability, housing instability and transportation insecurity, and food insecurity and transportation insecurity (Fig 1).

In unadjusted analyses accounting for facility-level clustering, patients who reported presence of one or more than one SRF exhibited significantly greater odds of nonreceipt of PD as compared with patients who reported no SRFs [OR 1.46 (95% CI 1.12-1.91) and 2.15 (1.30-3.56), respectively, Table 2]. When accounting for age, sex, race/ethnicity, dialysis vintage, and comorbid conditions, the association of one or more than one SRF with nonreceipt of PD remained similar and statistically significant [adjusted odds ratio (aOR) 1.46 (95% CI 1.10-1.92) and 2.27 (95% CI 1.35-3.82), respectively, Table 2]. The variance of the random-effect was estimated to be 3.8 with standard error 0.5 (P < 0.001). This yielded a MOR of 6.35 and ICC of 0.53. In the adjusted model, American Indian, Hispanic, Non-Hispanic Black, and patients of Other/Unknown race/ethnicity exhibited higher odds of nonreceipt of PD as compared with their non-Hispanic White counterparts; however, no significant interaction was observed between SRF burden and race/ethnicity. Discussion

In this study of a national cohort of maintenance dialysis patients, the presence of one and more than one SRF was associated with 46% and 127% higher odds of nonreceipt of PD, respectively, as compared with the absence of any SRFs when accounting for sociodemographic characteristics and comorbid conditions. The overall presence of one or more patient-level SRFs in our cohort was 12%, with the most common being food insecurity and housing instability. Nearly one-fifth of patients who reported food insecurity simultaneously reported housing instability or transportation insecurity. Similarly, nearly one-fifth of patients who reported housing instability also reported transportation insecurity. Among specific SRFs, individuals who reported housing instability and transportation insecurity were more likely to not receive PD.

Quantifying national prevalence of patient-level SRFs in the dialysis population is difficult because of the lack of standardized assessment, reporting, and sharing of risk measure data. Although the “Screening for Social Drivers of Health” reporting measure was briefly incorporated into the ESRD quality incentive program (ESRD QIP) and into the CMS 2728 medical evidence form, the reporting measure was removed during calendar year 2025, with CMS noting that “the burden of collecting data for this measure may outweigh the benefits.”18 This data deficit is compounded by the fact that social risk is often measured at the neighborhood- or dialysis-facility level, which may not reflect patient-level risk.11,19 In this context, our results are similar to that of a single-center study of patients undergoing dialysis access surgery at a safety-net hospital in Baltimore, Maryland, a presumably high-risk population, that quantified the prevalence of one or more SRFs as 22%, with the most common risk factors being transportation and food insecurity.20

We observed that housing instability and transportation insecurity were associated with higher odds of nonreceipt of PD. Because PD requires a clean and reliable environment with adequate storage space for supplies, unstable housing and/or risk of unstable housing is a logical risk factor for nonreceipt of PD. The association of transportation insecurity is less clear because prior studies have associated greater distance from dialysis facility and rurality of primary residence with increased utilization of home modalities, including PD.4,19 Relevant to this study, transportation insecurity was present when patients indicated a lack of reliable transportation to “…. medical appointments, meetings, work or from getting things needed for daily living,” encompassing more than just transportation to and from dialysis. Additionally, we note that only 11% of our cohort received dialysis care at a rural dialysis clinic that did not have its own PD program or a PD program within 30 miles of the dialysis clinic, and we accounted for dialysis facility-level factors (which would include rurality of the dialysis clinic and proximity to a PD program) in our analysis. In this context, transportation insecurity may associate with higher odds of nonreceipt of PD because of a persistent lack of access, particularly for rural patients, to PD programs; however, we additionally hypothesize that transportation insecurity reflects patients’ greater lack of access to medical and surgical appointments necessary for PD catheter placement, access to PD supplies, and possible global burden of social risk, thus influencing nonreceipt of PD in both direct and indirect ways.

We additionally observed that non-Hispanic Black, American Indian, and Hispanic patients reported higher presence of SRFs as compared with their non-Hispanic White counterparts, and the association of race/ethnicity with PD nonutilization remained statistically significant even after accounting for SRFs and other covariates. We hypothesize that these observed differences in the odds of nonreceipt of PD among various racial and ethnic subgroups may in part reflect implicit biases that affect clinical care decisions, historic health inequities incompletely captured by the covariates included in our analysis, or some combination thereof. Subgroup analysis was not performed because the interaction between SRF burden and race/ethnicity was not significant. However, statistical significance in this context may be limited by the small number of patients in some of the race/ethnicity categories and/or the small number of patients who reported one or more SRFs. Therefore, the absence of a statistically significant interaction does not exclude the possibility of effect modification of the relationship between SRF burden and nonreceipt of PD by race/ethnicity. Clearly, our findings suggest that patient-level SRFs do not completely account for differences observed in nonreceipt of PD, highlighting the complexity of addressing disparities in kidney care.

Lastly, we noted that when accounting for facility-level clustering, dialysis facility-level factors accounted for over half of all the variability in nonreceipt of PD. This implies a clinically-relevant degree of heterogeneity between facilities, which may be due to PD program availability, practice patterns (clinician familiarity with, preference for PD), and differential state Medicaid expansion decisions, all of which influence nonreceipt of PD.21 Accounting for dialysis-facility level factors did not, however, substantially affect the association of social risk burden with nonreceipt of PD.

Our findings may have implications for clinical care. First, some SRFs are potentially modifiable with pre-existing infrastructure that can be used for at-risk patients (ie, affordable housing vouchers, transportation assistance to medical appointments and procedures, and community home hemodialysis programs).22,23 In the context of existing infrastructure to alleviate social risk burden and our findings that housing instability and transportation insecurity independently associated with nonreceipt of PD after adjustment for sociodemographic and clinical factors, screening patients for SRFs as part of routine clinical care could better assist clinicians in identifying and referring at-risk patients to existing resources, potentially improving rates of peritoneal dialysis use.

The possible implications of our work in the dynamic landscape of dialysis health policy are less clear. CMS discontinued mandatory national reporting of patient-level SRFs in the dialysis population during calendar year 2025, although facilities and organizations remain able to query patients regarding these items.18 Without knowledge of SRFs, evaluating the impact of SRFs and designing and evaluating strategies to intervene are challenging. Furthermore, in the absence of social risk data, stratifying benchmarking of current or future quality metrics and pay-for-performance indicators may be limited. For example, in the ESRD Treatment Choices model, which aimed to increase use of home dialysis and kidney transplantation among Medicare beneficiaries, stratified benchmarks based on facility-level percentage of dual eligibility and low-income subsidy recipients resulted in fewer financial penalties for dialysis facilities serving disproportionally disadvantaged populations.24,25 In the same vein, it is possible that patient-level SRFs, when aggregated at the facility-level, may also serve to stratify benchmarks for existing quality outcomes in the ESRD QIP, and by doing so, limit the disproportionate financial penalties incurred by dialysis facilities that serve more disadvantaged populations. Fewer financial penalties may afford dialysis facilities that serve highly disadvantaged populations more resources to promote peritoneal dialysis use.

The main strengths of this study are the size and socioeconomic diversity of the national cohort, in addition to ascertainment of SRFs at the patient-level, avoiding misclassification of the exposure by extrapolating patient-level risk from neighborhood-level measures. Although residual misclassification of the exposure may have occurred if patients were uncomfortable disclosing presence of SRFs to their dialysis care team, observer bias was minimized by using a standardized, validated screening tool to assess social risk burden. The main limitations of this work include its cross-sectional nature, precluding causal inferences of the associations identified, and the potential for residual confounding, specifically as it pertains to clinical factors that may affect clinical appropriateness of PD such as history of abdominal pathology, cognitive impairment, active psychiatric or substance use disorders, frailty, and caregiver support, which are difficult to capture in the electronic health record. Additionally, nonreceipt of PD assessed at a single point in time does not encompass the full complexity of modality decision making or longevity including eligibility, education, provider biases, patient choice, and treatment modality failure.

In conclusion, presence of one or more individual-level SRFs significantly associated in a dose-dependent manner with nonreceipt of PD as compared with the absence of social risk after adjusting for sociodemographic and comorbid conditions. This finding should be replicated in longitudinal analyses to evaluate the causality of the association identified. Implications of this work and future analyses may include development of targeted interventions to address modifiable SRFs and policy reform pertaining to financial incentives associated with value-based care models to promote PD use among patients receiving maintenance dialysis.
