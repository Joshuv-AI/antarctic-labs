 Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

 This CRADA will focus on processing, advanced characterization, and testing of photovoltaic
 materials and devices to understand and improve REEL CdTe solar technology. This will include
 examining process variations and different buffer, absorber, and contact layers from REEL and
 NLR to maximize performance. The unique and diverse advanced characterization tools at NLR,
 such as time-resolved photoluminescence, capacitance-voltage measurements, electron beam
 scattered diffraction, cathodoluminescence, electron microscopy, TOF-SIMS, and other
 measurements will be applied to characterize REEL processing to improve understanding and
 guide experimental directions. Accelerated stability and potential induced degradation tests will
 be used to analyze metastability, short-and-long term degradation, and improve bankability. A
 second and major thrust this period will be joint development of Si/CdTe tandem solar cells to
 overcome industry wide terrestrial solar efficiency limits with the two lowest cost and
 manufacturable solar materials today. This will include developing novel transparent back
 contacts that can be incorporated into tandem structures and other novel solar applications,
 detailed analysis of designs and configurations for CdTe/Si tandem modules, and prototyping
 REEL CdTe Technology with Si bottom cells in tandem structures.

 Identify performance-limiting attributes over a range of common and uncommon CdTe device

 successful, will allow the CdTe technology to be on trajectory to meet the DOE SunShot goal of

 Summary of Research Results:

 Task 1: Consult with REEL on methods to increase performance, including photocurrent,
 fill factor, voltage, and efficiency.

 Task 1 Results:

 Photoluminescence measurements were performed on 3C SiC epilayers grown on 6H and 4H
 substrates. The spectra varied between samples and as a function of the position within the
 sample in some instances. In some cases where 3C epilayers were grown on 4H substrates, the
 epilayers had low PL intensity that could not be distinguished from the substrate (see Fig. 4).

 Task 2: Provide REEL with design and configuration options and analysis for Si/CdTe
 tandem structures.

 Task 2 Results:

 A four-terminal configuration was proposed where the Si bottom cell and CdTe top cell are
 mechanically stacked, with each subcell contacted separately. This design allowed independent
 operation of the top and bottom cells, reducing spectral mismatch losses and optimizing energy
 yield. The fabrication process used a PERC (passivated emitter rear contact) Si cell as the bottom
 subcell. The CdTe absorber and the transparent conducting oxide (TCO) layers were deposited
 for the CdTe top cell. An ethyl vinyl acetate (EVA) interlayer was used to laminate the two
 subcells. There were variations in CdTe absorber thickness for optimizing light management.
 The impact of minority carrier lifetime in CdTe was explored and showed how material quality
 affects overall device performance. It was determined that including bandgap tuning in CdTe
 (e.g., alloying with Mg or Zn to widen the bandgap) could be a potential future improvement for
 better performance with Si.

 NLR performed scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) combined with energy

 image of the CdTe device to bring out contrast between the back contact, CdS, and CdTe layers.
 Individual elements were mapped on the same area of the device, and Figure 2(b) shows the

 HADDF image of a different device area in Figure 2(d) shows noticeable voids near the front
 interface. Arrows point to these defects, which could reduce device efficiency. The information
 gleaned from STEM helped REEL identify areas for improvement in their fabrication process.

 Costs were calculated per square meter (a) and per Watt (b) for PERC Si, CdTe, and CdTe/Si
 tandem modules showed the tandem module was competitive with the single junctions
 incumbents on a purely $/W bases, without considering BOS adjustments. (See Figure 5)

 Transmission measurements were also performed on NLR IT on glass and REEL device stacks.
 An image of the ITO side of REEL devices is shown in Figure 3(a) and a schematic is shown in
 Figure 3(c). The graph in Figure 3(b) is transmission versus wavelength of measurements taken
 from the glass side of the sample. It shows that the sub-bandgap transmission is less than 50%
 for wavelengths from 825 (corresponding the ~1.5 eV bandgap) to 1000 nm, compared to the

 For improved tandem performance, the sub-bandgap absorption in CdTe should be minimized to
 allow greater light transmission to the bottom Si cell.

 Task 5: Provide Si bottom cells and Si/CdTe prototype integration for tandem photovoltaic
 applications.

 Task 5 Results:

 The tandem option was explored during 2018. But despite the hopes and initial published
 concept by Adele (JPV, that CdTe/Si tandem makes some sense) it was soon established
 experimentally that the CdTe by REEL was not suitable for tandem due to the huge below-gap
 absorption.

 The bottom PERC silicon cell was modeled starting with a standard PERC PC1D model [16].
 Recombination parameters were adjusted until cell performance (i.e., JV and EQE curves)
 matched representative best-in-class performance of lab scale cells [16]. To compute the PERC
 Si bottom cell performance under CdTe filtered light in PC1D, it was necessary to compute the
 device performance using the remaining spectrum, without the electrical influence of the CdTe
 cell.

 Following the guidelines described in [17], a PC1D model was inserted in a slab of material with
 CdTe’s optical properties, but with electronic properties like the front surface of the PERC Si
 cell. We decreased the minority carrier lifetime and mobility until exactly all optically generated
 carriers recombined within the slab. To avoid convergence issues that arise from high carrier
 densities or band discontinuities, recombination was set to zero at both slab surfaces and set the
 slab doping level equal to the PERC front surface doping level. Light passing through the cell
 was assumed to enter the PERC cell with no optical loss, neglecting the potential impact of grid
 shading from the CdTe cell and other module-related optical losses. All device modeling
 assumed an input spectrum of one sun at AM1.5G and a cell temperature of 25 ◦C.

 The modeling results include the behavior of the Si bottom cell operating under the filtered
 spectrum. This spectral adjustment was critical for tandem integration. It was important for there
 to be spectral and bandgap compatibility between the Si bottom cell and the CdTe top cell to
 ensure optimal energy harvesting.

 Task 7: Characterize cells, modules, and systems to examine long-term stability, fast
 degradation, metastability, and system issues such as potential induced degradation.
 Understand and resolve the physical mechanisms that cause problems through processing
 and characterization at REEL and NLR.

 Task 7 Results:

 Evaluation of the interaction of Na migration to the transparent conductive oxide (TCO) in
 potential-induced degradation (PID) testing and exposure to relative humidity to cause TCO
 corrosion. PID stress was applied in a front-grounded configuration for 96 hours. The voltage
 was - 1000V, relative humidity (RH) was 3%, and temperatures varied at 60°C, 75°C, and 82°C.
 After each PID stress, a RH test was performed. The temperature was set to 60°C and increased
 the RH in steps (3%, 6%, 9%, 12%, 15%...) until failure or a maximum of 24% RH), maintaining
 each RH for a defined number of hours. Figure 6(a) shows a graphical example of the test
 procedure with stress at 75°C (blue trace, left axis). The red trace (right axis) shows the relative
 humidity steps.

 In view of these results, it is seen that system voltage stress can lead to damage that only
 manifests after diffusion of humidity into the module at a later time. There also appears to be a
 threshold humidity level for immediate TCO corrosion to occur after the system voltage stress.
 Delamination is clearly visible in Figure 6(b), which shows an optical image of one of the
 samples when exposed to moisture after PID test.
