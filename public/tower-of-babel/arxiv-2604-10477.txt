Probing the Origin of Magnetar X-ray Polarization Diversity: A Multi-wavelength Geometrical Study of 1E 1547.0-5408 and 1E 2259+586 2026 Probing the Origin of Magnetar X-ray Polarization Diversity: A Multi-wavelength Geometrical Study of 1E 1547.0-5408 and 1E 2259+586 – 7
Biao-Peng Li, Zhi-Fu Gao, Wen-Qi Ma, Wei-Feng Zhang

Abstract
The exceptionally high X-ray polarization ( 65% at 2 keV) recently detected in the magnetar 1E 1547.0 − - 5408 is considered a strong candidate signature of quantum electrodynamic (QED) vacuum birefringence, an interpretation that hinges critically on the source’s viewing geometry. This stark contrast to the typically lower polarization degrees seen in other magnetars prompts a fundamental question: to what extent does viewing geometry, rather than intrinsic physics, drive the observed polarization diversity? To answer this, we perform a systematic, comparative geometrical analysis of two magnetars representing opposite extremes: the high-polarization source 1E 1547.0 − - 5408 and the low-polarization source 1E 2259+586. The data are modelled within a unified Bayesian framework with both the classical rotating vector model (CRVM) and a twisted-magnetosphere extension (MRVM). For 1E 2259+586, both models favour a geometry with moderate magnetic inclination and viewing angles but a small impact angle. By combining the single-epoch phase-resolved fit with three-epoch phase-averaged position angle measurements, we find no significant secular evolution of the twist parameter λ \lambda and derive a conservative upper limit of | Δ ​ λ | < 0.79 |\Delta\lambda|<0.79 at the 95 per cent level over ∼ 26 \sim 26 day. For 1E 1547.0 − - 5408, the observed position angle curve is already well reproduced by the CRVM, while the MRVM shows no statistically significant advantage. When radio-informed priors are imposed, the posterior shifts towards a nearly aligned configuration consistent with the radio constraints. Both sources show no evidence for strong, static global twists in the current epoch. The observed polarization dichotomy arises from the confluence of viewing geometry, intrinsic surface emission physics, and magnetospheric propagation effects. Our methodology and the established precise geometric baseline for 1E 1547.0-5408 pave the way for dynamic magnetospheric studies with next-generation missions like eXTP .



1
Introduction

Magnetars are neutron stars powered by the decay of their immense magnetic fields (
B\sim 10^{14}
–
10^{15}
G), serving as nature’s premier laboratories for studying matter under conditions unattainable on Earth. Their luminous, persistent X-ray emission, thought to originate from the thermal radiation of a heated surface and magnetospheric currents, encodes critical information about the star’s interior, magnetosphere, and the physics of strong-field quantum electrodynamics (QED). For decades, however, our understanding of this emission has been largely informed by spectroscopy and timing, leaving fundamental aspects of the radiation geometry and magnetic field topology ambiguous. Related studies further suggest that neutron-star radiation properties can be strongly state dependent and should ultimately be interpreted together with long-term magnetic and rotational evolution
(
31
;
32
;
15
)
.

The advent of the
Imaging X-ray Polarimetry Explorer (IXPE)
has fundamentally altered this landscape by adding the crucial dimension of polarization. X-ray polarization is exquisitely sensitive to the morphology of magnetic fields and the microphysics of emission and propagation in magnetized plasmas. Initial IXPE observations painted a surprising picture of dichotomy: while a majority of the observed magnetars, such as 4U 0142+61, exhibited relatively modest time-averaged polarization degrees (PD
\lesssim 20\%
), the radio-loud magnetar 1E 1547.0
-
5408 stood in stark contrast with an exceptionally high PD of
\sim 65\%

(
22
;
26
)
.
This has been interpreted as potential evidence for a condensed, highly conducting surface, which is expected to emit low-polarization radiation, or for significant
depolarization due to mode mixing within a magnetized atmosphere.

In stark contrast to this emerging norm, a recent study of the radio-loud magnetar 1E 1547.0
-
5408 by
22
reported an unusually high phase-averaged PD of
\sim 65\%
at 2 keV, reaching nearly
\sim 80\%
at some rotational phases. Interpreted together with radio-based geometrical constraints, these results were argued to be consistent with a significant role for vacuum birefringence (VB), a fundamental QED effect by which the quantum vacuum becomes birefringent in ultra-strong magnetic fields. In particular, the radio analysis suggested that 1E 1547.0
-
5408 is a nearly aligned rotator (
\chi\approx 3.4^{\circ}
) viewed close to its spin axis (
\zeta\approx 7.5^{\circ}
), a configuration that can reduce phase-averaged depolarization from an extended emitting region. However, a subsequent IXPE-based reanalysis by
25
reached a more cautious interpretation: although the data remain suggestive of QED effects, the phase-dependent X-ray PA favours a more inclined geometry, under which the high observed polarization alone can no longer be regarded as compelling evidence for magnetospheric VB. This tension underscores that the inferred magnetic geometry, and consequently the physical interpretation of the polarization signal, can be highly sensitive to the assumptions and prior information used in the analysis. It therefore calls for a systematic, comparative study within a unified and statistically rigorous framework, which is the primary objective of this work.

The discovery in 1E 1547.0-5408 resolves a long-standing theoretical quest but immediately raises two profound and interconnected questions central to understanding magnetar emission diversity. First, to what extent is this extreme geometric configuration a prerequisite for such high observed polarization? Is the predominance of lower PDs in other magnetars simply a consequence of less favorable viewing angles, or does it indicate genuinely
different surface or magnetospheric physics? Second, what is the large-scale magnetic topology of magnetars? Theoretical models frequently invoke globally twisted magnetic fields to explain their bursting activity and radiative properties. Such a twist should imprint a characteristic, time-dependent
signature on the sweep of the X-ray polarization position angle (PA)
(
28
)
. Searching for this signature provides a direct observational test
of the global magnetosphere model, distinct from inferences based on timing or spectroscopy.

Answering these questions requires moving beyond individual source studies. A comparative, diagnostic investigation of magnetars with starkly different
observed polarization signatures, analyzed within a unified, rigorous framework, is essential to disentangle the conflated effects of viewing geometry,
global magnetic topology, and intrinsic emission physics.

In this paper, we focus on two magnetars, 1E 2259+586 and 1E 1547.0
-
5408, both of which have
IXPE
observations suitable for phase-resolved geometrical modelling, but which probe rather different aspects of the problem. For 1E 2259+586, the available X-ray PA measurements allow a comparison between the classical rotating vector model and a twisted-magnetosphere extension, while multi-epoch phase-averaged PA measurements make it possible to place a first constraint on the temporal variation of the twist parameter. For 1E 1547.0
-
5408, the higher-quality phase-resolved X-ray PA curve provides a useful test of whether a twisted model is actually required by the data, and the existence of radio polarization constraints offers an independent geometrical reference against which the X-ray inference can be compared.
Our work not only probes the static magnetic skeleton of these objects but also establishes the analytical "baseline" necessary for the next
frontier: time-resolved magnetospheric studies. We therefore contextualize our findings within the prospects offered by the future
Enhanced X-ray
Timing and Polarimetry (eXTP)
mission
(
7
)
, which will enable dynamic tracking of magnetic field evolution following magnetar outbursts.

The paper is structured as follows. Section
2
details the X-ray (IXPE, NICER) and radio (Parkes) observations and data reduction.
Section
3
introduces the Rotating Vector Model, its twisted magnetosphere extension, and our Bayesian joint-fitting framework.
Results for 1E 2259+586 and 1E 1547.0-5408 are presented in Section
4
. In Section
5
, we discuss the implications for
magnetar magnetic topology, present a unified interpretation of polarization diversity, and outline future directions with eXTP. We conclude in Section

6
.

3
Methodology: Geometric Modeling and Bayesian Analysis

3.1
The Rotating Vector Model and its Extension

In the ultra-strong magnetic environment surrounding a magnetar, radiation propagates in two normal polarization modes, namely the ordinary (O) mode and the extraordinary (X) mode. In the former, the electric vector oscillates in the plane defined by the local magnetic field and the photon wave vector, whereas in the latter it oscillates perpendicular to that plane
(
8
;
16
)
. Owing to vacuum birefringence, the polarization vector is expected to follow the direction of the local magnetic field adiabatically until the so-called polarization-limiting radius
(
11
;
12
)
. For keV photons from typical magnetars, this radius is expected to be of the order of a few hundred stellar radii, where the large-scale field is dominated by its dipolar component
(
24
;
10
)
. As a result, the polarization measured by a distant observer is expected to be either parallel or perpendicular to the instantaneous projection of the magnetic axis onto the plane of the sky, depending on whether the escaping radiation is dominated by the O or X mode. Under these conditions, the phase modulation of the polarization angle is largely decoupled from the evolution of the polarization degree and intensity, and is expected to trace mainly the viewing geometry. This makes the rotating vector model (RVM) a natural starting point for describing the phase dependence of the polarization angle in magnetars
(
19
;
18
;
26
;
9
)
.

We model the phase-resolved polarization position angle curves of both sources using two geometrical descriptions: the classical rotating vector model (CRVM) and a twisted-magnetosphere rotating vector model (MRVM). The CRVM provides the baseline dipole-dominated geometry, while the MRVM introduces a first-order correction associated with a globally twisted magnetosphere.

For the CRVM, we adopt the standard expression
(
19
;
14
)
.

\Psi_{\rm CRVM}(\phi)=\Psi_{0}+\arctan\!\left[\frac{-\sin\chi\,\sin(\phi-\phi_{0})}{\sin\zeta\cos\chi-\cos\zeta\sin\chi\cos(\phi-\phi_{0})}\right],

(1)

where
\Psi
represents the position angle,
\Psi_{0}
is the position angle of the pulsar spin axis,
\chi
is the magnetic inclination angle,
\zeta
is the angle between the pulsar spin vector and the line of sight,
\phi
is the pulse phase, and
\phi_{0}/2\pi
is the phase (in units of the rotational cycle) at which the magnetic pole is closest to the observer.

To account for possible magnetospheric twist, we further consider the MRVM proposed by
28
. In this approximation, the observed PA is written as

\Psi_{\rm MRVM}(\phi)=\Psi_{\rm CRVM}(\phi)+\Delta\Psi_{\rm twist}(\phi),

(2)

where the leading-order twisted contribution is approximated by

\Delta\Psi_{\rm twist}\simeq-\frac{8}{9}\lambda\sin^{2}\theta_{\rm obs},

(3)

where
\theta_{\rm obs}
is the colatitude of the line of sight in the magnetic frame which is
\cos\theta_{\rm obs}=\cos\chi\cos\zeta+\sin\chi\sin\zeta\cos(\phi-\phi_{0})
.
The
\lambda
is a dimensionless parameter that parametrizes the twist of the magnetic field through
\lambda=\sqrt{(35/16)(1-n)}
, where
n=1
corresponds to the pure dipole limit,
n=0
is the split monopole case, and
0<n<1
is the general twisted dipole case.

In the present work, we use this first-order correction as a phenomenological extension of the CRVM. Its role is not to provide a complete description of the magnetar magnetosphere, but to test whether the available X-ray PA data require a statistically significant departure from the dipole-dominated case.

For both models, we also report the derived impact angle

\beta=\zeta-\chi
, which measures the minimum angular separation between the line of sight and the magnetic axis.

3.2
Bayesian inference method

The analyses of 1E 2259+586 and 1E 1547.0
-
5408 are performed within a common Bayesian inference framework, differing only in the auxiliary constraints included for each source. In the case of 1E 2259+586, multi-epoch phase-averaged polarization position angle measurements are incorporated to place a first constraint on the temporal evolution of the magnetospheric twist parameter. For 1E 1547.0
-
5408, radio-derived geometrical constraints are introduced in addition to the X-ray data, allowing both a cross-band comparison and an explicit examination of the prior sensitivity of the inferred geometry.

The parameter vectors of the two models are
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{\rm CRVM}=(\Psi_{0},\chi,\zeta,\phi_{0}/2\pi)
,
and
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{\rm MRVM}=(\Psi_{0},\chi,\zeta,\phi_{0}/2\pi,\lambda)
.

Because the PA is defined modulo
180^{\circ}
, a naive linear residual can introduce artificial discontinuities near branch boundaries. To avoid this, we define the angular residual in the principal interval through

\Delta_{i}=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{atan2}\!\left[\sin(2\delta_{i}),\cos(2\delta_{i})\right],\qquad\delta_{i}={\rm PA}_{{\rm obs},i}-{\rm PA}_{{\rm mod},i},

(4)

so that
\Delta_{i}
is always wrapped into the physically relevant range. Although some PA curves are displayed in an unwrapped form for clarity, all statistical inferences are performed using this periodic angular residual.

Assuming independent Gaussian measurement uncertainties, the log-likelihood is written as

\ln\mathcal{L}=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\left[\frac{\Delta_{i}^{2}}{\sigma_{{\rm PA},i}^{2}}+\ln\!\left(2\pi\sigma_{{\rm PA},i}^{2}\right)\right],

(5)

where
{\rm PA}_{{\rm obs},i}
and
\sigma_{{\rm PA},i}
are the observed PA and its uncertainty in the
i
th phase bin, and
{\rm PA}_{{\rm mod},i}
is the model prediction.

For the baseline analysis, we adopt uniform priors

\Psi_{0}\in(-180^{\circ},\,180^{\circ}),\,\chi\in(0^{\circ},\,180^{\circ}),\,\zeta\in(0^{\circ},\,180^{\circ}),\,\phi_{0}/2\pi\in(-0.5,\,0.5)
.
For the MRVM, we further impose

\lambda\in(0,\,1.48)
,
which corresponds to the physically relevant range
0<n<1
. The posterior distribution is therefore

p(\boldsymbol{\Theta}\mid\mathcal{D})\propto\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{D}\mid\boldsymbol{\Theta})\,\pi(\boldsymbol{\Theta}),

(6)

where
\mathcal{D}
denotes the phase-resolved PA data and
\pi(\boldsymbol{\Theta})
is the prior. For 1E 1547.0
-
5408, we additionally perform a second analysis using Gaussian priors for
\chi
and
\zeta
informed by the radio constraints (
\chi\sim\mathcal{N}(3.4^{\circ},1.3^{\circ}),\ \zeta\sim\mathcal{N}(7.5^{\circ},3.0^{\circ}
);
(
22
)
), in order to assess the prior sensitivity of the X-ray geometrical inference.

3.3
Sampling strategy

We first use differential evolution to identify a high-likelihood region in parameter space
(
23
)
. The best solution obtained in this stage is then used to initialize an ensemble Markov chain Monte Carlo sampler based on
emcee

(
5
)
. Posterior summaries are reported using the median together with the 16th and 84th percentiles, which we quote as the central value and the corresponding
1\sigma
credible interval.

To estimate the Bayesian evidence, we also perform nested-sampling calculations using
dynesty

(
21
)
. This allows us to compare the global statistical support for the CRVM and MRVM rather than relying only on best-fitting solutions.

3.4
Model comparison and posterior predictive checks

We compare the CRVM and MRVM using both information criteria and Bayesian evidence. For a model with
k
free parameters and maximum likelihood
\mathcal{L}_{\max}
, the Akaike information criteria and the Bayesian information criteria are defined as
(
1
;
20
)

{\rm AIC}=2k-2\ln\mathcal{L}_{\max},

(7)

and

{\rm BIC}=k\ln N-2\ln\mathcal{L}_{\max},

(8)

where
N
is the number of phase bins. These criteria quantify the trade-off between improved fit quality and increased model complexity.
For the Bayesian evidence, we focus on

\Delta\ln Z=\ln Z_{\rm MRVM}-\ln Z_{\rm CRVM},

(9)

where
\ln Z
denotes the logarithm of the marginal likelihood. A positive
\Delta\ln Z
favours the MRVM, while a negative value favours the CRVM. We also quote the corresponding Bayes factor
(
13
;
29
)

{\rm BF}=\exp(\Delta\ln Z).

(10)

Finally, we generate posterior predictive distributions (PPDs) by drawing model realizations from the posterior samples and evaluating the PA curve at the observed phase bins
(
4
;
6
)
. The median and central credible intervals of these realizations provide a direct visualization of the phase-dependent range allowed by the data. This representation is useful not only for assessing the adequacy of an individual model, but also for identifying where the CRVM and MRVM make distinguishable predictions in phase space.

4.1
Results for 1E 2259+586

4.1.1
Single-epoch constraints from phase-resolved PA fitting

We first fit the phase-resolved PA measurements of 1E 2259+586 in the 2–8 keV band with both the CRVM and the MRVM. The posterior constraints are summarized in Table
4
, while the corresponding posterior predictive distributions and corner plots are shown in the Appendix.

Although the two models do not return identical posterior centres, they imply a broadly consistent geometrical picture. In the CRVM fit, the posterior medians are
\chi\approx 47^{\circ}
and
\zeta\approx 55^{\circ}
, corresponding to an impact angle
\beta=\zeta-\chi\approx 8^{\circ}
. In the MRVM fit, the posterior medians shift to
\chi\approx 33^{\circ}
and
\zeta\approx 38^{\circ}
, giving
\beta\approx 5^{\circ}
. In both cases, the line of sight therefore passes relatively close to the magnetic axis during the rotation cycle. This small-impact-angle geometry provides a natural explanation for the smooth phase modulation seen in the observed PA curve.

A noticeable difference between the two models is that the inclusion of the twist parameter
\lambda
broadens the posterior distributions of the geometrical angles. This behaviour indicates a stronger coupling between the global geometry and the twist amplitude in the MRVM. The current data therefore allow a non-zero twist correction, but do not constrain its magnitude tightly enough to establish a strongly distorted large-scale field geometry from the single-epoch phase-resolved data alone.

4.1.3
Constraint on the temporal variation of the twist parameter

We next use the three-epoch phase-averaged PA measurements to place a first constraint on the temporal evolution of the twist parameter. The idea is to adopt the best-fitting MRVM geometry from the single-epoch phase-resolved analysis as a reference geometry, and then interpret any inter-epoch shift of the phase-averaged PA as a consequence of a change in
\lambda
.

Since the linear polarization angle is defined modulo
180^{\circ}
, we do not compute the phase-averaged PA through a direct arithmetic average. Instead, we combine the phase-resolved polarization directions in the
Q
–
U
plane and reconstruct the corresponding mean angle from the summed Stokes-like quantities:

\overline{\Psi}_{\rm mod}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{atan2}\left[\sum_{i}w_{i}\sin\!\bigl(2\Psi_{i}(\lambda)\bigr),\sum_{i}w_{i}\cos\!\bigl(2\Psi_{i}(\lambda)\bigr)\right].

(11)

Because detailed phase-resolved polarized flux weights are not available, we adopt the equal-weight approximation, i.e.
w_{i}={\rm const.}
for all phase bins.

To convert the observed PA drift into a twist variation, we evaluate the effective response coefficient around the reference solution

K_{\rm eff}=\left.\frac{{\rm d}\overline{\Psi}_{\rm mod}}{{\rm d}\lambda}\right|_{\lambda_{\rm ref}},

(12)

Using a finite difference step
\delta\lambda=10^{-2}
, we obtain
K_{\rm eff}=-37.60~{\rm deg}\,\lambda^{-1}
.
This implies that, under the adopted reference geometry, an increase in the twist parameter shifts the phase-averaged PA towards smaller values.

We then fit the three phase-averaged PA measurements with a weighted linear model,

\overline{\Psi}_{\rm obs}(t)=a+b(t-t_{c}),

(13)

where
t_{c}
is the mean midpoint of the three epochs. The fit gives
a=-75.10^{\circ},\,b=-0.24^{\circ}\pm 0.70^{\circ}~{\rm day}^{-1}
. The corresponding weighted linear fit to the three epoch-averaged PA measurements is shown in Fig.
1
.

Because the fitted slope is much smaller than its uncertainty, the data do not show any significant secular evolution of the phase-averaged PA.

Mapping this slope to a twist variation rate through

\dot{\lambda}\simeq\frac{b}{K_{\rm eff}}
,
we obtain

\dot{\lambda}=(6.29\pm 18.38)\times 10^{-3}\ {\rm day}^{-1}
.
The central value is consistent with zero. Under a Gaussian approximation, the corresponding one-sided 95 per cent upper limit is

|\dot{\lambda}|_{95}<3.03\times 10^{-2}\ {\rm day}^{-1}
.
Over the full time span of
\Delta t=26.08
day, this translates into
|\Delta\lambda|_{95}<0.79
.

This result shows that the presently available multi-epoch phase-averaged PA measurements do not require any significant twist evolution in 1E 2259+586. In other words,
\Delta\lambda=0
remains fully compatible with the data, and the allowed variation of the twist parameter is at most of order unity over the observed interval. When combined with the weak statistical preference for the MRVM in the single-epoch fit, the overall picture is therefore one in which the source is consistent with a dipole-dominated large-scale geometry, possibly supplemented by a modest twist whose temporal evolution is slow and limited in amplitude.

It should be emphasized, however, that this upper limit remains model-dependent. The derivation assumes that the large-scale geometry does not vary significantly between epochs, that the equal-weight approximation is adequate for constructing the mean PA, and that the leading-order Tong-type twist correction captures the dominant effect of magnetospheric twist on the observed PA. The limit should therefore be regarded as a conservative constraint under the present modelling assumptions rather than as a direct measurement of twist evolution.

A useful way to interpret the present upper limit is to express it as a precision requirement on future phase-averaged PA measurements. Since
\Delta\Psi\simeq K_{\rm eff}\,\Delta\lambda
, the one-sided
95\,\%
sensitivity to twist evolution scales as

|\Delta\lambda|_{95}\simeq\frac{1.645\,\sigma_{\Delta\Psi}}{|K_{\rm eff}|},

(14)

where
\sigma_{\Delta\Psi}\simeq(\sigma_{{\rm PA},1}^{2}+\sigma_{{\rm PA},2}^{2})^{1/2}
. With
|K_{\rm eff}|=37.60\,{\rm deg}\,\lambda^{-1}
, achieving
|\Delta\lambda|_{95}<0.1
requires
\sigma_{\Delta\Psi}<2.29^{\circ}
, corresponding to
\sigma_{\rm PA}\lesssim 1.6^{\circ}
per epoch for two measurements of comparable quality. Reaching
|\Delta\lambda|_{95}<0.05
would require
\sigma_{\rm PA}\lesssim 0.8^{\circ}
per epoch. In contrast to the sub-degree precision required to detect subtle twist evolution, the currently available data, with epoch-averaged PA uncertainties of
\sim 10^{\circ}
–
20^{\circ}
, can only constrain order-unity changes in
\lambda
. This indicates that the present upper limit is not yet a stringent test of weak twist evolution, but rather a quantitative baseline for the polarimetric precision required by future observations.

4.1.4
Summary for 1E 2259+586

The results for 1E 2259+586 can be summarized as follows. First, both the CRVM and the MRVM provide an acceptable description of the phase-resolved PA curve and favour a geometry with moderate
\chi
, moderate
\zeta
, and a small impact angle. Secondly, although the MRVM yields a slightly better fit and is mildly favoured by the information criteria and Bayesian evidence, the statistical support for a global twist remains weak. Thirdly, the multi-epoch phase-averaged PA measurements do not show significant long-term drift, allowing only a conservative upper limit of
|\Delta\lambda|_{95}<0.79
over
\sim 26
d. Taken together, these results suggest that the X-ray polarization geometry of 1E 2259+586 is broadly consistent with a low-order dipolar structure, while allowing for at most a modest and slowly varying twist contribution. At the same time, the present multi-epoch data constrain only order-unity changes in
\lambda
over the observed interval. Future phase-averaged PA measurements reaching sub-degree to few-degree precision will be needed before weak twist evolution can be tested stringently.

4.2.3
Implications for the geometry of 1E 1547.0
-
5408

The results for 1E 1547.0
-

First, the source exhibits clear prior sensitivity: the geometry inferred from the X-ray PA data changes significantly when radio-informed constraints are imposed. Secondly, in neither the flat-prior nor the radio-informed analysis does the MRVM show a statistically significant advantage over the CRVM. The safest interpretation is therefore not that a twisted magnetosphere is required by the current data, but rather that the presently available X-ray PA measurements are compatible with a dipole-dominated large-scale geometry and do not yet constrain the twist strongly.

At the same time, the mismatch between the flat-prior X-ray posterior and the radio-informed solution may carry physical information. One possibility is that the radio and X-ray emission sample different effective regions of the magnetosphere, so that the two wavebands are not equally sensitive to the same global geometry. Another possibility is that propagation effects in the magnetosphere affect the inferred PA swing differently at radio and X-ray energies. While the present data do not allow us to distinguish between these scenarios, they do show that multiwavelength comparisons are essential when interpreting magnetar polarization geometry.

Our results for 1E 1547.0
-
5408 should be viewed in the context of the recent deep IXPE analysis by
25
, who also found that the phase-dependent X-ray PA can be reproduced within a rotating-vector framework, but inferred a geometry in which both the magnetic axis and the line of sight are appreciably misaligned with respect to the spin axis. In that interpretation, the high observed X-ray polarization cannot be regarded as a smoking-gun signature of magnetospheric vacuum birefringence on geometrical grounds alone, although the overall energy and phase dependence of the polarization signal still remains suggestive of QED effects. By contrast, when radio-informed priors are imposed in our analysis, the posterior shifts toward a nearly aligned configuration close to the geometry inferred from the contemporaneous radio observations. The comparison therefore indicates that the geometrical inference for 1E 1547.0
-
5408 is prior-sensitive at the current level of X-ray data quality, and that radio and X-ray polarization may not probe identical effective magnetic geometries. In this sense, our Bayesian framework quantifies the prior sensitivity and provides a coherent bridge between the radio-informed and the X-ray-only geometric solutions, highlighting that the presence of a strong global twist is not demanded by the X-ray PA data in either scenarios.

-

The main conclusions for 1E 1547.0
-

Under flat priors, the X-ray PA data favour a geometry with a relatively small magnetic inclination but a broad and weakly constrained viewing angle, leading to a large inferred impact angle. In this regime, the CRVM already provides an adequate description of the observed PA curve, and the MRVM does not yield a meaningful statistical improvement. Overall, the source provides no compelling evidence for a globally twisted magnetosphere, but instead highlights both the present power and the current limitations of X-ray polarimetry as a probe of magnetar radiation geometry.

5
Discussion

5.1
A Static, Large-Scale Dipolar Field in Two Magnetars

Our comparative study yields two key findings regarding the large-scale magnetic field of magnetars in their quiescent state. First, for both 1E 2259+586 and 1E 1547.0
-
5408, the current phase-resolved X-ray PA data are consistent with a dipole-dominated geometry, showing no statistically compelling evidence for a strong, static global twist. For both 1E 2259+586 and 1E 1547.0
-
5408, our results suggest that the answer is, at present, no. Although the MRVM yields a slightly better fit for 1E 2259+586, the improvement in
\chi^{2}
, AIC, BIC, and Bayesian evidence remains modest. For 1E 1547.0
-
5408, the CRVM and MRVM are statistically almost indistinguishable, and the best-fitting MRVM solution effectively collapses back to the classical dipole limit. Taken together, these results indicate that the currently available
IXPE
PA data do not yet provide compelling evidence for a strong global twist in either source. This consistency with a dipole-dominated field reinforces the geometric foundation for interpreting the high PD in 1E 1547.0
-
5408 as a signature of vacuum birefringence, an effect which relies on photons propagating through a predictable, large-scale dipolar field to achieve mode locking
(
22
)
.

For 1E 2259+586, the additional use of multi-epoch phase-averaged PA measurements provides information of a different kind. Even though these data do not retain the full phase structure of the PA swing, they enable a test of whether the large-scale polarization geometry evolves significantly over the observing span. The derived upper limit,
|\Delta\lambda|_{95}<0.79
over about 26 d, indicates that the current data do not require substantial secular evolution of the twist-related parameter on this timescale.

These conclusion should not be interpreted as evidence that magnetospheric twist is absent. Magnetars are widely expected to host current-carrying and possibly twisted external fields, especially during or after outbursts and in connection with long-term radiative activity
(
27
;
30
)
. Rather, our results imply that, for the present data quality and phase resolution, the dominant observable imprint on the X-ray PA curve comes from the gross viewing geometry, while any twist-induced correction remains subdominant. In this sense, the current observations are primarily sensitive to the large-scale orientation of the magnetic and rotational axes, not yet to weak departures from an underlying dipolar structure.

The lack of a detectable twist in the current, quiescent-state data does not rule out the twisted magnetar model. It implies that any strong twist may be: (1) highly localized (e.g., confined to small bundles of field lines); (2) dynamic, existing primarily during and shortly after outbursts; or (3) of a global configuration that projects a minimal deviation from the dipole PA swing along the particular line of sight to these sources. Our work establishes a crucial "quiet-state" baseline against which future observations during outbursts can be compared.

5.2
Deciphering the Polarization Dichotomy: Geometry as the Primary Driver

The stark contrast in PD between the two magnetars finds a coherent explanation primarily in their differing viewing geometries. This is exemplified by 1E 1547.0
-
5408, whose exceptionally high polarization is a direct consequence of its unique nearly aligned, pole-on geometry (
\chi\approx 3^{\circ},\zeta\approx 9^{\circ}
). In this configuration, the line of sight aligns almost with the magnetic axis, pointing toward a single magnetic pole. This minimizes the integration over a wide range of surface magnetic field directions, drastically reducing the intrinsic geometric depolarization that would arise from an extended hot-spot. The resulting clean propagation channel allows the high intrinsic polarization from a magnetized atmosphere to be preserved, and
potentially enhanced by magnetospheric vacuum birefringence, ultimately producing the observed extreme PD
(
22
)
.

For 1E 2259+586, our analysis indicates a geometry with moderate and similar magnetic inclination and viewing angles (
\chi\sim\zeta
). In such an intermediate or grazing configuration, the line of sight samples a broad swath of the visible hot-spot, integrating over a wide range of magnetic field orientations. This leads to strong geometric depolarization, even if the local emission at the surface is highly polarized. Consequently, the low time-averaged
PD observed in 1E 2259+586 (and similarly polarized magnetars) may not necessarily indicate a condensed surface; it could primarily be a result of this less avorable viewing geometry. Disentangling geometric depolarization from intrinsically low-polarization emission will require the broadband spectro-polarimetric capabilities of future missions like eXTP.

5.3
Implications for Magnetar Physics and Population Studies

Our study underscores that the observed X-ray polarization from a magnetar is not dictated by a single factor, but arises from the interplay of at least three elements: (i) intrinsic emission physics at the surface (e.g., atmosphere versus condensed matter), (ii) the global viewing geometry (
\chi
,
\zeta
), and (iii) magnetospheric propagation effects (VB, current-induced twists). The case of 1E 1547.0
-
5408 demonstrates that when the viewing geometry is highly favorable, effect (iii) can become the dominant observable signature. For most other sources, effect (ii) likely acts as the primary suppressor of the observed PD.

This interpretative framework carries important implications for population studies. The observed distribution of PDs among magnetars may largely reflect the random distribution of their spin-axis orientations relative to our line of sight, rather than an intrinsic diversity in surface emission properties. The joint-fitting methodology developed in this work establishes a methodological template for future systematic measurements of
\chi
and
\zeta
in radio-loud magnetars, which is essential for rigorously testing this hypothesis.

6
Conclusions

In this work, we have performed a systematic, comparative study of the viewing geometry and large-scale magnetic field topology in two magnetars with contrasting polarization signatures: the high-PD source 1E 1547.0
-
5408 and the low-PD source 1E 2259+586. Our analysis, based on phase-resolved IXPE observations within a unified Bayesian framework, yields key insights into the origin of magnetar polarization diversity and establishes a robust methodological baseline for future studies. Within a unified Bayesian framework, we compared the classical rotating vector model (CRVM) with a twisted-magnetosphere extension (MRVM), and explored whether the currently available data require a statistically significant departure from a dipole-dominated large-scale geometry.

For 1E 2259+586, both the CRVM and the MRVM provide acceptable descriptions of the observed phase-resolved PA curve. The inferred geometry is characterized by moderate magnetic inclination and viewing angles, together with a relatively small impact angle, implying that the line of sight passes close to the magnetic axis during the rotation cycle. The MRVM yields a modest improvement in fit quality relative to the CRVM. However, the corresponding preference in the information criteria and Bayesian evidence remains weak. The current single-epoch X-ray polarimetric data therefore do not provide compelling evidence for a strongly twisted large-scale magnetosphere. Furthermore, the combination of single-epoch geometry fitting with multi-epoch phase-averaged PA measurements reveals no statistically significant secular evolution of the magnetospheric twist parameter
\lambda
. We place a conservative upper limit of
\Delta\lambda_{95}<0.79
over the
\sim 26
-day observing span. This result indicates that, during the quiescent state observed, any global twist evolution is either absent or occurs at a rate below the current detection threshold.

For 1E 1547.0
-
5408, the phase-dependent X-ray PA curve is already well reproduced by the CRVM under flat priors, although the inferred geometry remains substantially degenerate. The MRVM shows no statistically significant advantage: its best-fitting solution approaches the dipole limit, and its posterior predictive distributions are nearly indistinguishable from those of the CRVM. Hence, the present X-ray data do not require a global twist component for this source.
Furthermore, the imposition of radio-informed priors shifts the posterior towards a nearly aligned geometry, demonstrating that the X-ray geometrical inference is inherently and strongly sensitive to the adopted prior information. This prior sensitivity underscores the necessity of multi-wavelength constraints for a robust physical interpretation.
In light of the recent IXPE-only reanalysis by
25
, this comparison suggests that radio and X-ray polarization may not probe identical effective geometries, and that multi-wavelength information is essential for a physically complete interpretation.

Taken together, our study of 1E 2259+586 and 1E 1547.0
-
5408 supports a coherent framework in which the observed polarization diversity of magnetars is governed by the confluence of viewing geometry, intrinsic surface-emission physics, and magnetospheric propagation effects. For 1E 2259+586, the lower polarization is consistent with significant geometric depolarization in an intermediate viewing geometry. For 1E 1547.0
-
5408, the high polarization does not necessitate a large-scale global twist, but its definitive physical interpretation, particularly regarding the role of vacuum birefringence, is inherently sensitive to the adopted geometrical priors. Crucially, in both sources, we find no evidence for strong, static global magnetic twists in the current epoch.

More broadly, this work establishes and demonstrates the power of a unified Bayesian framework for magnetar polarimetry. This methodology provides a rigorous tool for comparing X-ray-only and multi-wavelength geometrical inferences and, fundamentally, for quantifying the prior-sensitivity of the results, a key insight for the field. The precise geometric baselines determined here, combined with this analytical framework, will be pivotal for future studies. Upcoming missions like eXTP will leverage this approach to extend such analysis to fainter magnetars, enable time-resolved tracking of magnetospheric evolution, and ultimately disentangle the intertwined roles of geometry, surface physics, and magnetospheric QED in shaping neutron-star polarization signatures.
