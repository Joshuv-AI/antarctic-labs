Risk assessment for hydrophobic disinfectants and antiseptics as contaminants in freshwater using risk quotient and network pharmacology approaches
Authors: Songyi Wei, Yinan Wang, Zerong Zhu, Jun Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryIncreasing public use of disinfectants and antiseptics (DAs) has raised concerns about their environmental contamination and adverse effects on non-target organisms, despite critical knowledge gaps in the ecological risks posed by DAs. This study adopted the risk quotient (RQ)-based assessment and a network pharmacology-based framework to screen potential ecological risks of 42 hydrophobic DAs. Among 27 RQ-available DAs, 19 DAs were predicted to pose high ecological risks, with chlorhexidine, dimethyldioctadecylammonium bromide, cetylpyridinium chloride, triclosan, and triclocarban exhibiting RQ > 100. Based on the screening criteria that the 50% effective concentrations for targets were equal to or below the predicted fish plasma concentrations, the final environmental risk-based network retained 140 targets and 214 DA-target interactions for 10 DAs. Functional analyses identified 8 core targets of the DA mixtures in fish, which were related to the behavior, endocrine, nervous system, digestive system, cardiovascular system, skeletal system, and eyes in exposed zebrafish.

Introduction

As chemical biocidal agents that inactivate or destroy microorganisms on non-living surfaces and living tissues, respectively,1 disinfectants and antiseptics (DAs) are routinely, even increasingly, consumed by the general public in a wide array of medical, cleaning, and personal care products for preservative and antimicrobial applications.2,3,4,5 Consequently, the environmental release of DAs has attracted growing concern regarding their potential risks to the ecosystem and human health.6,7,8,9,10 DAs can pour into the environment through multiple pathways, including domestic and hospital wastewater discharges, direct disposal of unwanted DA-containing products as solid waste, effluents from manufacturing and agricultural activities, runoff from outdoor surfaces sprayed with DAs, and wastewater disinfection process in sewage treatment facilities, and so on.6,11,12,13

To date, DAs have been frequently detected in aquatic and terrestrial environmental matrices, including indoor dust, wastewater, surface water, sediments, sewage sludge, urban soils, and even in biological samples, with residual concentrations reaching up to tens of mg/L or hundreds of mg/kg.10,12,14,15,16 As emerging contaminants that are generally not covered by regulatory frameworks or comprehensive environmental risk assessments (ERAs),17 DAs exhibit pseudo-persistence in the environment due to their unrestricted and continuous discharge.18 Given their intrinsic biocidal properties, even trace concentrations of DAs in the environment have long been a concern for their potential to exacerbate antimicrobial resistance in environmental and symbiotic microbiomes.2,18,19 Over the past few decades, growing experimental evidence has demonstrated the adverse ecotoxicological risks of specific DAs as contaminants to non-target organisms, including mutagenicity, neurotoxicity, endocrine-disrupting effects, metabolic toxicity, and developmental toxicity, based on their physiological and biochemical activities.7,12,20,21 Previous relevant studies have primarily focused on triclosan,20,22 benzalkonium chlorides,12,23,24 and toxic disinfection by-products derived from residual chlorine-based disinfectants.25 However, currently available DAs comprise an extremely diverse range of active chemical agents with different physicochemical, structural, and toxicological properties.26 In particular, hydrophobic pollutants are generally characterized by low degradation rates, high bioaccumulation potential, and elevated toxicity in the environment.27,28,29 Therefore, hydrophobic DAs with higher octanol-water partition coefficient (logKow) values require special attention with respect to their potential environmental risks.30,31 Furthermore, in real-world environmental samples, various DAs typically coexist as mixtures and are highly prone to interact with one another, which may result in additive or synergistic ecotoxicological effects.32 Therefore, despite ongoing experimental research efforts, substantial knowledge gaps persist in the current understanding of the ecological risks posed by DAs in the environment.

As a widely used indicator that employs a single-factor index to predict the ecotoxicological effects of emerging contaminants at specific concentrations, the risk quotient (RQ) is defined as the ratio of the maximum measured environmental concentration (MEC) to the predicted no-effect concentration (PNEC), and serves as a convenient and simple ERA tool.33,34 For pharmaceuticals as emerging contaminants (PECs) in the environment, their intrinsic pharmacological activities motivated Marmon et al.35 to develop a novel network pharmacology (NP)-based ERA framework. This framework integrates mechanistic and mode-of-action information of specific pharmaceuticals by incorporating drug pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic features, with explicit consideration of PEC concentrations in the plasma of exposed fish, rather than in ambient water. Guided by the premise that PEC-mediated adverse effects are caused by the perturbation of a network of drug targets, an initial hazard-based bioactivity network encompassing all drug-target interactions is generated based on pharmacodynamic considerations. Importantly, taking into account the predicted fish steady state plasma concentrations (FSSPCs) inside PEC-exposed organisms, each drug-target interaction node in the hazard-based bioactivity network is then filtered based on pharmacokinetic principles to generate an environmental risk-based network containing only those interactions that occur at concentrations equal to or lower than the FSSPCs. This approach enables the prediction of environmental risks posed to fish by PECs and their mixtures under realistic internal exposure scenarios, thereby outperforming the traditional RQ-based ERA method, which only accounts for the toxicity of individual compounds measured experimentally via simple assays targeting apical endpoints.35 Such an NP-based ERA framework has since been applied to the ERA of non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, antidepressants, and other neuroactive pharmaceuticals.35,36,37 As biologically active substances, environmental DAs share similarities with PECs, as they can interfere with the physiological and biochemical systems of non-target organisms (e.g., fish),7,10,26 thereby posing adverse impacts on aquatic ecosystems. The present study adopted both RQ-based and NP-based assessments to predict the ecological risks of hydrophobic DAs with logKow values greater than 1 as environmental contaminants (Figure 1). Results

Environmental concentrations and predicted fish blood concentrations of selected DAs

A total of 42 hydrophobic DA substances were identified and included in the study, encompassing 2 alcohol compounds, 1 aldehyde, 3 biguanides, 2 diamidines, 19 quaternary ammonium compounds (QACs), 4 phenols, 3 quinolines, 3 halophenols, 1 bisphenol, 1 anilide, and 3 other compounds, as presented in Table 1. Following an extensive literature review, 32 relevant articles that documented the occurrence of 28 out of the 42 selected hydrophobic DAs in surface water samples across 12 countries worldwide (Figure 2) were ultimately included for data extraction. The collected occurrence data are summarized in Table 2. Specifically, 23 of these DAs were detected in wastewater treatment plant (WWTP) effluents, and 26 in surface water matrices. Maximum concentrations of DA residues in freshwater environments ranged from 0.49 ng/L to 82 μg/L. Hazard-based bioactivity network of DA mixtures

Of the included 42 hydrophobic DAs, 9 compounds were excluded from the DA-target interaction analysis due to insufficient bioactivity data in the ChEMBL and Toxcast databases. The comprehensive DA-target interaction network, constructed to predict the potential ecotoxicological profile of the mixture of the remaining 33 DA compounds, revealed that DA mixtures might exert biological hazards through diverse molecular mechanisms (Figure 3). Overall, this hazard-based bioactivity network comprised 300 distinct molecular targets and 1395 interactions associated with the mixture of 33 DAs. Dodecyltrimethylammonium chloride exhibited the highest number of recorded target interactions (n = 137), followed by pentachlorophenol (n = 130). Conversely, guaiacol displayed the fewest interactions (n = 1). Among all molecular targets, 229 were shared by at least two DAs, while the remaining 71 targets were uniquely modulated by individual DA compounds. Notably, estrogen receptors (ESR1 and ESR2), androgen receptor (AR), cytochrome P450 (CYP) 19A1, and progesterone receptor (PGR) were shared by more than 20 DAs, suggesting that endocrine disruption may be a key mechanism underlying the adverse effects induced by DA mixtures. A total of 21 targets were shared by 10 or more DAs, and the majority of these were associated with endocrine regulation and transcriptional processes. Among the 33 DAs, 12 compounds interacted with unique targets not shared by any other DA. Chlorhexidine exhibited the greatest number of unique interactions (n = 12), followed by dodecyltrimethylammonium chloride (n = 9). Risk-based bioactivity network of DA mixtures

Based upon the aforementioned hazard-based network, irrespective of information about the concentration of DAs in the environment, each interaction was further filtered by incorporating the predicted blood concentrations of DA contaminants in fish exposed to their environmentally measured levels in treated WWTP effluents or surface waters. This refinement enabled the construction of a risk-based bioactivity network, tailored to predict the potential ecotoxicity of DAs under realistic aquatic exposure scenarios.

As illustrated in Figure 4, a total of 214 interactions involving 140 targets are potentially modulated in wild fish following exposure to 10 DAs in a freshwater environment. The refined risk-based network revealed that 54 targets are affected by multiple DAs, while the remaining 86 are exclusively targeted by a single DA. To further identify core targets involved in risks posed by DA mixtures, all 54 shared targets in the risk-based network were uploaded to the STRING database with a high-confidence threshold (>0.7) to construct the PPI network. After removing disconnected nodes, the network contained 120 nodes and 592 edges, and 6 core subnetworks involving 48 nodes were identified by the MCODE plug-in (Figure 5). The GO analysis covers three functional dimensions: biological processes (BP), cellular components (CC), and molecular functions (MF).

For the targets excluded after filtering by environment-related concentrations, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis showed that the top 10 significantly enriched pathways were predominantly associated with neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction, calcium signaling pathway, and multiple cancer-related pathways (Figure 6C). This finding indicated that many targets involved in neural signaling and tumorigenesis were effectively filtered out under environmentally relevant concentrations, implying that DAs may exert more selective biological effects at environmental exposure levels rather than broadly interfering with neural or oncogenic pathways.

Risk-based DA mixture-core target interaction network construction

To clarify the risk association characteristics between the 8 screened core targets and DAs, a DA mixture-core target interaction network was constructed, and the environmental risk level of each interaction was quantified. As shown in Figure 7, a total of 21 interactions involving 8 core targets were retained for 7 DA compounds. In terms of the number of interactions, ESR1 stood out as a common target of five distinct DAs, followed by TP53, CYP1A2, and F3, indicating that these targets may be key nodes in the DA-induced risk response. Discussion

As a distinct group of pharmacologically active substances, DAs, together with pharmaceuticals, belong to a prominent category of environmental emerging contaminants termed “pharmaceuticals and personal care products (PPCPs).” Even at trace concentrations, DAs and pharmaceuticals in the environment have been demonstrated to exert analogous ecotoxicological effects on non-target organisms, while also accelerating the spread of antimicrobial resistance.10,68 Compared with PECs that have been long regarded as a typical group of emerging contaminants, DAs in the environment have only recently garnered intensive attention, primarily due to the marked surge in disinfectant usage driven by the COVID-19 pandemic.4 DAs are not targeted in the current water monitoring programs. A critical knowledge gap exists in experimental ecotoxicological data for DAs under environmentally realistic exposure scenarios, where these compounds typically persist at low residual concentrations and often co-occur as complex mixtures. This paucity of data poses a significant scientific challenge for their effective environmental risk management.68 Computer-aided approaches are characterized by high efficiency, accessibility, cost-effectiveness, feasibility, and safety,34,69 thereby offering novel insights and opportunities for the rapid prediction and comprehensive assessment of potential environmental risks posed by DAs. The present study employed RQ-based and NP-based in silico approaches to screen the potential environmental risks of 42 hydrophobic DAs as freshwater contaminants, with a particular focus on their potential adverse effects on fish exposed to DAs and their mixtures under environmentally realistic conditions.

Owing to the limited ecotoxicological data available, previous studies evaluating the RQs of DAs have been constrained. Alvarado-Flores et al.70 assessed the ecological risks of 7 DAs used in freshwater fish farming, including chloramine T, benzalkonium chloride, chlorine dioxide, glutaraldehyde, phenol, sodium hypoclorite, and iodine. Results showed that 4 out of these 7 DAs (benzalkonium chloride, glutaraldehyde, phenol, and iodine) in fish farming posed high ecological risks to aquatic organisms and thus should be prioritized in future regulatory frameworks. In a recent study, Li et al.68 investigated 27 QACs in various types of water samples collected from New York State, USA, and found that the highest calculated RQ values of QACs reached 4960 and 5260 in river and lake waters, respectively. Additionally, Welch et al.71 estimated a maximum RQ value of 7.5 for chlorhexidine, a widely used biguanide DA, as a surface water contaminant, based on predicted environmental concentrations calculated from sales volume data. Other studies have also indicated that triclocarban and triclosan exhibited moderate to high risks in surface water samples.64,72 Given their hydrophobic property, triclocarban, triclosan, phenol, chlorhexidine, and QACs, including benzalkonium chloride, were examined in these previous studies and were also included in the present study. Specifically, this study calculated the RQs of 27 DAs for freshwater ecosystems, with both environmental occurrence data and PNEC values available from the Norman Ecotoxicology Database. Among these 27 DAs, 19 were found to exhibit high ecological risks (RQ > 1), suggesting that the majority of hydrophobic DAs may pose substantial ecological risks to aquatic organisms based on their concentrations in freshwater. In particular, triclosan, triclocarban, chlorhexidine, and two QACs (dimethyldioctadecylammonium bromide and cetylpyridinium chloride) had expected potential for adverse effect (RQ > 100), and were therefore identified as primary toxicants in freshwater. These findings were consistent with previous studies that reported high RQ values for QACs, triclocarban, and triclosan in environmental matrices.64,68,70,72 Notably, this study is the first to highlight expected potential adverse effects of chlorhexidine as a contaminant on non-target aquatic organisms, with an RQ as high as 297.65, which is nearly 40-fold higher than the previously reported finding.71 Whereas, RQ-based analysis in this study indicated residual phenol, thymol, guaiacol, and chloroxylenol in the surface water environment might be safe to ecosystem.

The mechanisms underlying the biocidal activities of DAs are highly complex and divergent, and the bactericidal mechanisms and interacting targets vary significantly among different DAs.26 Moreover, 28 out of the 42 hydrophobic DAs considered in the present study have been detected in surface water environments worldwide, suggesting that the issue of DA mixtures may be of considerable significance. Multiple DA contaminants coexisting in the receiving environment could exhibit unpredictable interactions, thereby aggravating and amplifying their ecological risks. Therefore, it can be speculated that the adverse effects associated with DA mixtures as environmental contaminants on non-target organisms, arising from the unintended interactions between DAs and complex targets, might be extremely diverse. As expected, this study generated a bioactivity network for hazard assessment that revealed mechanistic promiscuity for a mixture of 33 DAs, explicitly demonstrating that DAs can act on a broad spectrum of distinct targets and possess a complex pharmacological profile. After considering the blood concentrations of DAs in fish exposed to environmentally realistic scenarios, the complexity of the mechanistic network was significantly reduced. Fish exposure to high-risk DAs such as triclosan and triclocarban at environmentally relevant concentrations in freshwater was predicted to mediate the modulation of specific targets. Based on the FSSPCs-to-effect value ratios in DA-target interactions, HMGCS2 and EPHX2 emerged as the first two critical targets that might mediate the potential ecological hazards posed by triclosan and triclocarban, respectively, in exposed fish. Murine experimental findings have demonstrated that HMGCS2-mediated ketogenesis impairment played a critical role in promoting renal fibrosis following oral triclosan exposure (100 mg/kg per day for 13 weeks).73 Moreover, triclocarban has been identified as a potent EPHX2 inhibitor, as evidenced by studies in mice administered 5 mg/kg triclocarban via gavage74 and in humans who used soap containing 0.6% triclocarban during showering.75 Consistent with these previous findings, our results provide new insights into the involvement of HMGCS2 and EPHX2 in the ecotoxicological risks of DAs to fish, when DAs exist as freshwater contaminants at environmentally relevant concentrations.

However, unlike the specific group of PECs which specifically act on primary targets,35,36,37 the mechanistic complexity of DAs results in a large number of targets still being found likely to be modulated in wild fish exposed to environmentally relevant concentrations of DAs. Therefore, PPI network analysis and GO/KEGG enrichment analyses, which are commonly used in NP to identify core targets involved in pivotal BP, MF, CC, and signaling pathways,76 were employed in this study to further refine the DA mixture-core target interaction network. Through the integration of PPI network analysis, functional enrichment, and quantification of DA mixture-core target interactions, the refined risk-based analysis identified eight key targets that may contribute to multiple potential ecotoxicological risks of the 7 DA mixtures to exposed fish under environmentally relevant concentrations. These seven DA compounds included four QACs, pentachlorophenol, triclosan, and triclocarban, suggesting that these DAs represent the compounds with the highest risk, particularly when they co-occur as mixtures in the environment. However, whether their interactions exhibit additive or synergistic effects, as well as the specific mechanisms underlying these interactions, require further experimental verification. On the other hand, the refined eight key targets are involved in distinct BPs, including inflammatory response (IL6), tumorigenesis (TP53), estrogen-disrupting effects (ESR1), CYP pathway (CYP1A2, CYP2B6, CYP2C9), and plasminogen activation cascade (F3 and PLAU). A zebrafish-specific target-to-phenotype association analysis, focusing on three analyzable gene targets (ESR1, TP53, and F3) modulated by environmentally relevant concentrations of DAs, Owing to the paucity of relevant fish data, the NP framework employed in the present study mainly relies on human/mammalian bioactivity data (ChEMBL/ToxCast), which is consistent with the approach adopted in previous researches that used NP-based ERA metrics for pharmaceuticals in fish.35,36,37 Although substantial zebrafish-human orthology has been well-documented,77 molecular targets in fish and humans may exhibit differential sensitivity to the same compound. Marmon et al.,35 who developed this novel pharmacology-informed ERA framework, argued that, from a precautionary principle perspective, this discrepancy would only represent a potential concern if the AC50 (or IC50) values of fish targets are significantly lower than those detected in human targets; however, no empirical evidence has been reported to corroborate this conjecture. Regarding the filtered human “Hub Targets” (ESR1, TP53, and F3) in this study that may be modulated by environmentally relevant concentrations of DA mixtures in exposed fish, it has been demonstrated that zebrafish ESR1 shares 47.1% identity with human ESR178; TP53 is highly conserved in zebrafish, particularly in the protein DNA-binding domain, where approximately 90% of the mutations identified in human tumors are localized79; and zebrafish F3 exhibits 31% overall identity with its human ortholog.80 Therefore, despite potential gaps that still exist in the conservation of binding sites and the identity of physiological roles between humans and the diverse aquatic taxa represented in the PNEC data, it can be speculated that exposure of zebrafish to DA mixtures in freshwater environments is associated with adverse phenotypes in behavior, the endocrine system, nervous system, digestive system, cardiovascular system, skeletal system, and eyes. This finding is partially consistent with previous experimental evidence documenting the diverse ecotoxicological risks of specific individual DAs.7,12,20,21 The phenotypic predictions presented herein may provide valuable guidance for the design of tailored in vivo experimental strategies. Previous experimental studies on mammalian models have reported that DAs represented by QACs and triclosan can alter estrogenic signaling and exert endocrine-disrupting effects as xenoestrogens, even at environmentally relevant concentrations.23,81,82 For instance, a 48-h exposure to a mixture of benzyldimethyldodecylammonium chloride, benzyldimethyltetradecylammonium chloride, and benzyldimethylhexadecylammonium chloride at concentrations of 0.5–1.5 mg/L significantly increased the expression of ESR1 in human breast cancer MCF-7 cells.23 Consistent with these findings, the present study revealed that the perturbation of ESR1 appeared to be involved in the ecological risks of at least five hydrophobic DAs. This effect was particularly prominent for cetylpyridinium chloride and benzyldimethylhexadecylammonium chloride, where the predicted fish blood concentrations exceeded the AC50/IC50 values for ESR1 by 40.88- and 7.67-fold, respectively. These results collectively suggest that estrogen-disrupting effects likely serve as a key mechanism underlying the environmental risks of DAs and their mixtures, and thus merit further attention in future ecotoxicological research.

In conclusion, the present study employed RQ-based and NP-based approaches as predictive strategies to screen 42 hydrophobic DA substances with logKow values greater than 1 for their potential ecological risks to non-target freshwater organisms such as fish. Among these 42 selected hydrophobic DAs, our analyses focused on 28 compounds that have been detected in global surface freshwater environments, as documented in the literature published between 2005 and 2025. In the RQ-based analysis, five Das, including triclosan, triclocarban, chlorhexidine, and two QACs (dimethyldioctadecylammonium bromide and cetylpyridinium chloride), were identified as the most significant toxicants to freshwater organisms among the included substances, with RQ values exceeding 100. Subsequently, an NP-based analysis was conducted to integrate pharmacological and mechanistic considerations into the ERA of DAs, based on their intrinsic biological properties. Results showed that the constructed hazard-based bioactivity network, developed without incorporating residual concentration data, comprised 300 potential molecular targets and 1395 DA-target interactions associated with 33 hydrophobic DAs and their mixtures. To refine this network under realistic aquatic exposure scenarios, hazard-based DA-target interactions were filtered by integrating the predicted blood concentrations of DA contaminants in fish exposed to environmentally measured DA concentrations in surface freshwaters. After filtering, 214 interactions (involving 140 targets and 10 DAs) were retained in the refined environmental risk-based network. From a mixture perspective, 54 targets shared by multiple DAs were extracted from the risk-based network and further refined using PPI network analysis and GO/KEGG enrichment analyses, aiming to specifically address the ecological risks posed by DA mixtures in the environment. Results indicated that 8 of these targets might be modulated as hubs by DA mixtures in fish. Additionally, target-to-phenotype analysis revealed that environmentally relevant concentrations of DA mixtures in surface freshwater samples could potentially impact behavior, endocrine function, the nervous system, the cardiovascular system, and developmental processes in zebrafish.

Limitations of the study

This study is the first to comprehensively assess the risks of hydrophobic DAs as contaminants in surface freshwater using RQ-based and NP approaches. Rigorous literature screening, selection of raw measured data, and the use of average detected concentrations in surface freshwater from multiple studies ensured the reliability of the analysis. Nevertheless, some limitations existed in this study. First, due to the limited availability of published occurrence data for the target DAs, global average maximum values across all relevant studies were used for calculation, which might compromise spatial realism. Furthermore, as shown in Table 2, only 6 out of the available literature (18.8%) reported post-2020 environmental concentrations of the target DAs.10,41,42,43,44,45 The scarcity of sufficient post-pandemic monitoring data for subsequent RQ analysis and FSSPC prediction may not capture the increased environmental discharge associated with the extensive use of DAs following the COVID-19 pandemic, thus potentially leading to an underestimation of the current actual ecological risks. Future research should be based on more adequate post-pandemic data; additionally, sensitivity or scenario analyses (e.g., employing scaling factors to simulate elevated environmental loads) would be beneficial for obtaining a comprehensive risk profile in the post-COVID context. Second, a readily available fish plasma model, originally validated for neutral pharmaceuticals and relying primarily on the contaminants’ water concentrations and logKow values, was employed in this study for predicting the FSSPCs.35,36,37 The reliance on such a hydrophobic partitioning framework may introduce uncertainties when applied to QACs included in the study (e.g., cetylpyridinium chloride). As cationic surfactants, QACs exhibit strong ionizable properties, which may result in distinct distribution kinetics and membrane interactions. In addition, QACs interact predominantly with environmental matrices through electrostatic adsorption onto negatively charged mineral and organic surfaces, rather than via simple hydrophobic partitioning. The total organic carbon and sediment adsorption of QACs in the environment may reduce their bioavailability in natural freshwater systems. Consequently, the actual FSSPCs of QACs may be lower than those estimated in this study. Furthermore, the lowest freshwater PNEC value obtained from NORMAN SusDat, representing idealized laboratory toxicity data, was adopted for RQ calculation by comparing it with average maximum environmental detections. This approach might lead to overestimated RQs relative to actual environmental exposure levels. Therefore, our findings may represent a worst-case scenario for the environmental risks posed by DAs, especially ionizable surfactants such as QACs. Future improvements should incorporate sorption coefficients (Kd) and explicitly address the contribution of electrostatic interactions to better characterize the environmental fate of QACs and reduce biases in risk estimation. Third, the current risk assessment only considers parent DAs and neglects their environmental transformation products. Many DAs, especially triclosan and chlorhexidine, undergo rapid photolysis and microbial degradation to form products (e.g., methyl-triclosan and chlorinated phenols), which are often more persistent or possess distinct toxicological profiles. Focusing solely on parent compounds, therefore, underestimates the full environmental hazard footprint of DAs. Future studies should integrate the occurrence, persistence, and toxicity of transformation products to achieve a more comprehensive ecological risk assessment for DAs. In addition, the in silico findings of this study need to be further validated by experimental research.

Method details

Identification of hydrophobic DAs

To identify currently available DAs, chemicals labeled as “disinfectant” or “antiseptics” in the Drugbank database83 was selected and classified according to the categories mentioned in a previous review.2 Hydrophobic DA substances with logKow values greater than 1 (logKow values were predicted using EPI Suite) were included, and their IUPAC names, CAS numbers, and SMILES strings were retrieved from PubChem.84

Literature review and surface water occurrence data extraction

The literature published in English between January 2005 and July 2025 on the occurrence of the included hydrophobic DA in global surface waters was retrieved, with maximum measured concentrations extracted. Bibliographic searches were conducted in PubMed and Google Scholar using “water/aquatic environment” and the selected DA names as search terms. Any article containing the exact maximum concentration values of target DA residues in treated WWTP effluents and surface freshwater was included. The arithmetic mean of the reported maximum concentrations for each included DA was used in the subsequent calculation of RQs and the prediction of blood concentrations in wild fish.

Assessment of the ecological risk of DAs in freshwater via RQ analysis

RQ was calculated as the ratio of MEC to PNEC, thereby reflecting the ecological risks posed by DAs to sensitive aquatic organisms.92 The MEC was calculated as the arithmetic mean of the maximum concentrations compiled for each target DA in the above literature review. To reflect a worst-case exposure scenario, the PNEC for each DA compound was defined as the lowest predicted no effect concentration in freshwater, which was directly retrieved from the open database NORMAN SusDat.85 The risk classification criteria were as following92: RQ values ranging from 0.1 to 1 indicate a medium ecological risk; RQ < 0.1 denotes a low risk; and RQ > 1 signifies a high ecological risk.

Prediction of blood concentrations of DAs in exposed fish based on environmentally measured levels

Using the improved fish plasma model that predicts the FSSPC of contaminants based on their water concentrations and logKow values,93 the arithmetic mean of the reported maximum measured concentrations for each included DA was used for prediction of blood concentrations in wild fish. The FSSPC was determined according to the following formulas:LogPBlood:Water=0.73×logKow−0.88FssPC=[Waterconcentration,μg/L]×PBlood:Water

For example, triclocarban had a logKow of 4.90, and its PBlood:Water was calculated to be approximately 497.737. As shown in Table 2, the arithmetic means were separately computed based on five WWTP effluent and six surface water concentration records for triclocarban. The higher of these two mean values (7760.6 ng/L, as presented in Table 3) was selected as the input water concentration for the model. Consequently, the FSSPC of triclocarban was estimated to be approximately 3862.74.

Construction of hazard-based and environmental risk-based DA-target interaction networks

Using the network-centred approach developed by Marmon et al.,35 that was anchored in the bioactivity mechanisms underlying PEC-mediated adverse effects, two types of DA-target interaction networks were generated. The first was a hazard-based network for DA mixtures, constructed without relying on effect concentration data; the second was a further refined risk-based network that only displays DA-target interactions predicted to occur under the defined exposure scenario.

Specifically, IC50 or AC50 values for the selected 42 DAs were retrieved from the Toxicity Forecaster (ToxCast) and CHEMBL databases,86 to construct hazard-based DA-target interaction networks. Only experimentally verified IC50 or AC50 records for human (homo sapiens) individual proteins with unambiguous activity annotations and explicit numerical values were included. For each target, the lowest reported value was used in the formal analysis. To reduce potential discrepancies arising from variations in assay platforms and data processing protocols across the two databases, the GeneCards database was employed to unify gene symbol nomenclature and ensure consistent target identification throughout datasets.87 Subsequently, the hazard-based DA-target interaction network was visualized and analyzed using Cytoscape software.88 The IC50 or AC50 data used in the extraction of interactions were provided in Supplementary File 1.

To evaluate the environmental risks of DAs under realistic exposure scenarios, all interaction nodes in the hazard-based interaction network were filtered using the previously calculated FSSPC values. Thus, the refined environmental risk-based network contains only interactions that occur at concentrations equal to or below the environmental exposure levels. The ratio of the FssPC to effect concentration was calculated to determine the risk value for each screened interaction.

PPI network construction

To further facilitate the exploration of hub molecular targets potentially involved in the environmental risks of DA mixtures, the targets shared by multiple DAs in the risk-based network were submitted to the STRING database for analyzing known protein interactions and constructing the PPI network,89 limited to homo sapiens and filtered with a high STRING confidence interaction score threshold (>0.7).94 The resulting PPI network was then visualized using Cytoscape, and nodes were clustered through centrality metrics to highlight significant protein interaction relationships. Topological parameters, including degree, betweenness centrality, closeness centrality, and clustering coefficient, were calculated using the NetworkAnalyzer plugin. Additionally, the MCODE plug-in in Cytoscape was employed to extract core subnetworks, and the nodes with top degree in each subnetwork were defined as the hub targets in the PPI network. Parameters of the MCODE plug-in were configured as follows: degree cutoff = 2, node score cutoff = 0.2, and K-core = 2.

Functional enrichment analysis

GO and KEGG enrichment analyses of targets in the core subnetworks were conducted using the DAVID database to identify biological attributes enriched among the overlapping targets affected by DA mixtures, including BP, CC, MF, and signaling pathways.90 To explore the types of targets or interactions most likely to be filtered out by environment-related concentrations, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was also performed for targets excluded by the risk-based network. A false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 was applied as the selection condition to ensure the statistical robustness of the reported p-values and pathway associations. The results of the enrichment analysis were visualized using Weishengxin platform.

Risk-based DA mixture-core target interaction network construction

To concentratedly reflect the effect intensity of DA mixtures on core targets, the interactions related to the core targets shared by multiple DAs in risk-based network were extracted to construct DA mixture-core targets interaction network.

Target-to-phenotype analysis

To explore the phenotypic implications of the extracted core potentially involved in the environmental risks of DA mixtures, the Monarch Initiative platform, an open analytical platform integrating phenotypes, genes, and diseases across species,91 was employed for gene-phenotype anchoring analysis. As zebrafish is a commonly used model organism in environmental toxicology research of aquatic contaminants, it was selected as the target species for database retrieval. All available zebrafish-specific phenotypic data associated with the target genes were identified and analyzed.

Quantification and statistical analysis

No statistical hypothesis tests were performed, and no formal assessments were conducted to verify whether the data met the assumptions of the statistical approach.
