Susceptible-Infected Epidemics on Evolving Graphs at Critical Infection Rate
Wenze Chen, Haojie Hou, Ruibo Ma, Dong Yao

Abstract
. Consider an SI process on a graph G G where each S–I connection becomes I–I at rate λ \lambda . Here S and I stand for “susceptible” and “infected” respectively. The evoSI model is a modification of the SI model in which S–I edges are broken at rate ρ \rho and the “S” connects to a randomly chosen vertex. It is proven in Durrett and Yao [2022, Electron. J. Probab.] that, for the supercritical evoSI process on the configuration model, there exists a quantity Δ \Delta depending on the first three moments of the degree distribution such that the sign of Δ \Delta governs the continuity of the phase transition of the final epidemic size near the critical infection rate λ c \lambda_{c} . In this paper, we consider the critical evoSI model on the configuration model, i.e., λ = λ c \lambda=\lambda_{c} . We show that, if Δ > 0 \Delta>0 , then the probability of a major outbreak starting from a single infected individual is C n − 1 / 3 ( 1 + o ( 1 ) ) Cn^{-1/3}(1+o(1)) for some explicit constant C > 0 C>0 , where n n is the size of the graph. On the contrary, if Δ < 0 \Delta<0 , then this probability is o ( n − 1 / 3 ) o(n^{-1/3}) . The case Δ < 0 \Delta<0 is reminiscent of the critical Erdős-Rényi graphs, where the probability for the size of the largest component to be of order n n decays exponentially in n n .



1.
Introduction

Susceptible-Infected (SI) and Susceptible-Infected-Recovered (SIR) models are valuable tools for understanding processes such as information diffusion and diseases spreading in social networks.
Mathematically, they correspond to
interacting particle systems on graphs
, where the set of vertices represents individuals, and the set of edges reflects the connections between them. In the SI model, each S–I edge transitions to an I–I edge at a rate
\lambda
, i.e., after an independent exponential time with parameter
\lambda
. Once a vertex enters the infected state I, it remains infected indefinitely. In the SIR model, individuals who become infected transition to the recovered state R at a rate of
\gamma
and subsequently remain in the recovered state forever.
Over the past two decades, significant progress has been made in the rigorous analysis of epidemic models on random graphs. See
[
7
]
for an overview.

In recent years, there has been a growing interest in studying epidemic models on
evolving
graphs. See, e.g.,
[
4
,
24
,
16
,
11
,
9
]
and the references therein. One type of evolving mechanism, proposed in
[
19
]
, goes as follows.
In addition to the epidemic dynamics, the graph structure itself is allowed to evolve as follows: S–I edges are broken at a rate
\rho
and the susceptible individuals affected by the broken edges establish new connections with a uniformly randomly selected individual in the graph. The SI and SIR models on such evolving graphs are termed
evoSI
and
evoSIR
, respectively.

Of great interest in the mathematical analysis of epidemics is the
final epidemic size
(denoted by
\Lambda_{n}
if the underlying graph
G_{n}
has size
n
), by which we mean the number of vertices that are eventually infected (in SI epidemics) or recovered (in SIR epidemics). Since the real-world networks are usually huge, we are mostly interested in the large
n
behavior of
\Lambda_{n}
. This leads to the concept of a
major outbreak
, by which we mean
\Lambda_{n}/n>\varepsilon
for some
\varepsilon>0
independent of
n
. The critical infection rate
\lambda_{c}
is then defined as the smallest
\lambda
such that a major outbreak occurs with positive probability,

\lambda_{c}:=\inf\left\{\lambda>0:\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}_{1}(\Lambda_{n}/n>\varepsilon)>0\right\},

where the subscript
1
in
\mathbb{P}_{1}
indicates that initially a uniformly chosen vertex is infected.

An interesting feature of epidemic models on evolving graphs is that they may exhibit discontinuous phase transitions in the fraction of the final epidemic size
\Lambda_{n}
, i.e., as
\lambda\searrow\lambda_{c}
, conditioned on a major outbreak,
\Lambda_{n}/n
does not converge to
0
in probability. For the evoSIR model on Erdős-Rényi random graphs
G(n,\mu/n)
(a random graph where every pair of vertices is connected with probability
\mu/n
), Jiang et al.
[
19
]
showed that its critical value equals
(\gamma+\rho)/(\mu-1)
. Moreover, it was conjectured in
[
19
]
(via simulations) that there exists a discontinuous phase transition in
\Lambda_{n}
when
\mu=5
and
\rho=4
. This conjecture was later proven by Ball and Britton
[
2
]
, which provides a sufficient condition for a discontinuous phase transition for a slightly generalized version of evoSIR on Erdős-Rényi graphs. The condition in
[
2
]
was later shown to be also necessary in the work
[
5
]
by Hou and the first and fourth authors of the present paper.

Durrett and Yao
[
9
]
turned to the case of SI dynamics and analyzed the evoSI model on the Newman–Strogatz–Watts (NSW) version of the configuration model
\mathbb{CM}(n,\mathbf{D}_{n})
(see Section
1.2
for its definition). They showed that there exists an explicit quantity such that the continuity of the phase transition of
\Lambda_{n}
is governed by its sign.

The current paper deals with the evoSI model in the configuration model with the critical infection rate
\lambda=\lambda_{c}
. Our main result Theorem
1.1
below shows that, for the quantity
\Delta
given in (
1.8
), the probability of a major outbreak for the evoSI model on the configuration model is of order
n^{-1/3}
if
\Delta>0
; while the outbreak probability is
o(n^{-1/3})
if
\Delta<0
. The proof of Theorem
1.1
demonstrates that the critical behavior of the evoSI model can be characterized by three phases: a near-critical random walk phase, a diffusion phase, and a deterministic growth phase. We expect the analysis in this paper to be useful for studying other stochastic processes on random graphs, especially when one can couple the process and the graph structure together.

a_{n}=O(b_{n})

1.1.
Statement of the main result

In this paper, we consider the evoSI process in the configuration model. Given a non-negative integer
n
and a sequence of (deterministic) degrees
d_{1,n},\ldots,d_{n,n}
, we attach
d_{1,n},\ldots,d_{n,n}
half-edges to
n
vertices labeled
1,2,\ldots,n
, respectively, before pairing these half-edges uniformly at random. Two paired half-edges form an edge in
G_{n}
. Letting
\mathbf{d}_{n}
denote the degree sequence, we call the generated graph the configuration model on
n
vertices with the prescribed degree sequence
\mathbf{d}_{n}
and denote it by
\mathbb{CM}(n,{\mathbf{d}_{n}})
. This model is also called the Molloy–Reed configuration graph. For more details on the configuration model, we refer the reader to
[
13
, Chapter 7]
.

We will impose the following regularity conditions on
\mathbf{d}_{n}
. Define

p_{k,n}:=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}[d_{i,n}=k],\hskip 10.00002ptk\in\mathbb{N}:=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}.

Our standard Assumption
(H)
is as follows:

(H1)

(Finite exponential moment) There exist two positive constants
\eta_{\ref{exp1}}
and
C_{\ref{exp1}}
such that for all sufficiently large
n
,

(1.1)

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{\eta_{\ref{exp1}}d_{i,n}}\leq C_{\ref{exp1}}.

(H2)

(Convergence of the degree sequence) There exists a probability distribution
\{p^{*}_{k},k\in\mathbb{N}\}
such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}p_{k,n}=p_{k}^{*},\hskip 10.00002pt\forall k\in\mathbb{N}.

(H3)

(Concentration of the degree sequence) There exists a constant
C_{\ref{concern1}}\in(0,2/3)
such that for all sufficiently large
n
,

(1.2)

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}(k+1)^{4}\left|np_{k,n}-np^{*}_{k}\right|\leq n^{C_{\ref{concern1}}}.

Remark 1

(Bounds for the tail and maximum degree)
.

Assumption
(H1)
implies that for sufficiently large
n
,

(1.3)

p_{k,n}\leq C_{\ref{exp1}}\exp(-\eta_{\ref{exp1}}k),\hskip 10.00002pt\forall k\in\mathbb{N}.

Combining this with
(H2)
, we see that

(1.4)

p^{*}_{k}\leq C_{\ref{exp1}}\exp(-\eta_{\ref{exp1}}k),\hskip 10.00002pt\forall k\in\mathbb{N}.

Also, from
(H1)
, the maximal degree

D_{n,\mathrm{max}}:=\max_{1\leq i\leq n}d_{i,n}

must satisfy

(1.5)

D_{n,\mathrm{max}}\leq\frac{\log(nC_{\ref{exp1}})}{\eta_{\ref{exp1}}}.

Remark 2

(On the verification of
(H3)
)
.

Suppose that
(H1)
and
(H2)
hold. Then it follows from (
1.3
) and (
1.4
) that for all sufficiently large
n
,

\displaystyle n\sum_{k>\log n/\eta_{\ref{exp1}}}(k+1)^{4}\left|p_{k,n}-p^{*}_{k}\right|\leq n\sum_{k>\log n/\eta_{\ref{exp1}}}(k+1)^{4}\max\{p_{k,n},p^{*}_{k}\}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{\ref{exp1}}n\sum_{k>\log n/\eta_{\ref{exp1}}}(k+1)^{4}\exp(-\eta_{\ref{exp1}}k)=\frac{C_{\ref{exp1}}}{\eta_{\ref{exp1}}^{4}}O\left(\log^{4}n\right),

where the implied constant in
O(\log^{4}n)
is universal. Therefore, given
(H1)
and
(H2)
, we can find a constant
C_{\ref{equiva_cond}}=C_{\ref{equiva_cond}}(\eta_{\ref{exp1}})>0
such that a sufficient condition for
(H3)
is

(1.6)

\left|np_{k,n}-np^{*}_{k}\right|\leq C_{\ref{equiva_cond}}n^{C_{\ref{concern1}}}/\log^{5}n\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{ for all }0\leq k\leq\log n/\eta_{\ref{exp1}}.

For
r\geq 1
, let
m_{r}:=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}k^{r}p^{*}_{k}
be the
r
-th moment of the distribution
p^{*}_{\cdot}
. Using the same approach as in
[
9
]
, under
(H1)
and
(H2)
, the critical infection rate of the evoSI model on the configuration model
\mathbb{CM}(n,\mathbf{d}_{n})
is given by

(1.7)

\lambda_{c}:=\frac{\rho m_{1}}{m_{2}-2m_{1}}.

Our main result is stated in the following theorem.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\{\mathbf{d}_{n},n\geq 1\}
be degree sequences that satisfy
(H)
. Define

(1.8)

\Delta:=-\frac{m_{3}-3m_{2}+2m_{1}}{m_{1}}+3(m_{2}-2m_{1}).

Consider evoSI on the random graph
\mathbb{CM}(n,\mathbf{d}_{n})
with
\lambda=\lambda_{c}
.

(1)

If
\Delta>0
, then there exist two constants
C_{\ref{eq:del>0case}},\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that for any fixed
0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}
,

(1.9)

\lim_{n\to\infty}n^{1/3}\mathbb{P}_{1}(\Lambda_{n}/n>\varepsilon)=C_{\ref{eq:del>0case}}.

(2)

If
\Delta<0
, then for any
\varepsilon>0
,

(1.10)

\lim_{n\to\infty}n^{1/3}\mathbb{P}_{1}(\Lambda_{n}/n>\varepsilon)=0.

Remark 3
.

The constant
C_{\ref{eq:del>0case}}
is given by (
6.8
). Under
\mathbb{P}_{1}
, the initial infection is assigned to a randomly chosen vertex in the graph
\mathbb{CM}(n,\mathbf{d}_{n})
—and crucially,
\mathbb{P}_{1}
explicitly incorporates two sources of randomness: the inherent randomness of the graph
\mathbb{CM}(n,\mathbf{d}_{n})
itself, and the randomness of the initially selected infected vertex.

1.2.
Results for random graphs with a random degree sequence

We note that
the convergence rate in Theorem
1.1
is actually
uniform
over the degree sequence. In other words, given
\{p_{k}^{*}\}
(with
\Delta>0
),
for any
\delta>0
, there exists
N
(which depends only on
\{p_{k}^{*}\}

C_{\ref{exp1}},C_{\ref{concern1}}

\left|n^{1/3}\mathbb{P}_{1}(\Lambda_{n}/n>\varepsilon)-C_{\ref{eq:del>0case}}\right|\leq\delta.

) apply to them as well.

1.3.3.
SI-
\rho

Ball and Britton
[

]
considered a generalized version of the evoSI model, called

. Then, with probability

and

. In fact,

2
]
showed that
\Lambda_{n}
converges to a solution to some explicit equation. For

(where the model reduces to the evoSI model), the aforementioned condition is
\mu>3/2
, which coincides with
[
9
]
(with
D
distributed as
\text{Poisson}(\mu)
).

If we also allow infected vertices to recover at a rate
\gamma
in evoSI or SI-

model, then we get the so-called

or

]

. Later,

]

is sufficient for a discontinuous

. Chen, Hou and Yao proved in
[

]
that (

We conjecture that, similarly to Theorem

1.4.
Proof of Theorem
1.1
: an outline

In this section, we will explain the main ideas and key steps in the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

1.4.1.
Bounding evoSI by avoSI and AB-avoSI models

Inspired by the approach of
[
9
]
, we bound the number of infected vertices in evoSI between two other models, the avoSI model and the AB-avoSI model. These two models are Markov processes and can be defined by coupling the graph exploration process with the epidemic. For
1\leq i\leq n
, vertex
i
has
d_{i,n}
half-edges and they are all unpaired at time
0
. This resembles the construction of the configuration model. These half-edges, however, remain unpaired until a later random time. We say that a half-edge is infected if it is attached to an infected vertex. In the avoSI model, the following two types of random events may happen.

•

At rate
\lambda
, each infected half-edge
h
pairs with another randomly chosen half-edge. If the vertex
y
connected to the other half-edge is susceptible, then it becomes infected. The two paired half-edges form an edge. Note that if vertex
y
changes from state S to I, then all half-edges connected to
y
become infected half-edges.

•

Each infected half-edge is removed from its vertex at rate
\rho
and immediately attaches to a randomly chosen vertex. Note that the state of the half-edge changes to the state of the newly attached vertex.

As for the AB-avoSI model, we need two indices for each half-edge
h
: the infection index
A(h,t)
and rewiring index
B(h,t)
.

•

The infection index
A(h,t)=0
if
h
has not been infected by time
t
. If
h
first becomes infected at time
s
, we set
A(h,t)=s
for
t\geq s
.

•

The rewiring index
B(h,t)=0
if the half-edge
h
has not been rewired by time
t
. If
h
attaches to a random vertex at time
s
, we update
B(h,t)
to
s

1.4.2.
The three-stage analysis of the avoSI model

We will focus on the avoSI model and give a detailed proof of Theorem
1.2
. The analysis of the AB-avoSI model is similar and will be sketched in Section
7
. Let
X_{t}
be the number of half-edges in the model at time
t
, and let
X_{I,t}
be the number of infected half-edges at time
t
. As in
[
9
]
and
[
18
]
, we apply a time-change by multiplying all transition rates by
(X_{t}-1)/(\lambda X_{I,t})
. This time change simplifies the analysis while keeping the final epidemic size
\widehat{\Lambda}_{n}
intact.

From now on, we focus on the avoSI model after the time-change. We will slightly abuse the notation by
using the same symbols for the avoSI process before and after the time-change. Since the avoSI process stops after there are no infected half-edges, i.e.,
X_{I,t}=0
, it is natural to investigate the evolution of
X_{I,t}
. In particular, consider the time

\gamma_{n}:=\inf\{t\geq 0\mid X_{I,t}=0\}.

Let
I_{t}
and
S_{t}
be the number of infected vertices and susceptible vertices at time
t
. Define
X_{S,t}
and
S_{t,k}
as the number of susceptible half-edges and susceptible vertices with
k
half-edges at time
t
, respectively. Note that
n
, the size of the graph
\mathbb{CM}(n,\mathbf{d}_{n})
, is omitted from these expressions.

We will see in (
3.5
) that the evolution of
X_{I,t}
is given by

(1.17)

\mathrm{d}X_{I,t}=\left(-2(X_{t}-1)+\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}k^{2}S_{t,k}-\frac{\rho}{\lambda}\cdot\frac{X_{t}-1}{n}S_{t}\right)\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{d}M_{I,t},

where
M_{I,t}
is a martingale. Our analysis will show that the term in the large bracket on the right-hand side of (

), called the

, is roughly
m_{1}\Delta n\mathrm{d}t
, while the order of
\mathrm{d}M_{I,t}
is
\sqrt{Cn\mathrm{d}t}
for some constant
C>0
. Therefore, we opt to analyze the model in the following three stages.

(i)

The time
0\leq t\leq qn^{-1/3}
for some small constant
q>0
. During this stage,
the martingale term is dominant on the right-hand side of (
1.17
).

(ii)

The time
qn^{-1/3}\leq t\leq Qn^{-1/3}
for some large constant
Q
. During this stage, the drift term and the martingale term have the same order of influence as
n\to\infty
.

(iii)

The time
Qn^{-1/3}\leq t\leq c
for another small constant
c>0
. During this stage, the drift term is dominant on the right-hand side.

Define

(1.18)

\sigma^{2}:=\frac{\sum_{k\geq 0}k(k-2)^{2}p^{*}_{k}}{(1+\rho/\lambda)m_{1}}+\frac{\rho}{\rho+\lambda_{c}}.

In the first stage, we study the tail probability of
\mathbb{P}_{1}(\gamma_{n}>qn^{-1/3})
, stated as follows.

Theorem 1.4
.

Fix any
0<q<1
. There exist two positive constants
c_{q,Y},c_{q,Z}
such that

(1.19)

c_{q,Z}\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}n^{1/3}\mathbb{P}_{1}(\gamma_{n}>qn^{-1/3})\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}n^{1/3}\mathbb{P}_{1}(\gamma_{n}>qn^{-1/3})\leq c_{q,Y}

and that

\lim_{q\to 0}c_{q,Y}q^{1/2}=\lim_{q\to 0}c_{q,Z}q^{1/2}=\left(\frac{\pi\sigma^{2}}{2m_{1}}\left(1+\frac{\rho}{\lambda_{c}}\right)\right)^{-1/2}.

Let
B_{1}^{+}
be the Brownian meander at time
1
with density function
x\exp(-x^{2}/2)1_{[0,\infty)}(x)
.
Define

(1.20)

N_{q}:=\left(1+\frac{\rho}{\lambda}\right)m_{1}qn^{2/3}.

Let
\xrightarrow{\mathcal{D}}
denote ‘convergence in distribution’.
Theorem
1.4
leads to the following corollary.

Corollary 1.1
.

There exist two random variables
\chi_{q}
and
\zeta_{q}
such that
\chi_{q},\zeta_{q}\xrightarrow{\mathcal{D}}B^{+}_{1}
as
q\to 0
, and that for any bounded, continuous and monotonically increasing function

(1.21)

\begin{split}\frac{c_{q,Z}}{c_{q,Y}}\mathbb{E}(f(\zeta_{q}))&\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{1}\left(f\left(\frac{X_{I,qn^{-1/3}}}{\sigma\sqrt{N_{q}}}\right)|\gamma_{n}>qn^{-1/3}\right)\\
&\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{1}\left(f\left(\frac{X_{I,qn^{-1/3}}}{\sigma\sqrt{N_{q}}}\right)|\gamma_{n}>qn^{-1/3}\right)\leq\frac{c_{q,Y}}{c_{q,Z}}\mathbb{E}(f(\chi_{q})).\end{split}

The appearance of the Brownian meander should not be surprising, since it is the limit of a zero-mean random walk conditioned to stay positive over a large time interval (see, e.g.,
[

]
and
[

]

For the second stage, we show that the behavior of

can be approximated by a diffusion process.
Define a function

where

and

Note that if

Theorem 1.5
.

For any
0<q<1
,
we have

\displaystyle\frac{c_{q,Z}}{c_{q,Y}}\mathbb{E}(F_{1}(\zeta_{q},q))

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{Q\to\infty}\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}_{1}\left(X_{I,Qn^{-1/3}}\geq m_{1}\Delta Q^{2}n^{1/3}/4\middle|\gamma_{n}>qn^{-1/3}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{Q\to\infty}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}_{1}(X_{I,Qn^{-1/3}}\geq m_{1}\Delta Q^{2}n^{1/3}/4|\gamma_{n}>qn^{-1/3})

In particular, for the case

\lim_{Q\to\infty}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}_{1}(X_{I,Qn^{-1/3}}\geq m_{1}\Delta Q^{2}n^{1/3}/4\mid\gamma_{n}>qn^{-1/3})=0.

Consider the case

1.2

Remark 4
.

As we discussed in Section

, we may compare the result in
[

]
regarding critical Erdős-Rényi graphs with ours on the critical evoSI model. The breadth-first walk process there is replaced by

2.1.
The number of jumps in
[0,qn^{-1/3}]

Recall that we are interested in the behavior of
X_{I,t}
. Its value may change when the two types of random events (rewiring and infection) defined in the avoSI model occur. We say that we witness a “jump” each time a random event occurs. In this section, we bound the number of jumps over the time interval
[0,qn^{-1/3}]
.
The total jump rate is

\left(\lambda X_{I,t}+\rho X_{I,t}\right)\frac{X_{t}-1}{\lambda X_{I,t}}=\left(1+\frac{\rho}{\lambda}\right)(X_{t}-1),

where the factor
(X_{t}-1)/(\lambda X_{I,t})
is due to the time-change in the analysis of avoSI model.
Therefore, when
t
is small, the jump rate is approximately
(1+\rho/\lambda)m_{1}n
and the number of jumps in
[0,qn^{-1/3}]
is around
(1+\rho/\lambda)m_{1}qn^{2/3}=N_{q}
(defined in (
1.20
)) by the law of large numbers. Define two events

(2.1)

\begin{split}\widehat{\Omega}_{\ref{jctl}}:=\{\text{The number of jumps in }[0,qn^{-1/3}]\text{ is in }[N_{q}-n^{0.6},N_{q}+n^{0.6}]\},\\
\Omega_{\ref{jctl}}:=\{\text{The number of jumps in }[0,qn^{-1/3}]\text{ is less than or equal to }N_{q}+n^{0.6}\}.\end{split}

The following lemma shows the concentration of the number of jumps.

Lemma 2.1
.

There exist constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
such that for large
n
,

\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{\ref{jctl}}^{c})+\mathbb{P}(\{\gamma_{n}\geq qn^{-1/3}\}\cap\widehat{\Omega}_{\ref{jctl}}^{c})\leq C_{1}\exp(-C_{2}n^{8/15}).

Proof.

Note that after each jump, the total number of unpaired half-edges
X_{t}
either remains unchanged or decreases by
2
. By the degree sequence concentration assumption
(H3)
, it follows that initially the number of unpaired half-edges
X_{0}=\sum_{k\geq 0}kp_{k,n}\in[m_{1}n-n^{C_{\ref{concern1}}},m_{1}n+n^{C_{\ref{concern1}}}]
.

Let
\delta_{i}
be the time lapse between the
(i-1)
-th and the
i
-th jump (with the
0
-th jump set at time
0
, which does not count). Moreover,
\delta_{i}
is set as
\infty
if there are no infected half-edges left after
(i-1)
-th jump.
For all
1\leq i\leq N_{q}+n^{0.6}+1
,
the number of half-edges at time
t_{i}
would fall in the interval

(2.2)

[X_{0}-2i,X_{0}]\subset[m_{1}n-C_{\ref{hatx-1}}n^{2/3},m_{1}n+n^{C_{\ref{concern1}}}],

where
C_{\ref{hatx-1}}
is some positive constant. Based on this observation, we construct two i.i.d. sequences
\{y_{1},y_{2},\ldots\}
and
\{z_{1},z_{2},\ldots\}
, where
y_{i}\sim\textnormal{Exp}((1+\rho/\lambda)(m_{1}n+n^{C_{\ref{concern1}}}))
and
z_{i}\sim\textnormal{Exp}((1+\rho/\lambda)(m_{1}n-C_{\ref{hatx-1}}n^{2/3}))
.
Then we can make a coupling such that
y_{i}\leq\delta_{i}\leq z_{i}
for
i\leq N_{q}+n^{0.6}+1
(the second inequality also requires the presence of infected edges).

We now use this coupling to bound the probabilities that the number of jumps is too high (by the comparison with
y_{i}
) or too low
(by the comparison with
z_{i}
).
Let
\omega_{i}\sim\textnormal{Exp}((1+\rho/\lambda)m_{1})
be i.i.d. for all
1\leq i\leq N_{q}+n^{0.6}+1
, which are standardized versions of
y_{i}
and
z_{i}
. By Bernstein’s inequality (Lemma
A.1
), there exist constants
C_{\ref{y1++}\textnormal{a}}
,
C_{\ref{y1++}\textnormal{b}}>0
such
that the probability of having a high number of jumps satisfies

(2.3)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{\ref{jctl}}^{c})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\bigg(\sum_{1\leq i\leq N_{q}+n^{0.6}+1}y_{i}\leq qn^{-1/3}\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\bigg(\Big|\sum_{1\leq i\leq N_{q}+n^{0.6}+1}\Big(\omega_{i}-\frac{\lambda}{m_{1}(\lambda+\rho)}\Big)\Big|>C_{\ref{y1++}\textnormal{a}}n^{0.6}\bigg)\leq 2\exp(-C_{\ref{y1++}\textnormal{b}}n^{8/15}).

By the same argument, there exist some constants
C_{\ref{ctlZ}\textnormal{a}},C_{\ref{ctlZ}\textnormal{b}}>0
such that

(2.4)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\{\gamma_{n}>qn^{-1/3}\}\cap\widehat{\Omega}_{\ref{jctl}}^{c})\leq\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{1\leq i\leq N_{q}-n^{0.6}+1}z_{i}>qn^{-1/3}\Big)+\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{\ref{jctl}}^{c})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\bigg(\Big|\sum_{1\leq i\leq N_{q}-n^{0.6}+1}\Big(\omega_{i}-\frac{\lambda}{m_{1}(\lambda+\rho)}\Big)\Big|>C_{\ref{ctlZ}\textnormal{a}}n^{0.6}\bigg)+\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{\ref{jctl}}^{c})

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp(-C_{\ref{ctlZ}\textnormal{b}}n^{8/15})+2\exp(-C_{\ref{y1++}\textnormal{b}}n^{8/15}),

which, together with (
2.3
), completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Let
\mathfrak{t}(\ell)

Recall that

I_{t}
and

are the numbers of infected vertices and susceptible vertices at time

\ell\geq 1

(1)

For all

(k-2)

\lambda X_{I,t}\cdot\frac{kS_{t,k}}{X_{t}-1}\cdot\frac{X_{t}-1}{\lambda X_{I,t}}=kS_{t,k}.

(Z_{\ell})
,
(U_{\ell})
, and
(Y_{\ell})
on a common probability space such that on
\Omega_{\ref{u1xit}}
,

(2.7)

Z_{\ell}\leq U_{\ell}\leq Y_{\ell}

holds for all
\ell
up to time
qn^{-1/3}
. Recall that the goal of this section—Theorem
1.4
—involves the processes
(Y_{\ell})
and
(Z_{\ell})
. The following lemma characterizes the expected values of their respective increments.

Lemma 2.3
.

There exist two positive constants
C_{\ref{limy}},C_{\ref{limz}}
(independent of
q
) such that

(2.8)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}n^{1/3}\mathbb{E}[Y_{\ell}-Y_{\ell-1}]=C_{\ref{limy}}q,

(2.9)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}n^{1/3}\mathbb{E}[Z_{\ell}-Z_{\ell-1}]=-C_{\ref{limz}}q.

Moreover, we have

(2.10)

\limsup_{n\to\infty}n^{1/3}\left|\mathbb{E}[(Y_{\ell}-Y_{\ell-1})^{2}]-\sigma^{2}\right|+\limsup_{n\to\infty}n^{1/3}\left|\mathbb{E}[(Z_{\ell}-Z_{\ell-1})^{2}]-\sigma^{2}\right|<\infty,

where
\sigma^{2}
is defined in (
1.18
).

The proof of Lemma
2.3
is available in Section
A.3
. Define

J_{Y}:=\{Y_{\ell}>0\text{ for all }0\leq\ell\leq N_{q}-n^{0.6}\},\hskip 10.00002ptJ_{Z}:=\{Z_{\ell}>0\text{ for all }0\leq\ell\leq N_{q}+n^{0.6}\}.

According to (
2.7
), on
\Omega_{\ref{u1xit}}
it holds that

(2.11)

\mathbbm{1}[J_{Z}]\leq\mathbbm{1}[\gamma_{n}>qn^{-1/3}]\leq\mathbbm{1}[J_{Y}].

Since
\Omega_{\ref{u1xit}}
holds with probability at least
(1-n^{-0.4})
, we deduce that

(2.12)

\mathbb{P}(J_{Z})-n^{-0.4}\leq\mathbb{P}(\gamma_{n}>qn^{-1/3})\leq\mathbb{P}(J_{Y})+n^{-0.4}.

2.3.
Analysis for the upper bound
(Y_{\ell})

In Sections
2.3
and
2.4
, we study the stochastic processes
(Y_{\ell})
and
(Z_{\ell})
, respectively. Recall the definition of
\widehat{\Omega}_{\ref{jctl}}
. On this event, the number of jumps in the interval
[0,qn^{-1/3}]
lies between
\lfloor N_{q}-n^{0.6}\rfloor
and
\lfloor N_{q}+n^{0.6}\rfloor
. Here we introduce a process
(\overline{Y}(t))_{0\leq t\leq 1}
. For integers
0\leq k\leq N_{q}-n^{0.6}
, define

(2.13)

\overline{Y}\left(\frac{k}{N_{q}-n^{0.6}}\right):=\frac{Y_{k}}{\sigma\sqrt{N_{q}-n^{0.6}}}=\frac{Y_{k}-\mathbb{E}(Y_{k})}{\sigma\sqrt{N_{q}-n^{0.6}}}+\frac{\mathbb{E}(Y_{k})}{\sigma\sqrt{N_{q}-n^{0.6}}}.

Furthermore, we linearly interpolate the value of
\overline{Y}(t)
between the points in (
2.13
). For
\frac{\lfloor N_{q}-n^{0.6}\rfloor}{N_{q}-n^{0.6}}\leq t\leq 1
, we set
\overline{Y}(t):=\overline{Y}\left(\frac{\lfloor N_{q}-n^{0.6}\rfloor}{N_{q}-n^{0.6}}\right)
, where
\lfloor x\rfloor
denotes the floor function (integer part of
x
). By Lemma
2.3
,

\mathbb{E}(Y_{k})=\mathbb{E}(Y_{0})+kC_{\ref{limy}}qn^{-1/3}+o(n^{-1/3})\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ as }n\to\infty.

Therefore, by Donsker’s Theorem,
(\overline{Y}(t))
converges in distribution to

(2.14)

B_{t,q}:=B_{t}+C_{\ref{cqdef}}(q)t,

where
(B_{t})
is a standard Brownian motion, and
C_{\ref{cqdef}}(q):={C_{\ref{limy}}}{\sigma^{-1}}\sqrt{(1+\rho/\lambda)m_{1}}q^{3/2}
.
Moreover, we have the following lemma, whose proof is postponed to Section
A.4
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Conditioned on the event

\{Y_{\ell}>0\text{ for all }0\leq\ell\leq N_{q}-n^{0.6}\}=\{\overline{Y}(t)>0\text{ for all }0\leq t\leq 1\},

we have the convergence in distribution,

(2.15)

\overline{Y}(1)=\frac{Y_{[N_{q}-n^{0.6}]}}{\sigma\sqrt{N_{q}-n^{0.6}}}\xrightarrow{\mathcal{D}}\chi_{q}:=B_{1+T_{q},q}-B_{T_{q},q}=B_{1+T_{q}}-B_{T_{q}}+C_{\ref{cqdef}}(q),

where
T_{q}:=\inf\{t\geq 0:B_{u,q}\geq B_{t,q}
for all
t\leq u\leq t+1\}
, and
T_{q}<\infty
almost surely.

Notice that when
Y_{0}=1
, Lemma
2.4
follows from the same argument as in
[
3
]
, since (3.1) of
[
3
]
holds for i.i.d. sequences, and the arguments of Theorem 3.2 in
[
3
]
rely only on Donsker’s Theorem. Actually a stronger conclusion hold true: the conditional distribution of the procress
(\overline{Y}(t))_{0\leq t\leq 1}
converges to

B_{t+T_{q},q}-B_{T_{q},q}=B_{t+T_{q}}-B_{T_{q}}+C_{\ref{cqdef}}(q)t.

However, since our initial value
Y_{0}=O(\log n)
depends on
n
, we need to be careful for the weak convergence—that is precisely what Lemma
2.4
establishes.

The following lemma provides a bound for the tail probability of
T_{q}
.

Lemma 2.5
.

There exist two constants
c_{\ref{tailtq}},C_{\ref{tailtq}}>0
such that for all
r>1
,

(2.16)

\sup_{q\in(0,1)}\mathbb{P}(T_{q}>r)\leq C_{\ref{tailtq}}e^{-c_{\ref{tailtq}}r}.

Proof.

For any
N>0
, define a new measure
\mathbb{P}_{q,N}
by

(2.17)

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{q,N}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}}:=\exp\{-C_{\ref{cqdef}}(q)B_{N+1}-C_{\ref{cqdef}}(q)^{2}(N+1)/2\}.

By Girsanov’s Theorem, the process
(B_{t,q})_{0\leq t\leq N+1}
is a standard Brownian motion up to time
N+1
under
\mathbb{P}_{q,N}
, which implies that the distribution of
T_{q}\wedge N
(as defined in Lemma
2.4
) under
\mathbb{P}_{q,N}
is independent of
q
. Therefore,
a:=\mathbb{P}_{q,1}(T_{q}<1)
is a constant independent of
q
. Note that
\{T_{q}<1\}
is measurable with respect to the
\sigma
-field generated by
(B_{t,q})_{0\leq t\leq 2}
. By Lemma 2.2 of
[
3
]
, we have
a>0
. Applying Hölder’s inequality,

(2.18)

0<a=\mathbb{P}_{q,1}(T_{q}<1)=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{q,1}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}}1_{\{T_{q}<1\}}\right]\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{P}(T_{q}<1)}\cdot\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{q,1}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}}\right)^{2}}.

Since
B_{2}
follows a normal distribution with mean
0
and variance
2
, substituting (
2.17
) into the expectation yields,

(2.19)

\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{q,1}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}}\right)^{2}=\mathbb{E}[\exp(-2C_{\ref{cqdef}}(q)B_{2}-2C_{\ref{cqdef}}(q)^{2})]=\exp(2C_{\ref{cqdef}}(q)^{2}).

Combining (
2.18
), (
2.19
) and the definition of
C_{\ref{cqdef}}(q)
, for all
0<q<1
,

(2.20)

\mathbb{P}(T_{q}<1)\geq a^{2}\exp(-2C_{\ref{cqdef}}(q)^{2})\geq a^{2}\exp(-2C_{\ref{cqdef}}(1)^{2})=:C_{\ref{ptq<1}}.

As
(B_{t,q})_{0\leq t\leq 2}
has stationary and independent increments under
\mathbb{P}
, for
r>2
,

\mathbb{P}(T_{q}>r)=\mathbb{P}\bigg(\inf_{t\leq u\leq t+1}B_{u,q}<B_{t,q}\text{ for all }0\leq t\leq r\bigg)\leq\mathbb{P}(T_{q}\geq 1)^{\lfloor r/2\rfloor}\leq(1-C_{\ref{ptq<1}})^{\lfloor r/2\rfloor}.

The desired result follows immediately.
∎

As established in Lemma
2.4
, the distribution of
\chi_{q}
is of great interest, and its convergence as
q\to 0
is described in the following lemma.

Lemma 2.6
.

Given any continuous function
g
with at most polynomial rate of growth at
\infty
, we have

\lim_{q\to 0}\mathbb{E}[g(\chi_{q})]=\mathbb{E}[g(B^{+}_{1})],

where
B^{+}_{1}
is the Brownian meander at time
1
.

Proof.

The proof contains two steps.

Step 1.
We first show that
\chi_{q}\xrightarrow{\mathcal{D}}B_{1}^{+}
as
q\to 0
. Let

T_{0}:=\inf\{t\geq 0:B_{u}\geq B_{t}\text{ for all }t<u<t+1\}\textnormal{.}

Then,
B_{1}^{+}
is equal in law to
(B_{1+T_{0}}-B_{T_{0}})
. Let
f
be a bounded and continuous function, and fix a constant
N>0
. We decompose
\mathbb{E}[f(B^{+}_{1})]
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[f(B^{+}_{1})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[f(B_{1+T_{0}}-B_{T_{0}})]

(2.21)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[f(B_{1+T_{0}}-B_{T_{0}})\mathbbm{1}[T_{0}\leq N]]+\mathbb{E}[f(B_{1}^{+})\mathbbm{1}[T_{0}>N]]\textnormal{.}

Similarly,

(2.22)

\mathbb{E}[f(\chi_{q})]=\mathbb{E}[f(\chi_{q})\mathbbm{1}[T_{q}\leq N]]+\mathbb{E}[f(\chi_{q})\mathbbm{1}[T_{q}>N]]\textnormal{.}

Since
T_{0}<\infty
a.s., and
f
is bounded, it follows from Lemma
2.5
that

(2.23)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[f(B_{1}^{+})\mathbbm{1}[T_{0}>N]]=\lim_{N\to\infty}\sup_{0<q<1}\mathbb{E}[f(\chi_{q})\mathbbm{1}[T_{q}>N]]=0\textnormal{.}

By the definition of
\mathbb{P}_{q,N}
(cf
.
(
2.17
)), we obtain

(2.24)

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}[f(\chi_{q})\mathbbm{1}[T_{q}\leq N]]\\
=&\mathbb{E}_{q,N}[f(B_{1+T_{q},q}-B_{T_{q},q})\mathbbm{1}[T_{q}\leq N]\exp(C_{\ref{cqdef}}(q)B_{N+1}+C_{\ref{cqdef}}(q)^{2}(N+1)/2)]\\
=&\mathbb{E}_{q,N}[f(B_{1+T_{q},q}-B_{T_{q},q})\mathbbm{1}[T_{q}\leq N]\exp(C_{\ref{cqdef}}(q)B_{N+1,q}-C_{\ref{cqdef}}(q)^{2}(N+1)/2)]\\
=&\exp({-C_{\ref{cqdef}}(q)^{2}(N+1)/2})\mathbb{E}[f(B_{T_{0}+1}-B_{T_{0}})\mathbbm{1}[T_{0}\leq N]\exp[C_{\ref{cqdef}}(q)B_{N+1}]]\textnormal{,}\end{split}

where
\mathbb{E}_{q,N}
denotes the expectation with respect to the probability measure
\mathbb{P}_{q,N}
.
In order to take the
q\to 0
limit in (
2.24
), we note that

\left|f(B_{1+T_{0}}-B_{T_{0}})\mathbbm{1}[T_{0}\leq N]\exp\bigg[C_{\ref{cqdef}}(q)B_{N+1}\bigg]\right|\leq\left(\sup_{x\geq 0}|f(x)|\right)\exp\bigg[C_{\ref{cqdef}}(1)|B_{N+1}|\bigg]\textnormal{.}

By the Dominated Convergence Theorem and the fact
C_{\ref{cqdef}}(q)\to 0
as
q\to 0
, we get

(2.25)

\mathbb{E}\bigg[f(\chi_{q})\mathbbm{1}[T_{q}\leq N]\bigg]\to\mathbb{E}\bigg[f(B_{T_{0}+1}-B_{T_{0}})\mathbbm{1}[T_{0}\leq N]\bigg],\hskip 10.00002pt\text{as }q\to 0\textnormal{.}

Combining (
2.21
), (
2.22
), (
2.23
), and (
2.25
), we get

(2.26)

\mathbb{E}[f(\chi_{q})]\to\mathbb{E}[f(B^{+}_{1})],\hskip 10.00002pt\text{as }q\to 0\textnormal{,}

which implies that
\chi_{q}\xrightarrow{\mathcal{D}}B_{1}^{+}
as
q\to 0
, as desired.

Step 2.
We next show that for any function
g
satisfying the assumption of Lemma
2.6
,
g(\chi_{q})
is uniformly integrable in
q\in(0,1)
. By the polynomial growth condition on
g
, for some
C,r>0

g(x)^{2}\leq C(|x|^{r}+1),\hskip 10.00002pt\forall x\in\mathbb{R}.

Without loss of generality, we can assume that
r\geq 1
. Using the inequality
(a+b)^{r}\leq 2^{r-1}(a^{r}+b^{r})
for
a,b\geq 0
, we see that

\displaystyle\sup_{0<q<1}\mathbb{E}[g(\chi_{q})^{2}]\leq

\displaystyle C\sup_{0<q<1}\mathbb{E}[(|\chi_{q}|^{r}+1)]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C+C\sup_{0<q<1}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(2\sup_{0\leq s\leq 1+T_{q}}|B_{s}|+C_{\ref{cqdef}}(1)\right)^{r}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C^{\prime}+C^{\prime}\sup_{0<q<1}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq s\leq 1+T_{q}}|B_{s}|^{r}\right],

where
C^{\prime}
is another (large) constant. Therefore, to prove
\sup_{0<q<1}\mathbb{E}[g(\chi_{q})^{2}]<\infty
, it suffices to control

\sup_{0<q<1}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq s\leq 1+T_{q}}|B_{s}|^{r}\right],

which can be bounded by

\displaystyle\sup_{0<q<1}\left[\sum_{N=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sup_{0\leq s\leq N+1}|B_{s}|^{r}\right)\mathbbm{1}[T_{q}\in[N-1,N)]\right]\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{N=1}^{\infty}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq s\leq N+1}|B_{s}|^{2r}\right]\right)^{1/2}\sup_{0<q<1}\left(\mathbb{P}(T_{q}>N-1)\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C^{\prime\prime}\sum_{N=1}^{\infty}(N+1)^{r/2}\exp(-c_{\ref{tailtq}}(N-1)/2)<\infty.

Therefore,
\{g(\chi_{q}),q\in(0,1)\}
is uniformly integrable, which together with
Step 1
completes the proof of Lemma
2.6
.
∎

Recall that

J_{Y}=\{Y_{\ell}>0\textnormal{ for all }0\leq\ell\leq N_{q}-n^{0.6}\}\textnormal{.}

We now study the asymptotics of
\mathbb{P}(J_{Y})
.

Proposition 2.1
.

The limit

(2.27)

c_{q,Y}=\lim_{n\to\infty}n^{1/3}\mathbb{P}(J_{Y})

exists, and it satisfies

(2.28)

\lim_{q\to 0}q^{1/2}c_{q,Y}=\sigma^{-1}\sqrt{\frac{2m_{1}}{\pi\left(1+\frac{\rho}{\lambda}\right)}}\textnormal{.}

Proof.

We first prove the existence of the limit of
q^{1/2}c_{q,Y}
.
Let
\phi_{n}(\theta)
be the moment generating function of
-n^{-1/3}(Y_{\ell}-Y_{\ell-1})
, i.e.,

\phi_{n}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}[\exp(-\theta n^{-1/3}(Y_{\ell}-Y_{\ell-1}))].

Combining Lemma
2.3
with the fact
\left|Y_{\ell}-Y_{\ell-1}\right|=O(\log n)
, for any
\left|\theta\right|=O(1)
, we have

(2.29)

\phi_{n}(\theta)=1-\theta C_{\ref{limy}}\cdot qn^{-2/3}+\frac{\theta^{2}\cdot n^{-2/3}}{2}\sigma^{2}+o(n^{-2/3})\textnormal{.}

In particular, (
2.29
) implies the existence of two constants
0<\theta_{*}<\theta^{*}
, such that

(2.30)

\phi_{n}(\theta_{*})<0,\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\phi_{n}(\theta^{*})>0,\hskip 10.00002pt

for all large
n
. Combining (
2.30
) with the convexity of
\phi_{m}(\theta)
(in
\theta
), we deduce that there exists a unique positive solution
\theta_{n}
to the following equation

\mathbb{E}[\exp(-\theta n^{-1/3}(Y_{\ell}-Y_{\ell-1}))]=1\textnormal{.}

By (
2.29
), as
n\to\infty
, we have

(2.31)

\theta_{n}=2C_{\ref{limy}}q/\sigma^{2}+o(1)\textnormal{.}

By the definition of
\theta_{n}
, the process
\{\exp(-\theta_{n}n^{-1/3}Y_{\ell})\}_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}
is a martingale. Define

\Gamma:=\inf\{\ell\geq 0:Y_{\ell}=0\}\wedge\lfloor N_{q}-n^{0.6}\rfloor\textnormal{.}

Then by the Optional Stopping Theorem,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\exp(-\theta_{n}n^{-1/3}Y_{0})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\exp(-\theta_{n}n^{-1/3}Y_{\Gamma})]

\displaystyle=1-\mathbb{P}(J_{Y})+\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(-\theta_{n}n^{-1/3}Y_{\lfloor N_{q}-n^{0.6}\rfloor})\middle|J_{Y}\right]\mathbb{P}(J_{Y})\textnormal{.}

Solving the above equation yields

(2.32)

\mathbb{P}(J_{Y})=\frac{\mathbb{E}(1-\exp(-\theta_{n}n^{-1/3}Y_{0}))}{1-\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(-\theta_{n}n^{-1/3}Y_{[N_{q}-n^{0.6}]})\middle|J_{Y}\right]}\textnormal{.}

Note that
Y_{0}=d_{u,n}\leq\log(nC_{\ref{exp1}})/\eta_{\ref{exp1}}
by (
1.5
). Combining
(H3)
and (
2.31
), we see that

(2.33)

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left[1-\exp\left(-\theta_{n}n^{-1/3}Y_{0}\right)\right]=&\theta_{n}n^{-1/3}\mathbb{E}[Y_{0}]+O(n^{-2/3}\log^{2}n)\\
=&\theta_{n}n^{-1/3}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}kp_{k}^{*}+o(n^{-1/3})\\
=&2C_{\ref{limy}}qm_{1}n^{-1/3}/\sigma^{2}+o(n^{-1/3})\textnormal{.}\end{split}

By Lemma
2.4
, as
n\to\infty
,

(2.34)

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(-\theta_{n}n^{-1/3}Y_{[N_{q}-n^{0.6}]})\middle|J_{Y}\right]\to\mathbb{E}[\exp(-2C_{\ref{cqdef}}(q)\chi_{q})]\textnormal{.}

Combining (
2.32
), (
2.33
) and (
2.34
), we conclude that

(2.35)

\lim_{n\to\infty}n^{1/3}\mathbb{P}(J_{Y})=\frac{2C_{\ref{limy}}qm_{1}/\sigma^{2}}{1-\mathbb{E}[\exp(-2C_{\ref{cqdef}}(q)\chi_{q})]}=:c_{q,Y}\textnormal{,}

which implies (
2.27
).

Next, we prove (
2.28
). Recall that
B^{+}_{1}
(the Brownian meander at time
1
) has the density function
x\exp(-x^{2}/2)\mathbbm{1}[(0,+\infty)]
. See, e.g.,
[
8
, equation (1.1)]
. Thus
\mathbb{E}(B_{1}^{+})=\sqrt{\pi/2}
and
\mathbb{E}[(B^{+}_{1})^{2}]<\infty
.
By Lemma
2.6
(with the test function
g(x)
taken to be
x
or
x^{2}
),

(2.36)

\lim_{q\to 0}\mathbb{E}[\chi_{q}]=\mathbb{E}[B_{1}^{+}]=\sqrt{\pi/2},\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\lim_{q\to 0}\mathbb{E}[\chi_{q}^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[(B_{1}^{+})^{2}]<\infty\textnormal{.}

Combining the definition of
C_{\ref{cqdef}}(q)
and (
2.36
) and the elementary inequality

\left|\exp(-x)-(1-x)\right|\leq x^{2},\hskip 10.00002pt\forall\,x\geq 0,

we have that, as
q\to 0
,

\displaystyle 1-\mathbb{E}[\exp(-2C_{\ref{cqdef}}(q)\chi_{q})]

\displaystyle=2C_{\ref{cqdef}}(q)\mathbb{E}[\chi_{q}]+O(q^{3})

(2.37)

\displaystyle=C_{\ref{limy}}\sigma^{-1}q^{3/2}\sqrt{2\pi\left(1+\frac{\rho}{\lambda}\right)m_{1}}+O(q^{3})\textnormal{,}

proving (
2.28
) by the definition of
c_{q,Y}
in (
2.35
).
∎

2.4.
Analysis for the lower bound
(Z_{\ell})

Similar to the previous section on the upper bound
(Y_{\ell})
and
(\overline{Y}(t))
, we introduce a stochastic process
(\overline{Z}(t))_{0\leq t\leq 1}
as follows. For integers
0\leq k\leq N_{q}+n^{0.6}
, let

(2.38)

\overline{Z}\left(\frac{k}{N_{q}+n^{0.6}}\right):=\frac{Z_{k}}{\sigma\sqrt{N_{q}+n^{0.6}}}=\frac{Z_{k}-\mathbb{E}Z_{k}}{\sigma\sqrt{N_{q}+n^{0.6}}}+\frac{\mathbb{E}Z_{k}}{\sigma\sqrt{N_{q}+n^{0.6}}}\textnormal{,}

and the value of
\overline{Z}(t)
between the points in (
2.38
) is linearly interpolated.

Proposition 2.2
.

There exists a constant
c_{q,Z}>0
such that

c_{q,Z}\leq\liminf_{n\to+\infty}n^{1/3}\mathbb{P}(J_{Z})\leq\limsup_{n\to+\infty}n^{1/3}\mathbb{P}(J_{Z})\leq c_{q,Y}

and that

(2.39)

\lim_{q\to 0}q^{1/2}c_{q,Z}=\lim_{q\to 0}q^{1/2}c_{q,Y}\textnormal{.}

Proof.

By (
2.12
) and (
2.27
), we must have

(2.40)

\liminf_{n\to+\infty}n^{1/3}\mathbb{P}(J_{Z})\leq\limsup_{n\to+\infty}n^{1/3}\mathbb{P}(J_{Z})\leq c_{q,Y}\text{.}

It remains to find a proper lower bound for the left hand side of (
2.40
).
Combining Lemma
2.3
and the fact
Z_{\ell}-Z_{\ell-1}=O(\log n)
, we obtain

\mathbb{E}[\exp(\theta n^{-1/3}(Z_{\ell}-Z_{\ell-1}))]=1-\theta C_{\ref{limz}}qn^{-2/3}+\frac{\theta^{2}\cdot n^{-2/3}}{2}\sigma^{2}+o(n^{-2/3}),\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ as }n\to\infty\text{.}

As in the proof of Proposition
2.1
, there exists a unique positive solution
{\theta}=\widetilde{\theta}_{n}
to the equation

\mathbb{E}[\exp(\theta n^{-1/3}(Z_{\ell}-Z_{\ell-1}))]=1\textnormal{,}

for all large
n>0
, which satisfies

(2.41)

\widetilde{\theta}_{n}=2C_{\ref{limz}}q/\sigma^{2}+o(1),\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{as }n\to\infty\text{.}

Thus we can define a new probability
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}
by

(2.42)

\frac{\mathrm{d}\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}}:=\exp\left[\widetilde{\theta}_{n}n^{-1/3}(Z_{\ell}-Z_{0})\right]\textnormal{.}

Clearly
Z_{0}
has the same law under
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}
as
\mathbb{P}
.
Combining Lemma
2.3
and
|Z_{\ell}-Z_{\ell-1}|=O(\log n)
,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}(Z_{\ell}-Z_{\ell-1})=\mathbb{E}\left((Z_{\ell}-Z_{\ell-1})\exp(\widetilde{\theta}_{n}n^{-1/3}(Z_{\ell}-Z_{\ell-1}))\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(Z_{\ell}-Z_{\ell-1})+\widetilde{\theta}_{n}n^{-1/3}\mathbb{E}(Z_{\ell}-Z_{\ell-1})^{2}+O(n^{-2/3}\log^{3}n)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-C_{\ref{limz}}qn^{-1/3}+2C_{\ref{limz}}qn^{-1/3}+o(n^{-1/3})=C_{\ref{limz}}qn^{-1/3}+o(n^{-1/3}).

Similar to (
2.14
), under the measure
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}
,
(\overline{Z}(t))
converges in law to the process

(2.43)

\widetilde{B}_{t,q}:=B_{t}+\frac{C_{\ref{limz}}}{\sigma}\sqrt{(1+\rho/\lambda)m_{1}}q^{3/2}t=:B_{t}+C_{\ref{cqdef-tilde}}(q)t,

Therefore, by Markov’s inequality, it holds that

(A.34)

\begin{split}\lim_{K\to\infty}\sup_{N>N_{*}}\sup_{0<y<a_{N}}\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{N}>\sigma\sqrt{N}K\mid\min_{j\leq N}Y_{j}>0,Y_{0}=y\right)=0.\end{split}

Now for any fixed
K_{1},K_{2}\in(0,\infty)
, by (
A.33
),

(A.35)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\sup_{0<y<a_{N}}\left|\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{N}\leq\sigma\sqrt{N}K_{1}\mid\min_{j\leq N}Y_{j}>0,Y_{0}=y\right)}{\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{N}\leq\sigma\sqrt{N}K_{2}\mid\min_{j\leq N}Y_{j}>0,Y_{0}=y\right)}-\frac{\int_{0}^{K_{1}}ze^{C_{\ref{cqdef}}(q)z-z^{2}/2}\mathrm{d}z}{\int_{0}^{K_{2}}ze^{C_{\ref{cqdef}}(q)z-z^{2}/2}\mathrm{d}z}\right|=0.

For any
\varepsilon>0
, let
K_{2}=K_{2}(\varepsilon)
be the constant such that the probability in (
A.34
) is bounded above by
\varepsilon
, i.e.,

\sup_{N>N_{*}}\sup_{0<y<a_{N}}\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{N}>\sigma\sqrt{N}K\mid\min_{j\leq N}Y_{j}>0,Y_{0}=y\right)\leq\varepsilon,

and that

\int_{K_{2}}^{\infty}ze^{C_{\ref{cqdef}}(q)z-z^{2}/2}\mathrm{d}z<\varepsilon.

For large enough
N
, by (
A.35
),

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{N}\leq\sigma\sqrt{N}K_{1}\mid\min_{j\leq N}Y_{j}>0,Y_{0}=y\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{N}\leq\sigma\sqrt{N}K_{1}\mid\min_{j\leq N}Y_{j}>0,Y_{0}=y\right)}{\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{N}\leq\sigma\sqrt{N}K_{2}\mid\min_{j\leq N}Y_{j}>0,Y_{0}=y\right)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\varepsilon+\frac{\int_{0}^{K_{1}}ze^{C_{\ref{cqdef}}(q)z-z^{2}/2}\mathrm{d}z}{\int_{0}^{K_{2}}ze^{C_{\ref{cqdef}}(q)z-z^{2}/2}\mathrm{d}z}\leq\varepsilon+\frac{\int_{0}^{K_{1}}ze^{C_{\ref{cqdef}}(q)z-z^{2}/2}\mathrm{d}z}{\int_{0}^{\infty}ze^{C_{\ref{cqdef}}(q)z-z^{2}/2}\mathrm{d}z-\varepsilon},

which leads to (by sending
N\to\infty
first and then
\varepsilon\to 0
),

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\sup_{0<y<a_{N}}\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{N}\leq\sigma\sqrt{N}K_{1}\mid\min_{j\leq N}Y_{j}>0,Y_{0}=y\right)\leq\frac{\int_{0}^{K_{1}}ze^{C_{\ref{cqdef}}(q)z-z^{2}/2}\mathrm{d}z}{\int_{0}^{\infty}ze^{C_{\ref{cqdef}}(q)z-z^{2}/2}\mathrm{d}z}.

We can also obtain a similar lower bound and conclude that

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\sup_{0<y<a_{N}}\bigg|

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{N}\leq\sigma\sqrt{N}K_{1}\mid\min_{j\leq N}Y_{j}>0,Y_{0}=y\right)-\frac{\int_{0}^{K_{1}}ze^{C_{\ref{cqdef}}(q)z-z^{2}/2}\mathrm{d}z}{\int_{0}^{\infty}ze^{C_{\ref{cqdef}}(q)z-z^{2}/2}\mathrm{d}z}\bigg|=0,

which implies (
A.32
) and this completes the proof of the lemma.
∎
