Multiconic Optimization for Symmetric Cones and Hyperbolic Coupling
Marianna E.-Nagy, Yurii Nesterov, Petra Renáta Rigó

Abstract




Abstract.
We develop a new interior-point algorithm for solving multiconic optimization problems using the parabolic target space approach.
The feasible cone in these problems is composed as a direct product of many small-dimensional cones.
Our approach is based on a new concept, called the hyperbolic coupling. This provides a new framework that has an advantage of interdependent pairs of primal-dual variables. In this way, their behaviour is much more controllable. We justify all main steps in the complexity analysis of the algorithm and prove that the overall complexity of solving this type of large-scale nonlinear problems by our algorithm is comparable with the best known complexity for solving linear programming problems of the same dimension.

Keywords.
Multiconic optimization, hyperbolic coupling, interior-point algorithm, functional proximity measure.

Introduction

The development of interior-point algorithms (IPAs) for linear programming (LP) originated with the seminal work of Karmarkar
[
7
]
, who introduced a novel projective method. Later on, several IPAs have been published for different classes of problems, see from
[
9
,
13
,
15
]
. The most important results related to IPAs are summarized in the monographs of Roos et al.
[
16
]
and Ye
[
17
]
.

In multiconic optimization, we consider a direct product of small-dimensional symmetric cones. Hence, LP is a special case of multiconic optimization, where all cones are one-dimensional. Multiconic optimization often arises in practical applications, see
[
8
]
.

Note that in 2016, the horizontal linear complementarity problem over
Cartesian product of symmetric cones
has been introduced by Asadi et al.
[
2
]
. Later on, several IPAs have been proposed for solving these types of problems, see
[
1
,
3
]
.

Nesterov and Nemirovskii
[
18
]
introduced the framework of self-concordant functions, which is a key element to extend several classes of IPAs from LP to nonlinear settings. Nesterov and Todd
[
11
,
12
]
provided a theoretical foundation for the study of efficient IPAs for problems that are extensions of LP. They considered self-scaled cones, which include as special cases the cone of positive semidefinite matrices, the second-order cone, and the nonnegative orthant. Güler
[
6
]
showed that the family of self-scaled cones is identical to the symmetric cones.

Nesterov
[
19
]
introduced the so-called parabolic target space (PTS) approach for LP, which can start at an arbitrary strictly feasible solution. He
embedded the problem into a higher-dimensional one and introduced modeling variables according to the PTS. The proposed method is based on a parabolic barrier function, which plays a key role in the algorithm. In
[
4
]
, we extended this IPA for weighted monotone LCPs. In
[
5
]
, we proposed a new PTS IPA for LP problems based on a new direction, called the universal tangent direction. In
[
14
]
, Nesterov showed that the IPA presented in
[
5
]
has favorable local behavior under some non-degeneracy assumptions.

In this paper, we propose a new IPA in the PTS framework for solving multiconic optimization problems. We introduce a new concept, called
hyperbolic coupling
. This gives a new framework, which has an advantage that the primal-dual variables are interdependent between the cones. In this way, their behaviour is much more controllable. We also propose the new inequality (
2.13
), which plays a key role in the complexity analysis of the new IPA.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we present the notations used in the paper, and give the definition of the self-concordant functions and barriers, respectively. Section
3
presents the theory of self-scaled barriers, while Section
4
contains the new concept of hyperbolic coupling. In Section
5
we present the multiconic optimization problem
[
10
]
and the new PTS approach. In Section
6
we show how the search directions are computed in the new algorithm. Section
7
presents the functional proximity measure and the main parts of the complexity analysis of the algorithm. In Section
8
we present the strategy for choosing the initial point. After that, the concluding remarks and further research topics are presented. In Appendix, we show how to compute the scaling point for the Lorentz cone, which seems to be absent in the literature.

For a linear operator

. We write

if

.

In case of

, we have
\mathbb{E}^{*}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
and

\langle s,x\rangle\;=\;\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}s^{(i)}x^{(i)},\quad\|x\|\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\;\langle x,x\rangle^{1/2},\quad s,x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Vector
e\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the vector of all ones, and
e_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,n
, are coordinate vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

For function

with open domain
{\rm dom\,}f\subseteq\mathbb{E}
, we denote by

\begin{array}[]{rcl}D^{k}f(x)[h_{1},\dots,h_{k}],\quad x\in{\rm dom\,}f,\;h_{i}\in\mathbb{E},\;i=1,\dots,k,\end{array}

its
k
th directional derivative at
x
along
k
directions. It is a symmetric
k
-linear form. If all directions are the same, we use notation
D^{k}f(x)[h]^{k}
. Thus, under the mild assumptions, for the gradient and the Hessian of function

, we have the following relations:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}Df(x)[h]&=&\langle\nabla f(x),h\rangle,\quad D^{2}f(x)[h]^{2}\;=\;\langle\nabla^{2}f(x)h,h\rangle.\end{array}

Using the third derivative, we can define the following objects:

\displaystyle D^{3}f(x)[h_{1},h_{2},h_{3}]

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\langle D^{3}f(x)[h_{1},h_{2}],h_{3}\rangle

(2.1)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\langle D^{3}f(x)[h_{1}]h_{2},h_{3}\rangle,\quad h_{1},h_{2},h_{3}\in\mathbb{E}.

Thus,

and

is a self-adjoint linear operator from
\mathbb{E}
to
\mathbb{E}^{*}
. Note that

D^{3}f(x)[h_{1},h_{2}]=D^{3}f(x)[h_{1}]h_{2},\quad h_{1},h_{2}\in\mathbb{E}.

Self-concordant functions

Let us recall some facts from the theory of self-concordant functions (e.g.
[
20
,
18
]
).

Definition 2.1
.

Let a closed convex function
f\in{\cal C}^{3}(\mathbb{E})
have an open domain. It is called
self-concordant
(SCF) if its third directional derivative is bounded by an appropriate power of the second one:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}D^{3}f(x)[h]^{3}&\leq&2\Big(D^{2}f(x)[h]^{2}\Big)^{3/2},\quad x\in{\rm dom\,}f,\;h\in\mathbb{E}.\end{array}

(2.2)

If
{\rm dom\,}f
contains no straight line, then its Hessian is positive definite at any
x\in{\rm dom\,}f
. In this paper, we mainly deal with such functions. However,
{\rm dom\,}f
still can be unbounded. In this case, the following relation is important:

\langle\nabla^{2}f(x)d,d\rangle^{1/2}\leq-\langle\nabla f(x),d\rangle

for any
x\in{\rm int\,}{\rm dom\,}f
and any
recession direction

d
of the set
{\rm dom\,}f
.

The dual function
f_{*}(s)=\max\limits_{x\in{\rm dom\,}f}[-\langle s,x\rangle-f(x)]
for any SCF is also self-concordant. Note that
-\nabla f(x)\in{\rm dom\,}f_{*}
for all
x\in{\rm dom\,}f
, and

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\nabla f_{*}(s)&=&-x(s)\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\;-\arg\max\limits_{x\in{\rm dom\,}f}[-\langle s,x\rangle-f(x)],\quad s\in{\rm dom\,}f_{*}.\end{array}

For any
x\in{\rm dom\,}f
and
s\in{\rm dom\,}f_{*}
, we have

\displaystyle f(-\nabla f_{*}(s))

\displaystyle=\langle s,\nabla f_{*}(s)\rangle-f_{*}(s),

\displaystyle f_{*}(-\nabla f(x))

\displaystyle=\langle\nabla f(x),x\rangle-f(x),

\displaystyle\nabla f(-\nabla f_{*}(s))

\displaystyle=-s,

\displaystyle\nabla f_{*}(-\nabla f(x))

\displaystyle=-x,

(2.3)

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}f(-\nabla f_{*}(s))

\displaystyle=[\nabla^{2}f_{*}(s)]^{-1},

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}f_{*}(-\nabla f(x))

\displaystyle=[\nabla^{2}f(x)]^{-1}.

One of the main properties of SCF is that the
Dikin Ellipsoid
defined as

\begin{array}[]{rcl}W^{f}_{r}(x)&=&\{y\in\mathbb{E}:\;\langle\nabla^{2}f(x)(y-x),y-x\rangle\leq r^{2}\},\quad x\in{\rm dom\,}f,\end{array}

belongs to
{\rm dom\,}f
for any
r\in[0,1)
.

In this paper, we often use the local norms defined by the Hessians of SCF. For
x\in{\rm dom\,}f
,
s\in{\rm dom\,}f_{*}
,
u\in\mathbb{E}
, and
g\in\mathbb{E}^{*}
, we adopt the following notation:

\begin{array}[]{rclrcl}\|u\|_{\nabla^{2}f(x)}&=&\langle\nabla^{2}f(x)u,u\rangle^{1/2},&\quad\|g\|_{\nabla^{2}f(x)}&=&\langle g,[\nabla^{2}f(x)]^{-1}g\rangle^{1/2},\\
\\
\|g\|_{\nabla^{2}f_{*}(s)}&=&\langle g,\nabla^{2}f_{*}(s)g\rangle^{1/2},&\quad\|u\|_{\nabla^{2}f_{*}(s)}&=&\langle[\nabla^{2}f_{*}(s)]^{-1}u,u\rangle^{1/2}.\end{array}

If no ambiguity arise, we use the corresponding shortcuts
\|u\|_{x}
,
\|g\|_{x}
,
\|g\|_{s}
, and
\|u\|_{s}
. Hence, the sense of notation
\|a\|_{b}
depends on the sets and the spaces containing
a
and
b
.

For SCF, we often use the following relations:

(1-r)^{2}\nabla^{2}f(x)\preceq\nabla^{2}f(y)\;\preceq\;\frac{1}{(1-r)^{2}}\nabla^{2}f(x),\quad x\in{\rm dom\,}f,

which are valid for all
y\in W^{f}_{r}(x)
and
r\in[0,1)
. The following inequalities are also important:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\omega(r)&\leq&f(y)-f(x)-\langle\nabla f(x),y-x\rangle\;\leq\;\omega_{*}(r),\end{array}

(2.4)

where
x\in{\rm dom\,}f
,
y\in W^{f}_{r}(x)
,
r\in[0,1)
, and

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\omega(\tau)&=&\tau-\ln(1+\tau),\quad\omega_{*}(\tau)\;=\;-\tau-\ln(1-\tau),\quad\tau\in[0,1).\end{array}

Sometimes the following variant of inequalities (
2.4
) is useful:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\omega(\delta)\;\leq\;f(x)-f(y)-\langle\nabla f(y),x-y\rangle&\leq&\omega_{*}(\delta),\end{array}

(2.5)

with
\delta=\|\nabla f(y)-\nabla f(x)\|_{x}
(for (
2.5
2
), we need
\delta<1
, see Theorem 5.1.12 in
[
20
]
).

Self-concordant barriers

Let us recall now the properties of
self-concordant barriers
(SCB) for convex cones.

Definition 2.2
.

A self-concordant function
F(\cdot)
is called a
self-concordant barrier
, if there exists a constant

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\nu&\geq&1\end{array}

(2.6)

such that for all
x\in{\rm dom\,}F
and
h\in\mathbb{E}
we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\langle\nabla F(x),h\rangle^{2}&\leq&\nu\langle\nabla^{2}F(x)h,h\rangle.\end{array}

(2.7)

The constant
\nu
is called the
parameter
of the barrier.
If
\nabla^{2}F(x)\succ 0
, then the condition (
2.7
) is equivalent to the following:

\lambda_{F}^{2}(x)\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\;\|\nabla F(x)\|_{x}^{2}\leq\nu,\quad x\in{\rm dom\,}F.

This value is responsible for the complexity of the set
{\rm dom\,}F
for corresponding IPMs.

In the theory of IPMs, the most important feasible sets are convex cones. A cone
K
is called
proper
if it is closed convex and pointed (contains no straight lines). For such cones, SCBs possess a natural property of
logarithmic homogeneity
:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}F(\tau x)&\equiv&F(x)-\nu\ln\tau,\quad x\in{\rm int\,}K,\;\tau>0.\end{array}

(2.8)

This identity has several important consequences: for all
x\in{\rm int\,}K
and
\tau>0
, we have

\displaystyle\nabla

\displaystyle F(\tau x)={1\over\tau}\nabla F(x),

\displaystyle D^{k}

\displaystyle F(\tau x)={1\over\tau^{k}}D^{k}F(x),\;\;k\geq 2,

(2.9)

\displaystyle\langle\nabla

\displaystyle F(x),x\rangle=-\nu,

\displaystyle\langle\nabla^{2}

\displaystyle F(x)x,x\rangle=\nu,\qquad\qquad\qquad\|\nabla F(x)\|^{2}_{x}=\nu.

(2.10)

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}

\displaystyle F(x)x=-\nabla F(x),

\displaystyle D^{3}

\displaystyle F(x)[x]=-2\nabla^{2}F(x).

(2.11)

\displaystyle D^{4}

\displaystyle F(x)[x]=-3D^{3}F(x),

\displaystyle D^{4}

\displaystyle F(x)[x,x]=6\nabla^{2}F(x).

(2.12)

Thus, the parameter of a logarithmically homogeneous SCB for a proper cone (which we call
regular barrier
) is equal to the degree of logarithmic homogeneity.

It is important that the dual barrier
F_{*}(\cdot)
for the regular barrier
F(\cdot)
is a regular barrier for the dual cone

K^{*}\;=\;\Big\{s\in\mathbb{E}^{*}:\;\langle s,x\rangle\geq 0,\;\forall x\in K\Big\},

with the same value of barrier parameter.

Example 1
.

Let
\mathbb{S}^{n}=\{X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}:\;X=X^{T}\}
. Denote
\mathbb{S}^{n}_{+}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\{X\in\mathbb{S}^{n}:\;X\succeq 0\}
. It is a proper cone, which admits the following self-concordant logarithmically homogeneous barrier:

F(X)\;=\;-\ln\det X,\quad\nu=n.

Note that
(\mathbb{S}^{n}_{+})^{*}=\mathbb{S}^{n}_{+}
and
F_{*}(S)=-\ln\det S-n
. The derivatives of these barriers are very simple:

\nabla F(X)=-X^{-1},\quad\nabla^{2}F(X)H\;=\;X^{-1}HX^{-1},

[\nabla^{2}F(X)]^{-1}H=XHX,\quad X\succ 0,\;H\in\mathbb{S}^{n}.\quad\hfill\Box

Using a stronger version of the Fenchel inequality and also considering its dual side, we obtain the following new two-sided bound.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
K
be a proper cone and
F:{\rm int\,}K\to\mathbb{R}
be a self-concordant barrier function with parameter
\nu
. For all
x\in{\rm int\,}K
and
s\in{\rm int\,}K^{*}
, we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\nu\ln{\nu\over\langle s,x\rangle}&\leq&F(x)+F_{*}(s)+\nu\;\leq\;\nu\ln{\langle\nabla F(x),\nabla F_{*}(s)\rangle\over\nu},\end{array}

(2.13)

where the equality in both sides is achieved only for
s=-\mu\nabla F(x)
with some
\mu>0
.

Proof.

Note that
F
is logarithmically homogeneous. Therefore, we use the classical Fenchel inequality for
\tau x
and
s
. We obtain the tightest bound with
\tau=\frac{\nu}{\langle x,s\rangle}
, which is the left-hand side of (
2.13
).

Now we use the dual version of the left-hand side inequality (
2.13
). For any
x\in{\rm int\,}K
and
s\in{\rm int\,}K^{*}
, from the dual Fenchel inequality we have

\displaystyle\langle\nabla F(x),\nabla F_{*}(s)\rangle\;

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\;-F(-\nabla F_{*}(s))-F_{*}(-\nabla F(x))

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-FDual})}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\;-\langle s,\nabla F_{*}(s)\rangle+F_{*}(s)-\langle\nabla F(x),x\rangle+F(x)\;

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-HomFX})}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\;2\nu+F(x)+F_{*}(s).

Replacing
x
by
x/t
with
t>0
and minimizing the resulting inequality in
t
, we get the right-hand side of inequality (
2.13
).
As we used Fenchel inequalities, (
2.13
) holds with equality if and only if
s
and
\nabla F(x)
are proportional.
∎

Self-scaled barriers

The most important convex cones are
symmetric
. Recall that cone
K
is symmetric if and only if it admits a normal
self-scaled barrier

[
11
,
12
]
.

Definition 3.1
.

Function
F(\cdot)
is called a
\nu
-self-scaled barrier for the proper cone
K
if it is a
\nu
-normal barrier for
K
and for all
x,w\in{\rm int\,}K
we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\nabla^{2}F(w)x&\in&{\rm int\,}K^{*},\\
\\
F_{*}(\nabla^{2}F(w)x)&=&F(x)-2F(w)-\nu.\end{array}

(3.1)

It can be proved that for any
w\in{\rm int\,}K
we have
K^{*}=\nabla^{2}F(w)K
. For any pair of points
x\in{\rm int\,}K
,
s\in{\rm int\,}K^{*}
there exists a unique
scaling point

w\in{\rm int\,}K
such that

\begin{array}[]{rcl}s&=&\nabla^{2}F(w)x.\end{array}

(3.2)

Moreover, the gradients and the Hessians at these points are related as follows:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\nabla F(x)&=&\nabla^{2}F(w)\nabla F_{*}(s),\\
\\
\nabla^{2}F(x)&=&\nabla^{2}F(w)\nabla^{2}F_{*}(s)\nabla^{2}F(w).\end{array}

(3.3)

Example 2
.

Let
K\equiv\mathbb{S}^{n}_{+}
be the cone of positive-semidefinite
n\times n
-matrices. Then the following barrier is self-scaled
[
11
]

F(X)=\;-\ln\det X,\quad F_{*}(S)\;=\;-\ln\det S-n,\;X\in{\rm int\,}K,\;S\in{\rm int\,}K_{*}=\mathbb{S}^{n}_{+}.

For two positive-definite matrices
S
and

, the scaling point

can be computed by the following expression:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}W&=&X^{1/2}[X^{1/2}SX^{1/2}]^{-1/2}X^{1/2}.\hskip 21.52771pt\Box\end{array}

(3.4)

Note that the dual function
F_{*}(\cdot)
is a self-scaled barrier for the cone
K^{*}
. Since the relation (
3.2
) can be rewritten in the dual form

x=\nabla^{2}F_{*}(w_{*})s,\quad w_{*}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}-\nabla F(w)\in{\rm int\,}K^{*},

we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}F([\nabla^{2}F(w)]^{-1}s)&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-FDual})}}{{=}}&F(\nabla^{2}F_{*}(w_{*})s)\;=\;F_{*}(s)-2F_{*}(w_{*})-\nu\\
\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-HomFX})_{1}}}{{=}}&F_{*}(s)+2F(w)+\nu.\end{array}

(3.5)

In the sequel, we need the following
Exchanging Rule
for self-scaled barriers.

Lemma 3.1
.

For all
x,u\in{\rm int\,}K
, we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\langle\nabla^{2}F(u)x,x\rangle&=&\langle\nabla F(u),[\nabla^{2}F(x)]^{-1}\nabla F(u)\rangle.\end{array}

(3.6)

Moreover,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\nabla^{2}F(u)x&=&-\mbox{${1\over 2}$}D^{3}F(x)\Big[[\nabla^{2}F(x)]^{-1}\nabla F(u)\Big]^{2}.\end{array}

(3.7)

Proof.

Denote
s=-\nabla F(x)\in{\rm int\,}K^{*}
. Let
s=\nabla^{2}F(w)u
. Therefore,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}x&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-FDual})_{3}}}{{=}}&-\nabla F_{*}(s)\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-MapGH})_{1}}}{{=}}\;-[\nabla^{2}F(w)]^{-1}\nabla F(u),\\
\\
\left[\nabla^{2}F(x)\right]^{-1}&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-FDual})_{6}}}{{=}}&\phantom{.}\nabla^{2}F_{*}(s)\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-MapGH})_{2}}}{{=}}[\nabla^{2}F(w)]^{-1}\nabla^{2}F(u)[\nabla^{2}F(w)]^{-1}.\end{array}

Thus,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\langle\nabla F(u),[\nabla^{2}F(x)]^{-1}\nabla F(u)\rangle&=&\langle\nabla F(u),[\nabla^{2}F(w)]^{-1}\nabla^{2}F(u)[\nabla^{2}F(w)]^{-1}\nabla F(u)\rangle\\
\\
&=&\langle\nabla^{2}F(u)x,x\rangle.\end{array}

Further, differentiating identity (

) at point

along direction

, we get

\begin{array}[]{rcl}2\langle\nabla^{2}F(u)x,h\rangle&=&-\langle\nabla F(u),[\nabla^{2}F(x)]^{-1}D^{3}F(x)[h][\nabla^{2}F(x)]^{-1}\nabla F(u)\rangle\\
\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{def-D3})}}{{=}}&-D^{3}F(x)\Big[h,[\nabla^{2}F(x)]^{-1}\nabla F(u),[\nabla^{2}F(x)]^{-1}\nabla F(u)\Big],\end{array}

and this is (

\hfill\Box

4
Hyperbolic coupling

In our approach, we define a new family of central paths by joining the elements of the primal-dual pair
(x,s)\in{\rm int\,}(K\times K^{*})
and introducing an additional control variable
v\in\mathbb{R}
. We will denote by

Definition 4.1
.

For the symmetric cone
K\subset\mathbb{E}

{\cal C}(K)\;=\;\{z=(x,s,v)\in K\times K^{*}\times\mathbb{R}:\;x+v^{2}\nabla F_{*}(s)\in K\}

is called the

of the primal-dual cone
K\times K^{*}

Furthermore, let us introduce the following notations

{}x(z)\;=\;x+v^{2}\nabla F_{*}(s)\quad\text{ and }\quad s(z)\;=\;s+v^{2}\nabla F(x).

We can show that the unique scaling point

is the same as that of

and

.
Indeed, if
s=\nabla^{2}F(w)x
, then by (
3.3
)
1

\displaystyle s(z)\;

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;s+v^{2}\nabla F(x)=\nabla^{2}F(w)x+v^{2}\nabla^{2}F(w)\nabla F_{*}(s)\;\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\nabla^{2}F(w)(x+v^{2}\nabla F_{*}(s))=\nabla^{2}F(w)x(z).

For symmetric cone

.

Let
K=\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
F(x)=-\ln x
. Then

{\cal C}(K)\;=\;\{z=(x,s,v)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}:\;xs\geq v^{2}\}.

This construction explains our terminology.

\hfill\Box

Let us prove that
{\cal C}(K)
is a convex cone. For that, we need the following lemma.

Lemma 4.2
.

For
\hat{s}=(s,v)
, the
mapping
G(\hat{s})=v^{2}\nabla F_{*}(s):{\rm int\,}K^{*}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{E}
is concave with respect to
K
:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}D^{2}G(\hat{s})[h]^{2}&\preceq_{K}&0,\quad\forall h=(h_{s},\tau)\in\mathbb{E}^{*}\times R.\end{array}

(4.10)

Consequently, for any
\alpha\in[0,1]
, we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}G(\alpha\hat{s}_{1}+(1-\alpha)\hat{s}_{0})&\succeq_{K}&\alpha G(\hat{s}_{1})+(1-\alpha)G(\hat{s}_{0}).\end{array}

(4.11)

Proof.

Indeed, for arbitrary
h=(h_{s},\tau)\in\mathbb{E}^{*}\times R
, we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}DG(s,v)[h]&=&2v\tau\nabla F_{*}(s)+v^{2}\nabla^{2}F_{*}(s)h_{s},\\
\\
DG^{2}(s,v)[h]^{2}&=&2\tau^{2}\nabla F_{*}(s)+4v\tau\nabla^{2}F_{*}(s)h_{s}+v^{2}\nabla^{3}F_{*}(s)[h_{s}]^{2}\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-Hom1})}}{{=}}&\nabla^{3}F_{*}(s)[vh_{s}-\tau s]^{2}\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-Expand1})}}{{\preceq}}_{K}\;0.\end{array}

Further, denote
h=\hat{s}_{1}-\hat{s}_{0}
and
\hat{s}_{\alpha}=\hat{s}_{0}+\alpha h
,
\alpha\in[0,1]
. Then, by Taylor formula,

G(\hat{s}_{1})-G(\hat{s}_{0})-DG(\hat{s}_{0})[h]\;=\;\int\limits_{0}^{1}(1-\tau)D^{2}G(\hat{s}_{\alpha}))[h]^{2}d\alpha\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-GConc})}}{{\preceq}}_{K}\;0.

Therefore, for any
\alpha\in[0,1]
, we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}G(\hat{s}_{1})&\;{\preceq}_{K}&G(\hat{s}_{\alpha})+DG(\hat{s}_{\alpha})[\hat{s}_{1}-\hat{s}_{\alpha}]\;=\;G(\hat{s}_{\alpha})+(1-\alpha)DG(\hat{s}_{\alpha})[h],\\
\\
G(\hat{s}_{0})&\;{\preceq}_{K}&G(\hat{s}_{\alpha})+DG(\hat{s}_{\alpha})[\hat{s}_{0}-\hat{s}_{\alpha}]\;=\;G(\hat{s}_{\alpha})-\alpha G(\hat{s}_{\alpha})[h].\end{array}

Multiplying the first inequality by
\alpha
and the second one by
1-\alpha
and adding the results, we get (
4.11
).

\hfill\Box

Corollary 4.3
.

The cone
{\cal C}(K)
is convex.

Proof.

Denote
\hat{s}_{i}=(s_{i},v_{i})
and
\hat{z}_{i}=(x_{i},\hat{s}_{i})
,
i=0,1
. Let the points
\hat{z}_{i}\in{\cal C}(K)
,
i=0,1
.
Denote
\hat{z}_{\alpha}=\alpha\hat{z}_{1}+(1-\alpha)\hat{z}_{0}
.
Then, for any
\alpha\in[0,1]
, we have

x_{\alpha}+G(\hat{s}_{\alpha})\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-GConc0})}}{{\succeq}}_{K}\;\alpha(x_{1}+G(\hat{s}_{1}))+(1-\alpha)(x_{0}+G(\hat{s}_{0}))\;\succeq_{K}\;0.\hskip 21.52771pt\Box

In order to use the cone
{\cal C}(K)
in interior-point methods, we need to endow it with a self-concordant barrier. The most natural candidate for this role is as follows:

\Phi(z)\;=\;\Phi(x,s,v)\;=\;F(x+v^{2}\nabla F_{*}(s))+F_{*}(s)\;=\;F(x(z))+F_{*}(s).

(4.12)

Theorem 4.4
.

Function
\Phi(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)
is a
2\nu
-logarithmically homogeneous at the cone
{\cal C}(K)
. It has the following dual representation:

\Phi(z)\;=\;\Phi(x,s,v)=F_{*}(s+v^{2}\nabla F(x))+F(x)\;=\;F_{*}(s(z))+F(x).

Proof.

Indeed, for the primal-dual pair
(x,s)\in{\rm int\,}(K\times K^{*})
there exists a scaling point
w\in{\rm int\,}K
such that
s=\nabla^{2}F(w)x
. Then, using (
4.9
) we obtain

\displaystyle{}F_{*}(s(z))+F(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle F_{*}\Big(\nabla^{2}F(w)x(z)\Big)+F(x)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-IncSSB})}}{{=}}

\displaystyle F(x(z))-2F(w)-\nu+F\Big([\nabla^{2}F(w)]^{-1}s\Big)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-RepSSB})}}{{=}}

\displaystyle F(x(z))+F_{*}(s).

Further, for any
\tau>0
, from the first identity of (
2.9
) we have

x(\tau z)=\tau x(z),

hence using (
2.8
) we get

\Phi(\tau z)=F(\tau x(z))+F_{*}(\tau s)=\Phi(z)-2\nu\ln\tau.

Thus, the degree of logarithmic homogeneity of this function is equal to
2\nu
.

\hfill\Box

Example 4

Let
K=\mathbb{S}_{n}^{+}
. Then,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\Phi(X,S,v)&=&-\ln\det(X-v^{2}S^{-1})-\ln\det S-n\\
&=&\;-\ln\det\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}X&vI_{n}\\
vI_{n}&S\end{array}\right)-n.\end{array}

Clearly, this is a self-concordant function with parameter

.

\hfill\Box

In the following example we give an explicit form of the barrier (

) for the Hyperbolic Coupling of the Lorentz cone.

Example 5
.

Let us look at the Lorentz cone
{\cal L}_{n}=\{x=(x_{0},x_{1})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}:x_{0}\geq\|x_{1}\|\}
. It admits the following self-concordant barrier:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}F(x)&=&-\ln(x_{0}^{2}-\|x_{1}\|^{2}),\quad F_{*}(s)\;=\;-\ln(s_{0}^{2}-\|s_{1}\|^{2})-2+2\ln 2.\end{array}

Denote
\omega_{x}=x_{0}^{2}-\|x_{1}\|^{2}
and
\omega_{s}=s_{0}^{2}-\|s_{1}\|^{2}
. Then
\nabla F_{*}(s)={2\over\omega_{s}}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}-s_{0}\\
s_{1}\end{array}\right)
, and

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}x_{0}\\
x_{1}\end{array}\right)+v^{2}\nabla F_{*}(s)&=&{1\over\omega_{s}}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\omega_{s}x_{0}-2v^{2}s_{0}\\
\omega_{s}x_{1}+2v^{2}s_{1}\end{array}\right).\end{array}

Hence,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\Phi(x,s,v)&=&-\ln\Big(\underbrace{(\omega_{s}x_{0}-2v^{2}s_{0})^{2}-\|\omega_{s}x_{1}+2v^{2}s_{1}\|^{2}}_{\equiv r}\Big)+\ln\omega_{s}-2+2\ln 2.\end{array}

Note that

\begin{array}[]{rcl}r&=&\omega_{s}^{2}x_{0}^{2}-4v^{2}\omega_{s}x_{0}s_{0}+4v^{4}s_{0}^{2}-\omega_{s}^{2}\|x_{1}\|^{2}-4v^{4}\|s_{1}\|^{2}-4v^{2}\omega_{s}\langle x_{1},s_{1}\rangle\\
\\
&=&\omega_{s}\Big[\omega_{x}\omega_{s}-4v^{2}(x_{0}s_{0}+\langle x_{1},s_{1}\rangle)+4v^{4}\Big].\end{array}

Thus, for Lorentz cone, we have

\Phi(x,s,v)=-\ln\Big[\underbrace{\omega_{x}\omega_{s}-4v^{2}(x_{0}s_{0}+\langle x_{1},s_{1}\rangle)+4v^{4}}_{\equiv\delta(x,s,v)}\Big]-2+2\ln 2.

Note that

\displaystyle\delta(x,s,v)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(x_{0}s_{0}+\langle x_{1},s_{1}\rangle-2v^{2})^{2}-\|s_{0}x_{1}+x_{0}s_{1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\|x_{1}\|^{2}\,\|s_{1}\|^{2}-\langle x_{1},s_{1}\rangle^{2}.

Note that if
n=1
, then
\delta(x,s,v)
is the difference of two squares, hence
\Phi(x,s,v)
is a self-concordant function.

\hfill\Box

It is not straightforward that
\Phi
is self-concordant in general. We can prove this property under an additional assumption.

Assumption 1
.

For any
x\in{\rm int\,}K
,
h_{x}\in\mathbb{E}
, and
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}D^{4}F(x)[h_{x}][\alpha x+\beta h_{x}]^{2}&\preceq_{K^{*}}&-3D^{3}F(x)[\alpha x+\beta h_{x}]^{2}\cdot\|h_{x}\|_{x}.\end{array}

(4.13)

If cone
K
is symmetric, then verification of Assumption
1
is easier.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let the barrier
F(\cdot\textasciiacute)
be self-scaled. Then Assumption
1
is valid if and only if there exists a point

\bar{x}\in{\rm int\,}K
such that for all
h_{x}\in\mathbb{E}
, and
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}D^{4}F(\bar{x})[h_{x}][\alpha\bar{x}+\beta h_{x}]^{2}&\preceq_{K^{*}}&-3D^{3}F(\bar{x})[\alpha\bar{x}+\beta h_{x}]^{2}\cdot\|h_{x}\|_{\bar{x}}.\end{array}

(4.14)

Proof.

First of all, let us show that the relation (
4.13
) is valid is and only if there exists
\bar{s}\in{\rm int\,}K^{*}
such that

\begin{array}[]{rcl}D^{4}F_{*}(\bar{s})[h_{s}][\alpha\bar{s}+\beta h_{s}]^{2}&\preceq_{K}&-3D^{3}F_{*}(\bar{s})[\alpha\bar{s}+\beta h_{s}]^{2}\cdot\|h_{s}\|_{\bar{s}}.\end{array}

(4.15)

for all
h_{s}\in\mathbb{E}^{*}
and
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
. Indeed, for any
x\in{\rm int\,}K
and
\bar{s}\in{\rm int\,}K^{*}
there exist
w\in{\rm int\,}K
such that
\bar{s}=\nabla^{2}F(w)x
. Let us choose an arbitrary direction
p\in K
. Then, by differentiation identity (
3.1
) along this direction, we get

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\langle\nabla F_{*}(\nabla^{2}F(w)x),\nabla^{2}F(w)p\rangle&\equiv&\langle\nabla F(x),p\rangle.\end{array}

Further, by differentiating this identity two times along direction
q=\alpha x+\beta h_{x}
, we get

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\langle D^{3}F_{*}(\nabla^{2}F(w)x)[\nabla^{2}F(w)q]^{2},\nabla^{2}F(w)p\rangle&\equiv&\langle D^{3}F(x)[q]^{2},p\rangle.\end{array}

Differentiating this identity again along direction
h_{x}
, we get

\langle D^{4}F_{*}(\nabla^{2}F(w)x)[\nabla^{2}F(w)h_{x}][\nabla^{2}F(w)q]^{2},\nabla^{2}F(w)p\rangle\equiv\langle D^{4}F(x))[h_{x}][q]^{2},p\rangle.

(4.16)

Denote by
h_{s}=\nabla^{2}F(w)h_{x}
. Then
\nabla^{2}F(w)q=\alpha\bar{s}+\beta h_{s}
, and by Assumption
1
, we have

\begin{array}[]{cl}0\geq&\langle D^{4}F(x)[h_{x}][q]^{2}+3D^{3}F(x)[q]^{2}\cdot\|h_{x}\|_{x},p\rangle\\
\\
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-Replace})}}{{=}}&\langle D^{4}F_{*}(\bar{s})[h_{s}][\alpha\bar{s}+\beta h_{s}]^{2}+3D^{3}F_{*}(\bar{s})[\alpha\bar{s}+\beta h_{s}]^{2}\cdot\|h_{x}\|_{x},\nabla^{2}F(w)p\rangle.\end{array}

Note that

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\|h_{x}\|^{2}_{x}&=&\langle\nabla^{2}F(x)h_{x},h_{x}\rangle\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-MapGH})_{2}}}{{=}}\;\|h_{s}\|^{2}_{\bar{s}}.\end{array}

Since
p
can be any vector from cone
K
, which has nonempty interior, and
\nabla^{2}F(w)K=K^{*}
, we get the relation (
4.15
). Repeating our reasoning again with the relation (
4.15
), we get the relation (
4.14
).

\hfill\Box

Corollary 4.6
.

Let the barrier
F(\cdot)
be self-scaled. Then Assumption
1
is valid if and only if there exists a point

\bar{x}\in{\rm int\,}K
such that for all
h_{x}\in\mathbb{E}
, and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

{}\nabla^{2}F(\bar{x})h_{x}\;\preceq_{K^{*}}\;-\nabla F(\bar{x})\|h_{x}\|_{\bar{x}}.

(4.17)

and

{}D^{4}F(\bar{x})[h_{x}][\alpha\bar{x}+h_{x}]^{2}\;\preceq_{K^{*}}\;-3D^{3}F(\bar{x})[\alpha\bar{x}+h_{x}]^{2}\cdot\|h_{x}\|_{\bar{x}}.

(4.18)

Proof.

We have two cases,
\beta=0
and
\beta\neq 0
. If
\beta=0
, then using (
2.9
)-(
2.12
) relation (
4.14
) simplifies to the first relation of the corollary. For
\beta\neq 0
it is enough to check
\beta=1
, which is the second relation of the corollary.

\hfill\Box

Assumption
1
is crucial for proving self-concordance of function

. Before showing this, let us check how it works for the two most important cones.

Example 6
.

Let us show that the relation (
4.14
) is valid for the SDP case.

Denote
F(X)=-\ln\det X
. Then, for
X\succ 0
and

\displaystyle D^{3}F(X)[Q]^{2}

\displaystyle=-2X^{-1}QX^{-1}QX^{-1},\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt

\displaystyle D^{4}F(X)[H_{X}][Q]^{2}

\displaystyle=2X^{-1}H_{X}X^{-1}QX^{-1}QX^{-1}+2X^{-1}QX^{-1}H_{X}X^{-1}QX^{-1}\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt

\displaystyle+2X^{-1}QX^{-1}QX^{-1}H_{X}X^{-1}.

In view of the result of Lemma

Now, we need to differentiate the equation this along direction
h

\displaystyle\langle D^{4}F(\bar{x})[h][q]^{2},y\rangle

\displaystyle={6\over\omega_{\bar{x}}^{4}}\big\langle\omega_{\bar{x}}^{\prime},h\big\rangle\big\langle\omega_{\bar{x}}^{\prime},y\big\rangle\big\langle\omega_{\bar{x}}^{\prime},q\big\rangle^{2}-{2\over\omega_{\bar{x}}^{3}}\big\langle\omega_{h}^{\prime},y\big\rangle\big\langle\omega_{\bar{x}}^{\prime},q\big\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle-{4\over\omega_{\bar{x}}^{3}}\big\langle\omega_{\bar{x}}^{\prime},y\big\rangle\big\langle\omega_{\bar{x}}^{\prime},q\big\rangle\big\langle\omega_{h}^{\prime},q\big\rangle-{4\over\omega_{\bar{x}}^{3}}\big\langle\omega_{\bar{x}}^{\prime},h\big\rangle\big\langle\omega_{q}^{\prime},y\big\rangle\big\langle\omega_{\bar{x}}^{\prime},q\big\rangle

\displaystyle+{2\over\omega_{\bar{x}}^{2}}\big\langle\omega_{q}^{\prime},y\big\rangle\big\langle\omega_{h}^{\prime},q\big\rangle-{4\over\omega_{\bar{x}}^{3}}\big\langle\omega_{\bar{x}}^{\prime},h\big\rangle\big\langle\omega_{\bar{x}}^{\prime},y\big\rangle\,\omega_{q}+{2\over\omega_{\bar{x}}^{2}}\big\langle\omega_{h}^{\prime},y\big\rangle\,\omega_{q}.

Let us prove now the main theorem of this section.

Theorem 4.7
.

Let the self-scaled barrier
F(\cdot)
for the cone
K
satisfy Assumption
1
. Then function
\Phi(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)
is a self-concordant barrier for the cone
{\cal C}(K)
with parameter
2\nu
.

Proof.

For further investigation of the properties of the function
\Phi(\cdot)
, it is convenient to represent it in the following form:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\Phi(z)&=&\Psi(z)+F(x),\quad\Psi(z)\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\;F_{*}(s(z)),\\
\end{array}

Let us compute derivatives of this function along direction
\hat{h}=(h_{x},h_{s},h_{v})
. For the first derivative of
\Psi(\cdot)
, we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}D\Psi(z)[\hat{h}]&=&\langle\nabla F_{*}(s(z)),\omega^{\prime}\rangle,\\
\\
\sigma^{\prime}&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}&Ds(z)[\hat{h}]\;=\;h_{s}+2vh_{v}\nabla F(x)+v^{2}\nabla^{2}F(x)h_{x}.\end{array}

(4.20)

For the second derivative of this function, we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}D^{2}\Psi(z)[\hat{h}]^{2}&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-DPsi1})_{1}}}{{=}}&\langle\nabla^{2}F_{*}(s(z))\sigma^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime}\rangle+\langle\nabla F_{*}(s(z))\sigma^{\prime\prime}\rangle,\\
\end{array}

(4.21)

where

\sigma^{\prime\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}D\sigma^{\prime}[\hat{h}]\;=2h_{v}^{2}\nabla F(x)+4vh_{v}\nabla^{2}F(x)h_{x}+v^{2}D^{3}F(x)[h_{x}]^{2}.

(4.22)

Note that

\displaystyle D^{3}F(x)[h_{v}x-vh_{x}]^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle h_{v}^{2}D^{3}F(x)[x]^{2}-2vh_{v}D^{3}F(x)[x,h_{x}]+v^{2}D^{3}F(x)[h_{x}]^{2}

(4.23)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-Hom2})}}{{=}}

\displaystyle 2h_{v}^{2}\nabla F(x)+4vh_{v}\nabla^{2}F(x)h_{x}+v^{2}D^{3}F(x)[h_{x}]^{2}\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-DPsi2b})_{\;}}}{{=}}\sigma^{\prime\prime}.

In view of relation (
3.8
), this means that
\sigma^{\prime\prime}\in-K^{*}
. Consequently, by Theorem on Recession Direction, we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\langle\nabla^{2}F_{*}(s(z))\sigma^{\prime\prime},\sigma^{\prime\prime}\rangle^{1/2}&\leq&\langle\nabla F_{*}(s(z)),\sigma^{\prime\prime}\rangle.\end{array}

(4.24)

In paricular, this implies that function
\Psi(\cdot)
is convex.

Finally, for the third derivative of function
\Psi(\cdot)
, we have

D^{3}\Psi(z)[\hat{h}]^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-DPsi2})_{1}}}{{=}}D^{3}F_{*}(s(z))[\sigma^{\prime}]^{3}+3\langle\nabla^{2}F_{*}(s(z))\sigma^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime\prime}\rangle+\langle\nabla F_{*}(s(z)),\sigma^{\prime\prime\prime}\rangle.

(4.25)

Since
D\Big(h_{v}x-vh_{x}\Big)[\hat{h}]=0
, we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\sigma^{\prime\prime\prime}&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-FOmega2})}}{{=}}&D^{4}F(x)[h_{x}][h_{v}x-vh_{x}]^{2}.\end{array}

Let us come back to the function
\Phi
, we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\delta_{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}D^{2}\Phi(z)[\hat{h}]^{2}&=&\langle\nabla^{2}F_{*}(s(z))\sigma^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime}\rangle+\langle\nabla F_{*}(s(z)),\sigma^{\prime\prime}\rangle+\langle\nabla^{2}F(x)h_{x},h_{x}\rangle\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}&a^{2}+b+r^{2}.\end{array}

Denote
\tilde{x}=h_{v}x-vh_{x}
. In view of Assumption
1
, we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\sigma^{\prime\prime\prime}=D^{4}F(x)[h_{x}][\tilde{x}]^{2}&\succeq_{K^{*}}&3rD^{3}F(x)[\tilde{x}]^{2}\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-FOmega2})}}{{=}}\;3r\sigma^{\prime\prime}.\end{array}

Therefore,

\displaystyle{}\delta_{3}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}D^{3}\Phi(z)[\hat{h}]^{3}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-DPsi3})}}{{\leq}}

\displaystyle D^{3}F_{*}(s(z))[\sigma^{\prime}]^{3}+3\langle\nabla^{2}F_{*}(s(z))\sigma^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle 3r\langle\nabla F_{*}(s(z)),\sigma^{\prime\prime}\rangle+D^{3}F(x)[h_{x}]^{3}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{def-SCF})}}{{\leq}}

\displaystyle 2r^{3}+2a^{3}+3a\langle\nabla F_{*}(s(z))\sigma^{\prime\prime},\sigma^{\prime\prime}\rangle^{1/2}+3rb

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-RDir})}}{{\leq}}

\displaystyle 2r^{3}+2a^{3}+3ab+3rb=(a+r)(2(a^{2}+r^{2}-ar)+3b).

In view of definition of
\delta_{2}
, since

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\delta_{3}&\leq&(a+r)(2\delta_{2}-2ar+b)\;=\;(\delta_{2}-b+2ar)^{1/2}(2\delta_{2}-2ar+b).\end{array}

Denoting

5
Multiconic optimization and target-following approach

In this paper, we consider optimization problems with variables from linear vector spaces with explicit

multiplex structure
:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathbb{E}&=&\mathbb{E}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathbb{E}_{n}.\end{array}

Thus, for
x\in\mathbb{E}
, we have a natural decomposition

\begin{array}[]{rcl}x&=&(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}),\quad x_{i}\in\mathbb{E}_{i},\quad i=1,\dots,n.\end{array}

Similarly, any linear function
g\in\mathbb{E}^{*}=\mathbb{E}_{1}^{*}\times\dots\times\mathbb{E}^{*}_{n}
, can be decomposed as follows:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}g&=&(g_{1},\dots,g_{n}),\quad g_{i}\in\mathbb{E}_{i}^{*},\quad i=1,\dots,n.\end{array}

In such spaces, we consider a problem of
Multiconic Optimization
, where the primal feasible cone
K\subset\mathbb{E}
is formed as a direct product of
n
symmetric cones satisfying Assumption
1
:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}K&=&K_{1}\times\dots\times K_{n}.\end{array}

Note that Assumption
1
holds for the Lorentz cone and positive semidefinite cone, as well, see Examples
6
and
7
. Using (
3.8
),
if Assumption
1
holds for the cones
K_{i}
, then it holds for the product of the cones
K_{i}
.

Thus, the standard problem of
Multiconic Optimization
looks as follows:

\begin{array}[]{c}\min\limits_{x\in K}\Big\{\langle c,x\rangle:\;\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}(x_{i})=b\Big\},\end{array}

(5.1)

where
b\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
A_{i}:\mathbb{E}_{i}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
i=1,\dots,n
. We assume that for all
i=1,\ldots,n
,
A_{i}
have full column-rank. Thus, we can write the dual problem as follows:

\max\limits_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\Big\{\langle b,y\rangle:\;s_{i}+A^{*}_{i}(y)=c_{i},\;i=1,\dots,n\Big\}.

(5.2)

Denote
{\cal F}_{p}=\{x\in{\rm int\,}K:\;A(x)=b\}
and
{\cal F}_{d}=\{(y,s)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\times{\rm int\,}K^{*}:\;s+A^{*}(y)=c\}
, where

{A(x):\mathbb{E}\to\mathbb{R}^{m},\quad A(x)\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}(x_{i})}

and
A^{*}(y)=\left(A^{*}_{1}(y),\dots,A^{*}_{n}(y)\right)\in\mathbb{E}^{*}
. We assume that
A
has full row-rank.

Example 8
.

Let
K_{i}=\mathbb{S}^{p_{i}}_{+}
,
p_{i}\geq 1
, and
A_{i,j}\in\mathbb{S}^{p_{i}}
for
i=1,\dots,n
and
j=1,\dots,m
. Then, for any
1\leq i\leq n
, we can define the components of
A_{i}(x_{i})
as

\begin{array}[]{rcl}A_{i}^{(j)}(x_{i})&=&\langle A_{i,j},x_{i}\rangle,\quad j=1,\dots,m.\end{array}

In this case,
A^{*}_{i}(y)=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m}A_{i,j}y^{(j)}\in\mathbb{S}^{p_{i}}
.

\hfill\Box

In what follows, we always assume that the problems (
5.1
)-(
5.2
) have a strictly feasible primal-dual point
(\hat{x},\hat{s},\hat{y})
such that

\hat{x}\in{\rm int\,}K,\quad\hat{s}\in{\rm int\,}K^{*},\quad A(\hat{x})=b,\quad\hat{s}+A^{*}(\hat{y})=c.

(5.3)

For all symmetric cones
K_{i}
, we know the self-scaled barriers
F^{i}(\cdot)
with parameters
\nu_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,n
.
Our approach for solving the problems (
5.1
)-(
5.2
) can be seen as an extension of the
Parabolic Target Space
technique (
[
4
,
5
,
19
]
). It is based on the following lemma.

Lemma 5.1
.

For any
\bar{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{++}
, there exists a unique
u(\bar{v})=(x(\bar{v}),y(\bar{v}),s(\bar{v}))\in{\cal F}_{p}\times{\cal F}_{d}

such that

\begin{array}[]{rcl}s_{i}(\bar{v})&=&-\bar{v}^{(i)}\;\nabla F^{i}(\bar{x}_{i}),\quad i=1,\dots,n.\end{array}

(5.4)

Proof.

Indeed, denote
F_{\bar{v}}(x)=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\bar{v}^{(i)}F^{i}(x)
and consider the problem

\min\limits_{x:\,Ax=b}\Big\{\langle c,x\rangle+F_{\bar{v}}(x)\Big\}.

(5.5)

Note that for all feasible
x
, we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\langle c,x\rangle+F_{\bar{v}}(x)&\geq&\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\Big[\langle c_{i},x\rangle-\langle\hat{s}_{i},x\rangle-F_{*}^{i}(\hat{s}_{i})\Big]\\
\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{ass-Strict})}}{{=}}&\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\Big[\langle c_{i},x\rangle-\langle c_{i}-A^{*}_{i}(\hat{y}),x\rangle-F_{*}^{i}(\hat{s}_{i})\Big]\;=\;\langle b,\hat{y}\rangle-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}F^{i}_{*}(\hat{s}_{i}).\end{array}

Thus, the objective function of problem (
5.5
) is below bounded. Since the function
F_{\bar{v}}(\cdot)
is self-concordant, we conclude that the point

\bar{x}=\arg\min\limits_{x\in{\cal F}_{p}}\Big\{\langle c,x\rangle+F_{\bar{v}}(x)\Big\}
does exist. From the first-order optimality condition, we know that there exists a vector of dual multipliers
\bar{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that

\begin{array}[]{rcl}c_{i}+\bar{v}^{(i)}\;\nabla F^{i}(\bar{x}_{i})&=&A^{*}_{i}(\bar{y}),\quad i=1,\dots,n.\end{array}

Hence,
\bar{s}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}c-A^{*}(\bar{y})\in{\rm int\,}K^{*}
and it satisfies relations (
5.4
).

\hfill\Box

In Section
4
, we introduced a new type of self-concordant function using the hyperbolic coupling. More precisely, Theorem
4.7
shows that we have the following self-concordant function for each cone:

\Phi_{i}(x_{i},s_{i},v^{(i)})=F^{i}_{*}\Big(s_{i}+(v^{(i)})^{2}\nabla F^{i}(x_{i})\Big)+F^{i}(x_{i}),\quad i=1,\dots,n.

Let us introduce the control variable
w=(v_{0},v)\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
and our main variables
u=(x,y,s)\in\mathbb{E}\times\mathbb{E}^{*}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
Combining the self-concordant functions
\Phi_{i}
we obtain the the following barrier function:

\hat{F}(u,w)\;=\;\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\Phi_{i}\Big(x_{i},s_{i},v^{(i)}\Big)-\ln(v_{0}-\langle c,x\rangle+\langle b,y\rangle).

Denote by
{\cal F}={\rm dom\,}\hat{F}
and

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\varphi(w)&=&\min\limits_{u\in{\cal F}_{p}\times{\cal F}_{d}}\{\hat{F}(u,w):(u,w)\in{\cal F}\}.\end{array}

(5.6)

Let
\nu\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\nu_{i}
and

\|v\|^{2}_{\nu}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\nu_{i}(v^{(i)})^{2}
for
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Theorem 5.2
.

We have
\varphi(w)=-(\nu+1)\ln\rho(w)-\nu
with
\rho(w)\;=\;{v_{0}-\|v\|^{2}_{\nu}\over\nu+1}
for all

\begin{array}[]{rcl}w&\in&{\rm dom\,}\varphi=\Big\{(v_{0},v)\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}:\;v_{0}>\|v\|^{2}_{\nu}\Big\}.\end{array}

The optimal solution
u^{*}(w)=(x^{*}(w),y^{*}(w),s^{*}(w))
of (
5.6
) satisfies the equations

\displaystyle s^{*}_{i}(w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\bar{v}^{(i)}(w)\;\nabla F^{i}(x^{*}_{i}(w)),

\displaystyle\bar{v}^{(i)}(w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(v^{(i)})^{2}+\rho(w),\quad i=1,\dots,n.

Proof.

Let us choose
\bar{v}>v^{2}
and
v_{0}>\|v\|^{2}_{\nu}
. Then, by Lemma
5.1
, there exists
u(\bar{v})
satisfying the relations (
5.4
). Therefore,

\displaystyle\Phi_{i}(x_{i}(\bar{v}),s_{i}(\bar{v}),v^{(i)})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle F^{i}_{*}\Big(-\bar{v}^{(i)}\nabla F^{i}(x_{i}(\bar{v}))+(v^{(i)})^{2}\nabla F^{i}(x_{i}(\bar{v}))\Big)+F^{i}(x_{i}(\bar{v}))

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{def-HomB})}}{{=}}

\displaystyle-\nu_{i}\ln\left(\bar{v}^{(i)}-(v^{(i)})^{2}\right)+F^{i}_{*}\Big(-\nabla F^{i}(x_{i}(\bar{v}))\Big)+F^{i}(x_{i}(\bar{v}))

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-HomFX})_{1}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle-\nu_{i}\ln\left(\bar{v}^{(i)}-(v^{(i)})^{2}\right)-\nu_{i}.

At the same time,
\langle c,x(\bar{v})\rangle-\langle b,y(\bar{v})\rangle=\langle s(\bar{v}),x(\bar{v})\rangle=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\langle s_{i}(\bar{v}),x_{i}(\bar{v})\rangle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-HomFX})_{1}}}{{=}}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\nu_{i}\bar{v}^{(i)}
. Thus,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}F(u(\bar{v}),w)&=&-\nu-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\nu_{i}\ln\Big(\bar{v}^{(i)}-\left(v^{(i)}\right)^{2}\Big)-\ln\Big(v_{0}-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\nu_{i}\bar{v}^{(i)}\Big).\end{array}

This expression is minimal for optimal
\bar{v}^{(i)}
satisfying the following equation:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\bar{v}^{(i)}-\left(v^{(i)}\right)^{2}&=&\Big[v_{0}-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\nu_{i}\bar{v}^{(i)}\Big].\end{array}

These optimal values are as follows:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\bar{v}^{(i)}&=&\left(v^{(i)}\right)^{2}+\rho(w),\quad i=1,\dots,n,\end{array}

where
\rho(w)={v_{0}-\|v\|^{2}_{\nu}\over\nu+1}
.
In this case,
\langle s(\bar{v}),x(\bar{v})\rangle={\nu v_{0}+\|v\|^{2}_{\nu}\over\nu+1}
, and we conclude that

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\varphi(w)&\leq&-\nu-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\nu_{i}\ln\rho(w)-\ln\rho(w)\;=\;-(\nu+1)\ln\rho(w)-\nu.\end{array}

On the other hand, we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\Phi_{i}(x_{i},s_{i},v^{(i)})&=&F^{i}_{*}\Big(s_{i}+(v^{(i)})^{2}\nabla F^{i}(x_{i})\Big)+F^{i}(x_{i})\\
\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-HFenTwo})}}{{\geq}}&-\nu_{i}\ln\langle s_{i}+(v^{(i)})^{2}\nabla F^{i}(x_{i}),x_{i}\rangle-\nu_{i}+\nu_{i}\ln\nu_{i}\\
\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-HomFX})_{1}}}{{=}}&-\nu_{i}\ln\Big[{1\over\nu_{i}}\langle s_{i},x_{i}\rangle-(v^{(i)})^{2}\Big]-\nu_{i}.\end{array}

Denoting by
\tau_{i}={1\over\nu_{i}}\langle s_{i},x_{i}\rangle
, we get

\begin{array}[]{rcl}F(u,w)&\geq&-\nu-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\nu_{i}\ln\Big(\tau_{i}-(v^{(i)})^{2}\Big)-\ln\Big(v_{0}-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\nu_{i}\tau_{i}\Big).\end{array}

As we have seen, the minimum of the right-hand side of this inequality in
\tau
is equal to

\begin{array}[]{c}-(\nu+1)\ln\rho(w)-\nu.\hskip 21.52771pt\Box\end{array}

6
Computing the Search Directions

We consider the primal-dual formulation of the multiconic problem from Section
5
:

\displaystyle{}\min_{u=(x,y,s)}\langle c,x\rangle

\displaystyle-\langle b,y\rangle

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}(x_{i})

\displaystyle=b,

\displaystyle s_{i}+A_{i}^{*}(y)

\displaystyle=c_{i},\quad y\in\mathbb{R}^{m},

\displaystyle\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0ptx_{i}\in K_{i},\;s_{i}\in

\displaystyle K_{i}^{*},\;i=1,\ldots,n.

(6.1)

Note that we can eliminate the variables
s_{i}
by using
s_{i}=c_{i}-A_{i}^{*}(y)
,
i=1,\ldots,n
. Let
\tilde{u}=(x,y)
and consider

\tilde{F}(\tilde{u},w)=\hat{F}(x,c-A^{*}(y),y,w)
.

As in Section 3 of
[
19
]
, for the computation of the predictor search direction, firstly we update
w
. After that, we want to determine
\tilde{u}(w)=(x(w),y(w))
, which is the unique optimum point of

\displaystyle\varphi(w)=\min\limits_{\tilde{u}}\Big\{\tilde{F}(\tilde{u},w):

\displaystyle\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}(x_{i})=b,

\displaystyle x_{i}\in K_{i},\;c_{i}-A_{i}^{*}(y)\in K_{i}^{*},\;i=1,\ldots,n,\;y\in\mathbb{R}^{m},\;(\tilde{u},w)\in\cal{\tilde{F}}\Big\},

where
\cal{\tilde{F}}={\rm dom\,}\tilde{F}
.
Hence, considering the first order optimality conditions of this problem we have

\displaystyle{}\nabla_{\tilde{u}}\tilde{F}(\tilde{u}(w),w)+A^{*}\lambda(w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

\displaystyle Ax(w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle b,

where
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
denotes the Lagrange multiplier.
From here we get the following system for the Jacobian
\tilde{u}^{\prime}(w)=(x^{\prime}(w),y^{\prime}(w))
:

\displaystyle{}\nabla^{2}_{\tilde{u}\tilde{u}}\tilde{F}(\tilde{u}(w),w)\tilde{u}^{\prime}(w)+\nabla^{2}_{\tilde{u}w}\tilde{F}(\tilde{u}(w),w)+A^{*}\lambda^{\prime}(w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

\displaystyle Ax^{\prime}(w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.

(6.2)

We use the following simplified notations:

H_{xx}=\nabla_{xx}^{2}\tilde{F}(x(w),y(w),w),\;H_{xy}=\nabla_{xy}^{2}\tilde{F}(x(w),y(w),w),

and

H_{yy}=\nabla_{yy}^{2}\tilde{F}(x(w),y(w),w).

Moreover, let

T_{x}=\nabla_{xw}^{2}\tilde{F}(x(w),y(w),w),\quad T_{y}=\nabla_{yw}^{2}\tilde{F}(x(w),y(w),w).

Therefore, (
6
) is equivalent to the following system:

\displaystyle{}H_{xx}x^{\prime}(w)+H_{xy}y^{\prime}(w)+T_{x}+A^{*}\lambda^{\prime}(w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

\displaystyle H_{yx}x^{\prime}(w)+H_{yy}y^{\prime}(w)+T_{y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

\displaystyle Ax^{\prime}(w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.

(6.3)

From the first equation of system (
6
) we have

{}x^{\prime}(w)=-H_{xx}^{-1}\left(H_{xy}y^{\prime}(w)+T_{x}+A^{*}\lambda^{\prime}(w)\right).

(6.4)

Hence, using this and the third equation of (
6
), we have

\displaystyle{}AH_{xx}^{-1}\left(H_{xy}y^{\prime}(w)+T_{x}+A^{*}\lambda^{\prime}(w)\right)=0.

(6.5)

This means that

\lambda^{\prime}(w)=-\left(AH_{xx}^{-1}A^{*}\right)^{-1}AH_{xx}^{-1}\left(H_{xy}y^{\prime}(w)+T_{x}\right)

and

\displaystyle{}H_{yy}y^{\prime}(w)+T_{y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle H_{yx}H_{xx}^{-1}\left(H_{xy}y^{\prime}(w)+T_{x}+A^{*}\lambda^{\prime}(w)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle H_{yx}H_{xx}^{-1}\left[H_{xy}y^{\prime}(w)+T_{x}-A^{*}\left(AH_{xx}^{-1}A^{*}\right)^{-1}AH_{xx}^{-1}\left(H_{xy}y^{\prime}(w)+T_{x}\right)\right].

Thus, we have to solve the following system:

\displaystyle{}\Big[H_{yy}-H_{yx}\Big(H_{xx}^{-1}-H_{xx}^{-1}A^{*}

\displaystyle\left(AH_{xx}^{-1}A^{*}\right)^{-1}AH_{xx}^{-1}\Big)H_{xy}\Big]y^{\prime}(w)

\displaystyle=H_{yx}\left[H_{xx}^{-1}-H_{xx}^{-1}A^{*}\left(AH_{xx}^{-1}A^{*}\right)^{-1}AH_{xx}^{-1}\right]T_{x}-T_{y}.

From here, we can see that for the computation of the search directions, we need to invert the operator

H_{xx}
and the following matrices:

AH_{xx}^{-1}A^{*}\text{ and }H_{yy}-H_{yx}\left(H_{xx}^{-1}-H_{xx}^{-1}A^{*}\left(AH_{xx}^{-1}A^{*}\right)^{-1}AH_{xx}^{-1}\right)H_{xy},

that are matrices of size
m\times m
. The first matrix is invertible, because
H_{xx}
is positive definite and the operator
A
has full row rank. The second matrix is invertible, because it is the sum of two positive definite matrices:

•

H_{yy}-H_{yx}H_{xx}^{-1}H_{xy}
, the Schur-complement of the positive definite operator
H
;

•

H_{yx}H_{xx}^{-1}A^{*}\left(AH_{xx}^{-1}A^{*}\right)^{-1}AH_{xx}^{-1}H_{xy}
, which is the transformation of the positive definite matrix
\left(AH_{xx}^{-1}A^{*}\right)^{-1}
by the
m\times m
-matrix
H_{yx}H_{xx}^{-1}A^{*}
.

Since we are not exactly at
(u(w),w)
, we solve system (
6
) using the Hessian
\nabla^{2}_{\tilde{u}\tilde{u}}\tilde{F}(\tilde{u},w)
and the Jacobian
\nabla^{2}_{\tilde{u}w}\tilde{F}(\tilde{u},w)
. By
U(\tilde{u},w)=(X\left(\tilde{u},w\right),Y\left(\tilde{u},w\right))
we denote the solution of the following system:

\displaystyle{}\nabla^{2}_{\tilde{u}\tilde{u}}\tilde{F}(\tilde{u},w)U(\tilde{u},w)+\nabla^{2}_{\tilde{u}w}\tilde{F}(\tilde{u},w)+A^{*}\Lambda(\tilde{u},w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

\displaystyle AX\left(\tilde{u},w\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.

Hence, the predictor search direction is
\Delta_{p}\tilde{u}=U(\tilde{u},w)\Delta w.

For our method, we do not need to compute the whole matrix
U(\tilde{u},w)
. Instead, we can find directly
\Delta_{p}\tilde{u}=(\Delta_{p}\tilde{x},\Delta_{p}\tilde{y})
from the following system:

\displaystyle{}\nabla^{2}_{\tilde{u}\tilde{u}}\tilde{F}(\tilde{u},w)\Delta_{p}\tilde{u}+\tilde{d}+A^{*}\Delta_{p}\tilde{\lambda}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

\displaystyle A\Delta_{p}\tilde{x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

(6.6)

where
\tilde{d}=\nabla^{2}_{\tilde{u}w}\tilde{F}(\tilde{u},w)\Delta w
.

In the corrector step, we fix
w
and aim to minimize the functional proximity measure in
u
. For this purpose, in the corrector step we use the Newton direction
\Delta_{c}\tilde{u}=(\Delta_{c}x,\Delta_{c}y)
, which is the solution of the following system:

\displaystyle{}\nabla^{2}_{\tilde{u}\tilde{u}}\tilde{F}(\tilde{u},w)\Delta_{c}\tilde{u}+\nabla_{\tilde{u}}\tilde{F}(\tilde{u},w)+A^{*}\lambda

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

\displaystyle A\Delta_{c}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.

(6.7)

Note that this has exactly the same structure as system (
6
). For this reason, we need to invert matrices of the size similar to the predictor step.

In the following example we show that in the SDP case the matrices and operators needed for the search directions are easily computable.

Example 9
.

In the case of semidefinite optimization, using the notations in Example
8

K=\mathbb{S}_{+}^{p_{1}}\times\ldots\times\mathbb{S}_{+}^{p_{n}}
. We have
s_{i}=c_{i}-\sum_{j=1}^{m}A_{i,j}y^{(j)}\in\mathbb{S}^{p_{i}}_{+}
and let
\bar{x}_{i}=x_{i}+(v^{i})^{2}\nabla F^{i}_{*}(s_{i})
,
i=1,\ldots,n
. Then,

\langle g,H_{xx}g\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle g_{i},\nabla^{2}F^{i}(\bar{x}_{i})g_{i}\rangle+\frac{\langle c,g\rangle^{2}}{(v_{0}-\langle c,x\rangle+\langle b,y\rangle)^{2}},\text{ for }g\in K.

Observe that the inversion of
H_{xx}
is not expensive, because it is the sum of a block diagonal and a one-rank operator. As it is shown above, the blocks correspond to the cones. Therefore, we can use the Sherman–Morrison formula.

\displaystyle{}\langle g,H_{xx}^{-1}g\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle g_{i},[\nabla^{2}F^{i}(\bar{x}_{i})]^{-1}g_{i}\rangle

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle c_{i}[\nabla^{2}F^{i}(\bar{x}_{i})]^{-1},g_{i}\rangle^{2}}{(v_{0}-\langle c,x\rangle+\langle b,y\rangle)^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle c_{i}[\nabla^{2}F^{i}(\bar{x}_{i})]^{-1},c_{i}\rangle}.

Furthermore,
AH_{xx}^{-1}A^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
is invertible, where for any
\ell,k=1,\ldots,m
we have

\displaystyle(AH_{xx}^{-1}A^{*})_{\ell,k}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}

\displaystyle\langle A_{\ell,i},[\nabla^{2}F^{i}(\bar{x}_{i})]^{-1}A_{k,i}\rangle

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\;\langle c_{i},[\nabla^{2}F^{i}(\bar{x}_{i})]^{-1}A_{\ell,i}\rangle\;\langle c_{j},[\nabla^{2}F^{j}(\bar{x}_{j})]^{-1}A_{k,j}\rangle}{(v_{0}-\langle c,x\rangle+\langle b,y\rangle)^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\,\langle c_{i},[\nabla^{2}F^{i}(\bar{x}_{i})]^{-1}c_{i}\rangle}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}

\displaystyle\langle A_{\ell,i},\bar{x}_{i}A_{k,i}\bar{x}_{i}\rangle-\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\;\langle c_{i},\bar{x}_{i}A_{\ell,i}\bar{x}_{i}\rangle\;\langle c_{j},\bar{x}_{j}A_{k,j}\bar{x}_{j}\rangle}{(v_{0}-\langle c,x\rangle+\langle b,y\rangle)^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\,\langle c_{i},\bar{x}_{i}c_{i}\bar{x}_{i}\rangle}.

The matrix
H_{yx}H_{xx}^{-1}A^{*}
can be computed in a similar way.

Note that the case when
p_{i}=1
,
i=1,\ldots,n
corresponds to the LP problem. If all
p_{i}
are small, then the computational cost of an iteration mainly depends on the number of constraints
m
and the number of cones
n
.

In accordance to Theorem

\displaystyle\Omega(u,w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{F}(u,w)-\varphi(w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\Phi_{i}(u_{i},v^{(i)})-\ln(v_{0}-\langle c,x\rangle+\langle b,y\rangle)+(\nu+1)\ln\rho(w)+\nu.

It allows to construct

Our algorithm is a sequence of interchanging Corrector and Predictor Stages, in accordance to the threshold values

•

At the Corrector Stage, we fix the control variable
w
and minimize the proximity measure
\Omega(u,w)
in

, up to the moment it becomes smaller than
\beta_{1}
. This minimization is done by a Damped Newton Method as applied to the function
\Omega(\cdot,w)
. The corresponding search direction is computed by the linear system (

When we are outside of the region of quadratic convergence, each iteration of the Damped Newton Method decreases the value of self-concordant function by an absolute constant. Hence, the upper bound on the total number of the corrector steps cannot be bigger than a constant proportional to the initial value of the proximity measure.

•

The Predictor Stage consists in a single predictor step, which is performed from a small neighborhood of the central path. Following the framework of
[
5
]
, we prove that this step reduces the following merit function:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mu^{*}(w)&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}&{\underline{\alpha}(w)\over\underline{\alpha}(w)-1}v_{0}={v_{0}^{2}\over v_{0}-\|v\|^{2}_{\nu}}>v_{0},\quad\underline{\alpha}(w)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}{v_{0}\over\|v\|^{2}_{\nu}}>1.\end{array}

(7.1)

8
Initialization of control variables

For starting our predictor-corrector scheme, we need to initialize the control variable
w
. Suppose we have a point
u=(x,y,s)\in{\rm rint\,}{\cal F}
, which can be extended up to the variable
\hat{z}=(v_{0},z)
with

and

,

. In this extension, it is natural to use the control variable

, which minimizes the value of proximity measure

.

Hence, we need to solve the following minimization problem:

\min\limits_{v_{0},v}\Big\{\Omega(\hat{z})=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\Phi_{i}(x_{i},s_{i},v^{(i)})-\ln(v_{0}-\langle s,x\rangle)+(\nu+1)\ln{v_{0}-\|v\|_{\nu}^{2}\over\nu+1}+\nu\Big\}

Note that

and

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\Omega^{\prime}_{v_{0}}(\hat{z})&=&-{1\over v_{0}-\langle s,x\rangle}+{\nu+1\over v_{0}-\|v\|_{\nu}^{2}}\;=\;{\nu v_{0}+\|v\|_{\nu}^{2}-(\nu+1)\langle s,x\rangle\over(v_{0}-\langle s,x\rangle)(v_{0}-\|v\|_{\nu}^{2})}.\end{array}

Therefore, the unique minimum in
v_{0}
is achieved at

. Thus,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\Omega(v_{0}^{*},z)&=&\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\Big[F_{*}^{i}\left(s_{i}+(v^{(i)})^{2}\nabla F^{i}(x_{i})\right)+F^{i}(x_{i})\Big]+\nu\ln{\langle s,x\rangle-\|v\|_{\nu}^{2}\over\nu}+\nu.\end{array}

Note that for
v=0
, we get the classical functional proximity measure, which estimates the distance from
u
to the main central path:

\begin{array}[]{c}\Omega(v_{0}^{*},z(x,s,0))\;=\;\Omega_{K}(x,s)\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\;\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\Big[F_{*}^{i}(s_{i})+F^{i}(x_{i})\Big]+\nu\ln{\langle s,x\rangle\over\nu}+\nu\\
\\
=\;\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\Big[F_{*}^{i}(s_{i})+F^{i}(x_{i})+\nu_{i}\ln{\langle s_{i},x_{i}\rangle\over\nu_{i}}+\nu_{i}\Big]-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\nu_{i}\ln{\langle s_{i},x_{i}\rangle\over\nu_{i}}+\nu\ln{\langle s,x\rangle\over\nu}\\
\\
=\;\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\Omega_{K_{i}}(x_{i},s_{i})+\beta(x,s),\end{array}

where
\beta(x,s)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\nu_{i}\ln{\langle s_{i},x_{i}\rangle\over\nu_{i}}+\nu\ln{\langle s,x\rangle\over\nu}\geq 0
is non-negative in view of convexity of function

.

By choosing an appropriate

, we could try to find a deviated central path which is much closer to the starting point

. From inequality (
2.13
), we can see that the distance to the main central path is zero if and only if

\begin{array}[]{rcl}s_{i}&=&-\mu\nabla F^{i}(x_{i}),\quad i=1,\dots,n,\end{array}

(8.1)

for certain

.

Let us show how we can choose an appropriate

. Since all variables except

\begin{array}[]{c}\min\limits_{\bar{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}}\Big\{\psi(\bar{v})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\Big[F_{*}^{i}(s_{i}+\bar{v}^{(i)}\nabla F^{i}(x_{i}))+F^{i}(x_{i})\Big]+\nu\ln{\langle s,x\rangle-\langle d,\bar{v}\rangle\over\nu}+\nu\Big\},\end{array}

(8.2)

where
\bar{v}^{(i)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}(v^{(i)})^{2}

, and

Note that the first-order optimality condition of problem (

Remark 8.1

Note that the last two terms in

\Omega(u^{s},w^{s})=0
. Let us assume that our starting point satisfies

{}s_{i}=-\mu_{i}\nabla F^{i}(x_{i}),\text{ with some }\mu_{i}>0\text{ for all }i=1,\dots,n.

(8.11)

This means that
\langle s_{i},x_{i}\rangle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-HomFX})_{1}}}{{=}}\mu_{i}\nu_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,n
. Furthermore, using (

), (

) and (

\displaystyle{}\Psi_{i}(\bar{v}_{i},\gamma)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle F_{i}^{*}(-(\mu_{i}-\bar{v}_{i})\nabla F_{i}(x_{i}))+F_{i}(x_{i})+\gamma(\nu_{i}\mu_{i}-\nu_{i}\bar{v}_{i})-\nu\ln\gamma

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nu_{i}\big[-1+\gamma(\mu_{i}-\bar{v}_{i})-\ln\left(\gamma(\mu_{i}-\bar{v}_{i})\right)\big]

Note that
g_{i}(\gamma)
is the minimum of
\Psi_{i}(\bar{v}_{i},\gamma)
in
\bar{v}_{i}
. The optimum point of this is

\bar{v}_{i}(\gamma)=\mu_{i}-\frac{1}{\gamma}.

(8.12)

Therefore,
g_{i}(\gamma)\equiv 0
for all

and
\gamma>0
. For any

, the nonnegative values
\bar{v}_{i}(\gamma^{*})
by (

) define control variable

given in (

.

Remark 8.2

In the case of linear programming we have

9
Conclusion and future research

We proposed a new interior-point algorithm for solving multiconic optimization problems using the parabolic target space approach. We introduced a new concept of hyperbolic coupling and presented the main steps of the complexity analysis of the algorithm. We proposed a new and efficient procedure for computing the starting values of control variables. For the future research, it would be interesting to investigate the possibilities for the local quadratic convergence of the method. Furthermore, our aim is to prove Assumption
1
in general for all symmetric cones.

Appendix: Computation of Scaling Point for Lorentz Cone

In this section, for the case
K={\cal L}_{n}
, we show how to compute the scaling point
w\in{\rm int\,}K
, which connects two points, the primal point
x\in{\rm int\,}K
and the dual point
s\in{\rm int\,}K^{*}
:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}s&=&\nabla^{2}F(w)x.\end{array}

In accordance to
[
11
]
, this point is a unique solution of the following convex optimization problem:

\min\limits_{w\in{\rm int\,}K}\Big\{\langle s,w\rangle-\langle\nabla F(w),x\rangle\Big\}.

(10.1)

Since
\nabla F(\tau w)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-Hom12})_{1}}}{{=}}{1\over\tau}\nabla F(w)
for any
\tau>0
, in this problem, we can replace
w
by
\tau w
and minimize the resulting objective in
\tau
. Then we come to the problem where the objective function is homogeneous of degree zero:

\min\limits_{w\in{\rm int\,}K}2\Big[\langle s,w\rangle\cdot\langle-\nabla F(w),x\rangle\Big]^{1/2}.

Therefore, we can add the constraint
\langle s,w\rangle=\langle s,x\rangle
to the minimization problem. The optimal point
w^{*}
of problem (
10.1
) can be represented as follows:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}w^{*}&=&\tau_{*}w_{*},\quad\tau_{*}\;=\;\sqrt{\langle-\nabla F(w_{*}),x\rangle\over\langle s,x\rangle},\\
\\
w_{*}&=&\arg\min\limits_{w\in{\rm int\,}K}\Big\{\langle-\nabla F(w),x\rangle:\;\langle s,w\rangle=\langle s,x\rangle\Big\}.\end{array}

(10.2)

Let us show that for the Lorentz cone the latter problem can be solved in a closed form.
Indeed, in this case our variable is
w=(w_{0},w_{1})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
and

\begin{array}[]{rcl}F(w)&=&-\ln\omega(w),\quad\omega(w)\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\;w_{0}^{2}-\|w_{1}\|^{2}.\end{array}

Then, the objective function of the problem (
10.1
) is as follows:

s_{0}w_{0}+\langle s_{1},w_{1}\rangle+{2\over\omega(w)}[w_{0}x_{0}-\langle w_{1},x_{1}\rangle].

Hence, the first-order optimality condition for this problem have the following form:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}s_{0}\\
s_{1}\end{array}\right)+{2\over\omega(w)}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}x_{0}\\
-x_{1}\end{array}\right)-{4\over\omega^{2}(w)}[w_{0}x_{0}-\langle w_{1},x_{1}\rangle]\left(\begin{array}[]{c}w_{0}\\
-w_{1}\end{array}\right)&=&0.\end{array}

Since
x
and
w
are in the interior of the cone
{\cal L}_{n}
, we have
w_{0}x_{0}-\langle w_{1},x_{1}\rangle>0
. Hence, the above equation can be written in the following form:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}w_{0}&=&{\omega^{2}(w)\over 4[w_{0}x_{0}-\langle w_{1},x_{1}\rangle]}s_{0}+{\omega(w)\over 2[w_{0}x_{0}-\langle w_{1},x_{1}\rangle]}x_{0},\\
\\
w_{1}&=&{-\omega^{2}(w)\over 4[w_{0}x_{0}-\langle w_{1},x_{1}\rangle]}s_{1}+{\omega(w)\over 2[w_{0}x_{0}-\langle w_{1},x_{1}\rangle]}x_{1}.\end{array}

In other words, the optimal solution of the problem (
10.1
) can be represented as follows:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}w^{*}_{0}&=&\alpha x_{0}+\beta s_{0},\quad w^{*}_{1}=\alpha x_{1}-\beta s_{1}\end{array}

for certain
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. The same is true for the optimal solution
w_{*}
of problem (
10.2
)
3
.

Thus, the optimization problem in (
10.2
)
3
is two-dimensional. Let us show that its solution can be found in a closed form. In accordance to (
10.2
)
3
, we need to solve the following problem:

\begin{array}[]{c}\min\limits_{\alpha,\beta\geq 0}\left\{2{x_{0}(\alpha x_{0}+\beta s_{0})-\langle\alpha x_{1}-\beta s_{1},x_{1}\rangle\over\omega(\alpha,\beta)}:\;s_{0}(\alpha x_{0}+\beta s_{0})+\langle s_{1},\alpha x_{1}-\beta s_{1}\rangle=\langle s,x\rangle\right\},\end{array}

where
\omega(\alpha,\beta)=(\alpha x_{0}+\beta s_{0})^{2}-\|\alpha x_{1}-\beta s_{1}\|^{2}
.
We use the notations

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\omega_{x}&=&x_{0}^{2}-\|x_{1}\|^{2},\quad\omega_{s}=s_{0}^{2}-\|s_{1}\|^{2},\quad\langle s,x\rangle=s_{0}x_{0}+\langle s_{1},x_{1}\rangle.\end{array}

Then our problem is as follows:

\begin{array}[]{c}\min\limits_{\alpha,\beta\geq 0}\left\{{\alpha\omega_{x}+\beta\langle s,x\rangle\over\alpha^{2}\omega_{x}+\beta^{2}\omega_{s}+2\alpha\beta\langle s,x\rangle}:\;\alpha\langle s,x\rangle+\beta\omega_{s}=\langle s,x\rangle\right\}.\end{array}

Let us substitute the value
\beta={\langle s,x\rangle\over\omega_{s}}(1-\alpha)
into objective function. Then

\displaystyle\alpha\omega_{x}+\beta\langle s,x\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha\omega_{x}+{\langle s,x\rangle^{2}\over\omega_{s}}(1-\alpha),

\displaystyle\alpha^{2}\omega_{x}+\beta^{2}\omega_{s}+2\alpha\beta\langle s,x\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha^{2}\omega_{x}+{\langle s,x\rangle^{2}\over\omega_{s}}(1-\alpha)^{2}+2\alpha(1-\alpha){\langle s,x\rangle^{2}\over\omega_{s}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha^{2}\omega_{x}+{\langle s,x\rangle^{2}\over\omega_{s}}(1-\alpha^{2}).

Denoting
\Delta=\langle s,x\rangle^{2}-\omega_{x}\omega_{s}
, we get the following objective function:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}f(\alpha)&=&{\langle s,x\rangle^{2}-\alpha\Delta\over\langle s,x\rangle^{2}-\alpha^{2}\Delta}.\end{array}

It is important that
\Delta>0

\displaystyle\Delta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[x_{0}s_{0}+\langle x_{1},s_{1}\rangle]^{2}-(x_{0}^{2}-\|x_{1}\|^{2})(s_{0}^{2}-\|s_{1}\|^{2})

\displaystyle\Big[\|x_{1}\|\,\|s_{1}\|+\langle x_{1},s_{1}\rangle\Big]\Big[2x_{0}s_{0}-\|x_{1}\|\,\|s_{1}\|+\langle x_{1},s_{1}\rangle\Big]+(x_{0}\|s_{1}\|-s_{0}\|x_{1}\|)^{2}\;>\;0,

where we used Cauchy-Schwartz inequality and
(x,s)\in\mathcal{L}

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{1\over\langle s,x\rangle^{2}-\alpha\Delta}&=&{2\alpha\over\langle s,x\rangle^{2}-\alpha^{2}\Delta}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\left(\alpha\over 1-\alpha\right)^{2}\;=\;{\langle s,x\rangle^{2}\over\omega_{x}\omega_{s}}.\end{array}

Thus, we come to the following rule:

\displaystyle\alpha_{*}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\langle s,x\rangle\over\langle s,x\rangle+[\omega_{x}\omega_{s}]^{1/2}},\quad\beta_{*}\;=\;{\langle s,x\rangle\over\omega_{s}}(1-\alpha_{*}),\quad\tau_{*}\;=\;\sqrt{{2\over\langle s,x\rangle}f(\alpha_{*})},

\displaystyle w^{*}_{0}

\begin{array}[]{rcl}f(\alpha_{*})&=&{\langle s,x\rangle^{2}(1-\alpha_{*})+\alpha_{*}\omega_{x}\omega_{s}\over\langle s,x\rangle^{2}(1-\alpha_{*}^{2})+\alpha_{*}^{2}\omega_{x}\omega_{s}}\;=\;{1\over 2\langle s,x\rangle\alpha_{*}}.\hskip 21.52771pt\Box\end{array}
