Finite-time transitions in optimal control and non-equilibrium relaxation
Jan Meibohm, Samuel Monter, Sarah A. M. Loos, Clemens Bechinger

Abstract
We theoretically and experimentally study finite-time optimal control of a colloidal particle steered through a spatially inhomogeneous environment, modeled by a position-dependent energetic cost at the final state. The competition between this state-dependent penalty and path-dependent dissipation gives rise to a sharp transition in the control strategy at a critical control duration. We further show that this transition can be linked to a dynamical phase transition in nonequilibrium relaxation after a quench, where the control cost maps onto the rate function governing rare trajectories.



Introduction

With ongoing miniaturization of technological devices, the optimization of noisy processes has become increasingly important. At small scales, strong fluctuations are unavoidable, and performance is constrained by energy dissipation. Similar constraints govern biological systems, such as molecular motors and microorganisms, which must operate efficiently under strong noise and finite-time conditions
Chi et al. 2024
;
Ju et al. 2025
;
Howard 2001
;
Jülicher et al. 1997
;
Soga et al. 2017
. A defining feature of such processes is that they occur over finite durations, leading to a fundamental trade-off between energetic cost and speed: faster protocols incur higher dissipation, whereas slower protocols reduce energetic cost at the expense of performance. Optimal control theory provides a natural framework to quantify and optimize such trade-off by identifying protocols that minimize a prescribed cost functional over a finite-time horizon
Fleming and Rishel 1975
;
Bechhoefer 2021
;
Alvarado et al. 2025
.

Even in simple settings, such as dragging colloidal particles or navigating active swimmers through fluctuating environments, optimal protocols can be nontrivial, with discontinuities arising at the protocol boundaries
Schmiedl and Seifert 2007
;
Loos et al. 2024
;
Colabrese et al. 2017
;
Garcia-Millan et al. 2025
;
Gupta et al. 2023
. In structured environments with spatially varying energetic cost, optimal control becomes further enriched: the optimization must specify not only how fast to drive the system, but also where to go, since the location of the endpoint is part of the optimization task. This competition between path-dependent dissipation and position-dependent energetic penalty gives rise to distinct optimal strategies.

Here we show that this trade-off leads to a finite-time transition between qualitatively distinct optimal protocols. For a broad class of control problems with nonconvex energetic penalties, we demonstrate theoretically and experimentally that a critical protocol duration separates a regime where it is optimal to accept an energetic penalty from one in which active steering towards less costly regions is favorable. For symmetric landscapes, this transition is accompanied by spontaneous symmetry breaking
Kappen 2005a
;
Kappen 2005b
of the optimal solution, reminiscent of a continuous phase transition.

Exploiting the connection between stochastic optimal control and large deviation theory
Fleming
;
Touchette 2009
;
Chetrite and Touchette 2015
, we further show that this control transition can be mapped onto a finite-time dynamical phase transition (FTDPT) in the relaxation dynamics of a freely diffusing stochastic system
Meibohm and Esposito 2022
;
Blom and Godec 2022
;
Meibohm and Esposito 2023
;
Vadakkayil et al. 2024
. In this mapping, the optimal control cost plays the role of a rate function, and the transition manifests as a nonanalyticity associated with a change in the dominant relaxation pathway, from rare fluctuations at short times to typical relaxation trajectories at longer times. While direct observation of such rare events requires exponentially many realizations, optimal control experiments access the same information through averages over controlled trajectories, thereby bypassing this sampling bottleneck. Our results thus establish optimal control as an experimentally accessible route to dynamical phase transitions and, more broadly, to rare event physics in mesoscopic systems. In a companion paper
Meibohm et al.
, we show that the finite-time control transition and its direct link to FTDPTs generalize to a much broader class of control problems.

Control problem

As a paradigmatic control scenario in a heterogeneous environment, we here consider the situation sketched in Fig.
1
. A microscopic particle (blue), suspended in a viscous fluid and subjected to thermal noise, is steered by a harmonic trap (red) whose time-dependent position
\lambda(t)
serves as control parameter. Starting in equilibrium at time
t=0
at position
x_{0}
, the particle moves with constant mean longitudinal velocity
v_{\parallel}
to the right and reaches an obstacle (green) after time
t_{f}
at distance
L=v_{\parallel}t_{f}
.
The obstacle is modeled by a structured energy landscape
V(x)
extending in the transverse direction with spatially varying cost. The average particle motion is fully determined by the trap position. While its longitudinal velocity
v_{\parallel}
is fixed, the transverse particle velocity
v_{\perp}(t)
is controlled to minimize a total cost comprising an energetic penalty
V(x_{f})
and the dissipated energy accumulated along the path.

As an illustrative example, we consider an overdamped particle dynamics in presence of
additive white noise
\xi(t)
, as described by the following Langevin equation

\displaystyle\dot{x}(t)=-\tau_{\text{p}}^{-1}[x(t)-\lambda(t)]+\sqrt{2k_{\text{B}}T/\gamma}\,\xi(t)\,,

(1)

which provides an accurate minimal model of our experimental setup.
Here,
T
denotes the ambient fluid’s temperature,
k_{\text{B}}
the Boltzmann constant, and
\tau_{\text{p}}=\gamma/\kappa
the particle relaxation time, determined by the particle’s friction coefficient
\gamma
and the optical trap stiffness
\kappa
. The work required for steering the particle in perpendicular direction is given by
W^{s}_{t_{f}}=\int_{0}^{t_{f}}\!\!\text{d}t\ \dot{\lambda}(\lambda-x)

Seifert 2012
;
Peliti and Pigolotti 2021
. The objective is to minimize the mean
\langle W_{t_{f}}\rangle
of the total work
W_{t_{f}}=W^{s}_{t_{f}}+V(x_{f})
over both the control protocol

\lambda(t)
and the mean particle position
u(t)=\langle x(t)\rangle
, for a fixed mean initial
condition
u_{0}=\langle x(0)\rangle
. The mean
\langle W_{t_{f}}\rangle
reads

\displaystyle{W}_{t_{f}}[u(t),\lambda(t)]=\langle W_{t_{f}}\rangle=\int_{0}^{t_{f}}\!\!\text{d}t\,\dot{\lambda}(t)[\lambda(t)-u(t)]+\tilde{V}(u_{f})\,,

(2)

where
u_{f}=\langle x(t_{f})\rangle
is the final mean position and
\tilde{V}(u_{f})=\langle V(x_{f})\rangle
is the noise-averaged penalty. We constrain the minimization of
{W}_{t_{f}}
so that
u(t)
satisfies the noise-averaged dynamics Eq. (
1
), by introducing a Lagrange multiplier.
Applying the Pontryagin principle
Fleming and Rishel 1975
;
Bechhoefer 2021
then yields a quadratic control Hamiltonian (Sec.
B
in
app
, whose optimal solution gives

\displaystyle{W}_{t_{f}}(u_{f},u_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma(u_{f}-u_{0})^{2}}{t_{f}}+\tilde{V}(u_{f})\,,

(3)

with
\lambda_{0}=u_{0}
and
\lambda_{f}=u_{f}
, reflecting that the system does not store excess potential energy at
t_{f}
. These boundary terms are not imposed, but are a direct result of the minimization.
The first term on the r.h.s. of Eq. (
3
) accounts for the total dissipative costs and corresponds to the obstacle-free solution
Schmiedl and Seifert 2007
, while the second term accounts for the mean penalty at
u_{f}
.

In a final step, we optimize
{W}_{t_{f}}
over
u_{f}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle{W}^{*}_{t_{f}}(u_{0})=\min_{u_{f}}{W}_{t_{f}}(u_{f},u_{0})\,,

(4)

to obtain the optimal total cost
{W}^{*}_{t_{f}}(u_{0})={W}_{t_{f}}(u^{*}_{f},u_{0})
, which solely depends on the initial particle position
u_{0}
. Here we denote by
u^{*}_{f}(u_{0},t_{f})=\text{argmin}_{u_{f}}{W}_{t_{f}}(u_{f},u_{0})
the associated minimizer.

Finite-time control transition

Examination of the cost function in such structured environment leads to a general observation: The competition between the position-dependent penalty
V(x_{f})
and viscous dissipation penalizing large displacements along
x
. As we will show below, this competition can lead to abrupt switches in the optimal control strategies upon changing of the overall process duration. To illustrate this, we consider a symmetric penalty function
V(x_{f})=V(-x_{f})
with
V^{\prime}(0)=0
and
V^{\prime\prime}(0)<0
. Setting the initial particle position to
x_{0}=\lambda_{0}=0
, a
zero-control
solution [
v_{\perp}(t)=0
] yields the maximal penalty since the particle moves towards the maximum of
V(x)
. From Eqs. (
3
), it follows that
u_{f}=0
satisfies
\partial_{u_{f}}{W}_{t_{f}}=0
, and is therefore a stationary point of
{W}_{t_{f}}
.

To assess its stability, we evaluate the second derivative

\displaystyle\partial^{2}_{u_{f}}{W}_{t_{f}}(0,0)=\frac{2\gamma}{t_{f}}+\tilde{V}^{\prime\prime}(0).

(5)

For short protocol durations
t_{f}\ll\tau_{\text{p}}
, the dissipative contribution dominates, rendering any displacement too costly and stabilizing the zero-control solution:
\lambda^{\ast}(t)=u^{\ast}(t)=0
. However, when
t_{f}
exceeds a critical time

\displaystyle t_{\text{c}}=\frac{2\gamma}{-\tilde{V}^{\prime\prime}(0)},

(6)

dissipation becomes sufficiently weak that the energetic penalty dominates, see Sec.
C
in
app
. Consequently, Eq. (
5
) changes sign and the zero-control solution becomes suboptimal.

As a consequence, for
t_{f}>t_{\text{c}}
a new strategy emerges. Now, it is favorable to steer the particle towards regions of lower penalty, so the optimal protocol is in general given by

\displaystyle\lambda^{*}(t)=\begin{cases}u_{0},&t=0,\\
t_{f}^{-1}(u_{f}^{*}-u_{0})\left(t+\tau_{\text{p}}\right)+u_{0}\,,&0<t<t_{f}\,,\\
u^{\ast}_{f}&t=t_{f}\,.\end{cases}

(7)

Thus,
\lambda^{\ast}(t)
becomes linear in time with discontinuities at the boundaries, similar to the optimal control without penalty
Schmiedl and Seifert 2007
. Recall that the final position
\lambda_{f}^{*}=u_{f}^{*}
is determined by minimizing the mean total energetic cost through Eq. (
4
).
In the limit
u_{0}\to 0
and
t_{f}\to t_{\text{c}}
, we recover
\lambda^{*}(t)\to 0
, such that the linear solution connects continuously to the zero-control protocol.

It is useful to define the magnitude of the optimal final particle position
|u_{f}^{*}|
as an order parameter for the transition, as it directly captures the onset of symmetry breaking. For a symmetric and locally concave penalty function, where
\tilde{V}(u_{f})
is even around
u_{f}=0
and
\tilde{V}^{\prime\prime}(0)<0
, the competition between dissipation and energetic cost leads to a bifurcation of the optimal solution. For
t_{f}<t_{\text{c}}
, the symmetric solution
u_{f}^{*}=0
is stable, whereas for
t_{f}>t_{\text{c}}
, two symmetry-broken solutions with
u_{f}^{*}\neq 0
emerge.

This behavior admits a direct interpretation in terms of a continuous phase transition. The control time
t_{f}
acts as a tuning parameter that shifts the balance between dissipation and energetic cost, leading to a loss of stability of the symmetric solution. At the critical time
t_{\text{c}}
, the curvature of
{W}_{t_{f}}
at
u_{f}=0
changes sign, analogous to the vanishing quadratic coefficient in a Landau expansion
Chaikin et al. 1995
.

Experimental results

We now turn to an experimental test of these predictions, employing a colloidal silica particle (diameter
\approx 2.73\,$\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}$
) that is dragged through a viscous fluid by optical tweezers. The fluid is confined in a sample cell with height
100\,$\mu\mathrm{m}$
and consists of a 1:1 (volume fraction) mixture of water and glycerol, resulting in a friction coefficient
\gamma=0.11\,$\mu\mathrm{N}\mathrm{s}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{m}$

532\,$\mathrm{nm}$
laser beam that generates a harmonic trapping potential

U(x,\lambda)=\frac{\kappa}{2}(x-\lambda)^{2}\,.

(8)

For trap stiffnesses in the range
\kappa=(0.51
–
0.62)\pm 0.05\,$\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro N}\text{\,}{\mathrm{m}}^{-1}$

1

\tau_{\text{p}}=(0.172
–
0.216)\pm 0.02\,$\mathrm{s}$
. Temporal control of
\lambda
was realized by deflecting the laser beam with an acousto-optical deflector (AOD), enabling dynamic positioning of the optical trap center with a spatial accuracy of 10
\mathrm{nm}
and a repetition rate of
10^{-4}~$\mathrm{s}$
. Particle positions are tracked using digital video microscopy with a spatial and temporal resolution of
10\,$\mathrm{nm}$
and
4\,$\mathrm{ms}$
, respectively. The sample was kept at a constant temperature of 25
\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}
. For details see Sec.
E.1
in
app

FTDPTs in non-equilibrium relaxation

The optimal cost
{W}^{*}_{t_{f}}
derived above results from a minimization over trajectories that balance viscous dissipation against a position-dependent energetic penalty. This structure is reminiscent of action functionals arising in large deviation descriptions of stochastic dynamics, where probabilities of trajectories are exponentially weighted according to a probabilistic cost. In particular, the quadratic contribution associated with dissipation and the final penalty term play roles analogous to those governing rare fluctuations in diffusive systems. Notably, this correspondence involves an exchange of boundary conditions: the penalty acting on the final position in the control problem appears as a probabilistic weight of the initial condition in the corresponding relaxation dynamics.

Motivated by this analogy, we map the optimal control problem onto a non-equilibrium relaxation process in which
{W}^{*}_{t_{f}}
acts as a rate function
Touchette 2009
for rare trajectories. Under this correspondence, the finite time-transition between optimal strategies indeed emerges as a dynamical phase transition in the dominant relaxation pathways. Specifically, we consider a colloidal particle initially prepared at equilibrium in the potential
V(x)
and subsequently released to diffuse freely. In the regime of weak thermal noise,
k_{\text{B}}T\ll V_{0}
, and short times
t_{f}\ll\tau_{\text{D}}=\gamma x_{\text{m}}^{2}/(k_{\text{B}}T)
compared to the characteristic diffusion time
\tau_{\text{D}}
over the distance
x_{\text{m}}
, the relaxation dynamics is governed by rare events, known as optimal fluctuations.

Within a large deviation framework, detailed in Sec.
D
of
app
, these optimal fluctuations are obtained by minimizing the Euler–Machlup functional over stochastic trajectories with fixed boundary conditions
x_{0}=x(0)
and
x_{f}=x(t_{f})
. This procedure closely mirrors the minimization of
{W}_{t_{f}}
in the control problem and yields the most probable relaxation pathways connecting
x_{0}
and
x_{f}
.

Their probability scales as
\propto\exp[-{V}_{t_{f}}(x_{f},x_{0})/(k_{\text{B}}T)]
, where

\displaystyle{V}_{t_{f}}(x_{f},x_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma(x_{f}-x_{0})^{2}}{4t_{f}}+V(x_{0})\,.

(10)

Maximizing this probability over the initial condition
x_{0}
yields the marginal probability
P(x_{f},t_{f})\propto\exp[-{V}^{*}_{t_{f}}(x_{f})/(k_{\text{B}}T)]
, characterized by the rate function

\displaystyle{V}^{*}_{t_{f}}(x_{f})=\min_{x_{0}}{V}_{t_{f}}(x_{f},x_{0})\,.

(11)

Comparing with Eqs. (
3
) and (
4
), the control problem maps directly onto the relaxation problem upon identifying
{W}\leftrightarrow{V}
,
\tilde{V}\leftrightarrow V
, and
u\leftrightarrow x
, up to a trivial time rescaling
t\to 4(t_{f}-t)
.
Accordingly, the transition of the optimal control protocol corresponds to a finite-time transition of the dominant relaxation pathway, signaled by a kink in
{V}^{*}_{t_{f}}(x)
at
t^{R}_{\text{c}}\approx t_{\text{c}}/4
, analogous to the kink in
{W}^{*}_{t_{f}}(u_{0})
shown in Fig.
2
. Such transitions, known as finite-time dynamical phase transitions
Meibohm and Esposito 2022
;
Meibohm and Esposito 2023
, have been observed primarily in spin systems
van Enter et al. 2002
;
Külske and Le
Ny 2007
;
Ermolaev and Külske 2010
;
Blom and Godec 2022
;
Vadakkayil et al. 2024
. By this correspondence, measuring
{W}^{*}_{t_{f}}
in Fig.
2
(c) provides not only a direct observation of the control transition but also an indirect signature of an FTDPT.

To probe this relaxation transition directly, we now turn to an experiment that accesses
{V}^{*}_{t_{f}}
. The main practical difference between
{W}^{*}_{t_{f}}
and
{V}^{*}_{t_{f}}
is that the latter characterizes the probability of rare relaxation events, while the former corresponds to a statistical average. Observing such rare events requires
\propto\exp[{V}^{*}_{t_{f}}(x_{f})/(k_{\text{B}}T)]\gg 1
measurements, which poses a serious experimental challenge. To overcome this, we employed an importance-sampling technique commonly used in numerical simulations
Bucklew 2004
: we recorded approximately
10^{3}
trajectories of duration
\approx 2\tau_{\text{D}}
of a freely diffusing Brownian particle with an arbitrary initial distribution (see Sec.
E.2.2
in
app
), and subsequently reweighted them such that the initial distribution corresponds to the Boltzmann weight
\propto\exp[-V(x)/(k_{\text{B}}T)]
. This way we circumvent the need to include an equilibration period in the experiment. Figure
3
(a) shows
{V}^{*}_{t_{f}}(x_{f})\sim-k_{\text{B}}T\log P(x_{f},t_{f})
obtained from reweighted experimental trajectories at times below and above
t^{R}_{\text{c}}
. The emergence of a kink at
x=0
for
t>t^{R}_{\text{c}}
is clearly visible.

We define an order parameter for the FTDPT by the absolute value
|x_{0}^{*}|
of the minimizer
x_{0}^{*}=\text{argmin}_{x_{0}}{V}_{t_{f}}(x=0,x_{0})
. For
k_{\text{B}}T\ll V_{0}
, the probability density
P(|x_{0}|,t_{f})
of initial positions leading to
x_{f}=0
at time
t_{f}
is sharply peaked around
|x_{0}^{*}|
. Figure
3
(b) shows
P(|x_{0}|,t_{f})
as a heat map, providing clear evidence of the transition at
t=t^{R}_{\text{c}}
.

The FTDPT in the relaxation experiment originates from a sudden change in the dominant relaxation pathway. Figure
3
(c) shows the density of trajectories conditioned on reaching
x_{f}=0
at time
t_{f}
. For
t_{f}<t^{R}_{\text{c}}
, the density is concentrated around an optimal fluctuation that starts at
x_{0}=0
and effectively remains there. Although rare, this is exponentially more likely than trajectories starting near
\pm x_{\text{m}}
and reaching
x=0
within the short time window. For
t_{f}>t^{R}_{\text{c}}
, by contrast, trajectories originate near
\pm x_{\text{m}}
and relax towards
x_{f}=0
along approximately linear paths (black), in analogy with the optimal control solution
u^{*}(t)
.

Finally, to characterize fluctuations of
|x_{0}^{*}|
near the critical point, we analyzed the susceptibility
\chi_{t}
extracted from experimental data [Fig.
3
(d)]. As
V_{0}/k_{\text{B}}T
increases,
\chi_{t}
develops a growing peak that shifts towards
t^{R}_{\text{c}}
(gray line). When rescaling
\chi_{t}
and
\delta t=(t-t_{c})/t
with
(V_{0}/k_{\text{B}}T)^{-1/2}
(inset),
\chi_{t}
collapses onto a scaling function, suggesting that the transition can be characterized by mean-field critical exponents. By contrast, the fluctuations in the optimal control experiment show no such critical behavior.

Conclusion

We theoretically and experimentally showed that optimal control of particles in structured environments exhibits finite-time transitions. The transition arises from a trade-off between dissipation along longer trajectories that avoid unfavorable regions and the energetic penalty of crossing them. This competition yielded distinct optimal protocols depending on control time. We further showed that these transitions are directly linked to finite-time dynamical phase transitions in non-equilibrium relaxation, reflecting a switch between dominant pathways at short and long times. In the companion paper
Meibohm et al.
, we demonstrate that these effects extend to a broader class of control problems. Our findings suggest that abrupt transitions are generic in mesoscopic control in structured environments and that optimal-control experiments provide access to dynamical phase transitions without sampling exponentially rare trajectories.

. Due to the linear Langevin dynamics [Eq. (
1
) in the main text], we know that
x_{f}
is Gaussian with stationary variance
\langle x_{f}^{2}\rangle-\langle x_{f}\rangle^{2}=k_{\text{B}}T/\kappa
. For the double-well form [Eq. (
9
) in the main text]

\displaystyle V(x)=\frac{V_{0}}{4}\left(\frac{x^{2}}{x_{\text{m}}^{2}}-1\right)\,,

(12)

we thus have

\displaystyle\langle V(x_{f})\rangle=\frac{V_{0}}{4}\left(\frac{\langle x_{f}^{4}\rangle}{x_{\text{m}}^{4}}-2\frac{\langle x_{f}^{2}\rangle}{x_{\text{m}}^{2}}+1\right)\,.

(13)

To obtain
\tilde{V}(u_{f})
, we need to express
\langle x_{f}^{2}\rangle
and
\langle x_{f}^{4}\rangle
in terms of
\langle x_{f}\rangle=u_{f}
. Using the Gaussian properties of
x_{f}
, we find

\displaystyle\langle x_{f}^{2}\rangle=

\displaystyle u_{f}^{2}+\frac{k_{\text{B}}T}{\kappa}\,,

(14)

\displaystyle\langle x_{f}^{4}\rangle=

\displaystyle u_{f}^{4}+6\left(\frac{k_{\text{B}}T}{\kappa}\right)u^{2}_{f}+3\left(\frac{k_{\text{B}}T}{\kappa}\right)^{2}\,.

(15)

We thus obtain for the noise-averaged potential
\langle V(x_{f})\rangle=\tilde{V}(u_{f})
in Eq. (
13
)

\tilde{V}(u_{f})=V(u_{f})+\frac{V_{0}}{4}\left(\frac{k_{\text{B}}T}{\kappa x_{\text{m}}^{2}}\right)\left(\frac{6u_{f}^{2}}{x_{\text{m}}^{2}}-2+\frac{3k_{\text{B}}T}{\kappa x_{\text{m}}^{2}}\right)\,.

(16)

Equation (
13
) shows that
u_{f}
experiences the noise-averaged penalty
\tilde{V}(u_{f})
that is in general different from the penalty of individual trajectories
V(x_{f})
. For weak noise,
k_{\text{B}}T\ll V_{0},\kappa x_{\text{m}}^{2}
, however, the second term in Eq. (
16

\tilde{V}(u_{f})\approx V(u_{f})

Appendix B
Derivation of optimal protocols and optimal work

Starting with Eq. (
2
) we perform an integration by part to remove the time derivative of
\lambda(t)
. We then introduce the Lagrange multiplier
\mu(t)
to enforce that
u(t)
must satisfy the noise-averaged dynamics

\displaystyle\dot{u}(t)=-\tau_{\text{p}}^{-1}[u(t)-\lambda(t)]\,.

(17)

This leads us to

{W}_{t_{f}}[u(t),\mu(t),\lambda(t)]=\\
\tau_{\text{p}}^{-1}\int_{0}^{t_{f}}\!\!\text{d}t\,\left\{\mu\dot{u}-[\mu(t)-\kappa\lambda(t)][\lambda(t)-u(t)]\right\}\\
+\kappa\lambda_{f}\left(\frac{\lambda_{f}}{2}-u_{f}\right)-\kappa\lambda_{0}\left(\frac{\lambda_{0}}{2}-u_{0}\right)+\tilde{V}(u_{f})\,.

(18)

To simplify the calculations that follow, we add

\displaystyle 0=\frac{\kappa}{2}(u_{f}^{2}-u_{0}^{2})-\frac{\kappa}{\tau_{\text{p}}}\int_{0}^{t_{f}}\!\!\!\text{d}t\ u(t)[\lambda(t)-u(t)]\,,

(19)

which gives

{W}_{t_{f}}[u(t),\mu(t),\lambda(t)]=\\
\tau_{\text{p}}^{-1}\int_{0}^{t_{f}}\!\!\text{d}t\,\left\{\mu\dot{u}-[\kappa u(t)-\kappa\lambda(t)+\mu(t)][\lambda(t)-u(t)]\right\}\\
+\frac{\kappa}{2}\left(\lambda_{f}-u_{f}\right)^{2}-\frac{\kappa}{2}\left(\lambda_{0}-u_{0}\right)^{2}+\tilde{V}(u_{f})\,.

(20)

Peforming a variation over
\lambda(t)
and setting
\delta_{\lambda(t)}{W}_{t_{f}}[u(t),\lambda(t),\mu(t)]\big|_{\lambda(t)=\lambda^{*}(t)}=0
, we find

\displaystyle\lambda^{*}(t)=\frac{1}{2\kappa}\left[\mu(t)+2\kappa u(t)\right]\,,\quad\lambda_{f,0}=u_{f,0}\,.

(21)

We thus have for
{W}_{t_{f}}[u(t),\mu(t)]={W}_{t_{f}}[u(t),\mu(t),\lambda^{*}(t)]
,

{W}_{t_{f}}[u(t),\mu(t)]=\int_{0}^{t_{f}}\!\!\text{d}t\,\left\{\dot{u}(t)\mu(t)-{H}[u(t),\mu(t)]\right\}\\
+\tilde{V}(u_{f})\,,

(22)

with the simple quadratic control Hamiltonian

\displaystyle{H}[u(t),\mu(t)]=\frac{\mu^{2}(t)}{4\gamma}\,.

(23)

Performing the variation over
u(t)
and
\mu(t)
while holding
u_{f}
and
u_{0}
fixed and requiring stationarity
\delta{W}=0
, we obtain Hamilton’s equations of motion

\displaystyle\dot{u}^{*}(t)=\frac{\partial{H}}{\partial\mu(t)}=\frac{\mu^{*}(t)}{2\gamma}\,,\qquad\dot{\mu}^{*}(t)=0\,,

(24)

from which we obtain the solutions

\displaystyle\mu^{*}(t)=\mu^{*}(0)\,,\qquad u^{*}(t)=\frac{\mu^{*}(0)t}{2\gamma}+u_{0}\,.

(25)

Evaluating at
t=t_{f}
and written in terms of
\mu^{*}(t_{f})=\mu^{*}(0)
, we find

\displaystyle\mu^{*}(t_{f})=\mu^{*}(0)=2\kappa(u_{f}-u_{0})\frac{\tau_{\text{p}}}{t_{f}}\,,

(26)

which gives the optimal solutions

\displaystyle u^{*}(t)=

\displaystyle(u_{f}-u_{0})\frac{t}{t_{f}}+u_{0}\,,

(27)

\displaystyle\lambda^{*}(t)=

\displaystyle t_{f}^{-1}(u_{f}-u_{0})\left(t+\tau_{\text{p}}\right)+u_{0}\,,

(28)

We observe that
u^{*}(t)
is continuous with
u^{*}(0)=u_{0}
and
u^{*}(t_{f})=u_{f}
. For the optimal protocol
\lambda^{*}(t)
, by contrast, we have the limits
\lambda^{*}(0^{+})=u_{0}+(u_{f}-u_{0})\tau_{\text{p}}/t_{f}
and
\lambda^{*}(t_{f}^{-})=u_{f}+(u_{f}-u_{0})\tau_{\text{p}}/t_{f}
. This shows that
\lambda^{*}(t)
has jumps of equal, size
\lambda^{*}(0^{+})-u_{0}=\lambda^{*}(t_{f}^{-})-u_{f}=(u_{f}-u_{0})\tau_{\text{p}}/t_{f}
, at the start and end points as depicted in Fig.
2
(a) and
2
(b) in the main text.

To derive the optimal work
{W}^{*}_{t_{f}}
[Eq. (
3
) in the main text], we rewrite

\int_{0}^{t_{f}}\!\!\text{d}t\,\dot{u}(t)\mu(t)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t_{f}}\!\!\text{d}t\,\left[\dot{u}(t)\mu(t)-u(t)\dot{\mu}(t)\right]\\
+\frac{1}{2}\left[u_{f}\mu(t_{f})-u_{0}\mu(0)\right]

(29)

Using the Hamiltonian equations (
24
), it is now simple to show that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t_{f}}\!\!\text{d}t\,\left[\dot{u}^{*}(t)\mu^{*}(t)-u^{*}(t)\dot{\mu}^{*}(t)\right]=\int_{0}^{t_{f}}\!\!\text{d}t\,{H}[u^{*}(t),\mu^{*}(t)]\,.

(30)

Substituting these relations into Eq. (
22
) gives for
{W}_{t_{f}}(u_{0},u_{f})={W}_{t_{f}}[u^{*}(t),\mu^{*}(t)]
:

\displaystyle{W}_{t_{f}}(u_{0},u_{f})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left[u_{f}\mu^{*}(t_{f})-u_{0}\mu^{*}(0)\right]+\tilde{V}(u_{f})\,,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma(u_{f}-u_{0})^{2}}{t_{f}}+\tilde{V}(u_{f})\,,

(31)

where we used Eqs. (
26
) in the final step. This completes the derivation of Eq. (
3
).

Appendix C
Derivation of the critical time

In order to establish from Eq. (
31
) [Eqs. (
3
) in the main text] the existence of a finite-time transition of the control protocol, we need to determine the stability of the zero-control solution
u_{0}=u_{f}=0
. To this end, we evaluate
{W}^{*}_{t_{f}}
at
u_{0}=0
and expand to second order in
u_{f}
. Assuming that
\tilde{V}(u_{f})
is even,
\tilde{V}(u_{f})=\tilde{V}(-u_{f})
, this gives

\displaystyle{W}_{t_{f}}(0,u_{f})

\displaystyle\sim\tilde{V}(0)+\left[\frac{\gamma}{t_{f}}+\frac{\tilde{V}^{\prime\prime}(0)}{2}\right]u_{f}^{2}\,.

(32)

For short times,
t\ll\tau_{\text{p}}
the factor in front of the
\propto u_{f}^{2}
term is positive. This implies that starting from
u_{0}=0
, the optimal solution will end at
u_{f}=0
, the minimum of the cost function. Hence, for short enough times, the zero-control protocol
\lambda^{*}(t)=u^{*}(t)=0
is optimal. However, the
\propto u_{f}^{2}
factor in Eq. (
32
) changes sign when
t_{f}
exceeds the critical time
t_{\text{c}}
, given by

\displaystyle t_{\text{c}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\gamma}{-\tilde{V}^{\prime\prime}(0)}\,,

(33)

which is Eq. (
6
) in the main text.

Appendix D
Rate functions and FTDPTs

In the weak noise-limit, the large deviations of the relaxation process that follows the quench are dominated by so-called optimal fluctuations, describing the most likely ways in which the system relaxes. These optimal fluctuations minimize the Onsager-Machlup action

\displaystyle{V}_{t_{f}}[x(t),\dot{x}(t)]=\int_{0}^{t_{F}}\!\!\text{d}t\ {L}[x(t),\dot{x}(t)]+V(x_{0})\,,

(34)

with the Lagrangian
{L}[x(t),\dot{x}(t)]=\gamma\dot{x}^{2}(t)/4
. We turn to a Hamiltonian description by performing the Legendre transform

\displaystyle{H}_{\text{R}}[x(t),p(t)]=

\displaystyle p(t)\dot{x}(t)-{L}[x(t),\dot{x}(t)]\,,

(35)

\displaystyle p(t)=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{L}}{\partial\dot{x}}\,,

(36)

yielding the Hamiltonian

\displaystyle{H}_{\text{R}}[x(t),p(t)]=\frac{p^{2}(t)}{\gamma}\,.

(37)

Rewriting Eq. (
34
) in terms of
{H}_{\text{R}}
then yields

\displaystyle{V}_{t_{f}}[x(t),p(t)]=\int_{0}^{t_{F}}\!\!\text{d}t[p(t)\dot{x}(t)-{H}_{\text{R}}[x(t),p(t)]+V(x_{0})\,,

(38)

with the particle position trajectory
x(t)
,
x(t_{f})=x_{f}
and the “conjugate momentum”
p(t)

Meibohm and Esposito 2022
. The latter represents the effect of the thermal noise in the weak-noise limit.

Comparison with Eqs. (
22
) and (
23
), shows that these formulations are identical up to a factor of
4
. Using the same steps to minimize
{V}_{t_{f}}[x(t),p(t)]
, we arrive at the rate function for fixed
x_{0}
and
x_{f}
:

\displaystyle{V}_{t_{f}}(x_{0},x_{f})

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma(x_{f}-x_{0})^{2}}{4t_{f}}+V(x_{0})\,,

(39)

in analogy with the stochastic work
{W}_{t_{f}}
in Eq. (
31
) [Eq. (
3
) in the main text], where the initial distribution
V
plays the role of the average final penalty
\tilde{V}(u_{f})
. In particular, after the critical time
t^{R}_{\text{c}}\approx t_{\text{c}}/4
, the rate function
{V}^{*}_{t}(x_{f})=\min_{x_{0}}{V}_{t}(x_{f},x_{0})
, obtained after minimizing over the initial position
x_{0}
, attains a kink analogous to the kink in
{W}^{*}_{t_{f}}(u_{f})
.

Appendix E
Details of experiments

E.1
Optical tweezers setup

A 532 nm laser (Coherent Verdi V2) is used as the coherent light source for the optical tweezers setup.
The beam intensity can be modulated and its angle deflected using an acousto-optic deflector (AOD, AA Opto-Electronic DTSXY-400).
A telescope composed of 2 inch lenses directs the deflected beam to the back aperture of the microscope objective (Olympus Apochromat MPLAPON-Oil 100x, NA = 1.45), accommodating deflection angles of up to ± 40
\mathrm{mrad}
.
The objective serves both for optical trapping and imaging.
The laser power is adjusted such that, at the highest AOD setting used in experiments, it reaches a maximum of 160
\mathrm{mW}
immediately before the back aperture.
Microscopy videos are recorded with a digital camera (Basler ace 2 a2A3840-45umPRO) at a frame rate of 250
\mathrm{Hz}
in the optimal control and 400
\mathrm{Hz}
in both relaxation experiments, with the region of interest adapted to the specific experimental requirements.
The sample temperature is controlled by resistive heating (Okolab H401-T-Penny) applied to both the sample stage and the objective, and is maintained at 25 ±0.05
\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}
throughout all experiments.
This configuration allows the laser focus to be shifted within the imaging plane by up to ±19
\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}
.
To suppress AOD-induced artifacts, a zero-intensity interval is inserted between consecutive positions in the control sequence, resulting in a pattern such as (position 1, zero, position 2, zero, …).

Calibration is required for two reasons, the first being that it enables the conversion between spatial positions within the camera’s field of view and the corresponding control frequencies applied to the AOD.
Second, the AOD exhibits a frequency-dependent diffraction efficiency, which induces systematic variations in the trap stiffness.
We use the same calibration method as in another recent experimental study
Monter et al. 2026
.
Calibration establishes the mapping between AOD control frequencies and trap positions by sampling a grid across the field of view and assigning time-averaged particle positions to the corresponding laser focus locations. A continuous mapping is then obtained via interpolation.
To account for spatial variations in trapping strength, the trap stiffness
\kappa_{i}
is characterized across the field of view and normalized to its mean value in each spatial direction
\langle\kappa_{i}\rangle
. An correction factor is defined for each frequency pair as

c(f_{x},f_{y})=\frac{\kappa_{x}/\langle\kappa_{x}\rangle+\kappa_{y}/\langle\kappa_{y}\rangle}{2}.

(40)

A smooth correction function
C(f_{x},f_{y})
is obtained by fitting a third order polynomial to
c(f_{x},f_{y})
.
C(f_{x},f_{y})
is then used to rescale the applied AOD amplitude according to

A_{\mathrm{apply}}=\frac{A_{\mathrm{set}}}{0.5+0.5\cdot C(f_{x},f_{y})}.

(41)

Using this approach, spatial variations in trap stiffness are reduced from ±20
\mathrm{\%}
to ±5
\mathrm{\%}
.

Sample suspensions are prepared by dispersing spherical silica micro particles (diameter
2.73\pm 0.12~$\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}$
, microParticles GmbH) in a water-glycerol mixture at a volumetric ratio of 1:1.
Measurement cells are assembled by filling glass capillaries (inner diameter 100
\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}
, width 1
\mathrm{mm}
; CM Scientific) with the prepared suspensions. The open ends of the capillaries are sealed using a combination of wax and epoxy resin to prevent evaporation.
The samples are allowed to equilibrate within the measurement setup for a minimum of 60 minutes prior to data acquisition.

E.2
Measurement Procedures

E.2.1
Optimal Control Experiment

After trapping a particle near the midplane of the sample cell (to avoid wall-interactions) a calibration measurement is performed as described in
E.1
.
To test the optimal control protocols for varying initial positions
u_{0}
and protocol durations
t_{f}
, the trap center
\lambda(t)
is moved according to the corresponding optimal protocol given in Eq. (
7
). Each experiment consists of both forward and backward implementations of this protocol. This avoids a continuous drift of the trap across the sample, which would be incompatible with the finite deflection range of the AOD and the limited field of view of the camera. In addition, averaging over forward and backward realizations reduces variations in trap stiffness are not fully accounted for by the calibration procedure.
Before, between and after each forward and backward execution, the trap is held fixed for 3
\mathrm{s}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{c}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{d}
to ensure that each realization starts from equilibrium. To obtain statistically significant averages, each forward/backward experiment is repeated at least 40 times, yielding 80 trajectories per protocol.

From the measured trajectories, we evaluate the stochastic work following
Crooks 1998
as

W=\sum_{i=1}^{N}U[x(t_{i}),\lambda(t_{i+1})]-U[x(t_{i}),\lambda(t_{i})]

(42)

where time is discretized into N intervals of duration
t_{f}/(N-1)=0.04~$\mathrm{s}$
defined by the acquisition rate of the camera.

E.2.2
Free relaxation experiment and reweighting

For the relaxation experiments, we first localized a single particle in the optical trap. We then repeatedly turned the trap on and off for time periods
t_{\mathrm{on}}=t_{\mathrm{off}}=0.5\,$\mathrm{s}$
, respectively. During the “off” time, we observed free diffusion of the particle with diffusion constant
D=k_{\text{B}}T/\gamma\approx 5.7\,$\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}$/$\mathrm{s}$
. After the time period
t_{\mathrm{on}}
, the trap was turned back on to prevent diffusion of the particle out of the field of view and to prevent the particle from settling outside the focal plane.

To compute the rate function
{V}_{t_{f}}^{*}(x_{f})
shown in Fig.
3
(a), we considered the probability density

\displaystyle P(x_{f},t_{f})=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\!\!\text{d}x_{0}\ P(x_{f},t_{f}|x_{0})P(x_{0})\,,

(43)

where
P(x_{0})
denotes the initial probability density at
t=0
and
P(x_{f},t_{f}|x_{0})
the transition probability from
x_{0}
at time
t=0
to
x_{f}
at time
t_{f}
. The initial distribution before the quench is given by the Boltzmann distribution

\displaystyle P(x_{0})=\frac{\text{e}^{-\frac{V(x)}{k_{\text{B}}T}}}{Z}\,,\qquad Z=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\!\!\text{d}x\ \text{e}^{-\frac{V(x)}{k_{\text{B}}T}}\,.

(44)

In the weak-noise limit
k_{\text{B}}T\ll V_{0}
, the rate function
{V}^{*}_{t_{f}}
is obtained from the large-deviation form

\displaystyle P(x_{f},t_{f})\propto\text{e}^{-\frac{{V}^{*}_{t_{f}}(x_{f})}{k_{\text{B}}T}}\,.

(45)

A similar form applies for the transition probability

\displaystyle P(x_{f},t_{f}|x_{0})\propto\text{e}^{-\frac{{V}_{t_{f}}(x_{f}|x_{0})}{k_{\text{B}}T}}\,.

(46)

From a saddle-point approximation of Eq. (
43

\displaystyle{V}_{t_{f}}^{*}(x_{f})=\min_{x_{0}}\left\{{V}_{t_{f}}(x_{f}|x_{0})+V(x_{0})\right\}\,.

(47)

With normal sampling, it is unfortunately challenging to obtain
{V}^{*}_{t_{f}}(x_{f})
with satisfactory accuracy. Instead, we obtained
{V}^{*}_{t_{f}}(x_{f})
by measuring the transition probability
P(x_{f},t_{f}|x_{0})

47

To this end, we note that translational and inversion invariance of free diffusion implies
P(x_{f},t_{f}|x_{0})=P(|x_{f}-x_{0}|,t_{f}|0)

{V}_{t_{f}}(x_{f}|x_{0})={V}_{t_{f}}(|x_{f}-x_{0}||0)

\displaystyle{V}_{t_{f}}^{*}(x_{f})=\min_{x_{0}}\left\{{V}_{t_{f}}(x_{0}|0)+V(x_{0}+x_{f})\right\}\,.

(48)

This implies that to obtain
{V}_{t_{f}}^{*}(x_{f})

x_{0}
and reweight the result with

48

in Eq. (
43

\displaystyle P_{s}(x_{f},t_{f})=P(x_{f},t_{f}|0)\,.

(49)

We can therefore compute

in Eq. (
48

\displaystyle{V}_{t_{f}}(x_{0}|0)\sim-k_{\text{B}}T\log P_{s}(x_{f},t_{f})|_{x_{f}=x_{0}}\,.

(50)

To further increase the sample size, we used the Markov property of the diffusion process to split long trajectories into snippets of the required time window

\approx 10^{6}

Similarly, to determine the order-parameter density
