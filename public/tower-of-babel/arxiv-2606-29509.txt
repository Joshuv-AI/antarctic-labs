Vortex NOON states for rotation sensing
Simon Dengis, Nathan Dupont, Peter Schlagheck, Nathan Goldman

Abstract
We introduce a scheme to generate NOON states of few-body bosonic vortices and demonstrate their application as high-precision rotation sensors. Our approach is based on cold atoms in a weakly anisotropic two-dimensional harmonic trap, where the single-particle p orbitals define an effective two-mode Bose–Hubbard model with vortex modes ( p x ± i p y ) (\text{p}_{x}\pm\text{i}\text{p}_{y}) carrying opposite circulation. In the self-trapping regime, we show that the NOON manifold becomes spectrally isolated, and collective tunneling processes give rise to highly entangled vortex NOON states. However, these states emerge on prohibitively long timescales. To overcome this limitation, we develop two complementary acceleration strategies: geodesic counterdiabatic driving for small particle numbers, and resonance- and chaos-assisted tunneling in the semiclassical regime at larger particle numbers. Both approaches enable the generation of NOON states on experimentally relevant timescales while preserving near-unit fidelities. Finally, we quantify the metrological advantage of vortex NOON states by introducing an interferometric protocol that exploits their intrinsic sensitivity to rotation, enabling the detection of infinitesimal external rotations at the Heisenberg limit. Our work opens the door to rotation sensors based on atomic NOON states, generically realizable in bosonic Josephson junctions with vortex‑type orbitals.

II.2
Rotation sensing with vortex NOON states

We now propose to exploit these bosonic vortices as a rotation sensor using the many-body Hamiltonian (
9
). The key ingredient is the last term
-R\hbar\Omega(\hat{n}_{+}-\hat{n}_{-})
, which imprints the effect of the external rotation
\Omega
onto the bosonic system. As
R=\bar{\omega}/\sqrt{\omega_{x}\omega_{y}}\simeq 1
is fixed for a given experimental setup, we absorb this coefficient into
\Omega
in the following (see Appendix
A
). Through the rotation, a Fock state
\ket{n_+, n_-}
(describing
n_{+}
and
n_{-}=N-n_{+}
bosons occupying the vortex state
\ket{\psi_\pm}
respectively) acquires an energy
-\hbar\Omega(n_{+}-n_{-})

\ket{\psi_a}
and
\ket{\psi_b}
with different population imbalances
\langle\hat{n}_{+}-\hat{n}_{-}\rangle_{\psi_{a,b}}
will develop a relative phase
\varphi
proportional to
\Omega
, that can then be measured from the interference of these states
90
. This phase encoding is naturally optimized for
\ket{\psi_a}=\ket{N,0}
and
\ket{\psi_b}=\ket{0,N}
, i.e. by the pure NOON state

\ket{\dNOON}=\frac{\ket{N,0}+\text{e}^{\text{i}\varphi}\ket{0,N}}{\sqrt{2}}.

(10)

For low anisotropy,
\Delta\ll NU
(see Section
II.3
), the two Fock components accumulate opposite phases
\pm N\Omega T
over an interrogation time
T

\varphi=2N\Omega T
. The full interferometric protocol is illustrated in Fig.
3

\ket{N,0}
(where all the bosons rotate in the same direction) a unitary operator
\hat{\mathcal{U}}
prepares the NOON state
\ket{\dNOON}=\hat{\mathcal{U}}\ket{N,0}

T
, the inverse
\hat{\mathcal{U}}^{\dagger}
maps the accumulated phase back onto the population of the fully polarized Fock state
P_{+}=|\langle N,0|\psi\rangle|^{2}
and
P_{-}=|\langle 0,N|\psi\rangle|^{2}
, yielding

P_{\pm}=\frac{1\pm\cos(\varphi)}{2},

(11)

from which
\varphi
, and thus
\Omega

III.1.1
Shortcut to adiabaticity

The possibility of adiabatically generating a NOON state is opened by the separation of the energy levels corresponding to the states of interest. The adiabatic theorem states that when the Hamiltonian varies slowly compared to the intrinsic timescale set by the level splitting between the energy eigenstates, the system remains in the instantaneous eigenstate in which it was initially prepared. The driving can therefore be optimized to vary slowly in the regions where diabatic transitions are likely, i.e. where the energy levels are closest, and faster elsewhere, where the levels are sufficiently separated such that transitions towards undesired states can be neglected. Several standard choices of driving functions exist and are commonly used
69
;
118
;
78
;
107
. However, it is possible to determine the optimal path in parameter space, minimizing the protocol duration
110
;
19
;
84
. To do so, one must understand the geometry of the system encoded in the Fubini-Study metric, which describes the distance between two quantum states in Hilbert space as a function of the time-dependent parameter
95
. To find the optimal
\Omega(t)
to apply to the system to steer it adiabatically, one has to solve the following equation defining geodesics in parameter space
82
;
110
:

\hbar^{2}g_{\Omega\Omega}\dot{\Omega}^{2}=\mathrm{const.}

(24)

where

g_{\Omega\Omega}=\text{Re}\sum_{m\neq n}\frac{\langle n|\partial_{\Omega}\hat{H}_{\text{red}}|m\rangle\langle m|\partial_{\Omega}\hat{H}_{\text{red}}|n\rangle}{(E_{n}-E_{m})^{2}},

(25)

is the only non-vanishing component of the metric tensor
82
. Here,
\ket{n}
and
\ket{m}
are the two instantaneous eigenstates of the reduced Hamiltonian (
19
) at
\Omega
, with eigenenergies
E_{n}
and
E_{m}

6
6

6

For the two-level reduced system of Eq. (
19
), the sum (
25
) reduces to a single term.
. Equation (
24
) gives a condition on how
\Omega(t)
should behave for the system to follow the optimal path, minimizing at each time the local infidelity, i.e. the distance between its current state and the target eigenstate.

For the reduced Hamiltonian (
19

N\hbar\Omega(t)=\mathcal{J}\tan(\alpha_i + (\alpha_f-\alpha_i)\,t/T),

While the geodesic parametrization (
26
) optimally guides the system along the instantaneous eigenstates, the adiabatic theorem still constrains its evolution to remain slow. This speed limitation can be overcome by introducing a counterdiabatic Hamiltonian (CDH), which compensates for the system’s inertia and allows one to manipulate the state as quickly as desired
12
. In the eigenbasis of the Bose-Hubbard Hamiltonian, the CDH takes the form

\hat{H}_{\text{CD}}(t)=i\hbar\sum_{n}\sum_{m\neq n}\frac{\langle m|\dot{\hat{H}}(t)|n\rangle}{E_{n}-E_{m}}|m\rangle\langle n|.

(27)

Thus, defining a new Hamiltonian
\hat{H}+\hat{H}_{\text{CD}}
cancels the diabatic couplings in the eigenbasis, protecting the system state from unwanted transitions between instantaneous eigenstates. While it is often difficult to construct the CDH for large-scale systems, approximations exist to circumvent the need for full knowledge of the exact spectrum
27
, or to select only the matrix elements relevant to the task
87

19

38

\hat{H}_{\text{CD}}(t)=\frac{\mathcal{J}N\hbar^{2}\dot{\Omega}(t)}{2\left(\mathcal{J}^{2}+(N\hbar\Omega(t))^{2}\right)}\,\hat{\sigma}_{y},

(28)

with
\hat{\sigma}_{y}
the Pauli matrix. The matrix elements of the counterdiabatic Hamiltonian are thus purely off-diagonal and imaginary. Since its purpose is to compensate for the inertia induced by excessively fast driving, it is proportional to the driving rate
\dot{\Omega}
and inversely proportional to the squared gap
\mathcal{J}^{2}+(N\hbar\Omega(t))^{2}
.

III.1.2
Geodesic counterdiabatic driving

A priori, the CDH is defined independently of the choice of the driving function
\Omega

64

37

38

The combination of these two driving strategies, known as geodesic counterdiabatic driving (GCD), makes it possible to render the counterdiabatic matrix element time-independent, which is clearly advantageous for an experimental implementation of the protocol. Indeed, the purpose of geodesic driving is precisely to suppress variations near the gap, where the matrix elements of the CDH become significant. Conversely, the elements of the CDH are nearly zero where
\dot{\Omega}
becomes very large. By inserting the expression of the geodesic driving (
26

28

\frac{\mathcal{J}N\hbar^{2}\dot{\Omega}(t)}{2\left(\mathcal{J}^{2}+(N\hbar\Omega(t))^{2}\right)}=\frac{(\alpha_{f}-\alpha_{i})\hbar}{2T},

(29)

The norm of the counterdiabatic term is therefore fully determined by the total evolution time
T

|\alpha_{f}-\alpha_{i}|\approx\pi

In our case, and more generally for a two-level system, the squared norm of the CDH can therefore be directly related to the geodesic condition, which requires that the quantity
\hbar^{2}g_{\Omega\Omega}\dot{\Omega}(t)^{2}=\Delta E^{2}

(36)

(37)

The frequency

IV
Conclusion

In this work, we demonstrated that microscopic vortex NOON states can be efficiently prepared in a weakly anisotropic two-dimensional trap and exploited as quantum-enhanced probes for rotation sensing. In the self-trapping regime, interactions isolate the NOON manifold and provide both protection against leakage and Heisenberg-limited sensitivity. To overcome the exponentially slow collective tunneling, we introduced two complementary acceleration strategies. For small particle numbers, geodesic counterdiabatic driving enables the deterministic preparation of high-fidelity NOON states on experimentally relevant timescales. For larger particle numbers, resonance- and chaos-assisted tunneling provide an alternative route in the near-semiclassical regime, leading to a strong enhancement of the tunneling dynamics — a mechanism in which the chaos-assisted component has already been observed with Bose-Einstein condensates in a driven optical lattice
8
. The preparation protocols developed here, which assume loading into the p-state manifold
60
;
75
;
83
;
116
;
114
, also provide natural starting points for further optimization using quantum optimal control techniques
7
;
70
;
41
;
44
, with the GCD or RAT-CAT protocols serving as initial guesses for the optimization procedure. Such an approach could further reduce preparation times, thereby increasing robustness against decoherence and unwanted background rotations while extending the accessible particle-number regime.

The interferometric protocol proposed here provides a direct operational use of vortex NOON states for rotation sensing. By preparing a coherent superposition of opposite circulation states, allowing a phase to accumulate under an external rotation, and recombining the two components, the protocol leads to a measurable population imbalance. Combined with the enhanced phase sensitivity of NOON states, this provides a realistic pathway toward Heisenberg-limited rotation sensing with interacting ultracold atoms. Further improvement of the sensitivity can be achieved by creating such microscopic vortex NOON states within each of the
L

106

\Delta
through the relative intensity of the laser-beam pairs, and thereby allowing one to probe the rotation
\Omega
through
\nu=L
parallel realizations of the NOON sensor (see Eq. (
13
)). This further reduces the uncertainty on the estimation of
\Omega
by a factor
\sqrt{L}

90
.

Beyond the specific realization considered here, the preparation and metrological protocols developed in this work are applicable to any effective two-mode platform supporting states with opposite circulation. This includes, for instance, ring-shaped Bose–Einstein condensates threaded by synthetic gauge fields
3
;
85
;
94
;
20
and
\pi
-flux plaquettes
39

Appendix A
Angular momentum in an anisotropic 2D harmonic potential

We detail here the action of the angular momentum operator
\hat{L}_{z}^{(\text{1b})}=\hat{x}\hat{p}_{y}-\hat{y}\hat{p}_{x}
on the vortex states
\ket{\psi_\pm}
(
5
), justifying both the eigenvalue
\pm R\hbar
quoted in Sec.
II.1
and the sense in which
\ket{\psi_\pm}
are approximate eigenstates of
\hat{L}_{z}^{(\text{1b})}
.

Expressed through the Cartesian ladder operators of Eq. (
2
), the angular momentum reads

\begin{split}\hat{L}_{z}^{(\text{1b})}&=\text{i}\hbar\,\frac{\omega_{x}+\omega_{y}}{2\sqrt{\omega_{x}\omega_{y}}}\left(\hat{a}_{y}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{x}-\hat{a}_{x}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{y}\right)\\
&+\text{i}\hbar\,\frac{\omega_{y}-\omega_{x}}{2\sqrt{\omega_{x}\omega_{y}}}\left(\hat{a}_{x}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{y}^{\dagger}-\hat{a}_{x}\hat{a}_{y}\right),\end{split}

(54)

with the same structure as the rotation term of Eq. (
2
): a first contribution that preserves the total excitation number
n_{x}+n_{y}
, and a second, proportional to the anisotropy
(\omega_{y}-\omega_{x})
, that changes it by
\pm 2
. Acting on the p-orbital states
\ket{\dpx}\equiv\ket{\phi_{10}}
and
\ket{\dpy}\equiv\ket{\phi_{01}}
, the first term couples them within the manifold,
\hat{a}_{y}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{x}\ket{\phi_{10}}=\ket{\phi_{01}}
and
\hat{a}_{x}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{y}\ket{\phi_{01}}=\ket{\phi_{10}}
, while the second promotes them to the third excited manifold,
\hat{a}_{x}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{y}^{\dagger}\ket{\phi_{10}}=\sqrt{2}\,\ket{\phi_{21}}
and
\hat{a}_{x}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{y}^{\dagger}\ket{\phi_{01}}=\sqrt{2}\,\ket{\phi_{12}}
(see Fig.
2
). Applying Eq. (
54
) to the vortex states (
5
) then gives

\begin{split}\hat{L}_{z}^{(\text{1b})}\ket{\psi_\pm}=&\pm\hbar\,\frac{\bar{\omega}}{\sqrt{\omega_{x}\omega_{y}}}\ket{\psi_\pm}\\
&\mp\hbar\,\frac{\omega_{y}-\omega_{x}}{\sqrt{2\omega_{x}\omega_{y}}}\,\frac{\ket{\phi_{12}}\mp\text{i}\ket{\phi_{21}}}{\sqrt{2}}.\end{split}

(55)

The first term identifies
\ket{\psi_\pm}
as quasi-eigenstates of
\hat{L}_{z}^{(\text{1b})}
with eigenvalue
\pm R\hbar
, where
R\equiv\bar{\omega}/\sqrt{\omega_{x}\omega_{y}}
and
\bar{\omega}=(\omega_{x}+\omega_{y})/2
; in the weak-anisotropy regime (
3
),
\bar{\omega}\simeq\sqrt{\omega_{x}\omega_{y}}
and hence
R\simeq 1
. The second term is the leakage of
\ket{\psi_\pm}
into the third excited manifold, suppressed by the small ratio
(\omega_{y}-\omega_{x})/\sqrt{2\omega_{x}\omega_{y}}
. It is the one-body counterpart of the inter-manifold coupling neglected in Eq. (
2
) under condition (
3
), and it is in this sense that
\ket{\psi_\pm}
are eigenstates of
\hat{L}_{z}^{(\text{1b})}
to a good approximation, with corrections of order
(\omega_{y}-\omega_{x})/\bar{\omega}\ll 1
.

Appendix B
Derivation of the Bose-Hubbard model

We detail the passage from the trapped rotating Bose gas described by the Hamiltonian (
1
) to the two-mode Bose-Hubbard Hamiltonian (
9
), and give the interaction energy
U
in terms of the trap frequencies and the
s
-wave scattering length
a_{s}
.

We consider a strong axial confinement along the third dimension
z
of the trap:
\omega_{z}\gg\omega_{x},\omega_{y}
, which freezes the
z
motion in the oscillator ground state
\phi_{0}(z)=(m\omega_{z}/\pi\hbar)^{1/4}\exp\{-m\omega_{z}z^{2}/2\hbar\}
. Writing
\hat{\psi}(\bm{r})=\hat{\psi}(x,y)\,\phi_{0}(z)
in the contact
interaction integrals (see Eq. (
7
) and Refs.
30
;
72
), with
g=4\pi\hbar^{2}a_{s}/m
, the axial integral factorizes and yields the effective two-dimensional interaction parameter

g_{2\text{D}}=g\!\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\differential z\,|\phi_{0}(z)|^{4}=2a_{s}\sqrt{\frac{2\pi\hbar^{3}\omega_{z}}{m}}.

(56)

Along
x
and
y
in the p-orbital manifold, the bosonic field reads

\begin{split}\hat{\psi}(x,y)&=\text{p}_{x}(x,y)\hat{b}_{x}+\text{p}_{y}(x,y)\hat{b}_{y}\\
&=\psi_{+}(x,y)\hat{b}_{+}+\psi_{-}(x,y)\hat{b}_{-},\end{split}

(57)

with

\displaystyle\text{p}_{x}(x,y)\equiv\phi_{10}(x,y)=\sqrt{\dfrac{2}{\pi}}\dfrac{m}{\hbar}\left(\omega_{x}^{3}\omega_{y}\right)^{1/4}

(58)

\displaystyle\times x\,\exp\left\{-\dfrac{m}{2\hbar}\left(\omega_{x}x^{2}+\omega_{y}y^{2}\right)\right\},

\displaystyle\text{p}_{y}(x,y)\equiv\phi_{01}(x,y)=\sqrt{\dfrac{2}{\pi}}\dfrac{m}{\hbar}\left(\omega_{x}\omega_{y}^{3}\right)^{1/4}

(59)

\displaystyle\times y\,\exp\left\{-\dfrac{m}{2\hbar}\left(\omega_{x}x^{2}+\omega_{y}y^{2}\right)\right\},

and the vortices
\psi_{\pm}(x,y)\equiv(\text{p}_{x}(x,y)\pm\text{i}\text{p}_{y}(x,y))/\sqrt{2}
(Eq. (
5
)) giving
\hat{b}_{\pm}^{\dagger}=(\hat{b}_{x}^{\dagger}\pm\text{i}\hat{b}_{y}^{\dagger})/\sqrt{2}
. Projecting
\hat{H}_{\text{1b}}
(
2
) onto this manifold directly yields Eq. (
6
), with
(E_{10}+E_{01})/2=2\hbar\bar{\omega}
,
E_{10}-E_{01}=-\Delta
,

\hat{n}_{x}-\hat{n}_{y}=\hat{b}_{+}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{-}+\hat{b}_{-}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{+}
and

\hat{L}_{z}=\text{i}R\hbar(\hat{b}_{y}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{x}-\hat{b}_{x}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{y})=R\hbar(\hat{n}_{+}-\hat{n}_{-}).

(60)

The interaction Hamiltonian (
7
) is derived from the two-mode bosonic field (
57
) as

\hat{H}_{\text{int}}=\dfrac{g_{2\text{D}}}{2}\iint_{-\infty}^{\infty}\differential x\,\differential y\,\hat{\psi}^{\dagger 2}(x,y)\hat{\psi}^{2}(x,y).

(61)

Expanding the fields in the
(x,y)
-basis, only even combinations of the odd wavefunctions
\text{p}_{x}(x,y)
and
\text{p}_{y}(x,y)
survive the integral (see Eqs. (
58
) and (
59
)). As these functions are real, only two terms remain:

\begin{split}\iint_{-\infty}^{\infty}\differential x\,\differential y\,\text{p}_{x}^{4}(x,y)&=\iint_{-\infty}^{\infty}\differential x\,\differential y\,\text{p}_{y}^{4}(x,y)\\
&=\dfrac{3}{8\pi}\dfrac{m}{\hbar}\sqrt{\omega_{x}\omega_{y}},\\
\iint_{-\infty}^{\infty}\differential x\,\differential y\,\text{p}_{x}^{2}(x,y)\text{p}_{y}^{2}(x,y)&=\dfrac{1}{8\pi}\dfrac{m}{\hbar}\sqrt{\omega_{x}\omega_{y}},\end{split}

(62)

which finally gives Eq. (
7
):

\begin{split}\hat{H}_{\text{int}}=\,&\dfrac{U}{2}(\hat{b}_{x}^{\dagger 2}\hat{b}_{x}^{2}+\hat{b}_{y}^{\dagger 2}\hat{b}_{y}^{2})+\dfrac{U}{6}(\hat{b}_{x}^{\dagger 2}\hat{b}_{y}^{2}+\hat{b}_{y}^{\dagger 2}\hat{b}_{x}^{2})\\
&+\dfrac{2U}{3}(\hat{n}_{x}\hat{n}_{y})\\
=&-\dfrac{U}{3}\left(\hat{b}_{+}^{\dagger 2}\hat{b}_{+}^{2}+\hat{b}_{-}^{\dagger 2}\hat{b}_{-}^{2}\right)+\dfrac{2}{3}U\hat{N}(\hat{N}-1),\end{split}

(63)

where the interactions become non-trivially diagonal in the vortex basis
72
, and with

U=\dfrac{3a_{s}}{2}\sqrt{\dfrac{m\hbar}{2\pi}}\sqrt{\omega_{x}\omega_{y}\omega_{z}}.

(64)

Importantly, Eq. (
64
) shows that
U
scales as
\sqrt{\omega_{x}\omega_{y}}
(at fixed
\omega_{z}
), which is the same in-plane scale appearing in the weak-anisotropy condition (
3
).
This condition thus plays a double role: first, it suppresses leakage out of the p-orbital manifold (see Sec.
II.1
and Appendix
A
), which validates the two-mode description underlying Eq. (
9
). Second, it makes
\Delta
small relative to the in-plane scale
\sqrt{\omega_{x}\omega_{y}}
that also sets
U
, working toward the self-trapping regime

(see Sec.
II.3
). Reaching this regime additionally requires sufficient interaction strength (scattering length

and out-of-plane confinement
\omega_{z}

N
.

Appendix C
Experimental parameters

The question that now arises is whether the typical frequency values considered in this work, and in particular the hierarchy (
38
), are plausible within a realistic experimental setting. In the article
54
, the authors investigate a bosonic gas of rubidium 87 confined in a quasi two-dimensional harmonic potential, realized using a one-dimensional optical lattice. The trapping frequencies associated with the three spatial directions are

\displaystyle\omega_{x}/2\pi=11\,\mathrm{Hz},

(65)

\displaystyle\omega_{y}/2\pi=130\,\mathrm{Hz},

(66)

\displaystyle\omega_{z}/2\pi=3600\,\mathrm{Hz},

(67)

which corresponds to a very strong confinement along the
z
axis, ensuring the quasi two-dimensional character of the system, and to a pronounced anisotropy in the transverse
(x,y)
plane. In this configuration, the total number of rubidium 87 atoms trapped in each optical well is of the order of
N\sim 10^{5}
.

For the atomic species
87
Rb, the contact interaction can be rewritten in terms of the
s
-wave scattering length,
a_{s}\simeq 5.29\cdot 10^{-9}\,\mathrm{m}
, and the atomic mass
m=1.44\cdot 10^{-25}\,\mathrm{kg}

61
, leading to an effective interaction strength

U/\hbar\simeq 4.19\,\mathrm{rad/s}.

(68)

The anisotropy associated with the trap is given by
\Delta=\hbar(\omega_{y}-\omega_{x})
, which leads to the numerical value

\Delta/\hbar\simeq 7.48\cdot 10^{2}\,\mathrm{rad/s}.

(69)

At first glance, this anisotropy appears to be much larger than the two-body interaction scale. However, it should be recalled that the self-trapping regime is a collective phenomenon, whose relevant criterion involves the total number of particles. The appropriate quantity to consider is therefore the ratio
NU/\Delta
. Under the experimental conditions of
54
, one finds
NU/\Delta\approx 560
,
indicating that the self-trapping regime is largely satisfied in this experimental setup. As an order-of-magnitude estimate, a ratio
NU/\Delta\approx 10
could already be achieved for a particle number of the order of
N\sim 10^{3}
. The self-trapping regime may also be reached by reducing the anisotropy
\Delta
, for instance by choosing a trapping frequency
\omega_{x}
closer to
\omega_{y}
.

Given estimation (
69
), one can determine the order of magnitude for different kind of external rotation. For example, the angular frequency associated with Earth’s rotation is given by
\omega_{\mathrm{\oplus}}=7\cdot 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{rad/s}
, leading to a perturbation amplitude of

\hbar\Omega_{\oplus}/\Delta\simeq 9.3\cdot 10^{-8}.

(70)

Using Eq. (
13
), one can therefore estimate the number of measurements
\nu
needed to achieve a sensitivity that would allow to detect Earth’s rotation. For
N=10
particles and an interrogation time of
T=10^{4}\,\hbar/\Delta
, a minimal number of measurements
\nu_{\mathrm{min}}\simeq 2.9\cdot 10^{3}
would be needed. Such a value is compatible with optical-lattice architectures
109
, where hundreds of identical NOON-state interferometers could in principle be operated in parallel.

For the previously established values, the frequency hierarchy (
38
) can also be fixed for an arbitrary GCD protocol duration
T=100\,\hbar/\Delta
and for
N=10
. In this case, the different characteristic frequencies of the system read

\displaystyle\omega_{\mathrm{GCD}}\sim 1/T=\Delta/(100\hbar)\approx 7.5\,\mathrm{rad/s},

(71)

\displaystyle\omega_{\mathrm{MB}}\sim 2U(N-1)/(3\hbar)\approx 25\,\mathrm{rad/s},

(72)

\displaystyle\bar{\omega}\approx 2\pi\cdot 440\,\mathrm{rad/s}.

(73)

These order-of-magnitude estimates confirm that the parameter regime considered here is compatible with experimentally accessible conditions, while allowing for a clear separation between the different timescales of the problem. The hierarchy (
38
) therefore provides a consistent framework for analyzing the dynamics induced by the GCD protocol.

Appendix D
Perturbation theory of the NOON coupling in the self-trapping regime

To define the reduced Hamiltonian (
19
) governing the Hilbert subspace
\{\ket{N,0},\ket{0,N}\}
in the self-trapping regime, we determine how the coupling of these states to the rest of the Hilbert space lifts the degeneracy between them. Setting
\Omega=0
, we split the Bose-Hubbard Hamiltonian (
III
) as
\hat{H}=\hat{H}_{\text{int}}+\hat{V}
, where

\hat{H}_{\text{int}}=-\dfrac{U}{3}\left(\hat{b}_{+}^{\dagger 2}\hat{b}_{+}^{2}+\hat{b}_{-}^{\dagger 2}\hat{b}_{-}^{2}\right)

(74)

is the interaction term, diagonal in the Fock basis with eigenvalues

\begin{split}E_{n_{+},n_{-}}&\equiv\bra{n_+,n_-}\hat{H}_{\text{int}}\ket{n_+,n_-}\\
&=-\dfrac{U}{3}\left[2n_{+}(n_{+}-N)+N(N-1)\right],\end{split}

(75)

with
n_{-}=N-n_{+}
, and

\hat{V}=-\dfrac{\Delta}{2}\left(\hat{b}_{+}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{-}+\hat{b}_{-}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{+}\right)

(76)

is the anisotropy-induced coupling, treated as a perturbation in the self-trapping regime
NU/\Delta\gg 1
. The interaction
\hat{H}_{\text{int}}
is symmetric under
+\leftrightarrow-
, so
\ket{N,0}
and
\ket{0,N}
share the same energy
E_{N,0}=E_{0,N}
. The perturbation
\hat{V}
couples them through the rest of the Hilbert space and lifts the degeneracy, enabling collective tunneling. Since
\hat{V}
transfers one boson at a time between the two modes, and all
N
bosons must change mode, the leading contribution connecting
\ket{N,0}
and
\ket{0,N}
is of order
N
, generated along the unique path

\ket{0,N}\to\ket{1,N-1}\to\ldots\to\ket{N-1,1}\to\ket{N,0}.

(77)

The off-diagonal element of the reduced Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{red}}
connecting the two states is then

\begin{split}\bra{0,N}&\hat{H}_{\text{red}}\ket{N,0}=\\
&\frac{\langle 0,N|\hat{V}|1,N-1\rangle\ldots\langle N-1,1|\hat{V}|N,0\rangle}{(E_{N,0}-E_{1,N-1})\ldots(E_{N,0}-E_{N-1,1})}.\end{split}

(78)

For
U>0
, each factor in the denominator of Eq. (
78
) is thus negative, with
E_{N,0}-E_{k,N-k}=-2Uk(N-k)/3<0
. The product of these
N-1
terms therefore carries a factor
(-1)^{N-1}
, while the numerator, built from
N
hopping amplitudes each proportional to
-\Delta/2
, carries
(-1)^{N}
. They combine to give a matrix element

\bra{0,N}\hat{H}_{\text{red}}\ket{N,0}=-\frac{N(\Delta/2)^{N}}{(N-1)!(2U/3)^{N-1}},

(79)

that is negative for all
N
, irrespective of parity. With the convention of Eq. (
19
),
\hat{H}_{\text{red}}
carries
-\mathcal{J}
on its off-diagonal, so

\mathcal{J}=\frac{N(\Delta/2)^{N}}{(N-1)!(2U/3)^{N-1}}>0.

(80)

This sign is fixed by the attractive nature of the interaction: it places the symmetric NOON state
(\ket{N,0}+\ket{0,N})/\sqrt{2}
as the ground state, in agreement with the exact diagonalization of (
III
) for both even and odd
N
. Note that a repulsive interaction would reverse the sign of the denominators, making the sign of
\mathcal{J}
alternate with
N
and exchanging which NOON superposition is the ground state. As each order corresponds to one ”back-and-forth” process of a boson, the natural structure of higher orders in perturbation theory is of the form

\mathcal{J}=\frac{N(\Delta/2)^{N}}{(N-1)!(2U/3)^{N-1}}\Big[1+c_{1}\Big(\frac{\Delta}{U}\Big)^{2}+c_{2}\Big(\frac{\Delta}{U}\Big)^{4}+\ldots\Big],

(81)

where the
c_{i}
coefficients depend on the number of particles
N
.

One can also investigate the corrections to the vortex modes energies through perturbation theory. Since the two energy levels are perfectly symmetric, only the bias
\Omega
can lift their degeneracy. At second order, the corrections
\delta E_{\pm}
to the
+
and
-
modes are given by

\displaystyle\delta E_{+}^{(2)}=\frac{N(\Delta/2)^{2}}{-2U(N-1)/3+2\hbar\Omega}

(82)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle|\hbar\Omega|\ll U}}{{=}}-\frac{3N\Delta^{2}}{8U(N-1)}-\frac{9N\Delta^{2}}{8U^{2}(N-1)^{2}}\hbar\Omega+\mathcal{O}(\hbar^{2}\Omega^{2})

\displaystyle\delta E_{-}^{(2)}=\frac{N(\Delta/2)^{2}}{-2U(N-1)/3-2\hbar\Omega}

(83)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle|\hbar\Omega|\ll U}}{{=}}-\frac{3N\Delta^{2}}{8U(N-1)}+\frac{9N\Delta^{2}}{8U^{2}(N-1)^{2}}\hbar\Omega+\mathcal{O}(\hbar^{2}\Omega^{2}).

The first term corresponds to a symmetric energy shift that has no effect on the dynamics. The second term, proportional to
\Omega
, leads to a renormalization of the bias
\pm N_{\mathrm{eff}}\hbar\Omega
, where

N_{\mathrm{eff}}=N-\frac{9N\Delta^{2}}{8U^{2}(N-1)^{2}}+\mathcal{O}(\Delta^{4}/U^{4}).

(84)

More generally, the perturbative corrections to the diagonal terms can be decomposed into a symmetric contribution (even in
\Omega
), which shifts the global energy, and an antisymmetric contribution (odd in
\Omega
), which renormalizes the effective bias.
