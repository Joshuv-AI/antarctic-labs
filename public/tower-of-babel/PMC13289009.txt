In-bedroom renewed air as anti-inflammatory adjuvant therapy in cancer survivors: protocol for the randomised, placebo-controlled BREATHS N-of-1 trial series
Authors: Eva Hernandez-Garcia, Andrew Edkins, Evangelia Chrysikou, José M Ordóñez-Mena, Larissa Nekhlyudov, Juyong Brian Kim, Gary R McLean, Hannah Frost, Dipak Panigrahy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionInflammation is causally related to cancer progression and cardiovascular toxicities of anticancer treatments. Fine particulate matter (PM2.5) air filtration lowers interleukin-6 and C reactive protein (CRP) levels in high-risk cardiopulmonary groups, though potential synergistic or confounding effects with routine medications—statins, cyclo-oxygenase-2 inhibitors, beta-blockers—remain poorly understood. Whether overnight in-bedroom PM2.5 air filtration effectively reduces inflammation and prothrombotic biomarkers in survivors of adult-onset cancer at high cardiovascular toxicity risk is unknown.Methods and analysisBREATHS is a series of randomised, adaptive, blinded, placebo-controlled N-of-1 trials conducted in densely populated urban areas of Valencia, Spain, during winter when PM2.5 concentrations historically exceed WHO guidelines. Eligible participants are aged ≥18 years with a history of breast, colorectal, prostate, lung or haematological cancer, prior cardiotoxic cancer therapy and CRP ≥3 mg/L. Each participant will undergo up to three cycles, each comprising two 14-day periods of advanced submicron air filtration (clean air delivery rate: 275 m3/hour nightly) or placebo (sham filtration), in random order. Sequence duration ranges from 4 weeks to 12 weeks, depending on whether a clinically meaningful CRP reduction (<2 mg/L or ≥35% relative to placebo) is achieved during active treatment of each cycle. Non-responders will enter an open-label phase (OLP) of two 14-day periods: no treatment followed by continuous filtration. The primary endpoint is change in CRP between active and placebo phases. Secondary endpoints include changes in serum amyloid A, haemoglobin A1c and blood pressure. Indoor and outdoor particulate exposure will be quantified using paired real-time monitors at each home.Ethics and disseminationThe trial protocol was approved by the research ethics committees of University College London (REC UCL: 22105/001), Doctor Peset University Hospital (CEIm: 044/25) and General University Hospital of Valencia (CEIm: 1/2026 EO-PS), Spain. Results will be presented at relevant conferences and published in peer-reviewed journals.Trial registration numberNCT06778122.

Introduction

Cancer survivors are at risk for recurrence,1 second cancers2 3 and late adverse effects, including atherosclerotic and thrombotic cardiovascular (CV) complications.4 5 Survivors of breast, colorectal, prostate, lung and haematological cancers experience a higher incidence of atherosclerosis, stroke, myocardial infarction, heart failure, venous thromboembolism (VTE) and cardiac arrest.69 Anticancer therapy provokes many survivors to have residual inflammatory risk—persistently elevated chronic inflammation, typically assessed by C reactive protein (CRP) ≥2 mg/L—associated with CV toxicities and cancer progression.10 11 Additional biomarkers in this clinical context include fibrinogen, D-dimer, serum amyloid A (SAA),1214 interleukins (IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8),1517 and haemoglobin A1c (HbA1c).18 Upregulated levels of CRP and D-dimer predict atherosclerotic CV events, VTE and cardiac dysfunction,101921 while abnormalities in CRP, IL-6 and HbA1c also contribute to hypertension,22 23 diabetes,2325 dyslipidaemia12 and metabolic syndrome.18

In clinical practice, residual inflammation is unmonitored, yet anti-inflammatory (eg, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), corticosteroids) and cardioprotective agents (eg, statins, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, β-blockers) are commonly prescribed for long-term complications in survivors.2628 Cancer trials, however, show that NSAIDs have no effect on recurrence or mortality in survivors of breast, colon and non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC),2933 and that adjuvant aspirin or celecoxib in breast cancer may worsen survival30 34 and increase CV toxicity.29 30 35 Statins may ameliorate chemotherapy-induced cardiotoxicity,3641 but survival improvements have not been consistent.42 43 Glucocorticoids have been linked to increased distant recurrence in colorectal cancer44 and reduced survival in NSCLC with immune-related adverse events.45 46 Taken together, the limited evidence base precludes their routine use for recurrence prevention or cardiotoxicity mitigation.47 48

Air pollution exposure has recently been recognised as an important environmental risk factor in cardio-oncology.49 Cancer survivors—particularly those treated with chemotherapy or radiation—are more susceptible to fine particulate matter ≤2.5 µm (PM2.5) and ultrafine particle (UFP; PM0.1)-induced cardiopulmonary morbidity and mortality than cancer-free individuals,49 even at exposures below regulatory standards.50 51 Exposure to PM2.5 activates multiple proinflammatory pathways in vascular endothelial cells, macrophages and neutrophils: nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain-like receptor family protein 3 (NLRP3)/IL-1β/IL-18-mediated pyroptosis,5255 IL-6/signal transducer and activator of transcription-3 (STAT3)56 and cyclooxygenases-2/prostaglandin E2 (COX-2/PGE2).57 58 In macrophages, PM2.5 stimulates the toll-like receptor 4/nuclear factor kappa B/COX-2 pathway, amplifying IL-6 production,59 and impairs the resolution-phase clearance processes.60 This shifts lipid mediator profiles toward arachidonic acid (AA)-derived pro-inflammatory oxylipins, while suppressing COX-derived PGE2 and specialised pro-resolving mediators (SPMs) such as resolvin D1.61 62

The distinction between inflammation suppression and resolution-targeted therapies is critical in cancer survivorship.63 Cytotoxic cancer therapies generate tumour cell debris that activates pro-inflammatory pathways—NLRP3/IL-1β, IL-6/STAT3, COX-2/PGE2—driving cancer cell repopulation64 65 and CV toxicity.66 Standard anti-inflammatory agents suppress these pathways early but fail to promote debris clearance necessary for complete resolution of inflammation and return to homeostasis.67 Impaired biosynthesis of SPMs or their precursors compromise antitumorigenic, antithrombogenic and antiatherosclerotic clearance mechanisms.6770 NSAIDs inhibit PG synthesis via COX-1 and/or COX-2; however, the latter is essential for SPM production and COX-2 inhibitors dose-dependently increase atherosclerotic and thrombotic events.48 71 Corticosteroids can suppress phospholipase A2, reducing AA release and downstream COX-2/PGE2 production.72 Certain pharmacological agents exhibit pro-resolving properties, with rosuvastatin promoting bioactive lipid mediators that attenuate IL-6-mediated pro-inflammatory signalling upstream of CRP and AA-derived pro-thrombotic eicosanoids driving atherosclerotic CV disease.69 These benefits are statin-specific, time-dependent and dose-dependent and modulated by individual risk factors.73 While adverse effects are generally manageable, prolonged use of these agents may result in musculoskeletal, metabolic, hepatic74 75 and immunosuppressive effects.76 77

Air filtration is a promising non-pharmacologic adjunct in cardio-oncology to inflammation-targeting therapy. In-home particulate filtration can reduce mean indoor PM2.5 levels by nearly 79%.78 Personal use of portable air filtration units (PAFUs) represents a practical, accessible and cost-efficient intervention with potential to improve the cardiorespiratory and metabolic profile through inflammation modulation.7982 PM2.5 filtration crossover trials in cardiopulmonary patients have demonstrated marked reductions in IL-6 and IL-8, with more modest but consistent effects on CRP.83 84 The magnitude of these effects varies with concomitant use of NSAIDs, statins and vasoactive drugs.85 In stable coronary artery disease, controlling for drug-filtration interactions (statins, aspirin or β-blockers) yielded clinically meaningful CRP reductions of 21–24%.86 Particulate filtration can enable dose adjustments or deprescribing of antihistamines and/or corticosteroids.87 In addition, omega-3 fatty acids may confer protective CV effects among PM2.5-exposed individuals by enhancing pro-resolving oxylipin biosynthesis.61 88 These findings highlight the importance of considering co-administered pharmacotherapy and supplement use when evaluating air filtration efficacy.

The anti-inflammatory and cardioprotective effects of particulate filtration therapy in cancer survivors—especially vulnerable to PM2.5-mediated CV toxicity—and its interactions with concurrent treatments remain of interest. The BREATHS trial is designed to determine whether overnight in-bedroom advanced submicron particulate filtration reduces circulating inflammatory and pro-thrombotic biomarkers in adult cancer survivors with persistently elevated CRP (≥3 mg/L) who are at high risk of CV toxicity and receiving standard care. The N-of-1 trial design allows for estimation of individual-level treatment effects, supporting personalised, evidence-based decision-making tailored to the clinical and environmental complexities of cancer survivorship.8992

Trial design

This is a series of randomised, adaptive, blinded, placebo-controlled N-of-1 trials. Participants will undergo three treatment sets (cycles), each comprising a 14-day period of advanced submicron particle filtration—delivered via a PAFU operating at 275 m3/hour nightly—and a 14-day period of placebo using sham filters (standard care), in random order. A 14-day run-in period will be considered to allow for washout of effects of medications a patient may have taken before the trial starts.

Following each cycle, an interim within-person analysis will be performed to establish whether the active treatment, compared with placebo, produces a clinically meaningful benefit. The BREATHS trial considers a minimal important change (MIC) as either achieving low residual inflammatory risk, defined as a cut-off value for CRP level <2 mg/L or a mean CRP reduction of ≥35% after active treatment versus placebo. Participants achieving the target MIC after treatment set one or two will be discontinued from the blinded N-of-1 sequence.

Participants who do not meet the MIC after completing treatment set three will enter an OLP, consisting of a 14-day no-treatment period followed by 14 days of nightly and daily air filtration therapy (in-bedroom PAFU continuously operated). To minimise potential carryover effects, a minimum 2-week washout period will separate the blinded and OLPs. This interval (a 4-week period without active treatment) is intended to reduce the influence of potential cumulative biomarker changes arising from reductions in PM2.5 exposure across preceding cycles, ensuring clearer interpretation of responses during the continuous filtration phase.

The total duration of participation will range from 4 weeks to 18 weeks, depending on the number of treatment sets completed, with an additional 2 weeks if the run-in period is required (figure 2).

At initial submission (1 October 2025), recruitment had begun but no participants were enrolled. The first participant was enrolled on 23 January 2026, and the study is expected to conclude by 31 July 2026.

Study setting and rationale for site and timing selection

The intervention will be delivered within the homes of cancer survivors residing in densely populated urban areas of Valencia, Spain, that exhibit the highest levels of local air pollution and who are registered at associated primary care practices in Malilla, Russafa and Gil y Morte.

Neighbourhood selection was guided by the Index of Impact of Pollutants on the Population (IIPP), which integrates nitrogen dioxide concentration with population density. The three selected neighbourhoods exhibit the highest IIPP scores (>125) among 69 neighbourhoods of Valencia, indicating that a larger proportion of residents are exposed to air pollution levels exceeding the WHO and European Union (EU) Directive 2008/50/EC limits.98

In addition to spatial criteria, seasonal air quality patterns were considered in determining the timing of trial delivery. PM2.5 concentrations in Valencia tend to be highest during the winter months—particularly in January, February and March—based on publicly available monitoring data from 2023,99 and annual mean concentrations of 12.3 µg/m³ have been reported,100 exceeding the 2021 WHO annual guideline of 5 µg/m³ by more than a factor of two.101 The trial intervention is scheduled to begin in winter (January–March) to maximise the potential observable effects.

Participants

Adult-onset cancer survivors (age ≥18 years at cancer diagnosis) will be eligible for enrolment if they have a history of breast, colorectal, prostate, NSCL, lymphoma and leukaemia cancer, completed definitive treatment at least 12 months prior to inclusion, a blood level of CRP of 3 mg/L or more at the entry and high risk of CV toxicity (non-exclusive, additional inclusion criteria). Participants must have permanent residence in their usual home at least 3 months prior to the first collected blood sample to be eligible. Concomitant prescribed medications and dietary supplements are permitted if the regimen was initiated at least 2 weeks before the trial and remain unchanged throughout the trial in terms of modality, dose and frequency.

If CRP test results are reported in the participant’s medical records, these values will be used to establish eligibility. When a CRP test is unavailable, additional inclusion criteria associated with high or very high CV toxicity risk factors will be applied to identify survivors who are likely to have increased CRP levels in the screening phase,102 as per the protocol. According to the 2022 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines on cardio-oncology, we will screen for prior exposure to cardiotoxic cancer therapies (ie, high-dose anthracycline ≥250 mg/m2 doxorubicin equivalent), evidence of CV toxicities detected during cancer treatment or within 3–12 months after its completion and high baseline CV risk (eg, pre-existing CV disease, prior cancer treatment).103

Major exclusion criteria are plans for additional cancer therapy and/or medication prescription to start during the trial, uncontrolled chronic medical conditions—defined by clinical practice guidelines and documented in medical records—within past 6 months to the screening visit(s) and trial period and residence at urban areas outside Russafa, Malilla and Arrancapins (Gil y Morte primary care practice) neighbourhoods. The trial will exclude cancer survivors from enrolment if they plan to move out of their usual home during the trial or are unable or unwilling to spend the night in their bedroom on a regular basis, including shift workers whose schedules involve night-shift duties.

Changes in standard clinical care (eg, dose adjustment of current medications), if unavoidable during the trial, will not result in exclusion, provided these are properly documented in the electronic case report form using the Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical classification system.104 Full inclusion and exclusion criteria are available in online supplemental material 3.

CRP-based participant selection, screening and enrolment

Interested participants will be scheduled for the screening visit(s) for CRP levels at the Malilla, Russafa and Gil y Morte primary care practices. The electronic informed consent process, digitally delivered via Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap), will clearly inform prospective participants that the trial enrolment is contingent on meeting CRP-based inclusion criteria. All participants will be required to read the ethically approved, version-controlled participant information sheet (PIS) and provide written informed consent before any study procedures (online supplemental material 4). Participation is voluntary, and no incentives are offered.

Exclusion of CRP test based on clinical context

CRP measurements, whether obtained during screening visits or extracted from electronic medical records, will be excluded from eligibility determination if taken under any of the following clinical circumstances107: (a) during unstable or uncontrolled periods of chronic disease as defined by the investigator in the eligibility criteria; (b) to help diagnose chronic inflammatory conditions or autoimmune diseases (eg, rheumatoid arthritis, lupus, inflammatory bowel disease, polymyalgia rheumatica); (c) for monitoring response to a new treatment; (d) to identify a flare-up; (e) to rule out bacterial, viral or fungal infections (eg, active respiratory infection), osteomyelitis or infection recovery from surgery or other invasive procedures; (f) in individuals requiring topical, inhaled or locally immune-modulating agents at doses likely to produce acute systemic effects (eg, high-dose oral corticosteroids).

Participants who meet the full eligibility criteria will be enrolled and proceed to the intervention phase, while those who do not qualify will be informed of the specific reason(s) for their exclusion from the trial.

Randomisation and masking

Block randomisation with a block size of two and a 1:1 allocation ratio will be used to determine the treatment sequence of three repeated blocks for each individual N-of-1 trial. The random allocation sequence will be generated using a computer-based tool (https://www.randomizer.at/) and stored securely and confidentially within REDCap system.

Allocation sequences will remain masked to participants, the research team, primary care physicians and residents throughout the study. Only the designated field researcher will be unmasked to ensure accurate treatment dispensing and to support safety monitoring purposes. The randomisation order will be disclosed to participants only on completion of their N-of-1 trial, as part of their personalised feedback report that will include both numerical and graphical summaries of individual outcomes.

Intervention description

During both the active treatment and placebo periods, a commercially available PAFU (IQAir Atem X v.00.61; dimensions: 688 (H) × 640 (W) × 254 (D) mm; weight: 13 kg), will be installed in each participant’s bedroom. This advanced air filtering system uses HyperHEPA HF filtration technology, effectively removing submicron particles down to the UFP size range (PM0.1, in addition to PM2.5) and exceeding the filtration efficiency of standard high efficiency particulate air (HEPA) filters, while operating at a comfortable noise level. The PAFU includes eight fan speed settings, with clean air delivery rate (CADR) values ranging from 110 m³/hour to 800 m³/hour, and sound pressure levels at 1 m (LPA) ranging from 15 dB(A) to 54 dB(A) for the lowest and highest fan speed settings, respectively.

Each participant will be provided with one PAFU and two filter sets: one set of HyperHEPA HF particle filters for the active treatment periods and one set of sham filters for the placebo periods. Filters will be identical in appearance and operational noise to preserve blinding integrity. Participants will be instructed to remain in the bedroom for at least seven consecutive hours each night, within a time window between 22:00 and 10:00, and to follow a consistent bedtime routine throughout the trial. Bedroom doors and windows should remain closed during each session. The PAFU will be set to turn on automatically at 21:00 at fan speed 6 (CADR: 550 m3/hour) for 60 min prior to the bedtime to precondition the room and then operate at fan speed 3 (24 dB(A); CADR: 275 m3/hour) during the overnight session.

Earlier bedtime routines (eg, beginning at 21:00) will be permitted if consistently maintained throughout the trial, and the PAFU schedule will be programmed accordingly to participant’s agreed-upon routine. Where noise disturbance is an issue for the participant, the air filtration will be delivered in doses decreasing sequentially from fan speed 3 to 2 (18 dB(A); CADR: 195 m³/hour). If necessary, fan speed mode 1 (15 dB(A); CADR: 110 m3/hour) may be allowed for more sensitive subjects with sleep disturbances, at the discretion of the research team.

During the OLP, the nightly filtration intervention will remain identical to that of the blinded treatments sets. Additionally, participants will be encouraged to remain in their bedrooms during daytime hours for potential activities when possible (eg, resting, reading or napping). They will keep an activity log documenting the time spent indoors, including time in the bedrooms and outdoors. As the effects of PM2.5 air filtration depend on cumulative inhaled dose—determined by exposure concentration and duration—the OLP is intended to increase the daily duration of reduced PM2.5 exposure, which may enhance biomarker responses.

Remote particulate matter monitoring

Indoor PM2.5 concentrations within the home-built environment (H-BE) can frequently exceed the WHO 24-hour guideline, driven by a combination of indoor emissions and infiltration of outdoor air.108 Indoor sources alone are estimated to contribute approximately 31% to total household PM2.5 levels.109 The urban neighbourhoods targeted for recruitment—Russafa, Arrancapins and Malilla—are characterised by predominantly older residential buildings, constructed between 30 years and 70 years ago,110 which tend to exhibit reduced energy-efficiency and poor airtightness. As a result, these H-BEs are more susceptible to substantial outdoor-to-indoor infiltration of airborne pollutants. To account for this, we will collect detailed data on H-BE characteristics—including building age, renovation year (if applicable), construction materials, visible cracks or wall openings, heating, ventilation and air-conditioning infrastructure—and co-locate both indoor and outdoor monitors to accurately estimate indoor/outdoor PM2.5 ratios.111

Continuous, real-time monitoring of indoor and outdoor airborne pollutants will be conducted using two separate environmental monitors (IQAir AirVisual Outdoor; size, 202 (L) × 162 (H) × 69 (W) mm), each equipped with sensors to record seven environmental parameters at all times: PM1, PM2.5, PM10, air quality index, temperature, humidity, air pressure and carbon dioxide concentrations. Devices will be connected to the IQAir cloud platform via Wi-Fi or 4G cellular connection. Data will be securely accessible only to the study team through the IQAir AirVisual Enterprise Dashboard for centralised monitoring and analysis.

Outcomes

Primary Outcome

The primary endpoint is the change in blood CRP after 14-day period of active filtration compared with placebo within each cycle, analysed as a continuous variable.

Rationale for establishing the minimal important change and operational outcome

The MIC threshold was predefined to reflect changes in CRP—based on both standard and high-sensitivity assays—that are clinically relevant for mitigating inflammation-driven risks of cardiac complications and cancer progression in survivors.112 This was informed by epidemiological and interventional trial data on the dose-response relationship between CRP levels, CV events and cancer outcomes.

Epidemiological evidence

Variability in the CRP-cancer survival relationship is attributed to different clinically relevant thresholds, particularly the categories used for CV risk prediction in clinical settings: low-risk (<1.0 mg/L), moderate-risk (1.0–3.0 mg/L) and high-risk (>3–10 mg/L).107 Findings from the Women’s Healthy Eating and Living study confirmed that CRP >3 mg/L was associated with increased cancer-specific mortality.113 A meta-analysis of over 4 80 000 subjects reported that CRP >3 mg/L was associated with a pooled relative risk of 1.32 (95% CI 1.21 to 1.45) for cancer mortality and 2.02 (95% CI 1.70 to 2.41) for CV mortality compared with the lower CRP group.114 Each 1 mg/L increase in CRP was linked to a 6% higher risk of cancer death. Additionally, breast and lung cancer survivors with CRP ≥2 mg/L had significantly reduced left ventricular ejection fraction and cardiac reserve and impaired diastolic function nearly 2 years post radiotherapy compared with those with lower CRP.10 These data underscore the prognostic value of CRP as an early biomarker of cancer therapy-induced cardiotoxicity.

Although no randomised clinical trials (RCTs) to date have assessed the impact of air filtration therapy on hard clinical endpoints and its correlation with CRP levels, findings from inflammation-lowering drug trials offer relevant benchmarks to inform and contextualise the BREATHS trial design115:

Cancer RCTs: in phase II and III trials (ABC, ADD-ASPIRIN, CALGB/SWOG, REACT, REMAGUS02, SURGUVANT and PRO-STAT) evaluating the effects of anti-inflammatory agents—celecoxib, aspirin, atorvastatin or taurolidine—on cancer recurrence or survival, none reported post-treatment inflammatory biomarkers.293133 43 116 117 The APC trial showed that CRP >3 mg/L predicted increased cardiotoxic risk from taking celecoxib in patients with colorectal adenoma.35

Remote home monitoring for physiological outcomes

As part of the decentralised N-of-1 trial design, physiological data will be collected using reliable, accurate, home-based methods.

Point-of-Care testing

Blood microsamples will be collected at baseline and on days 7 and 14 after following each active treatment and placebo period, within a fixed morning time window (07:00–10:00). If blood tests are taken during suspected acute inflammatory episodes, the underlying medical conditions will be documented.

Point-of-care testing (POCT) enables accurate, repeated measurement of CRP, SAA and HbA1c levels using capillary whole blood microsamples collected at home.128130 We will use the commercially available LS-4000 in vitro diagnostic analyser, which employs time-resolved fluorescence immunoassay (TRFIA) technology alongside minimally-invasive rapid test kits (PremaLabs Diagnostics, London, UK; Lansion Biotechnology, Nanjing, China). TRFIA has demonstrated clinical performance comparable to standard laboratory assays, enabling timely and accurate biomarker quantification from small volumes of capillary blood, and supports a home-based testing strategy for frequent, low-burden biomarker assessment (summarised in online supplemental table 1).129 131

Automated BP monitoring

Prior to blood collection, participants will self-measure systolic and diastolic BP under fasting conditions and before taking medications, at baseline and once weekly throughout the trial.

In accordance with European Society of Hypertension guidelines, BP will be recorded as the mean of three automated readings taken at 60 s intervals using the UA-1020-W oscillometric upper-arm cuff device (A&D Instruments, Abingdon, UK).132 133 This validated, automated monitor is well-suited for unsupervised home use and incorporates features to facilitate these multiple readings. Studies have shown that automated BP measurement avoids white-coat effects and provides more reliable estimates than 24-hour ambulatory BP monitoring.134 135 Regular home BP monitoring is critical for managing cancer treatment-related BP fluctuations and addressing prehypertension or high-normal BP ranges, especially the PM2.5-mediated prohypertensive changes,58 thereby empowering cancer survivors in the long-term CV risk management.136

Safety assessments and adverse event reporting

In the context of EU medical device legislation, this research trial is classified as low-risk as the procedures are not expected to introduce any harm beyond that encountered during usual care for the condition.138 Participants may experience physical and psychological effects, including injuries from trips or falls related to the placement of the PAFU, sound discomfort from the device’s operation and distress stemming from awareness of their elevated CRP levels at entry and the associated risks.

To minimise the potential for adverse events, the PAFU will be placed in a secure area without blocking passage and the participants’ sleep disturbance will be monitored and assessed using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), permitting CADR adjustments if necessary. If a participant exhibits signs of distress at any point during the trial, they will be asked about their comfort in continuing and reminded of their right to withdraw without penalty. If the participant wishes to continue but remains distressed, the research team will decide whether to end their trial cycle, following discussion of their concerns and ensuring they are not visibly distressed when leaving. If distress persists after withdrawal, their General Practitioner (GP) will be notified for further assessment and support. If unexpected results related to the CRP (>10 mg/L), SAA (>10 mg/L), HbA1c (>6.5% (42 mmol/mol)) and BP levels (systolic BP ≥160 mm Hg or ≥130 mm Hg without antihypertensive medications) are identified, the research team will immediately report them to the GP for appropriate triage.

Any serious adverse event related to the intervention or research procedure will be recorded (in the electronic case report form if it relates to the participant’s health status) and reported to the sponsor (University College London) within 24 hours of the research team becoming aware of the event.

Evaluation period

The cost-effectiveness assessment and delivery of individualised N-of-1 trial reports will take place after participants complete the final treatment phase.

Sample size estimation

Based on prior studies establishing the MIC for this trial, a sample size of eight participants will provide 80% power to detect a 1.1 mg/L reduction in CRP, corresponding to a ≥35% decrease between active filtration and placebo, assuming baseline CRP levels ≥3 mg/L at study entry. This assumption reflects the trial inclusion criteria and is consistent with a lower incidence of major adverse CV events (see the Primary outcome section). To account for an anticipated 20% dropout rate, we plan to enrol 10 participants.

Sample size estimation was conducted using equations from Yang et al, implemented via a publicly available R Shiny tool (https://jiabeiyang.shinyapps.io/SampleSizeNof1/).146 The Yang equations provide one of the few purpose-built, analytically derived approaches for sample size estimation in a series of N-of-1 trials, accounting for within-individual variance, autocorrelation and the repeated-crossover structure. A random effects model was used to estimate the population average treatment effect and determine the optimal combination of N-of-1 design components. A common treatment effect across participants and a random intercept were assumed, which provides a more stable estimation when the number of measurements per participant is small146—specifically, two measurements per period in the BREATHS trial. The final design was completed to also satisfy SE requirements for estimating individual-specific treatment effect estimation.

Statistical analysis

Individual N-of-1 Trials

Statistical analysis will be performed separately for each N-of-1 trial to estimate the individual treatment effect. Each participant’s treatment response will be assessed by comparing changes in the primary and secondary outcomes from baseline between active filtration and placebo periods within each treatment cycle. Means and 95% CIs will be calculated for the within-cycle differences for each outcome (biomarkers and BP).

Combined analysis of N-of-1 trials

Following the method of Zucker et al, individual N-of-1 trials will be aggregated using hierarchical Bayesian mixed-effects models to estimate the population average treatment effect that incorporates variance in the effect of the intervention on outcomes, compared with placebo, within and between participants.147 The average of the between-participant level values of the mean difference in outcome change will estimate the population average treatment effect. Within-participant level estimates will describe an improved estimate of each participant’s true effect assuming exchangeability across patients. All participants who complete at least one treatment set will be included in the aggregated analysis, including all available data.

Interim analysis

An interim analysis will be conducted following the completion of treatment set one and again after the treatment set two in each N-of-1 trial to evaluate whether the active filtration therapy shows a clinically meaningful benefit for the individual. If the MIC is achieved, participants will be advised to discontinue the N-of-1 trial.

Two cancer survivors were involved in assisting with the study proposal and their contribution ensured the trial design would be feasible for them to consider. Survivors checked the PIS and trial design-related documents for this purpose.

Discussion

Cancer survivors face a persistent yet under-monitored and inadequately managed burden of non-resolving inflammation, which contributes to both atherosclerotic/thrombotic CV events and cancer progression.149 150 Despite promising epidemiological and preclinical data, conventional anti-inflammatory and cardioprotective agents have not consistently demonstrated benefits in improving cancer survival or reducing CV toxicity when tested in RCTs involving cancer survivors.48 Pro-resolving therapies—such as aspirin and omega-3 fatty acids—have shown variable effects on SPM pathways in primary CV prevention, influenced by dose, body composition, sex and biological phenotypes (eg, hyper-reactive platelet).151157 Preclinical findings support the plausibility of SPM-enhancing interventions, yet their clinical potential in cancer-specific RCTs remains largely underexamined.158

Among non-pharmacological strategies, in-home air filtration has emerged as a practical, safe and cost-effective intervention to mitigate PM2.5-induced inflammation and CV toxicity.159 160 This is timely, given increasing mechanistic evidence linking PM2.5 exposure to dual detrimental effects: (1) activation and/or amplification of pro-inflammatory cascades (eg, NLRP3/IL-1β, IL-6/STAT3, COX-2/PGE2)161 and (2) suppression of resolution pathways critical for vascular homeostasis and debris clearance. While oxidative stress is an additional key mechanism in PM2.5 exposure,162 closely interacting with inflammatory pathways described above, it is not directly assessed in this trial. However, reductions in PM2.5 through filtration are expected to attenuate oxidative burden.163

Since 2010, PM2.5 has been broadly recognised as a modifiable cardiopulmonary risk factor by major societies of cardiology, including the American Heart Association (AHA),162 164 American Thoracic Society165 and ESC.166 Despite these acknowledgements, current cardio-oncology rehabilitation guidelines—including those from AHA, American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO), ESC/International Cardio-Oncology Society (ICOS) and European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO)—do not integrate air pollution exposure risk assessment or particulate filtration strategies103 167 168—likely reflecting the lack of RCTs evaluating its clinical efficacy in cancer survivor populations.169

The BREATHS trial addresses this evidence gap by testing whether personalised, in-bedroom, particulate filtration can reduce residual inflammation—measured by CRP and other circulating inflammatory and thrombotic biomarkers—in cancer survivors at high risk of CV toxicity and with persistently elevated inflammation. It aligns with core survivorship and CV care principles endorsed by ASCO and ESMO, and the AHA and ESC—emphasising personalised, risk-stratified, patient-centred approaches that support patient self-management and hybrid/hourome-based delivery.167 170 171 Furthermore, the 2017 Lancet Commission on Pollution and Public Health underscores the importance of empowering individuals to reduce pollutant exposure within their own H-BEs.172 Precisely, BREATHS operationalises these guiding principles through the integration of:

N-of-1 trial design, which accommodates the clinical heterogeneity among cancer survivors in terms of stable multimorbidity and polypharmacy,26 173 allowing individualised evaluation of how concurrent therapies (eg, statins, omega-3s) may interact with filtration in modulating inflammation.

The BREATHS trial has several potential limitations. The small sample size (n=10) and inclusion of participants from five cancer types may constrain the generalisability of findings. However, the N-of-1 trial design—with six repeated crossover periods per participant—enhances statistical power, supports within-person causal inference and focuses on inflammation as a shared pathophysiological mechanism, supporting the broader relevance of findings to cancer survivor populations at high risk for CV toxicity. Recruitment may also be challenging due to the requirement for elevated CRP (≥3 mg/L) confirmed within a narrow timeframe (28 days and 7 days before trial initiation). In addition, excluding participants without home internet access—necessary for remote air quality monitoring—may limit participation from underserved groups and affect sample representativeness. While participants will self-report behavioural and lifestyle factors weekly using validated instruments, and concurrent therapies are expected to remain stable, potential effect modification cannot be entirely ruled out. Participant adherence may also be influenced by the burden of weekly data collection. To mitigate this, participants will be instructed to maintain consistent habits throughout the trial, and questionnaires will be configured to auto-populate previous responses when no behavioural changes are reported.

BREATHS expands the scope of cardio-oncology rehabilitation by introducing a safe, scalable, non-pharmacologic intervention beyond conventional therapies. Findings may provide foundational evidence to support integration of H-BE intervention strategies for PM2.5 mitigation as personalised measured within next-generation, multimodal inflammation-focused cardio-oncology rehabilitation frameworks.
