Injective and pseudo-injective polynomial equations:From permutations to dynamical systems
Antonio E. Porreca, Marius Rolland

Abstract
We study the computational complexity of decomposing finite discrete dynamical systems (FDDSs) in terms of the semiring operations of alternative and synchronous execution, which is useful for the analysis of discrete phenomena in science and engineering. More specifically, we investigate univariate polynomials of the form P ⁡ ( X ) = B P(X)=B , that is with a constant side, first over the subsemiring of permutations and then over general FDDSs. We find a characterization of injective polynomials P P and efficient algorithms for solving the associated equations. Then, we introduce the more general notion of pseudo-injective polynomial, which is based on a condition on the lengths of the limit cycles of its coefficients, and prove that the corresponding equations are also solvable efficiently. These results also apply even when permutations are encoded in an exponentially more compact way.



keywords
dynamical systems, functional digraphs, direct product, computational complexity

Introduction

Finite discrete dynamical systems (FDDSs) are, from a mathematical point of view, simply (transition) functions on a finite domain of states. As the term “dynamical” implies, we are interested in the evolution of the states of the system under iterated applications of the transition function. The domain being finite, any such trajectory, after a transient phase, eventually ends up in a limit cycle. The study of the limit cycles (and, in particular, the fixed points, cycles of length
1
) of a FDDS is of great interest for applications, for instance when considering biological phenomena modeled as Boolean automata network.

When the FDDSs being analyzed become large, this analysis may become computationally intensive, and decomposing the FDDSs into smaller ones is often desirable, with the goal of establishing the global behavior from that of its subsystems. Several forms of decomposition are possible, but here we follow the approach pioneered by
2
and consider the category-theoretic operations of sum and product in the category of FDDSs, which correspond to their alternative and synchronous parallel execution, respectively. These endow FDDSs with a semiring algebraic structure, which allows us to express several decomposition problems in terms of (possibly multivariate) polynomial equations
P(\vec{X})=Q(\vec{X})
.

As already proved in
2
, these equations are undecidable in their general form by reduction from Hilbert’s tenth problem; they become decidable with an
\mathbf{NP}
upper bound when one of the two sides of the equation is constant (
P(\vec{X})=B)
, although this is
\mathbf{NP}
-complete even in the linear case for multivariate polynomials
(
1
)
.

However, several interesting restricted subclasses of equations have been found to admit polynomial-time solution algorithms.
10
prove that
k
-th root extraction (
X^{k}=B
) can be carried out in polynomial time if
B
is a permutation (which only consists in limit cycles, without transients).
Linear, univariate equations (
AX=B
) can be solved efficiently if
A
and
B
are dendrons (connected systems with a fixed point), as showed by
9
, or if
A
is a cycle and
B
several copies of the same cycle, even if their lengths are expressed in binary
(
3
)
, or again if
B
is a permutation where all cycles have powers of the same prime number as lengths
(
10
)
. Furthermore,
P(X)=B
can be solved in polynomial time if the coefficients of
P
and
B
only consist in sums of fixed points, which corresponds to equations over the natural numbers
(
10
)
.

In this paper we generalize several of the above-cited results. First of all, we prove that
P(X)=B
can be solved efficiently whenever
P
is an injective polynomial, together with a simple characterization of this class of polynomials: these are exactly the polynomials where a fixed point appears in one of its coefficients (except that of the constant monomial). This condition was proved to be sufficient, but not proved to be necessary, for arbitrary (not necessarily functional) digraphs by
6
;
7
, while
11
;
9
characterize cancelable FDDSs (i.e., injectivity for the restricted case of polynomials of the form
AX
) in terms of the same condition.

We also extend the notion of injectivity to pseudo-injectivity: all coefficients of
P
, excluding the constant monomial, must contain cycles of length multiple of the shortest one. We then prove that pseudo-injective polynomial equations with a constant side also admit a polynomial-time solution algorithm.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows: after introducing some preliminaries (
section 2
), we first consider polynomials over the permutations (
section 3
), a sufficient condition for their injectivity and a necessary one which also holds for arbitrary FDDSs, as well as pseudo-injective polynomials over the permutations, together with efficient algorithms for the corresponding equations. In
section 4
we prove that these equations can be solved efficiently even if the permutations are encoded compactly in binary.
In
section 5
we recall the notion of unroll of FDDSs, which allows us to define efficient algorithms that take their transient behavior into account, and prove that arbitrary polynomial equations over the unrolls can also be solved efficiently.
This is exploited in
section 6
to give efficient algorithms for pseudo-injective equations over arbitrary FDDSs, for which we also give a characterization of injective polynomials.
Finally, in
section 7
we suggest several open problems for further research.

This article is an extended version of the conference papers
12
;
13
, including new results (notably
51
and
Theorem 22
) and new tools (
30
) which allow us to describe a unified algorithm and proof technique schema for all problems investigated here, which is more intuitive and more modular in terms of presentation, with simplified proofs.

Preliminaries

A
finite deterministic dynamical system
(FDDS) is a pair
(A,f_{A})
where
A
is a finite set of states and
f_{A}:A\to A
is a total function called the
transition function
.
The set of FDDSs
up to isomorphism
, with mutually exclusive alternative execution as
sum
and synchronous parallel execution as
product
, forms a semiring
(
2
)
, denoted by
\mathbb{D}
.
The two operations above define a notion of algebraic decomposition of FDDSs.

The semiring of functional digraphs, with disjoint union for sum and direct product for multiplication, is isomorphic to
\mathbb{D}
.
We recall
(
5
)
that the direct product of two graphs
A,B
is the graph
C
such that
V(C)=V(A)\times V(B)
and

E(C)=\{((u,u^{\prime}),(v,v^{\prime}))\mid(u,v)\in E(A),(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})\in E(B)\}.

An example of the product of two FDDSs is depicted in
Figure 1
.
As for natural numbers, we sometimes employ the partial operation of subtraction, by writing
A-B=C
if and only if
A=B+C
.

Then, an FDDS can be seen as the sum of connected components, where each component consists of a cycle, representing the periodic behavior, and in-trees (trees directed from leaf to root) rooted in the cycle, representing the transient behavior.
In this paper, trees are represented by minuscule bold letters (for example
\mathbf{t}
) and forests (sums of trees) by majuscule bold letters (for example
\mathbf{F}
).

We can also identify two sets of special FDDSs.
First, the sets of FDDSs without transient nodes, which correspond to
permutations
; in other words, a permutation
A
is a sum of cycles.
In symbols, if
C_{a}
is the cycle of length
a
then
A=C_{a_{1}}+\cdots+C_{a_{m}}
with
a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}
the cycle lengths in
A
.
The second kind of special FDDSs are those where each component is a
dendron
, that is to say a connected component with a cycle of length
1
.
Note that both types of FDDSs are subsemirings of
\mathbb{D}
.

Given two FDDSs
A
and
B
, if there exists another FDDS
C
such that
A+C=B
then we say that
A
is a submultiset of
B
(more precisely, the connected components of
A
are a submultiset of the connected components of
B
).

Figure 1:
Product of two FDDSs
A
and
B
, where the states are given a temporary label in order to show how the result is computed (for brevity, the vertices
(u,v)
of the product are denoted here by
uv
). Remark that
B
is cancelable (state
6

AB
is pseudo-cancelable (
L(AB)=\{2,4\}
and
\ell(AB)=\min(L(AB))=2
).
A

L(A)=\{2\}
and
\ell(A)=2
).

The structure of the product is algebraically rich.
For example, this semiring does not admit unique factorizations into irreducibles, that is to say, there exist four irreducible FDDSs
A,B,C,D
such that
A\neq C
and
A\neq D
but
AB=CD
; furthermore, polynomial equations of the form
P(X)=B
may have multiple solutions (in other words, not all nontrivial polynomials are injective).
Moreover, while the periodic behavior of a product is easy to analyze, its transient behavior demands much more work.
Indeed, the product of two connected FDDS
A,B
, having cycles of length
p
and
q
respectively,
generates
\gcd(p,q)
connected components, each with a cycle of size
\lcm(p,q)

(
5
)
.

In this paper, we will give several efficient algorithms for solving the equation
P(X)=B
, with
P
a polynomial over FDDSs and
B
an FDDS.
More precisely, the equation will be solved in polynomial time with respect to the size (number of states or vertices)
|B|
of
B
, since this bounds the size
|P(X)|
of the left-hand side, allowing us to avoid potential super-polynomial computations (for instance, if the degree of
P
is exponential with respect to
|B|
).

Polynomials over the permutations

In this section we show that a polynomial over the permutations is injective if and only if at least one of its non-constant coefficients contains a fixed point.
We begin by showing that this is a sufficient condition.
The proof will be constructive and will allow us to formulate an efficient algorithm for solving
P(X)=B
when
P
respects the above constraint and
B
is a permutation; we first focus on polynomials of the form
X^{k}
(that is, on computing
k
-th roots) and then generalize.
We will also show that this condition for injectivity is necessary for arbitrary polynomials over the FDDSs, and not only over the permutations.

3.1
Roots over the permutations

In this section we consider polynomials
P
over the permutations of the form

k

Note that

however, the advantage of our algorithm is that it can be easily extended to more general injective polynomials

In the following proofs, we process the cycles of

More precisely we consider the

The first notion is important because we construct the solution starting from

This assertion is detailed in the next lemma.

Lemma 1
.

\Lambda_{p}\colon\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{D}
is a semiring endomorphism.

Proof.

Since

\Lambda_{p}(1)=\mathbf{1}
; furthermore
\Lambda_{p}(0)=\mathbf{0}
.
Since the sum in

\Lambda_{p}(A+B)=\Lambda_{p}(A)+\Lambda_{p}(B)

In order to prove that
\Lambda_{p}(AB)=\Lambda_{p}(A)\Lambda_{p}(B)
it suffices to consider the case of connected

and

All connected components of

have cycles of length

Hence, either
\lcm\{\ell(A),\ell(B)\}

\Lambda_{p}(AB)=AB

\Lambda_{p}(AB)=\mathbf{0}

But
\lcm\{\ell(A),\ell(B)\}

∎

Theorem 6

Proof.

As a consequence,

Since comparisons and well-defined subtractions of multisets can also be computed in polynomial time, the results follows.

3.2
Sufficient condition for the injectivity of polynomials over the permutations

In order to extend
3

to arbitrary polynomials over the permutations where a non-constant coefficient contains a fixed point, we begin by generalizing

Lemma 7
.

Let
P=\sum_{i=0}^{k}A_{i}X^{i}
be an injective polynomial over the FDDSs.
Let
X=X_{1}+\cdots+X_{m}
be an FDDS with connected components
X_{1}\leq_{\mathrm{ct}}\cdots\leq_{\mathrm{ct}}X_{m}
.
Finally, let
0\leq i<m

Then,
\ell(X_{i+1})=\ell(P(X)-P(\mathcal{\pi}_{i}(X)))=\ell(X_{i+1})

Proof.

Remark that
\ell(A_{j}(X^{j}-(\mathcal{\pi}_{i}(X))^{j}))
is greater than or equal to
\ell(A_{j})
and to
\ell(X^{j}-(\mathcal{\pi}_{i}(X))^{j})
for all

Hence, by

\ell(A_{j}(X^{j}-(\mathcal{\pi}_{i}(X))^{j}))\geq\ell(X_{i+1})

Since

\displaystyle P(X)-P(\mathcal{\pi}_{i}(X))=\sum_{j=1}^{m}A_{j}(X^{j}-(\mathcal{\pi}_{i}(X))^{j})

we deduce that
\ell(P(X)-P(\mathcal{\pi}_{i}(X)))\geq\ell(X_{i+1})

For the reverse inequality, since some

with

contains a fixed point, the term
A_{j}(X^{j}-(\mathcal{\pi}_{i}(X))^{j})
contains a cycle of length

and thus
\ell(P(X)-P(\mathcal{\pi}_{i}(X)))\leq\ell(X_{i+1})
.
∎

Proposition 10

L(A)-\{a\}
.
We define
J_{1},\ldots,J_{n}
as the subsets of
L(A)
such that
J_{i}=I_{i}\cup\{a\}
for all
i
.
Then
Y=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\beta_{I_{i}}C_{\lcm(I_{i})}+\beta_{J_{i}}C_{\lcm(J_{i})})
.
By distributing powers and products over the sum we obtain that
C_{a}Y^{k}
is equal to:

\displaystyle\underset{k_{1}+\cdots+k_{n}=k}{\sum}

\displaystyle\binom{k}{k_{1},\ldots,k_{n}}\Biggl(\Big(C_{a}(\beta_{I_{1}}C_{\lcm(I_{1})}+\beta_{J_{1}}C_{\lcm(J_{1})}\Big)^{k_{1}}

\displaystyle\prod_{i=2}^{n}\Big(\beta_{I_{i}}C_{\lcm(I_{i})}+\beta_{J_{i}}C_{\lcm(J_{i})}\Big)^{k_{i}}\Biggr).

From
11
we deduce that
C_{a}(\beta_{I_{1}}C_{\lcm(I_{1})}+\beta_{J_{1}}C_{\lcm(J_{1})})^{k_{1}}=C_{a}(\alpha_{I_{1}}C_{\lcm(I_{1})}+\alpha_{J_{1}}C_{\lcm(J_{1})})^{k_{1}}
.
Thus by replacing these terms in the previous equation, we obtain that
C_{a}Y^{k}
is equal to:

\displaystyle\underset{k_{1}+\cdots+k_{n}=k}{\sum}

\displaystyle\binom{k}{k_{1},\ldots,k_{n}}\Bigg(C_{a}\Big(\alpha_{I_{1}}C_{\lcm(I_{1})}+\alpha_{J_{1}}C_{\lcm(J_{1})}\Big)^{k_{1}}

\displaystyle\prod_{i=2}^{n}\Big(\beta_{I_{i}}C_{\lcm(I_{i})}+\beta_{J_{i}}C_{\lcm(J_{i})}\Big)^{k_{i}}\Bigg).

By repeating this substitution for all
i
between
2
and
n
, it follows that
C_{a}Y^{k}
is equal to:

C_{a}\underset{k_{1}+\cdots+k_{n}=k}{\sum}\binom{k}{k_{1},\ldots,k_{n}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}(\alpha_{I_{i}}C_{\lcm(I_{i})}+\alpha_{J_{i}}C_{\lcm(J_{i})})^{k_{i}}.

Since
X^{k}=\sum_{k_{1}+\cdots+k_{n}=k}\binom{k}{k_{1},\ldots,k_{n}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}(\alpha_{I_{i}}C_{\lcm(I_{i})}+\alpha_{J_{i}}C_{\lcm(J_{i})})^{k_{i}}
, we conclude that
C_{a}Y^{k}=C_{a}X^{k}
.

This property is true for each connected component
C_{a}
of
A
, therefore by summing all the terms and by making the necessary factorizations, we conclude that
AX^{k}=AY^{k}
.
∎

Notice that the product of a generic FDDS with a permutation has a particular form.
Indeed, if we consider an FDDS
A=A_{1}+\cdots+A_{m_{A}}
and a permutation
X=X_{1}+\cdots+X_{m_{X}}
such that each
A_{i}
and each
X_{j}
are connected then, for each
i
and
j
, the FDDS
A_{i}X_{j}
has
\gcd(\ell(A_{i}),\ell(X_{j}))
connected component with cycle length
\lcm(\ell(A_{i}),\ell(X_{j}))
and the sequence of the trees rooted in their cycle are the trees rooted in
A_{i}
repeated periodically.

Lemma 13

(
9
;
16
;
14
)
.

Let
A
be a connected FDDS and
X
a permutation.
Then, the rooted trees in the cycle of each connected component of
AX
are the sequence of rooted trees in the cycle of
A_{i}
periodically repeated. ∎

Corollary 14

(
9
;
16
;
14
)
.

Let
A
be a connected FDDS and
X,Y
be two permutations.
If
C_{\ell(A)}X=C_{\ell(A)}Y
then
AX=AY
.

Proof.

Suppose that
C_{\ell(A)}X=C_{\ell(A)}Y
.
Then, there exists a bijective function
f
between the connected components of
C_{\ell(A)}X
and those of
C_{\ell(A)}Y
such that
\ell(f(D))=\ell(D)
for all connected components
D
of
C_{\ell(A)}X
.

Now consider
AX
and
AY
.
There exist two bijective functions
g
and
h
from the set of connected components of
AX
and
AY
, respectively, to the connected components of
C_{\ell(A)}X
and
C_{\ell(A)}Y
, respectively, such that
g(D)=\ell(D)
and
h(D)=\ell(D)
.

By
13
, the rooted trees of each connected component of
AX
and
AY
are the sequence of the rooted trees in the cycle of
A
periodically repeated.
Therefore, for all connected components
D_{X}
of
AX
, the components
D_{X}
and
D_{Y}=h^{-1}(f(g(D_{X})))
have the same sequence of trees rooted in their cycle.
Furthermore, by definition,
D_{X}
and
D_{Y}
have the same cycle length.
Therefore,
D_{X}
=
D_{Y}
.
∎

Thanks to this property we can generalize
12
to the case where
A
is an FDDS without dendrons.
This gives us a necessary condition for the injectivity of monomials over FDDSs.

Proposition 15
.

Let
AX^{k}
with
k>0
.
If
A
does not contain a dendron then
AX^{k}
is not injective.

Proof.

Let
A=A_{1}+\cdots+A_{m_{A}}
, where each
A_{i}
is a connected component.
Suppose that
A
does not contain a dendron, that is, it does not contain a connected component with cycle length
1
.
Let
A^{\prime}=A_{1}^{\prime}+\cdots+A_{m_{A}}^{\prime}
be the permutation such that
\ell(A_{i}^{\prime})=\ell(A_{i})
for all
i
.
By the proof of
12
, there exist two different permutations
X
and
Y
such that
A_{i}^{\prime}X^{k}=A_{i}^{\prime}Y^{k}
.
Therefore, by
14
, we have that
A_{i}X^{k}=A_{i}Y^{k}
.
By summation,
AX^{k}=AY^{k}
.
∎

Corollary 16
.

Let
A
be an FDDS not containing a dendron.
Then
AX^{k}
is not injective for any integer
k>0
. ∎

To complete the necessary condition over the injectivity, we prove that a polynomial
P
is not injective if a monomial derived from
P
is not injective.
The coefficient of this monomial is just the sum of all the non-constant coefficients of the polynomial, and its degree is strictly positive.
That is, we will show that
P=\sum_{i=0}^{m}A_{i}X^{i}
is not injective if
(A_{1}+\cdots+A_{m})X^{k}
is not injective for any integer
k>0
.

Theorem 17
.

Let
P=\sum_{i=0}^{m}A_{i}X^{i}
be a polynomial over FDDSs.
If no non-constant coefficient of
P
contains a dendron then
P
is not injective.

Proof.

Consider the monomial
M=(\sum_{i=1}^{m}A_{i})X
.
Let
X
and
Y
be the permutations constructed in the proof of
15
.
As previously
X\neq Y
but
A_{i}X^{i}=A_{i}Y^{i}
for all
i
between
1
and
m
.
Therefore, by summation we have
P(X)=P(Y)
.
∎

3.4
Pseudo-injective polynomials over the permutations

By combining
Theorem 17
and
10
, we prove that a polynomial over the permutations is injective if and only if one of its non-constant monomials has a coefficient containing a fixed point.
From this characterization, we observe that the smallest cycle length, let us call it
g
, in the non-constant coefficients is a divisor of the set of cycle lengths of the non-constant coefficients because
g=1
.
We call polynomials satisfying this condition, without requiring
g=1
,
pseudo-injective
polynomials.
The number
g
will be called the
seed
of the polynomial.
Remark that the pseudo-injective polynomials of seed
1
are exactly the injective ones.
If the polynomial is a linear monomial, that is, if
P=AX
, we also say that the FDDS
A
is
pseudo-cancelable
, as this property is a generalization of cancelability.

It is important to notice that these notions are only based on the form of the polynomials and not on its “degree of injectivity”.
That is to say, they do not
a priori
limit the number of different FDDSs
X_{1},\ldots,X_{r}
such that
P(X_{1})=\cdots=P(X_{r})
.

In the following we show that we can efficiently solve the equation
P(X)=B
if
P
is a pseudo-injective polynomial over the permutations and
B
is a permutation.
We begin with the simplest case: the resolution of the equation
C_{a}C_{x}=bC_{b}
.
A simple method for solving these equations is to enumerate all the integers
j
between
1
and
b
and check for each of them if
C_{a}C_{j}=bC_{b}
.
This is polynomial-time over
b
.

Let us analyze what can be the possible values of
j
.
To do this, recall that if
C_{a}C_{x}=bC_{b}
, then
\lcm(a,x)=b
.
Therefore, by definition of
\lcm
, if we consider the prime factorizations
\prod p_{i}^{a_{i}}
and
\prod p_{i}^{x_{i}}
of
a
and
x
respectively, where
p_{i}
is the
i
-th prime, then
\lcm(a,x)=s_{x}s_{a}e
with

\displaystyle s_{x}=\prod_{a_{i}<x_{i}}p_{i}^{x_{i}}

\displaystyle s_{a}=\prod_{x_{i}<a_{i}}p_{i}^{a_{i}}

\displaystyle e=\prod_{a_{i}=x_{i}}p_{i}^{a_{i}}.

We deduce that
s_{x}
is the smallest integer such that
b=\lcm(a,s_{x})
.
In the following, if
a
divides
b
, this number
s_{x}
will be called the
anti-
\lcm
of
b
with respect to
a
.

Definition 18
.

Let
a,b>0
such that
a
divides
b
.
Then, the
anti-
\lcm
of
b
with respect to
a
, in symbols
\alcm_{a}b
, is the smallest
c
such that
\lcm\{a,c\}=b
.

For brevity, we also define the anti-
\lcm
of an FDDS
B
with respect to an FDDS
A
as
\alcm_{A}B=\alcm_{\ell(A)}\ell(B)
, and its anti-
\lcm
with respect to
a
as
\alcm_{a}B=\alcm_{a}\ell(B)
.

Notice that
\alcm_{a}b
is always well-defined thanks to the hypothesis that
a
divides
b
, which implies the existence of at least one
c
such that
\lcm\{a,c\}=b
, namely
b
itself; furthermore, since all such
c
are natural numbers, there exist a unique minimum one.

In the following, for better readability, we shorten
a=\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}p_{i}^{a_{i}}
as
a=\prod p_{i}^{a_{i}}
.
Furthermore, if we also have
b=\prod p_{i}^{b_{i}}
, then we write
\prod_{b_{i}>a_{i}}p_{i}^{b_{i}}
for the product of the primes having larger exponent in
b
than in
a
(taken with that exponent), and similarly for
\prod_{b_{i}\leq a_{i}}p_{i}^{b_{i}}
and
\prod_{b_{i}=a_{i}}p_{i}^{b_{i}}
.
Finally, recall that
\lcm\{a,b\}=\prod p_{i}^{\max\{a_{i},b_{i}\}}
and
\gcd\{a,b\}=\prod p_{i}^{\min\{a_{i},b_{i}\}}
.

Theorem 19
.

Suppose that
a
divides
b
and let
a=\prod p_{i}^{a_{i}}
and
b=\prod p_{i}^{b_{i}}
be their prime factorizations.
Then
\alcm_{a}b=\prod_{b_{i}>a_{i}}p_{i}^{b_{i}}
.

Proof.

First of all, let us prove that we indeed have
\lcm\big\{a,\prod_{b_{i}>a_{i}}p_{i}^{b_{i}}\big\}=b
.
We have

\displaystyle\lcm\Big\{a,\prod_{b_{i}>a_{i}}p_{i}^{b_{i}}\Big\}

\displaystyle=\lcm\Big\{\prod p_{i}^{a_{i}},\prod_{b_{i}>a_{i}}p_{i}^{b_{i}}\Big\}

\displaystyle=\lcm\Big\{\prod_{b_{i}\leq a_{i}}p_{i}^{a_{i}}\times\prod_{b_{i}>a_{i}}p_{i}^{a_{i}},\prod_{b_{i}>a_{i}}p_{i}^{b_{i}}\Big\}.

Since
a
divides
b
, we have
b_{i}\geq a_{i}
for all
i
and thus

\displaystyle\prod_{b_{i}\leq a_{i}}p_{i}^{a_{i}}=\prod_{b_{i}=a_{i}}p_{i}^{a_{i}}

which implies

\displaystyle\lcm\{a,\alcm_{a}b\}

\displaystyle=\lcm\Big\{\prod_{b_{i}\geq a_{i}}p_{i}^{a_{i}},\prod_{b_{i}>a_{i}}p_{i}^{b_{i}}\Big\}

\displaystyle=\prod_{b_{i}=a_{i}}p_{i}^{a_{i}}\times\prod_{b_{i}>a_{i}}p_{i}^{b_{i}}=\prod_{b_{i}\geq a_{i}}p_{i}^{b_{i}}=b.

Now suppose that
\lcm\{a,c\}=b
and let
c=\prod p_{i}^{c_{i}}
be its prime factorization.
Then
b_{i}=\max\{a_{i},c_{i}\}
for all
i
; hence, whenever
b_{i}>a_{i}
we have
b_{i}=c_{i}
and thus
b_{i}\leq c_{i}
;
this means that
\prod_{b_{i}>a_{i}}p_{i}^{b_{i}}
divides
c
, implying
\prod_{b_{i}>a_{i}}p_{i}^{b_{i}}\leq c
as required.
∎

This proof actually shows that
\alcm_{a}b
is not only the smallest integer having the properties required by
18
, but also the minimum one with respect to divisibility, which is a more useful property.

Theorem 20
.

The integer
\alcm_{a}b
is minimal with respect to the divisibility partial order among the
c
satisfying
\lcm\{a,c\}=b
.
∎

We can actually prove an even stronger property of the solutions of
\lcm\{a,c\}=b
.

Theorem 21
.

If
\lcm\{a,c\}=b
, then the integer
\frac{c}{\alcm_{a}b}
is a divisor of
a
and coprime with
\alcm_{a}b
.

Proof.

Notice that
\frac{c}{\alcm_{a}b}
is indeed an integer by
Theorem 20
.

By
Theorem 19
and by recalling that if
a=\prod p_{i}^{a_{i}}
,
b=\prod p_{i}^{b_{i}}
, and
c=\prod p_{i}^{c_{i}}
then
b_{i}=\max\{a_{i},c_{i}\}
for all
i
by a property of the
\lcm
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{c}{\alcm_{a}b}

\displaystyle=\frac{\prod p_{i}^{c_{i}}}{\prod_{b_{i}>a_{i}}p_{i}^{b_{i}}}=\frac{\prod_{b_{i}\leq a_{i}}p_{i}^{c_{i}}\times\prod_{b_{i}>a_{i}}p_{i}^{c_{i}}}{\prod_{b_{i}>a_{i}}p_{i}^{b_{i}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\prod_{b_{i}\leq a_{i}}p_{i}^{a_{i}}\times\prod_{b_{i}>a_{i}}p_{i}^{b_{i}}}{\prod_{b_{i}>a_{i}}p_{i}^{b_{i}}}=\prod_{b_{i}\leq a_{i}}p_{i}^{a_{i}}

which is, by inspection of its factorization, a divisor of
a
and coprime with
\alcm_{a}b
.
∎

From this, we can formulate an algorithm with polynomial runtime with respect to
\log b
for computing
\alcm_{a}b
.
Indeed, the computation of
\alcm_{a}b
can be obtained by reduction to a
\gcd
as follows.

Theorem 22
.

Let
a,b>0
such that
a
divides
b
and let
a=\prod p_{i}^{a_{i}}
and
b=\prod p_{i}^{b_{i}}
be their prime factorizations.
Then we have
\alcm_{a}b=\gcd\big\{\big(\frac{b}{a}\big)^{k},b\big\}
whenever
k\geq b_{i}
for all
i
.

Proof.

Remark that
\frac{b}{a}=\prod_{i=0}^{\infty}p_{i}^{b_{i}-a_{i}}
and
(\frac{b}{a})^{k}=\prod_{i=0}^{\infty}p_{i}^{k(b_{i}-a_{i})}
.
Let
p_{i}
be a prime number.
Then, either
b_{i}=a_{i}
, implying that
p_{i}
has exponent
0
in
\frac{b}{a}
and thus in
\gcd\big\{(\frac{b}{a})^{k},b\big\}
, or
b_{i}>a_{i}
, implying
k(b_{i}-a_{i})>b_{i}
by the hypothesis on
k
.
In the latter case,
p_{i}
has exponent
b_{i}
in
\gcd\big\{(\frac{b}{a})^{k},b\big\}
.
In other words, we have

\displaystyle\gcd\Big\{\Big(\frac{b}{a}\Big)^{k},b\Big\}=\prod_{b_{i}>a_{i}}p_{i}^{b_{i}}=\alcm_{a}b

as required.
∎

Theorem 23
.

The value of
\alcm_{a}b
can be computed in polynomial time with respect to
\log_{2}b
.

Proof.

Let
k=\lceil\log_{2}b\rceil
; then
k\geq b_{i}
for all
i
and hence is a suitable upper bound for computing
\alcm_{a}b
.
The value of
\frac{b}{a}
can be computed
\mathcal{O}(1)
time and the value of
\big(\frac{b}{a}\big)^{k}
in
\mathcal{O}(\log b)
time under the uniform cost model.
Finally,
\gcd\big\{\big(\frac{b}{a}\big)^{k},b\big\}
can be computed in polynomial time with respect to the number of bits of the operands, which is
\mathcal{O}(\log^{2}b)
and
\mathcal{O}(\log b)
respectively.
∎

Summarizing, in order to solve
C_{a}C_{x}=bC_{b}
, we can just compute
\alcm_{a}b
and check if
b/\gcd(a,\alcm_{a}b)
is coprime with
\alcm_{a}b
.
Indeed, if there exists a solution then
x=q\alcm_{a}b
with
q
a divisor of
a
coprime with
\alcm_{a}b
.
Furthermore, the number of copies of
C_{b}
in
C_{a}C_{x}
is
\gcd(a,q\alcm_{a}b)=\gcd(a,\alcm_{a}b)q
.

We can intuitively think that it would be possible to extend this reasoning to the case where
A
and/or
B
are arbitrary permutations.
This would give us a procedure similar to the following one.

Algorithm 24
.

Let
A
and
B
be permutations.
Let
X=\mathbf{0}
.
While
B\neq 0
:

•

let
a
and
b
be the multiplicities of

in
AC_{\alcm_{A}B}
and

respectively;

•

set

and

.

If at any moment
|A|>|B|
or
AC_{\alcm_{A}B}
is not a submultiset of
B
, then the quotient is undefined.
Otherwise, return the final value of
X
.

Theorem 25
.

24
runs in polynomial time.

Proof.

All operations on permutations can be computed in polynomial time, as can
\alcm_{A}B
by
Theorem 23
; furthermore, the number of iterations of the algorithm is bounded by the size of
B
.
∎

However, this intuition is incorrect.

Example 26
.

Consider the equation
(C_{2}+C_{3})X=5C_{6}
.
24
does not find a solution because
\alcm_{2}6=3
and
3C_{3}
is not a submultiset of
5C_{6}
.
But
(C_{2}+C_{3})C_{6}=5C_{6}
.

We will, however, prove that this approach does indeed work for
AX=B
if
A
is pseudo-cancelable.

Lemma 27
.

Let
A,X
and
B\neq 0
be permutations with
A
pseudo-cancelable.
If
AX=B
, then
C_{d\alcm_{A}B}\leq X
for some divisor
d
of
\ell(A)
coprime with
\alcm_{A}B
.

Proof.

Let
C_{b}
be a cycle of minimal length in
B
.
Then
C_{b}=C_{x}C_{a}
for some cycles
C_{x}\leq X
and
C_{a}\leq A
and
b=\lcm\{x,a\}
.
Since
d=\frac{x}{\alcm_{A}B}
is a divisor of
\ell(A)
coprime with
\alcm_{A}B
by
Theorem 21
, we have
C_{x}=C_{d}C_{\alcm_{A}B}=C_{d\alcm_{A}B}\leq X
.
∎

Although
27
partially describes a cycle of
X
, one unknown remains: what value to give to
d
.
In the following lemma, we show that we can resolve this unknown by setting the value of
d
to
1
.
Once again, this is only possible since
A
is pseudo-cancelable.
We have already illustrated this with
(C_{2}+C_{3})C_{6}=5C_{6}
in
26
.

Lemma 28
.

Let
A
and
X
be permutations with
A
pseudo-cancelable, let
B=AX
, and let
d
be a divisor of
\ell(A)
coprime with
\alcm_{A}B
.
Then
A(X+C_{d\alcm_{A}B})=A(X+eC_{\frac{d}{e}\alcm_{A}B})=B
for all divisors
e
of
d
.

Proof.

Let
C_{a}
be a cycle in
A
.
Since
d
is coprime with
\alcm_{A}B
, we have

\displaystyle\gcd\{d\alcm_{A}B,a\}=\gcd\{\alcm_{A}B,a\}\times\gcd\{d,a\}.

(1)

Since
A
is pseudo-cancelable,
\ell(A)
divides
a
; by transitivity,

,
e
, and
\frac{d}{e}
also divide

.
Hence
\gcd\{d,a\}=d
and
d=e\times\frac{d}{e}=e\times\gcd\big\{\frac{d}{e},a\big\}
, and
Equation 1
becomes

\displaystyle\gcd\{d\alcm_{A}B,a\}

\displaystyle=\gcd\{\alcm_{A}B,a\}\times d

\displaystyle=\gcd\{\alcm_{A}B,a\}\times e\times\gcd\big\{\textstyle\frac{d}{e},a\big\}

\displaystyle=\gcd\big\{\textstyle\frac{d}{e}\alcm_{A}B,a\big\}\times e.

Furthermore, since
\frac{d}{e}
divides
a
and is coprime with
\alcm_{A}B
, we have

\displaystyle\lcm\big\{\textstyle\frac{d}{e}\alcm_{A}B,a\big\}

\displaystyle=\lcm\big\{\lcm\big\{\textstyle\frac{d}{e},a\big\},\lcm\{\alcm_{A}B,a\}\big\}

\displaystyle=\lcm\{a,\lcm\{\alcm_{A}B,a\}\}

\displaystyle=\lcm\{\alcm_{A}B,a\}.

This implies

\displaystyle C_{a}C_{d\alcm_{A}B}

\displaystyle=\gcd\{d\alcm_{A}B,a\}\times C_{\lcm\{d\alcm_{A}B,a\}}

\displaystyle=\gcd\big\{\textstyle\frac{d}{e}\alcm_{A}B,a\big\}\times e\times C_{\lcm\{\frac{d}{e}\alcm_{A}B,a\}}

\displaystyle=C_{a}\times eC_{\frac{d}{e}\alcm_{A}B}

By summation on all cycles
C_{a}
of

, the result follows.
∎

Remark that
24
constructs a permutation by cycle length.
However, the order of generation is not necessarily given by
\leq_{\mathrm{ct}}
.
This is because, even for a fixed integer
g
, the function
\alcm_{g}x
is not monotonically increasing.

Example 29
.

For
g=2
, we have
4<6
but
\alcm_{2}4=4>\alcm_{2}6=3
.

In order to solve this problem we introduce a new total order over connected components, defined both in terms of a polynomial and of
\leq_{\mathrm{ct}}
.
This allows us to compare different related components with respect to a context.

Definition 30
.

Let
P=\sum_{i=1}^{k}A_{i}X^{i}
be a polynomial without constant term and let
X
and
Y
be two connected FDDSs.
Let
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
be, respectively, the smallest connected components of
P(X)
and
P(Y)
according to
\leq_{\mathrm{ct}}
.
We say that
X\preceq_{P}Y
if and only if
B_{1}<_{\mathrm{ct}}B_{2}
, or if
B_{1}=B_{2}
and
X\leq_{\mathrm{ct}}Y
.

If
P
is linear, i.e., if
P=AX
, then we also write
\preceq_{A}
for

Lemma 38

the number of copies of
C_{\alcm_{g}B}
found by the algorithm.
But this is direct since
n
is obtained by a binary search of
y_{i+1}
.
Indeed, by hypothesis,

P(X+nC_{p})-P(X)=P(\mathcal{P}_{i}(Y)+nC_{p})-P(\mathcal{P}_{i}(Y)).

Besides, by definition of
Y
, the set of cycles having minimal length in
B-P(\mathcal{P}_{i}(Y))
comes from a product with
Y_{i+1}
.
Thus, if
n>y_{i+1}
then
P(\mathcal{P}_{i}(Y)+nC_{p})-P(\mathcal{P}_{i}(Y))
is not a submultiset of
B-P(\mathcal{P}_{i+1}(Y))
and if
n<y_{i+1}
then
B-(P(\mathcal{P}_{i}(Y)+nC_{p})-P(\mathcal{P}_{i}(Y)))
contains some cycles of length
\ell(B-P(\mathcal{P}_{i}(Y)))
while it is not the case for
B-P(\mathcal{P}_{i+1}(Y))
.
The correctness of the algorithm follows.

To analyze the complexity, notice that the number of iterations of the loop is bounded by
c
.
In addition, in this loop we perform a binary search whose number of iterations is bounded by
\log q
.
Thus, by a similar reasoning as the proof of
39
, it follows that the operations in this binary search have complexity
\mathcal{O}(c\log(cp))
and that the other operations in the loop require
\mathcal{O}(\log d(\log\log d))
time.
In total, we have thus
\mathcal{O}(c^{2}\log(cp)\log q+c\log p(\log\log p))
time.
∎

Corollary 43
.

Let

be a pseudo-injective polynomial over the permutations, and let

be a permutation.
If

and

are given as input by their explicit graphs, then the computation of a

such that

5
Equations over the unrolls

In this section, we explain how we can manage the transient behaviors of an FDDS in order to extend
8

to pseudo-injective polynomials over general FDDSs.
For this, we will exploit the notion of
unroll
in order to take into account the transient behavior of an FDDS.

Definition 44

(Unroll)
.

Let
A=(A,f)
be an connected FDDS.
The
unroll
of
A
, denoted by
\mathcal{U}(A)
, is the infinite graph
G=(V,E)
whose nodes are the pairs
(a,k)
of states
a\in A
and nonnegative integers
k
such that
(a,k)\in V
if and only if the exists a node
u
of the limit cycle of
A
such that
f^{k}(a)=u
.
Moreover, the set
E
contains all the arcs from
(a,k)
to
(a^{\prime},k^{\prime})
such that
f(a)=a^{\prime}
and
k^{\prime}=k-1
.

We usually consider unrolls up to isomorphism, in other words without node names.
Since each connected FDDS only has one cycle, the definition of unroll implies that
\mathcal{U}(A)
is a forest of infinite trees where each tree has exactly one infinite branch, on which the trees representing the transient behavior of
A
are periodically rooted.
Remark that the unroll of a connected FDDS may contain isomorphic trees resulting from symmetries in the original graph.
We can generalized the notion of unroll to disconnected FDDSs by summing the unrolls of its connected components;
in symbols, if
A=A_{1}+\cdots+A_{n}
where each
A_{i}
is connected then
\mathcal{U}(A)=\mathcal{U}(A_{1})+\cdots+\mathcal{U}(A_{n})
.

This notion was originally introduced in
9
;
8
and exploited in
4
in order to show that any two solutions of
AX^{k}=B
have the same transient behavior;
that is, if
AX^{k}=B=AY^{k}
then
\mathcal{U}(X)=\mathcal{U}(Y)
.
To prove this property, a tree product, denoted by
\times
, such that
\mathcal{U}(A\times B)=\mathcal{U}(A)\times\mathcal{U}(B)
was introduced.
Intuitively, this product is the Cartesian product applied level by level.
In order to define it, let
0pt{v}
be the distance of the node
v
from the root of the tree.

Definition 45

(Product of trees)
.

Consider two trees
\mathbf{t}_{1}=(V_{1},E_{1})
and
\mathbf{t}_{2}=(V_{2},E_{2})
with roots
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
, respectively. Their
product
is the tree
\mathbf{t}_{1}\times\mathbf{t}_{2}=(V,E)
such that

V=\{(v,u)\in V_{1}\times V_{2}\mid 0pt{v}=0pt{u}\}
and

E=\{((v,u),(v^{\prime},u^{\prime}))\mid(v,u)\in V,(v^{\prime},u^{\prime})\in V,(v,v^{\prime})\in E_{1},(u,u^{\prime})\in E_{2}\}
.

Note that the product of two trees has the minimal depth of its factors, where the depth of a tree
\mathbf{t}
, denoted by
0pt{\mathbf{t}}
, is the greatest length of a path between a leaf and the root. We generalize the notion of depth to forests by taking the maximum depth of its trees.
As we will often compute the product of trees with different depths, we introduce the notion of
cut
of a tree
\mathbf{t}
to depth
d
, denoted by
\mathscr{C}_{d}(\mathbf{t})
, as the subgraph of
\mathbf{t}
induced by the nodes of
\mathbf{t}
having depth at most
d
.
Therefore, we have
\mathbf{t}_{1}\times\mathbf{t}_{2}=\mathscr{C}_{d}(\mathbf{t}_{1})\mathscr{C}_{d}(\mathbf{t}_{2})
where
d
the minimum of the depths of
\mathbf{t}_{1}
and
\mathbf{t}_{2}
.

The set of forests with the disjoint union as addition and the above levelwise product for multiplication is a commutative semiring, with
\mathbf{0}
being the empty forest and
\mathbf{1}
the infinite path.
Notice that the set of unrolls of FDDSs with the same operations is one of its sub-semirings.

A fundamental property which is useful when working with unrolls is that exists a total order
\leq
on trees compatible with the product (introduce in
9
), that is,
\mathbf{t}_{1}\leq\mathbf{t}_{2}
if and only if
\mathbf{t}_{1}\mathbf{t}\leq\mathbf{t}_{2}\mathbf{t}
for all tree
\mathbf{t}
.
By exploiting this fact,
9
;
8
prove the following lemma.

Lemma 46
.

Let
\mathbf{t}_{1},\mathbf{t}_{2}
and
\mathbf{t}_{3}
be trees.
Then,
\mathbf{t}_{1}\mathbf{t}_{3}\leq\mathbf{t}_{2}\mathbf{t}_{3}
if and only if
\mathscr{C}_{0pt{\mathbf{t}_{3}}}(\mathbf{t}_{1})\leq\mathscr{C}_{0pt{\mathbf{t}_{3}}}(\mathbf{t}_{2})
.

The order
\leq
on trees and the previous lemma play a major role in our next proofs.

Since our goal is to solve the equation
\sum_{i=0}^{m}\mathcal{U}(A_{i})\mathcal{U}(X)^{i}=\mathcal{U}(B)
efficiently with respect to the sizes of
A_{i}
and
B
, let us point out that we cannot directly use the unrolls themselves, as they are infinite objects.
However, we can extend
(
4
, Lemmas 5.5 5.6)
in order to show that only a finite portion is actually needed.

Theorem 47
.

Let
A_{0},\ldots,A_{m}
and
B
be FDDSs and let
n\geq 2\alpha^{2}+h
, where
\alpha
the number of nodes in the cycles of
B
and
h
the maximum depth of the trees rooted in the cycles of
B
.
Then
\sum_{i=0}^{m}\mathcal{U}(A_{i})\mathcal{U}(X)^{i}=\mathcal{U}(B)
admits a solution if and only if
\sum_{i=0}^{m}\mathscr{C}_{n}(\mathcal{U}(A_{i}))\mathbf{X}=\mathscr{C}_{n}(\mathcal{U}(B))
admits a solution.

Consequently, in order to solve the equation
\sum_{i=0}^{m}\mathcal{U}(A_{i})\mathcal{U}(X)^{i}=\mathcal{U}(B)
efficiently, we just need to efficiently solve the equation
\sum_{i=0}^{m}\mathscr{C}_{n}(\mathcal{U}(A_{i}))\mathbf{X}^{i}=\mathscr{C}_{n}(\mathcal{U}(B))
and build an FDDS from the solution of the latter equation.

Since we can use the same strategy of
4
to construct an FDDS
X
from the cut of its unroll to sufficient depth
n
(which can be done efficiently), it remains to prove that we can efficiently solve
\sum_{i=0}^{m}\mathscr{C}_{n}(\mathcal{U}(A_{i}))\mathbf{X}=\mathscr{C}_{n}(\mathcal{U}(B))
.
In fact, we will prove a more general property: namely, that we can efficiently solve
\sum_{i=0}^{m}\mathbf{A}_{i}\mathbf{X}^{i}=\mathbf{B}
for arbitrary finite forests
\mathbf{A}_{i}
and
\mathbf{B}
.
In the remainder of this section, we will prove multiple results by induction on the depth
d
of a forest
\mathbf{F}
.
In these proofs, we do not necessarily consider
\mathbf{F}
itself, but rather a subset of
\mathbf{F}
, denoted
\Gamma_{d}(\mathbf{F})
, containing all the trees of
\mathbf{F}

whose depth is at least
d
.
Note that the definitions of sum and product imply that
\Gamma_{d}(\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{B})=\Gamma_{d}(\mathbf{A})+\Gamma_{d}(\mathbf{B})
and
\Gamma_{d}(\mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{B})=\Gamma_{d}(\mathbf{A})\times\Gamma_{d}(\mathbf{B})
for all forests
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
and all integers
d
.

For our algorithm, we would like to be able to identify from
\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{t}_{1}+\ldots+\mathbf{t}_{n}
, where
\mathbf{t}_{i}\leq\mathbf{t}_{i+1}
, the smallest tree of
P(\mathbf{X})
originating from
\min(\mathbf{A}_{j})\mathbf{t}_{i}\mathbf{X}^{j-1}
, for some
j
and having the same depth as
\mathbf{t}_{i}
, and this for all possible
\mathbf{t}_{i}
.
The idea is that, in order to know each
\mathbf{x}_{i}
of
\mathbf{X}
, we perform a division by
\min(\mathbf{A}_{j})
to obtain a tree
\mathbf{t}
, then either compute its root, if
\mathbf{t}=\mathbf{t}_{i}^{j}
, or divide
\mathbf{t}
by the smallest tree from the already computed portion of
\mathbf{X}
, raised to the power
k-1
.

Lemma 48
.

Let
d\geq 0
be an integer and let
P=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbf{A}_{i}\mathbf{X}
be a polynomial over finite forests (without
\mathbf{A}_{0}
) such that all trees of
\mathbf{A}_{i}
have depth at least
d
.
Let
\mathbf{X}
be a forest of depth
d
.

Then, there exists an integer
1\leq r\leq m
such that every
\mathbf{x}_{j}
of
\mathbf{X}
having depth
h
satisfies

\displaystyle\min\big\{\mathbf{a}\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}_{j}\mid 1\leq i\leq m,\mathbf{a}\in\mathbf{A}_{i},\mathbf{x}\in\Gamma_{0pt{h}}(\mathbf{X})^{i-1}\big\}=

\displaystyle=\min(\mathbf{A}_{r})\min(\Gamma_{h}(\mathbf{X}))^{r-1}\mathbf{x}_{j}.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{x}_{j}
be a tree of
\mathbf{X}
of depth
h
.
Let
\mathbf{x}_{1}
be the smallest tree of
\mathbf{X}
with depth at least
h
, in symbols
\mathbf{x}_{1}=\min(\Gamma_{h}(\mathbf{X}))
.
Let us begin by proving the case where
\mathbf{x}_{j}=\mathbf{x}_{1}
.

Let
\mathbf{t}_{\mathrm{min}}=\min(\Gamma_{h}(P(\mathbf{X})))
.
Then, there exists an integer
1\leq r\leq m
such that the forest
\Gamma_{h}(\mathbf{A}_{r})\Gamma_{h}(\mathbf{X})^{r}
contains
\mathbf{t}_{\mathrm{min}}
.
Let
\mathbf{a}_{1}
be the smallest tree in
\mathbf{A}_{r}
.
Note that, by definition,
\mathbf{a}_{1}
has depth greater than or equal to
h
.

Since the order is consistent with the product, it follows that
\mathbf{x}_{1}^{2}\leq\mathbf{x}_{1}\mathbf{x}_{k}
for any tree
\mathbf{x}_{k}
of
\Gamma_{h}(\mathbf{X})
.
By induction, we deduce that
\mathbf{t}_{\mathrm{min}}
is a tree of
\Gamma_{h}(\mathbf{A}_{r})\mathbf{x}_{1}^{r}
.
Similarly, we also have
\mathbf{a}_{1}\mathbf{x}_{1}^{r}\leq\mathbf{a}_{k}\mathbf{x}_{1}^{r}
for all
\mathbf{a}_{k}\in\Gamma_{d}(\mathbf{A}_{r})
, and we conclude that
\mathbf{t}_{\mathrm{min}}=\mathbf{a}_{1}\mathbf{x}_{1}^{r}
.
The property is therefore true for
\mathbf{x}_{j}=\mathbf{x}_{1}
.

Now assume
\mathbf{x}_{j}\neq\mathbf{x}_{1}
, implying that
\mathbf{x}_{j}>\mathbf{x}_{1}
.
Using the result from the previous point, we deduce from
46
that

\mathscr{C}_{0pt{\mathbf{x}_{1}}}(\mathbf{a}_{1}\mathbf{x}_{1}^{r-1})\leq\mathscr{C}_{0pt{\mathbf{x}_{1}}}(\mathbf{a}\mathbf{x}_{1}^{i-1})

and this holds true for any integer
1\leq i\leq m
and any tree
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbf{A}_{i}
.
Note that by the definition of
\mathbf{x}_{1}
we have that
0pt{\mathbf{x}_{1}}\geq h
implying that

\mathscr{C}_{h}(\mathbf{a}_{1}\mathbf{x}_{1}^{r-1})\leq\mathscr{C}_{h}(\mathbf{a}\mathbf{x}_{1}^{i-1}).

Indeed, since the order comes from a breadth-first search (
9
), we are able to prove that
\mathbf{a}\leq\mathbf{b}
if and only if
\mathscr{C}_{n}(\mathbf{a})\leq\mathscr{C}_{n}(\mathbf{b})
for all integers
n\geq 0
.

Since
\mathbf{x}_{j}
has depth
h
, we have
\mathscr{C}_{h}(\mathbf{a}_{1}\mathbf{x}_{1}^{r-1})\mathbf{x}_{j}=\mathbf{a}_{1}\mathbf{x}_{1}^{r-1}\mathbf{x}_{j}
and
\mathscr{C}_{h}(\mathbf{a}\mathbf{x}_{1}^{i-1})\mathbf{x}_{j}=\mathbf{a}\mathbf{x}_{1}^{i-1}\mathbf{x}_{j}
.
We conclude that
\mathbf{a}_{1}\mathbf{x}_{1}^{r-1}\mathbf{x}_{j}\leq\mathbf{a}\mathbf{x}_{1}^{i-1}\mathbf{x}_{j}
.
And since
\mathbf{x}_{1}^{k-1}\leq\mathbf{x}
for all integer
k>0
and with
\mathbf{x}\in\Gamma_{h}(\mathbf{X})^{k-1}

∎

Thanks to
48
we can show the first main result of this section.
Note that the set of finite forests of depth at most
d_{\mathrm{max}}
with the disjoint union for addition and the product of trees of multiplication is a semiring.

Proposition 49
.

Let
P=\sum_{i=0}^{m}\mathbf{A}_{i}\mathbf{X}^{i}
with
d_{\mathrm{max}}=\max_{i>0}(0pt{\mathbf{A}_{i})}
and
0pt{\mathbf{A}_{0}}\leq d_{\mathrm{max}}
.
Then,

is injective on the semiring of finite forests of depth at most
d_{\mathrm{max}}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y}
be two finite forest of depth at most
d_{\mathrm{max}}
such that
P(\mathbf{X})=P(\mathbf{Y})
.
Remark that
P(\mathbf{X})-\mathbf{A}_{0}=P(\mathbf{Y})-\mathbf{A}_{0}
.
Therefore, we can assume without loss of generality that
\mathbf{A}_{0}=\mathbf{0}
.
Let
h
be the depth of
P(\mathbf{X})
;
then

, as no tree of
\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y}
and of the coefficients of
P
have depth larger than
d_{\mathrm{max}}
.
Since one of the coefficient of
P
contains a tree of depth
d_{\mathrm{max}}
, it follow that
h=0pt{\mathbf{X}}=0pt{\mathbf{Y}}
.
Thus,
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}
verify the condition on depth of
48
.

By
48
, there exist
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
with
i\leq j
such that the smallest tree with factor
\mathbf{x}_{k}
(resp.,
\mathbf{y}_{k}
) of
\Gamma_{d}(\sum_{1}^{m}\mathbf{A_{i}}\mathbf{X}^{i})
is
\mathbf{a}_{i,1}\mathbf{x}_{1}^{i-1}\mathbf{x}_{k}
(resp.,
\mathbf{a}_{j,1}\mathbf{y}_{1}^{j-1}\mathbf{y}_{k}
), where
\mathbf{a}_{i,1}=\min(\Gamma_{h}(\mathbf{A}_{i}))
and
\mathbf{a}_{j,1}=\min(\Gamma_{h}(\mathbf{A}_{j}))
.
We deduce that
\mathbf{a}_{i,1}\mathbf{x}_{1}^{i}=\min(\Gamma_{h}(P(\mathbf{X})))=\min(\Gamma_{h}(P(\mathbf{Y})))=\mathbf{a}_{j,1}\mathbf{y}_{1}^{j}
from the hypothesis.

We have
\mathbf{x}_{1}=\mathbf{y}_{1}
.
By contradiction and without loss of generality, we assume that
\mathbf{x}_{1}<\mathbf{y}_{1}
.
Then, it follows that
\mathbf{x}_{1}^{j}<\mathbf{y}_{1}^{j}
.
Since the order is compatible with the product, we deduce that
\mathbf{a}_{j,1}\mathbf{x}_{1}^{j}<\mathbf{a}_{j,1}\mathbf{y}_{1}^{j}=\mathbf{a}_{i,1}\mathbf{x}_{1}^{i}
.
Nevertheless,
\mathbf{a}_{j,1}\mathbf{x}_{1}^{j}\in\Gamma_{h}(P(\mathbf{X}))
, which is in contradiction with the minimality of
\mathbf{a}_{i,1}\mathbf{x}_{1}^{i}
.

Finally, since
\mathbf{x}_{1}=\mathbf{y}_{1}
, we deduce that
P(\mathbf{X})-P(\mathbf{x}_{1})=P(\mathbf{Y})-P(\mathbf{y}_{1})
.
If
P(\mathbf{X})-P(\mathbf{x}_{1})
contains another tree of depth
h
, then it is also the case of
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}
.
Indeed, if is not the case, all the trees of
\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{x}_{1}
and of
\mathbf{Y}-\mathbf{y}_{1}
have depth less that
h
.
Therefore, since at least one of these trees appear in each product of

and of

, these two forests contain only trees of depth smaller than
h
.

Assume that
P(\mathbf{X})-P(\mathbf{x}_{1})
contains a tree of depth
h
.
Then, by
48
and since the order is compatible with the product, the smallest tree of
\Gamma_{h}(P(\mathbf{X})-P(\mathbf{x}_{1}))
is
\mathbf{a}_{i,1}\mathbf{x}_{1}^{i-1}\mathbf{x}_{2}
with
\mathbf{x}_{2}=\min(\Gamma_{h}(\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{x}_{1}))
.
Likewise, the smallest tree of
\Gamma_{h}(P(\mathbf{Y})-P(\mathbf{y}_{1}))
is
\mathbf{a}_{j,1}\mathbf{y}_{1}^{j-1}\mathbf{y}_{2}
with
\mathbf{y}_{2}=\min(\Gamma_{h}(\mathbf{Y}-\mathbf{y}_{1}))
.
Therefore
\mathbf{a}_{i,1}\mathbf{x}_{1}^{i-1}\mathbf{x}_{2}=\mathbf{a}_{j,1}\mathbf{y}_{1}^{j-1}\mathbf{y}_{2}

Since
\mathbf{x}_{1}=\mathbf{y}_{1}
, it follows that
\mathbf{a}_{i,1}\mathbf{x}_{1}^{i}=\mathbf{a}_{j,1}\mathbf{x}_{1}^{j}
and

implies that

\mathscr{C}_{h}(\mathbf{a}_{i,1}\mathbf{x}_{1}^{i-1})=\mathscr{C}_{h}(\mathbf{a}_{j,1}\mathbf{x}_{1}^{j-1}).

Consequently and since the order is compatible with the product, we deduce that

\displaystyle\mathscr{C}_{h}(\mathbf{a}_{i,1}\mathbf{x}_{1}^{i-1})\mathbf{x}_{2}=\mathbf{a}_{i,1}\mathbf{x}_{1}^{i-1}\mathbf{x}_{2}=

\displaystyle=\mathbf{a}_{j,1}\mathbf{x}_{1}^{j-1}\mathbf{x}_{2}=\mathscr{C}_{h}(\mathbf{a}_{j,1}\mathbf{x}_{1}^{j-1})\mathbf{x}_{2}.

\mathbf{a}_{j,1}\mathbf{x}_{1}^{j-1}\mathbf{x}_{2}=\mathbf{a}_{j,1}\mathbf{y}_{1}^{j-1}\mathbf{y}_{2}

Inductively, we have
\Gamma_{h}(\mathbf{X})=\Gamma_{h}(\mathbf{Y})
.
We can apply inductively the same reasoning on

Corollary 50
.

All polynomials over the unrolls are injective.

Proof.

Suppose that
P(\mathcal{U}(X))=P(\mathcal{U}(Y))
.
Then, for all
n
, we have
\mathscr{C}_{n}(P(\mathcal{U}(X)))=\mathscr{C}_{n}(P(\mathcal{U}(Y)))
.
If
\mathcal{U}(X)
and
\mathcal{U}(Y)
are different, there must exist an integer
n
such that
\mathscr{C}_{n}(\mathcal{U}(X))\neq\mathscr{C}_{n}(\mathcal{U}(Y))
.
However, by
49
and since
\mathscr{C}_{n}(X)
are morphism, we conclude that
\mathscr{C}_{n}(\mathcal{U}(X))=\mathscr{C}_{n}(\mathcal{U}(Y))
for all

.
∎

The proof of
49
also suggests an algorithm (inspired by Algorithm 1 of
4
) for solving polynomial equations over forests of finite trees.
By starting from the maximal depth
d_{\mathrm{max}}
, we can solve the equation
\Gamma_{d_{\mathrm{max}}}(P(\mathbf{X}))=\Gamma_{d_{\mathrm{max}}}(\mathbf{B})
.
In fact, if there exists a solution
\mathbf{X}
, we find it by constructing first
\Gamma_{d_{\mathrm{max}}}(\mathbf{X})
.
In order to do this, we solve the equation
\min(\Gamma_{d_{\mathrm{max}}}(\mathbf{A}_{i}))\mathbf{X}_{1}^{i}=\min(\Gamma_{d_{\mathrm{max}}}(\mathbf{B}))
for a certain

.
This can be accomplished by dividing
\min(\Gamma_{d_{\mathrm{max}}}(\mathbf{B}))
by
\min(\Gamma_{d_{\mathrm{max}}}(\mathbf{A}_{i}))
then computing the

-th root of this quotient.
The division can be made in polynomial time by
9
and the root extraction by
4
.

We obtain the smallest tree
\mathbf{x}_{1}
of
\Gamma_{d_{\mathrm{max}}}(\mathbf{X})
, that is,
the smallest tree of the solution having maximal depth.
Then, by
48
, we can inductively construct
\Gamma_{d_{\mathrm{max}}}(\mathbf{X})
.
Indeed, the
j
-th tree
\mathbf{x}_{j}
of this forest is equal to

\cfrac{\min(\Gamma_{d_{\mathrm{max}}}(\mathbf{B})-\Gamma_{d_{\mathrm{max}}}(P(\mathbf{x}_{1}+\ldots+\mathbf{x}_{j-1})))}{\min(\Gamma_{d_{\mathrm{max}}}(\mathbf{A}_{i})\mathbf{x}_{1}^{i-1}}.

From this, we need to find all the trees of
\mathbf{X}
whose depth is less than
d_{\mathrm{max}}
.
We inductively build
\Gamma_{d}(\mathbf{X})
from
\Gamma_{d^{\prime}}(\mathbf{X})
where

is the depth of a tree of
\mathbf{B}
and

is the smallest depth of a tree of
\mathbf{B}
greater than
d
.
This can be obtained by by computing
\mathbf{x}=\min(\Gamma_{d}(\mathbf{X}))
, which requires us to consider three trees:

1.

\mathbf{x}_{m}=\min(\Gamma_{d^{\prime}}(\mathbf{X}))
, the smallest tree of
\mathbf{X}
of depth at least

,

2.

\mathbf{t}=\min(\Gamma_{d}(P(\mathbf{x}_{m}))
, the smallest tree of

of depth least

, and

3.

\mathbf{b}=\min(\Gamma_{d}(\mathbf{B}))
, the smallest tree of
\mathbf{B}
of depth at least
d
.

Two cases are then possible:

, and then

,
or there exists a coefficient
\mathbf{A}_{j}
such that
\mathbf{a}\mathbf{x}^{j}=\mathbf{b}
with

Therefore, it suffices to compute

in order to find

Now we can find the trees of

having depth

the same way as for

\cfrac{\min(\Gamma_{d}(\mathbf{b})-\Gamma_{d}(P(\mathbf{X}))}{\mathbf{a}\mathbf{x}^{j-1}}.

We can see directly that all the operations performed in this algorithm can be carried out in polynomial time.
Furthermore, since the number of iterations is bounded by the depth of

, we deduce that this procedure has a polynomial-time complexity if we manage to find a correct coefficient in polynomial time.
Indeed,

7
Conclusions

In this paper we have described how polynomial equations
P(X)=B
in one variable over the FDDSs can be solved efficiently when the polynomial is injective (also giving a characterization of this class of polynomials) and generalized this result to the larger class of pseudo-injective ones; furthermore, we have provided efficient algorithms for the compact representation of permutations as lengths of cycles in binary.

The first natural question for future work is whether there exist larger classes of polynomials in one variable over the FDDSs that admit efficient algorithms, and some classes of polynomials that do not, possibly without a constant side for the equations. Is there a suitable generalization of pseudo-injectivity, for instance with multiple seeds? Do any of our algorithms carry over to
systems
of multiple polynomial equations?

Furthermore, we conjecture that the polynomials which admit the maximum number of points having the same image are among the pseudo-injective ones, as suggested by
59
.

One other natural direction of research is to consider polynomials in several variables. What are, in this case, the injective ones and do they admit efficient algorithms?
Is there a natural notion of pseudo-injectivity? What are the limits of decidability?

Our result of the compact representation of permutations also suggests investigating the (presumably much higher) complexity of solving polynomial equations over FDDSs represented succinctly by Boolean circuits.

Another fundamental open problem, which is related but not directly expressible in terms of equations, is the complexity of deciding if a dynamical system is reducible, i.e., the product of two smaller nontrivial ones.

For all problems analyzed in this paper and all open problems mentioned above, it would be also interesting to consider
inequalities
rather than equations, and the
enumeration
of solutions rather than decision or search. Which ones admit efficient (for instance, polynomial-delay) enumeration algorithms?
