Destress II: using the Behaviour Change Wheel and preliminary testing to co-develop a complex intervention to improve the quality of primary care responses to people experiencing poverty-related mental distress
Authors: Kathryn Berzins, Ilse Lee, Katrina Wyatt, Alison McLoughlin, Jane Horrell, Oliver Hamer, Felicity Thomas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUK policy recommends non-pharmacological interventions as a first-line response to depression, yet antidepressant prescribing continues to rise, particularly in low income communities. Structural barriers, including short consultation times and long waiting lists for talking therapies, often lead to prescribing as a pragmatic but limited response to social suffering, costing the NHS approximately £230 million annually. While clinicians and patients seek more holistic approaches, there is a lack of actionable guidance on how to implement these alternatives within primary care service pressures. The aim of this study was to co-develop a theory-informed intervention to help primary care staff improve their understanding of how poverty and social determinants impact mental health. The intervention seeks to build empathy and trust within short consultations, promote more prudent antidepressant prescribing, and encourage the use of non-pharmacological support through enhanced team working. MethodsThe Behaviour Change Wheel was used to co-develop a complex intervention (Destress-II) aimed at primary care staff. A group of GPs (n = 19) and members of low-income communities (n = 17) followed BCW stages to create the intervention. In 2022-3, Phase 1 piloted an in-person iteration with 53 practices; qualitative data from trainers and participants were analysed using descriptive thematic analysis. Refined into a practice-delivered web-resource, the intervention was tested in a further 30 practices in Phase 2 and re-evaluated using interviews.ResultsBoth phases identified three main themes: content, delivery, and acceptability within time-pressured services. Participants valued the content and the team-discussion aspect, finding the brief duration appropriate for existing training meetings. Many appreciated the intervention for reinforcing holistic approaches rather than introducing entirely new concepts.ConclusionThe study successfully co-developed an acceptable and feasible intervention to improve clinical responses to mental distress exacerbated by poverty and wider social circumstances. The co-development approach to complex interventions is transferable to other clinical areas.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12875-026-03405-3.Keywords: General Practitioners, Primary care, Mental health, Poverty, Prescribing, Antidepressants, Social circumstances, Co-development, Intervention, Quality improvement.

Background

Research suggests that many people in the UK experiencing mental distress also have low socio-economic status (SES), with a clear association established between social adversity—such as poverty or isolation—and poor mental health [1]. However, the clinical response to distress remains strongly pharmaceutical and consequently, the United Kingdom has seen a prolonged population-wide increase in antidepressant prescribing [2]. This trend persists despite the ‘treatment-prevalence paradox,’ where increases in the provision of antidepressant and psychological treatments have not resulted in a measurable reduction in the prevalence of depression [3]. The trend is particularly evident in cases of mild-to-moderate depression, where evidence suggests limited efficacy and a risk of adverse side-effects [4]. In addition to the clinical implications, the financial burden on the healthcare system is substantial with annual spending on antidepressant medication in England reaching approximately £230 million, with over 86 million items dispensed each year [2]. When applied to individuals in social adversity, such prescribing practices fail to ameliorate the underlying psychosocial causes and may inadvertently medicalise social suffering [5].

Despite concerns regarding long-term efficacy and the potential harms of extended use [6], long-term antidepressant use is common in the UK [7]. In low-SES communities, this is compounded by structural barriers to deprescribing; while medication may offer initial symptomatic relief, the persistence of social stress and long waiting lists for talking therapies [8] often leads to patients remaining on antidepressants for years [7]. Safely deprescribing, or more prudent prescribing on initial presentation, poses significant challenges. Qualitative research into GP and patient perspectives has shown that clinicians can feel trapped by systemic constraints, including short consultation times and long waiting lists for psychosocial therapies, consequently, prescribing can become a pragmatic response to a lack of available alternatives [8]. Our previous research reported this dissatisfaction among both patients and healthcare providers, finding that consultations often inadvertently reinforce social distress as a medical issue [5]. While participants sought more holistic, non-pharmacological approaches, they identified a distinct lack of support to facilitate this shift [5]. As such, there is a clear rationale for a structured intervention to support clinical reasoning and practice.

While the UK National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) guidelines recommend non-pharmacological interventions as first-line treatment for mild-to-moderate depression [9], there is a lack of guidance on how to implement these alternatives within the service pressures of primary care. Research has shown that GPs and multidisciplinary teams require specific, actionable support to shift consultations away from being prescribing-focused to placing more emphasis on non-pharmacological options [10]. To address this gap, theory-based frameworks such as the Behaviour Change Wheel (BCW) [11] and the Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF) [12] can be used to support the systematic and transparent design of complex interventions. While these frameworks have been successfully used to modify prescribing behaviour in other clinical areas [13, 14], they have not yet been applied to the specific challenge of promoting more prudent antidepressant prescribing. The aim of this study was to address this gap by co-developing a theory-informed intervention: DeStress-II, designed to support primary care professionals to reduce antidepressant prescribing for poverty-related mental distress by focusing consultations on non-pharmacological options.

Methods

Study design

The Behaviour Change Wheel (BCW) Guide [11] is a systematic, eight-step framework that provides a transparent method for theory-based intervention development by identifying the specific capabilities, opportunities, and motivations (COM-B) required to change behaviour. It incorporates the Theoretical Domains Framework which consists of 14 domains that support the identification of specific factors of the COM-B which influence the target behaviour. To optimise both the development and evaluation of behaviour change interventions it is widely recommended they are theory-driven to ensure the active components of the process are both identifiable and replicable [15].

The BCW is structured as a series of concentric circles (See Fig. 1). At the core is ‘COM-B’ which is based on the premise that people require the Capability (knowledge and skills), Opportunity (environmental and social resources) and Motivation (beliefs and intentions) to perform the target Behaviour [11]. The next circle is the Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF) which consists of 14 more granular factors derived from theories of behaviour change, each one also aligned with capability, opportunity or motivation [12]. The next circle provides nine intervention functions that might change behaviour (education, persuasion, incentivisation, coercion, training, enablement, modelling, environmental restructuring and restrictions). The outermost layer is a range of policy categories that can be further used to support delivery. Working in parallel with the BCW is the Behaviour Change Taxonomy which details 93 specific, replicable, behaviour change techniques (BCT) [16], seen as the ‘active ingredients’ of an intervention, they are the smallest observable and replicable components of an intervention. These are categorised into 16 groups, although they do not map exactly to the TDF domains as some BCTs can support more than one domain.

Recruitment of co-development group

The intervention was co-developed by a group of GP trainers, community partners and researchers who were ultimately all involved in delivering and evaluating the intervention. The community partners were individuals from low-income communities with lived experience of mental distress; recruited through the team’s existing links within low-income communities and via National Institute for Health and Care Research Applied Research Collaboration networks rather than being patients at the participating practices. GP participants were invited through Primary Care Networks (clusters of practices that hold some central functions) and regional primary care training hubs. Three regions of England, all linked to the aforementioned Applied Research Collaborations were selected to provide a diverse range of socio-economic contexts to ensure the generalisability of the intervention (South West, North West Coast and North London). An email advert was sent out across these networks and further information was provided for potential participants to inform their decision to join the co-development group. Group meetings were facilitated by members of the research team and conducted over Zoom in November 2021; detailed notes were taken by research team members.

Development of the intervention

The use of the BCW to guide intervention development consists of eight steps [11] contained within three stages (See Fig. 2). Stage 1 includes the first four steps: the definition of the problem, the selection and specification of the target behaviour and recommends the use of group discussions to identify what needs to change. Stage 2 involves Steps 5 and 6 which identify the ‘intervention function’ and ‘policy categories’ to support behaviour change. Stage 3 consists of the final two steps (7 and 8) which are to identify the specific behaviour change techniques from the BCT Taxonomy [16] of 93 techniques and specify the way they will be operationalised within the intervention. During the co-development process, consideration was given to the APEASE criteria [11]: affordability, practicality, effectiveness, acceptability, safety and equity.

In a three-hour online workshop, the group reviewed existing evidence regarding stakeholder dissatisfaction with antidepressants as a response to social distress. Stage 2 (Steps 5–6): identifying intervention options

Building on the Stage 1 findings, we mapped the six identified COM-B components (knowledge, skills, social / professional role and identity, beliefs about consequences, social comparison, and environmental context and resources) onto four intervention functions: education, persuasion, training, and modelling. These were further supported by one policy category: ‘guidelines’.

To ensure these options were feasible within the specific constraints of primary care, such as restricted consultation times, we applied the APEASE criteria (affordability, practicality, effectiveness, acceptability, safety and equity). This process led to the exclusion of functions like incentivisation, coercion, and restriction, which were deemed neither practical nor acceptable in a general practice setting. Following the selection of BCTs, Step 8 involved determining the most effective mode of delivery. We evaluated potential content, providers and durations against the APEASE criteria to ensure the intervention was feasible within time-pressured primary care settings. This resulted in DeStress-II training intervention Version 1, a face-to-face session facilitated by a GP and a community partner with a researcher present.

Implementation of the intervention

Phase 1

The study was conducted in two phases to allow for iterative development. In Phase 1 (2022-3), the first iteration of the intervention was delivered to primary care teams recruited through Clinical Research Networks. Delivery was by a three-person team consisting of a GP trainer, a community partner, and a research team member. While the preference was for face-to-face sessions, service pressures and Covid-19 restrictions necessitated that most sessions were delivered online.

DeStress-II training intervention Version 1 was guided by a PowerPoint presentation taking one hour so it could be delivered within a routine practice meeting. It featured visual data, including maps of antidepressant prescribing rates overlaid on socio-economic deprivation data and UK prescribing trends. It also incorporated theoretical concepts focused on trust and engagement, alongside the RCGP ‘Five minutes to change your practice’ video (See Table 4). Informed by Cochrane strategies for changing professional practice, the sessions combined didactic information with discursive, small-group learning led by fellow GP ‘opinion leaders.’

To capture feedback to inform refinement, sessions were recorded using MS Teams or encrypted voice recorders in addition to fieldnotes from the delivery team. Two weeks post-intervention, participants were invited via email to participate in an individual interview. Staff who opted in provided informed consent via an electronic form and were interviewed, in private, by a member of the research team (IL, AM, OH, JH) over MS Teams using the interview schedule provided in Supplementary File 1. The interview questions were piloted during the first few interviews and minor amendments made. Automated transcripts were checked for accuracy by the staff member who carried out the interview and corrected and anonymised and stored in a secure digital environment. Due to the significant service pressures and time constraints faced by participating primary care staff, transcripts were not returned to participants for correction or asked to feedback on the findings.

These data were analysed by authors FT, KB, IL, AM and KW using descriptive thematic analysis, supported by NVivo to inform the transition to Version 2. This analysis followed the systematic approach to coding and theme development outlined by Boyatzis [17]. This ensured descriptive clarity and transparency through the creation of a structured coding ‘tree’ shared between authors. Unlike more reflexive approaches, this study focused on the consistent application of codes across the data to provide a reliable summary of participants' views. Data saturation was not used as a criterion for ending data collection as sample size was determined by the logistical requirements of the two-phase iterative development process and the aim of capturing a diverse range of perspectives across the different regions. The consistency of themes identified across both phases suggests that the findings provided a comprehensive summary of participant views regarding the intervention's content and feasibility.

Phase 2

In Phase 2, DeStress Version 2 was deployed in 30 different practices as an 11-section online multimedia resource designed for independent, practice-facilitated delivery (see Table 6). This iteration shifted to a model where a practice staff member led the session using a guidance manual, allowing for wider scale-up while maintaining core fidelity. Although the digital format provided flexibility for teams to tailor the session to specific interests and allowed participants to log back in post-delivery, it preserved the potential for one-hour duration and promotion of group discussion. Changes to the content included simplifying the illustrations of the bio-psycho-social model of mental health, the addition of a video of a simulated GP consultation, videos of community partner testimony and specific scripts to promote empathy; and removal of the RCGP video (See Table 5).

Reflexivity statement

The research team brought a multidisciplinary perspective to this study. As well as including individuals with clinical expertise, team members came from backgrounds in social sciences, public health and health services research. As such our collective orientation is towards a bio-psycho-social model of health, taking a holistic approach towards understanding mental distress in low-income communities which recognises the many systemic issues such as poverty and social isolation that contribute to poor mental wellbeing.

While we fully acknowledge the clinical utility of antidepressants in certain contexts, our prior research findings have described high levels of both patient and GP dissatisfaction with inappropriate over-medicalisation and this has influenced our focus on supporting the use of non-pharmacological alternatives. In acknowledgement of this stance, our inclusion of practicing GPs and GP trainers in the co-development and delivery phases ensured the intervention remained grounded in the realities of primary care. The inclusion of community partners with lived experience added further balance to the team.

All authors except OH are female, all have undergone research methods training and have substantive experience in the collection and analysis of qualitative data and all but one (IL) are post-doctoral academic researchers.

Results

Feedback on the intervention DeStress-II version 1

The intervention was delivered to 53 practices, reaching a total of 508 primary health care professionals (including 387 GPs) between November 2022 and February 2023. Twenty-two participants from Phase 1 were interviewed with interviews lasting between ten and 34 min. Exact numbers of individual refusals were not recorded as recruitment was conducted via email invitation to each practice. A detailed breakdown of interviewee demographics from Phase 1 and Phase 2, including age, sex, and ethnicity, is provided in Table 6.

During descriptive thematic analysis the data was categorised into three main themes relating to: Content, facilitation and acceptability.

Theme 1: Content

The content and message of the intervention was almost entirely favourably viewed. Some recipients experienced it as new information, particularly the information about the limited effectiveness of antidepressants for mild and moderate depression:

“I think stats, simple and memorable stats are really helpful (…) I find them really helpful with patients, telling a patient that for every seven prescriptions that I write for people in your situation, six people will not get any benefit at all (…) is incredibly powerful.” GP, male, White British, South West.

Others felt it reinforced what they were already trying to achieve within their consultations:

“it’s actually things that we’re doing already (…) it was good to have that forum to talk about (…) what we feel is, you know, the mainstay of our work as well as GPs (…) It wasn’t anything new.” GP, female, British Asian, London.

“I thought it was interesting, it was nicely delivered (…) good to be reminded of all those things.” GP, male, White British, North West.

With regard to increasing knowledge, some trainers felt that some of the initial information slides were too complex. As a result, some were simplified, including the bio-psycho-social influences on mental health and the content of some of the slides was reduced.

Several participants spoke about the benefits of watching another GP carry out a consultation and felt that would be a good addition to the intervention in terms of improving both knowledge and skills.

“we see people having a consultation and we try to sort of understand what’s going on and see where we can improve. I think that would have been really good.” Physician Associate, female, British Asian, London.

A simulated consultation with a real GP and person who had experienced poverty-related mental distress was subsequently included in the intervention Version 2.

Some trainers did not want to use the RCGP five-minute video as they felt it recapped what they were already saying and took up too much time within the duration:

“I thought the video was too long (…) we don’t have very much time (…) So I trimmed the video (…) we’ve got experience in the room.” Intervention delivery team member, GP, female, other white background, North West.

This video was removed from intervention Version 2.

The intervention presented the importance of improving trust and empathy with patients and several participants felt suggested scripts of phrases to use with patients would be helpful:

“suggestions of how to change wording (…) making it really practical (…) how do we actually do this within our consultation. Clinicians would find it useful to have some sort of 'catch phrases' they could use (…) in (…) supporting and enabling patients.” GP, female, White British, North West.

As a result, brief scripts were developed within the research team and refined by the GP trainers and community partners. For example, “I’m glad you came to see me today”, and, in response to hearing about people’s circumstances “I can understand why you feel upset about that”; “You seem to be going through a lot – I would find that very hard too”.

Theme 2: Facilitation

The fact it was a GP delivering the intervention along with a community partner was particularly valued by participants:

“Then the way the program was delivered was pretty good and because the GP that came too explained everything, she obviously was very (…) knowledgeable about the topic and had (…) her own experiences.” GP, female, White British, North West.

The presence of a community partner on the intervention team was also favourably commented on by many participants.

“I also liked that there was actually a patient that (…) had the time to speak about his experience.” Physician Associate, female, British Asian, North Thames.

Initially only GPs were invited to take part, but as sessions were delivered and feedback sought, participants said they thought it would be beneficial to invite all practice staff to participate, particularly as some practices had mental health practitioners and social prescribers and these were frequently referred to in the intervention sessions with GPs. The subsequent inclusion of different practitioners was not thought to require amendment to the content of the intervention, but it led to wider discussion about how the practice was working as a team to support patients experiencing mental distress. The opportunity to discuss the issue as a practice team was highly valued and some feedback reported that there needed to be more time for this within the session:

“It’s very hard to get people to interact and in and engage, and the discussion was actually what was really useful. So having someone come to a practice team and discuss it as a team, that was really useful and that’s the way I prefer to do it.” GP, Male, White British, North West,

Trainers subsequently sought to achieve a better balance between delivering information and providing opportunity for discussion. This discussion allowed for social comparison as people shared their experiences. Due to the scheduling of practice meetings, sometimes a community partner was not available to deliver the intervention with the GP trainer so a video was created that could be used instead.

Theme 3: Acceptability

In terms of the APEASE criteria, the intervention was seen as affordable, practical and acceptable due to its short duration, which enabled it to be incorporated into a routine practice meeting:

“Yeah (…) I think it was the right amount of time.” GP, female, White British, North West.

Feedback from participants was sought regarding how the intervention could be implemented at scale. Consideration was given as to whether it could be delivered by trainers from existing primary care training teams, but it was felt that the intervention could be made available directly to practices. The idea of an individual e-learning package was considered but the discussion between practice colleagues was highly valued and would be lost within this mode of delivery. One trainer suggested developing a resource that could be delivered within the practice by a staff member, during a routine team meeting, therefore preserving the space for discussion:

“I think the idea of a (…) standalone deliverable package is brilliant, in most practices, they have educational meetings and to have something that’s just ready to go that they can sit and watch (…) I think would fit in very well.” Intervention delivery team member, GP, male, White British, South West.

A practice-facilitated intervention therefore became the aim of the Version 2.

Feedback on the intervention DeStress-II version 2

DeStress-II Version 2 was tested in 30 GP practices. We do not have the exact number of people attending each session although it was in excess of 150. Thirty attendees were interviewed between June and September 2023, with interviews lasting between 15 and 37 min. Exact numbers of individual refusals were not recorded as recruitment was conducted via email invitation to each practice. Interviewee demographics are detailed in Table 4. Overall, the intervention received positive feedback, with data again following the same process of descriptive thematic analysis into three primary themes: content, facilitation, and acceptability.

Theme 1: Content

Participants gave favourable feedback about the information presented:

“It was clear, it was concise, it wasn’t long winded. The videos were bite sized. They were easy to understand (…) I wasn’t sitting there thinking ‘oh God when this is going to be over!’ Far from it, I was really fascinated by it.” Health care assistant, female, White British, London.

Some content was identified as being new information, e.g., information about antidepressant prescribing rates and effectiveness:

“…there were some facts and figures in the session that (…) I wasn’t really aware of (…) things like 1/6 of the population on antidepressants, like, you know, 8.3 million. You know (…) we work in primary care, we work in community pharmacy (…) we see these prescriptions day in day out. But the actual numbers (…) was kind of a shock (…) to be honest.” Pharmacist, male, British Asian, London.

As before, a small number of others felt the information presented was not new to them but they felt it reinforced what they tried to apply in their consultation;

“Yeah, hopefully just feeling a bit more confident in my consulting in terms of spending more time talking about alternatives to medication. But I like to think I was doing a bit of that anyway, but I think it just helps to sort of move along that a bit further really.” GP, female, white British, South West.

The scripts were the most commented upon aspect of the intervention and the feedback was all positive:

“I also liked the bit where it was talking about (…) ways that you can talk to people. For example, when it says: ‘with all the circumstances that you’re going through, I think that I would feel the same way’ (…) they’re very, very helpful. And I’ve actually started using some of those whilst I talk to patients, I took a little screen shot of it. So I found that really helpful.” Care coordinator, female, White British, South West,

The video clips of different GPs describing their different experiences were valued by participants.

“Very good. Like because the GPs were talking (…) from their own experience and (…) it wasn’t only anecdotal, they were able to put in a context with (…) evidence (…) they were able to match it with (…) the data.” GP, male, British Asian, London.

Participants also appreciated having a menu of different Community Partner experiences they could select to watch within the intervention. This led to more choice than in the Version 1:

“it was nice because (…) people doing the session with me could say: ‘ohh, I’d like to hear from [Community Partner]’ (…) and we could sort of choose people who looked a bit like our patients.” GP, female, White British, London.

Theme 2: Facilitation

Several facilitators within the practices reported having enjoyed taking on the role:

“I personally I would say enjoyed facilitating it. I felt it was good to (…) hear what other people have to say.” GP, female, British Asian, London.

“I didn’t do too much preparation (…) the instructions were really clear and (…) with little preparation, it was absolutely fine facilitating (…) the instructions were absolutely perfect (…) the approximate timings that were given for each of the sections were helpful.” Practice Manager, female, White British, North West.

The opportunity for discussion within the session continued to be highly valued by the majority of the participants. There were positive comments about sharing experiences and approaches as well as a number of comments about the benefits of having ringfenced time to reflect on one’s own practice:

“And then we had half an hour to (…) discuss things amongst this (…) to get to hear all kind of stories and (…) other people’s experiences (…) a 35 year old and a 60 year old in the same room (…) definitely they will have things (…) to teach each other.” Pharmacist, male, British Asian, London.

This discussion sometimes led to the session over-running although it is the responsibility of the facilitator to ensure this does not happen.

Theme 3: Acceptability

The feedback about acceptability was dominated by comments about the duration of the intervention:

“it’s all very time limited and (…) was so well designed. If it became too long, you’d lose interest.” GP, female, British Asian, London.

“I like the fact that it was (…) quite compact.” Practice Manager, male, White British, North West.

Intervention Version 2 was not considered to require any further refinement after the feedback. It has subsequently been validated by the RCGP and is freely available to access through the project website https://destressproject.org.uk/.

Discussion

The aim of this study was to co-develop an intervention aimed at GPs and other primary care staff to improve understanding of the impact of poverty and other social circumstances on mental health, build greater empathy within a short consultation, promote more prudent prescribing of antidepressants and encourage the use of non-pharmacological support. To date, this paper is the first to report use of the Behaviour Change Wheel (BCW) [11] to develop an intervention for primary care staff focused on antidepressant prescribing.

The use of the BCW incorporating the Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF) [12] supported the application of a rigorous theory-driven approach to structure the co-development of an intervention to improve primary care consultations with people experiencing distress as a result of poverty. In this study, the practical application of the BCW led to greater transparency and reproducibility. Our approach ensured that we clearly identified the target behaviour (inappropriate prescribing) and the domains likely to influence this (e.g., knowledge and skills) and what behaviour change techniques would be most likely to effectively target these domains and why (e.g., ‘Instruction on how to perform a behaviour’ and ‘Re-attribution’). This process also aligned with Medical Research Council guidance on developing complex interventions, i.e., all components of interventions are specified [18].

In addition, the use of the APEASE criteria meant that acceptability and feasibility were considered throughout, further ensuring our intervention was ready for practices to implement with only minimal adaptations. Co-developing an intervention as a standalone resource which can be delivered by a member of staff, means that the intervention can be easily used in primary care settings across the UK and accreditation by the Royal College of General Practitioners further adds to the intervention’s credibility. The simple editable web-based design means that it can be easily updated to prevent it becoming outdated.

The feedback from 70 participants leads us to believe that the DeStress-II intervention has great potential to change primary care staff behaviour. We found that primary care professionals liked the content, and that even when it was not new information it reinforced how people were already trying to shape their consultations. The scripts to prompt empathy were suggested by the Phase 1 participants and were highly valued in Phase 2. The overall design of the intervention was seen as attractive, as well as the way it was structured to allow practices to focus on areas of greater salience to them. The opportunity to reflect on their own practice as a team was valued, especially to consider non-pharmacological support, and the brief duration was crucial to ensure it could be accommodated within pressured services. Our short-term findings have evidenced this impact [19] and ongoing research is exploring the longer term effects of participation in the intervention.

The BCW approach has been used to develop other interventions in primary care settings, e.g., management of lower back pain [20] and reducing antibiotic use [21]. The TDF domains targeted in these interventions had a great deal of overlap with the current study leading to a consensus of what is likely to be most relevant to change behaviours in primary care setting, e.g., knowledge and skills. Jenkins et al. [20] also produced information for patients which was not a component of this intervention, but something that we also believe to be an important focus of future research.

The primary strength of the DeStress-II intervention is that it is was co-developed by GPs and community partners with experiences of the issue of poverty-related mental distress. This meant that we had a range of views and experiences of both the target of the intervention (primary care prescribers) and people with lived experience at the heart of the intervention. Another strength was the use of two rounds of testing, allowing for an iterative co-development process where the intervention was piloted as an expert-led session and based on participant feedback, subsequently refined into a practice-facilitated resource. A further strength is the adaptability of the intervention through the provision of choice of tailoring the content to a particular practice context to ensure aspects that are of most relevance or interest can be used.

Study limitations

One potential limitation was that our co-development group were highly motivated to improve such consultations and as such might not have identified all the barriers other GPs might face, although the scope for discussion within the delivery of the intervention did permit practice staff to raise their own issues. Similarly, as practices volunteered to take part in the evaluation of the intervention, they may have self-selected due to already having an interest in the subject and therefore have given more favourable responses. Testing the intervention in a trial setting would address this potential bias. A key limitation faced by this and all interventions that demand time in primary care, is service pressures leading to them not being feasible. However, being able to complete the intervention in one hour goes a long way to mitigate this.

There are two players in the consultation and this intervention only targets the professional. Information for patients about the effectiveness of antidepressants when distress is caused by social circumstances might further strengthen the intervention.

There is almost no research evidence about public perceptions of antidepressant use in the UK. Exploratory work is required in this area before patient behaviours can be effectively targeted. Further research is needed to test whether the DeStress-II intervention reduces the inappropriate prescribing of antidepressants and its impact on referrals to alternative supports.

This is the first intervention to be co-developed to support primary care consultations for people experiencing poverty-related mental distress. This process has led to the development of a brief complex intervention that has been found to be both feasible and highly acceptable for use in time-pressured primary care settings. It is believed that the primary reason for this success was the co-development of the intervention by both GPs and community partners with experience of the issues. The transparent method used can be applied to co-develop other similar interventions.

Conclusion

Antidepressant prescribing rates continue to rise in the UK, despite evidence that many people may not benefit from them, particularly if their mental distress is located in their social circumstances. This results in significant cost to both the National Health Service and to individuals who may not benefit from antidepressants, experience unpleasant side-effects and difficulties stopping their use. Using the Behaviour Change Wheel as a framework, the study successfully co-developed the DeStress-II intervention aimed at primary care staff to improve understanding of the impact of poverty on mental health, build greater empathy and trust within short consultations, promote prudent prescribing of antidepressants and encourage the use of non-pharmacological support. The intervention was particularly valued by participants for the practical scripts provided for use in consultations and the opportunity for multidisciplinary practice teams to collectively reflect on their prescribing habits. The intervention has been found to be acceptable and feasible for delivery in time-pressured primary care settings and the methods used are transferable to other health care settings. Further research is needed to test whether it reduces the prescribing of antidepressants and referrals to alternative services and whether patient information would be a beneficial addition to the intervention.
