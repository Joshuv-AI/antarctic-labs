From facilitating conditions to intention to use internet hospitals among doctors: multiple mediators of perceived risk and performance expectancy
Authors: Taiqing Luo, Qi Tang, Jun Lu, Anni Wang, Gang Chen, Liang Du, Yihan Chen, Zheming Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInternet hospital is an online medical platform where doctors can provide patients with convenient medical services. Facilitating conditions, perceived risk, and performance expectancy have been identified as factors associated with the intention to use new technologies. However, limited studies explore the effects of these three factors on doctors’ intention to use internet hospitals. This study aims to investigate the relationship between facilitating conditions, perceived risk, performance expectancy, and behavioral intention of doctors in public hospitals to use internet hospitals. Furthermore, it seeks to comprehend the multiple mediation effects of perceived risk and performance expectancy on the relationship between facilitating conditions and behavioral intention.MethodsA multicenter cross-sectional web-based survey was conducted from November to December 2022, using a sample of 233 doctors from 8 public hospitals in Shanghai. Structural equation modeling (SEM) and multi-group analysis were applied to test the proposed model and examine differences across professional title subgroups.ResultsThe findings revealed significant correlations between facilitating conditions, perceived risk, performance expectancy, and behavioral intention (β = 0.337, p < 0.001; β=-0.213, p < 0.001; β = 0.460, p < 0.001, respectively). Facilitating conditions was indirectly associated with intention to use internet hospitals through perceived risk (β = 0.044, p = 0.02) and performance expectancy (β = 0.210, p = 0.001), and these two variables formulated a sequential mediating pathway (β = 0.021, p = 0.02). The mediation effect accounted for 44.8% of the total effect of facilitating conditions. Among doctors with lower titles, the path coefficient from facilitating conditions to behavioral intention was significantly higher compared to doctors with higher titles (CR=-2.923, p = 0.003); the path coefficient from perceived risk to behavioral intention was significantly lower than doctors with higher titles (CR=-3.258, p = 0.001).ConclusionsThe findings suggest policymakers augment the doctors’ intention to utilize internet hospitals by optimizing the circumstances surrounding internet hospitals, establishing comprehensive support systems, enhancing the performance benefits, and alleviating the perception of risk.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14884-6.

Introduction

Background

With socioeconomic development and rapid population aging, China faces growing healthcare demands, particularly for chronic disease management [1, 2]. However, the uneven distribution of high-quality medical resources limits accessibility, especially across regions [3]. Internet healthcare—represented by internet hospitals—has emerged as a strategy to improve access by enabling patients to obtain medical services without geographical constraints [4–6]. Internet hospitals are online platforms affiliated with physical hospitals that integrate online and offline services [7], allowing doctors to deliver a range of services to patients, encompassing health consultations, appointment scheduling, follow-up consultations, and medication delivery [8]. The COVID-19 pandemic further accelerated their development by reducing cross-infection risks in traditional hospital settings. As of October 2023, more than 2,700 internet hospitals had been established in China [9].

Internet hospitals are similar to telemedicine models that have achieved widespread adoption in developed countries [8]. However, unlike conventional telemedicine platforms, internet hospitals function as comprehensive online medical institutions offering more integrated services and enabling closed-loop care for patients [10]. These distinctive features warrant further investigation. Notably, most internet hospitals in China are established within public hospitals, which form the backbone of the national healthcare system [11]; therefore, internet hospitals in public hospitals are the focus of attention.

Many studies have explored patients’ intentions and influencing factors regarding internet-based medical technologies [4, 12, 13]. However, whether doctors can successfully adapt their work habits to internet hospitals remains unclear. Preliminary evidence suggests that insufficient facilitating conditions, medical risks, and inadequate performance-based income may hinder doctors’ adoption [14, 15]. As primary participants, doctors’ willingness is essential for the effective promotion of these platforms [15]. While research has examined doctors’ intentions toward specific technologies like mHealth, EHR, and EMR [16–22], studies focusing specifically on the intentions and influencing factors of public hospital doctors to use internet hospitals remain scarce [14, 23].

Theoretical background

The unified theory of acceptance and use of technology (UTAUT) is a robust framework for examining technology adoption [16–19, 21]. While the original model includes four determinants [24], this study focused on facilitating conditions and performance expectancy as the core technology-related determinants, based on the institutional characteristics of China’s public hospital system. Given the significant inter-hospital variability in technical infrastructure, facilitating conditions is a critical prerequisite for internet hospital usage. Similarly, performance expectancy is a vital driver due to doctors’ high workloads and performance-based incentives, reflecting the technology’s potential to enhance efficiency and performance. In contrast, prior research has demonstrated that effort expectancy plays a weaker role in the acceptance of health-related technologies [25, 26]. This shift is largely attributed to the high standardization of modern information systems and the continuous improvement of users’ digital literacy [27]. Particularly for highly educated professionals such as doctors, the technical barriers to entry are relatively low, making effort expectancy a less differentiating factor in their adoption decisions. Furthermore, social influence was excluded because the promotion of internet hospitals in China is typically an institution-driven mandate; therefore, doctors’ usage behaviors are shaped more heavily by organizational infrastructure and technical readiness. Existing literature also corroborates that social influence exerts comparatively weaker effects in the process of healthcare technology adoption [28, 29].

Perceived risk has been identified as an important factor influencing intention to adopt new technologies [30–32]. Medical services are inherently characterized by high uncertainty and high stakes, involving diagnostic accuracy, data security, legal liability, and patient safety. In China, doctor–patient tensions, diagnostic responsibility in online consultations, and the protection of highly sensitive health data remain particularly salient concerns [33]. In the context of internet hospitals, doctors’ adoption decisions are therefore not only driven by perceived benefits but are also strongly constrained by perceived risks [34]. Accordingly, perceived risk was incorporated into the theoretical model of the present study.

However, most existing UTAUT-based studies primarily focus on the independent effects of multiple antecedents on behavioral intention, whereas the interrelationships among these antecedents and the underlying psychological processes are less frequently modeled explicitly. From the process-oriented perspective, this study therefore explored the associations among facilitating conditions, perceived risk, performance expectancy, and behavioral intention to construct a sequential psychological mechanism of doctors’ adoption of internet hospitals. Moreover, the structural equation modeling (SEM) is frequently employed to offer a comprehensive evaluation of complex models, as it can provide separate estimates of relationships among latent constructs [35]. Therefore, SEM was employed to examine the effects and interactions of facilitating conditions, performance expectancy, and perceived risk on doctors’ intention to use internet hospitals.

Facilitating conditions refers to the user’s knowledge about the new technology and the level of support provided by the relevant organization; performance expectancy refers to the the user’s level of expectation towards the technology [24]. It has been shown that both factors have a direct effect on behavioral intention [36, 37]. According to the stimulus-organism-response theory, environmental stimuli influence individuals’ internal cognitive and affective states, which subsequently shape their behavioral responses [38]. If doctors possess more favorable conditions or skills for using internet hospitals, such as more proficient operation and more secure system, they are likely to be more efficient in utilizing the technology, leading to higher performance outcomes. Consequently, we hypothesize that facilitating conditions is positively associated with performance expectancy, and that performance expectancy mediates the relationship between facilitating conditions and intention to use internet hospitals. This link is supported by prior research in digital health and smart systems [39, 40], which identified facilitating conditions as a key antecedent of performance expectancy.

Perceived risk refers to the uncertainty surrounding the possible negative consequences of using a product or service [41], which directly influences doctors’ intention to use the medical technology [42, 43]. Within an organizational support framework, facilitating conditions—such as secure systems, standardized protocols, and institutional safeguards—serves to reduce uncertainty and enhance controllability [44]. Furthermore, comprehensive training and operational guidelines can bolster doctors’ self-efficacy, thereby mitigating concerns regarding clinical safety and medical disputes [45]. Consequently, we hypothesize that facilitating conditions is negatively associated with perceived risk, and that perceived risk mediates the relationship between facilitating conditions and intention to use internet hospitals. This aligns with prior findings, such as Hsieh’s [46] emphasis on data security as a primary factor in reducing physicians’ perceived risk.

Furthermore, drawing on expectancy–value theory [47], individuals’ motivation to engage in a task is influenced by their expectancy beliefs regarding success. High levels of perceived uncertainty and potential loss (perceived risk) could systematically diminish an individual’s subjective probability of achieving positive task outcomes (performance expectancy) [48]. In this context of internet hospitals, concerns regarding diagnostic accuracy, data leakage, and legal liability may lead doctors to discount the perceived utility and efficiency gains of the platform. While an alternative sequence—where high performance expectancy might mitigate risk perception—is logically plausible in low-stakes settings, we argue that in the high-stakes clinical environment of public hospitals, risk evaluation logically precedes and constrains the appraisal of performance benefits. This negative correlation is supported by prior research on physicians’ adoption of new technologies [49, 50]. Consequently, we hypothesize that perceived risk is negatively associated with performance expectancy; performance expectancy mediates the relationship between perceived risk and intention to use internet hospitals; and perceived risk and performance expectancy act as sequential mediators between facilitating conditions and behavioral intention.

In addition, previous studies have shown that the willingness of doctors of different professional titles to adopt internet medical technology varies. In the study investigating factors influencing medical practitioners’ resistance to and adoption of internet hospitals, Deng et al. [14] found that the professional title significantly moderated the influencing mechanisms. Cui et al. [51] also identified the professional title as a key factor influencing the attitudes and perspectives of pediatricians toward internet hospitals. Doctors with different professional titles may have varying job responsibilities, work experience, and frequency of using internet hospitals. These differences can result in variations in their perceptions and willingness to use internet hospitals.

Objectives

In this study, we explored the associations among facilitating conditions, perceived risk, performance expectancy, and behavioral intention of doctors in public hospitals to use internet hospitals. Additionally, we analyzed the multiple mediating roles of perceived risk and performance expectancy between facilitating conditions and behavioral intention, as well as the differences in mechanisms among different professional title groups using multi-group analysis. We hoped to provide both theoretical insights into the underlying adoption mechanism and practical implications for promoting doctors’ engagement with internet hospitals.

Methods

Study design and sample

The multicenter cross-sectional study was conducted from November to December 2022. The list of hospitals carrying out internet hospital was acquired from the official website of the Shanghai Municipal Health Commission. A total of 84 hospitals in Shanghai had opened internet hospitals, including 76 public hospitals and 8 private hospitals. Given that public hospitals represented the majority of China’s healthcare delivery system, we focused mainly on public hospitals, which consisted of 28 secondary hospitals and 48 tertiary hospitals, with no primary included. Using the stratified sampling method, we selected three secondary hospitals and five tertiary hospitals, for a total of eight hospitals. A designated manager from each hospital was tasked with distributing questionnaires. Due to the lack of a full sampling list and practical constraints, convenience sampling was used to distribute questionnaires to eligible participants.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: doctors who were full-time employees of the public hospital, currently carried out diagnosis and treatment work, worked for at least 1 year, learned about the internet hospital, and agreed to participate in the survey. Exclusion criteria were as follows: non-doctor staff such as nursing, medical technology, and management, as well as non-hospital employed individuals such as training students and interns. The questionnaires were distributed online and filled out entirely by the respondents themselves. They were well-informed and participated voluntarily, with the option to terminate the questionnaire at any time. The study received ethical approval from the Medical Research Ethics Committee of the School of Public Health, Fudan University, China (Grant Number: IRB#2019-10-0783).

There are various rules for sample size calculation in SEM. Generally, a typical median sample size of 200 is recommended to for SEM analysis, and Kline also suggested 5 to 10 cases per free parameter [52]. There are 42 free parameters to be estimated in the latterly tested model, so the minimum sample size was calculated at 210. Combining these together, the minimum sample size should be 210. A total of 250 questionnaires were collected, of which 233 were valid, with an effective rate of 93.20%. As participants were recruited using convenience sampling from public hospitals in Shanghai, the representativeness of the sample may be limited. The findings of this study should therefore be interpreted with caution when generalizing to doctors in other hospitals or regions of China.

Data collection

The survey questionnaire consisted of three parts (Additional File 1). The first part demonstrated the purpose of the study and the concept of internet hospital. The second part included a series of questions aimed at capturing socio-demographic characteristics, involving gender, age, educational level, professional title, work tenure, monthly income, and hospital hierarchy [14]. The last part recorded the subjects’ perception of each construct in the model. The part was designed based on a literature review of technology usage intention and behavior. Specifically, we identified the constructs including facilitating conditions, performance expectancy, and behavioral intention from the UTAUT model [24, 36, 53], as well as the construct of perceived risk from relevant literature [22, 54]. See Table 1 for details. This part contained 17 items and was divided into four sections. Section 1 included four items pertaining to the facilitating conditions; Sect. 2 included six items pertaining to the perceived risk; Sect. 3 included four items pertaining to the performance expectancy; Sect. 4 included three items pertaining to the behavioral intention. A 5-point Likert scale ranging from (1) “strongly disagree” to (5) “strongly agree” was used for measuring the desired items. Participants were asked to indicate their level of agreement with each item.

The questionnaire development followed a multi-stage, iterative process. First, items from previous studies were compared and filtered for compatibility, then translated into Chinese and adapted to the research context. For example, specific technologies mentioned in the original items were replaced with “internet hospital” (details in Additional File 2). Second, two bilingual experts in healthcare management with extensive knowledge of internet hospitals and experience translating scales to Chinese independently reviewed the items for relevance, clarity, and contextual appropriateness, followed by in-depth group discussions to reach confirmation. Items were optimized, including merging analogous perceived risk items for clarity, over three iterative rounds until consensus was reached. Finally, before the formal survey, the questionnaire was pretested in two rounds through cognitive interviews conducted by the same researchers and two doctors to identify potential issues, such as clarity, comprehension, and contextual fit. Based on this process and to minimize potential common method bias, several procedural remedies were implemented: the item requesting respondents’ names was removed to ensure the anonymity and confidentiality, and the survey instruction explicitly emphasized that there were no right or wrong answers to reduce evaluation apprehension.

In this study, the questionnaire was edited on the Questionnaire Star, a widely utilized online questionnaire platform in China. The questionnaires were distributed via WeChat, a social networking platform developed by Tencent, extensively integrated into Chinese daily life and professional activities. We forwarded the questionnaire link to the designated contact person of each hospital, who then disseminated it directly to eligible individuals or through WeChat groups within the hospitals. No gifts or incentives were provided to the respondents to maintain impartiality and objectivity. Approximately 3–5 min were needed to complete the questionnaire. To prevent duplicate submissions, each IP address was restricted to only one response. A time restriction on completing the questionnaire was set. Responses completed in less than 90 s were excluded, as it was deemed nearly impossible for a respondent to read and complete the questionnaire within such a short timeframe.

Data analysis

Data were imported into SPSS 22.0 for cleaning and analysis. The extent of relationships among variables were evaluated by SEM using SPSS Amos 26.0. Internal reliability was examined using Cronbach α and composite reliability, employing a criterion of α and composite reliability values > 0.70. Convergent validity was assessed through the average variance extracted (AVE) and item loadings, with an AVE threshold of at least 0.50 and an item loading threshold of at least 0.70. Discriminant validity was evaluated using the square root of the AVE and cross-loading matrix. The square root of the AVE for each construct should be greater than its correlation with other constructs for the satisfactory discriminant validity [55]. All hypotheses from the study were also tested.

The chi-square (χ2), degrees of freedom (df), χ2/df, incremental fit index (IFI), comparative fit index (CFI), gamma goodness-of-fit index (GFI), the normed fit index (NFI), and root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) were employed as indicators of model fit, as previously recommended [52]. χ2/df < 5 and values of IFI, CFI, GFI, and NFI greater than 0.90 are considered to reflect a good model fit. RMSEA value less than 0.05 suggests good fit, while value up to 0.08 indicates reasonable errors of approximation. Common method bias (CMB) was assessed using Harman’s single-factor test. If an exploratory factor analysis of all measurement items shows that the first unrotated factor accounts for less than 50% of the total variance, CMB is generally not considered a serious concern [56].

To test the total, direct, and indirect effects of each variable, the bias-corrected bootstrapping approach was used. The 95% confidence intervals and p value stemming from the bootstrapping approach are considered more reliable [52]. The 95% confidence intervals and p value in this study were estimated based on 1,000 re-samples. Multi-group analysis was conducted to examine differences and similarities across different professional title subgroups [57]. Considering the samples sizes of each subgroup, we merged adjacent groups by combing the primary group and middle group into one group labeled “doctors with lower titles” and combing the deputy senior group and senior group into one group labeled “doctors with higher titles” [58]. The change in RMSEA (ΔRMSEA) and the change in CFI (ΔCFI) were used to assess the measurement invariance for the comparison of the less restricted model with the more constrained model, and ΔCFI < 0.010 and ΔRMSEA < 0.015 imply that measurement invariance has been successfully established [59]. The critical ratios (CR) of differences between parameters were calculated to evaluate group differences in regression coefficients for each path [60]. The significance level for all analyses was defined as p < 0.05.

Mediation model analysis

The effects of each variable on intention are summarized in Table 5. Seen in the satiated structural equation model, performance expectancy (β = 0.460), perceived risk (β=-0.313) and facilitating conditions (β = 0.611) were identified as predictors of behavioral intention. Specifically, perceived risk had a significant direct (β=-0.213) and an indirect effect (β=-0.100). Facilitating conditions had a significant direct (β = 0.337) and an indirect effect (β = 0.274), and the indirect pathway via performance expectancy accounted for the majority of indirect effect (β = 0.210). Perceived risk and performance expectancy acted as sequential mediators between facilitating conditions and behavioral intention (β = 0.021). The mediation effect took up 44.8% of the total effect of facilitating conditions.

Multi-group analysis

Table 6 presents the results of model invariance test. All ΔCFI values were less than 0.010 and all ΔRMSEA values were less than 0.015, implying that the measurement invariance was established across the models. Therefore, the multi-group restriction models have the property of constancy and can be estimated across group categories. As reported in Table 7, for doctors with lower titles, the pathway from facilitating conditions to perceived risk was not statistically significant; for doctors with higher titles, the pathway from facilitating conditions to behavioral intention was not statistically significant. Table 7 also presents the critical ratios of differences between path coefficients for multi-group comparison. For doctors with lower titles, the path coefficient from facilitating conditions to behavioral intention was significantly higher than doctors with higher titles (CR=-2.923, p = 0.003); the path coefficient from perceived risk to behavioral intention was significantly lower than doctors with higher titles (CR=-3.258, p = 0.001).

Discussion

Principal findings

This study investigated the associations of facilitating conditions, perceived risk, and performance expectancy with intention of doctors, as medical service providers, in using internet hospitals in Shanghai. The study further explored the mediating effects of perceived risk and performance expectancy in the association between facilitating conditions and behavioral intention, as well as potential differences across professional title groups. To the best of our knowledge, there are relatively few studies in this specific area. This work attempted to provide insights on how to encourage doctors to actively participate in internet hospitals.

Facilitating conditions exhibited a strongly positive association with behavioral intention with the largest total effect (β = 0.611). The indirect effect (β = 0.274) was slightly smaller than the direct effect (β = 0.337). Recent research has corroborated the importance of facilitating conditions as a key variable in predicting behavioral intentions to use various services, including telephone services [61], telemedicine [62], and e-prescribing [63]. In the present study context, the provision of appropriate hardware and software facilities, coupled with the availability of pertinent knowledge and skills, serves as the prerequisite and guarantee for doctors to utilize internet hospitals smoothly. These elements form the foundation for improving efficiency and saving time in doctors’ professional practices.

Perceived risk was negatively associated with behavioral intention. The finding is consistent with previous studies [43, 46]. Recognizing that their actions directly impact the health of patients, medical practitioners are keenly aware of their responsibility and the paramount importance of safety. Unlike face-to-face communication, internet hospitals provide an online diagnosis and treatment platform. This aspect makes some doctors suspicious, fearing that the quality of medical services cannot be guaranteed and that it may lead to medical disputes [64]. In addition, internet hospitals store the private information of both doctors and patients, leading to concerns among doctors about the risk of privacy leakage [65].

Performance expectancy showed the largest direct association with behavioral intention, indicating that, within this sample, doctors tend to place considerable importance on the extent to which internet hospitals can enhance work efficiency and productivity. This aligns with the findings of previous studies [39, 66]. Recent research has consistently highlighted the pivotal role of performance expectancy as a key variable in predicting behavioral intention to use internet hospitals [14], web-based assistants [67], and wearable health technologies [68]. Performance expectancy serves as the primary motivation for doctors to engage with internet hospitals in this study setting, as doctors are inclined to use internet hospitals only after they realize the value of these platforms to enhance their work efficiency.

Perceived risk and performance expectancy mediated the association between facilitating conditions and intention to use internet hospitals. Most existing studies treat perceived risk and performance expectancy as relatively independent variables, primarily focusing on their effects on behavioral intention. Few studies have integrated them within a single model to explore their relationship with facilitating conditions. In the study investigating pre-adoption views of electronic medical record systems by Canadian medical practitioner, Cocosila and Archer [69] found a direct positive effect of facilitating conditions on performance expectancy and a direct negative effect of perceived risk on performance expectancy. This is in accordance with our result. In the context of this study, the external institutional conditions (technical infrastructure, information systems, operating procedures, and legal support) and support for doctors’ knowledge and ability level (training, operational guidance, and user experience) may contribute to their perception of the performance and risk when using internet hospitals. Potential gains include time savings, efficiency improvement, and salary increase, while potential risks include diagnostic errors, operational difficulties, and information leakage. After considering these factors, doctors evaluate their intention to use internet hospitals. Furthermore, the mediating effect of performance expectancy was stronger than that of perceived risk, suggesting that facilitating conditions was more strongly associated with behavioral intention through performance expectancy than through perceived risk in this sample.

In addition, facilitating conditions was indirectly associated with behavioral intention through perceived risk and performance expectancy, forming a sequential mediating pathway. As mentioned above, few studies have examined the relationships among these four variables outside the framework of the UTAUT model. To the best of our knowledge, this relationship has not been confirmed in previous studies. If favorable conditions for using internet hospitals, such as relevant knowledge and skills, comfortable environment, smooth network and system, friendly human-computer interaction, and high security, are in place, doctors’ risk perception and concerns may be alleviated. This, in turn, is associated with elevated performance expectancy and a greater willingness to use internet hospitals. This sequential mediating pathway extends the application of the stimulus–organism–response theory by empirically demonstrating how facilitating conditions is associated with doctors’ cognitive evaluations, which are further linked to their intention to use internet hospitals. This has also been mentioned in a study on AI-based diabetes diagnostic intervention conducted in India [70].

Interestingly, in the multi-group analysis, the direct association between facilitating conditions and perceived risk was not significant among doctors with lower professional titles, indicating that enhancements in conditions are not necessarily associated with a reduction in doctors’ perception of risk. In addition, the absolute value of path coefficient from perceived risk to behavioral intention was significantly smaller than that of doctors with higher titles. The following interpretations are offered as contextual explanations of these statistical differences. A potential interpretation for this finding is that doctors with lower titles tend to use internet technologies more frequently and might have more faith in the safety of internet hospital, which could correlate with fewer concerns about risks. This is similar to the findings reported by the studies conducted in Saudi Arabia [49] and Ethiopia [71]. However, for doctors with higher titles, the direct association between facilitating conditions and behavioral intention was not significant and was significantly weaker than that observed among doctors with lower titles. While the underlying reasons require further empirical validation, this result could potentially reflect differences in clinical roles and work patterns, whereby senior doctors tend to engage less frequently in direct online service provision. In addition, senior doctors typically manage more complex clinical cases and could therefore report heightened concerns regarding treatment risks and service effectiveness [72], a trend consistent with a study on electronic medical records in Hong Kong [73]. It should be noted that explanations for subgroup differences remain speculative and warrant further investigation. As professional title serves as a composite proxy for seniority, workload, and institutional roles in the Chinese healthcare system, the observed differences should be interpreted as heterogeneity across ranks rather than definitive causal effects.

Implications

Based on the findings of this study, several practical suggestions can be considered for healthcare institutions and policymakers in similar contexts. Within this specific operational setting, facilitating conditions plays a key role in doctors’ intention to use internet hospitals, highlighting the importance of supportive infrastructure and organizational resources. To potentially promote adoption, local policymakers could focus on optimizing regulatory frameworks and health resource allocation [37, 74]. Meanwhile, medical institutions might consider establishing efficient, compatible systems to support smoother operations and data integration [75]. Continuous updates of information systems may also be beneficial to ensure that internet hospitals remain functionally relevant to clinical practice.

To alleviate doctors’ perceived risk toward internet hospitals, developing robust and systematic risk mitigation strategies could be considered. Medical institutions might benefit from establishing enhanced data security mechanisms, including encrypted transmission, access control, and regular security audits, to protect sensitive medical information. In addition, the development of clear and standardized protocols for online consultations may help regulate clinical procedures and clarify legal responsibilities, potentially reducing the likelihood of medical disputes. Meanwhile, strengthening the management and supervision of medical data storage could further support security, integrity, and traceability of electronic medical records [76]. The reasonable income distribution and performance evaluation are crucial factors affecting doctors’ active participation [77]. Therefore, the regional price management department could explore improving the pricing mechanism and consider appropriately expanding medical insurance coverage, especially for low-risk common and chronic diseases, to potentially broaden the application scenarios of internet hospitals [8].

Establishing comprehensive and continuous training and support systems may help promote doctors’ intention to use internet hospitals and enhance their confidence in the platforms’ safety, reliability, and clinical effectiveness. Hospitals could consider implementing structured, multi-level training programs that encompass platform operation, clinical workflow integration, and risk management. Meanwhile, on-site technical support teams and rapid-response help centers could be beneficial in addressing technical problems encountered in daily use, thereby reducing operational barriers and frustration [77]. Strengthening familiarity with platform functionalities, data security mechanisms, and standardized procedures is likely to be associated with improved performance expectancy and reduced perceived risk. Adoption strategies for internet hospitals could be tailored to doctors with different professional titles [14] within similar institutional environments. For junior and intermediate doctors, facilitating conditions appear to be more closely linked to behavioral intention. Prioritizing system usability and operational training may be more effective. In contrast, for senior doctors, strategies might prioritize reducing clinical, legal, and data security risks while emphasizing efficiency gains, clinical value, and professional benefits associated with internet hospital use.

Limitations and future research

Nevertheless, this study has some limitations as follows. First, convenience sampling was used to recruit participants, which may introduce selection bias and limit the generalizability of the findings. The results should be interpreted with caution. Future studies could adopt a strict random sampling for more representative and reliable conclusions. Although the sample size met commonly accepted methodological criteria for SEM, future studies with larger samples are still warranted to further enhance statistical power and generalizability. Second, the study focused on public hospitals in Shanghai, which is notable for its leading economic and healthcare development, along with distinct policy and cultural attributes. The findings might not be generalizable to private clinics, other healthcare settings, or to other regions of China. Future research should adopt multi-regional and multi-stakeholder designs—including patients and administrators—and include diverse types of healthcare institutions to validate the robustness of our findings and to generalize results across diverse healthcare systems. Third, although procedural remedies were applied during the survey design and Harman’s single-factor test suggested no serious common method bias, this post-hoc approach has limited sensitivity. Future studies may adopt more robust techniques to better detect and control for potential method bias. Finally, this study relied on cross-sectional data, which limits the ability to draw causal inferences. Future longitudinal studies are needed to explore the causal relationships among these variables over time and to assess the impact of ongoing interventions, policy changes, and technological advancements on doctors’ adoption of internet hospitals.

Conclusions

This study explored the factors associated with the intention of doctors in public hospitals in Shanghai to use internet hospitals. The findings revealed that facilitating conditions and performance expectancy were positively associated with behavioral intention, while perceived risk was negatively associated with behavioral intention. Perceived risk and performance expectancy served as sequential mediators in the association between facilitating conditions and behavioral intention. Among doctors with lower titles, the path coefficient from facilitating conditions to behavioral intention was significantly higher than that observed among doctors with higher titles; the path coefficient from perceived risk to behavioral intention was significantly lower than that observed among doctors with higher titles. To potentially enhance the intention to use internet hospitals in similar urban healthcare systems, health administrations could consider optimizing the conditions of internet hospitals, establishing comprehensive training and support systems, improving doctors’ performance benefits, and mitigating their perception of risk. Importantly, these strategies serve as context-specific references rather than universal directives. In addition, tailoring strategies based on professional titles may be beneficial. For doctors with lower titles, the focus could be placed on providing more convenient and user-friendly conditions, while for doctors with higher titles, the emphasis might shift towards enhancing performance and minimizing perceived risk. However, given the cross-sectional design of this study, causal inferences cannot be drawn, and the relationships observed should be interpreted as associative in nature.
