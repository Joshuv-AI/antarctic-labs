Newton’s Lantern: A Reinforcement Learning Framework for Finetuning AC Power Flow Warm Start Models
Shourya Bose, Helgi Hilmarsson, Dhruv Suri

Abstract
Neural warm starts can sharply reduce the number of Newton-Raphson iterations required to solve the AC power flow problem, but existing supervised approaches generalize poorly on heavily loaded instances near voltage collapse. We prove a lower bound on the Newton-Raphson iteration count that depends on the direction of the warm start error rather than on its magnitude, and show as a corollary that the bound becomes vacuous as the smallest singular value of the power-flow Jacobian shrinks, identifying the failure mode of supervised regression near the saddle-node bifurcation. Motivated by this analysis, we introduce Newton’s Lantern , a finetuning pipeline that combines group relative policy optimization with a learned reward model trained on perturbations of the base model’s predictions, using the iteration count itself as the supervisory signal. Across IEEE 118-bus, GOC 500-bus, and GOC 2000-bus benchmarks, Newton’s Lantern is the only method that converges on every test snapshot while attaining the smallest mean iteration count.



1
Introduction

The electric grid is instrumental in providing a reliable supply of electricity to billions of people worldwide. A critical component of grid operations is the AC power flow (ACPF) problem
(
Stott 1974
)
, which determines the steady-state operating point of the system. At its core, ACPF is a root-finding problem for a set of nonlinear equations
g_{D}(x)
describing the grid, wherein
x
represents the system state and
D
a single snapshot of the grid characteristics. Here,
x
comprises the bus voltage magnitudes and angles to be determined, while
D
encapsulates the grid’s physical and operational parameters. It is conventionally solved using the Newton-Raphson (NR) algorithm
(
Tinney and Hart 1967
)
. An important objective for utility companies and independent system operators is to determine a good starting point
x_{0}
for the NR algorithm such that it converges in the fewest iterations. The conventionally accepted solution is the
flat start

(
Tinney and Hart 1967
)
, which sets all voltage magnitudes to unity and angles to zero, or the
DC start
, which uses linear DC power flow to generate angles while retaining the unity voltage magnitudes. However, as the size of the grid (and therefore the resulting ACPF problem) grows, both methods can fail to achieve convergence. One category of solutions modifies the NR algorithm itself to improve its convergence characteristics
(
Iwamoto and Tamura 1981
)
, but these methods fail when the region of convergence is far from the flat start point. The second category of solutions, which encompasses the present work, exploits the structure of
g_{D}(\,\cdot\,)
or a dataset of solutions corresponding to different instances of
D
to generate a good
x_{0}
.

In the latter category, the analytical method of choice has conventionally been
continuation
power flow, which obtains the solution of a ‘difficult’ instance
D^{\prime}
by tracing a path homotopy from the solution of a related ‘easy’ instance
D

(
Ajjarapu and Christy 1992
;
Mehta et al. 2016
)
. Recently however, there has been increased interest in using learning-based methods to approximate the mapping
D\mapsto x_{0}
from datasets of pre-solved ACPF instances. While there exists a plethora of work in the supervised setting (see
(
Khaloie et al. 2025
)
for a detailed review), applications of reinforcement learning (RL) for this purpose
(
Yan et al. 2025
)
have been scarce. This is disappointing, since ACPF solvers produce rich traces during the iterative solution process, which can be used to improve model performance without explicit labels. Another aspect which has been understudied, and directly relates to the necessity of RL for this problem is the contracted region of convergence when the grid is near its
voltage collapse
point
(
Sauer and Pai 1990
)
. This implies that warm start models trained on grids operating under nominal conditions fail to generalize to the aforementioned cases.

We specifically focus on three shortcomings with the present literature.

1.

Supervised warm start models use ACPF solutions as targets:
A significant amount of prior literature on supervised warm start models for ACPF
(
Okhuegbe et al. 2024b
;
Okhuegbe et al. 2024a
;
Diehl 2019
)
uses the exact solution
x^{*}
from existing datasets as the target for the model. As will be shown in the sequel, this results in poor warm start performance when the unit vector
\frac{\hat{x}^{*}-x^{*}}{\left\lVert\hat{x}^{*}-x^{*}\right\rVert}
points in certain directions, where
\hat{x}^{*}
is the model prediction.

2.

Heavily loaded systems near voltage collapse are not considered:
Most ML-for-ACPF datasets are generated by uniformly perturbing a lightly loaded operating point, so the entire dataset stays in the voltage stability regime. The recent PF
\Delta
benchmark
(
Rivera et al. 2025
)
is a notable exception, using continuation methods to include near collapse cases, but the models trained therein are not used for warm starts. This results in a gap in literature concerning generalization of warm start models.

3.

Reinforcement learning has been underutilized:
To our knowledge, only two prior works apply RL to improve ACPF convergence:
Yan et al. 2025
modifies each NR step, while
Kaseb et al. 2025
requires a quantum-enhanced RL environment. Neither yields a plug-and-play warm start model which simply generates a
x_{0}
that minimizes the number of NR iterations.

To overcome these shortcomings, we introduce
Newton’s Lantern
, an RL framework for finetuning warm start models that are pretrained on datasets restricted to voltage stable operating points away from the voltage collapse point, and are subsequently finetuned on a difficult holdout dataset containing near collapse cases. This setup is representative of practical grid operating conditions, where overloading and voltage collapse occur only under rare adverse events such as extreme weather and demand spikes
(
Khazeiynasab and Qi 2021
)
. Our contributions are concretely as follows.

1.

Characterizing the shortcoming of ACPF targets:
We provide a theoretical result showing that the NR iteration count depends on the direction of the prediction error of warm start models trained with the ACPF solution
x^{*}
as the supervised target. A representative 2-bus example is analyzed in Appendix [FUTURE].

2.

Considering heavily loaded systems:
The above result implies that near the voltage collapse point, anisotropy of the region of convergence causes the NR iteration count to inflate severely in certain directions. We demonstrate experimentally that warm start models trained on voltage stable cases fail to produce a starting point for which NR converges, while supervised finetuning (SFT) on a holdout set of voltage collapse cases restores convergence.

3.

Reinforcement learning for improving convergence:
Our framework
Newton’s Lantern
uses group-relative policy optimization (GRPO) with a negative iteration-count reward to finetune pretrained warm start models on the voltage collapse holdout. Experiments show it outperforms SFT on multiple grid examples, as well as a proximal policy optimization (PPO) baseline with an oracle value function.

Notation.

\mathbb{R}^{n}
denotes
n
-dimensional Euclidean space and
S^{n-1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}\left\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\left\lVert v\right\rVert=1\right\}

\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert
are Euclidean unless an explicit subscript appears. The class

collects functions with continuous third derivatives. For a vector-valued map

, the Hessian
\nabla^{2}g

, and
\nabla^{2}g\,[v,v]\in\mathbb{R}^{m}

\ell

. A solution of
g_{D}(x)=0
is denoted

, a model’s prediction by

, and the NR warm start by
x_{0}
. The smallest singular value of a matrix

is
\sigma_{\min}(M)
.

Background

2.1
AC Power Flow

Let
\mathcal{G}=\left\{\mathcal{N},\mathcal{E}\right\}
be a power network with
N\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}|\mathcal{N}|
buses. The ACPF residual
g_{D}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}\begin{bmatrix}\Delta P^{\top}&\Delta Q^{\top}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
is given by the bus-wise mismatches

\displaystyle\Delta P_{i}

\displaystyle=P_{i}^{\text{spec}}-V_{i}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}}V_{j}\left(G_{ij}\cos(\theta_{i}-\theta_{j})+B_{ij}\sin(\theta_{i}-\theta_{j})\right),

\displaystyle\Delta Q_{i}

\displaystyle=Q_{i}^{\text{spec}}-V_{i}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}}V_{j}\left(G_{ij}\sin(\theta_{i}-\theta_{j})-B_{ij}\cos(\theta_{i}-\theta_{j})\right),

where
V_{i},\theta_{i}
are the voltage magnitude and angle at bus
i
,
P_{i}^{\text{spec}},Q_{i}^{\text{spec}}
are the specified active and reactive injections, and
G_{ij},B_{ij}
are the conductance and susceptance entries of the bus admittance matrix. The grid snapshot
D
collects these admittances, the specified injections, and a bus-type label (PQ, PV, or slack) per bus, which pins two of the 4-tuple
(V_{i},\theta_{i},P_{i}^{\text{spec}},Q_{i}^{\text{spec}})
:
(P,Q)
at PQ buses,
(P,V)
at PV buses, and
(V,\theta)
at the slack bus. The free unknowns therefore form an NR system of effective dimension
N_{\text{PV}}+2N_{\text{PQ}}
, where
N_{\text{PV}},N_{\text{PQ}}
count the respective bus types. Following standard convention in ACPF software and literature on warm start models, we represent the state in the over-parameterized space
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2N}
of all bus voltages and angles; entries pinned by
D
are overwritten before NR is invoked.

2.2
NR Method and Voltage Collapse

The NR method is an iterative root finding algorithm which, starting from an initial point
x_{0}
, proceeds as

\displaystyle x_{k+1}=x_{k}-\left[J_{D}(x_{k})\right]^{-1}g_{D}(x_{k}),

and terminates at iteration
k(x_{0},\tau)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}\inf\left\{k\geq 1:\left\lVert x_{k}-x_{k-1}\right\rVert<\tau\right\}
, where
\tau
is a predefined termination tolerance. Here,
J_{D}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}\partial g_{D}(x)/\partial x
is the Jacobian of the residual, commonly referred to as the
power-flow Jacobian
.
Voltage collapse
refers to the phenomenon wherein, as the load on the grid grows, bus voltages decline progressively until the ACPF loses its solution entirely, marking the steady-state stability limit
(
Sauer and Pai 1990
)
. Whether a given snapshot
D
lies near this limit is itself a property of
D
, since the loading, generation dispatch, and topology it encodes determine how close the system operates to collapse. This limit is characterized by the singularity of the power-flow Jacobian at the equilibrium. As
D
approaches a collapse condition, the smallest singular value
\sigma_{\min}(J_{D}(x^{*}))
shrinks to zero, and is widely used as a proximity index to voltage instability
(
Tiranuchit and Thomas 1988
)
. Figure
1
illustrates this on the IEEE 14-bus system. The minimum bus voltage and
\sigma_{\min}(J_{D}(x^{*}))
both collapse to small values as the loading factor
\lambda
approaches the bifurcation point at which the NR method ceases to converge. The contour in Figure
1(c)
further reveals that the convergence basin around the nominal load is highly
anisotropic
. NR iteration counts grow much faster along certain directions in
(P,Q)
than others, with the basin pinching off into a narrow tongue near the divergence boundary. This directional sensitivity, which is driven by the singular structure of
J_{D}
near collapse, will play a central role in the analysis that follows.

3
Convergence Anisotropy and the Failure of Distance Losses

Supervised warm start models for ACPF are trained against
x^{*}
as a regression target
(
Khaloie et al. 2025
;
Okhuegbe et al. 2024b
;
Diehl 2019
)
, under the implicit assumption that a smaller
\left\lVert\hat{x}^{*}-x^{*}\right\rVert
yields fewer NR iterations. This assumption presumes that the convergence region around
x^{*}
is isotropic, which Figure
1(c)
and the established literature on load flow fractals
(
Thorp and Naqavi 1989
)
show is not the case. We quantify the anisotropy through a lower bound on the NR iteration count that depends on the direction of the warm start error rather than its magnitude. We write the warm start error as
e_{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}x_{0}-x^{*}
in polar form
e_{0}=\rho v
with
\rho\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}\left\lVert e_{0}\right\rVert
and
v\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}e_{0}/\rho\in S^{2N-1}
. Newton’s method requires regularity of
g_{D}
and nonsingularity of
J_{D}(x^{*})

(
Ortega and Rheinboldt 2000
;
Kelley 2003
;
Deuflhard 2004
)
, which we make precise as the following two assumptions.

(A1)

g_{D}\in C^{3}
on a neighborhood of
x^{*}
. This holds automatically for ACPF, since
g_{D}
is polynomial in voltage magnitudes and trigonometric in angles
(
Tinney and Hart 1967
)
.

(A2)

The Hessian tensor
H_{D}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}\nabla^{2}g_{D}(x^{*})
satisfies
\inf_{v\in S^{2N-1}}\left\lVert J_{D}(x^{*})^{-1}H_{D}[v,v]\right\rVert\geq 2q
for some
q>0
. By
Sauer and Pai 1990
, this is equivalent to
x^{*}
being away from the saddle-node bifurcation, the loading at which
\sigma_{\min}(J_{D}(x^{*}))=0
.

Theorem 3.1

(Lower bound on NR iterations by direction)
.

Under (A1) and (A2), there exists
r_{0}>0
, depending only on the third-derivative bound of
g_{D}
and on
q
, such that for every warm start
x_{0}=x^{*}+\rho v
with
\rho\in(\tau,r_{0})
,

\displaystyle k(x_{0},\tau)\;\geq\;\log_{2}\!\left(\frac{\log(1/\tau)}{\log(1/\rho)-\Lambda(v;D)}\right)-1,

(1)

whenever
\log(1/\rho)-\Lambda(v;D)>0
. The function
\Lambda\colon S^{2N-1}\to\mathbb{R}^{1}
is a discounted average of
\log\left\lVert Q_{D}(\,\cdot\,)\right\rVert
along the orbit of
v
under the Newton-direction map on the sphere, where
Q_{D}(v)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}\frac{1}{2}J_{D}(x^{*})^{-1}H_{D}[v,v]\in\mathbb{R}^{2N}
. The contribution of the
j
-th iterate to
\Lambda
is weighted by
2^{-j-1}
. Definitions and proof are in Appendix
A
.

The bound is doubly logarithmic in the warm start magnitude
\rho
and only singly logarithmic in
\Lambda(v;D)
, so direction has the larger leverage. A distance loss
\left\lVert\hat{x}^{*}-x^{*}\right\rVert
carries no representation of
\Lambda
, and two models with identical mean squared error can produce warm starts requiring very different iteration counts.

Corollary 3.2

(Voltage collapse uniformly degrades the lower bound)
.

Let
\left\{D_{\lambda}\right\}_{\lambda\in[0,\lambda^{*})}
be a smooth path of grid snapshots approaching a saddle-node bifurcation at
\lambda=\lambda^{*}
, so
\sigma_{\lambda}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}\sigma_{\min}(J_{D_{\lambda}}(x_{\lambda}^{*}))\to 0^{+}
as
\lambda\to\lambda^{*}
. Then for
v
outside a Lebesgue-measure-zero subset of
S^{2N-1}
,

\displaystyle\Lambda(v;D_{\lambda})\;=\;\log(1/\sigma_{\lambda})+\mathcal{C}(v)+o(1),

(2)

where
\mathcal{C}(v)=O(1)
is bounded uniformly. The denominator of the bound in Theorem
3.1
therefore vanishes once
\sigma_{\lambda}\lesssim\rho
, at which point the bound becomes vacuous.

Near voltage collapse, the
\log(1/\sigma_{\lambda})
term dominates the bounded correction
\mathcal{C}(v)
for almost every direction, and the convergence basin contracts to size
\sim\sigma_{\lambda}
. A model trained on voltage stable cases produces
\hat{x}^{*}
with
\left\lVert\hat{x}^{*}-x^{*}\right\rVert\gg\sigma_{\lambda}
near
\lambda^{*}
, so the bound becomes vacuous and NR diverges. The measure zero exception consists of directions whose Newton-direction orbit stays orthogonal to the voltage collapse mode of
J_{D_{\lambda}}(x_{\lambda}^{*})

(
Gao et al. 1992
;
Dobson 1992
)
, and these correspond to the long axis of the narrow tongue in Figure
1(c)
.

collects the empirical content of this section on the IEEE 14-bus case. Figures

v(\theta)=\cos\theta\,w+\sin\theta\,v_{\perp}

The two results identify the iteration count, not the Euclidean distance to
x^{*}
, as the quantity a warm start model should optimize. The next section develops a GRPO finetuning pipeline that uses the iteration count directly as a reward signal.

4
Finetuning with Newton’s Lantern

Consider the setting in which a base warm start model
f_{\theta}
has been pretrained on a large supervised dataset of voltage stable operating points, and the modeler is granted a smaller holdout of more difficult instances on which to finetune. The role of the holdout is to expose
f_{\theta}
to the near voltage collapse regime identified in Section
3
as the failure mode of distance based losses. The PF
\Delta
benchmark of
Rivera et al. 2025
, populated by continuation power flow runs that approach the bifurcation, is one such source.

The simplest use of the holdout is supervised finetuning (SFT), in which
f_{\theta}
is updated against
x^{*}
with the same regression loss used during pretraining. We retain SFT as a baseline. By Theorem
3.1
and Corollary
3.2
, SFT is structurally limited because the regression loss carries no representation of the directional functional
\Lambda(v;D)
that controls the iteration count near the bifurcation. To break this degeneracy we move to a finetuning objective that depends on the iteration count itself.

Reinforcement learning formulation.

We cast finetuning as a single-step Markov decision process. The state is the grid snapshot
s\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}D
drawn from the holdout, and the action
a\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{2N}
is the warm start that the policy supplies to NR. The mean of the policy is the base model’s prediction
f_{\theta}(s)
, perturbed by a Gaussian whose state-independent log standard deviations
\log\sigma_{V}
and
\log\sigma_{\theta}
are optimized jointly with
\theta
,

\displaystyle a=f_{\theta}(s)+\sigma\odot\epsilon,\qquad\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I),

(3)

where
\odot
acts only on the free voltage and angle coordinates of the over parameterized state. The policy is updated with proximal policy optimization
(
Schulman et al. 2017
)
, whose clipped surrogate objective

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{PPO}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\min\!\big(\eta_{t}(\theta)A_{t},\ \mathrm{clip}\bigl(\eta_{t}(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon\bigr)A_{t}\big)\right],

(4)

employs the importance ratio
\eta_{t}(\theta)=\pi_{\theta}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})/\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})
relative to the data-generating policy, and an advantage
A_{t}
formed by subtracting a baseline from the action’s reward. The reward and the baseline are the two design choices that separate our two configurations below.

Configuration 1 (PPO with an oracle baseline).

Because the holdout ships with labeled solutions, an oracle baseline is available without a learned critic. For each snapshot
s
we precompute
V^{\star}(s)
, the iteration count of NR seeded from
x^{*}(s)
. The reward for a sampled action is a bounded saturating function of the iteration count,

\displaystyle r_{\mathrm{sat}}(s,a)=\begin{cases}R_{+}-g\bigl(k(a,\tau)\bigr),&\text{NR converged from }a,\\
-R_{-},&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}\qquad g(n)=\frac{n-1}{n-1+c},

(5)

where
c
is set to the holdout-mean of
V^{\star}
so that
g
reaches its half-saturation value at the typical iteration count and
R_{\pm}
keep the reward bounded. The advantage takes the form

\displaystyle A(s,a)=r_{\mathrm{sat}}(s,a)-r_{\mathrm{sat}}\bigl(s,x^{*}(s)\bigr),

(6)

which subtracts the per-snapshot oracle from the rollout reward. We denote this configuration
PPO+
V^{\star}
.

Configuration 2 (GRPO with a learned reward).

PPO+
V^{\star}
inherits two computational costs. Each rollout requires running NR to obtain
r_{\mathrm{sat}}
, and each holdout snapshot incurs an additional Newton solve to compute
V^{\star}(s)
. We address both with a pair of substitutions, replacing the per-snapshot oracle with a per-batch group baseline and replacing NR with a learned reward model.

For the baseline, we adopt group relative policy optimization
(
Shao et al. 2024
)
. For each state in the batch we draw
K
rollouts from the current policy, evaluate their rewards, and standardize within the resulting group,

\displaystyle A_{k}=\frac{r_{k}-\mu_{g}}{\sigma_{g}+\varepsilon_{g}},

(7)

with
\mu_{g}
and
\sigma_{g}
the within-group mean and standard deviation. The group baseline absorbs the state-dependent offset that
V^{\star}
would have removed, without consulting the labeled solution.

For the reward, we replace the NR call with a learned reward model
R_{\phi}
that maps the snapshot features and the warm start to a predicted iteration count. The model is trained on perturbations of the base model’s own predictions. For each holdout snapshot, we sample a grid of magnitudes and random directions in the free coordinate subspace, run NR once per perturbed warm start, and aggregate the resulting
(s,a,k(a,\tau))
triples into a regression dataset. Training
R_{\phi}
on this distribution calibrates the reward over the region the policy will subsequently visit.

The reward function used in this configuration is not the saturating
r_{\mathrm{sat}}
of Configuration 1. Rather, we adopt a piecewise linear reward with a convergence bonus,

\displaystyle r_{\mathrm{lin}}(s,a)=-R_{\phi}(s,a)+B\cdot\mathbf{1}\bigl[R_{\phi}(s,a)<k_{\max}\bigr],

(8)

where
k_{\max}
is the iteration cap that defines convergence and
B
is a constant on the order of
k_{\max}
. The choice was hand-tuned upon two empirical observations made during training. First,
r_{\mathrm{sat}}
is nearly flat over the iteration counts that GRPO rollouts typically produce on a holdout, and the within-group standardization
A_{k}
collapses to noise under such a flat reward; the linear form spreads the reward over the relevant range so that the standardized advantage is informative. Second, without an additional discrete signal at the convergence boundary, GRPO has no way to distinguish a marginally non-converging rollout from a comfortably converging one; the bonus
B
creates the necessary gap. We denote this configuration
GRPO+
R_{\phi}
.

Newton’s Lantern.

Newton’s Lantern is the GRPO+
R_{\phi}
configuration together with one additional ingredient. Every
T_{v}
outer iterations we evaluate the current policy on a held-out validation slice using real NR, and at the end of training we return the snapshot whose validation iteration count is smallest, rather than the policy of the final outer iteration. This decouples the returned policy from the noise of any single iteration. The full procedure is summarized in Algorithm

.

4.1
Models

The pipeline above does not depend on the architecture of the base model
f_{\theta}
beyond the requirement that
f_{\theta}(s)
produce a usable prediction of the ACPF solution. We instantiate Newton’s Lantern with two such backbones to span the practical range of warm start architectures in the literature.

The first backbone is a heterogeneous graph neural network (GNN) in the message passing style of
Piloto et al. 2024
, the architecture used by their CANOS solver for AC optimal power flow. Each bus is a node with features encoding demand, generation, and shunt, each line is an edge with features encoding line impedance, susceptance, transformer ratio, and rate limits, and the graph is heterogeneous in that PQ, PV, and slack buses carry distinct node embeddings. Per-node-type encoder multilayer perceptrons (MLPs) lift these inputs to a hidden representation
h^{(0)}_{v}
of dimension
d
. The network then performs
T
rounds of edge updates and node aggregations,

\displaystyle m_{e}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=\phi_{\mathrm{type}(e)}^{\,e}\bigl(h_{u(e)}^{(t)},\,h_{v(e)}^{(t)},\,e_{\mathrm{attr}}\bigr),

(9)

\displaystyle h_{v}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=\phi_{\mathrm{type}(v)}^{\,n}\!\left(h_{v}^{(t)},\,\frac{1}{|\mathcal{E}(v)|}\!\!\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}(v)}m_{e}^{(t+1)}\right),

(10)

where
\phi^{e}
and
\phi^{n}
are MLPs whose parameters are shared across edges or nodes of the same type, and
\mathcal{E}(v)
denotes the edges incident to
v
. After the final round, per-node-type decoder MLPs produce
(\hat{\theta}_{i},\hat{V}_{i})
at PQ buses,
(\hat{Q}_{i},\hat{\theta}_{i})
at PV buses, and the slack injections
(\hat{P}_{\text{slack}},\hat{Q}_{\text{slack}})
. The components of the over-parameterized state that are pinned by
D
(slack
V
and
\theta
, PV
V
) are then overwritten with their setpoints before
\hat{x}^{*}
is returned, so the same model can be queried for warm starts directly. To verify that any improvement we report is attributable to the GRPO pipeline rather than to the inductive bias of the GNN, we also instantiate
f_{\theta}
as a fully connected feedforward network (FCNN) on the smallest test grid.

6
Conclusion

We derived a lower bound on the Newton-Raphson iteration count for AC power flow warm starts that depends on the direction of the warm start error rather than on its magnitude. The directional analysis explained the empirical failure of supervised regression near voltage collapse, and motivated Newton’s Lantern, a GRPO finetuning pipeline with a learned reward model that uses iteration count as the supervisory signal. Across three grid sizes, Newton’s Lantern was the only method to converge on every test snapshot, while attaining the smallest mean iteration count. The narrow margins observed on the two GNN cases reflect the broader difficulty of training GNN policies with RL. Designing architectures and RL algorithms that handle this regime more gracefully is a natural direction for extending Newton’s Lantern to larger grids.

Proof of Theorem

and

are evaluated at the nonsingular root

Lemma A.1

(First-order Newton expansion)
.

The error sequence

of the Newton-Raphson iteration applied to

\displaystyle e_{k+1}=-Q_{D}(\hat{e}_{k})\,\rho_{k}^{2}+R_{3}(e_{k}),\qquad\left\lVert R_{3}(e_{k})\right\rVert\leq K_{3}\rho_{k}^{3},

(12)

and
\left\lVert J_{D}(x^{*})^{-1}\right\rVert

Proof.

The expansion is the standard third-order Taylor expansion of the Newton step at the nonsingular root

. The constant

aggregates the third-derivative bound
M_{3}
with the operator norm of

is compact and

Ortega and Rheinboldt 2000
and
Kelley 2003

∎

Lemma A.2

(Splitting magnitude and direction)
.

Under (A1) and (A2), the recursion of Lemma

\displaystyle\rho_{k+1}=\left\lVert Q_{D}(v_{k})\right\rVert\,\rho_{k}^{2}\,(1+O(\rho_{k})),\qquad v_{k+1}=\Phi_{D}(v_{k})+O(\rho_{k}),

(13)

where the

\displaystyle\Phi_{D}(v)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}-\frac{Q_{D}(v)}{\left\lVert Q_{D}(v)\right\rVert}.

(14)

Under (A2),

uniformly in

Proof.

Substituting

\displaystyle e_{k+1}=-Q_{D}(v_{k})\,\rho_{k}^{2}+O(\rho_{k}^{3}).

together with the uniform lower bound

provided by assumption (A2). Sphere maps of this form arise routinely in the analysis of Newton iterations

)

(
Thorp and Naqavi 1989
)
.

Definition A.3

(The direction functional)

For

\displaystyle\Lambda(v;D)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}2^{-j-1}\log\left\lVert Q_{D}(\Phi_{D}^{j}(v))\right\rVert.

(16)

From Lemma

\displaystyle\rho_{k+1}\leq\left\lVert Q_{D}(v_{k})\right\rVert\,\rho_{k}^{2}\,(1+O(\rho_{k})).

Iterating this inequality

\displaystyle\rho_{k}\leq\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}\left\lVert Q_{D}(v_{j})\right\rVert^{2^{k-1-j}}\cdot\rho_{0}^{2^{k}}\cdot(1+O(\rho_{0})).

Taking logarithms and dividing by

\displaystyle\frac{\log(1/\rho_{k})}{2^{k}}\;\geq\;\log(1/\rho_{0})-\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}2^{-j-1}\log\left\lVert Q_{D}(v_{j})\right\rVert+O(\rho_{0}).

Lemma

in the partial sum accumulates only bounded

\displaystyle\frac{\log(1/\rho_{k})}{2^{k}}\;\geq\;\log(1/\rho_{0})-\Lambda(v_{0};D)+O(2^{-k}).

The Newton iteration terminates at the first step

\displaystyle\frac{\log(1/\tau^{\prime})}{2^{k-1}}\;>\;\log(1/\rho_{0})-\Lambda(v_{0};D),

Taking

Verification of (A2) for ACPF.

The Hessian tensor
H_{D}=\nabla^{2}g_{D}(x^{*})
has entries that are products of bus voltages
V_{i}V_{j}
and trigonometric functions of angle differences
\theta_{ij}
, in the standard polar form ACPF conventions of
Tinney and Hart 1967
. These are bounded above and below in any compact operating region with
V_{i}>0
and
|\theta_{ij}|<\pi/2
. The product
J_{D}(x^{*})^{-1}H_{D}[v,v]
is then bounded below by
\sigma_{\min}(J_{D}(x^{*}))\cdot\inf_{v}\left\lVert H_{D}[v,v]\right\rVert/\left\lVert J_{D}(x^{*})\right\rVert^{2}
, which is positive whenever
J_{D}(x^{*})
is nonsingular. By

corresponds exactly to the saddle-node bifurcation, so (A2) holds at every operating point that is voltage stable in the standard sense. The constant

Appendix B
Proof of Corollary
3.2

Throughout this appendix,
w_{\lambda}
denotes the right-singular vector of
J_{D_{\lambda}}(x_{\lambda}^{*})
corresponding to its smallest singular value
\sigma_{\lambda}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}\sigma_{\min}(J_{D_{\lambda}}(x_{\lambda}^{*}))
. By
Sauer and Pai 1990
and
Gao et al. 1992
,
w_{\lambda}
converges as
\lambda\to\lambda^{*}
to the
voltage collapse mode

w^{*}
, the right-eigenvector of
J_{D^{*}}^{\top}J_{D^{*}}
at the bifurcation point. The saddle-node-bifurcation characterization that the proof relies on is the one given in
Dobson 1992
.

For each
v\in S^{2N-1}
, decompose
H_{D_{\lambda}}[v,v]
along
w_{\lambda}
and its orthogonal complement,

\displaystyle H_{D_{\lambda}}[v,v]=\alpha_{\lambda}(v)\,w_{\lambda}+r_{\lambda}(v),\qquad\alpha_{\lambda}(v)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}\langle w_{\lambda},H_{D_{\lambda}}[v,v]\rangle,\quad r_{\lambda}(v)\perp w_{\lambda}.

(25)

Using the SVD identity
J_{D_{\lambda}}^{-1}w_{\lambda}=\sigma_{\lambda}^{-1}\tilde{w}_{\lambda}
, where
\tilde{w}_{\lambda}
is the corresponding left-singular vector, applying
J_{D_{\lambda}}^{-1}
to each component gives

\displaystyle Q_{D_{\lambda}}(v)=\frac{\alpha_{\lambda}(v)}{2\sigma_{\lambda}}\,\tilde{w}_{\lambda}+\frac{1}{2}\,J_{D_{\lambda}}^{-1}r_{\lambda}(v).

(26)

The first term diverges as
\sigma_{\lambda}\to 0
, while the second remains uniformly bounded near
\lambda=\lambda^{*}
because
g_{D}
is
C^{3}
and
J_{D_{\lambda}}^{-1}
stays bounded on the orthogonal complement of
w_{\lambda}
even as
\sigma_{\lambda}
shrinks to zero
(
Stewart and Sun 1990
)
. The asymptotic behavior of
\left\lVert Q_{D_{\lambda}}\right\rVert
is therefore controlled entirely by the first term, except on the surface
\mathcal{Z}_{\lambda}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}\left\{v\in S^{2N-1}:\alpha_{\lambda}(v)=0\right\}
on which
H_{D_{\lambda}}[v,v]
is orthogonal to the collapse mode and the leading term vanishes. Because
\alpha_{\lambda}
is a quadratic form in
v
, the set
\mathcal{Z}_{\lambda}
is the zero locus of a real-analytic function on
S^{2N-1}
, and therefore has Lebesgue measure zero. For
v\notin\mathcal{Z}_{\lambda}
, the leading-order asymptotic gives

\displaystyle\left\lVert Q_{D_{\lambda}}(v)\right\rVert=\frac{|\alpha_{\lambda}(v)|}{2\sigma_{\lambda}}\,(1+o(1))

(27)

as
\sigma_{\lambda}\to 0
, and equivalently

\displaystyle\log\left\lVert Q_{D_{\lambda}}(v)\right\rVert=\log(1/\sigma_{\lambda})+\log|\alpha_{\lambda}(v)|-\log 2+o(1).

(28)

Substituting this expansion into Definition
A.3
gives

\displaystyle\Lambda(v;D_{\lambda})=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}2^{-j-1}\log\left\lVert Q_{D_{\lambda}}(\Phi_{D_{\lambda}}^{j}(v))\right\rVert.

(29)

For
v
outside a Lebesgue-measure-zero subset of
S^{2N-1}
, the orbit
\left\{\Phi_{D_{\lambda}}^{j}(v)\right\}_{j\geq 0}
avoids
\mathcal{Z}_{\lambda}
, so each term in the sum contributes
\log(1/\sigma_{\lambda})+O(1)
as
\sigma_{\lambda}\to 0
. Since the geometric weights
2^{-j-1}
sum to one, the leading
\log(1/\sigma_{\lambda})
contributions accumulate to a single
\log(1/\sigma_{\lambda})
, and the
O(1)
residuals collect into a direction dependent constant. Concretely,

\displaystyle\Lambda(v;D_{\lambda})=\log(1/\sigma_{\lambda})+\mathcal{C}(v)+o(1),

(30)

where
\mathcal{C}(v)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}2^{-j-1}\big(\log|\alpha^{*}(\Phi^{j}(v))|-\log 2\big)
, with
\alpha^{*}
and
\Phi
denoting the
\lambda\to\lambda^{*}
limits of
\alpha_{\lambda}
and
\Phi_{D_{\lambda}}
respectively. The function
\mathcal{C}
is bounded on
S^{2N-1}
, since
|\alpha^{*}|
is bounded above by the smoothness of
g_{D}
and bounded below away from zero outside a measure-zero set.

Substituting this asymptotic into the denominator of the lower bound in Theorem
3.1
gives

\displaystyle\log(1/\rho)-\Lambda(v;D_{\lambda})=\log(\sigma_{\lambda}/\rho)-\mathcal{C}(v)-o(1),

(31)

which is positive precisely when
\sigma_{\lambda}>\rho\cdot e^{\mathcal{C}(v)}
. Since
\mathcal{C}(v)
is bounded, the denominator becomes nonpositive once
\sigma_{\lambda}
falls below a constant multiple of
\rho
, and the bound of Theorem
3.1
ceases to constrain the iteration count. In practical terms, the warm start magnitude
\rho
must remain smaller than
\sigma_{\lambda}
, up to the direction dependent factor
e^{\mathcal{C}(v)}
, for the theorem to deliver a finite iteration-count guarantee.

Remark on orbits returning to
\mathcal{Z}_{\lambda}
.

Even when
v\in\mathcal{Z}_{\lambda}
, so that
\alpha_{\lambda}(v)=0
and the
j=0
term of the defining sum loses its

contribution, the iterate
\Phi_{D_{\lambda}}(v)
is generically not in
\mathcal{Z}_{\lambda}
because
\Phi_{D_{\lambda}}
does not preserve the surface
\alpha_{\lambda}=0
. The sum then still receives
\log(1/\sigma_{\lambda})
contributions from every
j\geq 1
, the only effect being a reduction of the leading coefficient from
1
to
2^{-1}
. The set of directions whose entire
\Phi_{D_{\lambda}}
-orbit lies in
\mathcal{Z}_{\lambda}
has Lebesgue measure zero, so the
\log(1/\sigma_{\lambda})
scaling holds outside a measure-zero subset of
S^{2N-1}
as claimed.
