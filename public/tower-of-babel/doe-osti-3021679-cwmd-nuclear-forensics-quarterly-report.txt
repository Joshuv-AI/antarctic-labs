 1. Project Overview
 Analysis and evaluation of nuclear debris is a key component to identify forensic signatures in a
 post-detonation environment. Currently, methods exist and are practiced in obtaining signatures
 from post-detonation debris. However, efforts to reduce the timeline in obtaining signatures is
 continuous within nuclear forensics research and will be forwarded by this research. This education
 and research project will explore combined material chemistry and separations of radionuclides to
 provide actionable signatures in less time than standard methods. The research will use the phase
 distribution of radionuclides in debris to develop targeted and rapid dissolution methods. These
 dissolution methods will be coupled with microfluidics to achieve samples suitable for analysis
 within reduced timelines.
 The research will be accomplished through three primary objectives. Correlation between
 radionuclide and debris phases will be identified. Using this information, rapid dissolution methods
 for the debris phases will be developed. The resulting dissolved phases will be chemically prepared
 for analysis using microfluidic platforms, providing samples for determining forensics signatures.
 The expertise to perform the necessary analyses and separations leverage past and current nuclear
 forensics research efforts. Once developed, the dissolution and separation methods will be tested at
 the LLNL research laboratory facility. Graduate and undergraduate students recruited into the
 RTNF program will participate in all phases of research under the direct supervision of UNLV
 radiochemistry faculty in partnership with their LLNL mentors. The tasks and milestones are
 designed so that the research performed at UNLV during the academic year can be continued by
 RTNF fellows during their summer experience at LLNL. This research will form the basis of the
 dissertations of the RTNF fellows. The project will leverage capabilities and expertise in
 radiochemistry education and research at the partner institutions. The UNLV and LLNL partners
 have a long-established interaction in nuclear forensics research, including hosting of students at
 the national laboratory and hiring of UNLV graduates into post-doctoral and staff positions. The
 project can utilize samples currently possessed by UNLV, as well as synthesized debris surrogates
 using established methods from other projects.

Task 1

Work for task 1intended to determine if Pu will precipitate out of solution when stoichiometric
amounts of Cl- are added to dissolved AgO to remove Ag prior to extraction with TBP/dodecane.
During work at LLNL low amounts of Pu were observed in the organic phases when Ag was
precipitated out with Cl- (Table 1). To determine if Pu was carried with the Ag into the AgCl lattice,
dissolution of the AgCl in concentrated H2SO4 was performed. With heating to 300 ℃ AgCl was
dissolved and allowed to cool back to room temperature. Aliquots of the aqueous solution were
removed and counted via LSC to determine if Pu was in the AgCl lattice. LSC results indicated that
Pu was not in the AgCl lattice. Further studies were performed at LLNL with no precipitation of
AgCl and are documented later in this report.

Task 2

the tetravalent state to the hexavalent state via NaBiO3 (5-10 mg) and AgO (5-10 mg) before extraction into a
30 % TBP/dodecane phase. Kinetic experiments were performed to determine the time required for both
systems to reach equilibrium. From Table 2 and Table 3 the systems reached equilibrium after 5 minutes of
contact time with the oxidant. This indicated that both systems can be used for oxidation of pre-dissolved Pu.

Peroxide fusion reactions of high fired NpO2 was performed to yield soluble peroxide compound for
dissolution into 7 M HNO3. The systems were tested with pXRD and proved largely amorphous. Upon contact
with HNO3 the systems slowly dissolved over a 16-hour period and indicated that the peroxide fusion was a
success.
Task 3
Separations of Pu and Am were conducted via oxidation to the hexavalent state via AgO, AgF 2, and
NaBiO3. AgO (5-10 mg), AgF2 (5-10 mg) was added to varying HNO3 acid concentrations in order
to determine the D-values for these oxidants. NaBiO3 (5-10 mg) was chosen as an oxidant for these
systems as literature has shown that it can be used to synthesize hexavalent materials of both Pu and
Am. From Table 4, Table 5, and Table 6 the D values for this system vary depending on the HNO 3
acid amount for the systems. For the NaBiO3 and AgO systems D-values greater than 1 were
calculated for all systems above 1.0 M HNO3. In general, the D-values for both NaBiO3 and AgO
agreed with literature values for Pu (VI). For all oxidants at 8 M HNO3 the D-values varied widely
and illustrated the low stability of Pu (VI) at higher acidities. For AgF 2 the D-values vary due to the
presence of F- which inhibited the extraction of Pu into the organic phase due to the presence of
anionic species in the system. At higher HNO3 concentrations the system began to behave closer to
that of Pu (VI), but was still below that of the other two oxidants.

Figure 2 is a visual representation of the D-values tabulated from the extraction of Pu compounds oxidized
with a variety of oxidants. NaBiO3 yields higher D-values for all HNO3 concentrations below 4 M HNO3 and
all systems yielded different D-values at high acidity (8 M HNO3). The lower extraction of Pu (VI) oxidized
by AgO is caused by the quantifiable extraction of Ag into the organic phase. Due to the competition of Pu
and Ag for free TBP, the reduced Pu extraction was expected.
