 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
The Challenge
 Nuclear facility projects completed in the last 30 years experienced
significant cost overruns and schedule delays. The traditional approach involves
hiring Engineering, Procurement, and Construction (EPC) firms with formal
nuclear quality accreditation (NQA-1) to manage projects from start to finish. For
a mid-size capital project like the Sample Preparation Laboratory (SPL) – a
$100–$200 million facility – this approach raised concerns about significant cost
overruns and schedule delays.
The Innovation
 Idaho National Laboratory (INL) took a different path. Instead of contracting
an EPC firm with a nuclear quality assurance program, INL applied its own
nuclear quality program to oversee a commercial EPC contractor. This approach
leveraged Commercial Grade Dedication (CGD) – an engineering-driven
acceptance methodology originally designed for replacing parts at operating
nuclear plants – as the primary procurement and acceptance strategy for a Hazard
Category 3 nuclear facility. This represents a first-of-a-kind (FOAK) application
at this scale for a construction project.
What Is CGD?
 CGD is fundamentally an engineering-focused quality approach where the
design authority identifies which specific characteristics of an item are "critical"
to performing its safety function. Rather than requiring suppliers to maintain
expensive nuclear-grade quality programs, CGD allows procurement from
commercial suppliers while verifying these critical characteristics through
testing, inspection, supplier audits, or source inspection/verification. Think of it
as targeted quality assurance: verify what matters for safety, document it
thoroughly, but don't over-engineer what doesn't affect performance.
Key Success Factors
 The SPL project benefited from four favorable conditions:
1. Design Authority Engagement: INL served as both the designer and design authority,
 ensuring clarity and control.
2. Mature CGD Program: INL had existing expertise and established processes for
 commercial grade dedication.
3. Dedication Expert: A subject-matter expert guided the CGD methodology through the
 entire project lifecycle.
4. Informed Regulator: Alignment with the regulator on implementation of the CGD
 process is a key enabler to success.
5. Strong Construction Management: Rigorous oversight ensured suppliers executed
 contract requirements correctly.
The Results
 The SPL was completed on time and within budget – a notable
achievement in the nuclear industry. CGD was successfully applied across all
major construction and fabrication scopes, including concrete, rebar, stainless

 iii

steel, carbon steel, lead shielding, weld filler, fasteners, fabricated items and
structures.
Cost Impact
 Quantitative analysis demonstrates substantial savings. For reinforced
concrete walls classified as safety-significant, CGD reduced the nuclear cost
escalation factor to 15% above baseline commercial costs, compared to
approximately 40% for traditional NQA-1 procurement – a reduction of roughly
two-thirds (63% improvement). Similar savings were achieved for fabricated
components by ensuring the design process was cognizant of commercial
industry offerings and capabilities thereby maximizing efficiency gains through
use of the commercial supply chain.
Key Insight for Other Industries
 Those who have worked in aerospace, oil and gas, or automotive industries
are familiar with risk-based quality approaches. CGD applies similar principles
to nuclear projects by identifying key characteristics required to ensure nuclear
safety during the engineering process, establishing clear acceptance criteria, and
verifying those characteristics throughout fabrication, and construction. The SPL
project demonstrates that nuclear projects need not be monolithic in their quality
requirements – differentiated approaches based on actual safety needs can deliver
results faster and more economically.
Why This Matters
 This white paper documents best practices for applying CGD effectively on
capital construction projects. The methodologies, lessons learned, and cost data
provided can serve as a blueprint for future nuclear projects seeking to balance
rigorous safety standards with realistic cost and schedule expectations.

 Application of CGD as the Primary Acceptance
 Method for the SPL EPC Project
 1. OVERVIEW
 The traditional paradigm applied to the construction of nuclear safety-related systems, structures, and
components (SSCs), like SPL, involves an owner/licensee hiring an EPC firm with an NQA-1 program to
manage the engineering, procurement, and construction of the desired SSC. This traditional approach
would have posed significant barriers to the successful completion of the SPL. As a smaller capital
project, in the $100–$200 million range, there was concern that employing one of the larger EPC
companies would escalate costs and extend the projected schedule beyond what would be feasible for the
Department of Energy (DOE).
 The traditional acceptance method employed for nuclear facilities with credited safety functions
requires the use of an EPC firm applying American Society of Mechanical Engineers (ASME) NQA-1
nuclear quality assurance (NQA) to accept safety SSCs. In lieu of this approach, INL elected to apply its
own NQA-1 program to hire an EPC firm operating a commercial quality program. This method used
commercial grade dedication (CGD) as outlined in NQA-1 which had not been applied at this scale. Use
of the commercial supply chain was identified as a key factor to the successful completion of SPL.
 Favorable conditions enabling the use of CGD included:
• INL serving as both the designer and the design authority
• Design created by an NQA-1 qualified source
• A mature CGD program
• Availability of a CGD subject-matter expert (SME)
• A strong construction management program.
 This white paper elaborates on the methodology employed in the construction of the SPL, the
successes achieved, and best practices implemented.
 2. CGD FOR EPC
 CGD is an engineering-driven acceptance process originally designed for the procurement of
replacement parts at nuclear power plants. When employed effectively, the CGD process is comprised of
a technical evaluation in which design basis and safety basis requirements are evaluated to identify the
critical characteristics (CCs) of items. If these characteristics are verified, they provide reasonable
assurance that the item will perform its intended safety function. An acceptance plan is then applied to the
selected CCs to ensure a quality process is employed for their verification. The fundamentals in place for
SPL providing favorable conditions for the CGD process included:
1. A completed design generated under an NQA-1 program and controlled by INL as the design
 authority.
2. A mature commercial grade dedication program capable of scaling up to support a capital project
 such as the SPL.
3. A CGD SME to design and guide the process through to success.
4. A strong construction management program.

 1

 These fundamental elements were recognized by management and leveraged at the earliest possible
point in the process to maximize effective implementation. The technical evaluation process described
above was conducted concurrently with design completion. The resulting technical evaluations, including
identified CCs and associated quality requirements, were incorporated into the construction specification
for execution by the commercial EPC contractor and overseen by INL quality assurance and engineering.
 As a result of this front-loaded approach to designing the acceptance process, the dedication effort
was transparent to the EPC commercial contractor. The construction contractor simply had to execute the
requirements of the contract.
 The culmination of this process is a seamless integration of engineering and quality requirements
detailed in the construction specification, ensuring that the provided SSCs met design specifications. The
application of commercial grade dedication through Methods 1, 2, or 3 articulated in ASME NQA-1
demonstrates a comprehensive approach to providing reasonable assurance that safety SSCs will perform
their intended safety functions.
 2.1. General Application of CGD Methods
2.1.1. CGD Method 1: Special Test(s), Inspection(s), and/or Analyses
 Method 1 is a CGD approach used to verify that a commercial-grade item will perform its intended
safety function(s) through direct testing, inspection, or analysis. It is best suited for simple components
where the safety function can be fully validated without relying on supplier quality systems or
documentation. This method is applied when CCs can be confirmed through measurable, observable
outcomes, and the component can be tested in its final form.
 It is particularly useful when other dedication methods, such as source verification or
commercial-grade surveys, are impractical or unnecessary. Method 1 provides acceptance of CCs, with
results that must be documented and traceable. Because it does not depend on supplier quality assurance
(QA) programs, it is ideal for items sourced from vendors without a nuclear QA program.
 While Method 1 can be used independently, it is often integrated with other methods, such as Method
3 (Source Verification), when not all CCs are testable or when additional assurance is required due to
component complexity or risk.
2.1.2. CGD Method 2: Commercial Grade Survey
 Method 2 is a CGD acceptance approach used to verify that a supplier has a documented QA program
and procedures that adequately control the CCs of an item or service. This method is most appropriate
when the item's safety function cannot be fully validated through testing alone, and when the supplier’s
processes play a critical role in ensuring the item's performance.
 A commercial grade survey involves an on-site evaluation of the supplier’s facilities, procedures, and
controls. It is typically used for complex components or assemblies where CCs are not easily testable or
observable. Method 2 provides assurance through objective, documented evidence that the supplier’s QA
program is robust and adequately governs the attributes necessary to support the credited safety function
of the item or service.
2.1.3. CGD Method 3: Source Verification
 Method 3, Source Verification, is a CGD acceptance method used to verify that a commercial-grade
item or service meets its CCs by directly observing the supplier’s processes during manufacturing,
assembly, or testing. This method is most appropriate when CCs cannot be fully verified after delivery,
and when the supplier’s real-time execution of processes is essential to ensure the item’s safety function.
 Source Verification involves on-site observation at the supplier’s facility, where personnel witness
specific activities such as fabrication, testing, or inspections. It is typically used for complex items where

 2

post-manufacture testing is impractical or insufficient. This method provides assurance through firsthand,
documented evidence that the supplier’s controls are effectively implemented and that the item conforms
to the defined acceptance criteria.
 To be effective, Method 3 must be planned and executed in accordance with a documented checklist
or plan. This includes identifying the item, its CCs, acceptance criteria, and any mandatory hold points.
 Unlike Method 2, which qualifies a supplier for multiple shipments, Method 3 is often applied to
single or infrequent procurements, or when a supplier lacks a formal QA program. Risk is mitigated by
confirming acceptability at the source, especially when the supplier is performing a process for the first
time or when the item’s performance depends on the precise execution of manufacturing steps
2.1.4. CGD Method 4: Acceptable Supplier item or Service Performance
 Record
 Method 4, Acceptable Supplier item or Service Performance Record, was not used on the SPL
project. Several reasons contributed to this decision, primarily that the items for SPL were FOAK and did
not warrant the use of supplier history or performance records.
 Method 4 requires extensive documentation, and the data must align with the intended use conditions
– such as equivalent service conditions, failure modes, and maintenance and testing requirements. While a
supplier may possess such records, they rarely share the performance data necessary to justify the use of
this method. Additionally, Method 4 cannot be used in isolation. Given the FOAK nature of the items,
variations in fabrication configurations, and the difficulty in obtaining the required documentation,
Method 4 was deemed cost-prohibitive and inefficient – especially when other CGD methods were
available.
 3. CONSTRUCTION IMPLEMENTATION STRATEGY
 The SPL acceptance strategy focused on two primary areas: construction and fabrication.
 The construction portion addressed concrete, rebar, and their placement. A hybrid approach was used:
acceptance of the concrete and rebar was performed through CGD, while their placement was carried out
under the oversight of the INL quality program.
 3.1. Concrete
 Concrete identified as safety significant was accepted via CGD Method 1 (see Section 5 for a
discussion of CGD methods) for the SPL. The technical evaluation for the concrete included evaluation of
the safety function, design bases, and code requirements.
 A common mistake when dedicating concrete is assuming that all constituents (e.g., fly ash, Portland
cement, sand, aggregate) require independent testing to verify code-specified properties. However, upon
evaluation, the applicable code specified inspections and tests for the concrete including compressive
strength, density, air content, and slump.
 The CGD technical evaluation concluded that the safety function of the concrete was to provide
shielding, and that the CCs necessary to support that function were compressive strength and density.
After addressing these two characteristics, a review of the code’s quality requirements revealed that the
only remaining gap between code-required testing and the pedigree necessary to accept the concrete as
safety significant was the quality standard under which the testing was performed.
 To address this gap, INL contracted with an ISO 17025-testing laboratory to perform the compressive
strength and density tests necessary to provide reasonable assurance that the concrete would perform its
safety function (see NQA-1 2022 subpart 2.19 and/or NEI 14-05). The testing and inspection criteria
required to establish pedigree were incorporated into the construction specification.

 3

 While the commercial contractor was not responsible for conducting the specified testing, they were
made aware that provisions would need to be made to accommodate the INL-specified tests. In parallel,
INL contracted with an ISO 17025-qualified laboratory to conduct the specified tests throughout the
course of construction. These tests were planned and organized to ensure traceability to the respective
concrete pour.
 While the above noted tests and inspections of critical characteristics were required to achieve
reasonable assurance that the SSC would perform its safety function, quality acceptance was not limited
solely to those features. Commercial quality measures were deployed to ensure code compliance, verify
adequacy of the mix design, and witness mixing of the first batch to verify compliance to the mix design.
 Documentation verifying that the CGD process was successfully completed was stored in the vendor
data system and later integrated with the CGD process documentation via the CGD closure process.
 3.2. Rebar
 Safety significant rebar was accepted via CGD Method 1 for SPL. The technical evaluation for the
rebar included evaluation of the safety function, design bases, and applicable code requirements for the
concrete structure with the embedded rebar reinforcement.
 Dedication of rebar within the nuclear industry has raised concerns due to instances of
non-conformant rebar passing through the dedication process. The primary cause of this issue has been
overreliance on material test reports without independent verification of the specified CCs by the
purchaser.
 To address this gap, INL contracted with an ISO 17025-accredited testing agency to verify the CCs of
dimensions and tensile strength (see NQA-1 2022 subpart 2.19 and/or NEI 14-05). Implementation of the
quality acceptance requirements was achieved by incorporating the testing requirements into the
construction specification.
 Additional quality requirements included marking and segregation of the material at both the
distributor and construction laydown yards. Testing was performed via CGD Method 1 by an
INL-qualified testing agency. Documentation was collected throughout the course of construction and
compiled into a CGD close-out package to demonstrate satisfactory completion of the dedication process.
 3.3. Concrete and Rebar Placement
 Acceptance of concrete and rebar placement was accomplished through implementation of INL’s QA
program. The construction contractor was required to perform work in accordance with the construction
quality program flowed from INL. Verification of program implementation, as well as satisfactory
performance of work performed in accordance with specified requirements, was conducted by INL
construction quality inspectors.
 A unique aspect of this acceptance process was the use of CGD as the primary method of acceptance
for the input constituents (e.g., concrete and rebar), followed by acceptance of their placement through
INL’s NQA-1 compliant quality program. To clarify this relationship, it is critical to understand the point
at which the concrete and rebar were accepted: rebar was accepted prior to placement, and concrete was
accepted via third-party testing performed by an ISO 17025-accredited laboratory.
 As such, the process used to verify the adequacy of placement remains constant, regardless of the
acceptance method employed for the constituent materials.
 4. FABRICATION IMPLEMENTATION STRATEGY
 Acceptance of fabricated items was accomplished via implementation of INL’s CGD program. While
the approach was similar to that used for civil/structural elements (e.g., concrete, rebar), the level of
complexity was significantly higher.

 4

 To ensure the shielding safety function was met, safety significant fabricated items included the raw
materials for the hot cell (e.g., stainless steel, carbon steel, mechanical components, weldments, complex
assemblies, lead filled weldments, refurbished windows), as well as additional appurtenances such as the
structural load-carrying portion of the cask cart. Only items that performed a structural function to
maintain shielding integrity were subject to the CGD process.
 Acceptance of these items employed CGD Methods 1, 2 and 3. Additionally, this novel approach
allowed the commercial contractor to procure safety significant weld filler and fasteners directly from
INL-qualified suppliers.
 4.1. Fabricated Items Dedication Strategy
4.1.1. Fabrication Services
 INL employed a bifurcated approach to acceptance of fabricated items. A two-part technical
evaluation process was employed to accomplish a complex dedication of the fabricated items. Several
dedication plans were developed to identify CCs and acceptance criteria for the various subcomponents
used to fabricate hot cell assemblies (e.g., stainless steel, carbon steel, weld studs, mechanical
components). Each of these plans referenced an overarching CGD plan (CGI-1028), which identified the
processes and controls necessary to accept the subcomponent critical characteristics via CGD Method 2.
 Acceptance requirements documented in the CGD plan were incorporated into the
construction/fabrication specification, ensuring that the QA program requirements, as well as the testing
and inspection requirements, were clear and executable by a competent fabricator with a suitable QA
program. To accept the fabricated items, the contractor was required to have a documented QA program

 Verification of these criteria was accomplished through a commercial grade survey. Examples of

 An alternate strategy was implemented for the SPL cask cart. In this case, the subcomponent CCs,
acceptance criteria, and the processes and controls necessary for acceptance were consolidated into a
single CGD plan.
4.1.2. Carbon Steel
 A bounding CGD plan was developed for all carbon steel shapes designated as safety significant in
the SPL design. This plan encompassed various shapes, sizes, and grades of carbon steel. CCs were
identified for these items, including verification of material strength, as failure due to material overload
was the only credible failure mechanism identified. The corresponding verification method was a
hardness test, based on the direct correlation between tensile strength and hardness as defined in ASTM
A370. Verification of material dimensions was not required as part of the CGD plan, as the fabrication
dedication plan ensured that specified design requirements related to dimension and size would be
verified throughout the fabrication.
 The resultant CGD requirements were embedded in the fabrication specification for implementation
by the fabricator. By incorporating all acceptance-related requirements into the specification, the project
enabled the use of a skilled fabrication contractor with an appropriate QA program to effectively execute
the contract. Additionally, the design documentation was updated to identify each line item in the drawing
bill of materials (BOM) with its associated safety function.
4.1.3. Stainless Steel
 A bounding CGD plan was developed to identify the CCs of all stainless steel components designated
as safety significant throughout the SPL design. Both strength and corrosion were identified as credible
failure mechanisms requiring the identification of CCs. Since the stainless steel elements were analyzed
in the annealed condition, it was determined that verifying material chemistry would provide reasonable
assurance that these elements would perform their safety functions.
 Similar to the approach taken with carbon steel, dimensional attributes were not identified as CCs in
this CGD plan. However, it was recognized that the correct dimensions of the finished fabricated items
would contribute to the safety function. To address this, the fabrication CGD plan identified processes
and controls to be specified in the contract and managed through CGD Method 2.
 The resulting CGD requirements were embedded in the fabrication specification for implementation
by the fabricator. By incorporating all acceptance-related requirements into the specification, the project
enabled the use of a skilled fabrication contractor with an appropriate QA program to effectively execute
the contract. Additionally, the design media was updated to identify each line item in the drawing BOM
with its associated safety function.
4.1.4.      Lead
 The primary safety function of lead in the SPL structure was to provide adequate shielding.
Accordingly, the identified credible failure mechanism was the presence of voids or inadequate density.
The corresponding CC was density. Lead was used in two primary applications within the design:
1. installation of lead sheets around the shield windows, and
2. pouring molten lead into fabricated stainless steel enclosures.

 6

 Each application employed a different dedication method. The lead sheet was installed by an NQA-1
qualified supplier. INL performed the technical evaluation to identify the CCs and incorporated them into
the contract. The supplier, Hot Cell Services, then incorporated those CCs into their dedication program
and applied a CGD acceptance plan. The Technical Evaluation section noted that the design authority had
provided the CCs for the lead sheet.
 The molten lead was accepted using CGD Method 3. Acceptance criteria were established in the
CGD plan and incorporated into the contract. For each fabricated assembly, both empty and full weights
were calculated. The acceptance method involved weighing the assemblies before and after the lead pour
to verify that the required density was achieved. Additional process controls were implemented through
engineering review and approval of the lead pour procedure, along with oversight during the pour to
ensure that process parameters remained within specified limits throughout the operation.
4.1.5. Weld Filler
 Due to the complex nature of dedicating weld filler material, the strategy for acceptance was to use
the construction subcontractor as the INL purchasing agent to procure the weld filler material from an
approved supplier. The construction specification required that the material be purchased from one of
three listed suppliers: Weldstar Company, Dubose National Energy Services, or Energy and Process
Corporation. INL reviewed and approved the purchase order(s) prior to placement to ensure the following
requirement was properly communicated:
• Weld filler material shall be procured from a supplier with a Quality Assurance Program that
 complies with ASME Section III.
 Additionally, INL required that the Certificate of Conformance and Certified Material Test Reports
for the weld filler material be submitted and reviewed for acceptance.
4.1.6.      Fasteners
 The acceptance strategy for fasteners mirrored the approach used for weld filler materials. The
subcontractor was required to procure fasteners from one of the following approved suppliers: Energy and
Process Corporation, Dubose National Energy Services, or Curtiss-Wright Nuclear Division (Nova
Machine). Purchase orders were reviewed and approved by INL prior to issuance to ensure the following
requirement was properly flowed down to the supplier:
• The supplier shall implement a quality system in accordance with ASME-NQA-1 2008, 1a 2009
 Addenda.
 INL also required that the Certificate of Conformance and Certified Material Test Reports for the
fasteners be submitted and reviewed for acceptance.
 5. CONSTRUCTION SPECIFICATION
 The required documentation and testing requirements were included in the construction specification,
streamlining it for a commercial construction contractor to fulfill the CGD documentation requirements.
The submittals called out in the specification provided the necessary documentation for the CGD
package. The goal was to make the performance of CGD transparent to the contractor. Implementation of
CGD would simply be manifest as contract requirements clearly specified to minimize the need for RFI’s
or clarifications.

 6. EPC COST SAVINGS FROM CGD
 Using CGD and avoiding the use of an NQA-1 contractor for EPC may result in cost savings due to:
1. Reduced contractor overhead – NQA-1 contractors typically command higher fees than commercial
 suppliers.
2. Improved engineering efficiency – Integrating CGD early in the engineering process establishes
 clear acceptance criteria in design outputs, including construction and fabrication specifications, and
 eliminates redundant reviews by the EPC contractor.
3. Elimination of duplicative efforts – By retaining responsibility for quality requirements definition,
 the Design Authority avoids lengthy EPC contractor submissions and resubmissions for CGD plan
 approvals.
4. Lower material costs – Application of CGD increases the number of available suppliers with the
 associated commercial pricing.
 A DOE-funded study initiated in 1978 and continued through the 1980s collected cost data from
several operating nuclear power plants, as well as other energy sources, built during or before that period.
This data, known as the Energy Economic Database (EEDB), was developed to provide representative
and consistent cost information for electricity generation in the United States (United Engineers &
Constructors, 1985).
 The EEDB remains the only publicly available source that can be used to quantify the nuclear cost
multiplier. Delene and Hudson (1993) analyzed this data to calculate nuclear and corresponding
non-nuclear costs for major materials and their associated installation labor rates. These unit costs are
presented in Table 1. Stewart (2022) later used this data to derive nuclear cost escalation factors,
expressed as the ratio of nuclear to non-nuclear costs, as shown in Figure 1.
 The data reveals significant cost escalations for nuclear-related commodities and labor. The most
pronounced increase is in structural steel labor, where costs are more than four times higher, likely due to
the elevated cost of nuclear welding labor. For concrete, the escalation is approximately 1.5x for materials
and 1.3x for labor, which remains substantial.

on NQA-1 contractors and suppliers. Cost data from INL’s capital projects is used to support these
comparisons.

6.1.1. Reinforced Concrete
 In traditional, large light water reactors, civil structures amount to roughly half of the total overnight
capital cost. Concrete is the most commonly used material in these structures and as a result, reducing
concrete costs can impact the overall budget.
 To estimate the savings in concrete costs from the use of CGD, cost data from SPL is used. A CGD
program was implemented during SPL’s construction, with commercial contractors and suppliers
handling both construction and material procurement.
 The savings from CGD were calculated by comparing the nuclear cost escalation factor associated
with CGD to that of using an NQA-1 supplier. The reduction in this escalation factor quantified the cost
savings attributable to CGD. Since NQA-1 suppliers were not used for SPL, escalation factors from
Delene and Hudson (1993), based on EEDB data, were used for comparison. SPL included several
reinforced concrete walls classified as safety significant. Because CGD was employed, the same
commercial supplier and constructor was used for both safety significant and non-safety significant walls.
As a result, there was no nuclear cost escalation in material or labor costs at SPL. Instead, the cost
escalation associated with CGD stemmed from the owner's expenses related to CGD plan development, as
well as non-destructive and destructive testing of concrete and rebar.

 These costs were quantified by reviewing invoices for material testing and estimating the labor hours
required for CGDS plan creation. Figure 1 presents these costs. Inspection services from Atlas were
evaluated using invoices, and CGD plan development was estimated at 150 labor hours each for concrete
and rebar, using a procurement engineering labor rate of $300/hr.
 The total CGD-related cost, including plan creation, oversight, and specified testing, amounted to
$264,238. The total construction cost of the safety significant walls was $1,463,204. Therefore, the CGD
nuclear cost escalation can be calculated as the ratio of CGD costs to the total cost:

 7. ACCEPTANCE OF SAFETY ITEMS FROM COMMERCIAL
 SUPPLIERS VIA APPLICATION OF NQA-1 PART I
 Acceptance of safety class / safety significant / safety related items from commercial suppliers via
Part I of NQA-1 is a novel approach in today’s nuclear world but has substantial guidance to underpin the
practice. That guidance exists in EPRI 5652 Rev. 1 Appendix F and in the EFCOG Report E-SG-QA-
PEQ-2023-03 “Procurement of Items and Services from Non-Nuclear Suppliers Using Part I of NQA-1”
where means other than use of CGD are identified to procure safety items and services. INL’s program
recognizes this and allowed its application for the procurement of through wall manipulators for SPL.
 A supplier on INL’s qualified supplier list, with a program found to be compliant to applicable NQA-
1 requirements, elected to procure from a commercial supplier via application of Part I of NQA-1
(without CGD). In order to provide reasonable assurance that the provided SSC would perform its safety
function, INL required that the supplier create a quality acceptance plan detailing the following criteria:
 • Specify the items’ safety function as provided by the design authority (INL)
 • Identify sub-components of the supplied SSC (through wall manipulator) credited with
 performing a safety function
 • Identify the characteristics of those sub-components critical to its ability to perform its safety
 function
 • Detail the methods of acceptance specified in NQA-1 Requirement 7 Para 502 that would be
 employed to accept the noted characteristics critical to provide assurance that the sub-component
 would perform its safety function.
 Elements of that quality plan are noted below:
 • Supplier Evaluation – The NQA-1 contractor assesses the commercial supplier's quality
 program and process controls, including material handling, procurement, calibration/maintenance,
 and receipt inspection procedures.
 • Certificate of Conformance – Per NQA-1 Requirement 7, Section 503, a Certificate of
 Conformance is required for all safety-significant items, confirming compliance with
 specifications.
 • Onsite Verification – The NQA-1 supplier conducts onsite evaluations to verify proper
 implementation of seismic analysis and procurement controls for lead and stainless steel
 materials.
 • Material Testing – For stainless steel fasteners, the NQA-1 supplier sources from qualified
 suppliers, performs material testing, and provides the resulting data directly to INL for additional
 verification.
 Throughout the process, the NQA-1 contractor may employ supplemental acceptance methods to
address any identified gaps and ensure full compliance.
 For further guidance, refer to EFCOG Report E-SG-QA-PEQ-2023-03, “Procurement of Items and
Services from Non-Nuclear Suppliers Using Part I of NQA-1.”
 8. SUMMARY
 8.1. Innovative CGD Strategy for SPL Construction
 INL employed an innovative approach for the Sample Preparation Laboratory (SPL), a Hazard
Category 3 facility: using Commercial Grade Dedication (CGD) as the primary procurement and

 14

acceptance strategy. Rather than contracting with an NQA-1 qualified EPC firm, INL leveraged its own
NQA-1 program to oversee a commercial EPC contractor using CGD methods.
 This first-of-a-kind approach successfully delivered the project on schedule and within budget while
maintaining nuclear quality assurance compliance. Three key factors enabled this success:
• A mature, established CGD program
• Completed NQA-1 design documentation
• Clear dedication requirements in construction specifications.
 8.2. Scope and Implementation
 CGD methods were applied to all construction and fabrication activities, including concrete, rebar,
stainless steel, carbon steel, lead shielding, weld filler, and fasteners. The process identified critical
characteristics through technical evaluation, then incorporated them into construction specifications and
verified them via testing, source verification, or commercial grade surveys.
 8.3. Results
 Quantitative analysis showed that CGD reduced the nuclear cost escalation factor by approximately
two-thirds compared to traditional NQA-1 procurement. Early integration of CGD planning into design
and procurement enabled efficient execution while ensuring safety function assurance for all
safety-significant systems, structures, and components.

 B-1. Procurement Engineering Guidance

 B-1.1 Guidance on Identification of Safety Significant and
 Safety-Class Items in Design Output Documents
 Safety-basis documents (i.e., safety analysis reports, DSAs, preliminary DSAs) identify the
system-safety functions and, in some cases, may address item-safety functions and requirements. These
documents are typically generated by nuclear safety engineers in parallel with nuclear facility or system
design. When additional clarity is needed regarding item-specific safety functions, design-output
documents (e.g., drawings, P&IDs, technical specifications) are the appropriate vehicle to convey those
safety designations and associated requirements. Identification of the critical characteristics in phase with
design and documenting them in applicable design output documents is key to an efficient, effective CGD
process.
 The designation of safety-significant, safety-class, and safety-related items in design-output
documents is essential for assigning appropriate quality requirements during procurement, acceptance,
and operations. These benefits are further discussed in the procurement and operations sections of this
document. This designation is typically performed by the CSE, with concurrence from the nuclear safety
engineer. It can be implemented by adding a flag note and individually flagging items in the BOM or on
the drawings. Alternatively, safety classification can be documented through other design-output
documents, such as a System Design Description (SDD) or system specifications. Clearly identifying
safety items in design documents enables efficient procurement without imposing excessive requirements,
regardless of the selected procurement-acceptance strategy (e.g., CGD or qualified supplier). See the
example in Appendix A.
 B-1.2 Guidance on Identification of Design Characteristics
 Design-verification activities, such as structural and seismic analyses, are required to ensure that
items credited with performing a safety function will operate as intended in response to normal and
design-basis events. When developing procurement acceptance requirements, MFC Procurement
Engineering personnel review the associated design-verification output documents to identify the design
characteristics relied upon to ensure that the procured items will fulfill their safety functions.
 In some cases, such as for items credited with structural or seismic safety, this effort typically
requires collaboration among the system engineer (SE), structural analyst, nuclear safety engineer, and
procurement engineer (PE).
 In an EPC project, documenting the salient design characteristics within the safety analysis as it is
being developed simplifies the process of selecting critical characteristics. This, in turn, supports the
definition of acceptance criteria during the procurement and construction phases of the project.

 24

 Example 1: During the performance of a shielding calculation, the analyst
 may determine that a specific material density and thickness are required to
 provide adequate shielding. Since these attributes are relied upon for
 shielding safety, the Procurement Engineer (PE) must ensure that
 acceptance planning includes verification of these attributes and requires that
 documentary evidence be provided and retained by the purchaser. Therefore,
 documenting the required thickness and density values in the design-output
 documents will assist the PE in developing an acceptance plan that
 appropriately bounds the features necessary to ensure nuclear safety.
 Example 2: During structural analysis of a reactor support structure, the
 responsible engineer may determine that, due to sustained high
 pressure-vessel temperatures, a low-carbon alloy, such as ASTM A240
 (Grade 304L), is required to mitigate stress corrosion cracking over the life of
 the structure. As a result, the PE would require documentary evidence
 confirming adherence to this critical design attribute in the delivered
 fabrication record package. This evidence can then be used to demonstrate
 compliance of the fabricated item to both the purchaser and the facility owner.
 B-1.3 Commercial-Grade Dedication Guidance
 Effective implementation of the CGD methodology in the EPC process is essential, given the current
state of the nuclear supply chain. Sound CGD processes are built on complete designs, beginning with a
clear understanding of the item’s safety function. The design authority should incorporate CGD
methodology into the design process with the procurement process in mind. Proper integration of these
processes ensures that procurement and acceptance activities are focused on the item’s safety function and
establishes a clear line of sight from the defined acceptance plan to the safety-basis requirements.
 Whether procuring from an NQA-1-qualified supplier or a commercial supplier, the design authority
is responsible for performing a technical evaluation to determine the item’s CCs. Performing this
evaluation in parallel with the design process improves both the efficiency and accuracy of these
determinations. In the procurement phase, clearly defined acceptance criteria, produced as a result of an
effective CGD process, enhance supplier bid accuracy and lead-time predictability, as suppliers can plan
definitively without the delays associated with CGD plan approvals.
 It is important to note that NQA-1 suppliers and manufacturers may use methods other than CGD to
procure safety-class and safety-significant items. This alternative methodology is detailed in EPRI 5652,
Rev. 1, Appendix F, and is permitted when performing supplier oversight.
 Additional CGD guidance is noted below:
• DOE-HDBK-1230-2019, Commercial Grade Dedication Application Handbook
• EPRI 5652, Rev. 1, Plant Engineering: Guideline for the Acceptance of Commercial Grade Items in
 Nuclear Safety-Related Applications: Revision 1 to EPRI NP-5652 and TR-102260
 B-1.4 Procurement Specification-Development Guidance
 Through the design process, Engineering is required to specify “Applicable design bases and other
requirements necessary to assure adequate quality” in procurement documents. Codes and standards
driving the design contain technical and quality requirements that, when invoked, are required to be
adhered to by INL.
 Example 1: ASME B31.3 for piping has documentation requirements
 dependent on the level of service of the piping system. For piping systems

 25

 where pressure is below a specified psi, the purchaser is required to verify
 markings. No further verification or documentation is required.
 Example 2: ASME Section VIII requires original material documentation for
 primary pressure-boundary materials coupled with finished-assembly
 hydrostatic and leak testing. An inspection and certification by an authorized
 inspector meet the final requirements for the vessel to be used in its end
 application.
 The two examples above identify design-bases requirements as expressed in their respective codes. A
purchaser or end user may require additional quality requirements dependent on the end item’s use.
 In Example 1, the purchaser may choose to perform additional material verification, such as chemical
testing (e.g., X-ray fluorescence, optical emission spectroscopy) or strength verification (e.g., tensile
testing, hardness testing), beyond the code requirements.
 In addition to supplementary tests and inspections, the purchaser may also require other quality
measures, including:
1. An on-site survey or audit of the supplier’s quality processes and controls
2. Submission of key procedures for review prior to use
3. Submission of additional test and inspection reports.
 These “other requirements necessary to assure adequate quality” are best selected in coordination
with the Procurement Engineering organization. Doing so increases the likelihood of a successful
procurement that meets specification requirements, on time and within estimated cost.
 Given the limited nature of the nuclear supply chain, build-to-print procurements issued to NQA–
1-qualified suppliers are required to address CGD. The purchaser should anticipate that the supplier will
need to apply CGD to complete the safety-related scope and should include appropriate provisions in the
procurement specification.
 The supplier may address CGD in one of two ways:
1. Design Authority Performs the CGD Technical Evaluation: In this approach, the design authority
 performs the CGD technical evaluation and identifies the CCs for acceptance. These CCs are included
 in the build-to-print procurement specification. The supplier then implements the Acceptance
 Plan portion of their CGD program, relying on the purchaser’s technical evaluation and CC
 identification.
2. Supplier Performs the CGD Technical Evaluation: Alternatively, the design authority may provide the
 supplier with the necessary design information to perform the CGD technical evaluation and require
 the supplier to submit the resulting CGD plans for purchaser approval. Design documentation
 required for an adequate technical evaluation includes design media such as drawings, piping and
 instrumentation diagrams (P&IDs), equipment specifications, design-verification output documents,
 and safety-basis documentation.
 The preferred method is the first. The design authority and design organization possess inherent
familiarity with the design data, enabling them to complete the CGD technical evaluation efficiently and
in alignment with expectations for rigor in the acceptance process.
 Conversely, if the supplier is tasked with performing the CGD technical evaluation, they must first
review and understand the design-output documents, introducing a learning curve. This evaluation would
then require review and approval by the design authority, increasing procurement lead time. Experience
shows that this process often requires multiple iterations before final approval, potentially delaying
procurement.

 26

 For build-to-print orders, the design must be complete prior to procurement. However, procurement
may proceed before design verification is finalized, provided that adequate controls are in place to
manage unverified portions of the design.
 B-1.5 General Design-Build Procurement Guidance
 Generation of a design-build specification shall be performed in accordance with the design
authority’s documented program. As such, it is important that the design-build specification adequately
incorporate the following.
 Safety-Basis Requirements: Safety-basis requirements, including seismic, environmental, and other
design-verification requirements, must be incorporated with design and design-verification documents
defined to meet project expectations and requirements.
 Technical Requirements: In addition to safety-basis requirements, technical requirements—e.g.,
design requirements, codes, standards, facility-interface requirements, and drawings—must be
incorporated into the procurement specification in a manner that clearly communicates the purchaser’s
technical requirements.
 Quality Requirements: Quality requirements must be incorporated in the procurement specification.
For items performing a safety function, it is expected that the design-build procurements will be issued to
suppliers with NQA-1 programs. Therefore, procurement documents must require NQA-1a 2009, and the
responsible engineer is must provide an applicable applicability matrix (Form 414.12F, “ASME NQA-1
2008 With NQA-1A-2009 Addenda Applicability Matrix”) to align with the scope of the item or service
being procured.
 Assuring adequate quality requires the purchaser to identify submittals and deliverables that ensure
supplier alignment with specification and design requirements as the procurement progresses. Care should
be taken to ensure submittals and deliverables required by design-basis requirements are incorporated. In
addition to these, the purchaser should determine which submittals and deliverables are required for final
acceptance, startup, and turnover. High-risk activities (e.g., CGD, special processes) should have
associated identified submittals and/or deliverables.
 An additional means of assuring adequate confidence that the delivered item or service will perform
its safety function is imposition of witness and hold points through the course of the procurement.
Activities associated with critical decision points and high-risk activities (e.g., kickoff meetings, design
reviews, first op weld, functional testing, factory-acceptance testing) should be considered witness and
hold points and incorporated into procurement documents.
