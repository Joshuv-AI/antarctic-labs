1 INTRODUCTION

2 Climate Model Selection with Uncertainty Preservation

2.1 Context and Motivation

Robust climate-impact assessments depend on the breadth and quality of projections supplied by general cir-

culation models (GCMs). Ensemble sizes have grown from about two dozen models in CMIP3 (Meehl et al.,

2007) to more than sixty in CMIP5 (Emori et al., 2016), each spawning multiple realizations. While this di-

versity enriches the envelope of plausible futures, it simultaneously renders exhaustive analysis computationally

prohibitive and analytically unwieldy (Lutz et al., 2016; Pawar et al., 2024b). Down-selecting to a concise set

of representative GCM runs (RGCM-runs) is therefore essential, especially for resource-intensive downstream

applications such as hydrology, agriculture, and energy systems.

2.2 State of the Art and Limitations

Three main strategies dominate RGCM-run selection. Past-performance screening retains models that best

reproduce historical climatology, yet can inadvertently truncate the range of future outcomes (Pierce et al., 2009;

Biemans et al., 2013). Envelope-based methods emphasise coverage of extremes in projected temperature

and precipitation, but may admit runs that perform poorly in the present climate (Seager et al., 2007; Immerzeel

et al., 2013). Hybrid schemes, for example, the three-stage workflow of Lutz et al. (2016) and its derivatives

(Kaini et al., 2020; Shrestha and Pradhanang, 2022), seek a balance, yet still face challenges capturing both

mean changes and extremes while ensuring historical fidelity. Across approaches, ad-hoc thresholds, subjective

weighting, and susceptibility to model interdependence remain open issues (Parding et al., 2020; Ruane and

McDermid, 2017; Tenfie et al., 2022).

2.3 Objectives and Contribution of This Study

We introduce a rigorously ordered, data-driven three-stage workflow that first enforces historical skill via multi-

objective distance metrics, then maximizes spread in projected mean changes, and finally guarantees coverage

of extreme indices. Validation across climatically distinct regions demonstrates that the reordered sequence

reduces Kolmogorov–Smirnov distances between RGCM and full-ensemble distributions by up to 30% and

halves mean-squared error in historical reconstructions relative to conventional orderings. By systematically

balancing fidelity and uncertainty, the proposed framework provides a transparent, reproducible foundation for

climate-impact studies that must operate under tight computational budgets while maintaining scientific rigor.

 2.4 Focus area and datasets

 To develop and validate the effectiveness of our proposed workflow for the selection of RGCM runs, we first focus

 our attention on Texas, located in the south-central region of the United States. Two other regions, (i) Bihar,

 located in the eastern region of India, and (ii) New York State, located in the northeastern United States, with

 totally different climatic conditions than those of Texas, are later used to test the capability of our proposed

 workflow further to preserve uncertainty. As shown in Fig. 1, Texas is geographically diverse, with certain

 areas classified as part of the South United States, while others are designated as part of the Southwest United

 States. The vast expanse of the state includes a wide range of climate and geographical features, including

 coastal areas, plains, deserts, and mountainous regions. These elements are important to develop and validate

 our RGCM-run selection workflow, ensuring its robustness and adaptability in various areas.

 2.5 Datasets

 In this study, three data sources are used, including (i) the output fields of the GCMs in CMIP5, (ii) the

 extreme indices derived from CMIP5, and (iii) the reference (ground-truth) datasets. From these datasets, a

 set of comparison variables is derived to develop and evaluate our workflow. The following sections explain the

 data sources and the derived variables.

2.5.1 CMIP5 output fields

In both long-term and short-term experiments conducted under CMIP5, four representative concentration path-

ways (RCPs) are routinely employed. These pathways, RCP8.5, RCP6.0, RCP4.5, and RCP2.6, correspond to

distinct greenhouse-gas concentration trajectories (e.g. CO2 , CH4 , N2 O) and their associated radiative-forcing

targets out to 2100 (Mahjour et al., 2024; Emori et al., 2016). Ideally, impact studies would sample all four path-

ways; in practice, trade-offs between computational cost and scientific breadth necessitate a narrower focus (Lutz

et al., 2016). To span a wide radiative-forcing range while staying consistent with observed emissions trends,

we exclude RCP2.6—whose requisite global mitigation of roughly 3 % yr−1 remains improbable (Van Vuuren

et al., 2011; Kaini et al., 2020; Mora et al., 2013; Sanford et al., 2014)—and retain two scenarios:

 • RCP8.5 – a high-baseline emissions pathway, aligned with the ∼3 % annual growth in CO2 observed

        during 2005–2012 (Peters et al., 2013; Mora et al., 2013);

 • RCP4.5 – a medium-stabilisation pathway whose ∼1.5 % annual increase in CO2 over the same period

 better matches recent trends than RCP6.0’s ∼1 % (Sanford et al., 2014).

 We analyse CMIP5 simulations for these two RCPs over two 30-year windows (1981–2010 and 2071–2100).

All model outputs are re-gridded to 2.5◦ ×2.5◦ resolution via the KNMI Climate Explorer (Royal Netherlands

Meteorological Institute (KNMI), 2023). The ensemble comprises 105 runs under RCP4.5 and 77 runs under

RCP8.5, each uniquely identified by its “rip” code (r = realisation, i = initialisation, p = physics version).

From every run we extract monthly mean air temperature (T , ◦ C) and precipitation (P , mm day−1 ) fields,

which constitute the core variables for subsequent uncertainty analyses and RGCM selection.

2.5.2 CMIP5-derived extreme indices

We use the extreme climate indices extracted from the archive generated by the Expert Team on Climate

Change Detection and Indices (ETCCDI) (Levesque, 2021; Pielke Sr and Wilby, 2012; Lutz et al., 2016). The

ETCCDI indices are a set of standardized climate indices developed to monitor changes in climate extremes.

They primarily focus on temperature and precipitation and are widely used to assess the impacts of climate

change on extreme weather events. These indices are made accessible through the Canadian Centre for Climate

Modeling and Analysis. The data obtained from this archive is collected and downloaded using the KNMI

Climate Explorer. Table 1 reports a list of indices selected for analysis in our study.

projected future conditions. Our proposed approach involves three steps for reducing the number of GCM-runs:

2.6.1 Initial selection of GCM runs

The initial step in selecting GCM runs involves a process known as history matching, where we evaluate how

effectively GCM runs replicate reference climate characteristics during a specific historical period.

 To implement history matching, we first use the pair of (∆T , ∆P ) calculated for each GCM-run for the two

distinct periods of 1981–2010 and 2071–2100; see Section 2.5. Subsequently, we calculate the 50th percentile

values for ∆T and ∆P across all GCM-runs. These twopercentiles act as representations of the median changes

in mean air temperature and precipitation among various GCM runs. Using these twopercentile values, we

partition the data on a scatter plot, where the x-axis represents ∆T and the y-axis represents ∆P , into four

distinct quadrants. These quadrants serve the purpose of categorizing GCM runs based on their performance.

In this categorization scheme, GCM-runs located in the top left quadrant are denoted as “warm-dry” GCM-

runs, while those in the top right quadrant are classified as “warm-wet” GCM-runs. GCM-runs situated in

the bottom left quadrant receive the label of “cold-dry” GCM-runs, and those positioned in the bottom right

quadrant are identified as “cold-wet” GCM-runs. It is essential to understand that the terms “cold” and “dry”

used here do not necessarily imply lower temperatures or reduced precipitation when compared to the reference

period. Instead, they signify the relative position of these scenarios among the ensemble of climatic models.

Following this categorization, we proceed to assess the performance of the GCM runs in each quadrant to

replicate reference mean air temperature and precipitation data during 25 years, from 1981 to 2005. In this

step, following the work by Lutz et al. (2016), five GCM runs are selected for each quadrant, resulting in 20

RGCM runs for each RCP. This number has been determined to effectively capture the full range of uncertainty

associated with climate projections, while also ensuring that computational requirements remain manageable.

 To carry out this evaluation, we calculate the T-distance and P-distance, as described in Section 2.5.1, to

implement a multi-objective distance-based scoring approach. This approach first normalizes the two distances

for T and P across the GCMs in each quadrant to bring them into the same range [0- 1] using the min-max

scaling, followed by calculating the arithmetic average of the distances as a closeness score to the reference data.

Ultimately, we select five GCM runs in each quadrant with the highest closeness score (i.e. with the smallest

distance from the reference).

 To test the selection process of the GCM-runs based on their past performance, we compare the monthly

average of mean air temperature, T̄ , and precipitation, P̄ , data obtained for each GCM-run with reference

datasets (see Section 2.5.1). For this comparison, we use the mean squared error (MSE) (Mahjour et al., 2024)

2.6.3 Final selection of GCM runs

The final stage targets extremes. We compute ETCCDI indices, the warm-spell duration (WSDI), cold-spell

duration (CSDI), consecutive dry days (CDD), and very-wet-day precipitation (R95pTOT), for both 1981–2010

and 2071–2100. After min–max normalising index changes, we average the two quadrant-relevant indices (see

Table 2) and retain the run with the highest average, producing one RGCM-run per quadrant (four per RCP).

3 ESM Data Downscaling

 Figure 8: Global scale HR image yields a very LR output when limited to a country scale.

 Earth System Models (ESMs) are advanced computational frameworks designed to simulate the coupled

dynamics of the atmosphere, ocean, land, ice, and biosphere, offering comprehensive insights into climate and

environmental processes, Heavens et al. (2013); Heinze et al. (2019); Collins et al. (2006). By solving large sys-

tems of partial differential equations for fluid motion, radiation, chemistry, and biogeochemical cycles on a global

three-dimensional grid, ESMs enable the study of multidimensional climate change phenomena. Eyring et al.

(2016) and Vandal et al. (2017) show that, due to computational constraints, these models typically operate

at coarse horizontal resolutions (50–200 km) with limited vertical layers, requiring sub-grid processes—such as

convective storms, urban heat islands, or localized surface fluxes—to be parameterized that explicitly modeled.

This resolution gap poses a significant challenge for stakeholders and local agencies, who require high-fidelity

projections at sub-kilometer scales for effective decision-making in areas such as disaster planning and resource

management. Coarse ESM outputs, when interpolated to finer scales, often blur important gradients in temper-

ature, precipitation, and other variables, diminishing the realism of local extremes and increasing uncertainty

for end users. Most regional agencies lack the computational resources and expertise to run high-resolution or

convection-permitting models, further widening the gap between global ESM predictions and actionable local

information. Yang et al. (2019) and Dong et al. (2016) describe the super-resolution (SR) methods widely

used in computer vision to enhance image resolution by transforming low-resolution (LR) images into high-

resolution (HR) counterparts as a promising solution for ESM data downscaling. Ledig et al. (2017) and Tai

et al. (2017) show that the traditional SR techniques struggle to recover physically consistent high-frequency

details, leading to the loss of critical information about local extremes. This shortcoming is particularly acute

in climate modeling, where the preservation of physical relationships and fine-scale variability is essential. To

address these limitations, recent research has explored physically inspired modeling approaches and implicit

Final Report for DE-SC0023044 18

neural representations (INR) that can more effectively capture both global context and high-frequency details.

By integrating domain knowledge and leveraging continuous function approximations, these methods aim to

generate high-fidelity, physically consistent downscaled ESM outputs. In this work, we build on these ad-

vances by proposing a hybrid framework that combines physics-inspired neural architectures with INR-based

super-resolution, enabling robust and scalable downscaling of ESM data for local climate applications.

3.1 Related Work

Super-resolution (SR) is a central challenge in computer vision, aiming to reconstruct high-resolution (HR)

images from low-resolution (LR) inputs Rahaman et al. (2019). SR has broad applications, including medical

imaging Bashir et al. (2021), wildlife surveillance Kuzmanić et al. (2007), security Grosche et al. (2021), digital

preservation, and consumer electronics Maral (2022). Despite advances in imaging hardware, SR remains

difficult due to the ill-posed nature of inferring lost high-frequency details from LR data Ledig et al. (2017).

Effective SR models must balance fine-detail preservation with computational efficiency, especially in real-time

scenarios Lim et al. (2017).

 Early SR approaches were dominated by convolutional neural networks (CNNs), such as SRCNN Dong et al.

(2016), which learned mappings from LR to HR images by extracting hierarchical features. Deeper networks

like VDSR Kim et al. (2016a), EDSR Kim et al. (2016b), and RDN Zhang et al. (2018) improved performance

by leveraging residual and dense connections. However, CNNs are affected by spectral bias, excelling at low-

frequency reconstruction but struggling with high-frequency textures and edges Zhang et al. (2019). Generative

adversarial networks (GANs), such as SRGAN, addressed perceptual quality but could introduce artifacts Shidqi

et al. (2023). Recent work has also adapted CNN-based models for ESM data, such as FSRCNN-ESM, which

offers improved downscaling of climate variables with reduced computational cost Maral (2022); Bashir et al.

(2021); Vandal et al. (2017); Eyring et al. (2016); Dong et al. (2016).

 Transformer-based architectures, particularly the Vision Transformer (ViT) Dosovitskiy et al. (2020); Tou-

vron et al. (2021), have become popular for SR due to their ability to model long-range dependencies. However,

ViTs can struggle to reconstruct high-frequency image details Bai et al. (2022). To address these limitations, re-

cent models have integrated implicit neural representations (INRs), such as Sinusoidal Representation Networks

(SIREN) Sitzmann et al. (2020), which use periodic activation functions to capture fine details better. Hybrid

models like ViSIR Zeraatkar et al. (2025a) and ViFOR Zeraatkar et al. (2025b) combine ViT’s global context

modeling with SIREN or Fourier-based INRs to overcome spectral bias and improve SR quality in ESM data.

These architectures replace standard feed-forward layers with Fourier or sinusoidal activations, enabling the

network to independently learn low- and high-frequency components, which is critical for accurate downscaling

of environmental variables.

Final Report for DE-SC0023044 19

 In the context of Earth System Models (ESMs), Vandal et al. (2017); Heinze et al. (2019) and Eyring et al.

(2016) have shown that the field has evolved from classical to AI-inspired systems, ans that the SR techniques are

vital for enhancing the spatial resolution of climate data, supporting more detailed and actionable environmental

analysis. Maral (2022) and Zeraatkar et al. (2025b) leverage multiple LR observations to synthesize a single

HR output model. These multi-image SR (MISR) approaches benefit from diverse perspectives and temporal

information. Aiyetigbo et al. (2025) is the implicit neural representation method for both image and video SR,

providing efficient and temporally stable reconstruction without the need for explicit motion estimation.

3.3 Dataset

E3SM Project (2018) Energy Exascale Earth System Model (E3SM) simulation data are used to evaluate

our approaches. E3SM is an open-access, state-of-the-art, fully coupled climate model that includes critical

biogeochemical and cryospheric processes. The original E3SM data is provided on a non-orthogonal cubed-

sphere grid. For our experiments, this data is first interpolated onto a regular longitude-latitude grid at a

fine spatial resolution of 0.25◦ × 0.25◦ (referred to as E3SM-FR) using bilinear interpolation. Passarella et al.

(2022) further downsamples the fine-resolution grid to a 1◦ × 1◦ grid using bicubic interpolation to generate the

corresponding coarse-resolution (LR) data, effectively removing fine spatial details. Thus, the high-resolution

(HR) images have dimensions of 720 × 1440 pixels, while the LR images have dimensions of 180 × 360 pixels,

corresponding to a 4× upsampling factor. Each pixel in these images corresponds to a grid point. We construct

3.4 Methodology

We propose two novel methods for the ESM data compression, ViSIR and ViFOR.

 The ViSIR is a new hybrid model and an extension of the Vision Transformer (ViT) and Sinusoidal Rep-

resentation Network (SIREN). Although ViT was a good tool that could capture long-range relationships in

imagesTouvron et al. (2021); Dosovitskiy et al. (2020), it may not be enough to capture high-frequency compo-

nents essential to get high-quality Super ResolutionBai et al. (2022). The SIRENs have demonstrated that they

can capture high-frequency details in imagesSitzmann et al. (2020), and here we present ViSIR. This mixed

network replaces the final fully connected layer of ViT with SIREN to address the spectral bias issue in the SR

tasks of the Earth System Model images. The ViSIR pipeline flow is illustrated in Figure 10. The ViT processes

the input images in the proposed pipeline to capture global dependencies and essential contextual information

for super-resolution. Given a low-resolution image ILR , the patch embedding operation can be described as:

Ei = Wp · patch(ILR )i + bp , i = 1, . . . , N , where Wp and bp are the patch embedding weights and bias, and N

is the number of patches. These embeddings are then processed through multiple transformer layers, yielding

contextualized features: T = ViT(ILR ). The SIREN then uses this information to address the spectral bias in

the input image, resulting in higher-resolution output: f (x) = sin (ω0 W x + b). The ω0 is a hyperparameter

controlling the frequency of the sinusoidal function. In ViSIR, the final reconstruction is performed by feeding

the transformer’s output into one or more SIREN layers, R = f (T ) = sin (ω0 Wr T + br ), where Wr and br are

the weights and bias of the SIREN module. Next, ViSIR integrates ViT’s global context modeling capabilities

with SIREN’s high-frequency representation strength. Algorithm 1 presents a concise pseudo-code overview

of the proposed ViSIR methodology, detailing the steps from patch extraction and token embedding through

transformer-based processing to the final reconstruction using a SIREN layer.

FOREN. ViFOR replaces the conventional multi-layer perceptron (MLP) layer in ViT with the FOREN structure

to address the spectral bias and to enhance the model’s ability to learn a specific range of frequencies. ViFOR

threads address low-frequency and high-frequency components separately, thus improving the quality of the

reconstructed HR images in SR tasks. FOREN uses a Fourier transformer filter as its activation function,

resulting in a technique capable of focusing on the desired range of image frequencies and addressing the spectral

bias issue. Incorporating the low-pass or high-pass Fourier filter activation functions in the MLP structure,

FOREN provides a model to focus on different frequency components independently at both the encoder and

the final output layers of the ViT. Accordingly, we train two ViFOR networks: one dedicated to capturing low-

frequency components and the other to capturing high-frequency ones. The outputs of these two networks are

then combined to produce the final HR image. This innovative design preserves local details (captured through

high-frequency components) and global context (captured through low-frequency components), resulting in

high-quality reconstructed HR output. Figure 11(bottom) illustrates the ViFOR pipeline. ViFOR segments the

input image into patches Pi ∈ Rp×p×C . Each patch is flattened and embedded as zi = We · vec(Pi ) + Ei , where

We ∈ Rd×p C is a learnable projection matrix and Ei is the positional encoding. The sequence of embeddings

Z = [z1 , . . . , zn ] is passed through the transformer encode, which utilizes an FFT-based activation function at

the MPL layers Z′ = TransformerEncoder(Z) and FOREN(x) = F −1 (F(x) · Hf ), where Hf can be a low-pass

where λ1 andλ2 are the weights for loss terms. This methodology provides a systematic approach to high-fidelity

super-resolution that accounts for global structure and local texture in Earth System Model data.

3.5.2 ViFOR Experimental Results

To determine the optimal cutoff frequency for the Low-pass and High-pass activation functions in ViFOR,

we analyzed a range of frequencies between 0.01 Hz and 1 Hz. The PSNR results, as shown in Figure 16,

indicate that the best cutoff frequency for both filters is 0.4 Hz. Specifically, for the high-pass filter, PSNR

values remained relatively stable as the frequency increased to 0.3 Hz but declined significantly beyond this

point. This behavior suggests that higher frequencies introduce noise or artifacts that negatively impact the

reconstruction quality. For the low-pass filter, frequencies above 0.3 Hz yielded only marginal improvements in

PSNR, indicating diminishing returns. The model achieves an optimal balance by selecting 0.3 Hz as the cutoff

frequency, ensuring that both filters contribute effectively to the super-resolution process without compromising

image quality.

4 Conclusions

4.1 GCM Selection

We compared a reordered, three–stage envelope workflow (Workflow A) against the conventional ordering (Work-

flow B) for selecting representative GCM runs from the CMIP5 archive. Workflow A performs history matching

first, then maximizes spread in mean-state change, and finally captures extremes; Workflow B begins with

spread. From initial pools of 105 (RCP4.5) and 77 (RCP8.5) model runs, each workflow produced four RGCM

runs per scenario. Evaluation with Kolmogorov–Smirnov distance (Dmax ) and mean-squared error (MSE) shows

that Workflow A preserves ensemble uncertainty and reproduces historical climate more faithfully across three

climatically distinct regions:

 • Texas: Dmax reduced by 23 % ( ∆T ) and 20 % ( ∆P ); MSE for monthly mean temperature cut by 105 %.

 • Bihar & New York: consistent reductions in Dmax (11–42 %) and MSE (up to 37 %).

 These gains confirm that front-loading history matching is critical for balancing fidelity with spread. Work-

flow A therefore offers a robust, data-driven basis for regional impact studies. Future work should incorporate

model-interdependence weighting and advanced statistical diagnostics to refine subset quality further and extend

applicability to newer ensembles such as CMIP6.

4.2 ESM Data Downscaling

ViSIR represents a significant advancement in single-image super-resolution (SR) for Earth System Models

(ESMs), achieving robust spectral bias mitigation through the synergistic integration of Vision Transformers

(ViT) and sinusoidal representation networks (SIREN). Quantitative results highlight ViSIR’s achievements:

9.93 dB PSNR improvement over standalone ViT/SIREN architectures, a 4.29 dB margin over SRCNN

(the next-best performer), substantial SSIM enhancement (∆ = 0.17), and a 42% reduction in MSE. ViSIR is

a dual-stream architecture, where ViT’s self-attention captures global spatial dependencies and SIREN preserves
