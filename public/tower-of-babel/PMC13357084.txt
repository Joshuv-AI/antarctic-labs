METTL3- and IGF2BP1-associated m6A regulation of FADS2 contributes to lipid droplet accumulation and malignant progression in non-small cell lung cancer
Authors: Yu Zhang, Lin Wang, Huanhuan Xu, Leping Fang, Yingying Jiang, Weifei Fan, Juqing Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNon-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) is the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, and its underlying molecular mechanisms remain incompletely defined. N6-methyladenosine (m6A) RNA modification has emerged as a key epigenetic regulator of tumor metabolic reprogramming. However, the roles of the m6A writer methyltransferase-like 3 (METTL3) and the m6A reader insulin-like growth factor 2 mRNA-binding protein 1 (IGF2BP1) in NSCLC, particularly in lipid metabolic regulation, remain poorly characterized. The present study aimed to investigate the functional significance of METTL3 and IGF2BP1 in NSCLC, with particular focus on their regulation of FADS2 expression and lipid metabolism.MethodsDifferentially expressed m6A-related genes were identified by analyzing RNA-seq data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), followed by validation in 60 paired NSCLC and adjacent normal tissues. Gene and protein expression were examined by qRT-PCR and western blotting, respectively, with immunohistochemistry (IHC) further confirming protein levels in tissue specimens. Functional assays, including colony formation, Transwell migration/invasion, and Oil Red O staining, were performed in A549 and H1299 cells following METTL3, IGF2BP1, or FADS2 perturbation. The m6A modification on FADS2 mRNA was assessed by MeRIP-qPCR, and its association with METTL3 and IGF2BP1 was examined by RIP-qPCR. RNA stability was evaluated via actinomycin D chase assays, and rescue experiments were conducted through co-transfection of shRNA and overexpression constructs. The in vivo role of FADS2 was assessed using a xenograft tumor model.ResultsMETTL3 and IGF2BP1 were significantly upregulated in NSCLC tissues and were associated with poor overall survival. Silencing either gene inhibited NSCLC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. FADS2 expression was markedly elevated in NSCLC and positively correlated with METTL3 and IGF2BP1 expression. Knockdown of FADS2 reduced lipid droplet accumulation and suppressed malignant phenotypes in vitro, while significantly inhibiting tumor growth in vivo. Mechanistically, m6A modification was enriched within the 3’-UTR of FADS2 mRNA, where both METTL3 and IGF2BP1 were found to bind. Loss of METTL3 or IGF2BP1 accelerated FADS2 mRNA decay, whereas their overexpression enhanced transcript stability. Rescue experiments further confirmed that METTL3 and IGF2BP1 cooperatively regulate FADS2 expression and thereby promote NSCLC progression.ConclusionsMETTL3-mediated m6A modification of FADS2 transcripts is recognized by IGF2BP1, resulting in enhanced mRNA stability, increased lipid accumulation, and NSCLC progression. Our findings suggest that the METTL3/IGF2BP1-FADS2 axis contributes to lipid metabolic alterations in NSCLC and may serve as a potential therapeutic target.Keywords: METTL3, IGF2BP1, N6-methyladenosine (m6A), FADS2, non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC)

Highlight box

Key findings

• METTL3 and IGF2BP1 are upregulated in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and predict poor prognosis. FADS2 is identified as a downstream effector of this N6-methyladenosine (m6A) axis. METTL3 mediates m6A modification on FADS2 mRNA, recognized by IGF2BP1 to stabilize the transcript, driving lipid droplet accumulation and malignant progression.

What is known and what is new?

• METTL3 is the catalytic core of the m6A methyltransferase complex, and IGF2BP1 functions as an m6A reader that modulates RNA stability. Lipid metabolism reprogramming is a hallmark of malignancy. FADS2, a key enzyme in polyunsaturated fatty acid biosynthesis, has been implicated in tumor progression and therapy resistance in multiple cancers.

• This study identifies FADS2 as a downstream target of the METTL3/IGF2BP1 m6A axis in NSCLC. Our data indicate that METTL3-mediated m6A modification on FADS2 mRNA, recognized by IGF2BP1, stabilizes the transcript and promotes lipid droplet accumulation—a previously unrecognized link between m6A regulation, lipid metabolism, and NSCLC progression.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Our findings suggest that the METTL3/IGF2BP1-FADS2 axis may serve as a prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target in NSCLC. Targeting this axis, particularly FADS2 or its upstream m6A regulators, could counteract lipid droplet deposition and suppress tumor aggressiveness. Further investigation into FADS2-associated lipid metabolic networks and high-resolution m6A mapping may inform the development of metabolism-targeted therapeutic strategies for lung cancer.

Introduction

Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, posing a serious threat to human health (1). In China alone, an estimated 1.0606 million new lung cancer cases and 733,300 cancer-related deaths were reported in 2022, with numbers continuing to rise annually (2,3). Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), with the most common subtypes including lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) and lung squamous cell carcinoma (LSCC), accounts for approximately 85% of all lung cancer cases (4). Despite advances in diagnosis and therapy, the prognosis for advanced NSCLC remains poor, with a 5-year survival rate of only 18.6% (5). Disease progression, metastasis and therapeutic resistance are the major contributors to mortality in NSCLC patients (6). Therefore, elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying NSCLC initiation and progression is crucial for the development of effective therapeutic strategies.

N6-methyladenosine (m6A) is the most common epigenetic modification in mammalian mRNA, occurring as an internal post-transcriptional change. It is involved in multiple aspects of RNA processing, such as mRNA stability, translation, precursor mRNA splicing, transport, and localization. The m6A modification is dynamically and reversibly regulated by methyltransferases (writers), demethylases (erasers), and m6A-binding proteins (readers). The m6A modification is involved in numerous physiological and pathological processes. In recent years, numerous studies have demonstrated that m6A is extensively involved in tumor regulation by modulating tumor metabolism, thereby influencing tumor initiation and progression (7). METTL3 is the core catalytic subunit of the methyltransferase complex that installs m6A modifications. As an m6A “writer”, METTL3 is involved in proliferation, migration, and other biological processes, indicating that it serves as a key regulator of tumorigenesis (8). In pancreatic cancer, Chen et al. found that METTL3 promotes proliferation and stemness through the post-transcriptional stabilization of ID2 mRNA (9). In breast cancer, Xu et al. demonstrated that METTL3 regulates tumorigenesis and glycolysis by mediating m6A methylation of the tumor suppressor LATS1 (10). In small cell lung cancer, Zhang et al. showed that METTL3 promotes chemoresistance by regulating mitophagy and mitochondrial damage (11). In addition to METTL3, IGF2BP1, which serves as a “reader” of m6A modification, has been shown to regulate multiple oncogenic processes (12). The precise roles and mechanisms of METTL3 and IGF2BP1 in NSCLC are not fully understood. Therefore, further investigation into their relevant molecular pathways is warranted.

In recent years, extensive research efforts have been focused on metabolic alterations related to cancer (13,14). Dysregulated metabolic pathways or alterations in specific metabolite concentrations may lead to tumor progression, immune evasion, and treatment resistance (15,16). Among these, lipid metabolism plays a crucial role in these processes (17,18). Tumor cells frequently reprogram lipid metabolism, including fatty acid synthesis, uptake, storage, and degradation, to sustain increased demands for energy production, biomass accumulation, and signaling transduction (19,20).

Fatty acid desaturase 2 (FADS2), a key enzyme in polyunsaturated fatty acid biosynthesis, has recently gained attention in oncology. Dysregulated FADS2 activity alters membrane composition and signaling pathways, enhances resistance to ferroptosis, and remodels the tumor microenvironment through cancer-associated fibroblast (CAF) infiltration (21-24). In our previous study, we identified several lipid metabolism-related genes associated with the prognosis of LSCC, including FADS2 (25). Here, we demonstrate that METTL3/IGF2BP1-mediated m6A modification stabilizes FADS2 mRNA, thus increasing lipid droplet accumulation and potentially promoting the malignant phenotype in NSCLC, which highlights its potential as a prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target. We present this article in accordance with the MDAR and ARRIVE reporting checklists (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-1-0380/rc).

Methods

Data processing

NSCLC RNA-sequencing data and matched clinical information were acquired from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) through the Genomic Data Commons (GDC) data portal (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). Fragments per kilobase million (FPKM)-normalized gene expression profiles were retrieved for downstream analytical processing. A panel of m6A RNA methylation-related genes was established based on public databases and published literature. Expression values of m6A-related genes were extracted from the FPKM expression matrix for further investigation. Differential expression analyses of m6A-related genes and FADS2 between tumor and adjacent normal tissues were performed using Student’s t-test. For survival analysis, patients were characterized into high-expression and low-expression subgroups according to the median expression level of each gene. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were constructed to evaluate the correlation between gene expression and overall survival (OS), and the log-rank test was used to examine intergroup significance. Potential interactions between FADS2 and m6A-related genes were predicted via the RPISeq database (http://pridb.gdcb.iastate.edu/RPISeq/), which employs machine learning-based classifiers, including random forest (RF) and support vector machine (SVM) algorithms, to estimate RNA-protein interaction probabilities. Interaction scores ranging from 0 to 1 were generated, with higher scores indicating a greater likelihood of interaction. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed using the GSEA software and Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) to investigate biological pathways associated with FADS2 expression. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.1; Posit Software). All statistical tests were two-sided, and P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Clinical samples

Sixty paired NSCLC tumor tissues and paired adjacent normal lung tissues were obtained from patients [32 males and 28 females, median age, 58 years (range, 34–77 years)] undergoing surgical resection at the Geriatric Hospital of Nanjing Medical University. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients, and the study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Geriatric Hospital of Nanjing Medical University (No. 031). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. For each patient, paired tumor tissues and histologically normal adjacent lung tissues (located at least 3 cm from the tumor margin) were collected intraoperatively. The diagnosis of NSCLC and confirmation of adjacent non-tumorous tissues were established based on histopathological examination. All specimens were immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 ℃ for a minimum of 72 hours prior to further analysis. Patients were eligible for inclusion if they met the following criteria: (I) histologically confirmed primary NSCLC; (II) scheduled for curative surgical resection; (III) no prior exposure to radiotherapy, chemotherapy, or other anticancer treatments; and (IV) signed informed consent for the use of resected tissues in research. Exclusion criteria included: (I) history of other malignancies; (II) presence of distant metastasis at diagnosis; (III) receipt of neoadjuvant therapy; and (IV) inadequate or poor-quality paired tissue specimens as determined by pathological assessment.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Paraffin-embedded NSCLC tissue sections (5 µm) were fixed, sectioned, and mounted on glass slides. The sections were dewaxed in xylene (two changes, 10 min each) and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series (100%, 95%, 85%, and 75%; 5 min each), followed by rinsing in distilled water. Antigen retrieval was performed using citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 95 ℃ for 20 min. After cooling to room temperature, endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked by incubation with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 15 min. Subsequently, the sections were blocked with 10% goat serum at room temperature for 30 min and then incubated overnight at 4 ℃ with primary antibodies against FADS2 (1:200, Proteintech, cat. no. 68026-1-Ig, RRID: AB_2923633), METTL3 (1:200, Abcam, cat. no. ab195352, RRID: AB_2721254), and IGF2BP1 (1:200, Abcam, cat. no. ab184305, RRID: AB_2893198). After washing with PBS, sections were incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody at room temperature for 1 h. Immunoreactivity was visualized using 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB), followed by counterstaining with hematoxylin. The sections were then dehydrated, cleared, and mounted. Stained sections were observed and imaged under a light microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining was independently evaluated by two experienced pathologists blinded to the clinical data. The staining intensity was scored as follows: 0 (no staining), 1 (weak staining), 2 (moderate staining), and 3 (strong staining). The percentage of positive tumor cells was estimated for each intensity category. The final H-score was calculated using the following formula: H-score = (1 × percentage of weak staining) + (2 × percentage of moderate staining) + (3 × percentage of strong staining), resulting in a total score ranging from 0 to 300 (26). Images were captured using an Olympus light microscope at both low magnification and high magnification, and scale bars were added to all images. High-magnification images were provided to better illustrate the subcellular localization and staining patterns of the target proteins. Corresponding low-magnification overview images were included in the lower-left corner of each panel to indicate the original tissue context.

Cell culture and transfection

Normal human bronchial epithelial (HBE) cells [HBE cell line (cat. no. TCH-C377)] and NSCLC cell lines [human NSCLC cell line (H1975; cat. no. TCH-C276), human LUAD cell line (A549; cat. no. TCH-C116), human lung carcinoma cell line (H1299; cat. no. TCH-C269) and human LUAD cell line (HCC827; cat. no. TCH-C128)] were purchased from Haixing Biosciences Co., Ltd. (Suzhou, China). All cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc., Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (100 U/mL) in a humidified incubator at 37 ℃ with 5% CO2. Cells were routinely passaged at 70–80% confluence(27). All cell lines were confirmed to be free of mycoplasma contamination using polymerase chain reaction (PCR)-based detection, and cell line authentication was performed by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling prior to use.

For transfection experiments, short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) targeting FADS2 (shFADS2#1–3), METTL3 (shMETTL3#1–3), IGF2BP1 (shIGF2BP1#1–3), as well as a non-targeting negative control (shNC), were cloned into the pLKO.1 lentiviral vector (Suzhou Starfish Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Suzhou, China). Overexpression plasmids (pcDNA3.1-based vectors) encoding METTL3 and IGF2BP1 were also constructed by the same company. Cells were seeded into 6-well plates and allowed to reach 50–70% confluence prior to transfection. Lipofectamine® 2000 (Invitrogen; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.) was used for shRNA plasmid transfection (10 µg; 1 µg/µL), while DNA transfection reagent (Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Mannheim, Germany) was used for overexpression plasmid transfection (4 µg; 1 µg/µL), according to the manufacturers’ instructions. Transfections were performed in serum-free medium, followed by incubation at 37 ℃ in a 5% CO2 incubator. For shRNA transfection, the culture medium was replaced with complete medium 6 h after transfection, whereas for overexpression plasmid transfection, the medium was not changed. Total RNA was extracted 24 h after transfection, and total protein was harvested 48 h after transfection for subsequent analyses (28).

Construction of plasmids and interference sequences

Short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) targeting FADS2, METTL3 and IGF2BP1, as well as a non-targeting negative control (shNC), were designed and synthesized by GeneCopoeia, Inc. (Rockville, MD, USA). The target sequences were as follows: for FADS2: shFADS2-1, 5'-GCCAGTTCACCAATCAGCAG-3'; shFADS2-2, 5'-TTGCCCACGAATTCCAGGTC-3'; shFADS2-3, 5'-AATGGTCCATCCAGCACCCG-3'. For METTL3: shMETTL3-1, 5'-CAGTGGATCTGTTGTGATACTCGAGTATCACAACAGATCCACTGTTTTTT-3'; shMETTL3-2, 5'-CAGACGAATTATCAATAAACTCGAGTTTATTGATAATTCGTCTGTTTTTT-3'; shMETTL3-3, 5'-GCAAGTATGTTCACTATGACTCGAGTCATAGTGAACATACTTGCTTTTTT-3'. For IGF2BP1: shIGF2BP1-1, 5'-GGCTCAGTATGGTACAGTACTCGAGTACTGTACCATACTGAGCCTTTTTT-3'; shIGF2BP1-2, 5'-AGCAAGATACCGAGACAAACTCGAGTTTGTCTCGGTATCTTGCTTTTTTT-3'; shIGF2BP1-3, 5'-CGGTGAACGAGTTGCAGAACTCGAGTTCTGCAACTCGTTCACCGTTTTTT-3'. The negative control sequence (shNC) was 5'-GCTTCGCGCCGTAGTCTTA-3'. All shRNA oligonucleotides were cloned into the pLKO.1 lentiviral vector for gene silencing. For overexpression assays, the full-length open reading frames (ORFs) of human METTL3 and IGF2BP1 were amplified and subcloned into the pcDNA3.1(+) expression vector (GeneCopoeia, Inc.). All recombinant plasmids were verified by Sanger sequencing prior to use.

RNA extraction and quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from NSCLC cell lines or tissue samples using TRIzol® reagent (Invitrogen; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc., Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.), and only samples with an A260/A280 ratio of 1.8–2.0 were used for further analysis. For cDNA synthesis, 1 µg of total RNA was reverse transcribed using the PrimeScript™ RT reagent Kit (Perfect Real Time) (cat. no. RR037A; Takara Bio, Inc., Shiga, Japan). The resulting cDNA was stored at −20 ℃ until use. Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed using TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ (Tli RNaseH Plus) (cat. no. RR420A; Takara Bio, Inc.) on an ABI 7500 Fast Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.). Each reaction (20 µL) contained 10 µL TB Green Premix, 0.4 µL forward and reverse primers (10 µM), 2 µL cDNA template and 7.2 µL nuclease-free water and was performed in triplicate. The cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 ℃ for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles at 95 ℃ for 5 s and 60 ℃ for 30 s. Melt curve analysis (60–95 ℃, 0.3 ℃/s) was conducted to confirm amplification specificity. Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was used as an internal reference gene, and relative mRNA expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCq method (29). The primer sequences used for qPCR were as follows: FADS2 forward, 5'-GACCACGGCAAGAACTCAAAG-3' and reverse, 5'-GAGGGTAGGAATCCAGCCATT-3'; METTL3 forward, 5'-CTGGGCACTTGGATTTAAGGAA-3' and reverse, 5'-TGAGAGGTGGTGTAGCAACTT-3'; IGF2BP1 forward, 5'-CGGCAACCTCAACGAGAGT-3' and reverse, 5'-GTAGCCGGATTTGACCAAGAA-3'; GAPDH forward, 5'-GGTGGTCTCCTCTGACTTCAA-3' and reverse, 5'-GTTGCTGTAGCCAAATTCGTTGT-3'.

Western blotting analysis

Cells were harvested and lysed in RIPA lysis buffer (cat. no. P0013B; Shanghai Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail (cat. no. C0001; TargetMol Chemicals Inc., Boston, MA, USA) and phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF; cat. no. ST506-2; Shanghai Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) on ice. The lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 ×g for 15 min at 4 ℃, and the supernatants were collected as total protein extracts. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Equal amounts of protein (30 µg per lane) were separated by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred onto methanol-activated 0.45 µm PVDF membranes (MilliporeSigma, Burlington, MA, USA). The membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST at room temperature (20–25 ℃) for 1 h, followed by incubation with primary antibodies at 4 ℃ overnight. The primary antibodies used were as follows: anti-FADS2 antibody (1:1,000; Affinity Biosciences, cat. no. ag27715), anti-METTL3 antibody (1:1,000; Abcam, clone ERP18810, cat. no. ab195352), anti-IGF2BP1 antibody (1:1,000; Abcam, clone ERP18791, cat. no. ab184305) and anti-GAPDH antibody (1:5,000; Abcam, cat. no. ab181602) as a loading control. After washing three times with TBST, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody (1:5,000; anti-rabbit IgG; Shanghai Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) at room temperature for 1 h. Following extensive washing, protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection kit (MilliporeSigma) and captured using a chemiluminescence imaging system (Tanon™ 5200; Tanon Science and Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software (version 1.53; National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) and normalized to GAPDH (26).

Oil Red O staining and quantification

Cells were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured to ~70–80% confluence prior to staining. After washing twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 15 min. Following fixation, cells were rinsed with PBS and incubated with 60% isopropanol for 5 min to enhance lipid staining efficiency. A working solution of Oil Red O was prepared by diluting a 0.5% (w/v) stock solution (Sigma-Aldrich; Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany) with distilled water at a ratio of 3:2, followed by filtration through a 0.22 µm filter prior to use. Cells were stained with the freshly prepared 0.3% Oil Red O solution at room temperature for 15–20 min. After staining, excess dye was removed by washing with distilled water, and nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin for 1 min. Lipid droplets were observed and imaged under a light microscope (Olympus BX53; Olympus Corporation) at ×20 magnification. Representative images were captured under identical microscope settings and exposure conditions. Intracellular lipid accumulation was quantified by analyzing the Oil Red O-positive area and relative staining intensity using ImageJ software (version 1.53; National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). At least three random fields per sample were analyzed, and experiments were performed in triplicate (30).

Colony formation assay

Cells (500–1,000 per well) were seeded in six-well plates and cultured at 37 ℃ with 5% CO2 for 10–14 days. Colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 0.1% crystal violet. After air-drying, colonies containing more than 50 cells were photographed and counted under a light microscope. Experiments were performed in triplicate (26).

Migration and invasion assays

Cell migration was assessed using Transwell chambers (8 µm pore size, Corning, Corning, NY, USA). For invasion assays, the upper chambers were pre-coated with Matrigel (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). Cells (2×104 for migration; 5×104 for invasion) suspended in serum-free medium were seeded into the upper chambers, while medium containing 10% FBS was added to the lower chambers as a chemoattractant. After incubation for 24 h (migration) or 48 h (invasion), non-migrated or non-invaded cells on the upper surface of the membrane were gently removed with cotton swabs. Cells on the lower surface were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 0.1% crystal violet. The stained cells were photographed and counted in five randomly selected fields under a light microscope (31).

Xenograft tumor model

All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Nanjing Medical University (No. 2506645) and performed in compliance with institutional guidelines for the care and use of animals. Male BALB/c nude mice (4–6 weeks old; Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) were housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions with free access to food and water. H1299 cells stably expressing shFADS2#3 or NC were harvested, resuspended in serum-free medium, and subcutaneously injected into the flank of each mouse (5×106 cells in 100 µL per mouse; n=5 per group). Tumor growth was monitored every 3 days using a digital caliper. Tumor volume was calculated using the formula: volume = (length × width2)/2. Mice were sacrificed when tumors reached a maximum volume of ~1,500 mm3 or at 28 days post-injection, whichever occurred first. At the experimental endpoint, tumors were excised, photographed, and weighed. Tumor tissues were subsequently fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and subjected to IHC analysis. All measurements were performed in a blinded manner (27).

m6A RNA immunoprecipitation (MeRIP-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from NSCLC cell lines using TRIzol® reagent (Invitrogen; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc., Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer, and only high-quality RNA (A260/A280 =1.8–2.0) was used. For MeRIP assays, 50–100 µg of total RNA was fragmented into ~100–200 nt fragments using RNA fragmentation buffer (10 mM ZnCl2, 10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.0) at 94 ℃ for 3 min, followed by immediate cooling on ice to terminate the reaction. Fragmented RNA was incubated with 5–10 µg anti-m6A antibody (ab151230, Rabbit polyclonal, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) or normal rabbit IgG (negative control) in IP buffer (150 mM NaCl, 10 mM Tris-HCl, 0.1% NP-40, RNase inhibitor) at 4 ℃ overnight with gentle rotation. Protein A/G magnetic beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.), pre-blocked with RNase-free BSA and yeast tRNA, were added and incubated for an additional 2 h at 4 ℃. The beads were washed 4–5 times with ice-cold IP buffer to minimize nonspecific binding. Bound RNA was eluted using elution buffer by incubation at 55 ℃ for 10 min. Both immunoprecipitated RNA and input RNA were purified and subjected to reverse transcription using the PrimeScript™ RT reagent kit (Takara Bio, Inc.). The FADS2 mRNA IP assay primers used are in Table S1. qPCR was performed using TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ (Takara Bio, Inc.) on an ABI 7500 Fast Real-Time PCR system. m6A enrichment was calculated as the percentage of input RNA (% input method): % input = 2[Ct (input) – Ct (IP)] × dilution factor. Results were further normalized to IgG controls and expressed as relative enrichment (26).

RIP

RIP assays were performed using the Magna RIP RNA-Binding Protein Immunoprecipitation Kit (Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, A549 and H1299 cells were lysed in RIP lysis buffer supplemented with RNase inhibitors and protease inhibitor cocktail. Cell lysates were incubated overnight at 4 ℃ with magnetic beads conjugated with anti-METTL3 antibody (1:50; Abcam, clone ERP18810, cat. no. ab195352), anti-IGF2BP1 antibody (1:30; Abcam, clone ERP18791, cat. no. ab184305) or normal rabbit IgG as a negative control. After washing, co-immunoprecipitated RNA was purified and reverse-transcribed into cDNA. The primers used are shown in Table S1. Quantitative PCR was subsequently performed to determine the enrichment of FADS2 transcripts in each immunoprecipitation group relative to IgG controls.

RNA stability assay

To assess the effect of METTL3 or IGF2BP1 modulation on FADS2 mRNA stability, NSCLC cells were seeded into 6-well plates and cultured overnight to reach approximately 70–80% confluence. Cells were subsequently transfected with overexpression plasmids, shRNAs, or corresponding negative controls as indicated. After transfection, cells were treated with actinomycin D (5 µg/mL; cat. no. HY-17559; MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA) at 37 ℃ to inhibit de novo RNA transcription. Total RNA was collected at the indicated time points (0, 1, 3, 6, and 9 h) following actinomycin D treatment. RNA extraction was performed using TRIzol® reagent (Invitrogen; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Subsequently, 1 µg of total RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the PrimeScript™ RT Reagent Kit (Perfect Real Time) (cat. no. RR037A; Takara Bio, Inc., Shiga, Japan). Quantitative PCR was performed using TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ (Tli RNaseH Plus) (cat. no. RR420A; Takara Bio, Inc.) on an ABI StepOne Plus Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.). FADS2 mRNA levels at each time point were normalized to GAPDH and expressed relative to the corresponding 0 h group, which was set to 100%. Relative FADS2 mRNA abundance over time was compared between experimental groups (METTL3/IGF2BP1 overexpression or knockdown) and their respective negative control groups to evaluate differences in transcript decay trends following transcriptional inhibition.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed with at least three independent biological replicates. Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 7.0 software (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). The normality of data distribution was assessed prior to statistical testing for comparisons between two groups, an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test was used for normally distributed data, while a two-tailed Mann-Whitney U test was applied for non-normally distributed data. Paired t-tests were used for matched samples when appropriate. For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was used for normally distributed data, whereas the Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn’s post hoc test was applied for non-parametric data. Correlation analyses were performed using Pearson’s correlation coefficients, depending on data distribution. Survival curves were analyzed using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared with the log-rank test, when applicable. Western blot band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software (version 1.53; National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) and normalized to the loading control (GAPDH). IHC staining was quantified using Image-Pro Plus software (Media Cybernetics, Rockville, MD, USA). A two-tailed P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

METTL3 and IGF2BP1 are upregulated in NSCLC and predict poor prognosis

Given emerging evidence that m6A modification contributes to tumor initiation and progression, we analyzed the expression profile of m6A-related genes in NSCLC based on the TCGA data. Multiple m6A-related genes exhibited aberrant expression in NSCLC tissues. Among them, METTL3, IGF2BP1, IGF2BP3, YTHDF1/2, KIAA1429, EIF3A, and IGF2BP2 were significantly upregulated, while METTL14, METTL16, FTO, WTAP, and ZC3H13 were downregulated (Figure 1A). To further validate the expression profiles of m6A-related genes in NSCLC, we detected the mRNA levels of representative m6A-related genes in 60 pairs of NSCLC and matched adjacent normal tissues using qRT-PCR. The results showed that METTL3, IGF2BP1, METTL14, YTHDF2 and EIF3A were significantly upregulated in tumor tissues compared with adjacent normal tissues. Notably, METTL3 and IGF2BP1 exhibited the most prominent upregulation, with expression levels approximately 847.4- and 185.7-fold higher than those in normal tissues, respectively (Figure 1B-1E, Figure S1). Considering their extremely elevated fold changes and consistent dysregulated expression pattern in both TCGA dataset and clinical validation samples, we specifically selected METTL3 and IGF2BP1 as the candidate targets for subsequent investigation, implying that these two m6A regulators may exert core and pivotal roles in the m6A modification network during NSCLC tumorigenesis and progression. We further validated the protein expression levels of METTL3 and IGF2BP1 in NSCLC tissues by IHC. Quantitative analysis demonstrated that the protein abundances of both METTL3 and IGF2BP1 were markedly increased in NSCLC tumor tissues compared with paired adjacent normal tissues (Figure 1F,1G). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis revealed that high expression of METTL3 or IGF2BP1 was significantly correlated with unfavorable OS of NSCLC patients (Figure 1H,1I). Collectively, these findings indicate that METTL3 and IGF2BP1 are markedly upregulated in NSCLC, and their upregulation is closely associated with poor clinical prognosis.

Similarly, IGF2BP1 knockdown was validated (Figure 3A-3C) and also suppressed these malignant phenotypes (Figure 3D-3I). Collectively, these findings indicate that both METTL3 and IGF2BP1 promote malignant progression in NSCLC cells.

FADS2 promotes intracellular lipid storage and malignant progression of NSCLC

Our previous study identified several lipid metabolism-related genes, including FADS2, that are associated with the prognosis of LSCC. In the present study, TCGA database analysis revealed that FADS2 expression was significantly upregulated in NSCLC tissues and correlated with poor patient prognosis (Figure S2A,S2B). GSEA further indicated that FADS2 was primarily involved in the biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids and cholesterol (Figure S2C,S2D). Given the critical regulatory functions of m6A in tumor metabolism, we further explored the potential association between m6A regulators and FADS2 in NSCLC. RPISeq database was used to predict the molecular interaction probability between FADS2 and m6A-related genes. Among these candidates, METTL3 and IGF2BP1 exhibited the highest interaction scores with FADS2, suggesting stronger potential molecular interactions between FADS2 and the two m6A regulators (Figure S2E). Consistent with the bioinformatics predictions, Pearson correlation analysis in 60 pairs of clinical NSCLC specimens further verified a significant positive correlation between FADS2 and both METTL3 and IGF2BP1. Specifically, FADS2 expression was positively correlated with METTL3 (r=0.8167, r2=0.6670, P<0.0001) and IGF2BP1 (r=0.8928, r2=0.7971, P<0.0001) (Figure S2F,S2G). These data suggested that FADS2 may serve as a potential downstream target regulated by the METTL3/IGF2BP1 axis. We next investigated the biological function of FADS2 in NSCLC. Compared with normal HBE cells, FADS2 expression was significantly elevated in multiple NSCLC cell lines, including A549 and H1299 cells, at both mRNA and protein levels (Figure S3A-S3C). Similarly, FADS2 was consistently upregulated in 60 pairs of clinical NSCLC tissues relative to adjacent normal tissues (Figure 4A-4D). To further characterize the functional role of FADS2, we established stable FADS2 knockdown cell models using three independent shRNAs (shFADS2#1–3) in A549 and H1299 cells. The knockdown efficiency was validated by qRT-PCR and western blotting (Figure S3D-S3F). Oil Red O staining showed that FADS2 knockdown markedly reduced intracellular lipid droplet accumulation in NSCLC cells (Figure 4E), indicating an impaired lipid storage phenotype and disrupted fatty acid homeostasis. In functional assays, FADS2 silencing significantly suppressed cell proliferation, colony formation, migration, and invasion (Figure 4F,4G). Consistent with the in vitro findings, xenograft tumor models demonstrated that stable knockdown of FADS2 in H1299 cells significantly inhibited tumor growth, as reflected by reduced tumor volume and weight (Figure 4H-4J). IHC staining further confirmed decreased FADS2 expression in xenograft tumors from the FADS2 knockdown group (Figure S3G,S3H). Collectively, these results demonstrate that FADS2 contributes to enhanced intracellular lipid storage and promotes the malignant progression of NSCLC. However, we did not compare lipid droplet changes or the effects of FADS2 knockdown in non-cancerous lung epithelial cells (HBE), leaving the cancer specificity of the observed phenotype uncertain.

To further verify our hypothesis, we knocked down METTL3 (shMETTL3#1–3) and IGF2BP1 (shIGF2BP1#1–3), respectively, to assess FADS2 expression and lipid droplet deposition in A549 and H1299 cells. Oil Red O staining analysis revealed that depletion of METTL3 or IGF2BP1 markedly decreased intracellular lipid droplet accumulation (Figure 5G-5J). Collectively, these data indicate that METTL3 and IGF2BP1 participate in regulating intracellular lipid droplet deposition and malignant behavior of NSCLC partly by maintaining FADS2 expression.

METTL3 and IGF2BP1 are associated with m6A-mediated regulation of FADS2 in NSCLC cells

Based on the above findings, we next investigated whether FADS2 mRNA undergoes m6A modification in NSCLC cells. MeRIP-qPCR assays performed in A549 and H1299 cells demonstrated significant enrichment of FADS2 transcripts in anti-m6A immunoprecipitates compared with IgG controls, particularly within the 2,928–3,048 nt region near the 3'-UTR, suggesting the presence of m6A modification within this region (Figure 6A,6B). To further explore the association of METTL3 and IGF2BP1 with FADS2 transcripts, RIP-qPCR assays were subsequently performed using antibodies against METTL3 and IGF2BP1. The results showed significant enrichment of FADS2 mRNA in both METTL3 and IGF2BP1 immunoprecipitates compared with IgG controls (Figure 6C-6F). Notably, the enriched binding regions of METTL3 and IGF2BP1 were generally consistent with the m6A-enriched region identified within the 3'-UTR of FADS2 transcripts, supporting a potential role for METTL3 and IGF2BP1 in the m6A-associated regulation of FADS2 in NSCLC cells. To investigate whether METTL3 and IGF2BP1 affect the stability of FADS2 transcripts, RNA decay assays were performed following actinomycin D treatment in A549 and H1299 cells. Compared with corresponding negative control groups, METTL3 or IGF2BP1 overexpression tended to maintain higher relative FADS2 mRNA levels over time, whereas knockdown of METTL3 or IGF2BP1 showed a trend toward faster reduction of FADS2 transcripts (Figure 6G-6J). These findings suggest that METTL3 and IGF2BP1 may contribute to the post-transcriptional maintenance of FADS2 mRNA in NSCLC cells. Taken together, these findings suggest that METTL3 and IGF2BP1 are associated with the m6A-mediated post-transcriptional regulation of FADS2 in NSCLC cells. METTL3 and IGF2BP1 cooperatively contribute to FADS2-associated intracellular lipid droplet deposition and malignant phenotypes in NSCLC

To further investigate the functional relationship between METTL3 and IGF2BP1 in regulating FADS2 expression, rescue experiments were performed in A549 and H1299 cells. Silencing of METTL3 or IGF2BP1 reduced FADS2 mRNA and protein expression, whereas overexpression of either factor increased FADS2 expression. Notably, IGF2BP1 overexpression partially restored FADS2 expression in METTL3-silenced cells, while METTL3 overexpression also partially reversed the reduction of FADS2 expression induced by IGF2BP1 knockdown, although the rescue effects were incomplete compared with the corresponding overexpression groups alone (Figure 7A-7C). Oil Red O staining showed similar trends in lipid droplet accumulation. Reduced lipid droplet deposition caused by METTL3 or IGF2BP1 knockdown was partially restored following overexpression of the corresponding interacting factor, although the restoration remained weaker than that observed in the respective overexpression groups (Figure 7D,7E). Consistently, METTL3 or IGF2BP1 overexpression promoted NSCLC cell proliferation, invasion, and migration, whereas simultaneous knockdown of the interacting factor attenuated these effects. However, the reversal effects remained partial and did not fully restore the phenotypes observed in the overexpression groups alone (Figure 7F-7K). Taken together, these findings suggest that METTL3 and IGF2BP1 cooperatively contribute to the regulation of FADS2 expression and associated malignant phenotypes in NSCLC cells.

Discussion

NSCLC poses a major threat to human health due to its high morbidity and mortality rates. Over the past few years, remarkable progress has been achieved in the treatment of NSCLC. In addition to conventional chemoradiotherapy, therapeutic strategies such as targeted therapy for oncogenic driver gene mutations and immunotherapy with immune checkpoint inhibitors have significantly expanded the treatment landscape (32). However, treatment failure frequently occurs because of either primary or acquired drug resistance, highlighting the urgent need to identify new therapeutic targets. Among emerging regulatory mechanisms, epigenetic modifications, particularly m6A, have gained increasing attention in tumor biology. Nevertheless, the involvement of m6A modification in NSCLC progression remains incompletely understood. Based on TCGA database analysis, we observed significant differences in m6A-related profiles between NSCLC and normal tissues. Previous studies have reported that METTL3 and IGF2BP1 may function cooperatively in regulating m6A-modified transcripts (33). Consistent with these findings, we confirmed that both METTL3 and IGF2BP1 are significantly upregulated in NSCLC and that elevated expression of either gene is associated with poorer patient survival. These observations prompted us to further investigate the potential roles of these two m6A regulators in NSCLC progression.

METTL3 is the core catalytic component of the methyltransferase complex responsible for m6A installation and has been implicated in multiple oncogenic processes (34,35). Insulin-like growth factor 2 mRNA-binding proteins (IGF2BPs, including IGF2BP1-3) represent a distinct family of m6A “readers” containing two RNA recognition regions (RRMs) and four heterologous ribonucleoprotein K homology (HK) domains, enabling them to bind m6A-modified transcripts and modulate RNA localization, translation, and stability (36,37). Through functional assays, we found that METTL3 and IGF2BP1 enhanced proliferation, invasion, and migration of NSCLC cells. Given the critical function of m6A modifications in cellular physiology and the systemic complexity of its regulatory network, selectively targeting METTL3 or IGF2BP1 proves to be an infeasible therapeutic approach (9). Therefore, identifying downstream effectors regulated by METTL3/IGF2BP1 may help clarify the mechanisms underlying NSCLC progression.

Altered lipid metabolism is increasingly recognized as an important feature of malignant tumors (19,38). In addition to serving as energy reservoirs, lipids contribute to membrane biosynthesis and intracellular signaling pathways involved in tumor progression and metastasis. Cancer cells may exhibit changes in fatty acid uptake, synthesis, oxidation, and storage to support rapid proliferation and adaptation to environmental stress (19,20,39). Our previous study suggested an association between FADS2 and lipid metabolism in lung cancer (25). Further literature review indicated that FADS2 may function as an important regulator of unsaturated fatty acid synthesis, potentially influencing membrane composition and lipid-associated signaling pathways (40-42). Increasing evidence suggests that FADS2 is associated with tumor progression and therapeutic resistance in several malignancies. For instance, certain cancers bypassed the canonical stearoyl-CoA desaturase (SCD)-dependent pathway by engaging FADS2 to synthesize atypical fatty acid sapienate, which increased membrane fluidity and promoted invasiveness. Moreover, inhibition of mTOR signaling suppressed FADS2 expression and sapienate synthesis (43). In colorectal cancer, FADS2 overexpression enhanced PGE2 metabolism, thereby promoting tumor growth, while in ovarian cancer, elevated FADS2 accelerated lipid metabolism, promoted invasion, and conferred platinum resistance (44). Conversely, FADS2 inhibition delayed tumor growth, reduced cancer stemness, and reversed drug resistance by downregulating GPX4 and the reduced glutathione/oxidized glutathione (GSH/GSSG ratio), thereby inducing lipid peroxidation and mitochondrial dysfunction (21). In the present study, we observed that FADS2 was highly expressed in NSCLC cell lines and clinical samples. Increased FADS2 expression was associated with enhanced lipid droplet accumulation, proliferation, invasion, and migration, and poor prognosis.

Based on these findings, we further explored the potential relationship between METTL3/IGF2BP1 and FADS2 in NSCLC. Bioinformatics analysis and histological validation demonstrated a significant correlation between these factors, suggesting that FADS2 may act as a downstream target of METTL3 and IGF2BP1. Functional experiments showed that silencing either METTL3 or IGF2BP1 significantly downregulated FADS2 expression, decreased lipid droplet accumulation, and attenuated malignant phenotypes. Conversely, overexpression of either METTL3 or IGF2BP1 increased FADS2 expression and was accompanied by enhanced lipid droplet accumulation and malignant behaviors.

Mechanistically, MeRIP-qPCR analysis revealed enriched m6A modification within the 3’-UTR region of FADS2 mRNA, while RIP-qPCR assays demonstrated the association of both METTL3 and IGF2BP1 with FADS2 transcripts in a similar region. RNA decay assays further showed that overexpression of METTL3 or IGF2BP1 maintained relatively higher FADS2 mRNA levels following actinomycin D treatment, whereas knockdown of either factor accelerated transcript degradation. These findings support a potential role for METTL3 and IGF2BP1 in the post-transcriptional stabilization of FADS2 mRNA in NSCLC.

Rescue experiments further suggested that METTL3 and IGF2BP1 cooperatively contribute to maintaining FADS2 expression and related malignant phenotypes in NSCLC cells. Similar coordinated “writer-reader” regulatory patterns have been described in multiple malignancies, in which methyltransferase-mediated m6A modification is subsequently recognized by specific m6A readers to stabilize target transcripts and facilitate tumor progression or metabolic alterations. For instance, in hepatocellular carcinoma, ALKBH5 downregulation enhanced m6A modification of LYPD1, which was stabilized by IGF2BP1 to promote tumorigenesis (45). Similarly, in intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, METTL3 upregulated NFAT5 expression in an IGF2BP1-dependent manner to drive glycolytic reprogramming (33). In our study, although overexpression of METTL3 or IGF2BP1 partially restored FADS2 expression and related phenotypes following knockdown of the interacting factor, the rescue effects were incomplete compared with the corresponding overexpression groups alone. These findings suggest that METTL3 and IGF2BP1 function cooperatively, but not exclusively, in regulating FADS2 expression and downstream malignant phenotypes. The incomplete rescue further implies that additional m6A regulators or RNA-binding proteins may also participate in the post-transcriptional regulation of FADS2, indicating the presence of a more complex regulatory network. Consistently, the observed dysregulation of METTL14, YTHDF2, and EIF3A in NSCLC (Figure S1A-S1F) further supports the existence of a broader and more intricate regulatory landscape. Collectively, these findings reveal that the METTL3/IGF2BP1-FADS2 axis promotes lipid droplet deposition and enhances tumor aggressiveness, highlighting its potential as both a biomarker and a therapeutic target in NSCLC.

Notably, this study has certain limitations. First, MeRIP-qPCR can only detect the relative enrichment of m6A-modification, lacking single-base resolution and absolute quantitative information of methylation modification, and cannot accurately locate the functional m6A sites of FADS2. Second, the present experiments did not directly verify the causal relationship whereby METTL3-mediated m6A-modification promotes FADS2 transcript stabilization in an IGF2BP1-dependent manner. Third, this study primarily evaluated lipid droplet accumulation and malignant phenotypes but lacked comprehensive lipidomic profiling and metabolic flux analyses to further elucidate the role of the METTL3/IGF2BP1-FADS2 axis in lipid metabolic reprogramming in NSCLC. Moreover, we have not established a direct causal relationship between lipid droplet accumulation and malignant phenotypes; it remains possible that FADS2 promotes tumor aggressiveness through other mechanisms, such as membrane fluidity or signaling pathways. These are the issues we need to address in future studies.

In conclusion, our data support a model in which METTL3- and IGF2BP1-associated m6A regulation contributes to enhanced FADS2 expression, thereby participating in lipid metabolic alterations and malignant progression in NSCLC (Figure 6K). Overall, these findings provide insight into the potential involvement of m6A-associated post-transcriptional regulation in NSCLC lipid metabolism and suggest that the METTL3/IGF2BP1-FADS2 axis may represent a potential therapeutic target for NSCLC. Further elucidation of FADS2-associated lipid metabolic networks and high-resolution m6A mapping may facilitate the development of metabolism-targeted therapeutic strategies in lung cancer.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our findings support a model in which METTL3 and IGF2BP1 cooperatively sustain FADS2 expression via m6A-mediated mRNA stabilization, thereby promoting lipid droplet accumulation and malignant progression in NSCLC. The METTL3/IGF2BP1-FADS2 axis may represent a potential prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target, and warrants further investigation to facilitate the development of metabolism-targeted therapeutic strategies.
