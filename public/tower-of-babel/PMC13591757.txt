Risk prediction models for early neurological deterioration after intravenous thrombolysis in acute ischemic stroke patients: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Yanqin Chen, Caiyue Li, Lijun Fan, Wan Zhang, Yujiao Ding, Hua Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAn increasing number of risk prediction models have been developed to predict early neurological deterioration (END) in patients with acute ischemic stroke after intravenous thrombolysis. However, the methodological quality and clinical applicability of these models remain unclear.ObjectiveTo systematically review studies on risk prediction models for END in patients with acute ischemic stroke treated with intravenous thrombolysis (IVT).DesignSystematic review and meta-analysis of observational studies.MethodsSeven databases (CNKI, Wanfang Database, VIP database, PubMed, Web of Science, the Cochrane Library, and Embase) were systematically searched from inception to December 20, 2025. Information extracted from the included studies comprised study design, data sources, outcome definitions, sample size, predictors, model development methods, and model performance. The Prediction Model Risk of Bias Assessment Tool (PROBAST) was used to assess evaluate the risk of bias and applicability of the included studies.ResultsEighteen studies were ultimately included, comprising 18 prediction models. All studies utilized logistic regression to predict END. The incidence of END after IVT among patients with acute ischemic stroke patients ranged from 7.7% to 31.3%. The baseline National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score was the most frequently identified predictor. All studies were rated as having a high risk of bias based on the PROBAST assessment, primarily due to inconsistent outcome definitions, inappropriate data sources, and inadequate reporting within the analysis domain. The pooled area under the curve (AUC) for the five validated models was 0.87 (95% CI: 0.84–0.91), suggesting moderate predictive performance.ConclusionAlthough the included prediction models showed moderate discriminative performance for END in patients with acute ischemic stroke following intravenous thrombolysis, all studies were assessed as having a high risk of bias based on the PROBAST checklist. These findings emphasize the need for nurse-led risk monitoring, standardized neurological assessments, and early preventive nursing interventions for high-risk patients.Clinical trial registrationThe protocol for this study is registered with PROSPERO (registration number: CRD42025628890).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12883-026-05096-w.Keywords: Stroke, AIS, Acute ischemic stroke, Early neurological deterioration, END, Thrombolysis, Risk prediction models

Introduction

Stroke is a critical global health concern and remains the second leading cause of death as well as the third most common cause of combined death and disability worldwide, with the majority of the stroke burden borne by China [1]. Ischemic stroke constitutes the largest proportion of incident strokes. Among treatments for acute ischemic stroke (AIS), IVT plays a significant role due to its function in systemic reperfusion [2], which is considered a key step in improving functional outcomes within a few hours after symptom onset. Appropriate patient selection and timely treatment have been shown to be crucial factors for better functional outcomes, quality of life, and neurological recovery [3]. Unfortunately, fewer than 5% of AIS patients in China receive IVT, primarily due to missing the optimal treatment window [4]. Furthermore, IVT does not always result in favorable outcomes; approximately 10% of patients undergoing thrombolysis may experience early neurological deterioration (END) [5].

END in patients with AIS following IVT is a common and serious complication, typically characterized by a decline in neurological function occurring within hours or days after treatment. The occurrence of END significantly increases the risk of severe disability and mortality [6]. As an operational concept, the definition of END varies depending on the clinical scale, cutoff values, and timing of assessment, with no formal consensus established to date. For instance, Saleem defined END as an increase of ≥ 2 points on the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score after stroke [7], whereas Simonsen described END as an increase of 4 or more points on the NIHSS within the first 24 h post-stroke [8]. Regardless of the definition applied, END is consistently associated with poorer functional outcomes and greater dependency. Early identification and prediction of END in AIS patients after IVT can enable healthcare providers to implement timely interventions and reduce the risk of further neurological decline. Nurses play a pivotal role in early neurological assessment, continuous monitoring, prompt escalation, and the implementation of preventive measures following intravenous thrombolysis. Therefore, it is essential to identify predictive factors for END in AIS patients after IVT.

Several complex mechanisms contribute to the development of END, including failure of collateral circulation, clot progression, recurrent stroke, increased intracranial pressure, seizures, and hemorrhagic transformation [9]. Previous studies have identified diabetes, hypertension, hyperuricemia, hypertriglyceridemia, and elevated homocysteine levels as known predictors of END [6, 10, 11]. Currently, END risk prediction models for AIS patients include the WORSEN score and the CNS-LAND score. The WORSEN score, proposed by Miyamoto et al. [12], and the CNS-LAND score, proposed by Jin et al. [13], were developed earlier to assess the risk of END in AIS patients. As new knowledge has emerged, numerous models predicting the risk of END in AIS patients after IVT have been developed. However, the methodological rigor and reporting quality of these models have not been systematically reviewed.

This study aims to systematically review the existing END risk prediction models developed and published for AIS patients following IVT. The findings will provide a theoretical foundation for clinical practice and future research.

Methods

This study protocol was registered on PROSPERO (register number: CRD42025628890).

Search strategy

We aimed to identify all studies by searching the China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), Wanfang Database, China Science and Technology Journal Database (VIP), PubMed, Web of Science, The Cochrane Library, and Embase from their inception to December 20, 2025. Relevant keywords and search terms included “stroke,” “AIS,” “acute ischemic stroke,” “early neurological deterioration,” “END,” “thrombolysis,” and “risk prediction models.” Further details on the search terms used are provided in the supplementary materials. Additionally, the reference lists of relevant articles were reviewed.

The PICOTS framework, as recommended by the Critical Appraisal and Data Extraction for Systematic Reviews of Prediction Modelling Studies (CHARMS) checklist, was applied to guide the conduct of this systematic review. The key components of the review are presented as follows:

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) studies involving patients with AIS who underwent IVT alone; (2) observational study design; (3) reporting a prediction model; and (4) END as the outcome. Studies were excluded if they: (1) did not develop a prediction model; (2) were not published in English or Chinese; or (3) had no accessible full text. (4) were clinical guidelines, case reports, consensus documents, clinical trials and reviews, conferences and congress abstracts.

Study selection and screening

Two reviewers independently conducted the study selection process. Duplicate records were first removed, followed by screening of titles and abstracts to identify potentially eligible studies. The full texts of these studies were then assessed according to the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Any discrepancies in study selection were revolved through discussion with the third reviewer until consensus was achieved.

Data extraction

Data extracted from the included studies were grouped into two categories: (1) Study characteristics, including author, publication year, study design, participants, data sources, and sample size. (2) Model characteristics, including the variable selection method, model development approach, validation type, model performance indicators, final predictors, and model presentation format. Particular attention was given to predictors relevant to nursing assessment and bedside monitoring, such as neurological scores, vital signs, and laboratory indicators. Data extraction was performed by one reviewer and cross-checked by a second reviewer to ensure accuracy and consistency.

Quality assessment

The methodological quality and potential risk of bias of the included studies were assessed using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) approach [14] and the available version of the Prediction Model Risk of Bias Assessment Tool (PROBAST) [15]. GRADE evaluates the quality of evidence based on six domains: study design, risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and other factors such as publication bias. PROBAST assesses risk of bias across four domains: participants, predictors, outcomes, and analysis.

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

A meta-analysis was conducted on the area under the curve (AUC) values reported by the included prediction models using Stata 17.0 (StataCorp, College Station, USA). Heterogeneity was quantified using the I² statistic, and publication bias was assessed with Egger’s test [16].

Results

Study selection

Figure 1 presents the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 flowchart, illustrating the comprehensive search process and results. The initial search yielded a total of 2,359 indexed records. After removing 620 duplicate records identified across all databases, 1,739 titles and abstracts were screened for eligibility. Following this screening, 74 articles were selected for further evaluation. During the subsequent assessment, 21 studies were excluded because they did not establish prediction models or focused solely on risk factors. Additionally, 11 studies were excluded due to inconsistencies with the review population: five studies involved populations that did not receive thrombolysis, two studies included fewer than two predictors, three studies reported outcomes limited to subgroups, and ten studies were reviews or conference proceedings. Ultimately, 18 studies encompassing 18 models were included in this study.

Study characteristics

Table 1 presents the design and participant characteristics of the 18 included studies. These studies were published between 2021 and 2024, with 17 conducted in China (eight published in Chinese) and one conducted in France. Among the included studies, two were prospective—one of which was a multicenter study—while the remaining studies were retrospective, including three multicenter studies. All studies focused on patients with acute ischemic stroke who received intravenous thrombolysis. Two studies specifically defined stroke subtypes: one targeted elderly patients with mild strokes, and another focused on patients with acute minor stroke and large vessel occlusion. Additionally, we summarized the various definitions of END used across the studies. The most common definition of END was an increase of more than 4 points in the NIHSS score within 24 h of thrombolysis compared to the admission score. Sample sizes ranged from 120 to 9,141 participants.

Model validation

Among the included studies, 12 performed internal validation, two conducted both internal and external validation, while four did not undergo any validation after development.

Results of quality assessment

According to GRADE, all studies included in this review were evaluated as having high certainty. Table 4 illustrates the risk of bias and applicability among the selected studies. In the participant domain, most studies were considered to have a high risk of bias due to inappropriate data sources. In the predictor domain, every study had a low risk of bias. In the outcome domain, six studies were found to have a high risk of bias because they did not report the consistency of outcome definition and ascertainment [17–23]. Additionally, all studies were evaluated as having a high risk of bias overall. Among them, 14 studies [13, 18–20, 23–32] had insufficient sample sizes, failing to meet the recommended guideline of more than 20 events per variable (EPV). Eleven studies [18–21, 24–28, 30, 33] did not report appropriate methods for addressing missing data. Two studies did not fully evaluate the predictive performance of their models [13, 18]. Five studies did not adequately address model overfitting or underfitting in model performance [19, 26, 27, 33, 34]. Four studies did not provide information on internal or external model validation of participant data [20, 24, 28, 32], and none of the studies accounted for complexities in the data. Regarding the applicability assessment of the 18 included studies, two were classified as having a high risk. In the participant domain, the inclusion criteria in these two studies were limited to specific subgroups of acute ischemic stroke [26, 33]. However, all models demonstrated good applicability in the other two domains (predictor and outcome domains).

Meta-analysis of validation models included in the review

Only 15 studies were eligible for synthesis due to insufficient reporting of model details. Among these, three studies employed multiple methods for model development using the same sample. The pooled AUC was calculated using a random-effects model, yielding a value of 0.87 (95% Confidence Interval: 0.84–0.91) (Fig. 2). However, there was a high degree of heterogeneity among the included studies (I² = 90.3%, P < 0.001).

Discussion

It is crucial to effectively assess the potential risk factors of END, as this can help medical staff understand the causal mechanisms and thereby prevent END. In this study, we identified 18 studies involving 18 models for predicting END, most of which are based on data from Chinese patients. According to the PROBAST checklist, all studies were evaluated as having a high risk of bias. The pooled area under the curve (AUC) value of the 15 validated models included in the meta-analysis was 0.87 (95% confidence interval: 0.84–0.91). However, the heterogeneity among these models was high, which may be related to differences in the definitions of END, included predictors, and methodologies.

The process of model development offers valuable insights. Firstly, machine learning algorithms were widely incorporated into the model development in the included studies. As a powerful analytical approach, machine learning delivers superior predictive accuracy and robust model performance, demonstrating significant potential for prognostic refinement and optimized clinical decision-making. Second, several high-quality studies adopted rigorous methodological designs. Wen et al. [34] enrolled a large sample from the Shenyang Stroke Emergency Map database, adopted the multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) approach to handle missing data, and conducted external validation to ensure broad applicability. This methodological step is frequently overlooked in other predictive studies. Besides, Gao et al. [21] conducted a prospective, multicenter, large-scale registry study with thorough assessments of model calibration and discrimination. Notably, they adopted SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis to improve the interpretability of black-box machine learning models and visualized SHAP values via a web-based application. Seners et al. further strengthened methodological robustness by recruiting two large multicenter cohorts and adopting standardized END definitions consistent with the European Cooperative Acute Stroke Study criteria [33]. Overall, most established END models achieved moderate to good performance; however, the overall risk of bias remained high across the included studies. Substantial heterogeneity was also observed, mainly attributable to inconsistent END definitions, and diverse modeling and validation strategies. Therefore, future model development should prioritize standardized data collection and rigorous data processing, unified outcome definition, and optimized study design for model construction, internal validation and external validation.

This study offers several important clinical implications. First, a high baseline NIHSS score was the most frequently documented factor predicting END, as it typically indicates more severe neurological deficits and is associated with an increased risk of malignant cerebral edema and hemorrhagic transformation [35]. Although the NIHSS provides a standardized measure of stroke severity, it is less sensitive to posterior circulation strokes, which may result in underrecognition of neurological impairments [36]. Therefore, acute stroke emergency management should prioritize comprehensive neurological examinations rather than relying solely on a single scoring scale, especially in cases of suspected posterior circulation stroke. Since nurses play a central role in ongoing neurological monitoring, integrating END risk prediction models into nursing practice could enhance early risk identification, support timely escalation of care, and promote safer, more effective patient management.

In addition, several examination and laboratory indicators—such as fasting plasma glucose (FPG), white blood cell (WBC) count, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), and systolic blood pressure (SBP)—were identified as independent predictors of END. One possible reason FPG affects END is that it can exacerbate anaerobic glycolysis, leading to intracellular acidosis in brain tissue and resulting in new infarctions in hypoperfused brain areas. WBC counts likely play a significant role in the occurrence of END by releasing various inflammatory substances that participate in the clotting process, predisposing patients to thrombus formation. Excessive WBC aggregation can simultaneously hinder the recovery of cerebral blood flow after IVT [21]. A high NLR indicates that the proinflammatory activity of neutrophils may surpass the regulatory function of lymphocytes, creating an environment conducive to uncontrolled peripheral inflammation spreading to a vulnerable brain [37]. Given that these metabolic, inflammatory, and hemodynamic indicators are routinely monitored in clinical practice, nurses play a pivotal role in early risk detection and ongoing patient surveillance. Integrating END risk prediction tools into nursing workflows may support proactive risk stratification, guide timely escalation of care, and facilitate targeted interventions such as glucose control, blood pressure optimization, and inflammatory risk monitoring—ultimately contributing to improved neurological outcomes and patient safety.

Demographic, clinical, and imaging-related factors—including advanced age, higher body mass index (BMI), concomitant atrial fibrillation (AF), and a positive hyperdense middle cerebral artery sign (HMCAS)—were associated with an increased risk of END. These factors likely reflect underlying vascular vulnerability, cardioembolic burden, and the severity of large-vessel occlusion, all of which predispose patients to rapid neurological decline. Additionally, smoking history, previous stroke, prolonged onset-to-treatment time (OTT), and extended onset-to-needle time (ONT) were significantly linked to END, underscoring the critical roles of cumulative vascular risk and timely reperfusion therapy in neurological outcomes. Collectively, these findings reinforce the multifactorial nature of END and highlight the need for a more nuanced and integrative approach to risk assessment. Given the complex interplay among patient characteristics, vascular pathology, treatment timeliness, and inflammatory and metabolic status, comprehensive and dynamic risk prediction tools are essential to support individualized clinical decision-making.

This meta-analysis highlights several implications for clinical practice and future research. The relatively high pooled AUC suggests that existing models have potential for predicting END in patients with acute ischemic stroke after intravenous thrombolysis. However, substantial heterogeneity and high risk of bias, along with the predominance of Chinese populations, limit the reliability and generalizability of these findings. Future studies should focus on improving methodological quality, including adequate sample size estimation, standardized handling of missing data, and robust external validation. Standardization of END definitions and outcome measures is also essential to enhance comparability across studies. Furthermore, integrating key clinical, laboratory, and imaging predictors, particularly through machine learning and multimodal data may improve predictive performance. Such advancements could facilitate early risk stratification, support personalized clinical decision-making, and ultimately improve neurological outcomes.

Limitations

This review has several potential limitations. First, most of the included studies were conducted in China, which may limit the generalizability of the prediction models to Western populations. It is essential for future research to develop risk prediction models for END in diverse populations. Second, due to differences in reporting transparency and methodology among the included studies, it is difficult to recommend one predictive model over another. Therefore, more rigorous methodologies and greater transparency in reporting are needed in future research.

Conclusion

This review includes 18 studies encompassing 18 models. The pooled area under the curve (AUC) of 15 validated models was 0.87 (95% CI: 0.84–0.91), indicating a moderate level of discrimination. According to PROBAST, all included studies were found to have a high risk of bias. Currently, prediction models for END in acute ischemic stroke patients do not meet PROBAST standards. Therefore, future prediction model development should involve larger sample sizes and more rigorous research designs. Although existing END prediction models demonstrate moderate discrimination, nurse-centered risk assessment tools and standardized monitoring protocols are essential to improve early detection and prevention of neurological deterioration.
