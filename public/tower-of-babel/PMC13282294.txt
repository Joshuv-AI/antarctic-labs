Adverse events and device malfunctions in robotic thyroidectomy: a decade-long analysis of the MAUDE database
Authors: Ling Zhan, Xianzhao Deng, Qixian Lin, Bojun Song, Zihan Lin, Lingzi Pan, Zhonghui Wu, Zhihong Wang, Zheng Ding, Bo Wu, Bomin Guo, Youben Fan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Robotic thyroidectomy (RT) has gained increasing patient interest and clinical adoption in recent years. However, device-related adverse events and malfunctions remain poorly characterized. A review of the Manufacturer and User Facility Device Experience (MAUDE) database was conducted using the keyword “robotic thyroidectomy” between January 2016 and December 2025. Eligible reports were manually reviewed and classified by malfunctioning domain, intraoperative management, clinical consequence, procedural outcome, post-event manufacturer evaluation, and reporting timeliness. Thirty-two reports were included, and all events occurred intraoperatively. Adjunct energy device accounted for the majority of reports (68.8%), followed by robotic system (25.0%) and access/interface component (6.3%). The most frequently described event pattern was fragment/breakage. Device/component exchange (56.3%) and fragment retrieval/removal (46.9%) were common management measures. Despite these events, 25 procedures (78.1%) were completed robotically as planned, three (9.4%) were completed under downgraded robotic conditions and only one (3.1%) required open conversion. No severe patient injury was noted. In terms of reporting timeliness, the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) received 13 reports (40.6%) over 30 days after the event occurred. Meanwhile, four of the 31 evaluable reports (12.9%) exceeded the manufacturer-to-FDA reporting window of 30 days. Although RT-related MAUDE reports were limited and most events appeared manageable, the associated surgical burden may include workflow disruption, repeated troubleshooting, device exchange, and downgraded robotic continuation. These findings highlight the need for RT-specific risk awareness, balanced patient counseling, operative preparedness, structured troubleshooting pathways and timely, standardized post-market surveillance.

Introduction

Robotic platforms have expanded the technical possibilities of minimally invasive surgery by offering greater precision, three-dimensional visualization, and enhanced manipulation [1]. At the same time, robotic systems, robot arms and instruments also introduce additional surgical complexity. This can result in technical failure, instrument-related issues or surgeon misuse, which could lead to surgical delay, procedural interruption or conversion, or even patient harm. Although previous studies have indicated that overall rates of injury, malfunction and conversion related to the robotic approach are extremely low [2], some procedures require closer examination due to their specific anatomical and procedural features. Robotic thyroidectomy (RT) is one such procedure. During thyroid surgery, the confined operative field in the neck and the close proximity of the recurrent laryngeal nerve (RLN), trachea, esophagus, and major vessels mean that even minor technical disruption can have serious consequences [3–5]. Furthermore, unplanned open conversion completely negates the defining cosmetic advantages of RT and fails to meet patient expectations [1]. The existing RT literature, which is largely based on institutional case series, has primarily focused on the feasibility, oncological efficacy, and clinical outcomes of the treatment. These studies typically report on operative time, length of hospital stay, number of retrieved lymph nodes, cosmetic satisfaction, and conventional complications such as RLN injury and hypoparathyroidism [6, 7]. In contrast, device- and component-related complications, which are relatively rare, are difficult to capture and have not been systematically described. As RT becomes more widely adopted, it is crucial to develop a clearer understanding of the risks associated with the relevant devices and components, in order to enable informed decision-making, preoperative consultations, surgical preparation, and risk management.

The United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) Manufacturer and User Facility Device Experience (MAUDE) database is a post-market surveillance platform that collects reports on medical device malfunctions and their impact on patients [8]. Although MAUDE is a passive surveillance system and is therefore susceptible to under-reporting and incomplete reporting, it can provide valuable insight into the real-world safety profiles of devices that are difficult to characterize in institutional clinical series. Accordingly, several studies have used the MAUDE database to examine adverse events related to the robotic approach in various surgical settings [9–11]. However, a dedicated analysis of RT remains lacking. The present study therefore aimed to characterize adverse event reports focused on RT recorded in the MAUDE database over the past decade, with particular attention to event types, procedural consequences, documented patient harm, and reporting timeliness. These findings could inform RT-specific operative preparedness, team training, structured troubleshooting pathways, preoperative counseling, and the timeliness and quality of post-market reporting.

Methods

Search term and criteria for inclusion

A search was performed on the MAUDE database (https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/scripts/cdrh/cfdocs/cfmaude/search.cfm) inspecting for RT safety profiles generated between January 2016 and December 2025 using the keyword “robotic thyroidectomy”. The initial search identified 56 adverse event reports over the past decade. Of these, 24 reports were subsequently excluded as follows: six were duplicate entries, eight were irrelevant, one could not be attributed to a device or use problem, one had conflicting narrative content, and one due to insufficient information. Seven reports were excluded as they were submitted by manufacturers who had noticed them in publications with no mention of, or proof of, device-related malfunction. A total of 32 events remained for further analyses (Fig. 1).

Event classification

All MAUDE reports were manually reviewed and categorized by two investigators (L. Z. and B. S.). Any inconsistencies were resolved through discussion, and a third reviewer (Q. L.) was consulted when necessary. The coding framework was adapted from previous similar studies [2, 9, 12, 13]. Each report was reviewed for event number, date of occurrence, date reported, timing of the issue, event description, management, procedural outcome, and post-event manufacturer evaluation. Event descriptions were analyzed further for failure features. Two reporting intervals were evaluated. Firstly, the real-world reporting delay was calculated as the interval between the event date and the date FDA initially received the report. Secondly, the manufacturer-to-FDA reporting lag was calculated as the interval between the date manufacturer was notified of the event and the date FDA receipt of the report [14].

Malfunctioning domain was categorized as robotic system, access/interface component, or adjunct energy device. Robotic system included the surgeon console, visualization system, robotic arm/manipulator, and electrosurgical unit or generator. Access/interface components included drape, trocar, and related interface items. Adjunct energy device category referred to non-native energy devices used during RT and was further categorized by energy type as ultrasonic, monopolar, or bipolar.

Intraoperative management measures were recorded according to the responses taken after the event. These included device/component exchange, fragment retrieval/removal, a workaround measure, no effective intraoperative resolution or not stated. These categories were not mutually exclusive. More than one management measure could be implemented in a single report when applicable. Device/component exchange entailed replacement of the affected operative component or device, including a robotic instrument, adjunct energy device, console-related component, electrosurgical/generator unit or access/interface component. A workaround measure referred to adjustments made during surgery to continue the procedure despite the malfunction.

Reports were further reviewed to identify whether patient harm had occurred, and whether subsequent treatment or intervention was required. Clinical consequence was assessed separately from the original FDA reporting classification. This is particularly true for fragment-related events. Reports initially submitted under the injury-related category could later describe successful retrieval without clearly documented patient harm, as determined by the narrative description and follow-up.

Procedural outcome was classified as follows: completed robotically as planned; completed robotically with a downgraded configuration or workaround; converted to another approach; or not clearly stated. A downgraded strategy referred to a procedure that was completed robotically, but under a reduced or altered configuration due to the malfunction rather than under the original setup. Conversion was defined as an unintended change from the planned robotic approach to another operative approach, such as open or endoscopic surgery.

Inter-rater reliability was assessed for classification using Cohen’s kappa [15]. The mean kappa value was 0.85, indicating good agreement. Ethics approval was waived for this retrospective analysis of the publicly available, anonymized reports from the MAUDE database.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.1). Figures were generated using R software (version 4.5.1) and GraphPad Prism (version 10.3.1). Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables were summarized as median with interquartile range (IQR).

Results

A total of 32 RT-focused MAUDE reports from the past decade were included in the final analyses. All events were detected intra-procedurally. The annual distribution of reports was shown in Fig. 2. Since the total number of RT performed during the study period was unknown, the percentages in this study only reflected the distribution of reported cases, not the true incidence. The median real-world reporting delay was 28 days (IQR, 24.3–33.0; range, 16–697 days). Thirteen reports (40.63%) were received by FDA more than 30 days after the event occurred. Manufacturer-to-FDA reporting lag was evaluable in 31 reports, as one report lacked the manufacturer notification date. The median reporting lag was 27 days (IQR, 24.0–28.0; range, 10–685 days), and four of the 31 reports (12.9%) were submitted outside of the 30-day regulatory deadline.

Of the three events related to the robotic arm/manipulator, one report described unintended motion of the universal surgical manipulator (USM) 3, which moved in the opposite direction. Although the issue was said to persist after a power cycle, no further details were provided regarding the subsequent intraoperative course. Nevertheless, the procedure was reported to have been completed robotically, using the same system configuration, with no documented patient harm. Later on-site evaluation did not reproduce the malfunction. The second case reported a loss of movement in USM 3. Although power cycling the system restored basic function, the site elected to continue without USM 3, and classified it as a downgraded robotic continuation. The post-event investigation confirmed logged communication-related errors. However, repeated field evaluations and failure analyses could not reproduce the malfunction, and the root cause remained undetermined. The only conversion in the cohort occurred during the third manipulator-related event, characterized by repeated instrument engagement/recognition errors involving USM 1. Two different instruments were tried without improvement before converting to open surgery. The root cause remained uncertain. Improper drape seating was suspected as a possible contributing factor, but this could not be confirmed. The system reportedly functioned normally after the case.

Access/interface events were uncommon. One event was drape-related and presented as persistent insertion resistance when using multiple instruments on the same USM. Troubleshooting involved replacing the cannula seal, reinstalling the adapters, redocking, and rebooting the system. Final resolution was only achieved after replacing the instrument arm drape. The procedure was completed robotically as planned, with no patient injury documented. The other event involved detached pieces of a broken trocar that were retrieved during surgery.

Of the 22 adjunct energy device reports, 21 involved ultrasonic energy devices, and one involved a bipolar energy device. The most common malfunction pattern was fragment/breakage. Examples included detached Teflon pads, broken curved blades, broken probe tips, and missing pieces. In most cases, the fragments were successfully retrieved without any clearly documented patient injury. However, two reports were classified as clinically unresolved fragment events due to unretrieved melted/powder-like material, which may have resulted from thermal decomposition. No additional surgical procedure or postoperative imaging was performed to confirm clearance, and no subsequent complications related to a retained foreign object were reported. Moreover, structural damage was sometimes accompanied by functional failure of the instrument. One report noted intraoperative bleeding. According to the description, the Harmonic scalpel malfunctioned during coagulation after tissue incision. Bleeding ensued, and hemostasis was achieved using another instrument. Post-event evaluation confirmed a broken blade. The bleeding was reportedly under control. No transfusion or additional intervention was required, and no postoperative complications were documented.

Intraoperative management measures were categorized in Table 1; Fig. 4A. Sometimes, more than one management response was implemented in a single report. Device/component exchange was the most frequently documented response, occurring in 18 reports (56.3%). This mainly involved replacement of adjunct energy device, but also included exchange of generator/electrosurgical unit, console substitution, and replacement of an arm drape. Fragment retrieval/removal was recorded in 15 reports (46.9%). Notably, four reports (12.5%) had no effective intraoperative resolution. These included two instances of melted detached pieces from the Harmonic scalpel that could not be retrieved, one instance of robotic arm/manipulator recognition failure that led to an open conversion, and one adjunct energy device event in which the problem recurred despite the exchange. Management was not clearly illustrated in three reports (9.4%).

Clinical consequences were shown in Fig. 4B. The most common consequence was retrieved fragment without documented injury, noted in 15 reports (46.9%). Fourteen reports (43.8%) stated that there was no patient injury. Despite these events, 78.1% of procedures were ultimately completed robotically as planned, while 9.4% were completed robotically under downgraded conditions (Fig. 4C). Post-event manufacturer evaluation confirmed 26 cases of malfunction or device damage (81.3%), while five reports (15.6%) remained unreproducible or undetermined after field service or failure analysis. One malfunctioning device (3.1%) was not returned and therefore could not be formally evaluated (Fig. 4D).

Discussion

As RT becomes more widely adopted, it is increasingly important to characterize the malfunctions and adverse events reported in association with this approach. Overall, institutional robotic surgical data suggest a generally reassuring safety profile, with reported device malfunction rates ranging from 0.4% to 8.0%, and failure-related conversion rates ranging from 0.1% to 2.7% [10]. One institutional series of robotic general surgery reported three malfunctions during trans-axillary thyroid procedures: Harmonic scalpel assembly difficulty, blurred vision due to focusing dysfunction, and Cadière forceps malocclusion. All of these were managed without adverse patient consequences or open conversion [16]. As these events have rarely been evaluated in conventional RT cohorts or institutional-based studies, the MAUDE database offers a complementary source for identifying safety signals related to devices or components. Furthermore, previous MAUDE-based analyses of robotic head and neck procedures have indicated that despite accounting for only a small proportion of reported adverse events, injury, death, and conversion rates within this category are relatively high [2, 10, 17]. This pattern may be partly attributed to features specific to RT. Unlike abdominal procedures performed within a natural cavity, RT is conducted through remote access routes within a surgically created and anatomically constrained working space [1]. The proximity of vital structures and vessels, together with the inherent procedural complexity, means that visualization loss or unintended motion can amplify the impact of minor intraoperative disruptions [4, 18]. Additionally, the limited haptic feedback during the robotic surgery makes troubleshooting more dependent on visual judgement, which is particularly challenging when visualization is impaired or complex manipulation is required. Therefore, further investigation of RT-focused data is warranted.

A review of the MAUDE database found 32 reports of adverse events during RT over the past decade. All of these occurred intraoperatively. Only one case required open conversion, and no severe patient injury was noted. However, conversion alone does not adequately reflect the surgical burden of these events. In the present study, the surgical burden related to malfunctions was conceptualized as the consequence of a device- or component-related event at the workflow level. To facilitate future safety analyses and improvements, the associated burden can be evaluated across several dimensions: troubleshooting demands, including repeated system checks, power cycling, redocking and seeking technical support; equipment burden, including device or component exchange and mobilization of backup instruments; field-management burden, including fragment searching, retrieval, or additional manipulation; and procedural-deviation burden, including downgraded robotic continuation, compromised system configuration, conversion, abandonment of the robotic approach, or unresolved intraoperative failure. This framework could help to capture clinically meaningful disruptions that would otherwise be ignored in conventional outcome reporting.

As in previous analyses, robot-core failures were uncommon [2, 10]. Nevertheless, when robotic system errors occurred, the procedure was often completed using workaround strategies rather than restoring normal function. In our series, these measures included downgraded continuation under monocular visualization, proceeding without one robotic arm, electrosurgical unit replacement, and console substitution. The only open conversion occurred due to persistent instrument engagement/recognition failure, despite several attempts to change instruments. These cases highlight that some system-level issues cannot be resolved quickly or easily through troubleshooting.

Not all events were related to failures of the robotic core platform. A notable finding of our study was the high proportion of events relating to fragments of adjunct devices and accessories. Although these devices are not core robotic components, such events are still directly relevant to RT as they occurred during surgery and could affect the procedural course. Similar patterns have been reported in previous MAUDE-based robotic safety studies. For example, Alemzadeh H et al. reported that broken or burnt pieces falling into the patient’s body accounted for 14.7% of adverse events over a 14-year study period, while Hsieh TY et al. reported a proportion approaching one-fifth over the past decade [2, 10]. Searching for and retrieving detached pieces may require additional field readjustment and instrument manipulation, which could increase the risk of instrument conflict, inadvertent traction, and thermal exposure [19]. Beyond the retrieval process itself, these events may necessitate instrument or accessory exchange, extra adjustments, and the coordination of additional personnel, all of which may have time and cost implications. Therefore, even when most detached pieces were retrieved without documented patient injury, the associated workflow burden should not be underestimated. These considerations emphasize the need for ready access to replacement devices, technical support, and other backup resources [12, 20]. The two reports describing melted or powder-like material that could not be retrieved intact highlight a further distinct concern. Although no immediate injury was documented, such dispersed material raises concerns about complete intraoperative clearance and delayed local consequences.

Another important point is that some post-event narratives suggested that the damage was related to use conditions, such as contact with an energized device or handling issues, rather than being caused by a manufacturing defect. Consistent with previous findings, adverse events in robotic surgery are often multifactorial, involving device performance, procedural complexity, setup conditions, and team response [10, 21]. Correa J et al. reported that 4% of malfunction events were due to misuse of the robotic system by surgeons or staff [9]. Two other studies found that operator-related factors accounted for 7.1% and 21% of injury reports, respectively [10, 22]. In such scenarios, the difference between a manageable interruption and an escalated event may depend on how quickly the operating team recognizes the problem, rules out malfunctions, mobilizes backup equipment, and determines whether to continue, downgrade, or convert. Therefore, the robotic skill training program should extend well beyond the console. It should also include structured preoperative system checks, troubleshooting techniques, familiarity with common failure types and replacement plans, and team-based rehearsal for emergency undocking and rapid conversion when necessary. Caution must be exercised as early as the setup phase because seemingly trivial steps in docking, drape positioning, or system preparation may compromise subsequent manipulation and workflow [19]. In this regard, analyses based on the MAUDE data could inform the development of standardized RT-specific malfunction checklists, as well as the incorporation of potential device failure scenarios into the formal robotic curriculum. Understanding recurring technical failure patterns can also provide insights into the design of future robotic systems with improved human-machine interfaces and safety mechanisms [10].

These findings also have implications for preoperative counseling and expectation management. Concerns about robotic malfunction have been identified in patient perception surveys [1]. However, counseling patients about the potential mechanical malfunctions of robotic surgery is still not routinely incorporated into preoperative discussions. According to an international survey, only 68.8% of surgeons provided such information during consultation [23]. Practically communicating these risks can be challenging, and the discussion should not focus solely on exact probabilities. Instead, preoperative communication should be framed around risk acknowledgement and preparedness. Surgeons should acknowledge that rare technical problems can occur and explain that most issues are manageable intraoperatively through timely troubleshooting, backup resources, and team-based response plans. Since one of the main reasons patients opt for RT is to avoid a cervical incision [1], patients should be clearly informed that, in rare circumstances, technical issues may require a downgrade or open conversion, which would negate the intended cosmetic advantage. By setting realistic expectations and addressing undue anxiety, surgeons can foster informed trust and improve postoperative satisfaction.

Reporting timeliness is another important issue highlighted by this study. In terms of reporting delays, FDA received 13 reports (40.6%) more than 30 days after the occurrence. Four of the 31 evaluable reports (12.9%) were submitted exceeded manufacturer’s 30-day reporting window. This suggests that device safety information may not become publicly available immediately following an incident. Even when the median manufacturer-to-FDA reporting lag was within the 30-day regulatory deadline, the wide range, including one prolonged lag of over 600 days, suggests substantial late reporting in individual MAUDE records. These findings should be interpreted cautiously, given the small sample size and possible inconsistencies in the date fields. Nevertheless, they underscore the importance of timely and standardized reporting for reliable post-market surveillance. Delayed adverse event reporting may impede the early detection of patient safety concerns. To strengthen the role of MAUDE as a surveillance tool, reporting should not only rely on manufacturers, but also involve healthcare professionals and hospital systems proactively. Submissions should be more accurate, timely, and standardized, providing sufficient clinical detail, contextual information and follow-up. Where feasible, they should also be supported by on-site evaluations. Such high-quality reporting would enable more credible root cause assessments and more informed responses regarding training, troubleshooting, preparedness, and safety improvement.

To our knowledge, this is the first decade-long, RT-focused evaluation of device-related adverse events and malfunctions reported to the MAUDE database. Such surveillance helps to alert surgeons to potential technical problems that may arise during RT. Although the true incidence of malfunctions cannot be determined from the MAUDE database, the retained reports were sparse, and most of the reported events appeared manageable or ultimately resolvable. Major system failure, severe injury, and conversion were uncommon. Instead, the practical burden lies in workflow disruption, repeated troubleshooting, downgraded treatment, equipment exchange, and hidden demands on operating room coordination and backup readiness. In addition, previous studies in other robotic surgical settings have reported that cases affected by malfunction may experience an additional delay of 20 to 25 min [11, 24]. These findings call for robust equipment maintenance, standardized training program, structured troubleshooting pathways, and rapid access to all kinds of support.

However, these findings should be considered within the well-recognized limitations of the MAUDE database [9, 14, 22, 25]. As a passive reporting system with limited enforcement policies, MAUDE is vulnerable to under-reporting, selective reporting, and incomplete or inaccurate information [26]. Furthermore, the narrative nature of the reports makes it difficult to precisely determine root causes, procedural consequences, and long-term clinical outcomes. Incomplete or inconsistent date fields may also have affected reporting lag evaluations. Although a specific and reproducible search strategy was maintained by using the single keyword “robotic thyroidectomy”, this may have resulted in under-ascertainment. Additionally, reports with insufficient details were excluded to improve case specificity. However, this decision may have introduced selection bias. Despite our efforts, misclassification and reviewer bias may still exist. Information that was often missing included the specific access route used (e.g., transoral, transbreast, etc.). This prevented approach-specific analysis. Furthermore, given the absence of patient-level data and institution-specific contextual factors, such as surgeon expertise and technical support availability, our findings should be regarded as descriptive and interpreted with caution. Nevertheless, MAUDE remains a valuable resource for identifying issues related to robotic platforms, devices, and accessories that might otherwise be overlooked in cohort series. Continued RT-focused surveillance is important for clarifying the technical challenges inherent to the approach and informing real-time troubleshooting.

Conclusion

This decade-long MAUDE database analysis identified a limited number of device-related events during RT, most of which were managed intraoperatively with rare conversion and no documented severe patient injury. However, such events can result in a certain surgical burden, including workflow disruption, repeated troubleshooting, device exchange, and downgraded robotic continuation. These findings highlight the importance of balanced preoperative counseling and patient expectation management. They may also provide a practical basis for developing an RT-specific malfunction checklist covering preoperative equipment checks, backup device availability, predefined troubleshooting steps, fragment management procedures, and criteria for procedural downgrade or conversion. In addition, the observation that 40.6% of reports were received by FDA more than 30 days after the event occurred emphasizes the need for timely and standardized post-market reporting to improve the reliability of device safety surveillance.
