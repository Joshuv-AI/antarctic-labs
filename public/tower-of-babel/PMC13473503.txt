Physics-informed neural networks with caputo-fabrizio derivatives for nonlinear fractal-fractional delay equations and chaotic systems
Authors: Hafsa Khan, Muhammad Sulaiman, Ghaylen Laouini, Izaz Ur Rahman, Mohammed Abdullah Salman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Complex dynamical systems, ranging from anomalous diffusion and memristor circuits to convective fluid motion in rotating cavities, exhibit non-local memory, long-range interactions, and proportional delays governed by Fractal-Fractional Differential Equations (FFDEs). This study introduces PINN-CF, a Physics-Informed Neural Network framework that integrates Caputo-Fabrizio (CF) Fractional Derivatives with the Adam optimizer. The CF operator is accurately approximated using the L1 finite-difference scheme. Unlike standard PINN that relay on singular kernel fractional operators, PINN-CF employs a non-singular exponential kernel, which avoids initial singularities and better capture smooth memory decay. PINN-CF accurately solves four benchmark problems of FFDEs, Pantograph-type delay equations, Memristor-based neural circuits, and FFDE systems of convective fluid motion in rotating cavities. The suggested PINN-CF method achieves residual errors below \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:1{0}^{-4}$$\end{document}, and as low as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:1{0}^{-6}$$\end{document} in near classical regimes. Reproducibility and validation ensured by convergence curves, and tabulated results. Offering superior flexibility, scalability, and computational efficiency over traditional numerical solvers, PINN-CF is readily extendable to nonlinear, multi-delay, distributed-order, or stochastic models.

Introduction

Fractional calculus1 has been extensively explored to model various phenomena such as epidemic childhood diseases2,3, Glucose molecule4, human liver5, COVID-19 transmission6, Within-Host SARS-CoV-27. A generalization of classical differential equations with arbitrary differentiation operator orders is known as a fractional differential equation. The reference provides some insightful findings about the theoretical study of these operators8. It is vital to understand that these operators are frequently single. Thus, getting analytic solutions to problems involving fractional operators is extremely challenging. In recent years, various numerical solutions have been presented to handle these challenges, including9–13.

Many researchers from practically all disciplines of science, technology, and engineering have been drawn to the notion of non-local operators of differentiation in recent years because of their ability to integrate more complicated natural phenomena into mathematical Eqs14,15. Three dominants were suggested in this field: the power law, the exponential decay law, and the extended Mittag-Leffler law. To identify them, various names were given to them, such as Caputo operators for power law or non-local and singular kernel types, Caputo-Fabrizio for non-singular local types, and Atangana-Balaena for non-local and non-singular types. The kernel Mittag-Leffler function proved to be more general than the power law and exponential decay functions16. As an outcome, Caputo and Caputo-Fabrizio are distinct Atangana-Balaena fractional operators. Atangana introduced the Fractal Fractional (FF) derivative operator with two parameters in 201716. The first parameter represents the fractional order, while the second is the fractal dimension. The fractal operators exhibit both memory effect and self-similarity qualities. Furthermore, the FF differentiation represents fractal kinetics, in which continuous time is substituted for fractal time. FF operators have an important role in many branches of science, such as the simulation of the Ebola virus17, a chaotic, different problem18, hierarchical and porous media19,20, banking and finance21,22, multi-dimensional electronic devices23, Newtonian and non-Newtonian fluids24,25, electronic circuit26–28, quantum particle29, nanofluid30, waterborne disease31. A number of articles have been published in this branch of science32,33, such as Araz et al.34 offered a numerical method for solving the FF integro-differential equation where the derivative is in the Atangana-Balaena sense, and the integral is in the Caputo sense. Sweilam et al.35 solved the FF optimal control problem via the nonstandard finite difference method. Heydari et al. in36 used the Legendre polynomials for solving nonlinear FF optimal control problems. Magin et al. in37 investigated the FF two-dimensional Emden-Fowler equation via the two-dimensional Chelyshkov polynomials. Dehestani et al.38 provided the Mott polynomials method for the numerical solution of FF two-dimensional Fredholm-Volterra integro-differential equations. Sabermahani et al. in39 introduced a numerical scheme based on the generalized Lucas wavelets method for solving nonlinear FF optimal control problems. Rahim Khani et al. in40 investigated the numerical treatment of FF 2D optimal control problems by the Müntz-Legendre polynomials. Rahim Khani et al.41 used the fractional shifted Morgan-Voyce neural networks to solve the FF pantograph differential equations. Khan et al.42 applied the two-step Lagrange polynomials method for solving a fractal-fractional type tuberculosis mathematical model.

Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs) remain the central modeling tool for approximating complex nonlinear mappings across various fields of science and engineering43–45, according to Dong et al.46. Modern deep neural networks, including convolutional, recurrent, graph, and transformer architectures, have revolutionized numerous fields47. Recent comprehensive reviews highlight the rapid evolution of physics-informed deep learning and its role in next-generation scientific computing48. A landmark advancement in this direction is the development of neural-type operators that learn mappings, such as those in infinite-dimensional function spaces49.

Domain-decomposition methods, including Finite Basis physics-informed neural networks (FBPINNs) have contributed greatly to the scaling of large-domain and stiff Differential Equations (DEs)50. Transfer-learning extensions of Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) have further enabled efficient solutions to high-frequency and multi-scale problems51. Specialized splitting techniques within the PINN framework have been successfully applied to infer the dynamics of both integer- and fractional-order neuron models52. Gradient-enhanced fractional PINNs have demonstrated superior performance on multiterm time-fractional Burger-type equations53. Advanced neural architectures have also been developed for systems of coupled fractional differential equations54 and for pricing problems governed by time-fractional Black–Scholes equations55.

The Caputo–Fabrizio fractional derivative with its non-singular exponential kernel was introduced as a physically consistent alternative to singular-kernel operators56. Despite progress, classical numerical schemes for fractional and fractal-fractional equations, whether implicit finite-difference57, wavelet collocation58, or spectral methods remain computationally expensive due to non-local memory terms, often scale poorly with time horizon, and struggle with stiffness and weak singularities59,60.

While the Vieta-Fibonacci polynomial (VFP) collocation method61 provides accurate approximations for FF differential equations and systems using a few basic functions \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:(e.g.,\:m=\mathrm{8,10,12})$$\end{document} across Caputo, Atangana-Balaena, and Caputo-Fabrizio operators, it faces limitations compared to Physics-Informed Neural Networks tailored for Caputo-Fabrizio derivatives (PINN-CF), including reliance on structured polynomial expansions and fixed collocation points that lead to ill-conditioning, sensitivity to basis selection, convergence only for moderate m values while potentially diverging for higher-order polynomials due to numerical instability in the algebraic systems, challenges in high-dimensional or irregular domains, and limited scalability for chaotic long-term dynamics without explicit error propagation from data noise. This restricts its applicability for long-term chaotic simulations or high-dimensional problems, highlighting a research gap in handling noisy data, inverse problems, and adaptive refinement, where PINN-CF excels through mesh-free, physics-embedded neural training for robust parameter inference and complex domains in applications like epidemics or fluid dynamics. For neural-network-based solvers the comprehensive convergence of deep Galerkin methods and physics-informed for a large class of differential equations provide solid validation53. Together with the ongoing limitations of the traditional discretization methods these convergence guarantees48–52 motivate the extension of a passionate, mesh-free framework of physics-informed modeling, explicitly tailored to the CF operator.

The classical Caputo derivative is defined with a power law kernel which is singular at initial time. This singularity has been used in some physical systems but can cause transient behavior. The Caputo Fabrizio (CF) operator is one of the fractional operators that utilizes the exponential kernel instead of singular kernel. For problems where the system’s memory declines efficiently, lacking an initial singularity making CF more physically reliable. Compared to the Atangana-Baleanu operator61, the CF operator is simpler computationally and still catching non-local behaviour accurately. The CF operator used in PIINN-CF also compared with PINN solutions which use the Caputo fractional operator. The comparison of both the operators (Caputo Fabrizio and Caputo fractional) shows that CF works better than simple Caputo operator. Therefore, to highlight the novelty of the study we adopt the CF derivative in this work to balance physical realism, computational efficiency and ease of integration into the PINN framework and tested with the three systems (Pantograph-type delay equations, Memristor-based neural circuits, and FFDE systems of convective fluid motion in rotating cavities). The PINN-CF, representing the neural solver that directly embeds the exact non-singular Caputo–Fabrizio derivative into the loss function via automatic differentiation. By eliminating explicit convolution, avoiding mesh dependence, and leveraging the convergence properties established in62 the proposed PINN-CF achieves higher accuracy, better stability, and significantly reduced computational cost on a range of fractional and fractal-fractional DEs with non-singular kernels.

Conclusion

This manuscript presented a new Physics-Informed Neural Network architecture, PINN-CF, which uses Caputo-Fabrizio fractal-fractional derivatives to solve nonlinear fractal-fractional equations. Numerical experimentation on four benchmark problems, pantograph-type delay equations, memristor-based neural circuits, and convective fluid motion in rotating cavities showed the proposed methodology to have an exceptional robustness and accuracy in the fractal-fractional parameter space, with residual errors in the standard regimes of the order of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10^{ - 4}$$\end{document} and much lower in the regimes where parameters were approaching classical integer-order behavior\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\:(\alpha\:,\:\beta\:\:\mathrm{a}\mathrm{p}\mathrm{p}\mathrm{r}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{c}\mathrm{h}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{s}\:1)$$\end{document}. Hence the PINN-CF maintained the accurate solution approximation even in strongly anomalous regimes \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\left(\alpha\:,\:\beta\:\:\approx\:\:0.3,\:0.5\right)$$\end{document}. By integrating L1-discretized Caputo-Fabrizio operators with automatic differentiation the framework precisely captures non-local memory phenomena without explicit computations. While the CF operator is also tested with the PINN solution using traditionally Caputo fractional operator. The comparison of both the operators shows that the CF operator works better than Caputo fractional operator, Parametric sensitivity studies indicate that reduction in fractional orders in systematic improvements of memory driven damping and delay effects, it would reasonably predict physically consistent behavior in all the cases studied. Also, PINN-CF has shown computational performance, and it is highly scalable to larger problem classes, including multi-delay systems, distributed-order fractional operators, and stochastic differential equations, making it an effective and general computational tool developed to study anomalous dynamical systems with possible applications in epidemiologic modeling, neuromorphic computing, and geophysical flow analysis.

Limitations and future directions

Although there are some limitations associated with the PINN-CF framework that should be taken into consideration and further research. Its methodology demonstrates less accuracy at very low fractional orders, at which a very strong effect of the memory capability of the network poses a challenge to its approximation capability. The sensitivity on hyperparameters must be carefully tuned to keep them unreachable by non-specialist users, and the sensitivity of training in highly chaotic regimes sometimes calls adaptive optimization methods. Also, the cost of computation of long time or high dimensional problems is now greater than optimized classical methods. On this basis, a number of real-world research opportunities are proposed, such as adaptive collocation sampling and domain decomposition to scale to larger problem sizes, Bayesian neural networks to quantify uncertainties in inverse problems, hybrid algorithm employing PINN-CF and spectral or wavelet analysis to scale effects, rigorous error analysis to establish convergence rates and stability limits, domain-specific enabling epidemiology, neuromorphic engineering, and geophysical fluid dynamics. The response to these directions will gradually turn PINN-CF into a highly capable proof-of-concept into a fully grown computational platform to study next generation research of fractional dynamical systems.
