Hybrid Policy Distillation for LLMs
Wenhong Zhu, Ruobing Xie, Rui Wang, Pengfei Liu

Abstract
Knowledge distillation (KD) is a powerful paradigm for compressing large language models (LLMs), whose effectiveness depends on intertwined choices of divergence direction, optimization strategy, and data regime. We break down the design of existing KD methods and present a unified view that establishes connections between them, reformulating KD as a reweighted log-likelihood objective at the token level. We further propose Hybrid Policy Distillation (HPD) , which integrates the complementary advantages of forward and reverse KL to balance mode coverage and mode-seeking, and combines off-policy data with lightweight, approximate on-policy sampling. We validate HPD on long-generation math reasoning as well as short-generation dialogue and code tasks, demonstrating improved optimization stability, computational efficiency, and final performance across diverse model families and scales. The code related to this work is available at https://github.com/zwhong714/Hybrid-Policy-Distillation .



1
Introduction

Recent advancements in large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated remarkable performance across text generation tasks
(
43
;
1
)
, largely driven by the massive scale of parameters and high-quality data
(
23
)
. However, such gains come at the cost of substantial computational and storage overhead
(
6
)
. Consequently, compressing LLMs by reducing the number of parameters while preserving their performance has become crucial for the practical deployment of these powerful models
(
13
)
.

Knowledge distillation (KD)
(
20
)
has emerged as a principled approach for transferring knowledge from powerful teacher models to lightweight student models. In black-box KD, the student is limited to the teacher’s generation, and distillation is typically carried out via supervised fine-tuning (SFT)
(
16
)
. In contrast, white-box KD can exploit access to the teacher’s predictive distributions, training the student to match the teacher through distribution-level objectives such as Kullback–Leibler divergence (KLD) on logits
(
24
)
. Recent studies on LLM distillation further emphasize the importance of selecting appropriate divergence measures for effective distribution matching
(
8
;
31
;
25
)
.

However, the choice of divergence alone is insufficient
(
51
)
. The behavior of divergence-based distillation for LLMs is governed by several tightly coupled design axes, including
(i)
the
direction
of divergence
(forward vs. reverse). In practice, effective distillation requires balancing the complementary inductive biases of forward and reverse KL
(
5
;
44
)
. Forward KL (FKLD) promotes mode coverage but often yields over-smoothed predictions
(
15
)
, whereas reverse KL (RKLD) emphasizes mode-seeking and distribution shaping, which can become unstable when the student–teacher gap is large
(
29
)
. (ii) The
optimization
strategy
of the divergence
(loss vs. reward), which leads to different optimization dynamics and gradient bias
(
3
;
38
)
.
(iii) The
data regime
(on-policy vs. off-policy). Off-policy distillation relies on an external data source that the student learns to imitate, but it often suffers from a train–inference behavior mismatch
(
2
)
. In contrast, on-policy distillation (OPD) samples rollouts from the student policy, which introduces a distribution shift on the teacher side for student-generated outputs and incurs substantial computational overhead
(
26
)
. These factors are often intertwined, jointly shaping the distillation behavior.

Motivated by these intertwined design axes, we first formalize a reweighted log-likelihood objective to analyze SFT, FKLD, and RKLD. Under this view, FKLD and RKLD exploit the teacher’s full predictive distribution to provide dense supervision, at the cost of losing the computational efficiency of one-hot targets used in SFT. We then propose
Hybrid Policy Distillation (HPD)
, which simultaneously accounts for both divergence directions and on-/off-policy data regimes by treating divergences as token-level reward signals, while retaining the efficiency of one-hot supervision. Specifically, HPD computes token-level reweighting signals using a negative
K_{1}
estimator
(
37
)
applied to both offline tokens and student-sampled tokens. For offline tokens, a positive value indicates that the ground-truth token is under-estimated (i.e., the student assigns it too low a probability) and triggers forward-KL learning, whereas negative values are used to suppress over-estimation. For student-sampled tokens, over-estimation is also penalized with suppressed probability mass redistributed toward the offline expert tokens.
This token-level hybridization combines the guidance of forward- and reverse-KL with the efficiency of one-hot supervision, while naturally handling both offline and lightweight on-policy sampling.
We validate HPD on long-generation math reasoning as well as short-generation dialogue and code tasks, demonstrating improved optimization stability, computational efficiency, and final performance across diverse model families and scales. In summary, our contributions are as follows:

•

We present a unified reweighted log-likelihood view that facilitates understanding existing distillation methods and systematically enables the development of new approaches across divergence directions, optimization strategies, and data regimes.

•

We propose HPD, which hybridizes forward and reverse KL across off-policy data and lightweight on-policy sampling, retaining the efficiency of one-hot supervision.

•

Experiments on math reasoning, dialogue, and code tasks show that HPD improves optimization stability and efficiency, and consistently matches or outperforms standard SFT + OPD pipelines with less cost. Moreover, HPD provides a strong initialization for OPD, leading to further performance enhancement.

Problem Formulation

Auto-regressive Language Models.

We model next-token generation as a sequential decision process.
Let
\tau=(s_{1},a_{1}^{*},s_{2},a_{2}^{*},\dots,s_{T},a_{T}^{*})
denotes a trajectory from an offline dataset
\mathcal{D}
,
where each state
s_{t}
represents the ground-truth prefix:
s_{t}=\mathbf{a}_{<t}^{*}=(a_{1}^{*},\dots,a_{t-1}^{*}),
and
a_{t}^{*}\in\mathcal{V}
is the expert token at step
t
drawn from a finite vocabulary
\mathcal{V}
. A pretrained LLM induces a policy
q_{\theta}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})
parameterized by
\theta
, which factorizes the likelihood of the expert trajectory as:

q_{\theta}(\tau)=\prod_{t=1}^{T}q_{\theta}(a_{t}^{*}\mid s_{t}).

The model is pretrained via teacher forcing, by minimizing the expected negative log-likelihood (NLL) over
\mathcal{D}
as
(
33
)
:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{pretrain}}(\theta)=-\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log q_{\theta}(a_{t}^{*}\mid s_{t})\right].

(1)

Limited Expressivity of the Student Model.
The presence of multiple valid continuations, together with label smoothing, results in an inherently soft supervision signal
(
54
)
. Consequently, in highly complex output spaces, the teacher distribution or the offline data distribution often exhibits pronounced multimodality, exceeding the modes a capacity-limited student can represent
(
15
)
. Accordingly, distillation dilutes probability mass across too many modes, ultimately harming generation quality.

3
Preliminary

We first review SFT as a special case of KD. While SFT provides sparse supervision on target tokens, KD leverages the teacher’s full predictive distribution to offer denser learning signals. However, exact divergence minimization is intractable in large-vocabulary settings, necessitating practical approximations such as Monte Carlo (MC) estimation.

3.1
KD via SFT

SFT trains a model on an offline dataset

\mathcal{D}=\{(s_{t},a_{t}^{*})\}

by minimizing the cross-entropy loss.
Specifically, the supervision signal is represented as a one-hot distribution

\boldsymbol{y}_{t}\in\{0,1\}^{|\mathcal{V}|}
,
where
y_{t,a_{t}^{*}}=1
and
y_{t,a}=0
for all
a\neq a_{t}^{*}
.
The resulting objective is

\min_{\theta}\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{SFT}}(\theta)=\min_{\theta}-{\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{s}}\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{a}\sim\mathcal{D}}}\left[\log q_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{a}|\boldsymbol{s})\right].

(2)

While effective and stable, SFT provides supervision only at a single target action, yielding a sparse learning signal that ignores alternative plausible actions.

3.2
KD via KL Divergence

By introducing the teacher distribution level signal, we can minimize a predictive divergence
\mathbb{D}
between the teacher distribution and the parameterized student distribution. The common use is FKLD and RKLD.

FKLD.

Forward KL measures how well the student distribution
q_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{a}\mid\boldsymbol{s})
covers the modes of the teacher distribution
p(\boldsymbol{a}\mid\boldsymbol{s})
, defined as follows
(
24
)
:

\min_{\theta}\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q_{\theta})=\min_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{s}}\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{a}\sim p(\cdot|\boldsymbol{s})}\left[\log\frac{p(\boldsymbol{a}|\boldsymbol{s})}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}q_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{a}|\boldsymbol{s})}}\right].

(3)

Recent studies
(
2
;
26
)
, approximate the distribution matching by minimizing
Equation

4
, assuming that the teacher’s distribution is similar to its training dataset
\mathcal{D}
for the sake of efficiency, which can be decomposed into a sum of token-wise distillation:

\displaystyle\min_{\theta}\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}|}\sum_{(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a})\in\mathcal{D}}\left[p(\boldsymbol{a}|\boldsymbol{s})\log\frac{p(\boldsymbol{a}|\boldsymbol{s})}{q_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{a}|\boldsymbol{s})}\right]

(4)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min_{\theta}\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}|}\sum_{(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{a})\in\mathcal{D}}\sum_{t}^{|\boldsymbol{a}|}\left[\sum_{a_{t}\in\mathcal{V}}p(a_{t}|s_{t})\log\frac{p(a_{t}|s_{t})}{q_{\theta}(a_{t}|s_{t})}\right]

The gradient of
Equation

3
is as follows:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(p\|q_{\theta}\right)=-\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{s}}\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{a}\sim p(\cdot\mid\boldsymbol{s})}\left[\nabla_{\theta}\log q_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{a}\mid\boldsymbol{s})\right]

(5)

It encourages the student to acquire new knowledge by covering all behaviors that the teacher considers likely, as it penalizes missing modes heavily
(
42
)
. However, if the student model lacks sufficient capacity to match the full support of the teacher distribution, it may exhibit an overly smooth distribution in an attempt to cover all possible modes
(
15
;
45
)
.

RKLD.

Reverse KL encourages the student to focus on the high-probability modes of the teacher, potentially ignoring less likely outputs, calculated as follows:

\min_{\theta}\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{\theta}\|p)=\min_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{s}}\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{a}\sim q_{\theta}(\cdot|\boldsymbol{s})}\left[\log\frac{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}q_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{a}|\boldsymbol{s})}}{p(\boldsymbol{a}|\boldsymbol{s})}\right].

(6)

The gradient is as follows:

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{\theta}\|p)=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{s}}\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{a}\sim q_{\theta}(\cdot\mid\boldsymbol{s})}

(7)

\displaystyle\Big[\nabla_{\theta}\log q_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{a}\mid\boldsymbol{s})\cdot(\log q_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{a}\mid\boldsymbol{s})-\log p(\boldsymbol{a}\mid\boldsymbol{s}))\Big].

This gradient structure biases the student toward the teacher’s dominant modes, underrepresenting low-probability but valid outputs
(
44
)
, and can further lead to unstable training when the student is poorly aligned with the teacher due to high-variance gradients induced by the unbounded log-ratio
(
26
)
.

3.3
Monte Carlo KLD Approximation

Exactly computing the KLD is often intractable, as shown in
Equation

4
, since it requires summing over the full vocabulary, sequence length, and batch size. Instead, the KLD can be approximated via MC sampling. The simplest such estimator, denoted as
K_{1}
, at a given time step
t
, is

K_{1}\triangleq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log\frac{q_{\theta}(a_{t}^{(i)}\mid s_{t})}{p(a_{t}^{(i)}\mid s_{t})},\quad a_{t}^{(i)}\sim q_{\theta}(\cdot\mid s_{t}),

(8)

which is an unbiased estimator of
\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{\theta}\|p)
but suffers from high variance, as the log-ratio term is negative for a substantial fraction of samples
(
37
)
.

K_{1}
Estimator as Reward.

Variant estimators of the KLD can be integrated into training either as a token-level reward penalty, as in PPO
(
36
)
, or as an explicit loss term, as popularized by GRPO
(
39
)
. Recently, the OPD framework computes negative
K_{1}
by evaluating the teacher’s log-probabilities on tokens
a_{t}
sampled from the student policy
(
29
)
as a reward. This approach enables efficient distillation, provides an unbiased gradient estimator, and improves training stability
(
38
)
. See Appendix
B.1
for derivation.

Our Method: HPD

4.1
Towards a Unified View of Distillation

From the perspective of reweighted likelihood optimization, SFT and KL distillation objectives can be unified as:

\mathcal{L(\theta)}=\min_{\theta}-\mathbb{E}_{(s_{t},a_{t})\sim\mathcal{D}^{\pi}}\Big[w(a_{t}\mid s_{t})\,\log q_{\theta}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})\Big],

(9)

where
\mathcal{D}^{\pi}
denotes the data source: for on-policy methods,
\mathcal{D}^{\pi_{\theta}}
is sampled from the current student policy; for off-policy methods,
\mathcal{D}^{\pi}
comes from a fixed dataset
\mathcal{D}
or from a teacher policy
\mathcal{D}^{\pi_{T}}
.
The reweighted term
w(a_{t}\mid s_{t})
captures a local discrepancy between the student and teacher policies at step
t
, quantifying how the probability assigned to action
a_{t}
by the student deviates from that of the teacher at state
s_{t}
.
For clarity, the specific choices of
w(a_{t}\mid s_{t})
for different objectives are summarized in
Table

1
.

Effectiveness of Positive and Negative Optimization.

By inspecting
Equation

9
, we observe that positive weights increase the likelihood of the corresponding actions, whereas negative weights suppress them. The resulting gradient on a sampled token
a_{t}
can be expressed as:

-\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\theta)}{\partial z_{v}}\propto\begin{cases}\hat{w}_{t}\cdot q_{v}\cdot\left(1-q_{v}\right),&\text{if }v=a_{t},\\[4.0pt]
-\hat{w}_{t}\cdot q_{a_{t}}\cdot q_{v},&\text{if }v\neq a_{t},\end{cases}

(10)

where
z_{v}
denotes the logit associated with token
v
and
\hat{w}_{t}
abbreviates
w(a_{t}\mid s_{t})
. See Appendix
D
for derivation.
This formulation shows that, although the objective directly targets a single token, the induced gradients propagate across the entire predictive distribution. In particular, when
\hat{w}_{t}<0
, the gradient actively suppresses the sampled token and redistributes probability mass to alternative tokens proportional to their current probabilities. As discussed in
Section

3.3
, the reverse
K_{1}
estimator naturally exhibits this behavior.

4.2
Hybrid Policy Distillation

We define
expert token
as the token from the teacher generation
(
24
)
or offline ground truth that approximated teacher distribution
(
20
)
, while a
non-expert token
is sampled from the student.

Hybrid Forward–Reverse KL.

Given a fixed offline expert dataset
\mathcal{D}=\{(s_{t},a_{t}^{*})\}
, we adopt the
negative
reverse
k_{1}
estimator:

k_{1}=q_{\theta}(a_{t}^{*}|s_{t})\big[\log p(a_{t}^{*}|s_{t})-\log q_{\theta}(a_{t}^{*}|s_{t})\big],

(11)

to measure the gap between the teacher and the student on the expert token
a_{t}^{*}
conditioned on state
s_{t}
.
A positive
k_{1}>0
indicates that the student underestimates the likelihood of the expert token. To correct this, we incorporate the reweighted term
p(a^{*}_{t}|s_{t})
and define the expert token weight
w_{t}^{*}
as:

w_{t}^{*}\leftarrow\begin{cases}p(a_{t}^{*}|s_{t})+k_{1},&\text{if }k_{1}>0,\\
k_{1},&\text{if }k_{1}\leq 0.\end{cases}

(12)

This design can be interpreted as a hybrid of forward- and reverse-KL distillation implemented via a reweighting mechanism. Unlike the weighted-sum divergence approach, which combines the two KL directions with fixed coefficients, we introduce a
masking
mechanism: when
k_{1}\leq 0
, the corresponding forward-KL weight is masked, reflecting that the student already overestimates the expert token and thus preventing conflicting gradient directions.

Hybrid Policy.

To improve distribution alignment under offline auto-regressive prefixes, we let the student
sample
a different token:
a_{t}\sim q_{\theta}(\cdot|s_{t}),\quad\text{s.t. }a_{t}\neq a_{t}^{*}
, given the ground-truth offline prefix
s_{t}
, and compute its
k_{1}
gap estimator as shown in
Equation

11
on that sampled token named
k_{1}^{\prime}
. We then mask positive values of
k_{1}^{\prime}
by setting

w_{t}\leftarrow\begin{cases}0,&\text{if }k_{1}^{\prime}\geq 0,\\
k_{1}^{\prime},&\text{if }k_{1}^{\prime}<0.\end{cases}

(13)

which prevents reinforcing non-expert tokens while suppressing unreasonable sampling.

As analyzed in
Section

4.1
, a negative weight redistributes probability mass across the vocabulary in proportion to the current model distribution.
When
k_{1}^{\prime}<0
, to explicitly encourage the expert action, we apply a
reinforcement
to the expert token by assigning it a doubled forward-KL weight when
k_{1}>0
, and maintain a forward-KL weight when
k_{1}=0
, yielding the following expert weight:

w_{t}^{*}\leftarrow\begin{cases}2p(a_{t}^{*}|s_{t})+k_{1},&\text{if }k_{1}>0\text{ and }k_{1}^{\prime}<0\\
k_{1},&\text{if }k_{1}<0.\\
p(a_{t}^{*}|s_{t})+k_{1},&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(14)

Finally, the two weights are integrated into reweighted likelihood optimization by binding them to their respective tokens:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{HPD}}

\displaystyle=\min_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{\left(s_{t},a_{t}^{*}\right)\sim\mathcal{D},a_{t}\sim q_{\theta}\left(\cdot\mid s_{t}\right)}

(15)

\displaystyle\left[-w_{t}^{*}\log q_{\theta}\left(a_{t}^{*}\mid s_{t}\right)-w_{t}\log q_{\theta}\left(a_{t}\mid s_{t}\right)\right].

Intuitive Explanation of HPD.

We illustrate the training procedure in
Algorithm

. HPD adopts an asymmetric token-level design. For each offline state
s_{t}
, the expert token
a_{t}^{*}
is the primary optimization, explicitly aligned with the teacher, while suppressing unnecessary learning that could lead to overestimation of the expert token. A student-sampled non-expert token
a_{t}\sim q_{\theta}(\cdot\mid s_{t})
is introduced only to identify and suppress unreasonable model behavior. When the student underestimates the expert token (
k_{1}>0
), the expert token is reinforced via a forward-KL weight. This weight is further strengthened when the student overestimates a sampled non-expert token (
k_{1}^{\prime}<0
), ensuring that suppressed probability mass is redirected toward the expert action.

Baselines.

From a unified perspective, all baselines considered in this work can be interpreted as approximations of a reweighted log-likelihood objective with different choices of weighting estimators. See Appendix

for explanation. In practice, directly optimizing the full KL objective under long auto-regressive generation is often prohibitively expensive in terms of both memory and computation
(
51
)
.
For the off-policy data, we approximate several representative methods as follows:

)
with the teacher probability
p

(

)
using the estimator

(
2
)

5.1
Off-policy Data for Reasoning

5.1.1
Setup

(1) Models and Datasets.
Our student models are drawn from two model families with varying parameter scales: the Qwen2.5 series (1.5B, 3B, and 7B)
(
49
)
and the LLaMA3 series (1B, 3B, and 8B)
(
10
)
. For each model family, we select the largest model as the teacher. We focus on the mathematics domain to enhance general LLM capabilities via long chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning. Specifically, we employ the OpenR1-Math-8192 dataset
(
12
)
.
(2) Implementation Details.
For generation efficiency, recent works (e.g.,
26
;
2
) often approximate the distribution under the assumption that the teacher distribution closely aligns with the empirical distribution of its training dataset. Accordingly, we first train the teacher model on the offline dataset and subsequently refine it using GRPO. HPD exhibits stable optimization dynamics and effective policy alignment.

Effectiveness and Robustness of HPD.

HPD consistently outperforms SFT across different model families and benchmarks, demonstrating strong robustness and scalability. Even in the purely off-policy data setting, HPD alone achieves higher performance than the two-stage baseline that first applies SFT and then OPD. When combined with OPD, HPD further amplifies these gains, attaining the highest average scores and surpassing the corresponding baselines by a substantial margin. Notably, these improvements are consistent across both in-domain and out-of-domain evaluations (GPQA), indicating that HPD does not merely overfit to the teacher’s behavior but instead transfers more generalizable decision signals.

Effectiveness of Student Sampling.

Without student sampling, the performance of the student model converges rapidly but quickly plateaus, failing to achieve sustained performance improvements.
This behavior suggests that directly optimizing toward the teacher distribution limits exploration and leads to premature convergence.
In contrast, enabling student sampling exposes the model to its own induced distribution, allowing it to explore diverse trajectories and continuously refine its policy, resulting in steady performance gains.

8
Related Work

Off-policy Distillation

KD
(
20
)
effectively compresses neural networks, allowing smaller student models to match the performance of larger teacher models. One popular direction of KD for LLMs is to directly harness teachers’ generation as SFT data
(
55
)
. When the teacher model is accessible, it can utilize divergence loss to align the student’s and teacher’s distributions. Recent studies
(
44
;
46
)
have focused on finding the proper objectives to improve off-policy distillation.
46
examined various f-divergences, including total variation distance and JSD, in auto-regressive LMs.
47
also provided adaptive KL to balance their early-stage behaviors of FKLD and RKLD.
44
formulates KD using an
\alpha
-
\beta
divergence, enabling principled control over the probability mass allocation between the teacher and student distributions.

On-policy Distillation

OPD encourages students to concentrate on a limited set of modes in the teacher’s distribution. Both MiniLLM
(
15
)
and GKD
(
2
)
utilize RKLD or JSD to mitigate the student model’s tendency to overestimate low-probability regions of the teacher. DistillLLM-2
(
25
)
proposes a contrastive distillation formulation that simultaneously increases the likelihood of teacher-generated responses while decreasing that of student-generated responses, leveraging both off-policy and on-policy data.

9
Conclusion

We present a unified reweighted log-likelihood view of KD for LLMs, clarifying how divergence direction, optimization strategy, and data regime jointly shape distillation behavior. We then propose HPD, which combines forward- and reverse-KL signals with off-policy data and lightweight on-policy sampling at the token level. Experiments on reasoning, dialogue, and code generation demonstrate the strong performance of HPD. Future work can explore applying HPD earlier in training, such as during mid-training or even pre-training, to assess its potential benefits for LLMs.

Impact Statement

This paper presents work whose goal is to advance the field of machine learning, specifically the efficiency and efficacy of LLMs. There are many potential societal consequences of our work, none of which we feel must be specifically highlighted here.

Appendix A
Positioning of Our Work

Our work focuses on distillation over off-policy data, augmented with a lightweight approximation of on-policy next-token sampling, which avoids full-sequence rollouts. While we acknowledge that full rollouts—such as those used in OPD
(
29
)
or reinforcement learning (RL)
(
36
;
39
)
—are necessary to further enhance distilled model performance, our method is designed to provide a more robust and efficient initialization for subsequent training, and in some cases achieves strong performance without requiring these rollout-based methods.

Appendix B
Limitation

Our work is limited to settings where the teacher and student models share the same tokenizer, which is a common assumption in white-box KD
(
22
;
44
;
26
;
25
)
. Due to the intractability of computing the full KL divergence, we instead adopt the corresponding estimators to provide distillation signals on the token level.

B.1
Gradient Analysis with
K_{1}
reward

In this section, we investigate the role of
K_{1}
as a reward signal in gradient analysis. Let

\tau=(s_{1},a_{1},s_{2},a_{2},\dots,s_{T},a_{T})
denote a trajectory sampled from the student policy
q_{\theta}
,
where each state
s_{t}
corresponds to the prefix
s_{t}=\mathbf{a}_{<t}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{t-1})
, and

a_{t}\in\mathcal{V}
is the token sampled at step
t
from a finite vocabulary
\mathcal{V}
. Let

\widehat{\mathrm{KL}}
denotes the Monte Carlo (MC) estimate of the KL divergence.

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim q_{\theta}}[\widehat{\mathrm{KL}}]

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\theta}\sum_{\tau}q_{\theta}(\tau)\,\widehat{\mathrm{KL}}(\tau)

(16)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\tau}\big(\nabla_{\theta}\widehat{\mathrm{KL}}(\tau)\big)\,q_{\theta}(\tau)+\sum_{\tau}\widehat{\mathrm{KL}}(\tau)\,\nabla_{\theta}q_{\theta}(\tau)

(17)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim q_{\theta}}\Big[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\nabla_{\theta}\widehat{\mathrm{KL}}_{t}\Big]}_{\text{path-wise derivative}}+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim q_{\theta}}\Big[\Big(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\widehat{\mathrm{KL}}_{t}\Big)\nabla_{\theta}\log q_{\theta}(\tau)\Big]}_{\text{score function derivative}}.

(18)

Here, the path-wise derivative corresponds to backpropagating directly through the estimator in the loss,
whereas the score function derivative corresponds to treating the estimator as a reward signal. We adopt the
K_{1}
estimator with
N=1
in
Equation

8
defined as

K_{1}\triangleq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log\frac{q_{\theta}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})}{p(a_{t}\mid s_{t})},\quad a_{t}\sim q_{\theta}(\cdot\mid s_{t}),

whose path-wise derivative is

\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim q_{\theta}}\Big[\nabla_{\theta}\sum_{t=1}^{T}{\mathrm{K_{1}}}_{t}\Big]=0,

and whose score function derivative is

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim q_{\theta}}\Bigg[\Big(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathrm{K_{1}}_{t}\Big)\cdot\nabla_{\theta}\log q_{\theta}(\tau)\Bigg]=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim q_{\theta}}\Bigg[\log\frac{q_{\theta}(\tau)}{p(\tau)}\cdot\nabla_{\theta}\log q_{\theta}(\tau)\Bigg].

(19)

Thus, incorporating the
K_{1}
estimator as a reward provides an unbiased estimate of the gradient for the KLD objective, whereas using
K_{1}
directly in the loss does not.

Appendix C
Gradient of Jensen-Shannon Divergence

Let
p(a_{t}\mid s_{t})
and
q_{\theta}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})
be the teacher and student distributions over tokens
a_{t}\in\mathcal{V}
given state
s_{t}
.
The Jensen-Shannon divergence (JSD) is defined as:

\mathrm{JSD}(p\parallel q_{\theta})=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(p\parallel M\big)+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q_{\theta}\parallel M\big),\quad M(a_{t}\mid s_{t})=\frac{p(a_{t}\mid s_{t})+q_{\theta}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})}{2}.

For discrete distributions, the JSD can be written as:

\mathrm{JSD}(p\parallel q_{\theta})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{a_{t}}p(a_{t}\mid s_{t})\log\frac{p(a_{t}\mid s_{t})}{M(a_{t}\mid s_{t})}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{a_{t}}q_{\theta}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})\log\frac{q_{\theta}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})}{M(a_{t}\mid s_{t})}.

The gradient w.r.t.
\theta
only depends on the second term, since the first term does not involve
\theta
:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\mathrm{JSD}(p\parallel q_{\theta})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{a_{t}}\nabla_{\theta}q_{\theta}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})\log\frac{q_{\theta}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})}{M(a_{t}\mid s_{t})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{a_{t}}q_{\theta}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})\nabla_{\theta}\log q_{\theta}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})\cdot\underbrace{\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{q_{\theta}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})}{M(a_{t}\mid s_{t})}}_{w_{\mathrm{JSD}}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{a_{t}\sim q_{\theta}(\cdot\mid s_{t})}\Big[\nabla_{\theta}\log q_{\theta}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})\cdot w_{\mathrm{JSD}}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})\Big].

Here,
w_{\mathrm{JSD}}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})=\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{q_{\theta}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})}{M(a_{t}\mid s_{t})}
can be interpreted as a token-level weight, analogous to the reward weights in SFT, FKLD, and RKLD.

Appendix D
Gradient contribution

The resulting gradient of
Equation

9
on a sampled token
a_{t}
can be expressed as:

-\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\theta)}{\partial z_{v}}\propto\begin{cases}\hat{w}_{t}\cdot q_{v}\cdot\left(1-q_{v}\right),&\text{if }v=a_{t},\\[4.0pt]
-\hat{w}_{t}\cdot q_{a_{t}}\cdot q_{v},&\text{if }v\neq a_{t},\end{cases}

(20)

where
z_{v}
denotes the logit associated with token
v
and
\hat{w}_{t}
abbreviates
w(a_{t}\mid s_{t})
.

Proof.

To compute the gradient with respect to
z_{v}
, we apply the chain rule:

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{t}}{\partial z_{v}}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{t}}{\partial q_{a_{t}}}\cdot\frac{\partial q_{a_{t}}}{\partial z_{v}}.

(21)

Since

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{t}}{\partial q_{a_{t}}}=-\hat{w}_{t}\frac{1}{q_{a_{t}}},

(22)

It remains to compute the derivative of the softmax function. We now derive
\frac{\partial q_{a_{t}}}{\partial z_{v}}
by considering two cases.

Case 1:
v=a_{t}
.

Using the quotient rule, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\partial q_{a_{t}}}{\partial z_{a_{t}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{a_{t}}}\left(\frac{e^{z_{a_{t}}}}{\sum_{k}e^{z_{k}}}\right)=\frac{e^{z_{a_{t}}}\sum_{k}e^{z_{k}}-e^{z_{a_{t}}}e^{z_{a_{t}}}}{\left(\sum_{k}e^{z_{k}}\right)^{2}}.

(23)

Simplifying yields

\frac{\partial q_{a_{t}}}{\partial z_{a_{t}}}=q_{a_{t}}(1-q_{a_{t}}).

(24)

Substituting back, we have

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{t}}{\partial z_{a_{t}}}=-\hat{w}_{t}(1-q_{a_{t}}),

(25)

and therefore

-\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{t}}{\partial z_{a_{t}}}\propto\hat{w}_{t}\,q_{a_{t}}(1-q_{a_{t}}).

(26)

Case 2:
v\neq a_{t}
.

Similarly,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial q_{a_{t}}}{\partial z_{v}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{v}}\left(\frac{e^{z_{a_{t}}}}{\sum_{k}e^{z_{k}}}\right)=-\frac{e^{z_{a_{t}}}e^{z_{v}}}{\left(\sum_{k}e^{z_{k}}\right)^{2}}.

(27)

Rewriting in terms of softmax probabilities gives

\frac{\partial q_{a_{t}}}{\partial z_{v}}=-q_{a_{t}}q_{v}.

(28)

Thus,

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{t}}{\partial z_{v}}=\hat{w}_{t}q_{v},

(29)

and equivalently,

-\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{t}}{\partial z_{v}}\propto-\hat{w}_{t}\,q_{a_{t}}q_{v},\qquad v\neq a_{t}.

(30)

Combining both cases yields the gradient expression in Eq. (
20
).
