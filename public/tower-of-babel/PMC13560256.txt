A silent epidemic? Poliomyelitis, disability burden, and social impact in Turkey (1942–1968)
Authors: Ayşe Erkmen, Nevim Tüzün, Osman Erkmen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHistorical scholarship has largely framed poliomyelitis through narratives of biomedical progress and vaccination success, often marginalising its long-term clinical and social consequences in the pre-vaccination era. This study examines the experience of poliomyelitis in Turkey between 1942 and 1968 by focusing on the discrepancy between official epidemiological indicators and the disease’s actual societal burden.MethodsUsing a historical epidemiology approach, the study integrates multiple source types, including official health statistics, medical reports, contemporaneous clinical assessments, and national press coverage. These sources were analysed to assess trends in reported cases and deaths, patterns of underreporting, and the broader social and clinical consequences of poliomyelitis beyond conventional epidemiological measures.ResultsThe analysis suggests that, despite relatively low reported case and mortality figures, poliomyelitis in Turkey generated a substantial cumulative burden through permanent disability, long-term care needs, and widespread social anxiety. A marked increase in reported cases in 1956 represents a non-linear trajectory characterised by persistence and periodic intensification rather than a single epidemic wave. Contemporary medical assessments consistently highlighted systematic underreporting, undermining the reliability of official statistics. Limited rehabilitation capacity, inadequate surveillance, and unequal access to healthcare shifted much of the long-term disease burden into the household sphere.ConclusionConceptualised as a ‘silent but destructive’ epidemic, poliomyelitis in Turkey can be interpreted as illustrating how low numerical visibility can coexist with substantial social impact. These findings underscore the importance of assessing epidemics beyond mortality- and incidence-based indicators, incorporating disability outcomes, care burdens, and structural inequalities into evaluations of public health impact.

Background

Poliomyelitis is an infectious disease caused by poliovirus, transmitted primarily via the fecal–oral route and predominantly affecting children. Its viral etiology was established in the early twentieth century through experimental studies demonstrating that the causative agent was filterable, thereby distinguishing it from bacterial pathogens [1, 2]. Although most infections are asymptomatic or mild, a small proportion involve the central nervous system, resulting in acute flaccid paralysis, permanent disability, and death [3, 4]. This clinical profile produces a fundamental disconnect between reported case numbers and the disease’s actual social burden.

Poliomyelitis gained global prominence through major outbreaks in Europe and North America in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, particularly the 1916 epidemic in the United States, which established the disease as a major public health concern [2, 5]. Social fear was driven less by mortality than by the sudden onset of irreversible paralysis in children and the long-term care demands imposed on families [6].

Throughout the twentieth century, the historiography of poliomyelitis was dominated by narratives of biomedical progress and vaccination success. The introduction of the Salk and Sabin vaccines from the 1950s onward contributed to the perception of poliomyelitis as a controllable disease, while the launch of the Global Polio Eradication Initiative in 1988 further reinforced this view [7]. Within this framework, however, the long-term social consequences of poliomyelitis in the pre-vaccination era were often marginalised.

Recent scholarship in historical epidemiology has shown that poliomyelitis cannot be adequately assessed through incidence and mortality figures alone. Permanent disability, lifelong care needs, social exclusion, and structural inequality constitute central dimensions of the disease’s true impact [8, 9]. In addition, studies of poliovirus transmission have highlighted the phenomenon of “silent circulation,” referring to the gap between observed cases and actual viral spread in populations where asymptomatic and abortive infections are common [10–14]. This discrepancy means that reliance on official notifications may substantially underestimate both viral circulation and disease burden. Similar patterns have been observed in the historiography of other diseases whose societal impact exceeds what mortality figures alone would suggest. Studies of conditions such as gout have demonstrated that low mortality does not necessarily imply low social or clinical burden, particularly in cases involving chronic impairment, stigma, or long-term disability [15]. This perspective provides a useful framework for interpreting poliomyelitis as a disease whose impact cannot be fully captured through conventional epidemiological indicators.

In Turkey, poliomyelitis appeared in official health statistics mainly during the 1940s and 1950s with relatively low reported case and mortality figures and was long regarded as a rare, non-epidemic disease [16, 17]. Contemporaneous medical assessments, however, indicate systematic underreporting. Bilsel estimated that at least two to three times as many cases went unrecorded due to abortive infections, diagnostic limitations, and failures to seek medical care [16]. Official statements by the Ministry of Health reinforced this perception by emphasising that poliomyelitis had “never occurred as an epidemic” in Turkey, despite growing public concern [16, 17].

This study examines poliomyelitis in Turkey between 1942 and 1968 by focusing on the discrepancy between reported epidemiological data and the broader societal burden of the disease. Clinical outcomes and social consequences are analysed as dimensions that help to interpret this gap. By conceptualising poliomyelitis as a historically situated “silent epidemic,” it suggests how low numerical visibility can coexist with substantial and lasting social impact, underscoring the importance of evaluating epidemics beyond conventional biomedical indicators.

Although dedicated historical studies focusing specifically on poliomyelitis in Turkey remain limited, available medical reports, ministry publications, and contemporaneous analyses provide valuable insights into the epidemiological and clinical characteristics of the disease within the national context.

Methods

This study adopts a mixed qualitative–quantitative design combining historical epidemiology with approaches from the social history of medicine to examine poliomyelitis in Turkey as both an epidemiological phenomenon and a socially experienced public health problem.

Quantitative data on poliomyelitis cases and deaths were drawn primarily from Bilsel’s epidemiological assessment [16], Sağlık ve Sosyal Yardım Bakanlığı (Ministry of Health and Social Welfare) reports, and contemporaneous medical publications. These sources reflect annual case distributions and the operation of the official notification system; given frequent references to underreporting and diagnostic limitations, official figures were treated as lower-bound indicators rather than precise measures of disease burden.

Public representations of poliomyelitis were analysed through systematic review of national newspapers and periodicals, most notably Milliyet (1950–1968) and Akis [18–20]. Clinical course and mortality patterns were assessed using contemporaneous medical literature and WHO publications to situate Turkey’s experience in a broader epidemiological context [21]. Case and death counts were analysed by year, and press materials were examined using qualitative content analysis to assess tensions between epidemiological indicators and social perception. Newspaper materials were reviewed for reports relating to poliomyelitis outbreaks, mortality, rehabilitation, vaccination campaigns, public health messaging, and the social consequences of disability. These sources were used qualitatively to contextualise official statistics and to examine public perceptions, caregiving burdens, fear, and disability-related experiences discussed in the Findings and Discussion sections.

As the study is based exclusively on publicly accessible historical sources, ethics committee approval was not required.

In this context, different types of evidence were treated with varying analytical weight. Bilsel’s estimates provide the only direct quantitative assessment of underreporting, whereas other claims are based on the triangulation of clinical observations, contemporaneous medical accounts, and press coverage. Given the fragmented nature of the available data, these sources do not constitute a systematic measurement of disease burden, but rather enable an interpretive reconstruction of underreporting and broader social impact.

The structure and operation of the disease notification system during the study period remain only partially documented in the available sources. While reporting of infectious diseases was formally required, contemporaneous accounts suggest that compliance was uneven and influenced by diagnostic capacity, access to healthcare, and regional infrastructure. The available data also do not allow a clear distinction between hospital-based and community-reported cases.

Findings: dynamics of cases and deaths

Official case and death data indicate that poliomyelitis in Turkey followed a fluctuating, non-linear trajectory rather than a steady increase. During the pre-vaccination period, prolonged phases of low reported incidence were intermittently interrupted by sharp increases, suggesting that the disease, despite being perceived as “rare,” retained the capacity to periodically intensify as a public health threat. Reported poliomyelitis cases remained extremely limited nationwide during the first half of the 1940s, reflecting a period of low statistical visibility rather than disease absence. From the early 1950s onward, case numbers increased gradually, culminating in a clear breakpoint in 1956 (Table 1). From this point, poliomyelitis became more visible within official statistics, no longer confined to isolated or incidental cases.

Although national-level figures appear modest, contemporaneous clinical and local assessments suggest temporal and spatial concentration. In Istanbul, for example, clinically documented admissions indicate local clustering that was not fully captured by the official notification system (Table 2). Ministry of Health assessments further identified a marked seasonal pattern, with case numbers peaking during summer months [18]. Mortality among severe hospitalised cases could be high; however, overall death counts remained limited, while a substantial proportion of reported cases resulted in permanent paralysis [16, 18]. This pattern indicates that poliomyelitis burden in Turkey was carried primarily through long-term disability rather than mortality.

The 1956 breakpoint and reporting limits

The year 1956 constituted a decisive quantitative and perceptual threshold. The abrupt rise in reported cases and deaths suggests that poliomyelitis reached a magnitude that could no longer be ignored even within official statistics. Contemporaneous medical evaluations highlighted uncertainty regarding the causes of this increase. Bilsel emphasised that poliomyelitis was not restricted to foreign contacts and occurred even in remote Anatolian villages, while noting that post-1945 increases could not be conclusively explained [16]. In this context, speculative explanations, such as imported foodstuffs, packaging materials, or atmospheric factors, entered public discourse, reflecting the period’s epidemiological uncertainty rather than evidence-based conclusions.

Press coverage from 1956 illustrates a contradictory framing of risk. While describing poliomyelitis as contagious and potentially severe, newspaper reports simultaneously sought to reassure the public by emphasising limited numbers and administrative control [19]. This dual rhetoric framed poliomyelitis as biologically threatening yet officially “under control,” reinforcing its low-visibility character.

Physicians repeatedly stressed that official figures excluded abortive cases, mild infections, and patients who did not seek medical care, implying that true incidence substantially exceeded reported numbers. The discrepancy between notification data and clinical estimates (Table 3) indicates that 1956 marked not only an epidemiological breakpoint but also a point at which the limits of surveillance and reporting capacity became more apparent.

It remains unclear whether this increase reflects a genuine epidemiological shift or improved reporting and visibility within the surveillance system.

At the same time, no clear archival or documentary evidence has been identified indicating a formal change in registration or reporting procedures in 1956. However, the possibility that increased awareness, improved detection, or gradual changes in reporting practices contributed to the observed rise cannot be excluded. The persistence of higher case numbers in subsequent years suggests that the increase likely reflects a combination of epidemiological and reporting-related factors rather than a single isolated epidemic event. This ambiguity further highlights the difficulty of distinguishing between epidemiological change and shifts in surveillance or reporting practices in historical data.

Post-1956 persistence and vulnerability patterns

After 1956, poliomyelitis cases continued to fluctuate rather than decline steadily, indicating that the increase was not a transient spike but part of an enduring public health problem. Following the introduction of vaccination programmes in the mid-1960s, reported cases displayed irregular, regional, and low-intensity patterns rather than large epidemic waves. Persisting cases in the late 1960s suggest that poliovirus circulation was not fully eliminated and could continue under conditions of limited surveillance.

Spatial and demographic evidence indicates that poliomyelitis did not spread homogeneously. Case visibility was higher in urban and socioeconomically disadvantaged areas, likely reflecting differences in healthcare access and reporting capacity. These regional differences can be explained by variations in healthcare access, diagnostic capacity, and reporting infrastructure, with higher case visibility in urban centres and underreporting more pronounced in rural areas. Press reports further suggest that infrastructural deficiencies, internal migration, and population mobility hindered access to vaccination and routine health services. Taken together, these findings indicate that poliomyelitis in Turkey operated within a multi-layered vulnerability regime shaped by time, place, age, and socioeconomic inequality.

Clinical course, permanent paralysis, and mortality

Poliomyelitis is characterised by a wide clinical spectrum, with most infections remaining asymptomatic or mild, while a small proportion progress to severe and lasting consequences when the central nervous system is involved [1, 3, 22]. Evidence from contemporaneous medical evaluations and press sources indicates that Turkey’s clinical experience largely mirrored this global pattern, while also reflecting context-specific vulnerabilities related to care conditions, rehabilitation capacity, and social support mechanisms [18, 20]. Even after the initiation of vaccination programmes in the mid-1960s, sporadic cases continued to produce severe individual outcomes, including permanent paralysis and lifelong sequelae [7, 16, 25].

Reported cases in Turkey typically began with non-specific symptoms such as fever, fatigue, and myalgia, followed in a subset by acute flaccid paralysis. Paralysis most commonly affected the lower extremities, while more severe cases involved upper limbs and truncal musculature [18, 19]. Press reports and physicians’ observations frequently emphasised abrupt loss of ambulation, muscle weakness, and visible atrophy in children [23, 26]. As in other pre-vaccination contexts, public visibility of poliomyelitis was shaped primarily by these dramatic and irreversible clinical presentations rather than by overall case numbers [1, 2].

Permanent paralysis emerged as the most enduring consequence of poliomyelitis in Turkey. A substantial proportion of patients experienced persistent muscle weakness, deformities, and gait disturbances, transforming the disease from an acute infection into a lifelong disability condition [16, 18]. Rehabilitation opportunities were extremely limited; insufficient physiotherapy services, restricted access to orthopaedic supports, and limited institutional care meant that long-term care was largely displaced into the household [16, 19]. Ministry of Health guidance documents from the 1960s indicate a limited but deliberate focus on functional rehabilitation, particularly through “therapeutic walking” practices using orthoses and assistive devices, reflecting recognition of poliomyelitis as a condition producing long-term functional loss [16, 18].

Mortality attributable to poliomyelitis remained numerically limited but was associated with clinically severe presentations, particularly respiratory muscle involvement and bulbar paralysis [16]. In the absence of advanced respiratory support technologies, such as mechanical ventilation, prognosis in severe cases was poor [17]. Media coverage of fatal cases amplified social fear, reinforcing perceptions of poliomyelitis as sudden, unpredictable, and life-altering despite relatively low death counts [20].

Overall, while the clinical course of poliomyelitis in Turkey closely resembled global patterns, limitations in care conditions, rehabilitation capacity, and social support mechanisms intensified the long-term individual and societal burden. These findings underscore the need to evaluate poliomyelitis not solely as an epidemiological phenomenon, but as a public health event defined by disability, care dependency, and structural vulnerability (Fig. 1).

Social impact: care burden, fear, and disability

The impact of poliomyelitis in Turkey extended far beyond reported case and mortality figures, profoundly shaping family life, caregiving practices, and social perception. The disease’s unpredictable clinical course, risk of permanent paralysis, and concentration among children transformed poliomyelitis into a persistent social experience rather than a short-lived medical event [2, 27]. Even in later eradication-era discourse, poliomyelitis continued to be framed as a devastating condition associated with disability and long-term social and economic burden, reflecting its enduring symbolic power in public health communication [28].

Caregiving burden and household-level consequences

Cases resulting in permanent paralysis placed the primary burden of care on families. During the 1950s and 1960s, the scarcity of rehabilitation services and institutional care meant that paralysed children were cared for almost entirely within the household [16–18]. This arrangement transformed everyday life, particularly for mothers, intensifying unpaid caregiving labour and often limiting participation in paid work and social life. Contemporary press reports provide concrete illustrations of this burden. For example, several accounts described families in which paralysed children required continuous care, often leading to the withdrawal of mothers from paid work and the reorganisation of household labour. Press reports further documented disruptions in schooling, social participation, and daily routines around care needs, highlighting the long-term economic impact of poliomyelitis beyond the acute phase of the disease [16, 19, 20].

The cumulative disability burden generated by poliomyelitis gradually necessitated institutional responses. The opening in 1964 of a specialised poliomyelitis hospital in Eğirdir illustrates the scale of unmet care needs: reported patient demand exceeded bed capacity on the first day of operation, indicating that long-term care requirements far surpassed official case counts [25]. The immediate overcapacity reported at the time further illustrates the extent of unmet need, suggesting that a significant proportion of long-term disability cases remained outside formal healthcare provision. While similar caregiving burdens were observed in the United States and Western Europe, limited social welfare mechanisms in Turkey further concentrated responsibility within the household, amplifying social and gendered inequalities [2, 29].

Fear, stigma, and social exclusion

Beyond caregiving demands, poliomyelitis generated widespread fear and uncertainty. Despite frequent official reassurances that there was “no epidemic,” families continued to perceive the disease as an unpredictable and uncontrollable threat [19, 20]. Press coverage frequently amplified this sense of fear by reporting individual cases of sudden paralysis in previously healthy children, often presented in emotive language. Such accounts contributed to a perception of poliomyelitis as an unpredictable and uncontrollable threat, reinforcing its psychological impact on families and communities. The sudden transformation of healthy children into permanently disabled bodies, combined with occasional fatal outcomes, sustained this climate of anxiety and reinforced polio’s visibility as a remembered risk in everyday life.

Children living with permanent paralysis also faced social stigma and exclusion. Limited access to education, rehabilitation, and social support translated physical impairment into long-term social inequality. Media representations often framed paralysed children through narratives of pity and tragedy, making disability visible while obscuring structural deficiencies and public responsibility [24, 30]. This pattern aligns with global disability scholarship describing post-polio disability as simultaneously visible and marginalised [13, 29].

Overall, poliomyelitis in Turkey functioned as a multi-layered social process that reshaped family relations, intensified care burdens, and sustained fear and inequality. These findings underscore the need to analyse poliomyelitis not only as an infection controlled through vaccination, but as a historical public health experience defined by disability, care dependency, and social vulnerability.

Discussion: visibility, disability, and social burden

This study situates the mid-twentieth-century experience of poliomyelitis in Turkey within the broader global literature by focusing on the discrepancy between epidemiological visibility and actual disease burden, drawing on clinical outcomes and social experience to interpret this gap.

These findings are consistent with patterns observed in other countries, where poliomyelitis was characterised by relatively low mortality but high levels of disability, social anxiety, and long-term care needs [1, 2]. Contemporary accounts from Europe and North America similarly emphasised the disproportionate social visibility of paralysis compared to overall case numbers.

While the Turkish case largely mirrors core epidemiological and clinical features observed elsewhere, it offers a distinctive perspective on public visibility, the tension between official discourse and lived experience, and the management of long-term disability burden [2, 27]. Clinical outcomes and social consequences should therefore be understood not as separate analytical domains, but as dimensions that reveal the limits of epidemiological reporting.

The non-linear trajectory of poliomyelitis in Turkey, particularly the pronounced increase in 1956, parallels epidemic dynamics documented in other pre-vaccination contexts. Global scholarship has shown that poliomyelitis often remained statistically limited for extended periods before intensifying abruptly once a critical threshold was crossed [1, 2]. Although absolute numbers in Turkey were lower, similar threshold dynamics operated, transforming a marginalised disease into a visible administrative and social concern.

Any interpretation of official poliomyelitis statistics in Turkey must also be considered in light of contemporaneous estimates of underreporting. Bilsel estimated that actual case numbers may have been two to three times higher than those recorded through the notification system, owing to abortive infections, diagnostic limitations, and failures to seek medical care [16]. Although this estimate cannot be validated systematically with the available evidence, it suggests that reported incidence figures should be interpreted as minimum estimates rather than comprehensive measures of disease occurrence. Consequently, fluctuations observed in official statistics may reflect a combination of epidemiological change and variations in case detection, reporting practices, and healthcare access.

Several mechanisms likely contributed to underreporting during the study period. Contemporary accounts suggest that abortive and mild infections frequently remained outside the notification system, while diagnostic limitations, unequal access to healthcare, regional disparities in reporting infrastructure, and variation in surveillance capacity reduced case ascertainment. These factors indicate that official statistics reflected not only disease occurrence but also differences in detection, reporting practices, and healthcare accessibility across regions.

The findings of this study suggest that poliomyelitis in Turkey may have exerted a social impact far exceeding reported case and mortality figures. As in other settings, this disproportionate impact stemmed primarily from the disease’s targeting of children and its production of permanent paralysis rather than high mortality [1, 3]. In Turkey, repeated official reassurances that the disease was “under control” coexisted with sustained public anxiety driven by media narratives, visible disability, and uncertainty regarding risk. Within this context, conceptualising poliomyelitis as a “silent but destructive” epidemic helps explain how limited numerical visibility could coexist with deep and lasting social effects.

In this respect, the Turkish case aligns with broader international observations, where the social meaning of poliomyelitis was shaped less by mortality than by the sudden onset of paralysis in children and its long-term consequences, including disability and care dependency [29].

The findings further indicate that the principal burden of poliomyelitis in Turkey was carried through permanent disability and long-term care needs. While rehabilitation services expanded in parts of Western Europe and North America during the 1950s, the more limited development of comparable infrastructure in Turkey displaced disability burden into households. As a result, poliomyelitis functioned not only as a clinical condition but as a structural public health issue generating prolonged caregiving demands, labour loss, and social inequality.

These patterns should be interpreted in light of the structural limitations of the disease notification system, including variable reporting practices and limited surveillance capacity. Rather than offering a precise quantification of disease burden, this study draws on multiple partial sources to suggest broader patterns of underreporting and social impact. While direct quantitative evidence remains limited, the convergence of clinical observations, press reports, and contemporaneous medical commentary consistently points toward substantial underreporting. In this respect, the Turkish experience highlights the limits of viewing poliomyelitis control solely through vaccination. Underreporting, abortive cases, and weaknesses in notification systems constrained recognition of the disease’s true burden, reinforcing the gap between official statistics and lived experience. These findings underscore the importance of state capacity, surveillance quality, and social policy in shaping epidemic outcomes.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the lack of systematic data on age, sex, and long-term disability outcomes limits the ability to quantify the full burden of poliomyelitis. Second, the analysis relies in part on contemporaneous press coverage and clinical observations, which may reflect selective reporting and narrative framing rather than systematic data collection. Third, the available evidence is likely affected by urban and reporting biases, as case visibility was higher in areas with better access to healthcare and surveillance systems. These limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings, which are intended to provide an interpretive rather than fully quantitative reconstruction of disease burden.

Conclusion

This study reassesses poliomyelitis in Turkey between the 1940s and late 1960s by integrating epidemiological data with clinical outcomes and social effects. Despite being long perceived as a rare and low-incidence disease, poliomyelitis in Turkey followed a fluctuating and persistent trajectory, with 1956 marking a critical breakpoint in both epidemiological visibility and social perception. The continuation of sporadic cases after the introduction of vaccination further indicates that poliomyelitis should be understood as a problem of delayed elimination and low-visibility circulation rather than simple control.

Although reported case and mortality figures were lower than those in many Western countries, systematic underreporting, abortive infections, and the cumulative impact of permanent paralysis suggest a substantial disease burden independent of scale. Disability, long-term care needs, and household-level consequences constituted the core dimensions of polio’s impact, outweighing mortality as determinants of social cost.

Taken together, the Turkish experience suggests that effective control of poliomyelitis cannot be reduced to vaccination technology alone. Surveillance capacity, rehabilitation infrastructure, risk communication, and social policy are equally decisive in shaping epidemic burden. By conceptualising poliomyelitis as a “silent but destructive” epidemic, this study offers a transferable framework for evaluating low-incidence but high-disability diseases in both historical and contemporary public health contexts.
