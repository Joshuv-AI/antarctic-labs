Pasture-based versus conventional broiler chicken production: Enterprise budget modeling and profitability analysis
Authors: Laya Kannan S. Alves, Tomislav Vukina, Brooke M. Bartz, James O. Eckberg, Allison N. Pullin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Broiler production systems differ substantially in management configuration, operational scale, and market orientation, which shape their economic performance. This study evaluated the economics of three broiler production systems: a large-scale conventional system, a medium-scale conventional system, and a pasture-based system. Enterprise budgeting was used to estimate total production costs, followed by break-even, profitability and sensitivity analysis. Unit production costs were lowest in the large-scale conventional system (USD 0.52 lb⁻¹), intermediate in the medium-scale system (USD 0.64 lb⁻¹), and highest in the pasture-based system (USD 1.92 lb⁻¹), reflecting differences in scale and cost structure. Under the conventional market price (USD 1.32 lb⁻¹), the large-scale and medium-scale systems generated positive economic profits of USD 2.12 million yr⁻¹ and USD 421,195 yr⁻¹, respectively, whereas the pasture-based system incurred an economic loss of USD 5,907 yr⁻¹ (return on investment of –48%). When a premium price (USD 6.09 lb⁻¹) was applied, the pasture-based system shifted to positive economic profit (USD 16,918 yr⁻¹; ROI = 138%), with its break-even production threshold declining from 2,058 to 445 birds yr⁻¹. Sensitivity analysis demonstrated that feed cost exerted the greatest influence on profitability in conventional systems, whereas labor cost had a proportionally greater effect on the pasture-based system; however, the relative economic performance of the modeled systems remained unchanged across evaluated sensitivity scenarios. Overall, production scale was a primary determinant of economic feasibility in conventional systems, whereas pasture-based systems depended on market differentiation and price premiums to offset higher production costs associated with management practices known to improve welfare status. These findings indicate that economic performance should be assessed within the context of production objectives and prevailing market price structures, rather than unit production cost alone.

Introduction

Broiler production plays a significant role in global food systems by providing a widely consumed and economically accessible source of animal protein (Govoni et al., 2021; Adaszyńska-Skwirzyńska et al., 2025). Over recent decades, global chicken meat consumption has increased steadily, largely driven by its affordability and broad consumer acceptance across diverse cultural and income contexts (Kleyn and Ciacciariello, 2021). As a result, the broiler sector has become increasingly important for food security, farm income generation, and international agricultural trade.

Conventional broiler production systems have become more intensified, characterized by short biological cycles, larger flock sizes, higher levels of automation, and strong vertical integration with large-scale commercial supply chains (Solano-Blanco et al., 2020; George and George, 2023). These attributes contribute to production stability, facilitate rapid adjustments to market demand, maximize biological efficiency, and reduce unit production costs (Solano-Blanco et al., 2020). At the same time, alternative production systems, such as pasture-based and free-range models, have expanded in response to changing consumer preferences for attributes related to animal welfare, environmental stewardship, and transparency in production practices (Erian et al., 2017; Elkhoraibi et al., 2017; Chan et al., 2022). Multiple management variables, including outdoor access and scale of production, differ between conventional and alternative systems. However, it remains unclear how these differences affect production economics.

To date, research comparing conventional and alternative broiler production systems, including organic, pasture-based, and free-range models, has focused predominantly on production performance (Bartlett et al., 2015; Sanchez-Casanova et al., 2021; Singh et al., 2021), animal welfare outcomes (Taylor et al., 2020; Sans, 2023; Tellis et al., 2024; Campbell et al., 2025) and meat quality and carcass characteristics (da Silva et al., 2017; Aksoy et al., 2021; Marchewka et al., 2023; Campbell et al., 2025). While this literature provides valuable biological and ethical insights, comparatively, fewer studies have evaluated the economic performance of alternative broiler production systems. Existing economic analysis have primarily focused on system-level comparisons, such as conventional versus organic production (Vukina et al., 2014a; 2014b) or conventional versus pasture-based systems (Elmore et al., 2023). However, these studies do not explicitly evaluate the role of outdoor access or production scale as independent management variables influencing production economics.

From a producer perspective, understanding the specific effects of outdoor access and production scale represents a critical gap, as decisions to adopt or transition between various production systems involve substantial financial risk associated with these management variables (Alves et al., 2025). Differences in production scale, input structure, management intensity, and marketing strategies create distinct cost profiles (Udo et al., 2011) and exposure to market variability that are rarely quantified in a systematic and operationally relevant manner to understand the specific role of outdoor access and production scale.

In this study, we applied an economic analysis to assess the financial consequences of different broiler production systems and to provide a basis for decision-support tools that reflect realistic production and market conditions. Direct comparisons are needed to provide detailed information on cost structures, break-even thresholds, and profitability indicators that support more informed farm-level planning and help mitigate uncertainty associated with system diversification or conversion. The objective of the study was to provide an economic characterization of broiler production systems that differ in management configuration (conventional versus pasture-based) and operational scale. Specifically, this study (i) estimated total production costs through enterprise budgeting; (ii) determined break-even prices under a zero-profit condition; and (iii) evaluated profitability indicators at prevailing market selling prices; and (iv) assessed the sensitivity of economic outcomes to variation in key biological and economic parameters.

Material and methods

Study design and overview

This study evaluated the economic performance of broiler production systems that differ in management configuration and operational scale. An enterprise-budgeting and profitability framework was applied to three representative production scenarios, ranging from large-scale conventional operations to small-scale pasture-based systems.

The methodological approach followed the enterprise budgeting procedures described by Vukina et al. (2014a; 2014b), which decompose production costs into variable and fixed components to estimate the total cost (TC) and the break-even price (BEP). Technical and financial parameters used in each model were based on representative economic inputs developed specifically for this study and implemented in an Excel-based model. These scenarios were intended to represent production archetypes rather than empirical observations from individual farms. Input values were derived from published literature, public databases, and expert consultation with extension specialists and industry professionals.

Profitability indicators were calculated following Alves et al. (2022; 2025), including total revenue (TR), benefit–cost ratio (B/C), return on investment (ROI), and production break-even point (BEPₚ). These metrics complement zero-profit analysis by assessing the potential economic return under realistic selling-price assumptions.

All simulations and calculations were performed in Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA) using cell-linked equations to ensure traceability. Monetary values were expressed in U.S. dollars (USD). Calculations were expressed on both a per-flock and per-pound live-weight basis to enable comparison across systems. A standardized set of technical and financial assumptions was applied to ensure methodological consistency.

Description of production scenarios

Three production configurations were modeled to assess the economic performance of alternative broiler production systems and to illustrate how production scale influences cost efficiency. The analysis was grounded in the enterprise-budgeting approach of Vukina et al. (2014a; 2014b), emphasizing the decomposition of total costs into variable and fixed components.

In all configurations, the producer was assumed to sell processed whole broilers (whole bird, without giblets; WOG), with prices expressed in U.S. dollars per pound of processed product. To capture economies of scale within conventional systems, a counterfactual scenario (A.1) was constructed by reducing producing capacity while maintaining key biological and managerial parameters constant as the large-scale baseline (Scenario A). This design isolates the pure effect of scale: when output increases, total cost rises at a slower rate, reducing unit cost; conversely, when scale decreases, unit cost rises even if technological efficiency remains unchanged. The pasture-based system (Scenario B.1), in contrast, differs in both management intensity and production environment, representing a distinct technological and operational model rather than a scaled-down version of the conventional system.

To enhance transparency and replicability, a common set of auxiliary assumptions were also standardized across scenarios. Land was valued at USD 5,150 acre⁻¹ (USDA-NASS, 2022a), corresponding to the 2022 state average for North Carolina farm real estate. The annual rental rate—used to represent the opportunity cost of owned land—was calculated as:RL=VLxiwhere VL= land value (USD acre⁻¹) and i= annual interest rate (0.05).

Substituting the values yields RL=5,150×0.05=257.50USDacre−1year−1. This rate was multiplied by the acreage required for each scenario to obtain total annual land cost.

Labor cost was estimated based on a homogeneous skilled-labor wage of USD 13.50 h⁻¹, derived from the Southeastern U.S. livestock-worker rate (USD 12.92 h⁻¹; USDA-NASS, 2022b), adjusted upward by 4.5% to reflect inflation from April 2022 to 2023 and the higher skill level required for broiler management. The total annual labor cost (CL) was computed as:CL=WhxHyWhere Wh is the hourly wage (USD h⁻¹) and Hy represents the total number of labor hours per farm per year.

Because disaggregated feed price data by growth phase were unavailable, feed cost was approximated using a weighted price index based on typical energy-to-protein proportions:PF=0.70PC+0.30PSwhere PC= corn price per lb and PS= soybean-meal price per lb.

Corn was priced at USD 6.50 bushel⁻¹ and converted to USD lb⁻¹ by dividing by 56 lb bushel⁻¹, resulting in PC=0.116USDlb−1. Soybean meal was priced at USD 415 ton⁻¹ and converted by dividing by 2,000 lb ton⁻¹, yielding PS=0.2075USDlb−1. Substituting these values into the equation resulted in an average feed cost of approximately USD 0.1435 lb⁻¹, which was applied consistently across scenarios. Corn and soybean-meal prices were obtained from the USDA-NASS Quick Stats database and reflect average market conditions for the first half of 2023 (USDA-NASS, 2023).

All parameters were entered as independent variables in the Excel-based model, allowing automatic recalculation of cost items and sensitivity testing under changing market conditions. In addition to these baseline and auxiliary assumptions, each scenario incorporated it owns technical coefficients related to housing, land use, flock size, mortality, feed conversion ratio (FCR), and number of production cycles per year. Values were drawn from published literature (Vukina et al., 2014a; Rothrock et al., 2019; Liles et al., 2015) and expert consultations with extension specialists and industry professionals.

Scenario A – Large-scale conventional broiler operation

Scenario A represents a fully integrated, large-scale conventional broiler farm and serves as the benchmark for all comparisons. The operation comprises four single-level, tunnel-ventilated houses measuring 66 × 600 ft (39,600 ft² each), totaling 158,400 ft² of usable growing area. Birds are stocked at a density of approximately one bird ft⁻², resulting in approximately 158,400 chicks placed per flock.

Each production cycle consists of a seven-week growing period followed by one week of downtime for litter management and disinfection, allowing five production cycles per year. Broilers are marketed at a target live weight of 6 lb. bird⁻¹, with a feed conversion ratio (FCR) of 1.7 lb feed lb⁻¹ gain and cumulative mortality of 5%. Marketed output was adjusted for mortality, whereas total feed purchased includes feed consumed by birds that did not reach market weight.

Houses are equipped with automatic feeders, nipple drinkers, tunnel ventilation, environmental control panels, and emergency power generators. Bedding consists of pine shavings (3 inches deep), capitalized as a fixed investment because the used litter is sold annually as fertilizer, offsetting replacement cost.

Land requirements, including service roads, buffer zones, and composting areas, total approximately 8 acres. Labor demand is equivalent to one full-time employee supported by family labor during seasonal activities such as catching and house cleaning. This configuration represents the technical and economic baseline for intensive commercial broiler production.

Scenario A.1 – Medium-scale conventional broiler operation

Scenario A.1 models a medium-sized conventional enterprise designed to isolate the effect of production scale while maintaining the same biological and management parameters as Scenario A. The farm includes two single-level houses (40 × 500 ft; 20,000 ft² each), totaling 40,000 ft² of usable floor space. Birds are stocked at an effective density of approximately 1 bird ft⁻² (1.05 birds ft⁻²), with identical performance assumptions (FCR = 1.7, mortality = 5%, live weight = 6 lb.), resulting in approximately 42,000 chicks placed per flock for each of five production cycles.

Reducing the number and size of houses directly decreases annual output by about 75% relative to the large-scale system. Land use is reduced to 3 acres, and hired labor is halved. Equipment density, housing type, and management practices remain identical to Scenario A, ensuring that any variation in cost structure reflects scale effects alone.

This counterfactual design quantifies how lower output capacity reduces the dilution of fixed costs and increases unit production cost, even under unchanged technical efficiency.

Scenario B.1 – Small-scale pasture-based operation (“pasture with pens”)

Scenario B.1 represents a small-holder, pasture-raised production system using mobile floorless pens (chicken tractors) that are manually moved daily to provide fresh forage and even manure distribution. The operation places approximately 1,250 broilers per year, divided into four seasonal batches (spring–summer) of about 312 birds each.

Each batch is reared for 56 days (8 weeks) to a target weight of 6 lb. bird⁻¹, with an FCR of 2.5 lb. feed lb⁻¹ gain and cumulative mortality of 15%, accounting for environmental exposure and predation. Pens (≈ 10 × 12 ft) are lightweight and manually moved once per day. Each pen contains portable feeders and gravity drinkers, no mechanical ventilation, automated feeding, or heating systems are used.

All labor is manual, requiring about 2 h day⁻¹ during the grow-out period, plus additional time for cleaning, preparation, and marketing. Land requirements total 1 acre, divided into four paddocks to allow grass recovery between batches. Equipment investment includes feed bins, portable fencing, and small tools.

This system captures both technological and scale-driven differences compared with indoor conventional production and provides insight into how outdoor-access systems affect cost composition and economic feasibility under small-scale conditions.

Cost estimation

Total production cost was calculated as the sum of variable and fixed components, following the enterprise-budgeting framework described by Vukina et al. (2014b). All values were expressed in U.S. dollars and estimated on both a per cycle and per-pound live-weight basis to ensure comparability across production systems. The total live weight (TLW) produced per cycle was determined as:TLW=Nx(1−m)xWwhere N is the number of chicks placed, m is cumulative mortality rate, and W is the final live weight (lb. bird⁻¹).

Variable costs (VC) included all expenses that changed in direct proportion to flock size or production scale. These comprised the purchase of chicks, feed, veterinary and sanitary inputs, energy and fuel, utilities, temporary labor, harvesting and transport, and miscellaneous farm supplies (e.g., cleaning materials, equipment maintenance, and communication services). Feed cost was calculated as the product of total feed consumed and the average feed price per pound, using the feed conversion ratio (FCR) specified for each system. Accordingly, total variable cost per cycle was obtained as:VCcyc=∑VCiand standardized per pound of live weight as:VClb=VCcycTLW

Fixed costs (FC) comprised expenditures that remained constant regardless of output volume, including depreciation of buildings and equipment, opportunity cost of capital, property taxes, insurance, and annual land rent. Depreciation was calculated using the straight-line method. The opportunity cost of capital was fixed at 5% of total invested assets, while property taxes and insurance were applied at 0.8% and 0.5% of asset value, respectively. The annual fixed cost was therefore obtained as:FCyr=Depbldg+Depequip+0.05A+0.008A+0.005Awhere Arepresents the total asset value of each system. Fixed cost per pound of live weight was then derived as:FClb=FCyrTLWyr

The total cost (TC) for each production system was the sum of variable and fixed costs:TC=VC+FC

The corresponding unit total cost (USD lb⁻¹ live weight) was expressed as:TC_lb=VC_lb+FC_lb

Finally, the break-even price (BEP) was defined as the unit total cost, representing the minimum price per pound of live weight required to cover total production cost:BEP(USDlb−1)=TClb

Profitability analysis

Profitability indicators were calculated under a non-zero profit condition using processed whole-bird prices, following the methods of Alves et al. (2022; 2025). Because live broilers are not traded in a spot market in the United States, total live-weight output was converted into processed weight prior to revenue estimation. Total processed whole-bird weight sold annually (TWWOG, lb yr⁻¹) was calculated as:TWWOG=TLWxYwhere Y is the processing yield. A constant yield of 0.75 was applied to convert live weight into whole bird, without giblets (WOG) weight.

The processed whole-bird selling price (PWOG, USD lb⁻¹) was obtained from the Monthly National Chicken Report published by USDA Agricultural Marketing Service (AMS, 2023a). National Composite Whole Bird (WOG) prices were calculated as the average for January-June 2023, ensuring consistency with the cost structure used in the enterprise budgets.

In the first stage of analysis, this benchmark price was applied uniformly to all production systems (Scenarios A, A.1, and B.1) to enable standardized comparison of cost efficiency and profitability under identical selling conditions. In the second stage, a premium-price simulation was conducted exclusively for the pasture-based system (Scenario B.1). Premium prices were based on observed retail prices for direct-to-consumer pasture-raised chicken reported in the USDA-AMS National Monthly Pasture Raised Poultry Report (AMS, 2023b). Average values for January-June 2023 were used to represent prevailing market conditions for pasture-raised poultry.

Under each price assumption, total revenue (TR) per year was calculated as:TR=PWOGxTWWOG

The gross profit (GP), or net operating result, was obtained by subtracting the total cost from total revenue:GP=TR−TCyrwhere TCyr is the total annual operating cost. A positive gross profit indicates economic feasibility of the system under the given market conditions.

To identify the minimum production scale required to cover costs at the observed selling price, the break-even production point (BEPₚ) was calculated as:BEPP=TCyrPWOGxWxYwhere W is the average live weight per bird (lb.). This formulation allows the break-even point to be expressed in number of live birds marketed per year while accounting for processing yield in the revenue term. Assuming constant target live weight per bird, this value represents the minimum number of birds that must be marketed per year to achieve the zero-profit threshold.

The benefit–cost ratio (B/C) was used to evaluate the efficiency of resource allocation:B/C=TRTCyr

With values greater than 1.0 indicating that the operation generates returns above production costs.

Finally, the return on investment (ROI) quantified the relationship between profit and total capital invested in each production system:ROI%=GPTC_yrx100

In this sense, ROI represents return relative to total production cost, capturing profitability in relation to both fixed investments and operating expenditures, including working-capital requirements.

All profitability indicators were calculated on an annual basis, assuming constant biological performance across flocks. This approach enables direct comparison among systems differing in scale and management intensity, providing a comprehensive assessment of their financial viability under both standard market and premium-price conditions.

Sensitivity analysis

A deterministic sensitivity analysis was conducted to evaluate the robustness of the economic outcomes under variation in key biological and economic parameters. Three variables were selected based on their relevance to broiler production economics and biological performance: feed cost, labor cost, and mortality rate. Each parameter was varied independently by ±20% relative to its baseline value while all other model inputs were held constant. For mortality scenarios, feed consumption and feed costs were automatically recalculated according to the number of surviving birds, whereas labor requirements were assumed to remain unchanged. Feed cost, labor cost, and mortality were selected because they represent major economic and biological drivers of broiler production and account for a substantial proportion of production costs and performance variability.

For each scenario, total cost (TC), total revenue (TR), gross profit (GP), and return on investment (ROI) were recalculated. Results were expressed as percentage variation relative to the baseline scenario and were used to assess the relative influence of each parameter on system profitability and the stability of conclusions across production systems.

DISCUSSION

The economic outcomes observed in this study highlight that profitability in broiler production is strongly dependent on market prices, feed, chicks and labor costs, which remain the primary drivers of revenue and expenditure across production systems (Karaman et al., 2023; Adaszyńska-Skwirzyńska et al., 2025). However, the relative importance of cost components shifts between conventional and pasture-based systems. Feed and chick purchases dominate total costs in conventional systems, whereas labor costs are proportionally greater in pasture-based systems, reflecting differences in technological intensity and management requirements. This difference in cost structure was also reflected in the sensitivity analysis, which showed that the magnitude of the response to parameter variation differed among production systems. Feed cost exerted the greatest influence on profitability in conventional systems, whereas labor cost had a proportionally greater effect on the pasture-based model. Despite these differences, moderate changes in feed cost, labor cost, and mortality did not alter the relative economic performance of the modeled systems under conventional market conditions, supporting the robustness of the main conclusions.

Nevertheless, the deterministic sensitivity analysis evaluated the independent effect of each parameter while holding all other variables constant and therefore did not account for potential interactions or correlations among economic variables under different market conditions. Consequently, the results presented here reflect the economic conditions of a specific time period and should be interpreted with appropriate caution. Fluctuations in input and output prices can substantially alter both the magnitude and direction of profitability indicators (Marmelstein et al., 2024; Das et al., 2024), underscoring the dynamic nature of economic feasibility in poultry systems.

Rather than indicating that one production system is intrinsically superior to another, the findings demonstrate that conventional and pasture-based systems operate under distinct technical, biological, and market structures. In the present simulations, the large-scale conventional system exhibited the lowest unit cost of production (USD 0.52 lb⁻¹), compared with the medium-scale system (USD 0.64 lb⁻¹) and the pasture-based model (USD 1.92 lb⁻¹). These differences are best interpreted as outcomes of scale, management intensity, and cost-dilution capacity, rather than as a ranking of production systems by merit or desirability (Houedjofonon et al., 2020). There are several types of pasture-based production systems, including organic, free-range, and other outdoor-access models, which may differ substantially in management practices, productivity, and economic performance. The pasture-based production system modeled in this study was intended to represent a generalized alternative production scenario that allowed comparison of common production variables, particularly scale and management configuration, within a standardized economic framework.

Economies of scale emerged as a central driver of the observed financial patterns. Similar patterns have been reported in previous economic assessments of commercial broiler production, in which increasing scale was associated with lower unit production costs and improved fixed cost dilution (Mendes et al., 2014; Adaszyńska-Skwirzyńska et al., 2025). The increase in break-even price from large to medium scale (+22.7%) and from conventional to pasture-based systems (+268.7%) illustrates how reductions in output capacity are associated with substantial increases in unit production costs. Larger systems benefit from spreading fixed investments in housing, equipment, and infrastructure over substantially greater volumes of live weight (Alves et al., 2022; Honig et al., 2024), while smaller and outdoor-oriented systems face structural constraints in cost dilution (Cobanoglu et al., 2014; Vukina et al., 2014a), even when capital requirements are lower in absolute terms.

At the same time, pasture-based production represents a distinct production logic rather than simply a scaled-down version of conventional indoor systems. Design elements commonly associated with alternative systems, such as lower stocking densities, greater space allowance per bird, access to outdoor areas, and the use of bedding materials, are implemented to support animal welfare, thermal comfort, litter quality, and the expression of natural behaviors (EFSA, 2023; Campbell et al., 2025). While biologically and ethically relevant, these features are typically associated with higher labor requirements, increased material inputs, and reduced throughput per production cycle. Consistent with this production logic, we observed a shift in cost structure, with labor comprising a greater proportion of total variable costs in pasture-based systems compared to conventional systems. This pattern was further supported by the sensitivity analysis, in which labor-cost variation produced proportionally larger changes in profitability indicators in the pasture-based system than in the conventional systems. Consequently, pasture-based systems tend to be more management- and labor-intensive, which is reflected in their cost structure and higher break-even thresholds (Cobanoglu et al., 2014). By evaluating three production scenarios in the present study, we compared the specific effects of scale (A vs. A.1) and of scale combined with management approach (conventional versus pasture-based).

Under the standard market price scenario, the modeled pasture-based system did not reach its break-even point, illustrating the financial challenges such systems face when competing in undifferentiated commodity markets where production costs increase but output prices remain largely insensitive to system-specific attributes (Vukina et al., 2014a). However, these results should not be interpreted as evidence of economic unviability. Rather they reflect the strong dependence of pasture-based systems on market segmentation and value-based differentiation.

This dependence became evident under the premium-price scenario. When a price of USD 6.09 lb⁻¹, corresponding to the average whole-bird pasture raised chicken price reported by the USDA (AMS, 2023b) was applied, the pasture-based system shifted to positive profitability (ROI = 138.5%), with the break-even production threshold declining from 2,058 to 445 birds yr⁻¹. This change positioned the modeled system above the minimum production level required for financial sustainability.

Beyond whole bird, evidence indicates that pasture-raised and specialty poultry products, particularly when sold as differentiated cuts or through direct-to-consumer channels, may achieve price multiples substantially higher than those modeled here. For example, according to data from the National Pasture-Raised Poultry Report, during the third trimester of 2025 the retail price of pasture-raised boneless chicken breast averaged USD 15.66 lb⁻¹, compared with USD 4.17 lb⁻¹ for conventional boneless breast products, representing an approximately fourfold price differential (AMS, 2025).

Taken together, these results underscore that unit production cost alone is not a sufficient metric for evaluating the economic sustainability of alternative broiler production systems. Systems characterized by higher biological efficiency and greater scale typically achieve lower unit costs, whereas systems oriented toward animal welfare, environmental stewardship, or niche market attributes operate under different economic logics (Vukina et al., 2014a). In such contexts, higher production costs may be economically rational when aligned with consumer willingness to pay, product differentiation, and targeted marketing strategies, as observed in pastured poultry systems adapted for sustainability and niche markets (Rothrock et al., 2019).

From a practical perspective, the results of this study provide useful guidance for producers evaluating potential transitions between production models. The structured decomposition of cost components, break-even thresholds, and profitability indicators offers a transparent framework for identifying the most influential drivers of financial performance (Alves et al., 2025). Rather than prescribing a superior system, this analysis highlights that economic outcomes depend on the alignment between production scale, management strategy, and market positioning.

Some limitations of this study should be acknowledged. The modeling approach relies on static biological and economic parameters and does not capture short-term volatility in feed markets, episodic disease outbreaks, labor shortages, or policy-driven costs. Although the sensitivity analysis demonstrated that the main conclusions were robust to moderate variation in feed cost, labor cost, and mortality, the model does not represent the full range of uncertainty encountered under commercial conditions. Furthermore, the analysis did not incorporate stochastic variation or explicit relationships among economic and biological parameters. Evaluating such interactions would require assumptions regarding parameter distributions and covariance structures that were beyond the scope of the present study.

In addition, regional variation in infrastructure, climate, and consumer demand were not explicitly incorporated, and the modeled pasture-based system should be interpreted as a representative alternative production scenario rather than a comprehensive representation of all pasture-based, free-range, or organic production systems. Furthermore, the present study focused exclusively on economic performance and did not incorporate animal welfare, environmental impacts, or other sustainability indicators. Finally, the modeled scenarios were not externally validated against individual farm datasets and therefore should be interpreted as comparative production archetypes rather than direct predictions of farm-level performance.

Despite these limitations, the present study provides a structured and transparent framework for comparing broiler production systems under standardized assumptions. By isolating the effects of production scale and management approach, the model offers a useful basis for future research incorporating farm-level validation, broader sustainability metrics, and more complex evaluations of uncertainty under commercial conditions. Future research should explore dynamic pricing scenarios, stochastic risk modeling, interactions among economic and biological variables under different market conditions, broader geographic applications, and integrated assessments that combine economic, welfare, and environmental outcomes.

Conclusion

Overall, this study demonstrates that the economic feasibility of broiler production systems is strongly context dependent. Larger-scale, highly intensified systems benefit from pronounced economies of scale, while alternative systems can achieve financial viability when supported by appropriate price premiums and differentiated market access.

More broadly, the findings suggest that economic sustainability in poultry production cannot be evaluated solely on the basis of biological efficiency or unit production cost. Rather, profitability emerges from the interaction among production scale, management strategy, and market positioning. For producers, these results highlight the importance of aligning production practices with target markets and consumer expectations. For researchers, the study provides a comparative framework and foundation for future investigations integrating economic performance with animal welfare, environmental sustainability, and risk assessment across alternative poultry production systems.

The results indicate that the economic viability of different production systems depends not only on production efficiency, but also on the ability to capture value through appropriate market channels and product differentiation. Consequently, production systems should be evaluated within the specific technical, economic, and market contexts in which they operate.
