Optimal Prediction-Augmented Algorithms for Testing Independence of Distributions
Maryam Aliakbarpour, Alireza Azizi, Ria Stevens

Abstract
Independence testing is a fundamental problem in statistical inference: given samples from a joint distribution p p over multiple random variables, the goal is to determine whether p p is a product distribution or is ϵ \epsilon -far from all product distributions in total variation distance. In the non-parametric finite-sample regime, this task is notoriously expensive, as the minimax sample complexity scales polynomially with the support size. In this work, we move beyond these worst-case limitations by leveraging the framework of augmented distribution testing . We design independence testers that incorporate auxiliary, but potentially untrustworthy, predictive information. Our framework ensures that the tester remains robust, maintaining worst-case validity regardless of the prediction’s quality, while significantly improving sample efficiency when the prediction is accurate. Our main contributions include: (i) a bivariate independence tester for discrete distributions that adaptively reduces sample complexity based on the prediction error; (ii) a generalization to the high-dimensional multivariate setting for testing the independence of d d random variables; and (iii) matching minimax lower bounds demonstrating that our testers achieve optimal sample complexity.

Formally, given samples from a joint distribution over multiple random variables, the goal is to determine whether the variables are statistically independent or exhibit some form of dependence.

While classical approaches like the

Instead, their validity stems from either restrictive parametric assumptions or asymptotic properties that hold only in the limit as the number of samples tends to infinity. To move beyond these worst-case limitations, a emerging line of work suggests that the analyst need not operate in a vacuum. In many modern data science contexts, one often possesses auxiliary information: public datasets gathered from disparate institutions, or a distribution originating from historical data, a generative model, or domain-specific heuristics. While such information may be abundant, it often lacks formal quality guarantees. A natural question is to determine if one can leverage such
untrustworthy
predictive information in a rigorous manner—designing tests that remain mathematically sound (and robust) when the prediction is poor, yet achieve significantly improved sample efficiency when the prediction is accurate.

Recently, the framework of

and beyond

In this work, we bring the framework of augmented distribution testing to the problem of independence testing over a discrete support. Our main contributions are as follows:

{i)}
We develop an independence tester for a bivariate distribution with finite sample complexity and worst-case error guarantees. {ii)}
We generalize this result to the high-dimensional setting, testing the independence of multiple random variables.

{iii)}
We provide matching lower bounds which indicate that our testers achieve the optimal sample complexity.

1.1
Problem Statement

We adopt the framework of property testing of distributions
(
34
)
. In this setting, given sample access to a distribution
p
, a tester, with high probability, must output
accept

accept
if
p
belongs to a property
\mathcal{P}
(viewed as a set of distributions) and
reject
if
p
is
\epsilon
-far in total variation distance from every distribution in
\mathcal{P}

\mathcal{P}
to be the property of

In the augmented setting
(
8
)
, in addition to sample access to the distribution
p
, we are provided with a predicted distribution
\hat{p}
and a suggested error bound
\alpha
representing the purported accuracy of
\hat{p}
. We assume full knowledge of
\hat{p}
(i.e., its probability mass function is known). In this framework, an augmented independence tester has a third option beyond outputting
accept
or

inaccurate information
if the actual quality of the prediction is worse than the suggested error
\alpha
. However, the tester is required to provide an answer whenever the prediction is sufficiently accurate (i.e., when the suggested error
\alpha
is correct). Crucially, the tester cannot output an incorrect answer; if the tester does decide to answer the test, its output must be correct with high probability, regardless of the quality of the prediction. Formally, we define the desired augmented tester as follows:

•

Remark 1

(Finding the prediction error.)
.

Note that in many practical settings, the prediction error

1.3
Technical Overview

1.3.1
Background: Flattening

In constructing our upper bounds, we use a technique from the literature of standard distribution testing known as
flattening

(
30
)
.
This technique transforms one distribution testing problem into an equivalent problem over a larger domain by redistributing the mass of high-probability elements of the original distribution. By significantly decreasing the
\ell_{2}
-norm of this distribution, flattening enables the use of sample-efficient testing algorithms.

Standard Flattening.

Flattening divides the probability mass of each domain element of a distribution into element-wise equal buckets, defining a new distribution with a larger domain. Formally, given a distribution
p
over
[n]
and a set of bucket sizes
b_{i}
for all
i\in[n]
, flattening maps each element
i
to a tuple
(i,\ell)
, where
\ell
is sampled uniformly from
[b_{i}]
.
This operation defines a new distribution
p^{(F)}
such that:

p^{(F)}(i,\ell)=\frac{p(i)}{b_{i}}

(2)

Flattening satisfies two key properties, proved by
30
, that enable its use in distribution testing:

1.

If two distributions
p
and
q
are flattened using the same bucket sizes, their
\ell_{1}
-distance is preserved. As a result, instead of providing
p
and
q
to a tester, we may provide
p^{(F)}
and
q^{(F)}
and expect the same result.

2.

One can utilize a preliminary set of samples from
p
to identify which elements possess large probability mass. By setting the bucket sizes
b_{i}
proportional to these empirical frequencies, one can effectively “break down” the high-probability elements across multiple domain points. This ensures that, in expectation, the resulting flattened distribution
p^{(F)}
has a sufficiently small
\ell_{2}
-norm to permit sample-efficient testing.

Augmented Flattening.

8
extend the flattening techniques of
30
to the augmented setting, allowing the prediction,
\hat{p}
, to guide the number of buckets created for each element.
In addition to using knowledge gathered from sampling from
p
, this approach flattens elements that have a large probability mass under the prediction. This allows for a greater reduction of the
\ell_{2}
-norm when the prediction is accurate.

The augmented flattening technique of
8
begins by drawing a sample set
\mathcal{S}
of size
\mathrm{Poi}(s)
from
p
. Then, for all
i\in[n]
, they let
N_{i}
be the number of occurrences of
i\in\mathcal{S}
and choose the number of buckets for all
i
as:

b_{i}=\left\lfloor{n\cdot\hat{p}(i)}\right\rfloor+N_{i}+1.

(3)

The first term of this expression accounts for the influence of the prediction, assigning more buckets to elements with higher predicted probability mass. The second term accounts for information from the sample set, assigning more buckets to elements more frequently seen in
\mathcal{S}
.

8
prove that, when
\hat{p}
is
\alpha
-close to
p
, the distribution
p^{(F)}
defined by this augmented flattening has a small
\ell_{2}^{2}
-norm:

\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{\left\|p^{(F)}\right\|^{2}_{2}}\right.}{\left\|p^{(F)}\right\|^{2}_{2}}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{\left\|p^{(F)}\right\|^{2}_{2}}\right]}\leq\frac{2\alpha}{s}+\frac{4}{n}.

(4)

See Fact
A.4
for further details.

Closeness Testing via Flattening.

The problem of testing the closeness of two distributions over
[n]
involves distinguishing whether
p=q
or
\|p-q\|_{1}\geq\epsilon
with probability at least
0.9
. The minimax sample complexity for this task is
\Theta(n^{2/3}/\epsilon^{4/3}+\sqrt{n}/\epsilon^{2})

(
30
)
. A key advancement in this area is the development of closeness testers whose sample complexity scales with the
\ell_{2}
-norms of the underlying distributions rather than the raw support size
(
26
)
. Specifically, if the distributions have
\ell_{2}
-norms at most
b
, they can be tested using
O(n\,b/\epsilon^{2})
many samples, which in certain regimes can be much more efficient than the worst-case bounds suggest. This relationship between
\ell_{2}
-norm and testing complexity is the core motivation for the flattening technique: by reducing the
\ell_{2}
-norm of the distributions, one can leverage these refined testers to achieve optimal sample complexity. Indeed, this approach was central to establishing the optimal bounds for standard independence testing in
30
.

Independence Testing via Flattening.

Independence testing can be viewed as the task of distinguishing whether a joint distribution
p
is equal to the product of its marginals,
p_{1}\times p_{2}
, or
3\epsilon
-far from it
(
13
)
. From this perspective, one can use samples from
p
to simulate samples from both
p
and
p_{1}\times p_{2}
, subsequently employing a closeness tester to determine independence.

While a direct flattening of the joint domain
[n]\times[m]
where
n\geq m
yields a suboptimal sample complexity,
30
achieved the optimal rate of

O\left(\frac{n^{2/3}m^{1/3}}{\epsilon^{4/3}}+\frac{\sqrt{nm}}{\epsilon^{2}}\right)\,.

Roughly speaking, their approach relies on constructing a
product of flattenings
: by flattening each dimension independently based on its respective marginal, they ensured that an independent distribution
p
is transformed into a joint flattened distribution with a low
\ell_{2}
-norm. This makes the distribution amenable to the refined closeness tester of
26
, which is highly efficient for distributions with small
\ell_{2}
-norms.

1.3.2
Overview of our Results

Upper Bound for Two-Dimensional Augmented Independence Testing.

Our approach combines augmented flattening with the product-flattening technique by using the prediction
\hat{p}
to achieve a more efficient reduction of the marginal
\ell_{2}
-norms. The correctness of this approach hinges on a critical validation step: if
\hat{p}
is accurate, the flattened marginals are guaranteed to have low
\ell_{2}
-norms. Conversely, a violation of this bound serves as a certificate of the prediction’s inaccuracy. Furthermore, if
p
is independent, the flattened joint distribution must also exhibit a low
\ell_{2}
-norm. Our algorithm explicitly estimates this joint norm; if it exceeds the expected threshold, it serves as a certificate of non-independence, allowing us to
reject
immediately. This ensures that we either detect dependence early through norm inflation or proceed with a “well-behaved” distribution that can be tested efficiently for closeness to its marginals.

Lower Bound for Two-Dimensional Augmented Independence Testing.

Our lower bound for two-dimensional augmented independence testing follows in two separate cases defined by the relationships between
n,m,\alpha
and
\epsilon
. The first case establishes a bound of
\Omega(\sqrt{nm}/\epsilon^{2})
and follows from a reduction from the hard instance of standard independence testing
2
;
30
.
The second case establishes a bound of
\Omega\left(n^{2/3}m^{1/3}\alpha^{1/3}\epsilon^{-4/3}\right)
. To prove this bound, we construct two families of distributions which are information-theoretically indistinguishable but which an augmented tester must be able to distinguish. This construction builds upon techniques established in prior works
(
50
;
15
;
30
)

wherein a distribution’s distance to a property is hidden among “light” elements with low probability mass, while the majority of the probability mass is concentrated in “heavy” rows that are identical across the two families. Here, by a row, we mean the probability distribution over the second coordinate obtained by fixing the first coordinate.

In the standard setting, the heavy rows follow a uniform distribution, while the light rows are uniform in one family and
\epsilon
-far from uniform in the other.
This construction forces a tester to draw enough samples to effectively uncover the heavy rows. However, in our augmented setting, the prediction may reveal the locations of the heavy rows to the tester. We extend this technique to the augmented setting by designing our families of distributions such that only a
\mathcal{O}(\alpha)
-factor of the rows are heavy. By choosing the prediction to be the uniform distribution, we trap the tester: this prediction provides no information about which rows are heavy or light, yet is
\alpha
-close to the true distribution. This forces the tester to output
accept
or
reject
, rather than
inaccurate information
—essentially requiring it to differentiate between the two families.

We then provide an information-theoretic argument to demonstrate that the two families are indistinguishable without sufficient samples.
Following the framework of
30
, we
bound the mutual information between an indicator of a distribution’s family and a sample set drawn from that distribution.
To do so, we show that the mutual information is only non-negligible when the tester sees a collision in (i.e. draws multiple samples from) a light row. Heavy rows contribute no mutual information because they’re identical across the families, while without collisions in a light row, a tester gains no information about whether that row is uniform or
\epsilon
-far from uniform.
Under our hard instance, the probability of the tester observing enough collisions in the light rows is small unless the sample complexity is large.

Upper Bound for
d
-Dimensional Augmented Independence Testing.

A naïve approach to extending augmented independence testing to a
d
-dimensional distribution is to apply augmented flattening independently to all marginals, and then perform closeness testing between the flattened joint distribution and the product of the flattened marginals. However, augmented flattening increases the domain size of each coordinate by at least a constant factor (greater than 2). This incurs an additional
2^{\mathcal{O}(d)}
factor in the sample complexity for general
d
, which becomes prohibitive for large
d
. In contrast, this overhead remains harmless in low dimensions. Indeed, we leverage this approach to obtain a three-dimensional tester, which serves as a building block for our higher-dimensional construction. To achieve the general tester, we partition the
d
coordinates into at most three groups, each having total domain size at most
\sqrt{N}
, where
N\coloneqq\prod_{i=1}^{d}n_{i}
. If any coordinate has a domain size greater than
\sqrt{N}
, we isolate it as a singleton set and aggregate the remaining coordinates into a second group. After this partitioning, we first invoke a two- or three-dimensional augmented tester to check independence across the resulting groups. If this test outputs
accept
, it remains to verify independence within each group. By construction, each group has a total domain size of at most
\sqrt{N}
, which allows us to learn the empirical distribution over each group up to
\ell_{1}
error of
\Theta(\epsilon)
using only
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{N}/\epsilon^{2})
samples.

By explicitly bounding the error introduced in learning the marginals, we show that for a product distribution the learned empirical distribution is close to the product of its marginal as well. Therefore, independence testing can be carried out on the empirical distribution.
This final step is entirely computational and requires no additional samples.

Lower Bound for
d
-Dimensional Augmented Independence Testing.

Our lower bound for
d
-dimensional augmented independence testing follows from a reduction from the two-dimensional augmented independence testing problem. Although this reduction does not apply to all distributions, we show that a
d
-dimensional tester can nevertheless distinguish the specific hard instances used to establish the lower bound in the two-dimensional case. In particular, we show that a two-dimensional distribution can be reshaped into a
d
-dimensional distribution while preserving both its distance from a product distribution and to the prediction. This argument relies on a structural property of the hard instances constructed in the two-dimensional setting, namely that the corresponding product distributions are uniform. As a consequence, we can solve two-dimensional augmented independence testing by reshaping two-dimensional samples into
d
-dimensional samples, applying a
d
-dimensional tester, and returning its output.

Lower Bounds for Multivariate Augmented Independence Testing

In this section, we give lower bounds for augmented independence testing in higher dimensions by proving that
d
-dimensional independence testing is at least as hard as two-dimensional independence testing. These lower bounds are tight, matching the upper bounds given in Theorem
6
.

Theorem 8
.

Let
p
be a distribution over the product domain
\prod_{i=1}^{d}[n_{i}]
, and
\hat{p}
be a prediction of
p
over the same domain. Assume w.l.o.g. that
n_{1}\geq n_{2}\geq\ldots\geq n_{d}\geq 2
. Let
N\coloneqq\prod_{i=1}^{d}n_{i}
denote the total domain size. Fix parameters
\alpha\in[0,1]
, and
\epsilon\in(0,1/3)
. Any
(\alpha,\epsilon,\delta)
-augmented independence tester must use
\Omega\left(\max_{j}\left\{\frac{\sqrt{N}}{\epsilon^{2}},\frac{n_{j}^{1/3}N^{1/3}\alpha^{1/3}}{\epsilon^{4/3}}\right\}\right)
samples to succeed.

Proof.

We reduce the hard instance of two-dimensional independence testing (as described in Section
4
) to
d
-dimensional independence testing. Assume that there is an algorithm,
\mathcal{A}_{d}
, for
d
-dimensional augmented independence testing of distributions over
[n_{1}]\times\ldots[n_{d}]
.
Consider the following algorithm for two-dimensional augmented independence testing which has sample access to a distribution
\tilde{p}
over
[n_{1}]\times\left[\prod_{i=2}^{d}n_{i}\right]
and takes as input a prediction
\hat{\tilde{p}}
, a proximity parameter
\epsilon
and an estimate of the prediction error
\alpha
:

1.

Draw
k
samples from
\tilde{p}
.

2.

Reshape each sample to a sample over
\left[n_{1}\right]\times\left[n_{2}\right]\times\ldots\times\left[n_{d}\right]
by leaving the first coordinate unchanged and mapping the second coordinate to a tuple in
\left[n_{2}\right]\times\ldots\times\left[n_{d}\right]
. This mapping unflattens the second dimension into dimensions
2
to
d
, varying the last dimension the fastest.

3.

Reshape
\hat{\tilde{p}}
to
\hat{{p}}
via the same mapping.

4.

Return the result of
\mathcal{A}_{d}
on the reshaped samples, the reshaped prediction,
\epsilon
and
\alpha
.

We complete our reduction by proving that this algorithm succeeds on the hard instance of two-dimensional augmented independence testing. Let
\tilde{p}
be the hard instance of two-dimensional independence testing over
[n_{1}]\times\left[\prod_{i=2}^{d}n_{i}\right]
with proximity parameter
\epsilon
. If
\alpha\leq\frac{\sqrt{\prod_{i=2}^{d}n_{i}}}{\sqrt{n_{1}}\epsilon^{2}}
, consider
\tilde{p}
as the hard instance of the first case of the two-dimensional lower bound, described in Section
4.1
. Else, consider
\tilde{p}
as the hard instance of the second case of the two-dimensional lower bound, described in Section
4.2
. Let
p
be the distribution of the reshaped samples. We prove that if the
d
-dimensional tester succeeds on
p
, then the two-dimensional tester succeeds on
\tilde{p}
.

First, observe that the reshaping described is a mapping between domain elements of the distributions and does not affect the probabilities of these elements.
Because total variation distance is preserved under this reshaping, the error of the reshaped prediction, or its distance from the reshaped distribution, matches the error of the original prediction.

Then, consider the
accept
instance of each case of the two-dimensional lower bound.
For the first case of this bound (Section
4.1
), recall that the
accept
instance is the uniform distribution. Under the reshaping,
p
remains uniform and, consequently, independent.
In the second case (Section
4.2
), the
accept
instance is the product of two independent distributions, where the first marginal is random and the second is uniform. Reshaping this uniform second marginal produces a uniform—and therefore, a product—distribution over
[n_{2}]\times\ldots\times[n_{d}]
. Because the first marginal was independent of the original second marginal, it must be independent of the reshaped marginals. Therefore, the reshaped distribution is a product distribution.

For the
reject
instance, in both cases of the lower bound, we know that
\tilde{p}
is
\epsilon
-far from any product distribution and want to show that
p
is also
\epsilon
-far from any product distribution. We will prove the contrapositive of this implication: if there exists a product distribution
q
over
[n_{1}]\penalty\ \times\penalty\ \ldots\penalty\ \times\penalty\ [n_{d}]
such that
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(p,q\right)\leq\epsilon
, there must exist a product distribution
\tilde{q}
over

[n_{1}]\times\left[\prod_{i=2}^{d}n_{i}\right]
such that
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(\tilde{p},\tilde{q}\right)\leq\epsilon
. Assume such a distribution
q
exists. Following the mapping described above, reshape
q
to a distribution
\tilde{q}
over
[n_{1}]\times\left[\prod_{i=2}^{d}n_{i}\right]
. Because
q
is a product distribution, its coordinates are all mutually independent. In particular, its first coordinate is independent of the tuple of the remaining coordinates and
\tilde{q}
, whose marginals correspond to the distributions of the first coordinate and the tuple of remaining coordinates, is a product distribution.
Further, because the reshaping preserves total variation distance,
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(\tilde{p},\tilde{q}\right)=\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(p,q\right)\leq\epsilon
. Therefore,
\tilde{p}
is
\epsilon
-close to a product distribution and the contrapositive is proved.

Ultimately, we have: (i) the distance between the distribution and the prediction is unchanged after the reshaping; (ii) when
\tilde{p}
is a product distribution,
p
is also a product distribution; and (iii) when
\tilde{p}
is
\epsilon
-far from all product distributions over its support,
p
is far from all product distributions over its
support. As
\mathcal{A}_{d}
is an augmented
d
-dimensional independence tester,
\mathcal{A}_{2}
can distinguish between
p
being a product distribution or being
\epsilon
-far from a product distribution.
In other words, a correct decision by
\mathcal{A}_{d}
on the reshaped distribution
p
leads to a correct decision by
\mathcal{A}_{2}
on the hard instance
\tilde{p}
. Therefore,
\mathcal{A}_{2}
is an augmented two-dimensional independence tester that succeeds on
\tilde{p}
. We can thus lower bound the sample complexity of augmented
d
-dimensional independence testing of distributions by the sample complexity of augmented independence testing of
\tilde{p}
. Substituting the bounds established in Section
4
, we have that any algorithm for augmented independence testing over
[n_{1}]\times\ldots[n_{d}]
with proximity
\epsilon
and prediction error
\alpha
requires at least
\Omega\left(\max_{j}\left\{\frac{\sqrt{N}}{\epsilon^{2}},\frac{n_{j}^{1/3}N^{1/3}\alpha^{1/3}}{\epsilon^{4/3}}\right\}\right)
samples.

∎

is
\textnormal{{Poi}}(k\cdot q(i,j))
and the size of
\mathcal{S}
is sum of all

. Because the sum of Poisson random variables is itself a Poisson random variable,

\left|{\mathcal{S}}\right|=\sum_{(i,j)}a_{i,j}\sim\textnormal{{Poi}}\left(\sum_{(i,j)}k\cdot q(i,j)\right)=\textnormal{{Poi}}\left(k\cdot\left\|q\right\|_{1}\right)

(98)

To prove that
\left|{\mathcal{S}}\right|
is at least
k/100
with high probability, we prove via a Hoeffding bound that
\left\|q\right\|_{1}
is
\Theta(1)
and apply a Chernoff bound for Poisson random variables. Consider the norm of
q

\left\|q\right\|_{1}=\sum_{i\in[n]}\sum_{j\in m}\left|{c_{i}\cdot\mu(i,j)}\right|=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{H}}c_{i}\sum_{j\in m}\left|{\mu(i,j)}\right|+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{H}^{\mathsf{c}}}c_{i}\sum_{j\in m}\left|{\mu(i,j)}\right|.

(99)

When
X=0
,
\mu(i,j)
is always
1/m
. Therefore, (
99
) is:

\left\|q\right\|_{1}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{H}}c_{i}\sum_{j\in[m]}\frac{1}{m}+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{H}^{\mathsf{c}}}c_{i}\sum_{j\in[m]}\frac{1}{m}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{H}}c_{i}+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{H}^{\mathsf{c}}}c_{i}=C.

(100)

On the other hand, when
X=1
,
\mu(i,j)
depends on

, when

\mu(i,j)
is
(1+\epsilon^{\prime}_{ij})/m
. In this case, (

\displaystyle\left\|q\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{H}}c_{i}\sum_{j\in[m]}\frac{1}{m}+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{H}^{\mathsf{c}}}c_{i}\sum_{j\in[m]}\left|{\frac{1+\epsilon^{\prime}_{ij}}{m}}\right|

(101)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{H}}c_{i}\sum_{j\in[m]}\frac{1}{m}+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{H}^{\mathsf{c}}}c_{i}\sum_{j\in[m]}{\frac{1+\epsilon^{\prime}_{ij}}{m}}

(102)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{H}}c_{i}+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{H}^{\mathsf{c}}}c_{i}+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{H}^{\mathsf{c}}}c_{i}\sum_{j\in[m]}\frac{\epsilon^{\prime}_{ij}}{m}

(103)

\displaystyle=C+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{H}^{\mathsf{c}}}\sum_{j\in[m]}\frac{\epsilon^{\prime}_{ij}}{nm}.

(104)

As each

, we may apply a Hoeffding bound to their sum:

\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{\left|{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{H}^{\mathsf{c}}}\sum_{j\in[m]}\frac{\epsilon^{\prime}_{ij}}{nm}}\right|\geq 0.1}\right.}{\left|{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{H}^{\mathsf{c}}}\sum_{j\in[m]}\frac{\epsilon^{\prime}_{ij}}{nm}}\right|\geq 0.1}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{\left|{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{H}^{\mathsf{c}}}\sum_{j\in[m]}\frac{\epsilon^{\prime}_{ij}}{nm}}\right|\geq 0.1}\right]}\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{0.005\cdot n^{2}m}{\left|{\mathcal{H}^{\mathsf{c}}}\right|\epsilon^{{}^{\prime}2}}\right).

From (

\left|{\mathcal{H}^{\mathsf{c}}}\right|\leq n
, or, equivalently,

. Substituting this inequality simplifies our Hoeffding bound to:

(106)

where the final inequality holds provided

is sufficiently large.
Combining this bound with the bounds on

established in (

) and (

), we have, when

,

(107)

and, when

(108)

Because

with high probability, regardless of

with high probability. We will now prove a high probability lower bound of

\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{\left|{\mathcal{S}}\right|\leq\frac{k}{100}\mid 0.9\leq\left\|q\right\|_{1}}\right.}{\left|{\mathcal{S}}\right|\leq\frac{k}{100}\mid 0.9\leq\left\|q\right\|_{1}}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{\left|{\mathcal{S}}\right|\leq\frac{k}{100}\mid 0.9\leq\left\|q\right\|_{1}}\right]}=\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}_{S\sim\textnormal{{Poi}}\left(k\left\|q\right\|_{1}\right)}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{S\leq\frac{k}{100}\mid 0.9\leq\left\|q\right\|_{1}}\right.}{S\leq\frac{k}{100}\mid 0.9\leq\left\|q\right\|_{1}}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{S\leq\frac{k}{100}\mid 0.9\leq\left\|q\right\|_{1}}\right]}\leq\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}_{S\sim\textnormal{{Poi}}\left(0.9k\right)}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{S\leq\frac{k}{100}}\right.}{S\leq\frac{k}{100}}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{S\leq\frac{k}{100}}\right]}.

Applying a Chernoff bound specific to Poisson distributions (see, for example, Theorem 5.4 of

(110)

where the final inequality holds for any

. Combining (

) and (

2

(111)

Proof.

The standard definition of mutual information is given by:

\displaystyle I(X;A)

\displaystyle=\sum_{v}\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}}\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{A=v,X=x}\right.}{A=v,X=x}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{A=v,X=x}\right]}\log\left(\frac{\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{A=v,X=x}\right.}{A=v,X=x}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{A=v,X=x}\right]}}{\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{X=x}\right.}{X=x}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{X=x}\right]}\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{A=v}\right.}{A=v}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{A=v}\right]}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{v}\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}}\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{X=x}\right.}{X=x}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{X=x}\right]}\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x}\right.}{A=v\mid X=x}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x}\right]}\log\left(\frac{\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x}\right.}{A=v\mid X=x}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x}\right]}}{\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{A=v}\right.}{A=v}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{A=v}\right]}}\right)

For simplicity, we introduce the following notation:

•

\beta=\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\max}(v)}\right.}{A=v\mid X=x_{\max}(v)}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\max}(v)}\right]}

•

\gamma=\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\min}(v)}\right.}{A=v\mid X=x_{\min}(v)}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\min}(v)}\right]}

•

p=\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{A=v}\right.}{A=v}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{A=v}\right]}=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\max}(v)}\right.}{A=v\mid X=x_{\max}(v)}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\max}(v)}\right]}+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\min}(v)}\right.}{A=v\mid X=x_{\min}(v)}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\min}(v)}\right]}=\frac{\beta+\gamma}{2}

Substituting these into the definition of mutual information gives:

\displaystyle I(X;A)

\displaystyle=\sum_{v}\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}}\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{X=x}\right.}{X=x}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{X=x}\right]}\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x}\right.}{A=v\mid X=x}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x}\right]}\log\left(\frac{\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x}\right.}{A=v\mid X=x}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x}\right]}}{\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{A=v}\right.}{A=v}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{A=v}\right]}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{v}\frac{1}{2}\beta\log\left(\frac{\beta}{p}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\gamma\log\left(\frac{\gamma}{p}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{v}\frac{1}{2}\beta\log\beta+\frac{1}{2}\gamma\log\gamma-\frac{\beta+\gamma}{2}\log p

\displaystyle=\sum_{v}\frac{\beta-p}{2}\log\beta+\frac{\gamma-p}{2}\log\gamma+\frac{p}{2}\left(\log\beta+\log\gamma\right)-p\log p

\displaystyle=\sum_{v}\frac{\beta-\gamma}{4}\log\beta+\frac{\gamma-\beta}{4}\log\gamma+p\log\sqrt{\beta\gamma}-p\log p

\displaystyle=\sum_{v}\frac{\beta-\gamma}{4}\log\left(\frac{\beta}{\gamma}\right)+p\log\left(\frac{\sqrt{\beta\gamma}}{p}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{v}\frac{\beta-\gamma}{4\ln(2)}\left(\frac{\beta}{\gamma}-1\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{v}\frac{1}{4\ln(2)}\frac{(\beta-\gamma)^{2}}{\gamma}

\displaystyle=\sum_{v}\frac{\gamma}{4\ln(2)}\left(\frac{\beta}{\gamma}-1\right)^{2},

where the last inequality holds since
\ln(x)\leq x-1
and the geometric mean is always less than or equal to the arithmetic mean, i.e.,
\sqrt{\beta\gamma}\leq p=\frac{\beta+\gamma}{2}
. Substituting the expressions for
\beta
and
\gamma
gives the desired upper-bound on the mutual information:

\displaystyle I(X;A)

\displaystyle=\sum_{v}\mathcal{O}\left(\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\min}(v)}\right.}{A=v\mid X=x_{\min}(v)}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\min}(v)}\right]}\left(1-\frac{\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\max}(v)}\right.}{A=v\mid X=x_{\max}(v)}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\max}(v)}\right]}}{\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\min}(v)}\right.}{A=v\mid X=x_{\min}(v)}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\min}(v)}\right]}}\right)^{2}\right).

∎

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\left(1-\frac{\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{\left|{A}\right|=0\mid X=x_{\max}(\mathbf{0}),c_{i}=1/n}\right.}{\left|{A}\right|=0\mid X=x_{\max}(\mathbf{0}),c_{i}=1/n}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{\left|{A}\right|=0\mid X=x_{\max}(\mathbf{0}),c_{i}=1/n}\right]}}{\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{\left|{A}\right|=0\mid X=x_{\min}(\mathbf{0}),c_{i}=1/n}\right.}{\left|{A}\right|=0\mid X=x_{\min}(\mathbf{0}),c_{i}=1/n}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{\left|{A}\right|=0\mid X=x_{\min}(\mathbf{0}),c_{i}=1/n}\right]}}\right)^{2}\right).

(120)

To bound this expression, we calculate
\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{\left|{A}\right|=0\mid X=0,c_{i}=1/n}\right.}{\left|{A}\right|=0\mid X=0,c_{i}=1/n}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{\left|{A}\right|=0\mid X=0,c_{i}=1/n}\right]}
and
\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{\left|{A}\right|=0\mid X=1,c_{i}=1/n}\right.}{\left|{A}\right|=0\mid X=1,c_{i}=1/n}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{\left|{A}\right|=0\mid X=1,c_{i}=1/n}\right]}
. As, conditioned on
X=x
, each
a_{ij}
is i.i.d., the probability that
\left|{A}\right|=0
is the product of the probabilities that
a_{ij}=0
. As a result, we have:

\displaystyle\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{\left|{A}\right|=0\mid X=0,c_{i}=1/n}\right.}{\left|{A}\right|=0\mid X=0,c_{i}=1/n}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{\left|{A}\right|=0\mid X=0,c_{i}=1/n}\right]}=\prod_{j=1}^{m}\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{a_{i,j}=0}\right.}{a_{i,j}=0}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{a_{i,j}=0}\right]}=\prod_{j=1}^{m}e^{-\frac{k}{nm}}=e^{-\frac{k}{n}},

and

\displaystyle\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{\left|{A}\right|=0\mid X=1,c_{i}=1/n}\right.}{\left|{A}\right|=0\mid X=1,c_{i}=1/n}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{\left|{A}\right|=0\mid X=1,c_{i}=1/n}\right]}

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{m}\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{a_{i,j}=0}\right.}{a_{i,j}=0}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{a_{i,j}=0}\right]}

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{m}\frac{1}{2}\left(e^{-\frac{k(1+\epsilon)}{nm}}+e^{-\frac{k(1-\epsilon)}{nm}}\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{m}e^{-\frac{k}{nm}}\left(\frac{e^{-\frac{k\epsilon}{nm}}+e^{\frac{k\epsilon}{nm}}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=e^{-\frac{k}{n}}\cdot\cosh^{m}\left(\frac{k\epsilon}{nm}\right).

As
\cosh(x)\geq 1
, we know
x_{\max}=1
and
x_{\min}=0
. The ratio between the probabilities in question is therefore upper-bounded as:

\cosh^{m}\left(\frac{k\epsilon}{nm}\right)\leq e^{\frac{m}{2}\left(\frac{k\epsilon}{nm}\right)^{2}}\leq 1+\frac{k^{2}\epsilon^{2}}{n^{2}m},

where we used
\cosh(x)\leq e^{x^{2}/2}
, and
e^{x}\leq 1+2x
for all
x\in[0,1]
.
This implies that (
120
) satisfies:

\mathcal{O}\left(\left(1-\frac{\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{\left|{A}\right|=0\mid X=x_{\max}(\mathbf{0}),c_{i}=1/n}\right.}{\left|{A}\right|=0\mid X=x_{\max}(\mathbf{0}),c_{i}=1/n}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{\left|{A}\right|=0\mid X=x_{\max}(\mathbf{0}),c_{i}=1/n}\right]}}{\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{\left|{A}\right|=0\mid X=x_{\min}(\mathbf{0}),c_{i}=1/n}\right.}{\left|{A}\right|=0\mid X=x_{\min}(\mathbf{0}),c_{i}=1/n}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{\left|{A}\right|=0\mid X=x_{\min}(\mathbf{0}),c_{i}=1/n}\right]}}\right)^{2}\right)\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{k^{4}\epsilon^{4}}{n^{4}m^{2}}\right).

∎

Lemma C.3
.

For an arbitrary
i\in[n]
, let
A\coloneqq A_{i}
be a vector of
m
counts, such that
a_{i,j}\sim\textnormal{{Poi}}(kq(i,j))

). For all

x_{\min}(v)
and

as in (

\displaystyle\sum_{v:\left\|v\right\|_{1}=1}\mathcal{O}

\displaystyle\left(\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\min}(v)}\right.}{A=v\mid X=x_{\min}(v)}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\min}(v)}\right]}\left(1-\frac{\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\max}(v),c_{i}=1/n}\right.}{A=v\mid X=x_{\max}(v),c_{i}=1/n}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\max}(v),c_{i}=1/n}\right]}}{\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\min}(v),c_{i}=1/n}\right.}{A=v\mid X=x_{\min}(v),c_{i}=1/n}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\min}(v),c_{i}=1/n}\right]}}\right)^{2}\right)

Proof.

There are
\binom{m}{1}
standard basis vectors such that
\lvert v\rvert_{1}=1
, each having a single 1 in coordinate
j^{\prime}
and zeros elsewhere. The probability of
A=v
conditioned on
X=x
and

can be calculated considering the independence of each
a_{i,j}
conditioned on
X=x
as:

\displaystyle\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{A=v\mid X=0,c_{i}=1/n}\right.}{A=v\mid X=0,c_{i}=1/n}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{A=v\mid X=0,c_{i}=1/n}\right]}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{a_{i,j^{\prime}}=1}\right.}{a_{i,j^{\prime}}=1}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{a_{i,j^{\prime}}=1}\right]}\prod_{j\neq j^{\prime}}\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{a_{i,j}=0}\right.}{a_{i,j}=0}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{a_{i,j}=0}\right]}

\displaystyle=\frac{k}{nm}e^{-\frac{k}{nm}}\left(e^{-\frac{k}{nm}}\right)^{m-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{k}{nm}e^{-\frac{k}{n}},

(121)

\displaystyle\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{A=v\mid X=1,c_{i}=1/n}\right.}{A=v\mid X=1,c_{i}=1/n}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{A=v\mid X=1,c_{i}=1/n}\right]}=\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{a_{i,j^{\prime}}=1}\right.}{a_{i,j^{\prime}}=1}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{a_{i,j^{\prime}}=1}\right]}\prod_{j\neq j^{\prime}}\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{a_{i,j}=0}\right.}{a_{i,j}=0}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{a_{i,j}=0}\right]}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{k(1+\epsilon)}{nm}e^{-\frac{k(1+\epsilon)}{nm}}+\frac{k(1-\epsilon)}{nm}e^{-\frac{k(1-\epsilon)}{nm}}\right)\prod_{j\neq j^{\prime}}\frac{1}{2}\left(e^{-\frac{k(1+\epsilon)}{nm}}+e^{-\frac{k(1-\epsilon)}{nm}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{k}{nm}e^{-\frac{k}{nm}}\left(\cosh\left(\frac{k\epsilon}{nm}\right)-\epsilon\sinh\left(\frac{k\epsilon}{nm}\right)\right)\prod_{j\neq j^{\prime}}e^{-\frac{k}{nm}}\cosh\left(\frac{k\epsilon}{nm}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{k}{nm}e^{-\frac{k}{n}}\cosh^{m}\left(\frac{k\epsilon}{nm}\right)\left(1-\epsilon\tanh\left(\frac{k\epsilon}{nm}\right)\right),

(122)

We now bound the ratio between probabilities in (
C.4
) and (
C.4
) by setting

and applying the inequalities

, and

for all

. Note that

since
\frac{k}{n}\leq 0.5
.

\displaystyle 1-\epsilon u

\displaystyle\leq\cosh^{m}(u)\left(1-\epsilon\tanh(u)\right)\leq e^{\frac{mu^{2}}{2}}\left(1-\epsilon u+\epsilon\frac{u^{3}}{3}\right)

\displaystyle 1-\epsilon u

\displaystyle\leq\cosh^{m}(u)\left(1-\epsilon\tanh(u)\right)\leq e^{\frac{\epsilon u}{4}}\left(1-\frac{11}{12}\epsilon u\right)\leq 1

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\quad\frac{1}{1-\epsilon u}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\cosh^{m}(u)\left(1-\epsilon\tanh(u)\right)}\geq 1

(123)

Since

, we have

. Therefore, we can use the inequality

and thus:

(124)

The next step is to demonstrate that
\sum_{v:\left\|v\right\|_{1}=1}\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\min}(v)}\right.}{A=v\mid X=x_{\min}(v)}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\min}(v)}\right]}=\mathcal{O}\left(k/n\right)

\displaystyle\sum_{v:\left\|v\right\|_{1}=1}\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\min}(v)}\right.}{A=v\mid X=x_{\min}(v)}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\min}(v)}\right]}\leq\sum_{v:\left\|v\right\|_{1}=1}\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{A=v\mid X=0}\right.}{A=v\mid X=0}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{A=v\mid X=0}\right]}.

\displaystyle\sum_{v:\left\|v\right\|_{1}=1}\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{A=v\mid X=0}\right.}{A=v\mid X=0}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{A=v\mid X=0}\right]}

(129)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\cdot\left(1-\frac{\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\max}(v),c_{i}=1/n}\right.}{A=v\mid X=x_{\max}(v),c_{i}=1/n}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\max}(v),c_{i}=1/n}\right]}}{\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\min}(v),c_{i}=1/n}\right.}{A=v\mid X=x_{\min}(v),c_{i}=1/n}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\min}(v),c_{i}=1/n}\right]}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{k}{n\alpha}\right)\cdot\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\min}(v),c_{i}=1/n}\right.}{A=v\mid X=x_{\min}(v),c_{i}=1/n}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\min}(v),c_{i}=1/n}\right]}\cdot\left(1-\frac{\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\max}(v),c_{i}=1/n}\right.}{A=v\mid X=x_{\max}(v),c_{i}=1/n}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\max}(v),c_{i}=1/n}\right]}}{\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\min}(v),c_{i}=1/n}\right.}{A=v\mid X=x_{\min}(v),c_{i}=1/n}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\min}(v),c_{i}=1/n}\right]}}\right)^{2}.

Thus far, we have shown that, for a fixed
v
such that
\left\|v\right\|_{1}\geq 2
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\min}(v)}\right.}{A=v\mid X=x_{\min}(v)}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\min}(v)}\right]}\left(1-\frac{\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\max}(v)}\right.}{A=v\mid X=x_{\max}(v)}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\max}(v)}\right]}}{\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\min}(v)}\right.}{A=v\mid X=x_{\min}(v)}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\min}(v)}\right]}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{k}{n\alpha}\right)\cdot\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\min}(v),c_{i}=1/n}\right.}{A=v\mid X=x_{\min}(v),c_{i}=1/n}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\min}(v),c_{i}=1/n}\right]}\cdot\left(1-\frac{\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\max}(v),c_{i}=1/n}\right.}{A=v\mid X=x_{\max}(v),c_{i}=1/n}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\max}(v),c_{i}=1/n}\right]}}{\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\min}(v),c_{i}=1/n}\right.}{A=v\mid X=x_{\min}(v),c_{i}=1/n}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\min}(v),c_{i}=1/n}\right]}}\right)^{2}

(130)

We can apply this bound to bound the sum over all
v
with
\left\|v\right\|_{1}\geq 2
as:

\displaystyle\sum_{v:\left\|v\right\|_{1}\geq 2}

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\left(\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\min}(v)}\right.}{A=v\mid X=x_{\min}(v)}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\min}(v)}\right]}\left(1-\frac{\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\max}(v)}\right.}{A=v\mid X=x_{\max}(v)}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\max}(v)}\right]}}{\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\min}(v)}\right.}{A=v\mid X=x_{\min}(v)}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\min}(v)}\right]}}\right)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{v:\left\|v\right\|_{1}\geq 2}\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{k}{n\alpha}\cdot\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\min}(v),c_{i}=1/n}\right.}{A=v\mid X=x_{\min}(v),c_{i}=1/n}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\min}(v),c_{i}=1/n}\right]}\cdot\left(1-\frac{\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\max}(v),c_{i}=1/n}\right.}{A=v\mid X=x_{\max}(v),c_{i}=1/n}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\max}(v),c_{i}=1/n}\right]}}{\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\min}(v),c_{i}=1/n}\right.}{A=v\mid X=x_{\min}(v),c_{i}=1/n}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\min}(v),c_{i}=1/n}\right]}}\right)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{k}{n\alpha}\right)\sum_{v}\mathcal{O}\left(\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\min}(v),c_{i}=1/n}\right.}{A=v\mid X=x_{\min}(v),c_{i}=1/n}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\min}(v),c_{i}=1/n}\right]}\cdot\left(1-\frac{\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\max}(v),c_{i}=1/n}\right.}{A=v\mid X=x_{\max}(v),c_{i}=1/n}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\max}(v),c_{i}=1/n}\right]}}{\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\min}(v),c_{i}=1/n}\right.}{A=v\mid X=x_{\min}(v),c_{i}=1/n}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\min}(v),c_{i}=1/n}\right]}}\right)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{k}{n\alpha}\right)\Theta\left(I\left(X;A\mid c_{i}=\frac{1}{n}\right)\right).

(131)

The chain rule for mutual information allows us to bound
I(X;A\mid c_{i}=1/n)
as:

I\left(X;A\mid c_{i}=\frac{1}{n}\right)\leq\sum_{j=1}^{m}I\left(X;a_{i,j}\mid c_{i}=\frac{1}{n}\right)

When
c_{i}=1/n
,
a_{i,j}
follows the distribution of the random variable considered in Lemma
C.6
. This lemma allows us to bound the mutual information between
X
and each element of row
i
in the sample set.

Applying the bound given in the lemma, we have:

\sum_{j=1}^{m}I\left(X;a_{i,j}\mid c_{i}=\frac{1}{n}\right)\leq\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\epsilon^{4}k^{2}}{n^{2}m^{2}}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\epsilon^{4}k^{2}}{n^{2}m}\right)

(132)

Ultimately, combining Equation (
131
) and Equation (
132
), we have our desired bound:

\sum_{v:\left\|v\right\|_{1}\geq 2}\mathcal{O}\left(\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\min}(v)}\right.}{A=v\mid X=x_{\min}(v)}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\min}(v)}\right]}\left(1-\frac{\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\max}(v)}\right.}{A=v\mid X=x_{\max}(v)}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\max}(v)}\right]}}{\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\min}(v)}\right.}{A=v\mid X=x_{\min}(v)}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x_{\min}(v)}\right]}}\right)^{2}\right)\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\epsilon^{4}k^{3}}{n^{3}m\alpha}\right)

∎

Lemma C.5
.

Let
x\in\{0,1\},v\in\mathbb{N}^{m}
such that
\left\|v\right\|_{1}\geq 2\}
. Let
c_{i}
be a random variable and
q
a distribution, defined according to (
31
) where
X=x
. For an arbitrary
i\in[n]
, let
A\coloneqq A_{i}
be a vector of
m
counts, such that
a_{i,j}\sim\textnormal{{Poi}}(kq(i,j))
for all
j\in[m]
. Then, the following holds.

\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{A=v,c_{i}=1/n\mid X=x}\right.}{A=v,c_{i}=1/n\mid X=x}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{A=v,c_{i}=1/n\mid X=x}\right]}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{k}{n\alpha}\right)\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{A=v,c_{i}=1/k\mid X=x}\right.}{A=v,c_{i}=1/k\mid X=x}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{A=v,c_{i}=1/k\mid X=x}\right]}.

Proof.

The right-hand side of the equation is independent of the particular value of
X
, since it corresponds to the rows with
c_{i}=1/k
. For such rows, each element follows a Poisson distribution
a_{i,j}\sim\textnormal{{Poi}}\left(1/m\right)
.

\displaystyle\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{A=v,c_{i}=1/k\mid X=x}\right.}{A=v,c_{i}=1/k\mid X=x}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{A=v,c_{i}=1/k\mid X=x}\right]}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{c_{i}=1/k}\right.}{c_{i}=1/k}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{c_{i}=1/k}\right]}\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x,c_{i}=1/k}\right.}{A=v\mid X=x,c_{i}=1/k}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{A=v\mid X=x,c_{i}=1/k}\right]}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha k}{n}\prod_{j=1}^{m}\left(\frac{1}{m}\right)^{v_{j}}e^{-\frac{1}{m}}\frac{1}{v_{j}!}=\frac{\alpha ke^{-1}}{nm^{\lVert v\lVert_{1}}}\prod_{j=1}^{m}\frac{1}{v_{j}!}

(133)

To establish the lemma, we upper-bound the left-hand side for both cases
X=0
and
X=1
, and then show that the larger of these two bounds meets the claimed asymptotic condition.

\displaystyle\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{A=v,c_{i}=1/n\mid X=0}\right.}{A=v,c_{i}=1/n\mid X=0}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{A=v,c_{i}=1/n\mid X=0}\right]}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{c_{i}=1/n}\right.}{c_{i}=1/n}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{c_{i}=1/n}\right]}\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{A=v\mid X=0,c_{i}=1/n}\right.}{A=v\mid X=0,c_{i}=1/n}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{A=v\mid X=0,c_{i}=1/n}\right]}

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{\alpha k}{n}\right)\prod_{j=1}^{m}\left(\frac{k}{nm}\right)^{v_{j}}e^{-\frac{k}{nm}}\frac{1}{v_{j}!}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{k}{nm}\right)^{\lVert v\lVert_{1}}e^{-\frac{k}{n}}\prod_{j=1}^{m}\frac{1}{v_{j}!}

(134)

\displaystyle\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{A=v,c_{i}=1/n\mid X=1}\right.}{A=v,c_{i}=1/n\mid X=1}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{A=v,c_{i}=1/n\mid X=1}\right]}

\displaystyle\quad=\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{c_{i}=1/n}\right.}{c_{i}=1/n}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{c_{i}=1/n}\right]}\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{A=v\mid X=1,c_{i}=1/n}\right.}{A=v\mid X=1,c_{i}=1/n}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{A=v\mid X=1,c_{i}=1/n}\right]}

\displaystyle\quad=\left(1-\frac{\alpha k}{n}\right)\prod_{j=1}^{m}\frac{1}{2}\left(\left(\frac{k\left(1+\epsilon\right)}{nm}\right)^{v_{j}}e^{-\frac{k(1+\epsilon)}{nm}}+\left(\frac{k\left(1-\epsilon\right)}{nm}\right)^{v_{j}}e^{-\frac{k(1-\epsilon)}{nm}}\right)\frac{1}{v_{j}!}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\left(\frac{k}{nm}\right)^{\lVert v\lVert_{1}}e^{-\frac{k}{n}}\prod_{j=1}^{m}\frac{(1+\epsilon)^{v_{j}}}{2}\left(e^{-\frac{k\epsilon}{nm}}+\left(\frac{1-\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}\right)^{v_{j}}e^{\frac{k\epsilon}{nm}}\right)\frac{1}{v_{j}!}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\left(\frac{k(1+\epsilon)}{nm}\right)^{\lVert v\lVert_{1}}e^{-\frac{k\left(1-\epsilon\right)}{n}}\prod_{j=1}^{m}\frac{1}{v_{j}!}

(135)

It is trivial that the upper-bound in (
C.4
) is greater than the upper-bound in (
C.4
). Therefore, it suffices to show that the ratio of the probabilities in (
C.4
) and (
C.4
) is of order
O(\frac{k}{n\alpha})
for any
v
such that
\lVert v\lVert_{1}\geq 2
:

\displaystyle\frac{\left(\frac{k(1+\epsilon)}{nm}\right)^{\lVert v\lVert_{1}}e^{-\frac{k\left(1-\epsilon\right)}{n}}\prod_{j=1}^{m}\frac{1}{v_{j}!}}{\frac{\alpha ke^{-1}}{nm^{\lVert v\lVert_{1}}}\prod_{j=1}^{m}\frac{1}{v_{j}!}}=\frac{k}{n\alpha}\cdot\left(1+\epsilon\right)^{2}\left(\frac{k(1+\epsilon)}{n}\right)^{\lVert v\lVert_{1}-2}e^{1-\frac{k(1-\epsilon)}{n}}\leq 4e\frac{k}{n\alpha}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{k}{n\alpha}\right)

(136)

∎

Lemma C.6
.

Let
\alpha\in[0,1],\epsilon\in(0,1),k\in\mathbb{N}
.
Let
X
be either
0
or
1
with equal probability. Let
q
be a distribution over
[n]\times[m]
constructed as in (
31
) using
X
. For an arbitrary
i\in[n]
corresponding to rows with
c_{i}=\frac{1}{n}
, let
A\coloneqq A_{i}
be a vector of
m
counts, such that
a_{i,j}\sim\textnormal{{Poi}}(kq(i,j))
for all
j\in[m]
.
Then, the mutual information between
X
and
a_{ij}
is bounded as

I\left(X;a_{ij}\mid c_{i}=\frac{1}{n}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\epsilon^{4}k^{2}}{n^{2}m^{2}}\right)

Proof.

For all
l
, define
x_{\min}(l)
and
x_{\max}(l)
as:

\displaystyle x_{\min}(l)=\operatornamewithlimits{arg\,min}_{x\in\{0,1\}}\left(\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{a=l\mid X=x}\right.}{a=l\mid X=x}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{a=l\mid X=x}\right]}\right),\quad x_{\max}(l)=\operatornamewithlimits{arg\,max}_{x\in\{0,1\}}\left(\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{a=l\mid X=x}\right.}{a=l\mid X=x}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{a=l\mid X=x}\right]}\right).

(137)

Using an argument similar to that in Appendix
C.3
, we can show that the mutual information is given by:

I(X;a)=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{O}\left(\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{a=l\mid X=x_{\min}(l)}\right.}{a=l\mid X=x_{\min}(l)}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{a=l\mid X=x_{\min}(l)}\right]}\left(1-\frac{\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{a=l\mid X=x_{\max}(l)}\right.}{a=l\mid X=x_{\max}(l)}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{a=l\mid X=x_{\max}(l)}\right]}}{\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{a=l\mid X=x_{\min}(l)}\right.}{a=l\mid X=x_{\min}(l)}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{a=l\mid X=x_{\min}(l)}\right]}}\right)^{2}\right)

(138)

For any
l
, we have that:

\displaystyle\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{a=l\mid X=0}\right.}{a=l\mid X=0}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{a=l\mid X=0}\right]}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-\frac{k}{nm}}\left(\frac{k}{nm}\right)^{l}}{l!}

(139)

\displaystyle\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{a=l\mid X=1}\right.}{a=l\mid X=1}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{a=l\mid X=1}\right]}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{e^{-\frac{k(1+\epsilon)}{nm}}\left(\frac{k(1+\epsilon)}{nm}\right)^{l}}{l!}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{e^{-\frac{k(1-\epsilon)}{nm}}\left(\frac{k(1-\epsilon)}{nm}\right)^{l}}{l!}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-\frac{k}{nm}}\left(\frac{k}{nm}\right)^{l}}{l!}\left(\frac{e^{-\frac{k\epsilon}{nm}}(1+\epsilon)^{l}+e^{\frac{k\epsilon}{nm}}(1-\epsilon)^{l}}{2}\right)

(140)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{a=l\mid X=1}\right.}{a=l\mid X=1}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{a=l\mid X=1}\right]}}{\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{a=l\mid X=0}\right.}{a=l\mid X=0}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{a=l\mid X=0}\right]}}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-\frac{k\epsilon}{nm}}(1+\epsilon)^{l}+e^{\frac{k\epsilon}{nm}}(1-\epsilon)^{l}}{2}

\displaystyle=\cosh\left(\frac{k\epsilon}{nm}\right)\left(\sum_{i=0}^{\left\lfloor{l/2}\right\rfloor}\binom{l}{2i}\epsilon^{2i}\right)-\sinh\left(\frac{k\epsilon}{nm}\right)\left(\sum_{i=0}^{\left\lfloor{\frac{l-1}{2}}\right\rfloor}\binom{l}{2i+1}\epsilon^{2i+1}\right)

(141)

We will split the sum in (
138
) over
l=0
,
l=1
,
2\leq l<2/\epsilon
, and
l\geq 2/\epsilon
. For
l=0
, the ratio in (
C.4
) is equal to
\cosh\left(\frac{k\epsilon}{nm}\right)
which is always greater than 1 and less than
1+\left(\frac{k\epsilon}{nm}\right)^{2}
. Therefore, the contribution to the mutual information can be bounded as

\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{a=0\mid X=x_{\min}(l)}\right.}{a=0\mid X=x_{\min}(l)}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{a=0\mid X=x_{\min}(l)}\right]}\left(1-\frac{\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{a=0\mid X=x_{\max}(l)}\right.}{a=0\mid X=x_{\max}(l)}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{a=0\mid X=x_{\max}(l)}\right]}}{\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{a=0\mid X=x_{\min}(l)}\right.}{a=0\mid X=x_{\min}(l)}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{a=0\mid X=x_{\min}(l)}\right]}}\right)^{2}\leq\left(\frac{k\epsilon}{nm}\right)^{4}.

(142)

For
l=1
, using reasoning analogous to the derivation of (
C.4
), we can verify that
x_{\max}(l)=0
and
x_{\min}(l)=1
, and we can bound the reciprocal of the ratio in (
C.4
) as follows:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{a=1\mid X=1}\right.}{a=1\mid X=1}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{a=1\mid X=1}\right]}}{\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{a=1\mid X=0}\right.}{a=1\mid X=0}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{a=1\mid X=0}\right]}}

\displaystyle=\cosh\left(\frac{k\epsilon}{nm}\right)-\epsilon\sinh\left(\frac{k\epsilon}{nm}\right)

\displaystyle=\cosh\left(\frac{k\epsilon}{nm}\right)\left(1-\epsilon\tanh\left(\frac{k\epsilon}{nm}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\geq 1\cdot\left(1-\frac{k\epsilon^{2}}{nm}\right)

\displaystyle\implies\frac{\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{a=1\mid X=x_{\max}(l)}\right.}{a=1\mid X=x_{\max}(l)}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{a=1\mid X=x_{\max}(l)}\right]}}{\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{a=1\mid X=x_{\min}(l)}\right.}{a=1\mid X=x_{\min}(l)}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{a=1\mid X=x_{\min}(l)}\right]}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\cosh\left(\frac{k\epsilon}{nm}\right)-\epsilon\sinh\left(\frac{k\epsilon}{nm}\right)}\leq\frac{1}{1-\frac{k\epsilon^{2}}{nm}}\leq 1+\frac{2k\epsilon^{2}}{nm}.

Therefore, the contribution to the mutual information can be bounded as

\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{a=1\mid X=x_{\min}(l)}\right.}{a=1\mid X=x_{\min}(l)}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{a=1\mid X=x_{\min}(l)}\right]}\left(1-\frac{\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{a=1\mid X=x_{\max}(l)}\right.}{a=1\mid X=x_{\max}(l)}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{a=1\mid X=x_{\max}(l)}\right]}}{\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{a=1\mid X=x_{\min}(l)}\right.}{a=1\mid X=x_{\min}(l)}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{a=1\mid X=x_{\min}(l)}\right]}}\right)^{2}\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{k^{2}\epsilon^{4}}{n^{2}m^{2}}\right).

(143)

For
2\leq l<2/\epsilon
, using the inequality
\cosh(u)\leq 1+u^{2}
for all
u\in[0,1]
, we can bound the ratio in (
C.4
) as follows:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{a=l\mid X=1}\right.}{a=l\mid X=1}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{a=l\mid X=1}\right]}}{\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{a=l\mid X=0}\right.}{a=l\mid X=0}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{a=l\mid X=0}\right]}}

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+\left(\frac{k\epsilon}{nm}\right)^{2}\right)\left(\sum_{i=0}^{\left\lfloor{l/2}\right\rfloor}\binom{l}{2i}\epsilon^{2i}\right)

(144)

\displaystyle=1+\epsilon^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{\left\lfloor{l/2}\right\rfloor}\binom{l}{2i}\epsilon^{2i-2}+\left(\frac{k\epsilon}{nm}\right)^{2}\sum_{i=0}^{\left\lfloor{l/2}\right\rfloor}\binom{l}{2i}\epsilon^{2i}

(145)

Now, we bound each term in the right-hand side of (
145
) as follows:

\sum_{i=1}^{\left\lfloor{l/2}\right\rfloor}\binom{l}{2i}\epsilon^{2i-2}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\left\lfloor{l/2}\right\rfloor}\left(\frac{el}{2i}\right)^{2i}\epsilon^{2i-2}=l^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{\left\lfloor{l/2}\right\rfloor}\left(\frac{el\epsilon}{2i}\right)^{2i-2}(\frac{e}{2i})^{2}\leq l^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{e}{i}\right)^{2i}\leq 12l^{2}.

(146)

The first inequality follows from the standard Stirling-type bound
\binom{n}{m}\leq\left(\frac{en}{m}\right)^{m}
. The second inequality uses the assumption that we are in the regime
l<2/\epsilon
, which implies
\frac{el\epsilon}{2i}\leq\frac{e}{i}
. Finally, the last inequality holds because the series converges to a finite constant (in fact, less than
12
), which follows directly from the root test.

\sum_{i=0}^{\left\lfloor{l/2}\right\rfloor}\binom{l}{2i}\epsilon^{2i}=\frac{(1+\epsilon)^{l}+(1-\epsilon)^{l}}{2}\leq(1+\epsilon)^{l}\leq e^{l\epsilon}\leq e^{2}

(147)

The equality follows from the binomial expansions of
(1+\epsilon)^{l}
and
(1-\epsilon)^{l}
, where the odd-degree terms cancel due to opposite signs and only the even-degree terms remain.
The first inequality holds since
(1-\epsilon)^{l}\leq(1+\epsilon)^{l}
.
The second inequality uses the standard bound
1+u\leq e^{u}
.
The last inequality follows from the fact that
l<2/\epsilon
.

Substituting the above bounds into (
145
), we obtain:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{a=l\mid X=1}\right.}{a=l\mid X=1}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{a=l\mid X=1}\right]}}{\mathrm{\mathbf{Pr}}\boldsymbol{\left[\vphantom{a=l\mid X=0}\right.}{a=l\mid X=0}\boldsymbol{\left.\vphantom{a=l\mid X=0}\right]}}

\displaystyle\leq 1+\epsilon^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{\left\lfloor{l/2}\right\rfloor}\binom{l}{2i}\epsilon^{2i-2}+\left(\frac{k\epsilon}{nm}\right)^{2}\sum_{i=0}^{\left\lfloor{l/2}\right\rfloor}\binom{l}{2i}\epsilon^{2i}

\displaystyle\leq 1+\epsilon^{2}\left(12l^{2}+e^{2}\left(\frac{k}{nm}\right)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=1+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2}l^{2})

(148)

We then upper-bound the contribution to the mutual information from this part by calculating the fourth moment of the Poisson random variable with mean
\lambda=\frac{k}{nm}
. This calculation utilizes the fact that for
l\geq 2
, the ratio of
l^{4}
and
l(l-1)(l-2)(l-3)+l(l-1)
is bounded by a constant, specifically
1\leq\frac{l^{4}}{l(l-1)(l-2)(l-3)+l(l-1)}\leq 15
:
