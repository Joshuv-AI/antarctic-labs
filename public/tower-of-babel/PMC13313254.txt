OTUD5 promotes AML progression by stabilizing SLC7A11 to suppress ferroptosis
Authors: Xiaowan Chen, Bingjie Zhao, Wenjing Yang, Zhenzhen Sun, Meng Hu, Pei Xu, Jia Guo, Tao Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAcute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a highly aggressive malignancy with frequent therapeutic resistance, necessitating the identification of novel molecular targets. This study aims to elucidate the role of the deubiquitinase OTUD5 in AML progression by regulating SLC7A11 to suppress ferroptosis. We analyzed OTUD5 expression in AML patient samples and cell lines using RNA sequencing and quantitative PCR. Functional roles were assessed through OTUD5 silencing and overexpression in AML cell lines (THP-1, HL-60), followed by proliferation, colony formation, and ferroptosis assays (ROS, labile Fe2 +, GSH, MDA). Co-immunoprecipitation and ubiquitination assays confirmed OTUD5-SLC7A11 interactions, while in vivo xenograft models validated findings. Molecular docking and transmission electron microscopy further elucidated mechanisms. OTUD5 was significantly upregulated in AML, correlating with ferroptosis suppression. OTUD5 directly interacted with and deubiquitinated SLC7A11, enhancing its stability and promoting AML cell survival. OTUD5 silencing induced ferroptosis, marked by increased labile iron, ROS, and mitochondrial damage, which was reversed by SLC7A11 overexpression or GSH supplementation. In vivo, OTUD5 knockdown reduced tumor growth, an effect mitigated by SLC7A11 overexpression or GSH. The OTUD5-SLC7A11 axis drives AML progression by suppressing ferroptosis, offering a novel therapeutic target to exploit ferroptosis sensitivity and overcome treatment resistance in AML.KEYWORDS: Acute myeloid leukemia (AML), OTUD5, SLC7A11, ferroptosis, deubiquitination

Introduction

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML), a highly aggressive hematological malignancy characterized by uncontrolled proliferation of immature myeloid cells, remains associated with poor clinical outcomes and significant therapeutic resistance [1–3]. Despite advances in targeted therapies, AML patients frequently experience relapse due to the emergence of drug-resistant leukemic clones [4,5], underscoring the urgent need to understand the underlying molecular mechanisms driving AML progression and therapy resistance. Ferroptosis, a recently characterized iron-dependent programmed cell death modality triggered by lipid peroxidation, has emerged as a promising therapeutic vulnerability in cancer treatment [6,7]. Mechanistically, ferroptosis is driven by intracellular iron accumulation, reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, and oxidative damage to membrane phospholipids [8,9]. In contrast, cancer cells can evade ferroptosis through activation of antioxidant defense systems, particularly the glutathione (GSH)-dependent glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) pathway, which detoxifies lipid peroxides and maintains redox homeostasis [9,10]. Increasing evidence suggests that dysregulation of ferroptosis contributes to tumor progression, metabolic adaptation, and therapeutic resistance in multiple malignancies, including AML [11].

The amino acid transporter SLC7A11 (also known as xCT), a core component of the cystine/glutamate antiporter system xc−, is a key negative regulator of ferroptosis through its critical role in cystine uptake, glutathione synthesis, and maintenance of the SLC7A11–GSH–GPX4 antioxidant pathway [12,13]. Overexpression of SLC7A11 has been observed in various malignancies and correlates with increased resistance to ferroptosis-inducing therapies [14,15]. In AML, elevated SLC7A11 levels confer enhanced resistance to oxidative stress and promote leukemic cell survival, suggesting that targeting SLC7A11-mediated ferroptosis resistance could represent a novel therapeutic strategy [16,17]. Yet, the molecular mechanisms underlying aberrant SLC7A11 stabilization and its precise regulation in AML cells remain incompletely understood, presenting a significant knowledge gap. Given that maintenance of SLC7A11 protein stability is essential for sustaining the SLC7A11–GSH–GPX4 antioxidant defense pathway, dysregulation of SLC7A11 post-translational regulation may critically contribute to ferroptosis resistance and leukemic cell survival. In particular, ubiquitination-dependent control of SLC7A11 stability has recently emerged as an important mechanism regulating ferroptosis sensitivity in cancer cells [14]. However, whether deubiquitinase-mediated stabilization of SLC7A11 contributes to ferroptosis evasion in AML remains largely unknown.

Deubiquitinases (DUBs), enzymes responsible for removing ubiquitin moieties from protein substrates, are increasingly recognized as critical regulators of cancer-associated proteins, modulating their stability and functional activities [18,19]. Accumulating evidence indicates that several deubiquitinases (DUBs) participate in ferroptosis regulation and tumor progression through modulation of protein stability and redox homeostasis. For example, OTUB1 has been reported to stabilize SLC7A11 and suppress ferroptosis in multiple cancer types [20–22], whereas BAP1 promotes ferroptosis through epigenetic repression of SLC7A11 transcription [23]. In addition, USP7 [24], USP9X [25] and USP10 [26] have been implicated in oxidative stress adaptation, iron metabolism, or cancer cell survival in a context-dependent manner. Nevertheless, the majority of these studies have focused on solid tumors, and the specific involvement of DUB-mediated ferroptosis regulation in AML remains poorly understood.

In our preliminary transcriptomic analysis of AML patient samples, OTUD5 emerged as one of the significantly upregulated deubiquitinases (DUBs) and was strongly associated with ferroptosis-related signaling pathways based on GO and GSEA analyses. Previous studies have implicated OTUD5 in the progression of several solid tumors through regulation of protein stability and oncogenic signaling [27–30]; however, its role in hematologic malignancies, particularly in ferroptosis regulation in AML, remains largely unexplored. These observations prompted us to investigate whether OTUD5 contributes to AML progression through modulation of ferroptosis.

Using integrated in vitro and in vivo approaches, we investigated the expression pattern, biological function, and molecular mechanisms of OTUD5 in AML cells and patient-derived samples, with particular focus on its potential association with the ferroptosis regulator SLC7A11. Our findings identify OTUD5 as a previously unrecognized regulator of ferroptosis in AML and provide evidence supporting a functional link between OTUD5 and SLC7A11-mediated antioxidant signaling. Collectively, this study expands the current understanding of deubiquitinase-mediated ferroptosis regulation in hematologic malignancies and suggests that targeting the OTUD5-associated ferroptosis pathway may represent a potential therapeutic strategy for AML.

Methods and materials

Patient samples and ethical approval

Bone marrow samples were collected from acute myeloid leukemia (AML) patients and healthy donors at The First Affiliated Hospital and College of Clinical Medicine of Henan University of Science and Technology, with written informed consent obtained in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All procedures were approved by the Institutional Review Board (2024–0554).

For transcriptomic sequencing analysis, bone marrow mononuclear cell samples from AML patients (n = 5) and healthy donors (n = 5) were subjected to RNA-seq. In addition, an independent validation cohort consisting of AML patients (n = 25) and healthy donors (n = 10) was collected for qPCR, western blotting, ROS detection, labile Fe2 + assays, GSH measurements, and other biochemical analyses.

Fresh bone marrow samples were processed within 2 h after collection and stored at −80°C until use. Mononuclear cells were isolated using Ficoll-Paque PLUS (GE Healthcare, #17–1440-03) density gradient centrifugation according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Cell lines and culture

Human AML cell lines (THP-1, NB4, HL-60, U937), HEK293T cells, and normal bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) and bone marrow mononuclear cells (BMMNCs) were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). Cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, #11875093) or DMEM (Gibco, #11965092) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, #10091148), 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, #15140122), at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2.

RNA sequencing and data analysis

Total RNA was extracted from bone marrow mononuclear cells using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, #15596018). RNA integrity was assessed by Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies). RNA-seq libraries were prepared using NEBNext Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit (NEB, #E7530L) and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Quality control was performed with FastQC (v0.11.8); differential expression analysis used DESeq2 (v1.26.0). Gene Ontology (GO) and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) were performed using ClusterProfiler (v3.14.3).

Quantitative PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA was reverse-transcribed with the PrimeScript RT reagent kit (Takara, #RR037A). qPCR was performed using TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (Takara, #RR820A) on a QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher). Primers are listed in Supplementary Table S1. Relative mRNA expression was calculated by the 2^–ΔΔCt method, normalized to GAPDH.

Western blotting(WB)

Proteins were extracted in RIPA buffer (Thermo Fisher, #89900) with protease inhibitors (Roche, #04693159001). Equal protein amounts (20–40 μg) were separated by SDS-PAGE, transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, #IPVH00010), and probed overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against OTUD5 (Abcam, #ab126595), SLC7A11 (Cell Signaling Technology, #12691), GAPDH (Proteintech, #60004–1-Ig), and other targets as indicated. HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Jackson ImmunoResearch) were used, and bands were visualized by ECL (Thermo Fisher, #32109).

Flow cytometry

Cells (1 × 106) were stained for reactive oxygen species (ROS) using 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescin diacetate (DCFDA, Sigma-Aldrich, #D6883) or for labile Fe2 + using FerroOrange (Dojindo, #F374). Data were acquired on a BD FACSCanto II (BD Biosciences) and analyzed with FlowJo (v10.7.1).

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining

Cells or cytospun bone marrow samples were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature, followed by permeabilization with 0.2% Triton X-100 for 10 min. Samples were then blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 1 h at room temperature. For ROS detection, cells were incubated with DCFDA (10 μM; Sigma-Aldrich, #D6883) for 30 min at 37°C in the dark and washed three times with PBS before imaging. For co-localization studies, samples were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against OTUD5 (Abcam, #ab126595, 1:200 dilution) and SLC7A11 (Cell Signaling Technology, #12691, 1:200 dilution). After washing three times with PBS, samples were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488- conjugated secondary antibodies (Invitrogen, 1:500 dilution) for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 5 min. Fluorescence images were captured using a Zeiss LSM 880 confocal microscope under identical exposure settings for all groups. For quantitative analysis, fluorescence intensity was measured using ImageJ software (NIH). At least five randomly selected fields per sample were analyzed, and mean fluorescence intensity was normalized to the control group.

Glutathione (GSH), Malondialdehyde (MDA), and NADPH assays

Cells or blood samples were lysed according to the manufacturers’ protocols, and supernatants were collected after centrifugation for biochemical analysis. Intracellular reduced glutathione (GSH) levels were measured using a GSH assay kit (Beyotime, #S0053) based on the reaction between GSH and 5,5′-dithiobis-(2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB), which generates a yellow-colored product measurable at 412 nm. Malondialdehyde (MDA), a lipid peroxidation marker, was quantified using an MDA assay kit (Beyotime, #S0131S) based on the thiobarbituric acid (TBA) reaction, with absorbance measured at 532 nm. NADP+ /NADPH levels were determined using a commercial assay kit (Abcam, #ab65349), which detects NADPH-dependent colorimetric changes through enzymatic cycling reactions, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance was measured using a SpectraMax i3 plate reader (Molecular Devices). All metabolite levels were normalized to total protein concentration for quantitative comparison between groups.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Cells were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde, post-fixed with 1% osmium tetroxide, dehydrated, and embedded in Epon 812 resin. Ultrathin sections were stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate and examined on a JEOL JEM-1400 electron microscope at 80 kV.

Gene silencing and overexpression

siRNA targeting OTUD5 and non-targeting controls were purchased from GenePharma. Cells were transfected with Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Thermo Fisher, #13778150) following manufacturer’s protocol. For CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knockout, sgRNAs targeting OTUD5 were cloned into lentiCRISPRv2 (Addgene, #52961) and packaged in HEK293T cells with psPAX2 and pMD2.G. Cells were selected with puromycin (2 μg/mL). For overexpression, Flag-OTUD5 and Myc-SLC7A11 plasmids (GeneChem) were transfected using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher, #L3000015).

CCK-8 proliferation assays

For proliferation, 5 × 103 cells/well were seeded in 96-well plates; viability was assessed at 24, 48, and 72 h using Cell Counting Kit-8 (Dojindo, #CK04) and measured at 450 nm.

Cell migration assays

Cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 with 10% FBS, serum-starved for 24 h, and resuspended at 5 × 105 cells/mL in serum-free medium. Transwell assays used 24-well inserts with 5-μm pores (Corning, #3421). The lower chamber contained 600 μL RPMI-1640 with 100 ng/mL SDF-1α. 100 μL cell suspension (5 × 104 cells) was added to the upper chamber and incubated for 4 h at 37°C, 5% CO2. Migrated cells were collected from the lower chamber, plated on poly-L-lysine-coated slides, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, and counted in five fields per slide using ImageJ (200× magnification).

Co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) and ubiquitination assays

Cells were lysed in IP lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher, #87787) with protease inhibitors. Lysates were incubated with anti-OTUD5 (Abcam, #ab126595), anti-SLC7A11 (CST, #12691), or control IgG, followed by Protein A/G beads (Thermo Fisher, #88802). Bound proteins were analyzed by WB. For ubiquitination assays, cells were co-transfected with HA-ubiquitin-K48 (Addgene, #17605), and immunoprecipitates were probed with anti-HA antibody (CST, #3724).

Protein pull-down and mass spectrometry

OTUD5-interacting proteins were isolated from cell lysates using an anti-OTUD5 antibody and Protein G beads, resolved by SDS-PAGE, and visualized by Coomassie staining. Unique bands were excised, digested with trypsin, and analyzed on a Thermo Orbitrap Fusion mass spectrometer.

Molecular docking

Molecular docking simulations of OTUD5 and SLC7A11 interactions were performed using AutoDock Vina (v1.1.2). Protein structures were obtained from the Protein Data Bank or predicted by AlphaFold.

In vivo xenograft mouse models

To establish an in vivo leukemia model, NOD/SCID mice (6 weeks old, female) were intravenously injected via tail vein with HL-60 cells that were stably transduced with shNC, shOTUD5, shOTUD5 combined with SLC7A11 overexpression (SLC7A11OE), or shOTUD5 plus glutathione (GSH) supplementation. Each experimental group contained 5 mice. For GSH treatment, mice were intraperitoneally administered GSH (100 mg/kg) every other day following cell injection. Mice were monitored daily for general health, weight loss, and signs of leukemia progression. After 6 weeks, animals were sacrificed, and bone marrow, liver, and spleen were harvested for histological, immunohistochemical, and biochemical analyses.

All mice were randomly assigned to experimental groups using a computer-generated randomization schedule prior to treatment initiation. To minimize experimental bias, all outcome assessments—including monitoring of peripheral blood parameters, spleen weight, and histopathological evaluation of bone marrow, liver, and spleen—were performed by investigators who were unaware of the treatment-group allocation. Mice were monitored daily for signs of distress, including >20% body-weight loss, impaired mobility, severe lethargy, failure to eat or drink, hunched posture, ruffled fur, ulceration, or tumor size exceeding institutional limits. Animals meeting any of these criteria were humanely euthanized according to institutional guidelines. These details have been included in the revised Methods section to ensure transparency and compliance with ethical standards.

Histology and immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Bone marrow and spleen tissues were fixed in 10% formalin, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned at 5 µm thickness. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed to evaluate leukemic infiltration and organ pathology. Immunohistochemistry was conducted using antibodies against CD14 to assess macrophage infiltration. Sections were visualized with DAB and counterstained with hematoxylin. Images were captured under a light microscope.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SEM. Comparisons between two groups were performed using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test. For multiple groups, one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test was used. Statistical analysis was conducted in GraphPad Prism (v10.0). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All experiments were performed in triplicate unless otherwise stated. Statistical significance: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001.

Results

OTUD5 is highly upregulated in AML and associated with ferroptosis-related signaling

To elucidate the molecular alterations in AML, we performed RNA-seq on bone marrow samples from five AML patients and five healthy donors. Differential expression analysis revealed a distinct transcriptional landscape in AML, with a significant upregulation of OTUD5 (Figure 1(A)). GO enrichment analysis of the differentially expressed genes highlighted multiple biological processes, notably including pathways involved in ferroptosis (Figure 1(B)). Notably, GSEA further indicated that gene sets associated with the negative regulation of ferroptosis were significantly enriched in AML samples (Figure 1(C)), suggesting a potential link between OTUD5 upregulation and ferroptosis suppression in AML. To further validate the robustness of the RNA-seq findings in an independent cohort, we analyzed OTUD5 expression using publicly available transcriptomic data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) AML cohort. Stratification of TCGA-AML patients based on OTUD5 expression revealed that high OTUD5 levels were associated with adverse overall survival (Figure S1A). In line with these observations, correlation analysis demonstrated a significant positive association between OTUD5 expression and gene signatures related to the negative regulation of ferroptosis in the TCGA cohort Figure S1B). Collectively, these independent dataset analyses further corroborate the upregulation of OTUD5 in AML and support its potential involvement in ferroptosis suppression.

To validate the expression of OTUD5, qPCR analysis demonstrated markedly increased OTUD5 mRNA levels in AML bone marrow compared to controls (Figure 1(D)), which was corroborated by WB showing elevated OTUD5 protein in AML patient samples (Figure 1(E)). Consistently, both qPCR and WB assays in AML cell lines (THP-1, NB4, HL-60, U937) revealed significantly higher OTUD5 expression compared to normal BMSCs and BMMNCs (Figures 1(F,G)). These findings collectively indicate that OTUD5 is robustly upregulated in AML.

AML bone marrow exhibits ferroptosis-associated metabolic alterations

To further investigate the role of ferroptosis in AML, we assessed ROS and labile Fe2 + levels in bone marrow samples from AML patients and healthy donors. Flow cytometry analysis using FITC-ROS probes revealed that ROS levels were significantly higher in AML samples than in healthy controls (Figure 2(A)). This result was corroborated by IF staining, which showed a dramatic increase in ROS fluorescence intensity in AML bone marrow cells compared to normal samples (Figure 2(B)). Furthermore, detection of labile Fe2 + demonstrated greater accumulation of reactive iron in AML cells, as evidenced by numerous areas of intense iron staining (yellow arrows) absent in healthy controls (Figure 2(C)). Interestingly, quantitative analysis of reduced GSH, a key antioxidant, showed that GSH levels were also significantly elevated in AML samples relative to healthy bone marrow (Figure 2(D)). Together, these data indicate that the AML bone marrow microenvironment is characterized by increased ROS production, enhanced iron availability, and compensatory elevation of GSH, underscoring a metabolic landscape intimately linked to ferroptosis regulation in AML.

OTUD5 silencing inhibits AML cell proliferation and triggers ferroptosis in AML

To delineate the functional role of OTUD5 in AML cell fate and ferroptosis regulation, we silenced OTUD5 in THP-1 and HL-60 cells using siRNA. qPCR and WB analyses confirmed efficient knockdown of OTUD5 at both mRNA and protein levels (Figures 3(A,B)). Functionally, CCK-8 assays showed that OTUD5 silencing markedly suppressed cell proliferation in both AML cell lines over 72 hours (Figure 3(C)), while cell migration assays further demonstrated reduced migratory capacity upon OTUD5 knockdown (Figures 3(D)). Mechanistically, OTUD5 silencing led to a significant reduction in intracellular ROS levels as measured by flow cytometry and fluorescence imaging (Figure 3(E,F)), but paradoxically, was accompanied by increased accumulation of labile Fe2 + (Figure 3(G)), a hallmark of ferroptosis. All ROS and Fe2 + assays were conducted 2 h after transfection, a time window during which cell proliferation and viability remained unchanged, supporting a direct regulatory role of OTUD5 in redox and iron homeostasis. Strikingly, transmission electron microscopy revealed classic ferroptosis-associated mitochondrial alterations in OTUD5-deficient cells, including shrunken mitochondria, increased membrane density, and diminished or vanished cristae structures (Figure 3(H)). Collectively, these data demonstrate that OTUD5 supports AML cell proliferation while restraining ferroptosis-like processes.

OTUD5 physically interacts and co-localizes with SLC7A11 in AML cells

To investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying OTUD5-mediated regulation of ferroptosis, we performed protein pull-down assays using an OTUD5-specific antibody followed by Coomassie brilliant blue staining, which revealed distinct protein bands absent in IgG controls (Figure 4(A)). Subsequent mass spectrometry analysis identified SLC7A11 as a major OTUD5-interacting protein, with specific SLC7A11-derived peptides detected in the OTUD5 pull-down complex (Figure 4(B)). Reciprocal co-IP assays in both THP-1 and HL-60 cell lysates confirmed a robust endogenous interaction between OTUD5 and SLC7A11 (Figure 4(C)). Furthermore, co-IP experiments using co-transfected Myc-tagged SLC7A11 and Flag-tagged OTUD5 constructs in cells validated their physical association in both pull-down directions (Figure 4(D)). IF analyses demonstrated that OTUD5 and SLC7A11 were predominantly co-localized in the cytoplasm of THP-1 and HL-60 cells, as evidenced by substantial signal overlap in merged images (Figure 4(E)). Finally, molecular docking simulations further supported a strong and specific binding interface between OTUD5 and SLC7A11 (Figure 4(F)). These results establish that OTUD5 directly interacts and co-localizes with SLC7A11 in AML cells, suggesting a mechanistic link between OTUD5 and the modulation of ferroptosis through SLC7A11 regulation.

OTUD5 enhances SLC7A11 protein stability via deubiquitination

To dissect the mechanism by which OTUD5 regulates SLC7A11, we first performed co-transfection experiments in HEK293T cells and THP-1. Overexpression of wild-type OTUD5 (Flag-OTUD5), but not its catalytically inactive mutant (CS), markedly increased SLC7A11 protein levels in a dose-dependent manner (Figures 5(A,B))), highlighting the requirement for OTUD5 deubiquitinase activity. In AML cells, CRISPR-mediated OTUD5 knockout (sgOTUD5) reduced endogenous SLC7A11 expression, whereas Flag-OTUD5 overexpression restored SLC7A11 levels (Figure 5(C,D)). CHX chase assays revealed that OTUD5 overexpression significantly prolonged the half-life of SLC7A11 protein compared to empty vector, indicating that OTUD5 protects SLC7A11 from degradation (Figure 5(E)). Conversely, OTUD5 knockdown diminished SLC7A11 stability, and this effect was counteracted by proteasome inhibition with MG132 (Figure 5(F)), supporting a role for the ubiquitin-proteasome system. Ubiquitination assays demonstrated that wild-type OTUD5, but not the CS mutant, decreased K48-linked polyubiquitination of SLC7A11 (Figure 5(F)), while OTUD5 knockdown or MG132 treatment led to increased SLC7A11 ubiquitination (Figure 5(G)). Collectively, these results establish that OTUD5 stabilizes SLC7A11 by removing K48-linked polyubiquitin chains, thereby protecting it from proteasomal degradation in AML cells.

SLC7A11 overexpression reverses OTUD5 knockdown-induced ferroptotic changes in AML cells

To further dissect the functional interplay between OTUD5 and SLC7A11 in ferroptosis regulation, we performed rescue experiments by overexpressing SLC7A11 in OTUD5-silenced THP-1 and HL-60 cells. CCK-8 assays showed that SLC7A11 overexpression significantly restored cell viability, which was suppressed upon OTUD5 knockdown (Figure 6(A)). Similarly, colony formation assays confirmed that SLC7A11 overexpression counteracted the reduction in clonogenic potential caused by OTUD5 deficiency (Figure 6(B)). IF analysis of intracellular ROS levels demonstrated that OTUD5 knockdown led to a substantial increase in ROS fluorescence intensity in both THP-1 and HL-60 cells, while SLC7A11 overexpression markedly reduced this ROS accumulation (Figures 6(C,D)). Flow cytometry further validated these findings, showing that SLC7A11 overexpression alleviated the elevated ROS levels induced by OTUD5 knockdown (Figure 6(E)). In parallel, the increased labile iron (Fe2 +) content observed in OTUD5-deficient cells was also reduced by SLC7A11 overexpression (Figure 6(F)). Finally, TEM revealed that the classic ferroptosis-associated mitochondrial damage induced by OTUD5 silencing–including shrunken mitochondria, increased membrane density, and loss of cristae–was largely reversed by SLC7A11 overexpression (Figures 6(G,H)). Collectively, these results indicate that SLC7A11 overexpression effectively mitigates the ferroptosis-promoting effects of OTUD5 deficiency in AML cells.

SLC7A11 overexpression or GSH supplementation reverses OTUD5 knockdown-induced ferroptosis and tumor suppression in vivo

To evaluate the in vivo consequences of OTUD5 loss, we established xenograft leukemia models with OTUD5 knockdown, with or without SLC7A11 overexpression or GSH supplementation. H&E staining of bone marrow revealed that OTUD5 knockdown markedly reduced leukemic infiltration, while both SLC7A11 overexpression and GSH supplementation restored leukemic features to levels comparable with OTUD5 knockdown groups (Figure 7(A)). Consistently, liver and spleen sizes were significantly reduced in shOTUD5 mice, reflecting disease suppression, but were rescued in the shOTUD5+SLC7A11OE and shOTUD5+GSH groups (Figures 7(B,C)). Immunohistochemistry further confirmed reduced CD14+ cell infiltration upon OTUD5 knockdown, which was reversed by SLC7A11 overexpression or GSH supplementation (Figure 7(D)). Moreover, spleen H&E staining supported these findings, showing restoration of leukemic infiltration in rescue groups (Figure 7(E)). Western blotting verified downregulation of both OTUD5 and SLC7A11 in shOTUD5 groups, with recovery of SLC7A11 expression in the SLC7A11OE group (Figure 7(F)). Mechanistically, ferroptosis-related markers indicated that OTUD5 knockdown increased labile iron and MDA, while decreasing antioxidant defenses (GSH and NADPH). These alterations were largely reversed by either SLC7A11 overexpression or GSH supplementation (Figures 7(G–J)). Together, these data confirm that SLC7A11 overexpression and GSH supplementation effectively rescue OTUD5 knockdown-induced ferroptosis and leukemia suppression in vivo, underscoring the central role of the OTUD5–SLC7A11 axis in AML progression.

Discussion

Our study identifies OTUD5 as a crucial regulator of ferroptosis in AML, acting through stabilization of the amino acid transporter SLC7A11. The discovery of this novel OTUD5-SLC7A11 regulatory axis provides a compelling mechanistic explanation for the evasion of ferroptosis observed in AML, addressing a previously unexplored molecular mechanism underlying aggressive leukemic progression. By directly interacting with and stabilizing SLC7A11, OTUD5 suppresses ferroptosis, thus promoting AML cell proliferation and tumor growth. This finding significantly advances our understanding of ferroptosis modulation in cancer biology and highlights a promising therapeutic vulnerability.

Our results demonstrate a significant elevation of OTUD5 in AML patient samples and cell lines compared to healthy controls, implicating its relevance to disease pathogenesis. Notably, upregulated OTUD5 correlated with enriched gene sets involved in ferroptosis suppression, strongly suggesting that OTUD5 may contribute to AML progression by modulating ferroptotic sensitivity. Experimental validation confirmed that OTUD5 silencing markedly impaired AML cell proliferation and induced classical ferroptotic alterations, such as increased labile Fe2 + accumulation and distinct mitochondrial damage. Importantly, these effects were rescued by re-expression of SLC7A11, firmly establishing SLC7A11 as a critical downstream effector mediating OTUD5’s regulatory role in ferroptosis.

Mechanistically, we revealed that OTUD5 directly interacts with SLC7A11, effectively removing K48-linked ubiquitination, thereby significantly prolonging SLC7A11 protein stability. The resulting stabilization of SLC7A11 enhances its ability to mediate cystine uptake and glutathione synthesis, pivotal processes for maintaining redox homeostasis and preventing ferroptosis. Consistently, in vivo experiments confirmed that disrupting the OTUD5-SLC7A11 axis significantly inhibited tumor growth through ferroptosis induction, an effect reversed by either SLC7A11 overexpression or GSH supplementation, highlighting the importance of this pathway in maintaining AML cell survival.

Previous studies have identified SLC7A11 as a key negative regulator of ferroptosis, frequently upregulated in various malignancies, including AML [31,32]. Elevated SLC7A11 expression confers resistance to oxidative stress and ferroptosis induction [33,34]. However, the precise molecular mechanisms governing its aberrant stabilization remained unclear. Our findings now bridge this gap by identifying OTUD5 as a previously unrecognized regulator of SLC7A11 protein stability through deubiquitination, thus positioning OTUD5 as a crucial node in ferroptosis resistance mechanisms. Although several deubiquitinases, such as USP18 and OTUB1, have previously been implicated in modulating SLC7A11 stability in solid tumors [35,36], , , these studies primarily focused on epithelial cancers and did not address hematologic malignancies. Importantly, none of these reports identified OTUD5 as a regulator of SLC7A11, nor did they examine ferroptosis control in the context of AML. Our study is therefore fundamentally distinct in several aspects. We identify OTUD5 as a previously unrecognized DUB for SLC7A11, extending current knowledge of SLC7A11 deubiquitination. In contrast to earlier studies that described DUB-mediated SLC7A11 regulation mainly under oxidative stress, our findings demonstrate a direct mechanistic link between OTUD5-mediated SLC7A11 stabilization and ferroptosis resistance in AML, a lineage in which ferroptosis control has not been clearly elucidated.Together, these distinctions position our study as the first to define an OTUD5-dependent deubiquitination mechanism controlling SLC7A11 in AML, thereby providing a uniquely hematopoietic and ferroptosis-centered regulatory model that is mechanistically and biologically distinct from earlier DUB–SLC7A11 studies conducted in solid tumors.

Interestingly, while elevated ROS and labile iron typically predispose cells to ferroptosis [8,11], , , AML cells paradoxically exhibit elevated antioxidant defenses, specifically increased GSH levels, presumably mediated by SLC7A11 stabilization. Thus, our study reconciles these seemingly contradictory observations by demonstrating how OTUD5-driven SLC7A11 stabilization enables AML cells to sustain elevated oxidative environments while resisting ferroptotic cell death.

This study presents several significant innovations. Firstly, it is the first to implicate OTUD5 as a critical regulator of ferroptosis in AML, expanding the biological significance of this DUB beyond previously characterized functions in solid tumors. Secondly, by uncovering the OTUD5-SLC7A11 axis, we illuminate an entirely novel regulatory mechanism of ferroptosis suppression through ubiquitination dynamics. This discovery opens new avenues for targeted therapeutic intervention, potentially transforming ferroptosis modulation into a viable clinical strategy for AML treatment.

Nevertheless, several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, the number of AML patient samples included in our transcriptomic and validation analyses was relatively limited, and larger independent clinical cohorts will be required to further validate the clinical significance of OTUD5 expression and its association with ferroptosis-related pathways in AML. In addition, while our study highlights OTUD5 as a potential therapeutic target, ferroptosis regulation in AML is likely governed by complex and compensatory signaling networks. Future studies should therefore investigate additional ferroptosis-targeting strategies, including pharmacological inhibition of the SLC7A11–GSH–GPX4 antioxidant pathway, modulation of iron metabolism, and enhancement of lipid peroxidation. Moreover, evaluating OTUD5 inhibitors or SLC7A11 modulators in clinically relevant AML models will be essential for therapeutic translation. Addressing these questions will further advance our understanding of ferroptosis biology and the feasibility of ferroptosis-based therapeutic strategies in AML.

In conclusion, we have identified OTUD5 as a novel, essential mediator of AML progression via stabilization of SLC7A11, which suppresses ferroptosis. Our findings clarify a crucial mechanism by which AML cells evade ferroptotic cell death, representing a significant step toward understanding therapeutic resistance in this aggressive malignancy. By highlighting the OTUD5-SLC7A11 axis as a targetable vulnerability, this work paves the way for the development of innovative treatments designed to exploit ferroptosis in AML therapy, potentially transforming the clinical management and improving patient outcomes in hematological malignancies.
