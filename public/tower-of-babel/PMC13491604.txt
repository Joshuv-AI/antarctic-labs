Predictive value of critical illness scores for outcomes of patients with community-acquired pneumonia: analysis of critical care data from the MIMIC-IV database
Authors: Yao-Tung Wang, Yuan-Fu Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study evaluated the predictive abilities of critical illness scoring instruments in intensive care unit (ICU) patients with community-acquired pneumonia.MethodsIn this population-based retrospective study, patients with community-acquired pneumonia admitted to the ICU were extracted from the fourth edition of the Medical Information Mart for Critical Care (MIMIC IV) database from 2008 to 2019. Critical illness scores used were the Simplified Acute Physiologic Score II (SAPS II), Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation III (APACHE III), Sequential organ failure assessment (SOFA), Oxford Acute Severity of Illness Score (OASIS), and logistic organ dysfunction score (LODS), CURB-65, and CHA2DS2-VASc scoring systems.ResultsPatients’ mean age was 65.1 years, 56.7% were male, 66.1% were white, and 53.8% were admitted to the ICU from the emergency department. All seven critical illness scores were significantly higher in non-survivor patients who had cardiovascular events and those who developed atrial fibrillation than in those who did not. APACHE III (AUROC 0.761) and LODS (0.742) showed relatively higher AUROCs for predicting 90-day mortality than the other evaluated scores, although their discrimination remained fair. For predicting incident atrial fibrillation, CURB-65 (0.672), CHA₂DS₂-VASc (0.655), and APACHE III (0.625) had AUROCs above 0.6, indicating modest discrimination.ConclusionsAPACHE III and LODS showed relatively higher, but still fair, discrimination for 90-day mortality compared with the other evaluated scores. All scores demonstrated poor discrimination for incident cardiovascular events. CURB-65 and CHA₂DS₂-VASc showed relatively higher but modest discrimination for incident atrial fibrillation, suggesting limited utility as stand-alone predictive tools.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12890-026-04399-5.

Background

Community-acquired pneumonia is a common acute disease caused by infection of the lung parenchyma by pathogens encountered outside of the hospital, and is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, especially in immunocompetent and immunocompromised patients [1]. The estimated global incidence of community-acquired pneumonia varies between 1.5 and 14 cases per 1000 person-years, with an annual incidence of 24.8 cases per 10,000 adults in the United States (US) and higher rates with older age. However, lack of public awareness and lack of advocacy for the disease raise concerns about identifying community-acquired pneumonia and providing early treatment [2]. Because community-acquired pneumonia presents differently from patient to patient, from mild cases that can be treated as outpatients to severe cases that require treatment in an intensive care unit (ICU), early diagnosis and appropriate and timely treatment are essential for improving outcomes [3].

Community-acquired pneumonia has a wide range of clinical manifestations, ranging from mild pneumonia characterized by fever and cough to severe pneumonia characterized by sepsis and respiratory failure, depending primarily on individual immune response [4]. Pathogens responsible for community-acquired pneumonia vary but can generally be divided into two groups, including frequently identified typical pathogens such as Streptococcus pneumoniae and Hemophilus influenzae, and Gram-negative bacteria, and atypical pathogens such as Legionella, Mycoplasma, and Chlamydia pneumoniae [5]. Influenza, severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), and other respiratory viruses are increasingly detected by molecular detection methods as pathogens causing community-acquired pneumonia. In particular, Streptococcus pneumoniae and Hemophilus influenzae are identified as the main causes of acute bacterial pneumonia [6]. Community-acquired pneumonia is suspected based on acute onset of signs or symptoms of respiratory infection, including cough, fever, sputum production, dyspnea, and chest pain; however, definitive diagnosis of community-acquired pneumonia requires radiographic evidence of pulmonary infiltrates on chest x-ray, chest CT, or lung ultrasonography [7]. While community-acquired pneumonia can be caused by bacteria, fungi, or viruses, it is the eighth leading cause of death and the first among infectious causes of death, and is preventable via vaccination with pneumococcal, influenza, or COVID-19 vaccines [8].

Previous epidemiological studies have reported that about one-fifth of hospitalized patients with community-acquired pneumonia require admission to the ICU, and nearly half of patients in the ICU with community-acquired pneumonia die within a year [9, 10]. Many patients with severe enough conditions to require hospitalization will experience cardiovascular events; the risk of death is high among these patients [11, 12]. Clinically, a poor prognosis for community-acquired pneumonia is closely associated with complications, including respiratory failure, sepsis, and cardiovascular events such as acute coronary syndrome, which accounts for 30% of long-term mortality in these patients [13, 14]. Cardiovascular events may include deep vein thrombosis and stroke, atrial fibrillation, congestive heart failure, and acute myocardial infarction [15]. Therefore, early identification and stratification of patients with community-acquired pneumonia admitted to the ICU may help to guide clinicians to formulate timely individualized treatment strategies for patients at different risk levels.

Multiple serum biomarkers and several established risk scores have been used as critical illness instruments to assess the severity of community-acquired pneumonia to improve its management. Several prognostic scoring models have been developed, including the Simplified Acute Physiologic Score II (SAPS II), Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation III (APACHE III), Sequential organ failure assessment (SOFA), Oxford Acute Severity of Illness Score (OASIS), and Logistic organ dysfunction score (LODS), CURB-65, and CHA2DS2-VASc [16–19]. However, the predictive value of these scoring models for mortality and cardiovascular events in patients with community-acquired pneumonia has not been evaluated comprehensively. Therefore, this study aimed to assess and compare the predictive abilities of SOFA, SAPS II, OASIS, LODS, CURB-65, CHA2DS2-VASc, and APACHE III scoring models for 90-day mortality, cardiovascular events, and atrial fibrillation in patients admitted to ICU with community-acquired pneumonia using patient data from the fourth edition of the Medical Information Mart for Critical Care (MIMIC IV) database.

Methods

Study design and data source

This population-based retrospective cohort study included patients admitted to the ICU with community-acquired pneumonia whose data were identified in and extracted from MIMIC IV, the most recent updated version of MIMIC-III, with preexisting Institutional Review Board approval (Massachusetts Institute of Technology, no. 0403000206; Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center, 2001P001699) [20]. The MIMIC IV database incorporates several improvements, including simplifying the structure, adding new data elements, and improving the usability of previous data elements. MIMIC-IV contains comprehensive, high-quality, and de-identified data of patients admitted to the ICU or emergency department of the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center between 2008 and 2019. The authors completed the required Collaborative Institutional Training Initiative examination and were then permitted to access the database for data extraction.

Study cohort

Patients admitted to the ICU were identified in the MIMIC-IV database from 2008 to 2019, and then screened for diagnosis of community-acquired pneumonia. Adult patients (age, 18–89 years) from ICU admissions of MIMIC-IV diagnosed with community-acquired pneumonia, defined using the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Version (ICD-9) codes of 481, 482, 485, and 486 or ICD-10 codes of J13, J15, and J18, only from a first ICU admission, were included. Patients admitted to the ICU for less than 24 h were excluded.

Study variables

The following variables were extracted from MIMIC-IV database for each patient’s first day of ICU admission: age, sex, race/ethnicity, body mass index (BMI), marital status (single, married, divorced, or widowed), source of admission (ward or step-down unit, emergency room, office or operating room, or transferred from another hospital). Data on critical illness scores were also extracted for each patient, including APACHE III, SAPS II, SOFA, OASIS, LODS, CURB-65, and CHA2DS2-VASc. These scores were calculated using clinical and laboratory data from the first 24 h after ICU admission, based on the worst recorded values within this period when applicable. Comorbidities, including congestive heart failure, hypertension, diabetes mellitus (DM), ischemic heart disease, history of stroke, severe liver disease, chronic kidney disease (CKD), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and anemia, were identified by ICD-9 and ICD-10 codes.

Outcome measures

The major study outcomes were: (1) 90-day mortality; (2) incident cardiovascular event; and (3) incident atrial fibrillation, defined by ICD-9 codes and ICD-10 codes. The ICD codes used are provided in Supplementary Table 1. Diagnoses were identified from all available ICD diagnosis fields.

Statistical analysis

Continuous data are presented as means ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical data are presented as numbers and percentages. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses were performed. Area under the ROC (AUROC) was used to determine the predictive abilities of the critical illness scores for 90-day mortality, incident cardiovascular events, and atrial fibrillation. Pairwise comparisons of AUROCs were performed using the DeLong test for correlated receiver operating characteristic curves.

The predictive performance of the critical illness scores for 90-day mortality was further evaluated in patient subgroups stratified by age, sex, and BMI categories. The model was considered fair if the AUROC was 0.7–0.8, good if the AUROC was 0.8–0.9, and excellent if the AUROC was > 0.9, as previously described [21].

BMI was not included as a covariate in the primary predictive analyses because of substantial missingness. BMI-stratified subgroup analyses were performed only among patients with available BMI data and were therefore considered complete-case subgroup analyses.

All statistical analyses were performed using the statistical software package SAS software version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, US). A two-sided P-value of < 0.05 was established as statistical significance.

Results

Patient selection

Figure 1 shows the flow diagram of patient selection. The data of 7,707 ICU patients aged 18–89 years with community-acquired pneumonia between 2008 and 2019 were extracted initially. After excluding patients with an ICU stay of less than 24 h (n = 1,009), the data of 6,698 patients were finally included in the analysis.

Characteristics of patients with community-acquired pneumonia admitted to the ICU

Table 1 summarizes the characteristics of the study cohort, including demographic data, comorbidities, and critical illness scores, stratified by 90-day mortality status, incident cardiovascular events, and incident atrial fibrillation. Patients’ mean age was 65.1 years, 56.7% patients were males, 66.1% were White, 47.4% were married, and 53.8% were admitted to the ICU from the emergency room. The most prevalent comorbidities were hypertension and anemia (62.0% and 50.7%, respectively). Mean ICU stays among 90-day survivors were shorter than those of non-survivors (6.6 days vs. 8.2 days, p < 0.001). All seven critical illness scores were significantly higher in non-survivors (vs. survivors), patients who had incident cardiovascular events, and patients who had atrial fibrillation than in those without cardiovascular events or atrial fibrillation.

Discussion

The present study used ICU patient data from the MIMIC-IV database to evaluate and compare the predictive ability of critical illness scores for 90-day mortality, cardiovascular events, and atrial fibrillation associated with community-acquired pneumonia. Among the evaluated scoring systems, APACHE III and LODS had the highest AUROCs for predicting 90-day mortality. However, their AUROCs of approximately 0.74–0.76 indicate only fair discrimination. Therefore, although these scores performed relatively better than the other evaluated scoring systems, their predictive ability should be interpreted as moderate rather than strong, and their clinical utility for individual-level risk prediction may be limited. The AUROCs of all seven critical illness scores for predicting cardiovascular events were lower than 0.6, indicating poor predictive ability for this outcome.

Poorer clinical outcomes for community-acquired pneumonia are associated with age and comorbidities, but not necessarily with multidrug-resistant organisms [22]. Pneumonia may generate a systemic inflammatory response, leading to possible multi-organ failure [23]. Late admission to the ICU has been associated with increased mortality in community-acquired pneumonia. Therefore, assessing the severity of community-acquired pneumonia has become pivotal for protecting patients’ safety and realizing the opportunity for early, adequate treatment, and several critical illness scores are readily available for that purpose. The critical illness scores evaluated in the present study may help physicians identify high-risk patients among those admitted to the ICU for community-acquired pneumonia.

The causative mechanism underlying community-acquired pneumonia is multifaceted and complicated, beginning with a strong systemic inflammatory response to the causative pathogen, accompanied by rapid increases in proinflammatory cytokines, C-reactive protein, interleukin, and tumor necrosis factor, finally leading to a systemic inflammatory syndrome and multiorgan failure, including sepsis and septic shock [24, 25]. In addition, the systemic inflammation results in hypoxemia, transiently elevated serum concentrations of the vasoconstrictor endothelin-1, and increased coagulation cascades [26]. The prothrombotic state after community-acquired pneumonia, mediated by increased concentrations of thrombin-antithrombin complexes and D-dimer, is associated with poor prognosis in community-acquired pneumonia patients [27]. The pathology of community-acquired pneumonia-related cardiovascular complications stems from atherosclerotic processes that lead to endothelial injury and myocardial ischemia or dysfunction [28]. Multiple organ dysfunction is defined as altered organ function in acutely ill patients. Several studies have shown that the leading cause of death is suggested to be not the underlying disease, but the result of progressive multiple organ dysfunction [29–31]. Multiple organ failure is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in ICU patients [31]. ICU mortality is associated with the amount of organ failure and the degree of organ dysfunction [30]. Assessment of organ dysfunction may provide important information for inter-ICU comparisons, triage of patients for clinical trials, and clinical decision-making [29].

The SOFA score included in the present study was initially designed by Vincent et al. [32] to objectively quantify the degree of organ dysfunction/failure over time [32]. SOFA scoring is applied to help define organ dysfunction/failure and to assess the impact of new treatments on the organ dysfunction/failure process. In other words, it not only predicts outcomes but describes a spectrum of complications in critically ill patients. Therefore, SOFA scores do not describe organ dysfunction/failure in terms of mortality alone, but complement other existing severity indices rather than competing with them.

A new simplified acute physiology score, SAPS II, was developed and validated using a large sample of surgical and medical patients, and the instrument provides a method for converting the score to risk of hospital mortality [33]. SAPS II contains 17 variables, including 12 physiological variables and three underlying-disease variables. In that original study, the scoring system performed well in development samples and was well validated in assessing independent patient samples.

The OASIS system has shown good prognostic value in critically ill patients without the support of laboratory testing [34]. Also, a previous study evaluated the performance of LODS in assessing multiple organ dysfunction/failure morbidity and mortality in an independent database, finding that LODS focuses on the amount and level of organ dysfunction and is well able to quantify the severity of organ dysfunction to predict survival and non-survival [35].

Multiple serum biomarkers and several established risk scores have been used to assess the severity of community-acquired pneumonia to improve the management of community-acquired pneumonia patients. In particular, several scoring systems have been used to predict mortality in patients with community-acquired pneumonia. The Pneumonia Severity Index (PSI) consists of 20 clinical and laboratory parameters as recommended by the American Thoracic Society (ATS). Later, the British Thoracic Society recommended a system using the presence of confusion, urea levels, respiratory rates, blood pressure and age ≥ 65 years (CURB-65) in a simplified scoring system (compared to PSI) to help manage community-acquired pneumonia [36]. Meanwhile, APACHE III scores have been widely used to predict clinical outcomes in critically ill patients [37]. A previous study was the first to validate the discriminative power of APACHE III in patients with sepsis-associated acute lung injury [38]. That study demonstrated that the APACHE III score has moderate predictive value for in-hospital mortality in adults with sepsis-related acute lung injury. Another previous study systematically reviewed the properties of commonly used risk stratification scales [39]. In that study, PSI had the highest sensitivity and lowest specificity for mortality, and CURB-65 was the most specific test.

A previous systematic review and meta-analysis analyzed data from clinical trials to evaluate the performance of prognostic rules to predict the need for ICU admission, intensive treatment, or early death in patients with community-acquired pneumonia, finding that PSI and CURB-65 scores best predicted ICU admission [40]. However, PSI was not evaluated in the present study. In our cohort, CURB-65 showed poor discrimination for 90-day mortality and incident cardiovascular events, whereas it had the highest AUROC among the evaluated scores for incident atrial fibrillation, although its overall discrimination remained modest. Another study noted that, despite early diagnosis and appropriate antibiotic therapy, mortality in conventionally treated community-acquired pneumonia was exceptionally high as predicted by PSI, CURB-65 or APACHE II, which performed similarly in predicting 28-day and in-hospital mortality [41]. However, differences were observed in the severity of community-acquired pneumonia, and significant differences in mortality were predicted by CURB-65 and PSI.

The CHA2DS2-VASc score (congestive heart failure, hypertension, age ≥ 75 years, DM, previous stroke, vascular disease, age 65–74 years, sex category) was originally developed to assess stroke risk in patients with atrial fibrillation. The CHA2DS2-VASc stroke score is included in most guidelines for risk stratification in atrial fibrillation, because atrial fibrillation is a significant independent risk factor for one-year mortality in patients with first acute ischemic stroke [42]. A previous large prospective cohort study investigated the independent predictive value of such patient scores, noting that the CHA2DS2-VASc score provided more accurate risk stratification [43]. Another study that investigated the role of the CHA2DS2-VASc score in predicting new-onset atrial fibrillation in patients hospitalized with community-acquired pneumonia found a direct relationship between CHA2DS2-VASc score and risk of new-onset atrial fibrillation [44]. The CHA2DS2-VASc score is an accurate and independent predictor of new-onset atrial fibrillation in community-acquired pneumonia patients, and scores help to identify a population at higher risk of developing arrhythmias during hospitalization, suggesting that this scoring system should be included in the evaluation of inpatients with community-acquired pneumonia.

From a clinical perspective, the relatively higher AUROC of CHA2DS2-VASc for incident atrial fibrillation is biologically plausible because its components, including older age, congestive heart failure, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, vascular disease, and prior stroke, reflect underlying cardiovascular vulnerability and atrial fibrillation susceptibility. In patients with CAP requiring ICU admission, systemic inflammation, hypoxemia, sympathetic activation, myocardial stress, and fluid shifts may further trigger atrial fibrillation. However, our findings should not be interpreted as a recommendation to routinely calculate all seven scores. Rather, score selection should be outcome-specific: APACHE III and LODS may be more informative for 90-day mortality risk stratification, whereas CURB-65 and CHA2DS2-VASc may provide modest information regarding incident atrial fibrillation risk. Because most AUROCs were only fair or modest, these scores should be used as supportive tools rather than stand-alone decision-making instruments. Routine manual calculation of multiple scores may have limited practicality, whereas automated electronic health record–based calculation may improve feasibility and should be evaluated in future studies.

Cardiovascular events are common during hospitalization for community-acquired pneumonia, and new-onset atrial fibrillation is an important complication. In the present study, all evaluated scores showed poor discrimination for incident cardiovascular events. For incident atrial fibrillation, CURB-65 and CHA₂DS₂-VASc had relatively higher AUROCs than the other scores, but their discrimination remained modest. Subgroup analyses were generally consistent with the main analysis, although these analyses were exploratory and no formal interaction tests were performed.

The study period of 2008–2019 represents a pre-COVID cohort. This period was selected to evaluate ICU patients with community-acquired pneumonia before the emergence of SARS-CoV-2 infection, which may represent a distinct clinical entity with different epidemiology, treatment patterns, and outcome determinants. Nevertheless, changes in CAP epidemiology and ICU practice after the COVID-19 pandemic may limit the applicability of these findings to contemporary populations. Future studies should validate these results in post-pandemic cohorts.

Although newer risk prediction frameworks, including SAPS-3, APACHE IV, dynamic prediction models, and machine learning–based approaches, are increasingly used in critical care research, the present study focused on scoring systems that are widely available and readily applicable using routine clinical data. These conventional scores may still be useful in settings where real-time electronic health record integration or advanced computational models are not available. However, future studies should compare these traditional scores with contemporary dynamic or machine learning–based prediction models.

Strengths and limitations

The main strength of this study is its large-scale, diverse population and a population-based study design using real-world data, increasing the credibility of the analyses. Multiple subgroup analyses were performed, and the results of the subgroup analyses were similar to the overall results, indicating good reliability of the study outcomes.

Several limitations should be noted. First, this was a single-center retrospective study, and external validation was not performed, limiting generalizability and the ability to rule out selection bias. Second, the diagnosis of community-acquired pneumonia, comorbidities, and outcomes relied on administrative ICD codes, which may have introduced misclassification. Third, BMI was missing in a substantial proportion of patients, likely because height and body weight are not routinely measured or consistently documented during ICU admission; therefore, BMI-stratified analyses were restricted to complete cases and should be interpreted cautiously. Fourth, subgroup analyses were exploratory and descriptive, and no formal interaction tests were performed. Fifth, the cohort was derived from 2008 to 2019 and therefore represents a pre-COVID population; validation in post-pandemic cohorts is needed. Sixth, we evaluated conventional scoring systems and did not compare them with newer tools such as SAPS-3, APACHE IV, dynamic prediction models, or machine learning–based models. Seventh, the included scores were conceptually heterogeneous, and their comparison should be interpreted as pragmatic rather than implying that these tools are interchangeable. Finally, because the best AUROC values remained within the fair range, these scores may support risk stratification but are insufficient as stand-alone tools for clinical decision-making.

Conclusions

APACHE III and LODS showed relatively higher, but still fair, discrimination for 90-day mortality compared with the other evaluated scores. All scores demonstrated poor discrimination for incident cardiovascular events. CURB-65 and CHA₂DS₂-VASc showed relatively higher but modest discrimination for incident atrial fibrillation, suggesting limited utility as stand-alone predictive tools. These conventional scoring systems may support risk stratification but should not be used alone for clinical decision-making.
