Geometry of ample/lopsided sets
Hans–Jürgen Bandelt, Victor Chepoi, Andreas Dress, Jack Koolen

Abstract
. Lopsided sets were introduced by Jim Lawrence in 1983 when he studied the subsets L L of { − 1 , + 1 } E \{-1,+1\}^{E} that encode the intersection pattern of a convex set K K with the orthants of ℝ E {\mathbb{R}}^{E} . He identified a strong combinatorial condition on L L (that he dubbed lopsidedness ) that is necessary (yet, as he showed, not sufficient) for the existence of such a convex set K K , gave a number of equivalent conditions, and noted its close connections with various other topics studied in combinatorics. Lopsided sets have been independently rediscovered by several other authors, in particular by Andreas Dress in 1995, who called them ample sets. Dress defined ample sets as the set families satisfying equality in a combinatorial inequality, which holds for all set families. Ample/lopsided sets can be regarded as the isometric subgraphs of hypercubes for which the intersections with all subhypercubes yield isometric subgraphs. Alternatively, they correspond to set families L L such that when a subset A A of the ground set E E is shattered by L L , then A A is strongly shattered by L L , i.e., some subhypercube defined by A A is fully included in L L . In a previous article we characterized ample sets in various combinatorial and graph-theoretical ways. In this paper we study geometric realizations of ample sets as cubihedra (cube complexes), which yields several new characterizations. One such characterization establishes that the cubihedra of ample sets endowed with the intrinsic ℓ 1 \ell_{1} -metric are exactly the isometric subspaces of ℓ 1 \ell_{1} -spaces (which we call, weakly convex sets). We also view the barycenter maps of faces of cubihedra of ample sets as collections of { ± 1 , 0 } \{\pm 1,0\} -sign vectors and, in analogy with the characterization of oriented matroids by the covectors and the cocircuits. Moreover, we characterize the collections of { ± 1 , 0 } \{\pm 1,0\} -sign vectors corresponding to barycenter maps of all faces and all maximal faces of an ample set. Furthermore, we show that any ample set L ⊆ { − 1 , + 1 } E L\subseteq\{-1,+1\}^{E} is realizable as the intersection pattern of a weakly convex set K K with the orthants of ℝ E {\mathbb{R}}^{E} . All this testifies that the concept of ample sets is quite natural in the context of cube complexes.

Ampleness

Throughout this paper,
E
denotes a finite set with

n:=\#E
elements,
\{\pm 1\}^{E}
is the set of all maps from
E

into
\{\pm 1\}=\{-1,+1\}
,
\{\pm 1,0\}^{E}
is the set of all maps from
E

into
\{\pm 1,0\}=\{-1,0,+1\}
. Thus
{\mathbb{R}}^{E}
is the vector space consisting of all maps from
E
into
\mathbb{R}
. The
solid hypercube

H(E)=[-1,+1]^{E}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{E}
consists of all maps from
E
into
the interval
[-1,+1]
. We consider
H(E)
as a cube complex, i.e., as a convex polyhedron together with its face structure. The 0-skeleton of
H(E)
is
\{\pm 1\}^{E}
, its 1-skeleton

is the
graphic hypercube
, and
\{\pm 1,0\}^{E}
is the set of sign maps of the barycenters of faces of
H(E)
. For any two maps
r^{\prime},r^{\prime\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{E}

their
\ell_{1}
-distance
||r^{\prime},r^{\prime\prime}||_{1}=\sum_{e\in E}|r^{\prime}(e)-r^{\prime\prime}(e)|
. Two vertices
s^{\prime},s^{\prime\prime}\in\{\pm 1\}^{E}
define an edge (of length 2) of
{H}^{(1)}(E)

if and only if
d(s^{\prime},s^{\prime\prime})=2.

In most of the paper, we will consider subsets
{L}
of
\{\pm 1\}^{E}
. Alternatively, such a set

can be viewed as a collection of subsets of
E
. The set-theoretic complement of

is written as

{L}^{*}:

{L}^{*}:=\{\pm 1\}^{E}-{L}.

We denote by
G({L})
the subgraph of the graphic hypercube
{H}^{(1)}(E)
induced by

. The set

is
called
connected

if
G({L})
is connected, and it is called

if every
pair of vertices
s^{\prime},s^{\prime\prime}
of

can be connected in
G({L})

by a path of length

Given any subset

of

one can always associate two subsets

{L}_{A}
and

of
\{\pm 1\}^{E-A}
with an arbitrary set

{L}\subseteq\{\pm 1\}^{E}
of sign maps:

\displaystyle{L}_{A}:=\{t\in\{\pm 1\}^{E-A}:\mbox{ some extension }s\in\{\pm 1\}^{E}\mbox{ of }t\mbox{ belongs to }{L}\},

\displaystyle{L}^{A}:=\{t\in\{\pm 1\}^{E-A}:\mbox{ every extension }s\in\{\pm 1\}^{E}\mbox{ of }t\mbox{ belongs to }{L}\}.

{L}_{A}=\{s|_{E-A}:~s\in{L}\}
encodes the projection of
L
onto
\{\pm 1\}^{({E-A})}.

In contrast to
{L}_{A}
, the smaller set
{L}^{A}
requires the existence of a full fiber
isomorphic to
\{\pm 1\}^{A}
within
L
rather than just one point from
L
.
The two operators
{L}_{A}
and
{L}^{A}
suggest two ways
to derive an abstract simplicial complex from

\displaystyle\overline{X}({L}):=\{A\subseteq E:~{L}_{E-A}=\{\pm 1\}^{A}\},

\displaystyle\underline{{X}}({L}):=\{A\subseteq E:~{L}^{A}\neq\varnothing\}.~~~~~~~~~~~

Simplices of
\overline{{X}}({L})
are usually called sets
shattered
by
L
and simplices of
\underline{{X}}({L})
are called sets

by
L

[
8
,
17
]
. The largest size of a simplex of
\overline{{X}}({L})
is called the

Vapnik-Chervonenkis dimension
(

for short) of

(viewed as a set family).

Example 1
.

Let
E=\{1,2,3\}
and consider the subset
L
of
\{\pm 1\}^{E}
that consists
of all sign maps except the two constant ones. Then

encodes an
isometric 6-cycle in

For every singleton

L

onto
\{\pm 1\}^{E-A}
is surjective, but

does not include a full fiber isomorphic to this 2-cube.
Therefore
\overline{{X}}({L})

whereas

In

9
]
and

it is shown that

holds for any

(the inequality

[

). A set

is called

if the equality

holds. Ampleness
turned out to be preserved when passing to the complementary set

and to
the sets

, and to imply connectedness (and, even more,
isometricity) of

. It followed that
{L}_{A}
and
{L}^{A}
had to be connected (isometric) subsets of
\{\pm 1\}^{E-A}

for every ample subset
{L}
of
\{\pm 1\}^{E}
. Conversely,
connectivity (or isometricity) of
{L}^{A}
for all

turned out to imply ampleness, suggesting to call such subsets

superconnected
or
superisometric
. Further
investigation finally resulted in recognizing that our ample sets
coincided exactly with Lawrence’s lopsided sets and that an
amazingly rich and multi–facetted theory regarding such subsets
of
\{\pm 1\}^{E}
could be developed. Here is a list of the most remarkable
properties of ample sets established in
[
3
]
, each of which could
be used to define them:

(1)
Superisometry:

{L}^{A}
is isometric for all
A\subseteq E,

(2)
Superconnectivity:

{L}^{A}
is connected for all

A\subseteq E,

(3)
Isometric recursivity:

{L}
is isometric, and both

{L}_{e}
and
{L}^{e}
are ample for some
e\in E
,

(4)
Connected recursivity:

{L}
is connected,
and
{L}^{e}
is ample for
every
e\in E,

(5)
Commutativity:

({L}^{A})_{B}=({L}_{B})^{A}
holds for
any disjoint
A,B\subseteq E,

(6)
Ampleness:

\#{L}=\#\overline{X}({L}),

(7)
Sparseness
:
\#{L}=\#\underline{{X}}({L}),

(8)
Lopsidedness
: for all
A,B\subseteq E
with
A\cap B=\varnothing
and
A\cup B=E
, either
A\in\underline{{X}}({L})
or
B\in\underline{{X}}({L}^{*})
.

(9)
Hereditary Euler characteristic 1:
for every face
F
of
H(E)
intersecting
L
,

\sum_{i\geq 0}(-1)^{i}f_{i}({L}\cap F)=1,

where
f_{i}({L}\cap F)
counts the number of graphic
i
-cubes contained in the
intersection of
L
with
F
.

The second-last characterization was the original definition of lopsidedness by Lawrence
[
14
]
. The last characterization was first proved by Wiedemann
[
22
]
. We also recall the following nice characterization of ample sets due to Lawrence
[
14
]
:

(10)
Total asymmetry:
If the intersection of
{L}
with a face
F
of
H(E)
is closed by taking antipodes, then either
F\cap L
is empty or coincide with
F\cap\{\pm 1\}^{E}
.

Lawrence
[
14
]
presented several examples of ample sets. In particular, he proved that the sign vectors of the closed orthants of
\mathbb{R}^{E}
intersecting a convex set
K
is ample (he also presented an example of an ample set not realizable in this way). Additionally, in
[
3
]
, we proved that vertex-sets of median graphs (CAT(0) cube complexes) are ample, and, more generally, that convex geometries and their bouquets (which we called conditional antimatroids) are ample.

Our results

Every subset
{L}
of
\{\pm 1\}^{X}
gives rise to a (not necessarily connected)

cube complex
comprising all faces of the hypercube
{H}(E)
all of whose vertices
belong to
{L};
cf.
[
21
]
. This (compact) cubical polyhedron
(a
cubihedron
for short) will be denoted by
|{L}|
and
called the
geometric realization
of
L
. The vertices of
|{L}|

are exactly the elements in
{L}
. The 1-skeleton of this cubihedron is the graph
G({L})
defined above. If

L
is connected, then
|{L}|
is connected
as well and therefore can be endowed with an intrinsic

\ell_{1}
-metric
d_{|{L}|}
. The resulting metric space

(|{L}|,d_{|{L}|})
is complete and
geodesic but is not
necessarily
path-
\ell_{1}
-isometric
(in the sense that any
r^{\prime},r^{\prime\prime}\in|{L}|

can be connected in
|{L}|
by an
\ell_{1}
-geodesic).
For example, if
L
is defined as in Example
1
,
then
|{L}|
is a solid 6-cycle of
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}.
The
\ell_{1}
-distance between the midpoints of two opposite
sides of this cycle is 4, while the intrinsic
\ell_{1}
-distance is 6.

In this paper, we will establish
that path-
\ell_{1}
-isometricity of the associated cube complex
|{L}|

is yet another characteristic feature of ampleness,
thus demonstrating that ample sets constitute a fundamental
domain for
\ell_{1}
-geometry:

(11)
Path-
\ell_{1}
-isometricity:

(|{L}|,d_{|{L}|})
is a
path-
\ell_{1}
-isometric subspace of the
\ell_{1}
-space
({\mathbb{R}}^{E},d)
.

Since the metric space
(|{L}|,d_{|{L}|})
is complete, by Menger’s theorem
[
15
]
,
the path-
\ell_{1}
-isometricity of
|L|
is equivalent to its Menger convexity, which in our particular
\ell_{1}
-case will be dubbed weak convexity:
K\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{E}
is called
weakly convex
if for any
r^{\prime},r^{\prime\prime}\in|{L}|
there exists

r\in|{L}|
different from
r^{\prime}
and
r^{\prime\prime}
such that
d(r^{\prime},r^{\prime\prime})=d(r^{\prime},r)+d(r,r^{\prime\prime})
. Convex sets of
{\mathbb{R}}^{E}
are weakly convex, explaining the chosen name.

Weak convexity is not the weakest requirement to
|L|
, which still characterizes ampleness of
L
. We call a subset
K
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{E}

sign-convex
if for any
r^{\prime},r^{\prime\prime}\in K
and every
e\in E
with
r^{\prime}(e)r^{\prime\prime}(e)<0
there exists some
r\in K
with
r(e)=0
and
\sign(r(f))\in\{\sign(r^{\prime}(f)),\sign(r^{\prime\prime}(f))\}
for all
f\in E
with
r^{\prime}(f)r^{\prime\prime}(f)\geq 0
.

(12)
Weak and sign convexity:

|{L}|
is
weakly convex (equivalently,
|{L}|
is sign-convex).

While weakly convex subsets of
{\mathbb{R}}^{E}
are obviously connected, sign-convexity is more admissive because finite subsets of
{\mathbb{R}}^{E}
may be sign-convex. In fact we show that sign-convexity characterizes the set
\Baryc(L)
of the barycenter maps of faces of
|L|
of ample sets
L
.

Lawrence
[
14
]
showed how to derive ample sets
L(K)
from convex subsets
K
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{E}
. There is also a
canonical construction which allows to derive ample sets
L(J)
from certain subsets
J
of
\{\pm 1,0\}^{E}
. Consider the standard ordering
\prec
of signs
-1,+1,0
for which
-1
and
+1

are incomparable,
0\prec-1
and
0\prec+1
and endow
\{\pm 1,0\}^{E}
with the product ordering, denoted also by
\prec
. The
upward closure

\uparrow\hskip-2.84544ptJ
of a subset
J\subseteq\{\pm 1,0\}^{E}
consists of all
t^{\prime}\in\{\pm 1,0\}^{E}
such that
t\prec t^{\prime}
for some
t\in J
. If
L
is ample, then
L
can be retrieved from the set
\Baryc(L)
as
L=\Baryc(L)\cap\{\pm 1\}^{E}=\uparrow\hskip-2.84544pt\Baryc(L)\cap\{\pm 1\}^{E}
. Therefore, one may ask for which subsets
J
of
\{\pm 1,0\}^{E}
, the set
\uparrow\hskip-2.84544ptJ\cap\{\pm 1\}^{E}
is ample. We prove that sign-convexity of the upper closure of
J
is sufficient to characterize ampleness of
\uparrow\hskip-2.84544ptJ\cap\{\pm 1\}^{E}
:

(13)
Sign-convexity of upward closure:

L(J)
is ample if and only if
\uparrow\hskip-2.84544ptJ
is sign-convex.

Furthermore,
J
can be retrieved from
L=\uparrow\hskip-2.84544ptJ\cap\{\pm 1\}^{E}
as

\Baryc(L)
. The previous result can be viewed as an analog of the characterization of oriented matroids via covectors, see
[
5
]
.

To characterize the subsets
J
of
\{\pm 1,0\}^{E}
for which
\uparrow\hskip-2.84544ptJ\cap\{\pm 1\}^{E}
is ample, we need yet another relaxation of convexity in
{\mathbb{R}}^{E}
:
K\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{E}
is called
0
-convex
if for any
r^{\prime},r^{\prime\prime}\in K
and every
e\in E
with
r^{\prime}(e)r^{\prime\prime}(e)<0
there exists some
r\in K
with
r(e)=0
and
\sign(r(f))\in\{0,\sign(r^{\prime}(f)),\sign(r^{\prime\prime}(f))\}
for all
f\in E\setminus\{e\}
.
Each weakly convex or sign-convex subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{E}
is 0-convex but the converse is not true. In case of subsets of
\{\pm 1,0\}^{E}
, 0-convexity is analogous to the “weak elimination” axiom for signed circuits in oriented matroids
[
5
]
and is equivalent to the following
signed-circuit axiom (SCA)
:

Signed-circuit axiom:
for all
t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime}\in{J}
and
e\in E
with

r^{\prime}(e)\cdot t^{\prime\prime}(e)=-1
there exists some

t\in{J}
such that
t(e)=0
and
t(f)\in\{0,t^{\prime}(f),t^{\prime\prime}(f)\}

for all
f\in E.

(14)
0-Convexity and (SCA):

\uparrow\hskip-2.84544ptJ\cap\{\pm 1\}^{E}
is ample if and only if
J
is 0-convex, i.e.,
J
satisfies (SCA).

Every subset
J
of
\{\pm 1,0\}^{E}
can be extended to the cubihedron
[{J}]

consisting of all cubes whose
barycenters belong to
J
. From the previous results it follows that the cubihedron
[{J}]
is
path-
\ell_{1}
-isometric if and only if
J
is 0-convex.

Ample sets
L
can also be characterized via their “cocircuits”, i.e. the set
\Cocirc(L)
of
barycentric maps of the facets (maximal faces) of the associated cubihedron

|{L}|
. From our previous characterizations we deduce that the cocircuits of ample sets are exactly the sign maps that satisfy the signed-circuit axiom (SCA) and consist of pairwise minimal (for
\prec
) maps :

(15)
Cocircuits:

\Cocirc(L)
satisfies (SCA) and
t,t^{\prime}\in\Cocirc(L)
with
t\prec t^{\prime}
implies
t=t^{\prime}
.

Furthermore, if
J\subset\{\pm 1,0\}^{E}
satisfies (SCA) and pairwise minimality, then
\Cocirc(L(J))=J
.

The
characterization of cocircuits
{R}\subseteq\{\pm 1,0\}^{E}
of
oriented matroids also involves maximality, the signed-circuit
axiom, but, additionally, requires the symmetry (
r\in{R}
implies that

-r\in{R}
)
[
5
]
. Nevertheless, the lack of symmetry implies that ample sets can differ
substantially from oriented matroids regarding their combinatorial and geometric structure.

The primary motivation of
Lawrence in
[
14
]
was to investigate and generalize
those subsets

{L}(K):=\{s\in\{\pm 1\}^{E}:\{t\in K:t(e)s(e)\geq 0\mbox{ for all }e\in E\}\neq\varnothing\}

of
\{\pm 1\}^{E}
which represent the closed orthants of
{\mathbb{R}}^{E}

intersecting a convex subset
K
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{E}
. He (as well as Wiedemann
[

]
) showed that such sets
are ample. However, not every ample set encodes
the orthant intersection pattern for a convex set in Euclidean
space; see
[
14
]
. It comes close, though. As we will show below, in order
to have a full geometric representation, one has to resort to a
weaker concept of weak convexity:

(16)
Realizability by weakly convex sets:

L
encodes the orthant
intersection pattern for some weakly convex (path-
\ell_{1}
-isometric) set
K
of

({\mathbb{R}}^{E},||\cdot||_{1}),
that is, a sign
vector

exactly when the orthant determined by

We also show that for a subset
L
of
\{\pm 1\}^{E}
the (orthogonal) projection and geometric

is ample:

(17)
Commutativity of projection and geometric realization:

|{L}|_{A}=|{L}_{A}|
holds for all
A\subseteq E
.

Furthermore, instead of
|{L}|_{A}=|{L}_{A}|
, it is sufficient to consider only the equality between the topological dimensions of
|{L}|_{A}
and
|{L}_{A}|
:

(18)

(Personal) historical note

Our journey in the world of ample/lopsided sets started in December 1995, when A. Dress sent us a fax with the inequality
\#\underline{{X}}({L})\leq\#{L}\leq\#\overline{{X}}({L})
and the suggestion to investigate the set families
L
for which
\#{L}=\#\overline{{X}}({L})
. Soon after we come up with the largest part of the characterizations and examples presented in
[
3
]
and several results of the present paper. In 1996 we discovered the paper by Lawrence
[
14
]
and showed that his lopsided sets and our ample sets are the same. This somehow diminished our enthusiasm, nevertheless we continued working on the subject (as certified, among other documents, by a 100 pages fax sent to each of us by A. Dress from New York in 1998). In 2006 we completed the first part of the paper, which appeared in
[
3
]
(some preliminary results have been announced in
[
9
]
). Soon after the publication of
[
3
]
, each of us got a letter from R. Wiedemann with a copy of his PhD
[
22
]
from 1986, informing us that in
[
22
]
he rediscovered ample sets
under the name “simple sets”. In 2012 we finished the first version of the current paper and we came across the master
thesis of S. Moran
[
16
]
, from which we learned that the ample/lopsided sets have been also independently rediscovered by Bollobàs and Ratcliffe
[
6
]
under the name “extremal sets” and that the inequality
\#{L}\leq\#\overline{{X}}({L})
was previously established by Pajor
[
19
]
. The inequality

\#{L}\leq\#\overline{{X}}({L})
becomes of some importance in combinatorics and computational learning theory, in particular since it implies the classical Sauer-Shelah-Perles
inequality
\#L\leq\binom{n}{\leq d}
(where
d
is the VC-dimension of
L
), which is thoroughly used in the theory of VC-dimension. Indeed, the simplices of
\overline{{X}}({L})
are the shuttered by
L
subsets of
E
and the size of a largest simplex of
\overline{{X}}({L})
is the VC-dimension
d
. Do to this (and in analogy to Sauer-Shelah-Perles
inequality), we suggest to call
\#\underline{{X}}({L})\leq\#{L}\leq\#\overline{{X}}({L})
the
Dress-Pajor inequality
.

The set families
L
for which
\#L=\binom{n}{\leq d}
are called “maximum sets”
[
11
]
and they are ample. Maximum sets have been investigated in
[
11
]
and they represent important concept/hypothesis classes for which the
sample compression conjecture

[
12
]
from computational learning theory was established. This also motivated the investigation and the solution of the sample compression conjecture for ample sets
[
17
]
(see also
[
8
]
for the unlabeled version of this conjecture).

Even though this article was not submitted for publication earlier, the obtained results inspired two of us and K. Knauer to introduce the complexes of oriented matroids (called also conditional oriented matroids and abbreviated COMs)
[
4
]
. COMs are defined in terms of covectors (
\{\pm 1,0\}^{E}
-vectors) using similar axioms as the Oriented Matroids (OMs)
[
5
]
, only global symmetry and the existence of the
zero sign vector, required for Oriented Matroids, are replaced by local relative conditions. COMs represent a far reaching common generalization of ample sets, OMs, rankings, and CAT(0) Coxeter zonotopal complexes. COMs can be endowed with the structure of a cell complex, in which each cell is an OM, and this cell complex is contractible.
The ample sets are exactly the COMs in which all cells are cubes.
The graphs of topes of COMs have been characterized by Knauer and Marc
[
13
]
. Currently, both ample sets and COMs are subjects of active studies.

present characterizations of ample cubihedra via metric conditions, weak and sign-convexities, and via projections. Then the geometric
realization of ample sets in terms of intersection pattern with orthants is established in Section
7
. Section

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Maps and sign maps

Given a finite set
E
, we denote by
\#E
its cardinality,
by
\{\pm 1\}^{E}
the set of all maps from
E

into
\{\pm 1\}=\{-1,+1\}
, by
\{\pm 1,0\}^{E}
the set of all maps from
E

into
\{\pm 1,0\}
, and by
{\mathbb{R}}^{E}
the vector space consisting of all maps
r

from
E
into
\mathbb{R}
. Clearly,
\{\pm 1\}^{E}\subset\{\pm 1,0\}^{E}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{E}
.
For
r\in{\mathbb{R}}^{E}
and
e\in E
, we denote by
\sign(r(e))\in\{-1,0,+1\}
the sign of
r(e)
.
To each map
r\in{\mathbb{R}}^{E}
we associate its
support

\supp(r)=\{e\in E:r(e)\neq 0\}
and its
sign vector

\sign(r)\in\{\pm 1,0\}^{E}
whose coordinates are
\sign(r(e)),e\in E
.
For any
r^{\prime},r^{\prime\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{E}
, we denote by
\Delta(r^{\prime},r^{\prime\prime})=\{e\in E:r^{\prime}(e)\neq r^{\prime\prime}(e)\}
their
difference set
and by

d(r^{\prime},r^{\prime\prime})
their
\ell_{1}
-distance
||r^{\prime},r^{\prime\prime}||_{1}=\sum_{e\in E}|r^{\prime}(e)-r^{\prime\prime}(e)|=\sum_{e\in\Delta(r^{\prime},r^{\prime\prime})}|r^{\prime}(e)-r^{\prime\prime}(e)|
. Let also
[r^{\prime},r^{\prime\prime}]=\{r\in{\mathbb{R}}^{E}:d(r^{\prime},r)+d(r,r^{\prime\prime})=d(r^{\prime},r^{\prime\prime})
. Note that
[r^{\prime},r^{\prime\prime}]
is an axis-parallel box.

Each
e\in E
defines a coordinate hyperplane
H_{e}=\{r\in{\mathbb{R}}^{E}:r(e)=0\}
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{E}
, which partitions
{\mathbb{R}}^{E}-H_{e}
into
positive
and
negative
open halfspaces. The set
{\mathcal{H}}=\{H_{e}:e\in E\}
is a
central arrangement
of hyperplanes;
{\mathcal{H}}
indices a partition
{\mathcal{R}}
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{E}
into open regions and recursively into regions defined by the intersections of some hyperplanes of
{\mathcal{H}}
. More specifically, each region
R\in\mathcal{R}
is the intersection of a set
H_{e},e\in E^{\prime}
of hyperplanes of
{\mathcal{H}}
and a set of positive or negative halfspaces, one halfspace for each
H_{f},f\in E-E^{\prime}
. The dimension of each
R
varies between
\#E
and
0
. The regions of
\mathcal{R}
of maximal dimension are the
2^{\#E}
open orthants of
{\mathbb{R}}^{E}
.

For every point
r
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{E}
, the sign vector
\sign(r)
is the sign map

s\in\{\pm 1,0\}^{E}
such that
s(e)=0
if
r\in H_{e}
,
s(e)=+1
if
r
belongs to the positive halfspace of
H_{e}
and
s(e)=-1
if
r
belongs to the negative halfspace of
H_{e}
. All points
r
belonging to the same region
R
of
{\mathcal{R}}
have the same sign vector and any two points belonging to distinct regions of
{\mathcal{R}}
have distinct sign vectors. In particular, all points belonging to the same open orthant
{O}
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{E}
have the same sign vector

s\in\{\pm 1\}^{E}
and
{O}
can be retrieved from
s
as

\displaystyle{O}=\{r\in{\mathbb{R}}^{E}:~r(e)\cdot s(e)>0\mbox{ for all }e\in E\}.

The closure
\widetilde{{O}}
of
{O}
is a closed orthant of
{\mathbb{R}}^{E}
and
\widetilde{{O}}
can be also identified by the sign map
s
as
\widetilde{{O}}={O}(s)
, where

\displaystyle{O}(s):=\{r\in{\mathbb{R}}^{E}:~r(e)\cdot s(e)\geq 0\mbox{ for all }e\in E\}.

Conversely, given
r\in{\mathbb{R}}^{E},
the set

\displaystyle\Sign(r):=\{s\in\{\pm 1\}^{E}:~r(e)\cdot s(e)\geq 0\mbox{ for all }e\in E\},

indicates the closed orthants of
{\mathbb{R}}^{E}
to which
r
belongs.

2.2.
The hypercube
H(E)
and its faces

By
H(E)
we denote the
solid hypercube

[-1,+1]^{E}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{E}
and consider
H(E)
as a cube complex. By
H^{(1)}(E)
we denote the graph representing the
1-skeleton
of
H(E)
, i.e., the graph with vertex-set

\{\pm 1\}^{E}
and whose edge set consists of all pairs
\{s,s^{\prime}\}
of
\{\pm 1\}^{E}
such that
d(s,s^{\prime})=2
(equivalently,
\Delta(s,s^{\prime})=1
). This implies that the standard graph distance in
H^{(1)}(E)
between any two vertices
s^{\prime},s^{\prime\prime}\in H^{(0)}
is equal to
\frac{1}{2}d(s^{\prime},s^{\prime\prime})
.

Definition 1

(Faces of
H(E)
and their barycenters)
.

A
face

F
of the hypercube

is a
A
-fiber of the form

{F}=H(A)\times s_{0}\mbox{ for some }s_{0}\in\{\pm 1\}^{E-A}

with
A\subseteq E.
By convention,
H(E)
is its
E
-fiber and its vertices are the faces that are
\varnothing
-fibers. The
barycenter
of the face
F
is the map
s\in\{\pm 1,0\}^{E}
such that
s(e)=0
if
e\in A
and
s(e)=s_{0}(e)\in\{-1,+1\}
if
e\in E-A
. We say that two
faces
F^{\prime},F^{\prime\prime}
of
H(E)
are
parallel
if there exist
A\subseteq E
and
s^{\prime}_{0},s^{\prime\prime}_{0}\in\{\pm 1\}^{E-A}
such that
{F}^{\prime}=H(A)\times s^{\prime}_{0}
and
{F}^{\prime\prime}=H(A)\times s^{\prime\prime}_{0}
. Parallel faces thus arise by intersecting
the hypercube with parallel (affine)
A
-
planes

\{p\in{\mathbb{R}}^{E}:~p|_{E-A}=c\}\mbox{ for }c\in H(E-A).

For each
r\in H(E)
denote by
F(r)
the (necessarily unique) smallest face of
H(E)
containing
r
.

For each point
r
of
H(E)
, the face
F(r)
is determined by the coordinates
r(e)
(
e\in E
) for which
-1<r(e)<+1:

F(r)=H(E(r))\times r|_{E-E(r)},\mbox{ where }

E(r):=\{e\in E:~-1<r(e)<+1\},\mbox{ and }

r|_{E-E(r)}\in\{\pm 1\}^{E-E(r)}.

The barycenter of the face
F(r)
is given by the map from
E
to
\{\pm 1,0\}
that is the zero map on
E(r)
and coincides with
r|_{E-E(r)}
elsewhere.

(a) Ordering of signs. (b) Product ordering on
\{\pm 1,0\}^{2}
; the shaded nodes correspond to the barycenter maps of an ample set.

The set of all barycenters of faces of
H(E)
is
\{\pm 1,0\}^{E}
. In order to express inclusion of faces of
H(E)
in terms of the corresponding barycenter maps we use the standard ordering
\prec
of signs
-1,+1,0
for which
-1
and
+1

are incomparable,
0\prec-1
and
0\prec+1
; see Fig. 1(a). The product ordering
\prec^{E}
on
\{\pm 1,0\}^{E}
will also be denoted by
\prec
. The undirected Hasse diagram of
(\{\pm 1,0\}^{E},\prec)
is a grid graph (viz., the Cartesian

-power of a path
with two edges) and will be denoted by
G(\{\pm 1,0\}^{E})
; see Fig. 1(b) for
\#E=2
. Thus,
t_{1}\prec t_{2}
for two maps
t_{1},t_{2}\in\{\pm 1,0\}^{E}
holds if and only if
t_{1}(x)\in\{0,t_{2}(x)\}
for all

or equivalently, if for the associated faces the inclusion

2.3.
Subsets of sign maps, their cubihedra and barycentric completions

For a subset
{L}
of
\{\pm 1\}^{E}
, we denote by
{L}^{*}:=\{\pm 1\}^{E}-{L}
the set-theoretic complement of
L
and by
G({L})
the subgraph of
{H}^{(1)}(E)
induced by
L
,
i.e., the graph with vertex-set
L
and edge-set consisting of all edges of
{H}^{(1)}(E)
between two vertices of
L
(again we suppose that the edges of
G(L)
have length 2). The set
L
is
called
connected

if
G({L})
is connected, and it is called
isometric
if every
pair of vertices
s^{\prime},s^{\prime\prime}
of
L
can be connected in
G({L})

by a path of length
d(s^{\prime},s^{\prime\prime}).

Definition 2

(The cubihedron)
.

The
geometric realization
(a
cubihedron
for short) of a set
L\subseteq\{\pm 1\}^{E}
is the cube complex
|{L}|\subseteq H(E)

consisting of all faces
F
of the hypercube
{H}(E)
for which
{F}\cap\{\pm 1\}^{E}\subseteq{L}.
The vertices of
|{L}|
are the elements of
{L}

and the 1-skeleton
|{L}|^{(1)}
of
|{L}|
coincides with
G(L)
. The
facets
of
|L|
are the maximal by inclusion faces of
|L|
.

Notice that for each point
r
in
|L|
, its smallest face
F(r)
in the hypercube
H(E)
necessarily belongs to
|L|
.

Definition 3

(The barycentric completion)
.

For a subset
L
of
\{\pm 1\}^{E}
, the subset of
\{\pm 1,0\}^{E}
consisting of all barycenters of the faces of
|L|
is called the
barycentric completion
of
L
and is denoted by
\Baryc(L)
(or
\Baryc(|L|)
).

Definition 4

(The upward closure and the set of minima)
.

For a subset
J\subseteq\{\pm 1,0\}^{E}
, the
upward closure

\uparrow\hskip-2.84544ptJ
of
J
relative to the ordering
\prec
is defined by

\uparrow\hskip-2.84544ptJ=\{t^{\prime}\in\{\pm 1,0\}^{E}:~t\prec t^{\prime}\mbox{ for some }t\in J\}.

Then
J\subseteq\{\pm 1,0\}^{E}
is called
upward closed
if
\uparrow\hskip-2.84544ptJ=J
.

Denote also by
\Min(J)=\{t\in J:~t^{\prime}\prec t\mbox{ implies }t^{\prime}=t\mbox{ for all }t^{\prime}\in J\}
the set of all minimal elements of the poset
(J,\prec)
.

We continue with two simple properties of upward closure:

Lemma 1
.

For any subset
J
of
\{\pm 1,0\}^{E}
,
\Baryc(\uparrow\hskip-2.84544ptJ\cap\{\pm 1\}^{E})=\uparrow\hskip-2.84544ptJ
.

Proof.

Indeed,
\uparrow\hskip-2.84544ptJ
consists of the barycenters
t^{\prime}
of faces
F(t^{\prime})
included in faces
F(t)
of
H(E)
with
t\in J
. These are exactly the barycenters of faces
F
of
H(E)
for which
F\cap\{\pm 1\}^{E}\subseteq\uparrow\hskip-2.84544ptJ\cap\{\pm 1\}^{E}
, i.e.,

\Baryc(\uparrow\hskip-2.84544ptJ\cap\{\pm 1\}^{E})
.
∎

Lemma 2
.

For any subset
L
of
\{\pm 1\}^{E}
,
\Baryc(L)
is an upward closed subset of
\{\pm 1,0\}^{E}
.

Proof.

If
t\in\Baryc(L)
and
t\prec t^{\prime}
, then
t^{\prime}
is the barycenter of the face
F(t^{\prime})
contained in
F(t)
. Since
F(t)
belongs to
|L|
,
F(t^{\prime})
also belongs to
|L|
, whence
t^{\prime}\in\Baryc(L)
. This shows that

\uparrow\hskip-2.84544pt\Baryc(L)\subseteq\Baryc(L)
. The converse inclusion is obvious.
∎

of

and

of

induced by the arrangement

, respectively.

Definition 5

(

and

)
.

Given any subset

of

\displaystyle L(K):=\bigcup_{r\in K}\Sign(r)=\{s\in\{\pm 1\}^{E}:~~\exists r\in K\mbox{ with }r(e)\cdot s(e)>0~\forall e\in\supp(r)\}

and

for all

. Note also that

and

.

To any subset

and the corresponding geometric realization

of

. This realization can be compared to the cube complex

, which is defined as the union of the smallest faces

of

containing

One can retrieve the upward closure
\uparrow\hskip-2.84544pt{J}

\displaystyle\uparrow\hskip-2.84544pt{J}=J([J])=\Baryc([J]).

We now introduce the notation

-plane:

2.5.
Intrinsic metric

Let
(M,\rho)
be a metric space,
I\subset{\mathbb{R}}
a non-empty interval
and
\gamma:I\rightarrow M
a curve (i.e. a continuous map). We define the length
\ell(\gamma)\in[0,\infty]

of
\gamma
by
\ell(\gamma)=\sup\sum_{i=1}^{k}\rho(\gamma(t_{i-1},t_{i})
, where the supremum is taken over all
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
and all sequences
t_{0}\leq t_{1}\leq\ldots t_{k}
in
I
. We say that
\gamma
is
rectifiable
if

\ell(\gamma)<\infty
. The
intrinsic metric
associated with
\rho
is the function
\delta_{M,\rho}:M\times M\rightarrow[0,\infty]
defined by
\delta^{M,d}(x,y)=\inf\ell(\gamma)
, where the infimum is taken over all rectifiable curves
\gamma:[0,1]\rightarrow M
from
x
to
y
,
i.e.
\gamma(0)=x,\gamma(1)=y
.
(M,\rho)
is called a
length space
if
\delta^{M,d}=\rho
(see the Appendix for other related notions and results).

A
geodesic
of
(M,\rho)
is the image of a continuous map
\gamma:[0,\alpha]\rightarrow M
with
\alpha=\rho(r^{\prime},r^{\prime\prime})
such that
\gamma(0)=r^{\prime},\gamma(\alpha)=r^{\prime\prime}
and
\rho(\gamma(s),\gamma(t))=|s-t|
for all
s,t\in[0,\alpha]
. A metric space
(M,\rho)
is a
geodesic space

[
7
]
if any two points
r^{\prime},r^{\prime\prime}
can be connected in
M
by a geodesic. In this paper, we consider the geodesic metric space
({\mathbb{R}}^{E},d)
, where
d
is the
\ell_{1}
-distance. An
\ell_{1}
-geodesic
is a geodesic of
({\mathbb{R}}^{E},d)
.

A metric space
(M,d)
is
Menger-convex
if for any two distinct points
x,y\in M
there exists some point
z

between

x
and
y,
that is,
z
belongs to the
segment

[x,y]_{M}=\{z\in M:~d(x,z)+d(z,y)=d(x,y)\}

such that, in addition,
z
is different from
x
and
y
. Menger
[
15
]
has shown that Menger-convexity in a complete metric space
(M,d)

entails that
(M,d)
is geodesic (see also
[
1
]
or
[
20
, §18.5]
).

3.
Weak and sign convexities

In this section we consider several relaxations of classical convexity in metric spaces.
We consider every subset
K
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{E}
as a metric space
(K,d)
relative to the metric induced on
K
by the
\ell_{1}
-metric
d
(we denote the induced metric also by
d
). Recall that a subset
K
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{E}
is called
convex
if it contains the linear segment between any two points
r^{\prime},r^{\prime\prime}\in K
. Analogously,

K\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{E}
is called
\ell_{1}
-convex
if it contains all
\ell_{1}
-geodesics between any two points
r^{\prime},r^{\prime\prime}\in K
. Since the linear segments are
\ell_{1}
-geodesics, every
\ell_{1}
-convex set is convex.
For any
e\in E
, the coordinate hyperplane
H_{e}
, the open halfspaces
H^{-}_{e},H^{+}_{e}
, and the closed halfspaces
\overline{H}^{-}_{e},\overline{H}^{+}_{e}
defined by
H_{e}
are
\ell_{1}
-convex. Furthermore, the faces of the hypercube
H(E)
are also
\ell_{1}
-convex. It is known (and easy to prove) that the
\ell_{1}
-convex subsets of
{\mathbb{R}}^{E}
are gated in the following sense. A subset
K
of any metric space
({\mathbb{R}}^{E},d)
is called
gated

[
10
]
if for
every point
r\in{\mathbb{R}}^{E}
there exists a (necessarily unique) point
r^{\prime}\in K,
the
gate
of
r
in
K
, for which

d(r,q)=d(r,r^{\prime})+d(r^{\prime},q)\mbox{ for all }q\in K.

Now, we consider weaker versions of convexity.

Definition 6

(Weak convexity)
.

A subset
K
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{E}
is called
weakly convex
if
(K,d)
is complete and Menger-convex, i.e., if for any two distinct points
r^{\prime},r^{\prime\prime}\in K
there exists some point
r\neq r^{\prime},r^{\prime\prime}
such that
d(r^{\prime},r^{\prime\prime})=d(r^{\prime},r)+d(r,r^{\prime\prime})
.

Clearly, every convex subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{E}
is weakly convex. The following lemma can be viewed as a reformulation of the definition of weak convexity and follows from Menger’s theorem
[
15
]

mentioned above:

Lemma 3
.

For a subset
K
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{E}
such that
(K,d)
is complete, the following conditions are equivalent:

(i)

K
is weakly convex;

(ii)

(K,d)
is a length space;

(iii)

K
is path-
\ell_{1}
-isometric in the sense that the restriction of the
\ell_{1}
-metric
d
on
K
constitutes the intrinsic metric
\delta^{K,d}
of
K
.

We continue with two combinatorial relaxations of weak convexity:

Definition 7

(Sign convexity)
.

A subset
K
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{E}
is called
sign-convex
if for any two distinct points
r^{\prime},r^{\prime\prime}\in K
and every
e\in E
with
r^{\prime}(e)r^{\prime\prime}(e)<0
there exists some point
r\in K
with
r(e)=0
and
\sign(r(f))\in\{\sign(r^{\prime}(f)),\sign(r^{\prime\prime}(f))\}
for all
f\in E
with
r^{\prime}(f)r^{\prime\prime}(f)\geq 0
(and no condition on
\sign(r(f))
in case
r^{\prime}(f)r^{\prime\prime}(f)<0
and
f\neq e
).

Lemma 4
.

Every weakly convex (and hence every convex) subset
K
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{E}
is sign-convex.

Proof.

Pick any
r^{\prime},r^{\prime\prime}\in K
and
e\in E
with
r^{\prime}(e)r^{\prime\prime}(e)<0
. Since
K
is weakly convex,
r^{\prime}
and
r^{\prime\prime}
can be connected in
K
by an
\ell_{1}
-geodesic
\gamma(r^{\prime},r^{\prime\prime})
. Consider the coordinate hyperplane
H_{e}
defined by
e
. Since
r^{\prime}(e)r^{\prime\prime}(e)<0
,
r^{\prime}
and
r^{\prime\prime}
belong to distinct open halfspaces defined by
H_{e}
. Therefore
\gamma(r^{\prime},r^{\prime\prime})
intersects
H_{e}
in a point
r
. Then
r(e)=0
, thus
\sign(r(e))=0
. Now, consider any
f\in E
such that
r^{\prime}(f)r^{\prime\prime}(f)\geq 0
. This implies that
r^{\prime}
and
r^{\prime\prime}
belong to the same closed halfspace defined by the hyperplane
H_{f}
, say
r^{\prime},r^{\prime\prime}
belong to the closed negative halfspace
\overline{H}_{f}^{-}
. Since
\overline{H}_{f}^{-}
is
\ell_{1}
-convex, the geodesic
\gamma(r^{\prime},r^{\prime\prime})
as well as the point
r
also belong to
\overline{H}_{f}^{-}
. Therefore
r(f)\leq 0
and thus
\sign(r(f))\in\{-1,0\}
. If one of the points
r^{\prime},r^{\prime\prime}
belong to
H_{f}
and the second point belong to the open halfspace
H^{-}_{f}
, then
\{\sign(r^{\prime}(f)),\sign(r^{\prime\prime}(f))\}=\{-1,0\}
, yielding
\sign(r(f))\in\{\sign(r^{\prime}(f)),\sign(r^{\prime\prime}(f))\}
. If both points
r^{\prime},r^{\prime\prime}
belong
to
H_{f}
, then
r
also belongs to
H_{f}
because

4.
Intrinsic path metrics

In this section we recall some basic notions about intrinsic path metrics and the length of paths, which are relevant for the intrinsic metrics of cubihedra.

In an arbitrary metric space
(M,d)
one can trivially define the
length
\ell(P)
of a finite
x,y
-path

P
of points
x=:t_{0},t_{1},\ldots,t_{k}:=y
as the sum

\ell(P):=\sum_{i=1}^{k}d(t_{i-1},t_{i}).

The
modulus
of this path
P
is the maximum of all single step lengths
d(t_{i-1},t_{i})
for
i=1,...,k.
Then the infimum

\mbox{inf}\{\ell(P):~P\mbox{ is a finite }x,y\mbox{-path in }M\mbox{ with modulus}(P)<\epsilon\}

of the lengths of all finite paths between two points
x
and
y
in
M
having modulus smaller than
\epsilon
exists because the
length of every finite path between
x
and
y
is bounded below by
d(x,y)
by virtue of the triangle inequality. Then the supremum

\delta_{M,d}(x,y):=\mbox{sup}\{\mbox{inf}\{\ell(P):~P\mbox{ is a finite }x,y\mbox{-path in }M\mbox{ with modulus}(P)<\epsilon\}:~\epsilon\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\}

will be called the
(intrinsic) finite-path distance
between
x
and
y
in the metric space
(M,d)
, which may formally take
the value
\infty
of the extended real line when the supremum does not exist. If, however, all values
are real numbers then we could speak of the
(intrinsic) finite-path metric
of
(M,d)
, since evidently
(M,d)
satisfies the triangle inequality.

If there exists a (general)
x,y
-
path
in
(M,d)
, that is, a continuous map
\gamma:[0,1]\rightarrow M
with
\gamma(0)=x
and
\gamma(1)=y
, then its length
[
19
, p.11]

L(\gamma):=\sup\Big\{\sum\limits^{k}_{i=1}d(\gamma(t_{i-1}),\gamma(t_{i})):0\leq t_{0}\leq t_{1}\leq\ldots\leq t_{k}\leq 1\mbox{ where }k\geq 1\Big\}

is an upper bound for the lengths
\ell(P)
of all finite
x,y
-paths
P
contained in
\gamma
. If an
x,y
-path
\gamma
exists with
L(\gamma)<\infty
, then
x
and
y
are said to be
connected by a rectifiable path
in
(M,d)

[
19
, p.35]
. Taking the infimum over all general
x,y
-paths, we obtain

\delta^{M,d}(x,y):=\mbox{inf}\{L(\gamma):~\gamma\mbox{ is an }x,y\mbox{-path in}~M\},

referred to as the
path
(or
length
)
distance
in
M
relative to
d
(in the book
[
19
]
the length distance is denoted by
d_{\ell}
).
If each pair of points in
M
is connected by a rectifiable path, then by
[
19
, Proposition 2.1.5]

is a metric on
M
, referred to
the
(intrinsic) path metric
of
M
associated to
d.
We thus have the inequalities

d(x,y)\leq\delta_{M,d}(x,y)\leq\delta^{M,d}(x,y),

with the understanding that either
\delta
value could equal
\infty
. In the extreme case,
\delta_{M,d}
could be a metric whereas

\delta^{M,d}
is the constant
\infty
map on pairs of distinct points. Consider, for example, the rational unit interval
M=\mathbb{Q}\mbox{[}0,1]

with its natural metric
d
: since paths of any modulus exist,
the intrinsic path metric coincides with the natural metric, but geodesics between distinct points do not exist, whence the intrinsic length distance is infinite.
Therefore the advantage of using
\delta_{M,d}
rather than
\delta^{M,d}
is that we do not need to impose existence of rectifiable paths. In the
important case that
(M,d)
is a
length
(or

[
19
, p.35]
, that is,

coincides with
\delta^{M,d}
,
both concepts of intrinsic metrics can be equated with the original metric

The property
\delta_{M,d}=d
carries over to dense subspaces of

(M,d)
. In an arbitrary length space
(M,d)
general
x,y
-paths of length
d(x,y)
, that is,
x,y
-geodesics, need not exist. When however
they exist for all pairs of points then the space is called
geodesic
. A geodesic space

is called a

if every
geodesic constitutes the unique path between its end points. Note that a compact real tree
({M},d)
may have infinitely many

branching points
, i.e. points

for which
{M}-\{p\}
has at least three connected components.

Every finite graph

has a trivial geometric realization as a 1-dimensional cell complex obtained by replacing each edge
\{x,y\}
by a solid link, that is, a
copy
[u_{x},u_{y}]
of the unit interval
[0,1]
of the real line such that two copies intersect in an endpoint exactly when the corresponding edges are incident. The
resulting geometric graph (alias network)

inherits its metric

from the standard-graph metric

of

and the usual distance of

Informally speaking,

|p-u_{v}|+d(v,x)+|q-u_{x}|,|p-u_{v}|+d(v,y)+|q-u_{y}|,|p-u_{w}|+d(w,x)+|q-u_{x}|,

More formally, one can apply the general construction of the

Similarly, in ad hoc manner, one could deal with the geometric realization of

Completeness is an essential prerequisite in Menger’s proof that complete Menger-convex spaces are geodesic. Indeed, the intersection

(M,d)

6.
Projection and dimension

We will now show that for a subset
L
of
\{\pm 1\}^{E}
the operators of (orthogonal) projection and geometric
realization commute exactly when
L
is ample. To this end we will make use of the calculus involving sets of the form
({L}^{B})_{A}
, as developed
in
[
3
]
. First observe that, essentially by definition, we have

(1)

\displaystyle|{L}|=\bigcup\limits_{B\subseteq E}\bigcup\limits_{s\in{L}^{B}}H(B)\times s.

Therefore the topological dimension of the cubihedron
|L|
can be expressed as

\dim|{L}|=\max\,\{\#B:{L}^{B}\neq\varnothing\}=\max\,\{\#B:B\in\underline{{X}}({L})\},

using the terminology of
[
3
]
; see also the Introduction. Applying the above equation to
{L}_{A}
instead of
L
yields

(2)

\displaystyle|{L}_{A}|=\bigcup\limits_{B\subseteq E-A}\bigcup\limits_{t\in({L}_{A})^{B}}H(B)\times t.

For the projection from
H(E)
to
{H}(E-A)
applied to
K=|L|
we compute

\displaystyle|{L}|_{A}

\displaystyle=\left(\bigcup_{B\subseteq E}\bigcup_{s\in{L}^{B}}H(B)\times s\right)_{A}

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{B\subseteq E}\bigcup_{s\in{L}^{B}}H(B-A)\times s|_{(E-B)-A}

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{B\subseteq E}\bigcup_{t\in({L}^{B})_{A-B}}H(B-A)\times t

\displaystyle\subseteq\bigcup_{B\subseteq E}\bigcup_{t\in(L^{B-A})_{A}}H(B-A)\times t

because for every subset
B
of
E
we have the inclusion

({L}^{B})_{A-B}=(({L}^{B-A})^{B\cap A})_{A-B}\subseteq(({L}^{B-A})_{B\cap A})_{A-B}=({L}^{B-A})_{A}.

Note that here the first and last equations use formulas from (13) of
[
3
]
, whereas the inclusion is
derived from formula (12) of
[
3
]
. If
B
is chosen to be disjoint from
A
, then the preceding chain
of expressions just collapses to
(L^{B})_{A}
and
H(B)
equals
H(B-A).
This shows that actually
equality holds above:

(3)

\displaystyle|{L}|_{A}=\bigcup_{B\subseteq E-A}\bigcup_{t\in(L^{B})_{A}}H(B)\times t.

Since
(L^{B})_{A}\subseteq(L_{A})^{B}
by (13) of
[
3
]
, we infer from (2) and (3) the inclusion

|L|_{A}\subseteq|L_{A}|.

Using (3), the dimension of the projected cubihedron can be expressed as

(4)

\displaystyle\dim|L|_{A}

\displaystyle=\max\{\#B:~(L^{B})_{A}\neq\varnothing\mbox{ for some }B\subseteq E-A\}

\displaystyle=\max\{\#B:~B\subseteq E-A\mbox{ with }L^{B}\neq\varnothing\}.

Now, the prerequisites for proving the announced result are all in place.

Theorem 3
.

For a subset
{L}\subseteq\{\pm 1\}^{E},

the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

{L}
is ample;

(ii)

|{L}|_{A}=|{L}_{A}|
holds for all
A\subseteq E
;

(iii)

\dim|{L}|_{A}=\dim|{L}_{A}|
holds for
all
A\subseteq E
.

Proof.

If
L
is ample, then
(L^{B})_{A}=(L_{A})^{B}
according to
[
3
, Theorem 2]
and consequently

|L|_{A}
and
|L_{A}|
are equal by (2) and (3). The latter equality trivially implies equality of the corresponding dimensions.

To complete the proof assume that

\displaystyle\dim|L|_{E-A}=\dim|L_{E-A}|\mbox{ for all }A\in\overline{X}(L),

that is,
A\subseteq E
with
L_{E-A}=\{\pm 1\}^{A}.
Then

\displaystyle\#A

\displaystyle=\dim H(A)=\dim|{L}_{E-A}|

\displaystyle=\dim|{L}|_{E-A}

\displaystyle=\max\{\#B:~B\subseteq A\mbox{ with }L^{B}\neq\varnothing\},

whence
L^{A}\neq\varnothing,
that is,
A\in\underline{X}(L).
Therefore
L
is ample by
[
3
, Theorem 2]
.
∎

7.
Orthant intersection pattern

Given any
subset
K
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{E},
the set

\displaystyle L(K)=\{s\in\{\pm 1\}^{E}:~K\cap{O}(s)\neq\varnothing\}

encodes the intersection pattern of
K
with the closed orthants of
{\mathbb{R}}^{E}

determined by the sign maps of
\{\pm 1\}^{E}
.
This construction was performed for convex sets by Lawrence
[
14
]
. He showed that
L(K)
is ample whenever
K
is a convex set
in the Euclidean space
{\mathbb{R}}^{E}
, but not every ample set
L
can be realized in this way.

The clue for a realization within a wider class
of subsets of
{\mathbb{R}}^{E}
with some weaker convexity properties comes from the rather obvious realization

(5)

\displaystyle L(|L|)=L\mbox{ if }L\subseteq\{\pm 1\}^{E}\mbox{ is ample}.

Indeed, the inclusion
L\subseteq L(|L|)
is trivial. Now, if
t\in{L}^{*}=\{\pm 1\}^{E}-L,
then the corresponding
closed orthant
{O}(t)
does not intersect any fiber
F\subseteq L
of
\{\pm 1\}^{E}
and hence is disjoint from the face
{F}
of
|L|,

whence
t\notin|L|.

We will now show that for a closed subset
K
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{E}
weak convexity suffices to ensure that
L(K)
is ample. To
this end we may consider only compact subsets of
H(E)
. Since
L(K)
is finite, there exists a finite subset
K_{0}
of

K
with
L(K_{0})=L(K).
We may scale
K
with some
\lambda>0
such that
\lambda K_{0}\subseteq H(E).
Then

\displaystyle\widetilde{K}:=H(E)\cap\lambda K

is a compact weakly convex subset of
H(E)
with
L(\widetilde{K})=L(K).

Theorem 4
.

A subset
{L}
of
\{\pm 1\}^{E}
is ample
if and only if there exists a weakly convex subset
K

of
\mathbb{R}^{E}
(or, equivalently, a compact weakly convex subset
K
of
H(E)
)
with
L=L(K)
.

Proof.

It remains to show that
L:=L(K)
is ample whenever
K
is a closed weakly convex subset of
H(E).
We proceed by induction on
\#E.
Since
K
is weakly convex,
K
is path-
\ell_{1}
-isometric, thus there exists
e\in E
with
L^{e}\neq\varnothing
. We claim that
L^{e}=L(K^{\prime})
, where

K^{\prime}=\{r|_{E-\{e\}}:~r\in K\mbox{ with }r(e)=0\}.

Clearly,
\{\pm 1\}\times L(K^{\prime})\subseteq L
. Conversely, if
s^{\prime}\in L^{e},
then both extensions
s_{1},s_{2}\in\{\pm 1\}^{E}
of
s^{\prime}
(with
s_{1}(e)=-1
and

s_{2}(e)=+1
) belong to
L
. Hence there exist
r_{1},r_{2}\in K
with
r_{1}(e)=-1,r_{2}(e)=+1
and
r_{1}(x)\cdot s^{\prime}(x)\geq 0,

r_{2}(x)\cdot s^{\prime}(x)\geq 0
for all
x\in E-\{e\}.

Then any geodesic
\gamma
connecting
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
in
K
must contain a point
r
with
r(e)=0,
so that necessarily
r(x)\cdot s^{\prime}(x)\geq 0
for all
x\in E
holds as well. This establishes
L^{e}=L(K^{\prime}).
Obviously, the intersection of
K
with the
e
-hyperplane through
0
is path-
\ell_{1}
-isometric, and thus weakly convex. Therefore
L^{e}

is ample by the induction hypothesis.

Finally, to prove that
L
is isometric, assume that for some subset
A
with
\#A>1
we have
s_{1},s_{2}\in L
with
s_{1}|_{E-A}=s_{2}|_{E-A}
and
\{s_{1}(y),s_{2}(y)\}=\{\pm 1\}

for all
y\in A.
For notational convenience, assume that
s_{1}(y)=-1
and
s_{2}(y)=+1
for all
y\in A.
By definition of
L
there exist
r_{1},r_{2}\in K
with

r_{i}(x)\cdot s_{i}(x)\geq 0
for
i=1,2
and all
x\in E-A
but
r_{1}(y)\leq 0\leq r_{2}(y)
for all
y\in A.
Any geodesic
\gamma
connecting
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
in
K
contains
a point
r
with
r(z)=0
for some
z\in A
such that all interior points on the subgeodesic of
\gamma
connecting
r_{1}
and
r
have negative coordinates at
A.

Necessarily
r(x)\cdot s_{i}(x)\geq 0
for
i=1,2
and all
x\in E-A.
Therefore the neighbor
s
of
s_{1}
on a shortest path between
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
in the graphic
hypercube
{H}^{(1)}(E)
satisfying
s(z)=+1
and
s|_{E-\{z\}}=s_{1}|_{E-\{z\}}
belongs to
\Sign(r)\subseteq L.
A trivial induction on
\#A
thus shows
that
L
is isometric, and thus connected. Then by
[
3
, Theorem 4]
we conclude that
L=L(K)
is ample.
∎

8.
Circuits and cocircuits

In this section we will characterize the ample sets
L
via the set
\Cocirc(L)
of the barycenter maps of the facets (maximal faces) of their cubihedra
|L|
. They correspond to the set
\Min(\Baryc(L))
of minimal elements in
\Baryc(L)
relative to the order
\prec
on
\{\pm 1,0\}
(see Definition
4
):
\Cocirc(L)=\Min(\Baryc(L))
.
The members of
\Cocirc(L)
are referred to as the
cocircuits
of
L
, since their definition bears resemblance with the one of cocircuits in oriented matroids
[
5
]
. Then

\displaystyle\Baryc(L)=\uparrow\hskip-2.84544pt\Cocirc(L)

\displaystyle=\{t\in\{\pm 1,0\}^{E}:~\uparrow\hskip-2.84544pt\{t\}\cap\{\pm 1\}^{E}\subseteq L\}

\displaystyle=\{t\in\{\pm 1,0\}^{E}:~t|_{E-E(t)}\in L^{E(t)}\}

\displaystyle=\{t\in\{\pm 1,0\}^{E}:~t|_{E-E(t)}\notin(L^{*})_{E(t)}\},

where
{L}^{*}=\{\pm 1\}^{E}-L
and
E(t)=\{x\in E:~-1<t(x)<+1\}=t^{-1}(\{0\})
for
t\in\{\pm 1,0\}
were defined previously.

The set
\Circ(L)
of
circuits
of
{L}
is
defined to be the set
\Cocirc(L^{*})
of cocircuits of
{L}^{*}
.
Clearly,
t\in\{\pm 1,0\}^{E}
is contained in
\Cocirc(L^{*})

if and only if
t|_{E-E(t)}\notin{L}_{E(t)}
holds, that is, if and only
if, for every
s\in L
, there exists some
x\in E
with

t(x)\cdot s(x)=-1
or – equivalently – if and only if
s\in\{\pm 1\}^{E}
and
t\prec s
implies
s\notin L.
So,
\Circ\,({L})
consists of the minimal elements in
\{\pm 1,0\}^{E}
with that
property. It is also easy to see that
L
coincides with the set of all
sign maps
s\in\{\pm 1\}^{E}
with
t\prec s
for some
t\in\Cocirc({L})

as well as with the set of all sign maps
s\in\{\pm 1\}^{E}
with

t\nsucc s
for all
t\in\Circ(L)
. We then obtain our final result
essentially as a corollary to Theorem
2
:

Theorem 5
.

The following statements are equivalent for a set
L\subseteq\{\pm 1\}^{E}
:

(i)

{L}
is ample;

(ii)

\Baryc({L})
satisfies (SCA);

(iii)

\Cocirc({L})
satisfies (SCA);

(iv)

\Baryc({L}^{*})
satisfies (SCA);

(v)

\Circ({L})
satisfies (SCA).

Furthermore, if
J\subset\{\pm 1,0\}^{E}
satisfies (SCA) and
\Min(J)=J
, then the set
L=\uparrow\hskip-2.84544ptJ\cap\{\pm 1\}^{E}
is ample and
\Cocirc(L)=J
.

Proof.

Given a set
L\subseteq\{\pm 1\}^{E}
, the associated subset
J:=\mbox{Baryc}(L)
is upward closed, i.e.,

\uparrow\hskip-2.84544ptJ=L
, and yields
L
back as
J\cap\{\pm 1\}^{E}
. In particular,
[J]=|L|
holds by definition of the two cubihedra.
Therefore, if
L
is ample, then condition (vi) of Theorem
2
is satisfied. This establishes (i)
\Rightarrow
(ii) (or (iii), respectively).
Trivially,
\uparrow\hskip-2.84544pt\mbox{Cocirc}(L)=L,
whence (ii)
\Longleftrightarrow
(iii) immediately follows from the equivalence of (iii) and (iv) in Theorem
2
. If
L
satisfies (SCA), then
L
is ample by the implication from (ii) to (vi) in Theorem
2
. Summarizing, we have shown that the first three statements
(i),(ii),(iii) are equivalent. Since
\mbox{Circ}(L)=\mbox{Cocirc}(L^{*})
and
L
is ample exactly when its complement
L^{*}
is
(cf.
[
3
, Theorem 2]
), statements (iv) and (v) are also equivalent to (i). If
J\subset\{\pm 1,0\}
satisfies (SCA) and
\Min(J)=J
, then
L=\uparrow\hskip-2.84544ptJ\cap\{\pm 1\}^{E}
is ample and
\Baryc(L)=\uparrow\hskip-2.84544ptJ
by Theorem
2
. Since
\Min(\uparrow\hskip-2.84544ptJ)=\Min(J)
, we also have
\Cocirc(L)=J
.
∎
