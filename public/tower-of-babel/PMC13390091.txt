Selective use of dynamic navigation for anatomically challenging immediate loading of all-on-four implants: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Karen Supply, Simon Corthals, Araceli Diez-Fraile
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeEdentulism compromises oral function and quality of life. The All-on-4 concept offers a fixed alternative to conventional dentures under immediate loading. However, evidence on outcomes in anatomically challenging arches, particularly with dynamic navigation, remains limited. This study reports early clinical and radiographic outcomes, and the feasibility, of immediate-loading all-on-4 in a real-world setting using an anatomy-driven allocation strategy with freehand and navigation workflows.Materials and MethodsThis retrospective cohort included consecutive patients treated between November 2023 and February 2025. Dynamic navigation was selectively applied in unfavorable arches; freehand surgery was used otherwise. Four implants per arch supported screw-retained provisional prostheses delivered within 24-36 hours. Primary outcomes were implant and arch-level survival and marginal bone level (MBL) change. Secondary outcomes included complications and provisional prosthesis survival.ResultsTwenty-two patients (23 arches; 92 implants) were analyzed: 13 arches freehand and 10 navigation. Median follow-up was 5.11 months. Five implants failed (5.43%), all in the maxilla, affecting four arches (one navigation, three freehand). Implant survival was 94.23% (freehand) and 95.00% (navigation). MBL change was negligible, consistent with early remodeling and measurement variability. Biological complications occurred in 8 implants (8.70%), and two provisional fractures were repaired; 22 of 23 prostheses remained functional (95.65%).ConclusionImmediate-loading All-on-4 with selective navigation achieved high short-term implant and prosthesis survival and minimal MBL variation. These findings support the feasibility of navigation in demanding arches in routine practice, warranting confirmation in prospective studies with longer follow-up.Keywords: dental implants, computer-assisted surgery, edentulous jaw, dental prosthesis, implant-supported, alveolar bone loss

Introduction

Total edentulism compromises mastication, esthetics, nutrition, and quality of life (1, 2). Although conventional complete dentures remain widely used, their functional performance and stability are often limited, particularly in patients with unfavorable anatomy (1, 2). Implant-supported prostheses improve retention and patient-reported outcomes compared with conventional dentures (3, 4, 5). Among full-arch protocols, the All-on-4 concept, two anterior axial and two posterior tilted implants supporting an immediately loaded prosthesis, was developed to optimize the use of available bone and avoid grafting procedures in atrophic jaws (6). Predictable outcomes depend on prosthetically driven implant positioning; however, reduced bone volume, sinus pneumatization, or proximity to the mandibular canal may complicate placement and increase biological or mechanical risks (7).

Digital workflows, including dynamic navigation, enhance planning transfer and intraoperative control, and have shown high accuracy compared with freehand placement (8, 9, 10). Yet, evidence on early clinical outcomes in immediate-loading full-arch All-on-4 rehabilitation, particularly reflecting real-world selective use in anatomically demanding arches, remains limited.

This study aimed to report short-term clinical and radiographic outcomes of immediate-loading All-on-4 rehabilitation in a retrospective cohort where dynamic navigation was selectively used in unfavorable anatomy and freehand surgery in more favorable cases. Accordingly, outcomes are described within a real-world, anatomy-driven workflow rather than as a head-to-head comparison between techniques. Because of the retrospective anatomy-driven allocation and the non-comparability of cohorts, analyses were considered exploratory and no confirmatory hypothesis testing was planned.

Materials and Methods

Ethical Statement

This study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was waived by the Local Ethics Committee of General Hospital of Sint-Lucas, Bruges, Belgium (Project AZSTL2025‑014) due to its retrospective design.

Study design and patient selection

This retrospective cohort study followed STROBE guidelines, including patients who underwent immediate-loading full-arch implant rehabilitation in the maxilla and/or mandible using the All-on-4 concept at a single maxillofacial surgery department (General Hospital of Sint Lucas, Bruges, Belgium) between November 2023 and February 2025. Immediate loading was defined as delivery of a screw-retained provisional prosthesis within 24-36 hours after implant placement. Exclusion criteria were clinical follow-up <3 months, delayed loading, or serious adverse events unrelated to implant therapy during follow-up. Group allocation was based on preoperative CBCT assessment. Dynamic navigation (X-Guide system; X-Nav Technologies, Lansdale, PA, USA) was used in cases with unfavorable anatomy (Table 1), while others underwent conventional freehand surgery as a reference cohort (Figure 1). All surgeries were performed by one experienced surgeon (K.S.; >14 years of experience in full‑arch implant rehabilitation). Records were reviewed up to August 1, 2025 to confirm follow-up and outcomes.

Surgical protocol and navigation workflow

All procedures followed a standardized All-on-4 protocol with four implants per arch; two anterior implants placed axially and two posterior implants tilted 30-45°, positioned near the anterior sinus wall (maxilla) or anterior to the mental foramen (mandible). Surgery and immediate loading were identical in both groups, except for dynamic guidance (Figure 2).

Schematic overview of the workflow steps for dynamic navigation and freehand All-on-4 surgery. The surgical and immediate-loading protocol was identical in both groups, except for the navigation-specific planning, tracking, registration/calibration, and real-time guidance steps.

Implants were placed with insertion torque ≥35 Ncm and connected to multi-unit abutments. Systems included Nobel Active, Nobel Parallel CC (Nobel Biocare), and Straumann BLT (Straumann). Surgeries were performed under local anesthesia with oral sedation; impressions were taken post-surgery, and a screw-retained provisional prosthesis was immediately delivered within 24-36 hours. Definitive prostheses were provided at least 6 months postoperatively, after confirming osseointegration.

For navigation cases, planning was performed in DTX Studio Implant using CBCT and intraoral scans. Optical trackers were fixed to the jaw and handpiece, and registration used intraoral landmarks or radiopaque screws in edentulous cases. Osteotomies and implant placement were guided in real time with the X-Guide system after drill calibration.

Provisional prosthesis fabrication

Until July 2024, immediate screw-retained provisionals were milled from pre-polymerized polymethyl methacrylate discs with prefabricated denture teeth and reinforced with an internal cobalt-chromium framework; titanium multi-unit abutments were used. From August 2024 onward, provisionals were 3D-printed from reinforced hybrid resin within the same All-on-4 protocol. Gingival areas were characterized with pink composite, teeth stained, and a protective coating applied after finishing. In both workflows, titanium temporary cylinders were embedded for connection to multi-unit abutments. All provisionals were screw-retained full-arch restorations delivered within 24-36 hours post-surgery. The change in fabrication reflected a time-based protocol update at the fabrication laboratory.

Outcome measures and radiographic assessment

Primary outcomes were implant survival, arch-level survival, and marginal bone level (MBL) change. Implant survival was defined as absence of failure; failures were diagnosed clinically and confirmed at explantation. Arch-level survival was defined as ≥1 implant failure within an arch during follow-up. MBL change was measured per implant by comparing baseline and follow-up digital panoramic radiographs, which were the only imaging modality consistently available at both time points. The baseline image was defined as the earliest image within 1 week postoperatively, and follow-up images were obtained 3 to 9 months postoperatively.Implants that failed before follow-up imaging were excluded from MBL analysis

For MBL, the distance from the implant platform to the most coronal bone-to-implant contact was measured mesially and distally, averaged per implant, and calibrated per image using the actual implant length divided by the radiographic length to correct magnification. Imaging contrast/brightness was adjusted as needed. One calibrated examiner performed all measurements in Sectra IDS7 (v25.2). Measurements were repeated on two occasions with a 2-week wash-out period with prior values not visible, and the mean of the two independent readings was used for analysis. Representative images are shown in Figure 3. Positive values indicate bone loss and negative values bone gain, respectively. Small changes were considered compatible with early remodeling and/or measurement variability. To avoid pseudoreplication, per-arch summaries were the median of implant-level MBL variation within each arch. Secondary outcomes included provisional prosthesis survival (in function at last follow-up irrespective of interim repairs/adjustments) and mechanical/technical complications (e.g., chipping or fracture).

Statistical analysis

No formal sample size calculation was performed due to the retrospective design. Analyses were primarily descriptive, and effect estimates with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported where applicable. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (range), and categorical variables as n (%). Implant survival was summarized using crude proportions and Kaplan-Meier estimates; survival curves were presented descriptively. Implant-level MBL change was calculated as the mean of mesial and distal measurements; two readings were obtained per site. Intra-observer reliability was evaluated using intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) and Bland-Altman analysis. To avoid pseudoreplication, arch-level summaries were calculated as the median implant-level MBL change within each arch. Exploratory between-group differences in arch-level MBL were expressed as Hodges-Lehmann median differences with 95% CI via nonparametric bootstrap. The proportion of arches with ≥1 implant failure was summarized descriptively. Analyses were performed in Python (v3.13.2) using pandas, NumPy, SciPy, lifelines, pingouin, and matplotlib.

Results

Patient and implant characteristics

During the study period, 33 patients (37 arches, 148 implants) undergoing full-arch All-on-4 rehabilitation were screened. After applying predefined exclusions, 22 patients treated under an immediate-loading protocol were included, contributing 23 arches and 92 implants (Figure 1). Allocation by technique was freehand (13 patients, 13 arches, 52 implants) and navigation (9 patients, 10 arches, 40 implants), with one individual contributing two arches within the navigation group. The overall mean age was 67.01 years, slightly higher in the navigation group, and the median clinical follow-up was 5.11 months (Table 2). Sex distribution was predominantly female in the navigation group, while freehand showed a more balanced profile. Comorbidities were modest: hypertension was the most frequent condition, followed by type II diabetes mellitus. Most rehabilitated arches were maxillae (78.26%), with similar distribution across groups. A total of 92 implants were placed, mainly Straumann BLT, followed by Nobel Parallel CC; Nobel Active was used only once. Surfaces were overwhelmingly conventional (sandblasted, large-grit, acid-etched), with very few active-surface implants. Implant dimensions were consistent between groups, with a median length of 12.00 mm and a median diameter of 4.10 mm.

Implant and arch survival, and MBL change

At last follow-up, overall implant survival was 94.23% (49/52) in the freehand group and 95.00% (38/40) in the navigation group. Five of 92 implants failed (5.43%), all located in the maxilla and affecting four arches. Kaplan-Meier analysis at 6 months showed high survival in both workflows, with curves presented descriptively (Table 3; Figure 4). At the arch level, one or more implant failures occurred in 3 of 13 freehand arches (23.08%) and 1 of 10 navigated arches (10.00%) (Figure 5).

Measurement reproducibility for MBL was verified before analysis. Intra-observer agreement improved from ICC(3,1) = 0.45 to ICC(3,2) = 0.62 when averaging independent repeated readings, with negligible systematic bias (mean difference 0.02 mm) and limits of agreement of -0.46 to 0.49 mm. The standard error of measurement was 0.17 mm. Averaged values were therefore used for subsequent analyses. MBL change was minimal across techniques, jaws, and positions, with median values near zero and narrow interquartile ranges (Table 3; Figure 6). The Hodges-Lehmann median difference between workflows (navigation minus freehand) was 0.06 mm (95% CI -0.05 to 0.10); given the ±0.50 mm limits of agreement and the minimal magnitude of observed bone-level changes, this estimate cannot support claims of similarity or difference between workflows (Figure 5).

Biological and mechanical complications, and prosthetic outcomes

Biological complications occurred in 8 of 92 implants (8.70%) across six patients, including peri-implant infection in seven implants (7.61%) and one mobility event (1.09%). Peri-implant infection affected both workflows (freehand: 5 implants; navigation: 2 implants), while the mobility event occurred in the freehand group. All biological events involved maxillary implants. Two patients experienced infections affecting multiple implants: one in the freehand anterior maxilla (one implant salvaged, one explanted) and one in the navigation posterior maxilla (both implants lost). Overall, five implants failed (5.43%), four associated with infection and one due to lack of osseointegration. Three infections were successfully treated, and failed implants were replaced after 2-4 months of healing (Table 3).

At the prosthetic level, two fractures of immediate provisional prostheses were recorded (8.70%), both in freehand cases, in the maxilla and in the context of peri-implant infection. Fractures were repaired and the bridge trimmed so it could be reinserted on three implants, maintaining function. One provisional was removed following the loss of two posterior implants in a smoker treated with navigation; a removable interim prosthesis was used during healing, followed by delivery of a definitive fixed bridge after implant reintegration. At last follow-up, 22 of 23 provisionals remained functional (95.65% survival).

Discussion

Early outcomes of immediate-loading All-on-4 rehabilitation under routine conditions were acceptable, including cases in which dynamic navigation was selectively applied in anatomically demanding arches. Implant survival exceeded 94% across workflows, biological complications were uncommon, and provisional failures were rare. MBL changes were clinically negligible, with values near zero and variation largely attributable to early remodeling and measurement limits. Overall, these results suggest that immediate-loading All-on-4 with selective navigation is feasible in anatomically demanding arches in this small, real-world cohort, while emphasizing the need for longer-term evaluation.

This pattern aligns with established evidence supporting immediate-loading All-on-4 protocols. Long-term series report implant survival >95% and prosthetic success over extended follow-up (6, 11, 12, 13, 14), while systematic reviews describe mean MBL loss of 0.7-1.0 mm at 12 months (14). In this cohort, short-term MBL changes were undetectable beyond the ±0.50 mm limits of agreement observed in reproducibility analysis, which is expected given the brief observation period and reliance on panoramic imaging.

Evidence on dynamic navigation in full-arch immediate-loading settings remains limited. Most studies address positional accuracy in single-tooth or partial cases, where navigation improves accuracy compared with freehand and approaches static guidance (8, 9, 10, 15, 16). Reports in full-arch protocols are scarce; one X-Guide series noted two posterior maxillary implant losses in a smoker, with otherwise stable outcomes over 1-3 years (17). Our data extend this literature by providing real-world observations under selective use. In this cohort, complications were concentrated in the maxilla, with failures mainly affecting posterior sites. Early survival and complication profiles were otherwise broadly similar across workflows. However, these patterns should be interpreted cautiously, given potential confounding by jaw, bone quality, and patient factors that also influenced allocation. This maxillary distribution is anatomically plausible, given reduced cortical thickness, lower trabecular density, and sinus pneumatization, factors that constrain implant positioning and primary stability (18).

Beyond the anatomical distribution of failures, the co-occurrence of peri-implant infection and provisional fracture in two cases is noteworthy; prior studies suggest biological complications may precede technical events such as screw loosening or fracture. Although event counts were low, these findings underscore the need for close monitoring in high-risk maxillary scenarios, particularly when infection develops during the provisional phase.

Patient- and site-related factors likely influenced allocation and outcomes. Among recognized risk factors, smoking remains particularly important for peri-implant infection and implant failure; recent meta-analyses report higher odds and greater MBL changes in smokers (19, 20, 21). In this cohort, the single navigation patient with two implant losses was a smoker, but numbers were too small for interaction analysis.

Limitations include the non-randomized, anatomy-driven allocation, which precludes causal inference and limits any direct comparison between navigation and freehand cases. Follow-up was short, so these data reflect early outcomes only. Radiographic assessment relied on panoramic imaging, which is inherently less accurate than standardized periapical or CBCT views (22, 23), and reproducibility was moderate. Finally, the provisional fabrication protocol changed during the study period, which may have influenced prosthesis-related maintenance events but could not be analyzed separately. Taken together, these limitations mean that the findings should be interpreted in light of the cohort's descriptive nature and the potential influence of unmeasured confounders.

Conclusion

Immediate-loading All-on-4 rehabilitation with anatomy-driven selective use of dynamic navigation showed high early implant and prosthesis survival, with negligible MBL changes beyond measurement variability. These findings suggest the feasibility of selective navigation in anatomically demanding arches; in this series, all complications occurred in the maxilla, with failures mainly in posterior maxillary sites. Prospective studies with longer follow-up and standardized imaging are needed to clarify whether dynamic navigation provides measurable clinical benefit in full-arch immediate-loading protocols.
