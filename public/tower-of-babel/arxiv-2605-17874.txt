Realizing crosscap transpositions as monodromies of singular fibrations
Kenta Hayano

Abstract
. We introduce a new type of singularity for smooth maps from 4 4 -manifolds to surfaces, called an M M -singularity , whose critical locus is a circle contained in a single fiber. We show that the monodromy around an M M -singularity is a crosscap transposition in the mapping class group of a non-orientable surface. We also introduce M M -fibrations , namely smooth maps whose singularities consist only of M M -singularities, and prove that relations among crosscap transpositions give rise to such fibrations on non-orientable 4 4 -manifolds. We then study handle decompositions associated with M M -fibrations and their orientation double coverings. In particular, we describe the attaching circles and framings of the two 2 2 -handles arising from the orientation double cover of an M M -singularity. Using this description, we construct a closed non-orientable 4 4 -manifold which admits an M M -fibration but admits no Lefschetz fibration. We further discuss singularity-theoretic properties of the local model of an M M -singularity, namely its infinite 𝒜 e \mathcal{A}_{e} -codimension and an explicit stable perturbation.



1.
Introduction

It is well known that the mapping class group of an oriented surface is generated by Dehn twists
[
2
,
14
]
.
Moreover, Dehn twists naturally appear as monodromies around Lefschetz singularities; see, e.g.,
[
9
]
.
This correspondence between singular fibers and mapping class groups plays a fundamental role in the study of
4
-manifolds.
Indeed, relations among Dehn twists give rise to Lefschetz fibrations and pencils, and hence to symplectic
4
-manifolds
[
5
]
.
Using this correspondence, various interesting Lefschetz fibrations/pencils and symplectic
4
-manifolds have been constructed and studied via monodromy factorizations in mapping class groups, including those of
[
10
,
1
]
.

On the other hand, the mapping class group of a non-orientable surface is not generated by
Dehn twists alone. It is known that one also needs additional generators such as
Y
-homeomorphisms
or crosscap transpositions
[
13
,
18
]
. Since Lefschetz
singularities realize Dehn twists as local monodromies, it is natural to ask whether these additional
generators can also be realized geometrically as monodromies of singular fibers.
However, realizing these mapping classes as monodromies of isolated singularities appears to be
difficult from a topological viewpoint.
Indeed, a neighborhood of an isolated (singular) point in a manifold is locally orientable, and hence nearby regular fibers inherit local orientations.
In contrast, the additional generators above are supported on one-holed Klein bottles, which are non-orientable.
This suggests that one should consider singularities with non-isolated critical loci in order to realize such mapping classes as monodromies.

In this paper, we introduce a new type of singularity, called an
M
-singularity
, whose
critical locus is an embedded circle contained in a single fiber.

i)

The symbol ”

” comes from the fact that a tubular neighborhood of the critical circle is diffeomorphic to the product of a Möbius band and
\mathbb{C}
; see

We show that the monodromy

).
As in the case of Lefschetz fibrations, this result enables us to construct fibered non-orientable

More precisely, we introduce a class of smooth maps called
M
-fibrations
, namely smooth maps whose singularities consist only of

This provides a new construction method for non-orientable

It is worth noting that, although the mapping class group of a non-orientable surface cannot be generated solely by Dehn twists or solely by Y-homeomorphisms, it was shown in
[

that, for genus at least seven, it is generated by crosscap transpositions alone.
From this viewpoint, realizing crosscap transpositions as monodromies already provides a broad source of non-orientable fibrations arising from mapping class group relations.

The orientation double covering of an

M
-fibrations and their monodromies

In this section, we introduce

\widetilde{M}
-singularities and

We define their monodromies and show that the monodromy around each singular value is a crosscap transposition.
We also explain how to construct

Let

We denote the orbit space of the action

(resp.

The quotient maps

Note that the map

defined by

We define a map

(i.e.,

It is easy to check that
\operatorname{Crit}(F_{m})
and
\operatorname{Critv}(F_{m})
are respectively equal to
\{([0,s]_{m},0)\in M\times\mathbb{C}~|~s\in\mathbb{R}\}\cong S^{1}
and

.

Construction of

-fibrations from relations in mapping class groups

As in the case of Lefschetz fibrations (see, e.g.,

as follows.
From a genus-

-fibration

of crosscap transpositions in

along a loop homotopic to
\partial D^{2}

The factorization

of a monodromy along

In the same manner, we can also obtain a sequence of crosscap transpositions from a genus-
g

M
-fibration

.
We call the corresponding factorization of

of

.
Suppose that a genus-
g

M
-fibration

has sections

The monodromy factorization
\xi_{1}\cdots\xi_{n}=1

\mathcal{M}(N_{g}^{b})

\widetilde{\xi}_{i}
is a crosscap transposition in

is some orientation of
\nu\delta_{i}
, and

(with some orientation) up to sign.
We call this factorization (of

Theorem 3.6
.

For any sequence

It suffices to show the theorem for the case

In the proof of

Since

∎

Corollary 3.8

Let

Remark 3.9
.

In view of the corresponding uniqueness result for Lefschetz fibrations, it is natural to expect that the isomorphism class of an
M
-fibration is determined by its monodromy factorization if its genus is at least three (cf.
[
7
]
).
The author indeed expects that such a uniqueness statement holds.
However, the main purpose of this paper is to introduce
M
-fibrations and to clarify the difference between
M
-fibrations and Lefschetz fibrations, as illustrated by
Theorem

5.2
.
For this purpose, the uniqueness problem is not needed, and we leave it for future work.

Let us only point out one issue which seems to arise in proving such a uniqueness statement.
In the Lefschetz case, one uses the fact that a Dehn twist determines the isotopy class of its underlying simple closed curve (cf.
[
15
]
).
Thus, in order to adapt the same argument to
M
-fibrations, one would first need to understand whether a crosscap transposition determines, in an appropriate sense, the isotopy class of the pair of curves used to define it.

Let

Proposition 4.1
.

The restriction of

to

Since

,

is contained in

.
The following holds under the local coordinates

(4.1)

h\circ f\circ\Phi_{k}^{-1}(r,s,x,y)=-r\cos(\pi s)-x^{2}+y^{2}+1/2.

The gradient of the above function is
(-\cos(\pi s),\pi r\sin(\pi s),-2x,2y)
, and therefore
\nu S_{k}
has a unique critical point

The Hessian matrix of the above function at this critical point is
\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}\begin{smallmatrix}0&\pi\\
\pi&0\end{smallmatrix}&0\\
\hline\cr 0&\begin{smallmatrix}-2&0\\
0&2\end{smallmatrix}\end{array}\right)
, which is non-degenerate and has two negative eigenvalues.
∎

Furthermore, by

2

6.
Problems and future directions

The results of this paper lead to several natural questions concerning

M
-fibrations and singular fibers on non-orientable
4
-manifolds.
We conclude this paper by listing some of them.

As discussed in Remark 3.9, the author expects that a suitable analogue of the
uniqueness theorem for Lefschetz fibrations should hold for
M
-fibrations.

Problem 6.1
.

Is the isomorphism class of an
M
-fibration determined by its monodromy
factorization (if the genus of an
M
-fibration is at least three)?

As explained in
Remark

3.9
, one difficulty is that it is not clear whether a crosscap transposition determines, in an appropriate sense, the isotopy classes of the curves used to define it.

Theorem 5.2 shows that there exist non-orientable
4
-manifolds admitting

M
-fibrations but no Lefschetz fibrations.
It would be interesting to understand to what extent
M
-fibrations and Lefschetz fibrations differ from the viewpoint of existence problems.

Problem 6.2
.

Which non-orientable
4
-manifolds admit
M
-fibrations?
In particular, does there exist a closed non-orientable
4
-manifold which
admits a Lefschetz fibration but does not admit an
M
-fibration?

The mapping class group of a non-orientable surface is generated not only by Dehn twists but also by additional generators such as
Y
-homeomorphisms and crosscap transpositions. The present paper realizes crosscap transpositions as monodromies of singular fibers, while the corresponding problem for
Y
-homeomorphisms remains open.

Problem 6.3
.

Does there exist a singular fiber whose monodromy is a
Y
-homeomorphism?

Appendix A
Singularity-theoretic properties of
M
-singularities

In this appendix, we discuss several singularity-theoretic aspects of the local model

F_{m}:M\times\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
of an
M
-singularity introduced in
Section

3
.
More specifically, we compute the
\mathcal{A}_{e}
-codimension of the map-germ
F_{m}
at an
M
-singularity, and construct an explicit perturbation of
F_{m}
into a stable map.
We use several standard notions and notations from singularity theory, including
\mathcal{E}_{n}^{p}
,
tf
,
\omega f
, and
\mathcal{A}_{e}
-codimension.
For precise definitions, we refer the reader to, for example,
[
19
]
.

\mathcal{A}_{e}
-codimension

We first study the
\mathcal{A}_{e}
-codimension of the map-germ associated with
F_{m}
.
Since the quotient map
\pi_{m}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to M
is a local diffeomorphism, it is enough to consider the map-germ
f:(\mathbb{R}^{4},0)\to(\mathbb{R}^{2},0)
defined by
f(r,s,x,y)=(r\cos s+x^{2}-y^{2},r\sin s+2xy)
, where
z=x+\sqrt{-1}y
.

Proposition A.1
.

The map-germ
f
has infinite
\mathcal{A}_{e}
-codimension.

Proof.

The
\mathcal{E}_{4}
-module
\mathcal{E}_{4}^{2}
admits the following direct sum decomposition.

\mathcal{E}_{4}^{2}\cong\left<(\cos s,\sin s)\right>_{\mathcal{E}_{4}}\oplus\left<(-\sin s,\cos s)\right>_{\mathcal{E}_{4}}\cong\mathcal{E}_{4}\oplus\mathcal{E}_{4}.

Let
\rho:\mathcal{E}_{4}^{2}\to\mathcal{E}_{4}
be the projection to the second component.
By direct calculation, one can check that the following formulas hold:

\rho(tf(e_{1}))=r,\quad\rho(tf(e_{2}))=0,\quad\rho(tf(e_{3}))=2(y\cos s-x\sin s),\quad\rho(tf(e_{4}))=2(x\cos s+y\sin s).

We thus obtain the isomorphism
\mathcal{E}_{4}^{2}/tf(\mathcal{E}_{4}^{4})\cong\mathcal{E}_{4}/\left<r,x,y\right>_{\mathcal{E}_{4}}\cong\mathcal{E}_{1}
.
Since
\rho(f)
is contained in
\left<r,x,y\right>_{\mathcal{E}_{4}}
, we obtain the following isomorphism as
\mathbb{R}
-vector spaces:

\mathcal{E}_{4}^{2}/tf(\mathcal{E}_{4}^{4})\supset T\mathcal{A}_{e}f/tf(\mathcal{E}_{4}^{4})\cong\left<\cos s,\sin s\right>_{\mathbb{R}}\subset\mathcal{E}_{1}.

By the third isomorphism theorem, we obtain:

\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\frac{\mathcal{E}_{4}^{2}}{T\mathcal{A}_{e}f}\right)=\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\frac{\mathcal{E}_{4}^{2}/tf(\mathcal{E}_{4}^{4})}{T\mathcal{A}_{e}f/tf(\mathcal{E}_{4}^{4})}\right)=\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\frac{\mathcal{E}_{1}}{\left<\cos s,\sin s\right>_{\mathbb{R}}}\right)=\infty.\qed

Perturbation into a stable map

We next give a stable perturbation of
F_{m}
.
For
\epsilon>0
, we define
\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}:M\times\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
by
\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}([r,s]_{m},z)=re^{\pi is}+z^{2}+\varepsilon e^{4\pi is}
.
Taking the coordinates
(r,\theta=\pi s,x,y)
(
\theta\in[0,\pi]
) and
\mathbb{C}\cong\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we have

\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}(r,\theta,x,y)=(r\cos\theta+x^{2}-y^{2}+\varepsilon\cos 4\theta,r\sin\theta+2xy+\varepsilon\sin 4\theta).

Proposition A.2
.

For a sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
, the critical point set of
\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}
is a circle consisting only of folds and three cusps.
Moreover, its image has no self-intersections.
Consequently,
\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}
is infinitesimally stable.

In particular, this proposition shows that the critical value set
of the stable perturbation above is topologically equivalent to
the one arising from a wrinkling, which is a stable perturbation of a Lefschetz
singularity introduced in
[
11
]
.

Proof.

We will use Saji’s criteria
[
17
]
for folds and cusps.
In what follows, we adopt the notation used in
[
17
]
.
By direct calculation, we obtain:

\displaystyle d\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}(\partial_{r})=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta),\quad d\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}(\partial_{x})=(2x,2y),\quad d\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}(\partial_{y})=(-2y,2x),

\displaystyle d\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}(\partial_{\theta})=(-r\sin\theta-4\varepsilon\sin 4\theta,\,r\cos\theta+4\varepsilon\cos 4\theta).

Since
d\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}(\partial_{r})\neq 0
, the rank of
d\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}
is at least
1
everywhere.
Furthermore, calculating the determinants, one can check that
\operatorname{Crit}(\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon})
is equal to
\{(r,\theta,0,0)~|~r=-4\varepsilon\cos 3\theta\}
.

We put

\xi_{1}=\partial_{r},\eta_{1}=\partial_{x},\eta_{2}=\partial_{y},\eta_{3}=\partial_{\theta}+4\varepsilon\sin 3\theta\,\partial_{r}
.
It is easy to check that these vector fields are adapted with respect to the map-germ
\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}
at any critical point.
Let
\lambda_{i}=\det\bigl(d\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}(\xi_{1}),d\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}(\eta_{i})\bigr)
for
i=1,2,3
, which are calculated as follows:

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}=

\displaystyle\det\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&2x\\
\sin\theta&2y\end{pmatrix}=2(y\cos\theta-x\sin\theta),

\displaystyle\lambda_{2}=

\displaystyle\det\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-2y\\
\sin\theta&2x\end{pmatrix}=2(x\cos\theta+y\sin\theta),

\displaystyle\lambda_{3}=

\displaystyle\det\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-r\sin\theta-4\varepsilon\sin 4\theta\\
\sin\theta&r\cos\theta+4\varepsilon\cos 4\theta\end{pmatrix}=r+4\varepsilon\cos 3\theta.

The Jacobi matrix of
\Lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3}):\mathbb{R}^{4}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
at a critical point
(r=-4\varepsilon\cos 3\theta,\theta,0,0)
is

\begin{pmatrix}0&0&-2\sin\theta&2\cos\theta\\
0&0&2\cos\theta&2\sin\theta\\
1&-12\varepsilon\sin 3\theta&0&0\end{pmatrix},

whose rank is
3
.
Hence every critical point of
\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}
is non-degenerate in the sense
of
[
17
, Definition 2.3]
.

By the direct calculation, one can show that the following equality holds at a critical point
(-4\varepsilon\cos 3\theta,\theta,0,0)
:

\mathcal{H}:=(\eta_{i}\lambda_{j})_{1\leq i,j\leq 3}=\begin{pmatrix}-2\sin\theta&2\cos\theta&0\\
2\cos\theta&2\sin\theta&0\\
0&0&-8\varepsilon\sin 3\theta\end{pmatrix}.

Hence we obtain
H:=\det\mathcal{H}=32\varepsilon\sin 3\theta
and a critical point
(-4\varepsilon\cos 3\theta,\theta,0,0)
is
2
-singular in the sense of
[
17
]
if and only if
\sin 3\theta=0
.
By
[
17
, Theorem 3.1]
,
(-4\varepsilon\cos 3\theta,\theta,0,0)
is a fold if and only if
\sin 3\theta\neq 0
.

Suppose now that
\sin 3\theta=0
.
Then
H=0
, so the point
p=(-4\varepsilon\cos 3\theta,\theta,0,0)
is
2
-singular.
Since
S(\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon})=\operatorname{Crit}(\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon})=\{(r,\theta,0,0)\mid r=-4\varepsilon\cos 3\theta\}
, we may regard
H|_{S(\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon})}
as a function of
\theta
and we obtain
d(H|_{S(\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon})})_{p}=96\varepsilon\cos 3\theta
.
Since
\sin 3\theta=0
,
\cos 3\theta=\pm 1
and thus every
2
-singular point is
2
-non-degenerate.
Moreover, since
S_{2}(\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon})
is a finite set, any

2
-non-degenerate point cannot be
3
-singular.
By
[
17
, Theorem 3.1]
, every
2
-singular point is a cusp.

As explained above,
\operatorname{Crit}(\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon})
is parameterized by
\theta
, and so is
\operatorname{Critv}(\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon})
.
the image of the critical point
(-4\varepsilon\cos 3\theta,\theta,0,0)
by
\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}
is

\gamma(\theta)=\bigl(-\varepsilon\cos 4\theta-2\varepsilon\cos 2\theta,\,-\varepsilon\sin 4\theta+2\varepsilon\sin 2\theta\bigr)=-\varepsilon e^{4i\theta}-2\varepsilon e^{-2i\theta}.

Suppose
\gamma(\theta_{1})=\gamma(\theta_{2})
for some
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in[0,\pi]
with
\theta_{1}\neq\theta_{2}
.
Putting
w_{i}=e^{2i\theta_{i}}
, we obtain

w_{1}^{2}+2w_{1}^{-1}=w_{2}^{2}+2w_{2}^{-1}\Leftrightarrow(w_{1}-w_{2})\left(w_{1}+w_{2}-\frac{2}{w_{1}w_{2}}\right)=0.

Since
|w_{1}|=|w_{2}|=1
, the second factor vanishes only if

w_{1}=w_{2}
.
Hence the critical value set has no self-intersections.
