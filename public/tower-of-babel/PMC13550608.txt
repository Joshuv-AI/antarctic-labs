CrayStack: a simplified crayfish optimization driven stacking ensemble for prediction of machining quality characteristics under data scarcity
Authors: Ithipri Emonena, Xu Yong, Festus I. Ashiedu, Ikuobase Emovon, Olusegun D. Samuel, Manjunath Patel G. C., Christopher C. Enweremadu, Choon Kit Chan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Intelligent manufacturing demands accurate prediction of machining quality characteristics with conflicting behaviour, which is challenging with limited experimental data. Taguchi L27 experimental data sets were collected with 6 influencing variables (workpiece material: PA66, PA66 + GF30, PA66 + MoS₂, tool approach angle, tool nose radius, cutting speed, feed rate and depth of cut) and 8 machining quality characteristics (surface roughness, cutting force, temperature, amplitude of vibration, tool wear rate, specific cutting energy, material removal rate, and sound pressure level). The total 27 experimental datasets were stratified by material into a 3-fold cross-validation protocol. The present work develops five base learners such as Gaussian Process Regression (GPR), Least Squares Boosting (LSB), Support Vector Regression (SVR), Random Forest (RF) and extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) for predictions of machining quality characteristics. All three metaheuristic algorithms (genetic algorithm (GA), particle swarm optimization (PSO), and crayfish optimization algorithm (COA)) determine identical weights for three best predictive base learners (GPR, SVR, and LSB) for developing Ensemble model. The COA converge to a minimum composite cost with comparatively lesser computation time than GA and PSO. Therefore, CrayStack ensemble model is constructed with a hybrid combination of GPR, SVR, and LSB and COA methods. The COA efficiently optimizes the adaptive fusion weights by assigning a higher weight fraction to GPR and LSB for nonlinear models. CrayStack Ensemble predictions outperforms all individual learners (SVR, GPR, LSB, XGBoost, and RF) with material stratified three-fold cross validation across all eight outputs of training data. CrayStack Ensemble requires a total training cost of 16.13 s (which includes base model training: 94.92% & 15.31 s, COA weight optimization: 1.88% & 0.30 s, and bootstrap confidence interval estimation: 3.20% & 0.52 s) ensuring its practical usefulness. CrayStack Ensemble achieves near-instantaneous inference (0.003 ms/sample; 290,592 samples/s) with a speed of 58.33, 79.33, 4899.33, 5630.67, 5608.3 over GPR, SVR, LSB, RF and XGBoost ensuring practicality suitable for real-time monitoring systems. Wilcoxon single-rank test confirmed that improvements are statistically significant (with a preset confidence level, p < 0.05) for 7 of the 8 responses, validating the practical utility of the developed models. CrayStack Ensemble showed superior prediction performances against nine randomly generated test cases with a mean absolute percent error of 9.8%, followed by GPR, LSB, XGBoost, SVR, and RF of 12.69%, 13.81%, 15.39%, 21.52% and 42.73% considering all responses. The results demonstrated that the intelligent ensemble stack ensures robustness and higher prediction accuracy for limited experimental datasets offering a practical solution for industrial process optimization.

Introduction

The smart and data-driven manufacturing under Industry 4.0 demand accurate predictive models capable of enhancing machining performance with minimal experimental input-output data. Machining performance is affected by multiple quality characteristics (possessing conflicting behavior) such as surface roughness, cutting forces, tool wear, temperature, vibration, and acoustic emissions, which necessitate process optimization.

Optimizing the multiple performance quality characteristics results in increased productivity, cost reduction and better functional products in modern manufacturing systems1–3. However, experimental machining trials (exceeding 30–50) often lead to higher material wastage, machine downtime, and time-intensive trials4–6. Traditional (mechanistic and empirical) machining models use classical cutting theories and tool life equations that offer physical insights but rely on a few assumptions7–9. These assumptions limit the wider deployment of the models due to nonlinearity, material-dependence, and multiple-quality machining conditions.

The machining process modelling has evolved from physics-based formulations to advanced data-driven approaches. Classical mechanistic models, such as Merchant’s orthogonal cutting theory and Taylor’s tool life equation, provide foundational insights into chip formation and tool wear but require extensive calibration and are limited in handling complex materials and cutting conditions7,9. Statistical methods based on experimental datasets are classified mainly into linear methods: Taguchi, Plackett–Burman Design, and full-factorial design, and nonlinear models: central composite design and Box-Behnken Design. These statistical techniques introduce structured experimentation that improves experimental efficiency to provide detailed process insights (based on the determination of significant and insignificant factors)10–13. However, these methods are constrained by material-dependent machining phenomena, low-order interactions, and assume lower-order polynomial relationships that fail to capture strong nonlinearities—even in well-studied processes like electrical discharge turning14. Furthermore, the machining process is affected by multiple performance quality characteristics with conflicting requirements. The statistical experimental approaches model and analyze the relationships one response at a time, thus strong relative dependencies among multiple outputs, if present are unable to be captured. These statistical methods are offline monitoring tools and therefore fail to adjust the process conditions dynamically. Online monitoring of modern manufacturing systems meets present-day industrial requirements (Industry 5.0) that could control the process for better product quality, productivity, predictive maintenance, reduced downtime, sustainability ensuring resource efficiency, customization and so on. Therefore, advanced computing tools that can monitor or adjust the process conditions for better performance in the product are of industrial relevance.

Machine learning approaches (ANN, ANFIS, SVR, RF, GPR, RNN, LSTM, DNN, CNN, SVM, KNN, Naïve Bayes, RL, and LSB) address most of the shortcomings of statistical methods and are therefore are used in machining industries for predictions15–23. Table 1 presents a summary of ML methods applied to different machining applications. Recent advances have led to the widespread deployment of ML models for machining prediction. ANN, SVR, GPR, RF, and boosting (LSB, Extreme Gradient Boosting: XGB) based ensembles have been successfully applied to predict surface roughness, cutting forces, tool wear, and thermal responses across EDM processes18,22, turning16,24–27, grinding20, and milling19 process. Recent works further demonstrated that integrating the experimental (such as design of experiments for data collection and parametric analysis), prediction (machine learning approaches) and optimization (metaheuristic: generic algorithms) methods were found to produce better performance in machined quality25–27.

ANNs are known for their good generalization and require fine-tuning of the network parameters to avoid overfitting, which can result in local solutions28. SVR predictions’ performance was found to be acceptable for small datasets29. GPR enables accurate and consistent prediction of surface roughness through its probabilistic mechanism, allowing the algorithm to iteratively update and reduce errors, enhancing robustness and generalization21,30. RNNs are limited in capturing process insights from long-term sensor signals required to characterize progressive tool wear due to the highly sensitive and noisy machining environment31. These shortcomings necessitate hybridization with LSTM, which increases computational complexity and limits adaptability in an industrial environment32. KNN is highly sensitive to linear data types, affecting the prediction accuracy for parameter combinations maintained at extremely high values33. CNN predictions are sensitive to datasets (large size) and require an additional sequential model (such as LSTM) to capture long-term temporal dependencies in multi-sensor signals34. The hybrid SVR-PSO model performs better in tool wear prediction than DET, HSO and PSO35. The better prediction performance of hybrid models must be balanced with slower computation time36 and lower predictions with limited datasets, making them not practically feasible for online monitoring with slower feedback response. ML algorithms (SVM, RF, and LSB) accurately predict tool wear for influencing parameters (cutting forces, machining parameters and tool geometry) with limited datasets37. Boosting algorithms such as LSB have demonstrated superior predictive accuracy compared to a single ML algorithm by iteratively minimizing residual errors17. Each model has its advantages and limitations and performs better for specific domains (such as classification, prediction, adaptive control, etc.). Despite these advantages, the predictive performance of individual ML models deteriorates when dealing with small datasets under controlled machining environments38. The literature review above confirms that SVM, LSB, and GPR performed better in prediction when trained with limited datasets.

Ensemble learning offers a promising solution by combining multiple base learners or models to explore their strengths and reduce limitations (variance and bias)50. Techniques such as bagging, boosting, and RF have demonstrated improved stability and accuracy in manufacturing applications (tool condition monitoring, surface quality prediction and cutting force estimation)51–53. Among widely deployed ensemble techniques, stacking ensembles use different combinations of base learners (SVR, RF, GPR and so on) integrated with metaheuristic search algorithms to capture nonlinear process dynamics54–56. However, such approaches typically target single-process, single-material, single-response scenarios and rely on fixed-weight fusion or sequential modeling. Traditional stacking methods further rely on fixed or linearized meta-models that often fail to predict multiple responses subjected to small and multi-response datasets57. In Ensemble model, combining diverse base predictive learners require assigning weights to each model and tune hyper parameters. The linear stacking learns weights through least-squares optimization which require tuning hyper parameters and assume linearity constraints58. The simple averaging method employ assigning equal weights to base learners may not always produce better predictions compared to the weighted averaging method59–61. The major limitations of weighted methods (simple and weighted averaging methods) consider tuning of hyper parameters and weighting of base learners as two different or independent steps rather than an integrated process. These traditional weighting methods failed to capture the interdependence between model configurations and contribution weights resulting in poor generalization prediction performance and reduced adaptability to complex data distributions61. The above literature confirms the need for adaptive ensemble weight-driven techniques that are capable of performing accurate predictions even for small datasets.

Metaheuristic algorithms (genetic algorithm, particle swarm optimization and teaching learning-based optimization) were applied to predict optimized machining conditions62,63. Recent studies integrating stacking ensembles with GA and PSO optimizers demonstrated improved predictive accuracy in the machining process64. However, these approaches often suffer from premature convergence, sensitivity to hyperparameter tuning, and increased computational overhead, particularly when dealing with ultra-small datasets.

These algorithms conduct optimal search at many spatial locations simultaneously capable of finding global solutions, but their potential has rarely been applied to ensemble weight optimization. Their computational complexity also limits practical deployment when optimization dimensionality is low. Jia et al.65 proposed the Crayfish Optimization Algorithm (COA) which simulates the social foraging and competitive behaviour of crayfish to achieve a balance between exploration and exploitation. COA has demonstrated strong convergence characteristics in numerical and engineering optimization problems. Many researchers have introduced enhanced variants of COA to improve global search capability and robustness66–68. Compared with conventional optimizers such as GA and PSO, COA offers improved population diversity, faster convergence, and enhanced search stability due to its adaptive foraging strategy and stochastic perturbation mechanisms. These attributes make COA particularly suitable for low-dimensional optimization problems such as ensemble weight tuning. Despite these advances, COA has not yet been applied to ensemble learning in machining, nor has it been tailored for low-dimensional regression problems. The original COA incorporates multiple behavioral phases and adaptive mechanisms that may introduce unnecessary computational overhead in low-dimensional optimization problems, such as three-model stacking ensembles. To address this, the present study proposes a Simplified Crayfish Optimization Algorithm (sCOA), in which redundant components particularly the summer resort (long-range random walk) phase, are removed while preserving the core exploration–exploitation balance, thereby enhancing convergence speed, computational efficiency, and optimization stability.

In machining multiple quality characteristics affect product quality, productivity, and cost. Each quality characteristic is reliant on the others. For example, SR of machined parts is influenced by factors such as tool wear (which deteriorates tool life), cutting temperature (increasing the thermal load), cutting forces (mechanical load), and tool vibrations (affecting dimensional accuracy of the work piece)48. Similarly, TWR is governed by cutting forces and machining parameters that affect energy consumption (energy efficiency) and MRR (productivity)37. Existing data-driven approaches such as single ML models, often struggle to capture complex interactions and nonlinear relationships among outputs with limited datasets, leading to poor interpretability and high monitoring costs68,69. This limitation hinders the use of ML models for onsite monitoring in machining industries. The significant gaps identified from the literature review are: (a) Lack of ensemble learning strategies explicitly designed for ultra-small machining datasets. (b) Limited use of metaheuristic optimization algorithms (CAO, GA, PSO) for adaptive ensemble weighting in multi-response regression. (c) Insufficient validation frameworks (random experimental cases, bootstrap confidence interval analysis, computational cost analysis) addressing material-dependent generalization under small-sample constraints. (d) Absence of COA-driven stacking ensemble models tailored for machining applications and low-dimensional optimization problems.

The present work proposes a novel stacking ensemble framework that integrates base learners such as GPR, LSB, XGB, RF and SVR with adaptive weights optimized using a simplified COA, GA and PSO (Fig. 1). Three composite samples (PA66 + PA66 + MoS2, PA66 + GF30) possessing different properties were subjected to machining. Taguchi L27 experimental trials composed of different combinations of influencing variables (workpiece material, cutting speed, feed rate, depth of cut, tool nose radius, and tool approach angle) on machining performances (surface roughness, cutting force, tool wear rate, temperature, vibration, specific cutting energy, sound pressure level, and material removal rate). These datasets were used to develop GPR, LSB, RF, XGBoost and SVR models for the machining process. The model prediction performances are finally examined with material-driven cross-validation using performance metrics (R2, MAE, MAPE, and RMSE). Metaheuristic algorithms (GA, PSO, COA) were used to determine optimal weights for the best performing three base learners across each response. The algorithm performances were evaluated to minimize the composite cost (comprising R2, RMSE and MAE with an importance assigned to 50%, 25% and 25%) and computational time across each response. An ensemble stacking model was developed comprising three best learners and their contribution (adaptive optimal weight) determined through the best performing metaheuristic algorithm. The ensemble model predictions were compared with base learners (GPR, LSB, RF, XGBoost and SVR) by conducting statistical validation analysis through four performance metrics (R2, MAE, RMSE, and MAPE), Wilcoxon Single rank, paired Cohen’s d effect sizes, bootstrap confidence intervals, residual analysis for error distribution and computational cost analysis. Furthermore, the practical utility of trained six models (GPR, LSB, RF, XGBoost and SVR) was tested for material stratified nine random experimental cases (which are material-classified and not used during training) and recommend the best predictive model for industrial application. The performance of the ensemble offers an integrated approach that ensures experimental design, predictive modelling, and validation enabling the investigators to make response specific predictions in machining application with small datasets.

Experimental framework

Material selection, machine setup and tooling configuration

Machining experiments were conducted on three workpiece compositions (PA66, PA66 + GF30, and PA66 + MoS₂) using an AJAX Evolution AJEV-310 CNC lathe in a dry cutting environment. The experimental set-up with three workpiece materials and response measurement devices is shown in Fig. 1. The mechanical and thermal properties of the composite samples are presented in Table 2. The machining performances are affected by material compositions and properties which present significant challenges for establishing a generalized predictive model. The cylindrical work surfaces (diameter: 45 mm, machining length: 400 mm) were machined to a depth of 30 mm for nine intervals separated by 10 mm relief grooves to present thermal interference between cuts. TNMG TM LF9218 carbide insert tool geometry (approach angles: 75°, 93°, and 105°) using ISO-driven standard turning holders with a 16 mm shank were employed. The nose radii of 0.4 mm, 0.8 mm, and 1.2 mm were selected for machining and performance analysis.

Experimental design and factor configuration

The six influencing parameters (covering material characteristics, tool geometry and cutting variables) and their operating three levels that affect the eight machining performances (covering surface integrity, mechanical and thermal behavior, and tool degradation) were presented in Table 3. The Taguchi L27 experimental matrix (exploring the range of variables with resource savings or constraints) was used for experimentation and recording the machining performances. The selected design enables realistic industry trial limits for polymer-based composites that ensures sufficient samples for data-driven modelling.

The data sets from an experimental matrix cover a balanced representation of all three materials (PA66, PA66 + GF30, PA66 + MoS₂), with nine trials conducted per polymer. The equal classification for each material ensures investigators can perform three-fold cross-validation with the ensemble stacking framework (Sect. 3). Each fold contains an equal number of samples for each material class, limiting data outflow and enabling realistic quality assessment for model generalization across materials with limited data samples.

Measurement strategy and instrumentation

The accuracy of the model’s predictions depends on the quality and distribution of datasets. To ensure quality, the machining outcomes were recorded (three times for each experimental run) using calibrated sensors and measurement systems that ensure higher accuracy and repeatability under dry turning conditions. Response measurements were taken in a steady-state cutting environment, neglecting transient effects. The response measurements were either directly taken or computed from consistent physical relationships in machining. Multi-response output definition and physical significance

In the present work eight machining responses were investigated including SR, MRR, SCE, TWR, SPL, Fc, vibration amplitude and cutting temperature. The responses (such as Fc and Ra) were obtained directly from the instruments while the remaining outcomes were computed from machining relationships using experimental data. The collection of such derived datasets is commonly practiced in the machining environment, as it maintains the physical insights that facilitate comparison between materials and support integration within the multi-response prediction using independent models.

Tool wear rate

Tool Wear Rate (TWR) provides detailed insights into the rate at which the tool material is lost per unit machining time (g/min), allowing investigator to gather continuous information about tool degradation based on physics. The predictive nature of tool life estimation and the adaptive process control mechanism determine the remaining tool life for machining. Surface roughness (Rₐ)

Surface roughness, as measured by Ra, is a key factor in determining the average surface profile deviation that impacts surface integrity. It is influenced by various factors such as feed rate, tool-workpiece interaction, vibration, and progressive tool wear. The contact type stylus profilometer, with a range of 0.1–800 μm and a resolution of ± 0.01 μm, allows for the measurement of micro-scale surface irregularities on machined surfaces. The evaluation and optimization of surface roughness play a crucial role in determining the functional performance of machined parts, affecting properties such as wear resistance, fatigue life, friction characteristics and dimensional accuracy.

Cutting force

Cutting force is a measure of machinability and process stability that directly impacts the tool-workpiece interaction, chip formation, and load transfer to the machine tool. Optimal cutting force minimizes power consumption, tool wear, chatter and vibration. Cutting force was measured using a DY920B piezoelectric dynamometer (0–1000 N, ± 0.5% FS). Prior to experimentation, the dynamometer was calibrated and force data were continuously acquired during the steady-state condition for each parametric condition. The average values of three cutting force data correspond to each machining condition were taken for analysis and optimization.

Vibration amplitude

Vibration during machining captures the dynamic response of the machining system ensuring machine stability. Excessive machine vibration degrades machined surface quality, accelerates tool wear and induces chatter. Vibrations during machining were recorded using a BENETECH GM63B vibrometer (0.1–199.9 m/s², ± 2%). Measurements were taken under steady-state conditions to limit transient effects at tool entry and exit. The vibration amplitude corresponding to each trial was recorded and correlated with machining parameters and surface quality indicators.

Cutting temperature

Cutting temperature determines the thermomechanical behaviour of machining, directly impacting tool wear, chip formation and surface integrity particularly under dry cutting environments. Accurate temperature estimation is essential to monitor thermal loads, tool wear, and process reliability. The present work estimates temperature (measured from a fixed distance and emissivity setting for repeatability) using an ANENG TH20 infrared thermometer (–50 to 400 °C, ± 1.5 °C).

Sound pressure level

SPL determines machining dynamics by capturing the acoustic emissions generated by tool-workpiece interaction, chip breakage, and structural vibrations. SPL measurements were conducted using a FLUS MT-911 A sound level meter (35–135 dB, ± 0.7 dB). Measurements were taken under steady-state machining conditions with a consistent microphone orientation positioned at a fixed distance from the cutting zone to limit air flow and machine noise vibrations.

Ensemble model architecture

Model framework overview

The hybrid ensemble stacking technique is designed for small datasets (L27 machining experiments presented in Table 5) for performing predictions. The proposed framework applies independent stacking ensembles for each of the eight responses. In contrast to joint multi-output learning frameworks, this formulation enables the metaheuristic algorithms (COA, GA, and PSO) to perform response-specific weight optimization, thereby allowing different base learners (XGBoost, GPR, SVR, LSB and RF) to capture distinct physical phenomena in response behavior. For example, LSB is more effective for nonlinear behaviour of responses, while GPR better captures responses that possess smooth probabilistic nature. Moreover, response-specific adaptive weighting ensures practical alternative to multi-output learning models that enables flexibility and interpretability of ensemble behavior across nonlinear machining responses. Therefore, ensemble stacking comprises three best predictive base learner models (among five: GPR, LSB, SVR, XGBoost, and RF) with adaptive weights determined using best performing metaheuristic algorithm (among GA, PSO, COA). Three-fold cross-validation was designed accounting for three materials (each material with nine experimental trials together constituting 27 datasets) to enable the investigator to balance training and validation while reducing variance and preventing overfitting. Figure 2 summarizes the five-stage ensemble stacking workflow that traces the process starting from data-preprocessing to final performance examination, ensuring its systematic structure and practical relevance in industrial machining contexts.

Simplified crayfish optimization algorithm (COA)

The original COA consists of three phases: the summer resort stage, competition stage, and foraging stage. To enhance computational efficiency and reproducibility, the original COA was systematically simplified into a variant (simplified COA) tailored for low-dimensional ensemble weight optimization. Redundant behavioral components, particularly the summer resort phase (long-range random walk), were removed due to their limited usage in small search spaces and their contribution to unnecessary computational overhead. The competition phase was retained but reformulated using a lightweight differential mutation strategy, enabling efficient exploration while accelerating convergence. In addition, the original multi-parameter adaptive control mechanism was reduced to a single linear decay factor, providing a transparent and stable exploration–exploitation balance with minimal tuning requirements. Boundary handling was further redefined using simplex projection to strictly enforce feasibility constraints (∑wₘ = 1, wₘ ≥ 0), thereby eliminating invalid candidate solutions. Adaptive weight distribution and response-specific behaviour in Ensemble framework

The ensemble framework integrates the three best prediction learners (GPR, LSB, SVR) and adaptive weights derived through 27 experimental datasets using metaheuristic algorithms such as COA, GA, and PSO. The main goal of metaheuristic algorithms is to determine the optimal contribution of the best performing three individual models (GPR, LSB, and SVR) that maximizes predictive accuracy with limited experimental datasets.

The performances of metaheuristic algorithms were compared by computing the composite cost and solution accuracy. The mathematical formulation of composite cost function to simultaneously minimize prediction error and maximize goodness-of-fit is computed using Eq. 17. The composite cost function is used as an objective function by metaheuristic optimizers to determine best ensemble weights. The metaheuristic algorithms determine the weights [W = WGPR, WLSB, WSVR] by minimizing the composite cost objective function based on out-of-fold predictions obtained via 3-fold cross-validation. All three algorithms conduct a population-based stochastic search mechanism that determines weights within the space [0, 1]3. At each cycle, the candidate weight vectors associated with base learners (GPR, LSB, SVR) are randomly initialized and evaluated for composite cost (treated as fitness). The candidate solution corresponding to the composite cost is treated as the best solution, and the resulting weights are normalized to satisfy the unity constraint. The metaheuristic algorithm iteratively updates and refines the search mechanism (neglecting weak learners and promoting dominant models), thereby enabling output specific and data-driven ensemble weights.

GPR excels in smooth learning and continuous functional relationships (tool–material interactions) with limited data and therefore such relationships were found to receive dominant weights derived for Ra, Fc, TWR, and Vibration. This explains its dominant contribution in this case. Zero or near-zero weights assigned to certain learners are not indicative of model weakness but reflect their limited contribution to specific response structures. This represents an implicit model selection mechanism within COA, which suppresses redundant learners to enhance predictive accuracy and generalization. Overall, the non-uniform distribution of weights (including zero, unity, and hybrid combinations) demonstrates that the proposed framework performs both optimal weighting and adaptive model selection, guided by the physical and statistical nature of each machining output. Therefore, the ensemble framework was established by integrating the best performing base learners (GPR, LSB, and SVR) and the metaheuristic algorithm (COA) referred to as the CrayStack ensemble.

Response-wise ablation analysis of optimized weights

The ensemble stacking demonstrated that multiple base learners receives non-zero weights and balanced weights for responses (such as Fc, TWR, Vib, SPL, and MRR) reflecting complementary learning insights. The contribution of multiple models to the ensemble stacking framework enables the metaheuristic algorithm to understand the non-linear behaviour and achieve a better bias-variance trade-off resulting in enhanced generalization and prediction stability. It is important to note that all three base learners contribute to ensure the effectiveness of the ensemble stacking framework across responses such as SPL and MRR. The COA algorithms assign full weight (1.0) to LSB for SCE and temperature, depicting the ensemble model transforming into a single model (LSB). Finally, response-wise ablation analysis confirmed the multi-model dominance (Fc, Vib, SPL, and MRR), partial dominance (Ra, TWR) and single model dominance (SCE and Temperature), depicting the benefits of the ensemble framework being response-driven, and governed by the complexity of response behavioural patterns (interaction, and non-linearity) and diversity among base learners.

Statistical validation of craystack ensemble model

The base learner models (GRP, LSB and SVR) with the CrayStack ensemble model were evaluated on the identical combination of experimental data (same 3-fold with test points) ensuring an accurate comparison of prediction errors. Therefore, the CrayStack ensemble was assessed using pairwise bootstrap (1,000 resamples) to compute 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for R², RMSE, MAE, and MAPE (refer to Table 10). The present work examined the Wilcoxon signed-rank tests to validate the CrayStack ensemble model with individual models (GRP, LSB, and SVR) using absolute prediction errors without normality assumptions. (ML models rarely follow a normal distribution particularly for small datasets and nonlinear behaviour); ensuring robustness and realistic comparison. Effect sizes for significant Wilcoxon results were quantified using paired Cohen’s d, with |d| > 0.5 (medium) and ≥ 0.8 (large) indicating practically meaningful improvements beyond statistical significance.

The Holm–Bonferroni method (to update p-values) and Benjamini–Hochberg correction were used as a secondary validation approach to monitor the multiple comparisons across 24 Wilcoxon tests (8 responses × 3 pairwise comparisons). The SVR vs. Ensemble showed statistically significant after Holm–Bonferroni correction, whereas the GPR and LSB comparison became insignificant after correction confirming the ensemble predictions improvement over SVR. The Wilcoxon tests ensure pairwise comparison of CrayStack ensemble with individual outputs, producing p value tables presented in Table 10. The statistical significance was evaluated with a pre-set confidence level of 95% (p-value < 0.05). The Wilcoxon analysis confirmed that CrayStack ensemble consistently outperformed SVR for all 8 machining output predictions (confirmed p < 0.001), with large effect sizes (|d| = 1.11–2.18). Comparisons (SVR vs. Ensemble with p < 0.001) showed significance after Holm–Bonferroni correction. The results clearly depict that the ensemble consistently mitigates SVR’s limitations in capturing nonlinear behaviour and dependent interrelationships between outputs.

Comparing GRP and LSB, most outputs (excluding SCE and Temp for GRP, and MRR for LSB) exhibit insignificance (as their p-values > 0.05) depicting that CrayStack ensemble preserves the predictive strength of the best predicted model (from GRP, LSB) to ensure more stability. It is noteworthy that the CrayStack ensemble ensures specific improvements that are statistically validated for SCE versus GRP (p = 0.001, d = 0.58, medium) and for MRR versus LSB (p = 0.037, d = -0.23, small but directionally favorable), where there exists a strong parameter relationship and response variability. One-way ANOVA results confirmed that all models (GRP, SVR, and LSB) exhibit statistically comparable prediction performances (p > 0.85). Note that better prediction by the CrayStack ensemble is attributed to the derived adaptive ensemble weight rather than the individual dominant model.

Superior predictive performance by the CrayStack ensemble is attributed to the hybrid framework combining base learners and COA. The CrayStack ensemble combines the strengths of LSB (effective in capturing nonlinear interactions)75, GPR (enabling probabilistic prediction and smooth function approximation)76,77, and the adaptive weighting scheme of COA (assigning weights to individual base learners based on predictive capability) scheme of COA that minimizes overall prediction errors. Furthermore, the CrayStack ensemble simultaneously minimizes the model-specific bias and variance resulting in improved prediction of eight machining quality characteristics. The CrayStack Ensemble predicts with an overall MAPE of 9.8% considering all responses depicting better generalizability despite the model trained with limited experimental datasets. Collecting large experimental machining datasets is often impractical due to material waste, time consumption, and high cost. Therefore, CrayStack Ensemble achieving MAPE less than 10% considering all responses confirm the model practical usefulness for informed decision making through predictions with reduced experimental dependency and thereby lower the overall machining cost.

Conclusion, implications and recommendations

The present study developed base learners (GPR, LSB, SVR, XGBoost, and RF) and adaptive weight optimization for the best performing base learners using metaheuristic algorithms (GA, PSO, and COA) necessary for establishing an ensemble model to predict multiple machining quality characteristics using limited datasets (27 samples). The following conclusions are drawn from the study :

The Taguchi L27 method was used to collect experimental data consisting of 6 influencing and 8 machining quality characteristics. The 27 experimental datasets were material stratified into a 3-fold cross-validation protocol for training the developed ensemble and base learner models. The boosting techniques (LSB and XGB) showed similar performance metrics (R2, RMSE, MAE, and MAPE) and outperformed RF prediction. Three best performing base learners (GPR, LSB, and SVR) were selected to develop the ensemble predictive model.Metaheuristic algorithms (COA, GA and PSO) were utilized to determine adaptive weights based on the contributions of the best performing base learner models (GPR, LSB, and SVR), enabling output specific and data-driven ensemble weights. All three algorithms converged to identical composite cost solutions (ensemble weights and performance metrics, demonstrating the robustness of the stacking framework and validating that the solutions are not algorithm dependent. COA exhibited excellent computational efficiency compared to PSO and GA across 8 responses, with an average computation time of 0.096 s, 0.104 s, and 0.126 s, respectively.Statistical validation through Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, paired Cohen’s d effect sizes, and bootstrap 95% confidence intervals confirmed the robustness of the improvements, including significant gains over SVR (p < 0.001, large effect size).The composite-objective optimization strategy, combining R², RMSE, and MAE, enabled balanced and reliable evaluation across responses. The optimized ensemble weights revealed response-specific dominance of base learners, reflecting the ability of the CrayStack ensemble to capture distinct nonlinear machining dynamics.Residual analysis and material-classified 3-fold cross-validation confirmed the strong generalization of CrayStack ensemble without overfitting.Computational cost analysis showed near-instantaneous inference latency) and negligible optimization overhead, demonstrating efficient real-time applicability under data-scarce conditions.CrayStack ensemble demonstrated superior prediction performances against nine randomly generated test cases, with a MAPE of 9.8%, showcasing enhanced prediction accuracy trained with limited datasets that minimizes experimental costs and favor process optimization.The results demonstrated that the intelligent ensemble stack ensures robustness and higher prediction accuracy for limited experimental datasets offering a practical solution for industrial process optimization.Future research should focus on validating the proposed framework using larger and more diverse datasets that further improve generalization capability. Extending research work to multi-output model or multi-task learning frameworks could better capture interdependencies among machining responses. Incorporating additional and more diverse base learners that further enhance predictive performance. Moreover, the integration of advanced adaptive optimization strategies and uncertainty quantification techniques could improve model reliability and interpretability. Finally, deployment in real industrial environments and smart manufacturing systems is recommended to assess practical applicability and real-time performance. –.Future research should focus on validating the proposed framework using larger and more diverse datasets, extending research to multi-output models or multi-task learning frameworks, incorporating additional diverse base learners, and integrating advanced adaptive optimization strategies and uncertainty quantification techniques. Deployment in real industrial environments and smart manufacturing systems is recommended to assess practical applicability and real-time performance.
