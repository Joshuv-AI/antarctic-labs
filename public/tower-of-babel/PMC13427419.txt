Protocol for studying Chemerin 15-enhanced microglial phagocytosis in cerebral ischemia-reperfusion injury via the ChemR23/p38 MAPK pathway
Authors: Changhao Mao, Jun Li, Yixuan Zhou, Yan Huang, Shuang Li, Lan Wen, Gang Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryChemerin 15 (C15), a chemerin-derived peptide, enhances microglial phagocytosis through the ChemR23/p38 MAPK pathway to alleviate brain damage after cerebral ischemia-reperfusion (I/R) injury. Here, we present a protocol for studying C15-enhanced microglial phagocytosis in cerebral I/R injury via the ChemR23/p38 MAPK pathway. We describe steps for establishing in vivo and in vitro models, detecting microglial phagocytic activity, verifying the underlying signaling pathway, and evaluating therapeutic effects. We detail procedures for model construction, functional detection, molecular mechanism verification, and outcome assessment.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Yang et al.1

Before you begin

Cerebral ischemic stroke leads to massive neuronal death, releasing damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) and reactive oxygen species (ROS) that exacerbate inflammation and neurological deficits.2,3,4 Microglia, the resident phagocytes of the central nervous system (CNS), clear dead neurons and debris to maintain CNS homeostasis.5,6 Chemerin 15 (C15), a bioactive peptide targeting the G protein-coupled receptor ChemR23, has been shown to enhance macrophage phagocytosis in peripheral tissues.7,8,9 During the process of brain I/R injury, we experimentally investigated its role in microglial phagocytosis.1

This protocol focuses on three core objectives:

Establishing reliable in vivo (mouse transient middle cerebral artery occlusion, tMCAO) and in vitro (BV2 microglia OGD/R) models of cerebral I/R injury.

Quantifying microglial phagocytosis using immunofluorescence (IBA1/CD68 co-staining) and pHrodo Red Zymosan bioparticle assays.

The experimental subjects were intervened with receptor inhibitors (α-NETA) and pathway inhibitors (SB203580), and the ChemR23/p38 MAPK pathway was verified by Western blotting and qPCR experiments.

Innovation

Existing protocols about microglial phagocytosis after cerebral ischemia are often constrained by unclear mechanisms, single-model design, rough detection methods, inadequate verification, and incomplete evaluation dimensions. Through key innovations including matched in vivo and in vitro models, precise quantification of phagocytic function, dual blockade verification of the ChemR23/p38 MAPK pathway, and long-term neurological function assessment, this protocol establishes a standardized experimental system.

One key advancement of this protocol is that it performs behavioral evaluation and pathological analysis of brain tissue in mice after ischemic cerebral infarction for up to 28 days. Compared with observations limited to the acute and subacute phases (1–3 days), this long-term monitoring can more robustly demonstrate the long-term effects of Chemerin 15 on ischemic cerebral infarction.

Finally, this protocol is designed to clarify how Chemerin 15 enhances microglial phagocytosis and protects against cerebral ischemia-reperfusion injury through the ChemR23/p38 MAPK pathway, with greater translational value for clinical application.

2.tMCAO surgery:a.Ligate the ECA with silk suture at its first bifurcation.b.Clamp the ICA temporarily with a micro heamostatic clip.c.Ligate the CCA with silk suture 5 mm from its bifurcation into the ICA and ECA.d.Make a small incision with micro scissor on the CCA 2-4 mm from its bifurcation.e.Insert a suture filament into the ICA and remove the microvascular clip.f.Advance the suture filament from the bifurcation of the CCA to the origin of the middle cerebral artery (MCA).g.Ligate the ICA to fix the intraluminal suture filament.CRITICAL: A label on the suture filament is useful to indicate the depth of insertion. Or the suture filament can be advanced until it meets resistance. The typical insertion depth in mice is 18±2 mm. Insufficient insertion depth or too much resistance during the insertion of suture filament indicates that the filament has entered an incorrect vessel (CCA or pterygopalatine artery), inserted between the vascular membrane and the vessel wall, or penetrated the vessel lumen, which may lead to subarachnoid hemorrhage. Figure 1B, 1C and 1D shows the position and the insertion process of the suture filament.Note: Microscope can be used to achieve more accurate and straightforward operations. Skilled operators can also perform CCA incision and filament insertion with the naked eye.h.Suture the neck skin and disinfect the incision.Note: Postoperative care includes heated cage, meloxicam analgesia, saline hydration, soft food, 72 h monitoring, and humane endpoint (weight loss>20%).i.Maintain occlusion for 60 min, then withdraw the suture filament to restore blood flow (reperfusion).CRITICAL: The suture filament should be pulled out gently. Excessive traction may rupture the carotid artery and cause mouse death.Note: According to our experience, novices require 20–30 training animals to achieve stable infarcts. Experienced operators have a success rate about 80% while novices achieve 40%–50% initially.3.Exclude mice with unsuccessful modeling:a.Exclude all the mouse with SAH, premature death, or no neurological deficit in 24 h.b.Use mNSS score and Longa score to confirm successful stroke.10,11 Note: An experienced operator can achieve a success rate about 80%, and the mortality rate is typically 5%–10%. Subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) caused by intraluminal suture insertion easily leads to death in mice. 4.Divide mice into 4 groups (n=6–8 per group):a.Sham group: No occlusion; treated with PBS (5 μL, intranasal).b.I/R group: tMCAO + PBS (5 μL, intranasal) at 1 h after reperfusion.c.I/R + C15 group: tMCAO + C15 (1 μg/kg in 5 μL PBS, intranasal) at 1 h after reperfusion.d.I/R + C15 + α-NETA group: tMCAO + α-NETA (10 mg/kg, i.p.) 30 min before C15 administration; then C15 (1 μg/kg, intranasal) at 1 h after reperfusion.b. For pathway inhibition experiments, an additional group is included: I/R + C15 + SB203580 (2 mg/kg, i.p.) administered 1 h before C15 treatment.5.Intranasal administration:a.Anesthetize mice with pentobarbital (50 mg/kg, stored at 4°C for up to 1 week, i.p.).Note: Disappearance of the toe pinch reflex indicates adequate anesthetic depth. A heating pad (37°C) is used to maintain body temperature of mice.b.Prewarm drug solution to 20–25 °C.c.Fix mice in an upright vertical position with the head upward and the neck slightly extended.d.Place pipette tip close to the nostril orifice without inserting into the nasal cavity.e.Administer the liquid dropwise at an interval of 30–60 s per drop.Note: It is prohibited to insert the pipette tip or capillary deep into the nasal cavity to avoid nasal mucosal injury. Mice should remain in an upright, head-up position for 1–2 min after administration to prevent liquid outflow. Pressure or rubbing on the nose is not allowed. The conventional single dose per unilateral nasal cavity is 5–10 μL, and the total bilateral dose should not exceed 20 μL per mouse.6.Sample collection:a.Anesthetize mice at 0 h, 6 h, 12 h, 24 h, 3 days, 7 days, 14 days, or 28 days after reperfusion.b.Transcardially perfuse mice with 20–30 mL 0.9% NaCl for pre-perfusion.c.Transcardially perfuse mice with 20–30 mL 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for tissue fixation.Note: The rate of perfusion should be 3–5 mL/min.Note: A successful perfusion is defined as: (1) the liver and spleen turning completely from red to yellowish/whitish. Construction and key steps of the mouse transient middle cerebral artery occlusion (tMCAO) model for cerebral ischemia-reperfusion (I/R) injury

(A) Dissect the subcutaneous tissue and see the CCA accompanies the vagus nerve.

(B) the anatomical relationship between CCA, ECA, ICA, and vagus nerve when suture filament inserted.

(C) Suture filament is inserted into the ICA and pushed to the MCA via CCA.

(D) intraoperative photographs for every key step: (a) Photograph of the surgical setup; (b) Anesthetize mice, shave the neck fur and disinfect the skin; (c) Make a midline incision between the mandible and the sternum (20 mm); (d) Dissect the subcutaneous tissue to expose the carotid sheath; (e) Dissect the CCA; (f) Dissect and ligate the ECA; (g) Dissect the ICA; (h) Expose the CCA, ECA and ICA; (i) Block the ICA with a micro heamostatic clip; (j) Ligate the CCA; (k) Make a small incision with micro scissor on the CCA; (l) insert a suture filament into the ICA; (m) remove the microvascular clip, and advance the suture filament to a proper depth; (n) Ligate the ICA; (o) Suture the neck skin and disinfect the incision.

Expected outcomes

Microglial phagocytosis

In vivo: After I/R, the number of IBA1+CD68+ phagocytic microglia peaks at 24 h and gradually decreases. C15 administration increases IBA1+CD68+ cells and IBA1+CD68+NeuN+ cells, while α-NETA or SB203580 reverses this effect.

In vitro: OGD/R increases BV2 cell phagocytosis of Zymosan bioparticles; C15 further enhances this, and inhibition of ChemR23 or p38 MAPK abolishes the effect.

Pathway activation

C15 reduces membrane-bound ChemR23 (via internalization) and increases p-p38/p38 ratio in BV2 cells and mouse brain tissue. α-NETA or SB203580 downregulates p-p38 expression. qPCR shows C15 upregulates phagocytosis-related genes (Clec7a, H2-DMb2, Prg2) and p38 MAPK pathway genes (Atf2, Map3k5) in BV2 cells.

Therapeutic effects

C15 reduces infarct volume (H&E staining) and increases Nissl bodies (Nissl staining) compared to the I/R group; α-NETA reverses these effects. C15 lowers mNSS scores and corner turn rates, improving neurological function.

C15 decreases ROS production (DHE staining) and HMGB1 levels (Western blotting) in the infarct penumbra.

Limitations

Model limitations: The tMCAO model mimics focal cerebral ischemia but does not fully replicate human stroke complexity (e.g., comorbidities like hypertension). The BV2 cell line may not fully represent primary microglia; future studies should use primary microglia.

Receptor specificity: This protocol uses α-NETA (a competitive ChemR23 inhibitor) but does not include microglia-specific ChemR23 knockout mice. Conditional knockout models would better confirm cell-specific effects.

Translation gap: The therapeutic effect of C15 is demonstrated in mice; further studies in large animals (e.g., monkeys) are needed to validate clinical potential.

Problem 1

Due to factors including cell line heterogeneity, culture conditions, and cell cycle status, BV2 cells may show low phagocytic activity after OGD/R modeling (related to step 10).

Potential solution
