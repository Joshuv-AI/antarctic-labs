Tripterygium wilfordii glycoside tablets ameliorate interstitial pulmonary fibrosis via regulating TGF-β/IL-17 signaling and Th17-mediated inflammatory response
Authors: Liming Chen, Shuping Cheng, Pengcheng Liu, Juan Jiang, Houhui Jiang, Rui Wu, Shujiao Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAs a progressive and fatal interstitial lung disease, the treatment options for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) are currently limited. Tripterygium wilfordii polysaccharide tablets (TWGT) have shown potential in anti-fibrosis, but their specific mechanism in pulmonary fibrosis is still unclear. This research aimed to evaluate the therapeutic potential of TWGT in bleomycin-caused pulmonary fibrosis, as well as to delineate its related molecular pathways.MethodsA rat IPF model was built via intratracheal instillation of bleomycin (5 mg/kg), and rats were divided into sham, IPF, IPF + TWGT (6 mg/kg), IPF + prednisolone acetate (PA) (3 mg/kg), and IPF + TWGT + PA groups. Lung tissues were collected at 7, 14, and 28 days post-modeling for histopathological evaluation, transcriptome sequencing, and immunofluorescence staining. Serum levels of cytokines were measured by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), and Th17 cell proportions were analyzed by flow cytometry.ResultsHistopathological results showed that TWGT significantly alleviated alveolar structural damage, inflammatory cell infiltration, and collagen deposition at 7, 14, and 28 days post-modeling. Transcriptome analysis identified 364 and 2,271 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in IPF vs. sham and IPF + TWGT vs. IPF, which were enriched in signaling pathways of interleukin (IL)-17, tumor necrosis factor (TNF), transforming growth factor β (TGF-β), and myofibril. ELISA demonstrated that TWGT downregulated TNF‑α, IL‑1β, IL‑17 (IL-17A), and TGF‑β, and restored epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) markers. Furthermore, TWGT significantly suppressed Th17 cell expansion, and combination with PA enhanced this inhibitory effect.ConclusionsThese data show an association between TWGT treatment and changes in the TGF-β/IL-17 axis and Th17 responses, suggesting that this pathway may be involved.

Key findings

• Tripterygium wilfordii glycosides has anti-inflammatory and anti-fibrotic properties.

Introduction

Interstitial lung disease (ILD) encompasses a heterogeneous group of disorders characterized primarily by pathological lesions in the lung interstitium as well as alveolar spaces, with a pathological spectrum spanning from early inflammation to irreversible late-stage fibrosis (1,2). As the most prognostically unfavorable subtype of ILD, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) exhibits an extremely complex pathogenesis involving multi-level, multi-target pathophysiological processes.

In the early stages of the disease, various endogenous or exogenous damaging factors (e.g., environmental toxins, autoantibodies, mechanical stress) trigger alveolar epithelial cell injury, initiating an inflammatory cascade. Damaged epithelial cells release damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), recruiting inflammatory cells like macrophages, neutrophils, as well as lymphocytes to infiltrate the alveolar lumen (3,4). With disease progression, activated immune cells, and injured epithelial cells within the inflammatory microenvironment secrete pro-fibrotic factors such as transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), connective tissue growth factor (CTGF), as well as platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF) (5), which promote fibroblast proliferation as well as phenotypic transformation into myofibroblasts. Upon activation, myofibroblasts gather at the injured locus and excessively synthesize and release diverse ECM molecules, like fibronectin, hyaluronic acid, elastin, type I/III collagen, and proteoglycans (6). In the end-stage phase of the disorder, these cumulative pathological changes induce abnormal thickening of alveolar walls, a reduction in the quantity of gas-exchange units, and a marked decrease in the compliance of lung tissue. The alveolar structure is gradually destroyed and replaced by fibrotic tissue, forming a pathological characteristic of “honeycomb lung” changes, irreversible remodeling of lung tissue structure (7), ultimately resulting in progressive respiratory dysfunction, respiratory failure, and even death. Despite the deepening understanding of interstitial pulmonary fibrosis, existing treatment options are still limited. At present, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved mainly include pirfenidone and nintedanib. Although they can slow down the rate of lung function decline, they cannot reverse the already formed fibrosis and have significant adverse reactions (8). Therefore, exploring new treatment strategies, especially drugs with multi-target effects, is of great significance for improving patient prognosis.

Tripterygium wilfordii Hook. f. (TwHF) is known as the “king of TCM”, and its extract, Tripterygium wilfordii glycosides (TWG), is proven to have multiple pharmacological activities. Contemporary pharmacological investigations have shown that TWG possesses prominent anti-inflammatory, immunomodulatory, as well as anti-fibrotic activities. Despite its significant pharmacological activities in anti-inflammation, immunomodulation, as well as anti-fibrosis, specific mechanisms of TWG in treatment of pulmonary fibrosis remain incompletely elucidated. In particular, the effects of TWG on key cellular signaling pathways, inflammation-fibrosis transition mechanisms, and ECM remodeling during pulmonary fibrosis require further investigation.

Therefore, this study was designed to systematically explore the therapeutic potential of Tripterygium wilfordii glycoside tablets (TWGT) for pulmonary fibrosis by employing a bleomycin-induced IPF rat model. Furthermore, we intended to comprehensively explore its underlying mechanisms from multiple dimensions, identify the therapeutic targets and molecular pathways of TWGT in ameliorating pulmonary fibrosis, thereby providing a scientific rationale for the clinical application of TwHF in IPF management. We present this article in accordance with the ARRIVE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-0696/rc).

Methods

Establishment of a rat IPF model induced by bleomycin, and grouping

Fifty male Sprague-Dawley rats [specific-pathogen free (SPF) grade, 180–220 g] were obtained from SLAC Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) and kept under regulated environmental parameters (24±2 ℃ temperature, 50%±5% humidity, and a 12-h light/dark cycle), with unrestricted food and water availability. Following 7 days of acclimation, the animals were randomly assigned to five experimental groups (10 rats/group): sham control, IPF model, IPF + TWGT, IPF + prednisolone acetate (PA), and IPF + TWGT + PA. All animal experiments were performed under a project license (No. CDYFY-IACUC-202505GR004) granted by the Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University, in compliance with institutional guidelines for the care and use of animals.

The establishment of a rat model of IPF was carried out in accordance with previously reported protocols (9). Briefly, all rats were anesthetized with isoflurane in an anesthesia chamber until loss of spontaneous locomotor activity. The trachea was exposed via a midline cervical incision, as well as a catheter was inserted into the tracheal lumen. Subsequently, sterile 26G syringes were used for intratracheal injection [saline for the sham group, and 5 mg/kg bleomycin (Macklin, Shanghai, China) for the other groups] at a slow infusion rate. This low injection speed was specifically designed to mitigate adverse effects including bradycardia, cardiac dysfunction, pulmonary edema, and experimental animal mortality. Immediately following the intratracheal injection, rats were held upright and subjected to gentle rotation to guarantee that bleomycin was evenly distributed in both lung lobes. The surgical incision was then closed with medical sutures to achieve a secure closure. After modeling, the rats in the IPF + TWGT, IPF + PA, and IPF + TWGT + PA groups were administered 6 mg/kg TWGT (cat. no. 1453013, Guizhou Hanfang Yaoye Co. Ltd., Guizhou, China), 3 mg/kg PA (Xianju Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Zhejiang, China), and a combination of 6 mg/kg TWGT and 3 mg/kg PA, respectively, via intragastric gavage once a day for 28 consecutive days. At 24 h, 7 days, 14 days, and 28 days post-modeling, lung tissues were harvested from rats for subsequent histopathological analysis and molecular biological assays. Additionally, we collected serum specimens and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) from the experimental rats to conduct follow-up biochemical measurements and flow cytometric detection.

Pathological histology examination

Lung tissues obtained from the animals were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde over a 24-h period, after which they underwent dehydration and were embedded in paraffin wax. The paraffin-embedded tissues were sliced into 4-µm sections, which were heated at 70 ℃ for 30 min to enhance adherence, then processed through dewaxing and rehydration steps. For hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining analysis, the sections were stained with hematoxylin for 10 minutes and eosin for 90 s; after dehydration, clearing, and coverslipping, microscopic imaging was conducted using an optical microscope (Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). For Masson’s trichrome staining to assess collagen deposition, the sections were treated with potassium dichromate mordant overnight. After washing, the sections were stained with Weigert’s iron hematoxylin for 10 min, rinsed, and differentiated using 1% hydrochloric acid alcohol. They were then stained with Lichun red acid fuchsin for 10 min, washed again, and treated with phosphomolybdic acid for 2 min, followed by aniline blue staining for 60 s. A 10-s treatment with 1% glacial acetic acid and ethanol dehydration preceded mounting with neutral balsam, and the stained sections were examined and imaged under an optical microscope (Olympus Corporation).

Transcriptome sequencing and analysis

The lung tissues in the sham, IPF and IPF + TWGT were sent to Shanghai OE Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) for transcriptome sequencing. Firstly, total RNA was isolated from the tissue samples using the RNAiso Plus kit (Trizol, Takara, Beijing, China) in line with the recommendations of the manufacturer. Then, the purity and quantification of the isolated RNA were assessed by an ultra-micro spectrophotometer (OD260/280 nm), as well as the integrity of the isolated RNA was determined using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer software (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). After library construction with the VAHTS Universal V6 RNA-seq Library Prep kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China), the purified RNA-seq library products were used to perform pair-end sequencing (150 bp, PE150) on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform at OE Biotech Co., Ltd.

The sequencing raw data were submitted to FastQC (V0.11.9) for the assessment of raw data (Phred score ≥ Q30), and then clean reads were obtained by removing low-quality sequences (Q <20) and joint contamination using fastp (v0.23.2). Thereafter, the HISAT2 software (https://ccb.jhu.edu/software/hisat2/index.shtml) was used for mapping, and then the fragments per kilobase million (FPKM) value of each gene was calculated using the HTSeq-count (v0.13.5). Then, the DESeq2 software was applied to identify the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) with the thresholds of fold change (FC) >2 or <0.5 and false discovery rate (FDR) <0.05. These identified DEGs were subjected to Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) analyses. The standard of the significant pathways was set at P<0.05.

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining of lung tissues

The slices of lung tissues were deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated through a descending ethanol series, as well as underwent heat-induced antigen retrieval using citrate buffer solution. Following sealing with the endogenous peroxidase inhibitor (3% H2O2) at 37 ℃ for 30 min, the slices were added with 0.3% Triton X-100, and after washing, the slices were incubated with the primary antibodies at 4 ℃ overnight, followed by fluorescently-labeled secondary antibodies at 37 ℃ in the dark for 60 min. The primary antibodies contained antibodies of anti-interleukin (IL)-17 (1:200, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), anti-IL-1β (1:200, Proteintech), anti-TGF-β (1:500, Proteintech), anti-E-cadherin (1:1,000, Proteintech), and anti-Vimentin (1:1,000, Proteintech). Moreover, the secondary antibodies were fluorescently [594]-labeled goat anti-mouse IgG (Abcam, Cambridge, UK) and fluorescently [488]-labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG (Abcam). After washing, cell nuclei were stained with DAPI for 5 min in the dark. Finally, cell images were observed under a fluorescence microscope (IX70, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan), and then analyzed using ImageJ software.

Enzyme linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The serum samples obtained from different groups were used for the determination of IL-1β, IL-17, tumor necrosis factor α (TNF-α), TGF-β and VEGFA levels using their corresponding ELISA assay kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China) based on the manufacturer’s instructions.

Flow cytometry

The isolated cells were resuspended in an appropriate volume of sterile medium, followed by the addition of stimulation reagents at the indicated final concentrations: phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) (25 ng/mL), ionomycin (1 µg/mL), as well as brefeldin A (10 ng/mL). Then, the cells were incubated at a 37 ℃ with 5% CO2 for 4 h to activate cells and induce intracellular antigen expression. After centrifuged at 2,000 rpm for 5 min, cells were washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to eliminate unbound stimulation reagents. Subsequently, cells were resuspended in 200 µL PBS, and the suspension was aliquoted into flow cytometry tubes. Add an appropriate amount of surface antibody: allophycocyanin (APC) anti-rat CD3 antibody (2 µL/test, BioLegend, San Diego, USA) and phycoerythrin (PE) anti-rat CD4 antibody (1.25 µL/test, BioLegends). Incubate the test tube in the dark at 4 ℃ for 30 min to ensure that the antibody fully binds to the cell surface antigen. After centrifugation at 1,500 rpm for 5 min, add 0.5 mL of fixative/penetrant reagent to each test tube, and then incubate in the dark at 4 ℃ for 50 min to disrupt the cell membrane and allow antibodies to penetrate into the cytoplasm. After incubation, add 1 mL of cleaning reagent, centrifuge at 2,000 rpm for 5 min, and then add 200 µL of PBS to resuspend the cells. Then, intracellular antibodies targeting IL-17A (0.5 µL/Test, Invitrogen, California, USA) were added to each test tube and incubated in the dark at 4 ℃ for 30 min to promote specific binding with intracellular antigens. After centrifugation at 1,500 rpm for 5 min, resuspend the cells in 200 µL PBS and transfer them to a flow cytometry tube. Finally, flow cytometry analysis was performed using a flow cytometer to evaluate the expression of surface and intracellular antigens based on fluorescence signal intensity and distribution characteristics.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted in triplicate with independent repetitions, and data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (IBM Corp., USA), while all graphical illustrations were generated with GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, USA). Prior to formal statistical analysis, a normality test and homogeneity of variance test were conducted on the data to verify compliance with the assumptions of parametric tests. For comparisons between two independent groups, an unpaired t-test was utilized. For multiple comparisons involving more than two groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s test was employed. A two-tailed P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Bleomycin-mediated successful construction of a rat IPF model

The rat IPF model was constructed via receiving a bleomycin solution. At 24 h post-modeling, rats were anesthetized and euthanized, followed by immediate collection of lung tissue samples (Figure 1A). Then, the collected lung tissue samples were submitted for histopathological analyses, including Masson’s trichrome staining and HE staining. Among them, HE staining showed focal alveolar dilation, alveolar septal disruption and fusion, uneven thickening of alveolar septa, dilation of small blood vessels, and mild inflammatory cell infiltration in localized areas of the lungs in the IPF rats (Figure 1A). Furthermore, Masson’s trichrome staining demonstrated increased deposition and disorganized distribution of blue-stained collagen fibers in the lung interstitium of the IPF rats, indicating excessive ECM accumulation (Figure 1A). These pathological characteristics were consistent with the early pathological changes of IPF.

Dynamic pathological changes of pulmonary fibrosis under different treatments

In order to systematically assess the pathological status of alveolitis and pulmonary fibrosis, all mice were subjected to distinct interventions at 24 h post-modeling, as well as euthanized at 7, 14, and 28 days post-modeling, followed by the collection of lung tissue specimens for HE and Masson’s trichrome staining.

At 7 days post-modeling, the rats in the IPF group exhibited typical pathological manifestations of alveolitis and early-stage pulmonary fibrosis compared with the sham group. Specifically, HE staining revealed that the alveolar architecture in the lesion areas of the IPF rats was markedly disrupted, accompanied by focal sheet-like proliferation of interstitial fibrous tissue and inflammatory cell infiltration. Moreover, As evidenced by Masson’s, collagen fibers accumulated in a bundle-shaped or sheet-shaped distribution pattern (Figure 1B). Notably, the pathological alterations in all intervention groups were alleviated relative to the IPF group. For example, rats in the IPF + TWGT, IPF + PA, and IPF + TWGT + PA groups presented with less severe alveolar structural damage, reduced inflammatory cell infiltration, and a smaller area of the collagen deposition (Figure 1B). These findings suggest that all the intervention regimens could effectively ameliorate bleomycin-induced interstitial inflammation, as well as collagen deposition during the early stage of pulmonary fibrosis.

At 14 days post-modeling, the fibrotic process in the IPF group progressed continuously, with the nearly completely collapsed alveolar architecture, significant hyperplasia of interstitial fibrous tissue, increased inflammatory cell infiltration, a further increase in the area of collagen deposition, and distinct reticular structures formed by the collagen bundles (Figure 1C). In contrast, rats in IPF + TWGT, IPF + PA, and IPF + TWGT + PA groups exhibited milder alveolitis and pulmonary fibrosis compared with the IPF group: analysis via HE staining indicated a more pronounced reduction in inflammatory cell infiltration, as well as a certain degree of recovery of alveolar structure in these intervention groups; as well as under Masson’s, collagen fiber hyperplasia was found, but this distribution tended to be regular (Figure 1C). Notably, the combination of TWGT and PA displayed the optimal therapeutic effect, with the most significant reduction in collagen deposition area as well as partial preservation of alveolar structure (Figure 1C). These results indicated that both TWGT and PA exerted a potent inhibitory impact in pathological progression of pulmonary interstitial fibrosis during this active fibrotic phase.

At 28 days post-modeling, the rats in the IPF group progressed to the late stage of pulmonary fibrosis, with more extensive collagen deposition throughout the lung parenchyma; and attenuated inflammatory responses accompanied by prominent fibroblast proliferation (Figure 1D). In contrast, rats in the IPF + TWGT, IPF + PA, and IPF + TWGT + PA groups displayed relatively milder alveolitis and pulmonary fibrosis compared with the IPF rats. HE staining showed partial restoration of alveolar architecture and reduced inflammatory cell infiltration in these intervention groups, as well as the collagen fiber content in these groups was evidently decreased relative to IPF rats (Masson’s), with alveolar structural remodeling observed in localized areas (Figure 1D). Collectively, these findings further verify that TWGT, PA monotherapy, and their combination therapy could exert ameliorative effects against bleomycin-induced alveolitis and pulmonary fibrosis, with the combined regimen yielding the most favorable therapeutic outcomes.

Identification of DEGs in the comparison groups of IPF + TWGT vs. IPF as well as IPF vs. sham

For a more in-depth elucidation of the molecular underpinnings of pulmonary fibrosis initiation and progression, along with the putative therapeutic mechanisms of TWGT in ameliorating this condition, we performed an exhaustive transcriptomic profiling of lung tissues from sham, IPF, and IPF + TWGT-treated rats. In the comparison group of IPF vs. sham, a total of 364 DEGs were screened, including 146 upregulated (Klra17, Myh1, Myh2, LOC100910628, and Trh) and 218 downregulated ones (Plin1, Klkb1, Xist, Nppa, and Mb) (Figure 2A). The heatmap of the identified DEGs between the IPF as well as sham rats showed these DEGs could significantly distinguish the samples in the IPF group from the samples in sham (Figure 2B). In addition, in the comparison group of IPF + TWGT vs. IPF, 2,271 DEGs were identified, composed of 998 upregulated (Cyp1a1, IL17, Mb, Cd300c, and Nrap) and 1,273 downregulated genes (Slc1ab, Wfdc18, Tnni1, R3hdml, and Tsbp1) (Figure 2C). Moreover, the heatmap of these DEGs displayed that these DEGs could evidently differentiate the IPF samples from the IPF + TWGT samples (Figure 2D).

Functional analyses of the identified DEGs

Further, the screened DEGs in the comparison groups of IPF vs. sham as well as IPF + TWGT vs. IPF were subjected to KEGG pathway enrichment analyses. It was found the DEGs between the IPF and sham groups (Figure 3A) were primarily enriched in “cGMP-PKG signaling pathway”, “bile secretion”, “Apelin signaling pathway”, “Sphingolipid signaling pathway”, as well as “PPAR signaling pathway”. Additionally, the identified DEGs between the IPF + TWGT and IPF groups were closely associated with “cell cycle”, “TNF signaling pathway”, “ECM-receptor interaction”, “IL-17 signaling pathway”, “complement and coagulation cascades”, as well as “cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction” (Figure 3B).

In addition, these DEGs were employed for GSEA enrichment analysis, and we observed that myofibril, as well as signaling pathways of TNF, TGF-β, as well as IL-17 were significantly enriched (Figure 3C-3F). Figure 3C displayed the enrichment score of TGF-β signaling pathway in the comparison group of IPF + TWGT vs. IPF was 0.217, and this pathway showed an inhibitory trend in the IPF + TWGT group (NES =−1.01, P=0.42, FDR =0.583). Focusing on pathways related to muscle fibrosis, myofibril was significantly enriched (NES =2.29, P<0.001, FDR <0.001 for IPF + TWGT vs. IPF; as well as NES =−2.0, P=0.002, FDR =0.013 for IPF vs. sham) (Figure 3D), which indicated myofibril plays important roles in pulmonary fibrosis progression. Additionally, TNF signaling pathway for IPF + TWGT vs. IPF was NES =−2.04, P<0.001, FDR =0.0; as well as for IPF vs. sham was NES =−1.64, P<0.001, FDR =0.045 (Figure 3E); IL-17 signaling pathway for IPF + TWGT vs. IPF was NES =−2.28, P<0.001, FDR <0.001, as well as for IPF vs. sham was NES =−1.68, P<0.001, FDR =0.034 (Figure 3F). This confirmed inflammatory response became an essential driving factor in pulmonary fibrosis, thereby involving IPF occurrence and progression.

Effects of TWGT on the expression of related proteins

IF staining found the fluorescence intensity of IL-1β as well as IL-17 in lung tissues was evidently enhanced in the IPF rats, with their expression levels exhibiting a distinct dynamic change trend over time (Figure 4A). In contrast, their fluorescence intensity was reduced in all drug intervention groups, including IPF + TWGT, IPF + PA, and IPF + TWGT + PA groups (Figure 4A). At each of the designated time points (7, 14, and 28 days following model induction), the expression levels of both IL-1β and IL-17 in the sham group stayed continuously low and showed minimal fluctuations. Notably, their expression was markedly elevated in IPF model rats in comparison with sham rats, but were evidently declined in the IPF + TWGT, IPF + PA and IPF + TWGT + PA groups relative to IPF rats (P<0.01, Figure 4A). For TGF-β, relative with the sham rats, the green fluorescence intensity of TGF-β in IPF lung tissues was significantly enhanced at 7 days post-modeling, indicating an elevated expression level of this cytokine (Figure 4B). As the disease progressed, the fluorescent signals of TGF-β in the IPF model group were further strengthened at 14 and 28 days post-modeling (Figure 4B). This dynamic trend of alterations clearly indicated that the TGF-β signaling remained continuously activated as pulmonary fibrosis progressed. Notably, all drug intervention groups exhibited significant therapeutic effects. Quantitative analysis results showed that during the 7-, 14-, and 28-day post-modeling observation period, the expression level of TGF-β in the sham group remained essentially at the baseline level; the TGF-β expression was evidently upregulated in IPF model rats (P<0.05); as well as was obviously downregulated after TWGT, PA and combination treatments (Figure 4B). When examining the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) markers E-cadherin and Vimentin, we observed that with the advancement of interstitial pulmonary fibrosis in the IPF group, E-cadherin expression in rat lung tissues exhibited a progressive downward trend, while Vimentin expression showed a continuous upward trend (Figure 4C,4D). Under fluorescence microscopy, on the 7th day, the green fluorescence intensity of E-cadherin in IPF mode rats was significantly declined in comparison with sham rats; as well as on the 14th and 28th days, the fluorescence signal of E-cadherin was almost unobservable, indicating that the degree of pulmonary fibrosis gradually worsened, normal alveolar tissue continued to decrease, and fibrous tissue continued to increase. There was an inverse correlation between E-cadherin expression and the pathological progression of pulmonary fibrosis. Relative to the IPF model rats, the green fluorescence intensity of E-cadherin was higher in the IPF + TWGT, IPF + PA and IPF + TWGT + PA groups, and the degree of decrease in E-cadherin expression gradually slowed down (Figure 4C). However, the tendency of Vimentin expression in different groups at different time points was opposite to that of E-cadherin expression (Figure 4D). Furthermore, monotherapy (TWGT or PA) had significant inhibitory effects on Vimentin expression, while the efficacy of combination therapy needed further verification.

Effects of TWGT on the serum levels of inflammatory cytokines

In comparison with the sham rats, IL-1β as well as TNF-α levels were remarkedly increased in IPF rats, whereas were evidently reduced after TWGT, PA and combination treatments at day 14 (P<0.05, Figure 5A,5B). Then, we analyzed the IL-17 family genes (IL-17d, IL-17rc, IL-17f, IL-17a) as well as IL-6 in lung tissues from the sham, IPF and IPF + TWGT groups based on the sequencing data. It was found IL-17a as well as IL-6 were evidently elevated (P<0.05) in the IPF model group compared to the other two groups (Figure 5C), indicating IL-17a and IL-6, key components of the IL-17 signaling pathway, may function crucial action in IPF pathogenesis. For the serum level of TGF-β, its serum level was higher in the IPF rats, while was significantly decreased after TWGT treatment at day 7, 14, and 28 relative to the IPF rats (P<0.05, Figure 5D).

Effects of TWGT on the proportion of Th17 cells

The proportion of Th17 cells in rats from each experimental group was assessed via flow cytometry. As presented in Figure 6, at each of the observation time points (7, 14, and 28 days post-model establishment), the percentage of Th17 cells in the IPF model group was significantly higher than that in the sham group (P<0.05). In contrast, the proportion of Th17 cells was decreased to varying degrees in all drug intervention groups relative to the IPF model group, with statistically significant differences (P<0.001, Figure 6). These results indicate that both TWGT and PA could effectively block the expansion of Th17 cells, as well as their combined administration exerted a synergistic effect. This modulatory effect on Th17 cells may act as a critical mechanism that underlies the attenuation of inflammatory responses in pulmonary fibrosis.

Discussion

IPF is an irreversible, progressive ILD with poor outcomes, characterized by aberrant inflammation, excessive ECM deposition, as well as pathological remodeling of lung tissue (10,11). Although pirfenidone and nintedanib have been approved as first-line therapies, emerging clinical evidence indicates that the response to these antifibrotic agents is notably heterogeneous across patients. Factors such as treatment tolerability, dose adjustments due to adverse events, and long-term drug persistence significantly influence real-world outcomes (12,13). A subset of patients experiences limited benefit or disease progression despite standard therapy, highlighting an unmet clinical need for adjunctive or alternative multi-target strategies (14,15). Therefore, our finding that TWGT effectively suppresses the TGF-β/IL-17 axis and Th17-mediated inflammation may offer a complementary approach, particularly for patients who do not fully respond to or cannot tolerate existing antifibrotics.

It is widely believed that bleomycin induced pulmonary fibrosis is a classic model for studying IPF, which can effectively simulate the pathological evolution of human IPF, including early inflammatory infiltration, fibroblast activation, and excessive collagen deposition (16,17). Our histopathological analysis at 24 h after modeling confirmed the successful establishment of IPF model: the IPF rats exhibited focal alveolar dilation, diaphragm rupture, mild inflammatory cell infiltration, and increased collagen deposition, consistent with the pathological features of early IPF (18). As the disease progressed to 14 and 28 days, the IPF group displayed progressive alveolar collapse, extensive collagen fiber deposition (reticular and sheet-like patterns), and prominent fibroblast proliferation, reflecting the transition from acute inflammation to irreversible fibrosis (19). These findings validate the suitability of this model for investigating IPF pathogenesis as well as evaluating therapeutic interventions. Moreover, our dynamic pathological observations (at 7, 14, and 28 days after modeling) demonstrated that TWGT monotherapy, PA monotherapy, and their combination therapy effectively mitigated bleomycin-induced lung injury. It is worth noting that TWGT treatment reduced alveolar structural damage, inflammatory cell infiltration, and collagen deposition at all time points, with the TWGT + PA combination group showing the most significant therapeutic effect. This is consistent with previous reports that TWG exert anti-fibrotic effects in various fibrotic disorders (20). Importantly, the superior efficacy of the combination regimen may be attributed to synergistic effects: PA, a classic glucocorticoid, rapidly suppresses acute inflammation (21), while TWGT may target multiple downstream pro-fibrotic pathways, collectively halting the inflammation-fibrosis transition. Taken together, the time-dependent pathological improvement suggests that TWGT may effectively delay or even partially reverse the progression of pulmonary fibrosis via continuously inhibiting the inflammation fibrosis cascade reaction. In this preclinical study, prednisolone was included primarily as a pharmacological tool for mechanism validation, rather than as a clinically relevant comparator for IPF. It is well recognized that the bleomycin‑induced fibrosis model has a prominent acute inflammatory component. Including a known broad‑spectrum anti‑inflammatory agent such as a corticosteroid helps to validate the inflammatory responsiveness of our model system and to differentiate between anti‑inflammatory and antifibrotic effects. However, it must be clearly stated that corticosteroids are not recommended for the treatment of IPF. Multiple clinical studies and systematic reviews (22) have demonstrated that corticosteroids do not improve survival or delay lung function decline in IPF, and are associated with significant adverse effects. Therefore, the positive effects of prednisolone observed in this model should not be interpreted as supporting its clinical use in IPF. The value of this comparator is confined to drug effect comparison in the present preclinical setting.

To reveal the molecular basis of the anti-fibrotic effect of TWGT, we performed transcriptome sequencing on lung tissues from the sham, IPF, and IPF + TWGT groups. Differentially expressed analysis identified 346 and 2,271 DEGs respectively in the comparison groups of IPF vs. sham and IPF + TWGT vs. IPF, which enriched in signaling pathways of cGMP-PKG, peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor (PPAR), TNF, IL-17, and ECM-receptor interaction. Further GSEA results emphasized that the TGF-β and IL-17 signaling pathways are inhibited by TWGT. The cGMP-PKG signaling pathway functions crucial action in alleviating inflammation caused by acute lung injury and promoting recovery by promoting the conversion of macrophages from their pro-inflammatory M1 state to the anti-inflammatory, tissue-restorative M2 state (23). Zeng et al. (24) observed by activating PPARγ to suppress the AKT and MAPK pathways, asparanin could exert an attenuating effect on bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis. For ECM-receptor interaction, it is found to be closely associated with IPF progression, and its related genes CALD1, CDH2 as well as POSTN are upregulated in IPF lung tissues (25). TGF-β, TNF-α, and PDGF are the main regulatory factors of IPF, which mediate myofibroblast differentiation as well as promote pulmonary fibrosis remodeling (26). Furthermore, the TGF-β signaling pathway is a central driver of pulmonary fibrosis, promoting fibroblast activation, EMT, and ECM synthesis (27,28). The IL-17 signaling pathway, primarily mediated by Th17 cells and pro-inflammatory cytokines (for example, IL-17A, IL-6), amplifies alveolar inflammation and accelerates fibrosis progression (29,30). Combined with our results, it can be inferred that TWGT may exert its therapeutic effects on IPF by concurrently targeting ECM-receptor interaction as well as the signaling pathways of cGMP-PKG, PPAR, TNF, TGF-β, and IL-17. However, the roles of these pathways in pulmonary fibrosis require to be further explored.

Consistent with the transcriptomic data, IF staining and ELISA confirmed that TWGT evidently declined the levels of pro-inflammatory (IL-1β, IL-17A, TNF-α) as well as pro-fibrotic (TGF-β) cytokines in both lung tissues and serum. IL-17A, a key effector cytokine of Th17 cells, had essential actions in IPF by inducing epithelial cell injury, as well as recruiting inflammatory cells (29,31). RNA-sequencing analysis further validated that IL-17A and IL-6 (a downstream target of IL-17 signaling) were upregulated in IPF model rats, and downregulated in the TWGT-treated rats, highlighting their roles as key mediators of TWGT’s therapeutic effects. Furthermore, flow cytometric analysis revealed that TWGT inhibited the expansion of Th17 cells in IPF rats. Th17 cells, a subset of CD4+ T cells, can secrete IL-17A as well as promote chronic inflammation (32). In IPF, the accumulation of Th17 cells disrupts immune homeostasis [e.g., Th17/regulatory T cell (Treg) imbalance] and exacerbates lung injury (33). Our results demonstrated that TWGT, either alone or in combination with PA, remarkedly declined the proportion of Th17 cells at 7, 14, and 28 days post-modeling, indicating that Th17 cell suppression is a critical mechanism by which TWGT alleviates pulmonary inflammation. This is in line with a prior exploration that triptolide (a primary active component of TWGT) inhibits Th17 cell differentiation by suppressing STAT3 phosphorylation (34). Taken together, we speculate that TWGT may delay IPF development via downregulating proinflammatory and pro-fibrotic cytokines, as well as suppressing Th17-mediated inflammatory response.

EMT, the process through which alveolar epithelial cells undergo transdifferentiation into mesenchymal cells such as myofibroblasts, is a key contributor to IPF onset and pathogenesis (35). Our IF staining showed the epithelial marker E-cadherin expression gradually decreased in IPF rats over time, whereas the mesenchymal marker Vimentin expression progressively increased, indicating ongoing EMT progression. TWGT treatment reversed this trend, preserving E-cadherin expression and reducing Vimentin levels. In the pulmonary fibrosis pathophysiological process, E-cadherin serves as a canonical epithelial cell marker, and its downregulation is indicative of the phenotypic transition of epithelial cells to a mesenchymal state (36). In contrast, Vimentin functions as a mesenchymal marker, and its upregulation reflects enhanced trans-differentiation toward myofibroblasts (37). By maintaining high E-cadherin expression while inhibiting the abnormal elevation of Vimentin (38), TWGT may inhibit bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis through effectively blocking the EMT process.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this study is mainly based on the animal model of pulmonary fibrosis induced by bleomycin, which, although internationally recognized as the standard model for studying IPF, still has certain differences from human spontaneous pulmonary fibrosis (39). It is important to note that the bleomycin‑induced fibrosis model is driven by an acute inflammatory response, which differs substantially from the chronic, progressive, and relatively inflammation‑independent pathobiology of human IPF. This distinction is particularly relevant when interpreting the robust effects of TWGT on the IL-17/Th17 axis observed in this study. The prominence of these anti‑inflammatory effects may be amplified by the model’s acute inflammatory milieu and may not fully translate to the low‑inflammation environment of established IPF. Accordingly, while our results suggest that TWGT can modulate this axis under inflammatory conditions, their direct clinical relevance to IPF remains hypothesis‑generating and requires further exploration in models that more faithfully recapitulate chronic fibrosis. Future research may consider using spontaneous pulmonary fibrosis models or humanized mouse models to better simulate the human disease process. Second, although this study identified multiple key pathways involved in TWGT action, the crucial regulatory nodes in specific pathways as well as the interaction networks between different pathways have not been fully elucidated. Our study, while mechanistically informative, was conducted in a homogeneous animal model and does not capture the inter-patient variability in drug response, adherence, or dose modification observed in clinical practice. Future translational studies should incorporate patient-derived samples or real-world data to better contextualize TWGT’s potential role in the current treatment landscape.

Conclusions

In summary, TWGT mitigates bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis in rats through multiple mechanisms: suppressing the TGF-β/IL-17 axis to reduce ECM synthesis/deposition, inhibiting pro-inflammatory and pro-fibrotic cytokines, reversing EMT, and attenuating Th17 cell expansion. TWGT plus PA exerts synergistic anti-fibrotic effects. These findings support the clinical translation of TWGT for IPF treatment. Future studies should validate these mechanisms in human cell models and clinical trials.
