First 100 brachial plexus surgeries in Angola: Population impact and lessons learned
Authors: Adilson J.M. de Oliveira
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe authors present a multicenter national prospective cohort of neonatal and adult BPI in Angola from January 2021 to December 2025, focused on clinical data (patient profile and surgical results using the MRC scale) and lessons learned (geographic distribution and logistics) that can be applied to other low- and lower-middle-income countries.MethodsThis is a national multicenter prospective cohort of neonatal and adult BPI from January 2021 to December 2025. All patients were evaluated prior to surgery using clinical evaluation, always performed by the same nerve surgeon. All adult patients underwent electromyography. Neonatal patients were evaluated using clinical assessment only. All surgeries were performed by the same surgeon, and postoperative clinical follow-up for assessment of surgical results was also performed by the same surgeon.ResultsSixteen neonatal BPI cases were included; 10 were female, and the mean age was 13 months (range, 8–18 months). One patient had a complete injury and underwent reconstruction with grafts; the other 15 had upper trunk injuries and underwent the Oberlin procedure. All pediatric surgeries were performed in Luanda, and all patients were from Luanda. The mean follow-up was 12 months (range, 8–28 months), and an MRC grade of 3 or higher was achieved in 7 patients (43%). Among the 86 adult patients operated on, 76% had complete BPI, and 43% underwent distal transfers with grafts; data regarding type of injury and surgical approach are presented in Tables 2 and 3 The mean time from injury to surgery was 14 months (range, 6–24 months). Almost all patients had neuropathic pain (85 patients, 98%); among these, 78 (90%) showed improvement in pain.Discussion and conclusionThis series shows very low rates of functional recovery, which is an expected result given the long interval from injury to surgery in both adult and pediatric patients. The focus of this paper is on the lessons learned, highlighting that a surgical team alone is not sufficient, with the need for a dedicated pediatric team, public–private partnerships, and a team for secondary procedures. These first 100 brachial plexus surgeries represent only a small step in the development of brachial plexus surgery in Angola, and the lessons learned need to be applied to improve patient care.

Introduction

Brachial plexus injuries (BPI) are disabling, generally affecting young patients and caused by motorcycle-related traffic accidents (Faglioni et al., 2014). These lesions are increasing in incidence worldwide (Garozzo, 2019). In low-income countries, BPI are challenging because they are often complete injuries with associated lesions due to high-energy trauma (Khu et al., 2025).

Brachial plexus surgery is very complex and demands a specialized surgical team for adult and pediatric patients, as well as experienced physiotherapy and occupational therapy teams for rehabilitation (de Santana Chagas et al., 2022).

Angola is a lower-middle-income country with 34 million inhabitants, tens of thousands of medical doctors, 24 neurosurgeons, and a single brachial plexus surgeon. Scarcity of specialists has been a very important problem in Angola since independence. The country has been dealing with this through international cooperation, mainly with Portuguese-speaking countries (DE Oliveira and Fresta, 2020),(Alves et al., 2017).

Until 2015, Angola had no local team to manage neonatal or adult BPI. International cooperation, mainly with the Alex Mosset Foundation, was the alternative. This scenario was not an isolated condition, as many other medical specialties faced the same problem, with a lack of doctors and periodic international missions as the main alternative (McCauley et al., 2018). This was a problem because very few patients were submitted to surgical treatment during these missions, which were performed annually, and many patients remained without treatment.

The authors present a multicenter national prospective cohort of neonatal and adult BPI in Angola from January 2021 to December 2025, focused on clinical data (patient profile and surgical results using the MRC scale) and lessons learned (geographic distribution and logistics) that can be applied to other low- and lower-middle-income countries. To the best of our knowledge, there are no previous data regarding brachial plexus surgery in Africa and very limited data from other low- and lower-middle-income countries.

Methods

Study design

This study is a national multicenter prospective cohort including neonatal and adult brachial plexus injuries (BPI) treated surgically in Angola between January 2021 and December 2025.

Population

All patients who underwent surgical treatment for BPI in Angola between 2021 and 2025 were included, regardless of age. Two entities were considered: neonatal BPI and traumatic BPI in adults.

Data acquisition

All patients were evaluated prior to surgery through clinical assessment, always performed by the same nerve surgeon. All adult patients underwent electromyography as part of the preoperative evaluation. Neonatal patients were evaluated exclusively through clinical assessment.

All surgical procedures were performed by the same surgeon. Postoperative clinical follow-up and assessment of surgical outcomes were also conducted by the same surgeon.

As Angola has only one nerve surgeon, surgeries were performed in nine hospitals—three private and six public—across three provinces:1.Luanda, the capital city of Angola: three private and four public hospitals2.Cuanza Sul, located approximately 330 km south of Luanda: one public hospital3.Benguela, located approximately 600 km south of Luanda: one public hospital

Data regarding sociodemographic characteristics, clinical variables, and surgical outcomes were prospectively recorded in a database for subsequent analysis.

Variables

1.Sociodemographic variables: age, sex, provenance, and employment status. Provenance was categorized as the same province where surgery was performed or a different province. Employment status was categorized as pre-injury (employed/unemployed) and post-injury (employed/unemployed).2.Clinical variables: type of injury (categorized as complete or partial), affected trunk (upper, middle, or lower), and type of surgery performed (neurolysis, grafting, or distal nerve transfers).3.Surgical outcomes: functional outcome assessed using the Medical Research Council (MRC) scale and duration of follow-up (in months).

Exclusion criteria

Patients with less than six months of postoperative follow-up were excluded from the analysis.

Statistical analysis

The database was analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 24 (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical variables were expressed as numbers and percentages. Continuous variables were expressed as mean and standard deviation, with ranges reported where appropriate.

Ethics

All patients, or parents/legal guardians in neonatal cases, signed informed consent forms to participate in the study. No experimental procedures were performed, and all surgical indications and techniques followed conventional approaches previously described in the literature and widely used worldwide. The study was approved by the local ethics committee protocol number 0027/2025.

Challenges faced in implementation of brachial plexus surgery in Angola

The main challenge faced in this process was the wrong knowledge that Brachial Plexus injuries has not solution, this information was diffused in medical community and in general population, other big challenge was the unavailability of MRI with protocol to brachial plexus injuries.

Specific facilities for BP surgeries

Brachial plexus (BP) surgery does not require the same level of infrastructure as cranial or spinal surgery; however, some basic resources are essential:

Magnification: Surgical microscopes are available in only seven surgical centers across the country (including both public and private systems). As an alternative, the surgical team routinely uses surgical loupes for magnification.

Electromyography: Electromyography is available in only three centers, all located in the capital city (Luanda). As a result, many surgeries are performed based solely on clinical evaluation.

Fibrin glue: Although available, fibrin glue is associated with high costs and is not routinely used. Since 2022, nerve repairs have been performed exclusively using microsurgical suturing techniques.

Lessons learned

•A surgical team alone is not enough: We currently have a structured surgical team, but we do not have a well-organized team for diagnostic assessment and adequate rehabilitation.•Moving the surgical team is not effective: Over the past four years, the surgical team moved around the country to identify patients and perform surgeries. We believe this is not the best strategy; the next step is to create a reference center, with patients referred to this center, which will be more effective.•A single surgeon is not enough: For a country with almost 40 million inhabitants, where road traffic accidents represent a major health problem (de Oliveira and Solari, 2020), there is an urgent need for additional nerve surgeons specialy focused on secondary procedures, as the current team do not have experience perfoming it.•The same team for adults and neonates is not effective: Neonatal BPI is a different entity, with distinct diagnostic, treatment, and rehabilitation approaches (Shah et al., 2021; Yang, 2014; van der et al., 2020; Boetto et al., 2024; Smith et al., 2018). A dedicated pediatric team is needed to improve results.•Public and private investment is urgent: The public health system alone is not sufficient to address BPI; private investment is also necessary, as in other diseases (Dy et al., 2020).•It is not possible to achieve satisfactory results without secondary procedures: Secondary procedures for BPI, such as free muscle transfers, arthrodesis, and others, have well-established results (Carlsen et al., 2009; Werthel et al., 2018; Iamaguchi et al., 2024; Oliver et al., 2020). Currently, our team does not include surgeons trained to perform these procedures. We evaluate many patients with more than two years since injury who could be candidates for these surgeries.

•It is not possible to achieve satisfactory results without secondary procedures: Secondary procedures for BPI, such as free muscle transfers, arthrodesis, and others, have well-established results (Carlsen et al., 2009; Werthel et al., 2018; Iamaguchi et al., 2024; Oliver et al., 2020). Currently, our team does not include surgeons trained to perform these procedures. We evaluate many patients with more than two years since injury who could be candidates for these surgeries.

These lessons learned are very important and, we believe, can be applied to other low- and lower-middle-income countries.

Conclusion

These first 100 brachial plexus surgeries represent only a small step in the development of brachial plexus surgery in Angola. The lessons learned from this experience need to be applied to improve patient care in low and low-middle income contries.
