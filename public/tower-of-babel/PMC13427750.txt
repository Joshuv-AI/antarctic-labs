Research on the pathways through which rural large-scale sporting events enhance the well-being of local residents—a grounded theory study based on Guizhou, China
Authors: Xuyao Ding, Yingbo Zhu, Shu Wen, Zehui Wen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe rapid development of large-scale rural sporting events has created important opportunities to enhance the multidimensional well-being of rural residents. Taking residents as the primary subjects of analysis, this study adopts a qualitative approach to explore how sporting events become embedded in rural life and how they shape residents’ emotional experiences and everyday practices. Drawing on the Cognitive-Affective Processing System theory and grounded theory, and using Guizhou Province as the empirical context, this study develops a localized explanatory framework for understanding how large-scale rural sporting events influence residents’ well-being. Based on in-depth interviews with 30 local residents, the findings show that this process follows a dynamic logic in which local contexts trigger cognitive and emotional responses, which in turn stimulate behavioral participation. This mechanism is realized through multiple pathways, including the reconstruction of local identity, the elicitation of positive emotions, and the promotion of social interaction. Methodologically, this study combines grounded theory with a cognitive-affective perspective. Theoretically, it reveals the complex and dynamic meaning-construction process through which sporting events contribute to residents’ well-being. By offering a qualitative interpretation grounded in local experience, this study contributes to a deeper understanding of the socio-psychological role of sport in rural revitalization.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-57429-x.Keywords: Rural areas, Large-scale sporting events, Residents’ well-being, Grounded theory, GuizhouSubject terms: Psychology, Human behaviour

Introduction

With economic and social development and the growing spiritual and cultural needs of residents, well-being has become an important indicator for assessing people’s quality of life and psychological condition. Existing research suggests that during the low-income stage, material consumption plays a dominant role in shaping residents’ well-being. However, at the middle- and high-income stages, the marginal contribution of spiritual and cultural factors to well-being gradually increases, whereas the contribution of material consumption declines relatively1. A longitudinal study of Chinese residents further shows that the deepening of spiritual and cultural development constitutes one of the key pathways for enhancing residents’ Subjective Well-Being2. As an important vehicle for the development of spiritual civilization, sporting events have played an increasingly prominent role in both urban and rural development in recent years. From international events such as the Olympic Games and the FIFA World Cup to national activities such as marathons, sporting events, through their broad participation and social influence, have become important opportunities for shaping city images, promoting industrial upgrading, and enhancing service capacity3–6. Relevant studies have shown that sporting events not only directly improve residents’ livelihood satisfaction through mechanisms such as infrastructure improvement and the integration of sport and tourism7, but also enhance residents’ sense of gain and well-being by strengthening community belonging8 and increasing economic income6.

In the context of China’s rural revitalization strategy, rural sporting events, as an emerging socio-cultural phenomenon, embody both villagers’ aspirations for a better spiritual life and an important means of promoting the coordinated development of material and spiritual civilization. These events are not only widely embraced at the grassroots level, but have also attracted growing policy attention and support, gradually becoming an important cultural practice for enhancing rural residents’ sense of gain and well-being9. Given that residents’ well-being is a multidimensional construct closely associated with cultural environment, social relationships, and local life contexts, particular attention should be paid to the pathways through which it can be enhanced in rural contexts10. In this study, residents’ well-being is used as an overarching concept to capture not only happiness and life satisfaction, but also psychological growth, social belonging, local identity, and perceived contribution. Against this backdrop, the present study takes Guizhou Province as the empirical context and focuses on residents in event-hosting areas. Using grounded theory as the primary methodological approach, this study seeks to construct a conceptual model explaining how rural sporting events influence residents’ well-being. In addition, it draws on the Cognitive-Affective Processing System (CAPS) theory to explain the underlying mechanism of this process. The study aims to provide both a theoretical basis and practical implications for understanding how sporting events can contribute to rural revitalization and improve residents’ well-being.

Theoretical background

Conceptualizing residents’ well-being

In the literature, well-being is generally understood as a broad and multidimensional construct. Two major traditions are often distinguished: the hedonic tradition, which emphasizes happiness, pleasure, positive affect, and life satisfaction, and the eudaimonic tradition, which emphasizes meaning, self-realization, and optimal functioning11. Subjective well-being is usually associated with individuals’ own evaluations of their lives, including positive affect, negative affect, and life satisfaction12. By contrast, psychological well-being focuses more on personal growth, autonomy, environmental mastery, positive relations with others, purpose in life, and self-acceptance12. Social well-being further extends the concept of well-being to individuals’ evaluations of their social relationships, social contribution, social integration, social acceptance, and social actualization13.

Related concepts such as happiness, mental health, life satisfaction, and quality of life should therefore be distinguished from well-being rather than used interchangeably. Happiness usually refers more directly to positive affective experiences and feelings of pleasure, whereas life satisfaction represents a cognitive evaluation of one’s overall life conditions and is often treated as a component of subjective well-being12. Mental health concerns people’s ability to cope with life stress, realize their abilities, learn and work effectively, and contribute to their communities14. Quality of life is broader and refers to individuals’ perceptions of their position in life within specific cultural and value contexts, including their goals, expectations, standards, and concerns15.

Based on these distinctions, the present study adopts residents’ well-being as the primary analytical focus. This broader concept is more appropriate for the present study because rural sporting events influence residents not only through immediate happiness or life satisfaction, but also through local identity, social belonging, perceived contribution, self-realization, community participation, and everyday behavioral change. Accordingly, subjective well-being, psychological well-being, and social well-being are treated as related but distinct manifestations of residents’ broader well-being. Meanwhile, these concepts serve as sensitizing concepts rather than predetermined coding categories, while the specific pathways among context, cognition, emotion, behavior, and well-being are generated inductively through grounded theory coding16.

Sporting events and residents’ well-being

With the growing influence of international sporting events, cities in China and elsewhere have increasingly regarded the hosting of large-scale sporting events as an important opportunity to stimulate development and enhance their public image. Existing research shows that large-scale sporting events not only generate long-term positive effects in tangible domains such as the economy and infrastructure, but also contribute to intangible dimensions such as cultural identity and social cohesion, thereby producing broad and far-reaching impacts17. Against this backdrop, increasing scholarly attention has been paid to how sporting events influence residents’ well-being and how they may contribute to enhancing residents’ quality of life.

Existing studies have examined the well-being effects of sporting events through several related but distinct conceptual lenses. First, some studies have focused on subjective well-being and happiness by examining how sport participation or event spectatorship generates positive emotions, enjoyment, and life satisfaction. For example, active participation in sport can alleviate psychological stress and promote mental health18, while indirect participation, such as watching televised sporting events, can positively affect residents’ subjective well-being19. Studies of major football events have also shown that sporting events may enhance life satisfaction among highly engaged fans, although such effects may vary across groups with different levels of sport interest20. Second, sporting events have also been linked to psychological well-being and mental health. In this line of research, scholars are concerned not only with whether residents feel happy during or after events, but also with whether event-related participation strengthens self-efficacy, psychological resilience, identity recognition, and perceived personal value. Different participant roles, such as fans, non-fans, volunteers, athletes, and spectators, may lead to substantial variation in emotional experiences, self-recognition, and satisfaction levels21. For instance, volunteers who engage more deeply in event-related practices are more likely to derive unique emotional experiences and a stronger sense of personal meaning from their participation22. Third, from the perspectives of social well-being and quality of life, sporting events have been examined as community-level interventions that reshape residents’ social relationships and perceptions of local development. Unlike studies that focus mainly on individual pleasure or life satisfaction, this line of research emphasizes how events foster community belonging, collective identity, social cohesion, and perceived social value. In addition, improvements in infrastructure, public services, sport–tourism integration, and local economic opportunities are often understood as external conditions that support residents’ quality of life rather than as well-being itself5,7. Therefore, the social and quality-of-life effects of sporting events should be understood as important contextual foundations through which broader residents’ well-being may be shaped.

Although existing studies have laid an important foundation for understanding the relationship between sporting events and well-being-related outcomes, several limitations remain. First, most studies have focused on urban contexts and mega-sport events, primarily examining the relationships among urban residents’ participation behaviors, identity roles, and well-being outcomes. A qualitative review reported that, among the 70 studies included, more than 70% concentrated on mega-sport events such as the Asian Games, the Copa América, and Grand Prix events23. This indicates that previous research has been highly concentrated in terms of research objects and empirical contexts. Second, although existing studies have examined subjective well-being, mental health, life satisfaction, and quality of life, they have paid relatively limited attention to how these well-being-related experiences are generated through the interaction between local context, residents’ cognition, emotional experience, and behavioral participation. Third, research on the relationship between rural sporting events and residents’ well-being remains limited, particularly with regard to the underlying mechanisms linking the two21,24.

Against this background, and grounded in the realities of rural China, the present study seeks to uncover the key pathways and mechanisms through which rural sporting events influence residents’ well-being. Rather than treating well-being as a single outcome variable, this study understands residents’ well-being as a multidimensional and locally embedded construct. By using grounded theory, the study aims to generate an explanatory framework from residents’ lived experiences, while drawing on CAPS theory to interpret the dynamic relationships among local context, cognition, emotion, behavior, and well-being.

Cognitive-affective processing system theory

The CAPS theory, proposed by the American psychologist Walter Mischel in 1995, was developed to reconcile the tensions between traditional trait theory and social-cognitive theory. It reexamines the relationships among situational contexts, personality dispositions, individual motivations, and behavioral consistency, while introducing a system of cognitive-affective variables to explain the mechanisms underlying individual differences25. CAPS theory establishes a multi-level and parallel interactive framework of “cognition–emotion–behavior” (Fig. 1), thereby revealing the dynamic relationship between individuals’ psychological processes and behavioral responses in specific contexts26. Mischel argued that situational features, such as time, place, social actors, and event themes, activate individuals’ cognitive and emotional processes, which are shaped by situational interpretations grounded in prior experience27. Under the influence of goals and values, individuals tend to actively seek out or create situations that correspond to their internal expectations. However, situations do not directly determine behavior; rather, they interact in complex ways with individuals’ Cognitive-Affective Units (CAUs), thereby shaping subsequent behavioral responses.

The event-hosting area serves not only as the material carrier and emotional point of departure for residents’ social memory, but also as a site in which collective practice and emotional bonding are sedimented into shared memories28. The local context embedded in rural sporting events can reactivate, awaken, reconstruct, and even create residents’ existing cognitive schemas, thereby eliciting positive emotions such as local identity and a sense of belonging. At the same time, emotional interactions and affective marking practices during the event process further facilitate the emergence and consolidation of these positive emotions among residents. From this perspective, the influence of rural sporting events on residents’ well-being follows a dynamic pathway of “situational activation–emotional arousal–behavioral reinforcement,” involving multiple dimensions of CAPS theory, including context, cognition, emotion, and behavior. Accordingly, drawing on CAPS theory, this study seeks to systematically elucidate the underlying pathways through which rural sporting events influence residents’ well-being, with the aim of providing theoretical support and practical insights for promoting rural revitalization through sport and improving residents’ well-being.

Research design

Case selection

Case study research requires the selected case to possess a high degree of typicality and representativeness in order to effectively address the research question and fulfill the research objective. Guizhou’s “Village BA,” characterized by its distinctive rural cultural attributes, broad-based public participation, and strong social influence, constitutes an ideal case for examining the pathways through which large-scale rural sports events enhance the well-being of residents in host communities, as well as the localized mechanisms underlying this process.

On the one hand, Guizhou’s “Village BA” exhibits the typical characteristics of rural sports events. Deeply embedded in the ethnic cultural traditions of southeastern Guizhou, the basketball competition is closely intertwined with displays of intangible cultural heritage, such as Miao folk songs, Feige singing, and embroidery. At the same time, “Village BA” is spontaneously organized, jointly funded, and concretely implemented by local villagers, who assume multiple roles as participants and volunteers, thereby reflecting a strong sense of community participation and local agency.

On the other hand, Guizhou’s “Village BA” has attracted substantial public attention and generated extensive social influence. Official statistics show that in 2023, driven by “Village BA,” Taijiang County recorded a year-on-year increase of 74.87% in tourist arrivals and 94.66% in total tourism revenue; since the event gained widespread visibility, the county’s overall tourism revenue has increased by 66.14%. In addition, after rapidly gaining attention through online dissemination in 2022, “Village BA” generated more than 55 billion online impressions in 2023 and was selected as one of the Fifth Batch of National Typical Cases of Rural Public Services. Overall, in terms of cultural typicality, public participation, and social demonstration effects, Guizhou’s “Village BA” is highly representative and conforms to the case selection criteria of this study.

Research method and data collection

Research method

This study adopts grounded theory as its research method. Originally proposed by Glaser and Strauss in 1967, grounded theory emphasizes the generation of theoretical explanations through the systematic collection and analysis of empirical data, with concepts and categories inductively derived from raw materials29. More specifically, this study follows the procedural grounded theory approach developed by Strauss and Corbin, employing three-stage coding and the constant comparative method as its principal analytical strategies to progressively construct an explanatory framework from empirical data.

Grounded theory is particularly suitable for examining how individuals perceive, interpret, and assign meaning to the external world, and is therefore well aligned with the study of residents’ well-being, which is characterized by strong subjectivity and contextuality. At the same time, as an emerging socio-cultural phenomenon, large-scale rural sporting events still lack a mature and systematic theoretical explanation regarding their influence on the well-being of residents in host communities. In this respect, grounded theory provides effective methodological support for identifying relevant concepts, revealing relationships among variables, and constructing a localized theoretical framework.

It should also be noted that the influence of large-scale rural sporting events on local residents’ well-being is not a simple linear process; rather, it involves multiple dimensions, including cognition, emotion, and behavior. Relying solely on the micro-level induction of grounded theory makes it difficult to fully capture the overall pathway through which this influence unfolds. Therefore, this study further introduces CAPS theory as an auxiliary analytical framework. CAPS theory conceptualizes the mechanism of influence at the macro level by distinguishing three interrelated dimensions—cognition, emotion, and behavior—whereas grounded theory facilitates an in-depth exploration of the key factors within each dimension and their internal relationships on the basis of empirical data situated in the real context of rural sporting events in Guizhou. The combination of the two enhances the systematicity and explanatory power of the research framework, thereby enabling a more comprehensive understanding of the pathways through which large-scale rural sporting events improve the well-being of host-community residents.

Data collection

Based on the methodological framework outlined above, this study proceeded to data collection. Following the principle of theoretical saturation, in-depth semi-structured interviews were conducted with 30 local residents in Taijiang County, Guizhou Province. The basic information of the interviewees is presented in Table 1. To ensure the appropriateness and diversity of the sample, participants were required to meet the following criteria. First, they had to be native residents of Guizhou Province who had lived continuously in the local area for at least 10 years. Second, they needed to display a certain degree of diversity in demographic characteristics, including gender, age, income level, educational background, and occupation, and to have participated in local sporting events in different roles—such as spectators, participants, or service providers—within the past two years. Third, they were expected to have a certain degree of attention to, or participation in, local sporting events so that they could articulate their experiences and perceptions in a sufficiently detailed manner.

During the interview process, each interview lasted between 45 and 90 min and was extended where necessary depending on the specific circumstances. All interviews were audio-recorded with the informed consent of the participants, and transcription together with preliminary data organization was completed within 12 h after each interview. In total, approximately 172,000 Chinese characters of textual data were obtained. To ensure the rigor of the subsequent coding analysis and theoretical construction, 24 interview transcripts were selected for three-stage coding and model construction, while the remaining 6 were reserved for theoretical saturation testing. The semi-structured interview guide is provided in Appendix A. Where the study involved the English translation and presentation of Chinese interview materials, a translation–back translation–comparison, revision, and proofreading procedure was adopted to ensure the accuracy of the translation and the integrity of the original meanings.

Grounded theory construction process

After completing data collection, this study followed the procedural grounded theory approach proposed by Strauss and Corbin to conduct open coding, axial coding, and selective coding. Combined with the constant comparative method, these procedures were used to identify core categories and construct the theoretical model. Of the interview materials, 24 transcripts were used for the three-stage coding and model construction, while the remaining 6 were reserved for theoretical saturation testing.

First, during the open coding stage, the interview transcripts were analyzed line by line to identify initial concepts related to the formation and enhancement of residents’ well-being. These concepts were then categorized and conceptualized through constant comparison in order to preserve the original empirical meanings as fully as possible. Second, during the axial coding stage, the logical relationships among concepts and categories were further examined. Similar concepts were integrated and clustered into major categories, and their relationships with event context, residents’ cognition, emotional experience, and behavioral participation were analyzed. Third, during the selective coding stage, the relationships among the major categories were integrated around the core category to construct a pathway model explaining how large-scale rural sporting events influence the well-being of residents in host communities. At this stage, the core storyline was also refined to reveal the dynamic generative logic of “cognition–emotion–behavior.” Finally, supplementary coding and verification were conducted on the reserved transcripts. If no new significant concepts, categories, or relationships emerged, the theoretical model was considered to have reached saturation.

During data analysis, NVivo software was used to organize, code, and categorize the materials. In addition, repeated comparison, cross-checking, and constant reference to the original data were employed to enhance the reliability of the coding process and the robustness of the findings.

Research procedure

Open coding

Open coding is the foundational stage of grounded theory analysis. Its core task is to interpret the raw interview texts word by word, sentence by sentence, and paragraph by paragraph in order to identify and extract meaning units relevant to the research topic, which are then further developed into initial concepts and categories30. To preserve the authenticity of participants’ expressions and the specificity of the local context, this study used participants’ original wording as coding labels whenever possible. For example, the statement, “Seeing the village organize a competition, it was livelier than the New Year, and I felt particularly happy,” was coded as “Event Atmosphere,” while the statement, “As a member of the village basketball team, stepping onto the court, I felt especially proud,” was coded as “Event Identity Recognition.” Through a systematic review of the interview texts, 218 original statements were extracted and further condensed into 64 initial concepts. On this basis, using the constant comparative method, nodes with similar or repetitive meanings were merged, while concepts only weakly related to the core theme or occurring fewer than four times were removed on the basis of theoretical sensitivity. Ultimately, 31 subcategories were generated. These subcategories capture the specific and discrete cognitive, emotional, and behavioral elements involved in residents’ perceptions of the event and the formation of well-being, thereby providing the basis for subsequent analysis.

To illustrate the coding decision-making process more clearly, the formation of the subcategory “Hometown Identity” is used here as an example. During the open coding stage, several raw statements expressing a strong sense of identification with one’s hometown were identified. For instance, one participant stated, “When I saw our ethnic songs and dances performed at the opening ceremony of the event, I truly understood what cultural inheritance means,” which was coded as “Cultural Deep Understanding.” Another participant remarked, “I used to think our village was remote, but now, because of hosting the event, many people from outside have come, and I realize how unique our place is,” which was coded as “Rediscovery of Local Value.” A third participant stated, “As a local volunteer introducing the event’s history to outsiders, I felt a special sense of confidence,” which was coded as “Identity Transmission and Pride.” Through constant comparison, it became clear that although these initial concepts differed in expression, they shared a common underlying meaning: residents were using the event as a medium through which to deepen their identification with, and sense of belonging to, their hometown’s culture, values, and social identity. These concepts were therefore integrated and abstracted into the subcategory “Hometown Identity.” In the subsequent axial coding stage, because of its role as a key affective outcome, “Hometown Identity” was incorporated into the broader subcategory “Social Well-Being” and ultimately became an important component of the main category “Emotional Aggregation.” This process illustrates how specific and contextualized local expressions were progressively abstracted into theoretically meaningful categories. Axial coding

Axial coding aims to identify and establish the underlying relationships among categories, thereby generating higher-order categories with greater explanatory power31. In this study, drawing on the analytical framework of the paradigmatic model, the 31 subcategories identified in the open coding stage were further clustered and reorganized around the logical sequence of phenomenon–causal conditions–context–intervening conditions–action/interaction strategies–outcomes. For example, subcategories such as “Event Architecture Context,” “Event Street Context,” and “Event Venue Context” were grouped into the broader category “Field Context,” which collectively describes the specific situational setting in which the focal phenomenon occurred. Similarly, subcategories such as “Sense of Self-Accomplishment,” “Entrepreneurial Self-Efficacy,” and “Sense of Self-Realization” were grouped into the broader category “Psychological Well-Being,”reflecting the key affective and psychological outcomes experienced by residents through participation. Through this process, the potential causal and relational links among categories were preliminarily clarified.

Ultimately, the 31 subcategories were integrated into 11 broader categories, which were then further distilled into four main categories: Local Context Presentation as the contextual and stimulus source, Cognitive-Emotional System Interaction as the core underlying mechanism, Participation Behavior Expression as the external action/interaction strategy, and Well-Being Achievement and Reinforcement as the outcome dimension. In this coding structure, “Emotion” refers to the affective-experiential dimension through which residents’ well-being becomes visible within the CAPS framework, rather than implying that well-being is equivalent to emotion. The narrative surrounding this core category can be summarized as follows: rural sporting events, as causal conditions, activate residents’ cognitive appraisals and affective experiences within the local contextual framework in which they are embedded. The interaction between cognition and affect constitutes the core underlying mechanism in the formation of residents’ well-being, which in turn stimulates specific forms of participatory behavior. Behavior, as the external expression of cognitive-affective activation and the practical carrier of meaning, further reinforces residents’ experiences of pleasure, self-recognition, belonging, and perceived contribution through social feedback and embodied practice. Through this process, residents’ well-being is not merely generated as a short-term emotional response, but is progressively formed, enhanced, and reinforced as a multidimensional experience embedded in local life. This storyline reveals a progressive pathway of influence: Local Context → Cognitive-Emotional Interaction → Behavioral Feedback → Well-Being Reinforcement.

Theoretical saturation testing

Theoretical saturation is a crucial step in grounded theory research. It is reached when additional data no longer generate new concepts, categories, or meaningful theoretical insights32,. In this study, a systematic procedure with explicit criteria was adopted to assess theoretical saturation. After constructing the preliminary model on the basis of 24 interview transcripts, the remaining 6 transcripts (20% of the total sample) were reserved as independent materials for saturation testing. This proportion is broadly consistent with common practices in grounded theory research. To enhance the objectivity of the assessment, the saturation test was conducted by researchers who had not participated in the initial coding process.

Theoretical saturation was evaluated according to two criteria. First, whether the reserved materials generated new concepts or categories not covered by the existing model. Second, whether they revealed new phenomena that required revisions to the core pathway of “Local Context → Cognitive-Emotional Interaction → Behavioral Feedback → Well-Being Reinforcement.” The results showed that all meaningful concepts derived from the reserved sample could be incorporated into the established framework. For example, the statement “Relatives working away from home often called to inquire about the event” was categorized under “Enhanced Social Connectivity,” while “Event-related improvements to roads and lighting” was categorized under “Actual Experience.” Overall, all narratives in the reserved materials could be adequately explained by the existing model, and no new categories or structural relationships were required. On this basis, the study concludes that the theoretical model has reached saturation, demonstrating a satisfactory degree of completeness and stability.

Model explanation

Drawing on CAPS theory, this study analyzed the interview data through the three stages of grounded theory coding and identified four core dimensions—context, cognition, emotion, and behavior—as well as their interrelationships. The findings suggest that the influence of rural sporting events on residents’ well-being follows a progressive mechanism of situational activation, cognitive-emotional interaction, and behavioral reinforcement. Residents’ well-being is primarily shaped through the interaction between cognition and emotion, while local context provides the initial stimulus and behavioral practice supplies the feedback mechanism through which this process is reinforced over time. Compared with previous studies that have mainly focused on urban settings and proximal outcomes33, this study further shows that in rural contexts, the well-being effects of sporting events unfold through a socially and culturally embedded process.

Situational activation: the triggering mechanism of residents’ well-being

The enhancement of residents’ well-being through rural sporting events begins with the activation of individuals’ cognitive-affective systems by local context. According to CAPS theory, the meaning of situational inputs depends on the relatively stable CAUs formed through prior experience34. In this study, these units are reflected in the positive memory schemas residents have developed through long-term participation, through which sporting events become closely associated with their hometown’s spatial setting, interpersonal interactions, and festive culture. Each time an event is held, these schemas are reactivated and reinforced, making local context the starting point that triggers subsequent cognitive appraisals and emotional responses.

Field context constitutes the physical and emotional foundation of this activation process. Through the mechanism of place attachment, it activates cognitive-affective units related to emotional connection35. Because the event takes place in spaces that are highly familiar to residents, this familiarity forms a type of “place script” within their cognitive schema. As one respondent noted, “When the sporting event is held in our hometown, many people come to watch, but if it were held in the city, hardly anyone would want to go.” When residents are immersed in an event atmosphere infused with hometown elements, intense multisensory stimuli—such as “the cheering was deafening, and the scene was magnificent”—not only evoke immediate emotional reactions, but also deepen the appraisal that “this place is relevant to me,” thereby strengthening belonging and attachment. This finding supports the CAPS perspective that specific physical contexts can activate cognitive-affective units associated with place attachment and facilitate positive emotional responses.

Personal context injects dynamic socio-emotional energy into the process. The event temporarily transforms kinship and family networks embedded in rural society into highly focused interaction rituals36. Residents’ strong attention to family members participating in the event, together with their identification with the “hometown team,” generates a powerful emotional bond. As one participant stated, “When I saw my hometown team win, I felt even more excited than they did.”This reflects the CAPS mechanism through which social contextual inputs, such as interpersonal attention and collective identification, activate emotional units related to relational identity and collective pride, thereby generating sustained emotional energy.

The deeper significance of event context lies in its capacity to create a collective cultural imprint that can be repeatedly reactivated. When the event becomes deeply integrated with local folk traditions such as the “June 6th New Rice Festival,” it transcends ordinary sporting activity and becomes a form of collective cultural ritual37. Within this shared experience, residents may enter a state of emotional resonance akin to collective effervescence, which not only enhances immediate pleasure but also produces enduring collective memories. This reveals that culturally ritualized contexts strongly activate clusters of cognitive-affective units related to cultural identity and festive celebration. More broadly, it suggests that the well-being effects of rural sporting events do not arise from sport participation alone, but from the deep embeddedness of sport in local memory, festive culture, and rural social relations.

Cognitive-affective units: the internal mechanism underlying the emergence of residents’ well-being

CAUs constitute the internal mechanism through which rural sporting events influence residents’ well-being. Well-being is shaped neither by cognition nor by emotion alone; rather, it emerges through the dynamic interaction between local cultural contexts and individuals’ CAUs. Based on the coding results, this process can be divided into two interconnected stages: cognitive activation and emotional consolidation.

Cognitive activation stage: forming the cognitive foundation of residents’ well-being

The cognitive activation stage refers to the process through which cognitive units are activated in response to situational inputs. In this study, contextual changes brought about by rural sporting events prompted residents to compare prior experiences with present perceptions, thereby activating cognitive evaluation and revision across three dimensions: the event, the rural environment, and the self. This process laid the cognitive foundation for the formation of residents’ well-being.

The transformation of event cognition marks a shift from external behavioral observation to internal psychological motivation. At a preliminary level, residents are inspired by the perseverance of participants and interpret it as a struggle for collective honor. At a deeper level, such observations are internalized as psychological resources for coping with adversity. This reflects the CAPS proposition that salient behavioral role models can activate cognitive units related to meaning interpretation and self-motivation, thereby transforming external observation into internal motivational scripts. The restructuring of rural cognition stems from residents’ reassessment of changes in the objective environment. Improvements in infrastructure, such as transportation and public facilities, strengthened residents’ positive evaluations of their living conditions. At the same time, governance practices centered around the event enabled residents to perceive more concretely the effectiveness of local development and the enhancement of their hometown’s public image. This suggests that signals of environmental change can activate cognitive units such as public service evaluation and governance effectiveness perception, thereby updating residents’ sense of belonging and confidence in their hometown’s future.

The updating of self-cognition occurs through expanding social interaction and the redefinition of social roles. Through contact with visitors from outside the community, residents absorb new ideas and broader developmental perspectives. Meanwhile, the event provides visible platforms for social participation, including for older residents, prompting a shift from traditional role identification toward a self-positioning as valuable contributors. This reveals how new patterns of social interaction and changing cultural contexts can activate cognitive units related to self-efficacy and social role recognition, thereby encouraging individuals to redefine their personal value and the possibilities available at their current life stage. Overall, the cognitive activation stage can be understood as the process through which external contextual signals are filtered through individuals’ internal systems of appraisal. Drawing on their existing perceptual schemas and value orientations, residents selectively encode and assign meaning to the event context, thereby shaping the direction and tone of subsequent emotional consolidation. In this sense, the findings not only support the CAPS proposition that cognitive appraisal precedes and guides emotional responses, but also clarify how, in the rural sporting-event context, collective observation becomes individual internalization, environmental perception becomes systematic evaluation, and role participation becomes self-reconstruction.

Emotional consolidation stage: the affective manifestation of residents’ well-being

According to CAPS theory, once cognitive units are activated, they in turn activate related emotional units, thereby forming specific patterns of emotional response. This study finds that, in the context of rural sporting events, residents’ experiences following cognitive appraisal are not reducible to a one-dimensional sense of pleasure. Rather, they develop along three interrelated dimensions: social connectedness, personal growth, and positive affect, which correspond broadly to social well-being, psychological well-being, and subjective well-being, respectively, in the literature on positive psychology (Zhou, Z. M et al.38,. These dimensions should not be understood as emotions alone, but as affectively expressed manifestations of residents’ broader well-being. Taken together, they constitute the principal affective manifestations of residents’ well-being within the CAPS framework.

The enhancement of social well-being is reflected in residents’ strengthened social belonging, perceived value contribution, and sense of collective identity (C. L. M. Keyes39,. Once residents cognitively affirm the positive significance of the event for their hometown, they become more willing to engage in volunteer service, community collaboration, and other forms of participation. Such practices strengthen residents’ social connectedness and their sense of contributing to the collective. By taking on specific roles, such as organizers or volunteers, they align their personal capacities with collective needs and experience the positive feelings associated with being needed and useful. This illustrates how cognitive appraisals of local value and concrete opportunities for action jointly activate affectively meaningful experiences of social recognition, belonging, and altruistic efficacy.

The generation of psychological well-being is closely related to residents’ experiences of self-efficacy, competence, achievement, and self-realization13. This process stems from residents’ cognitive affirmation of their own agency in the event. Participants may experience competence and control through the sense of achievement associated with competition, while entrepreneurs and service providers may derive satisfaction from the economic returns and social recognition generated by the event. In this sense, rural sporting events become a setting in which residents enact and validate their own potential, thereby gaining positive experiences of growth and self-realization. This reflects the CAPS proposition that cognitive appraisals related to goal attainment and competence validation can activate corresponding affective responses.

The emergence of subjective well-being is primarily associated with immediate pleasure, positive affect, and physical and mental comfort12. This is the most widespread and contagious well-being-related experience within the event context. Residents experience emotional release and heightened pleasure through immersive participation, such as cheering and becoming absorbed in intense competition. Such experiences not only generate immediate positive emotions, but also contribute to the development of healthier life orientations, including a greater appreciation of physical activity. This suggests that highly arousing and collectively meaningful contexts, such as the atmosphere of collective excitement created by the event, can quickly activate positive affective experiences such as joy and excitement.

In summary, the emotional consolidation stage demonstrates the CAPS process through which cognitive appraisal gives rise to affectively meaningful experiences. By providing diverse roles, interactions, and participatory opportunities, rural sporting events create context-specific pathways through which residents’ broader well-being becomes expressed and reinforced. Social well-being, psychological well-being, and subjective well-being are not isolated from one another; rather, they jointly constitute the affective manifestations of residents’ broader well-being. This finding not only refines the understanding of how residents’ well-being is expressed in rural sporting events, but also supports the CAPS proposition that cognition and emotion interact dynamically in shaping human responses.

Behavioral feedback and reinforcement: the practical pathway through which residents’ well-being is realized

Within the framework of CAPS theory, behavior represents the ultimate output and external manifestation of activated cognitive-affective units. Based on the grounded theory analysis, this study classifies residents’ behaviors in the event context into triggered behavior and habitual behavior. This distinction not only captures differences in behavioral expression, but also reveals the cyclical closure of the context–cognition–emotion–behavior chain in the CAPS model: behavior is both an outcome of cognitive-emotional activation and a source of new situational feedback that continuously reshapes individuals’ cognitive-affective units.

Triggered behavior reflects the immediate externalization of high-arousal emotions in ritualized contexts. According to Interaction Ritual Chains theory, the collective focus of attention and the strong emotional bonds formed at the event site inject intense emotional energy into participants36. Once residents cognitively affirm the value of the event and become emotionally immersed in the collective atmosphere, this energy is rapidly externalized into immediate and reactive behaviors, such as spontaneous cheering, real-time sharing of exciting moments on social media, or actively recommending the event to others. As one respondent stated, “When I saw my hometown team win, I was so excited that I immediately posted on my WeChat Moments.” Within the CAPS framework, such behavior can be understood as the natural behavioral output of high-arousal positive emotional units, such as collective pride and excitement, activated by specific situational cues. In this sense, triggered behavior functions as a release mechanism for emotional energy, and its intensity and immediacy vividly reflect the peak activation of the cognitive-emotional system in the event context.

Habitual behavior, by contrast, marks the gradual transformation of emotional energy into stable behavioral patterns. When residents participate continuously and repeatedly derive satisfaction from emotional units such as social belonging and self-realization, the initial passion associated with the event gradually develops into a relatively stable emotional-behavioral linkage. Event-related practices evolve from isolated experiences into recurring components of everyday life and may even become an important dimension of personal identity. For example, some residents have shifted from being occasional spectators to regular exercisers or long-term community volunteers. This process is consistent with the CAPS proposition that repeated behavioral outputs can gradually shape new behavioral scripts, which in turn become relatively stable internal contexts capable of reactivating positive emotional experiences. Habitual behavior thus indicates that residents’ well-being is no longer dependent solely on the high-intensity stimulation of specific events, but has instead been internalized into sustainable psychological-behavioral habits and everyday lifestyles.

More importantly, behavior continuously shapes and reinforces residents’ well-being through feedback loops. Together, triggered behavior and habitual behavior form the essential feedback component of the CAPS model and sustain the ongoing operation of the system. Triggered behavior, such as word-of-mouth promotion, amplifies the event’s positive social evaluation and directly reinforces cognitive-affective units associated with hometown identity, thereby strengthening residents’ pride and attachment. Habitual behavior, such as regular exercise, reinforces emotional units related to self-efficacy and social connection by generating tangible health benefits and stronger interpersonal ties. At the same time, the experiences and reflections accumulated through behavioral practice also update residents’ cognitive structures, for example by deepening their understanding of health, participation, and community relationships. In this way, rural sporting events, through a spiraling cycle of local context activation → cognitive-emotional interaction → behavioral output → new situational feedback, systematically promote the transformation of residents’ well-being from short-term emergence to sustained consolidation and eventual deep internalization.

These findings both confirm and extend existing research on sporting events and residents’ well-being. Previous studies have shown that sporting events can promote positive emotions, life satisfaction, and related forms of well-being, but most have focused on urban contexts and mega-events. By contrast, the present study shows that in rural contexts, the influence of sporting events on residents’ well-being unfolds through a dynamic process involving local context, cognitive-emotional interaction, and behavioral reinforcement. It also highlights the contextual specificity of rural sporting events, which are deeply embedded in local memory, kinship networks, festive rituals, and collective identity. Finally, the findings underscore the theoretical importance of behavioral feedback by showing that behavior is not only an outcome of cognition and emotion, but also a mechanism through which residents’ well-being is reinforced and gradually internalized. In this respect, the study not only confirms the explanatory value of CAPS theory, but also extends it by showing how context, cognition, emotion, and behavior interact recursively in the sustained formation of residents’ well-being in rural socio-cultural settings.

Conclusion

Rooted in the socio-cultural context of rural China and centered on residents’ well-being, this study employs grounded theory to uncover the pathways through which rural sporting events influence residents’ well-being and to construct a theoretical model of “local context–cognitive-emotional interaction–behavioral feedback.” In doing so, it addresses a theoretical gap in existing research on the relationship between rural sporting events and residents’ well-being. The findings indicate that rural sporting events effectively activate and reinforce residents’ positive emotional experiences by systematically awakening local attachment and collective memory through event context, personal context, and activity context. In this way, the study provides important theoretical support for understanding how sporting events contribute to the well-being of rural residents.

At the theoretical level, this study innovatively applies CAPS theory to the field of rural sport and well-being research, thereby extending the explanatory scope of CAPS theory within sports sociology and rural studies. More specifically, the study reveals the internal logic through which rural sporting events enhance residents’ well-being at both the micro-psychological level and the meso-level of social interaction. By offering a qualitative interpretation grounded in local experience, it contributes to both academic discussion and practical understanding in this field.

Limitations and future directions

This study constructs a theoretical model based on a small-scale, in-depth interview approach. However, several limitations should be acknowledged with regard to sample selection, research design, and theory construction. First, in order to obtain rich and detailed narratives, the sampling criteria required participants to have a relatively high level of interest in sport and active involvement in local events. Although this helped the study address its research questions more effectively, it may also have introduced a sample bias toward residents with relatively positive sport-related experiences, and may not fully capture the perspectives of those with neutral or negative attitudes, thereby affecting the explanatory scope of the model across different groups. Second, all participants in this study were drawn from Guizhou Province. Although diversity within Taijiang County was taken into account as much as possible, substantial regional differences exist across rural China in terms of cultural traditions, economic development, and sport resources. Therefore, the participants and conclusions of this study have limited representativeness for rural residents in other regions of the country. Third, as an exploratory study, this research primarily adopts qualitative methods aimed at identifying key phenomena and constructing an explanatory theoretical model, rather than establishing strict causal relationships. The pathway of “local context–cognition–emotion–behavior” proposed in the model should therefore be understood as a relational explanation derived from participants’ narratives and the researchers’ theoretical interpretation. Its causal direction and strength require further examination through large-scale quantitative research.

Despite these limitations, this study still offers valuable preliminary insights into the mechanisms through which rural sporting events influence the well-being of rural residents and provides a foundation for future research. Subsequent studies may extend this line of inquiry in at least three directions. First, future research may adopt large-scale, cross-regional quantitative designs to empirically test the pathways identified in this study and to strengthen causal inference while controlling for relevant variables. Second, cross-regional and cross-cultural comparative research may be conducted to examine the differential mechanisms and boundary conditions through which sporting events influence residents’ well-being in different socio-economic and cultural contexts. Third, future studies may further integrate perspectives from rural governance and community development theory in order to better explain the role of sporting events in promoting social integration, cultural identity, and endogenous rural development, thereby enriching the theoretical framework at the intersection of sport and social development.
