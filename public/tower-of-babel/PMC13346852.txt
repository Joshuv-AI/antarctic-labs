Factors associated with successful removable partial denture treatment: an analysis of clinical outcomes and post-insertion events
Authors: Navodika Yaparathna, Ruvini Hattiarachchi, Iresha Udayamalee, Chamilka Bandaranayake, Santosh Kumar Tadakamadla, Steve An, Menaka Abuzar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PURPOSEThe purpose of this research was to evaluate the clinical, functional, and esthetic outcomes of removable partial dentures (RPDs), as well as post-insertion events, and to identify the potential factors and their multifactorial interactions that influence overall RPD success, using real-world, evidence-based support better evidencebased clinical practice in RPD treatment.MATERIALS AND METHODSA retrospective analysis was performed using data from a university dental clinic’s electronic patient management system. Ten outcomes and 23 variables related to patient, oral status, denture, and clinical process were assessed using bivariate tests and logistic regression.RESULTSThe study included 317 patients aged 34 – 90 years (mean age 69.7), with most patients aged 56 – 75 years (53.63%). Significant predictors of RPD success included the arch receiving the prosthesis, the opposing arch configuration, and the number of post-insertion visits. Single-arch dentures showed higher success. Lower RPDs opposed by complete maxillary dentures and cases involving RPDs in both arches showed reduced success. Maxillary RPD success decreased as the number of missing maxillary teeth increased and more pre-insertion visits were required. The Kennedy classification influenced outcomes only in the maxilla, with Class II and III demonstrating higher success than Class I. No other assessed variables showed significant associations, although descriptive patterns suggested variation across age groups.CONCLUSIONThese findings highlight the multifactorial nature of RPD success, the predictive value of post-insertion events, and the need to assess the maxilla and mandible both separately and together, as different factors influence success in each arch.

INTRODUCTION

Removable partial dentures (RPDs) remain a widely used treatment modality for replacing missing teeth and associated structures including alveolar ridges that have undergone post extraction resorption.123 Despite advancements in fixed prosthodontics including implantology, RPDs continue to represent the most cost-effective and accessible solution for managing partial edentulism, particularly for individuals with financial limitations.4 Additionally, RPDs offer a conservative alternative by avoiding the biomechanical, surgical, and irreversible risks sometimes associated with implants and fixed prostheses.345

With improvements in preventive and restorative dental care, a demographic shift from complete to partial edentulism has been observed among patients seeking prosthodontic rehabilitation. The prevalence of RPD use has also increased alongside population aging and longer life expectancy.6 According to the 2019 Australian National Oral Health Survey, approximately 25% of adults commence wearing RPDs before the age of 75, and this figure rises to nearly 50% among individuals older than 75 years.7 Comparable trends have been reported within Queensland, Australia, where the current study population resides.7

Successful rehabilitation of partial edentulism requires restoring mastication, speech, esthetics, and, importantly, oral health-related quality of life (OHRQoL).35891011 Longterm patient acceptance is influenced by how well the prosthesis harmonizes with oral and facial tissues, including the shade and form of the denture teeth and base.1213 Considerable clinical time, resources, and cost may be wasted if a patient ultimately discontinues wearing their denture.

Although many studies have explored factors associated with RPD outcomes, most have focused on a relatively narrow set of variables.14 Research to date has predominantly examined patient satisfaction, largely through patient-related outcome measures (PROMs), and satisfaction has consistently been shown to be a complex, multifactorial construct.15161718 A recent systematic review identified only fifteen studies on RPD-related PROMs, most of which were methodologically weak, highlighting a significant evidence gap and the need for more robust investigations.15 Similarly, another review reported on the limitations of available evidence supporting associations between PROMs and biological or technical RPD parameters.16

While satisfaction assesses PROMs, RPD success requires satisfactory outcomes from both the patient’s and the clinician’s perspective, incorporating PROMs as well as clinical, technical, biomechanical, functional, and esthetic criteria. Despite this distinction, the literature has rarely examined RPD success as a combined clinician and patient outcome14 and has not evaluated the complex interplay of clinical, prosthetic, and patient-related variables contributing to true treatment success.

In addition, although adaptation to a new RPD can be challenging, especially during the early adjustment phase, post-insertion care remains significantly understudied. Only one published study has examined post-insertion adjustments of RPDs, and it was limited to metal based RPDs with a small sample size.19 Importantly, no published research has assessed immediate post-insertion events or explored how multiple interrelated factors collectively influence overall RPD success.

Given these gaps and the discrepancy often observed between clinician rated and patient reported outcomes, there is a clear need for real-world evidence evaluating the multifactorial determinants of RPD success, not just satisfaction. According to the knowledge of the authors, the factors associated with the success of newly provided RPDs, including detailed post-insertion events, have not been reported previously. Therefore, the current retrospective study was designed to provide clinically meaningful insights by comprehensively evaluating clinical records of patients receiving RPDs. This study aimed to:

1. Evaluate the clinical, functional, and esthetic outcomes and post-insertion events of RPDs.2. Assess the association between potential influencing factors and overall RPD success.3. Examine the multifactorial interplay among factors influencing overall RPD success.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The current retrospective study involved reviewing patients’ records from a university dental clinic in Australia. Data from the electronic patient management system (Titanium Solutions; Auckland, New Zealand) were used with administrative and ethical approval from the University Human Research Ethics Committee (GU Ref No: 2022/883).

The university’s patient management system maintains comprehensive records, including patient demographics, clinical notes, radiographs, clinical photographs, and dental charts for all individuals treated at the clinic. Detailed clinical note taking and review appointments are mandatory within this clinical setting. Relevant data were extracted through detailed review of patient records by an experienced clinician, with clarifications provided by a second clinician to ensure accuracy. Clinical notes were examined thoroughly to capture both patient-reported concerns and operator-documented complaints at each appointment, from the initial denture consultation through to the three-month follow-up period. Data were extracted into a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet (Version 16.100.2, Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA) that was specifically developed for this study. The data collection template was prepared through consensus among the research team (which included experienced clinicians in prosthodontics), following a targeted literature review to identify the most relevant factors to include in the analysis. In the absence of validated RPD-specific success criteria, treatment success was operationally defined as a composite outcome encompassing clinician-assessed clinical, functional, and esthetic parameters alongside prosthesis performance and patient-reported outcomes. While this does not constitute a formally validated success instrument, it represents a pragmatic, clinically grounded framework appropriate for retrospective data analysis in this study.

Records of all patients who received RPDs fabricated by undergraduate dental students at the university clinic between February 2021 and November 2022 were reviewed. Patients presenting with temporomandibular disorders, a history of radiotherapy to the head and neck region, severe xerostomia, or disabilities that could impair RPD maintenance such as advanced arthritis, severe depression, or psychological disorders were excluded. Cases involving refabrication of dentures within the warranty period and immediate dentures were also excluded.

To assess the clinical, functional, and esthetic outcomes of RPDs, following ten parameters were identified for data extraction from patient records: retention of maxillary RPD (URPD), stability of URPD, retention of mandibular RPD (LRPD), stability of LRPD, chewing ability, speaking ability, satisfaction with the color of denture teeth, size of denture teeth, color of denture base and denture failure indicating refabrication due to major errors. Overall, RPD success was determined using these outcome measures, based on comprehensive review of clinicians’ notes and thorough assessment of patient feedback and perspectives recorded by the clinician. RPDs were classified as successful if all parameters were rated satisfactory by both clinician and patient.20 If any parameter was recorded as unsatisfactory by either party, the RPD was classified as unsuccessful. Retention, stability, support, and occlusion were assessed using standard clinical examination techniques. Chewing ability was evaluated from the patient’s perspective, based on their experience of masticatory function during their normal daily diet. Clinical records were systematically reviewed to extract and classify all outcome parameters. If one variable was missing, overall satisfaction was based on the remaining parameters; cases with two or more missing variables were excluded from the final analysis of denture success.

To evaluate the impact of potential factors on the above identified denture outcomes and to evaluate post-insertion complications, 23 factors were considered under four categories (patient-related, oral status-related, denture-related and clinical process-related) (Table 1).

A pilot study (n = 15) was conducted to evaluate the adequacy and feasibility of the data extraction instrument and to identify limitations in the data collection strategy. The pilot sample was independent and did not overlap with the primary study cohort. During data collection, clinical notes were reviewed in detail by the clinician responsible for data extraction. During data cleaning, cases with substantial missing data across key variables were excluded.

Descriptive and inferential statistical analyses were performed using Stata Statistical Software (Release 19. StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA) to explore the association of multiple patient-related, oral status-related, denture-related and clinical process-associated factors with denture success. Chi-square test of independence was used for categorical variables, while two sample t-test was applied to continuous variables when exploring their association with denture success. Logistic regression was employed to evaluate the multifactorial interplay among factors influencing overall RPD success. Variables included in the multivariable regression models were selected based on statistical significance in bivariate analyses with age and gender retained as priori covariates. In the logistic regression analysis, adjusted estimates were obtained to control the effect of other variables and regression analyses were conducted for the overall cohort along with maxillary and mandibular subgroups separately. Three distinct models were developed to enhance analytical precision: Model 1, which included the overall sample; Model 2, which focused on URPDs; and Model 3, which examined LRPDs. This approach enabled both comprehensive and arch-specific analyses.

RESULTS

A total of 317 patients were included in the study. Participants ranged in age from 34 to 90 years, with a mean age of 69.7 years, and the most common age group seeking RPD treatment was 56 – 75 years (53.63%). The sample demonstrated a balanced gender distribution, comprising 157 males (49.53%) and 160 females (50.47%). Nearly all patients (98.11%) received financial support for their dentures through Queensland Health, while only six patients (1.89%) received private care. Majority of patients resided in urban areas.

Regarding systemic health, most patients (222; 70.25%) reported three or fewer medical conditions, while 76 (24.05%) presented with no comorbidities and 18 (5.70%) had more than three. Hypertension and cardiovascular disease were the most common conditions, followed by diabetes. Additional comorbidities such as depression, respiratory disease, and osteoporosis were noted. Polypharmacy was present in 84 patients (26.58%), while 232 patients (73.42%) were not classified as polypharmacy users.

Oral health assessments showed considerable variability at baseline. Patients presented with 1 to 27 natural teeth, with a mean of 15.5 teeth (± 6.21). The mean number of missing teeth in the maxilla was 7 (± 4.30), whereas the mandible had a mean of 5 missing teeth (± 2.86). Notably, almost 90% of the patients who requested LRPDs were missing at least 9 mandibular teeth and 14 maxillary teeth. The number of carious teeth at presentation ranged from 0 to 10 (mean = 2.2 ± 2.05), and the number of periodontally involved teeth ranged from 0 to 20 (mean = 4.4 ± 3.42). Data relating to social habits indicated the presence of smoking, alcohol consumption, combined alcohol-and-smoking behaviors, and illicit drug use.

In total, 462 RPDs were delivered (221- maxillary and 241 - mandibular). Among these, 145 patients received both URPDs and LRPDs, 74 received only a URPD, and 38 received only a LRPD. Two URPDs were opposed by mandibular complete dentures (LCDs), while 58 LRPDs were opposed by maxillary complete dentures (UCDs). Most patients (276; 87.03%) had previous denture experience, whereas 41 patients (12.97%) were first-time denture wearers. Among URPD recipients, 11.82% were new denture users, while 10.37% of LRPD recipients were first-time wearers. The distribution of edentulous areas showed notable differences between arches (Table 2). In the maxilla, the most common classification was Kennedy Class III, (50.68%) whilst in the mandible, Kennedy Class I (40.25%).

Clinical workflow data indicated that most dentures were constructed over five visits (n = 230), with fewer patients requiring fewer than five (n = 27) or more than five (n = 60) appointments. The wax try-in stage was the most repeated step in the fabrication process.

Post-insertion denture review appointments ranged from 0 to 8. 13 patients did not come for any review appointments and stated their satisfaction over the phone, while 111 patients required one visit, 106 required two, 51 required three, 26 required four, and a ten required five or more than five visits. Some patients did not have any complaint (n = 96) during post-insertion stages. Soreness and ulcers were the most common complaints (n = 178). Looseness of denture (n = 78), food trapping (n = 48), occlusal imbalance (n = 32), excessive tightness and pressure (n = 26), cheek biting (n = 24), bulkiness (n = 8), and gagging (n = 5) were the other complaints found isolated or in combination. Most of the dentures needed minor adjustments to the teeth and denture bases, and few needed tightening and loosening of clasps. Only three patients needed relining. Comparative analysis of URPDs and LRPDs demonstrated similar patterns in the distribution of follow-up appointments.

Within the sample of 317, 15 patients (11 URPDs and 11 LRPDs) required remaking due to various clinical or technical issues. Among these, seven patients had RPDs in both the upper and lower arches, while four had only a maxillary denture and the other four had only a mandibular denture.

Tables 3A, 3B, and 3C show association of multiple factors and RPD success. Age, gender, polypharmacy and the number of medical conditions showed no significant influence on RPD success (Table 3A). The total number of teeth present at baseline and the number of missing maxillary teeth significantly affected the RPD success (Table 3B). Mandibularonly and maxillaryonly RPD wearers had significantly higher success rates. Patients receiving both arches showed more dissatisfaction and combined configurations such as maxillary RPD + LCD and LRPD + UCD showed less favorable success rates. Previous RPD experience was not associated with success (Table 3C).

All clinical and functional outcomes individually demonstrated excellent results (Table 4). When comparing URPDs and LRPDs, URPDs consistently showed higher success rates across most parameters, with the exception of stability, which was similar in both arches. The most notable difference between arches was retention, with URPDs exhibiting higher retention than LRPDs.

Further analysis of the overall functional, esthetic, and clinical outcomes showed that 225 patients (70.98%) achieved successful results across all measures. In contrast, 92 patients experienced unsuccessful outcomes in one or more of the outcomes assessed in this study.

Tables 5A, 5B and 5C show the logistic regression analysis of factors determining the successful treatment outcome of dentures. In the overall model (N = 317), prosthesis configuration and the number of post-insertion review visits emerged as significant predictors of overall treatment success. Patients who received RPDs in both arches demonstrated significantly lower odds of satisfaction (OR = 0.17, P = .011), as did those with a LRPD opposing a complete maxillary denture (OR = 0.11, P = .016). Additionally, each additional post-insertion appointment was associated with reduced odds of satisfaction (OR = 0.39, P < .001). No significant associations were found for gender, age category, denture adjustments, previous RPD experience, total number of teeth, or number of pre-insertion visits (all P > .05) (Table 5A).

In the maxillary RPD model (URPD subgroup; N = 221), both pre-insertion visits (OR = 0.39, P = .022) and post-insertion visits (OR = 0.28, P < .001) remained significant negative predictors of success. Kennedy classification demonstrated a noteworthy effect: Kennedy Class II (OR = 5.80, P = .016) and Class III (OR = 4.80, P = .024) were associated with substantially higher odds of success compared with the reference classification, which was class I. Maxillary missing teeth, RPD material, demographic factors, and all the other variables showed no significant associations (Table 5B).

In the LRPD model (LRPD subgroup; N = 241), post-insertion visits were again the only consistent predictor, with increased visit frequency reducing the odds of satisfaction (OR = 0.44, P < .001). No other variables—including age, gender, polypharmacy, mandibular Kennedy class, RPD material, missing teeth, number of teeth present, or prior RPD experience demonstrated statistically significant associations (Table 5C).

Across all models, the most robust and consistent finding was that a greater number of post-insertion review appointments were strongly associated with lower levels of success.

DISCUSSION

Despite the widespread use of removable partial dentures (RPDs), the evidence identifying predictors of treatment success remains limited. Strengthening this evidence base is important to minimize failures, particularly given the extensive reliance on this treatment modality. The present study helps address this gap by providing an overview of factors associated with satisfactory outcomes among patients receiving newly fabricated RPDs at Australian university dental clinic. Importantly, this study offers realworld evidence and is, to our knowledge, the first to examine immediate post-insertion events in both maxillary and mandibular RPDs.

The American College of Prosthodontists’ Parameters of Care for the Specialty of Prosthodontics21 defines successful treatment for the partially dentate patient as a “shared outcome achieved through collaboration between the prosthodontist and the patient”. Despite a thorough literature search, studies directly assessing RPD success were found to be scarce, and no consensus-based standardized RPD specific success criteria were identified.

In contrast, a limited body of literature on complete denture success has adopted a multidimensional approach. Diehl et al.22 proposed an explanatory model incorporating multiple contributing factors, while another study evaluated both patient- and prosthesis-related variables.23 Similarly, Roessler24 described denture success as a combined measure incorporating both patient and practitioner perspectives. A systematic review by Papaspyridakos et al.25 further emphasized that success of implants should be assessed across multiple domains, including prosthetic complications, biological and technical outcomes, functional and esthetic performance, and patient satisfaction, highlighting the need for comprehensive success criteria in prosthodontic care. Furthermore, several studies have examined multiple factors influencing RPD satisfaction.

In the absence of RPD specific criteria, the factors assessed in this study were therefore derived from these established frameworks on RPD satisfaction, complete dentures and implant prosthodontics, reflecting the recognition that RPD success remains an evolving concept that warrants further systematic investigation.21

Overall, 70.98% of patients achieved a successful clinical, functional and esthetic outcomes, a rate consistent with those reported in a recent systematic review, which found satisfaction levels between 50% and 81%.16 The literature has reported conflicting findings regarding the influence of demographic factors such as age,2627 gender,28 medical status, medication use, polypharmacy, and income, on RPD satisfaction.6172930 In the present study, none of the sociodemographic or systemic factors assessed were significant determinants of RPD success. However, descriptive patterns suggested potentially higher success rate among individuals aged 56 – 75 years, while younger adults tended to exhibit lower success. Although not statistically significant, this trend may merit further investigation with larger samples or more granular age categories.

Among the assessed denture-related factors, one of the strongest findings was the significant influence of RPD and the opposing arch configuration on treatment success. Patients who received only one RPD, regardless of arch, reported higher success rates compared with those who received RPDs in both arches. Notably, LRPDs opposed by UCDs demonstrated the lowest success rate. This combination has been previously examined in only one study, which assessed only masticatory efficiency and concluded that it does not improve masticatory performance but does enhance oral health-related quality of life (OHRQoL).31 The present findings add a different dimension to the understanding of this clinical scenario by highlighting its broader clinical relevance. These results reinforce the need for strategic treatment planning, thorough evaluation of occlusal relationships, and realistic expectation setting for patients when a LRPD opposes a complete maxillary denture. Such cases may require additional biomechanical considerations and a more structured follow up approach to support optimal outcomes.

Other denture-related factors, such as denture base material and previous denture experience, did not influence success in this cohort, which contrasts with earlier studies suggesting that prior familiarity with dentures may facilitate adaptation.1332 Although the literature generally reports a preference for metalbased RPDs,19 no significant differences emerged across different denture base materials in relation to RPD success in the present study, a finding consistent with the systematic review by Almufleh et al.15 Furthermore, while previous studies have reported improved comfort and appearance with flexible denture bases, these trends were not observed here, likely due to the small number of flexible RPDs included in the sample.

The influence of oral status on RPD success also revealed important insights. While previous work13 reported that the number of missing teeth does not predict denture satisfaction, the bivariate findings in this study suggested that a greater number of remaining teeth was associated with higher success. These associations, however, lost significance in the multivariable regression model, indicating that tooth number alone is not an independent predictor once all the other factors are accounted for. However, according to archwise regression analysis, greater maxillary tooth loss corresponded with lower success. This suggests that dentition status may exert an indirect or confounded influence that warrants further investigation in future controlled studies.

The number of carious or periodontally compromised teeth at the initial visit was not associated with treatment success. Given that RPDs in this university setting were provided only after caries and periodontal conditions were stabilized, this finding suggests that baseline oral disease does not adversely influence final outcomes when appropriate disease control is achieved beforehand. This is an area not explored in previous RPD research.

This study also addressed an underexplored area by examining the relationship between Kennedy classification and RPD success. Although the distribution of Kennedy Classes I, II and III was similar across arches, maxillary RPDs showed significantly higher success in Class II and Class III compared with Class I. LRPDs showed no significant differences across classes. This finding contrasts with existing literature that describes mandibular Kennedy ClassI as the least favorable due to biomechanical factors.4 This finding highlights the need for clinicians to exercise additional care when managing patients with different maxillary Kennedy classifications, from treatment planning and exoectation setting to post-insertion evaluation of technical and biomechanical factors.

RPD fabrication requires multiple clinical stages. In this cohort, patients needed an average of five visits for fabrication, with the wax tryin stage being the most commonly repeated clinical step. The overall number of pre-insertion visits was not associated with success; however, in maxillary RPDs, a higher number of pre-insertion visits predicted lower success. This, together with the finding that the trialdenture stage was the most frequently repeated appointment, highlights the importance of thoroughly reassessing the prosthesis whenever a patient expresses dissatisfaction at this stage. In such cases, it is essential for the clinician to reevaluate whether the denture teeth have been arranged in accordance with established prosthodontic principles including intraarch and interarch relationships, occlusal scheme, esthetics, and biomechanical considerations, before proceeding to final processing.

Post-insertion review appointments for RPDs have been minimally explored in the literature. Only one previous study, using a questionnaire based approach with a smaller sample size, emphasized the importance of follow-up appointments to optimize patient outcomes.33 One of the most noteworthy findings of our study was that a greater number of post-insertion review appointments were consistently and strongly associated with lower success across the overall, maxillary, and mandibular models. This pattern suggests that cases requiring numerous post-insertion adjustments likely reflect underlying technical challenges or adaptation difficulties that subsequently reduce RPD success. This previously underexplored finding will provide valuable insights for clinical practice by highlighting the importance of post-insertion appointments and enabling clinicians to anticipate major adjustments or potential failures when patients remain dissatisfied after several minor adjustments to their RPDs.

The most frequently reported post-insertion issues in this cohort were soreness and ulceration needing minor denture adjustments, followed by cheek biting and perceived occlusal imbalance needing minor adjustments to the teeth. Looseness and excessive tightness or pressure from the denture was another commonly occurred complaint. Even if some dentures needed multiple adjustments, the final denture was successful. Collectively, these findings reflect the functional and biomechanical challenges that commonly arise as patients adapt to new prostheses and increased follow-up commonly represents the need to manage discomfort, instability, or functional concerns.

Although several additional factors, such as number of teeth present and retention of URPDs showed significant associations with success rates in bivariate analyses, these associations did not persist in the multivariable model. Only the variables such as the arch RPD was provided (single arch or both arches), configuration of the opposing arch, and the number of post-insertion visits remained significant predictors in the adjusted analyses throughout. Success of URPDs were associated with few additional variables: Kennedy classification, pre-insertion visits and a number of missing teeth. The differences between unadjusted and adjusted findings highlight the multifactorial and complex nature of RPD success, where the influence of individual factors may diminish when broader patient-related, oral status-related, denture-related and clinical process-related variables are considered together. This underscores the importance of interpreting RPD success through a multifaceted lens rather than focusing on isolated factors. Furthermore, these in-depth upper and lower archwise analyses imply the importance of assessing the arches differently.

A major strength of this study is the comprehensive assessment of 33 potential variables (10 outcomes and 23 factors) using realworld clinical data. Several variables examined here such as detailed post-insertion events, the effect of opposing arch configuration, and arch specific Kennedy classification pattern, have not been previously addressed in the literature. These findings provide strong evidence with direct clinical implications for planning and designing, patient education, and post-insertion management.

Despite these strengths, the study has limitations. Denture design features (e.g., major connector type, clasp design, and support elements) and occlusal schemes were not assessed, although they may influence comfort, biomechanics, and long-term performance. These parameters warrant investigation in future prospective studies. Although several factors demonstrated statistically significant associations, the regression analyses showed wide confidence intervals, reflecting small sample sizes within certain subgroups, particularly patients with Kennedy Class IV and who received both URPD and LCD. Additionally, the dentures were fabricated in a university student clinic, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Future studies employing larger sample sizes across experienced clinical settings and incorporating detailed prosthesis design variables are recommended to strengthen the evidence base and enhance external validity. A fundamental challenge in evaluating RPD treatment outcomes is the absence of consensus-based, RPD-specific success criteria, which represents an inherent limitation of the present study. The operational definition employed, incorporating clinician-assessed parameters, patient reported outcomes, and RPD performance alongside with post-insertion events, was informed by established outcome frameworks developed for complete dentures and implant prosthodontics, where multidimensional success models have been more systematically defined. The absence of a validated RPD-specific instrument inevitably limits direct comparability with future studies. Therefore, development and validation of standardized RPD success criteria remain a priority. Future research should also explore the qualitative distinction between clinical success and patient satisfaction, as these constructs do not always align. Understanding why a prosthesis may be clinically successful yet not satisfactory to the patient or vice versa may reveal important nuanced factors that are not captured through quantitative metrics alone. Such insights could help reduce unnecessary remakes, prevent inefficient use of clinical resources, and ultimately improve patient-centered care in prosthodontics.

CONCLUSION

The arch in which the RPD was provided, the configuration of the opposing arch, and the number of post-insertion visits have a significant association with RPD success. Dentures provided in a single arch were more successful than dentures provided in both arches, and LRPDs opposed by complete maxillary dentures showed notably lower success. However, LRPDs have less retention compared to URPDs.

Success of URPDs was also influenced by additional factors such as Kennedy classification, number of missing teeth, and number of pre-insertion visits. Kennedy Class II and Class III cases showed better outcomes compared with Kennedy Class I. The number of missing teeth, as well as both pre-insertion and post-insertion visits, demonstrated an inverse relationship with the success of URPDs.

None of the sociodemographic or systemic factors assessed in this study showed any influence individually, on the final success of RPD treatment. These observations collectively emphasized the complex and multifactorial nature of RPD success.

These findings provide valuable guidance for clinical practice, especially since the study used real life evidence and included an adequate sample size. However, the authors recommend that future work includes qualitative assessment and larger data sets to further strengthen the evidence and to better understand the reasons behind the observed patterns.
