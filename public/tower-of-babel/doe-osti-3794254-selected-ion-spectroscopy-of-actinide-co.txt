 ACCOMPLISHMENTS
1. What are the major goals of the project?

The goal of these studies is an increased understanding of the fundamental interactions and electronic structure involved
in actinide metal bonding, coordination and solvation. Actinide metal and metal oxide cation-molecular complexes are
studied in the gas phase to investigate their properties without the complicating influences of bulk solvents, counterions,
or mixtures with other metal components. Cation-molecular complexes of the form Mn+(L)y, where M = U or Th in singly-
or doubly-charged states, and L = small molecules such as H2O, CO, N2, CO2, O2, CH3CN, or benzene are produced in a
molecular beam by pulsed laser vaporization of solid metal targets. Similar methods are used to produce metal oxide
complexes. Complexes containing a metal or oxide core ion with a specific number of ligand or solvent molecules are
cooled by a supersonic expansion, size-selected in a time-of-flight mass spectrometer, and studied with infrared or UV-
visible laser photodissociation spectroscopy. The vibrational spectra reveal the shifts that occur for ligand/solvent
vibrations upon binding to these metals and how these vary with the charge state, the number of ligands or solvent
molecules present, the geometric and electronic structures of complexes, and the possible occurrence of ligand reactions
mediated by the metal center. UV-visible spectra reveal the excited states of the same kinds of complexes and their
photodissociation thresholds. Additional experiments employ photofragment imaging technology to further investigate
cation-molecular bond energies and photodissociation dynamics. The experimental work is complemented by
computational chemistry, with careful attention to relativistic and spin-orbit effects. Benchmark experimental data can
be used to validate and refine the capabilities of theory for these hard-to-study actinide metal systems.

2. What was accomplished under these goals?
Research in our lab has included three main areas, each using a different specialized molecular beam instrument. To
explore the structures of actinide ion complexes with small-molecule ligands, we use infrared laser photodissociation
spectroscopy of mass-selected ions. To explore excited electronic states of these systems, we use UV-visible laser
photodissociation spectroscopy of mass-selected ions. In certain systems, these experiments provide bond dissociation
energies through the determination of photodissociation thresholds. Bond dissociation energies are also determined in a
third experiment, where photofragment imaging of mass-selected ions is employed to investigate their dissociation
dynamics. All of these experiments are complemented by computational quantum chemistry calculations.

Infrared spectroscopy experiments investigated U+(N2)n complexes in the N−N stretching region, finding frequencies
shifted lower than the corresponding value for the isolated N2 molecule. The coordination was completed with eight
ligands in a square antiprism structure. Similar experiments were conducted on U+(CO2)n, UO+(CO2)n and UO2+(CO2)n
complexes in the region of the asymmetric stretch of CO2. In the first data for doubly charged ions, UO2+ was also found
to have a coordination number of eight ligands, but with a lower symmetry structure.

Electronic spectroscopy experiments were conducted on U+, UO+ and UO2 + complexes with benzene. Each of the metal
ions were found to have a quasi-continuous density of electronic states and therefore no discrete electronic or vibrational
structure could be detected. Instead, these spectra were characterized by a sudden onset of photodissociation signal
follow by a continuous level of signal at higher energies. The threshold energies were interpreted to derive the bond
energies for these complexes, which represent the first data for cation- bonding interactions in these systems. The bond
energies determined [U+−(benzene): 42.5  0.3 kcal/mol; UO+−(benzene): 41.0  0.3 kcal/mol; UO 2+−(benzene): 39.7  0.3
kcal/mol] are comparable to those of transition metal ion-benzene complexes. Computational studies at the DFT/B3LYP
level complement the experiments, predicting dissociation energies in reasonably good agreement with the experiments.
Experiments and theory agree that the U+(benzene) complex is more strongly bound than its corresponding oxide ions.
This new thermochemistry on actinide bonding should stimulate higher-level computational studies on these systems.

Additional work has continued to develop our capabilities in photofragment velocity-map imaging on mass-selected ion
beams. Using the instrument developed with DOE funding, we have studied a variety of metal cation-molecule complexes
as well as carbon atom clusters. Photofragment imaging experiments on zinc cation complexes with methanol provided
new data on the dissociation energy of this system. Most interestingly, we found unexpected photodynamics for the
dissociation of carbon cluster cations. The structures of carbon cluster ions were known from previous measurements of
ion mobility. Smaller cluster ions (<10 atoms) have linear structures, whereas larger ones (10−20 atoms) have monocyclic
ring structures. We found that all of these cluster ions required multiple photons for photodissociation. Linear species
exhibited small amounts of kinetic energy release upon photodissociation, whereas cyclic species produced much greater
amounts. Surprisingly, the larger clusters were more efficient at absorbing multiple photons and they produced larger and
larger amounts of kinetic energy. This was interpreted to arise at least partially from the release of steric ring strain when
dissociation occurred.

3. What opportunities for training and professional development has the project provided?

Seven graduate students were supported by this project at different times. They each have received technical training in
the areas of vacuum science, optics, lasers, infrared and UV-visible spectroscopy, mass spectrometry, ion imaging and
computational chemistry. The also received instruction in the preparation of manuscripts for publication and the
preparation of presentations for conferences. Several students presented papers on this work at the International
Symposium on Molecular Spectroscopy at the University of Illinois, in June every year, and two students presented this
work at the Gordon Research Conference on Gaseous Ion Chemistry in Ventura, CA in February 2023. Two additional
students presented posters at the 37th International Symposium on Free Radicals, in Snowbird, UT, in August of 2025. Two
undergraduate research students also took part in this research.

4. How have the results been disseminated to communities of interest?

This research has been published in several peer-reviewed publications in journals such as the Journal of Chemical
Physics, the Journal of Physical Chemistry A and the Journal of Physical Chemistry Letters. Papers were presented by the
graduate students at the International Symposium on Molecular Spectroscopy at the University of Illinois, at the Gordon
Research Conference on Gaseous Ion Chemistry in Ventura, CA in February 2023, and at the 37th International Symposium
on Free Radicals, in Snowbird, UT, in August 2025. Details of the new photofragment imaging instrument were
incorporated into a graduate course taught in spring 2023 by Prof. Duncan on Kinetics and Molecular Reaction Dynamics.

 IMPACT
1. What is the impact on the development of the principal discipline(s) of the project?

Theory on actinide molecules and their solvation phenomena is an especially challenging area, but one which is essential
for the simulations of the chemical species and environmental effects of nuclear waste disposal. The effects of d
electrons on chemical reactivity, as well as the effects of strong spin-orbit interactions and relativity, lie at the frontier of
what present levels of theory can handle. Our work provides benchmark numbers on the structures, coordination numbers
and bond energies of actinide systems with which to guide the further development of theory. The infrared spectroscopy
measurements provide the structures of metal-ligand complexes and their electronic states, while the tunable UV-visible
laser photodissociation thresholds provide metal-ligand bond energies. We use the benchmark experimental data and
presently available computational methods to evaluate the current capabilities of theory and to focus the attention of
theoretical experts on specific problem areas. In addition to the actinide chemistry, a significant part of our work has
been on the development and application of a photofragment imaging instrument for mass-selected ions that can be
applied to a wide range of fundamental problems in photochemical dynamics.

2. What is the impact on other disciplines?

The advances in the understanding of actinide chemistry and the theoretical description of it have broad implications for
how simulations are done on radioactive materials that are difficult to handle experimentally. Nuclear waste materials in
solution, such as those in the tanks at the Hanford Nuclear Facility, must be evaluated in light of the best chemical
understanding, which will also guide disposal strategies. Simulations on this chemistry underpin all evaluations and
plans, and these simulations are based on many individual chemical interactions from theory. The refinement of theory
guided by our experiments is a critical piece in this overall puzzle.

3. What is the impact on the development of human resources?

Students from our research group are trained in various aspects of vacuum technology, lasers, optics, spectroscopy, mass
spectrometry, and computational chemistry. These skills make them uniquely qualified for employment in the high-tech
marketplace. Two recent Ph.D. graduates have gone to jobs at Intel (Portland, OR), one has gone to work for Brucker
(Boston), one has taken an assistant professor position at Florida International University, and one has recently accepted a
job at Mohawk Industries. Six graduate students and one undergraduate are presently in the group and are continuing to
develop their skills and experience.

4. What is the impact on physical, institutional, and information resources that form infrastructure?

A new instrument has been constructed several years ago with DOE funds which is employed for photofragment imaging
experiments on mass-selected ions. This remains the only instrument like it world-wide. Continued experiments are
allowing the capabilities and limitations of this instrument to be evaluated.
