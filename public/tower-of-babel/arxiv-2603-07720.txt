Low Mach Number Limit and Convergence Rates for a Compressible Two-Fluid Model with Algebraic Pressure Closure
Yang Li, Mária Lukáčová-Medviďová, Ewelina Zatorska

Abstract
. We study the low Mach number limit for a viscous compressible two-fluid model with algebraic pressure closure in the three-dimensional torus 𝕋 3 \mathbb{T}^{3} . The pressure is determined implicitly through the densities of the two phases, which makes the singular limit substantially more delicate than for models with explicit pressure laws. Working in the framework of local-in-time strong solutions, we prove that, for well-prepared initial data, solutions to the rescaled compressible two-fluid system exist on a time interval independent of the Mach number and converge to the solution of the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations as the Mach number tends to zero. In addition, we establish explicit convergence rates for the densities and the velocity field. The proof relies on uniform high-order energy estimates and a relative energy argument adapted to the implicit structure of the pressure law. These results provide a rigorous justification of the low Mach number limit for the compressible two-fluid model with algebraic pressure closure.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Background

We study the evolution of two immiscible viscous compressible fluids in the three-dimensional periodic domain
\Omega=\mathbb{T}^{3}
for
t\geqslant 0
. The two phases share a common velocity field and are coupled through an algebraic pressure closure. In Eulerian coordinates, the model reads

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}(\alpha_{\pm}\varrho_{\pm})+\operatorname{div}(\alpha_{\pm}\varrho_{\pm}\mathbf{u})=0,\\
\partial_{t}[(\alpha_{+}\varrho_{+}+\alpha_{-}\varrho_{-})\mathbf{u}]+\operatorname{div}[(\alpha_{+}\varrho_{+}+\alpha_{-}\varrho_{-})\mathbf{u}\otimes\mathbf{u}]+\nabla p=\mu\Delta\mathbf{u}+(\mu+\lambda)\nabla\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u},\\
\alpha_{+}+\alpha_{-}=1,\quad\alpha_{\pm}\geqslant 0,\\
p=p_{+}=p_{-}.\end{cases}

(1.1)

Here
\alpha_{\pm}=\alpha_{\pm}(t,x)
denote the volume fractions of the two phases,
\varrho_{\pm}=\varrho_{\pm}(t,x)
their mass densities,
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{u}(t,x)
the common velocity field, and
p
the common pressure. The constants
\mu
and
\lambda
are the shear and bulk viscosity coefficients, respectively.

Following Bresch et al.
[
1
]
, it is convenient to introduce the conservative variables

\displaystyle R=\alpha_{+}\varrho_{+},\quad Q=\alpha_{-}\varrho_{-},\quad Z=\varrho_{+}.

(1.2)

Then system (
1.1
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}R+\operatorname{div}(R\mathbf{u})=0,\\
\partial_{t}Q+\operatorname{div}(Q\mathbf{u})=0,\\
\partial_{t}[(R+Q)\mathbf{u}]+\operatorname{div}[(R+Q)\mathbf{u}\otimes\mathbf{u}]+\nabla p=\mu\Delta\mathbf{u}+(\mu+\lambda)\nabla\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}.\end{cases}

(1.3)

The algebraic pressure closure means that the phase pressures coincide. Assuming both fluids are isentropic, we have

\displaystyle p_{+}=(\varrho_{+})^{\gamma_{+}}=p_{-}=(\varrho_{-})^{\gamma_{-}},\quad\gamma_{\pm}>1.

(1.4)

As a consequence, the common pressure can be expressed as

\displaystyle p(Z)=Z^{\gamma_{+}},

(1.5)

where
Z
is determined implicitly by
(R,Q)
through

\displaystyle\begin{cases}Q=\left(1-\frac{R}{Z}\right)Z^{\gamma},\quad\gamma=\frac{\gamma_{+}}{\gamma_{-}},\\
R\leqslant Z.\end{cases}

(1.6)

Bresch et al.
[
1
]
showed that (
1.6
) uniquely determines
Z
, and so there exists some function
\mathcal{Z}(\cdot,\cdot)
such that
Z=\mathcal{Z}(R,Q)
.

The system (
1.3
)-(
1.6
) has attracted considerable attention in recent years. For semi-stationary flows, Bresch et al.
[
1
]
proved the existence of finite-energy weak solutions in three space dimensions. For viscous compressible two-fluid systems with general pressure laws, including the algebraic closure considered here, Novotný and Pokorný
[
17
]
established the existence global finite-energy weak solutions. Li and Zatorska
[
16
]
obtained a conditional weak–strong uniqueness principle, and this was recently strengthened to an unconditional weak–strong uniqueness result by Li et al.
[
12
]
via a suitable relative entropy inequality. In the inviscid case, Li and Zatorska
[
15
]
proved the existence of infinitely many weak solutions in three dimensions. In one spatial dimension, Li, Sun, and Zatorska
[
14
]
studied global weak solutions and their large-time behavior for large initial data. In the class of strong solutions, Piasecki and Zatorska
[
19
]
proved local well-posedness for large initial data and global well-posedness for small data.

It is also useful to place (
1.3
)-(
1.6
) within the broader literature on compressible two-fluid models. A first important example is the fluid-particle model

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}R+\operatorname{div}(R\mathbf{u})=0,\\
\partial_{t}Q+\operatorname{div}(Q\mathbf{u})=0,\\
\partial_{t}[(R+Q)\mathbf{u}]+\operatorname{div}[(R+Q)\mathbf{u}\otimes\mathbf{u}]+\nabla P(R,Q)=\mu\Delta\mathbf{u}+(\mu+\lambda)\nabla\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u},\end{cases}

(1.7)

with pressure law

\displaystyle P(R,Q)=R^{\gamma}+Q^{\beta},\quad\gamma,\beta\geqslant 1.

(1.8)

For this model, by developing the variable reduction argument, Vasseur, Wen, and Yu
[
20
]
proved the global existence of finite-energy weak solutions under suitable restrictions on
\gamma
and
\beta
,
namely either

\displaystyle\gamma,\beta>\frac{9}{5},\quad\max\left\{\frac{3\gamma}{4},\gamma-1,\frac{3(\gamma+1)}{5}\right\}<\beta<\min\left\{\frac{4\gamma}{3},\gamma+1,\frac{5\gamma}{3}-1\right\},

(1.9)

or

\displaystyle\gamma>\frac{9}{5},\quad\beta\geqslant 1,\quad\frac{1}{c_{0}}R_{0}\leqslant Q_{0}\leqslant c_{0}R_{0}\quad\text{ for some }c_{0}\geqslant 1.

(1.10)

Wen
[
22
]
later improved this result to
\gamma,\beta\geqslant\frac{9}{5}
, with all constraints between adiabatic constants or two densities removed. His result implies, in particular, that transition to each single-phase flow is allowed.
We also mention the existence theory for dissipative weak solutions due to Li and She
[
13
]
, allowing to obtain global-in-time existence result for all finite energy initial data and all
\beta,\gamma>1
.
Finally, in the framework of finite energy weak solutions, the inviscid incompressible limit for general initial data in three-dimensional whole space was justified by Kwon and Li
[
8
]
.

A second representative example is the liquid-gas model

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}R+\operatorname{div}(R\mathbf{u})=0,\\
\partial_{t}Q+\operatorname{div}(Q\mathbf{u})=0,\\
\partial_{t}(R\mathbf{u})+\operatorname{div}(R\mathbf{u}\otimes\mathbf{u})+\nabla P(R,Q)=\mu\Delta\mathbf{u}+(\mu+\lambda)\nabla\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u},\end{cases}

(1.11)

where

\displaystyle P(R,Q)=C\left(-b(R,Q)+\sqrt{b^{2}(R,Q)+c(R,Q)}\right),

(1.12)

with

\displaystyle b(R,Q)=k_{0}-R-a_{0}Q,\quad c(R,Q)=4k_{0}a_{0}Q,

(1.13)

and
k_{0},a_{0},C
are positive constants.

This model arises naturally in the description of well and pipe liquid-gas two-phase flows. Global weak solutions were established in one dimension by Evje and Karlsen
[
2
]
, and in two dimensions under smallness assumptions by Yao, Zhang, and Zhu
[
23
]
. In the class of strong solutions, Yao, Zhu, and Zi
[
24
]
justified the incompressible limit toward the classical incompressible Navier–Stokes equations. We also refer to Wen, Yao, and Zhu
[
21
]
for a broader overview of related results on the liquid-gas models.

A third related class is the one-velocity Baer–Nunziato type system for mixtures of non-interacting compressible fluids:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\varrho+\operatorname{div}(\varrho\mathbf{u})=0,\\
\partial_{t}z+\operatorname{div}(z\mathbf{u})=0,\\
\partial_{t}\alpha+\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\alpha=0,\\
\partial_{t}[(\varrho+z)\mathbf{u}]+\operatorname{div}[(\varrho+z)\mathbf{u}\otimes\mathbf{u}]+\nabla p(f(\alpha)\varrho,g(\alpha)z)=\mu\Delta\mathbf{u}+(\mu+\lambda)\nabla\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u},\end{cases}

(1.14)

where
f,g:[0,1]\mapsto[0,\infty)
are given functions.
Under suitable structural assumptions, Novotný
[
18
]
proved the existence of global finite-energy weak solutions for an initial-boundary value problem for (
1.14
). Weak–strong uniqueness was later obtained by Jin and Novotný
[
4
]
; further existence results under general boundary conditions and for dissipative turbulent solutions may be found in
[
3
,
7
]
. For a non-isentropic version, Kwon, Novotný, and Cheng
[
9
]
proved weak sequential stability, while Kalousek and Nečasová
[
5
]
recently established existence of global weak solutions.

1.2.
Motivation and main results

Despite the substantial progress described above, the low Mach number limit for the two-fluid model with algebraic pressure closure remains poorly understood. The main difficulty comes from the highly nonlinear and implicit structure of the pressure law: unlike models with an explicit pressure
P(R,Q)
, here the pressure is given by
p(Z)=Z^{\gamma_{+}}
, where
Z={\mathcal{Z}}(R,Q)
is defined only implicitly by (
1.6
). This feature makes both the singular limit and the derivation of quantitative estimates significantly more delicate.

The purpose of this paper is to rigorously justify the low Mach number limit for (
1.3
)-(
1.6
) in the framework of local-in-time strong solutions, and to obtain explicit convergence rates. To this end, we first introduce the nondimensional scaling

\displaystyle R(t,x)\longmapsto R_{\varepsilon}(\varepsilon t,x),\quad Q(t,x)\longmapsto Q_{\varepsilon}(\varepsilon t,x),\quad\mathbf{u}(t,x)\longmapsto\varepsilon\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}(\varepsilon t,x),

(1.15)

together with

\displaystyle\mu\longmapsto\varepsilon\mu_{\varepsilon},\quad\lambda\longmapsto\varepsilon\lambda_{\varepsilon},\quad\mu_{\varepsilon}>0,\quad 2\mu_{\varepsilon}+3\lambda_{\varepsilon}\geqslant 0.

(1.16)

Then
(R_{\varepsilon},Q_{\varepsilon},u_{\varepsilon})
formally satisfy

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}R_{\varepsilon}+\operatorname{div}(R_{\varepsilon}\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon})=0,\\
\partial_{t}Q_{\varepsilon}+\operatorname{div}(Q_{\varepsilon}\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon})=0,\\
\partial_{t}[(R_{\varepsilon}+Q_{\varepsilon})\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}]+\operatorname{div}[(R_{\varepsilon}+Q_{\varepsilon})\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}\otimes\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}]+\frac{\nabla p(Z_{\varepsilon})}{\varepsilon^{2}}=\mu_{\varepsilon}\Delta\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}+(\mu_{\varepsilon}+\lambda_{\varepsilon})\nabla\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon},\end{cases}

(1.17)

where
p(Z_{\varepsilon})=Z_{\varepsilon}^{\gamma_{+}}
and
Z_{\varepsilon}
is linked to
(R_{\varepsilon},Q_{\varepsilon})
through

\displaystyle\begin{cases}Q_{\varepsilon}=\left(1-\frac{R_{\varepsilon}}{Z_{\varepsilon}}\right)Z_{\varepsilon}^{\gamma},\quad\gamma=\frac{\gamma_{+}}{\gamma_{-}},\\
R_{\varepsilon}\leqslant Z_{\varepsilon}.\end{cases}

(1.18)

For simplicity, throughout the paper we assume
\mu_{\varepsilon}=\mu
and
\lambda_{\varepsilon}=\lambda
are constants satisfying

\mu>0,\qquad 2\mu+3\lambda\geq 0.

Accordingly, we work with the following equivalent system

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}R_{\varepsilon}+\operatorname{div}(R_{\varepsilon}\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon})=0,\\
\partial_{t}Q_{\varepsilon}+\operatorname{div}(Q_{\varepsilon}\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon})=0,\\
\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}+\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}+\frac{\nabla p(Z_{\varepsilon})}{\varepsilon^{2}(R_{\varepsilon}+Q_{\varepsilon})}=\frac{\mu}{R_{\varepsilon}+Q_{\varepsilon}}\Delta\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}+\frac{\mu+\lambda}{R_{\varepsilon}+Q_{\varepsilon}}\nabla\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}.\end{cases}

(1.19)

If

\displaystyle R_{\varepsilon}\rightarrow 1,\quad Q_{\varepsilon}\rightarrow 1,\quad\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}\rightarrow\mathbf{u}\quad\text{ as }\varepsilon\rightarrow 0,

(1.20)

then the expected limit system on
\mathbb{T}^{3}
is the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{u}+\nabla\Pi=\frac{\mu}{2}\Delta\mathbf{u},\\
\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}=0.\end{cases}

(1.21)

Our first main result proves that, for well-prepared initial data, system (
1.19
) admits a unique classical solution on a time interval independent of
\varepsilon
, together with uniform estimates sufficient to pass to the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
. In particular,
R_{\varepsilon}
and
Q_{\varepsilon}
converge strongly to the constant state
1
, while
\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}
converges to the strong solution of (
1.21
). Our second main result provides quantitative convergence rates. More precisely, under an additional smallness assumption on the initial energy, we show that

\|R_{\varepsilon}-1\|_{H^{s}}^{2}+\|Q_{\varepsilon}-1\|_{H^{s}}^{2}\leq C\varepsilon^{2},\qquad\|\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}-\mathbf{u}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\|\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}-\mathbf{u}\|_{H^{1}}^{2}\,d\tau\leq C\varepsilon,

and

\|\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}\|_{H^{s-1}}\leq C\varepsilon.

These results appear to be the first quantitative low Mach number limit for the compressible two-fluid model with algebraic pressure closure in the strong-solution setting considered here. Compared with the incompressible-limit result for the liquid-gas model obtained in
[
24
]
, our convergence estimates do not require a uniform bound on the quotient of the initial densities. We also note that a recent preprint by Lebot
[
10
]
studies a related low Mach number problem for a compressible two-phase flow system with algebraic closure and identifies an inhomogeneous incompressible Navier–Stokes system as the limit for well-prepared data. In contrast, our result yields explicit convergence rates and only assumes
\gamma_{\pm}>1
.

From the technical viewpoint, the proof combines uniform high-order energy estimates with a relative energy argument. The main obstacle is that the pressure is not an explicit function of
(R,Q)
; instead, one has to exploit the structure of the implicitly defined map
(R,Q)\mapsto{\mathcal{Z}}(R,Q)
and carefully control its derivatives in the singular regime
\varepsilon\to 0
. This makes the commutator estimates and the closure of the energy method substantially more involved than in single-fluid or explicitly closed two-fluid models.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we state the main results. Section
3
is devoted to the uniform estimates and the proof of the existence theorem on an
\varepsilon
-independent time interval. In Section
4
we establish the relative energy inequality and derive the convergence rates.

2.
Statement of the main results

For an integer
s\geq 0
, we introduce the energy functionals

\displaystyle\mathscr{E}_{s}(R,Q,\mathbf{u})

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{|\alpha|\leqslant s}\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}|\nabla^{\alpha}(R-1)|^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}|\nabla^{\alpha}(Q-1)|^{2}+|\nabla^{\alpha}\mathbf{u}|^{2}\right)dx,

(2.1)

\displaystyle\mathscr{F}_{s}(R,Q,\mathbf{u})

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{|\alpha|\leqslant s}\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{\gamma_{+}\mathcal{Z}^{\gamma_{+}-1}(\partial_{R}\mathcal{Z})(R,Q)}{\varepsilon^{2}R}|\nabla^{\alpha}(R-1)|^{2}\right)dx

(2.2)

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{|\alpha|\leqslant s}\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{\gamma_{+}\mathcal{Z}^{\gamma_{+}-1}(\partial_{Q}\mathcal{Z})(R,Q)}{\varepsilon^{2}Q}|\nabla^{\alpha}(Q-1)|^{2}+(R+Q)|\nabla^{\alpha}\mathbf{u}|^{2}\right)dx.

(2.3)

When
R
and

remain sufficiently close to

is the natural energy generated by the symmetrized structure of system (

),

3.
Proof of Theorem
2.1

To simplify the presentation, throughout this section we assume that

\gamma_{+}<\gamma_{-},\qquad\text{equivalently, }\gamma=\frac{\gamma_{+}}{\gamma_{-}}<1.

By symmetry, the same argument also applies in the case
\gamma_{+}>\gamma_{-}
.
Unless otherwise specified,
C>0
denotes a generic constant, independent of

\varepsilon
, whose value may change from line to line. The proof combines a
uniform high-order energy method with a contraction argument for a suitable linearized
problem, and is partly inspired by the incompressible limit analysis for the Oldroyd–B
model in
[
11
]
and the viscous liquid-gas model in
[
24
]
.

3.1.
Uniform estimates

Let us define
\mathcal{A}_{T_{\ast},\varepsilon}(R_{0,\varepsilon},Q_{0,\varepsilon},\mathbf{u}_{0,\varepsilon},\delta,C_{1},C_{2})
as the subset of
L^{\infty}(0,T_{\ast};H^{s}(\Omega))\cap\text{Lip}([0,T_{\ast}];H^{s-1}(\Omega))
with
s\geqslant 4
such that

\displaystyle\begin{cases}|R_{\varepsilon}-1|+|Q_{\varepsilon}-1|<\varepsilon\delta,\\
\mathscr{E}_{s}(R_{\varepsilon},Q_{\varepsilon},\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon})(t)+\int_{0}^{t}\left(\mu{\left\|\nabla\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}\right\|}_{H^{s}}^{2}+(\mu+\lambda){\left\|\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}\right\|}_{H^{s}}^{2}\right)d\tau\leqslant C_{1},\\
\mathscr{E}_{s-1}(\partial_{t}R_{\varepsilon},\partial_{t}Q_{\varepsilon},\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon})(t)+\int_{0}^{t}\left(\mu{\left\|\nabla\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}\right\|}_{H^{s-1}}^{2}+(\mu+\lambda){\left\|\operatorname{div}\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}\right\|}_{H^{s-1}}^{2}\right)d\tau\leqslant C_{2}\end{cases}

(3.1)

for all
t\in[0,T_{\ast}]
. For
(r_{\varepsilon},q_{\varepsilon},\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon})\in\mathcal{A}_{T_{\ast},\varepsilon}(R_{0,\varepsilon},Q_{0,\varepsilon},\mathbf{u}_{0,\varepsilon},\delta,C_{1},C_{2})
, we define the map

\displaystyle\chi:(r_{\varepsilon},q_{\varepsilon},\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon})\mapsto(R_{\varepsilon},Q_{\varepsilon},\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}),

(3.2)

where
(R_{\varepsilon},Q_{\varepsilon},\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon})
is the unique solution to the following linear problem

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}R_{\varepsilon}+\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla R_{\varepsilon}+r_{\varepsilon}\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}=0,\\
\partial_{t}Q_{\varepsilon}+\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla Q_{\varepsilon}+q_{\varepsilon}\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}=0,\\
\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}+\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}+\frac{\gamma_{+}[\mathcal{Z}^{\gamma_{+}-1}(\partial_{R}\mathcal{Z})](r_{\varepsilon},q_{\varepsilon})}{\varepsilon^{2}(r_{\varepsilon}+q_{\varepsilon})}\nabla R_{\varepsilon}+\frac{\gamma_{+}[\mathcal{Z}^{\gamma_{+}-1}(\partial_{Q}\mathcal{Z})](r_{\varepsilon},q_{\varepsilon})}{\varepsilon^{2}(r_{\varepsilon}+q_{\varepsilon})}\nabla Q_{\varepsilon}\\
\hskip 18.49988pt\hskip 18.49988pt\hskip 18.49988pt\hskip 18.49988pt=\frac{\mu}{r_{\varepsilon}+q_{\varepsilon}}\Delta\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}+\frac{\mu+\lambda}{r_{\varepsilon}+q_{\varepsilon}}\nabla\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon},\end{cases}

(3.3)

with initial data

\displaystyle R_{\varepsilon}(0,\cdot)=1+\widetilde{R_{0,\varepsilon}},\quad Q_{\varepsilon}(0,\cdot)=1+\widetilde{Q_{0,\varepsilon}},\quad\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}(0,\cdot)=\mathbf{u}_{0}+\widetilde{\mathbf{u}_{0,\varepsilon}}.

(3.4)

Our aim is to show that, for properly chosen
T_{\ast},\delta,C_{1},C_{2}
independent of
\varepsilon
,
\chi
maps
\mathcal{A}_{T_{\ast},\varepsilon}(R_{0,\varepsilon},Q_{0,\varepsilon},\mathbf{u}_{0,\varepsilon},\delta,C_{1},C_{2})
into itself and is a contraction in suitable norm.

For clarity of notations, we denote by
D
the differential operator of space-time.
Applying the operator
D^{\alpha}
to (

2.6
), we readily get

\displaystyle\left|\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}(R_{\varepsilon}+Q_{\varepsilon})\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}\cdot\Big(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{u}\Big)\,dxd\tau-\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}(R_{\varepsilon}+Q_{\varepsilon})\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}\otimes\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}:\nabla\mathbf{u}\,dxd\tau\right|

(4.43)

\displaystyle\quad\leqslant C\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\left|\sqrt{R_{\varepsilon}+Q_{\varepsilon}}\,\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}-\sqrt{2}\,\mathbf{u}\right|^{2}dxd\tau+C\varepsilon.

(4.44)

Based on (
4.21
), (
4.26
), (
4.29

), we see from (

) that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\left|\sqrt{R_{\varepsilon}+Q_{\varepsilon}}\,\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}-\sqrt{2}\,\mathbf{u}\right|^{2}\,dx

(4.45)

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{\Omega}\left[\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\frac{1}{\gamma_{+}-1}\left(\frac{R_{\varepsilon}}{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}\right)^{\gamma_{+}}\alpha_{\varepsilon}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\frac{1}{\gamma_{-}-1}\left(\frac{Q_{\varepsilon}}{1-\alpha_{\varepsilon}}\right)^{\gamma_{-}}\left(1-\alpha_{\varepsilon}\right)\right]\,dx

(4.46)

\displaystyle\qquad+\mu\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}-\nabla\mathbf{u}\right|^{2}\,dxd\tau+(\mu+\lambda)\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}(\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon})^{2}\,dxd\tau

(4.47)

\displaystyle\quad\leqslant C\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\left|\sqrt{R_{\varepsilon}+Q_{\varepsilon}}\,\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}-\sqrt{2}\,\mathbf{u}\right|^{2}\,dxd\tau+C\varepsilon,

(4.48)

from which we infer by Grönwall’s inequality that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\left|\sqrt{R_{\varepsilon}+Q_{\varepsilon}}\,\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}-\sqrt{2}\,\mathbf{u}\right|^{2}dx

(4.49)

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{\Omega}\left[\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\frac{1}{\gamma_{+}-1}\left(\frac{R_{\varepsilon}}{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}\right)^{\gamma_{+}}\alpha_{\varepsilon}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\frac{1}{\gamma_{-}-1}\left(\frac{Q_{\varepsilon}}{1-\alpha_{\varepsilon}}\right)^{\gamma_{-}}\left(1-\alpha_{\varepsilon}\right)\right]dx

(4.50)

\displaystyle\qquad+\mu\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}-\nabla\mathbf{u}\right|^{2}\,dxd\tau+(\mu+\lambda)\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}(\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon})^{2}\,dxd\tau

(4.51)

\displaystyle\quad\leqslant C\varepsilon.

(4.52)

4.3.

To begin with, we observe that

\displaystyle{\left\|R_{\varepsilon}-1\right\|}_{H^{s}}^{2}+{\left\|Q_{\varepsilon}-1\right\|}_{H^{s}}^{2}\leqslant C\varepsilon^{2}

(4.63)
