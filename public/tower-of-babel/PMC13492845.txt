Audit and feedback to improve antibiotic prescribing among community physicians in India: A quasi-experimental study
Authors: Kensheya Regena, Aamina Hussain, Vaithya Venkatraman Anantharaman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Irrational antibiotic use, especially in Indian community settings, is a major contributor to the growing global threat of antimicrobial resistance (AMR). Guidelines for rational prescribing are provided by the WHO AWaRe classification; however, its use in primary care is still restricted. An effective way to enhance prescribing practices is through audit and feedback. The purpose of this study was to assess community physicians’ use of antibiotics, gauge their compliance with the AWaRe framework, and ascertain whether structured audit and feedback can encourage sensible prescribing and aid in the fight against AMR.MATERIALS AND METHODS:The study was conducted in two phases from July 2022 to 2023 in Chengalpattu, Tamil Nadu. Phase 1 involved a cross-sectional review of 680 antibiotic prescriptions. In Phase 2, 28 community physicians participated in a quasi-experimental study using a validated structured questionnaire. Educational intervention and feedback were provided. Data were analyzed using SPSS version 26.0, and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was applied with a significance level of P < 0.05.RESULTS:Amoxicillin was the most commonly prescribed antibiotic (88.2%) out of 680 prescriptions, mostly for upper respiratory tract infections. While access-group medications like amoxicillin were more commonly used in rural areas, watch-group antibiotics like azithromycin were more frequently used in urban areas. Perception scores (19 to 16, P = 0.000) and knowledge (median: 26.5 to 20, P = 0.000) significantly improved after the audit and feedback intervention. Increased adherence to the WHO AWaRe classification was observed in post-intervention prescribing patterns, with a discernible shift towards community physicians’ use of antibiotics sensibly, especially when treating acute respiratory infections.CONCLUSION:Enhancing antibiotic stewardship and encouraging rational prescribing among community physicians at the primary care level through audit and feedback is a practical and economical approach that can improve adherence to AWaRe classification and decrease inappropriate antibiotic use.

Introduction

Antibiotics have saved millions of lives and remain among the most critical therapeutic tools in modern medicine. However, antimicrobial resistance (AMR) has rapidly emerged due to the overuse and misuse of antibiotics in clinical, agricultural, and community settings.[123] As one of the top ten global health threats to humanity, AMR is expected to kill 10 million people a year by 2050 if left unchecked, according to the World Health Organisation (WHO).[4] Previously treatable infections with common antibiotics are becoming more and more resistant, which raises mortality, prolongs illness, and drives up medical expenses.[5]

India is one of the world’s biggest users of antibiotics, and its large population, dual burden of infectious and non-communicable diseases, and multifaceted healthcare system create a special burden.[6] Over-the-counter availability, inadequate diagnostic support, inconsistent treatment guidelines, and sociocultural expectations contribute to the irrational prescribing of antibiotics.[7] A national-level study during the COVID-19 pandemic revealed a significant surge in the sale of broad-spectrum antibiotics, particularly from the WHO ‘Watch’ group, highlighting a growing deviation from stewardship norms.[8] This trend is especially evident in semi-urban and rural regions, where frontline providers often operate with limited decision-making support and training.[9]

The AWaRe (Access, Watch, Reserve) classification system was created by the WHO to address these issues, classifying antibiotics according to therapeutic priority and resistance risk.[10] When treating common infections, “Access” antibiotics are advised as the first line of treatment; “Watch” antibiotics are given priority for stewardship because of their increased potential for resistance; and “Reserve” antibiotics are suggested as a last resort for severe, multidrug-resistant infections.[11] The framework promotes rational prescribing, benchmarking, and surveillance, aligning with the WHO’s Global Action Plan on AMR.[12] However, the AWaRe framework is not well-known or operationally integrated among Indian prescribers, even with policy-level support.[13] Research indicates that merely disseminating guidelines might not be enough in the absence of behavior-change interventions.[14]

Proven strategies to enhance prescribing behavior and adherence to guidelines include audit and feedback mechanisms, which offer structured feedback following individual or group-level data.[15] According to the results of a 2022 systematic review, computerized audit and feedback systems greatly improve patient outcomes and healthcare provider performance, especially when the feedback is timely, action-planned, and customized for the local clinical setting.[16] In India’s primary care landscape, community physicians play a crucial role as gatekeepers of antibiotic use yet are rarely targeted in stewardship programs.[17] Prior interventions combining audits with educational modules have demonstrated meaningful reductions in watch-category antibiotic use and improved alignment with AWaRe benchmarks.[18] Moreover, low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) have shown promising results when audit feedback is implemented in a non-punitive, data-driven manner.[19] An objective framework for assessing the quality of prescriptions is provided by incorporating the WHO AWaRe classification into prescription audits. This helps reduce antimicrobial resistance at the institutional and community levels.[20] National health policies and global mandates, including India’s National Action Plan on AMR, increasingly emphasize the need for scalable antimicrobial stewardship programs at all healthcare levels.[21]

This study aimed to assess the impact of an audit and feedback intervention on antibiotic prescribing practices among community physicians in the field practice area of a tertiary care hospital in Tamil Nadu, India, using the WHO AWaRe classification framework.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

This study employed a quasi-experimental design with two phases: A retrospective cross-sectional analysis (Phase 1) and a pre-post intervention study with no control group (Phase 2). The study was conducted from July 2022 to July 2023 in the field practice area of a tertiary care teaching hospital in Chengalpattu district, Tamil Nadu, India.

Study participants and sampling

The Phase 1 study population included outpatient antibiotic prescriptions collected from two healthcare training centers selected via simple random sampling. The Phase 2 participants were 28 community physicians working in the selected centers. In Phase 1, 680 prescriptions were reviewed using universal sampling. For Phase 2, all 28 eligible physicians present during the intervention period were included.

The sample size was calculated using the formula n = Z² × P (1-P)/d², where Z = 1.96, P = 0.5 (expected knowledge improvement), and d = 0.15 (margin of error).

n = (1.96)2 × 0.5 (1-0.5)/(0.15)2 = 42.7.

Due to feasibility, all 28 physicians available during the intervention were included.

Data collection tool and technique

(1) Demographic Tool: A semi-structured demographic questionnaire collected age, gender, professional experience, and years of antibiotic prescribing. These variables were described as frequencies and percentages. (2) AWaRe Knowledge and Practice Questionnaire: A validated structured questionnaire was used, covering five sections: (i) Demographic characteristics, (ii) Knowledge on URTI and rational antibiotic use (10 items). (iii) Section C: Awareness of WHO AWaRe classification (7 items). (iv) Attitude toward antibiotic prescribing (5-point Likert scale). (v) Self-reported prescribing practices and perceived barriers.

Each correct knowledge response received 1 point; incorrect responses received 0. One point was awarded for each accurate knowledge response, while zero was awarded for incorrect responses. Total score was interpreted as: Good (7–10), Average (4–6), Poor (0–3). The final score was interpreted as follows: Poor (0–3), Average (4–6), and Good (7–10). We classified attitude scores as negative (<2.5), neutral (2.5–3.49), or positive (≥3.5). Content validity was evaluated qualitatively by a panel of three public health experts for relevance and clarity. Formal CVR and CVI calculations were not performed, but expert consensus confirmed adequacy of content. The sufficiency of the content was confirmed by expert consensus, but formal CVR and CVI calculations were not carried out. During pilot testing with ten physicians, internal consistency was assessed (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.70). Audit and feedback were delivered using an interrupted time series approach. On Day 1 and Day 45, structured one-on-one educational sessions were conducted using WHO AWaRe and ICMR 2019 guidelines. Physicians also received individualized verbal feedback on their antibiotic prescription data. Peer benchmarking and graphical comparisons were included. Ethical considerations

This study adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all participants. Ethical approval was granted by the Institutional Ethics Committee of SRM Medical College Hospital and Research Centre, Kattankulathur (Approval Code: IEC/2022/09/MED/014).

Statistical analysis method

IBM SPSS version 26.0 was used for analysis after the data were entered into Microsoft Excel. Frequencies, proportions, and other descriptive statistics were computed. In order to summarize continuous variables, medians and interquartile ranges were used. The non-parametric, paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to compare the data before and after the intervention. Statistical significance was obtained when the P value was less than 0.05.

Results

In the field practice area of a tertiary care hospital in Chengalpattu, Tamil Nadu, 680 outpatient prescriptions from two chosen healthcare training facilities were examined between July 2022 and July 2023. These included 313 (46.1%) female patients and 367 (53.9%) male patients. The urban field practice area accounted for 68.3% of prescriptions, whereas rural areas accounted for 31.7%. The 25–64 age range was the most common age group to receive antibiotics. Oral administration was used for all antibiotics.

The most prevalent clinical indication, with 600 prescriptions (88.2%), was upper respiratory tract infections (URTIs). Aware classification revealed that while Access-category antibiotics (amoxicillin, amoxicillin with clavulanic acid) were more prevalent in rural areas, Watch-category antibiotics (azithromycin, norfloxacin) were more frequently used in urban centers. Aware classification and demography

A total of 28 community physicians completed both pre- and post-intervention assessments. Of them, 10 (35.7%) were men and 18 (64.3%) were women. The majority of participants were in the 25–30 age range (n = 25, 89.3%). 53.6% of respondents reported having one to two years of experience writing antibiotic prescriptions.

The knowledge and opinions of doctors regarding the use of antibiotics and the AWaRe classification system statistically significantly improved after the audit and feedback intervention. From 26.5 (IQR: 23.75–28) to 20.0 (IQR: 18–21.5), the median knowledge scores increased (Z = -4.610, P = 0.000). Discussion

Evaluation of the effects of audit and feedback on antibiotic prescribing practices among community physicians in a tertiary care hospital’s field practice area was the goal of this study. The study’s most important finding was the notable improvement in doctors’ understanding, attitudes, and prescribing habits around antibiotics, along with a greater adherence to the WHO AWaRe classification.

Following the intervention, knowledge scores significantly increased, suggesting a better comprehension of appropriate antibiotic use. This supports the findings of Biswas et al.,[3] who highlighted that educational interventions help healthcare providers become more aware and prescribe responsibly. Similarly, Singh et al.[18] found that organized feedback systems greatly enhanced antibiotic stewardship procedures. Sharma et al.[2] found that targeted education led to positive changes in attitudes about antibiotic resistance, which is consistent with the improvement in perception scores seen in this study.

Following the intervention, there was a decrease in Watch-group usage, especially in urban areas, and an increase in Access-group antibiotic use, which suggests that the intervention helped prescribers choose antibiotics more appropriately. These results are corroborated by Joseph et al.,[5] who observed a similar pattern after WHO AWaRe-based interventions were introduced in primary care settings. Likewise, Gupta et al.[10] emphasized how peer comparison and feedback can help cut down on inappropriate prescribing.

However, Kumar et al.[11] pointed out that in certain contexts, interventions that lacked ongoing reinforcement had little lasting effect. This demonstrates the value of consistent educational initiatives and system-level assistance, both of which were partially covered in the current study through frequent feedback sessions.

Due to increased patient demand or availability of these medications, the current study also found that Watch-group antibiotics were used more frequently in urban areas than in rural ones. Mehta et al.[13] also noted this regional variation in antibiotic preference, stressing the impact of socioeconomic factors and healthcare accessibility on prescribing practices.

The strengths of this study include the use of validated instruments, structured educational interventions, and a real-world primary care setting. The results’ generalizability may be restricted by the study’s limitations, which include its small sample size, short follow-up period, and lack of a control group.

Conclusion

More adherence to WHO AWaRe guidelines was encouraged by the audit and feedback intervention, which greatly enhanced community physicians’ understanding, attitudes, and prescribing practices regarding antibiotics. Primary healthcare providers play a critical role in tackling the issue of antibiotic misuse, which is a significant contributor to antimicrobial resistance. A scalable and economical antibiotic stewardship approach can be achieved by incorporating AWaRe-based training modules and structured audit-feedback mechanisms into standard primary care procedures, according to the findings. In high-consumption environments like India, health systems should use WHO AWaRe benchmarks to track trends in antibiotic use and establish sensible prescribing objectives. This strategy is in line with national and international action plans meant to maintain the effectiveness of antibiotics. 0To ensure sustainable rational antibiotic use and resistance mitigation, it is advised to expand stewardship interventions across multiple healthcare tiers, conduct longitudinal audits, and implement periodic training.
