Contrasting endovascular and open approaches for adventitial cystic disease of the femoropopliteal arteries
Authors: Jessica Campisi, Max Murray-Ramcharan, Mina Iskaros, Thomas Bernik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdventitial cystic disease is a rare nonatherosclerotic cause of lower extremity claudication, classically managed with open resection and reconstruction. We present two cases of popliteal artery adventitial cystic disease treated with different modalities: endovascular stenting in a young, otherwise healthy patient with mid-superficial femoral artery involvement, and interposition vein bypass in an older, comorbid patient with popliteal disease. Both approaches resulted in durable symptom resolution at 1 year. This series highlights that treatment should be individualized, as endovascular repair may be effective in select anatomic settings, whereas open reconstruction remains essential for lesions unsuitable for stenting.Keywords: Acute limb ischemia, Advential cystic disease, Balloon angioplasty, Endovascular, Popliteal bypass, Stent

Adventitial cystic disease (ACD) is a rare, nonatherosclerotic vascular disorder characterized by mucin-filled cysts within the arterial adventitia, most commonly affecting the popliteal artery.1 Several theories of etiology have been proposed—including systemic, synovial/ganglionic, developmental, and traumatic mechanisms—with the traumatic theory most widely supported.2 ACD accounts for approximately one in 1200 cases of claudication in young patients and typically affects healthy, middle-aged men, with a 5:1 male predominance.3,4 Symptoms often arise from intermittent arterial compression, presenting as progressive unilateral claudication or, less commonly, acute limb ischemia.5

Diagnosis relies on duplex ultrasound, computed tomography angiography, and magnetic resonance angiography, with magnetic resonance angiography serving as the diagnostic standard. A characteristic “scimitar sign” or hourglass narrowing helps differentiate ACD from atherosclerotic or thromboembolic disease.5 Standard management traditionally involves open excision of the cyst with reconstruction using a vein or prosthetic conduit because of high recurrence with incomplete cyst removal.6 Endovascular approaches have been rarely described and remain controversial.7

Here, we present two cases of popliteal and femoropopliteal ACD managed with contrasting strategies; endovascular stenting in a young patient and open bypass in an older patient with significant comorbidities. Both patients provided written publication consent for this case report. This series highlights how individualized decision-making based on lesion location and patient factors can broaden therapeutic options beyond traditional paradigms.

Case report

Patient 1

A 40-year-old nonsmoking female presented with 7 days of right leg claudication at one to two blocks. Motor and sensory exams were normal, though distal pulses on the right were diminished. Arterial duplex demonstrated a short-segment mid-superficial femoral artery (SFA) occlusion with collateralization and a halo sign (Fig 1). Computed tomography angiography confirmed a 0.9-cm focal occlusion with distal reconstitution. Workup for embolic sources was negative.

Arterial duplex of the right lower extremity of patient 1 showing short-segment mid-superficial femoral artery occlusion with collateralization and a halo sign.

Given the patient’s age, lack of comorbidities, and preference to avoid open surgery, an endovascular strategy was selected. Via left common femoral artery access, right lower extremity angiography confirmed focal mid-SFA occlusion with robust tibial runoff (Fig 2). The lesion was crossed with a 0.035-inch wire, and a 6 × 60 mm self-expanding stent was deployed. Completion angiography showed excellent inline flow (Fig 3).

Right lower extremity angiogram of patient 1 demonstrating focal mid-superficial femoral artery occlusion with robust tibial runoff.

Completion right lower extremity angiogram of patient 1 after a 6 × 60 mm self-expanding stent deployed in R mid-superficial femoral artery.

The patient’s distal pulses normalized, and she was discharged on dual antiplatelet therapy. At 1-year follow-up, the SFA stent remained widely patent with complete symptom resolution.

Patient 2

A 70-year-old woman with diabetes, hypertension, smoking history, and prior carotid stenting presented with 1 week of left leg claudication after one block. Distal pulses were absent, with monophasic Doppler signals. Duplex ultrasound demonstrated cystic compression of the mid-popliteal artery with >70% stenosis.

Diagnostic angiography revealed a focal popliteal artery occlusion behind the knee with distal reconstitution. Given the lesion’s location in the popliteal fossa, open reconstruction was chosen.

After harvesting the ipsilateral great saphenous vein, the patient was placed prone, and a posterior “lazy-S” incision exposed the popliteal artery. Dense periadventitial inflammation and thrombotic occlusion were noted, consistent with ACD. The diseased segment along with cystic contents was resected (Fig 4), and a reversed great saphenous vein interposition graft was tunneled and anastomosed end to end (Fig 5). Completion angiography confirmed graft patency.

Intraoperative imaging of patient 2 demonstrating the diseased segment of the left popliteal artery and cystic contents.

Intraoperative imaging of patient 2 following end-to-end anastomosis of a greater saphenous vein interposition graft.

The postoperative course was uncomplicated. Pathology confirmed ACD. At 1 year, the bypass graft remained widely patent with full symptom resolution.

Discussion

Management of ACD varies widely and depends on location, extent of disease, and patient characteristics. Standard treatment favors open resection with revascularization, typically bypass or interposition grafting, because complete cyst removal minimizes recurrence.4,6 A systematic review suggested that resection with reconstruction confers the best long-term patency, whereas recurrence after simple drainage may reach 10%.8, 9, 10

Although endovascular approaches have been described, their durability remains uncertain. Balloon angioplasty and stenting may restore luminal patency, but recurrence and later need for open reconstruction are well documented.11, 12, 13 These limitations stem from the extrinsic, nonatherosclerotic nature of ACD and the frequent involvement of the popliteal fossa, an anatomically dynamic region prone to stent fracture or deformation.3

Our cases demonstrate that both endovascular and open approaches can be appropriate when tailored to lesion location and patient factors. In case 1, the lesion’s mid-SFA location, an uncommon site for ACD, favored endovascular therapy, with a self-expanding nitinol stent offering resistance to external compression within Hunter’s canal.14 The young, healthy patient avoided the morbidity of open bypass and achieved excellent early term and midterm results.

In case 2, classical popliteal fossa involvement and the anatomic unsuitability for endovascular repair justified open bypass despite comorbidities. This traditional approach offered durable revascularization and definitive treatment. These contrasting strategies challenge conventional assumptions that young patients should always undergo open repair and that endovascular therapy should be reserved for high-risk patients. Instead, anatomical considerations, especially lesion location, may play a larger role than age alone in guiding management.15

Conclusions

ACD is a rare cause of claudication and acute limb ischemia in otherwise healthy patients. While open resection with reconstruction remains the standard of care, endovascular therapy may be a viable alternative in select anatomic locations and patient profiles. Our cases demonstrate that individualized, anatomy-driven decision-making can yield favorable outcomes with either modality. More robust comparative data are needed to define long-term outcomes and refine treatment strategies for this uncommon vascular disorder.
