A comparative study of quantum-inspired PSO and EA with their binary variants for heart disease classification
Authors: Serkan Örücü
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Feature selection and hyperparameter optimization are widely used in heart disease prediction, but comparative findings are often affected by differences in preprocessing, search space design, and evaluation budget. This study compared quantum-inspired particle swarm optimization and evolutionary algorithms with their binary variants, namely QI-PSO, QI-EA, QI-BPSO, and QI-BEA, under the same evaluation protocol for joint feature selection and discrete hyperparameter tuning on the UCI Cleveland Heart Disease dataset. The feature mask and classifier hyperparameters were optimized simultaneously in a unified search space using the same preprocessing scheme, inner-validation protocol, and evaluation budget. Decision Tree (DT), Logistic Regression (LR), Support Vector Machine (SVM), and K-Nearest Neighbors (K-NN) classifiers were evaluated using a fixed train-test split. Held-out test performance was reported with 95% confidence intervals. In addition, a no-feature selection baseline was evaluated for each classifier using the final hyperparameter configuration of the best selected feature QI model for the corresponding classifier while retaining all 13 input variables. Precision-recall curves, threshold sensitivity profiles, outer-trial CV Accuracy, runtime, selected feature counts, convergence behavior, and cost-performance distributions were also analyzed. Statistical comparisons of outer-trial distributions were performed using Kruskal-Wallis tests followed by Holm-adjusted Mann-Whitney U post-hoc comparisons. The held-out test results showed classifier-dependent differences among the QI variants. For DT and K-NN, the highest selected-feature test Accuracy was obtained with QI-PSO, whereas for SVM it was obtained with QI-BPSO. In the K-NN setting, QI-BEA, defined in this study as the modified binary implementation of the QI-EA update strategy, produced higher selected-feature Accuracy, F1-score, and MCC than QI-EA. For DT, QI-BPSO yielded the highest ROC-AUC, while for LR all four QI methods produced identical held-out test metrics. Compared with the corresponding no-feature selection baselines, the selected feature QI models showed a clear held-out advantage for K-NN. In DT, the no-feature selection baselines matched the best selected feature accuracy, whereas QI-BPSO yielded the highest DT ROC-AUC. In LR and SVM, the no-feature selection baselines produced stronger held-out results than the selected feature configurations. Precision-recall and threshold sensitivity analyses further showed classifier-dependent overlap and separation patterns beyond single point estimates. At the outer-trial level, QI-BPSO occupied the lowest runtime range, while QI-BEA yielded the highest median CV accuracy for DT, K-NN, and LR. These findings show that the relative behavior of QI variants depends on the classifier family and on whether emphasis is placed on held-out test performance, operating-threshold behavior, inner-loop validation level, or computational cost.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-57736-3.

Introductıon

Cardiovascular diseases rank among the leading causes of mortality worldwide. According to the data reported by the World Health Organization, in 2022 approximately 19.8 million deaths, corresponding to about 32% of all deaths, were associated with this disease group1. The same source reports that the majority of these deaths are attributable to myocardial infarction and stroke. Consequently, early risk identification and accurate classification remain clinically important. At the same time, non-specific symptom profiles, heterogeneity of risk factors, and differences across patient subgroups continue to complicate early diagnosis and consistent risk stratification2–6.

To address this difficulty, the literature increasingly emphasizes machine learning based diagnostic and prognostic systems that can support physician decision making by reducing time burden, improving analytical efficiency, and enabling earlier intervention2,7–10. In this context, predictive models attempt to derive disease-risk categorization from routinely collected clinical variables. In practice, however, particularly in small to medium sized biomedical datasets, limited sample size, missing values, inter-variable correlation, and sensitivity to model settings can restrict generalization capacity and complicate the interpretation of reported gains2,6,11. These issues make controlled benchmark protocols important, because methodological comparisons require the same dataset structure, preprocessing workflow, validation design, and evaluation budget across competing methods. In this context, the UCI Cleveland Heart Disease dataset remains a widely used reference benchmark for controlled methodological comparison in the heart disease prediction literature3,6,9. In the present study, following common practice, the Cleveland Heart Disease dataset was used, and the original target structure was reduced to a binary classification problem in which disease presence was treated as a single class.

In heart disease classification, model behavior is shaped jointly by preprocessing choices, feature representation, classifier family, hyperparameter configuration, and feature selection (FS) strategy8,12,13. Among these components, FS occupies a particularly important role because it seeks to improve generalization and reduce model complexity by removing irrelevant or redundant variables. Medical wrapper-based FS studies evaluate selected feature size, classification accuracy, and computational cost together, indicating that feature reduction is not only a predictive issue but also a computational design issue14. This induces a natural trade-off in which classification performance is expected to be preserved or improved while the number of retained variables is reduced13. Since this decision is inherently expressed in a binary mask space, FS becomes a discrete combinatorial search problem7,11,13,15. In wrapper-based FS designs, candidate subsets are evaluated through classifier training and validation10,14. For this reason, optimization based FS studies often report computational time together with selected feature size or ratio and classification accuracy14,16,17. Population based search methods are therefore frequently used in this setting because of their natural compatibility with binary feature mask representations7,11,13,15. Nevertheless, searching directly in the feature mask space can still be affected by premature convergence and local-optimum entrapment7,11,13,15,18, as well as by sensitivity of the fitness estimate under limited sample conditions19,20. In medical and related FS studies, metaheuristic search operators have been modified to improve population diversity, exploration-exploitation behavior, and binary subset search performance14,17. Accordingly, methods that can preserve search diversity while managing the exploration-exploitation balance have attracted increasing attention in binary and wrapper-based FS settings7,11,13,15,18.

QI metaheuristics extend classical-computing search behavior through probabilistic representation, sampling based candidate generation, and rotation or perturbation based update mechanisms, without requiring quantum hardware21–23. Within this broader line, the methodological appeal of QI optimization lies in its ability to represent multiple candidate tendencies within a single probabilistic state and then generate concrete solutions by sampling21,22. This makes the approach especially relevant to discrete or binary search spaces, including feature mask optimization, where search diversity and transition behavior matter directly22. In the context of FS, binary QI variants are additionally important because FS is inherently a binary decision problem and the search space is discrete in the form of a feature mask7,11–13,22. Wu et al. (2019) demonstrate, within the binary quantum particle swarm line, how a probabilistic representation can be converted into a binary solution through sampling and used in feature subset search12. Considering these lines together, QI formulations operating in binary space within both the particle swarm and evolutionary search families can be seen as naturally connected to the FS problem12,13,22. Recent biomedical classification and optimization studies continue to use QI and quantum-behaved (QB) formulations to address exploration-exploitation balance, premature convergence, global search behavior, and compact subset discovery in small or medium sized datasets23–25.

At the same time, the broader QI line should be distinguished from the narrower (QB) line. In the general QI line, probabilistic representation and update ideas can be embedded into different search families beyond PSO, including salp swarm, seagull optimization, and evolutionary search21,22,26,27. By contrast, QB optimization denotes a narrower probabilistic formulation. In the QPSO literature, candidate generation is governed by a probability model and by iteratively updated parameters that define the sampling behavior28,29. Thus, QB methods should not be treated as synonymous with the entire QI literature.

Recent application studies in cardiovascular prediction reflect both lines. QB formulations have been reported in heart disease prediction settings, particularly in SVM based pipelines6,8. At the same time, QI designs beyond the QPSO SVM pattern have also appeared in the literature, including classifier specific settings in which QI optimization was coupled with alternative learning models in cardiovascular prediction tasks27. These studies show that heart disease prediction work increasingly frames classification performance as an outcome of both the learning model and the optimization strategy.

Recent cardiovascular and heart disease prediction studies increasingly treat model performance as the result of an integrated pipeline in which preprocessing, FS, classifier design, hyperparameter optimization, and metaheuristic search jointly shape the reported outcomes. Navita et al. (2025) used SMOTE-ENN based data balancing, Chi-square based FS, stacking ensemble learning, and Grid Search Cross-Validation in a cardiovascular disease detection pipeline30. Gabriel and Anbarasi (2024) used a dual-tier FS strategy with Optuna tuned LightGBM for cardiovascular disease prediction31, while Praveen et al. (2024) combined enhanced FS and ensemble learning in the same disease prediction context32. Cao et al. (2025) proposed an MFS-DLPSO-XGBoost model in which Pearson correlation analysis and XGBoost feature importance were used for multiple FS, and an improved PSO strategy was used to tune XGBoost hyperparameters for cardiovascular disease prediction33. Hidayat et al. (2024) used IG based feature selection and GA based CNN feature engineering for CHD prediction, showing another example in which feature engineering and metaheuristic hyperparameter optimization were combined34.

In parallel, recent optimization based studies have examined different search mechanisms for heart disease classification. Asha and Ramya (2025) combined Artificial Flora Optimization with SVM for FS based cardiovascular disease classification on the Cleveland dataset35. Khan et al. (2024) used a modified Artificial Bee Colony strategy with K-NN for optimal FS in heart disease prediction36. Narasimhan and Victor (2025) evaluated SelectKBest with GA, PSO, and ACO optimized Random Forest models on the Cleveland heart disease dataset9.

Quantum-oriented cardiovascular prediction studies have also appeared in this recent line. Banumathy et al. (2024) applied a QI seagull optimization strategy to tune a deep belief network for cardiovascular disease prediction27, and Kumar et al. (2025) compared classical and QI branches in a heart disease prediction framework by combining GA, PSO, QGA, and QPSO based optimization components6. These studies show that AI based cardiovascular prediction has moved toward hybrid and optimization supported workflows. Their results also remain difficult to compare directly because classifier families, preprocessing choices, FS strategies, optimization procedures, validation designs, metric sets, and evaluation budgets differ across studies. This observation motivates the present controlled comparison, where the dataset, preprocessing workflow, classifier families, candidate representation, validation design, fitness definition, and evaluation budget are fixed to examine the behavior of continuous and binary QI representations and their search mechanisms under a matched protocol.

However, selected application studies and related methodological discussions also suggest recurring limitations in reporting and evaluation design3,6,19,22. First, metaheuristic and QI components are often reported within a single classifier centered pipeline, which makes it difficult to disentangle the effect of the search representation from the effect of the classifier family itself. Optimization setups centered on Random Forest3,9 and configurations in which QPSO is combined with SVM8 are typical examples of this pattern. Second, even within the same dataset context, preprocessing decisions, splitting strategies, and validation designs vary substantially across studies. As a result, reported gains may reflect not only representation choice, but also classifier dependence, protocol heterogeneity, or differences in model selection design6,8,37. Hence, in experimental frameworks targeting comparability, the protocol should be explicitly specified and the model selection process should be separated from final performance reporting20. This issue is particularly important in biomedical machine learning settings, where inappropriate FS or cross-validation procedures can bias reported performance19,20.

These methodological gaps motivate the present study, which develops a systematic and methodologically controlled framework for comparing QI optimization variants within the QI metaheuristic family under a matched protocol, without claiming a universally superior heart disease predictor. The research gap addressed in this study is the difficulty of isolating QI representation effects from classifier family, preprocessing workflow, search space definition, validation design, and evaluation budget in heart disease prediction and QI based FS studies3,6,8,9,12,22. The purpose of this study is not only to apply existing QI algorithms to the Cleveland Heart Disease dataset, but to examine how continuous and binary QI representations behave when they are decoded into the same joint FS and discrete hyperparameter tuning task under the same experimental conditions.

The methodological contribution is the isolation of QI representation and search-behavior effects within a matched wrapper-based FS and discrete hyperparameter-tuning benchmark, together with the joint evaluation of predictive, statistical, computational, threshold-dependent, and feature-subset result layers.

The study was guided by the working premise that continuous probability based and measurement based binary QI representations may produce classifier-dependent differences even when the data partition, preprocessing workflow, classifier families, feature mask search objective, hyperparameter candidate sets, fitness definition, and evaluation budget are kept constant. Under this premise, differences among QI-PSO, QI-EA, QI-BPSO, and QI-BEA are interpreted as representation level behavior observed under the fixed benchmark protocol rather than as evidence of a universally superior heart disease predictor12,18,22.

To this end, four representations, namely QI Particle Swarm Optimization (QI-PSO), QI Evolutionary Algorithm (QI-EA), QI Binary Particle Swarm Optimization (QI-BPSO), and QI Binary Evolutionary Algorithm (QI-BEA), were evaluated for joint FS and discrete hyperparameter tuning on the UCI Cleveland Heart Disease dataset3,6,8,9,12,22. In this study, QI-BEA denotes the modified binary implementation of the QI-EA update strategy in the common experimental design. It retains the elite-guided update logic of QI-EA while using the measurement based binary QI representation adopted for binary search. Thus, QI-BEA is used as a binary adaptation of QI-EA for the present joint FS and discrete hyperparameter tuning task, which enables a direct representation level comparison with QI-EA under the same experimental protocol. The comparison therefore covers continuous and binary implementations of particle-swarm-based and evolutionary QI update strategies under the same search task. LR, SVM, K-NN, and DT were selected as classifier families.

In accordance with reporting lines in the literature that use holdout-based experimental settings, this study was conducted under a single stratified train-test partition3,6,8. The same train-test split was kept constant for all methods and classifiers so that differences associated with QI representation and search behavior could be examined under a common benchmark protocol. For all compared methods, the preprocessing workflow, classifier set, search space definition, and evaluation budget were explicitly fixed, while the held-out test subset was kept outside the optimization and model selection process to reduce information leakage and optimistic performance estimation19,38,39. In this way, the study was designed to support a controlled comparison of representation effects under a single benchmark protocol.

Materıals and methods

This section describes the components and execution flow of the experimental design developed on the Cleveland heart disease dataset. The study was designed as a controlled within-family comparison of four QI optimization variants under matched conditions. Accordingly, the same classifier set, preprocessing workflow, search space definition, evaluation budget, and validation protocol were used for all compared methods. This design was structured to support a controlled comparison of representation variants from the same QI line under a common experimental protocol.

The section is organized as follows. First, the dataset structure, the binary reformulation of the target variable, and the preprocessing pipeline are presented. Next, the unified candidate-solution structure is defined to show how FS and discrete classifier hyperparameter tuning were handled jointly in the same search space. This is followed by the algorithmic description of QI-PSO, QI-EA, QI-BPSO, and QI-BEA under a common representational logic. The experimental design then specifies the fixed train-test partition, the stratified internal cross-validation protocol, seed handling, and the evaluation budget used to ensure method-to-method comparability. Finally, the no-FS baseline, the reported performance metrics, the statistical analysis procedures, the computational complexity perspective, and the implementation environment are described so that the full evaluation setting remains explicit and auditable.

Dataset and problem definition

In this study, the processed Cleveland subset of the UCI Heart Disease dataset was used40. The dataset contains 303 patient records, 13 clinical input attributes, and one target variable. The original target variable contained five class values. In the processed file, 164 records were labeled as 0, 55 records as 1, 36 records as 2, 35 records as 3, and 13 records as 4. After binary conversion, the dataset contained 164 disease-absent records and 139 disease-present records. Thus, the binary classification task was performed on the full set of 303 records using the 13 clinical input variables. The 13 clinical input attributes are age, sex, chest pain type, resting blood pressure, serum cholesterol, fasting blood sugar, resting electrocardiographic result, maximum heart rate achieved, exercise-induced angina, ST depression induced by exercise relative to rest, slope of the peak exercise ST segment, number of major vessels colored by fluoroscopy, and thallium-related diagnostic status. In the processed Cleveland file, these variables are denoted as age, sex, cp., trestbps, chol, fbs, restecg, thalach, exang, oldpeak, slope, ca., and thal, respectively.

The Cleveland subset is widely used in heart disease prediction studies as a benchmark dataset for methodological comparison3,6,8,9. In the present study, the dataset was used to construct a controlled binary classification task in which the compared QI variants were evaluated under the same data structure and target definition.

In the original dataset, the target variable is represented as a multiclass disease-status variable. A value of 0 indicates the absence of heart disease, whereas values from 1 to 4 indicate the presence of heart disease at different severity levels. Following common practice in Cleveland-based heart disease prediction studies, all values from 1 to 4 were merged into a single disease-present class, and the target variable was converted into a binary classification label2,3,6,8,9. Data preprocessing

Missing entries in the processed Cleveland data file used in this study were coded as question marks and were converted into a numerical missing-value format before model training. Prior work on the same Cleveland dataset has also reported missing values specifically in the ca. and thal attributes2. In the processed file used in this study, missing entries were present only in ca. and thal. The ca. attribute contained four missing values, and the thal attribute contained two missing values. Under the fixed stratified train-test split with a test ratio of 0.20 and random seed 42, the training subset contained 242 records, including 131 disease-absent and 111 disease-present cases. The held-out test subset contained 61 records, including 33 disease-absent and 28 disease-present cases. In the same split, one ca. value and one thal value were missing in the training subset, while three ca. values and one thal value were missing in the held-out test subset. Therefore, these attributes were converted to numerical type after missing-value coding so that imputation and subsequent modeling steps could be applied consistently.

Unified candidate solution representation

Recent studies have increasingly adopted unified representations that combine FS and discrete hyperparameter optimization in wrapper-based and metaheuristic settings6,46. In this study, FS and classifier hyperparameter tuning were handled as parts of the same candidate solution. This representation follows the wrapper-based FS setting, where the feature subset and classifier configuration are evaluated jointly through the same learning objective12,13,47. After decoding, each candidate solution was represented by two components, which are a feature mask and a classifier-specific hyperparameter vector. The unified decoded candidate solution is defined in (6).6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{C}_{i}=\left({m}_{i},{\lambda\:}_{i}\right),\:\:\:{m}_{i}\in\:{\left\{\mathrm{0,1}\right\}}^{p},\:\:\:{\lambda\:}_{i}=\left({\lambda\:}_{i,1},{\lambda\:}_{i,2},\dots\:,{\lambda\:}_{i,R}\right)$$\end{document}

In (6), Ci denotes the decoded candidate solution of the i-th individual or particle, mi denotes the binary feature mask, p denotes the number of clinical input attributes, λi denotes the hyperparameter vector, and R denotes the number of optimized hyperparameters for the classifier under evaluation. The feature mask determines which of the 13 clinical attributes are retained, while the hyperparameter vector determines the discrete configuration of the classifier being optimized. Also, λi should be interpreted as the decoded classifier-specific hyperparameter vector of candidate solution Ci rather than as a single scalar coefficient. Each component of λi is selected from the finite candidate set of the corresponding classifier hyperparameter.

A decoded feature mask was required to contain at least one selected feature. Since an empty subset cannot be used for classifier training in a wrapper-based FS setting, an all-zero mask was repaired deterministically. This validity condition is given in (7)12,13,48.7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\sum\:_{j=1}^{p}{m}_{i,j}\ge\:1$$\end{document}

In (7), mi, j=1 denotes the binary decision indicating whether the j-th clinical attribute is selected in candidate solution Ci. The binary feature mask formulation was adopted by drawing on wrapper-based feature subset search, where candidate attribute subsets are evaluated through classifier performance12,13. The condition in (7) requires that at least one attribute remains selected before model evaluation. If a decoded candidate solution produced an empty attribute mask, the mask was repaired deterministically as an implementation rule. In the continuous QI representations, the attribute with the highest decoded attribute value was activated. In the binary QI representations, the first attribute position in the dataset order was activated as the fixed fallback attribute. This fallback was not used as an attribute-preference mechanism, but only as a deterministic validity repair for otherwise infeasible empty masks. No random repair operation was applied. This rule prevents classifier training with an empty input matrix, and the same repair rule was applied to all QI variants.

The hyperparameter component was defined over finite discrete candidate sets. In this study, this design was adopted by drawing on wrapper-based optimization and QI-based FS settings in which candidate solutions are searched over discrete alternatives12,47,49,50. For the r-th hyperparameter, the candidate set is defined in (8).8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{H}_{r}=\left\{{h}_{r,0},\:{h}_{r,1},\:\dots\:{h}_{r,{K}_{r}-1}\right\},\:\:\:{\lambda\:}_{i,r}\in\:{H}_{r}$$\end{document}

In (8), Hr denotes the candidate list of the r-th hyperparameter, Kr denotes the number of admissible values in that list, and λi, r denotes the selected value of the r-th hyperparameter for candidate Ci. Thus, hyperparameter tuning was formulated as discrete index selection rather than continuous parameter estimation.

For the continuous QI formulations, namely QI-PSO and QI-EA, hyperparameter values were obtained by mapping probability-based components to discrete candidate indices. This step follows the probabilistic representation and sampling logic used in QI search formulations12,22. If \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$u_{i,p} + r \in [0,1]$$\end{document} denotes the component assigned to the r-th hyperparameter after the first p feature mask components, the decoding rule is given in (9).9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{idx}_{i,r}=min\left(\lfloor {U}_{i,p+r}{K}_{r} \rfloor,\:\:{K}_{r}-1\right),\:\:\:\:{{\uplambda\:}}_{i,r}={H}_{r}\left[{idx}_{i,r}\right]$$\end{document}

The minimum operation ensures that the index remains in the valid interval even when the mapped value reaches the upper boundary. Therefore, continuous probability-based components are converted into admissible discrete hyperparameter values.

For the binary QI formulations, namely QI-BPSO and QI-BEA, the candidate solution was obtained from a measured bit string. This binary representation follows the general QI evolutionary and binary QI search logic in which probabilistic states are converted into binary decisions through measurement or sampling12,51–53. In this study, each binary candidate was generated by first defining the total bit length and then sampling each bit from the probability induced by the corresponding angle component, as given in (10).10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:L=p+\sum\:_{r=1}^{R}{b}_{r},\:\:\:{b}_{r}=⌈{log}_{2}{K}_{r}⌉,\:\:\:\:{b}_{i,j}\sim Bernoulli\left({\mathrm{sin}}^{2}\left({\theta\:}_{i,j}\right)\right),\:\:\:j=1,\dots\:,\:L$$\end{document}

In (10), L denotes the total length of the measured bit string, p denotes the number of feature mask bits, and br denotes the number of bits allocated to the r-th hyperparameter, and bi, j denotes the j-th measured bit of the i-th candidate. The value sin2(θi, j) defines the probability that the corresponding bit takes the value 1. The first p sampled bits form the feature mask, whereas the remaining sampled bit blocks are used to encode the hyperparameter indices.

The four QI variants differ in candidate generation and update dynamics, but they share the same final decoded structure in (6). In QI-PSO and QI-EA, an angle-based probabilistic representation is transformed into probability-like values through a sine-squared mapping12,22,28,51. The first part of the resulting vector is used for the feature mask, and the remaining part is decoded by (9). In QI-BPSO and QI-BEA, the same probabilistic representation is measured into a binary bit string through Bernoulli sampling according to (10)12,51–53. The first p bits form the feature mask, and the remaining bit blocks are decoded into valid hyperparameter indices using (11) and (12).

This formulation allows continuous and binary representations to be compared under the same final task definition. Despite differences in representation and update dynamics, all four methods are evaluated after decoding into the same two components, namely selected features and classifier hyperparameters. As a result, method comparisons are made over the same joint FS and discrete hyperparameter-tuning problem rather than over different search tasks.

For decoded-candidate validity, two implementation rules were applied. First, at least one clinical feature was required to remain selected according to (7). Second, classifier-specific validity constraints were enforced after hyperparameter decoding. For LR, incompatible solver and penalty combinations were corrected deterministically so that each decoded candidate could be evaluated by the learning algorithm. These validity rules were kept fixed for all QI variants.

Quantum-ınspired particle swarm optimization (QI-PSO)

QI-PSO used in this study is an angle-based probabilistic search formulation in which each particle represents a candidate solution through an angle vector rather than through a deterministic position vector. This formulation follows the QI representation principle in which candidate tendencies are encoded through probability-based states and updated through controlled changes in that representation22,51. In the particle swarm branch, swarm-based guidance is incorporated through personal-best and global-best information, while candidate generation remains based on the angle-based probability profile12.

For the continuous QI-PSO formulation, each particle i at iteration t is represented by a d-dimensional angle vector. This representation was parameterized according to the Q-bit probability-amplitude principle described in QI evolutionary formulations as in22,51. The angle vector used in this study is defined in (13).13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\theta\:}_{i}\left(t\right)=\left({\theta\:}_{i,1}\left(t\right),\:{\theta\:}_{i,2}\left(t\right),\:\dots\:{\theta\:}_{i,d}\left(t\right)\right),\:\:\:{\theta\:}_{i,j}\left(t\right)\in\:\left[0,\frac{\pi\:}{2}\right]$$\end{document}

In (13), θi(t) denotes the angle vector of particle i, j denotes the dimension index, and d denotes the total number of components used to encode the feature mask and the classifier hyperparameter selections. In the continuous representation, the first p dimensions correspond to the feature mask, and the remaining dimensions correspond to the discrete hyperparameter selections.

The angle vector is converted into probability-based values through the sine-squared mapping. The mapping used in this study is defined in (14), following the Q-bit probability-amplitude principle in QI evolutionary representations, where squared amplitudes determine measurement probabilities22,51.14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{u}_{i}\left(t\right)=\left({u}_{i,1}\left(t\right),{u}_{i,2}\left(t\right),\dots\:{u}_{i,d}\left(t\right)\right),\:{\:u}_{i,j}\left(t\right)={sin}^{2}\left({\theta\:}_{i,j}\left(t\right)\right),\:{u}_{i,j}\left(t\right)ϵ\left[\mathrm{0,1}\right],\:\mathrm{j}=1,\dots\:,\mathrm{d}$$\end{document}

In (14), ui(t) denotes the probability-based representation vector of particle i at iteration t, and ui, j(t) denotes the probability based value generated from the corresponding angle component θi, j(t). Since θi, j(t) is bounded in [0,π/2], the sine-squared transformation maps each component into the unit interval. In the FS part, larger values of ui, j(t) increase the tendency of the corresponding feature to be retained after thresholding.

For the FS part, the first p components of ui(t) are converted into a binary feature mask by thresholding. The binary mask representation was used by taking wrapper-based feature subset search as the methodological basis, where the selected subset is evaluated through the learning objective12,13. The thresholding rule used in this study is given in (15).15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{m}_{i,k}\left(t\right)=\left\{\begin{array}{c}1,{u}_{ik}\left(k\right)>0.5\\\:0,\:{u}_{ik}\left(k\right)\le\:\:0.5\end{array}\:\:\:(k=1,\dots\:,p)\right.$$\end{document}

In the Eq. (15), mi, k(t) indicates whether feature the k-th clinical attribute is selected by particle i at iteration t. If the resulting mask violates the non-empty mask condition in (7), the deterministic repair rule described in Sect. 2.3 is applied. The remaining components of ui(t) are decoded into classifier hyperparameter values using the discrete indexing rule in (9). Thus, each particle is evaluated after being decoded into the candidate structure defined in (6).

The fitness of a decoded particle is computed using stratified internal cross-validation on the training data. Since the same fitness definition is used for all four QI variants, the common objective is expressed in (16).16\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:F\left({C}_{i}\right)=\frac{1}{K}\sum\:_{l=1}^{K}{Acc}_{i}^{\left(l\right)},\:\:\:{Acc}_{i}^{\left(l\right)}=\frac{{TP}_{i}^{\left(l\right)}+{TN}_{i}^{\left(l\right)}}{{TP}_{i}^{\left(l\right)}+{TN}_{i}^{\left(l\right)}+{FP}_{i}^{\left(l\right)}+{FN}_{i}^{\left(l\right)}}$$\end{document}

In (16), F(Ci) denotes the fitness value of candidate solution Ci, K denotes the number of internal validation folds, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{Acc}_{i}^{\left(l\right)}$$\end{document} denotes the validation accuracy obtained in fold l. The terms TP, TN, FP, and FN denote true positive, true negative, false positive, and false negative counts, respectively. This wrapper-based formulation evaluates the feature subset and classifier hyperparameter configuration jointly through the same learning objective12,13,23.

After fitness evaluation, each particle stores its personal-best state and the swarm stores the global-best state. The personal best of particle i is the angle vector associated with the highest fitness value obtained by that particle up to iteration t. The global-best is the angle vector associated with the highest fitness value obtained by any particle in the swarm up to iteration t. These two reference states guide the next angle update.

The QI-PSO update is performed in the angle space. First, the current angle vector is blended with the persona-best and global-best angle vectors. This step incorporates the particle swarm memory structure into the QI representation, since PSO-based search uses the particle-level best state and the swarm-level best state to guide subsequent updates12. The blending rule used in this study is shown in (17).17\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\stackrel{\sim}{\theta\:}}_{i}\left(t\right)=\frac{1}{2}{\theta\:}_{i}\left(t\right)+\frac{1}{2}\left({w}_{p}{\theta\:}_{pbest,i}\left(t\right)+{w}_{g}{\theta\:}_{gbest}\left(t\right)\right),\:\:\:{w}_{p}+{w}_{g}=1$$\end{document}

In (17), \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\stackrel{\sim}{\theta\:}}_{i}\left(t\right)$$\end{document} denotes the intermediate angle vector after blending, θpbest, i(t) denotes the personal best angle vector of particle i, and θgbest(t) denotes the global-best angle vector of the swarm. The coefficients wp and wg determine the relative contribution of the personal-best and global-best directions and are normalized so that their sum is one. This blending step is the particle swarm guidance component of the method, whereas the candidate generation remains governed by the QI probability profile in (14).

After blending, each angle component is adjusted by a rotation-based steering rule toward the probability profile of the global-best particle. Rotation-based probability adjustment is a common mechanism in QI evolutionary representations, where probability amplitudes are modified to bias the search toward better solutions22,51. The rotation rule used in this study is given in (18).18\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\theta\:}_{i,j}^{rot}\left(t+1\right)=\left\{\begin{array}{c}\mathrm{min}\left(\frac{\pi\:}{2},{\stackrel{\sim}{\theta\:}}_{i,j}\left(t\right)+\varDelta\:\left(t\right)\right),\:\:{sin}^{2}\left({\theta\:}_{gbest,j}\right(t\left)\right)>{sin}^{2}\left({\stackrel{\sim}{\theta\:}}_{i,j}\right(t\left)\right)\\\:\mathrm{max}\left(0,{\stackrel{\sim}{\theta\:}}_{i,j}\left(t\right)-\varDelta\:\left(t\right)\right),\:\:{sin}^{2}\left({\theta\:}_{gbest,j}\left(t\right)\right)<{sin}^{2}\left({\stackrel{\sim}{\theta\:}}_{i,j}\right(t\left)\right)\\\:{\stackrel{\sim}{\theta\:}}_{i,j}\left(t\right),\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:in\:case\:of\:a\:tie\end{array}\right.$$\end{document}

In (18), \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\theta\:}_{i,j}^{rot}\left(t+1\right)$$\end{document} denotes the intermediate angle component after rotation, and ∆(t) denotes the iteration dependent rotation step. The rule increases the angle when the global-best probability is higher, decreases it when the global best probability is lower, and leaves it unchanged when the two probabilities are equal. This update moves the particle probability profile toward the global-best profile through controlled angle changes.

To reduce update intensity as the inner-loop progresses, a time-dependent scaling term was used. As discussed in QI particle swarm-based search formulations, coefficient settings can regulate search behavior by affecting convergence and exploration12,23. Therefore, in this study, the effective rotation step was decreased linearly across iterations, as defined in (19).19\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\beta\:\left(t\right)={\beta\:}_{max}-\left({\beta\:}_{max}-{\beta\:}_{min}\right)\frac{t-1}{T-1},\:\:\:\varDelta\:\left(t\right)=\beta\:\left(t\right){\varDelta\:}_{0}$$\end{document}

In (19), T denotes the total number of inner-loop iterations, βmax and βmin denote the upper and lower bounds of the scaling schedule, and Δ0 denotes the sampled base rotation step. This schedule decreases the effective rotation step over iterations and therefore gradually shifts the search toward more conservative updates.

Finally, Gaussian perturbation is added to preserve diversity in the angle representation, and the resulting angle vector is clipped to the valid interval. The reason for using Gaussian randomness is that it is also used in QI particle swarm-based search to improve the exploration and convergence behavior23. The perturbation and clipping rule used in this study is given in (20).20\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\theta\:}_{i}\left(t+1\right)=clip\left(\:{\theta\:}_{i}^{rot}\left(t+1\right)+{\epsilon\:}_{i}\left(t\right),0,\frac{\pi\:}{2}\right),\:\:\:{\epsilon\:}_{i}\left(t\right)\sim N\left(0,{\sigma\:}^{2}{I}_{d}\right)$$\end{document}

Through this step, QI-PSO combines exploitation guided by the personal-best and global-best states with stochastic perturbation that helps preserve diversity in the probability-based representation.

Quantum-ınspired evolutionary algorithm (QI-EA)

QI-EA used in this study is a probabilistic evolutionary search approach that iteratively improves the solution space through a QI state representation rather than through the parent-selection and crossover loop of classical evolutionary approaches. In this formulation, candidate tendencies are represented through Q-bit based probability states, and the population is gradually biased toward better solutions by updating this representation51,57–61. The broader QI evolutionary line reports the use of population structure, representation, and update operators to guide the search process while maintaining diversity51,57–60. In the present study, QI-EA was adapted to the joint FS and discrete hyperparameter-tuning task. QI and evolutionary feature subset selection studies commonly evaluate feature masks through classifier performance in a wrapper-based search process35,62–64. Accordingly, QI-EA employs rotation-based steering toward the elite solution as the exploitation component and Gaussian perturbation as the diversity-preserving exploration component.

As in QI-PSO, each individual i at iteration t is represented by the same d-dimensional angle-vector structure defined in (13), and the probability-based representation vector is obtained using the sine-squared mapping in (14). The first p components of ui(t) are converted into a binary feature mask using (15), whereas the remaining components are decoded into classifier hyperparameter values using (9). If the decoded mask violates the non-empty mask condition in (7), the deterministic repair rule described in Sect. 2.3 is applied. The resulting candidate solution is evaluated using the common wrapper-based fitness function in (16).

The QI-EA population uses bounded angle vectors as the individual representation, drawing on the Q-bit based population representation and operator structure reported in QI evolutionary algorithms51,60. In this study, the initial population was generated around a central angle value with small random perturbations. The initialization rule is given in (21).21\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\theta\:}_{i}\left(0\right)=clip\left({\theta\:}_{init}{1}_{d}+{n}_{i},0,\frac{\pi\:}{2}\right),\:\:\:{n}_{i}\sim N\left(0,{\sigma\:}_{init}^{2}{I}_{d}\right)$$\end{document}

In (21), θi(0) denotes the initial angle vector of individual i, θinit denotes the central initial angle value, 1d denotes a d-dimensional vector of ones, ni denotes the initialization perturbation vector, σinit denotes the initial noise intensity, and Id denotes the d-dimensional identity matrix. The clipping operation bounds every angle component within [0,π/2]. This initialization produces a bounded population of QI probability-based candidate representations while preserving initial diversity.

At each iteration, all individuals are decoded and evaluated according to the common candidate representation and fitness definition. QI evolutionary algorithms commonly update probability-based representations by using information from high quality or best observed solutions51,58–61. In this study, the elite reference was defined as the best solution reached up to iteration t. Quantum-ınspired binary particle swarm optimization (QI-BPSO)

QI-BPSO used in this study applies particle swarm-based guidance to a measurement based binary QI representation. Unlike the continuous QI-PSO formulation, the candidate solution in QI-BPSO is obtained as a measured bit string. The first p bits determine the feature mask, and the remaining bit blocks determine the classifier hyperparameter indices. This binary formulation draws on binary QI FS and QI-based binary search settings in which probabilistic representations are converted into binary candidate solutions for discrete optimization tasks12,51–53,55.

As described in Sect. 2.3, each particle is represented by an L-dimensional angle vector, and the measured bit string is generated using the Bernoulli sampling rule in (10). The first p measured bits form the feature mask, while the remaining bit blocks are decoded into discrete hyperparameter values using (11) and (12). If the measured feature mask violates the non-empty mask condition in (7), the deterministic repair rule described after (7) is applied so that at least one feature enters model evaluation as described in Sect. 2.3. The resulting candidate solution is evaluated using the common wrapper-based fitness function in (16).

After fitness evaluation, QI-BPSO stores both particle-level and swarm-level best solutions. The personal best of particle i is the measured bit string that produced the highest fitness value for that particle up to iteration t. The global best is the measured bit string that produced the highest fitness value among all particles up to iteration t. Quantum-ınspired binary evolutionary algorithm (QI-BEA)

In this study, QI-BEA denotes the modified binary implementation of the QI-EA update strategy in the common experimental design. It combines the measurement-based binary QI representation described in Sect. 2.3 with the elite-guided evolutionary update used in QI-EA. Each individual is represented by an L-dimensional angle vector, and the candidate solution is obtained by measuring this probabilistic representation into a binary bit string. The first p bits form the feature mask, and the remaining bit blocks are decoded into discrete classifier hyperparameter indices using the binary decoding rules defined in (10) to (12). This binary representation draws on Q-bit based population representations and binary QI search formulations used for discrete optimization problems51–53,55.

The modification relative to QI-EA is mainly representational, but it also changes the form of the elite reference used during the update. QI-EA decodes the continuous probability-based representation directly into feature and hyperparameter decisions and steers individuals toward an elite angle or probability profile. By contrast, QI-BEA first measures the angle-based state into a binary bit string and then uses the best measured binary pattern as the elite reference for angle-space updates. QI-BEA is not introduced as a new optimization paradigm. It is used as a binary adaptation of the QI-EA update strategy for the joint FS and discrete hyperparameter-tuning task.

The use of QI-EA design in discrete combinatorial optimization has been reported in improved QI-EA studies58, and QI-EA based variants have also been used in assignment and balancing type optimization settings55,61. In QI-BEA, the best binary solution observed up to iteration t is used as the elite reference pattern, and the population is steered toward this binary elite pattern through rotation-based probability adjustment. Population diversity and operator design are further supported by QI-EA studies reporting diversity-preserving and reinitialization operators for reducing premature convergence60.

At each iteration, every individual is measured into a bit string using the Bernoulli sampling rule in (10). The measured bit string is then decoded into the candidate structure defined in (6). If the measured feature mask violates the non-empty mask condition in (7), the deterministic repair rule described after (7) is applied so that at least one attribute enters model evaluation. The resulting candidate solution is evaluated using the common wrapper-based fitness function in (16).

Experimental design

The experimental design was implemented in a Python 3.12 environment using scikit-learn components for preprocessing, stratified data splitting, classifier construction, internal cross-validation, and final metric computation. The experiments were executed on a computer equipped with an Intel i7 2.3 GHz processor and 16 GB RAM. The design addressed joint FS and classifier hyperparameter tuning on the Cleveland heart disease dataset in a single unified search space. QI-PSO, QI-EA, QI-BPSO, and QI-BEA were compared using the same evaluation protocol.

The dataset was divided into training and held-out test subsets using a fixed stratified split so that the class distribution was preserved in both subsets. Stratified partitioning has been preferred in clinical machine-learning studies to preserve class balance between training and test subsets, and the same principle was adopted in this study65,66. The test ratio was fixed at 0.20, and the split was generated using a single fixed seed value. To keep data exposure identical for all methods, the same fixed stratified partition was used for all QI variants and classifier families66,67. The training subset was used for candidate evaluation and optimization, and the held-out test subset was reserved for final post-optimization assessment. The experimental workflow is shown in Fig. 2.

Fig. 2Experimental Workflow of the QI-Based Feature selection and Hyperparameter-Tuning Design.

Experimental Workflow of the QI-Based Feature selection and Hyperparameter-Tuning Design.

As shown in Fig. 2, the QI variants operate in the training-stage optimization block. Candidate solutions are generated, decoded into feature masks and classifier-specific hyperparameter configurations, evaluated by internal cross-validation, and updated through the corresponding QI mechanism. The best selected solution is then used to train the final classifier model before held-out test evaluation. The held-out test subset remains outside the optimization loop and is used only for final post-optimization performance assessment. During optimization, candidate fitness was computed only on the training subset using stratified 5-fold internal CV. The internal CV structure was generated with shuffling and the fixed random seed value of 42. In each fold, the decoded feature mask and classifier-specific hyperparameter configuration were applied within the training-stage evaluation procedure, and the fold accuracies were averaged to obtain the candidate fitness value. The held-out test subset was not used during internal CV, candidate fitness computation, FS, hyperparameter selection, or optimizer update.

The fixed experimental settings were defined before the outer-loop algorithm-setting trials were executed. These settings established the common evaluation environment used for all QI variants and classifier families. These selections were drawn from predefined finite candidate sets so that FS and discrete hyperparameter tuning could be handled jointly in the same candidate solution. The candidate sets in Table 3 defined the classifier-level part of the decoded candidate solution. During candidate decoding, the selected feature mask determined the clinical variables retained for model fitting, and the classifier-specific hyperparameter indices determined the model configuration. Thus, each candidate was evaluated as a joint feature subset and hyperparameter-configuration solution under the internal CV Accuracy objective. Classifier hyperparameter tuning was handled as discrete candidate selection within the unified candidate-solution representation rather than as a separate post-hoc grid search. For each classifier family, the admissible hyperparameter values were predefined as finite candidate sets, as reported in Table 3. During optimization, each QI method decoded both the feature mask and the classifier-specific hyperparameter vector from the same candidate solution. Therefore, FS and hyperparameter tuning were performed jointly under the same fitness function, internal CV structure, and evaluation budget.

The complete outer-loop workflow combined the fixed settings in Table 1, the sampled optimizer settings in Table 2, and the classifier-specific candidate sets in Table 3. At the beginning of each outer-trial, one algorithm-setting combination was sampled from Table 2. The sampled setting was assigned to the corresponding QI variant, and the fixed experimental settings in Table 1 remained unchanged. The complete outer-loop workflow is illustrated in Fig. 3.

Outer-loop workflow of the experimental design.

As shown in Fig. 3, each outer-trial started with algorithm-setting sampling and then proceeded to inner-loop optimization. During the inner-loop, candidate solutions were generated, decoded into a feature mask and classifier-specific hyperparameter configuration, repaired when necessary, evaluated by internal CV Accuracy, and updated according to the corresponding QI search mechanism. At the end of the inner-loop, the best candidate solution from that outer-trial was recorded together with its internal CV Accuracy, selected feature count, decoded hyperparameters, and runtime. The outer loop recorded optimizer outcomes across sampled optimizer-control settings under the fixed benchmark protocol.

During each inner-loop optimization run, the corresponding QI variant generated or updated a population of candidate states using the algorithm settings sampled at the outer level. Each candidate state was decoded into the unified candidate representation defined in Sect. 2.3, consisting of a feature mask and a classifier-specific hyperparameter configuration. The decoded candidates were then passed through the same validity checking, internal validation, fitness computation, and update sequence. The inner-loop candidate-evaluation workflow is shown in Fig. 4.

Fig. 4Inner-loop candidate-evaluation workflow.

Inner-loop candidate-evaluation workflow.

As shown in Fig. 4, candidate evaluation began after decoding. The decoded feature mask and hyperparameter configuration were first checked for validity according to the rules described in Sect. 2.3. At least one feature was required to remain selected, and classifier-specific validity constraints were applied before model evaluation. This step was necessary because an infeasible decoded candidate cannot be evaluated through the common wrapper-based classifier pipeline.

After validity checking, each candidate was evaluated on the training subset using stratified 5-fold internal CV Accuracy, following optimization designs in which candidate feature subsets or parameter configurations are evaluated through cross-validation during model selection68–70. In each internal fold, the classifier was trained using only the selected features and the decoded hyperparameter configuration, and fold-level accuracy was computed on the corresponding validation fold. The candidate fitness value was then obtained as the mean accuracy over the five internal validation folds. If fitting or prediction failed in any internal validation fold, the accuracy of that fold was assigned as 0.0.

As described in Sect. 2.3, classifier-specific validity constraints were also enforced after hyperparameter decoding. This was required for LR because the decoded solver and penalty values do not form a fully unrestricted search space in the LR implementation used in this study. According to71, elastic-net regularization is supported only by the saga solver, liblinear supports L1 and L2 penalties but not elastic-net, and lbfgs, newton-cg, and sag support only L2 or no-regularization settings. Incompatible LR solver-penalty combinations were corrected deterministically before model evaluation so that every decoded LR candidate could be executed under the same internal CV protocol. This validity rule was applied before fitness computation and was kept fixed for all QI variants.

A conservative failure-handling rule was applied during internal validation because some decoded configurations may still fail during model fitting or prediction owing to numerical instability, convergence failure, or classifier-specific implementation constraints. Similar implementation-level issues have been reported in optimized LR experiments, where some hyperparameter combinations were incompatible or did not converge during testing72. Therefore, if model fitting or prediction failed in any internal validation fold, the accuracy of that fold was assigned as 0.0. This value was used because accuracy is bounded below by zero, and a candidate that cannot be successfully evaluated under the common CV protocol cannot provide valid predictive evidence for that fold. The zero-fold score penalized unstable candidates under the same CV Accuracy objective and avoided introducing an additional manually tuned penalty coefficient. Candidate fitness was then computed as the mean of the five fold-level accuracy values, including any zero-scored failed folds. The same failure-handling rule was applied to all QI variants and classifier families.

The resulting fitness value was returned to the corresponding QI update mechanism. In QI-PSO and QI-BPSO, the fitness value was used to update personal-best and global-best information. In QI-EA and QI-BEA, it was used to guide population-level selection and update operations. The inner-loop continued until the fixed iteration budget was reached. The methods differed in their representation and update dynamics, but each decoded candidate was evaluated through the same wrapper-based internal validation procedure.

CV Accuracy was used as the internal fitness criterion because wrapper-based optimization requires candidate feature masks and hyperparameter configurations to be ranked by a single scalar score during the training-stage search. A scalar internal validation score allows heterogeneous candidate solutions to be compared in the same optimization loop, even when they contain different feature subsets and classifier configurations. Similar designs have used cross-validation accuracy to evaluate candidate feature subsets or parameter configurations during optimization, while keeping a separate test or external validation set for final assessment68–70. CV Accuracy was computed only on the training subset. The held-out test subset was not used for candidate generation, fitness computation, feature mask selection, hyperparameter selection, or QI-variant selection, in order to avoid information leakage and optimistic post-selection performance estimation19,20,39.

After all outer-trials had been completed, the final selected solution was determined according to the internal CV Accuracy criterion during the training-stage optimization process. For each classifier family and QI variant, this solution contained the selected feature mask and the decoded classifier-specific hyperparameter configuration. The final selected feature model was refitted on the training subset using only the selected Cleveland variables and the corresponding decoded hyperparameters.

A matched no-FS baseline was also evaluated for each final selected feature model to contextualize the selected-subset results against full-feature model behavior, as similar FS studies have compared selected feature configurations with full-feature or non-FS settings35,73. In this baseline, the selected feature mask was replaced with the full set of 13 Cleveland clinical variables. No-FS operation was applied to the baseline model. The classifier family and the final decoded hyperparameter configuration were kept the same as those of the corresponding selected feature QI model. Thus, the selected feature model and its matched no-FS baseline differed only in the feature subset used for training and testing. Both models were evaluated on the same held-out test subset using the same final metric set. The held-out test subset was not used to select the feature mask, tune the hyperparameters, choose the QI variant, or adjust the baseline configuration. The no-FS comparison was used only as a post-optimization baseline analysis and did not change the selected feature masks, decoded hyperparameters, internal CV fitness values, or final model selection criterion.

Final model performance was evaluated using multiple held-out test metrics rather than Accuracy alone. Accuracy was retained because it was also used as the common internal fitness criterion, but it was not used as the only final evaluation measure. In healthcare classification studies, sensitivity, specificity, precision, recall, F1-score, MCC, and ROC-AUC have been reported together with accuracy to describe positive-case detection, negative-case discrimination, class-wise balance, error trade-offs, and threshold-dependent separability36,68,74. Accuracy, Precision, Recall, Specificity, F1-score, MCC, and ROC-AUC were computed for each final selected feature model and for each matched no-FS baseline. The same metric set was applied to all classifier families and QI variants so that final held-out test comparisons were based on the same post-optimization evaluation protocol.

After fixed-decision-rule test metrics were computed, precision-recall curves and threshold sensitivity profiles were generated on held-out test scores as post-hoc analyses. The fixed test metrics were obtained from class labels produced by the default classifier decision rule. No operating threshold was optimized on the held-out test subset, because threshold selection on the same test labels could bias label-based metrics75–81. The threshold profiles were used only to describe how Accuracy, Precision, Recall, Specificity, F1-score, and MCC changed across decision thresholds. This post-hoc threshold analysis did not reselect the final model, alter the selected feature masks, or change the decoded hyperparameters.

Feature subset summaries were generated after the final selected solutions had been obtained. For each classifier family and QI variant, the decoded feature mask of the final selected candidate was recorded. A feature was counted as selected when its corresponding mask value was equal to 1, as defined in Sect. 2.3. The selected feature count and the identities of the selected Cleveland variables were then summarized for each final model. Since the final models had already been selected according to internal CV Accuracy before held-out test evaluation, the feature subset summaries were used only to characterize the final selected solutions and to examine whether some clinical variables appeared repeatedly among QI variants or classifier families.

The recurrent feature patterns were interpreted as final-subset consensus patterns, not as full FS stability estimates. This distinction was made because FS stability is evaluated from the variation or similarity of selected subsets over repeated selection outputs or perturbed data conditions82. Experimental FS studies have also used repeated independent runs and feature-frequency scores to identify robust feature sets83. In the present study, complete trial-wise feature mask histories were not archived for all outer-loop runs. Therefore, the reported summaries should be read as descriptive final-subset patterns under the fixed benchmark protocol, not as trial-level selection-frequency or split-independent stability estimates. No additional features were added, removed, or re-ranked after the held-out test results were computed. Thus, the feature subset analysis complemented the performance results without changing the selected feature masks, decoded hyperparameters, final classifier configurations, or held-out test metrics.

Runtime and computational cost were recorded for each outer-trial because wrapper-based FS and metaheuristic optimization require repeated candidate evaluation rather than a single model fit. Each candidate solution must be decoded, converted into a feature subset and classifier configuration, trained under the selected feature space, and evaluated through the internal validation procedure. Medical wrapper-based FS studies have reported computational cost together with accuracy and selected feature size, and binary wrapper optimization studies have also emphasized that repeated classifier-based subset evaluation increases computational burden14,17.

In the present study, runtime described the empirical computational burden of QI search under the fixed execution environment. The recorded time captured the elapsed optimization time required by the sampled optimizer settings, fixed population size, fixed iteration budget, and stratified internal CV structure. Runtime was not interpreted as a hardware-independent algorithmic property because it also depends on implementation, processor, memory, classifier family, selected feature count, candidate evaluations, and validation design. CV Accuracy, selected feature count, and runtime were recorded together so that validation level, subset compactness, and computational burden could be interpreted jointly16,84,85.

The theoretical computational cost of the proposed design is dominated by repeated wrapper-based candidate evaluation. Let O denote the number of outer-trials, T the number of inner-loop iterations, P the population size, K the number of internal validation folds, and Cclf the classifier training and validation cost for one decoded candidate in one fold. Since each inner-loop candidate is decoded and evaluated through stratified internal cross-validation, the dominant training-stage cost can be expressed as O(O×T×P×K×Cclf). This formulation follows the computational reasoning used in wrapper-based FS complexity analysis, where the fitness assessment is treated as the dominant cost component16,86. Because wrapper-based medical FS studies have also reported computational cost together with classification accuracy and selected feature size, runtime was additionally recorded in the present study as an empirical cost measure under the fixed experimental protocol14. Decoding, validity repair, QI state update, and result logging introduce additional implementation costs, but these are lower-order operations relative to repeated classifier fitting and validation under the internal CV procedure.

Outer-trial aggregation was performed after all optimizer-control setting trials had been completed. Each outer-trial produced three main summaries: the best internal CV Accuracy reached during inner-loop optimization, the number of selected features in the corresponding decoded solution, and the elapsed runtime. These quantities were summarized over outer-trials for each QI variant and classifier family. This aggregation characterized repeated optimizer behavior across sampled optimizer-control settings while the evaluation protocol remained fixed. Even when the data partition, validation design, population size, iteration budget, and fitness definition are fixed, population-based metaheuristic optimizers may follow different search trajectories because their search process depends on stochastic initialization, exploration-exploitation dynamics, and randomized solution updates86,87. Since repeated metaheuristic runs are commonly summarized through fitness values, classification accuracy, selected feature count, convergence behavior, computational time, mean values, standard deviations, boxplots, and non-parametric tests, the present study also treated the repeated outer-trial outputs as distributional optimizer results rather than as single-run outcome14,86–88.

Statistical comparisons were applied to the outer-trial distributions rather than to the held-out test results. This separation was necessary because the held-out test subset was reserved for final evaluation after model selection19,20,39, while the outer-trial distributions describe repeated optimization outcomes obtained under the same training subset and internal validation structure. The statistical analysis of outer-trial CV Accuracy and selected feature outcomes was used to evaluate differences in validation-level search behavior and feature subset compactness among QI variants under matched experimental conditions. Runtime was summarized as an empirical computational-cost measure under the same protocol. The detailed statistical thresholds and post-hoc comparison procedure are described in Sect. 2.9.

Reproducibility was supported by using a fixed random seed throughout the experimental pipeline and by saving the main configuration and result artifacts for each experimental run. Fixed random-state handling was used to keep the data partition, internal validation structure, stochastic QI operations, and classifier-level random states reproducible under the implemented protocol, as reported in66,67. The same seed was used for the stratified train-test split, internal validation structure, stochastic QI operations, and classifier-level random states when applicable. The saved configuration included the classifier family, seed value, test ratio, internal fold count, number of outer-trials, inner-loop iteration count, population size, data path, and feature names. Each QI variant also produced output files containing outer-trial records, aggregated outer-trial summaries, best sampled algorithm configuration, best decoded solution, and inner-loop history of the best trial. Final held-out test outputs included the test metrics, selected features, decoded hyperparameters, classification report, confusion matrix, and ROC curve when score-based evaluation was available. These artifacts were used to trace the experimental process from fixed settings to selected solutions and then to final test evaluation.

The reproducibility statement was limited to the implemented experimental environment, fixed seed handling, and the experimental settings reported in the manuscript. Runtime values may still vary by hardware, operating system, library builds, and processor load. For this reason, runtime was interpreted as an empirical cost measurement under the stated execution environment rather than as a hardware-independent algorithmic property.

Overfitting and information leakage were controlled by separating training-stage optimization from held-out test evaluation. The held-out test subset was not used for candidate generation, fitness computation, FS, hyperparameter selection, QI-variant selection, threshold optimization, or outer-trial statistical comparison. During internal CV, the Pipeline was fitted separately in each training fold so that imputation and, where applicable, standardization were learned only from fold-training data. After optimization, the final selected model was refitted on the full training subset and evaluated once on the held-out test subset. This design separated training-stage search, final held-out evaluation, and post-hoc analyses.

The additional analyses were interpreted according to their methodological role. Outer-trial summaries characterized optimizer behavior, runtime characterized empirical cost, feature subset summaries described final-subset patterns, threshold analyses described post-hoc operating behavior, and matched no-FS baselines provided full-feature comparisons. None of these analyses changed the selected masks, decoded hyperparameters, or final held-out test metrics.

Statistical analysis and ınterpretation

The statistical analysis was designed to separate final held-out test performance from repeated training-stage optimizer behavior under sampled optimizer-control settings. For each final selected solution, Accuracy, Precision, Recall, Specificity, F1-score, MCC, and ROC-AUC were computed on the reserved held-out test subset. These values were reported as fixed test-set performance summaries under the predefined benchmark protocol. For the held-out test metrics, 95% confidence intervals were reported to describe estimation uncertainty around the observed test performance. The 95% confidence level corresponds to an uncertainty level of α = 0.05 and was interpreted descriptively as the range of plausible values around each reported metric under the applied interval-estimation procedure. Wider intervals were interpreted as greater estimation uncertainty, and narrower intervals were interpreted as more stable held-out estimates37. Confidence intervals were not used as the sole basis for declaring statistical significance between methods26,84,86. Since each held-out test metric was computed once after final model selection on the fixed held-out test subset, between-method significance testing was not applied to the held-out test point estimates. Instead, statistical significance analysis was applied to the repeated outer-trial distributions, because these distributions represent optimizer outcomes obtained under sampled optimizer-control settings while the training subset, internal CV structure, search space, and evaluation budget were kept fixed. Held-out test metrics were therefore interpreted as final post-optimization performance, whereas outer-trial CV Accuracy, runtime, and selected-feature count were treated as repeated optimizer outcomes under sampled optimizer-control settings and were summarized over 100 trials.

For the repeated outer-loop analysis, each QI method was executed using the same training subset, internal cross-validation structure, search space, and evaluation budget. The resulting distributions of best inner-loop CV Accuracy, runtime, and selected feature count were summarized using mean, standard deviation, median, and interquartile range. The mean and standard deviation were used to describe the average level and variability of the outer-trial outcomes. The median and interquartile range were additionally reported because stochastic optimization outputs may be skewed or affected by occasional extreme runs. The interquartile range was defined as the difference between the 75th percentile and the 25th percentile, and it was used as a robust summary of the central 50% of the outer-trial distribution. Similar repeated-run summaries, convergence profiles, runtime reports, and non-parametric comparisons have been used in metaheuristic and wrapper-based FS studies7,11,86.

Accordingly, the significance tests were used to support comparisons of repeated training-stage optimization behavior rather than to claim split-independent superiority from a single held-out test partition. Between-method differences in outer-trial distributions were evaluated separately for each classifier and outcome variable using the Kruskal-Wallis test as the omnibus comparison. A p-value lower than 0.05 was interpreted as evidence of a statistically significant difference among the compared QI methods for the corresponding classifier and outcome variable. When the omnibus test was significant, pairwise post-hoc comparisons were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test. Holm correction was applied to the post-hoc p-values to control the family-wise error rate over multiple pairwise comparisons. Pairwise differences were interpreted as statistically significant only when the Holm-adjusted p-value was lower than 0.05. This workflow follows recent applied machine-learning and metaheuristic optimization studies that used Kruskal-Wallis omnibus testing, Mann-Whitney U post-hoc comparisons, and Holm or Holm-Bonferroni correction for repeated algorithmic performance comparisons, together with the original Holm multiple-testing procedure89–91.

The statistical comparisons were interpreted in relation to the outer-trial outcomes on which they were computed. A significant difference in CV Accuracy was interpreted as a difference in repeated inner-loop validation outcomes between QI methods. A significant difference in runtime was interpreted as a difference in computational cost distributions. A significant difference in selected feature count was interpreted as a difference in feature subset compactness over repeated trials. Thus, the non-parametric tests were used to characterize stochastic optimization behavior under the fixed experimental protocol.

Dıscussıon

The main finding of this study is that QI-PSO, QI-EA, QI-BPSO, and QI-BEA showed classifier-dependent behavior when joint FS and discrete hyperparameter tuning were evaluated under the same fixed train-test split, preprocessing scheme, search space, fitness definition, and evaluation budget. This finding indicates that QI representation and search behavior cannot be interpreted independently of classifier family, operating metric, threshold behavior, feature-reduction objective, and computational cost. The study is best read as a fixed-protocol comparison of QI representation variants in a wrapper-based FS and hyperparameter-tuning setting, not as a search for a universally superior heart disease predictor.

This scope is directly linked to the validation design. The fixed split kept data exposure identical across methods and classifiers, which strengthens internal comparability because each QI variant was evaluated with the same training subset, held-out test subset, internal validation structure, and search budget. At the same time, the design supports within-split comparison rather than split-independent interpretation. The held-out results represent final performance estimates under the fixed benchmark protocol, while the outer-trial summaries represent repeated optimization outcomes obtained under the same split.

Classifier-dependent QI behavior

The classifier-level pattern was visible in the selected-feature held-out results. QI-PSO produced the strongest selected-feature Accuracy for DT and K-NN, whereas QI-BPSO produced the strongest selected-feature Accuracy for SVM. In LR, all four QI methods produced identical held-out test metrics, although their selected feature counts were not identical. This pattern indicates that the predictive effect of each QI variant depended on how the decoded feature mask and classifier-specific hyperparameter configuration interacted with the learning mechanism of the corresponding classifier.

This classifier dependence is methodologically important because all QI variants were evaluated under the shared protocol. The observed differences cannot be attributed to different train-test splits, validation procedures, or hyperparameter candidate spaces. They reflect representation-classifier interaction under controlled benchmark conditions.

The conceptual basis of the QI methods explains why this interaction can occur. Han and Kim (2002) introduced the QI evolutionary algorithm as a probabilistic evolutionary structure in which candidate solutions are encoded by quantum-bit individuals, observed as executable binary solutions, evaluated through a fitness function, and then updated by quantum gates toward better states51. This design matters for the present comparison because the feature mask is generated from a probabilistic representation whose sampling and update behavior can change which variables are retained and which hyperparameter configuration is evaluated. Tayarani-N and Akbarzadeh-T (2014) later showed that QI evolutionary algorithm performance is affected by population structure, diversity preservation, reinitialization, and operator design because these mechanisms control how the search avoids local optima and preserves exploration capacity60. Gharehchopogh (2023) placed these mechanisms within the broader QI metaheuristic literature by showing that quantum-computing concepts are used to improve convergence, exploration, and exploitation in metaheuristic search, and that QI variants include binary, chaotic, hybrid, and multi-objective forms22. In the quantum-behaved particle-swarm line, sampling-based position dynamics and parameter settings also shape search behavior because particle movement is governed by probabilistic sampling around attractor states instead of the velocity-update structure of classical PSO28,29. These evolutionary and particle-swarm mechanisms explain why binary and non-binary QI variants can produce different final masks and classifier-specific hyperparameter combinations even when the experimental protocol is shared.

The K-NN result provides a clear example of representation-dependent behavior within the evolutionary QI line. QI-BEA produced higher selected-feature Accuracy, F1-score, and MCC than QI-EA under the same classifier family and experimental protocol. Since QI-BEA was defined as the measurement-based binary implementation of the QI-EA update strategy, this result suggests that replacing continuous probability-based decoding with binary measurement can alter the final selected-feature error profile. This advantage did not extend to ROC-AUC and did not appear uniformly across all classifier families. The result is better interpreted as classifier-specific representation behavior rather than as a general superiority claim.

The LR result provides the opposite case. Different selected subsets and different optimizer trajectories can still lead to equivalent final LR behavior when the classifier is less sensitive to those mask differences under the fixed split used in this study. Thus, LR appeared less sensitive to QI representation and search-mechanism differences at the final selected-feature level than DT, K-NN, and SVM.

Baseline comparison and threshold effects

The no-FS comparison shows that FS did not provide a uniform advantage across classifier families. For K-NN, selected-feature QI configurations generally produced stronger Accuracy, F1-score, and MCC than their matched no-FS baselines. This result is interpretable through the geometry-sensitive nature of K-NN. Because K-NN assigns class labels through neighborhood relations, weakly informative or redundant dimensions may distort distance calculations and reduce local class separability. Xie et al. (2021) note that high-dimensional feature sets can increase overfitting risk, weaken generalization, and reduce classification performance when redundant or contradictory features are retained10. The selected-feature advantage observed for K-NN suggests that feature reduction made the neighborhood structure more discriminative under the selected hyperparameter configurations.

For DT, the comparison was metric-dependent because selected-feature QI-PSO matched the no-FS Accuracy level, whereas QI-BPSO provided the strongest score-based discrimination. This pattern shows that selected-feature DT behavior cannot be summarized by default-threshold Accuracy alone. A model can provide better score-based discrimination without producing the highest label-based Accuracy under the default decision rule.

The LR and SVM results show the opposite side of the same FS problem. In these classifier families, the no-FS baselines produced stronger held-out results than the selected-feature configurations across the main reported metrics. This pattern suggests that the selected subsets did not preserve, or did not expose to these learners, the predictive information available in the full 13-variable representation. The comparison shows that feature removal improved some models but weakened others under the same fixed protocol. FS is better interpreted as a classifier-dependent modeling choice rather than as an automatically beneficial preprocessing step.

The threshold-dependent results further restrict any single-metric interpretation. Accuracy, ROC-AUC, precision-recall behavior, and threshold sensitivity do not measure the same property of a classifier. Accuracy is computed from predicted class labels under a specific decision rule, while ROC-AUC summarizes score-based discrimination across thresholds, as discussed in92. In medical risk prediction, threshold moving changes the class-label decision rule and can alter threshold-dependent label metrics, whereas ROC-AUC and PR-AUC summarize performance across operating thresholds93. This distinction explains why a selected configuration can perform better in score-based discrimination without producing the highest default-threshold Accuracy. The practical value of a QI variant depends on the intended operating objective, including balanced classification, score-based discrimination, sensitivity-oriented screening, or a specific decision threshold.

Repeated optimizer behavior

The outer-trial analysis adds a distributional layer to the final held-out results. It evaluates whether the QI variants produced different CV Accuracy, runtime, and selected-feature-count distributions while searching the same candidate space. This layer is distinct from final held-out performance because it characterizes repeated training-stage optimizer behavior rather than the final selected models alone.

This separation is methodological rather than corrective. It does not imply that leakage occurred in the present protocol. It clarifies why held-out results and repeated outer-trial distributions answer different questions. Leakage during FS, model selection, or validation can inflate performance estimates, and validation design can affect error estimation under limited-sample conditions19,20,38,39. In the present analysis, the held-out results represent the final selected configurations, while the outer-trial distributions characterize repeated training-stage search behavior.

The statistical results show that QI representation and search behavior were associated with differences in repeated validation distributions for some classifier families, runtime behavior across all classifier families, and selected-subset compactness for DT, LR, and SVM. The LR case is especially informative because identical final held-out behavior occurred despite differences in repeated validation distributions. This means that equivalent final test behavior can arise from different training-stage search behavior.

Selected feature count distributions also require joint interpretation with held-out behavior. A smaller subset reduces dimensionality, but its predictive value depends on whether the retained variables preserve the information required by the classifier. Dimensionality reduction is relevant because irrelevant or redundant variables can slow learning, increase overfitting risk, and degrade model performance in medical data, as expressed in94. Subset composition is equally important because Noroozi et al. (2023) showed on the Cleveland heart disease dataset that different FS methods can produce different subset sizes and different retained-variable compositions even when they are applied to the same feature space95. A compact subset is useful only when dimensionality reduction preserves the predictive information required by the corresponding classifier.

Clinical relevance of recurrent selected features

Supplementary Table S1 and Table 9 show that the final selected subsets did not vary only at the method level, but also formed classifier-level recurrence patterns within the Cleveland variable space. The recurrent variables were concentrated around symptom presentation, exercise-induced ischemic response, angiographic vessel burden, thallium or perfusion-related diagnostic findings, and demographic risk. This pattern gives the optimized masks a clinically interpretable structure within the Cleveland benchmark.

These recurrent patterns are reported as final-subset consensus observations rather than formal FS stability estimates. Formal FS stability requires trial-wise selection-frequency or similarity-based analysis across repeated runs, whereas Table 9 identifies variables retained together in the final optimized configurations. FS stability is usually defined as the extent to which a selection algorithm preserves its feature preferences when the training data or stochastic search conditions change. Nogueira et al. (2017) describe this problem through repeated selected feature sets and frequency-based or similarity-based comparisons96, while Büyükkeçeci and Okur (2023) define stability as the variation in selection outputs caused by small changes in the training data82. More recently, Łukaszuk and Krawczuk (2024) showed why this distinction matters for classifier evaluation, because models can maintain similar predictive accuracy while relying on different selected-feature patterns97. These recurrent variables are then interpreted within the clinical-marker structure of the Cleveland benchmark.

The clinical interpretation of these retained variables is supported by both Cleveland-based FS studies and CAD prediction literature. Cleveland-based FS studies have reported that selected subsets of the Cleveland variable set can improve predictive performance under particular classifier and FS configurations73. Another Cleveland-based analysis identified cp., exang, oldpeak, ca., and thal among significant predictors, described ca. as the strongest feature, and reported chest pain repeatedly in positive heart-disease rules98. Age, sex, and chest pain or angina type are core variables in models estimating obstructive CAD probability and in stable chest pain assessment99–102. Exercise-induced angina and ST-segment depression, corresponding to exang and oldpeak in the Cleveland variable set, are clinically meaningful exercise-test markers103,104. The ca. variable reflects angiographic vessel burden105, while thal is linked to thallium or perfusion-related diagnostic assessment in CAD evaluation106,107.

The common subsets differed across classifier families, which supports the central finding that variable retention was shaped by the interaction between QI-guided wrapper search and classifier family. The recurrence of symptom, exercise-test, angiographic, thallium-related, and demographic variables supports clinical readability of the selected masks. This feature-level analysis should be read as final-subset consensus and clinical interpretability analysis, not as causal importance, model-specific feature importance, or formal FS stability.

Runtime, convergence, and computational trade-off

The runtime and convergence results show that computational efficiency and predictive behavior should be interpreted jointly. Wrapper-based FS is computationally costly because each candidate solution must be decoded, transformed into a feature subset and hyperparameter configuration, trained, and evaluated through internal CV. This cost structure follows the wrapper-based FS logic because candidate subsets are decoded as feature masks and then assessed through classifier performance7,11,14,16,17. Mandal and Chakraborty (2025) describe QI feature subset selection as an iterative process that combines candidate subset generation, QI updating, binary measurement, fitness evaluation, and stopping criteria62. Vivek et al. (2025) further frame QI evolutionary FS as a representation-driven search process in which subset encoding and update design affect how the feature space is explored63. In the present design, the computational burden was increased because FS and discrete hyperparameter tuning were optimized jointly for every decoded candidate.

The runtime results show a practical cost advantage for QI-BPSO, but the fastest method did not consistently produce the strongest validation-level or held-out behavior. This separation indicates a trade-off between computational cost and predictive behavior. A method can be attractive under a runtime constraint while another method can be stronger under a validation-level or held-out-performance criterion.

The convergence curves also show that early plateau formation and final validation performance are not the same quantity. A method that reaches a plateau earlier does not necessarily reach the best final validation level. The convergence curves should be interpreted together with final CV scores and held-out results, rather than as a standalone speed ranking.

These convergence patterns can be interpreted through the algorithmic components that control QI search behavior. Han and Kim (2004) showed that changes in termination criterion, gate design, and two-phase initialization can alter convergence speed, fitness, and robustness108. Tayarani-N and Akbarzadeh-T (2014) showed that population structure, diversity preservation, reinitialization, and operator design affect QI evolutionary algorithm performance because these mechanisms influence exploration capacity and local-optimum avoidance60. Gharehchopogh (2023) also frames QI metaheuristics as algorithms in which quantum-computing concepts are used to improve convergence, exploration, and exploitation across evolutionary, swarm-based, binary, hybrid, and multi-objective forms22. Figure 7 should not be read only as a speed comparison. It shows how different QI representations reached their final validation levels through different optimization trajectories.

The runtime and convergence findings show that computational cost, convergence trajectory, final best CV Accuracy, and held-out performance did not always favor the same QI variant. This pattern should be interpreted with attention to QI search mechanisms because probabilistic sampling, binary measurement, gate-based updating, population structure, and diversity-preserving mechanisms can influence both optimization trajectory and computational cost. QI representation and search behavior should be interpreted according to the practical priority of the modeling task, including held-out Accuracy, validation-level behavior, runtime, feature reduction, and threshold-dependent operation.

Cross-layer synthesis of QI representation behavior

The result layers show that QI variants should be interpreted across interacting methodological dimensions rather than through a single final metric. Held-out performance, no-FS comparison, threshold behavior, outer-trial distributions, runtime, convergence, and selected-subset recurrence each describe a different part of the same optimization problem. A single method-level ranking would omit relevant predictive, statistical, computational, and feature-subset information.

The preferred QI variant depended on classifier family, evaluation metric, decision-threshold requirement, feature-reduction objective, and computational budget. Faster convergence or lower runtime did not always coincide with stronger validation or held-out performance, and clinically interpretable recurrent variables still differed by classifier family. The study supports a conditional interpretation of QI representation and search behavior under the shared wrapper-based FS and discrete hyperparameter-tuning protocol.

Lımıtatıons and future work

This study was designed as a controlled within-family benchmark of QI-PSO, QI-EA, QI-BPSO, and QI-BEA for joint FS and discrete hyperparameter tuning on the Cleveland heart disease dataset. The shared split, preprocessing pipeline, classifier families, search space, fitness definition, and evaluation budget were used to examine QI representation and search behavior under comparable conditions. The analysis included held-out evaluation, no-FS baselines, threshold behavior, outer-trial distributions, runtime, convergence, and final selected feature subset consensus. The findings should be interpreted within this fixed-protocol QI comparison setting rather than as a broad ranking of FS metaheuristics.

The main limitations follow from this controlled scope. First, the analysis was performed on a single heart disease dataset and under one fixed train-test partition. Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted within the single-dataset and fixed-partition setting, rather than as dataset-independent, split-independent, or population-independent results. Second, the study did not include a heterogeneous cross-family benchmark against conventional PSO, genetic algorithms, or other non-QI FS metaheuristics. Such a comparison requires harmonized population sizes, iteration budgets, fitness definitions, classifier settings, hyperparameter tuning depth, stopping criteria, and runtime reporting for the compared algorithm families. Third, the feature-level analysis was limited to final-subset consensus and clinical interpretability. Formal model-specific feature-importance or contribution-level analyses were not computed in the present fixed-protocol benchmark.

The next study will be designed as an independent cross-family benchmarking study to address these limitations in a broader experimental design. In that study, the QI variants examined here will be compared with conventional and non-QI metaheuristics on multiple cardiovascular and non-cardiovascular disease datasets. The design will include repeated train-test partitions, nested validation schemes, and external validation cohorts to assess whether the observed performance patterns persist over independent data partitions and disease-specific prediction tasks. Complete trial-level feature mask histories will also be archived to enable formal FS stability analyses, including selection-frequency curves, Jaccard similarity, Kuncheva-type stability, and perturbation-based robustness estimates. Model-specific feature-importance analyses, such as SHAP and permutation importance, will also be included to complement final-subset consensus with post hoc contribution-level interpretation. In the same independent benchmark, threshold-dependent operating behavior will be evaluated using validation-derived thresholds and independent held-out or external test data.

Conclusıons

This study presented a controlled comparison within the QI metaheuristic family by evaluating QI-PSO, QI-EA, QI-BPSO, and QI-BEA for joint FS and discrete hyperparameter tuning on the Cleveland heart disease dataset. All methods were evaluated across the same classifier families under the same fixed train-test split, preprocessing pipeline, search space definitions, fitness definition, and evaluation budget. This design made it possible to examine QI representation and search behavior under a shared wrapper-based FS and hyperparameter-tuning protocol.

The results showed that QI behavior was classifier-dependent. The selected-feature results did not support a single best QI variant across all classifier families. QI-PSO was favored by held-out Accuracy for DT and K-NN, whereas QI-BPSO was favored by held-out Accuracy for SVM. LR provided a contrasting case because all four QI variants produced identical held-out test metrics despite different selected feature counts. These findings show that QI representation and search behavior should be interpreted through their interaction with the classifier family rather than as independent optimizer properties.

The no-FS and threshold-dependent analyses further showed that FS and model ranking depended on the evaluation context. Feature reduction was most consistently favorable for K-NN, DT showed metric-dependent behavior, and the full 13-variable representation produced stronger held-out results for LR and SVM. Accuracy, ROC-AUC, precision-recall behavior, and threshold sensitivity also did not always support the same method preference. Thus, the practical interpretation of each QI variant depends on the operating metric and decision-threshold requirement.

The repeated outer-trial, runtime, and convergence analyses showed that final held-out performance and optimization behavior should be interpreted as related but distinct result layers. QI-BPSO was the most runtime-efficient variant across classifier families, whereas QI-BEA showed favorable median CV Accuracy for DT, K-NN, and LR. Earlier convergence, lower runtime, higher validation-level performance, and stronger held-out performance did not always occur in the same method. This finding indicates that computational budget and predictive objective should be considered jointly when selecting a QI variant.

The final selected feature subsets showed clinically interpretable recurrence patterns within the Cleveland benchmark. Recurrent variables were concentrated around symptom presentation, exercise-related ischemic response, angiographic vessel burden, thallium or perfusion-related diagnostic information, and demographic risk. These patterns should be interpreted as final-subset consensus observations rather than as feature-importance scores or formal FS stability estimates.

The main conclusion is that QI representation and search behavior should not be reduced to a universal optimizer ranking. In this fixed-protocol benchmark, the preferred QI variant depends on classifier family, evaluation metric, decision-threshold requirement, feature-reduction objective, and computational budget. The study provides a controlled reference point for understanding how binary and non-binary QI variants behave in wrapper-based FS and discrete hyperparameter tuning for heart disease classification.
