Pyridoxal photoenzymes for asymmetric radical–radical cross-couplings
Authors: Cole C Sorensen, Suhao Wang, Yao Ouyang, Saim Waheed, Claire G Page, Greg Mann, Simon Allmendinger, Todd K Hyster
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNon-native photoenzymes have enabled myriad asymmetric bond-forming events that are otherwise challenging or at present impossible with small-molecule catalysis1,2. These reactions require enzymes with cofactors that are strong absorbers in the visible region with reasonably long-lived excited states, such as flavin and nicotinamide. However, there exists a substantial chromophoric cofactor ‘dark space’ in which no known photoenzymatic activity has been characterized1. Increased knowledge of the photophysics of the cofactors in the ‘dark space’ would increase the types of bonds that photoenzymes can form by accessing new excited-state intermediates in enzyme classes with divergent reactivities and selectivities. Here, we establish pyridoxal 5′-phosphate (PLP) as a photoenzymatic cofactor by leveraging the excited-state quinonoid intermediate as a potent single-electron reductant. We overcome the poor photophysical properties of the native quinonoid intermediate by using non-native benzylamine substrates and exploiting Förster resonance energy transfer mechanism from an exogenous photosensitizer to access the quinonoid excited state. This redox neutral approach enables an asymmetric radical–radical cross-coupling between benzylamines and reductive radical precursors through concomitant generation and localization of a radical pair in an enzyme active site—overcoming the typical challenges associated with this reaction by removing the necessity for radical sorting and the persistent radical effect3. The emergent photoexcited intermediates of PLP identified in this work greatly expand the potential avenues for valuable bond-forming events by PLP-dependent enzymes.Subject terms: Biocatalysis, Stereochemistry

Main

Nature uses a small but powerful suite of photoenzymes to carry out challenging transformations with remarkable selectivity4,5. These enzymes rely on flavin, nicotinamide and chlorophyll-like tetrapyrrole chromophores to capture upwards of 70 kcal mol−1 of energy within the protein active site and funnel it into challenging chemical transformations, such as fatty acid hydrodecarboxylation6, chlorophyll biosynthesis7 and repair of cyclobutene lesions in DNA8. Inspired by this reactivity, researchers have elicited emergent photoenzymatic activity in native ‘dark’ enzymes to accomplish highly selective radical transformations for addressing outstanding selectivity challenges in organic synthesis1,9. These enzymes have relied on the same cofactors as native photoenzymes because of their robust photophysical properties and established excited-state reactivity (Fig. 1a). However, there exists a substantial chromophoric cofactor ‘dark space’, where no known photoenzymatic activity has been characterized because of their poor molar absorptivity, short excited-state lifetime, dominating non-radiative decay pathways, or lack of a cofactor excited state in the catalytic cycle10, but which present an opportunity for photoenzymatic activity. As the field of photobiocatalysis matures, transitioning cofactors out of this dark space will serve to greatly expand the structural diversity of potential photoenzymes, excited state intermediates and emergent reactivity.

Pyridoxal 5′-phosphate (PLP, vitamin B6) is a mechanistically promiscuous cofactor that accounts for more than 140 distinct classified enzyme activities11,12. Essential to these mechanisms is the formation of the electron-rich, conjugated quinonoid intermediate (Fig. 1a, [Q]) by a precisely coordinated substrate deprotonation, decarboxylation or retro-aldol cleavage that subsequently reacts with electrophiles (for example, α-synthases, transferases, oxidases), protons (for example, transaminases, racemases, decarboxylases) or basic residues (for example, β- or γ-synthases, eliminases)13–17. The mechanisms accessible by the PLP cofactor were recently expanded by cooperative photobiocatalysis strategies that exogenously generate carbon-centred radicals in free solution, which engage with either the quinonoid18–20 or aminoacrylate enzyme-bound intermediates21–24 for the enantioselective construction of C(sp3)–C(sp3) bonds. Using an exogenous photocatalyst decouples radical generation from the native function of the cofactor but makes radical colocalization more challenging. However, PLP lies within this cofactor dark space, where little is known about the potential photochemistry of the enzyme-bound internal aldimine, external aldimine and quinonoid cofactor intermediates25,26 (Fig. 1a). The robust spectral properties of PLP intermediates (Fig. 1a) provide an avenue for photoenzymatic activity that serves to expand the mechanisms available to this rich protein family and for accessing synthetically useful transformations that complement existing nicotinamide- and flavin-dependent photoenzymes.

We became interested in using the excited-state quinonoid (1[Q]*) intermediate of PLP for photoenzymatic activity because the electron-rich nature of [Q] lends itself to being a strong reductant in its photoexcited state with desirable visible light absorption in the 450–600 nm range27,28 (Fig. 1a). An important feature of using 1[Q]* as a photoreductant is concomitant oxidation of [Q] to the semiquinone radical (SQ)18–20. We proposed that we could exploit this phenomenon for an asymmetric radical–radical cross-coupling (ARC) by using 1[Q]* to reduce a reductive radical precursor to co-generate both substrate radical and the SQ in the active site, thereby allowing the enzyme to control the selectivity of the radical–radical recombination. ARC is a highly desirable reaction class because of its efficient stereoselective construction of complex, chiral C–C bonds under mild conditions29–31. ARC reactions of free radicals are elusive32 because of the stringent requirements for controlling barrierless radical recombination between two distinct carbon-centred radicals33,34 with chemo- and stereoselectivity. Typical strategies to control these transformations rely on radical sorting and the persistent radical effect3. However, these selectivity challenges could be overcome by cogenerating and colocalizing the radical pair with a PLP-photoenzyme 1[Q]* intermediate to facilitate the ARC between benzylamines and reductive radical precursors while also removing the need for radical sorting and the persistent radical effect.

The low absorbance and poor photostability of [Q] have presented a long-standing challenge for direct excitation and access to 1[Q]* for productive chemistry35,36. We envisioned overcoming these limitations using two strategies. First, to increase the lifetime of 1[Q]*, which is reported to be (τ = 0.2 ns) for the glycine-derived quinonoid20, we proposed to develop an enzyme that accepts benzylic amines as quinonoid precursors with extended conjugation. Second, to address the low absorbance, we were inspired by how nature uses Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET) mechanisms to overcome the limitation of poorly absorbing cofactors37. For example, DNA photolyase is a flavoprotein that uses two light-harvesting cofactors to repair damaged DNA thymine dimers8 (Fig. 1b). Although anionic flavin adenine dinucleotide (FADH−) can be directly excited, the enzyme instead uses 8-hydroxydeazaflavin (8-HDF) cofactor to harvest light because of its favourable spectroscopic properties, then undergoes FRET to access the excited state 1FADH*− hydroquinone38. Realizing a similar mechanism for accessing 1[Q]* presents a desirable approach for the development of a PLP-dependent photoenzyme for ARC.

To achieve our desired reaction, we sought a photosensitizer with strong fluorescence overlap with the absorbance of quinonoid species derived from pyrimidine 2-methylamine (2a) but is unable to directly reduce pyridinium salt (1a). Rhodamine 6G (Rh6G) provides the proper spectral properties and is unable to reduce 1a (Ered = −0.8 V versus SCE) when photoexcited39,40 (Eox* = −0.5 V versus SCE). Testing pyrimidine 2-methylamine (2a) against our in-house library of l-threonine aldolases (Supplementary Fig. 1) with Rh6G as our photosensitizer and benzyl pyridinium salt 1a afforded C(sp3)–C(sp3)-coupled product 3a in high enantioselectivity (5% yield, 99:1 enantiomeric ratio (e.r.)) with wild-type TmLTA (Fig. 2a). In the absence of Rh6G, product is formed in only 1% yield because of its poor absorbance and photostability under reaction conditions. Although this is the same enzyme engineered in our previous study for binding benzylamines for native aldolase reactivity, the previously engineered variant TmLTA EAGD provides no product in this reaction27. Attempts to directly excite alanine-derived quinoids for photoenzymatic activity failed to yield product, highlighting the need for additional excited-state stabilization (Supplementary Fig. 11). Control experiments illustrated that light and TmLTA were required for promoting the reaction.

To improve the basal activity of TmLTA, we subjected the enzyme to directed evolution using site saturation mutagenesis following a reduced codon strategy (Fig. 2b). Targeted residues for mutation were selected based on known relevance to activity (for example, K199, H83, R316) and proximity to the bound cofactor using the wild-type and pyrimidine-bound variant TmLTA crystal structures as reference27,41. All existing radical PLP systems require greater than 5 equiv. of amine to increase the concentration of the reactive quinonoid intermediate, so we sought to engineer for 2 equiv. of 2a to further improve the synthetic value of our method. The tyrosine at position 30 was mutated to glutamate (Y30E), yielding an increase to 8% yield while maintaining selectivity. Mutation of the conserved carboxylate-binding arginine to glutamine (R316Q) further improved the activity42 (13% yield, 99:1 e.r.). Additional mutations at N308L and I124S led to our fourth-round variant in good yield and selectivity (30%, 99:1). A final mutation at valine 310 to leucine (V310L) afforded product in high yield and enantioselectivity (90%, 99:1 e.r.). All five mutations are located on three separate loops lining the substrate tunnel predicted by CAVER and KVFinder (Fig. 2c). Mutations generally reduced protein expression but maintained similar levels of soluble protein (Supplementary Fig. 15). Our final variant maintains synthetically useful yield and selectivity at lower enzyme (0.5 mol%, 79%, 99:1 e.r.) and amine loading (1 equiv. amine, 38%, 99:1 e.r.). Reactivity is maintained in cell-free lysate (93%, 99:1 e.r.) down to 0.01 mol% enzyme loading with up to 700 total turn-over number (TTN) (Fig. 2d).

Our engineered variant, abbreviated TmLTA EQLSL, was tested with a suite of pyrimidine and pyridinium precursors (Fig. 3). The model substrate 2a was amenable to preparatory-scale reactions (250 μmol, 86%, 99:1 e.r.). Pyrimidines were tolerant of substitutions at both the 4- and 5-positions (3b–g). For some lower-performing substrates, amine loading can be increased from 2–5 equiv. to improve yield (Fig. 3, yield in parenthesis). The halogen series of 3e, 3f and 3g showed decreasing yield and enantioselectivity. Sterically encumbered substitutions were well tolerated as well (3h,i) with the meridianin-like 4-indanol pyrimidine 3i providing 51% yield albeit in reduced enantioselectivity. The reaction tolerates a wide variety of radical precursors in good yield and enantioselectivity (3j–w). Meta-substituted benzyl radicals exhibited modest yield and high enantioselectivity (3r–t) and ortho-substituted radicals observed a slight erosion of selectivity (3q, 3t). Pyridyl and allyl radicals were well tolerated and provided an avenue for future engineering to access other challenging radical precursors. A sterically challenging addition with a methyl benzyl radical was observed in high enantioselectivity and diastereoselectivity (3w).

Phenyl, pyridyl and pyrimidyl represent the three most common six-membered aromatic moieties in the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved drugs43. We predicted a de-aza substrate walk to expand our method into these high-value functional groups. A substantial challenge is their decreasing withdrawing character, and thus increasing pKa (Supplemental Fig. 5; ref. 44). We found subjecting pyridine methylamine (4a) to our directed evolution series with TmLTA (Supplementary Fig. 21) showcased similarly increasing yield and near-perfect enantioselectivity (32% yield, 99:1 e.r.). Increasing amine loading for pyridines to 10 equiv. and subjecting the enzyme to another round of directed evolution (+S6G) led to a final yield of 78% and 99:1 e.r.

TmLTA EQLSLG was generally active for pyridines with similar reactivity trends to that of the pyrimidines (5a–k). Similar to the pyrimidines, 5-substitution led to reduced yield. Investigating our mapped quinonoid onto TmLTA EQLSLG suggests the 5-position points directly into loop D, causing reduced quinonoid formation and resulting in lower yield (Supplementary Figs. 18 and 19). 3-Substituted pyridines were especially well tolerated, with the exception of a fluorinated variant, which provides no yield. 6-Substituted pyridines afforded product in good yield, but with complete loss of enantioselectivity (5g and 5h), suggesting crucial interactions at the basic pyridine nitrogen. Brominated 5i and 5j similarly showed racemic products but with lowered yield due to product photoreduction to 5e (25% and 24% yield 5e, respectively), however, with a total yield of 41% and 42%, respectively, indicating good initial binding and product formation. A similar loss of enantioselectivity was observed with 5k. Benzyl-substituted 5l afforded an alpha-tertiary amine product with near-perfect enantioselectivity (8%, 99:1 e.r.). This class of product is generally exceedingly challenging to synthesize and cannot be accessed by classical biocatalysts responsible for functional group inversion, such as transaminases and imine reductases.

Next, we conducted mechanistic studies to determine how the enzyme was catalysing this reaction (Fig. 4a). Direct excitation of [Q] with 505 nm LEDs leads to product in modest yield (16%) due to its poor absorbance under relevant reaction conditions (Supplementary Fig. 32). However, exciting Rh6G with 470 nm LEDs leads to significantly more product formation (90%). Stern–Volmer quenching experiments confirm the enzyme-bound quinonoid is the only reaction component able to quench the Rh6G excited state (Fig. 4b). On the basis of this observation, we proposed that FRET between 1Rh6G+* and [Q] to produce 1[Q]* was responsible for excited-state quenching. We observed FRET from 1Rh6G+* to [Q] using fluorescence spectroscopy (Fig. 4c) and confirmed that its efficiency is due to the good spectral overlap between donor and acceptor (Fig. 4c, inset). The rate of 1Rh6G+* fluorescence quenching increased over the course of the engineering campaign by a dynamic quenching mechanism (Fig. 4b). We propose that the introduction of multiple flexible non-polar residues improves the FRET efficiency by expanding the active site volume (Supplementary Fig. 12) and increasing the conformational freedom of the quinonoid intermediate allowing for easier dipole alignment with 1Rh6G+*. Initially, we expected engineering directly for quinonoid formation would be key to improving yield. However, over the course of our evolution campaign, there was no relationship between equilibrium quinonoid formation and yield (Supplementary Fig. 42). Moreover, the anaerobic photostability of the quinonoid intermediate under 470 nm irradiation without 1a present decreased with each mutation and was exacerbated by the presence of Rh6G (Supplementary Figs. 35 and 44).

When examining the mechanism by which 1[Q]* is formed, we found that excitation of enzyme-bound external aldimine [A] (Fig. 4a) with 445 nm LEDs without a photosensitizer also leads to substantial product formation (62% yield) and improved concurrently during the engineering campaign (Supplementary Fig. 20). Previous work identified that the free aldimine in solution can act as a photoreductant, but not necessarily the enzyme-bound aldimine [A]20. Photoexcitation of [A] has been shown to access a photoacidic 3[A]* triplet state that accelerates catalysis through increased rates of [Q] formation or promotes substrate decarboxylation in free solution, which could explain the moderately high yield in our reaction, but not the mechanism in which bond formation occurs45–48. Fluorescence spectroscopy showed an intra-enzyme FRET mechanism in which 1[A]* transfers energy (Fig. 4d) within the TmLTA homotetramer to a neighbouring [Q]. 1[A]* presents a two-phase decay at 4.87 ns and 1.35 ns (Supplementary Fig. 34). This intramolecular mechanism is highly efficient as the three neighbouring active site cofactors are roughly 3 nm, 4 nm and 5 nm in distance and well aligned (Fig. 4e). This phenomenon demonstrates the ability to leverage protein quaternary structure for the development of photoenzymatic mechanisms, instead of relying on native dual cofactor systems (for example, DNA photolyase), and robust communication between distal active sites with FRET.

We then investigated the photophysical properties of the newly established class of photoenzyme based on 1[Q]* and its ability to act as a photoreductant. 1[Q]* fluorescence is effectively quenched by pyridinium salt 1a (Fig. 5a) in a non-linear downward trend by Stern–Volmer experimentation, potentially due to the poor solubility of 1a in a mixed buffer solution, with product formation indicative of 1a reduction. 1[Q]* derived from WT TmLTA and 2a undergoes a biexponential decay of 0.2 ns and 3.2 ns in roughly a 96:4 ratio (Fig. 5b, pink trace). The slow decay of 1[Q]* is sufficiently long-lived to carry out intermolecular reactivity and is an order of magnitude longer than the reported lifetimes of the glycine-derived quinonoid20 (τ = 0.2 ns). Engineered variant EQLSL with 2a yielded an excited state 1[Q]* with a higher population of the relevant slow decay (45%) and a lifetime of 3.6 ns (Fig. 5b, red trace). A similar biexponential decay was observed with the pyridine methylamine 4a-derived 1[Q]* from EQLSLG (Fig. 5b, blue trace) but with a reduced lifetime of 2.4 ns.

The strong reducing abilities of 1[Q]* in the excited state (Eox* = −1.8 V versus SCE) were calculated from the density functional theory predicted ground-state redox potentials and the measured excitation energy from the intersection of [Q] absorbance and emission spectra (Supplementary Fig. 46). A redox ladder was prepared to validate the reduction potential. Enzyme-mediated reactivity was observed with homobenzyl-derived thiathrenium salt (Ered = −1.3 V versus SCE) to afford C(sp3)–C(sp3)-coupled product with moderate selectivity, but in reduced yield because of N-alkylation (Fig. 5d). Photoenzymatic reduction by 1[Q]* is further supported by the direct reductive coupling of 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline N-oxide (Ered = −1.9 V versus SCE) (Supplementary Information section 3.13). Attempts to directly react benzyl radicals with the quinonoid of 2a and TmLTA failed to produce product and only led to the formation of the homocoupled bibenzyl product (Fig. 5e), further suggesting a photoenzymatic mechanism distinct from previously reported radical-PLP systems due to the use of non-native amine substrates and a FRET mechanism18–20 (Fig. 5f).

We sought to show the utility of the method by synthesizing the highly functionalized core of lenacapavir (5m) (Fig. 5g), an FDA-approved HIV prevention drug49. The reported three-step route requires a chiral auxiliary, organometallic reagents and deprotection for 40% isolated yield and an unreported enantiomeric ratio, which presents an opportunity for alternative synthetic disconnections and yield improvements49. A recently reported enzymatic strategy using an engineered transaminase was able to achieve an enantiopure 5m in greater than 90% isolated yield with 0.25 mol% enzyme loading but requires the synthesis of the ketone-containing carbon scaffold over three steps and 32% overall yield50. Our one-step photoenzymatic strategy yielded 5m in 82% analytical yield and 98:2 e.r. by combining C–C bond formation and C–N stereoinduction, presenting a concise synthesis towards 5m.

In conclusion, we have identified an example of a PLP-dependent photoenzyme and leveraged its reducing excited state to facilitate an ARC between benzylamines and pyridinium salts to forge C(sp3)–C(sp3) bonds. Owing to the unique mechanism of bond formation, this work enables the synthesis of chiral unprotected primary amines and represents a pioneering advancement in controlling free radical–radical coupling reactions within an enzyme (Fig. 5f). Overall, this work pulls PLP out of the chromophoric cofactor dark space and explains interesting reactivity by using non-native benzylamine substrates that uniquely stabilize the quinonoid excited-state intermediate. We expect a rich development of PLP-dependent photoenzymatic reactivity stemming from the excited-state quinonoid and further expansion of photobiocatalysis into new reaction frontiers with unusual FRET-driven mechanisms.
