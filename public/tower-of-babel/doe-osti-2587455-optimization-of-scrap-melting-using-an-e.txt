 ABSTRACT

Steel industry is crucial to the national economy and security. Around 67% of crude steel in the U.S is
produced in electric arc furnaces (EAF), which is energy intensive ([1] – [3]). Around 140 EAFs operate in
the U.S., consuming about 8.6x107 MMBtu/year of electricity. One of major challenges for EAFs includes
maximizing the efficiency of the electrical energy provided in the form of electric arcs to melt various scrap
mixes ([4] – [21]). To address this issue, a computational fluid dynamics (CFD) methodology is chosen to
analyze scrap melting using the electric arc. Due to complex furnace phenomena and the wide variety of
potential scenarios, high performance computing (HPC) is essential to yield comprehensive and detailed
CFD analyses and systematic parametric studies for optimized EAF operation. The objectives are to 1)
simulate scrap melting using electric arc, 2) evaluate electrode/arc position for optimum scrap melting and
3) establish reduced order model for CFD data-base for fast model calculation.

 1. INTRODUCTION

 The direct current electric arc furnace (DC EAF) is a critical technology in modern
steelmaking. It uses direct current to generate an electric arc for heating and melting scrap steel.
Although the DC EAF offers advantages such as improved production efficiency and reduced
energy consumption, accurately simulating the scrap melting process remains a challenge. Existing
models often oversimplify important features, including electrode motion, variation in arc delivery
with current and arc length, and the layered, non-uniform structure of scrap. These simplifications
limit the applicability of such models in real industrial settings.
 There is a need for a comprehensive and realistic modeling approach that can capture complex
thermal and physical interactions in a DC EAF. Most previous studies have assumed stationary
electrodes, uniform scrap layers, and constant arc energy input, which do not reflect actual furnace
behavior. Developing a high-fidelity and validated simulation model would bridge this gap,
offering steelmakers a valuable predictive tool for optimizing operational parameters, improving
energy efficiency, and supporting informed decision-making in furnace operations.
 This project developed a detailed three-dimensional computational fluid dynamics (CFD)
model that integrates multiple physical sub-models to simulate the scrap melting process in an
industrial-scale DC EAF. The electric arc model calculates dynamic energy distributions as a
function of current and arc length. The coherent jet model simulates heat transfer from oxy-fuel
burners to the scrap. The scrap melting model accounts for realistic scrap behavior including
collapse, melting, and the effects of non-uniform layering. The fully integrated model was applied
to a real tap-to-tap operation conducted by SSAB. Validation against operational data showed only
an 11 percent discrepancy in melting rate and less than 5 percent difference in electrode position
after bore-in. The model captured key phenomena such as increased scrap temperature during
hold-fire burner operation, reduced melting under the electrode during bore-in due to re-
solidification, and a bottom-up, inside-out melting pattern. These results demonstrate the model’s
potential to provide detailed insight into scrap melting dynamics and to support optimization of
DC EAF processes.
 After validating CFD predictions against actual data, simulations of scenarios with modified
arc power (10% and 20% reduction from baseline) are performed. All other conditions such as
burner power and charge recipe remain the same. Results indicate that each 10% power reduction
extends the melting time for 60 tonnes of scrap by two minutes. Also, a boost in melting

performance is observed when the average molten bath temperature rises above the liquidus
temperature. Non-uniformity of arc heating is also described and potential optimization of EAF
melting is discussed.
 Using CFD data, a reduced order model has been developed that represents the relationship
between the inputs and outputs of CFD data-base. The idea is to use the reduced order model to
realize a fast calculation effect given the inputs to the model. Once the reduced order model is
obtained it will be used as the starting point of an inverse modeling based closed loop control to
realize an optimal operation for electric arc furnaces. In this context, the reduced order model will
provide an effective bridge for CFD models to be used in a real-time control mode.

2.3 DC Electric Arc Model
 A constant impedance control strategy is used to maintain zero difference between measured
arc voltage and current. The electrode adjusts to maintain the arc length between the scrap surface
and electrode tip during melting stage. The arc impinges on the liquid bath is modeled as additional
momentum pl applied to the liquid bath, incorporated into the momentum conservation equation.
The expression of this momentum is:

 Total arc power dissipates through convection, radiation, and electron flow. Convection and
electron flow affect the liquid steel and scrap below the electrode tip, while radiation impacts all
scrap and liquid steel around the electrode tip. Determining the DC arc heating distribution is
crucial for ascertaining heat loss and propagation direction. Directly simulating this in a 3D EAF
model is time-consuming and complex, so a step-by-step approach was adopted. Namely, the
approach involved creating a database of DC parameters at EAF industry scale, which was
integrated into the existing scrap melting model. The DC arc heating was first modeled in a lab-
scale 2D DC electric arc model, where the channel arc model determined the heat transfer
distribution, producing a database for industrial power conditions. The dataset was then used to
determine the energy contributions of each heat transfer mechanism for a given DC arc length and
current during the industry-scale EAF simulation.

2.4 Parametric Study Methodology

 Fig 2. Flowchart for the Effect of Electrode Power on Melting Performance
 Figure 2 demonstrates the flowchart specific to this study for investigating the effect of electric
arc power on melting performance. After preprocessing the operational data provided by SSAB,
the arc profile, scrap layering condition, and burner profile needed for the simulation are provided
as inputs to the electric arc model, scrap melting model, and coherent jet model, respectively. The
outputs of the simulation model, such as melting rate, temperature, and electrode motion, can be
used for validation by comparing results with real-world data. Once the model is validated,
scenario cases with different electrode power profiles are established to study the impact of
electrode power.

2.5 Tap-to-tap Simulation

 Fig 3. Timeline of a tap-to-tap EAF operation performed by SSAB

 The DC tap-to-tap process performed by SSAB starts with the charging of the scrap. After this,
the charge is exposed to burners acting in hold-fire mode for approximately 40 mins. At 40 mins,
the DC electrode is activated initiating the melting stage for around 37 mins. The last 27 mins of
this stage include oxygen injection and refining reactions. Figure 3 illustrates the timeline of the
tap-to-tap process. The tapping occurs 87 mins after the charge of the 1st bucket. The simulation
focused on the first 77 mins of operation, dismissing the 10 mins short break seen before the
tapping.

2.6. Data-preprocessing

different functions. Arc current and arc length are essential for calculating the arc heating
distributions. Additionally, arc length is used to update the electrode position. The arc power

 3. CFD RESULTS

3.1 Validation

3.1.1 Melting Rate and Liquid Temperature

 Results shown in this section are up to 4500 seconds, representing approximately 96% of the
actual SSAB operation. The method for validation is to compare the melting rate predicted by the
CFD simulation with the one expected in actual SSAB operation. Also, the average melting
temperature is compared with data collected near tapping.

 Fig 7. Simulation Results: Liquid Mass, Solid Mass & Average Liquid Temperature

 Figure 7 shows the solid mass and liquid mass over time from the CFD results, while Table 3
presents the differences between the SSAB data and simulation results. The average melting rate
for the first 2170 seconds of melting in this case is 59 kg/s. For SSAB’s industrial operation,
assuming all scrap is melted at the end of the heat, the average melting rate is 68 kg/s. Therefore,

the difference at this stage is approximately 13.2%. It should be noted that the current case does
not include the effect of arc shrouding by foamy slag, which is to be considered in future
simulations. This effect is anticipated to reduce energy losses and increase the simulated average
melting rate. Figure 7 also shows the average liquid temperature over time. The temperature
increases rapidly during the first 400 seconds, then stabilizes. In SSAB heat the liquid temperature
reached 1902 K near the end of the operation. Therefore, the current difference between average
liquid temperature in CFD simulation and the one provided by SSAB probe is 4.5%.

3.1.2 Electrode Position

 A second part of the validation involves comparing the electrode position during the scrap
melting stage. The electrode activates at 2350 seconds; therefore, validation focus on the 2350 to
4500 seconds stage. Figure 7 shows the comparison of electrode position throughout the scrap
melting stage.

 During the bore-in phase (2350 – 2600 sec range), results indicate that the bore-in rate from
the CFD model is faster than SSAB rate. This discrepancy arises because the initial volume
fractions of the scrap are lower than expected, suggesting that the actual scrap density is higher.
Additionally, our model does not account for side collapse. Figure 8 illustrates the solid scrap at
2500 seconds, where a pit has formed and surrounding scrap would typically cave in at this stage,
slowing the actual bore-in rate compared to the simulation. In the main melting stage (after 3000
sec), the difference between model and actual data is less than 5%, indicating that the CFD results
align closely with real data once the electrode stabilizes.

 Figure 13 shows the simulation results for the three cases with different electrode power inputs.
The bore-in rate for the baseline case (100% power) is the fastest (see left figure), as expected.
Regarding energy transfer, the total energy transferred from the electrode to the solid scrap and

 7. DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSIONS

A comprehensive DC EAF model is used to simulate the tap-to-tap industrial EAF operation
performed by SSAB. The validation of the CFD model is achieved by comparing the CFD results
with actual data. The power profiles, charge recipe and burners profile provided by SSAB were
used as inputs in the EAF model. Results of the validations show the following:
 ‒ The average melting rate differs ~13% , liquid temperature differs ~4.5% between the CFD
 and actual data
 ‒ The electrode lowest position differs by less than 5% throughout the main melting stage.
 ‒ Large differences in electrode position are seen between the two approaches during the
 electrodes bore-in. One reason for these differences are scrap cave-ins that are not captured
 by the CFD model.
The validated model was then used to study the melting performance of the DC tap-to-tap process.
Results of this simulation show:
 ‒ Before the activation of the electrode, the burners acting on hold-fire mode increase the
 average scrap temperature from 300 K to 466 K and the average scrap volume fraction
     from 0.15 to 0.17.
 ‒ During the bore-in stage, less scrap melted due to re-solidification beneath the electrode
 tip. In the main melting stage, the melting pattern was bottom-up and inside-out.
 ‒ The liquid steel beneath the electrode tip was at least 200 K higher than the average liquid
 steel temperature and 250 K higher than the regions near the furnace wall. Large
 temperature increasing of molten steel near the electrode resulted of the large heating
 concentration provide by the 1-electrode operation.
 ‒ Electrode power input was identified as a major determinant of melting performance.
 Variations in DC power input result in different arc current and arc length values, directly
 influencing the melting rate. It was found that each 10% reduction in arc energy increased
 the melting time by approximately two minutes. Additionally, a peak in the melting rate
 was observed during the bore-in stage, and a significant boost in the melting rate was
 triggered once the average molten bath temperature exceeds the liquidus point.
Overall, the CFD results show a highly non-uniform heating and melting process, which is
constantly changing as solid and liquid mass evolve during the operation. The DC-EAF model can
capture main features of the melting process and DC arc heating, and results can be applied to
determine operation parameters for process optimization.
In terms of reduced order modeling, a set of promising results have been obtained showing their
potential to be used in a closed loop control way to help optimize the process operation. Indeed,
the results can be further enhanced by using the probability density function control idea to train
the reduced order model ([22] – 24]).
