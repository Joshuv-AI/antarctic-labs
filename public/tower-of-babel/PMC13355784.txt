The extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation outcomes database
Authors: Chloe R Skidmore, Christopher M. Evola, Anna L. Ciullo, Heidi Alvey, Brian Grunau, Kyle Gunnerson, Peter England, Nicholas J. Johnson, Joseph E. Tonna
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCardiac arrest impacts more than 500,000 people in the United States annually. Overall, cardiac arrest, particularly when it occurs outside of a hospital, is associated with low rates of survival and poor neurologic recovery. Extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation (ECPR), or the use of extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) during the cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) process, has increased in recent years. The Extracorporeal Resuscitation Outcomes Database (EROD) aims to serve as a source of highly detailed, granular data for research on ECPR. This paper represents the inaugural report from EROD.MethodsWe created EROD, which collects prospective data on adult cardiac arrest cases treated with ECPR, including demographics, medical history, cardiac arrest characteristics, the ECMO cannulation process, post-cannulation care, hospital treatments and diagnostics, neuroprognostication, complications, and outcomes.ResultsFive centers in North America contributed data (n = 113) to EROD between March 2017 and March 2022. The majority White (64%) and male (78%) sample had a significant medical history prior to their cardiac arrest (73%). Most patients arrested pre-hospital (65%) and received bystander CPR (81%). ECMO cannulation most often took place in an emergency department (67%) and was done by a Cardiothoracic Surgery provider (76%). Common complications included acute kidney injury (49%), major bleeding (29%), vascular injury (19%), and limb ischemia (14%), among others. One quarter of patients survived to hospital discharge (25%) and had an average hospital length of stay of 22 days (SD 15.5). Of the whole sample, there was an average time of 84 min from time of cardiac arrest to ECMO cannulation (SD 54) and patients spent an average of 107 h and 56 min on ECMO (SD 73:53). Of the 25% who survived (n = 28), the majority had positive neurologic outcomes with a Cerebral Performance Category (CPC) score of 1 (n = 15), indicating no or mild cerebral disability, or a CPC of 2 (n = 5), indicating moderate cerebral disability.ConclusionThis paper introduces EROD and presents data from the initial 113 patients entered into the registry from 2017 to 2022. Initial analysis demonstrated survival rates that align with national averages, even with longer durations of CPR prior to ECMO cannulation. Utilization of this registry, as well as others, is crucial in identifying and establishing standardized ECPR practices that deliver the best possible outcomes for patients.

Introduction

The American Heart Association estimates that annually more than 360,000 people in the United States experience an out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA),1 while another 200,000 have an in-hospital cardiac arrest (IHCA).2 Only 10.8% of individuals who have had an OHCA will survive to hospital discharge, and the majority report functional impairments and/or a decrease in quality of life.2 Notably, these numbers vary significantly by region, with ranges in survival-to-discharge from 3.4% to 22% and functional recovery from 0.8% to 20.1%.3 Increasingly, extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation (ECPR), which describes the application of extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) during cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR), has been used to treat patients with refractory cardiac arrest. ECPR is associated with higher survival than historically reported conventional CPR in observational studies,4, 5 as well as in a single randomized control trial by Yannopoulos et al.: advanced reperfusion strategies for patients with out-of-hospital cardiac arrest and refractory ventricular fibrillation (ARREST).6, 7

ECPR is inherently complex; patients who undergo ECPR can have disparate arrest etiologies, approaches to cannulation, cannulator skill level, locations for cannulation, and subsequent post-arrest therapies. Some of these characteristics are independently associated with patient survival.8, 9, 10 For ECPR candidates who experience a cardiac arrest in the out-of-hospital setting or soon after emergency department (ED) arrival, there are added challenges with incomplete information regarding past medical history and cardiac arrest characteristics. Further, ECPR survival varies as much as 44% among hospitals within the international Extracorporeal Life Support Organization (ELSO) Registry, even when adjusting for patient characteristics.8 This variation highlights the opportunity to further optimize care surrounding ECPR to achieve sustainable practices that improve outcomes. To address these needs, the Extracorporeal Resuscitation Consortium was established in 2017 to collect prospective data on patients undergoing ECPR.11 The Consortium comprises ten centers across the United States, as well as additional centers in Canada and Europe (https://www.edecmocollaborative.org). Uniquely, the Extracorporeal Resuscitation Consortium obtains data at a granularity beyond what is available in existing registries. Our goal in establishing the Extracorporeal Resuscitation Outcomes Database (EROD) is to make highly detailed data available for ECPR research. This manuscript introduces EROD and presents the registry report.

Methods

Study design and setting

Extracorporeal Resuscitation Outcomes Database (EROD) collects research-grade prospective data on adult patients treated with ECPR. Cases are eligible for registry inclusion if they satisfy all of the following: (1) age ≥ 18; (2) experienced cardiac arrest; (3) cannulated for ECMO in the context of ongoing cardiac arrest. Patients are eligible for inclusion if cannulated immediately following cardiac arrest if, in the opinion of the clinician, the patient has not yet achieved sustained ROSC (≥20 min) and is hemodynamically unstable. This definition was determined by the leadership at the creation of the Registry prior to subsequent clarifying definitions.

Data collection

Patients were identified at each site under IRB approval for observational data collection. Identified patients were recorded using a numerical identifier for each site, with subsequent limited identification upon data submission, using only dates and times. Data were entered into the REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture) tool across the various institutions. Standardized definitions have been established amongst the Consortium to ensure uniform data collection and entry across sites. Data quality is ensured through numerous checkpoints throughout data entry and analysis, which review the completeness of the data and evaluate its integrity. EROD data also integrates with the existing ELSO (Extracorporeal Life Support Organization) Registry main data collection form. Variables are defined in the same way and names are mapped, such that data entered into EROD that overlaps with ELSO can be exported (utilized) from EROD to an ELSO upload. At the time of creation, EROD contained significantly more variables and detail than the main ELSO case report from (CRF). In 2020, ELSO updated their ECPR data collection through an ECPR Addendum Version 2.0, which, co-created by Dr. Tonna, contains many similar elements to EROD. EROD contains data fields not collected by ELSO though.12 The value of the uniqueness of the EROD dataset, in comparison to ELSO, was highlighted at the NIH NHLBI ECPR Workgroup.13

Collected data elements included demographic information, past medical history, details of the cardiac arrest, details of ECMO cannulation, utilization of cardiac catheterization and interventions, initial hospital course, significant hospital events, complications, neuroprognostication, and outcomes. Demographic data included age, sex, height, weight, and race/ethnicity. Relevant past medical history was obtained from the patient, the patient’s family/friends, or through electronic medical records review.

Data surrounding cardiac arrest were collected to evaluate and identify trends to improve risk stratification strategies for ECPR utilization. These include location of arrest, whether the arrest was witness or unwitnessed, if bystander CPR was utilized, mechanism of cardiac arrest, if return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) of any duration was achieved, and also if sustained ROSC (at least 20 min) was achieved prior to ECMO initiation, initial rhythm, medications and doses administered, whether compressions were manual or mechanical, time of arrest and time of patient arrival to the hospital, intubation status, hemodynamics, and initial laboratory values primarily through arterial or venous blood gas testing. Data elements in relation to ECMO cannulation were collected to evaluate which cannulation strategies and components may improve outcomes. These elements were the location of cannulation, vascular access method, cannula sizes, presence of echocardiogram, administration and dose of heparin, time of ECMO flow, specialty of the cannulator, utilization of distal perfusion catheter or left ventricular decompression device, hemodynamics, and laboratory values through arterial or venous blood gas testing after ECMO flow initiation. Data were collected regarding the evaluation of reversible myocardial ischemia via cardiac catheterization, timing of catheterization, whether a culprit lesion was identified, and whether percutaneous intervention or coronary artery bypass were performed.

During the first three days of hospitalization, extensive data were recorded to evaluate early interventions that may improve outcomes, as well as evaluate early indicators for improved or worsened clinical course. These included the patient’s hospital unit location, laboratory evaluation of blood counts, renal function, liver function, and blood gas analysis, Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score, delirium and sedation scores, daily intake and output, ECMO circuit flows and sweep gas, vasopressor and inotrope utilization including maximum dose, echocardiogram results, hemodynamics, respiratory support devices and settings, and mobility scoring. Additionally, significant hospital events and when they took place were recorded. Events such as ECMO decannulation, discontinuation of vasopressors or inotropes, extubation, ICU discharge, total amounts of each blood product administered, and signs of improved mental status or mobility were characterized as significant hospital events. Complications were tracked to provide additional information about the rate of various complications and improve risk stratification strategies. These complications include transient ischemic attack (TIA) or cerebrovascular attack (CVA), brain death, pneumothorax, pulmonary hemorrhage, acute renal injury and whether renal replacement therapy was utilized, major bleeding, limb ischemia and the need for fasciotomy and/or amputation, thoracotomy, cardiac tamponade, aortic dissection, vascular injury, thrombosis, and intracardiac clot. Testing results for neuroprognostication were collected and included computed tomography (CT) scans, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), electroencephalograms (EEG), neuron-specific enolase levels, somatosensory evoked potentials, and formal neurologic examination findings. Lastly, data related to outcomes were recorded. This included date and time of hospital discharge or date and time of death, the type of facility the patient was discharged to, if the patient was an organ donor, and cerebral performance category at the time of discharge. Statistical analyses

Analyses were performed with Microsoft Excel 2024 software. The averages were calculated using comprehensive data from all sites. Continuous data are reported as mean (SD, standard deviation) or median (IQR, interquartile range), with categorical data as counts (%, percentages). Comparisons were made using standard descriptive statistics, and all tests were two-tailed. P values of <0.05 were considered significant. Chi-squared tests of independence were performed using an alpha value of 0.05 to establish critical values, and chi-squared values were calculated to accept or reject the null hypothesis.

Results

Complete data is currently available for the period between March 2017 and March 2022. Data collection was de-prioritized in 2023 with the addition of the ELSO ECPR Version 2 Addendum, which provided considerable data that while, not identical or as complete, provided more center breath and substantial ability to perform analyses. As such, data collection to EROD is still possible, but was de-prioritized among the Extracorporeal Resuscitation Consortium in favor of ELSO. During this time, among all centers in the US, Canada and Europe in the Extracorporeal Resuscitation Consortium, five centers in the US and Canada entered data into the registry, including 113 adults treated with ECPR. Site 1 contributed 17 patients, Site 2 contributed 35 patients, Site 3 contributed 8 patients, Site 4 contributed 33 patients, and Site 5 contributed 20 patients. Over the years 2017–2022, sites contributed between 2.6 and 7.2 patients per year.

As illustrated in Table 2, the predominant demographic was composed of white males with an average age of 49 years. Nearly three-quarters of this patient population had a significant past medical history, including hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and diabetes as the three most common. Of the 113 patients enrolled in this registry, the majority (75%, n = 85) had out of hospital cardiac arrests, with 97 (86%) had a witnessed arrest and 81% of the patients receiving bystander CPR (Table 2). More than half had a presenting rhythm of either ventricular fibrillation or ventricular tachycardia, with the most common etiology being myocardial infarction (38%). Nearly all patients received epinephrine during cardiac arrest with an average dose of 5.9 mg (mg). The average ICU length of stay in surviving patients was 17 days, with a duration of ECMO support being 4.5 days (Table 5). Overall survival in this registry was 25% (n = 28), with a majority of these patients being discharged to home (n = 17, 61%). Of the 28 surviving patients, 12 were discharged from the ICU with a Cerebral Performance Category (CPC) score of 1 (good cerebral performance with no or slight disability), 5 with a CPC score of 2 (moderate cerebral disability), 6 with a CPC score of 5 (severe cerebral disability), and 5 had unknown CPC scores. At hospital discharge, the majority of patients had a CPC score of 1 (n = 15), 5 had a CPC score of 2, 6 had a CPC score of 3, and 2 had unknown CPC scores. Six patients had CPC scores that improved from ICU to hospital discharge.

Discussion

The Extracorporeal Resuscitation Outcomes Database (EROD) provides unique insight into patients with refractory cardiac arrest treated with ECPR. By systematically collecting and analyzing data on patient outcomes, procedural techniques, and complications, this registry aims to provide a comprehensive understanding of the effectiveness and safety of ECPR. This data-driven approach intends to enable healthcare providers to identify best practices, assess and minimize variations in care delivery, and refine treatment protocols.

Interestingly, the survival rate within this registry stands at approximately 25%, aligning closely with national averages reported in other registries. This is despite a wide range of practice variations as outlined above, including compression techniques, post-arrest PCI, left ventricular decompression, as well as cannulator specialty and location. A notable contrast emerges when comparing outcomes to those observed in the ARREST Trial, wherein this single-center randomized controlled trial had survival rates exceeding 40%, with minimal practice variation evident.6 Such findings lend credence to the proposition that minimizing practice variation, as identified in data registries, may yield optimal outcomes.

We note that in the EROD data, the prevalence of non-shockable rhythms, including PEA and asystole are present, despite not being part of recent ECPR guidelines14 and consensus statements,15 and being de-prioritized in recent RCTs,6, 16 though not all.7 At the time of creation of the EROD registry, the data suggesting that shockable rhythms had more favorable outcomes were not as clearly established, and the goal of EROD was to capture real world use of ECPR at the time.

Another noteworthy observation from this registry is the time elapsed from cardiac arrest to ECMO initiation, which averaged approximately 90 min among surviving patients. This duration drastically exceeds data reported in the three other ECPR randomized control trials, where the timeframe ranged from 59 to 74 min.6, 7, 16 We suggest that this longer duration reflects both that the EROD centers were early ECPR adopters performing ECPR regularly by 2016. As has been previously shown, in 2016, the use of ECPR across the US was limited at best.11 Additionally, we note that as a Registry report, the EROD outcomes are collected and published independent of any measure of success. As is known, many publications are subject to publication bias favoring positive outcomes. Certainly the cannulation times in EROD will be longer than are see in either the ARREST or Prague trials, which were highly practiced closed systems, but even longer than in INCEPTION (non-closed system, though still a controlled RCT) and other observational studies also.

Despite this prolonged low-flow cardiac state, 61% (n = 17) of survivors in this registry were discharged to home, suggesting favorable neurological outcomes. Additionally, these patients had slightly reduced intensive care unit (ICU) and hospital length of stay at 17 days and 22 days, respectively, compared to other studies. Data within this registry could be utilized not only to help minimize variation and optimize outcomes, but could also help guide the decision to initiate ECPR and prognostication once ECPR has been initiated.

EROD, however, is not the sole registry delving into cardiac arrest and ECPR outcomes. Other extensive databases in this domain encompass the international Extracorporeal Life Support Organization (ELSO) and the Cardiac Arrest Registry to Enhance Survival (CARES). While similarities exist amongst these databases, EROD is unique in its scope and enhanced data collection over the first three days of treatment following cardiac arrest. This detailed data, including laboratory and imaging studies, bedside assessments, procedures, treatments, medications, and others, provides insight into the most meaningful early interventions to improve patient outcomes. Additionally, EROD has a higher granularity of data preceding and surrounding the initiation of ECPR, including vascular access method, types of chest compressions, specific PCI treatments, and others. EROD was created at a time when ECPR was relatively infrequent among US hospitals, and all participating centers in EROD had only recently started performing ECPR.11

EROD, in combination with these other registries, provides a robust platform for prospective research aimed at examining various iterations of care, thereby facilitating the identification of optimal treatment strategies. This capability enables clinicians and researchers to systematically evaluate different approaches to care delivery, considering factors such as timing, protocols, and adjunct therapies. By conducting prospective studies within the EROD framework, healthcare professionals can gather valuable insights into the comparative effectiveness of different care modalities, ultimately enhancing patient outcomes and refining standards of care for ECPR.

Limitations

This report should be interpreted in the context of several limitations. First, EROD currently reflects a relatively small sample of 113 patients from five North American centers, which may limit generalizability to other programs, those with higher ECPR volumes, centers with different ECPR selection criteria, or health systems with different EMS and post-arrest care structures. Second, although data were prospectively collected using standardized definitions, registry-based data remain subject to missingness, variation in local documentation practices, and residual differences in interpretation across sites. Third, the cohort spans 2017 through 2022, a period during which ECPR programs, cannulation workflows, patient selection strategies, and post-arrest care practices evolved substantially; therefore, outcomes may not fully reflect contemporary practice. Fourth, this registry report is descriptive and was not designed to establish causal relationships between specific interventions, timing metrics, complications, or outcomes. Important unmeasured confounders, including institutional experience, prehospital systems of care, clinician decision-making, and reasons for ECPR candidacy or non-candidacy, may influence the observed associations. Fifth, EROD includes only patients who were cannulated for ECMO in the setting of refractory cardiac arrest and does not capture patients considered for but not treated with ECPR, limiting assessment of selection bias and preventing comparison with conventionally treated cardiac arrest patients. Finally, neurologic outcomes were assessed at ICU and hospital discharge using CPC, which provides a useful but limited measure of recovery and does not capture longer-term survival, functional status, neurocognitive outcomes, quality of life, or caregiver burden. Despite these limitations, EROD provides highly granular data on ECPR processes, early post-cannulation care, complications, and hospital outcomes that can complement larger registries and support future hypothesis-generating analyses.

Conclusions

ECPR is a promising therapy for patients experiencing refractory cardiac arrest; nevertheless, ensuring consistent and reproducible outcomes remains a considerable challenge. As such, the utilization of a data registry, such as EROD, is instrumental. Collecting granular-level data pertaining to the treatment and outcomes of patients undergoing ECPR serves as a crucial asset in the establishment of standardized care protocols for this complex intervention.
