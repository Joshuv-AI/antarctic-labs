Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

Poly(vinylidene chloride) (PVDC) is a major polymer product produced by Syensqo. PVDC-
containing plastics are not commonly recycled. To overcome the challenges associated with
recycling PVDC and multi-layer materials, we propose a tandem catalytic-biological process to
convert PVDC-containing waste to upcycled, tunable products such as polyhydroxyalkanoates
(PHAs). PHAs are commercially relevant, biodegradable polymers that are useful in packaging,
biomedical, and personal care applications. This technology, which we have named oxidative
funneling (OxFun), uses a metal-promoted autoxidation step to depolymerize co-mingled
polymers to a mixture of oxygenated compounds, which an engineered bacterium can funnel to a
single bioproduct: here, medium chain length polyhydroxyalkanoates (mcl-PHAs). The proposed
oxidation chemistry is amenable to the inclusion of additional polymers, and the biocatalyst can
be engineered to convert the deconstruction products to a variety of valuable chemicals, thus
presenting a tunable system for both feedstock variability and target product. Initial proof of
concept work was published by our team in Science in 2022, demonstrating the successful
conversion of a mixture of polystyrene (PS), high-density polyethylene (HDPE), and
polyethylene terephthalate (PET) to PHAs (Figure 1).

Pseudomonas putida KT2440 – a metabolically robust microbe that has been engineered and
proven viable for upcycling deconstructed PS, HDPE, and PET– can be engineered with
enzymes capable of de-chlorinating PVDC deconstruction products (e.g., 2,2-dichloroacetate) in
addition to PE-derived substrates into mcl-PHAs. Thus, we proposed a collaboration with Syesqo
(Figure 2) to explore the oxidative deconstruction of PVDC-based multi-layer films (Phase 1)
and microbial engineering of Pseudomonas putida KT2440 to convert these products into high-
value bioproducts (Phase 2).

Summary of Research Results:

Phase 1, Task 1: Oxidation of PVDC and PVDC/PE multi-layer films

Summary:

In Phase 1, we employed a Go/No-Go approach for 6 months to first ascertain if autoxidation is
viable for PVDC. This approach utilizes O2 (in air) as the oxidant, a radical initiator (such as N-
hydroxyphthalimide or sodium bromide), and transition metal catalysts (such as Co(II) and
Mn(II)) to guide reaction selectivity towards C–C cleavage in a homogeneous system at
moderate temperatures and pressures (T<200°C, P<102 bar).

Results & Discussion:

Oxidative deconstruction experiments were conducted using IXAN PV910, IXAN multi-layer
film grade (IXAN MLF), and PVDC/PVC blister films. Oxidation conditions were optimized to
achieve the highest possible conversion of solid polymer products to liquid, oxygenated
intermediates (determined by mass).

IXAN PV910 (PVDC only)

Based on known oxidation mechanisms, we hypothesized that oxidation of PVDC would result
primarily in dichloroacetic acid. IXAN PV910 (i.e., PVDC only) oxidation experiments resulted
in a mixture of diacids, chlorinated small molecules, oligomers, and crosslinked materials as
detected by gas chromatography mass spectroscopy (GC-MS). Unexpectedly, chloroacetone and
several chlorinated aromatic species were detected from PVDC oxidation experiments (Scheme
1). This was concerning as these materials are highly toxic and may not be digestible by
microbes. We suspected that the aromatic species were forming in the column of the GC-MS
which we explored in later experiments with IXAN MLF substrates.

The screening conditions and resulting weight percent conversion to liquid products are
presented in Table 1 for IXAN PV910. For all conditions, at least 20% solids remained, even
with metal catalysts and temperatures as high as 180C. This is a strong indicator that in the
PVDC-only oxidation experiments, crosslinking of the material is occurring and resulting in an
insoluble solid material. The mechanism for PVDC crosslinking under oxidation is well known
and previously published.2 We hypothesized that in the IXAN multi-layer films (IXAN MLF),
there would be less crosslinking and more controlled product distribution due to the lower
loadings of PVDC and the presence of other polymer substrates and radicals. Thus, we moved on
from the IXAN PV910 materials and focused on the IXAN MLF for further oxidation screenings
and optimization.

IXAN MLF

IXAN MLF films are multi-layer films containing ~84% HDPE, ~15% PVDC, and ~1%
ethylene vinyl acetate (EVA). IXAN MLF oxidation experiments resulted in much higher
conversions to liquid products, achieving nearly 100% conversion under conditions with NaBr
initiator, Co and Mn catalysts, 160C and 4 hours (Table 2). This is very promising for
downstream microbial digestion/conversion processes to be explored in Phase 2. Under these
optimum conditions, we detected the following compounds: From HDPE and EVA, we observed
mixtures of dicarboxylic acids, lactone acids, and hydroxy acids, all of which have microbial
pathways to create bioproducts, as already demonstrated by our team’s previous work.1 From the
PVDC component, we observed similar compounds as IXAN PV910 with much higher
concentrations of chloroacetic acid and parts per billion (ppb) levels of chloroacetone. In the GC-
MS chromatograms, we still detected chlorinated aromatic structures. To prove our hypothesis
that these molecules were potentially forming in the GC-MS column due to high temperatures,
we characterized the oxidized products using nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR). The proton
NMR spectra did not show any signs of aromatic protons, and we only detected aliphatic
oxidized species (Figure 3). This was excellent news as the chlorinated aliphatic compounds can
be microbially digested.

Microbial Conversion & Bio-Products

Initial screenings of model chlorinated compounds were conducted with Pseudomonas putida
KT2440 (target bacteria for microbial conversion) to determine whether the presence of free
chloride ions would inhibit cell growth or be detrimental to the bacterial activity. To do this, we
dosed NaCl in a buffer with KT2400 and monitored the OD600. OD600 is a measurement
technique that uses a spectrophotometer to determine the concentration of cells in a liquid, such
as bacteria, by measuring the optical density (OD) at a wavelength of 600 nanometers (nm). The
results presented in Figure 4 show that the KT2400 maintains active cells with loadings of NaCl
as high as 500 mM. This demonstrates high tolerance of the KT2400 to chloride ions, which is
promising for downstream microbial conversion studies in Phase 2.

 Figure 4. OD600 vs. time for KT2440 when exposed to various concentrations of NaCl.

In Phase 2 of our collaboration with Syensqo, we propose a microbial engineering approach to
convert the oxidized products from IXAN MLF to target bioproducts. This is a highly tunable
process, and a myriad of bioproducts can be targeted. Initial proof of concept work targeted PHA
polymers as the bioproduct to demonstrate that mixed plastics waste today could be converted to
bioplastics for the future. However, other bioproducts that may be of interest to the Syensqo
