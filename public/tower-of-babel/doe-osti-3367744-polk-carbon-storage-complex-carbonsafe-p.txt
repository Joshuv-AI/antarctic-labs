 Abstract
 This report summarizes the workplan, technical progress, and high-level findings for the Polk Carbon
 Storage Complex (PCSC) CarbonSAFE Phase III project. The project was designed to advance a
 commercial-scale geologic CO₂ storage by drilling and completing two characterization wells to
 strengthen an already-submitted Underground Injection Control (UIC) Class VI permit application,
 acquiring new subsurface data, advancing National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA) requirements,
 progressing CO₂ transportation engineering, and developing commercial and community
 engagement frameworks.
 Prior to mutual project termination, the project team completed foundational work across most
 workstreams. This included finalizing the Project Management Plan (PMP), initiating NEPA pathway
 activities, advancing the Class VI review process by responding to the Environmental Protection
 Agency’s (EPA) Request for Additional Information (RAI), designing the three dimensional (3D)
 seismic survey and initiating the associated permitting process, and preparing drilling permits for
 the two planned characterization wells. The team also prepared and submitted a Class V permit
 application for the characterization wells to the State of Florida. The project team conducted
 extensive planning and procurement for the first characterization well drilling which provided
 location-specific insights to inform future drilling campaigns for similar projects in Florida.
 The work completed provides valuable regulatory, technical, and commercial findings and identifies
 important challenges. This work and associated findings also support future carbon capture and
 storage (CCS) evaluation efforts that assist the United States Department of Energy (DOE) with
 reviewing project feasibility under their CarbonSAFE program.

 1. Executive Summary
 This Close-Out Report summarizes the work completed under the DOE-funded CarbonSAFE Phase
 III project during the first nine months of execution, before mutual termination of the cooperative
 agreement. The project aimed to advance a commercial-scale geologic carbon dioxide (CO₂) storage
 hub by drilling two characterization wells to strengthen an already submitted EPA UIC Class VI permit
 application, acquiring new subsurface data, advancing NEPA requirements, supporting CO₂
 transport engineering, and developing the business and community engagement frameworks.
 Although early termination prevented field activities, the team completed several key preparatory
 tasks that support future assessment of the storage site.

 1.1. Project Overview and Early Termination
 The CarbonSAFE Phase III effort was designed to reduce regulatory, technical, and commercial
 uncertainty associated with the proposed storage complex. Two new characterization wells were
 planned to provide critical subsurface data for updating the geologic model, refining reservoir
 simulations, confirming injectivity, and validating seal performance for UIC Class VI permitting.
 Approximately nine months into the project, Phase III work was halted due to strategic and
 commercial considerations. As a result, drilling, seismic acquisition, testing, and related data
 integration were not performed.

 1.2. Summary of Accomplishments
 Project Management Plan: A comprehensive PMP was completed at project initiation, detailing
 organizational structure, project schedule, work breakdown, communication pathways, risk register,
 and DOE reporting protocols. This facilitated coordinated early-stage execution across technical,
 regulatory, and commercial teams.
 NEPA Activities: The team initiated the NEPA pathway and compiled baseline environmental
 information relevant to drilling operations, seismic acquisition, site access, and pipeline routing.
 Early environmental screening, scoping considerations, and agency coordination planning were
 completed. Full NEPA documentation was not initiated due to the early end of the project.
 UIC Class VI Permit to Construct: A UIC Class VI permit application for the primary injection well
 had already been submitted prior to Phase III. Planned Phase III activities focused on strengthening
 the application through supplemental data obtained from the two proposed characterization wells.
 Work completed included advancing the Class VI review process by responding to EPA’s RAI for the
 UIC Class VI permit, planning for model updates with future data, gap analysis of required supporting
 datasets, and continued coordination with EPA Region 4 staff. The additional data needed for final
 permit strengthening were not collected as the wells were not drilled.

 8

 Detailed Site Characterization: Existing geologic, petrophysical, and well data were consolidated
 to support early model refinement and well design planning. Phase III characterization activities
 progressed to the preparatory stage, including:
 • Designing the 3D seismic survey and initiating the associated permitting process.
 • Preparing drilling permits for the two planned characterization wells.
 • Preparing and submitting a Class V permit application to the State of Florida.
 These activities were aimed at enabling acquisition of new subsurface data for updated geologic and
 reservoir models. No field data collection occurred before project termination.
 Storage Field Development Planning: A conceptual development plan was prepared, outlining
 proposed injection well configuration, monitoring well locations, and a preliminary monitoring,
 reporting, and verification (MRV) framework. Anticipated injection rates, pressure management
 considerations, and early monitoring concepts were developed based on available data. Further
 refinement was planned once new well and seismic data became available.
 Pipeline FEED Study: Initial front-end engineering design (FEED) work was completed for the CO₂
 pipeline connecting the source to the storage site as part of CD-0000007 and made available to this
 award. Tasks included route screening, development of the design basis memorandum, review of
 regulatory requirements, and preliminary engineering assumptions regarding pipeline materials,
 diameter, pressure, and routing constraints.
 Business and Financial Planning: Preliminary business structures and ownership models were
 evaluated. No advanced negotiations or financial commitments were pursued during the shortened
 project timeline.
 Community Benefits Plan (CBP): An update of the initial CBP framework was started in alignment
 with DOE’s Justice40 principles. This included stakeholder mapping, identification of community
 partners, development of communication materials, and planning for early engagement activities.
 Prior to mutual termination of cooperative agreement Tampa Electric Company (TEC) was directed
 by DOE to suspend activities and reporting related to the CBP.
 Key Gaps and Insights: Without drilling the two characterization wells or acquiring the 3D seismic
 survey, major subsurface uncertainties remain regarding injectivity, reservoir heterogeneity,
 pressure response, and confining unit performance. Supplemental data needed for the UIC Class VI
 permit strengthening were not collected. NEPA documentation, reservoir model updates, and MRV
 refinement similarly depend on new data. Commercial and community engagement activities
 remained in early stages and require further development for full project readiness.

 1.3. Close-Out Statement
 Despite the abbreviated timeline, the project generated meaningful preparatory analyses and
 advanced key planning tasks that clarify regulatory pathways, technical requirements, and
 commercial considerations for storage site development. The work completed provides DOE and
 stakeholders with a valuable framework for reassessing or reactivating the project in the future. The
 team appreciates DOE’s support and submits this Close-Out Report summarizing progress, insights,
 and lessons learned during the initial Phase III period.
 2. Project Introduction and Objectives
 TEC is an investor owned, regulated utility serving customers in west central Florida. TEC brings a
 deep understanding of the requirements for successful integration of new technologies into an
 existing generating fleet and is a proven industry leader in deploying new technologies for power
 generation.
 TEC assembled a highly qualified team of consultants, in addition to their highly skilled internal
 team, to execute a detailed Site Characterization Study for the proposed PCSC near their Polk Power
 Station (PPS), an existing 1,190 MW natural gas combined cycle (NGCC) power station located in
 Mulberry, FL. The proposed project was expected to store a minimum of 150 million metric tonnes
 of CO2 over 30 years.
 The objective of the project was to assess the technical and commercial feasibility of developing a
 CO2 storage facility near PPS. The Project team was to complete a detailed geological
 characterization of the proposed PCSC through the drilling of up to two stratigraphic test wells,
 collecting a baseline 3D seismic survey of the proposed storage complex and surrounding areas, and
 conducting rigorous geological and geochemical lab testing.
 Activities in this Project were designed to advance the commercial project from a site
 characterization and permitting phase to construction, testing, and validation. Specifically, this
 project was to address any gaps in technical knowledge, obtain required surface and subsurface
 permits for construction, and garner stakeholder acceptance to enhance the Project’s social and
 economic viability. The Project objectives aligned with overall CarbonSAFE program objectives.
 The development of the PCSC is an extension of the on-going work being conducted by TEC to assess
 CO2 Capture funded under DOE cooperative agreement DE-FE0032224 and CD-0000007. The TEC
 CarbonSAFE III Project (the “Project”) would enable TEC to evaluate the technical and economic
 feasibility of a CO2 storage field capable of permanently sequestering a minimum of 3 MMTpa of CO2
 emissions from TEC’s proposed projects at PPS.
 Project tasks were aimed to characterize the storage complex including the reservoir and caprock,
 provide a site-specific risk assessment, update the Storage Field Development Plan (SFDP), conduct

 10

 thorough business financial analysis, and plan and conduct public outreach and stakeholder
 engagement.
 The summary of project objectives (SOPO) was divided into nine tasks to be completed over two
 budget periods. Each project task was designed to allow the Project to meet the CarbonSAFE Phase
 III objectives as well as the Recipient’s commercial development timelines. The Project would begin
 by ensuring all the contractual agreements and NEPA documentation were in place to drill
 stratigraphic test wells. These wells would be permitted under the UIC Class V program with the
 Florida Department of Environmental Protection (FDEP). The stratigraphic test wells would be
 constructed to United States EPA UIC Class VI standards.
 The Recipient submitted a UIC Class VI permit application for the storage complex to EPA Region 4
 prior to the start of the Project to stay on track with the overall commercial project development
 timeline.
 The Recipient was to gather additional seismic data to tie into overall site characterization efforts.
 Data collected from these activities would be used to update existing geological and reservoir
 models at the PCSC to allow reassessment of project area of review (AoR), support continued
 correspondence with EPA Region 4 during the technical review of the UIC Class VI Application to
 Construct, and development of the SFDP.
 Subsequent tasks would seek to incorporate ongoing efforts on CO2 capture and transport FEED
 studies into the SFDP in order to assess key project risks and develop a realistic commercial
 development timeline and costing schedule.
 The Recipient was to build upon ongoing Community Benefits Outcomes and Objectives efforts to
 implement a public outreach and engagement strategy that would focus on local stakeholders and
 regulators. The project team was to conduct workshops to disseminate project information, gather
 feedback, and synthesize it into actionable objectives to incorporate into the overall project
 development plan. Additional efforts that span the period of performance would include technology
 transfer and outreach and coordination with other DOE/ National Energy Technology Laboratory
 (NETL) projects. Per memorandums issued by DOE, TEC suspended activities and reporting related
 to CBP.

 3.2. Subrecipient Monitoring

 3.2.1. Project Oversight, Management, and Recurring Reporting

 TEC established a procedure for subrecipient monitoring. The purpose of the procedure was to
 provide clear guidance to all TEC members who were directly involved in the development and
 execution of projects involving federal awards. TEC’s monitoring of subrecipients included review of
 financial reports, follow-up and ensuring subrecipients took appropriate action on all deficiencies
 pertaining to the federal award, collecting detailed subrecipient information at time of subaward,
 and evaluating subrecipient risk of noncompliance through various factors, such as prior experience
 with similar subawards and audit results.
 Weekly meetings between TEC and key project consultants and other relevant vendors were
 conducted to focus on individual tasks, while monthly meetings were used for evaluating progress
 across all aspects of the project. In addition, design reviews were performed on a quarterly basis or
 as needed via videoconference or in-person. All vendors submitted monthly or quarterly reports
 detailing current and upcoming work schedules, any risks associated with completing tasks, and
 shared results. TEC had regular bi-weekly calls with the DOE Project Manager to provide status
 updates.
 Throughout the Project, TEC managed technical, schedule, and cost risks to ensure that the project
 milestones and goals were met. During regularly scheduled meetings, the current risks associated
 with activities were determined, evaluated and, if necessary, a mitigation plan was put in place. The
 status of project risks and mitigation plans were communicated to the DOE Project Manager through
 quarterly progress reports.

 3.5. Task 2 – National Environmental Policy Act
 3.5.1. Environmental Information Volume Overview

 In accordance with the NEPA (42 U.S.C. § 4321 et seq.), the PCSC Project team, as part of the
 CarbonSAFE Phase III program, continued coordination with the DOE to assemble
 environmental documentation in support of potential future carbon capture, transport, and
 storage deployment at Tampa Electric’s Polk Power Station in Polk County, Florida.
 The objective of this work was to provide DOE with the comprehensive environmental data and
 analyses necessary to support NEPA determination; Categorical Exclusion, Environmental
 Assessment, or Environmental Impact Statement – for all major components of a future
 integrated CCS project. The EIV consolidates the environmental baseline, identifies potential
 environmental effects from construction, operation, and decommissioning of the project, and
 establishes the framework for regulatory and stakeholder coordination across all three
 elements of CCS:
 • CO₂ capture at the existing Polk Power Station;

 3.6. Task 3 – UIC Class VI Permit to Construct
 3.6.1. PCSC Site Characterization

 The site characterization summarized here was originally completed in support of the UIC Class VI
 permit for construction of the PCSC injection wells and predates the CarbonSAFE Phase III activities.
 This characterization involved a comprehensive assessment of both the regional and local geology
 to establish a defensible framework for storage zone and confining unit properties.

 24

 The proposed geologic CO₂ storage site is located in southwestern Polk County in south-central
 Florida.
 The upper storage complex, Upper Cretaceous Permeable Zone 1 (UCPZ-1), composed primarily of
 the Lawson Formation, was the only interval considered for CO₂ storage at this stage of the project.
 Although the lower storage complex, Upper Cretaceous Permeable Zone 2 (UCPZ-2), may offer
 additional storage capacity, it was not included in the injection or modeling scope. UCPZ-1 is overlain
 by the Cedar Key Formation, which consists predominantly of a thick, laterally extensive section of
 crystalline anhydrite that serves as the primary confining unit.
 Static Earth Model
 The Static Earth Model (SEM) for the PCSC was generated in Schlumberger’s Petrel. The purpose of
 the model was to generate representative petrophysical properties (porosity and permeability).
 The framework for the PCSC SEM was based on contoured surfaces derived from the interpretation
 of well logs and formations tops from deep regional wells. The PSC_IW1 and PSC_IW2 wells, plus
 nine other wells, serve as the basis for the 50-mile by 55-mile SEM’s framework.
 The average distance between the TEC-existing wells with well log data and the nearest legacy oil
 and gas wells used for petrophysical characterization is approximately 18.5 miles. In a
 heterogeneous carbonate reservoir system, where porosity, permeability, facies distribution, and
 diagenetic alterations can vary considerably over short distances, such large well-to-well spacing
 significantly increases uncertainty in any interpolated petrophysical properties. Long-range
 interpolation is unlikely to capture localized heterogeneities, abrupt facies transitions, or small-
 scale diagenetic overprints that strongly influence fluid flow and pressure response.
 To reduce this uncertainty and further strengthen the existing Class VI reservoir model, two new
 characterization wells were proposed. These wells were supposed to provide direct, high-resolution
 data within the project footprint, including core, logs, pressure tests, and formation fluid samples.
 Incorporating these site-specific measurements would have enabled more accurate calibration of
 petrophysical properties, improve storage zone and confining unit characterization, reduce
 uncertainty in pressure evolution forecasts, and enhance the robustness of AoR evaluations and
 long-term site performance assessments. Overall, the additional data points were expected to
 significantly refine and reinforce the technical foundation of the current modeling framework.

 3.6.2. Dynamic Reservoir Model Setup

 The 3D dynamic reservoir simulation model for the project incorporated detailed well logs, core data,
 spinner logs and operational history from two nearby wastewater injection wells, along with regional
 seismic and geologic information. The simulation used the Computer Modelling Group (CMG-GEM)
 multiphase compositional reservoir simulator. It accounted for CO₂ and brine movement,

 25

 dissolution, and trapping, while assuming mineralization effects would be minimal within the
 project’s timeframe. Open boundary conditions were applied, as no significant faults were identified
 within the storage unit. Injection bottomhole pressures were constrained to no more than 90% of the
 fracture pressure gradient. The PCSC, area of review was delineated by using EPA’s pressure‑front
 method because threshold or critical pressure plume was larger than the CO2 plume.

 3.7.1. Seismic Design and Vendor Overview

 Seismic Acquisition Objectives
 The program integrates a 3D survey with supplemental two-dimensional (2D) acquisition, including
 ten profiles, designed to fully characterize the subsurface, image faults, structural dip, large-scale
 depositional features, and provide a baseline for future time-lapse monitoring of the CO₂ plume
 footprint. The 3D survey is designed to achieve vertical resolution, accurately imaging the Cedar Keys
 Formation anhydrite caprock and underlying storage formations. Source and receiver placements
 minimize surface gaps, reduce interpolation needs, and utilize energy sources capable of
 penetrating anhydrite layers while achieving Quantitative Interpretation compliance.
 Vendor Selection
 TEC, in partnership with ARI, conducted a competitive procurement process in July 2023 to select a
 vendor to permit, acquire, and deliver 2D and 3D seismic data for subsurface characterization of the
 proposed CO₂ Storage Complex (CSC). Three vendors were solicited. Vendor selection was based
 on technical capabilities to execute industry-standard 2D/3D acquisition and knowledge of local
 Florida conditions, including land use, access, and logistical constraints. ARI recommended
 SAExploration (SAE) due to its demonstrated technical expertise, prior experience in complex Florida
 settings, multi-state operations, and ability to deliver within budget and schedule constraints.
 Vendor Scope and Design
 SAE’s turnkey services include permitting, drilling, dynamite or vibroseis sources, geophones,
 recording systems, field staff, and project management. The program employs smaller vibrator
 trucks to minimize road impacts, with planning accounting for Florida-specific challenges, including
 phosphate-mined areas, water bodies, golf courses, industrial lands, a federal aviation site, marsh
 support, specialized drilling requirements, and hurricane season impacts.
 Project Phases and Operations
 Phase 1 focuses on permitting, requiring vendor knowledge of local geology, wetlands, sinkholes,
 protected species, and cultural resources, with all state-required bonds and environmental
 approvals. Phase 2 covers acquisition, including health, safety, and environmental support,
 coordination with local and state agencies, site surveying, private land access and compensation,
 and scheduling to avoid wet ground and hurricane seasons. Reporting requirements include detailed

 28

 acquisition reports, source/receiver layouts, fold maps, shapefiles, and field tapes, with secure
 deletion upon project completion.
 Summary
 This seismic program provides high-resolution imaging necessary for site characterization, risk
 assessment, and reservoir evaluation, while integrating environmental and logistical constraints,
 HSE best practices, and regulatory compliance measures. The combined 3D/2D survey design
 enhances structural understanding, supports accurate reservoir modeling, and enables safe,
 efficient, and minimally disruptive operations at the PCSC.

 The PCSC project is designed to store up to 128 MMT of CO₂ over a 31-year injection period followed

 Injection Wells: Three injection wells will target UCPZ-1, with possible future recompletion to UCPZ-

 Injection Well Plugging: Before plugging, external mechanical integrity will be verified using DTS,
 temperature logs, PNC logs, or Cement Bond Logs. Any issues identified will be corrected prior to
 abandonment. The tubing and packer will be removed where possible; if not, tubing will be cut and
 a cement-retainer method will be used. Cement plugs will be set using CO₂-resistant EverCRETE
 cement or a similar product in the storage complex and Class A cement will be used in shallower
 intervals. Plugs will be placed using balanced-plug procedures. Well control requirements—
 including blow out protector testing, continuous pressure monitoring, and maintaining two
 barriers—will be followed throughout the abandonment. After plugs are verified, final casing cuts will
 be made, the surface will be restored, and a certified plugging report will be submitted.
 Post-Injection Site Care and Site Closure: Once injection ceases, all CO₂ injection wells will be converted
 to monitoring wells for the Post Injection Site Care (PISC) period utilizing the existing technology
 within the well to collect data. External mechanical integrity will be confirmed before plugging using
 DTS or an equivalent method. Above-zone monitoring will include continuous pressure
 measurements in the first permeable unit above the caprock, annual geochemical sampling via U-
 tubes, and optional shallow groundwater sampling. Pressure dissipation will be tracked in injection
 and in-zone wells, and plume evolution will be assessed through surface seismic surveys every ten
 years during PISC.
 Site closure will follow a phased approach: removal of non-monitoring equipment after injection
 stops, decommissioning of monitoring facilities after PISC, plugging and abandonment of all wells,
 and full site restoration to pre-project conditions.

 3.9.1.1. Background and Purpose

 TEC engaged Sargent and Lundy (S&L) to perform a FEED Study for the implementation of an

 TEC is committed to providing reliable electricity in a manner that supports long-term

 evolving regulations. TEC has identified carbon capture as a technology that could help address
 these needs. As such, this FEED Study was conducted as part of the DOE NETL issued award

 The ION CO2 capture system will be designed to treat 100% of flue gas from all four combustion

 constructing a CO2 capture plant that is fully integrated into the existing PK2 facility.

 First, in the DCC, recirculating process water cools the flue gas and removes residual acid gases
 (Figure 3). The pH is balanced through caustic addition, and heat is indirectly rejected from the
 process water loop via a set of plate and frame heat exchangers supplied with cooling water
 from the BOP open-loop Cooling Water System. The Flue Gas Booster Fan moves flue gas from
 the DCC to the CO2 Absorber Contactor where the ION solvent selectively absorbs CO 2 via an
 exothermic, reversible reaction. Within the CO 2 Absorber Contactor, the flue gas enters above
 the CO2 absorption zone. The ION solvent exiting the CO2 absorption zone is now rich with CO 2
 and is collected in the CO2 Absorber Contactor sump before being pumped to the Regeneration
 System.
 The solvent is pumped from the CO 2 Absorber Contactor sump through heat exchangers and
 then to the Stripper Contactor. At the bottom of the Stripper Contactor, low pressure steam is
 added to the Stripper Reboiler System (or Stripper Steam Heaters) to indirectly heat the solvent
 and release CO2. The low-pressure steam is extracted from the PK2 Steam Turbine Generator
 and is transported to the CO 2 Island. The condensate from the Stripper Steam Heaters is
 returned to a Condensate Break Tank in the BOP System and eventually rejoins the Steam
 Turbine Condensate System. The CO 2-lean solvent is pumped from the Stripper Contactor
 sump, heat exchangers, then back to the CO 2 Absorber Contactor for further capture.
 The released CO2 exits the top of the Stripper Contactor and enters the Stripper Overhead (OH)
 Condenser where virtually all the remaining water is condensed. The CO2 stream then passes on to
 the CO2 Compressor, which is an electric motor-driven, integrally geared centrifugal type
 compressor (IGC). After the initial stages of compression (referred to as “LP” for “low pressure”), the

 38

 CO2 stream exits compression for interstage cooling and dehydration and then reenters the IGC for
 the final stages of compression to supercritical pressures (referred to as “HP” for “high pressure”).
 The CO2 stream is dried by a triethylene glycol (TEG) Dehydration System upstream of the HP IGC
 stages to mitigate water condensation and hydrate formation. Upon exiting the HP compression
 stages, the CO2 is a stream of dense-phase supercritical CO2, which is cooled for further
 pressurization by the sCO2 Pumps for transport to sequestration.
 3.9.1.3. Flue Gas Design Basis

 A flue gas design basis was developed to outline the flue gas characteristics including but not limited
 to flow, temperature, pressure, composition, and emissions that will be sent to the CCS.
 Development of the flue gas design basis was an iterative process through evaluation of multiple
 operating scenarios of PK2 to determine the selected design. Flows relative to maximum and
 minimum operating cases, frequency of operation, along with steam extraction considerations were
 factored into the decision process. The final design of the flue gas was selected based on the results
 of the Steam and Electric Power Sourcing Study, which utilized advanced gas path (AGP) upgrades
 to the combustion turbines. Several operating conditions were considered when selecting the design
 case, which is the flue gas design basis that established the major process vessel sizing. The ideal
 design case should allow for CCS operation through the full range of operating conditions, with an
 optimal balance between capital expenses through equipment sizing and high capture efficiency at
 the maximum operating conditions. For the FEED study, the CO2 capture island is sized with a
 capture rate of 95% of the CO2 produced. Additional operating cases were provided as part of the
 flue gas design basis document to set rating cases for the CCS design and account for impacts where
 the flue gas and constituents deviate from design. Additionally, care was taken to ensure that the
 CCS equipment is sized to ensure that the CCS can accommodate the full range of operating
 conditions – from the minimum turndown case to the maximum operating condition. The final Flue
 Gas Design is shown below for the range of operating conditions considered. Additional cases
 developed are included in the Flue Gas Design Basis from DE-FE0032224.

 Summary
 Source feasibility has been studied previously and TEC’s Polk Power Station is the subject of a DOE
 funded NGCC Post Combustion Carbon Capture FEED study under DE-FE0032224. The Polk Power
 Station Carbon Capture FEED Study evaluates the technical and economic feasibility of retrofitting
 Tampa Electric Company’s Polk Unit 2, a four-combustion-turbine natural gas combined cycle plant
 in Mulberry, Florida, with an amine-based post-combustion CCS using ION Clean Energy’s ICE-31
 solvent technology. The CCS is configured as two parallel 50% trains to treat 100% of flue gas from

 40

 all four combustion turbines, capturing and compressing CO₂ to pipeline specifications for off-site
 transport and storage.
 A comprehensive Project Design Basis was developed, covering ambient and site conditions, flue
 gas characteristics, and BOP systems including new flue gas ductwork and booster fans, a new
 cooling water system with mechanical draft cooling towers, makeup and demineralized water tie-
 ins, wastewater handling, solvent storage and maintenance systems, steam extraction from the
 existing steam turbine, and condensate return back to the base plant.
 Several scope definition studies were completed to arrive at an optimized integration concept. For
 steam and power sourcing, the study selected steam extraction from the steam turbine IP–LP
 crossover in combination with combustion turbine AGP upgrades as the preferred option, as it
 provides a favorable balance of performance, plant heat rate impact, and CCS integration complexity
 compared with auxiliary boilers or separate Combined Heat and Power options. Cooling options
 were screened, and mechanical draft cooling towers were chosen over dry, hybrid, once-through,
 and wet surface air cooler alternatives based on site-specific water availability, footprint, auxiliary
 load, and overall economic performance.
 Additional FEED work addressed integration with the existing reclaimed water treatment plant,
 evaluated multiple CCS train configurations (confirming a 2×50% train design as the best trade-off
 between cost, flexibility, and outage risk), and developed multi-discipline engineering deliverables
 in process, mechanical, electrical, instrumentation and controls, and civil/structural, along with
 constructability plans and a project execution strategy built around multiple coordinated contracts
 rather than a single Engineering, Procurement, and Construction model.
 The study also conducted a formal Process Hazard Analysis (PHA)/HAZOP, identifying
 enhancements but confirming that the CCS and BOP systems are generally well-designed from a
 safety and operability standpoint. DOE-required assessments were completed for the business
 case, life-cycle greenhouse gas impacts, environmental health and justice, economic revitalization
 and job creation, technology maturation, and workforce readiness, showing that the project can
 substantially reduce net CO₂ emissions while supporting local economic benefits and leveraging a
 maturing solvent technology.
 On the cost side, the FEED produced an AACE Class 3 estimate that reflects a large, capital-intensive
 project with substantial but manageable operating costs; when annualized, these costs yield a cost
 of capture that is intended to be evaluated against available tax incentives, CO₂ sales, and broader
 decarbonization objectives. Collectively, the completed FEED supported moving into integrated
 FEED (including the CO₂ pipeline), more detailed engineering, and ultimately potential construction
 and commissioning of this first-of-a-kind NGCC carbon capture project at PK2.

 4. Significant Findings and Impacts
 This section summarizes the key technical and non-technical findings that could impact future
 CCS projects and outlook in the region. This section is focused on emphasizing the value of DOE
 dollars spent to advance their programmatic objectives.
 The project generated several important technical findings that advance DOE’s programmatic goals
 for safe, predictable, and commercially scalable carbon storage. A major contribution was the
 improved understanding of Area of Review delineation in geologic settings characterized by open
 boundaries and laterally extensive carbonate formations. High-resolution 3D reservoir simulations
 revealed that AoR estimates are strongly influenced by the size and configuration of the
 computational domain—an issue not well recognized in earlier research studies. This behavior
 highlights that the selection of domain size and boundary conditions for regional-scale formations
 is not trivial and remains an active area of research, especially for reservoirs where large-scale
 connectivity or partial boundary openness may influence far-field pressure distribution. These
 findings collectively underscore the need for multi-dimensional, site-specific modeling when
 defining AoR for Class VI applications in Florida and the broader Gulf Coast region.
 In addition to reservoir and well integrity modeling, the project advanced the geologic and monitoring
 framework for future CCS deployment in central and southern Florida. The modeling work clarified
 how low-permeability intervals influence pressure equilibration rates and the need for strategically
 placed in-zone monitoring wells to observe pressure behavior in real time. Insights gained from
 integrating legacy Class I well data and developing a comprehensive monitoring network design will
 benefit future Class VI applicants and reduce permitting uncertainty.
 On the non-technical side, the project achieved outcomes that support DOE’s broader mission of
 enabling the safe and commercially viable deployment of CCS technologies. Engagement with
 regional stakeholders—including water management districts, state regulatory agencies, and
 institutional partners—helped clarify permitting pathways, data expectations, and long-term
 monitoring requirements for Class VI projects in Florida. These interactions strengthened
 interagency coordination and improved the regional understanding of CCS technical and regulatory
 frameworks. The project also contributed meaningfully to workforce development, providing
 engineers, geologists, and students with practical experience in advanced reservoir modeling, well
 integrity assessment, and regulatory documentation. Collectively, these outcomes demonstrate
 that DOE’s investment has produced both scientific advancements and strengthened institutional
 and technical capacity, thereby facilitating more efficient planning, characterization, and permitting
 of future CCS projects in Florida and similar geologic settings.
