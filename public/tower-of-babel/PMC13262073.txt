Synthesis and biological evaluation of novel β-keto ether-triazole hybrids as dual antimicrobial and anticancer agents
Authors: Bakr F. Abdel-Wahab, Mahmoud H. Hendy, James C. Fettinger, Abdelbasset A. Farahat, Ahmed F. Mabied
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The growing need for multifunctional agents to overcome antibiotic resistance and infection-related complications in immunocompromised cancer patients necessitates the development of dual-action therapeutics. Herein, a series of β-keto ether–triazole hybrids incorporating a 5-methyl-1-(4-nitrophenyl)-1 H-1,2,3-triazole moiety were synthesized via a one-step protocol and characterized by IR, NMR, and single-crystal X-ray diffraction. Crystallographic and Hirshfeld surface analyses revealed well-defined geometries stabilized by C–H···O and O–H···O interactions. DFT calculations showed excellent agreement with experimental structures (RMSD < 0.04 Å) and HOMO–LUMO energy gaps of 3.04–3.50 eV. MEP analysis indicated localized electrophilic regions in compound 3 and greater charge delocalization in compound 9. Biologically, compound 3 displayed potent antibiofilm activity, achieving 97% inhibition of methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus, while compound 9 showed moderate yet selective cytotoxicity toward hepatocellular carcinoma cells (HepG2) (IC₅₀ = 63.28 µg/mL) with low toxicity to normal Wi38 cells. ADME profiling correlated solubility with antimicrobial activity and lipophilicity with cytotoxic effects, revealing that the high solubility of compound 3 favours antibacterial activity, while the greater lipophilicity and metabolic stability of compound 9 support its anticancer profile. These findings highlight β-keto ether–triazole hybrids as promising scaffolds for dual antimicrobial and anticancer applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13065-026-01797-y.

Introduction

The rapid increase and worldwide spread of antibiotic resistance have emerged as serious public health issues, significantly harming the success rate of currently available therapeutic agents [1, 2]. Multidrug-resistant bacterial and fungal diseases continue to emerge, resulting in increasing morbidity, mortality, and medical expenses. Although various classes of antimicrobial medicines are therapeutically available, many suffer from substantial limitations, including resistance development, toxicity, a limited antibacterial spectrum, and significant drug-drug interactions [1, 3]. These challenges underscore the urgent need for the discovery and development of new chemotherapeutic agents with improved efficacy, selectivity, and safety profiles [2, 3].

Heterocyclic compounds remain a cornerstone in antimicrobial drug discovery due to their structural diversity, favourable physicochemical properties, and capacity to interact with diverse biological targets [4]. Among these, triazole rings particularly 1,2,3- and 1,2,4-triazoles are recognized as important scaffolds in medicinal chemistry, owing to their metabolic stability, strong hydrogen-bonding potential, and ability to coordinate with metal centres in enzyme active sites [5–8]. The clinical success of triazole-based antifungals highlights this scaffold’s therapeutic value. For example, Fluconazole and Itraconazole were among the first widely used azoles that inhibit fungal lanosterol 14α-demethylase (CYP51), disrupting ergosterol biosynthesis and weakening cell membranes. More recent triazole antifungals such as Voriconazole, Posaconazole, and Isavuconazole have expanded clinical utility against resistant fungal species and improved pharmacokinetic profiles. Continuous structural modification of the triazole nucleus therefore remains a promising strategy to enhance antimicrobial potency and overcome these limitations agents [5–8].

Beyond antifungal therapy, the 1,2,4-triazole scaffold is widely recognized as a privileged structural motif in medicinal chemistry. Numerous triazole derivatives have demonstrated antibacterial activity through mechanisms involving enzyme inhibition, interference with nucleic acid synthesis, or disruption of membrane integrity. However, several reported antimicrobial triazoles exhibit moderate potency, limited selectivity, or unfavourable pharmacokinetic properties, which restrict their clinical translation. Accordingly, rational molecular design and hybridization approaches are increasingly explored to generate more effective antimicrobial agents [9–11]. Triazole derivatives also possess notable anticancer potential. The triazole moiety is present in clinically used anticancer agents such as the aromatase inhibitors Anastrozole and Letrozole, which suppress estrogenic biosynthesis and are widely employed in the management of hormone-dependent breast cancer. In addition, numerous synthetic triazole-based compounds have been reported to exert antiproliferative effects through diverse mechanisms, including induction of apoptosis, inhibition of key signalling pathways, and interference with DNA-related processes. Nevertheless, many investigational triazole derivatives display only moderate cytotoxic activity or limited tumor selectivity, highlighting the need for novel structural modifications to enhance their therapeutic potential [12, 13].

Molecular hybridization has emerged as an effective strategy in drug discovery, enabling the integration of distinct pharmacophoric fragments into a single molecular framework to achieve synergistic biological effects [14]. Given this extensive pharmacological precedent, triazoles represent an attractive framework for scaffold-based drug design targeting both microbial and neoplastic disease. In the present study, a molecular hybridization strategy was utilized, coupling the biologically validated triazole core with a β-keto ether moiety known to modulate electronic distribution and enhance target engagement. In this context, incorporation of a β-keto ether moiety into a triazole scaffold may modulate electronic distribution, hydrogen-bonding capacity, and lipophilicity, potentially improving membrane permeability and biological target interactions [15–18]. The β-keto ether functionality can also serve as a versatile structural element capable of influencing molecular conformation and binding affinity [11, 19, 20].

The rationale for the present study is driven by the urgent need to develop multifunctional agents that exhibit both antimicrobial and anticancer activities, particularly for immunocompromised patients, such as those undergoing chemotherapy who are highly susceptible to opportunistic infections. Based on these considerations, we designed and synthesized a novel series of β-keto ether–triazole hybrids incorporating a 5-methyl-1-(4-nitrophenyl)-1 H-1,2,3-triazole core. The triazole nucleus was selected for its well-established pharmacological relevance, metabolic stability, and ability to participate in key hydrogen-bonding and π–π interactions, while the β-keto ether fragment was introduced to modulate electronic properties and enhance biological responsiveness. Strategic incorporation of electron-withdrawing substituents was further intended to fine-tune electrophilicity and optimize interactions with microbial and cancer-associated molecular targets.

The present work reports the synthesis of novel β-keto ether–triazole derivatives through a straightforward one-step protocol, followed by comprehensive structural characterization using spectroscopic techniques and single-crystal X-ray diffraction. Hirshfeld surface analysis and density functional theory (DFT) calculations were applied to elucidate intermolecular interactions and electronic properties. The synthesized hybrids were subsequently evaluated for their antibiofilm and anticancer activities, demonstrating promising dual biological potential as multifunctional therapeutic candidates for further investigation. In addition, ADME (the absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion) predictions were conducted to clarify how key structural features influence pharmacokinetic behaviour and to correlate these properties with the observed antimicrobial and anticancer performance.

Materials and methods

Synthesis

General

The infrared (IR) spectra were recorded using a Bruker Invenio-S FTIR spectrometer covering a wide range from 4000 to 400 cm− 1, with calibration performed using a polystyrene film to ensure accuracy. The NMR spectra (500 MHz for 1H and 125 MHz for 13C) of 3, 5 and 7 were performed on a JEOLNMR spectrometer. While the NMR spectra for compound 9 were performed on a 600 MHz Bruker spectrometer. The chemical shift (δ) was reported in ppm and coupling constant (J) was measured in Hz. The NMR spectra were recorded in DMSO-d6. Compounds 1 and 6 were obtained from the NRC-Fine Organic Chemicals Unit at the National Research Centre, Egypt, following established procedures [21]. The synesis and progress of the reactions was monitored by the reaction method, including reaction time and conditions, was adopted from our previously published work [21, 22].

For further characterization and to obtain crystals suitable for X-ray analysis, a portion of the product was recrystallized from DMF using a slow evaporation technique. X-ray crystallography

The crystals were approximately in orthogonal dimensions of 0.116 × 0.317 × 0.379, 0.148 × 0.160 × 0.343, and 0.166 × 0.349 × 0.589 mm³ for 3, 5, and 9, respectively. The selected single crystals were placed and optically centred on the Bruker APEXII CCD and Photon2 CMOS systems; the data were collected and processed through the APEX-3 software package [23–25]. The structural determination and refinement were performed using SHELX software [26, 27]. We used the crystallographic software PLATON [28], Mercury [29], and ORTEP-3 for Windows [30] for the analysis and graphical presentation of the structure. We carried out and illustrated the Hirshfeld surface investigation using the CrystalExplorer software and related 2D fingerprint plots [31]. The corresponding crystallographic data have been deposited in the Cambridge Crystallographic Data Centre (CCDC) under the accession numbers CCDC 2,446,245 (compound 3), CCDC 2,446,246 (compound 5), and CCDC 2,446,247 (compound 9). These data can be obtained free of charge from the CCDC via www.ccdc.cam.ac.uk/data_request/cif.

Computations

The density functional theory (DFT) method employing B3LYP with the 6-311G(d, p) basis set was applied to optimize the molecular geometry of the compounds examined in their ground state [32–34]. All computations were conducted utilizing the Gaussian 09 software package with the molecular visualization tool Gauss View 05. The identical basis set of density functional theory (DFT) was employed to calculate both the molecular electrostatic potential (MEP) and the frontier molecular orbitals (FMOs) [35, 36]. Comparison between X-ray and DFT molecular geometry was done using Qmol, which is a Windows-based program for molecular visualization [37].

The absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME) profiles of the synthesized compounds were assessed utilizing the SwissADME web application (http://www.swissadme.ch) [38]. SMILES notations for each compound were utilized to infer their physical features, including lipophilicity, aqueous solubility, pharmacokinetics, drug-likeness, and medicinal chemistry characteristics. The analyzed key descriptors comprised molecular weight (MW), topological polar surface area (TPSA), partition coefficient (Log P), solubility (Log S), gastrointestinal (GI) absorption, blood–brain barrier (BBB) permeability, P-glycoprotein (P-gp) substrate status, and cytochrome P450 (CYP) inhibition potential. The data were analyzed in accordance with the Lipinski, Ghose, Veber, and Egan criteria to evaluate drug similarity and oral bioavailability [38, 39].

Bioactivity

Biofilm inhibition assay

Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) was selected as the target organism for this study due to its resistance to methicillin and multiple classes of antibiotics, making its infections particularly difficult to treat [40, 41]. MRSA is a clinically significant pathogen responsible for a broad spectrum of infections, including skin, respiratory, and bloodstream infections, in both healthcare and community settings [11, 42]. Its multidrug resistance and strong biofilm-forming ability render it an ideal model organism for assessing the antimicrobial potential of newly synthesized compounds. Moreover, MRSA infections are especially problematic in immunocompromised individuals and cancer patients, aligning with the dual antimicrobial and anticancer focus of the present work. To assess the efficacy of compound3 (HGB743), compound5 (HGB746), and compound9 (HGB763) in inhibiting or diminishing bacterial biofilm formation, we employed microtiter plate (MTP) technology against methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), a clinical isolate recognized for its significant biofilm-forming capabilities. Our experimental protocol was adapted from prior research [43]. Specifically, MTPs, coupled with tryptic soy broth (TSB) enriched with 1% glucose, were exposed to varying concentrations of silver nanoparticles. The microorganisms were inoculated into the MTPs and incubated for 48 h at 37 °C, followed by a dilution (1:100) in TSB to achieve a target inoculum size of 1.5 × 108 CFU/mL. After incubation, we carefully removed the well contents without damaging the established biofilms. The biofilms were then fixed for 10 min using 200 µL of 95% methanol and subsequently washed three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at pH 7.4. Each well was filled with 200 µL of a 0.3% w/v crystal violet solution and incubated for 15 min at 37 °C. For quantitative assessment of biofilm formation, 30% acetic acid was applied to the wells after rinsing with distilled water. Absorbance readings were taken using a microplate reader (STATFAX-USA) at OD 540 nm, with results evaluated by comparing treated wells to untreated controls [44].

Cytotoxic and anticancer activity

The American Type Culture Collection provided two distinct cell cultures (HepG2): adenocarcinoma of colorectal epithelium and normal Wi38 cells. To establish a confluent monolayer, 1 × 105 cells (in a 100 µL volume) were seeded into 96-well tissue culture plates and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C. Once a confluent layer was achieved, the growth medium was removed, and the cell monolayer was washed twice with a washing buffer. The test sample was divided and diluted into RPMI medium containing 2% serum (maintenance medium). Each dilution was introduced in 0.1 mL increments into several wells, while three wells received only the maintenance medium as controls. Following incubation at 37 °C, the plates were assessed for physical indicators of cytotoxicity, including cell shrinkage, pruning, granule formation, and partial or complete loss of the monolayer. A 5 mg/mL solution of MTT was prepared in PBS. The resulting formazan, a metabolic byproduct of MTT, could be redissolved in 200 µL of DMSO. The solution was shaken for 5 minutes at 150 rpm to ensure complete mixing of formazan and solvent. After eliminating background light, optical density was measured at 560 nm, with cell count expected to correlate directly with optical density [45, 46]. Crystallographic analysis

X-ray diffraction is a crucial technique for following the structural changes in materials and their effect on the properties and applications [47, 48]. The determination of the crystal structure of biological materials is of significant importance for the advancement of pharmaceutical materials [49]. The crystal structure provides essential insights into the molecule’s arrangement, which affect its interactions with biological targets [50]. Furthermore, Hirshfeld surface analysis, a method for visualising and quantifying molecular interactions, is utilised to augment crystallographic data and assist in molecular identification processes [51]. Three compounds 3, 5, and 9 were successfully analyzed by single crystal X-ray diffraction. Compound 3 crystallizes in the triclinic system with space group P-1, while compounds 5 and 9 adopt monoclinic systems with space groups P2₁/n and P2₁/c, respectively. Each crystal structure contains four molecules per unit cell, and the refinement parameters confirm the high quality of the collected data. Complete crystallographic data, including cell parameters, bond lengths, and angles, are provided in the supplementary information.

The ORTEP plots (Figs. 1, 2 and 3) illustrate the molecular structures with displacement ellipsoids drawn at the 50% probability level, showing a general planarity for every individual moiety of the compounds. Bond lengths and angles agree with standard values, indicating well-defined molecular geometries, and are also consistent with the reported structures with similar moieties [52–54]. Intermolecular interactions were further analysed using hydrogen bonding parameters, as illustrated in Tables 1 and 2, and 3, and Figs. 4 and 5, and 6. In compound 3, the main interactions are C-H–O bonds with a bond distance of 3.037–3.188 Å, while compound 5 shows C-H—O/N and O-H—O hydrogen bonds, the latter with a short H—A distance of 1.78 Å and a strong angle of 155°. Compound 9 exhibits a more diverse hydrogen bonding network, including C-H—O, C-H—N, and C-H—Cl interactions with bond distances up to 3.8350 Å. These structural features contribute to stabilizing the crystal packing in each compound.

Hirshfeld analysis

Hirshfeld surface analysis provided deeper insights into the examined substances’ intermolecular interactions and crystal packing. The study identified specific areas of tight contact, illustrated by red patches on the normalized contact distance (dnorm) surface, signifying robust hydrogen bonding and other non-covalent interactions. The red color represents shorter contacts, blue represents more extended contacts, and white represents contacts close to dnorm equal to zero. The shape index and curvedness maps further emphasize the existence of π-π stacking and van der Waals interactions, substantially stabilizing the crystal lattice. As illustrated in Fig. 7, dnorm surfaces showed compound 3 having bright red areas that suggest strong hydrogen bonding. Conversely, compounds 5 and 9 display weaker spots. The shape index maps show areas with higher and curved surfaces, especially compound 3, which means strong π-π stacking interactions exist.

The fingerprint plot results provide a quantitative analysis of substantial proportions for many bond types, including C-C, C-N, N-H, N-O, and C-O. The varied percentages of C-C bonds indicate differing conditions or strengths of these bonds within the molecules. The O-H bonds have a greater proportion (33%) in compound 9, indicating a strong interaction within that particular molecule. The C-H interactions are routinely reported with values between 12% and 21%, perhaps indicating several hydrogen bonding situations. The recurring sequences of values for O-H signify stable hydrogen bonding patterns and other non-covalent interactions, which correlate with their observed bioactivities. Compound 9 exhibited the strongest hydrogen bonding, as evidenced by prominent red regions on its dnorm surface and a higher proportion of O-H bonds (33%). These robust interactions likely enhance its stability and reactivity, contributing to its superior anticancer activity against biological targets. Also, the delocalized charge distribution, revealed by molecular electrostatic potential (MEP) analysis, further supports its efficacy by facilitating interactions with biological targets.

Compound 3 demonstrated the highest antibiofilm activity against MRSA, which could be attributed to its electrophilic nitro and carbonyl groups, which can be attributed to its robust hydrogen bonding and π-π stacking interactions, as evidenced by the bright red regions on the Hirshfeld surface, indicating stronger and more directed intermolecular forces. These interactions likely improve the compound’s stability and binding affinity to bacterial biofilm matrices, disrupting their structural integrity. Additionally, the molecular electrostatic potential (MEP) and frontier molecular orbital (FMO) analyses highlighted the electrophilic nature of compound 3, further supporting its reactivity and potential to interfere with bacterial cell communication and biofilm formation.

Compound 5, with intermediate Hirshfeld features, displayed balanced bioactivity, bridging the strong antibiofilm properties of Compound 3 and the potent anticancer effects of Compound 9. These findings underscore the role of molecular interactions, as elucidated by Hirshfeld analysis, in tailoring compounds for specific biological applications, offering a promising avenue for dual-function therapeutics in infection and cancer treatment.

Computational analysis

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations, incorporating geometry optimisation, molecular electrostatic potential (MEP), and frontier molecular orbital (FMO) analyses of the electronic properties and energies of compounds, are utilised to determine bonds and interactions with target proteins [55]. The application of integrated methodologies is essential in pharmaceutical research and in evaluating these molecules’ therapeutic qualities [56–59]. DFT modeling geometrically optimized the structures of the studied compounds and showed high consistency with experimental results, particularly regarding bond lengths and angles. The corresponding root mean square errors (RMSE) are 0.032, 0.033, and 0.031 for the structures of the compounds 3, 5, and 9 respectively, as shown in Fig. 8. The difference between the two data sets is due to the different conditions under which they were obtained, as the experimental results were for the solid phase, while the theoretical calculations were for the vacuum phase (gas phase). The crystal surroundings greatly affect the shape and size of molecules in the solid state, and the interactions between molecules are essential for holding them together in the crystal structure.

Molecular electrostatic potential (MEP)

MEP offers a technique for evaluating molecular relative polarity by correlating the total density surface with the electrostatic potential energy surface, revealing molecular dimensions, morphology, charge distribution, and reactive sites. MEP enables the formation of hydrogen bonds and structure-activity interactions while preserving the integrity of the molecular charge distribution, unaffected by external test charges. It exhibited a robust link among dipole moment, electronegativity, partial charge, and electrostatic potential, pinpointing active regions of positively or negatively charged chemicals.

Figure 9 shows the results of MEP computations, demonstrating unique charge distributions among compounds 3, 5, and 9, emphasising their reactive sites and polarity. Compound 3 showed significant negative potential near the nitro and carbonyl groups, signifying strong electrophilic areas, whereas compound 5 exhibited moderate polarity attributed to the hydroxyl group’s effect. Conversely, 9 showed a more delocalised charge distribution, owing to its supplementary nitro and chloro substituents.

Compound 3 exhibited strong negative potentials near the nitro and carbonyl groups, indicating electrophilic regions conducive to hydrogen bonding and π-π interactions, consistent with its crystallographic data. Compound 5 displayed moderate polarity due to the hydroxyl group, while compound 9 featured a more delocalized charge distribution, attributed to its additional nitro and chloro substituents. This delocalization likely enhances its reactivity and interaction with biological targets, particularly cancer cells. The pronounced negative regions in compound 9’s MEP map facilitate stronger interactions with cellular biomolecules. Furthermore, the presence of electron-withdrawing groups (nitro and chloro) in compound 9 enhances its electrophilic character, promoting covalent or electrostatic interactions with nucleophilic sites in cancer cells, thereby explaining its lowest IC50 value (63.28 µg/mL) against HepG2 cells.

Frontier molecular orbitals (FMOs)

Figure 10 visualizes the frontier molecular orbital (FMO) examination of compounds 3, 5, and 9, demonstrating unique electronic characteristics associated with their biological activities.

The energy gaps between the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) were determined to be 3.04 eV, 2.6 eV, and 3.5 eV for compounds 3, 5, and 9, respectively. The energy gap (3.5 eV) of compound 9 compared to compounds 3 and 5 suggests greater thermodynamic stability and targeted reactivity, aligning with its superior anticancer activity. The HOMO-LUMO distributions indicated concentrated electron density around the triazole and nitrophenyl groups, which are essential for interactions with biological targets. In this study, the correlation between bioactivity and FMOs is evident from the calculated HOMO–LUMO energy gaps of compounds 3, 5, and 9. Compound 3, with a moderate energy gap (3.04 eV), showed the strongest antibiofilm activity against MRSA (IC₅₀ = 14 µg/mL), suggesting that balanced electronic reactivity enhances bacterial target interaction. In contrast, compound 9, which exhibited the highest energy gap (3.50 eV), demonstrated superior anticancer activity against HepG2 cells (IC₅₀ = 63.28 µg/mL) along with greater thermodynamic stability and delocalized electron distribution. These results indicate that optimized HOMO–LUMO characteristics, particularly energy gap magnitude and orbital distribution over the triazole and nitrophenyl moieties, directly influence biological performance and target selectivity.

The overall computational findings align with recent DFT-based investigations on heterocyclic hybrids that relate electronic properties to biological [60]. Collectively, the DFT data support the experimental observations, providing insight into how substituent-driven electronic effects govern the dual antimicrobial and anticancer potential of the synthesized β-keto ether–triazole derivatives.

Bioactivity

Antibiofilm

The antibiofilm efficacy of the studied compounds 3, 5, and 9 against methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) yielded varying results, as illustrated in Fig. 11 and the experimental picture in Fig. 12 as shown in Table 4. Notably, when administered at double doses, compound 3 demonstrated the highest effectiveness in inhibiting biofilm formation by MRSA, with reductions of 97%, 94.2%, 92%, 76.6%, 67.95%, 62.75%, 51.35%, and 43.1% at concentrations ranging from 7.8 to 1,000 µg·mL⁻¹, the IC50 value recorded was 14 µg·mL⁻¹. Similarly, compound 5 decreased biofilm formation by 99%, 94.15%, 86.5%, 75%, 69.75%, 64.15%, 51.35%, and 33.45% across the same concentration range; the IC50 value recorded was 14 µg·mL⁻¹. In contrast, compound 9 resulted in reductions of 98.5%, 74.49%, 60.58%, 51.23%, 32.82%, 27.16%, 19.21%, and 15.51% at doses from 7.8 to 1,000 µg·mL⁻¹; the IC50 value recorded was 120 µg·mL⁻¹. These compounds could potentially be utilized on the surfaces of medical devices to prevent biofilm formation. The inhibition of biofilms observed through our crystal violet assays aligns with findings reported by Khan et al. [61]. Biofilms are significant in medical contexts due to their role in bacterial pathogenesis and their resistance to antibiotic treatment [40]. The intricate process of biofilm formation is regulated by bacterial cell-to-cell communication [41]. Approximately 80% of infections are associated with biofilm development. The longevity of these biofilms is heavily influenced by the composition of the extracellular matrix, which primarily consists of extracellular polymeric substances (EPS), proteins, lipids, and extracellular DNA (eDNA) [42]. Due to the presence of both a β-keto ether and a triazole moiety, several mechanisms may be at play. The signaling molecules auto-stimulators, which are employed in quorum sensing, share structural similarities with beta-keto esters [11]. The beta-keto ether derivative may bind to Lux R-type proteins by imitating these auto-stimulators, hindering coordinated biofilm formation and bacterial communication [11]. Membrane-targeting properties of triazole derivatives have been demonstrated to cause cell membrane disruption [62].

ADME study

In modern drug discovery, it is important to understand the absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion (ADME) properties of candidate molecules to predict their biochemical and pharmacokinetics performance and therapeutic potential. ADME profiling helps to identify compounds with appropriate solubility, permeability and metabolic stability, which are important to achieve effective bioavailability and safety in vivo. Computational prediction tools, provide valuable insight into these parameters in the early stages of drug design, guiding the optimization of molecular structures towards improved drug similarity and efficacy [39, 64]. ADME computations were performed to elucidate how the structural features of the synthesized β-keto ether–triazole hybrids influence their pharmacokinetic behavior and correlate with their observed antimicrobial and anticancer activities [38, 39].

Physicochemical properties and lipophilicity: All compounds meet key criteria for drug similarity, indicating potential oral bioavailability. Compound 3 showed high solubility and moderate lipophilicity (log P = 0.93), while compound 5 and compound 9 showed high lipophilicity (log P = 3.19 and 2.19, respectively) with TPSA values within the acceptable range. These values suggest an optimal balance between hydrophilicity and membrane permeability, which is important for bioactivity.

Solubility and permeability: Compound 3 were highly soluble (log S ≈ − 2.8), thus facilitating transport across biological membranes. Compound 5 and compound 9, with lower solubility (log S between − 5.4 and − 6.8), may show slower diffusion rates but improved target binding affinity due to more hydrophobic interactions. None of the compounds was able to cross the BBB, reducing the risk of undesirable kinetics. Compound 3 pharmacokinetic behavior showed significant gastrointestinal absorption and was identified as a potential P-glycoprotein substance.

Compound 5 and compound 9 showed low GI absorption but were non-P-gp substrates, suggesting increased intracellular persistence. Compound 5 and compound 9 both inhibited multiple CYP isoforms, indicating high metabolic stability, while compound 3 did not inhibit major CYP enzymes, indicating safe metabolic compatibility. ADME properties correlate strongly with the observed bioactivity. Compound 3 with higher solubility and GI absorption showed better antibiofilm activity against MRSA due to increased bacterial membrane penetration. Conclusions

This study reports the synthesis and comprehensive characterization of a new series of β-keto ether–triazole derivatives incorporating the 5-methyl-1-(4-nitrophenyl)-1 H-1,2,3-triazole scaffold. Combined single-crystal X-ray diffraction and density functional theory (DFT) analyses provided consistent structural and electronic insights, clarifying the relationship between molecular architecture and biological function. Crystallographic and Hirshfeld analyses confirmed that hydrogen bonding, π–π stacking, and charge delocalization stabilize the crystal lattice, while MEP and FMO results identified electrophilic and nucleophilic regions relevant to biological interactions. Biological evaluation revealed that compound 3 exhibited the strongest antibiofilm activity against methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA, 97% inhibition), whereas compound 9 showed the highest cytotoxicity toward HepG2 cells (IC₅₀ = 63.28 µg mL⁻¹) with minimal effects on normal Wi38 fibroblasts. ADME profiling supported these findings, indicating that compound 3’s high solubility and polar surface area enhance antimicrobial action, while compound 9’s lipophilicity and metabolic stability favor anticancer activity. These combined results establish a clear structure–property–bioactivity relationship across the synthesized derivatives. The strong agreement between computational predictions and experimental outcomes demonstrates the value of integrating theoretical and empirical approaches in rational drug design. DFT and ADME parameters accurately predicted physicochemical features governing biological performance, highlighting the predictive utility of computational chemistry. Overall, these β-keto ether–triazole hybrids represent promising dual-function therapeutics, particularly suited for immunocompromised or oncology patients prone to infection.

Future work will focus on rational structural optimization and comprehensive biological validation, including extended in vitro and in vivo studies with evaluation across multiple cancer cell lines, as well as mechanistic investigations such as cell cycle analysis. Molecular docking and related computational studies will further elucidate the mechanisms underlying the dual antimicrobial and anticancer activities. Collectively, this integrated strategy will support the development of β-keto ether–triazole scaffolds as promising platforms for multifunctional therapeutic agents.
