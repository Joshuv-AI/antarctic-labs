Reproducible benchmarking of four machine-learning classifiers on a large synthetic heart-disease competition dataset

Raed M Ennab

ABSTRACT
Background: Public synthetic datasets enable transparent model comparison but cannot establish performance in clinical populations. We asked how four fixed classifiers compare under one leakage-controlled validation framework on a large synthetic heart-disease competition dataset. Methods: The official training set contained 630,000 observations (44.83% outcome prevalence) and 13 predictors; the 270,000-row test set had no outcome labels. L2-regularized logistic regression, HistGradientBoosting, CatBoost, and LightGBM were evaluated using identical stratified five-fold outer splits. Logistic-regression preprocessing was fitted within outer-training data, and early stopping used outer-training data only. The primary measure was pooled out-of-fold (OOF) AUROC; secondary measures were average precision, Brier score, calibration intercept and slope, fold variability, and train–validation optimism. Duplicate/overlap, identifier-only, and permuted-outcome controls assessed leakage. Results: Pooled OOF AUROC was 0.955443 for CatBoost, 0.955324 for LightGBM, 0.955066 for HistGradientBoosting, and 0.952874 for logistic regression. Corresponding average precision ranged from 0.945912 to 0.948828 and Brier scores from 0.081116 to 0.083518. Calibration intercepts ranged from −0.001299 to 0.002153 and slopes from 0.998896 to 1.004388. Mean optimism was 0.000017–0.002686. No duplicated identifiers, duplicated feature profiles, or train–test feature-profile overlap were found; identifier-only AUROC was 0.500121 and mean permuted-outcome AUROC was 0.500615. Conclusions: All four models discriminated strongly and showed close internal calibration, while gradient-boosting models had only marginally higher AUROC than logistic regression. The principal contribution is a fully rerunnable, controlled benchmark rather than a novel algorithm or a clinically validated prediction model. External performance in real clinical cohorts remains unknown.

1. Introduction Cardiovascular prediction is a prominent application of machine learning, but performance estimates depend as much on the data-generating setting and validation design as on the algorithm itself [ 1 – 3 ]. Public synthetic datasets are useful for transparent, low-barrier benchmarking because investigators can share complete workflows without patient privacy concerns. They do not, however, recreate every dependency, measurement process, subgroup, or prevalence pattern of a clinical cohort [ 4 , 5 ]. Gradient-boosted decision trees are strong candidates for mixed-type tabular data. LightGBM emphasizes histogram-based computational efficiency, whereas CatBoost provides dedicated categorical-feature handling [ 6 , 7 ]. A regularized logistic model remains an important comparator because it establishes whether the additional nonlinearity and computational cost of boosting produce a material gain. Evaluation should extend beyond a single discrimination statistic: calibration, probability accuracy, fold variability, and transparent overfitting checks are central to reliable prediction-model reporting [ 8 – 10 ]. This study does not propose a new classifier and is not presented as clinical model development. Its contribution is an audit-oriented comparison generated by one fully rerunnable notebook with shared saved folds, fold-contained fitted preprocessing, out-of-fold predictions, calibration measures, training–validation optimism, and negative controls. The research question was: under a single leakage-controlled five-fold validation framework applied to the 13 official predictors, how do logistic regression, HistGradientBoosting, CatBoost, and LightGBM compare in discrimination, calibration, apparent overfitting, and computational cost? 2. Methods 2.1. Study design and data source We conducted a retrospective computational benchmarking study using the official Kaggle Playground Series Season 6 Episode 2 ‘Predicting Heart Disease’ files [ 11 ]. The organizers describe the data as synthetic and generated from a model trained on a heart-disease dataset. The training file contained 630,000 rows and 15 columns (identifier, 13 predictors, and outcome); the test file contained 270,000 rows and 14 columns (identifier and the same 13 predictors). Test outcomes were unavailable. Heart Disease was encoded Presence = 1 and Absence = 0. The training set contained 282,454 positive observations (44.83%) and 347,546 negative observations (55.17%). No resampling or class weighting was used because this benchmark was moderately balanced. Predictors and preprocessing Every model used the same 13 supplied predictors; the identifier was excluded. Eight low-cardinality variables were modeled as categorical: sex, chest pain type, fasting blood sugar over 120, electrocardiographic results, exercise angina, ST-segment slope, fluoroscopic vessel count, and thallium category. Data-integrity and split-comparability checks Before modeling, we counted missing and non-finite values; duplicate identifiers; repeated full rows; repeated feature profiles; cross-file identifier overlap; and exact cross-file feature-profile overlap using row hashes. Continuous train–test differences were summarized using absolute standardized mean differences and Kolmogorov–Smirnov statistics. Categorical differences were summarized by total variation distance. These test-file comparisons assessed structural similarity only; they did not estimate predictive performance because test outcomes were unavailable. 2.4. Shared validation design A single set of five stratified, shuffled outer folds (random state 42) was saved and reused for every workflow. Each outer fold contained 126,000 validation observations and one out-of-fold prediction was generated for every training row. All fitted preprocessing components and all model-selection steps used the corresponding outer-training data. For LightGBM, categorical dtype levels were assigned once from the observed predictor codes without using outcomes; every code occurred in every outer-training fold. For CatBoost and LightGBM, a stratified 10% inner holdout from each outer-training fold selected the early-stopping iteration; the chosen count was then fixed and a new model was fitted on all 504,000 outer-training observations before prediction of the untouched outer fold. HistGradientBoosting used its internal 10% early-stopping subset drawn only from outer-training data. The competition test predictions were retained for reproducibility but were not used to estimate performance. 2.5. Model specifications Logistic regression used L2 regularization (C = 1.0), the lbfgs solver, and a maximum of 1,000 iterations. Within each outer fold, continuous predictors were standardized and categorical predictors were one-hot encoded with unknown categories ignored. HistGradientBoostingClassifier used a maximum of 1,000 iterations, learning rate 0.06, 31 leaf nodes, minimum 20 observations per leaf, L2 regularization 0.01, internal early stopping with a 10% validation fraction, and 30 non-improving iterations. The eight categorical predictors were identified to the estimator and the random state was 42. CatBoost used binary log loss with AUROC monitoring, native categorical handling, at most 5,000 iterations, learning rate 0.02, depth 6, bagging temperature 0.2, CPU execution, and 200-round early stopping. Deterministic fold-specific seeds 43–47 were used for inner selection and outer refitting. LightGBM used a binary objective with AUROC monitoring, native categorical handling, at most 5,000 estimators, learning rate 0.02, maximum depth 8, 31 leaves, minimum 400 observations per leaf, feature and row subsampling of 0.8, subsampling frequency 1, L1 regularization 0, L2 regularization 2, and deterministic column-wise CPU training. Deterministic fold-specific seeds 43–47 were used, with the same inner-selection/refit design and 200-round early stopping. 2.6. Single-predictor analysis To describe standalone signal without imposing ordinal scores on categorical codes, each predictor was also evaluated in a separate logistic-regression workflow using the same five outer folds. Continuous predictors were standardized and categorical predictors one-hot encoded within each training fold. Pooled out-of-fold AUROC was calculated from the resulting probabilities. This analysis is descriptive and is neither a causal importance analysis nor a SHAP analysis. 2.7. Performance and calibration measures The primary measure was pooled AUROC calculated from the complete out-of-fold prediction vector. Secondary measures were mean and sample standard deviation of the five fold AUROCs, average precision (AP), Brier score, calibration intercept and slope, mean training AUROC, and mean optimism (training minus validation AUROC averaged across folds) [ 8 , 12 ]. Calibration intercept and slope were estimated by logistic regression (C = 10 6 ; lbfgs; maximum 1,000 iterations) of the observed outcome on the logit of out-of-fold probabilities clipped to [10 −6 , 1 − 10 −6 ]. Calibration curves used ten equal-frequency bins. Because no paired inferential comparison was planned for the canonical analysis, between-model differences are described numerically rather than as statistically significant. 2.8. Leakage and negative-control analyses Leakage safeguards comprised fold-contained fitted preprocessing, inner iteration selection isolated from each outer-validation fold, duplicate and cross-file feature-profile screening, an identifier-only AUROC, and five logistic-regression runs after independent permutations of the training outcome (seeds 20260815–20260819). Training–validation optimism provided a model-specific check for apparent overfitting. 2.9. Software and reproducibility The successful canonical run used Python 3.12.13, NumPy 2.0.2, pandas 2.3.3, SciPy 1.16.3, scikit-learn 1.6.1, Matplotlib 3.10.0, Pillow 11.3.0, CatBoost 1.2.10, and LightGBM 4.6.0 on CPU without an accelerator. The notebook saves fold assignments, individual and combined predictions, fold metrics, calibration-bin values, model settings, software versions, figures, input hashes, and a SHA-256 output manifest. The run completed in 10,629.1 seconds. The canonical source notebook is supplied as S1 File ; core outputs and combined predictions are supplied as S2 File , S1 – S3 Data , and a concise successful-run record is supplied as S3 File . 2.10. Ethics statement The analysis used publicly accessible synthetic competition data and involved no human participants, identifiable records, biological specimens, or clinical intervention. Institutional ethics approval and informed consent were therefore not applicable. 3. Results 3.1. Dataset integrity and comparability The prevalence of Presence was 44.83% (282,454/630,000). No missing or non-finite feature values, duplicate identifiers, cross-file identifier overlap, duplicated full training rows, duplicated feature profiles, or train–test feature-profile overlap were found. Train and test distributions were very similar: the largest absolute standardized mean difference among continuous predictors was 0.003846 (blood pressure), and the largest total variation distance among categorical predictors was 0.003089 (ST-segment slope). These findings support a clean same-generator benchmark split but do not constitute external validation. 3.2. Standalone predictor discrimination Single-predictor pooled out-of-fold AUROC was highest for thallium category (0.800037), followed by chest pain type (0.767724), maximal heart rate (0.755862), ST depression (0.733585), and fluoroscopic vessel count (0.714529) ( Fig 1 ). Blood pressure was near chance (0.499550), while fasting blood sugar over 120 yielded 0.508857. Discussion 4.1. Principal findings All four fixed classifiers achieved high and stable internal discrimination in this large synthetic benchmark. The ordering favored CatBoost numerically, but the entire AUROC range was only 0.002569, and logistic regression approached the boosted models at a fraction of their computational cost. The single-predictor analysis also showed that several supplied variables carried substantial standalone signal. The practical result is therefore not that one complex model was demonstrably superior, but that model choice altered performance only slightly under a shared, tightly controlled validation design. Calibration intercepts and slopes were close to their ideal values in out-of-fold data, and Brier scores were similar across workflows. Identifier and label-permutation controls remained at chance, while duplicate and cross-split overlap screens were negative. These checks increase confidence that the internal estimates were not driven by an obvious leakage mechanism. They cannot establish calibration or discrimination after transport to a different clinical population, where prevalence, measurement, case mix, and missingness may differ [ 4 , 5 , 8 ]. 4.2. Implications for reproducible benchmarking and future validation The comparison illustrates why a common validation scaffold matters. Every workflow used the same saved outer folds, all fitted preprocessing components used outer-training data, and the boosting procedures that needed explicit iteration selection were refitted on the complete outer-training data before validation. Full out-of-fold predictions allow the reported discrimination and calibration statistics to be recomputed directly. The small performance differences also make computational cost relevant: LightGBM approached CatBoost’s AUROC with markedly lower CPU time, whereas logistic regression provided a particularly efficient baseline. The analysis intentionally did not include SHAP, causal attribution, subgroup-fairness analysis, or a post hoc rule-based score. Fig 1 describes single-predictor discrimination only. A clinically useful next study would require temporal and geographic validation in real cohorts, reassessment of average precision and calibration under realistic prevalence, prospectively specified subgroup analyses, and decision-curve evaluation at clinically meaningful thresholds. TRIPOD+AI and PROBAST+AI provide appropriate reporting and appraisal frameworks for that work [ 9 , 10 ]. 4.3. Strengths and limitations The study’s main strengths are a single locked and fully rerun notebook, identical saved outer folds for all classifiers, fold-contained fitted preprocessing, inner early stopping with full outer-training refitting for CatBoost and LightGBM, complete out-of-fold predictions, multiple discrimination and calibration measures, explicit train–validation optimism, leakage audits, permuted-outcome controls, and cryptographic input and output manifests. Several limitations remain. The data are synthetic; their 44.83% outcome prevalence and feature relationships may not reflect any clinical population. Cross-validation estimates internal performance only, and the unlabeled competition test set derives from the same data-generation process; neither provides external or prospective validation. The model and hyperparameter set was fixed before the canonical rerun but was not chosen under a registered protocol and was not exhaustive; no causal, subgroup-fairness, decision-curve, or SHAP analysis was performed; and close internal calibration may not persist under prevalence or case-mix shift. These limitations constrain interpretation to comparative reproducibility within this benchmark. 5. Conclusions Within this synthetic benchmark, all four classifiers achieved high discrimination and close internal calibration, with only small gains for gradient boosting over L2-regularized logistic regression. The study’s contribution is a fully rerunnable comparison using shared folds, fold-contained iteration selection, saved out-of-fold outputs, and explicit leakage controls. These results provide a reproducible internal benchmark comparison, while performance in real clinical cohorts remains unknown. Supporting information S1 Fig Internal out-of-fold calibration curves.
