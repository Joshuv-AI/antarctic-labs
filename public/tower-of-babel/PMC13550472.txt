EEG activities and connectivities associated with surtitle presentation during live opera viewing: an exploratory analysis of cognitive absorption
Authors: Jared Boasen, Felix Giroux, Marie-Odile Duchesneau, Xavier Roy, Sylvain Sénécal, Pierre-Majorique Léger
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCharacterizing brain activities underlying text availability in an ecologically valid context and identifying whether aspects of these activities are influenced by one’s state of cognitive absorption (CA) are important yet unexplored lines of research in cognitive neuroscience. This study investigated the oculometric behavior and electroencephalographic (EEG) activities during periods with vs. without surtitle presentation in 25 human subjects while they viewed a live theatrical stage performance, and further explored relationships with self-reported CA. Behaviorally, subjects anticipated the appearance of text, and oriented their gaze toward them when they were present regardless of their level of CA . Neurophysiologically, text presence/absence was differentiated primarily by EEG theta activities and beta connectivities in or between visuospatial processing areas. Importantly, text presence-associated increases in theta activity in the frontal eye field and beta connectivity between the precuneus and the primary visual cortex did not significantly vary with self-reported CA, suggesting that these processes may support the automatic allocation of attention to available text.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-56620-4.Subject terms: Cognitive neuroscience, Attention, Reading, Human behaviour

Introduction

Modern cognitive neuroscience is increasingly concerned with characterizing brain activities underlying complex real-world human behaviors in order to build more robust cognitive models and inform the design of emerging technologies such as educational platforms, immersive media, and or adaptive user interfaces1–3. One behavior of broad relevance in such contexts is the allocation of visual attention to available text. In everyday environments, text often appears alongside rich and dynamic non-text information, and people must decide whether to orient toward it. Although controlled neuroimaging studies over the past several decades have identified cortical systems involved in processing text and other visual stimuli4–7, less is known about how text availability shapes gaze and brain activity in ecologically valid environments where multiple sources of information compete simultaneously for attention4–7.

Studying text-related processing in real-world settings is challenging for several reasons. First, text does not exist alone. It occupies the visual field simultaneously and dynamically with non-text visual information, each vying for our attention. Both text and non-text visual information can require the decoding of similar aspects of visual information such as color, shape, symbolism, or semantic qualities, which recruit similar or neighboring brain areas for processing4,7. Second, text can coincide with changes in other stimulus dimensions perceived by other sensory modalities. While co-occurring sensory stimulation with text can increase attention to it, concurrent stimulation could act as a distractor8. Third, electroencephalographic (EEG) imaging, whose size and portability are more practical than other modalities for real-world environments, may not provide the spatial fidelity necessary to clarify these aspects of text vs. non-text visual processing4.

Further complicating matters is the fact that visual information processing can vary according to the extent one is engaged or absorbed in the experience9,10. One concept that is relevant to this issue is cognitive absorption (CA)11, a multidimensional construct that encompasses immersion, attention, and curiosity toward visual stimuli. The stronger the state of CA, the more one becomes engrossed in the experience while disconnecting from the outside world and dissociating from the normal passage of time. Differences in CA levels have reportedly been associated with differences in EEG theta, alpha and beta oscillatory power, or more problematically, topographical asymmetries in alpha band activity12–14. Thus, estimations of text processing based on neurophysiological measures such as EEG could be confounded by the extent to which subjects were cognitively absorbed in the visual content. This is a problem because CA may be highly subjective and thus variable depending on the stimulus and the individual viewing it. Nevertheless, it is widely recognized that text seeking and reading behaviors are automatic, even in the presence of competing non-text visual stimuli15–17. This suggests that there are aspects of text processing that may be independent of CA.

These points raise two important questions: 1) Can we distinguish between the brain activities underlying text vs. non-text visual processing in a real-world environment? 2) If so, do these brain activities vary with CA?

Despite the shared aspects of processing text and non-text visual information, one potential differentiator between them may be the finite oculomotor control required to focus on and orthographically decode a string of text. Both functional and encephalographic studies have reported the importance of the frontal and supplementary eye field (FEF, SEF) activities and connectivities for orthographic processing of text5,18,19. Broad increases in intra and interhemispheric EEG coherence have also been observed in conjunction with increased orthographic text processing demands20. Thus, text processing may be distinguished from that of non-text in a real-world environment according to orthographic-related demands. However, it remains to be seen whether these observations would hold in free gaze conditions with dynamic and simultaneous presentation of text and non-text visual stimuli.

Meanwhile, we should also consider that the acquisition of visual information is a potentially rewarding endeavor which could help to resolve uncertainties or make predictions about one’s environment. Neurophysiologically, reward-mediated brain signaling has long been thought to influence visual activity due to the dense populations of mu-opioid receptors in visual processing areas21,22. Cortical areas involved in reward mediation are thought to comprise the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) and the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC)23–25. Strong evidence has now emerged that brain activity in these areas drives primates to seek and process visual information26. Therefore, given the inherent richness of text information, its underlying processing may be distinguished from that of non-text according to the interplay of brain activities in visual and reward processing networks. Notably, brain processing in these respective networks has been linked to visual attention27,28 and interest29, principal defining constructs of CA9.

In social sciences and information systems research, which focuses strongly on ecologically valid tasks, CA is typically assessed via self-reported scales. Perhaps the most well-known CA scale is the one developed by Agarwal & Karahanna, which captures five dimensions of temporal dissociation, focused immersion, control, enjoyment, and curiosity30. This scale has frequently been used in text-based and technology-mediated tasks, and has precedent in studies involving EEG11,31.

The present study sought to investigate oculometric behavior and EEG cortical activities and connectivities during a live operatic performance in which text (i.e., surtitles) was presented at a fixed location above the stage. This setting provided a rare opportunity to investigate neurophysiological and oculometric responses associated with periods of text availability in an immersive, social, and dynamic real-world environment. Oculometry was used to characterize gaze orientation toward the surtitle area, and CA was indexed via a post-experiment, self-reported scale. We assessed differences in EEG cortical activities and connectivities in the theta (5-7 Hz), alpha (8-13 Hz) and beta (15-29 Hz) bands between time periods when surtitles were and were not being presented (present vs. absent). We hypothesized that visual processing related to text might be distinguished from that of non-text visual stimuli according to differential processing in reward and oculomotor processing areas. Finally, we investigated how these neurocorrelates varied according to the self-reported CA scores.

Results

During Act I of five live performances of Bizet’s opera, Carmen, oculometry and EEG were simultaneously and synchronously recorded from five volunteer, healthy, right-handed, adult subjects each performance (N = 25 total). The five subjects sat together in adjacent seats in the rear row of the first floor of the opera hall (see Fig. 1). The subjects had a high self-reported familiarity with Carmen (6.04 ± 1.28 pts on a 7-point Likert scale with 7 being high familiarity). Oculometric instruments were calibrated to ensure that gaze fixations on both the stage and the surtitle banner above the stage would be captured. Equipment limitations necessitated the use of two different oculometric systems (i.e., Smart Eye and SMI) to satisfy the simultaneous five subject collection requirements. A 90-120 second period of eyes-open rest and fixation upon the surtitle banner prior to the opening of the curtain was used to standardize EEG activity. The recording was terminated at the performance intermission, whereupon subjects completed the CA scale. Note that auditory linguistic and musical stimuli were present throughout the opera both when surtitles were present and absent.

Fixation rates reveal text seeking and processing behaviors

Fixations were standardized as quasi-rates (hereafter fixation rate) and analyzed via RM ANOVA according to the within-subject two-level factors of surtitle presence (present vs. absent), the fixation zone (stage vs. surtitle banner), and, out of caution due to the two device types used, the between subjects two-level factor of device type (Smart Eye vs. SMI). There was a significant between-subjects effect of device type (F(1, 15) = 4.907, p = 0.043), with values from SMI devices having higher magnitudes and variance than those from Smart Eye devices (5.285 ± 0.078 vs. 5.059 ± 0.066, mean ± SE for SMI vs. Smart Eye, respectively). There was a significant effect of fixation zone (F(1, 15) = 55.735, p < 0.001), with higher fixation rates for the stage than the surtitle banner (7.993 ± 0.415 vs. 2.352 ± 0.344, mean ± SE). Simultaneously, there was a significant effect of surtitle presence (F(1, 15) = 19.98, p = 0.003), with higher overall fixation rates when surtitles were absent vs. present (5.723 ± 0.209 vs. 4.622 ± 0.108, mean ± SE), indicating that a lack of surtitles coincided with a comparatively higher rate of visual scanning.

There was also a significant interaction between fixation zone and surtitle presence (F(1, 15) = 139.334, p < 0.001). Simple main effects testing according to fixation zone revealed a significantly higher fixation rate after Benjamini-Hochberg False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction (q = 0.05, with corrected p values presented as pcorr) on the surtitle banner and a significantly lower fixation rate on the stage when surtitles were present vs. absent, respectively (2.886 ± 0.471 vs. 1.817 ± 0.278, pcorr = 0.008; 6.357 ± 0.332 vs. 9.629 ± .0554, pcorr < 0.001, respectively, mean ± SE). In other words, fixation rates were generally higher for the stage than the surtitle banner but decreased for the stage and increased for the surtitle banner when surtitles were present, a sign that the text was being read. Notably, the rate of surtitle banner fixations when surtitles were absent was not zero, indicating that subjects seemed to continually scan the surtitle banner in anticipation of the appearance of text. Horizontal displacement corroborates differing gaze behaviors for surtitle banner vs. stage

As a measure of gaze fixation spatial dynamics, the mean, absolute, horizontal displacement between consecutive fixations were calculated for each subject separately for surtitle banner and stage fixations when surtitles were present and absent. These values were then normalized for each subject separately as a ratio of the mean across the four conditions and then analyzed via RM ANOVA according to the within-subjects two-level factors of surtitle presence (present vs. absent) and fixation zone (stage vs. surtitle banner), and the between-subjects two-level factor of device type (Smart Eye vs. SMI).

There was a significant effect of surtitle presence (F(1, 15) = 7.406, p = 0.016), with narrower horizontal displacement, regardless of fixation zone, when surtitles were present vs. absent (0.240 ± 0.004 vs. 0.260 ± 0.004, mean ± SE) (see Fig. 3, right).There was also a significant effect of fixation zone (F(1, 15) = 164.210, p < 0.001), regardless of surtitle presence, with higher normalized horizontal displacement for the surtitle banner compared to the stage (0.331 ± 0.006 vs. 0.169 ± 0.006) (see Fig. 3, right). This means that, despite the similar if not wider spread of fixations for the stage area (see Fig. 2), consecutive fixations on the stage were comparatively more locally clustered, producing shorter stepwise displacements than those observed across the surtitle banner. Contextual differences in visual stimulus presentation likely drove these observed differences in horizontal displacement, with more targeted fixations used to track the protagonist on the stage and comparatively wider sweeping fixations used to scan for text information across the surtitle banner.

Meanwhile, there was no significant between-subjects effect of device type (F(1,15) = 0.000, p =1.00) as the overall means were coincidentally identical. There was also no significant interaction between device type and surtitle presence (F(1, 15) = 1.238, p = 0.283), nor between surtitle presence and fixation zone (F(1, 15) = 0.851, p = 0.371). Nor was there a three-way interaction between fixation zone, surtitle presence, and device type (F(1, 15) = 0.635, p = 0.438). However, there was a significant interaction between device type and fixation zone (F(1, 15) = 76.145, p < 0.001). Simple main effects tests revealed that this device-type interaction was driven by larger differences in magnitude of horizontal displacement between the surtitle banner and the stage fixation zones for the SMI devices compared to the Smart Eye devices (see supplementary Fig. S1, right). Nevertheless, the directionality of the main within-subjects effects was unaffected (see supplementary Fig. S1, right). Correspondingly, device type was omitted as a factor from downstream analyses with CA scores.

Cortical theta activities increased when surtitles were present

Raw EEG during Act I of the performance was marked according to whether surtitles were present or absent. Then, 163 four-second epochs from each of these periods were randomly selected from each subject and EEG signals time-frequency decomposed as normalized theta (5-7 Hz), alpha (8-12 Hz), and beta (15-29 Hz) activity envelope amplitudes across 15002 cortical voxels (see Fig. 4, Pipeline A).

EEG processing pipeline. Sections of the EEG recording were separated according to the presence or absence of surtitles, and then divided into 4 s epochs of cortical activity. 163 epochs from each condition were randomly selected post cleaning (* ~80% retention), and activity in 15002 vertices and 62 cortical areas based on the Mindboggle cortical atlas was decomposed into theta, alpha, and beta band frequency envelopes. Then, activity envelopes in each frequency band were averaged across epochs, averaged over time (0–4 s) and normalized as a percentage of mean baseline (BL) activity (A). Separately, phase locking value coherence was calculated across epochs for each brain area to all other areas, resulting in 1891 connections for testing for each frequency band for each condition (B). The resulting values from A and B processing were used in statistical comparisons between conditions.

Theta (5-7 Hz)

The theta-band contrast revealed widespread medial and right prefrontal/premotor regions where theta activity differences exceeded the height threshold, and in which theta was higher when surtitles were present vs. absent. These regions extended medially from medial occipital visual processing areas, through the corpus callosum, and into the anterior cingulate and medial frontal areas. There were three significant clusters. One centered on the right caudal middle frontal cortex (CMF), extending into the anterior edge of the precentral gyrus (MNI: 36, 5, 42). Incidentally, this location corresponds to hemodynamic imaging-derived locations of the FEF32,33, a cortical area responsible for top-down control of eye movement34. The remaining two clusters were situated bilaterally in the precuneus (PCu) (Left MNI: -14, -49, 35; Right MNI: 7, -59, 39). In all clusters, theta activity was higher when surtitles were present vs. absent. See Fig. 5 for a summary of theta activity results.

The effect of CA on fixation behavior and neurophysiology

After identifying oculometric and neurophysiological differences associated with surtitle presence, we sought to clarify whether any of these differences were influenced by CA. Therefore, using only the factors and variables which exhibited significant differences in the aforementioned results, RM analyses of covariance (ANCOVAs) were performed with CA score as a covariate. The effect of the CA covariate, and its interaction with other factors are reported. In the case of a significant interaction, the parameter estimates of individual variables exhibiting significant relationships with immersion are also reported. Additionally, due to the observation of a negative relationship between men and CA scores (see Methods for further details), post hoc bivariate correlation analyses were performed against age (across all subjects and sex separated) in cases of significant CA effects or interactions.

Text seeking and processing behaviors did not vary with CA

As for fixation rate, there was no overall covariance with CA (F(1, 14) = 3.399, p = 0.087), nor were there significant interactions between fixation zone (F(1, 14) = 0.957, p = 0.344) or surtitle presence (F(1, 14) = 2.537, p = 0.133). As for horizontal displacement of consecutive fixations, there was also no overall covariance with CA (F(1, 14) = 0.000, p = 1.000), nor were there significant interactions between fixation zone (F(1, 14) = 0.867, p = 0.368) or surtitle presence (F(1, 14) = 1.179, p = 0.296). Thus, the significant differences observed with oculometry were not significantly influenced by CA.

Surtitle associated theta increases in the fef did not vary with CA

Theta (5-7 Hz) activity envelope amplitudes in the identified significant clusters exhibited no significant overall covariance with CA (F(1, 18) = 2.950, p = 0.103), nor was there a significant interaction between CA and surtitle presence (F(1, 18) = 0.342, p = 0.566). However, there was a significant interaction between CA and cluster (F(2, 36) = 2.995, p = 0.063), and a significant three-way interaction between CA, cluster, and surtitle presence (F(2, 36) = 2.826, p = 0.072). Parameter estimates revealed that the covariance with CA was driven by negative relationships with theta activity in the PCu clusters, although these relationships failed to reach significance after Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction (q = 0.05) (left PCu: β = −1.153, pcorr = 0.057; right PCu: β = −1.153, pcorr = 0.059). Simply put, as CA decreased, theta activities in the bilateral PCu clusters increased. Notably, as the three-way interaction between CA, cluster, and surtitle presence appears to reveal, the slope of the negative relationship between CA and theta activity trended steeper when surtitles were absent compared to present in the bilateral PCu clusters (absent vs. present, left PCu: β = −1.223 vs. −1.083; right PCu: β = −0.979 vs. −0.894). In other words, the effect of increased theta activity when surtitles were present compared to absent tended to weaken bilaterally in the PCu as CA decreased. This may underscore the significant relationships observed between CA and beta connectivities in the PCu described in the next section. The covariance of CA with theta activity in the left PCu cluster is illustrated in Fig. 5 (bottom left). Meanwhile, the effect of increased theta activity in the right FEF when surtitles were present compared to absent did not vary with CA (overall: FEF: β = 0.042, pcorr = 0.945; surtitle present vs. absent: β = 0.007, pcorr = .990 vs. β = 0.077, pcorr = .902, respectively), highlighting FEF theta activity as a potential EEG target for distinguishing text over imagery processing in real-world environments or where inter-subject differences in CA would be difficult to control.

Cortex-specific sensitivity of occipital beta connectivities to CA

Beta PLVs for identified cortical connections exhibited an overall positive covariance with CA (F(1, 18) = 6.183, p = 0.023), and there was a significant interaction between CA and cortical connection (F(5, 90) = 1.936, p = 0.096). However, there was no significant interaction between CA and surtitle presence (F(1, 18) = 0.436, p = 0.517), nor three-way interaction between CA, surtitle presence, and cortical connection (F(5, 90) = 0.171, p = 0.973). Thus, the strength of covariance with CA differed according to cortical connection, but not according to surtitle presence. Moreover, parameter estimates revealed only two connections between the right PCu and the right and left Cu to have significantly positive relationships with CA (β = 0.003, pcorr = 0.027 and β = 0.004, pcorr = 0.012, respectively). In short, while there was an overall positive trend toward higher beta PLV with higher CA score across all connections, this effect was driven by the two aforementioned connections. Discussion

Characterizing how brain activities vary with text availability in a real-world environment, and whether these measures might be impacted by CA, are important yet unexplored topics in cognitive neuroscience. The present study investigated text vs. imagery gaze behaviors and neurophysiological processing during a live operatic stage performance in which surtitles appeared on a fixed location (i.e., surtitle banner) above the stage. Oculometric gaze fixation behavior, along with EEG cortical theta, alpha, and beta band power activities and connectivities were assessed according to the presence or absence of surtitles. Effects were further assessed for their relationship with CA, which was indexed via self-reported CA. Readers should note that, as is typical for opera, auditory linguistic and musical stimuli were present throughout the performance both when surtitles were present and absent.

Subjects had higher overall fixation rates when surtitles were absent compared to present, and continuously scanned the surtitle banner in anticipation of text appearance. However, when surtitles appeared, overall fixation rates dropped and became more focused on the surtitle banner. This shift was accompanied by a significant decrease in the magnitude of horizontal displacement of consecutive fixations, in line with prior findings that saccade amplitude (for which displacement between consecutive fixations can be considered a spatial proxy of) is smaller when processing text compared to free exploration35. Interestingly, this reduction in horizontal displacement between consecutive fixations was irrespective of the fixation zone, meaning that it occurred for fixations on the surtitle banner and the stage. Given that the opera surtitles appeared when the protagonists were singing, as is typical for operas, globally decreased horizontal displacement between consecutive stage fixations could have coincidentally been observed due to increased focus on the protagonists during song. However, the present findings could also indicate a shift toward a more targeted fixation strategy when an information rich visual stimulus of text is present in the overall visual field. Indeed, previous oculometric studies have observed general decreases in saccade amplitude in association with increased visual information density, such as high vs. low fidelity images36 or higher vs. lower object numbers in an image37. Importantly, these changes in fixation behavior did not vary with CA scores, corroborating the automatic allocation of attention to available text15–17 and suggesting the presence of potentially CA-independent brain activities or connectivities.

We hypothesized that text-related neurophysiological processing may be driven by either recruitment of brain areas critical for orthographic decoding of text or by the interplay between the visual and reward systems. Theta activity contrasts lent some credence to the latter idea, revealing higher theta activity when surtitles were present vs. absent broadly extending along the dorsal visual path and into the mPFC, a critical area of the reward system23–25. However, clusters where this trend was significant were located along the ventral visual path38, bilaterally in what could be associated with the PCu, and in a right CMF area which appears to have spatial correspondence with the FEF32,33, thereby implicating orthographic decoding as the more prominent differential factor of text vs. non-text visual information processing in the theta band.

Indeed, the PCu, a principal associative cortex and part of the default mode network, is well-known for its role in visuo-attentional processing39. In particular, recruitment of the PCu appears important for orienting shifts in spatial attention40,41, including between object features42,43, precisely the kind of cognitive processing one would expect in visual fixation and orthographic processing of text. Moreover, the PCu is known to be strongly interconnected with the prefrontal cortex, including the FEF39, an area which provides gaze-related motor commands to the eyes34 and is widely reported to play a crucial role in the orthographic processing of text5,18,19. Notably, right-lateralized recruitment of the FEF (i.e., ipsilateral to the dominant hand) has frequently been reported in association with attentional shifts as well as target detection40,44, and all subjects in the present study were right-handed. Additionally, it is worth reminding the reader that auditory linguistic and musical stimuli were present continuously in both conditions and therefore were not isolated by the experimental contrast. Thus, a lack of differential activity in canonical auditory regions associated with speech and music processing, including bilateral auditory cortex/Heschl’s gyrus and superior temporal cortex, was not surprising. Moreover, although dual-stream models identify a strongly left-lateralized dorsal pathway linking auditory speech representations with frontal articulatory networks, this pathway is most relevant to speech motor mapping and task dependent phonological processing rather than to the present contrast between periods with and without available visual text45–47.

As to theta oscillatory activity specifically, increased activity or synchronization is thought to support the encoding of sensory information and its transmission and downstream processing by other cognitive systems48–50. Increased posterior theta activity (5-7 Hz) for visual processing of words compared to pictures has previously been reported51. Additionally, disruption of theta activity over the precuneus with continuous theta burst stimulation at 6 Hz has been demonstrated to impair spatial orienting52, and transcranial magnetic stimulation at 6 Hz over the right FEF has been shown to impede visual search ability53 and reduce contralateral target identification response times54,55. This latter point in particular lends support for right FEF involvement in the present study, as French surtitles were always displayed from the upper left side of the surtitle banner. Altogether, we posit that the higher theta activity in areas corresponding to the left and right PCu and right FEF when surtitles were present vs. absent are consistent with the heightened orthographic processing demands for reading text compared to looking at live action imagery.

Interestingly, surtitle-associated increases in theta activity did not vary with CA scores in the right FEF, although they did in the PCu where they became masked by theta synchronization as CA scores decreased. Decreased CA can be interpreted as a sign of cognitive disengagement. Theta synchronization related to disengagement due to drowsiness, fatigue, and boredom has been widely reported56–59. It is thought to originate from fatigue-mediated changes in thalamic activity and is considered functionally distinct from task-directed processing60. The fact that surtitle-related theta activity increases in the FEF did not vary with CA scores suggests that brain processes drawing gaze to text information are automatic and insulated from fatigue-mediated changes. Conversely, the fact that PCu theta activity levels were correlated with CA scores functionally suggests that the visuospatial and associative processing of viewed information is dependent on CA and may well happen downstream of FEF driven fixation control. This makes intuitive sense, as scanning the visual field for potentially useful information is automatic, but further in-depth processing of that information only occurs if it is of interest.

Despite the apparent importance of the PCu and the FEF for distinguishing text vs. non-text processing in the present study, whatever cortical communication may have transpired along the known cortical pathways between them was not significantly different between surtitle present and absent conditions according to our PLV measures in any frequency band. Instead, text vs. non-text visual processing was differentiated by beta-band connectivity between the PCu (particularly the right PCu) and medial occipital visual processing areas of the PEC, home of the primary visual cortex61, and the extrastriate visual cortices of the Cu and LG62. In all cases, beta PLVs were higher when surtitles were present compared to absent (see Fig. 6). Past research has demonstrated the relevance of beta connectivity in linguistic processing63, with recent work in non-human primates further implicating beta connectivity in top-down feedback processing between cortical areas64,65. Continuing our interpretation of apparent PCu involvement as a sign of increased visuospatial processing demands for text over non-text visual information, it seems logical that the need for feedback processing from the PCu to visual processing areas would be greater when processing text compared to viewing imagery. Nevertheless, it is also possible that increased beta connectivities in these brain regions reflect audiovisual integration of surtitles and opera singing66,67. That said, beta PLVs exhibited general sensitivity to CA, significantly increasing with higher CA scores, regardless of surtitle presence. However, this relationship was predominantly driven by connectivity between the right PCu and the bilateral Cu. The Cu is a crucial hub linking the visual and memory systems, with rich connections not only with the PeC and PCu but also with areas related to the reward network such as the limbic system and the PFC68. Indeed, memory processing is thought to be mediated by reward signaling69, and activation in the Cu and PFC have been observed to underpin interest in visual information70. In short, while CA did not influence the extent to which subjects regarded surtitles, the extent to which CA influenced cognitive processing of surtitles appears to have been delineated at the interface between the visuospatial and the visual-memory systems. Notably, connectivity between the right PCu and left and right PEC, home of the primary visual cortex61, did not significantly vary with CA, suggesting that visuospatial feedback to the primary visual cortex was less related to engagement, further corroborating automatic text fixation behavior. The reason for the apparent right lateralization of the present connectivity relationships cannot be disentangled with the design of the present study, but we speculate it may somehow be associated with the observed right hemispheric involvement of the FEF and the aforementioned ideas of contralateral surtitle presentation and or the right-handedness of the subjects.

The findings of the present study offer new ideas for neuroimaging research on text processing in naturalistic environments, particularly in the field of Neuroergonomics, where researchers seek to interpret neurophysiological signals in the context of real-world stimuli. In the field of user research, for example, understanding the extent to which text is processed, and how its presentation moderates other psychological factors such as cognitive load, is very important but challenging to achieve when the interface is freely navigated and the text is intermixed with other multimodal stimuli such as video. In the emerging subfield of augmented reality (AR) research, where text can be superimposed over the real-world on the screen of AR glasses, a crucial area of research is to understand the extent to which text is processed and how its presence impacts other real-world interactions. In these contexts where visual stimuli are dynamic and not spatially fixed, interpreting visual processing based on oculometry alone is difficult. The use of EEG as a complementary or even alternative metric to oculometry is desirable, and a text processing index which is insensitive to CA would be valuable.

Finally, it should be mentioned that the present study offers a rare opportunity to explore the social aspect of the shared operatic experience due to the use of EEG hyperscanning. That is, the EEG recordings of the five subjects for each performance were all synchronized. It is known that people sharing the same experience will often exhibit similar neuropsychophysiological responses71. In the present study, we confirmed a strong correlation between CA scores and the night of performance based on linear regression (R = 0.823, p < 0.001; see supplementary Fig. S2). Due to concerns of multicollinearity, and the fact that CA was a much more granular index of psychological state, we did not incorporate the night of the performance as a predictor into our statistical models. However, the relationship between CA and the night of the performance is consistent with the possibility that shared neurophysiological signaling may exist and also suggests the possibility that factors such as performance quality or crowd size/energy affected CA scores. A separate investigation in this vein on the present dataset is ongoing.

There are several limitations that should be acknowledged in the present study. First, we could not control luminance, which can affect early visual processing72–74. However, post-hoc comparisons of relative luminance measures of the theatrical performance based on a high-definition webcam recording revealed no significant differences between time periods when surtitles were present compared to absent (see appendix). Moreover, differences in the luminance of viewed stimuli have typically been associated with changes in both theta and alpha spectral power75–77. However, alpha activity did not significantly differ due to surtitle presence/absence. Thus, even if there was an intrinsic difference in luminance between periods where surtitles were present and absent, it seems unlikely that this was a major contributor to the brain activity differences we observed. Second, the K-means clustering approach used, although beneficial for oculometric spatial analysis, does not permit insights into temporal gaze dynamics. More advanced classification methods such as Hidden Markov Models or Dynamic Time Warping account for the temporal structure of the data and may have enriched insight into text processing behavior in the present study. Third, EEG source estimation was based on only a 32-channel whole head montage, which is below the electrode density typically used for high-density EEG recordings. However, while this level of spatial localization accuracy is not acceptable for clinical purposes, evidence indicates that 32 channels are sufficiently accurate for source estimates based on broad cortical areas, such as those used in the present study78. Nevertheless, readers should use caution regarding the precise spatial location of the present findings. Finally, EEG and oculometry were impacted by noise and other technical challenges inherent to in-field data collection. Nevertheless, standard practices were followed, and the surviving data should be considered reliable.

In conclusion, the present study investigated the oculometric behavior and EEG neurocorrelates associated with periods of text availability in a real-world setting. Oculometry revealed that subjects anticipated the appearance of text. Neurophysiologically, text presence/absence was differentiated primarily by theta activities and beta connectivities in or between cortical areas associated with visuospatial processing such as the FEF and the PCu. CA appeared to be more strongly related to PCu-related measures than others, particularly connectivity with the visual-memory system via the Cu. Importantly, text presence-associated increases in theta activity in an area consistent with the FEF and beta connectivity between the PCu and areas associated with the primary visual cortex did not significantly vary with CA, suggesting underlying roles in automatic attention allocation to available text. Further studies are needed to verify our results and establish to what extent the present effects could potentially be used as targets to objectively quantize attention to available text in other real-world environments.

Methods

Subjects

The present study targeted 25 healthy, right-handed adult subjects (F: 14, M: 11) between 35 and 68 years old (mean ± SD: 48.4 ± 10.4 years). There was no significant difference in age between sex groups according to independent t test (p = 0.849). We recruited subjects and assessed their participation eligibility via an online questionnaire in French using the software Qualtrics (Provo, UT, USA). The link to the questionnaire was distributed through the Opéra de Montréal’s newsletter and social media network. Inclusion criteria were that the subjects had native or fluent French speaking and reading ability, moderate or greater appreciation of opera (based on a five-point scale: 1 = not at all, 2 = low, 3 = moderate, 4 = high, 5 = very high), and no previously diagnosed neurological disorder. Subjects were also rated their familiarity with the viewed opera (see Stimuli and Setup) according to a 7-point Likert scale, with 1 being low and 7 being high. Only one subject rated their familiarity with Carmen at 1. All the rest of the subjects rated their familiarity at 5 or above. The mean and standard deviation were 6.04 ± 1.28 pts. Hence, the subjects were predominantly opera afficionados and were well acquainted with Carmen. To ensure that subjects could clearly read the surtitles and see and enjoy the performance, they were allowed to wear prescription glasses. Informed consent was obtained from all subjects prior to participation in this study, which was approved by the ethics review committee of our institution and conformed to the 1964 Helsinki declaration and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards.

Stimuli and setup

The audiovisual stimulus for the present study was five live performances of Bizet’s Carmen, directed by Charles Binamé, and presented by the Opéra de Montréal on May 4, 7, 9, 11, and 13, 2019 in Wilfred Peltier Hall at the Place des Arts in Montréal QC, Canada. Note that Carmen is a French opera. Surtitles were present during the sung dialog and absent during the unsung dialog. Surtitles were projected during the performance simultaneously in both French (left side) and English (right side) on a surtitle banner 9.75 meters wide and 1.07 meters high positioned at a height of 8.34 meters above the stage floor approximately 32 meters from the subjects. The corresponding vertical visual angle of the surtitle banner was approximately 14.32 degrees relative to the stage floor positioned at the horizontal line of sight (approximately 0 degrees). The horizontal visual angle of the surtitle banner was approximately 1.90 degrees. The surtitles were projected as white 22-point Arial font on the unlit banner, which correspondingly appeared black (See Fig. 1). Subjects were not instructed to restrict their reading of the surtitles to a single language. Five subjects were recorded each night. The subjects sat in five adjacent seats at the rear of the performance hall. A rail was constructed for the installation of oculometric cameras such that they were at an appropriate height to capture the targeted visual field of the stage and surtitle banner, and equidistant from the subjects. Two SMI and three Smart Eye oculometric systems were used in conjunction with a 9-point calibration protocol with point spacing designed to coincide with the visual field.

The calibration protocol was standardized and designed to allow for rapid calibration with two different oculometric systems in a real-world environment. The Smart Eye Pro world model provides a way to capture gaze onto physical objects in the environment rather than onto a display. In our context, the real-world object of interest was a theatre stage. Because it was not realistic to install a physical screen as large as the stage and perform a full screen-based calibration immediately before an opera performance, the world model was especially useful. For the SMI RED250mobile, the logic is different because it does not offer a 3D world-model approach like Smart Eye Pro. The iViewRED software allows defining a screen width, height, and relative position of the oculometric camera. Therefore, for both the Smart Eye and SMI occulometric systems, we defined a screen, with a size of 1.21 m wide by 0.68 m high, which was positioned 0.11 m above the cameras. Both the Smart Eye Pro and iViewRED software allow a 9-point calibration in which these 9 points are evenly placed in a 3 × 3 grid spanning the full physical screen area. We built a mock-up calibration screen out of cardboard by placing the 9 calibration points spaced 0.3 m horizontally and 0.17 m vertically. The calibration screen and oculometric cameras were placed at 0.69 m from the subject, in accordance with both the Smart Eye and SMI guidelines. The angle of the oculometric cameras to subjects’ head was adapted to subjects’ height. We performed multiple pretest calibrations to ensure accurate calibration across both oculometric systems and subjects of varying heights. For the experimental calibration, we positioned the calibration screen such that its boundaries aligned with the bottom-left, bottom-right, top-left, and top-right corners of the visual field, including the stage and surtitle banner. Figure 1 shows the positioning of one subject in the performance hall, the oculometric equipment, and the visual field in conjunction with the calibration screen.

Procedures

Subjects were greeted by researchers at the entrance of the performance hall 90 minutes before the curtain opening and guided to their seats in the performance hall. There, written informed consent was obtained, and the head size of the subjects was measured for EEG cap preparation. Then, subjects underwent oculometry calibration procedures. Approximately 65 minutes before the curtain opening, subjects were guided to a backstage room in the performance hall for EEG installation. Note that the performance hall was closed to the general public until approximately 30 minutes from the curtain opening.

One by one, the subjects were fitted with their appropriately sized EEG cap (EASYCAP, BrainProducts GmbH, Munich, Germany) pre-installed with a 32-channel electrode bundle (actiCAP, Brain Products GmbH, Munich, Germany) according to 32-channel Standard Cap layout for actiCAP. Electrode gel was applied, and impedance checked in accordance with the manufacturer. Then, the 3D positions of fiducials and electrodes were digitized using CapTrak (BrainProducts GmbH, Munich, Germany) for use with source estimation.

Approximately 15 minutes before the curtain opening, subjects were guided back out to their seats in the performance hall. Their EEG bundles were connected to their corresponding amplifiers and the recording software (BrainVision Recorder, BrainProducts GmbH, Munich, Germany) and final impedance checks were made. We initialized EEG recording at 5 minutes before the curtain opening, followed immediately by the initialization of Observer/MediaRecorder (Noldus, Leesburg, VA, United States). This triggered a TTL pulse to be sent to the EEG recording software and commenced recording of video of the stage via a HD webcam (640*380 resolution at 30 fps) and an audio recording (2.0 stereo at 44.1 kHz) of the room via a BlueYeti (Blue Microphones, California, USA) microphone. Then, one-by-one, the oculometric recordings for each of the five systems were synchronized with manually synchronous trigger input into Observer/Media recorder. We estimate the precision of this manual procedure at within 250 ms, which was deemed negligible for the long surtitle events, which averaged approximately 3900 ms in length.

Finally, subjects were reminded to keep their heads, trunks, and extremities as still as possible during the performance, and just use their eyes to scan the visual field. Then, for the two minutes preceding curtain opening, subjects were instructed to fixate the center of the surtitle banner for use in baseline brain activity calculations. Then the curtain opened, and the performance began.

The recording of the present study targeted only Act I of the performance. However, intermission did not commence until the end of Act II. Upon the commencement of intermission, the recordings were stopped, the EEG bundles disconnected from the amplifiers, and the subjects guided back to the EEG preparation room. There, the EEG equipment was uninstalled and subjects were asked to complete the CA questionnaire quietly in an isolated part of the room without interacting with other subjects. Once the CA questionnaire was completed, subjects were permitted to return to their seats and enjoy the rest of the performance.

Surtitle event identification

Surtitle onset and offset timing were determined for each performance separately based on frame by frame manual visualization of the HD webcam recordings mentioned in the previous section. Because the surtitles were displayed manually to align with the opera singing, the proportion of time during which surtitles were present vs. absent varied slightly across performance nights. From the first to the fifth night, surtitles were present for 67.35%, 65.19%, 64.68%, 64.90%, and 64.58% of the performance, respectively, yielding an average presence rate of 65.34% across nights. Frames were converted to a time in milliseconds. Then, these times were synchronized to the EEG and oculometric recordings by adjusting the time according to the TTL markers sent from Observer with respect to the onset of the video recording. The first time period targeted was a surtitles-absent time period, which commenced immediately after the curtain was raised.

Cognitive absorption measurement and processing

CA was assessed using the CA scale10. It measures CA via five cognitive dimensions of temporal dissociation, focused immersion, enjoyment, control, and curiosity, using 10 items, each on a 10-point Likert scale. The questions were modified to match the context of the present experiment. See supplemental tables, S1 and S2 for the details of the questions and responses, respectively, from all subjects. The score on each question was summed to produce a total CA score for each subject, which was used in statistical analyses. Overall, 24 subjects completed the CA assessment (one subject did not fully complete the questionnaire, Cronbach’s Alpha = 0.685, mean = 74.88, SD = 9.73). There was no significant difference in CA score between sex groups (independent t test, p = 0.787) and no correlation between CA score and age for either the full sample of 24 subjects or the 16 subjects included in the EEG analyses (Pearson’s correlation R = −115, p = 0.593 (N = 24); R = −.476, p = 0.062 (N = 16)).

Oculometry recording and processing

Raw oculometric data were recorded at a sampling rate of 60 Hz and 120 Hz for the SMI RED250mobile (herein: SMI) and Smart Eye Pro (herein: Smart Eye) systems, respectively, via the SMI iViewRED and Smart Eye Pro 5.0 software. The software packages used by these oculometric systems in the present study output the raw gaze data as either fixations (SmartEye) or points of regard (SMI). Processing of the raw oculometric data was performed in MATLAB R2022b (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA). As mentioned above, the onset of the oculometric recording for each of the five oculometers was marked in Observer, thereby permitting calculation of the onset and offset timing of surtitle events. Based on this, the oculometric time-series data from each subject was amended with markers for time periods when surtitles were present and absent. Then, the data was visualized as a scatter plot of all the x and y fixations (Smart Eye) and points of regard (SMI; hereafter fixations) coordinate positions from the start through the end of Act I of the performance. Based on these visualizations, 17 (Smart Eye: 10, SMI: 7) of the 25 subjects exhibited clearly distinct fixation populations with Y-axis separation indicative of their correspondence with the stage and surtitle banner, and were thus selected for further analysis. Of these 17 subjects, only 1 was not represented in the EEG sample (see EEG Recording and Processing section below), for a data correspondence rate of > 94%.

Establishing a fixed Y-axis threshold for vertical differentiation of fixation populations across all subjects was not possible in the present study because each subject sat in front of a different oculometer at slightly different angles to the stage and with the subjects’ heads at different vertical angles to the oculometric cameras. Objective separation of the individual fixation data points in each population was achieved using K-means clustering with overfitting. K-means clustering is a basic machine learning method for partitioning an x-by-y data matrix into distinct population clusters. There is precedent for using it in oculometry studies79. Overfitting was used to ensure that the resulting clusters would be sufficient in number not to comprise fixations across both the visually distinct populations. The number of centers required to achieve this differed across subjects according to their unique distribution of fixations. K-means clusters associated with the upper Y-axis population of fixations were designated as surtitle banner fixations. K-means clusters associated with the lower Y-axis fixation population were designated as stage fixations (see Fig. 2). K-means clusters not associated with any distinct population of fixations were excluded from further analysis.

Based on the K-means clustering population designations and the markers for surtitle onset and offset, four arrays of raw oculometric data were extracted according to their association with the surtitle banner and the stage when surtitles were present and absent. Thus, there were four fixation count values for each subject associated with the following four conditions: (1) surtitle banner, surtitle present; (2) surtitle banner, surtitle absent; (3) stage, surtitle present; and (4) stage, surtitle absent. Using the arrays from these four conditions, two oculometrics were calculated: fixation rate, and horizontal displacement of consecutive fixations.

Although we refer to data from both systems as fixations, the manner in which they are represented in the raw data is not the same, as mentioned above (i.e., fixations for Smart Eye and points of regard for SMI). Consequently, the raw fixation counts for the four conditions above varied not only across subjects, but also between oculometric systems, with higher fixation counts derived from SMI-based data (as can be discerned in Fig. 2). Therefore, fixation counts for each condition in each subject were standardized as a percentage of the total number of fixations during Act I according to the following equation, , where f is the fixation count for any given condition and Totf is the total fixation count recorded during Act I.

Finally, to permit statistical comparison of fixation percentages, they needed to be further corrected according to the amount of time surtitles were present/absent during Act I, which was not the same across performances. Therefore, the percentage of time surtitles were present and absent in each performance was calculated and divided by a factor of 10. The resulting correction factors for surtitle present and absent time periods for each of the five performances were: Present, Absent; (1) 6.74, 3.26; (2) 6.52, 3.48; (3) 6.47, 3.53; (4) 6.49, 3.51; (5) 6.46, 3.54. Fixation percentages for surtitle present and absent conditions were then divided by their corresponding correction factor for their associated performance to produce an adjusted fixation rate for each subject for each condition. These values were then used in the statistical analysis of gaze fixation behavior.

Meanwhile, to further characterize the spatial dynamics of fixation behavior, the horizontal displacement between consecutive fixations in the aforementioned arrays for each condition was calculated. Fixation displacement is an established oculometric of spatial gaze variability and can be interpreted as a proxy for the intervening saccade, albeit without its angular and temporal components80. This was achieved by taking the absolute value of the difference between the x-axis coordinates of consecutive fixations in the arrays for the four conditions and then calculating the mean of the resulting values. The resulting values for each condition were normalized within subjects as a ratio of the sum across all conditions for that given subject, (example for condition 1, where N is normalized horizontal displacement (HD) and the numerals represent the four conditions). Although not representative of temporal dynamics, this approach characterizes spatial dynamics while remaining complementary to the K-means approach employed. Horizontal displacement was chosen over vertical displacement, as gaze motion was dominant in the horizontal axis for both the surtitle banner and stage fixation populations (see Fig. 2). As a supplemental quality-control step, we plotted the distributions of x-axis gaze coordinates for both oculometric systems. Histograms were generated using pooled valid observations from each system with bin widths of 0.025 x-coordinate units. Histograms were plotted as probability densities rather than raw counts, with each bin height calculated as the number of observations in the bin divided by the product of the total number of observations and the bin width. Thus, the area under each histogram summed to 1, allowing comparison across oculometric systems with different numbers of valid samples (see Supplementary Fig. S4).

EEG recording and processing

EEG was recorded raw using the actiCAP system (BrainProducts GmbH, Munich, Germany), which has a designated ground and reference electrode, at a sampling rate of 500 Hz using BrainVision Recorder (BrainProducts GmbH, Munich, Germany). All EEG data processing was performed with Brainstorm81 running on MATLAB R2022b (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA). The EEG data from five subjects were excluded: four due to excessive physiological noise and one due to technical problems with digitization of electrode positions (explained further below in the paragraph regarding artifact rejection). Thus, EEG analyses comprise data from 20 subjects. Of these 20 subjects, 16 were represented in the oculometric data sample, resulting in a data correspondence rate of 80%.

EEG processing proceeded as follows. First, noisy or dead channels were removed based on visual inspection and power spectrum density analysis across the entire recording. Dead channels were defined as those with near zero signals and flatline power spectrum density curves. Noisy channels were those whose signals were consistently above 120 microvolts for the entirety of the recording. Identification of noisy channels was further confirmed via observation of markedly deviant and high power spectrum density curves compared to the rest of the channels. Then, a signal space projector (SSP) for the removal of physiological and recurrent noise was calculated by performing independent component analysis (ICA) using the Infomax method in the Brainstorm software at a target frequency band of 1-40 Hz (the same used in band-pass filtration below). Artifacts were identified based on both their topographical and waveform patterns. On average, one to two components were removed from each recording, the most common being blink artifacts. Event markers were programmatically placed using Matlab scripts at four second intervals from the start of the baseline period and throughout Act I of the performance according to the start of each period where surtitles were present or absent. Again, the onset of each surtitle present or absent period was identified and synchronized to the EEG recording according to the procedure described above in the Surtitle Event Identification section. Once markers were placed, data were band-pass filtered from 1-40 Hz and the SSP calculated from the ICA was applied.

Electrode positions and fiducials were imported from the subject’s corresponding CapTrak file and co-registered to a template brain (ICBM152). An EEG-appropriate head model was computed based on OpenMEEG BEM forward modeling, which compensates for tissue conductance. A noise covariance matrix was computed based on 90s of noise-free data during the baseline period. Minimum-norm estimation was used to calculate cortical currents without dipole orientation constraints (as per Brainstorm software) at 15,002 vertices across the cortical surface of the template brain. The cleaned and filtered cortical activity time-series was then epoched at -1 to 5 s relative to each marker in accordance with event type (surtitle present/absent). Epoch duration was decided according to our previous studies using similar EEG analysis strategies targeting the theta band82. This epoch length was sufficient for multiple cycles of theta activity but also short enough to ensure that noisy epochs could be rejected during visual inspection without significant loss of data. The number of potential epochs for surtitle present and absent conditions differed slightly with each performance, respectively ranging from 398 to 410 and 188–207. All epochs were visually inspected, and those with residual artifacts were removed. The decision to remove an epoch was made when more than one channel exceeded 120 microvolts. Three subjects had extremely high epoch rejection rates at over 80%, and two other subjects had rejection rates of 50% and 30%. Out of concern that all surtitle present and absent time periods would not be equally represented, these five subjects were excluded from further analysis. Of the remaining 20 subjects, the minimum number of clean epochs was 163, equating to approximately 80% ± 3% retention depending on the performance. For subjects with more than 163 clean epochs in either the surtitle present or absent conditions, clean epochs were randomly selected for removal such that 163 clean epochs remained for both surtitle present and absent conditions for all 20 of the retained subjects, with representation across all the surtitle present and absent periods, thereby ensuring equal weighting of averages. 90s of noise-free brain activity during the baseline period was also marked at four-second intervals and epoched −1 to 5 s relative to each marker and used for computing baseline brain activity.

The time-series of cortical currents at each voxel were decomposed for each epoch into the theta (5-7 Hz), alpha (8-12 Hz) and beta (15-29 Hz) frequency bands, and their corresponding envelopes computed using Hilbert transform. Time-frequency envelopes in each frequency band were averaged within subjects across all targeted epochs for surtitle present and absent, and baseline conditions separately for each of the 15,002 vertices. The resulting mean time-frequency cortical activity envelopes for surtitle present and absent, and baseline events were averaged over time from 0-4s in each subject and used as event-related brain activity. Event-related cortical activity in surtitle present and absent conditions was standardized across subjects as a percent deviation from baseline using the following equation, , where x is the mean activity of the condition of interest, and μ is the mean activity during the baseline period. The resulting normalized cortical activity maps for the surtitle present and absent conditions were exported as GIFTI files for use in statistical analyses. A diagram summarizing the EEG processing pipeline used to derive these values is shown in Fig. 4, path A.

Meanwhile, non-time-averaged time-frequency decomposed cortical surface activity in each epoch was parcellated into 62 areas based on the Mindboggle cortical surface atlas provided with Brainstorm. Using the mean cortical time-series envelopes of each brain area, non-time-resolved phase locking values (PLV) from all 62 areas to all other areas (1891 non-same connections) were calculated for each epoch in each frequency band (using Hilbert transform-based time-frequency decomposition) separately for each subject. The PLV method corresponds to that published by Tass et al., 199883, and is included in Brainstorm software. The resulting 1891 PLVs were averaged across epochs in each frequency band separately for surtitle present and absent conditions separately for each subject. These values were then used in subsequent statistical analyses of connectivity (see Fig. 4, Path B).

Statistical analyses

The effect of surtitle presence on gaze fixation behavior was assessed via two-way repeated measures analyses of variance (RM ANOVA) of fixation rates and horizontal displacement of consecutive fixations according to the main within-subjects factors of fixation zone (stage vs. surtitle banner) and surtitle presence (present vs. absent). Additionally, out of caution toward the fact that two different oculometric systems were used, a between-subjects factor of device type (SMI vs. Smart Eye) was also incorporated. In the case of a significant interaction between main factors, significant simple main effects are reported. Significant simple main effects measures were furthermore assessed for their relationship with CA via RM ANCOVA.

To identify cortical areas associated with real-world processing of text , paired t tests were performed separately for the theta, alpha, and beta frequency bands in SPM12 (v7771, run on MATLAB R2022b) by constructing a paired t model for normalized cortical activities in both conditions (surtitle present vs. surtitle absent), and then estimating a classical inference model. Then, F contrasts were mapped for each frequency band separately using a height threshold of p = 0.01 (uncorrected), with significant clusters determined based on a cluster-defining threshold of p < 0.005, followed by a cluster-wise family-wise error (FWE) corrected threshold of p < 0.05, with a minimum cluster extent of 50 voxels. In the case of significant clusters, brain activity in each cluster for each condition was extracted to assess its relationship to self-reported levels of CA using a RM ANCOVA [Cluster x Surtitle Presence/Absence WITH Cognitive Absorption]. In the case of a significant interaction between Cluster and CA, simple main effects were assessed using parameter estimates.

Additionally, brain area connections associated with text processing were identified via paired t tests comparing PLV values for surtitle present and surtitle absent conditions for all 1937 brain area connections separately for the theta, alpha, and beta frequency bands. The resulting p values from these paired t tests were corrected for multiple comparisons separately for each frequency band using Benjamini & Hochberg’s FDR correction at a rate of q = 0.01. Connections with significantly different PLVs between surtitle present and absent conditions following FDR correction are reported along with their FDR corrected p values. Paired t tests and FDR correction (fdr_bh, Version 2.3.0.0 by David Groppe) were performed respectively using native and openly available scripts in MATLAB R2022b. Significant connections were assessed for their relationship to CA using a RM ANCOVA [Connection x Surtitle Presence/Absence WITH Cognitive Absorption]. In the case of a significant interaction between cluster and CA, simple main effects were assessed using parameter estimates.

All RM ANOVAs, and RM ANCOVAs were performed using SPSS 22 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). The significance threshold for interactions was set at p ≤ 0.1, and that for main effects and simple main effects p ≤ 0.05. Simple main effects tests were furthermore corrected for multiple comparisons using Benjamini-Hochberg-based FDR (q = 0.05). Note that the FDR for PLV paired t tests was purposely set to be stricter than that used for simple main effects tests to constrain the number of significant results and reduce the complexity of downstream RM ANCOVA models. Note, as no significant relationship was observed between age and sex, and the correlation between CA and age was not significant, these variables were not included as covariates in the main analyses. To ensure that this decision did not mask potential effects, post hoc independent-samples t tests comparing by sex groups, as well as Pearson correlations with age, were conducted for all measures that showed significant effects in the main analyses. No significant results were observed in these tests.
