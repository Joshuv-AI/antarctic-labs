Blow-up suppression for the nematic liquid crystal flow via Couette flow on ℝ 2 \mathbb{R}^{2}
Yubo Chen, Wendong Wang, Juncheng Wei, Guoxu Yang

Abstract
. As is well known, for the harmonic heat flow or liquid crystal flow in two-dimension, the solution may blow up when the initial energy is greater than 8 ​ π 8\pi . Motivated by Lai–Lin–Wang–Wei–Zhou (CPAM, 2022), where singular solutions were constructed in the presence of small-scale velocity fields, it is natural to ask whether large-scale velocities may play a stabilizing role, preventing the concentration of blow-up. Here we show that the blow-up phenomenon can be suppressed by a Couette flow whose amplitude is large enough under a weak assumption on the anisotropic norm of the initial data. In particular, we construct examples with initial energy exceeding 8 ​ π 8\pi that satisfy our assumptions.



Keywords:
blow-up suppression; nematic liquid crystal flow; stability; Couette flow

1.
Introduction

Liquid crystals represent a state of matter exhibiting properties intermediate between those of conventional liquids and solid crystals. Among the various mesophases, the nematic phase is the most prominent, characterized by rod-like molecules that possess long-range orientational order but lack positional order. The theoretical description of nematic liquid crystals is generally categorized into a hierarchy of three models: the microscopic Doi–Onsager kinetic theory
[
50
,
24
]
, the macroscopic Landau–de Gennes Q-tensor theory
[
21
]
, and the Ericksen–Leslie vector theory
[
25
,
40
]
. The first provides a molecular kinetic description, while the latter two offer macroscopic continuum descriptions.

In this paper, we
consider a simplified version of the Ericksen–Leslie system, which models the flow of nematic liquid crystals in the whole plane:

(1.1)

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}v+v\cdot\nabla v-\nu\Delta v+\nabla P&=-\lambda\nabla\cdot(\nabla n\odot\nabla n)&&\text{ in }\,\mathbb{R}^{2}\times(0,+\infty),\\
\nabla\cdot v&=0&&\text{ in }\,\mathbb{R}^{2}\times(0,+\infty),\\
\partial_{t}n+v\cdot\nabla n&=\gamma\left(\Delta n+|\nabla n|^{2}n\right)&&\text{ in }\,\mathbb{R}^{2}\times(0,+\infty),\end{aligned}\right.

where
v(x,y,t):\mathbb{R}^{2}\times(0,+\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
represents the velocity field of the flow,
n(x,y,t):\mathbb{R}^{2}\times(0,+\infty)\rightarrow S^{2}
, the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, is a unit-vector field that represents the macroscopic molecular orientation of the liquid crystal material and
P(x,y,t):\mathbb{R}^{2}\times(0,+\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
represents the pressure function. The constants
\nu,\lambda
, and
\gamma
are positive constants that represent viscosity, the competition between kinetic energy and potential energy, and microscopic elastic relaxation time for the molecular orientation field.
\nabla n\odot\nabla n
denotes the
2\times 2
matrix whose
(i,j)
-th entry is given by
\nabla_{i}n\cdot\nabla_{j}n
for
1\leq i,j\leq 2
.

The system described above is a simplified version of the Ericksen–Leslie model,
originally developed between 1958 and 1968
[
25
,
40
]
. In the static case, this model reduces to the Oseen–Frank model. It provides a macroscopic continuum description of the material’s evolution, governed by the interaction between the fluid velocity field
v(x,y,t)
and the macroscopic director field
n(x,y,t)
, which represents the microscopic orientation of rod-like liquid crystals. Structurally, system (
1.1
) represents a coupling between the non-homogeneous Navier–Stokes equations and the transported flow of harmonic maps. The mathematical analysis of (
1.1
) was initiated by Lin–Liu
[
43
,
44
]
in a series of papers during the 1990s. In two dimensional space, global existence of weak solutions of (
1.1

[
45

and Hong in
[
28
]

[

and Xu–Zhang in
[

[

[

and Wang–Wang
[

independently, and later the uniqueness result was obtained by Wang–Wang–Zhang in
[

[

Motivated by
[

1.1
) with a class of large data of the velocity.
For simplicity, assume that
\nu=\lambda=\gamma=1
in system (
1.1
). It is easy to verify that
(\tilde{v},\tilde{n})=(U,e_{1})
with the Couette flow
U=(Ay,0)
is a stationary solution to the system (
1.1
), where
A
is the amplitude of the Couette flow. Denote the perturbation
u(t,x,y)=v(t,x,y)-U(y)
and
d(t,x,y)=n(t,x,y)-e_{1}

\left(u,d\right)
satisfies

(1.2)

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{t}u+Ay\partial_{x}u+\binom{Au^{2}}{0}+u\cdot\nabla u-\Delta u+\nabla p=-\nabla\cdot\left(\nabla d\odot\nabla d\right),\\
\nabla\cdot u=0,\\
\partial_{t}d+Ay\partial_{x}d+u\cdot\nabla d-\Delta d=|\nabla d|^{2}(d+e_{1}),\\
\left.u\right|_{t=0}=u_{\text{in}},\quad\left.d\right|_{t=0}=d_{\text{in}}.\end{array}\right.

Direct computations imply

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot(\nabla d\odot\nabla d)=\nabla\frac{|\nabla d|^{2}}{2}+\binom{\partial_{x}d\cdot\Delta d}{\partial_{y}d\cdot\Delta d}.

Define

\omega=\partial_{y}u^{1}-\partial_{x}u^{2},\quad u=\nabla^{\perp}\Phi=\left(\partial_{y}\Phi,-\partial_{x}\Phi\right)^{T},

then

\Delta\Phi=\omega

and
\omega

\partial_{t}\omega+Ay\partial_{x}\omega-\Delta\omega+u\cdot\nabla\omega=-\partial_{y}\left(\partial_{x}d\cdot\Delta d\right)+\partial_{x}\left(\partial_{y}d\cdot\Delta d\right).

After the time rescaling

(1.3)

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{t}\omega+y\partial_{x}\omega-\frac{1}{A}\Delta\omega=-\frac{1}{A}\left[u\cdot\nabla\omega-\partial_{x}\left(\partial_{y}d\cdot\Delta d\right)+\partial_{y}\left(\partial_{x}d\cdot\Delta d\right)\right],\\
u=\nabla^{\perp}\Delta^{-1}\omega,\\
\partial_{t}d+y\partial_{x}d-\frac{1}{A}\Delta d=-\frac{1}{A}\left[u\cdot\nabla d-|\nabla d|^{2}(d+e_{1})\right].\end{array}\right.

1.1.
Main result

To state the main theorem, we first introduce some notations. Denote
D_{x}:=-i\partial_{x}

(1.4)

\displaystyle\|f\|_{Y_{m,\epsilon}}:=\left\|\langle D_{x}\rangle^{m}\langle\frac{1}{D_{x}}\rangle^{\epsilon}f\right\|_{L^{2}},

and the space-time norm

\displaystyle\|f\|_{X_{a,m,\epsilon}}^{2}

\displaystyle:=\left\|e^{aA^{-\frac{1}{3}}\left|D_{x}\right|^{\frac{2}{3}}t}\langle D_{x}\rangle^{m}\langle\frac{1}{D_{x}}\rangle^{\epsilon}f\right\|_{L^{\infty}L^{2}}^{2}+\frac{1}{A}\left\|e^{aA^{-\frac{1}{3}}\left|D_{x}\right|^{\frac{2}{3}}t}\langle D_{x}\rangle^{m}\langle\frac{1}{D_{x}}\rangle^{\epsilon}\nabla f\right\|_{L^{2}L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{A^{\frac{1}{3}}}\left\|e^{aA^{-\frac{1}{3}}\left|D_{x}\right|^{\frac{2}{3}}t}\langle D_{x}\rangle^{m}\langle\frac{1}{D_{x}}\rangle^{\epsilon}\left|D_{x}\right|^{\frac{1}{3}}f\right\|_{L^{2}L^{2}}^{2}+\left\|e^{aA^{-\frac{1}{3}}\left|D_{x}\right|^{\frac{2}{3}}t}\langle D_{x}\rangle^{m}\langle\frac{1}{D_{x}}\rangle^{\epsilon}\partial_{x}\nabla\Delta^{-1}f\right\|_{L^{2}L^{2}}^{2}.

.

When the system (

Remark 1.4

(Physical mechanism of blow-up suppression)
.

Intuitively, the mechanism by which the Couette flow suppresses the potential blow-up of the nematic liquid crystal director field can be understood as a competition between the
nonlinear focusing effect
and the
shear-induced mixing
.
In the absence of the background shear flow, the harmonic map heat flow may develop singularities in finite time due to energy concentration.
However, the large shear flow
U=(Ay,0)
acts as a transport mechanism that rapidly mixes the fluid and the director field. This shearing effect stretches the spatial scales of the director field in the streamwise direction (
x
-direction), effectively transferring energy from low frequencies to high frequencies (large
k
).
Since the dissipation term
-\Delta d
is significantly more efficient at high frequencies, this cascade of energy to smaller scales results in a much faster decay rate—known as
enhanced dissipation
—than mere diffusion would provide.
If the shear amplitude
A
is sufficiently large, this enhanced dissipation happens fast enough to disperse the energy concentration before a singularity can form.

Remark 1.5

(Motivation for the
|D_{x}|^{1/3}
index)
.

The choice of the fractional derivative index
1/3
in the norms (e.g.,
\||D_{x}|^{1/3}d\|
) is intrinsic to the enhanced dissipation properties of the Couette flow.
The multiplier method (see (
1.16
) below) allows us to recover a fractional dissipation term with a coefficient scaling as
A^{-1/3}
:

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}k\partial_{\xi}\mathcal{M}(k,\xi)|\hat{f}|^{2}d\xi dk\gtrsim\frac{1}{A^{1/3}}\||D_{x}|^{1/3}f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Thus, the
|D_{x}|^{1/3}
norm represents the maximal gain of regularity in the
x
-direction provided by the shear flow, which is crucial for closing the nonlinear energy estimates against the potential blow-up.

Remark 1.6

(The anisotropic norm)
.

Theorem
1.1
and Corollary
1.2
contain an anisotropic norm

\|f\|_{Y_{m,\epsilon}}=\left\|\langle D_{x}\rangle^{m}\langle\frac{1}{D_{x}}\rangle^{\epsilon}f\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})},\quad\frac{1}{3}<\epsilon<\frac{1}{2}<m,

whose weight function in Fourier variables is defined as

\Lambda_{m,\epsilon}(k)=(1+|k|^{2})^{m}(1+|k|^{-2})^{\epsilon},

which plays an important role in dealing with the velocity by Biot-Savart’s law. For example, see the term of

\displaystyle\left\|e^{aA^{-\frac{1}{3}}|l|^{\frac{2}{3}}t}|l|\left\|\Delta_{l}^{-1}\omega_{l}\right\|_{L_{y}^{\infty}}\right\|_{L_{l}^{1}}

in Lemma
4.4
, (
4.17
).

At high frequencies (
|k|\gg 1
),
\Lambda_{m,\epsilon}(k)\sim|k|^{2m}
, enforcing
m
-th order regularity in the
x
-direction, while at low frequencies (
|k|\ll 1
),
\Lambda_{m,\epsilon}(k)\sim|k|^{-2\epsilon}
, which penalizes concentration near
k=0
. Since
2\epsilon<1
, this singularity is integrable and does not exclude typical
L^{2}
functions. In contrast to the isotropic Sobolev norm with weight
(1+|k|^{2}+|\xi|^{2})^{s}
, the
Y_{m,\epsilon}
-norm is anisotropic and acts only in
x
. In particular,

\|f\|_{H_{x}^{s}L_{y}^{2}}\lesssim\|f\|_{Y_{m,\epsilon}},\qquad-\epsilon\leq s\leq m,

so
Y_{m,\epsilon}
controls a whole interval of
x
-regularities. Typical examples include
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
,
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, Gaussian profiles, functions
f=\partial_{x}g
with
g\in L^{2}
, and
L^{2}
functions with zero
x
-average, while nonzero constants are excluded.

Remark 1.7

(Examples of initial data with arbitrarily large energy)
.

Let
\phi,\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
be Schwartz functions satisfying:

•

\operatorname{supp}\widehat{\phi}\subseteq[1,2]

•

\|\phi\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}=\|\varphi\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}=1

Define the perturbation

\displaystyle d^{\lambda,N}_{\rm in}(x,y):=\lambda^{\theta}\phi(\lambda x)\varphi(y)\cos(Ny)e_{1}.

Note that the Fourier transform of

is supported in

•

\langle k\rangle\approx 1
.

•

.

Also, the

.

(i) Estimate of the Low-Order Term (
L
):
Consider the term

. The operator multiplier is

.

\displaystyle L(\lambda)

\displaystyle:=\left\||D_{x}|^{1/3}d_{\text{in}}\right\|_{Y_{m,\epsilon}}

\displaystyle\approx\lambda^{\theta}\cdot\underbrace{\lambda^{-\epsilon}}_{\text{weight}}\cdot\underbrace{\lambda^{1/3}}_{\text{derivative}}\cdot\underbrace{\lambda^{-1/2}}_{L^{2}\text{ mass}}\cdot\|\varphi\cos(Ny)\|_{L^{2}_{y}}

\displaystyle\leq C\lambda^{\theta-\epsilon-1/6}.

(Note:

).
To ensure

(ii) Estimate of the High-Order Term (

Consider

The Dirichlet energy is dominated by the

\displaystyle E(\lambda,N)

\displaystyle:=\|\nabla d_{\text{in}}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\geq\|\partial_{y}d_{\mathrm{in}}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

.

Parameter Selection and Verification.

According to Theorem

, a valid parameter

.

where

1.2.
Stability mechanisms

The above results show that the Couette flow plays an important role in the stability theory. In fact,
the stability of Couette flow governed by the Navier–Stokes equations has been a prominent topic in fluid mechanics since the pioneering works of Rayleigh
[
52
]
, Kelvin
[
33
]
, Orr
[
51
]
, and Sommerfeld
[
54
]
. Consider the vorticity formulation of the 2D linearized Navier–Stokes equations around the Couette flow:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{t}\omega+y\partial_{x}\omega-\nu\Delta\omega=0,\\
\left.\omega\right|_{t=0}=\omega_{\mathrm{in}}.\end{array}\right.

By Fourier transform, the Kelvin’s solution is

\hat{\omega}(t,k,\xi)=\hat{\omega}_{\mathrm{in}}(k,\xi+kt)e^{-\nu\int_{0}^{t}|k|^{2}+|\xi+k(t-s)|^{2}ds},

which satisfies the following two linear estimates:

(1.12)

\displaystyle|\hat{\omega}(t,k,\xi)|\leq C\left|\hat{\omega}_{\mathrm{in}}(k,\xi+kt)\right|e^{-c\nu^{\frac{1}{3}}|k|^{\frac{2}{3}}t},

(1.13)

\displaystyle|\hat{\phi_{0}}(t,k,\xi)|\leq C\langle t\rangle^{-2}\frac{1+|k|^{2}+|\xi+kt|^{2}}{|k|^{4}}\left|\hat{\omega}_{\mathrm{in}}(k,\xi+kt)\right|e^{-c\nu^{\frac{1}{3}}|k|^{\frac{2}{3}}t},

for some
c>0
, where
\phi_{0}=\Delta^{-1}\omega
is the stream function.

1.2.1.
Enhanced dissipation

Inequality (
1.12
) represents the enhanced dissipation estimate. Its associated time scale is
O\left(\nu^{-1/3}\right)
, which is significantly shorter than the standard heat dissipation time scale
O\left(\nu^{-1}\right)
for small
\nu
, implying a much faster decay. The phenomenon of enhanced dissipation has been widely observed and studied in the physics literature (see, e.g.,
[
55
,
53
,
8
,
38
]
).
Recently, it has garnered considerable attention on the stability of the 2D Couette flow for the Navier–Stokes equation. For example, if the perturbation for vorticity is in Gevrey-
s
with
s<2
, Bedrossian–Masmoudi–Vicol
[
5
]
showed that the solution is stable for
\gamma=0
on a periodic domain, and more developments can be found in
[
7
,
12
,
48
,
63
]
. When
\Omega=\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
, Arbon–Bedrossian
[
1
]
and Li–Liu–Zhao
[
42
]
obtained almost sharp stability threshold. For further references,
we refer the reader to
[
23
,
62
,
13
,

1.3.
The key idea in the proof

The proof of the main theorem relies on a bootstrap argument involving carefully constructed space-time norms. To close the energy estimates, particularly for the strong coupling between the Navier–Stokes equations and the director field equation, we introduce several technical innovations.

1.3.1.
Construction of the multipliers

The first key idea is the construction of time-dependent Fourier multipliers to capture the stabilizing effects of the Couette flow. Motivated by the works on the stability of shear flows
[
3
,
23
,
63
]
, we introduce two self-adjoint Fourier multipliers acting as “ghost weights” to provide additional dissipation properties.

For
k,\xi\in\mathbb{R}
and the shear amplitude
A>0
, we define:

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{1}(k,\xi)

\displaystyle:=\arctan\left(A^{-\frac{1}{3}}|k|^{-\frac{1}{3}}\operatorname{sgn}(k)\xi\right)+\frac{\pi}{2},

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{2}(k,\xi)

\displaystyle:=\arctan\left(\frac{\xi}{k}\right)+\frac{\pi}{2}.

The total multiplier is defined as
\mathcal{M}:=\mathcal{M}_{1}+\mathcal{M}_{2}+1
, satisfying the boundedness condition

(1.14)

\displaystyle 1\leq\mathcal{M}\leq 1+2\pi.

•

\mathcal{M}_{1}
is the
enhanced dissipation multiplier
. It captures the enhanced dissipation time scale
O(\nu^{-1/3})
, which is significantly shorter than the standard heat dissipation time scale. This is crucial for suppressing the blow-up of the director field.

•

\mathcal{M}_{2}
is the
inviscid damping multiplier
. It tracks the mixing of vorticity induced by the shear flow, analogous to Landau damping.

A crucial feature of these multipliers is their commutator with the transport operator
\partial_{t}+y\partial_{x}
:

(1.15)

2\Re\big(\left(\partial_{t}+y\partial_{x}\right)f\mid\mathcal{M}_{i}f\big)=\frac{d}{dt}\left\|\sqrt{\mathcal{M}_{i}}f\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}k\partial_{\xi}\mathcal{M}_{i}(k,\xi)|\hat{f}|^{2}dkd\xi,

for a sufficiently smooth function
f=f(t,x,y)
on
(0,+\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{2}
. As shown in
[
15
,
42
]
, we have the coercivity property:

(1.16)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}k\partial_{\xi}\mathcal{M}(k,\xi)|\hat{f}|^{2}dkd\xi\geq\frac{1}{4A^{\frac{1}{3}}}\left\||D_{x}|^{\frac{1}{3}}f\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}-\frac{1}{2A}\left\|\partial_{y}f\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\left\|\partial_{x}\nabla\Delta^{-1}f\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

This allows us to recover a fractional derivative term
\left\||D_{x}|^{1/3}f\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}
with a positive sign, which is essential for controlling the high-frequency interactions in the bootstrap argument.

1.3.2.
Frequency-based two-layer decomposition

To address the complex nonlinear terms, particularly the geometric constraint term
|\nabla d|^{2}d
in the director equation and the stress tensor
\nabla\cdot(\nabla d\odot\nabla d)
in the fluid equation, which involve convolutions in the frequency space that are difficult to estimate using standard paraproducts, we introduce the following frequency-based two-layer decomposition.
Motivated by
[
42
]
, for a fixed frequency
k
and a convolution variable
l
, we divide the integration domain into three distinct regions based on the relative sizes of frequencies:

•

Region I (Near-resonant interactions):

\mathcal{R}_{\text{res}}(k,l):=\mathbb{R}\times\{l:\frac{1}{2}|k-l|\leq|k|\leq 2|k-l|\}
. In this region, the interacting frequencies are comparable, i.e.,
|k|\sim|k-l|
.

•

Region II (High-Low interactions):

\mathcal{R}_{\text{HL}}(k,l):=\mathbb{R}\times\{l:|k|>2|k-l|\}
. Here, the output frequency
k
is much larger than the difference
k-l
, implying
|k|\sim|l|
.

•

Region III (Low-High interactions):

\mathcal{R}_{\text{LH}}(k,l):=\mathbb{R}\times\{l:2|k|<|k-l|\}
. Here, the output frequency is small compared to the inputs, implying
|k-l|\sim|l|
.

One new observation
is the following
frequency-based two-layer decomposition method
. Consider the form in Region I of like

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{R}_{\text{res}}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\cdots d\eta\right)dkdl

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{R}_{\text{res}}}\left(\int_{\{\eta:\,\frac{1}{2}|k-l-\eta|\leq|k-l|\leq 2|k-l-\eta|\}}\cdots d\eta\right)dkdl

(1.17)

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\mathcal{R}_{\text{res}}}\left(\int_{\{\eta:\,|k-l|>2|k-l-\eta|\}}\cdots d\eta\right)dkdl+\int_{\mathcal{R}_{\text{res}}}\left(\int_{\{\eta:\,2|k-l|<|k-l-\eta|\}}\cdots d\eta\right)dkdl

which allows us to balance the weights
\langle k\rangle^{m}\langle 1/k\rangle^{\epsilon}
and the derivative losses across different frequency modes effectively.
For example, in the proof of Lemma
3.2
,
using Fourier transform we obtain

\displaystyle\quad\left|\Re\left(|D_{x}|^{\frac{1}{3}}\left(|\partial_{x}d|^{2}d\right)\left\lvert\,\mathcal{M}e^{2aA^{-\frac{1}{3}}\left|D_{x}\right|^{\frac{2}{3}}t}\langle D_{x}\rangle^{2m}\langle\frac{1}{D_{x}}\rangle^{2\epsilon}|D_{x}|^{\frac{1}{3}}d\right.\right)\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}e^{2aA^{-\frac{1}{3}}|k|^{\frac{2}{3}}t}\langle k\rangle^{2m}\langle\frac{1}{k}\rangle^{2\epsilon}|k|^{\frac{2}{3}}

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\quad\times\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{M}\left(k,D_{y}\right)d_{k}(y)\cdot d_{l}(y)\left(k-l-\eta\right)\eta d_{k-l-\eta}(y)\cdot d_{\eta}(y)dy\right|dkdld\eta

By (
2.6
) and Corollary
2.6
(
\alpha=\beta=\frac{1}{3}
), the term on the right hand in
\mathcal{R}_{\text{res}}(k,l)
is controlled by

\displaystyle C\left\|e^{aA^{-\frac{1}{3}}|k|^{\frac{2}{3}}t}\langle k\rangle^{m}\langle\frac{1}{k}\rangle^{\epsilon}|k|^{\frac{2}{3}}\|d_{k}\|_{L_{y}^{2}}\right\|_{L_{k}^{2}}\left\|e^{aA^{-\frac{1}{3}}|l|^{\frac{2}{3}}t}\|d_{l}\|_{L_{y}^{\infty}}\right\|_{L_{l}^{1}}

\displaystyle\quad\times\left\|e^{aA^{-\frac{1}{3}}|k-l|^{\frac{2}{3}}t}\langle k-l\rangle^{m}\langle\frac{1}{k-l}\rangle^{\epsilon}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|k-l-\eta||\eta|\|d_{k-l-\eta}\|_{L_{y}^{2}}\|d_{\eta}\|_{L_{y}^{\infty}}d\eta\right\|_{L_{k-l}^{2}},

where the last term on the right side can be estimated using the idea from (
1.17
) again ( more details can be found in Lemma
3.4
). We remark that the method for handling the convolution of multiple functions could potentially be applied to many more general physical models, such as general Ericksen–Leslie systems with Leslies coefficients, the Landau–Lifshitz–Gilbert equation, and others.

1.3.3.
Energy transfer mechanism and blow-up suppression

Through the energy estimates derived in Sections
3
–
6
, we demonstrate that the enhanced dissipation effect, quantified by the term
A^{1/6}\left\||D_{x}|^{1/3}d\right\|
, dominates the nonlinear focusing effects when
A
is sufficiently large. Specifically, the strong shear flow mixes the director field
d
rapidly in the
x
-direction, transferring energy to high frequencies where dissipation is efficient. This mechanism prevents the concentration of energy required for the formation of singularities, thereby extending the local solution to a global one under the condition
A\gg 1
.

The final key idea is utilizing the large shear parameter
A
to suppress finite-time blow-up. The coupling between the fluid and the crystal induces a complex energy transfer mechanism, which we quantify through the following hierarchy of estimates:

•

Horizontal Regularity (Propositions
3.1
):

The proposition control the horizontal derivative
|D_{x}|^{1/3}d
. The enhanced dissipation provides strong decay factors with respect to
A
. Schematically we have:

\left\||D_{x}|^{1/3}d\right\|_{X}^{2}\lesssim\|\text{data}\|^{2}+A^{-1/2}\|\omega\|_{X}\left\||D_{x}|^{1/3}d\right\|_{X}+A^{-1/6}\|\text{nonlinear terms}\|,

where the factor
A^{-1/6}
is crucial for absorbing the nonlinear growth.

•

Mixed Regularity (Proposition
4.1
):

This proposition handles the mixed derivative
\nabla^{2}|D_{x}|^{1/3}d
. Due to the shear transport term
y\partial_{x}
, a derivative loss of order
A
typically occurs when estimating vertical derivatives. We obtain:

\left\|\partial_{y}|D_{x}|^{1/3}\nabla d\right\|_{X}^{2}\lesssim\|\text{data}\|^{2}+A\left\||D_{x}|^{1/3}d\right\|_{X}\left\|\partial_{y}|D_{x}|^{1/3}\nabla d\right\|_{X}+\dots,

where the second item from the right is
the most troublesome one
.
Although a factor of
A
appears, it is multiplied by the lower-order norm
\left\||D_{x}|^{1/3}d\right\|_{X}
, which is small (controlled by Proposition
3.1
). This allows us to close the estimate despite the shear-induced growth.

•

Fluid Vorticity (Proposition
5.1
):

The fluid vorticity
\omega
is driven by the Leslie stress
\nabla\cdot(\nabla d\odot\nabla d)
. The estimate takes the form:

\|\omega\|_{X}^{2}\lesssim\|\text{data}\|^{2}+A^{-1/2}\|\omega\|_{X}^{3}+A^{-1/3}\|\omega\|_{X}\left(\left\||D_{x}|^{1/3}\nabla d\right\|_{X}^{2}+\left\||D_{x}|^{1/3}\left(\partial_{x}^{2},\partial_{y}^{2}\right)d\right\|_{X}^{2}\right),

which shows that the feedback from the director field to the fluid is suppressed by
A^{-1/2}
or
A^{-1/3}
.

The system exhibits a cyclic dependency: the director field estimates rely on the smallness of the velocity field
\|\omega\|_{X}
, while the vorticity estimate (Prop.
5.1
) relies on the control of the director field stress
\left\|\nabla^{2}|D_{x}|^{1/3}d\right\|_{X}
.
By choosing
A
sufficiently large, the decay factors (
A^{-1/6},A^{-1/2}
) in the nonlinearities and coupling terms dominate the destabilizing factors (such as the linear growth
A
), thereby closing the bootstrap argument in Proposition
2.8
.

Lemma 4.2
