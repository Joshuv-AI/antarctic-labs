Compression-shear behavior of high-resistance backfill walls in gob-side entry retaining for thick seams
Authors: Lei Sun, Fan Qiao, ShuaiShuai Jiang, Chong Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To address the insufficient bearing capacity of roadside backfill bodies and the tilting or failure induced by uneven pressure relief of the coal seam during gob-side entry retaining in thick coal seams with hard roofs, combined compression–shear loading tests incorporating rapid resistance build-up and varying inclination angles were performed. A novel Compression Shear Coupling Test system (CSCT) was developed, and a fitted relationship between backfill width and roof subsidence was established. The strength degradation behavior of backfill specimens subjected to different shear stress components was systematically investigated. The results reveal that the peak strength of the specimens declines with increasing shear stress component, and the failure mode transitions progressively from compressive to shear-dominated failure. The high-resistance backfill material derived from this study was implemented at the N2302 gob-side entry retaining working face, accompanied by an anti-tilting design for the backfill wall. The measured roof subsidence was reduced by 59.4% relative to the theoretically predicted value, and no evident signs of failure or deterioration were observed in the backfill body. These findings provide both data support and theoretical reference for gob-side entry retaining under similar mining conditions.

Introduction

Gob-side entry retaining, as a pillar-less mining technique, can significantly improve the coal resource recovery rate, alleviate the imbalance between excavation and extraction, and enhance underground ventilation conditions. It has therefore received extensive attention and application in thick coal seam mining both domestically and internationally1–4. However, for gob-side entry retaining under a hard roof in a thick coal seam, the roof typically exhibits large thickness, high strength, wide hanging area, and large breaking interval, resulting in significant lateral abutment pressure on the goaf side and intense dynamic pressure effects5–9. As the key load-bearing structure of gob-side entry retaining, the roadside backfill body directly withstands the rotation, subsidence, and shearing action of the roof strata, and its mechanical performance and stability are the core factors determining the success or failure of the retained entry10–14.

Field practices indicate that under a hard roof in a thick coal seam, conventional backfill bodies commonly suffer from three prominent issues. First, the insufficient early-age load-bearing capacity of the backfill body makes it difficult to rapidly increase resistance so as to restrain the severe early roof subsidence. Second, the large mining height in thick coal seam extraction induces significant horizontal displacement and overturning moment among the roof, backfill body, and floor; together with the uneven compressive deformation of the coal seam itself, this easily causes overturning instability of the backfill body. Third, during the rotation of the key block formed after hard roof breakage, a non-negligible shear stress component is imposed on the backfill body. This shear stress, acting in combination with the vertical compressive stress, accelerates shear failure of the backfill body and seriously threatens the safety of the retained entry15–19. Therefore, revealing the damage evolution laws of the backfill body under combined compression-shear stress states, and developing backfill materials with both high early strength and good deformation adaptability, have become critical scientific issues to be urgently addressed.

To tackle these problems, numerous studies have been conducted by domestic and international scholars. Regarding optimization of backfill material proportioning, previous research has investigated the effects of water-to-cement ratio, early-strength agent dosage, and fiber content on the uniaxial or triaxial compressive performance of backfill materials, finding that appropriate addition of early-strength agents and fibers can effectively enhance the early strength and post-peak ductility of the backfill wall20–24. In terms of the mechanical response of backfill bodies, some researchers have analyzed their strength characteristics, deformation behavior, and failure modes through uniaxial, conventional triaxial, and cyclic loading–unloading tests25–28. However, most of these studies are based on pure compression or quasi-static loading conditions, failing to fully account for the realistic combined compression-shear stress path induced during the rotation and subsidence of a hard roof. In practice, the sidewall of the backfill body for gob-side entry retaining is commonly subjected to both the inclined shear force from the roof and the vertical compressive stress, and its stress state is closer to combined compression-shear loading29–31. To date, systematic experimental studies on the strength degradation law of backfill bodies under different shear stress components remain very limited, and dedicated testing equipment capable of applying controlled shear stress is lacking. Moreover, existing anti-overturning designs mostly focus on increasing the strength of the backfill body itself or widening the wall, whereas research on the overall stability control method of the backfill body under combined compression-shear loading is still incomplete.

In view of this, this study takes the N2302 working face of the Gucheng Coal Mine, which features a thick coal seam and a hard roof, as the engineering background. Systematic combined compression-shear loading tests are carried out on the roadside backfill wall material for gob-side entry retaining. First, a novel Compression Shear Coupling Test system (CSCT) loading system is developed to achieve stable loading under different preset shear stress components. Second, the backfill material proportion is optimized through orthogonal tests, obtaining material parameters that enable rapid resistance increase and high early strength. Then, combined compression-shear loading tests at different inclination angles (i.e., different shear stress components) are performed to reveal the degradation laws of shear stress on the peak strength, peak strain, and failure mode of the backfill body. Finally, the optimized high-resistance backfill body is applied in the field, with corresponding anti-overturning technical measures designed to verify its engineering applicability. The research findings aim to provide a theoretical basis and experimental data support for the design and stability control of backfill bodies for gob-side entry retaining under hard roofs in thick coal seams, as well as for engineering applications under similar conditions.

Project overview

At the N2302 working face, top-coal caving is conducted along the floor of the No. 3 coal seam. The working face exhibits a general topographic dip from south to north, with the No. 3 coal seam approximating a monoclinal structure. The average dip angle is 5°, and the burial depth ranges from 530 to 600 m. The total thickness of the coal seam varies between 3.35 and 9.37 m, averaging 6.35 m.

The immediate roof of the coal seam is mainly composed of sandy mudstone and mudstone, with local siltstone intercalations. Its thickness ranges from 0 to 7.45 m (average 4.03 m), characterized by poor thickness stability, a loose structure, water-softening behavior, and low strength. The immediate floor consists of sandy mudstone, mudstone, and locally siltstone, classified as soft rock, with a thickness varying from 0 to 2.45 m (average 0.70 m).

The N2302 working face has a length of 302 m. The belt entry of the working face is designed as a gob-side entry retaining, with a roadway width of 6 m and a height of 3.8 m. A 1.5-m-wide C30 concrete wall is adopted as the retaining structure for the gob-side entry. The layout of the working face is presented in Fig. 1.Fig. 1Layout of the working face.

Water‑cement ratio parameters

The water‑cement (w/c) ratio directly affects concrete strength. A high w/c ratio dilutes the colloidal hydration products of the cementitious material, preventing adequate filling of voids between concrete particles. Conversely, a low w/c ratio leads to incomplete hydration and agglomeration of the cementitious material, resulting in internal voids. Moreover, the concrete paste becomes overly thick, exhibits poor fluidity, and is difficult to mix, all of which significantly reduce concrete strength. To determine the optimal w/c ratio under the test conditions, six gradient levels (0.35, 0.40, 0.45, 0.50, 0.55, and 0.60) were tested in preliminary trials. These tests established a suitable w/c range to inform the w/c parameters for subsequent orthogonal experiments. The test mixes consisted of cement, sand, and water, stirred until homogeneous. Fiber

Basalt fiber (Fig. 4) was selected as the target fiber material. Basalt fiber is manufactured by drawing natural basalt rock at high temperatures. It offers advantages such as high production capacity, abundant raw materials, low cost, and environmental friendliness. In addition, basalt fiber exhibits excellent mechanical properties, including good wear resistance, oxidation resistance, compressive strength, and shear strength. Because the fiber is made from basalt, it has high rock affinity and can effectively enhance the crack resistance and fatigue resistance of concrete.Fig. 4Chopped Basalt Fiber.

Chopped Basalt Fiber.

Fiber content was set at three levels: 0%, 0.3%, and 0.6% by volume of the concrete block. The fiber density ranges from 2.65 to 3.0 g/cm3; a value of 2.8 g/cm3 was used to calculate the fiber mass for each content level. Long fibers tend to bend and agglomerate during mixing, reducing their toughening effect on concrete. Therefore, 6‑mm chopped fibers were used throughout the tests. These experiments investigated the effect of fiber content on concrete strength.

CSCT test apparatus

The CSCT system used in this study was developed based on the C-CAST system at the University of New South Wales32–34. A new pin connection structure was designed and incorporated into the adapters of the CSCT system to match the loading end of the MTS testing machine at China University of Mining and Technology, thereby eliminating the loosening and slippage issues associated with rope fixation in the original system. In addition, the cylindrical variable‑angle fixture was redesigned into a cubic version suitable for cubic specimens. The cubic specimen is fixed by pre‑set limiting grooves within the fixture. Both the upper and lower adapters consist of a base and a matching positioning dial; the two adapters are identical in structure and are arranged symmetrically during testing. Specimen preparation and testing procedure

Specimen preparation followed the Chinese standard for concrete specimen making and curing (GB/T50081‑2019). Specimens were prepared in two batches for 7‑d and 28‑d testing, respectively; the preparation process is shown in Fig. 9. During preparation, materials were weighed and mixed according to the experimental parameters. The molds were oiled to facilitate demolding. After concrete placement, the molds were vibrated on a vibrating table to compact the concrete. Vibration continued until the surface showed paste, and no large bubbles emerged, thereby reducing the influence of internal voids on test accuracy. After molding, the specimen surfaces were troweled to ensure flatness. The specimens were demolded 24 h later and immediately placed into a standard curing chamber at 20 °C ± 2 °C and a relative humidity > 95%. Finally, 7‑d and 28‑d concrete specimens were obtained.Fig. 9Diagram of Concrete Specimen Preparation Process.

Results and discussion

Analysis of uniaxial compressive strength and resistance-increasing characteristics

Macroscopic failure characteristics under uniaxial compression

Figure 11 illustrates the failure modes of the concrete specimens under uniaxial compression, while Fig. 12 presents the corresponding mechanical response curves. As observed, the unreinforced specimens exhibited severe crack propagation, extensive macroscopic spalling, and a high degree of fragmentation. In contrast, the specimens reinforced with 0.3% and 0.6% basalt fibers demonstrated restricted crack development, maintained robust overall structural integrity, and showed no significant block detachment.Fig. 11Uniaxial compression failure morphology of specimens.Fig. 12Uniaxial compressive stress–strain curves of specimens.

Uniaxial compression failure morphology of specimens.

Uniaxial compressive stress–strain curves of specimens.

Mechanistically, unreinforced specimens are susceptible to rapid crack coalescence and localized spalling after reaching peak strength, which is characteristic of a pronounced brittle failure mode. The incorporation of basalt fibers introduces a three-dimensional, randomly distributed bridging network within the cementitious matrix. During the initiation and propagation of microcracks, these fibers mitigate further crack extension via bridging, pull-out, and frictional energy dissipation, thereby retarding the formation of macroscopic fracture planes. Furthermore, the interfacial bonding between the fibers and the matrix continues to transfer tensile and shear stresses during the post-peak stage. This mechanism enables the specimen to preserve substantial integrity and residual load-bearing capacity even beyond peak strength.

Correlating these observations with the 28-day mechanical response curves reveals that the load-bearing capacity of the unreinforced specimens (Y1, Y2, and Y3) declined precipitously post-peak, indicating inferior residual strength. Conversely, specimens with 0.3% and 0.6% fiber contents exhibited a more gradual post-peak stress drop and superior structural cohesion. Therefore, the fundamental contribution of basalt fibers lies not merely in enhancing crack initiation resistance, but more critically in augmenting post-peak toughness and fracture resistance. This enhancement is of paramount importance for maintaining the structural stability and load-bearing capacity of backfill walls in gob-side entry retaining subjected to continuous roof subsidence.

Peak strain

Peak strain reflects the deformation resistance of a material. Figure 13 presents the peak strain values for different mix proportions. With increasing curing time, the peak strain of the 28‑d groups increased to a certain extent compared to the 7‑d groups. Among all groups, Y6 (w/c = 0.50, early‑strength agent = 0.6%, fiber = 0.3%) exhibited relatively larger peak strain. In the 7‑d groups, Y4 (w/c = 0.55, early‑strength agent = 1.2%, fiber = 0.3%) had the smallest peak strain, while in the 28‑d groups, Y5 (w/c = 0.60, early‑strength agent = 0%, fiber = 0.3%) showed the smallest peak strain. These results indicate that peak strain is jointly influenced by w/c ratio, early‑strength agent content, and fiber content. Among the tested groups, the mixed proportion of Y6 provided the greatest improvement in peak strain.Fig. 13Peak strain of different groups.

Peak strength

Table 4 lists the uniaxial compressive strength of concrete at 7 d and 28 d, and Fig. 14 shows the strengths and their ratios. The average 7‑d uniaxial compressive strength ranged from 19.28 to 26.84 MPa, and the average 28‑d strength from 30.08 to 34.26 MPa. The 7‑d strength reached 63–78% of the 28‑d strength. Macroscopic failure characteristics

As shown in Fig. 15, which illustrates the failure modes on the front and back faces, the first group without fiber exhibited a higher degree of fragmentation than the other groups, confirming that fiber addition enhances crack resistance. After failure at 20°, the specimens maintained good overall integrity, exhibiting only a pronounced trend of single‑crack lateral through‑failure. Industrial test

Field test

Crack initiation and strength deterioration in the backfill body typically occur at an early stage before it attains a certain load‑bearing capacity. Based on the comparative results between the optimal concrete mix (Y6) and the control group (Y1), a mix proportion with a water‑cement ratio of 0.50, 0.6% early‑strength agent, and 0.3% fiber content was adopted to construct the gob‑side entry retaining wall in the main haulage gateway of the N2302 working face at Gucheng Coal Mine. The wall width was reduced from 1.5 to 1.2 m to lower backfilling costs. In addition, a foundation reinforcement treatment was applied to the bottom area of the backfill body: a groove was excavated in advance, and concrete was poured to form a foundation, thereby enhancing the tilt resistance of the backfill wall. As shown in Fig. 18a, the foundation increases the contact area with the floor coal, reduces the bearing pressure at the bottom, and mitigates tilting induced by floor coal deformation.Fig. 18Construction diagrams of the flexible‑formwork concrete backfill.

Construction diagrams of the flexible‑formwork concrete backfill.

Meanwhile, temporary support consisting of a one-beam and three-prop structure was combined with the installation of tied anchor bolts within the flexible formwork. Steel strips, bearing plates, and bolts were fastened at both ends of the tied anchors, with an anchor spacing of 800 mm × 900 mm, as illustrated in Fig. 18b. This configuration facilitates the development and exertion of the supporting resistance of the backfill body. Based on the backfill material test results, field backfilling tests were conducted. The construction process of the flexible‑formwork concrete in the N2302 transportation gateway and the on‑site operations are shown in Fig. 18c.

Conclusions

To determine the parameters of a backfill material for gob‑side entry retaining that exhibits rapid resistance increase and high early strength, and to reveal the deterioration patterns of the shear stress component on the peak strength, peak strain, and failure mode of the backfill body, an orthogonal experiment was conducted to develop a high‑strength backfill material for thick coal seams with hard roofs. The following conclusions are drawn:Under uniaxial compression, fiber incorporation significantly improves the integrity and crack resistance of concrete specimens and mitigates post‑peak strength degradation. The group with 0.6% early‑strength agent shows a notable improvement in 7-day strength, whereas excessive early‑strength agent hinders normal strength development.Range and variance analyses of the 7‑d and 28‑d uniaxial compressive strength indicate that the factors are ranked in descending order of influence as: water‑cement ratio > early‑strength agent content > fiber content. The optimal mix proportion is a water‑cement ratio of 0.50, 0.6% early‑strength agent, and 0.3% fiber content. As the angle increases from 10° to 20°, longitudinal through‑cracks decrease while lateral through‑cracks increase, and the specimens maintain high integrity with a single‑crack lateral through‑failure characteristic. With a larger angle, the compressive strength reduction becomes more pronounced, and the failure mode progressively transitions from compression‑dominated to shear‑dominated. Appropriate water‑cement ratio, early‑strength agent dosage, and fiber content can effectively enhance the compression‑shear strength of concrete.

Following the field application of the optimized high-resistance backfill material and the implementation of corresponding anti-tilting technical measures, the deformation of the roadway surrounding rock was monitored using the cross-point layout method. The monitoring results reveal that the floor heave of the roadway is approximately 290 mm, while the roof subsidence and rib convergence are approximately 340 mm and 180 mm, respectively. With the backfill width reduced to 1.2 m, the measured roof subsidence exhibits a 59.4% reduction relative to the theoretically predicted value. The overall deformation control of the roadway is deemed effective, the backfill support remains structurally stable, and no visible indications of failure or deterioration are observed.
