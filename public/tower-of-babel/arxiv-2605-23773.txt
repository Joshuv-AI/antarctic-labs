A Balancing Theorem for Spanning Trees of Rectangular Grid Graphs
Jiechen Zhang

Abstract
We prove that, among rectangular grid graphs with a fixed number of vertices, the number of spanning trees increases when the side lengths are made more balanced. In particular, among all rectangular grid graphs with n 2 n^{2} vertices, the square n × n n\times n grid has the largest number of spanning trees. The proof starts with the Laplacian product formula, passes to hyperbolic coordinates, and compares logarithms by separating a discrete-concavity term from a positive decreasing residual term.



1
Introduction

Let
P_{r}
be the path graph on
r
vertices, and let

R_{\ell,m}=P_{\ell}\square P_{m}

be the
\ell\times m
rectangular grid graph formed by their Cartesian product. Let
\tau(G)
denote the number of spanning trees of a finite connected graph
G
, and abbreviate

\tau(\ell,m)=\tau(R_{\ell,m}).

The guiding question is whether the square shape maximizes the number of spanning trees. To state the question in a more general induced-grid form, let
\mathcal{L}
be the infinite square-lattice graph with vertex set
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.

Conjecture 1.1
.

Let
n\geq 1
be an integer. Let
S\subset\mathbb{Z}^{2}
be a finite set of vertices such that
|S|=n^{2}
, and suppose that the induced subgraph
\mathcal{L}[S]
is connected. Then

\tau(\mathcal{L}[S])\leq\tau(R_{n,n}).

This work proves the conjectured square-maximal principle within the family of rectangles. More precisely, we prove the following stronger balancing statement: among rectangular grids with fixed area, moving the side lengths closer together cannot decrease the number of spanning trees.

The directly related background is the Matrix–Tree Theorem of Kirchhoff
[
2
]
, which expresses the spanning-tree count as a Laplacian cofactor, together with classical product formulas and asymptotic studies for spanning trees on rectangular grids and lattices
[
6
,
7
,
4
,
1
]
. The broader square-maximal principle for induced grid graphs has also appeared in the redistricting literature
[
3
]
; recent boundary-sensitive bounds of Tapp
[
5
]
are close in spirit, although they do not imply the exact rectangular comparison proved here.

Theorem 1.2

(Balancing Theorem)
.

Let
\ell,m,\ell^{\prime},m^{\prime}
be positive integers such that

\ell m=\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime},\qquad\ell\leq\ell^{\prime}\leq m^{\prime}\leq m.

Then

\tau(\ell,m)\leq\tau(\ell^{\prime},m^{\prime}).

In words, among rectangles of fixed area, increasing the shorter side and decreasing the longer side cannot decrease the number of spanning trees.

Corollary 1.3
.

If
\ell m=n^{2}
, then

\tau(\ell,m)\leq\tau(n,n).

Proof.

Since
R_{\ell,m}\cong R_{m,\ell}
, we may assume
\ell\leq m
. From
\ell m=n^{2}
it follows that
\ell\leq n\leq m
. Applying
Theorem

1.2
with
\ell^{\prime}=m^{\prime}=n
gives the claim.
∎

2
Preliminaries

For
r\geq 1
and
0\leq j\leq r-1
, set

\lambda_{j}(r)=2-2\cos\frac{\pi j}{r}.

Thus
\lambda_{0}(r)=0
, and for
j\geq 1
,

\lambda_{j}(r)=4\sin^{2}\frac{\pi j}{2r}.

Proposition 2.1
.

For all positive integers
\ell,m
,

\tau(\ell,m)=\prod_{j=1}^{\ell-1}\prod_{k=1}^{m-1}\bigl(\lambda_{j}(\ell)+\lambda_{k}(m)\bigr).

Proof.

For the path spectrum, the case
r=1
is immediate. Assume
r\geq 2
. The Laplacian eigenvalues of
P_{r}
are the numbers
\lambda_{j}(r)
,
0\leq j\leq r-1
. Indeed, if the vertices are
1,\ldots,r
and

u_{x}^{(j)}=\cos\left(\left(x-\frac{1}{2}\right)\frac{\pi j}{r}\right),

then a direct substitution gives
(Lu^{(j)})_{x}=\lambda_{j}(r)u_{x}^{(j)}

at every interior vertex. The same identity holds at the endpoints using the reflecting boundary
relations
u^{(j)}_{0}=u^{(j)}_{1}
and
u^{(j)}_{r+1}=u^{(j)}_{r}
.
Indeed, at
x=1
one has
(Lu^{(j)})_{1}=u^{(j)}_{1}-u^{(j)}_{2}=2u^{(j)}_{1}-u^{(j)}_{0}-u^{(j)}_{2}
,
and similarly at
x=r
. Thus the endpoint equations agree with the same second-difference eigenvalue equation. The
r
values
\lambda_{j}(r)
are distinct. Hence the corresponding eigenvectors are linearly independent; since
P_{r}
has
r
vertices, they form a basis.

The Laplacian of a Cartesian product is
L(P_{\ell}\square P_{m})=L(P_{\ell})\otimes I_{m}+I_{\ell}\otimes L(P_{m})
.
Hence the Laplacian eigenvalues of
R_{\ell,m}
are

\lambda_{j}(\ell)+\lambda_{k}(m),\qquad 0\leq j\leq\ell-1,\quad 0\leq k\leq m-1.

Since
R_{\ell,m}
is connected, the only zero Laplacian eigenvalue occurs at
(j,k)=(0,0)
. Kirchhoff’s Matrix–Tree Theorem
[
2
]
gives

\tau(\ell,m)=\frac{1}{\ell m}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq j\leq\ell-1\\
0\leq k\leq m-1\\
(j,k)\neq(0,0)\end{subarray}}\bigl(\lambda_{j}(\ell)+\lambda_{k}(m)\bigr).

All products below are interpreted as empty products when one of the side
lengths is equal to
1
. Thus the argument also covers the degenerate
rectangular grids
R_{1,m}
and
R_{\ell,1}
.

It remains only to cancel the one-dimensional factors. For every
r\geq 1
,

\prod_{j=1}^{r-1}\lambda_{j}(r)=r,

where the case
r=1
is an empty product. For
r\geq 2
, this follows from
\lambda_{j}(r)=4\sin^{2}\frac{\pi j}{2r}

and the identity

\prod_{j=1}^{r-1}2\sin\frac{\pi j}{2r}=\sqrt{r}.

To prove this identity, recall that the roots of
z^{2r}-1=0
are the complex roots of unity
e^{\pi iq/r}
for
0\leq q<2r
. Factoring the polynomial and dividing by the trivial root
(z-1)
yields the standard result:

\frac{z^{2r}-1}{z-1}=\prod_{q=1}^{2r-1}\bigl(z-e^{\pi iq/r}\bigr)

and letting
z\to 1
and taking absolute values gives

2r=\prod_{q=1}^{2r-1}\left|1-e^{\pi iq/r}\right|.

The middle factor, corresponding to
q=r
, is
|1-e^{\pi i}|=2
. For
1\leq j\leq r-1
, the factors indexed by
j
and
2r-j
are equal in absolute value, and
\left|1-e^{\pi ij/r}\right|=2\sin\frac{\pi j}{2r}
.
Pairing these factors gives

2r=2\prod_{j=1}^{r-1}\left(2\sin\frac{\pi j}{2r}\right)^{2}.

The nonzero factors with
k=0
, where
1\leq j\leq\ell-1
, multiply to
\prod_{j=1}^{\ell-1}\lambda_{j}(\ell)=\ell
,
and the nonzero factors with
j=0
, where
1\leq k\leq m-1
, multiply to
\prod_{k=1}^{m-1}\lambda_{k}(m)=m
.
These two one-dimensional products cancel the prefactor
1/(\ell m)
, leaving only the terms with
j,k\geq 1
.
∎

Thus the zero-mode contributions have canceled the Kirchhoff normalization, leaving only the interaction of the positive modes in the two coordinate directions.

Hyperbolic Form and Discrete Concavity

For
r\geq 1
, define

q_{r}(x)=\prod_{j=1}^{r-1}\bigl(x+\lambda_{j}(r)\bigr).

Then
Section

2
can be written as

\tau(\ell,m)=\prod_{j=1}^{\ell-1}q_{m}(\lambda_{j}(\ell))=\prod_{k=1}^{m-1}q_{\ell}(\lambda_{k}(m)).

(3.1)

Lemma 3.1
.

If
x=2\cosh\theta-2
with
\theta>0
, then

q_{r}(x)=\frac{\sinh(r\theta)}{\sinh\theta}.

Proof.

Let
p_{1}(x)=1
,
p_{2}(x)=x+2
, and

p_{r+1}(x)=(x+2)p_{r}(x)-p_{r-1}(x).

By induction,
p_{r}
is monic of degree
r-1
. If
x=2\cosh\theta-2
, the identity

\sinh((r+1)\theta)=2\cosh\theta\sinh(r\theta)-\sinh((r-1)\theta)

shows that
p_{r}(x)=\sinh(r\theta)/\sinh\theta
.

For
r=1
, both
p_{1}
and
q_{1}
are the constant polynomial
1
, so the claim is immediate. We therefore assume
r\geq 2
in the root identification below. To identify
p_{r}
with
q_{r}
, substitute
x=2\cos\phi-2
. The same recurrence gives

p_{r}(2\cos\phi-2)=\frac{\sin(r\phi)}{\sin\phi}

whenever
\sin\phi\neq 0
. Taking
\phi=\pi j/r
for
1\leq j\leq r-1
shows that
p_{r}(-\lambda_{j}(r))=0
. Thus
p_{r}
and
q_{r}
are monic polynomials of degree
r-1
with the same
r-1
roots, namely
-\lambda_{j}(r)
for
1\leq j\leq r-1
. Hence
p_{r}=q_{r}
.
∎

Equivalently,

q_{r}(x)=U_{r-1}\left(\frac{x+2}{2}\right),

where
U_{r-1}
is the Chebyshev polynomial of the second kind. The proof above is included to keep the normalization and boundary convention explicit.

Define

c(x)=\arcosh\left(2-\cos(\pi x)\right)=2\arsinh\left(\sin\frac{\pi x}{2}\right),\qquad 0\leq x\leq 1.

Here
\operatorname{arcosh}
and
\operatorname{arsinh}
denote the
inverse hyperbolic cosine and sine, respectively.

For
1\leq j\leq r-1
, the number
c(j/r)
is the hyperbolic parameter corresponding to the
j
-th positive eigenvalue of
P_{r}
. Then

\lambda_{j}(r)=2\cosh c(j/r)-2.

(3.2)

Lemma 3.2
.

The function
c
satisfies
c(0)=0
,
c^{\prime}(x)\geq 0
, and
c^{\prime\prime}(x)\leq 0
on
[0,1]
.

Proof.

The value
c(0)=0
is immediate. Differentiating the
2\arsinh(\sin(\pi x/2))
expression gives

c^{\prime}(x)=\frac{\pi\cos(\pi x/2)}{(1+\sin^{2}(\pi x/2))^{1/2}}\geq 0

and

c^{\prime\prime}(x)=-\frac{\pi^{2}\sin(\pi x/2)}{(1+\sin^{2}(\pi x/2))^{3/2}}\leq 0.

∎

Recall that for a function
f
on
[0,1]
with
f(0)=0
, the expression
\frac{1}{r}\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}f(j/r)
is the standard left Riemann sum. As
r\to\infty
, this sum converges to
\int_{0}^{1}f(x)\,dx
. The following lemma shows that if
f
is increasing and concave, then these left Riemann sums form a monotonically nondecreasing sequence in
r
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
f\in C^{2}[0,1]
satisfy
f(0)=0
,
f^{\prime}\geq 0
, and
f^{\prime\prime}\leq 0
. For
r\geq 1
, set

A_{r}(f)=\frac{1}{r}\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}f(j/r).

Then
A_{r}(f)\leq A_{r+1}(f)
for every
r\geq 1
. Consequently, if
r\leq s
, then

\frac{1}{r}\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}f(j/r)\leq\frac{1}{s}\sum_{j=1}^{s-1}f(j/s).

Proof.

We first verify the claim for two elementary building blocks. For the identity function
\operatorname{id}(x)=x
,

A_{r}(\operatorname{id})=\frac{1}{r}\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}\frac{j}{r}=\frac{r-1}{2r},

which is increasing in
r
.

Now fix
u\in[0,1]
and consider the building block
x\mapsto\min(x,u)
. Put

B_{r}(u)=\frac{1}{r}\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}\min(j/r,u).

The case
u=1
is the already treated linear building block, so assume
0\leq u<1
and let
a=\lfloor ru\rfloor
. Then

B_{r}(u)=\frac{1}{r}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{a}\frac{j}{r}+(r-1-a)u\right)=\frac{a(a+1)}{2r^{2}}+\frac{r-1-a}{r}u.

We compare this expression with the corresponding one for
r+1
. Since
a=\lfloor ru\rfloor
, we have

\frac{a}{r}\leq u<\frac{a+1}{r}.

On this interval,
\lfloor(r+1)u\rfloor
can only be
a
or
a+1
. If
\lfloor(r+1)u\rfloor=a
, then

B_{r+1}(u)-B_{r}(u)=\frac{(a+1)\bigl(2r(r+1)u-a(2r+1)\bigr)}{2r^{2}(r+1)^{2}}\geq 0,

because
u\geq a/r
. If
\lfloor(r+1)u\rfloor=a+1
, then
B_{r+1}(u)-B_{r}(u)
is linear in
u
with slope
-(r-a-1)/(r(r+1))\leq 0
on this interval. Its infimum is therefore attained at the right endpoint in the closure, namely at
u=(a+1)/r
, where the limiting value is

\frac{(a+1)(a+2)}{2r^{2}(r+1)^{2}}\geq 0.

Thus
B_{r}(u)\leq B_{r+1}(u)
for every
u\in[0,1]
.

It remains to express an arbitrary admissible
f
as a nonnegative combination of these building blocks. Since
f(0)=0
, repeated integration by parts, or equivalently, direct differentiation, yields the following representation for
0\leq x\leq 1
:

f(x)=f^{\prime}(1)x+\int_{0}^{1}\min(x,u)(-f^{\prime\prime}(u))\,du.

Indeed, the right-hand side evaluates to
0
at
x=0
. Since
f^{\prime\prime}
is continuous, differentiating with respect to
x
is justified
and gives

f^{\prime}(1)+\int_{x}^{1}(-f^{\prime\prime}(u))\,du=f^{\prime}(x).

Since both sides share the same derivative and the same initial value at
x=0
, the representation follows.

Averaging this representation over the points
j/r
gives

A_{r}(f)=f^{\prime}(1)A_{r}(\operatorname{id})+\int_{0}^{1}B_{r}(u)(-f^{\prime\prime}(u))\,du.

The coefficient
f^{\prime}(1)
is nonnegative, and
-f^{\prime\prime}\geq 0
. Since both building blocks have averages that are nondecreasing in
r
, it follows that
A_{r}(f)\leq A_{r+1}(f)
. The final assertion follows by iteration.
∎

The main proof uses this averaging inequality only for the function
c
. Put

C_{r}=\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}c(j/r).

By
Sections

3
and
3
, if
r\leq s
, then

rC_{s}-sC_{r}\geq 0.

(3.3)

4
Proof of the Balancing Theorem

Proof of
Theorem

1.2
.

Step 1. Setup.
Set

A=\ell,\qquad a=\ell^{\prime},\qquad b=m^{\prime},\qquad B=m.

Thus the two rectangles are
R_{A,B}
and
R_{a,b}
. Here
A,B
denote the side lengths of the original rectangle, while
a,b
denote the side lengths of the more balanced rectangle. The hypotheses of the theorem become

AB=ab,\qquad A\leq a\leq b\leq B.

Then the two side-length ratios agree:

t\coloneqq\frac{a}{A}=\frac{B}{b}.

If
t=1
, then the two rectangles are identical. We therefore assume
t>1
.

Step 2. Convert to logarithms.
Recall from (
3.1
) that, for positive integers
u,v
,

\tau(u,v)=\prod_{k=1}^{v-1}q_{u}(\lambda_{k}(v))=\prod_{j=1}^{u-1}q_{v}(\lambda_{j}(u)).

Also recall that (
3.2
) gives
\lambda_{k}(b)=2\cosh(c(k/b))-2
, and
Section

3
says that if
x=2\cosh\theta-2
, then
q_{r}(x)=\sinh(r\theta)/\sinh\theta
. For
1\leq k\leq b-1
, we have
0<k/b<1
, hence

c(k/b)>0
. Similarly, for
1\leq j\leq A-1
, we have

0<j/A<1
, hence
c(j/A)>0
. Thus
Section

3
applies with positive hyperbolic parameter in all terms below. Therefore, for
1\leq k\leq b-1
,

\frac{q_{a}(\lambda_{k}(b))}{q_{A}(\lambda_{k}(b))}=\frac{\sinh(ac(k/b))}{\sinh(Ac(k/b))}=\frac{\sinh(t\,Ac(k/b))}{\sinh(Ac(k/b))}.

Then define

H_{t}(z)=\log\left(\frac{\sinh(tz)}{\sinh z}\right),

which records the logarithmic gain from multiplying one side length by
t
. Taking logarithms in the preceding product ratio gives

\log\left(\frac{\tau(a,b)}{\tau(A,b)}\right)=\sum_{k=1}^{b-1}H_{t}\bigl(Ac(k/b)\bigr).

(4.1)

Similarly, using
\lambda_{j}(A)=2\cosh(c(j/A))-2
, for
1\leq j\leq A-1
we have

\frac{q_{B}(\lambda_{j}(A))}{q_{b}(\lambda_{j}(A))}=\frac{\sinh(Bc(j/A))}{\sinh(bc(j/A))}=\frac{\sinh(t\,bc(j/A))}{\sinh(bc(j/A))}.

Thus changing the second side from
b
to
B=tb
gives

\log\left(\frac{\tau(A,B)}{\tau(A,b)}\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{A-1}H_{t}\bigl(bc(j/A)\bigr).

(4.2)

Step 3. Combine into one equation.
Subtracting (
4.2
) from (
4.1
) gives

\log\left(\frac{\tau(a,b)}{\tau(A,B)}\right)=\sum_{k=1}^{b-1}H_{t}\bigl(Ac(k/b)\bigr)-\sum_{j=1}^{A-1}H_{t}\bigl(bc(j/A)\bigr).

(4.3)

It remains to prove that the right-hand side is nonnegative.

Step 4. Split the function.
The split comes directly from the exponential form of the hyperbolic sine. For
z>0
,

H_{t}(z)=\log\left(\frac{e^{tz}-e^{-tz}}{e^{z}-e^{-z}}\right)=\log\left(e^{(t-1)z}\frac{1-e^{-2tz}}{1-e^{-2z}}\right).

Hence

H_{t}(z)=(t-1)z+G_{t}(z),\qquad G_{t}(z)=\log\left(\frac{1-e^{-2tz}}{1-e^{-2z}}\right).

(4.4)

By extracting the dominant linear term
(t-1)z
, we can control it directly via the discrete concavity of
c
. The nonlinear complexity is thus concentrated into the residual term
G_{t}(z)
. Because
t>1
implies
1-e^{-2tz}>1-e^{-2z}
, the residual satisfies
G_{t}(z)>0
and is controlled entirely by its positivity and the fact that it is decreasing.

It remains only to record that
G_{t}
is decreasing. Differentiating gives

G_{t}^{\prime}(z)=\frac{2t}{e^{2tz}-1}-\frac{2}{e^{2z}-1}.

For
y>0
, the power series
\frac{e^{y}-1}{y}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{y^{k-1}}{k!}
is strictly increasing in
y
(one can also see this via
\frac{e^{y}-1}{y}=\int_{0}^{1}e^{sy}\,ds
). Since
t>1
, this implies

\frac{e^{2tz}-1}{2tz}>\frac{e^{2z}-1}{2z},

which rearranging yields
G_{t}^{\prime}(z)<0
.

Recall that

C_{r}=\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}c(j/r).

Substituting (
4.4
) into (
4.3
) gives

\log\left(\frac{\tau(a,b)}{\tau(A,B)}\right)=(t-1)(AC_{b}-bC_{A})+\Gamma,

(4.5)

where the residual contribution is

\Gamma=\sum_{k=1}^{b-1}G_{t}\bigl(Ac(k/b)\bigr)-\sum_{j=1}^{A-1}G_{t}\bigl(bc(j/A)\bigr).

Step 5. Prove the linear part is nonnegative.

The inequality recorded in (
3.3
), obtained from
Sections

3
and
3
, says that if
r\leq s
, then

rC_{s}-sC_{r}\geq 0.

Applying this with
r=A
and
s=b
gives

AC_{b}-bC_{A}\geq 0.

Equivalently,
C_{b}/b\geq C_{A}/A
. Thus the first term on the right-hand side of (
4.5
) is nonnegative.

Step 6. Prove the residual part is positive.
It remains to show that
\Gamma\geq 0
. Since
b\geq A
and
G_{t}
is decreasing,

G_{t}\bigl(bc(j/A)\bigr)\leq G_{t}\bigl(Ac(j/A)\bigr)\qquad(1\leq j\leq A-1).

Hence

\Gamma\geq\sum_{k=1}^{b-1}F(k/b)-\sum_{j=1}^{A-1}F(j/A),\qquad F(x)=G_{t}(Ac(x)).

(4.6)

By
Section

3
, the function
c
is increasing on
[0,1]
. Step 4 showed that
G_{t}
is decreasing on
(0,\infty)
. Since
A>0
, the composite function
F(x)=G_{t}(Ac(x))
is therefore nonincreasing on
(0,1]
; because
G_{t}>0
, the function
F
is positive there.

We now use a simple matching principle: if
F
is positive and nonincreasing on
(0,1]
and
b\geq A
, then

\sum_{k=1}^{b-1}F(k/b)\geq\sum_{j=1}^{A-1}F(j/A).

Indeed, match the point
j/A
to the point
k_{j}/b
, where
k_{j}=\left\lfloor\frac{jb}{A}\right\rfloor
. These indices are distinct. Indeed, using the elementary inequality
\lfloor x+y\rfloor-\lfloor x\rfloor\geq\lfloor y\rfloor
, we have, for
1\leq j\leq A-2
,

k_{j+1}-k_{j}=\left\lfloor\frac{jb}{A}+\frac{b}{A}\right\rfloor-\left\lfloor\frac{jb}{A}\right\rfloor\geq\left\lfloor\frac{b}{A}\right\rfloor\geq 1.

Moreover, since
1\leq j\leq A-1
, we have
1\leq k_{j}\leq b-1
. Also
k_{j}/b\leq j/A
, so monotonicity gives
F(k_{j}/b)\geq F(j/A)
. The unmatched terms in the left-hand sum are positive, proving the principle.

Applying this principle to
F(x)=G_{t}(Ac(x))
gives the nonnegativity of the right-hand side of (
4.6
). In fact, the inequality is strict when
t>1
. Indeed, for
t>1
, we have
b\geq a>A
. Thus the left-hand sum contains
b-1
terms, while only
A-1
of them are matched to terms in the right-hand sum. At least one unmatched term remains, and every unmatched term is positive because
F>0
on
(0,1]
. Hence
\Gamma>0
.

Step 7. Conclusion.
By (
4.5
), the linear term is nonnegative and the residual term is positive in the nontrivial case
t>1
. Hence

\log\left(\frac{\tau(a,b)}{\tau(A,B)}\right)>0,

and therefore
\tau(A,B)<\tau(a,b)
. If
t=1
, then the two rectangles are identical. Returning to the original notation gives

\tau(\ell,m)\leq\tau(\ell^{\prime},m^{\prime}),

as desired.
∎

5
Discussion

The proof of the Balancing Theorem relies on the Cartesian-product structure of rectangular grid graphs. This structure separates the Laplacian spectrum and leaves, after the cancellation of the one-dimensional factors, a product that can be compared using the hyperbolic factorization and the discrete concavity of
c
.

For general induced grid graphs, elementary extremal considerations point in the same direction but do not prove
Section

1
. One useful fact is that the square is already extremal for edges, and therefore for cycle rank. Let
G=\mathcal{L}[S]
be connected with
|S|=n^{2}
, let
E=|E(G)|
, and let

\rho(G)=|E(G)|-|V(G)|+1=E-n^{2}+1

be its cycle rank. Then

E\leq 2n(n-1),\qquad\rho(G)\leq(n-1)^{2},

with equality only for the square
R_{n,n}
up to translation.

Here is the short proof. Let
w
and
h
be the numbers of occupied columns and rows. Since
G
is connected, its projections onto the horizontal and vertical axes are sets of consecutive integers. Hence the occupied bounding box contains
wh
lattice points, and since it contains
S
, we have
wh\geq n^{2}
. Let
b
be the number of ordered pairs
(v,u)
such that
v\in S
,
u\notin S
, and
u
is a grid-neighbor of
v
. In each occupied row, the leftmost and rightmost occupied vertices have horizontal neighbors outside
S
. Hence each occupied row contributes at least two horizontal boundary incidences. Similarly, each occupied column contributes at least two vertical boundary incidences. Thus

b\geq 2w+2h\geq 4\sqrt{wh}\geq 4n.

(5.1)

The identity
4n^{2}=2E+b
follows by summing, over all vertices in
S
, the four incident lattice directions: internal edges are counted twice, while boundary incidences are counted once. Thus
E\leq 2n(n-1)
and then
\rho(G)\leq(n-1)^{2}
. Equality in (
5.1
) forces equality throughout the chain, and hence
w=h=n
and
wh=n^{2}
