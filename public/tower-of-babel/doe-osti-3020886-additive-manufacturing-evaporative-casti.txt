 ABSTRACT

Traditional lost foam casting has been around for decades. The process uses foam forms blown by an
injection molding like process or CNC milled into the desired shape, then pouring molten metal over the
foam pattern to create a metallic object. Additive Manufacturing Evaporative Casting (AMEC) is a new
process that eliminates foam forms by using additive manufacturing to 3D print the desired cast geometry.
This not only saves time and money but allows for more advanced and complex designs that can’t be
achieved by carving foam. Additionally, AMEC doesn’t require molds and tooling like other casting and
foundry options. Because the AMEC process is new, extensive testing is needed to develop a better
understanding of the process to minimize defects, quantify material properties, and start computer
modeling for the process. This CRADA (collaborative research and development agreement) between
ORNL and Skuld seeks to improve the process, develop a computational model, characterize material
properties, and explore new applications.

 1. ADDITIVE MANUFACTURING EVAPORATIVE CASTING

 This technical collaboration project (CRADA NFE-24-10037) began on January 16, 2024, and
concluded in January 2026. The collaboration partner, Skuld LLC, is a small business. The results from
this project include advanced material characterization and calibration for the AMEC process, a basic
computational model for the AMEC process, and material qualification data.

1.1 BACKGROUND

 Skuld LLC is small business operating a foundry located in Piqua, Ohio. With a rich history in
foundry operation, they invented the AMEC process as a way to decrease the time and lower the cost to
develop complex metallic geometries. In 2023, Skuld was selected as part of the Innovation Crossroads
Cohort 2023. This gave them the opportunity to partner with researchers at ORNL to further develop the
AMEC process via CRADA.

 The CRADA between ORNL and Skuld had four primary tasks: AMEC process improvements,
computational model development, process and application window, and material characterization and
qualification. Process improvements included slicing and toolpathing, alternative polymers, and
alternative slurry coatings. The basic computational model was for developing a fundamental simulation
model to represent how the AMEC process works for various input geometries. The process and
application window was focused on qualifying the capabilities and testing new applications. Lastly, the
material characterization and qualification task was about understanding the material performance of cast
specimens. Ultimately, this project focused on testing, qualifying, and improving the AMEC process to
prove commercial viability.

1.2 TECHNICAL RESULTS

1.2.1 AMEC Process Improvements

 The AMEC process starts with 3D printing a model of the desired cast geometry. Because the 3D
printed model will be vaporized by the molten metal, it’s beneficial to minimize the amount of polymer
used to make the 3D printed model. This is primarily done by printing thin walls, minimizing solid
surfaces, and reducing infill to the lowest percentage possible. An additional opportunity is to perforate
the surface or make many small holes in the surface. This minimizes the polymer used and also creates a
path for gases to escape during vaporization. While parts can be perforated during design, this doesn’t
translate well to toolpath generation for 3D printing. Instead, it’s best to add the functionality during or
after slicing. Initially it was planned to implement this functionality to the ORNL Slicer software
package, but a faster and simpler solution was developed in the form of a post-processor. This takes a g-
code file as an input, allows the user to define settings for how the perforation works, and outputs a g-
code file with small holes in the surface.
 The polymer used for 3D printing in the AMEC process is PLA (polylactic acid); a material made
from corn. PLA has a low melting point and was found to only take approximately 15 seconds to fully
vaporize when testing with the TGA (thermogravimetric analysis). This TGA was used to determine how
the PLA performed when ramping temperature at a maximum speed of 500C. Testing was conducted with
a variety of PLA materials to understand if and how colorant impacted the burn off. Natural PLA, with no
colorant, left no material behind after vaporizing. White, which typically requires the most colorant to
manufacture, left the largest amount of residual material behind (approximately 6.4% of the starting
weight) as shown in Figure 1. In comparison foams such as expanded polystyrene (EPS) and extruded
polystyrene (XPS) leave no remnants behind. This is most desirable because any material left behind will
likely become an inclusion the cast metal object.

 Figure 1: TGA results from white PLA filament.

After a component is 3D printed for the AMEC process, it needs to be coated with a ceramic slurry before
it can be buried in sand for the casting process. This coating helps create a smooth outer surface for the
casting, prevent sand erosion, and allow gas to escape during vaporization. Skuld conducted some testing
of these coatings with a focus on improving the drying time for the coating. The results showed that
alcohol-based coatings could reduce the drying time by up to 90%.

1.2.2 Basic Computational Model

 Development of the computational model began with an input of material property data for the 3D
printing filament, PLA. This included thermodynamic parameters for pyrolysis and material kinetics. This
data reaffirms the use of PLA because of the limited char yield as well as limited condensable and non-
condensable gases (40% and 58% respectively). These qualities of PLA can be attributed to its low
melting point and corn-based precursor material rather than hydrocarbons as seen in other polymers.
 The material parameters for PLA were then combined with the material properties for sand,
aluminum 356, and IN17-4PH (a martensitic, precipitation hardening stainless steel). This includes
parameters such as density, permeability, thermal conductivity, and specific heat. With these parameters,
modeling could begin with the interaction at the surface between the molten metal and the polymer shell.
 With a fundamental understanding of the material properties and their behavior, a basic model was
built using this information as an input. The goal of this model is to understand how the molten metal
flows and interacts with the polymer shell. To test the model, a mesh representation of a plate with riser
and sprue was designed (Figure 2). Multiple simulations were then conducted to understand how this
would behave in a sand mold with varying fraction solid values (Figure 3).

 At this time, the model is still in its infancy. It has the ability to simulate specific geometries, such
as that of Figure 2. With additional development, the model and simulation will provide an understanding
of how a specific part will behave with a specific polymer while casting a specific metal. During the
process, it is known that the polymer will pyrolyze, but how quickly does that happen, how much heat can
the polymer absorb, how much gas is created and where, and what stress concentrations are formed and
where. These are just some of the questions that a computational model and simulation will help answer.
As the model and simulation become more robust, they will allow for inputs of specific geometries to
identify potential design changes to improve the end result.
 A more extensive writeup of the results from this task is available as a separate technical report
published by ORNL.

1.2.3 Applications and Process Window

 The application and process window for AMEC looks to be quite broad but has primarily been
tested by Skuld at various foundry locations such as their own facility or at Ohio State University. To
prove the capability, and also showcase it in a mobile capacity, Skuld helped ORNL restore a mobile
foundry developed by the US Army and housed within two shipping containers. ORNL inherited the
mobile foundry from Rock Island Arsenal in early 2025 in a non-working state (Figure 4). The foundry
was originally configured with a furnace for aluminum, a furnace for steel, sand mixing, shaking, and
blasting equipment for patterns, and a large chiller. The sand-handling equipment was removed as it was
not needed for the AMEC process. With help from the team at Skuld, the systems within the container
including furnaces, chiller, and other auxiliary equipment, were able to be repaired and returned to an
operational state.

 Figure 4: Mobile foundry unit during repairs and restoration.

 In addition to restoring the foundry container, ORNL staff needed training on how the AMEC
process works and how to conduct general foundry activities. A team of five engineers from ORNL
traveled to Skuld’s Ohio foundry for three days of classroom and hands-on training. The team learned
how to assemble a casting pattern with gates, vents, and down sprue, how to properly coat the pattern

with a ceramic slurry, how to setup barrels with sand and bury the casting pattern, and finally how to melt
and pour the metal safely over the 3D printed pattern. Another critical component of the training was
operational safety and proper personal protective equipment (PPE). At the end, each team member had
successfully cast a bevel gear from aluminum (Figure 5).

 Figure 5: The ORNL team with completed castings at the Skuld facility.

 With the mobile foundry operational and the team members trained, it was time to take the system
on the road and test new applications for the AMEC process. ORNL sent the mobile foundry to Schofield
Barracks in Hawaii to work directly with the US Military as part of a larger exercise for expeditionary
manufacturing training. There were two goals for the exercise: gain additional experience and knowledge
of using the AMEC process in an expeditionary environment and demonstrate knowledge transfer of the
AMEC and foundry processes to enlisted military personnel. At the exercise, the aluminum furnace was
used, and various components were investment cast including bilge plugs, wrenches, manifolds, and
mounting brackets. Several military members were able to be trained and achieve proficiency such that
they could conduct the entire process without support from ORNL staff. The exercise was very
successful. Pictures from the exercise cannot be shared at this time.

1.2.4 Material Characterization and Qualification Data

 Material characterization and qualification is critical for all manufacturing processes. If a
manufactured component can’t meet the required properties, it will fail in its operational environment.
Regulated industries such as aerospace, energy, and defense have strict guidelines on material and
component performance that must be met for a part to be certified.
 Because the AMEC process is very comparable to lost foam casting, with the resultant part being a
casting made by pouring molten metal similar to any other casting process, the resultant material
properties are very comparable. This was initially conducted by Skuld when developing the AMEC
process. Now, more scrutinous testing and review is needed to characterize and qualify the materials with
more thorough datasets that can be provided to potential partners.
 To do this, Skuld manufactured a variety of samples to be sent to an independent testing lab for
verification. These samples included square and round tensile testing bars. Collected data includes tensile

strength, yield strength, elongation in 4D, area location, and fracture location. A summary of the test
results can be seen in Table 1. The full datasets from the reports generated by NSL Analytical, an

1.3 IMPACTS

 The success of this project has proved the viability of the AMEC process. Because AMEC is a
casting process, materials are easily qualified and can use establish properties for cast components. This
greatly shortens the qualification time in comparison to metal components made entirely with additive
manufacturing.
 Further expansion and distribution of the AMEC process will help recover the foundry industry in
America. Reports suggest that a large majority of American foundries have closed in the last century,
including more than a third of steel foundries closing since 2000. Most foundry work has gone offshore,
but the AMEC process shows potential to reshore foundry capabilities.
 This project and the opportunities through Innovation Crossroads have also been impactful for
Skuld. During the duration of this project, Skuld has been awarded contracts and secured additional
funding to continue growing the company and investing in AMEC. This includes the first sale of their
Lightning Metal LM-16 system which is now at the University of Tennessee. The company has now
grown to more than 20 employees.

1.5 CONCLUSIONS

 This CRADA project was completely successfully and focused on improving, documenting, and
creating a better understanding of several aspects of the AMEC process. A new software post-processor
was developed to allow for automated perforating of 3D prints to save material and help gas escape when
the polymer vaporizes. Extensive testing was conducted on various PLA materials using TGA. The
results showed that natural PLA, without any added colorant, is safest and most effective for the casting
process. Conversely, white PLA (one of the most common colors), is the worst at leaving remnants

behind after vaporization. A basic computational model was developed to understand how the AMEC
process works and can be used to predict how geometries will perform. This includes understanding gas
formation and buildup due to the polymer pyrolyzing as well as stress concentrations and locations. Skuld
helped ORNL resurrect a mobile foundry and learn the necessary operations to begin expeditionary
manufacturing trials. Additionally, Skuld has expanded the AMEC process capabilities to achieve 150lb
steel parts and added new materials such as 6061 and 7075 aluminums. Future work will focus on
continuing to scale up the process, add new material capabilities, and develop automated systems such as
the Lightning Metal LM-16.
