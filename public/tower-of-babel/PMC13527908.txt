Perceived risk of developing lung cancer and its association with nicotine dependence and smoking behavior among adults who smoke in Golestan Province, Iran
Authors: Mousa Ghelichi-Ghojogh, Mohammad Hossein Rajaei, Abdurrahman Charkazi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPerceived risk of developing lung cancer is a critical factor influencing smoking cessation and prevention efforts. This study aimed to examine the association between nicotine dependence, stages of change in smoking behavior, and perceived risk of developing lung cancer among adults who smoke in Golestan Province, Iran.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted with 339 adults who smoke, recruited via convenience sampling. Data were collected using self-administered questionnaires assessing demographic characteristics, smoking history, nicotine dependence (Fagerström Test for Nicotine Dependence, FTND), stages of change, and perceived developing risk of lung cancer. Pearson correlation and linear regression analyses were used to evaluate associations between variables.ResultsThe mean (SD) age of the samples was 32.04 (12.07) with a range of 18–74 years. The mean perceived risk of developing lung cancer was 7.29 ± 4.81 (out of 20), indicating a low to moderate risk perception among participants. Nicotine dependence was positively associated with perceived risk, with each one-unit increase in FTND score corresponding to a 0.44-unit increase in perceived risk. Current adults who smoke showed a marginally higher perceived risk score compared to those reporting no smoking behavior (β = 1.57, p = 0.062). No significant associations were observed between perceived risk and age, smoking duration, or stages of change.ConclusionIn this study sample, adults who smoke in Golestan Province exhibited low to moderate perceived risk of developing lung cancer. Higher nicotine dependence and current smoking status are associated with increased risk perception. These findings underscore the importance of educational and behavioral interventions aimed at raising awareness of smoking-related health risks and reducing nicotine dependence. Future studies with larger, representative samples and longitudinal designs are recommended to further explore causal relationships and inform targeted public health strategies.

Introduction

Tobacco use remains one of the leading preventable causes of morbidity and mortality worldwide. It is estimated that between 850 million and 1.1 billion people globally use tobacco products, including cigarettes, waterpipes (hookah), smokeless tobacco, electronic cigarettes, and cigars. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), approximately 9% of all global deaths are attributable to tobacco use, resulting in nearly 7 million deaths annually. If current patterns of tobacco consumption persist, tobacco use is projected to cause more than one billion deaths during the twenty-first century [1, 2].

Although the prevalence of cigarette smoking has declined in several developed countries, the absolute number of adults who smoke worldwide has increased due to population growth. For example, in the United States, the prevalence of cigarette smoking decreased from 42% in 1965 to 15% in 2015; however, approximately 40 million individuals continue to smoke, indicating only a modest reduction in the total number of adults who smoke over this period [3]. Globally, the number of adults who smoke increased from 721 million in 1980 to 967 million in 2012 [4].

Tobacco use continues to pose a significant public health challenge in Iran. Prevalence rates vary widely depending on the population; for instance, cigarette smoking was reported at 3.4% among university students [5] and 13.1% among male workers [6]. National estimates indicate a broad prevalence range of 12.3% to 27.7% [7], with large-scale studies showing a notable gender gap, with prevalence as high as 26% among men and 2.7% among women [8].

While pooled analysis of STEPs survey data indicated a declining trend in cigarette smoking prevalence in Iran between 2005 and 2016 [9], recent meta-analyses show that prevalence remains considerable, particularly among university students [10].

Cigarette smoking is associated with numerous adverse health outcomes, including cardiovascular diseases, lung cancer, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, cancers of the oral cavity and esophagus, and periodontal diseases. Approximately 10% of deaths due to cardiovascular diseases are attributable to cigarette smoking [11]. Moreover, adults who smoke are nearly 20 times more likely to develop lung cancer than adults who not smoke [12].

Despite these well-established health risks, evidence suggests that adults who smoke often underestimate their personal susceptibility to smoking-related diseases. Studies have shown that adults who smoke perceive their risk of developing lung cancer and cardiovascular diseases to be lower than that perceived by Individuals who do not smoke [13, 14]. Chen and colleagues reported that nearly 60% of respondents believed their likelihood of developing lung cancer was lower than that of the average person of the same age, while 28.9% perceived their risk as similar to their age-matched peers [15]. This phenomenon, commonly referred to as optimistic bias, may undermine smoking cessation efforts by reducing perceived personal vulnerability [16].

Nicotine, the primary addictive component of cigarettes, plays a crucial role in the persistence of smoking behavior and the development of nicotine dependence. Emerging evidence suggests that nicotine dependence is associated with increased risk of lung cancer, potentially linked to more intensive smoking behaviors such as deeper inhalation and higher daily cigarette consumption among highly dependent individuals, which may lead to greater exposure to carcinogenic substances [17]. Interestingly, the level of nicotine dependence appears to be related to perceived health risk. Adults who smoke with higher nicotine dependence are often associated with a higher perceived risk of developing lung cancer compared to those with lower levels of dependence, which may reflect the greater awareness of withdrawal severity and the challenges associated with quitting smoking [18].

Understanding the interplay between nicotine dependence, smoking behavior, and perceived risk is essential for developing effective public health interventions in Iran. By identifying how these factors relate, policymakers and health professionals can design more targeted educational programs aimed at increasing risk awareness and enhancing smoking cessation success. Given the limited research on this relationship in the Iranian context, this study aimed to investigate the relationship between nicotine dependence and perceived risk of developing lung cancer among participating adults who smoke in Golestan Province, Iran.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted in Golestan Province, northern Iran, using a convenience sampling method. Data collection was performed from October to December 2025. The study included two groups of participants: current adults who smoke cigarettes and former adults who smoked cigarettes (defined as individuals who had smoked at least 100 cigarettes in their lifetime). Individuals who had never smoked were excluded. To ensure data integrity, the online questionnaire was designed with mandatory fields, resulting in no incomplete responses. A total of 339 eligible participants were recruited and completed the study.

Procedure

Following approval of the study protocol and receipt of an ethics approval code, an online version of the study questionnaire was developed. The questionnaire link was distributed via social media platforms, including Telegram, WhatsApp, Instagram, and the domestic messaging application Eitaa, to individuals who smoke cigarettes. Participants were encouraged to share the link with friends or acquaintances who also smoked. Additionally, a paper-based version of the questionnaire was used. In this phase, the researcher visited public gathering places such as cafés and parks, invited eligible individuals to complete the questionnaire, and asked them to introduce other adults who smoke among their acquaintances.

Before completing the questionnaire, participants were informed about the objectives and importance of the study. Verbal informed consent was obtained from all participants, and they were assured of the anonymity of the questionnaires and the confidentiality of their information. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Deputy for Research and Technology at Golestan University of Medical Sciences (approval code: IR.GOUMS.REC.1404.365).

Measures

A structured online questionnaire was employed to collect data on perceived risk, stages of change, nicotine dependence, and demographic characteristics. Perceived risk of smoking was assessed using a questionnaire developed based on a literature review and adapted from instruments designed by Wong and Cappella, as well as Kotz and colleagues [19, 20]. In Iran, this instrument has previously been used by Zarghami et al. to assess lung cancer risk [21, 22].

20.

Perceived risk was assessed using two specific items: [1] “If you continue smoking, compared to non-smokers of your same age, how likely do you think you are to develop lung cancer in the future?” and [2] “How likely do you think you are to survive for at least 5 years following a lung cancer diagnosis?“. For the first item, responses were recorded on a scale of 0 (not at all likely) to 10 (extremely likely). For the second item, to ensure that a higher score consistently represents a higher perceived risk, the response scale was reverse-coded: a score of 0 indicated a very high likelihood of survival, while 10 indicated a very low likelihood of survival. The total perceived risk score was calculated as the sum of these two items, with a possible range from 0 to 20, where higher scores indicate a greater perceived risk of lung cancer and mortality.”

Stages of change in smoking behavior were assessed using the Smoking Stages of Change Questionnaire developed by Prochaska and DiClemente which has five smoking stages of change: (1) precontemplation (no intent to quit), (2) contemplation (considering quitting within 6 months), (3) preparation (planning to quit within 30 days), (4) action (recent quit attempt), and (5) maintenance (sustained abstinence > 6 months) [23, 24].

previously applied in Iran by Charkazi et al. [25]. Nicotine dependence was measured using the FTND. This six-item scale is one of the most widely used tools for assessing nicotine addiction and can be easily administered as self-report questionnaire without the need for clinical assessments. It evaluates physiological indicators of addiction and has been extensively utilized in research. The FTND assesses the number of cigarettes smoked per day (fewer than 10, 11–20, 21–30, or more than 30, scored 0–3, respectively), the time to the first cigarette after waking (scored 3 if smoked within 5 min, 2 for 6–30 min, 1 for 31–60 min, and 0 for more than 60 min), smoking behavior when ill (Yes = 1, No = 0), smoking in prohibited places (Yes = 1, No = 0), difficulty refraining from smoking in the morning or at other times (Yes = 1, No = 0), and enjoyment of the first cigarette in the morning or subsequent cigarettes (Yes = 1, No = 0). The total score ranges from 0 to 10, with higher scores indicating greater nicotine addiction [26]. The validity and reliability of this questionnaire in Iran have been confirmed by Sarbandi et al. [27]. The questionnaire also collected demographic and smoking-related variables, including age, gender, education level, occupation, duration of smoking, daily cigarette consumption, and age at smoking initiation.

Statistical analysis

Collected data were entered into SPSS version 26 for analysis. Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation and categorical variables as frequency (percentage). Independent-samples t-tests were used for comparisons between two groups, and one-way ANOVA was applied for comparisons across more than two groups. Correlations between continuous variables were assessed using Pearson’s correlation coefficients as appropriate. Prior to conducting parametric analyses, assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variances were evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test, Q–Q plots, and Levene’s test. To examine the potential effects of independent variables on perceived risk of lung cancer, linear regression analyses were performed. A significance level of p < 0.05 was considered for all statistical tests.

Results

The mean (SD) age of the samples was 32.04 (12.07) with a range of 18–74 years. Of the 339 participants included in the study, 244 (72.0%) were male, 224 (66.1%) were single, and 201 (59.3%) had a university-level education. Regarding occupation, 103 participants (30.4%) were employed as workers, and approximately one-third of the participants (33.6%) reported having no income or a monthly income below 60 million Iranian Rials (46 USD).

Among the participants, 303 (89.4%) were current adults who smoke cigarette, and 157 (46.3%) reported using other forms of tobacco products, including waterpipe (hookah), Nass, or vaping, in addition to cigarettes. Regarding the stages of change in smoking behavior, 180 participants (53.1%) were in the precontemplation stage (Table 1).

Discussion

Risk perception is a central construct in numerous health behavior theories. In the context of cigarette smoking, perceived risk reflects individuals’ cognitive and emotional evaluations of the associated harms [28]. The present study investigated the relationship between nicotine dependence, stages of change in smoking behavior, and the perceived risk of lung cancer among individuals who smoke in Golestan Province.

Overall, the findings revealed a low level of perceived lung cancer risk among participants, with a mean score of 7.29 ± 4.81 out of 20. This level is notably lower than those reported by Zarghami et al., who observed mean scores of 12.97 and 10.60 ± 4.77 in previous studies [21, 22]. These results align with existing literature suggesting that individuals who smoke often underestimate their susceptibility to smoking-related diseases [13, 14]. Specifically, research has shown that those who engage in smoking perceive a lower risk of cardiovascular and lung diseases compared to individuals who do not smoke [29]. While low perceived risk is typically associated with increased initiation and decreased motivation to quit, higher perceived risk may function as a preventive factor, potentially discouraging tobacco use or promoting cessation among current users [30–32].

No significant association was observed between age and the perceived risk of lung cancer in the current study. This stands in contrast to findings by Zarghami et al., who reported that each additional year of age was associated with a 0.13-unit increase in perceived risk [22]. Furthermore, research by Hahn et al. [33], Ayanian and Cleary [14], and Romer and Jamieson [34] also indicated that older individuals perceive a higher risk of lung cancer than younger counterparts, further diverging from our results. To elucidate these discrepancies, longitudinal studies with larger cohorts are warranted.

Regarding smoking duration, no significant relationship was identified between the length of smoking and perceived lung cancer risk. This contradicts previous Iranian studies, which indicated that perceived risk decreases as smoking duration increases [21, 22]. Conversely, Chen et al. reported an opposing trend in the United States, where perceived risk increased with smoking duration [15]. The absence of an association in this study suggests that other variables, such as personality traits and social context, may influence risk perception and merit investigation in future research.

These discrepancies may stem from differences in public health interventions; for instance, US-based media campaigns have more effectively heightened awareness of smoking harms than similar initiatives in Iran. Additionally, the absence of immediate clinical symptoms may lead individuals to underestimate long-term risks [35]. The age profile of our participants (mean = 32.03 years) also plays a role, as lung cancer is less common in younger populations [36] and our sample was notably younger than the cohort studied by Zarghami et al. (mean = 35.57 years) [22].

The current study found no significant association between the perceived risk of lung cancer and the stages of change in smoking behavior. This contradicts findings by Hahn et al., who reported that individuals in the preparation and contemplation stages exhibited higher perceived risk and were more likely to engage in lung cancer screening [33]. Similarly, Ziebarth et al. observed that individuals without intention to quit perceived a lower risk [37], a trend also supported by Zarghami et al., who identified higher perceived risk among adults intending to quit [21]. Although a higher perceived risk was observed in the preparation stage compared to the contemplation and precontemplation stages, this difference did not reach statistical significance. This inconsistency may be attributed to the limited number of participants in the preparation and contemplation stages, which accounted for only 33.1% of the study population. Future research incorporating larger cohorts across all stages including action and maintenanceis essential to achieve a more robust understanding of how perceived risk evolves throughout the stages of change.

The study also revealed that perceived lung cancer risk was significantly lower among individuals who do not smoke compared to those who are currently smoking. Linear regression analysis demonstrated that those who do not engage in smoking had perceived risk scores 1.57 units lower than those who currently smoke. This finding aligns with expectations, as individuals who have ceased smoking may perceive a reduced personal risk due to the avoidance of high-risk behaviors, distinguishing their current health status from their period of active smoking.

Furthermore, a significant positive association was identified between perceived risk of lung cancer and nicotine dependence; specifically, each one-unit increase in the Fagerström Test for Nicotine Dependence (FTND) score was associated with a 0.44-unit increase in perceived risk. This relationship may be driven by the fact that heightened nicotine dependence, often linked to longer smoking durations, intensifies an individual’s awareness of smoking-related harms. Specifically, individuals with greater nicotine dependence may experience more severe withdrawal symptoms and face greater challenges during cessation attempts, potentially leading to a heightened acknowledgment of the associated health risks [38].

Study strengths

This study possesses several key strengths. First, it addresses an understudied area in Iran by examining the relationship between perceived lung cancer risk and nicotine dependence among individuals who smoke, providing novel regional evidence. Second, the use of validated instruments, such as the FTND and the Stages of Change questionnaire, ensures the reliability and comparability of the findings. Third, incorporating multiple variables including smoking status, daily consumption, age at initiation, and stages of change facilitated a comprehensive assessment of risk perception. Additionally, utilizing both online and face-to-face recruitment increased sample diversity and improved access to individuals who smoke. Finally, the application of multivariable regression analysis strengthened the findings by accounting for the simultaneous effects of nicotine dependence and current smoking behavior.

Study limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these results. The use of convenience sampling may limit the representativeness of the sample and restrict the generalizability of the findings to the broader population of individuals who smoke in Golestan Province. Furthermore, the relatively small sample size, particularly within the preparation and action stages, may have reduced the statistical power to detect significant differences between groups. As data were collected via self-administered questionnaires, the results may be subject to recall and social desirability biases. In research involving individuals who smoke, response bias is often prevalent, as participants might underreport nicotine dependence or smoking frequency to minimize perceived health risks. Additionally, the cross-sectional design precludes establishing causal relationships between nicotine dependence and perceived risk. The relatively young mean age of the participants may also have influenced perceptions of long-term health risks. Finally, the reliance on self-reported measures with limited scoring ranges may not have fully captured the multidimensional nature of risk perception and nicotine dependence. Due to recruitment constraints, the final sample size (n = 339) was slightly lower than the intended target (n = 385), necessitating a cautious interpretation of the results.

Conclusion

This study indicates that perceived lung cancer risk among individuals who smoke in Golestan Province is generally low to moderate. Higher nicotine dependence was significantly associated with increased perceived risk; specifically, each one-unit increase in the FTND score corresponded to an approximate 0.44-unit increase in perceived risk. Furthermore, individuals who are currently smoking reported higher perceived risk scores than those who do not smoke.

Given the importance of risk perception in smoking prevention, these findings highlight the need for educational and behavioral interventions to raise health awareness and reduce nicotine dependence. Such initiatives can help prevent smoking-related complications and guide public health policies aimed at motivating individuals who smoke toward cessation. To build on these findings, future research should: (1) employ random sampling to improve generalizability; (2) conduct longitudinal studies to examine causal relationships and changes in risk perception and nicotine dependence over time; (3) increase sample sizes in the preparation, action, and maintenance stages for more precise analysis; (4) assess psychological and social factors, such as personality traits and social context, that influence risk perception; (5) utilize multidimensional tools to capture the emotional and cognitive aspects of nicotine dependence; and (6) design and evaluate targeted educational programs and policy initiatives. Future studies should also explore how public health campaigns and social norms shape the risk perception and behavior of individuals who smoke.
