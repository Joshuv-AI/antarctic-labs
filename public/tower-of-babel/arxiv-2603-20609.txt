Abstract




Abstract

In light of prevailing data regulations, consumer mobility across diverse markets inherently endogenizes market segmentation. Considering such strategic interactions, we define a market segmentation as strategy-proof when no consumer (with positive measure) has an incentive to deviate to another market. We show that in every strategy-proof market segmentation, the producer surplus remains at the uniform monopoly level, and the consumer surplus is bounded between the buyer-optimal level and the uniform monopoly level. Remarkably, no consumer is worse off than in the case of a uniform monopoly. We also construct a family of strategy-proof segmentations to realize every possible welfare outcome.

Keywords:
Price discrimination, Market segmentation, Strategic consumer, Welfare;

D42, D83, L12

Introduction

Online sellers can leverage consumer data for market segmentation and price discrimination, potentially harming consumer welfare. Consequently, scholars and competition authorities have proposed the prohibition of price discrimination as a straightforward means to safeguard consumers.

Recent privacy protection advancements like GDPR offer an alternative approach: enabling consumers to strategically switch among market segments, which potentially enhances consumer surplus. The first step of such mobility,
the Right to be Forgotten
, is already legally mandated.
1
1

1

All iOS apps on the App Store are required to offer an in-app account deletion option by July 30, 2022 (see
https://developer.apple.com/support/offering-account-deletion-in-your-app
). Moreover, all iOS apps should let people use them without a login (see App Store Review Guideline 5.1.1(v) in
https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/#data-collection-and-storage
).

Considering consumers’ decentralized, self-interested, and insignificant nature, can they be effectively protected against a monopolist who engages in price discrimination? To explore this issue, we study an extreme scenario where consumers face no costs when changing the market segment. This would provide a (non-cooperative) decentralized foundation for the market segmentation.

In our model, a monopolistic producer sells a homogeneous product to a large number of non-negligible consumers, each with a unit demand. The production exhibits constant marginal cost, which can be normalized to zero. The reservation price of each consumer takes the value from a finite set. The timing is as follows. In the first stage, each consumer simultaneously selects one market to join. In the second stage, the producer sees the exact composition of each market segment and offers different take-it-or-leave-it prices in each market. Finally, each consumer makes the purchase decision.

To reconcile the “large number” and “non-negligible” characteristics of consumers, we model the consumer population as a continuum. Meanwhile, we say a
profitable unilateral deviation
exists if a consumer with an infinitesimal positive measure can shift to a different market segment to achieve a strictly higher payoff. Here, the unilateral deviation can change the prices in markets since sellers online typically adopt algorithmic pricing (see e.g.,
23
), and consumers are rational in realizing this fact. We define a market segmentation as
strategy-proof
if it satisfies two conditions: (i) the producer’s strategy assigns an optimal price in every market segment (including off-path ones), and (ii) no profitable unilateral deviations are possible for consumers.

The seller can only implement third-degree price discrimination. Another interpretation is that the seller possesses precise knowledge of the consumers’ valuations but faces regulatory constraints that prevent perfect price discrimination. Instead, the seller is restricted to setting uniform prices within each market segment. For example, certain legal provisions in jurisdictions such as New York and California prohibit the use of specific personal characteristics (e.g., gender) for pricing purposes, even when these traits are correlated with willingness to pay or when individual valuations are observable. Moreover, product prices on Steam are tailored based on users’ IP addresses with no intra-regional price discrimination. In this case, consumers can alter their IP addresses (to low-income countries) through virtual private networks (VPNs) to receive a lower price.
2
2

2

Additionally, technical constraints may also prevent perfect price discrimination at the individual level. In the Steam example, producers can only aggregate data by IP address, and identifying individual consumers is technically impossible.

Our main result is the characterization of the welfare consequences of all strategy-proof market segmentations. With consumers engaging in strategically switching market segments before the producer sets the price, producer surplus is always fixed at the uniform monopoly profit, while consumer surplus can range from the uniform monopoly surplus to the maximum consumer surplus attainable under arbitrary segmentation (known as the
buyer-optimal
outcome). Moreover, no consumer would face a higher price than the uniform monopoly price, suggesting that all consumers are weakly better off.

We know that at least the uniform monopoly and buyer-optimal outcomes are achievable under strategy-proof market segmentation. When there is no market segmentation, the producer will charge the uniform monopoly price. Any deviation would require creating a new market, which is not profitable for a consumer. For the buyer-optimal outcome, we can partition the aggregate market into several
extremal markets
, where every valuation on the support is optimal. The producer merely charges the minimum valuation in each market.
3
3

3

Consider all markets whose optimal price equals the minimum possible willingness-to-pay. Since deviation cannot lead to a lower price, all consumers in these markets have no incentive to leave. Therefore, by iteratively considering markets from the lowest price to the highest one, we confirm that the above construction is strategy-proof.
Prices in extremal markets are sensitive to entry, which enables strategy-proof segmentation to have different prices for different markets.

With the above two attainable outcomes, we construct a family of strategy-proof segmentation to implement any outcome in between.
4
4

4

We cannot immediately conclude that any outcome in between is feasible since a convex combination of strategy-proof market segmentation may not necessarily be strategy-proof.
We start with the buyer-optimal segmentation mentioned above. We then merge markets with the highest prices to create a new market with a share equal to a parameter between zero and one. Assuming that the critical market can be proportionally divided, the consumer surplus in the construction is continuous in the parameter. Moreover, two extreme cases of this construction correspond to the uniform monopoly and buyer-optimal outcomes, respectively. Thus, any consumer surplus in between can be obtained.

Model

A monopolistic producer sells a product to
N
consumers, with a constant marginal cost normalized to zero. Since
N
should be very large in the real world, we can approximate that the seller serves a continuum of consumers for convenience.
8
8

8

We can alternatively study an
N
-consumer model directly without approximation. As
N
grows, the results in an
N
-consumer model will converge to the results in the approximated model with a continuum of consumers, which can be rigorously proven by the epsilon-
N
method. However, the continuum approximation is easier to understand since it avoids tedious rounding procedures.
Consumers are uniformly distributed within
[0,1]
, each with unit demand. Consumers’ willingness-to-pay of the product is determined by a function
V:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
. The range of
V
is finite and it is
\{v_{1},v_{2},\cdots,v_{K}\}
with
v_{k-1}<v_{k}
. We use
\theta\in[0,1]
to denote a representative consumer.

In this setting, a
market

X
is a subset of
[0,1]
. We assume that there are
n
market segments, indexed by
i=1,\dots,n
, where
n\geq K
is exogenously given. A
market segmentation
is a collection of markets
\mathcal{P}=\{X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\}
such that
X_{i}\cap X_{j}=\emptyset
for all
i\neq j
, and
\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}=[0,1]
. Each set
X_{i}
represents the group of consumers who choose segment
i
. We say that a consumer
\theta
is assigned to segment
i
if
\theta\in X_{i}
. The producer observes the composition of each segment and can set different prices across segments.

Timeline.

The timeline of the extensive-form game is summarized as follows.

1.

Each consumer
\theta\in[0,1]
chooses a segment
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
.

2.

This induces a market segmentation
\{X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\}
, where
X_{i}
is the set of consumers assigned to segment
i
. The producer observes the composition of each segment and sets a price for each segment.

3.

Each consumer makes a purchase decision.

Producer’s Strategies.

In stage 2, the producer offers a take-it-or-leave-it price to maximize the profit in each market. We say
p^{*}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
is an
optimal price
of market
X
if

p^{*}\in\arg\max_{p\geq 0}\int_{X}p\mathbb{I}\{V(\theta)\geq p\}d\theta.

Multiple optimal prices might exist for a market. Let
\phi
denote the deterministic pricing strategy of the producer, which maps every market to a non-negative price. If
\phi(X)
is an optimal price of market
X
for any
X\subseteq[0,1]
, the strategy
\phi
is called
rational
.

Consumer’s Strategies.

We only consider deterministic strategies of consumers. In stage 1, each consumer
\theta\in[0,1]
independently chooses one of
n
market segments to join. The segment that consumer
\theta
joins is denoted by
\sigma(\theta)\in\{1,2,\cdots,n\}
. Given strategy profile
\sigma
, we say
\mathcal{P}
is the induced market segmentation if
X_{i}=\{\theta|\sigma(\theta)=i\}
. In stage 3, consumer
\theta
makes a purchase decision if the price offered by the producer
\phi(X_{\sigma(\theta)})
is no larger than the willingness-to-pay
V(\theta)
.

Payoffs.

Let
u,\pi,
and
w
denote the consumer surplus, producer surplus, and total surplus, respectively. Given producer’s strategy
\phi
and consumers’ strategies
\sigma
, the producer surplus is
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi(X_{i})\int_{X_{i}}\mathbb{I}\{V(\theta)\geq\phi(X_{i})\}d\theta
. Meanwhile, the payoff of consumer
\theta
is denoted by
u_{\theta}(\phi,\sigma)=\max\{V(\theta)-\phi(X_{\sigma(\theta)}),0\}
. Hence, the consumer surplus is
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{X_{i}}u_{\theta}(\phi,\sigma)d\theta
; and the social welfare is
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{X_{i}}V(\theta)\mathbb{I}\{V(\theta)\geq\phi(X_{i})\}d\theta
.

For the aggregate market, we make a generic assumption that the optimal uniform price
v^{*}
is unique. We assume
v^{*}>v_{1}
to avoid trivial analyses. Under the uniform price monopoly, we use
u^{*},\pi^{*}
to denote the consumer surplus and the producer surplus, respectively. Let
\overline{w}
denote the maximum social welfare, i.e.
\int_{0}^{1}V(\theta)d\theta
.

Solution Concept.

We first introduce how we model the individual deviation. With
N
consumers, the market share of an individual consumer is
\varepsilon=\frac{1}{N}\rightarrow 0^{+}
, which is small but non-negligible. When approximating discrete consumers with a continuum framework, each individual consumer in the discrete setting corresponds to a positive but infinitesimal measure of consumers in the continuous setting.
9
9

9

This framework can also be understood through the following analogy. In discrete consumer models, each individual is the “smallest” set with a positive market share. Correspondingly, in continuous consumer models, this “smallest” set is represented by an infinitesimal yet positive measure of consumers.

We begin by defining the profitable deviation of a positive measure of consumers. Only when the measure of consumers is positive can such deviation fundamentally change the market segmentation, the prices, the consumer surplus, and the producer surplus.

Definition 1
.

Given the strategy profile
(\phi,\sigma)
, we call a set of consumers
D\subseteq[0,1]
a
profitable deviation set
, if (i) the measure of
D
is positive; (ii) there exists another strategy of consumers
\sigma^{\prime}
such that

•

\sigma^{\prime}(\theta)\neq\sigma(\theta)
if and only if
\theta\in D
,

•

u_{\theta}(\phi,\sigma^{\prime})-c>u_{\theta}(\phi,\sigma)
for all
\theta\in D
.

The parameter
c\geq 0
represents the deviation cost, which is zero during the main analysis. Since we adopt the non-cooperative game-theoretical approach, such a deviation should strictly benefit all consumers in the set
D
, featuring the strong Nash solution.

We then define the profitable unilateral deviation to capture an individual’s deviation.

Definition 2
.

We say there is a
profitable unilateral deviation
if for any
\varepsilon>0
there exists a deviation set with a measure smaller than
\varepsilon
.

Now, we can formally define our solution concept.

Definition 3
.

Given a strategy profile
(\phi,\sigma)
, we say it induces a
strategy-proof
market segmentation
\mathcal{P}
if (i)
\phi
is rational; (ii) there is no profitable unilateral deviation.

The Main Result and the Proof

We first present the main result of this paper and then provide the proof.

Theorem 1
.

There exists a strategy profile
(\phi,\sigma)
that induces strategy-proof segmentation
if and only if
producer surplus
\pi=\pi^{*}
and consumer surplus
u\in[u^{*},\overline{w}-\pi^{*}]
. No positive measure of consumers is worse off compared with the uniform monopoly outcome.

Prior to presenting the comprehensive proof, we illustrate our result and intuitions using a market with consumers having either high (
v_{H}
) or low (
v_{L}
) willingness-to-pay. To avoid trivial analysis, we assume that
v_{H}
is the optimal price without price discrimination. Without loss of generality, the aggregate market is divided into two segments, with consumers freely choosing which segment to join. Let market 1 contain masses
\alpha
and
\beta
of high- and low-type consumers, respectively. If
v_{L}(\alpha+\beta)\geq\alpha v_{H}
, the producer can charge price
v_{L}
in market 1. Since
v_{H}
is the uniform monopoly price, market 2 must include a positive measure of high-type consumers.

We examine whether
v_{L}
in market 1 can be maintained. If so, how is a high-type consumer in market 2 deterred from switching to market 1? First, we find that a profitable unilateral deviation exists when
v_{L}(\alpha+\beta)>\alpha v_{H}
, because a high-type consumer with an infinitesimal measure can join market 1 without affecting its
v_{L}
price. However, when
v_{L}(\alpha+\beta)=\alpha v_{H}
, any such deviation triggers a price increase to
v_{H}
. Consequently, this equality is necessary for a strategy-proof segmentation with price
v_{L}
in market 1. Moreover, for a given
\beta
, the entire market segmentation is uniquely pinned down.

As a result, the producer surplus equals that of the uniform monopoly case, as
v_{H}
remains optimal in market 1. Meanwhile, the consumer surplus depends on the value of
\beta
. At
\beta=0
, the outcome coincides with the uniform monopoly, while at
\beta
equal to the total mass of low-type consumers, the buyer-optimal outcome is achieved. By varying
\beta
, the full spectrum of intermediate outcomes between these two extremes is attainable.

Comparison with Subgame Perfect Equilibrium

In this section, we study subgame-perfect equilibrium and show that strategy-proof market segmentation, as a solution concept, is a refinement of subgame-perfect equilibrium.

Definition 4
.

A strategy profile
(\phi,\sigma)
is a
subgame perfect equilibrium
if (i)
\phi
is rational; (ii) for any
\theta\in[0,1],k\in\{1,2,\cdots,n\}
,

u_{\theta}(\phi,\sigma(\theta),\sigma_{-\theta})\geq u_{\theta}(\phi,k,\sigma_{-\theta})
where
\sigma_{-\theta}
is the strategy profile of all consumers except for consumer
\theta
.

The distinction between strategy-proof segmentation and subgame perfect equilibrium lies in their respective approaches to modeling individual deviations. In the context of strategy-proof segmentation, an individual’s deviation constitutes a minimal deviation set, which consequently alters the canonical representation of market segmentation. In contrast, under the framework of subgame perfect equilibrium, an individual deviation does not modify the canonical representation of market segmentation. Yet, it should be noted that when multiple optimal prices exist within a market, the prevailing price may vary before and after a consumer’s participation in one market.

In the following, we show that any strategy-proof segmentation
\mathcal{P}
can be induced by a subgame-perfect equilibrium, but not vice versa. Thus, strategy-proof segmentation can be regarded as a refinement of the subgame perfect equilibrium.

Proposition 2
.

For any strategy-proof market segmentation
\mathcal{P}
, there exists subgame-perfect equilibrium
(\phi,\sigma)
that induces
\mathcal{P}
.

Concluding Remarks

In this paper, we introduce a solution concept called strategy-proof market segmentation. This concept is proposed to capture the fact that each consumer remains non-negligible whenever moving between different market segments, even if the population of consumers is very large. Strategy-proof segmentation can be viewed as a refinement of subgame perfect equilibrium. Under a strategy-proof segmentation, producer surplus remains at the uniform monopoly level, while consumer surplus is at least the uniform monopoly level. Some strategy-proof segmentations can reach the efficient frontier.

However, there are some limitations regarding implementing our solution concept in practice. First, we assume that the producer is fully rational, a premise that may not hold in real-world scenarios. Specifically, we posit that the producer can accurately derive the exact value distribution of each market segment, and that her pricing algorithm can respond to even minor changes in this distribution by adjusting prices instantaneously. Second, we require that the prices set by the producer be discrete, meaning that any response from the producer must result in a price jump. If these assumptions are violated, the only feasible outcome collapses to the uniform monopoly outcome.

Nevertheless, with technological advancement, the aforementioned limitations are becoming less of a concern. Data-related services empower Internet platforms to aggregate consumer data, enabling real-time estimation of consumer value distribution within market segments. Furthermore, these platforms typically possess substantial computing power and advanced algorithms, which facilitate rational decision-making and allow for immediate responses to consumer actions.
