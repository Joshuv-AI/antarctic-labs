On a Central Limit Theorem and Sanov’s principle for quantum neural networks
Anderson Melchor Hernandez

Abstract
. In this work, we study the fluctuations of a Mixture of Experts (MoE) generated by a quantum neural network trained via gradient flow on supervised learning problems. Our main results establish the Central Limit Theorem (CLT), and Sanov’s principle for an MoE as the number of experts diverges. We demonstrate that the fluctuations of the empirical measure of its parameters close to its corresponding limit probability measure solve a linear transport equation. As a byproduct, we show that the MoE converges to a limit function which solves an evolution equation governed by the neural tangent kernel associated with the quantum neural network.

1.
Introduction

The growing interest in Quantum Machine Learning (QML) has catalyzed extensive research into the training dynamics and generalization behavior of Quantum Neural Networks (QNNs)
[
10
,
32
,
28
,
13
,
19
]
. Among emerging branches of artificial intelligence, QML stands out for its integration of classical machine learning techniques with quantum computing paradigms
[
10
,
32
,
28
]
. The core idea behind QML is to harness quantum algorithms and quantum-mechanical phenomena—such as superposition, entanglement, and quantum parallelism—to enhance the performance of deep learning models
[
4
]
. One of the most prominent QML frameworks is the quantum neural network, which serves as the quantum counterpart to classical deep neural networks. The output of a QNN is defined as the expectation value of a quantum observable measured on the quantum state produced by a parameterized quantum circuit. This circuit comprises tunable one-qubit and two-qubit gates, with parameters encoding both input data and the trainable components of the model
[
1
,
6
]
. These parameters are typically optimized using gradient-based techniques to minimize a cost function and improve the circuit’s ability to process and analyze data
[
17
,
31
,
33
]
.

In this paper, we investigate the fluctuations of a parametric model which may be implemented by a quantum neural network. Let us consider a finite set
\mathcal{X}
of possible inputs, and let
\Theta
represent the vector of circuit parameters. As a parametric model, we consider a function
x\mapstochar\rightarrow f(\Theta,x)
. Suppose we have a training set
\{(x^{(i)},\,y^{(i)}):i=1,\dots,n\}
, where
x^{(i)}\in\mathcal{X}
, and
y^{(i)}\in\mathbb{R}
. The aim of supervised learning is to find parameters
\Theta
such that
f(\Theta,x)
closely matches the labels
y^{(i)}
. It is a customary approach to minimize the empirical quadratic loss

(1.1)

\mathcal{L}(\Theta)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(f(\Theta,x^{(i)})-y^{(i)}\right)^{2}

using gradient-based optimization.
Recent research has investigated quantum neural networks for their potential to combine the computational advantages of quantum mechanics with the expressive power of deep learning models
[
20
]
. Despite these promising developments, fundamental challenges persist—particularly in identifying and efficiently optimizing the parameters of quantum circuits
[
7
]
.

Reference
[
13
]
investigates quantum neural networks trained on supervised learning tasks, where the objective function is defined as the expected value of the sum of single-qubit observables across all qubits, normalized so that its variance at initialization scales as
{\mathcal{O}}(1)
. The authors analyze the training dynamics of these networks and establish their trainability in the infinite-width limit, provided the circuit depth is allowed to scale with the number of qubits
m
and barren plateaus are avoided.
More specifically,
[
13
]
demonstrates that the probability distribution of the function produced by the trained network converges in law to a Gaussian process, with analytically computable mean and covariance
[
13
, Theorem 4.15]
. Building on this result,
[
23
, Theorem 5.1, 5.2]
provides quantitative bounds for the convergence in terms of the Wasserstein-1 distance.
Subsequently,
[
24
]
introduces an efficient classical algorithm for estimating the Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) associated with quantum neural networks composed of arbitrary unitary operators from the Clifford group, interleaved with parametric gates defined by time evolution under Hamiltonians from the Pauli group. The key insight behind the algorithm is that the average over the initialization parameter distribution in the NTK definition can be exactly substituted by an average over just four discrete values, so that the resulting parametric gates are themselves Clifford operations. This substitution enables efficient classical simulation of the quantum circuit.
When combined with prior results establishing the equivalence between wide quantum neural networks and Gaussian processes
[
13
,
23
]
, this approach allows for efficient computation of the expected output of wide, trained quantum neural networks. Consequently, it demonstrates that such architectures cannot achieve quantum advantage.
In this paper, we pursue an alternative perspective on quantum neural network training by analyzing the fluctuations, grounded in the concept of the
m
ean field limit, which has been extensively investigated in the context of classical deep neural networks
[
34
,
26
,
25
,
22
,
21
]
.
Roughly speaking, in the mean field limit, the empirical distribution of a network’s neurons is approximated by a smooth probability measure. From this perspective, each neuron can be interpreted as a particle evolving under a suitable gradient flow in parameter space. As the network width tends to infinity, one can derive a limiting description of the training dynamics in terms of a partial differential equation. More concretely, consider a multilayer feedforward neural network. When the number of neurons per layer grows to infinity—under appropriate scaling of weights and biases—the empirical distribution of neurons in each layer converges to a smooth measure. The function computed by the network then becomes governed by this limiting measure, allowing one to track its evolution through mean-field gradient flow equations. This framework offers a rigorous mathematical foundation for understanding why highly overparameterized networks can often avoid poor local minima, achieve low training error, and even exhibit strong generalization performance
[
22
,
29
,
34
,
3
]
.
Here, we explore the fluctuations of the mean-field viewpoint for quantum neural networks. We consider a uniform mixture of
N
identical experts:

(1.2)

F_{N}(\Theta,x)\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}f(\theta^{i},x),

where
x
is the input,
\theta^{i}
are the parameters of the
i
-th expert, and
f
is the model function of a single expert generated by a quantum neural network. That is, each expert is a parametric quantum circuit with model function
f
defined in (
2.10
) below. When the parameters
\Theta^{N}\coloneqq(\theta^{1},\ldots\theta^{N})
are trained by gradient flow, the result is an updated collection of parameter
\Theta_{t}^{N}\coloneqq(\theta_{t}^{1},\ldots,\theta_{t}^{N})
that define a mixture of experts. This, in turn, yields an updated model function
F_{N}(\Theta_{t},x)
. Previous work has shown that, as the number of experts
N\rightarrow+\infty
, the mixture of experts satisfies a principle of propagation of chaos, with the empirical measure
\mu_{\Theta_{t}^{N}}
converging to the unique solution
\mu_{t}
of a nonlinear continuity equation
[
16
]
.
In this work, we establish the Central Limit Theorem (CLT) for an MoE as the number of experts
N
diverges. We demonstrate that the fluctuations of the empirical measure of its parameters, close to its corresponding limit probability measure, solve a linear transport equation. Specifically, we define the fluctuation term as
\delta_{t}^{N}\coloneqq\sqrt{N}(\mu_{\Theta_{t}^{N}}-\mu_{t})
. We prove that this sequence of measures converges weakly in law to a limiting fluctuation process
\delta_{t}
. In our approach, we make use of a functional setup suitable to prove that the limiting process
\delta_{t}
can be characterized, and that it solves a transport equation.

1.1.
Our results

In this paper, we prove the Central Limit Theorem (CLT) for the sequence
\delta_{t}^{N}
in the limit of infinitely many experts, where the parameters of the QNN evolve according to the gradient flow associated with the minimization of a quadratic cost function in a supervised learning problem. We show that, at any fixed time
t>0
, the limiting process
\delta_{t}
solves a linear transport equation. Without delving into all the technical details, we establish the following informal result (see
Proposition 3.1
, and
Theorem 3.1
for a formal statement).

Theorem 1.1
.

Consider the MoE
F(\Theta,x)
induced by the set of
N
identical experts
\{f(\theta^{i},x):i=1,\ldots,N\}
where
x
represents a generic input,
\Theta\coloneqq(\theta^{1},\ldots\theta^{N})
is the vector of parameters supported on the Torus
\mathbb{T}^{d}
of dimension
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
with period
2\pi
, and
f
is generated by a quantum neural network. Let each component of
\Theta
be initialized by sampling it from the uniform distribution, and let then
\Theta
be trained via gradient flow:

(1.3)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\frac{{\rm d}{\mathcal{L}}(\Theta_{t}^{N})}{{\rm d}t}=-N\nabla_{\Theta}{\mathcal{L}}(\Theta_{t}^{N}),\\
&{\mathcal{L}}(\Theta_{t}^{N})\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(F(\Theta_{t}^{N},x^{(i)})-y^{(i)}\right)^{2}.\end{aligned}

Then, the stochastic process
\delta_{t}^{N}
converges in law to a limit process
\delta_{t}
as
N\rightarrow+\infty
, and whose limit solves the transport equation

(1.4)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\frac{{\rm d}\delta_{t}(\theta)}{{\rm d}t}=-\nabla_{\theta}\cdot(\nabla\mathcal{V}(\theta,\mu_{t})\delta_{t}+\mu_{t}\nabla\mathcal{G}(\theta,\delta_{t})),\\
&\mathcal{G}(\theta,\mu)\coloneqq-{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta^{\prime}\sim\mu}[W(\theta,\theta^{\prime})],\,W(\theta,\theta^{\prime})\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{n}f(\theta,x_{j})f(\theta^{\prime},x_{j}),\\
&V(\theta^{\ell})\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{n}f(\theta^{\ell},x_{j})y_{j},\,\mathcal{V}(\theta,\mu)\coloneqq V(\theta)-{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta^{\prime}\sim\mu}[W(\theta,\theta^{\prime})],\end{aligned}

where
\mu_{t}
is the limit of the empirical measure
\mu_{\Theta_{t}}^{N}
associated to the MoE
F_{N}(\Theta_{t},x)
, and it solves the continuity equation

(1.5)

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}\mu_{t}(\theta)}{{\rm d}t}=-\nabla_{\theta}\cdot\left(\nabla\mathcal{V}(\theta,\mu_{t})\mu_{t}\right).

Our CLT provides a quantitative statement that the fluctuations of
\mu_{\Theta_{t}^{N}}
around
\mu_{t}
are of order
\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}
. This result contrasts with previous analyzes where the proximity between
\mu_{\Theta_{t}^{N}}
and
\mu_{t}
was measured using the Wasserstein distance of order 2, denoted as
{\mathcal{W}}_{2}
(for details on the definition of this distance, see for instance
[
2
,
27
,
30
]
). More precisely, the authors showed that there exists a positive constant
C
depending on the dimension
d
such that

(1.6)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}{\mathcal{W}}_{2}^{2}\left(\mu_{\Theta_{t}^{N}},\mu_{t}\right)\leq C2N^{-\frac{2}{d}}.

Our Central Limit Theorem provides a tighter approximation rate,
O(N^{-\frac{1}{2}})
, for the fluctuations of the empirical measure. Crucially, in the high-dimensional setting where the number of parameters
d
is greater than
4
, the term
N^{-2/d}
dominates the convergence rate of
{\mathcal{W}}_{2}^{2}
, making it slower than the
N^{-1/2}
rate established by our CLT since
2/d<1/2
. Therefore, our result establishes a superior asymptotic rate for the convergence of the empirical measure, especially in the parameter-rich regime typical of practical quantum neural networks. Moreover, we characterize the limit of the fluctuations around
\mu_{t}
as a process solving the equation (
1.4
). Furthermore, as a byproduct of the CLT for the empirical measure, we also establish the convergence of the MoE model function. We define the fluctuations of the MoE output
F_{N}(\Theta_{t},x)
around its mean-field limit
f_{t}(x)\coloneqq{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu_{t}}[f(\theta,x)]
as

(1.7)

\displaystyle\psi_{N}(t,x)\coloneqq\sqrt{N}(F_{N}(\Theta_{t},x)-f_{t}(x))

and show that, in the limit
N\rightarrow+\infty
, this fluctuation process converges in law:

(1.8)

\displaystyle\psi_{N}(t,x)\rightarrow\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}f(\theta,x)\delta_{t}(\theta)\quad\text{in law.}

This result confirms that the
O(1/\sqrt{N})
rate of convergence propagates from the parameter measure to the model’s output, providing the finite-size stochastic effects of the quantum neural network mixture. On the other hand, we establish a large deviation principle for the law of the process
\mu_{\Theta_{\cdot}^{N}}:[0,T]\ni t\mapstochar\rightarrow\mu_{\Theta_{t}^{N}}
. We derive this result by introducing a suitable metric on the space of curves defined on the interval
[0,T]
with values in the space of probability measures on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
. Let us now informally state our large deviations result (see
Theorem 3.2
for a precise formulation).

Theorem 1.2
.

Assume the same setting as in
Theorem 1.1
. Then the law of the empirical process

\mu_{\Theta_{\cdot}^{N}}
satisfies a large deviation principle with speed
N
and an explicit rate functional given by the relative entropy. Furthermore, there exists a
sequence
(\overline{\Theta}_{t}^{N})_{t\in[0,T]}
of independent random variables such that

\mu_{\Theta_{\cdot}^{N}}
and
\mu_{\overline{\Theta}_{\cdot}^{N}}
are exponentially
equivalent at speed
(\log N)^{\beta}
for
0<\beta<1
.

Let us briefly comment on this result. We emphasize that it depends on the weak convergence
of
\mu_{\Theta_{\cdot}^{N}}
. Thus, we first identify the appropriate space in which this
weak convergence holds, in order to apply effectively the abstract result proved in

[
9
]
. On the other hand, the exponential equivalence between
\mu_{\Theta_{\cdot}^{N}}
and
\mu_{\overline{\Theta}_{\cdot}^{N}}
at speed
(\log N)^{\beta}
follows from the explicit bound (
1.6
), which prevents us from obtaining the natural speed of order
N
usually expected in this context (see, for instance,
[
11
, Definition 4.2.10]

2.4.
Quantum neural networks

In what follows, we denote by
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
the number of qubits of the quantum circuit implementing each expert. Let
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
be the Hilbert space of a single qubit. We consider an observable
{\mathcal{O}}
on the Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}=\left({\mathbb{C}}^{2}\right)^{\otimes m}
with
\|{\mathcal{O}}\|\leq 1
.
The model function of each expert is then

(2.10)

\displaystyle f(\theta,x)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left\langle{0^{m}}\right|U^{{\dagger}}(\theta,x){\mathcal{O}}\,U(\theta,x)\left|{0^{m}}\right\rangle,

where
U
is a unitary operation given by the expression

(2.11)

\displaystyle U(\theta,x)\coloneqq V_{d}(x)e^{-\frac{i\theta_{d}\mathcal{G}_{d}}{2}}V_{d-1}(x)\cdots V_{1}e^{-\frac{i\theta_{1}\mathcal{G}_{1}}{2}}V_{0}(x)

where
V_{j}(x)\in\mathcal{L}({\mathcal{H}})

are unitary operations, and
\mathcal{G}_{i}
hermitian operators such that
\left\lVert\mathcal{G}_{i}\right\rVert\leq 1
for all

. Now, we consider the quantum neural network defined as a classical mixture of
N
identical quantum experts with independent parameters.
The corresponding model function is given by

(2.12)

F_{N}(\Theta,x)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\langle{0^{m}}\right|U^{{\dagger}}(\theta^{i},x){\mathcal{O}}\,U(\theta^{i},x)\left|{0^{m}}\right\rangle.

Furthermore, we consider the mean square error that is defined as

(2.13)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}(\Theta)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(F_{N}(\Theta,x_{j})-y_{j}\right)^{2}.

Expanding the squared term of the cost function, we can express
{\mathcal{L}}(\Theta)
as follows:

(2.14)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}(\Theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(F_{N}(\Theta,x_{j})-y_{j}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(y_{j})^{2}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}F_{N}(\Theta,x_{j})y_{j}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left\{\sum_{\ell,k=1}^{N}\frac{f(\theta^{\ell},x_{j})f(\theta^{k},x_{j})}{N^{2}}\right\}

(2.16)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(y_{j})^{2}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{n}f(\theta^{\ell},x_{j})y_{j}+\frac{1}{2N^{2}}\sum_{\ell,k=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{n}f(\theta^{\ell},x_{j})f(\theta^{k},x_{j}).

This expansion separates the total cost into three components: a constant term, a term linear in the expert functions

, and and a term quadratic in them. This decomposition is instrumental for analyzing the mean-field limit of the system. Let us define the following terms to simplify the expression for

(2.17)

\displaystyle V(\theta^{\ell})\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{n}f(\theta^{\ell},x_{j})y_{j},

(2.18)

\displaystyle W(\theta^{\ell},\theta^{k})\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{n}f(\theta^{\ell},x_{j})f(\theta^{k},x_{j}).

Here

represents the linear contribution in the expert functions weighted by the target labels,

. The term

represents the correlation (or interaction) that arises due to the different parameters

,
\theta^{\prime}
in the quadratic part in the experts. Then, the cost function becomes:

(2.19)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}(\Theta)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(y_{j})^{2}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}V(\theta^{\ell})+\frac{1}{2N^{2}}\sum_{\ell,k=1}^{N}W(\theta^{\ell},\theta^{k}).

2.5.
Gradient flow and propagation of chaos

The evolution of the composite parameter vector
\Theta

(2.20)

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}\Theta_{t}}{{\rm d}\,t}=-N\nabla_{\Theta}{\mathcal{L}}(\Theta_{t}),\hskip 5.69046pt\Theta_{t}\in(\mathbb{T}^{d})^{N}.

This is the microscopic dynamic equation for the system of
N

)) with respect to the parameters of the

-th expert,

(2.21)

\displaystyle\partial_{\theta^{i}}{\mathcal{L}}(\Theta)=-\frac{1}{N}\partial_{\theta^{i}}V(\theta^{i})+\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\partial_{\theta^{i}}W(\theta^{i},\theta^{k}).

which allows us to write the flow for the

-th expert’s parameter,

. In the previous work

Propagation of chaos is a crucial phenomenon in many-particle systems with mean-field interaction, where, as the number of particles grows, the behavior of any finite subset of particles becomes increasingly independent and identically distributed. This principle connects microscopic dynamics to macroscopic statistics, demonstrating that the collective evolution of a large system can often be effectively approximated by a limiting equation that governs the distribution of a single particle

15

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathcal{L}(\delta)\right\rVert_{S^{\prime}}\leq C\left\lVert\delta\right\rVert_{S^{\prime}}\hskip 5.69046pt\text{for all $\delta\in S^{\prime}$.}

Proof.

Since

is dense in

with

3.
Central Limit theorems for quantum neural networks

In the next, we are interested to study th asymptotic behavior of
\mu_{\Theta_{t}^{N}}
around its limit
\mu_{t}
, and also to study its relation with those of
F_{N}(\Theta,x)
towards
f_{t}(x)
for any
t\in[0,T]
. Let us define the term

(3.1)

\displaystyle\delta_{t}^{N}\coloneqq\sqrt{N}(\mu_{\Theta_{t}^{N}}-\mu_{t}).

The scaling factor
\sqrt{N}
is introduced because, according to the standard Central Limit Theorem, fluctuations around the mean typically decay at a rate of
N^{-\frac{1}{2}}
. The term
\delta_{t}^{N}
can be expressed as a sum of centered and rescaled Dirac measures:

(3.2)

\displaystyle\delta_{t}^{N}=N^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\delta_{\theta_{t}^{i}}-\mu_{t}\right).

The goal is to prove that this sequence of measures converges weakly in law to a limiting fluctuation process
\delta_{t}
. That is,

(3.3)

\displaystyle\delta_{t}^{N}\rightarrow\delta_{t}\,\text{weakly as $N\rightarrow+\infty$.}

To characterize the limit process, we make use of the continuity equation equation (
2.26
) for the empirical measure
\mu_{\Theta_{t}^{N}}
:

(3.4)

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}\mu_{\Theta_{t}^{N}}}{{\rm d}t}=-\nabla_{\theta}\cdot\left(\nabla\mathcal{V}(\theta,\mu_{\Theta_{t}^{N}})\mu_{\Theta_{t}^{N}}\right).

By subtracting the continuity equation for the limit
\mu_{t}
, and multiplying by
\sqrt{N}
, the evolution of
\delta_{t}^{N}
is derived. So that,

(3.5)

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}\delta_{t}^{N}}{{\rm d}t}

\displaystyle=-N^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla_{\theta}\cdot\left(\mu_{\Theta_{t}^{N}}\nabla\mathcal{V}(\theta,\mu_{\Theta_{t}^{N}})-\mu_{t}\nabla\mathcal{V}(\theta,\mu_{t})\right)

(3.6)

\displaystyle=-N^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla_{\theta}\cdot\left(\left(\mu_{\Theta_{t}^{N}}-\mu_{t}\right)\nabla\mathcal{V}(\theta,\mu_{t})+\mu_{\Theta_{t}^{N}}\left(\nabla\mathcal{V}(\theta,\mu_{\Theta_{t}^{N}})-\nabla\mathcal{V}(\theta,\mu_{t})\right)\right).

The core of the derivation involves linearizing the flux term
\mu\nabla\mathcal{V}(\theta,\mu)
where we have that

(3.7)

\displaystyle\nabla\mathcal{V}(\theta,\mu_{\Theta_{t}^{N}})-\nabla\mathcal{V}(\theta,\mu_{t})=\int\nabla W(\theta,\theta^{\prime})\left(\mu_{\Theta_{t}^{N}}-\mu_{t}\right)({\rm d}\theta^{\prime}),

and that
\mu_{\Theta_{t}^{N}}=\mu_{t}+N^{-\frac{1}{2}}\delta_{t}^{N}
. Let us define

(3.8)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(\theta,\mu)\coloneqq-{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta^{\prime}\sim\mu}[W(\theta,\theta^{\prime})].

Then we can express the continuity equation for
\delta_{t}^{N}
as follows:

(3.9)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{{\rm d}\delta_{t}^{N}}{{\rm d}t}&=-\nabla_{\theta}\cdot\left(\delta_{t}^{N}\nabla\mathcal{V}(\theta,\mu_{t})+\mu_{\Theta_{t}^{N}}\nabla\mathcal{G}(\theta,\delta_{t}^{N})\right)\\
&-\nabla_{\theta}\cdot\left(\delta_{t}^{N}\nabla\mathcal{V}(\theta,\mu_{t})+(\mu_{t}+N^{-\frac{1}{2}}\delta_{t}^{N})\nabla\mathcal{G}(\theta,\delta_{t}^{N})\right).\end{aligned}

The term
N^{-\frac{1}{2}}\delta_{t}^{N}\nabla\mathcal{G}(\theta,\delta_{t}^{N})
is a higher-order term that vanishes as
N\rightarrow+\infty
. The resulting equation for the limit
\delta_{t}
will be a linear, deterministic evolution equation on the space of signed measures. The analysis begins by establishing the convergence of the fluctuation at the initial time
t=0
. The existence of this limit is crucial for proving the Central Limit Theorem for the dynamics of the system, since it suggests that this property of convergence propagates in time when the parameters follow a gradient flow equation.

\displaystyle\sup_{N\in{\mathbb{N}}}\sum_{k\geq 1}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\left\langle\delta_{t}^{N},\phi_{k}\right\rangle^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\Big(1+144(nd)^{2}{\rm vol}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})\,T^{2}\Big)\exp\!\Big(72(nd)^{2}{\rm vol}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})\,T^{2}\Big)

(4.34)

\displaystyle\qquad\times\Big(2\,{\mathbb{E}}\!\left[\|\delta_{0}^{N}\|_{S^{\prime}}^{2}\right]+4(nd)^{2}T^{2}\Big).

This bound implies that there exists
\Omega_{0}\subset\Omega
(
\Omega
represents the ambient probability space) with
{\mathbb{P}}(\Omega_{0})=1
such that for all
\omega\in\Omega_{0}
one has

(4.35)

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq 1}\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\left\langle\delta_{t}^{N},\phi_{k}\right\rangle^{2}<+\infty.

In what follows, we prove that
t\mapstochar\rightarrow\delta_{t}^{N}(\omega)
is right-continuous. Let us fix
\omega\in\Omega_{0}
, and consider
t_{n}\downarrow t
in
[0,T]
. We want to prove that for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
n(\varepsilon)\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that for all
n\geq n(\varepsilon)
one has
\left\lVert\delta_{t_{n}}^{N}(\omega)-\delta_{t}^{N}(\omega)\right\rVert_{S^{\prime}}<\varepsilon
. Indeed by the Parseval identity one has that

(4.36)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\delta_{t_{n}}^{N}(\omega)-\delta_{t}^{N}(\omega)\right\rVert_{S^{\prime}}^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}\left\langle\delta_{t_{n}}^{N}(\omega)-\delta_{t}^{N}(\omega),\phi_{k}\right\rangle^{2}.

By (
4.35
), we then choose
M
large enough such that

(4.37)

\displaystyle\sum_{k>M}\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\left\langle\delta_{t}^{N},\phi_{k}\right\rangle^{2}<\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{6}.

Then,

(4.38)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \left\lVert\delta_{t_{n}}^{N}(\omega)-\delta_{t}^{N}(\omega)\right\rVert_{S^{\prime}}^{2}&=\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}\left\langle\delta_{t_{n}}^{N}(\omega)-\delta_{t}^{N}(\omega),\phi_{k}\right\rangle^{2}\\
&\leq\sum_{k=1}^{M}\left\langle\delta_{t_{n}}^{N}(\omega)-\delta_{t}^{N}(\omega),\phi_{k}\right\rangle^{2}+2\left\{\sum_{k>M}\left\langle\delta_{t_{m}}^{N},\phi_{k}\right\rangle^{2}+\left\langle\delta_{t}^{N},\phi_{k}\right\rangle^{2}\right\}.\end{aligned}

Now since the process
t\mapstochar\rightarrow\left\langle\delta_{t}^{N},\phi_{k}\right\rangle
is right continuous, we can take some
n(k,\varepsilon)
such that for all
n\geq n(k,\varepsilon)
one has

(4.39)

\displaystyle|\left\langle\delta_{t_{n}},\phi_{k}\right\rangle-\left\langle\delta_{t},\phi_{k}\right\rangle|^{2}\leq\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{3M}.

Therefore, by (
4.38
) we conclude that

(4.40)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \left\lVert\delta_{t_{n}}^{N}(\omega)-\delta_{t}^{N}(\omega)\right\rVert_{S^{\prime}}^{2}&\leq\sum_{k=1}^{M}\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{3M}+\frac{2\varepsilon^{2}}{3}\\
&=\varepsilon^{2}.\end{aligned}

Since we can use the reasoning to prove that
t\mapstochar\rightarrow\delta_{t}^{N}(\omega)
is left-continuous, we are done.
∎

Let us also notice that by
Lemma 4.2
, and by Markov inequality we have that the sets

(4.41)

\displaystyle K_{n}\coloneqq\{\psi\in S^{\prime}:\left\lVert\psi\right\rVert_{S^{\prime}}^{2}\leq 2^{n}C\}

where

(4.42)

\displaystyle C\coloneqq\sup_{N\in{\mathbb{N}}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\left\lVert\delta_{t}^{N}\right\rVert^{2}\right]<+\infty

satisfy that for all
n,N\in{\mathbb{N}}

(4.43)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left\{\exists\,t\in[0,T]:\delta_{t}^{N}\notin K_{n}\right\}\leq 2^{-n}.

Furthermore, by Markov inequality, we can prove the following:

Lemma 4.4
.

(4.44)

\displaystyle\lim_{M\rightarrow+\infty}\sup_{N\in{\mathbb{N}}}{\mathbb{P}}\left[\left\{\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}|\left\langle\delta_{t}^{N},\phi\right\rangle|>M\right\}\right]=0.

Proof.

Recall that

(4.45)

\displaystyle\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}|\left\langle\delta_{t}^{N},\phi\right\rangle|\leq\left\lVert\phi\right\rVert_{S}\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\left\lVert\delta_{t}^{N}\right\rVert_{S^{\prime}}.

Applying Markov’s inequality to the random variable
\big(\sup_{t}|\langle\delta_{t}^{N},\phi\rangle|\big)^{2}
, we obtain

(4.46)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\!\left(\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}|\left\langle\delta_{t}^{N},\phi\right\rangle|>M\right)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{P}}\!\left(\big(\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}|\left\langle\delta_{t}^{N},\phi\right\rangle|\big)^{2}>M^{2}\right)

(4.47)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{{\mathbb{E}}\!\left[\big(\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}|\left\langle\delta_{t}^{N},\phi\right\rangle|\big)^{2}\right]}{M^{2}}.

Moreover,

(4.48)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\!\left[\left(\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}|\langle\delta_{t}^{N},\phi\rangle|\right)^{2}\right]\leq\left\lVert\phi\right\rVert_{S}^{2}\,{\mathbb{E}}\!\left[\sup_{t}\left\lVert\delta_{t}^{N}\right\rVert_{S^{\prime}}^{2}\right]\leq\|\phi\|_{S}^{2}\,C.

Hence,

(4.49)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\!\left(\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}|\left\langle\delta_{t}^{N},\phi\right\rangle|>M\right)\leq\frac{\left\lVert\phi\right\rVert_{S}^{2}\,C}{M^{2}},\qquad\forall\,N\in\mathbb{N}.

Taking the supremum over
N
and then the limit as
M\rightarrow+\infty
, we obtain

(4.50)

\displaystyle\lim_{M\rightarrow+\infty}\sup_{N\in\mathbb{N}}{\mathbb{P}}\!\left(\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}|\left\langle\delta_{t}^{N},\phi\right\rangle|>M\right)=0.

∎

Notice that if we prove that for all
\phi\in S
, the process
\{\left\langle\delta^{N},\phi\right\rangle\}_{N\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is relatively compact together to (
4.43
), we obtain that
\delta^{N}
is relatively compact.

Lemma 4.5
.

The following holds true: For all
\phi\in S
, for all
\varepsilon>0
there exists
\delta>0
and
M>0
such that

(4.51)

\displaystyle\sup_{N>M}{\mathbb{P}}\left[\left\{\sup_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle s,t\in[0,T]}}{{|t-s|<\delta}}}\left|\left\langle\delta_{t}^{N},\phi\right\rangle-\left\langle\delta_{s}^{N},\phi\right\rangle\right|\geq\varepsilon\right\}\right]\leq\varepsilon.

Proof.

Fix
\phi\in S
. By the weak formulation we have the integral representation

(4.52)

\displaystyle\left\langle\delta^{N}_{t},\phi\right\rangle-\left\langle\delta^{N}_{s},\phi\right\rangle=\int_{s}^{t}A_{u}^{N}(\phi)\,{\rm d}u,

where

(4.53)

\displaystyle A_{u}^{N}(\phi)=\left\langle\delta_{u}^{N},\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{V}(\cdot,\mu_{u})\cdot\nabla\phi\right\rangle+\left\langle\mu_{u}+N^{-1/2}\delta^{N}_{u},\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{G}(\cdot,\delta^{N}_{u})\cdot\nabla\varphi\right\rangle.

By the estimates already derived in
Lemma 4.1
, there exists a constant
C>0

(depending on
\phi
,
n
, and
T
, but
not
on
N
) such that for every

(4.54)

\displaystyle|A_{u}^{N}(\phi)|\leq C\big(1+\left\lVert\delta_{u}^{N}\right\rVert_{S^{\prime}}\big).

Hence, for
0\leq s\leq t\leq T
,

(4.55)

\displaystyle\left\langle\delta^{N}_{t},\varphi\right\rangle-\left\langle\delta^{N}_{s},\varphi\right\rangle\leq C\int_{s}^{t}\big(1+\left\lVert\delta_{u}^{N}\right\rVert_{S^{\prime}}\big)\,{\rm d}u.

Square both sides and use Jensen inequality to obtain

(4.56)

\displaystyle\big|\langle\delta^{N}_{t},\varphi\rangle-\langle\delta^{N}_{s},\varphi\rangle\big|^{2}\leq C^{2}|t-s|\int_{s}^{t}\big(1+\|\delta^{N}_{u}\|_{S^{\prime}}\big)^{2}{\rm d}u\leq C^{2}|t-s|\int_{0}^{T}\big(1+\|\delta^{N}_{u}\|_{S^{\prime}}\big)^{2}{\rm d}u.

Taking the supremum over
s,t
with
|t-s|<\delta
and then expectation yields

(4.57)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sup_{|t-s|<\delta}\big|\langle\delta^{N}_{t},\varphi\rangle-\langle\delta^{N}_{s},\varphi\rangle\big|^{2}\right]\leq C^{2}\delta\;\mathbb{E}\!\left[\int_{0}^{T}\big(1+\|\delta^{N}_{u}\|_{S^{\prime}}\big)^{2}du\right]\leq C^{\prime}\,\delta\,\Big(1+\mathbb{E}\!\big[\sup_{0\leq u\leq T}\|\delta^{N}_{u}\|_{S^{\prime}}^{2}\big]\Big).

By
Lemma 4.1
and
Lemma 4.2
the quantity
\sup_{N}\mathbb{E}[\sup_{0\leq u\leq T}\|\delta_{u}^{N}\|_{S^{\prime}}^{2}]

is finite. Therefore, there is a finite constant
C
(independent of

) such that

(4.58)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sup_{|t-s|<\delta}\big|\langle\delta^{N}_{t},\varphi\rangle-\langle\delta^{N}_{s},\varphi\rangle\big|^{2}\right]\leq C\,\delta.

Applying Markov’s inequality to
\sup_{|t-s|<\delta}\big|\langle\delta^{N}_{t},\varphi\rangle-\langle\delta^{N}_{s},\varphi\rangle\big|^{2}
,
we get for every

(4.59)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\!\Big(\sup_{|t-s|<\delta}\big|\langle\delta^{N}_{t},\varphi\rangle-\langle\delta^{N}_{s},\varphi\rangle\big|\geq\varepsilon\Big)\leq\frac{C\delta}{\varepsilon^{2}}.

(4.60)

\displaystyle\sup_{N>M}\;\mathbb{P}\!\Big(\sup_{|t-s|<\delta}\big|\langle\delta^{N}_{t},\varphi\rangle-\langle\delta^{N}_{s},\varphi\rangle\big|\geq\varepsilon\Big)\leq\varepsilon.

Proposition 4.1

Let

Then equation (

(4.62)

\displaystyle\delta_{\cdot}\in C([0,T];(\mathbb{W}({\mathbb{T}}^{d}))^{\prime})\quad\text{such that}\quad\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left\lVert\delta_{t}\right\rVert_{(\mathbb{W}({\mathbb{T}}^{d}))^{\prime}}<\infty.

Proof.

Let
\delta_{t}^{(1)}
and

\delta_{0}
,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\xi_{t}=-\nabla\!\cdot\!\big(\nabla\mathcal{V}(\theta,\mu_{t})\,\xi_{t}+\mu_{t}\nabla\mathcal{G}(\theta,\xi_{t})\big),\hskip 5.69046pt\xi_{0}=0.

For every

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\left\langle\xi_{t},\phi\right\rangle=\left\langle\xi_{t},\nabla\mathcal{V}(\theta,\mu_{t})\cdot\nabla\phi\right\rangle+\left\langle\mu_{t},\nabla\mathcal{G}(\theta,\xi_{t})\cdot\nabla\phi\right\rangle.

5.
Conclusions

In this paper we have studied the fluctuations of mixtures of identical experts given by a quantum neural network. We have obtained a CLT when the parameters follows a gradient flow equation. Within such a framework, the parameters of a single expert are considered as the spatial coordinates of a particle, and the overall training is described by the dynamics of a system of particles induced by a vector field in the sense of (
2.22
). As a consequence, a general equation of continuity can be defined. In
Proposition 3.1
, we show that the empirical measure
\mu_{\Theta_{0}^{N}}
associated to the initial parameters, defined in (
2.23
), weakly converges to a Gaussian variable as in the case of the classical CLT for real valued random variables. Furthermore, in
Theorem 3.1
, we show that the stochastic process
\delta_{t}^{N}
converges in law to a limiting process
\delta_{t}
proving that the fluctuations of
\mu_{\Theta_{t}^{N}}
around its mean field limit
\mu_{t}
is of order
\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}
. Differently from
[
13
,
23
]
, the training of the quantum neural network considered here does not happen in the lazy regime, enabling representation learning. Our techniques do not allow us to study the joint limit of infinite depth and width.

Our result open the way to several possible research directions:

•

A critical next step is determining the convergence of the fluctuation process
\delta_{t}^{N}
uniformly in time, and to compare this result with a time-uniform upper bounds for the
{\mathcal{W}}_{2}
Wasserstein distance between the empirical distribution of the parameters and the limit probability measure. Establishing such time-uniform bounds would be crucial, as it would prove that the mean-field approximation holds even for
t\rightarrow\infty
. This is particularly relevant at the end of the training process, when the generated function perfectly reproduces the training examples (i.e., when the loss converges to zero).

•

Extending our results to the setting where the number of parameters of each expert grows with
N
. This setting would allow us to consider the joint limit of infinite depth and width, and to scale the complexity of each expert with
N
. In this setting, the convergence of the empirical distribution of the parameters is ill-defined, and the probability distribution of the generated function would have to be considered.

•

A natural avenue for future research is extending our results to the infinite-dimensional limit, where the parameter dimension
d\rightarrow+\infty
. This extension would require a careful analysis of the continuity equation for the limit measure
\mu_{t}
in the infinite-dimensional space
{\mathbb{R}}^{\infty}
, following approaches such as those considered in
[
18
]
.
