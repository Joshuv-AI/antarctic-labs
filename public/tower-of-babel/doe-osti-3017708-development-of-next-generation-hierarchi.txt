Title: Development of Next Generation Hierarchical Hybrid Cu-Si anode Batteries via
Direct-Ink Writing Application

Background:

Sustainable renewable energy continues to be in dire need to effectively combat global
warming. Emerging technology for electric vehicles/ devices remains in high demand that is
not only lower in cost, more efficient, but safer in comparison to commercial materials on
the market. Although first-generation lithium-ion batteries have exhibited extensive
commercial application, conventional graphite no longer meets this increasing demand as an
efficient anode material. Due to the fact that graphite has a subpar theoretical specific
capacity (372 mAh g-1), thus significant limitations in rate capability (for potential faster
charging at higher C-rates currently commercially available.).

Alternatively, silicon has gained significant attention as a superior candidate to potentially
surpass graphite. Due to silicon’s exceedingly high theoretical capacity (4,200 mAh g-1) in
comparison to standard graphite, its abundance thus in turn it’s low-cost, in addition to
exhibiting a significantly low working potential (< 0.4 V vs Li/Li+). However, one of the
main (and most detrimental) challenges is silicon’s tendency to expand in volume (> 300%)
upon discharge as it begins the lithiation process. As a direct result, it causes not only for the
particles to both crack and pulverize under mechanical stress as the volume continues to
expand and contract during cycling. Upon assembling the cell, it needs to undergo
‘charging’ for initially discharging/ ‘activating’ the cell, otherwise commonly known as the
‘formation’ step. As a result a solid electrolyte interface (SEI) layer begins to form at the
anode surface because some of the electrolyte begins to react during the formation process.
However, this (SEI) layer is deemed as a ‘protective’ interlayer because in theory it prevents
further reaction as the cell continues to cycle. However, due to the volume change it causes
significant degradation at the interface. As cracking starts to occur at the anode surface, it
results in a ‘new’ altered surface with each cycle that causes further reaction with the
electrolyte as a byproduct quickly consuming active Li/ and more electrolyte. Thus,
fracturing this ‘protective layer,’ causing significantly higher impedance as a result, and in
turn a decline in capacity due to active Li-loss. In addition to the active material exfoliating
off from the current collector, further contributing to the steady decline in capacity and
overall performance. [1, 2, 3, 12]

Current state of the art Si-anode batteries on the market range between a maximum content
of 5-10 Si wt%. It has been previously reported Tesla has utilized SiOx-C anodes containing
5 wt% Si within their ‘Model 3/ Model X’ electric vehicles. However, more recent ‘Model
3’ vehicles have started to incorporate 10 Si wt%, in which they were able to increase their
energy density upwards by approximately 30%. Recent effort has been focused on
continuing to increase the wt% of Si being utilized, eventually to 100 wt% of Si, to
maximize the energy density even further.[1, 12]

Motivation:

 1

Several approaches have been explored to address and mitigate the volume expansion issue,
initial effort has focused on optimizing the overall structure of the Si material itself to
withstand structural collapse. Using not only Si, SiOx derivatives, but also Si/C composites:
from bulk Si, porous Si, sandwich-like structures, core-shell, nanowires, nanotubes, and
nanoparticles amongst others. Although, a good start, usually requires complex synthesis
methods for large-scale commercial application. However, the development of metal alloy
composites such as (Si-Cu, Si-Ti, Si-Ni, Si-Fe, Si-Mg, and Si-Al etc) amongst others has
gained significant attention.[4] By alloying the metals it increases not only the mechanical
strength of the material (I.e providing greater structural stability) but also enhances the
overall conductivity as well. Rather than ‘intercalate’ the Li ions into the host structure with
minimal structural change, the lithium ions further react/ alloys with silicon. Causing bonds
to break within the host material, thus causing significant structural change in the overall
framework itself. It is crucial to note the limitations of insertion of Li-ions via intercalation
due to the limited available intercalation sites within the host structure, thus causing a fairly
low specific capacity as a result. [3, 4, 7, 10, 12, 13, 14, 8, 9, 10, 11]

Due to significant structural change in the host material, a higher concentration of Li-ions an
estimated (4.4 neighboring Li atoms per Si atom) is able to be utilized within this (Li22Si5)
phase. It is important to note (Li22Si5) only forms at temperatures ranging between
(415-450℃), thus not commonly observed within standard battery operating conditions.
However, at room temperature, further studies have demonstrated that (Li22Si5) is not the last
phase that is fully lithiated as previously thought, as demonstrated in (Figure 1). Obrovac et
al. was the first to successfully show that if the electrode potential decreases (< 0.05 V vs
Li/Li+) it causes a highly lithiated ‘metastable’ (Li15Si4) phase to form instead.[5] Chevrier et
al. further supports this experimental data by proposing that for every Si atom each has a
neighboring (12) Li atoms. Thus, it has been deemed kinetically more favorable for this
metastable phase to form vs. the more thermodynamically favorable (Li22Si5) phase.[6]
However, once delithiation occurs, the crystalline (Li15Si4) phase then transitions back to its
original amorphous state.[3, 11, 13, 14]

Copper has been deemed an ‘ideal’ metal to alloy with silicon, due to the fact it is: already a
widely used anode current collector, exhibits excellent ductility, and relatively good
conductivity. Although it is electrochemically inactive, it acts as a ‘skeleton host’ that can
further aid in the design of the material. The architecture of different frameworks has been
explored and designed to generate as much ‘free space’ by increasing the pores/ voids to
minimize as much structural collapse as possible. (Figure 2) highlights the extensive work
that has been conducted on lower dimensional structures from: (0D) nanoparticles, (1D)
nanowires, nanofibers, nanotubes, to (2D) thin-film materials. Notably, it appears that
majority of the ‘lower-dimensional’ (0D-2D) composites have shown to exhibit relatively
higher capacities with shorter cycle lifetimes. As opposed to its (3D) counterparts which
have lower capacities with longer lifetimes, despite a few key exceptional outliers for both
cases. Thus, significant attention has been shifted to (3D) frameworks, in effort to continue
to improve the performance. It is also important to note, silicon itself is a poor electronic
conductor, that cannot conduct electrons sufficiently on its own due to its strong covalent
bonds. As a result, the electrons cannot move ‘freely’ within the rigid crystal lattice. Thus,
the different structural dimensions is not only meant to only address the mechanical stress/
degradation induced by the volume expansion, but also the electronic conductivity. As
opposed to the limitations of (0D) materials, (3D) structured materials exhibit greater
‘connectivity’ within the framework itself via electronic percolation. Thus, generating a
more conductive network throughout the material, as another strategy in overcoming this
limitation.[3, 4, 7, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 8, 9, 10, 11]

 ●​ (Figure 8) The coulombic efficiency obtained thus far within a half cell
 configuration determines the ratio of Li-ions stored that is available for discharge vs.

 regarding the battery life: the higher the efficiency, the less degradation observed per
 cycle number.
