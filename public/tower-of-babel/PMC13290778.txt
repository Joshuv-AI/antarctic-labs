From relapse to survival: real-world clinical impact of a multimodal approach in first-time recurrent glioblastoma
Authors: Jean-Baptiste Barbe-Richaud, Hélène Cebula, Julien Todeschi, Marie des Neiges Santin, Seyyid Baloglu, Roland Schott, Mathieu Cotton, Aurélien Blouet, Maxime Bobin, Georges Noël, Laura Somme
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGlioblastoma (GBM) is the most common malignant brain tumor in adults. The current treatment is surgery followed by chemoradiotherapy (CRT) and adjuvant temozolomide (TMZ). Three out of four patients experience recurrence within the year following diagnosis. The management of first GBM recurrence remains challenging with no standardized guidelines. This study aimed to evaluate multimodal strategies combining systemic therapy, radiotherapy, surgery as a potential promising approach to enhance patient outcomes.MethodsThe FROST study is a real-life multicenter study. All patients with primary GBM treated for recurrence after prior CRT were included. The Kaplan–Meier method, Cox regression model, and propensity score were employed.ResultsTwo hundred seventy-one patients were included. One hundred ninety (70%) patients benefited from systemic treatment. Fifty-six patients (21%) received a multimodal approach, and 25 (9%) either radiotherapy (n = 14) or surgery (n = 11) alone. The median OS was 9.9 months (8.2–11.2) from first recurrence. The median OS for systemic treatment was 7.9 months (7.1–10.4) and 12.7 months (10.8–19.6) for the local/multimodal approach (p = 1e− 03). Using local/multimodal treatment as reference, the Cox-stratified hazard ratio (HR) for systemic treatment was 2.64 (1.42–4.89). The multimodal approach was more effective than the local strategy alone, with an OS of 16.2 months (11.2–22.9) versus 9.3 months (8.2–18.7) (p = 0.03), and a multivariate HR of 1.88 (1.05–3.4) for local strategy alone.ConclusionIntegrating systemic therapy, radiotherapy and surgery within a multimodal framework at first GBM recurrence seems to offer meaningful clinical benefit.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11060-026-05635-y.

Introduction

Central nervous system (CNS) tumors represent approximately 1% of all newly diagnosed cancers [1]. The fifth edition of the World Health Classification (WHO) classification of CNS tumors, published in 2021, redefines the nomenclature by the addition of molecular features to histological examination to determine the tumor family and grade [2].

Glioblastoma (GBM), defined as an isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH) wild-type de novo grade 4 tumor, is the most common malignant brain tumor in adults and usually occurs after 65 years of age [3]. The management of this grade 4 tumor is based on a multimodal approach that combines as much resection as possible followed by concomitant radiotherapy and temozolomide (TMZ) (i.e., chemoradiotherapy or CRT) followed by adjuvant TMZ [4, 5]. The extent of previous surgical resection and O6-methylguanine–DNA methyltransferase (MGMT) status are key prognostic factors [6, 7].

Little progress has been made since the advent of CRT as the cornerstone of treatment. Recently, Tumor Treating Fields, improved progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS), with a median OS of 20.9 months versus 16.0 months (HR = 0.63, 95% CI, 0.53–0.76) [8].

However, tumor recurrence occurs in approximately 75% of patients within the year following diagnosis and is responsible for a poor prognosis, with a median OS of less than 2 years (4,7).

Management of GBM recurrence has been challenging with no strict guidelines. Chemotherapy regimens based on irinotecan or lomustine combined with bevacizumab, a monoclonal antibody targeting vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), are commonly used [9, 10], as well as TMZ rechallenge [11].

For a subset of patients, local treatment based on reirradiation (Re-RT) or repeat surgery is performed and is sometimes followed by systemic therapy [12, 13].

To date, few data comparing different strategies for the management of recurrence are available. Herein the FROST study, a retrospective study aimed to evaluate the impact of multimodal versus single therapeutic approaches on survival outcomes in patients with recurring GBM.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

The FROST study is a real-life, retrospective and multicenter study conducted in two centers in France (Institut de Cancérologie Strasbourg Europe and Centre d’Oncologie du Pays Basque). All patients with a histologically confirmed diagnosis of GBM between January 2017 and December 2023 were identified through registry data. The inclusion criteria were as follows: age > 18 years, de novo grade 4 IDH-wild-type glioblastoma according to the 5th edition of the WHO classification of CNS tumors, and initial treatment with a surgical approach (biopsy, partial or complete resection) followed by chemoradiation according to the Stupp or Perry protocol (i.e., 75 mg/m² TMZ combined with 60 Gy in 30 fractions, i.e., long-course radiation, or 40 Gy in 15 fractions, i.e., short-course radiation, followed by adjuvant TMZ (150 mg/m² to 200 mg/m², 5 days of a 28-day cycle, up to 6 or 12 cycles)), and documented recurrence or progression that was treated with either systemic treatment (i.e., chemotherapy and/or monoclonal antibody) and/or radiotherapy (Re-radiotherapy; Re-RT) and/or surgery. All patients were presented in a multidisciplinary team meeting that includes neurooncologists, radiation therapists, neurosurgeons, neuro radiologists and nuclear radiologists to assess the recurrence and its management based on morphological pattern of recurrence (i.e. lobe of the brain, size), clinical features (i.e. ECOG, neurological deficits) and re-irradiation and surgery eligibility. Because of the modification of the 2021 WHO nomenclature, patients with a previous diagnosis of IDH-mutant GBM and secondary GBM were excluded.

Objectives

The objective of this study was to determine the optimal therapeutic approach in terms of OS at the first recurrence. The following treatments were evaluated: an exclusive systemic treatment (i.e., temozolomide rechallenge, other chemotherapeutic agent and/or bevacizumab), local monotherapy (e.g., radiotherapy or surgery), an exclusive local strategy (surgery or radiotherapy), and a multimodal approach including surgery and/or radiotherapy and systemic treatment.

Overall survival was assessed each time based on the time between the beginning of the initial CRT until death from any cause and the time between the start of therapy for tumor relapse and death from any cause.

Treatment at relapse

Intensified TMZ was defined as 150 mg/m² 7 days of a 14-day cycle [14]. Standard dose of TMZ was defined as 200 mg/m², 5 days of a 28-day cycle Bevacizumab was administered at 15 mg/kg (21-day cycle) or 10 mg/kg (14-day cycle). Irinotecan was administered at 125 mg/m2 (with bevacizumab at 10 mg/kg, 14-day cycle), and lomustine was administered at 90 mg/m² (42-day cycle, with bevacizumab 10 mg/kg every 15 days). Bevacizumab monotherapy was administered at 10 mg/kg every 15 days and lomustine monotherapy at 90 mg/m² (42-day cycle). Re-RT was delivered depending on the physician’s choice and dosimetry reports.

Covariates / definitions

Recurrence was defined as a composite of imaging and clinical features leading the physician to modify the specified Stupp or Perry protocol after the review of a multidisciplinary team. Patients diagnosed with GBM recurrence were considered to have surgery (partial or complete) in the ‘local’ or ‘multimodal’ group only if the pathological examination revealed residual tumor; otherwise, it was not considered (i.e., biopsy, surgical specimens with no tumor infiltration or only radionecrosis were excluded). Complete surgical resection was defined as surgical resection with no residual tumor or contrast enhancement on postoperative magnetic resonance imaging (MRI); otherwise, partial resection was considered. Multifocal GBM was defined as the presence of at least two separate lesions. To take into account the time to recurrence as a factor [15], we considered the median time of recurrence observed in our cohort as the reference. Patients presenting a recurrence before the median were defined as ‘early recurrence’, and those relapsing after the median as ‘late relapse’.

Statistical analysis

Patients were followed until death or data cutoff on April 1, 2025.

The Kaplan–Meier method was used to estimate survival outcomes. The log-rank test was used to compare OS between groups. The Greenwood formula was employed to estimate the associated confidence interval.

Comparisons of the baseline characteristics and clinical features were performed via the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test (for categorical variables) and t test (for continuous variables).

Sensitivity analyses were performed on our primary outcome; first, we used a Cox regression model, adjusting for potential confounding factors (i.e., prognosis factors), which included age (considered a quantitative variable), MGMT status, type of surgical resection (biopsy versus partial/complete surgery), ECOG performance status (0–1 versus 2 and more), radiotherapy schema (long course versus short course) and time of relapse (considered a qualitative variable dichotomized at the median time for relapse in our cohort). The validity of this model (i.e., proportional hazard assumption and number of variables) was verified. All patients had full available data for the Cox models variables, except for MGMT status. Patients with missing MGMT status data were excluded from the Cox regression model.

Second, we applied a propensity score approach after excluding patients with missing data. Covariates included in the model were chosen on the basis of univariate analysis conducted on the outcome (survival). The “nearest” method with a 0.1 caliper was applied (“matchit” package) before the use of a new Cox stratified regression model on the matched sample. An exhaustive description of the procedure is available in the Supplementary Appendix (Fig. 1, Supplementary Appendix).

The significance level for all analyses was p < 0.05. All the statistical analyses and plots were performed with R software (version 4.3.2).

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the institutional review board of each participating center. Informed consent was obtained from all patients prior to their inclusion in the study (IRB 2024-23, Institut de Cancérologie de Strasbourg Europe). A multi-site agreement was signed by both institutions. The Institut de Cancérologie de Strasbourg Europe (ICANS) is committed to adhering to the reference methodology MR-004 published by CNIL.

Results

Patient inclusion

Three hundred ninety-nine patients were identified, among whom 271 (68%) had recurrent GBM and underwent a specific treatment; 190 patients (70%) benefited from systemic therapy, 25 patients (9%) benefited from a local strategy and 56 patients (21%) from a multimodal strategy.

Systemic therapy included a TMZ-based regimen for 60 patients (30%). One hundred thirty patients benefited from other systemic regimens (70%), including irinotecan combined with bevacizumab for 90 patients (69%) and lomustine combined with bevacizumab for 27 patients (21%). Ten patients (8%) received bevacizumab and three patients (2%) lomustine as monotherapy.

A multimodal approach was used for 56 patients (69%), among whom surgery associated with systemic therapy was predominant (n = 25, 45%), followed by surgery associated with radiotherapy (n = 12, 21%), surgery associated with radiotherapy and systemic therapy (n = 11, 20%) and radiotherapy associated with systemic therapy (n = 8, 14%). Twenty-five patients (21%) received only local treatment, which was radiotherapy for more than half of them (n = 14). Re-RT was performed for 45 patients (combined or not), among whom 37 (82%) received 37.5 Gy in 6 fractions.

Patient characteristics

The patients were mostly men (n = 177, 65%), with a median age of 64 years (Q1–Q3, 55–71). Glioblastoma was frontal or temporal for 74% of the patients. MGMT was methylated in 41% (n = 110) of the patients. Two hundred twenty-five patients (83%) received long-term radiation (Stupp protocol) after surgery. One hundred twenty-three patients (45%) benefited from partial resection, 72 (27%) benefited from complete resection, whereas 76 (28%) benefited only from biopsy. The full baseline characteristics are described in Table 1.

Comparisons of baseline characteristics between patients who received local or multimodal treatment and those who received only a systemic approach revealed a significant difference in terms of MGMT status and type of surgery (partial or complete resection versus surgical biopsy) (p = 0.02 and p < 1e− 03, respectively). See Table 1.

The median time between the beginning of CRT and the initiation of subsequent therapy for the entire cohort was 9.2 months (Q1–Q3, 5.8–14.1), 7.8 months (Q1–Q3, 5.2–11.1) for exclusive systemic treatment and 12.5 months (Q1–Q3, 9.1–23.1) for the local and multimodal strategies (p < 0.05).

Overall survival

At the data cutoff, 232 patients had died (86%). The median OS from the initiation of CRT in the entire cohort was 20.6 months (95% CI, 18.5–23.3) and 9.9 (95% CI, 8.2–11.2) from the first confirmed recurrence (Fig. 2. A et and 2. B).

Systemic versus local/multimodal strategies at recurrence

The median OS from the initiation of CRT for patients who received only systemic treatment at recurrence (n = 190) was 17.8 months (95% CI, 16.1–20.5), whereas it was 31.6 (95% CI, 25.6–35.8) for patients who received local or multimodal treatment (n = 81) (p = 4e− 08) (Fig. 3A).

The median OS from the first confirmed recurrence for patients who received only systemic therapy was 7.9 months (95% CI, 7.1–10.4), whereas it was 12.7 (95% CI, 10.8–19.6) for patients who received local or multimodal treatment (p = 1e− 03) (Fig. 3B).

A Cox regression model adjusted for confounders was constructed for 219 patients. Systemic treatment at first recurrence was associated with an unfavorable outcome (HR 1.62, 95% CI 1.2–2.3; p = 0.01) compared with the use of a local/multimodal strategy from the start of CRT (Fig. 2, Supplementary Appendix). However, statistical significance was not reached when the same model was applied from the beginning of first-line therapy (HR 1.41, 098–2.03; p = 0.06) (Fig. 3, Supplementary Appendix).

The propensity score was calculated for 219 patients following a “nearest” approach. The HRs for death calculated from the stratified Cox regression were 1.89 (95% CI, 1.07–3.34) and 2.64 (1.42–4.89) for the use of the systemic strategy alone at recurrence compared with the local strategy, from the start of CRT (p = 1e− 03) and from the time of recurrence (p = 0.03), respectively.

Local strategy alone versus multimodal strategy at recurrence

The median OS from the beginning of CRT for patients who received only local treatment (surgery or Re-RT) was 22.4 months (95% CI, 19.4–35.8), whereas the OS for patients who received multimodal treatment was 33.4 (95% CI, 28.1–44.1) (p = 0.04) (Figs. 4A, Supplementary Appendix).

The median OS from the initiation of therapy at recurrence for patients receiving only local treatment (surgery or Re-RT) was 9.3 months (95% CI, 8.2–18.7) versus 16.2 (95% CI, 11.2–22.9) for patients receiving multimodal treatment (p = 0.03) (Figs. 4B, Supplementary Appendix).

The Cox regression model calculated with the same prognosis factors for 75 patients depicted HRs for death of 1.96 (95% CI, 1.06–3.61) and 1.88 (95% CI, 1.05–3.4) for the local strategy alone compared with the multimodal local strategy from the start of CRT (p = 0.03) and from the start of the first recurrence (p = 0.03), respectively (see Figs. 5 and 6, Supplementary Appendix). Systemic therapy versus multimodal strategies at recurrence

The median OS from the beginning of the CRT for patients who benefited from systemic therapy was 17.8 months (95% CI, 16.1–20.5) and 33.4 (95% CI, 28.1–44.1) for those who received multimodal treatment (p = 6e− 08).

The median OS from recurrence for patients treated with systemic treatment was 7.9 months (95% CI, 7.1–10.4) versus 16.2 (95% CI, 11.2–22.9) for patients who benefited from a multimodal approach. (p = 2e− 04).

The Cox regression model (n = 195) revealed hazard ratios (HRs) for death of 1.81 (95% CI, 1.2–2.8; p = 6e− 03) from the beginning of CRT and 1.68 (95% CI, 1.1–2.57; p = 0.02) from recurrence, indicating that systemic treatment alone was not as effective as the multimodal approach. Discussion

The results of this study suggest that a multimodal approach, including different treatment modalities (surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy), may be the best strategy for first-time recurrent GBM.

Local strategies, especially a multimodal approach, including local treatment combined with systemic treatment (mainly TMZ in our cohort), were superior to systemic treatment alone or local treatment alone, aligning with the Stupp or Perry protocol of combined treatment modalities. However, owing to the retrospective nature of this study, potential selection biases may affect the generalizability of the findings. We also mixed all the combination of the treatment modalities, thus preventing us from precisely exploring the most effective combination of treatments. In addition, almost one-third of the patients (n = 76) in the cohort benefited initially from surgical biopsy because the topography of the GBM prevented resection. Apart from the poor prognosis of those patients, they could not technically benefit from surgical resection at recurrence, as patients who presented poor ECOG scores or leptomeningeal dissemination. In our cohort, there were no difference according to ECOGS status between patients who received a multimodal/local treatment compared to those who received only a systemic treatment. The main differences were MGMT status, age, and Stupp or Perry protocol at frontline strategy. This suggests that only a specific population would be eligible for a local treatment (and thus multimodal), based on pattern of recurrence and also on clinical and biological data, excluding frail patients and extensive recurrence to receive a multimodal management. We acknowledge that our Cox model evaluating the benefit from multimodal/local treatment compared to systemic treatment from the beginning of first-line therapy didn’t reach significance (HR 1.41, 098–2.03; p = 0.06). This result is possibly linked to a type II error (false negative) due to our sample size. Despite not exceeding the benchmark of 0.05, the trend of the p value and the HR are both in favor of a meaningful benefit from local/multimodal strategy. Additionally, the ‘complete case analysis’ method was applied for our Cox models (i.e. exclusion of patients with missing MGMT status) and may have led to a selection bias.

Finally, the definition of progression leading to a new treatment intervention could be a source of bias. Nonetheless, we considered the most relevant prognostic factors in GBM from the literature to adjust all Cox models and used a propensity score approach for sensitivity analysis in addition to log-rank tests and focused only on overall survival.

With no strict guidelines, the management of GBM recurrence has always been challenging. Many therapeutic innovations fail to revolutionize outcomes. One of the main challenges remains the blood–brain barrier (BBB), which prevents penetration of therapeutic agents [16]. Small chemotherapeutic agents, such as TMZ and nitrosoureas, are able to cross the barrier, and these agents are important for effective chemotherapy in GBM [10, 11]. Antiangiogenic agents, alone or in combination with other drugs, are still among the cornerstones of GBM recurrence treatment [17, 18]. Bevacizumab is the most commonly used antiangiogenic agent because of the high toxicity and/or poor efficacy of other antiangiogenic agents [19]. When combined with radiotherapy at recurrence, bevacizumab appears to be safe and effective [20]. However, a phase II trial did not report improved OS with the addition of bevacizumab to reirradiation in patients with GBM recurrence [21]. Other targeted therapies have been explored, mainly those targeting the Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor (EGFR) because of the overactivation of the EGFR pathway in half of GBM patients [22] as well as EGFR mutations, most notably the EGFRvIII mutation, that affect exons 1 to 8 [23]. None of the trials, including those involving tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs), monoclonal antibodies or antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs), reported improved outcomes [24–26]. Immune checkpoint inhibitors, especially programmed death-1 (PD-1) inhibitors, have also been evaluated as monotherapies or in combination with chemo-radiation or bevacizumab as well as in patients with recurrent GBM for whom no benefit has yet been identified [27–29]. Novel therapeutics, including vaccines show controversial results [30, 31]. Chimeric antigen receptor-T (CAR-T) cells, which target EGFR and/or Interleukin-13 receptors, are promising [32, 33]; however, challenges concerning safety and cost remain [34].

Glioblastoma is characterized by its invasive nature; therefore, recurrence mostly occurs locally, making focal treatment a solid option. Owing to both its efficient and noninvasive characteristics, reirradiation is a strong option for recurrence; however, it is limited by risks including damage to healthy brain tissue, radionecrosis and neurological toxicity. New advances in radiation science have been made both in the delineation of target volumes and dose delivery, making Re-RT effective and safe [12, 35]. Re-irradiation could be limited by larger recurrence or multifocal dissemination. A prospective study by Tong et al. in 2024 revealed 74 patients who underwent Re-RT with a median PTV of 135 cm3, with both improved OS and no major toxicity, underscoring its feasibility [36]. What remains uncertain is the benefit derived from the combination of bevacizumab and Re-RT [21, 37]. In nonrandomized studies, bevacizumab is given in combination mainly with Re-RT with large PTVs [36, 38], with no specific clinical trial aiming to evaluate its benefit in cases of PTV > 100 cm3 (median PTV of 54 cm3 in the RTOG12025 trial [21]). Given the well-demonstrated benefit of radiotherapy in GBM management, ongoing studies are evaluating the potential of new radiation techniques as intraoperative and FLASH radiotherapy [39, 40].

Another goal is to repeat surgery in patients with recurrent GBM. The literature is unanimous about the benefit in OS [41–44]. However, patients should be carefully selected for major surgery (MGMT, ECOG and time to initial diagnosis) [45]. Little evidence in the literature is in favor of the combination of bevacizumab with surgery [46–48]. In terms of adjuvant chemotherapy, a retrospective study, suggested a potential benefit from adjuvant TMZ after repeat surgery [49]. The ongoing phase II RESURGE trial (NCT 02394626) will explore the benefit of surgery followed by adjuvant chemotherapy versus systemic treatment in patients with recurrent GBM.

In the future, prospective trials should aim to explore the effectiveness of multimodal treatment of first-time recurrent GBM.

Conclusion

This real-world study highlights the promising clinical outcomes of multimodal strategies in first-time recurrent GBM, in which local and/or systemic are combined. Prospective trials comparing combined strategies (i.e. systemic treatment associated with surgery or radiotherapy) to a single one (i.e. surgery or radiotherapy alone) are essential to further optimize treatment strategies and improve outcomes in GBM patients.
