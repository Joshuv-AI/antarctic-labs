Single-cell analyses identify the ginseng embryonic protoderm as a native compartment for high-efficiency ginsenoside production
Authors: Juan Liu, Ning Zhai, Shiyi Zhang, Yosuke Tamada, Tonghui Li, Linfan Zhang, Tong Chen, Chenglin Wang, Jian Yang, Jiaqi Gao, Xiang Li, Junhui Zhou, Yonghong Zhang, Yu Liu, Yuan Wang, Jiří Friml, Eva Benková, Chen Li, Lin Xu, Luqi Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ginseng (Panax ginseng) derives its renowned therapeutic properties from ginsenoside metabolites. However, the long cultivation cycle and susceptibility to diseases hinder the advancement of the ginseng industry. Here, we demonstrate that the embryonic protoderm of ginseng can efficiently produce ginsenosides. Single-cell transcriptome and mass spectrometry imaging analyses reveal that ginsenosides accumulate in the protoderm of ginseng embryonic callus (EC) at levels comparable to those in forest ginseng. Epigenetic analyses indicate that elevated histone acetylation and enhanced chromatin accessibility at regeneration- and ginsenoside metabolism-related gene loci are associated with the ginsenoside-producing capacity of EC. Increasing histone acetylation levels or overexpressing the regeneration-related WUSCHEL-RELATED HOMEOBOX11 (WOX11) gene further enhances ginsenoside production in EC. Our findings suggest that the protoderm of EC could serve as an in situ biological compartment for high-efficiency ginsenoside producion, offering a complementary approach to traditional ginseng cultivation.

Introduction

Plant natural products serve as key sources for pharmaceuticals, food additives, and novel biofuels, making them a major focus in cell factory research1. Optimizing biotechnological manufacturing alternatives for plant natural products is essential to protect endangered medicinal plants, lower production costs, and ensure sustainability2–4.

Ginsenosides, predominantly obtained from Panax ginseng C. A. Mey5,6, comprise three core types: protopanaxadiol (PPD)-type, protopanaxatriol (PPT)-type, and oleanane (OA)-type, all valued for their wide-ranging pharmacological effects7,8. Notably, ginsenoside Ro exhibits anti-inflammatory and anti-obesity effects9,10, whereas chikusetsusaponin IVa demonstrates osteoprotective and anti-aging properties11,12. Additionally, ginsenoside Re shows anti-diabetic, neuroregulatory, and anti-cancer activities13, while Rh1 possesses immunomodulatory, antioxidant, and cardiovascular benefits14. Due to these diverse health benefits, ginseng has become a high-value crop, with its global demand rising significantly in recent years. Nonetheless, the cultivation of ginseng faces substantial challenges, such as lengthy harvest cycles (usually 4 to 25 years), strictly optimized cultivation conditions, replanting failures, susceptibility to pathogens, and environmental stressors, all of which severely hinder the development of ginseng industry15,16. To address these issues, eco-friendly and cost-effective ginseng cell cultures have already been utilized across multiple industries, including food, cosmetics, and pharmaceuticals3,17. However, ginseng cell cultures face several key challenges3, including limited cell regeneration capacity and low yields of ginsenosides and other secondary metabolites, which ultimately prevent industrial-scale production.

Many secondary metabolites exhibit compartmentalization in various cell types, which may serve as a protective mechanism for plants against the toxicity of such compounds18. Investigating the cell types responsible for compartmentalizing plant secondary metabolites can help elucidate regulatory mechanisms and improve their production in ginseng cell cultures. Ginsenoside biosynthesis has been reported to involve multicellular compartmentalization19; however, the original cell types involved in this compartmentalization remain unclear. Somatic embryogenesis, an asexual developmental process whereby somatic cells differentiate into embryo-like structures20,21, serves as an efficient platform for enhancing the production of plant natural products through genetic engineering22. Ginseng embryogenic cultures, first established in 198023, exhibit unique regenerative potential and ginsenoside-producing ability23,24. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying embryonic callus (EC) regeneration and its capacity to produce pharmaceutical metabolites in ginseng remain largely unexplored.

Studies from the model plant Arabidopsis thaliana indicate that auxin rapidly reprograms the cellular totipotency network through chromatin accessibility remodeling to drive somatic embryogenesis25, during which several master transcriptional factors associated with the auxin pathway orchestrate developmental processes26, notably including AGAMOUS-LIKE15 (AGL15)27, FUSCA3 (FUS3)28, LEAFY CONTYLEDON1/2 (LEC1/2)28,29, ABSCISIC ACID INSENSITIVE3 (ABI3)28, BABY BOOM (BBM/PLT4)28, PLETHORA TFs (PLTs)30, WUSCHEL (WUS)31, WUSCHEL RELATED HOMEOBOX TFs (WOXs)32, LATERAL ORGAN BOUNDARIES DOMAIN 40 (LBD40)33, INDOLE-3-ACETIC ACID INDUCIBLE30 (IAA30)34, and YUCCA35. In addition, epigenetic regulations, particularly histone acetylation, are highly involved in somatic embryogenesis to regulate the transcription of key genes36.

Here, we obtained ginseng EC and non-embryonic callus (NEC) from leaves, showing distinct capacities for regeneration and ginsenoside profiles. Single-cell and multi-omics analyses revealed that ginsenoside biosynthesis is epigenetically regulated during cell reprogramming, providing a molecular foundation for innovative tissue culture systems and ginsenoside production optimization.

Results

Ginseng embryonic callus produces ginsenosides

To test the possibility of using callus as the ginsenoside-producing cell-cultured tissue, we cultured ginseng leaf on the Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium supplemented with 2 mg/L of 1-naphthaleneacetic acid (NAA) and 0.5 mg/L of 6-benzylaminopurine (6-BA) and obtained the leaf-derived callus (Fig. 1a). The leaf-derived callus was further cultured under two conditions. In the first culture conditions using MS medium with 2.0 mg/L of 2,4-dichlorophenoxyacetic acid (2,4-D) and 0.5 mg/L of kinetin (KT), we obtained white and translucent NEC, which had a loose structure and lacked the capacity to regenerate shoots. In the second culture conditions with the hormone-free MS medium for 5 months, we obtained the EC, characterized by its hard, yellow appearance and continuous production of somatic embryos (Fig. 1a; Supplemental Fig. S1a, b). The somatic embryos from EC could further develop to form the shoot upon the application of 0.1 mg/L NAA and 0.5 mg/L KT (Supplemental Fig. S1c). We then tested the ginsenoside-producing ability of EC and NEC using the forest ginseng and garden ginseng as the controls. The ultra-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS/MS) analysis showed that the total content of ginsenosides of EC is similar to that of the forest ginseng, and significantly higher than that of garden ginseng and NEC (Fig. 1c). Among various types of ginsenosides, EC contained higher levels of the primary ginsenoside Re (Fig. 1d), OA-type ginsenosides (including Ro and Iva) (Supplemental Fig. S1d), and minor ginsenosides (Rg2, Rh1, F2, and F1) (Supplemental Fig. S1e) compared to forest ginseng, and significantly higher levels than garden ginseng or NEC. Regarding total polysaccharides, EC’s content was significantly higher than that of NEC but lower than that of forest ginseng and garden ginseng (Supplemental Fig. S1f). Furthermore, EC contained significantly higher levels of total flavonoids than all other samples (forest ginseng, garden ginseng, and NEC) (Supplemental Fig. S1g).

This data suggests that EC might serve as a potential artificial tissue that is able to produce ginsenosides similar to those of the forest and garden ginseng but with higher efficiency.

Ginsenosides are produced in the embryonic protoderm

To analyze the molecular and cellular basis of effective ginsenoside production in EC but not in NEC, we performed RNA-seq and single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) of EC and NEC.

The marker gene analysis revealed that the cell cluster 0 as unknown cells because of low number of expressed genes and lack of the qualified marker genes, the cell cluster 1 as the ground tissue expressing the marker gene FATTY ACID DESATURASE 2 (FAD2)44, the cell cluster 2 as the protoderm expressing the marker genes ARABIDOPSIS THALIANA MERISTEM LAYER1 (ATML1)45 and PROTODERMAL FACTOR2 (PDF2)46, the cell clusters 3 to 5 as the embryonic meristems expressing the marker genes PLT3B47, ANT147, WOX11A46, WOX11B46, ABI3B48, PLT149 and AIL149, the cell cluster 6 as the G2/M phase cells expressing the marker genes α-tubulin (TUA2)50 and Cyclin-Dependent Kinase Subunit 2 (CKS2)51, the cell cluster 7 as the S phase cells expressing the marker genes histone H2A gene (HTA2)50 and histone H3 gene (H3.1)50, and the cell clusters 8 and 9 identities were currently unknown (Fig. 2e, f; Supplemental Figs. Enhanced chromatin accessibility ensues ginsenoside production

Chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by sequencing (ChIP-seq) analysis showed that the genome-wide H3K9ac modification was enriched around the transcription start site (TSS) in EC but not in NEC (Fig. 4b). Detailed analysis of the ChIP-seq data showed that H3K9ac modification was highly enriched on the gene loci involved in the ginsenoside metabolism such as β-AS1, CYP716A52v2B, CYP716A47A, and CYP716A47B as well as the gene loci involved in regeneration such as WOX11B, PLT3A, FUS3, and BBM (Fig. 4c).

Since histone acetylation may change the chromatin accessibility, we then tested the chromatin accessibility level by the assay for transposase-accessible chromatin with sequencing (ATAC-seq) of EC and NEC. The result showed that the overall chromatin accessibility level was higher in EC than in NEC (Fig. 4d), and many of the ginsenoside metabolism-related genes and regeneration-related genes exhibited a higher chromatin accessibility level in EC than in NEC (Fig. 4e, f).

The above data indicate that EC harbors a high histone acetylation level and high chromatin accessibility levels on ginsenoside metabolism-related genes and regeneration-related genes as compared with NEC. This might be a chromatin basis that ensures embryonic identity and ginsenoside metabolism in EC.

Embryonic protoderm as an in situ ginsenoside biosynthetic compartment

Based on the chromatin analysis and regeneration-related gene analysis, we strived to artificially manipulate the embryonic protoderm of EC as an in situ biological compartment to further increase the ginsenosides’ production.

First, we applied the curcumin (CUR), a histone acetyltransferase inhibitor55, and Trichostatin A (TSA), a histone deacetylase inhibitor56, on EC and NEC. The result showed that TSA treatment was able to upregulate the ginsenoside biosynthesis-related gene expression and promote ginsenoside production in both EC and NEC after ten days, while CUR treatment could downregulate the ginsenoside biosynthesis-related gene expression and inhibit ginsenoside production in EC (Fig. 5a, b; Supplemental Fig. S24). This confirms that histone acetylation levels in EC may be a key factor that controls ginsenoside production. Second, we overexpressed WOX11B as a tool to promote the regeneration abilities of EC. Indeed, WOX11B overexpression enhanced both the regeneration efficiency and gene transformation of EC (as assessed by the green fluorescence protein (GFP) marker), and evidently promoted the hairy root formation (Fig. 5d, e; Supplemental Fig. S25). Notably, the ginsenoside metabolism-related gene expression levels and the ginsenoside production ability were both elevated by using this WOX11B molecular tool (Fig. 5f, g).

The above data show that either TSA treatment or WOX11B overexpression is a useful tool to further enhance the ability of the EC protoderm as the source to produce ginsenosides.

Discussion

Numerous pharmaceuticals, health supplements, foods, and cosmetic products are enriched with natural plant-derived compounds57–59. Plant cell factories offer an economical, environmentally friendly, and sustainable avenue for the production of a plethora of valuable natural products1,60. Although cell factory systems entail higher costs compared to traditional cultivation, the substantial biomass yield achieved through bioreactor cultivation—coupled with its independence from seasonal constraints—demonstrates the broad commercial prospects of plant cell and organ culture technology for producing secondary metabolites when compared to field-based methods61. This approach stands in contrast to traditional plant cultivation, which can be fraught with uncertainties due to fluctuating climatic conditions and susceptibility to plant pathogens2.

In this study, we show by a single-cell analysis that embryonic protoderm derived from ginseng could serve as an in situ biological compartment that can produce ginsenosides. The cells of embryonic protoderm have been shown to be highly enriched in lipids and their derivatives62 (Fig. 1b); therefore, is suitable as an in situ biological compartment for producing plant lipid-soluble secondary metabolites, such as ginsenoside Ro and chikusetsusaponin IVa. We speculate that the embryonic protoderm might be a suitable in situ biological compartment not only to produce ginsenosides in ginseng, but also to produce other lipid-based secondary metabolites in other plant species. Interestingly, multicellular compartmentalization of ginsenosides has also been demonstrated in ginseng plants19, which supports our results that these compounds were biosynthesized in specific cell types.

The change of the chromatin status is a key step in reversing the fate of somatic cells to enter the embryo stage63. Here, we show that the genome-wide elevation of the histone acetylation level is associated with the formation of EC in ginseng. The elevated histone acetylation levels likely cause the high accessibility of chromatin, resulting in the release of gene transcription involved in embryo formation and ginsenosides metabolism. Therefore, an increase in histone acetylation, for example, by treatment with histone deacetylase inhibitors such as TSA, is a simple way to enhance embryonic protoderm as an in situ biological compartment of ginsenoside production.

Regeneration-related factors can serve as another tool to facilitate embryonic protoderm as the in situ biological compartment of ginsenoside production. We show that WOX11 overexpression is a candidate tool that enhances regeneration as well as ginsenosides metabolism in the EC of ginseng. Besides WOX11, many other regeneration-related genes, for example, PLTs and LECs, might also serve as potential molecular tools in the use of protoderm as a source for ginsenoside production. It will be interesting to optimize the combinations of these factors and test their abilities in embryonic protoderm in diverse plant species in the future.

Methods

Plant materials

Ginseng (Panax ginseng) initial calli derived from a 3-year-old Jilin ginseng leaf were cultured on MS medium supplemented with 2 mg/L NAA, 0.5 mg/L 6-BA, and 3% (w/v) sucrose in darkness at 25 °C ± 1 °C. Ginseng NEC was formed after more than 10 rounds of subculture (5-week intervals) in medium supplemented with 1.0 mg/L 2,4-D and 0.5 mg/L KT under the same conditions. Ginseng EC was derived from leaf calli after more than 5 months of culturing without hormones. All tissue samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at –80 °C. Metabolome and RNA-seq analyses were performed using three biological triplicates, whereas scRNA-seq, ATAC-seq, and ChIP-seq analyses were conducted with two biological replicates each.

Shoot regeneration assay

Ginseng EC samples were cultured on MS medium in plates (4.42 g MS basal medium with vitamin powder and 8 g/L agar, pH 5.8) in darkness. Ginseng NEC samples were cultured in plates containing MS medium (4.42 g MS basal medium with vitamin powder and 8 g/L agar, pH 5.8) supplemented with 1 mg/L 2,4-D and 0.5 mg/L KT in darkness for 1 week. The ginseng EC and NEC samples were transferred to SIM medium (4.42 g MS basal medium with vitamin powder, 20 g/L sucrose, 0.1 mg/L NAA, 0.5 mg/L KT, and 8 g/L agar, pH 5.8) and cultured for 1 month. The number of explants and regenerated shoots was recorded. The regeneration rate was calculated as the number of regenerated shoots divided by the total number of explants. Twenty-six biological replicates were analyzed.

Metabolome analyses of ginsenosides

We performed ginsenoside metabolome analysis using the previously established method64. Separation was performed on a Waters ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.8 μm) at 40 °C with 0.4 mL/min flow rate. The mobile phase consisted of 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (A) and 0.1% formic acid in water (B) using the following gradient: 28–31% A (0–2.5 min), 31–35% (2.5–3 min), 35–36% (3–4.5 min), 36–37% (4.5–6 min), 37–50% (6–7 min), 50–98% (7–9 min), 98% (9–10 min), returning to 28% (10–11 min), and equilibration at 28% (11–13 min). Injection volume was 1 μL. Analysis was performed in negative ion mode with optimized parameters for Q1 mass, Q3 mass, DP (V), CE (eV), and CXP (V) (Supplemental Tables 1−4).

Freeze-dried samples (20–30 mg) were extracted with 1.5 mL 75% methanol overnight, followed by 30 min sonication (500 W, 40 kHz, 25 °C). After centrifugation (13,000 rpm, 20 °C, 10 min), supernatants were filtered (0.22 μm) and stored at 4 °C for UPLC (Waters ACQUITY I Class)-MS/MS (AB SCIEX QTRAP 6500) analysis (Supplemental Fig. S26; Supplemental Tables 1−3). Twenty-four ginsenoside standards were dissolved in 75% methanol to prepare individual stock solutions. Each working solution (20 μg/mL each) was prepared and stored at 4 °C for UPLC (Waters ACQUITY I Class)-MS/MS (AB SCIEX QTRAP 6500) analysis (Supplemental Fig. S27; Supplemental Table 4).

Data acquisition used Waters MassLynx 4.1 software. Peak identification and quantification were performed using AB SCIEX MultiQuant with parameters: RT window = 3.0 sec, noise threshold = 40%, baseline window = 2 min, peak splitting = 2. Peaks with area >2×10³ were considered valid.

RNA sequencing

Total RNA was extracted using the EASYspin Plus Plant RNA Kit (Aidlab Biotechnologies Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). The integrity of the extracted RNA was assessed using the RNA Nano 6000 Assay Kit and the Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA). For each sample, 0.5 μg RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the cDNA Synthesis Kit (TaKaRa, Japan). Following an RNase treatment to remove residual RNA, cDNA was synthesized using DNA polymerase and dNTPs. After the end repair, A-tailing, and indexing adapter ligation steps, the products were purified using a PCR extraction kit and amplified to generate cDNA libraries. The constructed libraries were sequenced using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 system. The RNA-seq raw data were deposited in the Genome Sequence Archive (GSA)65 of the National Genomics Data Center66, China National Center for Bioinformation / Beijing Institute of Genomics, Chinese Academy of Sciences (CRA008967; publicly accessible at https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/gsa). The transcriptome data (i.e., sequence reads) were mapped to the P. ginseng genome using HISAT2 and Samtools67.

Protoplast isolation and single-cell RNA sequencing

To release protoplasts, 5 g of each sample was chopped into 1 mm small pieces and incubated in cell wall digesting solution (1.5% cellulase R-10, 0.4% macerozyme R-10, 0.5 M mannitol, 20 mM KCl, 10 mM CaCl2, and 0.1% bovine serum albumin)68 before being shaken at 50–60 rpm for 2.5 h. The protoplasts were filtered through 40 μm cell strainer to remove clumped cells, centrifuged at 120 × g for 5 min, and washed three times with 8% mannitol at room temperature. After draining the supernatant, a small amount of 8% mannitol solution was used to resuspend the protoplasts. Protoplasts with a viability of ≥80% were processed with the SeekOne® MM Large-Pore High-Throughput Single-Cell Transcriptome Kit Protocol (Seekgene, Beijing) after trypan blue staining was used to estimate their concentrations.

The scRNA-seq libraries were processed using the SeekOne® MM single-cell sequencing kit, following the manufacturer’s instructions (Seekgene, Beijing). The DNA libraries were qualitatively evaluated with an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer, and paired-end reads were generated with an Illumina Hiseq2000 sequencer. The scRNA-seq raw data were deposited in the GSA (CRA026200: publicly accessible at https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/gsa65).

Fastp v0.20.1 was used to trim primer sequence and low-quality bases of raw reads69. We processed the cleaned reads using the Seekone Tools pipeline (http://seeksoul.seekgene.com/en/v1.3.0/index.html) to generate a transcript expression matrix. First, cell barcodes and UMI sequences were extracted based on the predefined patterns for barcode, linker, and UMI locations within each read. The barcodes were then corrected using a predefined whitelist. Subsequently, the corrected barcodes along with their corresponding UMIs were embedded into the headers of the respective reads. Next, the reads were aligned to the reference genome using STAR v2.5.1b70. The aligned reads, now annotated with barcode and UMI information, were assigned to transcriptomic features using featureCounts from the Subread package v1.6.471 with default parameters except for “-s1--fracOverlap 0.5”. Finally, similar to the raw_feature_bc_matrix results of Cell Ranger72, a raw UMI count matrix containing cellular barcodes and transcripts was generated.

The UMI count matrix was loaded into Seurat (version 5.1.0) for downstream analysis73. All samples were merged. Cells were filtered using at least 100 expressed genes as threshold. The standard Seurat pipeline was used for normalization, scaling data, and reducing dimensions (https://satijalab.org/seurat/). To compare the differences between EC and NEC, all samples were integrated using the IntegrateLayers function with CCAIntegration method. Top 30 dimensions after integration were used for the FindNeighbors function. Cell clusters were identified using the FindClusters function with a resolution as 0.5. The cell abundance differential analysis was performed using miloR (version 1.10.0)74. Cell neighborhood computation was based on the integrated Seurat objectives.

Sudan Red 7B staining

Ginseng EC and NEC were stained with Sudan Red 7B according to a previously described method (Thermo Scientific)37,75. Briefly, EC and NEC samples were dehydrated in a series of isopropanol solutions with different concentrations (20, 40, and 60%; 20 min at each concentration) and then incubated in 60% isopropanol containing 0.5% Sudan Red 7B for 16 h. All samples were subsequently rehydrated through a reverse process and washed three times with distilled water before being examined.

Scanning electron microscopy

Ginseng EC and NEC samples were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde under vacuum conditions for 1 h at room temperature. The samples were subsequently rinsed in PBS and dehydrated for 15 min in a series of ethanol solutions with different concentrations (20, 40, 60, 80, 90, and 100%), and then in acetone. The samples were dehydrated using the EM-CPD-030 CO2 critical point dryer (Leica, Heidelberg, Germany) before being sputter-coated with gold using the IB-2 ion coater (Giko Engineering Co., Tokyo, Japan). All samples were examined using the JSM-6390 scanning electron microscope (JEOL Ltd., Tokyo, Japan).

ATAC sequencing

Nuclei freshly purified for the ATAC-seq analysis were kept on ice before the transposase integration reactions and the construction of sequencing libraries, which were completed as previously described76. For the transposase integration reaction, 50 ng P. ginseng sample was included in each 50 μL reaction involving Nextera reagents (Illumina, FC-121-1030) and a 30 min incubation at 37 °C. Tagmented DNA was purified using 11 μL elution buffer from the MiniElute PCR Purification Kit (Qiagen), Each sample was amplified using the High Fidelity PCR Mix (NEB) and custom barcoded primers for 10–12 total PCR cycles. The resulting ATAC-seq libraries were purified using AMPure XP beads (Beckman Colter) and quantified using the NEB Next Library Quantification Kit (NEB) and the Bioanalyzer High Sensitivity DNA Chip (Agilent), which was followed by pooling and sequencing. The ATAC-seq raw data were deposited in the GSA (CRA008969: publicly accessible at https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/gsa65).

Protein extraction and immunoblotting assays

Extraction buffer [0.1 M Tris-HCl, pH7.5, 0.3 M NaCl, 2 mM EDTA, 10% glycerol, 2% SDS, 1% protease inhibitor (EDTA-free tablets; Roche), and 1% TritonX-100] was used to extract protein from callus frozen in liquid nitrogen. The extracted proteins were used for the immunoblot analysis. Specifically, protein samples were subjected to gradient gel electrophoresis and transferred to PVDF membranes, which were blocked for 1 h using 5% skim milk. After washing, the following antibodies were added to the membranes: anti-H3 (Abcam, ab1791, dilution: 1/2000), anti-H3K9ac (ThermoFisher, AM-39137, dilution: 1/2000), anti-H3K9me2 (ThermoFisher, AM-39239, dilution: 1/2000), anti-H3K4me3 (Abcam, ab8580, dilution: 1/2000). The membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C and washed with 1 × TBST. The corresponding secondary antibodies were added and the membranes were incubated at room temperature for at least 1 h. After washing with 1 × TBST, the developer was added and the membranes were exposed to film or analyzed via chemiluminescence.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing

The ChIP-seq analysis was conducted using the universal Plant ChIP-Seq kit (Diagenode, C01010152). Briefly, approximately 5 g ginseng samples were vacuum infiltrated for 15 min in cross-linking solution at room temperature for a total of five times. Samples were ground to a fine powder in liquid nitrogen. The extracted chromatin was sheared (200- to 500-bp fragments) via sonication (ten cycles of 30 s on/off) using the Bioruptor UCD 200 instrument (Diagenode, Sparta, NJ). Chromatin-associated DNA was immunoprecipitated using 5 μL mouse anti-H3K9ac monoclonal antibodies (Abcam, ab10812).

Sequencing libraries were constructed according to the Illumina TruSeq ChIP Sample Prep Set A protocol and then sequenced using the Illumina HiSeq3000 platform (PE150). The Trimmomatic package (version0.35) was used to remove low-quality reads and adapters from the raw data. The clean reads were mapped to the ginseng reference genome (NCBI, BioSample Accession Number, SAMN20855168) using Bowtie2 (version 2.2.5), with fewer than two mismatches allowed. Potential PCR duplicates were removed using Samtools (version 0.1.19). The MACS software (version 1.4.2) was used to locate the enriched regions (Khib peaks) on the basis of a comparison of the reads from the immunoprecipitated sample and the reads from the input sample. The ChIP-seq raw data were deposited in GSA (CRA008968: publicly accessible at https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/gsa65).

Bioinformatics analysis

The raw fastq data of each library were trimmed using the default parameters of fastp (version 0.20.0). For the ATAC-seq analysis, the adapter sequence parameters were set as previously described69. FastQC (version 0.11.7) and MultiQC (version 1.6) were used to assess quality and obtain clean fq files77. Reads were aligned to the ginseng genome (NCBI, BioSample Accession Number, SAMN20855168) using Bowtie2 (version 2.3.4.3) (for ATAC-seq and ChIP-seq) or HISAT2 (version 2.1.0) (for RNA-seq)67. The resulting SAM file was converted into BAM format, sorted, and indexed using Samtools (version 1.9)78. Two biological replicates were merged using Samtools (version 1.9). For the normalization and visualization of the individual and merged replicate datasets, the BAM files were converted to bigwig files using bamCoverage in deepTools (version 3.1.2), with a 10-bp bin and data normalized based on fragments per kilobase of exon model per million mapped fragments (for RNA-seq) or bin per million mapped reads (for ATAC-seq and ChIP-seq)79. The “Plot Profile” and “Plot Heatmap” functions in deepTools were used to check sample quality and score the associated genomic regions. All signal plots were visualized using Integrative Genomics Viewer80. The GO and gene set enrichment analyses of the DEGs and the clustering were performed using clusterProfiler (version 3.14.0) and org.At.tair.db (version 3.10.0)81. The GO analysis of ATAC-seq and ChIP-seq datasets was performed according to the methods used for the RNA-seq analysis.

Vector construction and ginseng transformation

To produce the ginseng WOX11B overexpression construct, full-length WOX11B cDNA was cloned into the binary vector pH7WG2D using Gateway technology. The control and WOX11B overexpression constructs were inserted into Agrobacterium rhizogenes strain C58C1 cells using a freeze-thaw transformation method. Ginseng EC was chopped into multiple pieces (approximately 0.1 cm3) and co-cultivated with A. rhizogenescells in half-strength MS medium for 2 days. After 8 weeks of culturing, the transformation of ginseng EC was confirmed by screening for green fluorescence and by conducting PCR and quantitative PCR (qPCR) analyses.

In situ hybridization (ISH)

The EC samples were fixed with FAA (50% ethanol, 5% acetic acid, 3.7% formaldehyde), dehydrated through a graded ethanol series from 50% to 100%, followed by gradient replacement with xylene. Subsequently, the samples underwent paraffin infiltration and embedding and were finally sectioned into paraffin slices. Then, ISH was performed as previously described using digoxigenin-labeled probes82. Briefly, a linear DNA fragment of the target gene was amplified by PCR using primers flanking the gene insert while preserving RNA polymerase binding sites within the amplified region. Digoxigenin (DIG)-labeled antisense and sense RNA probes were synthesized in vitro using SP6 and T7 RNA polymerases (Roche), respectively. Probe hybridization and immunological detection followed established protocols83,84. Hybridized probes were detected with an anti-DIG-alkaline phosphatase antibody and visualized using NBT/BCIP (4-nitroblue tetrazolium chloride and 5-bromo-4-chloro-3-indolyl-phosphate). Signal development was monitored and imaged under a light microscope (AX10, Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany).

Mass spectrometry imaging (MSI)

Ginseng embryogenic callus (EC) specimens were embedded in 5% carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC), cryoimmobilized, and sectioned into 30 μm thick slices using a MICROM HM550 cryostat (Walldorf, Germany). Following ambient temperature desiccation, bright-field microscopy was performed using a Leica DM500 system (Wetzlar, Germany). Matrix application was achieved using an optimized spray-coating protocol with 2,5-dihydroxybenzoic acid (DHB; 30 mg/mL in methanol) at 20 μL/min flow rate under 5 L/min nitrogen gas flow (50 min/slide deposition time). Post-coating, samples were precisely positioned on the target plate using integrated optical alignment features. High-resolution mass spectrometry imaging was conducted on a TransMIT AP-SMALDI 5AF atmospheric pressure ion source (Giessen, Germany) interfaced with a Q Exactive hybrid quadrupole-Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Bremen, Germany). The instrument parameters were set as: Full MS scan mode with m/z 400–1200 range, positive ion detection mode, 10 × 10 μm spatial resolution, and 4 kV ion source voltage. Molecular ion distribution maps were reconstructed using Mirion software with automated peak detection and image generation algorithms. Spatial distributions were normalized to total ion current (TIC) and visualized using a linear color gradient scale.
