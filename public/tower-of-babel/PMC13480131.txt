Percutaneous Closure of Left Ventricular Apex Perforation After Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement Valve-in-Valve
Authors: Andrew Moussad, Kaleab Asrress, Ata Doost
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTranscatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) valve-in-valve (ViV) is a less invasive alternative to redo surgery for high-risk patients with failed bioprosthetic aortic valves. Left ventricular (LV) perforation is a rare but potentially life-threatening complication, typically requiring surgical repair. Percutaneous closure is seldom reported.Case SummaryAn 80-year-old man with NYHA functional class III symptoms from severe degenerative transvalvular bioprosthetic aortic regurgitation underwent TAVR ViV. Postprocedure transthoracic echocardiogram and computed tomography scan revealed a small, contained LV apex perforation, likely iatrogenic from guidewire manipulation. This was successfully closed percutaneously using an Amplatzer Vascular Plug II.DiscussionThis case highlights importance of high-quality post-TAVR ViV transthoracic echocardiography and safety of percutaneous closure of LV apex perforation compared with surgical repair.Take-Home MessagesLV apex perforation is a rare but serious complication of TAVR. Percutaneous closure may avoid high-risk surgery in suitable cases.

History of Presentation

An 80-year-old man with NYHA functional class III symptoms and severe degenerative transvalvular bioprosthetic aortic regurgitation (AR) was referred for consideration of transcatheter aortic valve replacement valve-in-valve (TAVR ViV). His symptoms included shortness of breath with minimal exertion, fatigue, and ankle swelling, which required hospital admission for treatment with intravenous diuretics. Past Medical History

The patient underwent coronary artery bypass grafting with a left internal mammary artery to left anterior descending (LAD) graft, bioprosthetic aortic valve replacement (AVR), and aortic root and hemiarch replacement 13 years ago, and had a history of hypertension, dyslipidemia, asbestosis, gastroesophageal reflux disease, and prostate cancer treated with radical prostatectomy, now in remission.

Medications

The medications included amlodipine 5 mg daily, aspirin 100 mg daily, candesartan 16 mg daily, cholecalciferol 25 μg daily, magnesium aspartate 1,000 mg daily, metoprolol 25 mg twice a day, mirabegron 50 mg daily, pantoprazole 40 mg daily, and rosuvastatin 40 mg daily.

Investigations

Initial work-up before TAVR ViV included electrocardiography, which showed sinus rhythm with no left ventricular (LV) hypertrophy or strain. Blood tests showed an estimated glomerular filtration rate of 63 mL/min/1.73 m2, a creatinine level of 95 μmol/L, and normal inflammatory markers. transesophageal echocardiogram showed normal LV size and systolic function; the bioprosthetic AVR was well seated. However, there was restriction of the bioprosthetic leaflet at the native noncoronary cusp site, severe eccentric centrally directed transvalvular AR directed toward the anterior mitral valve leaflet, favored to be degenerative in etiology, with no paravalvular leak, moderate centrally directed mitral regurgitation, and moderate centrally directed tricuspid regurgitation. Percutaneous coronary intervention of the ostial left circumflex coronary artery was performed with deployment of a single drug-eluting stent. Percutaneous coronary intervention of the ostial left circumflex coronary artery with 1 drug-eluting stent.

Management

The patient then underwent TAVR ViV with an Abbott Navitor 27-mm transcatheter heart valve, which was postdilated with a 24-mm noncompliant balloon (True Balloon) with pacing on an LV Safari XS wire (Boston Scientific), with no immediate complications. The valve positioning and function was confirmed with angiography, and transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) immediately after deployment showed no AR or pericardial effusion.

On day 1 postprocedure, routine TTE showed TAVR ViV well seated with normal gradients across the valve (AV Vmax 1.9 m/s, mean PG 9 mm Hg, aortic valve area velocity time integral 1.31 cm2, doppler velocity index 0.45) and no paravalvular leak. There was a small contained LV apex perforation with diastolic flow and no obvious pericardial effusion (Video 1). Deployment of an Amplatzer Vascular Plug Device

On day 3 postprocedure, serial TTE showed no evidence of diastolic flow or perforation in the LV apex. However, a CT cardiac scan showed a persistent small, contained perforation at the LV apex, similar in appearance to the previous one.

On day 6 postprocedure, the patient underwent percutaneous closure of the LV apex perforation. Right femoral artery access was gained via ultrasound with a 6-F sheath and then upsized to an 8-F 90-cm Destination sheath (Terumo). The TAVR ViV was crossed with a pigtail catheter, and LV angiography was performed, demonstrating the location and size of apical perforation. The pigtail catheter was exchanged to a 6-F multipurpose A (MPA) guide catheter. A Terumo guidewire was advanced into the apical contained perforation pericardial pocket, and the guide catheter was advanced into the pericardial cavity (Figure 2). An 8-mm Amplatzer Vascular Plug II (AVP II) device (Abbott) was successfully deployed across the perforation, which obliterated the defect (Figure 3). Outcome and Follow-Up

On day 1 after percutaneous LV perforation closure, TTE showed the AVP II well seated with no residual flow across the LV apex (Video 6). The patient was clinically stable and was discharged the following day. The patient had outpatient follow-up 4 weeks later including a repeat TTE, which showed that the AVP II device remained well seated with no perforation. The patient remains stable 8 months postprocedure.

Discussion

TAVR ViV is a less invasive alternative to redo surgery for high-risk patients with structural valve degeneration of bioprosthetic aortic valves.1 However, procedural complications can occur, including the rare but potentially life-threatening complication of the LV apex perforation, most often due to guidewire manipulation.2,3 Although typically associated with pericardial tamponade and requiring urgent surgical repair, contained LV perforation treated percutaneously is rare.4

An important procedural observation in this case was the marked angulation of the Safari XS guidewire within the LV, which formed an acute bend at the LV apical interface (Figure 5, Video 7). Although preshaped stiff wires are essential for device support during TAVR ViV, excessive angulation can create a focal point of mechanical stress against the LV wall. In this configuration, even minor forward wire migration during repositioning or repeated manipulation may be transmitted directly to the LV apex, increasing the risk of myocardial injury or perforation. In addition, chronic apical myocardial thinning, fibrosis, and reduced wall integrity from remote LAD-territory ischemia before coronary artery bypass grafting may have rendered the apex structurally vulnerable. Additional intraprocedural factors that may have contributed to the LV apex perforation include transmission of excessive torque or forward force during manipulation, particularly when seeking stable distal positioning. In such circumstances, the wire may assume an exaggerated angulated configuration against the ventricular apex, increasing the risk of focal myocardial injury.Figure 5Fluoroscopic Image Demonstrating the Steep Angulation of the Safari XS Guidewire Within the Left Ventricular Cavity, Resulting in a Sharp Bend

To mitigate this risk, careful attention to wire behavior under fluoroscopy in multiple orthogonal views is essential. Excessive advancement or forceful torquing of the wire within the LV apical region should be avoided. Early recognition of abnormal wire configuration should prompt immediate reassessment and repositioning. Maintaining a more controlled and proximal wire position until a stable trajectory is confirmed may further reduce the likelihood of acute angulation and potential myocardial perforation.

The LV apex perforation was subtle and identified incidentally on routine postprocedural TTE and confirmed with a CT cardiac scan. The patient was asymptomatic and hemodynamically stable, which is likely due to postsurgical AVR pericardial scarring containing the effusion and abating the hemodynamic compromise. This highlights the importance of high-quality TTE imaging and strong clinical awareness, as such complications can me missed without careful assessment. The perforation was no longer visualized on serial TTE but persisted on a repeat CT cardiac scan, highlighting the role of multimodal imaging and serial cross-sectional evaluation. Untreated contained perforation has unpredictable outcomes and can potentially lead to effusion expansion, cardiac tamponade, and death.

The patient remained asymptomatic, with normal right ventricular size/function and no evidence of increased pulmonary artery pressures. Given the unpredictability of its natural history and the risk of delayed expansion, tamponade, or rupture, the patient was referred for closure. Given the patient's extensive prior surgical history and prohibitive surgical risk profile, the heart team determined that transcatheter closure offered a safer alternative to emergent redo surgery. Percutaneous transvascular closure with an AVP II was successfully performed. A transapical approach was avoided as it would require a new surgical apical puncture, thereby creating a second apical injury and risking enlargement of the existing contained perforation. The method also carries a higher bleeding risk, including hemothorax, myocardial laceration, and persistent apical leakage. In patients with prior sternotomy, apical access is further complicated by adhesions and increased procedural complexity. Given the more invasive nature, a percutaneous transvascular strategy was deemed safer and more appropriate in this high surgical risk patient. The AVP II was selected based on its favorable device characteristics for this anatomy. Its short, cylindrical configuration allows secure sealing of narrow, tract-like perforations, and its controlled radial force provides effective occlusion without exerting excessive pressure on the thin apical myocardium. The absence of retention discs reduces the risk of impingement or further myocardial tearing, whereas its small delivery profile (5- to 7-F) facilitates transfemoral transvalvular access. In addition, the AVP II is known to endothelialize rapidly in small defects. In contrast, atrial septal defect and ventricular septal defect occluders are larger disc-based devices designed for flat septal surfaces rather than tubular myocardial tracts. Their discs may distort or injure the LV apex, and their higher radial forces increase the risk of enlarging or destabilizing a fragile perforation. For these reasons, an AVP II was considered the most appropriate device for this contained apical perforation.

Percutaneous closure of LV perforations after TAVI ViV is seldom described in the literature. Percutaneous closure of the LV apical perforation may be particularly well suited to patients with prior open-heart surgery, in whom repeat sternotomy carries increased technical complexity and operative risk. In addition, the presence of pericardial adhesions in the postsurgical setting may promote containment of the perforation, thereby potentially creating more favorable conditions for transcatheter closure. However, this strategy is not limited to patients with previous surgery. In selected individuals without prior cardiac surgery, percutaneous closure may also be feasible when the perforation is localized and contained, the patient is hemodynamically stable, and the anatomy is suitable for device delivery and deployment. Furthermore, this approach may also be suitable in patients without prior cardiac surgery in whom surgical intervention is associated with prohibitive morbidity and mortality risk. Given the limited published experience, careful patient selection and multidisciplinary heart team assessment remain essential when considering this approach. As TAVR ViV procedures become more frequent, especially in aging populations with prior surgical valve replacement, clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for rare complications. Early detection through high-resolution TTE and confirmatory cross-sectional imaging enables timely and less invasive management.

Conclusions

This case highlights a rare but important complication of TAVR ViV. The absence of symptoms and pericardial effusion emphasizes the need for high-quality TTE and clinical vigilance. Multimodal imaging, particularly serial cross-sectional assessment, is crucial for accurate diagnosis and monitoring. As TAVR ViV becomes more prevalent, awareness of such complications and familiarity with minimally invasive management strategies will be essential to optimizing patient outcomes.
