Simultaneous iron reinsertion and defect healing
in zeolite beta via post-synthetic hydrothermal treatment
Taiki Hayashi1, Saori Iwamoto1, Megumi Omori1 and Kenta Iyoki1,³
1

Department of Environment Systems, Graduate School of Frontier Sciences, The University of Tokyo,
5–1–5 Kashiwanoha, Kashiwa, Chiba 277–8563, Japan

A post-synthetic treatment strategy for modiﬁcation of zeolites, microporous ceramics, is attractive for
regulating the local environments of heteroatoms in zeolite frameworks. Herein, we report the post-synthetic
treatment of Fe-containing zeolite beta for changing the introduced Fe environment. The Fe-containing zeolite
beta is synthesized by a hydrothermal treatment of Si, Al, and Fe sources in the presence of tetraethylammonium
hydroxide (TEAOH), followed by calcination, which causes detachment of framework Fe and formation of
defect sites. Post-synthetic hydrothermal treatment of the Fe-containing zeolite beta in the presence of TEAOH
and ammonium ﬂuoride (NH4F) enables reinsertion of Fe species into the framework while simultaneously
healing defect sites. Fe incorporation can also be achieved by applying this treatment to commercially available
zeolite beta after impregnation with Fe species. This approach provides a strategy for controlling heteroatom
environments in zeolite frameworks, oﬀering a versatile platform for zeolite framework engineering and
materials design.
Keywords: Zeolite, Iron species, Reinsertion, Defect healing
[Received June 10, 2026; Accepted August 17, 2026]

1. Introduction
Zeolites, which are composed of crystalline microporous aluminosilicate frameworks, are useful materials
for a wide range of applications such as catalysis, ion exchange, and adsorption.1) Various heteroatoms such as Ge,
Sn, Ga, and Fe can be incorporated into zeolite frameworks. Fe-containing zeolites are especially useful for the
reduction of NOx with ammonia, N2O decomposition,
removal of volatile organic compounds, and oxidation of
hydrocarbons.2) Conventional preparation routes for Fecontaining zeolites include direct hydrothermal synthesis
using Fe-containing precursors3–7) or impregnation of Fe
species into as-synthesized zeolites.8–11) In the latter
approach, not all Fe species are incorporated into the
framework, and some Fe species often form extra-framework clusters. Furthermore, the framework-incorporated
Fe species are prone to removal during processing steps
such as calcination, making it diﬃcult to obtain uniform
and well-deﬁned Fe sites, although their local environments strongly inﬂuence catalytic performance.
Recent advances in post-synthetic treatments of zeolites
have opened new possibilities for their structural control.
For example, silylation of zeolites with chlorotrimethylsilane12) and the migration of silicate species within zeolite frameworks under low-temperature steaming condi³

2. Experimental procedure
2.1

Materials

The following reagents were used as received without
puriﬁcation: aluminum hydroxide [Al(OH)3, Sigma-Aldrich, ²90–¯100 %], fumed silica (Sigma-Aldrich),
iron(III) nitrate nonahydrate [Fe(NO3)3·9H2O, FUJIFILM
Wako Pure Chemical Corporation, >99.0 %], NH4F (Tokyo
Chemical Industry Co. Ltd., >97.0 %), TEAOH 35 wt %
aqueous solution (Sigma-Aldrich), and zeolite beta HSZ980HOA (Tosoh Corporation, SiO2/Al2O3 = 500).

2.2

Synthesis of Fe-containing zeolite beta

H2O (4.99 g, 277 mmol), TEAOH 35 wt % aqueous
solution (7.01 g, containing 16.7 mmol of TEAOH and 253
mmol of H2O), Al(OH)3 (0.13 g, 1.7 mmol), Fe(NO3)3·
9H2O [0.13 g, containing 0.32 mmol of Fe(NO3)3 and
2.9 mmol of H2O], and fumed silica (2.01 g, 33.5 mmol)
were added to a polypropylene vessel. The molar ratio was
1SiO 2:0.05Al(OH)3:0.01Fe(NO3)3:0.50TEAOH:16H2O.
The mixture was shaken by hand for several minutes and
then hydrothermally treated at 175 °C for 3 d under a static
condition using a Teﬂonμ-lined stainless-steel autoclave
(#4749, Parr). After the reaction, the sample was collected
by ﬁltration, washed with H2O, and dried at 80 °C to yield
Fe-Beta as a white powder (2.10 g). Fe-Beta was then
calcined at 550 °C for 6 h to yield Fe-Beta_cal as a white
powder.

2.3

Hydrothermal treatment of Fe-containing
zeolite beta in the presence of TEAOH
and NH4F

The hydrothermal treatment of Fe-Beta_cal in the presence of TEAOH and NH4F was performed according to a
previous report.15) The treatment solution was prepared
with a molar ratio of 1TEAOH:1NH4F:150H2O. Fe-Beta_
cal (1.00 g) was mixed with the solution (2.05 g). The
mixture was hydrothermally treated at 170 °C for 1 d under
a static condition using a Teﬂonμ-lined stainless-steel
autoclave (#4749, Parr). The sample was washed with H2O
and dried at 80 °C to yield Fe-Beta_cal-HT as a white
powder (0.95 g, 95 % yield based on the parent zeolite).
Fe-Beta_cal-HT was then calcined at 550 °C for 6 h to yield
Fe-Beta_cal-HT-cal as a white powder.

The SEM images of Fe-Beta and Fe-Beta_cal [Figs. 2(a)
and 2(b)] showed randomly aggregated spherical particles
with sizes of around 200 nm.
The UV–Vis spectrum of Fe-Beta [Fig. 3(a)] showed
two distinct peaks (213 and 240 nm) arising from tetrahedrally coordinated Fe3+ species.22,23) Peaks arising from
isolated octahedral Fe3+ species at extra-framework positions (around 275 nm) and small oligonuclear clusters

(FexOy) (around 380 nm)22,23) were negligible. After calcination, the peaks of isolated octahedral Fe3+ species and
FexOy were signiﬁcantly enhanced relative to those of
tetrahedrally coordinated Fe3+ species. These results indicated that the Fe species were partially removed from the
zeolite framework.
FTIR analysis was conducted to characterize the defect
sites of the zeolite samples. The FTIR spectrum of Fe-Beta
[Fig. 3(b)] showed bands arising from O–H stretching of
isolated silanol and hydrogen-bonded (H-bonded) silanol
groups,24) as well as C–H stretching of tetraethylammonium (TEA). After calcination, the C–H bands disappeared due to the removal of TEA. It is noteworthy that the
broad band of H-bonded silanol groups was still clearly
observed.

[Fig. 4(a)], the relative areas of Q4 (T1 and T2) and Q4

Furthermore, solid-state 29Si MAS NMR spectra were
compared between Fe-Beta and Fe-Beta_cal-HT, and
between Fe-Beta_cal and Fe-Beta_cal-HT-cal, pairs in
which the environments of the Al and Fe species were
found to be similar by UV–Vis and 27Al MAS NMR
analyses. In both cases, the relative peak area of the region
where signals derived from defect sites may appear
decreased, while the relative peak areas of Q4 (T1 and
T2) and Q4 (T3–T9) increased after the treatment, indicating that defect healing of the zeolite framework proceeded
simultaneously.
These phenomena were consistent with those observed
in the Al-containing MOR-type zeolite system.16) Although the reinserted Al and Fe species were partially
leached upon further calcination, the leaching may be
suppressed due to the progression of defect healing.
The acidity of the Fe-containing zeolite beta samples
was evaluated by NH3-TPD. In the NH3-TPD proﬁle of
Fe-Beta_cal (Fig. 5), the high-temperature peak (h-peak)
was observed at 369 °C. The amount of acid sites was
calculated to be 0.44 mmol g¹1. Previous studies reported
that the NH3-TPD proﬁles of Al-containing zeolite beta
exhibited desorption peaks at 365–395 °C.26) In addition,
for MFI-type zeolites, the NH3-TPD peak of Al-containing
zeolite appeared at higher temperatures than that of Fecontaining zeolite, while the introduction of both Al and
Fe resulted in a peak at an intermediate temperature.27,28)
Based on these results and previous studies, it is reasonable to assume that the h-peak of Fe-Beta_cal originates
from Fe and Al species in zeolite beta. The amount of acid
sites remained nearly unchanged after the treatment and
subsequent calcination (0.43 mmol g¹1). However, the
peak position shifted from 369 to 353 °C. These results
indicate that the hydrothermal treatment in the presence of
TEAOH and NH4F altered the local environments of Al
and Fe species through their reinsertion into the zeolite
beta framework, which consequently modiﬁed the acidity.
The shift in the h-peak position is likely attributable to
changes in the siting and distribution of Al and Fe species
within the zeolite beta framework upon reinsertion. In
support of this interpretation, density functional theory

Hydrothermal treatment conditions of Fe-Beta_cal were
varied to examine the eﬀect of each component of the
treatment solution. Fe-Beta_cal was mixed with (1) an
aqueous TEAOH solution, (2) an aqueous NH4F solution,
or (3) H2O alone at a weight ratio of 1:2, followed by
hydrothermal treatment (for details, see Procedure S1 in
the Supporting Information). In the case of the TEAOH
treatment, the sample remained almost unchanged in color
(white) after the reaction, and the yield was 92 % based
on the parent zeolite (Fig. S1 and Table S1). Negligible
changes were observed in the XRD pattern [Fig. S2(a)].
The UV–Vis spectrum was similar to that of Fe-Beta_calHT, showing mainly peaks of isolated tetrahedral Fe3+
species [Fig. S2(b)]. Similar changes in the Fe species
have also been reported for Fe-containing MFI-type
zeolite.30)
When Fe-Beta_cal was treated with the NH4F solution,
the sample became brownish after the reaction (Fig. S1).
The yield was lower (85 %) than that obtained under the
other treatment conditions mentioned above. The XRD pattern was broader than those of the samples treated with both
TEAOH and NH4F or with TEAOH alone [Fig. S2(a)]. The
UV–Vis spectrum mainly exhibited peaks derived from
isolated tetrahedral Fe3+ species, while the peak intensities
of isolated octahedral Fe3+ species and FexOy were higher
than those of the two samples treated in the presence of
TEAOH [Fig. S2(b)].
In the absence of both TEAOH and NH4F, the color
changed to reddish-brown, and the sample yield was 96 %
(Fig. S1). No signiﬁcant changes in XRD patterns were
observed [Fig. S2(a)]. Notably, in the UV–Vis spectrum,
peaks of FexOy were predominantly observed [Fig. S2(b)].
In a previous report,15) hydrothermal treatment of Alcontaining MFI-type zeolites using a TEAOH solution
resulted in dissolution of the zeolite framework, whereas,
in the present Fe-containing zeolite beta system, the framework was retained and the heteroatoms at extra-framework
positions were reinserted. The much higher SiO2/Al2O3
ratio of the MFI-type zeolite (³1500) might have rendered
its framework more susceptible to dissolution. This difference may also be related to the role of TEAOH as a
structure-directing agent for the reconstruction of zeolite
beta. In the hydrothermal treatment using NH4F, the XRD
pattern became broader and the yield decreased, suggesting partial dissolution of the zeolite framework. In the
absence of both TEAOH and NH4F, the yield remained
almost unchanged, whereas peaks attributed to FexOy
species were predominantly observed in the UV–Vis
spectrum, suggesting that Fe species were removed from
the zeolite framework through hydrolysis.

4. Conclusion
We demonstrated that hydrothermal treatment in the
presence of TEAOH and NH4F is an eﬀective postsynthetic strategy for controlling the local environment of
Fe species in zeolite beta. In Fe-containing zeolite beta
synthesized by direct hydrothermal synthesis, frameworkincorporated Fe species were partially removed during
calcination. Hydrothermal treatment in the presence of
TEAOH and NH4F enabled the reinsertion of Fe species
into the zeolite framework, accompanied by the simultaneous healing of framework defects. This concept was
validated using commercially available zeolite beta impregnated with Fe species, where the treatment similarly
promoted the framework incorporation of Fe species. This
strategy provides a general post-synthetic route for controlling heteroatom incorporation and stability in zeolite
frameworks, thereby opening new avenues for frameworklevel heteroatom design.
