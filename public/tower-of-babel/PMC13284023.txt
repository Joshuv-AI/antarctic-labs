Mako robot-assisted unicompartmental knee arthroplasty mitigates the impact of surgeon handedness
Authors: Shujun Chen, Xiao Huang, Lingjun Jiang, Zhongyi Chen, Jiayan Chen, Chenglong Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Conventional manual unicompartmental knee arthroplasty (CM-UKA) is technically demanding, and surgeon handedness may affect component alignment. Whether Mako robot-assisted UKA (MA-UKA) can mitigate this handedness effect remains unclear. A total of 391 patients undergoing medial UKA performed by five right‑handed surgeons were enrolled in this retrospective analysis. These individuals were allocated to four study arms. Perioperative outcomes, radiographic parameters and functional scores were compared. MA groups had longer operative time but similar tourniquet time, shorter hospital stay, and less blood loss than CM groups (all P<0.05). MA-left and MA-right showed no differences in any radiographic or functional parameters (P>0.05). In contrast, CM-left performed significantly worse than CM-right in terms of implant positioning accuracy (P<0.05). Functional outcomes were better in MA groups, with no side‑related differences. In CM‑UKA, the surgeon’s hand dominance markedly influences component alignment precision, with inferior outcomes consistently appearing on the non‑dominant side. MA-UKA mitigates this handedness bias, achieving equally high accuracy on both sides, while also reducing blood loss, shortening hospital stay, and improving early functional recovery.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11701-026-03609-w.

Background

Unicompartmental knee osteoarthritis is common in middle-aged and older adults. For anteromedial compartment osteoarthritis, unicompartmental knee arthroplasty (UKA) is the standard treatment. When compared to total knee arthroplasty (TKA), UKA offers greater preservation of bone and retention of both cruciate ligaments, provides more physiological knee biomechanics, accelerates postoperative recovery, and is associated with fewer perioperative complications [1–4]. UKA, however, poses considerable technical demands regardless of these benefits. Proper implant positioning and limb alignment directly affect implant survival and clinical outcomes [5, 6]. Many recent studies show that conventional manual (CM) UKA still has a high rate of radiographic outliers [7, 8]. Poor component placement and excessive overhang are key reasons for persistent pain, poor function, and higher revision rates [5, 9, 10].

Surgeon handedness matters. It influences precision, posture, and instrument handling [11, 12]. In TKA and THA, the effect is clear: when performing surgery on the non-dominant side, posture feels awkward, the view is asymmetric, and fine motor control drops. As a result, component positioning and postoperative alignment suffer [11–14]. In THA, handedness also affects cup positioning and stem fit, raising dislocation risk [13, 14]. Our previous work found the same for CM‑TKA: handedness significantly affects alignment accuracy and leads to more outliers [15]. In UKA, Cao et al. [16] reported that right‑handed surgeons doing CM-UKA placed the femoral component worse on the non‑dominant side.

CM joint replacement relies on the surgeon’s eye and hand, guided by basic instruments [17, 18]. The error linked to handedness is systematic, and neither experience nor traditional tools can fully overcome it. Robotic systems like Mako have changed this. Mako robot-assisted (MA) TKA reduces postoperative pain, improves component alignment (especially in the sagittal plane), and speeds up early functional recovery [19–22]. Several studies show that MA‑UKA also cuts down alignment and overhang outliers and makes component positioning more consistent [7, 8, 23, 24]. Robotic systems are not affected by surgical field visibility, the surgeon’s posture, or the learning curve. They give stable, standardized, reproducible results. Through its quantitative, image‑guided surgical technique, MA‑TKA already attenuates laterality discrepancies stemming from surgeon handedness, yielding comparable alignment accuracy on both the preferred and non‑preferred sides [15]. Nevertheless, it remains uncertain whether robotic assistance can directly mitigate the adverse influence of handedness in the context of UKA.

Accordingly, our primary objective was to determine whether MA‑UKA could mitigate the negative influence of hand dominance on component positioning precision and short‑term clinical results. We postulated that the operative bias introduced by handedness would be diminished by MA‑UKA, thereby facilitating earlier postoperative functional improvement.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study was done at three hospitals within one large medical center in China. All three followed standardized protocols for serology, imaging, and measurements. We initially screened patients who had primary medial UKA from January 2024 to December 2025. Patients qualified for enrollment if they met the following: a confirmed diagnosis of anteromedial osteoarthritis, availability of complete preoperative records, and at least one month of follow‑up data. The exclusion criteria consisted of: (1) a fixed varus angle exceeding 10° or a valgus angle exceeding 5°, or the presence of grade IV patellofemoral chondrosis; (2) neuromuscular conditions, for instance paralysis, myolysis, or muscle weakness; (3) a history of ipsilateral limb trauma or prior surgical intervention; (4) cruciate ligament deficiency or impairment, rheumatoid arthritis, post‑traumatic arthropathy, or a peri‑articular knee neoplasm; (5) septic arthritis of the knee; and (6) missing clinical records. Following the application of these criteria, 391 individuals were retained for analysis. All surgical procedures were performed by five seasoned, right‑hand‑dominant orthopedic surgeons. Based on the utilization of Mako robotic assistance and the laterality of the operative side, patients were assigned to one of four study arms: MA‑left (n = 32), MA‑right (n = 33), CM‑left (n = 170), and CM‑right (n = 156).

Surgical technique

All five participating surgeons were right‑hand dominant. Each surgeon had performed more than 50 MA-UKA procedures prior to this study, ensuring that they were beyond the initial learning curve. Regarding the positioning of the doctors, perioperative management was based on our previous research [15].

For MA‑UKA, patients had a preoperative CT scan. DICOM data were loaded into the Mako plasty workstation to segment and reconstruct patient‑specific 3D bone models. Intraoperatively, we used a dynamic reference array and range‑of‑motion measurements to check soft‑tissue balance, then used the haptic‑guided robotic arm for precise bone cuts. Given that posterior tibial slope tends to be larger in Asian individuals relative to Western counterparts [25–27], a 7° sagittal inclination was chosen for the proximal tibial osteotomy, thereby safeguarding the knee’s native soft‑tissue balance. Following this, the RESTORIS MCK prosthesis (Stryker Corp., USA) was implanted, with a fixed‑bearing polyethylene. For those receiving conventional manual UKA (the CM cohort), we employed standard surgical instruments and all underwent implantation of a mobile‑bearing Oxford Phase 3 prosthesis (Zimmer‑Biomet, USA).

Data collection

Perioperative outcome assessment

The operative time, tourniquet time, hospital stay and total blood loss were calculated and collected from relevant literature [15, 28].

Radiographic measurement

GE Healthcare Systems (Chicago, IL, USA) was employed for all radiographic assessments. A reliability test was first conducted by three orthopedic surgeons. Using a two-way random-effects model for absolute agreement (single measurement), the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was calculated for each radiographic parameter. The ICC results are presented in Supplementary Table 1. Subsequently, the three surgeons evaluated all radiographic parameters across the entire patient cohort.

Radiographic assessments were conducted according to the criteria established by Kazarian et al. [5] and our own previously reported methodology [22]. The following parameters were recorded: (a) mechanical tibiofemoral angle (mTFA), mechanical lateral distal femoral angle (mLDFA), and medial proximal tibial angle (MPTA); (b) femoral coronal angle (FCA), femoral sagittal angle (FSA), tibial coronal angle (TCA), and tibial sagittal angle (TSA) on radiographs. These measurements applied to both fixed‑bearing and mobile‑bearing UKA prostheses. Reference values for optimal alignment and acceptable overhang were derived from the Oxford surgical manual. Outlier thresholds were defined as follows: FCA deviation exceeding ±10° from the neutral axis; FSA greater than 15° of flexion; TCA deviation beyond ±5° from the neutral axis; TSA deviation beyond ±5° from the target of 7°. For component overhang, medial and posterior protrusions exceeding 2 mm were considered outliers, while anterior overhang outliers were those greater than 3 mm. The same software was used for both bearing types, except that 30° was added to FSA for MA‑UKA (based on the implant’s design and clinical discussion) to account for design differences.

Functional outcome assessment

Preoperative International Knee Documentation Committee (IKDC) scores were collected, with a second assessment performed on postoperative day (POD) 30. For the five‑level EuroQol five‑dimensional questionnaire (EQ‑5D‑5L), Knee Society Score (KSS) and visual analog scale (VAS), measurements were taken before surgery, on POD 1, and again at POD 30.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 26.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) was used for all analyses. Continuous data are expressed as mean ± SD, and categorical data as frequencies and percentages. For normally distributed data with equal variances, one‑way ANOVA with LSD post‑hoc tests was applied; otherwise, the Kruskal‑Wallis test was used. Categorical variables were compared using Fisher’s exact or chi‑square test. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Comparison of functional outcomes

Discussion

This study looked at whether surgeon handedness affects alignment accuracy in CM‑UKA and whether MA‑UKA can offset that effect. Four main findings emerged. First, handedness clearly influences CM‑UKA radiographic results: right‑handed surgeons had worse alignment and more outliers on the left (non‑dominant) knee. Second, MA‑UKA mitigated the laterality difference, giving consistently high accuracy on both sides. Third, compared with CM, MA led to less blood loss, shorter hospital stay, and better early functional recovery. Fourth, while handedness strongly affected implant positioning, its impact on short‑term clinical function was limited.

Comparison of clinical outcomes

Robotic cases took a bit longer, but tourniquet time did not differ (Tables 2, 3, Fig. 1). The extra time likely came from system registration and planning, not from more ischemia; safety was not compromised. MA groups had significantly less bleeding and shorter hospital stays (Tables 2, 3, Fig. 1). They also had more accurate implant placement and fewer outliers (Tables 4, 5, Fig. 2). On POD 1 and POD 30, MA groups had higher KSS and EQ‑5D‑5L scores and lower VAS pain scores, showing a clear early rehabilitation benefit (Tables 6, 7). These findings agree with earlier reports that MA enables more precise bone cuts and less soft‑tissue trauma [29, 30]. Interestingly, although handedness significantly affected alignment, it did not significantly affect short‑term function (Tables 4, 7). Accurate implant placement is known to be closely linked to lower revision rates and better long‑term survival [5, 31, 32]. Specifically, Kazarian et al. reported that any alignment outlier significantly increased failure risk compared to implants with no errors, and that posterior overhang and medial overhang were also significant risk factors [5]. Lee et al. further demonstrated, in a cohort with mean 6-year follow-up, that femoral coronal angle (FCA) outliers were significantly associated with higher revision rates independent of surgical technique [33]. A separate study on tibial component overhang found that medial overhang ≥3 mm increased revision risk by over sevenfold with a revision rate of 20.9% [34]. Moreover, a large retrospective study with minimum five-year follow-up identified prosthesis malposition as a high-risk factor for UKA failure, with the malposition group showing a significantly lower five-year survival rate [35]. However, the clinical impact of such deviations—particularly the outliers observed in the CM-left group in the present study—typically manifests as prosthetic wear and aseptic loosening in the medium to long term (>5–10 years), rather than in the short term. Our results suggest that the precision advantage of MA-UKA, together with its mitigation of handedness effects, could lead to lower revision rates and better implant survival over the long term, but this remains a hypothesis that requires verification by future long-term prospective studies with follow-up beyond five years.

Impact of handedness on CM‑UKA

When the surgeon operates on the non-dominant side, the stance, viewing angle, and instrument control are all less favorable. This predisposes to osteotomy errors and poor implant placement. CM arthroplasty relies on visual estimation and hand‑guided instruments, with no quantitative feedback to compensate for these variations [17, 18]. In our study, the CM‑left group had significantly higher outlier rates than the CM‑right group for mTFA, TCA, FSA, TSA, and medial/posterior overhang (Table 4, Fig. 2). This matches previous findings in THA and TKA [11–15]. It also aligns with Cao et al. [16], who pointed out that handedness has a greater impact on the femoral components after UKA. Our earlier TKA study [15] and the present one both show that even experienced senior surgeons cannot fully avoid this systematic bias. Handedness‑related error seems inherent to conventional manual techniques and hard to overcome solely by experience.

Mechanisms by which MA mitigates handedness effects

Why does Mako work? Because it standardizes and objectifies the whole process. First, CT‑based 3D planning is done before surgery, with no influence from the surgeon’s stance, view, or which side they are operating on. This removes subjective variability from the start [15, 22]. Second, intraoperative haptic feedback keeps bone cuts strictly within a pre‑defined safe zone. It compensates for any hand instability or awkward posture, so the actual cuts match the virtual plan regardless of handedness [7, 8, 15]. Third, the robot quantifies flexion‑extension gap balance. Instead of relying on “feel”, it gives objective, measurable data, reducing handedness bias during soft‑tissue balancing [36, 37]. Fourth, the robotic arm is highly reproducible. Left or right knee, the cutting and implant steps are performed with identical precision, turning a “subjective, feel‑based” surgery into a “data‑driven, objective” one [15, 38, 39].

Cost‑effectiveness and operational considerations of MA‑UKA

While our results demonstrate that MA‑UKA offers better alignment accuracy, less blood loss, shorter hospital stay, and improved early functional recovery, we acknowledge two major drawbacks of robotic surgery: prolonged operative time and higher costs. In our study, the operative time of MA-UKA was longer than that of CM-UKA, which is consistent with previous research reports [40]. However, tourniquet time did not differ significantly between groups, suggesting that the additional time was primarily due to robotic registration and planning rather than increased ischemia. Regarding costs, the Mako system requires significant capital investment and disposable expenses [36]. Nevertheless, recent analyses suggest that robotic UKA can be cost‑effective in high‑volume settings and when accounting for shorter hospital stays and potential long‑term revision reduction [39, 41, 42]. Nevertheless, because healthcare systems vary in reimbursement structures and volume thresholds, the cost‑effectiveness of MA‑UKA should be assessed locally. Future prospective studies incorporating formal health economic evaluations are needed to determine whether the clinical advantages of MA‑UKA justify its additional costs in different healthcare settings.

All together, the Mako robot creates a consistent, standardized surgical system that successfully reduces the impact of handedness. In TKA, we already showed that MA‑TKA mitigates the handedness effect seen in CM‑TKA, giving consistent alignment on both knees [15]. In THA, Ong et al. [43] found that a novel fluoroscopy‑based robotic system reduced laterality differences in cup placement compared with manual THA. Our study now provides direct evidence for the same concept in UKA.

Limitations

A number of limitations warrant acknowledgment. First, the retrospective design introduces possible selection bias. Second, the observation period was brief (only up to postoperative day 30), which is the most important limitation of this study. Medium- to long-term follow-up (≥5 years) is necessary to determine whether the radiographic outliers observed in the CM-left group ultimately translate into higher rates of aseptic loosening, wear, or revision. Consequently, the functional advantages demonstrated in the MA-UKA groups should be interpreted as early recovery benefits, and not as evidence of long-term superiority over CM-UKA. Third, all five operating surgeons were right‑hand dominant; left‑handed practitioners were not included. Consequently, our study only demonstrates that Mako robot-assisted UKA mitigates the impact of right‑handed surgeon handedness. Whether left‑handed surgeons make similar errors on the right knee (i.e., the bilateral symmetry of the dominant hand theory) remains unknown. This limits the generalizability of our conclusions to the broader surgical population, including left‑handed surgeons. Future prospective studies that include left‑handed surgeons with adequate sample sizes are needed to validate whether the mitigating effect of robotic assistance on handedness bias is truly symmetrical across both dominant hand types. Fourth, because only experienced surgeons performed the procedures, our findings may not be extendable to those with less clinical experience.

Conclusion

Handedness significantly affects component alignment in CM‑UKA, with worse deviations on the non‑dominant side. MA‑UKA markedly mitigates those handedness‑related errors, giving equally high accuracy on left and right knees. MA‑UKA also reduces blood loss, shortens hospital stay, and accelerates early functional recovery. It is an effective tool to overcome the variability caused by surgeon handedness, improve implant positioning consistency, and raise overall surgical quality. MA‑UKA deserves wider clinical adoption.
