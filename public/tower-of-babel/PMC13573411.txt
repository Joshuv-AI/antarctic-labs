Integrated physiological, biochemical, and transcriptomic analyses reveal lead-induces stress responses and tolerance of bread wheat (Triticum aestivum L.)
Authors: Md. Ramjan Sheikh, Md. Mukul Hossain, Akhi Akter, Md. Ahsan Rezwan Rahman, Abdullah Al Ahsan Alvee, Md. Imrul Quayes Adnan, Md. Ekhlas Uddin, Md. Abu Sayed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLead (Pb) pollution is a severe environmental condition that inhibits the growth of plants by causing oxidative stress and interference with cellular and molecular functions. Genotypic variation in stress tolerance has been identified in wheat varieties (Triticum aestivum L.), but there has been limited evidence of integrated physiological, biochemical, and transcriptomic responses to Pb.ResultsPb exposure (0–3 mM lead acetate) had significant effect on shoot height and root length in all wheat varieties with root being more sensitive than shoot. The BARI Gom 30 genotype variety showed a relatively higher tolerance with BARI Gom 25 being the most sensitive. The markers of oxidative stress (H2O2 and MDA) were increased in a dose-dependent manner, which indicates enhanced cellular damage under Pb stress. The initial increase in antioxidant enzymes (CAT, POD, and APX) occurred at moderate intensity and declined at high concentrations, indicating that defense capacity is broken down at high levels of stress. Transcriptomic analysis of wheat root tissues (GEO accession: GSE235844) identified differentially expressed genes that were related to regulation of oxidative stress, ion transport, protein degradation, and transcriptional control. These molecular responses were correlated with observed physiological and biochemical alterations, which indicate well-coordinated mechanisms of stress-responses. Dataset integration revealed the important pathways that are involved in redox balance and stress adaptation.ConclusionThe physiological, biochemical, and molecular responses involved in Pb toxicity in wheat are interconnected with each other. Genotypic differences in tolerance highlight the importance of genetic factors in stress adaptation. This integrative analysis provides mechanistic insights into Pb stress responses and supports future breeding of heavy metal–tolerant wheat varieties.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-08957-1.

Introduction

Heavy metal pollution is the most serious environmental issue threatening the sustainability of agriculture and the environment worldwide. Lead (Pb) is a highly toxic and widely distributed pollutant primarily caused by industrial activities, vehicle emissions, mining, and excessive use of contaminated fertilizers [1]. Pb contamination is a new challenge in Bangladesh because of the rapid industrialization, urbanization and intensive agricultural activities which have caused accumulation of heavy metals in agricultural soils. Even though Pb is not a necessary nutrient in the growth of plant life, as a nutrient readily absorbed through the soil, it can be deposited in any plant-based tissues, leading to severe phytotoxic effects. Overall, the level of Pb exceeding 30-50 mg/kg in soil can be defined as toxic to most plant species, which leads to the significant compromising of physiological and biochemical processes [2].

Pb toxicity has negative effects on seed germination, seedling growth, and morphology of plants through interference with phytochrome synthesis and cellular metabolism. It significantly reduces root and shoot growth, biomass accumulation, nutrient uptake, and photosynthetic efficiency, ultimately resulting in decreased crop yield and quality [3]. The extent of Pb-induced toxicity largely depends on several factors, including its concentration, chemical form, soil characteristics, and plant species [2]. The excessive generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), including hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) is one of the key mechanisms of Pb-induced damage, that causes oxidative stress, lipid peroxidation, and cell damage. The build-up of malondialdehyde (MDA) is generally regarded as a sign of lipid peroxidation [4, 5]. The plants have antioxidant defense systems to mitigate oxidative stress, which includes enzymes (ascorbate peroxidase (APX), catalase (CAT), and peroxidase (POX)) and non-enzymatic defense mechanisms like concentrative vacuoles resembling stomata [6].

Nevertheless, with high levels of Pb, these defense systems may be overloaded, causing growth retardation and dysfunction of metabolism. One of the most significant staple crops in the world and in Bangladesh is wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) which is especially sensitive to contaminated soils containing high amounts of Pb [7]. It is also essential to understand the physiological and biochemical responses of wheat to Pb stress to develop ways to provide enhanced tolerance and sustain a productive state [8]. Even though several studies have managed to report physiological responses of wheat to Pb stress, relatively very little is available in the forms of integrated physiological integrated with transcriptomic approaches, especially in the 13 different varieties of wheat [9]. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the effects of Pb stress on wheat germination, seedling growth, ROS (H₂O₂) accumulation, lipid peroxidation (MDA content), and antioxidant enzyme activities (APX, CAT, and POX), along with transcriptomic insights to better understand the underlying stress response mechanisms. The hypothesis is that various wheat varieties have different tolerance levels to Pb stress, which is correlated with various physiological responses and patterns of gene expression.

Materials and methods

Experimental design and plant material

This experiment was conducted under controlled conditions of the laboratory at the Department of Biochemistry and Molecular Biology, Gono Bishwabidyalay with Randomized Complete Block Design (RCBD), followed by Sheikh et al. [10]. Four genotypes varieties of wheat, BARI Gom 25, BARI Gom 30, BWMRI Gom 1 and BWMRI Gom 2 were collected from the wheat germplasm center of Hajee Mohammad Danesh Science and Technology University, Dinajpur, Bangladesh.

Seed sterilization and germination setup

To eliminate all the contaminants, wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) seeds were surface sterilized in 70% ethanol (1 min) followed by 3 times with sterile distilled water. The sterilized seeds were then used in the germination process. To germinate, twenty seeds were sown on two sheets of filter paper in sterile plastic Petri dishes. Each treatment was carried out with three biological replicates. Lead (Pb) stress was introduced using aqueous solutions of lead acetate [Pb(C2H3O2)2] at concentrations of 0 (control), 0.5, 1.5, 2.0 and 3.0 mM. These concentrations were chosen according to previous research and reference to the growing degrees of Pb toxicity. Lead acetate was used because it dissolves easily in water and provides a consistent source of Pb ions. The corresponding treatment solution was added to each Petri dish every day, to ensure that the filter paper was kept moist all through the experiment. The pH of all solutions was kept at approximately 6.0 to ensure proper germination of seed and the presence of Pb. To eliminate the influence of light on the germination process and early growth of the seedlings, the Petri dishes were incubated in darkness at 25 ± 2 °C. To conduct the experiment, the experimental duration was 12 days, which is adequate to determine the initial reactions of wheat seedlings to Pb stress.

Seedling growth parameters

Seedling growth parameters were measured 12 days after sowing. The seedlings were carefully uprooted and gently washed with distilled water to remove adhering particles. The root length (cm) and shoot length (cm) were measured using a ruler. Mean values were done on randomly taken seedlings in each of the treatment groups.

Determinations of antioxidant enzyme activities and oxidative markers

Preparation of plant extracts for enzyme assays

Homogenization of leaf samples was done at 4 °C in 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer solution at pH 8.0, containing 1% (w/v) polyvinylpyrrolidone and 5 mM cysteine. Again, the mixture after homogenization was filtered and centrifuged for 20 min at 15,000×g, and the supernatant was used for antioxidant enzyme assay.

Determination of catalase (CAT) activity

Catalase (EC: 1.11.1.6) activity was calculated as described earlier Sheikh et al. [10], with necessary modifications. Exactly a volume of 0.7 ml of 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 8.0) was poured into a cuvette, where 0.1 ml of EDTA, along with 0.1 ml of H2O2, was added and mixed well. 4 °C temperature was maintained in all stages. Reaction was initiated by adding 0.1 ml of enzyme extract, and then changes were recorded in absorbance exactly at 240 nm at an interval of 30 s for two minutes. Finally, the catalase activity was calculated from the reduction in absorbance per minute when the extinction coefficient of H2O2 was 40 M−1cm− 1. Determination of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) activity

H2O2 and MDA content were identified according to the suggestion of Sheikh et al. [10]. To identify H2O2 content, Homogenization of 0.1 g of fresh leaf sample in a mortar with pestle using 1 ml of 0.1% tri-chloro-acetic acid (TCA) is done at 4 °C. The samples underwent centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 15 min, followed by the supernatant being kept in the dark for 1 h after mixing with phosphate buffer (10 mM, pH 7.0) and potassium iodide (1 M) (In the ratio 0.5 ml: 0.5 ml: 1 ml). Absorbance of the final solution was measured at 390 nm. All the steps were performed at 4 °C, apart from absorbance measurement.

Determination of the malondialdehyde (MDA) content

About 0.1 g of leaf tissue (with similar age, and young expanded leaf) was ground into powder with liquid nitrogen, and then the powder was put into a tube containing 1 ml 0.1% (w/v) TCA and mixed by inverting the tube to homogenize the leaf tissue [11]. It was centrifuged and homogenized the samples at 10,000×g for 10 min, and the supernatant was transferred to a new tube. 4 ml of 20% TCA containing 0.5% TBA was added to the supernatant and mixed well. The mixture was boiled at 95 °C for 15 min and quickly cooled on ice (TBA can interact with MDA and result in a red compound in acidic buffer, so the content of MDA can be calculated by measuring the density of the resulting red compound with a spectrophotometer at 532 nm. The high temperature can accelerate the reaction, and the low temperature can inhibit it. The mixture was centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 5 min, and the supernatant was transferred to a new tube. Absorbance of the resulting solution was recorded at 532 nm. All the steps were performed at 4 °C except the absorbance measurement.

Transcriptomics data analysis and in silico validation

Preprocessing and acquisition of data

Available transcriptomic information of bread wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) was retrieved from the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database [12]. The data set that was used in this study was a high-throughput RNA sequencing experiment (accession: GSE235844) which comprised of the gene expression profiles of the study which were gathered using the root tissues of several genotypes varieties of wheat. Although the dataset has not been generated under stress conditions of lead (Pb), it was selected based on the grounds of its biological relevance. It has been demonstrated that Pb toxicity primarily affects the morphology of root, nutrient uptake and ion homeostasis and thus root transcriptomic datasets can serve as important biological proxy to examine conserved responses to stress associated with molecular processes. This type of proxy-based method has been widely employed in the field of plant stress biology when datasets that are condition-specific are unavailable, especially in the identification of common pathways such as oxidative stress regulation, ion transport disruption, and transcriptional reprogramming [13, 14]. GEO supplementary files obtained data in raw count format and extracted these data locally. The individual sample count files were imported into R (v.4.3.0) and files of gene-level count matrices were built by combining all the samples based on the known gene identifier. Other columns like gene length that did not express were eliminated and only columns of biological replicates were retained. The genes that were expressed in all the samples were conserved to provide consistency to further downstream analyses. FilterByExpr function of edgeR package was used to further improve statistical power by filtering out lowly expressed genes [15]. The method to correct the compositional differences between samples was the trimmed mean of M-values (TMM) to normalize the library size.

Exploratory data analysis

Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted on the log2-transformed values of the counts per million (logCPM). The PCA was calculated with the help of prcomp function, and the first two principal components were represented with the help of bar graphs. Analysis of differentially expressed genes. Analysis of the differential gene expression was carried out according to the limma-voom pipeline that is optimized to work with the RNA-seq data [16]. Normalized count data were transformed with the voom function to estimate the relationship between means and variances as well as produce precision weights. Design matrix was built upon predetermined experimental groups which were based on clustering of genotypes varieties. The transformed expression data were fitted using the lmFit function followed by empirical Bayes of moderation using the eBayes to enhance the precision of variance estimation. Using the following thresholds, differently expressed genes (DEGs) were detected: absolute log2 fold change greater than or equal to 1 and adjusted p-value (BenjaminiHochberg false discovery rate) less than 0.05. Identification of candidate genome and functional annotation. As there were no freely available fully curated functional annotation databases of wheat, gene annotation was conducted using the IWGSC reference genome annotation file. Where possible, gene identifiers were also mapped to existing descriptions. Manual screening of significant differentially expressed genes in their role in stress-responsive pathways was further screened using published literature and biological databases. Special focus was given to genes linked with metal ion transport (such as ZIP, NRAMP, and ABC transporter), oxidative stress response (including superoxide dismutase, catalase, and ascorbate peroxidase) and transcriptional control (including WRKY, MYB and NAC transcription factor).

Data visualization

Transcriptomic data were visualized using R packages, such as ggplot2 [17], and pheatmap. Principal component analysis plots were applied to assess sample clustering, volcano plots were used to visualize differential gene expression, heatmaps were produced to display the patterns of expression of the top differentially expressed genes using Z-score normalization and MA plots were used to assess the distribution of patterns of variation of the top differentially expressed genes using Z-score normalization. All the figures were created at a resolution of 300 dpi to conform to the requirements of publication. Mechanistic inference / in silico validation. To test biological relevance, identified differentially expressed genes/associated pathways were compared to already published studies on heavy metal stress responses in wheat and related cereal crops. The interpretation of potential Pb-associated molecular responses was interpreted using predicted stress-response mechanisms that are conserved, such as oxidative stress signaling, ion homeostasis perturbation, and transcriptional reprogramming. The transcriptional signatures observed though not Pb-specific were interpreted in the context of experimentally observed phenotypic effects, such as root growth inhibition and reduced physiological performance under Pb exposure. In this integrative method, a mechanistic inference can be made using conserved networks of stress-responsive genes. Statistical analysis All the statistical calculations were done using the R package (R v.4.3.0). As a test-correction method to control the false discovery rate, multiple testing correction was done using the Benjamini-Hochberg method [18]. Statistical significance was an adjusted p-value, which was below 0.05.

Results

Stress effects of Pb on plant growth

Two-way ANOVA showed that Pb treatment and wheat variety significantly affected plant height (PH) (p < 0.05), but root length (RL) was significantly affected by Pb treatment only (p < 0.01) (Table 1). The interaction between variety and treatment was not significant (p > 0.05), suggesting a consistent response pattern among varieties. The growth of the plants progressively reduced with increasing Pb levels (Table 2). Under control conditions (T1), all varieties showed the highest PH and RL with higher Pb levels (T4-T5) showing significant growth inhibition. Among the varieties, the PH of BARI Gom 30 under stress remained relatively higher compared to BARI Gom 25, which has a greater reduction. BWMRI Gom 1 and BWMRI Gom 2 showed medium responses. Compared to plant height, root length was more sensitive to Pb stress, and the decline was attenuated despite moderate levels of the treatment. The root development was highly suppressed at the maximum concentration of Pb in all varieties. Index of tolerance and rate of reduction. The rate of reduction together with the index of tolerance (TI) further explained varietal reactions under Pb pressure (Table 3). The percentage decrease of both PH and RL was dependent on the concentration of Pb, with the values of TI decreasing respectively. The TI values were relatively higher in BARI Gom 30 with high Pb levels compared to when under low Pb levels, showing a higher tolerance. Accordingly, BARI Gom 25 exhibited significant tolerance to TI, indicating more sensitivity, whereas BWMRI Gom 1 and BWMRI Gom 2 showed mild tolerance.

Graphical presentations of morphological responses

Graphical analysis verified an obvious dose-dependent relationship between the concentration of Pb and the inhibition of growth (Fig. 1a-e). Scatter and line plots have shown that PH and RL decreased consistently, but the reduction in RL was sharper. These results were also supported by morphological observations (Fig. 2). The control seedlings exhibited normal growth, with the accumulating levels of Pb affecting the reduction of vigour, retarded shoot and poorly developed root systems. BARI Gom 30 was relatively better maintained in its morphology during the stressful condition whereas BARI Gom 25 had showed significant growth suppression.

Lead acetate effects on antioxidant enzymes and oxidative markers

Two-way ANOVA showed that treatment, variety, and their interaction had significant effects on CAT, POD, and APX activities (p < 0.001; Table 4). The levels of enzyme activities were observed to increase between T1 and T3 and decrease with higher levels of stress (T4-T5) (Fig. 3). Optimal enzyme activities were found at T3, which shows that antioxidant defense is optimally activated under moderate stress (Table 5). BWMRI Gom 1 and BWMRI Gom 2 exhibited relatively higher enzyme activities during severe stress, whilst BARI Gom 30 experienced relatively low responses.

Discussion

This study found lead (Pb) toxicity had a significant adverse effect on wheat growth, with root being more sensitive than shoot. This could be attributed to the fact that roots are in direct contact with Pb in the growth medium. The low mobility of Pb leads to its potential accumulation in root tissues and disruption of cell division, cell elongation, and cell membrane stability. Consequently, root functions become affected, and water and nutrient uptake is impaired, which ultimately leads to a decline in overall growth in plants [19, 20]. The identified decreases in plant height and root length at higher Pb levels are in line with established toxic threshold levels of Pb, where the metal inhibits enzyme activity and hormonal balance [21]. The problem is especially topical in Bangladesh where the concentration of Pb in the agricultural soil is rising because of industrial discharge and urbanization. High concentrations of Pb in soil are threatening to crop productivity and food safety and therefore wheat is a significant model crop in such studies [22].

The decrease in tolerance index (TI) with increasing Pb levels further confirms the negative effects of Pb stress. Nevertheless, variation between the varieties of wheat indicates that adaptive capacity can vary. The tolerance in this study was relatively higher in BARI Gom 30, and it was more sensitive in BARI Gom 25. This variation might relate to a disparity in metal sequestration, antioxidant defense, and stress signaling, although these processes were not directly measured [23, 24]. Pb stress has been known to cause oxidative stress by increasing the formation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), such as hydrogen peroxide (H2O2). This in turn results in the activation of antioxidant enzymes such as catalase (CAT), peroxidase (POD) and ascorbate peroxidase (APX). That these enzymes are more effective under moderate Pb stress, would suggest that an active system that gets detoxified through the action of these enzymes. During moderate stress conditions an increase in the activity of antioxidant enzymes (CAT, POD, APX) was observed, which is an indication of activation of the plant defense system against ROS.

Nevertheless, the activities were reduced, perhaps due to excess oxidative damage, enzyme inactivation, and missing vital substrates, indicating that the antioxidant defense system had started to break down in extreme stress. This exposition is supported by the fact that the build-up of H2O2 and malondialdehyde (MDA) which are resultant indicators of oxidative damage and membrane lipid peroxidation have increased. The increase in these markers in the higher levels of the sample, but not in other varieties, indicates a poorer antioxidant cover and a higher detoxification, respectively. This association is further supported by correlation analysis which reveals higher activity of antioxidant enzymes is correlated with lower oxidative damage [25]. Principal component analysis (PCA) also differentiated stress levels and related types of tolerance exhibiting stronger antioxidant activity. It implies that antioxidant efficiency is a significant constituent of Pb tolerance, but other mechanisms can also play a role [24].

To further enhance the understanding, the analysis also used transcriptomic data of wheat root as a means of examining the potential underlying mechanisms. The dataset was not grown in Pb stress, but the data shows the general stress-responsive responses. The differentially expressed genes (DEGs) identified comprise the transport processes, stress signaling, transcriptional regulation, and protein turnover. An example of this is the observed upregulation in the expression of sugar transporter genes (members of the SWEET family) when exposed to a stressor. Likewise, the presence of ubiquitin-related proteins implies activation of pathways involved in protein degradation that help to eliminate damaged proteins and maintain cellular homeostasis [13, 14]. Interestingly, there were also differentially expressed some of the genes, which were generally known to resist diseases. This contributes to the idea of a crosstalk between biotic and abiotic response of a given specimen to stress. Recent studies indicate that this defense-related signaling usually associated with pathogen response can be provoked by reactive oxygen species (ROS) signaling, which is triggered in the case of Pb stress [13].

Moreover, the fact that transcription factors were detected among the DEGs indicates the coordination of the regulation of stress responses, the genes related to lipid metabolism and membrane processes could help to maintain cellular integrity under stress conditions. However, some DEGs remain uncharacterized, and their functions are not yet clear. Furthermore, the transcriptomic results were not obtained on Pb-treated plants; therefore, these findings are to be understood with caution. There are probably more general responses involving stress-response systems than Pb-specific responses [14]. In general, in this work, it is proposed that Pb stress inhibits wheat growth primarily by damaging roots and inducing oxidative stress. The variation in the tolerance across species seems to be correlated with the effectiveness of antioxidant defense mechanisms. Although integration of physiological and transcriptomic measurements offers valuable insight, further experiments involving Pb-specific genes expression analysis and functional validation are required to confirm these mechanisms.

Limitations and future directions

This study has some limitations. The transcriptomic data was found on publicly available data that were not produced under the stress of Pb, limiting direct mechanistic interpretation. The brief experimental period (12 days) does not indicate the long-term effects but rather that of a brief period. Also, candidate genes were not functionally validated and whereas controlling conditions may not be fully applicable to field conditions. In the future, it is important to conduct Pb-specific transcriptomic analyses at multiple time points and further validate key genes functionality. It would be better to extend the period of experiment and use field tests to enhance applicability. Multi-omics strategy would also help us better understand the Pb tolerance mechanisms in wheat.

Conclusion

This experiment shows that Pb stress has a pronounced dose-dependent growth-retarding effect on wheat, with root being the most sensitive parts of the plant. Significant varietal differences depict that genetic variability will play an important role in Pb tolerance under severe environment and BARI Gom 30 has achieved superior growth performance with the comparatively high stress tolerance being under severe conditions. Exposure to Pb resulted in oxidative stress in the form of increased levels of H2O2/MDA, and elevated activity of antioxidant enzymes (CAT, POD and APX). Nevertheless, the deterioration of the enzyme action under the severe stress indicates a limitation of the antioxidant defense system beyond the critical point. All these findings could be further supported by transcriptomic analysis, which examined stress-responsive genes in the context of redox control, ion movement, and transcriptional control. In general, the findings suggest that the coordinated regulation of antioxidant defense and stress-responsive gene expression regulates the Pb tolerance in wheat. These results present support of future functional research and can be reflected in developing Pb-tolerant wheat cultivars.
