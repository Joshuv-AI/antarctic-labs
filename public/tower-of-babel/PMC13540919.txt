Long-term epidemiology of bacterial pathogens and antimicrobial resistance in a secondary hospital in China: a 12-year retrospective study
Authors: Li Yingxing, Guo Lina, Li Fangyuan, Li Jie, Guo Wenting, Zhao Hongmei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAntimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a growing global health threat. However, long-term epidemiological data from secondary hospitals—a critical component of the healthcare system—remain limited, particularly in China, where national surveillance is largely based on data from tertiary hospitals. Understanding the local epidemiology in these settings is vital for developing effective regional control strategies and guiding empirical antibiotic therapy.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective analysis of 2,661 clinical bacterial isolates collected from a secondary hospital in Shandong Province, China, between 2014 and 2025. Pathogen distribution was analyzed across patient sex, age groups, specimen types, and temporal trends. Antimicrobial susceptibility data from 2017 to 2025 were used to assess resistance profiles of major pathogens. Key resistant organisms, including methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL)-producing Escherichia coli and Klebsiella pneumoniae, carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales (CRE), carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii (CRAB), and carbapenem-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa (CRPA), were further evaluated.ResultsGram-negative bacteria predominated (83.1%), with E. coli (30.9%) and K. pneumoniae (25.6%) as the most common pathogens. Distinct ecological niche patterns were observed: E. coli was overrepresented in urinary and wound samples and in female patients, whereas K. pneumoniae, P. aeruginosa, and A. baumannii were associated with respiratory samples and elderly male patients. Over the 12-year period, the detection rate of K. pneumoniae increased gradually, while that of S. aureus declined. Marked interspecies differences in antimicrobial resistance were evident, with A. baumannii exhibiting high resistance rates (> 60%) to most tested antibiotics, and E. coli showing substantial resistance to commonly used agents, including ampicillin, fluoroquinolones, and gentamicin (> 50%). Among the selected priority resistant pathogens, CRAB showed the highest detection rate (67.1%), while CRE demonstrated a gradual upward trend. ESBL-producing E. coli was the major contributor to AMR burden across most specimen types, particularly in urinary tract infections, whereas ESBL-producing K. pneumoniae and CRAB predominated in respiratory samples, highlighting niche-specific resistance patterns.ConclusionOur findings reveal ecological and temporal patterns of bacterial distribution and antimicrobial resistance in a secondary hospital setting. By integrating long-term surveillance with stratified analyses across patient populations and specimen types, this study provides context-specific epidemiological evidence that may inform empirical antimicrobial therapy and antimicrobial stewardship, strengthen local AMR surveillance in county-level secondary hospitals, and complement national surveillance programs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12866-026-05445-2.

Introduction

Bacterial infectious diseases remain a major global public health challenge, and the continued spread of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) has further exacerbated this crisis, imposing substantial clinical and economic burdens worldwide [1–3]. A recent global burden analysis reported that bacterial AMR was associated with 4.71 million deaths worldwide in 2021, including 1.14 million deaths directly attributable to AMR, and is projected to cause a substantial increase in mortality by 2050 [4]. In addition to increased mortality, AMR is associated with prolonged hospitalization, higher rates of intensive care unit (ICU) admission, increased risk of treatment failure, and dramatically elevated healthcare costs, collectively threatening both global health security and economic sustainability [5, 6].

The emergence and spread of multidrug-resistant (MDR), extensively drug-resistant (XDR), and pan-drug-resistant (PDR) pathogens have further complicated the management of bacterial infections [7–9]. According to the Bacterial Priority Pathogens List (BPPL, 2024) released by the World Health Organization (WHO), the critical priority pathogens include carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii (CRAB), carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales (CRE), and third-generation cephalosporin-resistant Enterobacterales, which are particularly concerning due to their high mortality rates, limited therapeutic options, and frequent association with healthcare-associated infections [10, 11]. Beyond the critical group, the WHO BPPL also highlights several high-priority pathogens, including carbapenem-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa (CRPA), vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus faecium (VRE) and methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), all of which contribute significantly to healthcare-associated and community-acquired infections worldwide [10, 11].

The epidemiology of AMR varies substantially across geographic regions, bacterial species, and clinical contexts. Although strengthened infection prevention and antimicrobial stewardship programs have contributed to the control of selected resistant pathogens in some high-income settings, low- and middle-income countries continue to experience a disproportionate burden of AMR [12]. Notably, carbapenem-resistant organisms are widely distributed across regions, with comparable overall burdens reported in Asia-Pacific, Europe, North America, and Latin America [13–15]. However, this burden is driven mainly by non-fermenting Gram-negative pathogens, particularly Acinetobacter baumannii and Pseudomonas aeruginosa, which consistently demonstrate markedly higher resistance rates than Enterobacterales and account for the majority of carbapenem-resistant infections [13, 16]. Furthermore, resistance patterns vary depending on the site of infection, with higher rates observed in respiratory infections than in bloodstream infections, suggesting that surveillance limited to specific specimen types may underestimate the true burden of AMR [17]. These findings indicate that AMR is shaped not only by regional differences but also by pathogen biology and infection context, underscoring the need for comprehensive and context-aware surveillance systems.

Despite continuous progress in national AMR surveillance, important evidence gaps remain in China. Surveillance programs such as the China Antimicrobial Surveillance Network (CHINET) and the China Antimicrobial Resistance Surveillance System (CARSS) have substantially improved our understanding of bacterial pathogen epidemiology and antimicrobial resistance [18]. However, secondary hospitals remain disproportionately underrepresented in national surveillance networks relative to their role in China’s healthcare system. China has approximately 12,294 secondary hospitals, nearly three times the number of tertiary hospitals (4,111) [19], whereas among the 74 hospitals participating in the 2024 CHINET surveillance network, only 13 (17.6%) were secondary hospitals, compared with 61 (82.4%) tertiary hospitals [20]. This marked imbalance suggests that current national surveillance data may not fully capture the epidemiology and resistance characteristics of bacterial pathogens in secondary healthcare settings.

County-level secondary hospitals serve as frontline healthcare providers for large populations and manage substantial numbers of community-acquired infections as well as common healthcare-associated infections. Differences in patient populations, referral patterns, healthcare resources, and antimicrobial prescribing practices between these hospitals and tertiary referral centers may result in distinct pathogen distributions and AMR profiles. However, while numerous English-language studies have characterized long-term pathogen distributions and AMR trends in Chinese tertiary hospitals, comprehensive longitudinal surveillance studies from county-level secondary hospitals remain scarce. This evidence gap limits a comprehensive understanding of regional AMR epidemiology and the development of context-specific antimicrobial stewardship and infection control strategies. Long-term surveillance in county-level secondary hospitals is therefore needed to complement existing national surveillance networks and improve understanding of AMR epidemiology across different levels of the healthcare system. This need is particularly relevant to the implementation of the WHO BPPL framework, because the burden and distribution of priority pathogens may vary across healthcare settings and cannot be fully characterized by surveillance data derived predominantly from tertiary referral hospitals. Setting-specific surveillance of WHO priority pathogens in county-level secondary hospitals is therefore important for identifying local epidemiological patterns and informing context-appropriate antimicrobial stewardship and infection control strategies.

To address these knowledge gaps, we conducted a comprehensive 12-year retrospective analysis of all clinical bacterial isolates collected from a county-level secondary hospital in Shandong Province, China. We systematically characterized the distribution patterns of bacterial isolates across patient sex, age groups, and specimen types, and evaluated temporal trends in isolation rates over the study period. In addition, we assessed antimicrobial resistance profiles of major pathogens against commonly used antibiotics. Particular emphasis was placed on key resistant organisms, including MRSA, ESBL-producing E. coli, ESBL-producing K. pneumoniae, CRE, CRAB and CRPA, with a focus on their temporal trends as well as their distribution and burden across different patient age groups and specimen types. Through this analysis, we identified both consistent epidemiological patterns aligned with national and global observations, as well as distinct features that may be characteristic of secondary hospital settings compared with tertiary care centers. Our findings provide real-world epidemiological evidence to support rational antimicrobial use, optimize infection control strategies, and strengthen AMR surveillance in secondary hospitals, while offering insights to inform regional and national AMR control efforts.

Materials and methods

Study setting and isolate collection

All clinical isolates included in this study were collected from patients at Xintai Second People’s Hospital (Shandong Province, China) between January 1, 2014 and December 31, 2025. Patient demographic information, including age and sex, as well as relevant clinical data, were retrieved from electronic medical records.

Deduplication strategy

To minimize bias introduced by repeated sampling from the same patient, deduplication was performed using two complementary strategies depending on the analytical objective (Figure S1). For analyses independent of specimen type (temporal trends, age- and sex-specific distributions, and antimicrobial resistance patterns), only the first isolate of a given bacterial species from each patient within a calendar year was included, regardless of the anatomical site of infection. This approach reduces overrepresentation of repeated isolates of the same bacterial species from the same patient and ensures comparability across patient-level characteristics. For specimen-specific analyses, the first isolate of each bacterial species from each patient within each specimen type per calendar year was retained, whereas additional isolates of the same species within the same specimen type during the same year were excluded. This strategy preserves biologically meaningful differences across anatomical sites and enables accurate characterization of ecological niche-specific distributions of pathogens. These complementary deduplication approaches were applied to balance the need to reduce within-patient sampling bias while maintaining site-specific resolution of pathogen distribution.

Specimen classification

Clinical specimens were categorized into six groups based on anatomical origin. Respiratory tract specimens included sputum, throat swabs, and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid. Sterile body fluids comprised bile, drainage fluids, puncture fluids, synovial fluid, pleural and ascitic fluid, and cerebrospinal fluid. Wound/pus specimens included wound secretions, pus, and tissue exudates. Urinary tract specimens were derived from urine samples, while blood specimens were obtained from peripheral blood cultures. Reproductive tract specimens included vaginal secretions.

Bacterial identification and antimicrobial susceptibility testing

Bacterial identification and antimicrobial susceptibility testing were performed using the XK automated microbiology system (Shandong Xinke, China), a National Medical Products Administration (NMPA)-approved in vitro diagnostic (IVD) device, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Previous studies have demonstrated good agreement between the XK system and the VITEK 32 automated microbiology system for bacterial identification and antimicrobial susceptibility testing [21].

Bacterial identification was based on automated biochemical colorimetric assays, in which biochemical reaction profiles were generated by photoelectric detection and matched against the manufacturer’s reference database for species identification. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed using the broth microdilution-based method implemented in the automated XK system. Species-specific identification and antimicrobial susceptibility testing panels supplied by the manufacturer were selected according to the bacterial species, including the Enterobacteriaceae panel (XK-96 A-C), non-fermenter panel (XK-96 A-F), Staphylococcus panel (XK-96 A-L), and Streptococcus panel (XK-96 A-P). Each isolate was tested using the corresponding manufacturer-provided susceptibility testing panel according to Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines. Minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) were determined automatically. The laboratory information system exported the original MIC values for all isolates.

Before statistical analysis, all MICs were retrospectively interpreted as susceptible, intermediate, or resistant according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) M100, 35th edition (2025) to ensure consistent breakpoint criteria across the entire study period and to avoid artificial temporal variation resulting from changes in CLSI breakpoint definitions over time. Routine internal quality control was performed using Escherichia coli ATCC 25,922, Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 25,923, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa ATCC 27,853 in accordance with CLSI recommendations. Detailed antimicrobial susceptibility analyses were restricted to isolates collected between 2017 and 2025 because routine reporting of MIC values became available only after an upgrade of the automated microbiology system in December 2016.

Statistical analysis

Raw patient information and antimicrobial susceptibility testing data were initially organized and curated using Microsoft Excel. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.2), and graphical visualizations were generated using R or GraphPad Prism (version 8.0). A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

To assess differences in the distribution of bacterial species across patient sex, age groups, and specimen types, contingency tables were constructed and analyzed using Pearson’s chi-square (χ²) test. When expected cell counts were less than 5, Fisher’s exact test was applied as appropriate.

To further characterize distribution patterns and identify specific overrepresentation or underrepresentation of bacterial species and AMR phenotypes across clinical subgroups, standardized residuals from the chi-square test were calculated. Residuals with an absolute value greater than 1.96 (|Z| > 1.96) were considered indicative of significant overrepresentation (positive values) or underrepresentation (negative values).

Temporal trends in bacterial species distribution and antimicrobial resistance phenotypes were evaluated using the Cochran–Armitage trend test and further quantified using univariable logistic regression models, with calendar year entered as a continuous predictor. Analyses of bacterial species distribution covered 2014–2025, whereas AMR trend analyses covered 2017–2025. Odds ratios (ORs) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) represent the annual change in odds associated with each one-year increase in calendar year. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

For AMR burden analysis, the proportion of isolates exhibiting each resistance phenotype relative to the total number of isolates within each group was calculated and compared across patient age groups and specimen types. As resistance phenotypes are not mutually exclusive, individual isolates may contribute to multiple AMR categories.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Xintai Second People’s Hospital, Tai’an, China (reference number 2026-LLYJ-01-01). Although the clinical isolates were collected between 2014 and 2025, this retrospective study was initiated in 2026, and ethical approval was obtained before retrospective extraction and analysis of the de-identified clinical data. The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study and the use of anonymized data. Before statistical analysis, all direct patient identifiers and temporary linkage codes, including patient names, hospital patient identification numbers, and specimen accession numbers, were permanently removed from the analytical dataset. Exact specimen collection dates were converted into calendar-year information for temporal analyses. The final analytical dataset contained only de-identified microbiological and demographic information required for the predefined analyses, and no identifiable personal information was accessible during data analysis.

Results

Distribution features of clinical pathogens from 2014 to 2025

Between 2014 and 2025, a total of 2,661 clinical isolates were recovered from patients across various specimen types in our hospital. Gram-negative bacteria accounted for the majority of isolates (83.1%), whereas Gram-positive bacteria comprised 16.9% (Fig. 1A). Nine bacterial species accounted for more than 1% of all clinical isolates, including Escherichia coli (30.9%), Klebsiella pneumoniae (25.6%), Staphylococcus aureus (10.9%), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (8.6%), Acinetobacter baumannii (5.8%), Proteus mirabilis (4.3%), Enterobacter cloacae (2.1%), Staphylococcus epidermidis (1.5%), and Klebsiella oxytoca (1.4%) (Fig. 1B).

Among all isolates, 55% were obtained from male patients and 45% from female patients. E. coli was significantly overrepresented in female patients (male: female = 44.3%:55.0%), whereas K. pneumoniae (64.1%:35.3%), P. aeruginosa (62.6%:37.4%), and A. baumannii (66.5%:33.5%) were significantly overrepresented in male patients. In contrast, S. aureus and P. mirabilis showed no significant sex-based differences (Fig. 2A and Figure S2A).

To investigate age-related distribution patterns, patients were stratified into four groups: ≤17 years (16.1%), 18–44 years (10.9%), 45–64 years (31.1%), and ≥ 65 years (41.9%). Significant differences in age-specific distribution were observed for most pathogens (P < 0.05), except for P. mirabilis (Fig. 2B and Figure S2B). E. coli was primarily overrepresented in young and middle-aged adults but was significantly underrepresented in both pediatric and elderly populations. In contrast, K. pneumoniae and A. baumannii were significantly overrepresented in elderly patients (≥ 65 years), while being underrepresented in younger groups. S. aureus exhibited a marked predominance in pediatric patients, with reduced prevalence in older age groups. P. aeruginosa showed lower proportions in younger patients and a tendency toward higher prevalence in older adults.

Regarding specimen types, respiratory samples accounted for the largest proportion (50.1%), followed by wound/pus (20.6%), urinary tract (12.7%), blood (8.1%), sterile body fluids (6.1%), and genital tract specimens (2.5%). The distribution of pathogens differed significantly across specimen types, with standardized residual analysis further revealing distinct patterns of specimen-specific overrepresentation (Fig. 2C and Figure S2C). A. baumannii (89.7%), K. pneumoniae (74.5%), and P. aeruginosa (83.1%) were significantly overrepresented in respiratory samples, whereas E. coli (25.7%) and P. mirabilis (25.6%) were strongly overrepresented in urinary specimens. S. aureus (41.4%) showed the most pronounced overrepresentation in wound/pus samples. In contrast, E. coli (31.8%) was significantly underrepresented in respiratory samples, while P. aeruginosa (0.9%) and A. baumannii (1.8%) were underrepresented in urinary samples. These findings indicate clear ecological specialization of major pathogens across specimen types.

Temporal trend analysis revealed distinct patterns among major pathogens over the study period (Fig. 2D), with detailed logistic regression results summarized in Supplementary Table S1. K. pneumoniae demonstrated a significant increasing trend (OR per year = 1.03, 95% CI: 1.00–1.06; P = 0.026), indicating its growing clinical importance. P. aeruginosa also showed a statistically significant upward trend (OR per year = 1.10, 95% CI: 1.05–1.14; P < 0.001), although this increase was largely restricted to the pre-2020 period. In contrast, S. aureus exhibited a significant decreasing trend over time (OR per year = 0.93, 95% CI: 0.89–0.96; P < 0.001). No significant temporal trends were observed for E. coli or A. baumannii, both of which displayed fluctuating detection rates without a consistent directional change. Collectively, these results suggest a dynamic shift in pathogen distribution, characterized by increasing prevalence of K. pneumoniae and P. aeruginosa and a decline in S. aureus.

Antimicrobial resistance patterns of major bacterial pathogens (2017–2025)

Because standardized broth microdilution-based MIC testing was implemented in our hospital after 2016, AMR analyses were restricted to isolates collected between 2017 and 2025. The resistance profiles of the five major pathogens against different classes of antibiotics are summarized in Table 1. E. coli exhibited a high level of resistance overall, with the highest resistance rate observed for ampicillin (87.2%), followed by ciprofloxacin (62.3%) and trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole (61.8%). Notably, resistance to fluoroquinolones was consistently high, with both ciprofloxacin and levofloxacin exceeding 50%. In contrast, resistance to aminoglycosides varied markedly, with a much higher rate for gentamicin (53.0%) compared with amikacin (6.9%). K. pneumoniae showed comparatively lower resistance rates than E. coli across most antibiotics. The highest resistance rates were observed for trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole (42.8%), ciprofloxacin (34.5%), and amoxicillin–clavulanic acid (32.4%). S. aureus demonstrated high resistance to several commonly used antibiotics, particularly penicillin G (87.0%), erythromycin (82.3%), and azithromycin (80.0%). Encouragingly, no resistance to vancomycin or linezolid was detected among S. aureus isolates. P. aeruginosa exhibited relatively moderate resistance levels compared with other pathogens, with the highest resistance rates observed for levofloxacin (23.8%), ciprofloxacin (23.4%), and ceftazidime (17.0%). In contrast, A. baumannii displayed a markedly high resistance profile. Except for piperacillin–tazobactam (51.5%), resistance rates exceeded 60% for nearly all tested antibiotics, including aminoglycosides, cephalosporins, carbapenems, fluoroquinolones, and trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole, highlighting its broad resistance profile.

According to the WHO Bacterial Priority Pathogens List, we focused on selected critical- and high-priority pathogens and analyzed their temporal trends in detection rates from 2017 to 2025, including carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii (CRAB), carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales (CRE), ESBL-producing Escherichia coli, ESBL-producing Klebsiella pneumoniae, and methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) (Table 2). Overall, CRAB showed the highest detection rate (67.1%), followed by ESBL-producing E. coli (52.8%), ESBL-producing K. pneumoniae (25.8%), MRSA (21.4%), and CRE (3.2%). Trend analysis revealed that only CRE exhibited a significant increasing trend over the study period (P < 0.01), corresponding to an estimated annual increase of approximately 19% (OR per year = 1.19, 95% CI: 1.05–1.37). In contrast, no significant temporal trends were observed for CRAB, ESBL-producing E. coli, or ESBL-producing K. pneumoniae (Table S2). The marked year-to-year variability in CRAB detection rates should be interpreted cautiously, as it may reflect unmeasured changes in patient composition, infection control practices, antimicrobial use, or transient clonal dissemination. Although MRSA showed a decreasing tendency, this trend did not reach statistical significance (Table S2). These findings suggest that CRE may represent an emerging threat in this clinical setting. Detailed antibiotic-specific resistance trends for E. coli and K. pneumoniae are provided in Supplementary Tables S3 and S4.

To further characterize the distribution patterns of key resistant pathogens, we quantified the AMR burden across different age groups (Fig. 3A). Overall, the total AMR burden was comparable among age groups, with a slightly higher burden observed in patients aged 18–44 years (34.2%), while the lowest burden was found in the pediatric group (0–17 years, 27%). Across all age groups, ESBL-producing E. coli consistently represented the largest contributor to AMR burden, reflecting its high prevalence among clinical isolates. Notably, the burden of CRAB increased progressively with age, rising from 0% in children to 6.5% in patients aged ≥ 65 years. A similar, albeit less pronounced, trend was observed for CRPA. In contrast, MRSA showed higher burden in younger populations, particularly in the 0–17 (3.7%) and 18–44 (5.7%) age groups, compared to older patients. The burden of CRE remained relatively stable across age groups. ESBL-producing K. pneumoniae exhibited a bimodal distribution, with higher burden in pediatric (7.4%) and elderly patients (8.2%), but markedly lower levels in the 18–44 age group (2.6%). To identify the AMR phenotypes contributing to the overall associations, standardized residual analyses were performed, revealing distinct age-specific (Fig. 3C) overrepresentation and underrepresentation patterns.

We next examined AMR burden across different specimen types (Fig. 3B). Urinary tract specimens exhibited the highest overall AMR burden (45.1%), predominantly driven by ESBL-producing E. coli (34.6%). In contrast, respiratory tract specimens showed a distinct resistance profile, characterized by higher contributions from ESBL-producing K. pneumoniae (12.2%) and CRAB (9.1%), while ESBL-producing E. coli contributed substantially less (5.5%). This marked divergence highlights strong ecological niche specificity in the distribution of resistant pathogens. In urinary tract samples, the AMR burden was largely dominated by ESBL-producing E. coli, with additional contributions from ESBL-producing K. pneumoniae (7.5%) and CRE (2.0%). Conversely, respiratory samples were overrepresented for non-fermenting Gram-negative pathogens and K. pneumoniae. MRSA was most prominently observed in wound/pus samples (5.4%), consistent with its known association with skin and soft tissue infections. CRPA was primarily detected in respiratory (3.0%) and wound/pus (0.7%) specimens, but was rarely identified in other sample types. CRE displayed a relatively uniform distribution across specimen types. These findings were further corroborated by standardized residual heatmap analysis, which demonstrated significant specimen-specific overrepresentation patterns for multiple resistant pathogens (Fig. 3D). To facilitate interpretation of the AMR burden analyses, the corresponding resistance proportions within each bacterial species (or bacterial group), together with the numbers of resistant and total isolates (n/N), are provided in Supplementary Tables S5 and S6.

Further analysis was conducted to evaluate antimicrobial resistance patterns in E. coli and K. pneumoniae across different specimen types (Tables 3 and 4). For both species, resistance to levofloxacin varied significantly by specimen type, with the highest resistance observed in urinary isolates (E. coli, 64.8%; K. pneumoniae, 46.5%) and the lowest in blood isolates (E. coli, 40.7%; K. pneumoniae, 16.0%). In addition, E. coli exhibited significant variation in resistance to aztreonam, with the highest resistance rates detected in respiratory (49.5%) and urinary (46.2%) specimens. Similarly, K. pneumoniae showed marked differences in resistance to trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole, with urinary isolates demonstrating the highest resistance (61.0%) and blood isolates the lowest (32.0%). In contrast, resistance of E. coli to amoxicillin–clavulanic acid displayed an opposite trend, with the highest rates observed in blood (53.6%) and sterile body fluids (51.1%) isolates, and the lowest in respiratory (29.7%) and urinary (32.6%) specimens. Overall, these findings indicate that resistance profiles of major Enterobacterales vary significantly across specimen types, and that the specimen type associated with the highest resistance may differ depending on the antimicrobial agent.

Discussion

In this study, we provide a comprehensive overview of pathogen distribution and AMR patterns in a secondary hospital over a 12-year period. Gram-negative bacteria predominated, with E. coli and K. pneumoniae as the leading pathogens. Clear demographic and specimen-specific patterns were observed: E. coli was mainly associated with female patients and non-respiratory samples, whereas K. pneumoniae, P. aeruginosa, and A. baumannii were overrepresented in male, elderly, and respiratory specimens. Temporally, K. pneumoniae increased while S. aureus declined. Notably, substantial interspecies differences in resistance were identified, with A. baumannii exhibiting high resistance rates across multiple antimicrobial classes and E. coli showing consistently high resistance to commonly used antibiotics. Among the selected antimicrobial-resistant phenotypes, CRAB had the highest detection rate, and CRE showed a gradual increase. AMR burden further demonstrated strong variation across age groups and specimen types, highlighting context-specific epidemiological patterns in this secondary hospital setting.

Our findings are broadly consistent with national surveillance data reported by CHINET and CARSS, which similarly document the predominance of Gram-negative bacteria and the leading roles of E. coli and K. pneumoniae in clinical infections [20, 22]. The high prevalence of ESBL-producing Enterobacterales and the substantial burden of CRAB observed in our study are also in line with nationwide trends. The prevalence of MRSA in our hospital was lower than that reported by national surveillance programs (approximately 28–40%), although the declining trends in S. aureus and MRSA detection rates were consistent with the global decrease in MRSA infections reported in both hospital-acquired and community-associated settings [10, 23]. CRAB remained highly prevalent at levels broadly comparable to national surveillance data (approximately 60–80% in CHINET and 50–60% in CARSS), indicating that carbapenem-resistant A. baumannii continues to represent a substantial challenge even in secondary hospitals, likely because of its remarkable ability to persist in the hospital environment and spread through healthcare-associated transmission [24].

CRE showed an increasing trend during the study period, consistent with the rapid global rise of CRE, particularly in the Asia–Pacific region over the past decade [10]. Nevertheless, its overall prevalence remained notably lower than that reported by CHINET. This difference was particularly evident for CRKP, whereas carbapenem resistance in E. coli was generally comparable to national surveillance findings. The relatively low CRKP prevalence should also be interpreted in the context of pronounced regional heterogeneity in China. CARSS data indicate that Shandong Province has consistently reported a CRKP prevalence of approximately 5–6%, substantially lower than that in several metropolitan regions (approximately 20–30%) and below the national average (~ 10%). Thus, the low CRKP prevalence observed in our hospital may reflect both the regional epidemiological background and characteristics of secondary hospitals, including fewer critically ill patients and potentially lower carbapenem selection pressure.

Resistance to several clinically important antimicrobial agents remained substantial, including high resistance to third-generation cephalosporins among E. coli and substantial erythromycin resistance in S. aureus. Moreover, the increasing trend in CRE highlights the need for continued vigilance even in regions where its current prevalence remains relatively low. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that secondary hospitals face a substantial AMR burden characterized by both persistent resistance among established pathogens and emerging resistance trends, underscoring the importance of sustained antimicrobial stewardship, surveillance, and infection prevention and control measures across all levels of healthcare institutions.

Our study revealed pronounced ecological niche specificity in pathogen distribution across different patient populations and specimen types. E. coli was predominantly overrepresented in urinary and wound-related specimens and was more frequently observed in female and middle-aged patients, supporting its well-established role as a major pathogen in community-acquired infections [25]. In contrast, K. pneumoniae, P. aeruginosa, and A. baumannii were preferentially associated with respiratory specimens and elderly male patients, suggesting a strong link with hospital-acquired infections, particularly ventilator-associated pneumonia and other healthcare-associated conditions [26]. S. aureus was more commonly detected in pediatric patients and was overrepresented in wound/pus specimens, consistent with its known epidemiology in skin and soft tissue infections [27]. These distinct distribution patterns highlight the strong influence of ecological and clinical context on pathogen prevalence. From a clinical perspective, such stratified patterns may facilitate more accurate empirical prediction of likely pathogens based on infection site and patient characteristics, thereby supporting more targeted and effective antimicrobial therapy in secondary hospital settings.

Beyond the niche-specific distribution of bacterial species, antimicrobial-resistant pathogens also exhibited distinct patterns across clinical specimen types. ESBL-producing E. coli was overrepresented in urinary specimens, whereas CRAB, CRPA and ESBL-producing K. pneumoniae were predominantly associated with respiratory specimens, and MRSA was more frequently detected in wound specimens. These patterns may reflect differences in infection-site ecology, healthcare-associated interventions, antimicrobial selective pressures, and pathogen-specific colonization and transmission characteristics. The overrepresentation of ESBL-producing E. coli in urinary specimens may be related to urinary catheterization and antimicrobial exposure favoring resistant Enterobacterales [28], whereas the predominance of CRAB, CRPA and ESBL-producing K. pneumoniae in respiratory specimens may reflect their association with prolonged hospitalization, mechanical ventilation, broad-spectrum antimicrobial exposure, and healthcare-associated transmission [26, 29]. Similarly, the predominance of MRSA in wound specimens is consistent with its established role in skin and soft tissue infections and may be promoted by disruption of skin barriers, repeated wound manipulation, and healthcare-associated transmission [30].

From a clinical and public health perspective, several findings warrant particular attention. First, the extremely high detection rate of CRAB, especially in elderly patients and respiratory samples, underscores its critical role as a major nosocomial pathogen and highlights the urgent need for strengthened infection control measures. Second, the increasing trend of CRE suggests a growing threat that may be influenced by antimicrobial selection pressure, clonal dissemination, or other unmeasured epidemiological factors within healthcare settings. Third, the substantial burden of ESBL-producing E. coli, particularly in urinary tract infections, has important implications for empirical therapy. Fourth, resistance to commonly used antibiotics such as ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, gentamicin and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole was also consistently high, particularly in E. coli. This observation is supported by our previous study on Streptococcus agalactiae, which also demonstrated markedly elevated fluoroquinolone resistance rates in China compared with European countries, suggesting that high levels of fluoroquinolone resistance may represent a broader regional phenomenon rather than being restricted to specific pathogens [31]. These findings have practical implications for clinical management and antimicrobial stewardship in secondary hospitals. Empirical antimicrobial therapy should integrate infection site, patient characteristics, and local resistance epidemiology. In particular, the high prevalence of CRAB and other resistant Gram-negative pathogens in respiratory specimens and the substantial burden of ESBL-producing E. coli in urinary specimens should be considered when selecting initial empirical therapy, especially when rapid molecular resistance diagnostics are unavailable. From an antimicrobial stewardship perspective, hospital-specific antibiograms should be regularly updated, microbiological specimens should be obtained before antimicrobial therapy whenever feasible, and empirical regimens should be promptly reassessed and de-escalated according to culture and susceptibility results. The increasing trend in CRE further supports strengthened local surveillance of carbapenem resistance, whereas the persistently high fluoroquinolone resistance rates underscore the need for cautious empirical use of this antimicrobial class.

This study has several limitations. First, as a single-center retrospective analysis, the generalizability of our findings may be limited. The observed pathogen distribution, antimicrobial resistance patterns, and temporal trends may be influenced by local patient populations, referral patterns, and institutional clinical practices. Multicenter studies involving different hospital levels and geographic regions are needed to validate the observed epidemiological patterns. Second, although phenotypic antimicrobial susceptibility testing allowed comprehensive surveillance of resistance trends, no molecular characterization, such as PCR or whole-genome sequencing, was performed. Consequently, we were unable to identify the specific resistance determinants underlying ESBL, CRE, CRAB, or other resistant phenotypes, or to distinguish between clonal dissemination and horizontal gene transfer as the primary drivers of resistance dissemination. Third, because hospital-level antimicrobial consumption data were unavailable, we could not directly evaluate the relationship between antimicrobial selective pressure and the temporal resistance trends observed in this study. In addition, detailed patient-level clinical information, including comorbidities, prior antimicrobial exposure, intensive care unit admission, and invasive device use, was not consistently available throughout the study period. Therefore, our stratified analyses primarily describe epidemiological associations rather than independent patient-level risk factors. Despite these limitations, the major strengths of this study include its 12-year continuous surveillance period, large sample size, standardized deduplication strategy, and comprehensive stratified analyses across patient age, sex, specimen type, temporal trends, and antimicrobial resistance patterns. Together, these findings provide valuable real-world evidence regarding pathogen distribution and antimicrobial resistance in a secondary hospital and complement existing national surveillance data by providing detailed epidemiological information from an underrepresented healthcare setting. Our results may facilitate more evidence-based empirical antimicrobial therapy and antimicrobial stewardship, particularly in resource-limited secondary hospitals where long-term local surveillance remains an essential component of clinical decision-making.

In conclusion, our findings provide long-term real-world epidemiological insights into bacterial distribution and antimicrobial resistance patterns in a secondary hospital in eastern China. While these findings should not be generalized to all healthcare settings nationwide, they provide context-specific evidence that may support empirical antimicrobial therapy, antimicrobial stewardship, and local AMR surveillance in county-level secondary hospitals with similar patient populations and healthcare resources. Together with national surveillance programs, long-term surveillance at different levels of the healthcare system can provide a more comprehensive understanding of antimicrobial resistance epidemiology and support evidence-based infection prevention and antimicrobial stewardship strategies.
