Abstract
The identification and modeling of time-varying systems is a fundamental challenge in signal processing and system identification. To address this challenge, we propose a class of time-varying state-space model (SSM) based neural networks in which the neurons’ states are governed by time-varying dynamics. The proposed model provides the learnable time-varying dynamics through a dictionary of basis functions, where each basis function evolves differently over time. We evaluate the proposed approach on both synthetic data from switching systems and a speech denoising task where real audio is corrupted with switching dynamics noise. The results show that the proposed time-varying model consistently outperforms its time-invariant counterparts while maintaining comparable computational complexity. Our investigations also reveal which aspects of the time-varying dynamics of the data most need to be captured by the proposed time-invariant models, how the additional freedom provided by time-varying basis functions should be allocated across model components, and to what extent larger models can compensate for time-invariant limitations.

Introduction

Left: Example network architecture with an input layer with a single channel, two hidden layers with SSM neurons and an output layer of two channels. Middle: An SSM neuron with time-varying dynamics. Right: Illustration of the proposed basis function expansion for a single time-varying element of the SSM state transition matrix.

Many real-world processes are inherently time-varying, meaning that their underlying dynamics evolve over time. This time-varying behavior appears in various domains, such as financial markets, weather forecasting, energy consumption, and neural activity analysis. A central challenge in system identification is therefore to build models that are flexible enough to capture such evolving dynamics
7
;
9
.

State Space Models (SSMs) provide a principled and interpretable framework for system identification. In their classical form with linear state transitions, often referred to as Linear Dynamical Systems (LDS)
8
, they assume fixed time-invariant transition dynamics.

A common extension of LDSs for handling time-varying data is the Switching Linear Dynamical System (SLDS)
18
, where the dynamics transition between multiple LDS modes.
While effective in capturing regime changes, learning SLDSs remains challenging, as it requires estimating the number of modes, the model order of each mode, and the corresponding parameters
14
.

Another extension of LDS, distinct from SLDS, is deep-SSMs. These models stack LDS blocks into layers with nonlinear transformations and have recently achieved performance comparable to transformers on long-sequence modeling tasks
6

16
, as well as have been applied to nonlinear system identification
1
. Extensions to Deep-SSMs include input-dependent mechanism
4
and input timescale invariant formulation
3
.

Traditionally, system identification research has long explored time-varying and non-stationary behavior through methods such as adaptive filtering, basis function expansions
19
;
2
;
13
;
23
, and change point detection
20
.
More recently, deep learning has also become a powerful tool for system identification
10
, including approaches based on time-varying neural networks, such as dynamic neural networks
7
and non-stationary transformer architectures
9
.

Our work brings the time-varying basis function expansion methodology into contemporary deep SSM architectures and illustrates the performance for system identification tasks.
Unlike explicit modeling through SLDSs, our framework requires no explicit specification of switching modes and instead models smooth, continuously time-varying dynamics, enabling efficient training through standard backpropagation through time (BPTT).
In contrast to modeling time variation in deep SSMs through input-dependence, our framework explicitly models time-dependent dynamics independent of the input. Figure
1
provides a visualization of our proposed framework.

The key contributions of this paper are as follows:

•

We propose a novel time-varying deep SSM framework using learnable basis functions to parameterize state transition, input, and output matrices over time.

•

We provide experimental validation on both synthetic and real-world data on audio denoising scenarios with non-stationary noise dynamics.

•

We explore the trade-offs in the proposed model architecture, including effects of different parts of SSM dynamics and basis function allocation, and compare it with its time-invariant counterpart.

Overall, our results show that the proposed time-varying model
consistently outperforms its time-invariant counterparts under switching dynamics data while retaining comparable computational complexity.

Methods

2.1
Preliminaries

2.1.1
Time-invariant SSM model:

A time-invariant linear discrete-time SSM is given by
11

\displaystyle\bm{x}[t]

\displaystyle=\bm{A}\bm{x}[t-1]+\bm{B}\bm{u}[t-1]

(1a)

\displaystyle\bm{y}[t]

\displaystyle=\bm{C}\bm{x}[t],

(1b)

with possibly learnable matrix parameters

\bm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
,
\bm{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n_{in}},\bm{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{out}\times n},\bm{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{out}\times n_{in}}
, input
\bm{u}[t]\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{in}\times 1}
, state variable
\bm{x}[t]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times 1}
, and output
\bm{y}[t]\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{out}\times 1}
.

We refer to the SSM in (
1
) as a neuron in the context of a SSM based neural network.
Multiple neurons with such SSM dynamics may be used to form a SSM layer, see Figure
1
. Layers with SSM
dynamics are combined with possibly non-linear activation function layers and mixing layers to form a deep SSM network
6

16
.

2.1.2
Basis function expansion:

Basis function expansion is a way of representing a function,
f(t)
, as a linear combination of simpler functions,
\phi^{(k)}(\cdot)
, called basis functions, as follows:

\displaystyle f(t)=\sum^{K}_{k=1}\alpha^{(k)}\times\phi^{(k)}(t),

(2)

where
\alpha_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
are the coefficients.
Basis functions may assume various forms, such as with Fourier series, Gaussian functions, polynomial functions, rational orthogonal functions, and radial basis functions.
For example, we can have

\phi^{(k)}=\mathcal{N}(t|\mu_{k},\sigma_{k}^{2})
for a dictionary of Gaussian functions with
\mathcal{N}(\cdot)
representing the shape of a Gaussian function and

\phi^{(k)}=\sin(w_{k}t+\phi_{k})
for a dictionary of sine functions,
where
\mu_{k},\sigma_{k},w_{k},\phi_{k}
are fixed pre-defined scalars.

2.2
Proposed time-varying SSM model

We propose the following time-varying SSM model

\displaystyle\bm{x}[t]

\displaystyle=\bm{A}[t]\bm{x}[t-1]+\bm{B}[t]\bm{u}[t-1]

(3a)

\displaystyle\bm{y}[t]

\displaystyle=\bm{C}[t]\bm{x}[t],

(3b)

where
\bm{A}[t],\bm{B}[t],\bm{C}[t]
have the same dimensions as the time-invariant model in (
1
). However, each element of these matrices is now a linear combination of basis functions. In particular, for
\bm{A}[t]
, we have

\displaystyle[\bm{A}[t]]_{ij}=a_{i,j}[t]=\sum^{K_{A}}_{k=1}a_{i,j}^{(k)}\times\phi_{A,i,j}^{(k)}[t]

(4)

where
[\bm{A}[t]]_{ij}=a_{i,j}[t]
denotes the
i
th row
j
th column element of the matrix
\bm{A}[t]
.
An illustration of the expansion in (
4
) is provided in Figure
1
.
We note that the number of basis functions
K_{A}
is independent of any other dimensions in the model. Here we present the general case where the basis function
\phi_{A,i,j}^{(k)}
may differ across SSM matrices
(A,B,C)
, indices
(i,j)
, and expansion terms
(k)
. In practice, this heterogeneity can be reduced by using a smaller shared dictionary of basis functions.

Similar to
\bm{A}[t]
, we have the following:

\displaystyle[\bm{B}[t]]_{ij}

\displaystyle=b_{i,j}[t]=\sum^{K_{B}}_{k=1}b_{i,j}^{(k)}\times\phi_{B,i,j}^{(k)}[t]

(5)

\displaystyle[\bm{C}[t]]_{ij}

\displaystyle=c_{i,j}[t]=\sum^{K_{C}}_{k=1}c_{i,j}^{(k)}\times\phi_{C,i,j}^{(k)}[t].

(6)

We note that
K_{A}
,
K_{B}
,

K_{C}
can be chosen independently of each other.
Hence, a subset of
\bm{A}[t],\bm{B}[t],\bm{C}[t]
can remain time-invariant as in the time-invariant model (
1
) independently of the others.

2.3
Stability

A central question in deep SSM development is to ensure stability of the SSM dynamics. The stability of the dynamics in (
3
) is controlled by
\bm{A}[t]
.
For simplicity of presentation, we now assume that
\bm{A}[t]
is a diagonal matrix and the basis functions satisfy
|\phi_{A,i,j}^{(k)}[t]|\leq 1
. To ensure stability, the modulus of the diagonal entries (eigenvalues) of
\bm{A}[t]
must be strictly smaller than
1
, i.e.,
|a_{i,i}[t]|<1
. Using the triangle inequality, we have

\displaystyle|a_{i,i}[t]|

\displaystyle=|\sum^{K_{A}}_{k=1}a_{i,i}^{(k)}\times\phi_{A,i,i}^{(k)}[t]|

(7a)

\displaystyle\leq\sum^{K_{A}}_{k=1}|a_{i,i}^{(k)}\times\phi_{A,i,i}^{(k)}[t]|\leq\sum^{K_{A}}_{k=1}|a_{i,i}^{(k)}|

(7b)

Hence, if
\sum^{K_{A}}_{k=1}|a_{i,i}^{(k)}|<1
is guaranteed, then
|a_{i,i}[t]|<1
, which imply stability of the dynamics governed by
\bm{A}[t]
.
During training, we enforce the stability condition,
\sum^{K}_{k=1}|a_{i,i}^{(k)}|<1
, by checking it once per forward pass rather than at each time step. If this condition is violated, i.e.,
\sum^{K}_{k=1}|a_{i,i}^{(k)}|=c>1
, then we apply a scaling strategy to enforce the constraint by redefining the coefficients as
\widehat{a}_{i,i}^{(k)}=\frac{1}{c+\epsilon}a_{i,i}^{(k)}
. With this scaling, we have the following that ensure stability

\displaystyle\sum^{K}_{k=1}|\widehat{a}_{i,i}^{(k)}|=\frac{1}{c+\epsilon}\sum^{K}_{k=1}|a_{i,i}^{(k)}|=\frac{1}{c+\epsilon}c<1.

(8)

2.4
Parameter count

In (
1
), each element of
\bm{A}
,
\bm{B}
,
\bm{C}
is a single scalar learnable parameter, while each element of
\bm{A}[t]
,
\bm{B}[t]
and
\bm{C}[t]
matrices in (
3
) is a linear combination of
K_{A}
,
K_{B}
and
K_{C}
basis functions where each coefficient is trainable, hence it corresponds to
K_{A}
,
K_{B}
and
K_{C}
learnable parameters per element, respectively.
Hence, the proposed model increases the trainable parameters associated with
\bm{A}
,
\bm{B}
and
\bm{C}
by
K_{A}
,
K_{B}
and
K_{C}
per neuron, respectively.

.

2.5
Inference complexity

The learnable coefficients
a_{i,j}^{(k)},b_{i,j}^{(k)},c_{i,j}^{(k)}
are determined during the training based on training data. For the inference phase, i.e. when the model performs a forward pass to evaluate on test data,
the matrices
\bm{A}(t),\bm{B}(t),\bm{C}(t)
can be pre-computed using these coefficients. Hence, during inference, the number of Multiply–Accumulate (MAC) operations is the same for the time-invariant model in (
1
) and the proposed time-varying model (
3
) if the network architecture is otherwise the same.

Hence, even if the number of trainable parameters for the time-varying model is higher than the invariant model for a fixed
n,n_{in},n_{out}
, the inference MAC complexity of the time-varying model is the same. Nevertheless, under this strategy of pre-computation of
\bm{A}(t),\bm{B}(t),\bm{C}(t)
, the space complexity of the time-invariant model is higher, scaling with sequence length
T
, due to storage of time-dependent matrix parameters.

3
Experimental set-up

3.0.1
Model initialization and training

For all experiments in Section
4
, we use a network with an input layer, one or two hidden layers, and an output layer. The input and output layers correspond to the data channels and the task-specific output channels, respectively. Each hidden layer contains
h
SSM neurons with either identity or The Gaussian Error Linear Unit (GELU) activation.
The trainable parameters in the networks are the following:

\bm{A}_{\text{coeff}},\bm{B}_{\text{coeff}},\bm{C}_{\text{coeff}}
for each SSM neuron,
the weight connections between layers
\bm{W}
and the normalization layers’ mean and variance scale.
We initialize
\bm{B}_{\text{coeff}}
as ones,
\bm{C}_{\text{coeff}}\sim U[0,1)
,
\bm{C}_{bias}
as zeros, and
\bm{A}_{\text{coeff}}
using the real part of S4D-Lin initialization
5
, expanded across the
K_{A}
dimension by setting
a_{i,j}^{k}=a_{i,j}/K_{A}
. We initialize
\bm{W}
uniformly, use batch normalization between layers, and train with BPTT and AdamW. All models are trained on an NVIDIA Tesla T4 GPU (16GB RAM). Further details are in Appendix
B
.

3.0.2
Choice of basis functions

For the basis function expansion for the elements of
\bm{A}[t],\bm{B}[t],\bm{C}[t]
, we use dictionary of Gaussian functions and an additional constant function. Specifically, if
K
is the number of basis functions and
T
is the sequence length, then per element we have
1
constant function and
K-1
Gaussian-shaped functions with amplitude
1
, mean
\in U(0,T)
, and standard deviation
\in U(\frac{T}{5(K-1)+1},\frac{T}{(K-1)/3+1})
.

3.0.3
Performance metrics for evaluation

The Mean-Square-Error (MSE)
\in[0,\infty)
is calculated between the vectors
\bm{s}
and
\widehat{\bm{s}}
per time step, where MSE
=0
implies a perfect match i.e.
\bm{s}=\widehat{\bm{s}}
.
Signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and Scale-invariant signal-to-noise ratio (SI-SNR), are used as defined in
15
. Higher dB indicates cleaner speech. While task-dependent, SI-SNR values
<0
dB imply the estimate is worse than the noise,
0-5
dB imply low interpretability of speech,
5-15
dB imply decent enhancement and
15-20
dB imply very good enhancement
17
;
21
. All performance values reported use the testing subset of the datasets and are averages over multiple model initializations.

3.0.4
Trainable parameter count fairness

In a set of experiments in Section
4.2
, we match the number of trainable model parameters between time-varying and time-invariant models. Following Section
2.4
, we keep the layer architecture the same and for each SSM neuron, we set
p_{invary}=p_{vary}
, i.e.,
n_{invar}=\frac{n_{vary}}{3}(K_{A}+K_{B}+K_{C})
.

Dataset 1: Four mode system

We generate a dataset from an SLDS with

Inputs to the SLDS are linear combination of two sinusoidal sequences with time steps

. The input then passes through the four mode SLDS and creates the output

.
We consider variations where each of (
\bm{A}
,
\bm{B}
,
\bm{C}
) independently switches or remains fixed.
The dataset consists of
2000
input-output pairs (

20\%
test), and the task is to reproduce the SLDS output given the input.
Further details are provided in Appendix

.

Conclusions

We have proposed time-varying SSM networks parameterized by a dictionary of basis functions with learnable coefficients.
Our investigations illustrate that the proposed models can learn and adapt to periodically switched dynamics, whereas time-invariant SSMs cannot capture such switching behavior.
Extensions to non-stationary settings with non-periodic behavior, as well as exploring different families of basis functions, are important directions for future work.
Benchmarking against both simpler regression methods and more sophisticated architectures such as non-stationary transformers would further clarify the trade-offs of the proposed approach.
