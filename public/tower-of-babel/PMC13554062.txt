Evaluation of practical equivalent seismic input methods for fault-crossing bridges considering dynamic and permanent ground displacement
Authors: Xiaojun Li, Rui Sun, Ning Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDuring major earthquakes, fault ruptures frequently lead to significant differences in ground motions across the fault trace. As a result, fault-crossing bridges are subjected to complex seismic demands characterized by the combined effects of transient ground shaking and permanent fault displacement. Accurately representing such coupled effects remains a major challenge due to the limited availability of spatially consistent ground motion records and the complexity of the underlying mechanisms. To address this issue, this study evaluates two practical equivalent seismic input frameworks that explicitly incorporate permanent fault displacement while preserving the essential characteristics of ground motions. The seismic excitation is decomposed into two components: (i) dynamic inertial component induced by ground motion without permanent displacement, and (ii) fault-induced relative displacement component between the two sides of the fault, introduced either as a quasi-static input or as a time-dependent dynamic input. Based on this decomposition, the total structural response is obtained through response-level superposition. Four seismic input modes are systematically investigated, including non-uniform excitation (benchmark), uniform excitation, quasi-static equivalent input, and dynamic equivalent input. A three-dimensional finite element model of a simply supported bridge crossing a strike-slip fault is developed in OpenSees to assess the equivalent input frameworks. Results indicate that uniform seismic input significantly underestimates both peak and residual bridge responses, particularly for structural members located near the fault. In contrast, the two equivalent input approaches effectively reproduce the response characteristics of non-uniform excitation, with deviations within acceptable engineering limits. Thereby, the equivalent input frameworks provide a practical yet reliable seismic input representation for both numerical simulations and experimental studies of fault-crossing bridges.Keywords: Fault-crossing bridges, Equivalent seismic input, Spatially varying ground motion, Permanent ground displacement, Seismic response analysisSubject terms: Engineering, Natural hazards, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

With the rapid development of transportation infrastructure, bridges play a critical role in lifeline systems by maintaining regional connectivity and post-earthquake functionality1–3. In principle, bridge alignments should avoid active fault zones. However, in seismically active regions with complex geological conditions, such as western China, California US, and Japan, increasing transportation demands make it inevitable for bridges to be constructed near or across seismogenic faults. Consequently, an increasing number of bridges are exposed to the combined effects of strong ground shaking and fault rupture during major earthquakes.

Post-earthquake reconnaissance from major events has demonstrated that bridges crossing active faults suffer significantly more severe damage than those located away from fault zones. Severe damage, including bearing failure, excessive girder displacement, and structural collapse, was reported during the 1995 Kobe earthquake4, the 1999 Chi–Chi earthquake5–7, the 2008 Wenchuan earthquake8–10, and the 2016 Kumamoto earthquake11–13. These observations highlight the critical role of fault rupture effects in addition to transient dynamic excitation, underscoring the necessity of explicitly incorporating permanent ground displacement into seismic input models14–16.

Ground motions in near-fault regions exhibit distinctive characteristics, including velocity pulses, rupture directivity effects, and permanent displacement17,18. As a result, fault-crossing bridges often experience residual deformations and asymmetric structural response, posing challenges for seismic analysis and design. Gu, et al.19 investigated the seismic responses of a three-span cable-stayed bridge subjected to strike-slip faulting using nonlinear time-history analyses with recorded near-fault ground motions. Comparisons were made between fault-crossing and near-fault bridge configurations, considering the effects of permanent ground displacement and fault crossing angle. The results indicated that fault-crossing bridges exhibit higher seismic demands, especially in girder and bearing displacements. Hui, et al.20 explored the seismic response of multi-span continuous girder bridges under near-fault motions with permanent displacement. It is revealed that permanent displacement attenuation induces substantial residual deformations in girders and increases bending demands at pier bases. These findings indicate that ground motions on opposite sides of a fault differing in amplitude, frequency content, and displacement history, may lead to severe damage to infrastructure systems such as pipelines, tunnels, and bridges21–24.

Despite the recognized importance of fault rupture effects, accurately representing the combined effects of spatial variability and permanent fault displacement remains challenging due to limitations in ground motion availability and modeling complexity. Conventional non-uniform excitation analysis is generally regarded as the most rigorous approach for fault-crossing bridge assessment. However, its practical application is often constrained by two major challenges: (1) the limited availability of spatially consistent ground motion records on both sides of a fault, and (2) the difficulty of reproducing such excitation conditions in shaking table tests. To address this issue, various simplified seismic input methods have been proposed to investigate the seismic behavior of fault-crossing structures. Early work by Gloyd, et al.25 introduced fault-rupture‐related load group (VIIFR) into the Caltrans design provisions. Their framework incorporated both probabilistic and deterministic fault displacement demands into bridge design. Although this framework represented a major step toward integrating fault rupture mechanics into bridge design practice, fault displacement and seismic ground motion were treated as separate or partially combined actions, without explicitly capturing time-dependent coupling during earthquake rupture. Goel and Chopra26,27 further developed a series of approximate analysis procedures for ordinary bridges crossing faults, including response spectrum analysis, linear static analysis, modal pushover analysis, and linear dynamic analysis. The California Department of Transportation (Caltrans) proposed a linear pseudo-static simplified method for ordinary bridges crossing strike-slip faults as a supplement to the AASHTO standards and Caltrans seismic design criteria in 201228. More recent studies have attempted to incorporate dynamic effects using non-uniform ground motion input or equivalent excitation methods. For instance, Li, et al.29 investigated the seismic response of fault-crossing bridges using synthesized non-uniform ground motions and demonstrated that uniform excitation without permanent displacement leads to an underestimation of bridge response. In their study, the permanent displacement was applied by linearly increasing the displacement over a specified time interval until the target residual value was reached. Their results showed that superimposing uniform seismic excitation with this static permanent displacement generates responses close to those obtained under non-uniform excitation. Beyond numerical simulations, Saiidi, et al.30 conducted a large-scale shake table test on a reinforced concrete bridge model to investigate fault rupture effects. The experiment included multi-support excitation, displacement-controlled input with permanent offset, and an incremental loading protocol designed to preserve residual displacement. The results demonstrated that fault rupture fundamentally altered damage patterns compared with uniform excitation and confirmed that dynamic analysis is necessary to capture such effects accurately.

Although these approaches provide useful insight into deformation mechanisms induced by fault offset, permanent displacement is often introduced as a separate static or staged load, neglecting its time-dependent interaction with dynamic ground shaking. The influence of the loading history of fault displacement on structural response has not been systematically investigated. Therefore, there remains a need to evaluate equivalent seismic input methods that can approximate the response induced by non-uniform excitation while remaining feasible for practical engineering analysis and experimental implementation.

As such, a three-dimensional finite element model of a fault-crossing bridge is developed to evaluate the equivalent seismic input frameworks that decompose seismic excitation into a dynamic component and a fault-induced relative displacement component. The main objectives of this study are: (1) to clarify the relative contributions of dynamic ground motion and fault-induced displacement to bridge response; (2) to evaluate the validity of response superposition in equivalent seismic input methods; and (3) to quantify the influence of displacement loading history on structural response. The validated equivalent seismic input modes thereby provide a practical and physically interpretable seismic input scheme for engineering analysis and shake-table-compatible input representation of fault-crossing bridges.

Seismic hazard and structural layout

In this study, the seismic response of a fault-crossing bridge is investigated based on the Haiwen Bridge (also known as the Puqian Bridge), a major sea-crossing bridge connecting Wenchang City and Haikou City in Hainan Province, China. With a total length of approximately 3.96 km, the bridge is the largest cross-sea bridge in the province and plays a critical role in the regional transportation network (Fig. 1).

Location of the active fault trace and the studied fault-crossing bridge segment.

The bridge site is located within the epicentral region of the 1605 Qiongshan earthquake (Mw 7.5), one of the most destructive historical earthquakes in southern China. The fault ruptures associated with this earthquake include the northeast-trending Maniao-Puqian Fault and the northwest-trending Puqian-Qinglan Fault. The bridge is situated near the intersection of these two faults, both of which have been identified as Quaternary-active based on the Seismic Hazard Assessment and Active Fault Investigation of Haikou City31.

Due to constraints related to urban planning and local geomorphology, complete avoidance of the active fault for this bridge was infeasible. According to investigations conducted by the Hainan Earthquake Agency, the Puqian-Qinglan fault intersects the bridge alignment at an approximately perpendicular angle. To mitigate seismic risk, the main bridge is located on one side of the fault with an appropriate setback distance, while a shorter-span, easily replaceable simply supported segment is adopted to cross the fault zone. This configuration provides a representative engineering case to investigate the seismic behavior of fault-crossing bridges and evaluate different seismic input strategies.

Computational model and analysis methodology

The numerical model is developed using OpenSees based on the Haiwen Bridge. A six-span segment of the bridge crossing a strike-slip fault is selected for detailed analysis. The span arrangement is 2 × 58 m + 60 m + 3 × 58 m, with the fault intersecting the bridge perpendicularly beneath the third span (Fig. 2). Piers P2-P3 are located on the active fault side, while P4-P6 are on the passive side.

Finite element model of the fault-crossing bridge segment.

The bridge superstructure consists of simply supported steel box girders with a deck width of 32 m and a girder height of 2.8 m. All piers are designed as double-column reinforced concrete hollow piers with a uniform wall thickness of 0.55 m. The pier heights vary from 27.2 m to 33.1 m.

To ensure computational efficiency and focus on the influence of seismic input strategies, both the steel girders and reinforced concrete piers are modeled using linear elastic beam-column elements. This assumption is justified by preliminary investigations indicating that structural nonlinearity is primarily concentrated in the bearings, while the piers remain essentially elastic under moderate earthquake conditions. To represent the weak interaction between adjacent simply supported spans, neighboring girders are connected by elasticBeamColumn elements assigned extremely small elastic stiffness. These elements are introduced solely to maintain numerical stability and ensure model connectivity during the finite-element analysis. Owing to their negligible stiffness, virtually no force is transferred between adjacent spans, and the intended simply supported behavior of the bridge system is preserved.

The interaction between the superstructure and substructure is modeled using zero-length elements representing isolation bearings. These elements allow relative horizontal displacement while releasing bending moments at the pier-girder interface. The bearing behavior is modeled using the Giuffré-Menegotto-Pinto constitutive model (Steel02), with key parameters including an initial stiffness of 21.6 kN/mm, a post-yield stiffness of 3.4 kN/mm, and a yield strength of 432.0 kN (Fig. 3).

Elevation view of prototype bridge column and bearing model with force-displacement relationship.

The pier-foundation interaction is simplified as linear elastic springs using zero-length elements, with fixed boundary conditions applied at the far-field ends. Structural damping is represented using Rayleigh damping, with a 5% critical damping ratio assigned to the first two vibration modes. All key modeling parameters are summarized in Table 1.

Equivalent seismic input method for fault-crossing bridges

Seismic excitation acting on fault-crossing bridges is inherently complex due to the simultaneous presence of transient ground shaking and fault-induced permanent displacement. Conventional uniform seismic input neglects both spatial variability and permanent ground deformation, while non-uniform excitation, although physically rigorous, is often impractical due to the scarcity of spatially consistent ground motion records and the difficulty of implementing synchronized multi-support excitation in experiments. To address this challenge, two equivalent seismic input frameworks are evaluated in this study. Rather than replacing non-uniform excitation analysis, these approaches offer a practical and physically interpretable approximation that explicitly incorporates fault-induced permanent displacement, captures the dominant response mechanisms, and substantially simplifies seismic input implementation.

Conceptual decomposition of seismic response

Based on the observations, the total structural response of fault-crossing bridges can be approximately decomposed into two dominant components: (1) the dynamic response ud(t), induced by transient ground motion without permanent displacement, and (2) the deformation (t), resulting from the fault-induced relative displacement. In scenarios where structural nonlinearity remains moderate, these two components exhibit weak coupling, allowing for a reasonable approximation via response-level superposition:

As demonstrated in the subsequent numerical results, the approximation remains sufficiently accurate for the bridge system and seismic scenarios considered herein.

Definition of seismic input modes

To evaluate the applicability of two equivalent seismic input methods, four seismic input modes are defined:

Mode 1: Non-uniform seismic input (Benchmark), where ground motions with permanent displacement are applied to supports on the active fault side, while motions without permanent displacement are applied on the passive side.

Mode 2: Uniform seismic input, where identical ground motions without permanent displacement are applied to all supports, neglecting both spatial variability and fault displacement.

Mode 3: Quasi-static equivalent seismic input, obtained by superimposing the dynamic response under Mode 2 with the quasi-static relative displacement between motions with and without permanent displacement.

Mode 4: Dynamic equivalent seismic input, in which the relative displacement is applied in a dynamic manner (time-dependent displacement input), representing the temporal evolution of fault slip, and combined with Mode 2.

In this study, the fault is assumed to be a strike-slip fault, with the fault-induced offset applied in the transverse direction of the bridge. For Mode 3 and 4, prescribed displacements (quasi-static or dynamic) are imposed on the supports located within the active fault block in the transverse direction, while the supports on the passive side remain fixed. This treatment ensures that the internal structural deformation reflects the permanent tectonic ground displacement across the rupture zone.

Mode 1 is taken as the benchmark solution representing the most realistic seismic input condition with non-uniform seismic excitation considering permanent fault displacement. Mode 2 employs uniform excitation only to quantify the error introduced by neglecting permanent displacement. Modes 3–4 are used to assess influence of time-dependent evolution of fault-induced relative displacement on structural response, as well as the validity of simplified equivalent seismic input methods. The applicability of Eq. (1) is assessed by comparing Mode 3 and Mode 4 with the benchmark solution (Mode 1).

Ground motion selection and synthesis methodology

To ensure consistency with the equivalent seismic input framework, ground motions used in this study explicitly incorporate both transient shaking and permanent ground displacement. Recent advances in near-fault ground motion simulation and baseline correction techniques enable the generation of such records.

The ground motion record (NGA Record No. 879) of the 1992 Mw 7.3 Landers earthquake is adopted as the seed motion. This record is characterized by a Joyner-Boore distance of approximately 2.2 km and a pronounced velocity pulse exceeding 100 cm/s. The synthesized ground motions are generated by modifying the seed motion in the time domain to match prescribed seismic hazard parameters, including the target response spectrum and peak ground acceleration (PGA). The synthesis procedure follows the methodology proposed by Chen, et al.34.

Based on the site-specific seismic hazard assessment corresponding to a 2% probability of exceedance in 50 years, the target PGA is specified as 0.60 g. To investigate structural sensitivity to different hazard characteristics, two representative cross-fault ground-motion scenarios with distinct target response spectra and permanent displacement demands are generated. Specifically, the fault-parallel permanent displacement on the active plate (left side) is taken as 0.81 m for Ground Motion 1 and 1.465 m for Ground Motion 2.

Two horizontal bedrock motion components, namely the longitudinal and transverse directions, are considered in the analysis, while the vertical component is neglected due to its generally limited influence on the global response of simply supported bridges. Structural response under different input modes

The seismic responses of the bridge are evaluated under the four input modes, including non-uniform excitation (Mode 1), uniform excitation (Mode 2), quasi-static equivalent input (Mode 3), and dynamic equivalent input (Mode 4). Response quantities including relative displacements between pier top and bottom, bearing deformations, and internal forces at the pier base (shear force, bending moment, and torsional moment). Particular attention is given to piers adjacent to the fault (P3 and P4), where the effects of spatial variability and permanent displacement are most pronounced.

Pier displacement response

The transverse relative displacements between the top and base of piers P3 and P4 are shown in Fig. 7. Before reaching the peak response, only minor differences are observed between Mode 1 and Mode 2, indicating that transient peak displacement is primarily governed by dynamic ground shaking. However, as permanent ground displacement develops, the responses begin to diverge significantly, particularly at piers adjacent to the fault (P3 and P4). Mode 1 produces substantially larger residual displacements than Mode 2, demonstrating that fault-induced permanent displacement and spatial variability of ground motion dominate the residual response.

Bearing deformation

The maximum and residual transverse bearing deformations are summarized in Fig. 9. The bearing deformation refers to the relative displacement between the bearing top node (girder side) and bottom node (pier side) in the transverse direction, which coincides with the strike-slip fault movement direction. It is calculated as the difference between the transverse displacements of the two nodes. The maximum bearing deformation is defined as the maximum absolute relative displacement during the entire time-history analysis, while the residual bearing deformation is taken as the relative displacement at the end of the analysis. For piers closest to the fault on either side (P3 and P4), Mode 2 results in relatively smaller bearing deformations due to synchronous movement of all supports. This highlights the localized nature of fault effects, which diminish rapidly with distance from the fault.

Maximum and residual bearing deformation in the transverse direction for (a) Ground Motion 1 and (b) Ground Motion 2.

Mode 3 shows excellent agreement with Mode 1, with maximum deviations within 3.3%, accurately capturing the critical deformation demand at near-fault bearings (P3Right and P4Left). This confirms that bearing residual deformation is primarily driven by quasi-static fault slip rather than dynamic interaction.

When the fault displacement is applied dynamically (Mode 4), the responses remain almost unchanged compared to Mode 3, where peak and residual differences are consistently small. These results further demonstrate that loading rate has a negligible influence on bearing deformation, supporting the use of quasi-static equivalent input.

A consistent trend is observed across all internal force components. Compared with Mode 2, Mode 1 results in significantly larger peak and residual responses, particularly at piers adjacent to the fault (P3 and P4). This indicates that fault-induced permanent ground displacement plays a critical role in both transient and residual internal force demands, which cannot be captured by uniform seismic input.

The equivalent input Mode 3 reproduces the internal force responses of Mode 1 with good overall accuracy. For shear force and bending moment, both peak and residual values are closely matched, demonstrating that the superposition of uniform ground motion and static fault displacement is sufficient to capture the dominant response mechanism.

For torsional moment, Mode 3 still provides a reasonable approximation, although deviations of up to -17.3% at P4 for Ground motion 2 are observed. This suggests that torsional response is more sensitive to spatial variability and interaction effects, especially in near-fault regions where structural asymmetry and multi-support excitation become significant.

The comparison between Mode 3 and Mode 4 further clarifies the role of loading rate. The two equivalent input modes produce nearly identical results, with Mode 4 yielding slightly higher values. This indicates that the structural response is primarily governed by the amplitude of the imposed displacement, while the temporal evolution of loading plays a secondary role. Overall, the influence of loading rate appears limited for the response quantities investigated in this study.

Conclusions

This study proposes and evaluates two equivalent seismic input frameworks as an efficient alternative to the explicit modeling of non-uniform ground motions with permanent displacement in fault-crossing bridge analysis. By decomposing seismic excitation into dynamic inertial and fault-induced deformation components and recombining them at the response level, these methods preserve the essential physical mechanisms of fault-crossing bridge behavior. Based on the numerical investigations, the following conclusions are drawn:

Permanent fault displacement has a significant impact on the seismic response of fault-crossing bridges. It not only amplifies response amplitudes but also induces pronounced spatial asymmetry and residual deformations in piers and bearings. Neglecting such effects leads to a systematic underestimation of post-seismic demand.

The proposed equivalent seismic input frameworks provide a simplified yet effective alternative for fault-crossing bridge analysis. Compared with benchmark non-uniform excitation analysis (Mode 1), they require only uniform ground motion combined with prescribed fault displacement, facilitating numerical simulation and experimental implementation. The quasi-static formulation (Mode 3) is generally sufficient for moderate seismic intensity and limited nonlinearity. The dynamic formulation (Mode 4) may be adopted for cases involving stronger nonlinear behavior or situations where the temporal evolution of fault slip is expected to play a more significant role. However, the applicability of response superposition may decrease as structural nonlinearity becomes more pronounced, such as in cases of pier yielding, nonlinear bearing behavior, or large displacement effects induced by fault rupture.

The present study is based on a simply supported bridge model and a limited number of ground-motion scenarios. Therefore, the results should be interpreted as mechanism-oriented rather than fully statistical conclusions. Future research should consider a broader range of bridge configurations, fault types, and seismic records, as well as systematic investigation of intermediate fault-slip loading rates, to further assess the robustness of the proposed frameworks.
