Impact of delayed blood donation sample processing and testing on SARS‐CoV‐2 serological assay results in an evolving pandemic response
Authors: Marion C Lanteri, Valerie Green, Hasan Sulaeman, Mars Stone, James Haynes, Clara Di Germanio, Sakina Smith, Patricia G Villaflor, Jamel Groves, Phillip C Williamson, Susan L Stramer, Melissa M Coughlin, Jefferson M Jones, Eduard Grebe, Michael P Busch, Paula Saa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe two largest US blood collection organizations led a multiphased, multicenter nationwide SARS‐CoV‐2 blood donor‐based serosurveillance program. The potential impact of delayed sample processing and prolonged refrigerated or frozen storage on anti‐SARS‐CoV‐2 antibody detection was investigated.MethodsAnti‐spike (S) IgG and anti‐nucleocapsid (N) total immunoglobulin levels were compared for 20 paired plasma/serum samples tested fresh and frozen after separation from cellular components on Day 5 or after ≤40 days of 4°C storage; 10 paired plasma/serum samples separated on Day 5 and tested immediately or after ≤67 days of 4°C storage; and 85 plasma and 950 serum samples tested fresh on Day 1 and after ≤209 days of −20°C storage post‐separation. Proportional Bland–Altman analyses were used to estimate average bias and 95% limits of agreement (LOA).ResultsAntibody reactivity was stable over time regardless of serum or plasma processing delays or prolonged refrigerated or frozen storage. Bland–Altman analyses suggest only minor deviations for anti‐S antibodies for samples separated within 5 days and tested frozen compared to fresh (bias: 4.23%, 95% LOA: −10.02; 18.47), after ≤40 days of storage at 4°C pre‐separation (9.51%, [−73.20; 92.22]) and after ≤67 days at 4°C post‐separation (−14.04%, [−28.12; 0.04]). Similar observations were obtained for anti‐N antibodies including samples retested after ≤209 days of storage at −20°C (−20.3%, [−81; 40.5]).ConclusionDespite challenges encountered in sample access and processing during a rapidly evolving pandemic, sample processing variability and testing delays minimally impacted serologic results and thus provide evidence for the validity of results in studies requiring variable sample handling conditions.

BACKGROUND

Nationwide seroprevalence studies in blood donors have been used in many countries to monitor population immunity to infectious diseases including COVID‐19. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 In 2020, as SARS‐CoV‐2 emerged, Vitalant, the American Red Cross (ARC) and other US blood collection organizations implemented anti‐SARS‐CoV‐2 antibody donor testing. Vitalant Research Institute (VRI) and the ARC Scientific Support Office (SSO) with the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), Westat, and Creative Testing Solutions (CTS), launched a collaborative program to estimate prevalence of cumulative SARS‐CoV‐2 infection and vaccination rates in the US blood donor population based on test results and donor reported survey data. Blood donation‐derived samples were captured, aliquoted, characterized, and banked through the successive waves of SARS‐CoV‐2 variants through December 2023, responding to an evolving pandemic in a phased approach. 7 Changes were necessary due to evolving priorities, such as differentiating vaccination from natural infection at first and then identifying reinfections. The program started as a nationwide cross‐sectional blood donor serosurveillance (NBDS) program based on anti‐spike (S) and anti‐nucleocapsid (N) antibody testing from July 2020 through December 2021. 8 , 9 Sample capture and testing evolved during that period as detailed in Fink et al. 10 In quarter (Q)3 2021, the program moved to a longitudinal nationwide repeat blood donor cohort program to enable detection of reinfections and infections following vaccination with a single donation per donor per quarter selected for retrospective anti‐S and anti‐N antibody batch testing of frozen samples through Q2 2023, before real‐time testing of fresh samples was initiated starting in July 2023 to enable more timely analyses through December 2023. Trying to respond to these changes, the respective research operations under this large multicenter program experienced unique challenges impacting sample processing and storage variability. As a result, some samples were tested outside the assay manufacturer's recommendations after prolonged periods of storage pre‐ or post‐separation.

This study was conducted to address the concerns that the variability in sample processing through the different phases of the larger serosurveillance program may raise for SARS‐CoV‐2 antibody results and study findings. The ascertainment of vaccination and infection events through anti‐S and anti‐N antibody development and vaccination, infection, and reinfection events through anti‐S and anti‐N antibody boosting requires reliable serological testing results. Reassuring reports demonstrating the stability of SARS‐CoV‐2 antibodies post‐collection either in convalescent plasma, serum, or heparinized‐plasma samples over shorter periods of storage at −70, −20, 4°C, room temperature, or after multiple freeze/thaw cycles were previously published. 11 , 12 However, the impact of delayed sample processing over extended periods of storage including variable short‐term refrigerated pre‐ or post‐separation or frozen storage periods on SARS‐CoV‐2 serological assay results was unclear. Three experiments were conducted to mimic the sample handling variations experienced over the different periods of the evolving serosurveillance program; the first experiment was designed to address the impact of delayed plasma and serum processing and freezing before testing, the second was designed to evaluate the impact of delayed testing after extended refrigerated storage post‐separation of plasma and serum samples, and the last one, the impact of delayed processing and extended frozen storage on serological testing results.

STUDY DESIGN AND METHODS

Samples processed and acquired through the course of the serosurveillance program

All samples were collected from blood donors as part of standard voluntary blood donations. Whole blood tubes associated with each donation were shipped overnight to one of CTS' blood donor screening laboratories for routine screening testing. Tubes were centrifuged upon receipt based on assay manufacturers' requirements for plasma or serum separation before loading on respective blood screening platforms. Study samples were captured after routine blood donation screening was completed. 10 Samples captured over the period Q3 2021 through 2022 were transferred to the CTS Research and Development (CTS R&D) Department (Tempe, AZ) or ARC SSO (Gaithersburg, MD) for processing, which included centrifugation at 1000‐3000 g for 10 min and separation of residual plasma or serum for testing and biorepository storage. Processing of residual donation samples occurred at variable times after capture. Residual donation samples were received refrigerated at CTS R&D no earlier than on day 4 post‐collection and kept refrigerated until they were aliquoted and frozen at CTS on average 9 (median = 9; range [4–25]) days post‐collection in 2022, whereas samples were received refrigerated at ARC SSO on day 6–18 post‐collection and kept refrigerated until they were aliquoted using an automated TECAN liquid handler after variable times from sample receipt before they were frozen on average 8 (median = 8; Range [6–18]) days post‐collection. In Q1 2023, processing changed and was performed solely at CTS with samples processed and frozen on average 17 (median = 14; range [5–40]) days post‐collection before they were thawed for batch serology testing. In Q3 2023, serology testing was performed on fresh unfrozen samples after serum and plasma separation which occurred at variable times post‐collection and freezing occurred on average 19 (median = 17; range [4–51]) days post‐collection.

Serology testing

Testing was performed using the VITROS Anti‐SARS‐CoV‐2 IgG Quantitative test (Ortho‐Clinical Diagnostics, Inc., Rochester, NY) on neat or diluted samples (1:20 and 1:400) and the VITROS Anti‐SARS‐CoV‐2 Total N Antibody test (Ortho‐Clinical Diagnostics, Inc., Rochester, NY) on undiluted samples.

Anti‐S IgG antibody results were reported in binding antibody units (BAU)/mL accounting for any dilution factor. Dilutional testing was used to expand the assay dynamic range from 2 to 200 BAU/mL with neat testing to upper limits of 4,000 BAU/mL after a 1:20 dilution and 80,000 BAU/mL after a 1:400 dilution, as previously described. 13

Anti‐N antibody testing was performed using undiluted samples (80 μL serum or K2‐EDTA plasma). Results were reported as signal‐to‐cutoff (S/CO) ratios ranging from 0 to 300 with an S/CO ≥1 considered reactive. Successive dilutional reflex testing was performed using a 1:20 dilution for samples with reported S/CO values ≥100, and a 1:400 dilution if S/CO was still ≥100 following testing of the 1:20 dilution, thus expanding the upper range of the assay to an S/CO ratio of 2,000–6,000 after a 1:20 dilution and to an S/CO ratio of 40,000–120,000 after a 1:400 dilution.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.3.1). 14 Different analyses were performed to address various comparisons. Antibody reactivity measured in fresh (neither frozen nor delayed) versus frozen plasma or serum samples (immediately frozen upon plasma or serum separation or after delayed separation), and antibody reactivity following different storage times (before or after separation), was compared using Bland–Altman analyses. Proportional Bland–Altman plots and statistics were computed with bias point estimates and 95% limits of agreement (LOA). The p‐values were from paired‐sample t‐tests for two treatments. Paired t‐tests measured the consistency of bias in the Bland–Altman results. p‐values of < 0.05 were considered significant. To evaluate public health impact, we applied the worst case declines reactivity observed in our experiment to anti‐N and anti‐S results obtained in Q4 2023 of the program and compared the prevalence of detectable antibodies found with testing of fresh samples versus what would have been obtained with the worst‐case sample treatment (e.g. retested after a 209 day frozen storage period post‐separation for anti‐N and after a 67 day refrigerated storage period after separation for anti‐S).

DISCUSSION

Large multicenter studies require careful attention to consistent and appropriate sample handling procedures to ensure reliable results. In the context of an emergency public health response, operational consistency can be especially challenging. SARS‐CoV‐2 serosurveillance programs necessitated responsiveness to changing public health needs in an evolving pandemic, including unavoidable modifications to sample handling and testing. Real‐life operational challenges related to staffing, shipping, instrument downtime, and reagent availability can impact sample processing and testing capacity. A strength of the program was flexibility to meet study aims, while a challenge was the inability to pilot the impact of evolving sample handling on antibody test results.

This study evaluated the impact of delayed processing and testing of plasma and serum samples from nationwide cross‐sectional and longitudinal cohorts of repeat blood donors on the results of Ortho anti‐S IgG and anti‐N total Ig assays. Serological assay performance was not meaningfully impacted by either delayed plasma and serum processing after extended refrigerated storage pre‐ or post‐separation and freezing before testing, or delayed plasma and serum sample testing after extended refrigerated storage post‐separation over two controlled experiments. Serological assay performance was also not impacted by a freeze–thaw cycle on samples that were processed within 5 days or 6–18 days post‐collection. Most samples retained in frozen storage for up to 209 days post‐collection prior to retesting resulted in similar assay performance overall with some reduction in signal most notable in those with lower antibody reactivity.

Based on the package inserts of the emergency use authorization (EUA) assays, it was expected that refrigerated storage for up to 7 days and up to five freeze/thaw cycles for the Ortho quantitative anti‐S IgG assay and one freeze/thaw cycle for the Ortho anti‐N total Ig assay would not impact assay performance. 15 , 16 Our findings demonstrate that for serosurveillance application, extended refrigerated storage of whole blood tubes for up to 40 days before plasma or serum separation or separated plasma and serum for up to 67 days post‐collection did not impact results sufficiently to meaningfully alter public health conclusions. Even where the biases were statistically significant, they were not large enough to frequently change categorical interpretation. Moreover, near negative value samples saw the largest differences (Figure 6B), which is expected and constitutes a small fraction of samples in a large serosurveillance program of adults. Serological assay results were also not meaningfully impacted by a freeze–thaw cycle on samples that were processed within 5 days or 6–18 days post‐collection and then retained in frozen storage for up to 209 days post‐collection prior to retesting at different sites. Throughout, the Bland–Altman analyses reported only small biases of less than 20% with tight LOAs, where most variability was observed for samples with low‐level antibodies. These biases should be interpreted in the light of assay reproducibility in the package insert reporting coefficients of variation of 8 to 15%. 15 , 16 Although a freeze thaw cycle on samples processed within 5 days seemed to have more significantly impacted anti‐N antibody S/CO ratios, an 8% bias would not constitute a meaningful change as part of the overall serosurveillance program. Noteworthy, in this study, very few samples changed anti‐N antibody reactivity interpretation between different treatments, with only 2.7% of low reactivity samples falling below the threshold for positive reactivity. Reassurance that serological assay results did not vary when samples were handled under different processing and storage conditions was a key finding of this study.

Furthermore, the results of this study demonstrate that variations in sample handling due to logistical constraints did not significantly impact the findings of the serosurveillance program. Indeed, projecting the worst‐case scenarios identified in this study such as the impact of a 67 day delay in anti‐S antibody testing after plasma/serum sample separation or the impact of testing samples thawed after a < 209 day frozen storage period on anti‐N antibodies, we identified that among the 29,531 samples collected during Q4 2023 and tested for both anti‐S and anti‐N antibody levels, if a 14.04% decline had taken place among the 96.3% samples found positive for anti‐S antibodies, the prevalence estimate would have been 96.1% (a 0.2% relative difference in the point estimate). Similarly, among the 82.2% tested positive for anti‐N antibodies, if reactivity declined 20.3%, the prevalence estimate would have been 81.7% and that represents a 0.6% relative change in the point estimate. The reliability of the anti‐SARS‐CoV‐2 antibody data obtained using the Ortho VITROS assays is not surprising. Other studies have demonstrated the stability of SARS‐CoV‐2 antibodies after blood collection. Stadlbauer et al. reported on the stability of anti‐SARS‐CoV‐2 antibodies in convalescent plasma when stored at 4°C for at least 6 weeks. 17 Focosi et al. further reported a 30% decline in anti‐SARS‐CoV‐2 Spike protein S1 subunit IgG and IgA titers in serum samples over a longer 95 day period of storage at 4°C. 18 Kanji et al. demonstrated the stability of SARS‐CoV‐2 IgG in serum, plasma, and heparinized‐plasma samples collected from COVID‐19 recovered individuals and tested upon collection or after storage at −70, −20, 4°C, and room‐temperature for 10–12 days or after up to 12 freeze/thaw cycles using four high‐throughput commercial assays. 11 Niedrist et al. investigated the stability of anti‐N antibody levels in samples after short‐term refrigerated storage or short‐ and long‐term frozen storage and concluded that anti‐N antibodies are stable during short‐term storage at −70 and 2–10°C with a slight increase in the proportion of those testing positive potentially observed after long‐term frozen storage up to 443 days. 11 , 12

The preanalytical stability of antibodies is not specific to anti‐SARS‐CoV‐2 antibodies. While delayed whole blood processing beyond 4–5 days post‐collection may adversely impact the detection of low‐level RNA of pathogens such as HIV using molecular testing, antibody levels do not significantly change with delayed sample processing as shown for syphilis antibodies, 19 , 20 or for the detection of antibodies to papillomaviruses, polyomaviruses, Epstein–Barr virus, and Helicobacter pylori antigens in samples stored at either room temperature or at 4°C for up to 6 days before processing. 21 In addition, antibody levels remain stable in samples taken through multiple freeze/thaw cycles as shown by Pinksky et al. for the detection of measles, mumps, and rubella virus IgG antibodies in serum samples taken through 10 freeze–thaw cycles, 20 or as reported by Torelli et al. for the detection of specific influenza antibodies present in serum samples stored at −20°C through 14 freeze–thaw cycles. 22

Although demonstration of analyte stability in this study was consistent across the experiments, the study has limitations. First, sample sizes were relatively small in the two controlled experiments performed in this study. Second, we were not able to compare anti‐S IgG antibody data for the 1035 samples retested after extended period of frozen storage; anti‐N antibody results obtained on fresh versus after extended frozen storage were available from this subset analysis during the NBDS phase of the program, which did not include anti‐S IgG antibody testing. Third, the evaluations of anti‐N Total Ig levels using S/CO ratios to inform semiquantitative analysis using a qualitative assay can be questioned but were previously evaluated. 23 Finally, this study was not exhaustive in its evaluation of all of the conditions that may have impacted antibody results; it did not evaluate inherent assay precision, it only focused on preanalytical conditions, and among the many preanalytical conditions that could have impacted antibody results, it only evaluated specific sample processing and testing conditions relevant to our study processing and storage extremes considered to have been the most impactful during the larger serosurveillance program. Awareness of challenges encountered during the program such as sample testing after extended 4°C storage pre‐ or post‐ serum and plasma separation influenced the choice of conditions tested in this study.

The use of samples and survey data collected from convenient US blood donor populations and captured through routine blood bank operations presents with unique opportunities to inform nationwide public health programs but can be met with unique logistical constraints because sample access and handling procedure change over time depending on the evolving needs of the program. Confirming the reliability of the anti‐SARS‐CoV‐2 antibody data obtained on samples potentially impacted by processing delays was important as these data were used to estimate SARS‐CoV‐2 infections, vaccine uptake and effectiveness, and correlates of protection in blood donors as representatives of the US population. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 This study provides further evidence that sample processing variability and testing delays did not significantly impact serologic results for surveillance.
