Chest Wall and Diaphragmatic Injuries: Imaging Evaluation and Clinical Considerations
Authors: Jung Min Choi, Soyeoun Lim, Min Ae Keum, Tae Young Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This review summarizes skeletal chest wall injuries and diaphragmatic injuries that may occur in patients with chest trauma. In Korea, most chest trauma results from blunt mechanisms and is closely associated with high-energy injuries, such as traffic accidents and falls. Skeletal chest wall and diaphragmatic injuries have a substantial impact on patient prognosis. This review describes the principal radiologic findings of rib fractures, sternal fractures, and diaphragmatic injuries and emphasizes that these findings are directly incorporated into injury severity scoring systems, thereby influencing treatment strategies and risk stratification. Radiologists should provide clear, detailed descriptions of injury patterns to support accurate severity assessment and guide therapeutic decision-making. Such reporting also facilitates consistent and clear communication across clinical care, administrative, and reimbursement processes.

INTRODUCTION

Trauma remains the leading cause of death in Korea. According to the 2024 Cause-of-Death Statistics, injury-related deaths accounted for 8.3% of all deaths. Among injury-related deaths, slips and falls were the most common (40.0%), followed by blunt trauma (15.2%) and transportation-related accidents (15.1%) (1). The chest is the second most commonly injured body region in patients with severe trauma, with a reported incidence of 33.4%, second only to the head (41.6%). Additionally, the case fatality rate associated with chest trauma is relatively high, reaching 42.8% (2). Most thoracic injuries result from blunt trauma, which is closely associated with high-energy mechanisms, such as motor vehicle collisions, falls from height, and industrial accidents. These injuries are particularly common in patients with multiple traumas and substantially contribute to morbidity and mortality (3).

Blunt chest trauma encompasses a broad spectrum of injuries, ranging from simple soft tissue injury to life-threatening major organ disruption. Among these, skeletal injuries of the chest wall, including rib and sternal fractures, and traumatic diaphragmatic rupture are key determinants of initial management strategies and clinical outcomes. The number and distribution of rib and sternal fractures may indicate injury severity and energy transfer and suggest associated injuries, such as pulmonary contusion, hemothorax, or intercostal vessel injuries.

Diaphragmatic injury is relatively uncommon, occurring in approximately 1%–15% of patients with blunt thoracic trauma. However, delayed diagnosis may lead to serious complications, including visceral herniation and ileus, making it a clinically important injury (345). In this review, we focus on structural injuries to the chest wall skeleton and diaphragm in patients with blunt chest trauma. We summarize the key CT findings that radiologists should recognize and discuss clinically relevant considerations for radiology reporting.

STRUCTURE OF THE CHEST WALL, DIAPHRAGM, AND RESPIRATORY PHYSIOLOGY

The ribs and sternum serve two major functions. First, they protect the intrathoracic organs from external mechanical forces. Second, together with the diaphragm, they contribute to respiratory motion. Anatomically, the ribs and sternum, along with the thoracic spine posteriorly, form the thoracic cage, which protects the lungs, heart, aorta, and other major vessels.

The ribs are generally classified as true, false, or floating ribs according to their relationship to the costal cartilage. The first through seventh ribs are classified as true ribs because they articulate directly with the sternum anteriorly through their respective costal cartilages. The first rib is broader and shorter than the other ribs and is connected to the manubrium of the sternum by a synchondrosis, resulting in very limited mobility. This anatomical configuration contributes to the structural stability of the upper thoracic cage. The second through seventh ribs form synovial joints with the sternal manubrium or body of the sternum through their respective costal cartilages. In contrast, the eighth to tenth ribs are classified as false ribs because their costal cartilages attach to the cartilage of the rib above and articulate with the sternum indirectly. The eleventh and twelfth ribs, which lack anterior cartilaginous attachment to the sternum, are classified as floating ribs (67).

The lower thoracic cage is separated from the abdominal cavity by the diaphragm. The diaphragm is a dome-shaped musculotendinous structure located between the inferior thoracic cavity and superior abdominal cavity. It consists of a central tendon and surrounding muscular components. Three major openings traverse the diaphragm: the aortic, esophageal, and caval hiatuses for the inferior vena cava. Except for the bare area of the liver, most of the diaphragm is covered by the pleura or peritoneum (89).

The coordinated function of the thoracic cage and diaphragm is essential for respiration. During inspiration, the diaphragm contracts and descends in a piston-like manner, whereas contraction of the intercostal muscles expands the thoracic cage, facilitating lung expansion. During expiration, the intercostal muscles relax, the sternum moves posteriorly, and the diaphragm relaxes and ascends, thereby reducing thoracic volume and promoting air expulsion (10). Therefore, skeletal injury to the chest wall is not merely a source of structural disruption or pain. By impairing the coordinated motion of the thoracic cage and diaphragm, it can adversely affect respiratory physiology and the patient’s overall clinical status. Consequently, thoracic injury should be regarded not only as an anatomic injury but also as an important mechanism of respiratory compromise and poor prognosis. Therefore, systematic recognition and accurate imaging evaluation are essential.

IMAGING EVALUATION OF CHEST TRAUMA: ROLES AND LIMITATIONS

Chest radiography is the imaging modality most commonly performed during the initial evaluation of patients with blunt chest trauma. Because it is noninvasive, relatively inexpensive, and can be performed rapidly at the bedside, chest radiography is widely used as a first-line screening tool for thoracic trauma. It can demonstrate major findings such as pulmonary contusion, pneumothorax, hemothorax, and rib fractures.

CT is more sensitive and specific than radiography for the evaluation of chest trauma. Non-contrast chest CT can effectively diagnose most traumatic injuries, including rib fractures, pneumothorax, hemothorax, and pulmonary contusions; however, its sensitivity for vascular injury is relatively limited. In contrast, contrast-enhanced chest CT is more useful for the comprehensive assessment of thoracic trauma, including vascular injuries, and is therefore recommended as the standard imaging modality in patients with chest trauma. The 2020 American College of Radiology (ACR) ‘Appropriateness Criteria® for Major Blunt Trauma’ recommends chest CT with intravenous contrast as the initial imaging examination when thoracic injury is suspected in patients with major blunt trauma (11).

Multiplanar reconstruction (MPR) and three-dimensional reconstruction are widely used techniques in chest CT. These techniques facilitate lesion detection and visualization by providing coronal, sagittal, and three-dimensional reconstructed images, as well as axial images. In particular, because the ribs and diaphragm are anatomically oriented in a direction similar to the axial imaging plane, injuries may be difficult to recognize on axial images alone, even when present. Therefore, the active use of coronal and sagittal MPR images is important for confirming injuries and more accurately evaluating their location and extent. Recently introduced in-plane rib reformation techniques produce reconstructed images in which the ribs are unfolded bilaterally relative to the spine, thereby facilitating lesion localization and visualization (Fig. 1) (1213).

Extended Focused Assessment with Sonography for Trauma (eFAST) is a useful tool for the initial evaluation of patients with trauma and enables rapid detection of associated injuries, including pneumothorax, hemothorax, and hemoperitoneum (Fig. 2) (14). Although the primary purpose of eFAST is not to directly assess the extent of individual chest wall or diaphragmatic injuries, findings obtained during the examination may serve as clues that prompt retrospective evaluation of potentially overlooked injury sites (1516).

MRI is useful for evaluating bone marrow edema, cartilage injury, and soft tissue injury, which may be difficult to detect on chest radiography or CT. In diaphragmatic injury, MRI may serve as an adjunctive diagnostic modality in subacute or chronic settings because it provides superior soft-tissue contrast compared to CT. However, in the context of emergency trauma care, the routine use of MRI as a first-line imaging modality remains constrained by practical limitations, including limited accessibility, prolonged examination time, and difficulties in patient monitoring (8917).

Recent advances in AI have also been applied to trauma care. Systematic reviews evaluating the diagnostic accuracy of AI models for detecting rib fractures on chest radiographs and CT images have reported that the sensitivity of AI tools used alone may be significantly higher than that of clinicians alone, and that diagnostic performance may improve when clinicians use AI tools as an adjunct. These findings suggest that using AI models to evaluate patients with chest trauma improves the diagnostic accuracy for detecting rib fractures (181920).

RIB FRACTURES

Rib fractures are the most common form of thoracic skeletal injury in chest trauma and are identified in approximately 10% of all patients with trauma and 50% of patients with blunt chest trauma (21). In conscious patients presenting to the emergency department with blunt chest trauma, rib fractures may be clinically suspected based on history taking and focal tenderness on palpation. They can be diagnosed on chest radiography or rib series, which are widely used as first-line imaging examinations, based on findings such as cortical discontinuity or displaced fracture fragments. CT is useful regardless of the patient’s level of consciousness. It is particularly advantageous for detecting subtle fractures that are not clearly distinguishable on radiographs and for evaluating associated injuries, such as pulmonary contusion, pneumothorax, and hemothorax.

Therefore, when rib fractures are identified, a systematic description of the number, distribution, morphology, displacement, and continuity of the fractures is important for assessing the risk of associated injuries and guiding clinical decision-making (2223). At a minimum, the report should specify the side and number of fractured ribs and indicate whether the fracture is in the anterior, lateral, or posterior portion of each rib. Fractures at specific anatomical locations should also be separately mentioned, including fractures of the first rib, fractures near the costochondral joints, and fractures adjacent to the costovertebral joint, such as those involving the rib head or rib neck.

It is also important to describe the fracture morphology, including whether the fracture is displaced, nondisplaced, comminuted, or bifocal. In particular, displaced fractures involving three or more consecutive ribs and forming a flail chest may substantially influence treatment decisions, including the need for surgical stabilization (24).

The first rib is thicker and shorter than the other ribs and forms a relatively stable structure through strong synchondroses with the clavicle and sternum. It is also protected by the shoulder girdle, including the clavicle and scapula, as well as by the surrounding muscles. Therefore, a first-rib fracture usually suggests a high-energy mechanism of injury and is associated with an increased likelihood of concomitant injury to adjacent structures. In the past, fractures of the upper ribs, including the first rib, were considered potential predictors of major vascular injury, such as thoracic aortic injury. More recently, however, they have been regarded less as isolated predictors of a specific vascular injury and more as markers of high-energy trauma. Accordingly, when upper rib fractures are identified, radiologists should carefully evaluate for associated injuries to adjacent skeletal structures, including the clavicle, scapula, sternum, and cervical spine, related to whiplash mechanisms. Moreover, associated pulmonary and pleural injuries, such as pulmonary contusion, pneumothorax, and hemothorax, as well as mediastinal and great vessel injuries, should be systematically assessed (2526).

Flail chest has traditionally been defined as a condition in which three or more consecutive ribs are each fractured at two or more sites, resulting in a free-floating segment of the chest wall. Because of this instability, the flail segment moves paradoxically, being drawn inward during inspiration and moving outward during expiration, in contrast to the normal chest wall, which expands outward during inspiration and recoils inward during expiration. This abnormal chest wall motion can reduce effective ventilation and cause pendelluft, leading to carbon dioxide retention and hypoxemia. Therefore, flail chest has traditionally been regarded as a major indication for surgical rib fixation in treatment guidelines (142728). However, even when a flail-type fracture pattern is identified on imaging, clinically apparent paradoxical chest wall motion may be absent, depending on factors such as muscle tone and the extent of injury. Reflecting this distinction, recent guidelines recommend that radiology reports differentiate between a radiological flail segment, defined by consecutive multifocal rib fractures on imaging, and a clinical flail chest, which is accompanied by paradoxical chest wall motion (2930).

STERNAL FRACTURES

Sternal fractures occur in approximately 8% of patients with blunt chest trauma. They most commonly result from deceleration injury during motor vehicle collisions, with direct impact to the anterior chest wall by a seatbelt or steering wheel being the major injury mechanism (31). Although chest radiography is usually performed as the initial examination when sternal injury is suspected, accurate diagnosis of sternal fractures on radiographs alone is challenging because of the anatomical characteristics of the sternum, which is thin anteroposteriorly and elongated craniocaudally.

CT is considered the most effective imaging modality for diagnosing sternal fractures. Several studies have reported that sagittal reconstruction is particularly useful because it better reflects the anatomical location and sternal configuration (3233). Therefore, it is advisable to include sagittal-reconstructed images in the acquisition and interpretation protocols for CT scans of chest trauma (Fig. 3). Even when sagittal reconstructions are unavailable, associated findings related to anterior chest wall injury, such as retrosternal hematoma, mediastinal hematoma, or hemopericardium, should prompt careful evaluation for an accompanying sternal fracture (34).

TRAUMATIC DIAPHRAGMATIC INJURY

In blunt trauma, traumatic diaphragmatic injury typically occurs following high-energy mechanisms, such as motor vehicle collisions or falls from height. The injury is believed to result from a combination of an abrupt elevation of intra-abdominal pressure and shearing forces across the thoracoabdominal region (935). Although the reported frequencies vary across studies, left-sided injuries are generally more common than right-sided injuries. This difference may be attributable to the protective buffering effect of the liver on the right side, which can partially absorb direct traumatic forces. However, right-sided injuries may be more difficult to diagnose because hepatic herniation can be subtle on imaging. Several studies have also reported that the diagnostic sensitivity of CT is significantly higher for left-sided than for right-sided diaphragmatic injury (36373839). In clinical case reports, right-sided diaphragmatic injuries confirmed during surgical exploration have occasionally not been clearly recognized on preoperative CT. In such cases, associated findings, including pneumothorax, lower rib fractures, and liver injury, may serve as indirect clues to the diagnosis of right-sided diaphragmatic injury (40).

Traumatic diaphragmatic injury can manifest on CT with various direct and indirect signs. Because no single CT sign is sufficiently reliable on its own, diagnosis should be based on the combined assessment of multiple imaging findings in the clinical context, particularly following high-energy thoracoabdominal blunt trauma (41). Representative direct signs include the focal defect sign or focal discontinuity sign, which refers to focal interruption of the diaphragmatic continuity or a linear defect in the diaphragmatic contour. The margins of the defect may appear thickened due to edema, an intramuscular hematoma, or muscle retraction. This is the most commonly observed CT finding in blunt diaphragmatic injury, and some studies have reported sensitivity values exceeding 90% (3541). The segmental non-recognition sign, also referred to as an absent diaphragm segment, is broadly used as a concept similar to the focal defect sign (Fig. 4). One study reported that when diaphragmatic thickening, focal defect, and segmental non-recognition were all present, the diagnostic sensitivity for diaphragmatic rupture reached 100% (4142). However, these results should be interpreted with caution because diagnostic performance may vary depending on the study design and patient population. The dangling diaphragm sign is observed when the diaphragmatic tear is relatively large. It refers to the appearance of the torn edge of the diaphragm detaching from the chest wall and curling inward toward the abdominal cavity, creating a “dangling” configuration. In a single-center study, this sign demonstrated a sensitivity of 54% and a specificity of 98% and may be diagnostically useful when interpreted in conjunction with other imaging signs (43).

Even when a direct diaphragmatic defect is not clearly visualized, diaphragmatic injury may be suspected if herniation of the abdominal organs into the thoracic cavity is identified as an indirect sign of injury. The collar sign refers to the focal waist-like constriction of a herniated abdominal organ or herniated intra-abdominal fat at the site where it passes through the diaphragmatic defect (Fig. 4). Stomach herniation is a common finding in patients with left-sided defects. In right-sided defects, a portion of the liver may protrude upward through the defect, creating a mound-like configuration known as the hump sign; alternatively, a low-attenuation band may be observed along the hepatic parenchyma adjacent to the defect, known as the band sign (Fig. 5). The diagnostic performance of these findings varies by the side of injury. The reported sensitivity ranges from 36% to 85% for left-sided injuries and from 17% to 50% for right-sided injuries, whereas specificity is relatively high, ranging from 80% to 100% (354344). However, simple organ herniation alone is not sufficient to definitively diagnose traumatic diaphragmatic injury. Non-traumatic causes, such as hiatal hernia through the esophageal hiatus or paraesophageal hernia, as well as congenital diaphragmatic defects such as Bochdalek or Morgagni hernias, should also be considered in the differential diagnosis (945). Therefore, the diagnostic value for traumatic diaphragmatic injury increases when herniated organs are accompanied by constriction at the defect site, such as the collar sign.

The dependent viscera sign refers to the finding in which abdominal organs that would normally be supported by an intact diaphragm lose diaphragmatic support and come into direct contact with the posteroinferior chest wall and ribs in the supine position. On the left side, this is usually seen as direct contact between the stomach or spleen and the posterior ribs. On the right side, the upper one-third of the liver may displace the adjacent lung parenchyma and contact the posterior chest wall (3645). Other indirect findings suggestive of diaphragmatic injury include diaphragmatic thickening, hematoma or edema around the diaphragmatic defect, unilateral elevation of the diaphragm, and abnormal blunting or abrupt cut-off of the costophrenic angle in the presence of pleural effusion or hemothorax, known as the sinus cut-off sign (414245). However, these indirect signs have relatively low specificity. Given their limited specificity, these indirect findings are best interpreted in the context of other direct and indirect CT signs, rather than as standalone diagnostic criteria.

SCORING SYSTEMS FOR INJURY SEVERITY AND RADIOLOGY REPORTING

Injury severity scoring systems are tools used to quantify the severity of injury and the risk of adverse outcomes in patients with trauma. They can be broadly classified into anatomical, physiological, comorbidity-related, and combined scoring systems. Among these, the injury severity score (ISS) and abbreviated injury scale (AIS) are widely used indices for assessing patients with severe trauma in Korea (464748).

The ISS is calculated by dividing the body into six regions: head and neck, face, chest, abdomen, extremities, and external body surface. The highest AIS scores from the three most severely injured body regions are squared and then summed. An ISS of 15 or higher is generally classified as severe trauma (47). The AIS is the fundamental scale underlying anatomic injury scoring systems. It was originally developed by the Association for the Advancement of Automotive Medicine (AAAM) to standardize the classification and severity assessment of injuries resulting from motor vehicle collisions. AIS severity scores range from 1, indicating a minor injury, to 6, indicating an injury that is considered unsurvivable. Major acute traumatic thoracic injuries assessed by AIS include rib and sternal fractures, pneumothorax or hemothorax, pulmonary contusion, and major vascular injuries.

In this article, we focus on examples of AIS grading of fractures and diaphragmatic injuries (Table 1). The AIS score for rib fractures varies according to the number and morphology of the fractures. A simple nondisplaced fracture of a single rib is assigned a score of 1, fractures of two ribs are assigned a score of 2, and fractures of three or more ribs are assigned a score of 3. However, when a radiology report describes the finding only as “multiple rib fractures” without specifying the exact number, the injury is uniformly coded as a score of 2 because the number of fractures cannot be determined. The AIS score for flail chest also differs according to its extent. Unilateral flail chest involving three to five ribs is assigned a score of 3, unilateral flail chest involving more than five ribs is assigned a score of 4, and bilateral flail chest is assigned a score of 5. A sternal fracture corresponds to an AIS score of 2. However, because associated injuries to adjacent great vessels or the heart may carry higher AIS scores, the possibility of concomitant injury should always be considered. For diaphragmatic injury, a blunt traumatic diaphragmatic hematoma is assigned a score of 2, a simple laceration of 10 cm or less without visceral herniation is assigned a score of 3, and diaphragmatic injury with visceral herniation is assigned a score of 4 (46).

Therefore, when preparing radiology reports, radiologists should consider injury severity scoring systems and include structured and quantifiable information (Table 2). In particular, in patients with severe trauma for whom AIS-based assessment is applied, it is important to clearly describe the exact number of fractures, presence or absence of displacement, presence and extent of flail chest, and associated injuries to adjacent organs to improve the accuracy of injury severity assessment. In Korea, the number and location of rib fractures, as well as the presence or absence of flail chest, may influence treatment strategies, including surgical internal fixation and administrative evaluation processes. Therefore, providing these elements specifically in radiology reports can help improve consistency in clinical decision-making and reduce discrepancies in interpretation during clinical care and evaluation. Ultimately, the role of the radiologist in trauma imaging should be understood not merely as listing imaging findings, but as providing expert interpretation that supports injury severity assessment and treatment planning in patients with trauma.

CONCLUSION

In chest trauma, rib and sternal fractures and diaphragmatic injuries are common but clinically important lesions that directly influence patient outcomes and treatment decisions. CT is the most important imaging modality for evaluating these injuries, and radiologists should clearly and systematically communicate the extent, distribution, and clinical implications of the findings through comprehensive interpretation. An accurate understanding of imaging findings in chest trauma and standardized reporting can facilitate effective communication in a multidisciplinary setting and ultimately improve the quality of patient care.
