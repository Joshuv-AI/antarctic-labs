Acute medicine and the urgent care crisis: From pressure point to system solution
Authors: Anika Wijewardane, Mark Holland
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Acute medicine was established to deliver early specialist assessment and management for patients requiring urgent medical care, typically within the first 72 h of admission. Evidence demonstrates that structured acute medical units (AMUs) and same-day emergency care (SDEC) services are associated with reduced length of stay and improved patient flow. However, inconsistent early access to AMU and suboptimal SDEC utilisation have diluted this model within increasingly pressured urgent and emergency care systems. This article reviews the intended role of acute medicine and examines contemporary challenges in service configuration and delivery. Published evidence and national benchmarking data are considered, alongside a case study describing restoration of a structured AMU model in a large district general hospital. Interventions focused on strengthened clinical leadership, defined admission pathways, multidisciplinary team integration and explicit performance metrics aligned to early discharge. These changes were associated with reduced length of stay, increased direct discharge rates and improved patient flow from the emergency department into AMU. Re-establishing acute medicine’s core function offers a practical and scalable approach to improving urgent care performance.

Introduction

Pressures on urgent and emergency care are unprecedented, reflected in sustained growth in emergency attendances, rising non-elective admissions, and increasing time spent by patients in the emergency department (ED) (Fig. 1).1, 2 Corridor care, prolonged ambulance waits and exhausted teams are no longer exceptional, and for patients, the experience can feel fragmented and impersonal.3, 4 Yet within this strained system there remains scope to do better. A changed landscape of demand and risk

As the need for acute and emergency services increase, it is essential that the capacity and resilience of the system is able to meet this demand. Internal medicine admissions increased by 29.7% between 2011 and 2020,5 while the time patients wait for a bed following a decision to admit has continued to rise (Fig. 2).2 Prolonged waits in the ED after the decision to admit are associated with increased mortality, patient harm and poorer patient experience.4, 6 Inpatient admission is not without risk, including hospital-acquired infection, deconditioning and loss of independence, particularly for frail patients.7, 8 As a result, the way that acute care is structured and delivered, and how well it integrates with pre-hospital services and other specialties, matter now more than ever.Fig. 2Increasing time spent by patients in the emergency department after a decision to admit.2Reproduced with permission from the House of Commons Library.

The role of acute medicine

Acute medicine as a specialty was established to deliver specialist care early, typically over the first 72 h, when patients first require urgent assessment, investigation, risk stratification and treatment.9 This care is delivered in dedicated acute medical units (AMUs) and, more recently, in medical same-day emergency care (SDEC) units, intended to deliver early expert decision-making, treatment and discharge.5, 10, 11

Acute medicine, when delivered effectively, has been demonstrated to improve patient outcomes, with shorter lengths of stay, fewer unnecessary admissions, and reduced mortality.5, 12 Recent data show that approximately 50% of non-elective medical admissions have a length of stay of less than 72 h,5 while up to a third are seen within SDEC.13

Acute medicine should therefore be understood not just as a specialty within the hospital, but as a time-critical clinical system concentrated at the front of the patient journey, responsible for optimising the first 72 h of care. Concentrating skills and resources within the AMU and SDEC should therefore enable the care of most non-elective admissions to be completed within the acute environment, without transfer to general medical wards.

The components of a high-functioning AMU are well described in national guidance5, 11 (Fig. 3, Fig. 4). The current challenge

Despite this, acute medicine has often drifted from its intended role. AMUs are not consistently accessed early, SDECs are often occupied by lower-acuity patients, and a substantial proportion of unplanned medical admissions do not pass through AMU.13, 15 This is reflected in data from the SAM Benchmarking Audit (SAMBA), where only 15% of unplanned medical admissions have their first medical assessment in the AMU, while over 50% are initially assessed in the ED (Fig. 5).16 Approximately 29% of acute medicine patients spend no time in AMU during their admission15 and therefore bypass focused acute medical care. System pressures may have contributed to this shift. 4-hour performance standards can incentivise rapid movement out of the ED into the first available bed rather than the most appropriate setting, leading to direct admission to downstream wards, delayed senior input and, ultimately, longer lengths of stay. At the same time, the policy and commissioning emphasis on 7-day availability and demonstrable SDEC activity can mask how SDECs are being used.11 Capacity is often inappropriately consumed by planned or semi-elective workstreams, such as intravenous therapies, anaemia pathways or imaging attendances,13 thereby limiting access for urgent acute patients and reducing the impact of SDEC on admission avoidance.

In appropriately selected populations, SDECs can expect conversion rates to admission of approximately 10–20%.10 Markedly lower conversion rates seen in some services may indicate a predominance of lower-acuity referrals, raising questions about whether current referral criteria reliably identify patients who would benefit most from acute physician SDEC care.

Taken together, reduced access to AMU and suboptimal use of SDEC limit early exposure to acute medical expertise. This contributes to fewer early discharges, higher bed occupancy and ED overcrowding, creating a cycle of pressure and delay that is associated with increased mortality.6

A step towards change

Recent recommendations from SAM10, 17 and the introduction of the Model Acute Pathway18 offer an opportunity to re-establish the role of AMU and SDEC in the early phase of acute illness, and to position acute physicians earlier in the patient pathway. Clinical judgement remains central to this, particularly for patients with multimorbidity, frailty or diagnostic uncertainty. Ensuring that the recommendations and standards translate into meaningful change will depend on AMUs and SDECs being resourced and supported to function as intended, allowing timely management and informed decisions early in the patient journey.

Restoring acute medicine alone will not resolve urgent and emergency care pressures; high numbers of patients in hospitals without criteria to reside, downstream bed constraints, workforce shortages and community capacity remain central system challenges that must be addressed in parallel to delivering efficient acute medical care.

The evidence base for acute medicine

Since the advent of evidence-based medicine, randomised controlled trials (RCTs) have dominated the medical research landscape.19 While large RCTs have delivered a wide range of effective therapies, there are fundamental problems with the compatibility of RCTs and acute medicine. Moreover, when it comes to systematic reviews, the methodologies used to evaluate the effectiveness of acute medicine, such as retrospective observational studies, will frequently be at a disadvantage in the evidence-base hierarchy.20 Pragmatically, the financial resources from industry-funded research have never been, and probably never will be, available to acute medicine.

Several systematic reviews of AMUs have confirmed their benefit.21, 22, 23, 24 These include reduced length of stay, mortality and time to be transferred from ED to a medical bed. In addition, more patients are discharged directly from AMU, while readmissions are not increased. There is also some evidence of improved patient and staff satisfaction within the AMU model,21 although this is not definitive.22 There was heterogeneity in the structure and function of AMUs, which made it difficult to say which components contribute to their success. However, the systematic review by Reid et al24 found benefits associated with dedicated pharmacy and occupational therapy services, rapid-access clinics and formal handovers, as well as some evidence that consultant presence in AMU is associated with reduced mortality and length of stay.

The landmark study by Rooney et al25 showed a 44.4% relative reduction in all-cause hospital mortality from 12.6% in 2002 to 7.0% in 2006, a trend which has continued over 23 years.12 The narrative review by Jones called for more evidence, emphasising the need to address what components of the acute medical care process do and don’t make a positive difference.26 Byrne and Silke acknowledged the limitation of AMU-focused research being non-randomised controlled.27 Pragmatically, they argued that an evidence base was necessary and could be found in large observational databases. They also drew attention to hospitalist care in North America, which has shown reductions in length of stay, mortality and readmission rates, postulating that benefits might be related to physicians having time dedicated to acute care and/or developing expertise in acute medical care.

With the complex heterogeneity of acute medical services, it is difficult to replicate RCTs for pathways found in discrete conditions such as hyperacute stroke. In such models therefore, where interventions are multifaceted and context-dependent, improvement is often best understood through a combination of observational data, operational insight and qualitative experience, rather than experimental study design alone.

Therefore, evidence is more likely to be transferable as opposed to generalisable. Acute medicine is delivered by the MDT and there is evidence for the success of this model.28, 29 Qualitative research methodologies may be more useful to acute medicine than other medical specialties. Accepting that acute medical care is a complex heterogenous process, with an emphasis on the performance of healthcare professionals and the patient experience, qualitative research can help us understand team dynamics, the lived experiences of patients and professionals, and the barriers to optimal care provision. For example, Lees-Deutsch et al provide a rich understanding of the poor experience, and necessary remedial actions, when patients are moved from an AMU to a discharge lounge.30

In summary, the AMU is associated with reduced mortality, length of stay and readmission rates. With the emergence of SDEC and virtual wards, further research is necessary to understand how acute medicine’s services can be optimally delivered.

A case study: a model from a large district general hospital

To illustrate how restoring the core principles of acute medicine can translate into measurable improvement, we describe the service redesign undertaken in a large district general hospital. This case study is presented as an illustrative service example rather than a formal evaluation, with detailed methodology and analysis previously published.14

As in many organisations, the AMU had drifted from its role. Consultant ward rounds extended into the afternoon, MDT working was inconsistent, lengths of stay were longer than expected, and increasing numbers of patients bypassed the AMU, limiting access to early specialist decision-making.

A structured redesign was undertaken, to restore the core function of the AMU, focusing on high-quality care in the first 72 h of acute illness. Multidisciplinary board rounds were introduced, consultant and MDT rotas were more closely aligned with peak demand, and real-time data was used to understand flow and identify delay. A bespoke format was created for the board round, which enabled 42 patients to be discussed succinctly in 30 min (Fig. 6).Fig. 6A bespoke board round structure created as a guide for the MDT.

SDEC access and its operational model were also redesigned. The model shifted from a push-based system, dominated by lower-acuity patients, to a pull-based approach, with acute physicians identifying those patients most likely to benefit from focused acute input in SDEC based on clinical presentation, for example, those with concerning features or diagnostic uncertainty. Direct referral access from general practice was strengthened through defined referral criteria, while also supporting earlier clinician-to-clinician discussion and more appropriate patient selection.

These changes delivered sustained and measurable improvement. Average AMU length of stay fell from 3.5 days to under 2 days. Flow from ED into AMU doubled, rising from 30% to 60%, while daily AMU discharges also doubled.14 Currently, 63% of patients admitted to the AMU are discharged home directly (Fig. 7). Patients initially managed in the AMU and subsequently transferred to another ward had a total length of stay 1.2 days shorter than those admitted directly to base wards, suggesting potential additional hospital-wide benefits of effective acute care delivery within the AMU.14 There was also an improvement of the national 4 h standard (the national performance metric used across NHS organisations, measuring the percentage of patients who are admitted, transferred or discharged from the ED within 4 h of arrival) from 30% to 70% (data derived from the internal trust ED dashboard 2023–24; unpublished). While improvements within the AMU were a key component, they formed part of a wider trust-level UEC improvement programme aimed at restoring patient flow and reducing system delays. Direct general practice access to SDEC increased from 20% to 50% (data derived from internal dashboard). The transformation was enabled by both cultural and structural change. Central to success was restoring the voice of acute medicine at organisational level. This created the conditions for constructive, credible dialogue between senior clinicians and trust executives, allowing acute medicine to be positioned deliberately at the heart of organisational strategy rather than as a service reacting to pressure. Data underpinned the improvement at every level, informing operational decisions as well as strategic direction. Together, these elements delivered sustainable improvement through clarity of purpose, consistent application of the core principles of acute medicine, and explicit organisational commitment to doing the fundamentals well (Fig. 8).Fig. 8Principles that underpinned sustained improvement at the district general hospital.

Conclusion

Pressures across urgent and emergency care continue to intensify, and how we respond to this challenge matters. In many systems, AMUs and SDECs no longer consistently function as early points of expert assessment and decision-making for acutely unwell medical patients, limiting the impact that acute medicine can have at the beginning of the patient pathway. Drawing on national data, the existing evidence and a case study of redesign, this paper has described how restoring the core function of acute medicine can offer a practical and immediately available opportunity to improve flow and patient experience.

These gains do not depend on new services or radical redesign. They arise from restoring clarity of purpose, aligning operational practice with clinical intent, and embedding acute medicine within organisational strategy. At a time of sustained system pressure, allowing acute medicine to function as intended remains a powerful lever to support improvements in both patient experience and hospital performance alike.
