Remotely delivered resistance training program in adult sarcoma survivors: a pilot study
Authors: Lara E. Davis, Shannon C. Austin, James B. Hayden, Yee-Cheen Doung, Kenneth R. Gundle, Christopher W. Ryan, Sydnee A. Stoyles, Kerri M. Winters-Stone
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTreatment of sarcoma often leads to serious long-term health consequences, including decreased physical functioning. Supervised progressive resistance training (PRT) has been shown to improve physical functioning in survivors of carcinomas. We conducted a single-arm, pre-post study to assess the feasibility of remote delivery of a supervised, individualized resistance training program in sarcoma survivors.MethodsSurvivors of extremity sarcomas who were more than 2 years from final sarcoma treatment with no evidence of recurrent disease were eligible. Participants followed a PRT program of exercises that were tailored to each patient’s exercise capacity. Each week for 12 weeks, participants engaged in one unsupervised session and one supervised session delivered remotely by videoconferencing. Feasibility was measured by retention rate, session completion rate, and safety. Changes in self-reported physical function, health-related quality of life, and fatigue were assessed with patient reported outcome measures, while objective physical function was assessed by the short physical performance battery (SPPB). The COVID pandemic emerged during the accrual period, requiring protocol modification to objective assessments.ResultsTen participants aged 27–69 enrolled in the study; the majority had a lower extremity tumor (70%) and all participants had undergone surgery and chemotherapy for sarcoma. Nine participants completed end-of-intervention assessments (90% retention). Adherence to twice-weekly exercise sessions was 86%. Significant improvements in pre-post 36-Item Short Form Survey (SF-36) physical component summary score showed a moderate-to-large effect size (Cohen’s d > 0.50). Self-reported fatigue also improved (FACIT-Fatigue 9 point improvement, d = 0.61, p = 0.06). SPPB data was incomplete due to COVID-related study disruption of in-person testing.ConclusionRemote delivery of PRT was feasible and safe in sarcoma survivors and improved several domains of health-related quality of life. A larger, definitive exercise trial could inform specific exercise prescription(s) for sarcoma patients, who currently have little evidence-based exercise guidance.Trial registrationRegistered with ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04247425) on 2020-01-24.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12885-026-16399-z.

Introduction

Sarcomas are a collection of connective tissue cancers that make up 15–20% of pediatric and ~ 1% of adult malignancies [1]. Curative-intent sarcoma treatment requires surgical resection and may also include radiation and chemotherapy. Although current surgical approaches often avoid amputation, surgery to remove a sarcoma of the extremity or limb girdle may profoundly influence physical function [2–7]. As a result of cancer treatment, extremity sarcoma survivors are often left with poor cardiovascular fitness, balance, and strength leading to lower physical health-related quality of life compared with the general population [8–16].

A few small studies in pediatric sarcoma patients suggest that exercise before or during sarcoma treatment could provide health-related benefits [17–19], but no studies have examined the feasibility and potential efficacy of exercise to improve health-related quality of life in sarcoma survivors. Despite the high risk for reduced physical functioning post-treatment, no strategies for improving physical function have been developed for sarcoma survivors, and clinical guidelines are bereft of recommendations to manage functional consequences of treatment.

The American College of Sports Medicine reviewed existing research on exercise in adult cancer patients and survivors and published a consensus statement highlighting multiple research gaps, but emphasized the lack of harm and likely exercise-induced improvements across multiple measures [20]. Specifically, resistance (strength) training reduces frailty [21] and improves strength [22, 23] and physical functioning [22, 24] in breast cancer survivors. A prospective trial of resistance training in young adult survivors of childhood cancer, including sarcoma, demonstrated increased lean mass and strength over time [25]. Exercise rehabilitation could play an important role in helping sarcoma survivors improve health-related quality of life, regain physical function and return to participation in life and work roles [26–31]. Resistance training is the best exercise type for improving physical functioning, which is a primary limitation among sarcoma survivors [2, 5, 22, 24, 25].

The implementation of accessible, tolerable exercise interventions that individuals can adhere to has been challenging. Unsupervised home-based programs maximize ease of use at the expense of adherence and ultimate benefit, while supervised training at a central facility is more rigorous but less convenient, leading to less uptake. Sarcomas are rare cancers and are thus scattered geographically, further reducing the feasibility of requiring travel to a central location.

We hypothesized that a partially supervised resistance training program administered through a virtual platform would improve physical function and health-related quality of life for sarcoma survivors. We thus conducted a pilot study to determine the feasibility and preliminary efficacy of an individually tailored progressive resistance training (PRT) exercise program delivered through videoconferencing to sarcoma survivors.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a single-arm, prospective pilot study (Fig. 1; full protocol available in Supplemental Materials). The study was approved by the Oregon Health & Science University (OHSU) institutional review board (STUDY00019942), registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04247425) [32]. The study was conducted in accordance with recognized ethical guidelines (Belmont Report and U.S. Common Rule).

Population and recruitment

Potential patients for the study were identified by the study investigators through the OHSU Sarcoma Survivorship Registry and the OHSU Multidisciplinary Sarcoma Clinic. Potentially eligible participants were approached by study staff or investigators to determine degree of interest and offered additional information on the trial. All patients provided written informed consent, and assent if appropriate, before enrollment. Eligible participants were over age 15 and had been treated for sarcoma with surgery, radiation and/or chemotherapy, had no current evidence of recurrent or residual disease, and completed sarcoma treatment at least two years prior to study enrollment. The sarcoma location must have been in the extremities, body wall, pelvic/shoulder girdle, or axial skeleton. At baseline, eligible participants had to engage in less than an average of one hour of planned resistance training per week over the past 4 weeks as measured by self-report and be willing to commit to twice-weekly exercise sessions for 12 weeks. Exclusion criteria included medical contraindication(s) to resistance training as determined by the treating physician, non-English speaking (due to limited interpretation resources), and dependence on a mobility device (e.g., crutches, wheelchair) for independent activities of daily living. Patients with intra-thoracic, intra-abdominal, or cranial sarcomas were not eligible. If a participant needed equipment to connect to video-conferenced based exercise sessions a laptop or cellular enabled tablet was provided to them for the duration of the study.

Resistance training intervention

Participants were expected to complete 2 exercise training sessions per week for a total of 24 exercise sessions over a 12-week period with at least 1 full day of rest between each session. Each session was approximately one hour in duration. Using videoconferencing software (Zoom), one weekly supervised exercise coaching session was conducted remotely with the exercise physiologist (EP). The second weekly exercise session was self-supervised. Both participants and the trainer recorded session attendance and compliance (ability to complete prescribed exercises, sets and reps) to the prescribed session.

The basic resistance training program utilized in this study was based on American College of Sports Medicine guidelines for progressive resistance training in healthy older adults and for exercise in cancer survivors [20, 33]. The program consisted of 3 upper body, 4 lower body and 2 core exercises using bodyweight and resistance bands; free weights (dumbbells and/or weight vest) were available if participants needed additional resistance to meet the target training intensity. Each participant’s PRT prescription was based on an initial set and volume of exercises outlined in Table 1 and the study protocol (Supplemental file). The initial program might be modified based on a physician’s recommendation (i.e., low intensity), participant limitations (i.e., obesity) and the EP’s observation of their capacity during initial testing (i.e., chair stand instead of squat for persons with poor balance). The intensity goal for the program was to reach and train at a moderate intensity, where participants could complete 12–15 repetitions of each exercise with proper form (i.e., 12–15 repetition maximum) for 2–3 sets. Depending upon initial response to the program further modifications could be made, including up- or down- regulating exercise intensity to achieve the target 12–15 RM intensity. Thus, when a participant was able to complete two sets of 15 repetitions of an exercise, a 3rd set was added; when a participant was able to complete 3 sets of 15 reps of an exercise, the intensity was increased (i.e., harder band, heavier dumbbells). Repetitions progressed until the next intensity increase, unless they reached a point where further increases in intensity might lead to intolerance (i.e., fatigue, pain, etc.). All participants were provided with the equipment necessary for their individualized exercise program based on their initial capacity and preference (e.g., a set of resistance bands, dumbbells, weighted vests). For example, a participant with a low starting capacity and no familiarity with resistance training would start with bands and body weight; whereas a participant with a higher level of baseline functioning and some prior experience with resistance training might be provided with dumbbells and a weight vest.

Measures

Feasibility (Supp Table). Retention, adherence and safety data were collected using study records and exercise logs maintained by the EP. Participants completed logs on their unsupervised days to report exercise completion. The EP was able to track weekly progress and make adjustments based on information and feedback from participants as well as weekly exercise session notes that were recorded during all supervised sessions. Participants were instructed to report all adverse effects (AEs) during the study to the EP as soon as possible after they occurred. Additionally, participants completed an electronic bi-weekly AE survey. AEs reported through this survey would be followed up by study staff with a phone call when self-reported severity was a 5 (moderate) or greater to determine reportability to the IRB. Participants also had the opportunity to report AEs to the EP during weekly supervised exercise sessions.

Patient reported outcomes (Supp Table). Cancer-specific physical function: The Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information-Cancer-specific (PROMIS-Ca) physical function measure is a 45-item questionnaire that assesses self-reported physical capability, focusing on activities of daily living [34]. For example, respondents are asked if their health limits them from doing housework or jobs around the house with answer choices ranging from “not at all” to “cannot do.” PROMIS-Ca scores are transformed into a T-score with a mean of 50 and a standard deviation of 10, where higher scores indicate better physical function. A difference of 2 to 6 PROMIS T-score points suggests meaningful change [35]. Scores were calculated via HealthMeasures Scoring Service (www.assessmentcenter.net/ac_scoringservice). Health-related quality of life: The 36-Item Short Form Survey (SF-36) is a survey tool designed for use in the general population that has been used frequently to measure health in studies of cancer patients and survivors with high reliability, and has been validated in sarcoma patients [36–45]. The SF-36 is composed of eight scales: physical functioning, role limitations physical, vitality, bodily pain, general health, mental health, role limitations mental, social function. Summary scores for physical and mental health are also calculated from the subscales to create Physical Component Summary (PCS) and Mental Component Summary (MCS) scales. The SF-36 scale scores range from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating higher functioning. The minimally clinically important difference, or the smallest difference in an SF-36 score that is considered clinically relevant, is typically in the range of 4 to 5 points for orthopedic oncology patients [46, 47]. Fatigue: The Functional Assessment of Chronic Illness Therapy – Fatigue (FACIT-Fatigue) scale is a 13 item, unidimensional, 5-point Likert scale, measuring physical fatigue over the past week [48]. The scale has high internal consistency and is widely used in exercise studies in cancer survivors [49]. FACIT-Fatigue scores range from 0 to 52 with higher scores indicating less fatigue and better quality of life. Minimally important difference for FACIT-Fatigue is 3–6 [50].

Objective physical functioning. The short physical performance battery (SPPB) consists of three timed tests: chair stands, standing balance, and usual gait speed [51, 52]. Chair stands time how long it takes participants, in seconds, to rise and sit from a chair five consecutive times as fast as possible. Standing balance includes a sequence of timed stance tests of increasing difficulty. Gait speed (m/sec) takes the average of two repeated walks along a 4-meter course at a person’s typical pace. Each test is scored 0 (unable) to 4, based on quartiles of performance and then summed for an overall score of 0 to 12 where higher values indicate higher physical functioning. While initially planned for baseline and end of intervention assessments, objective measurements were incomplete due to pandemic-related protocol modifications.

Statistical considerations

Enrolled participants that completed baseline assessments and at least one day of exercise intervention were considered evaluable. The primary objective of feasibility took into consideration retention rate, exercise adherence rate and exercise-related serious adverse events (AEs). Feasibility was defined as achieving 90% participant retention [i.e., 9 of 10 participants completing end-of-intervention assessments] with more than 75% exercise adherence rate [i.e., each participant that completed end-of-intervention assessments also completed at least 18 of 24 prescribed training sessions] without related serious AEs.

Descriptive statistics were completed and reported as means (standard deviations; SD) or frequencies. Feasibility data were reported as percentages. Effect sizes and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated from baseline to post-intervention for self-reported outcomes using the effsize package in R, specifying the data is paired [53]. Effect sizes were reported as those initially suggested by J Cohen and expanded by SS Sawilowsky (0.01: very small; 0.20: small; 0.50: medium; 0.80: large; 1.20: very large; 2.0: huge) [54, 55]. Paired t-tests were conducted to compare outcomes between baseline and post-intervention. The alpha level was established at 0.05 for all analyses. Analyses were completed in R version 4.3.2 [56].

Results

Ten sarcoma survivors were pre-screened by their treating physician as likely to meet eligibility criteria and be interested in research and approached for potential participation. All 10 provided informed consent, met study eligibility and enrolled in the study (100% conversion). COVID-19 related shutdown of in-person human subject research occurred during the study period and disrupted collection of objective physical functioning tests. However, the intervention and patient reported outcome assessments were still deliverable. Baseline demographics and clinical characteristics are in Table 2.

Baseline demographics and clinical characteristics (n = 10)

All 10 participants completed the baseline assessments and received an individualized resistance training prescription. Treating physician evaluations for all 10 participants indicated activity as tolerated was allowable, with no physician-defined exercise modifications required. Individual physical limitations varied substantially, including severe obesity, peripheral neuropathy, lymphedema, and weakness in specific muscle groups (hip adductors, knee extensors). Though it is beyond the scope of the paper to report every modification for every participant, general types of modifications occurred such as allowing an obese participant to perform some exercises seated (i.e., chest press) or with a shallower range of movement; having patients with peripheral neuropathy hold onto a chair during exercises that challenged balance; and decreasing the intensity of exercises in body regions with lymphedema.

One participant withdrew in week 5 due to a cancer recurrence, yielding a 12-week retention rate of 90%. Among the 9 participants who completed the 12-week intervention, overall adherence to twice weekly exercise sessions was 86.0% [95% CI 72.7–99.3%]. Five participants completed all 24 of the prescribed exercise sessions (100% adherence), 1 participant completed 23 total sessions (95% adherence), 1 participant completed 19 total sessions (79%), and 2 participants completed 12 sessions (50% adherence). No related serious adverse events were reported by any of the participants.

Over the 12-week intervention period, participants reported improvements in all self-report measures (Table 3). Changes in physical functioning were of a small effect size, with slightly larger effect using the SF-36 subscale (Cohen’s d = 0.27, 95% CI -0.03-0.57) than the PROMIS-Cancer specific scale (Cohen’s d = 0.11, 95% CI -0.19-0.42). Moderate effect sizes were found for improvements in all other SF-36 subscales, except for vitality (Cohen’s d = 0.38, 95% CI -0.55–1.33) [47]. There was a statistically significant change and a large effect size for bodily pain (p = 0.03, Cohen’s d = 0.93, 95% CI 0.00-1.84). The improvements in the PCS and MCS achieved the minimal clinically important difference for people with sarcoma (4 points) [46] and changes were significant for the PCS (p = 0.02). Fatigue, using the FACIT-Fatigue, improved by 9 points, exceeding the minimally important difference [50], yielding a moderate effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.68, 95% CI 0.06–1.43).

Discussion

This pilot study delivered a partially supervised resistance training program through a virtual platform to sarcoma survivors with a variety of physical limitations. Among our small sample, retention and adherence were both strong, PRT prescriptions were easily tailored, and there were no exercise-related serious adverse events. Complex physical limitations within a heterogeneous sarcoma population have made it challenging to design and study a broadly applicable exercise intervention that could also be replicable and scalable. Concerns related to adherence and tolerability with an unsupervised home-based program versus supervised training at a less convenient central facility have also likely contributed to a lack of prospective exercise studies in sarcoma survivors. However, our pilot data suggest that a home-based, individualized, partially supervised resistance training program for sarcoma survivors is feasible and safe, potentially bridging the gap between a desire for exercise supervision and ensuring programs are accessible to this population.

Many cancer survivors report exercising less frequently post-diagnosis compared with pre-diagnosis but, despite barriers to exercise such as fatigue and lack of time, most are interested in learning about exercise and in exercising more frequently [31]. Trial eligibility required that participants were engaged in less than one hour per week of exercise prior to the study, but adherence to twice weekly resistance training exceeded average exercise adherence among cancer survivors [57–59]. Further, adherence exceeded the 75% threshold shown in a Cochrane review to be associated with better outcomes in adult cancer survivors [60].

This is not the first study to examine feasibility of remote exercise training for cancer survivors, though at the time our study was among the first to explore this method of delivery pre-COVID [61]. It is, however, the first to assess the feasibility of videoconferencing to deliver home-based training programs to sarcoma survivors. Our findings validate existing literature supporting safety of remote exercise training [62], and it expands options for patients and providers by offering videoconferencing as a convenient delivery method for exercise instruction. The remote exercise instruction was particularly timely given the unanticipated changes faced during the COVID-19 pandemic; while data collection procedures had to be modified, the study intervention continued without disruption. Remote exercise instruction could dramatically increase the number of survivors with access to high quality exercise interventions [63]. Participants in this study were located across a large catchment area and most were of middle to low socioeconomic status facing both geographic and financial barriers to engaging in supervised exercise.

Importantly, this study suggests that even in a heterogenous sarcoma population, it might be possible to start with a basic set of resistance exercises for all participants that are then modified based on individual need. If future studies confirm the effectiveness of this approach, it can be implemented in a systematic way by trained staff and is thus scalable. Future studies could incorporate aerobic exercise as well to target a broader set of outcomes that would test a program that is similar to that currently recommended for all cancer survivors by the American College of Sports Medicine [64].

This pilot was not powered to determine efficacy, although efficacy measures were important secondary objectives. The 12-week exercise intervention was not expected to yield significant improvement in objective measures of physical function and thus while the inability to collect these data from many subjects was disappointing, it does not substantially reduce the impact of the study. However, patient reported improvement in physical function (as assessed by PROMIS and the SF-36 subscale) was smaller than anticipated. It is possible that this is related to the younger age of our study population (mean 47, range 27–69) than other populations studied by our group [65]. Nonetheless, the overall SF-36 Physical Component Summary (PCS) indicates a statistically significant and clinically important pre-post difference with moderate effect size. Some subscales had substantial pre-post improvement, most notably in physical role functioning (e.g., assessing physical health impacts on work or regular daily activities) and bodily pain. The statistical significance of results from t-test analyses should be interpreted cautiously due to the small sample size, yet when considered in conjunction with pre-post differences that exceed the minimal clinically important difference and moderate Cohen’s effect size, the SF-36 PCS may indicate a true and significant improvement in these participants’ overall experience of physical health.

One unanticipated observation during this study was the positive effect on the mental and emotional well-being of participants. Previous research has established that supervised programs lead to better outcomes than unsupervised exercise, which may be attributable to better exercise adherence and motivation [66, 67]. Informal feedback from this study’s participants supports this finding. For example, one participant said that she was more motivated to exercise when she was unable to leave her house due to COVID-19 because she looked forward to the social connection with the EP. Another participant said that his regular weekly exercise sessions provided a source of stability when everything else in his world was unstable. The SF-36 Mental Component Summary documented a clinically important pre-post difference and a moderate-large Cohen’s effect size, supporting these observations.

Conclusion

This pilot study demonstrates the feasibility of resistance training delivered remotely to engage sarcoma survivors in physical activity. Even though our sample was small, adherence was high, supporting the desire for access to high-quality, low-cost, at-home exercise instruction among sarcoma survivors, and patient reported outcomes indicate a beneficial effect on health-related quality of life. The minimal exercise modifications required for study participants and the success of remote delivery will enable the expansion of this approach to the larger sarcoma population. Development of a definitive trial of remote exercise instruction for sarcoma survivors is warranted.
