On matrix valued (asymmetric) truncated Toeplitz operators
NİHAT GÖKHAN GÖĞÜŞ, REWAYAT KHAN

Abstract
. Matrix valued (asymmetric) truncated Toeplitz operators are generally not complex symmetric. In this paper, we define a new conjugation with unique properties and study its relation to matrix valued asymmetric truncated Toeplitz operators. We also explore the connections between matrix valued asymmetric truncated Toeplitz operators and Hankel operators with matrix symbols.



2010 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary 47B35, Secondary 47B32, 30D20

1.
Introduction

Let
H^{2}
be the classical Hardy space in the unit disk
\mathbb{D}=\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}:|\lambda|<1\}
. Truncated Toeplitz operators (TTO’s) and asymmetric truncated Toeplitz operators (ATTO’s) are compressions of multiplication operator to the backward shift invariant subspaces of
H^{2}
(with two possibly different underlying subspaces in the asymmetric case). Each of these subspaces is of the form
K_{\theta}=(\theta H^{2})^{\perp}=H^{2}\ominus\theta H^{2}
, where
\theta
is a complex-valued inner function:
\theta\in H^{\infty}
and
|\theta({z})|=1
a.e. on the unit circle
\mathbb{T}=\partial\mathbb{D}=\{{z}\in\mathbb{C}:|{z}|=1\}
. Since D. Sarason’s paper
[
30
]
TTO’s, and later on ATTO’s
[
8
,
7
,
3
]
, have been intensely studied (see
[
1
,
10
,
12
,
15
,
17
,
31
]
and
[
6
,
18
,
19
,
20
,
21
,
26
,
27
]
).

It is natural to consider TTO’s and ATTO’s defined on subspaces of vector valued Hardy space
H^{2}(\mathcal{H})
with
\mathcal{H}
a separable complex Hilbert space. A vector valued model space
K_{\Theta}\subset H^{2}(\mathcal{H})
is the orthogonal complement of
\Theta H^{2}(\mathcal{H})
, that is,
K_{\Theta}=H^{2}(\mathcal{H})\ominus\Theta H^{2}(\mathcal{H})
. Here
\Theta
is an operator-valued inner function: a function with values in
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
(the algebra of all bounded linear operators on
\mathcal{H}
), analytic in
\mathbb{D}
, bounded and such that the boundary values
\Theta({z})
are unitary operators a.e. on
\mathbb{T}
. These spaces appear in connection with model theory of Hilbert space contractions (see
[
28
]
). Let
P_{\Theta}
be the orthogonal projection from
L^{2}(\mathcal{H})
onto
K_{\Theta}
.

For two operator valued inner functions
\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2}\in H^{\infty}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}))
and
\Phi\in L^{2}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}))
(again, see Sections
2
and
for definitions) let

(1.1)

A_{\Phi}^{\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2}}f=P_{\Theta_{2}}(\Phi f),\quad f\in K_{\Theta_{1}}\cap H^{\infty}(\mathcal{H}).

The operator
A_{\Phi}^{\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2}}
is called a matrix valued asymmetric truncated Toeplitz operator (MATTO), while
A_{\Phi}^{\Theta_{1}}=A_{\Phi}^{\Theta_{1},\Theta_{1}}
is called a matrix valued truncated Toeplitz operator (MTTO, see
[
25
]
). Both are densely defined on
K_{\Theta_{1}}
. Let
\mathcal{MT}(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})
be the set of all MATTO’s of the form (
1.1
) which can be extended boundedly to the whole space
K_{\Theta_{1}}
and for
\Theta_{1}=\Theta_{2}=\Theta
let
\mathcal{MT}(\Theta)=\mathcal{MT}(\Theta,\Theta)
.

Two important examples of operators from
\mathcal{MT}(\Theta)
are the model operators

(1.2)

S_{\Theta}=A_{z}^{\Theta}=A_{zI_{\mathcal{H}}}^{\Theta}\quad\text{and}\quad S_{\Theta}^{*}=A_{\bar{z}}^{\Theta}=A_{\bar{z}I_{\mathcal{H}}}^{\Theta}.

It is known that each
C_{0}
contraction with finite defect indices is unitarily equivalent to
S_{\Theta}
for some operator-valued inner function
\Theta
(see
[
28
, Chapter IV]
). On the other hand, operators from
\mathcal{MT}(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})
with certain bounded analytic symbols appear as the operators intertwining
S_{\Theta_{1}}
and
S_{\Theta_{2}}
(see
[
2
, p. 238]
).
Some algebraic properties of MTTO’s were studied in
[
25
]
, while the asymmetric case was investigated in
[
24
]
.

The plan of the paper is the following:
In Sections 2 and 3, we briefly give some background details of vector valued function spaces and their operators. In Section 4, we define a new conjugation corresponding to the decomposition of model space and discuss its properties. The connection between MATTO’s, vectorial Hankel operators and conjugations are discussed in Section 5.

2.
Vector valued function spaces and their operators

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a complex separable Hilbert space. In what follows,
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{H}}
and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
will denote the norm and the inner product on
\mathcal{H}
, respectively. Moreover, we will assume that
\dim\mathcal{H}<\infty
. The space
L^{2}(\mathcal{H})
can be defined as

L^{2}(\mathcal{H})=\{f\colon\mathbb{T}\to\mathcal{H}:f\text{ is measurable and}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\|f({z})\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\,dm({z})<\infty\}

(
m
being the normalized Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{T}
). As usual, each
f\in L^{2}(\mathcal{H})
is interpreted as a class of functions equal to representing
f
a.e. on
\mathbb{T}
with respect to
m
. The space
L^{2}(\mathcal{H})
is a (separable) Hilbert space with the inner product given by

\langle f,g\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathcal{H})}=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\langle f({z}),g({z})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\,dm({z}),\quad f,g\in L^{2}(\mathcal{H}).

Equivalently,
L^{2}(\mathcal{H})
consists of elements
f:\mathbb{T}\to\mathcal{H}
of the form

(2.1)

\begin{array}[]{rl}f({z})=\sum\limits_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}a_{n}{z}^{n}&(\text{a.e. on }\mathbb{T})\\
\text{with}&\{a_{n}\}\subset\mathcal{H}\text{ such that}\sum\limits_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\|a_{n}\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}<\infty.\end{array}

The
n
-th Fourier coefficient
a_{n}
of
f\in L^{2}(\mathcal{H})
is determined by

(2.2)

\langle a_{n},x\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\overline{{z}}^{n}\langle f({z}),x\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}dm({z})\quad\text{for all }x\in\mathcal{H}.

If
f\in L^{2}(\mathcal{H})
is given by (
2.1
), then its Fourier series converges in the
L^{2}(\mathcal{H})
norm and

\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{H})}^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\|f({z})\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\,dm({z})=\sum\limits_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\|a_{n}\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}.

Moreover, for
\displaystyle g({z})=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}b_{n}{z}^{n}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{H})
we have

\langle f,g\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathcal{H})}=\sum\limits_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\langle a_{n},b_{n}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}.

For
\mathcal{H}=\mathbb{C}
we denote
L^{2}=L^{2}(\mathbb{C})
.

The vector valued Hardy space
H^{2}(\mathcal{H})
is defined as the set of all the elements of
L^{2}(\mathcal{H})
whose Fourier coefficients with negative indices vanish, that is,

H^{2}(\mathcal{H})=\left\{f\in L^{2}(\mathcal{H}):f({z})=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}{z}^{n}\right\}.

Each
f\in H^{2}(\mathcal{H})
,
\displaystyle f({z})=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}{z}^{n}
, can also be identified with a function

f(\lambda)=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}\lambda^{n},\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{D},

analytic in the unit disk
\mathbb{D}
(the boundary values
f({z})
can be obtained from the radial limits, which converge to the boundary function in the
L^{2}(\mathcal{H})
norm). Denote by
P_{+}
the orthogonal projection
P_{+}:L^{2}(\mathcal{H})\to H^{2}(\mathcal{H})
,

P_{+}\left(\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}a_{n}{z}^{n}\right)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}{z}^{n},

and let
H^{2}=H^{2}(\mathbb{C})
.

We can also consider the spaces

L^{\infty}(\mathcal{H})=\left\{f\colon\mathbb{T}\to\mathcal{H}:\begin{array}[]{l}f\text{ is measurable and}\\
\ \|f\|_{\infty}=\sees_{{z}\in\mathbb{T}}\|f({z})\|_{\mathcal{H}}<\infty\end{array}\right\}

(clearly,
L^{\infty}(\mathcal{H})\subset L^{2}(\mathcal{H})
) and

H^{\infty}(\mathcal{H})=L^{\infty}(\mathcal{H})\cap H^{2}(\mathcal{H}),

the latter seen also as the space of all bounded
\mathcal{H}
–valued functions which are analytic in
\mathbb{D}
.

Now let
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
be the algebra of all bounded linear operators on
\mathcal{H}
equipped with the operator norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})}
. In the case
\dim\mathcal{H}=d<\infty
, each element of
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
can be identified with a
d\times d
matrix. Denote

L^{\infty}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}))=\left\{{\mathbf{F}}\colon\mathbb{T}\to\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}):\!\!\begin{array}[]{l}{\mathbf{F}}\text{ is measurable and}\\
\ \|{\mathbf{F}}\|_{\infty}=\sees_{{z}\in\mathbb{T}}\|{\mathbf{F}}({z})\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})}<\infty\end{array}\right\}

(a function
{\mathbf{F}}\colon\mathbb{T}\to\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
is measurable if
{\mathbf{F}}(\cdot)x\colon\mathbb{T}\to\mathcal{H}
is measurable for every
x\in\mathcal{H}
).
Each
{\mathbf{F}}\in L^{\infty}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}))
admits a formal Fourier expansion (a.e. on
\mathbb{T}
)

(2.3)

\mathbf{F}({z})=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}{F}_{n}{z}^{n}\quad\text{with }\{F_{n}\}\subset\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})

defined by

(2.4)

F_{n}x=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\overline{{z}}^{n}\mathbf{F}({z})x\,dm({z})\quad\text{for }x\in\mathcal{H}

(integrated in the strong sense). Let

H^{\infty}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}))=\left\{{\mathbf{F}}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})):{\mathbf{F}}({z})=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}F_{n}{z}^{n}\right\}.

The space
H^{\infty}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}))
can equivalently be defined as the space of all analytic functions
{\mathbf{F}}:\mathbb{D}\to\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
such that

\|{\mathbf{F}}\|_{\infty}=\sup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{D}}\|{\mathbf{F}}(\lambda)\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})}<\infty.

Each such bounded analytic
{\mathbf{F}}
is of the form

(2.5)

{\mathbf{F}}(\lambda)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{F}_{n}\lambda^{n},\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{D},

and can be identified with the boundary function

(2.6)

{\mathbf{F}}({z})=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{F}_{n}{z}^{n}\in L^{\infty}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})).

Conversely, each
{\mathbf{F}}\in L^{\infty}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}))
given by (
2.6
) can be extended by (
2.5
) to a function bounded and analytic in
\mathbb{D}
. In each case the coefficients
\{F_{n}\}
can be obtained by (
2.4
) and the norms
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
of the boundary function and its extension coincide (see
[
2
, p. 232]
).

Note that for each
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}
the function
{\mathbf{k}}_{\lambda}(z)=(1-\bar{\lambda}z)^{-1}I_{\mathcal{H}}
belongs to
H^{\infty}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}))
. Moreover, for every
x\in\mathcal{H}
the function
{\mathbf{k}}_{\lambda}x:z\mapsto{\mathbf{k}}_{\lambda}(z)x
belongs to
H^{\infty}(\mathcal{H})
and has the following reproducing property

\langle f,{\mathbf{k}}_{\lambda}x\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathcal{H})}=\langle f(\lambda),x\rangle_{\mathcal{H}},\quad f\in H^{2}(\mathcal{H}).

For each
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}
we can consider

{\mathbf{k}}_{\lambda}^{\Theta}(z)=\tfrac{1}{1-\bar{\lambda}z}(I_{\mathcal{H}}-\Theta(z)\Theta(\lambda)^{*})\in H^{\infty}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})).

For
x\in\mathcal{H}
we will denote the function
z\mapsto{\mathbf{k}}_{\lambda}^{\Theta}(z)x
simply by
{\mathbf{k}}_{\lambda}^{\Theta}x
. Then, for each
x\in\mathcal{H}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}
, the function
{\mathbf{k}}_{\lambda}^{\Theta}x=P_{\Theta}({\mathbf{k}}_{\lambda}x)
belongs to
K_{\Theta}^{\infty}=K_{\Theta}\cap H^{\infty}(\mathcal{H})
and has the following reproducing property

\langle f,{\mathbf{k}}_{\lambda}^{\Theta}x\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathcal{H})}=\langle f(\lambda),x\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\quad\text{for every }f\in K_{\Theta}.

It follows in particular that
K_{\Theta}^{\infty}
is a dense subset of
K_{\Theta}
.

To each
{\mathbf{F}}\in L^{\infty}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}))
there corresponds a multiplication operator
M_{\mathbf{F}}:L^{2}(\mathcal{H})\to L^{2}(\mathcal{H})
: for
f\in L^{2}(\mathcal{H})
,

(M_{\mathbf{F}}f)({z})={\mathbf{F}}({z})f({z})\quad\text{a.e. on }\mathbb{T}.

We will write
{\mathbf{F}}f
instead of
M_{\mathbf{F}}f
. For a constant
{\mathbf{F}}
, that is,
{\mathbf{F}}(z)=F
a.e. on
\mathbb{T}
for some
F\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
, we will also write
Ff
instead of
{\mathbf{F}}f
. By
T_{{\mathbf{F}}}
we will denote the compression of
M_{{\mathbf{F}}}
to the Hardy space:
T_{{\mathbf{F}}}:H^{2}(\mathcal{H})\to H^{2}(\mathcal{H})
,

T_{{\mathbf{F}}}f=P_{+}M_{{\mathbf{F}}}f\quad\text{for }f\in H^{2}(\mathcal{H}).

It is clear that
(M_{\mathbf{F}})^{*}=M_{{\mathbf{F}}^{*}}
and
(T_{\mathbf{F}})^{*}=T_{{\mathbf{F}}^{*}}
, where
{\mathbf{F}}^{*}({z})={\mathbf{F}}({z})^{*}
a.e. on
\mathbb{T}
. It is also not difficult to verify that for
{\mathbf{F}}\in L^{\infty}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}))
we have that
{\mathbf{F}}\in H^{\infty}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}))
if and only if
M_{{\mathbf{F}}}(H^{2}(\mathcal{H}))\subset H^{2}(\mathcal{H})
. In particular, for
M_{z}=M_{zI_{\mathcal{H}}}
we have
M_{z}^{*}=M_{\bar{z}}=M_{\bar{z}I_{\mathcal{H}}}
and
M_{z}(H^{2}(\mathcal{H}))\subset H^{2}(\mathcal{H})
. The operator
S=T_{z}=M_{z|H^{2}(\mathcal{H})}
is called the (forward) shift operator. Its adjoint, the backward shift operator
S^{*}=T_{\bar{z}}
, is given by the formula

S^{*}f(z)=\bar{z}\big(f(z)-f(0)\big).

We can consider
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
as a Hilbert space with the Hilbert–Schmidt norm and we may also define the spaces
L^{2}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}))
and
H^{2}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}))
as above. Recall that the norm and the corresponding inner product are defined as follows: for Hilbert-Schmidt operators
A,B\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
we have

\|A\|^{2}_{2}=tr(A^{*}A)=\sum_{e\in\varepsilon}\langle Ae,Ae\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}

and

\langle A,B\rangle=tr(B^{*}A)=\sum_{e\in\varepsilon}\langle Ae,Be\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}

\varepsilon
being any orthonormal basis for
\mathcal{H}
(see
[
15
, Chapter 3]
). Hence for
{\mathbf{F}},{\mathbf{G}}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}))

\displaystyle\langle{\mathbf{F}},{\mathbf{G}}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}))}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\langle{\mathbf{F}}(z),{\mathbf{G}}(z)\rangle_{2}dm(z)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\sum_{e\in\varepsilon}\langle{\mathbf{F}}(z)e,{\mathbf{G}}(z)e\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}dm(z).

Since
\langle A,B\rangle_{2}=\langle B^{*},A^{*}\rangle_{2}
, it follows that
\langle{\mathbf{F}},{\mathbf{G}}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}))}=\langle{\mathbf{G}}^{*},{\mathbf{F}}^{*}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}))}
.

Since here the Hilbert–Schmidt norm and the operator norm are equivalent, we have

L^{\infty}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}))\subset L^{2}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})),\quad H^{\infty}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}))\subset H^{2}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})).

Moreover, it is not difficult to verify that if
{\mathbf{F}}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}))
is given by

{\mathbf{F}}(z)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}F_{n}z^{n},\quad F_{n}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}),

where the series is convergent in the
L^{2}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}))
-norm, then

{\mathbf{F}}^{*}(z)={\mathbf{F}}(z)^{*}=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}(F_{-n})^{*}z^{n}.

We thus have

L^{2}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}))=\left[zH^{2}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}))\right]^{*}\oplus H^{2}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})).

For
{\mathbf{F}}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}))
the operators
M_{{\mathbf{F}}}
and
T_{{\mathbf{F}}}
can be densely defined on
L^{2}(\mathcal{H})
and
H^{2}(\mathcal{H})
, respectively.
For more details on spaces of vector-valued functions, we refer the reader to
[
2
,
28
,
29
]
.

Let us now consider conjugations. A conjugation
\Gamma
in a Hilbert space

\mathcal{H}
is an antilinear map
\Gamma:\mathcal{H}\longrightarrow\mathcal{H}
such that
\Gamma^{2}=I_{\mathcal{H}}
and

\langle\Gamma f,\Gamma g\rangle=\langle g,f\rangle\quad\text{for all}\quad f,g\in\mathcal{H}.

The importance of
conjugations comes, for example, from their connection with complex
symmetric operators. Recall that a bounded linear operator
T:\mathcal{H}\longrightarrow\mathcal{H}
is said to be
\Gamma
-symmetric (
\Gamma
being a conjugation on
\mathcal{H}
) if
\Gamma T\Gamma=T^{*}
. We say that
T
is complex symmetric if it is
\Gamma
-symmetric with respect
to some conjugation
\Gamma
(see, e.g.,
[
16
]
for more details on conjugations
and complex symmetric operators).

In
[
5
]
the authors consider certain classes of conjugations in
L^{2}(\mathcal{H})
. One such conjugation is
\widetilde{{\mathbf{J}}}:L^{2}(\mathcal{H})\to L^{2}(\mathcal{H})
defined for a fixed conjugation
\Gamma
in
\mathcal{H}
by

(2.7)

(\widetilde{{\mathbf{J}}}f)(z)=\Gamma(f(z))\quad\text{a.e. on}~~\mathbb{T}.

The other one
is
{\mathbf{J}}^{*}:L^{2}(\mathcal{H})\to L^{2}(\mathcal{H})
defined by

(2.8)

({\mathbf{J}}^{*}f)(z)=\Gamma(f(\overline{z}))\quad\text{a.e. on}~~\mathbb{T}.

It is not difficult to verify that for
f(z)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}a_{n}z^{n}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{H})
we have

({\mathbf{J}}^{*}f)(z)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\Gamma(a_{n})z^{n}.

Hence,
{\mathbf{J}}^{*}
is an
{\mathbf{M}}_{z}
-commuting conjugation, i.e,
{\mathbf{J}}^{*}{\mathbf{M}}_{z}={\mathbf{M}}_{z}{\mathbf{J}}^{*}
, and
{\mathbf{J}}^{*}(H^{2}(\mathcal{H}))=H^{2}(\mathcal{H})
,
{\mathbf{J}}^{*}P_{+}=P_{+}{\mathbf{J}}^{*}
(see
[
5
, Section 4]
). The conjugations
\widetilde{{\mathbf{J}}}
and
{\mathbf{J}}^{*}
was considered in
[
4
]
for
\text{dim}~\mathcal{H}=1
.

For
{\mathbf{F}}\in L^{\infty}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}))
and an arbitrary conjugation
\Gamma
in
\mathcal{H}
let

(2.9)

{\mathbf{F}}_{\Gamma}(z)=\Gamma{\mathbf{F}}(z)\Gamma\quad\text{a.e on}~~~\mathbb{T}.

Then
{\mathbf{F}}_{\Gamma}\in L^{\infty}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}))
. As observed in
[
5
]
,
{\mathbf{F}}_{\Gamma}\in H^{\infty}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}))
if and only if
{\mathbf{F}}^{*}\in H^{\infty}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}))
, and
{\mathbf{F}}_{\Gamma}
is an inner function if and only if
{\mathbf{F}}
is. Clearly,
({\mathbf{F}}_{\Gamma})_{\Gamma}={\mathbf{F}}.
Let us also observe that if
{\mathbf{F}}
is
\Gamma
-symmetric, that is,
\Gamma{\mathbf{F}}(z)\Gamma={\mathbf{F}}(z)^{*}
a.e on
\mathbb{T}
(or equivalently
{\mathbf{F}}(\lambda)
is
\Gamma
-symmetric for
\lambda
in
\mathbb{D}
, see
[
5
]
), then
{\mathbf{F}}_{\Gamma}=\widetilde{{\mathbf{F}}}
, where
\widetilde{{\mathbf{F}}}(z)={\mathbf{F}}(\bar{z})^{*}
.
In particular

(2.10)

{\mathbf{J}}^{*}{\mathbf{M}}_{\mathbf{F}}={\mathbf{M}}_{\mathbf{F}}{\mathbf{J}}^{*}

Note that
{\mathbf{F}}_{\Gamma}
is also defined for
{\mathbf{F}}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}))
.

3.
Model spaces and their decompositions

In this section, we discuss the decomposition of model spaces corresponding to the product of inner functions.
Let
\Theta,\Lambda\in H^{\infty}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}))
be two inner functions. Then we say that
\Lambda
divides
\Theta
if there exists an inner function
\Psi\in H^{\infty}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}))
such that
\Theta=\Lambda\Psi
. Th scalar case was considered in
[
6
]
.

Recall the definition of
\Gamma
-symmetry. We say that
\Phi\in L^{2}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}))
is
\Gamma
-symmetric if
\Gamma\Phi(z)\Gamma=\Phi(z)^{*}
a.e. on
\mathbb{T}
.

If
\Lambda
divides
\Theta
, then we have
K_{\Lambda}\subset K_{\Theta}
. Let
\Theta\Lambda^{*}=\Phi
and
\Lambda^{*}\Theta=\Psi
, then the following result shows that if
\Theta,\Lambda
, and
\Psi
are
\Gamma
-symmetric then
\Psi
and
\Lambda
commutes, and hence
\Phi=\Psi
. Moreover, the two statements in
[
5
, Lemma 7.2]
can be reduced to only one.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\Theta,\Lambda
, and
\Psi
be
\Gamma
-symmetric inner functions such that
\Theta=\Lambda\Psi
. Then,
\Lambda
and
\Psi
commute.

Proof.

Let
\Theta,\Lambda
, and
\Psi
be
\Gamma
-symmetric inner functions such that
\Theta=\Lambda\Psi
.
Now,
\Theta
is
\Gamma
-symmetric inner function, we have

\Theta^{*}=\Gamma\Theta\Gamma=\Gamma(\Lambda\Psi)\Gamma=\Gamma\Lambda\Gamma.\Gamma\Psi\Gamma=\Lambda^{*}\Psi^{*}=(\Psi\Lambda)^{*}.

It follows that
\Theta=\Psi\Lambda
. We also have
\Theta=\Lambda\Psi=\Psi\Lambda
.
∎

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
\Theta,\Lambda
, and
\Psi
be inner functions such that
\Theta=\Lambda\Psi
. Then, for any
x\in\mathcal{H}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}
, the following hold:

(1)

K_{\Theta}=K_{\Lambda}\oplus\Lambda K_{\Psi}.

(2)

P_{\Theta}=P_{\Lambda}+\Lambda P_{\Psi}\Lambda^{*}.

(3)

k_{\lambda}^{\Theta}(z)x=k_{\lambda}^{\Lambda}(z)x+\Lambda(z)k_{\lambda}^{\Psi}(z)\Lambda(\lambda)^{*}x.

(4)

\widetilde{k}_{\lambda}^{\Theta}(z)x=\widetilde{k}_{\lambda}^{\Lambda}(z)\Psi(\lambda)x+\Lambda(z)\widetilde{k}_{\lambda}^{\Psi}(z)x.

(5)

P_{\Lambda}(k_{\lambda}^{\Theta}(z)x)=k_{\lambda}^{\Lambda}(z)x,\quad P_{\Lambda}(\widetilde{k}_{\lambda}^{\Theta}(z)x)=\widetilde{k}_{\lambda}^{\Lambda}(z)\Psi(\lambda)x.

Proof.

The proof of (1) and (2) are easy.

(3) Consider the reproducing kernel for
K_{\Theta}
, given by

\displaystyle k_{\lambda}^{\Theta}x

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-\overline{\lambda}z}(I-\Theta(z)\Theta(\lambda)^{*})x

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-\overline{\lambda}z}(I-\Lambda(z)\Psi(z)\Psi(\lambda)^{*}\Lambda(\lambda)^{*})x

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-\overline{\lambda}z}(I-\Lambda(z)\Lambda(\lambda)^{*}+\Lambda(z)\Lambda(\lambda)^{*}-\Lambda(z)\Psi(z)\Psi(\lambda)^{*}\Lambda(\lambda)^{*})x

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-\overline{\lambda}z}[(I-\Lambda(z)\Lambda(\lambda)^{*})+\Lambda(z)(I-\Psi(z)\Psi(\lambda)^{*})\Lambda(\lambda)^{*})]x

\displaystyle=k_{\lambda}^{\Lambda}x+\Lambda(z)k_{\lambda}^{\Psi}\Lambda(\lambda)^{*}x.

(4) It follows by similar arguments as in (3).

Item (5) follows from the orthogonal projection of the vectors in (3) and (4) on the model space
K_{\Lambda}
.
∎

4.
Conjugations on model spaces

In the scalar case, each model space
K_{\theta}
is equipped with a natural conjugation
C_{\theta}
, defined in terms of boundary functions by

(C_{\theta}f)(z)=\theta(z)\overline{z}\overline{f(z)}
. Conjugation
C_{\theta}
was considered in
[
30
]
. If
\Theta\in H^{\infty}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}))
is an inner function and
\Gamma
is a conjugation in
\mathcal{H}
, we can similarly define
{\mathbf{C}}_{\Theta}:L^{2}(\mathcal{H})\to L^{2}(\mathcal{H})
by

(4.1)

({\mathbf{C}}_{\Theta}f)(z)=\Theta(z)\overline{z}\Gamma(f(z))\quad\text{a.e. on }~~\mathbb{T}.

Although
{\mathbf{C}}_{\Theta}
is obviously an antilinear isometry, it is in general not an involution. A simple computation shows that
{\mathbf{C}}_{\Theta}
is an involution (and therefore a conjugation) if and only if
\Theta(z)\Gamma\Theta(z)\Gamma=I_{\mathcal{H}}
a.e. on
\mathbb{T}
, i.e., if and only if
\Theta
is
\Gamma
-symmetric. Conjugation like (
4.1
) was previously considered in
[
5
]
.

If
\Theta
is
\Gamma
-symmetric, then
{\mathbf{C}}_{\Theta}(\Theta H^{2}(\mathcal{H}))=H^{2}(\mathcal{H})^{\perp}
and so

{\mathbf{C}}_{\Theta}(K_{\Theta})=K_{\Theta}.

Recall that in the scalar case
\mathcal{H}=\mathbb{C}
every TTO on the model space
K_{\theta}
is
C_{\theta}
-symmetric, i.e.,

C_{\theta}A_{\varphi}^{\theta}C_{\theta}=(A_{\varphi}^{\theta})^{*}=A_{\overline{\varphi}}^{\theta}

(see, e.g.,
[
30
]
). In that case however, the only conjugation in
\mathcal{H}
we need to consider is
\Gamma(z)=\overline{z}
(and each
\varphi\in L^{2}
is
\Gamma
-symmetric). In the vector valued case, the equality

(4.2)

C_{\Theta}A_{\Phi}^{\Theta}C_{\Theta}=A_{\Phi^{*}}^{\Theta}

is not necessarily true for an arbitrary
\Phi\in L^{2}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}))
(even though we assume here that
\Theta
is
\Gamma
-symmetric). However, it is satisfied if also
\Phi
is
\Gamma
-symmetric and
commutes
with
\Theta
(see
[
25
]
).

Note that in that case,

{\mathbf{C}}_{\Theta}={\mathbf{J}}^{*}\tau_{\Theta},

where
\tau_{\Theta}:L^{2}(\mathcal{H})\to L^{2}(\mathcal{H})
given by

(4.3)

(\tau_{\Theta}f)(z)=\bar{z}\Theta(\bar{z})^{*}f(\bar{z})\quad a.e.~~\text{on}~~\mathbb{T},

is a unitary operator which maps
K_{\Theta}
onto
K_{\widetilde{\Theta}}
, where
\widetilde{\Theta}(z)=\Theta(\bar{z})^{*}
.

In this section, we study a new conjugation on model spaces. We define a conjugation that corresponds to the orthogonal decomposition of model spaces, corresponding to the inner function
\Theta=\Lambda\Psi=\Psi\Lambda
, where
\Theta,\Lambda
, and
\Psi
are
\Gamma
-symmetric inner functions. Conjugation in Theorem
4.1
was considered in
[
6
]
in case
\text{dim}~\mathcal{H}=1
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\Theta,\Lambda
, and
\Psi
be
\Gamma
-symmetric inner functions such that
\Theta=\Lambda\Psi
. Then, the operator

C_{\Lambda,\Psi}:K_{\Theta}=K_{\Lambda}\oplus\Lambda K_{\Psi}\to K_{\Theta}=K_{\Lambda}\oplus\Lambda K_{\Psi}

defined by

C_{\Lambda,\Psi}=C_{\Lambda}\oplus\Lambda C_{\Psi}\Lambda^{*}=\begin{bmatrix}C_{\Lambda}&0\\
0&\Lambda C_{\Psi}\Lambda^{*}\end{bmatrix}

is a conjugation on
K_{\Theta}
.

Proof.

To show that
C_{\Lambda,\Psi}
is a conjugation on
K_{\Theta}
, we have to show that
\Lambda C_{\Psi}\Lambda^{*}
is a conjugation on
\Lambda K_{\Psi}
. Since
\Lambda
and
\Psi
are
\Gamma
-symmetric, we have
C_{\Lambda}
and
C_{\Psi}
as conjugations on
K_{\Lambda}
and
K_{\Psi}
, respectively. Let
\mathcal{C}=\Lambda C_{\Psi}\Lambda^{*}
. First we have to show that
\mathcal{C}(\Lambda K_{\Psi})=\Lambda K_{\Psi}
.
Let
f\in\Lambda K_{\Psi}
, then
f=\Lambda g
for some
g\in K_{\Psi}
. Then

\mathcal{C}(f)=\Lambda C_{\Psi}\Lambda^{*}(\Lambda g)=\Lambda C_{\Psi}g\in\Lambda K_{\Psi}.

It follows that
\mathcal{C}(\Lambda K_{\Psi})\subset\Lambda K_{\Psi}
.
The operator
\mathcal{C}
is involution because

\mathcal{C}^{2}=\Lambda C_{\Psi}\Lambda^{*}\Lambda C_{\Psi}\Lambda^{*}=\Lambda C_{\Psi}^{2}\Lambda^{*}=I_{\Lambda K_{\Psi}}.

Hence, we have

\mathcal{C}(\Lambda K_{\Psi})=\Lambda K_{\Psi}.

The antilinearity of
\mathcal{C}
follows from the antilinearity of
C_{\Psi}
.

Now, we prove that
\mathcal{C}
is isometric, i.e.,
\|\mathcal{C}f\|=\|f\|
for all
f=\Lambda g\in\Lambda K_{\Psi}
, where
g\in K_{\Psi}
. Consider

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{C}f\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\langle\mathcal{C}f,\mathcal{C}f\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\Lambda C_{\Psi}\Lambda^{*}\Lambda g,\Lambda C_{\Psi}\Lambda^{*}\Lambda g\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle C_{\Psi}g,C_{\Psi}g\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle g,g\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\Lambda g,\Lambda g\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle f,f\rangle

\displaystyle=\|f\|^{2}.

Hence,
\mathcal{C}
is a conjugation, which follows that
C_{\Lambda,\Psi}
is a conjugation.
∎

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
\Lambda,\Theta
, and
\Psi
be
\Gamma
-symmetric inner functions such that
\Theta=\Lambda\Psi
. Then, for
any
f_{\Lambda}\in K_{\Lambda}
and
f_{\Psi}\in K_{\Psi}
, the conjugation

C_{\Theta}:K_{\Theta}=K_{\Lambda}\oplus\Lambda K_{\Psi}\to K_{\Theta}=K_{\Psi}\oplus\Psi K_{\Lambda}
has the following properties:

(1)

C_{\Theta}(f_{\Lambda}+\Lambda f_{\Psi})=C_{\Psi}(f_{\Psi})+\Psi C_{\Lambda}(f_{\Lambda}).

(2)

C_{\Theta}(f_{\Psi}+\Psi f_{\Lambda})=C_{\Lambda}(f_{\Lambda})+\Lambda C_{\Psi}(f_{\Psi}).

Proof.

(1) Let
f_{\Lambda}\in K_{\Lambda}
and
f_{\Psi}\in K_{\Psi}
. Then,

\displaystyle C_{\Theta}(f_{\Lambda}+\Lambda f_{\Psi})

\displaystyle=\Theta\overline{z}\Gamma(f_{\Lambda}+\Lambda f_{\Psi})

\displaystyle=\Theta\overline{z}\Gamma(f_{\Lambda})+\Theta\overline{z}\Gamma(\Lambda f_{\Psi})

\displaystyle=\Theta\Lambda^{*}\Lambda\overline{z}\Gamma(f_{\Lambda})+\Theta\overline{z}\Lambda^{*}\Gamma(f_{\Psi})

\displaystyle=\Psi C_{\Lambda}(f_{\Lambda})+\Theta\Lambda^{*}\overline{z}\Gamma(f_{\Psi})

\displaystyle=\Psi C_{\Lambda}(f_{\Lambda})+\Psi\overline{z}\Gamma(f_{\Psi})

\displaystyle=\Psi C_{\Lambda}(f_{\Lambda})+C_{\Psi}(f_{\Psi}).

(2) This part is a symmetric version of (1).
∎

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
\Lambda,\Theta
, and
\Psi
be
\Gamma
-symmetric inner functions such that
\Theta=\Lambda\Psi
. Then, the operators
C_{\Lambda,\Psi}C_{\Theta}:K_{\Theta}=K_{\Psi}\oplus\Psi K_{\Lambda}\to K_{\Theta}=K_{\Lambda}\oplus\Lambda K_{\Psi}
and

C_{\Theta}C_{\Lambda,\Psi}:K_{\Theta}=K_{\Lambda}\oplus\Lambda K_{\Psi}\to K_{\Theta}=K_{\Psi}\oplus\Psi K_{\Lambda}
are unitary, and have the following properties:

(1)

C_{\Lambda,\Psi}C_{\Theta}=P_{\Lambda}\Psi^{*}+\Lambda P_{\Psi},

(2)

C_{\Theta}C_{\Lambda,\Psi}=P_{\Psi}\Lambda^{*}+\Psi P_{\Lambda}.

Proof.

It is easy to prove that
C_{\Lambda,\Psi}C_{\Theta}
and
C_{\Lambda,\Psi}C_{\Theta}
are unitary operators.

To prove (1), let
f_{\Lambda}\in K_{\Lambda}
and
f_{\Psi}\in K_{\Psi}
. Then, by Theorem
4.2
, we have

\displaystyle C_{\Lambda,\Psi}C_{\Theta}(f_{\Psi}+\Psi f_{\Lambda})

\displaystyle=C_{\Lambda,\Psi}(C_{\Lambda}(f_{\Lambda})+\Lambda C_{\Psi}(f_{\Psi}))

\displaystyle=C^{2}_{\Lambda}(f_{\Lambda})+\Lambda C^{2}_{\Psi}(f_{\Psi}))

\displaystyle=f_{\Lambda}+\Lambda f_{\Psi},

and

\displaystyle(P_{\Lambda}\Psi^{*}+\Lambda P_{\Psi})(f_{\Psi}+\Psi f_{\Lambda})

\displaystyle=P_{\Lambda}(\Psi^{*}f_{\Psi})+P_{\Lambda}(f_{\Lambda}))+\Lambda P_{\Psi}(f_{\Psi})+\Lambda P_{\Psi}(\Psi f_{\Lambda})

\displaystyle=P_{\Lambda}(f_{\Lambda})+\Lambda P_{\Psi}(f_{\Psi})

\displaystyle=f_{\Lambda}+\Lambda f_{\Psi}.

It follows (1).

(2) It follows from analogous discussion as in (1).

∎

Theorem 4.4
.

[
5
, Theorem 4.8]

Let
C
be an antilinear operator on
L^{2}(\mathcal{H})
. Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

C
is conjugation on
L^{2}({\mathcal{H}})
such that
M_{z}C=CM_{\bar{z}}
.

(2)

There is an antilinear function
C_{0}\in L^{\infty}({\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})})
such that
C=A_{C_{0}}
and
C_{0}(z)
is a conjugation for a.e. on
\mathbb{T}
.

(3)

For any conjugation
\Gamma
in
\mathcal{H}
, there is
U\in L^{\infty}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}))
such that
U(z)
is a
\Gamma
-symmetric unitary operator, and
C=M_{U}\widetilde{{\mathbf{J}}}=\widetilde{{\mathbf{J}}}M_{U^{*}}
.

We know that
{\mathbf{C}}_{\Theta}={\mathbf{J}}^{*}\tau_{\Theta}
, where
{\mathbf{J}}^{*}
is an
M_{z}
-commuting conjugation. Hence,
{\mathbf{C}}_{\Theta}
is an
M_{z}
-conjugation, i.e.,
{\mathbf{C}}_{\Theta}M_{z}=M_{\bar{z}}{\mathbf{C}}_{\Theta}
. It follows from Theorem
4.4
that there is
U\in L^{\infty}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}))
such that
U(z)
is a
\Gamma
-symmetric unitary operator, and
{\mathbf{C}}_{\Theta}=M_{U}\widetilde{{\mathbf{J}}}=\widetilde{{\mathbf{J}}}M_{U^{*}}
.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
\Lambda,\Theta
, and
\Psi
be
\Gamma
-symmetric inner functions such that
\Theta=\Lambda\Psi
. Then, there are
\Gamma
-symmetric unitary functions
U,V\in L^{\infty}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}))
such that

{\mathbf{C}}_{\Lambda,\Psi}=M_{U}\widetilde{{\mathbf{J}}}\oplus M_{V}\widetilde{{\mathbf{J}}}=\widetilde{{\mathbf{J}}}M_{U^{*}}\oplus\widetilde{{\mathbf{J}}}M_{V^{*}}

.

Proof.

Denote
{\mathbf{M}}_{z}=\begin{bmatrix}M_{z}&0\\
0&M_{z}\end{bmatrix}
.
Let
\Lambda,\Theta
, and
\Psi
be
\Gamma
-symmetric inner functions such that
\Theta=\Lambda\Psi
. Then,

{\mathbf{C}}_{\Theta}
and
{\mathbf{C}}_{\Psi}
are
M_{z}
- conjugations. It implies that the conjugation

{\mathbf{C}}_{\Lambda,\Psi}=C_{\Lambda}\oplus\Lambda C_{\Psi}\Lambda^{*}=\begin{bmatrix}{\mathbf{C}}_{\Lambda}&0\\
0&\Lambda{\mathbf{C}}_{\Psi}\Lambda^{*}\end{bmatrix}

is also an
{\mathbf{M}}_{z}
-conjugation. By Theorem
4.4
, there are
\Gamma
-symmetric unitary functions
U,U^{\prime}\in L^{\infty}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}))
such that
{\mathbf{C}}_{\Theta}=M_{U}\widetilde{{\mathbf{J}}}=\widetilde{{\mathbf{J}}}M_{U^{*}}
, and
{\mathbf{C}}_{\Psi}=M_{U^{\prime}}\widetilde{{\mathbf{J}}}=\widetilde{{\mathbf{J}}}M_{U^{\prime*}}
.

It follows that

\displaystyle{\mathbf{C}}_{\Lambda,\Psi}

\displaystyle=C_{\Lambda}\oplus\Lambda C_{\Psi}\Lambda^{*}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}{\mathbf{C}}_{\Lambda}&0\\
0&\Lambda{\mathbf{C}}_{\Psi}\Lambda^{*}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}M_{U}\widetilde{{\mathbf{J}}}&0\\
0&\Lambda M_{U^{\prime}}\widetilde{{\mathbf{J}}}\Lambda^{*}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}M_{U}\widetilde{{\mathbf{J}}}&0\\
0&\Lambda M_{U^{\prime}}\Lambda\widetilde{{\mathbf{J}}}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}M_{U}\widetilde{{\mathbf{J}}}&0\\
0&M_{V}\widetilde{{\mathbf{J}}}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}M_{U}&0\\
0&M_{V}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{{\mathbf{J}}}&0\\
0&\widetilde{{\mathbf{J}}}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=M_{U}\widetilde{{\mathbf{J}}}\oplus M_{V}\widetilde{{\mathbf{J}}}

where
V=\Lambda M_{U^{\prime}}\Lambda
is unitary function in
L^{\infty}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}))
. It is easy to verify that
V
is also
\Gamma
-symmetric because
U^{\prime}
and
\Lambda
are
\Gamma
-symmetric. The same steps follow for the proof of other representation, i.e.,
{\mathbf{C}}_{\Lambda,\Psi}=\widetilde{{\mathbf{J}}}M_{U^{*}}\oplus\widetilde{{\mathbf{J}}}M_{V^{*}}
.
∎

5.
Connection with matrix valued Hankel operators

In this section, we find the connection between the conjugation, matrix valued asymmetric truncated Toeplitz operators, and matrix valued Hankel operators.

Let
P_{-}=I-P_{+}
denote the orthogonal projection from
L^{2}(\mathcal{H})
on
H_{-}^{2}(\mathcal{H})=[H^{2}(\mathcal{H})]^{\perp}
. For
\Phi\in L^{2}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}))
, we define

H_{\Phi}:H^{2}(\mathcal{H})\to H^{2}_{-}(\mathcal{H}),\quad\quad H_{\Phi}f=P_{-}(\Phi f);

for
f\in H^{2}(\mathcal{H})
.

Similarly, for
\Psi\in L^{\infty}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}))
,

\widetilde{H}_{\Psi}:H^{2}_{-}(\mathcal{H})\to H^{2}(\mathcal{H}),\quad\quad\widetilde{H}_{\Psi}f=P_{+}(\Psi f).

Remark 5.1
.

Let
f\in K_{\Theta}
and
\Phi\in L^{\infty}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}))
. Then

\displaystyle\widetilde{H}_{\Theta}H_{\Theta^{*}\Phi}f

\displaystyle=\widetilde{H}_{\Theta}P_{-}(\Theta^{*}\Phi f)

\displaystyle=P_{+}(\Theta P_{-}(\Theta^{*}\Phi f))

\displaystyle=P_{+}(\Theta(I-P_{+})(\Theta^{*}\Phi f))

\displaystyle=(P_{+}-\Theta P_{+}\Theta^{*})(\Phi f)

\displaystyle=P_{\Theta}(\Phi f)

\displaystyle=A_{\Phi}^{\Theta}f.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
\Lambda,\Theta
, and
\Psi
be
\Gamma
-symmetric inner functions such that
\Theta=\Lambda\Psi
. Then

(1)

P_{\Theta}=\Theta P_{-}\Theta^{*}-P_{-},

(2)

P_{\Theta}=\Theta P_{-}\Theta^{*}P_{+},

(3)

P_{\Theta}=P_{+}\Lambda P_{-}\Lambda^{*}+\Lambda P_{\Psi}\Lambda^{*}=P_{+}\Theta P_{-}\Theta^{*},

Proof.

By using the identities
P_{\Theta}=P_{+}-\Theta P_{+}\Theta^{*}
and
P_{-}=I-P_{+}
, the required proof follows.
∎

Theorem 5.3
.

[
23
, Theorem 4.5]

Let
\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2}\in H^{\infty}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}))
be two inner functions, and let
\Gamma
be a conjugations in
\mathcal{H}
such that
\Theta_{1}
and
\Theta
are
\Gamma
-symmetric. A bounded linear operator
A:K_{\Theta_{1}}\to K_{\Theta_{2}}
belongs to
\mathcal{MT}(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})
if and only if
C_{\Theta_{2}}AC_{\Theta_{1}}
belongs to
\mathcal{MT}(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})
. More precisely,
A=A_{\Phi}^{\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2}}\in\mathcal{MT}(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})
if and only if
C_{\Theta_{2}}AC_{\Theta_{1}}=A_{\Psi}^{\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2}}\in\mathcal{MT}(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})
with

(5.1)

\Psi(z)=\Gamma\Theta_{2}(z)^{*}\Phi(z)\Theta_{1}(z)\Gamma=\Theta_{2}(z)\Gamma\Phi(z)\Gamma\Theta_{1}(z)^{*}\quad\text{a.e. on }\mathbb{T}.

For the conjugation
C_{\Lambda,\Psi}
, we no longer have the equality in general, i.e.,
A_{\Phi}^{\Theta,\Lambda}C_{\Lambda,\Theta}\neq C_{\Lambda,\Theta}A_{\Phi^{*}}^{\Lambda,\Theta}
. In the following theorem, we find the difference of these two operators.

Theorem 5.4
.

Let
\Lambda,\Theta
, and
\Psi
be
\Gamma
-symmetric inner functions such that
\Theta=\Psi\Lambda
. Let
A_{\Phi}^{\Theta,\Lambda}\in\mathcal{T}(\Theta,\Lambda)
and
f\in K_{\Theta}
. Then we have

(A_{\Phi}^{\Theta,\Lambda}C_{\Lambda,\Theta}-C_{\Lambda,\Theta}A_{\Phi^{*}}^{\Lambda,\Theta})f=(\widetilde{H}_{\Lambda}H_{\Phi}C_{\Theta}-\widetilde{H}_{\Theta}H_{\Phi}C_{\Lambda}P_{\Lambda})f.

Proof.

First we have

\displaystyle A_{\Phi}^{\Lambda}P_{\Lambda}+P_{\Lambda}(\Phi\Lambda P_{\Psi}(\Lambda^{*}I_{K_{\Theta}}))

\displaystyle=A_{\Phi}^{\Lambda}P_{\Lambda}+P_{\Lambda}(\Phi\Lambda P_{\Psi}(\Lambda^{*}I_{K_{\Theta}}))

\displaystyle=A_{\Phi}^{\Lambda}P_{\Lambda}+P_{\Lambda}(\Phi(P_{\Theta}-P_{\Lambda}))

\displaystyle=A_{\Phi}^{\Theta,\Lambda}.

It follows that

A_{\Phi}^{\Lambda}C_{\Lambda,\Psi}{\mid_{K_{\Lambda}}}=A_{\Phi}^{\Lambda}C_{\Lambda}=C_{\Lambda}A_{\Phi^{*}}^{\Lambda}=C_{\Lambda,\Psi}A_{\Phi^{*}}^{\Lambda}.

Now, we have

(5.2)

\displaystyle A_{\Phi}^{\Theta,\Lambda}C_{\Lambda,\Psi}f

\displaystyle=A_{\Phi}^{\Lambda}C_{\Lambda,\Psi}f+P_{\Lambda}(\Phi\Lambda P_{\Psi}(\Lambda^{*}C_{\Lambda,\Psi}f),

and

\displaystyle P_{\Lambda}(\Phi\Lambda P_{\Psi}(\Lambda^{*}C_{\Lambda,\Psi}f)

\displaystyle=P_{\Lambda}(\Phi\Lambda P_{\Psi}(\Lambda^{*}C_{\Lambda,\Psi}(f_{\Psi}+\Psi f_{\Lambda}))

\displaystyle=P_{\Lambda}(\Phi\Lambda P_{\Psi}\Lambda^{*}(C_{\Lambda}f_{\Lambda}+\Lambda C_{\Psi}f_{\Psi}))

\displaystyle=P_{\Lambda}(\Phi\Lambda P_{\Psi}\Lambda^{*}(\Lambda\bar{z}\Gamma(f_{\Lambda})+\Lambda C_{\Psi}f_{\Psi}))

\displaystyle=P_{\Lambda}(\Phi\Lambda P_{\Psi}(\Lambda^{*}\Lambda\bar{z}\Gamma(f_{\Lambda})+\Lambda^{*}\Lambda C_{\Psi}f_{\Psi}))

\displaystyle=P_{\Lambda}(\Phi\Lambda P_{\Psi}(\bar{z}\Gamma(f_{\Lambda})+C_{\Psi}f_{\Psi}))

\displaystyle=P_{\Lambda}(\Phi\Lambda P_{\Psi}(\bar{z}\Gamma(f_{\Lambda}))+\Phi\Lambda P_{\Psi}(C_{\Psi}f_{\Psi}))

\displaystyle=P_{\Lambda}(\Phi\Lambda P_{\Psi}(C_{\Psi}f_{\Psi}))=P_{+}\Lambda P_{-}\Lambda^{*}(\Phi\Lambda(C_{\Psi}f_{\Psi}))

\displaystyle=P_{+}\Lambda P_{-}(\Lambda^{*}\Lambda\Phi(C_{\Psi}f_{\Psi}))=P_{+}\Lambda P_{-}(\Phi(C_{\Psi}f_{\Psi})).

Therefore, (
5.2
) becomes

(5.3)

A_{\Phi}^{\Theta,\Lambda}C_{\Lambda,\Psi}f=C_{\Lambda,\Psi}A_{\Phi^{*}}^{\Lambda}f_{\Lambda}+P_{+}\Lambda P_{-}(\Phi(C_{\Psi}f_{\Psi})).

Similarly, we have

A_{\Phi}^{\Lambda,\Theta}=A_{\Phi^{*}}^{\Lambda}+\Lambda P_{\Psi}\Lambda^{*}\Phi^{*}P_{\Lambda}.

Therefore,

(5.4)

C_{\Lambda,\Psi}A_{\Phi^{*}}^{\Lambda,\Theta}=C_{\Lambda,\Psi}A_{\Phi^{*}}^{\Lambda}+C_{\Lambda,\Psi}\Lambda P_{\Psi}\Lambda^{*}\Phi^{*}P_{\Lambda}.

We also have

P_{+}\Lambda P_{-}\Lambda^{*}+\Lambda P_{\Psi}\Lambda^{*}=P_{\Theta}=P_{+}\Theta P_{-}\Theta^{*}.

By taking the difference of (
5.3
) and (
5.4
), we have

\displaystyle(A_{\Phi}^{\Theta,\Lambda}C_{\Lambda,\Psi}-C_{\Lambda,\Psi}A_{\Phi^{*}}^{\Lambda,\Theta})f

\displaystyle=P_{+}\Lambda P_{-}(\Phi(C_{\Psi}f_{\Psi}))-C_{\Lambda,\Psi}\Lambda P_{\Psi}(\Lambda^{*}\Phi^{*}P_{\Lambda}f)

\displaystyle=P_{+}\Lambda P_{-}(\Phi(C_{\Psi}f_{\Psi}))+P_{+}\Lambda P_{-}(\Phi\Psi(C_{\Lambda}f_{\Lambda}))

\displaystyle-P_{+}\Lambda P_{-}(\Phi\Psi(C_{\Lambda}f_{\Lambda}))-\Lambda C_{\Psi}P_{\Psi}(\Lambda^{*}\Phi^{*}f_{\Lambda})

\displaystyle=P_{+}\Lambda P_{-}(\Phi(C_{\Psi}f_{\Psi}+\Psi C_{\Lambda}f_{\Lambda}))

\displaystyle-P_{+}\Lambda P_{-}(\Phi\Psi(C_{\Lambda}f_{\Lambda}))-\Lambda C_{\Psi}P_{\Psi}(\Lambda^{*}\Phi^{*}f_{\Lambda})

\displaystyle=P_{+}\Lambda P_{-}(\Phi(C_{\Psi}f_{\Psi}+\Psi C_{\Lambda}f_{\Lambda}))

\displaystyle-P_{+}\Lambda P_{-}(\Psi\Phi(C_{\Lambda}f_{\Lambda}))-\Lambda C_{\Psi}A_{\Lambda^{*}\Phi^{*}}^{\Lambda,\Psi}f_{\Lambda}

\displaystyle=P_{+}\Lambda P_{-}(\Phi C_{\Theta}f)-P_{+}\Lambda P_{-}\Lambda^{*}(\Theta\Phi(C_{\Lambda}f_{\Lambda}))

\displaystyle-\Lambda C_{\Psi}A_{\Lambda^{*}\Phi^{*}}^{\Lambda,\Psi}f_{\Lambda}.

By using Theorem
5.3
, we have
C_{\Psi}A_{\Lambda^{*}\Phi^{*}}^{\Lambda,\Psi}=A_{\Omega}^{\Lambda,\Psi}C_{\Lambda}
, where

\Omega=\Psi\Gamma\Lambda^{*}\Phi^{*}\Lambda\Gamma=\Psi\Gamma\Phi^{*}\Gamma=\Psi\Phi=\Lambda^{*}\Theta\Phi.

Hence,

\displaystyle(A_{\Phi}^{\Theta,\Lambda}C_{\Lambda,\Psi}-C_{\Lambda,\Psi}A_{\Phi^{*}}^{\Lambda,\Theta})f

\displaystyle=P_{+}\Lambda P_{-}(\Phi C_{\Theta}f)-P_{+}\Lambda P_{-}\Lambda^{*}(\Theta\Phi(C_{\Lambda}f_{\Lambda}))

\displaystyle-\Lambda C_{\Psi}A_{\Lambda^{*}\Phi^{*}}^{\Lambda,\Psi}f_{\Lambda}

\displaystyle=P_{+}\Lambda P_{-}(\Phi C_{\Theta}f)-P_{+}\Lambda P_{-}\Lambda^{*}(\Theta\Phi(C_{\Lambda}f_{\Lambda}))

\displaystyle-\Lambda A_{\Omega}^{\Lambda,\Psi}C_{\Lambda}f_{\Lambda}

\displaystyle=P_{+}\Lambda P_{-}(\Phi C_{\Theta}f)-P_{+}\Lambda P_{-}\Lambda^{*}(\Theta\Phi(C_{\Lambda}f_{\Lambda}))

\displaystyle-\Lambda P_{\Psi}(\Lambda^{*}\Phi\Theta C_{\Lambda}f_{\Lambda})

\displaystyle=P_{+}\Lambda P_{-}(\Phi C_{\Theta}f)-(P_{+}\Lambda P_{-}\Lambda^{*}+\Lambda P_{\Psi}\Lambda^{*})(\Theta\Phi(C_{\Lambda}P_{\Lambda}f))

\displaystyle=P_{+}\Lambda P_{-}(\Phi C_{\Theta}f)-P_{\Theta}(\Theta\Phi(C_{\Lambda}P_{\Lambda}f))

\displaystyle=P_{+}\Lambda P_{-}(\Phi C_{\Theta}f)-P_{+}\Theta P_{-}\Theta^{*}(\Theta\Phi(C_{\Lambda}P_{\Lambda}f))

\displaystyle=P_{+}\Lambda P_{-}(\Phi C_{\Theta}f)-P_{+}\Theta P_{-}(\Phi(C_{\Lambda}P_{\Lambda}f))

\displaystyle=(\widetilde{H}_{\Lambda}H_{\Phi}C_{\Theta}-\widetilde{H}_{\Theta}H_{\Phi}C_{\Lambda}P_{\Lambda})f.

∎
