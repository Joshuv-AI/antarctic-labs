A mitochondrial function-based prognostic model for hepatocellular carcinoma uncovers MRPS15 as a key driver of tumor progression through oxidative phosphorylation
Authors: Weifei Liang, Shi Zhang, Yuyang Zhang, Donglin Sun, Fang Wang, Hong Lin, Yunhong Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is highly heterogeneous with unpredictable outcomes. Mitochondrial dysfunction drives HCC progression, but its prognostic implications remain unclear. This study aimed to develop a mitochondrial-related prognostic model for HCC.MethodsWe analyzed transcriptomic data from 370 HCC patients from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) using consensus clustering of mitochondrial pathway genes. A mitochondrial-associated scoring model (MASM) was developed via least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) and Cox regression, validated across independent cohorts [GSE116174 and the International Cancer Genome Consortium Liver Cancer-RIKEN, Japan (ICGC-LIRI)]. Functional roles of MRPS15, a key gene in the model, were investigated through in vitro experiments and subcutaneous tumor formation experiment in nude mice.ResultsConsensus clustering identified three mitochondrial subtypes with distinct metabolic profiles: subtype A exhibited elevated oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) and preserved metabolic homeostasis; subtype B showed adaptive metabolic reprogramming; subtype C displayed selective activation of biosynthetic pathways despite widespread mitochondrial dysfunction. The MASM prognostic signature, comprising eight mitochondrial genes, achieved predictive accuracy with area under the curve (AUC) values of 0.805, 0.751, and 0.742 for 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival, respectively, and showed favorable trends compared with clinical staging. MRPS15 was validated as a critical regulator of HCC cell proliferation, invasion, migration, and apoptosis, primarily through sustaining OXPHOS and redox homeostasis.ConclusionsThis study establishes a mitochondrial-based classification and eight-gene MASM model for HCC, which stratifies patients into three subtypes with distinct metabolic, genomic, and immune profiles. The model outperforms traditional staging in survival prediction. MRPS15 is validated as a key oncogenic driver sustaining OXPHOS. These findings provide a framework for personalized therapy and warrant prospective clinical validation.

Highlight box

Key findings

• This study identified three hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) subtypes (A, B, C) with distinct mitochondrial functions, metabolic profiles, and TP53 mutation rates.

• We developed and validated an 8-gene mitochondrial-associated scoring model (MASM) model for superior prognostic prediction (area under the curve >0.74).

• MRPS15 has been validated as an oncogenic driver that promotes HCC via oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS).

What is known and what is new?

• Mitochondrial dysfunction is linked to HCC, but its heterogeneity and prognostic potential are poorly understood. Current prognostic models often overlook mitochondrial pathways.

• This study provides the first comprehensive classification of HCC based on mitochondrial function, linking specific subtypes to TP53 mutation status and immune landscapes. This study introduces the MASM model for precise risk prediction and functionally validates MRPS15 as a causal driver of HCC via OXPHOS regulation, demonstrating that its oncogenic function can be therapeutically modulated.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Our findings establish mitochondrial function as a critical axis for patient stratification, moving beyond a one-size-fits-all approach.

• The MASM model and subtype classification can guide personalized treatment, identifying patients who may benefit from OXPHOS inhibitors, immunotherapy, or metabolic modulators.

• MRPS15 is pinpointed as a novel therapeutic target and predictive biomarker. Future clinical practice should consider integrating mitochondrial functional signatures into routine molecular profiling to optimize treatment selection and improve outcomes in HCC. Prospective trials targeting the MRPS15-OXPHOS axis in high-risk patients are now warranted.

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the most common type of liver cancer, accounting for 85–90% of all primary liver cancers. This cancer constitutes a major health issue around the world (1). It is the second or fourth most common cause of death from cancer, depending on how the data are organized. Recent figures indicate that HCC comprises roughly 850,000 new cases each year, with the disease burden escalating in several countries where most patients continue to arrive at advanced stage (2).

The current prognostic evaluation for HCC patients is based on recognized clinical staging methods, with the Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) staging system functioning as the primary clinical algorithm for patient classification concerning prognosis and therapy assignment (3). For a complete evaluation of the disease, complementary staging methods like the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) Tumor-Node-Metastasis (TNM) classification take into account other factors such as tumor size, number, vascular invasion, and liver function status. Even though these staging systems are well-known, new research in molecular profiling has shown that HCC has a lot of genetic and transcriptome differences. Different molecular subtypes respond differently to treatments and have different survival rates (4,5).

Even with these improvements, there are still limitations with the current molecular prognostic models for HCC. Most of the models that we have now only look at certain pathways or cell processes and do not fully explain how mitochondrial failure leads to liver cancer (6). Genes related to mitochondria are important for basic cell functions like making energy, dealing with stress, and controlling cell death. These genes have not been properly included in the liver cancer prediction models (7,8).

Many genomic characterization studies, including those from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), have revealed molecular issues in HCC. However, there has not yet been a full investigation of mitochondrial pathway genes for patient classification (9). Additionally, while problems with mitochondria have been linked to the development of HCC and changes in metabolism, there has not been enough detailed research on how mitochondrial gene expression patterns can be used to predict outcomes and guide treatment (10).

This study aims to develop and validate a comprehensive prediction model based on mitochondrial-related gene expression for patients with HCC. We advocate that a thorough examination of mitochondrial pathway gene expression can precisely delineate several molecular subtypes of HCC, which demonstrate significantly different survival outcomes and treatment responses. We expect that changes in mitochondrial-related genes will be linked to more aggressive tumors, poorer outcomes, and different responses to treatment. We aim to create a strong prognostic signature that surpasses conventional clinical indicators in predicting patient survival by combining multi-omics data from TCGA with independent validation cohorts, including the International Cancer Genome Consortium (ICGC). We believe that this classification based on mitochondria will help us understand tumor behavior and the immune environment better, and it will also help us find new treatment targets for personalized medicine in HCC treatment. We present this article in accordance with the ARRIVE and MDAR reporting checklists (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-0426/rc).

Methods

Data collection

Transcriptomic and clinical data were obtained from multiple publicly available databases to ensure comprehensive analysis. The main dataset included 370 HCC patients from TCGA-LIHC database, which has genomic, transcriptomic, proteomic, and methylation data for more than 20,000 primary tumors (http://cancergenome.nih.gov/). Validation datasets were retrieved from the SurvivalML program, including GSE116174 (64 HCC patients) and the ICGC Liver Cancer-RIKEN, Japan (ICGC-LIRI) cohort (232 HCC patients), both containing matched transcriptomic and clinical information. Mitochondrial-related gene annotations were obtained from the MitoCarta 3.0 database (https://www.broadinstitute.org/mitocarta/mitocarta30-inventory-mammalian-mitochondrial-proteins-and-pathways), yielding 1,136 mitochondrial-related genes and 149 mitochondrial-associated pathways. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. Ethical approval was not required, as all data were derived from publicly available databases in accordance with their respective publication guidelines and data use policies.

Data preprocessing and normalization

Raw count data from TCGA-LIHC were converted to fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM) format to facilitate Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) and subsequent subgroup identification. The ICGC-LIRI cohort underwent similar FPKM conversion for cross-platform compatibility.

Datasets from the SurvivalML project were processed using standardized protocols. RNA-sequencing data, no matter if it started as raw counts or FPKM, were changed into transcripts per million (TPM) values and adjusted using a log transformation to match the patterns seen in microarray data. Microarray datasets were handled with specific R packages for each platform: affy for Affymetrix® arrays, lumi for Illumina® arrays, and limma for Agilent® arrays, which included checks for data quality and adjustments for any batch effects.

To ensure cross-platform comparability and minimize technical variations, z-score normalization was applied to all gene expression datasets across different data types and platforms.

Consensus clustering analysis

Single-sample Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (ssGSEA) enrichment scores were calculated for each HCC patient using the GSVA R package and the mitochondrial pathway gene sets derived from MitoCarta 3.0. Before enrichment scoring, the gene expression matrix was log-transformed when appropriate and standardized to ensure comparability across samples. The resulting patient-by-pathway enrichment matrix was used as the input for unsupervised consensus clustering with the ConsensusClusterPlus R package. Consensus clustering was performed by repeated resampling of the samples and pathway features to evaluate cluster stability across candidate cluster numbers. The optimal number of mitochondrial subtypes was determined by jointly assessing the consensus matrix, cumulative distribution function (CDF) curves, the relative change in the area under the CDF curve, the proportion of ambiguous clustering (PAC) score, and NbClust validation indices. A three-cluster solution was selected because it showed stable intra-cluster consensus, clear inter-cluster separation, and consistent biological interpretability. The final cluster labels were then used for downstream comparisons of survival, clinicopathological characteristics, genomic alterations, metabolic pathway activity, immune signatures, and drug sensitivity.

Identification of mitochondrial-associated genes

Mitochondrial-associated gene expression profiles were extracted from three independent datasets: TCGA-LIHC, ICGC-LIRI, and GSE116174. Gene symbols were harmonized across datasets, and only genes annotated as mitochondrial-related genes in MitoCarta 3.0 and detectable in all three cohorts were retained. For genes represented by multiple probes or transcript identifiers, expression values were collapsed to a single gene-level value using the probe or transcript with the highest average expression, thereby reducing redundancy while preserving the most informative measurement. Genes with missing expression values in any validation cohort were excluded to ensure that the same feature space could be applied across the training and validation datasets. This filtering procedure yielded 842 mitochondrial-associated genes commonly expressed in TCGA-LIHC, ICGC-LIRI, and GSE116174, which were subsequently used for prognostic screening and construction of the mitochondrial-associated scoring model (MASM).

Tumour mutation analysis

By using the maftools package in R software, the top 20 genes with highest tumor mutation frequency were identified and visualized through oncoplots. TP53 mutation sites were analyzed using lollipop plots generated by the lollipopPlot() function, displaying mutation distribution across protein domains with color-coded mutation types (missense, nonsense, frameshift, and splice site variants). Somatic mutation rates were calculated to assess TP53 alteration patterns across patient subtypes.

Statistical analysis and feature selection

A type of analysis called univariate Cox proportional hazards regression was done using the “survival” R package to find genes that are strongly linked to how long patients survive. To avoid overfitting and improve the selection of features, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression was used with the “glmnet” package and included a method called 10-fold cross-validation. Next, a multivariate Cox regression analysis was done to find factors that independently affect survival and to create the final risk model.

The section focuses on the construction and validation of the prognostic risk model.

A prognostic risk score model was created using a formula that combines the expression levels of certain genes and their corresponding coefficients from the Cox regression analysis. Patients were stratified into high-risk and low-risk groups based on the median risk score calculated from the training cohort (TCGA-LIHC). The model’s effectiveness was thoroughly tested using methods like Kaplan-Meier survival analysis, log-rank tests, and time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves to check how accurately it predicts survival rates at 1, 3, and 5 years for both the training and validation groups.

Nomogram development and validation

An integrated nomogram was constructed using the “rms” R package to predict individual patient survival by combining clinical variables with the established mitochondrial-related risk score. Clinical parameters, including age, tumor stage, and other relevant variables, were incorporated with appropriate weightings based on their prognostic contributions. The nomogram’s performance was rigorously evaluated using calibration plots and decision curve analysis (DCA) at 1-, 3-, and 5-year time points to assess both predictive accuracy and clinical utility.

Drug sensitivity analysis

To assess therapeutic response disparities across mitochondrial subtypes and risk stratification groups, we utilized the “oncoPredict” R package for comprehensive drug sensitivity prediction. This computational tool calculates half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values, representing the drug concentrations required to inhibit 50% of cell viability, enabling quantitative assessment of therapeutic efficacy across multiple agents. We investigated drug sensitivity profiles across the three mitochondrial subtypes and high-risk versus low-risk groups defined by our MASM model, analyzing a comprehensive panel of therapeutic compounds including targeted agents, chemotherapeutics, and novel experimental drugs. IC50 values were calculated for each patient sample, and statistical significance of sensitivity differences was evaluated using the Kruskal-Wallis test. This analysis aimed to identify subtype-specific therapeutic vulnerabilities and create a pharmacogenomic framework for personalized treatment selection. By correlating drug sensitivity patterns with mitochondrial functional profiles and risk stratification categories, this approach enables the identification of optimal therapeutic strategies tailored to individual patient molecular characteristics, ultimately supporting precision medicine approaches in HCC management.

Cell culture and transfection

Human liver cancer cell lines Huh-7 and SNU-398 cells (Shanghai Fuheng Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA, 11965092) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, 10099141), 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, 15140122) at 37 ℃, 5% CO2. For MRPS15 knockdown, lentiviral shRNAs (Tsingke Biotechnology, Beijing, China) targeting MRPS15 (shRNA-1: GAGAAACATCGAAAGGACAAA; shRNA-2: CCAAGTTTCCTTTCAACCAGT) or non-targeting control were transfected into HEK293T using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA, L3000015). Viral supernatants were transduced into Huh-7 and SNU-398 cells with 8 µg/mL polybrene (Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA, TR-1003). Stable pools were selected with 2 µg/mL puromycin (Sigma, P8833) for 7–10 days. Knockdown was confirmed by quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) and Western blot.

qPCR

Total RNA was extracted using RNAiso Plus (TaKaRa, Kusatsu, Shiga, Japan), and reverse transcription was performed using a reverse transcription kit (TaKaRa). Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was used as the internal control, and gene expression levels were quantified using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Results were presented as fold changes relative to the control group. The sequences of all primers are as follows: MRPS15-forward: 5'-CCGATTCGTGACCAAGAAGGCT-3'; MRPS15-reverse: 5'-TCTGGGTTCCTCCGCTTTGCTT-3'; GAPDH-forward: 5'-CCAGAACATCATCCCTGCCT-3'; GAPDH-reverse: 5'- CCTGCTTCACCACCTTCTTG-3'.

Western blot assay

Total protein was extracted from cells using Racial and Identity Profiling Act (RIPA) lysis buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China, P0013B) supplemented with 1% protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA, 78440). The protein concentration was determined using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 23225). Equal amounts of protein (20–30 µg) were separated by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) using a pre-stained protein ladder (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #26616) as a molecular weight reference and subsequently transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA, IPVH00010). The membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween-20 (TBST) for 1 hour at room temperature and then incubated overnight at 4 ℃ with the following primary antibodies diluted in 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in TBST: anti-MRPS15 (Rabbit monoclonal, Abcam, Cambridge, UK, ab137070, 1:1,000), anti-NDUFB8 (Mouse monoclonal, Abcam, ab110242, 1:1,000), anti-SDHB (Rabbit monoclonal, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA, 11998, 1:1,000), anti-UQCRC2 (Rabbit polyclonal, Proteintech, Rosemont, IL, USA, 14742-1-AP, 1:1,000), anti-COX IV (Rabbit monoclonal, Cell Signaling Technology, 4850, 1:1,000), anti-ATP5A (Mouse monoclonal, Abcam, ab14748, 1:1,000), anti-phospho-AMPKα (Thr172) (Rabbit monoclonal, Cell Signaling Technology, 2535, 1:1,000), anti-AMPKα (Rabbit monoclonal, Cell Signaling Technology, 2532, 1:1,000), anti-phospho-S6 ribosomal protein (Ser235/236) (Rabbit monoclonal, Cell Signaling Technology, 4858, 1:2,000), anti-S6 ribosomal protein (Rabbit monoclonal, Cell Signaling Technology, 2217, 1:1,000), anti-VDAC1/porin (Mouse monoclonal, Abcam, ab186321, 1:1,000), anti-GAPDH (Rabbit monoclonal, Cell Signaling Technology, 2118, 1:5,000), anti-β-actin (Mouse monoclonal, Abcam, ab8226, 1:50,000).

After three washes with TBST, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (Goat anti-Rabbit IgG-HRP, HUABIO, HA1001, 1:5,000; Goat anti-Mouse IgG-HRP, HUABIO, HA1006, 1:5,000) for 1 hour at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection kit (Millipore, WBKLS0500) and imaged with a chemiluminescence imaging system (Tanon, Shanghai, China, 5200). Densitometric analysis was performed using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health).

Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

Cell viability and proliferation were evaluated using the CCK-8 (Beyotime Biotechnology). Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1×103 cells per well and incubated for 24 hours at 37 ℃ in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. Following treatment, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well, and the cells were incubated for an additional 1–4 hours at 37 ℃. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (BioTek Instruments, Winooski, VT, USA) to assess cell viability. To monitor proliferation dynamics, measurements were taken at multiple time points (days 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5), and relative cell viability was determined by normalizing absorbance values to those of the control group.

Cell migration and invasion assay

Cell migration and invasion abilities were evaluated using Transwell chambers (Corning, NY, USA). For the migration assay, chambers with 8 µm pore polycarbonate membranes were used. For the invasion assay, the membranes were pre-coated with Matrigel (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA) to simulate the extracellular matrix barrier. Briefly, cells were harvested and resuspended in serum-free medium. A total of 5×104 cells in 200 µL of serum-free medium were seeded into the upper chamber. The lower chamber was filled with 600 µL of complete medium containing 10% FBS, which served as a chemoattractant. After incubation for 24 hours at 37 ℃ with 5% CO2, non-migrated or non-invaded cells on the upper surface of the membrane were carefully removed using a cotton swab. Cells that had migrated or invaded to the lower surface were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 minutes and then stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 30 minutes at room temperature. The stained cells were imaged under a light microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). The number of migratory or invasive cells was quantified by counting cells in five randomly selected fields per membrane. Each experiment was performed in triplicate and repeated at least three times independently.

Annexin V and propidium iodide (PI) staining for apoptosis detection

Apoptosis was detected using an Annexin V-FITC/PI apoptosis detection kit (Elabscience, Wuhan, China, E-CK-A211). Cells were harvested, washed with cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and resuspended in 1× binding buffer at a concentration of 1×106 cells/mL. Annexin V-FITC and PI were added to the suspension, followed by incubation in the dark at room temperature for 15–20 minutes. After staining, cells were analyzed by flow cytometry within 1 hour. Apoptotic status was determined based on fluorescence: live cells (Annexin V⁻/PI⁻), early apoptotic cells (Annexin V⁺/PI⁻), and late apoptotic or necrotic cells (Annexin V⁺/PI⁺).

Measurement of intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels

ROS levels were measured using the more stable, low-background fluorescent probe 2’,7’-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (H2DCFH-DA, Sigma-Aldrich, D6883) and quantified by flow cytometry. Briefly, logarithmic-phase stable hepatoma cells (shCtrl, sh-MRPS15-1, and sh-MRPS15-2) were trypsinized, counted, and resuspended at a density of 1×106 cells per tube in 1 mL of serum-free medium or Hanks’ Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS) containing 10 µM H2DCFDA. Cells were incubated in the dark at 37 ℃ under 5% CO2 for 30 minutes. During this period, the probe diffused into cells and was deacetylated by intracellular esterases to form the cell-impermeable compound dichlorodihydrofluorescein (H2DCF). After loading, cells were washed twice with pre-warmed complete medium to remove excess extracellular probe, resuspended in fresh complete medium, and returned to the incubator for an additional 15–30-minute incubation in the dark to ensure complete hydrolysis and stabilization of the fluorescent baseline. Finally, cells were washed once with ice-cold PBS, resuspended in 500 µL of ice-cold PBS, kept on ice in the dark, and analyzed within 1 hour. To accurately quantify ROS levels and correct for cellular autofluorescence, an unstained control (without H2DCFDA loading) was prepared in parallel for each genetic cell line (shCtrl, sh-MRPS15-1, and sh-MRPS15-2). All samples were analyzed using a BD LSRFortessa flow cytometer. Upon oxidation by intracellular ROS, non-fluorescent H2DCF is converted to highly fluorescent dichlorofluorescein (DCF). Samples were excited with a 488 nm laser, and emission was collected through a 530/30 nm bandpass filter (standard FITC channel).

Seahorse XF Cell Mito Stress Test and Glycolysis Stress Test

Cellular metabolic function was assessed using an XFe96 Seahorse Extracellular Flux Analyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). For both assays, cells were seeded in XF96 cell culture microplates (Agilent, #101085-004) at optimized densities (SNU-398: 2.0×104 cells/well; Huh-7: 1.5×104 cells/well) and allowed to adhere overnight.

Mitochondrial Stress Test [oxygen consumption rate (OCR) measurement]

On the day of the assay, the growth medium was replaced with pre-warmed Seahorse XF Base Medium (Agilent, #102353-100) supplemented with 10 mM glucose (Sigma, G8769), 1 mM sodium pyruvate (Sigma, P8574), and 2 mM L-glutamine (Sigma, G8540), and the pH was adjusted to 7.4. Cells were incubated in this unbuffered medium at 37 ℃ in a non-CO2 incubator for 45–60 minutes prior to the assay. The sensor cartridge was loaded with mitochondrial modulators from the Seahorse XF Cell Mito Stress Test Kit (Agilent, #103015-100) to achieve the following final concentrations in the well: 1.5 µM oligomycin (Port A), 1.0 µM FCCP (Port B), and 0.5 µM rotenone/0.5 µM antimycin A (Port C). The OCR was measured under basal conditions and following the sequential injection of these compounds.

Glycolysis Stress Test [extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) measurement]

Cells were equilibrated in Seahorse XF Base Medium supplemented with 2 mM L-glutamine only (pH 7.4). Compounds from the Seahorse XF Glycolysis Stress Test Kit (Agilent, #103020-100) were loaded to achieve the following final concentrations: 10 mM glucose (Port A), 1.0 µM oligomycin (Port B), and 50 mM 2-deoxy-d-glucose (2-DG, Port C). The ECAR was measured to assess glycolytic function.

For both assays, data were normalized to the total protein content per well, quantified by a BCA assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #23225) following the Seahorse run. Key metabolic parameters (e.g., basal respiration, ATP-linked respiration, maximal respiration, glycolysis, glycolytic capacity) were calculated using the Seahorse Wave Desktop Software (Agilent Technologies).

Xenograft experiments

Six-week-old male BALB/c nude mice (purchased from Guangzhou Ruige Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Guangzhou, China) were housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions with a 12-hour light/dark cycle, controlled temperature (22±1 ℃) and humidity (50%±10%), and ad libitum access to food and water. After an acclimatization period of at least one week, the mice were randomly assigned into three groups (n=6 per group) using a computer-generated random number sequence.

To establish xenograft tumors, mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of freshly prepared tribromoethanol (Avertin, 1.25% in sterile saline) at a dose of 0.2 mL per 10 g body weight. Under anesthesia, Huh-7 cells (2×106) suspended in 100 µL of PBS/MATRIGEL mixture were subcutaneously inoculated into the flanks of the mice. The three groups consisted of: (I) mice inoculated with Huh-7 cells stably expressing a non-targeting control shRNA (shCtrl); (II) mice inoculated with Huh-7 cells stably expressing MRPS15-targeting shRNA sequence #1 (shMRPS15-1); and (III) mice inoculated with Huh-7 cells stably expressing MRPS15-targeting shRNA sequence #2 (shMRPS15-2).

Tumor growth was monitored for 25 days post-inoculation. Tumor dimensions were measured every three days using a caliper by an investigator blinded to group allocation, and the volume was calculated using the formula: Volume (mm3) = (Length × Width2)/2. Predefined humane endpoints (tumor volume >1,500 mm3, severe weight loss >20%, or signs of distress) were in place, though no animal met these criteria before the planned endpoint. At the end of the study (day 25), all mice were euthanized by gradual displacement of cage air with compressed carbon dioxide at a flow rate not exceeding 30% of the chamber volume per minute, followed by cervical dislocation, in accordance with the AVMA Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals. The tumors were then excised, weighed, and processed for histological analysis. The animal experiments were performed by the authors at the animal facility provided by Ruige Biotechnology, a commercial contract research organization (CRO). All animal experiments were performed under a project license (No. 20251020-002) granted by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Ruige Biotechnology, in compliance with relevant institutional and national guidelines for the care and use of animals.

Histological and immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis

Following excision, tumor tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24–48 hours, processed through a graded ethanol series, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned into 4-µm thick slices.

For histological evaluation, sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining according to standard protocols.

For IHC analysis, deparaffinized sections underwent antigen retrieval using citrate buffer (pH 6.0) heated in a microwave. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched with 3% H2O2. The sections were then blocked with normal goat serum and incubated overnight at 4 ℃ with the following primary antibodies:

❖ Anti-Ki67 (Rabbit monoclonal, Abcam, ab16667; dilution 1:200);

❖ Anti-Ki67 (Rabbit monoclonal, Abcam, ab16667; dilution 1:200);

❖ Anti-cleaved caspase-3 (c-caspase-3) (Rabbit monoclonal, Cell Signaling Technology, 9664S; dilution 1:400).

❖ Anti-cleaved caspase-3 (c-caspase-3) (Rabbit monoclonal, Cell Signaling Technology, 9664S; dilution 1:400).

After washing, the sections were incubated with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody followed by development with diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate. Finally, the sections were counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, and mounted.

Slides were imaged using a light microscope. The expression levels of Ki67 (a proliferation marker) and c-caspase-3 (an apoptosis marker) were evaluated based on the percentage of positive cells and staining intensity.

Statistical analysis

All data processing, statistical analyses, and visualization were conducted using R software version 4.4.3. Independent experiments are biological replicates (repeated at least three times). Correlations between continuous variables were evaluated using Pearson’s correlation coefficients. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test when needed, while continuous variables were analyzed with the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for non-normal distributions or Student’s t-test for normal distributions. Survival analyses, including Cox proportional hazards regression and Kaplan-Meier survival curves, were performed using the survival package. Optimal cut-off values for risk stratification were determined using the survminer package. We created ROC curves to predict yes or no outcomes using the pROC package, and we calculated time-dependent area under the curve (AUC) values for survival results with the timeROC package. Data visualization was accomplished using multiple R packages: heatmaps were generated using the pheatmap package, volcano plots were constructed to visualize differential expression patterns, and additional plots were created using ggplot2. All statistical tests were two-sided, with significance levels denoted as *, P<0.05; **, P<0.01; and ***, P<0.001.

Results

Development and validation of mitochondrial function-based consensus clusters

The analysis of 370 TCGA-LIHC samples using mitochondrial pathway gene sets identified three distinct subtypes through consensus clustering based on CDF analysis and delta area plots (Figure 1A,1B). The consensus matrix demonstrated robust cluster stability with high intra-cluster consensus (>0.85) and low inter-cluster consensus (<0.15) (Figure 1C), establishing three mitochondrial function subtypes: subtype A (n=100, 27.0%), subtype B (n=135, 36.5%), and subtype C (n=135, 36.5%). This stable clustering architecture provides a reliable foundation for clinical risk stratification, enabling oncologists to categorize patients into distinct prognostic groups based on tumor mitochondrial characteristics.

Mitochondrial function-based consensus clustering and clinical validation. (A-C) Consensus clustering analysis identifying optimal k=3 subtypes: cumulative distribution function curves, delta area plot, and consensus matrix showing clustering stability (intra-cluster consensus >0.85, inter-cluster consensus <0.15). (D) Heatmap showing distinct mitochondrial pathway enrichment profiles across three subtypes (A: n=100, B: n=135, C: n=135) with clinical annotations. (E) Kaplan-Meier survival curves demonstrating significant prognostic differences among subtypes (P<0.001). (F-I) Proportions of tumor stage (F), pathological grade (G), and gender (H) are shown for subtypes A-C. Age distribution is shown in (I) with Kruskal-Wallis test (P=0.11). CDF, cumulative distribution function; LIHC, liver hepatocellular carcinoma; TCGA, The Cancer Genome Atlas.

Molecular characterization revealed distinct mitochondrial functional profiles across subtypes (Figure 1D), each carrying specific clinical implications for patient management. Subtype A was characterized by elevated oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS)-related pathways, indicating preserved OXPHOS capacity and metabolic homeostasis, suggesting these patients may benefit from metabolic-targeting therapies. Subtype B demonstrated predominant upregulation of metabolic reprogramming pathways, suggesting enhanced adaptive metabolic flexibility that may confer therapeutic responsiveness to conventional treatments. In contrast, subtype C exhibited significant enrichment in mitochondrial dynamics and surveillance pathways, potentially reflecting compensatory mechanisms activated in response to mitochondrial dysfunction, indicating the need for more aggressive therapeutic interventions.

Kaplan-Meier survival analysis revealed significant prognostic differences among the three subtypes (P<0.001), with subtype B demonstrating the most favorable overall survival (OS), followed by subtype A, while subtype C exhibited the poorest prognosis (Figure 1E). Analysis of clinical characteristics including stage, grade, gender, and age across the three mitochondrial subtypes revealed no significant associations, indicating that the subtypes may provide independent prognostic information (Figure 1F-1I). These findings provide critical prognostic information that can guide clinical decision-making, allowing clinicians to identify high-risk patients (subtype C) who may require intensive monitoring and aggressive treatment strategies, while recognizing favorable-prognosis patients (subtype B) who may benefit from standard-of-care approaches.

To validate the clinical utility of this classification system, we performed independent validation using the ICGC-LIRI cohort, which confirmed the reproducibility of the three-subtype classification and demonstrated consistent prognostic significance (P=0.044) (Figure S1). This independent validation establishes the clinical robustness and transferability of our classification system, supporting its potential implementation in routine clinical practice as a standardized prognostic tool for HCC patient management.

Genomic alterations and metabolic reprogramming patterns across mitochondrial subtypes

To elucidate the genomic foundations underlying mitochondrial subtype differentiation and their clinical predictive value, we characterized the mutational landscape across the three subtypes. Analysis of tumor mutational burden (TMB) revealed distinct mutational patterns among subtypes (Figure 2A), providing genomic biomarkers that can inform treatment selection. Waterfall plots demonstrated that missense mutations were the predominant genetic alteration across all subtypes, with consistently high overall alteration rates: 91.67% (88/96 samples) in subtype A, 86.47% (115/133 samples) in subtype B, and 89.31% (117/131 samples) in subtype C. TP53, CTNNB1, and TTN emerged as the three most frequently mutated genes across all subtypes, though their relative frequencies varied between groups, providing molecular markers for subtype identification in clinical settings.

Of particular clinical significance was the subtype-specific pattern of TP53 mutations (Figure 2B), which serves as a powerful prognostic biomarker with immediate clinical applicability. The somatic mutation rates varied dramatically: 20.83% in subtype A, 15.79% in subtype B, and remarkably 51.15% in subtype C. These mutations were distributed across key functional domains, including P53_TAD, P53, and P53_tetramer regions, with missense mutations predominating, followed by frameshift deletions, nonsense mutations, and splice site mutations. Crucially, TP53 mutation frequency showed a strong inverse correlation with clinical outcomes, where subtype C, harboring the highest TP53 mutation burden (51.15%), exhibited the shortest OS, while subtype B, with the lowest mutation rate (15.79%), demonstrated the most favorable prognosis. This TP53 mutation stratification can be readily implemented in clinical laboratories to support treatment decision-making and patient counseling.

We conducted comprehensive metabolic pathway analysis utilizing 113 metabolic signatures from the IOBR database to understand how distinct mitochondrial functional profiles correspond to metabolic reprogramming patterns, providing insights for metabolic-targeted therapeutic strategies. Kruskal-Wallis testing revealed significant metabolic disparities among subtypes (all P<2.2e−16) for essential representative pathways. The complete metabolic atlas exhibited distinct subtype differentiation through unsupervised hierarchical clustering (Figure 2C), creating a metabolic roadmap for personalized treatment selection.

Heatmap visualization revealed a progressive pattern of metabolic reprogramming that directly correlated with mitochondrial functional profiles and correlates with therapeutic vulnerability patterns: subtype A demonstrated robust metabolic activity across various pathways (predominantly red signature), consistent with its preserved OXPHOS capacity and suggesting responsiveness to OXPHOS inhibitors; subtype B exhibited intermediate metabolic states with mixed pathway activities (alternating red and blue patterns), indicating enhanced metabolic reprogramming capability and potential responsiveness to metabolic modulators; whereas subtype C showed significant metabolic suppression (predominantly blue signature) with selective pathway upregulation in specific biosynthetic processes, indicating potential targets for combination therapies.

Analysis of three representative metabolic pathways illustrated these unique reprogramming patterns with direct therapeutic implications (Figure 2D). OXPHOS patterns directly validated the mitochondrial functional characterization: subtype A exhibited the highest activity (median score ~8), subtype B demonstrated the most significant suppression (median score ~−2) despite having the best prognosis, while subtype C displayed intermediate yet positive activity (median score ~2) despite having the worst prognosis (P<2.2e−16). This counterintuitive finding indicates that metabolic reprogramming ability in subtype B confers survival advantages through alternative metabolic pathways, independent of OXPHOS activity, suggesting that therapeutic success may depend on targeting alternative metabolic pathways rather than OXPHOS itself.

Lipid metabolism pathways exhibited subtype-specific adaptive responses with clear therapeutic targeting opportunities. Glycerolipid metabolism showed a gradual decrease from subtype A to subtype C, while glycerophospholipid metabolism demonstrated a markedly reversed pattern, with subtype C displaying significantly elevated activity compared to the reduced levels in subtypes A and B (P<2.22e−16). This metabolic reconfiguration provides mechanistic insights into prognostic disparities and identifies phosphorylation-dependent pathways as potential therapeutic targets for high-risk subtype C patients.

Immune microenvironment characterization and therapeutic implications of mitochondrial subtypes

To comprehensively characterize the biological implications of mitochondrial subtypes and their impact on immunotherapy decision-making, we examined immune system and DNA repair pathways, revealing distinct functional characteristics among the three subtypes (Figure 3A). Hierarchical clustering identified unique pathway activation patterns for each subtype, establishing clear associations between mitochondrial function and immune microenvironment composition that directly inform immunotherapy selection strategies.

Immune microenvironment and therapeutic response profiles. (A) Immune infiltration analysis by CIBERSORT algorithm showing subtype-specific tumor microenvironment compositions and DNA repair pathway activities. (B) Pathway enrichment analysis of tumor biology signatures, including ferroptosis, hypoxia response, and exosome-related pathways. (C) Drug sensitivity profiles across multiple therapeutic agents demonstrating differential treatment responsiveness among subtypes (P<2.2e−16).

Subtype A exhibited minimal immune infiltration signatures across various immune cell types, characterized by reduced tumor-infiltrating immune cell signatures and notably low levels of CD8+ T cells, Th2 cells, and T helper cell infiltration, corresponding to an immune-excluded or “cold” tumor phenotype. This subtype demonstrated reduced activity in DNA damage response pathways including nucleotide excision repair, mismatch repair, and homologous recombination, indicating genomic stability consistent with its low TP53 mutation rate (20.83%). Clinically, these patients may be poor candidates for immunotherapy but could benefit from immune-activating combination strategies or metabolic interventions to enhance immune infiltration.

Subtype C showed the most pronounced immune activation, demonstrated by elevated immune infiltration markers including increased CD8+ T cell infiltration, T follicular helper (TFH) cells, and abundant regulatory T cells. This subtype exhibited elevated levels of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and enhanced cell cycle regulation, suggesting a complex immunosuppressive milieu despite abundant immune cell presence. Moreover, subtype C demonstrated enhanced DNA repair mechanisms, correlating with its elevated TP53 mutation burden (51.15%) and persistent genomic instability. These characteristics suggest that subtype C patients may respond to immune checkpoint inhibitors but may require combination approaches targeting immunosuppressive mechanisms to overcome MDSC-mediated resistance.

Subtype B exhibited intermediate immune infiltration patterns, indicating a balanced immunological state characterized by moderate activation of both immune responses and DNA repair mechanisms, consistent with its lowest TP53 mutation rate (15.79%) and optimal OS. This balanced immune profile suggests these patients may respond well to standard immunotherapy approaches without requiring intensive combination strategies.

Further examination of tumor biology pathways revealed distinct molecular signatures among the three subtypes (Figure 3B), with subtype C demonstrating the most aggressive and complex biological profile that requires specialized therapeutic approaches. Subtype C exhibited pronounced activation of tumor-promoting and stress-response pathways, including comprehensive upregulation of ferroptosis signatures and robust activation of multiple hypoxia response pathways (Winter_hypoxia_signature, Hu_hypoxia_signature, and Nature_metabolism_Hypoxia), indicating adaptation to oxygen-limited microenvironments characteristic of aggressive tumors. These pathway activations identify ferroptosis inducers and hypoxia-targeting agents as promising therapeutic options for subtype C patients.

Exosome-related pathway analysis revealed that subtype C had the strongest activation of intercellular communication mechanisms, with significant upregulation of exosomal secretion, extracellular vesicle biogenesis, and exosome assembly pathways. This enhanced exosomal activity, coupled with elevated m6A RNA modification signatures, indicates extensive post-transcriptional regulation and stress-induced cellular communication networks. Subtype C also displayed the most complex immune landscape with strong infiltration of multiple immune cell types and pronounced activation of pro-inflammatory pathways and complement cascades, creating a highly inflammatory tumor microenvironment that may contribute to both immune evasion and tumor progression. These findings suggest that exosome-targeting therapies and m6A modification inhibitors could provide novel therapeutic avenues for managing high-risk subtype C patients.

Drug sensitivity analysis across multiple therapeutic agents validated these molecular distinctions and provided actionable insights for clinical treatment selection (Figure 3C). The Kruskal-Wallis test (P<2.2e−16) identified significant differences in drug sensitivity profiles across tested compounds, creating a pharmacogenomic framework for personalized treatment decisions. Subtype A displayed moderate and relatively consistent drug sensitivity across most therapeutic agents, reflecting its stable metabolic and genomic state and suggesting that standard treatment protocols may be appropriate. Subtype B exhibited the most favorable drug sensitivity profile with enhanced responsiveness to multiple therapeutic categories, consistent with its optimal OS outcomes and supporting the use of conventional therapeutic approaches with confidence in treatment efficacy. Subtype C demonstrated the most complex drug sensitivity pattern, characterized by increased resistance to many conventional therapeutic agents but selective sensitivity to specific drug categories, aligning with its aggressive molecular profile and poor clinical outcomes. This resistance pattern indicates that subtype C patients should be prioritized for clinical trials of novel agents or combination therapies targeting their specific vulnerabilities.

The comprehensive molecular characterization of subtype C, distinguished by hypoxia adaptation, enhanced intercellular communication, complex immune infiltration, and variable drug responsiveness, underscores its aggressive nature and provides a roadmap for precision oncology approaches. Our findings suggest that personalized therapeutic strategies targeting ferroptosis pathways, hypoxia signaling, or exosome biogenesis may provide novel treatment opportunities for this high-risk subgroup. Importantly, this mitochondrial function-based classification system can be integrated into existing clinical workflows through routine molecular profiling, enabling oncologists to make evidence-based treatment decisions, optimize patient stratification for clinical trials, and improve overall patient outcomes through personalized medicine approaches. These findings establish mitochondrial function-based subtypes as both prognostically significant and therapeutically relevant classifications that bridge the gap between molecular research and clinical practice, providing oncologists with a powerful tool for advancing precision medicine in HCC.

Building the prognostic MASM model

While the three mitochondrial subtypes demonstrated significant prognostic value, clinical practice requires more precise, individualized risk assessment tools that can provide continuous risk stratification for personalized patient management. To address this clinical need and enhance prognostic precision for individual patients, we developed a quantitative mitochondrial-based prognostic signature.

We randomly assigned 339 TCGA-LIHC patients to the training group or the internal validation group at a ratio of 0.65. We used univariate Cox regression analysis to build the prognostic model from the training cohort (Figure 4A). This study examined the relationship between HCC patients’ survival and mitochondrial-associated genes. We set a significance level of P<0.05. To reduce gene overfitting and identify the optimal model, we employed LASSO Cox regression analysis. Figure 4B,4C illustrate the lambda selection process and coefficient shrinkage. Subsequently, multivariate Cox regression analysis finally selected the prognostic model genes. Based on these findings, we developed a scoring model called MASM. We employed the identical methods to compute risk scores for each TCGA-LIHC participant and categorized patients into low- and high-risk groups according to the median risk score. Figure S2A depicts the survival results and clinical features of these groups, which displayed divergent gene expression patterns as evidenced by the expression profile heatmaps. Patients in the low-risk group exhibited elevated expression levels of GOT2 and ALAS1, whereas the other MASM model genes were primarily expressed in the high-risk group. This expression pattern was uniformly detected across all three validation datasets: the internal validation set (TestSet, Figure S2B), ICGC-LIRI (Figure S2C), and GSE116174 (Figure S2D).The risk stratification had considerable prognostic significance, with elevated risk scores markedly correlated with greater mortality rates, whereas the low-risk cohort displayed significantly improved OS (Figure 4D). The ROC curve analysis of the training group showed AUC values of 0.805, 0.751, and 0.742 for predicting survival at 1, 3, and 5 years, respectively, as shown in Figure 4E-4G, which means the MASM model is very good at making these predictions.

Validation of prognostic MASM model

The MASM prognostic signature was validated across three separate cohorts: an internal validation set (TestSet) and two external validation cohorts (ICGC-LIRI and GSE116174). All validation studies utilized the same risk score characteristics and cutoffs defined in the training cohort, categorizing patients into low-risk and high-risk groups appropriately (Figure S3). The internal validation cohort (TestSet) confirmed that higher risk scores were closely linked to increased death rates, while the low-risk group showed better OS compared to the high-risk group. The ROC curve analysis demonstrated robust predictive efficacy for 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival outcomes. The external validation groups (ICGC-LIRI and GSE116174) confirmed that the MASM signature is strong and can be applied to different populations and technology platforms. The consistent ROC curve evaluations in all validation groups confirmed that the prognostic model can reliably predict outcomes and is useful in clinical settings.

Development and evaluation of a prognostic nomogram

We performed univariate and multivariate Cox regression analysis to ascertain the independence of the MASM model from traditional clinical criteria (Figure 5A,5B). We identified staging and a risk score as substantial factors. We developed a nomogram to forecast the 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival rates of patients in the TCGA-LIHC cohort. This nomogram integrated the MASM Riskscore with essential clinical characteristics (Figure 5C). The results revealed the significant utility of the MASM model in practical applications.

In vitro functional validation of MRPS15 in HCC cell proliferation, invasion, migration and apoptosis

Among the eight genes constituting the MASM signature, MRPS15 demonstrated one of the highest positive coefficient in the risk score formula. We obtained MRPS15 mRNA expression data from the TCGA database, which revealed significantly elevated expression in HCC tissues compared to normal tissues (Figure S4A,S4B). Furthermore, MRPS15 expression showed a positive correlation with pathological stage (Figure S4C). Analysis of OS indicated that HCC patients with high MRPS15 expression had better OS than those with low expression (Figure S4D), suggesting its potential role as a key contributor to high-risk prognosis in HCC.

To investigate the biological function of MRPS15, which was identified as a potential oncogene through our bioinformatic analysis, we established stable MRPS15-knockdown HCC cell lines using shRNA. The knockdown efficiency was confirmed at both the mRNA and protein levels (Figure S5A,S5B).

The oncogenic role of MRPS15 was further investigated by assessing its impact on key malignant phenotypes of HCC cells. Stable knockdown of MRPS15 significantly impaired the proliferative capacity of both SNU-398 and Huh-7 cells in a time-dependent manner, as evidenced by CCK-8 assays over a 5-day period (Figure 6A). Furthermore, MRPS15 deficiency markedly reduced the invasive and migratory capabilities of HCC cells. Transwell assays demonstrated a substantial decrease in the number of invading (Figure 6B,6C) and migrating (Figure 6D,6E) cells upon MRPS15 knockdown compared to the control groups. Consistent with the suppression of growth and motility, MRPS15 knockdown also promoted cell death. Flow cytometry analysis using Annexin V/PI staining revealed a significant increase in the total apoptosis rate (early + late apoptosis) in MRPS15-deficient SNU-398 and Huh-7 cells (Figure 6F,6G).

In vivo, MRPS15 deficiency impairs the subcutaneous tumor-forming capacity of HCC cells

To investigate the role of MRPS15 in tumorigenesis in vivo, we established a xenograft model by subcutaneously injecting Huh-7 cells with or without MRPS15 knockdown into nude mice. The tumor growth was markedly inhibited in the shMRPS15 group compared to the control group.

Representative images of the resected tumors at the endpoint clearly illustrated the reduction in tumor size upon MRPS15 depletion (Figure 7A). Consistent with this observation, the tumor growth curves, measured over 25 days, demonstrated a significant and sustained suppression of tumor growth in the knockdown group (Figure 7B). At the termination of the experiment, the tumors were excised and weighed. The average tumor weight in the shMRPS15 group was significantly lower than that in the control group (Figure 7C), further corroborating the inhibitory effect of MRPS15 silencing on tumor growth.

MRPS15 drives HCC progression by orchestrating oxidative metabolism and redox balance

To further investigate the biological functions associated with MRPS15 expression, we performed differential gene expression analysis and GSEA in HCC patients from TCGA cohort. Patients were stratified into high- and low-MRPS15 expression groups based on the top and bottom 30% of MRPS15 mRNA levels, respectively. Volcano plot analysis revealed a significant upregulation of multiple genes involved in mitochondrial OXPHOS, including ATP2A, PAGE1, MAGEA4, and ANKFN1, in the high-MRPS15 group (Figure 8A). Conversely, several immune-related and tumor suppressor genes such as CLEC4M and MT1B were downregulated. GSEA confirmed that the hallmark gene set “oxidative phosphorylation” was significantly enriched in the high-MRPS15 expression group (normalized enrichment score =0.67, false discovery rate q-value <0.05; Figure 8B). Additionally, other signaling pathways such as MYC targets and E2F targets were also enriched, suggesting that elevated MRPS15 may promote a pro-tumorigenic metabolic state characterized by enhanced mitochondrial respiration and cell proliferation. We first confirmed this link at the protein level. In SNU-398 and Huh-7 cells, MRPS15 knockdown specifically decreased the expression of key subunits from mitochondrial DNA-encoded OXPHOS complexes, including NDUFB8 (Complex I), UQCRC2 (Complex III), and ATP5A (Complex V). In contrast, the protein level of SDHB (Complex II), which is exclusively nuclear-encoded, remained unchanged, serving as a critical internal control (Figure 8C). This loss of OXPHOS components was accompanied by hallmarks of energetic stress: increased phosphorylation of AMPK (p-AMPK) and decreased phosphorylation of the ribosomal protein S6 (p-S6), indicating activation of catabolic and inhibition of anabolic signaling, respectively (Figure 8C). To functionally assess mitochondrial capacity, we performed Seahorse XF metabolic flux analyses. The mitochondrial stress test, performed by sequentially injecting oligomycin (ATP synthase inhibitor), FCCP (uncoupler), and rotenone/antimycin A (Complex I/III inhibitors), revealed that MRPS15 knockdown severely compromised OXPHOS in both SNU-398 and Huh-7 cells (Figure 8D,8E, left panels). Specifically, the knockdown cells exhibited significantly lower basal respiration (measured before oligomycin), proton leak (respiration after oligomycin minus non-mitochondrial respiration), ATP-linked respiration (basal minus oligomycin-inhibited respiration), and maximal respiratory capacity (FCCP-induced peak respiration minus non-mitochondrial respiration). This comprehensive impairment confirmed a profound defect in mitochondrial electron transport chain function and ATP production efficiency. Concurrently, the Glycolysis Stress Test, performed by sequentially injecting glucose, oligomycin, and 2-DG, demonstrated a compensatory upregulation of glycolytic metabolism upon MRPS15 knockdown (Figure 8D,8E, right panels). Both basal glycolysis (after glucose) and glycolytic capacity (after oligomycin) were significantly elevated, indicating that cells shifted towards glycolysis to meet energy demands when OXPHOS was impaired. Consistent with the observed respiratory defect, intracellular ATP levels were drastically reduced in MRPS15-knockdown cells from both lines (Figure 8F,8G). Notably, this mitochondrial dysfunction also perturbed redox balance. Using the DCFH-DA probe, we detected a significant increase in intracellular ROS in MRPS15-knockdown SNU-398 and Huh-7 cells compared to their respective controls (Figure 8H,8I). This suggests that the disruption of the electron transport chain led to increased electron leak and oxidative stress. In summary, these results establish that MRPS15 is a crucial regulator of mitochondrial metabolism in HCC. Its expression is necessary to maintain the integrity of the OXPHOS machinery, support efficient ATP production via OXPHOS, and prevent metabolic shift to glycolysis and deleterious ROS accumulation. Loss of MRPS15 triggers a state of bioenergetic and oxidative stress, compromising the fitness of liver cancer cells.

Restoration of OXPHOS function reverses the tumor-suppressive effects of MRPS15 knockdown

To establish a causal link between MRPS15-enhanced OXPHOS and HCC progression, we performed rescue experiments using the pharmacological OXPHOS activator bezafibrate. Consistent with our metabolic findings, MRPS15 knockdown in SNU-398 cells increased p-AMPK and decreased p-S6 levels, which were significantly reversed by bezafibrate treatment (Figure 9A). Notably, the co-administration of the ATP synthase inhibitor oligomycin abolished this restorative effect, confirming that Bezafibrate acted specifically through the OXPHOS pathway. Importantly, the protein levels of OXPHOS subunits (ATP5A, COX IV) remained low and unchanged across all knockdown groups, indicating that the rescue was mediated by functional activation rather than re-synthesis of the complexes (Figure 9A). Functionally, bezafibrate treatment largely restored the proliferative capacity of MRPS15-knockdown SNU-398 and Huh-7 cells at both 24 and 48 hours, as measured by CCK-8 assay (Figure 9B). This pro-proliferative effect was completely abrogated when oligomycin was added concurrently, underscoring the strict OXPHOS-dependence of the rescue (Figure 9B). Furthermore, bezafibrate markedly recovered the impaired invasive capability caused by MRPS15 knockdown, and the addition of oligomycin neutralized this effect of bezafibrate (Figure 9C,9D). Similarly, bezafibrate significantly restored the impaired migratory capability induced by MRPS15 deficiency, and this restorative effect was also abolished by oligomycin co-treatment (Figure 9E,9F). Finally, the high rate of apoptosis induced by MRPS15 knockdown was significantly suppressed by bezafibrate, and this protection was eliminated by oligomycin, as demonstrated by flow cytometry analysis (Figure 9G,9H). Collectively, these rescue experiments demonstrate that the oncogenic functions of MRPS15—promoting proliferation, invasion, migration, and survival—are mechanistically dependent on its ability to sustain OXPHOS. The specific reversibility of these phenotypes by OXPHOS activation, and their re-suppression by OXPHOS inhibition, provides definitive genetic and pharmacological evidence that MRPS15 drives HCC progression primarily through enhancing mitochondrial OXPHOS.

Discussion

This study successfully developed and validated a comprehensive MASM for HCC prognosis, identifying three distinct mitochondrial function subtypes with unique metabolic, genomic, and clinical characteristics. The MASM model, comprising eight mitochondrial-related genes, demonstrated robust predictive performance across multiple independent cohorts, achieving AUC values exceeding 0.74 for 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival predictions. When integrated with clinical parameters through nomogram construction, the model significantly outperformed traditional staging systems, establishing its potential for clinical implementation.

Several studies have developed metabolic or mitochondrial gene–based prognostic models in HCC, including a 10-gene metabolic signature validated in TCGA/ICGC/GEO cohorts (11), a 6-gene metabolism-associated model (12), and a 17-gene mitochondrial energy metabolism risk score (13). These models demonstrate the general prognostic relevance of metabolism in HCC. While direct numerical comparison of AUC and C-index is limited by differences in cohorts and validation strategies, the MASM model presented here shows comparable predictive performance and adds interpretations at the level of mitochondrial functional subtypes. Moreover, MASM exhibits prognostic independence beyond conventional clinical features and demonstrates consistent trends when compared with traditional staging in our validation analyses.

The identification of three mitochondrial function subtypes provides novel insights into the heterogeneous nature of mitochondrial dysfunction in HCC progression. Our findings reveal a complex metabolic reprogramming pattern: subtype A maintained robust OXPHOS capacity with favorable prognosis, representing metabolically stable tumors; subtype B exhibited the most severe OXPHOS suppression yet paradoxically demonstrated the best OS, indicating successful metabolic adaptation through alternative pathways; subtype C displayed selective metabolic activation despite widespread dysfunction, corresponding to the poorest outcomes (14). This counterintuitive relationship between OXPHOS activity and prognosis in subtype B challenges the traditional view that mitochondrial dysfunction directly correlates with poor outcomes. Instead, our results suggest that the capacity for metabolic reprogramming, rather than preserved mitochondrial function per se, may confer survival advantages. The dramatic elevation in glycerophospholipid metabolism observed in subtype C, despite overall metabolic suppression, indicates adaptive membrane biosynthesis responses to mitochondrial stress, potentially representing a compensatory mechanism that ultimately fails to prevent tumor progression.

These findings align with recent studies demonstrating the complex role of mitochondrial metabolism in cancer progression (15), but extend beyond previous work by establishing distinct prognostic subtypes based on comprehensive mitochondrial pathway analysis. While Ashton et al. (16) identified OXPHOS as an emerging therapeutic target, our study reveals that therapeutic success may depend more on targeting metabolic reprogramming capacity than OXPHOS activity itself. This interpretation is supported by the superior outcomes observed in subtype B despite severe OXPHOS suppression, suggesting that metabolic flexibility confers survival advantages independent of mitochondrial respiratory function.

The genomic characterization revealed striking differences in TP53 mutation frequencies across subtypes (20.83% in subtype A vs. 51.15% in subtype C), establishing a clear link between mitochondrial dysfunction severity and genomic instability. This observation supports previous evidence linking mitochondrial dysfunction to genomic alterations (17,18), but provides the first systematic demonstration of subtype-specific mutation patterns in HCC. The progressive increase in TP53 mutations from subtype A to C suggests that mitochondrial dysfunction may drive genomic instability through oxidative stress-mediated DNA damage, creating a vicious cycle that promotes tumor progression. This finding contradicts earlier assumptions that genomic instability is merely a consequence of advanced disease, instead positioning it as an integral component of mitochondrial dysfunction-driven carcinogenesis.

Our immune microenvironment analysis revealed distinct immunological landscapes associated with each mitochondrial subtype, with important therapeutic implications. Subtype A exhibited minimal immune infiltration characteristic of “cold” tumors, suggesting poor immunotherapy responsiveness but potential benefit from immune-activating strategies. Subtype C demonstrated the highest immune cell infiltration but with prominent immunosuppressive features, including elevated regulatory T cells and MDSCs, indicating potential responsiveness to immune checkpoint inhibitors combined with immunosuppressive targeting agents. These findings extend previous observations linking metabolic dysfunction to immune evasion (19,20) by providing specific immune profiles that can guide immunotherapy selection. The complex inflammatory milieu in subtype C, characterized by simultaneous activation of pro-inflammatory pathways and immunosuppressive mechanisms, may explain the aggressive behavior despite abundant immune infiltration.

The differential drug sensitivity patterns observed across subtypes provide a foundation for precision medicine approaches in HCC. Low-risk patients demonstrated enhanced responsiveness to multiple therapeutic agents, including tozasertib, osimertinib, and ABT737, consistent with preserved cellular stress responses and apoptotic machinery (21,22). Conversely, high-risk patients showed resistance to conventional therapies but selective sensitivity to specific drug categories, suggesting the need for alternative therapeutic strategies targeting their unique vulnerabilities. These findings support the growing evidence that metabolic status influences treatment efficacy and provide actionable guidance for treatment selection.

Functional validation of MRPS15 substantiated its oncogenic role through regulation of OXPHOS, ATP production, and ROS balance. While MRPS15 is a known mitochondrial ribosomal protein essential for OXPHOS assembly (23,24), our study extends its biological relevance into the oncogenic context of HCC through several layers of innovation. First, we establish MRPS15 as a novel prognostic driver in HCC, where its overexpression correlates with advanced stage and poor survival—an association previously unreported in this malignancy. Second, beyond confirming its necessity for OXPHOS, we demonstrate the sufficiency of OXPHOS activity in mediating MRPS15-driven tumor phenotypes. Using a pharmacological rescue approach, we showed that activating OXPHOS with bezafibrate reversed the anti-proliferative, anti-migratory, and pro-apoptotic effects of MRPS15 knockdown, and this rescue was specifically abolished by concurrent OXPHOS inhibition. This causal evidence positions MRPS15 not merely as a structural component but as a functional regulator of OXPHOS-dependent oncogenesis. Third, our data suggest that MRPS15 sustains a permissive OXPHOS functional state beyond mere subunit synthesis, as indicated by the restoration of cellular phenotypes without replenishment of OXPHOS protein levels. Finally, these findings propose MRPS15 as a potential biomarker for OXPHOS-dependent tumors and highlight the MRPS15-OXPHOS axis as a targetable vulnerability in HCC, offering a rationale for testing OXPHOS-directed therapies in MRPS15-high subgroups.

The main strengths of this study include its comprehensive multi-omics approach integrating metabolic, immune, and genomic analyses, validation across multiple independent cohorts using different platforms (25), and the development of a clinically applicable nomogram that outperforms existing prognostic systems. The identification of actionable therapeutic vulnerabilities for each subtype provides immediate clinical utility. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. The retrospective nature limits causal inference regarding mitochondrial dysfunction and disease progression. Reliance on transcriptomic data may miss important post-translational modifications and metabolic flux changes that affect mitochondrial function. Additionally, functional validation was limited to one gene, restricting comprehensive mechanistic confirmation of all identified pathways.

Our findings have significant clinical implications for HCC management. The MASM model provides superior patient stratification that could facilitate personalized treatment decisions, potentially improve outcomes while reducing unnecessary toxicity. Integration of mitochondrial signatures with clinical parameters offers enhanced prognostic accuracy beyond current staging systems. Future research should focus on prospective clinical validation of the MASM model, comprehensive functional characterization of all identified genes, and exploration of mitochondria-targeted therapeutic strategies (26,27). Direct assessment of mitochondrial function and metabolomics studies would provide additional validation of our transcriptomic findings and support the development of personalized treatment protocols for HCC based on mitochondrial functional status.

Conclusions

This study developed and validated a mitochondrial function-based classification system and an associated prognostic model (MASM) for HCC. We identified three molecularly and clinically distinct mitochondrial subtypes, each with unique metabolic, genomic, and immune features. The MASM model, comprising eight mitochondrial-related genes, reliably predicted patient survival and outperformed traditional staging methods. Functional characterization established MRPS15 as a key driver of HCC progression through sustaining OXPHOS and cellular redox balance. These findings position mitochondrial dysfunction as a central determinant of HCC heterogeneity and provide a framework for risk stratification and personalized therapeutic strategy. Prospective clinical studies are needed to translate these insights into routine oncology practice.
