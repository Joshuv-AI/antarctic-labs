Bivariate range functions with superior convergence order Journal: GMP 2026 To appear in GMP 2026 Proceedings. This arXiv version has additional experimental data in Appendix C.
Bingwei Zhang, Thomas Chen, Kai Hormann, Chee Yap

Abstract
Range functions are a fundamental tool for certified computations in geometric modeling, computer graphics, and robotics, but traditional range functions have only quadratic convergence order ( m = 2 m=2 ). For “superior” convergence order (i.e., m > 2 m>2 ), we exploit the Cornelius–Lohner framework in order to introduce new bivariate range functions based on Taylor, Lagrange, and Hermite interpolation. In particular, we focus on practical range functions with cubic and quartic convergence order. We implemented them in Julia and provide experimental validation of their performance in terms of efficiency and efficacy.

Many problems in geometric modeling, computer graphics,
and robotics, such as surface-surface intersection,

for isolating real and complex roots

for the problems mentioned before

,
23
]

In particular,

for the isotopic approximation of curves

,
1
,
2
]
,
and they can be used for finding the roots of mappings from

[

Clearly, the efficiency of these algorithms depends on
(1) the efficiency of
evaluating the range functions and
(2) the efficacy (or tightness) of the estimated ranges.
A simple measure of tightness is the order of convergence.
The classic approach to range functions for
f
is based on
the Taylor expansion of

to some degree

.
Unfortunately, the order of convergence
remains quadratic (
m=2
), regardless of

[

]
.

8

that finally achieves

due to the underlying implicit computational model,

Superior convergence orders have an efficiency cost.
So in any application, one must choose a “sweet spot”
convergence order that achieves practical trade-offs
among efficiency, efficacy, and ease of implementation.
Typically, the sweet spot is some small

, say
m<6
.
E.g., for the EVAL root isolation algorithm

[
13
]
,
such considerations led to the sweet spot order of

Thus, in our current

Although our techniques are in principle able to reach

below that their implementation will be much more involved.

In this paper, we focus on the bivariate setting
and derive new range functions that advance the state of the art
in terms of convergence order.
Specifically, we introduce bivariate range functions with
cubic and quartic convergence orders that are
based on Taylor, Lagrange, and Hermite interpolation.
The geometric modeling literature has a
well-known “Bernstein range”
for any polynomial

B

But our experiments will not compare this

We offer two justifications for this omission.
First, the Bernstein approach is fundamentally different from the
interpolation approach of our methods, since it works
only for polynomials and we know of no range functions
based on the Bernstein approach with
a superior convergence order.
Second, the comprehensive study of

17

compared a set of 30 range functions for
tracing planar curves, and they concluded that
the Bernstein method performs comparably to the
centered form Taylor method, but the latter is easier to implement.
This centered form Taylor method is just
{\!\;\framebox{\phantom{$I$\!\!\,}}\!\;}^{T}_{2,n}

5.5
Experimental limitations

Although Theorem
2

provides a complete CL theory that can be implemented
in practical arithmetic models (say, bigFloat libraries),
our implementation adopts the “standard expedient” of
computing with the IEEE arithmetic model (Julia’s Float64).
At present, our SLP implementation only supports
polynomial functions.
Nevertheless, the underlying theory developed in this paper
applies to general analytic functions.
More precisely,
to compute
{\!\;\framebox{\phantom{$I$\!\!\,}}\!\;}f(B)={\!\;\framebox{\phantom{$I$\!\!\,}}\!\;}g(B)+{\!\;\framebox{\phantom{$I$\!\!\,}}\!\;}R_{g}(B)
,
we compute
{\!\;\framebox{\phantom{$I$\!\!\,}}\!\;}g(B)
(and similarly for
{\!\;\framebox{\phantom{$I$\!\!\,}}\!\;}R_{g}(B)
)
as follows:
If
g(B)=[a,b]
and
g(x,y)
is a low degree polynomial,
then we have exact algebraic expressions
for
a
and
b
(see the Appendices).
Then a valid range function would be

{\!\;\framebox{\phantom{$I$\!\!\,}}\!\;}g(B)=[\tilde{a}-\varepsilon,\tilde{b}+\varepsilon]

where
\tilde{a},\tilde{b}
are IEEE evaluation of the expressions
for
a,b
and
{\lvert{a-\tilde{a}}\rvert},{\lvert{b-\tilde{b}}\rvert}\leq\varepsilon
.
Although
\varepsilon
could be
determined for any
g
, we took the shortcut of
ignoring this
\varepsilon
-correction.
Let us suppose that all these errors are less than some
\varepsilon_{0}
. Is this amount of error still “correct”? This question can only be answered in specific applications of these range functions.

6
Conclusion

This paper initiates the study of
range functions with superior convergence order for bivariate functions.
Our experimental study shows the practicality of these
functions for cubic and quartic convergence order.
Similar to the univariate setting
[
13
,
14
]
,
the sweet spot, that is, the best trade-off between efficiency and efficacy, and ease of implementation, seems to be the cubic convergence order, since fourth order methods give only marginally tighter ranges, are generally slower, and more complicated to implement (see
B.4
and
B.5
).

We conclude with some directions for future work:

1.

Clearly, the ideas presented in this paper extend to higher convergence orders
and to multivariate functions. In particular, Lagrange interpolation at the nodes of a regular
m\times m
grid with
m>3
can be used to define recursive Lagrange forms with order
m
convergence, and similarly for Hermite interpolation of higher order partial derivatives at the corners of the box.
However, the
practicality of such extensions may not be obvious
and ought to be explored.

2.

This paper reports the raw performance of our range functions
on individual boxes. A more holistic evaluation
would be to compare them in the context of an application.
As in
13
,
14
,
our Lagrange and Hermite forms are expected to
outshine Taylor forms in subdivision applications
such as curve tracing
[
20
,
1
,
2
]
.

We plan to write follow-up papers, in which we will also include details
about our SLP implementation.
Moreover, we will extend our framework so as to handle more general functions beyond polynomials.

3.

The concept of levels may also be exploited in applications.
For the root isolation application,

14

showed empirically that for several classes of polynomials,
for a given convergence order
m
, there is an optimal
level
n
(
m\leq n\leq d
) with optimal speedup.

Appendix A
Exact ranges of univariate polynomials with low degree

We recall the following results from
[
13
]
. Let

I=[a,b]
be a real interval with
radius

r=(b-a)/2
and

midpoint

m=(a+b)/2
. Given a polynomial
p
over
I
, let

\alpha_{0}\mathrel{:=}\min\{p(a),p(b)\},\qquad\beta_{0}\mathrel{:=}\max\{p(a),p(b)\}

be the minimal and the maximal value of
p
at the endpoints of
I
.

A.1
Linear polynomials

The exact range of the linear polynomial

p(x)=c_{0}+c_{1}(x-m)

is
p(I)=[\alpha_{0},\beta_{0}]
, because
p
is monotonic. The range can
also be expressed as

p(I)=c_{0}+r[-1,1]c_{1}.

A.2
Quadratic polynomials

To determine the exact range
p(I)=[\alpha,\beta]
of the quadratic
polynomial

p(x)=c_{0}+c_{1}(x-m)+c_{2}{(x-m)}^{2},

we first set
\alpha\mathrel{:=}\alpha_{0}
,
\beta\mathrel{:=}\beta_{0}
. Next, we observe
that the extremum of
p
occurs at

x^{\ast}=m-\frac{c_{1}}{2c_{2}},

which is inside
I
, if and only if
{\lvert{c_{1}}\rvert}<2{\lvert{c_{2}}\rvert}r
. If

x^{\ast}\notin I
, then
p
is monotonic on
I
and our initial
assignment of
\alpha
and
\beta
gives the correct range.
Otherwise, we check the sign of
c_{2}
to see if
p
has a minimum
(
c_{2}>0
) or a maximum (
c_{2}<0
) at
x^{\ast}
and accordingly replace

\alpha
or
\beta
with

p(x^{\ast})=c_{0}-\frac{c_{1}^{2}}{4c_{2}}.

Note that the special case of
p
being linear (or constant) with

c_{2}=0
is handled correctly by this procedure.

A.3
Cubic polynomials

To find the exact range
p(I)=[\alpha,\beta]
of the cubic polynomial

p(x)=c_{0}+c_{1}(x-m)+c_{2}{(x-m)}^{2}+c_{3}{(x-m)}^{3},

we distinguish between the case
c_{3}=0
, in which we use the procedure in

A.2
, and the case
c_{3}\neq 0
, in which we first set
\alpha\mathrel{:=}\alpha_{0}
and
\beta\mathrel{:=}\beta_{0}
. If the discriminant

\Delta=c_{2}^{2}-c_{1}c_{3}
of the quadratic equation
p^{\prime}(x)=0
is
non-positive, then
p
is monotonic on
I
and the initial assignment
of
\alpha
and
\beta
gives the correct range. Otherwise,
p
has a
local maximum at
x^{+}
and a local minimum at
x^{-}
, where

x^{\pm}=m-\frac{c_{2}\pm\sqrt{\Delta}}{3c_{3}}.

It remains to check if these stationary points lie inside
I
and to
adjust
\alpha
and
\beta
accordingly.

Appendix B
Exact ranges of bivariate polynomials with low degree

Let
B=[a_{x},b_{x}]\times[a_{y},b_{y}]
be a real, rectangular
box

with
midpoint

(m_{x},m_{y})=(a_{x}+b_{x},a_{y}+b_{y})/2
and
radii

(r_{x},r_{y})=(b_{x}-a_{x},b_{y}-a_{y})/2
. Given a bivariate polynomial
p

over
B
, let

\displaystyle\alpha_{0}

\displaystyle\mathrel{:=}\min\{p(a_{x},a_{y}),p(a_{x},b_{y}),p(b_{x},a_{y}),p(b_{x},b_{y})\},

\displaystyle\beta_{0}

\displaystyle\mathrel{:=}\max\{p(a_{x},a_{y}),p(a_{x},b_{y}),p(b_{x},a_{y}),p(b_{x},b_{y})\}

be the minimal and the maximal value of
p
at the corners of
B
.

B.1
Linear polynomials

The exact range of the linear polynomial

p(x,y)=c_{0,0}+c_{1,0}(x-m_{x})+c_{0,1}(y-m_{y})

is
p(B)=[\alpha_{0},\beta_{0}]
, because
p
is monotonic in
x
and in

y
. The range can also be expressed as

p(B)=c_{0,0}+[-1,1](r_{x}{\lvert{c_{1,0}}\rvert}+r_{y}{\lvert{c_{0,1}}\rvert}),

and if
B
is a
square
with radius
r=r_{x}=r_{y}
, then this
simplifies to

p(B)=c_{0,0}+r[-1,1]({\lvert{c_{1,0}}\rvert}+{\lvert{c_{0,1}}\rvert}).

For example, if
p
is the linear Taylor polynomial
T_{1}
of
f

about the midpoint
\bm{m}=(m_{x},m_{y})
of
B
, then the coefficients to
be used in the procedure above are

c_{0,0}=f(\bm{m}),\qquad c_{1,0}=f^{(1,0)}(\bm{m}),\qquad c_{0,1}=f^{(0,1)}(\bm{m}).

B.2
Quadratic polynomials

To determine the exact range
p(B)=[\alpha,\beta]
of the quadratic
polynomial

\displaystyle p(x,y)=c_{0,0}

\displaystyle+c_{1,0}(x-m_{x})+c_{0,1}(y-m_{y})

\displaystyle+c_{2,0}{(x-m_{x})}^{2}+c_{1,1}(x-m_{x})(y-m_{y})+c_{0,2}{(y-m_{y})}^{2},

we initially set
\alpha\mathrel{:=}\alpha_{0}
,
\beta\mathrel{:=}\beta_{0}
and then
search for extrema on the boundary of
B
. To this end, we consider
the four univariate quadratic polynomials

\displaystyle p_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=p(x,a_{y}),

\displaystyle\qquad x

\displaystyle\in I_{1}=[a_{x},b_{x}],

\displaystyle p_{2}(x)

\displaystyle=p(x,b_{y}),

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle\in I_{2}=[a_{x},b_{x}],

\displaystyle p_{3}(y)

\displaystyle=p(a_{x},y),

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle\in I_{3}=[a_{y},b_{y}],

\displaystyle p_{4}(y)

\displaystyle=p(b_{x},y),

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle\in I_{4}=[a_{y},b_{y}],

analyse them as described in
A.2
,
and update
\alpha
and
\beta
accordingly.

To further check for extrema inside
B
, we compute the

discriminant
of
p
,

D_{p}=\begin{vmatrix}p_{xx}&p_{xy}\\
p_{yx}&p_{yy}\end{vmatrix}=4c_{2,0}c_{0,2}-c_{1,1}^{2}.

If
D_{p}<0
, then the unique stationary point of
p
is a saddle
point, and if
D_{p}=0
, then
p
either has no stationary points or
there exists a line of infinitely many stationary points with a
common critical value. In both cases we can be sure that
p
obtains
its minimal and maximal value on the boundary of
B
, and the exact
range
p(B)
is correctly determined by the previous boundary
analysis.

If
D_{p}>0
, then
p
has a unique stationary point at

(x^{\ast},y^{\ast})=(m_{x},m_{y})-\frac{(2c_{1,0}c_{0,2}-c_{0,1}c_{1,1},2c_{0,1}c_{2,0}-c_{1,0}c_{1,1})}{D_{p}},

which is inside
B
, if and only if

{\lvert{2c_{1,0}c_{0,2}-c_{0,1}c_{1,1}}\rvert}<D_{p}r_{x}\qquad\text{and}\qquad{\lvert{2c_{0,1}c_{2,0}-c_{1,0}c_{1,1}}\rvert}<D_{p}r_{y}.

In this case, we check the sign of
c_{2,0}
to see if
p
has a
minimum (
c_{2,0}>0
) or a maximum (
c_{2,0}<0
) at
(x^{\ast},y^{\ast})

and accordingly replace
\alpha
or
\beta
with

p(x^{\ast},y^{\ast})=c_{0,0}-\frac{c_{1,0}^{2}c_{0,2}-c_{1,0}c_{0,1}c_{1,1}+c_{0,1}^{2}c_{2,0}}{D_{p}}.

Note that the special case of
p
being linear (or constant) with

c_{2,0}=c_{1,1}=c_{0,2}=0
is handled correctly by this procedure.

For example, if
p
is the quadratic Taylor polynomial
T_{2}
of
f

about the midpoint
\bm{m}=(m_{x},m_{y})
of
B
, then the coefficients to
be used in the procedure above are

\displaystyle c_{0,0}

\displaystyle=f(\bm{m}),

\displaystyle\qquad c_{1,0}

\displaystyle=f^{(1,0)}(\bm{m}),

\displaystyle\qquad c_{0,1}

\displaystyle=f^{(0,1)}(\bm{m}),

\displaystyle c_{2,0}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}f^{(2,0)}(\bm{m}),

\displaystyle c_{1,1}

\displaystyle=f^{(1,1)}(\bm{m}),

\displaystyle c_{0,2}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}f^{(0,2)}(\bm{m}).

B.3
Biquadratic polynomials

The exact range of the biquadratic polynomial

\displaystyle p(x,y)=c_{0,0}

\displaystyle+c_{1,0}(x-m_{x})+c_{0,1}(y-m_{y})

\displaystyle+c_{2,0}{(x-m_{x})}^{2}+c_{1,1}(x-m_{x})(y-m_{y})+c_{0,2}{(y-m_{y})}^{2}

\displaystyle+c_{2,1}{(x-m_{x})}^{2}(y-m_{y})+c_{1,2}(x-m_{x}){(y-m_{y})}^{2}+c_{2,2}{(x-m_{x})}^{2}{(y-m_{x})}^{2}

can only be computed numerically, since determining the stationary
points of
p
requires finding the roots of a polynomial of degree
five. Instead, we propose to split
p
into the quadratic Taylor
polynomial
q
of
p
about
\bm{m}
and the remainder
r=p-q
, that
is,

\displaystyle r(x,y)=c_{2,1}{(x-m_{x})}^{2}(y-m_{y})+c_{1,2}(x-m_{x}){(y-m_{y})}^{2}+c_{2,2}{(x-m_{x})}^{2}{(y-m_{x})}^{2},

and to approximate the range of
p
by
q(B)+r(B)\supseteq p(B)
.
While the range of
q
can be computed as described in

B.2
, a careful analysis of
r

(finding the stationary points, checking the discriminant, etc.)
reveals that it does not have isolated local extrema. The range of

r
can hence be found by analyzing
r
over the boundary of
B
, as
explained in the first step of the procedure in

B.2
.

For example, if
p
is the biquadratic Lagrange interpolant of
f
at

\{a_{x},m_{x},b_{x}\}\times\{a_{y},m_{y},b_{y}\}
, then the coefficients of
p

are

\displaystyle c_{0,0}=f_{1,1},\qquad c_{1,0}=\frac{f_{2,1}-f_{0,1}}{2r_{x}},\qquad c_{0,1}=\frac{f_{1,2}-f_{1,0}}{2r_{y}},

\displaystyle c_{2,0}=\frac{f_{2,1}-2f_{1,1}+f_{0,1}}{2r_{x}^{2}},\qquad c_{1,1}=\frac{f_{2,2}-f_{0,2}-f_{2,0}+f_{0,0}}{4r_{x}r_{y}},\qquad c_{0,2}=\frac{f_{1,2}-2f_{1,1}+f_{1,0}}{2r_{y}^{2}},

\displaystyle c_{2,1}=\frac{f_{2,2}-2f_{1,2}+f_{0,2}-f_{2,0}+2f_{1,0}-f_{0,0}}{4r_{x}^{2}r_{y}},\qquad c_{1,2}=\frac{f_{2,2}-2f_{2,1}+f_{2,0}-f_{0,2}+2f_{0,1}-f_{0,0}}{4r_{x}r_{y}^{2}}

\displaystyle c_{2,2}=\frac{f_{2,2}-2f_{1,2}+f_{0,2}-2f_{2,1}+4f_{1,1}-2f_{0,1}+f_{2,0}-2f_{1,0}+f_{0,0}}{4r_{x}^{2}r_{y}^{2}},

where

f_{i,j}=f(m_{x}+(i-1)r_{x},m_{y}+(j-1)r_{y}),\qquad 0\leq i,j\leq 2.

B.4
Cubic polynomials

To determine the exact range
p(B)=[\alpha,\beta]
of the cubic
polynomial

\displaystyle p(x,y)=c_{0,0}

\displaystyle+c_{1,0}(x-m_{x})+c_{0,1}(y-m_{y})

\displaystyle+c_{2,0}{(x-m_{x})}^{2}+c_{1,1}(x-m_{x})(y-m_{y})+c_{0,2}{(y-m_{y})}^{2}

\displaystyle+c_{3,0}{(x-m_{x})}^{3}+c_{2,1}{(x-m_{x})}^{2}(y-m_{y})+c_{1,2}(x-m_{x}){(y-m_{y})}^{2}+c_{0,3}{(y-m_{x})}^{3},

we initially set
\alpha\mathrel{:=}\alpha_{0}
,
\beta\mathrel{:=}\beta_{0}
and then
search for extrema on the boundary of
B
, adjusting
\alpha
and

\beta
accordingly, like in
B.2
, but
taking into account that the four polynomials are cubic and have to
be analysed with the procedure in
A.3
.
To further check for extrema inside
B
, we have to determine the
common roots of the first order partial derivatives of
p
. To this
end, we express the latter as polynomials in
x
,

A(x)\mathrel{:=}p^{(1,0)}(x+m_{x},y+m_{y})=a_{0}x^{2}+a_{1}x+a_{2},\qquad B(x)\mathrel{:=}p^{(0,1)}(x+m_{x},y+m_{y})=b_{0}x^{2}+b_{1}x+b_{2},

with coefficients in
y
,

\displaystyle a_{0}

\displaystyle\mathrel{:=}3c_{3,0},

\displaystyle a_{1}

\displaystyle\mathrel{:=}2c_{2,0}+2c_{2,1}y,

\displaystyle a_{2}

\displaystyle\mathrel{:=}c_{1,0}+c_{1,1}y+c_{1,2}y^{2},

\displaystyle b_{0}

\displaystyle\mathrel{:=}c_{2,1},

\displaystyle b_{1}

\displaystyle\mathrel{:=}c_{1,1}+2c_{1,2}y,

\displaystyle b_{2}

\displaystyle\mathrel{:=}c_{0,1}+2c_{0,2}y+3c_{0,3}y^{2},

and consider the determinant of the Sylvester matrix,

\begin{vmatrix}a_{0}&a_{1}&a_{2}&0\\
0&a_{0}&a_{1}&a_{2}\\
b_{0}&b_{1}&b_{2}&0\\
0&b_{0}&b_{1}&b_{2}\end{vmatrix}={(a_{0}b_{2}-a_{2}b_{0})}^{2}-(a_{0}b_{1}-a_{1}b_{0})(a_{1}b_{2}-a_{2}b_{1}).

The roots of this quartic polynomial (in
y
) are the
y
-coordinates
of the stationary points of
p
, and they can be found, for example,
with
Ferrari’s method
. To find the corresponding

x
-coordinates, we need to substitute each of the (up to four) roots
into the coefficients of
A(x)
and solve for
x
. While
A

certainly vanishes at the resulting (up to eight) pairs of
coordinates, we must further check that also
B(x)
evaluates to
zero, which reduces the number of stationary points of
p
to at most
four, as guaranteed by Bézout’s theorem. For those stationary
points that lie inside
B
, we finally use the second derivative test
to find out if they are local extrema and update
\alpha
and
\beta

accordingly.

For example, if
p
is the cubic Taylor polynomial
T_{3}
of
f
about
the midpoint
\bm{m}=(m_{x},m_{y})
of
B
, then the coefficients to be
used in the procedure above are

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} c_{0,0}&=f(\bm{m}),&\qquad c_{1,0}&=f^{(1,0)}(\bm{m}),&\qquad c_{0,1}&=f^{(0,1)}(\bm{m}),\\
c_{2,0}&=\tfrac{1}{2}f^{(2,0)}(\bm{m}),&c_{1,1}&=f^{(1,1)}(\bm{m}),&c_{0,2}&=\tfrac{1}{2}f^{(0,2)}(\bm{m}).\end{aligned}

\displaystyle c_{3,0}=\tfrac{1}{6}f^{(3,0)}(\bm{m}),\qquad c_{2,1}=\tfrac{1}{2}f^{(2,1)}(\bm{m}),\qquad c_{1,2}=\tfrac{1}{2}f^{(1,2)}(\bm{m}),\qquad c_{0,3}=\tfrac{1}{6}f^{(0,3)}(\bm{m}).

B.5
Bicubic polynomials

For a bicubic polynomial

\displaystyle p(x,y)=c_{0,0}

\displaystyle+c_{1,0}(x-m_{x})+c_{0,1}(y-m_{y})

\displaystyle+c_{2,0}{(x-m_{x})}^{2}+c_{1,1}(x-m_{x})(y-m_{y})+c_{0,2}{(y-m_{y})}^{2}

\displaystyle+c_{3,0}{(x-m_{x})}^{3}+c_{2,1}{(x-m_{x})}^{2}(y-m_{y})+c_{1,2}(x-m_{x}){(y-m_{y})}^{2}+c_{0,3}{(y-m_{x})}^{3}

\displaystyle+c_{3,1}{(x-m_{x})}^{3}(y-m_{y})+c_{2,2}{(x-m_{x})}^{2}{(y-m_{y})}^{2}+c_{1,3}(x-m_{x}){(y-m_{x})}^{3}

\displaystyle+c_{3,2}{(x-m_{x})}^{3}{(y-m_{y})}^{2}+c_{2,3}{(x-m_{x})}^{2}{(y-m_{y})}^{3}+c_{3,3}{(x-m_{x})}^{3}{(y-m_{y})}^{3},

we proceed as for biquadratic polynomials in

B.3
. We split
p
into the cubic Taylor
polynomial
q
of
p
about
\bm{m}
and the remainder
r=p-q
, that
is,

\displaystyle r(x,y)

\displaystyle=c_{3,1}{(x-m_{x})}^{3}(y-m_{y})+c_{2,2}{(x-m_{x})}^{2}{(y-m_{y})}^{2}+c_{1,3}(x-m_{x}){(y-m_{x})}^{3}

\displaystyle+c_{3,2}{(x-m_{x})}^{3}{(y-m_{y})}^{2}+c_{2,3}{(x-m_{x})}^{2}{(y-m_{y})}^{3}+c_{3,3}{(x-m_{x})}^{3}{(y-m_{y})}^{3},

and approximate the range of
p
by
q(B)+r(B)\supseteq p(B)
.
While the range of
q
can be computed as described in

B.4
, a slight variation of this procedure
can be used to determine the range of
r
. The restriction of
r
to
the boundary of
B
gives four cubic polynomials, whose extrema are
determined in the same way. To find the extrema of
r
inside
B
, we
observe that the first order partial derivatives of
r
are

r^{(1,0)}(x+m_{x},y+m_{y})=yA(x),\qquad r^{(0,1)}(x+m_{x},y+m_{y})=xB(x),

where

A(x)\mathrel{:=}a_{0}x^{2}+a_{1}x+a_{2},\qquad B(x)\mathrel{:=}b_{0}x^{2}+b_{1}x+b_{2}

are polynomials in
x
with coefficients in
y
,

\displaystyle a_{0}

\displaystyle\mathrel{:=}3c_{3,1}+3c_{3,2}y+3c_{3,3}y^{2},

\displaystyle a_{1}

\displaystyle\mathrel{:=}2c_{2,2}y+2c_{2,3}y^{2},

\displaystyle a_{2}

\displaystyle\mathrel{:=}c_{1,3}y^{2},

\displaystyle b_{0}

\displaystyle\mathrel{:=}c_{3,1}+2c_{3,2}y+3c_{3,3}y^{2},

\displaystyle b_{1}

\displaystyle\mathrel{:=}2c_{2,2}y+3c_{2,3}y^{2},

\displaystyle b_{2}

\displaystyle\mathrel{:=}3c_{1,3}y^{2}.

The determinant of the corresponding Sylvester matrix is
y^{4}D(y)
,
where
D
is a quartic polynomial, leading to at most four stationary
points of
r
, which need to be further checked. The additional
stationary point of
r
at

can be ignored, because

r(m_{x},m_{y})=0

r(x,m_{y})=r(m_{x},y)=0
for any

From the data in Table
4
, we again observe that the efficacy (tightness) is greater than
1
for all methods of higher convergence order, as expected. In this additional set of examples, the efficacy ratios lie in the range
[1.0669,1.1606]
. For
{\!\;\framebox{\phantom{$I$\!\!\,}}\!\;}^{\mathrm{T}}_{3}
, this improvement in tightness is achieved without sacrificing efficiency: its speedup ratios range from
1.06
to
1.35
, and its memory usage remains very close to that of the baseline
{\!\;\framebox{\phantom{$I$\!\!\,}}\!\;}^{\mathrm{T}}_{2}
. For
{\!\;\framebox{\phantom{$I$\!\!\,}}\!\;}^{\mathrm{T}}_{4}
, the efficacy is slightly better than that of
{\!\;\framebox{\phantom{$I$\!\!\,}}\!\;}^{\mathrm{T}}_{3}
, but the gain is marginal, while the running time and memory usage are somewhat worse. Thus, as in the main text, the improvement from order
m=3
to
m=4
appears to be relatively modest.

By contrast, for
{\!\;\framebox{\phantom{$I$\!\!\,}}\!\;}^{\mathrm{L}}_{3}
and
{\!\;\framebox{\phantom{$I$\!\!\,}}\!\;}^{\mathrm{H}}_{4}
without sharing, the tradeoff between tightness and efficiency is again clearly visible. Their efficacy is competitive and often slightly better than that of the Taylor forms of the same order, but this comes at a noticeable runtime cost, especially for
{\!\;\framebox{\phantom{$I$\!\!\,}}\!\;}^{\mathrm{H}}_{4}
, whose speedup drops to
0.34
on the octic-flower example and whose memory usage becomes substantially larger. However, once data sharing is enabled,
{\!\;\framebox{\phantom{$I$\!\!\,}}\!\;}^{\mathrm{L}}_{3}
becomes the most efficient method overall in these experiments: its speedup ratios are close to
2
for all three examples, while its memory usage is reduced dramatically. The shared version of
{\!\;\framebox{\phantom{$I$\!\!\,}}\!\;}^{\mathrm{H}}_{4}
also benefits substantially from reuse, although for the more complicated octic-flower example it still remains slower and considerably more memory-intensive than the other methods.

Therefore, these additional experiments lead to the same qualitative conclusions as those in Table
2
:
(1) the efficacy generally improves with the convergence order, but the improvement from
m=3
to
m=4
is limited;
(2) among the methods without sharing,
{\!\;\framebox{\phantom{$I$\!\!\,}}\!\;}^{\mathrm{T}}_{3}
offers the best balance between tighter enclosures and computational efficiency; and
(3) with data sharing,
{\!\;\framebox{\phantom{$I$\!\!\,}}\!\;}^{\mathrm{L}}_{3}
is again the most efficient method in terms of both runtime and memory usage.
