Braided quantum SU ⁡ ( 2 ) \mathrm{SU}(2) group – a case study
Jacek Krajczok, Piotr M. Sołtan

Abstract
. We continue the study of the braided compact quantum group SU q ​ ( 2 ) \mathrm{SU}_{q}(2) for complex q q satisfying 0 < | q | < 1 0<|q|<1 introduced by Kasprzak, Meyer, Roy and Woronowicz ( [ 19 ] ). We address such aspects as existence of the Haar measure, construct the scaling group, the antipode and its polar decomposition and describe the related braided Hopf algebra. We also study when the braided flip extends to a completely bounded map and establish equivalence between the two approaches to bosonization and braided tensor product taken in the literature ( [ 19 ] vs. [ 30 , 36 , 11 ] ).

1.
Introduction

The general theory of compact quantum groups was introduced in
[
43
]
as an extension of the theory of compact groups to the framework of non-commutative topology (let us also note here that the paper
[
43
]
circulated in preprint form many years before its actual publication). Later the scope of the theory was enlarged to encompass locally compact objects (
[
25
,
28
]
). A prime example of compact quantum group is the quantum group
\mathrm{SU}_{q}(2)
(
[
39
]
), with
real
deformation parameter satisfying
0<|q|<1
. It is described by the universal unital
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra
\C(\mathrm{SU}_{q}(2))
generated by elements
\alpha
and
\gamma
making the matrix
u=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\alpha&-q\gamma^{*}\\
\gamma&\alpha^{*}\end{smallmatrix}\right]
unitary, together with a comultiplication
\C(\mathrm{SU}_{q}(2))\to\C(\mathrm{SU}_{q}(2))\otimes\C(\mathrm{SU}_{q}(2))
characterized by the property that
u
is a unitary representation of
\mathrm{SU}_{q}(2)
on
\mathbb{C}^{2}
. The authors of
[
19
]
extended this construction to
complex
deformation parameter
0<|q|<1
– this time however,
\mathrm{SU}_{q}(2)
is a
braided
compact quantum group. This means that the comultiplication is a map
\C(\mathrm{SU}_{q}(2))\to\C(\mathrm{SU}_{q}(2))\boxtimes\C(\mathrm{SU}_{q}(2))
, where
\boxtimes
is the braided tensor product – a construction of which depends on
q
and an action of
\mathbb{T}
on
\C(\mathrm{SU}_{q}(2))
(alternatively we might look at the action of Drinfeld double
\D(\mathbb{T})
on
\C(\mathrm{SU}_{q}(2))
, see Section
2
and Appendix
A.1
). This objects fits into a more general framework of
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebraic braided quantum groups studied in
[
42
,
31
,
36
]
and was also studied in the framework of quantum groups with projection on the von Neumann algebra level in
[
21
]
(see also
[
20
]
).

In our work we continue the study of the braided compact quantum group
\mathrm{SU}_{q}(2)
undertaken in
[
19
]
. Our results go roughly in three directions.

First, we show that the dense unital
*

\Pol(\mathrm{SU}_{q}(2))
generated by
\alpha
and
\gamma
coincides with the space spanned by matrix coefficients of finite dimensional unitary representations (Proposition
3.24
). On this space we can introduce a version of the antipode map
S
and a braided flip
\chi^{\boxtimes}
. Together with the counit (Proposition
3.21
) it can be understood as a braided Hopf algebra, i.e. a Hopf algebra in the braided monoidal category
\sideset{{}_{\mathbb{Z}}^{\mathbb{Z}}}{}{\operatorname{\mathcal{YD}}}
of Yetter-Drinfeld modules over

acts in the way known from non-braided setting on matrix coefficients of representations (Proposition

Next, as in the non-braided setting, we introduce scaling automorphisms
(\tau_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
which form a group of automorphisms of
\C(\mathrm{SU}_{q}(2))
, and define the unitary antipode
R
which is a bounded map on
\C(\mathrm{SU}_{q}(2))
(sections

3.7

Finally, we study the Haar measure
{\boldsymbol{h}}
on
\mathrm{SU}_{q}(2)
which is defined via the bosonization (see Section

\D(\mathbb{T})
-invariant KMS state on
\C(\mathrm{SU}_{q}(2))
, and using it one can define the von Neumann algebra
\Linf(\mathrm{SU}_{q}(2))
via the GNS representation. The Haar measure is left and right invariant:
(\mathrm{id}\boxtimes{\boldsymbol{h}})\Delta_{A}(a)={\boldsymbol{h}}(a)\mathds{1}=({\boldsymbol{h}}\boxtimes\mathrm{id})\Delta_{A}(a)
for all

. We provide two arguments for this result.

In the first appendix, we carefully check that the two approaches to braided tensor product (the one used in

]

For a locally compact quantum group
\mathbb{G}
, we denote by

\mathbb{G}\ni g\mapsto\alpha_{g}\in\operatorname{Aut}(\mathsf{A})
, where
\alpha_{g}=(\operatorname{ev}_{g^{-1}}\otimes\mathrm{id})\circ\alpha
. The inverse in this formula is needed, so that

is multiplicative. We will stick to this convention throughout the paper.

If
\mathbb{G}=\D(\mathbb{H})
is the Drinfeld double of a locally compact quantum group

(

[

]

[

in which this condition simplifies: the action
{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{\mathsf{A}}\in\Mor(\mathsf{A},\D(\mathbb{T})\otimes\mathsf{A})
corresponds to a pair of commuting actions
\rho^{\mathsf{A}}\in\Mor(\mathsf{A},\C(\mathbb{T})\otimes\mathsf{A})
and
\tilde{\rho}^{\mathsf{A}}\in\Mor(\mathsf{A},\mathrm{c}_{0}(\mathbb{Z})\otimes\mathsf{A})

to the closed subgroups

,

1.1.
Further notational conventions

Whenever
X
and
Y
are subspaces of an algebra, the symbol
XY
will denote
XY=\operatorname{span}\{xy\,|\,x\in X,\>y\in Y\}
.

In the first paper
[
42
]
dealing with braided tensor products on
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra level (referred to “crossed products” in that article) written by S.L. Woronowicz, a central role was played by the inclusion morphisms of the factor
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras into the braided product. The notation used in that paper was
j_{1}\in\Mor(\mathsf{A},\mathsf{A}\otimes_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{C}}\!\mathsf{B})
and
j_{2}\in\Mor(\mathsf{B},\mathsf{A}\otimes_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{C}}\!\mathsf{B})
. The theory of braided tensor products of
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras was further developed in
[
19
,
30
,
31
]
and
[
11
]
, where the notation for the braided product utilizes the symbol
\boxtimes
(or
\boxtimes_{\zeta}
in
[
19
]
, see Section
A.1
) and variously
\iota_{1}
,
\iota_{2}
etc. or
\iota_{A}
,
\iota_{B}
for the inclusion morphisms. The useful convention of denoting the inclusion morphism with a symbol which contains a symbol designating the
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra in question, like
\iota_{\mathsf{A}}
, adopted e.g. in
[
30
,
31
,
11
]
is not sufficiently precise when we are dealing with the braided tensor product of a given
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra
\mathsf{A}
with itself:
\mathsf{A}\boxtimes\mathsf{A}
. In that case the notation
j_{1}
and
j_{2}
seems more appropriate, but it becomes increasingly clumsy when multiple braided products are considered. The solution of using notation of the form
\iota_{\mathsf{A},1}
,
\iota_{\mathsf{B},3}
etc. (e.g. to denote inclusion morphisms from
\mathsf{A}
and
\mathsf{B}
into
\mathsf{A}\boxtimes\mathsf{B}\boxtimes\mathsf{B}
) solves the notational dilemmas, but is rather heavy and most of the time unnecessary. We propose to pass seamlessly between the convention of including the symbol for the
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra in the notation and only indicating its position in the braided product depending on the context. Although this is not a coherent notational scheme, we feel it will not cause any confusion to the reader, as the context always clearly explains which algebra is being included at what position in the braided tensor product. Sometimes (e.g. in the proof of Proposition
A.9
) we will use a combination of these two variants of notation simultaneously.

2.
The braided quantum group
\mathrm{SU}_{q}(2)

2.1.
Definition

For
q\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
the (
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebraic) braided compact quantum group
\mathrm{SU}_{q}(2)
was introduced in
[
19
]
: the
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra
A
is defined as the universal unital
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra generated by
\alpha,\gamma
such that

(2.1)

\displaystyle\alpha^{*}\alpha+\gamma^{*}\gamma

\displaystyle=\mathds{1},

\displaystyle\alpha\gamma

\displaystyle=\overline{q}\gamma\alpha,

\displaystyle\alpha\alpha^{*}+|q|^{2}\gamma^{*}\gamma

\displaystyle=\mathds{1},

\displaystyle\gamma\gamma^{*}

\displaystyle=\gamma^{*}\gamma.

or, equivalently, the matrix
\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\alpha&-q\gamma^{*}\\
\gamma&\alpha^{*}\end{smallmatrix}\right]
is unitary (note that the relation
\alpha\gamma^{*}=q\gamma^{*}\alpha
follows from the remaining ones, see e.g.
[
37
, Remark 3.1]
). The
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra
A
could also be denoted by the symbol
\C(\mathrm{SU}_{q}(2))
, but we will stick to
A
in this paper to lighten the notation. Before defining the comultiplication we need to introduce the action
\rho^{A}
of
\mathbb{T}
on
A
:

(2.2)

\rho^{A}(\alpha)=\mathds{1}\otimes\alpha,\quad\rho^{A}(\gamma)={\boldsymbol{z}}\otimes\gamma

where
{\boldsymbol{z}}
is the canonical generator of
\C(\mathbb{T})
. This way
A
becomes an object of the category of
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras endowed with an action of
\mathbb{T}
(and equivariant morphisms of
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras) on which the
braided tensor product
is defined. This construction depends on the choice of number
\zeta\in\mathbb{T}
– we discuss this notion and its more general version in detail in Appendix
A.1
. At this point let us use the simplified approach to the braided tensor product as presented in
[
19
, Section 1]
: first we put
\zeta=q/\overline{q}
(where
q
is the deformation parameter in (
2.1
)). Then, given two
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras
\mathsf{X}
and
\mathsf{Y}
equipped with actions
\rho^{\mathsf{X}}
and
\rho^{\mathsf{Y}}
of
\mathbb{T}
, we define
\iota_{\mathsf{X}}\in\Mor(\mathsf{X},\C(\mathbb{T}^{2}_{\zeta})\otimes\mathsf{X}\otimes\mathsf{Y})
and
\iota_{\mathsf{Y}}\in\Mor(\mathsf{Y},\C(\mathbb{T}^{2}_{\zeta})\otimes\mathsf{X}\otimes\mathsf{Y})
by

\iota_{\mathsf{X}}(x)=U^{n}\otimes x\otimes\mathds{1},\quad\iota_{\mathsf{Y}}(y)=V^{m}\otimes\mathds{1}\otimes y,

where
x\in\mathsf{X}
and
y\in\mathsf{Y}
are homogeneous elements of degrees
n
and
m
respectively (
\rho^{\mathsf{X}}(x)={\boldsymbol{z}}^{n}\otimes x
,
\rho^{\mathsf{Y}}(y)={\boldsymbol{z}}^{m}\otimes y
) and
\C(\mathbb{T}^{2}_{\zeta})
is the quantum torus:

\C(\mathbb{T}^{2}_{\zeta})=\mathrm{C}^{*}\bigl\langle U,V,\mathds{1}\,\bigr|\bigl.\,U^{*}U=UU^{*}=V^{*}V=VV^{*}=\mathds{1},\>UV=\zeta VU\bigr\rangle.

Then the
braided tensor product

\mathsf{X}\boxtimes\mathsf{Y}
of
\mathsf{X}
and
\mathsf{Y}
is defined as the closed linear span of elements of the form
\iota_{\mathsf{X}}(x)\iota_{\mathsf{Y}}(y)
in
\M(\C(\mathbb{T}^{2}_{\zeta})\otimes\mathsf{X}\otimes\mathsf{Y})
, in symbols
\mathsf{X}\boxtimes\mathsf{Y}=\overline{\iota_{\mathsf{X}}(\mathsf{X})\iota_{\mathsf{Y}}(\mathsf{Y})}
. Having defined
\mathsf{X}\boxtimes\mathsf{Y}
we consider
\iota_{\mathsf{X}}
and
\iota_{\mathsf{Y}}
as morphisms from
\mathsf{X}
and
\mathsf{Y}
to
\mathsf{X}\boxtimes\mathsf{Y}
respectively. By definition

\iota_{\mathsf{X}}(x)\iota_{\mathsf{Y}}(y)=\zeta^{\deg(x)\deg(y)}\iota_{\mathsf{Y}}(y)\iota_{\mathsf{X}}(x)

for homogeneous
x\in\mathsf{X}
and
y\in\mathsf{Y}
of degrees respectively
\deg(x),\deg(y)\in\mathbb{Z}
.

The comultiplication on
A
is the unital
*
-homomorphism
\Delta_{A}
from
A
to
A\boxtimes A
defined by

\Delta_{A}(\alpha)=\iota_{1}(\alpha)\iota_{2}(\alpha)-q\iota_{1}(\gamma^{*})\iota_{2}(\gamma),\quad\Delta_{A}(\gamma)=\iota_{1}(\gamma)\iota_{2}(\alpha)+\iota_{1}(\alpha^{*})\iota_{2}(\gamma).

As explained in Section
1.1
, we have switched to the notation
\iota_{1}
,
\iota_{2}
as in the case of braided tensor product
A\boxtimes A
having the same factors, the symbol
\iota_{A}
becomes ambiguous. The braided tensor product is a bifunctor on the category of
\mathbb{T}
-
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras and hence we can take braided tensor product of (equivariant) morphisms (we can take several versions of morphisms, in particular equivariant c.p. maps – see
[
30
, Section 5.2]
and
[
11
, Proposition 5.1]
). This allows us to state that
\Delta_{A}
is coassociative:
(\Delta_{A}\boxtimes\mathrm{id})\circ\Delta_{A}=(\mathrm{id}\otimes\Delta_{A})\circ\Delta_{A}
. Moreover the “cancellation laws” hold, i.e.
\overline{\iota_{1}(A)\Delta_{A}(A)}=A\boxtimes A=\overline{\Delta_{A}(A)\iota_{2}(A)}
.

A further study of the braided quantum group
\mathrm{SU}_{q}(2)
including description of some of its representations, actions and homogeneous spaces is contained in
[
37
]
.

2.2.
Bosonization

The authors of
[
19
]
define the bosonization of
\mathrm{SU}_{q}(2)
as the compact quantum groups described by
(B,\Delta_{B})
, where
B
is universal unital
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra
B
with generators
\alpha,\gamma,z
where
\alpha,\gamma
satisfy relations (
2.1
) together with

z\alpha z^{*}=\alpha,\quad z\gamma z^{*}=\zeta^{-1}\gamma,\quad zz^{*}=z^{*}z=\mathds{1}

and
\Delta_{B}
acts as follows:

(2.3)

\Delta_{B}(\alpha)=\alpha\otimes\alpha-q\gamma^{*}z\otimes\gamma,\quad\Delta_{B}(\gamma)=\gamma\otimes\alpha+\alpha^{*}z\otimes\gamma,\quad\Delta_{B}(z)=z\otimes z.

We will discuss an alternative approach to the bosonization in Section
A.2
.

In fact, as noted in
[
19
, Section 6]
, the compact quantum group described by
(B,\Delta_{B})
is the co-opposite of the compact quantum group
\mathrm{U}_{q}(2)
studied in
[
45
]
via
\alpha\mapsto a^{*}
,
\gamma\mapsto b^{*}
,
z\mapsto D^{*}
.

Let us denote by
\kappa
the obvious map
A=\C(\mathrm{SU}_{q}(2))\to B
. Furthermore let
{\boldsymbol{n}}
be the canonical (unbounded) generator of
\mathrm{c}_{0}(\mathbb{Z})
, i.e. the function
\mathbb{Z}\ni n\mapsto n\in\mathbb{C}
.

Proposition 2.1
.

The map
\kappa
is injective.

Proof.

We have a natural isomorphism
B\simeq\mathbb{Z}\ltimes A
, for the action
\tilde{\rho}^{A}
of
\mathbb{Z}
on
A
given by
\alpha\mapsto\mathds{1}\otimes\alpha
,
\gamma\mapsto\zeta^{\boldsymbol{n}}\otimes\gamma
. Indeed
\mathbb{Z}\ltimes A=\overline{\tilde{\rho}^{A}(A)(\C(\mathbb{T})\otimes\mathds{1})}
is generated by
\mathsf{x}=\tilde{\rho}^{A}(\alpha)
,
\mathsf{y}=\tilde{\rho}^{A}(\gamma)
and
\mathsf{z}=(z\otimes\mathds{1})
and it is the universal
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra for the relations

\displaystyle\mathsf{x}^{*}\mathsf{x}+\mathsf{y}^{*}\mathsf{y}

\displaystyle=\mathds{1},

\displaystyle\mathsf{x}\mathsf{y}

\displaystyle=\overline{q}\mathsf{y}\mathsf{x},

\displaystyle\mathsf{x}\mathsf{x}^{*}+|q|^{2}\mathsf{y}^{*}\mathsf{y}

\displaystyle=\mathds{1},

\displaystyle\mathsf{y}\mathsf{y}^{*}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{y}^{*}\mathsf{y},

\displaystyle\mathsf{z}\mathsf{x}\mathsf{z}^{*}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{x},

\displaystyle\mathsf{z}\mathsf{y}\mathsf{z}^{*}

\displaystyle=\zeta^{-1}\mathsf{y},

\displaystyle\mathsf{z}\mathsf{z}^{*}

\displaystyle=\mathds{1},

\displaystyle\mathsf{z}^{*}\mathsf{z}

\displaystyle=\mathds{1}

because
\mathbb{Z}
is amenable (
[
38
, Theorem 7.13]
). Under this isomorphism
\kappa(A)\subseteq B
corresponds to
A\subseteq\mathbb{Z}\ltimes A
and
z
corresponds to the generator
\lambda(1)\in\mathrm{C}^{*}_{\text{r}}(\mathbb{Z})\subseteq\mathbb{Z}\ltimes A
. It follows that
\kappa
is injective.
∎

In what follows we will denote by
\mathscr{A}
the algebra generated by
\alpha,\alpha^{*},\gamma
and
\gamma^{*}
inside
A
. This is a dense unital
*
-subalgebra and we have

Corollary 2.2
.

The system

(2.4)

\bigl\{\alpha^{n}\gamma^{m}{\gamma^{*}}^{k}\,\bigr|\bigl.\,n,m,k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}\bigr\}\cup\bigl\{{\alpha^{*}}^{n}\gamma^{m}{\gamma^{*}}^{k}\,\bigr|\bigl.\,n\in\mathbb{N},\>m,k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}\bigr\}

is a basis of
\mathscr{A}
.

Proof.

Since
\kappa\colon A\to B
is injective and the system (
2.4
) is linearly independent in
B\simeq\C(\mathrm{U}_{q}(2))
(
[
45
, Theorem 2.2]
), it is also linearly independent in
A
. Furthermore it is easy to see that the space spanned by (
2.4
) is invariant under taking adjoints and multiplication by
\alpha
and
\gamma
which ends the proof.
∎

There is an action of
\mathbb{T}
on
B
given by

\rho^{B}(\alpha)=\mathds{1}\otimes\alpha,\quad\rho^{B}(\gamma)={\boldsymbol{z}}\otimes\gamma,\quad\rho^{B}(z)={\boldsymbol{z}}\otimes z,

which has the following relation to the comultiplication
\Delta_{B}
:
(\rho^{B}\otimes\mathrm{id})\circ\Delta_{B}=(\mathrm{id}\otimes\Delta_{B})\circ\rho^{B}
. It follows from this that if
{\boldsymbol{h}}_{B}
denotes the Haar measure of the quantum group described by
(B,\Delta_{B})
then
{\boldsymbol{h}}_{B}
is
\mathbb{T}
-invariant: since

(\mathrm{id}\otimes{\boldsymbol{h}}_{B}\otimes\mathrm{id})(\rho^{B}\otimes\mathrm{id})\Delta_{B}(b)=(\mathrm{id}\otimes{\boldsymbol{h}}_{B}\otimes\mathrm{id})(\mathrm{id}\otimes\Delta_{B})\rho^{B}(b)=\bigl((\mathrm{id}\otimes{\boldsymbol{h}}_{B})\rho^{B}(b)\bigr)\otimes\mathds{1}

for any
b\in B
, for any state
\omega\in\C(\mathbb{T})^{*}
we have

\bigl(((\omega\otimes{\boldsymbol{h}}_{B})\circ\rho^{B})\otimes\mathrm{id}\bigr)\Delta_{B}(b)=\bigl((\omega\otimes{\boldsymbol{h}}_{B})\rho^{B}(b)\bigr)\mathds{1},\qquad b\in B,

so the state
(\omega\otimes{\boldsymbol{h}}_{B})\circ\rho^{B}
is right invariant. By uniqueness of the Haar measure
(\omega\otimes{\boldsymbol{h}}_{B})\circ\rho^{B}={\boldsymbol{h}}_{B}
(for all
\omega
) and hence
(\mathrm{id}\otimes{\boldsymbol{h}}_{B})\rho^{B}(b)={\boldsymbol{h}}_{B}(b)\mathds{1}
for all
b\in B
. Also by
[
14
, Theorem 2.8]
the Haar measure
{\boldsymbol{h}}_{B}
is faithful if
q
is not a root of unity. We will from now on assume that
0<|q|<1
.

The modular group
\sigma^{{\boldsymbol{h}}_{B}}
of the Haar measure can be calculated based on the results of
[
14
]
and the isomorphism
B\to\C(\mathrm{U}_{q}(2)^{\operatorname{op}})
taking
\alpha
to
a^{*}
,
\gamma
to
b^{*}
, and
z
to
D^{*}
(see above):

\sigma^{{\boldsymbol{h}}_{B}}_{t}(\alpha)=|q|^{-2\mathrm{i}t}\alpha,\quad\sigma^{{\boldsymbol{h}}_{B}}_{t}(\gamma)=\gamma,\quad\sigma^{{\boldsymbol{h}}_{B}}_{t}(z)=z,\qquad t\in\mathbb{R}.

3.
Further results

3.1.
The Haar measure

We define a state
{\boldsymbol{h}}
on
A
by
{\boldsymbol{h}}={\boldsymbol{h}}_{B}\circ\kappa
, where
\kappa
is the inclusion
A\hookrightarrow B
discussed in Subsection
2.2
. The state
{\boldsymbol{h}}
is faithful. Also, the modular group
\sigma^{{\boldsymbol{h}}_{B}}

Proposition 3.1
.

The action of
\mathbb{T}
on
A
extends to a point-weak
∗
continuous action on
M
.

Proof.

For each
\mu\in\mathbb{T}
the automorphism
\rho^{A}_{\mu}
preserves
{\boldsymbol{h}}
, hence it extends to unital, normal
*
-homomorphism
\rho^{M}_{\mu}\colon M\to M
. One easily checks that this gives a group action and
{\boldsymbol{h}}
(extended to a state on
M
) is
\rho^{M}
-invariant.

Point-weak
∗

x\in M
,
\omega\in M_{*}
, and let
\mu_{n}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\mu
in
\mathbb{T}
. Next given
\varepsilon>0
, by a standard approximation argument, we can find
y,y^{\prime}\in\mathscr{A}
such that
\|\omega-\omega_{\Lambda_{\boldsymbol{h}}(y),\Lambda_{\boldsymbol{h}}(y^{\prime})}\|\leq\varepsilon
, where
\Lambda_{\boldsymbol{h}}
is the GNS map for
{\boldsymbol{h}}
. Then using the fact that
{\boldsymbol{h}}
is
\rho^{M}
-invariant we have

\displaystyle\Bigl|\bigl\langle\rho^{M}_{\mu}(x)-\rho^{M}_{\mu_{n}}(x),\omega\bigr\rangle\Bigr|

\displaystyle\leq 2\|x\|\varepsilon+\Bigl|\bigl\langle\rho^{M}_{\mu}(x)-\rho^{M}_{\mu_{n}}(x),\omega_{\Lambda_{\boldsymbol{h}}(y),\Lambda_{\boldsymbol{h}}(y^{\prime})}\bigr\rangle\Bigr|

\displaystyle=2\|x\|\varepsilon+\Bigl|{\boldsymbol{h}}\Bigl(y^{*}\bigl(\rho^{M}_{\mu}(x)-\rho^{M}_{\mu_{n}}(x)\bigr)y^{\prime}\Bigr)\Bigr|

\displaystyle=2\|x\|\varepsilon+\Bigr|{\boldsymbol{h}}\Bigr(\sigma^{\boldsymbol{h}}_{\mathrm{i}}(y^{\prime})y^{*}\bigl(\rho^{M}_{\mu}(x)-\rho^{M}_{\mu_{n}}(x)\bigr)\Bigr)\Bigr|

\displaystyle=2\|x\|\varepsilon+\biggl|{\boldsymbol{h}}\biggl(\Bigl(\rho^{M}_{\overline{\mu}}\bigl(\sigma^{\boldsymbol{h}}_{\mathrm{i}}(y^{\prime})y^{*}\bigr)-\rho^{M}_{\overline{\mu_{n}}}\bigl(\sigma^{\boldsymbol{h}}_{\mathrm{i}}(y^{\prime})y^{*}\bigr)\Bigr)x\biggr)\biggr|,

but
\sigma^{\boldsymbol{h}}_{\mathrm{i}}(y^{\prime})y^{*}\in\mathscr{A}\subseteq A
and hence
\mu^{\prime}\mapsto\rho^{A}_{\mu^{\prime}}(\sigma^{\boldsymbol{h}}_{\mathrm{i}}(y^{\prime})y^{*})
is norm continuous and we obtain

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\bigl|\langle\rho^{M}_{\mu}(x)-\rho^{M}_{\mu_{n}}(x),\omega\rangle\bigr|\leq 2\|x\|\varepsilon.\qed

We will now prove that

We begin with Corollary

Proposition 3.2
.

The state
{\boldsymbol{h}}
is left invariant, i.e.
(\mathrm{id}\boxtimes{\boldsymbol{h}})\Delta(a)={\boldsymbol{h}}(a)\mathds{1}
for all

Proof.

Define

\psi\colon A\boxtimes A\longrightarrow B\otimes B

as the composition

\textstyle{A\boxtimes A\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{A\boxtimes A\boxtimes\C(\mathbb{T})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\Psi}
\textstyle{\bigl(A\boxtimes\C(\mathbb{T})\bigr)\otimes\bigl(A\boxtimes\C(\mathbb{T})\bigr)=B\otimes B,}

where the first map is the canonical inclusion and

is introduced in Proposition

. Observe that

\psi\circ\Delta_{A}=\Delta_{B}\circ\iota_{A}.

Indeed, this follows from the calculations:

\displaystyle\psi\bigl(\Delta_{A}(\alpha)\bigr)

\displaystyle=\psi\bigl(\iota_{1}(\alpha)\iota_{2}(\alpha)-q\iota_{1}(\gamma)^{*}\iota_{2}(\gamma)\bigr)

\displaystyle=\bigl(\iota_{A}(\alpha)\otimes\mathds{1}\bigr)\bigl((\iota_{\C(\mathbb{T})}\otimes\iota_{A})\rho^{A}(\alpha)\bigr)-q(\iota_{A}(\gamma^{*})\otimes\mathds{1})\bigl((\iota_{\C(\mathbb{T})}\otimes\iota_{A})\rho^{A}(\gamma)\bigr)

\displaystyle=\iota_{A}(\alpha)\otimes\iota_{A}(\alpha)-q\iota_{A}(\gamma^{*})\iota_{\C(\mathbb{T})}({\boldsymbol{z}})\otimes\iota_{A}(\gamma)=\Delta_{B}\bigl(\iota_{A}(\alpha)\bigr)

and

\displaystyle\psi\bigl(\Delta_{A}(\gamma)\bigr)

\displaystyle=\psi\bigl(\iota_{1}(\gamma)\iota_{2}(\alpha)+\iota_{1}(\alpha^{*})\iota_{2}(\gamma)\bigr)

\displaystyle=\bigl(\iota_{A}(\gamma)\otimes\mathds{1}\bigr)\bigl((\iota_{\C(\mathbb{T})}\otimes\iota_{A})\rho^{A}(\alpha)\bigr)+\bigl(\iota_{A}(\alpha^{*})\otimes\mathds{1}\bigr)\bigl((\iota_{\C(\mathbb{T})}\otimes\iota_{A})\rho^{A}(\gamma)\bigr)

\displaystyle=\iota_{A}(\gamma)\otimes\iota_{A}(\alpha)+\iota_{A}(\alpha^{*})\iota_{\C(\mathbb{T})}({\boldsymbol{z}})\otimes\iota_{A}(\gamma)=\Delta_{B}\bigl(\iota_{A}(\gamma)\bigr).

It follows that for any
a\in A

(3.2)

(\mathrm{id}\otimes{\boldsymbol{h}}_{B})\psi\bigl(\Delta_{A}(a)\bigr)=(\mathrm{id}\otimes{\boldsymbol{h}}_{B})\Delta_{B}\bigl(\iota_{A}(a)\bigr)={\boldsymbol{h}}_{B}\bigl(\iota_{A}(a)\bigr)\mathds{1}={\boldsymbol{h}}(a)\mathds{1}.

Next we claim that

(3.3)

(\mathrm{id}\otimes{\boldsymbol{h}}_{B})\circ\psi=\iota_{A}\circ(\mathrm{id}\boxtimes{\boldsymbol{h}})

as maps

. It is enough to check equality (
3.3
) on elements of the form
\iota_{1}(x)\iota_{2}(y)
with

are homogeneous of degrees

\displaystyle(\mathrm{id}\otimes{\boldsymbol{h}}_{B})\psi\bigl(\iota_{1}(x)\iota_{2}(y)\bigr)

\displaystyle=(\mathrm{id}\otimes{\boldsymbol{h}}_{B})\bigl((\iota_{A}(x)\otimes\mathds{1})(\iota_{\C(\mathbb{T})}\otimes\iota_{A})\rho^{A}(y)\bigr)

\displaystyle=(\mathrm{id}\otimes{\boldsymbol{h}}_{B})\bigl(\iota_{A}(x)\iota_{\C(\mathbb{T})}({\boldsymbol{z}}^{\deg(y)})\otimes\iota_{A}(y)\bigr)

\displaystyle=\delta_{\deg(y),0}\,\iota_{A}(x){\boldsymbol{h}}(y)=\iota_{A}(\mathrm{id}\boxtimes{\boldsymbol{h}})\bigl(\iota_{1}(x)\iota_{2}(y)\bigr)

since both
{\boldsymbol{h}}
and
{\boldsymbol{h}}_{B}
are invariant under the actions of
\mathbb{T}
. This proves equality (
3.3
). Consequently, using (

) and (

)

\iota_{A}\bigl((\mathrm{id}\boxtimes{\boldsymbol{h}})\Delta_{A}(a)\bigr)=(\mathrm{id}\otimes{\boldsymbol{h}}_{B})\psi\bigl(\Delta_{A}(a)\bigr)={\boldsymbol{h}}(a)\mathds{1},\qquad a\in A

and since

is injective, we obtain

for all

∎

Corollary 3.3
.

The state
{\boldsymbol{h}}
is the Haar measure of
\mathrm{SU}_{q}(2)

Proof.

As observed in

on

3.2.
The scaling group

The name
scaling group
of a locally compact quantum group
\mathbb{G}
comes from the fact that this one-parameter group of automorphisms of
\C_{0}(\mathbb{G})
(or
\Linf(\mathbb{G})
) scales the Haar weights by a constant. The group itself is introduced in terms of the polar decomposition of the antipode (
[
25
, Section 5]
,
[
28
]
). In case
\mathbb{G}
is compact, the scaling is actually trivial and the name does not reflect the nature of this object. In this section we will define the appropriate analog of the scaling group which will, in particular, leave the Haar measure invariant as in the case of non-braided compact quantum groups. The role of the scaling group in the polar decomposition of the antipode will be described in Section
3.7
.

The scaling group of the quantum group
\mathrm{SU}_{q}(2)
(for real
q
) is well known to be defined by

(3.4)

\tau_{t}(\alpha)=\alpha,\quad\tau_{t}(\gamma)=|q|^{2\mathrm{i}t}\gamma,\qquad t\in\mathbb{R}

(cf. e.g..
[
33
, Example 1.7.8]
). We will use the same formulas to introduce this group in the case of complex
q
(satisfying
0<|q|<1
).

Proposition 3.4
.

There is a point-norm continuous one-parameter group
\tau=\{\tau_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
of automorphisms of
A
such that

(1)

for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
we have
\tau_{t}(\alpha)=\alpha
and
\tau_{t}(\gamma)=|q|^{2\mathrm{i}t}\gamma
,

(2)

each automorphism
\tau_{t}
globally preserves the subalgebra
\mathscr{A}
,

(3)

we have
\tau_{t}=\rho^{A}_{|q|^{-2\mathrm{i}t}}
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
, in particular
\tau_{t}
is
\mathbb{T}
and
\D(\mathbb{T})
-equivariant for each
t
,

(4)

for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
we have
{\boldsymbol{h}}\circ\tau_{t}={\boldsymbol{h}}
and hence
\tau
extends to a point-weak
∗
continuous one-parameter group of automorphisms of
M
,

(5)

\tau_{t}
and
\sigma^{\boldsymbol{h}}_{s}
commute for all
s,t\in\mathbb{R}
,

(6)

we have

(3.5a)

\displaystyle\Delta_{A}\circ\tau_{t}

\displaystyle=(\tau_{t}\boxtimes\tau_{t})\circ\Delta_{A}=(\sigma^{\boldsymbol{h}}_{t}\boxtimes\sigma^{\boldsymbol{h}}_{-t})\circ\Delta_{A},

(3.5b)

\displaystyle\Delta_{A}\circ\sigma^{\boldsymbol{h}}_{t}

\displaystyle=(\tau_{t}\boxtimes\sigma^{\boldsymbol{h}}_{t})\circ\Delta_{A}=(\sigma^{\boldsymbol{h}}_{t}\boxtimes\tau_{-t})\circ\Delta_{A}.

Proof.

Existence of the automorphisms
\{\tau_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
of
A
satisfying (
3.4
), the point-norm continuity of the group and the fact that it preserves the subalgebra
\mathscr{A}
are standard. Also it is clear from the definition that
\tau_{t}=\rho^{A}_{|q|^{-2\mathrm{i}t}}
for all

and then existence of point-weak
∗

follows from general principles:
\tau_{t}
is spatially implemented by the unitary
\Lambda_{\boldsymbol{h}}(a)\mapsto\Lambda_{\boldsymbol{h}}(\tau_{t}(a))
. Finally the fact that the groups
\tau
and
\sigma^{\boldsymbol{h}}
pointwise commute is also obvious.

To prove the formulas in point (
6
) it is enough to check them on generators. Note that they are well defined – automorphisms
\tau_{t}
and
\sigma^{\boldsymbol{h}}_{t}
are equivariant. For
t\in\mathbb{R}
we have

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \Delta_{A}\bigl(\tau_{t}(\alpha)\bigr)&=\Delta_{A}(\alpha)=\iota_{1}(\alpha)\iota_{2}(\alpha)-q\iota_{1}(\gamma^{*})\iota_{2}(\gamma),\\
(\tau_{t}\boxtimes\tau_{t})\Delta_{A}(\alpha)&=\iota_{1}\bigl(\tau_{t}(\alpha)\bigr)\iota_{2}\bigl(\tau_{t}(\alpha)\bigr)-q\iota_{1}\bigl(\tau_{t}(\gamma)^{*}\bigr)\iota_{2}\bigl(\tau_{t}(\gamma)\bigr)=\iota_{1}(\alpha)\iota_{2}(\alpha)-q\iota_{1}(\gamma^{*})\iota_{2}(\gamma),\\

and

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \Delta_{A}\bigl(\tau_{t}(\gamma)\bigr)&=|q|^{2\mathrm{i}t}\bigl(\iota_{1}(\gamma)\iota_{2}(\alpha)+\iota_{1}(\alpha^{*})\iota_{2}(\gamma)\bigr),\\
(\tau_{t}\boxtimes\tau_{t})\Delta_{A}(\gamma)&=\iota_{1}\bigl(\tau_{t}(\gamma)\bigr)\iota_{2}\bigl(\tau_{t}(\alpha)\bigr)+\iota_{1}\bigl(\tau_{t}(\alpha)^{*}\bigr)\iota_{2}\bigl(\tau_{t}(\gamma)\bigr)=|q|^{2\mathrm{i}t}\bigl(\iota_{1}(\gamma)\iota_{2}(\alpha)+\iota_{1}(\alpha^{*})\iota_{2}(\gamma)\bigr),\\
(\sigma^{\boldsymbol{h}}_{t}\boxtimes\sigma^{\boldsymbol{h}}_{-t})\Delta_{A}(\gamma)&=\iota_{1}\bigl(\sigma^{\boldsymbol{h}}_{t}(\gamma)\bigr)\iota_{2}\bigl(\sigma^{\boldsymbol{h}}_{-t}(\alpha)\bigr)+\iota_{1}\bigl(\sigma^{\boldsymbol{h}}_{t}(\alpha)^{*}\bigr)\iota_{2}\bigl(\sigma^{\boldsymbol{h}}_{-t}(\gamma)\bigr)=|q|^{2\mathrm{i}t}\bigl(\iota_{1}(\gamma)\iota_{2}(\alpha)+\iota_{1}(\alpha^{*})\iota_{2}(\gamma)\bigr)\end{aligned}

which proves (

). Next

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \Delta_{A}\bigl(\sigma^{\boldsymbol{h}}_{t}(\alpha)\bigr)&=|q|^{-2\mathrm{i}t}\bigl(\iota_{1}(\alpha)\iota_{2}(\alpha)-q\iota_{1}(\gamma^{*})\iota_{2}(\gamma)\bigr),\\
(\tau_{t}\boxtimes\sigma^{\boldsymbol{h}}_{t})\Delta_{A}(\alpha)&=\iota_{1}\bigl(\tau_{t}(\alpha)\bigr)\iota_{2}\bigl(\sigma^{\boldsymbol{h}}_{t}(\alpha)\bigr)-q\iota_{1}\bigl(\tau_{t}(\gamma)^{*}\bigr)\iota_{2}\bigl(\sigma^{\boldsymbol{h}}_{t}(\gamma)\bigr)=|q|^{-2\mathrm{i}t}\bigl(\iota_{1}(\alpha)\iota_{2}(\alpha)-q\iota_{1}(\gamma^{*})\iota_{2}(\gamma)\bigr),\\

and

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \Delta_{A}\bigl(\sigma^{\boldsymbol{h}}_{t}(\gamma)\bigr)&=\iota_{1}(\gamma)\iota_{2}(\alpha)+\iota_{1}(\alpha^{*})\iota_{2}(\gamma),\\
(\tau_{t}\boxtimes\sigma^{\boldsymbol{h}}_{t})\Delta_{A}(\gamma)&=\iota_{1}\bigl(\tau_{t}(\gamma)\bigr)\iota_{2}\bigl(\sigma^{\boldsymbol{h}}_{t}(\alpha)\bigr)+\iota_{1}\bigl(\tau_{t}(\alpha)^{*}\bigr)\iota_{2}\bigl(\sigma^{\boldsymbol{h}}_{t}(\gamma)\bigr)=\iota_{1}(\gamma)\iota_{2}(\alpha)+\iota_{1}(\alpha^{*})\iota_{2}(\gamma),\\

3.3.
The antipode

Recall that the
*
-algebra
\mathscr{A}
was defined in Section
2
as the
*
-subalgebra of
A
generated (algebraically) by
\alpha
and
\gamma
. We first note the following fact:

Proposition 3.5
.

\mathscr{A}
is the universal unital algebra generated by
\alpha
,
\alpha^{*}
,
\gamma
and
\gamma^{*}
satisfying (
2.1
) and
\alpha\gamma^{*}=q\gamma^{*}\alpha
.

Proof.

For real
q
this is
[
39
, Theorem 1.3]
. Denote by
\widetilde{\mathscr{A}}
the universal unital algebra generated by elements
\tilde{\alpha}
,
\tilde{\alpha}^{*}
,
\tilde{\gamma}
,
\tilde{\gamma}^{*}
satisfying

\displaystyle\tilde{\alpha}^{*}\tilde{\alpha}+\tilde{\gamma}^{*}\tilde{\gamma}

\displaystyle=\mathds{1},

\displaystyle\tilde{\alpha}\tilde{\gamma}

\displaystyle=\overline{q}\tilde{\gamma}\tilde{\alpha},

\displaystyle\tilde{\alpha}\tilde{\gamma}^{*}

\displaystyle=q\tilde{\gamma}^{*}\tilde{\alpha},

\displaystyle\tilde{\alpha}\tilde{\alpha}^{*}+|q|^{2}\tilde{\gamma}^{*}\tilde{\gamma}

\displaystyle=\mathds{1},

\displaystyle\tilde{\gamma}\tilde{\gamma}^{*}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\gamma}^{*}\tilde{\gamma}.

We have the canonical unital algebra homomorphism
\pi\colon\widetilde{\mathscr{A}}\to\mathscr{A}
which is surjective. Take

x=\sum_{n,m,k\geq 0}x_{n,m,k}\tilde{\alpha}^{n}\tilde{\gamma}^{m}(\tilde{\gamma}^{*})^{k}+\sum_{n>0,\>m,k\geq 0}x_{n,m,k}(\tilde{\alpha}^{*})^{n}\tilde{\gamma}^{m}(\tilde{\gamma}^{*})^{k}

, which shows that

for all

,

, and hence

.
∎

3.3.1.
Definition of the antipode

The goal of this section is to introduce the antipode of the braided quantum group
\mathrm{SU}_{q}(2)
. In analogy to the non-braided case we want to have
S\colon\mathscr{A}\to\mathscr{A}
such that

(1)

S
is unital, linear and
(S\otimes\mathrm{id})u=u^{*}
, where
u=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\alpha&-q\gamma^{*}\\
\gamma&\alpha^{*}\end{smallmatrix}\right]
is the fundamental representation od
\mathrm{SU}_{q}(2)
(cf. Sections
2
,
3.5
and
[
37
, Section 5]
),

(2)

S
is braided-anti-multiplicative, i.e.
S(ab)=\zeta^{-\deg(a)\deg(b)}S(b)S(a)
for homogeneous
a,b\in\mathscr{A}
(see equation (
3.8
)).

We note that (
1
) forces

(3.6)

S(\alpha)=\alpha^{*},\quad S(\alpha^{*})=\alpha,\quad S(\gamma)=-\overline{q}\gamma,\quad S(\gamma^{*})=-\tfrac{1}{q}\gamma^{*},

while (
2
) is satisfied in algebraic setting
[
15
, Proposition 3.2.12]
.

Theorem 3.6
.

There exists a unique
S\colon\mathscr{A}\to\mathscr{A}
which is unital, linear, preserves the degree, satisfies (
3.6
) and
S(ab)=\zeta^{-\deg(a)\deg(b)}S(b)S(a)
for homogeneous
a,b\in\mathscr{A}
.

Proof.

Uniqueness of
S
is clear. Let
\mathscr{B}
be a vector space isomorphic to
\mathscr{A}
with isomorphism
\mathscr{A}\ni a\mapsto\flat(a)\in\mathscr{B}
and let us equip
\mathscr{B}
with multiplication

\flat(a)\flat(b)=\zeta^{-\deg(a)\deg(b)}\flat(ba)

for homogeneous
a,b\in\mathscr{A}
. This makes
\mathscr{B}
into a unital associative algebra generated by elements
\bigl\{\flat(\alpha^{*}),\flat(\alpha),\flat(-\overline{q}\gamma),\flat(-q^{-1}\gamma^{*})\bigr\}
with unit
\flat(\mathds{1})
. These elements satisfy

\displaystyle\flat(\alpha)\flat(\alpha^{*})+\flat(-q^{-1}\gamma^{*})\flat(-\overline{q}\gamma)-\flat(\mathds{1})

\displaystyle=\flat\bigl(\alpha^{*}\alpha+(\overline{q}/q)\zeta\gamma\gamma^{*}-\mathds{1}\bigr)=0,

\displaystyle\flat(\alpha^{*})\flat(\alpha)+|q|^{2}\flat(-\overline{q}\gamma)\flat(-q^{-1}\gamma^{*})-\flat(\mathds{1})

\displaystyle=\flat\bigl(\alpha\alpha^{*}+|q|^{2}(\overline{q}/q)\zeta\gamma^{*}\gamma-\mathds{1}\bigr)=0,

\displaystyle\flat(-\overline{q}\gamma)\flat(-q^{-1}\gamma^{*})-\flat(-q^{-1}\gamma^{*})\flat(-\overline{q}\gamma)

\displaystyle=(\overline{q}/q)\flat(\zeta\gamma^{*}\gamma-\zeta\gamma\gamma^{*})=0,

\displaystyle\flat(\alpha^{*})\flat(-\overline{q}\gamma)-\overline{q}\flat(-\overline{q}\gamma)\flat(\alpha^{*})

\displaystyle=-\overline{q}\flat(\gamma\alpha^{*}-\overline{q}\alpha^{*}\gamma)=0,

\displaystyle\flat(\alpha^{*})\flat(-q^{-1}\gamma^{*})-q\flat(-q^{-1}\gamma^{*})\flat(\alpha^{*})

\displaystyle=-q^{-1}\flat(\gamma^{*}\alpha^{*}-q\alpha^{*}\gamma^{*})=0.

The universal property of
\mathscr{A}
(Proposition
3.5
) gives us a linear, unital, multiplicative map
\tilde{S}\colon\mathscr{A}\to\mathscr{B}
such that

\tilde{S}(\alpha)=\flat(\alpha^{*}),\quad\tilde{S}(\alpha^{*})=\flat(\alpha),\quad\tilde{S}(\gamma)=\flat(-\overline{q}\gamma),\quad\tilde{S}(\gamma^{*})=\flat(-q^{-1}\gamma^{*}).

Now we define
S=\flat^{-1}\circ\tilde{S}\colon\mathscr{A}\to\mathscr{A}
which, by its construction, satisfies (
3.6
). Let us check that
S
is braided-anti-multiplicative: take homogeneous
a,b\in\mathscr{A}
. Then

(3.7)

\displaystyle\flat\bigl(S(ab)\bigr)

\displaystyle=\tilde{S}(ab)=\tilde{S}(a)\tilde{S}(b)=\flat\bigl((\flat^{-1}\circ\tilde{S})(a)\bigr)\flat\bigl((\flat^{-1}\circ\tilde{S})(b)\bigr)

\displaystyle=\zeta^{-\deg((\flat^{-1}\circ\tilde{S})(a))\deg((\flat^{-1}\circ\tilde{S})(b))}\flat\bigl((\flat^{-1}\circ\tilde{S})(b)(\flat^{-1}\circ\tilde{S})(a)\bigr)

\displaystyle=\zeta^{-\deg(S(a))\deg(S(b))}\flat\bigl(S(b)S(a)\bigr).

To finish the proof, we need to show that
\deg(S(a))=\deg(a)
for any homogeneous
a
. This is easily shown on the basis (
2.4
) of
\mathscr{A}
using equation (
3.7
).
∎

We note a corollary of the above proof that
S
defined in Theorem
3.6
is equivariant and preserves the degree.

In the following sections we will analyze

and prove a number of its properties which include its relationship with representation theory of the braided compact quantum group
\mathrm{SU}_{q}(2)
(Section

) and the notion of a braided Hopf algebra (Section

).

3.3.2.
Braided flip

We already observed in the Proof of Theorem
3.6
that the action of
\mathbb{T}
on

considered in this paper restricts to an action on

of

Proposition 3.7
.

There is a well defined linear isomorphism

(3.9)

\phi_{\otimes,\boxtimes}\colon\mathscr{A}\otimes_{\textrm{alg}}\mathscr{A}\ni a\otimes b\longmapsto\iota_{1}(a)\iota_{2}(b)\in\mathscr{A}\boxtimes_{\textrm{alg}}\mathscr{A}.

Proof.

A linear map (
3.9
) is well defined and unique by the universal property of the algebraic tensor product. It is also surjective, so we only need to prove that it is injective.

Take
x\in\ker{\phi_{\otimes,\boxtimes}}
and write
x=\sum\limits_{n,m=-\infty}^{\infty}x_{n,m}
where

x_{n,m}=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{n,m}}a_{n,m}^{i}\otimes b_{n,m}^{i}

for some
a_{n,m}^{i},b_{n,m}^{i}\in\mathscr{A}
with
\deg(a_{n,m}^{i})=n
and
\deg(b_{n,m}^{i})=m

Then viewing

as a subalgebra of

2

This implies

for all

∎

Proposition 3.8
.

There is a unique linear map
S\boxtimes S\colon\mathscr{A}\boxtimes_{\textrm{alg}}\mathscr{A}\to\mathscr{A}\boxtimes_{\textrm{alg}}\mathscr{A}
such that

Proof.

Uniqueness of

is obvious and its existence is proved by defining
S\boxtimes S=\phi_{\otimes,\boxtimes}\circ(S\otimes S)\circ\phi_{\otimes,\boxtimes}^{-1}

Definition 3.9
.

We define the

Theorem 3.23

(
[
19
, Theorem 6.1]
)
.

Let
\mathsf{H}
be a Hilbert space equipped with unitary representation

. There is a bijection between unitary representations
u
of
\mathrm{SU}_{q}(2)
on
\mathsf{H}
and unitary representations
v
of the compact quantum group described by
(\widetilde{B},\Delta_{\widetilde{B}})
satisfying
(\pi\otimes\mathrm{id})v=U
. This bijection is given by

(3.16)

v=\bigl((\iota_{A}\otimes\mathrm{id})u\bigr)\bigl((\iota_{\C(\mathbb{T})}\otimes\mathrm{id})U\bigr).

Proof.

Let
u
be a unitary representation of
\mathrm{SU}_{q}(2)
on
\mathsf{H}
and define
v
via (
3.16
). It is a unitary element of
\M(\widetilde{B}\otimes\mathscr{K}(\mathsf{H}))
. We have

(\pi\otimes\mathrm{id})v=\bigl((\pi\iota_{A}\otimes\mathrm{id})u\bigr)\bigl((\pi\iota_{\C(\mathbb{T})}\otimes\mathrm{id})U\bigr)=\bigl(\mathds{1}\otimes(\varepsilon\otimes\mathrm{id})u\bigr)U=U

as claimed. Next we check that

is a representation. The invariance condition on

(Definition
3.18
) reads
U^{*}_{13}((\rho^{A}\otimes\mathrm{id})u)U_{13}=u_{23}

(\rho^{A}\otimes\mathrm{id})u=U_{13}u_{23}U_{13}^{*}.

Using this, the expression
\Delta_{\widetilde{B}}=\Psi\circ(\Delta_{A}\boxtimes\mathrm{id})
(see Section

) and Proposition

(3.17)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} (\Delta_{\widetilde{B}}&\otimes\mathrm{id})v\\
&=(\Delta_{\widetilde{B}}\otimes\mathrm{id})\Bigl(\bigl((\iota_{A}\otimes\mathrm{id})u\bigr)\bigl((\iota_{\C(\mathbb{T})}\otimes\mathrm{id})U\bigr)\Bigr)\\
&=\Bigl((\Psi\otimes\mathrm{id})\bigl(((\iota_{12}\circ\Delta_{A})\otimes\mathrm{id})u\bigr)\Bigr)\Bigl((\Psi\otimes\mathrm{id})\bigl((\iota_{3}\otimes\mathrm{id})U\bigr)\Bigr)\\
&=\Bigl((\Psi\otimes\mathrm{id})(\iota_{12}\otimes\mathrm{id})\bigl(((\jmath_{1}\otimes\mathrm{id})u)((\jmath_{2}\otimes\mathrm{id})u)\bigr)\Bigr)\Bigl((\Psi\otimes\mathrm{id})\bigl((\iota_{3}\otimes\mathrm{id})U\bigr)\Bigr)\\

&=\bigl((\iota_{A}\otimes\mathrm{id})u\bigr)_{13}\bigl((\iota_{\C(\mathbb{T})}\otimes\mathrm{id})U\bigr)_{13}\bigl((\iota_{A}\otimes\mathrm{id})u\bigr)_{23}\bigl(\iota_{\C(\mathbb{T})}\otimes\mathrm{id})U\bigr)_{23}=v_{13}v_{23}\end{aligned}

which proves that
v
is a representation of the compact quantum group described by
(\widetilde{B},\Delta_{\widetilde{B}})
.

Conversely, assume that
v
is a unitary representation of
(\widetilde{B},\Delta_{\widetilde{B}})
on
\mathsf{H}
satisfying
(\pi\otimes\mathrm{id})v=U
and define the unitary

\widetilde{u}=v\bigl((\iota_{\C(\mathbb{T})}\otimes\mathrm{id})U\bigr)^{*}\in\M\bigl(\widetilde{B}\otimes\mathscr{K}(\mathsf{H})\bigr).

Next observe that for

\displaystyle\bigl(\mathrm{id}\otimes({\boldsymbol{h}}_{\mathbb{T}}\circ\pi)\bigr)

\displaystyle\Delta_{\widetilde{B}}\bigl((\mathrm{id}\otimes\omega_{\xi,\eta})\widetilde{u}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\bigl(\mathrm{id}\otimes({\boldsymbol{h}}_{\mathbb{T}}\circ\pi)\otimes\omega_{\xi,\eta}\bigr)\Bigl(v_{13}v_{23}\bigl((\iota_{\C(\mathbb{T})}\otimes\mathrm{id})U\bigr)^{*}_{23}\bigl((\iota_{\C(\mathbb{T})}\otimes\mathrm{id})U\bigr)^{*}_{13}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=(\mathrm{id}\otimes{\boldsymbol{h}}_{\mathbb{T}}\otimes\omega_{\xi,\eta})\Bigl(v_{13}U_{23}U^{*}_{23}\bigl((\iota_{\C(\mathbb{T})}\otimes\mathrm{id})U\bigr)^{*}_{13}\Bigr)=(\mathrm{id}\otimes\omega_{\xi,\eta})\widetilde{u}

*

Thus we have
v=((\iota_{A}\otimes\mathrm{id})u)((\iota_{\C(\mathbb{T})}\otimes\mathrm{id})U)

U_{13}^{*}\bigl((\rho^{A}\otimes\mathrm{id})u\bigr)U_{13}=u_{23}.

Using (

\displaystyle(\mathrm{id}\otimes\iota_{A}\otimes\mathrm{id})

and this is equal to (using (

\displaystyle v_{13}v_{23}

\displaystyle=\bigl((\iota_{A}\otimes\mathrm{id})u\bigr)_{13}\bigl((\iota_{\C(\mathbb{T})}\otimes\mathrm{id})U\bigr)_{13}\bigl((\iota_{A}\otimes\mathrm{id})u\bigr)_{23}\bigl((\iota_{\C(\mathbb{T})}\otimes\mathrm{id})U\bigr)_{23}

\displaystyle=(\Psi\otimes\mathrm{id})\bigl((\iota_{1}\otimes\mathrm{id})u\bigr)(\Psi\otimes\mathrm{id})\bigl((\iota_{2}\otimes\mathrm{id})u\bigr)\bigl((\iota_{\C(\mathbb{T})}\otimes\iota_{\C(\mathbb{T})}\otimes\mathrm{id})(U_{13}U_{23})\bigr)

\displaystyle=(\Psi\otimes\mathrm{id})\Bigl((\iota_{12}\otimes\mathrm{id})\bigl(((\jmath_{1}\otimes\mathrm{id})u)((\jmath_{2}\otimes\mathrm{id})u)\bigr)\Bigr)\bigl((\iota_{\C(\mathbb{T})}\otimes\iota_{\C(\mathbb{T})}\otimes\mathrm{id})(U_{13}U_{23})\bigr).

(\Psi\otimes\mathrm{id})\Bigl(\bigl((\iota_{12}\circ\Delta_{A})\otimes\mathrm{id}\bigr)u\Bigr)=(\Psi\otimes\mathrm{id})\Bigl((\iota_{12}\otimes\mathrm{id})\bigl(((\jmath_{1}\otimes\mathrm{id})u)((\jmath_{2}\otimes\mathrm{id})u)\bigr)\Bigr).

Since
\Psi
and

are injective, this proves (

). Clearly both passages from
u
to
v
and from

to

are one another’s inverses.
∎

As a corollary we get the following nice result.

Proposition 3.24
.

The
*
-subalgebra
\mathscr{A}\subseteq A
coincides with the set of matrix elements of all finite-dimensional unitary representations of
\mathrm{SU}_{q}(2)
.

Proof.

Write
\mathcal{P}
for the space of matrix elements of all finite-dimensional unitary representations of
\mathrm{SU}_{q}(2)
. The possibility of taking direct sums and tensor products implies that
\mathcal{P}
is an algebra
and since
\mathscr{A}
is generated by
\{\mathds{1},\alpha,\alpha^{*},\gamma,\gamma^{*}\}
and these are matrix elements of the fundamental representation, we easily get
\mathscr{A}\subseteq\mathcal{P}
(note that we do not need to involve the notion of the contragredient representation here).

Conversely, let
u
be a representation of
\mathrm{SU}_{q}(2)
on a finite-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathsf{H}
with a representation
U
of
\mathbb{T}
and take
(\mathrm{id}\otimes\omega)u\in\mathcal{P}
for some
\omega\in\B(\mathsf{H})^{*}
. Then by Theorem
3.23

v=\bigl((\iota_{A}\otimes\mathrm{id})u\bigr)\bigl((\iota_{\C(\mathbb{T})}\otimes\mathrm{id})U\bigr)

is a finite-dimensional unitary representation of the compact quantum group described by
(\widetilde{B},\Delta_{\widetilde{B}})
. Since
(\widetilde{B},\Delta_{\widetilde{B}})
is isomorphic to
\mathrm{U}_{q}(2)^{\text{op}}
(in a concrete way, see Section
2.2
), we can use results about that quantum group. In particular we see from the classification result
[
14
, Theorem 4.17]
that
\Pol(\widetilde{B},\Delta_{\widetilde{B}})=\iota_{A}(\mathscr{A})\iota_{\C(\mathbb{T})}(\Pol(\mathbb{T}))
. Consequently, after decomposing
v
into a direct sum of irreps, we conclude that matrix elements of
v
belong to
\iota_{A}(\mathscr{A})\iota_{\C(\mathbb{T})}(\Pol(\mathbb{T}))
. It follows that

\iota_{A}\bigl((\mathrm{id}\otimes\omega)u\bigr)=(\mathrm{id}\otimes\omega)\Bigl(v\bigl((\iota_{\C(\mathbb{T})}\otimes\mathrm{id})U\bigr)^{*}\Bigr)\in\iota_{A}(\mathscr{A})\iota_{\C(\mathbb{T})}\bigl(\Pol(\mathbb{T})\bigr)=\Pol(\widetilde{B},\Delta_{\widetilde{B}}),

and
\iota_{A}((\mathrm{id}\otimes\omega)u)
is a polynomial in
\alpha,\alpha^{*},\gamma,\gamma^{*},z
and
z^{*}
(here
\alpha,\gamma
and
z
are generators of
\widetilde{B}\simeq B
as in Section
2.2
, cf. also Section
A.2
). Consider the action of
\mathbb{T}
on
\widetilde{B}\simeq B
given by
z\mapsto{\boldsymbol{z}}\otimes z
and
\alpha\mapsto\mathds{1}\otimes\alpha
,
\gamma\mapsto\mathds{1}\otimes\gamma
. In particular the action on
\iota_{A}((\mathrm{id}\otimes\omega)u)\in\iota_{A}(A)
is trivial. The conditional expectation onto the fixed point subalgebra for this action allows us to deduce that
(\mathrm{id}\otimes\omega)u\in\mathscr{A}
.
∎

To proceed further, let us introduce some more terminology and braided analogs of known results. From now on we will consider only finite-dimensional representations.

Definition 3.25
.

Let
u
and
v
be finite-dimensional, unitary representations of
\mathrm{SU}_{q}(2)
on Hilbert spaces
\mathsf{H}
and
\mathsf{K}
equipped with representations for
U
and
V
of
\mathbb{T}
. We say that
T\in\B(\mathsf{H},\mathsf{K})

intertwines

and
v
if

(1)

T\in\Mor_{\mathbb{T}}(U,V)
, i.e.
T
is equivariant for the
\mathbb{T}
-representations
U
and
V
,

(2)

we have
(\mathds{1}\otimes T)u=v(\mathds{1}\otimes T)
.

The set of all intertwiners for
u
and
v
will be denoted by
\Mor(u,v)
and by
\End(u)
in case
v=u
. We say that
u
is

if
\End(u)=\mathbb{C}\mathds{1}
.

Remarks 3.26
.

(1)

The equivariance condition on intertwiners seems to be important – without it e.g. Proposition
3.28
below fails.

(2)

It is easy to construct examples where (
2
) holds, but (
1
) fails (e.g.
u=v
the trivial representation on
\mathbb{C}^{2}
with non-equivalent
U
and
V
).

(3)

T
intertwines
u
and
v
iff it is a morphism of associated representations of
(\widetilde{B},\Delta_{\widetilde{B}})
. In particular,
u
is irreducible iff the associated representation of
(\widetilde{B},\Delta_{\widetilde{B}})
is irreducible.

.

Let
u
,

and

be finite-dimensional unitary representations of
\mathrm{SU}_{q}(2)
on
\mathbb{T}
-Hilbert spaces

\mathsf{H}_{v}

The first point is trivial. The second can be proven exactly as its non-braided version (see e.g.
[

).
∎

Proposition 3.28
.

Let
u
be a finite-dimensional unitary representation of
\mathrm{SU}_{q}(2)

is equivalent to a direct sum of irreducible representations.

Proof.

This decomposition is performed exactly as in the non-braided case (e.g.

(by assumption), hence we can indeed define smaller representations

(where

given by

.
∎

(1)

S

3.6.
Braided Hopf algebra structure on
\mathscr{A}

In this section we will show that
\mathscr{A}
is a braided Hopf algebra, i.e. a Hopf algebra in
\mathcal{C}=\sideset{{}_{\mathbb{Z}}^{\mathbb{Z}}}{}{\operatorname{\mathcal{YD}}}
in the sense of
[
15
, Definition 1.6.6]
.
2
2

2

Our
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebraic approach favors the compact group

, but the definitions in
[
15
]
are such that the discrete group
\mathbb{Z}
is more prominent.

This requires shifting point of view a little: for example in the algebraic setting of
[
15
]
the algebraic tensor product is used (with twisted multiplication), while in analytic setting it is better to have twisted version

First, we need to understand

has the structure of a Yetter-Drinfeld module over

-grading

\mathscr{A}=\bigoplus_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\bigl\{a\in\mathscr{A}\,\bigr|\bigl.\,\deg(a)=k\bigr\},

where

corresponds to the
\mathbb{T}
-action

. Next there is the
\mathbb{C}[\mathbb{Z}]
-module structure given by

k\cdot a=\rho^{\mathscr{A}}_{\zeta^{k}}(a)=(\operatorname{ev}_{\zeta^{-k}}\otimes\mathrm{id})\rho^{\mathscr{A}}(a),\qquad a\in\mathscr{A}.

Let us explain why the
\mathbb{C}[\mathbb{Z}]
-module structure is given by this formula. The action of

on

restricts to an action on
\mathscr{A}
and then further to actions
\rho^{\mathscr{A}}
and

of the subgroups
\mathbb{T}
and
\mathbb{Z}
of

(see Sections

These structures make
\mathscr{A}
into Yetter-Drinfeld module over
\mathbb{C}[\mathbb{Z}]
(
[
15

) hence an object of
\sideset{{}_{\mathbb{Z}}^{\mathbb{Z}}}{}{\operatorname{\mathcal{YD}}}

\sideset{{}_{\mathbb{Z}}^{\mathbb{Z}}}{}{\operatorname{\mathcal{YD}}}
are
\mathbb{Z}
-graded,
\mathbb{Z}
-equivariant linear maps (so in our case

-equivariant linear maps).

Next we claim that
\mathscr{A}
is an algebra, a coalgebra, a bialgebra, and finally a Hopf algebra in
\sideset{{}_{\mathbb{Z}}^{\mathbb{Z}}}{}{\operatorname{\mathcal{YD}}}
(
[
15
, Page 40]
):

•

Algebra: the multiplication

and the unit map
\mathbb{C}\ni z\mapsto z\mathds{1}\in\mathscr{A}
are morphisms in

because the Yetter-Drinfeld structure on the tensor product is diagonal and the
\mathbb{T}
-action is by homomorphisms

15
, Page 29]
. Algebra axioms clearly hold.

•

Coalgebra: as the comultiplication and counit we take

and
\varepsilon\colon\mathscr{A}\to\mathbb{C}

is equivariant, and so is

, we conclude that above

is equivariant.
\varepsilon
is equivariant by Proposition

and Proposition

•

Bialgebra: we need to argue that

and
\varepsilon
are algebra maps. It is clear that

is an algebra map (it is unital and multiplicative). To show that

is an algebra map, we need to prove

(see
[
15

) which we check on simple tensors using Sweedler notation:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Delta}\bigl(\widetilde{\mu}(a\otimes b)\bigr)

\displaystyle=\phi_{\otimes,\boxtimes}^{-1}\Delta_{A}(ab)=\phi_{\otimes,\boxtimes}^{-1}\bigl(\Delta_{A}(a)\Delta_{A}(b)\bigr)

\displaystyle=\sum\phi_{\otimes,\boxtimes}^{-1}\bigl(\iota_{1}(a_{(1)})\iota_{2}(a_{(2)})\iota_{1}(b_{(1)})\iota_{2}(b_{(2)})\bigr)

\displaystyle=\sum\zeta^{-\deg(a_{(2)})\deg(b_{(1)})}\phi_{\otimes,\boxtimes}^{-1}\bigl(\iota_{1}(a_{(1)}b_{(1)})\iota_{2}(a_{(2)}b_{(2)})\bigr)

\displaystyle=\sum\zeta^{-\deg(a_{(2)})\deg(b_{(1)})}a_{(1)}b_{(1)}\otimes a_{(2)}b_{(2)}

and

\displaystyle(\widetilde{\mu}\otimes\widetilde{\mu})

\displaystyle\Bigl((\mathrm{id}\otimes c_{\mathscr{A},\mathscr{A}}\otimes\mathrm{id})\bigl((\widetilde{\Delta}\otimes\widetilde{\Delta})(a\otimes b)\bigr)\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\sum(\widetilde{\mu}\otimes\widetilde{\mu})\bigl((\mathrm{id}\otimes c_{\mathscr{A},\mathscr{A}}\otimes\mathrm{id})(a_{(1)}\otimes a_{(2)}\otimes b_{(1)}\otimes b_{(2)})\bigr)

\displaystyle=\sum\zeta^{-\deg(a_{(2)})\deg(b_{(1)})}(\widetilde{\mu}\otimes\widetilde{\mu})(a_{(1)}\otimes b_{(1)}\otimes a_{(2)}\otimes b_{(2)})

Hopf algebra: as the antipode we take

, which we know to be linear and

-equivariant. The antipode condition is equivalent to the conclusion of Theorem

.

Thus we proved the following:

\widetilde{\Delta}

\sideset{{}_{\mathbb{Z}}^{\mathbb{Z}}}{}{\operatorname{\mathcal{YD}}}

Remark 3.33
.

We are not aware of any literature proposing a definition of a braided Hopf
*
-algebra. In the case of
(\mathscr{A},\widetilde{\mu},\mathds{1},\widetilde{\Delta},\varepsilon,S)
we do have

(Proposition

(and consequently

However, if we define a new involution

In what follows, we will use complex powers of

Definition 3.35

Remarks 3.36

(1)

(2)

Lemma 3.37
.

For homogeneous
a,b\in\mathscr{A}
we have

\displaystyle\deg\bigl(\vartheta(a)\bigr)

\displaystyle=\deg\bigl(\vartheta^{-1}(a)\bigr)=\deg(a),

\displaystyle\vartheta(ab)

\displaystyle=\zeta^{\deg(a)\deg(b)}\vartheta(a)\vartheta(b),

\displaystyle\vartheta^{-1}(ab)

\displaystyle=\zeta^{-\deg(a)\deg(b)}\vartheta^{-1}(a)\vartheta^{-1}(b),

\displaystyle\vartheta(a)^{*}

\displaystyle=\zeta^{-\deg(a)^{2}}\vartheta(a^{*}),

\displaystyle\vartheta^{-1}(a)^{*}

\displaystyle=\zeta^{\deg(a)^{2}}\vartheta^{-1}(a^{*}).

Furthermore,
{\boldsymbol{h}}\circ\vartheta={\boldsymbol{h}}\circ\vartheta^{-1}={\boldsymbol{h}}
and
\vartheta
is

-equivariant.

Proof.

The first two equations are immediate. The next two properties follow from the calculation:

\vartheta(ab)=\zeta^{\frac{1}{2}\deg(ab)^{2}}ab=\zeta^{\deg(a)\deg(b)}\zeta^{\frac{1}{2}\deg(a)^{2}}a\zeta^{\frac{1}{2}\deg(b)^{2}}b=\zeta^{\deg(a)\deg(b)}\vartheta(a)\vartheta(b)

and hence

\displaystyle\vartheta^{-1}\bigl(\vartheta(a)\vartheta(b)\bigr)

\displaystyle=\zeta^{-\deg(a)\deg(b)}\vartheta^{-1}\bigl(\vartheta(ab)\bigr)=\zeta^{-\deg(a)\deg(b)}ab

\displaystyle=\zeta^{-\deg(\vartheta^{-1}(a))\deg(\vartheta^{-1}(b))}\vartheta^{-1}\bigl(\vartheta(a)\bigr)\vartheta^{-1}\bigl(\vartheta(b)\bigr).

Observe that as
\operatorname{Log}(\zeta)\in\mathrm{i}\mathbb{R}
, we have
\overline{\zeta^{x}}=\overline{e^{x\operatorname{Log}(\zeta)}}=e^{-x\operatorname{Log}(\zeta)}=\zeta^{-x}

. Using this and the fact that

we obtain

\vartheta^{-1}\bigl(\vartheta(a)\bigr)^{*}=a^{*}=\zeta^{\deg(a)^{2}}\vartheta^{-1}\bigl(\zeta^{-\deg(a)^{2}}\vartheta(a^{*})\bigr)=\zeta^{\deg(a)^{2}}\vartheta^{-1}\bigl(\vartheta(a)^{*}\bigr).

It this section we will prove Proposition
3.11
thus showing that the braided flip
\chi^{\boxtimes}
extends to a completely bounded map on
A\boxtimes A
precisely when
\zeta=q/\overline{q}\in\mathbb{T}
is a root of unity. Our reasoning uses the notion of completely bounded multipliers which we will now briefly recall. For the proofs of the facts listed below and additional information see
[
16
,
17
,
7
,
9
]
. We follow the notation of
[
9
]
.

Let
\mathbb{G}
be a locally compact quantum group. The
Fourier algebra
of
\mathbb{G}
is defined as
\A(\mathbb{G})=\bigl\{(\omega\otimes\mathrm{id})\mathrm{W}^{\widehat{\mathbb{G}}}\,\bigr|\bigl.\omega\in\Linf(\widehat{\mathbb{G}})_{*}\bigr\}\subseteq\C_{0}(\mathbb{G})
(see e.g.
[
8
, Section 1]
). When equipped with the multiplication of
\C_{0}(\mathbb{G})
and the operator space structure of
\Linf(\widehat{\mathbb{G}})_{*}
, it becomes a completely contractive Banach algebra. Next, one defines the space
\M^{l}_{\cb}(\A(\mathbb{G}))
of
(left) completely bounded multipliers
, as the set of elements
b\in\Linf(\mathbb{G})
satisfying the following condition:
ba\in\A(\mathbb{G})
whenever
a\in\A(\mathbb{G})
and the resulting map
\A(\mathbb{G})\to\A(\mathbb{G})
is completely bounded. Since
\A(\mathbb{G})\simeq\Linf(\widehat{\mathbb{G}})_{*}
, the dual of the map
a\mapsto{ba}
is a normal c.b. map
\Linf(\widehat{\mathbb{G}})\to\Linf(\widehat{\mathbb{G}})
which we denote by
\Theta^{l}(b)
.

The space
\M^{l}_{\cb}(\A(\mathbb{G}))
equipped with the norm
\|b\|_{\cb}=\|\Theta^{l}(b)\|_{\cb}
and the multiplication of
\Linf(\mathbb{G})
becomes a completely contractive Banach algebra and we have
\A(\mathbb{G})\subseteq\M^{l}_{\cb}(\A(\mathbb{G}))\subseteq\M(\C_{0}(\mathbb{G}))
. We note that for each
b\in\M^{l}_{\cb}(\A(\mathbb{G}))
the map
\Theta^{l}(b)
restricts to
\C_{0}(\widehat{\mathbb{G}})
and we have
\|b\|_{\cb}=\bigl\|\bigl.\Theta^{l}(b)\bigr|_{\C_{0}(\widehat{\mathbb{G}})}\bigr\|_{\cb}
.

A particularly nice family of completely bounded multipliers forms the
Fourier-Stieltjes algebra
of
\mathbb{G}
, defined as
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{G})=\bigl\{(\omega\otimes\mathrm{id})\mathds{W}^{\widehat{\mathbb{G}}}\,\bigr|\bigl.\,\omega\in\C_{0}^{\text{\rm u}}(\widehat{\mathbb{G}})^{*}\bigr\}\subseteq\M(\C_{0}(\mathbb{G}))
(also c.f.
[
8
, Section 1]
). It is a completely contractive Banach algebra, when equipped with the multiplication of
\M(\C_{0}(\mathbb{G}))
and the operator space structure of
\C_{0}^{\text{\rm u}}(\widehat{\mathbb{G}})^{*}
. Furthermore,
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{G})\subseteq\M^{l}_{\cb}(\A(\mathbb{G}))
and we have a concrete formula for the associated c.b. maps: if
b=(\omega\otimes\mathrm{id})(\mathds{W}^{\widehat{\mathbb{G}}})\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{G})
, then
\bigl.\Theta^{l}(b)\bigr|_{\C_{0}(\widehat{\mathbb{G}})}=(\omega\otimes\mathrm{id})\circ\Delta^{\text{\rm u,r}}_{\widehat{\mathbb{G}}}
, where

\Delta_{\widehat{\mathbb{G}}}^{\text{\rm u,r}}\in\Mor\bigl(\C_{0}(\widehat{\mathbb{G}}),\C_{0}^{\text{\rm u}}(\widehat{\mathbb{G}})\otimes\C_{0}(\widehat{\mathbb{G}})\bigr)

is the half-lifted version of the comultiplication (
[
10
, Section 2]
). In general the inclusion
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{G})\subseteq\M^{l}_{\cb}(\A(\mathbb{G}))
is strict, but if
\mathbb{G}
is amenable, then
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{G})=\M^{l}_{\cb}(\A(\mathbb{G}))
and this identification is completely isometric (see
[
7
, Theorem 7.2]
for the general and
[
4
]
for the classical case).

To prove Proposition
3.11
we start with a lemma concerning completely bounded multipliers on
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.

Lemma A.10
.

Fix
\zeta\in\mathbb{T}
and
z,z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathbb{C}
with
|z|,|z_{1}|,|z_{2}|\leq 1
. Let
g_{z}\colon\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{C}
be defined by

g_{z}(n)=\begin{cases}z^{n}&n\geq 0\\
\overline{z}^{-n}&n<0\end{cases}.

Furthermore let
g_{z_{1},z_{2}}\colon\mathbb{Z}^{2}\ni(n,m)\mapsto g_{z_{1}}(n)g_{z_{2}}(m)\in\mathbb{C}
and
f_{\zeta}\colon\mathbb{Z}^{2}\ni(n,m)\mapsto\zeta^{nm}\in\mathbb{C}
. Then

(1)

The function
g_{z}
belongs to
\M^{l}_{\cb}(\A(\mathbb{Z}))
and we have
\|g_{z}\|_{\cb}\leq\tfrac{1+|z|}{1-|z|}
for
|z|<1
and
\|g_{z}\|_{\cb}=1
when
|z|=1
,

(2)

the function
g_{z_{1},z_{2}}
belongs to
\M^{l}_{\cb}(\A(\mathbb{Z}^{2}))
and
\|g_{z_{1},z_{2}}\|_{\cb}=\|g_{z_{1}}\|_{\cb}\|g_{z_{2}}\|_{\cb}
,

(3)

if
\zeta\in\mathbb{T}
is a root of unity with
\zeta=\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}n/d}
with
\gcd(n,d)=1
, then
f_{\zeta}\in\M^{l}_{\cb}(\A(\mathbb{Z}^{2}))
and
\|f_{\zeta}\|_{\cb}=d
,

(4)

if
\zeta\in\mathbb{T}
is not a root of unity, then
f_{\zeta}\not\in\M^{l}_{\cb}(\A(\mathbb{Z}^{2}))
.

Proof.

Since
\mathbb{Z}
is amenable, we can work with the Fourier-Stieltjes algebra
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{Z})
which is completely isometric to
\C(\mathbb{T})^{*}
, identified with the space of Borel, regular, complex measures on
\mathbb{T}
. Unraveling these identifications, we see that a measure
\rho\in\C(\mathbb{T})^{*}
viewed as an element of
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{Z})
corresponds to the function
\mathbb{Z}\ni n\mapsto\rho({\boldsymbol{z}}^{-n})\in\mathbb{C}
, where
{\boldsymbol{z}}
is the canonical coordinate on
\mathbb{T}
. An analogous observation applies to
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
and
\mathbb{T}^{2}
.

Ad (
1
). If
|z|=1
, then
g_{z}(n)=z^{n}=\delta_{\overline{z}}({\boldsymbol{z}}^{-n})
hence
\|g_{z}\|_{\cb}=\|\delta_{z}\|=1
. In the case
|z|<1
, consider the measure
\rho=\sum\limits_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}g_{z}(n){\boldsymbol{h}}_{\mathbb{T}}({\boldsymbol{z}}^{n}\cdot)
(
{\boldsymbol{h}}_{\mathbb{T}}
is the normalized Haar measure on
\mathbb{T}
). Since
\rho({\boldsymbol{z}}^{-n})=g_{z}(n)
and
\|\rho\|\leq\sum\limits_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\bigl|g_{z}(n)\bigr|=\tfrac{1+|z|}{1-|z|}
, the claim follows.

Point (
2
) follows from the fact that
\Theta^{l}(g_{z_{1},z_{2}})=\Theta^{l}(g_{z_{1}})\otimes\Theta^{l}(g_{z_{2}})
.

Ad (
3
). Assume that
\zeta=\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}n/d}
, where
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
,
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
\gcd(n,d)=1
. Define the measure
\rho=\tfrac{1}{d}\sum\limits_{k,l=0}^{d-1}\zeta^{-kl}\delta_{(\zeta^{k},\zeta^{l})}
(
\delta_{(\zeta^{k},\zeta^{l})}
is the Dirac measure at the point
(\zeta^{k},\zeta^{l})\in\mathbb{T}^{2}
). Since

\rho({\boldsymbol{z}}_{1}^{-n}{\boldsymbol{z}}_{2}^{-m})=\tfrac{1}{d}\sum_{k,l=0}^{d-1}\zeta^{-kl}\zeta^{-kn}\zeta^{-ml}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq k\leq d-1\\
k\equiv-m\,\text{mod}\,d\end{subarray}}\zeta^{-kn}=\zeta^{nm}=f_{\zeta}(n,m)

for
(n,m)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, we see that
\rho\in\C(\mathbb{T}^{2})^{*}
corresponds to
f_{\zeta}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
and
\|f_{\zeta}\|_{\cb}=\|\rho\|
. The upper bound
\|\rho\|\leq d
is trivial, for the converse inequality choose a function
u\in\C(\mathbb{T}^{2})
with
\|u\|=1
and
u(\zeta^{k},\zeta^{l})=\zeta^{kl}
for
0\leq k,l\leq d-1
, then
\rho(u)=d
.

Ad (
4
).
Assume by contradiction that
f_{\zeta}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
. Then
f_{\zeta}
is weakly almost periodic (
[
22
, Proposition D.8]
), i.e. the set
\bigl\{f_{\zeta}((n,m)+\cdot)\,\bigr|\bigl.\,(n,m)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\bigr\}\subseteq\ell^{\infty}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
has compact closure in the weak topology. Hence Grothendieck’s double limit criterion
[
22
, Theorem D.4]
(applied to the Stone-Čech compactification
\beta\mathbb{Z}^{2}
of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
) implies that
(n_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(m_{l})_{l\in\mathbb{N}}
are sequences of integers such that the limits

(A.9)

\lim_{k\to\infty}\lim_{l\to\infty}f_{\zeta}(n_{k},m_{l}),\quad\lim_{l\to\infty}\lim_{k\to\infty}f_{\zeta}(n_{k},m_{l})

exist, then they are equal.

We will now construct sequences
(n_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
,
(m_{l})_{l\in\mathbb{N}}
for which the limits in (
A.9
) exist, but are different. More precisely, we will construct sequences such that the following statement

\left.\begin{array}[]{l}n_{R}\in\mathbb{N}\\[2.0pt]
m_{R}\in 2\mathbb{Z}_{+}+1\\[2.0pt]
\forall\>s\in\{1,\dotsc,R-1\}\ \bigl|\zeta^{n_{s}m_{R}}-1\bigr|\leq\tfrac{1}{R}\\[2.0pt]
\forall\>t\in\{1,\dotsc,R\}\ \bigl|\zeta^{n_{R}m_{t}}+1\bigr|\leq\tfrac{1}{R}\end{array}\right\}

is true for all
R\geq 1
.

For
R=1
, set
m_{1}=1
and choose
n_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
|\zeta^{n_{1}}+1|\leq 1
. Next, assume that we have constructed
m_{1},\dotsc,m_{R}
and
n_{1},\dotsc,n_{R}
such that the conditions
(\star_{1}),\dotsc,(\star_{R})
hold. Choose
p\in\mathbb{N}
in such a way that
\bigl|\zeta^{2p}-\zeta^{-1}\bigr|\leq\tfrac{1}{(R+1)\max\{n_{1},\dotsc,n_{R}\}}
and define
m_{R+1}=2p+1\in 2\mathbb{N}+1
. For
1\leq s\leq R
we have

\bigl|\zeta^{n_{s}m_{R+1}}-1\bigr|=\bigl|(\zeta^{m_{R+1}})^{n_{s}}-1^{n_{s}}\bigr|\leq n_{s}\bigl|\zeta^{m_{R+1}}-1\bigr|=n_{s}\bigl|\zeta^{2p+1}-1\bigr|\leq\tfrac{1}{R+1}.

Next choose
n_{R+1}\in\mathbb{N}
so that
\bigl|\zeta^{n_{R+1}}+1\bigr|\leq\tfrac{1}{(R+1)\max\{m_{1},\dotsc,m_{R+1}\}}
. Then for
1\leq t\leq R+1

\bigl|\zeta^{n_{R+1}m_{t}}+1\bigr|=\bigl|(\zeta^{n_{R+1}})^{m_{t}}-(-1)^{m_{t}}\bigr|\leq m_{t}\bigl|\zeta^{n_{R+1}}+1\bigr|\leq\tfrac{1}{R+1},

because each
m_{t}
is odd. This proves
(\star_{R+1})
and ends the construction. Using conditions
(\star_{R})
we see that

\lim_{k\to\infty}\lim_{l\to\infty}f_{\zeta}(n_{k},m_{l})=\lim_{k\to\infty}\lim_{l\to\infty}\zeta^{n_{k}m_{l}}=1

and

\lim_{l\to\infty}\lim_{k\to\infty}f_{\zeta}(n_{k},m_{l})=\lim_{l\to\infty}\lim_{k\to\infty}\zeta^{n_{k}m_{l}}=-1,

hence we obtain a contradiction.
∎

We will need one more simple lemma, which is a consequence of the proof of
[
19
, Theorem 2.3]
and
[
24
, Equation (2.6)]
.

Lemma A.11
.

For
q\in\mathbb{C}
with
0<|q|<1
we have
\operatorname{Sp}(\gamma)=\{0\}\cup\bigl\{\lambda|q|^{k}\,\bigr|\bigl.\,\lambda\in\mathbb{T},\>k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}\bigr\}
.

With these preliminaries, we can prove Proposition
3.11
.

Proof of Proposition
3.11
.

Assume first that
\zeta
is a root of unity and write
\zeta=\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}n/d}
for
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
,
d\in\mathbb{N}
with
0\leq n\leq d-1
and
\gcd(n,d)=1
. If
\zeta=1
(i.e.
q\in\mathbb{R}
) the claim is trivial, hence assume that
d\geq 2
.

As explained in Section
2
we can realize
\mathscr{A}\boxtimes_{\textrm{alg}}\mathscr{A}\subseteq A\boxtimes A\subseteq\C(\mathbb{T}^{2}_{\zeta})\otimes A\otimes A
. Recall (equation (
3.10
)) that
\chi^{\boxtimes}
acts as

\chi^{\boxtimes}\colon\iota_{1}(a)\iota_{2}(b)=U^{\deg(a)}V^{\deg(b)}\otimes a\otimes b\longmapsto\iota_{2}(a)\iota_{1}(b)=V^{\deg(a)}U^{\deg(b)}\otimes b\otimes a

for homogeneous
a,b\in\mathscr{A}
. Since
\zeta
is a root of unity, we can use
[
3
, Corollary 1.12]
and identify
\C(\mathbb{T}^{2}_{\zeta})
with
\C^{*}({\boldsymbol{z}}_{1}\otimes U_{0},{\boldsymbol{z}}_{2}\otimes V_{0})\subseteq\C(\mathbb{T}^{2})\otimes\Mat_{d}(\mathbb{C})
, where

U_{0}=\begin{bmatrix}0&1&0&\cdots&0\\
0&0&1&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\cdots&1\\
1&0&0&\cdots&0\end{bmatrix},\quad V_{0}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&\cdots&0\\
0&\zeta&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&\zeta^{d-1}\end{bmatrix}.

Let
\{e_{k}\}_{k=0,\dotsc,d-1}
be the standard basis of
\mathbb{C}^{d}
(it will be convenient to treat
k
as a cyclic variable in
\mathbb{Z}_{d}
). Define linear maps
\mathcal{O},\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\colon\mathbb{C}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}^{d}
by

\mathcal{O}e_{k}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{l=0}^{d-1}\zeta^{kl}e_{l},\qquad\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}e_{k}=e_{-k}\in\mathbb{C}^{d}

(c.f.
[
3
, Equation (1.11)]
). It is straightforward to check that
\mathcal{O}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}
are unitary and

\mathcal{O}U_{0}\mathcal{O}^{*}=V_{0}^{-1},\quad\mathcal{O}V_{0}\mathcal{O}^{*}=U_{0},\quad\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}U_{0}\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}^{*}=U_{0}^{*},\quad\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}V_{0}\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}^{*}=V_{0}^{*}.

Next we define a linear map
\phi_{0}\colon\Mat_{d}(\mathbb{C})\to\Mat_{d}(\mathbb{C})

\phi_{0}(x)=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\mathcal{O}x\mathcal{O}^{*})^{\top}\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}^{*},

where
\top
denotes the transposition. Then
\phi_{0}
is completely bounded with
\|\phi_{0}\|_{\cb}=d
(
[
12
, Proposition 2.2.7]
). Furthermore we have

(A.10)

\displaystyle\phi_{0}(U_{0}^{n}V_{0}^{m})

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\mathcal{O}U_{0}^{n}V_{0}^{m}\mathcal{O}^{*})^{t}\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}^{*}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(U_{0}^{m})^{t}(V_{0}^{-n})^{t}\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}^{*}=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}U_{0}^{-m}V_{0}^{-n}\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}^{*}=U_{0}^{m}V_{0}^{n}

for any
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Next let
F\colon\C(\mathbb{T}^{2})\to\C(\mathbb{T}^{2})
be the automorphism associated to the homeomorphism
\mathbb{T}^{2}\ni(z_{1},z_{2})\mapsto(z_{2},z_{1})\in\mathbb{T}^{2}
and put
\widetilde{\phi}=F\otimes\phi_{0}\colon\C(\mathbb{T}^{2})\otimes\Mat_{d}(\mathbb{C})\to\C(\mathbb{T}^{2})\otimes\Mat_{d}(\mathbb{C})
.

Since
F
is a
*
-homomorphism, the map
\widetilde{\phi}
is completely bounded with
\|\widetilde{\phi}\|_{\cb}=d
. Denoting the coordinate functions on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
by
{\boldsymbol{z}}_{1}
and
{\boldsymbol{z}}_{2}
and using (
A.10
) we obtain

\displaystyle\widetilde{\phi}(U^{n}V^{m})=\widetilde{\phi}({\boldsymbol{z}}_{1}^{n}{\boldsymbol{z}}_{2}^{m}\otimes U_{0}^{n}V_{0}^{m})

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{z}}_{2}^{n}{\boldsymbol{z}}_{1}^{m}\otimes U_{0}^{m}V_{0}^{n}

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{z}}_{1}^{m}{\boldsymbol{z}}_{2}^{n}\otimes U_{0}^{m}V_{0}^{n}=U^{m}V^{n}=\zeta^{nm}V^{n}U^{m}

for all
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}
. By Lemma
A.10
(
3
) the map
\Theta^{l}(f_{\overline{\zeta}})\colon\C(\mathbb{T}^{2})\to\C(\mathbb{T}^{2})
acting by

{\boldsymbol{z}}_{1}^{n}{\boldsymbol{z}}_{2}^{m}\longmapsto f_{\overline{\zeta}}(-n,-m){\boldsymbol{z}}_{1}^{n}{\boldsymbol{z}}_{2}^{m}=\zeta^{-nm}{\boldsymbol{z}}_{1}^{n}{\boldsymbol{z}}_{2}^{m}

satisfies
\|\Theta^{l}(f_{\overline{\zeta}})\|_{\cb}=d
. It follows that
(\Theta^{l}(f_{\overline{\zeta}})\otimes\mathrm{id})\circ\widetilde{\phi}
restricts to a completely bounded map
\phi\colon\C(\mathbb{T}^{2}_{\zeta})\to\C(\mathbb{T}^{2}_{\zeta})
which satisfies

\phi(U^{n}V^{m})=V^{n}U^{m},\qquad n,m\in\mathbb{Z}

with
\|\phi\|_{\cb}\leq d^{2}
. Finally observe that for homogeneous
a,b\in\mathscr{A}
we have

\displaystyle(\phi\otimes\chi)\bigl(\iota_{1}(a)\iota_{2}(b)\bigr)

\displaystyle=(\phi\otimes\chi)\bigl(U^{\deg(a)}V^{\deg(b)}\otimes a\otimes b\bigr)

\displaystyle=V^{\deg(a)}U^{\deg(b)}\otimes b\otimes a=\iota_{2}(a)\iota_{1}(b)=\chi^{\boxtimes}\bigl(\iota_{1}(a)\iota_{2}(b)\bigr),

where
\chi
is the standard tensor flip on
A\otimes A
. It follows that the restriction of
\phi\otimes\chi
to
A\boxtimes A
defines an extension of
\chi^{\boxtimes}
with c.b. norm
\leq d^{2}
. This ends the proof of the first part.

Assume now that
\zeta
is not a root of unity, and assume by contradiction that
\chi^{\boxtimes}
extends to a c.b. map on
A\boxtimes A
which we will denote by the same symbol. Then the map
(\chi^{\boxtimes})^{2}
on
A\boxtimes A
is completely bounded and acts by

A\boxtimes A\ni\iota_{1}(a)\iota_{2}(b)\longmapsto\zeta^{-2\deg(a)\deg(b)}\iota_{1}(a)\iota_{2}(b)\in A\boxtimes A

for homogeneous
a,b\in\mathscr{A}
. Lemma
A.10
tells us that
f_{\zeta^{-2}}\not\in\M^{l}_{\cb}(\A(\mathbb{Z}^{2}))
and hence
[
5
, Proposition 7.2]
implies that the map
U^{n}V^{m}\mapsto f_{\zeta^{-2}}(n,m)U^{n}V^{m}
does not extend to a linear, completely bounded map on
\C(\mathbb{T}^{2}_{\zeta})
. Thus for a fixed
N\geq 2
we can find matrix
a=[a_{k,l}]_{k,l=1}^{p}\in\Mat_{p}(\C(\mathbb{T}^{2}_{\zeta}))
with
\|a\|_{\Mat_{p}(\C(\mathbb{T}^{2}_{\zeta}))}=1
with each matrix entry

(A.11)

a_{k,l}=\sum_{n,m\in\mathbb{Z}}a_{k,l}^{n,m}U^{n}V^{m}

(each
a_{k,l}
is a finite linear combination of generators
U^{n}V^{m}
) and such that

(A.12)

\left\|\biggl[\,\sum_{n,m\in\mathbb{Z}}\zeta^{-2nm}a_{k,l}^{n,m}U^{n}V^{m}\biggr]_{k,l=1}^{p}\right\|_{\Mat_{p}(\C(\mathbb{T}^{2}_{\zeta}))}\geq N.

Next, let us define

b=[b_{k,l}]_{k,l=1}^{p}=\biggl[\,\sum_{n,m\in\mathbb{Z}}a^{n,m}_{k,l}U^{n}V^{m}\otimes\tilde{\gamma}^{n}\otimes\tilde{\gamma}^{m}\biggr]_{k,l=1}^{p}\in\Mat_{p}\bigl(\C(\mathbb{T}^{2}_{\zeta})\otimes A\otimes A\bigr),

where we use the convenient notation
\tilde{\gamma}^{n}=\gamma^{n}
if
n\geq 0
and
\tilde{\gamma}^{n}=\gamma^{*|n|}
if
n<0
.

Consider now

\mathsf{B}=\overline{\operatorname{span}}\bigl\{\iota_{1}(\tilde{\gamma}^{n})\iota_{2}(\tilde{\gamma}^{m})\,\bigr|\bigl.\,n,m\in\mathbb{Z}\bigr\}

which is a unital
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-subalgebra in
A\boxtimes A
. We can write
b_{k,l}=\sum\limits_{n,m\in\mathbb{Z}}a^{n,m}_{k,l}\iota_{1}(\tilde{\gamma}^{n})\iota_{2}(\tilde{\gamma}^{m})
, hence in fact we have

b\in\Mat_{p}(\mathsf{B})\subseteq\Mat_{p}\bigl(\C(\mathbb{T}^{2}_{\zeta})\otimes\mathrm{C}^{*}(\mathds{1},\gamma)\otimes\mathrm{C}^{*}(\mathds{1},\gamma)\bigr)\simeq\Mat_{p}\bigl(\C(\mathbb{T}^{2}_{\zeta})\otimes\C(\Sp(\gamma))\otimes\C(\Sp(\gamma))\bigr),

where
\operatorname{Sp}(\gamma)=\{0\}\cup\bigl\{\lambda|q|^{k}\,\bigr|\bigl.\,\lambda\in\mathbb{T},\>k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}\bigr\}
(Lemma
A.11
).

Next, we can identify

\Mat_{p}\bigl(\C(\mathbb{T}^{2}_{\zeta})\otimes\C(\Sp(\gamma))\otimes\C(\Sp(\gamma))\bigr)\simeq\C\bigl(\Sp(\gamma)\times\Sp(\gamma),\Mat_{p}(\C(\mathbb{T}^{2}_{\zeta}))\bigr)

(cf.
[
32
, Theorem 6.4.17]
)
and hence

\displaystyle\|b\|

\displaystyle=\sup\Bigl\{\bigl\|(\mathrm{id}\otimes\ev_{z_{1}}\otimes\ev_{z_{2}})b\bigr\|\,\Bigr|\Bigl.\,z_{1},z_{2}\in\Sp(\gamma)\Bigr\}

\displaystyle=\sup\left\{\left\|\biggl[\sum_{n,m\in\mathbb{Z}}a^{n,m}_{k,l}U^{n}V^{m}\ev_{z_{1}}(\tilde{\gamma}^{n})\ev_{z_{2}}(\tilde{\gamma}^{m})\biggr]_{k,l=1}^{p}\right\|_{\Mat_{p}(\C(\mathbb{T}^{2}_{\zeta}))}\,\vline\,z_{1},z_{2}\in\Sp(\gamma)\right\}.

Now we note that
\ev_{z}(\tilde{\gamma}^{n})=\begin{cases}z^{n},&n\geq 0\\
\overline{z}^{-n},&n<0\end{cases}
which is exactly
g_{z}(n)
, where the function
g_{z}
was introduced in Lemma
A.10
. Using again
[
5
, Proposition 7.2]
and the fact that
\|a\|=1
we obtain

\displaystyle\|b\|

\displaystyle=\sup\left\{\left\|\biggl[\,\sum_{n,m\in\mathbb{Z}}a^{n,m}_{k,l}U^{n}V^{m}g_{z_{1},z_{2}}(n,m)\biggr]_{k,l=1}^{p}\right\|_{\Mat_{p}(\C(\mathbb{T}^{2}_{\zeta}))}\,\vline\,z_{1},z_{2}\in\Sp(\gamma)\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{z_{1},z_{2}\in\Sp(\gamma)}\|g_{z_{1},z_{2}}\|_{\cb}=\left(\tfrac{1+|q|}{1-|q|}\right)^{2}.

On the other hand, applying
p\times p
matrix amplification of
(\chi^{\boxtimes})^{2}
we have

\bigl((\chi^{\boxtimes})^{2}\bigr)^{(p)}(b)=\biggl[\,\sum_{n,m\in\mathbb{Z}}\zeta^{-2nm}a_{n,m}^{k,l}U^{n}V^{m}\otimes\tilde{\gamma}^{n}\otimes\tilde{\gamma}^{m}\biggr]_{k,l=1}^{p}\!\!\in\Mat_{p}\bigl(\C(\mathbb{T}^{2}_{\zeta})\otimes\mathrm{C}^{*}(\mathds{1},\gamma)\otimes\mathrm{C}^{*}(\mathds{1},\gamma)\bigr)

and we can bound the norm of this matrix from below using (
A.12
) as follows:

\displaystyle\bigl\|((\chi^{\boxtimes})^{2})^{(p)}(b)\bigr\|

\displaystyle=\sup\left\{\left\|(\mathrm{id}\otimes\ev_{z_{1}}\otimes\ev_{z_{2}})\bigl(((\chi^{\boxtimes})^{2})^{(p)}(b)\bigr)\right\|_{\Mat_{p}(\C(\mathbb{T}^{2}_{\zeta}))}\,\vline\,z_{1},z_{2}\in\Sp(\gamma)\right\}

\displaystyle\geq\bigl\|(\mathrm{id}\otimes\ev_{1}\otimes\ev_{1})\bigl(((\chi^{\boxtimes})^{2})^{(p)}(b)\bigr)\bigr\|_{\Mat_{p}(\C(\mathbb{T}^{2}_{\zeta}))}

\displaystyle=\left\|\biggl[\,\sum_{n,m\in\mathbb{Z}}\zeta^{-2nm}a_{n,m}^{k,l}U^{n}V^{m}\biggr]_{k,l=1}^{p}\right\|_{\Mat_{p}(\C(\mathbb{T}^{2}_{\zeta}))}\geq N.

This shows that the c.b. norm of
(\chi^{\boxtimes})^{2}
is not smaller than
N\left(\tfrac{1+|q|}{1-|q|}\right)^{-2}
. Since
N
was arbitrary, we obtain a contradiction.
∎
