Sarcopenic obesity in cirrhosis: prognostic value and future perspectives
Authors: Yanhua Chen, Ziheng Liao, Cong Wang, Yuanyuan Zhang, Rui Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSarcopenic obesity (Sa-O) is an emerging prognostic factor in patients with cirrhosis, yet its diagnostic criteria and clinical significance remain incompletely defined. This narrative review synthesizes evidence from the past five years on the pathogenesis, diagnostic criteria, and prognostic implications of Sa-O in cirrhosis. Diagnostic criteria were categorized according to their definitions of sarcopenia and obesity, and the clinical utility of assessment tools was evaluated. Prognostic value was examined across seven clinical domains. Existing evidence indicates that Sa-O is associated with increased mortality and adverse post-transplant outcomes. Limited data suggest potential links with classic cirrhosis complications, while cardiovascular events remain underexplored as primary endpoints. Current studies on acute-on-chronic liver failure focus on sarcopenia or obesity in isolation rather than their coexistence. Health-related quality of life and the integration of Sa-O into established prognostic score models are rarely addressed. In summary, Sa-O should be recognized as a key prognostic factor in cirrhosis and represents a modifiable target for early lifestyle intervention.Keywords: Sarcopenic obesity, Cirrhosis, Sarcopenia, Obesity

Introduction

Liver cirrhosis is an end-stage liver disease characterized by scarring and permanent damage. In 2021, the global number of cases of cirrhosis and other chronic liver diseases was estimated at 1.7 billion [1]. The age-standardized incidence rate of cirrhosis and other chronic liver diseases was 724.31 per 100 000 population [2]. As the 11th most common cause of death globally, the age-standardized death rate of cirrhosis was 16.64 per 100 000 population [2, 3]. Between 1990 and 2021, both global cirrhosis incidence and mortality increased significantly, with incidence growing from 36.9 million to 58.4 million and deaths rising from 1.02 million to 1.43 million [4]. And the presence of cirrhosis markedly elevates the risk of mortality by 5- to 10- fold due to related complications, including ascites, variceal bleeding, hepatic encephalopathy, kidney dysfunction and infections [5]. Cirrhosis also ranks as the 15th leading cause of disability-adjusted life-years worldwide, with the healthcare costs continuing to climb [5].

Sarcopenia is a skeletal muscle disorder characterized by the accelerated loss of muscle mass and function. The global prevalence of sarcopenia in cirrhosis is approximately 40%, increasing with age and disease severity [6]. Obesity affects 35–40% acutely ill cirrhotic patients, and is expected to become more prevalent in the future [7]. Sarcopenic obesity (Sa-O) refers to the co-occurrence of excess adiposity alongside low muscle mass or reduced muscle function [8]. The pathogenesis of Sa-O in cirrhosis is multifactorial, driven by mechanisms such as insulin resistance, chronic inflammation, and malnutrition, which collectively promote skeletal muscle loss and fat accumulation, thereby establishing a vicious cycle that worsens clinical outcomes [9, 10]. Reported prevalence rates of Sa-O in patients with cirrhosis ranged from 13% to 25.3% [11–14]. Despite variations in prevalence due to heterogeneous study populations and differing diagnostic criteria, it remains higher than the prevalence of 9.9% in the general population [15].

Both sarcopenia and obesity are associated with adverse outcomes in cirrhotic patients [16, 17]. Studies have suggested that cirrhotic patients with Sa-O face a higher risk of adverse outcomes than those without Sa-O, or those with only sarcopenia or obesity [13]. Sa-O is associated with elevated overall mortality from cirrhosis, acute-on-chronic liver failure (ACLF), and post-liver transplant causes [18–20]. It also predisposes patients to complications including ascites, variceal bleeding, hepatic encephalopathy, hepatorenal syndrome and infections [19]. Moreover, Sa-O is correlated with disease severity assessed by Model for End-Stage Liver Disease (MELD) or Child-Pugh [21], and contributes to diminished health-related quality of life by promoting frailty and impairing physical function [22]. However, the prognostic significance of Sa-O remains contentious. While both sarcopenia and obesity have been individually recognized as important prognostic factors in cirrhosis, their coexistence as Sa-O represents a distinct clinical phenotype that may not be fully captured by either condition alone. In this context, excess adiposity may mask the presence of reduced muscle mass, while the combination of metabolic dysfunction, systemic inflammation, and impaired muscle function may exert synergistic effects on disease progression. While some studies proposed Sa-O as an independent predictor in cirrhosis [18, 19], others reported no statistically significant association [23, 24] or even suggested a protective effect, termed the “obesity paradox” [24]. Although several reviews have addressed Sa-O in liver disease, important gaps remain in the current literature. Previous studies have often focused on either sarcopenia or obesity alone, or have not clearly distinguished between direct evidence from cirrhosis-specific populations and extrapolated findings from related conditions. Moreover, the heterogeneity of diagnostic criteria for Sa-O in cirrhosis has not been critically compared. Therefore, this narrative review aims to provide an updated and clinically oriented synthesis by (1) comparing the existing diagnostic criteria for Sa-O in cirrhosis, (2) differentiating between direct evidence and indirect evidence, and (3) integrating current data on prognostic implications across multiple clinically relevant outcomes. By emphasizing these aspects, we seek to provide a more structured and critical framework for understanding Sa-O in cirrhosis and to highlight key areas for future research.

Methods

This study was conducted as a narrative review to summarize current evidence on Sa-O in cirrhosis. A literature search was performed using the PubMed database to identify relevant studies published within the past five years. The search strategy combined the terms “sarcopenic obesity” and “cirrhosis” restricted to the title and abstract fields. No restrictions on study design were applied. Original studies, including retrospective and prospective observational studies, as well as relevant reviews, were considered. The search yielded 35 records, which were manually reviewed by the authors. Studies were included if they evaluated sarcopenic obesity or its components (sarcopenia and obesity) in patients with cirrhosis. Of the included studies, 12 studies that explicitly reported diagnostic criteria for Sa-O were summarized in Table 1. Table 2 further included studies reporting prognostic outcomes. Given the limited number of cirrhosis-specific studies on Sa-O, selected studies on related chronic liver diseases were also included to provide supportive and contextual evidence. These were clearly interpreted as indirect evidence.

Pathogenesis of sarcopenic obesity

The pathogenesis of Sa-O, characterized by a vicious cycle of fat accumulation and muscle loss in the general population, is markedly exacerbated in cirrhosis (Fig. 1).

In the general population

Much of the current mechanistic understanding of Sa-O is derived from studies in the general population, and should be interpreted as indirect evidence when applied to cirrhosis. The pathogenesis of Sa-O within the general population is driven by the interplay of three primary factors: excessive lipid deposition, aging-related hormone changes, and environmental influences, predominantly lifestyle.

Excessive lipid deposition acts as a core engine driving disease progression through two principal pathways. The first pathway centers on intramyocellular lipid (IMCL) accumulation. Aging and obesity induce the atrophy of fast-twitch type II muscle fibers and a shift toward slow-twitch type I fibers, and neurodegeneration, which collectively diminish the muscle neurotrophic effects and further promote IMCL deposition [25]. When IMCLs exceed the oxidation capacity, it may contribute to mitochondrial dysfunction and increased reactive oxygen species production, which may promote myocyte damage and accelerate muscle loss [26]. Concurrently, adipose tissue–derived proinflammatory cytokines can enhance macrophage activation and sustain chronic inflammation, further exacerbating insulin resistance and proteolysis [27]. And this state aggravates IR and lipotoxicity, thereby accelerating muscle protein degradation and apoptosis [25]. Increased fatty acid oxidation also leads to the accumulation of ROS. The resulting oxidative stress may induce endoplasmic reticulum stress, impairing both proteasomal degradation and autophagy [27]. The second pathway involves ectopic fat accumulation surrounding muscle, which directly promotes muscle proteolysis by enhancing the nuclear translocation of the fork-head box O (Fox O) transcription factors, subsequently upregulating the expression of atrophy-related proteins [26]. Notably, insulin suppresses Fox O family through the caspase-3 and ubiquitin-proteasome proteolytic pathways. Thus, IR disrupts this suppression, linking IR to accelerated muscle loss [27]. And the resulting sarcopenia leads to a decreased metabolic rate, reduced physical activity, and myokine deficiency. These changes collectively create a proinflammatory and metabolically dysregulated state that in turn exacerbates obesity.

The aging process is frequently accompanied by hormonal alterations including decreased testosterone in males and reduced estradiol in females, which compromises the protein synthesis [27]. Moreover, obesity itself is associated with the conversion of testosterone to estradiol via aromatization in adipose tissue; separately, menopausal women exhibit increased level of follicle-stimulating hormone and androgen that exacerbate the fat deposition [26, 27]. Additional endocrine disorders, including declines in thyroid hormone, growth hormone (GH), and insulin-like growth factor (IGF)−1, along with elevated cortisol also contributes to the development of this condition [25].

Environmental factors, primarily lifestyle-related, contain dietary habits and physical activity levels. A high-fat and low-protein diet will aggravate obesity and muscle loss, while insufficient physical activity further compromises muscle function and strength. Furthermore, a range of chronic diseases may also contribute to the development of Sa-O through the pathways described above.

In cirrhosis

As liver is the metabolic hub connecting various tissues, cirrhotic patients tend to be more susceptible to obesity. On the one hand, the ectopic fat accumulation and muscle loss may form a vicious cycle. Less muscle reduces physical activity, leading to higher obesity risk, while more visceral fat triggers inflammation, contributing to muscle loss [9]. On the other hand, the IR, inflammation, and oxidative stress due to elevated IMCLs are more likely to appear in cirrhosis. IR is characterized by high level of insulin, which results from increased secretion and decreased hepatic clearance. In cirrhosis, liver can only degrade 40% insulin during the first passage, whereas the normal rate is 60% [10]. Cirrhosis is also a proinflammatory state, as there are not only increased cytokines but also more damage-associated molecular patterns produced by necrotic hepatocytes [10]. And the two conditions are linked by oxidative stress, thus further worsening the disease progress [9].

As for hormonal changes, cirrhosis is associated with aging and testosterone reduction, which are the risk factors to sarcopenia. Furthermore, cirrhosis can induce resistance to GH, and impaired GH-IGF-1 system, in turn, leads to cirrhosis complications [9].

Cirrhosis further aggravates Sa-O via hyperammonemia, a distinct and self-aggravating pathway. Due to impaired urea cycle and portosystemic shunts, the serum ammonia level in cirrhosis is abnormally high. Excessive ammonia uptake in myocytes will trigger mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and increased myostatin expression, initiating a cascade which promotes obesity and inhibits protein synthesis. The muscle loss then diminishes the ammonia detoxification, further elevating ammonia levels and forming a deleterious feedback loop [10].

Diagnosis of sarcopenic obesity

Although Sa-O is commonly defined as the co-existence of sarcopenia and obesity, its practical diagnosis lacks standardization. Consensus exists for diagnosing sarcopenia using skeletal muscle index (SMI) cutoffs (< 38 cm2/m2 for women and < 42 cm2/m2 for men in Asian population, < 39 cm2/m2 for women and < 50 cm2/m2 for men in Caucasian population) [28, 29]. In contrast, how to define the obesity component remains controversial due to varying parameters and cutoffs. This is particularly challenging in patients with cirrhosis, where ascites, edema and other complications can significantly distort body composition indicators.

In the general population

According to the latest consensus from the European Society for Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism and the European Association for the Study of Obesity, the diagnosis of Sa-O should integrate both skeletal muscle functional and body composition parameters. For functional assessment, muscle strength and chair-stand test are recommended. For body composition, dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry (DXA) is the preferred method, with bioelectrical impedance analysis as a practical alternative. When feasible, computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) can provide more detailed evaluation. In clinical screening, waist circumference and body mass index (BMI) are also considered effective tools [30].

In cirrhosis

The diagnosis of Sa-O in cirrhosis lacks standardized criteria, leading to significant heterogeneity in research methodologies. As summarized in Table 1, the diagnostic criteria vary significantly in their chosen parameters and thresholds.

Muscle mass is the most frequently assessed dimension. The majority of studies employed CT-based SMI, using standard and population-derived cutoffs, which is considered the golden standard for diagnosing sarcopenia in isolation. Variations include the application of study-specific cutoffs. While the customized cutoffs may suit a particular study population, they limit clinical generalizability and hinder comparisons across different studies. Alternative method, such as the DXA-based ratio of appendicular lean mass to BMI, have also been applied, though their diagnostic thresholds require further validation [31]. Though incorporating BMI into sarcopenia criteria can adjust for the confounding effect of obesity, it also introduces the limitations of BMI’s inaccuracy in cirrhotic patients, whose body composition is highly susceptible to metabolic disorders. This methodological limitation may explain why Sa-O defined by such criteria in this study showed a significant association with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) and liver fibrosis, but not consistently with cirrhosis. Besides, one study employed ultrasound, defining sarcopenia based on low arm or quadriceps muscle index using cutoffs set at the 5th percentile of a small and healthy reference cohort [19]. While portable and radiation-free, the generalizability of this ultrasound-based approach is currently limited by the small and non-diverse validation cohort. And it requires further standardization for wider clinical use.

The assessment of muscle quality represents a more advanced but less commonly applied diagnostic dimension. One study defined sarcopenia using a dual-threshold criterion: low muscle volume (< 25th percentile of the UK Biobank) and high muscle fat infiltration (> 75th percentile) [32]. By integrating both mass and composition, this criterion directly targets the pathological core of sarcopenic obesity, making it theoretically superior to methods relying on muscle quantity alone. However, the clinical translation of this criterion is contingent on the verification and standardization of its cutoffs.

Muscle function assessment is insufficiently emphasized in existing studies. Among the reviewed studies, merely three included evaluations of muscle strength, with a single study classifying the decline in muscle mass and strength as sarcopenia and dynapenia respectively [24]. The most applied method to test muscle strength is handgrip strength (Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia: <28 kg for men and < 18 kg for women, European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People: <27 kg for men and < 16 kg for women) [33, 34]. Physical performance tests are rarely included primarily due to practical constraints and the perspective that they are not indispensable to diagnosis.

As for obesity, BMI remains the most commonly used method due to its convenience. Recognizing its distortion by ascites and edema, some studies have attempted corrections. Some studies subtracted estimated weight attributable to ascites (5% for mild, 10% for moderate, and 15% for severe ascites) from the measured scale weight, and an additional 5% weight reduction was applied if bilateral pedal edema was present [18, 35]. Another just simply excluded patients with ascites or edema [24]. These corrected BMI values demonstrated superior prognostic value over the uncorrected ones in the reviewed studies. However, BMI is still uncapable to differentiate the adipose tissue distribution, which may contribute to phenomena like the “obesity paradox”, as the metabolic and prognostic impacts of visceral and subcutaneous adipose tissue are quite different.

To overcome the limitations of BMI, several studies have utilized CT-based measures. Visceral adipose tissue area (VATA), with a diagnostic cutoff (> 100 cm2, recently updated to > 80 cm2), and visceral-to-subcutaneous adipose area ratio (VSR) have been applied [36]. However, the lack of standardized and universally accepted cutoffs for these novel parameters remains a major barrier. One research team applied different VSR cutoffs across studies to adjust for the effect of substantial variability in subcutaneous adipose tissue area (SATA), which was susceptible to the severity of liver disease. Given that patients with end-stage liver disease tend to exhibit greater depletion of subcutaneous adipose tissue, a higher cutoff was necessary for this cohort. This appears to support that VSR has a greater advantage than VATA considering the overall condition of cirrhotic patients. Another study defined obesity as a state in which both VATA and SATA exceeded the mean values of the matched control group [32]. This approach exhibited methodologically validity yet lacks generalizability in practice, given its dependence on an ideal matched reference group.

One study adopted DXA-based body fat (≥ 25% for men, ≥ 35% for women) to diagnose obesity [31]. This method also suffers from a lack consensus regarding optimal thresholds in cirrhosis. The variability in both method (e.g., corrected BMI, CT/MRI parameters, body fat percentage) and cutoffs is a fundamental reason why current studies can’t be directly compared or synthesized in meta-analysis.

For patients with cirrhosis undergoing routine CT or MRI, it is advisable to utilize the available imaging data particularly SMI and VATA for the assessment of Sa-O. While consensus cutoffs for SMI and VATA exist for the general population to assess sarcopenia and obesity respectively, their validity and optimal thresholds specifically in cirrhosis remain to be established. Handgrip strength is recommended when feasible. VSR shows promising potential but requires validation through multi-center, large-cohort studies to define standardized thresholds.

Mortality

Sa-O is an independent predictor of increased all-cause and liver-related mortality in cirrhosis, with a prognostic value exceeding that of either sarcopenia or obesity alone [22]. In compensated cirrhosis, studies indicate that the transition to a Sa-O body composition is significantly associated with increased liver-related mortality, with this association being more pronounced in patients with well-compensated (Child-Pugh A) cirrhosis [9, 37]. In decompensated cirrhosis and post-transplant settings, Sa-O remains a high-risk phenotype. The one-year and three-year survival rates of decompensated cirrhotic patients with Sa-O after liver transplantation were significantly lower. And the risk of death was markedly higher in patients with Sa-O who were in urgent need of transplantation [22]. The association between Sa-O and mortality persists after adjustment for key confounders including age, gender, complications, comorbidities and severity of liver disease, confirming Sa-O as a robust and independent prognostic factor across the entire spectrum of cirrhosis [11, 12]. In line with this, our previous study has identified four independent predictors of Sa-O and developed an ABAH sore, which effectively predicts all-cause mortality in cirrhosis [38]. This further highlights the clinical importance of assessing body composition.

While numerous studies have demonstrated that sarcopenia is associated with worse prognosis in cirrhotic patients, several studies found that obesity exerts a protective effect, a phenomenon termed the “obesity paradox”. One study, which defined obesity using BMI, observed this paradox in an older population aged over 65 years [24]. This effect may partly reflect the limitation of BMI in capturing the precise distribution of adipose tissue, as some studies have shown that SATA is correlated with a lower risk of death [23]. Given that the obesity paradox was observed in older populations, it may also relate to age-specific factors such as the frailty, which is more prevalent among elderly individuals.

Acute decompensation and acute-on-chronic liver failure

Acute decompensation (AD) and ACLF represent the sudden and severe deteriorations in cirrhosis. While direct evidence linking Sa-O to a higher incidence of AD or ACLF is currently lacking, the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms suggest a probable association. Sa-O can promote conditions that predispose to decompensation though gut dysbiosis-induced endotoxemia, chronic inflammation, and IR. The resulting metabolic dysfunction and impaired immunity likely heighten the vulnerability to ACLF, especially in patients already compromised by malnutrition. The individual components of Sa-O are established risk factors. Sarcopenia is a significant predictor of ACLF, and is associated with more severe complications in ACLF patients, such as hepatorenal syndrome-acute kidney injury and variceal bleeding [19]. Obesity is also an independent predictor of ACLF in cirrhosis and of AD in patients with MASLD-related cirrhosis. These mechanisms may potentially increase susceptibility to ACLF and AD, although this relationship remains to be established in cirrhosis-specific Sa-O populations.

Complications

Hepatic complications

The diverse complications associated with Sa-O in cirrhosis are primarily driven by the core pathophysiological mechanisms, including a chronic inflammatory environment, IR, malnutrition, hyperammonemia and other metabolic disturbances. IR serves as a key driver of liver-related diabetes, and together with hyperammonemia, plays a critical role in increasing the risk of hepatic encephalopathy. In line with these mechanisms, previous studies have demonstrated a significant association between Sa-O and hepatic encephalopathy [35]. Regarding the classic cirrhotic complications (ascites, variceal bleeding, spontaneous bacterial peritonitis), sarcopenia alone is a well-established risk factor. For Sa-O, current evidence suggests an association with an increased risk of variceal bleeding, whereas associations with other complications remain suggestive and have not yet reached statistical significance [35, 37]. Beyond these findings, Sa-O has also been linked to portopulmonary hypertension, which is correlated with a higher prevalence of portosystemic shunts and larger inferior vena cava diameters [23].

Extrahepatic complications

Emerging evidence indicates that Sa-O is correlated with higher prevalence of hepatorenal syndrome-acute kidney injury [19]. The systemic inflammation and metabolic disturbances in Sa-O impair the immune function, elevating the risk of infections. Moreover, Sa-O often coexists with myosteatosis (muscle fat infiltration), thereby increasing the risk of hepatic encephalopathy and severe infections, including septicemia and toxemia [9]. In addition, through mechanisms involving angiosteosis and abnormal bone metabolism, Sa-O may be associated with reduced bone mineral density and a higher incidence of osteoporosis [39].

Malignancy-related outcomes

Both sarcopenia and obesity are independent risk factors for hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). However, evidence regarding the association between Sa-O and cancer clinical outcomes remains scarce, with available data largely derived from studies in the general population. These studies suggest a potential association between Sa-O and an increased risk of HCC in cirrhosis, a more specific and clinically relevant population [40, 41].

Liver transplantation

As one of the definitive outcomes for end-stage cirrhosis, liver transplantation has also been shown to be affected by Sa-O. Among patients on the waitlist, Sa-O is associated with an increased risk of death and delisting, as well as a decreased likelihood of receiving a transplant [12]. In the post-transplant period, Sa-O adversely affects recovery, leading to prolonged hospitalization and a higher incidence of both surgical complications (e.g., hepatic artery thrombosis, portal thrombosis, minimal liver syndrome) and postoperative metabolic syndrome (e.g., diabetes, dyslipidemia) [13, 42]. While sarcopenia often persists after transplantation, obesity independently contributes to an elevated risk of infections, bacteremia and HCC recurrence.

Health-related quality of Life

Sa-O negatively impacts functional status and health related quality of life in aging population [43]. In cirrhosis, patients with Sa-O manifests reduced handgrip strength and poorer muscle morphology, reflecting the combined deterioration of muscle mass and quality that influence the physical function [35]. This functional impairment, compounded by the prevalent sedentary lifestyle in this population, may establish a vicious cycle that perpetuates Sa-O. Furthermore, patients with Sa-O potentially report more severe fatigue than those with either sarcopenia or obesity alone, further diminishing the health-related quality of life [22].

Non-hepatic outcomes

Sa-O significantly contributes to non-hepatic outcomes in cirrhosis. Cardiovascular disease represents the foremost among these. Sa-O elevates cardiovascular risk mainly through key mediating pathways: IR, chronic inflammation, and associated metabolic disorders [9]. These conditions induce excessive adipose tissue deposition. This abnormal accumulation, in turn, directly promotes the development of atherosclerosis, dyslipidemia and hypertension, all of which are major risk factors for cardiovascular disease [23, 44]. Consistently, clinical studies have demonstrated that Sa-O is significantly associated with a higher prevalence of coronary heart disease, a common and severe manifestation of such cardiovascular events [13]. Sa-O is also linked to reduced bone mineral density and an increased risk of osteoporosis. This association is particularly pronounced after liver transplantation, during which accelerated bone loss substantially raises the susceptibility to low-impact fractures [42].

Prognostic scores

The Child-Pugh and MELD scores are the cornerstone for prognostic assessment in cirrhosis. Research has consistently shown that Sa-O is significantly associated with higher Child-Pugh, MELD, and MELD-Na scores [19, 21]. While prognostic models have begun to integrate sarcopenia, such as MELD-sarcopenia scores, parameters that include obesity or myosteatosis are still lacking. Given the observed correlation between Sa-O and conventional scores, future research should evaluate whether adding Sa-O, or its individual components could provide incremental prognostic value for risk stratification. This may be accomplished by: (1) incorporating quantitative body composition parameters (e.g., SMI and VATA) into existing models as continuous or categorical variables, or (2) developing a dedicated Sa-O score based on routinely available clinical and imaging data. Prior to clinical implementation, prospective cohort studies with head-to-head comparisons of model performance are warranted to validate predictive accuracy and clinical utility.

Future perspectives

Increasing attention has been paid to muscle quality, particularly myosteatosis, as an important component of body composition in cirrhosis. Myosteatosis, characterized by increased lipid infiltration within skeletal muscle and reduced muscle radiodensity, has been associated with adverse clinical outcomes, including mortality and complications, independent of muscle mass. In the context of Sa-O, the coexistence of excess adiposity and impaired muscle quality may further exacerbate metabolic dysfunction, systemic inflammation, and physical frailty. This suggests that muscle quantity alone may not fully capture the prognostic impact of altered body composition in cirrhosis. However, current definitions of Sa-O are primarily based on the combination of low muscle mass and obesity, without incorporating measures of muscle quality. Whether myosteatosis should be included as an additional diagnostic criterion for Sa-O remains an area of ongoing debate. Incorporating muscle quality parameters, such as radiodensity or intramuscular fat content, may improve risk stratification, but standardized definitions and cut-off values are still lacking. Therefore, future studies are needed to determine whether integrating myosteatosis into the diagnostic framework of Sa-O would enhance its clinical relevance and prognostic utility in cirrhosis.

Conclusion

Sa-O has emerged as a potentially intervenable condition with significant prognostic implications in patients with cirrhosis. Throughout this review, we distinguish between evidence derived from cirrhosis-specific studies of Sa-O and findings extrapolated from related conditions, given the current limitations of the literature. Accumulating evidence indicates that Sa-O is associated with increased mortality and adverse post-transplant outcomes. Evidence has also suggested potential associations between Sa-O and selected classic and specific complications of cirrhosis, although the supporting data remain relatively limited. While cardiovascular disease has been reported in this context, it has rarely been addressed as a primary outcome. Moreover, current evidence regarding AD and ACLF mainly focuses on the individual effects of sarcopenia or obesity, rather than Sa-O as a combined entity. Scarce data exist on health-related quality of life and the incorporation of Sa-O into established prognostic scoring systems, underscoring the need for more targeted investigations. From a clinical perspective, these findings highlight Sa-O as a promising parameter for risk stratification and potentially for transplant listing decisions. Meanwhile, lifestyle interventions, including adequate protein intake, fat restriction, and regular physical activity, may play a meaningful role in preventing or mitigating the progression of Sa-O in cirrhosis. However, the lack of standardized assessment methods and recognized cutoffs continues to limit the clinical applicability of Sa-O. Future multi-center, prospective studies are essential to establish unified and practical criteria, facilitating the integration of Sa-O into routine clinical practice and prognostic evaluation.
