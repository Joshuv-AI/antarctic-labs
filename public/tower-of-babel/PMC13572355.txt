A pan-cancer analysis of MEX3D in human tumors
Authors: Xuezhong Zhang, Wen Xu, Xiaolei Wang, Chengbin Lei, Meilin Gao, Tingting Chu, Xuebin Zhang, Tonggang Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMEX3D, a member of the MEX3 RNA-binding protein family, has emerged as a potential regulatory molecule in cancer. However, its role across different tumor types remains largely unexplored.MethodsWe conducted a pan-cancer analysis of MEX3D using transcriptomic and proteomic data from the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx), and Clinical Proteomic Tumor Analysis Consortium (CPTAC). Expression patterns, clinical correlations, survival outcomes, genetic alterations, RNA modification associations, immune infiltration, and functional enrichment were systematically evaluated.ResultsMEX3D was significantly dysregulated in numerous cancers at both mRNA and protein levels. Its expression correlated with tumor stage in ACC, LIHC, OV, SKCM, and THCA. Elevated MEX3D expression was associated with poor overall survival (OS) and disease-specific survival (DSS) in multiple malignancies, including ACC, LGG, LUAD, and MESO. Genetic alteration analysis revealed frequent amplifications and mutations, particularly in SARC and OV. MEX3D was positively correlated with RNA modification-related genes (m1A, m5C, m6A) and immune regulatory genes such as CD276, TGFB1, VEGFA, and ICOSLG. Additionally, MEX3D expression showed significant associations with tumor mutational burden (TMB), microsatellite instability (MSI), and cancer-associated fibroblast infiltration. Functional enrichment analyses indicated that MEX3D-related genes are involved in reproductive cellular processes, RNA binding, the Hippo signaling pathway, and microRNA-related oncogenic pathways.ConclusionThis pan-cancer analysis highlights the heterogeneous expression and cancer-specific prognostic significance of MEX3D. MEX3D is associated with immune infiltration, immune regulatory genes, RNA modification-related genes, TMB/MSI, and pathways involved in gene regulation and tumor progression. These findings suggest that MEX3D may participate in cancer-specific post-transcriptional and microenvironmental regulatory networks.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s12672-026-05511-1.Keywords: MEX3D, Pan-cancer analysis, Gene expression, Tumor microenvironment, Immune infiltration, Biomarker

Introduction

The advent of bioinformatics has revolutionized cancer research by enabling the systematic analysis of genetic, transcriptomic, and epigenetic alterations across diverse cancer types [1, 2]. Pan-cancer analyses provide a holistic perspective by investigating the shared and unique molecular mechanisms underlying different tumor types [3–7]. Such studies utilize extensive datasets, including those from TCGA and GTEx project, to identify patterns in gene expression, mutational landscapes, and their clinical relevance.

The human MEX3 (muscle excess 3) family, a subset of the evolutionarily conserved RNA-binding protein (RBP) family, comprises four isoforms (MEX3A-D) that encode phosphorylated proteins with distinct expression patterns [8]. Each MEX3 protein contains two K homology (KH) domains for RNA binding and is uniquely characterized by a C-terminal RING domain functioning as a ubiquitin E3 ligase domain [9, 10]. As one molecule of MEX3 family, MEX3D, located on chromosome 19p13.3, has recently drawn attention for its potential role in tumorigenesis and cancer progression. MEX3D, in particular, has shown preliminary evidence of dysregulation in several cancers, including CESC [11], LIHC [12], LUAD [13], LUSC [13] and PRAD [14], suggesting its involvement in cancer biology. However, a comprehensive understanding of MEX3D’s role across multiple human cancers remains unexplored. This study aims to address this gap by utilizing bioinformatics tools to investigate the relationship between MEX3D expression and tumor biology across diverse human cancers.

Understanding the role of MEX3D in a pan-cancer framework holds significant implications for cancer research and clinical practice. By uncovering the molecular mechanisms underlying its functions, researchers can identify novel biomarkers for early diagnosis, prognosis, and therapeutic response prediction. Furthermore, characterizing MEX3D’s interactions with other key regulatory molecules and pathways may illuminate new targets for precision medicine. The systematic bioinformatics analysis of MEX3D across human tumors thus represents a critical step toward advancing our understanding of cancer biology and improving patient outcomes through targeted interventions.

Materials and methods

Gene expression analysis

RNA-seq data for ENSG00000181588 (MEX3D) in transcripts per million (TPM) format were sourced from UCSC Xena (https://xenabrowser.net/datapages/) for the TCGA and GTEx datasets, including TCGA-ACC, TCGA-BLCA, TCGA-BRCA, TCGA-CESC, TCGA-CHOL, TCGA-COAD, TCGA-DLBC, TCGA-ESCA, TCGA-GBM, TCGA-HNSC, TCGA-KICH, TCGA-KIRC, TCGA-KIRP, TCGA-LAML, TCGA-LGG, TCGA-LIHC, TCGA-LUAD, TCGA-LUSC, TCGA-MESO, TCGA-OV, TCGA-PAAD, TCGA-PCPG, TCGA-PRAD, TCGA-READ, TCGA-SARC, TCGA-STAD, TCGA-SKCM, TCGA-TGCT, TCGA-THCA, TCGA-THYM, TCGA-UCEC, TCGA-UCS, and TCGA-UVM, and processed using the Toil pipeline [15]. Comprehensive statistical analyses were performed, and the expression levels of MEX3D across various cancer types are summarized in Supplementary Table 1. MEX3D expression was analyzed across various sample types, including cancerous and normal tissues. The analysis employed R software (version 4.2.1) and the ggplot2 package (version 3.4.4). Expression data retrieved from the TCGA_GTEx dataset were filtered using a threshold of |log2 fold change (LogFC)| ≥ 1, with statistical significance defined as P < 0.01. Unpaired Wilcoxon rank-sum and signed-rank tests were performed, and boxplots were created using ggplot2. Violin plots displaying MEX3D expression across cancer stages were generated using GEPIA2 (http://gepia2.cancer-pku.cn/#analysis) [16]. Protein expression analysis was conducted using the UALCAN portal (http://ualcan.path.uab.edu/analysis-prot.html) and the CPTAC dataset [17].

Survival prognosis analysis

The TCGA Pan-Cancer dataset was retrieved from UCSC Xena (https://xenabrowser.net/) [15]. MEX3D expression data, along with associated survival information, were extracted, excluding samples with follow-up durations of fewer than 30 days. Survival analysis was performed using the survival package (version 3.3.1) in R, with statistical significance assessed through the log-rank test (P < 0.05). Heatmaps and forest plots visualizing the findings were generated using ggplot2 (version 3.4.4). Supplementary Table 2. The relationship between MEX3D and overall survival (OS), and disease specific survival (DSS) in each cancer type.

Genetic alteration analysis

Data on genetic alterations, including mutation frequency, mutation types, site-specific mutations, and copy number variations (CNA), were collected from cBioPortal [cBioPortal for Cancer Genomics: MEX3D in Cervical Squamous Cell Carcinoma (TCGA, PanCancer Atlas) and 31 other studies [18], utilizing TCGA datasets spanning multiple cancer types. Cancer types summary of MEX3D genetic alteration are detailed in Supplementary Table 3. Comparisons of overall survival (OS) and disease-specific survival (DSS) between tumors with and without MEX3D alterations were conducted using the “Comparison” tool in cBioPortal.

RNA modification, immune checkpoints, and immune moderator genes of MEX3D

A standardized pan-cancer dataset was downloaded from UCSC Xena [15], including expression data for ENSG00000181588 (MEX3D) and 44 marker genes linked to RNA modifications (m1A, m5C, and m6A) [19]. Supplementary Table 4. The correlation coefficient and P value of RNA modification analysis of MEX3D in various tumor types. Additionally, expression data for 150 immune moderator genes across five pathways (chemokine, receptor, MHC, immunoinhibitor, and immunostimulator) and 60 immune checkpoint genes were extracted [20]. The expression values were log2-transformed, and correlations were analyzed using the corrplot (version 0.90) and psych (version 2.1.6) packages in R. Heatmaps illustrating the results were generated with ggplot2 (version 3.4.4).

Immune infiltration analysis

The relationship between MEX3D expression and immune cell infiltration was assessed using TIMER2 (https://compbio.cn/timer2/MEX3D) [21]. Correlations were visualized through heatmaps and scatter plots. Immune infiltration levels were quantified using algorithms including TIMER, EPIC, XCELL, MCPCOUNTER, CIBERSORT-ABS, QUANTISEQ, and CIBERSORT.

Tumor mutational burden (TMB) and microsatellite instability (MSI)

Expression data for MEX3D were extracted from the TCGA Pan-Cancer dataset via UCSC Xena. TMB data, acquired from the GDC portal, were processed using MuTect2 [22] and analyzed using the maftools package (version 2.8.05). MSI scores were retrieved from prior research [23]. Correlations between MEX3D expression and TMB or MSI were examined using R. Supplementary Table 7. The origion data of MEX3D expression correlated withTumor Mutational Burden (TMB) expression in cancer. Supplementary Table 8. The origion data of MEX3D expression correlated with Microsatellite Instability (MSI) expression in cancer.

MEX3D-related gene enrichment analysis

The STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org/) was used to identify proteins interacting with MEX3D, with parameters set to a minimum interaction score of 0.150 and a maximum of 50 interactors (https://cn.string-db.org/cgi/network?taskId=bEXQar3dJ0ik&sessionId=bod6bAtozByS) [24]. GEPIA2 identified the top 100 genes correlated with MEX3D in TCGA datasets (http://gepia2.cancer-pku.cn/#similar) [16]. Pearson correlation analysis evaluated the relationships between MEX3D and the selected genes, and a dot plot of mean log2TPM values was generated. Heatmaps were produced using the “Gene_Corr” module in TIMER2. Partial correlation coefficients and P-values were calculated via tumor purity-adjusted Spearman’s rank correlation. Functional enrichment analyses, including Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) [25] and Gene Ontology (GO) [26], were performed using the clusterProfiler package (version 4.4.4). Circular charts illustrating these analyses were created using ggplot2 (version 3.4.4). Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05 and a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.25. The detailed data of GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses about MEX3D related-genes are shown in Supplementary Table 9.

Results

Gene expression analysis data

MEX3D expression was analyzed using the TCGA and GTEx datasets from the UCSC database, comparing cancerous tissues with their normal counterparts. As shown in Fig. 1A, MEX3D expression was significantly elevated in several cancers, including ACC, BLCA, BRCA, CESC, CHOL, COAD, DLBC, ESCA, GBM, HNSC, KIRC, KIRP, LGG, LIHC, LUAD, LUSC, OV, PAAD, PCPG, PRAD, READ, STAD, THCA, and THYM. Conversely, its expression was significantly reduced in LAML, SKCM, TGCT, and UCS.

Survival prognosis analysis

The prognostic significance of MEX3D expression across cancers was evaluated by stratifying tumors into high and low expression groups using TCGA data. As depicted in Fig. 2A, elevated MEX3D expression was significantly associated with poorer OS in ACC (P = 0.0050), LGG (P = 0.0003), LUAD (P = 0.0442), MESO (P = 0.0003), and SARC (P = 0.0095). Conversely, reduced MEX3D expression correlated with worse OS in UVM (P = 0.0342).

Relationship Between MEX3D Expression and Tumor Prognosis in TCGA. The association between MEX3D gene expression and survival outcomes in various tumors within the TCGA dataset was explored. Both overall survival (A) and disease-specific survival (B) were included in the study. (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001)

High MEX3D expression was linked to poorer DSS in ACC (P = 0.0065), KIRP (P = 0.0302), LGG (P = 0.0005), LUAD (P = 0.0423), MESO (P = 0.0007), READ (P = 0.0427), and SARC (P = 0.0217). In contrast, reduced MEX3D expression was associated with worse DSS outcomes in UVM (P = 0.0141) (Fig. 2B). These findings suggest that MEX3D serves as a key prognostic biomarker, influencing both OS and DSS in multiple cancers.

Genetic alteration analysis

The frequency and types of MEX3D gene alterations across cancers were analyzed using TCGA data. As illustrated in Fig. 3A, the highest alteration frequency was observed in SARC (7.45%), primarily due to “amplification,” followed by CESC (4.38%) and OV (3.42%), which exhibited “deep deletion.” Additional cancers, such as LGG and STAD, showed alteration frequencies of 2.92% and 2.5%, respectively, with “amplification” and “mutation” being the predominant genetic variations in these cases. In certain cancers, including UCS, AML, TGCT, LUAD, KIRP, and KIRC, “mutation” was the sole detected genetic alteration. Meanwhile, “amplification” was the only alteration in cancers such as PCPG, UVM, MESO, ACC, and THYM.

RNA modification

Emerging evidence highlights the crucial role of RNA modifications in cancer progression, including angiogenesis [27, 28]. As depicted in Fig. 4, a strong positive correlation exists between MEX3D and several RNA modification-related genes, particularly those involved in m1A, m5C, and m6A modifications. These correlations were statistically significant in multiple cancers, including DLBC, THYM, UCEC, KIRP, LIHC, GBM, OV, PCPG, ACC, KIRC, BLCA, HNSC, TGCT, UVM, CHOL, PRAD, THCA, SARC, UCS, LAML, BRCA, PAAD, MESO, LUSC, LUAD, COAD, STAD, READ, and SKCM. Furthermore, MEX3D exhibited a positive association with specific m6A-modifying enzymes, such as ELAVL1, across all examined cancers. To further validate the correlations observed in the TCGA dataset, we analysed independent ICGC cohorts of LIHC, PAAD, and READ. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 1, MEX3D expression was significantly positively correlated with the RNA modification-related genes ELAVL1 and YTHDF1 across all three cancer types. In LIHC, the correlation coefficients were R = 0.320 for ELAVL1 and R = 0.637 for YTHDF1 (Supplementary Fig. 1A). In PAAD, MEX3D showed significant positive correlations with ELAVL1 (R = 0.209) and YTHDF1 (R = 0.233) (Supplementary Fig. 1B). Similarly, in READ, MEX3D expression was significantly positively associated with ELAVL1 (R = 0.281) and YTHDF1 (R = 0.453) (Supplementary Fig. 1C). These results collectively support the association between MEX3D and RNA modification genes across several cancer types. MEX3D expression was broadly associated with RNA modification-related genes, suggesting that MEX3D may participate in RNA modification-related regulatory network. The current analysis cannot determine whether MEX3D acts upstream of RNA modification regulators, is a downstream target, or is simply co-expressed with these genes.

Using computational algorithms, including TIMER, CIBERSORT, CIBERSORT-ABS, TIDE, XCEL, MCPCOUNTER, QUANTISEQ, and EPIC, we analyzed the relationship between immune cell infiltration and MEX3D expression across various cancers in the TCGA dataset. As shown in Fig. 5A and B, MEX3D expression was positively correlated with cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) in BRCA-LumA, CESC, ESCA, HNSC, HNSC-HPV-, KIRC, KIRP, OV, and STAD. These findings highlight the intricate interactions between MEX3D and the TME in diverse cancer types.

Discussion

In this pan-cancer study, we systematically evaluated the expression pattern, prognostic value, genetic alteration, immune relevance, RNA modification-related associations, and potential biological pathways of MEX3D. Overall, our findings suggest that MEX3D is not uniformly oncogenic or tumor suppressive across all malignancies. Instead, its expression and clinical relevance appear to be highly cancer-type dependent, consistent with the context-specific functions commonly observed for RNA-binding proteins.

MEX3D showed a heterogeneous expression pattern across cancer types. Its mRNA expression was elevated in multiple tumors, including ACC, BLCA, BRCA, CESC, CHOL, COAD, ESCA, GBM, HNSC, KIRC, KIRP, LGG, LIHC, LUAD, LUSC, OV, PAAD, PRAD, READ, STAD, THCA, and THYM, whereas reduced expression was observed in LAML, SKCM, TGCT, and UCS. At the protein level, increased MEX3D abundance in BRCA, GBM, KIRC, LUAD, and LUSC further supported its possible involvement in tumor biology in these cancer types. However, the opposite expression trend in LAML, SKCM, TGCT, and UCS indicates that MEX3D may function differently depending on tissue lineage, cellular state, and tumor-specific regulatory networks. This heterogeneity is consistent with the broader biology of RNA-binding proteins, whose functions are often determined by the transcriptomic and proteomic context of individual tissues [8]. Therefore, MEX3D should not be interpreted as a universal oncogene; rather, its role requires cancer-specific evaluation. The prognostic analyses further support this context-dependent interpretation. High MEX3D expression was associated with poorer OS in ACC, LGG, LUAD, MESO, and SARC, and with poorer DSS in ACC, KIRP, LGG, LUAD, MESO, READ, and SARC. In contrast, lower MEX3D expression was associated with unfavorable OS and DSS in UVM. These divergent survival associations suggest that MEX3D may promote aggressive tumor behavior in some cancers while maintaining protective or homeostatic functions in others. The association between MEX3D expression and tumor stage in ACC, LIHC, OV, SKCM, and THCA also suggests a potential link between MEX3D and tumor progression. Nevertheless, these associations should be interpreted cautiously, as the present study is based on retrospective bioinformatic analyses and does not establish causality. Future studies using longitudinal cohorts and functional models are needed to determine whether MEX3D directly contributes to tumor progression or primarily reflects underlying biological states.

Genetic alteration analysis also revealed cancer-specific patterns [27]. MEX3D amplification was observed in several tumor types, including SARC, PCPG, UVM, MESO, ACC, and THYM, whereas mutation or deep deletion events were observed in other cancers. These findings suggest that different types of genomic alterations may have distinct biological consequences. Amplification may contribute to increased MEX3D expression and potential gain-of-function effects in selected cancers, whereas mutation or deletion may reflect different mechanisms of dysregulation. The G311R/W missense mutation identified in STAD is of interest, although no significant survival difference was observed between patients with and without MEX3D alterations in this cancer type. Functional studies are required to determine whether specific MEX3D mutations affect its RNA-binding ability, E3 ligase activity, subcellular localization, or interaction with partner proteins.

An important finding of this study is the association between MEX3D and immune infiltration or immune regulatory genes. MEX3D expression correlated with cancer-associated fibroblast infiltration in several cancers, including BRCA-LumA, CESC, ESCA, HNSC, HNSC-HPV−, KIRC, KIRP, OV, and STAD. Because CAFs contribute to extracellular matrix remodeling, immune exclusion, angiogenesis, and therapeutic resistance [28, 29], the association between MEX3D and CAF infiltration suggests that MEX3D may be linked to stromal remodeling in specific tumor microenvironments. However, this relationship may also reflect the enrichment of stromal or mesenchymal transcriptional programs rather than a direct regulatory role of MEX3D. Experimental validation, such as MEX3D perturbation in tumor cells followed by co-culture with fibroblasts or spatial transcriptomic analysis, would help clarify whether MEX3D actively influences CAF recruitment or activation.

MEX3D also showed correlations with immune regulatory genes, including immunoinhibitory, immunostimulatory, chemokine receptor, and MHC-related genes. Positive correlations with molecules such as CD276, TGFB1, and VEGFA suggest that MEX3D may be associated with immunosuppressive and angiogenic features of the tumor microenvironment. At the same time, correlations with immune stimulatory genes, including HMGB1, TNFSF9, TNFSF4, CX3CL1, and ICOSLG, indicate that MEX3D may also be linked to immune activation signals in certain contexts. This dual pattern suggests that MEX3D is unlikely to function simply as an immune suppressor or immune activator across all cancers. Instead, it may be associated with broader immune remodeling programs that differ by tumor type. In cancers where MEX3D positively correlates with MHC genes and chemokine receptors, it may be linked to antigen presentation and immune cell recruitment. In cancers where negative correlations predominate, MEX3D may instead be associated with immune escape. These findings provide a rationale for future mechanistic studies, but they do not yet support direct clinical use of MEX3D as an immunotherapy biomarker.

The observed associations between MEX3D and TMB/MSI further suggest a possible relationship between MEX3D and tumor genomic instability or DNA repair-related states. Because TMB and MSI are clinically relevant biomarkers in selected cancers, the correlation between MEX3D and these indicators may have potential implications for patient stratification. However, the direction and strength of these associations varied among cancer types, indicating that MEX3D should not be considered a universal surrogate marker for TMB or MSI. Instead, MEX3D may participate in, or reflect, cancer-specific molecular programs related to mutation burden, mismatch repair deficiency, or immune recognition. Further studies integrating MEX3D expression with genomic instability signatures, DNA repair pathway activity, and immunotherapy response cohorts will be necessary to clarify this possibility.

Our analysis also identified significant correlations between MEX3D and genes involved in RNA modifications, including m1A, m5C, and m6A-related regulators. RNA modifications regulate RNA stability, splicing, translation, and degradation, and they are increasingly recognized as important contributors to cancer progression [30, 31]. As a member of the MEX3 family, MEX3D contains RNA-binding KH domains and a C-terminal RING finger domain, suggesting that it may connect post-transcriptional RNA regulation with ubiquitin-mediated protein regulation. This domain organization is shared with other MEX3 family members, which have been implicated in RNA fate control, cell proliferation, differentiation, and tumor biology. Therefore, the correlation between MEX3D and RNA modification-related genes may indicate that MEX3D participates in RNA regulatory networks rather than acting as an RNA modification enzyme itself.

Several testable hypotheses arise from these findings. First, RNA modifications may regulate MEX3D expression by altering the stability or translation of MEX3D mRNA. Second, MEX3D may preferentially bind modified transcripts through its KH domains and influence their stability or translation. Third, MEX3D may indirectly affect RNA modification pathways by regulating the expression or turnover of RNA modification regulators. These possibilities could be tested using MEX3D knockdown or overexpression models combined with RNA immunoprecipitation, CLIP-seq, m6A/m5C/m1A profiling, and transcriptome-wide RNA stability assays. Such experiments would help determine whether the observed correlations reflect direct molecular interactions or broader co-regulated transcriptional programs.

Functional enrichment analyses provided additional clues regarding the biological processes potentially associated with MEX3D. The enrichment of pathways such as Hippo signaling, transcriptional misregulation in cancer, and microRNAs in cancer suggests that MEX3D may be connected to key regulatory networks controlling proliferation, cell fate, and gene expression. The Hippo pathway is particularly relevant because it regulates tissue growth, organ size, and tumorigenesis [32]. If MEX3D influences transcripts or proteins involved in Hippo signaling, it may affect tumor cell proliferation, survival, or metastatic potential. Enrichment of microRNA-related pathways also supports a possible role for MEX3D in post-transcriptional gene regulation. In addition, GO terms related to germ cell development and reproductive cellular processes may reflect developmental or stemness-associated programs that are reactivated in cancer. These enrichment results should be considered hypothesis-generating and require experimental validation.

From a therapeutic perspective, our findings suggest potential future directions but do not establish MEX3D as a clinically validated target. Given the structure of MEX3D, more specific targeting strategies could focus on its functional domains. In tumors where high MEX3D expression is associated with poor prognosis, one approach could be to disrupt its KH domain-mediated RNA-binding activity, thereby interfering with the recognition or regulation of oncogenic transcripts. Another possibility is to target the RING finger domain to modulate its putative E3 ubiquitin ligase activity and downstream protein substrates. In addition, RNA-based approaches such as siRNA, antisense oligonucleotides, or targeted degradation strategies may be explored in preclinical models with MEX3D dependency. However, because low MEX3D expression was associated with poor prognosis in UVM, therapeutic inhibition may not be appropriate in all contexts. Any MEX3D-targeted strategy would therefore need to be guided by cancer type, expression level, molecular context, and functional validation.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, most analyses were based on public multi-omics datasets and computational immune infiltration estimates, which may be influenced by tumor purity, batch effects, and differences in analytical platforms. Second, the correlations between MEX3D and immune regulators, RNA modification genes, TMB/MSI, and enriched pathways do not prove direct mechanistic relationships. Third, the prognostic relevance of MEX3D was not validated in independent clinical cohorts or experimental models. Finally, although the domain structure of MEX3D provides plausible mechanistic hypotheses, its direct RNA targets, protein substrates, and cancer-specific regulatory partners remain largely unknown.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this pan-cancer analysis highlights the heterogeneous expression and cancer-specific prognostic significance of MEX3D. MEX3D is associated with immune infiltration, immune regulatory genes, RNA modification-related genes, TMB/MSI, and pathways involved in gene regulation and tumor progression. These findings suggest that MEX3D may participate in cancer-specific post-transcriptional and microenvironmental regulatory networks. Future studies should focus on defining the direct RNA targets and protein substrates of MEX3D, clarifying the functional roles of its KH and RING domains, and validating its biological effects in cancer-specific experimental models. Such work will be essential before MEX3D can be considered a reliable biomarker or therapeutic target.
