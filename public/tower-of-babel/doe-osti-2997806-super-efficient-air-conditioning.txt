 1. Accomplishments:

 What was done? What was learned?

 During the project, the team was able to achieve all of the milestones and the final
 project goal. The final effect of the project is an air conditioning module with a capacity
 of 10 kW (34,120 Btu/hr) operating in a real environment achieving 60-80% energy
 savings in comparison with the traditional system. At this stage the team is focused on
 development of the commercial prototype and continuation of testing and improving of
 different and more advanced versions of the prototypes. Tests were performed in order
 to improve the current design of the heat exchangers and develop better and more
 reliable solutions. The first commercial prototype of the unit was developed and it was
 tested during the last quarter of the project. The team continues their discussions with
 one of the world’s leading outsource manufacturing companies to pilot the commercial
 development of the technology.

 a. What are the major goals and objectives of this project?
 The objective of this project is to develop and validate a fully functional 10 kW (34,120
 Btu/hr) air conditioning unit prototype capable of >60% average energy savings in
 comparison to baseline traditional residential and commercial cooling equipment, with
 a cooling coefficient of performance (COP) >12.5 in humid climatic conditions, net-
 zero water consumption, scalable to medium and large-scale units for different building
 types, and an installed cost of ≤$12/kBtu/h.

 b. Project summary

 • First year of the project

The major achievements during the first year of the project was development and validation
of numerical models and testing of the crucial materials for the future prototypes.

 1. Thermal model

The numerical description of the BDC unit is based on convective heat transfer coefficient
model. The general assumptions of the model used in this study are presented below:
 • Operation in steady state,
 • Airflow is modeled as an ideal gas,
 • Properties of water and air streams are equal to bulk averaged values,
 • The heat losses to the surrounding zone are neglected,
 • Thermal conductivity along the channel length through the plate and water stream is
 negligible.
 • The mass transfer of the water vapor is driven by the partial pressure gradient which
 corresponds to the humidity ratio gradient.

Validation
The model was validated against existing experimental data. Since there are no existing
evaporative cooling units, the model was adjusted and compared to a unit with a more simple
structure, but operating under the same principles (evaporation of water to cool the airflow).
The experiments chosen for comparison are:
 • Experiment performed by Riangvilaikul et al. (B. Riangvilaikul, S. Kumar, An
 experimental study of a novel dew point evaporative cooling system, Energy and
        Buildings, 42 Issue 5 (2010) 637-644.)
 • Experiment performed by Hsu et al. (S. Hsu, Z. Lavan, W. Worek, Optimization of wet-
        surface heat exchangers, Energy 14 (1989) 757–770.)
 • Experiment performed by Dr. Demis Pandelidis, General Manager of the Project (D.
 Pandelidis, A. Cichoń, A. Pacak, S. Anisimov, P. Drag, Performance comparison
 between counter- and cross-flow indirect evaporative coolers for heat recovery in air
 conditioning systems in the presence of condensation in the product air channels,
 International Journal of Heat and Mass Transfer 130 (2019) 757–777)
 • Experiment performed Martinez et al. (F.J. Rey Martinez, E. Velasco Gómez, R.
 Herrero Martin, J. Martinez Gutiérrez, F. Varela Diez, Comparative study of two
 different evaporative systems: an indirect evaporative cooler and a semi-indirect
 ceramic evaporative cooler, Energy and Buildings 36 (2004) 696–708)

In all cases studied, the discrepancies between proposed models and experimental results did
not exceed 5%. The expected accuracy in accordance with G/N-G decision point number 1, the
discrepancy should not exceed 10%. Obtained results fulfill the required conditions; therefore,
this part of G/N-G 1 point is accomplished.

 2. CFD model: water distribution

Proper water distribution is essential for the BDC unit to work properly. For this purpose, a CFD
model in OpenFOAM environment was developed to analyze water distribution inside the
channels. OpenFOAM is an open-source environment with fully changeable root code, which
allows to include authors’ numerical codes if it is needed. The main advantage which follows
from this option is the ability to implement boundary conditions and source codes when it is
needed, which is impossible to obtain with commercial programs (e.g., ANSYS). Low-velocity
flow regimes (𝑀𝑎 < 0.05) are resolved utilizing two-phase simulations based on the Volume
of Fluid (VoF) methodology. This approach models incompressible multiphase flow and
incorporates gravitational body forces."The two-phase simulations are modeled using the
Volume of Fluid (VoF) method, which determines the type of flow. The considered flow
regimes include bubble flow, transient flow, multi-layer flow and wave flow.
The results of the simulations (app. 0.5 sec of operation) are presented below:

the general effectiveness of the system. Due to the complicated nature of the two-phase

simulations can establish results for stabilized conditions.

results show that Sample 2, 3, 4, and 5 may be used as a wet channels filling, when water is
delivered by from upwards to downwards, because it allows distributing water much faster
than other materials.

 2. Commercial prototype development

Following an in-depth scale-up and commercialization analysis conducted by the engineering
and product development team, the design of the BDC unit was thoroughly refined to ensure its
technical and economic feasibility for large-scale deployment in commercial and industrial

environments. This extensive effort involved iterative prototyping, rigorous performance
validation, and close examination of real-world integration requirements. The culmination of
these efforts was the development of the first fully functional commercial prototype of the BDC
unit, which incorporates a series of critical engineering optimizations designed to enhance not
only overall system performance but also operational flexibility and maintainability.

A key objective throughout the design process was to ensure that the BDC precooler would be
highly versatile and adaptable, allowing seamless integration with a wide spectrum of
dehumidification technologies. These include conventional mechanical vapor-compression
refrigeration systems, advanced membrane-based separation units, and both solid and liquid
desiccant technologies. By achieving broad compatibility with multiple approaches to
dehumidification; the BDC unit significantly extends its potential market reach and improves
its suitability for a diverse array of climate control applications in commercial buildings,
industrial process cooling, and data center humidity management.

In addition to its core role in air dehumidification, the BDC unit was strategically designed to
operate as a high-efficiency air precooler for gas turbines and other high-throughput HVAC
systems. This function is particularly valuable in hot and humid regions, where elevated
moisture content in intake air can cause substantial reductions in turbine efficiency and output.
By precooling and partially dehumidifying the incoming air, the BDC unit can substantially
mitigate performance losses, improve fuel utilization, and extend the operational envelope of
gas turbine systems.

As detailed in the previous report, the current commercial iteration of the BDC system has been
engineered to handle an air supply stream of 8000 m³/h. The unit architecture is based on a
modular design consisting of six independent BDC cooling elements, with each functioning as
a standalone sub-unit equipped with two high-efficiency EBM EC fans. These fans are arranged
in a fan-wall configuration – a design strategy that offers numerous benefits, including enhanced
redundancy, better airflow modulation, and significant reductions in operational noise levels
(as shown in Fig. 3a). This modularity also allows for simplified maintenance and scalable
deployment, enabling users to tailor system capacity based on specific project requirements.

Each cooling module is enclosed in a robust, professionally bent stainless steel frame, which
provides mechanical integrity and resistance to corrosion in demanding operating
environments. Inside each module, a custom-designed extruded plastic heat exchanger
facilitates efficient heat transfer while minimizing weight and manufacturing cost (Fig. 3b). The
integrated water distribution system, as presented in Figure 1b, ensures uniform water delivery
across all heat exchange surfaces, thereby optimizing thermal performance and maintaining
stable operating conditions. In addition, the unit is outfitted with dedicated stainless-steel
connection branches for streamlined integration into broader HVAC and water circulation
systems.

The final assembled commercial prototype is scheduled for comprehensive field testing in May
2025. These tests will include performance benchmarking under a range of environmental
conditions, validation of integration protocols with various dehumidification technologies, and
endurance trials to assess system reliability and longevity. The results of these evaluations will
inform the final production version and help guide the roadmap for commercial launch and
market entry.
