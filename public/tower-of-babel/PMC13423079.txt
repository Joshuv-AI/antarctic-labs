Introduction

Unfractionated heparin (UFH) is the most frequently used intravenous anticoagulant due to its widespread affordability, availability, rapid onset of action and reversibility with an available antidote. It is most often employed for treatment and prevention of thrombotic conditions including acute coronary syndrome, ischaemic stroke and venous thromboembolism (VTE). There is significant interpatient variability in response to UFH, and the potential for unpredictable pharmacokinetics results in a narrow therapeutic index. As a result, UFH is restricted to the inpatient setting where close monitoring is possible.1

Parenterally administered UFH has an immediate onset of its anticoagulant effects. The activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT) is the traditional clot-based assay used for estimating the degree of anticoagulation and adjustments. Monitoring heparin with the aPTT involves secondary titration, with prolongation of the clotting assay representing a surrogate marker of the desired primary physiologic outcome: inhibition of coagulation in vivo.2 The aPTT assay requires individual laboratory calibration to a specific heparin concentration to account for the variable aPTT responses between labs due to different reagents or instruments. The institution or lab target aPTT is calibrated to a therapeutic heparin concentration (0.3–0.7 U/mL).3

As a global assessment of coagulation, the aPTT depends on both the intrinsic and common pathways of the coagulation cascade, which is influenced by many factors. The pre-analytic contributions (sample processing), analytic effects (laboratory instruments and reagents) and patient variables (increased endogenous levels of clotting factors or heparin binding proteins) all contribute to interpatient variability in results. Certain clinical scenarios that can result in a prolongation of the aPTT that is not representative of an anticoagulant effect, such as a lupus anticoagulant, can pose additional challenges in the use of this assay for heparin management.4 The use of the aPTT assay in these conditions may result in overestimations of the anticoagulant effect and potential underdosing of heparin. The opposite scenario (excess heparin administration) may occur if the aPTT fails to appropriately prolong in response to increasing doses of heparin in settings of possible heparin resistance.5

The possibility of false reassurance or therapeutic misguidance from the aPTT assay highlights the value of alternate heparin monitoring strategies. An alternative assay (anti-Xa assay) is available in this setting.6 7 The anti-Xa assay is a chromogenic measurement of the degree of inhibition of activated factor X, which provides a more specific reflection of plasma heparin concentration. While some of the same monitoring limiting factors for the aPTT also apply to the anti-Xa assay, the interpatient physiologic contributions are significantly reduced in comparison with the aPTT assay.3 The anti-Xa assay is considered a clinical equivalent for heparin monitoring and titration, but it is much less known and used among healthcare providers. The anti-Xa protocol is more specific and sensitive than the aPTT algorithm.8 9 Prospective studies of heparin monitoring and titration are needed to determine the most reliable laboratory evaluation. The increasing complexity and number of critically ill patients requiring intravenous heparin who are also at risk for aPTT confounding leaves providers facing uncertainty about the reliability of aPTT monitoring for heparin.

Use of the aPTT versus anti-Xa protocols has not been compared in a prospective, randomised fashion.8–10 High-quality comparative effectiveness evidence is needed to inform optimal monitoring of intravenous UFH for systemic anticoagulation in hospitalised patients. In this study, we use the Learning Health System11 infrastructure and health information technology to evaluate these two assays in this ongoing randomised, pragmatic clinical trial at a single centre.

Study design

The CHAMP trial is a prospective, single-centre, pragmatic, unblinded, randomised and controlled clinical trial being conducted in adult inpatient units at Vanderbilt University Hospital. The trial compares the effectiveness of the aPTT and anti-Xa laboratory protocols for optimal monitoring and titration of intravenous UFH for systemic anticoagulation in hospitalised patients. The primary outcome is time to first reach therapeutic anticoagulation range. The protocol was approved by the VUMC Institutional Review Board (IRB) and registered with ClinicalTrials.gov prior to enrolment starting (NCT06329921). The trial will conduct a single interim analysis with pre-specified efficacy stopping rules, and an independent data and safety monitoring board (DSMB) monitors trial progress and enrolment.

Study population

Adult inpatients at Vanderbilt University Hospital aged 18 years and older are screened for eligibility if the treating team initiates administration of intravenous UFH using the standard aPTT protocol adjusted by nursing staff. The study is limited to patients on heparin protocols developed for acute coronary syndrome (low dose protocol) or VTE (high dose protocol). Patients receiving intravenous heparin for neurologic indications (acute stroke) or extracorporeal membrane oxygenation are not eligible. Laboratory requirements include a baseline aPTT of ≤36.0 s (hospital reference range 24.0–33.5 s), and a baseline anti-Xa value ≤0.3 units/mL (reference range ≤0.3 units/mL). Patients may also be excluded if their provider determines they are not appropriate for the study (table 2).

Screening and enrolment

Study screening is automated through the EHR to ensure that only patients for whom either protocol would be appropriate are enrolled and randomised. Though both the aPTT and anti-Xa protocols are available and employed in usual care, the nursing managed aPTT is considered routine and the most frequently used protocol in our facility. As the anti-Xa protocol is only requested in specific routine clinical scenarios when there are concerns for aPTT confounding, provider-initiated anti-Xa protocols are excluded from study screening. When a provider initiates an order for intravenous UFH managed via the nurse-managed aPTT protocol for a patient who is 18 years or older, a clinical decision support (CDS) tool alerts the provider of the patient eligibility for the study and queries the provider regarding the presence of any exclusion criteria (figure 1). If none are present, the CDS tool will prompt the ordering provider to submit an order for a single anti-Xa level laboratory order as this result is required for eligibility considerations. If the baseline coagulation laboratory values fall within the specified inclusion range, indicating that the patient is appropriate for monitoring by either protocol, the patient is enrolled and randomised in a 1:1 fashion to either the aPTT or anti-Xa monitoring protocol (figure 2).

Some patients may be discharged, readmitted and receive UFH in a subsequent admission during the study period. In these instances, if the patient meets inclusion criteria and does not meet exclusion criteria during the second admission, the patient will be randomised again, and the readmission will be considered a new instance/encounter. Patients whose heparin is discontinued and re-initiated within the same encounter will not be re-assessed for eligibility or enrolled and randomised again.

Study procedures

Both the aPTT and anti-Xa monitoring protocols (online supplemental appendices A and B) are employed in this study exactly as they are used in clinical practice. In this medical centre, adjustments to heparin on the aPTT protocol are managed by bedside registered nurses, and the anti-Xa protocol is managed by clinical pharmacists. As a pragmatic investigation, the study is embedded within the usual clinical workflow with broad eligibility criteria to maximise generalisability.

Randomisation and treatment allocation

Eligible patients are randomised in a 1:1 ratio to the aPTT guided arm or the anti-Xa guided arm. Treatment arm assignments are generated by computerised randomisation in a 1:1 ratio using an EHR based randomization tool. As patient characteristics can necessitate either a high or low dose heparin protocol, randomisation will be stratified by initial order for high and low dose protocols with randomisation to the study arm performed within each stratum. Randomisation will occur only once at the encounter level. Study group assignments will be concealed until it is confirmed that the patient meets all inclusion criteria and no exclusion criteria and the patient has been enrolled.

Intervention delivery

Patients randomised to the aPTT arm will remain on this protocol as originally ordered by the provider. For patients randomised to the anti-Xa protocol, the CDS tool will send an automated message to the pharmacist assigned to the primary managing team alerting them that the patient has been assigned to the pharmacy managed anti-Xa protocol. The primary pharmacist for the team will discontinue the nursing managed aPTT protocol and order the pharmacy consult for management by the anti-Xa protocol, as is the standard clinical process for initiating the anti-Xa protocol at Vanderbilt University Hospital. There are no additional prompts or CDS alerts if the UFH is discontinued as it is frequently ordered and discontinued as part of usual clinical care.

Blinding

As this study relies on using two different heparin management protocols, which both must be known by a provider to be properly executed, it is not possible to blind nursing or pharmacy providers in this study. Additionally, patients will not be explicitly blinded, though it is not common in general practice to discuss the method of monitoring heparin with patients. Since management of the protocols is largely via bedside nursing or pharmacy adjustments, other clinical providers may not be aware of the randomisation group, although it is not hidden from providers in the medical record.

Data collection

All data are collected as part of routine clinical care. The data are extracted using queries of the electronic health record and stored in a secure online database. Operational data routinely used to monitor heparin protocol compliance will be leveraged to inform intervention fidelity.

Variables collected

Collected at baseline: age, sex, patient-reported race and ethnicity, body mass index (BMI), sequential organ failure assessment (SOFA) score, active comorbidities (Elixhauser Comorbidity Index), active liver disease, most recent haemoglobin, most recent platelet count and the heparin target dose (eg, high dose or low dose) ordered at baseline.

Collected from the time of initiation of the UFH infusion to 48 hours after the heparin protocol is discontinued: date/time of heparin protocol initiation and discontinuation during encounter, coagulation lab values, UFH rate changes, thrombotic events, haemoglobin, transfusions of red blood cells, operational and lab metrics describing protocol compliance.

Data monitoring

Given the minimal risk of the study procedures, the Principal Investigator (PI) is responsible for overseeing the safety of this trial on a daily basis. The PI will regularly monitor trial data, assess trial operations and record and report adverse events to the IRB in accordance with local regulations.

DSMB

An independent DSMB composed of experts in haematology, pharmacy, biostatistics and clinical trials will monitor the trial progress. As this study is expected to pose minimal risk, the principal role of the DSMB is to monitor the data and integrity of the trial.

Primary outcome

The primary outcome is time to reach the first therapeutic anticoagulation range by coagulation assay. By definition, every patient begins out of therapeutic range. As UFH takes effect rapidly,13 it can be presumed that patients found to be in therapeutic range became so shortly after their heparin was administered or adjusted. Patients found to be in therapeutic range at the first lab measurement will be considered to reach therapeutic range 10 min after the heparin infusion initiation. Patients found to be in therapeutic range at subsequent measurements will be considered in range 10 min after the last heparin infusion rate adjustment.

Secondary outcomes

Secondary effectiveness outcomes include: (1) Percent of laboratory measurements (aPTT or anti-Xa) within the therapeutic range, as defined by protocol, (2) The total number of coagulation laboratory measurements while receiving UFH for overall in-hospital coagulation time (ie, while receiving intravenous UFH infusion) and (3) The total number of heparin rate changes for overall in-hospital coagulation time.

Bayesian sequential clinical trial design and interim analysis

This study uses a Bayesian sequential clinical trial design to compare two coagulation assay methods (ie, the aPTT guided arm and the anti-Xa guided arm) through simulation studies using preliminary data collected during the 6 months preceding study initiation. The maximum sample size is set at 700 patients over a 24 month study period, based on the estimated enrolment rate of approximately 30 eligible patients per month. Interim analysis

One interim analysis will be conducted in a blinded manner within the Bayesian framework after 500 patients have been enrolled and their primary outcome data have been completely collected. At this interim, a Bayesian survival analysis for the primary outcome will be performed. A sceptical prior will be placed on log HR, the parameter of interest for comparing the two arms, specified as a normal distribution with mean 0 and SD 0.35. This prior reflects the belief that extreme effects are unlikely, corresponding to a 5% probability that HR >2 or HR <0.5. We will also calculate the posterior predictive probability of success at the final analysis given the interim data, where success is determined if the predictive probability that HR <1 exceeds 0.975 or the predictive probability that HR >1 exceeds 0.975 at the planned maximum sample size. At the interim analysis, two rules will be applied:

Efficacy stopping rule (binding): the trial will be stopped early for superiority of one arm over the other if the posterior probability that HR >1 exceeds 0.975 or the posterior probability that HR <1 exceeds 0.975.

Efficacy stopping rule (binding): the trial will be stopped early for superiority of one arm over the other if the posterior probability that HR >1 exceeds 0.975 or the posterior probability that HR <1 exceeds 0.975.

Futility decision rule (non-binding): a decision to enrol the remaining patients up to the sample size of 700 will be guided by the posterior predictive probability of success. If this probability is <0.20 at the planned maximum sample size, the trial will be considered futile. Because this rule is non-binding, the final decision at the interim will take into account the totality of evidence.

Statistical analysis plan

The full statistical analysis plan (SAP V.2.0, finalised 7 April 2026) is available in online supplemental file 2. Patient baseline demographic and clinical data will be described overall and by the study arm using descriptive statistics. Descriptive analysis of safety, exploratory and fidelity outcomes will be performed by study arm as well as low versus high dose heparin protocol. The full analysis set will include all randomised participants who are eligible and did not meet exclusion criteria specified in the study protocol. All statistical analyses will be performed using the full analysis set with the study arms as originally assigned, even if participants randomised to anti-Xa guided arm never initiate the anti-Xa protocol (ie, these participants remain on the aPTT protocol) or if the participants in the aPTT protocol get switched to anti-Xa monitoring for clinical purposes.

Main analysis of the primary, secondary and exploratory outcomes

The interim and main analyses for the primary outcome will be conducted using a Bayesian approach, while analyses of all other outcomes will be performed within a frequentist framework.

Primary outcome

A survival analysis using a Bayesian approach will be conducted to assess whether the time to reach the therapeutic anticoagulation range differs between the aPTT guided and anti-Xa guided arms. The analysis will adjust for the following covariates: age, sex, race, ethnicity, BMI, baseline SOFA score, Elixhauser comorbidity score, duration of heparin protocol (≥24 hours vs <24 hours) and baseline heparin target dose (high vs low). A sceptical prior (as defined in the Interim Analysis section) will be placed on the parameter for the treatment arm assignment, while weakly informative priors will be used for all other parameters.

Secondary outcomes

Analyses of the secondary effectiveness outcomes will be conducted using generalised linear models with appropriate link functions, within a frequentist framework, adjusting for the same covariates listed above. For analyses of the number of coagulation laboratory measurements and the number of heparin rate changes, overall in-hospital coagulation time will be included as an offset term to account for differences in duration of observation. Descriptive analysis of secondary safety endpoints will be performed by study arm as well as low versus high dose heparin protocol.

Handling intercurrent events

The death rate is expected to be very low. If death occurs, it will be handled using a while on treatment strategy.

Handling of missing data

We do not expect missingness in the primary and secondary outcomes. For missing covariates, if the missingness occurs in <5% of the sample, a single imputation will be used to impute missing values; otherwise, multiple imputation will be used for missing covariates.

Differential treatment effects

To assess whether the effect of two coagulation assay methods on outcomes is modified by patient characteristics, the models for the primary and secondary outcomes will include interaction terms between study arm assignment and the following covariates: baseline heparin target dose (low- vs high-dose), duration of heparin protocol (≥24 hours vs <24 hours), and baseline SOFA score. A sensitivity analysis will be conducted for SOFA score to evaluate potential nonlinearity. Unless there is evidence for nonlinearity, the model will assume a linear interaction between study arm assignment and SOFA score.

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analyses of the primary and secondary effectiveness outcomes will be conducted for patients on low- versus high-dose heparin protocols, as well as for patients with protocol duration ≥24 hours versus <24 hours, if sufficient evidence supports differential treatment effects in these groups.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of the research; however, the Vanderbilt Centre for Learning Healthcare has public and community representation on the LHS Steering Committee, which contributes to the design of all LHS-supported research projects.

Ethics, waiver of informed consent and dissemination

Waiver of informed consent

This study compares two standard-of-care protocols currently being used in clinical practice at Vanderbilt University Hospital to manage UFH in hospitalised adults. Both the aPTT and anti-Xa protocols are considered clinically acceptable methods for anticoagulation monitoring both at this hospital and nationally. UFH is administered to patients in whom an immediate onset of anticoagulation is desired. The clinical scenarios include patients at high risk for complications or adverse outcomes if care is delayed. As patients would receive an accepted standard of care in either arm, the research risks are not greater than those incurred in routine clinical care. Additionally, as providers may choose to exclude their patient or change the heparin monitoring assay used based on clinical indications, the risks of the research would not be different from what patients would experience as part of their standard clinical care and provider autonomy is preserved.

Obtaining informed consent for participation in this study would be impracticable, as this would delay the initiation and initial rate changes for the heparin infusion being used in the patients’ routine care. UFH is administered emergently after it is ordered; attempting to consent patients for randomisation to a heparin monitoring protocol would result in a detrimental delay of care solely for research purposes, as patients are not consented in clinical practice for its administration or monitoring. Furthermore, obtaining consent would introduce a delay in obtaining the anti-Xa level (being run using the commercially available assay on plasma sample already drawn for clinical purposes) and a potential delay in initiating the anti-Xa monitoring protocol, decreasing the potential benefit of monitoring heparin infusions via anti-Xa protocols.

The rights and welfare of participants would not be adversely affected by the study, as these assays and protocols are normally encountered in usual care and participants are not involved in the decision of how to monitor intravenous heparin dosing. Additionally, the pre-specified stopping rules incorporated in this adaptive trial design will help ensure that patient participation yields clinically and scientifically meaningful findings and that no more patients than are necessary to answer the question are enrolled.

Because the study is expected to pose minimal risk, would not adversely affect the rights and welfare of patients, and prospective informed consent is considered to be impracticable, a waiver of informed consent was requested and granted by the VUMC IRB (#232192).

Protocol changes

Any changes to the CHAMP protocol will be documented, and amendments will be posted to ClinicalTrials.gov.

Dissemination

Upon conclusion, the CHAMP trial will be the first study directly comparing the aPTT and anti-Xa protocols within clinical practice in a prospective and randomised fashion. Randomisation within our study provides more reliable and robust data to inform providers of the best option for heparin titration. After trial completion and data analysis, the findings of the CHAMP trial will be submitted for presentation at local, national and international medical conferences, as well as academic institutions through visiting and invited lectureships. Trial results will be submitted to a peer-reviewed journal for consideration of publication for distribution to a broad clinical audience. Following publication of primary results, deidentified data will be made available on reasonable request to the corresponding author in accordance with institutional regulations.

Discussion

The increasing complexity and number of hospitalised patients requiring intravenous heparin who are also at risk for variable aPTT reliability and confounding leaves providers facing uncertainty about the use of aPTT monitoring for heparin. Alternative strategies using anti-Xa monitoring are also used in current clinical care. To determine the most reliable laboratory monitoring approach, prospective studies of heparin monitoring strategies are needed. Reaching therapeutic anticoagulation more quickly and reliably may decrease a patient’s risk of experiencing thrombosis or haemorrhage and also impact labour and workflow considerations for health system personnel. As such, the results of this pragmatic trial will inform optimal strategies for monitoring of UFH in acutely ill hospitalised patients.

This trial has many strengths. The pragmatic design, conducted within the clinical care of hospitalised patients, incorporates the use of broad eligibility criteria in real-world setting and population. EHR-based screening for eligible patients and automated messages to providers facilitate the delivery of the assigned intervention. These elements increase the generalisability and external validity of the findings. Additionally, individual patient randomisation balances patient, hospital and clinical team factors and allows for direct comparison of the two monitoring strategies and potential assessment of a causal relationship between monitoring strategy and outcomes.

The trial also has limitations. This is a single-centre trial excluding patients with ischaemic stroke or on extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, limiting the generalisability to these groups. Although randomisation is concealed, clinical providers and patients are not blinded to the assigned monitoring strategy. The two monitoring strategies also involve different providers, which limits our ability to directly compare the monitoring labs or the providers. This difference only allows comparison of the combination strategies of the monitoring lab with the managing providers, namely aPTT monitoring via nursing with anti-Xa monitoring by pharmacy. However, this reflects the real-world practice of these heparin monitoring strategies in the study hospital. Finally, the incidence of relevant thrombotic or haemorrhagic events is expected to be quite low, reducing the power to detect clinically meaningful differences in these important outcomes. Given this, the study is powered to detect a difference in time to reach therapeutic range, which correlates with a lower risk of bleeding or recurrent thrombosis.
