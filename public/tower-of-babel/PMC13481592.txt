The impact of hurricanes on established mental health diagnoses and psychiatric care: a case report
Authors: Rebecca Hoffman, Lilliana Franklin, Bishoy Kolta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOver the years, numerous high-intensity hurricanes have struck Florida, United States of America, resulting in catastrophic damage. This is expected to surge, as climate change causes storms of greater intensity. Damage associated with hurricanes is typically based on destruction of property and loss of life; however, the impact on the mental health of those affected is more challenging to assess. The following case demonstrates a patient who survived a hurricane but emerged from the storm with an exacerbation of previously diagnosed mental health conditions. In the days following a major hurricane strike, a young male in his 20s required inpatient psychiatric hospitalization after reporting a suicide attempt by consuming large amounts of alcohol and cocaine. The patient, who lived on a boat, experienced total loss of housing and personal belongings following the hurricane, a stressor that contributed to significant psychosocial distress and a subsequent suicide attempt. The patient reported previous diagnoses of bipolar disorder and anxiety, for which he was currently being treated with psychotropic medications. At the initial presentation, his affect was flat, and he appeared guarded towards the staff. Adjustments to his psychotropic medication regimen were made throughout his admission. After the third day of admission, he was deemed stable for discharge. This case demonstrates the fragility of mental health in the setting of natural disasters and the complexity of maintaining mental health care in the aftermath. Most studies have focused on the incidence of mental health conditions after storms have caused areas of devastation, rather than on how storms precipitate decompensation in patients with pre-existing mental health illnesses, including substance use. This report explores management options and highlights the need for new policies and resources.Keywords: Hurricane, Natural disasters, Trauma, Public health

Introduction

Over the past century, more than 100 high-intensity hurricanes have hit the Florida coast, leaving catastrophic damage in their wake. This number is expected to continue increasing, as climate change has been shown to cause increased storm intensity from higher water temperatures and other environmental factors [3, 8, 10]. The damage associated with major hurricanes can be estimated based on destruction, property loss, and loss of life,however, the impact on the mental health of those affected is more difficult to assess. Many studies have begun to scratch the surface, as there has been a positive correlation found between storm severity and diagnosis of posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) [3]. Additionally, individuals with mental health conditions diagnosed before hurricanes are announced are at high risk of decompensation. In fact, those with pre-existing mental health conditions were 6.8 times more likely to develop another mental health condition compared to those with no psychiatric history after exposure to hurricane-related stressors [9]. Contributing factors include disruption of care, life-changing adversities, and loss of loved ones or property [8]. The following case study demonstrates a patient who survived a category 3 hurricane but emerged from the storm with an exacerbation of previously diagnosed mental health conditions.

Case report

A male patient in his 20s presented to the emergency department after a dangerous Category 3 hurricane impacted Florida. An emergency room physician placed him under an involuntary psychiatric admission after the patient reported consuming alcohol and cocaine in an attempt to take his life, after feeling like he had nowhere to go. The patient had been living on a boat when the hurricane hit, and the vessel flooded and sank, causing him to lose everything he owned due to the storm. He reported feeling depressed and suicidal after the realization that he was now homeless and no longer had any of his belongings, including medications. In addition, he was unable to get in contact with family for assistance, as he believed they had lost power due to the storm. He was transferred to an inpatient psychiatric hospital the following day.

The patient reported past psychiatric diagnoses of bipolar disorder, unspecified type, and anxiety. He denied prior suicide attempts. He recalled currently taking olanzapine and lorazepam, but stated there were additional prescribed medications he could not recall. Alongside his psychiatric diagnoses, this patient had a long history of substance use with methamphetamine, cocaine, and marijuana since his early teenage years. He had attended rehab for a month but relapsed one week after being discharged. The patient endorsed drinking multiple beers one or two times a week, smoking half a pack of cigarettes a day, vaping nicotine daily, and taking MDMA occasionally. The patient was single, lived alone, and had no kids. He was currently unemployed, earning income through intermittent side jobs.

Once admitted to the unit, the patient was started on a regimen of olanzapine 10 mg at bedtime. This was given to him since it was one of the medications he could recall taking and benefiting from in the past. Benzodiazepines were not initiated because there was no confirmation of the prescription on the Prescription Drug Monitoring Program. On this first day, he had a flat affect and displayed guarded and withdrawn behavior towards staff and other patients on the unit. He rated his depression as a 10/10 and admitted he did not want to live anymore. When observed on the unit, he was only seen getting food and sleeping in bed. He refused to take showers, give blood to be drawn for lab work, or give a urine sample for further evaluation.

On the following day, he continued to experience feelings of depression. He was prescribed escitalopram 10 mg daily in conjunction with olanzapine to help improve his mood. This medication was chosen, as there were no documented manic or hypomanic episodes in the patients’ records. Antidepressants are not recommended as monotherapy in bipolar disorder, as they can cause a shift to mania or rapid cycling. There is conflicting evidence on its use as adjunct therapy [4]. With no documented manic or hypomanic episodes, it was also considered whether his substance use contributed to his mood fluctuations. The patient continued to refuse lab work and self-isolate on the unit, while still endorsing suicidal ideations. When offered a chance to discuss substance use programs with a peer specialist, he denied wanting to quit using substances at this time.

By day three in the unit, the patient was reported to be interacting with peers and staff instead of spending the day sleeping in his bed. His medication regimen was adjusted to olanzapine 15 mg at bedtime to continue to improve his mood symptoms. He denied any suicidal thoughts but still appeared guarded and with a blunted affect. He endorsed feeling stable and requested discharge, as he was anxious about continuing to stay in the hospital.

After three days in the unit, the patient was considered stable and discharged to a family member’s house. Electronic prescriptions for escitalopram 10 mg daily and olanzapine 10 mg at bedtime were sent to his preferred pharmacy. He was discharged on this lower dose of 10 mg of olanzapine due to reporting hypersomnolence during the daytime. He endorsed improvements in mood and displayed euthymic affect. Both the provider and case manager discussed the importance of adhering to medications and following up with a psychiatric provider. The patient reported being established at a community mental health center, so contact information to set up an appointment with this facility was provided to the patient upon discharge. No damage after the storm was reported from the center, so it was considered a reliable location for the patient to follow up. Additional patient education documents about the warning signs of suicide, mental health support groups, and recovery from addiction were also included in the discharge packet.

Discussion

Most studies have focused on mental health conditions developing after storms have impacted certain areas of devastation. For example, numerous studies have examined the mental health effects of Hurricane Katrina. Hurricane Katrina made landfall in Louisiana in 2005 as a catastrophic Category 3 storm. This storm caused more than 500,000 people to be evacuated from their homes and caused land to be declared disaster areas equivalent to the same square miles as the United Kingdom. Over 1600 individuals were confirmed dead, and 1000 individuals remained missing at the time the articles were published [2, 5]. With all this devastation and loss, it was found that survivors had elevated rates of anxiety, PTSD, and other mood disorders post-hurricane, especially when linked to the impact zones [2, 3, 8]. In addition, survivors of Hurricane Harvey experienced thoughts of suicide more than 2.5 times the general population in the United States [1].

While this information is also pertinent, it is also vital to consider those individuals with pre-existing mental health conditions prior to a natural disaster, as they are more vulnerable than the general population. This patient’s case demonstrated the fragility of mental health when met with the unavoidable stressor of a natural disaster, such as a severe hurricane. Hurricanes are a major public health concern, as they damage the water supply, cause injury and death, and lead to a devastating loss of property and possessions. These consequences of Mother Nature have a profound impact on the well-being of the community. In this case, the patient lost his home and all his belongings, feeling as though he had no place to turn and no family support at that time. With his previous diagnoses and substance use, he was more prone to depressive symptoms and at an increased risk of negative reactions to hardships, such as exacerbation of drug use. This is especially relevant in Florida, and it has been found that repeated exposures to hurricanes show additive negative effects on mental health over time [3]. As individuals have more exposure to hurricanes, this creates more anxiety and forecasts negative reactions to future storms [10]. It should also be noted, however, that surviving traumatic events does not universally result in only negative outcomes. Some individuals report post-traumatic growth, experiencing positive psychological changes such as greater self-awareness, increased appreciation of life, and stronger interpersonal relationships following intense stressors like natural disasters [6]. Thus, while disaster exposure can worsen symptoms in vulnerable individuals, post-traumatic growth highlights that new strengths can also emerge in the aftermath of trauma.

Alongside experiencing potentially negative reactions to difficult situations, these patients are likely to have their treatment for their mental health interrupted due to canceled appointments or issues receiving their medications. Once treatment is interrupted, they are less likely to re-establish relationships with their providers to continue their care post-hurricane [3, 8]. This could be for many reasons, such as feeling hopeless after a loss, not having a way to establish contact, or financial barriers. In some cases, including the present case, patients may be unable to recall their full medication regimen, complicating efforts to restore prior stability. Many complex mental health conditions require a regimen of multiple different medication types and doses that can be difficult to remember. This is especially difficult to determine when a patient is often displaced from their initial home and may no longer be in direct contact with family members, as was the case with this patient. He reported trying to contact his mother while he was in the psychiatric hospital but was unable to reach her and considered the possibility that there was a power outage in the area.

This patient case is representative of challenges faced by individuals living in disaster-prone regions and underscores the importance of community outreach for those with established mental health conditions. A support system and sense of community are shown to be protective factors against thoughts of suicide [1]. Policies should be implemented in areas with a higher incidence of natural disasters to bridge the gap between treatment plans before disaster strikes and to reconnect patients with their providers and other needed resources [3]. Although there are emergency services that assist with mental health support in some capacity, many responders lack specialized training to adequately address complex psychiatric needs in disaster settings. There are also no structured plans, and not enough mental health professionals are trained to provide psychological support to the magnitude of individuals exposed to such traumatic events. One potential solution is to distribute tasks and give more training to healthcare workers who have volunteered to help in the aftermath of storms [8]. For example, the Psychological First Aid program was created to assist with the care of survivors after a major disaster and has been widely implemented in the training of first responders. This program outlines five pertinent principles, including establishing a sense of safety, calmness, connectedness, a sense of self and community efficacy, and hope [11]. Medication management before storms hit is another concern. Implementation of a plan to get all necessary medications distributed beforehand could make a major impact on the lives of those struggling with mental health conditions. These patients mustn’t lose access to mental health medications that are important to allow them to function daily [7]. In this case, the patient lost access to his prescribed medications following the storm. With more robust pre-disaster planning and targeted outreach to individuals with known psychiatric conditions, some hospitalizations related to post-disaster psychiatric crises may be preventable.

There are a few limitations to this case report. The objective status of his mental health before the hurricane struck is unknown, as he was from out of town, and his paperwork was limited. As previously stated, the patient was guarded, which made history-gathering challenging. Additionally, there was no confirmed diagnosis of bipolar disorder, as we did not have access to any outpatient care records. The documents from the transferring hospital did not list a history of manic or hypomanic episodes. Therefore, his past psychiatric history was obtained mostly through self-report, which may compromise accuracy.

Conclusion

As climate change continues to impact our changing world, it is pertinent to consider the effects major natural disasters can have on public health. Each of the vulnerable populations must be considered when life-altering storms create chaos in communities. While most people consider individuals of lower socio-economic status, patients who suffer from mental health crises must also be considered for emergency outreach. This patient’s case highlighted the need for states prone to natural disasters to implement a strategy of preparation to care for the specific needs of those who suffer from mental illness. Patients need support and have the right to receive life-saving care when going through hardships. Community support, immediate access to providers or resources, and continued medication availability could make a major difference in the future of storm preparation and aftermath.
