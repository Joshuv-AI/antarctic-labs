From anatomy to vulnerability: A comprehensive appraisal of coronary physiology and plaque characterization☆
Authors: Simone Fezzi, Elena Coradello, Jiayue Huang, Shengxian Tu, William Wijns
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A substantial proportion of acute coronary syndromes arise from non-obstructive plaques with high-risk biological, morphological, and biomechanical features. This review summarizes contemporary concepts of plaque vulnerability, including plaque rupture, plaque erosion, and calcified nodules, and examines the role of invasive imaging and computational techniques in identifying lesions at increased risk of future events. Intravascular ultrasound, near-infrared spectroscopy, and optical coherence tomography provide complementary information on plaque burden, lipid content, fibrous cap thickness, macrophage infiltration, calcification, and thrombotic substrates. However, their routine use may be limited by cost, procedural complexity, contrast requirements, and operator dependency. Emerging angiography-derived approaches, particularly radial wall strain, offer a simplified method to assess plaque deformability and biomechanical instability from standard coronary angiography. Elevated radial wall strain has shown correlation with optical coherence tomography-defined vulnerable plaque features and independent prognostic value for adverse cardiovascular outcomes, including in non-flow-limiting lesions. Integration of coronary physiology, plaque morphology, and biomechanical indices may therefore refine lesion-level risk stratification and support a more personalized approach to coronary artery disease management. Further prospective studies are needed to determine whether vulnerability-guided diagnostic and therapeutic strategies can improve clinical outcomes beyond current anatomy- and ischaemia-based paradigms.

Introduction

Acute coronary syndromes (ACS) remain a leading cause of mortality and morbidity worldwide and encompass a spectrum of clinical conditions ranging from unstable angina to myocardial infarction (MI). Although the underlying mechanisms are heterogeneous, including plaque rupture, plaque erosion, calcified nodules, and non-atherosclerotic causes such as Takotsubo syndrome, they converge on a common final pathway of acute myocardial ischemia due to critical reductions in blood supply [1]. While the management of ischemic heart disease has evolved considerably, challenges persist in both diagnosis and treatment. These challenges are due, in part, to the complex pathophysiology of coronary artery disease (CAD), which extends beyond simple luminal obstruction and includes processes like atherosclerosis progression, inflammation, and thrombosis [2].

Traditionally, the clinical approach to CAD has centered on identifying and treating angiographically severe coronary stenoses shown to limit myocardial perfusion and causing ischemia, long regarded as the principal targets in both chronic and acute coronary syndromes. Invasive coronary angiography (ICA), historically considered the gold standard for CAD diagnosis, provides detailed visualization of luminal narrowing, but only in two dimensions. This represents a fundamental limitation, as ICA cannot characterize the vessel wall, plaque burden, or plaque composition, and therefore fails to capture the full biological complexity of the disease [3].

Recent insights have reshaped this paradigm. Multiple natural history studies have demonstrated that a substantial proportion of ACS arise from plaques deemed mild based on angiographic obstruction severity [4], [5], [6]. Atherosclerotic plaques are now recognized as complex and biologically active entities, influenced by several mechanisms such as vasomotor changes, inflammation, lipid accumulation, and extracellular matrix degradation [7]. Consequently, there is now a growing recognition that a narrow focus restricted to angiographic severity must be enhanced by a comprehensive assessment of plaque burden and plaque composition, hence vulnerability.

In this review, we will examine the concept of invasive plaque vulnerability assessment methods based on miniaturized catheters and imaging-derived computational techniques.

Vulnerable plaque

The concept of plaque vulnerability was first introduced by Constantinides in 1964, when he described fissured atherosclerotic plaques as a potential substrate for human coronary thrombosis [8]. Since then, substantial progress has been made in elucidating the morphological, molecular, and biomechanical features that render certain plaques prone to rupture or erosion, ultimately precipitating ACS. Despite major advances in both invasive and non-invasive imaging techniques, the ability to prospectively identify plaques truly destined to precipitate major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) remains modest, the positive predictive value of nearly all risk assessment variables being too low to be clinically meaningful [9]. By clinical understanding, vulnerable plaques are atherosclerotic lesions that, under specific hemodynamic and inflammatory conditions, can progress to thrombosis and result in MACE [2]. Pathologically, these have been characterized through autopsy studies of fatal myocardial infarction, by three principal features that underly mechanisms of coronary thrombosis: plaque rupture, plaque erosion, and calcified nodules (see Fig. 1), accounting for approximately 70%, 25%, and 5% of all thrombotic events, respectively [2]. In practice, non-obstructive vulnerable plaques represent a persistent clinical dilemma: they are many and because they do not limit maximal coronary flow, they do not currently warrant revascularization. Plaque rupture and thin-cap fibroatheroma

Plaque rupture represents the most frequent cause of coronary thrombosis and cardiovascular death, responsible for nearly 70% of fatal ACS. It is defined as a structural discontinuity (e.g., a gap or fissure) in the fibrous cap that separates the lipid-rich necrotic core from the arterial lumen. The prototypical lesion predisposing to rupture is the thin-cap fibroatheroma (TCFA), characterized by a large necrotic core containing cholesterol crystals, macrophagic foam cell infiltration, smooth muscle cell accumulation, and proteoglycan-rich extracellular matrix, separated from the lumen by a fibrous thin cap, less than 65 μm thick [2], [10], [11]. The cap is typically infiltrated by activated macrophages and T lymphocytes, with additional features including spotty calcifications, neovascularization, intraplaque hemorrhage, and adventitial inflammation.

Plaque rupture is frequently localized in regions of low endothelial shear stress, such as proximal coronary segments and arterial bifurcations, where oscillatory flow promotes inflammation and proteolytic activity. Autopsy and imaging studies have consistently shown that rupture-prone plaques are often angiographically mild due to compensatory (expansive) positive remodeling [12], [13], [14]. Triggers of rupture include acute hemodynamic stress and sympathetic activation [15], [16], [17], [18], [19].

Plaque erosion and fibrotic plaques

Plaque erosion constitutes the second most common substrate for coronary thrombosis, accounting for approximately 25% of ACS [20]. Unlike rupture, erosion is characterized by endothelial denudation with preservation of the fibrous cap. The exposed subendothelial matrix facilitates platelet adhesion and thrombus formation [21]. Histologically, erosion-prone plaques tend to be eccentric, rich in smooth muscle cells and proteoglycans, with minimal necrotic core, low macrophage content, and limited inflammation or calcification [22], [23].

Autopsy studies suggested that plaque erosion was more frequent in younger individuals and women [24], although more recent in vivo OCT studies show conflicting results, identifying erosion in younger male smokers without traditional risk factors such as diabetes or hypertension [25]. Clinical presentation also differs: approximately 70% of plaque ruptures manifest as ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), whereas 60–85% of erosions result in non-ST-elevation ACS (NSTE-ACS) [21]. The reduced thrombogenicity and more preserved lumen architecture of eroded plaques explain this difference. With widespread statin use and improved lipid control, the prevalence of fibrotic and erosion-prone plaques appears to be increasing, potentially shifting the phenotype of culprit lesions toward NSTE-ACS [26]. Currently, no imaging modality reliably identifies erosion-prone lesions before thrombosis.

Calcified nodules

Calcified nodules (CNs) are a rare cause of coronary thrombosis (≈5%) and typically occur in older individuals with heavily calcified and tortuous arteries [27]. CNs are a specific type of heavily calcified lesion that protrudes into the coronary artery lumen [28]. CNs can be classified into eruptive and non-eruptive subtypes. Eruptive calcified nodules (CNs) are characterized by multiple fragmented calcium deposits that penetrate and disrupt a thin fibrous cap, typically accompanied by endothelial denudation and overlying luminal thrombus. In contrast, non-eruptive CNs exhibit a thick, intact fibrous cap that effectively isolates the calcified area from the arterial lumen, maintaining endothelial continuity and showing no evidence of thrombus formation [29]. Mechanistically, CNs typically form at hinge points within coronary arteries, where tensile strength is reduced due to loss of collagen matrix. Repeated stress can fracture the collagen-free calcium, leading to fibrous cap disruption and the development of eruptive CNs [29]. Such disruptions are frequently accompanied by intraplaque hemorrhage, observed in approximately 40% of cases. Over time, organization and healing of the hemorrhage may promote osteogenic transformation of blood components, with neointimal tissue covering the calcified mass and resulting in a non-eruptive CN. This sequence supports the concept that non-eruptive CNs represent the healed or mature stage of previously eruptive lesions [27].

Structural determinants of plaque rupture

Plaque rupture is primarily determined by the complex interplay between plaque composition, arterial remodeling, inflammatory activity, and biomechanical stress, with several key structural features conferring vulnerability. Central to this process is the formation of a lipid-rich necrotic core, which arises during atherogenesis when modified atherogenic lipoproteins accumulate within the deeper abluminal regions of the intima, attracting macrophages that ingest lipids and release proteolytic enzymes before undergoing apoptosis [30], [31]. Inefficient clearance of apoptotic cells by phagocytes, namely efferocytosis, leads to progressive plaque necrosis, generating a cholesterol-rich, acellular cavity devoid of structural collagen support, a process further amplified by intraplaque hemorrhage from erythrocyte extravasation. Overlying the necrotic core, the fibrous cap acts as the primary mechanical barrier between thrombogenic plaque components and the circulating blood; when this cap becomes critically thin, typically <65 μm, it defines the thin-cap fibroatheroma, the archetypal rupture-prone lesion. These high-risk plaques often undergo expansive (positive) arterial remodeling, a compensatory vessel enlargement that preserves luminal caliber and renders them angiographically silent, in contrast to the constrictive remodeling typically associated with more stable, flow-limiting lesions [32]. Calcification further modulates plaque stability in a pattern-dependent manner: while extensive, dense calcification generally reflects healed or stable disease, focal or “spotty” microcalcifications, particularly within the fibrous cap, can act as stress concentrators, locally amplifying mechanical stress and promoting cap rupture despite a lower overall calcium burden [21], [33], [34]. Angiogenesis and inflammation at the plaque base further contribute to instability, as fragile neovessels originating from the adventitial vasa vasorum are prone to leakage, promoting intraplaque hemorrhage, necrotic core expansion, and rapid lesion progression. Finally, perivascular adipose tissue (PVAT) exerts an important “outside-in” influence on plaque vulnerability; through oxidative stress, adipokine release, and immune cell recruitment, dysfunctional PVAT amplifies vascular inflammation, alters remodeling patterns, and promotes plaque destabilization [2], [35], [36].

Imaging of the vulnerable plaque – intravascular imaging

Intravascular ultrasound – near infrared spectroscopy

Invasive intravascular imaging has played a pivotal role in advancing the understanding and identification of vulnerable coronary plaques, with intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) and near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS) providing complementary structural and compositional insights beyond conventional angiography. The introduction of greyscale IVUS in the late 1990s enabled, for the first time, in vivo visualization of the coronary vessel wall, allowing accurate assessment of plaque burden, vessel remodeling, and lumen dimensions, thereby revealing that atherosclerosis is frequently diffuse and underestimated by angiography alone [37], [38], [39]. Although greyscale IVUS is limited in its ability to characterize plaque composition due to limited axial resolution, its prognostic relevance was firmly established in landmark studies such as REVERSAL, which demonstrated that intensive lipid-lowering therapy could halt plaque progression as quantified by IVUS-derived plaque volume [39]. Subsequent developments led to “virtual” histology IVUS (VH-IVUS), which applies spectral analysis of backscattered ultrasound profiles to classify plaque components into fibrous, fibrofatty, necrotic core, and dense calcium, compared to histopathology, thus enabling indirect identification of high-risk phenotypes such as thin-cap fibroatheroma (TCFA). Large prospective studies, including PROSPECT, VIVA, PREDICTION, and ATHEROREMO-IVUS, consistently demonstrated that angiographically mild, non-culprit lesions characterized by large plaque burden (≥70%), small minimal lumen area (<4 mm2), and TCFA morphology were associated with future major adverse cardiovascular events, underscoring the biological vulnerability of plaques that may not be flow-limiting at baseline [40], [41], [42]. Parallel to these structural advances, the introduction of NIRS provided a novel biochemical dimension to plaque assessment by enabling direct detection of lipid-rich plaques through spectral analysis, quantified by the lipid core burden index (LCBI). NIRS has shown superior accuracy in identifying lipid content compared with IVUS or OCT, even in the presence of heavy calcification, and may also capture biological processes such as inflammation and apoptosis within the fibrous cap. Prognostic studies, including ATHEROREMO-NIRS, LRP, and PROSPECT II, demonstrated that lesions with high LCBI or maxLCBI₄mm (>400) values carry an increased risk of future lesion-level and patient-level events, with lipid-rich plaques conferring up to a four-fold higher risk of adverse outcomes [5], [12], [17], [43]. The integration of NIRS with IVUS has therefore emerged as a powerful, operator-independent approach that combines detailed anatomical assessment with biochemical characterization, enabling comprehensive identification of rupture-prone plaques.

Optical coherence tomography

Optical coherence tomography (OCT) is currently the highest-resolution invasive intracoronary imaging modality available for plaque assessment, enabling detailed characterization of coronary atherosclerosis at the microstructural level. By analyzing back-scattered near-infrared light, OCT achieves an axial resolution of approximately 10–20 μm, nearly one order of magnitude higher than intravascular ultrasound, making it the only technique capable of accurately measuring true fibrous cap thickness, a key determinant of plaque rupture [44], [45]. In addition, OCT allows reliable differentiation between lipid-rich and fibrous tissue and direct visualization of morphological features associated with plaque vulnerability, including macrophage accumulation, microchannels, neovascularization, plaque disruption, and intracoronary thrombosis. The histopathological validity of OCT was first established by Yabushita et al., who demonstrated high diagnostic accuracy for identifying fibrous, lipid-rich, and calcified plaques [46]. Subsequent prospective studies have consistently shown that OCT-defined high-risk features are associated with adverse clinical outcomes. In the CLIMA registry, proximal coronary lesions with high-risk morphological features, including small minimal lumen area (<3.5 mm2; HR 2.1, 95% CI 1.1–4.0), thin fibrous cap (<75 μm; HR 4.7, 95% CI 2.4–9.0), wide lipid arc (>180°; HR 2.4, 95% CI 1.2–4.8), and macrophage infiltration (HR 2.7, 95% CI 1.2–6.1) were associated with a substantially increased risk of cardiac death or target-segment myocardial infarction (HR 7.54; 95% CI 3.1–18.6) [47]. Although severely stenotic lesions carry a higher per-lesion risk of occlusion, the high prevalence of non-obstructive plaques underscores their substantial contribution to overall event burden. Despite their low prevalence (3.6%), the simultaneous presence of all four OCT-derived high-risk features was a strong independent predictor of cardiac death or target-segment myocardial infarction at five-year follow-up (HR 4.3; 95% CI 2.01–9.33; p < 0.001). Thin-cap fibroatheroma (TCFA) was more common (18.3%) and was similarly associated with adverse outcomes (HR 3.04; 95% CI 1.86–4.96; p < 0.001) [48]. Importantly, the COMBINE OCT-FFR study shifted lesion-level risk stratification from an ischaemia-centric paradigm toward plaque morphology, demonstrating that TCFA conferred a significantly higher risk of MACE in diabetic patients, even in the absence of functionally significant stenosis [49]. Despite inherent limitations related to limited tissue penetration and the need for contrast-based blood clearance, OCT has emerged as a reference standard for in vivo identification of rupture-prone plaques and has fundamentally advanced the understanding of plaque vulnerability beyond angiographic severity or physiological significance alone. In this context, computational tools derived from intravascular imaging, increasingly supported by artificial intelligence–based surrogates of plaque vulnerability, are being developed to translate OCT-validated morphological risk features into standardized, quantitative, and less operator-dependent metrics suitable for routine clinical practice, thereby mitigating the limitations related to operator experience and expertise in image interpretation [50] (see Fig. 2).Fig. 2Morphological substrates for acute coronary syndromesSchematic representation of the principal pathological mechanisms underlying ACS. Angiography-derived plaque vulnerability and radial wall strain

Background and proof of concept

All of the aforementioned modalities are associated with high costs, time-consuming analysis and procedural risks [51]. Historically, the biomechanical assessment of plaque vulnerability has relied on sophisticated imaging- and modeling-based approaches. Early studies using intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) elastography and palpography showed that high-strain patterns were associated with vulnerable plaque phenotypes, supporting the concept that plaque deformation may reflect underlying structural instability [52]. In parallel, computational fluid dynamics–based analyses of endothelial shear stress and plaque stress further advanced the field by demonstrating that local biomechanical forces contribute to plaque progression and destabilization. More recently, 3-dimensional angiography-based techniques, including superficial wall strain/stress (SWS), have attempted to translate these concepts into the catheterization laboratory by integrating geometric reconstruction with biomechanical analysis [53]. Despite their mechanistic appeal, however, these techniques remain constrained by time-consuming workflows, technical complexity, and, in some cases, the need for dedicated invasive imaging or heavy off-line computational analysis, all of which limit their applicability in routine clinical practice. This unmet need has paved the way for radial wall strain (RWS), a simplified angiography-derived index that quantifies cyclic variation in lumen diameter from a routine single high-quality angiographic projection acquisition [18].

RWS quantifies the variation in lumen diameter within a single cardiac cycle using a single angiographic view [54]. Plaque composition and circumferential wall stress, induced by pulsatile blood pressure, are key determinants of plaque deformability, an attribute that can be biomechanically assessed using RWS. Elevated radial strain has been associated with increased plaque vulnerability and may contribute to endothelial dysfunction and plaque rupture through progressive degradation of the fibrous cap, ultimately leading to ACS [55]. A high-strain pattern, defined as a maximum RWS (RWSmax) exceeding 13%, has demonstrated strong concordance with OCT-based markers of plaque vulnerability (plaque burden, lipid-to-cap ratio) and to correlate negatively with fibrous cap thickness [18], [19], [56], [57]. Available evidence

Yang et al. [58] demonstrated that fractional flow reserve (FFR) ≤ 0.80 or the presence of high-risk plaque (HRP), defined as a combination of minimal lumen area < 4 mm2 and plaque burden ≥70%, is associated with a higher probability of elevated RWSmax. A RWSmax > 14% was linked to an increased risk of target vessel failure (composite of target vessel revascularization, myocardial infarction or cardiac death), independently of FFR and HRP. Furthermore, RWSmax > 14% improved the ability of FFR ≤ 0.80 or HRP to accurately classify high-risk lesions, while also maintaining independent prognostic value. Assessing RWS prior to physiological testing or intravascular imaging may assist interventional cardiologists in clinical decision-making, suggesting that RWS could serve as a complementary tool alongside physiological indices and plaque imaging to identify lesions at risk for future coronary events.

The prognostic value of RWS was previously demonstrated by Wang et al. [56] in a retrospective analysis of 808 intermediate coronary lesions (ICLs) in 603 patients, where ICLs were defined as 30–70% diameter stenosis based on visual estimation. In this study, the presence of RWSmax > 12.6% (high strain pattern) was associated with a sensitivity of 65.3%, specificity of 80.8%, and a positive predictive value (PPV) of 18% for lesion progression. Notably, the negative predictive value (NPV) was 97.3%, highlighting the potential of RWS as a reliable marker for excluding lesions unlikely to progress.

In a post-hoc analysis of the FAVOR III China trial, Tu et al. [57] [21] retrospectively evaluated Murray's law-based quantitative flow ratio (μFR) and RWS in 986 vessels to assess the value of RWS in the risk stratification of ICLs. The study demonstrated that the incidence of vessel-oriented composite endpoints (VOCE) within 1-year follow-up was significantly higher in patients with RWSmax > 12% and that RWS was highly predictive of ischemia-driven target vessel revascularization. RWSmax showed a NPV of 97.1% at 1-year follow-up. Moreover, the incidence of VOCE was significantly lower in vessels with both μFR > 0.80 and RWSmax ≤ 12%, compared to vessels with preserved μFR alone.

RWSmax has been shown to localize predominantly in the proximal and throat segments of stenotic lesions [55], [56], regions known for elevated wall shear stress and commonly associated with plaque rupture. As a result, RWSmax > 12% also holds predictive value for acute myocardial infarction (AMI) events as first reported by Li et al. [55] in a retrospective study involving 1981 patients.

In line with this integrative view, a recent post-hoc analysis of the COMBINE OCT-FFR study [59] demonstrated that RWS has emerged as a significant predictor of lesion-oriented composite endpoints in diabetic patients with non–flow-limiting coronary stenoses, despite preserved coronary physiology. Lesions exhibiting RWSmax ≥ 13% were associated with a higher risk of cardiac death, target-vessel MI, and target lesion revascularization. Notably, RWS correlated with OCT-defined plaque vulnerability features yet retained independent prognostic value beyond morphological assessment, suggesting that biomechanical stress captures a complementary dimension of plaque instability. These findings indicate that angiography-derived RWS analysis may aid in identifying OCT-detected vulnerability features, particularly TCFA, and in recognizing non–flow-limiting lesions associated with an increased risk of adverse cardiac events, supporting a multimodal approach integrating functional, morphological, and biomechanical indices to refine risk stratification of ICLs [59], [60].

Huang et al. (TARGET All Comers AC trial, 2024) assessed angiography-derived RWS in non-target vessels of 1169 patients with 5-year follow-up. RWS ≥13% was associated with significantly higher event rates, including non-culprit lesion revascularization (12.3% vs 5.5%, p = 0.0005), non-target vessel myocardial infarction (7.7% vs 3.7%, p = 0.0105), and their composite (28.2% vs 14.6%, p < 0.0001). Major adverse outcomes were also increased (death/NTV MI/NCL revascularization: 25.9% vs 14.2%; death/MI/revascularization: 50.0% vs 26.3%; both p < 0.0001). Elevated RWS independently predicted events (HR ≈ 2.3), with stronger prognostic value in high-quality angiograms (HR up to 3.52).

Lastly, the feasibility and accuracy of RWS biomechanical analysis may be influenced by angiographic image quality and acquisition protocols, and success rates may vary when analyses are performed on retrospectively acquired angiograms rather than prospectively optimized imaging. In available datasets, approximately 35% of vessels could not be analyzed, most commonly due to poor image quality with blurred lumen contours (≈20%), suboptimal angiographic projections with vessel overlap or foreshortening (≈10%), or inadequate contrast opacification (≈5%). In addition, most available evidence derives from observational or post hoc analyses, and randomized trials evaluating the clinical impact of RWS-guided strategies are still lacking. A prospective, multicenter randomized trial (FAVOR V AMI, NCT05669222) has been designed to evaluate whether a strategy integrating angiography-derived radial wall strain and quantitative flow ratio (FAST technique) to guide PCI improves clinical outcomes compared with standard treatment strategies.

When combined with physiological and imaging modalities, RWS enhances plaque risk stratification, supporting its role as a complementary tool in clinical decision-making (see Fig. 3). Conclusions

Myocardial revascularization strategies based solely on angiography-derived stenosis severity are increasingly outdated. It is fundamental to assess physiological parameters, plaque burden, and morphological characteristics to guide treatment decisions and identify high-risk or vulnerable lesions. A variety of invasive and non-invasive tools have been developed, providing complementary insights that improve patient selection and procedural outcomes. Importantly, the adoption of these advanced modalities helps reduce futile invasive treatments and unnecessary interventions, which in turn decreases healthcare costs and optimizes resource utilization. Integrating comprehensive physiological and morphological evaluations into clinical practice holds significant promise for enhancing both the effectiveness and efficiency of coronary artery disease management.
