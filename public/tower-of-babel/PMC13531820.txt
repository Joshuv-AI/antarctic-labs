Nonlinear association between the stress hyperglycemia ratio and 180-day mortality in critically ill patients undergoing extracorporeal circulation during open heart surgery: a MIMIC-IV cohort study
Authors: Peipei Zhang, Xuping Zhang, Hongyao Zhao, Yaxin Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimsThe stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR) has been associated with adverse outcomes in patients with cardiovascular diseases. Recent studies have also linked higher SHR to increased mortality after cardiac surgery, including analyses using the MIMIC-IV database, and in patients undergoing CABG for acute myocardial infarction. Therefore, the present study should be viewed as an incremental and exploratory analysis rather than as a wholly novel investigation. However, the dose–response pattern and clinically relevant risk thresholds of SHR in an undifferentiated extracorporeal circulation (ECC)-assisted open-heart surgery population remain incompletely characterized. Given the uncertain perioperative timing of glucose measurements in MIMIC-IV, SHR was interpreted as an aggregated prognostic marker rather than a direct measure of intraoperative or immediate postoperative metabolic stress. This study aims to investigate the association between SHR and short-term mortality risk in this patient cohort.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted by analyzing data from 1,221 patients who underwent extracorporeal circulation during open-heart surgery, obtained from the MIMIC-IV (version 3.1) database. Patients were divided into quartiles based on SHR levels. Cox proportional hazards models, including a segmented variant, were used to evaluate the association between SHR and 180-day mortality. Glucose and HbA1c values were obtained from available laboratory records within the first 24 h of ICU admission; however, precise timing relative to surgery, ECC initiation, or ICU arrival could not be standardized. Important intraoperative parameters, including cardiopulmonary bypass duration and aortic cross-clamp time, were not available as reliable structured variables and therefore could not be adjusted for. In addition, a substantial proportion of potentially eligible patients were excluded because SHR could not be calculated owing to missing glucose or HbA1c measurements, which may have introduced selection bias.ResultsWithin the study cohort, 63 patients (5.16%) died within 180 days. Following multivariable adjustment, SHR showed a nonlinear, threshold-like association with 180-day mortality, with an estimated inflection point at 0.97. The increase in mortality risk was more clearly observed at SHR levels above this point, whereas the association below this point was not statistically significant. Therefore, the spline-derived point should be interpreted as exploratory rather than as a clinically validated cutoff or treatment target. Compared to participants with SHR levels below the inflection point, those with higher SHR levels exhibited a fourfold increased risk of 180-day mortality (HR 4.62; 95% CI 2.67–7.95).ConclusionOur findings indicate that an elevated SHR, measured by the glucose/HbA1c ratio, is associated with an increased risk of short-term mortality in patients undergoing extracorporeal circulation during open-heart surgery. Because of the retrospective design, lack of precise perioperative glucose timing, substantial exclusion of patients with missing glycemic data, uncertain completeness of post-discharge mortality ascertainment, and inability to adjust for key intraoperative variables, these findings should be interpreted as prognostic and hypothesis-generating rather than causal. The observed nonlinear pattern may help refine risk stratification, but the proposed inflection point requires validation in prospective studies with standardized perioperative glucose monitoring.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13019-026-04488-w.

Introduction

Extracorporeal circulation (ECC) in open-heart surgery involves using a heart–lung machine to temporarily take over the functions of the heart and lungs during the procedure. This enables surgeons to operate on a motionless and bloodless heart, facilitating precise surgical interventions like valve repairs or coronary artery bypass grafting [1]. ECC is a vital component of open-heart surgery, facilitating the temporary replacement of cardiac and pulmonary functions during complex procedures. While ECC maintains systemic circulation and oxygenation, it also induces substantial physiological stress, including inflammatory responses, oxidative stress, and hormonal imbalances [2]. One notable metabolic consequence of this stress is the development of stress hyperglycemia (SH)—an acute increase in blood glucose levels resulting from elevated catecholamines, cortisol, and insulin resistance during surgery [3].

SHR is a relative glycemic index that reflects acute glucose elevation in relation to chronic glycemic status [4]. Understanding and monitoring SHR in patients undergoing ECC during open-heart surgery provides essential insights into individual metabolic responses and assists in guiding perioperative glycemic management, thereby enhancing patient outcomes [5]. This relationship underscores the importance of integrating metabolic considerations into the broader context of ECC-assisted surgeries [6].

Stress-induced hyperglycemia emerges from inflammation and neurohormonal disruptions during critical illness, reflecting heightened inflammatory and neurohormonal activity in response to severe stress [7]. Notably, emerging evidence has linked higher SHR to adverse prognosis in cardiac surgery populations, including analyses using the MIMIC-IV database. For example, Pei et al. reported that SHR was associated with increased all-cause mortality after cardiac surgery and developed a machine-learning prediction model, while Zhang et al. focused on patients undergoing CABG for acute myocardial infarction and found higher SHR to be associated with higher 90-day and 180-day mortality [8, 9]. We acknowledge that these studies substantially overlap with the research question addressed in the present manuscript. Therefore, the novelty of the present study is limited and should be understood mainly in terms of its exploratory characterization of the dose–response pattern in a broader ECC-assisted open-heart surgery cohort rather than in identifying SHR as a new prognostic factor. Despite these advances, it remains unclear whether SHR exhibits a non-linear dose–response relationship and whether an identifiable risk threshold exists in an undifferentiated ECC-assisted open-heart surgery cohort, where perioperative metabolic stress may differ across procedure types and indications. Moreover, in large critical care databases, glycemia is often measured at heterogeneous time points relative to surgery, potentially reflecting distinct physiological states and complicating interpretation. Thus, we aimed to explore the association between SHR and short-term mortality in patients undergoing extracorporeal circulation auxiliary to open heart surgery by using data from the MIMIC-IV database, while explicitly acknowledging these temporal limitations.

Materials and methods

Data source and study population

This retrospective cohort study utilized data extracted from the MIMIC-IV (version 3.1) database, a comprehensive resource developed by the MIT Computational Physiology Laboratory. The database comprises anonymized health records for over 60,000 ICU admissions at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center [10]. Due to the retrospective nature of this investigation, the Institutional Review Board of Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center waived the requirement for informed consent [11]. Therefore, this study did not require patient consent or ethics approval. One author, Peipei Zhang, completed the online course on Data or Specimens Only Research and gained access to the database for data extraction after passing the Collaborative Institutional Training Initiative (CITI) exam (Certification No: 60227204). The exact calendar recruitment period of the analytic cohort could not be reliably reconstructed from the extracted de-identified dataset used for this analysis. Because temporal changes in surgical techniques, perfusion strategies, ICU care, and perioperative glucose management may influence outcomes, the absence of clearly reportable recruitment dates was considered an important limitation.

Inclusion criteria and exclusion criteria

Patients who underwent extracorporeal circulation during open-heart surgery were identified using the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth and Tenth Revisions (ICD-9 and ICD-10) codes, as detailed in the supplementary material. To address the heterogeneity of surgical treatment, the cardiac procedures identified in this cohort were descriptively categorized according to procedure codes into the following broad types: coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG), valve surgery, combined CABG and valve surgery, and other extracorporeal circulation-assisted open-heart procedures. These procedure categories were used only to describe the surgical composition of the cohort and were not used for procedure-specific subgroup or stratified survival analyses. For individuals with multiple ICU admissions, only data from the first hospitalization were included. Patients younger than 18 years or those with incomplete glucose and HbA1c records were excluded. After applying these criteria, a total of 1,221 patients were included in the final cohort. These patients were subsequently stratified into quartiles based on their stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR) levels for comparative analysis (Fig. 1). Because SHR calculation required both glucose and HbA1c values, a large proportion of otherwise potentially eligible patients were excluded owing to incomplete glycemic data. Data collection

Data extraction and processing were conducted using PostgreSQL (v. 14; PostgreSQL Global Development Group, Berkeley, California, USA). Diagnoses were identified using ICD-9 and ICD-10 codes through Structured Query Language (SQL). Baseline characteristics and laboratory indicators records were retrieved using item IDs and subsequently matched to individual patients through unique identifiers (subject_id) within the MIMIC-IV database. Variables extracted included the following categories: demographics (age, gender, height, weight); clinical scores (Oxford Acute Severity of Illness Score (OASIS), Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA), Simplified Acute Physiology Score II (SAPS-II), Acute Physiology Score III (APSIII), Charlson Comorbidity Index); comorbidities (hypertension, heart failure (HF), Type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM), Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), acute kidney injury (AKI), myocardial infarction (MI), atrial fibrillation (AF)); key laboratory measurements (red blood cell counts (RBC), white blood cell counts (WBC), red blood cell distribution width (RDW), electrolytes such as calcium, chloride, potassium, and sodium, hematocrit (HCT), hemoglobin (HB), platelet count (PLT), creatinine, glucose, and hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c)); medication use (angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEI), angiotensin II receptor blockers (ARB), beta receptor-blockers (βRB)); surgical procedure type, including CABG, valve surgery, combined CABG and valve surgery, and other ECC-assisted open-heart procedures; and patient outcomes (hospital and ICU admission and discharge dates, and date and time of death).

We acknowledge that OASIS, SOFA, SAPS-II, and APSIII are general ICU severity scores rather than cardiac surgery-specific preoperative risk assessment tools. In routine cardiac surgery practice, the STS score is widely used in the United States, whereas EuroSCORE II is commonly used in Europe for preoperative risk stratification. However, neither the STS score nor the EuroSCORE II is provided as a precomputed structured variable in the MIMIC-IV database. Moreover, the complete set of clinical, operative, and anatomical variables required to accurately reconstruct these scores retrospectively was not consistently available in the structured MIMIC-IV tables. Therefore, STS score and EuroSCORE II could not be incorporated into the present analysis. The ICU severity scores included in this study were used only to characterize and adjust for the overall burden of acute illness and organ dysfunction after ICU admission, and should not be interpreted as substitutes for cardiac surgery-specific preoperative risk scores.

Although cardiopulmonary bypass duration and aortic cross-clamp time are clinically important intraoperative parameters that may affect inflammatory activation, neuroendocrine stress responses, and perioperative glucose fluctuations, these variables were not available as reliably extractable structured data in the MIMIC-IV dataset used in this study. Therefore, they could not be incorporated into the multivariable models or subgroup analyses. This limitation was considered when interpreting SHR as an aggregated prognostic marker rather than a direct measure of intraoperative metabolic stress. All measurements were collected within the first 24 h of ICU admission to standardize time points across subjects. Regarding the timing of HbA1c measurement, for SHR calculation in this study, HbA1c values were primarily derived from laboratory results recorded within the first 24 h of ICU admission (i.e., early postoperative ICU measurements). HbA1c values were extracted from available laboratory records within the first 24 h of ICU admission. Because preoperative outpatient HbA1c values were not consistently available, HbA1c timing could not be standardized to the preoperative period.

Variable definitions

The body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg) divided by the square of height (m). SHR was determined using the following formula: [admission blood glucose (mmol/L)/(1.59 × HbA1c (%) – 2.59)] [12, 13]. Diabetes was defined as either a documented history of diabetes or an HbA1c level exceeding 6.5%. It is important to emphasize that glucose and HbA1c values were extracted from available laboratory records within the first 24 h of ICU admission. The exact timing of glucose measurement relative to anesthesia induction, ECC exposure, surgery completion, ICU admission, insulin administration, or postoperative complications could not be determined. Therefore, SHR in this study reflects an early ICU aggregated glycemic marker rather than a standardized perioperative or intraoperative metabolic measurement. Given the highly dynamic nature of postoperative glycemia after cardiac surgery, this temporal imprecision is a major methodological limitation that may affect exposure classification, effect estimation, and clinical interpretation.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes assessed were all-cause mortality at 180 days post-admission, determined based on follow-up death records. Mortality status was determined using the death information available in the MIMIC-IV database. However, the database does not provide detailed information on how post-discharge vital status was ascertained for each patient, nor does it allow direct assessment of loss to follow-up after hospital discharge. Therefore, incomplete or differential ascertainment of post-discharge mortality cannot be excluded.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as means with standard deviations (SD) or medians with interquartile ranges (IQR), while categorical variables were presented as frequencies with percentages, stratified by baseline SHR quartiles. Intergroup differences among SHR quartiles were evaluated using chi-squared tests for categorical variables, one-way ANOVA for normally distributed data, and the Kruskal–Wallis H test for non-normally distributed data. The third SHR quartile (Q3; 0.94 ≤ SHR < 1.12) served as the reference group in all comparative analyses because it is centered around SHR ≈ 1.0, which physiologically reflects admission glucose being close to the HbA1c-derived baseline glycemic level (i.e., a clinically "neutral" state without marked relative stress hyperglycemia), and it represents the mid-distribution, thereby reducing undue influence from extreme quartiles.

To examine the relationship between SHR levels and mortality risk at 180 days, multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models were employed to calculate hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Model 1 included no covariate adjustments. Model 2 adjusted only for age and gender. Model 3 additionally adjusted for HB, RBC, HCT, BMI, Chloride, Creatinine, Heart failure and T2DM. To address whether the observed prognostic association of SHR was driven primarily by HbA1c itself, we performed an additional sensitivity analysis using HbA1c as a single exposure variable. HbA1c was evaluated using Kaplan–Meier survival analysis, restricted cubic spline analysis, and Cox proportional hazards regression models with the same stepwise adjustment strategy. To address multicollinearity, variables with a variance inflation factor (VIF) exceeding five were excluded from the final models.

Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was conducted to assess endpoint occurrence rates across SHR quartile levels. The distribution of missing values for the variables is shown in Table S1. Parameters with more than 20% missing values were excluded from the analysis to minimize potential bias. For variables with less than 20% missing data, multiple imputation was applied to handle missing values, which is a widely accepted approach in clinical prediction modeling.

Cardiac biomarkers, including cardiac troponin T and BNP/NT-proBNP, were also searched in the MIMIC-IV laboratory tables. Although these variables were available for a subset of patients, they contained a high proportion of missing values in the eligible cohort. According to our predefined missing-data handling strategy, variables with more than 20% missing values were excluded from the main analysis to reduce selection bias and model instability. Therefore, cardiac troponin T and BNP/NT-proBNP were not incorporated into the multivariable models, correlation analyses, or subgroup analyses.

In addition, hemodynamic instability was not treated as an independent endpoint in this study. Although MIMIC-IV contains time-varying hemodynamic-related information, a uniformly coded perioperative hemodynamic instability variable was not available in the structured dataset used for this analysis. Retrospectively defining hemodynamic instability based on blood pressure, vasoactive medication, or lactate values would require additional assumptions regarding perioperative timing relative to surgery and extracorporeal circulation. Therefore, the present study did not formally evaluate the correlation between SHR and hemodynamic instability.

All analyses were conducted using DecisionLinnc1.0 software (https://www.statsape.com/) with a two-sided p-value of < 0.05 considered statistically significant [14].P values were reported as exact values whenever available. For very small values, p < 0.001 was retained only when the statistical output did not provide a more precise value or when reporting exact values was not practical.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Table 1 provides a comprehensive overview of the baseline characteristics for the cohort of 1,221 patients, stratified by SHR quartiles (Q1 < 0.77, 0.77 ≤ Q2 < 0.94, 0.94 ≤ Q3 < 1.12, and 1.12 ≤ Q4). Participants had a mean age of 64.47 ± 10.91 years, with males comprising 71.1% of the cohort. The cohort consisted of patients undergoing different types of ECC-assisted open-heart procedures, including CABG, valve surgery, combined CABG and valve surgery, and other open-heart procedures requiring extracorporeal circulation. These surgical categories were described to clarify the composition of the study population; however, no procedure-specific mortality analysis was performed. Patients in the highest SHR quartile (Q4) presented with elevated levels of hemoglobin (HB), hematocrit (HCT), creatinine, and glucose. Additionally, these patients exhibited significantly lower levels of HbA1c, calcium, and chloride compared to those in the lower SHR quartiles. Regarding hematologic indicators, patients in the highest SHR quartile did not show evidence of more pronounced anemia based on hemoglobin or hematocrit values. In fact, hemoglobin and hematocrit were higher in Q4 than in the lower SHR quartiles. RDW differed significantly across SHR quartiles, suggesting that erythrocyte heterogeneity and systemic illness burden may be related to stress glycemic responses. However, because RDW and anemia-related variables were not the primary exposures, and the number of outcome events was limited, we did not perform additional RDW- or anemia-specific interaction Association between SHR and mortality

Kaplan–Meier (KM) survival analysis revealed significant differences in 180-day all-cause mortality among patients stratified by SHR quartiles (log-rank p = 0.0063), with notably lower mortality observed in quartiles Q1, Q2, and Q3 compared to Q4 (Fig. 2). Table 2 presents multivariable Cox regression analyses for 180-day all-cause mortality. Adjusting for potential confounders, higher SHR levels remained significantly associated with increased short-term mortality following extracorporeal circulation during open-heart surgery. Specifically, patients in the highest SHR quartile (Q4) had a substantially increased risk of mortality at 180 days compared to the reference quartile. For example, in Model 3, the adjusted hazard ratio (HR) for 180-day mortality in Q4 was approximately 3.07 (95% CI: 1.31–7.18, p = 0.01). This association held true even when SHR was analyzed as a continuous variable, supporting SHR’s role as a key prognostic indicator. When SHR was treated as a continuous variable, the restricted cubic spline (RCS) model demonstrated a nonlinear association with 180-day all-cause mortality. Adjusted analyses revealed an inflection point at 0.97; below this threshold, the HR was about 0.26 (95% CI: 0.05–1.24), not statistically significant. However, once SHR exceeded 0.97, the HR increased sharply to 4.61 (95% CI: 2.67–7.95, p < 0.001) (Fig. 3). Although the spline curve suggested a possible U-shaped tendency, the evidence for increased mortality risk was more robust at higher SHR levels. Therefore, we describe this finding more cautiously as a nonlinear, threshold-like prognostic association rather than as definitive evidence of a strictly U-shaped relationship. Notably, the spline-identified inflection point (≈0.97) fell within Q3 (0.94–1.12), which further supports our use of Q3 as the clinically neutral reference range for interpreting risk increases at both lower and higher SHR levels. Additionally, a combination of Cox proportional hazard models with a two-segmented Cox proportional hazards model was employed to study the non-linear relationship between SHR levels in patients undergoing extracorporeal circulation auxiliary to open-heart surgery and the mortality rate mentioned above (P for log-likelihood ratio < 0.05). Subgroup analyzes

In the subgroup analyzes (Fig. 4), stratified by specific comorbidities, most subgroups showed no statistically significant heterogeneity in hazard ratios (HRs) related to higher SHR, as the majority of interaction p-values exceeded 0.05. Subjects were stratified into two age groups: those younger than 65 and those 65 years or older. The group aged < 65 exhibited an HR of approximately 2.12 (95% CI: 0.68–6.66), whereas the group aged ≥ 65 exhibited an HR of approximately 3.05 (95% CI: 1.70–5.47), suggesting a numerically higher risk in the latter group; however, the interaction p-value of 0.574 indicates this difference is not statistically significant and should be interpreted with caution. When stratified by BMI, subjects with a BMI < 30 exhibited an HR of approximately 2.66 (95% CI: 1.25–5.68), whereas subjects with a BMI ≥ 30 exhibited an HR of approximately 2.92 (95% CI: 1.41–6.05), both groups showing significant risk increase. The interaction p-value of 0.861 confirms no meaningful difference between BMI groups. Regarding gender, females exhibited an HR of approximately 1.6 (95% CI: 0.61–4.19), which was not statistically significant, whereas males exhibited an HR of approximately 3.89 (95% CI: 2.07–7.30), signifying a significant risk elevation. Nevertheless, the interaction p-value of 0.113 suggests that the risk difference between genders is not statistically significant.Fig. 4Subgroup analysis with 180-day mortality as the outcome event

Other clinical features—including atrial fibrillation, Type 2 diabetes, acute kidney injury, the use of ACEIs/ARB/β-blockers, and heart failure—were also analyzed. Most interaction p-values exceeded 0.05, indicating no significant heterogeneity across subgroups. The increased risk (HR > 1) associated with higher SHR is consistent across different populations. The association between higher SHR and 180-day mortality appeared numerically stronger in patients without type 2 diabetes mellitus, those presenting with acute kidney injury, individuals receiving beta-blocker therapy, and those not administered angiotensin II receptor blockers (ARBs). However, the absence of significant interaction effects warrants cautious interpretation, and these findings should be regarded as exploratory.

Sensitivity analysis

To ensure the robustness of our findings and to comply with the commonly applied ten-events-per-variable rule, we performed a sensitivity analysis using the LASSO method. This procedure reduced the number of candidate variables to six, thereby satisfying the criterion. A subsequent Cox regression analysis based on the LASSO-selected variables produced results that were consistent with those of the primary analysis (Table S2).

Furthermore, to evaluate the potential impact of missing data, we conducted a complete-case analysis by excluding all observations with missing values, followed by Cox regression analysis. The findings from this approach were also in agreement with the main results. These consistent outcomes across different analytical strategies provide additional support for the stability and reliability of our conclusions (Table S3).

In response to the possibility that HbA1c itself may predict mortality, we further performed a sensitivity analysis using HbA1c as a single exposure variable. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis showed no significant difference in 180-day survival between patients with higher and lower HbA1c levels (log-rank p = 0.29) (Figure S1). Restricted cubic spline analysis also did not demonstrate a significant overall or nonlinear association between HbA1c and 180-day mortality (P for overall = 0.728; P for nonlinear = 0.768) (Figure S2). In Cox regression analyses, HbA1c was not significantly associated with 180-day mortality in the unadjusted model (HR 1.064; 95% CI 0.899–1.259; p = 0.473), the age- and sex-adjusted model (HR 1.073; 95% CI 0.898–1.281; p = 0.438), or the fully adjusted model (HR 1.166; 95% CI 0.948–1.435; p = 0.146) (Table S4). These findings suggest that HbA1c alone did not show a significant association with 180-day mortality in this cohort, supporting the incremental prognostic relevance of SHR as a relative glycemic stress marker rather than merely reflecting chronic glycemic status.

Discussion

These findings suggest the prognostic relevance of SHR for short-term mortality following extracorporeal circulation during open-heart surgery. However, the present study does not establish SHR as a novel biomarker, because previous studies have already examined SHR and mortality after cardiac surgery, including analyses based on MIMIC-IV. The incremental contribution of this study is limited to an exploratory evaluation of the nonlinear dose–response pattern in a broader ECC-assisted open-heart surgery cohort.

Clinically, our study identifies a potential prognostic inflection point at SHR = 0.97, above which mortality risk significantly increases. This point should not be interpreted as a clinically validated threshold, therapeutic target, or decision-making cutoff. Based on the shape of the spline curve, the risk increase appeared broad and gradual, and the evidence for excess risk was more convincing at higher SHR levels than around the inflection point itself. Therefore, the clinical relevance of the 0.97 cutoff is limited and requires external validation.

A critical limitation of this study is the absence of precise information regarding the timing of glycemia measurement relative to the surgical procedure and ECC exposure. Glycemia may vary substantially across hospitalization, and measurements obtained within the first 24 h of ICU admission may represent preoperative stress, immediate postoperative physiology, or early critical illness. In the present study, glucose values recorded within the first 24 h of ICU admission may represent heterogeneous perioperative states rather than a uniform exposure window. This is not a minor limitation but a fundamental issue affecting the internal validity and interpretability of the primary exposure variable [16, 17]. Consequently, SHR should be considered an aggregated early ICU prognostic marker rather than a direct measure of perioperative metabolic stress.

Our findings are consistent with and extend recent MIMIC-IV-based evidence. Pei et al. reported an association between higher SHR and increased mortality after cardiac surgery and further incorporated SHR into a machine-learning prediction framework, while Zhang et al. demonstrated a similar relationship in the specific context of CABG performed for acute myocardial infarction. Because of this existing literature, our study should be framed as confirmatory and exploratory rather than highly novel. In contrast to these more procedure- or modeling-focused analyses, our study examined an undifferentiated ECC-assisted open-heart surgery cohort and specifically characterized the nonlinear dose–response association using restricted cubic splines and a two-piece Cox model, identifying an inflection point (≈0.97) beyond which the mortality hazard increased sharply. Because the increased risk was more clearly supported at higher SHR levels, we interpret this pattern cautiously as a nonlinear prognostic association rather than as conclusive evidence of a strictly U-shaped relationship.

The observed nonlinear pattern, rather than definitive evidence of a strictly U-shaped relationship, highlights the complexity underlying the association between SHR and clinical outcomes. Mild-to-moderate elevations in SHR appear to have minimal impact; however, beyond a specific threshold, the mortality risk increases markedly. At the lower end of SHR, the association with mortality was not statistically significant in the segmented analysis; therefore, potential explanations related to hypoglycemia, aggressive glucose-lowering interventions, or malnutrition should be regarded as speculative. Hypoglycemia, for instance, can exacerbate hemodynamic instability and impair tissue oxygenation, leading to further organ dysfunction. Aggressive glucose-lowering treatments may also result in insulin-induced hypoglycemia, contributing to worsening outcomes. Accordingly, our findings support the use of SHR for risk stratification and hypothesis generation, but they do not establish an optimal SHR range or justify treatment decisions based on this threshold alone [18–21].

These results align with previous studies that associate perioperative hyperglycemia with adverse outcomes in cardiac surgery [22, 23]. Mechanistically, stress-induced hyperglycemia is associated with heightened inflammatory responses, oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, and impaired immune competence, each of which may exacerbate organ injury and increase vulnerability to infections and thrombotic events [24, 25]. Thus, glycemic management may represent a modifiable risk factor capable of improving postoperative outcomes.

The relationship between ECC and SHR is of significant clinical interest in the context of open-heart surgery. ECC, while essential for maintaining systemic circulation and oxygenation during cardiac procedures, induces substantial physiological stress through mechanisms such as inflammatory activation, oxidative stress, and hormonal dysregulation. These stress responses can lead to SH, a common phenomenon during ECC [26]. However, it is crucial to emphasize that the exact temporal relationship between SHR measurement and the ECC procedure remains unclear. Given that glycemic fluctuations may vary perioperatively, relying on a single admission-based SHR might not accurately reflect the dynamic changes in glucose levels during or after surgery. The potential for such a temporal limitation to attenuate or bias the observed associations should be considered, and further studies with more precise timing of SHR measurements relative to the perioperative stages (pre-, intra-, or postoperative) are needed to improve the accuracy of this metric in reflecting perioperative metabolic stress. Therefore, future studies should focus on more precise timing of SHR measurement relative to the ECC procedure to better understand the glycemic responses and their association with clinical outcomes.

The potential association between SHR and hematologic abnormalities also deserves consideration. Anemia may aggravate tissue hypoxia and myocardial oxygen supply–demand imbalance after cardiac surgery, whereas elevated RDW has been regarded as a marker of inflammation, oxidative stress, nutritional status, and general physiologic vulnerability. In the present cohort, RDW differed significantly across SHR quartiles, whereas patients in the highest SHR quartile did not have lower hemoglobin or hematocrit values. Thus, elevated SHR was not clearly more common in patients with anemia based on the available baseline hematologic data. The observed RDW differences may indicate shared systemic stress or inflammatory pathways, but causality cannot be inferred.

Postoperative myocardial injury biomarkers are also clinically relevant in this context. Cardiac troponin T is widely used as a marker of myocardial injury and has prognostic value after cardiac surgery, while BNP and NT-proBNP reflect myocardial wall stress and cardiac dysfunction [27]. These biomarkers may theoretically be associated with SHR because perioperative myocardial injury, ventricular dysfunction, systemic inflammation, and neurohormonal activation can all contribute to postoperative stress hyperglycemia. However, although troponin T and BNP/NT-proBNP data were present in MIMIC-IV for some patients, these variables had excessive missingness in the eligible cohort and were excluded according to our predefined missing-data criterion. Therefore, we were unable to reliably assess the correlation between SHR and troponin T or BNP/NT-proBNP. Future prospective studies should include serial measurements of glucose, HbA1c, troponin T, BNP/NT-proBNP, and hemodynamic parameters to clarify whether myocardial injury and circulatory instability mediate the association between SHR and postoperative mortality.

In addition to the timing of glycemia assessment, operative and perfusion-related parameters may also influence stress hyperglycemia. In particular, cardiopulmonary bypass duration and aortic cross-clamp time are important markers of surgical complexity and ischemia–reperfusion burden, and longer exposure may intensify inflammatory activation, catecholamine release, insulin resistance, and postoperative metabolic dysregulation. These factors could therefore influence both SHR and mortality risk. However, these variables were not available in the structured MIMIC-IV data used for this analysis, preventing adjustment for cardiopulmonary bypass duration or aortic cross-clamp time. As a result, residual confounding related to operative complexity and intraoperative stress cannot be excluded. This limitation further supports interpreting the observed association between SHR and 180-day mortality as prognostic and hypothesis-generating rather than causal.

SHR provides a standardized measure of hyperglycemia relative to the patient’s baseline glycemic status. Unlike absolute blood glucose levels, SHR accounts for preoperative glycemic variability, offering a more nuanced understanding of the body’s metabolic response to surgical stress and ECC. Research has demonstrated that a higher SHR is independently associated with worse postoperative outcomes, including increased risks of infection, prolonged mechanical ventilation, acute kidney injury, and mortality [28, 29]. However, SHR is calculated using a formula derived from Roberts et al. (2015), which has not been universally validated. Variability in glucose and HbA1c measurement timing (within 24 h of ICU admission) may introduce measurement bias. The timing of glucose measurement—whether preoperative or postoperative—should be considered when interpreting the results.

The SHR formula, as proposed by Roberts et al. (2015), has not been universally validated across all populations or laboratory methodologies. Variability in HbA1c and glucose measurement techniques can introduce bias in the SHR calculation. It is crucial to acknowledge these methodological issues and the potential impact they may have on the robustness and generalizability of the findings. The timing of glucose measurement and the consistency of HbA1c testing across diverse settings are important factors that may influence SHR accuracy. Therefore, further studies are necessary to assess the formula’s consistency and validate it across different patient populations and lab methodologies.

During ECC, the interplay of stress hormones (e.g., cortisol and catecholamines), insulin resistance, and impaired glucose utilization are amplified, particularly due to the contact of blood with artificial surfaces and the use of cardioplegia. These factors collectively contribute to elevated SHR values. Furthermore, systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) triggered by ECC exacerbates glucose dysregulation, highlighting the critical role of ECC in driving metabolic stress during surgery [30].

From a clinical perspective, SHR may help identify patients requiring closer glycemic monitoring. Elevated SHR may serve as a biomarker for identifying high-risk patients, enabling targeted interventions such as optimized glucose control strategies (e.g., insulin therapy, or tighter glycemic monitoring). Additionally, understanding the relationship between ECC and SHR emphasizes the importance of tailoring perioperative care to mitigate the adverse effects of stress hyperglycemia. Future studies focusing on SHR in the ECC setting may provide further insights into improving patient outcomes and refining glycemic management protocols.

Subgroup analyses revealed that elevated stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR) was consistently associated with increased mortality risk across most patient groups, particularly in those aged ≥ 65 (HR 3.05, p = 0.005) and males (HR 3.89, p < 0.001). However, interaction tests (p > 0.05) indicated no statistically significant differences in risk between subgroups, suggesting that SHR’s predictive value remains generally consistent, although exploratory regardless of age, BMI, sex, or comorbidities (e.g., diabetes, kidney injury, or medication use). The association was especially pronounced in patients without type 2 diabetes, those with acute kidney injury, beta-blocker users, and non-ARB recipients. These findings underscore SHR as a reliable, independent predictor of mortality risk. Nonetheless, as with overall findings, residual confounding cannot be excluded.

These findings emphasize that SHR serves as a consistent and potential prognostic marker of mortality risk, irrespective of baseline characteristics or comorbidities. However, it is critical to reiterate that these associations do not imply causality, and possible confounders may influence outcomes.

Future research should focus on further elucidating the mechanistic pathways that contribute to the nonlinear association between SHR and mortality. Additionally, clinical trials assessing the impact of targeted glycemic control strategies in patients with elevated SHR are needed to validate these findings and improve perioperative care in cardiac surgery patients. Again, the observational design of this study necessitates caution in drawing causal conclusions, and future studies should aim to address potential confounders.

A further methodological issue concerns the selection of clinical severity scores. The reviewer correctly noted that OASIS, SOFA, SAPS-II, and APSIII are not routinely used as preoperative risk assessment tools in cardiac surgery. In clinical cardiac surgery practice, the STS score and EuroSCORE II are more appropriate and widely used instruments for preoperative risk stratification. However, the present study was based on the MIMIC-IV database, in which the STS score and EuroSCORE II are not provided as structured or precomputed variables. In addition, several components required to reconstruct these scores, particularly detailed cardiac surgical and operative variables, were unavailable or incompletely captured in the structured dataset. Therefore, we were unable to adjust for STS score or EuroSCORE II in the present analysis. The severity scores used in this study should therefore be interpreted as markers of general ICU illness severity and organ dysfunction rather than cardiac surgery-specific preoperative risk scores. This distinction is important when interpreting the prognostic role of SHR in this cohort.

We performed additional sensitivity analyses using HbA1c as a single exposure variable. In contrast to SHR, HbA1c alone was not significantly associated with 180-day mortality in Kaplan–Meier analysis, restricted cubic spline analysis, or multivariable Cox regression. These results suggest that the prognostic association observed for SHR was unlikely to be explained solely by chronic glycemic status as reflected by HbA1c. Rather, SHR may capture the relative degree of acute glycemic elevation in relation to background glycemia, which may be more relevant to short-term prognosis in critically ill postoperative patients. Nevertheless, because HbA1c measurement timing and perioperative glucose dynamics remain heterogeneous in this retrospective database, this interpretation should remain cautious and hypothesis-generating.

Limitations

Although this study provides valuable insights, several limitations should be emphasized. First, this was a retrospective observational study, and no causal inference can be made. The most important limitation is the lack of precise perioperative timing for glucose measurement. SHR was calculated using glucose values obtained within the first 24 h of ICU admission, but the exact timing relative to anesthesia induction, ECC exposure, surgery completion, ICU admission, insulin therapy, and postoperative complications could not be determined. Because postoperative glycemia after cardiac surgery is highly dynamic, often changing substantially within hours, this temporal imprecision may lead to exposure misclassification and substantially limits the internal validity and interpretability of SHR as the primary exposure. Therefore, SHR should be interpreted only as an aggregated early ICU prognostic marker rather than as a direct measure of perioperative metabolic stress.

Second, a substantial proportion of potentially eligible patients were excluded because SHR calculation required both glucose and HbA1c measurements. As noted by the reviewer, nearly 70% of the original potentially eligible database population was excluded because of incomplete glycemic data. This creates a serious risk of selection bias, because patients with available HbA1c and glucose measurements may differ systematically from those without such measurements in terms of diabetes history, illness severity, perioperative management, monitoring intensity, and prognosis. This limitation threatens internal validity and may restrict generalizability.

Third, the exact calendar recruitment period of the analytic cohort was not reported in the extracted de-identified dataset used for this analysis. Historical timeframes may influence surgical outcomes because surgical techniques, cardiopulmonary bypass management, ICU care, transfusion practice, and perioperative glycemic protocols evolve over time. The inability to clearly report and adjust for calendar periods is therefore an important limitation.

Fourth, mortality was ascertained using death information available in MIMIC-IV. However, the specific source and completeness of post-discharge death ascertainment could not be verified for each patient, and the database did not allow formal evaluation of loss to follow-up after discharge. Because many patients would have been discharged before 180 days, incomplete or differential post-discharge mortality ascertainment cannot be excluded.

Fifth, the surgical procedures included CABG, valve surgery, combined CABG and valve surgery, and other ECC-assisted open-heart procedures. These procedures may differ in surgical indication, operative complexity, ECC duration, myocardial ischemia–reperfusion burden, transfusion requirement, inflammatory response, and postoperative risk. However, procedure-specific mortality analyses were not performed because of limited event numbers and insufficient granular operative data.

Sixth, important intraoperative and perioperative variables were unavailable in the structured MIMIC-IV data used for this analysis. These included cardiopulmonary bypass duration, aortic cross-clamp time, intraoperative glucose values, insulin protocols, glucose monitoring frequency, perioperative corticosteroid exposure, transfusion details, and surgical urgency. Their absence may have resulted in residual confounding related to operative complexity and perioperative metabolic stress.

Seventh, cardiac surgery-specific risk scores, such as the STS score and EuroSCORE II, could not be incorporated because they are not available as structured precomputed variables in MIMIC-IV, and the available data did not allow reliable reconstruction. Therefore, adjustment relied on general ICU severity scores and comorbidity indicators, which should not be considered substitutes for cardiac surgery-specific preoperative risk models.

Eighth, the spline-derived inflection point around 0.97 should be interpreted cautiously. The observed curve suggested a broad nonlinear pattern, but the increase in mortality risk was more clearly supported at higher SHR values, whereas the lower-SHR segment was not statistically significant. Therefore, this cutoff should not be considered clinically validated or used to guide treatment decisions.

Finally, although HbA1c alone was not significantly associated with 180-day mortality in sensitivity analyses, HbA1c measurement timing could not be fully standardized. Therefore, the absence of a significant HbA1c association should not be interpreted as evidence that chronic glycemic status has no prognostic relevance in cardiac surgery populations.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that SHR is associated with short-term mortality in critically ill patients undergoing extracorporeal circulation during open-heart surgery. Elevated SHR levels, particularly those exceeding identified thresholds, correlate with substantially increased 180-day mortality risks. Furthermore, the absence of significant effect modification across a wide range of clinical subgroups indicates that the prognostic value of SHR is broadly consistent. By characterizing a nonlinear association, with the increase in mortality risk mainly observed at higher SHR levels above an inflection point around 0.97, our findings complement prior cardiac-surgery SHR evidence (including recent MIMIC-IV analyses) and may refine prognostic interpretation in this perioperative setting. However, this inflection point should not be interpreted as a validated clinical cutoff or therapeutic target. Because the study was retrospective, overlapped with prior SHR research using similar data sources, lacked precise perioperative glucose timing, excluded a large proportion of patients because of missing glycemic data, could not report reliable recruitment dates, could not fully verify post-discharge mortality ascertainment, and could not adjust for key operative variables such as cardiopulmonary bypass duration and aortic cross-clamp time, the findings should be considered hypothesis-generating. Prospective studies with standardized serial glucose monitoring, detailed operative data, complete follow-up, and cardiac surgery-specific risk adjustment are needed to validate these observations.
