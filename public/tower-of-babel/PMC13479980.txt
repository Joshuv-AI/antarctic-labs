Determinants of workplace violence against nurses and mitigation strategies in hospitals in developing countries: a scoping review
Authors: Fatemeh Mahmoudi, Mahnaz Ashoorkhani, Nadia Saniee, Narjes Razavi, Ali Mohammad Mosadeghrad, Gholamreza Garmaroudi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundViolence against nurses has become a serious concern, especially in developing countries. Given the critical role that nurses play in healthcare, it is essential to implement interventions aimed at reducing this violence. The objective of this study was to identify the causes of violence against nurses in hospitals in developing countries and to propose mitigation strategies.MethodsThis scoping review was conducted in accordance with Arksey and O’Malley framework and reported based on PRISMA-ScR guideline in 2024. A comprehensive search was carried out across international databases including PubMed, Scopus, PsycInfo, and Web of Science. The search strategy involved a combination of keywords related to “nurses” and “violence,” covering studies published between 2003 and 2024. Data extraction was performed using a structured form developed in Microsoft Word 2016. Thematic content analysis was employed to synthesize the findings.ResultsDuring the search, 2658 records were identified, with 414 duplicates removed. Ultimately, 160 full texts were reviewed, and 74 records were deemed eligible for inclusion in the study. The main causes of violence against nurses in developing countries categorized by patients and companions’ causes (e.g., unmet expectations of the patient and their companion), nurses’ causes (e.g., stress due to workload pressure), organizational causes (e.g., crowded work environment and excessive noise), social causes (e.g., low level of public awareness), and political causes (e.g., political issues). The most important interventions for reducing violence against nurses were categorized into three levels: before the occurrence of violence (e.g., restriction of patient- companions’ mobility), during the occurrence of violence (e.g., calling security personnel to the scene of violence), and after the occurrence of violence (e.g., reporting incidents of violence).ConclusionThe results revealed that violence against nurses in developing countries is a complex, multifactorial issue rooted in individual, organizational, social, and political domains. A holistic and context-sensitive strategy is required to address workplace violence against nurses effectively. Interventions must go beyond individual behavior and encompass organizational reforms, public awareness, and policy development. Future research should prioritize evaluating interventions during and after violence, assessing long-term impacts, and exploring the role of leadership and organizational culture.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-026-04835-1.

Introduction

In healthcare, violence is recognized as a major global public health issue, manifesting as verbal and physical aggression toward Healthcare Workers (HCWs), most often by patients and visitors [1]. The WHO’s World Report on Violence and Health analyzes various types of violence, including interpersonal, self-directed, and collective violence, exploring their magnitude, risk factors, and prevention efforts [2]. Recent systematic reviews highlight the high prevalence of workplace violence against nurses, physicians, and dental healthcare providers [1].

Workplace Violence (WPV) directed at nurses is particularly pervasive. Studies show that 73–76% of nurses experience some of violence during their careers [3]. A global review reported exposure rates of 36.4% for physical violence, 66.9% for nonphysical violence, 39.7% for bullying, and 25% for sexual harassment [4]. Patients and their families are responsible for more than 90% of these incidents. The consequences are severe, with over 20% of nurses leaving their profession due to escalating violence. Underreporting remains a critical issue, with over 80% of cases going unreported [5]. Identified risk factors include working in emergency departments, geriatric units, and psychiatric facilities, as well as personal characteristics such as single marital status and non-local ethnicity [3, 4].

In developing countries, WPV against nurses is equally concerning, with prevalence rates ranging from 43.1% to 56% in some developing countries. Verbal abuse is the most common form, followed by physical violence, bullying, and sexual harassment [6, 7]. Patients and their relatives are typically the main perpetrators of verbal and physical abuse, whereas colleagues are more often responsible for sexual harassment. Risk factors include female gender, younger age, alcohol consumption, and employment in high-stress units. Notably, training in violence prevention and control has been shown to reduce the risk of victimization by 40% [8].

Efforts to address WPV against nurses include standalone training sessions, structured education programs, and multicomponent interventions [9]. While educational interventions may improve nurses’ perceived preparedness for violent situations, their impact on reducing actual incidents remains limited [10]. More comprehensive interventions particularly those incorporating organizational changes and stakeholder involvement are considered [9]. Recommended strategies include zero-tolerance policies, workplace protection plans, and leadership practices that model respectful behaviors [11]. However, the evidence supporting the effectiveness of these approaches remains inconclusive [12]. Further research, particularly multi-site and multidisciplinary action-oriented studies, is urgently needed to develop an evidence base for preventing and mitigating violence against nurses.

Nurses, because of their frequent and close interactions with patients, are particularly vulnerable and require focused protection to preserve their physical and psychological well-being. A safe working environment is also crucial for maintaining high quality healthcare services. In developing countries, where healthcare systems often constrained by limited resources, workforce shortages, and inadequate infrastructure, understanding the scope and causes of WPV against nurses and identifying strategies to address it is especially critical. Despite the importance of this issue, the existing literature shows a lack of comprehensive scoping reviews that examine both the underlying causes and potential interventions for violence against nurses in developing countries. Therefore, the objective of this study is to identify the root causes of nurse-directed violence in developing countries and to identify effective strategies for its reduction through a scoping review.

Methods

Design

This scoping review was conducted following the Arksey and O’Malley framework [13], which involves six key stages: identifying the research question, conducting a comprehensive search to identify relevant studies, selecting studies based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, charting key data from the included studies, collating, summarizing, and reporting the results to map existing evidence and identify gaps, and consultation with stakeholders. This review was reported in accordance with the PRISMA extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) [14]. A scoping review was chosen because it enables a systematic exploration of a broad and complex research question, the causes of violence against nurses and strategies to mitigate it in developing countries, while supporting the identification of knowledge gaps and providing a comprehensive mapping of the existing evidence. The protocol of this scoping review was not registered on any protocol registration websites.

Search strategy

A comprehensive search was conducted across major international databases, including PubMed, Scopus, PsycInfo, and Web of Science. The search strategy combined keywords related to “nurses” and “violence,” along with their corresponding Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms. We selected the 2003–2024 period to capture contemporary evidence, as formal international guidelines on workplace violence in the health sector were first issued in 2002 by the ILO, WHO, ICN, and PSI, which helped standardize research and interventions in this field. This resulted in a rise of the publication of intervention-focused studies on reducing violence against healthcare personnel, particularly nurses, from 2003 onwards [15]. The search strategy was developed based on the PCC (Population, Concept, and Context) framework. All searches were conducted by F.M. The search strategies used for each database are presented in Appendix 1. To ensure comprehensiveness, the reference lists of relevant articles were also screened manually to identify additional eligible studies.

Eligibility criteria

The eligibility criteria were developed based on the research question.

Inclusion criteria

Research articles, review papers, and gray literature addressing violence (C) against nurses, regardless of their specific roles (P), across all types of hospitals (C), as well as interventions aimed at reducing such violence in developing countries. For the purpose of this study, ‘developing countries’ were defined according to the United Nations classification of countries located in developing regions. According to the United Nations Department of Economic and Social Affairs (UNDESA), the term developing countries refers to countries located in Africa, Asia (excluding Japan), Latin America and the Caribbean, as well as Melanesia, Micronesia and Polynesia, while countries in Europe, Northern America, Australia/New Zealand, and Japan are considered developed regions [16]. Exclusion criteria

Case studies, letters to the editor, editorials, commentaries, and perspective papers were excluded from the review.

Study selection and data extraction

All identified records were imported into EndNote X8 software. After removing duplicates, approximately 25 titles/abstracts was screened by all reviewers, discrepancies were discussed and criteria refined, full screening proceeded only when ≥ 75% agreement was achieved. Then, titles and abstracts were screened by one reviewer to identify studies focusing on the causes of violence against nurses and strategies for its reduction in developing countries. The full texts of potentially relevant studies were then independently reviewed by two reviewers. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer. A data extraction form was developed in Microsoft Word 2016 to record key bibliographic and study details, including first author, year of publication, place of the study, research objective, methodology, and findings related to the causes of violence and corresponding reduction strategies.

Critical appraisal

Given the nature of this scoping review and the diversity of research methods employed in the included studies, a formal critical appraisal was not conducted.

Data synthesis

Data were synthesized using qualitative thematic content analysis, following the framework proposed by Braun and Clarke [17]. This process included: (1) familiarization with the data, (2) generation of initial codes, (3) identification of semantic units within the documents, (4) review and refinement of these units, (5) categorization and definition and coding of categories, and (6) reporting the findings. Causes of violence and mitigation strategies were treated as overarching categories, with specific elements extracted as subcategories. Overlapping subcategories were subsequently merged. Data extraction was conducted using Microsoft Word 2016.

Results

A total of 2,658 records were initially retrieved across all databases. After removing 414 duplicates, 2,244 titles and abstracts were screened. The majority of excluded records did not address either the causes of violence against nurses or interventions for its prevention. Following full-text assessment of 160 potentially relevant articles, 74 studies met the inclusion criteria and were incorporated into this review (Fig. 1).

The main causes of violence against nurses in developing countries

A total of 31 patient and companion related causes were identified including unmet expectations of care, mental illness, substance use, low health literacy, dissatisfaction with services, long waiting times, and cultural or language barriers. Patient demographics (male gender, extreme age groups, and psychiatric ward admission) also emerged as risk factors.

Staff-related causes (28 codes) included poor communication with patients, high stress from workload, job burnout, insufficient training in violence prevention, lack of clinical competence, younger or inexperienced staff, and limited managerial support. Notably, some studies reported that nurses considered violence to be an inherent part of their professional role, which discouraged reporting.

Organizational causes (33 codes) were the most frequently reported contributors, spanning increased workload, inadequate infrastructure, long waiting times, insufficient staffing, poor management of violent incidents, weak security measures, and lack of clear guidelines for patients and companions.

At the broader level, social causes (6 codes) included negative media portrayals of healthcare staff, public misconceptions about the nursing profession, low societal awareness, and widespread normalization of violence. Finally, political causes (10 codes) were highlighted, such as weak legislative frameworks, absence of workplace protection policies, poor documentation of violent incidents, lack of accountability for perpetrators, and limited governmental support.

Interventions for managing violence against nurses in developing countries

Preventive strategies (before violence occurs)

Most interventions focused on prevention. At the patient-companion level (9 codes), strategies included limiting the number and mobility of companions, enhancing health literacy, providing patient safety measures, pain management, and flagging high-risk patients. At the staff level, 17 strategies were identified, emphasizing workforce adequacy, continuous training in violence management, improving communication, stress and anger management programs, self-care, and fair workload distribution. Enhanced recruitment, higher salaries, and professional development opportunities were also cited as protective measures. Organizational interventions (16 codes) involved strengthening hospital infrastructure, installing surveillance and alert systems, establishing reporting mechanisms, reducing patient waiting times, and introducing clear triage and visitation regulations. Administrative measures such as management training workshops and risk assessment protocols were also recommended. Social interventions (4 codes) emphasized community education, awareness campaigns about the nursing profession, and cultural programs to promote respect for healthcare workers. At the political level (7 codes), proposed measures included implementing workplace violence regulations, institutionalizing accountability, expanding healthcare centers in underserved regions, incorporating violence management into nursing curricula, and supporting action research.

Interventions during violent incidents

During violent events, only a limited number of interventions were reported. At the staff level, these included de-escalation through active listening and calming techniques, as well as immediate mobilization of security personnel. At the organizational level, the establishment of efficient incident-reporting systems was highlighted. Political-level interventions focused on ensuring financial and psychological support to affected staff.

Interventions after violent incidents

Post-incident interventions were sparse. Reported strategies included mandatory reporting of violent events, organizational follow-up on submitted reports, and political measures such as providing psychological and financial support to staff.

Discussion

This scoping review explored the causes of violence against nurses in developing countries and interventions implemented to address this challenge. Our findings highlight that violence against nurses arises from a complex interplay of individual, organizational, social, and political factors, and that interventions must be multifaceted, spanning prevention, immediate response, and post-incident support. At the individual level, 28 individual factors associated with healthcare staff, such as emotional stress, burnout, inadequate training on violence prevention, and inadequate communication skills. These factors not only increase staff vulnerability but may also unintentionally escalate conflicts. Enhancing staff support systems, continuous professional development, and fostering a positive organizational culture are therefore critical components in mitigating workplace violence. Consistent with these findings, McGregor et al. concluded that healthcare staff are especially vulnerable to violence due to stress, burnout, inadequate training, and ineffective communication skills [91]. However, managers often view workplace violence as “part of the job” and face challenges in allocating resources, especially for staff working alone or making home visits [92]. WPV incidents continue to increase in severity and frequency, necessitating a multi-stakeholder approach to address this complex issue [93].

Organizational factors represented the largest category, with 33 identified causes, underscoring systemic issues such as staffing shortages, inadequate security, overcrowded settings, and poor infrastructure. These conditions contribute to a high-pressure environment that increases the likelihood of violent incidents. Tackling these organizational challenges requires a strong institutional commitment to resource allocation, environmental improvements, and the enforcement of effective policies. Social causes, though fewer, reveal the broader societal context influencing workplace violence. Negative media portrayals and public misconceptions about nursing roles can erode trust and respect, while societal violence and easy access to weapons contribute to a general climate of aggression. Political factors such as inadequate legislation, lack of protective policies, and poor governmental support further compound these challenges, indicating the need for systemic reforms at the policy level. In this regard, Stathopoulou pointed out that violence and aggression in healthcare settings, particularly emergency departments and psychiatric units, are significant concerns. Organizational factors such as overcrowding, long waiting times, and inadequate staffing contribute to increased risk of violent incidents [94]. Overall, these findings emphasize that tackling violence against nurses in developing countries demands a holistic approach encompassing individual education and support, organizational improvements, societal awareness, and robust political action.

Violence in developing and developed countries stems from distinct socioeconomic, political, and institutional factors, with poverty and inequality playing a more pronounced role in developing nations. The evidence reveals several key differences: F. Stewart et al., found that developing countries experience violence primarily due to political, social, and economic inequalities between groups, with extreme poverty and economic stagnation as critical drivers. Fox et al. [95] further demonstrated that countries with “hybrid” political orders experience higher social violence rates, particularly in weakly institutionalized democracies. In contrast, developed countries show different violence patterns. Silva et al. [96] noted that mass shootings in developed countries are more likely to involve ideological motives, fame-seeking, and workplace/relationship problems. The comparative evidence suggests complex, context-specific violence mechanisms that defy simple categorization.

The findings highlight the multifaceted nature of interventions needed to effectively reduce violence against nurses in developing countries. Organizing these interventions into pre-violence, during-violence, and post-violence phases provides a comprehensive framework for both prevention and response, capturing the complex dynamics of workplace violence in healthcare settings. The predominance of interventions in the pre-violence phase underscores the critical importance of proactive strategies. These efforts are essential to equip nurses and other healthcare workers with the skills and support needed to prevent escalation and ensure a safer work environment. Among staff related strategies, raising nurses’ salaries and offering professional development opportunities can help reduce workplace violence. Fair pay and career advancement boost job satisfaction and morale, enhance nurses’ skills and authority, and improve staff retention, all of which help lower stress and conflict in healthcare settings. Furthermore, these investments signal organizational support, which can deter aggressive behavior from patients and visitors. Recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses emphasize the importance of comprehensive interventions to address workplace violence against nurses. Educational programs and communication skills training have emerged as key strategies for both prevention and management [87, 97]. While standalone training can yield positive outcomes, multicomponent interventions that incorporate organizational changes and stakeholder engagement are significantly more effective [9]. These may involve establishing reporting systems, improving staffing levels, and fostering a culture of collaboration and mutual respect. The framework for developing violence prevention programs should be built on scientific evidence, regulatory guidance, and locally generated practice evidence [98].

Organizational interventions during this phase such as infrastructure improvements, enhanced security measures, workflow optimization, and the implementation of incident reporting systems highlight the importance of institutional commitment in creating a safe and supportive work environment. Social interventions that increase public awareness of emergency services and the role of nurses also help shape positive perceptions and reduce societal drivers of violence. Political interventions, including policy development, institutional accountability, and the integration of violence management into nursing education, offer a foundational framework for achieving lasting, systemic change. Anderson et al. indicated that workplace violence against nurses, particularly in emergency departments, is a prevalent issue affecting job satisfaction and patient care quality [12]. While many nurses experience assaults, reporting rates remain low despite educational interventions [99]. Effective interventions to address this problem include environmental changes, policy implementation, and skills training [12]. Multicomponent interventions involving organizational changes and comprehensive training programs have shown the most promise in combating workplace violence [9].

In contrast, the relatively limited number of interventions identified during violent incidents highlights the inherent challenges of managing violence in real time. Staff-centered actions such as de-escalation through effective communication and the timely involvement of security personnel are essential for immediate incident control. Organizational and political responses, including the establishment of efficient reporting systems and the provision of financial and psychological support for affected staff, play a critical role in mitigating the impact of violence and supporting recovery. Workplace violence in emergency departments (EDs) is a significant concern, with nurses particularly vulnerable due to their frontline position [9]. While numerous interventions have been proposed to address this issue, evidence of their effectiveness remains limited [100]. Recent studies suggest that multicomponent interventions, which combine staff training with organizational changes, may be more effective than standalone training programs [9, 101]. Future studies should focus on implementing and assessing comprehensive strategies that address both immediate incident management and long-term prevention of workplace violence in EDs [100, 101].

Post-violence interventions highlight the importance of a comprehensive response to incidents, including robust reporting mechanisms, organizational follow-up, offender accountability, and ongoing psychological and financial support for staff. The relatively small number of interventions in this phase suggests potential gaps in post-incident care and institutional learning both of which are essential for preventing future occurrences. Post-violence interventions in organizations are crucial for staff well-being and prevention of future incidents. Thomas et al. [102] highlighted the importance of comprehensive reporting mechanisms, organizational follow-up, and support systems. Barriers to effective follow-up include inefficient reporting processes and lack of clear organizational action, while enablers involve hospital initiatives supporting staff well-being. Early interventions are most effective when tailored to organizational culture, supported by management, and leveraging existing peer support systems [103]. However, the relatively limited research on post-incident violence and institutional learning points to significant gaps in this area, highlighting the need for greater investment in innovative programs and high-quality evaluations to inform and strengthen efforts to prevent violence against healthcare staff.

Overall, this phased and multi-level approach aligns with best practices in workplace violence management and underscores that reducing violence against nurses requires coordinated efforts across individual, organizational, social, and political domains. The concentration of interventions before violence highlights the emphasis on prevention, yet effective management during and after incidents remains essential. Future research should evaluate the effectiveness and feasibility of specific interventions in diverse contexts to inform tailored strategies that address the unique challenges faced by healthcare systems in developing countries.

This scoping review has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the review protocol was not registered in a systematic or scoping review registry. Second, the CINAHL database could not be searched due to access limitations. Third, although the inclusion of studies with diverse methodologies and contextual backgrounds enriched the breadth of the evidence, it also posed challenges for synthesizing findings into a coherent and uniform framework. Fourth, many of the included studies provided limited detail regarding the implementation processes or outcomes of the interventions, restricting the ability to assess their practical effectiveness. Finally, as this review focused exclusively on developing countries, the findings may not be generalizable to high-income settings with different healthcare infrastructures, workforce dynamics, and sociopolitical contexts. Future research should employ more rigorous evaluation designs, examine the real-world effectiveness of interventions, and draw on a broader range of data sources to improve the robustness and applicability of the evidence base.

Conclusion

The results show that violence against nurses in developing countries is a pervasive and complex challenge rooted in individual, organizational, social, and political factors. While most interventions to date focus on prevention, strategies to support nurses during and after violent incidents remain underdeveloped. This imbalance limits the capacity of health systems to protect their frontline workforce. A sustainable response requires a comprehensive, multi-phase approach that integrates preventive training, real-time crisis management, and robust post-incident support. Governments, healthcare organizations, and community stakeholders must collaborate to establish protective legislation, improve working conditions, and foster public respect for the nursing profession. Future research should prioritize evaluating interventions during and after violence, assessing long-term impacts, and exploring the role of leadership and organizational culture. Addressing these gaps will be essential for creating safer work environments, reducing nurse turnover, and ultimately strengthening healthcare delivery in developing countries. By adopting holistic, context-specific strategies, healthcare systems can move beyond piecemeal solutions and work toward ensuring that nurses as the backbone of healthcare can practice safely and effectively.
