Lower Bounds on Inverse Cellular Automata via Proof Complexity
Maryia Kapytka

Abstract
We study the complexity of inverse cellular automata on configurations of bounded size. Deciding injectivity in this setting is co-NP-complete by a theorem of Durand [ 6 ] . We give a simpler proof of this theorem by a direct reduction from UNSAT \mathrm{UNSAT} to this problem, avoiding more complicated intermediate constructions. We also show that one direction of the reduction can be formalized in the weak theory of bounded arithmetic V 0 V^{0} . Durand’s coNP-completeness result allows one to view inverse cellular automata acting on bounded size configurations as propositional proofs, cf. Cavagnetto [ 2 , 3 ] , and we prove lower bounds on their size. The proof uses known lower bounds for bounded-depth Frege systems together with the Paris–Wilkie translation of arithmetic proofs into propositional proofs, which allows us to transfer proof complexity lower bounds to our setting.



1
Introduction

Cellular automata are simple models of systems that change in steps, widely used in computer science and physics. They capture basic features of nature like local interactions, uniform rules, and parallel updates. A key concept is the Garden of Eden, a configuration that cannot arise from any previous state. The Garden of Eden theorem characterizes when such configurations exist. A cellular automaton is surjective if and only if every configuration has a predecessor; that is, if and only if it has no Garden of Eden configuration. This is further linked to local injectivity: two finite patterns are called twins if they can be swapped anywhere without affecting future configurations; the automaton is locally injective if it has no twins. The theorem states that surjectivity and local injectivity are equivalent. In two dimensions, Kari
[
7
]
showed that checking injectivity or surjectivity is undecidable, meaning no algorithm can solve it in general.

However, the situation changes when we restrict attention to bounded configurations. In this setting, the problem becomes decidable but remains computationally difficult. In particular, Durand
[
6
]
proved that deciding injectivity of two-dimensional cellular automata on bounded configurations is coNP-complete, implying that, unless
\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}
, this problem is computationally hard. His proof uses a complex multi-step reduction from the nondeterministic halting problem via tiling systems.

In this paper, we give a more direct reduction. More precisely, we introduce a polynomial reduction of the coNP-complete set
\mathrm{UNSAT}
(the set of unsatisfiable CNF formulas) to the set of injective two-dimensional cellular automata with von Neumann neighbourhood on configurations of bounded size. Moreover, we show that one direction of this reduction (namely, that if the formula is satisfiable, then the cellular automaton is not injective) can be formalized in the theory
V^{0}
, a weak theory of bounded arithmetic. Since
V^{0}
is extremely weak, formalizing such a complexity-theoretic result within it is not straightforward. While several unprovability results are known for
V^{0}
, few non-trivial theorems of this kind have been shown to be provable inside it.

Durand
[
6
, Sec. 4]
posed the question of whether the inverse automaton to a cellular automaton operating on bounded configurations, whose transition function is computed by a simple algorithm, is also computed by a simple algorithm. Cavagnetto
[
2
, Sec. 3.3]
showed, using the computational complexity hypothesis that a one-way function exists, that this is not the case if by an algorithm one means a Boolean circuit. In addition, he showed that Durand’s coNP-completeness results still hold in such a setup. The question of the size of inverse cellular automata for the original representation of the transition function by its table was left open in both
[
6
,
2
]
. We shall use proof complexity to solve this problem.

The motivation for our approach comes from the PhD thesis of Stefano Cavagnetto
[
2
]
, which was later published in
[
3
]
. In that work, inverse cellular automata were viewed as propositional proofs in the sense of Cook–Reckhow
[
5
]
. We follow this idea.

We show that, when we restrict ourselves to configurations of bounded size, cellular automata in our construction have the property that inverse automata to them cannot be in general of subexponential size. Indeed, their size is exponential, and, in particular, their neighborhood must grow polynomially.

To prove this, we consider the unsatisfiable CNF formula

\varphi=\lnot\mathrm{ontoPHP}_{k}
, which expresses that there is a bijection between
k+1
and
k
. This formula has size polynomial in
k
but requires
exponential-size bounded-depth Frege refutations by known strengthenings of a theorem of Ajtai
[
1
]
. Then we use the Paris–Wilkie translation
[
9
]
to simulate our inverse cellular automaton proof system by a bounded-depth Frege system
F_{d}
, thereby transferring this lower bound to our setting.

For background on cellular automata, see for example
[
7
]
, and for background on bounded arithmetic and proof complexity, see
[
8
]
.

2.1
Cellular automata

We briefly recall the basic notions of cellular automata that will be needed later.
The definitions are standard and follow
[
6
]
, but we include them here
to fix notation and terminology. We also record a simple bound on the size
needed to encode a two-dimensional cellular automaton with a fixed neighbourhood.

Definition 2.1

(Cellular automaton)
.

A cellular automaton is formally defined as a quadruplet

(d,S,N,f)
:

•

The integer

is the

•

•

The

is a

•

The

A

.
For a configuration

the value

is called the

.
We refer to the position

.

If

is a configuration, we identify

with its graph

\{\,((i,j),C(i,j))\mid(i,j)\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\,\}.

All automata considered in this paper are two-dimensional, i.e.

.

Sometimes, a state

for which

is distinguished
in

and is called

quiescent
state. A

is a configuration which has only a finite number of cells in a non-quiescent state. If there exist two integers

and

such that all non-quiescent cells of the

, then we say that the

is
inside
[i]\times[j]

Lemma 2.2
.

(
[
6
]
)
For fixed

(of size

) the size of a binary string sufficient to code a cellular automaton of dimension

is bounded above by

, where

is the size of

.

Proof.

We have

and a fixed neighbourhood N
(lexicographically ordered), so the local function can be represented by a table that has

rows. Any value of the function is encoded by

∎

2.2
Propositional proof complexity

Propositional proof complexity studies the length and structure of proofs of propositional tautologies in different proof systems. In this subsection we recall the notion of
a propositional proof system and introduce Frege and bounded-depth Frege systems. These concepts will later be linked to bounded arithmetic via the Paris–Wilkie translation.

The language we use in this subsection is the standard DeMorgan propositional language, consisting of propositional variables
x,y,z,\dots
, the logical constants
\top
and
\bot
, the connectives
\neg
,
\wedge
, and
\vee
, and parentheses.

2.2.1
Proof systems

Let
\TAUT
denote the set of propositional tautologies and let
\SAT
denote the set of satisfiable propositional formulas. We identify formulas with binary strings that encode them.

Definition 2.3

(Propositional proof system
[
5
, Definition 1.3]
)
.

A
relational propositional proof system
is a binary relation

Q\subseteq\{0,1\}^{*}\times\{0,1\}^{*}
, where
\{0,1\}^{*}
denotes the set of all finite binary strings, such that:

•

Q
is p-time decidable;

•

for any
w,\alpha
, if
Q(w,\alpha)
holds then
\alpha\in\mathrm{TAUT}
;

•

for any
\alpha\in\mathrm{TAUT}
, there is a
w\in\{0,1\}^{*}
such that
Q(w,\alpha)
holds.

The second condition gives the soundness of
Q
and the third its completeness.

We will often shorten the terminology
propositional proof system
to
proof system
.

A
refutation
of a formula
\varphi
in
Q
is a proof

w\in\{0,1\}^{*}
such that

Q(w,\neg\varphi)

holds.
Equivalently, a refutation of
\varphi
is a proof of the tautology

\neg\varphi
.

The size of a proof
w
(respectively, a formula
\varphi
) is the number of symbols it contains.

To compare the strength of different propositional proof systems, we use the notion
of polynomial simulation.

Definition 2.4

(p-simulation)
.

Let
P
and
Q
be two propositional proof systems.
A p-time function
f:\{0,1\}^{*}\to\{0,1\}^{*}
is a
p-simulation
of
Q
by
P
if and only if for all strings
w,\alpha
,

Q(w,\alpha)\;\rightarrow\;P(f(w,\alpha),\alpha).

If such
f
exists, we say that
P

p-simulates

Q
.

We now recall the definition of a Frege proof system, a standard example of a propositional proof system.

A formula
B
is
logically implied
by a formula
A
, written
A\models B
, if every assignment that makes
A
true also makes
B
true.
If
A=A_{1}\wedge A_{2}\wedge\dots\wedge A_{k}
with
k\geq 2
, we write
A_{1},A_{2},\dots,A_{k}\models B
instead of
A_{1}\wedge A_{2}\wedge\dots\wedge A_{k}\models B
. If
k=0
, we write
\models B
and this means that
B
is a tautology.

Let
k\geq 0
.
A
k
-ary Frege rule
is a
(k+1)
-tuple of formulas
(A_{0},A_{1},\dots,A_{k})
, written

\frac{A_{1},\dots,A_{k}}{A_{0}},

such that

A_{1},\dots,A_{k}\models A_{0}.

A
0
-ary Frege rule is called
an axiom
.

A standard example of a Frege rule is
modus ponens
:

\frac{p,\;\neg p\vee q}{q}.

Let
F
be a finite set of Frege rules.
An
F
-proof
of a formula
C
from formulas
B_{1},\dots,B_{\ell}
is a finite
sequence
(D_{1},\dots,D_{m})
such that:

1.

D_{m}=C
;

2.

for each
i\leq m
, either

(a)

D_{i}=\sigma(A_{0})
for some rule

\frac{A_{1},\dots,A_{k}}{A_{0}}\in F
, indices
j_{1},\dots,j_{k}<i
, and substitution
\sigma
with

\sigma(A_{r})=D_{j_{r}}
for all
r\leq k
, or

(b)

D_{i}\in\{B_{1},\dots,B_{\ell}\}
.

We write

B_{1},\dots,B_{l}\vdash_{F}C

if there exists an
F
-proof of
C
from
B_{1},\dots,B_{l}
. When
F
is clear from the
context, we omit the subscript.

A
Frege proof system
is a finite set
F
of Frege rules that is

sound
and
(implicationally) complete
, meaning that for all formulas

B_{1},\dots,B_{\ell},C
,

B_{1},\dots,B_{\ell}\vdash_{F}C\;\Rightarrow\;B_{1},\dots,B_{\ell}\models C\quad\text{(soundness)}

and

B_{1},\dots,B_{\ell}\models C\;\Rightarrow\;B_{1},\dots,B_{\ell}\vdash_{F}C\quad\text{(completeness)}.

A key measure of the logical complexity of propositional formulas is their depth.
Bounded-depth Frege systems are defined by requiring that proofs use only formulas of a depth bounded by an independent constant. We therefore begin by defining the depth of a formula.

Definition 2.5

(Depth of a formula)
.

The notion of the
depth
of
A
, denoted by
\operatorname{dp}(A)
, is defined by the following inductive definition:

•

propositional variables and the constants have depth
0
;

•

\operatorname{dp}(\lnot B)=\operatorname{dp}(B)
if
B
starts with
\lnot
and
\operatorname{dp}(\lnot B)=1+\operatorname{dp}(B)
otherwise;

•

if
B(x_{1},\ldots,x_{t})
is built from propositional variables and disjunctions only and none of the formulas

C_{1},\ldots,C_{t}
starts with a disjunction, then

\operatorname{dp}\!\bigl(B(C_{1},\ldots,C_{t})\bigr)=1+\max_{i}\operatorname{dp}(C_{i});

•

a condition holds for conjunction analogous to the previous item.

For a Frege system
F
and
d\geq 0
we write
F_{d}
for the restriction of
F
in which proofs are allowed to use only formulas of depth at most
d
. Frege systems of this form are called
bounded-depth
(or
constant-depth
) Frege systems. Note that these systems are not complete for all tautologies (as these have unbounded depth), but it is well-known that they are complete for formulas of a bounded depth.

We now give a basic and well-known example from propositional proof complexity.
The pigeonhole principle is a standard source of hard formulas and is often used
to show lower bounds for proof systems, in particular for bounded-depth Frege systems.

Example 2.6

(Pigeonhole principle)
.

Consider the following CNF formula
\lnot onto\mathrm{PHP}_{n}
, consisting of clauses
over propositional variables
p_{ij}
, where
i~\in~[n+1]
and
j~\in~[n]
:

1.

\bigvee_{j}p_{ij}
, one clause for each
i
;

2.

\lnot p_{i_{1}j}\lor\lnot p_{i_{2}j}
, one clause for each triple

i_{1}<i_{2}
and
j
;

3.

\lnot p_{ij_{1}}\lor\lnot p_{ij_{2}}
, one clause for each triple

i
and
j_{1}<j_{2}
;

4.

\bigvee_{i}p_{ij}
, one clause for each
j
.

The variable
p_{ij}
is intended to express that pigeon
i
is assigned to hole
j
.
The clauses in (1) and (4) assert that every pigeon is assigned to some hole and that
every hole contains at least one pigeon, respectively. Clauses in (2) and (3) enforce
that no two pigeons are assigned to the same hole and that no pigeon is assigned to
more than one hole.

Clearly,
\neg onto\operatorname{PHP}_{n}
is unsatisfiable, since the number of pigeons exceeds the number of holes. In fact, the formula remains unsatisfiable even if only clauses
(1) and (2) are retained. This weaker principle is known as the
weak pigeonhole
principle
, denoted
\lnot\mathrm{PHP}_{n}
.

We introduce this example because of the following well-known result due to Ajtai,
which establishes super-polynomial lower bounds for bounded-depth Frege proofs.

Theorem 2.7

(Ajtai’s theorem)
.

[
1
]

Let
d\geq 2
and let
0<\delta<5^{-d}
. Then for all sufficiently large
n
the following holds.

Every
F_{d}
-refutation of
\lnot\mathrm{ontoPHP}_{n}
(that is, every
F_{d}
-proof of
\bot
from

\lnot\mathrm{ontoPHP}_{n}
) contains at least
2^{n^{\delta}}
pairwise different subformulas.

In particular, any such refutation has size at least
2^{n^{\delta}}
.

2.3
Bounded arithmetic

2.3.1
I\Delta_{0}
and
I\Delta_{0}(R)

Throughout this subsection we work in the language of Peano arithmetic

L_{PA}=\{0,1,+,\cdot,\leq\}.

A
bounded quantifier
is a quantifier of the form

\exists y\leq t(\mathbf{x})\,A(\mathbf{x},y)\quad\text{or}\quad\forall y\leq t(\mathbf{x})\,A(\mathbf{x},y),

where
t(\mathbf{x})
is an
L_{PA}
-term. It is an abbreviation for the formulas

\exists y\,(y\leq t(\mathbf{x})\wedge A(\mathbf{x},y))\quad\text{and}\quad\forall y\,(y\leq t(\mathbf{x})\to A(\mathbf{x},y)),

respectively.

The class of
bounded formulas

\Delta_{0}
is then defined as the smallest class of

L_{PA}
-formulas that contains all quantifier-free formulas and is closed under
DeMorgan connectives and bounded quantifiers.

Definition 2.8

(
I\Delta_{0}
)
.

The theory
I\Delta_{0}
is the theory in
L_{PA}
axiomatized by Robinson’s arithmetic
Q
:

\begin{array}[]{lll}\mathbf{B1}.&x+1\neq 0\\[2.0pt]
\mathbf{B2}.&x+1=y+1\rightarrow x=y\\[2.0pt]
\mathbf{B3}.&x+0=x\\[2.0pt]
\mathbf{B4}.&x+(y+1)=(x+y)+1\\[2.0pt]
\mathbf{B5}.&x\cdot 0=0\\[2.0pt]
\mathbf{B6}.&x\cdot(y+1)=(x\cdot y)+x\\[6.0pt]
\end{array}\qquad\begin{array}[]{lll}\mathbf{B7}.&(x\leq y\land y\leq x)\rightarrow x=y\\[2.0pt]
\mathbf{B8}.&x\leq x+y\\[2.0pt]
\mathbf{B9}.&0\leq x\\[2.0pt]
\mathbf{B10}.&x\leq y\;\lor\;y\leq x\\[2.0pt]
\mathbf{B11}.&x\leq y\;\leftrightarrow\;x<y+1\\[2.0pt]
\mathbf{B12}.&x\neq 0\;\rightarrow\;\exists y\leq x\,(y+1=x)\\[6.0pt]
\end{array}

and by the axiom scheme of induction
IND
, where
A
is a
\Delta_{0}
-formula:

\neg A(0)\vee(\exists y<x\wedge A(y)\wedge\neg A(y+1))\vee A(x).

(We implicitly assume that all free variables are universally quantiﬁed.)

Extend the language
L_{PA}
by a new binary relation symbol
R(x,y)
, and let

\Delta_{0}(R)
be the class of bounded formulas in the extended language.
Define
I\Delta_{0}(R)
as Robinson’s arithmetic
Q
together with the induction
scheme
IND
restricted to
\Delta_{0}(R)
-formulas.

In our application we will need to work with several additional relation symbols and constants.
Let
L_{PA}(\alpha)
denote the language obtained from
L_{PA}
by adding an arbitrary countable collection of new relation symbols and constants, where
\alpha
serves as a placeholder for this extension. We define the theory
I\Delta_{0}(\alpha)
to consist of Robinson’s arithmetic
Q
together with the induction scheme
IND
for all
\Delta_{0}(\alpha)
-formulas.

2.3.2
V^{0}

The following subsection is based on
[
4
, Section 5.1]
. We introduce theory
V^{0}
because it provides a convenient framework for formalizing arguments about the existence of sets. An important property of

is that it is conservative over
I\Delta_{0}(\alpha)
. This means that every sentence in the language
\Delta_{0}(\alpha)
that can be proved in
V^{0}
can already be proved in
I\Delta_{0}(\alpha)
.

This conservativity allows us to carry out certain arguments within
V^{0}
, and then transfer the resulting statements back to
I\Delta_{0}(\alpha)
. This will make several proofs in later sections simpler.

Two-sorted first-order logic is an extension of the usual single-sorted first-order
logic. Here there are two kinds of variables:

•

Number variables

x,y,z,\dots
range over natural numbers.

•

Set variables

X,Y,Z,\dots
range over finite subsets of natural numbers.

The language of
V^{0}
is

\mathcal{L}^{2}_{A}=\{\,0,1,+,\cdot,\mid\mid\ ;\ =_{1},=_{2},\leq,\in\,\}.

Here:

•

0,1,+,\cdot,=_{1}
come from
\mathcal{L}_{PA}
;
=_{1}
corresponds to
=
of
\mathcal{L}_{PA}

•

\lvert X\rvert
(the “length of
X
”) is a number-valued function and is intended to denote
the least upper bound of the set
X
(essentially the largest element plus one)

•

\in
is the usual membership predicate

•

=_{2}
is the equality for sets

We will write
=
for both
=_{1}
and
=_{2}
, the meaning
will be clear from the context.

We will use the abbreviation

X(t)
to denote
t\in X
. Thus we may think of
X(i)
as the

i
-th bit of the binary string
X
.

The theory
2
-BASIC extends basic arithmetic to sets.

Definition 2.9

(
2
-BASIC)
.

The theory
2
-BASIC consists of the axioms
\mathbf{B1\text{--}B12}
from
Definition
2.8
, together with the following axioms
for bounded sets:

\begin{array}[]{lll}\mathbf{L1}.&X(y)\;\rightarrow\;y<|X|,\\[4.0pt]
\mathbf{L2}.&y+1=|X|\;\rightarrow\;X(y),\\[4.0pt]
\mathbf{SE}.&\Bigl(\,|X|=|Y|\;\land\;\forall i<|X|\bigl(X(i)\leftrightarrow Y(i)\bigr)\Bigr)\;\rightarrow\;X=Y.\end{array}

These axioms formalize basic properties of finite sets: the elements of a set are within its length, the last element determines the size of the string the set represents, and sets are equal if they have the same length and elements.

Definition 2.10

(
\Sigma_{0}^{B}
-formulas)
.

\Sigma_{0}^{B}
is the set of
\mathcal{L}_{A}^{2}
-formulas whose
only quantifiers are bounded number quantifiers (there can be free string variables).

Similarly as bounded (number) quantifiers,

are quantifiers of the form

\exists X\leq t(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{Y})\,A(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{Y},X)\quad\text{and}\quad\forall X\leq t(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{Y})\,A(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{Y},X),

where

is an

-term. It is an abbreviation for the formulas

\exists X\,\bigl(|X|\leq t(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{Y})\wedge A(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{Y},X)\bigr)\quad\text{and}\quad\forall X\,\bigl(|X|\leq t(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{Y})\to A(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{Y},X)\bigr),

.

The bounded comprehension axiom scheme for

(

)

2.3.3
Coding of finite relations and functions

Note that
V^{0}
enables us to talk about finite subsets of natural numbers. In order to be able to discuss finite relations and bounded functions, we introduce their coding in
V^{0}
.

In order to define finite subsets of
\mathbb{N}^{k}
for
k\geq 2
, we use the usual
pairing function

\langle x,y\rangle\;:=\;\frac{(x+y)(x+y+1)}{2}+x,

which is a bijection between
\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}
and
\mathbb{N}
, with the
projections simply definable with a bounded graph:

p_{1}(z)=x\quad\text{if and only if}\quad\exists y\leq z\;\langle x,y\rangle=z,

and similarly for
p_{2}(z)=y
. Iterating this, we can encode
k
-tuples of numbers
for any fixed
k\geq 2
. Using this coding, a finite relation
R\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{k}
is represented as a
finite set of natural numbers coding the tuples in
R
.

Using the pairing function we can also code finite sequences of natural
numbers. A finite sequence

(a_{0},a_{1},\dots,a_{k-1})

is represented by the finite set

S=\{\langle 0,a_{0}\rangle,\langle 1,a_{1}\rangle,\dots,\langle k-1,a_{k-1}\rangle\}.

Thus finite sequences can be treated as finite relations

S\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{2}
that satisfy the usual uniqueness condition

\forall i\,\forall y_{1},y_{2}\;\bigl((\langle i,y_{1}\rangle\in S\wedge\langle i,y_{2}\rangle\in S)\rightarrow y_{1}=y_{2}\bigr).

Functions are represented by their graphs.
The
graph of a function
G
is a finite relation

G\subseteq M\times N
, where
M\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{k}
and

N\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{l}
are finite sets for some

\displaystyle\text{(Totality)}

2.4
Propositional translation of bounded arithmetic

In this subsection we explain how statements of bounded arithmetic are translated into
propositional formulas and how this translation connects provability in
V^{0}
with
propositional proof complexity. First, we recall the Paris–Wilkie translation, which
associates to each bounded arithmetic formula a propositional
formula of polynomial size and bounded depth.

2.4.1
Paris-Wilkie translation

The translation applies to all bounded formulas
\Sigma^{B}_{0}
with any number of free set variables. To keep the notation simple we shall consider just the example when the formula contains an unspecified binary relation
R
as that is the case needed for the translation of the pigeonhole principle.

The Paris–Wilkie translation
[
8
, Section 8.2]
assigns to any
\Delta_{0}(R)
-formula

A(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
and any
n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}\geq 0
a DeMorgan propositional formula

\langle A(\mathbf{x})\rangle_{n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}}

defined by induction on the logical complexity of
A
as follows.

1.

If
B
is one of the atomic formulas
t(\mathbf{x})=s(\mathbf{x})
or
t(\mathbf{x})\leq s(\mathbf{x})
, with
t
and
s
terms, then

\langle B\rangle_{n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}}:=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }B(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})\text{ is true,}\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

2.

If
B
is the atomic formula
R(t(\mathbf{x}),s(\mathbf{x}))
and
i
and
j
are the values of the terms
t(\mathbf{x})
and
s(\mathbf{x})
for
\mathbf{x}:=\mathbf{n}
, respectively, then put

\langle B\rangle_{n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}}:=r_{ij},

where
r_{ij}
are propositional variables.

3.

\langle\ldots\rangle
commutes with
\neg,\lor,\land.

4.

If
A(\mathbf{x})=\exists y\leq t(\mathbf{x})B(\mathbf{x},y)
then

\langle A(\mathbf{x})\rangle_{n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}}:=\bigvee_{m\leq t(\mathbf{n})}\langle B(\mathbf{x},y)\rangle_{n_{1},\ldots,n_{k},m}.

5.

If
A(\mathbf{x})=\forall y\leq t(\mathbf{x})B(\mathbf{x},y)
then

\langle A(\mathbf{x})\rangle_{n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}}:=\bigwedge_{m\leq t(\mathbf{n})}\langle B(\mathbf{x},y)\rangle_{n_{1},\ldots,n_{k},m}.

Lemma 2.14
.

Let
A(\mathbf{x})
be a
\Delta_{0}(R)
-formula. Then there are

such that, for all

•

|\langle A(\mathbf{x})\rangle_{n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}}|\leq(n_{1}+\cdots+n_{k}+2)^{c},

•

\mathrm{dp}(\langle A(\mathbf{x})\rangle_{n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}})\leq d.

Moreover,
A(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})
is true for all interpretations of
R
if and only if

\langle A(\mathbf{x})\rangle_{n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}}\in\mathrm{TAUT}.

The key result about the Paris–Wilkie translation is that translations
\langle A(\mathbf{x})\rangle_{n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}}

of bounded formulas
A(\mathbf{x})
whose universal closure is provable in
I\Delta_{0}(\alpha)
have constant depth and poly-size Frege proofs. We shall formulate the theorem for
V^{0}
; this follows from Paris-Wilkie theorem
[
9
]

using the fact that
V^{0}
is conservative over
I\Delta_{0}(\alpha)
.

Theorem 2.15
.

Let
F
be a Frege system in the DeMorgan language. Let
A(\mathbf{x})
be a
\Sigma^{B}_{0}
-formula and assume that
V^{0}
proves
\forall\mathbf{x}\,A(\mathbf{x})
. Then there are
c,d\geq 1
such that for each

there is an

-proof

of

such that

\mathrm{dp}(\pi_{n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}})\leq d\quad\text{and}\quad|\pi_{n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}}|\leq(n_{1}+\cdots+n_{k}+2)^{c}.

2.4.2
Coding of CNFs

Assume
\varphi
is a CNF formula with
n
clauses
C_{1},C_{2},\dots,C_{n}
and
m
propositional variables
x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{m}
. We encode it as a ternary relation
R_{\varphi}\subseteq\{1,2,\dots,n\}\times\{1,2,\dots,m\}\times\{-1,0,1\}
defined as follows:

\displaystyle(i,j,1)\in R_{\varphi}

\displaystyle\;\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\;x_{j}\in C_{i},

\displaystyle(i,j,-1)\in R_{\varphi}

\displaystyle\;\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\;\neg x_{j}\in C_{i},

\displaystyle(i,j,0)\in R_{\varphi}

\displaystyle\;\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\;x_{j}\notin C_{i}\ \wedge\ \neg x_{j}\notin C_{i}.

We will abuse the notation and use
\varphi
to denote both the formula
\varphi
and the corresponding relational encoding
R_{\varphi}
.

We say that a ternary relation
R\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{3}

encodes a CNF formula with
n
clauses and
m
propositional variables

if the predicate
\mathrm{CNF}(R,n,m)
holds, where

\displaystyle\mathrm{CNF}(n,m,R)\;:=\;

\displaystyle\Bigl(R\subseteq\{1,2,\dots,n\}\times\{1,2,\dots,m\}\times\{-1,0,1\}

\displaystyle\;\wedge\;R\text{ is a graph of a function }

\displaystyle\{1,2,\dots,n\}\times\{1,2,\dots,m\}\rightarrow\{-1,0,1\}\Bigr).

An assignment
\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{m})\in\{0,1\}^{m}

satisfies

\varphi
, iff
\mathrm{Sat}(n,m,\mathbf{a},\varphi)
holds, where

\displaystyle\mathrm{Sat}(n,m,\mathbf{a},\varphi)\;:=

\displaystyle\;\forall i\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}\;\exists j\in\{1,2,\dots,m\}\;\exists s\in\{-1,1\}\;

\displaystyle\bigl(R_{\varphi}(i,j,s)\wedge\big[(s=1\wedge a_{j}=1)\;\vee\;(s=-1\wedge a_{j}=0)\bigr]\bigr).

Moreover, in Subsection
2.4.2
we already introduced predicates:

•

a
\Delta_{0}(\varphi)

formalizing that

Theorem 2.16

(From Reflection to Simulation
[
8
, Section 8.4]
)
.

Let
Q
be an arbitrary proof system and let
\mathrm{Refut}_{Q}
be an arbitrary

NP

If
V^{0}
proves

then there exists

such that

p-simulates

with respect to refutations of CNF formulas.

This theorem is the key to our proof of the lower bound in Theorem
7.4
and thus,
for the benefit of the reader, we shall explain why it holds.

Assume the hypothesis of the theorem. By Theorem
2.15
,
the propositional translations of

\CNF\wedge\mathrm{Refut}_{Q}^{0}\;\rightarrow\;\neg\mathrm{Sat}

have polynomial-size proofs in
F_{d}
, for some fixed constant
d
.

Now fix a concrete CNF formula
\varphi
.
Suppose
\pi
is a

-refutation of
\varphi
,
with witnesses

showing that

From this data we obtain an

with parameters determined by
\varphi

\pi

G_{i}
.

In this refutation we substitute concrete bits:
for propositional variables describing
\varphi

\pi
, and

we plug in the bits coding these objects.
After this substitution, the propositional translation of

Any true propositional sentence has a short proof,
because we can simply evaluate it.
Using modus ponens, we then obtain a proof of the formula

This formula contains only the propositional variables corresponding to the unknown bits
of the assignment
\mathbf{a}

(the propositional variables describing
\varphi

From this we derive

\neg\varphi(\mathbf{p}).

The whole construction can be carried out by a polynomial-time algorithm.
Therefore we obtain a p-simulation.

The full technical details are in

4
From CNF to cellular automaton

We now turn the computation tables introduced in the previous section into objects
that can be checked locally by a cellular automaton.

We define a cellular automaton
A_{\varphi}
that will operate inside
[n+1]\times[m+2]
whose purpose is to verify whether a given configuration represents a correct computation of the truth value of
\varphi
for some assignment
\mathbf{a}
. These configurations are intended to encode computation tables
for
\varphi
as defined in the previous section. However, the cells will contain not only the information used for the verification of the correctness but also an additional bit called
label
.

The idea is that
A_{\varphi}
performs a local verification of the correctness. It inspects the neighbourhood of each cell and checks whether the local configuration is consistent with a correct computation. If yes, the automaton updates the label component of the cell’s state according to the labels of its neighbors. The consistency requirements are formalized by a collection of
local correctness rules
, which will be specified later in this section. On each cell
A_{\varphi}
will possibly modify only the label component of the cell state, leaving all other symbols unchanged.

In order to define the cellular automaton
A_{\varphi}
, we first formalize the notions
introduced in the previous section and fix some of its parameters.
The remaining components will be specified later.

In particular, we fix:

•

the dimension, which is
d=2
;

•

the neighbourhood, which is the
von Neumann neighbourhood

N=\{(0,0),(0,1),(0,-1),(1,0),(-1,0)\}.

For a cell with coordinates
(i,j)
we call
(i,j+1)
the right neighbour,
(i,j-1)
the left neighbour,
(i+1,j)
the below neighbour, and
(i-1,j)
the above neighbour.

The local transition function
f
and the set of states
S_{n,m}
will be defined in the following subsections.

4.1
Set of states

To define the
set of states
, we generalize the six types of cells described in
Subsection
3.1
. Each cell state is a tuple

s=\bigl((i,j),\,\mathrm{flag},\,a,\,\mathsf{pd},\,\mathsf{pc},\,\mathrm{label}\bigr),

whose components encode the information stored in a state of a single cell in a configuration. We use the symbol
\square
to indicate that a component is not present.
In particular, this allows us to define the
quiescent state

\mathbf{q}=((\square,\square),\square,\square,\square,\square,\square).

We describe the meaning of the individual components of a state
s
as follows:

•

Coordinates.

The component
\coord(s)=(i,j)\in([n+1]\cup\square)\times([m+2]\cup\square)
gives the position of the cell
in the computation table.

•

Variable flag.

The component
\mathrm{flag}(s)\in\{-1,0,1,\square\}
is intended to encode whether
the literal
x_{j}
,
\neg x_{j}
, or neither occurs in clause
C_{i}
.

•

Assignment value.

The component
a(s)\in\{0,1,\square\}
is intended to store the value
a_{j}
of the
variable
x_{j}
under the assignment
\mathbf{a}
.

•

Partial disjunction.

The component
\mathsf{pd}(s)\in\{0,1,\square\}
is intended to represent the truth
value of the partial disjunction

\bigl(\bigvee_{u\leq j}C_{i,u}\bigr)(\mathbf{a}).

•

Partial conjunction.

The component
\mathsf{pc}(s)\in\{0,1,\square\}
is intended to represent the truth
value of the partial conjunction

\bigl(\bigwedge_{v\leq i}C_{v}\bigr)(\mathbf{a}).

•

Label.

The component
\mathrm{label}(s)\in\{0,1,\square\}
is an auxiliary label used by the
cellular automaton.

We emphasize that these components store the
written values
in a configuration.
They may be incorrect. The role of the cellular automaton
A_{\varphi}
is to locally verify that the state of
each cell is consistent with the states of its neighbours in the von Neumann
neighbourhood.

Note that we refer to the components of a state
s
using the notation
\textit{component}(s)
. For example,
\mathrm{flag}(s)
denotes the value stored in
the component representing the variable flag. Formally, each component is a function on the set of states
S_{n,m}
(defined below)
with values in the domain of that component. That is,

\flag:S_{n,m}\to\{-1,0,1,\square\},

and similarly for the other components.

We now define seven subsets of the set of states
S_{n,m}
: six corresponding to the internal cell types described in Subsection
3.1
, and one representing the quiescent state surrounding the finite part of the configuration. These subsets are given as follows:

1.

Top-left cell:

S_{1}=\{((0,0),\square,\square,\square,\square,\mathrm{label})\mid\mathrm{label}\in\{0,1\}\}

2.

0
th column:

3.

0

S_{3}=\{((0,j),\square,a,\square,\square,\mathrm{label})\mid j\in\{1,2,\dots,m\},a,\mathrm{label}\in\{0,1\}\}

4.

S_{4}=\{((0,m+1),\square,\square,\square,\square,\mathrm{label})\mid\mathrm{label}\in\{0,1\}\}

5.

Last column:

S_{5}=\{((i,m+1),\square,\square,\mathsf{pc},\mathrm{label})\mid i\in\{1,2,\dots,n\},\mathsf{pc}\in\{0,1\},\mathrm{label}\in\{0,1\}\}

The cell with state
s
such that
\coord(s)=(n,m+1)
is called an

. Notice that this notion refers only to the written coordinates. Since coordinates are part of the written data and may be incorrect,
a configuration may contain several output cells, or none at all.

6.

Main body:

\displaystyle S_{6}={}

\displaystyle\{((i,j),\mathrm{flag},a,\mathsf{pd},\square,\mathrm{label})\mid i\in\{1,\dots,n\},\,j\in\{1,\dots,m\},

\displaystyle\mathrm{flag},a,\mathsf{pd},\mathrm{label}\in\{0,1\}\}

7.

Quiescent states around the finite part of the configuration:

S_{7}=\{\mathbf{q}\}\text{, where }\mathbf{q}=((\square,\square),\square,\square,\square,\square,\square)

The set of states is:

\displaystyle S_{n,m}=

\displaystyle S_{1}\cup S_{2}\cup S_{3}\cup S_{4}\cup S_{5}\cup S_{6}\cup S_{7}

(1)

\displaystyle\subseteq([n+1]\cup\{\square\})\times([m+2]\cup\{\square\})\times\{-1,0,1,\square\}\times\{0,1,\square\}^{4}

Again we distinguish between the values that a cell is
supposed to represent

and the values that are
actually written
in the computation table.
The written values are part of the cell’s state and may be incorrect. The names of subsets

are derived from the written value.
For example, a cell with a state

Since

can only inspect the neighbourhood of each cell, it verifies
correctness using a collection of
local correctness rules
.
These rules check, for example, that the values
a_{j}
are constant along each
column, that the coordinates
(i,j)
change in the expected way when moving
vertically or horizontally in the configuration, and that the values of the partial
disjunctions in the main body and the partial conjunctions in the last column are
computed correctly from neighbouring cells. We also impose several ”technical conditions” on the cells as not all cells are intended to contain all state components.
For example, the cell with a state
s
such that
\coord(s)=(0,0)
is required to contain only its coordinates, while all
other state components in this cell are set to
\square
.

Definition 4.1

(Local correctness)
.

Assume that all states mentioned below belong to
S_{n,m}
, and let

C\in\Config_{n,m}
be a configuration.

Let
c
be a non-quiescent cell with state
s
such that
\coord(s)=(i,j)
, where
i\in[n+1]
and
j\in[m+2]
.

We say that
c
is
locally correct
if the
following conditions hold. These conditions are
called the
local correctness rules
.

(A)

Index consistency.

Let
\coord(s)=(i,j)
and let
s_{1},s_{2},s_{3},s_{4}
be the states of the
right, left, below, and above neighbours of
c
, respectively.

If
i\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
and
j\in\{1,\dots,m\}
(that is,
c
is
not on the boundary), then

\coord(s)=(i,j)

if and only if

\displaystyle\coord(s_{1})

\displaystyle=(i,j+1)\;\wedge\;\coord(s_{2})=(i,j-1)\;\wedge

\displaystyle\coord(s_{3})

\displaystyle=(i+1,j)\;\wedge\;\coord(s_{4})=(i-1,j).

If
(i,j)
lies on the boundary (that is,
i=0
or
i=n
or

j=0
or
j=m+1
), then the same equivalence holds for the neighbours
inside the table, while every neighbour outside the finite part is in
the quiescent state
\mathbf{q}
.

For example,

\coord(s)=(0,0)

if and only if

\displaystyle\coord(s_{1})

\displaystyle=(0,1)\;\wedge\;s_{2}=\mathbf{q}\;\wedge

\displaystyle\coord(s_{3})

\displaystyle=(1,0)\;\wedge\;s_{4}=\mathbf{q}.

(B)

Formula consistency.

In the main body, the
\flag
component must correctly encode the
occurrence of variables in the formula
\varphi
.

More precisely, if

i\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}
and
j\in\{1,2,\dots,m\}
, then:

\displaystyle\flag(s)=1

\displaystyle\leftrightarrow\;x_{j}\in C_{i},

\displaystyle\flag(s)=-1

\displaystyle\leftrightarrow\;\neg x_{j}\in C_{i},

\displaystyle\flag(s)=0

\displaystyle\leftrightarrow\;\bigl(x_{j}\notin C_{i}\,\wedge\,\neg x_{j}\notin C_{i}\bigr).

(C)

Vertical consistency.

(C1)

If the column
j\in\{1,2,\dots,m\}
, then the
a
-value is constant along
the column. That is, for every neighbour
c^{\prime}
above or below
c

(with state
s^{\prime}
),

a(s^{\prime})=a(s).

(C2)

If
(i,j)=(r,m+1)
with
r\geq 2
, let
c^{\prime}
be the left neighbour
with state
s^{\prime}
and
c^{\prime\prime}
the upper neighbour with state
s^{\prime\prime}
.
Then the partial conjunction satisfies

\mathsf{pc}(s)=\mathsf{pc}(s^{\prime\prime})\wedge\mathsf{pd}(s^{\prime}).

Equivalently:

•

if
\mathsf{pc}(s^{\prime\prime})=0
, then
\mathsf{pc}(s)=0
;

•

if
\mathsf{pc}(s^{\prime\prime})=1
, then
\mathsf{pc}(s)=1
if and only if
\mathsf{pd}(s^{\prime})=1
.

(C3)

For
i=1
, the base case is checked directly. This means that if
(i,j)=(1,m+1)
, let
c^{\prime}
be the left neighbour with state

s^{\prime}
. Then

\mathsf{pc}(s)=1\;\leftrightarrow\;\mathsf{pd}(s^{\prime})=1.

(D)

Horizontal consistency.

(D1)

If
1\leq i\leq n
and
2\leq j\leq m
. Let
c^{\prime}
be the
left neighbour with state
s^{\prime}
. Then

\mathsf{pd}(s)=\bigl(\mathsf{pd}(s^{\prime})\;\vee\;(\flag(s)=1\wedge a(s)=1)\;\vee\;(\flag(s)=-1\wedge a(s)=0)\bigr).

Equivalently:

•

if
\mathsf{pd}(s^{\prime})=1
, then
\mathsf{pd}(s)=1
;

•

if
\mathsf{pd}(s^{\prime})=0
, then
\mathsf{pd}(s)=1
if and only if

\flag(s)=1\wedge a(s)=1
or

\flag(s)=-1\wedge a(s)=0
.

(D2)

If
(i,j)=(r,1)
with
r\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, then

\mathsf{pd}(s)=1\;\leftrightarrow\;\bigl(\flag(s)=1\wedge a(s)=1\bigr)\;\vee\;\bigl(\flag(s)=-1\wedge a(s)=0\bigr).

(E)

Technical conditions.

Depending on the position
(i,j)
, the unused components of
s

must be
\square
:

(E1)

If
j=0
, or
(i,j)=(0,0)
, or
(i,j)=(0,m+1)
,
all components except coordinates and label are
\square
.

(E2)

If
i=0
and
1\leq j\leq m
, all components except
coordinates,
a
, and label are
\square
.

(E3)

If
j=m+1
and
i\geq 1
, all components except
coordinates,
\mathsf{pc}
, and label are
\square
.

(E4)

If
1\leq i\leq n
and
1\leq j\leq m
, then
\mathsf{pc}(s)=\square
.

Each cell class (top-left,
0
th row, etc.) enforces the relevant subset of
(A)–(E).

In the following Lemma
4.3
we show that if all cells in a configuration
C\in\Config_{n,m}

satisfy the local correctness rules, then the configuration encodes a correct
computation of the value of
\varphi
on
\mathbf{a}
.

In particular, by correct computation we mean that for each

(i,j)\in[n+1]\times[m+2]
and each cell
c=(i,j,s)\in C
it holds that

\displaystyle\coord(s)

\displaystyle=(i,j),

\displaystyle\flag(s)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}k&\text{if }(i,j)\in\{1,\dots,n\}\times\{1,\dots,m\}\\
&\quad\text{and }(i,j,k)\in\varphi,\;k\in\{-1,0,1\},\\[6.0pt]
\square&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

\displaystyle a(s)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}a_{j}&\text{if }(i,j)\in[n+1]\times\{1,\dots,m\},\\[6.0pt]
\square&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

\displaystyle\mathsf{pd}(s)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\bigl(\bigvee_{u\leq j}C_{i,u}\bigr)(\mathbf{a})&\text{if }(i,j)\in\{1,\dots,n\}\times\{1,\dots,m\},\\[6.0pt]
\square&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

\displaystyle\mathsf{pc}(s)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\bigl(\bigwedge_{v\leq i}C_{v}\bigr)(\mathbf{a})&\text{if }i\in\{1,\dots,n\},\;j=m+1,\\[6.0pt]
\square&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(2)

Such configuration, however, is not unique: the labels of the cells are still arbitrary,
and therefore there are exponentially many configurations that contain the same correct
computation of
\varphi
on
\mathbf{a}
. We fix one canonical representative by requiring that every cell has label
0

and denote this configuration by
\Table_{\varphi}(\mathbf{a})
.

For configurations
C_{1},C_{2}\in\Config_{n,m}
, we write
C_{1}\sim C_{2}
if they agree on all components of their states except, possibly, the label component. In this case, we say
that
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are
similar
. In particular, a configuration similar to
\Table_{\varphi}(\mathbf{a})
encodes the correct computation, too, but may have different labels than just zeros. Clearly
\sim
is an equivalence relation on the set
\Config_{n,m}
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
C\in\Config_{n,m}
such that
C\sim\Table_{\varphi}(\mathbf{a})
. Then
C
is locally correct. In particular,
\Table_{\varphi}(\mathbf{a})
is locally correct.

Proof.

Fix a configuration
C
such that
C\sim\Table_{\varphi}(\mathbf{a})
. We show that each cell of
C
satisfies the local correctness rules.

Clearly, the statement holds for the
\coord
and
a
components from the definition. Moreover, for
\flag
the condition is exactly the same as in local correctness rules.

Fix
(i,j)\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}\times\{1,2,\dots,m\}
. Then
c=(i,j,s)\in C
for some
s\in S_{n,m}
and
\mathsf{pd}(s)=\bigl(\bigvee_{u\leq j}C_{i,u}\bigr)(\mathbf{a})
. Assume first that
j\geq 2
. Then

\displaystyle\mathsf{pd}(s)=

\displaystyle\bigl(\bigvee_{u\leq j}C_{i,u}\bigr)(\mathbf{a})=\bigl(\bigvee_{u\leq j-1}C_{i,u}\bigr)(\mathbf{a})\vee C_{i,j}(\mathbf{a})

\displaystyle=\mathsf{pd}(s^{\prime})\vee(\flag(s)=1\wedge a(s)=1)\;\vee\;(\flag(s)=-1\wedge a(s)=0),

where
s^{\prime}
is the state of cell
c^{\prime}=(i,j-1,s^{\prime})\in C
, which is exactly the left neighbour of
c
.

If
j=1
, then

\displaystyle\mathsf{pd}(s)=

\displaystyle C_{i,1}(\mathbf{a})=(\flag(s)=1\wedge a(s)=1)\;\vee\;(\flag(s)=-1\wedge a(s)=0).

Assume now that
i\geq 2
and
j=m+1
. Then

\displaystyle\mathsf{pc}(s)=\bigl(\bigwedge_{v\leq i}C_{v}\bigr)(\mathbf{a})=\bigl(\bigwedge_{v\leq i-1}C_{v}\bigr)(\mathbf{a})\wedge C_{i}(\mathbf{a})=\mathsf{pc}(s^{\prime})\wedge\mathsf{pd}(s^{\prime\prime}),

where
s^{\prime}
is the state of cell
c^{\prime}=(i-1,m+1,s^{\prime})\in C
(upper neighbour of
c
) and
s^{\prime\prime}
is the state of cell
c^{\prime\prime}=(i,m,s^{\prime\prime})\in C
(left neighbour of
c
).

The cases where
\mathsf{pc}(s)=\square
or
\mathsf{pd}(s)=\square
coincide with the same cases in local correctness rules.

All local correctness rules are satisfied in

C
.
∎

Moreover, the converse implication also holds: if a configuration is locally correct and its
0
th row encodes the assignment
\mathbf{a}
, then the whole configuration is uniquely determined except for the label components. In other words, local correctness forces the configuration to be similar to
\Table_{\varphi}(\mathbf{a})
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{m})\in\{0,1\}^{m}
and let

C\in\Config_{n,m}
.
If
C
is locally correct and, for every
j\in\{1,2,\dots,m\}
, every cell
with state
s
satisfying
\coord(s)=(0,j)
also satisfies
a(s)=a_{j}
, then

C
is similar to
\Table_{\varphi}(\mathbf{a})
.

Proof.

We show that every component of the state of each cell apart from label coincides with the corresponding entry
of
\Table_{\varphi}(\mathbf{a})
.

Coordinates.

We show that for every cell
c=(i,j,s)
with

(i,j)\in[n+1]\times[m+2]
it holds

\coord(s)=(i,j).

We first prove correctness of the row index by induction on
i
,
using local correctness rule (A).

Base case (
i=0
).

Let
c=(0,j,s)
for some
j\in[m+2]
, and let
c^{\prime}
be the cell above

c
. Since
C\in\Config_{n,m}
, the cell
c^{\prime}
is outside the finite
part of the table and therefore is in the quiescent state
\mathbf{q}
.
By index consistency (A), a cell whose above neighbour is in state

\mathbf{q}
must have first coordinate
0
. Hence

\coord(s)=(0,j)

for some
j
, and therefore the row index is correct.

Induction step.

Assume that for some
i\geq 0
every cell in rows
0,1,\dots,i
has correct
first coordinate in its state.

Let
c=(i+1,j,s)
and let
c^{\prime}=(i,j,s^{\prime})
be the
cell above
c
. By inductive assumption
\coord(s^{\prime})=(i,j)
. By rule (A), as
c
is below neighbour of
c^{\prime}
,
\coord(s)=(i+1,j)
.
Thus every cell in row
i+1
has the correct first coordinate.
This completes the induction on
i
.

An analogous induction on the column index
j
shows that the second
coordinate is also correct.

In the remainder of the proof, we rely on each cell’s state containing its correct coordinates.

Assignment values.

By assumption, the
0
th row contains the correct assignment values
a_{j}
.
By vertical consistency (C1), these values are propagated unchanged to all cells
below the
0
th row. By an easy induction on row index, every cell with coordinates
(i,j)
for
i\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}
and
j\in\{1,2,\dots,m\}
contains the
correct value
a_{j}
.

Partial disjunctions.

We show that for every
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
and every
j\in\{1,\dots,m\}
,
every cell with state
s
such that
\coord(s)=(i,j)
satisfies

\mathsf{pd}(s)=\bigl(\bigvee_{u\leq j}C_{i,u}\bigr)(\mathbf{a}).

The proof is by induction on
j
.

Base case
j=1
.

By rule (D2),
\mathsf{pd}(s)=1
if and only if either

\flag(s)=1
and
a_{1}=1
, or
\flag(s)=-1
and
a_{1}=0
.
In both cases, the literal in column
1
satisfies clause
C_{i}
,
so
C_{i,1}(\mathbf{a})=1
.
If none of these cases holds, then either
x_{1}
does not satisfy
C_{i}

or does not occur in it, and hence
C_{i,1}(\mathbf{a})=0
.
Thus
\mathsf{pd}(s)=C_{i,1}(\mathbf{a})
.

Induction step.

Assume the claim holds for column
k-1
, and let
s
be a state such that

\coord(s)=(i,k)
.
Let
s^{\prime}
be the state of its left neighbour.

Assume first
\mathsf{pd}(s^{\prime})=1
. (D1) implies that
\mathsf{pd}(s)=1
. Moreover, by inductive assumption
\bigl(\bigvee_{u\leq k-1}C_{i,u}\bigr)(\mathbf{a})=\mathsf{pd}(s^{\prime})=1
, which means

\bigl(\bigvee_{u\leq k}C_{i,u}\bigr)(\mathbf{a})=\bigl(\bigvee_{u\leq k-1}C_{i,u}\bigr)(\mathbf{a})\vee C_{i,k}(\mathbf{a})=1\vee C_{i,k}(\mathbf{a})=1.

Therefore
\mathsf{pd}(s)=\bigl(\bigvee_{u\leq k}C_{i,u}\bigr)(\mathbf{a})
in this case.

Assume
\mathsf{pd}(s^{\prime})=0
. Here (D1) yields that
\mathsf{pd}(s)=1
if and only if
\flag(s)=1\wedge a(s)=1
or
\flag(s)=-1\wedge a(s)=0
. Similarly as in the base step, if
\mathsf{pd}(s)=1
, then in both cases
a_{k}
satisfies
C_{i}
and

\bigl(\bigvee_{u\leq k}C_{i,u}\bigr)(\mathbf{a})=\bigl(\bigvee_{u\leq k-1}C_{i,u}\bigr)(\mathbf{a})\vee C_{i,k}(\mathbf{a})=\bigl(\bigvee_{u\leq k-1}C_{i,u}\bigr)(\mathbf{a})\vee 1=1.

If
\mathsf{pd}(s)=0
, then either
a_{k}
does not satisfy or does not belong to
C_{i}
. In both of these cases, as
\bigl(\bigvee_{u\leq k-1}C_{i,u}\bigr)(\mathbf{a})=0
by inductive assumption, then

\bigl(\bigvee_{u\leq k}C_{i,u}\bigr)(\mathbf{a})=\bigl(\bigvee_{u\leq k-1}C_{i,u}\bigr)(\mathbf{a})\vee C_{i,k}(\mathbf{a})=0\vee 0=0,

and we are done.

Partial conjunctions.

Finally, we show that for every
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
and every cell with
s
and

\coord(s)=(i,m+1)
,

\mathsf{pc}(s)=\bigl(\bigwedge_{v\leq i}C_{v}\bigr)(\mathbf{a}).

The proof is by induction on
i
.

Base case
i=1
.

By (C3)
\mathsf{pc}(s)=1
if and only if
\mathsf{pd}(s^{\prime})=1
for the left neighbour with state
s^{\prime}
. We have already shown that
\mathsf{pd}(s^{\prime})=1
if and only if
\bigl(\bigvee_{u\leq m}C_{1,u}\bigr)(\mathbf{a})=1
. It remains to notice that
(\bigvee_{u\leq m}C_{1,u}\bigr)
is exactly
C_{1}
and we are done.

Induction step.

Assume the claim holds for row
l-1
, and let
s
be a state such that

\coord(s)=(l,m+1)
. Let
s^{\prime\prime}
be the state of the above neighbour.

If
\mathsf{pc}(s^{\prime\prime})=0
, then according to (C2),
\mathsf{pc}(s)=0
. By inductive assumption
\bigl(\bigwedge_{v\leq l-1}C_{v}\bigr)(\mathbf{a})=\mathsf{pc}(s)=0
, therefore

\bigl(\bigwedge_{v\leq l}C_{v}\bigr)(\mathbf{a})=\bigl(\bigwedge_{v\leq l-1}C_{v}\bigr)(\mathbf{a})\wedge C_{l}(\mathbf{a})=0\wedge C_{l}(\mathbf{a})=0.

Assume
\mathsf{pc}(s^{\prime\prime})=1
. According to (C2)
\mathsf{pc}(s)=1
if and only if for left neighbour of
c
with state
s^{\prime}
it holds
\mathsf{pd}(s^{\prime})=1
. Similarly as in the base case, we have shown in the first part of the proof that
\mathsf{pd}(s^{\prime})=(\bigvee_{u\leq m}C_{l,u}\bigr)(\mathbf{a})=C_{l}(\mathbf{a})
. So
\mathsf{pc}(s)=1
if and only if
C_{l}(\mathbf{a})=1
. Then

\bigl(\bigwedge_{v\leq l}C_{v}\bigr)(\mathbf{a})=\bigl(\bigwedge_{v\leq l-1}C_{v}\bigr)(\mathbf{a})\wedge C_{l}(\mathbf{a})=1\wedge C_{l}(\mathbf{a})=C_{l}(\mathbf{a}).

This shows that
\mathsf{pc}(s)=1
if and only if
\bigl(\bigwedge_{v\leq l}C_{v}\bigr)(\mathbf{a})=1
and we are done.
∎

To define the local transition function, we must specify how

The idea is simple: if cell’s state violates at least one local correctness rule

Lemma 4.6
.

Assume
c\in\Config_{n,m}
is a blue cell. Let
s
be the state of

\coord(s)=(i,j)
for some
(i,j)\in[n+1]\times[m+2]

\coord(s^{\prime})=\mathrm{suc}(i,j)

Proof.

Since
c
is blue, it is locally correct and hence satisfies all local
correctness rules. In particular, by rule (A) (index consistency),
the coordinate component of the state of each non-quiescent neighbour
is uniquely determined by its relative position.

First assume that
(i,j)\in\{1,2,\dots,n-1\}\times\{1,2,\dots,m\}
, that is,
c
is not on the margin of the configuration.
Then all four von Neumann neighbours of

By rule (A), the coordinate components of their states are:

•

•

(i,j-1)

•

(i-1,j)
,

•

(i+1,j)

Hence exactly one of these neighbours has coordinate component

\mathrm{suc}(i,j)

Now assume that
c

Again by rule (A) at most one of its neighbours has coordinates

\mathrm{suc}(i,j)
.

states of its right, left, below and above neighbour cells,

In the same way, the colour of a cell depends only on its state and the states of its neighbours.

is

(s_{2},s_{3},s_{4},s_{5})
if every cell

whose
right, left, below and above neighbours have states

Finally, let

with respect to

(s_{2},s_{3},s_{4},s_{5})

according to Definition

, which
states that every blue cell has exactly one successor.

We are now ready to define the local transition function of the automaton.
Its purpose is to modify only the label component of each cell, while
leaving all other components of the state unchanged.

Intuitively, the automaton propagates information along the
successor relation defined above. If a cell is locally correct and
has a successor, then in one step the automaton updates its label
by taking the direct sum of its own
label and the label of its successor. In all other cases, the label
remains unchanged.

Thus, the new label of a cell depends only on its current label and
on the label of its uniquely determined successor.

Definition 4.7

(Local transition function

)
.

The local transition function

with respect to
(s_{2},s_{3},s_{4},s_{5})

We are now ready to define the cellular automaton

Lemma 4.10

Lemma 4.12
.

Let
C,C^{\prime}\in\Config_{n,m}
be configurations such that

, and let

\gamma=(c_{1},c_{2},\dots,c_{t}),\qquad t\in\mathbb{N},

For each

be the state of

in

and
let

be the state of
c_{i}
in
C^{\prime}
.
Assume that there exists an index

such that

\mathrm{label}(s_{k})\neq\mathrm{label}(s_{k}^{\prime}).

Then there exists an index
\ell\in\{1,\dots,t\}
such that the states

have different labels.

(Informally, the map

is injective when restricted to pointed chains.)

Proof.

Suppose that
A_{\varphi}(C)(c_{j})=A_{\varphi}(C^{\prime})(c_{j}^{\prime})
for all

. As their labels are computed using the local transition rule from Definition

, we can write it as

\displaystyle\bigl(\mathrm{label}(s_{1})

\displaystyle\oplus\mathrm{label}(s_{2}),\,\dots,\,\mathrm{label}(s_{t-1})\oplus\mathrm{label}(s_{t}),\,\mathrm{label}(s_{t})\bigr)

\displaystyle=\bigl(\mathrm{label}(s_{1}^{\prime})\oplus\mathrm{label}(s_{2}^{\prime}),\,\dots,\,\mathrm{label}(s_{t-1}^{\prime})\oplus\mathrm{label}(s_{t}^{\prime}),\,\mathrm{label}(s_{t}^{\prime})\bigl).

From the last coordinate we obtain
\mathrm{label}(s_{t})=\mathrm{label}(s_{t}^{\prime})
.
Next, comparing the
(t-1)
-th coordinates gives

\mathrm{label}(s_{t-1})\oplus\mathrm{label}(s_{t})=\mathrm{label}(s_{t-1}^{\prime})\oplus\mathrm{label}(s_{t}^{\prime}),

which implies

\displaystyle\mathrm{label}

\displaystyle(s_{t-1})=\bigl(\mathrm{label}(s_{t-1})\oplus\mathrm{label}(s_{t})\bigr)\oplus\mathrm{label}(s_{t})

\displaystyle=\bigl(\mathrm{label}(s_{t-1}^{\prime})\oplus\mathrm{label}(s_{t}^{\prime})\bigr)\oplus\mathrm{label}(s_{t})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{label}(s_{t-1}^{\prime})\oplus\bigl(\mathrm{label}(s_{t})\oplus\mathrm{label}(s_{t})\bigr)=\mathrm{label}(s_{t-1}^{\prime}).

By induction, we conclude

\mathrm{label}(s_{i})=\mathrm{label}(s_{i}^{\prime})\quad\text{for all }1\leq i\leq t.

Hence

\bigl(\mathrm{label}(s_{1}),\mathrm{label}(s_{2}),\dots,\mathrm{label}(s_{t})\bigr)=\bigl(\mathrm{label}(s_{1}^{\prime}),\mathrm{label}(s_{2}^{\prime}),\dots,\mathrm{label}(s_{t}^{\prime})\bigr),

∎

Lemma 4.14
.

If

contains a cycle, then every cell in

Proof.

Let

be a cycle in
C
.
Since successors of cells in a cycle also belong to the cycle, it suffices to show
that from any one cell in
\mathcal{Z}
we can reach all other cells of the finite
configuration by repeatedly following successor edges.

By the definition of a cycle,
\mathcal{Z}
contains at least one blue cell.
Since this cell is blue, the coordinate component of its state
s
is not
\square
.
First assume that
\coord(s)=(n,0)
.

First, we show by backward induction on

that
\mathcal{Z}
contains all cells
whose coordinate component of the state is
(i,0)
, for

.

Assume that a cell whose coordinate component of the state is
(i,0)
, for some

, belongs to
\mathcal{Z}
.
Then, by Definition

, the successor of this cell
has coordinate component

Since the successor of a cell in a cycle also belongs to the cycle,
there is a cell in
\mathcal{Z}
whose coordinate component of the state
is
(i-1,0)
.

Similarly, by induction on

, we show that
\mathcal{Z}
contains all cells
whose coordinate component of the state is

for

We now prove by induction on

that all cells whose coordinate
component of the state is
(i,j)
belong to
\mathcal{Z}
.

The case

was already shown.
Assume that for some

, all cells whose coordinate component
of the state is
(i,j)
, where

and

, belong to
\mathcal{Z}
.
We consider two cases.

•

If

is even, then the successor of the cell whose coordinate
component of the state is

has coordinate component

(k+1,m+1)
, and hence this cell belongs to
\mathcal{Z}
.
The proof that all cells whose coordinate component of the state

(k+1,j)
for

belong to

is analogous to the argument used above.

•

If

is odd, then the successor of the cell whose coordinate

has coordinate component

(k+1,1)
.
Hence this cell belongs to
\mathcal{Z}
.
Again, by repeatedly following successor edges, we obtain that all cells
whose coordinate component of the state is

for

\mathcal{Z}
.

In both cases, all cells whose coordinate component of the state

We have proved that if a cycle contains a cell whose coordinate

.

The choice of

was only for convenience.
The same argument applies to any cell whose coordinate component

contains all cells of

.
∎

An easy consequence of the previous lemma is that if a configuration contains at least one red cell, then it does not contain a cycle.

Theorem 4.15
.

Fix
C\in\Config_{n,m}
and let
U=\{C^{\prime}\in\Config_{n,m}\mid C^{\prime}\sim C\}
. The map
A_{\varphi}:U\to U
is injective if and only if
C
contains a red cell.

Proof.

The left to right direction was proven in Lemma
4.10
.

Assume than
C
contains at least one red cell. There are two options: either all the cells in
C
are red, or there is at least one blue cell.

In the first case
A_{\varphi}:U\to U
is an identity mapping and it is trivially injective.

In the second case consider a blue cell
c
. We want to show that it necessarily belongs to a pointed chain. As it is blue, it has a successor. Either the successor is red and we are done, or it is blue.
C
is finite and according to Lemma
4.14
,
c
is not in a cycle. Therefore iterating successors we reach a red cell or a quiescent cell. However, the last case contradicts the local correctness rules. Hence,
c
belongs to a pointed chain.
Therefore,
C
is a union of pointed chains.

Assume that for
C_{1},C_{2}\in U
, it holds
A_{\varphi}(C_{1})=A_{\varphi}(C_{2})
, but
C_{1}\neq C_{2}
.
Then there must exist a cell that appears in both
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
but has different labels in the two configurations.
Since this cell belongs to a pointed chain, this contradicts the injectivity of
A_{\varphi}
on pointed chains (Lemma
4.12
). Therefore,
A_{\varphi}:U\to U
is injective. ∎

We note that the next result is a version of Durand’s theorem (see
[
6
]
). In simple words, even if we only look at configurations of bounded size, it is still ”hard” to decide whether a given cellular automaton is injective on those configurations or not.

Theorem 4.16
.

A_{\varphi}:\Config_{n,m}\to\Config_{n,m}
is injective if and only if
\varphi
is not satisfiable.

Proof.

We prove both implications.

(
\Rightarrow
) Assume that
\varphi
is satisfiable. Then there exists an assignment
\mathbf{b}
such that
\varphi(\mathbf{b})=1
.

By Lemma
4.2
, the configuration
\Table_{\varphi}(\mathbf{b})
is locally correct. By the definition of
\Table_{\varphi}
, its output cell contains the value
\varphi(\mathbf{b})=1
.

By Lemma
4.10
, the restriction of
A_{\varphi}
to the set of configurations similar to
\Table_{\varphi}(\mathbf{b})
is not injective. Therefore, the map

A_{\varphi}:\Config_{n,m}\to\Config_{n,m}

is not injective.

(
\Leftarrow
) Now assume that
\varphi
is not satisfiable. This means that for each assignment
\mathbf{a}
we have
\varphi(\mathbf{a})=0
. Fix an arbitrary

\mathbf{a}
and consider a configuration
C
which has
\mathbf{a}
in the
0
th row.

If
C
is locally correct, then according to Lemma
4.3

C
is similar to
\Table_{\varphi}(\mathbf{a})
and therefore the value in the output cell is
\varphi(\mathbf{a})=0
. If
C
is not locally correct, this means that some cell does not satisfy local correctness rules.

In both cases according to Definition
4.4

C
contains a red cell. Therefore, by
Theorem
4.15
, for every configuration
C
the restriction

A:U\to U,\quad U=\{C^{\prime}\in\Config_{n,m}\mid C^{\prime}\sim C\},

is injective. Moreover, for any

, if
C^{\prime\prime}\not\sim C
, letting

the sets

and

, as well as

and

are disjoint. Hence

is injective.
∎

.

5.1.2
Coding of
\Table_{\varphi}(\mathbf{a})

Recall that a configuration is a function

Recall that a configuration

\Sigma^{B}_{0}

Lemma 5.4
.

The theory

(a)

(A)

\displaystyle\coord(s)=(i,j),

\displaystyle(i,j)\in\{1,\dots,n\}\times\{1,\dots,m\}\;\forall k\in

\displaystyle(\flag(s)=k\;\leftrightarrow\;(i,j,k)\in\varphi),

Theorem 5.5
.

V^{0}
proves that if
A_{\varphi}
is injective on
\Config_{n,m}
, then
\varphi
is not satisfiable.

Proof.

Assume that
\varphi
is satisfiable.
Then there exists
\mathbf{a}\in\{0,1\}^{m}
such that
\varphi(\mathbf{a})=1
. The predicate
\mathrm{Sat}(n,m,\mathbf{a},\varphi)
is a
\Delta_{0}
-formula
(see Subsection
2.4.2
),
hence this assumption is expressible in
V^{0}
.

We have shown in Subsections
5.1.1
and
5.1.2
that
V^{0}
proves the existence of
f_{\varphi}
and
\Table_{\varphi}(\mathbf{a})
. Similarly to
\Sigma^{B}_{0}
-definition of
\Table_{\varphi}(\mathbf{a})
in Lemma
5.4
, we can define by
\Sigma^{B}_{0}
-formula a configuration
C\sim\Table_{\varphi}(\mathbf{a})
such that the label of each cell’s state is

. Therefore
V^{0}
also proves the existence of such

.

In Lemma
4.2
we proved that
\Table_{\varphi}(\mathbf{a})
is locally correct. It remains to notice that this proof can be formalized in
V^{0}
: indeed, all arguments use only bounded quantification over
i\leq n
and
j\leq m
and simple identities defining
\mathsf{pd}
and
\mathsf{pc}
from neighbouring cells. The equalities

\bigvee_{u\leq j}C_{i,u}=\bigvee_{u\leq j-1}C_{i,u}\;\vee\;C_{i,j}\quad\text{and}\quad\bigwedge_{v\leq i}C_{v}=\bigwedge_{v\leq i-1}C_{v}\;\wedge\;C_{i}

-formula and is provable in
V^{0}
.

By definition of
f_{\varphi}
, the automaton never changes
any component of a state except possibly the label.
Hence it is enough to compare the labels after one step.

Let
c_{1}
be any cell of
\Table_{\varphi}(\mathbf{a})

with state

are

Therefore images of

are equal (to

6
Inverse automata

We have a CNF formula
\varphi
which determines parameters
n
and
m
and the automaton
A_{\varphi}=(2,S_{n,m},N,f_{\varphi})
we defined in the previous sections.
For simplicity, in this section we abbreviate the notation as follows:

\Config:=\Config_{n,m},\qquad A:=A_{\varphi},\qquad S:=S_{n,m},\qquad f:=f_{\varphi}.

Assume that
B
is a cellular automaton defined by

B=(2,M,S,g),

where
M
is a neighbourhood of size
\mu\geq 1
, and

g:S^{\mu}\to S

is the transition function. Again we study the action of
B
on
\Config
only.

For a cell
c\in[n+1]\times[m+2]
, we denote by
N(c)
the tuple of
cells in the von Neumann neighbourhood of
c
, in the same order as in
the definition of the neighbourhood. That is,

N(c)=(c,\,c+(0,1),\,c+(0,-1),\,c+(1,0),\,c+(-1,0)).

Similarly,
M(c)
denotes the tuple of cells in the
M
-neighbourhood of
c
.

When we apply set operations to these neighbourhoods, we implicitly
identify the tuple with the set of its elements. In particular, we define

N(M(c))\;:=\;\bigcup_{d\in M(c)}N(d).

Hence we may consider the composition

B\circ A:\Config\to\Config,\qquad C\mapsto B(A(C)).

Our goal is to express by a
\Sigma^{B}_{0}
-formula that
B
is an inverse of
A
.
More precisely, we want to find a
\Sigma^{B}_{0}
formula equivalent to the statement that for every configuration

C\in\Config
we have

B(A(C))=C.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
c\in[n+1]\times[m+2]
and let
C\in\Config
. The state of
c
in the
configuration
B(A(C))
depends only on the states of the cells from

N(M(c))
in
C
.

Proof.

Let
s
be the state of
c
in
B(A(C))
. By definition,

s=g(s_{1},s_{2},\dots,s_{\mu}),

where
s_{1},s_{2},\dots,s_{\mu}
are the respective states of the cells

c_{1},c_{2},\dots,c_{\mu}
in
A(C)
, where
M(c)=(c_{1},c_{2},\dots,c_{\mu})
.

For each
i\in\{1,2,\dots,\mu\}
, we have

s_{i}=f(s^{i}_{1},s^{i}_{2},s^{i}_{3},s^{i}_{4},s^{i}_{5}),

where
s^{i}_{1},s^{i}_{2},s^{i}_{3},s^{i}_{4},s^{i}_{5}
are the states of von Neumann neighbours of
c_{i}
in
C
.

Substituting these expressions into the definition of
s
gives

s=g\bigl(f(s^{1}_{1},s^{1}_{2},s^{1}_{3},s^{1}_{4},s^{1}_{5}),f(s^{2}_{1},s^{2}_{2},s^{2}_{3},s^{2}_{4},s^{2}_{5}),\dots,f(s^{\mu}_{1},s^{\mu}_{2},s^{\mu}_{3},s^{\mu}_{4},s^{\mu}_{5})\bigr).

Thus
s
depends only on the states of the neighbours of the cells

c_{1},\dots,c_{\mu}
in
C
. Since the von Neumann neighbours of
c_{i}
are exactly

N(c_{i})
, these cells form the set

N(c_{1})\cup N(c_{2})\cup\dots\cup N(c_{\mu})=N(M(c)).

∎

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
n,m,S,\Config,A,B
be as above. The following statements are equivalent:

(i)

B
is the inverse of
A
on
\Config
, i.e.

\forall C\in\Config\;B(A(C))=C.

(ii)

For every cell
c\in[n+1]\times[m+2]
, the following holds.
Let
M(c)=(c_{1},c_{2},\dots,c_{\mu})
. For each
i\in\{1,2,\dots,\mu\}
let

s^{i}_{1},s^{i}_{2},s^{i}_{3},s^{i}_{4},s^{i}_{5}\in S
be arbitrary states of the cells in

N(c_{i})
, where
s^{1}_{1}
is the state of
c
.
Then

s^{1}_{1}=g\bigl(f(s^{1}_{1},s^{1}_{2},s^{1}_{3},s^{1}_{4},s^{1}_{5}),f(s^{2}_{1},s^{2}_{2},s^{2}_{3},s^{2}_{4},s^{2}_{5}),\dots,f(s^{\mu}_{1},s^{\mu}_{2},s^{\mu}_{3},s^{\mu}_{4},s^{\mu}_{5})\bigr).

Proof.

Assume first that
B
is the inverse of
A
on
\Config
. Let
C\in\Config

and let
c
be a cell of
C
with state
s^{1}_{1}
. Since
B(A(C))=C
, the state
of
c
in
B(A(C))
is also
s^{1}_{1}
.

By the previous lemma, the state of
c
in
B(A(C))
is

g\bigl(f(s^{1}_{1},s^{1}_{2},s^{1}_{3},s^{1}_{4},s^{1}_{5}),f(s^{2}_{1},s^{2}_{2},s^{2}_{3},s^{2}_{4},s^{2}_{5}),\dots,f(s^{\mu}_{1},s^{\mu}_{2},s^{\mu}_{3},s^{\mu}_{4},s^{\mu}_{5})\bigr),

where for each
i\in\{1,2,\dots,\mu\}
the values

s^{i}_{1},s^{i}_{2},s^{i}_{3},s^{i}_{4},s^{i}_{5}
are the states of the cells in
N(c_{i})
in
C

and
M(c)=(c_{1},c_{2},\dots,c_{\mu})
. Since this state equals the state of
c
in
C
,
we obtain the required equality.

The converse direction follows immediately from the same expression for the
state of
c
in
B(A(C))
given by the previous lemma.
∎

6.1
Coding states in neighbourhood
M

We already introduced the coding of finite sequences in Subsection
2.3.3
. Using this coding we can encode
the neighbourhood
N(c)
of a cell
c
. However, the same method cannot be
used directly for the neighbourhood
M(c)
, since the size of
M(c)
is not some fixed constant, but may vary with
M
.
Therefore we need a way to encode sequences whose length is not fixed.

This is well-known in bounded arithmetic, cf.
[
8
, Lemma 9.3.2]
. Informally the idea of such a coding can be explained as follows.

Assume we have a sequence

(s_{1},s_{2},\dots,s_{\mu})\in S^{\mu}.

We encode it by a number
s
whose bit in position
\langle i,j\rangle
is the
i
-th bit of
s_{j}
. This can be done with
s
of length
less than
10\mu\log S
, i.e.:

s<|S|^{10\mu}.

The difficulty is that in
V^{0}
we cannot directly use the bound

|S|^{10\mu}
when
\mu
is a variable. To avoid this problem we introduce an
additional parameter
t
that serves as an upper bound for all such codes.

We define a
\Sigma^{B}_{0}
formula

\Inv(x,y,A,B,t)

to express that the automaton is locally invertible with respect to this
bound. Formally,

\Inv(x,y,A,B,t)\;:=\;|S|^{10\mu}<t\;\wedge\;(ii),

where
(ii)
is the condition from Lemma
6.2
, written using the coding above.

Condition (ii).

Condition from Lemma
6.2
, modified for
V^{0}
.

For all numbers
s_{1},s_{2},s_{3},s_{4},s_{5}<t
satisfying

\displaystyle s_{j}

\displaystyle=(s^{1}_{j},s^{2}_{j},\dots,s^{\mu}_{j})\qquad\text{for }j=1,\dots,5,

\displaystyle s^{1}_{1}

\displaystyle\text{ is the state of }c\text{ in }C,

\displaystyle(s^{1}_{1},s^{2}_{1},\dots,s^{\mu}_{1})

\displaystyle\text{ are the states of the cells in }M(c),

\displaystyle(s^{i}_{1},s^{i}_{2},s^{i}_{3},s^{i}_{4},s^{i}_{5})

\displaystyle\text{ are the states of the cells in }N(c_{i})

for every
i\in\{1,\dots,\mu\}
, where

M(c)=(c_{1},c_{2},\dots,c_{\mu}),

the following equality holds:

s^{1}_{1}=g\bigl(f(s^{1}_{1},s^{1}_{2},s^{1}_{3},s^{1}_{4},s^{1}_{5}),f(s^{2}_{1},s^{2}_{2},s^{2}_{3},s^{2}_{4},s^{2}_{5}),\dots,f(s^{\mu}_{1},s^{\mu}_{2},s^{\mu}_{3},s^{\mu}_{4},s^{\mu}_{5})\bigr).

6.2
Invertible automata are injective in
V^{0}

Lemma 6.3
.

V^{0}\vdash\Inv(x,y,A,B,t)\;\rightarrow\;\bigl[\forall C,C^{\prime}\in\Config\;(A(C)=A(C^{\prime})\rightarrow C=C^{\prime})\bigr].

Proof.

Assume
\Inv(x,y,A,B,t)
and let
C,C^{\prime}\in\Config
satisfy

A(C)=A(C^{\prime}).

Recall that we defined configuration as a map

C:\mathbb{Z}^{2}\to S
. Therefore in order to prove
C=C^{\prime}
it suffices to show that for every cell

c\in[n+1]\times[m+2]
we have

C(c)=C^{\prime}(c).

Fix such a cell
c
and write

M(c)=(c_{1},\dots,c_{\mu}).

For each
i\leq\mu
let
s^{i}_{j}
be the states of the cells in
N(c_{i})
in
C
, and let
t^{i}_{j}
be the states of the cells in
N(c_{i})
in
C^{\prime}
.

By definition of
A
we have

A(C)(c_{i})=f(s^{i}_{1},s^{i}_{2},s^{i}_{3},s^{i}_{4},s^{i}_{5})

and

A(C^{\prime})(c_{i})=f(t^{i}_{1},t^{i}_{2},t^{i}_{3},t^{i}_{4},t^{i}_{5}).

Since
A(C)=A(C^{\prime})
, it follows that for every
i\leq\mu
,

f(s^{i}_{1},s^{i}_{2},s^{i}_{3},s^{i}_{4},s^{i}_{5})=f(t^{i}_{1},t^{i}_{2},t^{i}_{3},t^{i}_{4},t^{i}_{5}).

Now apply condition (ii) from
\Inv(x,y,A,B,t)
to the
configuration
C
. We obtain

C(c)=g\bigl(f(s^{1}_{1},s^{1}_{2},s^{1}_{3},s^{1}_{4},s^{1}_{5}),\dots,f(s^{\mu}_{1},s^{\mu}_{2},s^{\mu}_{3},s^{\mu}_{4},s^{\mu}_{5})\bigr).

Applying the same condition to
C^{\prime}
gives

C^{\prime}(c)=g\bigl(f(t^{1}_{1},t^{1}_{2},t^{1}_{3},t^{1}_{4},t^{1}_{5}),\dots,f(t^{\mu}_{1},t^{\mu}_{2},t^{\mu}_{3},t^{\mu}_{4},t^{\mu}_{5})\bigr).

Since the corresponding arguments of
g
are equal, the two
expressions are equal. Hence

C(c)=C^{\prime}(c).

Since
c
was arbitrary, we conclude

C=C^{\prime}.

∎

7
Lower bound for inverse automata

Following the idea in Chapter
4
of
[
2
]
we shall interpret the inverse automaton to
A_{\varphi}
as a propositional refutation of
\varphi
.

Definition 7.1

(
P_{\mathrm{CA}}
)
.

We define a propositional proof system
P_{\mathrm{CA}}
as follows.

Let
\varphi
be a CNF formula. A refutation of
\varphi
in
P_{\mathrm{CA}}
is a cellular automaton

B=(2,S,M,g)

such that:

1.

S=S_{n,m}
is the set of states determined by
\varphi
as in Section
6
;

2.

if
t:=|B|
and
\mu
is the number of cells in
M
, then
\Inv(n,m,A_{\varphi},B,t)
holds.

Lemma 7.2
.

P_{\mathrm{CA}}
is a sound and complete proof system.

Proof.

The provability predicate is
\Sigma^{B}_{0}
and hence p-time decidable.

Suppose
\Inv(n,m,A_{\varphi},B,t)
holds.
By Lemma
6.3
and modus ponens,
this implies that
A_{\varphi}
is injective.
By Theorem
4.16
,
A_{\varphi}
is injective
if and only if
\varphi
is unsatisfiable.
Therefore
P_{\mathrm{CA}}
is sound.

For completeness, assume that the neighbourhood
M
is large enough so that
for every cell
c\in[n+1]\times[m+2]
the set
M(c)
contains the whole
region
[n+1]\times[m+2]
. Thus the function
g
”sees” the whole configuration

C
.

If
A_{\varphi}
is injective, then for every configuration
C\in A_{\varphi}(\Config)
there exists
exactly one configuration
C^{\prime}\in\Config
such that

A_{\varphi}(C^{\prime})=C.

We take this configuration
C^{\prime}
and define
g
so that,
when it is applied to the states of the cells in
M(c)
as they
appear in the configuration
C
, it outputs the state of the
cell
c
in
C^{\prime}
.

Thus
g
reconstructs the predecessor configuration cell by cell,
so it is the inverse of
A_{\varphi}
. Hence
\Inv(n,m,A_{\varphi},B,t)
holds. Therefore
P_{\mathrm{CA}}
is complete.
∎

Lemma 7.3
.

V^{0}
proves that
P_{\mathrm{CA}}
is sound.

\mathrm{Refut}_{P_{\mathrm{CA}}}(x,y,t,B,\varphi)\longrightarrow\varphi\notin\SAT

Proof.

Assume
\mathrm{Refut}_{P_{\mathrm{CA}}}(n,m,t,B,\varphi)
, where
x:=n
and
y:=m
are determined by
\varphi
.
By definition of
\mathrm{Refut}_{P_{\mathrm{CA}}}
(see Subsection
2.4.3
), this implies that

\Inv(n,m,A_{\varphi},B,t)
holds. By Lemma
6.3
,
V^{0}
proves that if

\Inv(n,m,A_{\varphi},B,t)
holds, then the cellular automaton
A_{\varphi}

is injective. By Theorem
5.5
,
V^{0}
proves that if

A_{\varphi}
is injective, then the formula
\varphi
is unsatisfiable.
Therefore
\varphi\notin\SAT
.
∎

Recall the formula
\lnot\mathrm{ontoPHP}_{k}
from Example
2.6
. Note that
\lnot\mathrm{ontoPHP}_{k}
has
k(k+1)
variables and
O(k^{3})
clauses.

Theorem 7.4
.

There exists a constant
\epsilon>0
such that the following holds for all sufficiently large
k
.
Let

\varphi:=\lnot\mathrm{ontoPHP}_{k},

and let
B=(2,S,M,g)
be a cellular automaton that is inverse to
A_{\varphi}
on
\Config_{n,m}
with
n,m
determined by
\varphi
. Let
\mu:=|M|
be the size of the neighbourhood of

, and let

be the size of

.

Then

t\geq 2^{k^{\epsilon}}.

In particular, for all sufficiently large
k
,

\mu\geq k^{\epsilon}.

Proof.

In the previous Lemma
7.3
we proved that

V^{0}\vdash\bigl[\mathrm{Refut}_{P_{\operatorname{CA}}}(x,y,s,B,\varphi)\rightarrow\varphi\notin SAT\bigr].

Thus
P_{\operatorname{CA}}
satisfies the reflection principle from
Theorem
2.16
. By Theorem
2.16
, there exists a constant
d

such that
F_{d}

P_{\operatorname{CA}}
for
refutations of CNF formulas.

We now apply this for
\varphi=\lnot\mathrm{ontoPHP}_{k}
and
B
. It is a CNF formula. By the simulation, there exists an
F_{d}
-refutation of
\varphi

whose size is at most
(t+|\varphi|)^{O(1)}=t^{O(1)}
as
t=|B|\geq|\varphi|
.

However, by Ajtai’s theorem (Theorem
2.7
), every
F_{d}
-refutation of
\lnot\mathrm{ontoPHP}_{k}
has size at least

for some constant

. It follows that

for any
\delta^{\prime}<\delta
and

, so every

\varphi
has

Putting this into the previous inequality and using the upper bound on

from Lemma

and the bound on the set of states from Equation (

), we obtain

. Since

.

This implies

In particular, there exists

such that for all sufficiently large

As

,

also holds and we are done.
∎
