Stability of Compensated Jump Integrals under Quadratic Variation Convergence
Philip Kennerberg

Abstract
We study the stability of compensated jump integrals under convergence in quadratic variation alone. Let X X and { X n } n ≥ 1 \{X^{n}\}_{n\geq 1} be càdlàg processes with jump measures μ , μ n \mu,\mu_{n} and predictable compensators ν , ν n \nu,\nu_{n} . Under the assumption [ X n − X ] t → 0 in probability , [X^{n}-X]_{t}\to 0\qquad\text{in probability}, we establish ucp convergence of compensated jump integrals of the form ∫ 0 . ∫ ℝ f n ( s , x ) ( μ n − ν n ) ( d s , d x ) \int_{0}^{.}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f_{n}(s,x)(\mu_{n}-\nu_{n})(ds,dx) under predictable locally bounded linear growth conditions together with locally uniform convergence assumptions on the integrands. The proof relies on two new structural mechanisms. The first is a Threshold Isolation Principle, showing that quadratic variation convergence prevents jumps from crossing suitably chosen moving threshold regions. The second is a compensator mass control mechanism, which constructs localizations whose complements have arbitrarily small probability and on which the large-jump compensator masses admit an almost surely summable dominating series, uniformly over the converging sequence. The results require neither semimartingale convergence, convergence of characteristics, uniform tightness, nor global structural assumptions such as independence, stationarity, or Markovianity. More broadly, they show that quadratic variation convergence imposes a stronger rigidity on the jump organization of càdlàg processes than one might initially expect.



1
Introduction

The stability theory of stochastic integrals and semimartingale functionals
has its origins in the structural developments of the general theory of
processes. During the 1960s and 1970s, foundational work of Meyer, Jacod,
Yor and others established the modern framework of random measures,
compensators and stochastic integration with respect to jump measures.
This led to the canonical decomposition theory underlying contemporary
stochastic calculus; see for example

Meyer 1966
;
Jacod and Shiryaev 2003
;
He et al. 1992
. Within this framework, compensated
jump integrals of the form

\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(s,x)(\mu-\nu)(ds,dx)

play a central role. Here
\mu
denotes the jump measure associated with an underlying càdlàg
process and
\nu
its predictable compensator. They arise naturally in Itô type decompositions,
semimartingale representations, Lévy type models, stochastic control,
filtering, mathematical finance and limit theory
(
Jacod and Shiryaev 2003
;
Ikeda and Watanabe 1989
;
Protter 2005
).

At the structural level, the classical theory is remarkably complete. The
existence and uniqueness of compensators, the construction of stochastic
integrals with respect to compensated jump measures, and the associated
local martingale properties are by now standard material.

Subsequent stability results, however, have typically relied on
substantially stronger convergence frameworks or additional structural
assumptions, such as semimartingale convergence, convergence of
characteristics, uniform tightness, or specific Markovian or Lévy
settings; see for example Kurtz and Protter (1991); Mémin (1980);
Liptser and Shiryaev (1989); Protter (2005).

By contrast, the present work returns to a more fundamental question:

To what extent does convergence in quadratic variation alone force stability
of compensated jump integrals?

At first sight, convergence in quadratic variation does not appear
to provide enough information to control compensator structure
or predictable jump organization. Quadratic variation only controls the aggregated
squared jump discrepancy and contains no explicit information about
compensators, predictable jump times, accessibility structure, or the fine
organization of atomic compensator masses. In particular, one might expect predictable jump times to drift,
disappear, or exchange accessibility properties along converging
sequences, even when convergence in quadratic variation holds.

The results show that convergence in quadratic variation retains enough structural information to establish stability of compensated jump integrals. In particular, although quadratic variation contains no explicit compensator information, it nevertheless imposes a nontrivial structural constraint on the organization of jumps and predictable compensator mass. The mechanism behind this phenomenon is not based on classical tightness or characteristic arguments, but instead on a threshold rigidity principle which forces a local alignment of large predictable jump times and allows one to recover compensator mass control directly from quadratic variation convergence.

The core of the argument consists of two mechanisms. The first is the
“Threshold Isolation Principle”, showing that quadratic variation convergence
prevents jumps from crossing suitably chosen threshold regions. The second is a
compensator mass control mechanism. This mechanism uses the Threshold Isolation Principle to locally align the relevant large jumps occurring at predictable times, and then shows that the corresponding
atomic compensator masses cannot accumulate uncontrollably.

Together, these mechanisms allow one to derive stability of compensated jump
integrals under assumptions considerably weaker than those typically used in
the literature.
Our main theorem establishes that

[X^{n}-X]_{t}\to 0\quad\text{in probability}

alone suffices to obtain ucp convergence of compensated jump integrals
under mild local assumptions on the integrands.

The main point is not merely that the assumptions used here are weaker than
those in the classical semimartingale stability literature. Rather, the
mechanism of control is different. Classical semimartingale convergence
yields ucp stability of stochastic integrals through the semimartingale
topology and associated characteristic control. The present result shows that, for compensated jump integrals,
quadratic variation convergence is sufficient to recover ucp
stability through an entirely different mechanism of control.

In the classical approach, jump structure is typically controlled through
characteristics, tightness, semimartingale convergence, or structural
assumptions on the driving process. In the present paper, no such
external control of the compensator or predictable jump structure is
imposed. Instead, the control is recovered internally from quadratic
variation convergence itself. The Threshold Isolation Principle provides the bridge from this limited pathwise information to a rigidity phenomenon for jumps.
This separation mechanism is then what makes the local alignment of
large jumps occurring at predictable times and the subsequent
compensator mass control possible.

The main result is particularly relevant in situations where one has access
to quadratic variation information but lacks a tractable semimartingale
topology or explicit control of characteristics. This includes
approximation schemes for jump processes, stability questions for
discontinuous stochastic systems under weak structural information,
and settings where only pathwise quadratic variation estimates are
available. In such situations, the theorem shows that compensated jump
integrals remain stable even without the classical machinery of
semimartingale convergence.

The mechanisms developed here may also be of independent interest.
In particular, the Threshold Isolation Principle and the compensator mass
control argument provide structural tools for extracting rigidity of
large-jump organization directly from quadratic variation convergence.
The techniques developed here may also be useful in related
stability problems for jump processes under weak structural
information.

The results suggest that quadratic variation
convergence imposes stronger restrictions on jump organization
than one might initially expect.

2
Overview of the paper

We begin with a brief preliminary section collecting the basic notation and
structural ingredients used throughout the paper. The subsequent section of
preparatory results develops the two main mechanisms underlying the proof:
the Threshold Isolation Principle and the compensator mass control mechanism.
The final section then combines these ingredients in the proof of the main
theorem.

The proof strategy depends crucially on two new techniques which build
progressively on one another.

The first is a Threshold Isolation Principle, showing that quadratic
variation convergence prevents jumps from crossing certain moving
threshold regions. This rigidity yields a threshold-separated
stabilization of the large predictable jump structure.

The second is a compensator mass control mechanism, which constructs
localizations with asymptotically full probability on which the large-jump
compensator masses admit an almost surely summable dominating series,
uniformly over the approximating sequence.
Rather than serving as isolated auxiliary observations, all lemmas and corollaries established below enter directly into the proof architecture of the main theorem.

In the main proof we first separate the error into an integrand
approximation part and a compensated jump measure approximation part.
The first term captures the discrepancy between
f_{n}
and
f

evaluated against the fixed compensated jump measure
(\mu-\nu)
,
while the second term contains the difference between the compensated
jump structures
(\mu_{n}-\nu_{n})
and
(\mu-\nu)
, evaluated with the
same integrand
f_{n}
.

The integrand approximation part is controlled by local uniform
convergence of
f_{n}
to
f
, the local linear growth bound, and the
quadratic variation of the limiting process. The compensated jump
measure approximation part is then split into small-jump and large-jump
regions. The small-jump contributions are controlled through the
Threshold Isolation Principle together with quadratic variation bounds,
whereas the large-jump contributions are handled through the
threshold-separated alignment of the large jumps occurring at predictable
times and the compensator mass control mechanism.

Finally, the aligned predictable jump structure is combined with the compensator mass control mechanism, allowing one to pass from atomwise convergence to convergence of the full compensated jump integrals through a dominated convergence argument for series.

Preliminaries

We assume that all processes are defined on a common filtered probability space

(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0},\mathbb{P})
, and that they are
adapted to the same filtration
\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
, satisfying the
usual hypotheses. Given a càdlàg process
X
and a stopping time
T
, we
write

X^{T}_{t}:=X_{t\wedge T}.

We also write

X^{*}_{t}:=\sup_{s\leq t}|X_{s}|

for the running supremum of
X
.

Definition 3.1
.

A property of a stochastic process is said to hold locally if there exists a
sequence of stopping times
T_{k}\uparrow\infty
such that the property holds
for the stopped process
X^{T_{k}}
for each
k
. It is said to hold
pre-locally if the corresponding property holds for
X^{T_{k}-}
for each

k
.

The following notation for jump measures will be used frequently. Whenever a
càdlàg process
X
with jump measure
\mu
is considered,
\nu
denotes
a predictable compensator of
\mu
.

Definition 3.2
.

Given a càdlàg process
X
with jump measure
\mu
and predictable
compensator
\nu
, we define

\mathcal{A}:=\{(s,\omega):s\leq t,\ \nu(\omega,\{s\},\mathbb{R})>0\}.

Thus
A
is the set of predictable times at which the compensator has an
atom.

The following proposition is a special case of Proposition 1.17(b) in

Jacod and Shiryaev 2003
.

Proposition 3.3
.

There exists a version of
\nu
such that

\nu(\{s\},\mathbb{R})\leq 1,\qquad s\leq t,

and such that
A
is a thin set exhausted by a sequence of predictable
stopping times.

Throughout the paper we work with such a version of the compensator.

Remark 3.4
.

Throughout the paper we use the convention
\inf\emptyset=\infty
.

The following elementary estimate will be used repeatedly in the proof of the
main theorem.

Lemma 3.5
.

Suppose that
X^{1},\ldots,X^{N}
and
\sum_{k=1}^{m}X^{k}
,
m\leq N
, all admit quadratic variation. Then

\left[\sum_{k=1}^{N}X^{k}\right]_{t}\leq\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}[X^{k}]_{t}^{1/2}\right)^{2}.

Proof.

Let
\pi=\{0=t_{0}<\cdots<t_{n}=t\}
be a partition of
[0,t]
, and set

a_{i}^{(k)}:=X^{k}_{t_{i}}-X^{k}_{t_{i-1}}.

Then Minkowski’s inequality in
\ell^{2}
gives

\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}a_{i}^{(k)}\right)^{2}\right)^{1/2}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}(a_{i}^{(k)})^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

Squaring both sides yields

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}(X^{k}_{t_{i}}-X^{k}_{t_{i-1}})\right)^{2}\leq\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X^{k}_{t_{i}}-X^{k}_{t_{i-1}})^{2}\right)^{1/2}\right)^{2}.

Passing to the limit along partitions yielding the quadratic variations
gives the result.
∎

4
Main result

For a function
g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, we write

\mathrm{disc}(g)
for its set of discontinuity points.

Theorem 4.1

(Stability of compensated jump functionals under quadratic variation convergence)
.

Let
X
and
\{X^{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
be real-valued càdlàg processes on

[0,t]
, with jump measures
\mu
and
\mu^{n}
, and predictable
compensators
\nu
and
\nu^{n}
, respectively. Assume that

[X^{n}-X]_{t}\longrightarrow 0\quad\text{in probability.}

Let
f
and
f_{n}
,
n\geq 1
, be predictable functions on

\Omega\times[0,t]\times\mathbb{R}
,
such that the compensated jump integrals

M_{t}:=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(s,x,\omega)(\mu-\nu)(ds,dx),

and

M^{n}_{t}:=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f_{n}(s,x,\omega)(\mu^{n}-\nu^{n})(ds,dx)

are well-defined purely discontinuous local martingales.
Assume that
{\mathbb{P}}\left(\exists s\leq t:\Delta X_{s}(\omega)\in\mathrm{disc}(f(s,.,\omega))\right)=0
.
Assume moreover that, for every
a>0
, there exists a nonnegative
predictable process
C^{a}
, locally bounded on
[0,t]
, such that, for
all
n\geq 1
,

|f_{n}(s,x,\omega)|+|f(s,x,\omega)|\leq C^{a}_{s}(\omega)|x|,\qquad s\leq t,\ |x|\leq a.

Assume also that there exists a set
\Omega_{0}\in\mathcal{F}
with

\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{0})=1
such that, for every
\omega\in\Omega_{0}
, every

s\leq t
, and every compact set
K\subset\mathbb{R}
,

\sup_{x\in K}|f_{n}(s,x,\omega)-f(s,x,\omega)|\longrightarrow 0.

Then

M^{n}\longrightarrow M\quad\text{ucp on }[0,t].

Remark 4.2
.

Fix
a>0
, and let
C^{a}
be the predictable locally bounded envelope
from the assumptions of Theorem 4.1. Define

s_{a,m}:=\inf\{s>0:C^{a}_{s}>m\}.

Since
C^{a}
is locally bounded on
[0,t]
, we have

\mathbb{P}(s_{a,m}\leq t)\to 0.

On
[0,s_{a,m})
,

|f_{n}(s,x,\omega)|+|f(s,x,\omega)|\leq m|x|,\qquad|x|\leq a.

Since
X^{n}
and
X
admit quadratic variation on
[0,t]
,

\int_{0}^{t}\int_{|x|\leq a}x^{2}\,\mu_{n}(ds,dx)<\infty,\qquad\int_{0}^{t}\int_{|x|\leq a}x^{2}\,\mu(ds,dx)<\infty\quad\text{a.s.}

Hence, after localization,

\int_{0}^{t\wedge s_{a,m}}\int_{|x|\leq a}|f_{n}(s,x,\omega)|^{2}\,\mu_{n}(ds,dx)<\infty,

and similarly for
f
and
\mu
. By the compensator identity, the same
local square-integrability holds with
\mu_{n},\mu
replaced by

\nu_{n},\nu
. Therefore the corresponding truncated compensated jump
integrals belong locally to the natural square-integrable martingale regime
associated with quadratic variation.

The local linear growth condition at the origin is not merely technical. It
is the natural scaling condition forced by the requirement that the
compensated jump integral remain in the quadratic-variation regime.

To see this, consider the totally inaccessible case, where

\nu(\{s\},dx)=0
for all
s>0
. If

M_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(s,x)(\mu-\nu)(ds,dx),

then

[M]_{t}=\sum_{s\leq t}|f(s,\Delta X_{s})|^{2}.

Thus finite quadratic variation of
M
requires the transformed jump
sequence
\{f(s,\Delta X_{s})\}_{s\leq t}
to be square summable.

Now suppose that near the origin
f(x)
behaves like
|x|^{\alpha}
with

\alpha<1
. Then the small-jump contribution to
[M]_{t}
is measured on
the scale

\sum_{s\leq t}|\Delta X_{s}|^{2\alpha}.

For càdlàg processes with finite quadratic variation but infinite

p
-variation for some
p<2\alpha
, this quantity may be infinite even
though

\sum_{s\leq t}(\Delta X_{s})^{2}<\infty.

Hence quadratic variation of the underlying process does not in general
control sublinear transformations of infinitely many small jumps.

The linear growth condition
f(s,x)=O(x)
at the origin is therefore not an
artifact of the proof. It is precisely the scaling condition that keeps the
transformed small-jump contribution on the same quadratic-variation scale as
the underlying process.

Remark 4.3
.

The local uniform convergence assumption in the
x
variable cannot in general
be replaced by pointwise convergence.

Indeed, let
N
be a Poisson process with intensity
1
, and define

X_{t}=N_{t},\qquad X^{n}_{t}=\left(1+\frac{1}{n}\right)N_{t}.

Then

[X^{n}-X]_{t}=\frac{1}{n^{2}}N_{t}\to 0\qquad\text{a.s.}

Now define

f\equiv 0,\qquad f_{n}(x)=1_{\{1+\frac{1}{n}\}}(x).

Then
f_{n}(x)\to f(x)
for every fixed
x\in\mathbb{R}
, and the functions

f_{n}
satisfy a uniform local linear growth condition.

However, if
\mu_{n},\nu_{n}
and
\mu,\nu
denote the jump measures and their
compensators associated with
X^{n}
and
X
, respectively, then

\nu_{n}(ds,dx)=ds\,\delta_{1+\frac{1}{n}}(dx),\qquad\nu(ds,dx)=ds\,\delta_{1}(dx),

and therefore

\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f_{n}(x)(\mu_{n}-\nu_{n})(ds,dx)=N_{t}-t,

while

\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)(\mu-\nu)(ds,dx)=0.

Hence the conclusion fails under pointwise convergence alone.

5
Preparatory Results

The first key technique, referred to as “Threshold Isolation Principle”, shows that for
every
r>0
one can construct threshold regions below
r
which are
eventually avoided by the jump sizes of both
X
and
\{X^{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}

with asymptotically full probability. This mechanism allows one to isolate and
control the small-jump contribution to the compensated jump integrals.

Lemma 5.1

(Threshold Isolation Principle)
.

Let
X
and
\{X^{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
be càdlàg processes on
[0,t]
such that

[X^{n}-X]_{t}\to 0\qquad\text{in probability}.

Fix
r>0
, and for each
k\geq 1
define

\delta(k,r):=\frac{r}{2^{k+2}},\qquad L(r,k):=r\Bigl(1-\frac{3}{2^{k+2}}\Bigr),

and

A_{k}(r):=\Bigl\{\omega:\bigl||\Delta X_{s}(\omega)|-L(r,k)\bigr|>\delta(k,r)\text{ for all }s\leq t\Bigr\}.

Then

\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\bigcup_{k\geq 1}A_{k}(r)\Bigr)=1.

Moreover, for each fixed
k\geq 1
, if we define

B_{n}(k,r):=\Bigl\{\sup_{s\leq t}|\Delta(X^{n}-X)_{s}|<\delta(k,r)\Bigr\},

then

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\bigl(B_{n}(k,r)\bigr)=1.

On the event
A_{k}(r)\cap B_{n}(k,r)
, the classification of jumps relative to the threshold

L(r,k)
is preserved, i.e.

|\Delta X_{s}|\leq L(r,k)-\delta(k,r)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad|\Delta X^{n}_{s}|<L(r,k),

and

|\Delta X_{s}|\geq L(r,k)+\delta(k,r)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad|\Delta X^{n}_{s}|>L(r,k),

for all
s\leq t
.

In particular, on
A_{k}(r)\cap B_{n}(k,r)
,

1_{\{|\Delta X^{n}_{s}|\leq L(r,k)\}}\leq 1_{\{|\Delta X_{s}|\leq L(r,k)\}}\qquad\text{for all }s\leq t,

and therefore

\sum_{s\leq t}(\Delta X^{n}_{s})^{2}1_{\{|\Delta X^{n}_{s}|\leq L(r,k)\}}\leq 2[X^{n}-X]_{t}+2\sum_{s\leq t}(\Delta X_{s})^{2}1_{\{|\Delta X_{s}|\leq L(r,k)\}}.

Proof.

Fix
r>0
. For each
k\geq 1
, set

I_{k}(r):=\left[r\left(1-\frac{1}{2^{k}}\right),\,r\left(1-\frac{1}{2^{k+1}}\right)\right).

Since

L(r,k)-\delta(k,r)=r\Bigl(1-\frac{3}{2^{k+2}}-\frac{1}{2^{k+2}}\Bigr)=r\left(1-\frac{1}{2^{k}}\right),

and

L(r,k)+\delta(k,r)=r\Bigl(1-\frac{3}{2^{k+2}}+\frac{1}{2^{k+2}}\Bigr)=r\left(1-\frac{1}{2^{k+1}}\right),

we have

[L(r,k)-\delta(k,r),\,L(r,k)+\delta(k,r)]=I_{k}(r).

Hence

A_{k}(r)=\Bigl\{\omega:|\Delta X_{s}(\omega)|\notin I_{k}(r)\text{ for all }s\leq t\Bigr\}.

Now suppose that

\omega\in\bigcap_{k=1}^{\infty}\left\{\exists s\leq t:\ |\Delta X_{s}(\omega)|\in I_{k}(r)\right\}.

Then for each
k
we may choose
s_{k}\leq t
such that

|\Delta X_{s_{k}}(\omega)|\in I_{k}(r).

Since the intervals
I_{k}(r)
are pairwise disjoint, the jump times
s_{k}
are pairwise distinct.
Moreover,

I_{k}(r)\subset\left[\frac{r}{2},r\right)\qquad\text{for all }k\geq 1,

so

|\Delta X_{s_{k}}(\omega)|\geq\frac{r}{2}\qquad\text{for all }k\geq 1.

Thus
\omega
would have infinitely many jumps of size at least
r/2
on
[0,t]
, which is
impossible for a càdlàg path. Therefore

\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\bigcap_{k=1}^{\infty}\{\exists s\leq t:\ |\Delta X_{s}|\in I_{k}(r)\}\Bigr)=0,

or equivalently,

\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\bigcup_{k\geq 1}A_{k}(r)\Bigr)=1.

Now fix
k\geq 1
. Since

\sup_{s\leq t}|\Delta(X^{n}-X)_{s}|\leq[X^{n}-X]_{t}^{1/2},

the assumption
[X^{n}-X]_{t}\to 0
in probability implies

\sup_{s\leq t}|\Delta(X^{n}-X)_{s}|\to 0\qquad\text{in probability}.

Hence

\mathbb{P}\bigl(B_{n}(k,r)\bigr)=\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\sup_{s\leq t}|\Delta(X^{n}-X)_{s}|<\delta(k,r)\Bigr)\to 1.

We now work on the event
A_{k}(r)\cap B_{n}(k,r)
. Let
s\leq t
.

If
|\Delta X_{s}|\leq L(r,k)-\delta(k,r)
, then

|\Delta X^{n}_{s}|\leq|\Delta X_{s}|+|\Delta(X^{n}-X)_{s}|<L(r,k)-\delta(k,r)+\delta(k,r)=L(r,k).

If
|\Delta X_{s}|\geq L(r,k)+\delta(k,r)
, then

|\Delta X^{n}_{s}|\geq|\Delta X_{s}|-|\Delta(X^{n}-X)_{s}|>L(r,k)+\delta(k,r)-\delta(k,r)=L(r,k).

Since on
A_{k}(r)
no jump of
X
lies in the isolated threshold

\bigl(L(r,k)-\delta(k,r),\,L(r,k)+\delta(k,r)\bigr),

it follows that on
A_{k}(r)\cap B_{n}(k,r)
the classification of jumps relative to

L(r,k)
is preserved.

In particular, on
A_{k}(r)\cap B_{n}(k,r)
,

|\Delta X^{n}_{s}|\leq L(r,k)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad|\Delta X_{s}|\leq L(r,k),

and hence

1_{\{|\Delta X^{n}_{s}|\leq L(r,k)\}}\leq 1_{\{|\Delta X_{s}|\leq L(r,k)\}}\qquad\text{for all }s\leq t.

Finally, on
A_{k}(r)\cap B_{n}(k,r)
,

\displaystyle\sum_{s\leq t}(\Delta X^{n}_{s})^{2}1_{\{|\Delta X^{n}_{s}|\leq L(r,k)\}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\leq t}(\Delta(X^{n}-X)_{s}+\Delta X_{s})^{2}1_{\{|\Delta X^{n}_{s}|\leq L(r,k)\}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{s\leq t}(\Delta(X^{n}-X)_{s})^{2}+2\sum_{s\leq t}(\Delta X_{s})^{2}1_{\{|\Delta X^{n}_{s}|\leq L(r,k)\}}

\displaystyle\leq 2[X^{n}-X]_{t}+2\sum_{s\leq t}(\Delta X_{s})^{2}1_{\{|\Delta X_{s}|\leq L(r,k)\}},

which proves the result.
∎

The above result does not only allow one to control the small jumps, but also to isolate the large jumps in a way that permits a direct comparison of the corresponding atomic contributions.

Corollary 5.2

(Pathwise convergence of threshold-separated jump atoms)
.

Let
X
and
\{X^{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
be càdlàg processes on
[0,t]
such that

[X^{n}-X]_{t}\to 0\qquad\text{a.s.}.

Let
f_{n}
and
f
be predictable functions on
\Omega\times[0,t]\times\mathbb{R}
. Assume that there exists a set

\Omega_{0}\in\mathcal{F}
with
\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{0})=1
such that, for every

\omega\in\Omega_{0}
, every
s\leq t
, and every compact set

K\subset\mathbb{R}
,

\sup_{x\in K}|f_{n}(s,x,\omega)-f(s,x,\omega)|\to 0.

Fix
r>0
,
k\geq 1
,
a>0
, and let
s\leq t
. Assume moreover that

f(s,\cdot,\omega)\text{ is continuous at }\Delta X_{s}(\omega)\qquad\text{a.s.}

Then

1_{A_{k}(r)}\left(\int_{L(r,k)<|x|\leq a}f_{n}(s,x)\,\mu_{n}(\{s\},dx)-\int_{L(r,k)<|x|\leq a}f_{n}(s,x)\,\mu(\{s\},dx)\right)\longrightarrow 0

almost surely on the event

\{(\Delta X)^{*}_{t}<a\}.

Proof.

Let
\Omega_{1}
be a full-measure event such that, for every

\omega\in\Omega_{1}
,

[X^{n}-X]_{t}(\omega)\to 0,

and such that, for every
s\leq t
,

f(s,\cdot,\omega)\text{ is continuous at }\Delta X_{s}(\omega).

We work on the full-measure event
\Omega_{0}\cap\Omega_{1}
, where
\Omega_{0}

is the event from the assumption.

Fix

\omega\in A_{k}(r)\cap\Omega_{0}\cap\Omega_{1}\cap\{(\Delta X)^{*}_{t}<a\}.

Since

\sup_{u\leq t}|\Delta(X^{n}-X)_{u}|\leq[X^{n}-X]_{t}^{1/2},

and

[X^{n}-X]_{t}(\omega)\to 0,

there exists
N_{0}(\omega)\in\mathbb{N}
such that, for all

n\geq N_{0}(\omega)
,

\sup_{u\leq t}|\Delta(X^{n}-X)_{u}|(\omega)<\delta(k,r).

Hence

\omega\in B_{n}(k,r)

for all sufficiently large
n
. Therefore, for all sufficiently large

n
,

\omega\in A_{k}(r)\cap B_{n}(k,r).

Since
\omega\in A_{k}(r)
, either

|\Delta X_{s}(\omega)|\leq L(r,k)-\delta(k,r),

or

|\Delta X_{s}(\omega)|\geq L(r,k)+\delta(k,r).

Case 1:

|\Delta X_{s}(\omega)|\leq L(r,k)-\delta(k,r).

Since

|\Delta X^{n}_{s}(\omega)-\Delta X_{s}(\omega)|<\delta(k,r)

for all sufficiently large
n
, we obtain

|\Delta X^{n}_{s}(\omega)|<L(r,k)

for all sufficiently large
n
.

Thus neither
\mu(\omega,\{s\},dx)
nor

\mu_{n}(\omega,\{s\},dx)
charges

\{x:L(r,k)<|x|\leq a\}.

Hence

\int_{L(r,k)<|x|\leq a}f_{n}(s,x,\omega)\,\mu_{n}(\omega,\{s\},dx)-\int_{L(r,k)<|x|\leq a}f_{n}(s,x,\omega)\,\mu(\omega,\{s\},dx)=0

for all sufficiently large
n
.

Case 2:

|\Delta X_{s}(\omega)|\geq L(r,k)+\delta(k,r).

Since

|\Delta(X^{n}-X)_{s}(\omega)|\leq[X^{n}-X]_{t}(\omega)^{1/2}\to 0,

we have

\Delta X^{n}_{s}(\omega)\to\Delta X_{s}(\omega)
.
Together with

|\Delta X_{s}(\omega)|\geq L(r,k)+\delta(k,r),

this implies

|\Delta X^{n}_{s}(\omega)|>L(r,k)

for all sufficiently large
n
.

Since

(\Delta X)^{*}_{t}(\omega)<a,

we have

|\Delta X_{s}(\omega)|<a.

Together with

\Delta X^{n}_{s}(\omega)\to\Delta X_{s}(\omega),

this implies that, for all sufficiently large
n
,

|\Delta X^{n}_{s}(\omega)|<a.

Therefore, for all sufficiently large
n
,

1_{\{L(r,k)<|\Delta X^{n}_{s}(\omega)|\leq a\}}=1_{\{L(r,k)<|\Delta X_{s}(\omega)|\leq a\}}=1.

Since

\mu(\omega,\{s\},dx)=\delta_{\Delta X_{s}(\omega)}(dx),

and

\mu_{n}(\omega,\{s\},dx)=\delta_{\Delta X^{n}_{s}(\omega)}(dx),

we get, for all sufficiently large
n
,

\displaystyle\int_{L(r,k)<|x|\leq a}f_{n}(s,x,\omega)\,\mu_{n}(\omega,\{s\},dx)

\displaystyle-\int_{L(r,k)<|x|\leq a}f_{n}(s,x,\omega)\,\mu(\omega,\{s\},dx)

\displaystyle=f_{n}(s,\Delta X^{n}_{s}(\omega),\omega)-f_{n}(s,\Delta X_{s}(\omega),\omega).

We show that the last expression converges to zero. Write

x_{n}:=\Delta X^{n}_{s}(\omega),\qquad x:=\Delta X_{s}(\omega).

Then
x_{n}\to x
. Hence there exists a compact set
K\subset\mathbb{R}

such that

x_{n}\in K\quad\text{for all sufficiently large }n,\qquad x\in K.

For all sufficiently large
n
,

\displaystyle|f_{n}(s,x_{n},\omega)-f_{n}(s,x,\omega)|

\displaystyle\leq|f_{n}(s,x_{n},\omega)-f(s,x_{n},\omega)|+|f(s,x_{n},\omega)-f(s,x,\omega)|

\displaystyle+|f(s,x,\omega)-f_{n}(s,x,\omega)|

\displaystyle\leq 2\sup_{y\in K}|f_{n}(s,y,\omega)-f(s,y,\omega)|+|f(s,x_{n},\omega)-f(s,x,\omega)|.

The first term tends to zero by local uniform convergence of

f_{n}(s,\cdot,\omega)
to
f(s,\cdot,\omega)
on
K
. The second
term tends to zero because
f(s,\cdot,\omega)
is continuous at

x=\Delta X_{s}(\omega)
. Therefore

f_{n}(s,\Delta X^{n}_{s}(\omega),\omega)-f_{n}(s,\Delta X_{s}(\omega),\omega)\to 0.

Consequently,

\int_{L(r,k)<|x|\leq a}f_{n}(s,x,\omega)\,\mu_{n}(\omega,\{s\},dx)-\int_{L(r,k)<|x|\leq a}f_{n}(s,x,\omega)\,\mu(\omega,\{s\},dx)\to 0.

Thus the asserted convergence holds for every

\omega\in A_{k}(r)\cap\Omega_{0}\cap\Omega_{1}\cap\{(\Delta X)^{*}_{t}<a\}.

Outside
A_{k}(r)
, the prefactor
1_{A_{k}(r)}
makes the assertion
trivial. Since

\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{0}\cap\Omega_{1})=1,

the result follows.
∎

The proof proceeds by first establishing a local alignment of the
threshold-separated large predictable jump times. This eventually makes
it possible to reduce the problem to a counting argument, showing that
only finitely many distinct large jump atoms can contribute
before the localization stops, which in turn yields the compensator
mass control.

Lemma 5.3

(mass control of compensator measures)
.

Let
X
and
\{X^{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
be càdlàg processes on
[0,t]
with jump measures

\mu,\mu_{n}
and compensators
\nu,\nu_{n}
, respectively, and assume that

[X^{n}-X]_{t}\to 0\quad\text{a.s.}

and let

\mathcal{A}:=\{(s,\omega):s\leq t,\ \nu(\omega,\{s\},\mathbb{R})>0\},\qquad\mathcal{A}_{n}:=\{(s,\omega):s\leq t,\ \nu_{n}(\omega,\{s\},\mathbb{R})>0\}.

For every
r>0
, there exists a family of stopping times
\{\theta_{q,k,\ell,n}\}_{q,k,\ell,n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
, depending on

, such that

\{s\in\mathcal{A}_{n}\cap[0,\theta_{q,k,\ell,n}):|\Delta X^{n}_{s}|>L(r,k)\}=\{s\in\mathcal{A}\cap[0,\theta_{q,k,\ell,n}):|\Delta X_{s}|>L(r,k)\}\quad\text{a.s.}

For every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
k_{0}
such that for all
k\geq k_{0}

there exists
\ell_{0}=\ell_{0}(k,\varepsilon)
such that for all
\ell\geq\ell_{0}

there exists
q_{0}=q_{0}(\ell,\varepsilon)
such that for all
q\geq q_{0}
,

\sup_{n\geq\ell}{\mathbb{P}}(\theta_{q,k,\ell,n}\leq t)<\varepsilon.

Moreover,

\sum_{s\leq t}\sup_{n\geq\ell}\bigl(1_{\{\cdot<\theta_{q,k,\ell,n}\}}\bigr)_{s-}\left(\int_{|x|>L(r,k)}\nu_{n}(\{s\},dx)+\int_{|x|>L(r,k)}\nu(\{s\},dx)\right)<\infty\qquad\textsf{a.s.}

Proof.

We first show that large predictable jump times coincide on the threshold-separated event.

Claim 5.4
.

For each
n\geq 1
, let
\{U_{m}\}_{m\geq 1}
and
\{U_{m}^{n}\}_{m\geq 1}
be exhausting sequences of predictable
stopping times for
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{A}_{n}
, respectively.

Fix

and

, and let

and

be as in

. Then for each

there exists a set

with

such that for every

Remark 5.5
.

By an exhausting sequence we mean that for almost every
\omega

Fix

and
k\geq 1
. For each

, since

is predictable, the map

yields

\displaystyle 0=\int_{\mathbb{R}}|x|\,1_{\{\nu_{n}(\{U_{m}\},\mathbb{R})=0\}}\,\nu_{n}(\{U_{m}\},dx)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\!\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}}|x|\,1_{\{\nu_{n}(\{U_{m}\},\mathbb{R})=0\}}\,\mu_{n}(\{U_{m}\},dx)\Bigm|\mathcal{F}_{U_{m}-}\right]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\!\left[1_{\{\nu_{n}(\{U_{m}\},\mathbb{R})=0\}}\,|\Delta X^{n}_{U_{m}}|\Bigm|\mathcal{F}_{U_{m}-}\right].

Since the conditional expectation is nonnegative, it follows that

1_{\{\nu_{n}(\{U_{m}\},\mathbb{R})=0\}}\,|\Delta X^{n}_{U_{m}}|=0\qquad\text{a.s.}

Let
\Omega^{(1)}_{m,n}
be a full-measure event on which the implication

Similarly, for each

, since

is predictable, the map

X
yields

\displaystyle 0=\int_{\mathbb{R}}|x|\,1_{\{\nu(\{U_{m}^{n}\},\mathbb{R})=0\}}\,\nu(\{U_{m}^{n}\},dx)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\!\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}}|x|\,1_{\{\nu(\{U_{m}^{n}\},\mathbb{R})=0\}}\,\mu(\{U_{m}^{n}\},dx)\Bigm|\mathcal{F}_{U_{m}^{n}-}\right]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\!\left[1_{\{\nu(\{U_{m}^{n}\},\mathbb{R})=0\}}\,|\Delta X_{U_{m}^{n}}|\Bigm|\mathcal{F}_{U_{m}^{n}-}\right].

Hence

1_{\{\nu(\{U_{m}^{n}\},\mathbb{R})=0\}}\,|\Delta X_{U_{m}^{n}}|=0\qquad\text{a.s.}

Let
\Omega^{(2)}_{m,n}
be a full-measure event on which the implication

|\Delta X_{U_{m}^{n}}|>0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\nu(\{U_{m}^{n}\},\mathbb{R})>0

holds.

Now define

\Omega_{n}:=\bigcap_{m=1}^{\infty}\Omega^{(1)}_{m,n}\cap\bigcap_{m=1}^{\infty}\Omega^{(2)}_{m,n}.

Since this is a countable intersection of full-measure events,

\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{n})=1.

We now prove that for every

\omega\in(A_{k}(r)\cap B_{n}(k,r))\cap\Omega_{n},

one has

\{U_{m}(\omega):|\Delta X_{U_{m}}(\omega)|>L(r,k)\}\subseteq\{U_{m}^{n}(\omega):|\Delta X^{n}_{U_{m}^{n}}(\omega)|>L(r,k)\}.

Fix such an
\omega
, and let
m\geq 1
satisfy

By Lemma
5.1
, the classification relative to

is preserved
on
A_{k}(r)\cap B_{n}(k,r)
, hence

|\Delta X^{n}_{U_{m}}(\omega)|>L(r,k).

In particular,

|\Delta X^{n}_{U_{m}}(\omega)|>0.

Since
\omega\in\Omega_{n}\subseteq\Omega^{(1)}_{m,n}

\nu_{n}(\omega,\{U_{m}(\omega)\},\mathbb{R})>0.

Because
\{U_{j}^{n}\}_{j\geq 1}
exhausts the predictable jump times of
\mathcal{A}_{n}
, there exists

j=j(\omega,m,n)

Moreover,

Thus

U_{m}(\omega)\in\{U_{j}^{n}(\omega):|\Delta X^{n}_{U_{j}^{n}}(\omega)|>L(r,k)\}.

Since

was arbitrary, the first inclusion follows.

We next prove that for every

\omega\in(A_{k}(r)\cap B_{n}(k,r))\cap\Omega_{n},

one has

\{U_{m}^{n}(\omega):|\Delta X^{n}_{U_{m}^{n}}(\omega)|>L(r,k)\}\subseteq\{U_{m}(\omega):|\Delta X_{U_{m}}(\omega)|>L(r,k)\}.

Fix such an
\omega
, and let
m\geq 1
satisfy

|\Delta X^{n}_{U_{m}^{n}}(\omega)|>L(r,k).

Again by Lemma
5.1
,

|\Delta X_{U_{m}^{n}}(\omega)|>L(r,k),

hence in particular

|\Delta X_{U_{m}^{n}}(\omega)|>0.

Since
\omega\in\Omega_{n}\subseteq\Omega^{(2)}_{m,n}

\nu(\omega,\{U_{m}^{n}(\omega)\},\mathbb{R})>0.

Because
\{U_{j}\}_{j\geq 1}
exhausts the predictable jump times of
\mathcal{A}

Thus

U_{m}^{n}(\omega)\in\{U_{j}(\omega):|\Delta X_{U_{j}}(\omega)|>L(r,k)\}.

Since
m
was arbitrary, the reverse inclusion follows.

Combining the two inclusions, we conclude that for every

\omega\in(A_{k}(r)\cap B_{n}(k,r))\cap\Omega_{n},

\{U_{m}^{n}(\omega):|\Delta X^{n}_{U_{m}^{n}}(\omega)|>L(r,k)\}=\{U_{m}(\omega):|\Delta X_{U_{m}}(\omega)|>L(r,k)\}.

This proves the claim.
∎

Fix
r>0
,
k\geq 1
, and
q,\ell\geq 1
. Define

\displaystyle N_{u}:=\sum_{s\leq u}1_{s\in\mathcal{A}}1_{|\Delta X_{s}|>L(r,k)},\qquad N_{u,n}:=\sum_{s\leq u}1_{s\in\mathcal{A}_{n}}1_{|\Delta X^{n}_{s}|>L(r,k)},

\displaystyle\tau_{q}^{X}:=\inf\{u\geq 0:N_{u}\geq q\},\qquad\tau_{q,n}:=\inf\{u\geq 0:N_{u,n}\geq q\}\wedge\tau_{q}^{X},

\displaystyle\sigma_{k}:=\inf\Bigl\{s\geq 0:0<\bigl||\Delta X_{s}|-L(r,k)\bigr|<\delta(k,r)\Bigr\},

\displaystyle\eta_{n,k}:=\inf\Bigl\{s\geq 0:(\Delta(X^{n}-X))^{*}_{s}\geq\delta(k,r)\Bigr\},

\displaystyle\eta_{k}^{(\ell)}:=\inf_{n\geq\ell}\eta_{n,k},\qquad\zeta_{\ell,k}:=\eta_{k}^{(\ell)}\wedge\sigma_{k},\qquad\theta_{q,k,\ell,n}:=\zeta_{\ell,k}\wedge\tau_{q,n}.

Since
N
and
N_{\cdot,n}
are adapted increasing càdlàg counting
processes, the hitting times

\tau^{X}_{q}:=\inf\{u\geq 0:N_{u}\geq q\},\qquad\inf\{u\geq 0:N_{u,n}\geq q\}

are stopping times. Indeed, for every
u\geq 0
,

\{\tau^{X}_{q}\leq u\}=\{N_{u}\geq q\}\in\mathcal{F}_{u},

and similarly for
N_{\cdot,n}
. Hence

\tau_{q,n}:=\inf\{u\geq 0:N_{u,n}\geq q\}\wedge\tau^{X}_{q}

is a stopping time.
With the above definitions we get

{\mathbb{P}}(\tau_{q,n}\leq t)\leq{\mathbb{P}}(N_{t}\geq q)+{\mathbb{P}}(N_{t,n}\geq q)\leq{\mathbb{P}}([X]_{t}\geq qL(r,k)^{2})+{\mathbb{P}}([X^{n}]_{t}\geq qL(r,k)^{2}).

The family
\{[X^{n}]_{t}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded in probability.
Indeed, since

[X^{n}]_{t}\leq 2[X]_{t}+2[X^{n}-X]_{t},

and
[X^{n}-X]_{t}\to 0
in probability, the family

\{[X^{n}-X]_{t}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded in probability. Hence, for
every
\varepsilon>0
, one can choose
K>0
such that

\sup_{n}\mathbb{P}([X^{n}]_{t}>K)<\varepsilon
. It follows that

\lim_{q\to\infty}\sup_{n}\mathbb{P}([X^{n}]_{t}\geq qL(r,k)^{2})=0.

Consequently,

\lim_{q\to\infty}\sup_{n}{\mathbb{P}}(\tau_{q,n}\leq t)=0
.

Moreover,

\{\theta_{q,k,\ell,n}\leq t\}\subseteq\{\sigma_{k}\leq t\}\cup\{\eta_{k}^{(\ell)}\leq t\}\cup\{\tau_{q,n}\leq t\}.

By Lemma
5.1
,

\mathbb{P}(\sigma_{k}\leq t)\to 0\qquad\text{as }k\to\infty.

For fixed
k
, since
[X^{n}-X]_{t}\to 0
a.s.,

\eta_{k}^{(\ell)}\uparrow\infty\qquad\text{a.s. as }\ell\to\infty,

and hence

\mathbb{P}(\eta_{k}^{(\ell)}\leq t)\to 0\qquad\text{as }\ell\to\infty.

Combining this with

\lim_{q\to\infty}\sup_{n}\mathbb{P}(\tau_{q,n}\leq t)=0,

we obtain the asserted localization property: for every
\varepsilon>0

there exists
k_{0}
such that for all
k\geq k_{0}
there exists

\ell_{0}=\ell_{0}(k,\varepsilon)
such that for all
\ell\geq\ell_{0}

there exists
q_{0}=q_{0}(\ell,\varepsilon)
such that for all
q\geq q_{0}
,

\sup_{n\geq\ell}\mathbb{P}(\theta_{q,k,\ell,n}\leq t)<\varepsilon.

We shall use two standard facts from the theory of predictable sets.
First, every thin predictable set admits an exhaustion by predictable
stopping times. Second, if
B
and
C
are predictable sets and

B
is thin, then
B\setminus C
is again a thin predictable set.

We shall also use the following standard fact. If
U
is a predictable
stopping time and
\tau
is a stopping time, then

\{U\leq\tau\}\in\mathcal{F}_{U-}
(see e.g.
Jacod and Shiryaev 2003
).
Hence the restricted time

U1_{\{U\leq\tau\}}+\infty 1_{\{U>\tau\}}

is predictable. Consequently, restricting a predictable graph to

\{s\leq\tau\}
preserves predictability.
For fixed
\ell
, let
\{T_{\ell,\ell,m}\}_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
be a
predictable exhaustion of the thin predictable set

\mathcal{A}_{\ell}\cap\left\{s:\int_{|x|>L(r,k)}\nu_{\ell}(\{s\},dx)>0\right\}\cap\{s\leq\tau_{q,\ell}\}.

Inductively, having chosen the families

\{T_{\ell,j,m}\}_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
,
\ell\leq j\leq n
, let

\{T_{\ell,n+1,m}\}_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
be a predictable exhaustion of
the thin predictable set

\mathcal{A}_{n+1}\cap\left\{s:\int_{|x|>L(r,k)}\nu_{n+1}(\{s\},dx)>0\right\}\cap\{s\leq\tau_{q,n+1}\}\setminus\bigcup_{j=\ell}^{n}\bigcup_{m\geq 1}[[T_{\ell,j,m}]].

Since the predictable
\sigma
-field is stable under countable unions
and set differences, each recursive remainder is again a thin
predictable set. Hence each such
remainder admits a predictable exhaustion, and the graphs

[[T_{\ell,n,m}]]
,
n\geq\ell
,
m\geq 1
, may be chosen pairwise
disjoint, up to evanescence.

By construction, no jump magnitude
|\Delta X_{s}|
before

\zeta_{\ell,k}
lies within distance
\delta(k,r)
of the threshold

L(r,k)
, while for all
n\geq\ell
,

\sup_{s<\zeta_{\ell,k}}|\Delta(X^{n}-X)_{s}|\leq\delta(k,r).

Therefore, by the threshold-separated alignment claim, outside a null
set, for every
n\geq\ell
,

\{s\in\mathcal{A}_{n}\cap[0,\zeta_{\ell,k}):|\Delta X^{n}_{s}|>L(r,k)\}=\{s\in\mathcal{A}\cap[0,\zeta_{\ell,k}):|\Delta X_{s}|>L(r,k)\}.

Restricting both sides to
[0,\theta_{q,k,\ell,n})
, and using

\theta_{q,k,\ell,n}\leq\zeta_{\ell,k}
, gives

\{s\in\mathcal{A}_{n}\cap[0,\theta_{q,k,\ell,n}):|\Delta X^{n}_{s}|>L(r,k)\}=\{s\in\mathcal{A}\cap[0,\theta_{q,k,\ell,n}):|\Delta X_{s}|>L(r,k)\}\quad\text{a.s.}

We next prove the counting bound

\textbf{Claim:}\qquad\sum_{n=\ell}^{\infty}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\bigl(1_{\{\cdot<\theta_{q,k,\ell,n}\}}\bigr)_{T_{\ell,n,m}-}\,1_{\{|\Delta X^{n}_{T_{\ell,n,m}}|>L(r,k)\}}\leq q+1.

Proof of the counting bound.

Fix
\omega
outside the null set on which the threshold-separated
alignment identities on
[0,\zeta_{\ell,k}(\omega))
fail. Let

\mathcal{U}_{\ell,q}(\omega):=\Bigl\{s\in\mathcal{A}(\omega):s<\zeta_{\ell,k}(\omega),\ s\leq\tau_{q}^{X}(\omega),\ |\Delta X_{s}(\omega)|>L(r,k)\Bigr\}.

Since
\zeta_{\ell,k}(\omega)\leq\sigma_{k}(\omega)
, no jump
magnitude
|\Delta X_{s}(\omega)|
with

s<\zeta_{\ell,k}(\omega)
lies within distance
\delta(k,r)
of

L(r,k)
. Since moreover
\zeta_{\ell,k}(\omega)\leq\eta_{k}^{(\ell)}(\omega)
, we have

\sup_{s<\zeta_{\ell,k}(\omega)}|\Delta(X^{n}-X)_{s}(\omega)|\leq\delta(k,r)\qquad\text{for all }n\geq\ell.

Therefore, for every
n\geq\ell
,

\Bigl\{s\in\mathcal{A}_{n}(\omega):s<\zeta_{\ell,k}(\omega),\ |\Delta X^{n}_{s}(\omega)|>L(r,k)\Bigr\}=\Bigl\{s\in\mathcal{A}(\omega):s<\zeta_{\ell,k}(\omega),\ |\Delta X_{s}(\omega)|>L(r,k)\Bigr\}.

We first bound the cardinality of
\mathcal{U}_{\ell,q}(\omega)
.
Since
\tau_{q}^{X}
is the first time that
N
reaches level
q
, there are at most
q
times

s\in\mathcal{A}(\omega)
such that

s\leq\tau_{q}^{X}(\omega),\qquad|\Delta X_{s}(\omega)|>L(r,k).

In particular,

\#\mathcal{U}_{\ell,q}(\omega)\leq q.

Now suppose that for some
n\geq\ell
and
m\geq 1
,

\bigl(1_{\{\cdot<\theta_{q,k,\ell,n}\}}\bigr)_{T_{\ell,n,m}-}(\omega)\,1_{\{|\Delta X^{n}_{T_{\ell,n,m}}(\omega)|>L(r,k)\}}=1.

Then

T_{\ell,n,m}(\omega)\leq\theta_{q,k,\ell,n}(\omega)\leq\zeta_{\ell,k}(\omega),\qquad T_{\ell,n,m}(\omega)\leq\tau_{q,n}(\omega)\leq\tau_{q}^{X}(\omega).

We distinguish two cases.

If

T_{\ell,n,m}(\omega)<\zeta_{\ell,k}(\omega),

then, by the exact jump matching identity above,

T_{\ell,n,m}(\omega)\in\mathcal{A}(\omega)\qquad\text{and}\qquad|\Delta X_{T_{\ell,n,m}}(\omega)|>L(r,k).

Since also
T_{\ell,n,m}(\omega)\leq\tau_{q}^{X}(\omega)
, it follows that

T_{\ell,n,m}(\omega)\in\mathcal{U}_{\ell,q}(\omega).

If instead
T_{\ell,n,m}(\omega)=\zeta_{\ell,k}(\omega)
, then the
exact matching identity on
[0,\zeta_{\ell,k}(\omega))
does not apply
at that time. However, by the pairwise disjointness of the graphs

[[T_{\ell,n,m}]]
, there is at most one index pair
(n,m)
for which

T_{\ell,n,m}(\omega)=\zeta_{\ell,k}(\omega)
. Thus the endpoint

\zeta_{\ell,k}(\omega)
contributes at most one additional term.

Therefore every nonzero term in the double sum corresponds either to a
time in
\mathcal{U}_{\ell,q}(\omega)
, or to the single exceptional
time
s=\zeta_{\ell,k}(\omega)
. Since the graphs

[[T_{\ell,n,m}]]
,
n\geq\ell
,
m\geq 1
, are pairwise disjoint, each
time in
\mathcal{U}_{\ell,q}(\omega)
is counted at most once. Hence

\sum_{n=\ell}^{\infty}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\bigl(1_{\{\cdot<\theta_{q,k,\ell,n}\}}\bigr)_{T_{\ell,n,m}-}(\omega)\,1_{\{|\Delta X^{n}_{T_{\ell,n,m}}(\omega)|>L(r,k)\}}\leq\#\mathcal{U}_{\ell,q}(\omega)+1\leq q+1.

This proves the claim.
∎

By monotone convergence,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{n=\ell}^{\infty}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\int_{|x|>L(r,k)}\bigl(1_{\{\cdot<\theta_{q,k,\ell,n}\}}\bigr)_{T_{\ell,n,m}-}\nu_{n}(\{T_{\ell,n,m}\},dx)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=\ell}^{\infty}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\int_{|x|>L(r,k)}\bigl(1_{\{\cdot<\theta_{q,k,\ell,n}\}}\bigr)_{T_{\ell,n,m}-}\nu_{n}(\{T_{\ell,n,m}\},dx)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=\ell}^{\infty}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\int_{|x|>L(r,k)}\bigl(1_{\{\cdot<\theta_{q,k,\ell,n}\}}\bigr)_{T_{\ell,n,m}-}\mu_{n}(\{T_{\ell,n,m}\},dx)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{n=\ell}^{\infty}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\int_{|x|>L(r,k)}\bigl(1_{\{\cdot<\theta_{q,k,\ell,n}\}}\bigr)_{T_{\ell,n,m}-}\mu_{n}(\{T_{\ell,n,m}\},dx)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{n=\ell}^{\infty}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\bigl(1_{\{\cdot<\theta_{q,k,\ell,n}\}}\bigr)_{T_{\ell,n,m}-}1_{|\Delta X^{n}_{T_{\ell,n,m}}|>L(r,k)}\right]\leq q+1,

implying
\sum_{n=\ell}^{\infty}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\int_{|x|>L(r,k)}\bigl(1_{\{\cdot<\theta_{q,k,\ell,n}\}}\bigr)_{T_{\ell,n,m}-}\nu_{n}(\{T_{\ell,n,m}\},dx)<\infty
a.s.. Since the graphs
[[T_{\ell,n,m}]]
exhaust, up to evanescence,

\bigcup_{n=\ell}^{\infty}\left(\mathcal{A}_{n}\cap\left\{s:\int_{|x|>L(r,k)}\nu_{n}(\{s\},dx)>0\right\}\cap\{s\leq\tau_{q,n}\}\right),

every nonzero term

\bigl(1_{\{\cdot<\theta_{q,k,\ell,n}\}}\bigr)_{s-}\int_{|x|>L(r,k)}\nu_{n}(\{s\},dx)

corresponds, outside an evanescent set, to exactly one graph

[[T_{\ell,n,m}]]
. Hence

\sum_{n=\ell}^{\infty}\sum_{s\leq t}\bigl(1_{\{\cdot<\theta_{q,k,\ell,n}\}}\bigr)_{s-}\int_{|x|>L(r,k)}\nu_{n}(\{s\},dx)<\infty\qquad\textsf{a.s.}

and therefore

\sum_{s\leq t}\sup_{n\geq\ell}\bigl(1_{\{\cdot<\theta_{q,k,\ell,n}\}}\bigr)_{s-}\int_{|x|>L(r,k)}\nu_{n}(\{s\},dx)\leq\sum_{n=\ell}^{\infty}\sum_{s\leq t}\bigl(1_{\{\cdot<\theta_{q,k,\ell,n}\}}\bigr)_{s-}\int_{|x|>L(r,k)}\nu_{n}(\{s\},dx)<\infty\quad\textsf{ a.s..}

Note that if
\{U_{l}\}_{l\in{\mathbb{N}}}
exhausts
\mathcal{A}\cap\{s\leq\tau_{q}^{X}\}
then by monotone convergence

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{s\leq\tau_{q}^{X}}\int_{|x|>L(r,k)}\nu(\{s\},dx)\right]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}\int_{|x|>L(r,k)}\nu(\{U_{l}\},dx)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\int_{|x|>L(r,k)}\nu(\{U_{l}\},dx)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\int_{|x|>L(r,k)}\mu(\{U_{l}\},dx)\right]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}1_{|\Delta X_{U_{l}}|>L(r,k)}1_{U_{l}\leq\tau_{q}^{X}}\right]={\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{s\in\mathcal{A}}1_{|\Delta X_{s}|>L(r,k)}1_{s\leq\tau_{q}^{X}}\right]\leq q,

where we also used that since for a fixed jump time
s
,
\mu(\{s\},dx)=\delta_{\Delta X_{s}}(dx)
.
This implies that

\sum_{s\leq\tau_{q}^{X}}\int_{|x|>L(r,k)}\nu(\{s\},dx)<\infty\quad\textsf{a.s..}

Since
\theta_{q,k,\ell,n}\leq\tau_{q}^{X}
for all
n\geq\ell
, the factor

\sup_{n\geq\ell}\bigl(1_{\{\cdot<\theta_{q,k,\ell,n}\}}\bigr)_{s-}

can be nonzero only at times
s\leq\tau_{q}^{X}
. Hence

\sum_{s\leq t}\sup_{n\geq\ell}\bigl(1_{\{\cdot<\theta_{q,k,\ell,n}\}}\bigr)_{s-}\int_{|x|>L(r,k)}\nu(\{s\},dx)\leq\sum_{s\leq\tau_{q}^{X}}\int_{|x|>L(r,k)}\nu(\{s\},dx)<\infty\quad\textsf{a.s.}.

∎

Having established the two core mechanisms underlying the argument, we now
turn to the proof of the main result.

6
Proof of Theorem
4.1

Proof.

Let
\{n_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
be an arbitrary subsequence. Passing to a further subsequence and relabelling, we may assume that

[X^{n}-X]_{t}\to 0\qquad\text{a.s..}

By the subsequence characterization of convergence in probability, it is enough to prove the asserted convergence under this additional almost sure assumption.
Let
T
be a jump time of
X
. Since

|\Delta(X^{n}-X)_{T}|\leq[X^{n}-X]_{t}^{1/2},

we have

\Delta X^{n}_{T}\xrightarrow{a.s.}\Delta X_{T}.

If we let
B^{n}_{a}=\left\{(\Delta X^{n})^{*}_{t}\vee\Delta X^{*}_{t}\leq a\right\}
then

\lim_{a\to\infty}\sup_{n}\mathbb{P}((B^{n}_{a})^{c})=0.

For
a>0
, let
C^{a}
be the predictable locally bounded envelope from
the assumptions of the theorem, and define

s_{a,m}:=\inf\{u>0:C^{a}_{u}>m\}.

Since
C^{a}
is locally bounded on
[0,t]
, for almost every

\omega
there exists
m_{0}(\omega)
such that

C^{a}_{u}(\omega)\leq m_{0}(\omega),\qquad u\leq t.

Hence

s_{a,m}>t

for all sufficiently large
m
, and therefore

\mathbb{P}(s_{a,m}\leq t)\to 0\qquad\text{as }m\to\infty.

On
[0,s_{a,m})
,

|f_{n}(u,x,\omega)|+|f(u,x,\omega)|\leq m|x|,\qquad u\leq t,\ |x|\leq a,\ n\geq 1.

Thus all estimates below may first be carried out for the stopped
integrands

1_{\{u<s_{a,m}\}}f_{n}(u,x,\omega),\qquad 1_{\{u<s_{a,m}\}}f(u,x,\omega),

where the deterministic bound
m|x|
holds on
|x|\leq a
. Let

Y^{n}_{u}:=\int_{0}^{u}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f_{n}(s,x)(\mu_{n}-\nu_{n})(ds,dx)-\int_{0}^{u}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(s,x)(\mu-\nu)(ds,dx).

For every
\varepsilon>0
,

\mathbb{P}\bigl((Y^{n})^{*}_{t}\geq\varepsilon\bigr)\leq\mathbb{P}\bigl((Y^{n})^{*}_{s_{a,m}-}\geq\varepsilon\bigr)+\mathbb{P}(s_{a,m}\leq t).

Since

\mathbb{P}(s_{a,m}\leq t)\to 0,

it suffices to prove the desired convergence for each fixed
m
after
localization. For notational simplicity, we suppress the stopping indicator in the sequel.

Throughout, square brackets denote quadratic variation. In particular, for compensated jump integrals, expressions of the form
[M]_{t}
refer to their quadratic variation processes. Using Lemma
3.5
, we now make the following bound

\displaystyle\left[\int_{0}^{.}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}f_{n}(s,x,\omega)(\mu_{n}-\nu_{n})(ds,dx)-\int_{0}^{.}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}f(s,x,\omega)(\mu-\nu)(ds,dx)\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}_{t}1_{B^{n}_{a}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left[\int_{0}^{.}\int_{|x|\leq a}\left(f_{n}(s,x,\omega)-f(s,x,\omega)\right)(\mu-\nu)(ds,dx)\right]_{t}^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left[\int_{0}^{.}\int_{|x|\leq a}f_{n}(s,x,\omega)(\mu-\nu)(ds,dx)-\int_{0}^{.}\int_{|x|\leq a}f_{n}(s,x,\omega)(\mu_{n}-\nu_{n})(ds,dx)\right]_{t}^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(2\sum_{s\leq t}\left(\int_{|x|\leq a}\left|f_{n}(s,x,\omega)-f(s,x,\omega)\right|\mu(\{s\},dx)\right)^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}+\left(2\sum_{s\leq t}\left(\int_{|x|\leq a}\left|f_{n}(s,x,\omega)-f(s,x,\omega)\right|\nu(\{s\},dx)\right)^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left[\int_{0}^{.}\int_{|x|\leq a}f_{n}(s,x,\omega)(\mu-\nu)(ds,dx)-\int_{0}^{.}\int_{|x|\leq a}f_{n}(s,x,\omega)(\mu_{n}-\nu_{n})(ds,dx)\right]_{t}^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(6.1)

Here we also used the fact that if
h|(s,x,\omega)|\leq H|x|
on
[0,t]\times[-a,a]
, for some
H\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
then

\displaystyle\left[\int_{0}^{\cdot}\int_{|x|\leq a}h(s,x,\omega)(\mu-\nu)(ds,dx)\right]_{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\leq t}\left(\int_{|x|\leq a}h(s,x,\omega)(\mu-\nu)(\{s\},dx)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\leq t}\left(\int_{|x|\leq a}h(s,x,\omega)\mu(\{s\},dx)-\int_{|x|\leq a}h(s,x,\omega)\nu(\{s\},dx)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{s\leq t}\left(\int_{|x|\leq a}|h(s,x,\omega)|\mu(\{s\},dx)+\int_{|x|\leq a}|h(s,x,\omega)|\nu(\{s\},dx)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{s\leq t}2\left(\int_{|x|\leq a}|h(s,x,\omega)|\mu(\{s\},dx)\right)^{2}+\sum_{s\leq t}2\left(\int_{|x|\leq a}|h(s,x,\omega)|\nu(\{s\},dx)\right)^{2},

which follows from the fact that compensated jump integrals are purely discontinuous local martingales, and therefore their quadratic variation is given by the sum of squared jumps.

Step 1: Integrand approximation terms.

For the first contribution on the right-hand side of (
6
),

\displaystyle\sum_{s\leq t}\left(\int_{|x|\leq a}\left|f_{n}(s,x,\omega)-f(s,x,\omega)\right|\mu(\{s\},dx)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{s\leq t}\mu(\{s\},[-a,a])\int_{|x|\leq a}\left|f_{n}(s,x,\omega)-f(s,x,\omega)\right|^{2}\mu(\{s\},dx)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{t}\int_{|x|\leq a}\left|f_{n}(s,x,\omega)-f(s,x,\omega)\right|^{2}\mu(ds,dx)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\leq t}\left|f_{n}(s,\Delta X_{s},\omega)-f(s,\Delta X_{s},\omega)\right|^{2}1_{|\Delta X_{s}|\leq a}.

where we used the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and the fact that
\mu(\{s\},[-a,a])\leq 1
.

We claim that, for every
a>0
,

\sum_{s\leq t}|f_{n}(s,\Delta X_{s},\omega)-f(s,\Delta X_{s},\omega)|^{2}1_{\{|\Delta X_{s}|\leq a\}}\longrightarrow 0.

Fix
a>0
and
\omega
outside the relevant null set. Let
0<r<a
. Write

\displaystyle S_{n}(\omega)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{s\leq t}|f_{n}(s,\Delta X_{s},\omega)-f(s,\Delta X_{s},\omega)|^{2}1_{\{|\Delta X_{s}|\leq a\}}

\displaystyle=S_{n,r}^{\mathrm{small}}(\omega)+S_{n,r}^{\mathrm{large}}(\omega),

where

S_{n,r}^{\mathrm{small}}(\omega):=\sum_{s\leq t}|f_{n}(s,\Delta X_{s},\omega)-f(s,\Delta X_{s},\omega)|^{2}1_{\{|\Delta X_{s}|\leq r\}},

and

S_{n,r}^{\mathrm{large}}(\omega):=\sum_{s\leq t}|f_{n}(s,\Delta X_{s},\omega)-f(s,\Delta X_{s},\omega)|^{2}1_{\{r<|\Delta X_{s}|\leq a\}}.

By the local linear bound,

\displaystyle S_{n,r}^{\mathrm{small}}(\omega)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{s\leq t}\bigl(|f_{n}(s,\Delta X_{s},\omega)|+|f(s,\Delta X_{s},\omega)|\bigr)^{2}1_{\{|\Delta X_{s}|\leq r\}}

\displaystyle\leq m^{2}\sum_{s\leq t}(\Delta X_{s})^{2}1_{\{|\Delta X_{s}|\leq r\}}.

On the other hand, the set

\{s\leq t:r<|\Delta X_{s}(\omega)|\leq a\}

is finite, since
X(\omega)
is càdlàg. For each such
s
, the local
uniform convergence in the
x
-variable gives

f_{n}(s,\Delta X_{s}(\omega),\omega)\longrightarrow f(s,\Delta X_{s}(\omega),\omega).

Therefore,

S_{n,r}^{\mathrm{large}}(\omega)\longrightarrow 0.

Consequently,

\limsup_{n\to\infty}S_{n}(\omega)\leq m^{2}\sum_{s\leq t}(\Delta X_{s})^{2}1_{\{|\Delta X_{s}|\leq r\}}.

Letting
r\downarrow 0
, and using

\sum_{s\leq t}(\Delta X_{s})^{2}<\infty,

we obtain

\sum_{s\leq t}(\Delta X_{s})^{2}1_{\{|\Delta X_{s}|\leq r\}}\longrightarrow 0.

Hence

\limsup_{n\to\infty}S_{n}(\omega)=0,

which proves

\sum_{s\leq t}|f_{n}(s,\Delta X_{s},\omega)-f(s,\Delta X_{s},\omega)|^{2}1_{\{|\Delta X_{s}|\leq a\}}\longrightarrow 0.

For the second contribution on the right-hand side of (
6
), note that

\displaystyle\sum_{s\leq t}\left(\int_{|x|\leq a}\left|f_{n}(s,x,\omega)-f(s,x,\omega)\right|\nu(\{s\},dx)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{s\leq t}\nu(\{s\},[-a,a])\int_{|x|\leq a}\left|f_{n}(s,x,\omega)-f(s,x,\omega)\right|^{2}\nu(\{s\},dx)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{t}\int_{|x|\leq a}\left|f_{n}(s,x,\omega)-f(s,x,\omega)\right|^{2}\nu(ds,dx),

where we used the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and the fact that

\nu(\{s\},[-a,a])={\mathbb{E}}\left[\mu(\{s\},[-a,a])\,|\,{\cal{F}}_{s-}\right]\leq 1.

For
q\in{\mathbb{N}}
, define the stopping times

t_{q}=\inf\left\{s>0:[X]_{s}\geq q\right\}.

Note that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\int_{0}^{t_{q}}\int_{|x|\leq a}\left|f_{n}(s,x,\omega)-f(s,x,\omega)\right|^{2}\nu(ds,dx)\right]={\mathbb{E}}\left[\int_{0}^{t_{q}}\int_{|x|\leq a}\left|f_{n}(s,x,\omega)-f(s,x,\omega)\right|^{2}\mu(ds,dx)\right],

where the right-hand side converges to zero by dominated convergence, since

\int_{0}^{t_{q}}\int_{|x|\leq a}\left|f_{n}(s,x,\omega)-f(s,x,\omega)\right|^{2}\mu(ds,dx)\leq m^{2}(q+a^{2}).

By the Markov inequality it follows that

\int_{0}^{t_{q}}\int_{|x|\leq a}\left|f_{n}(s,x,\omega)-f(s,x,\omega)\right|^{2}\nu(ds,dx)

converges to zero in probability for every
a
and
q
. By Markov’s inequality it follows that, for every fixed
a>0

and
q\in\mathbb{N}
,

\int_{0}^{t_{q}}\int_{|x|\leq a}|f_{n}(s,x,\omega)-f(s,x,\omega)|^{2}\nu(ds,dx)\overset{P}{\longrightarrow}0

as
n\to\infty
.

Combining this localized convergence with the stopping-time decomposition,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\int_{|x|\leq a}\left|f_{n}(s,x,\omega)-f(s,x,\omega)\right|^{2}\nu(ds,dx)\geq\epsilon\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(\int_{0}^{t_{q}}\int_{|x|\leq a}\left|f_{n}(s,x,\omega)-f(s,x,\omega)\right|^{2}\nu(ds,dx)\geq\epsilon\right)+{\mathbb{P}}\left(t_{q}\leq t\right),

which converges to zero since
t_{q}\uparrow\infty
as
q\to\infty
.

Step 2: The jump structure approximation term.

We next analyze the jump structure approximation term in (
6
). For any
r>0
and
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we consider the region

L(r,k)<|x|\leq a
, where the threshold
L(r,k)
is chosen according to the
Threshold Isolation Principle of Lemma
5.1
. On this
region, the difference between the two compensated jump integrals can be
decomposed into a
(\mu-\mu_{n})
term and a
(\nu-\nu_{n})
term. By Lemma
3.5
and a rearrangement, we have for any
r>0
and
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

\displaystyle\left[\int_{0}^{.}\int_{|x|\leq a}f_{n}(s,x,\omega)(\mu-\nu)(ds,dx)-\int_{0}^{.}\int_{|x|\leq a}f_{n}(s,x,\omega)(\mu_{n}-\nu_{n})(ds,dx)\right]_{t}^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\left[\int_{0}^{.}\int_{|x|\leq L(r,k)}f_{n}(s,x,\omega)(\mu-\nu)(ds,dx)\right]_{t}^{\frac{1}{2}}+\left[\int_{0}^{.}\int_{|x|\leq L(r,k)}f_{n}(s,x,\omega)(\mu_{n}-\nu_{n})(ds,dx)\right]_{t}^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle+\left[\int_{0}^{.}\int_{L(r,k)<|x|\leq a}f_{n}(s,x,\omega)(\mu-\mu_{n})(ds,dx)\right]_{t}^{\frac{1}{2}}+\left[\int_{0}^{.}\int_{L(r,k)<|x|\leq a}f_{n}(s,x,\omega)(\nu-\nu_{n})(ds,dx)\right]_{t}^{\frac{1}{2}},

(6.2)

We handle the four terms on the right-hand side of (
6
) separately. For the large-jump
\mu-\mu^{n}
term we first use the Threshold Isolation Principle to preserve the threshold classification of the jumps, reducing the
difference to a comparison of corresponding atomic contributions. The resulting
pointwise convergence of the atomic integrands is then obtained from Corollary

5.2
. For the large-jump
\nu-\nu^{n}
term we argue by localization together with the compensator mass control provided by Lemma
5.3
. Corollary
5.2
yields pointwise convergence of the threshold-separated atomic contributions, while Lemma
5.3
provides an almost surely summable dominating series for the corresponding compensator masses. This allows an application of dominated convergence for series.

Step 2a: Small jumps for the fixed jump measure.

For the first term on the right-hand side of (
6
) we have

\displaystyle\left[\int_{0}^{.}\int_{|x|\leq L(r,k)}f_{n}(s,x,\omega)(\mu-\nu)(ds,dx)\right]_{t}^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{s\leq t}\left(\int_{|x|\leq L(r,k)}f_{n}(s,x,\omega)(\mu-\nu)(\{s\},dx)\right)^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{s\leq t}\left(\int_{|x|\leq L(r,k)}\left|f_{n}(s,x,\omega)\right|\mu(\{s\},dx)+\int_{|x|\leq L(r,k)}\left|f_{n}(s,x,\omega)\right|\nu(\{s\},dx)\right)^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sqrt{2}\left(\sum_{s\leq t}\left(\int_{|x|\leq L(r,k)}\left|f_{n}(s,x,\omega)\right|\mu(\{s\},dx)\right)^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}+\sqrt{2}\left(\sum_{s\leq t}\left(\int_{|x|\leq L(r,k)}\left|f_{n}(s,x,\omega)\right|\nu(\{s\},dx)\right)^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sqrt{2}\left(\sum_{s\leq t}\mu(\{s\},{\mathbb{R}})\int_{|x|\leq L(r,k)}f_{n}(s,x,\omega)^{2}\mu(\{s\},dx)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}+\sqrt{2}\left(\sum_{s\leq t}\nu(\{s\},{\mathbb{R}})\int_{|x|\leq L(r,k)}f_{n}(s,x,\omega)^{2}\nu(\{s\},dx)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sqrt{2}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\int_{|x|\leq L(r,k)}f_{n}(s,x,\omega)^{2}\mu(ds,dx)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}+\sqrt{2}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\int_{|x|\leq L(r,k)}f_{n}(s,x,\omega)^{2}\nu(ds,dx)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(6.3)

We have

\displaystyle\left(\int_{0}^{t}\int_{|x|\leq L(r,k)}f_{n}(s,x,\omega)^{2}\mu(ds,dx)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\int_{0}^{t}\int_{|x|\leq L(r,k)}m^{2}x^{2}\mu(ds,dx)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq m\left(\sum_{s\leq t}(\Delta X_{s})^{2}1_{|\Delta X_{s}|\leq L(r,k)}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

which converges to zero as
r\downarrow 0
. For the second contribution on the right-hand side of (
6
), define the stopping times
\{t_{q}\}_{q}
. Note that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\int_{0}^{t_{q}}\int_{|x|\leq L(r,k)}f_{n}(s,x,\omega)^{2}\nu(ds,dx)\right]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left[\int_{0}^{t_{q}}\int_{|x|\leq L(r,k)}f_{n}(s,x,\omega)^{2}\mu(ds,dx)\right]

but

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t_{q}}\int_{|x|\leq L(r,k)}f_{n}(s,x,\omega)^{2}\mu(ds,dx)\leq m^{2}\sum_{s\leq t_{q}}(\Delta X_{s})^{2}1_{|\Delta X_{s}|\leq L(r,k)}\leq m^{2}(q+r^{2}),

Therefore, by dominated convergence,

\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}\sup_{n}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\int_{0}^{t_{q}}\int_{|x|\leq L(r,k)}f_{n}(s,x,\omega)^{2}\,\nu(ds,dx)\right]=0.

By Markov’s inequality it follows that, for every fixed
q
,

\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}\sup_{n}{\mathbb{P}}\left(\int_{0}^{t_{q}}\int_{|x|\leq L(r,k)}f_{n}(s,x,\omega)^{2}\nu(ds,dx)\geq\epsilon\right)=0.

Define

I_{r}^{n}:=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{|x|\leq L(r,k)}f_{n}(s,x,\omega)^{2}\,\nu(ds,dx).

Then

{\mathbb{P}}(I_{r}^{n}\geq\epsilon)\leq{\mathbb{P}}\left(\int_{0}^{t_{q}}\int_{|x|\leq L(r,k)}f_{n}(s,x,\omega)^{2}\,\nu(ds,dx)\geq\epsilon\right)+{\mathbb{P}}(t_{q}\leq t).

Hence

\limsup_{r\to 0^{+}}\sup_{n}{\mathbb{P}}(I_{r}^{n}\geq\epsilon)\leq\limsup_{r\to 0^{+}}\sup_{n}{\mathbb{P}}\left(\int_{0}^{t_{q}}\int_{|x|\leq L(r,k)}f_{n}(s,x,\omega)^{2}\,\nu(ds,dx)\geq\epsilon\right)+{\mathbb{P}}(t_{q}\leq t).

By the previous step, the first term on the right tends to
0
as
r\to 0^{+}
for each fixed
q
. Therefore

\limsup_{r\to 0^{+}}\sup_{n}{\mathbb{P}}(I_{r}^{n}\geq\epsilon)\leq{\mathbb{P}}(t_{q}\leq t).

Letting
q\to\infty
and using that
t_{q}\uparrow\infty
a.s., we conclude that

\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}\sup_{n}{\mathbb{P}}(I_{r}^{n}\geq\epsilon)=0.

Step 2b: Small jumps for the approximating jump measures.

The second term of (
6
) equals

\displaystyle\left(\left[\int_{0}^{.}\int_{|x|\leq L(r,k)}f_{n}(s,x,\omega)(\mu_{n}-\nu_{n})(ds,dx)\right]_{t}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{s\leq t}\left(\int_{|x|\leq L(r,k)}f_{n}(s,x,\omega)(\mu_{n}-\nu_{n})(\{s\},dx)\right)^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\int_{|x|\leq L(r,k)}f_{n}(s,x,\omega)^{2}\mu_{n}(ds,dx)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle+\sqrt{2}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\int_{|x|\leq L(r,k)}f_{n}(s,x,\omega)^{2}\nu_{n}(ds,dx)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(6.4)

We have

\left(\int_{0}^{t}\int_{|x|\leq L(r,k)}f_{n}(s,x,\omega)^{2}\mu_{n}(ds,dx)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq m\left(\sum_{s\leq t}(\Delta X^{n}_{s})^{2}1_{|\Delta X^{n}_{s}|\leq L(r,k)}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

According to Lemma
5.1
we have on the set
A_{k}(r)\cap B_{n}(k,r)
,

\displaystyle\sum_{s\leq t}(\Delta X^{n}_{s})^{2}1_{|\Delta X^{n}_{s}|\leq L(r,k)}\leq 2[X^{n}-X]_{t}+2\sum_{s\leq t}(\Delta X_{s})^{2}1_{|\Delta X_{s}|\leq L(r,k)}.

(6.5)

The first term converges to zero in probability as
n\to\infty
. The second
term does not depend on
n
. Moreover, since
L(r,k)<r
,

\sum_{s\leq t}(\Delta X_{s})^{2}1_{\{|\Delta X_{s}|\leq L(r,k)\}}\leq\sum_{s\leq t}(\Delta X_{s})^{2}1_{\{|\Delta X_{s}|\leq r\}},

the right-hand side converges to zero as
r\downarrow 0
. Thus the contribution of the second term can be made arbitrarily
small uniformly in
n
by choosing
r>0
sufficiently small.
Therefore, for every fixed
\epsilon>0
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(m\left(\sum_{s\leq t}(\Delta X^{n}_{s})^{2}1_{|\Delta X^{n}_{s}|\leq L(r,k)}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\geq\epsilon\right)

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{P}}\left(m\sqrt{2}[X^{n}-X]_{t}^{\frac{1}{2}}\geq\frac{\epsilon}{2}\right)+{\mathbb{P}}\left(\left(A_{k}(r)\cap B_{n}(k,r)\right)^{c}\right)

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{P}}\left(m\sqrt{2}[X^{n}-X]_{t}^{\frac{1}{2}}\geq\frac{\epsilon}{2}\right)+{\mathbb{P}}\left(A_{k}(r)^{c}\right)+{\mathbb{P}}\left(B_{n}(k,r)^{c}\right).

For the second term of the right-most side of (
6
), define the stopping times

\rho_{k}:=\inf\bigl\{s>0:|\Delta X_{s}|\in[L(r,k)-\delta(k,r),L(r,k)+\delta(k,r)]\bigr\},

\eta_{n,k}:=\inf\bigl\{s>0:|\Delta(X^{n}-X)_{s}|\geq\delta(k,r)\bigr\},

\sigma_{q}:=\inf\bigl\{s>0:[X]_{s}\geq q\bigr\},\qquad\sigma_{q,n}:=\inf\bigl\{s>0:[X^{n}]_{s}\geq q\bigr\},

and

\lambda_{q,n,k}:=\rho_{k}\wedge\eta_{n,k}\wedge\sigma_{q}\wedge\sigma_{q,n}.

Note,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\int_{0}^{\lambda_{q,n,k}-}\int_{|x|\leq L(r,k)}f_{n}(s,x,\omega)^{2}\nu_{n}(ds,dx)\right]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left[\int_{0}^{\lambda_{q,n,k}-}\int_{|x|\leq L(r,k)}f_{n}(s,x,\omega)^{2}\mu_{n}(ds,dx)\right]

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{E}}\left[\int_{0}^{\lambda_{q,n,k}-}\int_{|x|\leq L(r,k)}m^{2}x^{2}\mu_{n}(ds,dx)\right]

\displaystyle\leq m^{2}\left(2{\mathbb{E}}\left[[X^{n}-X]_{\lambda_{q,n,k}-}\right]+2{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{s<\lambda_{q,n,k}}(\Delta X_{s})^{2}1_{|\Delta X_{s}|\leq L(r,k)}\right]\right),

for every fixed
r,q,k
, the first term on the right-hand side converges to
zero as
n\to\infty
by dominated convergence, since

[X^{n}-X]_{\lambda_{q,n,k}-}\leq q
.
The second term does not depend on
n
, and converges to zero as

r\downarrow 0
by dominated convergence. By Markov’s inequality this implies that
\int_{0}^{\lambda_{q,n,k}-}\int_{|x|\leq L(r,k)}f_{n}(s,x,\omega)^{2}\nu_{n}(ds,dx)
converges to zero in probability by first letting
n\to\infty
and then
r\to 0^{+}
, for each
q
and
k
. Combining the previous localization estimate with the preceding convergence bound,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\int_{|x|\leq L(r,k)}f_{n}(s,x,\omega)^{2}\nu_{n}(ds,dx)\geq\epsilon\right)\leq{\mathbb{P}}\left(\int_{0}^{\lambda_{q,n,k}-}\int_{|x|\leq L(r,k)}f_{n}(s,x,\omega)^{2}\nu_{n}(ds,dx)\geq\epsilon\right)+{\mathbb{P}}\left(\lambda_{q,n,k}\leq t\right)

the first term has been shown to converge to zero for each
k
and
q
, for the second term we have

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\lambda_{q,n,k}<t\right)\leq{\mathbb{P}}\left(A_{k}(r)^{c}\right)+{\mathbb{P}}\left(B_{n}(k,r)^{c}\right)+{\mathbb{P}}\left([X^{n}]_{t}\geq q\right)+{\mathbb{P}}\left([X]_{t}\geq q\right).

we can first choose
n
large to make the second term small. We then choose
k
large to make the first term small and finally we choose
q
large to make the last two terms small, this is possible since
\{[X^{n}]_{t}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is bounded in probability due to the fact that
[X^{n}-X]_{t}\xrightarrow{{\mathbb{P}}}0
and
[X^{n}]_{t}\leq 2[X^{n}-X]_{t}+2[X]_{t}
.

We have

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\lambda_{q,n,k}\leq t\right)\leq{\mathbb{P}}\left(A_{k}(r)^{c}\right)+{\mathbb{P}}\left(B_{n}(k,r)^{c}\right)+{\mathbb{P}}\left([X^{n}]_{t}\geq q\right)+{\mathbb{P}}\left([X]_{t}\geq q\right).

Now fix
\eta>0
. First choose
k
so large that

{\mathbb{P}}\left(A_{k}(r)^{c}\right)<\eta.

For this fixed
k
, since
{\mathbb{P}}(B_{n}(k,r)^{c})\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, there exists
N_{1}
such that

{\mathbb{P}}\left(B_{n}(k,r)^{c}\right)<\eta\qquad\text{for all }n\geq N_{1}.

Moreover, since

[X^{n}]_{t}\leq 2[X]_{t}+2[X^{n}-X]_{t},

and
[X^{n}-X]_{t}\to 0
in probability, the family
\{[X^{n}]_{t}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded in probability. Hence we may choose
q
so large that

{\mathbb{P}}([X]_{t}\geq q)<\eta\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}{\mathbb{P}}([X^{n}]_{t}\geq q)<\eta.

Therefore, for all
n\geq N_{1}
,

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\lambda_{q,n,k}<t\right)<4\eta
. As
\eta
is arbitrary we conclude that

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\int_{|x|\leq L(r,k)}f_{n}(s,x,\omega)^{2}\nu_{n}(ds,dx)\geq\epsilon\right)

converges to zero as
n\to\infty
.

Step 2c: The large-jump
\mu-\mu^{n}
contribution.

Next, for the third term on the right-hand side of (
6
), we first write

\displaystyle\left[\int_{0}^{\cdot}\int_{L(r,k)<|x|\leq a}f_{n}(s,x,\omega)(\mu-\mu_{n})(ds,dx)\right]_{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\leq t}\left(f_{n}(s,\Delta X_{s},\omega)1_{\{L(r,k)<|\Delta X_{s}|\leq a\}}-f_{n}(s,\Delta X^{n}_{s},\omega)1_{\{L(r,k)<|\Delta X^{n}_{s}|\leq a\}}\right)^{2}.

On the event
A_{k}(r)\cap B_{n}(k,r)\cap B_{a}^{n}
, the Threshold Isolation Principle preserves the indicator
1_{\{L(r,k)<|\Delta X_{s}|\leq a\}}
.
Hence, on this event, the preceding expression is equal to

\sum_{s\leq t}\left(f_{n}(s,\Delta X_{s},\omega)-f_{n}(s,\Delta X^{n}_{s},\omega)\right)^{2}1_{\{L(r,k)<|\Delta X_{s}|\leq a\}}.

The latter sum contains only finitely many nonzero terms. For each such time

s
, Corollary
5.2
gives

f_{n}(s,\Delta X_{s},\omega)-f_{n}(s,\Delta X^{n}_{s},\omega)\to 0.

Therefore the sum converges to zero on
A_{k}(r)\cap B_{n}(k,r)\cap B_{a}^{n}
.
Since
P(B_{n}(k,r)^{c})\to 0
,
P((B_{a}^{n})^{c})\to 0
as
a\to\infty
, and

P(A_{k}(r)^{c})
can be made arbitrarily small by the choice of
k
, the third
term on the right-hand side of (
6
) converges to zero in probability.

Step 2d: The large-jump
\nu-\nu^{n}
contribution.

For the final term of (
6
) define

H^{n}_{u}:=\int_{0}^{u}\int_{L(r,k)<|x|\leq a}f_{n}(s,x,\omega)\,(\nu-\nu_{n})(ds,dx).

Since
(\nu-\nu_{n})
is purely atomic in time,
H^{n}
is a pure jump process and

[H^{n}]_{t}=\sum_{s\leq t}\left(\int_{L(r,k)<|x|\leq a}f_{n}(s,x,\omega),(\nu-\nu_{n})(\{s\},dx)\right)^{2}.

Now, since
f_{n}(s,x,\omega)1_{\{L(r,k)<|x|\leq a\}}
is predictable, the compensator identity yields, for
s
ranging over a thin predictable set,

\int_{L(r,k)<|x|\leq a}f_{n}(s,x,\omega)\nu(\{s\},dx)={\mathbb{E}}\!\left[\int_{L(r,k)<|x|\leq a}f_{n}(s,x,\omega)\mu(\{s\},dx)\Bigm|\mathcal{F}_{s-}\right],

and similarly

\int_{L(r,k)<|x|\leq a}f_{n}(s,x,\omega)\nu_{n}(\{s\},dx)={\mathbb{E}}\!\left[\int_{L(r,k)<|x|\leq a}f_{n}(s,x,\omega)\mu_{n}(\{s\},dx)\Bigm|\mathcal{F}_{s-}\right].

Hence

\int_{L(r,k)<|x|\leq a}f_{n}(s,x,\omega)(\nu-\nu_{n})(\{s\},dx)={\mathbb{E}}\!\left[\int_{L(r,k)<|x|\leq a}f_{n}(s,x,\omega)(\mu-\mu_{n})(\{s\},dx)\Bigm|\mathcal{F}_{s-}\right].

By Jensen’s inequality,

\left(\int_{L(r,k)<|x|\leq a}f_{n}(s,x,\omega)(\nu-\nu_{n})(\{s\},dx)\right)^{2}\leq{\mathbb{E}}\!\left[\left(\int_{L(r,k)<|x|\leq a}f_{n}(s,x,\omega)(\mu-\mu_{n})(\{s\},dx)\right)^{2}\Bigm|\mathcal{F}_{s-}\right].

On the set

\{(s,x):L(r,k)<|x|\leq a\},

the local linear growth assumption yields

|f_{n}(s,x,\omega)|^{2}\leq m^{2}x^{2}.

Using the inequality
(a-b)^{2}\leq 2a^{2}+2b^{2}
,

\displaystyle\left(\int_{L(r,k)<|x|\leq a}f_{n}(s,x,\omega),(\mu-\mu_{n})(\{s\},dx)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\left(\int_{L(r,k)<|x|\leq a}f_{n}(s,x,\omega),\mu(\{s\},dx)\right)^{2}+2\left(\int_{L(r,k)<|x|\leq a}f_{n}(s,x,\omega),\mu_{n}(\{s\},dx)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2f_{n}(s,x,\omega)^{2}1_{L(r,k)<|\Delta X_{s}|\leq a}+2f_{n}(s,x,\omega)^{2}1_{L(r,k)<|\Delta X^{n}_{s}|\leq a}\leq 4m^{2}a^{2},

which is locally integrable.

Fix
k\geq 1
, and let

\sigma_{k}:=\inf\Bigl\{u\geq 0:0<\bigl||\Delta X_{u}|-L(r,k)\bigr|<\delta(k,r)\Bigr\}.

Note that

\{\sigma_{k}\leq t\}\subseteq A_{k}(r)^{c}
. Since
{\mathbb{P}}(A_{k}(r)^{c})\to 0
as
k\to\infty
by the Threshold Isolation Principle, it follows that

{\mathbb{P}}(\sigma_{k}\leq t)\to 0\qquad\text{as }k\to\infty.

Let

\iota_{a}:=\inf\{u\leq t:|\Delta X_{u}|\geq a\}.

Then Corollary
5.2
, applied on
the stopped interval
[0,\sigma_{k}\wedge\iota_{a})
, yields, for each fixed
predictable jump time
s
ranging over the relevant thin predictable set,

\bigl(1_{\{\cdot<\sigma_{k}\wedge\iota_{a}\}}\bigr)_{s-}\int_{L(r,k)<|x|\leq a}f_{n}(s,x,\omega)\,(\mu-\mu_{n})(\{s\},dx)\to 0\qquad\text{a.s.}

Since

\bigl(1_{\{\cdot<\sigma_{k}\wedge\iota_{a}\}}\bigr)_{s-}

is
\mathcal{F}_{s-}
-measurable, the conditional dominated convergence
theorem yields

\bigl(1_{\{\cdot<\sigma_{k}\wedge\iota_{a}\}}\bigr)_{s-}\int_{L(r,k)<|x|\leq a}f_{n}(s,x,\omega)\,(\nu-\nu_{n})(\{s\},dx)\to 0\qquad\text{a.s.}

for each such fixed jump time
s
.

Set

a_{n}(s):=\bigl(1_{\{\cdot<\sigma_{k}\wedge\iota_{a}\}}\bigr)_{s-}\int_{L(r,k)<|x|\leq a}f_{n}(s,x,\omega)\,(\nu-\nu_{n})(\{s\},dx).

Then

a_{n}(s)\to 0

a.s. for each fixed
s
ranging over the relevant thin predictable set.

Apply Lemma
5.3
to

\{X^{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
, and let

\theta_{q,k,\ell,n}

denote the corresponding stopping times. Define

\theta^{a}_{q,k,\ell,n}:=\theta_{q,k,\ell,n}\wedge\iota_{a}.

Then

\sum_{s\leq t}\sup_{n\geq\ell}\bigl(1_{\{\cdot<\theta^{a}_{q,k,\ell,n}\}}\bigr)_{s-}\left(\int_{|x|>L(r,k)}\nu_{n}(\{s\},dx)+\int_{|x|>L(r,k)}\nu(\{s\},dx)\right)<\infty\qquad\textsf{a.s.}

If we let

c_{q,\ell,n}(s):=\bigl(1_{\{\cdot<\theta^{a}_{q,k,\ell,n}\}}\bigr)_{s-}\left(\int_{L(r,k)<|x|\leq a}f_{n}(x,s,\omega)\,(\nu-\nu_{n})(\{s\},dx)\right)^{2}.

then

\sum_{s\leq t}c_{q,\ell,n}(s)=[H^{n}]_{t},

on the event
\{\theta^{a}_{q,k,\ell,n}=\infty\}
.

Now let

b(s)=\sup_{n\geq\ell}\bigl(1_{\{\cdot<\theta^{a}_{q,k,\ell,n}\}}\bigr)_{s-}\left(\int_{L(r,k)<|x|\leq a}m|x|\nu_{n}(\{s\},dx)+\int_{L(r,k)<|x|\leq a}m|x|\nu(\{s\},dx)\right)^{2}

then, by using Cauchy-Schwarz,
\nu_{n}(\{s\},{\mathbb{R}})\leq 1
,
\nu(\{s\},{\mathbb{R}})\leq 1
a.s. and the elementary inequality
(a+b)\leq 2a^{2}+2b^{2}

\displaystyle b(s)

\displaystyle\leq 2\sup_{n\geq\ell}\bigl(1_{\{\cdot<\theta^{a}_{q,k,\ell,n}\}}\bigr)_{s-}\left(\int_{L(r,k)<|x|\leq a}m|x|\nu_{n}(\{s\},dx)\right)^{2}+2\sup_{n\geq\ell}\bigl(1_{\{\cdot<\theta^{a}_{q,k,\ell,n}\}}\bigr)_{s-}\left(\int_{L(r,k)<|x|\leq a}m|x|\nu(\{s\},dx)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=2\sup_{n\geq\ell}\bigl(1_{\{\cdot<\theta^{a}_{q,k,\ell,n}\}}\bigr)_{s-}\left(\int_{L(r,k)<|x|\leq a}m|x|\nu_{n}(\{s\},dx)\right)^{2}+2\sup_{n\geq\ell}\bigl(1_{\{\cdot<\theta^{a}_{q,k,\ell,n}\}}\bigr)_{s-}\left(\int_{L(r,k)<|x|\leq a}m|x|\nu(\{s\},dx)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sup_{n\geq\ell}\bigl(1_{\{\cdot<\theta^{a}_{q,k,\ell,n}\}}\bigr)_{s-}\int_{L(r,k)<|x|\leq a}m^{2}|x|^{2}\nu_{n}(\{s\},dx)+2\sup_{n\geq\ell}\bigl(1_{\{\cdot<\theta^{a}_{q,k,\ell,n}\}}\bigr)_{s-}\int_{L(r,k)<|x|\leq a}m^{2}|x|^{2}\nu(\{s\},dx)

\displaystyle\leq 2m^{2}a^{2}\sup_{n\geq\ell}\bigl(1_{\{\cdot<\theta^{a}_{q,k,\ell,n}\}}\bigr)_{s-}\left(\int_{|x|>L(r,k)}\nu_{n}(\{s\},dx)+\int_{|x|>L(r,k)}\nu(\{s\},dx)\right),

which implies that
\sum_{s\leq t}b(s)<\infty
a.s.. Note that
c_{q,\ell,n}(s)=a_{n}(s)^{2}
for
s<\theta^{a}_{q,k,\ell,n}
. Since
c_{q,\ell,n}(s)\leq b(s)
for all
n\geq\ell
, and

a_{n}(s)\to 0
a.s. as
n\to\infty
, it follows from dominated
convergence for series that

1_{\{\theta^{a}_{q,k,\ell,n}=\infty\}}\sum_{s\leq t}c_{q,\ell,n}(s)=[H^{n}]_{t}1_{\{\theta^{a}_{q,k,\ell,n}=\infty\}}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{a.s.}0.

This implies that, for given
\epsilon,\epsilon^{\prime}>0
, for
n\geq\ell

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left([H^{n}]_{t}\geq\epsilon\right)

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{P}}\left(1_{\theta^{a}_{q,k,\ell,n}=\infty}[H^{n}]_{t}\geq\epsilon\right)+{\mathbb{P}}\left(\theta^{a}_{q,k,\ell,n}\leq t\right)

and by first letting
n\geq n_{0}
the first term can be made less than
\epsilon^{\prime}
and then choosing
k_{0},q_{0},\ell_{0}
the second term can be made less than
\epsilon^{\prime}
for
q\geq q_{0}
,
k\geq k_{0}
,
\ell\geq\ell_{0}
and
a\geq a_{0}
. We conclude that the third term of (
6
) converges to zero in probability. To conclude we have thus shown that

\left[\int_{0}^{.}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}f_{n}(s,x,\omega)(\mu_{n}-\nu_{n})(ds,dx)-\int_{0}^{.}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}f(s,x,\omega)(\mu-\nu)(ds,dx)\right]_{t}1_{B^{n}_{a}}\xrightarrow{{\mathbb{P}}}0,

implying

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(\left[\int_{0}^{.}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}f_{n}(s,x,\omega)(\mu_{n}-\nu_{n})(ds,dx)-\int_{0}^{.}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}f(s,x,\omega)(\mu-\nu)(ds,dx)\right]_{t}\geq\epsilon\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(\left[\int_{0}^{.}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}f_{n}(s,x,\omega)(\mu_{n}-\nu_{n})(ds,dx)-\int_{0}^{.}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}f(s,x,\omega)(\mu-\nu)(ds,dx)\right]_{t}1_{B^{n}_{a}}\geq\epsilon\right)+\sup_{n^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{N}}}{\mathbb{P}}\left((B^{n^{\prime}}_{a})^{c}\right)

where the first term vanishes as
n\to\infty
and the second as
a\to\infty
.
We now let

T_{k}^{n}=\inf\left\{u>0:\left[\int_{0}^{.}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}f_{n}(s,x,\omega)(\mu_{n}-\nu_{n})(ds,dx)-\int_{0}^{.}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}f(s,x,\omega)(\mu-\nu)(ds,dx)\right]_{u}\geq k\right\},

so that
\lim_{k\to\infty}\sup_{n}{\mathbb{P}}\left(T_{k}^{n}\leq t\right)=0
. By the Markov Burkholder-Davis-Gundy inequality we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(\left(\int_{0}^{.}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}f_{n}(s,x,\omega)(\mu_{n}-\nu_{n})(ds,dx)-\int_{0}^{.}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}f(s,x,\omega)(\mu-\nu)(ds,dx)\right)_{T_{k}}^{*}\geq\epsilon\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\epsilon}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{.}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}f_{n}(s,x,\omega)(\mu_{n}-\nu_{n})(ds,dx)-\int_{0}^{.}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}f(s,x,\omega)(\mu-\nu)(ds,dx)\right)_{T_{k}}^{*}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{D}{\epsilon}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left[\int_{0}^{.}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}f_{n}(s,x,\omega)(\mu_{n}-\nu_{n})(ds,dx)-\int_{0}^{.}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}f(s,x,\omega)(\mu-\nu)(ds,dx)\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}_{T_{k}}\right]

where the right-most side converges to zero by dominated convergence. Therefore

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(\left(\int_{0}^{.}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}f_{n}(s,x,\omega)(\mu_{n}-\nu_{n})(ds,dx)-\int_{0}^{.}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}f(s,x,\omega)(\mu-\nu)(ds,dx)\right)_{t}^{*}\geq\epsilon\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(\left(\int_{0}^{.}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}f_{n}(s,x,\omega)(\mu_{n}-\nu_{n})(ds,dx)-\int_{0}^{.}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}f(s,x,\omega)(\mu-\nu)(ds,dx)\right)_{T_{k}}^{*}\geq\epsilon\right)+\sup_{n^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{N}}}{\mathbb{P}}\left(T_{k}^{n^{\prime}}\leq t\right)

where the first term vanishes as
n\to\infty
and the second term vanishes as
k\to\infty

∎
