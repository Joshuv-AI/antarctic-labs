Distributionally Robust Games via Coherent Risk Measures
Bharat Gangwani, Arunesh Sinha

Abstract
We study strategic interaction in data-driven games where players face uncertainty about payoff distributions inferred from finite samples. To model calibrated attitudes toward such uncertainty, we formulate distributionally robust games with a special focus on coherent utility (risk) measures, including Mean–semideviation and Conditional Value-at-Risk. This framework treats risk sensitivity as a primitive feature of player preferences while retaining a formal connection to distributional robustness. We make a number of contributions that are enumerated next. (1) We use prior results for the existence of distributionally robust equilibria to show the existence of equilibria in data-driven settings for various ambiguity sets, and (2) show that these games are inherently continuous, rather than finite matrix games, which fundamentally alters equilibrium structure and precludes direct extensions of standard correlated equilibrium notions. (3) We bound the loss in expected utility that a player can expect from being risk-averse. (4) We further characterize the computational complexity of equilibrium computation, proving PPAD-completeness in general and PPAD membership for several coherent utility measure games. (5) We present multilinear complementarity program formulations for several coherent utility measure games. (6) Numerical experiments reveal the robustness and out of sample performance of the game solutions. Our results unify risk-theoretic modeling and equilibrium analysis, providing a principled foundation for risk-aware strategic decision-making in data-driven environments.

Introduction

In recent years, data-driven decision-making has become a central paradigm across economics, operations research (OR), and artificial intelligence (AI). As decisions increasingly rely on empirical data rather than fully known models, understanding and mitigating the impact of distributional uncertainty has emerged as a crucial challenge. In the context of single-agent decision problems, a rich line of research has developed robust and risk-sensitive formulations, starting from classical Markowitz mean-variance
model
24
to the more recent unifying framework of coherent risk measures
5
, which includes risk measures such as Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR) or Mean-semideviation. These formulations have played a foundational role in capturing aversion to uncertainty in finance
1
, OR
10
, and also AI
18
.

However, the extension of such distributional robustness principles based on risk measures to multi-agent or strategic settings remains limited. While preliminary works have begun investigating robust equilibria and uncertainty-aware games
27
, the integration of coherent risk measures into game-theoretic formulations remains nascent. This gap is especially consequential because coherent risk measures combine axiomatic rigor with interpretability, features that are indispensable for modeling strategic decision-making under uncertainty. In particular, coherent risk measures provide an interpretable parameterization of risk attitudes, such as tail or downside sensitivity, so that even when equilibrium responses are non-monotone in the degree of risk aversion, changes in outcomes can be directly attributed to shifts in players’ risk preferences. By contrast, probability distance-based ambiguity sets (e.g., Wasserstein) entangle preference effects with statistical uncertainty, complicating comparative statics in strategic settings. We also note that, under standard duality results, optimization with a coherent risk measure is formally equivalent to a distributionally robust formulation with an appropriately constructed ambiguity set around a nominal distribution
29
, although our analysis adopts the risk-measure perspective rather than the distributionally robust optimization interpretation.

In this work, we explore distributional robustness in data driven games with a focus on risk-measure based games. In a reward setting, risk measures are called coherent utility measure. In the data-driven setting, we assume
K
samples of the payoff matrix and treat the empirical distribution as the nominal distribution for various ambiguity sets. However, as noted above, our focus is often on the coherent utility (risk-measure) perspective in which an inner optimization over the ambiguity set has a readily available closed form solution. These risk measures are widely studied in the finance and risk-management literature and offer easy interpretation, with mean–semideviation capturing downside risk and conditional value-at-risk (CVaR) quantifying tail risk. In this work, we will use three measures: (1) Mean-semideviation, (2) Mean-deviation, and (3) CVaR as three instances of coherent utility measures. The contributions in this work are listed below:

1.

Utilizing prior existence results in general
m
-player finite one-shot distributionally robust game (DRG) and distributionally robust equilibrium (DRE), we
show that in the data-driven setting with
K
samples, the existence result applies to various ambiguity sets, such as those formed using f-divergences, Wasserstein distance, and particularity coherent utility measures.

2.

We show that DRGs can be viewed as a continuous game “lifted” to the mixed strategy space, which precludes defining correlated equilibrium in DRGs using the standard approach in finite games, and requires continuous game machinery.

3.

We bound the loss in expected utility that a player can expect from being risk-averse in CUMGs (Theorem
7
).

4.

We show that approximate equilbrium computation in DRGs is in general PPAD-complete and for the subset of truly uncertain games (non-singleton ambiguity set) we show that our three Coherent Utility Measure Games (CUMG) are all in PPAD (Theorem
8
).

5.

We obtain finite multilinear complementarity programs for the three CUMGs we consider and note that these programs do not become linear for two players, unlike in standard games.

6.

Numerical experiments show that CUMG games can make the equilibria set more robust to data perturbations and improve out-of-sample (OOS) performance. CVaR games also allow for simultaneous calibration of tail-risk aversion and hedging behavior and provide probabilistic guarantees in terms of
\alpha-
quantile for payoffs in equilibrium.

The contributions above hint that this class of games appears to share characteristics of both finite and continuous games, placing these games in an intermediate regime between them. Overall, our framework unifies coherent risk modeling and strategic interaction, offering a systematic approach to incorporating interpretable risk aversion into data-driven games. By grounding strategic behavior in well-established risk-theoretic principles, it provides both interpretability and analytical structure, enabling uncertainty-aware equilibrium analysis without relying solely on probability distance based ambiguity set descriptions.

Preliminaries and Problem Formulation

Preliminaries of coherent utility (risk) measures
: Here we describe coherent risk measures as studied in a single agent setting. Further, we define them in terms of gain or utility (more common in game theory), which are sometimes referred to as coherent utility measures
9
, unlike the more popular cost setting in the risk-measure literature. Let
\bar{\mathbb{R}}
denote extended reals.
Let
\mathcal{X}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be the space of measurable functions on some sample space
\Omega
with a given
\sigma
-algebra; intuitively,
\mathcal{X}
can be thought of as all possible random variables over all possible probability measures. A utility functional
\rho:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\bar{\mathbb{R}}
is coherent iff it satisfies the following properties for any
X,Y\in\mathcal{X}
where
X,Y
represent random utilities:

(A1)

Concavity:

\rho(\alpha X+(1-\alpha)Y)\geq\alpha\rho(X)+(1-\alpha)\rho(Y)
, and
\alpha\in[0,1]

(A2)

Monotonicity:
If
Y(\omega)\geq X(\omega),\forall\omega\in\Omega
, then
\rho(Y)\geq\rho(X)

(A3)

Translation equivariance:
If
a\in\mathbb{R}
,
\rho(X+a)=\rho(X)+a

(A4)

Positive homogenity:
If
t>0
and
X\in\mathcal{X}
, then
\rho(tX)=t\rho(X)

Coherent utility measures as defined above also admit a dual form, which connects them to distributional robustness. Take any
nominal
probability measure
\mathbb{P}
and consider the space of measurable functions
\mathcal{L}^{p}(\Omega,\Sigma,\mathbb{P})
for
p\in[1,\infty)
, where
X\in\mathcal{L}^{p}(\Omega,\Sigma,\mathbb{P})
means that the p-absolute moment of
X
is bounded:
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[|X|^{p}]<\infty
.
Also, a concave function
f
is proper iff
\text{dom}(f)\neq\emptyset
and
f(\cdot)<+\infty
for all points in its domain. Here,
\text{dom}(f)=\{x\mid f(x)>-\infty\}
refers to the effective domain of
f
.
The following result follows from prior work:

Theorem 1
.

(
29
, Theorem 2.2)
Let
\mathcal{P}
be the set of probability measures over
(\Omega,\Sigma)
. Let
\mathcal{Y}=\left\{\mu\mid\frac{d\mu}{d\mathbb{P}}\in\mathcal{L}^{q}(\Omega,\Sigma,\mathbb{P})\right\}
be a set of measures, where
\frac{d\mu}{d\mathbb{P}}
is the Radon-Nikodym derivative (probability density in non-measure theoretic sense). A proper, upper-semicontinuous, and concave utility functional
\rho
is coherent if and only if it can be represented as

\rho(X)=\inf_{\mathbb{Q}\in U}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[X],\ \forall X\in\mathcal{L}^{p}(\Omega,\Sigma,\mathbb{P})

where
U=\left\{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{P}\cap\mathcal{Y}\mid\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[X]\geq\rho(X),\forall X\in\mathcal{L}^{p}(\Omega,\Sigma,\mathbb{P})\right\}
is a subset of probability measures.

The result above means that coherent utility (risk) measures are equivalent to a distributionally robust utility (cost) for a given uncertainty set
U
defined w.r.t. a nominal distribution
\mathbb{P}
. Notably, the Radon-Nikodym derivative (or density)
\frac{d\mathbb{Q}}{d\mathbb{P}}
exists and is bounded, i.e.,
\frac{d{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{Q}}}{d\mathbb{P}}\in\mathcal{L}^{q}(\Omega,\Sigma,\mathbb{P})
. In particular, a consequence of this existence, which we will use in the sequel, is that
\mathbb{Q}
must be absolutely continuous w.r.t.
\mathbb{P}
, meaning the support of
\mathbb{Q}
is a subset of the support of
\mathbb{P}
.

Structural Results

In this section, we establish structural results relating to Distributionally Robust Equilibria in general with a focus on coherent utility measures.

3.1
Nature of Distributionally Robust Game and Existence of Equilibrium

\displaystyle=\inf_{\mathbb{Q}\in U}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left[u_{i}(\lambda\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{i}+(1-\lambda)\mathbf{x}^{\prime\prime}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i}\mid\xi)\right]

\displaystyle=\inf_{\mathbb{Q}\in U}\left[\lambda\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left[u_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i}\mid\xi)\right]+(1-\lambda)\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left[u_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime\prime}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i}\mid\xi)\right]\right]

\displaystyle\geq\inf_{\mathbb{Q}\in U}\lambda\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left[u_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i}\mid\xi)\right]+\inf_{\mathbb{Q}\in U}(1-\lambda)\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left[u_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime\prime}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i}\mid\xi)\right]

\displaystyle=\lambda\rho_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i})+(1-\lambda)\rho_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime\prime}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i})

The inequality above follows from the standard result
\inf\sum f_{i}(x)\geq\sum\inf f_{i}(x)
.
Second, we show the
continuity
of
\rho_{i}
in its arguments using a much more succinct proof than shown in
28
. Recall that
\mu^{\mathbb{Q}}_{i}(\mathbf{a})=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[u_{i}(\mathbf{a}\mid\xi)]
, and consider the set of real-valued vectors
E=\left\{\left(\mu^{\mathbb{Q}}_{i}(\mathbf{a})\right)_{\mathbf{a}\in\mathbf{A}}\mid\mathbb{Q}\in U\right\}
. It can be easily seen that

\rho_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i})=\inf_{\mathbb{Q}\in U}\sum_{\mathbf{a}\in\mathbf{A}}\mu^{\mathbb{Q}}_{i}(\mathbf{a})\prod_{s=1}^{m}x_{s}(a_{j_{s}})=\inf_{(\mu^{\mathbb{Q}}_{i}(\mathbf{a}))_{\mathbf{a}\in\mathbf{A}}\in E}\sum_{\mathbf{a}\in\mathbf{A}}\mu^{\mathbb{Q}}_{i}(\mathbf{a})\prod_{s=1}^{m}x_{s}(a_{j_{s}}).

Because of the bounded first moment condition in the theorem,
E
is a bounded set in
\mathbb{R}^{|\mathbf{A}|}
. Consider the closure
\bar{E}
of set
E
, clearly
\bar{E}
is compact; also inf over
E
is the same as inf over
\bar{E}
, which implies that

\rho_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i})=\inf_{(v_{\mathbf{a}})_{\mathbf{a}\in\mathbf{A}}\in\bar{E}}\sum_{\mathbf{a}\in\mathbf{A}}v_{\mathbf{a}}\prod_{s=1}^{m}x_{s}(a_{j_{s}}).

Next, using the fact that
g(t)=\inf_{v\in V}f(t,v)
is continuous if
f
is continuous and
V
is compact, we can equate

to
(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i})
and

to
\bar{E}
to claim continuity of
\rho_{i}
.
Now, we have all the ingredients to invoke Kakutani’s Fixed Point Theorem, which proves the existence of a fixed point of the best response correspondence and hence the existence of equilibrium.
∎

Remark 1
.

Many cases easily fall out from the above result for the empirical distribution centered ball ambiguity set. Recall that
\mathbb{P}
is the empirical distribution, and since the samples are finitely many,
\mathbb{P}
has a finite support. Also, all samples have finite values, thus, we get that
\mu^{\mathbb{P}}_{i}(\mathbf{a})
is bounded. Below we show cases for which the condition in Theorem
6
is satisfied and hence DRE exists.

1.

(f-divergence ball) Any distribution
\mathbb{Q}
within the f-divergence ball (

) is absolutely continuous w.r.t.
\mathbb{P}
, meaning support of
\mathbb{Q}
is a subset of support of
\mathbb{P}
. Thus,
\mathbb{Q}
is a discrete probability distribution over a finite support. Thus, the first moment
\mu^{\mathbb{Q}}_{i}(\mathbf{a})
of any such distribution
\mathbb{Q}
is bounded.

2.

(Wasserstein ball) It is a well known result that
|\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}(g(\xi))-\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}(g(\xi))|\leq L_{g}W_{1}(\mathbb{Q},\mathbb{P})
for any random variable
\xi
and
L_{g}
-Lipschitz function
g
, which follows from Kantorovich duality for Wasserstein-1 distance, and also
W_{1}(\mathbb{Q},\mathbb{P})\leq W_{p}(\mathbb{Q},\mathbb{P})
for any
\mathbb{Q},\mathbb{P}
and any
p>1
. Taken together, this implies that if
u_{i}(\mathbf{a}\mid\xi)
is Lipschitz in
\xi
and
\mu^{\mathbb{P}}_{i}(\mathbf{a})
is bounded then
\mu^{\mathbb{Q}}_{i}(\mathbf{a})
is also bounded for any distribution
\mathbb{Q}
in the Wasserstein ball of finite radius centered at
\mathbb{P}
.

3.

(Coherent utility (risk) measures) As stated in the paragraph after Theorem
5
, coherent utility measures also have a distributionally robust form with the uncertainty set
U
containing distributions that are absolutely continuous w.r.t.
\mathbb{P}
. Then, as explained for f-divergence balls, the first moment
\mu^{\mathbb{Q}}_{i}(\mathbf{a})
of any such distribution
\mathbb{Q}\in U
is bounded.

3.2
Towards Correlated Equilibrium

In this section, we show that correlated equilibrium
6
for DRGs cannot be defined as in finite matrix games. Instead, DRG can be viewed as a lifted game with players’ action spaces redefined as their probability simplices, replacing their original pure action spaces. Consequently, DRG correlated equilibrium follows the standard definition of correlated equilibrium in continuous games
16
. Even in finite games, correlated equilbria can be defined using deviation functions and are a special case of the general
\Phi
-equilibrium
15
. In particular, given an action recommendation (sampled from the correlated equilibrium), it suffices to show that deviations to pure strategies are not beneficial. Due to the linearity of the mixed-strategy payoff in pure action profile payoffs, non-beneficial pure strategy deviations prevent the existence of profitable deviations to mixed strategies. As we had shown earlier, this linearity fails in DRGs and prevents us from using the same definition. We illustrate the issue here with the game from Example
4
.

Consider the correlated strategy
\mu
such that
\mu(U,L)=\mu(D,R)=0.5
in the two player DRG game (ambiguity set
[0.3,0.5]
) of Example
4
. The marginal strategies are
\mu(L\mid U)=1
and
\mu(R\mid D)=1
. From Equation
26
, it can be seen that
\rho_{i}(U,L)=0.3=\rho_{i}(D,L)
and also
\rho_{i}(D,R)=0.3=\rho_{i}(U,R)
for both players. Then, for the row player recommended to play
U
, we have
\mu(L\mid U)\rho_{1}(U,L)\geq\mu(L\mid U)\rho_{1}(D,L)
and when recommended to play
D
, we have
\mu(R\mid D)\rho_{i}(D,R)\geq\mu(R\mid D)\rho_{i}(U,R)
. Similar result holds for the column player, and hence this is a correlated equilibrium according to this finite matrix game definition of a correlated equilibrium. However, the mixed strategy
\mathbf{x}_{1}
for row player with
x_{1}(U)=0.5
gives
\rho_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1},L)=0.5
and then
\mu(L\mid U)\rho_{1}(U,L)\ngeq\mu(L\mid U)\rho_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1},L)
. Thus, deviation to
\mathbf{x}_{1}
is beneficial. This counterexample motivates the definition for a correlated equilibrium in DRG as defined for continuous games.

Correlated Equilibrium in Distributionally Robust Games
: Let
\phi_{i}:\mathbf{X}_{i}\rightarrow\mathbf{X}_{i}
be a measurable function.
A
correlated equilibrium
of a distributionally robust game is a joint probability measure
\mathbb{C}
over the space of players’ mixed strategies
\mathbf{X}
(in the lifted game) such that

\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{C}}[\rho_{i}(\phi_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i}),\mathbf{x}_{-i})-\rho_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i})]\leq 0

for all
i\in\{1,\dots,m\}
and all measurable
\phi_{i}
. Note that
\phi_{i}
here captures the notion of a player deviating from their “recommended” mixed strategy to another mixed strategy.
The measurability of
\phi_{i}
is a required property of deviations in continuous games
(
32
)
.

Infinite-support correlated equilibrium
:
We now revisit Example
4
in the mean-semideviation case with
\hat{p}=1/2
. Let
x_{1}(U)
denote the probability that the row player assigns to
U
and let
x_{2}(L)
denote the probability that the column player assigns to
L
. From the payoff expression derived for the MSD version of this example, both players have utility

\rho_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2})=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\gamma_{s}}{4}\left|\bigl(1-2x_{1}(U)\bigr)\bigl(1-2x_{2}(L)\bigr)\right|.

Thus, for any fixed
\mathbf{x}_{2}\in\mathbf{X}_{2}
, the row player’s utility is maximized by any
\mathbf{x}_{1}
satisfying
x_{1}(U)=1/2
. Similarly, whenever
x_{1}(U)=1/2
, the column player’s utility is equal to
1/2
for every
\mathbf{x}_{2}\in\mathbf{X}_{2}
.

Let
\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{1}\in\mathbf{X}_{1}
be given by
\bar{x}_{1}(U)=\bar{x}_{1}(D)=1/2
, and let
\mathbb{C}
be the probability measure obtained by placing
\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{1}
on the row player and drawing
\mathbf{x}_{2}
uniformly from
\mathbf{X}_{2}
. Equivalently,
\mathbb{C}
is supported on the segment
\{(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2}):\mathbf{x}_{2}\in\mathbf{X}_{2}\}
.
We show that
\mathbb{C}
satisfies the continuous-game correlated equilibrium condition. For player
1
, any measurable deviation
\phi_{1}:\mathbf{X}_{1}\rightarrow\mathbf{X}_{1}
maps the only recommended row strategy
\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{1}
to some mixed strategy
\phi_{1}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{1})
. Since
\bar{x}_{1}(U)=1/2
maximizes
\rho_{1}(\cdot,\mathbf{x}_{2})
for every
\mathbf{x}_{2}
, we have

\rho_{1}(\phi_{1}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{1}),\mathbf{x}_{2})-\rho_{1}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2})\leq 0\quad\text{for all }\mathbf{x}_{2}\in\mathbf{X}_{2}.

Taking expectation with respect to
\mathbb{C}
gives the correlated-equilibrium inequality for player
1
. For player
2
, since
\bar{x}_{1}(U)=1/2
, we have
\rho_{2}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{1},\phi_{2}(\mathbf{x}_{2}))=\rho_{2}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2})=1/2
for every measurable deviation
\phi_{2}:\mathbf{X}_{2}\rightarrow\mathbf{X}_{2}
. Hence the correlated-equilibrium inequality also holds for player
2
.

Therefore,
\mathbb{C}
is a correlated equilibrium of the lifted continuous game. Its support is the segment
\{(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2}):\mathbf{x}_{2}\in\mathbf{X}_{2}\}
, which is infinite. This illustrates that correlated equilibria in DRGs are naturally probability measures over the continuous strategy space
\mathbf{X}
, rather than finite distributions over the original pure-action profiles. While general approaches of computation of correlated equilibrium in continuous games can be used here
(
32
)
, further exploration of the computation of correlated equilibria specifically in distributionally robust games, as well as the existence of small-support correlated equilibria, remains open for future research.

4.1

In this sub-section, we assume that

Proposition 2

Under assumptions in this sub-section and if
\forall i

\rho_{i}
is polynomial time computable and Lipschitz, then finding an approximate DRE for data driven DRG games with any ambiguity set is PPAD-complete.

Proof.

The proof relies on prior results that approximate equilibrium in matrix games are PPAD-complete and also in concave games with strong separation oracle (
ConcaveGames with SO
) as defined in
26

\gamma

For our choice of empirical distribution as the nominal distribution, this means that the ambiguity set is just the empirical distribution and by choosing a single sample this distribution can be chosen to realize any expected payoff. Thus, any matrix game is also a DRG. This poly time reduction yields that DRGs are PPAD-Hard.

Next, DRGs can be reduced to concave games because of the following reasoning. It is straightforward to check the convexity and compactness of the strategy sets. A strong separation oracle, as defined in
26
,
for the strategy set is also available due to the convexity of the strategy set. The well-boundedness of the strategy set (as defined in
26

5
in the appendix. The proof of Theorem

are continuous and concave, and bounded (boundedness allows for scaling, if needed). The assumption ensures that utility is polynomial time comptuable and Lipschitz, and hence approximated by linear arithmetic circuits

While the above results is very general, it is also somewhat contrived as it relies on singleton ambiguity sets and leaves open the question about complexity of the subset of DRGs that have true uncertainty, that is, non-singleton ambiguity sets. However, any such result would require reasoning about properties of specific ambiguity set under consideration. As our focus in this work is on CUMG, we show PPAD complexity results for the three CUMG in Example

p_{k}\coloneq\mathbb{P}(\xi=\xi_{k})
. Define
\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},z)\coloneq z+\frac{1}{\alpha}\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{k}\,\min\!\big(0,\,u_{i}(\mathbf{x}\mid\xi_{k})-z\big)

Then, we can write

\mathrm{CVaR}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x})=\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}}\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},z).

Let
z^{\star}(\mathbf{x})\in\arg\max_{z}\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},z)
.
By Danskin’s theorem, a supergradient
g(\mathbf{x})\in\partial\,\mathrm{CVaR}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x})
is

g(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{\alpha}\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{k}\,\theta_{k}\,\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}u_{i}(\mathbf{x}\mid\xi_{k}),\quad\mbox{where}\quad\theta_{k}\in\begin{cases}\{1\},&u_{i}(\mathbf{x}\mid\xi_{k})<z^{\star}(\mathbf{x}),\\
\{0\},&u_{i}(\mathbf{x}\mid\xi_{k})>z^{\star}(\mathbf{x}),\\
[0,1],&u_{i}(\mathbf{x}\mid\xi_{k})=z^{\star}(\mathbf{x}).\end{cases}

Based on the reasoning above,
||\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}u_{i}(\mathbf{x}\mid\xi_{k})||\leq DM
for any
k
. Thus,
||g(x)||\leq DM/\alpha
. This is also the Lipschitz constant of
\mathrm{CVaR}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x})
.
Then,
(1-\gamma_{c})\,\mu_{i}^{\mathbb{P}}(\mathbf{x})+\gamma_{c}\,\mathrm{CVaR}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x})
is Lipschitz with constant
(1-\gamma_{c}+\frac{\gamma_{c}}{\alpha})DM
.
∎

4.2
Complementarity Program based Equilibrium Computation

In this sub-section, for the sake of easy notation, we assume that
0\leq u_{i}(\mathbf{a\mid\xi})\leq 1
for all
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbf{A}
.
We investigate computation of Distributionally Robust Equilibria (DRE) in coherent utility measures games, since these games provide a ready closed form utility for the inner optimization over the ambiguity set. In particular, we explore the technique of complementarity programs, focusing on the CUMG in Example
3
. Before diving into the details, we highlight a few high level aspects. As noted earlier in Proposition
3
, DRG (including CUMG) are continuous games and continuous games are typically not solved as a complementarity program due to the infinite (often uncountable) pure strategy space. However, DRGs can be viewed as a “lifted game” where the pure strategy space of the DRG is the mixed strategy space
\prod_{i=1}^{m}\mathbf{X}_{i}
of an underlying finite game. Indeed, Theorem
6
shows the existence of a pure strategy equilibrium in this lifted continuous game. In the sequel, we show that this view enables the complementary program approach. Further, since we showed that approximate DRE computation is PPAD complete, it is satisfying to obtain the multilinear complementarity program (MLCP) formulation for equilibrium computation.

First, note that for a fixed opponents’ profile
\mathbf{x}_{-i}
, player
i
’s best-response problem in the data-driven DRG is

\max_{\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathbf{X}_{i}}\rho_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i}),\text{ where }\rho_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i})=\rho(u_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i}\mid\xi))\text{ and }\mathbf{X}_{i}=\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}}:\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{i}=1,\ \mathbf{x}_{i}\geq 0\}.

Since
u_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i}\mid\xi)
is affine in
\mathbf{x}_{i}
and
\rho
is concave,
\rho_{i}(\cdot,\mathbf{x}_{-i})
is concave. Thus, the following lemma gives the KKT/complementarity characterization of DRE.

Lemma 2

(General complementarity forms for data-driven DRGs)
.

As defined earlier, consider a data-driven DRG induced by a coherent utility measure
\rho
with finite samples
\{\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{K}\}
and empirical nominal distribution
\mathbb{P}
. Then a profile
\mathbf{x}^{*}
is a DRE iff the following conditon holds

Primal form.
There exist
\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
and a supergradient
\mathbf{g}_{i}\in\partial_{\mathbf{x}_{i}}\rho_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{*},\mathbf{x}_{-i}^{*})
such that

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{*}=1,\qquad\mathbf{x}_{i}^{*}\geq 0,

(10a)

\displaystyle 0\leq\alpha_{i}-g_{i,l}\perp x_{i}^{*}(a_{l})\geq 0,\quad\forall l\in\{1,\dots,n_{i}\}.

(10b)

Here
\alpha_{i}
is the Lagrange multiplier for the simplex equality constraint in player
i
’s best-response problem at equilibrium. Moreover,
\alpha_{i}=\max_{1\leq l\leq n_{i}}g_{i,l}
; hence every action used with positive probability has maximal risk-adjusted marginal payoff.

Dual form.
For the case of CUMG we consider,
\rho
admits the dual representation in Theorem
5
with risk envelope
U
as defined earlier. Since the nominal distribution is supported on
\{\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{K}\}
, any
\mathbb{Q}\ll\mathbb{P}
is identified with a vector
\mathbf{q}=(q_{1},\dots,q_{K})
, where
q_{k}=\mathbb{Q}(\xi_{k})
. For player
i
, define worst-case dual distributions for player
i
at
\mathbf{x}^{*}
:

U_{i}^{*}(\mathbf{x}^{*})=\argmin_{\mathbf{q}_{i}\in U}\sum_{k=1}^{K}q_{i,k}u_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{*},\mathbf{x}_{-i}^{*}\mid\xi_{k}).

For
\mathbf{q}_{i}\in U_{i}^{*}(\mathbf{x}^{*})
, define risk-adjusted action values
\mathbf{v}_{i}(\mathbf{q}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i}^{*})\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}}
componentwise for each action
a_{l}
by

v_{i,l}(\mathbf{q}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i}^{*})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}q_{i,k}u_{i}(a_{l},\mathbf{x}_{-i}^{*}\mid\xi_{k}),\quad l\in\{1,\dots,n_{i}\}.

Then there exist
\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
and
risk-adjusted action values
\mathbf{v}_{i}\in\operatorname{conv}\{\mathbf{v}_{i}(\mathbf{q}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i}^{*}):\mathbf{q}_{i}\in U_{i}^{*}(\mathbf{x}^{*})\}
such that

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{*}=1,\qquad\mathbf{x}_{i}^{*}\geq 0,

(11a)

\displaystyle 0\leq\alpha_{i}-v_{i,l}\perp x_{i}^{*}(a_{l})\geq 0,\quad\forall l\in\{1,\dots,n_{i}\}.

(11b)

The multiplier
\alpha_{i}
in this dual form is the same simplex KKT multiplier as in the primal form; the difference is that the supergradient vector
\mathbf{g}_{i}
is represented through the vector
\mathbf{v}_{i}
.
In particular, if
U_{i}^{*}(\mathbf{x}^{*})=\{\mathbf{q}_{i}^{*}\}
is a singleton, then
v_{i,l}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}q_{i,k}^{*}u_{i}(a_{l},\mathbf{x}_{-i}^{*}\mid\xi_{k})
for every
l\in\{1,\dots,n_{i}\}
.

Proof.

Fix a player
i
and the opponents’ strategy profile
\mathbf{x}_{-i}^{*}
. By definition of DRE,
\mathbf{x}^{*}
is a DRE if and only if
\mathbf{x}_{i}^{*}
solves
\max_{\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathbf{X}_{i}}\rho_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i}^{*})
for every player
i
. Since
u_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i}^{*}\mid\xi)
is affine in
\mathbf{x}_{i}
and
\rho
is concave,
\rho_{i}(\cdot,\mathbf{x}_{-i}^{*})
is concave. Therefore, the KKT conditions for this concave maximization problem over the simplex are necessary and sufficient.

Let
\mathbf{g}_{i}\in\partial_{\mathbf{x}_{i}}\rho_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{*},\mathbf{x}_{-i}^{*})
. The KKT conditions imply that there exist
\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
and nonnegative multipliers
\beta_{i,l}\geq 0
for the constraints
x_{i}(a_{l})\geq 0
such that
g_{i,l}-\alpha_{i}+\beta_{i,l}=0
and
\beta_{i,l}x_{i}^{*}(a_{l})=0
for all
l
. Eliminating
\beta_{i,l}
gives
0\leq\alpha_{i}-g_{i,l}\perp x_{i}^{*}(a_{l})\geq 0
, together with
\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{*}=1
and
\mathbf{x}_{i}^{*}\geq 0
. This proves the primal/supergradient form. Since
\alpha_{i}-g_{i,l}\geq 0
for all
l
,
\alpha_{i}
is an upper bound on all
g_{i,l}
. Also, because
\mathbf{x}_{i}^{*}
lies on the simplex, at least one action has positive probability, and complementarity gives equality for every such action. Hence
\alpha_{i}=\max_{1\leq l\leq n_{i}}g_{i,l}
.

We now derive the dual form. By Theorem
5
,
\rho(X)=\inf_{\mathbb{Q}\in U}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[X]
. In the finite-sample setting, this is equivalently

\rho_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i})=\inf_{\mathbf{q}_{i}\in U_{K}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}q_{i,k}u_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i}\mid\xi_{k}).

For fixed
\mathbf{x}_{-i}^{*}
, the inner objective is affine in
\mathbf{x}_{i}
. Therefore, Danskin’s theorem gives

\partial_{\mathbf{x}_{i}}\rho_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{*},\mathbf{x}_{-i}^{*})=\operatorname{conv}\{\mathbf{v}_{i}(\mathbf{q}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i}^{*}):\mathbf{q}_{i}\in U_{i}^{*}(\mathbf{x}^{*})\}.

Substituting any vector from this superdifferential into the primal form gives (
11
). If the worst-case dual distribution is unique, the active set
U_{i}^{*}(\mathbf{x}^{*})
is a singleton, so the convex hull reduces to the single vector generated by
\mathbf{q}_{i}^{*}
. This proves the stated dual form.
∎

Given the above, however, another popular approach of support enumeration is not readily applicable. This is because support enumeration requires any pure strategy in the support of the equilibrium mixed strategy to have same payoff as the mixed strategy. This does not hold in CUMGs as shown in Example
3
. We defer investigation of any possible extension of support enumeration or other possible approaches, such as gradient based or regret based or homotopy continuation approaches, for future work.

Next, we derive complementarity programs for CUMG in Example
3
from first principles for the sake of clarity; these complementarity programs turn out to be multi-linear, making them amenable to general-purpose solvers such as PATH
(
14
)
. In Appendix
C
, we show that these can also be derived as special cases of the dual formulation above. We also show (in Appendix
C
) that certain CUMGs, such as order-p mean-semideviation, can be expressed only as
nonlinear
complementarity programs, thus, not all CUMGs admit a multi-linear complementarity program formulation for equilibrium computation.

Related Work and Conclusion

Ambiguity sets for distributionally robust games in the literature are often specified in two ways: by optimal transport/statistical-distance balls centered on a nominal distribution or restrictions on moments of the distributions within the ambiguity set. In most such settings, the uncertainty over the payoffs is exogenous to the game.

Conclusion
: This work advances the study of strategic interaction under payoff uncertainty by placing coherent risk measures at the core of distributionally robust equilibrium analysis. Our results show that incorporating risk-sensitive preferences fundamentally alters the geometry and interpretation of equilibria: distributionally robust games are neither classical matrix games nor arbitrary continuous games, but occupy an intermediate regime. Viewing robustness through the lens of coherent utility measures clarifies how parameters such as downside or tail aversion shape equilibrium outcomes.
At the same time, the continuous nature of these games introduces new conceptual and computational challenges, including the failure of standard correlated equilibrium definitions and the inapplicability of familiar techniques such as Lemke–Howson, even in two-player settings. These challenges point to several promising directions for future research, such as designing scalable algorithms that exploit problem structure beyond complementarity formulations. Finally, extending the game framework to dynamic, repeated, or information-asymmetric settings (analogous to works
18
;
30
in single player setting) would allow risk-sensitive distributional robustness to interact with incentives over time, further enriching the modeling of strategic behavior in data-driven environments.

7
Introduction

In recent years, data-driven decision-making has become a central paradigm across economics, operations research (OR), and artificial intelligence (AI). As decisions increasingly rely on empirical data rather than fully known models, understanding and mitigating the impact of distributional uncertainty has emerged as a crucial challenge. In the context of single-agent decision problems, a rich line of research has developed robust and risk-sensitive formulations, starting from classical Markowitz mean-variance
model
24
to the more recent unifying framework of coherent risk measures
5
, which includes risk measures such as Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR) or Mean-semideviation. These formulations have played a foundational role in capturing aversion to uncertainty in finance
1
, OR
10
, and also AI
18
.

However, the extension of such distributional robustness principles based on risk measures to multi-agent or strategic settings remains limited. While preliminary works have begun investigating robust equilibria and uncertainty-aware games
27
, the integration of coherent risk measures into game-theoretic formulations remains nascent. This gap is especially consequential because coherent risk measures combine axiomatic rigor with interpretability, features that are indispensable for modeling strategic decision-making under uncertainty. In particular, coherent risk measures provide an interpretable parameterization of risk attitudes, such as tail or downside sensitivity, so that even when equilibrium responses are non-monotone in the degree of risk aversion, changes in outcomes can be directly attributed to shifts in players’ risk preferences. By contrast, probability distance-based ambiguity sets (e.g., Wasserstein) entangle preference effects with statistical uncertainty, complicating comparative statics in strategic settings. We also note that, under standard duality results, optimization with a coherent risk measure is formally equivalent to a distributionally robust formulation with an appropriately constructed ambiguity set around a nominal distribution
29
, although our analysis adopts the risk-measure perspective rather than the distributionally robust optimization interpretation.

In this work, we explore distributional robustness in data driven games with a focus on risk-measure based games. In a reward setting, risk measures are called coherent utility measure. In the data-driven setting, we assume
K
samples of the payoff matrix and treat the empirical distribution as the nominal distribution for various ambiguity sets. However, as noted above, our focus is often on the coherent utility (risk-measure) perspective in which an inner optimization over the ambiguity set has a readily available closed form solution. These risk measures are widely studied in the finance and risk-management literature and offer easy interpretation, with mean–semideviation capturing downside risk and conditional value-at-risk (CVaR) quantifying tail risk. In this work, we will use three measures: (1) Mean-semideviation, (2) Mean-deviation, and (3) CVaR as three instances of coherent utility measures. The contributions in this work are listed below:

1.

Utilizing prior existence results in general
m
-player finite one-shot distributionally robust game (DRG) and distributionally robust equilibrium (DRE), we
show that in the data-driven setting with
K
samples, the existence result applies to various ambiguity sets, such as those formed using f-divergences, Wasserstein distance, and particularity coherent utility measures.

2.

We show that DRGs can be viewed as a continuous game “lifted” to the mixed strategy space, which precludes defining correlated equilibrium in DRGs using the standard approach in finite games, and requires continuous game machinery.

3.

We bound the loss in expected utility that a player can expect from being risk-averse in CUMGs (Theorem
7
).

4.

We show that approximate equilbrium computation in DRGs is in general PPAD-complete and for the subset of truly uncertain games (non-singleton ambiguity set) we show that our three Coherent Utility Measure Games (CUMG) are all in PPAD (Theorem
8
).

5.

We obtain finite multilinear complementarity programs for the three CUMGs we consider and note that these programs do not become linear for two players, unlike in standard games.

6.

Numerical experiments show that CUMG games can make the equilibria set more robust to data perturbations and improve out-of-sample (OOS) performance. CVaR games also allow for simultaneous calibration of tail-risk aversion and hedging behavior and provide probabilistic guarantees in terms of
\alpha-
quantile for payoffs in equilibrium.

The contributions above hint that this class of games appears to share characteristics of both finite and continuous games, placing these games in an intermediate regime between them. Overall, our framework unifies coherent risk modeling and strategic interaction, offering a systematic approach to incorporating interpretable risk aversion into data-driven games. By grounding strategic behavior in well-established risk-theoretic principles, it provides both interpretability and analytical structure, enabling uncertainty-aware equilibrium analysis without relying solely on probability distance based ambiguity set descriptions.

Preliminaries and Problem Formulation

Preliminaries of coherent utility (risk) measures
: Here we describe coherent risk measures as studied in a single agent setting. Further, we define them in terms of gain or utility (more common in game theory), which are sometimes referred to as coherent utility measures
9
, unlike the more popular cost setting in the risk-measure literature. Let
\bar{\mathbb{R}}
denote extended reals.
Let
\mathcal{X}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be the space of measurable functions on some sample space
\Omega
with a given
\sigma
-algebra; intuitively,
\mathcal{X}
can be thought of as all possible random variables over all possible probability measures. A utility functional
\rho:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\bar{\mathbb{R}}
is coherent iff it satisfies the following properties for any
X,Y\in\mathcal{X}
where
X,Y
represent random utilities:

(A1)

Concavity:

\rho(\alpha X+(1-\alpha)Y)\geq\alpha\rho(X)+(1-\alpha)\rho(Y)
, and
\alpha\in[0,1]

(A2)

Monotonicity:
If
Y(\omega)\geq X(\omega),\forall\omega\in\Omega
, then
\rho(Y)\geq\rho(X)

(A3)

Translation equivariance:
If
a\in\mathbb{R}
,
\rho(X+a)=\rho(X)+a

(A4)

Positive homogenity:
If
t>0
and
X\in\mathcal{X}
, then
\rho(tX)=t\rho(X)

Coherent utility measures as defined above also admit a dual form, which connects them to distributional robustness. Take any
nominal
probability measure
\mathbb{P}
and consider the space of measurable functions
\mathcal{L}^{p}(\Omega,\Sigma,\mathbb{P})
for
p\in[1,\infty)
, where
X\in\mathcal{L}^{p}(\Omega,\Sigma,\mathbb{P})
means that the p-absolute moment of
X
is bounded:
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[|X|^{p}]<\infty
.
Also, a concave function
f
is proper iff
\text{dom}(f)\neq\emptyset
and
f(\cdot)<+\infty
for all points in its domain. Here,
\text{dom}(f)=\{x\mid f(x)>-\infty\}
refers to the effective domain of
f
.
The following result follows from prior work:

Theorem 5
.

(
29
, Theorem 2.2)
Let
\mathcal{P}
be the set of probability measures over
(\Omega,\Sigma)
. Let
\mathcal{Y}=\left\{\mu\mid\frac{d\mu}{d\mathbb{P}}\in\mathcal{L}^{q}(\Omega,\Sigma,\mathbb{P})\right\}
be a set of measures, where
\frac{d\mu}{d\mathbb{P}}
is the Radon-Nikodym derivative (probability density in non-measure theoretic sense). A proper, upper-semicontinuous, and concave utility functional
\rho
is coherent if and only if it can be represented as

\rho(X)=\inf_{\mathbb{Q}\in U}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[X],\ \forall X\in\mathcal{L}^{p}(\Omega,\Sigma,\mathbb{P})

where
U=\left\{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{P}\cap\mathcal{Y}\mid\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[X]\geq\rho(X),\forall X\in\mathcal{L}^{p}(\Omega,\Sigma,\mathbb{P})\right\}
is a subset of probability measures.

The result above means that coherent utility (risk) measures are equivalent to a distributionally robust utility (cost) for a given uncertainty set
U
defined w.r.t. a nominal distribution
\mathbb{P}
. Notably, the Radon-Nikodym derivative (or density)
\frac{d\mathbb{Q}}{d\mathbb{P}}
exists and is bounded, i.e.,
\frac{d{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{Q}}}{d\mathbb{P}}\in\mathcal{L}^{q}(\Omega,\Sigma,\mathbb{P})
. In particular, a consequence of this existence, which we will use in the sequel, is that
\mathbb{Q}
must be absolutely continuous w.r.t.
\mathbb{P}
, meaning the support of
\mathbb{Q}
is a subset of the support of
\mathbb{P}
.

u_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i})=\sum_{j_{1}=1}^{n_{1}}\dots\sum_{j_{m}=1}^{n_{m}}u_{i}(a_{j_{1}},\dots,a_{j_{m}})\cdot x_{1}(a_{j_{1}})\cdot\dots\cdot x_{m}(a_{j_{m}})=\sum_{\mathbf{a}\in\mathbf{A}}u_{i}(\mathbf{a})\prod_{s=1}^{m}x_{s}(a_{j_{s}})

(21)

Random utilities in games
: We consider utilities that are affected by
randomness exogenous to the game
.
Let
\xi
denote a random vector supported on
\Xi\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{k}
for some positive integer
k
. Let
\mathcal{M}(\Xi)
be the set of all probability measures on
\Xi
. All players are aware of
\Xi
. We abuse notation slightly to use the same letter
\xi
for the random variable and its realization. Then, player
i
’s utility given pure strategy profile
\mathbf{a}
and a realization
\xi
is
u_{i}(\mathbf{a}\mid\xi)
, which is function from
\prod_{i=1}^{m}\mathbf{A}_{i}\times\Xi
to
\mathbb{R}
. Consequently, given the utility function and a realization
\xi
, the player
i
’s expected utility is defined in a similar way to Equation (
21
).

u_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i}\mid\xi)=\sum_{\mathbf{a}\in\mathbf{A}}u_{i}(\mathbf{a\mid\xi})\prod_{s=1}^{m}x_{s}(a_{j_{s}})

(22)

Given some probability measure
\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{M}(\Xi)
, we can write,

\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left[u_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i}\mid\xi)\right]=\sum_{\mathbf{a}\in\mathbf{A}}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[u_{i}(\mathbf{a}\mid\xi)]\prod_{s=1}^{m}x_{s}(a_{j_{s}})

(23)

Here, we introduce the shorthand
\mu^{\mathbb{Q}}_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i})=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[u_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i}\mid\xi)]
and

\mu^{\mathbb{Q}}_{i}(\mathbf{a})=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[u_{i}(\mathbf{a}\mid\xi)]
.

Distributionally robust game (DRG) and equilibrium (DRE)
: A
distributionally robust game
(DRG) is one where there is uncertainty about the distribution of
\xi
. The uncertainty is described by an ambiguity set
U\subseteq\mathcal{M}(\Xi)
for possible probability measures over
\Xi
and is known by all players. In a
distributionally robust equilibrium
(DRE), all players maximize their expected utility under the worst probability measure in the ambiguity set. Hence,
(\mathbf{x}^{*}_{i},\mathbf{x}^{*}_{-i})
is a DRE if:

\mathbf{x}^{*}_{i}\in\argmax_{\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathbf{X}_{i}}\inf_{\mathbb{Q}\in U}\mu^{\mathbb{Q}}_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}^{*}_{-i}),\;\forall i\in\{1,\dots,m\}

(24)

We also define
\rho_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i})\coloneq\inf_{\mathbb{Q}\in U}\mu^{\mathbb{Q}}_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i})=\inf_{\mathbb{Q}\in U}\sum_{\mathbf{a}\in\mathbf{A}}\mu^{\mathbb{Q}}_{i}(\mathbf{a})\prod_{s=1}^{m}x_{s}(a_{j_{s}})
.

Coherent utility measure game (CUMG) and distributional robustness
:
The utility of mixed strategy
(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i})
for player
i
using a coherent utility measure
\rho
is given as

\rho_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i})=\rho(u_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i}\mid\xi))

(25)

Note that we overload the notation
\rho_{i}
above, which is justified because the game with the above
\rho_{i}
utility is
equivalent to playing a distributionally robust game due to the result of Theorem
5
. We call these games as
coherent utility measure games
(CUMG) and by Theorem
5
, CUMG is a DRG. A key structural property of CUMGs is that the optimization over the ambiguity set
U
has a closed form solution. We further note (and later prove in Proposition
3
) that any DRG, including CUMG, is a continuous game and not a matrix game. This means that the strategy space of any DRG is the continuous space of mixed strategies
\prod_{i=1}^{m}\mathbf{X}_{i}
and the utility
\rho_{i}
generally
cannot
be written as a convex combination of the utility of pure strategies in the support of the mixed strategy, as done for empirical games in Equation
23
.

Nature of ambiguity set
: In data driven settings, often the ambiguity set is given as
U=\{\mathbb{Q}\mid d(\mathbb{Q},\mathbb{P})\leq\eta\}
where the nominal
\mathbb{P}
is chosen as the
empirical distribution
given by the data samples and
d
is a distance between probability distributions; common choices for
d
include Wasserstein distance, f-divergences such as KL divergence,
\chi^{2}
divergence, and others. We call such ambiguity sets as
empirical distribution centered balls
. In particular, for coherent utility measures, one has a closed form solution for the inner optimization over the ambiguity set, yielding closed form utilities as in Equation
25
, with popular examples shown in Example
3
. We assume
K
data samples with the corresponding samples of utilities for player
i
for all strategy profiles
\mathbf{a}
given as
u_{i}(\mathbf{a}\mid\xi_{1}),\ldots,u_{i}(\mathbf{a}\mid\xi_{K})
. Then,
\mu^{\mathbb{P}}_{i}(\mathbf{a})=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[u_{i}(\mathbf{a}\mid\xi)]=(1/K)\sum_{k=1}^{K}u_{i}(\mathbf{a}\mid\xi_{k})
.

9
Structural Results

In this section, we establish structural results relating to Distributionally Robust Equilibria in general with a focus on coherent utility measures.

9.1
Nature of Distributionally Robust Game and Existence of Equilibrium

We start by showing that distributionally robust games are continuous games and not matrix games. Recall the notation
\mu^{\mathbb{Q}}_{i}(\mathbf{a})=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[u_{i}(\mathbf{a}\mid\xi)]
for strategy profile
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbf{A}
.

Proposition 3

(DRG are continuous games)
.

In general, DRG games are continuous games with pure strategy space as
\prod_{i=1}^{m}\mathbf{X}_{i}
and they cannot be viewed as a matrix game in which the pure strategy space is
\mathbf{A}
and payoffs are component-wise infima
\inf_{\mathbb{Q}\in U}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[u_{i}(\mathbf{a}\mid\xi)]
over the ambiguity set
\mathbb{Q}
. Mathematically,

\rho_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i})=\inf_{\mathbb{Q}\in U}\mu^{\mathbb{Q}}_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i})\geq\sum_{\mathbf{a}\in\mathbf{A}}\inf_{\mathbb{Q}\in U}\mu^{\mathbb{Q}}_{i}(\mathbf{a})\prod_{s=1}^{m}x_{s}(a_{j_{s}})

Example 4
.

At this point, we present an illustrative example elaborating the above result about the continuous nature of DRGs, which we will revisit at various points in the sequel. Consider the two sample DRG shown below.

\begin{array}[]{c@{\qquad}c@{\qquad}c}\begin{array}[]{c}\textbf{State A}\\[4.0pt]
\begin{array}[]{c|cc}&L&R\\

D&(0,0)&(1,1)\end{array}\end{array}&\begin{array}[]{c}\textbf{State B}\\[4.0pt]

given by

Here

,
where

0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

For any mixed-strategy profile

\mu_{i}^{\mathbb{Q}}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i})=\sum_{\mathbf{a}\in\mathbf{A}}\Bigl(\mathbb{Q}(\xi_{1})\,u_{i}(\mathbf{a}\mid\xi_{1})+\mathbb{Q}(\xi_{2})\,u_{i}(\mathbf{a}\mid\xi_{2})\Bigr)\prod_{s=1}^{m}x_{s}(a_{j_{s}}).

For the ambiguity set

the distributionally robust utility is

Using the utility definition above, we get

\rho_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i})=\inf_{p\in[0.3,0.7]}\Big[p\bigl(x_{1}(U)x_{2}(L)+x_{1}(D)x_{2}(R)\bigr)+(1-p)\bigl(x_{1}(U)x_{2}(R)+x_{1}(D)x_{2}(L)\bigr)\Big].

Further, using the fact that mixed strategy probabilities sum to one, we get

\rho_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i})=\inf_{p\in[0.3,0.7]}\left[\frac{1}{2}+\frac{2p-1}{2}\,\bigl(1-2x_{1}(U)\bigr)\bigl(1-2x_{2}(L)\bigr)\right].

(26)

The payoff

For the mean–semideviation CUMG with

Equilibrium
: Next, we state a general equilibrium existence result that has appeared in prior work
28
. This also establishes the existence of equilibrium for any coherent utility measure, as shown in the remark after the theorem below. Recall the notation
\mu^{\mathbb{Q}}_{i}(\mathbf{a})=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[u_{i}(\mathbf{a}\mid\xi)]
. We still provide a part of the proof that is much more succinct that the proof in
28

Theorem 6

(
28
)
.

Any finite m-player distributionally robust game with an underlying non-cooperative, simultaneous move, one-shot matrix game and an ambiguity set
U
has a distributionally robust equilibrium in mixed strategies, if for all
\mathbb{Q}\in U
the first moment of utilities for any pure strategy
\mathbf{a}
is bounded, i.e.,
|\mu^{\mathbb{Q}}_{i}(\mathbf{a})|\leq M<\infty
for all
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbb{Q}\in U
for some
M
.

Proof.

Following many standard proofs of Nash-type equilibrium existence, there are two critical results needed to invoke Kakutani’s Fixed Point Theorem for the best response correspondence and prove the existence of an equilibrium. We show these two critical result.
First, we show
concavity
of
\rho
in
\mathbf{x}_{i}
,

\displaystyle\rho_{i}(\lambda\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{i}+(1-\lambda)\mathbf{x}^{\prime\prime}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i})

\displaystyle=\inf_{\mathbb{Q}\in U}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left[u_{i}(\lambda\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{i}+(1-\lambda)\mathbf{x}^{\prime\prime}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i}\mid\xi)\right]

\displaystyle=\inf_{\mathbb{Q}\in U}\left[\lambda\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left[u_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i}\mid\xi)\right]+(1-\lambda)\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left[u_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime\prime}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i}\mid\xi)\right]\right]

\displaystyle\geq\inf_{\mathbb{Q}\in U}\lambda\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left[u_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i}\mid\xi)\right]+\inf_{\mathbb{Q}\in U}(1-\lambda)\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left[u_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime\prime}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i}\mid\xi)\right]

\displaystyle=\lambda\rho_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i})+(1-\lambda)\rho_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime\prime}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i})

The inequality above follows from the standard result
\inf\sum f_{i}(x)\geq\sum\inf f_{i}(x)
.
Second, we show the
continuity
of
\rho_{i}
in its arguments using a much more succinct proof than shown in
28
. Recall that
\mu^{\mathbb{Q}}_{i}(\mathbf{a})=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[u_{i}(\mathbf{a}\mid\xi)]
, and consider the set of real-valued vectors
E=\left\{\left(\mu^{\mathbb{Q}}_{i}(\mathbf{a})\right)_{\mathbf{a}\in\mathbf{A}}\mid\mathbb{Q}\in U\right\}
. It can be easily seen that

\rho_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i})=\inf_{\mathbb{Q}\in U}\sum_{\mathbf{a}\in\mathbf{A}}\mu^{\mathbb{Q}}_{i}(\mathbf{a})\prod_{s=1}^{m}x_{s}(a_{j_{s}})=\inf_{(\mu^{\mathbb{Q}}_{i}(\mathbf{a}))_{\mathbf{a}\in\mathbf{A}}\in E}\sum_{\mathbf{a}\in\mathbf{A}}\mu^{\mathbb{Q}}_{i}(\mathbf{a})\prod_{s=1}^{m}x_{s}(a_{j_{s}}).

Because of the bounded first moment condition in the theorem,
E
is a bounded set in
\mathbb{R}^{|\mathbf{A}|}
. Consider the closure
\bar{E}
of set
E
, clearly
\bar{E}
is compact; also inf over
E
is the same as inf over
\bar{E}
, which implies that

\rho_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i})=\inf_{(v_{\mathbf{a}})_{\mathbf{a}\in\mathbf{A}}\in\bar{E}}\sum_{\mathbf{a}\in\mathbf{A}}v_{\mathbf{a}}\prod_{s=1}^{m}x_{s}(a_{j_{s}}).

Next, using the fact that
g(t)=\inf_{v\in V}f(t,v)
is continuous if
f
is continuous and
V
is compact, we can equate
t
to
(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i})
and
V
to
\bar{E}
to claim continuity of
\rho_{i}
.
Now, we have all the ingredients to invoke Kakutani’s Fixed Point Theorem, which proves the existence of a fixed point of the best response correspondence and hence the existence of equilibrium.
∎

Remark 2
.

Many cases easily fall out from the above result for the empirical distribution centered ball ambiguity set. Recall that
\mathbb{P}
is the empirical distribution, and since the samples are finitely many,
\mathbb{P}
has a finite support. Also, all samples have finite values, thus, we get that
\mu^{\mathbb{P}}_{i}(\mathbf{a})
is bounded. Below we show cases for which the condition in Theorem
6
is satisfied and hence DRE exists.

1.

(f-divergence ball) Any distribution
\mathbb{Q}
within the f-divergence ball (
d(\mathbb{Q},\mathbb{P})\leq\xi
) is absolutely continuous w.r.t.
\mathbb{P}
, meaning support of
\mathbb{Q}
is a subset of support of
\mathbb{P}
. Thus,
\mathbb{Q}
is a discrete probability distribution over a finite support. Thus, the first moment
\mu^{\mathbb{Q}}_{i}(\mathbf{a})
of any such distribution
\mathbb{Q}
is bounded.

2.

(Wasserstein ball) It is a well known result that
|\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}(g(\xi))-\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}(g(\xi))|\leq L_{g}W_{1}(\mathbb{Q},\mathbb{P})
for any random variable
\xi
and
L_{g}
-Lipschitz function
g
, which follows from Kantorovich duality for Wasserstein-1 distance, and also
W_{1}(\mathbb{Q},\mathbb{P})\leq W_{p}(\mathbb{Q},\mathbb{P})
for any
\mathbb{Q},\mathbb{P}
and any
p>1
. Taken together, this implies that if
u_{i}(\mathbf{a}\mid\xi)
is Lipschitz in
\xi
and
\mu^{\mathbb{P}}_{i}(\mathbf{a})
is bounded then
\mu^{\mathbb{Q}}_{i}(\mathbf{a})
is also bounded for any distribution
\mathbb{Q}
in the Wasserstein ball of finite radius centered at
\mathbb{P}
.

3.

(Coherent utility (risk) measures) As stated in the paragraph after Theorem
5
, coherent utility measures also have a distributionally robust form with the uncertainty set
U
containing distributions that are absolutely continuous w.r.t.
\mathbb{P}
. Then, as explained for f-divergence balls, the first moment
\mu^{\mathbb{Q}}_{i}(\mathbf{a})
of any such distribution
\mathbb{Q}\in U
is bounded.

9.2
Towards Correlated Equilibrium

In this section, we show that correlated equilibrium
6
for DRGs cannot be defined as in finite matrix games. Instead, DRG can be viewed as a lifted game with players’ action spaces redefined as their probability simplices, replacing their original pure action spaces. Consequently, DRG correlated equilibrium follows the standard definition of correlated equilibrium in continuous games
16
. Even in finite games, correlated equilbria can be defined using deviation functions and are a special case of the general
\Phi
-equilibrium
15
. In particular, given an action recommendation (sampled from the correlated equilibrium), it suffices to show that deviations to pure strategies are not beneficial. Due to the linearity of the mixed-strategy payoff in pure action profile payoffs, non-beneficial pure strategy deviations prevent the existence of profitable deviations to mixed strategies. As we had shown earlier, this linearity fails in DRGs and prevents us from using the same definition. We illustrate the issue here with the game from Example
4
.

Consider the correlated strategy
\mu
such that
\mu(U,L)=\mu(D,R)=0.5
in the two player DRG game (ambiguity set
[0.3,0.5]
) of Example
4
. The marginal strategies are
\mu(L\mid U)=1
and
\mu(R\mid D)=1
. From Equation
26
, it can be seen that
\rho_{i}(U,L)=0.3=\rho_{i}(D,L)
and also
\rho_{i}(D,R)=0.3=\rho_{i}(U,R)
for both players. Then, for the row player recommended to play
U
, we have
\mu(L\mid U)\rho_{1}(U,L)\geq\mu(L\mid U)\rho_{1}(D,L)
and when recommended to play
D
, we have
\mu(R\mid D)\rho_{i}(D,R)\geq\mu(R\mid D)\rho_{i}(U,R)
. Similar result holds for the column player, and hence this is a correlated equilibrium according to this finite matrix game definition of a correlated equilibrium. However, the mixed strategy
\mathbf{x}_{1}
for row player with
x_{1}(U)=0.5
gives
\rho_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1},L)=0.5
and then
\mu(L\mid U)\rho_{1}(U,L)\ngeq\mu(L\mid U)\rho_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1},L)
. Thus, deviation to
\mathbf{x}_{1}
is beneficial. This counterexample motivates the definition for a correlated equilibrium in DRG based on continuous games.

Correlated Equilibrium in Distributionally Robust Games
: Let
\phi_{i}:\mathbf{X}_{i}\rightarrow\mathbf{X}_{i}
be a measurable function.
A
correlated equilibrium
of a distributionally robust game is a joint probability measure
\mathbb{C}
over the space of players’ mixed strategies
\mathbf{X}
(in the lifted game) such that

\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{C}}[\rho_{i}(\phi_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i}),\mathbf{x}_{-i})-\rho_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i})]\leq 0

for all
i\in\{1,\dots,m\}
and all measurable
\phi_{i}
. Note that
\phi_{i}
here captures the notion of a player deviating from their “recommended” mixed strategy to another mixed strategy.
The measurability of
\phi_{i}
is a required property of deviations in continuous games
32
. Further exploration of the computation of correlated equilibria in distributionally robust games, as well as the existence of small-support correlated equilibria, remains open for future research.

9.3
Approximations with Coherent Utility Measures

In this sub-section, for the sake of easy notation, we assume that
0\leq u_{i}(\mathbf{a\mid\xi})\leq 1
for all
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbf{A}
.
We provide results for coherent utility measure games (CUMGs) where the utility functional

Observe that if the penalty term is absent, then the CUMG becomes an

. The empirical game is a

\mathbf{s}^{*}
, which could possibly be an equilibrium computed from samples of payoff,

switches to
b_{\rho}(\mathbf{s}^{*}_{-i})

maximizes
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\rho(\cdot,\mathbf{s}^{*}_{-i}})

\mathbf{s}^{*}_{-i}
.
We state the result in Theorem
7
below for the true unknown probability distribution
\mathbb{T}
. We present

.

A PPAD-completeness result that follows from prior work

Proposition 4
.

Under assumptions in this sub-section and if
\forall i

\rho_{i}
is polynomial time computable and Lipschitz, then finding an approximate DRE for data driven DRG games with any ambiguity set is PPAD-complete.

Proof.

The proof relies on prior results that approximate equilibrium in matrix games are PPAD-complete and also in concave games with strong separation oracle (
ConcaveGames with SO
) as defined in
26
. We first note that matrix games are a subset of DRGs; it is straightforward to see this when the ambiguity set is a singleton (in CUMG, this would imply the
\gamma

For our choice of empirical distribution as the nominal distribution, this means that the ambiguity set is just the empirical distribution and by choosing a single sample this distribution can be chosen to realize any expected payoff. Thus, any matrix game is also a DRG. This poly time reduction yields that DRGs are PPAD-Hard.

Next, DRGs can be reduced to concave games because of the following reasoning. It is straightforward to check the convexity and compactness of the strategy sets. A strong separation oracle, as defined in
26
,
for the strategy set is also available due to the convexity of the strategy set. The well-boundedness of the strategy set (as defined in
26

5
in the appendix. The proof of Theorem

shows that the utility functions

are continuous and concave, and bounded (boundedness allows for scaling, if needed). The assumption ensures that utility is polynomial time comptuable and Lipschitz, and hence approximated by linear arithmetic circuits

. Then, this sandwich between two problem classes of PPAD-complexity makes approximate DRE also PPAD-complete.
∎

While the above results is very general, it is also somewhat contrived as it relies on singleton ambiguity sets and leaves open the question about complexity of the subset of DRGs that have true uncertainty, that is, non-singleton ambiguity sets. However, any such result would require reasoning about properties of specific ambiguity set under consideration. As our focus in this work is on CUMG, we show PPAD complexity results for the three CUMG in Example

and show that these games are in PPAD. The proof for this works by showing that any of these CUMG class of games with

We follow the proof of Proposition

10.2
Complementarity Program based Equilibrium Computation

In this sub-section, for the sake of easy notation, we assume that
0\leq u_{i}(\mathbf{a\mid\xi})\leq 1
for all
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbf{A}
.
We investigate computation of Distributionally Robust Equilibria (DRE) in coherent utility measures games, since these games provide a ready closed form utility for the inner optimization over the ambiguity set. In particular, we explore the technique of complementarity programs for the CUMG in Example
3
. Before diving into the details, we highlight a few high level aspects. As noted earlier in Proposition
3
, DRG (including CUMG) are continuous games and continuous games are typically not solved as a complementarity program due to the infinite (often uncountable) pure strategy space. However, DRGs can be viewed as a “lifted game” where the pure strategy space of the DRG is the mixed strategy space
\prod_{i=1}^{m}\mathbf{X}_{i}
of an underlying finite game. Indeed, Theorem
6
shows the existence of a pure strategy equilibrium in this lifted continuous game. In the sequel, we show that this view enables the complementary program approach. Further, since we showed that approximate DRE computation is PPAD complete, it is satisfying to obtain the multilinear complementarity program (MLCP) formulation for equilibrium computation.

Given this, however, another popular approach of support enumeration is not readily applicable. This is because support enumeration requires any pure strategy in the support of the equilibrium mixed strategy to have same payoff as the mixed strategy. This does not hold in CUMGs as shown in Example
3
. We defer investigation of any possible extension of support enumeration or other possible approaches, such as gradient based or regret based or homotopy continuation approaches, for future work.

Experiments

Intuitively, one expects players in risk-averse games to obtain benefits similar to those found in single-agent variance-penalized optimization, such as out-of-sample performance improvements, variance reduction, and probabilistic guarantees. Our numerical experiments present some evidence for such advantages of CUMG games in data-driven settings. We implement and solve for the equilibrium in three games.
First, with the underlying distribution estimated closely (large-
K
), the mean-semideviation (MSD) equilibrium set in a cordination game setup is more robust to distributional uncertainty over the player’s payoffs. Second, in small-
K
setting in a variant of prisoner’s dilemma, the players choose conservatively and, hence, achieve out-of-sample performance improvement of their actual reward by not overfitting to sampling noise. Finally, the CVaR game demonstrates a monotonic decrease in the player’s payoff variance in robustness parameter
\gamma_{c}
at equilibrium and yields probabilistic guarantees on the payoff in a general sum game.

Related Work and Conclusion

Ambiguity sets for distributionally robust games in the literature are often specified in two ways: by optimal transport/statistical-distance balls centered on a nominal distribution or restrictions on moments of the distributions within the ambiguity set. In most such settings, the uncertainty over the payoffs is exogenous to the game.
27
build and solve a Wasserstein distance-based DRG in a data-driven environment;
28
;
3
;
33
use moment constrained ambiguity sets in a DRG set-up. In the limit, these models converges to a robust game
2
as the probability mass concentrates on the worst outcome in the support.
23
combine moment-constrained ambiguity sets and CVaR objective within their DRG model. Despite using CVaR, their approach precludes a tractable closed form solution for the inner infimum. Other simultaneous, one-shot distributionally robust game settings in the literature include ambiguity sets based on f-divergence
7
and chance constraints
31
, and maximisation over parameterised Bayesian posteriors
21
. Recent work on DRG by
19
makes the uncertainty endogenous by introducing distributional robustness over other players’ mixed strategy profile at equilibrium. Distributional robustness has also been explored in the Stackelberg game
22
;
4
. Finally, a number of works also consider transition uncertainty in a repeated single player setting
17
;
20
, which has recently been extended to Markov games
8
. In contrast to these settings, this paper introduces tractable formulations of
one-shot, simultaneous move
DRG by using
coherent utility measures
with interpretable risk-aversion parameters.

Conclusion
: This work advances the study of strategic interaction under payoff uncertainty by placing coherent risk measures at the core of distributionally robust equilibrium analysis. Our results show that incorporating risk-sensitive preferences fundamentally alters the geometry and interpretation of equilibria: distributionally robust games are neither classical matrix games nor arbitrary continuous games, but occupy an intermediate regime. Viewing robustness through the lens of coherent utility measures clarifies how parameters such as downside or tail aversion shape equilibrium outcomes.
At the same time, the continuous nature of these games introduces new conceptual and computational challenges, including the failure of standard correlated equilibrium definitions and the inapplicability of familiar techniques such as Lemke–Howson, even in two-player settings. These challenges point to several promising directions for future research, such as designing scalable algorithms that exploit problem structure beyond complementarity formulations. Finally, extending the game framework to dynamic, repeated, or information-asymmetric settings (analogous to works
18
;
30
in single player setting) would allow risk-sensitive distributional robustness to interact with incentives over time, further enriching the modeling of strategic behavior in data-driven environments.

(51d)

Proposition 5

The standard projection (drop a co-ordinate)

f
on the probaility simplex
\Delta_{n-1}
can be given as

. Clearly,

B.1
Lemma
10

Lemma 10

(Concentration for mean absolute deviation (MAD))
.

For simpler notation, let
X
be a real-valued
reward
random variable with a true distribution
\mathbb{T}
.
Let there be i.i.d. samples
X_{1},\dots,X_{K}\sim\mathbb{T}
.
Assume
X\in[0,1]
, and let
\mu:=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{T}}[X]
,
\bar{X}:=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{i=1}^{K}X_{i}
and
\mathbb{V}^{\mathbb{T}}(X)
denote variance.
Define the true and empirical
L_{1}
-deviation about the mean by

\mathrm{MAD}^{\mathbb{T}}:=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{T}}\big[|X-\mu|\big]
and
\mathrm{MAD}^{\mathbb{P}}:=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{i=1}^{K}|X_{i}-\bar{X}|
respectively.
Then, for any
\delta\in(0,1)
, with probability at least
1-\delta

\big|\mathrm{MAD}^{\mathbb{T}}-\mathrm{MAD}^{\mathbb{P}}\big|\;\leq\;\sqrt{\frac{2\ln(2/\delta)}{K}}\;+\;\sqrt{\frac{\mathbb{V}^{\mathbb{T}}(X)}{K}.}

(52)

Proof.

We decompose the error into a
bias
term and a
concentration
term:

\big|\mathrm{MAD}^{\mathbb{T}}-\mathrm{MAD}^{\mathbb{P}}\big|\;\leq\;\big|\mathrm{MAD}^{\mathbb{T}}-\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{T}}[\mathrm{MAD}^{\mathbb{P}}]\big|\;+\;\big|\mathrm{MAD}^{\mathbb{P}}-\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{T}}[\mathrm{MAD}^{\mathbb{P}}]\big|

(53)

To handle the bias,
let

A_{K}(\mu):=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{i=1}^{K}|X_{i}-\mu|.

We first relate
A_{K}(\mu)
to
\mathrm{MAD}^{\mathbb{P}}=A_{K}(\bar{X})
. Using the reverse triangle inequality

||u|-|v||\leq|u-v|
with
u=X_{i}-\mu
and
v=X_{i}-\bar{X}
, we have for each
i
,

\big||X_{i}-\mu|-|X_{i}-\bar{X}|\big|\leq|\mu-\bar{X}|.

Note that
|u|-|v|\leq||u|-|v||
and
|v|-|u|\leq||u|-|v||
. Using this for LHS and
averaging over
i
yields the sample-wise inequality

A_{K}(\mu)-\mathrm{MAD}^{\mathbb{P}}\leq|\bar{X}-\mu|\;\mbox{ and }\;\mathrm{MAD}^{\mathbb{P}}-A_{K}(\mu)\leq|\bar{X}-\mu|.

(54)

Taking expectations in (
54
) and using
\mathrm{MAD}^{\mathbb{T}}=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{T}}[|X-\mu|]=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{T}}[A_{K}(\mu)]
gives

\big|\mathrm{MAD}^{\mathbb{T}}-\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{T}}[\mathrm{MAD}^{\mathbb{P}}]\big|\leq\,\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{T}}[|\bar{X}-\mu|].

(55)

Finally, by Jensen’s inequality
\mathbb{E}[|Y|]\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}]}
,

\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{T}}[|\bar{X}-\mu|]\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{T}}[(\bar{X}-\mu)^{2}]}=\sqrt{\mathrm{V}^{\mathbb{T}}(\bar{X})}=\frac{\sqrt{\mathrm{V}^{\mathbb{T}}(X)}}{\sqrt{K}}.

Substituting into (
55
) yields the bias bound

\big|\mathrm{MAD}^{\mathbb{T}}-\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{T}}[\mathrm{MAD}^{\mathbb{P}}]\big|\leq\frac{\sqrt{\mathrm{V}^{\mathbb{T}}(X)}}{\sqrt{K}}.

(56)

We bound the concentration term via McDiarmid’s inequality.
Define the function

f(x_{1},\dots,x_{K}):=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{i=1}^{K}|x_{i}-\bar{x}|,\qquad\bar{x}:=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{i=1}^{K}x_{i}.

We show that
f
satisfies bounded differences with constants
c_{k}=2/K
.
Fix
k
and let
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{K})
and
x^{\prime}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{k-1},x_{k}^{\prime},x_{k+1},\dots,x_{K})
where

x_{k},x_{k}^{\prime}\in[0,1]
. Let
\bar{x}
and
\bar{x}^{\prime}
be the corresponding means. Then

|\bar{x}-\bar{x}^{\prime}|=|x_{k}-x_{k}^{\prime}|/K\leq 1/K
.

Using
||u|-|v||\leq|u-v|
, for each
i\neq k
,

\big||x_{i}-\bar{x}|-|x_{i}-\bar{x}^{\prime}|\big|\leq|\bar{x}-\bar{x}^{\prime}|\leq\frac{1}{K},

so summing over
i\neq k
gives at most
(K-1)\cdot(1/K)
.
For the
k
-th term,

\big||x_{k}-\bar{x}|-|x_{k}^{\prime}-\bar{x}^{\prime}|\big|\leq|(x_{k}-\bar{x})-(x_{k}^{\prime}-\bar{x}^{\prime})|\leq|x_{k}-x_{k}^{\prime}|+|\bar{x}-\bar{x}^{\prime}|\leq 1+\frac{1}{K}.

Combining these and dividing by
K
,

|f(x)-f(x^{\prime})|\leq\frac{1}{K}\left((K-1)\frac{1}{K}+1+\frac{1}{K}\right){\color[rgb]{0,0,0}=}\frac{2}{K}.

Thus we may take
c_{k}:=2/K
for all
k
and applying McDiarmid’s inequality implies that for all
\varepsilon>0
,

\mathbb{P}\Big(\big|\mathrm{MAD}^{\mathbb{P}}-\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{MAD}^{\mathbb{P}}]\big|\geq\varepsilon\Big)\leq 2\exp\!\left(-\frac{2\varepsilon^{2}}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}c_{k}^{2}}\right)=2\exp\!\left(-\frac{2\varepsilon^{2}}{K\cdot(2/K)^{2}}\right)=2\exp\!\left(-\frac{K\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\right).

Setting the right-hand side equal to
\delta
and solving for
\varepsilon
yields that with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\big|\mathrm{MAD}^{\mathbb{P}}-\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{MAD}^{\mathbb{P}}]\big|\leq\sqrt{\frac{2\ln(2/\delta)}{K}}.

(57)

Combining Equations (
53
), (
56
), and (
57
) gives Equation (
52
).

Lemma
11

Lemma 11

(Concentration for CVaR)
.

Let
X
be a real-valued
reward
random variable in
[0,1]
with true distribution
\mathbb{T}
.
We observe i.i.d. samples
X_{1},\dots,X_{K}\sim\mathbb{T}
and denote the empirical
distribution given by these
K
samples as
\mathbb{P}
.
Fix a level
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Let for each
z\in\mathbb{R}
, the function

g_{z}(x):=\min(0,x-z)
. As we defined earlier, the (reward) CVaR functional at level
\alpha
admits the form

\mathrm{CVaR}_{\alpha}^{\mathbb{Q}}(X)\;=\;\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}}\big\{z+\frac{1}{\alpha}\,\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\!\big[g_{z}(X)\big]\big\}
.
Then, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\big|\mathrm{CVaR}_{\alpha}^{\mathbb{P}}(X)-\mathrm{CVaR}_{\alpha}^{\mathbb{T}}(X)\big|\;\leq\;\frac{1}{\alpha}\,\sqrt{\frac{1}{2K}\,\ln\frac{2}{\delta}}\;,

(58)

Proof.

Let
F
denote the CDF of
X
and
F_{K}(x):=\tfrac{1}{K}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\mathbf{1}\{X_{i}\leq x\}
the CDF of the empirical distribution
\mathbb{P}
. Recall
g_{z}(x):=\min(0,x-z)
. We use the notation
\mathrm{CVaR}_{\alpha}(F)=\mathrm{CVaR}^{\mathbb{T}}(X)
.
Similarly, the
empirical
CVaR is

\mathrm{CVaR}_{\alpha}(F_{K})\;=\;\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}}\Big\{\phi_{F_{K}}(z)\Big\}\quad\text{with}\quad\phi_{F_{K}}(z)\;:=\;z+\frac{1}{\alpha}\,\mathbb{E}_{F_{K}}\!\big[g_{z}(X)\big].

(59)

Start from definition of
\mathrm{CVaR}_{\alpha}(F),\mathrm{CVaR}_{\alpha}(F_{K})
. By the basic inequality

\big|\sup_{z}a_{z}-\sup_{z}b_{z}\big|\leq\sup_{z}|a_{z}-b_{z}|
, we have

\displaystyle\big|\mathrm{CVaR}_{\alpha}(F_{K})-\mathrm{CVaR}_{\alpha}(F)\big|\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\,\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}}\big|\;\mathbb{E}_{F_{K}}[g_{z}(X)]-\mathbb{E}_{F}[g_{z}(X)]\;\big|.

(60)

As
X
stands for utility and as assumed for our utility and also in the lemma statement, we have
X\in[0,1]
.
We show the deterministic inequality

\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}}\big|\;\mathbb{E}_{F_{K}}[g_{z}(X)]-\mathbb{E}_{F}[g_{z}(X)]\;\big|\;\leq\;\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}|F_{K}(x)-F(x)|.

(61)

To see this, fix
z
.
Since
g_{z}(x)=\min(0,x-z)
is nondecreasing in
x
and bounded between
[-1,0]
, we may write its expectation as
a Lebesgue integral with respect to the CDF:

\mathbb{E}_{F}[g_{z}(X)]\;=\;\int_{[0,1]}g_{z}(x)\,dF(x),\qquad\mathbb{E}_{F_{K}}[g_{z}(X)]\;=\;\int_{[0,1]}g_{z}(x)\,dF_{K}(x).

\displaystyle\mbox{Hence, }\big|\mathbb{E}_{F_{K}}[g_{z}(X)]-\mathbb{E}_{F}[g_{z}(X)]\big|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{[0,1]}g_{z}(x)\,d\big(F_{K}-F\big)(x)\right|.

Integrating by parts we get

\left|\int_{[0,1]}g_{z}(x)\,d(F_{K}-F)\right|=\left|\left[g_{z}(x)(F_{K}-F)(x)\right]_{0}^{1}-\int_{[0,1]}(F_{K}-F)(x)dg_{z}(x)\right|

Since
F_{K}-F(x)\rightarrow 0
as
x\rightarrow 1
or
x\rightarrow 0
,

\displaystyle\left|\int_{[0,1]}g_{z}(x)\,d(F_{K}-F)\right|=\left|\int_{[0,1]}(F_{K}-F)(x)dg_{z}(x)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{x\in[0,1]}|(F_{K}-F)(x)|\int|dg_{z}(x)|

\displaystyle=\sup_{x\in[0,1]}|(F_{K}-F)(x)|\text{TV}(g_{z}(x)),

where
TV
is total variation.
Since
g_{z}(x)
is nondecreasing in
x
, TV
(g_{z}(x))=g_{z}(1)-g_{z}(0)\leq 1
since
x\in[0,1]
.
Therefore

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\big|\mathbb{E}_{F_{K}}[g_{z}(X)]-\mathbb{E}_{F}[g_{z}(X)]\big|}\;\leq\;\sup_{x\in[0,1]}\left|(F_{K}-F)(x)\right|

Thus, taking the supremum over
z
on both sides of the inequality above gives Equation (
61
).
Next, the Dvoretzky–Kiefer–Wolfowitz (DKW)
25
inequality states that, for any
\delta\in(0,1)
,
with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\sup_{x\in[0,1]}|F_{K}(x)-F(x)|\;\leq\;\sqrt{\frac{1}{2K}\,\ln\frac{2}{\delta}}\;.

(62)

Combining Equations (
60
), (
61
), and (
62
), we conclude that,
with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\big|\mathrm{CVaR}_{\alpha}(F_{K})-\mathrm{CVaR}_{\alpha}(F)\big|\;\leq\;\frac{1}{\alpha}\,\sqrt{\frac{1}{2K}\,\ln\frac{2}{\delta}}\;.

(63)

Lemma

Lemma 12

(McDiarmid concentration for empirical MAD with data–dependent strategies)
.

Let
S\coloneq\{\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{K}\}
be a set of i.i.d. samples from the true distribution
\mathbb{T}
.
Let
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}(S)
be a (possibly random) mixed strategy profile. Assume the utilities satisfy
0\leq u_{i}(\mathbf{a}\mid\xi)\leq 1
for all
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbf{A}
and
\xi\in\Xi
.
Define the random utilities and empirical MAD

X_{k}:=u_{i}(\mathbf{x}(\xi_{1:K})\mid\xi_{k})\in[0,1],\quad\bar{X}:=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}X_{k},\quad\mathrm{MAD}^{\mathbb{P}}\!\left(u_{i}(\mathbf{x}\mid\xi)\right):=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}|X_{k}-\bar{X}|.

For a dataset
S^{(k)}
obtained from
S
by replacing
\xi_{k}
with an independent copy,
define the stability of the strategy mapping by

\Gamma_{K}:=\max_{k}\ \sup_{S,S^{(k)}}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\big\|\mathbf{x}_{j}(S)-\mathbf{x}_{j}(S^{(k)})\big\|_{1},\mbox{ and }\Delta_{K}=\min(1,\Gamma_{K}).

Then, for any
\delta\in(0,1)
, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\Big|\mathrm{MAD}^{\mathbb{P}}\!\left(u_{i}(\mathbf{x}\mid\xi)\right)-\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{T}}\left[\mathrm{MAD}^{\mathbb{P}}\!\left(u_{i}(\mathbf{x}\mid\xi)\right)\right]\Big|\;\leq\;\sqrt{\frac{2\ln(2/\delta)}{K}}\,\big(1+(K-1)\Delta_{K}\big).

(64)

Proof.

This proof mimics the McDiarmid part of the proof of Lemma
10
in Section
B.1
, but taking care of dependence.
Define the function

f(S):=\mathrm{MAD}^{\mathbb{P}}\!\left(u_{i}(\mathbf{x}(S)\mid\xi)\right)=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}|X_{k}-\bar{X}|.

Since
f
is a deterministic function of independent inputs

\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{K}
, McDiarmid’s inequality applies once bounded
differences are established.

Fix
k
and consider datasets
S
and
S^{(k)}
differing only in
\xi_{k}
.
Let
X_{j}
and
X_{j}^{\prime}
denote the corresponding utilities, with means

\bar{X}
and
\bar{X}^{\prime}
.
Like proof of Lemma
10
,
using
||a|-|b||\leq|a-b|
and the definition of
\bar{X}
,

\displaystyle|f(S)-f(S^{(k)})|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{K}\sum_{j=1}^{K}|(X_{j}-\bar{X})-(X_{j}^{\prime}-\bar{X}^{\prime})|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{j=1}^{K}|(X_{j}-X_{j}^{\prime})-(1/K)\sum_{i=1}^{K}(X_{i}-X_{i}^{\prime})|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{K}\sum_{j=1}^{K}\big(|(X_{j}-X_{j}^{\prime})|+(1/K)|\sum_{i=1}^{K}(x_{i}-x_{i}^{\prime})|\big)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{K}\sum_{j=1}^{K}\big(|(X_{j}-X_{j}^{\prime})|+(1/K)\sum_{i=1}^{K}|(x_{i}-x_{i}^{\prime})|\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{K}\sum_{j=1}^{K}|X_{j}-X_{j}^{\prime}|.

Next we bound
\sum_{j}|X_{j}-X_{j}^{\prime}|
.
Recall that
X_{j}(S):=u_{i}(\mathbf{x}(S)\mid\xi_{j})
and

X_{j}(S^{(k)}):=u_{i}(\mathbf{x}(S^{(k)})\mid\xi_{j}^{(k)})
.
Fix
k
and consider datasets
S
and
S^{(k)}
that differ only in the

k
-th sample.

Case
j\neq k
.

For
j\neq k
, the realization
\xi_{j}
is the same under
S
and
S^{(k)}
,
so

|X_{j}-X_{j}^{\prime}|=\big|u_{i}(\mathbf{x}(S)\mid\xi_{j})-u_{i}(\mathbf{x}(S^{(k)})\mid\xi_{j})\big|.

Using the multilinear form of expected utilities,

u_{i}(\mathbf{x}\mid\xi)=\sum_{\mathbf{a}\in\mathbf{A}}u_{i}(\mathbf{a}\mid\xi)\prod_{s=1}^{m}x_{s}(a_{j_{s}}),
and above mixed strategies as
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}

and a telescoping argument that changes the players’ mixed strategies one at a time, we obtain

u_{i}(\mathbf{x}\mid\xi)-u_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\mid\xi)=\sum_{l=1}^{m}\Big(u_{i}(x_{1}^{\prime},\dots,x_{l-1}^{\prime},x_{l},x_{l+1},\dots,x_{m}\mid\xi)-u_{i}(x_{1}^{\prime},\dots,x_{l}^{\prime},x_{l+1},\dots,x_{m}\mid\xi)\Big).

Since,
0\leq u_{i}(\mathbf{a}\mid\xi)\leq 1
, each term in the summation above is bounded by
\|x_{l}-x_{l}^{\prime}\|_{1}
as conditional
expected payoffs are bounded by
1
, thus,

\big|u_{i}(\mathbf{x}\mid\xi)-u_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\mid\xi)\big|\leq\sum_{s=1}^{m}\|x_{s}-x_{s}^{\prime}\|_{1},\qquad\forall\,\xi\in\Xi.

Applying this with
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}(S)
and

\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=\mathbf{x}(S^{(k)})
yields

|X_{j}-X_{j}^{\prime}|\leq\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\big\|\mathbf{x}_{\ell}(S)-\mathbf{x}_{\ell}(S^{(k)})\big\|_{1}\leq\Gamma_{K},\qquad j\neq k.

Also, we always have the crude bound

|X_{j}-X_{j}^{\prime}|=\big|u_{i}(\mathbf{x}(S)\mid\xi_{j})-u_{i}(\mathbf{x}(S^{(k)})\mid\xi_{j})\big|\leq 1.

The above two give

|X_{j}-X_{j}^{\prime}|\leq\min(1,\Gamma_{K})=\Delta_{K}.

Case
j=k
.

When
j=k
, both the realization and the mixed strategy profile may change.
In this case we use only the crude bound:

|X_{k}-X_{k}^{\prime}|=\big|u_{i}(\mathbf{x}(S)\mid\xi_{k})-u_{i}(\mathbf{x}(S^{(k)})\mid\xi_{k}^{\prime})\big|\leq 1,

since
u_{i}(\mathbf{a}\mid\xi)\in[0,1]
for all
\mathbf{a},\xi
.

Combining the two cases.

Summing over
j=1,\dots,K
gives

\sum_{j=1}^{K}|X_{j}-X_{j}^{\prime}|\leq 1+(K-1)\Delta_{K}.

Combining the above,

|f(S)-f(S^{(k)})|\leq\frac{2}{K}\big(1+(K-1)\Delta_{K}\big)=:c_{k}.

Finally, apply McDiarmid.
Since
c_{k}
is the same for all
k
,

\sum_{k=1}^{K}c_{k}^{2}=Kc_{k}^{2}
.
McDiarmid’s inequality gives

\mathbb{P}\!\left(|f(S)-\mathbb{E}f(S)|\geq\varepsilon\right)\leq 2\exp\!\left(-\frac{2\varepsilon^{2}}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}c_{k}^{2}}\right)=2\exp\!\left(-\frac{K\varepsilon^{2}}{2(1+(K-1)\Delta_{K})^{2}}\right).

Solving for
\varepsilon
with right-hand side
\delta
yields
(
64
).

\min(0,u_{i}(\mathbf{x}(S)\mid\xi)-z)=u_{i}(\mathbf{x}(S)\mid\xi)-z
,
so the population objective becomes

z+\frac{1}{\alpha}\big(\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{T}}[u_{i}(\mathbf{x}(S)\mid\xi)]-z\big)=\frac{1}{\alpha}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{T}}[u_{i}(\mathbf{x}(S)\mid\xi)]+z\left(1-\frac{1}{\alpha}\right),

which is strictly decreasing in
z
because
\alpha\in(0,1)
implies
1-\frac{1}{\alpha}<0
; thus the max value
when
z\geq 1
is attained at
z=1
. The same argument applies to the empirical objective.
Hence, we use
\sup_{z\in[0,1]}
without any loss of generality.

As we aim to apply McDiarmid, we need to bound differences for
g(S)=A(S)-B(S)
. We do so by separately bounding differences for
A(S)
and
B(S)
.

Bounded differences for
B(S)
.

Fix
k
and consider two datasets
S
and
S^{(k)}
that differ only in the
k
-th sample.
Let
X_{j}:=u_{i}(\mathbf{x}(S)\mid\xi_{j})
and
X_{j}^{\prime}:=u_{i}(\mathbf{x}(S^{(k)})\mid\xi_{j}^{\prime})
denote the induced utilities,
where
\xi_{j}^{\prime}=\xi_{j}
for
j\neq k
and
\xi_{k}^{\prime}
is the replaced point.

Define for
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{K})\in[0,1]^{K}

\Phi(x):=\sup_{z\in[0,1]}\left\{z+\frac{1}{\alpha K}\sum_{j=1}^{K}\min(0,x_{j}-z)\right\},

so that
B(S)=\Phi(X_{1},\dots,X_{K})
and
B(S^{(k)})=\Phi(X_{1}^{\prime},\dots,X_{K}^{\prime})
.

For any
x,x^{\prime}\in[0,1]^{K}
, the map
\Phi
is
1/(\alpha K)
-Lipschitz in
\ell_{1}
:
indeed, for any
z\in[0,1]
,

\left|\sum_{j=1}^{K}\min(0,x_{j}-z)-\sum_{j=1}^{K}\min(0,x_{j}^{\prime}-z)\right|\leq\sum_{j=1}^{K}|x_{j}-x_{j}^{\prime}|,

since
\min(0,\cdot)
is 1-Lipschitz. Taking the supremum over
z
preserves the bound, hence

|B(S)-B(S^{(k)})|\leq\frac{1}{\alpha K}\sum_{j=1}^{K}|X_{j}-X_{j}^{\prime}|.

Now bound the sum. For
j=k
, both
X_{k}
and
X_{j}^{\prime}
lie in
[0,1]
, hence
|X_{j}-X_{j}^{\prime}|\leq 1
.
For
j\neq k
, the sample point is unchanged (
\xi_{j}^{\prime}=\xi_{j}
), so

|X_{j}-X_{j}^{\prime}|=\big|u_{i}(\mathbf{x}(S)\mid\xi_{j})-u_{i}(\mathbf{x}(S^{(k)})\mid\xi_{j})\big|.

By the standard telescoping/Lipschitz argument as in
Case
j\neq k
of the proof of Lemma
12
,
for each fixed
\xi
one has

\big|u_{i}(\mathbf{x}\mid\xi)-u_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\mid\xi)\big|\leq\sum_{s=1}^{m}\|\mathbf{x}_{s}-\mathbf{x}_{s}^{\prime}\|_{1}.

Applying this with
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}(S)
and
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=\mathbf{x}(S^{(k)})
gives, for all
j\neq k
,

|X_{j}-X_{j}^{\prime}|\leq\sum_{s=1}^{m}\|\mathbf{x}_{s}(S)-\mathbf{x}_{s}(S^{(k)})\|_{1}\leq\Gamma_{K}.

Also, we always have the crude bound

|X_{j}-X_{j}^{\prime}|=\big|u_{i}(\mathbf{x}(S)\mid\xi_{j})-u_{i}(\mathbf{x}(S^{(k)})\mid\xi_{j})\big|\leq 1.

The above two give

|X_{j}-X_{j}^{\prime}|\leq\min(1,\Gamma_{K})=\Delta_{K}.

Therefore,

\sum_{j=1}^{K}|X_{j}-X_{j}^{\prime}|\leq 1+(K-1)\Delta_{K},

and hence for every
k
,

|B(S)-B(S^{(k)})|\leq\frac{1+(K-1)\Delta_{K}}{\alpha K}.

(66)

Bounded differences for
A(S)
.

Define
Y(\xi):=u_{i}(\mathbf{x}(S)\mid\xi)
and
Y^{\prime}(\xi):=u_{i}(\mathbf{x}(S^{(k)})\mid\xi)
with
\xi\sim\mathbb{T}

independent of
S
.
As shown earlier, the population reward-CVaR functional is
1/\alpha
-Lipschitz in
L_{1}
:

\left|\mathrm{CVaR}^{\mathbb{T}}_{\alpha}(Y)-\mathrm{CVaR}^{\mathbb{T}}_{\alpha}(Y^{\prime})\right|\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[|Y(\xi)-Y^{\prime}(\xi)|].

Using again the Lipschitz bound of
u_{i}(\mathbf{x}\mid\xi)
in
\mathbf{x}
yields

|Y(\xi)-Y^{\prime}(\xi)|\leq\Delta_{K}
for all
\xi
, hence

|A(S)-A(S^{(k)})|\leq\frac{\Delta_{K}}{\alpha}.

(67)

Bounded differences for
g(S)=A(S)-B(S)
and McDiarmid.

By the triangle inequality and (
66
)–(
67
),

|g(S)-g(S^{(k)})|\leq|A(S)-A(S^{(k)})|+|B(S)-B(S^{(k)})|\leq\frac{\Delta_{K}}{\alpha}+\frac{1+(K-1)\Delta_{K}}{\alpha K}=:c.

McDiarmid’s inequality then gives that for all
\varepsilon>0
,

P\big(|g(S)-\mathbb{E}[g(S)]|\geq\varepsilon\big)\leq 2\exp\!\left(-\frac{2\varepsilon^{2}}{Kc^{2}}\right).

Setting the right-hand side equal to
\delta
yields that with probability at least
1-\delta
,

|g(S)-\mathbb{E}[g(S)]|\leq c\sqrt{\frac{K}{2}\ln\!\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right)}.

Finally, using
|g(S)|\leq|\mathbb{E}[g(S)]|+|g(S)-\mathbb{E}[g(S)]|
and
\mathbb{E}[g(S)]=\mathbb{E}[A(S)-B(S)]
gives

|A(S)-B(S)|\leq|\mathbb{E}[g(S)]|+c\sqrt{\frac{K}{2}\ln\!\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right)}.

The bound (
65
) follows since
A(S),B(S)\in[0,1]
gives
|\mathbb{E}[g(S)]|\leq 1
.
∎

Appendix C
Dual derivations for specific coherent utility measures

This appendix derives the complementarity formulations for specific coherent utility measures directly from their dual representations. The derivations show how the auxiliary variables in the complementarity programs can be interpreted as variables selecting a worst-case dual distribution.

C.1
Mean-semideviation

Following the dual representation of mean-semideviation in
29
, the order-
p
mean-semideviation utility admits a risk-envelope representation with density

\frac{d\mathbb{Q}}{d\mathbb{P}}=1+\gamma_{s}(\eta-\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[\eta])
, where
\eta\geq 0
and
\|\eta\|_{q}\leq 1
, with
1/p+1/q=1
. The mean-semideviation used here corresponds to the case
p=1
, so
q=\infty
and the constraint becomes
0\leq\eta\leq 1
. Hence, for

\rho_{\mathrm{MSD}}(X)=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[X]-\gamma_{s}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[\max(0,\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[X]-X)]
, the corresponding dual density is

\frac{d\mathbb{Q}}{d\mathbb{P}}=1+\gamma_{s}\left(\eta-\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[\eta]\right),\qquad 0\leq\eta\leq 1.

In the finite-sample setting, let
\eta_{k}=\eta(\xi_{k})
and define
\lambda_{i,k}=\gamma_{s}\mathbb{P}(\xi_{k})\eta_{k}
. Then
0\leq\lambda_{i,k}\leq\gamma_{s}\mathbb{P}(\xi_{k})
and

\mathbb{Q}_{i}(\xi_{k})=\mathbb{P}(\xi_{k})+\lambda_{i,k}-\mathbb{P}(\xi_{k})\sum_{r=1}^{K}\lambda_{i,r}.

Let
X_{k}=u_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i}\mid\xi_{k})
and recall

\mu_{i}^{\mathbb{P}}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{P}(\xi_{k})X_{k}
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}_{i}}[X]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(\mathbb{P}(\xi_{k})+\lambda_{i,k}-\mathbb{P}(\xi_{k})\sum_{r=1}^{K}\lambda_{i,r}\right)X_{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{P}(\xi_{k})X_{k}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\lambda_{i,k}X_{k}-\left(\sum_{r=1}^{K}\lambda_{i,r}\right)\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{P}(\xi_{k})X_{k}

\displaystyle=\mu_{i}^{\mathbb{P}}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i})+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\lambda_{i,k}X_{k}-\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\lambda_{i,k}\right)\mu_{i}^{\mathbb{P}}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i})

\displaystyle=\mu_{i}^{\mathbb{P}}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i})+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\lambda_{i,k}\left(X_{k}-\mu_{i}^{\mathbb{P}}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i})\right)

\displaystyle=\mu_{i}^{\mathbb{P}}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i})+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\lambda_{i,k}\left(u_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i}\mid\xi_{k})-\mu_{i}^{\mathbb{P}}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i})\right).

Since

u_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i}\mid\xi_{k})=\sum_{l=1}^{n_{i}}x_{i}(a_{l})u_{i}(a_{l},\mathbf{x}_{-i}\mid\xi_{k})

and

\mu_{i}^{\mathbb{P}}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i})=\sum_{l=1}^{n_{i}}x_{i}(a_{l})\mu_{i}^{\mathbb{P}}(a_{l},\mathbf{x}_{-i}),

we obtain

u_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i}\mid\xi_{k})-\mu_{i}^{\mathbb{P}}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i})=d_{i,k}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i}).

Hence,

\rho_{\text{MSD}}(u_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i}\mid\xi))=\min_{0\leq\lambda_{i,k}\leq\gamma_{s}\mathbb{P}(\xi_{k})}\left[\mu_{i}^{\mathbb{P}}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i})+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\lambda_{i,k}d_{i,k}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i})\right].

For fixed
\mathbf{x}_{i}
and
\mathbf{x}_{-i}
, the only variables in the inner dual problem are
\lambda_{i,k}
. Since

\min_{0\leq\lambda_{i,k}\leq\gamma_{s}\mathbb{P}(\xi_{k})}\left[\mu_{i}^{\mathbb{P}}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i})+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\lambda_{i,k}d_{i,k}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i})\right]

has a constant first term with respect to
\lambda_{i}
, its inner optimality conditions are exactly those of the separable linear problem

\min_{0\leq\lambda_{i,k}\leq\gamma_{s}\mathbb{P}(\xi_{k})}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\lambda_{i,k}d_{i,k}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i}).

Thus, for each
k
, the optimal
\lambda_{i,k}
solves

\min_{0\leq\lambda_{i,k}\leq\gamma_{s}\mathbb{P}(\xi_{k})}\lambda_{i,k}d_{i,k}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i})
. Hence, if
d_{i,k}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i})>0
, the minimizer is
\lambda_{i,k}=0
; if
d_{i,k}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i})<0
, the minimizer is
\lambda_{i,k}=\gamma_{s}\mathbb{P}(\xi_{k})
; and if
d_{i,k}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i})=0
, any value in the interval is optimal.

Introducing
z_{i,k}=\min(0,d_{i,k}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i}))
, these three cases can be written equivalently as

\displaystyle 0\leq\lambda_{i,k}\perp d_{i,k}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i})-z_{i,k}\geq 0,\quad\forall k\in\{1,\dots,K\},

(68a)

\displaystyle 0\leq\gamma_{s}\mathbb{P}(\xi_{k})-\lambda_{i,k}\perp-z_{i,k}\geq 0,\quad\forall k\in\{1,\dots,K\}.

(68b)

Indeed, if
d_{i,k}>0
, then
z_{i,k}=0
and the first complementarity condition forces
\lambda_{i,k}=0
. If
d_{i,k}<0
, then
z_{i,k}=d_{i,k}
and the second complementarity condition forces
\lambda_{i,k}=\gamma_{s}\mathbb{P}(\xi_{k})
. If
d_{i,k}=0
, then
z_{i,k}=0
and any
\lambda_{i,k}\in[0,\gamma_{s}\mathbb{P}(\xi_{k})]
satisfies the conditions.

Now define, for fixed
\mathbf{x}_{-i}
,

\phi_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\lambda_{i})=\mu_{i}^{\mathbb{P}}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i})+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\lambda_{i,k}d_{i,k}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i}).

The MSD utility is
\rho_{\mathrm{MSD}}(u_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i}\mid\xi))=\min_{\lambda_{i}}\phi_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\lambda_{i})
over the box constraints above. Since
\phi_{i}(\cdot,\lambda_{i})
is affine in
\mathbf{x}_{i}
, Danskin’s theorem implies that the gradient of
\phi_{i}
with respect to
\mathbf{x}_{i}
at any optimal
\lambda_{i}
is a supergradient of the MSD utility. Therefore, the
l
-th action risk-adjusted action value is

v_{i,l}(\mathbf{x}_{-i})=\frac{\partial\phi_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\lambda_{i})}{\partial x_{i}(a_{l})}=\mu_{i}^{\mathbb{P}}(a_{l},\mathbf{x}_{-i})+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\lambda_{i,k}\left(u_{i}(a_{l},\mathbf{x}_{-i}\mid\xi_{k})-\mu_{i}^{\mathbb{P}}(a_{l},\mathbf{x}_{-i})\right).

If the inner minimizer
\lambda_{i}
is not unique, then Danskin’s theorem gives the full superdifferential as the convex hull of the corresponding gradients over all optimal choices of
\lambda_{i}
. Thus, any such gradient can be used in the outer best-response KKT conditions, and the general case allows any convex combination of them.

Combining the inner optimality conditions for the MSD dual variables with the outer best-response KKT conditions over
\mathbf{X}_{i}
gives the following system for player
i
:

\displaystyle 0\leq\lambda_{i,k}\perp d_{i,k}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i})-z_{i,k}\geq 0,\quad\forall k\in\{1,\dots,K\},

(69a)

\displaystyle 0\leq\gamma_{s}\mathbb{P}(\xi_{k})-\lambda_{i,k}\perp-z_{i,k}\geq 0,\quad\forall k\in\{1,\dots,K\},

(69b)

\displaystyle 0\leq\alpha_{i}-v_{i,l}(\mathbf{x}_{-i})\perp x_{i}(a_{l})\geq 0,\quad\forall l\in\{1,\dots,n_{i}\},

(69c)

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{i}=1,\qquad\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{R}.

(69d)

The first two lines encode optimality of the worst-case MSD dual variables for the fixed profile
(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i})
. The third and fourth lines encode optimality of
\mathbf{x}_{i}
in player
i
’s best-response problem. Stacking (
69
) over all players yields the MSD mixed complementarity formulation.

C.2
Order-
p
mean-semideviation

This proof follows the same style of proof as the one with
p=1
; the reader is advised to read that before reading this terse proof.
For
1<p<\infty
, let
q
denote the conjugate exponent, so
1/p+1/q=1
. The order-
p
mean-semideviation utility is

\rho_{\text{MSD},p}(X)=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[X]-\gamma_{s}\left\|\max(0,\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[X]-X)\right\|_{p}.\;,

where
\|Y\|_{p}=(\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[|Y|^{p}])^{1/p}
. In the finite-sample setting, this norm is

\|Y\|_{p}=(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{P}(\xi_{k})|Y(\xi_{k})|^{p})^{1/p}
.
Its dual density can be written as
\frac{d\mathbb{Q}}{d\mathbb{P}}=1+\gamma_{s}(\eta-\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[\eta])
, where
\eta\geq 0
and
\|\eta\|_{q}\leq 1
. Hence, in the finite-sample setting, player
i
’s dual problem is

\rho_{\text{MSD},p}(u_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i}\mid\xi))=\min_{\eta_{i,k}\geq 0,\ \sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{P}(\xi_{k})\eta_{i,k}^{q}\leq 1}\left[\mu_{i}^{\mathbb{P}}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i})+\gamma_{s}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{P}(\xi_{k})\eta_{i,k}d_{i,k}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i})\right].

Unlike the
p=1
case, the inner minimization for
1<p<\infty
is not separable across samples because the weighted
\ell_{q}
constraint
\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{P}(\xi_{k})\eta_{i,k}^{q}\leq 1
couples the variables
\eta_{i,k}
.
Let
\theta_{i}\geq 0
be the multiplier for
\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{P}(\xi_{k})\eta_{i,k}^{q}\leq 1
. The inner KKT conditions are

\displaystyle 0\leq\eta_{i,k}\perp\gamma_{s}\mathbb{P}(\xi_{k})d_{i,k}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i})+q\theta_{i}\mathbb{P}(\xi_{k})\eta_{i,k}^{q-1}\geq 0,\quad\forall k\in\{1,\dots,K\},

(70a)

\displaystyle 0\leq\theta_{i}\perp 1-\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{P}(\xi_{k})\eta_{i,k}^{q}\geq 0.

(70b)

By Danskin’s theorem use (similar to
p=1
case), an optimal
\eta_{i}
induces

v_{i,l}(\mathbf{x}_{-i})=\mu_{i}^{\mathbb{P}}(a_{l},\mathbf{x}_{-i})+\gamma_{s}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{P}(\xi_{k})\eta_{i,k}\left(u_{i}(a_{l},\mathbf{x}_{-i}\mid\xi_{k})-\mu_{i}^{\mathbb{P}}(a_{l},\mathbf{x}_{-i})\right).

Therefore, the order-
p
MSD equilibrium conditions are

\displaystyle 0\leq\eta_{i,k}\perp\gamma_{s}\mathbb{P}(\xi_{k})d_{i,k}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i})+q\theta_{i}\mathbb{P}(\xi_{k})\eta_{i,k}^{q-1}\geq 0,\quad\forall k\in\{1,\dots,K\},

(71a)

\displaystyle 0\leq\theta_{i}\perp 1-\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{P}(\xi_{k})\eta_{i,k}^{q}\geq 0,

(71b)

\displaystyle 0\leq\alpha_{i}-v_{i,l}(\mathbf{x}_{-i})\perp x_{i}(a_{l})\geq 0,\quad\forall l\in\{1,\dots,n_{i}\},

(71c)

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{i}=1,\qquad\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{R}.

(71d)

Unlike the
p=1
case, (
71
) is a mixed nonlinear complementarity formulation because of the
\ell_{q}
constraint in the dual envelope.

C.3
CVaR

Let
X_{k}=u_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i}\mid\xi_{k})
and recall

\mu_{i}^{\mathbb{P}}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{P}(\xi_{k})X_{k}
.
Using the dual representation of CVaR, the risk envelope consists of densities satisfying
0\leq d\mathbb{Q}/d\mathbb{P}\leq 1/\alpha
and
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[d\mathbb{Q}/d\mathbb{P}]=1
. In the finite-sample setting, with
r_{k}=\mathbb{Q}(\xi_{k})
, this gives

\mathrm{CVaR}_{\alpha}[X]=\min_{\mathbf{r}}\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{K}r_{k}X_{k}:\sum_{k=1}^{K}r_{k}=1,\ 0\leq r_{k}\leq\frac{\mathbb{P}(\xi_{k})}{\alpha}\right\}.

Since
\rho_{\text{CVaR}}(X)=(1-\gamma_{c})\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[X]+\gamma_{c}\mathrm{CVaR}_{\alpha}[X]
, define
\lambda_{i,k}=\gamma_{c}r_{i,k}
. Following the Ruszczyński–Shapiro dual representation, the mixed mean-CVaR utility has a risk-envelope representation with density bounded as

1-\gamma_{c}\leq\frac{d\mathbb{Q}}{d\mathbb{P}}\leq 1-\gamma_{c}+\frac{\gamma_{c}}{\alpha}.

In the finite-sample setting, this means

(1-\gamma_{c})\mathbb{P}(\xi_{k})\leq q_{i,k}\leq\left(1-\gamma_{c}+\frac{\gamma_{c}}{\alpha}\right)\mathbb{P}(\xi_{k}),\qquad\sum_{k=1}^{K}q_{i,k}=1.

Writing
q_{i,k}=(1-\gamma_{c})\mathbb{P}(\xi_{k})+\lambda_{i,k}
gives

0\leq\lambda_{i,k}\leq\gamma_{c}\mathbb{P}(\xi_{k})/\alpha
and

\sum_{k=1}^{K}\lambda_{i,k}=\gamma_{c}
, which yields the formulation below.

\rho_{\text{CVaR}}(u_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i}\mid\xi))=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq\lambda_{i,k}\leq\frac{\gamma_{c}}{\alpha}\mathbb{P}(\xi_{k})\\
\sum_{k=1}^{K}\lambda_{i,k}=\gamma_{c}\end{subarray}}\left[(1-\gamma_{c})\mu_{i}^{\mathbb{P}}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i})+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\lambda_{i,k}u_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i}\mid\xi_{k})\right].

For fixed
\mathbf{x}_{i}
and
\mathbf{x}_{-i}
, this is a linear program in
\lambda_{i}
. Let
z_{i}
be the multiplier for
\sum_{k=1}^{K}\lambda_{i,k}=\gamma_{c}
, and let
-\nu_{i,k}\geq 0
be the multiplier for the upper bound
\lambda_{i,k}\leq\frac{\gamma_{c}}{\alpha}\mathbb{P}(\xi_{k})
. The KKT conditions of the inner dual problem are

\displaystyle 0\leq\frac{\gamma_{c}}{\alpha}\mathbb{P}(\xi_{k})-\lambda_{i,k}\perp-\nu_{i,k}\geq 0,\quad\forall k\in\{1,\dots,K\},

(72a)

\displaystyle 0\leq\lambda_{i,k}\perp u_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i}\mid\xi_{k})-z_{i}-\nu_{i,k}\geq 0,\quad\forall k\in\{1,\dots,K\},

(72b)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\lambda_{i,k}=\gamma_{c}.

(72c)

Using
u_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i}\mid\xi_{k})=\sum_{l=1}^{n_{i}}x_{i}(a_{l})u_{i}(a_{l},\mathbf{x}_{-i}\mid\xi_{k})
, For fixed
\mathbf{x}_{-i}
, define

\phi_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\lambda_{i})=(1-\gamma_{c})\mu_{i}^{\mathbb{P}}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i})+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\lambda_{i,k}u_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i}\mid\xi_{k}),

where
\lambda_{i}
solves the inner CVaR dual problem. Since
\phi_{i}(\cdot,\lambda_{i})
is affine in
\mathbf{x}_{i}
, Danskin’s theorem implies that the derivative of
\phi_{i}
with respect to
x_{i}(a_{l})
at an optimal
\lambda_{i}
gives a supergradient component of
\rho_{\mathrm{CVaR}}(u_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i}\mid\xi))
. Using
\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}(a_{l})}\mu_{i}^{\mathbb{P}}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i})=\mu_{i}^{\mathbb{P}}(a_{l},\mathbf{x}_{-i})
and
\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}(a_{l})}u_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i}\mid\xi_{k})=u_{i}(a_{l},\mathbf{x}_{-i}\mid\xi_{k})
, we obtain

v_{i,l}(\mathbf{x}_{-i})=\frac{\partial\phi_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\lambda_{i})}{\partial x_{i}(a_{l})}=(1-\gamma_{c})\mu_{i}^{\mathbb{P}}(a_{l},\mathbf{x}_{-i})+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\lambda_{i,k}u_{i}(a_{l},\mathbf{x}_{-i}\mid\xi_{k}).

If the inner CVaR dual minimizer
\lambda_{i}
is not unique, Danskin’s theorem gives the superdifferential as the convex hull of these gradients over all optimal choices of
\lambda_{i}
.
Therefore, the CVaR equilibrium conditions are

\displaystyle 0\leq\frac{\gamma_{c}}{\alpha}\mathbb{P}(\xi_{k})-\lambda_{i,k}\perp-\nu_{i,k}\geq 0,\quad\forall k\in\{1,\dots,K\},

(73a)

\displaystyle 0\leq\lambda_{i,k}\perp\sum_{l=1}^{n_{i}}x_{i}(a_{l})u_{i}(a_{l},\mathbf{x}_{-i}\mid\xi_{k})-z_{i}-\nu_{i,k}\geq 0,\quad\forall k\in\{1,\dots,K\},

(73b)

\displaystyle 0\leq\alpha_{i}-v_{i,l}(\mathbf{x}_{-i})\perp x_{i}(a_{l})\geq 0,\quad\forall l\in\{1,\dots,n_{i}\},

(73c)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\lambda_{i,k}=\gamma_{c},\qquad\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{i}=1,\qquad\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{R}.

(73d)

Stacking (
73
) over all players recovers the CVaR mixed complementarity formulation. In this derivation,
z_{i}
arises as the equality multiplier in the dual CVaR problem and coincides with the usual CVaR threshold variable in the primal representation.

C.4
Entropic value-at-risk

Define the entropic value-at-risk of a reward random variable
X
at level
\alpha\in(0,1)
by

\mathrm{EVaR}_{\alpha}[X]=\sup_{\theta>0}\left\{-\frac{1}{\theta}\log\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[\exp(-\theta X)]}{\alpha}\right)\right\}.

Equivalently, by the variational representation of relative entropy,

\mathrm{EVaR}_{\alpha}[X]=\inf_{\mathbb{Q}\ll\mathbb{P}}\left\{\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[X]:\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left[\log\left(\frac{d\mathbb{Q}}{d\mathbb{P}}\right)\right]\leq-\log\alpha\right\}.

In the finite-sample setting, write
h_{k}=(d\mathbb{Q}/d\mathbb{P})(\xi_{k})
and
r_{k}=\mathbb{Q}(\xi_{k})=\mathbb{P}(\xi_{k})h_{k}
. The constraint
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[h]=1
becomes
\sum_{k=1}^{K}r_{k}=1
, and the relative-entropy constraint becomes

\sum_{k=1}^{K}r_{k}\log(r_{k}/\mathbb{P}(\xi_{k}))\leq-\log\alpha
. Therefore,

\mathrm{EVaR}_{\alpha}[X]=\min_{\mathbf{r}}\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{K}r_{k}X_{k}:\sum_{k=1}^{K}r_{k}=1,\ r_{k}\geq 0,\ \sum_{k=1}^{K}r_{k}\log\left(\frac{r_{k}}{\mathbb{P}(\xi_{k})}\right)\leq-\log\alpha\right\}.

If one uses the same mean–tail interpolation as in the CVaR case, define

\rho_{\mathrm{EVaR}}(X)=(1-\gamma_{e})\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[X]+\gamma_{e}\mathrm{EVaR}_{\alpha}[X]
with
\gamma_{e}\in[0,1]
. For
\gamma_{e}>0
, set
\lambda_{i,k}=\gamma_{e}r_{i,k}
. Then player
i
’s dual problem is

\rho_{\mathrm{EVaR}}(u_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i}\mid\xi))=\min_{\lambda_{i}}\left[(1-\gamma_{e})\mu_{i}^{\mathbb{P}}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i})+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\lambda_{i,k}u_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i}\mid\xi_{k})\right],

subject to
\lambda_{i,k}\geq 0
,
\sum_{k=1}^{K}\lambda_{i,k}=\gamma_{e}
, and

\sum_{k=1}^{K}\lambda_{i,k}\log(\lambda_{i,k}/(\gamma_{e}\mathbb{P}(\xi_{k})))\leq-\gamma_{e}\log\alpha
.
Let
z_{i}
be the multiplier for
\sum_{k=1}^{K}\lambda_{i,k}=\gamma_{e}
and let
\theta_{i}\geq 0
be the multiplier for the relative-entropy constraint. In the nondegenerate case where the optimal
\lambda_{i,k}
are positive, the inner KKT conditions are

\displaystyle u_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i}\mid\xi_{k})-z_{i}+\theta_{i}\left(1+\log\left(\frac{\lambda_{i,k}}{\gamma_{e}\mathbb{P}(\xi_{k})}\right)\right)=0,\quad\forall k\in\{1,\dots,K\},

(74a)

\displaystyle 0\leq\theta_{i}\perp-\gamma_{e}\log\alpha-\sum_{k=1}^{K}\lambda_{i,k}\log\left(\frac{\lambda_{i,k}}{\gamma_{e}\mathbb{P}(\xi_{k})}\right)\geq 0,

(74b)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\lambda_{i,k}=\gamma_{e},\qquad\lambda_{i,k}>0,\quad\forall k\in\{1,\dots,K\}.

(74c)

Danskin’s theorem gives

v_{i,l}(\mathbf{x}_{-i})=(1-\gamma_{e})\mu_{i}^{\mathbb{P}}(a_{l},\mathbf{x}_{-i})+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\lambda_{i,k}u_{i}(a_{l},\mathbf{x}_{-i}\mid\xi_{k}).

Thus, an EVaR-based CUMG satisfies the mixed nonlinear complementarity system

\displaystyle u_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{-i}\mid\xi_{k})-z_{i}+\theta_{i}\left(1+\log\left(\frac{\lambda_{i,k}}{\gamma_{e}\mathbb{P}(\xi_{k})}\right)\right)=0,\quad\forall k\in\{1,\dots,K\},

(75a)

\displaystyle 0\leq\theta_{i}\perp-\gamma_{e}\log\alpha-\sum_{k=1}^{K}\lambda_{i,k}\log\left(\frac{\lambda_{i,k}}{\gamma_{e}\mathbb{P}(\xi_{k})}\right)\geq 0,

(75b)

\displaystyle 0\leq\alpha_{i}-v_{i,l}(\mathbf{x}_{-i})\perp x_{i}(a_{l})\geq 0,\quad\forall l\in\{1,\dots,n_{i}\},

(75c)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\lambda_{i,k}=\gamma_{e},\qquad\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{i}=1,\qquad\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{R}.

(75d)

Unlike the MSD and CVaR formulations above, the EVaR formulation is not an MLCP because the relative-entropy constraint introduces logarithmic terms. If no interpolation with the mean is desired, one can set
\gamma_{e}=1
.
