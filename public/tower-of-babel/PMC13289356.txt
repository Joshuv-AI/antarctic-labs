Rapid response intervention for assessing health needs in newly arrived migrants in Catalonia: a community-based, descriptive study

Introduction

In recent years, migration to and within the European Union and European Economic Area (EU/EEA) has increased significantly, making migration-related health policies a priority. The European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control published the ‘Public Health Guidance on Screening and Vaccination for Infectious Diseases in Newly Arrived Migrants within the EU/EEA’1 to provide scientific advice to facilitate effective screening and vaccination for priority infectious diseases among newly arrived migrant populations.

In Catalonia, Spain, an increasing influx of migrants arriving through unsafe routes has been documented,2 3 often exposing them to a higher risk of infectious diseases and other health conditions. These populations face unique challenges, including barriers to healthcare access, administrative hurdles, language and cultural differences and fear of deportation, which hinder timely medical attention and preventive care. Additionally, personal barriers such as limited health literacy, lack of information on infection screening and vaccination, and other priorities like employment, housing and legal status further restrict access to preventive healthcare services. Overcoming these barriers is essential to ensure equitable healthcare access and mitigate infectious disease transmission in migrant communities and the broader population.4

The Public Health Agency of Catalonia (PHAC) has developed guidelines to support coordinated clinical and epidemiological screening for newly arrived migrants, the Actions for Clinical and Epidemiological Screening of Migrants Arriving in Catalonia.3 This protocol includes a comprehensive screening approach addressing infectious diseases (eg, tuberculosis (TB) or vaccine-preventable diseases), and chronic conditions (eg, diabetes), which may have been exacerbated due to the migration pathway. The PHAC also implemented a community-based public model for the prevention and control of communicable diseases in migrant communities, with its main focus being the Public Health and Community Team, eSPiC from here on (equip de Salut Pública i Comunitària).5 This model aims to address these barriers by ensuring culturally and linguistically adapted interventions that facilitate healthcare access, as well as the prevention and control of infectious diseases within migrant populations.

This study describes a community-based intervention in Catalonia designed to assess health needs, facilitate access to primary healthcare services and increase TB awareness among migrant communities, as part of a rapid, culturally adapted public health response supporting early detection of communicable diseases. To overcome structural and cultural barriers hindering linkage to care, the intervention employed culturally sensitive approaches, simplifying testing and treatment pathways while fostering trust within migrant communities.

Methods

On 12 February 2024, the Tarragona Epidemiological Surveillance and Public Health Emergency Response Service activated a rapid response strategy in Coma-ruga, a village in Tarragona province, following the arrival of a group of migrants. Aligned with the PHAC guidelines, two key interventions were implemented: (1) clinical screening and (2) TB awareness and prevention.

For clinical screening, a context analysis was conducted, together with coordination across the Red Cross Refugee Programme, primary healthcare services within the Santa Tecla Network in Tarragona, the Tarragona Epidemiological Surveillance Service and eSPiC. Following this, initial contact with migrants was established by community health workers (CHWs), who explained the health screening process in their native languages. A symptom-based triage was performed to identify urgent health needs, such as suspected active infectious diseases (eg, TB, vaccine-preventable diseases), acute medical conditions requiring immediate attention and chronic disease exacerbations.3 Migrants requiring medical care were attended directly by the primary healthcare team on-site, and healthcare IDs were processed to facilitate access to healthcare. Standard referral pathways were followed for cases requiring further evaluation or specialised care. This rapid response phase prioritised urgent health needs and communicable diseases. Other health conditions, including mental health, psychosocial needs, vaccination status and chronic diseases, were addressed in subsequent phases of care through primary healthcare services.

For TB awareness and prevention, a workshop was organised in coordination with the Tarragona Epidemiological Surveillance Service, eSPiC, primary care and referral hospital, and the Tarragona Health Promotion Service. The intervention included a culturally adapted TB video developed through participatory action research,6 7 group discussions facilitated by CHWs in several languages (French, Mandinka, Soninke and Wolof), distribution of TB information leaflets, and symptom screening with contact history gathering. TB was prioritised to align with health policies considering the TB epidemiological situation in Catalonia.8

Given the rapid and operational nature of the intervention, no predefined outcome measures were established. Descriptive data were collected to document the intervention and the health needs identified.

Patients and the public were not involved in setting the research question or defining outcome measures. However, migrant communities played an active role in the intervention through CHWs and culturally adapted approaches.

Nine TB awareness workshops were conducted to strengthen prevention efforts and facilitate active TB surveillance. Through culturally adapted discussions, participants received detailed information on the symptoms and transmission of TB, which encouraged at-risk individuals to contact health services. Additionally, the workshops facilitated linkage to care, ensuring that individuals with suspected infections were referred for appropriate diagnostic evaluation and follow-up. Ultimately, four cases of TB infection were identified (two from Mali, one from Mauritania and one from Senegal), while active TB was ruled out.

Discussion

This intervention highlights the need to implement rapid and specific public health responses for newly arrived immigrants. Our strategy links vulnerable groups to the health system from their point of entry, facilitates timely healthcare access and supports early outbreak prevention by interrupting potential transmission chains.3 This community-based approach, integrating CHWs, leverages cultural and linguistic diversity to promote an inclusive and impactful health intervention.4 This model addresses immediate health needs and improves symptom recognition and TB prevention among migrant populations, in alignment with the current epidemiological situation of the disease in Catalonia.8

While this intervention provided valuable insights, it also faced challenges, particularly the uncertainty regarding the duration of migrants’ stay in reception facilities. This made it challenging to implement infectious disease screening guidelines, as the duration of stay complicated diagnosis, treatment initiation and overall planning. Loss to follow-up is a well-documented issue in migrant health interventions, as multiple factors, such as mobility, administrative barriers and lack of awareness, can disrupt linkage to care.4 9 Additionally, syndemic vulnerability—arising from the interaction of social, economic and structural determinants—exacerbates health conditions and complicates intervention effectiveness, underscoring the need for integrated, multi-level approaches.9

Evidence on the cost-effectiveness of migrant screening programmes remains limited, although available data suggest moderate to high cost-effectiveness depending on the population and disease targeted.1012 Across Europe, screening strategies often remain fragmented and focused on single diseases, resulting in limited coverage. Recent initiatives using digital clinical decision support systems have shown promising results in improving multi-disease screening and catch-up vaccination in primary care.13 14 In TB, screening remains an important tool not only for cost-effectiveness but also for improving health outcomes in populations experiencing vulnerability.15 16 Acceptability among migrants depends on trust in healthcare providers, knowledge of disease and perceived stigma.17

This study has several limitations. First, its descriptive design and absence of a comparison group limit the ability to assess the effectiveness of the intervention. Second, the short duration of follow-up and the high mobility of the population may have resulted in loss to follow-up and incomplete outcome assessment. Third, the intervention prioritised communicable diseases and urgent conditions, and did not systematically assess mental health, vaccination status or other non-communicable conditions, which may have limited opportunities for early prevention through catch-up vaccination. Finally, the specific migration context of the study population should be considered when interpreting the transferability of the findings.

The integration of cultural and social dimensions into public health interventions strengthens their effectiveness by fostering trust, improving communication and enhancing adherence to care.17 Without such adaptation, healthcare interventions may fail to engage migrant populations effectively, leading to underutilisation of services and missed opportunities for disease prevention.17 For TB prevention, community-based awareness interventions in native languages have increased symptom recognition and trust, which is crucial given the stigma associated with TB in many migrant communities.7 Although individuals in this intervention shared a similar migration context, they represented diverse cultural and linguistic backgrounds. The intervention’s core components, with the involvement of CHWs and culturally adapted community-based approaches and materials, allow adaptation to more heterogeneous migrant populations, provided that appropriate contextual and linguistic adjustments are made. Future research should incorporate predefined outcome measures to better assess the impact of such interventions. Public health efforts should proactively address migrant health needs to build resilient health systems.
