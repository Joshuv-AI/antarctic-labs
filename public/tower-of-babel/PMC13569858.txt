Assessing autonomic nervous system imbalance in long COVID-19 patients through heart rate variability during tilt testing
Authors: Samuel Minucci Camargo, Ana Leticia Gomes dos Santos, Stella Tassinari Maximo, Kelly Correa Baioco da Silva, Beatriz de Oliveira Machado, Christian Gonçalves Sassaki, Silvia Helena Bastos de Paula, José Luis Puglisi, Daniel Gustavo Goroso
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLong COVID-19 is recognized as a condition associated with autonomic nervous system (ANS) dysfunction. However, quantitative evidence of its impact on heart rate variability (HRV) and blood pressure (BP) regulation during postural changes remains limited. This study assessed autonomic imbalance in post-COVID-19 patients by evaluating HRV and BP responses during tilt table testing, comparing long COVID-19 patients with healthy controls. A total of 61 participants were enrolled, 39 long COVID-19 patients (Study Group, SG) and 22 healthy controls (Control Group, CG). HRV was analyzed using time- and frequency-domain parameters. BP monitoring evaluated systolic, and diastolic blood pressure (SBP, and DBP respectively), and Pulse pressure (PP = SBP—DBP) was calculated for each phase. SG participants exhibited marked autonomic dysfunction during tilt. In the upright phase, they showed a significant increase in mean RR intervals (p = 0.0136), reduced normalized low-frequency (LF†) with p = 0.0316, increased normalized high-frequency (HF†) with p = 0.0315, and a decreased low-frequency/high-frequency (LF/HF) ratio (p = 0.0316), indicating a blunted sympathetic response and impaired autonomic adaptation to orthostatic stress. BP responses were also impaired: SG demonstrated attenuated changes in PP (ΔPP) when transitioning from the upright position to the recovery phase (p < 0.037). Within-group analysis confirmed persistent RR interval instability (p < 0.0001), incomplete normalization of LF and HF components (both p < 0.0001), and delayed recovery of PP after return to supine position. BP responses were also diminished: SG showed smaller ΔPP when moving from standing upright to the recovery phase (p < 0.037). Baroreflex sensitivity values did not differ between groups. Long COVID-19 patients display significant autonomic dysregulation, characterized by reduced HRV, abnormal BP responses. These findings highlight the value of tilt testing in uncovering hidden dysautonomia and support the need for targeted interventions, including pharmacologic modulation and long-term HRV/BP monitoring, to improve cardiovascular stability in long COVID-19.Keywords: Autonomic nervous system (ANS), Heart rate variability (HRV), Long COVID-19, Postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome (POTS), Tilt table test, Cardiovascular dysregulationSubject terms: Cardiology, Cardiovascular biology, Diseases of the nervous system

Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic has not only caused acute infections but has also led to persistent, often debilitating symptoms in a significant proportion of recovered individuals, a condition now widely recognized as long COVID-19 or post-acute sequelae of SARS-CoV-2 infection (PASC)1,2. Among the most prevalent and debilitating manifestations of long COVID-19 is ANS dysfunction, which presents with symptoms such as fatigue, dizziness, palpitations, and orthostatic intolerance3–5. These symptoms are frequently associated with postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome (POTS), a disorder characterized by excessive heart rate (HR) increases upon standing, further complicating the clinical management of these patients6,7.

Emerging evidence suggests that autonomic dysfunction is a hallmark of long COVID-19, with studies reporting a prevalence of POTS and related conditions in 10–30% of post-COVID patients8,9. HRV, a non-invasive measure of ANS function, has emerged as a valuable tool for assessing autonomic balance and detecting abnormalities in parasympathetic and sympathetic regulation10,11. Reduced HRV, indicative of autonomic dysregulation, has been consistently observed in long COVID-19 patients, suggesting a persistent shift toward sympathetic dominance and impaired vagal tone12,13.

Recently, in a multi-center investigation by the Canadian Long COVID Autonomic Network (CanLoCAN), reported that long COVID patients with impaired orthostatic hemodynamics exhibited significantly attenuated cardiac autonomic modulation compared to controls14. Their study demonstrated reduced HRV indices and blunted BRS during tilt-table testing, reinforcing the role of ANS dysregulation in the clinical presentation of long COVID. These findings underscore the need for objective physiological assessment in post-COVID populations and inform the rationale for our present investigation14.

The tilt table test, a diagnostic tool commonly used to evaluate orthostatic intolerance, provides a controlled setting to assess autonomic responses during postural changes15. Recent studies have demonstrated that long COVID-19 patients exhibit abnormal HR and BP responses during tilt testing, further supporting the role of ANS dysfunction in this condition16,17. However, the mechanisms underlying these abnormalities remain poorly understood, and there is a critical need for quantitative assessments of autonomic function in long COVID-19 patients.

This study aims to explore the prevalence and severity of ANS dysfunction in long COVID-19 patients by analyzing HRV metrics, and BP signals during tilt table testing. By quantifying autonomic responses to postural changes, this research seeks to uncover the mechanisms of cardiovascular dysregulation in long COVID-19, contributing to a more comprehensive understanding of this condition and informing targeted therapeutic strategies. This study advances the understanding of autonomic dysfunction in long COVID-19 by providing a detailed assessment of autonomic responses during tilt testing, which may have significant clinical implications for managing symptoms such as fatigue, dizziness, and orthostatic intolerance in this population5,18. A unique aspect of this study is the combination of tilt testing with detailed HRV analysis and BP measurement which provides new insights into the autonomic dysregulation underlying long COVID-19. This approach allows for a comprehensive evaluation of both sympathetic and parasympathetic responses during postural changes, offering a deeper understanding of the pathophysiology of long COVID-19 and its clinical implications.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study enrolled 61 participants, 22 healthy controls (CG) and 39 individuals affected by long COVID-19 (SG). We defined long COVID-19 as condition where symptoms of COVID-19 persist for more than 12 weeks after the initial infection and cannot be explained by an alternative diagnosis5,18. It encompasses a wide range of symptoms affecting multiple systems, such as fatigue, dyspnea, chest pain, cognitive dysfunction (“brain fog”), sleep disturbances, and autonomic dysfunction5,18,19. The condition can occur even after mild or moderate acute COVID-19 infection, and its mechanisms are thought to involve persistent inflammation, autonomic imbalance, immune dysregulation, and possible viral persistence5,18. The study was conducted at the Center for Technological Research, Universidade de Mogi das Cruzes, Brazil. All participants provided written informed consent, and the study adhered to the principles outlined in the Helsinki Declaration20.

Inclusion Criteria. SG: Participants aged 18–75 years with a history of confirmed COVID-19 infection (diagnosed via PCR or genomic sequencing) and persistent long COVID-19 symptoms (e.g., fatigue, dyspnea, palpitations, ageusia, anosmia, joint pain, muscle pain, or headaches). CG: Healthy volunteers aged 18–75 years with no history of COVID-19 infection. CG participants were screened using a structured clinical interview confirming no previous symptoms compatible with COVID-19, no prior medical diagnosis or suspected infection, and no recent high-risk exposure. All CG participants were fully vaccinated, further reducing the likelihood of prior symptomatic infection. A PCR test was additionally performed to rule out active SARS-CoV-2 infection at the time of assessment.

Exclusion Criteria. Both groups excluded individuals with a history of stroke, neurological pathology, pneumonia, myocarditis, vertigo, hypertension, or clinically significant abnormalities in spirometry or radiological examinations. Participants taking medications affecting autonomic function (e.g., beta-blockers, amiodarone), pregnant or breastfeeding women, and those with mobility restrictions or recent surgeries were also excluded.

CG participants were screened using a structured clinical interview confirming no previous symptoms compatible with COVID-19, no prior medical diagnosis or suspected infection, and no recent high-risk exposure. All CG participants were fully vaccinated, further reducing the likelihood of prior symptomatic infection. A PCR test was additionally performed to rule out active SARS-CoV-2 infection at the time of assessment.

Pre-test preparation

In accordance with current recommendations for tilt-table testing and POTS evaluation, all participants were instructed to follow standardized pre-test conditions. Participants refrained from caffeine, alcohol, nicotine, and vigorous exercise for at least 24 h before the examination and arrived having consumed only a light meal / in a fasted state for 2–3 h. All assessments were performed at a fixed time of day to reduce circadian influences on autonomic function21,22.

Medication control

Chronic cardiovascular and psychotropic medications were kept stable for at least 2 weeks prior to testing; drugs with acute autonomic effects (e.g., beta-blockers, vasodilators, stimulants) were not initiated or withdrawn in the days immediately preceding the study. When clinically safe and ethically acceptable, short-acting agents with known autonomic effects were withheld for 4–5 half-lives (typically 24–48 h) before testing; otherwise, medication use was recorded and considered in the interpretation of results21,22.

Data acquisition

Heart rate was measured using the TEB PC1000 ECG system (Tecnologia Eletrônica Brasileira) with a sampling frequency of 1.5 kHz. BP was monitored at every minute using an automatic digital sphygmomanometer (Hem-7320-Br by OMRON™) on the left arm. Data were collected during a standardized tilt table test, which included three phases: 1 Supine position (15 min): Participants remained in a supine position to establish baseline cardiovascular parameters; (2) Upright Position (15 min): The bed was tilted to a 75-degree angle to simulate an upright position. HR and BP were continuously monitored; (3) Return to supine position (20 min): Participants were returned to the supine position. HR and BP were monitored until baseline values were restored.

HRV analysis

HRV was analyzed using both time-domain and frequency-domain parameters, following established standards10,11,23. For each phase of the tilt test, parameters were calculated from stable, artifact-free ECG segments: the last 10 min of the 15-min supine phase (minutes 6–15), minutes 2–15 of the 15-min upright tilt phase, and the last 15 min of the 20-min recovery phase (minutes 5–20)10,17,22.

Time-Domain Parameters: Included the standard deviation of NN intervals (SDNN), root mean square of successive differences between NN intervals (RMSSD), percentage of adjacent NN intervals differing by more than 50 ms (pNN50), and mean RR intervals10,11,23.

Frequency-Domain Parameters: Spectral density was calculated for Very Low Frequency (VLF, 0.003–0.04 Hz), Low Frequency (LF, 0.04–0.15 Hz), and High Frequency (HF, 0.15–0.4 Hz) bands. LF and HF were reported in normalized units (LF†, HF†) rather than absolute spectral power because normalized indices minimize inter-individual variability in total power and allow more accurate interpretation of sympathetic–parasympathetic balance during short-term HRV analysis and orthostatic stress. Absolute LF and HF values are strongly influenced by total power fluctuations, noise, and respiratory variability, making them less appropriate for between-group comparisons in this context. The LF/HF ratio was used to evaluate the balance between sympathetic and parasympathetic nervous system activity10,11,23.

BP analysis

Pulse Pressure (PP) was calculated as SBP–DBP in each phase of the tilt table testing24–26. Changes in PP when transitioning from phase 1 to phase 2 were noted as ΔPP1-2CG/SG for the CG and SG respectively. Likewise changes in PP from phase 2 to phase 3 were noted as ΔPP2-3CG/SG. BRS was calculated as the ratio ΔRR/ΔSBP for each transition phase and both group27–29. Values were reported as mean with 95% CI.

Data processing

ECG data were filtered to remove noise and artifacts following established guidelines10,11. Missing or irregular RR intervals were addressed using interpolation techniques to ensure data continuity30. HRV parameters, including time-domain and frequency-domain metrics, were calculated for each phase of the tilt test (resting, tilt, and recovery) using standardized methods31,32. PP signal was processed with a three-point moving average to calculate mean value and SD.

Statistical analysis

The prespecified primary outcome was the orthostatic change in RR interval (ΔRR) from Phase 1 to Phase 2, compared between COVID-19 and control participants. Secondary outcomes included BRS and ΔPP across transitions, and phase-specific autonomic measures (SDNN, LF†, HF†, LF/HF ratio) and PP. Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess data normality. Chi-square / Fischer’s exact test for proportions to demographic data. Group comparisons were conducted using Mann–Whitney U test for non-parametric data, and within-group comparisons were performed using Friedman test for repeated measures. To account for multiple testing across secondary/exploratory outcomes and phases, p-values were adjusted using an FDR procedure (Benjamini–Hochberg). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism v10.4.1. A significance level of p < 0.05 was used for all analyses. Data are presented as mean and 95% CI.

Results

Participants

A total of 61 participants were included: 22 healthy controls (CG) and 39 individuals with long COVID-19 (SG). Table 1 summarizes their baseline demographic, anthropometric, and cardiovascular characteristics. No significant differences were observed between groups regarding age, sex distribution, body weight, height, body mass index (BMI), resting HR, or SBP and DBP (all p > 0.05). The SG tended to be older (mean 39.4 vs. 33.1 years) and included a higher proportion of female (22/39 vs. 7/22), but these differences did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.062 and p = 0.065, respectively). All participants had received at least one dose of a COVID-19 vaccine. These results confirm that both groups were well matched for key baseline variables, minimizing potential confounding in the analysis of autonomic function during tilt-table testing. Discussion

Long-COVID patients exhibit clear autonomic dysregulation during orthostatic stress, demonstrated by altered HRV patterns and impaired ΔPP responses during tilt-table testing.

Autonomic dysregulation in Long COVID-19 patients

The upright phase during the tilt test reveals a blunted sympathetic activation, evidenced by increased RR intervals (Fig. 4A–B), reduced LF† (Fig. 5A–B), increased HF† (Fig. 5C–D) and a reduced LF/HF ratio (Table 3); patterns opposite to what is expected in healthy autonomic adaptation. These findings align with prior studies linking long COVID-19 to autonomic dysregulation22,33, and underscore the potential benefit of interventions such as HRV biofeedback, exercise therapy, and pharmacological support to restore balance34–36.

Although RMSSD and pNN50, parasympathetic markers, were slightly elevated in the Long COVID-19 group during phase 2, these differences were not statistically significant (Table 3) and should be interpreted with caution. Rather than indicating enhanced vagal tone, these findings may reflect a dysregulated autonomic response, where the expected sympathetic activation upon standing is blunted. These results merit a measured interpretation acknowledging the ongoing debate regarding the validity of these indices. As stated by Billman37, the LF/HF ratio is an oversimplified and often inaccurate metric for directly quantifying cardiac sympatho-vagal balance, as both spectral components can be influenced by a complex interplay of autonomic and non-autonomic factors.

Therefore, we did not rely on these metrics in isolation. Rather, the observed pattern, reduced LF† and LF/HF ratio alongside non-significant elevations in parasympathetic markers (RMSSD, pNN50), is interpreted as evidence of a broader autonomic dysregulation characterized by blunted sympathetic activation in response to orthostatic stress, rather than an enhanced vagal tone.

BRS, derived from ΔRR/ΔSBP, showed a non-significant trend toward higher values in controls compared with Long COVID-19 participants during the phase 2 to phase 3 transition (Table 4). This indicates that baroreflex gain remains largely preserved, implying that the dysautonomia seen in Long COVID-19 participants is more consistent with downstream autonomic or vascular dysfunction rather than primary baroreflex impairment.

Taken together, these findings, illustrated in Figs. 3–6 and summarized in Table 3, provide compelling evidence for impaired autonomic responses to postural changes in Long COVID-19 participants12,34. This likely contributes to the persistent symptoms of fatigue, dizziness, and orthostatic intolerance reported by patients.

Reduced HRV as a marker of ANS dysregulation

The observed reductions in HRV, including reductions in the LF† component (Fig. 5AB), indicate impaired autonomic modulation in long COVID-19 participants, particularly reflecting reduced vagal and baroreflex activity rather than sympathetic overactivity. These findings are consistent with prior studies reporting that COVID-19 is associated with lasting disruptions in autonomic control and diminished HRV, changes that have been linked to increased cardiovascular risk10,11,23. The HF† component, a marker of parasympathetic activity, exhibited a reduced decline during tilt in long COVID-19 participants (Fig. 5C–D), indicating impaired parasympathetic withdrawal and contributing to abnormal autonomic adaptation to orthostatic stress38. These abnormalities highlight the potential role of autonomic dysfunction in the pathophysiology of Long COVID-19 and underscore the need for targeted interventions to restore autonomic balance.

Potential mechanisms underlying autonomic dysfunction

The autonomic dysregulation observed in our long COVID-19 cohort may be contextualized within several mechanisms proposed in the literature, though our study does not provide direct evidence to substantiate them. Persistent low-grade inflammation35,39,40, autoimmune targeting of autonomic receptors41,42, and neuroinflammation have been suggested as contributors to autonomic dysfunction in post-COVID-19 conditions35,36. Such processes could impair neural signaling, reduce baroreflex efficiency (Table 3, Fig. 6), and disrupt sympathetic-parasympathetic balance (Table 4 and Fig. 5), potentially explaining the blunted autonomic responses seen during tilt testing14. Further research incorporating biomarker and imaging assessments is needed to clarify the pathophysiology underlying these functional alterations.

Baroreflex impairment and hemodynamic instability

Our findings indicate that long COVID-19 patients exhibit patterns of altered baroreflex engagement and impaired hemodynamic adaptation during tilt testing, rather than primary baroreflex failure. While overall mean BP levels were preserved between groups (Table 2), a finding consistent with some prior reports6, the long COVID-19 group (SG) demonstrated attenuated autonomic responses to orthostatic stress. This was characterized by a blunted sympathetic response, evidenced by a significant reduction in LF†, and impaired parasympathetic withdrawal, reflected by an elevated HF† during the upright tilt phase (Table 3; Figs. 5A–D). This resulted in a significantly decreased LF/HF ratio (Table 3), a pattern that opposes the expected sympathetic shift seen in healthy controls and aligns with observations of blunted autonomic reactivity in post-COVID cohorts 14,33.

Critically, the computed BRS, derived from the ΔRR/ΔSBP ratio, did not differ significantly between groups during either postural transition (Table 4). This key finding suggests that the fundamental baroreflex gain remains intact in long COVID-19, contrasting with some studies that reported reduced BRS13,14. This discrepancy may relate to methodological differences or cohort characteristics. Therefore, our observed dysregulation likely stems from downstream autonomic or vascular dysfunction, such as impaired efferent sympathetic recruitment, altered vascular responsiveness, or inadequate vagal withdrawal, rather than a primary defect in the baroreceptor reflex arc itself, a pathophysiology also suggested in other forms of dysautonomia41,42.

This downstream dysregulation is reflected in the dynamics of hemodynamic adjustment. While healthy CG showed a pronounced PP reduction during tilt and a robust recovery upon supine return (Fig. 6A), long COVID-19 participants SG exhibited a significantly attenuated change in PP during the recovery transition. This is quantified by a smaller ΔPP from Phase 2 to Phase 3 (ΔPP2-3) in the SG compared to the CG (Table 4), despite both groups reaching similar final PP values in Phase 3 (p = 0.242, Table 3). This blunted dynamic response suggests inefficient cardiovascular compensation and altered vascular reactivity during postural changes24, providing a physiological basis for the high prevalence of orthostatic symptoms like dizziness, fatigue, and exercise intolerance reported in this population22,34.

Taken together, these results, preserved BRS (Table 4) alongside abnormal LF†, and HF† (Table 3, Fig. 5) and an impaired dynamic PP response during recovery (Table 4), support the conclusion that long COVID-19 is associated with a discoordination of integrated autonomic and hemodynamic responses to orthostatic stress. This altered autonomic engagement, detectable through combined tilt testing, HRV, and analysis of pressure dynamics, represents a key mechanism underlying persistent orthostatic intolerance in these patients and highlights the value of multifaceted autonomic assessment to guide management15,21.

Clinical implications

Our findings have direct clinical implications for managing long-COVID-19 Syndrome. The tilt-test results, consistent with the literature14, point to autonomic dysfunction characterized by blunted sympathetic activation rather than generalized overactivity. This specific autonomic phenotype demands personalized therapeutic strategies.

First-line management should prioritize non-pharmacological interventions aimed at improving vascular and autonomic function without further depressing an already compromised sympathetic response. This includes structured physical reconditioning43,44, dietary optimization with omega-3 fatty acids19,44, adequate hydration and salt replenishment, and the use of compression therapy22.

Pharmacological interventions should be reserved for selected cases with objective indications, such as persistent symptomatic tachycardia or a documented hyperadrenergic subtype (e.g., POTS). In these situations, agents like ivabradine or low-dose beta-blockers may be considered under specialist supervision28,41. Anti-inflammatory therapies may also play a role when there is evidence of cytokine-mediated dysautonomia35,37.

Long-term autonomic monitoring, facilitated by wearable technology16,23, combined with periodic tilt-table testing, emerges as a valuable tool for tracking recovery, differentiating phenotypes, and guiding dynamic, individualized management.

In summary, the convergence of evidence, including the reduced LF† modulation and elevated HF† during tilt (Table 3, Fig. 5), the blunted RR-interval shortening upon standing (Fig. 4), the attenuated pulse-pressure (ΔPP) recovery in the post-tilt phase (Table 3, Fig. 6), and the overall blunted cardiovascular responses to orthostatic stress—reinforces autonomic dysregulation as a central pathophysiological axis in long COVID-19 Syndrome. Incorporating structured autonomic assessment into clinical practice is crucial not only for precise diagnosis but also for informing targeted interventions aimed at restoring cardiovascular balance and improving the quality of life for this patient population 33,34,38.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small, which may limit the generalizability of our findings. The lack of a priori matching or stratification for sex and BMI remains a limitation of our study design. Future investigations should consider propensity score matching or multivariate adjustment from the outset to strengthen causal inferences regarding autonomic dysfunction in long COVID-19. Second, the cross-sectional design prevents causal inferences regarding the relationship between long COVID-19 and autonomic dysfunction. Longitudinal studies are needed to characterize the temporal trajectory of autonomic recovery in individuals with long COVID-19. Third, limitations inherent to HRV analysis must be acknowledged. HRV is influenced by a variety of physiological and environmental factors such as age, gender, circadian rhythms, breathing patterns, stress, and medication use. These factors can confound results if not appropriately accounted for. Furthermore, discrepancies between short-term and long-term HRV recordings, variability in methodological approaches (e.g., time-domain vs. frequency-domain measures), and lack of standardization in data acquisition may affect reliability and comparability. Importantly, HRV is not a disease-specific biomarker and must be interpreted in conjunction with clinical and physiological data 10,11. Moreover, the interpretation of HRV during tilt table testing is limited by physiological variability and methodological challenges. Postural changes can provoke inconsistent autonomic responses, and HRV may not accurately reflect the dynamic balance between sympathetic and parasympathetic modulation under orthostatic stress45.

Finally, we did not assess the influence of specific comorbidities or medications on autonomic function, which may have introduced unmeasured confounding.

Despite these limitations, HRV remains a valuable, non-invasive tool for capturing dynamic changes in autonomic regulation. When interpreted within the context of tilt testing protocols and individual variability, HRV offers meaningful information about autonomic regulation, especially in conditions like long COVID-19 where dysautonomia may be subtle or heterogeneous.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that long-COVID patients exhibit a distinct pattern of autonomic dysregulation characterized by a blunted cardiovascular response to orthostatic stress. During tilt-table testing, patients showed reduced sympathetic activation (↓LF†) and attenuated parasympathetic withdrawal (↑HF†) (Table 3, Fig. 5), along with an impaired recovery of pulse pressure (ΔPP2-3) upon return to supine position (Table 4). BRS remained intact (Table 4), indicating dysfunction downstream of the baroreflex arc.

These alterations in both cardiac autonomic modulation and vascular dynamics explain key clinical symptoms such as orthostatic intolerance, dizziness, palpitations, and fatigue.

From a clinical standpoint, tilt testing combined with HRV and PP analysis is essential to detect this otherwise hidden dysautonomia. Management should prioritize non-pharmacologic strategies; ncluding graded exercise, fluid/salt optimization, and compression therapy, reserving pharmacologic interventions for severe or persistent cases.

In summary, long-COVID involves objective autonomic impairment that can be identified through structured testing, guiding targeted management to improve patient outcomes.
