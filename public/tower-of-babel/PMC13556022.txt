Assessment of oral health knowledge, attitude, and practices among school students in the Qassim Region: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Shaimaa Mohamed Saeed Ata, Medhat Ekrami Abouseif Mohamed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOral health is an essential component of general health and well-being, especially in childhood and adolescence. Evaluation of students’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) regarding oral health is critical to establishing effective school-based interventions. This cross-sectional study assessed oral health knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) among primary and intermediate school students in the Qassim Region of Saudi Arabia, with an emphasis on the students’ perceptions of causes and protective factors of tooth decay.MethodsA cross-sectional study design was conducted. The final sample involved 321 primary and intermediate school students aged 7–15 years from around the Qassim Region, Saudi Arabia. This study collected data through a structured, investigator-administered questionnaire and using clinical oral health indices (DMFT, DI, CI, and OHI). Descriptive statistics, along with chi-square tests, were applied to investigate any gender differences and distributions of responses. The researchers obtained ethical clearance, informed assent, and parental consent before the data collection.ResultsStudents had moderate-to-high levels of KAP regarding oral health. The most frequently identified causes of tooth decay by students were frequent consumption of sugary food (82.0%) and poor oral hygiene (77.0%), whereas regular tooth brushing (87.2%) and use of fluoridated toothpaste (76.3%) were the most frequently reported protective factors against tooth decay. There were no statistically significant differences (p < 0.05) by gender across each of the KAP items. Notably, while awareness of oral health in general was high, there were knowledge gaps regarding fluoride use and regular dental visits.ConclusionPrimary and intermediate school students in the Qassim Region have high levels of oral health knowledge, but gaps remain in oral health behaviors. This study highlights the need for comprehensive, integrated, school-based oral health approaches that focus on converting knowledge into long-term change. The data specific to the Qassim Region may inform the national oral health strategy and provide direction to oral health interventions that are culturally appropriate.

Introduction

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), oral health is defined as “a state of being free from chronic mouth and facial pain, oral and throat cancer, oral infection and sores, periodontal disease, tooth decay, tooth loss, and other diseases and disorders that limit an individual’s capacity in biting, chewing, smiling, speaking, and psychosocial well-being” [1]. This is because maintaining optimal oral health has been recognized as a basic human right at every stage of life, from childhood through old age [2]. The level of oral hygiene practices and their relevance to overall health can be established during childhood and adolescence, and these age groups are critical periods for the development of lifelong health behaviors [3]. Despite this necessity, oral health has frequently been overlooked in public health priorities, particularly in contexts where awareness, education, and preventive measures are limited [3, 4].

Qassim province is located in the central part of Saudi Arabia, near the geographic center of the Arabian Peninsula. The administrative capital is Buraydah, which hosts nearly half of the region’s population. Several studies in Saudi Arabia have demonstrated a significant variation in knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) related to oral health among school-aged populations, with some of these disparities compounded by socioeconomic status, cultural behaviors, and regional diversity affecting access to dental care services [2–4]. A recent systematic review by AlJasser et al. (2023) assessing oral health knowledge and practices among Saudi adolescents reported significant gaps in oral health awareness and preventive behaviors, highlighting the need for targeted educational interventions [2]. In a study conducted in Jeddah, the students had good knowledge outcomes and oral hygiene practices, and those practices among the female students were significantly better than those of male students [3].

A study conducted in the Asir Region among university students reported that those studying health sciences demonstrated better oral health knowledge compared to students from non-health disciplines [4]. Although the majority of students recognized that regular dental visits and effective brushing were important behaviors, many reported putting off seeing a dentist until pain was experienced [3]. Furthermore, the force of habit, such as traditionally using miswak, was mentioned multiple times by male students and was reflected by the cultural norm [5]. However, despite improvements in awareness, a considerable proportion of school-aged children continue to demonstrate gaps in oral health knowledge and preventive practices.

A study in the Qassim Region reported brushing regularly but not flossing or having a dental care checkup routinely: females tended to brush more often and males more often to use miswak [6]. A study in Abha showed over 80% acknowledged the interrelationship between oral and general health, but almost 70% of students from Abha admitted to seeking dental care only in emergencies [7]. Another study in Al-Kharj demonstrated poor hygiene practices and low levels of awareness among the public-school students, suggesting that intervention-based educational programs are needed [8]. AlHussain conducted a study that revealed that the oral health education initiative delivered online due to COVID-19 in Riyadh had more positive impacts on students (improvement of knowledge and oral health attitude) [9]. A study conducted by AlShaya et al. showed that teachers help in forming children’s health behaviors [10]. A recent study by Almalki et al. assessed the oral health knowledge in public school children of a Saudi subpopulation. The results indicated that although most children were aware of basic oral health concepts, such as the need for brushing, there was a considerable lack of awareness regarding other preventive practices like flossing, dietary habits related to oral health, and regular dental checkups. The study showed that these knowledge gaps are major contributors to poor oral health in children [11].

While many studies have explored oral health knowledge and behavior in a number of locations across Saudi Arabia, there is very little research on the oral health of school-aged children residing in the Qassim Region. Cultural attitudes about oral hygiene, accessibility to dental services, and the availability of local school programs all vary by region and will influence the oral health practices of local students, making regional data necessary. The present study was carried out to evaluate the knowledge, attitudes, and practices towards oral health among school students in Qassim. The research aims to highlight the gaps in knowledge and behavior, with a view to providing evidence for developing future educational programs or preventive interventions that will reduce oral health disparities and improve oral health behaviors among young people. It also attempted to focus on perceptions of causes and protective factors of tooth decay.

Methods

Study design and setting

A cross-sectional descriptive study was conducted in which oral health knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) were assessed among school children in primary and intermediate levels across public and private schools in the Qassim Region. This design was selected because it allowed for the capture of cross-sectional, population-based data at one time point for the determination of prevalent behaviors and knowledge gaps. The study was approved by the Ethical Scientific Research Committee (HAP-01-Q-116-), Buraydah Private Colleges, Saudi Arabia, on 24/02/2025 with approval registry number ESRC-005/2025.

Study population and participants

A stratified random sampling method was used, with stratification based on gender. Since schools in Saudi Arabia are gender-segregated, boys’ and girls’ schools were considered two separate strata. Within each stratum, schools were randomly selected, and from each selected school, students were randomly invited to participate. This ensured proportional representation of male and female students in the final sample.

A total of 530 students were invited to participate across the selected boys’ and girls’ schools, but only 470 accepted the invitation. Of these, 321 completed the questionnaire and clinical examination (response rate: 68.3%). Only complete questionnaires were included in the final sample.

Sample size and sampling technique

The sample size was determined by a standard calculation for cross-sectional study designs on the basis of stratified random sampling. A conservative prevalence of 50% was chosen in order to maximize the power and for representation in the major strata of interest. The decision to use 50% prevalence was motivated by previous studies carried out in Saudi Arabia [11] that showed moderate to low oral health knowledge among school-aged children. The sample size was calculated to include a 95% confidence level and a 5% margin of error, and the final sample of 321 students accounts for an adjustment of the sample size. A stratified random sampling method was applied to ensure representation from different genders.

Data collection

1- Questionnaire administration

A structured questionnaire was administered during normal school hours. The questionnaire was developed in Arabic to be appropriate to the language as well as the culture. For younger children, from 7 to 9 years old, interviews were completed with the help of an interviewer so that children could understand the questions being asked of them. In accordance with the Saudi educational system, the data collection was performed separately in male and female schools. Data for male schools was collected by male research assistants, while female researchers collected data for female schools. Data collection took place during regular school hours. The research team first assembled students in their classrooms so they could explain the purpose of the study to the students. After obtaining students’ assent, there was a structured ques○○tionnaire administered to students under the supervision of the research team, followed by clinical oral examinations that were completed using genuine clinical dental supplies in sufficient light. The questionnaire was piloted on a sample of 30 students to assess clarity, reliability, and time to completion. The pilot study included 30 participants aged between 7 and 15 years who were not part of the final study sample. The pilot testing and feedback required adjustment for unclear items. Fluoride use referred to the use of fluoridated toothpaste, professionally applied fluoride treatments, or other fluoride-containing dental products as reported by the students. The final instrument consisted of 15 questions and comprised four sections:

Items were adapted from previously validated questionnaires used in international studies assessing oral health knowledge, attitudes, and practices among school populations [12–13]. The questionnaire included multiple-choice and Likert scale items, thus allowing for quantitative comparison of responses.

Trained research assistants collected information in conjunction with the school administrator. Participants were told before completing the questionnaire about the purposes of the study and that participating was on a voluntary basis, as well as that all answers would be kept confidential. Once completed, they were collected immediately after completion of the questionnaires in order to reduce potential data loss and to encourage participants’ responses to be as honest as possible. To verify the validity of the survey instrument, expert review and pilot data collection (n = 30) were done. To determine the internal consistency of the questionnaire, the Cronbach’s alpha test was employed, yielding an acceptable reliability coefficient (α = 0.82).

2- Clinical examination

Before collecting any data, the examiners received training in standardized methods for using the DMFT and OHI indices in compliance with the World Health Organization guidelines. Calibration sessions were held before the study began in order to ensure that all of the examiners would be consistent in their measurements and assessment of patients’ dental health. A Cohen’s Kappa statistic was used to assess inter-examiner reliability and found to have adequate levels of agreement (kappa value > 0.80). Clinical oral examinations were conducted by four trained dental examiners under standardized conditions within the school environment. Oral examination was standardized for each participant based on the World Health Organization (WHO) criteria; dental caries experience was measured using the Decayed, Missing, Filled Teeth (DMFT) index. Oral hygiene was determined by the Oral Hygiene Index, which includes both the debris index and calculus index components.

Caries risk categorization was determined using a composite assessment approach based on DMFT scores, Oral Hygiene Index (OHI), and self-reported fluoride exposure. Students were classified as lower (low-risk), medium (medium-risk), or higher (high-risk) caries risks: those with lower DMFT scores and good oral hygiene were considered low risk, those with moderate DMFT values were considered moderate risk, and those with higher DMFT scores and poor oral hygiene indicators were classified as high risk. Each questionnaire was linked to the corresponding clinical examination findings for the same participant using an anonymous identification code.

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the Ethical Scientific Research Committee (HAP-01-Q-116-), Buraydah Private Colleges, Saudi Arabia, on 24/02/2025 with approval registry number ESRC-005/2025. Parents/guardians gave their informed consent, and children provided their oral assent. Confidentiality and anonymity were maintained throughout this study. The parental consent procedure involved Arabic-language written consent forms explaining the study procedures in terms of questionnaire administration, oral screening, and dental health education. These consent forms were sent home to parents/guardians for their signature in advance of the school-visit day. The form clarified that participation was voluntary, that data would be kept private, and that participants could withdraw without any negative consequences.

Then, parents were asked to choose whether to “Agree” or “Do not agree” and sign the document.

Verbal assent was also collected from each child before participating to make sure they understood the study in a simplified, age-appropriate manner. Purpose, procedures, and voluntary participation were explained to the participants by the investigators. Children received informed assent that they could opt out or withdraw at any time with no repercussions. This study included only those who had given verbal assent. This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Statistical analysis

Data were coded and subsequently captured into Microsoft Excel and exported to IBM SPSS Statistics version 25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) for analysis. We used descriptive statistics, which included frequency and percentage for sociodemographic variables as well as KAP responses. The chi-square test was employed to analyze associations between categorical variables, including gender and responses to knowledge, attitude, and practice items. Behavioral variables were coded numerically to allow correlation analysis. A Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to explore the relationships between oral health measures (Debris Index [DI], Calculus Index [CI], and DMFT score) and certain behavioral factors. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05, and a 95% confidence interval was used to determine significance.

Results

Participants’ characteristics

The study sample had 321 school-aged students, of which 68.2% were male and 31.8% were female. The distribution of students between public and private schools was analyzed; however, no statistically significant differences were observed in oral health knowledge and practices between the two groups. Based on the caries-risk assessment, most students were in the low-risk category (71.0%), followed by moderate risk (20.6%) and high risk (8.4%). With respect to fluoride exposure, only 11.8% of the students reported utilizing fluoride products, while 88.2% reported not utilizing any fluoride products at all (Table 1).

Perceptions regarding causes and protective factors of tooth decay

Comparison of participants’ responses to the items assessing perceived causes (Q10) and preventable factors (Q11) of tooth decay revealed no statistically significant gender differences. Frequent consumption of sweets (82.0%) and incorrect oral hygiene practices (77.0%), followed by not visiting the dentist (50.2%) and hereditary susceptibility (26.2%), were the top-cited causes. Similarly, the highest-perceived preventive factors were regular tooth brushing (87.2%) and using fluoride toothpaste (76.3%), with additional recognition of consuming calcium-rich food (61.7%) and drinking plenty of water (53.6%). The distribution of responses between male and female respondents was comparable across all options, indicating a predominantly shared belief regarding the causality and prevention of dental caries among school children.

Correlation analysis

Pearson correlation analysis was used to analyze the relationship between oral health indicators (the Debris Index—DI, Calculus Index—CI, and DMFT score) and selected behavioral variables (i.e., frequency of brushing teeth (Q7), flossing (Q8), consumption of sweetened candy (Q14), and use of fluoride products). There was a moderate negative relationship between brushing frequency and DI (r = -0.28, p < 0.05) and CI (r = -0.25, p < 0.05), indicating that the more frequently a person brushes their teeth, the lower their levels of debris and calculus are. According to the study’s findings, those who frequently flossed had lower DI (r = -0.21, p < 0.05) and CI (r = -0.19, p < 0.05) scores than those who did not. This indicates that good oral hygiene can typically be found in those who frequently floss. Conversely, high consumption of sugary candy was positively correlated to DI (r = 0.32, p < 0.05), CI (r = 0.29, p < 0.05), and DMFT scores (r = 0.22, p < 0.05), which indicates that people with high levels of sugar consumption tend to have poorer oral health. Also found an inverse relationship between fluoride use and DI (r = -0.18, p < 0.05) and CI (r = -0.15, p < 0.05). The total data suggest that there are statistically and clinically significant associations between conventional preventive behaviors and oral health, as measured by the clinical indicators of oral health in this study.

Discussion

In a cross-sectional study, primary and intermediate school students in the Qassim Region were evaluated for oral health knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors regarding prevention of dental caries. High awareness of the common causes and preventive measures of tooth decay was observed, with frequent consumption of sweets and sweets-containing foodstuffs and poor oral hygiene habits most often reported as etiologic factors. Daily tooth brushing and use of fluoridated toothpastes were firmly established as preventive measures, demonstrating compliance with global oral health policy and WHO recommendations [14–16].

The lack of statistically significant differences in gender for most variables indicates that oral health perceptions are relatively constant among males and females in this population. This differs from findings reported in Jeddah and Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, where female students tended to have a greater understanding of oral health knowledge and application of better, consistent oral hygiene practices [17, 18]. Similarly, research from different countries such as India, Nigeria, and Turkey showed that students’ oral health varies by gender [19–21]. These differences were explained by different social and cultural norms, the role of parents, and access to health education; however, the gender similarity we reported may reflect the standardization of health education programs or school-based awareness in the Qassim Region.

There were significant gaps in behavioral practices, despite good levels of knowledge. Most students knew fluoride is an important preventive measure and that regular visits to the dentist are important for maintaining oral health, but fewer students stated they regularly visited the dentist or used fluoride, indicating a knowledge-practice gap; this aligns with findings of previous studies in both high-income and middle-income countries [22–24] and highlights the need for programs and initiatives that not only provide information but also address gaps in behavioral practice. Other circumstances may involve fear of dental procedures, absence of parental support or follow-through, or limited access to preventive services; these issues should be explored further.

The combined use of validated clinical indices (DMFT, DI, CI, and OHI) and structured questionnaires enhances the reliability of our findings and facilitates the substantial comparison to international benchmarks. The mean DMFT score within this cohort was on par with WHO targets for schoolchildren, but the untreated decay remains troubling. The level of untreated decay implies that while the individuals are aware of the need for treatment, they are unable to gain timely access to restorative care, a problem found in other regional studies [25, 26].

The correlation analysis demonstrated that oral health behaviors such as regular tooth brushing and flossing were negatively associated with debris and calculus indices. This finding indicates that improved oral hygiene practices contribute to better clinical oral health outcomes among school students. School-based oral health promotion programs and preventive strategies are recommended to bridge the gap between knowledge and actual oral health practices.

From a public health perspective, the findings serve as an important indicator of the need for oral health education to become a component of school health education as a whole. Evidence from Finland and Japan indicates that integrating oral health promotion into an ongoing, sustained curriculum of oral health can lead to a significant improvement in knowledge and clinical outcomes [27, 28]. The impact of such initiatives may be significantly increased in Saudi Arabia by utilizing the school nurse as a resource, making use of the interactive digital tools, and educating and involving parents through community engagement.

In addition, the study provides the national oral health surveillance view of the value of locally specific information. Because of the clear difference in socioeconomic and cultural backgrounds of Saudi Arabia, localized assessment needs to be performed for both asset utilization and best intervention targeting. The findings of the study have merit for future policy formulation, particularly with regard to preventive care and planning of age-specific, culturally situated oral health education curricula.

Limitations

The purpose of this research was to find out what primary and intermediate children in Qassim have learned about their oral health, how they feel about that education, and their oral health practices. Several limitations are present in this study, including its cross-sectional design, which prevents conclusions about cause-and-effect relationships; the possibility of interviewer bias; and the relatively small sample size. The questionnaire was standardized but read aloud, which could introduce biases or influence students’ responses. Additionally, the study was conducted in a limited number of schools, making it difficult to generalize findings to other regions of the country or to different age groups in Saudi Arabia. Future research is encouraged to use a longitudinal approach, include a wider geographic area, or collect anonymous self-reports from participants. These changes would provide more diverse data and enhance the overall credibility of such studies.

Conclusion

The research shows that students in the Qassim Region have moderate to satisfactory knowledge about dental caries and overall oral hygiene. However, there are still gaps in oral health practices, especially in the use of fluoride and regular visits to the dentist. This highlights the need for targeted interventions that encourage behavioral change. Health education programs should be available for both boys and girls and should make it easy for students to access accurate information and preventive services. Teachers and policymakers must actively support and implement these programs.
