Explanatory Factors for Neck-Related Disability, Headache, and Pain After Cervical Decompression Surgery in Individuals With Cervical Radiculopathy and Neck-Related Headache: A National Registry-Based Study With 2-Year Follow-up
Authors: Jard Svensson, Håkan Löfgren, Anna Hermansen, Peter Zsigmond, Anneli Peolsson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo investigate explanatory factors associated with neck-related disability, headache, and neck and arm pain 2 years after anterior cervical decompression and fusion (ACDF) or posterior cervical foraminotomy (PCF) in individuals with cervical radiculopathy (CR) and neck-related headache. MethodsThis registry-based cohort study used prospectively collected data with a 2-year follow-up from the Swedish Spine Registry. Individuals who underwent ACDF (n=1,866) or PCF (n=357) for CR between January 2016 and March 2021 and reported preoperative neck-related headache were included. Standard analysis of covariance models were used to identify explanatory factors for the absolute values of Neck Disability Index (NDI), NDI-headache, Numerical Rating Scale (NRS) neck pain, and NRS arm pain at the 2-year follow-up after ACDF or PCF. ResultsExplanatory factors explained 33% and 32% of the neck-related disability, 20% and 35% of the headache, 21% and 13% of the neck pain, and 16% and 24% of the arm pain at 2-year follow-up after ACDF and PCF, respectively, in individuals with CR and neck-related headache. The preoperative value of these outcomes had the greatest impact, except for neck and arm pain after PCF, where neck-related disability had a greater impact. ConclusionNeck-related disability, headache, and neck and arm pain after ACDF and PCF in individuals with CR and neck-related headache are associated with preoperative levels of these outcomes, as well as demographic, lifestyle, surgical factors and self-reported health, that should be considered before surgery. The models explained 13%–35% of the variance in the outcomes, indicating that a substantial proportion remains unexplained.Keywords: Cervical radiculopathy, Disability evaluation, Headache, Pain, Decompression

INTRODUCTION

Approximately 50% of all individuals with cervical radiculopathy (CR) experience headache [1-3]. Physical function and quality of life can be reduced by headache associated with neck pain [4]. Anterior cervical decompression and fusion (ACDF) and posterior cervical foraminotomy (PCF) are 2 common surgical techniques for treating CR [5-7] by reducing the compression of the affected nerve [8,9].

Previous research has shown that male sex [10-12], nonsmoking [11-13], low preoperative disability [12,13], good hand strength, good active neck mobility, low preoperative pain intensity [12], and shorter preoperative pain duration [13] are predictive factors of a good outcome after ACDF. Few studies have investigated the predictors of a good outcome after PCF [14,15]. The risk of revision surgery after PCF decreases with a lack of preoperative neck pain [14], and symptom duration <3 months is associated with a better outcome [15].

For individuals with neck pain and headache, one common explanation for headache is referred pain from the cervical spine to the head via the trigeminal nerve and/or C1–3 spinal nerves [4,16]. For those with lower cervical spine pathology, possible explanations for headache include referred pain [2], muscle tension, kinematic abnormalities in the neck [16], and limited range of motion [2,17].

Despite approximately 50% of all individuals with CR reporting headache [1-3] and both ACDF [18-21] and PCF can improve headache [18], no previous studies have investigated the preoperative explanatory factors of a good outcome after ACDF and PCF in a population of individuals with CR and neck-related headache. In a large national registry-based study, we previously investigated and compared the outcomes of ACDF and PCF regarding neck-related disability, headache, and neck and arm pain in individuals with CR and neck-related headache. Both techniques improved neck-related disability, headache, and neck and arm pain, but PCF seems to decrease headache more over time [18]. However, we do not know which factors in that study were associated with the outcomes in neck-related disability, headache, and neck and arm pain. This is important knowledge for clinical practice, as it can help surgeons assess the potential outcomes of surgery and improve preoperative advice and information for patients. Therefore, the aim of the present study was to investigate explanatory factors associated with neck-related disability, headache, and neck and arm pain 2 years after ACDF or PCF in individuals with CR and neck-related headache.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This is a national registry-based cohort study of prospectively collected data with 2-year follow-up in Swespine, the Swedish Spine Registry, with a unique research question and using an exploratory approach. The study protocol was registered prior to data extraction and analysis (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT06113263). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Swedish Ethical Review Authority (Dnr: 2023-00687-01 and 2023-07534-02). All individuals registered in Swespine provided consent to participate in the registry using the opt-out principle and were informed that their data would be used for research purposes. The data were obtained after receiving ethical approval and an authorized application for data retrieval from Swespine.

The study sample included all individuals registered for their first diagnosis-specific episode of care in Swespine who underwent surgery for CR caused by a cervical herniated disc or cervical foraminal stenosis and fulfilled the following criteria: underwent ACDF or PCF for CR between January 2016 and March 2021, age≥18 years, and preoperatively rated 1 (I have slight headaches, which come infrequently) to 5 (I have headaches almost all the time) on the headache item of the Neck Disability Index (NDI) [22]. Exclusion criteria were myelopathy or previous neck surgery. Individuals registered for ACDF underwent one of the following procedures: cage without a plate (48%, n=901), cage with a plate (31%, n=577), autograft with a plate (14%, n=266), or autograft without a plate (7%, n=122).

1. Data Collection

Patient-reported data were obtained preoperatively and at the 2-year follow-up via written questionnaires sent by post to each individual. Surgeon-reported data were collected by questionnaires shortly after the surgery [23].

2. Outcome Measures

Neck-related disability was measured with the NDI, which consists of 10 items (pain intensity, personal care, lifting, reading, headache, concentration, work, driving, sleeping, recreation) [22,23]. Each item is rated on a scale from 0 to 5, resulting in a total point score of 50 (complete disability). This point score is then converted into a percentage score ranging from 0 (no disability) to 100 (complete disability) [24].

Headache was measured with the headache item of the NDI (NDI-headache). The item is rated as 0 (I have no headaches at all), 1 (I have slight headaches, which come infrequently), 2 (I have moderate headaches, which come infrequently), 3 (I have moderate headaches, which come frequently), 4 (I have severe headaches, which come frequently), or 5 (I have headaches almost all the time) [22,23]. Neck and arm pain intensity during the past week was measured on an 11-point Numerical Rating Scale (NRS; 0=no pain/no problem to 10=worst pain) [23,25].

Demographic variables included sex (male or female), age, level of education (primary education, high school, or college/university), and work status (working/student, age retirement, sick leave, disability pension). Lifestyle variables included body mass index (BMI, in kg/m2), smoking (yes or no), and walking ability (≤1 km or >1 km). Self-reported health variables included the duration of neck pain (<1 year, 1–2 years, and >2 years), duration of arm pain (<1 year, 1–2 years, and >2 years), health-related quality of life (EuroQol 5 dimensions and 3 levels [EQ-5D-3L] index; -0.594=worse than death to 1=best possible health status), comorbidities that greatly limit the quality of life (yes or no), and use of pain medication (no, yes sometimes, or yes regular). The surgical variable was the number of operated levels (1, 2, or 3–5). Diagnosis was a background characteristic (Table 1) [23].

3. Data Analysis

Background characteristics are presented as numbers, percentages, means, and standard deviations for all individuals and stratified by the type of surgery in Table 1. Independent-samples t-tests were used for continuous variables, and chi-square or Fisher exact tests for categorical variables, as appropriate, to compare background characteristics.

To identify explanatory factors for the absolute values of the dependent variables NDI, NDI-headache, NRS neck pain, and NRS arm pain at the 2-year follow-up after ACDF and PCF, standard analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) models were used. Variable selection was performed using LASSO (Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator) regression with 10-fold crossvalidation, conducted separately for each dependent variable and surgical procedure. Explanatory variables selected at the optimal penalty parameter (λ) were subsequently included as independent variables in ANCOVA models, with the 2-year follow-up value as the dependent variable and the corresponding preoperative value included as a covariate. All explanatory factors were preoperative values, except for the number of operated levels. Categorical variables were encoded as dummy variables before performing LASSO regression. These variables are presented in Supplementary Table 1, along with their corresponding descriptive statistics and the reference category for each categorical variable. The minimal detectable partial eta squared for each model was calculated and compared with the partial eta squared of each explanatory factor. The results of the final ANCOVA models are presented as beta coefficients (B), 95% confidence intervals (CIs), standardized beta coefficient (β), p-values, partial eta squared (ηp2), whether the minimal detectable partial eta squared threshold was reached, number (n), and adjusted R-squared values (Tables 2–5). Ten-fold cross-validation effectively replaced the bootstrap method by ensuring robust internal validation and correction for optimism bias. Therefore, the results of post hoc selection ANCOVA are exploratory and not confirmatory.

The independent variable working status was study-specific and created using data from Swespine regarding employment, retirement pension, and sickness benefits. Data cleaning involved adjusting individual incorrectly entered values. IBM SPSS Statistics ver. 26.0 (IBM Co., USA) and R packages was used for the calculations.

A Spearman correlation was calculated between NDI-headache and headache intensity measured with visual analogue scale (VAS) (100 mm) (average of headache intensity as current, best, and worst during the past week), using previously collected consecutive data from individuals with CR at a Swedish university hospital [26]. A strong correlation was found (Spearman ρ=0.86, p<0.001, n=118).

RESULTS

Between January 2016 and March 2021, a total of 3,524 adult individuals underwent ACDF or PCF for CR and were registered for their first diagnosis-specific episode of care in Swespine. Among these individuals, we know that 2,453 individuals (70%) reported some degree of neck-related headache preoperatively and, of these, 2,223 individuals met all the criteria (no previous neck surgery and/or myelopathy) for this study (ACDF, n=1,866; PCF, n=357) (Supplementary Fig. 1). The individuals who underwent surgery between January 2016 and March 2021 but did not meet all the criteria to participate in the study (n=1,301) were significantly older (mean age, 52 years) and more frequently male (58%) than those who were included (p<0.05). At the 2-year follow-up, 1,356 individuals (61%) remained (ACDF, n=1,130; PCF, n=226); 867 individuals (39%) were lost to follow-up (ACDF, n=736; PCF, n=131). Nonresponders at 2-years follow-up were significantly younger (mean age: 48 years vs. 51 years, p<0.05), but there was no significant difference in sex distribution (p>0.05). There were also no significant differences between responders and nonresponders in level of education, work status, diagnosis, BMI, and comorbidities (p>0.05). However, smoking was significantly more common among nonresponders (12.4% vs. 9.1%, p=0.015). Nonresponders at 2-year follow-up also had significantly more neck-related disability (NDI mean: 49 vs. 46, p<0.001), headache (NDI-headache mean: 2.7 vs. 2.6, p=0.012), neck pain intensity (NRS mean: 6.7 vs. 6.3, p<0.001), and arm pain intensity (NRS mean: 6.3 vs. 6.0, p=0.002) at baseline.

1. Study Participants

Of all included individuals (n=2,223), 46% (n=1,026) were male (ACDF, 45%; PCF, 52%) and the mean age was 50.4±8.9 years (ACDF, 49.6±8.6 years; PCF, 54.4±9.5 years). Surgery at 1 level was performed in 63% of the ACDF cases and in 50% of PCF cases. In contrast, 3–5 levels surgery was performed in 11% of PCF cases and in 2% of ACDF cases. There was no significant difference in neck-related disability, headache, neck pain or arm pain intensity preoperatively between ACDF and PCF (p>0.05) (Table 1).

2. Factors Associated With Neck-Related Disability Outcomes at 2-Year Follow-up

Higher preoperative neck-related disability, more than 2 years preoperative duration of neck pain, and the presence of preoperative comorbidities were significantly associated with worse NDI, whereas higher education and better preoperative walking ability were significantly associated with better NDI at 2-year follow-up after ACDF. The final ANCOVA model explained 33% of the variance in NDI at 2-year follow-up (adjusted R2=0.33). Of these factors, preoperative neck-related disability and comorbidities reached the threshold for minimal detectable partial eta squared (Table 2).

Higher preoperative neck-related disability was the only significantly associated factor with worse NDI at 2-year follow-up after PCF. The final ANCOVA model explained 32% of the variance in NDI at 2-year follow-up (adjusted R2=0.32). Preoperative neck-related disability reached the threshold for minimal detectable partial eta squared (Table 2).

3. Factors Associated With Headache Outcomes at 2-Year Follow-up

Higher preoperative headache was the only significantly associated factor with worse headache at 2-year follow-up after ACDF. The final ANCOVA model explained 20% of the variance in headache at 2-year follow-up (adjusted R2=0.20). Preoperative headache reached the threshold for minimal detectable partial eta squared (Table 3).

DISCUSSION

No previous study has described factors associated with neckrelated disability, headache, and neck and arm pain intensity at 2-year follow-up after ACDF and PCF in individuals with CR and neck-related headache, which makes this study unique. Preoperative values of these outcomes, together with demographic, lifestyle, surgical factors and self-reported health explained 13%– 35% of the variance in observed outcomes. As expected, the preoperative values of the dependent variables had the highest impact in all models, except for neck and arm pain after PCF, where neck-related disability had a greater impact than the preoperative value. More interestingly, several other explanatory factors, except for the preoperative value of the dependent value, were also associated with neck-related disability, headache, and neck and arm pain at 2-year follow-up after ACDF and PCF in individuals with CR and neck-related headache. However, these should be interpreted with caution given their smaller effect sizes and the fact that the majority of the explanatory factors did not reach the minimal detectable partial eta squared.

When comparing our results on individuals with CR and neckrelated headache with previous research on individuals with CR undergoing ACDF or PCF, we observed both similarities and differences. Interesting explanatory factors for outcomes after ACDF, apart from the preoperative values of each dependent variable, include higher education and comorbidities, which appear in 3 out of 4 models. These findings are in line with previous research on individuals with CR showing that individuals with a greater comorbidity burden have more postoperative complications after ACDF [27]. For individuals undergoing decompression surgery for lumbar spinal stenosis, those with no college education had significantly greater preoperative back and leg pain than those with a college education, on the other hand there was no significant difference regarding disability [28]. Other interesting explanatory factors after ACDF appear to be preoperative duration of neck pain, preoperative neck-related disability, and number of operated levels as they appear in at least 2 models. This is interesting because neck-related disability can easily be evaluated and tracked before surgery, and pain that does not respond to nonsurgical treatment is a commonly accepted reason for surgery [29]. In addition, duration of neck pain is also easy to evaluate preoperatively. Previous research has also shown that low preoperative disability [12,13] and a shorter duration of preoperative pain [13] are associated with better outcomes after ACDF, which is in line with our results, where higher preoperative neck-related disability and a longer preoperative duration of neck pain were associated with worse outcomes. One possible explanation for why higher preoperative neck-related disability and longer preoperative duration of neck pain are associated with worse outcomes is that this may reflect the overall impact of CR on the individual. In general, poorer preoperative status may be associated with worse postoperative outcomes. A greater number of operated levels is associated with worse outcomes in neck and arm pain. One possible explanation is that the rate of pseudarthrosis, which can lead to worse outcomes after ACDF [30], increases with the number of operated levels [31], which may partly explain these findings.

Interestingly, nonsmoking has previously been shown to be a predictive factor for good outcomes after ACDF [11-13], but in our analysis, it was only associated with better outcomes in neck pain and only after ACDF. The use of regular pain medication and health-related quality of life were not significantly associated with outcomes in any model after ACDF, which is consistent with previous research showing that regular pain medication does not predict improvement after ACDF [13], and that EQ-5D-3L does not predict patient satisfaction after ACDF [32]. Working status has also previously been shown not to affect surgical outcomes after ACDF [13], which is consistent with our findings.

Following PCF, no explanatory factor appeared in more than 2 models, however more preoperative neck-related disability was associated with worse outcomes in neck and arm pain after PCF, with greater impact on the model than the preoperative value of neck and arm pain. As discussed before, this is interesting because neck-related disability can easily be evaluated and tracked before surgery. This is also noteworthy, as previous research has shown that a symptom duration of <3 months is associated with better outcomes after PCF [15]. Although our results contribute new knowledge, as research on predictors of PCF outcomes in CR is limited, and there is a complete absence of studies on individuals with CR and neck-related headache, further research is still needed to establish relevant explanatory factor.

No other explanatory factor, except for preoperative headache, was significantly associated with headache at 2-year follow-up after either ACDF or PCF. This suggests that, among individuals with CR and neck-related headache, preoperative headache is an important factor to consider when predicting 2-year headache outcomes. BMI and sex were not associated with outcomes after ACDF or PCF in any model. This is slightly surprising, as previous studies have shown that male sex is a predictor of better outcomes after ACDF [10-12], and that individuals with CR and high BMI report greater neck and arm pain, reduced neck mobility, decreased grip strength, lower quality of life, and increased neckrelated disability [33]. Furthermore, after cervical spine surgery, individuals with high BMI tend to have longer operative times and a higher risk of postoperative complications [34]. In contrast, BMI has also been shown not to affect surgical outcomes [13,35]. However, our results pertain to individuals with both CR and neck-related headache.

In the previous study, we investigated and compared the outcomes of ACDF and PCF regarding neck-related disability, headache, and neck and arm pain in individuals with CR and neckrelated headache who underwent surgery between January 2014-March 2021 [18]. In contrast, this cohort includes individuals who underwent surgery between January 2016-March 2021 due to the addition of new outcomes of interest for this study in Swespine starting in 2016. However, the proportion of individuals with known reported neck-related headache, sex distribution and mean age are similar in both cohorts [18].

One limitation in this study is that the individuals were not screened for specific headache conditions, but all individuals had MRI verified disc disease that was compatible with the clinical findings from a preoperative neurological examination. In addition, all participants were asked to rate their headache in relation to their neck pain, which increases the likelihood that the reported headache was neck-related. This aligns with the purpose of studying individuals with CR and neck-related headache.

Another limitation of this study is that the choice in variables was governed by available register data and data quality. This means that some information—such as surgical details, radiological severity, psychosocial factors, and physical function (e.g., muscle tension, kinematic abnormalities of the neck, and limited range of motion)—that may influence the results could not be included. In the headache analyses, the operated cervical level would have been of interest to include, as it may help evaluate potential mechanisms of headache involving the upper and lower cervical spine. However, due to an imbalanced distribution toward lower cervical segments, this variable was not included in the analyses. The variables selected from the ones available were chosen based on previous knowledge in the research group, the literature, and a theoretical idea that they could affect dependent variables over time. For example, male sex [10-12], nonsmoking [11-13], low preoperative disability [12,13], low preoperative pain intensity [12], and shorter preoperative pain duration [13] are predictive factors of good outcomes after ACDF and symptom duration<3 months has been associated with better outcomes after PCF [15].

The aim of this study was to evaluate individuals with CR and neck-related headache. Therefore, we only included patients who reported headache preoperatively on the headache item of the NDI. Although this approach means we cannot determine how many individuals may develop headache postoperatively, it ensures that all included patients had some degree of self-reported neck-related headache preoperatively.

In the patient-reported data, the individuals were expected to answer the self-reported questions based on their neck pain [22,23]. We cannot be sure that the individual responded based only on their neck condition, as it is not straightforward for an individual to know which symptoms may be caused by another condition. This limitation is difficult to account for but should be considered when interpreting the results.

The NDI-headache item was used as a single outcome measure, even though the NDI is designed as a multi-item questionnaire. To explore and validate the ability of NDI-headache to measure headache, its correlation with VAS headache was assessed, as VAS headache has been used in previous studies to measure headache in individuals with CR [3,36]. A strong correlation was found, and this with the knowledge that previous research has also used the NDI-headache item as a single outcome measure [21,37], indicating that NDI-headache is a valid measure of headache in individuals with CR. However, this item does not capture the multidimensional nature of headache and the results should therefore be interpreted with caution.

Nonresponders at the 2-year follow-up were younger and more often smokers. In addition, nonresponders had more neck-related disability, headache, and neck and arm pain at baseline than responders. Previous analyses of individuals undergoing lumbar spine surgery using Swespine data indicate that nonresponders achieve outcomes comparable to responders, despite having more preoperative disability and being younger [38]. Furthermore, data from FinSpine, the Finnish spine registry, showed no differences in patient-reported outcomes between responders and nonresponders 1 year after ACDF [39]. Taken together, this suggests that the results of the present study may be generalizable to nonresponders as well. However, although previous registry studies indicate that outcomes in nonresponders may be comparable to those in responders, the baseline differences observed in the present cohort suggest that attrition bias cannot be excluded.

Regarding the number of observations in each model, we assume that we have sufficient statistical power to detect truly significant independent variables for both ACDF and PCF. Nevertheless, the larger sample size in the ACDF group might explain why more variables became significant after ACDF than after PCF, as a larger sample increases the ability to detect significant variables with smaller effect sizes. This is important to consider when interpreting differences in the number of independent variables identified as significant for each surgical method. Another possible explanation for differences in explanatory factors between the surgical procedures is that ACDF is a more musclesparing technique but may affect normal spinal kinematics due to the fusion, which can lead to adjacent segment disease. This risk may be mitigated with PCF, which, on the other hand, may involve a muscle-splitting approach [5]. Despite this, the findings of this study should not be interpreted as providing directly comparable or causal estimates across procedures. Rather, this study takes an exploratory approach to identify procedure-specific explanatory factors, independent of differences in surgical technique. This should be considered when interpreting the results, as these findings do not provide evidence that ACDF is associated with stronger or more clinically relevant explanatory factors.

The results of this study derived from a national registry with good coverage and completeness [40] are generalizable to other individuals suffering from CR and neck-related headache in similar contexts. However, because ACDF and PCF have clinically distinct diagnostic profiles, these procedure-specific models should not be interpreted as evidence that one procedure is superior to the other. In clinical practice, these findings can be utilized to better manage patient expectations regarding surgical outcomes and in making preoperative decisions. For the surgeon, modifiable factors in the short-term include how long they allow the patient to endure their neck-related disability, pain, and the choice of the number of levels to operate. It also highlights the importance of evaluating patients preoperatively to identify educational level, comorbidities, and preoperative status, including disability, and pain. The fact that the models in this exploratory study, evaluating explanatory factors after ACDF and PCF in individuals with CR and neck-related headache, explained 13%–35% of the variance in observed outcomes indicates that additional factors influencing outcomes remain unidentified, highlighting the need for further research.

CONCLUSION

For individuals with CR and neck-related headache, outcomes in neck-related disability, headache, and neck and arm pain at 2-year follow-up after ACDF or PCF are most strongly associated with the corresponding preoperative levels of these outcomes, except for neck and arm pain after PCF, where neck-related disability had a greater impact. Relevant explanatory factors for outcomes after ACDF include comorbidities, higher preoperative neck-related disability, longer preoperative duration of neck pain, and a greater number of operated levels, all of which are associated with worse outcomes. In addition, higher education is associated with better outcomes. For PCF, only higher preoperative neck-related disability appears to be an important factor, as it is associated with worse outcomes. Headache as inclusion criteria and outcome variable was assessed using the headache item of the NDI which reflects a limited aspect of headache and should be interpreted with some caution. The models explained 13%–35% of the variance in observed outcomes, indicating that a substantial proportion of outcome variability remains unexplained and is likely influenced by additional factors not captured in this exploratory study.
