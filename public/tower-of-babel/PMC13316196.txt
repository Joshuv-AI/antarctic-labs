Artificial platelets suppressing deep vein thrombosis via competitive adhesion
Authors: Shenglin Ye, Xiaolong Du, Sujun Chen, Kang Han, Zixiang Tang, Yanran Huang, Ning Zhu, Wendong Li, Tao Tang, Lun Xiao, Zhengwei Cai, Wenguo Cui, Nan Hu, Xiaoqiang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDeep vein thrombosis (DVT) remains a therapeutic challenge, primarily driven by pathological platelet adhesion at sites of vascular injury mediated by endothelial adhesion molecule overexpression. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying endothelial-dependent pathological platelet adhesion remain elusive. Here, integrating single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and functional validation, we identified aberrant activation of the stimulator of interferon genes (STING) as a critical driver of endothelial dysfunction and pathological platelet adhesion. Using STING knockout mice, we confirmed its central role in promoting a prothrombotic microenvironment via the NOD-like receptor family pyrin domain-containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome. Targeting this pathway, we identified forsythoside A (FA) as a potent STING inhibitor and constructed artificial platelets (Pm@Fng) via photopolymerization-membrane extrusion technology. Pm@Fng, consisting of FA-loaded nanogels cloaked with activated platelet membrane vesicles (APMVs), exhibited enhanced glycoprotein Ib alpha (GPIbα) -mediated binding affinity to von Willebrand factor (vWF), competitively blocking pathological platelet adhesion. Under thrombotic microenvironment (acidic pH and high reactive oxygen species), FA was released to modulate the STING - NLRP3 pathway, suppressing adhesion molecule expression. In a mouse DVT model, Pm@Fng rapidly targeted the endothelium, noticeably reducing thrombosis by 30% (p < 0.001) and preserving endothelial integrity. By exploiting competitive adhesion principles, this study presents a novel therapeutic strategy for DVT and other endothelial injury-related diseases.Keywords: Artificial platelets, Deep vein thrombosis, Competitive adhesion, Nanogel, Endothelial adhesion molecule, STING

Introduction

Deep vein thrombosis, the third leading cause of cardiovascular disease-related mortality, predominantly affects the lower extremities and poses a life-threatening risk due to its potential detachment, finally leading to pulmonary embolism (PE), which has become a significant and life-threatening complication in clinical practice [1,2]. The pathogenesis of DVT adheres to Virchow's triad, characterized by venous stasis, hypercoagulability, and endothelial injury [3]. Current clinical management primarily relies on acute invasive interventions (e.g., thrombectomy, catheter-directed thrombolysis) and long-term anticoagulation medications [4]. However, these approaches face critical limitations. First, acute invasive procedures, while effective in rapid thrombus removal, disrupt vascular integrity, inducing aberrant adhesion molecule expression (e.g., intercellular adhesion molecule 1 (ICAM-1), Vascular cell adhesion molecule 1 (VCAM-1)) that exacerbates secondary platelet aggregation at injury sites, driving recurrent thrombosis. Second, long - term anticoagulation treatments, like heparin, warfarin, and direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs), mainly aim at the coagulation cascade or platelet activation pathways. They can slow down thrombus progression, yet they might not be able to fix endothelial injuries. Stopping the medication often allows persistent endothelial injury sites to attract circulating platelet adhesion, leading to a 15% recurrence rate [4,5]. The core therapeutic dilemma lies in endothelial injury-induced adhesion molecule overexpression, which mediates pathological platelet-endothelial interactions, establishing a prothrombotic microenvironment [6,7]. Consequently, effectively blocking the pathological adhesion and aggregation between the platelets and damaged endothelium is pivotal for improving DVT outcomes. Nevertheless, the upstream molecular pathways driving sustained endothelial adhesion molecule expression, and ultimately leading to pathological platelet adhesion—remain elusive.

The overexpression of adhesion molecules driven by endothelial injury serves as the central trigger for the pathological platelet adhesion. When endothelial cells are damaged, they may trigger a cascading inflammatory storm, releasing various inflammatory mediators that promote the aberrant overexpression of adhesion molecules (e.g., ICAM-1, VCAM-1, P-selectin) and reshape the prothrombotic microenvironment [[7], [8], [9], [10], [11]]. Emerging evidence has highlighted the cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS)-STING pathway as a pivotal mediator of this process. Stress-induced release of double-stranded deoxyribonucleic acid (dsDNA) activates STING-dependent interferon regulatory factor 3 (IRF3) nuclear translocation, amplifying interferon-driven inflammatory cascades that transcriptionally enhance adhesion molecule expression [[12], [13], [14], [15], [16], [17]]. This signaling cascade exacerbates platelet pathophysiology through multiple pathways. It can enhance the platelet-endothelial anchoring mediated by integrin αIIbβ3/GPIb-IX-V [18], and simultaneously promote the burst of reactive oxygen species (ROS), activate the platelet P2Y12 receptor, finally enhancing its activation and adhesion aggregation ability [16]. Furthermore, it can also induce endothelial pyroptosis by activating the NLRP3 inflammasome [12], exposing the basal collagen and further recruiting platelets. The critical role of cGAS-STING pathway in endothelial dysfunction has been validated across diverse pathological models. Liu et al. have found in patients with STING-related vascular lesions that STING activation significantly increased the expression of endothelial activation markers (inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), tissue factor (TF)), adhesion molecules (E-selectin, ICAM-1), interferon (IFN) response genes, and endothelial apoptosis index [12]. Li et al. confirmed that endothelial-specific STING knockout could reduce plaque burden and vascular inflammation in the mouse atherosclerosis model [15]. The thrombotic coagulation disease associated with coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) infection is also considered to be closely related to endothelial injury caused by excessive activation of the STING signal [19]. Despite these advances, the precise mechanisms by which STING regulates endothelial-inflammatory-adhesion axes in DVT microenvironments remain undefined. The absence of clinically validated STING inhibitors further hinders therapeutic development. Thus, repurposing existing drugs to potentially screen STING inhibitors and engineer endothelial-stabilizing and platelet-adhesion-inhibition strategies represents a promising avenue for DVT treatment.

Drug screening represents a promising strategy to address these challenges, yet conventional high-throughput screening is constrained by exorbitant costs and low success rates, which fail to capture the endothelial-inflammatory crosstalk central to DVT. In contrast, artificial intelligence (AI)-driven drug screening integrates multiomics features with physics-based simulations (e.g., AlphaFold2-predicted molecular conformations) to enable intelligent virtual screening of compounds with target specificity and endothelial tropism. This approach not only accelerates drug discovery but also systematically explores chemical space, creating new opportunities for mechanism-driven antithrombotic therapies. Nevertheless, even such strategies may exhibit temporal lag in repairing endothelial damage and suppressing adhesion molecule expression. A critical unmet challenge remains: how to directly and efficiently block pathological platelet aggregation during the initiation phase of DVT. During DVT progression, platelets bind to collagen exposed at damaged endothelium via the GPIbα - vWF axis, providing a mechanistic target for intervention. APMVs demonstrated significantly enhanced GPIbα activity compared to native platelets, endowing them with unique competitive adhesion advantages [20]. These vesicles can firstly occupy endothelial injury sites, effectively outcompeting circulating platelets and blocking pathological adhesion. Furthermore, APMVs' propensity to rupture in thrombotic acidic microenvironments facilitates controlled drug release, while their natural absence of thromboxane/adenosine diphosphate (ADP) minimizes nonspecific thrombosis risk [21]. Thus, leveraging APMVs’ GPIbα-mediated competitive adhesion capability offers a pivotal strategy for targeted delivery and direct modulation of platelet-endothelial pathologic interactions.

This study utilized scRNA-seq to uncover the cGAS-STING-NLRP3 axis as a key driver of endothelial injury in mouse DVT, promoting adhesion molecule overexpression and pathological platelet adhesion. Using immunostaining, proteomics, and STING knockout models, we confirmed this axis triggers a cascade of DNA sensing, interferon response, and NLRP3 inflammasome activation, fostering a prothrombotic microenvironment. Targeting this pathway, we screened natural products and identified FA as a potent STING inhibitor using AlphaFold2-based predictions. To physically block platelet adhesion, we developed artificial platelets (Pm@Fng) by encapsulating FA in hyaluronic acid methacrylate (HAMA)- phenylboronic acid (PBA) nanogels coated with activated platelet membranes. Pm@Fng competes for vWF binding on damaged endothelium, blocking platelet adhesion, and releases FA in acidic thrombi to modulate the cGAS-STING-NLRP3 pathway, reducing adhesion molecule expression (Scheme 1). This study elucidates the cGAS-STING-NLRP3 pathway's role in DVT and presents a novel therapeutic strategy combining competitive adhesion and targeted inhibition.

Results and discussion

Single-cell atlas reveals molecular regulatory networks in deep vein thrombosis formation

To elucidate the critical regulatory mechanisms underlying DVT, particularly the microenvironment driving platelet aggregation and adhesion at thrombotic sites, we employed the inferior vena cava constriction (IVC) model to induce mouse DVT. At 7 days post-surgery, a time point representing a mature, organized thrombus, we collected inferior vena cava tissues, including both the vessel wall and the associated thrombus, for scRNA-seq. The experiment involved two groups: a sham-operated control (Con) and a thrombosis (DVT) group. For each group, two biological replicates were analyzed, where each replicate consisted of tissues pooled from three individual mice to ensure robustness and minimize inter-animal variability (Fig. 1A). After quality control, 29,420 high-quality cells were obtained (Con: 12,708; DVT: 16,712) (Supplementary Fig. S1A). Unsupervised clustering identified seven major cell populations: fibroblasts, endothelial cells, B cells, T cells, NK cells, neutrophils, and macrophages (Fig. 1B, Supplementary Fig. 1C). Cell identities were validated using canonical marker genes (Fig. 1D, Supplementary Fig. 1B).

Notably, IVC surgery significantly reshaped the cellular composition of the thrombotic microenvironment. Compared with the sham-operated control group (Con), the DVT group exhibited a marked increase in neutrophil (10.65% vs. 2.07%) and macrophage (22.80% vs. 1.46%) proportions, whereas B cell and T cell abundances decreased. The frequencies of endothelial cells (4.27% vs. 3.06%) and fibroblasts (17.3% vs. 12.3%) showed no significant differences between the two groups (Fig. 1C, Supplementary Fig. 1C). These results clearly indicated that the early thrombotic microenvironment in DVT is characterized by rapid and massive infiltration of neutrophils and macrophages, establishing a robust pro-inflammatory milieu dominated by innate immunity. Adaptive immune cells (B/T) played a relatively minor role. The stability of endothelial and fibroblast populations suggested that early thrombus formation primarily relies on the recruitment of circulating immune cells (particularly myeloid cells) rather than structural tissue remodeling. This intense inflammatory environment served as a critical foundation for subsequent key events, such as platelet activation and competitive adhesion.

Integrative analysis revealed that the thrombotic microenvironment exhibited significant transcriptional reprogramming. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) annotation revealed that inflammatory response and oxidative stress were core upregulated pathways (Fig. 1E and F). The cGAS-STING pathway, a key inflammation and stress sensor, has been demonstrated to influence vascular function in multiple diseases [12,15,17,18]. Similarly, through differential expression analysis of sequencing data, we identified 512 DVT-associated differential expression genes, including 305 upregulated and 207 downregulated genes (Fig. 1G). Among these, the pro-inflammatory gene Tmem173 (encoding the STING protein) was significantly upregulated in the DVT group and showed specific enrichment in the endothelial cell subpopulation at single-cell resolution (Fig. 1H). Further comparison revealed that Tmem173 expression in endothelial cells of the DVT group was significantly higher than in the control group, with downstream signaling molecules IRF3 and tank-binding kinase 1 (TBK1) also upregulated synchronously (Fig. 1I), suggesting specific activation of the cGAS-STING pathway in endothelial cells. To validate this finding, immunofluorescence staining detected double-stranded DNA damage signals in endothelial cells of the DVT group, and STING with phosphorylated TBK1 (p-TBK1) showed significant co-localization in the CD31+ endothelial cell marker region (Fig. 1J). Western blot analysis further confirmed that compared with the control group, STING protein expression and phosphorylation levels were significantly increased in thrombotic tissues of the DVT group. Meanwhile, phosphorylation levels of its downstream signaling molecules TBK1 and IRF3 were also upregulated synchronously, highly consistent with sequencing results (Fig. 1K, Supplementary Fig. 1D).

The DVT model used in this study is characterized by an acute phase of thrombus formation that occurs up to day 2 post-surgery, followed by a stabilization and organization phase extending to day 7 [22]. In our study, the primary analysis of the DVT model was conducted at day 7 post-IVC stenosis. This time point was specifically chosen to represent a mature and well-organized thrombus, allowing for a clear assessment of therapeutic efficacy on a stable pathological endpoint. We recognize, however, that DVT pathogenesis is a highly dynamic process, and the molecular events driving initial thrombus formation may differ from those involved in its subsequent stabilization and resolution. To validate that our proposed target—the STING signaling pathway—is relevant to the early, acute phase of the disease, we performed supplemental analysis on thrombus tissues at day 2 post-modeling. As confirmed by both immunofluorescence and immunoblotting analysis (Supplementary Fig. 2), the STING pathway was already significantly activated at this earlier time point. This finding indicates that the pathway is an early driver of DVT pathogenesis, not merely a characteristic feature of a chronic thrombus.

These findings systematically revealed the multi-dimensional activation of the endothelial cell cGAS-STING signaling axis in DVT. Given that endothelial activation and inflammatory factor release are key regulators of platelet recruitment, activation, and adhesion, activation of this pathway may profoundly influence subsequent platelet competitive adhesion processes by remodeling endothelial phenotypes or releasing specific mediators.

Genetic depletion of STING suppresses DVT

As a central regulator of innate immunity, STING has recently been shown to play pivotal roles in modulating inflammatory microenvironments via the cGAS-STING axis [23,24]. In this study, we employed scRNA-seq combined with functional experiments to systematically elucidate the activation patterns of this pathway in DVT. To mechanistically investigate its role, we generated STING knockout (STING−/−) mice and established a DVT model using the IVC constriction method to systematically parse the regulatory mechanisms of this pathway. Experimental data revealed that, 7 days post-surgery, STING−/− mice exhibited significantly reduced thrombus volume (Fig. 2A), decreased thrombus weight (20.63 ± 3.79 mg vs 8.69 ± 3.32 mg, p < 0.0001), shortened thrombus length (9.08 ± 1.80 mm vs 3.96 ± 1.65 mm, p < 0.0001), and markedly lower thrombosis incidence (88.89% vs. 56.25%, p < 0.0001) compared to wild-type (WT) controls (Fig. 2B–D). These phenotypic alterations strongly implicate STING as a critical prothrombotic factor in DVT.

Previously reported studies indicated that STING can recognize cytosolic DNA (e.g., mitochondrial DNA released from damaged cells) to trigger downstream signaling cascades, inducing type I interferons (IFN-α/β) and pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin 6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α) [25]. Our findings demonstrated reduced inflammatory cell infiltration in thrombi of STING knockout mice, particularly a decreased adhesion density of F4/80+ macrophages within the endothelial layer (Fig. 2E and F). This aligns with literature indicating STING promotes monocyte/macrophage chemotaxis and M1 polarization [26]. Macrophage accumulation on the endothelium not only amplifies inflammation but may also influence platelet adhesion through competitive or synergistic mechanisms. Furthermore, STING activation upregulates endothelial adhesion molecules such as VCAM-1 and ICAM-1 [12,17], which mediate leukocyte-endothelial and platelet-endothelial interactions. STING−/− thrombi showed markedly diminished VCAM-1/ICAM-1 expression (Fig. 2E–G), directly linking STING to endothelial activation and prothrombotic microenvironment formation. Notably, STING can enhance vWF expression [27], a critical bridge for platelet adhesion to damaged endothelium. Plasma vWF levels and thrombus vWF expression were significantly lower in STING−/− mice (Fig. 2I). Interestingly, we observed that thrombomodulin (TM), an anticoagulant protein, showed a relative downregulation in STING−/− thrombi (Fig. 2G and H). This could be attributed to the loss of STING signaling, which may reduce the negative feedback on procoagulant signals, thereby affecting TM expression. Specifically, the lack of STING may lead to an insufficient endothelial response to both exogenous and endogenous procoagulant factors, resulting in diminished TM expression. These findings suggest that STING plays an important role in balancing the procoagulant and anticoagulant pathways within the thrombotic microenvironment. Concurrently, plasma prothrombotic markers D-dimer and fibrin degradation products (FDP) were reduced (Fig. 2I), and histological analysis revealed decreased fibrin deposition in thrombi (Fig. 2E and F).

The NLRP3 inflammasome, another critical branch of the innate immune system, mediates the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines and the activation of caspase-1. Previous studies have demonstrated that NLRP3 plays a key role in the occurrence and development of thrombosis [28]. scRNA-seq analysis in this study revealed significantly upregulated expression of NLRP3, interleukin 1 beta (IL-1β), TNF-α, and other genes in DVT endothelial cells (Fig. 3A), with the pyroptosis pathway showing marked activation in GSEA (Fig. 3B). Immunofluorescence showed significant co-localization of NLRP3, GSDMD, caspase-1, and ASC with the endothelial marker CD31 (Fig. 3E). Studies have indicated that activation of the STING signaling pathway promotes NLRP3 inflammasome activation. Specifically, STING senses intracellular DNA via cGAS, activates STING, and subsequently initiates the downstream IRF3 signaling pathway to enhance the expression of inflammatory cytokines, thereby potentiating NLRP3 activation and forming the NLRP3 inflammasome [29] (Fig. 3G). STING can further exacerbate NLRP3 inflammasome function by upregulating the expression of cytokines such as IL-1β [30], creating a feedback loop that amplifies local and systemic inflammation. In STING knockout mice, we observed significant inhibition of the NLRP3 pathway. To validate the effect of STING on pyroptosis, we compared pyroptosis-related protein expression between STING knockout and wild-type mice 7 days post-DVT model induction. Results showed that STING deletion significantly suppressed the expression of NLRP3, apoptosis-associated speck-like protein containing a card (ASC), Gasdermin D (GSDMD), and IL-1β (Fig. 3F, Supplementary Fig. 3). Immunohistochemistry revealed reduced NLRP3 expression in STING knockout mice (Fig. 3C and D). Additionally, immunofluorescence results indicated high expression of pyroptosis-related factors in the model group, with co-localization to endothelial cells, while their expression was markedly reduced following STING deletion (Fig. 3E). To further confirm that this STING-dependent pyroptosis occurs specifically within endothelial cells and to provide more direct biochemical evidence, we performed targeted inhibition experiments in C166 cells. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 4, stimulation with IL-1β robustly induced the expression of NLRP3 and triggered pyroptosis, confirmed by the cleavage of IL-1β and the generation of the N-terminal fragment of GSDMD (GSDMD-N), the executor of pyroptotic pore formation. This was accompanied by the activation of the STING pathway, indicated by increased phosphorylation of TBK1 (P-TBK1). Crucially, pre-treatment with the specific STING inhibitor H-151 inhibited not only P-TBK1 but also all downstream pyroptosis markers. Conversely, the specific NLRP3 inhibitor MCC950 blocked GSDMD-N formation and IL-1β cleavage without affecting upstream P-TBK1 levels. These results provide direct, cell-intrinsic evidence that STING signaling acts upstream of the NLRP3 inflammasome to drive endothelial pyroptosis, strongly reinforcing the conclusions drawn from our in vivo knockout model. These findings indicated that endothelial cell damage triggers mtDNA release, activating the cGAS-STING axis, which in turn drives endothelial pyroptosis and inflammatory cytokine storms via NLRP3 inflammasome regulation. This robust endothelial injury and inflammatory milieu further disrupt endothelial barrier function, releasing more pro-thrombotic factors (e.g., vWF, TF), thereby indirectly but profoundly influencing platelet adhesion, activation, and thrombus stability.

Totally, STING gene deletion suppresses DVT formation through multiple mechanisms. Firstly, it can directly weaken the endothelial activation (downregulating VCAM-1/ICAM-1/vWF expression). Secondly, it can alleviate the pro-thrombotic state (downregulating vWF/FDP/D-dimer). Thirdly, it can inhibit the infiltration and adhesion of inflammatory cells (particularly macrophages) in the endothelial layer. Fourthly, it can mitigate endothelial pyroptosis and systemic inflammation by suppressing the NLRP3 inflammasome. Among these, direct regulation of endothelial adhesion molecules, disruption of endothelial barrier function and inflammatory microenvironments collectively act on the core component of platelet competitive adhesion, ultimately reducing thrombus burden. This provides new insights for targeting the STING-NLRP3 signaling network to regulate endothelial homeostasis and intervene in platelet adhesion processes for DVT prevention and treatment.

Identification and validation of STING inhibitors

Targeting the inhibition of STING signaling pathway activation offers a promising therapeutic strategy for mitigating endothelial injury in DVT. However, traditional drug discovery paradigms are constrained by challenges such as dynamic conformational resolution of target proteins and inefficient compound library screening, leading to slow progress in developing clinically viable STING inhibitors. To address these limitations, this study innovatively introduced AI-assisted high-throughput virtual screening technologies, employing a multi-layer computational pharmacology strategy to overcome these bottlenecks (Fig. 4A). First, AlphaFold2 was utilized to predict the active conformation of the mice STING protein (Fig. 4B), enabling the construction of dynamic pharmacophore models to precisely identify critical interaction sites, including the cGAMP-binding domain and dimerization interface. Subsequently, a deep neural network-driven molecular docking platform was employed to screen 807 clinically approved drugs based on binding free energy (ΔG ≤ −7.0 kcal/mol), yielding 20 candidate molecules (Fig. 4C, Supplementary Fig. 5A). Top 10 candidates underwent further virtual molecular docking analysis (Fig. 4E). The natural product FA was ultimately identified as the most promising STING inhibitor due to its optimal binding affinity (Fig. 4D). Structural analysis uncovers a complex network of non - covalent interactions, primarily hydrogen bonds, between FA and STING proteins. This suggests that the oxygen/nitrogen functional groups of the ligand engage in directional interactions with charged or polar residues within the pocket, functioning as "anchor points" to stabilize the binding conformation. Significantly, the participation of arginine (ARG) and glutamate (GLU) underscores the unique electrophilic environment of the pocket: the ARG side - chain donates hydrogen bonds and participates in aromatic interactions, whereas the GLU side - chain serves as a hydrogen acceptor in polar pairing. Collectively, these interactions reduce the binding free energy and enhance conformational convergence (Fig. 4F). This discovery underscored the potential of AI-driven approaches in accelerating the identification of STING modulators for DVT therapy.

As a major bioactive component of Forsythia suspensa, FA has been demonstrated to exhibit anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects. FA suppressed lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced pro-inflammatory cytokine production (e.g., TNF-α, IL-1β) in BV2 microglial cells by inhibiting nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) signaling while enhancing nuclear factor erythroid 2 - related factor 2 (Nrf2)/heme oxygenase - 1 (HO-1) antioxidant pathways [31]. Additionally, FA mitigated oxidative stress in H2O2-exposed PC12 cells [32] and alleviated LPS/D-galactosamine-induced hepatic injury in mice by reducing serum ALT/AST levels and hepatic MDA content [33]. Previous studies indicate that the concentration range of FA acting on cells is approximately 10-100 μM [[34], [35], [36]]. In vitro, different concentrations of FA were co-cultured with mouse vascular endothelial cells (C166). We found that the optimal drug concentration was 40 μM (Supplementary Fig. 5B). To determine the effect of FA on STING activation, we stimulated endothelial cells with 10 ng/mL IL -1β to simulate the thrombus microenvironment [7], and then treated them with FA for 4 h. Western blot showed that FA could effectively inhibit the activation of the cGAS-STING pathway. When FA was administered independently, no significant elevation was observed, suggesting that FA alone exerts limited activation on STING (Fig. 4G). To functionally confirm that FA's anti-inflammatory effect is mediated through the STING pathway, we conducted a rescue experiment using the canonical STING agonist cGAMP in C166 cells (Supplementary Fig. 6). As expected, cGAMP stimulation led to a robust phosphorylation of STING and its downstream effector TBK1. However, pre-treatment with FA significantly attenuated this cGAMP-induced activation, demonstrating that FA acts as a functional inhibitor of the STING signaling cascade.

Next, we sought to confirm that this functional inhibition stems from direct target engagement. A Surface Plasmon Resonance (SPR) assay provided unequivocal biophysical proof, demonstrating a direct, specific interaction between FA and purified STING protein with a dissociation constant (Kd) of 18.2 μM (Supplementary Fig. 7). Finally, to provide definitive genetic evidence for this mechanism, we utilized STING-targeting siRNA. While FA potently suppressed pyroptosis in control cells, its protective effect was almost entirely abolished upon STING knockdown, cementing the conclusion that FA's therapeutic activity is mediated in a STING-dependent manner (Supplementary Fig. 8 and 9).

In addition, FA treatment significantly down-regulated the expression of key molecules related to platelet adhesion in endothelial cells, including vWF (bridging platelet-endothelial interactions), P-selectin (mediating rolling and adhesion), TF (activating coagulation), and VCAM-1/ICAM-1 (facilitating leukocyte-platelets co-adhesion) (Fig. 4H). To assess FA's direct impact on cellular adhesion, calcein-AM-labeled RAW 264.7 macrophages were co-cultured with IL-1β-stimulated endothelial cells. Fluorescence microscopy demonstrated a significant reduction in immune cell adhesion to FA-treated endothelial monolayers (Fig. 4I), correlating with decreased secretion of VWF, TNF-α, and IL-1β (Supplementary Fig. 5C). These findings collectively confirmed FA as a dual-function agent that suppresses endothelial activation while mitigating inflammatory cell-platelet interactions, thereby reducing thrombotic burden.

Development and characteristics of artificial platelets

Having established the STING-NLRP3 axis as a critical pathological driver in DVT and identified FA as a potent STING inhibitor through AI-assisted screening, the next crucial step was to engineer a delivery system. This system needed to not only deliver FA specifically to the thrombotic site but also to physically intervene in the initial pathological event: platelet adhesion. To achieve this dual function, we designed biomimetic artificial platelets (Pm@Fng). The core of our biomimetic strategy lies in leveraging the natural properties of activated platelet membrane vesicles (APMVs). Endothelial injury triggers DVT by exposing subendothelial collagen fibers, which binds vWF. This, in turn, recruits natural platelets via their surface GPIbα receptors. By camouflaging our nanogel with APMVs, we aimed to create a competitor that uses the same GPIbα-vWF binding mechanism [[37], [38], [39], [40], [41]] to dock at the injury site, thereby physically blocking natural platelets. Moreover, this platelet-derived cloak offers several inherent advantages: 1) APMVs lack pro-thrombotic contents like ADP and thromboxane A2, eliminating the risk of exacerbating thrombosis [21]. 2)The presence of "self-marker" proteins like CD47 on the membrane surface is hypothesized to reduce phagocytic clearance, prolonging circulation time [42,43]. 3) APMVs undergo pH-dependent destabilization in acidic microenvironments, which facilitates their targeted delivery [44,45]. 4) The membrane's bioactive molecules may promote endothelial regeneration. These features positioned APMVs as the ideal carrier for our DVT-targeted therapy.

The preparation protocol of Pm@Fng is schematically illustrated in Fig. 5A. Briefly, functionalized polymer scaffolds (HAMA-PBA) were constructed via a two-step chemical modification of HA: (1) MA was grafted onto the HA backbone through esterification to form HAMA, followed by site-specific amidation of 3-aminophenylboronic acid (PBA) onto HA side chains [46]. Nuclear magnetic resonance hydrogen spectroscopy (1H NMR) confirmed the successful dual functionalization, with characteristic peaks at 5.71 ppm and 6.09 ppm attributed to the vinyl protons of MA, and multiplets in the 7.45–7.86 ppm range corresponding to the aromatic protons of PBA (Fig. 5B). By comparing the integral areas of the vinyl protons of the methacrylate group(δ 5.7–6.1 ppm) with the integral area of the N-acetyl methyl proton in the hyaluronic acid backbone (δ 1.9 ppm), the degree of methacrylation was calculated to be approximately 24%. Subsequently, FA was mixed with the above complexes, under mild conditions (25 °C, overnight). During this reaction process, the solution transformed from transparent to pale yellow due to the phenolic groups of PBA connecting with the catechol groups adjacent to FA, forming dynamic borate ester bonds and creating a secondary network (HAMA-PBA-FA) (Supplementary Fig. 10). This covalent strategy not only enables a high drug loading capacity but also serves as the foundation for the stimuli-responsive release characteristic of the platform. The boronate ester linkage is known to be labile under both acidic pH and high concentrations of ROS, conditions that are hallmarks of the thrombotic microenvironment. This targeted bond cleavage under stimulation is what drives the precise release of FA at the site of pathology. Next, three-dimensional crosslinked nanogels (Fng) were fabricated via free-radical polymerization of the HAMA-PBA-FA precursor under photo-initiation. Finally, for biomimetic functionalization, activated platelet membrane vesicles (APMVs) were prepared from mouse platelets activated by a low-temperature/ADP method, followed by freeze-thaw cycles and extrusion. The resulting Fng nanogels were then camouflaged with these APMVs through a strategy combining electrostatic adsorption and subsequent photo-crosslinking. The particle size distribution of Pm@Fng prepared with different membrane/nanogel ratios was analyzed by dynamic light scattering (DLS). And the DLS analysis demonstrated that the optimal ratio ranged between 1:1 and 1:3 (Fig. 5E). In the subsequent experiments, we prepared Pm@Fng using a 1:2 ratio. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed the Pm@Fng core-shell: a nanogel (core:∼50 nm) encapsulated within an ∼8 nm-thick membrane shell (Fig. 5C). As shown in Fig. 5F, the diameters of Ng, Fng, and Pm@Fng were approximately 106 nm, 182 nm, and 205 nm, respectively, all with narrow size distributions as indicated by their low polydispersity indices (PDI) of 0.255, 0.282, and 0.270. Moreover, DLS-measured hydrated particle diameter of Pm@Fng was 205 nm, which significantly exceeded TEM observations due to nanogel dehydration during sample preparation (Fig. 5F). Zeta potential analysis further confirmed the effectiveness of the membrane coating: the surface potential of Pm@Fng (−5.5 mV) was between that of Ng (−3.3 mV) and the pure membrane vesicle (−6.4 mV), confirming a partial membrane coating (Fig. 5D).

Beyond physical characterization, we evaluated the key functional attributes of Pm@Fng. First, we examined and compared the colloidal stability of the nanogel and Pm@Fng after storage at 37 °C for 7 d. In PBS, the particle sizes of Pm@Fng, Fng, and Ng increased slightly over 7 d (Fig. 5G). To further confirm the successful formation of stable, covalently cross-linked nanogels, as opposed to simple self-assemblies, we investigated their colloidal stability under serial dilution. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S11, the hydrodynamic diameter and PDI of the Pm@Fng particles remained virtually unchanged even after 1000-fold dilution in PBS. This remarkable stability under high dilution conditions provides definitive evidence that our nanoparticles are robust nanogels and not aggregates that would dissociate upon dilution, confirming their suitability for in vivo applications where significant dilution in the bloodstream occurs. HPLC analysis indicated that the drug entrapment efficiency (DEE) of FA in the nanogel was 73.5%, and the drug loading capacity (DLC) was 19.7%. Following established protocols [47,48], the stimuli-responsive drug release profile of Pm@Fng was evaluated to confirm its responsiveness to the thrombotic microenvironment (Fig. 5H). The Pm@Fng demonstrated high stability in a neutral physiological environment (PBS, pH 7.4). Drug leakage was minimal, with a cumulative release of only 12.3 ± 2.2% after 24 h. This indicates the boronate ester network remains largely intact under normal conditions, which is crucial for minimizing potential off-target effects. Under pH 6.5 conditions (simulating key features of the thrombotic microenvironment), FA release was substantially increased. The release pattern followed a sigmoidal curve, suggesting the pH sensitivity of the nanogel, driven by borate ester hydrolysis. We then tested the response in a ROS-rich environment (1 mM H2O2) [49]. A strong burst release was observed. Within the initial 4 h, 55.4 ± 4.5% of FA was released, a rate markedly faster than that induced by pH changes, and achieved 85.8 ± 2.9% within 24 h. These results suggest that oxidants drive cleavage of borate ester bonds. The most compelling results were seen when the Pm@Fng was exposed to both stimuli simultaneously (pH 6.5 + 1 mM H2O2), a condition that best reflects the actual DVT microenvironment. Here, we observed a clear synergistic effect. FA release was both faster and more extensive than with either single stimulus. The cumulative release reached 72.5 ± 4.8% in just 4 h and peaked at 96.5 ± 1.9% by 24 h. This dual-responsive behavior highlights that Pm@Fng is an intelligent delivery system, capable of recognizing a complex pathological signature and maximizing its therapeutic payload delivery at the target site. To evaluate nanoparticle cytotoxicity, CCK-8 assays and live/dead staining were performed by co-culturing Pm@Fng with C166 cells, revealing no adverse effects on cell proliferation or viability (Fig. 5I and J). Western blot analysis confirmed that key platelet membrane proteins (P-selectin, CD41, GPIbα) remained intact after coating treatment, whereas the intracellular skeleton protein β-actin was reduced in the membrane components (Fig. 5K and L). Co-localization of green fluorescently labeled platelet membranes (DIO-labeled) and red fluorescently labeled nanogels (ICG-labeled) demonstrated the successful association of the membrane vesicles with the nanogel (Fig. 5M).

Pm@Fng achieves precise targeting of injured endothelium via competitive adhesion

The therapeutic efficacy of our artificial platelets (Pm@Fng) is predicated on their function as a biomimetic competitor, physically occupying adhesion sites on activated endothelium to prevent the recruitment of native platelets. A fundamental prerequisite for this mechanism is the functional integrity of the key surface receptors on Pm@Fng, particularly GPIbα. To validate this at the most fundamental molecular level, we first conducted an in vitro binding assay. Confocal microscopy revealed that ICG-labeled Pm@Fng robustly adhered to surfaces coated with its physiological ligand, vWF, whereas minimal binding was observed on control surfaces or when using bare nanogels (Ng) (Supplementary Fig. 12A). Crucially, this interaction was abrogated when GPIbα was pre-blocked with a specific antibody. This visual observation was corroborated by a quantitative plate-reader assay, which confirmed that the binding of Pm@Fng to vWF was significantly higher than all control groups (p < 0.0001, Supplementary Fig. 12B). Together, these data provide unequivocal evidence that the GPIbα on the Pm@Fng surface is not merely present but is fully functional and capable of mediating specific ligand recognition.

With this foundational bioactivity confirmed, we progressed to evaluate the adhesion of Pm@Fng in more complex, disease-mimicking cellular environments. Pm@Fng demonstrated strong binding to both exposed type I collagen and IL-1β-activated endothelial cells (Figs. 6A and 9B), mimicking the initial steps of platelet recruitment at a vascular injury site. To dissect the molecular underpinnings of this cell-level adhesion, we performed a receptor-blocking study (Fig. 6C). As hypothesized, pre-treatment of the activated endothelial cells with an anti-vWF antibody, or pre-blocking of Pm@Fng with an anti-GPIbα antibody, both led to a significant reduction in Pm@Fng adhesion. This strongly indicates that the engagement of Pm@Fng with activated endothelium occurs via the canonical GPIbα-vWF axis. Finally, to directly demonstrate the therapeutic 'competitive inhibition' concept, we conducted a head-to-head competition assay in vitro (Fig. 6D). Natural platelets were first fluorescently stained with the Calcein-AM. The experiment was divided into four groups: blank control group, platelet control group, Pm@Fng control group and treatment group (where Pm@Fng and natural platelets were pre-mixed), followed by their addition to IL-1β-treated C166 cells. Experimental observations revealed that, compared to the platelet control group, the pre-occupation of binding sites by Pm@Fng in the treatment group significantly inhibited the adhesion of natural platelets. These data collectively provide direct evidence that Pm@Fng effectively suppresses thrombosis by competitively occupying vWF binding sites on activated endothelial cells, thereby preventing the recruitment of natural platelets.

Targeted homing of Pm@Fng to injured vascular endothelium

To assess in vivo targeting efficacy, we employed a mouse IVC stenosis model to simulate thrombotic formation. Following IVC stenosis-induced endothelial injury, ICG-labeled Fng or ICG-labeled Pm@Fng was administered via intravenous injection (Fig. 7A). Serial in vivo fluorescence imaging of the IVC was performed at designated time points (Fig. 7B). Platelet membrane-coated nanogels exhibited superior adhesive efficiency, demonstrating intense fluorescence signals as early as 5 min post-injection, with progressive enhancement peaking at 30 min and then the signals began to declining. Quantitative analysis revealed that Pm@Fng-treated groups exhibited 4-fold higher fluorescence intensity at the injury site compared to Ng-treated controls at the 30-min time point (Fig. 7C). This rapid enrichment property stems from the binding of GPIb complexes to vWF at damaged endothelial sites. To evaluate the in vivo metabolic pathways of the nanogels, fluorescence imaging of heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidneys was performed 24 h post-injection (Fig. 7D). Quantitative analysis revealed that the predominant metabolic organs were the liver and kidneys (Supplementary Fig. 13), consistent with the canonical nanoparticle clearance pathways [49]. Of particular note was the biodistribution in the spleen, a key organ of the mononuclear phagocyte system responsible for clearing foreign particles from circulation. Our data revealed a relatively low level of accumulation in the spleen. This observation is consistent with the "self-disguise" function of the platelet membrane coating. The presence of membrane-bound proteins such as CD47 on the surface of Pm@Fng likely provides a "don't eat me" signal to splenic macrophages, thereby helping the nanoparticles to evade phagocytic clearance and prolong their systemic circulation time. This immune-evasive property is crucial for ensuring that the nanoparticles can effectively reach the site of thrombosis before being cleared from the body.

Additionally, we isolated IVC segmented from both normal and model mice for in vitro testing. Ng and Pm@Fng solutions were injected into the vessels and incubated for 10 min, followed by three rinses with normal saline (Fig. 7E). Fluorescence intensity was applied to evaluate nanogel targeting efficiency. We observed lower fluorescence intensity in the Ng-treated group, whereas the Pm@Fng group exhibited significantly higher fluorescence intensity in the venous vessels of model mice compared to normal mice (Fig. 7F and G). This phenomenon suggested mechanical injury induced local collagen exposure, forming biomimetic targeting sites, and hemodynamic changes promoted passive retention of nanoparticles in vortical regions. These dual targeting mechanisms provided spatiotemporal precision for Pm@Fng's efficient thrombus intervention. Notably, stronger fluorescence signals were observed at the proximal and distal segments of the venous vessels, which might be attributed to suture ligation affecting rinsing efficiency at these sites. Collectively, Pm@Fng achieved precise enrichment in the thrombus core by specifically binding to vWF-collagen complexes and activated platelets/immune cells at endothelial injury sites. This capability to actively occupy key platelet adhesion targets underpins its high efficacy in thrombus intervention.

Pm@Fng effectively inhibits deep vein thrombosis via a platelet competitive adhesion mechanism

We evaluated the in vivo therapeutic efficacy of Pm@Fng. Using the IVC stenosis model, we successfully simulated the pathological processes of venous hemodynamic changes and endothelial injury. This model, which mechanically restricts venous return, accurately recapitulates key pathological features of human DVT formation, including blood stasis, vascular endothelial activation, and inflammatory cascade reactions [22], providing an ideal platform for dynamically monitoring thrombus evolution and evaluating the efficacy of therapeutic interventions. Here, C57BL/6 mice with successfully induced DVT were randomly divided into four groups (n = 10/group): (1) DVT model control group (treated with PBS). (2) DVT + Ng group (HAMA-PBA nanogel alone). (3) DVT + Fng group (FA-loaded nanogel). (4) DVT + Pm@Fng group (artificial platelets). Based on previous research, the therapeutic dosage range of FA in mice is found to be 10 - 40 mg/kg [[34], [35], [36],50]. In this study, 20 mg/kg was selected as the therapeutic dosage. According to the measured DLC of 19.7%, the mass of Pm@Fng used for treatment in this research was 100 mg/kg. The first dose of intervention was administered via the tail vein immediately after IVC ligation, followed by a daily timed and quantitative intravenous administration regimen. This sequential administration strategy aims to mimic the therapeutic window of clinical emergency thrombolytic therapy while ensuring sustained drug action during the critical phase of thrombus formation.

Continuous monitoring over 7 days revealed a thrombosis incidence rate of 90% in the PBS control group. The incidence rates in the Ng group, Fng group, and Pm@Fng group were reduced to 80%, 70%, and 60%, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 14.), with the Pm@Fng group exhibiting the most pronounced protective effect. Morphological parameter analysis of thrombi further confirmed that compared with the Ng and Fng groups, the Pm@Fng group showed a more significant reduction trend in both thrombus weight and length. Specifically, thrombus weight decreased from 17.29 ± 4.65 mg to 9.10 ± 3.57 mg (p < 0.01), and thrombus length decreased from 8.01 ± 1.30 mm to 2.98 ± 1.21 mm (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 8A–H, I), indicating stronger thrombus-inhibitory effects. Imaging ultrasound detection of thrombus size 2 days post-modeling (acute thrombosis phase) revealed that the Pm@Fng group displayed superior antithrombotic properties (Fig. 8B). Histopathological analysis via HE staining showed that the PBS group exhibited typical inflammatory changes in the vessel wall: disruption of endothelial continuity (Fig. 8C), edema of the smooth muscle layer, and massive inflammatory cell infiltration. After intervention, the number of inflammatory cells in the Ng and Fng groups decreased significantly, while the Pm@Fng group demonstrated optimal anti-inflammatory effects, with better maintenance of vascular wall structural integrity (Fig. 8C–J). This was directly associated with its competitive occupation of damaged endothelial sites, reducing local inflammatory triggers. Quantitative analysis by Masson's trichrome staining indicated that the area of fibrin deposition within thrombi in the Pm@Fng group was significantly lower than that in the Ng and Fng groups (Fig. 8D–K), confirming its ability to inhibit thrombus progression by regulating fibrin network formation. Innate immune responses are recognized as the first line of defense against endothelial injury, mediated by diverse cell subsets (macrophages, neutrophils). However, the recruitment of these innate immune cells following their sensing of damage-associated or pathogen-associated molecular patterns may also lead to tissue damage. Reactive oxygen or nitrogen species continuously produced by activated neutrophils disrupt cell-cell junctions, leading to severe tissue damage and endothelial dysfunction. Macrophages secrete inflammatory cytokines that trigger vasodilation and increase vascular permeability, thereby promoting the aggregation of circulating leukocytes. Here, we attempted to determine immune cell residency via tissue staining, which revealed that Pm@Fng reduced the infiltration of F4/80+ macrophages and Ly6G + neutrophils into the endothelial layer (Fig. 8E, F, L, M). This multi-targeted anti-inflammatory effect was attributed to the ability of its platelet membrane components to competitively bind to damaged sites, neutralize inflammatory chemokines, and thereby weaken the recruitment and activation of immune cells. Immunohistochemistry revealed that Pm@Fng significantly reduced the expression of endothelial activation markers VCAM-1 and ICAM-1 (Fig. 8G, H, N, O). This further demonstrated its ability to protect the endothelium through competitive adhesion, reducing the release of pro-inflammatory and pro-adhesive signals. Collectively, these findings indicate that the core mechanism of Pm@Fng lies in its competitive adhesion capability conferred by its platelet membrane. By efficiently competing to bind to damaged vascular endothelial sites, Pm@Fng achieves multiple synergistic antithrombotic effects: preserving endothelial integrity, inhibiting pathological leukocyte infiltration and endothelial activation, and regulating fibrin deposition, ultimately significantly reducing the risk of thrombus formation.

Our results show that Pm@Fng effectively reduces thrombus size in a mouse DVT model. This efficacy is achieved through a mechanism that is fundamentally different from standard anticoagulants like heparin or DOACs. These conventional drugs act by systemically inhibiting coagulation, a potent but non-specific approach that creates a significant risk of bleeding. In contrast, Pm@Fng works locally. It competitively binds to the activated endothelium at the injury site, physically blocking platelet adhesion. This targeted mechanism should, in theory, prevent thrombosis without impairing systemic hemostasis. We ran a series of safety studies to confirm this. First, to assess their suitability for intravenous administration, a hemolysis assay was performed. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S15, nanogels induced negligible hemolysis (<5%), demonstrating its excellent blood compatibility. To further evaluate the in vivo biocompatibility of Ng, Fng, and Pm@Fng, these materials were administered via intravenous injection via the tail vein to mice daily for one week. Following treatment with normal saline and the above materials, tissue samples from the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney were subjected to hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining. Compared with the control and model groups, there were no pathological differences or evidence of inflammatory cell infiltration in the tissue sections, demonstrating the excellent in vivo biocompatibility of this system (Supplementary Fig. 16). The key test was whether the targeted mechanism translates to a better safety profile in practice. A direct comparison with heparin was telling. As expected, heparin severely disrupted coagulation, leading to prolonged clotting times (aPTT, TT) and significantly increased bleeding. Pm@Fng, however, had no such effect. Mice treated with our nanogel showed coagulation profiles and bleeding times that were indistinguishable from the untreated control group (Supplementary Fig. 17).

These findings directly support our design concept. Pm@Fng's targeted action successfully separates its antithrombotic effect from the bleeding side effects that limit current therapies. This alone is a major advantage. Additionally, by delivering the FA, Pm@Fng is designed to promote vascular repair. This dual function—blocking thrombosis while facilitating healing—addresses the underlying pathology in a way that current anticoagulants cannot.

Pm@Fng restores endothelial homeostasis via coordinated modulation of the cGAS-STING-NLRP3 signaling axis

In the aforementioned studies, we demonstrated that the cGAS-STING-NLRP3 signaling axis drives DVT pathogenesis through dual mechanisms: STING-dependent type I interferon pathway activation driving endothelial inflammatory phenotypes, and NLRP3 inflammasome-mediated pyroptosis causing vascular barrier disruption. To validate this, we generated STING knockout mouse models, which revealed that inhibiting this pathway significantly attenuated thrombus formation and preserved endothelial integrity. Subsequently, through AI-assisted virtual drug screening, our study found that FA could effectively inhibit the phosphorylation of STING and its downstream signaling molecules TBK1 and IRF3 (Fig. 4G). Furthermore, a crucial aspect of our proposed mechanism is the precise mode of action for FA following its release from the Pm@Fng carrier. It is important to clarify that our findings indicate FA functions as an intracellular inhibitor rather than acting upon a specific cell surface receptor on injured endothelial cells. In this model, the therapeutic action unfolds in a two-step process. First, triggered by the acidic and high-ROS thrombotic microenvironment, FA is released locally from the nanocarrier. As a small molecule, it can then readily cross the cell membrane to enter the cytoplasm. Once inside the cell, FA exerts its pharmacologic effect by directly engaging its specific intracellular target, the STING protein, which is primarily located in the cytoplasm and on the endoplasmic reticulum membrane. This intracellular targeting mechanism is strongly supported by two key lines of evidence presented in our study. Our initial AI-assisted computational docking (Fig. 4A–F) was designed to model the direct binding of FA into the ligand-binding pocket of the STING protein itself, predicting a high-affinity interaction. This prediction was then functionally validated by our downstream signaling experiments, as the inhibition of the canonical STING-TBK1-IRF3 intracellular signaling cascade (Fig. 4G) provides compelling experimental evidence that FA's point of intervention occurs inside the cell. Therefore, our collective data coherently point to a mechanism where FA acts not through a surface receptor but by directly targeting the intracellular STING protein to suppress endothelial inflammation. This provides a new therapeutic strategy for the prevention and treatment of DVT.

Building on these findings, we investigated whether Pm@Fng could target the cGAS-STING-NLRP3 axis via platelet-competitive adhesion. Immunofluorescence staining demonstrated co-expression of STING and NLRP3 in CD31+ endothelial cells. Pm@Fng treatment markedly reduced STING and NLRP3 expression on endothelial cells (Fig. 9A), with superior efficacy compared to Ng and Fng groups. Furthermore, Western blot and immunofluorescence analyses confirmed that Pm@Fng suppressed phosphorylation of downstream signaling molecules TBK1 and IRF3, as well as STING itself (Fig. 9B, Supplementary Fig. 18A). This robust suppression of the STING pathway directly resulted from its competitive occupation of damaged endothelial sites, effectively blocking upstream signaling transduction. Subsequently, we evaluated the regulatory effects of artificial platelets on the NLRP3 inflammasome and its downstream effectors. Immunofluorescence staining revealed that Pm@Fng significantly inhibited caspase-1 (Casp-1) activation and GSDMD pore formation in CD31+ endothelial cells (Fig. 9A), critical steps in pyroptosis execution. Western blot quantitative analysis further demonstrated that Pm@Fng markedly downregulated expression levels of key NLRP3 inflammasome components (NLRP3, ASC, Casp1) and executioner protein GSDMD in endothelial cells (Fig. 9C, Supplementary Fig. 18B), with superior efficacy compared to other treatment groups. This outcome was attributed to the enhanced local enrichment enabled by platelet-competitive adhesion, which significantly improved targeted delivery efficiency to block inflammasome assembly. Collectively, these findings indicate that Pm@Fng exerts dual regulatory effects through competitive adhesion: (1) suppressing upstream cGAS-mediated sensing of cytoplasmic DNA to inhibit STING-TBK1-IRF3 axis activation and cytokine production, and (2) interfering with downstream NLRP3 inflammasome assembly to block Casp-1/GSDMD-mediated endothelial pyroptosis, thereby preserving vascular barrier integrity.

Pyroptosis, a form of programmed cell death characterized by nuclear pyknosis, DNA fragmentation, plasma membrane rupture, membrane vesicle formation, and release of ROS and inflammatory cytokines, has been implicated in endothelial injury [51]. Notably, cytoplasmic DNA-induced activation of the STING-IRF3 signaling axis further promotes ROS generation and pro-inflammatory cytokine production [52]. Our study revealed significantly activated ROS metabolic pathways and oxidative stress responses in DVT endothelial cells (Fig. 1F), suggesting that inhibition of the cGAS-STING-NLRP3 axis effectively blocks ROS biosynthesis. Crucially, the FA-PBA borate ester bond within the artificial platelet structure confers ROS-responsive properties, enabling targeted clearance of ROS in the endothelial microenvironment. This dual-modal regulatory mechanism allows the nanogel to both suppress ROS generation and chemically eliminate existing ROS, achieving dynamic modulation of endothelial oxidative stress. Experimental data demonstrated that IL-1β-stimulated endothelial cells exhibited marked ROS elevation detected by dihydroethidium fluorescence probe, which was significantly reduced following Pm@Fng intervention (Fig. 9D, Supplementary Fig. 18F). As a general indicator of cell death and loss of membrane integrity, flow cytometric Annexin V/PI double staining further confirmed the superior cytoprotective efficacy of Pm@Fng compared to Fng and Ng groups (Fig. 9E–F). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that artificial platelets successfully alleviate endothelial oxidative stress injury through multi-targeted regulatory strategies.

Subsequently, to systematically evaluate the endothelial repair efficacy of the nanogel, this study constructed an in vitro thrombotic microenvironment model: after 12 h of stimulation with IL-1β (10 ng/mL) on endothelial cells, different formulations were administered for 4 h of intervention. Six groups were included: normal control group, IL-1β model group, FA monotherapy group, Ng group, Fng group, and Pm@Fng group. Multidimensional functional analyses revealed: Western blot results (Supplementary Fig. 18C and D) showed that Pm@Fng significantly downregulated the expression of TF, a key mediator of thrombus formation, and endothelial activation markers (P-selectin, VCAM-1, ICAM-1), with inhibitory effects significantly superior to other treatment groups, indicating the formulation's dual role in synergistically inhibiting inflammatory responses and endothelial activation. In vitro adhesion assays confirmed that Pm@Fng most effectively blocked the recruitment of activated macrophages (RAW 264.7) by injured endothelial cells (Supplementary Fig. 18E and G), which was directly associated with its competitive occupation of adhesion sites and downregulation of adhesion molecule expression. Scratch wound healing assays demonstrated that Pm@Fng-treated groups significantly accelerated the migration and repair of damaged endothelial cells (Supplementary Fig. 18H and I). Collectively, these findings confirmed that Pm@Fng will efficiently target damaged endothelial sites through competitive adhesion with circulating platelets, not only directly blocking the activation of the cGAS-STING-NLRP3 pathway and endothelial pyroptosis but also synergizing with its ROS-responsive properties to achieve dynamic regulation of oxidative stress. Ultimately, this multi-targeted synergistic effect effectively inhibits pathological endothelial activation, reduces inflammatory infiltration, promotes functional repair, and achieves a comprehensive intervention, addressing both the prevention of pathological platelet adhesion and the restoration of endothelial function.

Transcriptional evidence of Pm@Fng-mediated endothelial phenotype reprogramming

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying Pm@Fng-mediated repair of DVT endothelial injury, RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) was performed on endothelial cells from three groups: physiological control (Con), IL-1β-induced thrombotic microenvironment (Model), and Pm@Fng-treated groups (n = 4 per group). Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and hierarchical clustering revealed distinct transcriptional profiles, with the Pm@Fng group demonstrating significant convergence toward the Con group and divergence from the Model group (Fig. 10A–C). This directly demonstrated Pm@Fng's capacity to reverse pathology-induced transcriptional dysregulation. Functional enrichment analysis of the top 50 differentially expressed genes (GO/KEGG) identified inflammation, vascular remodeling, cellular adhesion, and immune regulation as key pathways (Fig. 10D), aligning with the putative targets regulated by platelet-competitive adhesion.

GSEA pathway enrichment analysis showed that, compared with the Con group, the inflammatory response and oxidative stress-redox pathways in the Model group were significantly up-regulated (Fig. 10E–F). Strikingly, Pm@Fng intervention significantly inhibited these pathological pathways and simultaneously enriched repair-related mechanisms, including: the enhancement of the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, which promotes endothelial proliferation and migration through the activation of AKT1 and eNOS [53] (Supplementary Fig. 19A); the significant induction of the vascular remodeling signaling pathway (Supplementary Fig. 19B). Importantly, the NOD-like receptor signaling pathway was significantly down-regulated in the Pm@Fng group (Supplementary Fig. 19C), which was consistent with our previous finding that Pm@Fng reduces caspase-1-dependent endothelial pyroptosis by inhibiting the STING-NLRP3 crosstalk. The combined comparative analysis of differential genes revealed that there were 5535 differentially expressed genes between the Model group and the Con group, among which 2691 genes were up-regulated and 2844 genes were down-regulated; 5071 differentially expressed genes were identified in the Pm@Fng group compared with the Model group, among which 2605 genes were up-regulated and 2466 genes were down-regulated, indicating its strong transcriptional regulation ability. Furthermore, further analysis revealed that the upregulated inflammatory genes and adhesion molecules in the Model group were significantly suppressed in the Pm@Fng group. This indicates that Pm@Fng, through competitive adhesion-mediated targeted intervention, can successfully reprogram endothelial cells from a “pro-inflammatory/pro-adhesive” phenotype to an “anti-inflammatory/inhibitory-adhesion” phenotype. Collectively, the consistency between transcriptomic data and functional experiments further underscores that the platelet-competitive adhesion mechanism of Pm@Fng serves as the physical basis for its achievement of “endothelial phenotype reprogramming”.By efficiently anchoring to damaged endothelium, it not only blocks the transmission of pro-inflammatory/pro-adhesive signals (e.g., inhibiting the NLRP3 pathway) but also actively activates endogenous repair programs (e.g., PI3K-Akt, vascular remodeling), ultimately reprogramming pathologically altered endothelium to a repair steady state. This highlights its translational value in DVT treatment.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study warrant discussion, particularly concerning the experimental models, mechanistic interpretations, and translational potential.

First, our in vivo experiments were conducted exclusively in mice. These models may not fully recapitulate the complex hemodynamic and immunologic features of human DVT. To corroborate our mechanistic conclusions and enhance their translational relevance, future analyses of clinical specimens will be essential. Furthermore, this study utilized a global STING knockout mouse model. Although our in vitro data and immunofluorescence staining strongly implicate STING's critical role in endothelial cells, we cannot rule out contributions from STING signaling in other cell types, such as infiltrating immune cells, to the overall DVT pathology. Future investigations employing endothelial-specific STING knockout mice are therefore crucial for precisely delineating these cell-type-specific contributions.

From a drug development perspective, further validation is necessary. While AI-guided screening identified FA as a putative STING inhibitor, definitive target engagement, structure-based confirmation, and a comprehensive pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) characterization are required to establish its translational suitability. Similarly, the long-term biocompatibility, biodistribution, and potential immunogenicity of the Pm@Fng delivery vehicle must be systematically assessed, preferably in large-animal models.

An additional critical consideration is the therapeutic window of our strategy. In this study, Pm@Fng was administered immediately post-surgery to best validate the 'competitive adhesion' hypothesis, suggesting a potential prophylactic application in high-risk patients undergoing major surgery or with prolonged immobilization. For therapeutic intervention, its efficacy is likely highest during the initial stages of DVT formation. The competitive binding mechanism may be less effective against established, large thrombi, although the anti-inflammatory and endothelial-reparative effects of released FA could still offer benefits. Future studies could therefore explore combination therapies, wherein traditional thrombolytics are used to dissolve an existing clot, followed by Pm@Fng administration to prevent recurrence and promote vascular healing.

Addressing these gaps will be crucial for refining the platform and facilitating the clinical translation of this biomimetic platelet strategy.

Conclusion

This study provides novel insights into the critical role of the STING pathway in the pathogenesis of DVT. Our findings demonstrate that the STING pathway mediates endothelial pyroptosis through the NLRP3 inflammasome, a key process in the development of thrombotic diseases. Building upon this mechanistic understanding, we designed artificial platelets, Pm@Fng, which harness the competitive adhesion capabilities of APMVs. These artificial platelets effectively target damaged endothelial sites, responding to the acidic and ROS microenvironments by utilizing FA-PBA boronate ester bonds. The combination of physical blockage through competitive adhesion and chemical regulation via smart materials that inhibit the ROS/cGAS-STING-NLRP3 pathway offers a unique and effective therapeutic strategy.

In vitro and in vivo experiments, Pm@Fng demonstrated significant efficacy in competitively adhering to damaged endothelium, preventing platelet aggregation, and reducing thrombotic incidence. Specifically, the thrombotic formation rate decreased from nearly 90% to 60%, thrombus length reduced from 8.01 ± 1.30 mm to 2.98 ± 1.21 mm, and thrombus weight decreased from 17.29 ± 4.65 mg to 9.10 ± 3.57 mg. By targeting endothelial injury sites, Pm@Fng regulates key signaling pathways, mitigating endothelial activation, reducing inflammatory cell recruitment, and promoting endothelial repair and migration. This approach represents a comprehensive intervention, addressing both the prevention of pathological platelet adhesion and the restoration of endothelial function. In conclusion, our findings provide a promising new strategy for DVT treatment, overcoming the limitations of current therapies by specifically targeting and repairing endothelial damage. This approach sets the stage for the development of advanced nanomedicines for thrombotic diseases and broader endothelial injury-related conditions.
