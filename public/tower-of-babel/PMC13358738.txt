Pulmonary hypertension following lung transplant: a systematic review
Authors: Roudi Bachar, Dany Tager, Jana Berro, Neil Nero, Sameep Sehgal, Atul C. Mehta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPost-lung-transplant development of pulmonary hypertension (PH) is an increasingly recognized disease process with limited data regarding its etiologies and clinical course. We performed a systematic review to evaluate reported causes and patterns of PH following lung transplantation.MethodsThis systematic review was registered with PROSPERO (CRD42025649383) and conducted according to PRISMA guidelines. A comprehensive search strategy was developed with a medical librarian and applied to MEDLINE (Ovid), Embase (Ovid), and the Cochrane Library. Studies were screened and managed using Covidence. Two reviewers independently screened titles, abstracts, and full texts, with disagreements resolved by consensus or a third reviewer. Eligible studies included case reports, retrospective or cohort studies, randomized controlled trials, and conference abstracts reporting post-lung-transplant PH with defined diagnostic criteria and plausible etiology. Studies without full text or sufficient data, non-original analyses, animal models, or non-English articles were excluded. Data were extracted using standardized forms, and risk of bias was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale.ResultsFifteen studies met the inclusion criteria, including 12 case reports and 3 retrospective cohort studies, encompassing 223 patients. The most common etiology was chronic lung allograft dysfunction (CLAD), particularly bronchiolitis obliterans syndrome (BOS), accounting for 63% of cases. Other etiologies included vascular or anastomotic complications, recurrence of idiopathic pulmonary arterial hypertension, and recurrent thromboembolic disease.ConclusionsPost-lung-transplant PH most frequently occurs in association with CLAD/BOS. Prospective longitudinal studies are needed to better define the temporal relationship between CLAD/BOS and PH and to inform surveillance and management strategies.

Highlight box

Key findings

• In this systematic review of 15 studies including 223 lung transplant recipients, chronic lung allograft dysfunction (CLAD), particularly bronchiolitis obliterans syndrome (BOS), was the most frequently reported cause of post-transplant pulmonary hypertension (PH), accounting for 63% of cases.

• Other reported etiologies included vascular or anastomotic complications, recurrence of idiopathic pulmonary arterial hypertension, and recurrent thromboembolic disease.

What is known and what is new?

• PH can develop after lung transplantation and has been associated with adverse outcomes; however, its causes, prevalence, and clinical course have not been well characterized.

• This systematic review synthesizes the available literature on post-transplant PH and identifies CLAD/BOS as the predominant reported etiology, while also describing less common causes such as vascular complications, recurrent pulmonary arterial hypertension, and thromboembolic disease

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• The strong association between CLAD/BOS and post-transplant PH suggests that clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for PH in patients with allograft dysfunction. Prospective longitudinal studies are needed to clarify the temporal relationship between CLAD/BOS and PH, determine the role of routine surveillance, and guide future management strategies.

Introduction

Pulmonary hypertension (PH) is a progressive and potentially life-threatening condition characterized by elevated pulmonary arterial pressure. In the general population, the prevalence of PH is estimated to be about 1%, increasing to up to 7% in individuals older than 65 years (1,2). This prevalence is likely underestimated due to the underdiagnosis and underreporting of the condition, especially in developing countries where access to diagnostic and therapeutic resources is limited (3). The gold standard for diagnosis of PH is right heart catheterization (RHC) with a mean pulmonary arterial pressure (mPAP) greater than 20 mmHg (1). It has been phenotyped into five groups based on hemodynamic and clinical pathophysiology (1). Group 1 PH, sometimes referred to as pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH), is characterized by normal post-capillary pulmonary wedge pressure (PCWP <15 mmHg) and elevated pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR >2), without associated lung disease (1,2). Group 2 PH is described by increased PCWP with normal PVR and primarily cardiac etiology (2,4). This is the most prevalent form of PH, with 65–80% of cases being group 2 PH (2). Group 3 PH is primarily due to hypoxia from parenchymal lung disease, with increased PVR, and normal PCWP (2,5). Group 4 PH is due to increased vascular resistance from chronic thromboembolic disease or other vascular obstructions (1,2). Group 5 PH is defined by multifactorial or unclear mechanisms and does not have an associated hemodynamic pattern (2).

Lung transplant is a therapeutic option for patients with advanced group 1 and group 3 diseases and can be considered “curative”. However, lung transplant recipients remain at risk for PH due to any of these mechanisms. In this article, we reviewed available literature related to PH in lung transplant recipients post-lung transplantation. We present this article in accordance with the PRISMA reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-0713/rc).

Methods

System registration

This study was registered with PROSPERO maintained by the National Institute for Health and Care Research under the record number of CRD42025649383. There were no similar prior registered studies at the time of registration, and no changes were made to study design after initiation.

Search strategy

A comprehensive search strategy was developed with a medical librarian (N.N.) to identify potentially relevant articles discussing instances of PH following a lung transplant. Indexing terms and keywords with truncation were combined using Boolean operators. The full strategy is available in Appendix 1.

The following databases were searched from inception until October 17th, 2024: MEDLINE (Ovid), Embase (Ovid), and Cochrane Library. Covidence software was used to manage, screen, deduplicate, and document this systematic review. Search results were independently screened and assessed based on inclusion criteria by two authors (R.B. and D.T.). Full text of all titles that met inclusion criteria by a majority vote was obtained.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (I) case-report study, retrospective/cohort study, randomized controlled trial, conference abstracts; (II) diagnostic criteria for PH readily available; (III) etiology of recurrent PH with moderate-high likelihood of accuracy; (IV) low likelihood of non-PH disease causing primary problem with patient.

Exclusion criteria were as follows: (I) missing full text or data from article; (II) known meta-analysis or non-original data; (III) animal models; (IV) non-English articles.

Data extraction and risk of bias

A web-based platform, Covidence, was used for conducting a systematic review. Literature was screened and extracted independently by two researchers (R.B. and D.T.). Where there was disagreement between the two researchers, discussion was had. If there was still disagreement, a third party (A.C.M.) was consulted for the final decision. Titles and abstracts were screened for relevance before full-text review. Full-text articles were screened using the inclusion criteria above. If articles were deemed appropriate, final data extraction occurred. Article information included study author, year of publication, journal, design of study, number of included patients, population type, PH diagnostic criteria, etiology of PH, time from transplant until presentation of recurrence, PH type, WHO group classification, and mortality. Due to the nature of this review, descriptive statistics were used to determine the etiologies. A risk of bias analysis was conducted using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) (6) by two authors independently (RB & DT). Any disagreement would be discussed and differences corrected. If further disagreement occurred, a third author would have final say (AM).

Results

Study identification and screening

A total of 1,809 potentially relevant studies were identified. After removing duplicates, a total of 1,369 titles and abstracts were screened for eligibility. Thirty full-text articles were eventually assessed for inclusion, of which 15 studies met the predefined criteria and were included in this study (Figure 1).

PRISMA flow chart for systematic review.

Study properties and risk of bias

A total of 15 studies met the inclusion criteria (Table 1), comprising 12 case reports and three retrospective cohort studies (7-21). Collectively, these studies included 223 patients, of whom 12 were described in case reports and 211 were enrolled in observational cohorts. All studies were conducted in adult populations. Fourteen studies incorporated RHC data as part of the diagnostic evaluation for recurrent PH. Englmeier et al. (18), on the other hand, utilized RHC data obtained prior to lung transplantation and relied on transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) for the diagnosis of post-transplant PH, representing the only study to employ different diagnostic modalities before and after transplantation. In studies using RHC, PH was defined as mPAP >25 mmHg. Notably, the three retrospective cohort studies (18-20) were conducted prior to the updated hemodynamic definition of PH, of mPAP >20 mmHg (22). Risk of bias was assessed using the NOS, with all three cohort studies rated as having fair quality (Table 2).

Etiologies of PH and outcomes

The most common cause of PH was group 3 PH due to chronic lung allograft dysfunction (CLAD) from bronchiolitis obliterans syndrome (BOS) (Table 1). In the study by Englmeier et al. (18), 68 lung transplant recipients underwent echocardiographic screening for PH, of whom 15 (22%) screened positive. Within the overall cohort, 14 patients had BOS, while 54 did not. PH was identified in 7 of the 14 patients with BOS (50%), accounting for nearly half of all PH-positive cases. In contrast, only 8 of the 54 patients without BOS (15%) screened positive for PH. The mean time from lung transplantation to PH diagnosis was 4.5±0.3 years. Mortality outcomes were not reported. This study used echocardiography to establish a diagnosis of PH, which is a significant limitation.

In the study by Nathan et al. (19), 80 lung transplant recipients with BOS who were undergoing a retransplant evaluation were screened for PH, of whom 32.5% were found to have PH. Of note, this study does use the previous World Symposium on Pulmonary Hypertension (WSPH) definition of PH with a mPAP >25 mmHg. There is a significant cohort of 36.25% of patients who would meet the new definition with mPAP >20 mmHg. 42% of the cohort that did have PH by their definition also had elevated PCWP. No significant differences in PH prevalence were observed between single- and double-lung transplant recipients or across underlying primary disease etiologies. There was no correlation between forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1), forced vital capacity (FVC), or FEV1/FVC and the development of PH. There is no way to determine if a cohort with the new WSPH definition of PH would have associated changes in their pulmonary function tests (PFTs).

In 2008, Saggar et al. retrospectively reviewed 52 lung transplant recipients with available pathology (explants, autopsy or surgical biopsy) (20). Seventy-two percent of the 29 patients with BOS had PH diagnosed based on echocardiographic findings or RHC measurements, and 56% of patients also had right ventricular dysfunction. None of the patients without biopsy proven BOS had PH. Right ventricular systolic pressure (RVSP) was significantly higher in patients with BOS compared with those without BOS (53 vs. 33.1 mmHg, respectively). (20). The majority of patients with BOS had significant pulmonary arteriopathy (84%) and venopathy (77%) on pathology. They reported a significant association between pathological BOS with development of both pulmonary arteriopathy and venopathy on multivariate analysis.

CLAD was implicated in two additional case reports included in our study. One patient developed recurrent PH 22 years after transplantation in the setting of PAH, CLAD, and thromboembolic disease, and survived to undergo re-transplantation (15). In contrast, another patient with CLAD-associated recurrence diagnosed 8 years post-transplant died during plasmapheresis (12).

Idiopathic PAH recurrence was reported in two case reports. Recurrence was diagnosed at 12 months in one patient who underwent re-transplantation of double lung transplantation for PAH (21). In this case, both the original explant and post-mortem pathology did not reveal plexiform lesions. Kurn et al. (7) describe a case of recurrent IPAH 32 months after bilateral lung transplant. Biopsy in this case was negative for plexiform lesions, raising the strength of the underlying diagnosis by the authors. This patient improved with oral PAH therapy.

Recurrent pulmonary veno-occlusive disease was identified as the cause of PH recurrence in two cases. One patient was diagnosed 18 months after transplantation and died at 24 months (8), whereas the other was diagnosed at 3 months post-transplant with no report on mortality (14).

Five cases attributed PH to post-transplant anatomical abnormalities of the pulmonary vasculature. These included anastomotic pulmonary artery (PA) or pulmonary venous stenosis in three patients. These were diagnosed at 6 months (10), 5 years (9), and 6 years (11), respectively. One additional case involved an anastomotic PA “kink” identified within 3 hours of the surgery (13), while another described PA trunk stenosis secondary to donor–recipient size mismatch, diagnosed two weeks after surgery (16). In the case by Soriano et al. (11), there was notable stricture at the PA anastomosis with the authors hinting that this complication may have been due to site of anastomosis, specifically the upper-lobe takeoff. All cases with anatomically mediated recurrence reported survival.

A single case of a patient with idiopathic pleuroparenchymal fibroelastosis (iPPFE) who underwent single-lung transplantation was identified (17). Chest wall flattening led to pulmonary venous outflow obstruction, resulting in worsening PH and death from heart failure and infection 1.5 years after transplantation. Autopsy demonstrated secondary fibrotic remodeling of both arterial and venous structures in the transplanted lung, indicating that iPPFE can affect the pulmonary circulation even after a single lung transplantation.

Discussion

PH can occur in lung transplant recipients via several mechanisms and can develop in the early post-transplant period (<6 months) or many years after.

In our review of the published literature, anatomical or surgical malformations and recurrence of primary PH were reported in the early post-transplant period. However, this remains a rarely reported cause with data limited to case reports. There are also instances of this occurring after years of transplant, which may follow a U-shaped distribution of incidence. In both cases of recurrent IPAH, plexiform lesions were negative. There is ongoing discussion within the literature about whether plexiform lesions are a result of severe PAH or an entity of a specific pulmonary vasculopathy (23). The cases described give evidence that there may be an entity of severe IPAH that is not plexiform lesion driven. The histopathologic studies study by Saggar et al. (20) demonstrate that CLAD is not solely an airway-centered fibro-obliterative disease but also involves extensive arteriopathy and venopathy. This suggests that vascular remodeling parallels airway fibrosis and contributes directly to elevated PVR. These vascular changes are further amplified by chronic hypoxic vasoconstriction resulting from progressive airflow obstruction, air trapping, and ventilation-perfusion mismatch. Sustained hypoxia promotes endothelial dysfunction, smooth muscle proliferation, and impaired nitric oxide signaling, ultimately leading to fixed pulmonary vascular remodeling (24). Inflammatory and alloimmune pathways also appear central to the development of PH in CLAD/BOS. ELR+ CVC chemokines have been found to be persistently elevated in BOS and drive vascular remodeling during fibro-obliteration of the allograft airway. Persistent activation of the CXCR2/ELR+ CXC chemokine axis promotes aberrant angiogenesis and fibroproliferation within the allograft, linking another pathway to BOS and new PH (25,26). In murine models, anti-CXCR2 antibodies attenuated both vascular remodeling and fibro-obliteration, establishing a link between chemokine-mediated neovascularization and the fibroproliferative process (25). Although this mechanism is not fully established, other key inflammatory mediators in CLAD/BOS development of PH potentially include TGF-β, IL-6, IL-8, and TNF-α (24,27).

The most common etiology of PH in lung transplant recipients is group 3 PH due to CLAD/BOS, developing several years after lung transplant. It is unclear if these patients have symptoms of CLAD/BOS or worsening spirometry prior to their echocardiogram / RHC findings; however, we can postulate that the mechanism is PH due to underlying lung disease. There was no correlation between severity of PH and CLAD, although the data are very limited. The temporal relationship between CLAD/BOS and PH is unclear. In Nathan et al.’s study (19), there was no clear relationship between PFT abnormalities and PH in patients with CLAD/BOS. This hints that CLAD/BOS may be occurring in patients before PFT abnormalities occur. There are currently no guidelines for surveillance echocardiograms in post-transplant patients. This may be an area in the future for further study as CLAD/BOS may present in the form of PH prior to change in PFT’s. There are no current recommendations for treatment of PH specifically in the post-transplant patient. In the studies reviewed, patients with IPAH were treated with dual oral therapy or with re-transplantation. All patients with surgical abnormalities must be considered for re-transplantation or stenting. For patients with pre-capillary PH from CLAD/BOS there were no clear treatment regimens. inhaled treprostinil may be a suitable treatment for patients in this cohort due to similar mechanisms of group 3 PH (28). It should be noted that due to the nature of these treatments being from observational studies or case reports, prospective studies are needed to determine their true efficacy. However, we did not find any data in the literature supporting its use in lung transplant recipients.

This systematic review has inherent limitations due to a small number of included studies, heterogeneity in study designs, patient populations, diagnostic criteria, exclusion of non-English articles, variations in definitions of PH and differences in screening protocols. Approximately 95% of the patients in this review are from 3 cohort studies. The inherent selection bias of these studies, notably in Nathan et al. (19) and Saggar et al. (20), can limit the true prevalence of PH in post-lung transplant patient cohorts. Another limitation is the potential heterogeneity in quality of the 12 case reports in this study, including abstracts from conferences. There is no formal consensus on how to appraise these studies for quality or risk-of-bias (29).

Conclusions

PH is common in lung transplant recipients with BOS. Prevalence, risk factors, association with outcomes, and therapeutic options need to be studied.
