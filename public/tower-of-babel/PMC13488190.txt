The predictive power of first recruits to commercial trial performance
Authors: Martin J. Batty, James M. S. Wason, David J. Ward, Effrossyni Gkrania-Klotsas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe United Kingdom has a history of world-leading health research, showcased most recently in its response to the Covid pandemic. However, while the UK’s performance in academic clinical research remains strong, its global position in the delivery of commercial research has declined. In 2023 Lord O’Shaughnessy reviewed the barriers and drivers to the delivery of commercial trials, identifying 27 recommendations to improve commercial trial performance. Highlighted within the report was the importance of the UK being globally competitive in the delivery of its trials, including study set up and approval times.MethodsA dataset from the National Institute for Health and Care Research (NIHR), containing 28,188 site observations from 5,421 unique commercial studies provided data on first recruits to commercial studies spanning a 15-year period from 2010 to 2024. Where there were issues with data quality or reliability, data were cleaned, leaving 23,622 observations from 4,849 studies for analysis. Logistic regression and generalised linear mixed effects models were applied to determine whether sites that recruited their first participant to commercial studies more quickly had a higher probability of recruiting to time and target.ResultsSites that recruited their first participant more quickly were significantly more likely to recruit to time and target (RTT); (odds ratio per one-month increase in time 0.88, 95% CI 0.87 to 0.89, p < 0.0001). The model suggests that the predicted probability of RTT for a site falls to < 50% if it takes more than 8 months to recruit its first participant. A secondary analysis showed a highly significant increase in probability of a site meeting RTT if they recruited the ‘first’ Global, European or UK participants onto a study (odds ratio 2.96, 95% CI 2.20 to 4.05, p < 0.0001).ConclusionsSites that recruit first participants more quickly are more likely to recruit to time and target in commercial studies. This is likely to lead to them being selected by sponsors to participate in subsequent studies, increasing research opportunities for their patients and revenue for their organisation. Achieving quicker firsts requires proactive planning and coordination by R&D departments and study teams. Setting up sites in a timely manner is a key determinant of successful study delivery and a key component of the UK government’s 10-Year Health Plan for England and Life Sciences Sector Plan.Trial registrationNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12874-026-02907-z.

Background

The Covid-19 pandemic shone a spotlight on the importance of clinical trials in testing the efficacy and cost-effectiveness of health interventions in the prevention and treatment of ill health and disease [1]. During the pandemic, clinical trials were set up and delivered at unprecedented pace and scale. However, although the United Kingdom was at the vanguard of scientific innovation and discovery, its prioritisation of urgent public health studies over its more usual balanced portfolio of commercial/non-commercial research added further pressure to an already strained system and the UK’s subsequent commercial global standing. In December 2024 the Association of the British Pharmaceutical Industry [2] produced an extended report highlighting the value of commercial clinical trials to the economy of the United Kingdom. The report’s authors calculated that in 2022 alone, industry trials generated £7.4 billion and supported 65,000 jobs. Since its peak of 690 trials initiated in the UK during 2015, there has been a gradual decline in commercial activity, dropping by over two fifths to just 394 studies initiated in 2021.

In response to this decline in commercial activity, the UK Government commissioned a report to review the clinical trial landscape and ‘unlock UK health, growth and investment opportunities’ [3]. The report concluded that clinical trials were hampered by bureaucracy and delay and went on to make 27 separate recommendations to help restore the UK’s position. Some of these recommendations and areas of concern have been echoed by clinical academics, who have highlighted the need to streamline systems and remove bureaucracy [4, 5].

Although universities and healthcare settings have begun to implement some of O’Shaughnessy’s recommendations, sponsors are increasingly turning to other countries who are perceived as more reliable and better placed to deliver their studies to time and target. This has not gone unnoticed by the UK Government, who in their policy paper on transforming the UK clinical research system [6] vowed to ‘turbocharge research’ reducing bureaucracy and setup times for clinical research studies by over 100 days from March 2026 [7]. The rationale for this aspiration is clear; in a competitive environment, sponsors want to test and bring their innovations to market quickly and ahead of their rivals. Time, quite literally, is money. Sites that can set up studies quickly are more likely to have effective processes and oversight and maximise the sponsor’s chances of recruiting the number of participants needed to test the efficacy of their product. Following this logic, we analysed a large dataset of commercial firsts collected over a 15-year period by the National Institute for Health and Care Research (NIHR), the major funder of health and social care research in the UK. Our aims were twofold:

(i)To determine whether timely setup and in particular, recruitment of first participants was predictive of success, as measured by recruitment to time and target (RTT). As studies and organisations typically recruit participants across multiple sites, the primary analysis aimed to determine whether the time taken to recruit the first participant to a site (site level performance) was predictive of RTT.(ii)As there can only ever be one ‘first’ to a study, a second, smaller analysis using the subset of data available for commercial ‘first’ recruits (the first Global, European or UK participant recruited) investigated whether study (rather than site) ‘firsts’ were predictive of a success, again measured using recruitment to time and target.

Methods

Data on Recruitment to Time and Target (RTT) performance

To determine whether commercial firsts were predictive of sites recruiting to time and target (RTT), a comprehensive list of commercial site performance as recorded in the RD Performance Monitoring app in the NIHR’s Open Data Platform (ODP) was extracted. This covered recruitment data and target by site for every commercial study on the NIHR Portfolio, allowing every site to be categorised as either passing or failing to hit its recruitment target (or removed from analysis in the event of missing targets and/or ineligibility for RTT metrics).

Data on commercial firsts

The NIHR Coordinating Centre (CC) holds a central record of Commercial First recruits, which sites have notified them of. This record includes “Global” (the first participant recruited into a globally running study, e.g. a site in England has recruited the first participant in a study which is running in the UK and at least one other country), “European” (the first participant to be recruited into a European site in a global study, e.g. a study may have recruited a participant in the USA, but an English site recruited the first participant in participating European countries) and “UK Firsts” (the first participant to be recruited into a multi-site study open only in the UK).

This list of 440 “Firsts” is not always identified in a consistent way, or with established Trust/Site Codes as shown in the RTT data. To put these data into a usable format for matching with other datasets, the list was updated with manual checks on each study in turn to identify the correct site, its associated NHS Trust, and to code both site and Trust to allow for cross-referencing. Of these 440 firsts, 5 referred to studies not currently visible in ODP (most likely due to withdrawal of the study), which were excluded; 1 had the wrong study ID, but could easily be identified under its correct ID; 4 showed no recruitment against the site identified as achieving the first, or no recruitment against the study at all. These firsts were provisionally retained in the dataset, pending cross-reference with RTT data.

Data on first recruit by site

Due to system functionality and for reasons of commercial confidentiality, NIHR systems do not record the exact date of recruitment to commercial studies. However, it is possible to determine the month of a given recruit and therefore the month in which each site first recruited. This calculation generated 32,078 rows of data, covering all recruiting sites for all commercial studies.

Data matching

Using data on closed studies downloaded from ODP, it was possible to match 333 Firsts to closed sites, and thus to identify all 333 studies as having an identifiable First recorded. This could be used to control for variability in studies, and in particular, to create a subset of sites for which we could be certain whether the site had achieved a First or not given the sometimes unreliable historic recording of these achievements. For studies without a site identified as achieving a First, we are limited in what we can say, even though it is expected that one of those sites achieved a First in UK. However, with matching to months of first recruitment by site, these sites can offer a richer understanding of the effect of prompt recruitment that goes beyond a narrow binary distinction of “Firsts” and “Everyone else”.

Statistical methods

All statistical analyses were performed in R version 4.2.1. The R code used is provided in Supplementary File 1.

All analyses were a prior based on the putative relationship between time taken to set up a study and study success. To determine whether the time between a study opening and the site’s first recruit was associated with probability of RTT, we used a logistic regression. To assess whether a site being classified as a First was associated with RTT, we used logistic regression with an indicator variable denoting First or not as the independent variable and RTT as the dependent variable. As a way of allowing for data from sites within the same trial to be correlated, we also fitted a generalised logistic regression with site ID as a random effect in addition to the First indicator variable. This analysis used all data that passed the data cleaning process described above.

For the more restricted set of data with site opening times, we also used a logistic regression and generalised logistic regression.

In both cases, the estimated odds ratio, 95% confidence interval and p-value for testing the null hypothesis of no association is provided. All p-values are two-sided. No sample size calculation was performed, instead all available data was used.

Results

Table 1 shows the results of the planned analyses. There was a highly significant effect of the time between opening (both site and study) and first recruit on the probability of RTT (p < 0.0001 for all analyses, odds ratio per 1-month increase ranged between 0.84 to 0.94 with extremely tight confidence intervals). This indicates that the longer it takes for a site to get its first recruit, the lower the chance of it recruiting to time and target (see Table 1).

The most successful trust accounted for 18 firsts, and all but 5 trusts had fewer than 10 firsts. Of these 93 trusts, the median number of firsts was 2, and the median number of commercial studies they participated in was 118.

Discussion

The results from this analysis show clear associations. Sites that achieve commercial firsts are more likely to recruit their agreed sample size within the study’s timeframe - more commonly known as recruitment to time and target (RTT) - than sites that are slow to recruit their first participant. Conversely, the longer it takes for a site to achieve its first recruit, the lower the chance of it recruiting to time and target, dropping to less than 50% if it takes more than 8 months for a site to recruit its first participant. In the analysis of ‘firsts’ (study level), although the probability of recruiting to time and target was highest for sites that recruited global firsts, the effect also held true for European and UK firsts. Put simply, a site that recruits firsts of any description is more likely to successfully meet sponsors’ expectations.

These results are not wholly surprising. Achieving a commercial first, particularly a global first, is a prestigious accolade that is favourably viewed by sponsors and funders alike. Sites and organisations that achieve firsts are typically well run, with well-established processes, strong R&D oversight, and firm ‘buy in’ from investigators and study teams. They are also likely to already carry out many of the recommendations highlighted in the O’Shaughnessy report. On the other hand, sites that fail to recruit participants in a timely manner are less likely to have good R&D oversight or efficient processes, and more likely to have unrealistic targets and/or over-stretched teams and competing studies. As recruitment to commercial trials is competitive, these sites are also more susceptible to studies closing early if recruitment at other sites has progressed particularly quickly, compounding the issue further. Allied to this more generally is the issue of studies being withdrawn from countries where recruitment is considered commercially unviable, resulting in wasted - and unenumerated - time and resources.

While much of the attention on rejuvenating commercial trials has focused on lost revenue, it should be noted that commercial trials do much more than simply create income. Although in 2022/23 alone commercial trials helped generate £1.2 billion for the NHS, not to mention treatment saving costs to Trusts that were not included in this calculation [2], these trials also provided access to cutting-edge treatments for over 42,000 patients. Thus, while there are clear economic benefits and drivers associated with running commercial studies, benefits for patients and hospital trusts are also clear. Patients treated in research active hospitals typically have better outcomes and lower mortality [8] even after adjusting for other factors, including staffing [9]. The extent of patient benefit from participating in commercial trials relative to non-commercial trials is unclear, with some evidence to suggest more favourable efficacy results and conclusions for participating in commercial trials, but with bias that cannot be explained by standard bias assessments alone [10]. As with non-commercial trials, clinical benefit is very much dependent on trial design and outcome measures used [11].

Although the current study has its strengths, including a large dataset, taken from a NIHR database containing nearly five and a half thousand unique commercial study firsts over a 15-year period, it is not without its weaknesses. Most notably, the study’s findings are only applicable to England, particularly given the prominent role of the NIHR in UK research and its unique relationship with the NHS making comparison with other countries problematic. For example, an analysis of 22,511 trials over an 8-year period spanning 2006–2014 showed differences in the type of trials conducted between high and lower-income countries. Trials in lower income countries (27% of the sample) were more likely to be later phase (Phase 3 or Phase 4) and of longer duration, with higher numbers of participants than higher income countries [12]. Furthermore, variations in approval timelines and differences in the degree of oversight between coutries [13] further hamper generalisation. The data also required various cleaning and processing stages, each of which made assumptions and reduced the sample size. Nonetheless, given the robustness of the findings the data appear to be reliable and the central finding of first recruits being predictive of subsequent recruitment to time and target is likely to still be applicable to other countries despite these differences Similar analyses across a range of countries would however be needed to give a more definitive answer.

From a strategic perspective, the finding of a diminishing probability of achieving recruitment to time and target the longer the interval between a site/study opening and its first recruit may have practical implications for research organisations in their decision making. Given finite resources, it may benefit Trusts and research teams to prioritise their resources in favour of delivering trials that are more likely to be successful as recruitment is on, or nearly on track as this is likely to generate greater income as well as maximising funding allocations where models include performance-based elements such as RTT. Such an approach would not however be suitable across the board, as certain conditions, particularly rare diseases are, by default, difficult to recruit to.

Although the time taken to set up a study is an important predictor of its subsequent success, it should be noted that this it is not the only, or indeed necessarily the most import factor. Disease prevalence, trial design, study eligibility criteria and a range of other factors may also influence a trial’s probability of success [14] and were not assessed in the current analysis. Given the relatively small number of ‘firsts’ and the range of commercial studies in the dataset, any such analysis would further reduce power. One final factor to consider is whether the characteristics of the sites that recruit quickly differ to sites that are slower to recruit. Most sites (around 80%) were more successful in recruiting to time and target when they achieved a study ‘first’ (UK, European or Global), despite the number of studies achieving a first being a small subset of their total commercial activity. Given the breakdown of the different types of sites, a formal analysis would be underpowered, but there do not appear to be any glaring differences. Nonetheless, this would be an interesting avenue for further exploration.

Conclusions

Recruiting participants quickly to a site, and recruiting ‘first’ participants to commercial clinical trials is predictive of successful trial delivery. Given the onus on reinvigorating commercial trial performance within the United Kingdom, and the push to reduce study set up times outlined in the government’s 10-Year Health Plan for England [15] and the Life Sciences Sector Plan [16], this finding is particularly pertinent and highlights the importance of rapid site set up and recruitment.
