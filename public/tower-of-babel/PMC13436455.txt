Volatile Organic Compounds Emitted by Fungi Have Substantial Effects on Growth and Disease Resistance in Arabidopsis thaliana
Authors: Sara Yugueros, Asier Largo‐Gosens, Cristina Úbeda, Cristina Hernández‐Fernández, Alba Manga‐Robles, Diego Rebaque, Antonio Encina, Hugo Mélida
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTVolatile organic compounds (VOCs) emitted by certain fungi have previously been suggested to modulate plant development and their response to stresses. While some fungal species, such as Trichoderma harzianum, are known to produce VOCs, the production of VOCs in other phylogenetically divergent species, such as Mucor mucedo, was previously unknown. This study aimed to analyse the VOCs profiles emitted by these fungal species and examine their effects on the growth and defensive responses of Arabidopsis thaliana. Volatilome analysis revealed that the most abundant VOCs for both species were alcohols, ketones and esters. However, notable differences were also observed, particularly with regard to terpenes. We then observed that prolonged exposure to VOCs emitted by both fungal species had detrimental effects on A. thaliana growth and photosynthetic performance, whereas shorter exposures enhanced the expression of defense‐related genes and the plant defense against the phytopathogenic bacterium Pseudomonas syringae. This increased resistance does not appear to be mediated by canonical H2O2‐induced immunity, but rather by subsequent responses that trigger complex metabolic reprogramming, including glucosinolate biosynthesis. Therefore, our results confirm that VOC‐mediated plant‐fungus interactions are very relevant to plant fitness, highlighting the need to understand them in greater depth.

Introduction

Plants are able to interact with other plants and organisms, which can influence their development and physiology in a beneficial or detrimental way (Cheng et al. 2019; Ramos‐Cruz et al. 2021). Negative effects may result from the interaction of plants with certain microbes, which in turn can affect the environmental health and agriculture, causing large crop losses (Balestrini. 2021). On the other hand, there are several beneficial microbial species, such as mycorrhizal fungi, that establish symbiotic interactions with plants to obtain sugars and other compounds while providing the plants with essential soil elements and improved nutrient and water uptake (Priyashantha et al. 2023). Research into these types of associations has tended to focus on the interactions mediated by non‐volatile secondary metabolites that are exchanged between different tissues, for example during biotrophic phases, or across the rhizosphere (Hahn and Mendgen. 2001; Shahi and Mafu. 2021; Wang et al. 2022). However, research into plant‐microbe interactions mediated by volatile organic compounds (VOCs) emitted by microorganisms, such as certain fungi, has been less extensive (Duc et al. 2022). VOCs are usually emitted as a mixture of low molecular weight compounds, which may include alcohols, aldehydes, terpenes, thiols and other types of molecules (Hung et al. 2015). Although these compounds could have a beneficial effect on plants (Fincheira et al. 2021; Naznin et al. 2014), VOCs can also exert harmful effects (Tyagi et al. 2018). Consequently, plants need to be able to sense VOCs and respond appropriately, especially to avoid their potential harmful effects (Bergman et al. 2026).

To cope with these and other potential threats, plants have evolved various defense mechanisms, with innate immunity being the first barrier responsible for sensing and responding to external stimuli (Nürnberger et al. 2004; Zehra et al. 2021). This line of defense can be activated by the perception of microbe‐associated or damage‐associated molecular patterns (MAMPs or DAMPs, respectively) by pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) located on the membrane of plant cells (Alhoraibi et al. 2019; Zhou and Zhang. 2020). As a result of this interaction, pattern‐triggered immunity (PTI) is activated and can lead to signalling pathways involving processes such as the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and changes in gene expression (Jones et al. 2024; Ngou et al. 2022). However, an excessive accumulation of ROS can induce oxidative stress in plants, which can be counteracted by various antioxidant systems (Colville and Smirnoff 2008). One of the final outcomes following PTI activation and corresponding gene reprogramming is the alteration of plant metabolite profiles, including phytohormones such as salicylic acid (SA) and jasmonic acid (JA) (Bari and Jones 2009; War et al. 2012).

VOCs serve several roles in plant defense (Hammerbacher et al. 2019). Various studies report that plant‐derived VOCs, such as green leaf volatile esters, monoterpenes, and methyl jasmonate, correlate with the enhanced expression of defense genes (e.g., PR1, TomloxF and TPS5) and have direct antimicrobial effects that, in dosage‐dependent experiments, inhibit pathogen structures like conidial germination (López‐Gresa et al. 2017). Likewise, bacterial and fungal VOCs (e.g., 2,3‐butanediol and acetoin from Bacillus spp. and m‐cresol, methyl benzoate and 1‐octen‐3‐ol from Ampelomyces sp. and Cladosporium sp.) have been shown to trigger induced systemic resistance in plants, reducing disease severity after pathogen challenge (Naznin et al. 2014; Razo‐Belmán et al. 2023; Sharifi and Ryu 2016). Additionally, previous studies have shown that some symbiotic Trichoderma species are able to improve the defensive response of plants against aphids and other fungi by emitting certain VOCs and enhancing the production of defensive metabolites (Coppola et al. 2017; Malmierca et al. 2012; Zhou et al. 2018). It should be noted that some VOCs, such as trichodiene emitted by Trichoderma spp. and Fusarium spp., are classified as mycotoxins, a broad group of molecules that can be toxic to plants and other organisms (Bennett and Inamdar 2015; Lee et al. 2014; Malmierca, McCormick, Cardoza, Alexander, et al. 2015; Splivallo et al. 2007). In summary, reviews and meta‐analyses consistently describe that VOCs signals lead to primed pathogenesis‐related gene expression and enhanced resistance, thereby establishing a link between VOC emissions and diverse plant defense mechanisms in multiple plant‐pathogen systems (Hammerbacher et al. 2019; Quintana‐Rodriguez et al. 2014).

One application that has emerged from studying plant‐microbe interactions is the potential use of certain molecules, such as the VOCs emitted by fungi, as pretreatments to induce an immune response in plants. This allows them to respond more quickly and effectively to a second attack, a process known as defense priming. However, it has been more extensively studied in the context of non‐volatile molecules, such as β‐amino‐butyric acid (BABA) (Cooper and Ton 2022; Ton and Mauch‐Mani 2004). Even so, as mentioned above, there are already examples of the possible use of volatile molecules for this purpose (Naznin et al. 2014; Baazeem et al. 2021). Postharvest use of VOCs could also be useful against diseases in stored products (Kaddes et al. 2019). For example, the VOC N‐methyl‐N‐nitrosoisobutyramide (MNIBA) produced by the endophytic fungus Muscodor albus has been shown to have antimicrobial activity through a DNA methylation mechanism (Hutchings et al. 2017). Therefore, treatments with natural VOCs could reduce the use of current chemical products for crop protection that are harmful and pollute the environment. However, the technical complexity inherent in the study of volatile compounds makes this a field in which there is a great lack of knowledge in many aspects. In this regard, the recent development of a simpler and more effective coculture system, with a physical separation between the two organisms, provides a great opportunity to shed light on many aspects of VOC‐mediated plant‐fungal interactions (Álvarez‐García et al. 2022).

Certain ascomycete species, such as Trichoderma spp. and Fusarium spp., are known to emit VOCs that can have effects on plants, which may affect their growth, defensive responses or metabolomic profile (Coppola et al. 2017; Garnica‐Vergara et al. 2016; Malmierca et al. 2012; Pennerman et al. 2022). For example, Trichoderma harzianum is a fungus that is widely used as a biological control agent against phytopathogenic organisms and which is also known to produce a complex spectrum of VOCs (Vinale et al. 2008). The well‐documented effects experienced by the model plant species Arabidopsis thaliana when its roots are colonized by T. harzianum include enhanced growth and productivity, increased tolerance to abiotic stresses, and activation of plant defense pathways, notably those involving SA and JA signalling (Morán‐Diez et al. 2012; Poveda et al. 2019). Some of these effects are potentially mediated by VOCs. Based on these observations, our hypothesis was that fungal species other than ascomycetes are also capable of influencing plant behaviour through VOCs. However, the production of VOCs by phylogenetically distinct fungal species is much less studied. In this regard, Mucor mucedo is a common phytopathogenic zygomycete for which no VOCs production has been described, although the production of volatile hormones was suggested in a pioneer report (Mesland et al. 1974; Reyes 1990). On the other hand, there is documented interaction between A. thaliana and endophytic fungi from the Mucor genus (Rozpądek et al. 2018), and these associations can positively affect A. thaliana growth. Furthermore, it was also suggested that Mucor can modulate plant hormone metabolism and trigger immune responses in A. thaliana (Rozpądek et al. 2018). Therefore, a comparative analysis of T. harzianum and M. mucedo provides an opportunity to determine whether VOC‐mediated plant responses are lineage‐specific or reflect conserved metabolic reprogramming induced by rhizosphere‐dwelling fungi.

Given the increasing need to better understand the roles of fungal VOCs on plants, in this work we have performed cocultures (without physical contact) of T. harzianum and M. mucedo, soil inhabitants that can act as plant endophytes, with the model plant A. thaliana and studied several processes in the plant counterpart. These included growth, defense and metabolic reprogramming. The results provided here show that the VOCs produced by both fungal species had strong effects on the mentioned processes and were partially fungus specific. It is worth highlighting the improvement in the defense capacity of the plants against the phytopathogenic bacterium Pseudomonas syringae.

Material and Methods

Biological Materials

A. thaliana (Columbia‐0 ecotype) was used to analyse plant responses to fungal VOCs. Plants were grown in glass jars (946 ml capacity; PhytoTech Labs) in autoclaved substrate prepared with soil and vermiculite (3:1 proportion; Projar). For germination and growth experiments, 12 seeds were sown uniformly in each jar and were cocultured with fungi. For the remaining experiments, seeds were sown in three different positions on the substrate surface and maintained for 3 weeks in a growth chamber under controlled conditions (12 h of light and 12 of darkness, at 23°C and 21°C, respectively) before being cocultured with fungi. In each experiment, the dormancy of the seeds was broken immediately after sowing by means of cold stratification (4°C for 72 h in the dark).

The fungal isolates used in this study were T. harzianum (CECT 2413) and M. mucedo (CBS 109.16), obtained from the Spanish Type Culture Collection (CECT, Spain) and the Fungal Biodiversity Centre (CBS, The Netherlands), respectively. Both fungal species were grown from spores on potato dextrose agar (PDA; Difco) medium at 25°C in the dark. After several subculturing steps, the fungi were grown for 4 days before being cocultured with plants. Dishes containing only PDA medium were used as control condition. For VOCs extraction, the fungi were grown from spores on headspace vials with PDA for at least 1 week. Two days before the analyses, the vials were sealed with parafilm.

Pseudomonas syringae pv. tomato (DC3000) was grown from glycerol stocks in King's Basal medium (KB; 2% (w/v) protease peptone, 0.15% (w/v) MgSO4, 0.15% (w/v) KH2PO4, and 1.5% (v/v) glycerol) with 25 µg/ml of rifampicin and incubated at 28°C for 3 days before infection assays.

Fungal VOCs Analysis

The extraction of volatile compounds was carried out by Solid Phase Microextraction (SPME). First, 20 ml glass headspace (HS) vials containing the sample were placed in the thermostatized autosampler tray at 20°C for HS‐SPME sampling. The volatile compounds were extracted from the headspace of the vials by exposing a 1 cm Carboxen/DVB/PDMS 50/30‐µm SPME fibre (Supelco) during 30 min at 35°C and desorbed using the splitless mode with an injector temperature of 250°C for 3 min. Gas chromatography coupled to mass spectrometry (GC‐MS) analyses were carried out as previously described (Rubio‐Sánchez et al. 2023). The samples were analysed in triplicate recording all data using MS ChemStation software (Agilent). For the identification of the volatile compounds, NIST compounds library was employed. It was carried out by comparing the mass spectra obtained from each molecule with the reference spectra. The identification was confirmed based on comparisons of the linear retention indexes (LRIs) of standards with data from the literature and PubChem database (Supporting Information S1: Table S1). LRIs were calculated using the retention times of a series of n‐alkanes analysed under the same conditions as the samples. The data shown in this work are absolute areas.

Plant‐Fungus Coculture

Non‐vented coculture chambers were used to analyse the plant–fungus interaction mediated by VOCs (Álvarez‐García et al. 2022). This system consists of a glass jar in which the plant grows, a perforated central piece that allows the interaction via volatiles and a Petri dish in which the fungus grows (Supporting Information S1: Figure S1A). The coculture chambers were assembled under sterile conditions as indicated hereafter. The central piece was placed on the glass jar and the Petri dish containing the fungus was placed on top, inverted (Supporting Information S1: Figure S1B,C). To avoid cross‐contamination with fungal spores, a glass fibre filter was placed on top of the central piece. Coculture chambers were maintained in a plant growth chamber under the above conditions. For all experiments, except those described in Section 2.4, 3‐week‐old A. thaliana plants were cocultured with fully grown fungal plates for 48 h. After this time, the coculture chambers were dismantled, and the culture jars were left uncovered so that the corresponding experiments could be carried out.

Growth Parameters Determination

A. thaliana seeds were cocultured with fungi for 14 days. Germination percentages were measured every 2 days during the coculture by counting the germinated seedlings compared to the initially sown seeds (12 per jar). On Day 14, seedlings were carefully extracted from the jars and maintained at 40°C for 24 h to determine their dry weight. The experiment was repeated four times, with four chambers (48 plants) per condition (control, T. harzianum and M. mucedo) in each one.

Photosynthetic Efficiency Analysis

Photosynthetic parameters were measured in 14‐day seedlings after prolonged exposure to fungal VOCs using a fluorometer. Seedlings were acclimated to the dark for 30 min and the maximum quantum yield of photosystem II (PSII) was then measured. The effective quantum yield PSII was determined 30 s after one pulse of actinic light. Then, seedlings were acclimated to the dark for 30 min and the maximum quantum yield of photosystem II (PSII) was measured.

Gene Expression Analyses

The expression levels of the following genes were analysed by qRT‐PCR: FRK1 (At2g19190), PHI1 (At1g35140), WRKY33 (At2g38470), WRKY53 (At4g23810), CYP81F2 (At5g57220) and PAD3 (At3g26830), as early immunity markers and PR1 (At2g14610) and PDF1.2 (At5g44420), which are related to late immunity. Specific primers for each gene are listed in Supporting Information S1: Table S2. After 48 h of coculture, the chambers were dismantled, the A. thaliana rosettes were snap frozen and ground, and the tissues obtained were used for RNA extraction according to the protocol of the RNeasy Plant Mini Kit (QIAGEN). Retrotranscription was performed using the High‐Capacity RNA‐to‐cDNA kit (ThermoFisher). Finally, qPCR was performed by using the PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix (ThermoFisher) in a Step One Plus real‐time PCR system (Applied Biosystems). UBQ21 (At5g25760) and SAND (At3g28390) were used as housekeeping genes (Czechowski et al. 2005; Škiljaica et al. 2022). UBQ21‐normalized results were presented in the main text, while SAND‐normalized data were included in the supporting material. The experiments were repeated three times, with two chambers (six plants) per condition (control, T. harzianum and M. mucedo) in each one.

H2O2 Content and Production Measurements

H2O2 content was evaluated using the method xylenol‐orange described by Cheeseman 2006 both in seedlings cocultured for 14 days and 3‐week old plants cocultured for 48 h with fungi. After coculture, the chambers were dismantled, A. thaliana seedlings and plants were snap frozen and ground, and the tissues obtained were used for an antioxidant activities extraction using a buffer containing 0.1 M K‐phosphate and 5 mM KCN. The extracts were mixed with reaction buffer containing 100 µM xylenol orange, 100 µM d‐sorbitol, 250 µM Fe(II)SO4, 250 µM (NH4)2SO4 and 1% ethanol in 25 mM H2SO4 and incubated at room temperature for 40 min with shaking. Absorbance was measured at a wavelength of 550 nm, and H2O2 concentration was calculated using a standard curve. The H2O2 production after elicitation of 3‐week old A. thaliana plants as an immunity hallmark was determined after 48 h of coculture. For this purpose, at the end of the coculture, two to four discs per leaf were collected with a cork borer, transferred to a 96‐well plate, and incubated overnight in sterile tap water. H2O2 production in response to the peptide flagellin 22 (flg22) was determined by the luminol‐peroxidase assay using a microplate reader (Fuertes‐Rabanal et al. 2024). The experiments were repeated three times, with 4 chambers (12 plants) per condition (control, T. harzianum and M. mucedo) in each one.

Analysis of Phytohormones

The levels of the auxin indol‐3‐acetic acid (IAA), abscisic acid (ABA), the cytokinins (CKs) trans‐zeatin (tZ) and N6‐isopentenyladenine (iP) and the gibberellins 1 (GA1) and 4 (GA4) were analysed as follows. After 48 h of coculture, the chambers were dismantled, and the A. thaliana rosettes were snap frozen and ground. Powdered tissue (100 mg dry weight) was extracted in 80% methanol containing 1% acetic acid and internal standards by shaking for 1 h at 4°C. Extracts were stored overnight at −20°C, centrifuged, and the resulting supernatants were dried in a vacuum evaporator. The dry residues were dissolved in 1% acetic acid and sequentially purified using an Oasis HLB (30 mg; Waters) reverse‐phase column followed by an Oasis MCX cation‐exchange column. Basic fractions were eluted with 60% methanol containing 5% NH4OH, and acidic fractions with methanol. Samples were further purified on an Oasis WAX anion‐exchange column eluted with 80% methanol containing 1% acetic acid. Final residues were dried and reconstituted in 5% acetonitrile with 1% acetic acid. Phytohormones were separated by UHPLC (Vanquish system; Thermo Fisher Scientific) using an Accucore C18 column (2.6 µm, 100 mm; Thermo Fisher Scientific) with an acetonitrile gradient containing 0.05% acetic acid. For IAA, ABA and GAs, the gradient ranged from 2% to 55% acetonitrile at 400 µl min−1 over 18 min. For CKs, the gradient was 2% to 25% acetonitrile over 9.5 min. Hormone detection was performed using a Q‐Exactive mass spectrometer (Orbitrap detector; Thermo Fisher Scientific) operated in targeted Selected Ion Monitoring (tSIM; capillary temperature 300°C, S‐lens RF level 70, resolution 70 000). Electrospray ionization was applied in negative mode for acidic hormones and positive mode for CKs (spray voltage 3 kV, heater temperature 150°C, sheath gas flow 40 µl min−1, auxiliary gas flow 10 µl min−1). Hormone concentrations were quantified using embedded calibration curves and processed with FreeStyle 1.8 SP2 QF1 and TraceFinder 5.2 EFS. Deuterium‐labelled hormones were used as internal standards for all phytohormones. The experiment was performed with 6 chambers (18 plants) per condition (control, T. harzianum and M. mucedo) in each one.

Plant Protection and Microbial Interaction Assays

P. syringae was grown before its inoculation as described above. To prepare the bacterial solution, the plates were washed with 2 ml of MgCl2 (10 mM), adjusted to an OD600 of 0.1 and serial dilutions were performed.

For coculture with fungi, three drops (10 µl) of each dilution were transferred to KB plates. Immediately afterwards, the coculture system was assembled and maintained for 24 h. Colony‐forming units (CFUs) of the optimal dilution were counted using ImageJ software with the same established parameters for each drop. The experiment was performed with three chambers per condition (control, T. harzianum and M. mucedo).

For plant inoculation assays, the surfactant Silwet L‐77 (0.001% v/v; PhytoTech Labs) was added to the bacterial solution. Three‐week‐old A. thaliana plants were cocultured with fungi for 48 h. Afterwards, the coculture chambers were disassembled and vented for at least 30 min before the plants were inoculated with 2 ml of the P. syringae solution. Leaf discs (0.5 mm diameter) were collected at 0 (3 h post‐inoculation) and 3 days post‐inoculation (dpi), washed with ethanol (70%) and MgCl2 (10 mM), ground and plated. After 24 h at 28°C, CFUs were counted. The experiment was repeated three times, with 4 chambers (12 plants) per condition (control, T. harzianum and M. mucedo) and using two discs from two different leaves of the same plant.

Non‐Targeted Metabolomics Assay

To analyse the metabolome of the A. thaliana cocultured plants, a methanol (80%) based extraction (10 mg of freeze‐dried tissue/ml) was performed on the frozen and ground plant rosettes that had been cocultured with fungi for 48 h. After the addition of methanol, samples were vortexed for 1 min, centrifuged at 3220g for 5 min and then the supernatants were carefully transferred to opaque chromatography vials without any filtering in between. Non‐targeted analysis was carried out using a UHPLC‐IM‐QTof metabolomics platform (timsTOF, Bruker), consisting of an ultra‐high‐performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) system and a quadrupole time‐of‐flight (QTof) mass spectrometer equipped with an electrospray ionization source (ESI) and an ion mobility trap (TIM). An Intensity Solo 2 INT20051 C18 column (100 × 2.1 mm, Bruker) was used. The chromatographic method consisted of a separation gradient starting with 95% of eluent A (milliQ water with 0.1% formic acid) and 5% of eluent B (methanol with 0.1% formic acid), with a constant flow of 0.25 ml/min, reaching 100% of eluent B after 10 min and returning to initial conditions 4 min later to reach the final time of 17 min. The MS/MS method employed an ESI source with a capillary voltage of 3000 V and was focused on the mass range between 50 and 1000 m/z. Samples were analysed using positive and negative ion detection modes. For quality control, methanol (80%) measures were used as blanks during the experiment.

MetaboScape software (2021b version) was used to acquire and process the metabolomics data. The parameters used for identification and bucketing were as follows: a T‐ReX 2D/3D workflow, with a criterion of 11 minimum features for extraction and a minimum intensity threshold of 1000 counts, with 6 spectra being the minimum peak length for feature detection. Feature signal was quantified as intensity. To visualize the distribution of samples and analytes a principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using the Pareto scaling algorithm and with the 10 percent cross validation mode. Metabolites were identified by matching data against the ‘MassBank Europe Mass Spectral DataBase’ and the ‘Bruker NIST 2020 Mass Spectral Library’. Duplicated annotations were evaluated according to the MS/MS score, and only the metabolites with the highest scores were retained, with a minimum score threshold set at 800. Metabolites were extracted from tissues arising from two independent experiments, each with 4 chambers (12 plants) per condition.

Finally, a KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) enrichment analysis was performed using MetaboScape software. Metabolic pathways specific to A. thaliana were initially retrieved from the WikiPathways database. The pathway mapping tool within the software was used to analyse metabolite abundance across pathways, comparing control plants to each treatment condition independently. Log2 fold change values were calculated for each metabolite, with minimum and maximum values set at –1 and 1, respectively.

Results

T. harzianum and M. mucedo Have Different Profiles of Volatile Compounds

Before studying the possible effects of fungal VOCs on A. thaliana, we decided to analyse and compare those emitted by T. harzianum and M. mucedo. For this purpose, they were extracted by Solid Phase Microextraction and analysed by GC‐MS (Figure 1 and Table 1). Both species produced complex volatilomes. The analysis of fungal VOCs showed a more complex profile for T. harzianum, with 31 VOCs quantified compared to 20 in the case of M. mucedo (Table 1). In particular, alcohols were the most abundant VOCs produced by T. harzianum and M. mucedo, followed by ketones and esters (Figure 1). Regardless of ethanol, the most abundant VOC under all conditions, 3‐methyl‐1‐butanol and isobutanol (alcohols), acetoin (ketone) and ethyl acetate (ester) were produced in great abundance by both fungal species. The higher complexity observed in the VOCs profile of T. harzianum was mainly due to terpenes, as up to 11 different ones could be identified, while in M. mucedo, only a low amount of carbitol was identified. Among these, trans‐α‐bergamotene and β‐cedrene were the most abundant. Other VOCs, such as isoamyl acetate, 3‐octanol and 1‐octen‐3‐ol, were much more abundant in T. harzianum were than in M. mucedo. On the other hand, three VOCs were found in M. mucedo but not in T. harzianum. These were the esters ethyl 2‐methylbutyrate, ethyl isovalerate and ethyl benzoate, indicating that, although the total amount of esters did not differ between fungi, their diversity was higher in M. mucedo.

Since the culture medium itself may emit certain volatiles (Klemenz et al. 2019), these were analysed in vials containing only PDA. Of the 34 VOCs identified between the two fungal isolates, six were already released by the culture medium itself (Table 1). Among these, three (ethyl acetate, 3‐methyl‐1‐butanol and phenylethyl alcohol) were detected at levels much lower than those quantified in the presence of the fungi. The other three compounds (ethyl benzoate, ethanol and benzaldehyde) were detected in similar or even higher amounts than in the conditions with fungi.

Long Exposure to Fungal VOCs Has a Trade‐Off Effect on the Growth of Arabidopsis thaliana

Once the volatile profiles of both fungal species were known, the next question was whether they had effects on A. thaliana. For this purpose, we designed a long‐term coculture experiment (14 days) to evaluate possible effects on germination and initial growth. Seeds cocultured with T. harzianum showed a significant delay in germination compared to control conditions (Figure 2A and Supporting Information S1: Figure S2). On the other hand, the germination of seeds cocultured with M. mucedo was similar to that of control plants. These seeds germinated 3 days after coculture, while seeds exposed to T. harzianum VOCs started germinating on the ninth day of coculture. At the end of coculture (14 days), most replicates reached 100% germination. However, seedlings exposed to both fungi suffered a significant reduction in their growth measured as dry biomass, of about two‐thirds compared to the control seedlings (Figure 2B). The case of M. mucedo was particularly striking, as no delay in germination was observed, which would be the simplest explanation for lower biomass accumulation. In order to shed some light on the possible reasons for this effect, we measured the photosynthetic efficiency of seedlings exposed to fungal VOCs for 14 days (Figure 2C,D). The analyses showed that the maximum and effective quantum yield of photosystem II were significantly reduced in seedlings cocultured with both fungi.

Observing the germination and photosynthetic efficiency results, and taking into account the low amount of plant biomass produced in the presence of fungal volatiles, we considered using an experimental design in which 3‐week‐old plants were exposed to VOCs for a short period of time (48 h). In addition to solving the problem of low biomass recovery from long cocultures (necessary for subsequent experiments), this system would avoid possible phytotoxicity effects that are likely to occur in germinating seedlings overexposed to alcohols, ketones, and other VOCs. Considering this possible phytotoxic effect as the simplest explanation for the observed trade‐off, we measured the total amount of H2O2 in seedlings and plants subjected to both types of coculture (14 vs. 2 days) (Figure 2E,F). These results suggested that prolonged exposure of seedlings to volatiles caused oxidative stress reflected in a significant increase in total H2O2, while short‐term coculture did not show this effect. Therefore, the remaining coculture experiments presented in this study involved 48‐h exposures on 3‐week‐old plants. The coculture chambers were then dismantled, and follow‐up experiments were carried out.

In order to extend the understanding of the growth deficiency observed effect an analysis of main growth‐related phytohormones was conducted. The phytohormones examined were the auxin indole‐3‐acetic acid (IAA), abscisic acid (ABA), the cytokinins trans‐zeatin (tZ) and N6‐isopentenyladenine (iP), and the gibberellins 1 (GA1) and 4 (GA4) (Figure 3). The results showed that there were significant differences between control and VOCs‐exposed plants for cytokinins and gibberellins. tZ, the main cytokinin, was detected at significantly higher levels, while iP was detected in lower amounts, although only significant after cocultivation with T. harzianum (Figure 3A,B). With regard to gibberellins, GA1, which could not be detected under control conditions, accumulated significantly after cocultivation with both species, while GA4 was found over accumulated only after cocultivation with M. mucedo (Figure 4C,D). Finally, we found no significant differences for IAA and ABA in any case (Figure 3E,F).

Short‐Term Exposure to Fungal VOCs Enhanced the Defense of Arabidopsis thaliana against Pseudomonas syringae

Given the significant effect of fungal VOCs exposure on early‐stage A. thaliana growth, and considering the trade‐off phenomenon on growth when plant defense mechanisms are induced (Herms and Mattson 1992), we wondered whether impaired plant development following to exposure to VOCs might be associated with an increased ability to cope with pathogens.

In line with this hypothesis, we previously investigated the potential antimicrobial properties of VOCs produced by T. harzianum and M. mucedo. We performed cocultures between these species and P. syringae, which demonstrated the ability of the VOCs produced by both species to inhibit bacterial growth (Supporting Information S1: Figure S3). Considering that our approach aimed to determine the possible priming effect, and to avoid the direct antimicrobial effect of the fungal VOCs, the chambers were ventilated after 48 h of coculture just before P. syringae was inoculated to A. thaliana plants. As expected, the entry of bacteria into the plants, assessed 3 h after inoculation (0 dpi), was similar in all three conditions (Figure 4). However, at 3 dpi, plants cocultured with both fungi showed a reduction in bacterial growth compared to controls in terms of CFUs, even when the observed phenotype was not directly correlated with these results (Supporting Information S1: Figure S4), as previously reported (Kover and Schaal. 2002; Miller et al. 2020). Specifically, the values were reduced from 5.21 Log CFU/cm2 in the control to 4.25 and 4.87 in the plants exposed to T. harzianum and M. mucedo VOCs, respectively (Figure 4).

Exposure to Fungal VOCs Impaired Post‐Elicitation H2O2 Production and Misregulated Several Defense‐related Genes in A. thaliana Plants

To investigate whether the observed increased resistance of A. thaliana to P. syringae after exposure to fungal VOCs was associated with enhanced PTI responses, we first designed an H2O2 production experiment in A. thaliana plants that had previously been cocultured with the different fungi, in the same manner as in the previous protection assays. To induce the plant immune system, we used the 22‐amino acid bacterial‐derived peptide flg22, one of the most characterized and used MAMPs to trigger plant immunity in plant‐microorganism studies (Gómez‐Gómez and Boller. 2000; Tian et al. 2021). The production of H2O2 over time after elicitation with flg22 was higher in control plants than fungal cocultured plants (Figure 5A). To quantify the responses, the highest luminescence peaks were analysed, and a significant reduction in H2O2 production was observed in plants cocultured with both fungi, shifting from 412 × 103 RLUs in the control to 253 and 219 × 103 RLUs in the case of T. harzianum and M. mucedo cocultured plants, respectively (Figure 5B).

These results partially invalidated our hypothesis of increased PTI‐based resistance, at least that mediated by H2O2 production. Therefore, to better understand the basis of fungal VOC‐mediated resistance to P. syringae, we analysed the expression levels of several marker genes for defense processes. These were: FRK1, PHI1, WRKY33, WRKY53, CYP81F2 and PAD3 as early PTI markers (Asai et al. 2002; Boudsocq et al. 2010; Murray et al. 2007; Pfalz et al. 2009; Zheng et al. 2006; Zhou et al. 1999) and PR1 and PDF1.2 as late immunity markers (Manners et al. 1998; Rogers and Ausubel. 1997). qRT‐PCR assays showed changes in the expression of these genes in plants exposed to fungal VOCs. The expression of all genes, with the exception of PDF1.2, increased significantly in plants cocultured with T. harzianum (Figure 5C–J and Supporting Information S1: Figure S5), with a particularly marked increase in PAD3. On the other hand, M. mucedo VOCs also induced the overexpression of all the genes except PR1, whose expression remained unchanged (Figure 5C–J and Supporting Information S1: Figure S5). The overexpression of PDF1.2 was particularly striking, almost quadrupling that of the control. These results suggest that exposure to VOCs from T. harzianum and M. mucedo is capable of enhancing plant defense against P. syringae through a H2O2‐independent mechanism.

Exposure to Fungal VOCs Shifted the Metabolomic Profile of Exposed A. thaliana Plants

Finally, after observing the complex gene misregulation in A. thaliana plants exposed to fungal VOCs and trying to elucidate the mechanisms that mediate the observed enhanced defense response to P. syringae, we decided to use a metabolomics strategy. Again, we used the same experimental design as for the other experiments with adult plants, which were exposed to a coculture with the fungi at the end of their growth, just before harvest, to extract and analyse their metabolites. The non‐targeted metabolomics approach allowed us to detect 1306 metabolites in control plants and 1321 in the case of plants cocultured with T. harzianum and M. mucedo (Table S3). Next, in order to determine the degree of similarity between the different metabolomic profiles and to visualize it in a simple way, we decided to perform a PCA. This analysis revealed a sample distribution where PDA controls and fungi‐cocultured plants were clearly separated into groups (Figure 5A). This clustering confirmed that plants cocultured with fungi had a different metabolomic profile than control plants, and that this profile differed between plants exposed to VOCs from both fungal species. This segregation could be explained by analyzing the metabolites with the highest variance, which are the ones that are farthest away from the cloud point (Figure 5B). The identity of 66 differentially accumulated metabolites could be assigned by comparison of the mass spectra with the ‘MassBank Europe Mass Spectral DataBase’ and the ‘Bruker NIST 2020 Mass Spectral Library’ (Table 2). The compounds were classified into different groups according to the data available in PubChem (National Center for Biotechnology Information) and MassBank (High Quality Mass Spectral DataBase): amino acids (8), carboxylic acids (19), fatty alcohols (2), flavonoids (6), glucosinolates (11), glycerophospholipids (2), glycosides (9), phenols (2), vitamins (2), benzaldehydes (1), benzenesulfonates (1), nitrogen compounds (1), organic sodium salts (1) and organosulfurs (1). It is worth highlighting that 15 metabolites, which could not be identified, were detected only in fungi‐cocultured samples, while the peak intensity of these compounds was zero in all the replicates of the control plants (Table 3). KEGG enrichment analysis of identified metabolites indicated that the main altered pathways were the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle (general downregulation), the aromatic amino acid metabolism (upregulation of benzoic acid but not its product salicylic acid) and the glucosinolate biosynthesis pathway (upregulation) with a fold change of 1 in all cases (Supporting Information S1: Figures S6 and S7).

Discussion

The volatilome analysis carried out in this study showed that T. harzianum and M. mucedo emit a different range of VOCs and in different proportions. As reported in previous studies, terpenes were quite abundant in the profile of T. harzianum (Malmierca, McCormick, Cardoza, Alexander, et al. 2015), and we found that they were absent in M. mucedo cultures, except for carbitol. These compounds, which are also commonly emitted by plants (Arimura and Uemura 2024; Rosenkranz et al. 2021), are secondary metabolites that may have different functions. The most abundant sesquiterpene found in the volatilome of T. harzianum was trans‐α‐bergamotene, which along with trans‐β‐bergamotene, was identified as one of the precursors of fumagillin (Heddergott et al. 2014). Fumagillin, a mycotoxin produced by Aspergillus fumigatus, has been found to reduce plant development when applied to A. thaliana seeds (Ross et al. 2005). This may be consistent with the results obtained in the germination assays of this study, as germination delay was observed in plants exposed to T. harzianum, the only fungus found to produce the VOC trans‐α‐bergamotene.

Among the alcohols, 3‐methyl‐1‐butanol was the most abundant one in the volatilome of both fungi. Previous studies had found that this compound and others, such as 3‐methyl‐3‐butenol, 1‐hexanol, 3‐octanol or 1‐octen‐3‐ol, could be harmful to plants (Werner et al. 2016). Since all of these alcohols were identified in both fungal volatilomes, the reduction in plant growth, photosynthetic capacity and the H2O2 accumulation observed in this study could be explained by the accumulation of this type of compounds in the coculture chambers. The reduction in growth observed in plants cocultured with T. harzianum may also be due to the fact that exposure to VOCs caused a significant delay in germination, resulting in a lower dry weight at the time of measurement. These results are also consistent with previous studies that found that trichothecenes, terpenes produced by Trichoderma among others, could have phytotoxic effects on plants (Malmierca, McCormick, Cardoza, Monte, et al. 2015; Tijerino et al. 2011). Indeed, it would be interesting to consider the delay in germination and reduced growth in the study of biological compounds with herbicidal activity as control agents (Radhakrishnan et al. 2018) or in studies of the phytotoxic effects of VOCs when used as antimicrobial agents (Kaddes et al. 2019).

Therefore, in the case of exposure to VOCs for 14 days, the observed phenotype may reflect the phytotoxic effects of accumulated alcohols (Sánchez‐Ortiz et al. 2016; Roque‐Flores et al. 2025), in conjunction with H2O2 accumulation and reduction in photosynthetic efficiency. Due to the phytotoxic effects observed in long‐term cocultures, the following experiments in this study were conducted using short‐term cocultures. Thus, given the important role of phytohormones in regulating growth processes, the initial experiments using short‐term cocultures focused on the analysis of these molecules. It should be noted that this was not feasible in seedlings subjected to long‐term co‐cultures. This means that although the long‐term co‐culture caused a decrease in growth, this does not necessarily reflect the situation following a 48‐h exposure on 3‐week‐old plants. Indeed, the measured levels of GA1, GA4 and tZ demonstrated overaccumulation of these hormones after coculture with both fungi, especially in the case of M. mucedo. Taken together, the observed increase in tZ and GAs suggests that fungal VOCs initially stimulate growth‐related pathways; therefore, it would be interesting to study this effect long‐term following exposure to fungal VOCs. In line with this hypothesis, several studies on plant‐microorganism interactions have shown that fungal VOCs can promote plant growth, which could be explained by the use of different experimental designs or the concentration of plant‐available volatiles (Álvarez‐García et al. 2022; Fincheira and Quiroz 2018; Vinale and Sivasithamparam 2020). Taking these observations into account, it would be interesting to conduct dose‐response experiments to determine the effects of fungal VOCs on the promotion or inhibition of plant growth observed in different studies.

As for the relationship between GA accumulation and pathogen resistance, antagonistic relationships are commonly described (Navarro et al. 2008). That is, high levels of GAs are typically linked to growth rather than defense. In fact, GAs are often associated with the degradation of the DELLA proteins and lower activation of defense and pathogen resistance genes (Navarro et al. 2008). However, our data show that this is not always the case, as we have demonstrated greater activation of defense and P. syringae resistance genes. Interestingly, it has also been shown that elevated levels of tZ, which we found increased after cocultivation with both fungi, mediate an improved response against the aforementioned pathogen (Choi et al. 2010). Interestingly, we were able to demonstrate that plants cocultured with both fungi were better protected against P. syringae than control plants. Although there was a clear reduction in the number of CFUs, no phenotypic differences were observed between control and cocultured plants. This result is consistent with previous articles reporting that phenotype is not always correlated with bacterial abundance (Kover and Schaal 2002; Miller et al. 2020), which provides a robust quantitative measure of bacterial proliferation and, consequently, of plant resistance. The volatilome of the two fungal species may also explain, at least in part, the observed effects on plants cocultured with each fungus. For example, the volatile ketone acetoin and acetoin‐derived compounds have been previously reported to increase the protection of A. thaliana against bacterial and fungal infections (D'alessandro et al. 2014; Rudrappa et al. 2010; Yang et al. 2023). This effect is in line with the protection observed in fungi‐cocultured plants, as this compound was highly produced by both fungi according to the volatilome analysis. Nevertheless, because plants in our system were exposed to the complex blend of the fungal volatilome rather than isolated compounds, the specific contribution of acetoin remains speculative. The potential synergistic or additive effects of other co‐emitted volatiles cannot be ruled out, and the isolated role of acetoin must be rigorously explored in future research using pure compound assays. In view of this interesting result, it was tempting to speculate that volatiles might exert a priming effect, such that plants exposed to these VOCs might have an enhanced PTI response. Unfortunately, our strategy observed the opposite effect, ruling out a canonical PTI induction and pointing to a more complex explanation. Although in this study the H2O2 production was not increased but decreased, few studies have found an increase in H2O2 accumulation after plant elicitation with volatile compounds as a consequence of the induction of defense mechanisms (Contreras‐Cornejo et al. 2014; Wenke et al. 2012). Nevertheless, excessive accumulation of ROS can lead to the activation of plant antioxidant systems (Mastouri et al. 2012; Sidorova et al. 2022), thereby counteracting the production of these harmful compounds. In addition, it has been described that VOCs, as monoterpenes, have antioxidant activity, probably due to direct scavenging of ROS, and thus affect ROS accumulation after flg22 elicitation (Loreto. D'Auria 2022; Loreto and Velikova. 2001). These results suggest that VOC perception by plants may be different from the described PTI defense elicitors (Arimura and Uemura. 2024). Indeed, based on the description of VOC perception in vertebrates, it has been proposed that plants may possess protein transporters with the ability to bind VOCs and transport them to the unidentified VOC receptors, such as TOPLESS and TOPLESS‐like proteins, which are able to bind β‐caryophyllene and volatile analogs and act as correpressors of developmental and stress responses (Loreto and D'Auria 2022; Nagashima et al. 2019). Interestingly, caryophyllene, as well as most other terpenes, are volatile molecules present only in T. harzianum cultures.

Regarding gene expression, the analysis showed that every immunity gene studied was upregulated in plants cocultured with at least one of the fungi studied. FRK1 and PHI1, which belong to the early immunity genes group, showed increased expression in plants exposed to fungal VOCs compared to control plants. This could indicate that the PTI response has been triggered, as both genes are usually activated after the detection of MAMPs. Although microbial VOCs have not been formally described as playing this role, it has been demonstrated that they are capable of inducing defense priming (Bitas et al. 2013; Liu and Brettell. 2019). WRKY33 and WRKY53 belong to a family of transcription factors that are induced after PTI activation, subsequent to treatment with elicitors or pathogen infections (Dong et al. 2003). Both genes are also involved in the regulation of processes such as the expression of other defense genes or senescence (Miao and Zentgraf. 2007; Zheng et al. 2006). While WRKY33 is implicated in the defensive response against necrotrophic pathogens as Botrytis cinerea (Zheng et al. 2006), WRKY53 is involved in the resistance of A. thaliana to P. syringae (Murray et al. 2007). In this study, the expression of both transcription factors was significantly increased in plants cocultured with both fungi, which is consistent with the results observed in the infection experiment.

With regard to the genes implicated in the production of defensive metabolites, the increased expression of CYP81F2 is in line with the results observed by other authors in tomato plants (Malmierca, McCormick, Cardoza, Alexander, et al. 2015; Malmierca, McCormick, Cardoza, Monte, et al. 2015), where trichodiene and other compounds involved in the ergosterol synthesis pathway increased the expression of certain defense genes. In addition, this gene is involved in the synthesis of glucosinolates (Clay et al. 2009; Hiruma et al. 2010), antifungal metabolites that can be produced when the innate immunity mechanisms of plants are triggered (Tierens et al. 2001; Bednarek et al. 2009). It is worth highlighting that KEGG enrichment analysis of identified metabolites pointed to the glucosinolate biosynthesis pathway as the most upregulated in A. thaliana plants cocultures with both fungi. We found that indolylmethyl glucosinolate, an inactive precursor form of these antifungal molecules, accumulated slightly more in control plants (though not significantly). However, this precursor must be hydroxylated at C‐4 of the indole ring by members of the CYP81F1‐3 family to produce active glucosinolates (Pfalz et al. 2016). In the same way, we quantified more inactive forms, such as (methylsulfinyl)pentyl glucosinolate and 4‐methylthiobutyl glucosinolate, in control plants than in those exposed to fungi (Chhajed et al. 2019; Lv et al. 2022; Nguyen et al. 2020). Therefore, and although none of the active glucosinolates were identified in our metabolomic study, these results suggest that fungal VOCs may promote the synthesis of active forms of glucosinolates in A. thaliana. However, further research is needed to demonstrate this hypothesis. Another gene related with the production of antimicrobial compounds is PAD3, encoding a cytochrome P450 enzyme involved in the production of camalexin, a major phytoalexin in Arabidopsis (Schuhegger et al. 2006). This gene was also strongly upregulated in plants exposed to fungal VOCs, especially evident in plants cocultured with T. harzianum, which could partially explain the differences observed in the protection assay against P. syringae.

On the other hand, although it is very interesting to note that the defense‐related genes discussed above were overexpressed after exposure to VOCs from both fungi, the expression of genes related to late‐stage immunity was strain‐specific. PR1 was significantly upregulated in plants exposed to T. harzianum VOCs, while PDF1.2 showed a high increase in the expression level only in plants exposed to M. mucedo. PR1 is an SA‐inducible gene which belong to the pathogenesis‐related (PR) gene family and control plant responses to abiotic and biotic stresses, such as the systemic acquired resistance (SAR), which confers resistance against broad‐spectrum pathogens (Sels et al. 2008; Van Loon et al. 2006). Frequently, the perception of biotrophic pathogens and other stresses lead to the accumulation of SA and the overexpression of PR1 gene (Yan and Dong. 2014; Stroud et al. 2022). Interestingly, plants exposed to T. harzianum VOCs showed an increase in SA, the overexpression of PR1 gene and a higher resistance against P. syringae, suggesting an activation of SAR response. In line with this result, other authors observed that exposing A. thaliana plants to isoprene (a terpene not produced by them) increased their resistance to P. syringae via SAR (Frank et al. 2021; Harvey and Sharkey 2016). Furthermore, one of the mechanisms by which the volatile acetoin has been shown to enhance plant protection against Pseudomonas is the increased expression of these defense genes (Rudrappa et al. 2010). On the other hand, there is an antagonist pathway of SA, regulated by JA that is typically activated after the infection of necrotrophic pathogens (Stroud et al. 2022). PDF1.2, which is involved in defense responses regulated by JA (Naznin et al. 2014), was previously reported to be induced after pathogen infections (Val‐Torregrosa et al. 2022), which is also in line with the results obtained in plants exposed to M. mucedo VOCs. Thus, the analysis of the selected genes indicates that, in general, there is a pattern of overall overexpression of plant immunity‐related genes. The regulation of defense‐related genes appears to be a tightly controlled process, in which some pathways, such as glucosinolate biosynthesis, seem to be of great importance.

Previous reports have shown that T. harzianum and other fungi are able to induce metabolic changes in plants that increase their resistance to various pathogens (Zhou et al. 2018). These metabolic changes could lead to the accumulation of antimicrobial compounds such as phytoalexins, as previously referenced. The production of this type of compounds could contribute to explain the effect of protection observed in plants cocultured with fungi after infection with P. syringae, as well as the increase in the expression of defense‐related genes. To better understand these responses and to determine whether VOCs differentially alter the metabolomic profile of plants, an untargeted metabolomic approach was performed. Metabolomic research on biological systems, such as plants, allows the evaluation of their metabolite profile in two ways: targeted and untargeted (Cox et al. 2014). While targeted methods analyse a set of defined compounds, untargeted techniques can detect thousands of unknown metabolites, which complicates the interpretation of the results generated (Amara et al. 2022). For this reason, in this study, we focused on trying to understand whether coculture with fungi caused a differential change in the plant metabolome. The results showed a separate distribution of the different samples, which was explained by a different production of certain analytes. As this confirmed our hypothesis, that coculture with fungi could indeed reprogram the metabolome of A. thaliana plants, we next attempted to identify specific metabolites.

The identities of some differentially accumulated metabolites could be assigned by comparing the mass spectra with the Massbank library and using the strictest possible identification criteria to ensure accuracy, even if this leads to a lower number of identified metabolites. While the results in Table 2 suggest similar metabolomic dysregulation after exposure to VOCs from both fungi, PCA analysis of all metabolites (more than 1300) clearly demonstrated strain specificity. As discussed above, it is worth highlighting the identification of 11 glucosinolates. The metabolomics approach also detected kaempferitrin, two different glycosylated quercetins, two different glycosylated kaempferols and one dihydrokaempferol, which all belong to the class of flavonoids. Flavonoids are secondary metabolites involved in various processes such as plant development of reproductive organs, symbiotic communication between plants and organisms (Ramaroson et al. 2022) and nutrient uptake (Singh et al. 2021). In addition, these compounds may accumulate as a consequence of the methyl jasmonate signalling, a molecule induced during biotic stress (Petrussa et al. 2013). The flavonoids detected in this study were produced in different proportions, being both kaempferols overacummulated in plants cocultured with both fungi comparing to control plants. It has been suggested that there is a trade‐off between kaempferitrin and quercetin during plants responses against herbivore attack (Xiao et al. 2019). However, in this study, quercetin was detected at higher concentrations in plants exposed to either of the two fungi and kaempferitrin only in plants exposed to T. harzianum.

Other compounds previously associated with plant defense, including gentisic acid 5‐O‐glucoside, 4‐acetyl‐3‐hydroxy‐5‐methylphenyl β‐d‐glucopyranoside and 3‐hydroxybenzaldehyde, were overproduced in plants cocultured with at least one of the fungi. Glycosylated gentisic acid has been found to play a crucial role in regulating SA homeostasis when plant defense mechanisms are activated (Nutricati et al. 2023), which prevents overaccumulation of SA and regulates the expression of defense‐related genes. In contrast, certain phenyl glycosides from the Salicaceae family have been found to be involved in plant resistance against herbivores (Boeckler et al. 2011), although 4‐acetyl‐3‐hydroxy‐5‐methylphenyl β‐d‐glucopyranoside has not specifically been mentioned. Finally, benzaldehydes appear to exhibit potent antifungal activity (Kim et al. 2011) and have been proposed as plant growth regulators (Choi et al. 2016). The increase in these metabolites in plants exposed to fungi supports the hypothesis that coculture induces an enhanced defensive state, possibly through early activation of metabolic pathways and hormonal signalling. The accumulation of these compounds suggests not only an induced response to biotic stress, but also the involvement of less abundant secondary metabolites, whose free or conjugated forms may play direct defensive roles or function in signaling or storage. Moreover, the KEGG enrichment analyses of identified metabolites also pointed to a downregulation of the primary metabolism at the level of the TCA cycle, which would be expected when plant defense pathways are activated (Le Bot et al. 2009; Rojas et al. 2014). This downregulation of TCA intermediates, such as malic and succinic acid, reflects a metabolic shift characteristic of the primed state, where primary energy production is suppressed to boost the heightened synthesis of defensive secondary metabolites like glucosinolates.

Overall, the metabolomics data suggest that fungal VOCs trigger a broader defense‐associated metabolic reprogramming in A. thaliana, rather than only changing isolated metabolites. The separation between control and fungal cocultured plants (Figure 6), together with the enrichment of glucosinolate metabolism and the reduction in TCA cycle intermediates, is consistent with a shift from primary to defense‐related secondary metabolism. This interpretation is supported by the induction of CYP81F2 and PAD3, as well as enhanced resistance to P. syringae, contributing to a better understanding of the trade‐off between growth and defense. However, we acknowledge that the 66 identified metabolites reflect only a small portion ( ~ 5%) of the total detected metabolome, which inherently limits a comprehensive global interpretation of the network. Furthermore, while the accumulation of specific flavonoids and glucosinolate precursors strongly correlates with the restricted bacterial proliferation, a causal link remains to be functionally validated. Future research involving the exogenous application of these candidate metabolites, individually or in synthetic combinations, will be essential to definitively confirm their individual roles in plant protection.

The results obtained demonstrate that the selected fungi are capable of emitting VOCs and that they have profound effects on the fitness of A. thaliana (Figure 7). In synthesis, the data presented herein provide multi‐layered evidence supporting a time‐ and dose‐dependent trade‐off between plant growth and defense mediated by fungal VOCs. In long‐term exposures (14 days), the continuous accumulation of volatile alcohols and phytotoxic terpenes manifests as a clear phenotypic cost, characterized by severe biomass reduction, photosynthetic impairment, and sustained oxidative stress. Conversely, under short‐term exposure (48 h), it operates primarily at the metabolic and transcriptional levels before severe morphological penalties occur. This is evidenced by: (i) the transcriptional activation of both early and late defense marker genes (PR1, PDF1.2, PAD3); (ii) a significant systemic downregulation of primary metabolism via the TCA cycle (malic and succinic acids); and (iii) the concomitant diversion of resources toward defensive secondary pathways, specifically glucosinolate and flavonoid biosynthesis. Together, these coordinated shifts explain how short‐term exposure to the fungal volatilome successfully transitions A. thaliana into a primed, defensive state capable of restricting P. syringae proliferation, albeit at the expense of primary energy production. Furthermore, our results pave the way for further research that could contribute to the development of new biological control methods, such as the production of biostimulant compounds (Cordovez et al. 2018). Indeed, considering the importance of studying plant‐microorganism interactions and the novelty of the technologies used in this work, it would be interesting to follow up and further understand the details of the effects of VOCs on plants. For instance, further progress would require the identification of the biologically active VOCs to enable their application under field conditions. Currently, knowledge in this area of research is relatively limited, so the development of new technologies for the study of volatile‐mediated plant‐microorganism interactions and the results observed in preliminary studies such as the present work provide an initial basis for future research, which could evolve into studies on species of greater agricultural interest.
