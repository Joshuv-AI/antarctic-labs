Patellofemoral Instability in Children with Down Syndrome
Authors: Robert G. Yoder, Paraskevi M. Limberatos, Joseph A. Magoline, Melanie A. Morscher, Patrick M. Riley, Kenneth T. Bono, John D. Polousky, Robert G. Tysklind
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The incidence of patellofemoral instability (PFI) in patients with Down syndrome (DS) is 10% to 20%. Treatment of PFI in this cohort has evolved over the past four decades. Nonoperative management largely prevails today; however symptomatic patients with DS and recurrent instability may benefit from patellar stabilization surgery. Combined, physeal-sparing soft tissue procedures have become the focus of modern-day surgeons when operative intervention is pursued in skeletally immature patients. This review article outlines the seldom-discussed topic of PFI in patients with DS and highlights the need for high-quality, outcome-based research on the optimal management in this specific cohort.Key Concepts(1)Asymptomatic patients with Down syndrome (DS) and patellofemoral instability (PFI) are treated nonoperatively.(2)Surgical treatment of PFI has evolved and may benefit patients with DS and symptomatic PFI.(3)Approximately 20% of patients with DS develop PFI. Reported recurrence rates following surgical intervention range from 0% to 20%.(4)High-quality, outcome-based research in this population is needed.

Introduction

Patellofemoral instability (PFI) is a frequent orthopaedic knee issue, particularly in adolescents, with a peak incidence occurring between 14 and 18 years of age [1]. This condition can range from subluxation to complete patellar dislocation, often resulting in secondary complications such as osteochondral injuries with loose bodies, rupture of the medial patellofemoral ligament (MPFL), and an increased risk of patellofemoral arthritis with recurrent instability [2]. Several risk factors contribute to PFI, including patella alta, trochlear dysplasia, increased tibial tubercle to trochlear groove (TT-TG) distance, ligamentous laxity, and lower limb malalignment [3,4]. In the general population, the incidence of PFI is estimated to be 6–77 cases per 100,000 individuals and MPFL reconstruction has become the standard of care for the recurrent dislocator, significantly reducing risk of recurrence [5,6].

The rate of PFI is significantly higher in individuals with DS, affecting approximately 10% to 20% of this population. This increased prevalence is attributed to greater joint and ligamentous laxity, hypotonia, trochlear dysplasia, and other skeletal abnormalities associated with DS, all of which predispose these patients to PFI [7]. Other musculoskeletal conditions commonly encountered by the treating orthopaedic surgeon also include hip instability, slipped capital femoral epiphysis, pes planovalgus, atlantoaxial instability, and scoliosis [8].

The natural history of PFI in patients with DS is poorly understood. Early studies suggest that many patients remain functional and ambulatory despite recurrent instability, and as life expectancy in patients with DS continues to increase, the long-term consequences of chronic instability remain unknown [[9], [10], [11]]. Looking into the natural history of chronic PFI in patients without DS, studies have shown that chronic PFI is associated with an increased risk of patellofemoral degeneration and osteoarthritis, whether treated operatively or nonoperatively, although the extent to which these findings apply to patients with DS remains unclear [12].

Management of PFI in patients with DS presents unique challenges not encountered in the general population. Postoperative compliance and expectations are different in this patient population and require thorough discussion with the patients and caregivers. PFI in DS often manifests at a younger age; however early symptoms may be masked by hypotonia and generalized ligamentous laxity, as well as delayed ambulation and reduced symptom reporting, leading to delayed diagnosis. The frequent coexistence of associated skeletal abnormalities further complicates management. As in the general population, suboptimal management may lead to progressive osteoarthritis and knee dysfunction [10]. With life expectancy in patients with DS increasing from 25 years old in 1983 to 60 years old in 2020, it is paramount to determine appropriate strategies for this continually aging population [9]. Despite this, the literature regarding optimal management of PFI in patients with DS remains limited. In this review article, we examine existing literature-albeit scarce-to guide orthopaedic surgeons caring for patients with DS and PFI.

Natural history and etiology

The prevalence of PFI in patients with DS is approximately 10% to 20%, substantially higher than in the general population [7]. This increased prevalence is thought to result from characteristic musculoskeletal abnormalities associated with trisomy 21. Overexpression of collagen VI, encoded by genes located on chromosome 21, has been proposed as a contributor to the generalized ligamentous laxity and muscular hypotonia commonly observed in individuals with DS [13]. These features are associated with impaired function and an increased prevalence of musculoskeletal disorders involving the spine, hips, feet, and knees [14]. In a population-based study of 475 individuals with DS, PFI was identified in 22.4% of patients and was significantly associated with generalized joint laxity, supporting the role of soft tissue insufficiency in the development of instability [15]. Knee pathology affects up to 30% to 40% of patients with DS [10], and genu valgum is frequently observed in association with PFI [16]. More recently, Orozco et al. [17] demonstrated significantly greater valgus alignment in knees with PFI compared with knees without PFI, suggesting that coronal plane malalignment contributes to the development of instability.

Diagnosis

Presentation

Patients with DS are at increased risk for PFI for reasons described above; however, presenting clinical symptoms range dramatically compared to patients with PFI in the general population. In general, delayed ambulation and decreased activity levels compared to their peers-as well as intellectual disability-often leads to delayed orthopaedic attention in this cohort [7]. When caregivers do identify the problem and seek care, some patients present as habitual dislocators with no pain or functional deficits, calling into question the need for any orthopaedic intervention [10,11]. Other patients, however, display symptoms that significantly impair their quality of life in the form of chronic pain, frequent falls, and/or limping [18].

Physical examination

A thorough examination of the lower extremities is critical in new patients with DS presenting to clinic with knee pain or instability. This includes supine or prone hip range of motion as well as assessment of the overall alignment of the legs, looking for coronal malalignment issues such as genu valgum. Assessment of the patients’ gait should also be performed, looking for rotational abnormalities of the hips combined with abnormal tibial rotation, such as miserable malalignment. Additionally, altered gait is a common sign of PFI in this cohort. Other common exam features such as joint effusion, patellar apprehension, J-sign and increased lateral translation of the patella may also be encountered. Careful evaluation for patellar tracking must be performed. Patients with DS have a higher rate of habitual, or obligate, dislocation where the patella dislocates laterally with knee flexion and reduces with full knee extension [19]. This is typically associated with higher degrees of lateral soft tissue tightness, quadriceps shortening, and external rotation than the routine PFI. Habitual dislocation commonly presents after the patient begins to walk and is typically painless. This can progress to a fixed lateral dislocation with time in some patients [19].

Imaging

Standard X-rays remain the imaging modality of choice. This may include not only knee X-rays but also standing long-leg alignment X-rays. Advanced imaging may include magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) to fully evaluate the cartilage as well as assess for predisposing factors for PFI. Dejour et al. [20] described three radiographic factors to assess: patellar height, TT-TG distance, and trochlear dysplasia as having a role in the stability of the patellofemoral joint. However, these traditional risk factors may not contribute to instability in the same manner in patients with DS. Orozco et al. [17] demonstrated that knees with PFI exhibited significantly greater valgus deformity than knees without PFI and identified additional anatomic differences, including increased lateral tibial translation and depression of the medial tibial plateau. A recent study evaluating the DS cohort specifically, identified trochlear dysplasia as the most important anatomic parameter to assess, which is consistent with other PFI studies [21]. Their work suggests that patellar height is not a parameter to consider in this group, as patella alta is nearly ubiquitous in this population, and does not necessarily correlate to instability [21,22]. Instead, Reboucas Moreira et al. [21] conclude that patellar congruence angle and trochlear groove angle are more appropriate measurements to consider when evaluating for signs of PFI and to diagnose trochlear dysplasia in those patients with DS. The authors believe that trochlear dysplasia is so prevalent in patients with DS who dislocate because their trochlea does not receive the appropriate stimulus necessary to form a deepened groove [21]. Furthermore, recurrent dislocation in this population subsequently causes trochlear dysplasia, which had previously been thought of as a congenital predisposition for PFI in the general population [20]. Recent pediatric imaging studies by Richmond et al. [23] and Alaya et al. [24] demonstrate that trochlear morphology is established early through the cartilaginous template, and that normal trochlear deepening depends on appropriate patellar engagement during childhood. These developmental studies support the concept of repeated instability and maltracking in childhood directly contributing to the dysplastic trochlear anatomy seen in the DS population. More research is needed to discern if trochlear dysplasia is a cause or effect of recurrent instability in patients with DS, and clarity on this may influence early management in this cohort.

Dugdale and Renshaw classification system

In 1986, Dugdale and Renshaw [11] published a classification system for PFI in patients with DS to standardize the description of instability for future research. Grade 1 was a stable patellofemoral joint. Grade 2 indicated that the patella could be subluxated laterally more than one-half of the patellar width, but could not be dislocated. Grade 3 indicated a patella that could be dislocated. Grade 4 indicated a patella that was already dislocated, but could be manually reduced. Finally, grade 5 was a dislocated patella that could not be manually reduced, also considered a fixed lateral dislocation. This classification system emphasizes the role of generalized laxity and extensor mechanism malalignment and provides a foundation for classifying severity and timing of intervention.

Management

Nonoperative management

Historically, the management of PFI in patients with DS favored nonoperative treatment as it was thought that most patients lived without pain or loss of function [10,11]. Arguments against surgical stabilization include genetic pre-disposition for hyperlaxity and hypotonia, which cannot be corrected with surgery. In addition, postoperative compliance in this cohort is of concern where surgical management of PFI often necessitates careful adherence to postoperative restrictions and oftentimes bracing. Suboptimal early results with operative intervention, in conjunction with publications such as Dugdale's [11] case series concluding in acceptable results without surgery in this cohort, which further biased surgeons toward nonoperative measures. Mendez et al. [10] also reported satisfactory results with nonoperative management in patients with fair or good preoperative ambulatory status further emphasizing this position. They also concluded, however, that 80% of patients with baseline poor ambulatory status treated non-operatively failed to improve.

Today, with shared decision-making, it is paramount to discuss with caregivers what we know about the natural history of children with symptomatic and asymptomatic PFI as life expectancy has increased substantially since these early studies were published [9]. Treatment decisions should therefore be individualized based on patient symptoms, type of instability, functional limitations, and the available evidence regarding recurrence and complications discussed below.

Operative management

Operative management of PFI in patients with DS presents unique challenges due to the aforementioned ligamentous laxity, trochlear dysplasia, concurrent valgus in the coronal plane, developmental delays, and altered gait mechanics. Since the 1980s, surgical procedures have been described for these patients with varying results [10,11,16]. Importantly, interpretation of long-term outcomes, particularly the development of degenerative arthritis, is inherently limited by the marked heterogeneity of operative techniques described for patellar stabilization. This limitation is well recognized in the general PFI literature and is further amplified in the DS population, where small cohort sizes, mixed procedures, and variable functional baselines complicate meaningful comparisons. Moreover, it is imperative to differentiate the degree of instability (ie: acute, chronic, obligate and fixed), with the more complex types of instability more frequently requiring operative intervention and larger surgeries, further adding to the difficulty in studying this population.

Additional challenges are introduced in skeletally immature patients, requiring physeal-sparing techniques. In Dugdale and Renshaw's [11] 1986 series of 210 institutionalized and 151 noninstitutionalized individuals with DS, only 8 surgeries were performed. Patients in the study underwent various soft-tissue and bony balancing procedures, seeking to realign the extensor mechanism with a reported 50% satisfactory result. Mendez et al. [10], soon thereafter in 1988, retrospectively reviewed 26 knees in patients with DS that also underwent a variety of surgical procedures, and found that operative treatment resulted in good ambulatory ability in 86% of the knees with fair or poor preoperative ambulatory status. They did note that surgical intervention did not effectively prevent deformities that ultimately resulted in degenerative arthritis.

As mentioned earlier, PFI events during childhood may affect trochlear development; therefore, there is rationale for the protective nature of early stabilization in these children [23,24]. Although not yet studied directly in the DS population, these findings suggest that earlier, physeal-sparing soft-tissue stabilization may theoretically promote more normal trochlear development and prevent worsening dysplasia in high-risk children. Given the absence of a universally successful operative strategy and the difficulty in comparing long-term outcomes across disparate procedures, multiple operative approaches have been proposed. The most frequently described techniques are reviewed in the following sections. Patients with DS may benefit from isolated or combinations of these methods.

Roux–Goldthwait (RG) procedure

One of the earliest described distal soft-tissue realignment procedures for PFI was published by Roux et al., in 1888 and modified by Goldthwait in 1895. The modified procedure involves splitting the patellar tendon longitudinally; the lateral half is detached distally and passed under the intact medial half, then fixed to the tibia in a medialized position (Fig. 1) [25]. The goal is to medialize the line of pull of the extensor mechanism, countering lateral tracking and lateral patellar dislocations [26,27]. Bettuzzi et al. [28] first published their results utilizing a variation of this technique in a case series of 10 knees in patients with DS. They found, at a mean 8-year follow-up, that no redislocations were reported, median Lysholm scores improved significantly, and all patients maintained independent ambulation.Figure 1Images of the Roux–Goldthwait procedure: (A) cartoon of right knee courtesy of Pilone et al. Ruzzini et al. [29] also published on the modified Roux–Goldthwait procedure in the DS population in 2019. Importantly, the described procedure included an extensive lateral retinacular release and medial retinacular tensioning, in addition to distal patellar tendon transfer. Their series included 19 patients and 23 knees in patients with DS. With a mean follow-up of 11 years, they reported a redislocation rate of 0% and had significant improvements in Kujala and modified Lysholm scores and radiographic congruence angle. They also found that all but three patients performed recreational activities without pain or limping at last follow-up [29].

Galeazzi and Green procedures

Initially described by Galeazzi in 1922, the original procedure involves mobilization of the semitendinosus tendon with preservation of its distal attachment, passage beneath the fascia, and fixation into a periosteal groove along the medial border of the patella to medialize the extensor mechanism [30]. Letts et al. [31] studied a modified Galeazzi semitendinosus transfer (Fig. 2) [25] in skeletally immature recurrent dislocators (not DS-specific) and found a 3% redislocation rate. Green's quadricepsplasty (Fig. 3) [32] was first described in 1965 and involves quadriceps lengthening (often via V–Y plasty), extensive lateral release of the fascia lata, vastus lateralis, and iliotibial band, combined with medial capsular reefing and medialization of the quadriceps mechanism to permit central patellar tracking [33]. These two procedures were compared by Kocon et al. [34] in 2012, looking at 10 knees in patients with DS. They utilized an age-stratified approach and in younger children (<10 years), a Green's quadricepsplasty in isolation was performed (8 knees), and in older children/adolescents (>10 years), they added a modified Galeazzi procedure (2 knees). They had 2 failures in the younger group treated with quadricepsplasty alone, as defined by recurrent dislocations. However, they concluded that older children benefit from augmenting Green’s with Galeazzi’s, as there were no recurrent dislocations in either patient with that treatment.Figure 2Right knee cartoon depiction of the modified Galeazzi procedure, courtesy of Pilone et al. MPFLR

Medial patellofemoral ligament reconstruction (MPFLR) is the widely accepted standard of care for most recurrent PFI in patients without DS, with a large focus starting in the mid-2010s (Fig. 5) [25]. However, its use in the DS population was initially questioned due to patient's patellar hypoplasia, trochlear dysplasia, generalized ligamentous laxity, hypotonia, bone quality, and whether single procedures would be sufficient for patients with syndromic PFI. Our senior authors, however, favor allograft in the DS population due to concern for autograft tissue quality secondary to generalized ligamentous laxity and potential poor tissue quality.

To our knowledge, the first case report of single-procedure MPFLR in a patient with DS was reported by Petershofer et al. [35] in 2015. At one-year follow-up, no recurrent dislocation was reported and the patient could walk without the feeling of “giving way” of their patella. This placed emphasis on the anatomic soft-tissue reconstruction as a viable option, even in syndromic patients. Subsequent studies looked at MPFLR in other DS cohorts. Bitar et al. [36] in 2018 evaluated 6 knees in patients with DS, treating them with a patellar-tendon-based autograft reconstruction technique as well as additional soft tissue procedures as necessary. They found improved patellar stability, gait, and functional scores at an average 64-month follow-up. Although limited by small numbers, this series supports early MPFLR and soft-tissue balancing in DS PFI when indicated.

Most recently, in 2024, Zein et al. [18] published a series of 11 patients (12 knees) with DS and symptomatic PFI. They performed MPFLR with quadriceps tendon autograft, along with lateral release, Roux–Goldthwait distal realignment, and V–Y quadricepsplasty in the setting of obligatory dislocation. They too had 0 patellar redislocations, improvement in functional and radiographic outcomes, and no growth disturbances or graft failures reported. This is the largest modern series in patients with DS and reports success with combined proximal and distal realignment, including MPFLR, in this dysplastic, ligamentously lax population.

Four-in-one procedure

Given the success rates of these various procedures, a “four-in-one” procedure was proposed as a single-stage, combined soft-tissue realignment procedure. It addresses four separate anatomic problems in a single operation: lateral retinacular release, modified Insall's proximal “tube” realignment, semitendinosus tenodesis, and transfer of the lateral half of the patellar tendon (Fig. 4) [37]. It is indicated when the patella cannot maintain reduction with single procedures; therefore, it is most often used in severe cases of syndromic or congenital obligatory patellar dislocation, including DS, arthrogryposis, and Larsen syndrome [38]. We find that in fixed patellar dislocations, lengthening of the quadriceps tendon through a V-Y-lengthening or Z-plasty is required, especially in cases where the knee cannot be passively flexed to 90° in the operating room after centralizing the patella. Joo et al. [39] described this procedure in 2007 in 6 knees of patients with obligatory patellar dislocation, two of the patients had DS. They found that all patients returned to full activities postoperatively and there were 0 dislocations during a mean follow-up of 4.8 years. Gigi et al. [38] reproduced comparable clinical outcomes in their study published in 2024 using a similar four-in-one realignment procedure in a comparable pediatric population with obligatory and congenital patellar dislocations.Figure 4Right knee cartoon of the four-in-one procedure, courtesy of Chen et al. Guided growth

Guided growth procedures may be utilized to correct genu valgum in skeletally immature patients with PFI (Fig. 6), thus creating less lateral pull of the extensor mechanism through a more neutral mechanical axis [40]. Treatment algorithm for patellofemoral instability in children with Down syndrome.

The timing of guided growth alongside medial soft-tissue reconstruction is an important consideration. While data specific to DS are lacking, recent evidence in skeletally immature patients supports concurrent correction demonstrating that doing both procedures in a single stage safely lowers recurrent instability rates [42]. We favor addressing severe genu valgum during soft-tissue stabilization. Staged treatment leaves the patient at risk for continued instability while awaiting guided growth results. Surgeons can use plates or percutaneous transphyseal screws based on anatomy, surgeon preference, and other planned procedures.

Osteotomies

For patients with closed or nearly closed growth plates, osteotomies can be a valuable tool to help address PFI. Distal femoral osteotomies can address genu valgum, while tibial tubercle osteotomies (TTO) can help change the extensor mechanism. However, complications are a concern, particularly regarding postoperative compliance in patients with DS. No published studies currently evaluate osteotomy outcomes specifically for patients with DS. In our practice, TTO indications rely on radiographic parameters such as an elevated tibial tubercle–trochlear groove (TT-TG) distance. We most commonly perform a Fulkerson osteotomy (medialization with anteriorization) to optimize tracking, adding distalization if patella alta is present. Femoral osteotomies carry higher complication rates and are typically reserved for revision cases, requiring extensive preoperative counseling with patients and caregivers. Recurrence and complications

Few studies evaluate patellar stabilization in patients with DS, limiting definitive data on complication and recurrence rates. Despite these limitations, the available literature describes encouraging results. Petershofer et al. [35] reported no recurrent instability at 1-year follow-up after isolated MPFL reconstruction, while Bitar et al. [36] and Zein et al. [18] reported no recurrent dislocations at mean follow-up periods of 64 and 48 months, respectively. As mentioned previously, the DS population can have issues with compliance with postoperative restrictions and instructions, making recovery challenging for both patients and caregivers. In addition, generalized ligamentous laxity and hypotonia should be considered during surgical planning and postoperative rehabilitation. Addressing PFI at a young age can lead to potential physeal abnormalities, and care should be taken to avoid injury to the physes with surgical techniques. As seen in the general population undergoing PFI surgery, complications including cartilage injury, patellar fracture related to anchoring techniques, physeal injury, nonunion following osteotomy procedures, and recurrent instability may be encountered. Shared decision-making is essential to establish realistic expectations and identify potential barriers to adherence and recovery.

Summary

Very few studies to date have been conducted on MPFL reconstruction and patellar stabilization procedures in children with Down syndrome thus making it difficult to assess true rates of complications associated with these procedures. As mentioned previously, the DS population can have issues with compliance with postoperative restrictions and instructions, making it difficult for both the patient and caregiver. Combined with the hypotonia and generalized laxity these patients can have recurrent instability. Addressing patellar instability at a young age can lead to potential physeal abnormalities and care should be taken to avoid injury to the physes with surgical techniques. As is seen in the general population that undergoes patellar instability surgery similar complications can be encountered including cartilage injury or patellar fractures from anchoring techniques, physeal injury, nonunions, or recurrent instability. Shared decision making is essential in order to set expectations and potentially avoid barriers to adherence and recovery.

Treatment of PFI in patients with DS has evolved over the past four decades. Historical teachings of nonoperative management still prevail today; however, symptomatic individuals with DS and recurrent instability may benefit from patellar stabilization surgery. These patients are often complex, requiring more soft-tissue releases, extensor realignments or lengthenings and coronal alignment corrections, thus the surgeon should be aware of this when treating and counseling these patients and their families. Similar to PFI research in the general population, there is a lack of direct comparative studies looking at each individual treatment strategy as these patients often need a tailored surgical approach for their specific anatomy and functional needs.
