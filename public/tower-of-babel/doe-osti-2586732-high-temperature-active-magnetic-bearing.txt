 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
Hermetic machinery utilizing gas bearings for MW-scale supercritical CO2 (sCO2) machinery
applications can have significantly lower power loss and enable improved cycle efficiency
compared to conventional machinery with oil-lubricated bearings. Active magnetic bearings
(AMBs) are another option anticipated to have similar power loss and load capacity to gas
bearings as well as offering larger mechanical tolerances, the ability to tune properties, and high
reliability due to lack of mechanical wear. AMBs also have proven commercial experience at MW-
scale, though environments for high-temperature sCO2 power cycle machinery conditions are
novel. Besides the potential impact of AMBs for sCO2 turbomachinery, the technology also offers
promising benefits for steam and gas turbines for power generation, compressors and expanders
for industrial heat and power, and in other oil and gas and space applications.
The goals of this project were to conceptual design an AMB and perform material testing.
Conceptual designs for radial and thrust AMBs were produced based on a hermetically-sealed
sCO2 machinery waste-heat recovery (WHR) application for sizing and loads, and choosing a
target design temperature of 540°C useful for high-temperature sCO2 turbines for concentrating
solar power (CSP) applications. Conceptual designs were initially developed for multiple radial
and thrust AMBs with spreadsheet-based calculations before selecting one of each to develop
further using higher-fidelity design methods for magnetic and structural performance. It was
found that the radial AMB at 540°C was feasible, but the thrust AMB needed to be limited to
315°C for high-speed operation. The decision for a reduced-temperature thrust AMB was the
result of several significant conclusions: 1) Hiperco 50A, originally chosen for good magnetic
performance at high temperature, had insufficient strength for high-speed operation, so it was
replaced with 17-4 PH. 2) The reduced magnetic performance from 17-4 PH yielded a larger
bearing size, reducing the strength margin. 3) This ultimately led to a creative design
implementing a more-compact E-core topology, compared to the original (conventional) C-core,
and an integral shaft-disk with Hirth joint connection. These conceptual designs are unique for
the size and temperature in CO2, relevant for MW-scale CSP applications.
Long term, high temperature test data was generated for several materials, filling a void in the
current body of literature. Corrosion and magnetic performance measurements were produced
for PM materials (Alnico 5-7C, Alnico 9C, and SmCo) with and without nickel-coating for
environments of high-temperature CO2 up to 550°C at atmospheric pressure and 450°C at 103
bar for up to 6,000 hours. Corrosion measurements were also produced for Hiperco 50, a SM
material relevant for AMB laminations, with and without C5 coating. Comparisons were also
made for 450°C and 550°C, atmospheric pressure air exposures up to 5,000 hours. Results
generally show that coatings can be effective at improving oxidation resistance of the bare
materials, and Alnicos generally outperformed SmCo after high-temperature exposure.
In addition to technical feasibility demonstrated by the design, economic feasibility was
demonstrated by updating the TEA from the reference machine, re-evaluating it with CAPEX and
OPEX to reflect estimated changes from process-lubricated bearings to AMBs. AMBs were shown
to be comparable in performance to process-lubricated bearings, still showing a notable
improvement over conventional machinery architecture with oil-lubricated bearings.

1 BACKGROUND
A previous study [1] showed that hermetic machinery utilizing gas bearings for a notional 10
MWe supercritical CO2 (sCO2) power cycle drivetrain can provide order-of-magnitude reduction
in power loss compared to conventional machinery utilizing oil-lubricated bearings, which has
the potential for increasing overall cycle efficiency by several points. Like gas bearings, active
magnetic bearings (AMBs) can operate in the process fluid and are, therefore, an alternative to
enable hermetic machinery. AMBs are anticipated to have similar power loss and load capacity
to gas bearings as well as offering the following advantages: greater tolerance to misalignments
caused by tolerance stack-ups, thermal distortion, etc., due to order-of-magnitude larger
clearances; the ability to tune stiffness and damping properties for stability and control of
vibration amplitudes; “smart machine” and diagnostic ability; and high reliability due to lack of
mechanical wear [2].
Gas bearing technology for MW-scale sCO2 applications is at technology readiness level (TRL) 3
or 4 since development has been on a functional sub-component level or in component rig tests
in different environments than the final application. Current research has been addressing the
traditional limitations of gas bearings: namely, low damping from the gas film and insufficient
load capacity for larger turbomachines (e.g., journal diameters greater than 100 mm or 4 in.), by
incorporating sealed oil squeeze film dampers and hydrostatic lift features [1, 3–5]. In contrast,
AMB technology has significant commercial experience at that scale, though environments for
high-temperature (e.g., significantly higher than 200°C or 400°F) sCO2 power cycle machinery
conditions would be novel. It is noted that hermetic sCO2 turbomachines utilizing AMBs for heat
pump applications are commercially available, but they have been developed for process
temperatures only up to approximately 120°C (250°F)—e.g., [6-8].
AMBs operating in sCO2 up to 550°C (1020°F), a potential cooling temperature from the exhaust
stream of a sCO2 turbine, are not commercially available. However, feasibility at that
temperature has been demonstrated in ambient-pressure air [9–11], making the AMB system
TRL-4 for the present application. Others have researched 550°C (1020°F)-capable AMBs for sCO2
turbines [12], though some of this work is still very preliminary [13]. A technical challenge with
high-temperature AMBs for sCO2 is material compatibility. Magnetic materials have property
degradation and durability issues when operating at high temperature and/or in corrosive
environments caused by corrosion, oxidation, thermal aging, creep, etc. [14]. Soft magnetic
materials, such as FeCo for rotor laminations, show lower saturation flux density and reduced
mechanical strength that lowers high-speed rotor capability. Insulation and other materials also
require special consideration for high-temperature conditions, though ceramics and glass fiber
composites have been demonstrated [9–11].

2 PROJECT OBJECTIVES

2.1 Goals
The end goals of this project include conceptual design and material testing of a relevant AMB
for an sCO2 application. The design goals are to develop conceptual designs for high-temperature
(540°C) radial and axial AMBs and hermetically-sealed sCO2 machinery for a reference

                                         Page 11 of 123

application, to demonstrate feasibility, and to compare cost and performance with conventional
non-hermetic machinery concepts with oil-lubricated bearings. The reference application from
literature is a relevant sCO2 machinery use-case that can benefit from the use of process-
immersed bearings, such as AMBs. The high design temperature target extends the capabilities
of the AMBs for use in high-performance applications, such as concentrating solar power, where
turbine inlet temperatures can exceed 700°C. The material testing goals are to publish test data
for permanent magnets (PM) and magnetic bearing materials exposed to high-temperature CO2
up to 550°C at atmospheric pressure and 450°C at >80 bar for up to 6,000 hours. The study
includes different base materials as well as typical protective coatings. Permanent magnet
materials are applicable to permanent magnet-biased AMBs (however, this type of AMB is not
the current scope) as well as permanent magnet motors and generators in hermetic-sealed
applications, such as the reference considered by this project. Prior to this project, long-term
exposure studies for these materials have been lacking in the literature.

2.2 Impact
It is expected that high-temperature AMBs will enable superior cycle performance to oil bearings
and offer a competitive alternative to gas bearings. While gas bearings are currently being
developed for this application, AMBs at this scale have decades of proven performance. For sCO2
machinery applications, this project will advance potential materials from TRL-3 to TRL-5 and
establish a foundation to advance the AMB system from TRL-4 to TRL-5 or TRL-6 in follow-on
projects. Besides the potential impact of AMBs for sCO2 turbomachinery, the technology also
offers promising benefits for steam and gas turbines for power generation, compressors and
expanders for industrial heat and power, and in other oil and gas and space applications.

2.3 Tasks and Milestones
The conceptual design is executed in a “preliminary” and “final” stage. The preliminary
conceptual design (Task 1.0) of the rotor-bearings system incorporates rule-of-thumb AMB sizing
based on experience and spreadsheet-based calculations (Subtask 1.1), iterated with updated
shaft layouts and rotordynamic predictions (Subtask 1.2), to define the load requirements of all
the bearings of the system. An axial and radial AMB are developed further with greater fidelity in
the final conceptual design (Task 3.0, specifically Subtasks 3.1 and 3.2). Design milestones include
meeting load capacity and rotordynamic performance requirements. A technoeconomic analysis
is performed (Subtask 3.3) to compare cost and performance of AMB-based machinery with
conventional non-hermetic machinery concepts with oil-lubricated bearings; this includes a
levelized cost of energy (LCOE) milestone.
Separately, material exposure testing (Task 2.0) is performed to generate quantitative and
qualitative data on long-term exposure of permanent magnet and other materials, investigating
the effect of environment chemistry and temperature as well as the effectiveness of coatings.
Milestones for material performance include the number of unique test samples, sample
oxidation rates based on mass measurements, relative post/pre-exposure remanence values,
relative with/without coating remanence values, and qualitative assessments of any non-
permanent magnet materials.

3 PROJECT RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

3.1 Task 1.0 – Preliminary Conceptual Design

3.1.1 Overview
An existing sCO2 turbomachinery conceptual design is the basis of the current project; it
implements two hermetic machine trains with process-lubricated bearings that will be replaced
with AMBs. This task documents preliminary sizing calculations for all AMBs and modifies the
conceptual rotor and stator geometry accordingly. Design parameters for acceptable AMB
performance and rotordynamics are also presented. Finally, one radial and one axial1 AMB are
selected for further work in Task 3.0.
A majority of the Task 1.0 work was documented in a 2024 conference paper [15].
All Task 1.0 milestones are summarized in Section 3.1.4.

3.1.2 Subtask 1.1 – Rule of Thumb Sizing of AMBs Based on Existing Machine Requirements

3.1.2.1 Reference Machinery Requirements
Ertas et al. [16–20] describe a waste-heat recovery (WHR) application employing a cascaded
closed Brayton sCO2 cycle to convert gas turbine exhaust stream heat to electric power. Figure 1
shows a diagram of the cycle and the conceptual machinery designs from [16]2. While the sCO2
turbine inlet temperature for this specific application is only 465°C (869°F), the sCO2 machinery
is relevant for other applications, such as concentrating solar power, where turbine inlet
temperatures would be in excess of 700°C (1290°F) [21–24]. The presented design utilizes two
hermetic machines, a 12 krpm (low-speed, LS) turbine-generator and a 27 krpm (high-speed, HS)
turbine-compressor with a motor/generator, operating on process-lubricated bearings. The low-
speed machine includes four radial bearings and one thrust bearing, and the high-speed machine
includes three radial bearings and one thrust bearing—reference the markers in Figure 1 and

3.1.3.3 Selection of Thrust and Radial AMBs for Task 3.0
A single radial AMB and a single thrust AMB were selected for higher-fidelity analysis in Task 3.0.
The following includes a discussion of the various factors that were considered to choose the
most challenging design conditions, which largely is a trade between size/force and speed.
AMB load capacity directly correlates with the winding inductance and the size of the bearing
(length and outer diameter for a constrained shaft size). It is desirable to minimize the winding
inductance to minimize the excitation voltage required at high frequencies as well as minimize
the size of the bearing for packaging, rotordynamics (reduced axial space and mass), and
mechanical performance (reduced centrifugal load stresses, impact on interference fits). Higher
shaft speed often corresponds with lower load capacity requirements, but it contributes to an
exponential increase in mechanical stresses and strains, eddy current losses in the rotor and

                                            Page 23 of 123

stator, and more challenging electrical design (higher actuation frequencies, higher excitation
voltages, more challenging controller development). Shaft surface speed is a metric that captures
both size and rotational speed.

3.2 Task 2.0 – Material Testing and Evaluation

3.2.1 Overview
Originally, the proposed project considered the use of permanent magnet (PM)-biased AMBs and
PM material exposure testing to complement the design effort. This was enabled by a strategic
project team member organization, an AMB original equipment manufacturer (OEM), that had
PM-biased AMB design and manufacturing expertise. After award, this partner disassociated
from the project, and the only potential options to replace OEM capabilities were in the space of
electromagnetic (EM)-biased AMBs. Therefore, the design goals transitioned to the use of EM-
biased AMBs. It is noted that, regardless of AMB type, the value of PM material testing was
maintained since PM motors and generators, relevant to the hermetic machine architecture
capitalizing on the AMB technology, also utilize similar materials. More detailed justification and
context were presented in a previous report [29].
This task evaluates the performance of eight unique PM (or hard magnetic) and soft magnetic
(SM4) materials subjected to exposures up to 6,000 hours in CO2 at over 80 bar and 450°C and 1
bar at 550°C in addition to exposures up to 5,000 hours in air at 1 bar and 450°C and 550°C. The
samples include bare and coated materials, and data includes mass change and magnetic
property testing. Long-term exposure data for these materials (defined below) do not exist, so
the results of this work are novel to the literature.
Portions of the Task 2.0 work were documented in a 2025 conference paper [30].

3.2.2.1 Material Selection

3.2.2.1.1 PM Materials
The selection of PM materials was determined by considering factors such as maximum operating
temperature, remanence, coercivity, and energy density. The list of selected materials is shown
in Table 11. The maximum operating temperature of the selected materials is 550°C (1022°F),
aligning with the operational requirements of the rotor-bearing design. Remanence is the
residual magnetic flux density within a hysteresis loop when the external field returns to zero
from its saturation magnetization value. A higher remanence corresponds to a magnet's ability
to sustain a stronger magnetic field when magnetized, resulting in increased energy density and
magnetic flux density. The selected materials exhibit a remanence range of 0.83 T to 1.35 T.
Coercivity refers to the magnetic field intensity required to demagnetize a material. A higher
coercivity is preferred when selecting a material for PM motor application, as it is advantageous
to resist field weakening. The selected materials in Table 11 show coercivity ranging from 0.74
kOe to 7.7 kOe. Additionally, these materials display energy density values ranging from 36 kJ/m3
to 120 kJ/m3, where higher values indicate higher performance per unit volume.
Nickel plating is standard for high temperature SmCo PM materials. The test material matrix
includes SmCo 18-T550 PM with and without a 5-20 µm electroless nickel-boron plating for
coating effect study on CO2 corrosion resistance. Different from high SmCo, plating is generally
not used on Alnico. However, nickel or nickel alloys show enhanced supercritical CO 2 anti-
corrosion properties. For this reason, Alnico 5-7 and Alnico 9 materials with and without 10 µm
NiCuNi plating were selected to investigate the relative effect.

3.2.2.1.2 SM Materials
Hiperco 50 was the chosen SM material, which would be an applicable material for AMB rotor
and stator laminations. This material, a cobalt-iron alloy, is typically the preferred choice for high-
temperature AMB design due to its exceptional magnetic performance and stability at elevated
temperatures. Compared to silicon steels, a more traditionally-used SM material, the maximum
relative permeability is more than double. This, along with a higher saturation flux density and
Curie temperature, allows for AMBs with higher force densities and operating temperatures. A
comparison of magnetic properties with an example grade of silicon steel is provided in Table 13.

Other relevant variations of the Hiperco alloy are the 50HS and 50A designations. Hiperco 50HS
trades-off some magnetic performance for improved mechanical strength, making it particularly
useful for rotating components like the laminations of a radial AMB rotor. Hiperco 50A is available
in both strip and bar form. While the bar form is magnetically weaker than the strip form, it is
crucial for thrust magnetic bearings that require non-laminated geometry.
Hiperco alloys are notoriously expensive due to their cobalt content. Due to restrictions in the
project budget, it was not feasible to test all three alloy variations (50, 50HS, and 50A) with
different surface coatings. While each variation has different magnetic and structural properties,
it is expected that all three will perform similarly with regard to the study of long-term corrosive
effects of sCO2. Further, the type of surface coating used is expected to have a more significant
impact on corrosion rates. Therefore, it was decided to test 0.014-inch-thick strip specimens of
Hiperco 50 with and without coating. The coating is C5—Remisol EB 5308 from Rembrandtin
Coatings, an inorganic/organic hybrid varnish, class C-5 (ASTM 976)—which commonly serves as
electrical insulation for stacked laminations of magnetic materials. It was applied on the
specimens by dipping, followed by a curing process up to 240°C to form a 5-micron-thick coating
layer. Polaris Laser Laminations provided the C5 coating and curing services. All specimens were
ordered with coatings applied by the vendors to their standards—this was not specifically
controlled in this project.

3.2.2.3 CO2 Exposure Test Facilities
The materials were exposed to sCO2 at 842°F (450°C) and 1,500 psi (103 bar) in an autoclave or
to flowing gaseous CO2 at 1,022°F (550°C) and ambient pressure in a furnace. Industrial grade
CO2 was used for both exposures. The purity of the CO2 could be as low as 99% and no
composition analysis was provided by the vendor.
The sCO2 exposure was performed in a stainless steel autoclave that is 3 feet long with a heat
zone approximately 2 feet long, as shown in Figure 14a. The samples were placed on an all-thread
rod and were separated with spacers, as represented in Figure 14b. The specimens were placed
in the autoclave before it was sealed. The autoclave and the lines were purged with N2 three
times before being pressurized to 500 psi (34.5 bar) with CO2. The autoclave was heated to 842°F
(450°C), and the pressure was adjusted by adding or releasing CO2 to 1,500 psi (103 bar). The test
started once the pressure and temperature were reached and were stable. The pressure and
temperature were monitored during the exposure and CO2 was added to maintain the pressure,
if necessary. Upon completion, the heaters were shut off and the CO2 was released once the
autoclave was cool. The autoclave was opened, and samples were removed, and further
exposure testing was resumed for remaining samples as dictated by the test plan.

The specimens exposed to flowing gaseous CO2 were also mounted on all-thread rods. They were

was done to eliminate measurement errors caused by the magnetic fields of the specimens.

3.2.2.4 Air Exposure Test Facilities
Specimens for air exposure test were set up on a fixture shown schematically as Figure 16. Figure

14” x 15” x 15”. The furnace ambient environment did have humidity control. Test duration count

3.2.2.5 Magnetic Performance Test Facility
Magnetic property measurements of PM materials were conducted on a Lakeshore 7407

upon the sample shape dimensions [37] and applied to correct hysteresis loops in the data

High temperature oxidation behaviors are often compared by using parabolic rate constants. The
parabolic rate constant kp was derived using Eq. (14), with 𝑊 being the weight change, 𝐴 the
exposed area, and 𝑡 the time. The parabolic constant can only be used when it can be assumed
that the oxide formed is stable and will remain on the surface of the material, resulting in a mass

Mass change measurements of permanent and soft magnetic materials after exposure to sCO 2
and CO2 at different temperatures revealed that:
 • The most oxidation-resistant magnetic material is Alnico 9C.
 • The least oxidation-resistant magnetic material is SmCo 18-T550.
 • Exposure of SmCo 18-T550 magnets to sCO2 may lead to cracking, although the
 mechanisms are still unknown.
 • All coatings improve oxidation resistance except for SmCo 18-T550 specimens, as it was
 found to promote cracking; the improvement appears to be more significant for materials
 coated with nickel plating than for materials using the C5 coating.
 • The effect of temperature on oxidation outweighs the effect CO2 phase until 6,000 hours
 when the CO2 phase becomes more important.
Material performing in air exposure compared to CO2 exposure:
 • No consistent most-oxidation-resistant material was observed through all air exposure
 conditions. For 550°C exposure bare Alnico 5-7C shows most oxidation resistance for all
 durations.
 • The least oxidation-resistant magnetic material is SmCo 18-T550, the same conclusion
 from CO2 tests.
 • The finding of all cracked three bare SmCo18-T550 specimens after 5000-hr air exposure
 at 550°C indicates that this magnetic material cannot serve in this condition without
 protective treatment.
 • Coatings helped to improve oxidation performance of Hiperco 50 and SmCo materials but
 had the opposite effect on two Alnico materials for most test conditions. Two possible
 explanations could be speculated: bare Alnico materials could constantly experience scale
 spalling at certain level; or oxidation could keep occurring beneath Ni coating, and the
 coating prevents the oxidation from spalling.
 • Higher temperatures more significantly affected weight change trends than durations,
 resulting in higher oxidation rates.
 • Plots of square of mass change per unit area vs. time on air exposure results did not show
 obvious linear relationships for most materials except for Hiperco. In this case, parabolic
 constant Kps calculated from simple linear regressions on three duration data points may
 not be proper enough to interpret the oxidation dynamic mechanisms in this case.

3.3.2 Subtask 3.1 – Final Bearing Conceptual Designs

3.3.2.1 Radial AMB Design
The design of the radial AMB is guided by four primary criteria: 1) spatial constraints, 2) load
capacity and lift-off force, 3) rotordynamic performance, and 4) stress and contact analysis. The
first three criteria were addressed preliminarily in Task 1.0; greater fidelity is now applied to the
same criteria in the final conceptual design as well as the additional investigation of component
stresses (HS midspan radial AMB only). The design requirements followed for the final bearing
design are as follows. First, the design must remain within the spatial constraints defined by the
original design envelope. Second, it must meet the required load capacity, including both the lift-
off force and the capacity reported in the referenced study. From a structural standpoint, the
maximum stress at nominal operating speed (27,000 rpm) must remain below the material’s
creep strength at 540°C. At 115% overspeed (31,050 rpm), the maximum stress must stay below
the material’s yield strength at the same temperature. Finally, continuous contact between the
bearing thrust disk and the rotor lamination must be maintained under both nominal and
overspeed conditions to ensure reliable operation.

3.3.2.1.3 Stress and Contact Analysis
The rotor lamination in the radial AMB is subject to high stress due to centrifugal loading from
shaft rotation and the interference fit applied during the assembly of the rotor lamination onto
the shaft, as shown in Figure 52. The material selections for each part are also shown in the figure:

available for Hiperco 50 HS, so the magnetic properties of Hiperco 50 were used in simulation.

magnetic behavior of the alloys is similar. In addition, two different types of shaft material,
Inconel 718 and 17-4 PH, have been tested to identify the optimal design to meet the design
requirements. To accurately predict the maximum stress on the rotor lamination disk, the
material properties of the rotor lamination (Hiperco 50HS) and the shaft (Inconel 718 or 17-4 PH)

3.3.2.2.3 Updated Thrust AMB Stress Analysis
The baseline E-core HS thrust AMB shown on the right of Figure 62, was used as a starting point
for rotor stress analysis. Multiple rotor design iterations were run with the same simulation setup
for all cases. All simulations were run in two steps. The first, at the design speed of 27 krpm, was
used to analyze creep performance. The second, at an overspeed of 1.15 times design speed, was
used to analyze yield and burst performance. All simulations contained rotation effects and
temperature effects but no shaft interference effects.
There were three major design iterations of note that were developed during this analysis. The
first iteration, the baseline E-core, had poor stress performance. This was improved in the second
iteration by creating a stepped rotor. While stress performance in the second iteration was
improved, the amount of rotor growth at both temperature and speed made an interference fit
with the shaft infeasible. The third and final design iteration switched to a modified version of
the stepped rotor with a Hirth joint on either side. This joint allows radial growth while
maintaining radial centering with an appropriate preload. It is also capable of transmitting the
torque required at the center of this machine, though the analysis here is only focused on the
stress of the disk due to rotation. Preload and tooth stress analysis of the Hirth joint at design
torque was considered out of scope for this effort. In addition to the Hirth joint, the aspect ratio
of the windings was changed to reduce the outer diameter of the thrust disk at the cost of a slight
axial length increase. The stress response for this iteration, as summarized in Figure 63, was
deemed acceptable, so this design was selected as the final version of the HS thrust AMB. An
overview of the HS thrust AMB design evolution is shown in Figure 64.

There are several viable sensing technologies that would work for this application, but eddy
current probes and capacitive probes are seen as the leading options. Both technologies are
commercially available in packages that can handle 540°C. Some of their advantages and
disadvantages are summarized in Table 52.

Eddy current probes have been selected for the radial AMBs. The radial AMB magnetic fields
should be well contained within the air gap, so there should be minimal issues with magnetic
interference in these probes. Capacitive probes have been selected for the thrust AMB since their
low susceptibility to magnetic interference makes it theoretically possible to embed the probes
within the thrust AMB core. This would theoretically allow the probes to measure the thrust disk
itself and negate any issues with thermal growth between the thrust disk and a target at a

                                             Page 107 of 123

different axial location on the shaft. There are two downsides to this arrangement: first, there
will be a slight reduction (1-2%) in thrust AMB force output if the probes are embedded in the
core; second, the probes may need to be calibrated at several operating points depending on
how the dielectric properties of sCO2 change with temperature and pressure.
Auxiliary bearing selection and analysis was not considered in the project scope, but this topic is
complex enough for a project of its own. It is assumed that anti-friction-coated bushings need to
be used for this application given the high temperatures involved. Though the exact selection is
not certain currently, a graphite/carbon-based coating is a likely candidate. One potential
concept that deserves further study is the application of this coating to the pole faces of the
thrust AMB. It is unknown how well this would work in application, but it would theoretically
allow a more compact thrust AMB than using a separate thrust collar.
Controller design is yet another topic that was not considered in the present scope. Though
controller design for AMB systems is not a new subject given the current amount of AMB
machines in use, high-temperature systems have unique challenges that would need to be
addressed. First, any practical high-temperature AMB controller will need to apply temperature-
based gain scheduling to compensate for the change in AMB properties with temperature. The
significant drop in force output from room temperature to operating temperature makes it
unlikely that the AMB controller will perform optimally at or between both extremes with
constant gains. The thrust bearing, as designed, also requires operating in the nonlinear region
of the force curve to achieve the required load capacity. This makes it difficult to use a linearized
bearing model when designing the controller, so either a nonlinear controller or a current-based
gain scheduled controller would be required for the thrust AMB. The system will also need to
deal with flexible modes within the running range of the machine. While not a new requirement
for AMB machines, it does add another layer of complexity when operating at high temperature.

3.3.4 Subtask 3.3 – Technoeconomic Analysis

3.3.4.1 Introduction
Technoeconomic analysis (TEA) was performed to compare the conceptual hermetic machinery
with AMBs against conventional machinery with oil-lubricated bearings, and the established
milestone was to demonstrate levelized cost of energy (LCOE) reduction more than 0.1
cents/kWh. The following section presents the definition of LCOE as well as other TEA metrics
used in the present system analysis. Note that the technical aspects of the reference system’s
machinery were introduced in Section 3.1.2.1 of this report, and additional system details
relevant to the TEA are also found in [16, 31, 32], summarized in the following paragraph.
The heat source for the WHR application was the exhaust of a PGT25+ G4 gas turbine, which is
used to drive a centrifugal compressor at a natural gas booster station. With a turbine exhaust
condition of 510°C and 89 kg/s, the sCO2 bottoming cycle was developed capable of 8.91 MW of
gross shaft power from a high-speed train (including a 3.87 MW compressor and a 4.83 MW
turbine operating at 27 krpm) and a low-speed train (including a 7.95 MW turbine operating at
12 krpm). Both machine trains were hermetic, utilizing process gas-lubricated bearings to support
the shafts and with motors/generators also within the casing, immersed in CO 2. An estimate of

                                          Page 108 of 123

1.8% losses from the gross power was considered, yielding a net power of 8.75 MW contributed
by the WHR cycle for a 51.3% combined cycle efficiency (compared to 41.4% for simple cycle)
and normalized CO2 emissions of 0.398 kg/kWh (compared to 0.494 kg/kWh for simple cycle)5.
Comparisons of the hermetic sCO2 WHR system were made with an existing WHR system based
on an organic Rankine cycle (ORC) [35], which produced approximately 40% less net power with
40% larger installed cost (per kW) and five times the operating and maintenance cost compared
to estimates for the sCO2 WHR system.

3.3.4.4 CAPEX and OPEX Estimates for Hermetic sCO2 Machinery with AMBs
The previous TEA for the reference hermetic sCO2 system made assumptions for CAPEX ($10M-
$15M) and OPEX ($5k-$50k per year), which factor the entire WHR system cost, including the
bearing systems and the balance of all machinery components, support systems, etc. To extend
the TEA to the new concept that replaces gas bearings with AMBs, it is necessary to simply replace
the portions of CAPEX and OPEX of one technology for the other. Unfortunately, the previous
sources [16, 31, 32] do not provide a CAPEX/OPEX breakdown by component, so these must be
estimated.
Gas bearings have a similar operating principle to dry gas seal (DGS) technology, where a
hydrodynamic film generates pressure that maintains a small (order of 10 micron) gap between
the rotating and non-rotating surfaces. This requires high-precision manufacturing to achieve the
necessary tolerances as well as compliance features that permit dynamic adjustments of the
stator to motion of the rotor. The gas bearings of the reference system [16–20] also incorporate
hydrostatic features, which is also used by some DGS technologies. For the similarities involved,
the CAPEX for gas bearings are assumed to be on the order of DGS technology; it is further
assumed that this does not need to include the equivalent of a DGS supply system. For this study,
the gas bearing CAPEX ranges from $30k-$90k per bearing, accounting for uncertainty in the
estimate. OPEX also has uncertainty, but on the low end, maintenance could be nil since there
essentially is no wear in a non-contact bearing (due to hydrostatic lift). On the high end,
equipment supporting the hydrostatic functionality (boost compressors) would require
maintenance—a rough OPEX estimate is taken as $3k per bearing per year.
An AMB OEM was consulted to estimate CAPEX and OPEX for the AMBs. They considered
actuators with similar load capacity requirements from their existing experiences and products,
adjusted for load and size scaling to account for manufacturing cost impacts, etc. The minimum
estimates for CAPEX and OPEX (approximately $25k and $300/year per bearing, respectively)
include the actuators, sensors, backup bearings, cables and pass-through fittings, controller, and
backup power supply. The maximum estimates are simply a 4x factor to account for uncertainty
for high-temperature materials and other considerations for pressures and temperatures beyond
the OEM’s experience.
Table 54 summarizes the estimates used for the subsequent analysis. Item ‘a’ provides the total
original CAPEX and OPEX for the reference system, items ‘b’ and ‘c’ represent the current
estimates for the gas bearing systems, ‘d’ and ‘e’ represent the current estimates for the AMB
systems, and item ‘f’ lists the total estimate for the total CAPEX and OPEX for the new system
with AMBs. Simply, the values of ‘f’ are calculated by subtracting ‘b’ and ‘c’ (for all bearings) from
‘a’, then adding ‘d’ and ‘e’ (for all bearings). The low and high estimates encompass the extremes
of the combinations of the input data. Overall, the resulting system CAPEX and OPEX values (item
f) extend the original low and high estimates (item a)—on the high end, CAPEX is increased 4%
and OPEX is increased about 20%.

3.3.5 Subtask 3.4 – Technology Maturation Plan
This project demonstrated that there is still more research needed before AMBs could be
deployed commercially for high temperature CO2 conditions. More attention needs to be given
to the study of the core materials of the AMBs, Hiperco and 17-4 PH, which do not have readily-
available material data at the operating conditions for this application. The current project also
focused on conceptual-level design, so further detailed design effort is required. The following
are recommended for future research.
A broader material testing campaign and literature review is required. The structural and
magnetic properties of the Hiperco alloys and 17-4 PH need to be better understood at higher
temperatures. The stability of these material properties after long-term exposure in sCO2 also
needs to be studied in conjunction with shorter-term measurements to understand the real-word
viability of these materials. A larger test-matrix of potential lamination coatings should also be
studied for both corrosion resistance and electrical insulation integrity—coatings were shown to
improve oxidation resistance in most conditions and more thorough search may reveal even
better solutions. Future studies should also examine the microstructures of as-received material
specimens, with particular attention to the coating-substrate interface, to differentiate the
influence of intrinsic coating defects from that of environmental exposure. Electrical insulation
integrity was not a focus of this project, and understanding it is critical to preventing eddy
currents in the radial AMB laminations. In addition, auxiliary bearing coating materials should
also be studied for sCO2 compatibility and relative performance to the coatings of the current
project.
The AMBs designed for this project should be updated with more accurate material data. AMB
resizing efforts should be made with the updated magnetic data, and this will, in turn, lead to
further structural analysis with updated structural properties. In addition, a cooling system
should be developed for the thrust AMB, which was identified to be particularly challenged by
the 540°C design condition due to high centrifugal stresses. Since the rotating disk is the
component requiring cooling, it is anticipated that a conventional thermal management strategy
could be employed, where lower-temperature CO2 flows in the bearing cavity with seals
controlling interaction with higher temperatures. Auxiliary bearings also should be designed with
an appropriate coating material, and drop simulations should be incorporated in the final rotor-
AMB system designs to confirm adequate performance (transient dynamic response and
prediction of number of survivable events). As previously noted, controller design is an important
system-level development that was not considered in the present scope. Controller design is
anticipated to be fairly conventional, though complexities include flexible body control, potential
need for nonlinear force control, and the need for gain scheduling for temperature.
The above development would still be categorized as TRL-3 to TRL-5 for materials. Physical testing
is needed to advance the bearing design beyond TRL-3 and further. Radial and thrust AMB
designs should be tested at the required temperature, speed, load, and sCO 2 environment for
this application. Component-level testing is recommended to validate the model parameters and
controller performance for the range of conditions (up to TRL-4). Testing should also include
longer-term endurance testing for creep and fatigue failure and follow-up performance testing

                                         Page 116 of 123

to study the stability of the AMB properties over time (up to TRL-5). Successful completion of
component-level testing should be followed by rotor-bearing system testing (up to TRL-6).

3.3.6 Task 3.0 Summary
Radial and thrust AMBs concept designs were matured with higher-fidelity analyses including FEA
to validate structural and magnetic performance. The radial AMB was shown to be feasible for
the design temperature of 540°C, though meeting the precise design objectives for material
strength will require additional iteration in detailed design. The thrust AMB was particularly
challenged by the 540°C temperature due to high stresses, and the concept was modified in
several significant ways to arrive at a feasible design for the application. Notably, the disk
material was changed from Hiperco 50A to 17-4 PH (improved trade of structural to magnetic
performance), the topology was changed from a C-core to an E-core (able to reduce size, lower
stress), the disk attachment was changed from a sleeve to an integral hub with Hirth (smaller
inner diameter, lower stress), and the design temperature ultimately had to be reduced to 315°C
(increased strength). The reduced design temperature for the thrust AMBs means that a cooling
strategy will need to be incorporated for 540°C environments; this design aspect was not pursued
in the present scope.
TEA from the reference machine study was recreated as a baseline for re-evaluation with a
change from process-lubricated bearings to AMBs. With ranges of estimates for CAPEX and OPEX,
AMBs were shown to be comparable in performance to process-lubricated bearings, still showing
a notable improvement over conventional machinery architecture with oil-lubricated bearings.
Finally, the technology maturation plan identified the need to further design iteration, which also
includes additional TRL-3 to TRL-5 material research to confirm material properties for design
conditions. A design and test program is recommended to demonstrate TRL-4 to TRL-5 with
component-level AMB testing, including long-term exposure performance testing, prior to rotor-
bearing system testing up to TRL-6.

4 SIGNIFICANT ACCOMPLISHMENTS AND CONCLUSIONS
Conceptual designs for radial and thrust AMBs were produced on this project, targeting a
hermetically-sealed sCO2 machinery WHR application for sizing and loads, and choosing a target
design temperature of 540°C useful for high-temperature sCO2 turbines for CSP applications.
Conceptual designs were initially developed for multiple radial and thrust AMBs with
spreadsheet-based calculations before selecting one of each to develop further using FEA for
magnetic and structural performance. It was found that the radial AMB at 540°C was feasible
from a conceptual design standpoint, but the thrust AMB needed to be limited to 315°C for high-
speed operation. The decision for a reduced-temperature thrust AMB was the result of several
significant conclusions: 1) Hiperco 50A, originally chosen for good magnetic performance at high
temperature, had insufficient strength for high-speed operation, so it was replaced with 17-4 PH.
2) The reduced magnetic performance from 17-4 PH yielded a larger bearing size, reducing the
strength margin. 3) This ultimately led to a creative design implementing a more-compact E-core
topology, compared to the original (conventional) C-core, and an integral shaft-disk with Hirth
joint connection. These conceptual designs are unique for the size and temperature in CO2,
relevant for megawatt-scale CSP applications.
Long term, high temperature test data was generated for several materials, filling a void in the
current body of literature. Oxidation and magnetic performance measurements were produced
for PM materials (Alnico 5-7C, Alnico 9C, and SmCo) with and without nickel-coating for
environments of high-temperature CO2 up to 550°C at atmospheric pressure and 450°C at 103
bar for up to 6,000 hours. Oxidation measurements were also produced for Hiperco 50, a SM
material relevant for AMB laminations, with and without C5 coating. Comparisons were also
made for 450°C and 550°C, atmospheric pressure air exposures up to 5,000 hours. Results
generally show that coatings can be effective at improving oxidation resistance of the bare
materials, and Alnicos generally outperformed SmCo after high-temperature exposure.
Measured oxidation rates of Alnico magnets were comparable to oxidation rates of nickel alloy

In addition to technical feasibility demonstrated by the design, economic feasibility was

5 PATH FORWARD
The technology maturation plan listed several recommendations for further research and
development. First, broader material testing is required to characterize the structural and
magnetic properties of the Hiperco alloys and 17-4 PH, the core materials of the AMBs. Corrosion
resistance and electrical insulation integrity for lamination coatings, including others not included
in the current project, should also be studied. This material data is not readily-available for the
conditions targeted by these turbomachinery applications and still poses a notable technical risk.
Second, AMB actuator designs should be updated with the improved material data, also
expanding scope to include controller development, which may require nonlinear force control
and temperature-dependent gain scheduling. Finally, a test program is recommended to
demonstrate TRL-4 to TRL-5 with component-level AMB testing, including long-term exposure
performance testing, prior to rotor-bearing system testing up to TRL-6.
