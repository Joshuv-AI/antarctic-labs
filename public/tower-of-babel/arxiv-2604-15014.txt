Runtime-efficient zero-noise extrapolation from mixed physical and logical data
D. V. Babukhin, W. V. Pogosov

Abstract
Partial quantum error correction and quantum error mitigation are expected to coexist in the pre-fault-tolerant regime, yet the resource advantage of combining them remains insufficiently quantified. We study zero-noise extrapolation constructed from mixed datasets that contain a small number of error-corrected data points together with data obtained without error correction. The low-noise logical points anchor the extrapolation, while the higher-noise physical points enlarge the noise baseline at a much smaller runtime cost. Under a simple model in which error correction suppresses the effective gate error rate from p p to γ ​ p \gamma p , we derive the variance of the zero-noise estimator and compare the physical runtime required to reach a target precision. For Richardson extrapolation, the mixed-data strategy reduces variance amplification and can lower the required physical runtime by several orders of magnitude when γ ≲ 0.1 \gamma\lesssim 0.1 . As a proof of principle, we apply the method to digital quantum simulation of a six-spin transverse-field Ising model and find that mixed physical/logical datasets yield lower-variance zero-noise estimates and outperform extrapolation based only on error-corrected data in the parameter regime studied here. These results identify hybrid error correction and error mitigation as a practical route to resource-efficient quantum computation before full fault tolerance.

Introduction

Quantum error correction provides the long-term route to scalable and reliable quantum computing, but its full benefits require substantial overhead in qubits, gates, and control operations. Recently, several examples of running quantum error correction codes on real devices have been demosntrated

In the intermediate pre-fault-tolerant regime, some amount of error correction may already be available, yet the residual logical noise is still non-negligible and logical circuits remain expensive to execute. This raises a practical question: how should partial error correction be used before fully fault-tolerant computation becomes available?

Quantum error mitigation offers a complementary answer. Rather than protecting the full quantum state throughout the computation, error-mitigation protocols use additional noisy circuit executions and classical postprocessing to infer less noisy expectation values. Over the last few years, these methods have become a central part of near-term quantum computing research

Quantum error correction and quantum error mitigation therefore attack the same problem at different levels: the former modifies the encoding and operations on the device, whereas the latter modifies the way noisy data are processed. Progress in mitigation can thus complement progress in hardware, much as sophisticated statistical analysis complements technically demanding experiments in other areas of physics, for example, high-energy physics

Hybrid use of quantum error correction and mitigation is therefore especially appealing in the pre-fault-tolerant regime. Recent works have already shown that combining the two can improve the effective performance of quantum computations and reduce resource requirements

In this paper we address that question for zero-noise extrapolation (ZNE), one of the basic quantum error-mitigation techniques

We study a mixed-data strategy in which a small number of low-noise data points are obtained with partial error correction, while the remaining points are obtained without error correction at larger noise levels. The resulting dataset combines a low-noise anchor with a wider noise lever arm. We show that, for the extrapolation families considered here, this reduces the variance amplification of the zero-noise estimator. At the same time, because logical circuits are typically much slower than physical ones, replacing most error-corrected points by uncorrected ones can substantially reduce the physical runtime needed to reach a target estimator variance. This consideration is particularly relevant for platforms in which long circuit execution times are themselves a major bottleneck

II
Error correction and error mitigation

Before we proceed with the main idea, let us formulate main ideas behind quantum error correction and error mitigation.

Quantum error correction is a means of preserving coherence of qubit register, which is physically an open quantum system. The essence of quantum error correction is building logical qubits, composed of several physical qubits, thus introducing redundancy in storage of information via emerging a logical qubit in a high-dimensional space. Through measuring the logical qubits with specific observables, called syndromes, we can check if there was an error occurred on any of physical qubits inside the logical qubit, and where these errors occurred. After measuring all syndromes of an error-correction code, one can apply additional gates to compensate the occurred errors. If the noise level of physical qubits is less than a code-dependent threshold error value (provided by “threshold theorem”), then using the error-correction code leads to logical qubits with decreased (possibly to any desired value) error rate of logical gates. The price of using quantum error correction codes - we need many physical qubits (
10^{5}-10^{6}
for classically-intractable physical computations
14
).

Quantum error mitigation is an alternative approach to compensate effects of quantum noise during computation. Contrary to error correction codes, which allow to increase the fidelity of a quantum state of the qubit register, error mitigation is targeted to reduce noise-induced error in quantum observable, which is measured after running a target quantum circuit. This reduction of error is achieved via additional runs of target quantum circuit (possibly modified in some way) and classical postprocessing of measured results. The price of using quantum error mitigation is the need to make more runs of quantum device to obtain the accurate estimates of observable values.

While quantum error correction provides a path to achieve fault-tolerant quantum computing - a regime, where error rate of logical gates can be reduced to any desired negligible value - a transition to fault tolerance is not immediate. Building more physical qubits with controllable scaling of physical gate errors is the main technological challenge in the field of quantum computing nowadays. Different physical platforms follow their own pace of development
8
;
1
;
19
, but it is obvious that reaching the number of more than
10^{5}
physical qubits with below-threshold gate error rate is a moment of distant future. Until then, there will be only a partial error correction available, so gate error rate can be reduced to a small, but non-negligible value due to the shortage of physical qubits. During this period of pre-fault-tolerant quantum computing, we need to use quantum error mitigation methods along with available error correction to achieve the best possible results with available devices. during transition to the fault-tolerant quantum computing regime
26
.

III
Main result

III.1
The sketch of idea

The main idea we want to illustrate is the following: we can build a zero-noise extrapolation using data points, some of which are obtained using qubits without quantum error correction - i.e., physical qubits - and other are obtained using logical qubits for some error correction code. If we measure the same observable and run the same quantum circuit, the difference between the two types of data points is the overall level of noise - it is an order of magnitude lower for error-corrected qubits comparing to physical qubits. This allows us to build extrapolation on larger domain, which leads to more accurate zero-noise observable estimates. Before we provide a general formulation of this idea, we provide a geometric motivation, which is depicted in Fig.
1
on a qualitative level.

Figure 1
:

\mathcal{O}(\lambda)
. Green dots represent the estimates of the target observable based on the finite sample, and red segments represent the standard deviations of the estimates.

are the specified noise levels.

Consider the case where we compute a target observable on a noisy quantum processor at two noise levels, denoted by
\lambda_{A}
and
\lambda_{B}
,
\lambda_{A}<\lambda_{B}
. Since the observable is estimated over a finite number of quantum processor runs
N_{shots}
, the two resulting estimates have standard deviation
\sigma\sim\frac{\sigma_{s}}{\sqrt{N_{shots}}}
, where
\sigma_{s}
is a single-shot standard deviation. For the two resulting values we construct a linear extrapolation to zero noise for the dependence of the observable
\mathcal{O}
on the noise level
\lambda
. Within the standard deviation, any line passing through the interval
[\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\lambda)-\sigma,\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\lambda)+\sigma]
(where
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\lambda)
is an estimate of the value observed on a finite sample) is an acceptable extrapolating line.
Let us consider the extreme cases of two extrapolating lines passing through two pairs of points
(\lambda_{A},\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\lambda_{A})-\sigma)=A_{-}
and
(\lambda_{B},\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\lambda_{B})+\sigma)=B_{+}
, and
(\lambda_{A},\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\lambda_{A})+\sigma)=A_{+}
and
(\lambda_{B},\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\lambda_{B})-\sigma)=B_{-}
(see Fig.
1
). We can derive an upper bound on the standard deviation of the estimate of the observable
\mathcal{O}
at zero noise from geometric principles (see Appendix
A
):

std[\mathcal{O}(0)]=\frac{\sigma_{s}}{\sqrt{N_{shots}}}\frac{\lambda_{B}+\lambda_{A}}{\lambda_{B}-\lambda_{A}}

(1)

Now let us consider two cases of noise level scales
\lambda
:

1.

We obtain two data point from qubits with quantum error correction codes, so the overall level of noise of each of data point is low. Let us say that the target circuit consists of
n
, and each gate has an error probability
\gamma p
, where
p
is an error probability of gate without error correction (on physical qubits) and
\gamma<1
is a noise suppression prefactor from error correction code. We take two levels of noise as
\lambda_{B}=2\gamma np
and
\lambda_{A}=\gamma np
.
It that case the standard deviation of the zero-noise estimate is

std[\mathcal{O}(0)]_{1}=\frac{\sigma_{s}}{\sqrt{N_{shots,1}}}\frac{2\gamma np+\gamma np}{2\gamma np-\gamma np}=3\frac{\sigma_{s}}{\sqrt{N_{shots,1}}}

(2)

Here we explicitly denote a number of shots
N_{shots,1}
for this specific choice of noise levels
\lambda_{A}
and
\lambda_{B}
;

2.

We obtain two data points, but now we use different types of qubits - one data point comes from qubits with quantum error correction, while the other data point comes from physical qubits. The two kind of data points have different overall noise levels: the error-corrected point has a level
\lambda_{A}=\gamma np
and the physical-qubits point has a level
\lambda_{B}=np
. The standard deviation then reads

std[\mathcal{O}(0)]_{2}=\frac{\sigma_{s}}{\sqrt{N_{shots,2}}}\frac{np+\gamma np}{np-\gamma np}=\frac{\sigma_{s}}{\sqrt{N_{shots,2}}}\frac{1+\gamma}{1-\gamma}

(3)

Now let us draw the attention to the case, when
std[\mathcal{O}(0)]_{1}=std[\mathcal{O}(0)]_{2}
, i.e. the equivalent precision of the zero-noise estimate. We ask how much more shots do we need to make in case 1 (two error-corrected data points) to reach the precision of the case 2 (on data point is error-corrected and other is not). Using
2
and
3
we derive

N_{shots,1}=9\biggl(\frac{1-\gamma}{1+\gamma}\biggl)^{2}N_{shots,2}\approx 9N_{shots,2}\quad(\gamma\ll 1).

(4)

We can see that when the error correction is effective (
\gamma\ll
1, so the logical gate error is much less that physical gate error), we need effectively less number of shots per data point to reach the equivalent levels of zero-noise estimate precision for combined data from error-corrected and non-error-corrected qubits. That is, using combined data leads to a runtime economy.

The decrease of number of shots required is not the only runtime economy, derived from using of combined data. To see the other one, we need to investigate the physical time of running quantum circuit on error-corrected qubits and on physical qubits. In essence, using error correction codes means construction of a single logical qubit from several physical qubits. To run a quantum circuit on logical qubits, we construct logical quantum gates, which are composed of many physical quantum gates. In case of two-qubit gates and non-clifford gates, the logical gate has a greater physical time of running, because physical gates in a logical gate do not run in parallel
9
.
If we denote
\tau_{logical}
a physical time, required to run a quantum circuit on logical (error-corrected) qubits, and denote
\tau_{physical}
a physical time to run a quantum circuit on physical qubits that
\tau_{physical}\ll\tau_{logical}
.

Now consider the two cases of zero-noise extrapolation which we discussed before. To construct an estimate in the case 1, we need to spend

\tau_{case1}=2N_{shots,1}\tau_{logical}

(5)

physical time, while for an equivalent estimate in case 2 we need

\tau_{case2}=N_{shots,2}\tau_{logical}+N_{shots,2}\tau_{physical}\approx N_{shots,2}\tau_{logical}

(6)

physical time. Comparing the two time estimates and using
4
, we can derive a relation

\tau_{case1}=\frac{2N_{shots,1}}{N_{shots,2}}\tau_{case2}=18\tau_{case2}.

(7)

We see that from a perspective of physical runtime of a target quantum circuit, we obtain an order-of-magnitude reduction when we use combined data.

In the following section we provide a general description of this phenomenon for the zero-noise extrapolation with polynomials of arbitrary order.

III.2
Extrapolation with combined data

Before formulating the data combination method for general case, we need to elaborate on the noise scaling via quantum error correction. As a noise model of noisy quantum computing we consider here a simple model, where every gate in the circuit can work errorless with probability
1-p
and work with error with probability
p
. The overall noise in a quantum circuit with
n
gates we denote as
\lambda=np
, which is a typical approach in the quantum error mitigation literature
4
.
When we use quantum error correction code, we effectively reduce the gate error to the level of logical qubit

p_{L}=cp^{2}

(8)

where
c
is an upper bound for the number of 2-error events during the “correction - operation - correction” cycle
6
. The error correction is successful if
p_{L}<p
, which means
cp<1
. The limiting case, when error correction code does nothing, is defined with equality sign, and defines the threshold probability of the code:

cp_{th}=1.

(9)

Using this limiting case, we rewrite equation for logical gate rate (
8
) in such a form that clearly shows the reduction of the gate errors from using error correction:

p_{L}=\frac{p}{p_{th}}p=\gamma p\quad\gamma=\frac{p}{p_{th}}\leq 1

(10)

We consider equation (
8
) as our model of quantum error correction in this work.

Now let us explicitly formulate zero-noise extrapolation with combined data.
To make zero-noise extrapolation with combined data points we need to collect a data set of measured observable values from a noisy quantum processor.
This data set data set should consist of
N_{0}
points from a processor without quantum error correction and
N_{1}
points from a processor with quantum error correction.
Each of
N_{0}
(
N_{1}
) points corresponds to a result of running the same quantum circuit
U
with a scaled level of overall quantum noise (e.g., via identity insertion

).
Then we use these

\mathcal{O}(\lambda)
(e.g., a
N

\mathcal{O}(0)
.

This estimate is characterized with variance
Var[\mathcal{O}(0)]
. The variance depends on the function we use for extrapolation and on the noise levels where we obtain each of
N
data points. Using error correction code as a means of control for the noise levels, we obtain
N_{0}
points on qubits without quantum error correction and
N_{1}
points with error correction. If we use the same number of shots
N_{shots}

\sigma_{s}^{2}

Var[\mathcal{O}(0)]=\frac{\sigma_{s}^{2}}{N_{shots}}\#.

(11)

Here we denote
\#

Now let us compare variance for two choices of data points - all points from error-corrected qubits and points with different noise scales (combined data). For extrapolation with all data points from error-corrected qubits we write

Var[\mathcal{O}(0)]_{1}=\frac{\sigma_{s}^{2}}{N_{shots,1}}\#_{1}.

(12)

For
N

For

N_{1}
data points with error correction the physical time of gathering this data we denote as

\tau_{2}=N_{0}N_{shots,2}\tau_{physical}+N_{1}N_{shots,2}\tau_{logical}

\tau_{logical}

N_{shots,2}

data points.

Finally, we ask the question: if we demand the variance of zero-noise estimate to be the same in both cases, how the required physical times are related in two cases? Using the demanded equivalence of variances we have

\frac{Var[\mathcal{O}(0)]_{1}}{Var[\mathcal{O}(0)]_{2}}=1=\frac{N_{shots,2}}{N_{shots,1}}\frac{\#_{1}}{\#_{2}}\ \Rightarrow\frac{N_{shots,1}}{N_{shots,2}}=\frac{\#_{1}}{\#_{2}}.

\frac{\tau_{1}}{\tau_{2}}=\frac{N_{shots,1}}{N_{shots,2}}\frac{N\tau_{logical}}{N_{0}\tau_{physical}+N_{1}\tau_{logical}}\approx\frac{\#_{1}}{\#_{2}}\biggl(\frac{N}{N_{1}}\biggl)

V
Conclusion and outlook

We have studied zero-noise extrapolation in the pre-fault-tolerant regime where a limited degree of quantum error correction is available but logical errors remain non-negligible. The central observation is that the data used for extrapolation need not be entirely logical, i.e., obtained using quantum error correction. A more resource-efficient strategy is to anchor the extrapolation with a small number of low-noise error-corrected points and to supplement them with higher-noise points obtained without error correction.
For the extrapolation families analyzed here, this mixed-data construction reduces the variance amplification of the zero-noise estimator and therefore decreases the number of costly logical-circuit executions needed to reach a target precision.

In the case of Richardson extrapolation, this improvement can translate into a substantial reduction of physical runtime. Within the simple noise and runtime model considered here, the gain can reach several orders of magnitude when partial error correction suppresses the effective gate error rate by roughly one order of magnitude or more. Our six-spin transverse-field Ising example supports this picture: mixed physical/logical datasets yield smaller estimator variance than extrapolation based only on error-corrected data of the same order and, in the regime studied here, also improve the final zero-noise estimates.

The present work should be viewed as a resource-optimization result for hybrid QEC-QEM workflows. The analytical part establishes the variance/runtime advantage under explicit assumptions, while the numerical example shows that the corresponding estimate bias need not offset this advantage in a representative simulation. Several extensions are natural. It will be important to generalize the analysis to unequal single-shot variances and optimized shot allocation, more realistic code-dependent logical noise models, and platform-specific runtime estimates that include measurement, reset, and syndrome-extraction overheads. An experimental demonstration on hardware capable of operating in both physical and logical modes would be a particularly valuable next step. More broadly, the results suggest that partial quantum error correction can be used not only to improve individual data points, but also to optimize the design of error-mitigation protocols before full fault tolerance is available.
