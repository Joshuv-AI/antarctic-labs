Abstract
Dynamic radiography techniques have been instrumental in advancing the National Nuclear
Security Administration's (NNSA) mission since WWII. This paper explores the transformative
potential of quantum information science (QIS) to revolutionize dynamic radiography through
enhanced image processing, tomographic reconstruction, statistics and uncertainty quantification
(UQ), and artificial intelligence integration. By leveraging quantum algorithms to extract
previously inaccessible information from existing datasets, this interdisciplinary approach
promises unprecedented insights at the intersection of dynamic imaging, artificial intelligence, and
quantum information technologies.

1. Introduction
Dynamic radiography encompasses techniques, leveraging highly penetrating radiation such as X-
rays, charged particles, and others, captures high-speed, high-resolution images of rapidly evolving
systems, and provides critical data and information for scientific research and national security
mission. NNSA relies on advanced radiographic methods such as the Dual-Axis Radiographic
Hydrodynamic Test (DARHT) facility, proton radiography (pRad), and the developing Scorpius
platform to conduct essential experimental validations of weapon performance and safety
(Crawford et al., 2017). Despite significant technological advances, these techniques face
fundamental limitations in resolution, noise handling, and information extraction, such as
reconstruction of time-dependent 3D movies from an implosion.
Quantum information science (QIS) offers promising new disruptive approaches to overcome
these limitations. Recent breakthroughs in quantum sensing, quantum image processing algorithms,
and quantum machine learning present opportunities to extract previously inaccessible information
from radiographic data (Deffner & Campbell, 2021). Quantum algorithms offer mathematically
provable advantages for certain classes of linear algebra, optimization, and signal-processing
operations that lie at the core of tomographic reconstruction and uncertainty quantification (UQ).
When combined with artificial intelligence (AI) and large data (including scientific knowledge)
techniques, these quantum-enhanced methods could fundamentally transform our ability to
visualize dynamic processes with unprecedented clarity and insight.

2. Current Dynamic Radiography Landscape
2.1 Synchrotron-Based Techniques
Synchrotron radiation sources, such as the advanced photon source upgrade (APS-U) at the
Argonne National Laboratory, provide brilliant, tunable X-ray beams enabling high-resolution
dynamic imaging. APS and others, such as the European Synchrotron Radiation Facility, have
demonstrated temporal resolution of microseconds and spatial resolution approaching 1 μm in
dynamic experiments (Rack et al., 2024). Similarly, the Advanced Photon Source at Argonne
National Laboratory has pioneered techniques for in situ materials characterization during dynamic
events (Jensen et al., 2012), with applications to shock physics, additive manufacturing and others
(Branch et al., 2019, Dattelbaum et al., 2024).

2.2 DARHT and Related Technologies
The DARHT facility represents a cornerstone of NNSA's stockpile stewardship program, utilizing
two perpendicular electron accelerators to generate intense X-ray pulses with an end point energy
up to 20 MeV for radiography of imploding materials. These systems achieve 60-nanosecond
temporal resolution, capturing crucial data on material hydrodynamics under extreme conditions.
However, beam stability, detector limitations, and reconstruction algorithms currently constrain
the extractable information.

2.3 Proton Radiography
Proton radiography (pRad) leverages high-energy protons to image dense materials with sensitivity
to both density and electromagnetic fields (King et al., 1999, Schaeffer et al., 2022). Charged
particle radiography such as pRad, when coupled to the materials dynamics applications (Clarke
et al., 2013), can generate massive datasets that contain more information than current classical
analysis methods can effectively extract.

2.4 High-power laser facilities
Facilities such as NIF, OMEGA, and other high-energy-density testbeds in the LaserNetUS
integrate optical, x-ray, and particle-based imaging to study extreme states of matter. Their
diagnostics operate at the boundary of available detector technology and computational capability.
Quantum-inspired data processing may enable lower-noise reconstructions, faster uncertainty
propagation, and more efficient fusion of multimodal measurements.

2.5 Scorpius and next-generation radiography facilities
Scorpius represents the next major step in dynamic radiographic capability (Crawford et al., 2017),
providing higher frame rates, greater beam stability, and more sophisticated multi-pulse imaging.
The increase in temporal resolution produces significantly larger datasets, which stresses classical
pipelines. Quantum approaches offer potential pathways to accelerate large-scale inversion, reduce
noise, and enhance interpretability.

3. Quantum Information Science Applications in dynamic radiography

To our knowledge, applications of quantum information science and technology to dynamical
radiography is a largely unexplored open field. Quantum-enhanced imaging refers to techniques
that apply quantum resources, such as entanglement (Wang et al., 2021), squeezed state, quantum
sensing, quantum computing hardware, or quantum-inspired algorithms—to improve
measurement precision or signal extraction (Degen et al., 2017), which is beyond the reach of
classical hardware or algorithms.

3.1 Quantum Enhanced Imaging
Recent developments in quantum metrology have demonstrated that entangled photons can
overcome classical resolution limits. The quantum noise reduction achieved through squeezed
states and N00N states can enhance signal-to-noise ratios beyond classical bounds (Braun et al.,
2018). By implementing quantum illumination protocols, dynamic radiography could potentially
overcome the severe noise limitations encountered in low-dose or high-speed imaging scenarios.
A new theoretical framework based on undetected squeezed photons (Samimi et al., 2025) suggests
that applying quantum principles to radiography data could improve spatial resolution and other
metrics while maintaining the same radiation dose. This could represents a significant advantage
for applications where radiation exposure must be minimized.

3.2 Quantum Algorithms for Image Processing
Quantum algorithms offer exponential advantages for specific computational tasks relevant to
image processing due to the use of superposition of quantum states and inherent parallelism
(Caraiman, 2015). The quantum Fourier transform (QFT), which underlies many quantum
algorithms, provides efficiency gains for filtering and feature extraction in radiographic images
(Nielsen & Chuang, 2022). A recent work (Rohida et al., 2025) proposed a novel quantum
algorithm for edge detection in digital grayscale images, based on the sequency-ordered Walsh-
Hadamard transform, that outperforms QFT with potential applications to noisy images typical of
dynamic radiography.

Quantum principal component analysis (QPCA) represents another promising approach for
dimensionality reduction in the massive datasets produced by facilities like synchrotrons, DARHT
and pRad. Unlike classical PCA, QPCA can process exponentially larger datasets with comparable
computational resources (Lloyd et al., 2014). This capability is particularly valuable for identifying
subtle features in time-series radiographic data.

4. Quantum-Enhanced Tomographic Reconstruction
Tomographic reconstruction from limited projection data represents a significant challenge in
dynamic radiography, often resulting in artifacts and limited resolution. Quantum-inspired
optimization algorithms show promise for overcoming these limitations.

Recent work (Dremel et al., 2025) demonstrated that quantum annealing techniques can be used
in reconstruction from sparse projection data, compared to classical iterative methods (Zhao et al.
2025). The quantum tomographic reconstruction algorithm is less affected by artifacts or noise
than classical algorithms by using the square function of the difference between pixels obtained
by projecting CT images in quantum superposition states and pixels obtained from experimental
data (Ryou et al., 2025). These improvements are particularly pronounced when dealing with the
limited-angle problems common in dynamic experiments where geometry constraints limit
projection angles.
Quantum-inspired tensor network methods have also shown promise for representing high-
dimensional tomographic data with dramatically reduced memory requirements (Cichocki et al.,
2017). By exploiting quantum entanglement-like correlations in image data, these approaches
enable more efficient handling of the 4D (3D space + time) datasets typical in dynamic radiography.

5. AI Integration with Quantum Methods

5.1 Hybrid Quantum-Classical Neural Networks
The integration of quantum computing with neural networks creates powerful new tools for image
analysis. Hybrid quantum-classical neural networks leverage quantum processors for specific
computational bottlenecks while maintaining the trainability of classical networks (Havlíček et al.,
2019). Applied to dynamic radiography, these models demonstrate enhanced ability to identify
features in noisy data and generalize from limited training examples.
Research published in Nature Machine Intelligence (Senokosov et al., 2024) showed that quantum
convolutional neural networks can identify subtle material interfaces in simulated dynamic
radiography data with accuracy exceeding classical CNN approaches by approximately 15%.

5.2 Quantum Machine Learning for Anomaly Detection
Anomaly detection is critical for identifying unexpected phenomena in dynamic experiments.
Quantum machine learning approaches, particularly quantum support vector machines and
quantum k-means clustering, have demonstrated superior performance for identifying rare events
in high-dimensional data (Schuld & Petruccione, 2021).
Radiographic experiments at Los Alamos National Laboratory and elsewhere could benefit from
quantum-inspired image processing using tensor network and other advanced algorithms for
enhanced resolution and defect detection (Allegra, 2025).

6. Implementation Challenges and Disruptive Research Directions
While quantum approaches show immense promise, significant challenges remain before practical
implementation. Current quantum hardware suffers from noise limitations and qubit counts
insufficient for processing full radiographic datasets. Near-term applications will likely focus on
quantum-inspired classical algorithms and hybrid approaches that target specific computational
bottlenecks (Preskill, 2018).
Development of specialized quantum algorithms tailored to radiographic data characteristics
represents a key research direction. A major obstacle is due to the inefficiency of loading classical
data into quantum processors, which motivate direct coupling of quantum sensing to quantum
information processing (Wang, et al. 2025).

6.1 Hardware Maturity
Current quantum computing and sensing hardware are limited by noise, decoherence, qubit count,
and connectivity. Research opportunities include:

 • Benchmarking near-term devices for imaging-relevant workloads.
 • Developing error-mitigation strategies tailored to radiographic inversion and UQ.
 • Exploiting quantum-inspired classical algorithms as intermediate solutions.
6.2 Algorithm Scalability and Robustness
Many quantum algorithms provide asymptotic advantages but face practical constraints:
 • Ensuring sparsity, condition number, and matrix-encoding requirements match
 radiographic models.
 • Designing hybrid workflows that reduce quantum resource demand.
 • Quantifying robustness against experimental noise and non-ideal detector responses.

6.3 Integration with Existing Radiographic Pipelines
QIS methods must be interoperable with real-world systems:
 • Interfaces with data formats from DARHT, pRad, and Scorpius.
 • Incorporation into UQ pipelines and Bayesian frameworks.
 • Validation using archival datasets, synthetic campaigns, and cross-platform comparisons.
6.4 Interdisciplinary teaming
A successful QIS–radiography program requires expertise in physics, statistics, numerical
algorithms, quantum computing, and AI. Establishing cross-disciplinary working groups, training
initiatives, and collaborative testbeds will be essential.

7. Conclusion
The integration of quantum information science, artificial intelligence, and dynamic radiography
presents a transformative opportunity for NNSA's mission-critical imaging capabilities. By
applying quantum-enhanced algorithms to image processing and tomographic reconstruction,
researchers can extract previously inaccessible information from existing datasets while improving
the design of next-generation imaging systems.
This interdisciplinary frontier demands collaborative research spanning quantum information
theory, advanced imaging, and artificial intelligence. As quantum technologies mature and
algorithmic approaches evolve, dynamic radiography stands to benefit from a genuine quantum
leap in capabilities, enabling unprecedented insights into dynamic processes critical to scientific
discovery and national security.
