Reply to: Comments on “Effectiveness of Binaural Beats in Reducing Anxiety in Alcohol Withdrawal Patients: A Single-group Pre-post Interventional Study”
Authors: Prithiviraj R, Meinaakshi Coumaravelou, Arun Selvaraj, Souganya Vijayan, Reenaa Mohan
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We thank the commentators for their insightful comments on our article, “Effectiveness of binaural beats in reducing anxiety in alcohol withdrawal patients: A single-group pre-post interventional study.” 1 We appreciate their interest in our work and their constructive observations. Our study aimed to provide early evidence on how effective binaural beats are in reducing anxiety for patients going through alcohol withdrawal in a resource-limited setting. Below, we offer our response to their comments.

Discussion

We agree with the authors that alcohol withdrawal is a changing clinical state marked by fluctuations in symptom severity within short time frames. 2 It is important to note that some of the observed reduction in anxiety may relate to the natural course of withdrawal rather than just the effect of binaural beats. This is a significant limitation in single-group pre-post designs, which we acknowledged in our manuscript. 1 To reduce variability, we took several methodological steps. We conducted anxiety assessments and the intervention before starting pharmacological treatment to limit any effects from medication. We standardized the intervention setting by using the same isolated room, noise-canceling headphones, and consistent intervention duration for all participants. 2 Furthermore, withdrawal severity was systematically assessed using the Clinical Institute Withdrawal Assessment for Alcohol Scale-Revised (CIWA-Ar),³ which helped ensure uniform categorization of participants based on withdrawal severity.

The authors also make a valid point about regression to the mean. In our study, participants with severe withdrawal had higher baseline CIWA-Ar and Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale (HAM-A) scores and showed greater anxiety reductions after the intervention. While regression to the mean might partly account for this, it is also possible that individuals with more significant physiological and autonomic arousal from severe withdrawal responded better to calming auditory interventions. Nonetheless, we agree that randomized controlled trials with comparison groups are essential to clarify the specific effect of the intervention.

We chose the HAM-A for its wide acceptance as a clinician-rated measure of anxiety used in psychiatric practice. 4 We consistently applied the same assessment method across participants to minimize inter-rater and procedural differences. We recognize that there is limited evidence regarding its retest reliability over very short intervals in alcohol withdrawal populations. Future studies could benefit from incorporating both subjective anxiety scales and objective physiological markers, such as heart rate, blood pressure, galvanic skin response, and electroencephalographic measures.

We appreciate the comment regarding potential confounders such as duration and quantity of alcohol use and time since last intake. Assessments were conducted during withdrawal, before pharmacological intervention, under standard protocols. Patients with psychiatric comorbidities, complicated withdrawal, and other substance dependence were excluded. However, residual confounding cannot be excluded. Future studies incorporating laboratory screening, including urine toxicology, would strengthen findings.

We understand the concerns about our sampling method. Although we described the sampling procedure as simple random sampling, participants were actually recruited consecutively from eligible patients during the study period. We recognize that this approach might better fit the description of consecutive or convenience sampling, which could affect generalizability.

We also agree that estimating sample size based on effect size would be better for future interventional trials. However, since there have been no prior studies on binaural beats in alcohol withdrawal populations, we used a prevalence-based estimation during protocol development to support this exploratory research.

Regarding the observation about our objectives going beyond the primary aim, we included exploratory analyses to examine factors associated with changes in anxiety. This was done to understand potential predictors of intervention response better and to inform future hypothesis-driven research.

We appreciate the authors’ acknowledgment of the study’s originality, repeatable intervention protocol, and potential importance in settings with limited resources. We hope this initial work encourages more controlled studies on non-drug interventions in managing alcohol withdrawal.

Conclusion

The comments offer methodological insights that help contextualize our results. Our research provides preliminary data indicating that binaural beats can have an anxiolytic impact for the short term in people suffering from acute alcohol withdrawal if they serve as a non-pharmacological adjuvant therapy. Nevertheless, certain issues connected to the limitations of the sample design, regression to the mean, brief test period, and possible confounders should be taken into account when analyzing the results. Future randomized controlled studies incorporating physiological measures, longitudinal follow-up, and standardized control conditions are required to establish efficacy more conclusively.
