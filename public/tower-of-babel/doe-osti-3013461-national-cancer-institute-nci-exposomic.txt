 1. PURPOSE OF THE NCI EXPOSOMIC LINKAGE PROTOCOL

The purpose of this work is to create point-level linkages of residential history data, which is provided by
the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) program, to air pollution exposure data so that
we can develop longitudinal measures of exposure and investigate their effects on cancer incidence,
treatment response, and survival. The Louisiana, New Jersey, Kentucky, and Iowa registries were
previously linked to LexisNexis residential history data through the National Cancer Institute (NCI) [1].
We will enhance the utility of the existing residential location data by geocoding addresses based on data
from between 1995 and 2024 and spatially linking the locations to air pollution, indoor radon, and the US
Environmental Protection Agency’s (EPA) Risk-Screening Environmental Indicators (RSEI) exposure
data (Figure 1).

 Figure 1. Exposure to toxic releases (RSEI) from source polluters in Utah at the Uber H8 Level.

1.1 TASK 1. GEOCODE ALL RESIDENTIAL ADDRESSES WITH A STANDARD
 GEOCODER

Although residential history data has been geocoded by the NCI, the geocoders used vary by project,
registry, and time period. This leads to variability in positional accuracy, inconsistent spatial resolutions,
and temporal data mismatches. To solve this issue, we re-geocoded the full dataset by using the
ADDRESS (Automated Determination of Detailed Regional and Exact Spatial Segments) geocoder
developed at Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL). All addresses were geocoded to a point location
and to the H8 level.

 1

1.2 TASK 2. CONVERT ENVIRONMENTAL EXPOSURE DATA TO UBER H3 HEXES AT
 THE H8 LEVEL

Data from the following sources will be converted to Uber H3 hexes (Figure 2) [2]:

 The RSEI dataset is based on the Toxics Release Inventory data, which is approximately 4 TB and
 covers the US at an 800 m by 800 m resolution over 26 years [3].
 PM2.5 and ozone concentrations are measured at a daily 1 km by 1 km grid based on a generalized
 additive ensemble model developed by Di et al. [4].
 The indoor radon potential zones are based on EPA data [5].

 Figure 2. Uber H3 hexes scaled by population size for
 obfuscating identifiable information.

These datasets will be spatially joined to Uber H3 Hexes, and we will create estimates of average monthly
exposure for each hex across the observation time (we will include min, mean, median, max, standard
deviation, and number of days at high exposure for PM2.5 standards).

The datasets will also be joined to the LandScan data to perform population masking [6]. This population
masking will allow us to save on storage space by masking any unpopulated areas. It also allows for
efficient rescaling of Uber H3 hexes to aggregate exposure metrics in rural areas without being tied to
administrative boundaries. This process will enable privacy preservation when releasing data if the
Institutional Review Board (IRB) does not allow information to be released at the point level.

1.3 TASK 3. SPATIALLY JOIN RESIDENTIAL HISTORY TO EXPOSURE DATA

Once the exposure data has been converted to Uber H3 hexes, spatiotemporal linkages will be performed.
All addresses (point locations) will be spatially joined to the Uber H3 hexes, and measures of exposure
will be extracted monthly from the start date to the end date of each address. This design will enable us to
construct monthly measures of exposure across the life of a patient (Table 1).

1.3.1 Data stewardship and protection

ORNL will ensure compliance and adherence to ethical standards, IRB, and Data Use Agreement (DUA)
components. ORNL will follow all data privacy policies and procedures outlined in the DUA between
participating cancer registries and UT-Battelle. Data privacy and security are critical to the project. Data
will be housed in ORNL’s Knowledge Discovery Infrastructure (KDI) and CITADEL. The KDI is an
R&D platform for protected data. KDI systems process protected health information (PHI), personally
identifiable information (PII), and other sensitive datasets in accordance with DUAs, Interconnection
Security Agreements, Memorandums of Understanding, or Memorandums of Agreement with data
owners and research project sponsors. KDI meets the NIST 800-53 MODERATE security control
requirements, as defined by NIST 800-66 [7]. All systems are FIPS-199 Moderate for Confidentiality and
Integrity.

ORNL’s KDI and the Oak Ridge Leadership Computing Facility (OLCF) have partnered to provide
CITADEL as a framework of security protocols that enables KDI researchers to harness OLCF resources
for open-science projects that use protected data. The CITADEL framework developed at OLCF allows
PHI, PII, and other types of data that require privacy to be securely used on the OLCF supercomputing
resources and other OLCF systems with approved project allocations. Processes and policies in the
CITADEL framework have been developed to comply with relevant national data protection standards
(e.g., Federal Information Security Modernization Act, Health Information Technology for Economic and
Clinical Health Act, HIPAA).

1.3.2 Expected deliverables

The final product will be a relational database and workflows for data processing, linking, and extraction.
The final product for IRB-approved researchers will be residential history data linked to a range of
environmental exposures (Figure 3). The base tables serve as the foundational data structures within the
CONNECT database by storing data that can be accessed and manipulated for various downstream
research applications. This approach will enable easy retrieval for downstream analyses.

The RSEI table is in long format and will have one row per hex ID/chemical/year. All chemicals were
included in the data delivery (n chemical = 593). This can be narrowed down to the International Agency
for Research on Cancer (IARC) group 1 and 2A chemicals using the chemical_number.

The RSEI data is sparse, and we only store values if an exposure has occurred. Missing values indicate no
exposure occurred during the missing hex ID/chemical/year. This design choice will allow us to conserve
space. In summary, if a hex is missing from this table, then it can be assumed that there was no exposure
to a chemical during the full period of observation.

Built on AERMOD, the RSEI data includes a constant emission rate, which is then averaged and reported
in the column (hex_conc) [9]. No further averaging of the concentration is needed. Table 6 contains the
RSEI data attributes and their descriptions.

 APPENDIX A. CONSTRUCTING THE ANALYTIC FILE
CODE TO CONSTRUCT TRAJECTORIES OF EXPOSURE OF AN INDIVIDUAL

We constructed code to build residential exposure histories across the patient’s life course. This task was
done by creating a residential history table, followed by a complete patient history for time in each hex.
Residential histories for patients were loaded along with environmental datasets and standardized across
column names and data types. Residential histories were expanded to monthly records, and a new column
was created tracking if a patient moved. The first record of a patient in their history is dropped. If a
patient relocated partway through a month, measures were weighted based on the time spent at each
location, with new location’s hex being assigned. The hex change indicator column contains a True value
based on the hex and date (year-month) that the move happens in. The hex_id column is lagged by one
month when a change in hex occurs. If someone moves in February, the February hex_id is carried over
from January. Once the complete patient history table is finalized, the user has the choice to join to the
specific exposure datasets (i.e., Radon, SEDAC, or RSEI). Please see comments with the
Residential_History.ipynb for more details outlined as comments within the code blocks.

 Residential History Generation Process
 Kevin Henry and David Stitchcomb

This document describes an algorithm for generating individual residential histories from a collection of
individual address records. The algorithm generates a continuous address history from address data that
often has multiple address records for the same address at different times, more than one address for an
individual at a particular time, and gaps in time with no active address records. The resulting residential
history timelines describe how people move from one residence to the next over time with no gaps. The
algorithm currently does not attempt to identify people with more than one residence at a particular point
of time or to identify when people move back to a former residence.

Overview. The current algorithm consists of a series of steps to clean input files, identify duplicate
addresses for each individual, consolidate information for the duplicate addresses, and then construct
continuous residential histories. It is implemented as a series of SAS program steps. Duplicate addresses
are identified during an address matching step that can be done using either SAS or Match*Pro. The steps
are summarized in the following flow diagram:

 Figure D-1. Algorithm flow diagram.

In addition to the main algorithm, four optional reporting programs can be used to obtain additional
detailed information about the results. Throughout the SAS processing, warning messages about invalid
values or unexpected situations may be written to the log. These messages start with “***”. Any such
messages should be investigated and resolved.

For these specific programs, we assume that the cancer patient data has been extracted from the registry
DMS system (SEER*DMS for Louisiana) and information from the first tumor that occurred in the study

Address records are cleaned and checked for valid values in the city, state, and ZIP code fields. Each
address is assigned a unique 14-character address ID (AddrID). For LexisNexis addresses, the ID consists
of the 8-character PersID, the characters “LN”, and a 4-character numeric sequence number for this

numeric sequence number for this PersID, and either “Dx” for an address at diagnosis or “Cu” for a
current address.

We assume addresses have been run through a geocoding process although some geocoding results can be
missing. For LexisNexis addresses, the geocoding quality indicator from the geocoding process
(gis_quality) is converted to a text description of geocoding quality level (GeocodeLevel). The first
character of this text string should be a single digit with lower values indicating better quality geocodes.
In later processing, good quality geocodes are identified by comparing this initial digit with the
“GeoLvlMax” parameter that indicates the maximum acceptable geocoding quality level. For the cancer
address records for Louisiana, the gis_quality variable was not available. So, for cancer addresses, the
program assigns a GeocodeLevel value of “3:StreetAddr” if a geocoded latitude and longitude are present
and assigns a GeocodeLevel value of “9:Unknown” otherwise. If a gis_quality variable is available for
cancer addresses, the code should be modified to generate GeocodeLevel text descriptors like those
generated for the LexisNexis addresses.

Address information is stored in separate variables for the street number, the street name, the city name,
the 2-character state abbreviation, and the ZIP code as a 10-character variable with either the 5-digit ZIP
code (left-aligned) or the 9-digit ZIP code (with a hyphen). ZIP codes are compared with cities and states,
and any unexpected combinations are written to an Excel file for review.

A probabilistic match score is generated for the street name part of the address using the SAS SPEDIS,
SOUNDEX, and COMPARE functions. If both addresses have been geocoded, the distance between the
two addresses is calculated. A street name match indicator flag is set based on the match score, the
geographic distance, and the street name threshold parameters (“StrNameHigh”,
“StrNameLowSameCity”, and “StrNameLowDiffCity”). This indicator flag is set to 1 for matches, 0 for

the match score is less than the value of the “StrNameLowDiffCity” parameter, or the addresses are in the
same city, and the match score is less than the value of the “StrNameLowSameCity” parameter.

When dealing with large numbers of addresses, it is often desirable to take an automated approach and
skip the manual review. This can be accomplished by setting the three street name threshold parameters to
the same value. It is still important, however, what value is used. We have found that setting all three
values to 80 works fairly well for most common street names. If the study area includes cities with a large
number of numeric street names like 98th Street or 7th Avenue, we would recommend using a fairly high
value like 90 or even 95 so that only street names with minor differences are accepted as matches.

 APPENDIX E. GEOCODING RESIDENTIAL HISTORY ADDRESSES
 WITH ADDRESS
 Automated Determination of Detailed Regional and Exact Spatial Segments (ADDRESS)
 Josh Grant and Midgie MacFarland

Automated Determination of Detailed Regional and Exact Spatial Segments (ADDRESS) is a custom
geocoding system developed at Oak Ridge National Laboratory and built around a Nominatim instance
that utilizes OpenStreetMap (OSM) data. The system enhances traditional geocoding capabilities through
intelligent address parsing and multilevel geographic matching strategies. It operates as a three-container
architecture: a PostgreSQL database container, a Nominatim geocoding instance, and an API service
container. This containerized approach was deployed on ORNL’s STORM computing environment
within the Knowledge Discovery Infrastructure (KDI) enclave, providing the necessary computational
resources and security protocols for processing protected health information. The ADDRESS geocoding
workflow systematically processes addresses through component decomposition, breaking down each
address into constituent elements (house number, street name, city, state, postal code). The system then
creates permutations of these components, starting with the most specific address string and gradually
expanding scope by systematically dropping components until a successful match is achieved in the OSM
database. When an exact street address match is found, ADDRESS returns precise latitude and longitude

Geocoding Process:

The Louisiana, New Jersey, Kentucky, and Iowa registries have been linked to LexisNexis residential
history data. We utilize the ADDRESS geocoder to create point-level linkages of residential history data.
To ensure data quality and validate geocoding accuracy, we implemented a comprehensive quality
assurance framework. Geocoded coordinates from cancer registry data were compared with ADDRESS-
generated coordinates using haversine distance-based validation metrics. We established four quality
categories based on geographic proximity: Quality 0 (exact point matches with 0 m distance), Quality 1
(points within 50 m distance), Quality 2 (points within 100 m distance), and Quality 3 (points within
1,000 m distance). Additional validation included state-level verification using Federal Information
Processing Standards (FIPS) codes and hexagonal grid matching using Uber H3 level 8 resolution.
Historical ZIP code validation was implemented to address temporal changes in postal boundaries. We
integrated USPS ZIP code crosswalk data spanning 1990−2020 to identify and update obsolete ZIP codes
based on residential history dates. When ADDRESS geocoding resulted in ZIP-code-level matches only,
the system automatically checked for historical ZIP code changes and re-attempted geocoding with
updated postal codes, improving overall geocoding success rates. Data preprocessing included filtering to
exclude addresses dated prior to 1995 and those located outside the US.
