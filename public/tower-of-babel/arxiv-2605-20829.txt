Hamiltonian and Symplectic Tensors in the T-product Algebra
Susana López-Moreno, Taehyeong Kim

Abstract
We study Hamiltonian and symplectic tensor structures in the T-product algebra. We define T-Hamiltonian and T-symplectic tensors and characterize them through their Fourier-domain slices. For T-Hamiltonian tensors we establish the standard block form and the spectral symmetry of T-eigenvalues, while for T-symplectic tensors we derive the inverse and exponential-map properties. Our main result is a constructive T-Williamson normal form for tensors whose Fourier-domain slices are real symmetric positive-definite matrices. We also show that, under the Hermitian symplectic convention adopted here, this decomposition does not extend directly to arbitrary Hermitian positive-definite Fourier-domain slices, and we derive a real-valued recovery criterion under Fourier conjugate symmetry. Numerical experiments verify the construction, exhibit runtime trends consistent with the slice-wise complexity O ⁡ ( p ​ n 3 ) O(pn^{3}) , and illustrate the framework on a Fourier-domain encoding of covariance-matrix families arising in continuous-variable quantum dynamics.

Introduction

Hamiltonian and symplectic structures play a central role in classical mechanics, control theory, and structure-preserving numerical analysis; see, for example, the classical references
19
;
9
;
3
and recent developments in
29
;
23
;
20
.
For matrix-valued problems, these structures encode spectral symmetries, invariants, and canonical forms that are essential both in theory and in computation (
24
;
19
).

In many applications, however, one encounters not a single structured matrix but a family of matrices indexed by time, frequency, or a control parameter (
31
;
1
).
Such problems call for an algebraic setting that stores the family as one object while preserving the structural features of the underlying matrix theory (
6
;
4
).

The CANDECOMP/PARAFAC and Tucker decompositions, introduced by
17
;
7
, are effective for compression and representation, but they lack a closed algebraic structure that directly extends matrix multiplication.
Consequently, they are not suitable for transferring Hamiltonian or symplectic structures to tensor-valued data.
The T-product, given by
16
;
15
, is a better framework for this purpose, as it provides a matrix-mimetic algebraic structure.
Its Fourier-domain representation reduces tensor multiplication to independent matrix multiplications on frontal slices, which allows matrix concepts to be lifted to third-order tensors in a natural and computationally tractable way, as seen in
15
;
11
.

Recent work in the T-product setting has developed the notions of T-Hermitian, T-unitary, and T-positive-definite tensors, together with geometric and computational tools, such as the T-geometric mean (
11
).
Related results for Hamiltonian and symplectic tensors have also been studied in an Einstein-product framework in connection with multilinear control systems (
30
).
Nevertheless, a systematic Hamiltonian-symplectic theory in the T-product algebra, including a tensor analogue of Williamson’s normal form, is still missing.

We formulate T-Hamiltonian and T-symplectic tensors in the T-product algebra and study their structural properties through the Fourier-domain slice representation.
Our main result is a constructive T-Williamson normal form for tensors whose Fourier-domain slices are real symmetric positive-definite matrices.
The construction is done slice-wise in the Fourier domain and yields an algorithm with arithmetic complexity of order
O(pn^{3})
.
We also show that this result does not extend directly
to arbitrary Hermitian positive-definite Fourier-domain slices under the convention
S^{H}JS=J
.

The main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows:

•

We introduce T-Hamiltonian and T-symplectic tensors in the T-product algebra and characterize them by their Fourier-domain slices.

•

We establish basic structural properties, including the block form and spectral symmetry for T-Hamiltonian tensors, and the inverse and exponential-map properties for T-symplectic tensors.

•

We introduce a constructive T-Williamson normal form for tensors whose slices in the Fourier domain are real symmetric positive-definite matrices, and provide an explicit algorithm for its computation. We also derive the associated slice-wise algorithm, and show that an arbitrary Hermitian positive-definite extension fails under the convention
S^{H}JS=J

•

We derive a real-valued recovery criterion under Fourier conjugate symmetry and provide numerical validation, including an example from continuous variable quantum dynamics.

2
Preliminaries

In this paper, tensors are denoted by calligraphic letters (e.g.,
\mathcal{A}
), matrices by bold capital letters (e.g.,
\bm{A}
), and vectors by bold lowercase letters (e.g.,
\bm{a}
).
The space of third-order tensors of size
m\times n\times p
with complex entries is denoted by
\mathbb{C}^{m\times n\times p}
. The frontal slices of a tensor
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n\times p}
are denoted
\bm{A}^{(k)}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}
for
k=1,\dots,p
.
The inner product between two tensors
\mathcal{A}=[a_{ijk}]
and
\mathcal{B}=[b_{ijk}]
in
\mathbb{C}^{m\times n\times p}
is defined as
\langle\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\rangle_{F}=\sum_{i,j,k}\bar{a}_{ijk}b_{ijk}
, where
\bar{a}_{ijk}
is the complex conjugate of
a_{ijk}
.
The Frobenius norm associated with this inner product is expressed as
\left\lVert\mathcal{A}\right\rVert_{F}=\sqrt{\langle\mathcal{A},\mathcal{A}\rangle_{F}}
.

The T-product algebra is based on the mapping of tensors to block circulant matrices (
15
), and the
T-product
of two third-order tensors
\mathcal{A}*\mathcal{B}
can be defined as the operation such that
\operatorname{bcirc}(\mathcal{A}*\mathcal{B})=\operatorname{bcirc}(\mathcal{A})\operatorname{bcirc}(\mathcal{B})
, i.e., as the matrix multiplication of the corresponding block circulant matrices of the tensors.
The block-circulant representation is specially useful because it has a Fourier
diagonalization. After applying the DFT in the third mode, a block-circulant matrix
decomposes into independent matrix blocks, so tensor multiplication is reduced to slice-wise matrix multiplication.
This property is fundamental for the efficient computation of the T-product.
In fact, the DFT is just one of several linear transformations that can be used to define a tensor product;
alternative tensor multiplications can also be defined using other linear maps, as shown by
14
;
13
;
12
.
In this paper, we will exclusively use the definition of the T-product as the result of applying the DFT transform.
The following standard lemma from
16
records the Fourier block-diagonalization of block circulant matrices.

Throughout this paper, we will use
\widehat{\mathcal{A}}
to denote the tensor
\mathcal{A}
in the Fourier domain.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n\times p}
.
The block circulant matrix
\operatorname{bcirc}(\mathcal{A})
can be diagonalized as

\operatorname{bcirc}(\mathcal{A})=(\bm{F}_{p}^{H}\otimes\bm{I}_{m})\operatorname{diag}(\widehat{\bm{A}}^{(1)},\widehat{\bm{A}}^{(2)},\ldots,\widehat{\bm{A}}^{(p)})(\bm{F}_{p}\otimes\bm{I}_{n}),

(2.1)

where
\otimes
denotes the Kronecker product,
\bm{I}_{k}
is the
k\times k
identity matrix, and
\bm{F}_{p}
is the
p\times p
normalized DFT matrix with entries
(\bm{F}_{p})_{ij}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}\omega^{(i-1)(j-1)}
for
\omega=e^{2\pi\sqrt{-1}/p}
. Here,

\widehat{\bm{A}}^{(i)}
denotes the
i
-th frontal slice of
\widehat{\mathcal{A}}
. Following the standard convention in the T-product literature (
16
;
15
), the unitary similarity by
\bm{F}_{p}
produces diagonal blocks equal to the ordinary FFT coefficients of the frontal slices. The diagonal blocks
\widehat{\bm{A}}^{(i)}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}
are therefore the matrices

\widehat{\bm{A}}^{(i)}=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\omega^{(i-1)(j-1)}\bm{A}^{(j)},\quad i=1,\dots,p,

with inverse transformation

\bm{A}^{(j)}=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\omega^{-(i-1)(j-1)}\widehat{\bm{A}}^{(i)},\quad j=1,\dots,p.

(2.2)

Lemma
2.1
provides an equivalent, computationally efficient interpretation of the T-product. Using the block-diagonalization in Lemma
2.1
, the computation of
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{A}*\mathcal{B}
is performed by first converting the spatial-domain tensors
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
into the Fourier-domain tensors
\widehat{\mathcal{A}}
and
\widehat{\mathcal{B}}
. This step is efficiently implemented by applying the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) along each tube of
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
. Next, in the Fourier domain, the T-product becomes a series of independent matrix products on each frontal slice:

\widehat{\bm{C}}^{(i)}=\widehat{\bm{A}}^{(i)}\widehat{\bm{B}}^{(i)},\quad\text{for }i=1,\dots,p.

Finally,
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
is converted back to the spatial domain by the inverse DFT described in Lemma
2.1
,
which is efficiently implemented using the inverse Fast Fourier Transform (IFFT).

We now define other essential tensor operations based on
11
.

Definition 2.2
.

The
identity tensor

\mathcal{I}_{n,p}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n\times p}
is the tensor whose first frontal slice is the identity matrix
\bm{I}_{n}
and whose other frontal slices are zero matrices, such that
\mathcal{A}*\mathcal{I}_{n,p}=\mathcal{A}
for any compatible tensor
\mathcal{A}
.
The
zero tensor

\mathcal{O}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m\times p}
is defined as the tensor whose frontal slices are all the zero matrix.

Definition 2.3
.

For a tensor
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n\times p}
, let the
T-conjugate transpose
of
\mathcal{A}
be denoted by
\mathcal{A}^{H}
.
Then, each
k
-th frontal slice of
\mathcal{A}^{H}
, denoted by
(\bm{A}^{H})^{(k)}
, is defined as follows

(\bm{A}^{H})^{(k)}=\begin{cases}(\bm{A}^{(1)})^{H}&\text{if }k=1\\
(\bm{A}^{(p-k+2)})^{H}&\text{if }k\geq 2\end{cases}

This definition ensures that
\operatorname{bcirc}(\mathcal{A}^{H})=(\operatorname{bcirc}(\mathcal{A}))^{H}
.

Definition 2.4
.

A tensor
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n\times p}
satisfying
\mathcal{A}^{H}=\mathcal{A}
,
\mathcal{A}
is called
T-Hermitian
.
Equivalently,
\mathcal{A}
is T-Hermitian if and only if its block circulant matricization,
\operatorname{bcirc}(\mathcal{A})
, is a Hermitian matrix.

Definition 2.5
.

A tensor
\mathcal{U}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n\times p}
is
T-unitary
if it satisfies:

\mathcal{U}^{H}*\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{U}*\mathcal{U}^{H}=\mathcal{I}_{n,p}.

Definition 2.6
.

A T-Hermitian tensor
\mathcal{M}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n\times p}

\mathcal{M}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n\times p}
is strictly
T-positive-definite
if
\operatorname{tr}(\mathcal{X}^{H}*\mathcal{M}*\mathcal{X})>0
for every nonzero tensor
\mathcal{X}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times 1\times p}
.
Here, the trace operator is
\operatorname{tr}(\mathcal{A})=\operatorname{tr}(\operatorname{bcirc}(\mathcal{A}))
and the product
\mathcal{X}^{H}*\mathcal{M}*\mathcal{X}
yields a
1\times 1\times p
tubal scalar.

Equivalently, for every nonzero
\mathcal{X}
, the Frobenius inner product
\langle\mathcal{X},\mathcal{M}*\mathcal{X}\rangle
is positive.
Furthermore, a T-Hermitian tensor is T-positive-definite if and only if its block circulant matricization
\operatorname{bcirc}(\mathcal{M})
is Hermitian positive-definite, or equivalently, if all of its Fourier-domain slices
\widehat{\bm{M}}^{(i)}
are Hermitian positive-definite matrices.

3
Main Theoretical Framework

We first recall the structured matrix classes that will be lifted to the T-product setting.
Let
\bm{J}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2n}
be the standard symplectic unit matrix

\bm{J}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{0}&\bm{I}_{n}\\
-\bm{I}_{n}&\bm{0}\end{bmatrix}
.
A matrix
\bm{H}\in\mathbb{C}^{2n\times 2n}
is
Hamiltonian
if
(\bm{J}\bm{H})^{H}=\bm{J}\bm{H}
.
A matrix
\bm{S}\in\mathbb{C}^{2n\times 2n}
is
symplectic
if
\bm{S}^{H}\bm{J}\bm{S}=\bm{J}
.
We now define the tensor analogue of the matrix
\bm{J}
.

Definition 3.1
.

The
T-symplectic unit tensor
, denoted
\mathcal{J}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2n\times p}
, is the tensor whose every frontal slice in the Fourier domain is the standard symplectic unit matrix
\bm{J}
:

\hat{\bm{J}}^{(i)}=\bm{J}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{0}&\bm{I}_{n}\\
-\bm{I}_{n}&\bm{0}\end{bmatrix}\quad\text{for }i=1,\dots,p.

Remark 3.2
.

The structure of
\mathcal{J}
in the spatial domain follows directly from its definition in the Fourier domain.
Applying the inverse DFT reveals that its first frontal slice
\bm{J}^{(1)}
is the matrix
\bm{J}
, while all other frontal slices
\bm{J}^{(k)}
for
k>1
are zero matrices.
This makes
\mathcal{J}
a sparse tensor in the spatial domain. In addition, since

\widehat{\bm{J}}^{(i)}\widehat{\bm{J}}^{(i)}=\bm{J}^{2}=-\bm{I}_{2n}\quad\text{for each }i=1,\dots,p,

we also have

\mathcal{J}*\mathcal{J}=-\mathcal{I}_{2n,p}.

With this foundational structure, we can now define T-Hamiltonian and T-symplectic tensors, and then introduce the main result of this paper.

3.1
T-Hamiltonian Tensors

The definition of a T-Hamiltonian tensor is a natural extension of its matrix counterpart, designed to be compatible with the algebraic structure of the T-product.
By enforcing the Hamiltonian property on each Fourier-domain slice, where the T-product behaves as conventional matrix multiplication (
16
), we preserve the essential spectral characteristics.

Definition 3.3
.

A tensor
\mathcal{H}\in\mathbb{C}^{2n\times 2n\times p}
is said to be
T-Hamiltonian
if it satisfies the equation
(\mathcal{J}*\mathcal{H})^{H}=\mathcal{J}*\mathcal{H}
.

This definition implies that the tensor product
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{J}*\mathcal{H}
is T-Hermitian.
The main consequence of this definition is revealed in the Fourier domain.

Theorem 3.4
.

A tensor
\mathcal{H}\in\mathbb{C}^{2n\times 2n\times p}
is T-Hamiltonian if and only if each of its frontal slices in the Fourier domain,
\widehat{\bm{H}}^{(i)}
for
i=1,\dots,p
, is a Hamiltonian matrix.

Proof.

Set

\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{J}*\mathcal{H}
.
By Definition
3.3
, the tensor
\mathcal{H}
is T-Hamiltonian if and only if
\mathcal{K}
is T-Hermitian.

We now map to the Fourier domain. For each
i=1,\dots,p
, the
i
-th frontal slice of
\widehat{\mathcal{K}}
is

\widehat{\bm{K}}^{(i)}=\widehat{\bm{J}}^{(i)}\widehat{\bm{H}}^{(i)}.

By Definition
3.1
, we have
\widehat{\bm{J}}^{(i)}=\bm{J}
for every
i
, and therefore

\widehat{\bm{K}}^{(i)}=\bm{J}\widehat{\bm{H}}^{(i)}.

Next we characterize what
\mathcal{K}
being T-Hermitian means in terms of its Fourier-domain slices. By the definition of a T-Hermitian tensor,
\mathcal{K}
is T-Hermitian if and only if
\operatorname{bcirc}(\mathcal{K})
is Hermitian. Lemma
2.1
gives

\operatorname{bcirc}(\mathcal{K})=(\bm{F}_{p}^{H}\otimes\bm{I}_{2n})\operatorname{diag}(\widehat{\bm{K}}^{(1)},\dots,\widehat{\bm{K}}^{(p)})(\bm{F}_{p}\otimes\bm{I}_{2n}).

Since
\bm{F}_{p}
is unitary, the matrix
\operatorname{bcirc}(\mathcal{K})
is Hermitian if and only if the block diagonal matrix

\operatorname{diag}(\widehat{\bm{K}}^{(1)},\dots,\widehat{\bm{K}}^{(p)})

is Hermitian. The latter condition holds if and only if each diagonal block is Hermitian, that is,

(\widehat{\bm{K}}^{(i)})^{H}=\widehat{\bm{K}}^{(i)}\quad\text{for every }i=1,\dots,p.

Combining these identities, we conclude that
\mathcal{H}
is T-Hamiltonian if and only if

(\bm{J}\widehat{\bm{H}}^{(i)})^{H}=\bm{J}\widehat{\bm{H}}^{(i)}\quad\text{for every }i=1,\dots,p.

By the definition of a Hamiltonian matrix, this is equivalent to saying that each matrix
\widehat{\bm{H}}^{(i)}
is Hamiltonian. This proves the assertion.
∎

This theorem provides a practical way to construct or verify T-Hamiltonian tensors.
A common representation for Hamiltonian matrices involves a specific block structure
5
.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
\mathcal{H}\in\mathbb{C}^{2n\times 2n\times p}
.

\mathcal{H}
is T-Hamiltonian if and only if each Fourier-domain slice
\widehat{\bm{H}}^{(i)}
can be written in the block form
\widehat{\bm{H}}^{(i)}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{A}_{i}&\bm{B}_{i}\\
\bm{C}_{i}&-\bm{A}_{i}^{H}\end{bmatrix}
, where
\bm{A}_{i},\bm{B}_{i},\bm{C}_{i}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
and the matrices
\bm{B}_{i}
and
\bm{C}_{i}
are Hermitian for each
i=1,\dots,p
.

Proof.

Assume first that
\mathcal{H}
is T-Hamiltonian. By Theorem
3.4
, each matrix
\widehat{\bm{H}}^{(i)}
is Hamiltonian. Fix
i\in\{1,\dots,p\}
, and write

\widehat{\bm{H}}^{(i)}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{A}_{i}&\bm{B}_{i}\\
\bm{C}_{i}&\bm{D}_{i}\end{bmatrix},

where
\bm{A}_{i},\bm{B}_{i},\bm{C}_{i},\bm{D}_{i}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
. We compute

\bm{J}\widehat{\bm{H}}^{(i)}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{0}&\bm{I}_{n}\\
-\bm{I}_{n}&\bm{0}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{A}_{i}&\bm{B}_{i}\\
\bm{C}_{i}&\bm{D}_{i}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{C}_{i}&\bm{D}_{i}\\
-\bm{A}_{i}&-\bm{B}_{i}\end{bmatrix}.

Since
\widehat{\bm{H}}^{(i)}
is Hamiltonian, the matrix
\bm{J}\widehat{\bm{H}}^{(i)}
is Hermitian. Hence

\left(\begin{bmatrix}\bm{C}_{i}&\bm{D}_{i}\\
-\bm{A}_{i}&-\bm{B}_{i}\end{bmatrix}\right)^{H}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{C}_{i}&\bm{D}_{i}\\
-\bm{A}_{i}&-\bm{B}_{i}\end{bmatrix}.

Computing the conjugate transpose on the left-hand side gives

\begin{bmatrix}\bm{C}_{i}^{H}&-\bm{A}_{i}^{H}\\
\bm{D}_{i}^{H}&-\bm{B}_{i}^{H}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{C}_{i}&\bm{D}_{i}\\
-\bm{A}_{i}&-\bm{B}_{i}\end{bmatrix}.

Equality of the corresponding blocks yields

\bm{C}_{i}^{H}=\bm{C}_{i},\qquad-\bm{A}_{i}^{H}=\bm{D}_{i},\qquad\bm{D}_{i}^{H}=-\bm{A}_{i},\qquad\bm{B}_{i}^{H}=\bm{B}_{i}.

The second identity gives
\bm{D}_{i}=-\bm{A}_{i}^{H}
, and the first and fourth identities show that
\bm{C}_{i}
and
\bm{B}_{i}
are Hermitian. Therefore

\widehat{\bm{H}}^{(i)}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{A}_{i}&\bm{B}_{i}\\
\bm{C}_{i}&-\bm{A}_{i}^{H}\end{bmatrix},

with
\bm{B}_{i}^{H}=\bm{B}_{i}
and
\bm{C}_{i}^{H}=\bm{C}_{i}
.

Conversely, assume that for each
i=1,\dots,p
we have

\widehat{\bm{H}}^{(i)}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{A}_{i}&\bm{B}_{i}\\
\bm{C}_{i}&-\bm{A}_{i}^{H}\end{bmatrix},

where
\bm{B}_{i}^{H}=\bm{B}_{i}
and
\bm{C}_{i}^{H}=\bm{C}_{i}
. We compute

\bm{J}\widehat{\bm{H}}^{(i)}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{0}&\bm{I}_{n}\\
-\bm{I}_{n}&\bm{0}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{A}_{i}&\bm{B}_{i}\\
\bm{C}_{i}&-\bm{A}_{i}^{H}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{C}_{i}&-\bm{A}_{i}^{H}\\
-\bm{A}_{i}&-\bm{B}_{i}\end{bmatrix}.

Taking the conjugate transpose gives

\left(\begin{bmatrix}\bm{C}_{i}&-\bm{A}_{i}^{H}\\
-\bm{A}_{i}&-\bm{B}_{i}\end{bmatrix}\right)^{H}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{C}_{i}^{H}&(-\bm{A}_{i})^{H}\\
(-\bm{A}_{i}^{H})^{H}&(-\bm{B}_{i})^{H}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{C}_{i}^{H}&-\bm{A}_{i}^{H}\\
-\bm{A}_{i}&-\bm{B}_{i}^{H}\end{bmatrix}.

Using
\bm{C}_{i}^{H}=\bm{C}_{i}
and
\bm{B}_{i}^{H}=\bm{B}_{i}
, we obtain

(\bm{J}\widehat{\bm{H}}^{(i)})^{H}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{C}_{i}&-\bm{A}_{i}^{H}\\
-\bm{A}_{i}&-\bm{B}_{i}\end{bmatrix}=\bm{J}\widehat{\bm{H}}^{(i)}.

Hence
\widehat{\bm{H}}^{(i)}
is Hamiltonian for every
i
. Theorem
3.4
now implies that
\mathcal{H}
is T-Hamiltonian.
∎

The T-product framework allows for a natural extension of eigenvalue problems to tensors.
The study of tensor eigenvalues is an active field, with foundational work on variational approaches and spectral properties established for various tensor formats (
26
;
18
).
Tensor spectral theory has developed into a broad field that deals with various forms of tensor eigenvalue definitions and their properties (
25
).
In this paper, we adopt the T-spectrum induced by the T-Jordan canonical form. By the T-Jordan decomposition theorem introduced in
21
, every tensor
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n\times p}
admits a factorization

\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{P}^{-1}*\mathcal{C}*\mathcal{P},

where
\mathcal{P}
is invertible and
\mathcal{C}
is an F-upper-bi-diagonal tensor; equivalently, each Fourier-domain slice
\widehat{\bm{C}}^{(i)}
is in Jordan canonical form. This leads to the following two possible definitions for T-eigenvalue.

Definition 3.6
.

A
T-eigenvalue
of
\mathcal{A}
is any diagonal entry of the Jordan matrices
\widehat{\bm{C}}^{(i)}
,
i=1,\dots,p
.

Definition 3.7
.

A scalar
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
is a
T-eigenvalue
of a tensor
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n\times p}
if there exists a nonzero tensor
\mathcal{X}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times 1\times p}
(a T-eigenvector) such that

\mathcal{A}*\mathcal{X}=\lambda\mathcal{X}.

Proposition 3.8
.

Definition
3.6
and Definition
3.7
are mathematically equivalent.

Proof.

Assume first that
\lambda
is a T-eigenvalue in the sense of Definition
3.7
. Then there exists a nonzero tensor
\mathcal{X}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times 1\times p}
such that

\mathcal{A}*\mathcal{X}=\lambda\mathcal{X}.

After mapping to the Fourier domain and using the slice-wise form of the T-product, we obtain

\widehat{\bm{A}}^{(i)}\widehat{\bm{x}}^{(i)}=\lambda\widehat{\bm{x}}^{(i)}\quad\text{for every }i=1,\dots,p,

where
\widehat{\bm{x}}^{(i)}
denotes the
i
-th frontal slice of
\widehat{\mathcal{X}}
. Since
\mathcal{X}\neq\mathcal{O}
, the tensor
\widehat{\mathcal{X}}
is also nonzero. Hence, there exists at least one index
k
such that
\widehat{\bm{x}}^{(k)}\neq\bm{0}
. For this index
k
we have

\widehat{\bm{A}}^{(k)}\widehat{\bm{x}}^{(k)}=\lambda\widehat{\bm{x}}^{(k)},

so
\lambda
is an eigenvalue of the matrix
\widehat{\bm{A}}^{(k)}
. By the ordinary Jordan theory for matrices, the eigenvalues of
\widehat{\bm{A}}^{(k)}
are exactly the diagonal entries of its Jordan canonical form
\widehat{\bm{C}}^{(k)}
. Therefore
\lambda
appears among the diagonal entries of one of the matrices
\widehat{\bm{C}}^{(i)}
, and hence
\lambda
is a T-eigenvalue in the sense of Definition
3.6
.

Conversely, assume that
\lambda
is a T-eigenvalue in the sense of Definition
3.6
. Then there exists an index
k\in\{1,\dots,p\}
such that
\lambda
is a diagonal entry of
\widehat{\bm{C}}^{(k)}
. Again by ordinary Jordan theory for matrices,
\lambda
is an eigenvalue of
\widehat{\bm{A}}^{(k)}
. Hence there exists a nonzero vector
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
such that

\widehat{\bm{A}}^{(k)}\bm{v}=\lambda\bm{v}.

We define a tensor
\widehat{\mathcal{X}}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times 1\times p}
by prescribing its frontal slices as

\widehat{\bm{x}}^{(j)}=\begin{cases}\bm{v},&j=k,\\
\bm{0},&j\neq k.\end{cases}

The tensor
\widehat{\mathcal{X}}
is nonzero because
\widehat{\bm{x}}^{(k)}=\bm{v}\neq\bm{0}
. For
j\neq k
we have

\widehat{\bm{A}}^{(j)}\widehat{\bm{x}}^{(j)}=\widehat{\bm{A}}^{(j)}\bm{0}=\bm{0}=\lambda\bm{0}=\lambda\widehat{\bm{x}}^{(j)},

and for
j=k
we have

\widehat{\bm{A}}^{(k)}\widehat{\bm{x}}^{(k)}=\widehat{\bm{A}}^{(k)}\bm{v}=\lambda\bm{v}=\lambda\widehat{\bm{x}}^{(k)}.

Thus

\widehat{\bm{A}}^{(j)}\widehat{\bm{x}}^{(j)}=\lambda\widehat{\bm{x}}^{(j)}\quad\text{for every }j=1,\dots,p.

Applying the inverse DFT to map back to the spatial domain, we obtain

\mathcal{A}*\mathcal{X}=\lambda\mathcal{X},

where
\mathcal{X}
is the result of applying slice-wise the inverse transform of
\widehat{\mathcal{X}}
as in Lemma
2.1
.
Since
\widehat{\mathcal{X}}\neq\mathcal{O}
, also
\mathcal{X}\neq\mathcal{O}
. Therefore
\lambda
is a T-eigenvalue in the sense of Definition
3.7
.
∎

The T-eigenvalues of T-Hamiltonian tensors satisfy the following symmetry.

Theorem 3.9
.

Let
\mathcal{H}\in\mathbb{C}^{2n\times 2n\times p}
be a T-Hamiltonian tensor.
If
\lambda
is a T-eigenvalue of
\mathcal{H}
, then
-\overline{\lambda}
is also a T-eigenvalue of
\mathcal{H}
.

Proof.

Let
\lambda
be a T-eigenvalue of
\mathcal{H}
. By Proposition
3.8
, there exists an index
i\in\{1,\dots,p\}
such that
\lambda
is an eigenvalue of the matrix
\widehat{\bm{H}}^{(i)}
. Set

\bm{H}_{i}=\widehat{\bm{H}}^{(i)}
.
Since
\mathcal{H}
is T-Hamiltonian, Theorem
3.4
shows that
\bm{H}_{i}
is a Hamiltonian matrix, so

(\bm{J}\bm{H}_{i})^{H}=\bm{J}\bm{H}_{i}.

Because
\bm{J}^{H}=-\bm{J}
and
\bm{J}^{-1}=-\bm{J}
, this identity implies

\bm{H}_{i}^{H}\bm{J}^{H}=\bm{J}\bm{H}_{i}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad-\bm{H}_{i}^{H}\bm{J}=\bm{J}\bm{H}_{i},

and, hence,

\bm{H}_{i}^{H}=\bm{J}\bm{H}_{i}\bm{J}.

Therefore,

\bm{J}\bm{H}_{i}\bm{J}^{-1}=\bm{J}\bm{H}_{i}(-\bm{J})=-\bm{J}\bm{H}_{i}\bm{J}=-\bm{H}_{i}^{H}.

Thus
\bm{H}_{i}
and
-\bm{H}_{i}^{H}
are similar, so they have the same spectrum

\operatorname{spec}(\bm{H}_{i})=\operatorname{spec}(-\bm{H}_{i}^{H}).

Since
\operatorname{spec}(\bm{H}_{i}^{H})=\overline{\operatorname{spec}(\bm{H}_{i})}
, we obtain

\operatorname{spec}(\bm{H}_{i})=-\overline{\operatorname{spec}(\bm{H}_{i})}.

Because
\lambda\in\operatorname{spec}(\bm{H}_{i})
, it follows that
-\overline{\lambda}\in\operatorname{spec}(\bm{H}_{i})
. Applying Proposition
3.8
once more, we conclude that
-\overline{\lambda}
is a T-eigenvalue of
\mathcal{H}
.
∎

3.2
T-Symplectic Tensors

Analogous to the T-Hamiltonian case, a T-symplectic tensor is defined to ensure that in the Fourier domain, the frontal slices are symplectic in the matrix sense.
This preserves properties
inherent to symplectic structures.

Definition 3.10
.

A tensor
\mathcal{S}\in\mathbb{C}^{2n\times 2n\times p}
is said to be
T-symplectic
if it satisfies the equation
\mathcal{S}^{H}*\mathcal{J}*\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{J}
.

Moreover, from the previous definition we have the following results in the Fourier domain.

Theorem 3.11
.

A tensor
\mathcal{S}\in\mathbb{C}^{2n\times 2n\times p}
is T-symplectic if and only if each of its frontal slices in the Fourier domain
\widehat{\bm{S}}^{(i)}
, for
i=1,\dots,p
, is a symplectic matrix.

Proof.

Assume first that
\mathcal{S}
is T-symplectic. By Definition
3.10
,

\mathcal{S}^{H}*\mathcal{J}*\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{J}.

Mapping to the Fourier domain and using the slice-wise form of the T-product, we obtain

(\widehat{\bm{S}}^{(i)})^{H}\widehat{\bm{J}}^{(i)}\widehat{\bm{S}}^{(i)}=\widehat{\bm{J}}^{(i)}\quad\text{for every }i=1,\dots,p.

Since
\widehat{\bm{J}}^{(i)}=\bm{J}
for all
i
, the preceding identity becomes

(\widehat{\bm{S}}^{(i)})^{H}\bm{J}\widehat{\bm{S}}^{(i)}=\bm{J}\quad\text{for }i=1,\dots,p.

Therefore each
\widehat{\bm{S}}^{(i)}
is a symplectic matrix.

Conversely, assume that each matrix
\widehat{\bm{S}}^{(i)}
is symplectic. Then,

(\widehat{\bm{S}}^{(i)})^{H}\bm{J}\widehat{\bm{S}}^{(i)}=\bm{J}\quad\text{for }i=1,\dots,p.

Using again the identity
\widehat{\bm{J}}^{(i)}=\bm{J}
, we rewrite this as

(\widehat{\bm{S}}^{(i)})^{H}\widehat{\bm{J}}^{(i)}\widehat{\bm{S}}^{(i)}=\widehat{\bm{J}}^{(i)}\quad\text{for }i=1,\dots,p.

After applying the inverse DFT, these
p
slice-wise identities combine into

\mathcal{S}^{H}*\mathcal{J}*\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{J}.

Hence
\mathcal{S}
is T-symplectic.
∎

T-symplectic tensors possess several key properties that are direct extensions of their matrix counterparts.
We begin with the inverse formula.

Proposition 3.12
.

If
\mathcal{S}\in\mathbb{C}^{2n\times 2n\times p}
is T-symplectic, then it is invertible, and its inverse is given by
\mathcal{S}^{-1}=-\mathcal{J}*\mathcal{S}^{H}*\mathcal{J}
.

Proof.

Define

\mathcal{R}=-\mathcal{J}*\mathcal{S}^{H}*\mathcal{J}.

We first show that
\mathcal{R}
is a left inverse of
\mathcal{S}
. Since
\mathcal{S}
is T-symplectic,

\mathcal{S}^{H}*\mathcal{J}*\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{J}.

Multiplying this identity on the left by
-\mathcal{J}
, we obtain

(-\mathcal{J})*\mathcal{S}^{H}*\mathcal{J}*\mathcal{S}=(-\mathcal{J})*\mathcal{J}.

By associativity of the T-product and the identity
\mathcal{J}*\mathcal{J}=-\mathcal{I}_{2n,p}
from Remark
3.2
, the right-hand side becomes

(-\mathcal{J})*\mathcal{J}=-(\mathcal{J}*\mathcal{J})=-(-\mathcal{I}_{2n,p})=\mathcal{I}_{2n,p}.

Hence,

\mathcal{R}*\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{I}_{2n,p}.

Mapping to the Fourier domain gives

\widehat{\bm{R}}^{(i)}\widehat{\bm{S}}^{(i)}=\bm{I}_{2n}\quad\text{for every }i=1,\dots,p.

Therefore, each matrix
\widehat{\bm{S}}^{(i)}
is invertible and

\widehat{\bm{R}}^{(i)}=(\widehat{\bm{S}}^{(i)})^{-1}.

Consequently,

\widehat{\bm{S}}^{(i)}\widehat{\bm{R}}^{(i)}=\bm{I}_{2n}\quad\text{for every }i=1,\dots,p.

Applying the inverse DFT to this last expression, we obtain

\mathcal{S}*\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{I}_{2n,p}.

Thus
\mathcal{R}
is both a left inverse and a right inverse of
\mathcal{S}
and, therefore,

\mathcal{S}^{-1}=\mathcal{R}=-\mathcal{J}*\mathcal{S}^{H}*\mathcal{J}.

∎

A fundamental connection exists between Hamiltonian and symplectic structures via the matrix exponential. In the T-product setting, we define the tensor exponential by the absolutely convergent T-power series

\exp(\mathcal{H}):=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k!}\mathcal{H}^{*k},\quad\mathcal{H}^{*0}:=\mathcal{I}_{2n,p},

where
\mathcal{H}^{*k}=\overbrace{\mathcal{H}*\mathcal{H}*\cdots*\mathcal{H}}^{k~\text{times}}
, which is consistent with the general framework under the T-product in
11
. Equivalently, its slices in the Fourier domain are obtained by applying the matrix exponential slice-wise

\widehat{\exp(\mathcal{H})}^{(i)}=\exp(\widehat{\bm{H}}^{(i)}),

for
i=1,\dots,p
.
This relationship extends the classical Hamiltonian-symplectic correspondence to the tensor case.

Proposition 3.13
.

If
\mathcal{H}
is a T-Hamiltonian tensor, then
\mathcal{S}=\exp(\mathcal{H})
is a T-symplectic tensor.

Proof.

Let
i\in\{1,\dots,p\}
be fixed. Then, by Theorem
3.4
, the matrix
\widehat{\bm{H}}^{(i)}
is Hamiltonian. Set for
t\in\mathbb{R}

\bm{F}_{i}(t)=\exp(t\widehat{\bm{H}}^{(i)})^{H}\bm{J}\exp(t\widehat{\bm{H}}^{(i)}).

We differentiate
\bm{F}_{i}(t)
with respect to
t
. Since

\frac{d}{dt}\exp(t\widehat{\bm{H}}^{(i)})=\widehat{\bm{H}}^{(i)}\exp(t\widehat{\bm{H}}^{(i)})

and

\frac{d}{dt}\exp(t\widehat{\bm{H}}^{(i)})^{H}=\exp(t\widehat{\bm{H}}^{(i)})^{H}(\widehat{\bm{H}}^{(i)})^{H},

the product rule gives

\displaystyle\bm{F}_{i}^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\exp(t\widehat{\bm{H}}^{(i)})^{H}(\widehat{\bm{H}}^{(i)})^{H}\bm{J}\exp(t\widehat{\bm{H}}^{(i)})+\exp(t\widehat{\bm{H}}^{(i)})^{H}\bm{J}\widehat{\bm{H}}^{(i)}\exp(t\widehat{\bm{H}}^{(i)})

\displaystyle=\exp(t\widehat{\bm{H}}^{(i)})^{H}\left((\widehat{\bm{H}}^{(i)})^{H}\bm{J}+\bm{J}\widehat{\bm{H}}^{(i)}\right)\exp(t\widehat{\bm{H}}^{(i)}).

Since
\widehat{\bm{H}}^{(i)}
is Hamiltonian,

(\bm{J}\widehat{\bm{H}}^{(i)})^{H}=\bm{J}\widehat{\bm{H}}^{(i)}.

Because
\bm{J}^{H}=-\bm{J}
, the left-hand side is

(\bm{J}\widehat{\bm{H}}^{(i)})^{H}=(\widehat{\bm{H}}^{(i)})^{H}\bm{J}^{H}=-(\widehat{\bm{H}}^{(i)})^{H}\bm{J}.

Therefore

-(\widehat{\bm{H}}^{(i)})^{H}\bm{J}=\bm{J}\widehat{\bm{H}}^{(i)},

or equivalently,

(\widehat{\bm{H}}^{(i)})^{H}\bm{J}+\bm{J}\widehat{\bm{H}}^{(i)}=\bm{0}.

Substituting this identity into the formula for
\bm{F}_{i}^{\prime}(t)
, we obtain

\bm{F}_{i}^{\prime}(t)=\bm{0}\quad\text{for every }t\in\mathbb{R}.

Hence
\bm{F}_{i}(t)
is constant. Evaluating at
t=0
gives

\bm{F}_{i}(0)=\bm{I}_{2n}^{H}\bm{J}\bm{I}_{2n}=\bm{J},

and therefore

\bm{F}_{i}(1)=\bm{J}.

This means that

\exp(\widehat{\bm{H}}^{(i)})^{H}\bm{J}\exp(\widehat{\bm{H}}^{(i)})=\bm{J},

so
\exp(\widehat{\bm{H}}^{(i)})
is symplectic.

Since this holds for every
i=1,\dots,p
, each slice of
\exp(\mathcal{H})
in the Fourier domain is symplectic. Theorem
3.11
now implies that
\exp(\mathcal{H})
is T-symplectic.
∎

3.3
The T-Williamson Normal Form

We state the tensor analogue of Williamson’s theorem (
32
). The classical result is formulated for real symmetric positive-definite matrices and yields a symplectic congruence normal form in that setting (
31
;
22
).
Accordingly, if we assume that each slice in the Fourier domain is real symmetric positive-definite, the classical construction applies on the slices.
We also discuss the failure of a direct extension to arbitrary Hermitian positive-definite slices under the convention
\bm{S}^{H}\bm{J}\bm{S}=\bm{J}
.

Definition 3.14
.

A tensor
\mathcal{D}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n\times p}
is called
T-diagonal
if each frontal slice
\widehat{\bm{D}}^{(i)}
of
\widehat{\mathcal{D}}
is a diagonal matrix.

Theorem 3.15
.

Let
\mathcal{M}\in\mathbb{C}^{2n\times 2n\times p}
. Assume that for each
i=1,\dots,p
, the slice
\widehat{\bm{M}}^{(i)}
is a real symmetric positive-definite matrix. Then, there exist a T-symplectic tensor
\mathcal{S}
and a T-diagonal tensor
\mathcal{D}
such that

\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{S}^{H}*\mathcal{D}*\mathcal{S}.

(3.1)

Moreover, for each
i=1,\dots,p
, the
i
-th slice of
\mathcal{D}
in the Fourier domain has the form

\widehat{\bm{D}}^{(i)}=\begin{bmatrix}\widehat{\bm{\Lambda}}^{(i)}&\bm{0}\\
\bm{0}&\widehat{\bm{\Lambda}}^{(i)}\end{bmatrix},

(3.2)

where
\widehat{\bm{\Lambda}}^{(i)}=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_{1}^{(i)},\dots,\lambda_{n}^{(i)})
and
\lambda_{j}^{(i)}>0
for
j=1,\dots,n
.

Proof.

Let
i\in\{1,\dots,p\}
be fixed.
By hypothesis, the matrix
\widehat{\bm{M}}^{(i)}
is real symmetric positive-definite.
The classical Williamson theorem for real symmetric positive-definite matrices therefore yields a real symplectic matrix
\widehat{\bm{R}}^{(i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2n}
and a diagonal matrix

\widehat{\bm{\Lambda}}^{(i)}=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_{1}^{(i)},\dots,\lambda_{n}^{(i)})

with
\lambda_{j}^{(i)}>0
for
j=1,\dots,n
, such that

(\widehat{\bm{R}}^{(i)})^{T}\widehat{\bm{M}}^{(i)}\widehat{\bm{R}}^{(i)}=\begin{bmatrix}\widehat{\bm{\Lambda}}^{(i)}&\bm{0}\\
\bm{0}&\widehat{\bm{\Lambda}}^{(i)}\end{bmatrix}.

(3.3)

Set

\widehat{\bm{D}}^{(i)}=\begin{bmatrix}\widehat{\bm{\Lambda}}^{(i)}&\bm{0}\\
\bm{0}&\widehat{\bm{\Lambda}}^{(i)}\end{bmatrix}

and define

\widehat{\bm{S}}^{(i)}=(\widehat{\bm{R}}^{(i)})^{-1}.

Since
\widehat{\bm{R}}^{(i)}
is symplectic, we have

(\widehat{\bm{R}}^{(i)})^{T}\bm{J}\widehat{\bm{R}}^{(i)}=\bm{J}.

Multiplying this identity on the left by
(\widehat{\bm{R}}^{(i)})^{-T}
and on the right by
(\widehat{\bm{R}}^{(i)})^{-1}
yields

((\widehat{\bm{R}}^{(i)})^{-1})^{T}\bm{J}(\widehat{\bm{R}}^{(i)})^{-1}=\bm{J},

so
\widehat{\bm{S}}^{(i)}
is symplectic. Because
\widehat{\bm{S}}^{(i)}
is real, its transpose and conjugate transpose coincide. Multiplying the Williamson identity in (
3.3
) on the left by
(\widehat{\bm{R}}^{(i)})^{-T}
and on the right by
(\widehat{\bm{R}}^{(i)})^{-1}
, we obtain

\widehat{\bm{M}}^{(i)}=(\widehat{\bm{R}}^{(i)})^{-T}\widehat{\bm{D}}^{(i)}(\widehat{\bm{R}}^{(i)})^{-1}=(\widehat{\bm{S}}^{(i)})^{T}\widehat{\bm{D}}^{(i)}\widehat{\bm{S}}^{(i)}=(\widehat{\bm{S}}^{(i)})^{H}\widehat{\bm{D}}^{(i)}\widehat{\bm{S}}^{(i)}.

Thus, for the fixed index
i
, we have produced a real symplectic matrix
\widehat{\bm{S}}^{(i)}
and a matrix
\widehat{\bm{D}}^{(i)}
of the required diagonal form satisfying

\widehat{\bm{M}}^{(i)}=(\widehat{\bm{S}}^{(i)})^{H}\widehat{\bm{D}}^{(i)}\widehat{\bm{S}}^{(i)}.

We perform this construction for each
i=1,\dots,p
. Let
\widehat{\mathcal{S}}
and
\widehat{\mathcal{D}}
be the tensors whose
i
-th frontal slices are
\widehat{\bm{S}}^{(i)}
and
\widehat{\bm{D}}^{(i)}
, respectively, and let
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{D}
be the results of applying slice-wise the inverse transform of
\widehat{\mathcal{S}}
and
\widehat{\mathcal{D}}
as in Lemma
2.1
. Since every frontal slice of
\widehat{\mathcal{D}}
is diagonal, Definition
3.14
implies that
\mathcal{D}
is T-diagonal.

It remains to verify that
\mathcal{S}
is T-symplectic. For each
i=1,\dots,p
, the matrix
\widehat{\bm{S}}^{(i)}
is symplectic, and hence

(\widehat{\bm{S}}^{(i)})^{H}\bm{J}\widehat{\bm{S}}^{(i)}=\bm{J}.

Using Definition
3.1
, we have
\widehat{\bm{J}}^{(i)}=\bm{J}
for every
i
, so the preceding identity can be rewritten as

(\widehat{\bm{S}}^{(i)})^{H}\widehat{\bm{J}}^{(i)}\widehat{\bm{S}}^{(i)}=\widehat{\bm{J}}^{(i)}\quad\text{for every }i=1,\dots,p.

By Theorem
3.11
, this implies that
\mathcal{S}
is T-symplectic.

Define now

\mathcal{N}:=\mathcal{S}^{H}*\mathcal{D}*\mathcal{S}.

For each
i=1,\dots,p
, the
i
-th frontal slice of
\widehat{\mathcal{N}}
is

\widehat{\bm{N}}^{(i)}=(\widehat{\bm{S}}^{(i)})^{H}\widehat{\bm{D}}^{(i)}\widehat{\bm{S}}^{(i)}=\widehat{\bm{M}}^{(i)}.

Thus, the tensors
\widehat{\mathcal{N}}
and
\widehat{\mathcal{M}}
have identical frontal slices. Applying the inverse DFT yields

\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{S}^{H}*\mathcal{D}*\mathcal{S}.

∎

\mathcal{M}\in\mathbb{C}^{2n\times 2n\times p}
with real symmetric positive-definite Fourier-domain slices

Tensors
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{D}
such that
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{S}^{H}*\mathcal{D}*\mathcal{S}

i=1,\ldots,p

\mathcal{S},\mathcal{D}

The constructive proof gives the slice-wise
Algorithm
1
, where

\textnormal{{Williamson}}(\bm{A})
denotes the classical Williamson decomposition (
32
;
22
;
10
), returning a real symplectic matrix
\bm{R}
and a diagonal matrix
\bm{\Lambda}
such that
\bm{R}^{T}\bm{A}\bm{R}=\operatorname{blkdiag}(\bm{\Lambda},\bm{\Lambda})
.

The next proposition shows that the real-slice hypothesis is essential under the Hermitian symplectic convention adopted here (
28
;
8
).

Proposition 3.16
.

Under the convention
\bm{S}^{H}\bm{J}\bm{S}=\bm{J}
, a Williamson-type factorization of the form

\bm{M}=\bm{S}^{H}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\Lambda}&\bm{0}\\
\bm{0}&\bm{\Lambda}\end{bmatrix}\bm{S},\qquad\bm{\Lambda}=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}),\quad\lambda_{j}>0,

does not exist for every complex Hermitian positive-definite matrix
\bm{M}\in\mathbb{C}^{2n\times 2n}
.

Proof.

We provide a counterexample. Consider the Hermitian positive-definite matrix

\bm{M}_{0}=\begin{bmatrix}2&\sqrt{-1}\\
-\sqrt{-1}&2\end{bmatrix}.

Suppose, for contradiction, that there exist a matrix
\bm{S}\in\mathbb{C}^{2\times 2}
and a positive number
\lambda
such that

\bm{M}_{0}=\bm{S}^{H}\begin{bmatrix}\lambda&0\\
0&\lambda\end{bmatrix}\bm{S}

(3.4)

and

\bm{S}^{H}\bm{J}\bm{S}=\bm{J}.

Set

\bm{D}=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda&0\\
0&\lambda\end{bmatrix}.

Since
\bm{M}_{0}=\bm{S}^{H}\bm{D}\bm{S}
and
\bm{S}
is invertible, we have

\bm{M}_{0}^{-1}=\bm{S}^{-1}\bm{D}^{-1}\bm{S}^{-H}.

Multiplying by
\bm{J}
on the right yields

\bm{M}_{0}^{-1}\bm{J}=\bm{S}^{-1}\bm{D}^{-1}\bm{S}^{-H}\bm{J}.

From the symplectic identity
\bm{S}^{H}\bm{J}\bm{S}=\bm{J}
, multiplying on the left by
\bm{S}^{-H}
gives

\bm{J}\bm{S}=\bm{S}^{-H}\bm{J}.

Substituting this relation into the previous identity, we obtain

\bm{M}_{0}^{-1}\bm{J}=\bm{S}^{-1}\bm{D}^{-1}\bm{J}\bm{S}.

Hence,
\bm{M}_{0}^{-1}\bm{J}
is similar to
\bm{D}^{-1}\bm{J}
.

Now,

\bm{D}^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda^{-1}&0\\
0&\lambda^{-1}\end{bmatrix},

and therefore

\bm{D}^{-1}\bm{J}=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda^{-1}&0\\
0&\lambda^{-1}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0&\lambda^{-1}\\
-\lambda^{-1}&0\end{bmatrix}.

The characteristic polynomial of this matrix is

\det\begin{bmatrix}\mu&-\lambda^{-1}\\
\lambda^{-1}&\mu\end{bmatrix}=\mu^{2}+\lambda^{-2},

so its eigenvalues are
\pm\sqrt{-1}\,\lambda^{-1}
. Consequently, the spectrum of
\bm{D}^{-1}\bm{J}
, and hence also the spectrum of
\bm{M}_{0}^{-1}\bm{J}
, must be

\left\{\pm\frac{\sqrt{-1}}{\lambda}\right\}.

(3.5)

On the other hand, for the explicit matrix
\bm{M}_{0}
, we compute

\bm{M}_{0}^{-1}=\frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix}2&-\sqrt{-1}\\
\sqrt{-1}&2\end{bmatrix},

and hence

\bm{M}_{0}^{-1}\bm{J}=\frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix}\sqrt{-1}&2\\
-2&\sqrt{-1}\end{bmatrix}.

Therefore

\det\left(\mu\bm{I}_{2}-\bm{M}_{0}^{-1}\bm{J}\right)=\left(\mu-\frac{\sqrt{-1}}{3}\right)^{2}+\frac{4}{9}=(\mu-\sqrt{-1})\left(\mu+\frac{\sqrt{-1}}{3}\right).

Thus the eigenvalues of
\bm{M}_{0}^{-1}\bm{J}
are
\sqrt{-1}
and
-\frac{\sqrt{-1}}{3}
. These two numbers are not of the form
\pm\sqrt{-1}\,\lambda^{-1}
for a single positive number
\lambda
, which contradicts the necessary spectral form derived above in (
3.5
).
Therefore the decomposition in (
3.4
) cannot exist for
\bm{M}_{0}
.
∎

The following proposition identifies the necessary conjugate-symmetry condition in the Fourier domain that guarantees real-valued recovery in the spatial domain.

Proposition 3.17
.

Let
\widehat{\mathcal{A}}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n\times p}
, and let
\mathcal{A}
be the tensor obtained by applying the inverse DFT along the third mode, that is,

\bm{A}^{(k)}=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\omega^{-(i-1)(k-1)}\widehat{\bm{A}}^{(i)},\quad k=1,\dots,p.

Then,
\mathcal{A}
lies in
\mathbb{R}^{m\times n\times p}
if and only if

\widehat{\bm{A}}^{(1)}=\overline{\widehat{\bm{A}}^{(1)}}

and

\widehat{\bm{A}}^{(p-i+2)}=\overline{\widehat{\bm{A}}^{(i)}}\quad\text{for every }i=2,\dots,p.

Proof.

Assume first that
\mathcal{A}
is real-valued. For each
i=1,\dots,p
, the forward DFT formula in Lemma
2.1
gives

\widehat{\bm{A}}^{(i)}=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\omega^{(i-1)(j-1)}\bm{A}^{(j)}.

Since every
\bm{A}^{(j)}
is real, we have

\overline{\widehat{\bm{A}}^{(i)}}=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\overline{\omega^{(i-1)(j-1)}}\,\bm{A}^{(j)}=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\omega^{-(i-1)(j-1)}\bm{A}^{(j)}.

For
i=1
, this becomes

\overline{\widehat{\bm{A}}^{(1)}}=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\omega^{0}\bm{A}^{(j)}=\widehat{\bm{A}}^{(1)}.

Now let
i\in\{2,\dots,p\}
. Since
\omega^{p}=1
, we have

\omega^{-(i-1)(j-1)}=\omega^{(p-i+1)(j-1)}=\omega^{(p-i+2-1)(j-1)}.

Therefore

\overline{\widehat{\bm{A}}^{(i)}}=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\omega^{(p-i+2-1)(j-1)}\bm{A}^{(j)}=\widehat{\bm{A}}^{(p-i+2)}.

This proves the stated conjugate-symmetry condition.

Conversely, assume that

\widehat{\bm{A}}^{(1)}=\overline{\widehat{\bm{A}}^{(1)}}

and

\widehat{\bm{A}}^{(p-i+2)}=\overline{\widehat{\bm{A}}^{(i)}}\quad\text{for every }i=2,\dots,p.

Fix
k\in\{1,\dots,p\}
. Taking the complex conjugate of the inverse DFT formula gives

\overline{\bm{A}^{(j)}}=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\omega^{(i-1)(j-1)}\overline{\widehat{\bm{A}}^{(i)}}.

We separate the term
i=1
and use the assumption on
\widehat{\bm{A}}^{(1)}
:

\overline{\bm{A}^{(j)}}=\frac{1}{p}\widehat{\bm{A}}^{(1)}+\frac{1}{p}\sum_{i=2}^{p}\omega^{(i-1)(j-1)}\overline{\widehat{\bm{A}}^{(i)}}.

In the remaining sum, set
k=p-i+2
.
Then, as
i
runs from
2
to
p
, the index
k
also runs through the set
\{2,\dots,p\}
.
Moreover,
i-1=p-k+1
.
Hence,

\omega^{(i-1)(j-1)}=\omega^{(p-k+1)(j-1)}=\omega^{p(j-1)}\omega^{-(k-1)(j-1)}=\omega^{-(k-1)(j-1)},

because
\omega^{p(j-1)}=1
. Using the conjugate-symmetry assumption, we obtain

\displaystyle\overline{\bm{A}^{(j)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p}\widehat{\bm{A}}^{(1)}+\frac{1}{p}\sum_{k=2}^{p}\omega^{-(k-1)(j-1)}\widehat{\bm{A}}^{(j)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{k=1}^{p}\omega^{-(k-1)(j-1)}\widehat{\bm{A}}^{(k)}

\displaystyle=\bm{A}^{(j)}.

Thus, every frontal slice
\bm{A}^{(j)}
is real, and therefore
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n\times p}
.
∎

Corollary 3.18
.

Assume that
\mathcal{M}
satisfies the hypotheses of Theorem
3.15
and that its slices in the Fourier domain satisfy this conjugate-symmetry condition. Then the T-Williamson decomposition in Theorem
3.15
may be chosen so that the tensors
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{D}
are real-valued.

Proof.

Assume that
\mathcal{M}
satisfies the hypotheses of Theorem
3.15
and the above conjugate-symmetry condition.
We enforce the Williamson factors in the Fourier domain to satisfy the conjugate-symmetric condition of Proposition
3.17
.
First consider the self-conjugate slices. The condition for
i=1
gives

\widehat{\bm{M}}^{(1)}=\overline{\widehat{\bm{M}}^{(1)}},

so
\widehat{\bm{M}}^{(1)}
is a real symmetric positive-definite matrix. If
p
is even, then the index
i=\frac{p}{2}+1
satisfies
p-i+2=i
, and hence

\widehat{\bm{M}}^{(\frac{p}{2}+1)}=\overline{\widehat{\bm{M}}^{(\frac{p}{2}+1)}},

so this slice is also real symmetric positive-definite. For these self-conjugate slices we choose real symplectic Williamson factors and real diagonal factors.

Now let
i\in\{2,\dots,p\}
with
p-i+2\neq i
. We apply the classical Williamson factorization to the real symmetric positive-definite matrix
\widehat{\bm{M}}^{(i)}
, exactly as in the slice-wise construction used in the proof of Theorem
3.15
, and choose matrices
\widehat{\bm{S}}^{(i)}
and
\widehat{\bm{D}}^{(i)}
such that

\widehat{\bm{M}}^{(i)}=(\widehat{\bm{S}}^{(i)})^{H}\widehat{\bm{D}}^{(i)}\widehat{\bm{S}}^{(i)}.

Because the diagonal entries of
\widehat{\bm{D}}^{(i)}
are positive real numbers, the matrix
\widehat{\bm{D}}^{(i)}
is real. We then define

\widehat{\bm{S}}^{(p-i+2)}=\overline{\widehat{\bm{S}}^{(i)}},\qquad\widehat{\bm{D}}^{(p-i+2)}=\widehat{\bm{D}}^{(i)}.

Since
\bm{J}
is real, the symplectic identity is preserved under complex conjugation:

(\widehat{\bm{S}}^{(p-i+2)})^{H}\bm{J}\widehat{\bm{S}}^{(p-i+2)}=(\overline{\widehat{\bm{S}}^{(i)}})^{H}\bm{J}\,\overline{\widehat{\bm{S}}^{(i)}}=\overline{(\widehat{\bm{S}}^{(i)})^{H}\bm{J}\widehat{\bm{S}}^{(i)}}=\bm{J}.

Moreover,

\displaystyle(\widehat{\bm{S}}^{(p-i+2)})^{H}\widehat{\bm{D}}^{(p-i+2)}\widehat{\bm{S}}^{(p-i+2)}

\displaystyle=(\overline{\widehat{\bm{S}}^{(i)}})^{H}\widehat{\bm{D}}^{(i)}\overline{\widehat{\bm{S}}^{(i)}}

\displaystyle=\overline{(\widehat{\bm{S}}^{(i)})^{H}\widehat{\bm{D}}^{(i)}\widehat{\bm{S}}^{(i)}}

\displaystyle=\overline{\widehat{\bm{M}}^{(i)}}

\displaystyle=\widehat{\bm{M}}^{(p-i+2)}.

Thus, the factors can be chosen so that the slices of
\widehat{\mathcal{S}}
and
\widehat{\mathcal{D}}
in the Fourier domain satisfy the same conjugate-symmetry condition as
\widehat{\mathcal{M}}
. Applying Proposition
3.17
to
\widehat{\mathcal{S}}
and
\widehat{\mathcal{D}}
, we conclude that the tensors
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{D}
, obtained by applying the inverse DFT, are real-valued.
∎

4.3
Decohering Quantum System

The T-Williamson normal form can be used to organize a family of sampled covariance matrices once it is encoded in to the slices of a tensor in the Fourier domain. We illustrate this with a two-mode squeezed vacuum state interacting with a thermal environment, while keeping the physical interpretation at the level of the slices.
We emphasize that this construction is a Fourier-domain encoding.
The sampled covariance matrices are not obtained by applying the DFT to a real-valued tensor.
Instead, they are defined directly as the Fourier-domain slices of a tensor.
This choice is consistent with the hypotheses of Theorem
3.15
, which require the Fourier-domain slices to be real symmetric positive-definite.
If the sampled covariance matrices were assigned to the spatial-domain frontal slices of a real tensor and the DFT were then applied, the resulting Fourier-domain slices would generally be complex Hermitian rather than real symmetric.
In that case, Theorem
3.15
would not be applicable.

We take
n=2
,
p=64
, and set the physical parameters for the dynamics:
r=1
is the squeezing parameter of the initial state,
\bar{n}_{\text{th}}=0.5
is the mean thermal photon number of the environment, and
\kappa=0.3
is the decay rate governing the system-bath interaction.
In the phase-space ordering

[\hat{q}_{1},\hat{q}_{2},\hat{p}_{1},\hat{p}_{2}]^{T},

where the pair
(\hat{q}_{j},\hat{p}_{j})
refers to the position-like component and momentum-like component of the mode
j
, the initial covariance matrix of the two-mode squeezed vacuum state (
31
) is

\bm{M}_{0}=\begin{bmatrix}\cosh(2r)&\sinh(2r)&0&0\\
\sinh(2r)&\cosh(2r)&0&0\\
0&0&\cosh(2r)&-\sinh(2r)\\
0&0&-\sinh(2r)&\cosh(2r)\end{bmatrix}.

The interaction with a thermal bath is modeled by

\bm{M}(t)=e^{-\kappa t}\bm{M}_{0}+(1-e^{-\kappa t})(2\bar{n}_{\text{th}}+1)\bm{I}_{4},

where
(2\bar{n}_{\text{th}}+1)\bm{I}_{4}
is the thermal covariance for two modes (
31
).
We sample the time interval
[0,12]
at

t_{i}=\frac{12(i-1)}{p-1},\quad i=1,\dots,p.

We encode the sampled family slice-wise for a tensor in the Fourier domain by setting

\widehat{\bm{M}}^{(i)}=\bm{M}(t_{i}),

for
i=1,\dots,p.
This places the time-dependent covariance family in a single algebraic object. Since each matrix
\bm{M}(t_{i})
is real symmetric positive-definite, the hypothesis of Theorem
3.15
is satisfied slice-wise in the Fourier domain. Proposition
3.17
identifies an additional Fourier conjugate-symmetry condition that would force the spatial-domain tensor itself to be real-valued, but that condition is not imposed here.

To analyze the physical significance of the slice-wise symplectic eigenvalues, we apply Theorem
3.15
to decompose the covariance tensor as

\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{S}^{H}*\mathcal{D}*\mathcal{S},

where
\mathcal{S}
is a T-symplectic tensor and
\mathcal{D}
is a T-diagonal tensor. From this T-Williamson decomposition, we obtain the following diagonal matrix slices

\widehat{\bm{\Lambda}}^{(i)}=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_{1}^{(i)},\lambda_{2}^{(i)})

for each
i=1,\dots,p
. Because the Fourier-domain slices of
\mathcal{M}
are exactly the covariance matrices
\bm{M}(t_{i})
, the diagonal entries
\lambda_{1}^{(i)}
and
\lambda_{2}^{(i)}
are precisely the ordinary symplectic eigenvalues of
\bm{M}(t_{i})
.
Under the convention
\hbar=2
, where
\hbar
is the reduced Planck constant, the Heisenberg uncertainty principle requires

\lambda_{j}^{(i)}\geq 1,\quad j=1,2,

for each
i=1,\dots,p
. By using the relation
\lambda_{j}=2\bar{n}_{j}+1
, given by Williamson’s theorem on Gaussian states, on the entropy profile formula in
27
, we get

S_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}g(\lambda_{j}^{(i)}),

where
g(\lambda)=\frac{\lambda+1}{2}\log_{2}\left(\frac{\lambda+1}{2}\right)-\frac{\lambda-1}{2}\log_{2}\left(\frac{\lambda-1}{2}\right)
.
To compute the entanglement of the two pairs
(\hat{q}_{j},\hat{p}_{j})
,
j=1,2
, we introduce the partially transposed covariance matrix

\widetilde{\bm{M}}(t_{i})=\bm{P}\bm{M}(t_{i})\bm{P},\quad\bm{P}:=\operatorname{diag}(1,1,1,-1),

where
\bm{P}
sends
\hat{p}_{2}
to
-\hat{p}_{2}
, and denote its symplectic eigenvalues by
\tilde{\lambda}_{1}^{(i)}
and
\tilde{\lambda}_{2}^{(i)}
. The entanglement profile is then

E_{N}(\rho_{i})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\max(0,-\log_{2}(\tilde{\lambda}_{j}^{(i)})),

by
2
, where
\rho_{i}
is the Gaussian state associated to the covariance matrix
\bm{M}(t_{i})
.

The entropy and entanglement profiles
obtained from these sampled covariance matrices are illustrated in Figure
3
. For the chosen parameters, the entropy increases from
0
to approximately
2.826
over the sampled interval, whereas the entanglement profile
decreases from approximately
2.885
to
0
.
Thus, the two modes
(\hat{q}_{1},\hat{p}_{1})
and
(\hat{q}_{2},\hat{p}_{2})
of the system approach a thermal equilibrium and are no longer entangled after interacting with the thermal environment.
This behavior is consistent with the physical picture of decoherence: as
t
increases, the covariance matrix approaches the thermal limit

\bm{M}(t)\to(2\bar{n}_{\text{th}}+1)\bm{I}_{4},

the symplectic eigenvalues move toward the thermal value
2\bar{n}_{\text{th}}+1
, the entropy moves toward the corresponding thermal entropy, and the entanglement vanishes.
The example shows how the T-product formalism organizes and analyzes a sampled covariance family while keeping the physical interpretation at the level of the Fourier-domain slices.

Remark 4.3
.

Each covariance matrix
\widehat{\bm{M}}^{(i)}=\bm{M}(t_{i})
is real, but the inverse DFT of the family
\{\widehat{\bm{M}}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{p}
need not be real in the spatial domain.
The example is therefore formulated as a Fourier-domain encoding of a sampled covariance family rather than as a tensor-valued physical trajectory.
This is compatible with the present T-product framework over
\mathbb{C}
, where the quantum mechanical formulas remain the standard slice-wise Gaussian-state formulas and the tensor contribution is the T-Williamson decomposition of the encoded family.

5
Conclusion and Future Work

We introduced Hamiltonian and symplectic tensor classes in the T-product algebra and established their basic Fourier-domain characterizations.
We proved a constructive T-Williamson normal form for tensors whose Fourier-domain slices are real symmetric positive-definite matrices, together with an obstruction result showing that the same statement does not extend directly to arbitrary Hermitian positive-definite Fourier-domain slices under the convention
\bm{S}^{H}\bm{J}\bm{S}=\bm{J}
.
We also derived a real-valued recovery criterion under Fourier conjugate symmetry. Numerical experiments supported the constructive aspects of the theory and included a quantum example based on Fourier-domain tensor slices of a sampled covariance family.

Several questions remain open. Proposition
3.16
shows that the Hermitian complex case cannot be treated by a direct extension of the real case.
This suggests considering other symplectic conventions, such as the transpose-based complex symplectic group, or developing a normal form adapted to Hermitian positive-definite Fourier-domain slices.
The computational aspects also require further study. The present construction is carried out slice-wise and has arithmetic cost
O(pn^{3})
.
Faster methods may be possible when the Fourier-domain slices have additional structure, such as sparsity, low-rank perturbations, or block structure.
Finally, the framework may be applicable to other parameter-dependent structured matrix families, including those arising in control, wave propagation, and Gaussian quantum dynamics.
