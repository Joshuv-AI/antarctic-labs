Response-adapted perioperative therapy for resectable esophageal cancer in the immunotherapy era: a narrative review
Authors: Yuyang Yan, Jiahua Lyu, Yungchang Chen, Xuefeng Leng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectiveEsophageal cancer is a common malignancy worldwide, and surgery alone is insufficient for most patients with locally advanced disease. Multimodal treatment has therefore become standard practice. In the clinical immunotherapy era, however, it is more complicated to discuss now. There are wider concerns attached to it. The question is no longer limited to whether neoadjuvant therapy is indicated. Multidisciplinary teams must now weigh which treatment backbone is most appropriate, whether radiotherapy adds value over chemotherapy alone, and whether surgery can be delayed in carefully chosen patients. This review summarizes the changes taking place in perioperative care, approaches for making surgical recommendations, and the wider move toward customized control.MethodsThe databases of PubMed, Google Scholar, Web of Science, and Cochrane Library were delved into in depth. The inquiry expanded the period from January 1, 2005, to January 31, 2026. Priority was given to phase II/III trials, meta-analyses, guideline documents, and high-quality multicenter studies across time.Key Content and FindingsThe standard for esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) remains neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (nCRT). At the same time, neoadjuvant immunotherapy plus chemotherapy (nICT) emerged as a viable alternative approach with better pathologic response and no clear signal of greater perioperative morbidity. According to the ESOPEC trial, there is a stronger role for perioperative systemic therapy in adenocarcinoma, which was shown by survival being superior with fluorouracil, leucovorin, oxaliplatin, and docetaxel (FLOT) over CROSS-based chemoradiotherapy. The results highlight control of micrometastatic disease. Pathologic complete response (pCR) remains clinically relevant across histological subtypes, but it should not automatically assume that it serves as a surrogate for overall survival especially in the immunotherapy setting. Although organ-preserving approaches show promise, the evaluation of clinical complete response remains imperfect requiring robust surveillance strategies. Biomarkers like mismatch repair status, programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression, and circulating tumor DNA can assist in intensifying and de-escalating treatment.ConclusionsThe management of resectable esophageal cancer in the perioperative phase is becoming less fixed and more individualized and response-adapted. Further progress will depend on mature survival results, improved biologic selection, and more accurate evaluation of response after induction therapy.Keywords: Esophageal cancer, perioperative therapy, neoadjuvant therapy, immunotherapy, esophagectomy

Introduction

Esophageal cancer remains a major cause of cancer-related death worldwide, with more than 510,000 new cases and about 445,000 deaths each year (1). The epidemiology of this disease is not the same everywhere. In East Asia, most esophageal cancers are squamous cell carcinomas (ESCC), while Western countries are seeing increasing burden from esophageal adenocarcinoma (EAC) and gastroesophageal junction (GEJ) adenocarcinomas (2). Surgery on its own is rarely enough, largely because it cannot reliably control both locoregional disease and occult systemic spread (3,4). That is why multimodal treatment has become routine. Still, even the two main backbones in current practice—CROSS-type neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (nCRT) and perioperative FLOT chemotherapy—leave a good deal of room for improvement. In the 10-year update of CROSS, the absolute cure rate was 38%, and isolated distant recurrence was not significantly reduced (5). ESOPEC points in much the same direction: even with FLOT, 5-year overall survival (OS) was only around 50%, and recurrence still occurred in more than 40% of patients, most often at distant sites (6). Thus, local treatment has improved, but distant failure remains a major constraint on current multimodal therapy. This persistent gap explains much of the current interest in immunotherapy.

Against that background, perioperative decision-making has started to shift in ways that are highly relevant to day-to-day multidisciplinary decision-making. CheckMate 577 was one important turning point, establishing adjuvant nivolumab for patients without a pathologic complete response (pCR) after nCRT and surgery, with median disease-free survival improving from 11.0 to 22.4 months (7,8). At roughly the same time, ESCORT-NEO suggested that neoadjuvant camrelizumab plus chemotherapy could push pCR rates in ESCC up to 28.0%, compared with 4.7% for chemotherapy alone, which naturally raises the question of whether radiotherapy is always necessary in squamous disease (9). There is also a broader consequence of these deeper responses: organ preservation, once more theoretical than practical, is back in the conversation and is being tested in studies such as SANO (4,10).

So, the field has not simply added immunotherapy to older treatment models; it has opened up a series of important questions that many multidisciplinary teams (MDTs) now face routinely, sometimes before the guidelines have caught up. In ESCC, should radiation still be part of induction, or is chemoimmunotherapy sufficient for selected cases? When is the right time to operate after programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1)-based induction? Could biomarkers including programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) combined positive score (CPS), circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) clearance, tumor mutational burden (TMB), or immune gene signatures help identify which patients can undergo de-escalation and surveillance versus those needing intensification? And for patients with a clinical complete response (cCR), can esophagectomy be postponed or omitted safely under an active surveillance strategy?

In this context, the present review focuses on the most pressing issues in practice, namely whether the perioperative strategy is being reshaped by immunotherapy, if treatment sequencing may need to differ for ESCC and EAC, whether newly emerging biomarkers and response-assessment tools can support more individualized escalation or de-escalation, and if active surveillance after deep response can be incorporated safely into standard of care. We aim to offer a clinically oriented synthesis that reflects the real dilemmas faced in contemporary esophageal cancer care. We present this article in accordance with the Narrative Review reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-0508/rc).

Methods

PubMed, Web of Science, the Cochrane Library, and Google Scholar were searched. The search window for eligible studies spanned January 1, 2005 through January 31, 2026, and the electronic searches were performed from February 1 to February 25, 2026. English-language studies were prioritized.

The principal search string used in PubMed was: (“esophageal cancer” OR “oesophageal cancer” OR “esophageal squamous cell carcinoma” OR “esophageal adenocarcinoma”) AND (“perioperative therapy” OR “neoadjuvant therapy” OR “chemoradiotherapy” OR “chemotherapy” OR “immunotherapy” OR “immune checkpoint inhibitor” OR “active surveillance” OR “organ preservation” OR “circulating tumor DNA” OR “biomarker”). Equivalent combinations were adapted for the other databases.

We prioritized phase II and III trials, meta-analyses, consensus guidelines, and high-quality multicenter cohort studies. Conference abstracts, letters, editorials, and isolated case reports were generally excluded unless they were required to contextualize a rapidly evolving topic. The title-and-abstract reviewing step was followed by a full-text assessment of potentially eligible articles. The types of studies emphasized landmark trials, mature follow-up reports, and recent high-impact studies related to perioperative sequencing, endpoint interpretation, organ preservation, and biomarker-guided care. Endpoints in perioperative esophageal cancer trials

Perioperative therapy is central to the management of resectable, locally advanced esophageal cancer; however, the proliferation of nCRT, perioperative chemotherapy, and immunotherapy-based regimens has increased heterogeneity in trial endpoints. Within the immunotherapy setting, the predictive value of pCR appears less consistent, whereas OS, standardized surgical quality metrics, and patient-reported outcomes (PROs) are gaining prominence. Accordingly, endpoint frameworks are transitioning toward multidimensional, standardized, and comparable measures to distinguish practice-changing strategies and support future trial design. OS and event-based endpoints

OS remains the most reliable efficacy endpoint in perioperative trials for resectable esophageal cancer because it integrates local control, distant relapse risk, treatment-related mortality, and the effects of subsequent therapy. DFS and EFS get mature earlier and are often used as primary or co-primary endpoints. But numerous large randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and meta-analyses continue to show that OS offers an irreplaceable discrimination across treatment strategies, particularly since early event endpoints correlate less with OS in the immunotherapy combinations era.

Long-term follow-up from landmark trials underlines the central role of OS. Ten-year results of the CROSS trial showed that nCRT plus surgery improved OS and reduced esophageal cancer-specific mortality compared with surgery alone, with durable benefit that was not explained by subsequent salvage or adjuvant therapy (19). On the contrary, the final CheckMate 577 analysis revealed that although Adjuvant nivolumab’s DFS advantage was durable, it did not yield a statistically significant OS improvement (20). The fact the DFS and OS were not significantly related might indicate that event-based surrogates will not accurately capture long-term survival benefit during the immunotherapy era. It also reinforces the need to preserve OS as either a primary or co-primary endpoint in confirmatory phase III trial.

The ESOPEC trial provided pivotal head-to-head evidence in adenocarcinoma. In this multicenter phase III RCT (n=438), perioperative FLOT was compared directly with the CROSS-based nCRT pathway, with OS as the primary endpoint. OS was significantly superior with FLOT despite higher pCR rates in the CROSS arm. This finding cautions against overreliance on pathologic endpoints and reinforces OS as the decisive measure of net clinical benefit when defining standards of care (21). Contemporary real-world and registry analyses have reported similar OS advantages for FLOT, particularly in high-volume centers (22,23).

The introduction of immunotherapy further strengthens the rationale for OS-based assessment. CheckMate 577 established a DFS benefit for adjuvant nivolumab following nCRT and resection; however, subsequent analyses suggest that the immunotherapy-associated late separation of survival curves can weaken the correlation between DFS and OS (7,24). Meta-analyses of perioperative immunotherapy regimens also report heterogeneity in the relationship between DFS/EFS and OS, especially when effective post-progression treatments are available (25,26). Accordingly, many oncology frameworks recommend retaining OS as a primary or co-primary endpoint in confirmatory phase III trials in the immunotherapy era, while positioning DFS/EFS as supportive or exploratory endpoints (27,28).

Pathologic response endpoints

In the nCRT era, pCR, most commonly defined as ypT0N0 (post-treatment pathologic stage T0N0 per the AJCC y-prefix nomenclature), came to be seen as an encouraging post-treatment finding, largely because patients who achieved it often went on to have better long-term outcomes. Ten-year CROSS data showed substantial long-term survival advantages among patients achieving pCR, supporting its prognostic value in conventional chemoradiation (5). Nonetheless, this prognostic relationship should not be over-interpreted as evidence that pCR is a valid surrogate for OS. A crucial distinction is between prognostic value and surrogacy: the former states that patients with pCR tend to live longer, while the latter requires treatment-induced improvements in pCR to reliably predict treatment-induced improvement in OS across patients and across trials. Recent evidence suggests that this requirement is not met in esophageal cancer, giving rise to what may be termed the “pCR paradox”—namely, pCR is associated with favorable survival at the patient level, yet increases in pCR do not consistently translate into proportional OS gains at the trial level (29).

The paradox becomes more apparent once individual-level and trial-level surrogacy are separated rather than discussed as if they were the same thing. At the individual level, the association is intuitive enough: patients with pCR often live longer, presumably because pCR marks a disease phenotype that is especially sensitive to treatment. But that is not the same as proving surrogacy. In an integrated individual-patient-data analysis of phase III trials in resectable esophageal cancer, pCR was associated with OS, yet no convincing evidence of true individual-level surrogacy was found (30). More recently, a 2026 PRISMA-based systematic review and meta-analysis in randomized neoadjuvant trials of gastroesophageal adenocarcinoma reported an individual-level coefficient as low as 0.006 and a trial-level R2 of only 0.06 (31). Taken together, they suggest that pCR is better viewed as a marker of treatment response than as a validated surrogate for survival.

There are several biological and methodological reasons why pCR may fail as a surrogate endpoint. pCR is ultimately a binary, anatomically local endpoint; OS is neither. Survival after multimodal therapy reflects much more than complete eradication of viable cells in the resected specimen. Micrometastatic disease, occult nodal involvement, perioperative morbidity, treatment toxicity, and the impact of salvage or adjuvant therapy all shape the final outcome. The issue becomes even more obvious when nodal disease is taken into account. Schroeder et al. reported that patients with complete regression in the primary tumor but persistent nodal metastases (ypT0N+) fared substantially worse than those with ypT0N0 (32). Thus, any pathologic endpoint based solely on the primary lesion risks oversimplifying systemic disease biology.

Esophageal data in the immunotherapy setting also argue against an overly pCR-centric interpretation. In PALACE-1, preoperative pembrolizumab plus chemoradiotherapy achieved a striking pCR rate of 55.6% in resectable ESCC, highlighting the capacity of immunotherapy-based regimens to deepen pathologic regression (33). Yet high pCR rates alone do not prove that pCR fully explains long-term benefit. Conversely, CheckMate 577 demonstrated that patients with residual pathologic disease after nCRT and surgery could still derive substantial benefit from adjuvant nivolumab, with significantly prolonged disease-free survival versus placebo (7). Clinically, this is highly instructive: The DFS benefit observed in non-pCR patients indicates that absence of pCR should not be construed as therapeutic failure. This helps explain why survival benefit in the immunotherapy era may be partly driven by a subgroup of durable responders who do not necessarily meet conventional pCR criteria at resection.

For these reasons, MPR, commonly defined as ≤10% residual viable tumor, may be a more informative complementary endpoint than pCR in immunotherapy-based studies because it captures deep but incomplete responses that may still carry favorable biological significance (12). Even so, MPR should not yet be overstated as a fully validated surrogate for OS in esophageal cancer. From a clinical and trial-design perspective, pathologic response endpoints should therefore be interpreted hierarchically: pCR and MPR are valuable for estimating depth of local tumor eradication and for postoperative risk stratification, but they should remain subordinate to mature survival endpoints when judging true treatment benefit (34). Future studies should separately report primary-tumor and nodal response, distinguish prognostic association from surrogate validity, and harmonize the use of tumor regression grade (TRG), major pathologic response (MPR), and immune-related pathologic response criteria (irPRC) to improve cross-trial comparability in the era of neoadjuvant immunotherapy.

Surgical quality metrics and composite outcomes

When perioperative regimens are intensified, the R0 (microscopically margin-negative) resection rate alone cannot serve as an indicator of surgical safety and feasibility. To promote uniformity among studies and also act as a basis on which to report international outcomes, the Esophagectomy Complications Consensus Group (ECCG) has developed standard definitions and grading of the complications associated with esophagectomy (16).

The textbook outcome (TO) is a composite quality measure that builds on this framework to include important patient-level factors including R0 resection, lack of significant complications (Clavien-Dindo grade 3 or higher), no unplanned reintervention, no unplanned intensive care unit (ICU) admission and appropriate hospital stay length. Multicenter cohort studies show that achieving TO is associated with better long-term OS, suggesting that TO is both a short-term quality indicator and a marker linked to prognosis (35,36). Higher-volume centers report higher TO rates and improved survival, underscoring the importance of institutional experience when interpreting the surgical feasibility of neoadjuvant strategies (37).

Patient-reported outcomes and quality of life

With longer survival, health-related quality of life (HRQoL) has become an essential fourth dimension for assessing the net benefit of perioperative strategies. The European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer (EORTC) core questionnaire (QLQ-C30), together with esophageal cancer-specific modules (QLQ-OES18 or QLQ-OG25), is the most widely validated toolset to assess dysphagia, nutritional status, social function, and global health (38,39).

Long-term follow-up from large RCTs indicates that perioperative pathways differ in their HRQoL trajectories: swallowing function may recover more slowly after chemoradiation than after chemotherapy, but long-term differences tend to attenuate (40). Early HRQoL data for immunotherapy-based combinations suggest potential advantages in functional recovery and symptom control over conventional chemoradiation, but longer follow-up is needed (41). The routine integration of PROs will facilitate a shift in perioperative care from a focus on survival alone to a broader assessment of net clinical benefit.

Induction strategies for esophageal squamous cell carcinoma

In China and much of East Asia, ESCC accounts for most esophageal cancer cases. Because ESCC commonly spreads locally and to regional lymph nodes, outcomes after upfront esophagectomy alone remain poor, with 5-year OS generally below 30% (1,2). Therefore, the pivotal clinical challenge in resectable, locally advanced ESCC is the selection of an induction strategy. The subsequent sections first examine established regimens [nCRT and neoadjuvant chemotherapy (nCT)] and subsequently discuss emerging approaches incorporating immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), including neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy (nICT) and neoadjuvant immunochemoradiotherapy (nICRT), focusing on oncologic benefit, perioperative safety, and implications for surgical planning (4-6).

nCRT versus nCT

Historically, nCRT has been the standard option for resectable ESCC because it combines radiotherapy for local control with chemotherapy for systemic effect, improving pCR, R0 resection, and locoregional control. The phase III NEOCRTEC5010 trial enrolled 451 patients with resectable, locally advanced ESCC and showed that nCRT (vinorelbine plus cisplatin with 40 Gy radiotherapy) followed by surgery improved survival compared with surgery alone: 3-year OS increased from 59.8% to 68.6%, and the pCR rate reached 43.2% (42,43).

The Japanese JCOG1109 (NExT) three-arm phase III trial compared nCRT (cisplatin plus 5-fluorouracil with radiotherapy), doublet nCT (cisplatin plus 5-fluorouracil), and triplet nCT (docetaxel, cisplatin, and 5-fluorouracil). nCRT produced higher pCR rates than doublet nCT, whereas triplet nCT achieved OS outcomes comparable to, or slightly better than, nCRT, suggesting that intensified systemic chemotherapy may better address distant relapse (44).

Systematic reviews and meta-analyses further delineate where nCRT is most beneficial. In a meta-analysis of 19 RCTs (3,168 patients), Zhao and colleagues found that nCRT provided greater survival improvement in higher-risk settings—such as cT3–4a and/or node-positive disease, mid-to-lower esophageal tumors, and higher tumor burden—where local invasion risk is substantial and radiotherapy’s local control may add value (45). In earlier-stage disease (cT1–2N0–1) or upper esophageal tumors, potential benefits must be weighed carefully against radiotherapy-related pulmonary and cardiac toxicity and perioperative complications such as pneumonia and anastomotic leak. In the CROSS trial, while nCRT improved survival, perioperative mortality increased slightly and grade ≥3 pulmonary complications occurred in 29% of patients (46).

Real-world studies add a practical perspective. Across cohorts, nCRT consistently increases pCR, but may also increase perioperative morbidity, and long-term survival differences versus nCT are inconsistent. In a propensity score–matched analysis from China’s National Cancer Center, nCRT yielded higher pCR but was associated with longer postoperative hospitalization and higher complication rates, with no statistically significant difference in 5-year OS or relapse-free survival (RFS) (47,48). For older patients, those with limited physiologic reserve, or settings where radiotherapy access is constrained, regimen choice should emphasize feasibility and toxicity management alongside individualized recurrence risk.

Neoadjuvant immunotherapy combinations

Multiple phase III trials of considerable quality have initiated the utilization of PD-1 inhibitors in the neoadjuvant setting, thereby altering the state of play. A phase III ESCORT-NEO/NCCES01 randomized trial of 391 patients with resectable, locally-advanced ESCC compared camrelizumab plus chemotherapy versus chemotherapy alone. nICT significantly increased the pCR and major pathologic response (MPR) rates. Importantly, these pathologic benefits are associated with improved survival without a clear increase in peri-operative complications or surgical delays (9).

nICRT is also under investigation. The phase II PALACE-1 trial evaluated pembrolizumab combined with chemoradiotherapy and reported high pCR rates, although pulmonary toxicity remains a key concern when radiotherapy and immunotherapy are combined (49). According to retrospective studies, propensity score–matched studies, and meta-analyses, nICRT may provide higher pCR in locally high-risk disease, but more grade ≥3 AEs, especially radiation pneumonitis (50). At present, there is relatively little mature head-to-head phase III evidence; the data so far suggest that nICRT may be more reserved for those with very high locoregional risk, whereas nICT seems to have a more favorable risk–benefit trade-off for a broader population (51).

It is increasingly essential to employ biomarker-informed treatment methods. According to the studies, Patients with PD-L1 expression (CPS ≥10) and an inflamed tumor microenvironment may benefit more from combinations of immunotherapy. This supports strategies for de-escalation (omit radiotherapy) in selected patients (52,53). Overall, immunotherapy-based neoadjuvant and perioperative regimens show promise beyond traditional approaches: nICT is a strong option for stage II–III ESCC, while nICRT should be applied with careful toxicity monitoring in locally high-risk cases. Surgical considerations and perioperative management

As immunotherapy is adopted in perioperative care for resectable esophageal cancer, surgeons face new practical considerations. Fick and colleagues reported that adding ICIs to standard multimodality therapy produced morbidity and mortality rates similar to historical controls, with consistently high R0 resection rates and no marked increase in technical difficulty or perioperative mortality (57). This suggests that, although immunotherapy can induce inflammation and fibrosis in the operative field, experienced teams can safely perform curative resection.

However, immune toxicity also presents new perioperative threats. Pneumonitis, myocarditis and colitis are severe examples of immune-related adverse events (irAEs), which can affect scheduled operative times and cause more postoperative complications (58). It makes the close MDT coordination a requirement, which also includes clinicians with experience in handling the toxicity of immunotherapy. According to the study conducted by Shirakawa et al., initial MDT intervention prior to the nCT minimized the occurrence of adverse events during the course of treatment and restricted the amount of weight loss between chemotherapy and surgery (59). Other research indicates the same—that specialized perioperative support teams may help minimize treatment-related toxicity and postoperative complications (60). All these data support the need to provide care under MDT leadership through induction therapy and postoperative rehabilitation and personalize supportive interventions, including nutrition, prehabilitation, and preventing complications.

Adjuvant immunotherapy after surgery also has not been established yet, particularly among those patients who have undergone neoadjuvant immunotherapy without pCR. The purpose is to eliminate microscopic metastatic residual tumors and minimize recurrences. Analyses on individual patient data show that recurrence risk remains even after therapeutic esophagectomy and that most of these recurrences occur within 2 years (61). In a patient with high-risk without pCR following neoadjuvant immunotherapy, adjuvant maintenance immunotherapy can be a sensible intensification strategy. Perioperative immunotherapy has been reported to enhance survival rates in patients with gastroesophageal cancers in a meta-analysis, however, final decisions rest upon mature OS results of large RCTs (62). Until more conclusive data are obtained, there should be consideration of specific postoperative pathology, fitness of patients and a delicate equilibrium between benefit and risk in the context of MDT.

Adenocarcinoma: perioperative chemotherapy versus nCRT

Historical landscape: perioperative chemotherapy vs. nCRT

For resectable EAC and GEJ adenocarcinoma, two perioperative strategies have historically coexisted: perioperative chemotherapy exemplified by FLOT, and nCRT exemplified by CROSS. FLOT was based on data in gastric cancer patients and the FLOT4-AIO trial confirmed that it has a survival benefit over more traditional anthracycline-based therapies, which makes it one of the essential standards in Europe to treat adenocarcinoma perioperatively (63). In contrast, the CROSS regimen—carboplatin and paclitaxel with 41.4 Gy radiotherapy—demonstrated durable survival benefit in esophageal cancer; 10-year follow-up showed an absolute OS gain of about 13%, along with higher pCR rates than chemotherapy alone (5).

For several years now, it has remained uncertain which approach is better. Retrospective studies and meta-analyses did not consistently show differences in OS or recurrence-free survival, but CROSS usually achieved greater pathologic tumor regression and without a clear long-term survival benefit (64,65). As a result, treatment selection in practice frequently depended on institutional experience and MDT preference rather than definitive comparative evidence. This uncertainty began to change with the publication of the ESOPEC trial.

The ESOPEC trial: redefining the perioperative treatment paradigm

The phase III ESOPEC trial constituted the first head-to-head comparison of perioperative FLOT versus CROSS-based nCRT in resectable EAC. It enrolled patients with cT1N+ or cT2–4a (N0–1) disease without distant metastasis and used OS as the primary endpoint. After a median follow-up of 55 months, the 3-year OS rate was significantly higher with FLOT, with consistent advantages in progression-related outcomes (21).

These results provide high-level randomized evidence that, in resectable EAC, intensified perioperative systemic chemotherapy offers greater survival benefit than nCRT focused on locoregional control. This finding challenges the prior view that CROSS and chemotherapy are broadly equivalent and has prompted rapid updates in several national guidelines. For example, the latest Onkopedia guideline now recommends FLOT as the preferred perioperative regimen for resectable EAC (66). Commentaries have likewise interpreted ESOPEC as a shift in emphasis from radiotherapy to improved control of micrometastatic disease through systemic therapy (67,68).

Regarding safety, both strategies were associated with substantial treatment-related toxicity, but with different toxicity profiles. Although grade ≥3 adverse events were slightly more frequent with FLOT, 90-day postoperative mortality was lower than in the chemoradiotherapy arm, highlighting that the risk structures of the two approaches are not interchangeable (21). Moreover, the nationwide cohort study reported by the Dutch Cancer Registry has demonstrated a 3-year OS for CROSS in routine clinical practice which closely matches that of the control arm of ESOPEC. This lends external validity, further reinforcing the clinical relevance of FLOT’s survival benefit in the randomized setting (69).

A subsequent prespecified analysis of recurrence patterns in ESOPEC helps put this systemic advantage into better biological context. After a median follow-up of 55 months, locoregional recurrence was essentially comparable between the two strategies, with a 3-year cumulative incidence of 20.2% for FLOT and 17.4% for CROSS. The separation emerged elsewhere. Distant recurrence was substantially less frequent with FLOT—31.5% versus 47.2% with CROSS fewer hematogenous metastases, and this difference was largely accounted for by fewer hematogenous metastases (70).

In other words, CROSS-based nCRT and perioperative FLOT achieved comparable locoregional control, and FLOT’s survival benefit was attributable almost entirely to better control of occult micrometastatic disease. This pattern is biologically consistent with EAC, in which early hematogenous and peritoneal dissemination—rather than failure of locoregional therapy—is the dominant cause of treatment failure. Seen in that light, the result is consistent with biologic expectations. The chemotherapy used in CROSS—carboplatin (AUC 2) and paclitaxel (50 mg/m2)—is delivered in a radiosensitizing framework and at relatively limited systemic intensity. FLOT, by contrast, exposes patients to four preoperative and four postoperative cycles of full systemic chemotherapy. That difference in systemic pressure is likely more important than any intrinsic limitation of radiotherapy at the primary site. Put differently, ESOPEC is probably not best read as a verdict against radiotherapy itself. What it really underscores is that, in resectable EAC, durable cure is increasingly constrained by the ability to control micrometastatic spread rather than by further gains in locoregional therapy alone. Treatment completion, toxicity, and real-world selection

Treatment completion is a key determinant of real-world effectiveness. Observational studies consistently show that completion of the postoperative (adjuvant) component of FLOT is limited, with only about one-third to two-fifths of patients completing the full sequence, which may blunt its potential survival benefit (74,75). CROSS, delivered entirely before surgery, tends to have higher overall completion rates; nevertheless, hematologic toxicity and radiation-related complications remain important concerns, particularly in older patients and those with comorbidities (76,77).

In community settings, outcomes with either FLOT or CROSS may differ from those seen in tightly controlled trials. Patient fitness, nutritional support, and socioeconomic factors can meaningfully affect tolerance and outcomes. Individualized treatment selection should therefore integrate tumor factors, patient factors, and care goals. From a biologic perspective, pCR rates are generally lower in EAC than in squamous disease, limiting the likelihood of deep response with chemoradiotherapy alone (78). At the patient level, sarcopenia is common in gastrointestinal cancers and is linked to treatment tolerance and postoperative complications; while systematic nutritional intervention has not consistently improved survival, it may increase completion of planned perioperative therapy (79). For patients seeking deeper response and potential organ-preservation strategies, neoadjuvant immunotherapy combinations are promising but require better patient selection, and current evidence does not support routine replacement of established pathways for all EAC patients (80,81).

Perioperative chemoimmunotherapy in gastroesophageal adenocarcinoma

The addition of PD-1/PD-L1 blockade to systemic therapy is a parallel shift in perioperative treatment. Although the phase III MATTERHORN and KEYNOTE-585 trials are commonly taken to suggest different conclusions, the overall picture may be more aligned than it initially appears.

MATTERHORN, a global double-blind phase III trial, randomized 948 patients with resectable stage II–IVA gastric or GEJ adenocarcinoma to perioperative FLOT plus either durvalumab or placebo. With a median follow-up of 31.5 months, the addition of durvalumab significantly improved event-free survival, nearly tripled the pCR rate, and later translated into a significant overall-survival benefit in the ESMO 2025 analysis, supporting durvalumab-FLOT as a potential new standard (14).

KEYNOTE-585 enrolled 1,007 patients, although only a minority received FLOT. Its primary EFS analysis was numerically favorable for pembrolizumab, yet the result did not cross the prespecified superiority boundary. Final OS showed the same pattern: directionally positive, but not statistically significant (82,83). On paper, one trial is positive and the other is not. Biologically, though, the gap is less dramatic.

Much of the divergence seems to come from trial construction rather than a genuine disagreement about checkpoint inhibition in this disease (84). MATTERHORN used FLOT uniformly, whereas most patients in KEYNOTE-585 received older cisplatin-fluoropyrimidine doublets, with only a minority treated with FLOT. Given the superior efficacy of FLOT over older perioperative regimens, this likely diluted the observable contribution of immunotherapy in KEYNOTE-585 (71). The problem was worsened by its hierarchical alpha-split design after EFS missed the prespecified boundary, OS could no longer be formally claimed positive. In contrast to other studies, MATTERHORN implemented checkpoint blockade with the strongest, most widely used chemotherapy backbone and a clearer statistical framework.

There are probably other possibilities aside from the chemotherapy backbone and statistical architecture. Enrolment in the two trials differed in geographical distribution, proportion of tumors with high PD-L1 CPS, occurrence of diffuse type histology and rate of postoperative treatment completion. The event curves observed may have also been influenced by differences in MSI-H/dMMR prevalence and in the use of subsequent systemic therapy after recurrence. The oversimplification of a multifactorial result runs the risk of a single-factor explanation, whether this is backbone, design or biology. The take-home message is that future perioperative trials of gastroesophageal adenocarcinoma should prespecify both a uniformly active chemotherapy platform and stratification by key biomarker subgroups.

Emergence of organ preservation and active surveillance strategies

Exploring non-surgical management for complete responders

In patients with a cCR and/or pCR after nCRT, active surveillance is gaining attention as a possible alternative to upfront surgery. Building on successful experience from anal cancer and other settings, the goal is to avoid esophagectomy—and its attendant morbidity, mortality, and impact on quality of life—when disease control appears to be sustained (85). The fundamental criterion is to detect patients who have minimal or even no residual tumor burden after a neoadjuvant treatment and suggest them personalized, function-saving opportunity.

The phase III SANO trial provides the rigorous evidence on this strategy, but its findings merit a critical interpretation rather than an unqualified endorsement. In 309 patients with cCR after nCRT randomized at the cluster level across 12 Dutch hospitals, 2-year OS was 74% with active surveillance versus 71% with standard surgery, formally meeting the prespecified non-inferiority margin of 15%—a margin that is notably wide for curative-intent disease (86). More importantly, only 35% of surveillance-arm patients maintained sustained cCR; 48% developed isolated locoregional regrowth and required postponed esophagectomy, while another 17% developed distant metastases. Thus, SANO demonstrates that esophagectomy can often be safely postponed, but not that it can be safely omitted, in current practice. The central limitation is the accuracy of cCR ascertainment itself. In the preSANO diagnostic cohort, even the optimal combination of bite-on-bite biopsies with endoscopic ultrasound (EUS)-guided fine-needle aspiration missed 10% of TRG3–4 residual tumors, and approximately 23% of non-pCR patients were misclassified as cCR (87). The Asian preSINO validation in ESCC reported a false-negative rate of 13.5%, with 18.3% of non-pCR patients misclassified (88). These error rates are not trivial for a strategy in which missed residual disease can translate into unresectable regrowth or occult distant spread.

Two complementary avenues are emerging to lower this error rate. Tumor-informed ctDNA monitoring adds a molecular dimension to response assessment: in a prospective cohort of 132 patients with locally advanced ESCC, integrating a personalized ctDNA assay with conventional clinical response evaluation raised the sensitivity for detecting non-pCR from 78–81% to 92–93%, and post-nCRT ctDNA-positive status was associated with markedly higher distant-recurrence rates than ctDNA-negative status (89). Prospective validation of an integrated algorithm—serial ctDNA, AI-augmented endoscopy with bite-on-bite biopsies, and 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT)—is the logical next step before organ preservation can be extended beyond carefully selected research settings. In parallel, the ongoing SANO-2 prospective cohort is designed to refine exactly this implementation question in real-world practice (90).

Successful implementation depends on accurate staging and response assessment, together with an intensive, standardized follow-up program. Assessment typically uses multiple modalities, including endoscopy, biopsy, and imaging. Endoscopic evaluation is critical: the 12th edition of the Japanese Classification of Esophageal Cancer introduced “remarkable response” as a new endoscopic criterion to describe pronounced tumor regression after treatment and to aid in selecting patients who may be considered for nonoperative management (91). At the same time, artificial intelligence is being explored as a way to make response evaluation more objective. A nationwide multicenter retrospective analysis from 46 Japanese centers developed a deep neural network using preoperative endoscopic images to predict pCR, with a reported accuracy of around 85%, suggesting a possible role in treatment decision support (92). Similarly, in the imaging field, radiotherapy dose map–guided deep-learning models that incorporate CT and dose distribution data have demonstrated good performance in predicting pCR after nCRT, and may serve as a noninvasive adjunct to standard evaluation (93).

At present, active surveillance after deep response remains a primarily research strategy. While initial evidence looks good, long-term oncologic outcomes require confirmation in large prospective studies. The implementation also relies on experienced MDTs to enforce strict selection, accurate assessment and timely follow-up of patients; and be underpinned by informed consent so that patients are aware of benefits and risks. As assessment tools improve and phase III trial results mature, organ-preservation approaches may become an option for selected subgroups of esophageal cancer (57).

Organ preservation in the immunotherapy era

Neoadjuvant immunotherapy combinations—especially ICIs paired with chemotherapy or chemoradiotherapy—have increased pCR rates in resectable esophageal cancer, providing a stronger pathologic basis for organ preservation. Malekzada and colleagues summarized early trials reporting pCR rates approaching 60% when immunotherapy is added to chemoradiotherapy in ESCC, and similarly high pCR rates with dual immune checkpoint blockade in mismatch repair deficient (dMMR) adenocarcinoma (94). Such high pCR rates imply that many patients have no viable tumor cells in resection specimens after induction therapy. This challenges surgery-centered paradigms and makes watch-and-wait (active surveillance) approaches plausible in patients who achieve a cCR, with the goal of preserving esophageal function and improving quality of life (4).

However, higher pCR rates do not automatically indicate proportional gains in long-term survival, and this caution applies with particular force to organ-preservation strategies, which rely on the accurate identification of true responders. Case reports describe patients who achieved cCR after combined chemoradiotherapy and immunotherapy and entered surveillance, yet later developed metastases at occult sites such as the small bowel that may not be detected early by current monitoring tools (95). This suggests that recurrence patterns in the immunotherapy era may differ from those after conventional chemoradiation, and that the surveillance infrastructure designed around CROSS-era biology may need recalibration. Prospective trials are therefore needed to define long-term OS and DFS outcomes with active surveillance after immunotherapy-based induction (96).

The move toward organ preservation also reopens questions about patient selection, surveillance intensity, psychosocial support, and the timing of salvage therapy. Selection should take into account the clinical stage and histology, treatment response (endoscopy, imaging, and new biomarkers like ctDNA), along with patient preference and comfort with uncertainty (97). It is critical that surveillance is vigorous and multimodal—usually involving scheduled endoscopy [possibly with artificial intelligence (AI) support], imaging, and liquid biopsy approaches like ctDNA, to detect MRD early (4). Since regular screening may cause anxiety, organized psychosocial care is a fundamental component of every organ-preserving plan.

The best prompt to initiate salvage treatment is also under discussion. Kamarajah et al. have compared definitive chemoradiotherapy versus nCRT followed by esophagectomy in a national cohort study, which informs trade-offs between strategies (98). Surgically speaking, Hoeppner observed that salvage esophagectomy can be performed and may offer oncologic results similar to those of planned surgery but tends to be related to increased rates of complications (99). The individualized intervention thresholds should be clearly identified to ensure a balance between the risks of local recurrence and surgical complications. French multidisciplinary practice guidelines also state that immunotherapy opens up new possibilities of organ preservation but also complicates the situation, necessitating cautious, evidence-based decision-making by an MDT (100).

Future directions in biomarkers and personalized treatment

Predictive biomarkers for treatment response

Predictive biomarkers are critical in perioperative treatment and particularly when immunotherapy is introduced increasingly, as predictive biomarkers can help to provide customized care. The clinical biomarker most commonly used in immunotherapy is PD-L1 CPS (101). Nevertheless, its predictive value in the perioperative setting is not as obvious as in the first-line treatment of advanced disease, and the best cutoff has not been determined yet and requires future prospective validation (102). This ambiguity, as well as the heterogeneity in response to immunotherapy, highlights the necessity of additional or different biomarkers.

The other two predictive markers include TMB and microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H)/dMMR. Strickland and colleagues reported that dMMR/MSI-H occurs in approximately 8–22% of nonmetastatic gastroesophageal adenocarcinomas and is strongly associated with benefit from ICIs (103). For these tumors, perioperative chemotherapy may add little, whereas chemotherapy-free immunotherapy approaches are promising—supporting routine mismatch repair and microsatellite instability testing at diagnosis in gastroesophageal adenocarcinoma.

Molecular subtyping based on gene expression profiles offers a framework for more precise perioperative treatment selection. Because esophageal cancer is highly heterogeneous, management must account for anatomic location, histology, and patient comorbidities. The identification of particular subtypes to specifically target groups that might get the most benefit from chemotherapy, radiotherapy or immunotherapy and help in the selection of strategies (104). Furthermore, there are alterations which can be acted upon. For instance, GEJ tumors with human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) overexpression or Claudin 18.2 (CLDN18.2) positivity have matched. The next important step entails the integration of molecular classification into clinical decision-making to improve outcomes (105).

In addition to tumor intrinsic factors, host factors may guide perioperative management. The C-reactive protein-albumin-lymphocyte (CALLY) index is an indicator of systemic inflammation and nutrition, has been associated with postoperative complication risk and their survival in esophageal cancer (106). Similarly, analyses of computed tomography body composition indicate that the presence of sarcopenia and sarcopenic obesity predicts postoperative complications after surgery for locally advanced EAC (107). Overall, biomarker development will be multifaceted and dynamic, near-future personalized perioperative management will be based upon data obtained not only from tumor cells but also from the immune microenvironment and condition of individual patients.

New therapeutic targets and combination strategies

As impactful as the PD-1/PD-L1 blockade was for therapy, many patients are still resistant to treatment either initially or after development of resistance. Consequently, there is increasing interest in other immune checkpoints like lymphocyte activation gene 3 (LAG-3) and T cell immunoreceptor with Ig and ITIM domains (TIGIT) as biomarkers and treatment options (108). At the same time, Zhu et al. reviewed genetically engineered T-cell methods such as chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell treatment and T cell receptor (TCR) engineered T cells, as well as CAR natural killer (CAR-NK) cells therapy, as possible options to consider in esophageal cancer (109). These treatments are intended to channel immune cells into identifying and attacking tumor cells, but their perioperative applications, safety, and compatibility with current therapies need to be clinically evaluated with great rigor.

With deeper understanding of esophageal cancer biology, targeted therapies against specific drivers are becoming increasingly relevant for selected subgroups. Valkema and colleagues highlighted HER2 as an important target in EAC, among others (110). CLDN18.2 is another emerging target that is highly expressed in gastric and GEJ adenocarcinoma (111). Updated Onkopedia guidance notes that the anti-CLDN18.2 monoclonal antibody zolbetuximab combined with chemotherapy has shown survival benefit in CLDN18.2-positive GEJ adenocarcinoma (66).

In addition to validated targets, preclinical work continues to identify new candidates. For instance, fatty acid desaturase 1 (FADS1) has been implicated in tumor growth through lipid metabolism and signaling pathways including phosphoinositide 3-kinase/protein kinase B (PI3K/AKT) and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK); inhibiting FADS1 can suppress tumor growth, suggesting a potential precision-therapy target (112). Other molecules such as TRIM31, LMNB2, and ZIP8 have also been linked to proliferation, apoptosis, epithelial-mesenchymal transition, or resistance to ferroptosis in esophageal cancer, and may serve as future therapeutic targets or prognostic biomarkers (113-115). Combining such targeted approaches with chemotherapy or immunotherapy may further improve outcomes in molecularly defined subgroups.

The rationale for nICRT also draws on the idea that radiotherapy can act as an in situ vaccine, while immunotherapy provides systemic immune activation. Reviews of radiotherapy-immunotherapy synergy in ESCC note that radiotherapy not only kills tumor cells directly but can also induce immunogenic cell death, release tumor antigens, and alter the tumor microenvironment, possibly improving systemic antitumor immunity (116). An important concern is to maximize the dose, target volumes, and their order with immunotherapy. The combination of radiotherapy with chemoimmunotherapy in recurrent or metastatic esophageal cancer has been linked with better OS and progression-free survival (117), and meta-analyses indicate that systemic therapy combined with local treatment (such as radiotherapy) may enhance outcomes especially in oligometastatic or recurrent disease (118). The future research is needed to establish how to increase the maximum level of immune activation and reduce the injuries to the normally functioning tissue, as well as what sequence is most appropriate to help develop individualized perioperative programs.

Conclusions

The field of perioperative resectable esophageal cancer treatment is experiencing a paradigm shift due to the use of immunotherapy. The nICT has produced meaningful improvements in pCR without any apparent elevation in perioperative complications in ESCC and is moving towards a novel standard. Nevertheless, there are still important issues concerning the choice of patients, the best sequence, and direct comparison with traditional chemoradiation. Lastly, active surveillance and organ-preserving approaches in patients with cCR are a move towards function-preserving care, however, long-term survival advantages will need to be demonstrated on prospective RCTs.

The overall effect of these developments is to move away the common decision-making process towards personal decisions based on responses of individuals. The optimization of multimodality combinations, especially the comparison of immunotherapy plus chemotherapy versus immunotherapy plus chemoradiotherapy, and the determination of optimal treatment sequences have become the major priorities. Biomarker studies represent the critical stepping stone between bench and bedside: PD-L1, TMB, ctDNA, and other measurements could be useful to predict response and schedule the timing of surgery. With patients of cCR, it is still unknown if organ preservation can ensure long-term survival and enhance quality of life. Lastly, the need to understand and address resistance to immunotherapy, such as via rational combination strategies, will be imperative to better improve the results.

The future changes indicate that with the development of precision medicine and MDT models, the treatment of resectable esophageal cancer will be more personalized and functional. Incorporating molecular subtyping, imaging-based evaluation, and pathologic response measurement, clinicians can find a way to customize multimodal plans in order to be safe and effective to each patient-extend survival and enhance quality of life significantly.
