Graduating prelicensure nursing students: Compassion satisfaction, burnout, or secondary traumatic stress?
Authors: Brittany Nicole Hudgins-Graham
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundProfessional quality of life is a factor that may influence the well-being of nursing students as they transition into professional practice. Nursing students, especially those nearing graduation, face significant academic and clinical demands that may contribute to burnout and/or secondary traumatic stress, while also fostering compassion satisfaction.PurposeTo examine professional quality of life among graduating prelicensure nursing students and to explore student-recommended interventions to support stress management.MethodsAn exploratory descriptive study using a mixed-methods approach was conducted using a convenience sample of graduating students (N = 44) from a university located in the southeastern United States. Participants completed the Professional Quality of Life Scale (ProQOL-Version 5). Subscale scores were calculated at the individual level and categorized into low, moderate, and high levels. Descriptive statistics, including means, standard deviations, and confidence intervals were computed. Internal consistency reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha. An open-ended question inquiring about recommendations for managing compassion fatigue or stress was analyzed using thematic analysis.ResultsParticipants demonstrated high levels of compassion satisfaction (M = 36.52, SD = 10.91). Burnout scores were predominantly moderate (M = 26.91, SD = 5.65), with 81.8% in the moderate range. Secondary traumatic stress scores were also primarily moderate (M = 25.61, SD = 7.95), with 54.5% categorized as moderate. Reliability was strong for compassion satisfaction (α = 0.97) and secondary traumatic stress (α = 0.87), and acceptable for burnout (α = 0.79). Qualitative analysis identified three themes: need for education, coping and support, and consideration of non-academic responsibilities.ConclusionGraduating nursing students experience high compassion satisfaction alongside moderate burnout and secondary traumatic stress, highlighting the need for proactive, evidence-based strategies to support student well-being and transition into practice.Keywords: professional quality of life, nursing students, burnout, compassion satisfaction, secondary traumatic stress

Introduction

Nursing care requires the integration of clinical competence, empathy, and emotional resilience. Registered Nurses (RNs) routinely encounter complex patient situations that may contribute to burnout, compassion fatigue, and secondary traumatic stress. Prior to licensure, nursing students are introduced to these demands through rigorous academic coursework and clinical experiences. This combination of didactic requirements and clinical exposure may place students at risk for psychological strain. Previous literature has identified stressors, such as workload, emotional demands, and competing personal responsibilities as contributors to burnout in nursing students. 1 However, variability exists in how students experience and respond to these challenges. 2 Professional quality of life, commonly measured by the Professional Quality of Life Scale (ProQOL-Version 5), includes three domains: compassion satisfaction, burnout, and secondary traumatic stress. Understanding these concepts in relation to nursing students nearing graduation is particularly important, as this transition represents entry into professional practice. Since nursing students are involved in patient care while balancing rigorous academic demands, they may experience burnout, compassion fatigue, or secondary traumatic stress.

The concept of professional quality of life has been increasingly recognized as a critical factor influencing the well-being and performance of healthcare providers, including nursing students.3,4 Professional quality of life encompasses both the positive and negative consequences of providing care and is conceptualized through three constructs: compassion satisfaction, burnout, and secondary traumatic stress. 4 Compassion satisfaction reflects the pleasure and fulfillment derived from helping others and contributing meaningfully through one’s professional role. 4 In contrast, compassion fatigue represents the negative aspects of caregiving and comprises two distinct yet related dimensions: burnout and secondary traumatic stress. 4 Burnout develops gradually in response to chronic workplace stressors and is characterized by emotional exhaustion, frustration, and diminished effectiveness, whereas secondary traumatic stress results from indirect exposure to the traumatic experiences of others and may occur more acutely. 4 Although burnout and secondary traumatic stress arise through different mechanisms, they frequently coexist and contribute to compassion fatigue, while compassion satisfaction may help offset the emotional demands associated with caregiving. 4 Understanding these dimensions of professional quality of life during nursing education may help identify opportunities to better prepare nursing students for the emotional demands of professional practice.3,4

The transition from nursing student to practicing RN represents a critical developmental period where individuals assume increased responsibility for patient care while simultaneously adapting to complex healthcare organizations. Understanding students’ professional quality of life at the point of graduation is essential, as early experiences of burnout or secondary traumatic stress may persist in professional practice and contribute to decreased job satisfaction, increased turnover, and negative patient encounters. 4 On the contrary, compassion satisfaction may serve as a protective factor to support resilience and sustained engagement in the nursing profession. 4

Due to the rigorous and intense nature of prelicensure nursing education, students are exposed to a large amount of didactic content, along with long hours in practicum settings, which may cause stress, burnout, compassion fatigue, or secondary traumatic stress. Extensive clinical education and increased time spent in the clinical environment can be a significant source of burnout. 5 The combination of class and clinical schedules experiences can enhance learning but can also result in both cognitive and physical fatigue. Furthermore, the high-stake environment of nursing schools can result in high levels of stress for students. Notable themes surrounding student-perceived stressful issues include discontentment, lack of social connection, living conditions, and unmet basic physiological and psychosocial needs. 6

More recent literature further substantiates the relationship between academic and clinical demands and adverse psychological outcomes, including burnout and compassion fatigue, among nursing students. 7 Workload intensity, inflexible curricular structures, and clinical practice requirements have been identified as key contributors to academic burnout. 2 Similarly, nursing students frequently experience psychological distress associated with demanding educational expectations and clinical exposure, which may negatively impact both learning outcomes and overall well-being. 8 Additionally, burnout is prevalent among nursing students globally and is influenced by environmental and individual factors, such as coping strategies and learning sites. 3

In addition to burnout, nursing students may experience secondary traumatic stress due to repeated exposure to patient suffering, illness, and death during clinical training. Although more commonly studied among practicing nurses, emerging evidence suggests that students are also vulnerable to these emotional stressors. 3 At the same time, clinical experiences may foster compassion satisfaction through meaningful patient interactions, highlighting the dual nature of nursing education as both rewarding and emotionally demanding.3,5 This duality reflects the complex nature of nursing education, where students are required to simultaneously develop clinical competence while regulating emotional responses in patient care experiences. The ability to navigate these competing demands may be developed through institutional support systems.

Recent evidence continues to support the importance of examining professional quality of life as nursing students transition into professional practice. 9 Findings suggest that professional quality of life is influenced by individual experiences and may be enhanced through supportive work environments. 9 Further, compassion satisfaction may serve as a protective factor that mitigates the effects of secondary traumatic stress, particularly when supported by positive psychological factors. 10 Collectively, these findings reinforce that professional quality of life is a multidimensional construct in which both the positive and negative aspects of caregiving coexist. Understanding how these dimensions manifest among new nurses may inform workplace strategies that promote resilience, psychological well-being, and professional readiness. 9

Although the literature identifies numerous contributors to burnout among nursing students, no single factor fully explains its development. 11 Since there is difficulty identifying the source of burnout for nursing students, there is a need to seek data to determine if compassion satisfaction, compassion fatigue, or secondary traumatic stress may exist amongst nursing students prior to graduation, which may impact their work performance once hired as a new-graduate RN. Additionally, if compassion fatigue or secondary traumatic stress occurs, nurse educators should investigate interventions to assist students with managing these feelings. Although there is increased recognition of burnout among nursing students, there remains limited research examining the full spectrum of professional quality of life at the point of graduation. Many studies focus on stress broadly or examine students earlier in their academic trajectory, which limits understanding of how these constructs manifest as students transition into professional roles. Further, few studies have incorporated student perspectives regarding potential interventions, which may be helpful for informing educational strategies.

The increasing complexity of healthcare systems, coupled with rising patient acuity and workforce demands, further underscores the importance of preparing nursing students psychologically for their professional roles. As healthcare environments continue to evolve, the expectations placed on newly licensed nurses have intensified, which requires both psychomotor competency and emotional resiliency. Consequently, understanding factors that influence students’ psychological well-being during their educational preparation is a critical priority for nursing education and workforce sustainability. 3

The purpose of this descriptive, mixed-methods study was to investigate: (1) graduating Bachelor of Science in Nursing (BSN) students ProQOL-Version 5 survey responses and (2) student-recommended interventions to assist students with managing compassion fatigue or stress. The significance of administering the ProQOL-Version 5 was to determine if students are experiencing compassion satisfaction, burnout, or secondary traumatic stress at the time of graduation prior to licensure. The hypothesis was students would experience high compassion satisfaction, moderate burnout, and low secondary traumatic stress.

Methods

This study implemented convenience sampling with a mixed-methods design by utilizing the ProQOL-Version 5 to examine compassion, satisfaction, burnout, and secondary traumatic stress among graduating BSN students. The study was conducted between Fall 2022 and Spring 2025, with data collected from graduating prelicensure nursing students during the Fall 2022, Spring 2024, Fall 2024, and Spring 2025 academic semesters. Eligible participants were recruited and completed the electronic survey after providing electronic informed consent. The purposeful application of a mixed-methods approach allowed for the integration of quantitative outcomes with qualitative insights to better contextualize student experiences. This design was selected for exploratory investigation where both measurement of outcomes and understanding of participant perspectives are needed to elucidate a more comprehensive analysis.

Ethical considerations

Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval from the university was obtained prior to engaging in the research study (IRB Protocol Number: 2022-10-04-03). All study procedures were conducted in accordance with ethical standards for research involving human participants. Eligible participants received an email containing a link to the electronic survey. The first screen of the survey displayed the electronic informed consent document, which described the study purpose, procedures, potential risks and benefits, confidentiality protections, and the voluntary nature of participation. Participants provided electronic informed consent by selecting “Yes” to participate before proceeding to the survey. This electronic consent procedure was approved by the IRB. No incentives were provided for participation.

Setting and sample

A convenience sample of graduating nursing students from a four-semester BSN program in the southeastern United States (US) were invited to participate in the study. Of eligible participants, 44 consented to participate in the survey and completed ProQOL-Version 5 and its entirety. The specific inclusion criteria for the study were: (1) age 18 years or older; (2) provision of electronic informed consent to participate in the study; and (3) prelicensure student enrolled in their fourth and final semester of either the Accelerated Bachelor of Science in Nursing (ABSN) program or the Traditional Bachelor of Science in Nursing (TBSN) program. Exclusion criteria included students who were not enrolled in their final semester of the nursing program, individuals under the age of 18, and those who did not provide electronic consent or did not complete the survey in its entirety.

A formal a priori sample size calculation was not conducted because this study was exploratory and employed a mixed-methods design to investigate professional quality of life among graduating prelicensure nursing students and student-recommended interventions. All eligible participants enrolled during the designated data collection semesters were invited to participate. Although the convenience sample was relatively small, it reflected the population of graduating students available at the study site during the recruitment periods. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted within the context of this single-site sample and should not be generalized beyond similar educational settings.

Recruitment procedures

After obtaining IRB approval, potential participants (N = 124) received an invitation via direct email per the IRB protocol to participate in the study from their designated program director. The use of program leadership to distribute the survey was intended to enhance visibility and participation while maintaining voluntary engagement. To minimize potential coercion, it was emphasized that participation was voluntary and would not impact on participants’ academic standing. All responses were collected anonymously using a secure electronic survey platform via Qualtrics.

Data instruments

Survey participants completed an electronic version of ProQOL-Version 5. This tool is a widely used, validated instrument designed to assess positive and negative aspects of helping professions, including compassion satisfaction, burnout, and secondary traumatic stress. It consists of 30 items divided equally among three subscales to capture a distinct dimension of professional quality of life within the previous 30 days. The instrument has been extensively used across healthcare populations and has demonstrated strong psychometric properties across diverse samples. 4 Usage of the instrument in nursing student populations is particularly relevant, as students are actively engaged in patient care experiences that may influence their psychological well-being. Additionally, the instrument’s ability to simultaneously assess both positive (compassion satisfaction) and negative (burnout and secondary traumatic stress) dimensions provides a comprehensive evaluation of professional quality of life, aligning with the aims of the current study. Survey respondents were asked to rate each item using a Likert scale (1 = Never, 2 = Rarely, 3 = Sometimes, 4 = Often, 5 = Very Often). The use of Likert-type scaling is consistent with standard psychometric approaches for measuring subjective perceptions and experiences in healthcare research. 4 The instrument has demonstrated adequate internal consistency in prior studies, with Cronbach’s alpha (α) reliability coefficients reported as 0.88 for compassion satisfaction, 0.75 for burnout, and 0.81 for secondary traumatic stress. 4 These values indicate acceptable to strong reliability and support the use of the instrument in assessing professional quality of life constructs among helping populations. Items designated for reverse scoring were re-coded prior to subscale calculation to ensure accuracy of composite scores. Scores were then categorized into low (≤22), moderate (23–41), and high (≥42) levels.

In addition to the standardized ProQOL-Version 5 instrument, participants were asked to respond to a single open-ended question designed to elicit qualitative insight into student perspectives: “What intervention(s) do you feel would be beneficial for faculty to offer to assist students with managing compassion fatigue or stress?” This item was included to complement the quantitative results by capturing context-specific experiences and recommendations that may not be fully represented otherwise. The inclusion of an open-ended question allowed participants to reflect and offer student-based suggestions, which enhanced the depth and relevance of the quantitative data. While the open-ended question component was not intended to establish a traditional mixed-methods design, it provided invaluable complementary insight.

Data analysis

Prior to data analysis, survey responses were reviewed for completeness. Participants who did not complete the survey were excluded from the quantitative analyses because complete ProQOL-Version 5 subscale scores could not be calculated. For the statistical analysis of study data, subscale scores were calculated at the individual level by summing item responses in accordance with ProQOL scoring guidelines. All statistical analyses were conducted using Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA, USA). Descriptive statistics, including means, standard deviations (SD), ranges, and 95% confidence intervals (CIs), were computed for each subscale to characterize the distribution of scores within the sample. Internal consistency reliability for each subscale within the study sample was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha (α). A significance level was not established per the descriptive nature of the study.

Qualitative analysis included all available responses submitted to the optional open-ended survey question. Responses were analyzed using thematic analysis to identify recurring patterns and themes within participant responses. An iterative coding process was employed to enhance methodological rigor and ensure consistency in theme development. Initial coding was conducted through a line-by-line review of each response to identify meaningful concepts relevant to the open-ended question. Similar codes were compared across participant responses, grouped into broader categories, and iteratively refined into themes through repeated review and comparison of the data. Coding and theme development were conducted by the primary investigator. To enhance the credibility of the findings, the final themes were reviewed multiple times and compared with the original participant responses to ensure they accurately reflected participants’ experiences and recommendations. Data saturation was considered achieved after analysis of the first 30 participant responses, with no additional codes or themes identified during analysis of the remaining responses. This approach was consistent with the study’s descriptive mixed-methods design and supported the integration of qualitative findings to complement the quantitative results.

The mean compassion satisfaction score among participants was 36.52 (SD = 10.91; 95% CI: 33.21–39.84), with scores ranging from 13 to 50. Over half of the participants (52.3%) were categorized within the high range, while 29.5% fell within the moderate range and 18.2% within the low range. Regarding burnout, the mean score was 26.91 (SD = 5.65; 95% CI: 25.19–28.63), with scores ranging from 15 to 39. Most participants (81.8%) were classified within the moderate range, while 18.2% were categorized as low and 0% as high. Lastly, secondary traumatic stress scores averaged 25.61 (SD = 7.95; 95% CI: 23.20–28.03), with a range of 16 to 47. Most participants were categorized as the moderate range (54.5%), followed by low (40.9%) and high (4.5%) levels. Internal consistency reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha (α). Compassion satisfaction (α = 0.97) and secondary traumatic stress (α = 0.87) subscales demonstrated strong internal consistency, while the burnout subscale demonstrated acceptable reliability (α = 0.79) within the current sample. These reliability coefficients indicate that the ProQOL-Version 5 subscales performed well within this sample, supporting the internal consistency and stability of the measured constructs.

This provided insight into shared student experiences related to coping and perceived gaps in support within the nursing programs. Responses to the open-ended question were analyzed and three primary themes were identified: need for education, coping and support, and consideration of non-academic endeavors. Analysis of qualitative responses provided additional context to the quantitative findings by providing insight into students’ perspectives regarding interventions that nursing faculty could implement to better support student psychological well-being. The theme of need for education reflected participants’ desire for more structured, practical, and actionable content related to stress management, resilience, and self-care. Students emphasized the importance of integrating meaningful strategies into the curriculum that extend beyond theoretical discussions. Participants indicated that while concepts such as burnout, compassion fatigue, and stress are introduced in the curriculum, there remains a perceived gap in translating these concepts into real-world application. This suggests a need for more experiential or skills-based learning approaches to be developed and implemented by nursing faculty.

Next, the theme of coping and support highlighted the roles of both peer relationships and faculty engagement in managing stress. Participants expressed a need for increased opportunities for discussion, debriefing, and faculty check-ins related to clinical experiences. Further, participants described informal peer connections in nursing school as essential for processing clinical experiences, while also emphasizing the value of intentional, faculty-led support mechanisms. These results suggest that social factors, along with fostering supportive learning environments, may play a critical role in a nursing student’s ability to manage negative psychosocial experiences.

Finally, the theme of consideration of non-academic endeavors uncovered the impact of external responsibilities, including work, family obligations, and personal commitments. Participants indicated these competing demands may limit their ability to engage in self-care practices and described the challenge of balancing academic expectations with external responsibilities. Of note, time constraints and competing priorities were frequently mentioned as barriers to their ability to implement coping strategies. This theme highlights the multifaceted nature of nursing student stressors, extending beyond academic and clinical demands.

When considered together, the quantitative and qualitative findings provided a comprehensive understanding of nursing students’ professional quality of life. While quantitative results indicated moderate levels of burnout and secondary traumatic stress alongside high levels of compassion satisfaction, qualitative findings offered important context regarding how students experience and interpret these phenomena within the academic and clinical environment. For example, the predominance of moderate burnout scores corresponds with qualitative reports of academic demands, limited opportunities for rest, and challenges balancing personal and educational responsibilities. Participants’ perceptions of insufficient time for rest and recovery while enrolled in an intensively-structured nursing program provide insight into factors that may contribute to moderate levels of burnout. This convergence of data sources proposes burnout among nursing students may be influenced by both structural components of the curriculum and life circumstances. Similarly, the distribution of secondary traumatic stress scores is supported by qualitative accounts of emotional responses to exposure to patient suffering while in clinical. Participants’ expressed the need for structured debriefing and coping support, which aligns with the presence of moderate levels of secondary traumatic stress observed in the sample. These results suggest that emotional support and opportunities for reflection may be beneficial considerations within clinical education environments.

Discussion

The study hypothesized that students would report high levels of compassion satisfaction, moderate levels of burnout, and low levels of secondary traumatic stress. Consistent with expectations, participants demonstrated relatively high levels of compassion satisfaction with substantial proportion of the sample categorized within the high range. This finding may suggest nursing students derive meaning and professional fulfillment from their clinical experiences. In alignment with the hypothesized level, burnout scores were predominantly in the moderate range, which may indicate the presence of ongoing academic and clinical stressors without evidence of high burnout.

Contrary to the initial hypothesis, secondary traumatic stress was not low, but rather most participants fell within the moderate range. Additionally, a small portion of the population reported high secondary traumatic stress levels. These findings suggest that nursing students may experience a greater degree of indirect exposure to patient trauma and emotional strain than originally anticipated, which is consistent with previous studies documenting secondary traumatic stress and compassion fatigue among nursing students during clinical education experiences.5,7 The relatively high compassion satisfaction scores observed in this sample suggest that many graduating nursing students derive professional fulfillment and meaning from patient care experiences despite the academic and clinical demands associated with prelicensure nursing education. However, the variability in scores indicates that not all students experience professional quality of life similarly, which may reflect differences in coping strategies, support systems, and external responsibilities.

Burnout scores were predominantly within the moderate range, suggesting that students may experience ongoing academic and clinical stressors during the transition to professional practice. Although no participants demonstrated high burnout levels, the concentration of moderate scores may reflect early manifestations of emotional exhaustion that could progress if unaddressed. These results are consistent with previous literature identifying workload intensity, clinical responsibilities, and role transition as contributors to burnout among nursing students.2,3

The results of this study contribute to a growing body of literature examining the professional quality of life among nursing students and provide important insight into the presence of burnout, compassion satisfaction, and secondary traumatic stress prior to entry into professional practice.5,7,10 By examining ProQOL-Version 5 scores at the point of program completion, this study offers a unique perspective on the transitional phase from student to practicing nurse. Participants demonstrated high levels of compassion satisfaction despite moderate levels of burnout and secondary traumatic stress. This pattern is consistent with emerging evidence suggesting that these dimensions of professional quality of life are interrelated rather than mutually exclusive.9,10 Although compassion satisfaction reflects meaning and fulfillment derived from providing care, it does not eliminate the emotional demands associated with the intensity of nursing education, repeated exposure to patient suffering, and increasing professional expectations experienced by students nearing program completion.9,10 Notably, graduating nursing students may simultaneously experience professional fulfillment while navigating emotional exhaustion and indirect exposure to patient trauma. These findings reinforce the multidimensional nature of professional quality of life and support the importance of assessing compassion satisfaction, burnout, and secondary traumatic stress collectively rather than as isolated constructs.4,9,10 While the present findings identify patterns of professional quality of life within this sample, they do not establish causal relationships and should be interpreted as a descriptive snapshot of students’ experiences at the point of graduation.

Nursing students may be able to lower their burnout levels by engaging in specific activities to reduce their burnout. Survey participants’ responses to the open-ended question highlighted the need for interventions to be offered to assist them with managing stress and burnout. The predominance of moderate burnout scores observed in this sample aligns with existing literature suggesting that nursing students are vulnerable to emotional exhaustion due to the demands of rigorous academic coursework, clinical responsibilities, and role transition stressors, which have been consistently identified as key contributors to burnout in this population. 2 However, simply recommending strategies for stress reduction alone may not be enough because there is not a consideration of confounding variables, such as employment obligations, financial responsibilities, caregiving roles, and competing personal commitments. While high levels of burnout were not widely observed, the concentration of participants within the moderate range may reflect early manifestations of burnout that, if unaddressed, could progress over time. These results may indicate the value of exploring supportive strategies and coping skill development during prelicensure nursing education. Moderate levels of secondary traumatic stress identified in this study suggest that nursing students may experience emotional strain associated with repeated exposure to patient suffering and traumatic clinical situations. Although only a small proportion of participants demonstrated high secondary traumatic stress levels, these results emphasize the importance of providing students with structured emotional support and opportunities for reflection during clinical training experiences.

Additionally, the high levels of compassion satisfaction identified quantitatively are reflected in participants’ continued engagement and commitment to their roles as future nurses. Despite reported stressors, students can create meaningful connections with patients and find a sense of purpose in their work.9,10 This may imply that positive feelings may coexist with psychological strain associated with caregiving. These findings may be explained by the competing demands experienced during the final semester of nursing education, including intensive academic expectations, clinical responsibilities, job interviews, and preparation for licensure.2,3 Together, these results suggest that nursing students experience a complex relationship between burnout and secondary traumatic stress and compassion satisfaction.4,9,10 This highlights the importance of addressing both individual coping strategies and broader curricular and structural factors within nursing programs. Institutional and curricular factors, including supportive learning environments, structured opportunities for reflection, mentorship, and resilience-promoting educational strategies, may positively influence students’ professional quality of life during this critical transition.11,12 Overall, these results provide a nuanced understanding of student experiences at the point of program completion and may help inform the development of targeted educational interventions.

Participant responses to the open-ended question highlighted the need for interventions to assist them with managing stress and burnout. The need for education reflected students’ desire for more instruction and discussion related to stress management, resilience, and self-care. While nursing curricula often include content related to burnout and compassion fatigue, participants view this as insufficient. Increased compassion fatigue is often associated with decreased psychological resilience among nursing students. 12 This highlights a critical gap between knowledge acquisition and skill application, which suggests the need for self-care and resiliency experiential learning strategies such as simulation, guided reflection, and skills-based workshops. 13 Faculty and practicing nurses may play an important role in modeling healthy coping strategies and fostering supportive learning environments for nursing students during clinical education experiences. 7 Incorporating opportunities for guided reflection, mentorship, peer support, and self-care practices may help students develop resilience and strategies for managing stress during transition into professional practice.11,12,14

The theme of coping and support emphasized the importance of support systems. Faculty should be champions for creating open learning environments that support inclusivity and safety. 14 Establishing consistent opportunities for reflection and dialogue may enhance students’ ability to process clinical experiences and build resilience over time. Further, consideration of non-academic endeavors highlighted that students have responsibilities outside of nursing school. These findings suggest that external responsibilities and competing priorities may influence nursing students’ ability to engage in self-care and stress-management practices during their educational preparation.

Overall, these results should be interpreted within the descriptive scope of the study. While the hypothesis was partially supported, the observed distribution of scores reflects variability in student experiences rather than definitive confirmation of predicted outcomes. The results highlight the coexistence of both professional fulfillment and psychological strain among nursing students, which underscores the complexity of professional quality of life during workforce preparation. Collectively, the results suggest that graduating nursing students may simultaneously experience positive and negative aspects of professional quality of life. The simultaneous experience of compassion satisfaction, burnout, and secondary traumatic stress reflects the complex emotional demands associated with prelicensure nursing education and preparation for professional nursing practice.

Implications for nursing education

The results of this study suggest the need to intentionally integrate stress management, resilience training, and emotional preparedness into nursing curricula. Professional quality of life should be considered an important educational outcome in nursing education, as educational experiences and supportive learning environments may influence both positive and negative dimensions of caregiving.7,10,14 Nurse educators should consider moving beyond awareness-based approaches and implement evidence-based, skill-building interventions that equip nursing students with practical tools for alleviating burnout and reducing secondary traumatic stress symptoms. Early introduction of these strategies, coupled with reinforcement throughout the program, may enhance students’ ability to cope with the demands of both academic and clinical environments and better prepare them for the transition into professional nursing practice.9,11,12 Additionally, educators should critically evaluate the structure and intensity of nursing programs to identify opportunities to reduce unnecessary academic and clinical stressors and promote opportunities for rest, recovery, and overall student well-being.2,3,7,8 Institutional and curricular initiatives, including faculty mentorship, guided reflection, peer support, structured debriefing, and wellness-focused programming, may further support students’ professional quality of life by fostering resilience and equipping students with practical strategies to manage burnout and secondary traumatic stress.11–14 Faculty must be intentional in fostering inclusive, psychologically-safe learning environments that support open dialogue surrounding burnout, secondary traumatic stress, and mental health. Further, integrating wellness-focused initiatives into the curriculum may ensure that nursing student well-being is addressed as a core component of academic preparation rather than an ancillary consideration.

Limitations and future research

A small sample size was a limitation because it limited the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, a formal a priori sample size calculation was not conducted due to the exploratory, descriptive nature of the study. Thus, the findings should be interpreted with consideration of potential limitations in statistical power and generalizability. Further, because this study was conducted at a single university in the southeastern US, the findings may not be representative of graduating nursing students enrolled in other nursing programs or geographic regions. The demographic composition of the sample was consistent with national nursing education trends. 15 However, limited diversity within the sample may reduce generalizability to more diverse nursing student populations. Since the study was not designed to compare ABSN and TBSN students, potential differences between these groups were not examined and should be explored in future research.

The descriptive, cross-sectional design further limits the ability to determine causality or changes over time. Despite this limitation, the present results contribute to an emerging area of inquiry and highlight the need for continued investigation. Furthermore, the use of convenience sampling and a modest response rate may introduce selection bias, as those who chose to participate may differ from those who did not. The relatively low response rate may reflect survey fatigue, competing academic responsibilities, and time constraints commonly experienced by graduating nursing students during the final semester. 16 This limits generalizability and increases the potential for nonresponse bias. Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted within the context of the study’s single-site design, convenience sampling approach, and modest response rate.

Additionally, faculty may be appropriate candidates to examine professional quality of life. Future research could be conducted at other academic institutions to explore professional quality of life among graduate nursing students and compare experiences across different levels of nursing education. Multi-site, longitudinal studies are recommended to better understand how these concepts evolve across time and educational contexts, as well as to evaluate the effectiveness of targeted educational and wellness interventions designed to support student well-being. Lastly, future research should also explore the impact of specific curricular and institutional interventions on student outcomes to identify best practices for promoting professional quality of life and enhancing student well-being throughout nursing education.

Conclusion

If nursing students experience burnout or secondary traumatic stress, nurse educators must recognize these perceptions and implement evidence-based interventions to support students. Early identification and education may improve coping and reinforce the need for proactive, rather than reactive, approaches to student well-being. Findings also highlight the importance of creating supportive learning environments and preparing students cognitively and psychologically for the demands of professional nursing through resilience-building strategies and thoughtful curricular design.

A current gap in research related to nursing student experiences with burnout, fatigue, and secondary traumatic stress limits understanding of how to best identify and support future nurses. Addressing this gap is essential for developing evidence-based interventions that promote student well-being and professional readiness. Nurse educators must acknowledge the presence of burnout, examine how educational practices may contribute to it, and identify strategies to better prepare students for the workforce while maintaining optimal mental and physical health. Ultimately, fostering a balanced professional quality of life among nursing students is essential for both individual well-being and the long-term sustainability of the nursing workforce.
