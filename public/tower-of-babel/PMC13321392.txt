The YAP-TEAD4 inhibitor M511-0965 suppresses triple-negative breast cancer progression by downregulating TGFB2
Authors: Mengxing Cheng, Xinyu Li, Na Song, Xinxia Yang, Xiaolei Ma, Haocheng Zhang, Yating Lei, Yanxin Jia, Lina Ba, Ruipu Zhang, Lidan Bai, Wenxiao Xu, Guofen Qiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•High TEAD4 expression correlates with poor prognosis in breast cancer patients.•TEAD4 is upregulated in TNBC cells and promotes proliferation, migration, and EMT while inhibiting apoptosis.•Structure-based screening targeting the YAP/TEAD4 crystal structure (PDB: 6GE3) identified M511-0965 as a small-molecule inhibitor with potent anti-tumor activity in TNBC; L366 and F415 are critical residues for its binding.•M511-0965 exerts its anticancer effects primarily by suppressing the downstream gene TGFB2.

Introduction

Breast cancer (BC) is the leading cause of cancer-related mortality among women worldwide, with over 2.3million new cases in 2022 [[1], [2], [3]]. Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) accounts for 10–20% of all BCs and is defined by the absence of estrogen receptor, progesterone receptor, and HER2 expression [4,5]. TNBC predominantly affects premenopausal women under 40, exhibiting high malignancy, aggressive progression, and poor prognosis [[6], [7], [8]]. Approximately 46% of patients develop distant metastases, frequently to the liver, lung, and central nervous system [9]. Current standard treatment relies on chemotherapy with anthracyclines, taxanes, platinum agents, or capecitabine [10,11], however, conventional adjuvant therapies offer limited efficacy, and residual metastases often lead to recurrence [12,13]. Novel therapeutic strategies and molecular targets are urgently needed, and a deeper understanding of TNBC molecular underpinnings is critical for their development.

The Hippo pathway is a highly conserved mammalian signaling cascade regulating development, tissue homeostasis, immunity, and tumorigenesis [14]. Upon pathway inactivation, YAP/TAZ are dephosphorylated, translocate to the nucleus, and bind TEAD transcription factors to drive downstream gene expression [15,16]. Dysregulated Hippo signaling promotes tumorigenesis and breast cancer metastasis in multiple murine models [[17], [18], [19], [20], [21]]. Since YAP/TAZ lack DNA-binding domains, TEAD is indispensable for their oncogenic activity [22]. As a downstream effector of the Hippo pathway, the TEAD transcription factor is considered the most crucial output of this signaling cascade. Direct disruption of the YAP/TAZ-TEAD interaction thus represents a promising and potentially safer antitumor strategy. Identifying safe, effective inhibitors targeting this complex may offer new therapeutic opportunities for TNBC.

Conventional drug discovery relying on whole-plant extracts faces challenges including difficult purification, low efficacy, and poor targeting [23,24]. High-throughput screening (HTS) and small-molecule libraries have revolutionized early-stage drug discovery by systematically testing thousands of low-molecular-weight compounds against biological targets [25]. Computer-aided drug design (CADD) further accelerates this process via virtual screening (VS), prioritizing active compounds prior to HTS and thereby reducing the number of candidates requiring synthesis and experimental validation [[26], [27], [28]]. CADD also flags compounds with poor safety or efficacy profiles—major causes of clinical trial attrition—and facilitates lead optimization to enhance efficacy and minimize toxicity [29].

On the basis of the above-mentioned research background, this study focused on the virtual screening of small-molecule inhibitors of YAP/TEAD4 via molecular docking technology. The aim of this study was to identify compounds that can inhibit the expression of oncogenes downstream of TEAD, thereby providing new ideas for the clinical treatment of TNBC.

Results

The level of TEAD4 was significantly increased in TNBC cell lines

First, we searched for a clinical association between the TEAD family and BC survival via the KM plotter database. We found that the abnormal expression of TEAD1-3 was closely correlated with the overall survival (OS) of BC patients but not with relapse-free survival (RFS). In contrast, high TEAD4 expression was significantly correlated with both the RFS and OS of BC patients. Specifically, the OS and RFS were significantly reduced in patients with high TEAD4 expression (Fig. 1A-1D). Next, we queried the MuTarget database. The results indicated that TEAD4 was highly expressed in BCs with mutations in the TP53, OBSCN, DMD and DYNC2H1 genes (Figs. 1E-1H). Additionally, we investigated the impact of TEAD4 mutations on BC progression via the cBioPortal for Cancer Genomics database. We selected the BC clinical sample database containing 2509 samples (METABRIC, Nature 2012 & Nat Commun 2016) and the BC clinical sample database containing 1108 samples (TCGA, Firehose Legacy) for query. The results demonstrated that BC cases with TEAD4 mutations were mostly TNBC (Figs. 1I, 1J), accounting for more than 60% of all cases. The tumor metastasis index in BC patients with TEAD4 mutations was significantly elevated (Fig. 1K), the age at tumor diagnosis was significantly younger (Fig. 1L), and the Nottingham prognostic index was also significantly increased, suggesting a worse prognosis (Fig. 1M).

Furthermore, we investigated the expression of the TEAD family in different types of BC cells. We used normal mammary epithelium cells (MCF-10A), HER2+ BC cells (SK-BR-3), ER+ cells (MCF-7, ZR-75-1), and TNBC cells (MDA-MB-231, MDA-MB-468, and HCC-1937) for detection. The results of qRT-PCR revealed that only TEAD4 mRNA was highly expressed in all BC cells and that TEAD4 expression was most significantly upregulated in the TNBC cell lines (Fig. 1N-1Q). The protein levels of TEAD4 were consistent with the mRNA levels (Fig. 1R). The abnormal expression of TEAD4 in TNBC implies that intervening in the expression and biological function of TEAD4 holds promise as a novel tumor therapeutic target.

The decrease in TEAD4 levels inhibits the malignant biological behavior of TNBC

Taken together, these findings suggest that TEAD4 plays a key role in the progression of TNBC. To investigate its function, we designed siRNAs and overexpression plasmids targeting TEAD4 and confirmed their transfection efficiency in MDA-MB-231 cells (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Fig. 1A). Functional analyses showed that reducing TEAD4 levels could significantly impair the proliferation, migration, and invasion abilities of TNBC cells, and promote cell apoptosis (Fig. 2C-2G). Correspondingly, Western blot analysis showed that TEAD4 reduction downregulated the migration-related protein MMP2 and the anti-apoptotic protein BCL2, but upregulated the pro-apoptotic protein BAX (Fig. 2B). In addition, immunofluorescence and Western blot experiments were performed to detect the expression of EMT markers (E-cadherin, N-cadherin, and Vimentin) to evaluate the EMT capacity of cells, and the results showed that reducing TEAD4 levels could effectively inhibit the EMT process (Fig. 2B, 2H-J). In contrast, high expression of TEAD4 was shown to significantly promote the malignant biological functions of TNBC cells (Supplementary Fig. 1B-1I). To further confirm the in vivo role of TEAD4, we established stable TEAD4-knockdown and TEAD4-overexpressing MDA-MB-231 cell lines, and constructed a nude mouse xenograft model. Small molecule inhibitor screening on the basis of the YAP-TEAD4 structure

TEAD4 is the ultimate nuclear effector of the Hippo pathway, and its interaction with YAP is the key to regulating the Hippo pathway response. Therefore, we searched the PDB database of human TEAD4 sequences (https://www.rcsb.org) to find the 6GE3 model, which shows the complex structure of TEAD4 and YAP (Fig. 3A). We then built a complete structural model via MOE (version 2019) software and discovered that 37 amino acids in the TEAD4 protein are involved in binding to the YAP protein, which is essential for the transcriptional ability of YAP (Fig. 3B). Through MOE-SiteFinder, we identified two potential small-molecule binding pockets (Pocket 1 and Pocket 2) in the TEAD4 structure, which contain multiple YAP binding sites. We selected the Pocket 1 region, which possesses a relatively larger atomic space, for virtual screening (Fig. 3C, 3D). The top 30,000 compounds with the best affinity for TEAD4 were selected from Chemdiv, and the affinity distribution is shown in Fig. 3E. (Compounds with affinities less than -17.5kcal/mol are marked in red, and compounds with relatively low affinities and high drug risks are shown in green.) After conducting a correlation analysis of affinity and water solubility, the five drug-likeness principles, molecular weight, in vivo absorption rate, binding-blood-protein risk, drug metabolism risk and other indicators, we obtained 765 compounds with affinity scores less than -17.5 kcal/mol. A total of 343 compounds (marked in red) with undesirable properties, such as drug formation risk, poor water solubility, and metabolic risk, were excluded, and a total of 422 compounds were screened out (Fig. 3F). To further analyze the structural diversity of these 422 compounds, cluster analysis was performed on the basis of common substructures of compounds with similar skeletons. The similarity threshold was set at 0.6. Among the resulting clusters, the first group contained compounds without a common skeleton (Fig. 3G). According to the structure groups, the affinity, drug value, five principles of drug class and water solubility were sorted and analyzed. A total of 199 compounds were selected. Next, protein-ligand interface fingerprint (PLIF) analysis revealed that K344, V316, T332 and C367 were high-frequency interacting amino acid sites, interacting with 116, 63, 50 and 31 compounds, respectively (Fig. 3H). Connective tissue growth factor (CTGF) has been reported to be one of the main downstream target genes of YAP and TEAD. It is involved in many physiological and pathological processes. Current studies have demonstrated that the expression of CTGF in tumor stroma can affect the proliferation, migration, and EMT of tumor cells, and it plays a role as an oncogene in different types of tumors. Western blot and ChIP experiments have shown that in MDA-MB-231, TEAD4 can bind to the promoter region of CTGF and regulate its transcription (Figs. 4A-4C). We used 127 compounds with clear structures from the above-mentioned compound screening results to detect the expression level of CTGF after treatment. The results showed that 56 compounds, such as M511-0965, could significantly downregulate the expression of CTGF to less than 80%. The top 14 compounds with good inhibitory effects are listed in Fig. 4D. Since M511-0965 showed the best inhibitory effect, we chose it for further study. We used a CCK-8 assay to determine the IC50 of M511-0965, which was approximately 1.135 µM (Fig. 4E). Fig. 4F shows the two-dimensional structure of M511-0965. Next, we used molecular docking technology to simulate the theoretical binding mode of compound M511-0965 with TEAD4. TEAD4 was presented in ribbon form, and M511-0965 was presented as a green ball form (Fig. 4G), and we plotted its binding site, as shown in Fig. 4H. The amino acids F229, V316, T332, V334, K344, L366, C367, M370, I395 and F415 of TEAD4 form a hydrophobic pocket, which enables favorable hydrophobic interactions and plays a major role in the interaction between M511-0965 and TEAD4. Importantly, two key binding sites were established between M511-0965 and TEAD4 residues L366 and F415. To validate the key binding sites of the TEAD4-M511 complex, M511-0965 and L366A/F415A-TEAD4 mutants were characterized via microscale thermophoresis (MST), and the Kd values of the compounds were analyzed on the basis of their affinity to TEAD4. As predicted by molecular docking, the Kd value of the L366A/F415A-TEAD4 mutant was significantly greater than that of wild-type TEAD4 (Fig. 4I, 4J), and the binding affinity was significantly lower. M511-0965 impairs the biological functions of TNBC by restricting the binding of YAP-TEAD4

To verify whether M511-0965 can inhibit TNBC cells, MDA-MB-231 cells were treated with different concentrations of M511-0965 (0.5 µM, 1 µM, 2 µM). Western blot analyses demonstrated that M511-0965 decreased CTGF levels in a concentration-dependent manner, while also decreasing the expressions of N-cadherin, Vimentin, MMP2, and BCL2, and increasing the expressions of E-cadherin and BAX (Fig. 5A). Functional assays revealed that M511-0965 significantly reduced the proliferation, migration, invasion and EMT abilities, also promoted apoptosis of TNBC cells (Figs. 5B-5I). Subsequently, we constructed subcutaneous xenograft models to e verify whether M511-0965 exerts its antitumor effects through TEAD4 in vivo (Fig. 5K). The results indicated that the tumor weight and volume in the M511-0965 treatment group were significantly lower than those in the normal saline control group, whereas overexpression of TEAD4 reversed the in vivo tumor-inhibitory effect of M511-0965, further demonstrating that TEAD4 is a key target for M511-0965 to exert its functions (Fig. 5L-5M).

TGFB2 serves as a downstream target of M511-0965 inhibiting TNBC progression

To explore the specific regulatory mechanisms downstream of M511-0965, we conducted RNA-seq. First, the DEGs were clustered. Among them, 458 DEGs were upregulated and 570 DEGs were downregulated (Fig. 6A). We performed Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analyses of the downregulated genes. These genes were enriched mainly in the cell cycle, the mitotic cell cycle, cell division, cell aging, DNA replication and other processes (Fig. 6B, 6C). On the basis of the above results, we subsequently conducted qRT-PCR detection of genes differentially expressed in the cell cycle pathway. TGFB2 encodes transforming growth factor beta 2 (TGF-β2), a highly functional cytokine that plays an important role in cell morphogenesis, proliferation, and migration and is a key regulator of the immune system[30]. Owing to its role in immune and stem cell regulation and differentiation, TGF-β2 has become a highly valued cytokine in the fields of cancer, autoimmune diseases and infectious diseases[31]. Western blot results showed that overexpression of TEAD4 could reverse the inhibitory effect of M511-0965 on TGF-β2.

TGFB2 is a key mediator through which M511-0965 inhibits TNBC progression

Subsequently, we constructed a lentivirus for the overexpression of TGFB2, resulting in a successful enhancement of TGFB2 expression in TNBC cells (Fig. 7A). Western blot analysis demonstrated that TGFB2 overexpression decreased BAX levels, increased BCL2 expression, and consequently, inhibited apoptosis. Moreover, TGFB2 overexpression promoted the EMT process and accelerated metastasis in TNBC cells. Importantly, M511-0965 reversed the apoptosis inhibition and metastatic promotion induced by TGFB2 overexpression (Fig. 7B). Functional experiments verified that M511-0965 counteracted the promoting effects of TGFB2 overexpression on cell proliferation, invasion and migration (Fig.7C-7I). Subsequently, we constructed subcutaneous xenograft models to explore the function and mechanism of M511-0965 in vivo (Fig. 7J). The results indicated that in contrast to the control group, tumors in the TGFB2 overexpression group were heavier and larger, whereas tumors in the M511-0965 group were lighter and smaller. Discussion

TNBC is a type of breast cancer with a poor prognosis[32], short survival time, high recurrence rate, and a high tendency to develop distant metastasis[33]. At present, the available therapeutic methods are very limited[34], and it is necessary to explore programs and therapeutic targets actively. This study confirmed that TEAD4 is highly expressed in TNBC cells and that TEAD4 knockdown can inhibit the development of TNBC, providing a new perspective for exploring the pathological mechanism of TNBC and selecting clinical therapeutic targets. Molecular docking technology can be used to investigate and optimize the structure of target molecules and search for potential targeted drugs through virtual screening[35], which has become an inevitable part of the drug design stage. Early interventions targeting YAP/TAZ activity focused on disrupting YAP/TAZ-TEAD binding. Verteporfin, the first compound to effectively inhibit this interaction, has been used as a photosensitizer for the treatment of macular degeneration[36]. As a transcriptional inhibitor of YAP/ TEAD, it selectively inhibits tumor growth in YAP-driven hepatocellular carcinoma mouse models[37]. However, its clinical potential is limited because of its associated cytotoxicity[38]. Starting with the structure of YAP/TEAD4, a key downstream regulator of the Hippo pathway, we conducted virtual screening and molecular docking techniques to analyze a small-molecule compound library and screen for inhibitors. Through molecular biology, cell function experiments and in situ BC model construction techniques, M511-0965 was confirmed to inhibit tumor growth and cell migration, promote apoptosis, Its anti-tumor effect depends on the inhibition of the YAP-TEAD4 interaction, and TEAD4 is a critical target for M511-0965 function. M511-0965 suppressed the expression of CTGF, a downstream gene of TEAD4. Subsequently, we screened the downstream target genes of M511-0965 via RNA-seq and found that M511-0965 was closely related to the differential expression of cell cycle-related genes, among which the expression of TGFB2 was most significantly downregulated.

The TGF-β2 protein encoded by TGFB2 belongs to the TGF-β family. Although most human tissues express TGF-β ligands and their associated receptors, tumor cells are an important source of TGF-β production[39,40]. Secreted TGF-β binds to its receptor and initiates signaling cascades in the nucleus through cytoplasmic signaling mediators (Smads). TGF-β/Smad complexes regulate the expression of downstream target genes and regulate cell biological functions[41]. In malignant tumor cells, TGF-β/Smad pathway is usually mutated[42,43], and the degree of TGF-β overexpression is correlated with the degree of malignancy of the tumor[44]. TGF-β can affect the progression of BC by inducing antiapoptotic effects in BC cells[48], inducing immune escape[49], and promoting proliferation and migration[50]. This study confirms that TGFB2 overexpression reverses the tumor-suppressive effect of M511-0965, indicating that TGFB2 is an important downstream effector gene of M511-0965.

Value and specificity of TEAD4 as a biomarker for TNBC

The results of this study provide multiple lines of evidence supporting TEAD4 as a biomarker for TNBC. First, high TEAD4 expression is significantly associated with both recurrence-free survival and overall survival in breast cancer patients, whereas TEAD1-3 are only associated with overall survival and show no significant correlation with recurrence-free survival. This difference has important clinical implications—recurrence-free survival directly reflects the risk of recurrence after treatment and is a core endpoint for evaluating treatment failure and guiding adjuvant therapy decisions. Therefore, TEAD4 has unique value in predicting recurrence compared to other TEAD family members.

Second, more than 60% of breast cancer cases harboring TEAD4 mutations are TNBC, and these patients exhibit significantly higher tumor metastasis indices, younger age at diagnosis, and poorer Nottingham Prognostic Index scores. These data strongly suggest that TEAD4 mutation status is not only an enrichment marker for TNBC but also a driver of aggressive phenotypes. Furthermore, using qRT-PCR and Western blot, we confirmed that TEAD4 is highly expressed in TNBC cell lines but is expressed at relatively low levels in HER2+ or ER+ cell lines. This expression profile indicates that TEAD4 expression level itself can serve as an ancillary diagnostic marker to distinguish TNBC from other breast cancer subtypes.

TGFB2: a downstream target of M511-0965

This study demonstrates that TGFB2 is a key downstream effector molecule of M511-0965, and that TGFB2 overexpression can partially reverse the tumor-suppressive effect of M511-0965. Therefore, high TGFB2 expression may predict primary or acquired resistance to M511-0965 treatment. Moreover, TGFB2 itself is a member of the TGF-β family and plays important roles in the tumor microenvironment, immune escape, and EMT[[45], [46], [47]]. Detection of circulating TGF-β2 levels or TGFB2 expression in tumor tissue may help monitor treatment response and guide combination therapy.

M511-0965 does not alter SIAH2 protein levels: mechanistic specificity and biomarker implications

SIAH2 is an E3 ubiquitin ligase that activates YAP by promoting proteasomal degradation of LATS1/2[51]. In breast cancer, SIAH2 expression is regulated by multiple signals: estrogen induces SIAH2 transcription via ER[[52], [53], [54]], whereas EGF/EGFR signaling downregulates SIAH2[53]. Additionally, SIAH2 protein levels are negatively correlated with ER status and are high in ER-negative, basal-like breast cancer[54], whereas SIAH2 mRNA levels are positively correlated with ER positivity[55].

We examined SIAH2 protein levels in MDA-MB-231 cells treated with M511-0965. The results showed that even at the highest concentration used in this study (2 µM), SIAH2 protein expression levels did not change significantly compared with the control group (Supplementary Fig. 1M). This negative finding has important mechanistic implications: it excludes SIAH2 as a direct target of M511-0965 and confirms that M511-0965 acts by directly inhibiting the YAP-TEAD4 interaction. This differs from known SIAH2 inhibitors (e.g., vitamin K3), which stabilize SIAH2 protein by inhibiting its auto-ubiquitination[56]. Therefore, M511-0965 represents an intervention strategy that is independent of SIAH2 and may avoid efficacy differences caused by heterogeneous SIAH2 expression. Furthermore, the fact that M511-0965 does not alter SIAH2 protein levels simplifies its interpretation as a biomarker—the efficacy of this inhibitor does not depend on the patient's SIAH2 expression status.

Limitations and future directions

Although this study elucidates that M511-0965 exerts anti-TNBC effects by inhibiting the YAP-TEAD4-TGFB2 axis, several limitations need to be addressed in future research. First, we need to further explore the dose-dependent cytotoxicity and in vivo safety profile of M511-0965. In future experiments, we will set different dose gradients and use CCK-8 assays and systematic immunohistochemical analyses to determine the cytotoxic and in vivo safety range of M511-0965, thereby providing a basis for its clinical translation. Second, we will investigate the synergistic anti-cancer effects of M511-0965 combined with conventional chemotherapeutic agents or TGFB2 inhibitors. Given that YAP/TEAD4 hyperactivation is closely associated with chemoresistance, we hypothesize that inhibiting YAP-TEAD4 with M511-0965 may actually reverse, rather than increase, chemoresistance—a hypothesis that will be tested in our future experiments. Third, we will expand the anti-tumor spectrum study to verify the inhibitory effect of M511-0965 on other tumor types and explore its broader clinical application potential.

In summary, this study identifies a novel YAP-TEAD4 inhibitor, M511-0965, which effectively inhibits triple-negative breast cancer progression by downregulating TGFB2. Our findings provide new insights into targeted therapy for triple-negative breast cancer and lay a foundation for the development of novel therapeutic strategies against this aggressive disease.

Experimental model and study participant details

Chemicals

The 127 small molecule compounds used in this study were purchased from TargetMol Chemicals Inc (Shanghai, China).

Cell culture

All the cell lines were purchased from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd (Wuhan, China). MCF-7, SK-BR-3, MDA-MB-231, and MDA-MB-468 cells were maintained in DMEM (Gibco), ZR-75-1 and HCC-1937 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin-amphotericin B solution (Solarbio, China). MCF-10A cells were cultured in special medium purchased from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. The cells were cultured at 37°C in 5% CO2.

Cell transfection

All plasmids used were purchased from Genechem Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The cells (1×105) were seeded in 6-well plates one day before the transfection experiments and grown in the appropriate medium supplemented with 10% FBS. After the cells reached 40-50% confluence, they were transfected in serum-free medium by using Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (Invitrogen, USA) for 6 h. The medium was then replaced with fresh medium containing 10% FBS, and the cells were incubated for an additional 48 h for subsequent testing.

Lentivirus transfection

For lentivirus transfection, cells were seeded in six-well plates in culture medium supplemented with 10% FBS. At 24 h after seeding, the cells were treated with 2×107 titration units of lentivirus packaging and synthesized by GeneChem (Shanghai, China). Polybrene was added at a concentration of 5 ng/μl to increase the efficiency of viral infection. The cells were harvested 4 d after infection for either RNA extraction or protein lysate preparation.

Xenotype model of breast cancer

Female nude mice (4-week-old) were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). The samples were randomly grouped, placed in independently ventilated cages and raised under SPF conditions. After the mice were acclimated to the environment (approximately one week), breast cancer implantation was performed. Approximately 5×106 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were digested via pancreatic enzymes and resuspended in 200 μl of matrix glue (Corning, USA). The suspension was injected into the second breast fat pad on the right side. The tumor volume (length × width × width ×0.5) was measured 5weeks later. The tumor tissues were collected, weighed and photographed.

Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

The viability of the cells was assessed by via a CCK-8 assay (Meilunbio, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The cells were cultured in 96-well plates and then transfected or treated with M511-0965. A working solution containing 10% CCK-8 was prepared in serum-free medium. A total of 100 μl was added to each well, the mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 2 h, and then the absorbance at 450nm was measured with a microplate reader.

5-Ethynyl-20-deoxyuridine (EdU) assay

The proliferation of the cells was examined via an EdU assay kit (RiboBio, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The cells were seeded in 24-well plates, incubated with EdU for 2 h, and then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. The proliferated cells and cell nucleus were stained with Apollo Dye Solution and Hoechst 33342, respectively. The slices were removed from the 24-well plates, observed and photographed under a fluorescence microscope.

Wound-healing assay

The cells were seeded into a 6-well plate. Upon reaching 100% confluence, the cell monolayer was lightly and quickly scratched with a sterile 10 μl pipette tip. After the debris was removed, the cells were cultured in serum-free medium for 12 h. Finally, the scratch wounds were observed and imaged by microscopy at × 100 magnification.

Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase-mediated dUTP nick-end labeling (TUNEL) staining

After being fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30min, the cells were treated with 0.3% H2O2-formaldehyde solution (30% H2O2: formaldehyde = 1: 99) for 30 min and 0.3% Triton X-100 on ice for 2 min. The TUNEL reaction mixture was prepared, according to the instructions of the TUNEL apoptosis detection kit (green fluorescence, Roche, USA). Slices were photographed under a fluorescence microscope.

Immunofluorescence

The cells were fixed with methanol for 10 min at 4°C and permeabilized for 10 min with 0.1% Triton X-100. The cells were then incubated for 1 h in blocking buffer (1% BSA and 0.1% Triton X-100) at 4°C and for 2 h in serum at 37°C. For immunofluorescence (IF), the cells were incubated with antibodies against E-cadherin (#AF0131, Affinity Biosciences), N-cadherin (22018-1-AP, Proteintech), and vimentin (#AF7013, Affinity Biosciences) at 4°C overnight. The cells were then rinsed three times with PBS and incubated with the appropriate biotinylated secondary antibodies for 1 h at 37°C. An Alexa Fluor 488 (Life Technolegies, 1: 500) antibody was used as a tertiary antibody for 1 h. The cells were counterstained with 0.5 ng/ml DAPI for 15 min at room temperature. Slices were photographed under a fluorescence microscope.

Quantitative reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

In accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions, total RNA was extracted with TRIzol Reagent and complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized with high-capacity cDNA reverse transcription reagent kits (#TYB-FSQ-101, Toyobo, Japan). The SYBR Green PCR Master Mix Kit (#24759100, Roche Diagnostics, Switzerland) was used for qRT-PCR to quantify target genes via the Light Cycler® 96 Instrument system (Roche Diagnostics, Basel, Switzerland). The primers (Supplementary Table S1) were purchased from Invitrogen (Frederick, MD, USA). The data were normalized to that of GAPDH (internal control) via the 2−∆∆CT comparative method.

Western blot

Total protein was extracted with RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, China), crushed by ultrasonication, and then treated with a BCA protein analysis kit (Beyotime, China). The protein concentration was detected with an enzyme-labeled instrument (Molecular Devices, USA). The proteins were transferred to nitrocellulose filter membranes (Pall Corporation, USA) via SDS-PAGE and blocked with rapid blocking buffer (GenScript, USA) for 17 min. The membranes were subsequently incubated with primary and secondary antibodies. The films were scanned and analyzed with an Odyssey infrared Imager (LI-COR, USA).

Anti-BAX (60267-1-Ig) and anti-BCL2 (26593-1-AP) antibodies were purchased from Proteintech. Anti-TAED4 (NBP1-32765) and anti-MMP2 (#AF5330) antibodies were purchased from Novus Biologicals and Affinity Biosciences, respectively.

Coimmunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

For Co-IP, lysates of 5×106 MDA-MB-231 cells were immunoprecipitated with IP buffer containing IP antibody-coupled agarose beads (MCE, China, HY-K0202), and protein-protein complexes were subsequently subjected to Western blotting. The labeled protein membranes were observed and quantified. Here, IgG was used as a negative control. Anti-YAP1 (14074T) was purchased from Cell Signaling Technology.

Virtual screening

Processing of receptor files

We used the TEAD4 structure file generated by protonation and structure optimization and the MOE-site finder to determine the docking area on the target for this virtual screening. Openeye's (Release 3.2.0.2) apopdb2 receptor tool was used to process and generate virtual filtered receptor files.

Preparation of a small-molecule compound library

We selected the Chemdiv compound library data for the virtual screening, which contained 1,535,478 small molecule compounds. To ensure the global conformation of small-molecule compounds during the virtual screening process, we use the plugin omega2 (Ver 3.0.1.2) in Openeye software to perform multiple conformation generations for small-molecule compounds, with an average of approximately 50 conformations per small molecule. Finally, we generate a multiconformation 3D conformation file for each compound library, which contains the 3D structure and compound information of each conformation.

Molecular docking

Docking experiments were conducted by TargetMol Chemicals, Inc (Shanghai, China). Molecular docking works on workstations with the Ubuntu kylin 15.10 operating system (CPU: 40; Memory: 64; SSD: 512G). We used the software FRED (Ver 3.2.0.2) to perform molecular docking on the TEAD4 combination pocket. Finally, we obtain the small molecule compounds with the best affinity for the selected pocket region. Their scores and docking patterns were saved for subsequent analysis.

Detection of compound binding sites

Protein purification

The TEAD4 gene was synthesized by Gene creat (Jinkarui Bioengineering, WuHan, China). The L366A/F415A-TEAD4 gene presented alterations in amino acid sites 366 and 415. The gene was then cloned into pET28a digested with BamHI and NdeI. E. coli BL21 transformed with the recombinant plasmid was cultured at 16°C for 16 h in a medium containing 0.2 mM isopropyl-β-d-thiogalactoside (IPTG). The supernatant of the bacterial lysate was loaded onto a Ni-NTA column to purify the protein.

Microscale thermophoresis (MST)

MST was conducted by TargetMol Chemicals, Inc. (Shanghai, China). The Kd values for TEAD4 or L366A/F415A combined with M511-0965 were determined via the Monolith NT.115 Pico instrument (Nanotemper Technologies GmbH, Munich, Germany). According to the manufacturer's instructions (Monolith Protein Labeling Kit RED-NHS second Generation; NanoTemper Technologies GmbH), the labeled proteins were incubated with RED-NHS second generation dye for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. M511-0965 was serially diluted in reaction buffer (50 mM HEPES, pH = 7.4, containing 0.05% Tween 20). The 100 nM labeled proteins were then added to a series of diluents of the compound at a 1:1 volume ratio. After incubation at room temperature for 30 minutes, the sample was examined via Monolith NT.115 in a capillary tube (MO-K022) at medium MST power and 5% LED/ excitation power. The Kd values were determined via the Kd fit of the Monolith affinity analysis from a tripartite experiment.

Statistical analysis

All the experiments in this study were repeated three or more times. The data were analyzed via SPSS 17.0 and GraphPad Prism 8.0 software and presented as mean ± SDs. Statistical differences between the two experimental groups were assessed by two-tailed Student’s t-test. The statistical differences among the experimental groups were evaluated using ANOVA (Tukey's test). The criterion for statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.
