The Integer-valued Moving-Average Random Field
Angelika Silbernagel *, Christian H. Weiß †

Abstract
. An integer-valued moving average (INMA) model for count random fields is proposed and investigated. Closed-form expressions are derived for both its marginal distribution and spatial dependence structure, for arbitrary model order. In particular, general expressions for bivariate distributions and autocovariances are provided. It is shown that the INMA random field can be equipped (among others) with a Poisson marginal distribution. It is also illustrated that different and well-interpretable dependence structures are possible.

1.
Introduction

The first stochastic models for real-valued and continuously distributed (two-dimensional) random fields were developed several decades ago. In particular, “plane counterparts” to the classical autoregressive (AR) and moving-average (MA) models known from time series analysis have been discussed extensively by many researchers, such as
19
;
3
;
10
;
2
. The modeling of discretely distributed random fields, by contrasts, has been widely ignored for a long time.
The first integer-valued ARMA-type model for count random fields (i.e., where the outcomes are quantitative with range
\mathbb{N}_{0}=\{0,1,\ldots\}
) has been introduced by
8
. There, the authors proposed a spatial analog of the first-order integer-valued AR (INAR
(1)
) model being known from the field of count time series. More precisely, the count random variables
(X_{s,t})=(X_{s,t})_{s,t\in\mathbb{Z}=\{\dots,-1,0,1,\dots\}}
are located on a two-dimensional regular lattice (grid), and the authors refer to their model as “first-order spatial INAR (SINAR
(1,1)
)”. However, in what follows, we follow the terminology of
14
and refer to this model as the INAR
(1,1)

random field
, in order to avoid confusion with other types of spatial count model and to indicate the grid structure of the generated data.

The INAR
(1,1)
random field model of
8
has been studied, modified and extended in many subsequent works, including
5
;
9
;
13
;
6
;
11
;
15
;
7
;
20
;
14
. Interestingly, it seems that the literature solely focused on AR-type models for count random fields so far. For simulating spatially dependent count random fields, however, it would be more convenient to use an MA-type model for count random fields as burn-ins for INAR random fields are computationally expensive, and the simulation of multilateral INAR random fields as considered by
11
is even more demanding. Furthermore, it is generally difficult to derive the stationary marginal distribution of INAR random fields. Both issues could be solved by using integer MA-type random fields instead. To our knowledge, however, such INMA random field models have not been investigated in the literature so far, we are only aware of a few applications within simulation studies, such as in
16
.

Therefore, in what follows, we close this gap in the research literature by adapting the INMA time series model (see
17
for a detailed discussion) to a unilateral random field structure, and by comprehensively inspecting its stochastic properties. Similar to the time series model, the INMA random field is based on the
binomial thinning operator
denoted by “
\circ
”, which proved to be an adequate substitute to scalar multiplication for integer-valued time series models
(
18
)
. Let
X
be a discrete count random variable with range
\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
a constant. The thinned random variable
\alpha\circ X
is defined by
\alpha\circ X:=\sum^{X}_{i=1}Z_{i}
, where
(Z_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}=\{1,2,\ldots\}}
is a sequence of independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) Bernoulli random variables with
\mathbb{P}(Z_{i}=1)=\alpha
, being independent of
X
. Hence, the conditional distribution of
\alpha\circ X|X
is the binomial distribution
\text{Bin}(X,\alpha)
, what explains the term “binomial thinning”. Here, the boundary values
\alpha\in\{0,1\}
can be included by the conventions
0\circ X:=0
and
1\circ X:=X
.

We define the (unilateral)
INMA random field
of order
(q_{1},q_{2})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{2}
with
q_{1}+q_{2}\geq 1
, abbreviated as INMA
(q_{1},q_{2})
, by

X_{s,t}=\sum^{q_{1}}_{i=0}\sum^{q_{2}}_{j=0}\beta_{ij}\,\circ_{s,t}\,\varepsilon_{s-i,t-j},

(1)

where
\beta_{ij}\in[0,1]
denote the model parameters,
(\varepsilon_{s,t})=(\varepsilon_{s,t})_{s,t\in\mathbb{Z}}
is a sequence of i.i.d. non-negative integer-valued random variables with mean
\mu_{\varepsilon}=\mathbb{E}\,\varepsilon_{s,t}
and variance
\sigma^{2}_{\varepsilon}=\mathbb{V}(\varepsilon_{s,t})
, and all thinnings concerning
different

\varepsilon_{s,t}
are performed independently of each other and of
\varepsilon_{s,t}
.
In (
1
), we have added an index “
s,t
” below the operator “
\circ
” in order to indicate that the thinning is executed at point
(s,t)
; we omit the index whenever it is clear. Note that we allow for the possible dependence of those thinnings that are applied to the
same

\varepsilon_{s,t}
, i.e., for the thinnings being summarized in the
(q_{1}+1)(q_{2}+1)
-dimensional vectors

\mathbf{Y}_{s,t}=(\beta_{00}\circ_{s,t}\varepsilon_{s,t},\ \beta_{01}\circ_{s,t+1}\varepsilon_{s,t},\ \dots,\ \beta_{q_{1}q_{2}}\circ_{s+q_{1},t+q_{2}}\varepsilon_{s,t})^{\top}.

(2)

More precisely,
\mathbf{Y}_{s,t}|\varepsilon_{s,t}
might exhibit (conditional) cross-dependence while different vectors
\mathbf{Y}_{s,t}
are independent of each other (more precisely, the
(\mathbf{Y}_{s,t})
are i.i.d.).
The possible cross-dependencies within
\mathbf{Y}_{s,t}
as well as their consequences on the stochastic properties of the INMA
(q_{1},q_{2})
model (
1
) are further detailed in Section
4
below. Furthermore, note that we do not need to impose any further independence assumptions between the thinnings and the “past values” of
X_{s,t}
(unlike in the refined INAR random field model according to
14
), as these are fully implied by the INMA
(q_{1},q_{2})
structure.

The remainder of this article is organized as follows. In Sections
2
and
3
, we investigate fundamental stochastic properties of the INMA random field according to (
1
), namely its marginal distribution as well as its spatial dependence structure. In particular, we derive closed-form expressions for the bivariate distributions and autocovariances at a given spatial lag
(k,l)
. In Section
4
, we present several special cases of the INMA random field, which appear to be particularly relevant for applications. They cover the Poisson INMA random field on the one hand, and special cross-dependence structures for the thinnings in (
2
) on the other hand. Finally, we conclude in Section
5
and outline possible directions for future research.

2.
Marginal Properties of INMA Random Fields

In this section, we focus on the marginal distribution of the (strictly stationary) INMA random field according to (
1
). It shall get clear that the marginal distribution is fully known and can be expressed in terms of the probability generating function (pgf). In particular, it shall turn out that this marginal distribution is not affected by the actual cross-dependence structure according to (
2
).
The following Proposition
2.1
summarizes all relevant marginal properties of
(X_{s,t})
, which are derived in close analogy to the time-series case discussed by
17
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
(X_{s,t})
be a (strictly stationary) INMA
(q_{1},q_{2})
random field satisfying (
1
). Then,

\mu_{X}=\mathbb{E}X_{s,t}=\mu_{\varepsilon}\,\beta_{\bullet}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\sigma_{X}^{2}=\mathbb{V}(X_{s,t})=\mu_{X}+(\sigma^{2}_{\varepsilon}-\mu_{\varepsilon})\sum^{q_{1}}_{i=0}\sum^{q_{2}}_{j=0}\beta^{2}_{ij},

where
\beta_{\bullet}=\sum^{q_{1}}_{i=0}\sum^{q_{2}}_{j=0}\beta_{ij}
. Furthermore, the pgf of
X_{s,t}
, i.e.,
\operatorname{pgf}_{X}(u)=\mathbb{E}u^{X_{s,t}}
, satisfies

\operatorname{pgf}_{X}(u)=\prod^{q_{1}}_{i=0}\prod^{q_{2}}_{j=0}\operatorname{pgf}_{\varepsilon}\big(1+\beta_{ij}(u-1)\big),

where
\operatorname{pgf}_{\varepsilon}
denotes the pgf of the i.i.d. innovations
(\varepsilon_{s,t})
.

The proof of Proposition
2.1
is provided in Appendix
A.1
. Proposition
2.1
shows that having specified the innovations’ distribution, we immediately obtain the resulting observations’ distribution. For example, see the details in Example
4.1
below, Poisson-distributed innovations lead to Poisson observations. The availability of a closed-form expression for the marginal distribution constitutes a major advantage over the existing INAR random fields, where generally only mean and variance of the observations are known.

Remark 2.2
.

Another advantage of INMA compared to INAR random fields is their ease of simulation. If an
n_{1}\times n_{2}
rectangular grid of INMA counts needs to be simulated,
n_{1},n_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
, i.e., the counts
X_{1,1},\dots,X_{n_{1},n_{2}}
, then

(1)

we first simulate the
(n_{1}+q_{1})\times(n_{2}+q_{2})
rectangular grid of i.i.d. count innovations
\varepsilon_{1-q_{1},1-q_{2}},\ldots,\varepsilon_{n_{1},n_{2}}
.

(2)

Second, for each innovation
\varepsilon_{s,t}
, we generate the vector
\mathbf{Y}_{s,t}
of all thinnings applied to
\varepsilon_{s,t}
, recall (
2
).

(3)

Finally, we create the observations
X_{1,1},\dots,X_{n_{1},n_{2}}
by simply adding the appropriate thinnings from the vectors
(\mathbf{Y}_{s,t})
, namely according to

X_{s,t}=\sum^{q_{1}}_{i=0}\sum^{q_{2}}_{j=0}\beta_{ij}\,\circ_{s,t}\,\varepsilon_{s-i,t-j}=\sum^{q_{1}}_{i=0}\sum^{q_{2}}_{j=0}Y_{s-i,t-j}^{(i,j)},

(3)

where
Y_{s-i,t-j}^{(i,j)}
denotes the
(i,j)
th
entry of
\mathbf{Y}_{s-i,t-j}
.

So only a few additional innovations have to be simulated, but a costly burn-in can be avoided. In addition, although not further considered in the present research, the sketched simulation approach would be easily extended to a
multilateral INMA random field
of the form

X_{s,t}=\sum^{q_{1}}_{i=-p_{1}}\sum^{q_{2}}_{j=-p_{2}}\beta_{ij}\,\circ_{s,t}\,\varepsilon_{s-i,t-j},

(4)

where the observation
X_{s,t}
does not only depend on the “current” and “past” innovations
\varepsilon_{s-i,t-j}
with
i,j\geq 0
, but also on “future” innovations
\varepsilon_{s+k,t+l}
with
k>0
or
l>0
. Here, one just has to simulate additional innovations in the first step, the remaining steps are as before. The simulation of the multilateral INAR random field
(
11
, see)
, by contrast, is much more demanding. So altogether, the INMA random fields offer a simple way of simulating spatial dependence in grid data and are thus useful for, e.g., simulation-based power analyses of tests for spatial dependence, such as in
16
.

3.
Spatial Dependence in INMA Random Fields

Already the first-order INMA
(1,1)
random field

X_{s,t}=\beta_{00}\circ\varepsilon_{s,t}+\beta_{10}\circ\varepsilon_{s-1,t}+\beta_{01}\circ\varepsilon_{s,t-1}+\beta_{11}\circ\varepsilon_{s-1,t-1}

involves more than one thinning operator at a location
(s,t)
, even if we set
\beta_{0,0}\equiv 1
. Consequently, each innovation
\varepsilon_{s,t}
is involved in the four thinnings
\beta_{00}\circ_{s,t}\varepsilon_{s,t}
,
\beta_{10}\circ_{s+1,t}\varepsilon_{s,t}
,
\beta_{01}\circ_{s,t+1}\varepsilon_{s,t}
, and
\beta_{11}\circ_{s+1,t+1}\varepsilon_{s,t}
. For the general INMA
(q_{1},q_{2})
random field,
\varepsilon_{s,t}
is even involved in
(q_{1}+1)(q_{2}+1)
thinnings, namely
\beta_{kl}\circ_{s+k,t+l}\varepsilon_{s,t}
with
0\leq k\leq q_{1}
,
0\leq l\leq q_{2}
as summarized within the vector
\mathbf{Y}_{s,t}
from (
2
). These thinning operators are probabilistic, so in analogy to the classical INMA
(q)
time series model, their joint conditional distribution
(\beta_{kl}\circ_{s+k,t+l}\varepsilon_{s,t},0\leq k\leq q_{1},0\leq l\leq q_{2}\,|\,\varepsilon_{s,t})
has to be specified, which may lead to different types of model for the same model order
(q_{1},q_{2})
, see
17
for a comprehensive discussion in the time series case. Altogether, the final spatial dependence structure of an INMA
(q_{1},q_{2})
random field is not only determined by the actual MA-structure, but also by the possible cross-dependencies within the vectors
\mathbf{Y}_{s,t}
.

Let us first take a bird’s-eye view of the INMA
(q_{1},q_{2})
model’s dependency structure, namely by considering a full
n_{1}\times n_{2}
rectangular grid of observations,
n_{1},n_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
, which we may summarize in the
(n_{1}+q_{1})(n_{2}+q_{2})
-dimensional vector
\mathbf{X}=(X_{1,1},\dots,X_{n_{1},n_{2}})^{\top}
. As already explained in Remark
2.2
, these observations are determined once

(1)

the
(n_{1}+q_{1})\times(n_{2}+q_{2})
i.i.d. count innovations
\varepsilon_{1-q_{1},1-q_{2}},\ldots,\varepsilon_{n_{1},n_{2}}
have been generated, and

(2)

all corresponding thinnings have been executed.

In other words, as soon as the
(n_{1}+q_{1})\times(n_{2}+q_{2})
vectors
\mathbf{Y}_{1-q_{1},1-q_{2}},\ldots,\mathbf{Y}_{n_{1},n_{2}}
, each being of dimension
(q_{1}+1)(q_{2}+1)
, have been generated, the counts in
\mathbf{X}
are determined via (
3
). This relation between
\mathbf{X}
and the
\mathbf{Y}_{s,t}
can be expressed by a simple matrix multiplication. Let
\mathbf{Y}=(\mathbf{Y}_{1-q_{1},1-q_{2}},\dots,\mathbf{Y}_{n_{1},n_{2}})^{\top}
be the
(q_{1}+1)(q_{2}+1)(n_{1}+q_{1})(n_{2}+q_{2})
-dimensional vector being obtained by merging all individual vectors
\mathbf{Y}_{s,t}
. Then, (
3
) implies an
(n_{1}n_{2}\times(q_{1}+1)(q_{2}+1)(q_{1}+n_{1})(q_{2}+n_{2}))
-dimensional matrix
\mathbf{B}
consisting of only the values
0
and
1
such that

\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{B}\cdot\mathbf{Y}.

(5)

For illustration purposes, we state the representation for the case
n_{1}=n_{2}=2
and
q_{1}=q_{2}=1
. Then, (
5
) simplifies to

\begin{pmatrix}X_{1,1}&X_{1,2}&X_{2,1}&X_{2,2}\end{pmatrix}^{\top}\ =\ \mathbf{B}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{Y}_{0,0}&\mathbf{Y}_{0,1}&\mathbf{Y}_{0,2}&\mathbf{Y}_{1,0}&\mathbf{Y}_{1,1}&\mathbf{Y}_{1,2}&\mathbf{Y}_{2,0}&\mathbf{Y}_{2,1}&\mathbf{Y}_{2,2}\end{pmatrix}^{\top}

with
\mathbf{Y}_{s,t}=(\beta_{00}\circ_{s,t}\varepsilon_{s,t},\ \beta_{10}\circ_{s+1,t}\varepsilon_{s,t},\ \beta_{01}\circ_{s,t+1}\varepsilon_{s,t},\ \beta_{11}\circ_{s+1,t+1}\varepsilon_{s,t})^{\top}
and

\mathbf{B}=\begin{pmatrix}0001&0100&0000&0010&1000&0000&0000&0000&0000\\
0000&0001&0100&0000&0010&1000&0000&0000&0000\\
0000&0000&0000&0001&0100&0000&0010&1000&0000\\
0000&0000&0000&0000&0001&0100&0000&0010&1000\end{pmatrix}.

We can see, for example, that the components of
\mathbf{Y}_{1,1}
(corresponding to the fifth block of columns in
\mathbf{B}
) appear in all four observations, which may lead to dependence between these observations according to the actual model specifications. It is also clear that the distribution of
\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{B}\,\mathbf{Y}
is fully determined by the distribution of
\mathbf{Y}
, where the latter can be given after having fixed some notation. Let
(Z_{s,t;r}^{(i,j)})_{1\leq r\leq\varepsilon_{s,t}}
denote the counting series involved in the thinning applied to
\varepsilon_{s,t}
at point
(s+i,t+j)
for
0\leq i\leq q_{1}
and
0\leq j\leq q_{2}
, so
\beta_{ij}\circ_{s+i,t+j}\varepsilon_{s,t}=\sum_{r=1}^{\varepsilon_{s,t}}Z_{s,t;r}^{(i,j)}
with
\mathbb{P}(Z_{s,t;r}^{(i,j)}=1)=\beta_{ij}
. Then, the
(q_{1}+1)(q_{2}+1)
-dimensional vectors
\mathbf{Z}_{s,t;r}:=(Z_{s,t;r}^{(0,0)},\ldots,Z_{s,t;r}^{(q_{1},q_{2})})^{\top}
are i.i.d. for all
s,t
and
r
, but there might certainly be dependence between the components within
\mathbf{Z}_{s,t;r}
. Now,
\mathbf{Y}
consists of mutually i.i.d. vectors
\mathbf{Y}_{s,t}
, where
\mathbf{Y}_{s,t}=\sum_{r=1}^{\varepsilon_{s,t}}\mathbf{Z}_{s,t;r}
. So the distribution of
\mathbf{Y}
follows from the pgf of
\mathbf{Y}_{s,t}
, which is given by

\displaystyle\operatorname{pgf}_{\mathbf{Y}_{s,t}}(u_{00},\ldots,u_{q_{1}q_{2}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big(\cdots u_{ij}^{Y_{s,t;r}^{(i,j)}}\cdots\Big)=\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathbb{E}\Big(\cdots u_{ij}^{\sum_{r=1}^{\varepsilon_{s,t}}Z_{s,t;r}^{(i,j)}}\cdots\Big|\varepsilon_{s,t}\Big)\Big]

(6)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathbb{E}\Big(\cdots u_{ij}^{Z_{s,t;1}^{(i,j)}}\cdots\Big)^{\varepsilon_{s,t}}\Big]=\operatorname{pgf}_{\varepsilon}\big(\operatorname{pgf}_{\mathbf{Z}}(u_{00},\ldots,u_{q_{1}q_{2}})\big).

Let us now take a closer look at the INMA
(q_{1},q_{2})
model’s dependency structure.
Following the approach of
17
, we derive closed-form expressions for the autocovariance function (ACvF) and bivariate pgf without specifying the cross-dependence within
\mathbf{Y}_{s,t}
or
\mathbf{Z}_{s,t;r}
, respectively. Only later in Section
4
, we consider possible choices of distributions for
\mathbf{Z}_{s,t;r}
, which are natural in the sense that they constitute natural generalizations of established models in the time series case. Our main theorem is as follows.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
(X_{s,t})
be a stationary INMA
(q_{1},q_{2})
random field satisfying (
1
).

(i)

The bivariate pgf with spatial lag
(k,l)
,
\operatorname{pgf}(u_{1},u_{2};k,l):=\mathbb{E}(u_{1}^{X_{s,t}}u_{2}^{X_{s-k,t-l}})
, is given by the expression

\displaystyle\operatorname{pgf}(u_{1},u_{2};k,l)=

\displaystyle\prod^{k-1}_{i=0}\prod^{q_{2}}_{j=0}\operatorname{pgf}_{\varepsilon}\Big(1+\beta_{ij}\,(u_{1}-1)\Big)\cdot\prod^{q_{1}}_{i=k}\prod^{l-1}_{j=0}\operatorname{pgf}_{\varepsilon}\Big(1+\beta_{ij}\,(u_{1}-1)\Big)

\displaystyle\ \cdot\prod^{q_{1}+k}_{i=q_{1}+1}\prod^{q_{2}+l}_{j=l}\operatorname{pgf}_{\varepsilon}\Big(1+\beta_{i-k,j-l}\,(u_{2}-1)\Big)\cdot\prod^{q_{1}}_{i=k}\prod^{q_{2}+l}_{j=q_{2}+1}\operatorname{pgf}_{\varepsilon}\Big(1+\beta_{i-k,j-l}\,(u_{2}-1)\Big)

\displaystyle\ \cdot\prod^{q_{1}}_{i=k}\prod^{q_{2}}_{j=l}\operatorname{pgf}_{\varepsilon}\Big(1+\beta_{ij}\,(u_{1}-1)+\beta_{i-k,j-l}\,(u_{2}-1)

\displaystyle\hskip 71.13188pt+\mathbb{P}(Z^{(i,j)}_{s,t;1}=Z^{(i-k,j-l)}_{s,t;1}=1)\cdot(u_{1}-1)(u_{2}-1)\Big),

which covers the marginal pgf from Proposition
2.1
(with
u=u_{1}u_{2}
) for
k=l=0
(using the convention that empty products are equal to 1).

(ii)

The ACvF satisfies

\gamma(k,l)=(\sigma^{2}_{\varepsilon}-\mu_{\varepsilon})\sum^{q_{1}}_{i=k}\sum^{q_{2}}_{j=l}\beta_{i,j}\beta_{i-k,j-l}+\mu_{\varepsilon}\sum^{q_{1}}_{i=k}\sum^{q_{2}}_{j=l}\mathbb{P}(Z_{s-i,t-j;1}^{(i,j)}=Z_{s-i,t-j;1}^{(i-k,j-l)}=1),

(7)

which covers the variance formula from Proposition
2.1
for
k=l=0
.

4.
Special Cases of INMA Random Fields

In this section, we present a couple of important special cases. First, we consider particular types of marginal distribution. A natural distributional choice for INMA models is the compound Poisson (CP) family
(
18
, Section 2.1.3)
, which covers the popular Poisson and negative-binomial distribution as special cases. Due to the CP’s invariance properties given in Lemma 2.1.3.2 of
18
, namely the invariance with respect to addition and binomial thinning, it is clear that CP-distributed innovations
\varepsilon_{s,t}
in (
1
) also imply CP-distributed observations
X_{s,t}
. Here, results become particularly simple in the Poisson case.

Example 4.1
.

We refer to (
1
) as the
Poisson INMA
(q_{1},q_{2})
random field
if its innovations are Poisson-distributed,
\varepsilon_{s,t}\sim\text{Poi}(\mu_{\varepsilon})
with
\mu_{\varepsilon}>0
. Then, the pgf of
\varepsilon_{s,t}
is given by
(
18
, Example A.1.1)

\operatorname{pgf}_{\varepsilon}(u)=\exp\big(\mu_{\varepsilon}(u-1)\big).

(8)

Thus, it follows from Proposition
2.1
that

\operatorname{pgf}_{X}(u)=\prod^{q_{1}}_{i=0}\prod^{q_{2}}_{j=0}\operatorname{pgf}_{\varepsilon}(1+\beta_{ij}(u-1))=\prod^{q_{1}}_{i=0}\prod^{q_{2}}_{j=0}\exp\big(\mu_{\varepsilon}\beta_{ij}(u-1)\big)=\exp\big(\mu_{\varepsilon}\beta_{\bullet}(u-1)\big).

Since the pgf uniquely encodes the probability mass function of a distribution, it follows that
X_{s,t}\sim\text{Poi}(\mu_{X})
with
\mu_{X}=\mu_{\varepsilon}\beta_{\bullet}=\mathbb{E}X_{s,t}=\mathbb{V}(X_{s,t})

(
18
, Example A.1.1)
.

Example
4.1
shows that the Poisson INMA
(q_{1},q_{2})
random field has both Poisson-distributed innovations and observations. The possibility of having a Poisson marginal distribution is a major advantage over existing INAR-type models for random fields, where only some moments of the marginal distribution are known so far.

Also the Poisson INMA
(q_{1},q_{2})
’s spatial dependence structure is characterized by simple closed-form expressions, as summarized by the following corollary to Theorem
3.1
.

Corollary 4.2
.

Let
(X_{s,t})
be a stationary Poisson INMA
(q_{1},q_{2})
random field according to Example
4.1
.

(i)

The autocorrelation function (ACF) from Theorem
3.1
becomes

\rho(k,l)=\frac{1}{\beta_{\bullet}}\sum^{q_{1}}_{i=k}\sum^{q_{2}}_{j=l}\mathbb{P}(Z_{s-i,t-j;1}^{(i,j)}=Z_{s-i,t-j;1}^{(i-k,j-l)}=1).

(9)

(ii)

The bivariate pgf
\operatorname{pgf}(u_{1},u_{2};k,l)
from Theorem
3.1
simplifies to

\operatorname{pgf}(u_{1},u_{2};k,l)=\exp\big(\mu_{X}\,(u_{1}+u_{2}-2)\big)\,\exp\big(\mu_{X}\,\rho(k,l)\,(u_{1}-1)(u_{2}-1)\big),

which is the pgf of a bivariate Poisson distribution
(
18
, Example A.3.1)
.

(iii)

Conditional mean and variance are given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(X_{s,t}\,|\,X_{s-k,t-l}=x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mu_{X}\,\big(1-\rho(k,l)\big)+\rho(k,l)\cdot x

\displaystyle\text{and}\quad\mathbb{V}(X_{s,t}\,|\,X_{s-k,t-l}=x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big(1-\rho(k,l)\big)\,\big(\mu_{X}+\rho(k,l)\cdot x\big).

(iv)

The distribution of “jumps” between
X_{s,t}
and
X_{s-k,t-l}
is determined by

\mathbb{P}(X_{s,t}=X_{s-k,t-l}\pm j)=\exp\Big(-2\mu_{\varepsilon}\beta_{\bullet}\big(1-\rho(k,l)\big)\Big)\cdot I_{j}\Big(2\mu_{\varepsilon}\beta_{\bullet}\big(1-\rho(k,l)\big)\Big),\qquad j\in\mathbb{N}_{0},

where
I_{j}(z)=\sum^{\infty}_{r=0}(\tfrac{z}{2})^{2r+j}/\big(r!\cdot(r+j)!\big),j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, denotes the
modified Bessel function of the first kind
. Therefore,

\mathbb{P}(X_{s,t}<X_{s-k,t-l})=\mathbb{P}(X_{s,t}>X_{s-k,t-l})=\Big[1-\exp\Big(-2\mu_{\varepsilon}\beta_{\bullet}\big(1-\rho(k,l)\big)\Big)\cdot I_{0}\Big(2\mu_{\varepsilon}\beta_{\bullet}\big(1-\rho(k,l)\big)\Big)\Big]/2.

The proof of Corollary
4.2
is provided in Appendix
A.3
. Again, it is worth pointing out the analogy to the time-series case, see Examples 2.8 and 2.13 as well as Theorem 3.3 in
17
.

The next group of special cases refers to the possible dependence of those thinnings that are applied to the same innovation
\varepsilon_{s,t}
. The subsequent discussion is inspired by that of the time-series case in
17
, where the
\varepsilon_{s,t}
are understood as expressing the size of some kind of “population” at point
(s,t)
. A binomial thinning applied to
\varepsilon_{s,t}
is then understood as the outcome of some random Bernoulli experiment being applied to the individuals of the
(s,t)
th
population (“generation
(s,t)
”), namely as the number of individuals being “successful” within this experiment. Then, different experimental scenarios lead to different cross-dependencies between the thinnings being applied to
\varepsilon_{s,t}
, i.e., to different cross-dependencies within
\mathbf{Y}_{s,t}
and
\mathbf{Z}_{s,t;r}
, respectively. In
17
, altogether four such scenarios are discussed for the time-series case, where the following two cases extend naturally to random fields.

Example 4.3
.

In accordance to the
independence model
for the classical INMA(
q
) process in
17
, which was originally proposed by
12
, our first special case assumes that the components of
\mathbf{Z}_{s,t;r}
are mutually independent, i.e., the aforementioned experiment is applied to the individuals of generation
(s,t)
each location
(s+i,t+j)
anew,
i=0,\ldots,q_{1}
and
j=0,\ldots,q_{2}
.
In this case,

\mathbb{P}(Z_{s-i,t-j;1}^{(i,j)}=Z_{s-i,t-j;1}^{(i-k,j-l)}=1)=\mathbb{P}(Z_{s-i,t-j;1}^{(i,j)}=1)\mathbb{P}(Z_{s-i,t-j;1}^{(i-k,j-l)}=1)=\beta_{ij}\beta_{i-k,j-l},

and the ACvF in (
7
) simplifies to

\gamma(k,l)=\sigma^{2}_{\varepsilon}\sum^{q_{1}}_{i=k}\sum^{q_{2}}_{j=l}\beta_{i,j}\beta_{i-k,j-l}.

This further simplifies to
\rho(k,l)=\sum^{q_{1}}_{i=k}\sum^{q_{2}}_{j=l}\beta_{i,j}\beta_{i-k,j-l}/\beta_{\bullet}
if the innovations
\varepsilon_{s,t}\sim\text{Poi}(\mu_{\varepsilon})
follow a Poisson distribution, recall (
9
).

The second special case is inspired by the so-called
sale model
in
17
, which was originally proposed by
4
.
Here, the original intuition was to assume the innovations to be the number of new items of some product with a fixed shelf life (determining the order of the moving average), where each item can be sold at most once.
While the original motivation behind the sale model clearly assumes a time context, we can adapt it to the random-fields case by thinking of regional spread in an agricultural setup. For example, assume that seeds are spread over a field, with the number of seeds sown per grid cell being
\varepsilon_{s,t}
. Due to wind, some of the seeds spread to neighboring grid cells, which is expressed by the thinnings applied to
\varepsilon_{s,t}
. Then,
X_{s,t}
is the total number of seeds finally arriving in grid cell
(s,t)
.

Example 4.4
.

For the “spread model”, suppose that
\mathbf{Z}_{s,t;r}\sim\text{MULT}(1;\beta_{00},\dots,\beta_{q_{1}q_{2}})
is multinomially distributed with
\beta_{\bullet}\leq 1
and parameter 1, i.e., at most one component in
\mathbf{Z}_{s,t;r}
is equal to one, all others are equal to zero. This assumption corresponds to each “individual” being “active” in at most one grid cell, like it happens for seed being spread over a field. For this model, it holds that
\mathbb{P}(Z_{s-i,t-j;1}^{(i,j)}=Z_{s-i,t-j;1}^{(i-k,j-l)}=1)=0
for
(k,l)\neq(0,0)
, since it is impossible for an individual to be active twice. Hence, the ACvF in (
7
) simplifies to

\gamma(k,l)=(\sigma^{2}_{\varepsilon}-\mu_{\varepsilon})\sum^{q_{1}}_{i=k}\sum^{q_{2}}_{j=l}\beta_{i,j}\beta_{i-k,j-l}\quad\text{for}\quad(k,l)\neq(0,0).

If the innovations are additionally Poisson distributed, with
\sigma^{2}_{\varepsilon}=\mu_{\varepsilon}
, then parts (i) and (ii) of Corollary
4.2
imply that spatial dependence vanishes, so the
X_{s,t}\sim\text{Poi}(\mu_{X})
are simply i.i.d.

The remaining two special cases of INMA
(q)
time series models, the “lifetime model” of
4
and the “changing-states model” of
1
, are difficult to extend beyond a time context and are, thus, not further considered here.

Finally, let us have a look at the vectors
\mathbf{Y}_{s,t}
of all thinnings being applied to
\varepsilon_{s,t}
, the pgf of which is given in (
6
):

\operatorname{pgf}_{\mathbf{Y}_{s,t}}(u_{00},\ldots,u_{q_{1}q_{2}})=\operatorname{pgf}_{\varepsilon}\big(\operatorname{pgf}_{\mathbf{Z}}(u_{00},\ldots,u_{q_{1}q_{2}})\big).

This pgf is necessary if one wants to compute the joint probability of a full rectangle
\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{B}\cdot\mathbf{Y}
of INMA
(q_{1},q_{2})
-counts, recall (
5
). For the independence model according to Example
4.3
, we have

\operatorname{pgf}_{\mathbf{Z}}(u_{00},\ldots,u_{q_{1}q_{2}})=\mathbb{E}\Big(u_{00}^{Z_{s,t;r}^{(0,0)}}\cdots u_{q_{1}q_{2}}^{Z_{s,t;r}^{(q_{1},q_{2})}}\Big)=\mathbb{E}\Big(u_{00}^{Z_{s,t;r}^{(0,0)}}\Big)\cdots\mathbb{E}\Big(u_{q_{1}q_{2}}^{Z_{s,t;r}^{(q_{1},q_{2})}}\Big)=\prod_{i=0}^{q_{1}}\prod_{j=0}^{q_{2}}(1-\beta_{i,j}+\beta_{i,j}u_{ij}),

(10)

while for the spread model according to Example
4.4
, we have

\operatorname{pgf}_{\mathbf{Z}}(u_{00},\ldots,u_{q_{1}q_{2}})=1-\beta_{\bullet}+\sum_{i=0}^{q_{1}}\sum_{i=0}^{q_{1}}\beta_{i,j}u_{ij}.

(11)

If considering a Poisson INMA
(q_{1},q_{2})
random field according to Example
4.1
with
\operatorname{pgf}_{\varepsilon}(u)=\exp\big(\mu_{\varepsilon}(u-1)\big)
, recall (
8
), then (
10
) and (
11
) further simplify. In particular, for the spread model, we obtain

\operatorname{pgf}_{\mathbf{Y}}(u_{00},\ldots,u_{q_{1}q_{2}})=\exp\Big[\mu_{\varepsilon}\Big(\sum_{i=0}^{q_{1}}\sum_{i=0}^{q_{1}}\beta_{i,j}(u_{ij}-1)\Big)\Big]=\prod_{i=0}^{q_{1}}\prod_{i=0}^{q_{1}}\exp\big(\mu_{\varepsilon}\beta_{i,j}(u_{ij}-1)\big),

(12)

so
\mathbf{Y}_{s,t}
(and thus
\mathbf{X}
) consists of independent Poisson counts.

5.
Conclusions and Future Research

In this article, we introduced an INMA-type random field model for counts being observed on a two-dimensional regular grid. We derived closed-form expressions for both its marginal distribution and spatial dependence structure under arbitrary model order. In particular, general expressions for bivariate distributions (pgf) and autocovariances at a specified spatial lag
(k,l)
are provided. We showed that the INMA random field can be equipped with a Poisson marginal distribution, which differs from existing INAR-type random field models in the literature. We also illustrated that different and well-interpretable dependence structures are possible, by developing the special INMA cases of the independence and spread model.

While the present article solely focused on specifications and stochastic properties of INMA random fields, future research should be directed towards statistical inference. In particular, methods for parameter estimation should be developed and investigated. Furthermore, it would be of practical relevance if also INAR-type random fields with a Poisson (or another common) marginal distribution could be developed. Finally, the extension of INARMA approaches to random fields with other types of discrete-valued range (such as signed integers or bounded counts) would be an interesting task for future research.

A.1.
Proof of Proposition
2.1

The first expression is an immediate consequence of the fact that for all constants
\alpha\in[0,1]
and random variables
X
, it holds
\alpha\circ X|X\sim\text{Bin}(X,\alpha)
and hence, by the law of total expectation,
\mathbb{E}(\alpha\circ X)=\mathbb{E}(\mathbb{E}(\alpha\circ X|X))=\alpha\,\mathbb{E}X
. Since
(\varepsilon_{s,t})
is i.i.d., and since the thinnings applied to different
\varepsilon_{s,t}

are independent of each other as well, Lemma 1 of
14
implies

\mathbb{V}(X_{s,t})=\sum^{q_{1}}_{i=0}\sum^{q_{2}}_{j=0}\mathbb{V}(\beta_{ij}\circ\varepsilon_{s-i,t-j})=\sum^{q_{1}}_{i=0}\sum^{q_{2}}_{j=0}\Big(\beta_{ij}^{2}\sigma^{2}_{\varepsilon}+\beta_{ij}(1-\beta_{ij})\mu_{\varepsilon}\Big),

which leads to the desired expression. Note that for a random variable
X
and a constant
\alpha\in[0,1]
, it holds

\mathbb{V}(\alpha\circ X)=\mathbb{V}(\mathbb{E}(\alpha\circ X|X))+\mathbb{E}(\mathbb{V}(\alpha\circ X|X))=\mathbb{V}(\alpha\cdot X)+\alpha(1-\alpha)\mathbb{E}X\neq\mathbb{V}(\alpha\cdot X),

see
18
, for instance.
With regard to the pgf, first we consider
\operatorname{pgf}_{\beta\circ\varepsilon}(u)=\mathbb{E}(u^{\beta\circ\varepsilon})
. Let
(Z_{r})=(Z_{r})_{r\in\mathbb{N}}
denote the i.i.d. counting series corresponding to the binomial thinning
\beta\circ\varepsilon:=\sum^{\varepsilon}_{r=1}Z_{r}
. Then,

\operatorname{pgf}_{\beta\circ\varepsilon}(u)=\mathbb{E}\left(\mathbb{E}(u^{\sum^{\varepsilon}_{r=1}Z_{r}}\,|\,\varepsilon)\right)=\mathbb{E}\Big(\big(\mathbb{E}(u^{Z_{1}})\big)^{\varepsilon}\Big)=\mathbb{E}\Big(\big((1-\beta)u^{0}+\beta\,u\big)^{\varepsilon}\Big)=\operatorname{pgf}_{\varepsilon}\big(1+\beta(u-1)\big).

(13)

Hence, it follows that

\displaystyle\operatorname{pgf}_{X}(u)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(\prod^{q_{1}}_{i=0}\prod^{q_{2}}_{j=0}u^{\beta_{ij}\circ\varepsilon_{s-i,t-j}}\right)

\displaystyle=\prod^{q_{1}}_{i=0}\prod^{q_{2}}_{j=0}\mathbb{E}\left(\mathbb{E}\left(u^{\beta_{ij}\circ\varepsilon_{s-i,t-j}}\,\big|\,\varepsilon_{s-i,t-j}\right)\right)=\prod^{q_{1}}_{i=0}\prod^{q_{2}}_{j=0}\operatorname{pgf}_{\varepsilon}\big(1-\beta_{ij}(u-1)\big),

and the proof of Proposition
2.1
is complete.

\displaystyle\operatorname{pgf}(u_{1},u_{2};k,l)

The first four expected values can be determined by using (

\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{s-i,t-j}

Z_{r}^{(i,j)}=Z_{s-i,t-j;r}^{(i,j)}

Z_{r}^{(i-k,j-l)}=Z_{s-i,t-j;r}^{(i-k,j-l)}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big(u_{1}^{\beta_{ij}\circ\varepsilon}u_{2}^{\beta_{i-k,j-l}\circ\varepsilon}\big)

Since

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Z^{(i,j)}_{1}=1,Z^{(i-k,j-l)}_{1}=0)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big(u_{1}^{Z^{(i,j)}}u_{2}^{Z^{(i-k,j-l)}}\big)

\displaystyle\gamma(k,l)

\displaystyle\text{Cov}(\beta_{i,j}

\displaystyle\circ_{s,t}\varepsilon,\,\beta_{i-k,j-l}\circ_{s+k,t+l}\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\text{Cov}\Big(\mathbb{E}(\beta_{i,j}\circ_{s,t}\varepsilon\,|\,\varepsilon),\mathbb{E}(\beta_{i-k,j-l}\circ_{s+k,t+l}\varepsilon\,|\,\varepsilon)\Big)+\mathbb{E}\Big(\text{Cov}(\beta_{i,j}\circ_{s,t}\varepsilon,\,\beta_{i-k,j-l}\circ_{s+k,t+l}\varepsilon\,|\,\varepsilon)\Big)
