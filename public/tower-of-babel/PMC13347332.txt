Green Descaling of Gypsum Deposits in Industrial Pipelines: A Chelant-Free Approach
Authors: Faisal Alissa, Shahad Bamigdad, Manal Alqahtani, Martin Andersson, Abdulrhman Alsulmi, Abdualilah Al-baiz, Tariq Almubarak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Gypsum scale (CaSO4·2H2O) is a persistent and costly problem in industries such as oil and gas, power generation, and water treatment. Conventional removal strategies rely heavily on aminopolycarboxylate chelants such as EDTA, which, despite their effectiveness, present significant environmental drawbacks due to their persistence, potential for heavy metal mobilization, and high treatment costs. This study introduces a fully chelant-free gypsum remediation method based on potassium carbonate (K2CO3) combined with biodegradable carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC). Using in situ FT-IR spectroscopy, SEM/XRD characterization, and geochemical speciation coupled with microkinetic modeling, we elucidate the kinetics, mechanisms, and polymorph-selective pathways governing gypsum conversion. Carbonate bases, particularly K2CO3, achieved up to 96% conversion under ambient conditions, predominantly forming vaterite, which exhibited a markedly slower transformation to calcite compared to bicarbonate systems. The addition of CMC at optimized dosage prevented secondary blockages by maintaining converted particles in suspension, with only minimal impact on conversion efficiency. By integrating a chelant-free K2CO3 conversion process with mechanistic control over CaCO3 polymorphism and a biodegradable polymer-assisted dispersion step, this study delivers the first unified gypsum scale remediation strategy that achieves substantial cost savings, offering a promising chelant-free alternative for sustainable industrial flow assurance.

Introduction

Inorganic scale deposition, characterized by the precipitation of sparingly soluble mineral salts, constitutes a formidable obstacle to efficient operation in countless industrial processes. , From heat exchange surfaces in power plants to production pipelines in the oil and gas industry, scale buildup severely impairs thermal efficiency, reduces volumetric flow rates, increases frictional pressure drops, and can lead to catastrophic equipment failure. , The economic repercussions are staggering, encompassing not only direct remediation costs (e.g., chemical treatments, mechanical cleaning, equipment replacement) but also substantial indirect costs from energy inefficiencies and lost production. Among the most prevalent and challenging scale species is calcium sulfate (CaSO4), which can manifest in various polymorphic forms, notably gypsum (CaSO4·2H2O), bassanite (CaSO4·1/2H2O), and anhydrite (CaSO4). − Understanding the intricate mechanisms governing their formation, transformation, and dissolution is paramount for developing effective and sustainable scale management strategies.

The traditional industrial approach to scale removal has heavily relied on chelating agents. , Ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) and its salts are the most widely used for this purpose. EDTA salts function by sequestering Ca2+ ions, facilitating the crystal’s dissolution. , However, the widespread use of EDTA presents severe environmental and economic challenges. Its remarkable chemical stability, which contributes to its efficacy, also ensures its persistence in the environment. , Studies have shown that over 90% of EDTA escapes conventional wastewater treatment processes, leading to its accumulation in aquatic ecosystems. This persistence is concerning because EDTA is known to mobilize heavy metals from sediments, exacerbating their bioavailability and toxicity to microbial and aquatic life. Na4-EDTA represents a substantial fraction of the global EDTA market, which is estimated at approximately 80,000 metric tons per year, with market studies identifying detergents, water treatment, and industrial descaling as key application areas that collectively account for the majority of EDTA consumption. , This substantial volume amplifies its cumulative ecological footprint, demanding urgent re-evaluation and the development of environmentally superior alternatives.

The magnitude of the scale fouling challenge is evident across multiple sectors. In the oil and gas industry, for instance, scale fouling can block wellbores, pipelines, and heat exchangers, drastically impeding hydrocarbon flow, and processing. The widely adopted K2CO3/K4-EDTA protocol, incurs staggering reagent costs: approximately 40,000 USD per well treatment in the Niger Delta and up to 420,000 USD for an Ecuador surface-line cleaning (SI Table S1). Beyond reagent expenses, the energy penalties associated with scale deposition are immense; a mere 0.91 mm deposit on boiler and exchanger surfaces can add ≈370 kWh of energy expense, translating to over 150,000 USD per annum in operational energy costs (SI Table S2). These combined environmental liabilities and economic burdens underscore the critical need to replace EDTA and its salts with cheaper and greener alternatives.

This work addresses two persistent issues plaguing conventional chelant-based descaling: undesirable pseudoscale formation during dissolution and the adverse downstream fate of EDTA in wastewater. In response to evolving regulatory pressure on aminopolycarboxylate chelants such as EDTA, the integration of green chemistry metrics (e.g., E-factor, atom economy) and life-cycle assessment approaches is increasingly important for industrial adoption. Herein, we introduce a novel, chelant-free descaling formulation based on potassium carbonate (K2CO3) combined with carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC). CMC is a biodegradable polymer with low aquatic toxicity (Figure ) and has been widely employed as a dispersant for suspended solid particles in liquid systems. In this work, its incorporation improves suspension stability and mitigates pseudoscale redeposition during K2CO3-driven dissolution. Our approach achieves quantitative gypsum removal under ambient conditions, reducing total reagent consumption by approximately 20% and lowering operational chemical costs by up to 75% compared to the conventional K2CO3/K4-EDTA benchmark. The performance of the formulation was assessed through detailed kinetic and polymorph-selectivity studies, enabling a mechanistic understanding of the dissolution process. These results support the potential of the proposed system as a more environmentally favorable alternative to conventional chelant-based descaling strategies.

Materials and Methods

This section outlines the materials, meticulous experimental procedures, and advanced analytical and spectroscopic techniques employed to achieve the objectives of this research.

Materials

The primary scale material investigated in this study comprised cuboid-like gypsum crystals, specifically cleaved into precise dimensions (9–10 cm length, 1 cm width, and 1 cm height). All chemical reagents used in this study were of high purity and sourced from reputable suppliers: anhydrous potassium carbonate (99%), sodium carbonate (99%), sodium bicarbonate (99.7%), and sodium salt of carboxymethyl cellulose (99.5%) were acquired from Fisher Scientific. Sodium hydroxide (98%), potassium hydroxide (85%), and potassium bicarbonate (99.5%) were purchased from Sigma. Deionized (DI) water was used for all solution preparations.

Experimental Methods

The experimental procedures were designed to systematically investigate the calcium sulfate conversion process under various conditions, enabling detailed kinetic and mechanistic analysis. All experiments described in Sections –2.2.5 were conducted in triplicate unless otherwise stated. The reported values represent the arithmetic mean of three independent measurements (x̅=1n∑i=1nxi,n=3) , and the standard deviation was calculated as s=1n−1∑i=1n(xi−x̅)2 .

Batch and Kinetic Experiments

For each batch experiment, 0.015 or 0.075 mol of the selected base (NaOH, Na2CO3, NaHCO3, KOH, K2CO3, or KHCO3) was gradually added to 60 mL of DI water in a 100 mL beaker. Subsequently, crushed gypsum crystals (5 or 10 g) were introduced into the freshly prepared alkaline solution. The mixture was then stirred continuously at room temperature for 24 h. Following the reaction period, the resulting solution-solid mixture was subjected to vacuum filtration to separate the solid precipitate. To ensure the complete removal of any converted products (e.g., CaCO3 or Ca­(OH)2) adhered to the gypsum, the solid precipitate was thoroughly washed with 15 wt % hydrochloric acid (HCl) and refiltered. Finally, the treated gypsum crystals were air-dried overnight, and their final mass was weighed. The mass difference between the initial gypsum and the residual solid after acid washing and drying was used to determine the converted mass. Conversion yield was calculated by comparing this gravimetrically determined converted mass to the theoretical dissolved mass based on stoichiometric base-to-gypsum molar ratios.

Crystal Dissolution Experiments

These experiments focused on observing the morphological changes induced on intact gypsum crystals. Gypsum crystals were individually immersed in 60 mL solutions containing 0.015 mol of a specific base in DI water for 0.5, 6, and 24 h. After the immersion period, the crystals were carefully removed from the solution. The surface of the crystal reacted for 24 h was cleaned with 15% HCl to expose the underlying gypsum surface and reveal the extent of conversion. The crystals were then air-dried, and prepared for subsequent high-resolution morphological imaging using SEM.

Full Conversion Experiments

To assess the maximum achievable conversion, three grams of crushed gypsum scale were added to 60 mL of DI water containing a higher base concentration (0.10 mol). The mixture was continuously stirred for 24 h at room temperature. The solid products were then separated via vacuum filtration and air-dried for detailed analysis and characterization, including polymorph identification using XRD.

Kinetic Experiments in Viscous Fluids (CMC-Assisted)

To evaluate the impact of a green polymeric additive on conversion kinetics and byproduct dispersion, these experiments introduced CMC. A 0.015 mol of K2CO3 was dissolved in 60 mL of deionized water. Subsequently, varying amounts of sodium salt of carboxymethyl cellulose were added (150 mg (0.25%), 450 mg (0.75%), 600 mg (1.00%), or 900 mg (1.50%)), and the solution was thoroughly stirred until a homogeneous, viscous liquid was obtained. Five grams of crushed gypsum scale were then added to this solution, and the mixture was stirred for 2 h. The reaction progression was meticulously monitored in real-time using an in situ Fourier Transform Infrared (FT-IR) spectrometer. After the reaction, the solids were vacuum-filtered and air-dried for further analysis and characterization.

Suspension Stability Experiments

To evaluate the suspension stability and sedimentation behavior of gypsum conversion byproducts, sedimentation experiments were conducted under conditions identical to the optimized CMC-assisted system. A fixed concentration of K2CO3 (0.25 M) was dissolved in 35 mL of deionized water, followed by the addition of sodium salt of carboxymethyl cellulose at concentrations of 0, 0.25, 0.75, 1.00, and 1.50 wt %. The solution was stirred until complete homogenization. Subsequently, 2.9 g of crushed gypsum scale was added, and the mixture was stirred continuously for 2 h at room temperature to ensure complete reaction and uniform dispersion of the conversion byproducts. At the end of the reaction period, stirring was stopped simultaneously for all samples, and the suspensions were left undisturbed under quiescent conditions. The sedimentation behavior was monitored over time using continuous video recording. Time-resolved snapshots were extracted from the recorded videos to qualitatively assess the extent of particle settling and suspension stability. For clarity, deposited solids were visually highlighted during postprocessing to distinguish sedimented phases from suspended particles. The resulting images were used to compare the sedimentation profiles of CMC-containing and CMC-free systems.

Analytical Methods

A suite of advanced characterization techniques was employed to provide comprehensive insights into the chemical composition, crystalline phases, morphology, and real-time reaction kinetics.

X-ray Diffraction (XRD)

Polymorph identification and crystallinity assessment of the starting gypsum crystals and the formed products were performed using a Shimadzu XRD-7000 X-ray diffractometer. The instrument was equipped with a cobalt X-ray tube (Co Kα radiation) and a PANalytical X’Pert Pro MRD sample holder. Diffraction data were collected over a 2θ Bragg angle range from 5 to 60°, with a step size of 0.04° and a counting time of 1° per minute. This enabled precise identification of crystalline phases like gypsum, calcite, vaterite, and portlandite.

Real-Time FT-IR Spectroscopy

In situ reaction analysis and kinetic monitoring were performed using a Mettler Toledo ReactIR 702L mid-IR spectrometer, equipped with a DST AgX fiber conduit and a chemically inert diamond probe. The probe was immersed directly into the heterogeneous reacting solutions, enabling accurate in situ tracking of transformation kinetics without disrupting sampling. Data were meticulously collected every 15 min over 2 h, spanning the comprehensive wavenumber range of 650 to 4000 cm–1. to capture all relevant spectral changes. Following rigorous baseline corrections to ensure accurate peak quantification, the resultant real-time FT-IR spectra were extracted and analyzed using the integrated iC IR software. This powerful technique provided continuous, direct spectroscopic insights into the gypsum conversion rate by precisely monitoring the characteristic evolution of the sulfate ion band at approximately 1100 cm–1, reflecting the depletion of gypsum and the concurrent formation of new phases.

Scanning Electron Microscope (SEM)

A ThermoFisher Scientific Helios 5 DualBeam workstation was used to perform high-resolution morphological imaging of the gypsum crystals before and after treatment, as well as the postconversion byproducts. In-lens (immersion secondary electrons mode) SEM images were acquired at varying electron high tension (EHT) settings (1–10 kV) and working distances (WD) (3.8 to 6.4 mm) to capture detailed surface features, identify structural changes, and visualize pseudoscale formation.

Computational Methods

Computational modeling provided a powerful complement to experimental observations, enabling the prediction of geochemical equilibrium and the simulation of mineral growth rates at a molecular level, thus offering a deeper mechanistic understanding.

Geochemical Speciation

PHREEQC (v. 3.5.0), a robust geochemical software, was employed to calculate the equilibrium distribution of chemical species in our aqueous solutions. The standard PHREEQC thermodynamic database was utilized, with specific equilibrium details for vaterite added (a key calcium carbonate polymorph). This modeling allowed us to determine the saturation indices (SI) for various calcium carbonate polymorphs (calcite, vaterite, aragonite) and other relevant mineral phases, providing a thermodynamic basis for predicting precipitation propensity. The input files for the calculations for 0.25 M of each potassium base are included in SI S4.

Results and Discussion

Our comprehensive experimental workflow commenced with the thorough characterization of the starting gypsum material, followed by a systematic screening of six different bases for their calcium sulfate conversion efficiency. We then delved into the reaction kinetics using real-time spectroscopy and concluded with an in-depth analysis of the conversion reactions, employing advanced spectroscopic and computational methods to elucidate the underlying mechanisms and polymorph selectivity.

Gypsum Crystal Characterization

The pristine gypsum crystals (CaSO4·2H2O) used in this work were meticulously prepared and crushed to ensure consistent starting material for all experiments. Their crystallinity, chemical composition, and specific polymorph were rigorously confirmed using XRD. As depicted in Figure , the characteristic diffraction peaks unequivocally identify the material as gypsum, confirming its high purity and crystalline nature. Furthermore, the well-structured cuboid morphology of the as-received gypsum crystals was comprehensively validated using SEM. High-magnification SEM micrographs (Figure ) revealed a highly ordered surface, albeit with the presence of minor, shallow cracks and scattered small crystalline debris. These minor imperfections were primarily attributed to the mechanical cleaving process during sample preparation, but they did not compromise the overall integrity or representativeness of the gypsum crystals for our conversion studies.

Conversion Efficiency

To effectively induce the conversion of calcium sulfate (gypsum) and facilitate its removal, a series of six distinct sodium- and potassium-based bases were systematically evaluated: sodium hydroxide (NaOH), sodium carbonate (Na2CO3), sodium bicarbonate (NaHCO3), potassium hydroxide (KOH), potassium carbonate (K2CO3), and potassium bicarbonate (KHCO3). These bases exhibited considerable yet variable conversion performances. In a typical experimental setup, each hydroxide and carbonate base was mixed with crushed gypsum crystals in deionized water at a fixed molar amount of base. The quantities were not selected based on the stoichiometric ratios of the dissolution reactions, but rather applied uniformly across all trials to enable comparative assessment of reactivity. The underlying stoichiometric reactions are elaborated in eqs and , representing the conversion of gypsum to calcium hydroxide or calcium carbonate, respectively. 5 CaSO4(s)+2MOH→Ca(OH)2(s)+M2SO4 6 CaSO4(s)+M2CO3→CaCO3(s)+M2SO4 Where M represents a monovalent cation (Na+ or K+). Given the inherent variations in the stoichiometric ratios employed for different base types, it was crucial to establish a standardized and equitable evaluation criterion. We adopted the reaction conversion yield, as defined in eq , which considers the experimental dissolution mass and the theoretical dissolved mass. While the conversion yield provides a precise chemical assessment of reaction effectiveness, it is worth noting that the scale removal percentage (i.e., the mass ratio of dissolved calcium sulfate to its initial mass) is more commonly employed in industrial settings due to its practical convenience. 7 conversionyield(%)=(experimentaldissolvedmass)(theoreticaldissolvedmass)

These results primarily underscore the significant role of the anionic part of the base (i.e., OH–, HCO3 –, or CO3 2–), with the most superior performances consistently attained by carbonates, followed by hydroxides, and then bicarbonates. Conversion Kinetics

A highly effective method for evaluating the performance of gypsum converting agents is to probe the kinetics of the conversion reactions. For this, we leveraged real-time FT-IR spectroscopy, conducting several in situ measurements over a 2 h period. This allowed us to precisely monitor the base-induced changes in the reacting gypsum crystals by specifically tracking the evolution of the characteristic sulfate ion band (∼1100 cm–1) in absorbance (ξ) mode during the concurrent conversion reaction. During the real-time FT-IR experiments, an initial sharp increase in the 1100 cm–1 peak was observed within the first 15 min, signifying a rapid initial reaction phase, which was followed by a slower, steady increase. Concurrently, the formation of a pseudoprecipitate of calcium carbonate, CaCO3 or calcium hydroxide, Ca­(OH)2, depending on the specific base used, was visually confirmed, serving as another strong indication of the successful conversion reaction.

Focusing on the best-performing base, (K2CO3), the band of the sulfate ion almost plateaued after approximately 70 min, indicating the fastest conversion reaction among all tested bases. In contrast, when using Na2CO3, the second-best performing base, the plateau was reached after approximately 105 min, representing a significant 50% delay in reaction completion compared to K2CO3. It is noteworthy that the first 15 min of the reaction proved decisive in differentiating the performance of the experimented bases, with only the carbonate-based reactions achieving near-complete conversion within the 2 h monitoring period. Conversely, the hydroxide and bicarbonate bases exhibited significantly slower reaction kinetics and ultimately dissolved less gypsum overall within the time frame. The carbonate bases also induced a remarkably different surface structure on the gypsum crystals after removing the pseudoscale with 15% hydrochloric acid (HCl), as illustrated in Figure . This observation further corroborates their distinct and superior conversion mechanisms.

Morphological Analysis

A scanning electron microscope (SEM) was employed to analyze the morphological transformations to gain deeper insights into the physical changes induced on the gypsum crystals during conversion. We primarily focused on potassium-based converters (K2CO3, KHCO3, or KOH) due to their consistently superior performance over their sodium counterparts.

The SEM image of the gypsum crystal treated with K2CO3 (Figure ) reveals a remarkably uniform surface characterized by two distinct regions: (1) cavities and (2) an irregular outer surface. The regions exhibiting well-defined cavities presented a noticeably smoother crystalline surface compared to the areas with irregular surfaces. These clean, smooth cavity surfaces are attributed to areas initially covered by the accumulated pseudoscale (primarily vaterite CaCO3) that was subsequently and completely removed by HCl wash. The irregular surface, on the other hand, likely corresponds to poorly converted region.

In stark contrast, crystals treated with bicarbonates (KHCO3) appeared to have undergone several phases. Initially, the gypsum surface underwent etching by bicarbonate ions, leading to the formation of spherical pseudoscale depositspresumed; presumed to be vaterite based on their morphology. − Then, the spherical crystals seem to begin flatten and form a sheet-like, larger crystals. This feature was apparent in the crystal collected after 6 h of exposure to potassium bicarbonate. After 24 h and acid wash, the crystal surface showed uneven and rough texture indicating a less selective conversion reaction. Similarly, the crystal treated with KOH exhibited a nonuniform texture after washing with HCl (Figure ). However, the portlandite crystal growth was apparent in the crystals collected after 30 min, and 6 h. Nonetheless, these results suggest that the pseudoscales formed on gypsum when treated with bicarbonate and hydroxide bases possess distinct characteristics compared to those formed with carbonate bases. Furthermore, a complete removal of the paste-like pseudoscale from the gypsum crystal reacted with KOH using hydrochloric acid proved challenging, as visible in the SEM images, implying a more adherent or resistant byproduct formation.

To further assess the removability of the conversion products formed during base treatment, energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) was performed on samples after washing with 15% HCl (Figure S3). The K2CO3-treated surfaces showed no detectable carbon signal, indicating effective dissolution and removal of carbonate conversion products and exposure of the underlying CaSO4 surface. In contrast, KHCO3-treated samples retained a measurable carbon signal (∼4 atom %), suggesting that portions of the carbonate-derived surface layer remained attached despite acid washing. For KOH-treated samples, EDS could not directly distinguish Ca­(OH)2 from CaSO4 based solely on elemental composition; however, elemental mapping revealed localized sulfur-deficient regions enriched in calcium and oxygen. Considering the KOH-induced conversion pathway, such sulfur-deficient Ca–O-rich domains are consistent with the presence of hydroxide-derived conversion products remaining after acid treatment. This morphological evidence strongly supports the kinetic and conversion yield data, highlighting the superior efficacy of carbonate bases in facilitating robust and complete gypsum conversion.

Polymorph Characterization

Crystalline materials often exhibit the ability to exist in multiple crystalline forms with distinct lattice, morphology, and physical properties. Each crystal is referred to as polymorph. Just as CaSO4 can exist as gypsum, bassanite, and anhydrite, calcium carbonate (CaCO3) is known to exist in three common polymorphs: calcite, aragonite, and vaterite as well. Understanding which polymorph is formed during the conversion process is crucial for developing an efficient scale management strategy, as different polymorphs possess varying solubilities and morphologies.

We employed XRD to identify the specific polymorphs of CaCO3 and Ca­(OH)2 formed as byproducts of the conversion reactions. The XRD patterns for the carbonate and bicarbonate bases, presented in Figure , unequivocally demonstrate the successful formation of calcite and vaterite, two distinct polymorphs of CaCO3. Aragonite, under our experimental conditions (room temperature and atmospheric pressure), was not formed. In contrast, the reaction with hydroxide bases yielded only one polymorph: portlandite (Ca­(OH)2), shown as the blue pattern in Figure . Several bases demonstrated the remarkable capability to form a single polymorph by reacting with gypsum at room temperature. Notably, carbonate bases (e.g., K2CO3 and Na2CO3) selectively yielded a single calcium carbonate polymorph, specifically vaterite (indicated by yellow spectra in Figure ). On the other hand, potassium bicarbonate (KHCO3) selectively formed calcite (gray pattern), whereas its sodium analogue, sodium bicarbonate (NaHCO3), resulted in a mixture of vaterite and calcite (green pattern).

Our findings resonate with existing literature; for instance, high-purity synthesis of the vaterite polymorph using ammonium carbonate. This convergence of results, combined with our own observations, leads to a compelling hypothesis that vaterite can be predominantly produced by several carbonate bases (M2CO3, where M is a monovalent cation) under ambient conditions. Furthermore, other scientific studies have demonstrated the synthesis and conversion of calcite from gypsum using sodium carbonate, a high temperature (80 °C) for 14 days. On the contrary, our experiments were conducted for a mere 1 day at room temperature. This strongly suggests that the vaterite polymorph formed under our conditions is most likely an intermediate for calcite, given that calcite is the most thermodynamically stable CaCO3 polymorph under ambient conditions.

The observation that bicarbonate bases form calcite either wholly or to some extent further supports the idea that vaterite is initially formed for all carbonate and bicarbonate bases, but the subsequent transformation to calcite is significantly slower for the carbonate bases. This difference in transformation rate is likely a crucial factor in the observed surface morphologies after pseudoscale removal (Figure ), as well as the higher overall conversion performance and dissolution rates seen with carbonate bases (Figure ). In Figure , after 6 h, the carbonate base formed pseudoscale looking like spherical vaterite, confirmed with XRD in Figure . As the vaterite pseudoscale grows, the spherical shape allows dissolution and reprecipitation between spheres, which can account for the pronounced cavities observed after the acid washing. For bicarbonate bases, the initial pseudoscale formed in Figure after 30 min, looks similar to the carbonate pseudoscale at the same short time. In contrast, morphological changes are observed after 6 h, where another pseudoscale has formed on the surface, with higher surface coverage, confirmed via XRD in Figure to be calcite. This comparison supports a faster vaterite to calcite transformation for bicarbonate bases. The formed calcite covers the gypsum more efficiently, leading to slower dissolution and helps explain why no cavities are observed for bicarbonate addition.

The polymorph selectivity not only offers a deeper mechanistic understanding but also opens avenues for controlling the morphology and properties of the precipitated byproducts, which is critical for effective dispersion and removal.

Polymer-Assisted Dispersion (CMC)

Thus, far, our investigation has conclusively established the pivotal role of K2CO3 as the most effective base for converting gypsum crystals, achieving impressive conversion yields. However, a significant practical challenge remains: the formation of postconversion pseudoscaling byproducts, specifically the unsuspended vaterite powder or sediment. If left uncontrolled, these fine precipitates can lead to severe secondary pipeline blockages, negating the benefits of primary scale conversion. To mitigate this critical drawback, we aimed to enhance the dispersion and suspension quality of these postconversion byproducts by incorporating a green, polymeric additive: sodium salt of CMC, renowned for its efficient dispersant properties. Initially, we explored a relatively high CMC concentration of 1.5% in the presence of 0.25 M K2CO3 (Figure a), our identified optimum scale dissolver. The objective was to test its ability to improve the suspension quality of the pseudoscale byproducts and limit their undesired sedimentation. Interestingly, unlike the blank (CMC-free) sample, no postconversion precipitation was observed in the CMC-containing sample after 2 h of continuous reaction. This remarkable observation unequivocally underlines the notable role of the added CMC in significantly enhancing the dispersion and suspension quality of the gypsum conversion reaction byproducts. Nonetheless, a crucial trade-off was identified when assessing the kinetics of the CMC-containing reaction using real-time FT-IR, as shown in Figure a. The improved suspension quality came at the expense of the reaction conversion yield, which dropped significantly from 96% for the blank sample to 81% (Figure a). This noticeable 15% drop was attributed to an excess amount of CMC, where the free CMC acted as a thickening agent, inadvertently causing an unsought reduction in the conversion kinetics by increasing solution viscosity and potentially hindering mass transfer to the gypsum surface.

To optimize this balance, we systematically and gradually reduced the concentration of CMC from 1.5 to 1, 0.75, and 0.25%. All these experiments were performed in the presence of 0.25 M K2CO3. Remarkably, all CMC-containing samples, across the tested concentration range, expressed excellent dispersion stability with no observable pseudoscaling sedimentation. This observation is further supported by direct visual evidence of the suspension behavior over time (Figure S5, SI), where the CMC-containing samples across the investigated concentration range exhibit stable, homogeneous suspensions with negligible sedimentation. In contrast, the CMC-free system shows rapid settling and formation of a dense sediment layer. These results provide clear confirmation of the role of CMC in maintaining dispersion stability and preventing secondary particle accumulation. Crucially, the achieved conversion yield was higher than that obtained with the 1.5% CMC sample, demonstrating a clear inverse relationship between reaction kinetics/conversion yield and the amount of CMC used (Figure a). The 0.25% CMC sample showed the highest conversion yield (92%) among all CMC-containing samples, representing only a 4% reduction from the CMC-free samples. This limited drop in conversion efficiency is deemed an acceptable trade-off, given the substantial benefit of enhanced dispersion quality and the prevention of secondary blockages. Further reduction of CMC concentration (below 0.25%) unfortunately resulted in poor suspension quality, indicating this as an optimal concentration.

Furthermore, the slight degradation (4%, from 96% to 92%) in the conversion yield for the 0.25% CMC sample was microscopically investigated using SEM, comparing it to blank (CMC-free with 0.25 M K2CO3) samples. The corresponding SEM micrographs in Figure c provide clear evidence of the conversion reaction, as indicated by the formation of characteristic microcavities (>150 μm in width with variable depths) caused by the reaction of K2CO3, which is known to weaken the crystal structure and facilitate its mechanical removal. However, as shown in Figure c, the SEM images of the CMC-containing samples distinctly show the presence of adsorbed excess amounts of CMC on the gypsum surface. This adsorbed CMC layer, acting as a thickening agent and potentially a physical barrier, is likely responsible for hindering the conversion kinetics, as previously hypothesized. Nevertheless, the carefully optimized, reduced amount of CMC (0.25%) strikes an excellent balance, offering robust pseudoscale dispersion with minimal compromise to the overall gypsum conversion kinetics. This polymer-assisted dispersion strategy represents a crucial step toward a truly integrated and sustainable scale management solution.

Recommendation

For industrial deployment, the K2CO3/CMC chelant-free formulation should be prepared at concentrations optimized for the severity of scaling, ensuring complete dissolution of K2CO3 prior to injection. The CMC dosage should be maintained at approximately 0.25 wt % to achieve a balance between dispersion stability and reaction kinetics, as higher concentrations may reduce conversion efficiency due to increased viscosity. The treatment can be applied through continuous injection or staged batch dosing, allowing sufficient contact time for gypsum conversion under ambient or near-ambient conditions. Downstream byproduct management is recommended through filtration or settling systems to capture suspended vaterite for potential reuse as a nonhazardous material. Real-time monitoring, such as inline turbidity or ion-specific probes, should be employed to track conversion progress and adjust treatment parameters accordingly. The formulation is compatible with most industrial water handling systems and can be integrated into existing scale control or remediation programs without significant infrastructure modifications.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates a paradigm shift in calcium sulfate scale remediation, moving away from environmentally detrimental EDTA-based approaches toward green and sustainable chemical solutions. We show that common, readily available, hydroxide, bicarbonate, and particularly carbonate bases effectively convert gypsum (CaSO4·2H2O) into more manageable scale types. Our comparative analysis revealed that carbonate bases, especially K2CO3, exhibit unparalleled performance, achieving an impressive 96% gypsum conversion rate and significantly outperforming their hydroxide and bicarbonate counterparts. XRD and SEM characterization unveiled critical insights into pseudoscale polymorphism and distinct morphological signatures. Carbonate reactions predominantly yielded vaterite, a metastable calcium carbonate polymorph, that notably resisted transformation to thermodynamically favored calcite within our experimental time frame. In contrast, bicarbonates produced calcite or mixed vaterite/calcite pseudoscales, while hydroxides formed portlandite. These polymorph-specific formations directly influenced gypsum surface structures: carbonates induced pronounced, deep cavities, while hydroxides and bicarbonates resulted in more irregular, shallower textures. We attribute these stark morphological differences to the inherent spherical growth habit of vaterite compared to the typically more planar growth of calcite and portlandite. Our integrated geochemical speciation and microkinetic modeling provided compelling mechanistic explanations for observed polymorph transformations, precisely elucidating why the vaterite-to-calcite conversion is significantly slower in carbonate systems than bicarbonate systems, a crucial finding for tailoring future green inhibitors.

Importantly, this work also addresses a persistent Achilles’ heel of descaling: secondary blockages from insoluble pseudoscale byproducts. By strategically incorporating CMC, a biodegradable dispersant, we demonstrate a highly effective method to prevent the problematic accumulation and sedimentation of converted solids like vaterite. This innovative “polymer-assisted dispersion” strategy ensures not only efficient gypsum conversion but also robust flow assurance by maintaining the converted particles in suspension, thus minimizing the risk of downstream pipeline blockages. The profound understanding developed here, from fundamental conversion mechanisms and polymorphism to practical byproduct management, directly unlocks new avenues for more targeted, efficient, and sustainable scale removal strategies. The vast implications extend across diverse industrial settings, including oil and gas, power generation, and water treatment, where calcium sulfate scale remains a persistent and costly challenge.

By combining a chelant-free K2CO3 conversion process with mechanistic control over CaCO3 polymorphism and a biodegradable polymer-assisted dispersion step, this work delivers the first integrated gypsum scale remediation strategy that eliminates persistent pollutants, prevents secondary blockages, and delivers substantial cost savings, offering a promising chelant-free strategy for effective gypsum scale remediation while delivering substantial cost savings.
