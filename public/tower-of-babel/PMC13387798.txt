Respiratory syncytial virus infection in adults: Clinical manifestations, outcomes, and vaccines
Authors: Ching-Hao Hsu, Yi-Tien Hsuan, Yu-Jiun Chan, Hsin-Pai Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRespiratory syncytial virus (RSV) is an RNA virus transmitted via droplets and affects individuals across all age groups. Although RSV is increasingly being recognized as a key pathogen in adults, differentiating RSV infection from other acute viral infections solely on the basis of clinical features is challenging. Polymerase chain reaction is the gold standard for RSV detection. Notably, RSV accounts for 2% to 10% of all hospitalizations due to respiratory tract infections in adults. Among hospitalized adults with RSV infection, 18.9% require intensive care, experiencing a disease burden similar to that of influenza. Severe RSV infection disproportionately affects older adults with multiple comorbidities, such as chronic heart failure and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. This review focused on the manifestations and outcomes of RSV infection and on advances in the development of RSV vaccines. We comprehensively searched PubMed for relevant studies published between 1991 and 2024. Evidence specific to RSV treatments remains limited, with supportive care being the mainstay of management. In 2023, two protein subunit vaccines were approved for individuals aged ≥60 years; in addition, an mRNA-based vaccine that exerts substantial protective effects against severe RSV disease received approval in the United States. The development of novel antiviral therapies and vaccines holds promise for mitigating the burden of RSV infection. However, further research is required to evaluate the real-world effectiveness of vaccines, particularly in high-risk hospitalized patients requiring intensive care or mechanical ventilation, to optimize implementation strategies.Keywords: Disease burden, Hospitalization, Intensive care, Respiratory syncytial virus, Vaccination

1. INTRODUCTION

1.1. Background and clinical significance of RSV infection in adults

Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) was first identified in 1956 and belongs to the species Orthopneumovirus hominis in the family Pneumoviridae. RSV is an enveloped RNA virus that causes respiratory infections across all age groups and primarily infects the respiratory epithelium. The incidence of RSV infection is positively correlated with low temperatures and high relative humidity in tropical regions, with peak outbreaks occurring in winter in most countries worldwide.1–3 Traditionally considered a disease that primarily affects young children, RSV has recently been recognized as a key cause of morbidity and mortality in older adults, a shift driven by increased diagnostic testing and epidemiological research.4–6 Although RSV infection is generally mild and self-limiting, the virus can cause severe disease, particularly in individuals of advanced age and in those with comorbidities such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and chronic heart failure (CHF), leading to hospitalization and mortality.7–9

Strict public health measures imposed during the early phase of the COVID-19 pandemic markedly reduced the load of respiratory pathogens, including RSV, worldwide.10–12 However, after the relaxation of these measures, a rapid resurgence of RSV and shifts in its seasonality have been observed globally,13–19 necessitating efficient prevention and management strategies. Recent approvals of RSV vaccines for older adults and immunocompromised individuals represent a major step toward reducing disease severity. Novel therapeutic and diagnostic approaches for RSV are currently being researched.

1.2. Search strategy and selection criteria

This review was conducted to synthesize current evidence on the epidemiology of, clinical manifestations of, and available vaccines for RSV infection. A comprehensive literature search was conducted on PubMed to identify relevant studies published between January 1991 and December 2024. Search terms included combinations of “respiratory syncytial virus,” “RSV,” “adult,” “elderly,” “hospitalization,” “intensive care,” “clinical outcomes,” “antiviral therapy,” and “vaccine.” Only articles published in English were included. Original research articles, randomized controlled trials, observational studies, systematic reviews, and major clinical guidelines were reviewed. Studies focusing exclusively on pediatric populations without data on adults were excluded.

2. INCIDENCE OF RSV INFECTION AND RATE OF ASSOCIATED HOSPITALIZATIONS IN ADULTS

2.1. General population and older individuals

The global burden of RSV in adults, particularly in older adults and immunocompromised individuals, is substantial, although it is often underestimated because of diagnostic limitations.28 Every year, RSV causes approximately 267 hospitalizations, 200 emergency department admissions, and 2278 outpatient visits per 100 000 older adults (age ≥65 years) in the United States.20 A cross-sectional study revealed that the annual incidence of RSV-related hospitalization in US adults increased from 8.8 per 100 000 adults in 2016-2017 to 21.2 per 100 000 adults in 2022-2023, with 19.1% of hospitalized patients requiring intensive care unit (ICU) admission; the disease burden was highest among individuals aged ≥75 years.21 A global meta-analysis similarly reported that the rate of RSV-related hospitalizations was higher among individuals aged ≥65 years than among those aged 50 to 64 years (0.3-1.0 and 0.2-0.3 cases per 1000 individuals per year, respectively), with the burden being generally higher in developing countries than in developed countries. The rate of in-hospital mortality was markedly higher among individuals aged ≥65 years than among those aged 50 to 64 years (1.6%-9.1% and 1.4%-2.5%, respectively); this rate was generally higher in developing countries than in developed countries.22

In Taiwan, the incidence of RSV infection in hospitalized adults is highest between September and November, during the transition from warm to cooler weather. A study indicated that the overall incidence of RSV in adults with acute respiratory infection was approximately 1% to 2%; notably, the incidence was relatively low during the early phase of the COVID-19 pandemic, but this was followed by a surge during the autumn and winter of 2022.23–25 Approximately 18.9% of all hospitalized patients with RSV infection required ICU admission; the virus accounted for nearly 5% of all cases of non-COVID lower respiratory tract infections necessitating ICU admission, imposing a disease burden similar to that of influenza.3,26

2.2. Adults with comorbidities

RSV infection imposes a considerable disease burden on patients with various comorbidities, such as COPD, ischemic heart disease, CHF, stroke, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, obesity, and immunosuppression.29 Two major studies have evaluated the incidence of RSV in adults with comorbidities.27,28 A UK study (1995-2009) revealed that RSV-related hospitalizations and outpatient visits vary considerably by age and comorbidities.27 Among individuals without comorbidities, the average seasonal incidence of RSV-related hospitalizations ranged from 3 per 100 000 individuals in patients aged 18 to 49 years to 116 per 100 000 individuals in those aged ≥75 years. By contrast, among individuals with comorbidities, the incidence of RSV-related hospitalizations ranged from 10 per 100 000 individuals in patients aged 18 to 49 years to 338 per 100 000 individuals in those aged ≥75 years.27 In addition, a US surveillance study involving high-risk outpatients aged 18 to 59 years reported that the annual incidence of medically attended RSV cases ranged from 41.3 to 135.9 per 100 000 individuals.28

2.3. Adults with hematological disease and those undergoing solid organ transplantation

Immunodeficiency, particularly in patients undergoing hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) or solid organ transplantation, is a major risk factor for severe RSV infection and mortality30–33. Common outcomes of RSV infection in this patient population include progression to lower respiratory tract infection (40%), bacterial coinfection, and mortality (8%).31 Notably, RSV infection is associated with increased risks of acute rejection, chronic lung allograft dysfunction, and bronchiolitis obliterans in patients undergoing lung transplantation.33 Adults with solid tumors or leukemia and those requiring chronic immunosuppression for vasculitis have substantially higher risks of RSV-related hospitalizations than do those undergoing HSCT.32

3. PATHOGENESIS, CLINICAL CHARACTERISTICS, AND SEVERITY OF RSV INFECTION

3.1. Clinical manifestations of RSV infection in adults

In adults, RSV infection presents with a broad clinical spectrum ranging from mild upper respiratory tract disease to severe lower respiratory tract disease. Common symptoms include nasal congestion, cough, wheezing, dyspnea, sputum production, and fever.23,25

The clinical presentation of RSV infection in adults often overlaps with that of other respiratory viral infections, particularly influenza and COVID-19. Systemic symptoms such as myalgia and high-grade fever are less common than they are in influenza, whereas wheezing is more common.16 Because of this nonspecific presentation, clinical differentiation based solely on symptoms is unreliable, reinforcing the importance of laboratory confirmation, especially in hospitalized patients. Severe RSV infection in adults is associated with substantial morbidity, including respiratory failure requiring mechanical ventilation, prolonged hospitalization, and increased mortality, particularly among older adults with multiple comorbidities.

3.2. Targeting the respiratory epithelium

RSV primarily infects respiratory epithelial cells, causing localized infection with minimal systemic involvement.34 After the first infection during childhood, reinfections can occur throughout life; this is attributable to RSV’s ability to induce selective immunological amnesia despite minimal antigenic variation. Furthermore, dysregulated immune responses markedly contribute to acute disease and long-term respiratory complications.34–36

3.3. Diagnostic testing for RSV

Accurate diagnosis of RSV infection in adults relies on laboratory testing because clinical features alone are insufficient to distinguish RSV from other respiratory viruses. Reverse-transcription polymerase chain reaction (PCR) is considered the gold standard diagnostic modality owing to its high sensitivity and specificity. PCR assays can be performed on upper respiratory tract specimens, such as nasopharyngeal swabs, as well as lower respiratory tract samples, including sputum, endotracheal aspirates, or bronchoalveolar lavage fluid in critically ill patients.25

3.4. Age-related susceptibility to severe disease

Older adults are highly susceptible to severe RSV infection; this is attributable to age-related structural changes in the lungs, impairment of barrier integrity, and alterations in the respiratory microbiome. In this age group, the innate immune response is characterized by diminished pathogen recognition, such as phagocytosis or pattern-recognition signaling, chronic low-grade inflammation, and defective interferon-mediated responses.37,38 Moreover, adaptive immunity declines with age, as evidenced by reductions in the proportions of RSV-specific CD8+ T cells and peripheral interferon-γ-producing T cells.39 Concurrently, increased interleukin-10 and interleukin-13 production and weakened cytotoxic lymphocyte activity also predispose older adults to severe RSV disease.40

3.5. Prolonged infection and viral evolution

RSV infection and viral shedding can be substantially longer in immunocompromised individuals than in the general population. Prolonged infection poses considerable clinical challenges, particularly for patients with hematological disorders; in these patients, PCR for RSV typically yields positive results for ≥80 days.41 Chronic RSV infection facilitates intrahost viral evolution, a phenomenon observed in other RNA viruses, including severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2). This may lead to the induction of antiviral resistance and the emergence of immune-evasive variants.42,43 Ineffective viral clearance in immunocompromised patients underscores the importance of antibody function and the vulnerability of this population to prolonged and evolving infections.

Despite lifelong exposure to RSV, older adults, particularly those with comorbidities such as CHF and COPD, often exhibit low levels of neutralizing antibodies, which substantially increases the risks of RSV infection and severe disease.8 Impaired systemic or mucosal immunity may contribute substantially to chronic infection, highlighting the importance of robust antibody responses for effective viral clearance.

4. TREATMENT OPTIONS FOR RSV INFECTION

RSV contains three transmembrane surface glycoproteins: the attachment glycoprotein (G), fusion (F) protein, and small hydrophobic protein. The viral life cycle begins with attachment of RSV to the apical surface of polarized airway epithelial cells. The G protein facilitates initial attachment through interactions with host cell glycosaminoglycans. The F protein, which is initially folded into a metastable prefusion (preF) conformation and subsequently undergoes a rearrangement that results in a stable postfusion (postF) conformation, plays a central role in viral entry and membrane fusion.44

Although a definitive cellular receptor for RSV has not been identified, several candidate receptors and attachment factors have been proposed, including toll-like receptor 4, heparan sulfate proteoglycans, intercellular adhesion molecule-1, nucleolin, the epidermal growth factor receptor, and the insulin-like growth factor 1 receptor.45

RSV is classified into two major antigenic subgroups, namely RSV-A and RSV-B, which are primarily defined by sequence variability within the G protein.44 By contrast, the F protein is highly conserved between the two subgroups, providing a strong biological rationale for F-protein–based vaccines and antiviral agents with cross-protection against both RSV-A and RSV-B lineages.

Currently, treatment options for RSV infection are primarily limited to symptomatic and supportive care. Although adjunctive corticosteroid therapy has been used for SARS-CoV-2 and other respiratory viral infections, it has not demonstrated clinical benefits in RSV infection.46,47 RSV replication peaks approximately 5 to 6 days after infection, with symptom severity peaking around day seven and viral clearance typically occurring by day eight. This narrow therapeutic window presents a major challenge for effective antiviral therapy.47 Nebulized ribavirin, a guanosine nucleoside analog that inhibits viral polymerase, remains the only approved antiviral agent for RSV. It has demonstrated efficacy in patients undergoing HSCT and in those with hematologic malignancies.48,49 Palivizumab, a human monoclonal antibody targeting RSV, has been approved for preventing severe RSV infection in high-risk children but not in adults.50 Numerous anti-RSV agents are currently in preclinical or clinical development, including immunoglobulins and small-molecule inhibitors targeting various viral components, such as G protein, F protein, nucleocapsid protein, transcriptional regulator protein M2-1, and viral RNA–dependent RNA polymerase. These candidate drugs are designed to block viral entry into host cells or inhibit viral replication.

Because of the high conservation and limited antigenic variability of the F protein of RSV,51 most investigational antiviral agents target this protein. ALX-0171, an inhaled fusion inhibitor, exhibited acceptable safety but insufficient clinical efficacy in phase 2 trials.52 By contrast, sisunatovir (RV521), an oral small-molecule inhibitor targeting the F protein, significantly reduced RSV load and symptom severity in a phase 2a trial, with only mild (grade 1 or 2) adverse events.53 Other F-protein–targeting agents, such as AK0529 (ziresovir)54 and JNJ-53718678,55 have also exhibited strong antiviral activity against RSV. Moreover, novel antiviral agents targeting the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase of RSV, such as favipiravir, molnupiravir, remdesivir, and lumicitabine, have proven effective in preclinical and early clinical trials.56,57

Despite these therapeutic advancements, the rapid progression of RSV infection still poses a major challenge because the efficacy of antiviral treatment may be reduced by delayed treatment initiation. For example, a clinical trial evaluating the benefits of lumicitabine for hospitalized infants and neonates reported reduced treatment efficacy, which was attributable to the requirement for pretreatment PCR confirmation and the resulting delay in treatment after symptom onset.56 Because most treatment options for RSV infection are still in clinical development and will require several years before regulatory approval, effective RSV vaccines are urgently required to reduce RSV-related morbidity and mortality.

5. CHARACTERISTICS OF AND RECOMMENDATIONS FOR RSV VACCINES

PreF-specific monoclonal antibodies are markedly more potent than antibodies targeting other antigenic sites and account for most RSV-neutralizing activity after natural infection. Accordingly, they represent the principal antigenic target for neutralizing antibodies and current vaccine platforms. However, because preF is structurally unstable and readily refolds into the postF conformation, stabilization of the preF state is essential for reliable protein expression and effective subunit vaccine development.44

With recent advancements in RSV vaccine development, protein-based and mRNA-based vaccines have obtained regulatory approval as the primary platforms. These vaccines induce immune responses by targeting protein F. Currently, three RSV vaccines, namely GSK’s RSVPreF3/Arexvy, Pfizer’s RSVPreF/Abrysvo, and Moderna’s mRNA-1345/mResvia, have received approval from the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for use in adults aged ≥60 years. Arexvy consists of a preF protein subunit plus the AS01E adjuvant. It has been approved in multiple countries and regions, including the United States, the European Union, the United Kingdom, Canada, Japan, Australia, and Taiwan. The vaccine is administered as a single intramuscular dose of 0.5 mL. Before reconstitution, it must be stored at 2°C to 8°C; once reconstituted, it may be stored at 2°C to 8°C or at room temperature. Clinical trials have indicated that the efficacy of Arexvy is 82.6% against RSV-associated lower respiratory tract disease (RSV-LRTD) and 94.1% against severe RSV-LRTD during the first RSV season. This efficacy is sustained across two RSV seasons (67.2% and 78.8%, respectively), or approximately 18 months.58,61 Arexvy has also been evaluated for coadministration with influenza and herpes zoster vaccines; the results have revealed noninferior immune responses with no major safety concerns. Recent phase 3 trials have extended eligibility to include adults aged 50 to 59 years who have comorbidities associated with an increased risk of RSV.62 Consequently, the US FDA has approved Arexvy for not only adults aged ≥60 years but also those aged 50 to 59 years at increased risk of RSV-LRTD. The cost per dose of Arexvy is approximately US$294.

Abrysvo is a protein subunit vaccine containing 120 μg of preF antigen, with equal contributions from RSV-A and RSV-B (60 μg each). It is administered as a single 0.5-mL intramuscular dose. The vaccine should be stored at 2°C to 8°C before reconstitution and at 15°C to 30°C after reconstitution. In a phase 3 clinical trial, the efficacy of Abrysvo was 85.7% against RSV-LRTD inducing ≥3 symptoms and 62.1% against RSV-associated acute respiratory illness; protective efficacy was sustained for approximately 18 months.59 Abrysvo has also been evaluated for coadministration with inactivated influenza vaccines; the results have revealed noninferior immunogenicity without increased adverse effects. In contrast to Arexvy, Abrysvo does not include an adjuvant, which may influence the magnitude and duration of the immune response. In addition to approving Abrysvo for use in adults aged ≥60 years, the US FDA has approved it for the prevention of RSV-LRTD in high-risk individuals aged 18 through 59 years. Furthermore, Abrysvo has been approved for maternal immunization to confer passive immunity to infants through transplacental antibody transfer. In the MATISSE phase 3 trial, maternal immunization with Abrysvo conferred 82.4% protection against severe, medically attended RSV-LRTD in infants during the first 3 months of life, with protection declining to 70% by 6 months.63 However, concerns regarding a potential increase in the risk of preterm birth have led to differences in regulatory recommendations between the US FDA and the European Medicine Agency. The US FDA recommends vaccination between 32 and 36 weeks of gestation, whereas the European Medicines Agency recommends vaccination between 24 and 36 weeks. The cost per dose of Abrysvo is approximately US$295.

Notably, mRNA-based vaccines represent an innovative strategy for RSV immunization. These vaccines use lipid-nanoparticle-encapsulated mRNA encoding the stabilized RSV preF protein. Moderna’s mResvia (mRNA-1345) is the first mRNA-based RSV vaccine approved for use in older adults in the United States, with additional regulatory submissions pending in the European Union, Canada, Australia, and the United Kingdom. It is administered as a single 0.5-mL intramuscular dose. The vaccine should be stored at −40°C to −15°C before use. Once thawed, it must be stored at 2°C to 8°C, with prefilled syringes remaining stable for up to 30 days. Phase 2/3 clinical trials have revealed that the efficacy of mResvia is 83.7% against RSV-LRTD inducing ≥2 symptoms and 82.4% against RSV-LRTD inducing ≥3 symptoms. The vaccine’s efficacy in preventing RSV-associated acute respiratory disease was 68.4% (95% CI: 50.9-79.7) in a study with a median follow-up duration of 8.6 months.60 All available follow-up data (median duration: 18.8 months per participant; range: 0.5-24 months) indicate an efficacy of 47.4% for mResvia against RSV-LRTD with ≥2 symptoms and 48.4% against ≥3 symptoms, with the efficacy declining over time.64 Moreover, the efficacy of mResvia was higher against RSV-A than against RSV-B, indicating antigenic differences between the viral types and a relatively low prevalence of RSV-B during the study period. The US FDA approved mResvia for adults aged ≥60 years and for adults aged 18 to 59 years at increased risk of infection. The cost per dose of mResvia is approximately US$290. Ongoing studies are assessing the vaccine’s long-term protective effects, optimal revaccination intervals (1- and 2-year intervals), and potential coformulation with seasonal influenza or COVID-19 vaccines (mRNA-1045 and mRNA-1230).

The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) recommends administering RSV vaccines between late summer and early fall (August to October) to ensure immunity before the RSV season. In the United States, the Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices (ACIP) updated its recommendations in 2024, advising a single dose of any FDA-approved RSV vaccine for all adults aged ≥75 years and for adults aged 60 to 74 years who are at increased risk for severe RSV disease64; real-world data from the 2023 to 2024 RSV season have demonstrated the substantial effectiveness of adult RSV vaccines. Specifically, CDC estimates indicate that Arexvy and Abrysvo reduced RSV-associated emergency department visits and hospitalizations by approximately 70% to 80% among adults aged ≥60 years; however, real-world effectiveness data for mResvia are not yet available.65

Currently, RSV vaccination is not intended as an annual intervention; a single dose is considered sufficient, although ongoing studies are assessing the need for booster doses. RSV vaccines may be coadministered with other adult vaccines, such as influenza and COVID-19 vaccines. Preliminary data suggest that coadministration can slightly reduce antibody responses; however, the clinical relevance of this finding remains unclear.66 Healthcare providers should tailor vaccination strategies to patients’ risk profiles.

The CDC recommends a single dose of an RSV vaccine for not only individuals aged ≥75 years but also those aged 60 to 74 years with an increased risk of severe disease due to comorbidities or an immunocompromised status. Similarly, the Taiwan ACIP has issued guidelines for RSV vaccination for the following groups67: individuals aged ≥75 years; individuals aged 60 to 74 years with a high risk of severe RSV infection (eg, those with chronic pulmonary disease, cardiovascular disease, or chronic liver or kidney disease and those residing in long-term care or other institutional settings); and pregnant women between 28 and 36 weeks of gestation (to offer passive immunity to newborns during the first 6 months of life).

Currently, RSV vaccines are available in Taiwan as self-paid options. Arexvy was introduced in 2024, and Abrysvo became available in 2025. These vaccines are designed to strengthen the immune response against RSV, thereby reducing the risks of severe disease and complications.

6. CONCLUSION

RSV often causes severe respiratory illness in adults, particularly older adults and those with chronic medical conditions or immunocompromising conditions. Although RSV-specific treatments remain limited, the recent approval of protein-based and mRNA-based vaccines (Arexvy, Abrysvo, and mResvia) has advanced prevention strategies. These vaccines confer strong initial protection, but their efficacy declines over time, highlighting the need for further research into revaccination and optimal use in high-risk populations. In addition, direct head-to-head comparative studies of approved vaccines, as well as ongoing pharmacovigilance and real-world effectiveness studies, are required to more clearly define comparative performance, protection durability, and safety across diverse adult populations. Additional studies are also required to identify key risk factors for severe disease necessitating intensive care, evaluate the long-term efficacy of RSV vaccines, and facilitate the development of novel antiviral therapies. Insights from such studies may help reduce the overall burden of RSV infection.
