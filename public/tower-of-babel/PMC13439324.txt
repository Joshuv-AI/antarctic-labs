Axion electrodynamics in a topologically trivial antiferromagnet
Authors: Abhilash Mishra, Robin Karothiya, Syed Qamar Abbas Shah, Arti Kashyap, Christian Binek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Axion electrodynamics, originally proposed by Frank Wilczek in the context of high energy physics, can manifest in condensed matter systems as an isotropic linear magnetoelectric (ME) response. Yet, experimental realization of a continuous and purely monopolar ME response has remained elusive. Here, we demonstrate such a response by transforming the topologically trivial antiferromagnet chromia into an isotropic ME medium. Using low-frequency AC ME susceptometry, supported by Monte Carlo simulations, we reveal a monopolar ME response \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\alpha }_{{ij}}=\theta {\delta }_{{ij}}$$\end{document}αij=θδij resulting from suppressed quadrupolar contributions. The zero crossing of the temperature-dependent θ at 168 K provides direct evidence that the measured isotropic ME response arises from the axion contribution in chromia. These results establish a condensed-matter platform for axion electrodynamics and open pathways toward ME Hall effects, magnetophotovoltaic responses, and monopolar-based devices.

In macroscopic electrodynamics, the constitutive relations of a material can be derived from an effective Lagrangian density \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L({{\bf{E}}},{{\bf{B}}})$$\end{document}L(E,B) via \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\bf{D}}}=\frac{\partial L}{\partial {{\bf{E}}}},{{\bf{H}}}=-\frac{\partial L}{\partial {{\bf{B}}}}.$$\end{document}D=∂L∂E,H=−∂L∂B. For a constant axion field θ, the Euler–Lagrange equations reproduce the standard Maxwell equations in the bulk, while the constitutive relations include the pseudoscalar ME coupling. When θ varies in space or time, as occurs at surfaces of topological insulators, the θ-term produces the modified field equations of axion electrodynamics. In topologically trivial materials such as engineered Cr₂O₃ powder, θ is constant throughout the bulk and up to the surface, so the bulk Maxwell equations remain unchanged, and the effect of θ appears entirely through the constitutive relations. A medium whose constitutive tensor contains only the monopole component \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\alpha }_{{ij}}=\theta {\delta }_{{ij}}$$\end{document}αij=θδij was implicitly envisioned in Wilczek’s 1987 proposal3, where he speculated that “it is not beyond the realm of possibility that fields whose properties partially mimic those of axion fields can be realized in condensed-matter systems.” Our engineered material realizes the macroscopic response envisioned by Wilczek more directly than topological insulators, because it exhibits a bulk pseudoscalar ME response corresponding to a constant θ throughout the sample, without relying on surface-induced θ variations.

This debate was resolved only after measurements in Cr₂O₃ demonstrated a finite trace with the unambiguous experimental confirmation of a non-zero axion piece6,7. Here, the temperature-dependent parallel and perpendicular ME susceptibilities sum up to a finite trace of (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\rm{\alpha }}}_{{{\rm{ij}}}}$$\end{document}αij) such that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta=\frac{1}{3}\left(2{\alpha }_{\perp }+{\alpha }_{\parallel }\right)\ne 0$$\end{document}θ=132α⊥+α∥≠0. However, θ ≠ 0 does not preclude the presence of the ME quadrupole response. Hence, the ME properties of single-crystalline chromia are not fully captured by axion electrodynamics. Nevertheless, the observation of a non-zero axion component has motivated the search for ME materials with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\alpha }_{\perp }={\alpha }_{\parallel }$$\end{document}α⊥=α∥, where the quadrupolar ME response would be zero and only the pure monopolar ME response remains. Although single-crystalline chromia inevitably exhibits a quadrupolar response, it has been proposed that its ME susceptibility can be exploited to generate a magnetic field of a magnetic monopole8,9. This effect arises from the ME response to a mirror charge induced by a real point charge in proximity of the surface8. This prediction should not be conflated with the realization of a material exhibiting a purely monopolar ME response. Although related, the monopolar ME response and the monopole magnetic stray field are two distinct phenomena. We demonstrate a long theorized continuous, purely isotropic ME monopole response near room-temperature, distinct from the quantized low-temperature responses in topological materials6.

Previous efforts to realize ME isotropic response have included effective medium approaches using powder samples. Some of us recently demonstrated that ME annealing of powders derived from materials with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathop{{\mathrm{lim}}}_{T\to {T}_{N}^{-}}\frac{{\alpha }_{\parallel }(T)}{{\alpha }_{\perp }(T)} < 0$$\end{document}limT→TN−α∥(T)α⊥(T)<0, such as chromia (Néel temperature TN = 307 K), cannot produce isotropic ME response10. Intuitively, this is because ME annealing requires the application of electric and magnetic fields during cooling below TN. This protocol imposes a directional bias, thereby breaking isotropy. Decades earlier, Shtrikman and Treves noted that powders composed of diagonal ME materials with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathop{{\mathrm{lim}}}_{T\to {T}_{N}^{-}}\frac{{\alpha }_{\parallel }(T)}{{\alpha }_{\perp }(T)} > 0$$\end{document}limT→TN−α∥(T)α⊥(T)>0 could, in principle, exhibit isotropic ME behaviour in response to ME annealing11. However, no such material has been experimentally identified to date. Recently, metamaterials with broken parity and time inversion symmetry leading to axion response have been proposed but not experimentally realized as of today12.

Furthermore, a quantized isotropic ME response has been predicted for ideal topological insulator (TI) materials13. However, in realistic magnetic TI materials, this response rapidly decays due to disorder. In addition, magnetic TIs such as Cr-doped (Bi,Sb)₂Te₃ exhibit very low magnetic ordering temperatures (~15 K)14,15. This limits the practicality of TIs for realizing a monopolar ME response, particularly for applications near room temperature.

Here, we present the realization of an engineered, topologically trivial material that exhibits a continuous, pure, and robust ME monopole response up to the Néel temperature, making it promising for fundamental studies and practical applications. In contrast to the previously observed quantized, low-temperature topological ME effect16, the monopolar response reported here is non-quantized and is measured by quasi-static susceptometry up to 307 K.

ME annealing performed by cooling the powder from above to below TN in the presence of simultaneously applied E and B fields breaks this symmetry by selecting a preferred orientation, thereby enabling a net ME response but eliminating isotropy. Figure 1 illustrates this dilemma between isotropic ME null response (not shown), non-zero but anisotropic ME response (Fig. 1a), and non-zero isotropic ME response (Fig. 1b). Thermally activated Néel vector alignment follows a Boltzmann distribution. The energies entering the Boltzmann factor are determined by the product of the electric and magnetic fields projected onto and perpendicular to a grain’s c-axis. Thermal fluctuations can overcome the small energy imbalance between opposite Néel vector orientations. This increases the probability that grains, whose c-axes are tilted away from the external field direction, have their Néel vectors aligned opposite to the field. As a result, ME annealing of a powder leads to spatial variation in Néel vector orientation across the sample and thus anisotropic response. In Fig. 1a, this anisotropy manifests through an increased equatorial density of Néel vectors aligned antiparallel to the c-axis projection of the applied fields.

In ref. 10, we demonstrated how varying the E·B field strength during ME annealing of micron-sized, highly compressed polycrystalline Cr₂O₃ powder influences the ME response, particularly the temperature at which the grain distribution-averaged ME response crosses zero. In a sample powdered at T > TN, zero-crossing at T = 168 K is fundamentally inaccessible under any field-cooling protocol (see ref. 10). This limitation applies to all powders with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathop{{\mathrm{lim}}}_{T\to {T}_{N}^{-}}\frac{{\alpha }_{\parallel }(T)}{{\alpha }_{\perp }(T)} < 0$$\end{document}limT→TN−α∥(T)α⊥(T)<0, encompassing all experimentally investigated ME crystalline materials. Conversely, observing a zero-crossing at T = 168 K in a chromia-based engineered material indicates that pure isotropic ME response has been realized.

The present study identifies an unexplored pathway for engineering axion response of ME samples. The emergence of a pure ME monopole response in chromia-based samples leads to the realization of the predicted zero-crossing at T = 168 K, as confirmed by both experiment and simulation. Crucially, the origin of this monopole response can be traced to a subtle but decisive symmetry consideration. As illustrated in Fig. 1c, a π-rotation of an antiferromagnetic single-domain grain about an axis perpendicular to its threefold axis leaves the Néel vector, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\bf{L}}}={{{\bf{M}}}}_{{{\bf{1}}}}-{{{\bf{M}}}}_{{{\bf{2}}}}+{{{\bf{M}}}}_{{{\bf{3}}}}-{{{\bf{M}}}}_{{{\bf{4}}}}$$\end{document}L=M1−M2+M3−M4, invariant. During powdering, grains may undergo random C₂ rotations that alter their crystallographic orientation but preserve the sign of their ME response. As a result, the ensemble retains the single-domain polarity encoded during ME annealing, enabling a non-zero isotropic ME response. The result of powdering a ME-annealed single crystal is sketched in Fig. 1b. As illustrated, Néel vectors are oriented such that, upon applying an electric field in any radial direction, all grains aligned along the field lines contribute equally to the magnetization response. Strikingly, once the sample is heated above TN, its pure axion response is lost irreversibly, with no field cooling or known protocol capable of restoring it.

In the second simulated scenario, a single crystal is first cooled through TN under collinear electric and magnetic fields, forming a uniform single-domain state. In our simulation, this is implemented by treating the crystal as an ensemble of grains whose c-axes are perfectly aligned with the sample’s global z-axis. The single-domain state requires every grain’s Néel vector to align with the global +z direction (Fig. 3c). To understand the effect of this randomization, consider an illustrative case as shown in Fig. 1c, where a grain undergoes a full π-rotation during milling. Although its c-axis is inverted, the Néel vector locked to the domain structure formed during ME annealing remains aligned with the grain’s local c-axis and therefore continues to point in the global +z direction.

To reproduce this behavior in the simulation, the Néel-state polarity of each grain was held fixed during randomization of the grains’ c-axes orientations. All grains were forced to remain in the same antiferromagnetic state in which the z-component of every grain’s Néel vector points in the same direction (up, for example). These results quantitatively reproduce the experimental observations and demonstrate that the sequence of milling and annealing is the primary factor determining whether the ME response of Cr₂O₃ powder is anisotropic or isotropic.

The realization of a continuous isotropic ME response in a condensed-matter system establishes the platform envisioned by Wilczek for exploring axion electrodynamics. It opens the door to meaningful advances in our fundamental understanding of axion physics. Such advances may emerge, for example, from systematic studies of periodically driven monopolar MEs, where temporal modulation of the axion term exposes its dynamic character17–19. The availability of an isotropic ME response at and above room temperature also opens practical opportunities, including ME Hall phenomena, magnetophotovoltaic effects, and device concepts relying on monopolar magnetoelectricity. As noted by Khomskii20, an electric charge surrounded by an isotropic linear ME medium produces a striking magnetic texture of the magnetic monopole type. In such a scenario, the electric field of a point charge creates a radial magnetic polarization with novel dynamic consequences. Furthermore, in an isotropic ME medium with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\alpha }_{{ij}}=\theta {\delta }_{{ij}}$$\end{document}αij=θδij, one may anticipate a magnetophotovoltaic effect. Analogous to ferroelectrics, where photoexcited electrons and holes drift in opposite directions under an internal electric field, oppositely charged carriers in an isotropic ME medium would be accompanied by magnetic charges drifting in opposite directions under an applied magnetic field.

Methods

Experiment

The preparation of a sample exhibiting a ME monopole response follows this protocol. A commercially grown single crystal (Princeton Scientific Corporation) with dimensions 5 × 5 × 1 mm is covered with silver paste top and bottom electrodes and mounted in a Quantum Design MPMS-XL Superconducting Quantum Interference Device (SQUID). Electrical feedthroughs provide direct electric contacting of the sample. With the sample temperature stabilized at T = 360 K > TN = 307 K, a voltage of V = 500 V is applied, generating an electric field of E = 5 × 105 V m−1 along the crystallographic c-axis. Simultaneously, a magnetic field B = μ₀H = 7 T is applied along the same axis. The sample is cooled under these collinear fields to T = 200 K < TN preparing the crystal into an antiferromagnetic single domain state. Following this ME annealing protocol, the crystal is maintained at T ≈ 280 K (<TN) while electrodes are removed and the crystal is mechanically comminuted via ball milling into a powder with average grain size of 100 μm (see SI2). Note that the precise grain size distribution is of minor relevance in this context. Unlike ferromagnetic particles, antiferromagnetic grains exhibit no shape anisotropy and retain the single-domain state inherited from the ME-annealed single-crystal precursor. While maintained at T ≈ 280 K ( < TN), the powder was homogeneously dispersed in epoxy resin and cast into a rectangular solid of dimensions 5 × 5 × 2.5 mm. The solidified cuboid was electrically contacted and mounted in a modified Quantum Design MPMS-XL SQUID, enabling low-frequency AC measurements of all tensor elements of the static ME susceptibility, thereby confirming purely monopolar ME response, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\alpha }_{{ij}}=\theta {\delta }_{{ij}}$$\end{document}αij=θδij. The AC technique employs an external low-frequency (1 Hz) AC voltage applied across the sample which is maintained in a fixed position at the center of the SQUID gradiometer pickup coil. The sample’s ME response to the alternating electric field generates an oscillating magnetic flux, inducing an AC voltage in the SQUID pickup coils. This alternating signal is processed by a lock-in amplifier (Stanford Research SR830), with the reference provided by the AC power supply. Rather than optimizing the phase to maximize the x-channel signal, the lock-in amplifier is operated in polar mode, which renders the measurement independent of the relative phase between reference and input signals. For further experimental details, see ref. 10.

In addition, our setup allows direct measurement of the temperature dependence of the ME response and thus θ(T), revealing not only the predicted zero-crossing temperature at 168 K but also a subsequent increase in magnitude upon further cooling (see Fig. 2). To verify that the off-diagonal components of the ME tensor vanish, as required for a monopolar response, the AC electric-field excitation was applied normal to the y = 0 and x = 0 planes, while the pickup coils detected the flux change associated with the induced magnetization along the z-direction. The coordinate system is defined with the z-axis aligned along the axis of the gradiometer pickup coils, while the x- and y-axes lie perpendicular to z, forming a right-handed Cartesian frame with its origin at the center of the pickup coils. The diagonal tensor components were determined by applying the AC field normal to the z = 0 plane, with the pickup coils again measuring the z-component of the magnetization. Isotropy was assessed by physically removing the sample from the magnetometer while maintaining at T < TN, rotating it about a fourfold symmetry axis of the cuboid, re-establishing electrical contacts, and repeating measurements with the E-field applied normal to the z = 0 plane.

Theory

Monte–Carlo framework is employed to simulate the ME response of polycrystalline Cr2O3 under field-cooling protocols. The system comprised N = 20,000 non-interacting grains within a cubic simulation volume, each characterized by a crystallographic orientation (θ, ϕ) in spherical coordinates and a binary Néel vector state sp = ±1. Initial grain orientations were sampled uniformly from the solid angle, with spatial positions randomly distributed throughout the box.

Simulated annealing was performed by initializing the temperature at Tstart = 350 K (above TN) and reducing it linearly to Tend = 250 K over 1000 temperature steps. At each temperature, a Metropolis Monte–Carlo sweep was performed over all grains. For each grain, a trial Néel state flip (sp −sp) was proposed while keeping the crystallographic orientation (θ, ϕ) fixed. The associated energy change ΔF is computed from the ME free-energy expression. Flips that lower the energy are always accepted, while flips that raise the energy are accepted with probability \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${e}^{-\Delta F/{k}_{B}T}$$\end{document}e−ΔF/kBT. Below a chosen lock-in temperature slightly below TN, flip updates are frozen, and no further Monte Carlo updates were performed to mimic Néel vector freezing due to anisotropy.
