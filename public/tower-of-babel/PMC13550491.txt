Selphi, a tool for improving genotype imputation accuracy
Authors: Adriano De Marino, Abdallah Amr Mahmoud, Sandra Bohn, Jon Lerga-Jaso, Biljana Novković, Charlie Manson, Salvatore Loguercio, Andrew Terpolovsky, Mykyta Matushyn, Ali Torkamani, Puya G. Yazdi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Genotype imputation is a powerful tool for inferring missing genotype data in large-scale genetic studies. Over the last two decades, multiple imputation algorithms have been developed, steadily improving in speed and overall accuracy. However, accurate imputation of rare and infrequent variants remains a challenge, largely because existing methods rely on local haplotype matching within genomic windows and do not fully exploit the extended patterns of haplotype sharing that span entire chromosomes. Here we present Selphi, a new genotype imputation algorithm that combines the Positional Burrows-Wheeler Transform (PBWT) with a multi-stage haplotype selection heuristic operating across entire chromosomes. When compared to state-of-the-art methods Beagle 5.4, IMPUTE5, and Minimac4, Selphi showed higher accuracy on the 1000 Genomes Project and TOPMed datasets, across all super-populations and allele frequencies. Similarly, Selphi achieved higher accuracy than Beagle 5.4 on the UK Biobank dataset, which translated into improved concordance with hc-WGS GWAS summary statistics at known trait-associated loci and more accurate polygenic risk scores (PRS). Selphi outputs standard VCF files with genotype dosages (DS), haplotype-specific allele probabilities (AP1, AP2), and a per-variant dosage R-squared quality score (DR2), enabling direct integration with downstream analytical pipelines including standard post-imputation quality filtering.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-58420-2.

Introduction

Genomic medicine has grown rapidly since the completion of the Human Genome Project two decades ago, promising to deepen our knowledge of disease pathology, improve diagnostic speed and accuracy, and enable targeted disease treatment and preventive therapy1–7. However, as whole genome sequencing (WGS) is still prohibitively expensive, especially when it comes to large-scale population-wide screening, many academic and direct-to-consumer efforts rely on array-based SNP genotyping and low-coverage WGS. These approaches are cost-effective, but their utility depends critically on imputation methods used to infer un-genotyped variants. Therefore, imputation accuracy has a direct impact on all downstream applications, such as detecting associated variants in genome-wide association studies (GWAS) or calculating polygenic risk scores (PRS)8–12.

Over the last decades, multiple groups have developed imputation methods, the majority of which are based on the Li and Stephens Hidden Markov Model (HMM)13–19. These methods share a common framework: phased target haplotypes are matched against a reference panel, and a probabilistic model infers alleles at un-genotyped positions based on haplotype sharing patterns that reflect shared ancestry. To scale to large reference panels, modern tools employ the Positional Burrows-Wheeler Transform (PBWT)20 for efficient haplotype matching. However, to manage computational complexity, existing methods typically process the genome in overlapping windows or chunks: Beagle 5.4 uses sliding windows with a default size of approximately 40 cM17, IMPUTE5 processes the genome in chunks with buffer regions19, and Minimac4 similarly operates on segmented regions16.

While windowed approaches are computationally efficient, they impose a locality constraint on haplotype matching: the set of reference haplotypes considered at each position is determined only by matches within that window. This can lead to two related problems. First, short coincidental matches (identity by state, IBS) that arise by chance within a window may be indistinguishable from genuine long-range haplotype sharing (identity by descent, IBD). Second, matches near window boundaries may be truncated, potentially losing information about extended haplotype sharing that spans multiple windows, although overlapping buffer regions mitigate this to some extent.

These limitations are particularly relevant for rare variant imputation, given the medical significance of rare and low-frequency variants21–23, where the number of informative matching haplotypes is inherently small and the correct reference haplotypes must be identified with high precision. When the reference panel lacks close relatives of the target individual, common in non-European and underrepresented populations, the ability to exploit extended haplotype sharing becomes even more critical24–26. Recently, efforts to improve rare variant imputation have focused on broadening reference panels through cohort-specific panels27–29 or incorporating whole exome sequencing data30. However, improving imputation accuracy through algorithmic innovation, by better using the haplotype information already present in existing reference panels, has received less attention.

Here, we present Selphi, a genotype imputation tool that addresses this gap through a multi-stage haplotype selection strategy informed by cumulative match statistics computed over a wide genomic context. In this implementation Selphi performs PBWT matching in a single scan over the full chromosome and accumulates match statistics across that scan; the same algorithmic mechanism would operate within a sufficiently wide windowed setting. It then applies a cascade of frequency-normalized, adaptively-thresholded filters to select reference haplotypes at each marker, preferentially retaining those with longer and more consistent matches across the genomic context. This filtered subset of reference haplotypes, typically much smaller than the full panel, then enters a modified Li and Stephens HMM whose conditioning state set varies per marker.

We compare Selphi to state-of-the-art imputation methods Beagle 5.417, IMPUTE519, and Minimac416 using the 1000 Genomes Project31 and TOPMed32 datasets. We then impute UK Biobank33 genotyping data using Selphi and the next most accurate model, Beagle 5.4, to evaluate the applicability of Selphi to large-scale datasets and to assess concordance with high-coverage WGS (hc-WGS) GWAS summary statistics at known trait-associated loci, as well as PRS accuracy.

Methods

Selphi

Model overview

Selphi (v1.5.3) was developed in Python 3.11 and C, using vectorized sparse matrix operations from NumPy and SciPy for memory-efficient processing of large-scale genomic data. Performance-critical numerical routines are JIT-compiled with Numba. Selphi operates under the assumption that both reference and target genotypes are phased and non-missing.

We categorize markers as “reference markers” (genotyped in the reference panel), “target markers” (genotyped in the target samples), and “imputed markers” (present in the reference panel but un-genotyped in the target). The imputation process exploits patterns of haplotype sharing between target and reference haplotypes: at target markers where both the reference and target are genotyped, extended matching segments between target and reference haplotypes are identified. These matching segments, which are more likely to reflect shared ancestry (putative IBD) when they are long and consistent across many genomic positions, are then used to infer alleles at imputed markers.

PBWT matching

Selphi integrates a modified version of the PBWT20 implemented in C for computational efficiency. The PBWT provides an efficient algorithm for identifying long haplotype matches between a target haplotype and all reference haplotypes simultaneously.

Merged PBWT construction. The first step merges the reference panel haplotypes and target haplotypes into a single PBWT data structure, restricted to the set of markers shared between the reference and target. This merged PBWT maintains the positional prefix array (a sequence of haplotype indices sorted in reverse prefix order at each position) along with a divergence array that records where the last mismatch occurred for each pair of adjacent haplotypes in the sorted order.

Match identification. At each variant position k (for k ≥ L, where L is the minimum match length, default L = 5), the algorithm identifies all reference haplotypes that match the target haplotype for at least L consecutive variants ending at position k. A match is recorded when the target and a reference haplotype diverge (i.e., carry different alleles at position k) or at the last variant of the chromosome, provided the cumulative match length exceeds L. Formally, for each target haplotype t and reference haplotype r, a match of length l ending at position k is recorded if the sequences are identical from position k − l to k−1 and diverge at k.

Cumulative scoring. Selphi maintains a running total of match lengths for each reference haplotype across the entire chromosome: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$hap\_total\left[r\right]\:+=\:l$$\end{document} for each match of length l involving reference haplotype r. This cumulative score captures the global extent of sequence sharing between each reference haplotype and the target, a statistic that is unavailable to methods operating within genomic windows.

Ranked retention. At each variant, only the top K1 = 450 matches are retained, ranked primarily by match length and secondarily by the cumulative score (used as tiebreaker). This ensures that haplotypes with extensive chromosome-wide sharing are preferentially retained when local match lengths are equal, effectively prioritizing haplotypes that share longer putative IBD segments.

Backward re-scoring pass. After the forward scan completes, a second backward pass is performed over the retained matches. This pass re-evaluates all matches using the final cumulative scores (which are now complete) and reduces the retained set to K2 = 60 matches per variant. This two-pass strategy ensures that the global match statistics from the entire chromosome inform the final selection, even for matches found early in the forward scan when cumulative scores were still incomplete.

The result is a sparse matrix of dimensions (M × N), where M is the number of reference haplotypes and N is the number of shared variants, stored in compressed sparse column (CSC) format as .npz files. Each non-zero entry records the match length at that position.

Step 3: Top-k filtering and consistency check. After thresholding, the top K3 = 50 haplotypes per variant (by weighted match length) are retained. The algorithm then identifies haplotypes that appear at more than one variant position. Haplotypes appearing at only a single variant are discarded, as these are more likely to represent short coincidental matches rather than genuine haplotype sharing.

Step 4: Final frequency-based filtering. The remaining haplotype set is subjected to a final filter: the occurrence count of each haplotype across all variant positions is computed, and haplotypes below the 10th percentile of occurrence counts (or below the maximum count minus one, whichever is smaller) are removed. This eliminates low-frequency haplotypes that passed earlier filters but lack consistent support across the chromosome.

The final filtered set of reference haplotypes, which varies in size per target haplotype but is typically much smaller than the full reference panel, constitutes the set of hidden states for the HMM.

Hidden Markov model

The imputation component of Selphi uses a modified Li and Stephens HMM13,34. In this model, the hidden states at each target marker correspond to the filtered set of reference haplotypes. The observations are the target genotypes at shared markers.

This differs from standard implementations where all reference haplotypes are states at every marker.

Emission probabilities. At each marker, matching between the target allele and a reference haplotype allele is modeled with an error probability \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${p}_{err}$$\end{document} that is calibrated to the quality of local haplotype matches:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${p}_{err}=\frac{C}{N}$$\end{document}

where C is the 10th-percentile occurrence count from Step 4 of haplotype selection and N is the total number of shared variants. This adaptive parameterisation contrasts with the fixed emission rates used in Beagle 5.417 and IMPUTE519 (both \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\epsilon\:={10}^{-4}$$\end{document}) and with the panel-wide estimation used in Minimac16. In Selphi, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${p}_{err}$$\end{document} is higher when match quality is poor (fewer consistently matching haplotypes) and lower when matches are strong, allowing the HMM to appropriately weight its confidence in the matching haplotypes.

Forward-backward algorithm. Selphi implements the forward-backward algorithm operating on dense matrices reduced to only the filtered haplotype indices. Forward and backward passes are computed in chunks aligned with the interpolation breakpoints. Forward blocks are compressed with Zstandard (zstd) during computation and decompressed during the backward pass to reduce peak memory usage. The final haplotype weights at each marker are the element-wise product of the forward and backward probabilities, normalized to sum to one. Weights below 1/(H+ 1) (where H is the number of filtered haplotypes) are set to zero to maintain sparsity. The resulting weight matrices are stored as sparse CSR matrices for efficient downstream interpolation.

Interpolation and genotype calling

Between consecutive target markers, allele probabilities at imputed markers are computed by linearly interpolating the HMM-derived haplotype weights, following the approach used in Beagle 5.417 and IMPUTE519.

For an imputed marker at reference panel position j lying between target markers at positions k and k+1, the interpolated weight for reference haplotype r is:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${w}_{r}\left(j\right)={w}_{r}\left(k\right)+\left({w}_{r}(k+1)-{w}_{r}\left(k\right)\right)\cdot\:\frac{j-k}{(k+1)-k}$$\end{document}

where j, k, and k + 1 denote chromosomal positions (in base pairs) of the imputed marker and the two flanking target markers, respectively, so that (k + 1) − k corresponds to the genomic distance between consecutive target markers. The probability of the alternate allele at position j on the target haplotype is then computed as a weighted average over the reference panel, following the formulation used in Beagle 5.417 and IMPUTE519:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P(alt\hspace{0.17em}|\hspace{0.17em}j)=\frac{{\sum}_{r}^{}{w}_{r}\left(j\right)\hspace{0.17em}{x}_{r}\left(j\right)}{{\sum}_{r}^{}{w}_{r}\left(j\right)}$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${x}_{r}\left(j\right)\in\:\left\{\mathrm{0,1}\right\}$$\end{document} is the allele carried by reference haplotype r at position j (0 for reference, 1 for alternate). Interpolation is parallelized across genomic intervals using Python’s joblib library.

For all benchmarking analyses in this study, imputed dosages (DS) from Selphi were compared against dosages from competing methods. When comparing to true (masked) genotypes, hard-call concordance, squared correlation (R²) between imputed dosages and true allele counts, and imputation quality score (IQS) were computed.

Whole-chromosome processing and edge effects

In Selphi, haplotype matching and selection operate across the entire chromosome without segmenting it into windows. This design choice has two consequences:

No boundary artifacts. Methods that process the genome in windows or chunks may produce imputation inaccuracies near window boundaries, even when overlapping buffer regions are used. Selphi avoids this entirely because match statistics are computed globally and there are no boundaries to reconcile.Global match context. The cumulative scoring in the PBWT and the frequency normalization in the selection stage use match information from the entire chromosome. A reference haplotype that matches the target well in one region of the chromosome will receive a higher cumulative score, improving its chances of being retained at other positions where its local match may be shorter. This global context is particularly valuable for rare variant imputation, where the correct reference haplotype may be identifiable only through extended sharing patterns.

However, whole-chromosome processing also increases memory requirements compared to chunked approaches (see Computational Resources below). Selphi mitigates this through sparse matrix representations and chunked reference panel storage, but users should be aware that memory usage scales with chromosome length and reference panel size.

Regarding the potential for information leakage in imputation37, the whole-chromosome approach in Selphi uses the same information sources as windowed methods, target genotypes at array markers and reference panel haplotypes, and does not introduce additional data sources. The haplotype selection heuristic operates on match patterns at genotyped positions only. The HMM transition probabilities are based on genetic distances from public recombination maps, not on the imputed data itself. Therefore, the information leakage profile of Selphi is comparable to that of existing methods.

Datasets

1000 genomes project

The 1000 Genomes Project 30x dataset31 contains phased sequences of 3,202 individuals sampled from 26 different populations. To ensure that no related individuals were shared between the target and reference sets, which would artificially inflate imputation accuracy, we partitioned individuals using the pedigree file (http://ftp.1000genomes.ebi.ac.uk/vol1/ftp/technical/working/20130606_sample_info/20130606_g1k.ped).

Specifically, individuals whose Family ID differed from their Individual ID were assigned to the reference panel (N = 2,401), while individuals whose Family ID matched their Individual ID were used as the target dataset (N = 801, from 12 of the 26 populations; Figure S4). We acknowledge that partitioning by Family ID may not eliminate all cryptic relatedness in the 1000 Genomes dataset31, as some residual relatedness beyond declared family structures has been documented. The same partitioning strategy was applied identically across all four imputation tools, so any residual relatedness affects all methods equally.

The 1000 Genomes data were already phased as part of the 30x release, and we used the provided phased haplotypes directly for both the reference panel and target data.

We applied the following variant filters: (i) FILTER = PASS; (ii) genotype missingness < 5%; (iii) Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium P > 10 −10 in at least one super-population; (iv) Mendelian error rate ≤ 5%; and (v) minor allele count (MAC) ≥ 2 in the reference panel.

To assess imputation accuracy, we masked markers in the target samples to simulate genotyping data. Specifically, we retained only markers present on the Illumina Global Screening Array (GSA v3) and treated all other positions as imputation targets.

TOPMed dataset

To evaluate imputation performance with a larger, more diverse reference panel, we assembled a reference panel from the NHLBI TOPMed (Trans-Omics for Precision Medicine) Program, Freeze 8, GRCh3832. We accessed whole genome sequencing data from 32 studies (48 consent groups) available through dbGaP, totaling 90,527 samples, of which 5,000 MESA samples were used as imputation targets and 85,527 as the reference panel. TOPMed data were provided as VCF files through dbGaP. For details on the TOPMed Freeze 8 processing pipeline, see https://topmed.nhlbi.nih.gov/topmed-whole-genome-sequencing-methods-freeze-8. All study names and dbGaP accession IDs are listed in Table S1.

We focused on chromosome 20 for computational tractability: the TOPMed reference panel comprises 85,527 reference haplotypes per chromosome, and a chromosome-wide PBWT pass over all 22 autosomes against this panel was outside the compute budget available for benchmarking. Chromosome 20 is one of the smaller human autosomes (~ 62 Mb), so peak memory and runtime per chromosome are at the lower end of the autosomal range; the same single-chromosome runs are therefore tractable on the available hardware. We discuss the expected behavior on larger chromosomes in the corresponding Discussion subsection. Haplotype phasing of the TOPMed reference panel was performed using Beagle 5.417 with default parameters, as Beagle is optimized for phasing large datasets.

From the TOPMed dataset, we selected 5,000 samples from the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis (MESA)38,39 as target samples: White-Caucasian (N = 1,634), Black-African-American (N = 930), Hispanic (N = 862), and Chinese-American (N = 535); the remaining 1,039 samples were imputed and contributed to overall accuracy metrics but were not included in the race-stratified analyses (Fig. 2B, Table S5). Unrelatedness among the 5,000 MESA target samples and between target and reference samples was confirmed using KING40, excluding pairs with estimated kinship coefficient > 0.0884 (corresponding to second-degree relatedness or closer). The remaining 85,527 WGS samples served as the reference panel.

Quality control: (i) multi-allelic variants were split into bi-allelic forms using BCFtools41; (ii) Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium P < 10 −30 were excluded; (iii) variants with > 5% missing data (GQ = 0) were removed; (iv) abnormal heterozygosity (< 0.5 or > 1.5) were filtered; (v) alternative alleles with AA-score < 0.5 were excluded; (vi) only bi-allelic SNPs with FILTER = PASS were retained. The final reference panel for chr20 consisted of 85,527 samples and 17,900,635 bi-allelic SNPs.

The reference panel was converted to tool-specific formats: .bref3 for Beagle 5.4, .imp5 format for IMPUTE5, and .msav for Minimac4. Target genotypes were obtained by masking MESA samples to the GSA array positions.

UK Biobank

We used 150,119 WGS samples jointly called with GraphTyper v2.7.142, available as pVCF files on the UK Biobank RAP43. Haplotype phasing was performed using SHAPEIT v4.2.218 for all autosomal chromosomes.

Quality control: (i) multi-allelic variants were decomposed into bi-allelic forms using BCFtools41; (ii) Hardy-Weinberg P < 10 −30 excluded; (iii) > 5% missing data (GQ = 0) removed; (iv) heterozygosity < 0.5 or > 1.5 filtered; (v) AA-score < 0.5 excluded; (vi) only FILTER = PASS variants retained.

We selected 50,000 samples with White British ancestry that were (i) unrelated to any other individual in the dataset (confirmed using KING kinship estimates provided by the UK Biobank, excluding pairs with kinship > 0.0884) and (ii) had Axiom SNP array data available for imputation experiments. The remaining 100,119 WGS samples served as the reference panel.

Phased Axiom genotype data were obtained from the UK Biobank release33 and lifted over to GRCh38, with strand flips discarded, resulting in 657,354 autosomal markers for 487,442 samples (Table S2). After liftover, approximately 99.8% of original variants were retained.

GWAS analysis

Following imputation of all autosomal chromosomes for 50,000 White British UK Biobank individuals, we selected 50 candidate phenotypes (Table S7) with < 10% missing data across anthropometric traits and blood measurements. After applying the locus-selection criteria below, three phenotypes had no GWAS Catalog locus that passed quality control in all three datasets (hc-WGS, Selphi-imputed, Beagle 5.4-imputed) and were therefore not represented in the concordance analysis; the remaining 47 phenotypes form the basis of Fig. 3B and Table S8. To assess associations, we used plink247 with default parameters, incorporating sex, age, and the first 10 principal components as covariates. Because all 50,000 target samples were confirmed unrelated (KING kinship < 0.0884), standard linear/logistic regression with principal component adjustment is appropriate and mixed-model approaches (e.g., Regenie48) are not required.

The analysis reported here is a concordance analysis: imputed-genotype association statistics are compared against statistics obtained on the same individuals using high-coverage WGS (hc-WGS), restricted to a pre-specified set of known trait-associated loci. It is not a discovery analysis: we do not test the ability of either imputation method to identify novel signals or replicate previously unreported associations. The aim is to quantify how closely the GWAS statistics derived from imputed genotypes track those derived from hc-WGS, which serves as the reference truth.

We analyzed three datasets: hc-WGS, Beagle 5.4-imputed, and Selphi-imputed. Our locus selection criteria: (i) genome-wide significant loci (P < 5 × 10 −8) reported by the NHGRI GWAS Catalog (release 2023-08-26)49; (ii) strongest signal per locus (+/−100 kb) to select independent loci. Only imputed variants were considered (array variants excluded).

Beta values and P values from imputed datasets were compared to hc-WGS results using: (1) absolute beta values, and (2) -log10(P) on a log scale for nominally significant associations (P < 0.05). Concordance (R²) was assessed against the 1:1 identity line. All GWAS analyses were performed using the same pipeline (plink2) for both Selphi and Beagle 5.4 imputed data, ensuring that any differences in downstream results reflect imputation quality rather than analytical methodology.

PRS analysis

We generated PRS for seven phenotypes: atrial fibrillation, asthma, hypertension, type 2 diabetes, height, apolipoprotein B levels, and calcium. Quantitative traits were binarized: short stature (lowest 10% by height, sex-adjusted), hypercalcemia (calcium > 2.6 mmol/L), high ApoB (> 1.3 g/L).

Summary statistics were generated by meta-analyzing external datasets from the GWAS Catalog (Table S9). We implemented clumping plus thresholding (C + T) models exploring: R² thresholds {0.01, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 0.8, 0.95}, window sizes {50, 100, 200, 500}/R², 50 P value thresholds on a log-log scale, and 13 MAF thresholds (0.001 to 0.1), totaling 18,200 models per phenotype50. The 50,000 individuals were split 30,000/20,000 for training/testing.

PRS accuracy was assessed using: (i) relative risk (ratio of case proportion in 5th vs. 1st PRS quintile), and (ii) area under the ROC curve (AUC).

Absolute AUC and R² values for each phenotype and imputation source are reported in Table 4. Selphi-imputed PRS yielded AUC/R² values closer to hc-WGS than Beagle 5.4 in 6 of the 7 phenotypes evaluated. The absolute differences between the two imputation methods are small (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\le\:0.005$$\end{document} on either AUC or R²) and we report them descriptively; with seven phenotypes, formal tests of the per-phenotype difference have limited statistical power, so we refrain from claims of significant superiority on PRS at the phenotype level. The directional consistency (6 of 7) is consistent with the rare-variant accuracy improvements reported in the per-variant error counts (Tables 2 and 3) and in the GWAS concordance analysis (Fig. 3B, Table S8).

GWAS concordance with hc-WGS and PRS performance

To assess whether improved imputation translates to GWAS summary statistics that more closely track those obtained from hc-WGS, we used the 50,000 UK Biobank samples imputed by Selphi and Beagle 5.4 to conduct GWAS for 50 traits. As described in Methods, this is a concordance analysis restricted to known trait-associated loci from the GWAS Catalog, not a discovery analysis.

Across the 47 phenotypes that yielded at least two GWAS Catalog loci with imputed effect estimates in all three datasets (hc-WGS, Selphi, Beagle 5.4), the per-MAF-bin concordance R² between imputed-genotype and hc-WGS association statistics was higher for Selphi than for Beagle 5.4 in all three bins (Fig. 3B, Table S8). The difference was statistically significant after Bonferroni correction in the [2, 10)% and [10, 50]% bins (paired Wilcoxon P = 0.011 and 1.8 × 10−3, respectively; Selphi higher in 30/45 and 38/47 phenotypes). In the [0.5, 2)% bin the per-phenotype direction also favoured Selphi (mean ΔR² = +0.25; Selphi higher in 18/31 phenotypes), but the small number of GWAS Catalog loci per phenotype in this regime produced high variance, and the difference did not reach corrected significance (P = 1.0). Full per-bin statistics are in Table S8.

Discussion

Genotype imputation continues to play an important role in large-population genotyping studies as reference panels grow. In this work we describe Selphi, a genotype imputation tool that combines a chromosome-wide PBWT scan with a multi-stage haplotype-selection cascade and a Li and Stephens HMM whose hidden-state set varies per marker. We summarise where this design departs from the most widely used existing tools and discuss which components are most likely to be responsible for the differences in error counts reported above.

Statistical model. Selphi shares its statistical core with previous imputation methods: like Beagle 5.417, IMPUTE519, and Minimac416, the imputation is performed by a Li and Stephens HMM13,34 on a reduced set of conditioning haplotypes, and like IMPUTE5 the state-space reduction is informed by the Positional Burrows-Wheeler Transform20. What differs across methods is how the conditioning set is constructed and over what spatial granularity it is allowed to change, and how the HMM is parameterised on top of that set. Selphi’s contribution in these three areas is summarised in the following paragraphs.

Selection strategies in Beagle 5.4, IMPUTE5, Minimac4, and Selphi. Each comparator tool reduces the HMM state space using a different mechanism, but all three share one structural property: the conditioning set is constructed once per fixed-size genomic unit (window, region, or block) and is then held constant across all markers within that unit.

Beagle 5.4 processes the chromosome in 40 cM windows with 4 cM overlap17 and constructs \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$J=\mathrm{1,600}$$\end{document} composite reference haplotypes per window by identity-by-state (IBS) lookahead at 0.1 cM intervals: at each interval, reference haplotypes that are IBS with the target across seven (then six, then five, …) consecutive intervals are added to the composite set until J is reached. Selphi’s selection strategy differs in two structural ways. First, the PBWT match scan is performed once over the entire chromosome rather than within fixed-size windows; this produces, for every reference haplotype r, a chromosome-wide cumulative match-length statistic \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$hap\_total\left[r\right]={\sum}_{\left(matches\right)} {\ell}$$\end{document}. Second, this cumulative statistic feeds into a multi-stage filtering cascade (frequency normalisation rescaling match lengths by per-haplotype \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$hap\_total$$\end{document}; an adaptive knob threshold derived from the local match-length distribution; top-\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${K}_{3}=50$$\end{document} retention; a consistency filter requiring matches at more than one variant; a final occurrence cutoff at the 10th percentile) that produces, at every marker, a small subset of reference haplotypes \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${H}_{k}$$\end{document} that are simultaneously well-matched at that marker and well-supported across the chromosome. The HMM operates directly on this per-marker subset, with the transition probability re-normalised by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${H}_{k}$$\end{document} at every marker and the emission rate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${p}_{err}=C/N$$\end{document} calibrated to the cascade’s local match-quality summary.

The structural distinction is therefore not in the use of a Li and Stephens HMM, in the use of PBWT for matching, or in the use of state-space reduction (all three are shared with at least one comparator), but in the granularity at which the conditioning set adapts. Beagle 5.4, IMPUTE5, and Minimac4 each guarantee local relevance of the conditioning set through their respective construction (composite mosaics that adapt within the window, divergence-selected neighbours, or unique-prefix collapsing), but the set is fixed once per window/region/block. In Selphi, the cascade can drop or retain a different subset of reference haplotypes at every variant, and the HMM is parameterised to operate on a conditioning set that varies at this finer granularity.

Why this design produces more accurate imputation. Selphi’s accuracy comes from the integration of the cascade and the HMM, rather than from any single isolable component. The cascade delivers a small, locally-relevant per-marker conditioning set; the HMM is parameterised to operate on exactly this kind of input. The chromosome-wide PBWT scan is instrumental to this design because it produces the cumulative match-length statistic \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$hap\_total\left[r\right]$$\end{document} that feeds the cascade’s frequency normalisation; the per-marker granularity of the conditioning set is the property that distinguishes Selphi from Beagle 5.4, IMPUTE5, and Minimac4 at the structural level.

Component-level ablations on chromosome 22 (Supplementary Note A) support this interpretation. The cumulative-score signal enters the algorithm at two points: as a tiebreak in PBWT match retention, and as the basis of frequency normalisation in cascade Step 1; and removing either entry point alone leaves the signal flowing through the other; the chromosome-wide scan is therefore necessary to produce the signal but the tiebreak in isolation is not a separable contributor (overall \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varDelta\:{R}^{2}\approx\:+0.0003$$\end{document} on the 1000 Genomes 801-target benchmark when the tiebreak is disabled). The cascade’s frequency-normalisation and knob-threshold steps contribute meaningfully on small reference panels (a 10% error increase on the example dataset when these steps are disabled) but provide diminishing returns at biobank scale (near-zero change on 1000 Genomes), because downstream top-K retention and consistency filtering are sufficient at that scale to prune low-quality matches.

Forcing the conditioning set to be constant across markers (even at the same size as the typical baseline \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${H}_{k}$$\end{document}) produces a substantial accuracy degradation (overall \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${R}^{2}$$\end{document} from 0.480 to 0.082 on the 1000 Genomes benchmark; rare-variant bin from 0.268 to 0.014). The post-collapse accuracy is below what any of the comparator tools would deliver on the same data, which indicates that the test breaks a structural assumption shared by all HMM-based imputation methods: the HMM’s emission and transition probabilities require a conditioning set that is locally relevant to each marker. Beagle 5.4, IMPUTE5, and Minimac4 each enforce local relevance within their window, region, or block; Selphi enforces it at the per-marker level through the cascade. The per-marker granularity is therefore the structural distinction; the cascade and the HMM together implement it.

This wide-context strategy is particularly relevant for rare variant imputation. Rare variants are carried by few haplotypes in the reference panel; identifying these haplotypes requires using all available matching information, including long-range sharing that may extend well beyond a typical imputation window. The distinction from existing methods is best understood as a difference in haplotype selection strategy rather than as a fundamental difference in the type of genetic signal being exploited: all imputation methods implicitly exploit haplotype sharing reflecting shared ancestry (IBD), and the question is how effectively the algorithm identifies and prioritizes the correct shared segments. By accumulating match statistics over the full chromosome and retaining only haplotypes consistently matched at multiple positions, Selphi narrows the conditioning set toward references whose extended sharing pattern is unlikely to be coincidental, without requiring pedigree information or formal IBD segment calling.

Selphi achieved pronounced improvements in East Asian and African populations of the 1000 Genomes Project, and in Chinese Americans in the TOPMed dataset. These populations typically have fewer close matches in available reference panels52. The wide-context selection strategy is particularly beneficial in such cases because it can identify the correct reference haplotypes through cumulative evidence across many positions, even when individual matches are shorter due to greater genetic diversity or smaller representation in the reference panel. Unlike approaches that expand or manipulate reference panels27–30, Selphi improves accuracy within existing panels, which is important when panel expansion is not feasible, as is often the case for underrepresented populations53–55.

The TOPMed benchmark was run on chromosome 20 alone for compute-budget reasons, however, three lines of evidence support that the relative ordering of methods observed on chr20 can be expected to extend to other autosomes. First, on the 1000 Genomes Project benchmark, Selphi achieved the lowest error count on each of chromosomes 1–22 individually and across all super-populations (Fig. 2A, Table S4), indicating that the relative advantage is not specific to a particular chromosome. Second, the Selphi pipeline operates per chromosome with no chromosome-level dependencies, so the algorithmic behavior on chr20 is structurally identical to that on a larger chromosome, only \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${N}_{var}$$\end{document} and the absolute compute cost change. Third, longer chromosomes provide more opportunity for the chromosome-wide cumulative match scoring (Stage 1) and frequency-normalisation step (Stage 2 Step 1) to discriminate between reference haplotypes, so any contribution of those components to accuracy would, if anything, be at least as strong on chromosomes longer than chr20. We do not, however, claim quantitative equivalence of the per-MAF improvement margins across chromosomes; chromosome-by-chromosome margins on TOPMed remain to be measured.

The GWAS concordance pattern is consistent with the per-variant error counts in Fig. 2C and Table S6: where Selphi has a measurable advantage in raw imputation accuracy on UK Biobank, it also produces GWAS effect estimates closer to those obtained from hc-WGS (Fig. 3B, Table S8). For PRS we observed a directionally consistent ranking (Selphi closer to hc-WGS in 6 of 7 phenotypes; Table 4) with absolute differences too small relative to the phenotype count to support a formal significance claim. Together, these observations indicate that algorithmic improvements at the imputation stage can shift the concordance with hc-WGS-derived summary statistics without requiring expansion of the reference panel; previous work has reported analogous shifts driven by reference-panel improvements51,56.

Selphi’s whole-chromosome processing requires more memory than chunked approaches, which may limit scalability on memory-constrained systems. The current implementation is also substantially slower than the comparator tools, by factors of approximately 50×, 6×, and 7× over Beagle 5.4, IMPUTE5, and Minimac4 respectively on the 1000 Genomes benchmark (Table 1), owing to the single-threaded PBWT inner loop, the per-marker reindexing required by the variable hidden-state set, and serialisation across the Python ↔ C boundary. Reducing the runtime while preserving the cumulative-scoring and cascade design is the focus of an ongoing engineering effort, with targeted optimisations under development: (i) thread-parallel PBWT match identification across target haplotypes; (ii) replacing the Python-side cascade with a JIT-compiled implementation that operates on shared-memory arrays; (iii) optional chromosome-segmentation with overlap regions, allowing users to trade the chromosome-wide cumulative score for shorter wall time on large reference panels; and (iv) reducing the Python ↔ C serialization volume by streaming match data rather than materialising full per-haplotype matrices. Users who require shorter wall times today can also parallelise across chromosomes, since each chromosome is processed independently. Memory usage (3.3 GB peak) is already competitive with the comparator tools, and the current implementation addresses out-of-memory cases via automatic core reduction; memory reduction is therefore not a near-term priority. Selphi currently requires phased input and does not perform joint phasing and imputation.

The evaluation in this study compares imputed dosages against masked true genotypes using standard metrics (concordance, R², IQS). We did not apply quality-score filtering to imputed variants before evaluation, in order to assess the raw output quality of each method. In practice, users should apply quality filters appropriate to their analytical goals. Selphi reports a per-variant dosage R-squared quality score (INFO/DR2), estimated as the ratio of observed dosage variance to expected variance under Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium, analogous to the Rsq metric in Minimac4. Users can filter imputed variants by DR2 threshold (e.g., DR2 > = 0.3) prior to downstream analysis, following standard best practices.

In summary, we describe a genotype imputation method that combines cumulative match scoring computed over a wide genomic context with a multi-stage filtering cascade, used to construct a per-marker conditioning set for the Li and Stephens HMM. On the 1000 Genomes, TOPMed (chr20), and UK Biobank benchmarks reported here, this design yields lower per-variant error counts than Beagle 5.4, IMPUTE5, and Minimac4 across the MAF spectrum and across super-populations, at a runtime cost that is the focus of ongoing optimisation work.
