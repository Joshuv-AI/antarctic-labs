Posterior Refinement: Fast Language Generation via Any-Order Flow Maps
Jaehoon Yoo

Abstract
Non-autoregressive generation offers a powerful paradigm for iterative refinement, allowing models to recursively critique, erase and regenerate arbitrary subsets of tokens. However, existing non-autoregressive models fail to realize this potential. Masked Diffusion Models (MDMs) suffer from factorization error, causing sample quality to collapse when generating multiple tokens simultaneously. Flow Map Language Models (FMLMs) circumvent this bottleneck via joint sequence transport for excellent few-step generation, but sacrifice the inference-time flexibility of MDMs. We introduce FMLM + \text{FMLM}+ , a framework that bridges this gap by equipping FMLM with masking-style noise schedules. While generating the full sequence in a single step, FMLM + \text{FMLM}+ simultaneously scores the global consistency of each token a posteriori . We leverage this to introduce Posterior Refinement , a novel inference-time refinement strategy that enables the model to adaptively self-correct its outputs, matching the performance of discrete baselines with 32 × 32\times fewer NFEs. Across diverse benchmarks, we demonstrate that FMLM + \text{FMLM}+ with Posterior Refinement improves the speed–quality tradeoff over both MDM and FMLM families, providing a scalable foundation for high-fidelity language modeling. Beyond fast, parallel inference, non-autoregressive generation offers a more profound theoretical advantage over AR modeling: the capacity for

While traditional causal language models are fundamentally constrained by a one-way, left-to-right generation, an iterative refinement paradigm allows the model to globally critique, erase, and regenerate arbitrary subsets of tokens to continually improve its output.
This capacity directly mirrors human cognition; when composing complex text, humans rarely write perfectly in a single, unyielding left-to-right pass. Instead, we efficiently formulate a rough draft to capture the global context, and then iteratively assess, delete, and rewrite specific parts of text while keeping the overall picture in context

Motivated by this insight, we introduce

Subsequently,

Background

Let
\mathcal{V}
denote a vocabulary, with
|\mathcal{V}|=V
, and let
{\bf y}=({\bf y}^{l})_{l=1}^{L}\in\mathcal{V}^{L}
be a sequence of length
L
drawn from a data distribution
p({\bf y})
.

Autoregressive Models.

The AR paradigm models the data distribution through a left-to-right factorization

p({\bf y})=p({\bf y}^{1})p({\bf y}^{2}|{\bf y}^{1})\ldots p({\bf y}^{L}|{\bf y}^{<L})
,
and learns the conditional distributions
p({\bf y}^{l}\mid{\bf y}^{<l})
over tokens given their preceding context
[
32
,
18
,
7
]
.
Generation proceeds sequentially by sampling one token at a time conditioned on previously generated tokens.

Masked Diffusion Models.

Masked Diffusion Models (MDMs)
[
6
,
40
,
50
]
offer an alternative paradigm for discrete generative modeling, parameterizing marginals over clean tokens given a partially masked input.
MDMs extend the vocabulary
\mathcal{V}
with an extra
[\texttt{MASK}]
token. A monotonic fraction
\alpha(t)
of clean tokens
{\bf y}
is masked to produce
{\bf y}_{t}
.
The token-wise marginal
p_{\theta}({\bf y}^{l}\mid{\bf y}_{t})
is then trained via a classification loss over the masked positions,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{MDM}}(p_{\theta})=-\mathbb{E}_{t}\left[w(t)\sum_{l=1}^{L}\mathbf{1}[{\bf y}_{t}^{l}=\texttt{MASK}]\log p_{\theta}({\bf y}^{l}\mid{\bf y}_{t})\right],

(1)

where
w(t)>0
is some weighting function.

At inference, MDMs start from a fully masked sequence and generate tokens via an iterative unmask–remask procedure; at each step they first categorically sample from the marginal distribution for each token independently and then remask some tokens, repeating until all tokens are unmasked. A natural decoding strategy for MDMs is to commit tokens in decreasing order of confidence, usually using the maximum marginal probability
\max_{v}p_{\theta}({\bf y}^{l}=v\mid{\bf y}_{t})

[
59
,
55
]
.

Flow Matching and Flow Maps.

Flow matching
[
37
,
39
,
3
]
learns a continuous transport from a simple source distribution (e.g., Gaussian noise) to the target data distribution by regressing the velocity field of a probability flow ODE along a prescribed source–target interpolant.
We define
{\bf x}_{1}=f({\bf y})
as the one-hot representation of clean data, and sample
{\bf x}_{0}\sim\mathsf{N}(0,I)
as isotropic Gaussian noise of matching shape to construct the linear interpolant in Euclidean space:

\displaystyle I_{t}\coloneqq(1-t){\bf x}_{0}+t{\bf x}_{1},\qquad t\in[0,1].

(2)

This interpolant admits a deterministic probability flow ODE

\displaystyle\dot{\bf x}_{t}=b_{t}({\bf x}_{t}),\qquad b_{t}({\bf x})=\mathbb{E}[\dot{I}_{t}\mid I_{t}={\bf x}]=\mathbb{E}[{\bf x}_{1}-{\bf x}_{0}\mid I_{t}={\bf x}],

(3)

whose solution transports samples from the source distribution to the target data distribution
[
37
,
39
,
3
]
.
In practice, we parameterize the flow velocity
b_{t}
with a neural network, and inference requires a numerical integration of the probability flow ODE to generate samples.

The flow map
X_{s,t}:[0,1]^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{L\times V}\to\mathbb{R}^{L\times V}
associated with the velocity field
b_{t}
is defined as the solution operator that transports the state at time
s
to the state at time
t

[
9
]
:

\displaystyle X_{s,t}({\bf x}_{s})={\bf x}_{s}+\int_{s}^{t}b_{\tau}({\bf x}_{\tau})d\tau={\bf x}_{s}+(t-s)v_{s,t}({\bf x}_{s}),

(4)

where
v_{s,t}({\bf x}_{s})
is the average velocity (mean flow) between times
s
and
t

[
21
]
.
In the limit
s\to t
, the average velocity recovers the instantaneous velocity,
\lim_{s\to t}v_{s,t}({\bf x})=b_{t}({\bf x})
.
Crucially, this allows us to arbitrarily jump along the flow, including generating from the target distribution in a
single
function evaluation (
X_{0,1}
)
[
20
,
21
]
.

Flow Map Language Models.

Flow map language models (FMLM)
[
36
]
enable few-step language generation by parameterizing the
two-time denoiser
(TTD)
\delta_{s,t}:[0,1]^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{L\times V}\to\mathbb{R}^{L\times V}
,

\displaystyle\delta_{s,t}({\bf x})

\displaystyle\coloneqq{\bf x}+(1-s)v_{s,t}({\bf x}),

(5)

which extrapolates the flow-map transport to
t=1
and satisfies the boundary identity
\delta_{t,t}({\bf x})=\mathbb{E}[{\bf x}_{1}\mid I_{t}={\bf x}]

[
41
,
49
,
36
]
.
The TTD takes values on the per-token simplex,
\delta_{s,t}({\bf x})^{l}\in\Delta^{V-1}
for
l=1,\dots,L
, and obeys the semigroup identity

\displaystyle\delta_{s,t}({\bf x})=\gamma\,\delta_{s,u}({\bf x})+(1-\gamma)\,\delta_{u,t}(X_{s,u}({\bf x})),\qquad\gamma=\frac{(1-t)(u-s)}{(1-u)(t-s)},

(6)

for every
0\leq s\leq u\leq t\leq 1

[
36
]
.
In practice, we parameterize
{\delta}_{s,t}
with a token-wise softmax head and train it via a token-wise cross-entropy objective,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathsf{FMLM}}({\delta})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{t}\mathbb{E}_{{\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1}}\left[\text{CE}\left({\delta}_{s,s}(I_{s}),{\bf x}_{1}\right)\right]}_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{diag}}}+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{s,u,t}\mathbb{E}_{{\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1}}\left[\text{CE}\left({\delta}_{s,t}(I_{s}),\bar{\delta}_{s,t}\right)\right]}_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{off}}},

(7)

where
X_{s,u}({\bf x})\coloneqq\frac{1-u}{1-s}\,{\bf x}+\frac{u-s}{1-s}\,\delta_{s,u}({\bf x})
,
\bar{\delta}_{s,t}\coloneqq\text{stopgrad}\left({\gamma\,\delta_{s,u}(I_{s})+(1-\gamma)\,\delta_{u,t}(X_{s,u}(I_{s}))}\right)
.
Intuitively, this loss trains the two-time denoiser to reconstruct clean data along the diagonal
s=t
(
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{diag}}
) and to progressively distill a larger off-diagonal jump (
s\rightarrow t
) using two smaller jumps (
s\rightarrow u,u\rightarrow t
) as a teacher (
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{off}}
).

During inference, we can arbitrarily jump along the flow by recovering the flow map from the TTD,

\displaystyle X_{s,t}({\bf x})=\frac{1-t}{1-s}\,{\bf x}+\frac{t-s}{1-s}\,\delta_{s,t}({\bf x}).

(8)

This allows us to generate samples in as few as a
single
function evaluation (
\delta_{0,1}
).

FMLM+

Continuous diffusion and flow-based models typically denoise the full sequence in parallel
[
28
,
51
,
37
]
.
While effective for unconditional generation, this formulation has no native mechanism for conditioning on clean context.
Unlike autoregressive or masked models, where conditioning is a structural primitive, continuous-flow models often require architectural modifications, such as cross-attention or auxiliary guidance models, to incorporate conditional information
[
48
,
8
]
.
To address this limitation in flow-based language modeling, we introduce
\text{FMLM}+
, a generalization of FMLMs that natively learns to generate under diverse conditioning patterns (
Section

3.1
).
We show that this allows for flexible decoding strategies at inference time (
Section

3.2
), such as FMLM- and MDM- like inference, and further unlocks novel iterative refinement schemes (
Section

3.3
).

Training

The key idea underlying
\text{FMLM}+
is to generalize FMLMs by lifting the noising process to a masking-style design, preserving a set of clean tokens
{\bf y}^{\mathcal{C}}
at context positions
\mathcal{C}\subset\{1,\cdots,L\}
while noising the rest:

\displaystyle I_{t}^{l}\coloneqq\begin{cases}\mathbf{x}_{1}^{l},&\text{if }l\in\mathcal{C}\\
(1-t)\mathbf{x}_{0}^{l}+t\mathbf{x}_{1}^{l},&\text{else }(l\in[L]\setminus\mathcal{C}).\end{cases}

(9)

This enables
\text{FMLM}+
to condition on arbitrary clean context without additional architectural components.
In practice, this is implemented by converting the shared time conditioning in the DiT architecture
[
45
]
to a per-position conditioning with
t=1
for clean positions.
For brevity, we drop this implicit conditioning on
{\bf y}^{\mathcal{C}}
throughout the remainder of the paper, writing
\delta_{s,t}(\cdot)
instead of
\delta_{s,t}(\cdot|{\bf y}^{\mathcal{C}})
.

We train the two-time denoiser
\delta_{s,t}
with the following progressive self-distillation objective:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{FMLM}+}(\delta)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{C}}\Big[\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{t}\,\mathbb{E}_{{\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1}}\!\big[\mathrm{CE}(\delta_{s,s}(I_{s}),{\bf x}_{1})\odot m\big]}_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{diag}}}+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{s,u,t}\,\mathbb{E}_{{\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1}}\!\big[\mathrm{CE}(\delta_{s,t}(I_{s}),\bar{\delta}_{s,t})\odot m\big]}_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{off}}}\Big].

(10)

Compared to the vanilla FMLM objective
Equation

7
, we have an additional expectation over
\mathcal{C}
, the set of context tokens, and use
m\in\{0,1\}^{L}
with
m^{l}=\mathbf{1}[\,l\notin\mathcal{C}\,]
to mask the loss from tokens that are already clean.
To avoid extra complexity, we drop the time reparameterization trick from
36
and use the self-distillation objective (
10
) instead of the two-stage training in
36
for all
\text{FMLM}+
runs.

Leveraging Pretrained MDMs.

To accelerate the training of
\text{FMLM}+
, we propose a novel method to leverage pretrained MDMs based on the following observation.
At
s=t=0
, the off-diagonal term
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{off}}
in the
\text{FMLM}+
training objective
Equation

10
vanishes and the diagonal term
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{diag}}
reduces to:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{C}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{{\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1}}\left[\text{CE}(\delta_{0,0}({\bf x}_{0}),{\bf x}_{1})\odot m\right]\right].

(11)

This token-wise classification objective predicts the ground-truth tokens using only the context
{\bf y}^{\mathcal{C}}
and pure Gaussian noise.
Because the model must rely exclusively on the context, this formulation is equivalent to MDM training objective
Equation

1
, which similarly predicts targets from
{\bf y}^{\mathcal{C}}
and
[\texttt{MASK}]
tokens.

We use this equivalence to accelerate
\text{FMLM}+
training in two ways:

1.

Distillation.
We utilize an MDM as a teacher model, training the
\text{FMLM}+
student to match the MDM’s token probabilities for the same context. This approach reduces gradient variance and accelerates convergence
[
27
]
. Concretely, we use:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{Distill}}(\delta)=\mathcal{L}_{\text{\text{FMLM}+}}(\delta)+\lambda\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{C}}\mathbb{E}_{{\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1}}\left[\text{CE}(\delta_{0,0}(I_{0}),p_{\theta}({\bf y}_{0}))\right].

(12)

2.

Initialization.
We initialize
\text{FMLM}+
weights directly from a pretrained MDM checkpoint, leveraging the similarity between the masked diffusion and continuous flow objectives
[
44
]
. We show how the DiT architecture can mechanistically adapt MDM weights to handle noisy input in
Section

4.2
.

The core advantage of training
\text{FMLM}+
on diverse conditioning patterns is that it allows us to flexibly choose between various decoding strategies during inference.
As a simple example, we can recover the standard FMLM inference by defining a time grid
0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{N}=1
and denoising all unknown tokens simultaneously, jumping in
N
steps along the flow trajectory via
Equation

8
.

More importantly, this allows us to construct a mechanism for iterative refinement as a loop that performs parallel generation followed by committing a subset of tokens (
Section

3.2
).
At each iteration
i
, we propose a candidate
\hat{\bf x}_{1}
via FMLM inference.
Then, we select a subset
\mathcal{U}
to retain from this candidate sequence and round them into discrete tokens via an
\argmax
operation. Finally, we add them to our context set
\mathcal{C}\coloneqq\mathcal{C}\cup\mathcal{U}
and re-noise all remaining tokens
[L]\setminus\mathcal{C}
for the next iteration.
This, for example, allows for block-diffusion-style inference
[
5
]
by choosing
\mathcal{U}
as the next contiguous block of
B
tokens.

We may further perform MDM-like, confidence-based inference by noting the equivalence between
\delta_{0,0}
and MDMs (
11
).
Consequently, by sampling tokens from
\delta_{0,0}({\bf x}_{0})
and
by prioritizing positions with sharper
\delta_{0,0}({\bf x}_{0})
, we recover MDM’s confidence-based sampling
[
59
,
55
,
35
]
.
We note that this approach, applied either to
\text{FMLM}+
with
\delta_{0,0}
or MDMs, generates tokens and estimates confidence
a priori
, using only the partial context available at that step and without knowing what tokens will be generated at other positions.

Posterior Refinement

Beyond recovering existing inference schemes as in
Section

3.2
, we show that
\text{FMLM}+
enables a novel class of after-the-fact refinement strategies native to neither the MDM nor the FMLM paradigms.

During inference,
\text{FMLM}+
steps along the flow trajectory to simultaneously decode all positions.
At the final integration step, because the model operates on a nearly complete sequence, the model implicitly evaluates the conditional probability of each token given the rest of the generated text. The one-step generation
\delta_{0,1}
also captures this behavior since, ideally, the flow from
t=0
to
t=1
is equivalent to composing the transport from
0
to
t
and the flow map jump from
t
to
1
.
This enables us to compute token confidence conditioned on the fully generated sequence, effectively evaluating each token’s fit within the global context.
Reflecting the
a-posteriori
nature of these estimates, we term our approach
Posterior Refinement
(PR).

PR instantiates the inference loop in
Section

3.2
, choosing the retained set
\mathcal{U}
using the confidence of the predicted endpoint.
Concretely, at each iteration, we use the final flow map jump
\hat{\bf x}_{1}=\delta_{t_{N-1},1}({\bf x}_{t_{N-1}})
to set

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}=\{l\in[L]\setminus\mathcal{C}:p_{\text{max}}^{l}>p\},

(13)

where
p_{\text{max}}^{l}\coloneqq\max_{v}(\hat{\bf x}_{1}^{(i)})^{l,v}
is a measure of the a-posteriori confidence and
p
is some threshold for selecting confident tokens.
To avoid stalling when no position clears the threshold, we commit at least
k
positions per round, falling back to the top-
k
by confidence.

Interpretation.

Figure 5
:

Two types of confidence.
On a 2-mode toy problem, the one-step FMLM
\delta_{0,1}
outputs high
p_{\max}
for most inputs. Low-confidence regions concentrate near decision boundaries, where the endpoint is ambiguous, indicating
\delta_{0,1}
captures the epistemic confidence of the model. In contrast,
\delta_{0,0}
is flat for all inputs, reflecting its aleatoric nature.

An ideal FMLM would perfectly sample from its training distribution, always producing one-hot vectors.
In practice, however, the model outputs categorical distributions that are not strictly one-hot. To resolve this,
36
project each token to the nearest vertex of the probability simplex via the argmax operator.
We interpret the
rounding error
of this projection as a proxy for the model’s confidence.
This interpretation aligns with our empirical observations in Sudoku, where we find that incorrect tokens consistently exhibit high rounding error (
Figure

2
).

Concretely, this rounding error can be quantified using either the
\ell_{1}
distance or the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence between the generated distribution and the target one-hot distribution.
Under both metrics, the rounding error is a monotonically decreasing function of
p_{\max}^{l}
.
Consequently, applying a threshold to
p_{\max}^{l}
is mathematically equivalent to thresholding the rounding error under these metrics (
Proposition

A.1
).

Why a-priori confidence fails.

Because both the MDM denoiser
p_{\theta}
and the FMLM denoiser
\delta_{0,0}
predict token-wise marginals, independently sampling high-confidence tokens from these marginals cannot circumvent factorization error.
While this heuristic performs well on unimodal target distributions such as unique Sudoku solutions, it catastrophically fails on multi-modal distributions (
Table

6
).
We identify that MDMs, and equivalently
\delta_{0,0}
, conflate aleatoric uncertainty, the inherent randomness of the data distribution, with epistemic uncertainty, the model’s internal confidence
[
31
]
.
We hypothesize that by failing to perfectly learn the joint transport from Gaussian noise to clean one-hot data, the FMLM trajectory is able to surface the true epistemic uncertainty of the model through errors in its predictions (
Figure

5
).

We evaluate
\text{FMLM}+
on four benchmarks: TinyStories
[

]

]

Following

Training.

Conclusion

We introduce
\text{FMLM}+
, a generalization of flow map language models that equips continuous-flow language modeling with the any-order inference flexibility of masked diffusion, paving the way towards effective iterative refinement.
We further introduce
Posterior Refinement
, a novel, after-the-fact refinement strategy that re-noises and regenerates low-confidence tokens to self-correct in parallel.
Empirically,
\text{FMLM}+
improves the speed-quality tradeoff over both MDM and FMLM families across unconditional and conditional generation settings, matching or surpassing the strongest baselines with up to
32{\times}
fewer function evaluations.
Finally, we show that the masked diffusion objective is equivalent to a boundary case of the
\text{FMLM}+
training objective, and use this correspondence to derive two practical mechanisms based on distillation and transfer learning for accelerating
\text{FMLM}+
training using pretrained MDMs.

Future directions.

The correspondence between MDM and
\text{FMLM}+
offers a path to scale
\text{FMLM}+
to the billion-parameter regime using the growing pool of large pretrained masked diffusion models (LLaDA-8B
[
43
]
, Dream
[
56
]
, and others).
We also believe that the conditional nature of
\text{FMLM}+
can be leveraged to improve generation quality using techniques such as classifier-free guidance
[
29
]
.
Finally, Posterior Refinement exposes a form of inference-time scaling native to
\text{FMLM}+
, and we expect novel test-time strategies can be designed around such posterior-confidence signals.
We leave a systematic study of these directions to future work.

Limitations.

Methodologically, we follow the progressive self-distillation objective from
36
and do not explore alternatives such as the Lagrangian, Eulerian and Mean Flow variants
[
9
,
21
]
.
We conduct our experiments on a small scale on largely synthetic and small-sized datasets.
Thus, the scalability of our approach needs a more broad study with evaluation on downstream tasks.
While our current formulation of PR holds committed tokens fixed, alternative formulations can be explored to allow
\text{FMLM}+
to rectify previous errors.
Finally, we note that training from scratch is suboptimal compared to initializing with MDM weights, even at convergence.
This implies that there is room to improve
\text{FMLM}+
training with better model architecture and low-variance loss functions.

Appendix A
Theoretical details

Rounding error in terms of confidence

In
Section

3.3
we interpret the
rounding error
that occurs when model prediction is projected to its nearest one-hot vertex as a proxy for the model’s confidence. We claim that thresholding this error is equivalent to thresholding the top probability
p_{\max}^{l}
and show it here.

Fix a position
l
and write
q\coloneqq Q^{l}\in\Delta^{|V|-1}
for the predicted categorical distribution, with top probability
p_{\max}\coloneqq\max_{v}q^{v}
attained at
\hat{v}\coloneqq\argmax_{v}q^{v}
. Committing the token rounds
q
to the one-hot vertex
e_{\hat{v}}
, and the rounding error is the discrepancy between
q
and
e_{\hat{v}}
, measured either by the
\ell_{1}
distance or by the KL divergence.

Proposition A.1

(Rounding error is monotone in confidence)
.

With the notation above,

\displaystyle\bigl\|q-e_{\hat{v}}\bigr\|_{1}=2\,(1-p_{\max}),\qquad\mathsf{KL}(e_{\hat{v}}\>\|\>q)=-\log p_{\max}.

(14)

Both quantities are strictly decreasing in
p_{\max}\in(0,1]
, vanishing exactly at
p_{\max}=1
. Consequently, for any threshold
p\in(0,1]
,

\displaystyle p_{\max}\geq p\;\iff\;\bigl\|q-e_{\hat{v}}\bigr\|_{1}\leq 2(1-p)\;\iff\;\mathsf{KL}(e_{\hat{v}}\>\|\>q)\leq-\log p,

(15)

so thresholding the confidence
p_{\max}
and thresholding the rounding error under either metric retain exactly the same set of positions.

Proof.

The mass that
q
places off the top token is
\sum_{v\neq\hat{v}}q^{v}=1-p_{\max}
. For the
\ell_{1}
distance,

\displaystyle\bigl\|q-e_{\hat{v}}\bigr\|_{1}=\bigl|q^{\hat{v}}-1\bigr|+\sum_{v\neq\hat{v}}\bigl|q^{v}\bigr|=(1-p_{\max})+\sum_{v\neq\hat{v}}q^{v}=2\,(1-p_{\max}).

(16)

For the KL divergence, only the
v=\hat{v}
term survives, since
e_{\hat{v}}^{v}=0
for
v\neq\hat{v}
:

\displaystyle\mathsf{KL}(e_{\hat{v}}\>\|\>q)=\sum_{v}e_{\hat{v}}^{v}\log\frac{e_{\hat{v}}^{v}}{q^{v}}=1\cdot\log\frac{1}{q^{\hat{v}}}=-\log p_{\max}.

(17)

Both
2(1-p_{\max})
and
-\log p_{\max}
are continuous and strictly decreasing on
(0,1]
, so each is invertible; applying the inverse to either rounding-error threshold recovers the confidence threshold, giving the equivalences in
Equation

15
.
∎
