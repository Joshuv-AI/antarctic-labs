A semi-Lagrangian method for the polyatomic ESBGK model
Klaas Willems, Erik Arlemark, Giovanni Samaey, Axel Klar

Abstract
Polyatomic kinetic models are essential for accurately capturing the thermodynamic behavior of real gases, as internal energy modes significantly influence transport coefficients, relaxation processes, and non-equilibrium effects that cannot be represented by monoatomic models. The polyatomic ESBGK model describes molecular collisions as a relaxation towards a generalized Gaussian distribution with an anisotropic covariance matrix and an exponentially decaying internal energy distribution. We present a new semi-Lagrangian scheme for the polyatomic Ellipsoidal Statistical BGK (ESBGK) model of the Boltzmann equation. The semi-Lagrangian framework, being deterministic and grid-based, removes the time-step restriction associated with the linear transport term by following the method of characteristics. The potentially stiff relaxation term is treated using an implicit A-stable linear multistep method which, owing to the structure of the BGK operator, can be reformulated into a cheap time-stepping scheme. This yields a highly efficient and numerically stable method. The numerical method is asymptotic preserving and stiffly accurate, meaning the scheme asymptotically converges to a scheme for the Euler equations in the vanishing Knudsen limit. In addition, we prove that the first-order scheme, asymptotically converges to the compressible Navier-Stokes equation with correct transport coefficients. Finally, we propose inflow and outflow boundary conditions suitable for BGK-type kinetic equations. We perform simulations of the Fourier and Couette test case to compare the BGK model with Direct Simulation Monte Carlo (DSMC). To conclude, we demonstrate the method on a challenging orifice flow test case with moving boundaries.



Keywords.
Ellipsoidal Statistical BGK model, semi-Lagrangian scheme, polyatomic gas, kinetic theory of gases, asymptotic preserving

1
Introduction

Rarefied gas dynamics concerns flows in which non‑equilibrium molecular effects play a significant role. The degree of rarefaction is characterized by the Knudsen number

\mathrm{Kn}=\frac{\lambda}{L},

where
\lambda
denotes the mean free path and
L
is a characteristic macroscopic length. When
\mathrm{Kn}\ll 1
, particle collisions are frequent and continuum models such as the Euler or Navier–Stokes–Fourier equations accurately describe the evolution of density, momentum, and energy. For
\mathrm{Kn}=\mathcal{O}(1)
, however, the continuum hypothesis breaks down and kinetic models become necessary. Such conditions arise naturally in applications including high‑altitude aerodynamics and micro‑electromechanical systems (MEMS)
[
32
]
, either due to low pressure (yielding a large mean free path
\lambda
) or due to small geometric scales
L
.

The Boltzmann equation provides a fundamental statistical description of rarefied gases, but its collision operator contains a five‑fold integral, making direct numerical solution expensive. For this reason, relaxation‑type kinetic models—beginning with the BGK model—have been developed to simplify computations while retaining key physical properties such as conservation laws, entropy dissipation, and correct asymptotic behavior. The ESBGK model extends BGK by enabling correct Prandtl number matching. Since many gases of practical interest are polyatomic, further extensions are required to incorporate internal energy and additional degrees of freedom.

A range of numerical methods exists for kinetic equations. The Direct Simulation Monte Carlo (DSMC) method
[
5
]
provides a robust particle‑based approximation of the Boltzmann equation, particularly in high‑Mach or highly non‑equilibrium regimes, but may suffer from statistical noise or high cost for low-speed or transient flows. Deterministic methods, known as discrete velocity methods, provide for accurate alternatives
[
26
]
. They rely on a discretised velocity space, after which the advection and collision operators can be advanced separately using a time‑splitting procedure. For the Boltzmann equation, the collision step can be computed efficiently by exploiting the convolution structure of the collision operator
[
28
]
. In contrast, for BGK‑type models, the collision term is typically treated implicitly in time to circumvent the stiffness that arises in the small‑Knudsen‑number regime
[
29
]
. This idea has been explored in the context of finite volume schemes
[
8
,
17
,
39
]
.

A particularly well-known deterministic numerical method for BGK-type equations is the so-called semi-Lagrangian method
[
20
]
, which uses the method of characteristics to rewrite the equation as an ODE along characteristics. By tracing characteristics backwards to previous timesteps, a scheme is obtained that is not restricted by a CFL condition. The semi‑Lagrangian framework has been extended to conservative reconstructions
[
14
,
15
]
, to the ESBGK model
[
6
]
, to adaptive velocity grids
[
9
,
7
]
, and to BGK models for gas mixtures
[
13
]
.

Contributions.

The goal of this work is to extend the semi‑Lagrangian methodology to the polyatomic ESBGK model. The main contributions are:

1.

A new semi‑Lagrangian discretisation of the polyatomic ESBGK model, including an explicit treatment of the collision operator. We follow the procedure from
[
18
]
, in which higher-order moments of the local equilibrium distribution at the next time step are formulated explicitly as a function of moments of a known distribution at the previous time step. In contrast to
[
18
]
, in the polyatomic setting, we obtain a nonlinear system of equations for two temperatures which can be solved using a Newton method. For the velocity covariance matrix, we obtain an explicit expression that simplifies to the case of
[
18
]
for monoatomic particles.

2.

A proof that the first-order scheme converges to a consistent discretisation of the Navier–Stokes–Fourier equations with correct transport coefficients. We thereby generalize the proof from
[
6
]
for the ESBGK model, to the polyatomic setting. The proof follows a Chapman–Enskog expansion of the numerical scheme, but is more involved due to the presence of additional additional higher-order moments of the distribution function.

3.

An implementation of physically relevant boundary conditions, including diffusive–reflective walls and pressure-driven inflow/outflow conditions. For the diffuse–reflective boundary conditions, we avoid the use of iterative procedures or extrapolation to the boundaries as in
[
21
]
. Instead, the boundary conditions are imposed directly after the reconstruction step, prior to the collision step, resulting in a simple and efficient explicit treatment. This approach is then naturally extended to pressure-driven inflow/outflow boundary conditions. To the best of our knowledge, such inflow/outflow boundary conditions for deterministic solvers of BGK-type equations have not yet been reported in the literature.

4.

A comprehensive comparison of BGK, ESBGK, polyatomic ESBGK, DSMC, and Navier–Stokes models for canonical test cases such as Fourier and Couette flow
[
19
]
. This provides a consistent reference framework to assess how these models relate to each other and to benchmark results within the same numerical setting. We thereby aim to complement existing comparisons of polyatomic models using stochastic particle methods for more complex geometries
[
1
,
30
]
. We also note that preliminary polyatomic Couette flow simulations using a UGKS scheme have recently been reported in
[
4
]
.

5.

Simulations of stationary and moving orifice configurations, demonstrating the performance of the method in non-trivial geometries across continuum and transitional flow regimes. The results include comparisons with Navier–Stokes and DSMC reference data. The moving orifice case further illustrates the ability of the method to handle time-dependent geometries and capture complex flow behaviour such as expansion, recirculation, and shock structures.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section
2
presents the mathematical background, describing the Boltzmann equation, BGK, ESBGK, and polyatomic ESBGK models.
Section
3
introduces the semi‑Lagrangian discretisation. First, we introduce the relevant notation in Section
3.1
. Then, in Section
3.2
, we derive the first-order scheme. The implicit time integration of the polyatomic ESBGK collision operator is complicated by its dependence on higher-order moments of the distribution function, such as the internal temperature. To address this, we express these moments in terms of known quantities from previous time steps, which yields nonlinear equations for the two temperatures as well as an explicit expression for the velocity covariance matrix. The extension of the scheme to higher-orders, and a model order reduction technique known as a Chu-reduction are then subsequently discussed in Sections
3.3
and
3.5
. In Section
3.4
, we present the diffuse-reflective, and inflow-outflow boundary conditions that can be used for all deterministic methods for BGK-type equations. We then prove in Section
4
, as a generalization of the monoatomic case, that the first-order semi-Lagrangian scheme asymptotically converges to a consistent scheme for the Navier-Stokes equations. Section
5
provides the Fourier and Couette flow test cases, as well as the orifice simulations with moving boundaries. Finally, in section
6
, the conclusion is presented.

2
Mathematical Background

This section outlines the kinetic models that form the basis of the polyatomic ESBGK formulation used in this study.

2.1
The Boltzmann Equation

The foundational kinetic model for rarefied gases is the Boltzmann equation
[
12
]
,

\displaystyle\partialderivative{f}{t}+v\cdot\nabla_{x}f=\mathcal{Q}(f),

(1)

which governs the evolution of the distribution
f(t,x,v)
of monoatomic particles with velocity
v\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, through time
t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
and space
x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
. The Boltzmann collision operator
\mathcal{Q}(f)
is bilinear and contains a five-fold integral. The macroscopic quantities such as the density
\rho(t,x)
, bulk velocity
U(t,x)
and total energy
E(t,x)
are then obtained by taking moments of the particle distribution,

\displaystyle\left(\rho(t,x),\rho(t,x)U(t,x),E(t,x)\right)^{\top}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\phi(v)f(t,x,v)dv\hskip 9.24994pt\text{with}\,\phi(v)=\left(1,v,\frac{1}{2}\absolutevalue*{v}^{2}\right)^{\top}.

(2)

The temperature of the gas is related to the total energy in the following way

\displaystyle E(t,x)=\frac{1}{2}\rho(t,x)\absolutevalue{U(t, x)}^{2}+\frac{3}{2}R_{s}T(t,x),

(3)

where
R_{s}
is the specific gas constant. The elements of
\phi(v)
(
2
), are referred to as collision invariants, because they are conserved by the Boltzmann collision operator in the sense that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\phi(v)\mathcal{Q}(f)dv=0.

(4)

Hydrodynamic equations, including the Euler and Navier–Stokes formulations, can be systematically obtained from the Boltzmann equation by applying the Chapman–Enskog expansion
[
33
]
. This procedure involves expanding the distribution function in the Knudsen number,
f=f^{0}+\mathrm{Kn}f^{1}+\mathrm{Kn}^{2}f^{2}+\cdots
. At the zero-th order, the Euler equations are obtained. The first-order approximation gives the Navier-Stokes equations

\displaystyle\partialderivative{\rho}{t}+\Div_{x}\left(\rho U\right)=0,

(5)

\displaystyle\partialderivative{\rho U}{t}+\Div_{x}\left(\rho U\otimes U+pI_{3}\right)=\mathrm{Kn}\Div_{x}\left[\mu\sigma(u)\right],

\displaystyle\partialderivative{E}{t}+\Div_{x}\left((E+p)U\right)=\mathrm{Kn}\Div_{x}\left(\mu\sigma(U)U+\kappa\nabla_{x}T\right),

with the pressure
p=\rho R_{s}T
, the viscosity
\mu=\mu(T)
, the heat conduction
\kappa
, and the stress tensor
\sigma(U)
. An important quantity, the Prandtl number,

\displaystyle\Pr=\frac{5}{2}\frac{R_{s}\mu}{\kappa},

(6)

quantifies the main mode of diffusion. For
\Pr\ll 1
, thermal diffusion dominates, whereas for
\Pr\gg 1
, momentum diffusion dominates. Monoatomic gases typically have a Prandtl number of
\frac{2}{3}
. An important requirement for kinetic models is their ability to reproduce this physically correct Prandtl number.
A final property of the Boltzmann equation, is the so-called H-theorem. For space-homogeneous functions, we define the negative entropy

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f\log fdv.

(7)

It can be shown that solutions to the space-homogeneous Boltzmann equation satisfy the so-called H-theorem

\displaystyle\derivative{\mathcal{H}}{t}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\mathcal{Q}(f)\log fdv\leq 0.

(8)

Since the quantity
f\log f
is bounded from below, and
\mathcal{H}(t,x)
must decrease, the Boltzmann equation describes the evolution towards a state of minimum
\mathcal{H}
, known as thermodynamic equilibrium. At thermodynamic equilibrium, the distribution
f(t,x,v)
is the Maxwellian distribution

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(t,x,v)=\frac{\rho(t,x)}{\left(2\pi R_{s}T(t,x)\right)^{3/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{\absolutevalue{v - U(t,x)}^{2}}{2R_{s}T(t,x)}\right).

(9)

2.2
The Classical BGK Model

The observation that the distribution
f
tends to a Maxwellian distribution is the main motivation for BGK-type models, where the Boltzmann collision operator is replaced by a relaxation operator

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{\text{BGK}}(f)=\frac{1}{\tau}\left(\mathcal{M}(t,x,v)-f(t,x,v)\right),

(10)

with
\tau=\mu(T)/p
the mean free time. Due to the definition of the density, mean velocity (
2
) and temperature (
3
), the BGK equation is highly nonlinear. Nevertheless, due of the specific structure of its relaxation operator, the model admits to efficient numerical schemes and retains many essential properties of the Boltzmann equation. Analogous to the Boltzmann equation (
1
), the BGK model conserves mass, momentum and energy. Furthermore, a H-theorem for the BGK equation can be proven,

\displaystyle\derivative{\mathcal{H}}{t}

\displaystyle=\derivative{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\mathcal{Q}_{\text{BGK}}(f)\left(\log f+\log\mathcal{M}-\log\mathcal{M}\right)dv=-\derivative{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\mathcal{Q}_{\text{BGK}}(f)\left(\log\mathcal{M}-\log f\right)dv+0\leq 0,

(11)

by adding a zero under the integral and using the conservation of mass property. The main deficit of the BGK model is that it fails to reproduce the correct Prandtl number. Indeed, a Chapman-Enskog expansion of the BGK model to first-order yields a Prandtl number of 1.

2.3
The ESBGK Model for Monoatomic Gases

To treat the issue of the BGK model related to the Prandtl number, several solutions exist. In
[
25
]
, a velocity-dependent collision frequency is considered. Alternatively, a correct Prandtl number can also be obtained by correcting thermodynamic equilibrium in (
10
) with a term proportional to the heat flux. This model is known as the Shakhov model
[
31
]
. However, in this text, we consider the generalized BGK model that was introduced in
[
22
,
10
]
, known as the Ellipsoidal Statistical or Gaussian BGK model (ESBGK). This collision model, which is still a relaxation, replaces the local Maxwellian in the BGK model (
10
) with an anisotropic Gaussian,

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(t,x,v)=\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{\det(2\pi\Ttensor)}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left(v-U(t,x)\right)^{\top}\mathcal{T}^{-1}\left(v-U(t,x)\right)\right),

(12)

with covariance matrix
\mathcal{T}
and stress tensor
\Theta

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}

\displaystyle=(1-\nu_{\text{ES}})R_{s}TI_{3}+\nu_{\text{ES}}\Theta,

(13)

\displaystyle\rho(t,x)\Theta

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(v-U(t,x))\otimes(v-U(t,x))f(t,x,v)dv,

(14)

and free parameter
-0.5\leq\nu_{\text{ES}}<1
. The collision operator now becomes

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{\text{ESBGK}}(f)=\frac{1}{\tau(1-\nu_{\text{ES}})}\left(\mathcal{G}(t,x,v)-f(t,x,v)\right).

(15)

Note that for
\nu_{\text{ES}}=0
, the ESBGK model (
15
) reduces to the BGK model (
10
). The ESBGK model preserves the same collision invariants as the previous models, and the H-theorem was established in
[
3
]
. Moreover, a first-order Chapman-Enskog expansion yields a Prandtl number of
\Pr=\frac{1}{1-\nu_{\text{ES}}}
, which for
\nu_{\text{ES}}=-0.5
, yields the physically-correct value of
\Pr=2/3
. Extensions of both the BGK and ESBGK model to gas mixtures have been developed, see
[
11
,
35
]
and references therein. All models discussed so far are in principle only valid for monoatomic gases.

2.4
Polyatomic ESBGK Model

Since monoatomic gases constitute only a narrow subset of those encountered in practical applications, more general formulations applicable to polyatomic gases are required. In this work, we therefore consider the polyatomic extension of the ESBGK model from
[
2
]
. In the polyatomic case, the distribution function
f(t,x,v,I)
is extended to include the internal energy distribution
I^{2/\delta}
of gas particles, where
\delta
is the number of additional degrees of freedom. For example, for a diatomic gas,
\delta=2
. The collision invariants and associated macroscopic quantities (
2
) are now defined as

\displaystyle\rho(t,x)

\displaystyle=\left<\left<f(t,x,v,I)\right>\right>

(16)

\displaystyle\rho(t,x)U(t,x)

\displaystyle=\left<\left<vf(t,x,v,I)\right>\right>

(17)

\displaystyle E(t,x)

\displaystyle=\left<\left<\left(\frac{1}{2}\absolutevalue{v}^{2}+I^{2/\delta}\right)f(t,x,v,I)\right>\right>=\frac{1}{2}\rho(t,x)\absolutevalue{U(t, x)}^{2}+\rho(t,x)e(t,x),

(18)

under the new notation

\displaystyle\left<\left<\bullet\right>\right>=\int_{v\in\mathbb{R}^{3}}\int_{I\in\mathbb{R}^{+}}\bullet\;dvdI.

(19)

The specific internal energy
e(t,x)
and associated equilibrium temperature
T_{eq}
consist of two parts, a translational energy mode, and a internal (rotational) energy mode

\displaystyle e(t,x)

\displaystyle=e_{tr}(t,x)+e_{int}(t,x)

\displaystyle=\frac{3+\delta}{2}\,R_{s}\,T_{eq}(t,x),

(20)

\displaystyle e_{tr}(t,x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\rho(t,x)}\Big\langle\Big\langle\tfrac{1}{2}\lvert v-U(t,x)\rvert^{2}f(t,x,v,I)\Big\rangle\Big\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{3}{2}\,R_{s}\,T_{tr}(t,x),

\displaystyle e_{int}(t,x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\rho(t,x)}\Big\langle\Big\langle I^{2/\delta}f(t,x,v,I)\Big\rangle\Big\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta}{2}\,R_{s}\,T_{int}(t,x),

where the translational temperature
T_{tr}
is used to define the pressure
p=\rho R_{s}T_{tr}
. The polyatomic model introduces two relaxation parameters,
-0.5\leq\nu_{\text{pES}}<1
, and
0\leq\theta\leq 1
, which relate to the relaxation temperature
T_{rel}

\displaystyle T_{rel}(t,x)=\theta T_{eq}(t,x)+(1-\theta)T_{int}(t,x),

(21)

and the covariance matrix
\mathcal{T}

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}=(1-\theta)\left[(1-\nu)R_{s}T_{tr}(t,x)I_{3}+\nu\Theta\right]+\theta R_{s}T_{eq}(t,x)I_{3}.

(22)

Using these definitions, we can define the polyatomic Maxwellian
\mathcal{M}
and Gaussian
\mathcal{G}

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(t,x,v,I)

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho(t,x)\Lambda_{\delta}}{\left(2\pi R_{s}T_{eq}(t,x)\right)^{3/2}(R_{s}T_{eq})^{\delta/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{\absolutevalue{v - U(t,x)}^{2}}{2R_{s}T_{eq}(t,x)}-\frac{I^{2/\delta}}{R_{s}T_{eq}(t,x)}\right),

(23)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(t,x,v,I)

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho(t,x)\Lambda_{\delta}}{\sqrt{\det(2\pi\Ttensor)}(R_{s}T_{rel}(t,x))^{\delta/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left(v-U(t,x)\right)^{\top}\mathcal{T}^{-1}\left(v-U(t,x)\right)-\frac{I^{2/\delta}}{R_{s}T_{rel}(t,x)}\right).

(24)

The constant
\Lambda_{\delta}=\Gamma(\frac{\delta}{2}+1)
serves the purpose of normalising the term in the exponential related to the internal energy. Finally, we can define the ESBGK model for polyatomic gases

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{\text{pESBGK}}(f)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left(\mathcal{G}(t,x,v,I)-f(t,x,v,I)\right),

(25)

with
\varepsilon=\mu\left(1-\nu_{\text{pES}}+\theta\nu_{\text{pES}}\right)/p
. When the deriving the scheme, we allow the viscosity function to depend on the translational temperature
\mu=\mu(T_{tr})
as in
[
1
]
. Note that for
\delta=0
and
\theta=0
, we have
T_{eq}=T_{tr}
, and the polyatomic ESBGK model reduces to the monoatomic ESBGK model.

The polyatomic ESBGK model conserves mass, momentum and energy, and admits to a H-theorem that was proven in
[
2
]
. As before, for vanishing Knudsen numbers, the model reduces to the Navier-Stokes-Fourier equations with correct Prandtl number. Moreover, the additional free parameter in the polyatomic model enables matching a further transport coefficient, known as the second viscosity.

The semi-Lagrangian method and its properties

In this Section, we derive the semi-Lagrangian method for polyatomic ESBGK model. First, we introduce the notation. Then, in Section
3.2
, we derive the first-order method. In Section
3.3
, we extend the scheme to second-order in time and space using implicit linear multistep methods. In Section
3.4
, we discuss how diffuse-reflective and pressure driven inflow and outflow boundary conditions are applied. Finally, in Section
3.5
, we give a reduced order model of the polyatomic ESBGK equation for two-dimensional simulations in space.

3.1
Notation

For simplicity, we consider a one-dimensional spatial domain
[x_{L},x_{R}]
, with uniform grid spacing
\Delta x=\frac{x_{R}-x_{L}}{N_{x}}
such that

\displaystyle x_{i}=x_{L}+\left(i+\frac{1}{2}\right)\Delta x,\hskip 9.24994pti=0\ldots N_{x}-1.

(26)

The velocity grid
v\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
is first truncated to
[-v_{\text{vmax}},v_{\text{vmax}}]
and subsequently discretised with mesh spacing
\Delta v=\frac{2v_{\text{vmax}}}{N_{v}}
in each direction:

\displaystyle v_{j}=-v_{\text{vmax}}\left(1,1,1\right)^{\top}+\left(j_{1}\Delta v,j_{2}\Delta v,j_{3}\Delta v\right)^{\top},\hskip 9.24994pt0\leq j_{1},j_{2},j_{3}\leq N_{v}.

(27)

For more involved velocity discretisations, see
[
9
,
7
]
. Finally, we consider a fixed time step
\Delta t
, such that

\displaystyle t^{n}=n\Delta t,\hskip 9.24994ptn=0\ldots N_{t},\hskip 9.24994ptN_{t}\Delta t=t_{f}.

(28)

We keep the internal energy variable
I\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
continuous, but we omit it from the following sections, as it can be completely eliminated through a so-called Chu reduction
[
16
,
2
]
. Further details are provided in Section
3.5
. The numerical solution of
f
at
(t^{n},x_{i},v_{j})
is denoted by
f_{i,j}^{n}
. Macroscopic quantities are approximated by discretising integrals over velocity space with the composite midpoint rule, e.g.,

\displaystyle\left(\rho_{i}^{n},\rho_{i}^{n}U_{i}^{n},E_{i}^{n}\right)^{\top}=\left<\sum_{j}\left((1,v_{j},\frac{1}{2}\absolutevalue{v}^{2})+I^{2/\delta}\right)^{\top}f_{i,j}^{n}\Delta v^{3}\right>_{I},

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt\rho_{i}^{n}\Theta_{i}^{n}=\sum_{j}(v_{j}-U_{i}^{n})\otimes(v_{j}-U_{i}^{n})\left<f_{i,j}^{n}\right>_{I}\Delta v^{3}

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{i}^{n}=(1-\theta)\left[(1-\nu)R_{s}T_{tr,i}^{n}I_{3}+\nu\Theta_{i}^{n}\right]+\theta R_{s}T_{eq,i}^{n}I_{3},

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt\Sigma_{i}^{n}=\sum_{j}v_{j}\otimes v_{j}\left<f_{i,j}^{n}\right>_{I}\Delta v^{3}

(30)

\displaystyle T_{tr,i}^{n}=\frac{2}{3\rho_{i}^{n}R_{s}}\sum_{j}\frac{1}{2}\absolutevalue{v_j - U_i^n}^{2}\left<f^{n}_{i,j}\right>_{I}\Delta v^{3},

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994ptT_{int,i}^{n}\frac{2}{\delta\rho_{i}^{n}R_{s}}\sum_{j}\left<I^{2/\delta}f^{n}_{i,j}\right>_{I}\Delta v^{3}

\displaystyle T_{eq,i}^{n}=\frac{1}{3+\delta}\left(3T_{tr,i}^{n}+\delta T_{int,i}^{n}\right),

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994ptT_{rel,i}^{n}=\theta T_{eq}^{n}+(1-\theta)T_{int,i}^{n}.

This procedure yields spectral accuracy in velocity, as long as the velocity

is taken sufficiently large such that distribution

3.3
Extension to higher orders

We extend the first-order scheme from Section
3.2
to second-order in time using a BDF2 method

\displaystyle f^{n+1}_{i,j}-\frac{4}{3}f_{i,j}^{n,1}+\frac{1}{3}f_{i,j}^{n,2}=\frac{2}{3}\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{n+1}_{i}}\left(G_{i,j}^{n+1}-f^{n+1}_{i,j}\right),

(48)

where
f_{i,j}^{n,k}
is an approximation for
f(t^{n},x_{i}-kv_{j}\Delta t,v_{j},I))
. The quantities
f_{i,j}^{n,k}
are interpolated from the grid at the previous time steps using a third-order bicubic reconstruction routine. For more involved reconstruction procedures, see
[
20
,
14
,
15
]
. The scheme (
48
) can be written in the following way

\displaystyle f^{n+1}_{i,j}=\tilde{f}^{n}_{i,j}+\frac{2}{3}\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{n+1}_{i}}\left(G_{i,j}^{n+1}-f^{n+1}_{i,j}\right),\text{with }\tilde{f}^{n}_{i,j}=\frac{4}{3}f_{i,j}^{n,1}-\frac{1}{3}f_{i,j}^{n,2}.

(49)

Using the procedure outlined in Section
3.2
, the quantity
G_{i,j}^{n+1}
can again be computed explicitly, this time from the linear combination of moments of
f_{i,j}^{n,1}
and
f_{i,j}^{n,2}
through
\tilde{f}^{n}_{i,j}
. Furthermore, because BDF methods are asymptotic‑preserving (AP) and stiffly accurate (SA), they recover, in the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
, a scheme of the same order for the Euler equations.

Remark 1
.

We note that the central bicubic reconstruction proposed here will yield oscillations at shocks in the
\varepsilon\to 0
limit. Since our investigation is restricted to the slip and transition regime, where such features do not develop, central higher-order reconstructions are safe and efficient.

At a point boundary point, the characteristic feet for which
v_{j}\cdot\hat{n}\leq 0
, with
\hat{n}
the inward-pointing normal, lie inside the gas domain. The solution at these points can be reconstructed in the usual way. The characteristics satisfying
v_{j}\cdot\hat{n}\geq 0
fall outside the gas domain, see Figure
2
, and hence require a boundary condition. After applying the boundary condition, the full distribution
\tilde{f}^{n}_{i}
is known and the collision step can be performed, e.g. for the first order scheme see Equation (
47
).

In this text, we consider three types of boundary conditions: specular reflection, diffuse-reflection and subsonic inflow/outflow. In the case of specular reflection, the distribution function
\tilde{f}^{n}_{i,j}
is mapped onto
\tilde{f}^{n}_{i,k}
, where
v_{k}
is the velocity with the same tangential velocity and opposite normal velocity

\displaystyle v_{k}=v_{j}-2\hat{n}\left(v_{j}\cdot\hat{n}\right).

(50)

We note that at a specular boundary, the internal energy remains unchanged. In the case of diffuse-reflective boundary conditions, the distribution of particles striking the wall is reflected back inside the domain as a Maxwellian with wall velocity
U_{w}
and wall temperature
T_{w}
. The boundary condition reads

\displaystyle\tilde{f}^{n}_{i,j}=\mathcal{M}^{n}_{w,i,j}=\frac{\rho_{w}\Lambda_{\delta}}{\left(2\pi R_{s}T_{w}\right)^{3/2}(R_{s}T_{w})^{\delta/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{\absolutevalue{v_j - U_w}^{2}}{2R_{s}T_{w}}-\frac{I^{2/\delta}}{R_{s}T_{w}}\right),\hskip 9.24994ptv_{j}\cdot\hat{n}\geq 0.

(51)

The density of the gas that is reflected is such that the flux at the boundary is zero, or in other words, mass is conserved across the boundary,

\displaystyle\left<\sum_{j,(v_{j}-U_{w})\cdot\hat{n}>0}\mathcal{M}^{n}_{w,i,j}\,(v_{j}-U_{w})\cdot\hat{n}\right>_{I}+\left<\sum_{j,(v_{j}-U_{w})\cdot\hat{n}\leq 0}\tilde{f}^{n}_{i,j}\,(v_{j}-U_{w})\cdot\hat{n}\right>_{I}=0.

(52)

Equation (
52
) can be solved for the density
\rho_{w}
after reconstructing the distribution at the characteristic feet for all velocities that satisfy
v_{j}\cdot\hat{n}\leq 0
. Then, the distribution for the outgoing characteristics is obtained from Equation (
51
).

Finally, we consider the subsonic inflow and outflow boundary condition. We assume the distribution of the gas flowing in/out of the domain is in thermodynamic equilibrium. In other words, we assume the distribution
\tilde{f}^{n}_{i,j},v_{j}\cdot\hat{n}\geq 0
is a Maxwellian with some density
\rho_{f}
, velocity
U_{f}
and temperature
T_{f}
. The quantities
\rho_{f}
,
U_{f}
and
T_{f}
are then determined from boundary conditions for the Euler equations. First, the density, mean velocity, and temperature are extrapolated from inside the domain to the boundary. We denote these values with subscript
e
. Then, we define the extrapolated speed of sound and extrapolated pressure

\displaystyle p_{e}=\rho_{e}R_{s}T_{e}

(53)

\displaystyle c_{e}=\sqrt{\frac{\delta+5}{\delta+3}T_{e}R_{s}}.

(54)

Then, for the subsonic inflow boundary condition, given the inflow pressure
p_{in}
and inflow temperature
T_{in}
, the inflow quantities are defined as

\displaystyle\rho_{f}=\frac{p_{in}}{T_{in}R_{s}},\hskip 9.24994ptU_{f}=U_{e}+\frac{p_{in}-p_{e}}{\rho_{e}c_{e}}\hat{n},\hskip 9.24994ptT_{f}=\frac{p_{in}}{R_{s}\rho_{f}}.

(55)

In the case of subsonic outflow boundary conditions, we prescribe the outflow pressure
p_{o}
, and set the outflow quantities to

\displaystyle\rho_{f}=\rho_{e}+\frac{p_{o}-p_{e}}{c_{e}c_{e}},\hskip 9.24994ptU_{f}=U_{e}+\frac{p_{o}-p_{e}}{\rho_{e}c_{e}}\hat{n},\hskip 9.24994ptT_{f}=\frac{p_{in}}{R_{s}\rho_{f}}.

(56)

For a derivation of boundary conditions for the Euler equations, see
[
37
]
. We note that inflow/outflow boundary conditions based on the Euler equations are also used in DSMC simulations
[
36
,
27
]
.

Remark 2
.

It may happen that characteristics from points that do not lie on the boundary fall outside the gas domain. For simplicity, we limit the time step so that this does not occur. However, through the use of ghost points and additional interpolation, the procedure above can be generalised
[
21
]
.

Remark 3
.

At a boundary, in the case of the higher-order method, it is not possible to use a central third-order reconstruction. Instead, we resort to one-sided stencils. The use of different stencils breaks the translation invariance property of the scheme and may introduce conservation errors, especially in the
\varepsilon\to 0
limit
[
14
]
. Since our investigation is restricted to slip and transition flows, this is not an issue.

3.5
Chu-reduction

In the numerical examples in Section
5
we will restrict ourselves two-dimensional simulations in space. Consider the velocity component orthogonal to the spatial domain
v_{3}
, with
v=\left(v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}\right)^{\top}
, and consider the internal energy variable
I
. Although the simulations should include effects due to motion in three dimensions and internal energy modes, the full distribution in
v_{3}
or
I
is irrelevant. Ultimately, some specific moments, such as the mean velocity
U
or the internal temperature
T_{int}
are the quantities of interest. In addition, it is expensive to discretise the dimensions
v_{3}
and
I
. It turns out that for BGK-type models it is not necessary to consider the distribution
f(t,x,v,I)
’s dependence on
v_{3}
and
I
. Instead, the same result can be achieved using reduced distributions. This procedure is known as a Chu-reduction
[
16
,
2
]
. We define three reduced distribution functions

\displaystyle f_{1}(t,x,\underline{v})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}\crossproduct\mathbb{R}^{+}}f(t,x,v,I)dv_{3}dI,\hskip 9.24994ptf_{2}(t,x,\underline{v})=\int_{\mathbb{R}\crossproduct\mathbb{R}^{+}}v_{3}^{2}f(t,x,v,I)dv_{3}dI,

(57)

\displaystyle f_{3}(t,x,\underline{v})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}\crossproduct\mathbb{R}^{+}}I^{\delta/2}f(t,x,v,I)dv_{3}dI,

(58)

with
\underline{v}=\left(v_{1},v_{2}\right)^{\top}
. We assume the mean velocity in the direction orthogonal to the two-dimensional space
x
is zero. In addition, we assume the distribution function
f(t,x,v,I)
is symmetric in
v_{3}
. The conserved quantities defined in Section
2.4
now become

\displaystyle\rho(t,x)=\left<f_{1}\right>,\hskip 9.24994pt\rho(t,x)\underline{U}(t,x)=\left<f_{1}\right>,\hskip 9.24994ptE(t,x)=\left<\frac{1}{2}\absolutevalue{\vbar}^{2}f_{1}\right>+\left<\frac{1}{2}f_{2}\right>+\left<f_{3}\right>.

(59)

The two internal energy components become

\displaystyle e_{tr}(t,x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\rho(t,x)}\left<\tfrac{1}{2}\absolutevalue{\vbar- \underline{U}(t,x)}^{2}f_{1}\right>+\left<\frac{1}{2}f_{2}\right>=\frac{3}{2}\,R_{s}\,T_{tr}(t,x),

(60)

\displaystyle e_{int}(t,x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\rho(t,x)}\left<f_{3}\right>=\frac{\delta}{2}\,R_{s}\,T_{int}(t,x).

(61)

Finally, we define the reduced stress tensor
\underline{\Theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\crossproduct 2}
and reduced covariance matrix
\underline{\mathcal{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\crossproduct 2}
as

\displaystyle\rho(t,x)\Theta=\begin{pmatrix}\rho(t,x)\underline{\Theta}&0\\
0&\left<f_{2}\right>\end{pmatrix}\text{ with }\,\rho(t,x)\underline{\Theta}=\left<(\underline{v}-\underline{U}(t,x))\otimes(\underline{v}-\underline{U}(t,x))f_{1}\right>

(62)

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}=(1-\theta)\left[(1-\nu)R_{s}T_{tr}(t,x)I_{3}+\nu\Theta\right]+\theta R_{s}T_{eq}(t,x)I_{3}=\begin{pmatrix}\underline{\mathcal{T}}&0\\
0&\mathcal{T}_{33}\end{pmatrix}.

(63)

The reduced distribution functions
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
contain all mass and momentum, and the distribution function
f_{3}
only contributes to the internal energy. By multiplying the polyatomic ESBGK equation with
1
,
v_{3}^{2}
and
I^{2/\delta}
, and integrating over
v_{3}
and
I
, we obtain three BGK-type equations for the reduced distribution functions

\displaystyle\partialderivative{f_1}{t}+v\cdot\nabla_{x}f_{1}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}(G_{1}-f_{1}),\hskip 9.24994pt\partialderivative{f_2}{t}+v\cdot\nabla_{x}f_{2}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}(\mathcal{T}_{33}G_{1}-f_{2}),\hskip 9.24994pt\partialderivative{f_3}{t}+v\cdot\nabla_{x}f_{3}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}(\frac{\delta}{2}RT_{rel}G_{1}-f_{3}).

(64)

The attractor in Equation (
64
) is defined by

\displaystyle G_{1}=\frac{\rho(t,x)\sqrt{\mathcal{T}_{33}}}{2\pi\sqrt{\det(\Ttensor_i)}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left(\underline{v}-\underline{U}(t,x)\right)^{\top}\underline{\mathcal{T}}^{-1}\left(\underline{v}-\underline{U}(t,x)\right)\right).

(65)

Thus, we have reduced the dimension of the equation from eight to five, thereby significantly speeding up simulations.

4
Asymptotic preserving property and Navier-Stokes limit

In this section, we study several properties of the first-order scheme (
35
). The properties below are generalisations of the ESBGK model
[
18
,
6
]
.

Proposition 1
.

For
\tau>0
and
\Delta t>0
, the solution of (
35
) satisfies

\displaystyle 0\leq f_{i,j}^{n+1}\leq\max_{i,j}\left\{\norm{f^n_{i, j}},\norm{\mathcal{G}^{n+1}_{i, j}}\right\}.

(66)

Proof
The solution
f_{i,j}^{n+1}
is a convex combination of
\mathcal{G}^{n}_{i,j}
and
\tilde{f}_{i,j}^{n}
. The value
\tilde{f}_{i,j}^{n}
is the result of linear interpolation of values
f^{n}_{:,j}
. This implies the desired result.

The following proposition concerns the asymptotic‑preserving (AP) property of the scheme. A numerical scheme is said to be AP if, in the limit as the Knudsen number tends to zero, it naturally reduces to a consistent discretisation of the Euler equations. Before this limit is reached, the underlying kinetic equation converges asymptotically to the Navier-Stokes equations with well‑defined transport coefficients. The scheme under consideration can be shown to possess this property as well.

Proposition 2
.

Let
f^{n}=f(t^{n},x,v,I)
be a time-discretised solution of the polyatomic ESBGK equation. Then, assuming that the following quantities are bounded by positive constants
C_{1},C_{2},C_{3}
and
C_{4}
independent of
n
,

\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq\alpha\leq 2}\sup_{x,v,I}\left|\partial_{x}^{\alpha}f^{n}(x,v,I)\left(1+|v|^{2}\right)^{4}\right|<C_{1},\hskip 9.24994pt\sum_{0\leq\alpha\leq 2}\sup_{x,v,I}\left|\partial_{x}^{2}f^{n}(x,v,I)|v|^{2}I^{2/\delta}\right|<C_{2}

(67)

\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq\alpha\leq 2}\sup_{x,v,I}\left|\partial_{x}^{\alpha}\mathcal{M}^{\alpha}(x,v,I)\left(1+|v|^{2}\right)^{4}\right|<C_{3},\hskip 9.24994pt\sum_{0\leq\alpha\leq 2}\sup_{x,v,I}\left|\partial_{x}^{2}\mathcal{M}^{\alpha}(x,v,I)|v|^{2}I^{2/\delta}\right|<C_{4},

(68)

the first-order semi-Lagrangian scheme (
35
) asymptotically becomes a consistent approximation of the Navier-Stokes system (
5
).

Proof
We consider the first order scheme (
35
), and leave the space, velocity and internal energy variable continuous. We then take the moments with respect to the polyatomic collision invariants
\phi(v,I)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\crossproduct\mathbb{R}^{+}}\phi(v,I)f^{n+1}dvdI

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\crossproduct\mathbb{R}^{+}}\phi(v,I)\tilde{f}^{n}dvdI,

(69)

with
\tilde{f}^{n}=f(x-v\Delta t,v,I)
. Using a Taylor expansion, we expand the right hand side of Equation (
69
),

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\crossproduct\mathbb{R}^{+}}\phi(v,I)f^{n+1}dvdI

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\crossproduct\mathbb{R}^{+}}\phi(v,I)\left[f^{n}(x,v,I)-\Delta tv\cdot\nabla_{x}f^{n}(x,v,I)\right]dvdI+R,

(70)

where
R
is the remainder term in integral form. Using similar techniques from
[
6
]
, it is possible to bound the remainder term

\displaystyle\norm{R}_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3\pi\Delta t^{2}}{8}\sup_{x,v,I}\left|\partial_{x}^{2}f^{n}(x-v\Delta t,v,I)\left(1+|v|^{2}\right)^{4}\right|+\frac{\Delta t^{2}}{2}\sup_{x,v,I}\left|\partial_{x}^{2}f^{n}(x,v,I)|v|^{2}I^{2/\delta}\right|

(71)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(\Delta t^{2}\right).

(72)

We now treat the relaxation time
\varepsilon
as independent of
f^{n}(x,v,I)
and formally expand both the distribution function and the Gaussian in powers of
\varepsilon
,

\displaystyle f^{n}=\mathcal{M}^{n}+\varepsilon f^{n}_{1}+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2}),\hskip 9.24994pt\mathcal{G}^{n}=\mathcal{M}^{n}+\varepsilon\mathcal{G}^{n}_{1}+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2}).

(73)

By plugging these expansions in the definition of a collision invariant (
4
), we obtain the so-called compatibility conditions

\displaystyle\left<\left<f_{1}^{n}\left(1,v,\frac{1}{2}\absolutevalue{v}^{2}+I^{2/\delta}\right)^{\top}\right>\right>=\left<\left<\mathcal{G}_{1}^{n}\left(1,v,\frac{1}{2}\absolutevalue{v}^{2}+I^{2/\delta}\right)^{\top}\right>\right>=0.

(74)

Additionally, we expand the translational temperature, the internal temperature and the stress tensor like such

\displaystyle T_{tr}^{n}

\displaystyle=T_{eq}^{n}+\varepsilon T_{tr}^{\varepsilon,n}+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2}),

(75)

\displaystyle T_{int}^{n}

\displaystyle=T_{eq}^{n}+\varepsilon T_{int}^{\varepsilon,n}+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2}),

(76)

\displaystyle\Theta^{n}

\displaystyle=R_{s}\left(T_{eq}^{n}+\varepsilon T_{tr}^{\varepsilon,n}\right)I_{3}+\varepsilon\Theta^{n}_{1}+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2}).

(77)

A necessary condition to satisfy the compatibility conditions (
74
) is that
\tr\Theta^{n}_{1}=0
. Due to the definition of the internal energies and the stress tensor, we find that

\displaystyle\frac{3}{2}\rho^{n}R_{s}T_{tr}^{\varepsilon,n}

\displaystyle=\left<\left<\frac{|v-U|^{2}}{2}f_{1}\right>\right>,

(78)

\displaystyle\frac{\delta}{2}\rho^{n}R_{s}T_{int}^{\varepsilon,n}

\displaystyle=\left<\left<I^{2/\delta}f_{1}\right>\right>,

(79)

\displaystyle\rho^{n}R_{s}T_{tr}^{\varepsilon,n}+\rho^{n}\Theta^{n}_{1}

\displaystyle=\left<\left<(v-U^{n})\otimes(v-U^{n})f_{1}\right>\right>.

(80)

By substituting a zero-th order expansion
f^{n}=\mathcal{M}^{n}
into Equation (
70
), we obtain a consistent scheme for the Euler equations

\displaystyle\frac{\rho^{n+1}-\rho^{n}}{\Delta t}+\nabla_{x}\cdot(\rho^{n}U^{n})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(\Delta t)+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon),

(81)

\displaystyle\frac{\rho^{n+1}U^{n+1}-\rho^{n}U^{n}}{\Delta t}+\Delta t\nabla_{x}\cdot\left(\rho^{n}U^{n}\otimes U^{n}+pI_{3}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(\Delta t)+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon),

\displaystyle\frac{E^{n+1}-E^{n}}{\Delta t}+\nabla_{x}\cdot\left(U^{n}E^{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(\Delta t)+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon).

Inserting the first‑order expansion of the distribution function
f^{n}=\mathcal{M}^{n}+\varepsilon f^{n}_{1}
into Equation (
70

\displaystyle\frac{\rho^{n+1}-\rho^{n}}{\Delta t}+\nabla_{x}\cdot(\rho^{n}U^{n})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(\Delta t)+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2}),

6
Conclusion

In this work, we developed a semi-Lagrangian numerical scheme for the polyatomic Ellipsoidal Statistical BGK (ESBGK) model of the Boltzmann equation. By combining a characteristic-based treatment of the transport term with an implicit A-stable linear multistep discretization of the relaxation operator, the proposed method eliminates the restrictive time-step constraint typically associated with kinetic transport while efficiently handling the stiffness of the collision term. Owing to the structure of the BGK operator, the implicit formulation can be reformulated into a computationally inexpensive update, resulting in a numerically stable and efficient scheme.

The method is asymptotic preserving and stiffly accurate, ensuring that in the vanishing Knudsen number limit the scheme naturally transitions to a consistent discretization of the Euler equations. Moreover, we established that the first-order version of the scheme asymptotically converges to the compressible Navier–Stokes equations with the correct transport coefficients. Suitable inflow and outflow boundary conditions for BGK-type kinetic models were also introduced, enabling the treatment of open-domain problems.

Numerical experiments, including Fourier and Couette flow tests, demonstrated good agreement between the ESBGK model and Direct Simulation Monte Carlo (DSMC) results, confirming the physical fidelity of the approach. Finally, the method was successfully applied to a challenging orifice flow with moving boundaries, illustrating its robustness and flexibility for complex rarefied gas dynamics problems. These results indicate that the proposed scheme provides an effective deterministic framework for simulating polyatomic kinetic models across a wide range of flow regimes.
