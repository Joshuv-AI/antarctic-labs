Validation of chichewa version of the city birth trauma scale at postnatal clinics in Lilongwe, Malawi
Authors: Hilda Machika Kanjedza, Ditress Nyirongo, Genesis Chorwe-Sungani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPostpartum Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD) is a significant mental health concern for postpartum women. In Malawi, there were no data showing the prevalence of Postpartum Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder and no validated Chichewa tool for assessing Postpartum PTSD. City Birth Trauma Scale (CityBiTS) is a tool that is used to assess Postpartum PTSD. CityBiTS is the best choice because it was developed specifically to measure trauma related to childbirth, tailors to the unique psychological, physical and emotional experiences of postpartum women. The tool also adheres to DSM-5 Criteria and has been validated across cultures which has shown high reliability and credibility to be used in clinical practice and research.The study aimed at assessing the diagnostic accuracy of the Chichewa Version City Birth Trauma Scale at Postnatal clinics in Lilongwe District, Malawi. The study’s objectives were; to determine the prevalence of postpartum PTSD and establish the psychometric properties of Chichewa Version of the City Birth Trauma Scale in Malawi.MethodsIt was a cross-sectional study conducted at two hospital facilities and these were Bwaila Hospital and Area 25 Health Centre postnatal clinics in Lilongwe District, Malawi. English City Birth Trauma Scale was translated into Chichewa version and adapted. A total of 384 postnatal mothers were selected by systematic random sampling technique. Data was analysed by SPSS version 23 and Sensitivity Analysis was done to examine the psychometric properties.ResultsThe prevalence of Postpartum PTSD was 22.6% [95% CI 18.8% – 27.1%] on Structured Clinical Interview for Diagnostic Statistical for Mental Disorders Version 5 and 10.9% [95% CI 7.9% – 14.5% on City Birth Trauma Scale. The following psychometrics were established: Cronbach’s alpha 0.780, ROC 0.872, sensitivity 81.6% [95% CI 71.8%-89.1%], specificity 78.9% [95% CI 73.8%-83.4%], Positive Predictive Value 53.0% [95% CI 47%-58.9%], Negative Predictive Value 93.6%, [95% CI 90.4%-95.8%] optimum cut-off score > 23, Youden Index 0.605, Exploratory Factor Analysis for variance 65.6%.Factor 1 which are birth-related symptoms and factor 2 which are general symptoms emerged and all items loaded except items 10 and 17 and Convergent Validity at 0.65. The prevalence rate of Postpartum PTSD and Psychometric properties were consistent with other studies affirming its utility.ConclusionThe study determined the information on prevalence of Postpartum PTSD. It further demonstrated that Chichewa Version City Birth Trauma Scale is a reliable, accurate and valid tool for screening Postpartum PTSD with good psychometric properties for clinical practice and research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12884-026-09505-3.

Introduction

Postpartum Post Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD) is a childbirth related mental health condition that can affect women. It is caused by traumatic or disappointing experience during pregnancy, delivery, or postpartum. Clinically, it is important to know the true extent of Postpartum PTSD to raise awareness and provide appropriate interventions [1]. Research suggests between 20 and 40% of women find childbirth psychologically traumatic and the prevalence of Postpartum PTSD ranges from 3%−37% globally [2–4].

Despite its relatively high prevalence, Postpartum PTSD remains largely unrecognized in maternity services. This is because of lack of a validated screening measure that aligns with the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders-5 (DSM-5) diagnostic criteria. To bridge this gap, the City Birth Trauma Scale was developed specifically to assess Postpartum PTSD according to DSM-5 criteria and to measure the prevalence of childbirth-related PTSD across culturally diverse samples [5].

City Birth Trauma Scale provides a standardized and reliable tool for healthcare professionals and researchers to accurately assess and identify Postpartum PTSD cases, promoting a more comprehensive understanding of the disorder's global prevalence and facilitating targeted interventions and support systems for postnatal women. Furthermore, CityBiTS has shown having good psychometric properties across culturally diverse samples. [6–9].

The study aimed at assessing the diagnostic accuracy of the Chichewa Version City Birth Trauma Scale among postnatal mothers. Specifically, the objectives were to determine the prevalence of Postpartum PTSD and establish the psychometric properties of the Chichewa version City Birth Trauma Scale in postnatal mothers at Postnatal clinics in Lilongwe District, Malawi.

Data collection tools

Two questionnaires were used in the study to collect data through face-to-face interviews. These were Structured Clinical Interview for Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorder (DSM 5), (SCID-5) and City Birth Trauma Scale (CityBiTS). The DSM-5. is widely accepted standard for diagnosing Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder symptoms by mental health professionals when determining PTSD diagnosis. The DSM-5 outlines the specific criteria needed to make PTSD diagnosis based on a patient’s symptoms and experiences following a traumatic event. The City Birth Trauma Scale was developed by Ayers, Wright & Thomton to assess PTSD according to DSM-5 criteria. This questionnaire has previously been published [5]. The Scale assesses Postpartum PTSD and measures the prevalence of childbirth-related PTSD across culturally diverse samples which has shown having good psychometric properties. Both tools were used in this study to evaluate criterion validity of the translated tool. (See the questionnaires in a supplementary file).

Methods

Study design

This was a cross-sectional validation study that utilized quantitative data collection at one point in time. The study used Structured Clinical Interview for Diagnostic Statistical for Mental Disorders-5 (SCID-5) and City Birth Trauma Scale (CityBiTS) questionnaires to collect data through face-to-face interviews.

Study setting

The study was conducted in Lilongwe District and the study sites were Bwaila Maternity Hospital and Area 25 Health Centre postnatal clinics in Central Region of Malawi. These are urban-based facilities. These sites were chosen based on the number of childbirth deliveries conducted and postnatal clinical considerations at these sites whereas most of the women attend postnatal clinics where they delivered their babies. Lilongwe District has a total population of 2,770,840, and the Bwaila Maternity Hospital registers the total childbirth deliveries per month as 1500 −1700. Bwaila Maternity Hospital is a referral maternity district hospital in the Lilongwe District. The catchment population for Area 25 Health Centre is 222,064. This health facility caters for people from both urban and rural areas. Monthly childbirth deliveries range from to 500–600. These two facilities are the busiest maternity facilities in Lilongwe District.

Study population

The study targeted a population of postnatal mothers who attended postnatal clinics at Bwaila Maternity Hospital and Area 25 Health Centre from birth to 12 weeks. Systematic random sampling was chosen for this study because of its simplicity, efficiency and ability to provide a representative sample from large, organized population. By selecting every nth individual after a random start, it reduces researcher bias while saving time and resources. Then, systematic random sampling technique with the interval of 3 was done using a specific sampling frame from the sampling unit. In systematic random sampling, the sampling frame was created from a list of postnatal mothers attending the clinic on a particular day, the researcher set a chosen sample size, where a random number was selected for the first participant, and then a regular interval number was used to determine the participants of the sample set.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria for this study encompassed postnatal mothers who were in the timeframe of 0–12 weeks after childbirth irrespective of gestational age. Additionally, the participants were required to be at least 16 years of age and possess the ability to understand either Chichewa or English. In the case of postnatal mothers who were below 18 years of age, it is important to note that, as per the Laws of Malawi, individuals under the age of 18 are considered minors and do not have the legal capacity to provide consent. Instead, parental consent was obtained for their participation in this study, as per the guidelines stipulated in Daniel [6]. In addition to the inclusion criteria previously mentioned, the study employed specific exclusion criteria. Postnatal mothers with obstetrical complications such as gestational diabetes or fetal anomaly were excluded from the study. This is because; these conditions are already traumatic to mothers, as this would not give accurate results related to traumatic childbirth. This was done to obtain an accurate measure of postpartum PTSD using the DSM-5 criteria [10].

Translation process for city birth trauma scale into chichewa version

Before translating, permission was sought from the author [5]. Almost 20 million in Malawi people speak Chichewa. It is the official and widely spoken language. The translation of the scale was applied to this study to ensure it retains its conceptual, semantic and psychometric equivalence (validity and reliability) in the new language and cultural context for clinical practice. The parallel back translation technique was used according to Brislin Translation Model to provide systematic, multi-step quality control process that ensures cultural and semantic equivalence between the source and target language [11]. This translation process was recommended by International Survey of Childbirth-Related Trauma (INTERSECT) Project.

One bilingual expert (university lecturer) who was conversant in English and Chichewa translated the instrument from the source language (English) to Chichewa. After that, back translation was performed by another bilingual expert (secondary school teacher), who is a linguist, without access to the original version back-translated to English. Third, repeat translation was performed to identify if any error in meaning was found in the back-translated version compared to the original version, and the terms were in question to be re-translated and again blindly back-translated by another bilingual expert (internship secondary school teacher in languages). This iterative process was repeated until no error in meaning was found.

A pilot test of a translated tool was conducted at a similar study setting and study population to examine the reliability and applicability in this study. This helped the researcher to ensure that meaning, validity and reliability of the original tool were maintained [11].

Therefore, the researcher calculated subjects to sample in a study designed to estimate both the sensitivity and the specificity of a diagnostic test, given a specified precision and estimated disease prevalence [12].

The sample size was calculated by incorporating the prevalence of the disease into the sample size and calculating the sensitivity and specificity. The calculation helped to ensure that the data yielded sensitivity and specificity estimates with good precision. Data collection

Data were collected during the operational hours of postnatal clinics from Monday to Friday. The data were collected with the help of an interviewer-assisted questionnaire administration using two questionnaires through face-to-face interviews. All questionnaires were administered in Chichewa. The Principal Investigator and research assistants (mental health clinicians and mental health nurses) collected data from the respondents. One participant was asked questions from both questionnaires by two different interviewers who were in different rooms. SCID-5 was administered by mental health clinicians while CityBiTS was administered by mental health nurses.

Data analysis

This statistical technique of descriptive statistical (mean, standard deviation, frequency, and percentages) was used to characterize the sample.

The reliability of Chichewa Version City Birth Trauma Scale was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha which was performed to measure internal consistency [13].

Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) was conducted. A principal components with varimax rotation and the following criteria were used: Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) ≥ 0.60, significant Bartlett’s test of sphericity eigenvalues above 1 to compose the factors’ percentage of the variance of approximately 60% and item’s minimum factor loading of approximately 40%.[14]. These represent how much variance a factor explains in a variance acting as a correlation coeffient. Convergent validity was used to compare the City Birth Trauma Scale with the gold standard.

This is a statistical analysis which is used to identify the factors that explain the relationships between a set of observed variables. This two-factor analysis accounted for the total variance of data for general symptoms and birth-related symptoms [14]. This analysis helps the researchers to understand the structure of measurement instruments by revealing which item group together form meaningful variables thus contributing to the assessment of its construct validity.

We conducted criterion validation of the Chichewa Version City Birth Trauma Scale using DSM-5 Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder as the gold-standard diagnosis, evaluated using the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-5 Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder module. The overall performance of the measure was evaluated by constructing a Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve and calculating the Area Under Curve (AUC). The study determined sensitivity and specificity at each cut-off score on the City Birth Trauma Scale Additionally, the positive predictive values (PPV) and negative predictive values (NPV) were computed to assess the predictive ability of the Scale.

Youden Index is a statistical measurement that summarizes the performance of a diagnostic test. It was utilized to assess the effectiveness of a diagnostic maker and to identify the best threshold value for the maker which is the optimal cutoff score for the Chichewa Version City Birth Trauma Scale that achieved the best balance between sensitivity and specificity [15]. Youden Index is calculated using this formula; Youden Index (J) = Sensitivity + Specificity −1. The Index ranges from 0 to 1 where 0 indicates the test is no better than random guessing and 1 indicates a perfect test. This Index is used to determine the overall diagnostic accuracy of a test with a higher value representing a better diagnostic performance.

Prevalence estimates were calculated using SPSS version 23 for both DSM-5 Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder determined with SCID-5, and using the optimal cut-off determined in the criterion validation of City Birth Trauma Scale. We conducted exploratory univariate analyses of associations between demographic variables and Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder.

Ethical considerations of the study

The study was conducted according to ethical guidelines and was approved by College of Medicine Research and Ethics Committee (COMREC) with Protocol Number P.02/22/3588. The institutional clearance was obtained from the Director of Health and Social Services (DHSS) for Lilongwe District Health Office before data collection to ensure participants’ protection. The study was carried out according to the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki which described on good clinical practices [16]. Participation was voluntary and each participant signed the informed consent form after reading the detailed participant Information Sheet. The participants were free not to take part in the study or withdraw and were assured that they would not be denied health care services because of their withdrawal or refusal to participate in the study. Privacy and confidentiality during interviews were maintained.

Discussion

The study findings showed that postnatal women who participated in this study were within the normal childbearing age, parity and gestational weeks. Therefore, normal child-bearing age, parity and gestational age are good maternal attributes for women to have good maternal and neonatal outcomes which reduce childbirth traumatic symptoms [17]. All the participants of this study belonged to a certain religious belief and most of them and were married. These are good sources of social and psychological support. Some studies have indicated that the presence of social and psychological support influences good mental health of a person [6, 7]. However, the study found that most of the postnatal women who had primary education which is a lower level of education in Malawi and who were not employed had childbirth trauma symptoms following childbirth leading to Postpartum PTSD. According to other studies, lack of socioeconomics has been described as being related to increased risks of psychiatric mental health conditions including PTSD and linked to lack of financial empowerment leading to lower socioeconomic status [18]. However, the results were varied and not significant (p˃0.05). While lower education and unemployment appeared more frequently among women with PTSD, the associations did not reach statistical significance (p > 0.05), suggesting that these variables may warrant further investigation in a larger sample.

Prevalence of post-traumatic stress disorder mothers

The findings of this study revealed a significant prevalence rate of Postpartum Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder with 22.6% based on Structured Clinical Interview for DSM 5 (SCID-5) PTSD diagnosis and 10.9% based on the Chichewa version of the City Birth Trauma Scale. The prevalence on SCID-5 is higher than on CityBiTS. The reason could be the SCID-5 allows clinicians to probe deeper into subjective experience which can lead to higher rates of symptoms endorsement compared to self-report questionnaires like the CityBiTS. Therefore, there is need to emphasize the importance of recognizing and addressing this condition universally among postpartum women [5].

The study finding concurred with other studies which found the prevalence of PTSD symptoms following childbirth ranges from 3%s to 30% [19–21]. This high prevalence rate of postnatal mothers with PTSD diagnosis indicates that PTSD is a common phenomenon during postpartum period and it might get worse unless it is diagnosed and treated early. Some researchers have emphasized that there is a high prevalence of PTSD after childbirth in low and middle-income countries and the prevalence of Postpartum PTSD varies from country to country. This might be due to a lack of access to maternal mental health services and materials for detecting Postpartum PTSD such as lack of accurate, valid and reliable screening instruments. This is the case in Malawi as the study revealed a high prevalence of Postpartum PTSD indicating a pressing issue in the access to maternal mental health resources, specifically screening instruments.

There is no literature or data in Malawi to form a basis for a comprehensive understanding of Postpartum Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder. This is concurred by another study which indicated that there is not enough literature to provide a comprehensive understanding of Postpartum Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder in Low to Middle Income Countries despite being prevalent [22]. Literature in other studies also demonstrated that mothers developed childbirth-related Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder with clinically significant Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder symptoms observed in up to 16.8% of postnatal women [23]. This almost concurred with this study’s findings. The study found 22.6% of postpartum women with PTSD diagnosis which also concurred with some studies that were conducted in Low to Middle Income Countries which generally found higher prevalence rates of 20–37% [3, 24]. The findings of this study are consistent with previous research conducted in Low and Middle-Income Countries such as Iran and Turkey which reported prevalence rates of traumatic childbirth experiences and the development of Post-Trauma Stress Disorder symptoms ranging from 20% to 48.3% [25]. These results are further supported by a study conducted by Grekin & O'Hara [19] which found that the prevalence of Postpartum PTSD varied from 1 to 30%. These collective findings emphasize the significant mental health concern posed by PTSD among postpartum women highlighting the potential disease burden associated with this condition. It is crucial to address this issue and prioritize the provision of appropriate support and interventions to enhance the mental well-being of postpartum women [5].

Psychometrics properties of Chichewa version city birth trauma scale

The study showed that Chichewa Version City Birth Trauma Scale is a valid and reliable screening instrument for effectively detecting PTSD among women after childbirth. The sensitivity was estimated at 81.6% which means that 81.6% of postnatal mothers who truly have PTSD as successfully classified as having PTSD while the specificity was estimated as 78.9% that is 78.9% of non-Postpartum PTSD women were correctly classified as not having Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder. Positive Predictive Value was estimated at 53.0% meaning that 53.0% of postnatal mothers are positive for PTSD with a positive index test has the disease. Negative Predictive Value was estimated at 93.6% meaning that 93.6% of postnatal mothers are negative for PTSD with a negative index test that does not have the disease. The valid instrument should have the ability to measure what it is supposed to and this was determined by its sensitivity (Se), specificity (Sp), Positive Predictive Value and Negative Predictive Value [26]. The sensitivity of a screening instrument refers to the proportion of people, in this study postnatal mothers with the disease (Postpartum PTSD) that were identified (true positive) by the instrument while specificity is the proportion of postnatal mothers without the disease (Postpartum Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder) who had a negative result (true negatives).

Further, Positive Predictive Value and Negative Predictive Value measure the likelihood that a positive or negative screening result is accurate for the individual. Positive Predictive Value and Negative Predictive Value of a screening instrument depend on a disease in a population so Positive Predictive Value increases with increasing prevalence of the disease and Negative Predictive Value decreases with increasing prevalence. These predictive values are more useful measures of diagnostic accuracy in routine clinical practice because they assist healthcare providers to know the probability of the correct diagnosis being made. An instrument that has high sensitivity and Negative Predictive Value rules” OUT” the disease while the one with high specificity and Positive Predictive Value rules “IN” the disease. Therefore, the sensitivity and specificity of Chichewa Version City Birth Trauma Scale are determined by the results outcomes with the gold standard. For instance, the prevalence of Postpartum PTSD based on SCID-5 and CityBiTS were 22.6% and 10.9% respectively.

A good screening instrument should have both high sensitivity and specificity. Sensitivity, Specificity and Area Under Curve have been calculated to test for validity of City Birth Trauma Scale to the related birth-related symptoms. Nevertheless, sensitivity and specificity are often in balance (trade-off) and can vary with optimum scores determined. Both sensitivity and specificity are equally important although screening instruments can be very specific without being sensitive or they can be very sensitive without being specific. It is recommended that a suitable screening instrument should have a minimum acceptable balance of Sensitivity and Specificity [27].

Sensitivity and specificity are important measures of a diagnostic test because they give us an idea of how well a new diagnostic test performs when compared with an existing gold standard test. Sensitivity is defined as the proportion of subjects with the disease who have a positive test. Specificity is defined as the proportion of subjects without the disease who have a negative test.

Positive Predictive Value and Negative Predictive Value measure the likelihood that a positive or negative screening result is accurate for the individual. Positive Predictive Value and Negative Predictive Value of a screening instrument depend on the prevalence of a disease in a population. Therefore, Positive Predictive Value increases with increasing prevalence of the disease and Negative Predictive Value decreases with increasing prevalence. These predictive values are more useful measures of diagnostic accuracy in routine clinical practices like postnatal clinics because they assist healthcare providers to know the probability of the correct diagnosis being made. An instrument that has high sensitivity and Negative Predictive Value rules out the disease while the one with high specificity and Positive Predictive Value rules in the disease [27, 28]. Thus, a highly sensitive screening instrument is the most helpful to the health care providers when the result is negative because an individual who screens negatively is very unlikely to have the disease. Similarly, a screening instrument with high specificity is also most helpful to the health care provider when the result is positive because the individual who screens positive is likely to have a disease. Literature indicates that a screening instrument cannot valid without it reliably and consistently measuring what is supposed to measure. This suggests that for effective screening of Postpartum Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder in postnatal clinics, midwives must utilize accurate screening instruments for Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder. Assessing or screening for Postpartum PTSD using valid instruments can assist in accurately identifying postnatal mothers who need mental health interventions [5].

The findings of this study have shown that City Birth Trauma Scale has good internal consistency. The screening instrument with high consistency measures what it is supposed to measure. The Cronbach’s Alpha of this Chichewa version City Birth Trauma Scale is 0.780. These findings align with previous studies, further supporting the reliability of the City Birth Trauma Scale as a robust assessment tool for measuring birth trauma experiences. [23, 29–33]. Therefore, the researcher recommends that Chichewa version of City Birth Trauma Scale is a reliable tool as it a psychometrically sound. This psychometric sound indicates its utility for clinical practice and clinical research purposes. Furthermore, the optimum cut-off point > 23 was chosen to deliver a good balance between sensitivity and specificity of this Chichewa Version City Birth Trauma Scale.

Accuracy refers to the degree to which a measurement represents the true value of the attribute being measured and can be determined by a comparing result from a screening instrument which results generated by a gold standard using scores for the area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity and specificity. Area Under Curve scores are used to categorize the accuracy of the screening instrument as insignificant (< 0.30) this is a negligible correlation, weak (0.30–0.50) this is low positive, moderate (0.51–0.70) this is moderate positive, strong (0.71–0.90) this is high positive, very strong and excellent (˃ 0.9) this is very high positive. The higher the Area Under Curve score, the more accurate a screening instrument detects individuals with or without the condition being tested [26]. In this study, the researcher found Area Under Curve is 0.872 which categorized the accuracy as strong and highly positive. This study's findings concurred with other studies done in different countries. As such good accurate instruments are necessary for the screening for conditions like Postpartum Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder in postnatal clinics. Furthermore, Youden Index presented a good psychometric property because the finding is within normal ranges of Youden Indices. Therefore, the researcher recommends that Chichewa version of City Birth Trauma Scale is a valid and reliable screening instrument with good psychometric properties to assess Postpartum Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder in Postnatal clinics.

The study finding found the Exploratory Factor Analysis for reliability and validity of the two-factor structure was 65.5% of the variance of factor 1 of birth-related symptoms and factor 2 of general symptoms. The Convergent Validity was at 0.65 which indicated good correlation between symptoms and adequacy of psychometric attribute. Chichewa version City Birth Trauma Scale (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.7665) and two factors (Cronbach’s alpha for factor 1 = 0.781 and Cronbach’s alpha for factor 2 = 0.752 presented internal consistency. This study finding collaborated with studies conducted and presented appropriate psychometric properties to measure symptoms of Postpartum PTSD [7, 8, 34]. Therefore, Chichewa Version City Birth Trauma Scale is a reliable and valid instrument indicating its utility for clinical and research to assess Postpartum Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder.

Implications

For clinical practice, all nurses and clinicians should be oriented on CityBiTS and there is a need for routine screening for Postpartum PTSD in all Postnatal clinics in Malawi for early detection and intervention hence promoting maternal mental health to postnatal mothers.

For policymakers, there is a need for making some policies such as integrating maternal health and maternal mental health in the hospitals to address both general maternal health and maternal mental health to maintain health for all. There is a need for economic and political will in terms of financial budget in mental health sector because accurate estimates of prevalence enable researchers and policymakers to balance the cost of prevention and treatment against the public health consequences such as Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder.

For education/training institutions, there is need to orient students on CityBiTS to understand it for using it easily when there are allocated at clinical replacement.

In research, CityBiTS can be used for future research because this study has provided baseline information and it is now a validated tool with sound psychometric properties.

Strengths of the study

This study used an adequate sample size of 384 respondents, yielding statistically meaningful results. This is the first study to validate the CityBiTS in Malawi. This study used the SCID-5 for Trauma and Stress-related Disorders, which is a credible gold standard to validate CityBiTS. This study is a baseline for future studies.

Limitations of the study

Some of the constraints that were encountered in this study were; the study was confined to two health facilities only and were urban-based facilities though a small number of people from rural areas of part of Lilongwe attend one of these urban facilities as such the results cannot be generalized to the hard – to—reach rural health centres. However, the results would provide some information on the demographic characteristics of the respondent, the prevalence of Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder and the psychometric properties of City Birth Trauma Scale Chichewa version which is valid and reliable to be used in Postnatal clinics to assess PTSD after childbirth.

The study used two questionnaires to interview the participants and these questionnaires were long where some participants were observed to be tired and lost interest to answer the questions. However, the participants were encouraged to answer all the questions to achieve the intended goal of this study.

The results may also be used cautiously as the two settings used may not represent all health facilities in Malawi. However, the results remain credible as the study used a sample size that was deemed adequate.

Conclusion

The study found that there is no direct relation between some demographic characteristics of respondents and the prevalence of Postpartum PTSD. The study found that there is a high prevalence rate of Postpartum Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder in Malawi similar to other studies conducted in other countries. Finally, Chichewa version of City Birth Trauma Scale has been found to be valid and reliable for accurately assessing and detecting Postpartum PTSD. Therefore, Chichewa Version City Birth Trauma Scale is psychometrically sound for diagnostic accuracy. Therefore, this tool fills a gap in the current lack of validated instruments in the local context. which can be used for clinical at postnatal clinics, educational to teach students and research as a baseline for future research about Postpartum PTSD.
