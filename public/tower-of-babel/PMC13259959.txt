Development and Psychometric Properties of an Effective Evacuation Preparedness Checklist for Hospitals in Disasters: A Methodological Study
Authors: Fereshteh Taheri, Masoud Salehi, Mohsen Dowlati
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsDisasters such as earthquakes can severely disrupt hospital operations and endanger patient safety. Rapid evacuation of critically ill patients and medical equipment under pressure presents serious challenges. Strengthening preparedness through comprehensive training and systematic evaluation is essential. This study aimed to develop and validate a hospital evacuation preparedness checklist based on international standards to enhance hospital readiness and patient safety.MethodsA comprehensive review of international evacuation standards was conducted, focusing on guidelines from OSHA, ISO, and NFPA. These frameworks informed the development of a localized checklist aligned with Iran's regulatory context. The tool's content validity was evaluated by expert panels, and its reliability was confirmed through statistical analyses, ensuring its applicability for improving hospital evacuation preparedness in Iran.ResultsThe evaluation of the emergency evacuation checklist, structured across six stages, Preparedness, Decision, Warning, Withdrawal, Shelter, and Return, showed strong validity and reliability. The checklist achieved a face validity score of 4.56/5, confirming expert agreement on its relevance. It recorded a Content Validity Ratio of 0.90 and a Content Validity Index of 0.94, supporting comprehensive domain coverage. Test–retest reliability (r = 0.87, p < 0.001) indicated good temporal stability, while the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC = 0.89) reflected excellent inter‐rater consistency. Construct validity, assessed through expert consultation and exploratory factor analysis, confirmed that the checklist accurately represents the six key phases of hospital evacuation preparedness. These results underscore its robustness as a practical tool for hospital emergency management.ConclusionThe developed checklist demonstrates strong psychometric properties, proving effective for enhancing hospital emergency preparedness. High face and content validity, together with consistent test–retest and ICC results, confirm its reliability and usability. Continuous evaluation and adaptation of such tools remain vital for strengthening hospital safety and operational readiness.

Introduction

Disasters have the potential to cause sudden and extensive damage to critical infrastructure, resulting in profound economic and social consequences. While certain natural events, such as storms, may be anticipated, others, including earthquakes, occur abruptly, often without any prior warning. Accordingly, preparedness for disaster response constitutes a fundamental aspect of effective crisis management. Given their essential role in healthcare delivery, hospitals must remain operational during routine and emergency conditions alike. Any damage to hospital infrastructure or the advent of a disaster event can critically impair patient safety and necessitate prompt and comprehensive evacuation measures to safeguard lives [1, 2].

The significance of robust emergency preparedness within healthcare settings cannot be overstated, as emphasized by recent systematic reviews and empirical research highlighting the critical factors underpinning hospital staff readiness for patient evacuation during crises [3]. For example, a systematic review conducted by Moslehi et al. categorized essential components of hospital staff preparedness into four primary themes and nineteen sub‐themes encompassing management, communication, individual factors, and specialized training related to evacuation procedures. Their findings highlight the pivotal role of comprehensive training programs combined with effective management and communication strategies in facilitating the safe and efficient evacuation of patients in emergency scenarios [4].

Hospital incidents often result in multiple physical injuries, as the process of evacuating a healthcare facility tends to be both time‐intensive and complex. The transfer of patients during emergencies, particularly those housed in intensive care units (ICUs), poses significant challenges. Ensuring the safety of all individuals during a hospital evacuation is a multifaceted task, complicated by the fact that many patients may have limited mobility, thereby impeding their safe and efficient transfer. A distinguishing factor in hospital evacuations, as compared to other types of building evacuations, is the frequent need for assistance among hospitalized patients, who require swift and careful relocation to prevent further harm [5].

Hospitals are particularly vulnerable to fire hazards due to the widespread presence of flammable materials within their environments, often necessitating urgent emergency evacuations. The primary goal of such evacuations is to promptly relocate the maximum number of individuals to safety within the shortest possible timeframe, a task that requires a high degree of preparedness and expertise from hospital staff and management. Furthermore, hospital administrators must navigate complex challenges, including the need to transfer large volumes of critical medical equipment, address the specific requirements of critically ill patients during relocation, ensure patient safety throughout the evacuation process, and operate within the limited time windows imposed after the declaration of an emergency [6].

Preparation for evacuation directly impacts the reduction of human casualties and injuries. Proper planning can significantly decrease the damage and fatalities resulting from emergency incidents. Effective emergency evacuation planning encompasses various activities, including risk assessment, establishing clear evacuation routes, crafting effective communication systems, defining command structures, providing appropriate equipment, situational awareness, training, and conducting regular drills. These elements are critical in ensuring that staff can safely and efficiently evacuate the hospital during emergencies such as fires, earthquakes, or other hazardous situations [7].

Although the primary goal of emergency evacuation in hospitals is to relocate as many individuals as possible in the shortest amount of time, adequate attention must also be paid to the healthcare needs of patients who may require ongoing medical interventions during the evacuation. Several factors influence hospital evacuation during emergencies, including patient characteristics, which determine the number of personnel needed and the necessary equipment for relocation. Additionally, patients' familiarity with emergency exit routes impacts evacuation time; insufficient awareness regarding these routes can lead to delays in evacuation and decreased efficiency [8].

Iran is among the countries highly vulnerable to natural disasters, particularly severe earthquakes. Notable seismic events, such as the Bam earthquake in 2003 and the Kermanshah earthquake in 2017, underscore the urgent necessity for hospitals and healthcare facilities to be thoroughly prepared for swift and well‐coordinated evacuations. Historical incidents, including fires and other emergencies in hospitals across Iran, vividly illustrate their susceptibility to unexpected events, thereby emphasizing the critical importance of comprehensive planning and preparedness for hospital evacuations in times of emergencies [9].

On June 19, 2024, a fire broke out at Ghaem Hospital in Rasht, leading to the evacuation of 142 patients, including 33 individuals from ICUs. Tragically, the incident resulted in the deaths of nine patients, primarily caused by power outages in the ICU and smoke inhalation. This event underscores the critical importance of robust emergency preparedness and the need for comprehensive strategies to mitigate the risks associated with unforeseen disasters in healthcare facilities [10].

Similarly, the fire incident at Sina Mehr Medical Clinic in Tehran on July 1, 2020, resulted in the deaths of 19 individuals and injuries to 14 others, alongside significant direct and indirect costs. A recent study underscored the inadequacy of preparedness and the poor performance of clinic staff during this emergency situation, highlighting the pressing need for staff training, drills, and emergency response exercises [11]. Research indicates that adherence to comprehensive disaster risk management standards in hospitals across Iran is alarmingly low, signaling a lack of preparedness to handle disasters effectively. Major weaknesses include poorly developed preparedness and response plans, insufficient awareness of national preparedness guidelines, inadequate training and drills, and non‐structural vulnerabilities within hospital infrastructures [12]. Consequently, hospitals must prioritize the development of strong strategic emergency evacuation plans to enhance their readiness and response in crises [1].

Designing a checklist to assess the preparedness of hospitals for emergency evacuations can serve as a valuable resource for enhancing their evacuation programs. This checklist would offer clear guidelines to increase compliance with established standards, conserve resources, evaluate readiness levels, identify areas needing improvement, and implement effective measures to ensure greater safety for both staff and patients during evacuations. By establishing such a framework, hospitals can significantly bolster their preparedness for emergency situations and improve their overall response capabilities [13].

Method and Materials

Initial Study of International Standards

In the initial phase of the research, a thorough examination of international standards and guidelines related to emergency evacuation was conducted. Significant references identified included the Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) in the United States, which has been instrumental in developing guidelines and checklists that assist organizations in creating effective emergency evacuation plans [14]. The International Organization for Standardization (ISO) publishes global standards for various systems and processes, including crisis management and evacuation preparedness. Notably, standards such as ISO 22320:2018, which pertains to crisis management and incident response, have gained global acceptance and utilization. These standards serve as international benchmarks for enhancing crisis management practices due to their comprehensive nature and detailed recommendations [15].

Another critical organization is the National Fire Protection Association (NFPA), which publishes standards related to fire safety and crisis management. NFPA standards, such as NFPA 101, are widely recognized and utilized internationally as foundational guidelines for designing safety and evacuation programs due to their thoroughness and credibility [16].

Through this analysis, key components such as emergency action plans, safety equipment, evacuation routes, and early warning and notification systems were identified. The primary objective was to ascertain the needs, requirements, and best practices concerning emergency evacuations, ensuring that all essential aspects were thoroughly reviewed.

Assessment of the Current Situation in Iran

The next phase involved identifying the current situation in Iran regarding legal requirements and national guidelines for emergency management and hospital evacuations. The components highlighted in the initial examination of international documents were then aligned with the specific conditions present in Iran, including local regulations, requirements, and infrastructure. This stage focused on determining how to adapt the checklist components to the unique circumstances of hospitals in Iran, incorporating relevant legal requirements and regional needs.

After reviewing both international standards and Iranian legal requirements, a checklist was developed that emphasizes key components and ensures comprehensive coverage of critical aspects of evacuation preparedness. Subsequently, validation of the checklist was performed, which involved assessing its face and content validity, as well as its reliability.

Validating the Checklist

Face Validity

Face validity refers to the extent to which a tool appears to measure what it is intended to measure, based on expert judgment and subjective evaluation by key stakeholders. To assess the face validity of the checklist, a panel of 15 experts in various fields related to hospital management, disaster response, health and safety, and crisis management was invited to provide feedback on the checklist's clarity, word choice, comprehensibility, and relevance to the field. The panelists were asked to review each item individually and assess whether the statements were clear, concise, and whether they adequately captured the essential elements of hospital preparedness for evacuation.

This step allowed for identifying ambiguous items and making necessary revisions to improve the tool's usability and clarity. Expert feedback was incorporated into the checklist to refine the wording and ensure that all critical components of hospital preparedness, such as evacuation protocols, communication systems, and safety measures, were comprehensively addressed [17].

Content Validity

Content validity ensures that the checklist adequately represents the domain of hospital preparedness for evacuation. This was assessed using both qualitative and quantitative methods, specifically using the Lawshe Content Validity model.

Qualitative Assessment: In the qualitative assessment, the expert panel reviewed the checklist to ensure the terminology was appropriate and that each item aligned with the intended content area. They also examined the grammatical structure, item placement, and scoring appropriateness. Feedback from this process led to several revisions to improve the relevance and clarity of the items [18].

Quantitative Assessment: The quantitative assessment of content validity was based on two indices:

Content Validity Ratio (CVR) and Content Validity Index (CVI).

CVR: The experts were asked to rate each item on a three‐point scale: “Essential,” “Useful but not essential,” and “Not essential.” A CVR score of 0.78 or higher was considered acceptable for retaining an item. Construct Validity

Construct validity refers to how well the checklist truly captures the underlying concept of hospital evacuation preparedness across all key phases of an evacuation. To evaluate this, we followed a structured, multi‐step process. First, a panel of subject matter experts reviewed and refined the checklist to ensure its items were both relevant and comprehensive. Next, statistical analyses were performed to determine whether the checklist items aligned with the theoretical dimensions of evacuation preparedness.

In developing this assessment, we drew upon previously validated tools such as the instrument by Yaghoubi et al. [20], “Development and Psychometric Properties of Decision‐Making Scale for Emergency Hospital Evacuation in Disasters,” which established its construct validity through exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and reliability testing. We also considered the framework of the “Hospital Performance Evaluation Checklist in the Context of the COVID‐19 Pandemic: Design and Validation” (2023), which applied the known‐groups method to confirm construct validity in hospital preparedness research [21]. Accordingly, our construct validity process integrated expert validation with statistical and comparative analyses, including assessments of convergent and discriminant validity (or known‐groups comparison when applicable). This approach ensured that the checklist could both distinguish between different levels of hospital evacuation readiness and demonstrate conceptual alignment with established, validated instruments in the field.

Expert Validation of Checklist Structure

Initially, a comprehensive review of relevant international and national documents resulted in the identification of 247 components pertinent to emergency evacuation. These components were then refined through expert feedback, ultimately reducing the list to 130 unique components. The expert panel, consisting of professionals in hospital management, disaster response, and crisis planning, was engaged to evaluate the relevance, clarity, and alignment of these components with the six stages of evacuation: Preparedness for Evacuation, Decision‐Making for Evacuation, Warning, Withdrawal, Shelter, and Return. This iterative process ensured that each item appropriately reflected the stages and captured all critical aspects of hospital evacuation preparedness.

EFA

To statistically assess the construct validity of the checklist, an EFA was conducted using data from a pilot study involving 20 hospital administrators. The goal was to examine whether the 130 items on the checklist grouped into the expected six factors (representing the six stages of evacuation) and whether these factors accounted for a significant portion of the variance in the data. Items that did not load strongly on their respective factors (with a factor loading threshold of > 0.6) were either revised or excluded to ensure that the final checklist remained focused on the intended constructs.

Correlation With Established Instruments

To further validate the construct, the checklist was compared with established disaster preparedness tools, including those used in healthcare settings. A strong, positive correlation (r > 0.5) was expected between the checklist and these established tools, which would provide evidence that the checklist measures similar constructs.

Pilot Testing and Feedback

After the expert validation and factor analysis, the revised checklist was pilot‐tested to evaluate its practical applicability in real hospital settings. The pilot study was conducted at 4 general and teaching hospitals in Tehran, Iran, and involved 12 managers from various hospital departments who were directly engaged in emergency preparedness and response activities. These hospitals were purposefully selected because they had previously implemented preparedness initiatives, such as developing emergency response plans and participating in evacuation drills. Feedback was collected to assess how well the checklist reflected real‐world evacuation scenarios and operational challenges. Insights from participants provided additional evidence supporting the construct validity of the instrument and contributed to further refinement of the checklist to better address the practical needs of healthcare facilities.

Assessing Reliability

The reliability of the checklist was assessed using: Cronbach's alpha, item–total correlation analysis, test–retest reliability, and intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC).

Cronbach's Alpha (Internal Consistency Reliability)

To evaluate the reliability of the checklist, Cronbach's alpha was used as a measure of internal consistency. This analysis helped determine whether the items within the checklist were consistently assessing the same underlying construct. Alpha values above 0.70 are generally acceptable, and those exceeding 0.80 indicate good to excellent consistency [22].

Item–Total Correlation

To further assess the internal consistency reliability of the developed emergency evacuation preparedness checklist, an item–total correlation analysis was conducted in addition to Cronbach's alpha. This analysis evaluates the extent to which each item correlates with the overall checklist score, indicating how well individual items contribute to the total construct being measured. Pearson's correlation coefficients were calculated between each item and the total score (excluding that item). According to accepted psychometric standards, item–total correlation coefficients above 0.30 are considered acceptable, while values above 0.50 indicate strong consistency with the total scale [23].

Test–Retest Reliability

To assess the stability of the checklist over time, we employed the test–retest method. The checklist was administered twice to the same sample of 20 hospital administrators within a 1‐week interval. The correlation between the two sets of responses was calculated to assess the consistency of the checklist over time.

ICC

For internal reliability, the ICC was calculated by having three evaluators complete the checklist for eight different hospitals. The ICC is used to assess the degree of agreement or consistency between multiple raters or measurements. It ranges from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating better agreement.

Tools and Data Analysis

The research utilized several software applications, including Word 2021, Excel 2021, and SPSS 2022.

For statistical processing of the data collected during the reliability assessment phase, SPSS 22 statistical software was employed.

Expert Selection

A convenience sampling method was employed to select a diverse group of 15 specialists in health, safety, hospital management, disaster health, and crisis management [22]. This non‐random sampling technique ensured quick access to experts who were readily available to contribute to the study. While convenience sampling can introduce potential biases, it allows for a well‐rounded perspective when other sampling methods are impractical. The aim of this approach was to gather comprehensive insights from experts with relevant expertise concerning the research topic [17].

Ethical Considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Iran University of Medical Sciences (approval code: IR.IUMS.REC.1403.621). Prior to participation, all respondents were informed about the purpose and procedures of the study, and written informed consent was obtained from each participant. Participation was voluntary, and confidentiality of the collected data was strictly maintained.

Results

Results of the First Phase

In the first phase of this study, a comprehensive analysis of international standards and authoritative guidelines on emergency evacuation and crisis management was conducted. The review focused on three major global frameworks: OSHA, ISO, and NFPA, each contributing unique yet complementary insights that informed the conceptual foundation of the hospital evacuation preparedness checklist.

OSHA

The OSHA framework emphasizes the importance of Emergency Action Plans, employee training, clearly designated exit routes, safety equipment maintenance, and effective alarm and communication systems [14]. These recommendations highlight the practical need for documented procedures, periodic drills, and defined responsibilities during emergency evacuations. The human‐centered orientation of OSHA standards, rooted in preparedness through awareness and accountability, provided a practical reference for structuring the initial elements of our checklist, ensuring that safety protocols are both systematic and applicable in real hospital settings.

ISO Standards (Particularly ISO 22320:2018 and ISO 22330:2018)

The ISO standards offer a systems‐based approach that focuses on crisis management structure, incident command and coordination, and information flow during emergencies [15]. These standards create an integrated framework linking preparedness, response, and recovery, emphasizing not only operational readiness but also strategic coordination. By aligning our checklist with ISO principles, we ensured it covered both the practical steps and management aspects of hospital evacuation, which are vital for keeping coordination and leadership strong during emergencies.

NFPA Standards (Notably NFPA 101 and NFPA 99)

The NFPA guidelines address the technical and infrastructural dimensions of emergency evacuation, including fire safety protocols, evacuation route design, occupant load management, alarm systems, and emergency lighting [16]. The NFPA's focus on environmental and structural safety complemented the human and organizational focus of OSHA and ISO. Incorporating NFPA standards allowed the checklist to achieve a balance between human behavior, facility design, and operational resilience.

Focus and Detail: International Standards generally provide broad guidelines that cover various aspects of crisis management and require organizations to adapt them to local contexts. For example, NFPA 101 provides comprehensive fire safety codes that organizations worldwide can tailor to their specific environment. Local Regulations in Iran often focus on specific aspects relevant to national priorities, such as regulations surrounding patient safety in healthcare settings. For instance, stakeholder engagement is mandated in the development of local emergency plans, which may not always be emphasized in international documents.

Interpretation and Flexibility: international standards allow for flexibility in implementation, enabling organizations to adjust based on local infrastructure and resources. For instance, the requirement to “Identify and assess the characteristics of hazards” can be interpreted differently based on the local risk landscape. In contrast, local regulations may impose stricter requirements or specific methodologies, such as how hospitals must conduct annual drills without adaptation from international practice.

Legal Enforcement: While international standards serve as benchmarks, they lack direct legal enforcement. In contrast, Iranian local regulations are legally binding, meaning hospitals must comply or face penalties, which emphasizes their importance in national policy.

Training and Community Involvement: Both international and local frameworks emphasize the critical nature of staff training; however, international standards may specify broader training programs while local regulations may require training to include cultural and regional specifics. Community engagement often plays a more significant role in local regulations since, in times of crisis, local communities can be active participants in emergency response, a factor sometimes less emphasized in international guidelines.

The focus of the identified components showcases a robust emphasis on the preparedness phase of emergencies, indicating a strong foundation in training and preparedness measures, which is critical for effective evacuation protocols. Differences between international standards and local regulations illustrate varied approaches to emergency management, with local regulations offering higher specificity and legal backing to ensure compliance within the Iranian context. Effective integration of international best practices with local regulations will enhance the overall emergency preparedness and response capabilities of healthcare facilities in Iran, thereby ensuring better patient and staff safety in emergency situations.

Developing the Emergency Evacuation Checklist

The initial objective was to categorize these components according to their content and necessity within the framework of the five stages of evacuation: decision, warning, withdrawal, shelter, and return. However, subsequent analysis indicated that certain components were more appropriately aligned with the preparedness phase. As a result, the components were ultimately organized into six distinct stages:

Preparedness for Evacuation (49 components): This stage encompasses planning and readiness measures critical for ensuring that facilities are equipped to respond effectively to potential emergencies [25].

Decision‐Making for Evacuation (15 components): This stage outlines protocols and criteria that guide the determination of when an evacuation should be executed, thereby facilitating timely and effective responses [26].

Warning (13 components): This element focuses on the communication systems and alert mechanisms necessary for informing staff and patients about the imperative to evacuate [27].

Withdrawal (17 components): This stage involves guidelines governing the actual movement of individuals from potentially dangerous situations to safety [28].

Shelter (14 components): This phase addresses procedures for temporary sheltering during emergencies, ensuring the safety and comfort of individuals until the situation stabilizes [29].

Return (22 components): The final stage delineates protocols for the safe and orderly return of individuals to the facility once the emergency has been resolved [30].

Through this structured categorization, the checklist ensures comprehensive coverage of all critical aspects of evacuation preparedness. The integration of these components not only addresses immediate evacuation needs but also contributes to fostering a culture of safety within healthcare facilities. By aligning the checklist with international standards, its relevance and applicability are enhanced, ultimately aiming to improve patient safety and operational efficiency during emergency situations.

Construct Validity

The results from the assessment of construct validity provide strong evidence that the checklist effectively measures the key constructs associated with hospital evacuation preparedness. The findings from both the expert validation process and statistical analysis confirm the construct validity of the tool.

Factor Analysis: The EFA confirmed that the initial item pool of 247 statements could be meaningfully organized into six underlying factors corresponding to the theoretical stages of hospital evacuation: Preparedness for Evacuation, Decision‐Making for Evacuation, Warning, Withdrawal, Shelter, and Return. Following expert review and iterative refinement, the checklist was reduced to 130 items, which were then re‐analyzed to ensure conceptual and statistical coherence.

The results of the EFA revealed a clear and interpretable six‐factor structure consistent with the theoretical framework. Factor loadings ranged from 0.42 to 0.88, with the majority of items exceeding the recommended threshold of 0.60. Only five items showed loadings below 0.30; these items were conceptually reviewed in consultation with the expert panel. Two items were revised to improve wording and conceptual clarity, while the remaining three, due to redundancy or weak theoretical relevance, were excluded from the final version of the checklist.

Each factor demonstrated substantial internal consistency and conceptual integrity. For instance, the Preparedness for Evacuation factor, covering items related to planning, staff training, and resource allocation, exhibited high factor loadings (0.65–0.86), reflecting its central role in readiness for emergencies. Similarly, items under Decision‐Making and Warning factors showed coherent clustering around communication, coordination, and timely action elements.

Together, the six factors explained 68.4% of the total variance, indicating that the model captured the essential dimensions of hospital evacuation preparedness while maintaining theoretical alignment and empirical robustness. These results support the validity of the checklist's structure and confirm that its domains accurately reflect the practical and managerial stages of emergency evacuation in hospital settings.

Correlation With Established Instruments: To assess convergent validity, the checklist was compared with existing validated disaster preparedness tools used in healthcare settings. The results showed moderate to strong correlations (r = 0.58 to r = 0.75) between the checklist and these established instruments, providing additional support that the checklist effectively measures the same constructs as these tools.

To assess the temporal stability of the developed checklist, a test–retest reliability analysis was performed. The checklist was completed twice by the same group of 20 hospital administrators at a 1‐week interval. The correlation between the two administrations was calculated to evaluate the consistency of responses over time.

The results demonstrated a strong positive correlation between the two sets of scores (r = 0.87, p < 0.001), indicating good test–retest reliability (Table 3). These findings suggest that the checklist produces stable and reliable results over time, reflecting its internal coherence and temporal robustness.

For internal consistency, the result showed a Cronbach's alpha value of 0.86, which reflects a strong level of internal consistency across the six stages of the checklist—Preparedness, Decision‐Making, Warning, Withdrawal, Shelter, and Return. Thus, the obtained coefficient suggests that the items are highly interrelated and measure cohesive aspects of hospital evacuation preparedness.

Item–Total Correlation

The results of the item–total correlation analysis showed that all items were positively correlated with the total checklist score. The coefficients ranged from 0.32 to 0.74, with a mean of 0.56, suggesting that each item was moderately to strongly associated with the overall construct of emergency evacuation preparedness. No items exhibited correlations below the 0.30 threshold; therefore, no items were removed.

Discussion

Hospital preparedness for emergencies and the necessity of emergency evacuation are key topics in disaster management and health crises that have attracted the attention of researchers and health managers in recent years. This study has taken an important step by developing and validating a comprehensive checklist to assess hospital emergency evacuation preparedness, aimed at meeting both practical needs and rigorous psychometric standards [31].

Previous studies, including systematic reviews of hospital preparedness assessment tools, indicate that many existing checklists and instruments primarily focus on structural and procedural aspects, with less emphasis on comprehensive psychometric approaches and phased evaluation of the evacuation process. The checklist developed in this study integrates practical and scientific dimensions by precisely categorizing the evacuation sequence into six key stages (preparedness, decision‐making, warning, withdrawal, temporary shelter, and return) and applying factor analysis and correlation studies with established tools. These features, especially compared to standard instruments published by international and national organizations such as NFPA or ISO, constitute advantages for this tool and provide it with significant construct validity [32].

The study by Moslehi et al., which identified key components of staff and management preparedness during evacuation processes, highlights training, coherent communication, and effective leadership as among the most critical success factors. This aligns well with the high levels of expert satisfaction and conceptual overlap observed in the present checklist. Moreover, this tool has achieved high scores on psychometric indices such as CVI (CVI = 0.94) and reliability (Cronbach's alpha = 0.86), demonstrating attention not only to qualitative aspects but also proving strong statistical reliability [4].

Studies focusing on practical tests and real evacuation scenarios support our findings and emphasize that a true assessment of preparedness must cover operational and cultural dimensions to respond effectively to local challenges. Due to its adaptation to Iran's local conditions and consideration of legal and infrastructural differences, this tool is more practical than many of the introduced international examples [33].

This research also showed a strong correlation among evaluations by different raters and high stability in retest measures, confirming the tool's robustness and repeatability. These features address some of the main challenges of previous tools and enhance this checklist's usability in ongoing evaluations and managerial reporting.

Ultimately, the developed checklist has the potential to serve as a national standard tool for evaluating and improving hospital emergency preparedness and evacuation programs. It is recommended that future research conduct broader field testing and comparison with other recognized international tools to validate its generalizability and effectiveness in diverse contexts.

Conclusion

The findings of this study clearly demonstrate that the emergency evacuation checklist developed here is a reliable and valid tool for improving preparedness in healthcare settings. Experts rated it as having very high face and content validity, indicating its clarity, relevance, and applicability for use in real hospitals. Furthermore, the checklist fully covers all critical evacuation steps, ensuring a comprehensive assessment tool that aligns with the full evacuation process.

Its strong construct validity, coupled with excellent internal consistency and meaningful item correlations, reinforces the validity of the checklist design. High reliability over time, as evidenced by significant intraclass correlations and test–retest stability, further supports its usability across raters and settings.

Healthcare environments are dynamic, and emergency situations can evolve rapidly, so tools such as this checklist should be regularly updated and adapted to keep pace with these changes. Future research should prioritize the wider implementation of the checklist in different hospitals and its comparison with other existing tools to refine its application and maximize its effectiveness.

In summary, this checklist provides healthcare organizations with a practical, evidence‐based tool for systematically assessing and enhancing evacuation readiness. Its use can significantly contribute to the safety and resilience of healthcare delivery, ultimately protecting patients, staff, and visitors during emergencies.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, a convenience sampling method was used to recruit participants for both the expert validation and the pilot testing phases. Although this approach facilitated data collection from qualified respondents, it may limit the generalizability of the findings.

Second, the sample size of the pilot study used for EFA was relatively small. While the results provided meaningful insights into the factor structure of the checklist, future research should confirm these findings using larger and more diverse samples across multiple regions and hospital types.

Third, the study was conducted within hospitals located in a single city, which may not fully represent the diversity of organizational structures, preparedness levels, or operational challenges of hospitals in other parts of the country. Broader multi‐site studies are therefore recommended to enhance external validity.

Finally, due to practical and logistical limitations, the implementation of the checklist in real evacuation drills could not be comprehensively assessed. Future research should evaluate the checklist's performance and usability under real or simulated emergency conditions to confirm its practical applicability.

Despite these limitations, the study provides a robust foundation for future validation and application of the Hospital Evacuation Preparedness Checklist in broader contexts.
