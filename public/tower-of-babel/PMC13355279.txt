Impact of age on the effectiveness of intravesical Bacillus Calmette‑Guérin and gemcitabine/docetaxel in treatment-naïve high-risk non‑muscle‑invasive bladder cancer
Authors: Mohamad Abou Chakra, Amanda A. Myers, Kathryn A. Marchetti, Yousef Zakharia, Sarah L. Mott, Michael A. O’Donnell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIntravesical Bacillus Calmette‑Guérin (BCG) has long been the standard therapy for high‑risk non‑muscle‑invasive bladder cancer (HR‑NMIBC), yet treatment response may vary with age. Sequential intravesical gemcitabine/docetaxel (Gem/Doce) has emerged as a well‑tolerated and effective alternative, particularly during periods of BCG shortage, contributing to its increasing global adoption. However, the influence of age on oncologic outcomes with either regimen remains unclear, with prior studies reporting heterogeneous results, especially within BCG‑treated cohorts. This study evaluated the impact of age on the effectiveness of intravesical BCG and Gem/Doce in treatment‑naïve patients with HR‑NMIBC.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed data on treatment naïve HR-NMIBC patients treated with BCG or sequential Gem/Doce from 2011–2024. The BCG cohort received a 6‑week induction course followed by maintenance at 3, 9, and 15 months. Gem/Doce consisted of six weekly induction instillations, followed by monthly maintenance for up to 24 months in patients who responded to induction. Age groups were defined as <60, 60–69, 70–79, and ≥80 years. Recurrence‑free survival (RFS) and high‑grade RFS (HG‑RFS) were estimated using Kaplan-Meier analysis, and age‑related recurrence risk was evaluated using univariate Cox regression.ResultsA total of 401 patients were included (BCG =192; Gem/Doce =209), with median follow-up of 84 months [interquartile range (IQR), 54–127 months] and 44 months (IQR, 28–62 months), respectively. Age was not significantly associated with RFS or HG-RFS in either cohort (BCG: for RFS, continuous age P=0.13 and categorical age P=0.15; for HG‑RFS, continuous age P=0.07 and categorical age P=0.12; Gem/Doce: for RFS, continuous age P=0.62 and categorical age P=0.44; for HG‑RFS, continuous age P=0.51 and categorical age P=0.68). In the BCG cohort, descriptive analyses showed a progressive decline in RFS and HG-RFS with advancing age, particularly among patients ≥70 years, who demonstrated lower 12- and 24-month outcomes (e.g., 24-month RFS: 64% for <60 years vs. 58% for 70–79 years and 59% for ≥80 years). In contrast, age‑related differences in the Gem/Doce cohort were smaller and primarily evident during early follow‑up (12 months); patients aged 70–79 and ≥80 years maintained stable outcomes at later time points (24 and 36 months) and did not exhibit lower outcomes (e.g., 24-month RFS: 73% for <60 years vs. 80% for 70–79 years and 76% for ≥80 years).ConclusionsAge was not a statistically significant predictor of HG‑RFS following either BCG or Gem/Doce therapy when controlling for known clinicopathologic risk factors. Although not statistically significant, a trend toward lower recurrence outcomes in older BCG‑treated patients was observed and warrants further investigation.

Highlight box

Key findings

• Age was not a significant predictor of recurrence free survival (RFS) or high-grade RFS following intravesical Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG) or gemcitabine/docetaxel (Gem/Doce) in treatment-naïve high-risk non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC).

What is known, and what is new?

• The impact of age on recurrence outcomes in patients with NMIBC treated with BCG or intravesical Gem/Doce remains uncertain, given the inconsistent findings reported in BCG studies and the limited data available for Gem/Doce.

• This study provides a comprehensive age‑stratified evaluation of oncologic outcomes within BCG‑treated and Gem/Docetreated cohorts among patients with treatment‑naïve high‑risk NMIBC. Across both treatment groups, age was not significantly associated with recurrence‑free or high‑grade recurrence‑free survival, despite a descriptive trend toward lower outcomes among older patients treated with BCG. These findings support that chronological age alone should not be used as an exclusion criterion when selecting intravesical therapy

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Clinicians should exercise caution in using chronological age as a determinant for intravesical treatment selection in high-risk NMIBC, as age was not a predictor of recurrence for BCG or Gem/Doce.

Introduction

Despite its longestablished efficacy for treatment of high-risk (HR) non-muscle invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC) (1,2), the use of Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG) has become increasingly challenged by global supply shortages and a substantial sideeffect burden, with more than 60% of patients experiencing local adverse reactions and up to 20% discontinuing therapy (3). These limitations have accelerated interest in alternative intravesical regimens, and sequential gemcitabine/docetaxel (Gem/Doce) has emerged as a promising option for treatment-naïve HR disease. Contemporary data report a 2‑year recurrencefree survival (RFS) of 77%, accompanied by lower discontinuation rates than BCG (4). The combination of durable efficacy, improved tolerability, and reliable drug availability has contributed to the rapid and widespread adoption of Gem/Doce in clinical practice worldwide.

In the absence of reliable and affordable molecular urinary biomarkers to predict response to intravesical therapy, largely due to variable assay performance (5), clinicians continue to rely on established nomograms and riskstratification tools to estimate recurrence and progression risk in patients with NMIBC. The most widely used models, the European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer (EORTC) risk tables and the Club Urológico Español de Tratamiento Oncológico (CUETO) scoring system, integrate clinicopathologic variables to inform these estimates (6,7). Within these models, age has drawn attention as a potentially important factor due to its possible impact on immune responses necessary for intravesical BCG efficacy. Although both the original and updated EORTC and CUETO models reported no association between age and recurrence or progression in BCG‑treated NMIBC, subsequent studies in similar patient populations have yielded conflicting results. Some demonstrated higher recurrence rates in patients older than 70 years (8,9), whereas others found no association with recurrence or progression (10,11), or identified an impact of older age only on progression‑free, overall, and cancerspecific survival, without affecting tumor recurrence (12,13). These discrepancies are reflected in contemporary guidelines: the European Association of Urology (EAU) considers age >70 years a prognostic factor in NMIBC risk stratification, whereas the American Urological Association (AUA) does not incorporate age into its risk schema (1,2).

These conflicting clinical findings have renewed interest in the biological mechanisms through which age may influence BCG responsiveness. Age‑related immunosenescence, along with other ageassociated immunologic changes (14,15), provides a plausible explanation for the suboptimal clinical outcomes observed in older patients.

Taken together, the inconsistent clinical data and the biologic plausibility of age‑related immunosenescence underscore the need to understand how age influences response to intravesical therapies. Our study aimed to evaluate the impact of age on the efficacy of intravesical BCG and Gem/Doce in patients with treatment-naïve HR-NMIBC. By comparing outcomes across age groups, this analysis provides insight into whether age modifies treatment effectiveness and may help inform therapy selection in older versus younger patients. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://tau.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tau-2026-0216/rc).

Methods

Patient cohort and study design

We conducted a retrospective review of patients with BCG-naïve, HR-NMIBC, as defined by AUA guidelines, who underwent transurethral resection followed by intravesical therapy with either Gem/Doce or BCG between January 2011 and December 2024. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the University of Iowa Institutional Review Board (approval No. 201404766), and the requirement for individual informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the analysis.

Treatment schedule and dosing for Gem/Doce therapy

Our intravesical Gem/Doce regimen for NMIBC has been previously described (16). Briefly, each treatment consisted of 1 g of Gem diluted in 50 mL of saline, instilled and retained in the bladder for 60–90 minutes, followed by 37.5 mg of Doce in an equal volume for the same duration. Induction therapy included six weekly instillations. For patients without recurrence at initial follow-up, monthly maintenance using the same protocol was continued for up to 24 months.

Treatment schedule and dosing for BCG therapy

The intravesical BCG regimen followed in this study has been previously reported (17). In summary, patients received a 6-week induction course using a full dose of TICE strain BCG, with or without the addition of 50 million units of interferon-alpha 2b (IFN-α2b). When intolerance or supply limitations occurred, a reduced dose, typically one-third of the standard, was given. Maintenance therapy consisted of three weekly instillations at 3, 9, and 15 months after induction, using reduced doses ranging from one-third to one-tenth of the full dose. This approach has demonstrated efficacy comparable to the Southwest Oncology Group protocol (18,19).

Histopathological assessment

All tissue specimens obtained during the initial transurethral resection were reviewed by an expert genitourinary pathologist. A detailed microscopic evaluation was performed to assess lymphovascular invasion and any variant histology. Tumor stage and grade were assigned according to the 2009 tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) classification and the 2004 World Health Organization (WHO)/International Society of Urological Pathology (ISUP) criteria.

Statistical analysis

Survival probabilities were estimated and plotted using the Kaplan-Meier method. Estimates along with 95% pointwise confidence intervals were reported. Recurrence-free survival (RFS) and high-grade recurrence-free survival (HG-RFS) were defined as time from the start of induction to recurrence or HG recurrence, respectively. Recurrence was defined as histologically confirmed tumor relapse in the bladder or prostatic urethra in males. Cox regression models were used to evaluate the effect of patient, disease, and treatment characteristics on outcomes. All statistical testing was two-sided and assessed for significance at the 5% level using SAS v9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC).

A total of 401 patients with HR-NMIBC were included in the analysis: 192 received BCG and 209 received Gem/Doce (Table 1).

BCG cohort

Baseline characteristics

Within the BCG cohort, 156 patients (81.2%) were male and 36 patients (18.8%) were female. The median age at treatment initiation was 70 years [interquartile range (IQR), 63–77 years]. Age distribution was as follows: 16.7% of patients were <60 years, 26.6% were 60–69 years, 40.6% were 70–79 years, and 16.1% were ≥80 years.

Pre‑treatment tumor stage distribution showed that 26 patients (13.5%) presented with carcinoma in situ (CIS) alone, 38 (19.8%) had Ta disease, and 18 (9.4%) had Ta with concomitant CIS. Additionally, 70 patients (36.5%) had T1 disease without CIS, while 40 (20.8%) had T1 with concomitant CIS. All patients had HG disease. Variant histology was identified in 14 patients (7.3%). The median treatment year for the BCG cohort was 2015 (IQR, 2012–2018) (Table 1).

Treatment response and oncologic outcomes

Median follow‑up in the BCG cohort was 84 months (IQR, 54–127 months). At first surveillance, a complete response was observed in 132 patients (73.7%). Among those who achieved a complete response, 121 patients (91.7%) received maintenance therapy. During follow‑up, 80 patients experienced recurrence of any grade, and disease progression occurred in 20 patients. A total of 19 patients subsequently underwent radical cystectomy. Cancer‑specific death was recorded in 15 patients.

Treatment details overview

Completion of induction therapy, defined as receipt of at least 5 of the 6 planned instillations, was achieved in 94.8% of patients in the BCG cohort. Additionally, 65.6% of BCG‑treated patients began maintenance therapy, and 62.0% received at least 2 of the 3 scheduled maintenance instillations within the first nine months following the start of induction. These measures are reported to describe treatment exposure and were not intended to define adequate BCG therapy according to guideline-based criteria.

Age and treatment response

Age‑stratified estimates of RFS at 12 months were 74% for patients <60 years, 78% for those aged 60–69 years, 63% for those aged 70–79 years, and 59% for patients aged ≥80 years. At 24 months, RFS was 64%, 74%, 58%, and 59% across these same age groups. Across both time points, patients aged 70 years and older showed a descriptive pattern of lower RFS compared with younger age groups (Figure 1A; Table 2).

Gem/Doce cohort

Baseline characteristics

In the Gem/Doce cohort, 174 patients (83.3%) were male and 35 patients (16.7%) were female. The median age at treatment initiation was 75 years (IQR, 67–82 years). Age distribution included 10.0% of patients <60 years, 23.0% aged 60–69 years, 33.0% aged 70–79 years, and 34.0% aged ≥80 years.

Pre‑treatment staging showed that CIS alone was identified in 18 patients (8.6%). In terms of papillary tumors, 40 patients (19.1%) had Ta disease and 27 patients (12.9%) had Ta with concomitant CIS. T1 disease without CIS was observed in 89 patients (42.6%), while 35 patients (16.7%) had T1 disease with concomitant CIS. All patients had high‑grade disease. Variant histology was present in 19 patients (9.1%). The median treatment year for this cohort was 2021 (IQR, 2019–2022) (Table 1).

Treatment responses and oncologic outcomes

Median follow up in the Gem/Doce cohort was 44 months (IQR, 28–62 months). At first surveillance, a complete response was observed in 189 patients (91.3%). Among those who remained disease‑free, 169 patients (89.4%) received maintenance therapy. During follow-up, 57 patients experienced recurrence, and disease progression occurred in 5 patients. A total of 4 patients subsequently underwent cystectomy. Cancer-specific death was recorded in 2 patients.

Treatment details overview

In the Gem/Doce cohort, adequate induction was completed by 97.6% of patients. Maintenance therapy was administered to the majority of patients (89.4%), with treatment continuation extending for up to 24 months.

Age and treatment response in the Gem/Doce cohort

At 12 months, RFS estimates were 85% for patients <60 years, 87% for those aged 60–69 years, 83% for patients aged 70–79 years, and 79% for those aged ≥80 years. At 24 months, RFS was 73%, 82%, 80%, and 76% across these same age groups (Figure 2A; Table 2).

Discussion

Age was not significantly associated with recurrence in either treatment cohort included in the study, despite the observation that older patients in the BCG cohort exhibited lower recurrence outcomes. In the BCG group, patients ≥70 years, and particularly those ≥80 years, consistently demonstrated the lowest RFS and HG‑RFS throughout follow‑up. In contrast, the Gem/Doce cohort showed a different age‑related pattern: although recurrence estimates declined over time in all age groups, patients ≥70 years had 24‑ and 36‑month RFS and HG‑RFS outcomes comparable to those of younger patients.

Several studies have reported an association between older age and an increased risk of recurrence in NMIBC patients treated with BCG, although the data in this area remain heterogeneous. Interpreting the impact of age on BCG efficacy requires considering these findings within the broader context of prior research, including differences in study design, sample size, and the variability of BCG maintenance protocols across cohorts. Herr et al. reported an association between older age and higher recurrence risk in a study of 805 patients. However, the vast majority of patients (93%) had a history of recurrent bladder urothelial tumors rather than primary disease, and many had not received adequate prior BCG therapy or BCG maintenance, making the findings difficult to generalize (8). Similarly, in a large Japanese cohort of 3,283 NMIBC cases, the proportion of patients who received adequate BCG or completed full maintenance was not reported. BCG exposure was categorized simply as receiving ≥6 instillations, and age was dichotomized using a single cutoff of 75 years. Among patients with T1 HG tumors (n=1,437), age (<75 vs. ≥75 years) was associated with both recurrence and progression on univariate analysis [recurrence: hazard ratio 1.41, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.16–1.71, P<0.001; progression: hazard ratio 1.53, 95% CI: 1.14–2.03, P=0.004] (9). In contrast to these analyses, a previous national phase II multicenter trial evaluating BCG combined with interferon‑α (n=1,106) provides additional nuance by demonstrating a non‑linear relationship between age and RFS outcomes. In this study, both the youngest (≤50 years) and the oldest patients (>80 years) experienced poorer RFS outcomes compared with individuals aged 61–70 years. On multivariable Cox regression, patients >80 had significantly reduced RFS relative to the 61–70‑year reference group (adjusted hazard ratio 1.56; 95% CI: 1.06–2.30; P=0.022), and those ≤50 years showed similarly inferior outcomes (adjusted hazard ratio 2.14; 95% CI: 1.31–3.49; P=0.002). Importantly, this cohort included both BCG‑naïve and BCG‑failure patients, and details regarding maintenance therapy completion among BCGnaïve individuals were not provided, making it difficult to assess the adequacy of BCG treatment in this subgroup (19).

In contrast, two well‑designed studies in which BCG adequacy was clearly defined found that age did not impact oncologic outcomes among patients receiving BCG. Contieri et al. evaluated 632 patients treated with BCG, 33.1% of whom had recurrent tumors. On multivariable analysis, age (≤70 vs. >70 years) was not associated with HG recurrence or progression (HG recurrence: hazard ratio 1.05, 95% CI: 0.77–1.44, P=0.70; progression: hazard ratio 1.57, 95% CI: 0.87–2.81, P=0.13) (10). Similarly, Tavelli et al. analyzed data from 473 patients who received adequate BCG and found no correlation between age and HG recurrence (HG recurrence: hazard ratio 0.77, 95% CI: 0.59–1.02, P=0.06; progression: hazard ratio 1.17, 95% CI: 0.62–2.18, P=0.63) (11).

In the 2 large NMIBC cohorts treated with BCG that reported an association between age >70 years and tumor progression, several important methodological limitations warrant consideration (12,13). In the cohort analyzed by Gontero et al., 2,451 patients with T1G3 tumors were included, yet most (61.8%) received only a six‑instillation induction course without adequate maintenance, limiting interpretation of longterm outcomes (13). In the study by Oddens et al., using EORTC 30911 data, 546 patients with NMIBC received BCG with or without isoniazid; however, the age distribution was imbalanced (65.9% ≤70 years vs. 34.1% >70 years), and enrollment occurred between 1992 and 1997, when treatment practices differed from current standards. Only 3.7% of participants were older than 80 years, raising concerns regarding age‑selection bias. Additionally, the adequacy of BCG administration was not clearly reported, further complicating the assessment of agerelated effects on outcomes (12).

For the Gem/Doce cohort, our findings are consistent with prior reports assessing the efficacy of intravesical Gem/Doce in patients with NMIBC, whether in the treatmentnaïve or salvage setting. In these studies, age was not a predictor of tumor recurrence (20,21).

Importantly, emerging evidence on the mechanisms of action of intravesical BCG provides additional context for understanding the potential influence of age on treatment response. Beyond its well‑recognized immune‑mediated effects, BCG can be internalized by urothelial cancer cells and directly induces tumorcell cytotoxicity (15). This non-immune‑dependent mechanism may help offset the effects of agerelated immunosenescence, which has been suggested as a factor that could diminish BCG’s immune responsiveness in older patients with NMIBC (14).

Molecular profiling studies further highlight the complexity of agerelated differences in tumor biology. When a transcriptomic classifier was applied in an independent NMIBC cohort, older patients were more likely to possess class 2a and 3 tumors with higher mutation burdens and greater chromosomal instability, features that correlate with poorer oncologic outcomes and increased progression risk (22,23). Taken together, these findings suggest that higher progression rates observed in some elderly patients may reflect underlying tumor biology rather than diminished therapeutic efficacy. This distinction is critical, as it underscores the need to evaluate age not merely as a chronological factor but within the broader biological and molecular context of NMIBC.

In our BCG cohort, age did not emerge as a predictor of recurrence in the univariate Cox model, suggesting that age alone may not meaningfully influence BCG efficacy. However, the modest effect size of age, together with the overall limited number of recurrence events in the cohort, likely reduced our statistical power to detect a significant association, as reflected by the wide confidence intervals. Treatment‑selection bias may also have played a role. The age distribution differed markedly between treatment groups, and the BCG cohort contained fewer patients at the extremes of age (<60 or >80 years), categories previously shown to have significantly different outcomes compared with those aged 61–70 years. If these patients, who generally exhibit poorer responses to BCG, were more frequently directed toward alternative intravesical therapies such as Gem/Doce, their selective exclusion from the BCG group could have attenuated the apparent influence of age. We believe our research question is increasingly important in the era of emerging therapies for NMIBC. Clarifying the true influence of age on treatment efficacy may help guide the selection of patients most likely to benefit from BCG at diagnosis, as well as identify those who may be better candidates for alternative intravesical therapies such as Gem/Doce.

Prior data indicate that both BCG and Gem/Doce are safe and well tolerated. Discontinuation due to intolerance most commonly occurs during the induction phase rather than during the maintenance phase of therapy (24). In the present study, no patients discontinued maintenance therapy because of treatment-related adverse effects. As a result, potential age-related differences in maintenance treatment discontinuation, which may reflect variations in the tolerability of intravesical therapy, could not be assessed. Nevertheless, this remains an important question with potential implications for treatment selection in NMIBC.

Emerging evidence from clinical trials evaluating systemic immunotherapy, including durvalumab and sasanlimab in combination with intravesical BCG (POTOMAC and CREST trials, respectively), in HR-NMIBC suggests that the effect of age on treatment response may vary across agents, although these findings remain exploratory and require further validation (25,26). While distinct from conventional intravesical therapies, these results underscore the importance of better understanding how age may inform treatment selection within the evolving NMIBC landscape.

A key strength of our study is the long duration of follow‑up, with a median of more than 7 years in the BCG cohort and to our knowledge, the longest follow‑up reported for a Gem/Doce cohort. This extended surveillance period enhances the reliability of our recurrence estimates and helps overcome limitations seen in earlier studies with shorter follow‑up intervals. We evaluated the impact of age on two most commonly used intravesical therapies for treatment‑naïve NMIBC, BCG and Gem/Doce, providing comparative insight across regimens. Finally, by stratifying age into four categories (<60, 60–69, 70–79, and ≥80 years) rather than a single cutoff near age 70. we captured the full spectrum of older adults, including the growing subgroup of patients >80 years with bladder cancer (27), who have been underrepresented or routinely grouped with younger individuals. Notably, the difference in treatment periods between the BCG and Gem/Doce cohorts reflects evolving clinical practice patterns, particularly recurrent BCG shortages and the resulting increased use of alternative intravesical therapies.

Our study has several limitations. The retrospective, single‑center design and modest sample size introduce potential selection bias and unmeasured confounders, and they limit the generalizability of our findings. We also did not evaluate the impact of age on disease progression, as the number of progression events was insufficient to perform such an analysis. In addition, treatment‑related toxicity by age was not assessed within the BCG cohort. Nevertheless, prior studies indicate that BCGrelated toxicity does not differ substantially across age groups and rarely leads to treatment discontinuation in elderly patients (28,29).

Conclusions

In this contemporary cohort of treatment‑naïve HR-NMIBC, age was not significantly associated with recurrence after intravesical BCG or Gem/Doce and caution should be taken when using age alone to make treatment decisions. However, descriptive trends showed a decline in recurrence‑free outcomes with advancing age, most pronounced among patients aged ≥70 years receiving BCG, whereas results were more consistent across age groups in the Gem/Doce cohort. These findings are exploratory and should be validated in larger prospective studies.
