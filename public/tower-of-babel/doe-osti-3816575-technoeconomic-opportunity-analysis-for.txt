Executive Summary
Falls City is a small community in Nebraska interested in understanding how energy from solar
photovoltaic (PV) and battery energy storage systems (BESS) could support the community’s
economic development planning. With limited and often inadequate access to power supply for
potential load growth, the Falls City community coalition has identified immediate needs for
energy to overcome their energy challenges and to encourage economic development. To address
the current community needs and address the future energy demand, technical assistance
conducted through Communities Local Energy Action Program (Communities LEAP) assessed
the technical and economic opportunities of adding PV and BESS capacity to Falls City’s
municipally owned and operated electric utility system.

The analysis considered the technical and economic feasibility of PV and BESS using the
System Advisor Model. The analysis explored a combination of three technology configurations,
weather data, wholesale electricity cost data, and wholesale electricity cost escalation rates as
shown in the table below.

Results from the modeling performed showed that at current capital expenditure costs and
wholesale electricity costs only four of the 54 scenarios analyzed provide a positive net present
value (NPV); and these scenarios were largely driven by high wholesale costs and the higher
(2.5%) electricity escalation rate. The positive NPV scenarios were either PV or PV + BESS
configurations while BESS alone scenarios had no positive NPVs. Across all technology
configurations and scenarios, notable capital expenditure cost reductions would be required to
return an average positive NPV if wholesale market conditions endure with expected growth
between 0.3% and 2.5%. Notably under the assumed market conditions, investment in BESS is
not financially viable since modeled revenue does not cover operations and maintenance
expenses.

Identifying additional financial support, changes in market conditions (e.g., lower capital costs or
higher escalation of wholesale electricity prices), and tapping into additional revenue streams
(such as compensation for grid services provided) could result in more economically viable
projects. In locations where PV or BESS can receive compensation for other grid services and
capacity payments or wholesale rates increase consistently, projects will return better NPVs. The
technical assistance and modeling results presented here are based on fixed assumptions that are

subject to change. As actual market conditions vary, re-evaluation may be necessary and
opportunities for PV or BESS projects that provide greater economic benefits to the Falls City
community may materialize.

Introduction
Falls City is a rural community of approximately 4,000 residents in the far southeastern corner of
the state of Nebraska. The community pursued technical assistance to support their economic
development planning through Communities Local Energy Action Program (Communities
LEAP). Citing limited and often inadequate access to power for potential new load growth, the
Falls City community coalition has identified immediate needs for energy to overcome their
energy challenges and to encourage economic development. The Falls City community coalition
has identified long-term economic expansion opportunities focused on the development of an
industrial Rail Park intending to attract biofuel and biopolymer manufacturers to the region. In
addition to the corn-based feedstock, the production of biofuels, including sustainable aviation
fuel and ethanol, requires a significant amount of energy, amounts that are currently not available
to the Falls City municipally owned and operated electric utility (Muni). While the city has
decided to pursue transmission projects to address capacity needs on their existing grid,
redundancy and resilience concerns remain unaddressed. Further, implementation of energy
sources, including solar photovoltaic (PV) and battery energy storage (BESS) technologies,
could make Falls City more attractive to potential manufacturers and could provide cost savings
to ratepayers.

To address the current community needs as well as address potential future energy demand from
efforts to attract biofuel and biopolymer manufacturers, the Falls City community coalition is
exploring how to integrate PV and BESS technologies into their energy and economic
development planning. The technical assistance conducted through Communities LEAP assesses
the technical and economic opportunities of adding PV and BESS capacity to Falls City Muni
distribution system. The analysis provides Falls City community coalition with a more robust
understanding of the technical and financial considerations of deploying PV and BESS
technologies in their community and assists with the development of energy implementation
strategies.

Through consultation with the Falls City community coalition, which included the Falls City
Muni, it was determined that a project developed and owned by the city’s municipal utility was
most likely to be realized. The Falls City Muni provides electricity service to its customers using
multiple sources of generation. Falls City is included in the Southwest Power Pool (SPP)
regional transmission organization. This means that electricity purchased wholesale by the Falls
City Muni is provided through the SPP market and its transmission system. Falls City Muni
produces power using their own diesel generator and purchases additional power from the SPP.
The electricity purchased from the SPP comes in two forms, firm contracts with two separate
generators and purchasing real-time energy market electricity. PV and BESS would be able to
displace or reduce the amount of electricity purchased from the SPP real-time energy market.
Therefore, the business-as-usual case assumes Falls City Muni continues purchasing power from
SPP’s real-time energy market, and the analysis models the financial impact of installing and
operating PV and BESS relative to business-as-usual.

The techno-economic analysis in this report summarizes the methods and results of modeling PV
and BESS implementation on the Falls City Muni grid. The modeling provides insights by
highlights different possible system configurations, project economic considerations, and
projected financial savings for the municipal utility.

 1

Methods
The System Advisor Model (SAM) was utilized to conduct a techno-economic analysis. SAM is
an externally vetted, proven software developed by NLR to evaluate the performance and
financial feasibility of renewable energy systems. At the request of Falls City community
coalition, three distinct technology configurations were analyzed to explore a range of
deployment scenarios: PV (solar-only), PV + BESS (solar integrated with battery storage), and
standalone BESS systems (battery storage-only). Running different scenarios allowed assessment
of the techno-economic performance of each configuration, including metrics such as energy
production, net present value (NPV), and cash flow over time. The PV-only scenario evaluates
the feasibility of solar power in isolation, while the PV + BESS scenario provided insights on the
cost and operational benefits of hybrid systems, and the standalone BESS scenario assessed the
viability of using energy storage alone for energy arbitrage.

The techno-economic analysis incorporated rigorous tests to evaluate the performance of energy
systems under varying SPP energy market and local weather conditions. Hourly locational
marginal pricing (LMP) data for 2024, the most recent available year, was combined with typical
meteorological year (TMY) weather data for one scenario. TMY data represents the typical or
average weather patterns at a location based on 30 years of historical recordings providing a
reasonable estimate for simulating solar generation and battery performance. The 2024 LMP data
reflects the most recent wholesale market conditions and was paired with TMY data as actual
2024 weather data is not available at this time. Additional scenarios were run with hourly
weather data for eight years in the last decade (2015 through 2022) and were paired with the
respective year’s hourly LMP price data. Running these eight scenarios helps capture the
variations in climate conditions and market dynamics that impact project performance and
economics.

Each of these scenarios was run using a 25-year project life and the LMP market values were
modeled under two annual escalation rates, 0.3% (low-rise scenario) and 2.5% (high-rise
scenario). A summary of the scenarios modeled are laid out in Table 2 for reference throughout
the remainder of this report.

The high-rise scenario uses the national historical average 1 rate of increases in electrical retail
rates, 2.5%, as reported by the Lawrence Berkely National Laboratory and NLR (Forrester et al.
2024). The use of 2.5% is likely higher than expected as the cost of wholesale energy does not
include other factors like electrical distribution system maintenance, which is included in retail
rates. However, this value is meant as an upper bound of expected possible scenarios and not an
actual projection of what will occur.

Exploring the U.S. Energy Information Administration’s (EIA’s) 2025 Annual Energy Outlook,
the weighted projected compounding annual growth rate for the generation and transmission
portion of electricity costs over the next 26 years is 1.53% nationally (EIA 2025). This growth
rate falls somewhere in the middle of the high- and low-rise scenarios. Notably this analysis
looks over the life of the project and therefore accounts for the average escalation rate. While
specific years over the project life may see larger increases than even the 2.5% upper end
modeled, the average over the life is projected to fall somewhere between the low- and high-rise
scenario values.

SAM provides a comprehensive suite of built-in functionalities for modeling energy systems,
including PV arrays, BESS, and hybrid configurations of PV and BESS. One of the key strengths
of SAM is its ability to offer detailed outputs based on user-provided inputs such as system size,
location, financial parameters, and electricity market conditions. This makes it highly effective at
assessing techno-economic scenarios. However, SAM does not make decisions for the user nor
recommend optimal strategies; it does not inherently account for subjective considerations,
regulatory constraints, or site-specific challenges. Instead, the tool provides objective
performance and financial metrics that help inform decision-making by modeling systems under
varied assumptions. For this analysis, SAM generated results based on preset weather years,
system specifications, and revenue parameter sensitivities, enabling an in-depth examination of
project feasibility under different market conditions.

Leveraging its ability to simulate various configurations and technologies, the analysis was
centered on the “merchant plant” financial model 2 native to SAM. The merchant plant model
assumes that the facility sells power directly into the wholesale energy market under real time
market-based conditions. This is fitting as the PV and BESS will be displacing electricity
purchased from the SPP wholesale market. This model is particularly useful for understanding
how the project's financial viability is impacted by electricity price variations, system
performance, and market uncertainties, as it does not assume stable or predictable revenue
streams like those associated with power purchase agreements.

To assess the financial feasibility of the project configurations (PV, PV + BESS, and BESS),
NPV simulations were conducted over a 25-year ownership timeline for each scenario. The NPV
analysis incorporated revenue streams from energy sales and operational costs, capturing the

The systems were modeled assuming they would be installed at the Falls City Water Treatment
Plant, located at 70434 661 Ave, Rulo, NE 68431 (40.03°, -95.42°). The Falls City community
coalition shared four sites that were under consideration for development but determined that

 5

modeling a project at the water treatment plant was the best site at the time. The water treatment
plant could benefit from a possible future resilience component if a battery was determined to be
feasible.

All systems have a 1,000 kW-AC nameplate, even under a hybrid PV+BESS scenario, and the
BESS scenarios include a battery with a duration of two hours. The PV was assumed to face due
south (180° azimuth) in conjunction with a 33° tilt to maximize annual production. The BESS
system requires operational parameter inputs as well. The BESS can charge from the grid or the
PV in all PV+BESS scenarios (scenario 2); otherwise, the BESS will only charge from the grid
in BESS only scenarios (scenario 3). The battery has a 90% depth of discharge, which means that
functionally it can only use 90% of the 2,000 kWh-AC it has. We account for both the
degradation of the battery because of time (calendar degradation) and use via charging and
discharging (cycle degradation). We assume that the battery does not get replaced regardless of
how much it is degraded to reduce costs in future years. This means that the functional energy
available will reduce with time and use.

SAM provides several options for BESS dispatch. SAM attempts to mimic real world BESS
operations for a merchant based on market price signals. We used an optimistic model to
determine the best possible outcome for a battery’s dispatch assuming that the BESS system has
perfect foresight for 24 hours. This means that in each hour, the BESS controller will look
forward at 24 hours of wholesale electricity prices and determine if the battery should charge,
discharge, or do nothing in the current hour to maximize its profit over the next 24 hours.

The financial parameters for both PV and BESS were based on NLR’s annual technology
baseline (NLR 2024) and the Solar Photovoltaic System Cost Benchmarks from the U.S.
Department of Energy (U.S. DOE 2024). 3 Values from both reports were used and scaled to the
relevant system sizes modeled for use in SAM. The capital expenditure/cost (CAPEX) of
PV+BESS (scenario 2) is based on a combined total installed cost of both technologies on a per
PV DC nameplate. Furthermore, the BESS CAPEX components were discounted 10% from
BESS only costs due to efficiencies gained by building a hybrid system. These values along with
other financial parameters are listed in Table 5 by the three technology configurations.

Results
Running the numerous scenarios provided a series of results with varying outcomes. Based on
the methods laid out above we present technical and financial results of the SAM models for all
scenarios broken out by technology pairings.

The PV only scenarios resulted in largely negative NPVs for most years modeled. 2021 LMPs
and solar production returned a positive NPV for both the 0.3% and 2.5% LMP wholesale
escalation rates, while 2022 had a positive NPV for the 2.5% escalation rate only. The revenue
generated directly correlates with the NPV as the lower the revenue generated by PV the lower
the NPV will be. Annual PV production varies from year to year based on the actual weather for
each year, which also has an impact on the revenue generated. Notably, the TMY year results are
similar from the average of the eight historical years modeled, including the positive NPV
values.

From these scenarios it is immediately obvious that notable reductions in CAPEX, either via
market trends or external funding support, would be required to get to a positive NPV. In the
case of BESS, whether alone or in conjunction with PV, this trend is taken to the extreme with
all scenarios revealing no viable CAPEX.

The results and underlying technical and financial factors that drive them will be explored and
explained in the findings section.

Findings
The results of the analysis demonstrate that PV by itself is the most economically viable option
among the technology configurations analyzed, though achieving a positive NPV remains
challenging across most scenarios. The PV only configurations revealed negative NPVs with
notable exceptions in 2021 and 2022 due to advantageous market conditions including higher
than average LMPs. In 2021, the LMPs combined with solar production delivered positive NPVs
for both the low- and high-rise wholesale escalation rate scenarios. The 2022 scenario showed a
positive NPV only in the high-rise escalation scenario. The correlation between revenue
generated and NPV underscores the importance of strong PV production levels and favorable
(higher) electricity pricing conditions. In years with lower LMP prices or solar output the
economics of PV alone are less favorable. The importance of expected wholesale escalation rates
therefore also correlates with NPV outcomes. The higher the expected increase in LMPs year
over year drives increased project revenue. If the escalation rate stays low in SPP, like it has over
the past 11 years (2013–2024), then PV NPVs will remain largely negative.

The analysis of PV + BESS showed a similar trend to PV alone but with generally poorer
economics. The addition of BESS to a PV system generally degraded the overall financial
performance, with only the 2021 scenario under the high-rise escalation scenario yielding a
positive NPV. Notably, the energy generated by PV systems in these configurations remained
equal to the PV-only scenarios. However, the inclusion of BESS introduced additional
complexity and project costs without introducing notable financial benefits. The SAM model
chose to charge the battery from the grid rather than from PV most of the time. Pairing BESS
with PV in this scenario would largely provide value if the cost of energy from PV is lower than
LMP rates, allowing PV to charge the BESS at a low cost and sell it to the grid at peak times.
Based on the scenario results, this price paradigm is not common at the current LMP and
CAPEX. The inability to significantly increase revenues through BESS integration, coupled with
the higher CAPEXs inherent to BESS systems, fails to make a compelling economic case for the
PV + BESS pairing under the modeled scenarios.

The BESS-only configurations consistently returned the worst economic performance of the
three technology configurations, with no scenarios yielding positive NPVs. These systems rely
entirely on the differential in wholesale electricity prices during charging and discharging to
generate revenue. However, the combined effect of overall narrow price spreads and capital and
operational & maintenance costs made stand-alone BESS financially unfeasible across all
scenarios. While all scenarios modeled returned a negative NPV, the least poor years correlated
with greater variability in LMP prices. As can be seen in Table 16, the average LMP is not only
higher in 2022 and 2021, but the standard deviation in hourly LMPs is the largest in those years

Sensitivity analyses revealed that even substantial reductions in CAPEXs would often be
insufficient to bring BESS systems to a positive NPV, with many scenarios requiring CAPEXs to
fall to levels below zero before economic viability could be achieved. This underscores the
significant barriers that standalone storage systems face in competitive merchant markets without
additional incentives or revenue streams.

Across all configurations, this analysis highlights the critical role of external factors in
influencing financial outcomes. The results show that all else held constant, without reductions
in CAPEXs via market trends, funding support, or other avenues, positive NPVs are unlikely for
most scenarios. The potential for additional funding through state, federal, or non-governmental
programs are therefore a notable consideration. Such external support could significantly
improve the financial viability of PV and PV + BESS systems.

The sensitivity analysis highlights how the financial viability of PV-only, PV + BESS, and
standalone BESS systems depends on their CAPEXs relative to current CAPEX figures of
$1.54/WDC for PV-only, $2.50/WDC for PV + BESS, and $1.16/WDC for standalone BESS. These
sensitivity results demonstrate the net CAPEX from the project owner’s perspective necessary to
achieve positive NPV, which is essential for investment feasibility. Understanding these
thresholds can help decision-makers identify opportunities for cost savings, external incentives,
or market strategies.

For PV-only systems, the current CAPEX of $1.54/WDC which is relatively close to achieving
financial viability in some scenarios, such as the 2022 high rise escalation, where financial
viability could be reached with CAPEX reductions to approximately $1.00/WDC. However, less
favorable scenarios require more aggressive cost declines to an average of $0.44–$0.62/WDC or
as low as $0.1/WDC, which may not be attainable in the short term without external financial
support. Meanwhile, ongoing cost reductions in PV manufacturing and installation provide a
realistic path toward reaching 1.00/WDC, making PV-only systems possibly viable in the future.

For PV + BESS systems, the integration of energy storage raises the financial reduction
necessary. With the current CAPEX of $2.50/WDC this technology configuration depends on
lower PV and BESS costs. For instance, certain scenarios may become viable at system-wide

 15

CAPEX levels of $1.59/WDC, though others demand reductions to around $0.87/WDC, which
presents a much steeper challenge of nearly 65% reduction in CAPEX. While these reductions
may take time, the increasingly competitive battery storage market and opportunities to monetize
storage through grid services or capacity payments could make PV + BESS systems
progressively more viable.

Standalone BESS systems face the greatest economic challenges as modeled. At the current
CAPEX of $1.16/WDC, they have a lower upfront cost than PV + BESS systems but generate less
revenue, making them financially inviable under all modeled scenarios. Even with sharp
reductions in CAPEX assumptions, including up to a 100% reduction (essentially a no-cost
system) financial viability remains out of reach. This highlights the lack of revenue-generating
opportunities as a primary obstacle in addition to a need for lower operation and maintenance
costs before NPVs will turn positive. To address this shortfall, standalone BESS projects would
require alternative revenue streams such as capacity payments, grid services, or methods to stack
multiple revenue streams to close the cost-revenue gap.

Alternative to cost reductions, if wholesale energy costs in SPP increases at a faster rate than
modeled in the high-rise scenario, it is possible projects become viable. The Falls City Muni is
located centrally within the SPP service area. This is relevant as LMP values are driven by both
electricity production and transmission cost components. LMP average prices and the variability
in price hour to hour is affected by transmission congestion. Falls City Muni takes transmission
service from the MEAN_FCU_OPPD node in SPP, which is noted in Figure 2.

Past market conditions and modeled system performance provides valuable insights, but it does

the current CAPEX figures. PV-only systems pose the least financial barrier with some scenarios
returning a positive NPV. PV + BESS systems, while less favorable do still reveal one positive
NPV scenario. Standalone BESS systems appear economically unfeasible at today’s costs and
market structure.

Conclusion
To provide Falls City Muni and the Falls City community coalition with an understanding of the
financial potential of PV and BESS systems, a series of scenarios were modeled using SAM. The
models explored three technology configurations (PV, PV+BESS, and BESS) using multiple
years of historical weather and wholesale cost data, and two different wholesale escalation rates.

Results from the models showed that at current CAPEX costs and under current SPP wholesale
electricity costs local to Falls City, no technology configuration averaged across the scenarios
returns a positive NPV. While a few specific scenarios modeled for PV and PV + BESS returned
positive NPVs, these were largely driven by high wholesale costs in that year. Regardless of
wholesale costs or spread between high and low, BESS alone never provided positive
economics. Also modeled was the net project owner CAPEX necessary to obtain a positive NPV
for all scenarios run. Across all technologies and scenarios, notable CAPEX cost reductions
would be required to return a positive NPV if wholesale market conditions endure with expected
growth between 0.3% and 2.5%. Notably under the assumed market conditions, BESS will not
be financially viable under any CAPEX values above $0.

In addition to reductions in system costs, external financial support, changes in market
conditions, and additional revenue streams would help create more economically favorable
projects. Where PV or BESS can receive compensation for other grid services or capacity or SPP
LMP rates or congestion costs increase consistently, projects will return better NPVs. Falls City
community coalition should take the findings of this report in context and recognize that all
results are fixed in time and based on assumptions that are subject to change. As conditions
change re-evaluation may be necessary and the outcomes of this analysis may no longer apply as
widely.

Furthermore, the analysis performed is focused on the financial benefit of energy supply offsets
only. The costs and benefits of utilizing solar or storage for resilience was not included and
additional analysis would be necessary to determine the value. Falls City community coalition
would need to explore the cost premium of installing a system that can operate off grid as well as
the value of lost load that solar or storage would displace in a grid outrage. Understanding these
costs and benefits will enable a comparison to determine if resilience factors would close the
revenue gap necessary to create more positive project NPVs.
