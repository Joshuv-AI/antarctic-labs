Total saponins from Panax notoginseng leaves alleviate complex insomnia by modulating neuroendocrine function, synaptic plasticity, and energy metabolism
Authors: Yu-Peng Liu, Ling-Yun Chou, Wen-Jia Yuan, Bing-Chen Han, Han Cheng, Yue-Bin Ge, Jian-Bo Wan, Xian-Ju Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInsomnia is increasingly recognized as a multifactorial disorder characterized by neuroendocrine imbalance, synaptic dysfunction, and impaired energy metabolism, particularly under conditions of chronic stress. Qiye Shen'an Tablets, formulated from total saponins of Panax notoginseng leaves (PNL), are widely used in clinical practice, however, their active constituents and underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood. Notably, partial hydrolysis of saponins during the preparation process may modify their chemical composition, thereby potentially influencing therapeutic efficacy.MethodsTotal saponins before (TS-b) and after hydrolysis (TS-a), along with a commercial reference preparation (TS-a+b), were comparatively evaluated in a mouse model of insomnia induced by 4-chloro-DL-phenylalanine (PCPA) combined with systemic stress. Behavioral assessments, hematological analysis, neurotransmitter quantification, histological examination, and metabolomic profiling were conducted. In addition, key pathways associated with the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis and synaptic plasticity were investigated. The neuroprotective effect of notoginsenoside Fd, a major component of TS-a, was further examined in glutamate-injured hippocampal neurons.ResultsCompared with TS-b and TS-a+b, TS-a exhibited a more stable composition and superior therapeutic efficacy. It significantly improved sleep-related behaviors, alleviated emotional disturbances, and partially normalized hematological parameters and neurotransmitter levels. Mechanistic analyses indicated that TS-a attenuated dysregulation of HPA axis, mitigated hippocampal glucocorticoid receptor (GR) overactivation, and enhanced the PKA–CREB–BDNF pathway, accompanied by increased expression of synaptic proteins. Metabolomic profiling further suggested the involvement of pathways related to neurotransmission and mitochondrial energy metabolism. Notoginsenoside Fd conferred neuroprotection against glutamate-induced neuronal injury, potentiallythrough modulation of GR signaling.ConclusionTS-a relieves complex insomnia through coordinated regulation of stress response, synaptic function, and energy metabolism, thereby providing scientific basis for optimizing preparation processes and refining active constituents.

Introduction

Insomnia is a prevalent and multifactorial sleep disorder characterized by disturbances in neuroendocrine regulation, neural function, and energy metabolism [1]. With sustained psychosocial stress and profound lifestyle changes, its incidence has steadily increased, imposing a substantial public health burden and significantly compromising both physical and mental well-being [2]. Although conventional sedative–hypnotic agents can provide short-term symptomatic relief, their long-term use is frequently limited by tolerance, dependence, and cognitive adverse effects [3]. These limitations highlight the urgent need for safer therapeutic strategies that address the systemic nature of insomnia rather than targeting sleep symptoms alone.

Traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) emphasizes multi-component and multi-target regulation, offering a systems-level approach to complex disorders. Panax notoginseng, a medicinal plant widely used in China, is traditionally described as replenishing vitality, nourishing blood, and calming the mind [4]. Total saponins (TS) extracted from P. notoginseng leaves (PNL) have been developed into Qiye Shen'an Tablets, an approved drug in China for the treatment of insomnia, palpitations, and neurasthenia, particularly in patients with fatigue and functional decline (Zhang et al., 2024). Clinical observations indicate improvements in sleep quality, mood stabilization, and stress resilience. Notably, insomnia is often accompanied by fatigue, reduced endurance, and cognitive impairment—systemic manifestations that are increasingly associated with impaired oxygen utilization and altered cerebral energy metabolism. Despite their clinical application, the pharmacological basis and integrated neuroendocrine–metabolic mechanisms underlying the therapeutic effects of Qiye Shen'an Tablets remain insufficiently elucidated.

Our previous studies demonstrated that aqueous extraction of PNL induces the hydrolysis of major ginsenosides (Rb2, Rb3, and Rc) through selective cleavage of the β-(1 → 2) glycosidic bond at the C-3 position, generating corresponding deglycosylated saponins, including Rd2, Fd, and Fe [5,6]. This transformation is mediated by a chloroplast-localised β-glucosidase (PnGH1) and results in substantial alterations to the overall saponin profile [7]. According to the Chinese Pharmacopeia (2025 edition), total saponins from PNL are prepared by water extraction followed by macroporous resin purification, a process during which partial hydrolysis can occur. Consequently, commercial Qiye Shen'an Tablets contain a mixture of native and hydrolyzed saponins. Preliminary findings suggests that variations in relative proportions of these saponins may influence hypnotic efficacy and contribute to variability in clinical outcomes [8]. However, the effects of hydrolysis-induced compositional changes on therapeutic efficacy has not been systematically evaluated.

In the present study, we compared total saponins prepared before and after complete hydrolysis and identified a formulation with improved pharmacodynamic stability. By integrating compositional analysis with in vivo pharmacological evaluation, we systematically examined how processing-dependent alterations in saponin profiles influence therapeutic efficacy and underlying mechanisms. This work establishes a mechanistic framework for optimizing PNL-derived formulations for the treatment of complex insomnia.

Methods and materials

Preparation of TS samples and HPLC analysis

TS-a and TS-b were prepared as previously described [9,10]. For the preparation of TS-a, 60 g of PNL powder were soaked in 1000 mL of water at 50 °C overnight to allow complete hydrolysis of major ginsenosides, followed by ultrasonication at room temperature (1 h × 3 times). The combined extracts were filtered and concentrated under reduced pressure at 50 °C to yield the crude extract. For TS-b, 60 g of PNL powder were steamed at atmospheric pressure for 30 min to inactivate endogenous enzymes, and then extracted with 1000 mL water by ultrasonication (1 h × 3). Both crude extracts were subsequently purified using macroporous resin chromatography. After sample loading, impurities were removed by sequential washing with water and 20% ethanol, and saponins were eluted with 60% aqueous ethanol. The eluates were concentrated and freeze-dried or vacuum-dried to obtain purified TS-a and TS-b powders.

The chemical profiles of TS-a, TS-b, and commercial total saponins of PNL(TS-a+b) were analyzed by LC-UV using a Thermo Fisher Dionex UltiMate 3000 UHPLC system. Separation was performed on an ACQUITY HSS T3 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm) at 25 °C with a mobile phase of water (A) and acetonitrile (B) at 0.4 mL/min. The gradient program was: 0–3 min, 28–30% B; 3–18 min, 30–33% B; 18–23 min, 33–40% B; 23–30 min, 40–42% B; 30–35 min, 42–95% B. Detection was set at 203 nm with a 2 μL injection volume.

Chemicals and reagents

TS-a+b was purchased from Shanxi Dongke Pharmaceutical Co. Ltd (Shanxi, China). Notoginsenoside Fd (purity 98.93%, Lot 25032006) was obtained from Beijing Beina Chuanglian Biotechnology Institute (Beijing, China). Estazolam was purchased from Huazhong Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd. (Hubei, China). PCPA (C6506-5G) was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Cyclophosphamide monohydrate (CTX, 97%) and acetylphenylhydrazine (APH, 98%) were purchased from Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. and Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China), respectively. Pentobarbital sodium (99.03%) was used for the pentobarbital-induced sleep test.

Animals and treatments

Male C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old, 18–20 g) were obtained from Hubei Experimental Animal Center Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). Animals were housed under standard laboratory conditions (12 h light/dark cycle, lights on at 06:00; temperature 22 ± 2 °C; humidity 50 ± 10%) with free access to food and water. All procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of South-Central Minzu University (Approval No. 2025-SCUEC-045) and conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines. To screen the optimal saponin formulation, a composite insomnia model with systemic functional impairment was established (Fig. 1B). Briefly, APH was administered intraperitoneally (i.p.) at 20 mg/kg on day 1 and 40 mg/kg on day 4. CTX (25 mg/kg, i.p.) was injected once daily on days 1–4. PCPA (350 mg/kg, i.p.) was administered 30 min after CTX on days 1–3. All injections were performed at 0.1 mL/10 g body weight. The dosages of APH and CTX were optimized based on preliminary experiments to induce stable systemic impairment while avoiding excessive acute toxicity that could confound behavioral assessments [11,12]. In the preliminary screening, mice were randomly assigned to six groups: control, model, estazolam, TS-a, TS-b, and TS-a+b. During modeling, treatment groups received estazolam (0.2 mg/kg) or saponin preparations (182 mg/kg, gavage, once daily), while control and model groups received saline. Based on behavioral outcomes, TS-a was selected for further study. In the mechanistic experiment, mice were divided into six groups: control, model, estazolam, and TS-a at low (91 mg/kg), medium (182 mg/kg), or high (910 mg/kg) doses. Behavioral assessments

Behavioral tests were performed to evaluate sleep behavior, locomotor activity, anxiety-like behavior, and depression-like behavior in mice. The sucrose preference test (SPT) was used to assess anhedonia as previously described [13]. Five mice per group were randomly selected and habituated for 2 days. After the second intraperitoneal injection of PCPA, mice were presented with two bottles containing 1% sucrose solution and distilled water, with bottle positions alternated every 4 h. Following the third injection, mice were deprived of food and water for 24 h before testing. During the test period, mice had free access to both solutions, and bottle positions were switched hourly. Sucrose preference was calculated as the percentage of sucrose intake relative to total fluid consumption. The pentobarbital-induced sleep test (PIST) was conducted to evaluate sedative–hypnotic effects. After drug administration, mice received pentobarbital sodium (40 mg/kg, i.p.). Loss of the righting reflex was defined as sleep onset. Sleep latency was recorded as the time from pentobarbital injection to loss of the righting reflex, and sleep duration was defined as the interval between loss and recovery of the reflex. The open field test (OFT) was used to measure spontaneous locomotor activity and anxiety-like behavior. Mice were placed individually in the center of a white square arena (50 × 50 cm) and allowed to explore freely for 5 min. The first 2 min were considered adaptation, and data from the remaining 3 min were analyzed. Total distance traveled, movement speed, and time spent in the center were recorded using the ANY-maze system. The apparatus was cleaned with 75% ethanol between trials. Depression-like behavior was further evaluated using the tail suspension test (TST). Mice were suspended by the tail (1 cm from the tip) for 6 min, and immobility time was recorded and analyzed using ANY-maze [14]. Following standard testing guidelines, the behavioral assessments were conducted sequentially from least to most stressful to minimize confounding effects: OFT, TST, SPT, and the pharmacological intervention PIST.

Blood biochemistry

Whole blood was collected at sacrifice into anticoagulant tubes. Hematological parameters, including red blood cell count (RBC), hemoglobin (HGB), hematocrit (HCT), and red blood cell distribution width (RDW), were measured using an automated hematology analyzer according to standard procedures.

Histological analysis

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed to assess histopathological changes in the hippocampus, while Nissl staining was used to evaluate neuronal morphology and Nissl body distribution. Brains were fixed in paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 4 μm thickness. Sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and stained with hematoxylin–eosin or 1% toluidine blue. Images were captured under a light microscope to assess neuronal arrangement and structural integrity.

Immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence

To examine protein expression and localization, paraffin-embedded brain sections underwent antigen retrieval in sodium citrate buffer (95 °C, 10 min). For immunohistochemistry, endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% hydrogen peroxide. Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against BDNF (1:100) or MAP2 (1:500), followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Signals were visualized using DAB and counterstained with hematoxylin. For immunofluorescence, sections were blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin and incubated overnight with antibodies against glucocorticoid receptor (GR, 1:1000) and NeuN (1:500). Fluorescent secondary antibodies were applied, nuclei were stained with DAPI, and images were captured using a fluorescence microscope. For quantitative analysis of immunohistochemical and immunofluorescent staining, three sections per animal were analyzed, and five randomly selected fields of view were captured per section. To ensure objectivity, all image quantifications (using Image-Pro Plus 6.0 and ImageJ, respectively) were performed by an investigator who was blinded to the experimental treatment groups.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

ELISA was performed to quantify neurotransmitters and neuroendocrine-related factors (n = 5 per group) using commercial kits (Wuhan Enovabio Technology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China). Hippocampal tissues were analyzed for γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA), glutamate (Glu), and 5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT). Hypothalamic tissues were used to measure melatonin (MT) and adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH). Serum samples were analyzed for corticosterone (CORT), erythropoietin (EPO), and granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF).

Untargeted serum metabolomics

Serum samples (n = 4 biological replicates per group) were analyzed using UPLC–Q Orbitrap MS. Briefly, 100 μL serum was extracted with 300 μL cold methanol, incubated at −20 °C for 30 min, and centrifuged (2300 × g, 20 min, 4 °C). Supernatants were subjected to analysis. Quality control (QC) samples were prepared by pooling equal volumes of all samples. For batch drift control, all samples were analyzed in random order, and a QC sample was inserted every 5 samples throughout the analytical sequence to monitor signal stability. Chromatographic separation was performed on a Shimadzu Nexera X2 UHPLC system equipped with a Waters ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column, and mass spectrometry was conducted on a Q-Exactive Plus Orbitrap in positive and negative ion modes. Data were processed using MS-DIAL for peak detection and alignment, and metabolites were identified by accurate mass and MS/MS matching. To ensure strict quality control, features missing in >50% of any group were discarded, with remaining missing values imputed via the K-nearest neighbors (KNN) algorithm. Only robust metabolic features with a relative standard deviation (RSD) < 30% in the QC samples were retained. Multivariate analyses, including PCA and OPLS-DA, were performed to identify differential metabolic profiles. Prior to analysis, PCA score plots showed tight clustering of QC samples with a high correlation coefficient (r = 0.993) between QC replicates, confirming minimal intra-batch drift and reliable quantitative performance, with no outlying samples identified by Hotelling's T2 test.

Molecular docking and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations

To explore the interactions between PNL saponins and core targets, molecular docking analysis was performed. The three-dimensional crystal structure of the glucocorticoid receptor (GR) was retrieved from the RCSB Protein Data Bank (PDB ID:4HN5). Ligand structures were prepared and optimized using PyMOL. Molecular docking was executed via AutoDock Vina 1.2.0 to predict the binding modes and affinities.

To further evaluate the conformational stability of the ligand-receptor complex, a 100-ns MD simulation was conducted for the Fd–GR complex using the GROMACS 2022.3 software suite. The system was parameterized with the CHARMM36 force field and solvated using the TIP3P water model in a cubic simulation box. After energy minimization and sequential NVT and NPT equilibrations, the production simulation run was performed for 100 ns. Trajectories including root mean square deviation (RMSD), radius of gyration (Rg), and solvent accessible surface area (SASA) were extracted to analyze the structural binding stability.

Cell culture and treatment

Mouse hippocampal HT-22 cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin at 37 °C in 5% CO2. Cells were divided into four groups: control, glutamate (20 mM), Fd, and Fd + corticosterone (CORT,400 μM) [15,16]. After 24 h pretreatment, all groups except the control were exposed to glutamate (20 mM, 24 h) to induce excitotoxic injury.

Cell viability assessment

Cell viability was assessed by MTT assay and real-time cell analysis (RTCA). For MTT, treated cells were incubated with MTT (5 mg/mL) for 4 h, followed by DMSO dissolution. Absorbance was measured at 490 nm. For RTCA, cells were seeded into E-plates, and impedance-based cell index values were recorded continuously after treatment to monitor viability in real time.

Mitochondrial function and cellular injury

Mitochondrial membrane potential was assessed using a commercial kit. Intracellular Ca2+ levels were measured using a calcium detection kit. Nuclear morphology was evaluated by Hoechst 33258 staining. Fluorescence images were captured using a confocal microscope.

qRT-PCR

Total RNA from hippocampal tissues was extracted and reverse transcribed into cDNA. Gene expression of SLC17A7, BDNF, and PGC-1α was measured using SYBR Green–based qPCR (ABclonal Technology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China). The primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S1. Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method with β-actin as the reference gene.

Western blotting

Hippocampal tissues and treated HT-22 cells were lysed in RIPA buffer with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Proteins were separated by SDS–PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. After blocking, membranes were incubated with primary antibodies against p-PKA, PSD95, synaptophysin, GR, BDNF, p-CREB, and CREB (HuaAn Biotech, Hangzhou, China; Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China), followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Detailed information on the antibodies, including sources and dilution ratios, is provided in Supplementary Table S2. Bands were visualized using ECL and quantified with ImageJ. Tubulin served as the loading control. β-actin or α-tubulin were used as internal loading controls.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SEM. Behavioral and biochemical data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism. Normality and variance were assessed prior to analysis. Depending on the distribution, one-way ANOVA (with Dunnett's or Welch's post hoc tests) or Kruskal–Wallis tests were applied. A p < 0.05 was considered significant. Metabolomic data were analyzed using PCA and OPLS-DA. Differential metabolites were identified based on fold change >1, p < 0.05, and VIP >1.

Results

Chemical composition of TS samples

PNL is enriched in 20(S)-protopanaxadiol-type saponins (Fig. 1A). Refined total saponin extracts obtained from PNL before (TS-b) and after hydrolysis (TS-a) were prepared through controlled transformation and purified using microporous resin chromatography. The chemical profiles of TS-a and TS-b differed markedly (Fig. 1B). TS-b was dominated by intact ginsenosides, including G-Rb3, G-Rc, G-Rb2, G-Rb1, and G-Rd, which contain a β-(1 → 2) glucosidic linkage at the C-3 position. In contrast, TS-a primarily consisted of hydrolyzed ginsenosides, such as notoginsenosides Fd (NG-Fd) and Fe (NG-Fe), G-Rd2, gypenoside XVII (GYP-XVII), and G-F2, generated through regioselective cleavage of the β-(1 → 2) glycosidic bond at C-3. Commercially available total saponins of PNL (TS-a+b), prepared according to pharmacopoeial procedures, undergo partial hydrolysis and therefore contain a mixture of both intact and hydrolyzed ginsenosides.

Screening of TS samples in a composite insomnia model with systemic functional impairment

As shown in (Fig. 1C–F), all three TS samples improved sleep parameters in the pentobarbital-induced sleep test compared with the model group. Each formulation reduced sleep latency and prolonged sleep duration to varying degrees, indicating partial restoration of sleep initiation and maintenance. Among them, TS-a produced the most pronounced effect, particularly in shortening sleep latency, and outperformed TS-b and TS-a+b. Consistent with these findings, the sucrose preference test showed that TS-a markedly restored sucrose consumption, whereas TS-b and TS-a+b produced only modest improvements. Taken together, these behavioral results demonstrate that TS-a exhibited the greatest efficacy in alleviating sleep disturbance and anhedonia-like behavior in the composite insomnia model. Therefore, TS-a was selected for subsequent dose–response and mechanistic studies.

TS-a improves sleep and affective behavioral deficits

As shown in Fig. 2, TS-a significantly improved sleep and affective behaviors in mice subjected to composite insomnia with systemic functional impairment. In the pentobarbital-induced sleep test (Fig. 2A), model mice displayed prolonged sleep latency and reduced sleep duration compared with controls, indicating impaired sleep initiation and maintenance. TS-a treatment significantly shortened sleep latency and extended sleep duration, with the medium dose producing the strongest effect. Its hypnotic efficacy was comparable to that of estazolam. In the sucrose preference test (Fig. 2B), model mice exhibited a marked reduction in sucrose consumption, reflecting anhedonia-like behavior. TS-a administration significantly increased sucrose preference, suggesting improved reward responsiveness. In the tail suspension test (Fig. 2C), model mice showed increased immobility time, indicative of behavioral despair. TS-a treatment significantly reduced immobility duration. Similarly, in the open field test (Fig. 2E and F), model mice demonstrated decreased locomotor activity and reduced time spent in the center area, consistent with anxiety-like behavior. TS-a significantly increased total distance traveled and centre time without inducing hyperlocomotion, indicating an anxiolytic-like effect rather than nonspecific motor stimulation. TS-a restores hematological parameters and circulating biomarkers

To evaluate systemic functional impairment, hematological and circulating biomarkers were assessed. Compared with controls, model mice showed significant reductions in RBC, HGB, and HCT, along with an increase in RDW, indicating disrupted erythrocyte homeostasis and reduced oxygen-carrying capacity. TS-a treatment markedly reversed these abnormalities, increasing RBC, HGB, and HCT levels while normalizing RDW (Fig. 2D). In parallel, serum levels of EPO and G-CSF—key regulators of hematopoiesis and stress adaptation—were significantly decreased in model mice. TS-a administration restored both EPO and G-CSF toward physiological levels (Fig. 5A). Together, these findings indicate that TS-a not only improves behavioral deficits but also restores peripheral hematological balance and systemic physiological function under conditions of composite insomnia.

TS-a alleviates hippocampal neuronal damage and synaptic structural impairment

Histological analyses revealed marked hippocampal injury in model mice. H&E staining showed disorganized neuronal arrangement, shrunken cell bodies, and enlarged intercellular spaces, indicating structural damage (Fig. 3A). In contrast, TS-a treatment improved neuronal morphology, with more orderly cell alignment and reduced pathological features. Consistent with these observations, Nissl staining demonstrated a significant reduction in Nissl body density and staining intensity in the model group, reflecting impaired protein synthesis and neuronal dysfunction. TS-a administration markedly restored Nissl body abundance and neuronal structural integrity (Fig. 3B). To further assess synaptic integrity, immunohistochemical staining for BDNF and MAP2 was performed. Model mice exhibited significantly reduced expression of both proteins in the hippocampus, indicating compromised synaptic plasticity and dendritic stability. TS-a treatment significantly increased BDNF and MAP2 levels, suggesting improved neuronal survival and maintenance of synaptic structure (Fig. 3C and D). TS-a attenuates hippocampal GR overactivation and restores transcriptional markers of neuronal plasticity

Given the central role of glucocorticoid signaling in stress-related neuronal dysfunction, GR expression and localization were examined by immunofluorescence. In control mice, GR immunoreactivity was moderately distributed in hippocampal neurons. In contrast, model mice displayed markedly increased GR signal intensity, particularly within NeuN-positive neurons, indicating excessive GR activation under composite insomnia conditions. TS-a treatment significantly reduced GR immunofluorescence intensity in hippocampal neurons. Quantitative analysis confirmed a marked decrease in GR-positive neuronal signals compared with the model group (Fig. 3E–I). To evaluate downstream transcriptional changes, qRT-PCR analysis was performed. Model mice showed significant upregulation of SLC17A7 mRNA, consistent with elevated glutamate levels, along with marked reductions in BDNF and PGC-1α expression, indicating impaired synaptic plasticity and mitochondrial biogenesis. TS-a administration reversed these alterations by downregulating SLC17A7 and restoring BDNF and PGC-1α expression toward control levels (Fig. 5B). These results indicate that TS-a attenuates stress-associated GR overactivation and restores transcriptional programs supporting synaptic plasticity and energy metabolism in the hippocampus.

TS-a modulates energy Metabolism–Related pathways revealed by untargeted metabolomics

To investigate systemic metabolic alterations associated with composite insomnia and their modulation by TS-a, untargeted serum metabolomics was performed. Multivariate analysis demonstrated clear separation between control and model groups, confirming significant metabolic disruption. Notably, the metabolic profile of TS-a-treated mice shifted toward that of controls, indicating partial normalization of systemic metabolism (Fig. 4A–F). Differential metabolite analysis revealed that composite insomnia primarily affected pathways related to neurotransmitter metabolism, amino acid metabolism, lipid metabolism, and mitochondrial energy processes. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis highlighted disturbances in GABAergic synapse function, glutamate metabolism, sphingolipid metabolism, and energy-related pathways. TS-a treatment significantly reversed alterations in metabolites involved in inhibitory neurotransmission and membrane lipid composition. In addition, pathways associated with mitochondrial function and energy utilization were restored toward physiological states following TS-a administration (Fig. 4G and H). TS-a restores neurotransmitter balance and attenuates HPA axis dysregulation

To determine whether TS-a regulates central neurotransmitter homeostasis and hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis activity under composite insomnia conditions, key neurotransmitters and neuroendocrine factors were quantified in brain tissues and serum. In the hippocampus, model mice exhibited a clear imbalance between excitatory and inhibitory neurotransmission. GABA levels were significantly reduced, whereas glutamate levels were elevated. In addition, hippocampal 5-HT concentrations were markedly decreased, indicating impaired monoaminergic regulation associated with sleep disturbance and affective dysfunction. TS-a treatment significantly increased GABA and 5-HT levels while reducing excessive Glu accumulation, thereby restoring excitatory–inhibitory balance. At the neuroendocrine level, model mice showed significant reductions in hypothalamic MT and ACTH, accompanied by decreased serum CORT, reflecting disruption of circadian regulation and HPA axis homeostasis (Fig. 5A). TS-a administration normalized MT and ACTH levels and restored circulating CORT concentrations in a dose-dependent manner. These findings demonstrate that TS-a alleviates neurotransmitter imbalance and corrects HPA axis dysregulation induced by composite insomnia, supporting its role in modulating stress–sleep interactions.

TS-a restores PKA–CREB–BDNF signaling and synaptic protein expression

To further investigate the molecular basis of synaptic recovery, key signaling proteins involved in neuronal plasticity were examined (Fig. 5). Compared with controls, model mice exhibited significant reductions in phosphorylated PKA (p-PKA) and phosphorylated CREB (p-CREB), along with a decreased p-CREB/CREB ratio, indicating suppression of the PKA–CREB signaling pathway. TS-a treatment significantly increased p-PKA and p-CREB levels and restored the p-CREB/CREB ratio, suggesting reactivation of this pathway. Consistent with these signaling changes, hippocampal BDNF protein expression was markedly reduced in model mice but significantly elevated following TS-a administration. Synaptic structural proteins were also assessed. The expression of postsynaptic density protein 95 (PSD95) and synaptophysin was significantly decreased in the model group, reflecting synaptic impairment. TS-a treatment restored the expression of both proteins, indicating improved synaptic integrity (Fig. 5C and D). Together, these results show that TS-a reactivates PKA–CREB–BDNF signaling and restores synaptic protein expression in the hippocampus, providing a molecular basis for its protective effects in composite insomnia with systemic functional impairment.

Molecular docking and dynamics analysis suggest differential binding stability of representative saponins with GR

To explore potential molecular interactions between major saponins and central regulatory targets, molecular docking analysis was performed. As shown in Fig. 6A, several saponins demonstrated favorable binding energies toward proteins associated with synaptic plasticity, stress signaling, and energy metabolism. Notably, NG-Fd and G-Rb3 both exhibited relatively strong predicted affinities for the GR, suggesting that GR may serve as a shared target for structurally distinct saponins derived from PNL. Representative docking poses showed that both Fd and Rb3 occupied the GR ligand-binding pocket and formed interactions with key amino acid residues (Fig. 6B). To further assess the stability of these interactions, molecular dynamics simulations were conducted for the Fd–GR complex. Free energy landscape analysis based on root mean square deviation (RMSD) and radius of gyration (Rg) revealed well-defined low-energy basins (Fig. 6C), indicating stable conformational states during simulation. Over a 100-ns trajectory, RMSD, Rg, and solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) values fluctuated within a narrow range, and hydrogen bonds were maintained throughout the simulation (Fig. 6D), supporting structural stability of the Fd–GR complex. Although both Fd and Rb3 displayed comparable docking affinities, the dynamic simulation suggests that Fd forms a more stable complex with GR (Supplementary Fig. S1). Notoginsenoside Fd protects HT 22 cells against glutamate-induced excitotoxic injury

To validate the predicted interaction with GR and evaluate functional relevance, the protective effects of notoginsenoside Fd were examined in glutamate-induced excitotoxic injury in HT-22 cells. Exposure to glutamate (20 mM) significantly reduced cell viability, indicating marked excitotoxic damage (Fig. 6E–G). Pretreatment with Fd significantly improved cell survival, as demonstrated by both MTT and real-time cell analysis (RTCA). Continuous RTCA monitoring confirmed sustained cytoprotective effects rather than transient improvements. Notably, co-treatment with corticosterone partially diminished the protective effect of Fd, suggesting that excessive glucocorticoid signaling may interfere with its neuroprotective action. At the cellular level, increased intracellular Ca2+ levels (Fig. 6H) and glutamate exposure caused a significant loss of mitochondrial membrane potential (Fig. 6I), reflecting mitochondrial dysfunction and calcium overload. NG-Fd pretreatment stabilized mitochondrial membrane potential and reduced Ca2+ accumulation. Hoechst 33258 staining further showed that glutamate induced nuclear condensation and increased fluorescence intensity, indicative of apoptosis. NG-Fd pretreatment attenuated these nuclear changes (Fig. 6J). Quantitative fluorescence analysis confirmed the above changes (Fig. 6K). These protective effects were weakened in the presence of corticosterone. Western blot analysis revealed that NG-Fd partially reversed glutamate-induced alterations in GR, BDNF, and PSD95 expression (Fig. 6L), consistent with restoration of synaptic and stress-related signaling pathways. The reduced efficacy under corticosterone exposure suggests a potential association between GR signaling and the modulation of mitochondrial resilience. Together, these findings indicate that NG-Fd mitigates glutamate-induced excitotoxic injury by preserving mitochondrial function, maintaining calcium homeostasis, and modulating GR-related signaling, thereby supporting its role as a key active component of TS-a.

Discussion

Insomnia is increasingly recognized not merely as a disturbance of sleep behavior but as a systemic disorder involving dysregulation of neuroendocrine signaling, synaptic plasticity, and energy metabolism [17]. Chronic insomnia is closely associated with dysregulation of the HPA axis, imbalance between excitatory and inhibitory neurotransmission, impaired synaptic integrity, and compromised cerebral energy homeostasis [18]. Under persistent stress and metabolic burden, these disturbances may reinforce one another, contributing to severe and treatment-resistant insomnia phenotypes [19].

To better capture this complexity, we established a composite insomnia model that integrates pharmacologically induced sleep disturbance with systemic hematological stress. This approach produced pronounced sleep disruption accompanied by affective abnormalities, hematological alterations, and neurotransmitter imbalance, thereby recapitulating key features of clinically complex insomnia [20]. TS-a significantly improved sleep behavior and emotional performance while partially restoring hematological indices and neurochemical homeostasis. These findings indicate that its effects extend beyond sedation and involve coordinated regulation at both central and peripheral levels.

TS-a was selected following comparison of different extraction processes and was chemically characterized to ensure reproducible saponin composition. Given the multi-component nature of PNL saponins, variations in constituent ratios may substantially affect biological activity. Molecular docking and dynamics analyses revealed greater conformational stability of the Fd–GR complex compared with Rb3–GR, providing a potential molecular explanation for differences in efficacy among formulations. These results underscore the importance of standardized preparation and compositional control in achieving consistent pharmacological outcomes for PNL-derived products [21].

The composite model highlights the interplay between central and peripheral stressors. PCPA-induced serotonergic depletion impaired sleep propensity and disrupted neurotransmitter balance. When combined with CTX and APH-induced hematological stress, animals developed more severe behavioral and neurochemical abnormalities. Reduced oxygen-carrying capacity and increased systemic metabolic load likely diminished cerebral metabolic reserve [22], rendering central circuits more vulnerable to neuroendocrine overactivation. This central–peripheral coupling provides a mechanistic framework for understanding persistent insomnia accompanied by reduced physiological resilience.

Hematological abnormalities in model mice—including reduced red blood cell count, hemoglobin, and hematocrit, increased red cell distribution width, and altered erythropoietin and G-CSF levels—suggest impaired systemic support rather than direct bone marrow injury. Such peripheral dysfunction may indirectly compromise neuronal stability under stress [11,23]. TS-a partially restored these indices, indicating improvement of systemic physiological capacity.

This systemic improvement may also be relevant for clinical populations with comorbid fatigue or metabolic insufficiency, suggesting broader applicability of optimized formulations beyond sleep regulation alone.

At the central level, HPA axis dysregulation emerged as a key mechanism. Elevated hippocampal GR expression in model mice indicated enhanced glucocorticoid sensitivity and impaired negative feedback [24]. Excessive GR activation is known to suppress neurotrophic signaling and weaken synaptic plasticity. Consistent with this, we observed reduced BDNF expression, decreased synaptic protein levels, and structural neuronal alterations [25]. TS-a attenuated GR overactivation and restored BDNF and synaptic integrity, suggesting normalization of HPA–GR signaling as a core therapeutic mechanism [26].

The PKA–CREB–BDNF pathway represents a central regulator of synaptic plasticity, emotional behavior, and sleep homeostasis [27]. The composite model suppressed PKA activation and CREB phosphorylation, resulting in reduced BDNF expression [28]. TS-a reversed these changes, reactivating plasticity-related transcriptional signaling. The restoration of this pathway paralleled improvements in hippocampal morphology, neurotransmitter balance, and behavioral performance, highlighting CREB-dependent transcription as a mechanistic bridge linking stress signaling to synaptic remodeling.

Energy metabolism emerged as another critical dimension. Serum metabolomics revealed widespread alterations in amino acid, lipid, and mitochondrial pathways, consistent with systemic metabolic strain. Upregulation of PGC-1α following TS-a treatment suggests enhanced mitochondrial biogenesis and metabolic adaptability [29]. In vitro, notoginsenoside Fd protected HT-22 neurons from glutamate-induced excitotoxicity by preserving mitochondrial membrane potential, limiting intracellular Ca2+ overload, and maintaining nuclear integrity [30] [31]. The reduced efficacy under corticosterone exposure suggests a potential association between GR signaling and the modulation of mitochondrial resilience. Together, these findings indicate that TS-a supports neuronal energy homeostasis and protects against stress-induced metabolic dysfunction.

Collectively, our results support a multi-level mechanistic model in which systemic energy stress and central neuroendocrine dysregulation converge to impair synaptic plasticity and neurotransmitter balance, ultimately producing persistent sleep disturbance with systemic functional decline [32] [33]. TS-a acts across this network by improving peripheral physiological status, attenuating aberrant HPA–GR signaling, reactivating PKA–CREB–BDNF pathways, and stabilizing neuronal energy metabolism. This supports the development of quality control strategies that integrate chemical profiling with mechanism-based bioactivity evaluation, thereby facilitating standardization, industrial scalability, and clinical consistency of Qiye Shen'an Tablets.Importantly, the link between extraction-dependent saponin composition, differential GR binding stability, and pharmacological efficacy provides a mechanistic foundation for optimizing the formulation consistency of Qiye Shen'an Tablets [34].

However, several limitations of the present study should be noted. Because the current study did not employ specific GR antagonists or genetic knockdown approaches, we cannot completely rule out the possibility that TS-a also acts through other parallel neuroendocrine targets. Future studies utilizing targeted validation, such as loss-of-function models, are required to confirm whether the neuroprotective and metabolic-regulating effects of TS-a are strictly dependent on the GR signaling cascade.

Conclusion

In summary, TS-a alleviates composite insomnia by regulating stress signaling, synaptic plasticity, and neuronal energy metabolism. It ameliorates HPA axis dysregulation and suppresses hippocampal GR overactivation, restores the PKA–CREB–BDNF pathway, and improves mitochondrial function. These results support the optimization of saponin composition and preparation processes to enhance the efficacy and consistency of Qiye Shen'an Tablets.
