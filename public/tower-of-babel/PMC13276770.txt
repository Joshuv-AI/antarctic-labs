SynCAR platform for capturing neuronal synaptic proteopathic seeds
Authors: Rebecca M. Sebastian, Robert Kupp, Dhaval Nanavati, Iwan Parfentev, Thomas J. Krzystek, Nandini Romanul, Kiran Yanamandra, Christina N. Preiss, Justine D. Manos, Hai-yan Wu, Alexandra Volkova, Steven Crescenti, Katherine Titterton, Alessandra M. Welker, Matt Townsend, Eric Karran, Xavier Langlois, Jessica W. Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummarySynaptic proteins are critical for maintaining healthy neuronal transmission but can also drive prion-like trans-synaptic spreading of pathological aggregated proteins found in many neurodegenerative diseases, including Alzheimer’s disease. Recognizing technological limitations for in situ identification of synaptic proteins, we built a modular synaptic chimeric antigen receptor (synCAR) for capture of synaptic proteins by fusing a single-chain antibody fragment with the post-synaptic protein neurolignin-1 (NLGN1). Using our synCAR platform, we show that anchoring the tau antibody PHF1 to the synapse effectively captures pathogenic synaptic tau species. Expressing PHF1 synCAR at synapses in mouse primary and human neuronal tau seeding models results in increased tau aggregation, likely due to concentrating pathological tau seeds at the synapse. These findings provide the first published method for the isolation and modulation of synaptic protein function within relevant biological contexts, highlighting synCAR as a relevant instrumental platform for synaptic protein research and neurodegenerative disease drug development.

Introduction

Neuronal connection and communication are facilitated by synapses, which consist of a pre-synaptic and post-synaptic terminal separated by a ∼24 nm wide synaptic cleft.1,2,3 The physiological function of neuronal synapses requires the precise localization of post-synaptic receptors, ion-channels, and proteins involved in pre-synaptic release of neurotransmitters. Disruption in the localization or expression of synaptic proteins alters synaptic transmission and has been linked to neurological disorders including autism spectrum disorder, psychiatric disorders such as schizophrenia and depression, and neurodegenerative diseases.4 Physiological synapse function can also be hijacked to enable the prion-like spread of protein aggregates in neurodegenerative diseases, leading to the progressive accumulation of intracellular protein aggregates in the brain, neuronal dysfunction, and cognitive impairment.5,6

Aggregation of the microtubule-associated protein tau is a key feature in Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and other tauopathies.7 In these diseases, neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs) composed of hyper-phosphorylated and conformationally misfolded tau appear and progress through connecting neuronal circuits, leading to neuronal loss and cognitive decline.8 While physiological tau localizes to the axon, mis-localization and accumulation of aggregated and hyper-phosphorylated tau at post-synaptic terminals occurs prior to NFT deposition,9,10,11 suggesting that gradual spread of NFTs may be attributed to the trans-synaptic transmission of pathogenic tau.12,13 Identifying and removing propagating synaptic tau species may therefore prevent subsequent NFT formation. Initial efforts to develop tau antibodies targeting the N-terminal domain were effective in reducing tau seeding in transgenic mouse models but have since failed in clinical trials, suggesting these antibodies do not target the trans-synaptic tau species responsible for driving tau pathology propagation.14,15,16 Identifying the post-translational modification sites in pathogenic trans-synaptic tau species is thus critical for antibody selection in tau immunotherapy to block pathological tau propagation.

Currently, there are limited technologies for studying synaptic proteins in situ that would enable the identification of trans-synaptic tau species within a relevant biological context. Docking of fluorescent proteins onto a synaptic scaffold has been widely implemented for synaptic labeling.17,18 Despite their utility in mapping synapse connectivity, such docking strategies have not been applied for synaptic protein capture. Alternative strategies such as synaptosome purification or proximity labeling have been used to identify synaptic protein composition in health and disease. However, these approaches either utilize unspecific protein labeling methods or fail to capture proteins within their biological context.19 Within the field of cancer immunotherapy, chimeric antigen receptors (CARs) were designed for customizable in situ capture and response to endogenous proteins. CARs are synthetic proteins that consist of a single-chain variable fragment (scFv) linked to transmembrane and cytoplasmic activation domains to create a designer cell surface receptor.20,21 Modifications to either the transmembrane domain or intracellular domain can be used to customize intramembrane associations or downstream signaling cascades. While CARs have been used to activate and direct cytotoxic T-cells to eliminate tumor cells in cancer immunotherapy and pathological B-cells in autoimmune disorders,22 such CARs have not been leveraged to any degree in understanding the biology or to develop disease-modifying therapies in neurodegenerative disorders.

Here, we describe the design of a synaptic CAR (synCAR) technology for investigating synaptic proteopathic tau seeds. The synCAR is composed of a scFv anchored to the post-synaptic membrane via fusion with NLGN1 to tether target antibodies to the synaptic cleft. For proof-of-concept studies, we selected the PHF1 scFv based on unbiased phospho-proteomics of human AD post-synaptic densities (PSDs), which identified enrichment of the pS396/pS404 epitope of tau (recognized by the PHF1 antibody). We confirm that the PHF1 synCAR is localized to synapses, retains binding function to pS396/pS404 tau, and increases the rate of tau aggregation in murine and human iPSC neuronal seeding models of AD. These findings demonstrate the applicability of the synCAR method to isolate and modulate synaptic proteins for detailed studies in neurodegenerative disease drug development and for physiological synaptic protein research.

Results

Building synCAR to tether scFv to post-synaptic terminals

CARs are artificial immune receptors comprised of an extracellular scFv fused via a variable linker region to transmembrane and intracellular signaling domains.20,21 For our synCAR platform, we sought to develop a method for capturing synaptic proteins using the principles of CAR design to anchor scFv domains to the synapse. Synaptic labeling within mammalian systems has been previously performed by mammalian GFP reconstitution across synaptic partners (mGRASP) using neuroligin 1 (NLGN1) as a post-synaptic transmembrane carrier. In this system, the extracellular domain involved in neurexin interaction (residues 52–626) was exchanged with a split-GFP domain to enable synapse labeling.18 For our synCAR platform, we chose the same post-synaptic transmembrane carrier, NLGN1, with a scFv targeting a tau epitope (Figure 1A).Figure 1Design of chimeric synaptic-anchored antibodies(A) Anchoring anti-tau antibodies to the synaptic membrane enables selective targeting of trans-synaptic propagating tau species. To select which tau scFv antibody to use in our tau synCAR, we applied phospho-proteomics to identify PSD-enriched tau epitopes in AD brain tissues. Post-synaptic fractions were isolated from neuropathologically confirmed Braak stage VI AD and age-matched control brain tissues using a modified sucrose gradient separation protocol23,24,25 (Figure S1A and Table S3). Western blot analysis of the resulting fractions confirmed enrichment of synaptic PSD-95 in the PSD fraction, while pre-synaptic SV2A was restricted to the crude synaptoneurosome membrane fraction (SPM). Likewise, the cytosolic protein catalase and the ER protein calreticulin were exclusively found in lysate and cytosolic fractions (Figure S1B) and were not detected in the SPM or PSD fractions. Seeding competence of AD PSD tau was confirmed using a primary neuronal tau seeding assay in which neurons express wild-type human tau protein (hTau) in the absence of endogenous mouse tau.26 AD donor-derived PSDs induced the formation of insoluble tau aggregates within the soma and dendrites, confirming that tau species enriched in AD PSD are seeding competent (Figure S1C). Phospho-proteomic comparisons of AD and age-matched control PSD fractions identified 17 phospho-peptides that were significantly altered in the AD PSDs after multiple test correction, of which over half occurred within tau (Figure S1D and Table S2). Of the identified tau species, T212/T217, T231, and S262 and S396/S404 all showed a greater than 5-fold enrichment in the AD PSDs as compared to control (Figure S1E). The tau species S396/S404 was of particular interest as it was previously reported to be enriched in synaptosomes27 and is recognized by the well-established tau antibody PHF1. Since PHF1 immunotherapy has also been shown to reduce tau pathology in AD transgenic mouse models,28 and since immunoprecipitation of AD material using PHF1 reduces tau seeding in both in vitro and in vivo transgenic models,29 we chose to use PHF1 scFv in our synCAR constructs.

To confirm that the IgG-derived PHF1 scFv retained antigenic recognition specificity toward tau, we transiently transfected HEK293 cells with expression vectors encoding either PHF1 mIgG2a or engineered scFv constructs. Cell media containing the secreted antibodies was then used to detect tau in AD and control brain lysates (Figure S1F). Both the secreted parent PHF1 mIgG2a and engineered scFv formats detected phosphorylated tau in AD brain lysates, while no immunoreactivity was present in the age-matched control lysate, indicating the retention of PHF1 antigen recognition in the scFv format. Linker format has been shown to influence antigen binding within CARs by modifying scFv flexibility and distance from membrane surface, with hinge regions from constant domain of immunoglobulin (IgG) or spacers from the T cell receptors CD8 often chosen.30 Incorporation of PHF1 scFv into synCAR was therefore achieved through three linking strategies to span flexibility and distance considerations: a (Gly)x9 linker, a (Gly)x9 with the hinge region of murine IgG2a utilized in the parent PHF1 mAB, and a no-linker direct in frame fusion (Figure 1B). An HA-tag was added to the NLGN1 C-terminus to enable expression localization via immunocytochemistry (ICC). Following design, PHF1 synCAR constructs were packaged into recombinant adeno-associated virus (rAAV) vectors under a modified enhanced synapsin promoter (eSYN) to drive neuronal-specific expression.31

Although double labeling shows that the synCARs are trafficked to post-synaptic terminals, it does not differentiate whether the orientation of the scFv domain IS on the intracellular or extracellular side of the synaptic membrane. As our aim is to create a chimeric receptor for capturing extracellular synaptic proteins, we next performed a cell-surface labeling to verify that the PHF1 synCAR as correctly anchored to the extracellular side of the post-synaptic membrane. Immunostaining in primary neurons expressing PHF1 synCAR was performed using an antibody against the (G4S)4 linker within the scFV domain of PHF1 synCAR (Figure 1B) without membrane permeabilization. Confocal images showed robust (G4S)4 staining, indicating that PHF1 synCAR trafficked in the correct orientation for extracellular surface display at the post-synapse (Figure S2). Taken together, these data demonstrated our engineered PHF1 synCAR accurately retained the post-synaptic trafficking and orientation of the parent NLGN1 scaffold.

The synthesis and folding of foreign proteins in cells often perturbs protein homeostasis and triggers consequential cellular stress responses. Therefore, we measured neurite area, number of synapses, and ER stress in neurons expressing PHF1 synCARs to rule out these potential toxicities. MAP2 neurite quantification showed that PHF1 synCAR expression, regardless of linker choice, did not significantly reduce neurite area (Figures 3A and 3B). Consistently, synapse formation, as determined by PSD95 puncta number, was not impacted for either no linker or 9xGly linkers, while a small increase in synapse number was observed for the (Gly)x9 + IgG hinge linker (Figure 3C). There was also no induction of cellular ER stress response, as measured by the absence of upregulation and translocation of the transcription factor ATF4 in PHF1 synCAR-expressing neurons (Figures 3E and 3F). Expression of foreign proteins within the synapse also has the potential to alter synaptic signaling. To explore this possibility, we utilized a synthetic calcium imaging dye to compare key features of spontaneous network activity in hTau neurons expressing PHF1 synCAR as compared to control cells. Synchronous Ca2+ firing was observed in both no AAV and PHF1 synCAR expressing neuronal populations (Figure 3F, Videos S1 and S5). Features of Ca2+ dynamics, including spike amplitude, frequency, and network synchronicity, were similar between PHF1 synCAR and no AAV neuronal populations (Figure 3G). Activation of neuronal firing through the addition of the potassium channel blocker 4-AP (Figure 3H, Videos S2 and S6) resulted in similar impacts to Ca2+ dynamics in both PHF1 synCAR and no AAV neurons. Likewise, the inhibition of AMPA activity using the small-molecule antagonists DNQX (Figure 3H, Videos S3 and S7) and CP-46502233 (Figure 3I, Videos S4 and S8) both reduced or eliminated Ca2+ spikes to a similar degree in both neuronal populations. Overall, no significant differences were observed in average Ca2+ peak amplitude, frequency, or network synchronicity in synCAR-treated cells, suggesting a minimal impact of synCAR expression on basal synapse activity (Figures 3K–3M).

To determine the utility of synCARs beyond mouse primary neurons, we next characterized the expression of our synCAR constructs in an AD-relevant human neuron model system. To this end, we used human-induced pluripotent stem cell (hiPSC) differentiated cortical excitatory neurons (hiPSC-neurons). These neurons express wild-type hTau and have been shown to be associated with AD vulnerability.34,35 AAV9-expressing PHF1 (Gly)x9 synCAR was added to hiPSC-neurons at DIV 7. HA expression and synaptic compartment localization of the PHF1 synCAR were determined 3 weeks later at DIV 36. Consistent with the expression profile in hTau primary neurons, a synaptic-like puncta expression phenotype of PHF1 synCAR was observed in hiPSC-neurons (Figure 2E). HA co-localization with PSD95 confirmed the correct trafficking of PHF1 synCAR to the post-synaptic membrane (Figure 2F). Hence, we confirmed that the three PHF1 synCAR designs properly anchor to the post-synaptic terminals in two disease-relevant models, a human neuronal model and a post-mitotic primary murine neuronal model.

Next, we sought to confirm that the binding specificity of PHF1 scFv was retained within the synaptic CAR format. Murine primary neurons expressing AAV9-delivered PHF1 synCAR were incubated with biotinylated tau peptide containing phosphorylated S396 and S404 residues (pTau #1) or two negative control peptides against non-specific binding, a non-phosphorylated tau peptide (#2), and a phosphorylated scramble peptide (#3) (Figure 4A). For all three linker variants of PHF1 synCAR, binding of phosphorylated tau peptide (#1) was detected (Figure 4B), confirming that the PHF1 scFv domain retained binding to the pS396/pS404 epitope following incorporation onto the synaptic CAR scaffold and expression in primary neurons. Quantification of the biotinylated peptide indicated that pTau peptide binding was strongest with the (Gly)x9 linker variant (Figure 4C), suggesting that this linker had the least functional impact on PHF1 antibody binding affinity. No binding was observed for negative control peptides #2 and #3 (Figure 4D) nor in non-PHF1 synCAR expressing cells (Figure 4E). PHF1 synCAR increases tau aggregation in hTau primary neurons

If pS396/pS404 tau species recognized by the PHF1 antibody are the synaptic proteopathic seeds driving tau aggregation propagation, then increasing the relative concentration of these tau seeds at the synapse through the surface expression of PHF1 synCAR should increase tau aggregation (Figures 5A and 5B). To test this hypothesis, we expressed PHF1 synCAR in an hTau primary neuronal tau seeding model. This seeding model uses sarkosyl-insoluble aggregated tau isolated from AD brains (AD SP) as seeds to drive human tau aggregation in neurons to closely recapitulate AD tau pathology, making it an ideal model system to evaluate the pathogenicity of synaptic tau engagement by PHF1 synCAR. As a negative control for non-specific impacts on tau aggregation by AAV synCAR expression, we also incorporated the non-tau control antibody MSL109, which recognizes cytomegalovirus (CMV), into our platform to create MSL109 synCAR. For this study, hTau neurons were transduced with AAV6-expressing PHF1 or MSL109 synCARs. Immunostaining with MC1, a misfolded tau conformation-specific antibody, was used to detect endogenous tau aggregates (Figure 5C). We observed that PHF1 synCAR-expressing neurons developed a significantly higher level of tau aggregation (∼2× increase) as compared to MSL109 synCAR-expressing neurons (Figures 5D and 5E). Interestingly, the formation of large filamentous somatic aggregates was even more prominent with PHF1 synCAR expression, where an ∼3× increase was observed compared to MSL109 synCAR (Figure 5F). As MSL109 and PHF1 synCAR had comparable levels of protein expression in neurons (Figure 5G), the observed increase in tau aggregation is unlikely to be attributed to non-specific tau uptake resulting from synCAR expression. Indeed, tau aggregation formed as early as 7 days post-seeding (DIV 17) in PHF1 synCAR-expressing neurons, and not in MSL109 synCAR-expressing neurons (Figures S3A–S3D), suggesting that PHF1 synCAR captures and subsequently increases uptake of tau seeds, accelerating tau aggregation. An increase in tau aggregates was also observed with PHF1 synCAR at AD SP seeding concentrations as low as 1 pM (Figures S4A–S4C), highlighting the sensitivity of PHF1 synCAR in accelerating tau aggregation with synaptic tau capture. Likewise, reducing the MOI used for PHF1 synCAR transduction resulted in decreased tau aggregation (Figures S4D and S4E) and total synCAR expression (Figure S4F), confirming a dose-dependent relationship between the two parameters. Given that the number of somatic aggregates increases over time27 and that somatic aggregates are indicative of a more advanced stage of disease progression,8 this data further suggests that affecting tau aggregation through PHF1 synCAR expression further expedites tau aggregation kinetics.

Next, we tested whether PHF1 synCAR would also enhance tau aggregation in the human neuronal tau seeding model. Human iPSC-neurons were transduced with AAV6 at DIV 7, followed by the addition of AD SP seeds at DIV 16. Neurons were cultured for an additional 2 weeks prior to fixation with cold methanol (Figure 5G). Consistent with findings in the hTau model, expressing PHF1 synCARs in hiPSC neurons increased total MC1-positive tau aggregates by ∼72% at 2 weeks post-seeding in the 0.5 nM AD SP dose, in contrast to the MSL109 synCAR negative control (Figures 5H and 5I). As with the hTau seeding model, the expression of MSL109 synCAR and PHF1 synCAR was not substantially different in hiPSC neurons, and would therefore not account for the observed seeding increase with PHF1 synCAR (Figure 5J). The consistently increased tau aggregation phenotype observed in the two different neuronal models strongly demonstrates PHF1 synCAR effectively captures disease-relevant tau seeds and validates its applicability for synaptic proteopathic seeds studies in both mouse and human neuronal systems.

Discussion

Major therapeutic advances have been made in the fields of cancer immunotherapy and immunology utilizing molecularly engineered cytolytic T-cells harboring CARs (i.e., CAR-T) directed toward ubiquitously expressed B-cell antigens, which are retained on tumor cells (e.g., CD19 and BCMA)36 and auto-reactive immune disorders (i.e., systemic lupus).37 However, the adoption of the CAR technology beyond aberrant cell depletion has been limited and largely unexplored. In this study, we have successfully built a modular platform for capturing synaptic proteins in their biological context and developed related analytical readout assays using the “synCAR” platform. We validated our synCAR platform’s post-synapse localization in two in vitro neuronal systems of relevance to AD research. In both cell models, we demonstrated that the addition of an scFv domain does not alter NLGN1-based targeting to the post-synaptic membrane. We defined a protocol for the in situ confirmation of synCAR’s antibody binding function using immunofluorescence imaging of biotinylated peptide antigen, enabling screening and selection of optimal linkers between the NLGN1 targeting and scFv binding domains. Together, these assays provide the basis for the extension of the synCAR platform for studying other synaptic proteins of interest.

As a proof-of-concept, we designed our synCAR platform to capture synaptic pathogenic pS396/S404 tau by incorporating the PHF1 scFv antibody fragment. Several recent studies have proposed that tau pathology spreads trans-synaptically in AD.13,27,38 However, technological limitations have prevented direct confirmation of the mechanistic link between synaptic tau accumulation and associated tau aggregation. Capture of synaptic tau by synCAR circumvents this limitation by providing a platform for direct engagement and post-synaptic concentration of pathogenic tau involved in the trans-synaptic spread of pathology. We have shown that capture of pS396/S404 tau by PHF1 synCAR increases the aggregation of endogenous tau in two in vitro seeding models, suggesting that the concentration of this hyperphosphorylated tau species at the synapse leads to accelerated tau-seeded aggregation.

The expression of PHF1 synCAR promoted both an increase in overall MC1 positive aggregate intensity and altered the timeline of aggregate formation in two neuronal models of AD pathology. In the hTau neurons, PHF1 synCAR resulted in the appearance of small amorphous aggregates that were observed as early as 1 week post addition of tau seeds, while the appearance of mature, densely packed fibrillar aggregates within the soma was more prominent in later timepoints. The appearance of mature fibrils could be attributed to either an increase in aggregate formation or accelerated aggregate maturation, both of which could be a consequence of increased uptake in tau seeds. In contrast to hTau neurons, the formation of large somatic aggregates was less prominent in the hiPSC neurons. These findings may be attributed to the early 2-week time point assessed in our hiPSC seeding assay, as previous findings using this model have required longer post-seeding time periods prior to the appearance of somatodendritic aggregates.39

Using calcium imaging, we have shown that the expression of synCAR has minimal impact on basal synaptic signaling in hTau neurons (Figures 3G–3J). Alterations of neuronal activity have been previously shown to modulate tau spreading.40 Given that neuronal activity does not increase as a result of synCAR expression, we expect the dynamics of tau spread to be preserved. Any increases in tau aggregation following PHF1 synCAR expression can therefore be attributed to direct tau capture. Though we have seen no impact to basal neuronal signaling, further exploration of downstream synaptic signaling processes, such as long-term potentiation, will be required to further detail the mechanistic impacts of synCAR expression. Additionally, the impacts of increased tau uptake via our chimeric tau antibodies on intracellular pathways are currently not known. Synaptic tau shows domain-specific interactions with cytosolic proteins involved in pre-synaptic vesicle fusion, including components of the SNARE complex,41 resulting in associated pre-synaptic dysfunction and defects in neurotransmitter release.42 Likewise, impacts on post-synaptic function via disrupted localization of the post-synaptic kinase Fyn can be attributed to synaptic tau.43 Given that the scFv domain within our PHF1 synCAR construct is not exposed to the cytosol, we do not expect that PHF1 synCAR expression would alter in the protein-protein interactions of intracellular tau aggregates.

Several open questions remain regarding the mechanistic details driving accelerated tau aggregation with synCAR remain. One mechanistic explanation is that PHF1 synCAR binds to extracellular tau seeds at the synapse, after which the bound tau-synCAR complex undergoes physiological receptor recycling, facilitating the endo-lysosomal escape of tau seeds and subsequent intracellular tau aggregation.44 Alternatively, previous studies have implicated both bulk12,40 and receptor mediated45,46,47 endocytosis involvement in tau uptake. The expression of PHF1 synCAR acts may therefore act as a molecular sink to increase the concentration of tau seeds within the synaptic cleft, facilitating bulk or receptor-mediated tau uptake as described in the literature. While our current work does not distinguish between these two mechanisms, both pathways still highlight the impact of synaptic PHF1 tau species in facilitating tau seeding.

Given the role of synaptic pS396/S404 tau in accelerating mature aggregate formation, removal of this species would likely slow down disease progression. The synCAR platform thus provides a novel method to bind these phosphorylated and pathogenic synaptic tau species to develop disease-modifying drugs. The addition of degradation modalities to the synCAR construct would therefore be of immediate therapeutic benefit. Co-opting endogenous lysosomal or proteosomal degradation machinery would enable selective depletion of pathological synaptic proteins in a spatially restricted manner through a “catch-and-degrade” strategy. Potential approaches include the addition of modules directing engagement with lysosomal targeting receptors such as the calcium-independent mannose-6-phosphate receptor (CI-M6PR)48,49,50 or transmembrane E3 ligase RNF43,51 thereby removing pathogenic tau via lysosomal degradation of the bound tau-synCAR complex.

Modification of the synCAR platform with scFvs targeting other tau phosphorylation sites could therefore be used to screen for tau antibodies likely involved in synaptic tau transmission. Of immediate interest for future studies are the additional tau phosphorylation sites identified within the post-synaptic AD fractions, including pS202 (AT8), pT231 (AT180), pT212/S214 (AT100), and pS262. In addition to AD, prion-like spreading of pathogenic proteins is a common feature in multiple neurodegenerative disorders, such as Parkinson’s disease (a-synuclein), Huntington’s disease (huntingtin), and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS; phospho-TDP43).8,52,53,54 The modular nature of the synCAR platform enables the scFv domain to be readily adapted for the isolation and identification of epitopes of interest in propagating species such as a-synuclein, huntingtin, and phospho-TDP43.

Limitations of the study

Although largely applicable to other proteins than tau, the current design of the synCAR platform is limited to the capture of proteins within the synaptic cleft, such as proteins secreted from the pre- and post-synapses or post-synaptic transmembrane proteins with an extracellular domain large enough to engage with the synCAR. Further linker optimization may also be necessary when incorporating scFvs against other proteins to create the flexibility, if needed, for target engagement, such as when capturing targets with epitopes at the extracellular surface of the post-synaptic membrane. Though we have not explored the possibility of the incorporation of a C-terminal scFv domain to NLGN1, such modification is likely to disrupt post-synapse localization, therefore limiting interaction and capture of cytosolic proteins on the intracellular face of the post-synaptic membrane. To accommodate the synCAR designs, it is important to adapt scFvs that have good antigen binding specificity and affinity, which could be reduced in conversion from a parent monoclonal antibody. Finally, this system, as currently described, is most suitable for the identification of synaptic tau species capable of seeding tau aggregation. Additional experiments focusing on cellular responses to tau aggregates resulting from PHF1 synCAR expression, such as tau interactome analysis and mitochondrial respiration, could potentially expand the model applications.

Method details

Synaptosome preparation

PSD fractions were isolated using a sucrose-gradient based method as described previously.23,24,25 Briefly, 200 mg of tissue was minced in 4 ml of 0.32 M HEPES-buffered [4mM] sucrose (HB) with protease inhibitors (cOmplete protease inhibitor and PhoSTOP phosphatase inhibitor Roche). Tissue was homogenized using a glass/PTFE pestle Potter Elvehjem tissue grinder and centrifuged at 900 x g for 10 min at 4°C to remove residual unbroken cells and nuclear material. The S1 supernatant was transferred to a new tube and spun twice at 10,000 x g for 15 min at 4°C, yielding a P2′ pellet containing the crude synaptosome fraction. The P2′ pellet was homogenized by tissue grinder in 4 mL diH2O, after which the solution was brought back to 4 mM HEPES and rotated for 30 min at 4°C to ensure complete lysis. The homogenate was centrifuged at 25,000 x g for 20 min at 4°C to yield the P3 synaptoneurosomal pellet. The P3 pellet was suspended in 1 mL HB before separation by discontinuous sucrose gradient (1.2 M, 1 M, 0.8 M sucrose in 4 mM HEPES) at 150,000 x g for 2 h at 4°C, and the synaptic plasma membrane (SPM) removed from the 1.2M/1.0M gradient. A final before centrifugation at 167,000 x g for 30 min at 4°C. The supernatant was discarded, while the pellet was resuspended in 300 μl of 50 mM HEPES/2 mM EDTA. An additional 2.7 mL of 0.54% TritonX-100 in 50 mM HEPES/2 mM EDTA was added and sample rotated at 4°C for 20 min before centrifugation at 32,000 x g for 20 min at 4°C. The supernatant was discarded, and the resulting post-synaptic fraction pellet was suspended in 50 mM HEPES/2 mM EDTA containing 0.01% SDS buffer, measured for total protein concentration for usage.

Western blot

Samples from synaptosome fractionation were prepared for Western blot analysis using LDS sample buffer (ThermoFisher) and reducing agent containing DTT (ThermoFisher) before boiling at 95°C for 5 min on a heat block. Prepared lysates were then separated by gel electrophoresis on a 4-12% NuPAGE Bis-Tris gel (ThermoFisher). The gel was transferred using the iBlot2 dry blotting system (ThermoFisher) onto a pre-activated PVDF membrane using the P0 program. The membranes were then blocked using the Intercept® blocking buffer (LI-COR Biosciences) for 1 h at RT on a shaker and stained with SV2A (abcam, Cat #32942), PSD95 (abcam, Cat #ab2723), Catalase (Cell Signaling, Cat # 12980), and Calreticulin (Cell Signaling, Cat # 12238) The membranes were incubated with the primary antibody overnight on a shaker at 4°C. Membranes were washed with 1 × 0.05% TBST and incubated with Licor IRDye™ anti-mouse (680RD) and anti-rabbit (800CW) secondary antibodies (LI-COR Biosciences, NE) at 1:5000 for 1 h at RT. Membranes were washed with 1× TBST and imaged on the Licor Odyssey CLX system (LI-COR Biosciences,). For confirmation of PHF1 scFv functionality, AD and normal brain lysates were denatured in LDS sample buffer containing DTT reducing agents and resolved on gel electrophoresis as described for PSD fractions above. Membranes were incubated in cell media (HEK293) containing secreted HA-tagged PHF1 mIgG2a or scFV overnight at 4°C, followed by TBST washes and detection using HA primary (abcam 1190, 1:1000, PRID) and anti-rabbit secondary antibodies.

Phospho-proteomics

For the postsynaptic preparation, the PSD fraction was prepared following a published protocol, resulting in two fractions: the total lysate S1 fraction (in buffer contain 320 mM sucrose, 5 mM sodium pyrophosphate protease and phosphatase inhibitors) and the PSD preparation (in 1% Triton X-100 buffer). The S1 and PSD fractions were further solubilized in 5% SDS and 50 mM TEAB (triethylammonium bicarbonate buffer, pH 7.5), reduced with 20 mM DTT, and alkylated with 40 mM iodoacetamide. Samples were then acidified by adding phosphoric acid to a final concentration of 1.2% and then mixed with six volumes of binding buffer (90% methanol, 100 mM TEAB). After mixing, the protein suspension was loaded onto micro S-trap filters (ProtiFi) and spun at 2000 g for 30 seconds to capture precipitated proteins. The filters were washed four times with the binding buffer to remove salts and detergents. Finally, 1 μg of sequencing-grade trypsin in 100 μl of digestion buffer (100 mM TEAB) was added to the S-trap filters and incubated overnight at 37°C. The peptides were eluted by sequentially adding 100 μl of elution buffer (50 mM TEAB, 0.2% formic acid, and 50% acetonitrile) and spinning at 2000 g for 1 minute. The peptide solution was pooled and lyophilized. The lyophilized peptides were further enriched for phosphopeptides through sequential enrichment using IMAC and TiO2 beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Specifically, after IMAC enrichment the flow-through and washes from the IMAC enrichment were applied to TiO2 beads for orthogonal phosphopeptide enrichment. Eluates from both steps were pooled and analyzed on an Orbitrap Tribrid LUMOS mass spectrometer. The data were analyzed by the SEQUEST search engine (Proteome Discoverer 3.0) against the reference human proteome (uniprot.org), with variable modification of phosphorylation on serine, threonine, and tyrosine, a variable modification of methionine oxidation, and a fixed carbamidomethyl modification on cysteine. The protein identifications were parsed based on maximum parsimony rules, and only protein identifications with at least one unique peptide were considered valid. The false identification rate was set at 1% as in program percolator.

Proteomics analysis

Phosphopeptide results from Proteome Discoverer were analyzed using R. Peptide abundances were normalized by variance stabilization normalization using the vsn package. Phosphopeptide abundances were tested for differential expression using limma after filtering for at least three non-missing values per condition. Based on the main interest of phosphorylation status of Tau protein, tau phosphopeptide abundances were normalized for the Tau 2N4R isoform protein abundance and differential expression was determined excluding phosphopeptides from other proteins.

Generation of AAV vectors

rAAV vectors containing post-synaptic CAR constructs expressed under the eSYN promoter were gene-synthesized and packaged into either AAV9 or AAV6 by Vector Biolabs (Malvern, PA). AAV was diluted in NMM and added to cultures at either DIV 3 for hTau primary neuronal cultures, or DIV 7 for hiPSC neurons at indicated MOIs. Viruses were washed out after 24 h and cultures maintained with partial media changes performed every 3–5 days.

Biotin-labelled peptide binding

N-terminal biotin-labelled tau peptides were purchased from New England Peptide with following sequences: pTau 390-410 S396/S404 AEIVYK(pS)PVVSGDT(pS)PRHLSN, Tau 390-410 AEIVYKSPVVSGDTSPRHLSN, and phospho scramble AEPSVR(pS)YPLNGHK(pS)VTISVD. Neurons were transduced with AAV synCAR at DIV 3, washed with a complete media change after 24 h, then maintained using bi-weekly 50% media changes. Biotin-labelled peptides were added to cells on DIV 13. Peptides were thawed on ice and diluted in fresh NMM before adding to cell cultures at a final concentration of 400 nM. Peptides were incubated on cells for 30 min at room temperature before performing 2 x 5 min washes with NMM and fixing with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). For HEK293 binding assays, 400 nM biotin-labelled tau peptides were added 48 h after transfection before performing media changes as described above.

Preparation of AD brain-derived tau seeds

For sarkosyl-insoluble seeds (AD SP), brain tissue from pathology-confirmed AD donors (Braak stage V/VI) was homogenized in 5 μL/mg TBS lysate buffer using the Precellys Evolution (CKMix, 7 mL tube, 6600 speed, 2 cycles of 20 s, 80 s pause between each cycle, 2 rounds). Sarkosyl extraction was performed as previously described.56 Briefly, brain lysates were centrifuged at 27,000 x g for 20 min at 4°C in two successive rounds. The supernatants were pooled and incubated with 1% sarkosyl solution for 1.5 h. Samples were then centrifuged at 250,000 x g for 1.5 h at 4°C. The pellet was resuspended in TBS buffer and sonicated for a total of 1:20 minutes (qSonica Q125 model; pulse on 2 s, off 1 s, 30% amplitude), then centrifuged at 100,000 x g for 1 h at 4°C. The pellet was resuspended in sterile PBS and centrifuged at 10,000 x g for 30 min at 4°C, and the supernatant was used for seeding experiments. Enriched AD SP were characterized by MSD and SIMOA to confirm tau pathology prior to use (data not shown).

Tau-aggregation assay

For hTau primary neurons, AAV expressing post-synaptic CAR was added to cells at DIV 3. The following day a complete media change was performed, and cells were allowed to mature and express synaptic CAR for another 7 days before adding AD SP tau seeds were added on DIV 10 at 0.05 nM. Tau seeds were thawed on ice prior to use and diluted in fresh NMM before adding to cell cultures. A complete media change was performed after 24 h, followed by bi-weekly 50% media changes. Cells were cultured for an additional 1–2 weeks before fixation with ice-cold methanol. For hiPSC neurons, AAV expressing post-synaptic CAR was added to cells at DIV 7. The following day a complete media change was performed, and cells were allowed to mature and express synaptic CAR before adding tau seeds at 0.5 nM on DIV 16. A complete media change was performed after 72 h, followed by bi-weekly 50% media changes. Cells were cultured for an additional 2 weeks before fixation with ice-cold methanol.

Immunofluorescence staining

hTau and hiPSC neurons were fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) and incubated at room temperature for 15 min. Cells were subsequently permeabilized in 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min, washed in PBS, wrapped in parafilm, and stored at 4 °C prior to immune-staining. For tau aggregation studies, cells were fixed in 100% MeOH at 4 °C for 10 min prior to PBS wash and storage as above. Primary antibodies were diluted in 10% BSA and incubated on cells overnight at 4°C. AlexaFluor secondary antibodies (ThermoFisher) diluted in 10% BSA with 5% NGS (1:2000) were added to cells for 1 h at room temperature while protected from light. Nuclei were detected using DAPI (1:1000 in PBS, 10 min).

Calcium imaging and analysis

AAV-expressing PHF1 synCAR was added to hTau neurons at a 10k MOI on DIV 3. The following day a complete media change was performed, following which neurons were cultured for an additional 23 days with biweekly media changes. On DIV 26, neuronal cultures were rinsed twice with BrainPhys™ Imaging Optimized Medium (StemCell Technologies), supplemented with 10% B27 Plus and Glutamax. Cal Red™ R525/650 AM (AAT Bioquest) was added to each well at a final concentration of 10μM, followed by incubation for 30 minutes at 37°C and 5% CO2. After incubation, BrainPhys™ Imaging Optimized Medium, supplemented with either 0.1% DMSO (vehicle control), 100μM 4-aminopyridine (4-AP), 100μM DNQX, or 10μM CP465022, was added and neuronal imaging was initiated immediately. Ca2+ imaging was performed using Harmony v5.2 image acquisition software (PerkinElmer) on the Opera Phenix Plus (PerkinElmer), equipped with spinning disk confocal optics, automated pipettor, and environmental control at 37°C/5% CO2. Cal Red™ R525/650 AM fluorescence was captured using 488 nm excitation (7mW), and emission was recorded from both 515-550 nm (525 nm channel) and 650-760 nm (650 nm channel). Time-lapse recordings were conducted for 2 minutes, at 3.33 frames per second (total: 400 frames), using a 20× water immersion objective (NA 1.0, WD 1.7 mm, field of view ≈ 646μm × 646μm). Images were acquired with Andor Zyla x4 sCMOS cameras (16-bit, 2160 × 2160 pixel resolution).

Image processing was performed in ImageJ (https://imagej.nih.gov/ij/): time-lapse images were converted to stacks and thresholded based on the median fluorescence intensity in either detection channel. Regions of interest (ROIs) were generated automatically using object circularity and area filters to isolate somatic Cal Red™ R525/650 AM signals. ROIs identified from the 525 nm and 650 nm emission channels were cross-validated. For each ROI, mean intensities at both wavelengths across all frames were recorded, producing raw Ca2+ traces. Ratiometric signals (525 nm/650 nm) were derived, following which ΔF/F0 values were calculated. The fluorescence baseline (F0) was determined as the mean of the first and last 100 frames of each trace. Spike events were identified using a custom Python script as local maxima in ΔF/F0 exceeding 1.5× the standard deviation of resting signal. For each neuron (ROI), amplitude was determined as the average peak ΔF/F0 value, and frequency as the number of detected spikes per minute. Network synchronicity was calculated as the proportion of ROIs with peak ΔF/F0 occurring within ±0.3-second time bins.

Fluorescence microscopy

High content immunofluorescence imaging was conducted on the Opera Phenix instrument (PerkinElmer) using Harmony (PerkinElmer) for image acquisition and analysis. Immunofluorescence stained plates were brought to room temperature before imaging to prevent image-capture errors due to condensation. For AAV expression and co-localization studies, imaging was performed using a 63× water objective and confocal settings with a 6-layer z-stack (1 mm increments). For peptide binding and tau aggregation, imaging was performed using a 40× water objective and confocal settings with a 6-layer z-stack (1 mm increments). All fluorescence images are displayed as maximum projections of the collapsed z-stack.

Quantification and statistical analysis

For AAV expression studies, post synaptic-CAR HA+ expression was quantified by using the sum of the intensity as defined by Image Region. The thresholds for HA+ intensity were determined using a negative control without AAV on the same plate. Neurite area was quantified by first defining DAPI + nuclear region, followed by neurite tree analysis using the MAP2 stain. The sum of the MAP2+ neurite area was used for toxicity analysis. For co-localization analysis, both synaptic regions defined by PSD95 + staining and post-synaptic CAR regions defined by HA+ staining were identified using the Find Spot algorithm. Synaptic co-localization was then identified by masking synaptic spots by PSD95 spots based on overlap of geometrical center. Percent of co-localization was defined as the number of co-localized spots divided by total synaptic CAR spot number. For aggregation analyses, tau aggregates were defined by MC1+ staining using the Find Spot algorithm. Fluorescence intensity thresholds were set based on no seed controls on the same plate. Somatic tau aggregates were defined by gating against the soma, as defined by the expansion of the DAPI+ nuclear area by 3 μm. Statistical analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism software. Data are represented as mean ± SD unless specified otherwise. One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s or Šídák’s PostHoc tests were used to compare more than 2 groups. Significance was defined as p < 0.05 (∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗p < 0.05). N indicates independent measures from individual wells.
