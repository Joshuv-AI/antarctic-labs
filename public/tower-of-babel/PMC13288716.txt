High-throughput signal detection of hepatotoxic drug-drug interactions in hospitalized elderly patients: an NLP-driven pharmacovigilance study
Authors: Junlong Ma, Heng Chen, Chengxian Guo, Gefei He, Guoping Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundElderly patients are highly susceptible to drug-drug interaction (DDI)-induced liver injury, yet comprehensive real-world evidence remains scarce. This study aimed to leverage a natural language processing (NLP) model to efficiently identify hepatotoxicity-associated DDI signals in this population.MethodsThis retrospective study analyzed electronic health records from 109,263 elderly inpatients. An integrated approach combining laboratory thresholds and NLP was utilized to precisely identify liver injury cases. High-throughput case-control analyses were conducted using additive and multiplicative interaction models to screen for DDI signals. To minimize false positives, time-dependent causality inference and sensitivity analyses were performed, followed by validation using in vivo animal experiments.ResultsAmong 3,227 drug combinations evaluated, 111 signals were identified, 58 of which demonstrated consistent time-dependent risk trends. Notably, among the top 20 DDI signals with the strongest associations, a marked risk elevation was observed for cardiovascular medications such as aspirin (additive: 1.16; multiplicative: 2.49), clopidogrel (additive: 1.12; multiplicative: 2.12), and atorvastatin (additive: 0.52; multiplicative: 1.88) when co-administered with the antimicrobial agent piperacillin/tazobactam. Animal experiments and sensitivity analyses further corroborated these findings.ConclusionWe established a robust NLP-based framework to efficiently evaluate DDI-induced hepatotoxicity in elderly inpatients. By identifying novel high-risk combinations, this study provides critical insights for optimizing pharmacotherapy and highlights the potential of artificial intelligence technologies in real-world pharmacovigilance study.

Introduction

Drug-drug interactions (DDIs) are a major contributor to adverse drug reactions (ADRs), accounting for 16.6% of all reported cases [1]. Elderly patients, due to age-related health decline and physiological changes, are particularly prone to DDIs. This susceptibility is compounded by the high prevalence of chronic conditions in this population, which often necessitates polypharmacy. Global estimates indicate that over 60% of older adults are prescribed five or more medications simultaneously. The risk of ADRs, especially those arising from DDIs, escalates significantly with the number of medications: the probability of ADRs increases from 13% with two medications to 58% with five medications, and up to 82% when seven or more drugs are administered concurrently [2]. In addition to the medication burden, age-associated changes in pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics amplify the vulnerability to DDI-related ADRs. As the global population continues to age, identifying high-risk drug combinations and implementing effective preventative measures to reduce ADR incidence among this vulnerable demographic becomes increasingly urgent.

Studies leveraging global pharmacovigilance data have revealed that the coadministration of certain drugs can significantly alter the hepatic safety profile of medications [3]. Large-scale population studies indicate a sixfold increase in the risk of liver injury when medications with known hepatotoxic potential are used concurrently [4]. For elderly individuals, the combination of increased medication burden and diminished hepatic detoxification and elimination capacity places them at heightened risk of liver injury [5]. Such injuries can lead to severe outcomes, including acute liver failure and, in some cases, mortality. Addressing the risks associated with DDIs is therefore a critical and unmet need in clinical practice, particularly to enable robust risk assessment and management strategies for elderly patients.

A major limitation in DDI research is the underrepresentation of elderly individuals in clinical trials. Many studies exclude patients with comorbidities or restrict concurrent medications, hindering a comprehensive understanding of DDIs. Consequently, real-world data sources like electronic medical records (EMRs), which document polypharmacy and clinical care, have become invaluable for studying DDI-related liver injuries. However, utilizing EMR data for DDI analysis presents challenges, as critical ADR information often resides in unstructured clinical notes, and methods for its extraction are underdeveloped. Relying on diagnostic codes or lab results often underestimates ADR prevalence, as these capture less than half of ADRs identified through detailed medical record reviews [6]. Furthermore, analyzing DDIs in complex regimens is resource-intensive, requiring significant computational power. This underscores the need for automated methods to enhance detection efficiency. With advancements in machine learning, these technologies are increasingly applied in healthcare research [7]. Our team has developed a Chinese natural language processing (NLP) model to extract ADR data from clinical notes, enabling detection of liver injury-associated medications [8,9]. Building on this, an automated iterative computational framework could facilitate rapid identification of DDI combinations linked to liver injury in elderly patients.

In light of the rapidly aging global population and the substantial impact of DDI-induced hepatotoxicity, there is a critical need for comprehensive DDI analyses specifically targeting hospitalized elderly patients. To bridge this gap, this study utilizes an NLP framework to efficiently extract liver injury cases. We subsequently employed a high-throughput case-control design to identify in-hospital drug combinations that exacerbate the risk of liver injury. Drawing upon massive real-world data from over 100,000 elderly patients, we aim to provide evidence-based recommendations for optimizing pharmacotherapeutic regimens, thereby minimizing the risk of DDI-related adverse hepatic events in this vulnerable population.

Methods

Study population and cohort construction

The EMR dataset used in this study comprises comprehensive hospitalization data from the First Hospital of Changsha between January 2021 and April 2024, including diagnoses, medications, laboratory tests, clinical notes, and demographics. Among 198,298 patients, 109,263 individuals aged ≥60 years were included for analysis (Figure 1). The primary unit of analysis was the hospitalization episode. For patients with multiple admissions during the study period, each admission was treated as a discrete and independent observation to accurately capture the specific medication exposure and the corresponding clinical outcomes. All data were anonymized before analysis.

Flow chart of the study design.

Exclusion criteria were: 1) hospitalization <48 h (n = 4,716); 2) ICU admission (n = 10,419); 3) pre-existing liver diseases, including viral, autoimmune, alcoholic, or neoplastic etiologies (n = 6,438); 4) liver injury detected within 48 h of admission via NLP (n = 515); and 5) missing or abnormal liver function results within 48 h (n = 14,013).

The NLP model utilized for identifying liver injury cases was previously developed and validated within our institution. For the specific task of hepatotoxicity detection, the model demonstrated high diagnostic accuracy with a precision of 90.97%, a recall of 95.97%, and an F1-score of 93.35%. Furthermore, external validation yielded an F1-score of 95.10%, confirming the model’s robustness and generalizability.

Liver injury was identified through combined laboratory and NLP criteria. According to the criteria established by the International DILI Expert Working Group [10], laboratory thresholds were defined as ≥5-fold ALT elevation, ≥2-fold ALP or bilirubin elevation with ≥3-fold ALT rise. Using this integrated approach, 718 new inpatient cases of hepatic injury were identified.

DDI signal screening

Two-drug combinations were analyzed if both drugs had ≥1% usage frequency, were co-administered in ≥100 patients, and associated with ≥5 liver injury cases. In total, 3,227 combinations met these criteria. DDI signals were evaluated using logistic regression under additive and multiplicative models [11,12]: Log risk of the event = β0+ β1DrugA + β2DrugB + β3DrugA∗DrugB + baseline covariates Additive effect = expβ1 +β2 + β3− expβ1− expβ2+ 1 Multiplicative effect = expβ1 +β2 + β3/ exp β1* expβ2 = expβ3

Significance was defined as a 95% CI of OR (expβ3) > 1 for multiplicative effects, and for additive models, a 95% bootstrap CI (1,000 resamples) with the lower bound > 0. Only combinations significant in both models were retained. Models were adjusted for age, sex, alcohol use, observation period, and total unique drug exposures. To assess time-dependent risk, co-exposure duration was categorized into four groups: none, 1–2 days, 3–6 days, and >7 days. Drug pairs lacking a positive duration–response trend in hepatic injury incidence were excluded.

Experimental animals and treatment

Male C57BL/6J mice (12–14 weeks, 28–32 g) were obtained from Hunan SJA Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. They were housed in a temperature-controlled facility (22 ± 2 °C) on a 12-hour light–dark schedule, with free access to water and chow. After one week of acclimatization, mice were randomly assigned to six groups (n = 6): control, aspirin (50 mg/kg), clopidogrel (37.5 mg/kg), piperacillin/tazobactam (2025 mg/kg), aspirin + piperacillin/tazobactam, and clopidogrel + piperacillin/tazobactam. Aspirin and clopidogrel were administered daily via oral gavage, while piperacillin/tazobactam was administered via intraperitoneal injection. The treatments were continued for 14 consecutive days. Dosages were justified via human equivalent dose conversion to reflect clinical therapeutic levels. After a 12 h fast on the final day, mice were euthanized by rapid cervical dislocation. Liver and blood samples were collected for biochemical and histopathological assessment. The animal experiments and procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee on Animal Experimentation of Hunan SJA Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd (IACUC-SJA202409601). Liver tissues were fixed in 10% formaldehyde, paraffin-embedded, sectioned (5–6 μm), and stained with hematoxylin and eosin for microscopic evaluation.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using Python (version 3.10). Baseline characteristics were evaluated using the ‘scipy’ package. Continuous variables were expressed as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR), while categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages. For continuous variables with normal distributions, independent group t-tests were employed, whereas non-normally distributed data were analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were analyzed using chi-square tests.

To ensure comparability, exact matching of core covariates—including 25 medication-related and 25 diagnosis-related variables closely associated with the outcomes—was performed. Additional covariates included baseline characteristics such as sex, age, length of hospital stay, and the number of medication exposures. Propensity score matching (PSM) was performed using the greedy nearest-neighbor method with a caliper of 0.02. Covariate balance before and after matching was assessed using the Standardized Mean Difference (SMD), with an SMD value >0.1 indicating imbalance. Subgroup analyses were also conducted to evaluate variations in DDI signals across common comorbid conditions in the elderly population, including coronary artery disease, cerebral infarction, diabetes, and hypertension.

Results

The median ages of the two groups were 72 years (IQR: 66–79) and 71 years (IQR: 66–77), respectively. Compared with controls, the case group contained more males, had longer hospital stays, and was exposed to more unique drugs. No significant differences were observed in major comorbidities common among elderly populations. However, the liver injury group showed a lower prevalence of hyperlipidemia, accompanied by lower cholesterol and triglyceride levels. Baseline glucose and international normalized ratio (INR) were slightly elevated, while liver function indicators, though higher than controls, remained within normal ranges.

Vivo animal experiments

To validate the DDI signals, two combinations—piperacillin/tazobactam-aspirin (combination 1) and piperacillin/tazobactam-clopidogrel (combination 2)—were selected for in vivo testing. Histopathological analysis of liver samples stained with hematoxylin and eosin (Figure 5A) revealed focal hepatocellular necrosis, nuclear fragmentation, and dissolution, accompanied by mild inflammatory infiltration in the piperacillin/tazobactam-aspirin group. Hepatic microvesicular steatosis, cytoplasmic lipid droplets, hepatocellular edema, and cytoplasmic pallor were also observed under the microscope. Figure 5B showed a significant increase in liver organ coefficient, indicating edema and inflammation. Biochemical analysis (Figure 5C) revealed elevated AST levels, suggesting impaired liver function, though ALT, Tbili, and albumin levels did not significantly differ between combination and monotherapy groups.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study employing NLP techniques to comprehensively investigate DDI-induced hepatotoxicity in hospitalized elderly patients. We analyzed the potential hepatic injury risks associated with over 3,000 commonly prescribed drug combinations among more than 73,000 elderly inpatients and identified a series of novel potential DDI-related hepatotoxicity signals. Through time-dependent causality analysis, we refined and filtered these signals, bolstering the robustness of our findings. Among the top 20 DDI signals with the strongest association, we highlighted a notable risk elevation linked to cardiovascular medications such as aspirin, clopidogrel, and atorvastatin when combined with the antimicrobial agent piperacillin/tazobactam. Subgroup analyses and propensity score matching further supported these findings. Additionally, external animal experiments corroborated our results.

Current studies on hepatotoxicity based on EMRs primarily rely on laboratory findings and/or diagnostic codes [13], identifying cases that account for less than 1% of the population. This low detection rate is partly attributed to the reversible and minor elevations in aminotransferases (termed adaptation) that characterize many instances of drug-induced liver injury [14]. Traditional laboratory thresholds are no longer considered sufficient to detect the full spectrum of hepatotoxicity cases. Leveraging an NLP model combined with automated standardization algorithms [8,9], our study efficiently identified hidden hepatotoxicity cases within clinical documentation while incorporating conventional laboratory test indicators, thereby mitigating the risk of underestimating hepatotoxicity. Specifically, NLP identified 507 cases out of 718 (70.6%), laboratory tests identified 264 cases (36.8%), and both methods jointly identified 53 cases (7.4%).

Moreover, previous research has predominantly focused on single-drug analyses, lacking large-scale evaluations of DDIs contributing to hepatotoxicity in hospitalized patients. Our interaction analysis model rapidly assessed over 3,000 drug combinations. Additionally, failure to account for temporal relationships in DDI detection could inflate false-positive signals [15]. Temporal factors are critical for establishing the pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic relevance of drug interactions, as the co-exposure duration between two drugs significantly impacts the biological plausibility of the interaction. Ignoring these temporal variables risks compromising DDI detection accuracy, leading to false-positive or false-negative findings. Our study addressed this limitation by correlating the duration of co-exposure with the incidence of hepatotoxicity, filtering out 45% of potential false-positive signals and enhancing the robustness of our results.

In elderly populations, cholestatic liver injury emerges as the predominant type of hepatotoxicity, with a higher prevalence among males. Our findings also indicate a higher proportion of male patients among hepatotoxicity cases. Although the underlying mechanisms remain unclear, sex hormones are hypothesized to play a role in liver function regulation. Notably, we observed a lower prevalence of hyperlipidemia among hepatotoxicity cases, alongside reduced cholesterol and triglyceride levels—findings that contradict existing studies [16,17]. This discrepancy may stem from the presence of multiple comorbidities in elderly inpatients, some of which are significant risk factors for hepatotoxicity. Mediation analysis reveals that the impact of hyperlipidemia on hepatotoxicity is largely modulated by intermediary effects, such as coexisting potential risk factors for liver injury, including hypoalbuminemia or anemia [18]. Previous studies have demonstrated that elderly individuals with anemia exhibit lower levels of albumin and cholesterol, alongside higher rates of lipid catabolism compared to non-anemic individuals [19]. Additionally, hypoalbuminemia, indicative of poor nutritional status, accounts for its association with lipid levels and its role in modulating lipid synthesis, transport, and metabolism [20]. Accordingly, the mediating effect of comorbidities partially explains the lower prevalence of hyperlipidemia among patients with hepatotoxicity, while underscoring the complexity of hepatotoxicity mechanisms in elderly patients.

The prevalence of multimorbidity increases sharply with age, with nearly 80% of individuals aged 65 or older having at least two chronic conditions [21]. These comorbidities influence DDI-related risks. In our subgroup analysis, diabetic patients demonstrated a heightened susceptibility to DDI-related hepatotoxicity, with significantly elevated baseline glucose levels in the hepatotoxicity group. Previous research has suggested that diabetes mellitus exacerbates the severity and incidence of DILI. The Spanish DILI Registry identified diabetes mellitus as a factor that impedes liver recovery, resulting in chronic outcomes [22]. Similarly, a large prospective analysis by Chalasani et al. reported a higher prevalence of diabetes mellitus among severe DILI cases [23]. Potential mechanisms involve hyperglycemia-induced oxidative stress, increased pro-inflammatory activity, and insulin resistance, culminating in liver inflammation, fibrosis, and steatosis [24]. Consequently, elderly patients with hyperglycemia face elevated hepatotoxicity risks when exposed to high-risk drug combinations.

Among the top 20 DDI risk combinations, cardiovascular drugs like aspirin, clopidogrel, and atorvastatin were found to significantly increase hepatotoxicity risk when combined with piperacillin/tazobactam, a frequently prescribed antibiotic in hospitalized elderly patients. These findings warrant particular attention due to the high prescription rates of these drug combinations in this population. Studies from the French pharmacovigilance database and the European OPERAM trial similarly identified antithrombotic agents as a common class of DDI-related drugs [25,26]. A Korean retrospective study involving 256,598 patients reported piperacillin/tazobactam as the most frequent cause of DILI during hospitalization [27]. In China, approximately two-thirds of hospitalized patients receive antibiotic therapy, with overuse being a pervasive issue linked to increased hepatotoxicity risk [28].

Mechanistically, age-related impairment of the intestinal epithelial barrier leads to increased gut permeability, allowing pro-inflammatory bacterial products to enter the bloodstream, triggering chronic immune activation and immune-mediated liver injury. Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) exacerbate gut permeability, altering gut microbiota composition [29]. Similarly, statins have been shown to modify gut microbiota and bile acid metabolism [30]. Frequent antibiotic use in elderly patients also heightens the risk of dysbiosis, which, in combination with NSAIDs or statins, further increases gut permeability, bacterial translocation, and susceptibility to DILI. Alterations in gut microbiota, in turn, influence systemic drug exposure and amplify DDI risks. For instance, antibiotic-induced dysbiosis was found to increase systemic exposure to clopidogrel’s active metabolites in rats [31].

On a cellular level, mitochondria are a common target of DILI. Aging impacts both mitochondrial quantity and quality, leading to reduced mitochondrial DNA copy numbers and accumulation of heteroplasmy [32]. Consequently, elderly patients exposed to mitochondria-targeting drugs (which directly impair mitochondrial function) face an elevated risk of hepatocyte injury. The genetic and structural similarities between mitochondria and bacteria may explain the high incidence of antibiotic-induced DILI [33]. Aspirin, in addition to cyclooxygenase inhibition, directly targets mitochondria to induce cell death. Clopidogrel’s active metabolites promote mitochondrial damage through reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, while atorvastatin induces mitochondrial dysfunction by inhibiting the Nrf2 pathway [34]. Consequently, the co-administration of these cardiovascular drugs with antibiotics may enhance hepatotoxicity by impairing mitochondrial function. Our in vivo animal experiments further confirmed this finding, as co-administration of aspirin and piperacillin/tazobactam significantly exacerbated liver injury in mice. Although we observed an isolated elevation in AST without a significant concomitant increase in ALT, this biochemical profile may reflect the specific subcellular targets of the drugs involved. Aspirin is known to induce mitochondrial toxicity, which may lead to the preferential release of the mitochondrial isoenzyme of AST prior to massive cytosolic ALT release. Furthermore, the relatively short duration of exposure and moderate injury severity in our experimental setting may have limited the extent of cytolytic damage required to elicit significant ALT elevation.

This study has several limitations. First, as a retrospective analysis, it is inherently subject to selection bias and incomplete data availability, despite the large cohort size and strict inclusion criteria. Missing cases due to incomplete records may have influenced the representativeness of the study population. Second, the study predominantly focused on a Chinese population, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other ethnic groups. Third, the most significant limitation is that systematic causality assessment using the 2016 updated Roussel Uclaf Causality Assessment Method (RUCAM) was not performed [35]. The RUCAM remains the global gold standard for DILI causality assessment in clinical practice. However, its strict application was not feasible in our retrospective, high-throughput cohort due to the inherent lack of comprehensive exclusionary data that are uniformly required for an accurate RUCAM score. To partially compensate for the lack of formal RUCAM assessment, we addressed causality through sensitivity analysis, time-dependent causality inference, and in vivo animal validation to ensure the robustness of the identified DDI signals. Fourth, further mechanistic experimental studies are needed to bridge the gap between statistical signals and biological plausibility. Fifth, a methodological limitation persists regarding our in vivo validation due to the biochemical contradiction of an isolated AST elevation. We acknowledge that in C57BL/6J mice, ALT is recognized as a far more specific and sensitive biomarker for hepatocellular damage. This lack of ALT elevation limits the biochemical confirmation of severe, widespread liver injury. Moreover, although efforts were made to minimize confounding, potential extrahepatic contributions to AST elevation (such as subclinical muscle injury) cannot be entirely excluded. Additionally, important variables such as outpatient drug exposure, environmental factors, and lifestyle variables were not captured and should be explored in future research.

Conclusion

In conclusion, by integrating NLP models with interaction analysis algorithms, we established an efficient framework to comprehensively evaluate DDI-related hepatotoxicity in hospitalized elderly patients. Through time-dependent causal inference and rigorous sensitivity analyses, we identified novel DDI signals from a screening of over 3,000 drug combinations. These findings significantly expand the current evidence base regarding DDI risks in the geriatric population and offer valuable insights for optimizing combinatorial pharmacotherapy in clinical settings. Notably, our study underscores the pivotal role of AI technologies in advancing real-world pharmacovigilance. Further prospective studies and mechanistic investigations are warranted to validate these findings.
