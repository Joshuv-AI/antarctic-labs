A land of monotone plenty, bis repetita : from classical to weak optimal transport
Virginie Ehrlacher, Rodrigue Lelotte, Luca Nenna

Abstract
. The celebrated c c -cyclical monotonicity property is shown to boil down to the zeroth-order optimality condition for the optimal transport problem. More precisely, we show that optimality is equivalent to the non-negativity of the linear transport cost functional on the radial cone of admissible perturbations. We then utilise this point of view to extend the c c -cyclical monotonicity property to the weak optimal transport problem, for which it corresponds to the first-order optimality condition, namely to the non-negativity of the linearisation of the weak transport cost functional near the optimiser. Altogether, this sheds new light on this monotonicity concept. For both classical and weak optimal transport, we show that this property characterises (under suitable assumptions) optimal transport plans. In the classical case, we recover known results of the literature but with revisited proofs.



1.
Introduction

The notion of
c
-cyclical monotonicity
occupies a central role in optimal transport theory. A transport plan is said to be
c
-cyclically monotone if, in essence, local re-routing of the mass on the support of the transport plan leads to an increase of the overall cost of transport. Under suitable assumptions, this property is both necessary and sufficient for optimality. We refer the reader to the recent survey of De Pascale, Kausamo, and Wyczesany
[
14
]
for a broad overview of the subject.

1.1.
Contributions

The purpose of the present work is twofold. First, we revisit cyclical monotonicity from a perturbative perspective based on elementary optimality considerations. In particular, we show that this property characterises under suitable assumptions optimal transport plans. Although we recover known results of the literature, the proofs are revisited in light of our approach. Second, building on this approach, we extend the notion of
c
-cyclical monotonicity to the case of the
weak
optimal transport problem by introducing a notion of monotonicity which, contrary to previously existing notions in the literature, genuinely recovers the classical
c
-cyclical monotonicity property as a special case.

1.2.
The gist of it

Given two Polish spaces
X
and
Y
, two probability measures
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(X)
and
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(Y)
, and a Borel cost function
c:X\times Y\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
, the classical optimal transport problem reads

\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\mathscr{C}(\pi),\qquad\mathscr{C}(\pi):=\int_{X\times Y}c(x,y)\,\pi(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y),

where
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
denotes the set of transport plans with marginals
\mu
and
\nu
. Our starting point is deceptively simple. If
\pi
is an optimal transport plan and if
\eta
is an admissible perturbation preserving the marginal constraints, that is a signed Radon measure
\eta\in\mathcal{M}(X\times Y)
so that
\pi+t\eta\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
for
t>0
small enough, then it obviously holds

\mathscr{C}(\pi+t\eta)\geq\mathscr{C}(\pi).

This is just the
zeroth-order optimality condition
. In the classical optimal transport problem, the cost functional is linear, and therefore optimality is equivalent to the non-negativity condition

\mathscr{C}(\eta)\geq 0

for every admissible perturbation
\eta
. Now, it will follow from elementary manipulations that the
c
-cyclical monotonicity exactly rephrases as demanding that
\mathscr{C}
be non-negative for a special kind of “cyclical” perturbations
\eta
.
The gist of our work is to show that
\mathscr{C}
being non-negative on the set made of these special perturbations is sufficient for it to be non-negative over the entire set of admissible perturbations and vice-versa
, thus yielding sufficiency and necessity of
c
-cyclical monotonicity for optimality in the optimal transport problem. This gives a new and conceptually transparent route to the equivalence between optimality and cyclical monotonicity. Moreover, the same approach can be used to derive the analogue of
c
-cyclical monotonicity in other types of optimal transport problems.

Indeed, a central contribution of the paper concerns the weak optimal transport problem. We recall that this problem reads

\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int_{X}C(x,\pi_{x})\,\mu(\mathrm{d}x),

where
(\pi_{x})_{x\in X}
denotes the disintegration of
\pi
with respect to its first marginal and the function
C:X\times\mathcal{P}(Y)\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
is (typically) convex in its second argument.

A notion coined as
C
-monotonicity
was introduced in the literature by Backhoff-Veraguas, Beiglböck, and Pammer
[
2
]
and shown to be necessary and sufficient for optimality under suitable assumptions. However, this notion does not fully recover classical cyclical monotonicity when the weak transport problem reduces to the ordinary optimal transport problem (see Example
2

infra
). One of the contributions of the present work is to introduce a notion of monotonicity for weak optimal transport, which we call
C
-cyclical monotonicity
, that genuinely extends the classical notion of
c
-cyclical monotonicity to the weak setting. This notion is formulated in terms of the gradients (or subgradients) of the weak cost with respect to its measure argument. Concretely, if
\rho\mapsto C(x,\rho)
is differentiable in an appropriate sense (see Section
3.1.1
), then our monotonicity condition takes the form

\boxed{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nabla_{\rho}C(x_{i},\pi_{x_{i}})(y_{i})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nabla_{\rho}C(x_{i},\pi_{x_{i}})(y_{i+1}).}

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and all families
(x_{1},y_{1}),\dots,(x_{n},y_{n})\in\Gamma
where
\Gamma\subset X\times Y
is a measurable set so that
\pi(\Gamma)=1
. Here, we let
y_{n+1}:=y_{1}
. This property enjoys several important features. First, it exactly recovers the classical
c
-cyclical monotonicity when the weak problem boils down to the classical one, namely when the cost of transport is given by

C(x,\rho):=\int_{Y}c(x,y)\,\rho(\mathrm{d}y).

Indeed, in this case the gradient is given by
\nabla_{\rho}C(x,\rho)(y)=c(x,y)
, and one recognises that the above condition is exactly the
c
-cyclical monotonicity property. Second, we show that
C
-cyclical monotonicity is equivalent to optimality under assumptions comparable to those used in the classical setting.

Our
C
-cyclical monotonicity property is derived by looking at the
first-order optimality condition
for the weak optimal transport. Indeed, the objective of this problem is

\mathscr{C}(\pi):=\int_{X}C(x,\pi_{x})\mu(\mathrm{d}x),

which, contrary to the classical optimal transport problem, is no longer linear, so we need to go to the first-order to say anything meaningful. Whereas in the classical problem, optimality is encoded by the non-negativity of the cost on admissible perturbations, in the weak setting, the same mechanism survives after replacing the transport cost by the linearised quantity

\eta\mapsto\int_{X\times Y}\nabla_{\rho}C(x,\pi_{x})(y)\,\eta(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y).

The
C
-cyclical monotonicity introduced above is then derived from the non-negativity of the above functional once evaluated at these special “cyclical” perturbations already mentioned above.

1.3.
Previous works

It is possible that part of our arguments
may
not be completely new to some readers already familiar with the subject. In a way, a part of what we present in this work can be seen as a way of explaining what cyclical monotonicity is all about in a different perspective with a different terminology, thus shedding a new kind of light on this concept. Whether or not these ideas are known to specialists, they are certainly not widely familiar to a broader audience. During our investigation, we came across a couple of works of S. Bianchini and L. Caravenna
[
10
,
9
]
which share some common features with our own work here and that should be readily advertised. We shall also mention the work of
[
7
]
which utilises the ergodic theorem to prove sufficiency of the
c
-cyclical monotone property for optimality somehow in the same way as we do. Also – as the title of our paper is obviously a nod to this work — we shall also mention the seminal paper
[
5
]
— and also
[
21
]
. However, the unified formalism presented here and its use as a systematic recipe to derive the monotonicity property to other type of optimal transports is decidedly new — at least to the best of our knowledge.

1.4.
Outline of the paper

Section
2
revisits the
c
-cyclical monotonicity property in the classical optimal transport through the perturbative point of view. We introduce the radial cone of admissible perturbations and show that optimality is equivalent to the zeroth-order optimality condition on this cone. We then relate this condition to finite optimality and to
c
-cyclical monotonicity. Section
3
is devoted to weak optimal transport. We derive the first-order optimality conditions for weak transport problems, and use them to define the notion of
C
-cyclical monotonicity and establish its equivalence with optimality under suitable assumptions. We also discuss the case of the barycentric cost (which is non-differentiable).

2.
A perturbative approach to the classical optimal transport

2.1.
Setting and notations

For a Polish space
Z
, we write
\mathcal{M}(Z)
the set of finite (and signed) Radon measures on
Z
. By a
finite
Radon measure
\eta
, it is meant that
\|\eta\|_{TV}<\infty
where the total variation norm
\|\eta\|_{TV}
is defined as
\|\eta\|_{TV}:=|\eta|(Z)
where
|\eta|:=\eta_{+}+\eta_{-}
with
\eta_{+}
and
\eta_{-}
being respectively the positive and negative parts of the signed measure
\mu
. The space
\mathcal{M}(Z)
is equipped with the
vague topology
, that is in duality with
C_{0}(Z)
,
i.e.
the set of real-valued continuous functions on
Z
vanishing at infinity — when
Z
is compact, this set is simply the set of all continuous functions. We recall that finite radii balls
\{\eta\in\mathcal{M}(Z):|\eta|(Z)\leq r\}
for
0\leq r<\infty
are metrisable in this topology provided that
Z
is Polish and furthermore locally compact, see
e.g.

[
12
, Theorem 3.29]
. Finally, we write
\mathcal{P}(Z)\subset\mathcal{M}(Z)
be the subset of probability measures.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(X)
and
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(Y)
be probability measures on Polish (and locally compact) spaces
X
and
Y
, and let
c:X\times Y\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be a Borel-measurable cost of transport possibly assuming
+\infty
as value. We consider the classical optimal transport problem

(OT)

\min_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\mathscr{C}(\pi),\qquad\mathscr{C}(\pi):=\int_{X\times Y}c(x,y)\,\pi(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y)

where
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
is the standard set of
transport plans
, that is those probability measures
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(X\times Y)
so that the first marginal of
\pi
(
i.e.
with respect to
X
) is
\mu
and such that the second marginal of
\pi
(
i.e.
with respect to
Y
) is
\nu
. This also rewrites
{\rm pr}_{1}^{\sharp}\pi=\mu
and
{\rm pr}_{2}^{\sharp}\pi=\nu
where
{\rm pr}_{1}:X\times Y\to X
and
{\rm pr}_{2}:X\times Y\to Y
are the canonical surjections of the Cartesian product
X\times Y
onto its factors
X
and
Y
— and where we use the standard notation
f^{\sharp}\pi
for any measurable map
f:X\times Y\to Z
to mean the push-foward measure defined as
f^{\sharp}\pi(A):=\pi(f^{-1}(A))
for all Borel sets
B\subset Z
.

2.2.
The zeroth-order optimality condition for (
OT
)

We start with the following simple observation. If
\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
is optimal for the optimal transport problem (
OT
) and
\pi
has finite transport cost, meaning that
\mathscr{C}(\pi)<\infty
, then it must be that
\mathscr{C}(\pi+t\eta)\geq\mathscr{C}(\pi)
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
and
\eta\in\mathcal{M}(X\times Y)
such that
\pi+t\eta
is still an admissible transport plan, that is
\pi+t\eta\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
. By linearity of the cost functional
\mathscr{C}
, this is equivalent to saying that
\mathscr{C}(\eta)\geq 0
for such perturbations
\eta
provided that
t>0
. Let us then introduce for
\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
the set
\mathcal{R}_{\pi}\subset\mathcal{M}(X\times Y)
defined as

(1)

\boxed{\mathcal{R}_{\pi}:=\bigg\{\eta\in\mathcal{M}(X\times Y):\pi+t\eta\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)\text{ for some }t>0\bigg\}.}

Then, the previous statement rephrases as follows:
If
\pi
is optimal for the optimal transport problem (
OT
) and has finite transport cost, that is
\mathscr{C}(\pi)<\infty
, then
\mathscr{C}(\eta)\geq 0
for all
\eta\in\mathcal{R}_{\pi}
. In the context of convex analysis, the set
\mathcal{R}_{\pi}
is referred to as the
radial cone
to the (convex) set
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
at the point
\pi
. We refer to Remark
1
and (
3
)
infra
for a general definition of the radial cone to a convex set. Furthermore, the converse statement holds as well:
If
\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
is such that
\mathscr{C}
is non-negative on
\mathcal{R}_{\pi}
, then
\pi
is optimal in (
OT
)
— again, provided that
\mathscr{C}(\pi)<\infty
. Indeed, for all
\gamma\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
, one has
\mathscr{C}(\gamma)=\mathscr{C}(\gamma-\pi+\pi)=\mathscr{C}(\gamma-\pi)+\mathscr{C}(\pi)\geq\mathscr{C}(\pi)
since
\eta:=\gamma-\pi
is obviously an element of
\mathcal{R}_{\pi}
. Thus
\pi
is optimal. Altogether, we obtain the following proposition which is just the
zeroth-order optimality condition
for (
OT
):

Proposition 1
.

A transport plan
\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
with finite cost
\mathscr{C}(\pi)<\infty
is optimal in (
OT
) if and only if
\mathscr{C}(\eta)\geq 0
for all
\eta\in\mathcal{R}_{\pi}
, where
\mathcal{R}_{\pi}
is the radial cone to the feasible set
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
at the point
\pi
as defined in (
1
).

Remark 1
.

Proposition
1
is just one particular instance of a much general situation. Let
X
be a normed vector space,
L:X\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be a linear function on
X
and
C\subset X
be a convex set. Let us consider the minimisation problem

(2)

\min_{x\in C}Lx.

Then,
x\in C
is optimal for (
2
) if and only if (provided that
Lx<\infty
)
Lh\geq 0
for all
h\in R_{C}(x)
, where the radial cone
R_{C}(x)\subset X
(“to the convex set
C
at the point
x
”) is defined as

(3)

\mathcal{R}_{C}(x):=\left\{h\in X:x+th\in C\text{ for some $t>0$}\right\}.

(We remark passing by that by convexity of
C
, if there exists
t_{0}>0
such that
x+t_{0}h\in C
then
x+th\in C
for all
0\leq t\leq t_{0}
.) Now, let us assume that
x
is optimal, and let
h\in\mathcal{R}_{C}(x)
. Then, there exists a small enough
t>0
such that
x+th\in C
. By optimality of
x
(and the fact that
Lx<\infty
), we have
L(x+th)\geq L(x)
and thus
L(h)\geq 0
by linearity of
L
. The other direction is also straightforward. Indeed, assume that
L(h)\geq 0
for all
h\in\mathcal{R}_{C}(x)
. Then for all
y\in C
, we have
L(y)=L(x)+L(y-x)\geq L(x)
since
y-x\in\mathcal{R}_{C}(x)
, and thus
x
is optimal for the problem (
2
).

Let us now characterise the radial cone
\mathcal{R}_{\pi}
defined above in (
1
) explicitly. We have:

Lemma 1

(Characterisation of the radial cone
\mathcal{R}_{\pi}
)
.

Let any
\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
. The radial cone
\mathcal{R}_{\pi}
to the set of transport plan
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
at the point
\pi
— as defined in (
1
) — is characterised as the set of
signed
Radon measure
\eta=\eta^{+}-\eta^{-}
such that
\eta^{+}
and
\eta^{-}
share common marginals, that is
{\rm pr}_{i}^{\sharp}\eta^{+}={\rm pr}_{i}^{\sharp}\eta^{-}
for
i=1,2
, and such that
\eta^{-}\ll\pi
and
\frac{d\eta^{-}}{d\pi}\in L^{\infty}(\pi)
.

Here, we write
\eta^{-}
the negative part of
\eta
and correspondingly
\eta^{+}
its non-negative part. We use the standard notation
\eta^{-}\ll\pi
to mean that
\eta^{-}
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\pi
, and we write
\frac{\mathrm{d}\eta^{-}}{\mathrm{d}\pi}
the Radon-Nykodym density of
\eta^{-}
with respect to
\pi
. We remark that, in Lemma
1
, the fact that
\eta^{+}
and
\eta^{-}
share common marginals also rephrases as
{\rm pr}_{i}^{\sharp}\eta=0
for
i=1,2
, that is the (signed) measure
\eta
has
zero
marginals.

Proof of Lemma
1
.

Let
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{\pi}\subset\mathcal{M}(X\times Y)
be the set

(4)

\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{\pi}=\left\{\eta\in\mathcal{M}(X\times Y)\,\middle|\,{\rm pr}_{i}^{\sharp}\eta=0\text{ for }i=1,2,\quad\frac{\mathrm{d}\eta^{-}}{\mathrm{d}\pi}\in L^{\infty}(\pi)\right\}.

— where it is implicitly understood that
\eta^{-}\ll\pi
. Let us show that
\mathcal{R}_{\pi}=\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{\pi}
, which is exactly the thesis of Lemma
1
. Let
\eta\in\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{\pi}
and let us define
\pi_{t}:=\pi+t\eta
. We need to show that for a small enough
t>0
, the measure
\pi_{t}
is in the set of transport plans
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
. This means showing that
\pi_{t}
is positive and that it verifies the marginal constraints – note that, if we show these two properties hold, it will immediately follow that this measure is in fact a
probability
measure. Let us decompose
\eta:=\eta^{+}-\eta^{-}
. By assumption, the negative part
\eta^{-}
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\pi
and its Radon-Nykodym density is in
L^{\infty}(\pi)
. Therefore,
\pi_{t}
is positive for all
0\leq t\leq t_{0}
where we define
t_{0}^{-1}:=\|\frac{d\eta^{-}}{d\pi}\|_{L^{\infty}(\pi)}>0
. Now, the fact that
\pi_{t}
verifies the marginal constraints is immediate from the fact that
{\rm pr}_{i}^{\sharp}\eta=0
for all
i=1,2
. This shows that
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{\pi}\subset\mathcal{R}_{\pi}
. We now prove the converse inclusion. Let
\eta\in\mathcal{R}_{\pi}
. Then, there exists a (small enough)
t>0
such that
\pi_{t}\in\Pi
, where we let
\pi_{t}
as above. This (again) immediately implies that
{\rm pr}_{i}^{\sharp}\eta=0
for all
i=1,2
. Furthermore, because
\pi_{t}
must be a positive measure, it must be that
t\eta^{-}(A)\leq\pi(A)
for all Borel sets
A\subset X\times Y
, which means that
\eta^{-}
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\pi
and that its Radon-Nykodym density is uniformly bounded with respect to
\pi
— and moreover
\|\frac{d\eta^{-}}{d\pi}\|_{L^{\infty}(\pi)}\leq t^{-1}
. Hence
\mathcal{R}_{\pi}=\mathcal{R}_{\pi}^{\prime}
and the thesis of Lemma
1
follows.

2.3.
On
c
-cyclical monotonicity and its related notions

In this section, let us recall what
c
-cyclical monotonicity is all about and how it can be related to the perturbative approach outlined above. First, let us recall the mere definition of
c
-cyclical monotonicity:

Definition 1
.

A set
\Gamma\subset X\times Y
is said to be
c
-cyclically monotone
if for all couples of points
(x_{1},y_{1}),\dots,(x_{n},y_{n})
in
\Gamma
— where
n\in\mathbb{N}
is arbitrary (but finite) — we have

(5)

\sum_{i=1}^{n}c(x_{i},y_{i})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}c(x_{i},y_{i+1}),

where we let
y_{n+1}:=y_{1}
. A transport plan
\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
is said to be
c
-cyclically monotone if it is
concentrated
on a
c-
cyclically monotone set, that is if there exists
\Gamma\subset X\times Y
which is
c
-cyclically monotone such that
\pi(\Gamma)=1
.

It is known that a property equivalent to that of
c-
cyclical monotonicity is the following:

Definition 2
.

A transport plan
\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
is said to be
finitely optimal
if for all couples of positive measures
\alpha
and
\alpha^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}(X\times Y)
with
finite
supports sharing common marginals — that is
{\rm pr}_{i}^{\sharp}\alpha={\rm pr}_{i}^{\sharp}\alpha^{\prime}
for
i=1,2
— and such that the support of
\alpha
is contained in that of
\pi
, it holds that
\mathscr{C}(\alpha)\leq\mathscr{C}(\alpha^{\prime})
.

The fact that this property
1

[
5
]
— and
[
6
]

]
refers the reader to

\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
is finitely optimal, and let
(x_{1},y_{1}),\dots,(x_{n},y_{n})\in\Gamma
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
where
\Gamma:=\textsf{supp}(\pi)
. Then, let us define the positive and finitely supported measures

Finite optimality
of Definition

\eta:=\alpha^{\prime}-\alpha
where
\alpha
and
\alpha^{\prime}
are as in Definition

, then we see that
\eta
is a specific kind of perturbation of the constraint set
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
at the point
\pi
. Indeed, since
\alpha
and
\alpha^{\prime}
share common marginals, the measure
\pi+\eta
is a “coupling” of
\mu
and

, in the sense that its marginals are precisely

and
\nu
. Nevertheless, we stress out that it need
not
be a rightful element of the radial cone
\mathcal{R}_{\pi}
, because
\alpha
is purely atomic and therefore has no reason

to be absolutely continuous with respect to
\pi
. Another way to express this is by the fact that
\pi+\eta
may not be a

measure in this situation.

On the other side, in terms of the vocabulary of
[
5
]
, this means that the elements of the radial cone
\eta\in\mathcal{R}_{\pi}
are precisely those (signed) measure such that
\eta=\eta^{+}-\eta^{-}
and
\eta^{+}
is a
competitor
for
\eta^{-}
. In essence,
\eta^{+}
represents a re-routing of some amount of mass taken from the transport plan
\pi
, which is understood from the fact that
\eta^{-}
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\pi
, that is
\eta^{-}\ll\pi

is that it is demanded here that
\eta^{-}
be absolutely continuous, whereas in the case of finite optimality, it is demanded that
\eta^{-}
be finitely supported inside the support of
\pi

Definition 3

(Finite perturbations)
.

Let us define
\mathcal{F}_{\pi}\subset\mathcal{M}(X\times Y)
the set of perturbations pertaining to finite optimality, namely the set of (signed) purely atomic measures
\eta
with
finite
support — meaning that
\textsf{supp}(|\eta|)
is a finite set where we recall that
|\eta|=\eta^{+}+\eta^{-}
— such that
{\rm pr}_{i}^{\sharp}\eta=0
for
i=1,2
and such that
\textsf{supp}(\eta^{-})\subset\textsf{supp}(\pi)
. In the language of
[
5
]
, this means that
\eta^{+}
is a competitor to
\eta^{-}
. We will refer to elements of
\mathcal{F}_{\pi}
as
finite perturbations
.

By construction, it is obvious that
finite optimality as in Definition
2
is equivalent to demanding that
\mathscr{C}(\eta)\geq 0
for all
\eta\in\mathcal{F}_{\pi}
. We can also define the subset
\mathcal{C}_{\pi}
of
\mathcal{F}_{\pi}
of
cyclical perturbations
, to wit the set of (signed) purely atomic measures
\eta
of the form

\eta=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\delta_{(x_{i},y_{i+1})}-\delta_{(x_{i},y_{i})}).

for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
and family of points
(x_{1},y_{1}),\cdots,(x_{n},y_{n})\in\textsf{supp}(\pi)
with
y_{n+1}:=y_{1}
. It is then obvious that the
c
-cyclical monotonicity property is exactly equivalent to the fact that
\mathscr{C}(\eta)\geq 0
for all
\eta\in\mathcal{C}_{\pi}
.

Remark 2

(Equivalence between finite optimality and
c
-cyclical monotonicity)
.

We explained above that
finite optimality
implies
c
-cyclical monotonicity. Within our language, this trivially follows from the fact that
\mathcal{C}_{\pi}
is a subset of
\mathcal{F}_{\pi}
. The reverse statement also holds, and we refer the reader to
[
14
, Appendix D]
. We stress out that this necessitates no assumptions whatsoever on the cost of transport
c
. In fact, the rationale found in
[
14
]
relies on purely algebraic manipulations, and in fact it can be stated abstractly as follows:
If
L:\mathcal{M}(X\times Y)\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
is a (not necessarily continuous) linear functional, and if
L(\eta)\geq 0
for all cyclical perturbations
\eta\in\mathcal{C}_{\pi}
— provided
L\eta^{-}<\infty
so that the quantity
L(\eta)
makes sense as an element of
\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
—
then
L(\eta)\geq 0
for all finite perturbations
\eta\in\mathcal{F}_{\pi}
— again, so that
L(\eta^{-})<\infty
. Here, in our present context, the linear function
L
is simply given by
\mathscr{C}
the cost functional of (
OT
).

The game will be to show that (under suitable assumptions)
the cost functional
\mathscr{C}
being non-negative over the radial cone
\mathcal{R}_{\pi}
is equivalent to it being non-negative over the set of finite perturbations
\mathcal{F}_{\pi}
or equivalently on the set of cyclical perturbations
\mathcal{C}_{\pi}
. We will first show (under suitable assumptions) that
c
-cyclical monotonicity is necessary for optimality in Section
2.4
(“
If
\mathscr{C}
is non-negative over
\mathcal{R}_{\pi}
, then it is non-negative over
\mathcal{C}_{\pi}
(or equivalently over
\mathcal{F}_{\pi}
)
”) then prove that it is sufficient in Section
2.5
(“
If
\mathscr{C}
is non-negative over
\mathcal{C}_{\pi}
(or equivalently over
\mathcal{F}_{\pi}
), then it is non-negative over
\mathcal{R}_{\pi}
”). In fact, we will show every element of the radial cone
\mathcal{R}_{\pi}
is an accumulation point of
\mathcal{F}_{\pi}
(see Proposition
3

infra
). This last property is actually somehow completely agnostic to the cost of transport, but allows to retrieve the equivalence between optimality and
c
-cyclical monotonicity under appropriate continuity assumptions on the cost of transport
c
(see Proposition
4

infra
).

2.4.
Optimality implies
c
-cyclical monotonicity
(
\Rightarrow
)

We start by showing in our framework that optimality implies
c
-cyclical monotonicity. The most general result in this direction is that of
[
4
]
under the sole assumption that the cost of transport
c:X\times Y\to[0,\infty]
be Borel-measurable. Here, we revisit the proof that optimality implies
c
-cyclical monotonicity under the slightly stronger assumption that
c
is integrable with respect to
\mu\otimes\nu
:

Theorem 1
.

Let
c:X\times Y\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be Borel-measurable so that
c
is (locally) integrable with respect to
\mu\otimes\nu
. If
\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
is an optimal transport plan with
\mathscr{C}(\pi)<\infty
, then
\pi
is
c
-cyclically monotone.

We stress out that
c
is allowed to assume
+\infty
as value (and in fact we do not demand that
c
be either non-negative nor bounded from below at this stage). In the following proposition, we first prove Theorem
1
in the special case where the marginal spaces are Euclidean, that is
X=\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
Y=\mathbb{R}^{m}
for
d,m\in\mathbb{N}
. This assumption will be removed afterwards.

Proposition 2
.

Let
c:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be Borel-measurable so that
c
is (locally) integrable with respect to
\mu\otimes\nu
. If
\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
is an optimal transport plan such that
\mathscr{C}(\pi)<\infty
then
\pi
is
c
-cyclical monotone.

Proof of Proposition
2
.

Let us consider a (finite) sequence of points
(x_{1},y_{1})
, …,
(x_{n},y_{n})\in\Gamma
for a set
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that
\pi(\Gamma)=1
.
The precise definition of the set
\Gamma
is to be given at the end of the proof
. Here, recall that we let
y_{n+1}:=y_{1}
. Let
\varepsilon>0
and let
B_{\varepsilon}^{i}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
be the ball of radius
\varepsilon
centered at
(x_{i},y_{i})
. Again, let
B_{\varepsilon}^{n+1}:=B_{\varepsilon}^{1}
. Up to refining the set
\Gamma
, we may (and will) assume without loss of generality that
\pi(B_{\varepsilon}^{i})>0
for all
\varepsilon>0
and all
i=1,\dots,n
. Let us then define
\sigma_{i}^{\varepsilon}
to be the restriction of
\pi
to the ball
B_{\varepsilon}^{i}
normalised so as to make it a probability measure, that is
\sigma_{i}^{\varepsilon}=\pi(B_{\varepsilon}^{i})^{-1}\pi\restriction_{B_{\varepsilon}^{i}}
. We then let

\sigma_{\varepsilon}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}^{\varepsilon}.

We note that
\sigma_{\varepsilon}\ll\pi
and that the Radon-Nykodym derivative of
\sigma_{\varepsilon}
with respect to
\pi
is uniformly bounded since
\pi(B_{\varepsilon}^{i})>0
for all
i=1,\dots,n
. More precisely, we have
\|\frac{d\sigma_{\varepsilon}}{d\pi}\|_{L^{\infty}(\pi)}^{-1}=\min_{i=1,\dots,n}\pi(B_{\varepsilon}^{i})
. Now, we introduce
\xi_{i}^{\varepsilon}:={\rm pr}_{1}^{\sharp}\sigma_{i}^{\varepsilon}\otimes{\rm pr}_{2}^{\sharp}\sigma_{i+1}^{\varepsilon}
for all
i=1,\dots,n
, where again we let
\sigma_{n+1}^{\varepsilon}:=\sigma_{1}^{\varepsilon}
and let us define

\xi_{\varepsilon}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}^{\varepsilon}.

By construction
\sigma_{\varepsilon}
and
\xi_{\varepsilon}
share common marginals. Therefore
\eta_{\varepsilon}:=\xi_{\varepsilon}-\sigma_{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{R}_{\pi}
is an admissible perturbation at
\pi
— since
\pi+t(\xi_{\varepsilon}-\sigma_{\varepsilon})\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
for all
0\leq t<\|\frac{d\sigma_{\varepsilon}}{d\pi}\|^{-1}_{L^{\infty}(\pi)}
— and by optimality (and finiteness) of
\pi
it must be that
\mathscr{C}(\xi_{\varepsilon})\geq\mathscr{C}(\sigma_{\varepsilon})
. Let us show that
\mathscr{C}(\sigma_{\varepsilon})\to\sum_{i=1}^{n}c(x_{i},y_{i})
and
\mathscr{C}(\xi_{\varepsilon})\to\sum_{i=1}^{n}c(x_{i},y_{i+1})
as
\varepsilon\to 0
, so that the thesis of Proposition
2
will follow.

We now recall that by virtue of
Lebesgue differentiation theorem
for
\pi
-a.s. all point
(x_{0},y_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
one has

(6)

\frac{1}{\pi(B_{\varepsilon})}\int_{B_{\varepsilon}}c(x,y)\,\pi(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y)\xrightarrow[\varepsilon\to 0]{}c(x_{0},y_{0})

where
B_{\varepsilon}
is the ball of radius
\varepsilon
centred at
(x_{0},y_{0})
. This theorem is valid for any Radon measure on Euclidean spaces, see
e.g.

[
8
, Theorem 8.4.6]
. This is veracious since
\pi
has finite transport cost, meaning that
c\in L^{1}(\pi)
. Let then
\Gamma_{0}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
be the set of points
(x_{0},y_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that (
6
) holds — which has therefore full
\pi
-measure. Then — provided
(x_{i},y_{i})\in\Gamma_{0}
for all
i=1,\dots,n
— we have

\mathscr{C}(\sigma_{i}^{\varepsilon})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}}c(x,y)\,\sigma_{i}^{\varepsilon}(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y)\\
=\frac{1}{\pi(B_{\varepsilon}^{i})}\int_{B_{\varepsilon}^{i}}c(x,y)\,\pi(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y)\xrightarrow[\varepsilon\to 0]{}c(x_{i},y_{i}).

We have therefore obtain the sought-for claim that

\mathscr{C}(\sigma_{\varepsilon})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathscr{C}(\sigma_{i}^{\varepsilon})\to\sum_{i=1}^{n}c(x_{i},y_{i})

as
\varepsilon\to 0
. It remains to take care of the other limit.

We have

\displaystyle\mathscr{C}(\xi_{i}^{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}}c(x,y)\,{\rm pr}_{1}^{\sharp}\sigma_{i}^{\varepsilon}\otimes{\rm pr}_{2}^{\sharp}\sigma_{i+1}^{\varepsilon}(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}}c(x,y)\,d\left(\frac{1}{\pi(B_{\varepsilon}^{i})}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\pi_{|B_{i}^{\varepsilon}}(x,dy^{\prime})\right)\otimes\left(\frac{1}{\pi(B_{\varepsilon}^{i+1})}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\pi_{|B_{\varepsilon}^{i+1}}(\mathrm{d}x^{\prime},y)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(\pi\otimes\pi)(B_{\varepsilon}^{i}\times B_{\varepsilon}^{i+1})}\int_{B_{\varepsilon}^{i}\times B_{\varepsilon}^{i+1}}c(x,y)\,\pi\otimes\pi(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y^{\prime},\mathrm{d}x^{\prime},\mathrm{d}y).

We can then appeal again to Lebesgue differentiation theorem, applied this time to the probability measure
\pi\otimes\pi\in\mathcal{P}((\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{m})\times(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}))
and for the integrand
c
seen as the function
c(x,y^{\prime},x^{\prime},y):=c(x,y)
. Note that this is righteous since
c
is assumed to be (locally) integrable with respect to
\mu\otimes\nu
. For all
i=1,\dots,n
, we have
(x_{i},y_{i+1})\in B_{\varepsilon}^{i}\times B_{\varepsilon}^{i+1}
and letting
\varepsilon\to 0
yields that
\mathscr{C}(\xi_{i}^{\varepsilon})\to c(x_{i},y_{i+1})
, and therefore the claim that
\mathscr{C}(\xi_{\varepsilon})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathscr{C}(\xi_{i}^{\varepsilon})\to\sum_{i=1}^{n}c(x_{i},y_{i+1})
. This terminates (in essence) the proof of Proposition
2
.

To finish, let us discuss how to construct the set
\Gamma
introduced at the beginning of this proof. Let
\Gamma_{1}:=\bigcap_{i=1,2}{\rm pr}_{i}(\Sigma)
where
\Sigma\subset(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{m})\times(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{m})
is the set of points for which Lebesgue differentiation theorem holds this time for
\pi\otimes\pi
and where evidently we define
{\rm pr}_{1}(x,y,z,t)=(x,y)
and
{\rm pr}_{2}(x,y,z,t)=(z,t)
for all
(x,y,z,t)\in(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{m})\times(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{m})
. It is well-known that the sets
{\rm pr}_{1}(\Sigma)
and
{\rm pr}_{2}(\Sigma)
need not be Borel-measurable, but that they are certainly Lebesgue-measurable
[
13
, Theorem 2.12]
. It then suffices to consider
\Gamma:=\Gamma_{0}\cap\Gamma_{1}
where
\Gamma_{0}
was introduced above as the (Borel-measurable) set of points for which Lebesgue differentiation theorem holds (
6
) in the case of
\pi
. Note that
\Gamma
is
a priori
only Lebesgue-measurable. Nonetheless, as such there exists a Borel set
\Gamma^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
and a
\pi
-null set
N\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
so that
\Gamma=\Gamma^{\prime}\cup N
. Then
\Gamma^{\prime}
is the sought-for
c
-cyclically monotone Borel-measurable set over which
\pi
concentrates.
∎

In the preceding proposition, we have assumed that the marginal spaces where Euclidean so as to appeal to the standard Lebesgue differentiation theorem, see
[
8
, Theorem 8.4.6]
. In the
non
-Euclidean setting, this theorem is still much valid but requires additional assumptions either on the underlying space or on the probability measure under consideration. Typically, one demands that this measure be
doubling
, see
e.g.

[
16
, Chapter 2.9]
. But this is completely inadequate, since there are no reasons for a (optimal) transport plan to be doubling — and in fact it is easy to build counterexamples. The gap between the Euclidean and non-Euclidean setting in the Lebesgue differentiation theorem boils down to the choice of the
differentiation basis
—
i.e.
whether or not we can consider “nice” shrinking sets like balls or cubes. But for what matters to us, this is rather unimportant. More precisely, we can attach to each point a sequence of measurable sets specifically tailored to this point so that the Lebesgue differentiation theorem holds:

Lemma 2
.

Let
\gamma\in\mathcal{P}(Z)
where
Z
is a Polish space. Then, for
\gamma
-a.s all
z\in Z
, there exists a sequence
E_{k}(z)
of Borel sets all containing
z
such that
\gamma(E_{k}(z))>0
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
and such that for any function
\phi:Z\to\mathbb{R}
integrable with respect to
\gamma
, we have

(7)

\frac{1}{\gamma(E_{k}(z))}\int_{E_{k}(z)}\phi(w)\gamma(\mathrm{d}w)\xrightarrow[k\to\infty]{}\phi(z)

for
\gamma
almost-surely all
z\in Z
.

The proof of the technical Lemma
2
is postponed to Appendix
A
. We can prove Theorem
1
in full generality using this lemma:

Proof of Theorem
1
.

The proof is identical to that of Proposition
2
, but this time we need to care for which kind of neighbourhood we are restricting
\pi
onto. Let
(x_{1},y_{1}),\dots,(x_{n},y_{n})\in\Gamma
, where
\Gamma
is a set such that
\pi(\Gamma)=1
that will be constructed again
a posteriori
. For each
z\in X\times Y
, let
E_{k}(z)
be the Borel sets of Lemma
2
. Let us then write
E_{k}^{i}=E_{k}(z_{i})
where
z_{i}:=(x_{i},y_{i})
. Let then
\pi_{i}^{k}
be the normalised restriction of
\pi
to
E_{k}^{i}
and
\sigma_{k}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}^{k}
. Then, we let
\xi_{i}^{k}:={\rm pr}_{1}^{\sharp}\sigma_{i}^{k}\otimes{\rm pr}_{2}^{\sharp}\sigma_{i+1}^{k}
for all

(where
\sigma_{n+1}^{k}:=\sigma_{1}^{k}
) and let
\xi_{k}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}^{k}
. In virtue of Lemma

\mathscr{C}(\sigma_{k})\to\sum_{i=1}^{n}c(x_{i},y_{i})
as

. Also, again using Fubini’s theorem in the same spirit as in the proof of Proposition

(8)

\mathscr{C}(\xi_{i}^{k})=\frac{1}{\pi\otimes\pi(E_{k}^{i}\times E_{k+1}^{i})}\int_{E_{k}^{i}\times E_{k+1}^{i}}c(x,y)\,\pi\otimes\pi(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y^{\prime},\mathrm{d}x^{\prime},\mathrm{d}y)

Now, the thesis of Lemma
2
still holds here — we refer to Remark

in the Appendix. In particular, we have
\mathscr{C}(\xi_{i}^{k})\to c(x_{i},y_{i+1})
for all
i=1,\dots,n
and therefore
\mathscr{C}(\xi_{k})\to\sum_{i=1}^{n}c(x_{i},y_{i+1})
. This finishes the (core of the) proof. The set
\Gamma
is built exactly as in the proof of Proposition

, so we do not repeat the argument here.
∎

2.5.
c
-cyclical monotonicity implies optimality
(
\Leftarrow
)

We will now discuss the reverse implication, namely that
c
-cyclical monotonicity is a sufficient criterion to yield optimality under suitable assumptions on the cost
c
. The idea is as follows: provided the
c
-cyclical monotonicity property holds, that is
\mathscr{C}(\eta)\geq 0
for all
\eta\in\mathcal{C}_{\pi}
— or equivalently for all
\eta\in\mathcal{F}_{\pi}
— the game is to show that this implies that
\mathscr{C}(\eta)\geq 0
for all
\eta\in\mathcal{R}_{\pi}
, thus implying that
\pi
is optimal according Proposition
1
. In fact, we will first prove the following statement:

Proposition 3
.

For all
\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
, any element of the radial cone
\mathcal{R}_{\pi}
is an accumulation point of the set of finite perturbations
\mathcal{F}_{\pi}
.

Under the assumption that
\mathscr{C}
is continuous –
i.e.
when
c\in C_{0}(X\times Y)
– then it follows immediately from the above proposition that
c
-cyclical monotonicity implies optimality:

Proposition 4
.

Let
c:X\times Y\to\mathbb{R}
be in
C_{0}(X\times Y)
, i.e. continuous and vanishing at infinity (or yet that
c
is continuous and the marginals
\mu
and
\nu
are compactly-supported). If
\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
is
c
-cyclically monotone and
\mathscr{C}(\pi)<\infty
, then
\pi
is optimal.

Proof of Proposition
4
.

We need that show that
\mathscr{C}(\eta)\geq 0
for all
\eta\in\mathcal{R}_{\pi}
. But for any admissible perturbation
\eta\in\mathcal{R}_{\pi}
, according to Proposition
3
, we can find
(\chi_{n})_{n}\subset\mathcal{F}_{\pi}
such that
\chi_{n}\rightharpoonup\eta
in the limit
n\to\infty
. By
c
-cyclical monotonicity, we have
\mathscr{C}(\chi_{n})\geq 0
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. By the assumptions on the cost of transport
c
, the transport cost functional
\mathscr{C}:\mathcal{P}(X\times Y)\to\mathbb{R}
is vaguely continuous, which entails that
\mathscr{C}(\eta)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathscr{C}(\chi_{n})\geq 0
so that the thesis is proved.
∎

It is interesting to remark that the result of Proposition
3
is in a sense completely disconnected from the notion of
c
-cyclical monotonicity
per se
, since it has nothing to do with the cost of transport
c
. The
c-
cyclical monotonicity is certainly implied by it under continuity assumption on the cost of transport as shown above in Proposition
4
. We will show afterward how to obtain sufficiency of the
c
-cyclical monotonicity property for optimality without the stringent continuity assumptions on the cost of transport of Proposition
4
. First, we prove Proposition
3
by constructing explicitly for each
\eta\in\mathcal{R}_{\pi}
a sequence of finite perturbations vaguely converging to
\eta
.

2.5.1.
Proof of Proposition
3

We fix an admissible perturbation
\eta\in\mathcal{R}_{\pi}
. We remark that we may (and will) assume without loss of generality that
\eta^{+}
and
\eta^{-}
are normalised so that they are probability measures, that is
\int_{X\times Y}\eta^{+}=\int_{X\times Y}\eta^{-}=1
. By definition
\eta^{+}
and
\eta^{-}
share common marginals, that is
{\rm pr}_{i}^{\sharp}\eta^{+}={\rm pr}_{i}^{\sharp}\eta^{-}:=m_{i}
for
i=1,2
. We can then write
\eta^{-}=\eta^{-}_{y}(x)\otimes m_{2}(y)
the disintegration of
\eta^{-}
onto its second marginal
m_{2}
, and likewise
\eta^{+}=m_{1}(x)\otimes\eta^{+}_{x}(y)
the disintegration of
\eta^{+}
onto its first marginal
m_{1}
. We are going to build samples
Z_{0}^{-},\dots,Z_{n-1}^{-}
— resp.
Z_{0}^{+},\dots,Z_{n-1}^{+}
— from the probability measure
\eta^{-}
— resp.
\eta^{+}
— such that
(i)
the associated empirical measures

\eta^{-}_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\delta_{Z_{i}^{-}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\eta^{+}_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\delta_{Z_{i}^{+}}

vaguely converge to respectively
\eta^{-}
and
\eta^{+}
and
(ii)
such that these empirical measures share common marginals. Then,
\eta_{n}:=\eta^{+}_{n}-\eta^{-}_{n}
will be a rightful finite perturbation at
\pi
,
i.e.
an element of
\mathcal{F}_{\pi}
, and
\eta_{n}\rightharpoonup\eta
.

Let us now explain how to build such samples. First, assume that
Z_{0}^{-}:=(x_{0},y_{0})
is drawn according to the probability measure
\eta^{-}
. Now, draw
y_{1}\in Y
according to
\eta^{+}_{x_{0}}
and let
Z_{0}^{+}:=(x_{0},y_{1})
. Then, to build
Z_{1}^{-}
, we draw
x_{1}\in X
according to
\eta^{-}_{y_{1}}
and let
Z_{1}^{-}:=(x_{1},y_{1})
. This procedure can be further continued, yielding two sequences of points
(Z_{n}^{+})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(Z_{n}^{-})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
both inside the Cartesian product space
X\times Y
. The associated empirical measures
\eta^{-}_{n}
and
\eta^{+}_{n}
are such that their first marginals always coincide, i.e.
{\rm pr}_{1}^{\sharp}\eta_{n}^{-}={\rm pr}_{1}^{\sharp}\eta_{n}^{+}
— or otherwise stated
{\rm pr}_{1}^{\sharp}\eta_{n}=0
. Nevertheless, we have
{\rm pr}_{2}^{\sharp}\eta^{+}_{n}={\rm pr}_{2}^{\sharp}\eta^{-}_{n}-\frac{1}{n}\delta_{y_{0}}+\frac{1}{n}\delta_{y_{n}}
so that these two marginals do not
a priori
coincide except in the eventuality that
y_{0}=y_{n}
. We therefore need to correct this sampling procedure so as to ensure that the second marginals also coincide.

Before that, in all due respect, we need to put some rigour on the above construction. Let
\Omega:=\Omega^{-}\times\Omega^{+}
where
\Omega^{-}=\Omega^{+}:=X\times Y
, each of these spaces being equipped with the corresponding product
\sigma-
algebras. We let
\mathbf{Z}_{n}:=(Z_{n}^{-},Z_{n}^{+})\in\Omega_{-}\times\Omega_{+}
. Then, the sequence
(\mathbf{Z}_{n})_{n}
is a time-homogeneous Markov chain with canonical paths space
\Omega^{\mathbb{N}}
— equipped again with its standard product
\sigma-
algebra. Let us remark that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have
\textsf{Law}(Z_{n}^{-})=\eta^{-}
and
\textsf{Law}(Z_{n}^{-})=\eta^{-}
. Indeed, let
f:X\times Y\to\mathbb{R}
be a test function, say continuous and bounded. We have

\mathbb{E}[f(Z_{1}^{-})|Z_{0}^{-}=(x_{0},y_{0})]=\int_{X\times Y}f(x,y)\,\eta^{+}_{x_{0}}(\mathrm{d}y)\otimes\eta^{-}_{y}(\mathrm{d}x).

Therefore

\mathbb{E}[f(Z_{1}^{-})]=\int_{X\times Y}\left(\int_{X\times Y}f(x,y)\,\eta^{+}_{x_{0}}(\mathrm{d}y)\otimes\eta^{-}_{y}(\mathrm{d}x)\right)\eta^{-}(\mathrm{d}x_{0},\mathrm{d}y_{0})

where we used that by construction
\textsf{Law}(Z_{0}^{-})=\eta^{-}
. Let us then integrate over the variable
y_{0}\in Y
, so that

\mathbb{E}[f(Z_{1}^{+})]=\int_{X}\left(\int_{X\times Y}f(x,y)\,\eta^{+}_{x_{0}}(\mathrm{d}y)\otimes\eta^{-}_{y}(\mathrm{d}x)\right)\,m_{1}(\mathrm{d}x_{0})

Then, let us integrate over
x_{0}\in X
and then over
y\in Y

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[f(Z_{1}^{-})]

\displaystyle=\int_{X\times Y}f(x,y)\,\eta^{-}_{y}(\mathrm{d}x)\otimes\left(\int_{X}\eta^{+}_{x_{0}}\,m_{1}(\mathrm{d}x_{0})\right)(\mathrm{d}y)

(9)

\displaystyle=\int_{X\times Y}f(x,y)\,\eta^{-}_{y}(\mathrm{d}x)\otimes m_{2}(\mathrm{d}y)

(10)

\displaystyle=\int_{X\times Y}f(x,y)\eta^{-}(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y)

where we used disintegration of
\eta^{+}
— resp. reintegration of
\eta^{-}
— in (
9
) and (
10
). Therefore,
\textsf{Law}(Z_{1}^{-})=\eta^{-}
, and by an immediate induction
\textsf{Law}(Z_{n}^{-})=\eta^{-}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Using the same rationale, one shows that
\textsf{Law}(Z_{n}^{+})=\eta^{+}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

We stress out that the empirical measures
\eta^{-}_{n}
and
\eta^{+}_{n}
are
random measures

\eta^{-}_{n}=\eta^{-}_{n}(\omega)
and
\eta^{+}_{n}=\eta^{+}_{n}(\omega)
depending on the path
\omega\in\Omega^{\mathbb{N}}
. As noted above, we need to correct this sampling procedure so that the second marginals of these empirical measures coincide. Let us define

\chi^{-}_{n}:=\eta^{-}_{n}+\frac{1}{n}\delta_{(\overline{x},\overline{y})}+\frac{1}{n}\delta_{(x_{n},y_{n})},\qquad\chi^{+}_{n}:=\eta^{+}_{n}+\frac{1}{n}\delta_{(\overline{x},y_{0})}+\frac{1}{n}\delta_{(x_{n},\overline{y})}

where we choose any
(\overline{x},\overline{y})\in\textsf{supp}(\eta^{-})
here. What we have done here is to add
ad hoc
corrections to the empirical measures
\eta^{-}_{n}
and
\eta^{+}_{n}
so that now the resulting measure
\chi_{n}^{-}
and
\chi^{+}_{n}
verify that both of their marginals coincide, i.e.
{\rm pr}_{i}^{\sharp}\chi^{-}_{n}={\rm pr}_{i}^{\sharp}\chi^{+}_{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
i=1,2
. Furthermore, we duly emphasise that
\textsf{supp}(\chi_{n}^{-})=\{(x_{i},y_{i}):i=0,\dots,n\}\cup\{(\overline{x},\overline{y})\}
is contained in
\textsf{supp}(\eta^{-})
, which is itself contained in the support of
\pi
since
\eta\in\mathcal{R}_{\pi}
. Therefore, letting
\chi_{n}:=\chi^{+}_{n}-\chi^{-}_{n}
, we readily see that
\chi_{n}
is a rightful finite perturbation at
\pi
,
i.e.

\chi_{n}\in\mathcal{F}_{\pi}
. Now, let us rewrite
\chi^{-}_{n}=\eta^{-}_{n}+\varepsilon^{-}_{n}
where
\varepsilon^{-}_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\delta_{(\overline{x},\overline{y})}+\frac{1}{n}\delta_{(x_{n},y_{n})}
and likewise
\chi^{+}_{n}=\eta^{+}_{n}+\varepsilon^{+}_{n}
where
\varepsilon^{+}_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\delta_{(\overline{x},y_{0})}+\frac{1}{n}\delta_{(x_{n},\overline{y})}
. Since both
\varepsilon^{-}_{n}
and
\varepsilon^{+}_{n}
converge vaguely to zero as
n\to\infty
, if we can prove that the empirical measure
\eta^{-}_{n}
(resp.
\eta^{+}_{n}
) converge vaguely to
\eta^{-}
(resp.
\eta^{+}
) then the corrected measure
\chi^{-}_{n}
(resp.
\chi^{+}_{n}
) will converge to
\eta^{-}
(resp.
\eta^{+}
), henceforth
\chi_{n}
will converge vaguely to
\eta
. This would imply the thesis of Proposition
3
since
(\chi_{n})_{n}
would be a sequence of finite perturbations approaching
\eta
, thus proving that every element of the radial cone is an accumulation point of finite perturbations.

Nevertheless, some care should be given here, since it need
not
be true that the empirical measures
\eta^{-}_{n}
(resp.
\eta^{+}_{n}
) samples consistently the probability measures
\eta^{-}
(resp.
\eta^{+}
) as
n\to\infty
. This is easily foreseen from the fact that the underlying Markov chain need not be
ergodic
, as shown by the next example.

Example 1
.

Assume that
X=Y=[0,1]
. Let
S_{\alpha}(x):=x+\alpha\mod{1}
be a shift, where
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
. Let
\eta^{-}
be the uniform distribution over the diagonal
\Delta:=\{(x,x):x\in[0,1]\}
of the square
[0,1]\times[0,1]
and
\eta^{+}
be the uniform distribution over the shifted diagonal
\Delta_{\alpha}:=\{(x,S_{\alpha}(x)):x\in[0,1]\}
. Otherwise stated
\eta^{-}:=(Id,S_{0})^{\sharp}\lambda
and
\eta^{+}:=(Id,S_{\alpha})^{\sharp}\lambda
where
\lambda
is the Lebesgue measure on
[0,1]
. First, we remark that the initial condition
Z_{0}^{-}=(x_{0},x_{0})
determines the values of the entire chain. More precisely, we have
Z_{n}^{-}=(S_{\alpha}^{(n)}x_{0},S_{\alpha}^{(n)}x_{0})
and
Z_{n}^{+}=(S_{\alpha}^{(n-1)}x_{0},S_{\alpha}^{(n)}x_{0})
. If
\alpha=\frac{n}{m}\in\mathbb{Q}
is rational, then
T_{\alpha}^{(m)}=Id
and therefore a single chain will never sample entirely neither
\eta^{+}
nor
\eta^{-}
. On the contrary, it is well-known that if
\alpha
is irrational the underlying Markov chain is ergodic, and incidently
\eta^{+}
and
\eta^{-}
will be completely sampled from using a single chain. We note passing by that this example is closely related to a well-known counterexample in the literature to sufficiency of
c
-cyclical monotonocity to yield optimality, see
e.g.

[
4
, Example 1.2]
.

But the obstacle raised by the previous example can easily be bypassed for what matters to us — where all is fair game as long as we manage to sample from
\eta^{+}
and
\eta^{-}
. Indeed, the idea to swerve away from this problem is simply to run
multiple
and
independent
copy of our constructions. That is, we run several independent Markov chains
(\mathbf{Z}_{n}^{j})_{n}
for
j=1,\dots,M
where
\mathbf{Z}_{n}^{j}:=(Z_{n,j}^{-},Z_{n,j}^{+})
each chain starting from independently drawn initial conditions
Z_{0,j}^{-}\sim\eta^{-}
for all
j=1,\dots,M
. For each chain, let
\eta^{-}_{n,j}
and
\eta^{+}_{n,j}
be the empirical measures,
\chi^{-}_{n,j}
and
\chi^{+}_{n,j}
be the corrected empirical measures and
\varepsilon^{-}_{n,j}
(resp.
\varepsilon^{+}_{n,j}
) the said corrections. We then consider the
pooled
estimators

\underline{\eta}^{-}_{n,M}:=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\eta^{-}_{n,j},\quad\underline{\chi}^{-}_{n,M}:=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\chi^{-}_{n,j},\quad\underline{\varepsilon}^{-}_{n,M}:=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\varepsilon^{-}_{n,j}.

and their obvious counterparts
\underline{\eta}^{+}_{n,M}
,
\underline{\chi}^{+}_{n,M}
and
\underline{\varepsilon}^{+}_{n,M}
. We then let
\underline{\chi}_{n,M}:=\underline{\chi}^{+}_{n,M}-\underline{\chi}^{-}_{n,M}
. That is still an element of
\mathcal{F}_{\pi}
. Now, for
any
fixed
n\in\mathbb{N}
, it holds that the empirical measure
\underline{\eta}^{-}_{n,M}
(resp.
\underline{\eta}^{+}_{n,M}
) vaguely converge to
\eta^{-}
(resp.
\eta^{+}
) from shear independence of the chains in the limit
M\to\infty
and since
\textsf{Law}(Z_{n,j}^{-})=\eta^{-}
and
\textsf{Law}(Z_{n,j}^{+})=\eta^{+}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and all
j\in\mathbb{N}
. On this matter, we yet remind the reader that both
\underline{\eta}^{-}_{n,M}
and
\underline{\eta}^{+}_{n,M}
are
random
measures depending on
\omega\in\Omega^{\mathbb{N}}
, so that this convergence actually holds for almost-surely all
\omega
(with respect to the underlying path measure). This is a classical result, see
Varadarajan’s theorem

[
15
, Thm. 11.4.1]
. We emphasise here that this result has essentially
nothing
to do with the underlying Markovian structure of our construction, and is in essence just the law of large numbers. Moreover, by the exact same rationale, it holds that
\underline{\varepsilon}^{-}_{n,M}\rightharpoonup e^{-}_{n}
and
\underline{\varepsilon}^{+}_{n,M}\rightharpoonup e^{+}_{n}
as
M\to\infty
where
e_{n}^{-}=\frac{1}{n}\delta_{(\overline{x},\overline{y})}+\frac{1}{n}\eta^{-}
and
e_{n}^{+}=\frac{1}{n}\delta_{\overline{x}}\otimes m_{2}+\frac{1}{n}m_{1}\otimes\delta_{\overline{y}}
.

To finish, let us rewrite
\underline{\chi}_{n,M}=\underline{\eta}_{n,M}-\underline{\varepsilon}_{n,M}
where we let
\underline{\eta}_{n,M}:=\underline{\eta}^{+}_{n,M}-\underline{\eta}^{-}_{n,M}
and likewise
\underline{\varepsilon}_{n,M}:=\underline{\varepsilon}^{+}_{n,M}-\underline{\varepsilon}^{-}_{n,M}
. The double-entry sequence
(\underline{\chi}_{n,M})_{n,M}
verifies that
\underline{\chi}_{n,M}\rightharpoonup\eta^{+}-\eta^{-}-e^{+}_{n}+e^{-}_{n}
as
M\to\infty
and, since both
e^{-}_{n}
and
e^{+}_{n}
vanish vaguely in the limit
n\to\infty
, we obtain that the double (vague) limit
\lim_{n\to\infty}\lim_{M\to\infty}\underline{\chi}_{n,M}
is equal to
\eta^{+}-\eta^{-}
, that is
\eta
. Finally, we remark that
\|\underline{\chi}_{n,M}\|_{TV}\leq 2(1+2/n)\leq 6
for all
n,M\in\mathbb{N}
, where we recall that
\|\cdot\|_{TV}
denotes the standard total variation norm. We also recall that finite radii balls of
\mathcal{M}(X\times Y)
are metrisable for the vague topology. As in any metrisable space, we can perform a diagonal argument on the double-entry sequence
(\underline{\chi}_{n,M})_{n,M}
, so that there exists a sequence
(M_{n})_{n}
such that
\underline{\chi}_{n,M_{n}}\rightharpoonup\eta
. This sequence being in
\mathcal{F}_{\pi}
, we have proved that any admissible perturbation
\eta\in\mathcal{R}_{\pi}
can be approximated by finite perturbations, that is elements of
\mathcal{F}_{\pi}
, thus yielding the thesis of Proposition
3
.

Remark 3
.

A small technical remark is to made here. We cannot work here with the narrow topology, that is in duality with continuous and bounded functions
C_{b}(X\times Y)
. Indeed, neither the space
\mathcal{M}(Z)
of finite signed Radon measures is metrisable (except in the degenerate case where the base space
Z
is finite) nor its unit ball (except when the base space
Z
is compact, but in which case the narrow and vague topologies coincide). On this matter, see e.g.
[
19
]
.

2.5.2.
Sufficiency of
c
-cyclical monotonicity without continuity

It is shown above in Proposition
4
that
c
-cyclical monotonicity is a sufficient criterion for optimality under continuity assumption on the cost of transport. This was true in virtue of Proposition
3
, in which we proved that every admissible perturbation
\eta\in\mathcal{R}_{\pi}
can be approximated, in the vague topology, by finite perturbations,
i.e.
elements of the set
\mathcal{F}_{\pi}
. In this section, we would like to show how to remove these assumptions.

Theorem 2
.

Let
c:X\times Y\to[0,\infty]
be Borel-measurable and assume that
c
is finite
\mu\otimes\nu
-almost everywhere. Then, if
\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
is
c

\mathscr{C}(\pi)<\infty

\pi
is optimal for (

Remark 4
.

We remark that the weakest theorem in this direction is that of
[
4

\{c=\infty\}=F\cup N
where
F
is any closed set and
\mu\otimes\nu(N)=0

F

Let us scrutinise more closely the construction built in the proof of Proposition
3
. For the sake of completeness, we recall some elementary fact here on the ergodic theorem of Birkhoff and how to apply it in the context of Markov chains, see
e.g.

[
17

T:\Omega^{\mathbb{N}}\to\Omega^{\mathbb{N}}

T(z_{0},z_{1},\dots)=(z_{1},z_{2},\dots)

\Omega:=\Omega^{-}\times\Omega^{+}
where
\Omega^{-}=\Omega^{+}=X\times Y

(\mathbf{Z}_{n})_{n}

\Xi(B):=\int_{B}\,\eta^{-}(\mathrm{d}x^{-},\mathrm{d}y^{-})\otimes\big(\delta_{x^{-}}(\mathrm{d}x^{+})\otimes\eta^{+}_{x^{-}}(\mathrm{d}y^{+})\big)

for all Borel sets

\mathbb{P}_{\Xi}\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega^{\mathbb{N}})

3.
Extension of cyclical monotonicity to the weak optimal transport

3.1.
Setting and notations

We use the strategy introduced above to extend the cyclical monotonicity to the weak optimal transport problem, namely:

(WOT)

\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int_{X}C(x,\pi_{x})\mu(\mathrm{d}x)

where
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(X)
and
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(Y)
are the two marginals and
C:X\times\mathcal{P}(Y)\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
is throughout assumed to be convex with respect to its second variable, namely for every
x\in X
, the map
\rho\mapsto C(x,\rho)
is convex. Here
(\pi_{x})_{x\in X}
denotes the disintegration of the transport plan
\pi
onto its first marginal
\mu
so that
\pi(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y)=\mu(\mathrm{d}x)\otimes\pi_{x}(\mathrm{d}y)
. A
sort of
cyclical monotonicity property for (
WOT
) has been introduced and coined as
C
-monotonicity in
[
2
]
. It reads as follows:

Definition 4
.

A transport plan
\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
is said to be
C-
monotone
if there exists a set
\Gamma\subset X
with
\mu(\Gamma)=1
such that for any finite number of points
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in\textsf{supp}(\Gamma)
and measures
m_{1},\dots,m_{n}\in\mathcal{P}(Y)
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\pi_{x_{i}}
we have

(12)

\sum_{i=1}^{n}C(x_{i},\pi_{x_{i}})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}C(x_{i},m_{i})

It is known that this notion does
not
extend rightfully the classical notion of cyclical monotonicity, since one does
not
(always) recover it in the special where the weak optimal transport problem is actually the classical one, that is for the choice
C(x,\rho):=\int_{Y}c(x,y)\rho(\mathrm{d}y)
. The following counterexample is to be found in
[
3
, Example 5.3]
:

Example 2
.

We assume that the underlying marginal spaces are
X=Y=[0,1]
and that
C(x,\rho)=\int_{[0,1]}c(x,y)\rho(\mathrm{d}y)
where
c(x,y):=1-\mathds{1}_{\{x\}}(y)
. Let
\lambda
be the Lebesgue measure on
[0,1]
. Then, the transport plan
\pi:=\lambda\otimes\lambda\in\Pi(\lambda,\lambda)
is easily seen to be
C
-monotone in the sense of Definition
4
, but it is certainly not optimal for the optimal transport problem where
\mu=\nu=\lambda
and where the cost of transport is precisely given by
c
.

In contrast, we introduce in Definition
5
below a more consistent extension of the
c
-cyclical monotonicity property for the weak optimal transport, in the sense that it is equivalent to the classical cyclical monotonicity as in Definition
1
when the weak transport is actually the classical one. Before that, we discuss an
ad hoc
notion of differentiability that will be used throughout this section.

3.1.1.
A notion of differentiability

Let us explain the notion of differentiability that will be used throughout this section. Here, for each fixed
x\in X
, the convex functional
\mathcal{P}(Y)\ni\rho\to C(x,\rho)
is canonically extended to the set
\mathcal{M}(Y)
of all signed and finite Radon measures by setting
C(x,\rho)=+\infty
if
\rho\notin\mathcal{P}(Y)
. We first let
\partial_{\rho}C(x,\overline{\rho})
be the subdifferential at the point
\overline{\rho}\in\mathcal{P}(Y)
defined as

(13)

\partial_{\rho}C(x,\overline{\rho}):=\Bigg\{g\in C_{0}(Y):C(x,\rho)\geq C(x,\overline{\rho})\\
+\int_{Y}g(y)[\rho-\overline{\rho}](\mathrm{d}y),\,\forall\rho\in\mathcal{M}(Y)\Bigg\}

where we recall that
C_{0}(Y)
denotes the set of real-valued continuous functions on
Y
which vanish at infinity. The elements of the subdifferential are the so-called subgradients. For what matters to us, this definition is slightly too strong since we want to cope with subgradients which are
not
necessarily continuous. To this end, we define an
extended
subdifferential as follows.

Let us write
\mathfrak{M}(Y)
the set of Borel measurable functions
g:Y\to\mathbb{R}
. We say that
g\in\mathfrak{M}(Y)
is an
extended subgradient
of the function
\mathcal{P}(Y)\ni\rho\to C(x,\rho)
at the point
\overline{\rho}\in\mathcal{P}(Y)
if

C(x,\rho)\geq C(x,\overline{\rho})+\int_{Y}g(y)[\rho-\overline{\rho}](\mathrm{d}y)

for all
\rho\in\mathcal{P}(Y)

provided that
\int_{Y}|g|\overline{\rho}<\infty
and that
\int_{y}|g|\rho<\infty
as soon as
C(x,\rho)<\infty
.
The set of all
g
verifying the above inequality and assumptions will be denoted
\mathfrak{D}_{\rho}C(x,\overline{\rho})
and will be referred to as the
extended subdifferential
and its elements as
extended subgradients
. Obviously, this is a superset of the standard set of subgradients, that is
\partial_{\rho}C(x,\overline{\rho})\subset\mathfrak{D}_{\rho}C(x,\overline{\rho})
.

Now, we will say that
\rho\mapsto C(x,\rho)
is
differentiable
at
\overline{\rho}\in\mathcal{P}(Y)
if the extended subdifferential
\mathfrak{D}_{\rho}C(x,\overline{\rho})
consists in a single element (up to additive constants). In this case, we will write (a canonical representation of) this element
\nabla_{\rho}C(x,\overline{\rho})
and simply call it the
gradient.
It is a well-known fact of convex analysis that, assuming that
C(x,\overline{\rho})<\infty
, it holds

\lim_{t\downarrow 0^{+}}\frac{C(x,(1-t)\overline{\rho}+t\xi)-C(x,\overline{\rho})}{t}=\sup_{g\in\partial_{\rho}C(x,\overline{\rho})}\int_{Y}g(y)[\xi-\overline{\rho}](\mathrm{d}y)

for all
\xi\in\mathcal{P}(Y)
such that
C(x,\xi)<\infty
. Now, evidently, by definition of extended subgradients, we also have:

\lim_{t\downarrow 0^{+}}\frac{C(x,(1-t)\overline{\rho}+t\xi)-C(x,\overline{\rho})}{t}\geq\sup_{g\in\mathfrak{D}_{\rho}C(x,\overline{\rho})}\int_{Y}g(y)[\xi-\overline{\rho}](\mathrm{d}y)

This entails that

\sup_{g\in\partial_{\rho}C(x,\overline{\rho})}\int_{Y}g(y)[\xi-\overline{\rho}](\mathrm{d}y)\geq\\
\sup_{g\in\mathfrak{D}_{\rho}C(x,\overline{\rho})}\int_{Y}g(y)[\xi-\overline{\rho}](\mathrm{d}y)\\
\geq\sup_{g\in\partial_{\rho}C(x,\overline{\rho})}\int_{Y}g(y)[\xi-\overline{\rho}](\mathrm{d}y)

where, in the last inequality, we used that
\partial_{\rho}C(x,\rho)\subset\mathfrak{D}_{\rho}C(x,\rho)
. Therefore, we have equality of both
suprema
. In particular, if
\mathcal{P}(Y)\ni\rho\mapsto C(x,\rho)
is differentiable in the aforementioned sense at the point
\overline{\rho}\in\mathcal{P}(Y)
, then we have

(14)

\lim_{t\downarrow 0^{+}}\frac{C(x,(1-t)\overline{\rho}+t\xi)-C(x,\overline{\rho})}{t}=\int_{Y}\nabla_{\rho}C(x,\rho)(y)[\xi-\overline{\rho}](\mathrm{d}y).

provided that
C(x,\xi)<\infty
, where the gradient
\nabla_{\rho}C(x,\rho)
is understood in the extended sense,
i.e.
it is only considered measurable and thus may not be necessarily continuous.

3.1.2.
The
C
-cyclical monotonicity property

We are now ready to introduce the
C
-cyclical monotonicity
property, which will serve as the analogue of the
c
-cyclical monotonicity property of the classical optimal transport setting. This property reads as follows:

Definition 5
.

A transport plan
\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
is said to be
C
-cyclically monotone
if it is concentrated on a set
\Gamma\subset X\times Y
so that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and all
(x_{1},y_{1}),\dots,(x_{n},y_{n})\in\Gamma
, it holds that

(15)

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nabla_{\rho}C(x_{i},\pi_{x_{i}})(y_{i})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nabla_{\rho}C(x_{i},\pi_{x_{i}})(y_{i+1})

where we let
y_{n+1}:=y_{1}
and where
Y\ni y\mapsto\nabla_{\rho}C(x,\pi_{x})(y)
is the gradient (see
supra
) of the cost
C
(provided it exists) with respect to its second argument evaluated at the point
(x,\pi_{x})\in X\times\mathcal{P}(Y)
.

Example 3
.

(1)

In the special case where
C(x,\rho)=\int_{Y}c(x,y)\rho(\mathrm{d}y)
, that is when the weak optimal transport problem boils down to the classical one, then the gradient of the cost of transport
C
is simply the function
y\mapsto c(x,y)
for all
x\in X
, and (
15
) is therefore seen to be the usual, standard
c
-cyclical monotonicity as of Definition
1
.

(2)

In the case of the
barycentric quadratic cost
, namely
C(x,\rho):=\frac{1}{2}\|x-\int_{Y}z\rho(\mathrm{d}z)\|^{2}
, then
\nabla_{\rho}C(x,\rho)(y)=y^{\intercal}\left(\int_{Y}z\rho(\mathrm{d}z)-x\right)
for all
x\in X
and
\rho\in\mathcal{P}(Y)
and the
C
-cyclical monotonicity property (
15
) reads

\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}^{\intercal}\left(\int_{z}z\pi_{x_{i}}(\mathrm{d}z)-x_{i}\right)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i+1}^{\intercal}\left(\int_{z}z\pi_{x_{i}}(\mathrm{d}z)-x_{i}\right).

Our main result in this section is that (under suitable assumptions) the
C
-cyclical monotonicity property as defined above in Definition
5
is equivalent to optimality for the weak optimal transport problem (
WOT
). The necessary part will be stated and proved below in Theorem
3
and the sufficient part in Theorem
4
.

3.2.
The first-order optimality conditions for (
WOT
)

In this section, we follow the same strategy as in Section
2
where we derived the zeroth-order optimality condition for the classical OT problem. The main difference here is that the objective of the weak optimal transport problem

\mathscr{C}(\pi):=\int_{X}C(x,\pi_{x})\mu(\mathrm{d}x)

is
no longer linear. Nevertheless, under the assumption that
C
be convex with respect to its second variable, the function
\mathscr{C}
is convex, so that its minimum can be characterised using a
first-order
optimality condition. We will assume from now on that
\mathscr{C}
is extended from
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
to the entire space of (finite and signed) Radon measures
\mathcal{M}(X\times Y)
by letting
\mathscr{C}=+\infty
outside of the feasible set of transport plans. We start with the following lemma:

Lemma 3
.

Let
C:X\times\mathcal{P}(Y)\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be convex and differentiable with respect to its second variable. Then, the objective function
\mathscr{C}:\mathcal{M}(X\times Y)\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
of the weak optimal transport problem is convex. Furthermore, let
\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
be such that
\mathscr{C}(\pi)<\infty
and such that the following property holds

(A
π
)

\mathscr{C}(\gamma)<\infty\implies\int_{X\times Y}|\nabla_{\rho}C(x,\pi_{x})(y)|\gamma(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y)<\infty,

for all
\gamma\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
. It is implicitly assumed in (
Aπ
) that
X\times Y\ni(x,y)\mapsto\nabla_{\rho}C(x,\pi_{x})(y)
is jointly measurable. Then, for all
\gamma\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
such that
\mathscr{C}(\gamma)<\infty
, it holds that

(16)

\mathscr{C}(\gamma)\geq\mathscr{C}(\pi)+\iint_{X\times Y}\nabla_{\rho}C(x,\pi_{x})(y)[\gamma-\pi](\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y).

Furthermore, again for all
\gamma\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
such that
\mathscr{C}(\gamma)<\infty
, we have

(17)

\lim_{t\downarrow 0^{+}}\frac{\mathscr{C}((1-t)\pi+t\gamma)-\mathscr{C}(\pi)}{t}\\
=\int_{X\times Y}\nabla_{\rho}C(x,\pi_{x})(y)[\gamma-\pi](\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y).

Let us point out that the assumption (A
π
) (and the assumption (A’
π
) below) is only used to guarantee that the linearised functional is well-defined along all finite-cost competitors.

The inequality (
16
) of Lemma
3
would read that the function
g
is a
subgradient
(in the usual sense, that is in duality with
C_{0}(X\times Y)
) of the cost functional
\mathscr{C}
at the point
\pi
if it was furthermore known that
g
were
at least
continuous. Nevertheless, there is no reason for this to be true, since the measurable map
X\ni x\mapsto\pi_{x}\in\mathcal{P}(Y)
is
not
necessarily (vaguely) continuous. Instead,
g
should be thought as an “algebraic” sort of subgradient.

The technical condition (
Aπ
) ensures that the integral on the right-hand side of (
16
) makes sense as soon as the left-hand side is finite. We will make use of this assumption throughout our result. We remark that, in the case where
C(x,\rho)=\int_{Y}c(x,y)\rho(\mathrm{d}y)
, in which case
\nabla_{\rho}C(x,\rho)(y)=c(x,y)
, then this condition simply says that
\pi
as finite transport cost, and is therefore always true since it is precisely assumed that
\pi
has finite cost, that is
\int_{X\times Y}c\,d\pi<\infty
. It is also met for the barycentric squared cost if
\int_{Y}\|y\|^{2}\nu(\mathrm{d}y)<\infty
. Indeed, in this case
\nabla_{\rho}C(x,\rho)(y)=y^{\intercal}(\int_{Y}z\rho(\mathrm{d}z)-x)
and thus

\int_{X\times Y}|\nabla_{\rho}C(x,\pi_{x})(y)|\gamma(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y)\leq\\
\sqrt{\int_{X\times Y}\|y\|^{2}\gamma(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y)}\sqrt{\int_{X\times Y}\left\|x-\int_{Y}z\pi_{x}(\mathrm{d}z)\right\|^{2}\gamma(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y)}

by the standard Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. Therefore, marginalising the integrals in the above inequality, we obtain

\int_{X\times Y}\left\|x-\int_{Y}z\pi_{x}(\mathrm{d}z)\right\|^{2}\gamma(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y)\\
=\int_{X}\left\|x-\int_{Y}z\pi_{x}(\mathrm{d}z)\right\|^{2}\mu(\mathrm{d}x)=\mathscr{C}(\pi)<\infty

and

\int_{X\times Y}\|y\|^{2}\gamma(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y)=\int_{Y}\|y\|^{2}\nu(\mathrm{d}y)<\infty

Thus
\int_{X\times Y}|\nabla_{\rho}C(x,\pi_{x})(y)|\gamma(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y)<\infty
is finite for all
\gamma\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
provided that
\nu
has bounded second-moment. We note that this holds irrespective of whether or not
\mathscr{C}(\gamma)
is finite, so that the assumption (
Aπ
) is always verified. The same rationale can be applied to weak costs of transport of the form
C(x,\rho):=\theta(x-\int_{Y}z\rho(\mathrm{d}z))
where
\theta:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is convex and differentiable function that furthermore verifies
\|\nabla\theta\|^{2}\leq\theta
(up to a multiplicative constant). The assumption (
Aπ
) is also seen to hold for the
Marton cost
,
i.e.

C(x,\rho)=(\int_{Y}\mathds{1}_{\{x\}}(z)\rho(\mathrm{d}z))^{2}
— with underlying spaces
X=Y=[0,1]
. Altogether, this assumption is general enough to encompass important cases of applications of the weak optimal transport.

Proof.

Let
\pi,\gamma\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
with disintegrations
(\pi_{x})_{x\in X}
and
(\gamma_{x})_{x\in X}
with respect to the marginal
\mu
. Then, the disintegration of the convex combination
(1-t)\pi+t\gamma
with respect to
\mu
is simply given by the convex combination of the disintegrations, to wit
((1-t)\pi_{x}+t\gamma_{x})_{x\in X}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathscr{C}((1-t)\pi+t\gamma)

\displaystyle=\int_{X}C(x,(1-t)\pi_{x}+t\gamma_{x})\mu(\mathrm{d}x).

\displaystyle\leq(1-t)\underbrace{\int_{X}C(x,\pi_{x})\mu(\mathrm{d}x)}_{\mathscr{C}(\pi)}+t\underbrace{\int_{X}C(x,\gamma_{x})\mu(\mathrm{d}x)}_{\mathscr{C}(\gamma)}

where we used solely the convexity of
C
with respect to its second argument. This proves that
\mathscr{C}
is convex. Now, we also have that

\mathscr{C}(\gamma)-\mathscr{C}(\pi)=\int_{X}(C(x,\gamma_{x})-C(x,\pi_{x}))\mu(\mathrm{d}x)

for all
\gamma,\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
. Here, we note that we used that
\mathscr{C}(\pi)<\infty
, so that the above difference makes sense. For all
\gamma\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
, using the convexity and differentiability of
C
with respect to its second variable, we have

(18)

C(x,\gamma_{x})\geq C(x,\pi_{x})+\int_{Y}\nabla_{\rho}C(x,\pi_{x})(y)\,[\gamma_{x}-\pi_{x}](\mathrm{d}y)

We emphasise here that by the technical assumption (
Aπ
) the integrals on the right-hand side make sense.
Then, integrating with respect to
\mu
, we obtain

(19)

\mathscr{C}(\gamma)\geq\mathscr{C}(\pi)+\int_{X\times Y}\nabla_{\rho}C(x,\pi_{x})(y)\,\underbrace{[\gamma_{x}-\pi_{x}](\mathrm{d}y)\otimes\mu(\mathrm{d}x)}_{=[\gamma-\pi](\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y)}

Here, we relied implicitly (again) on the technical assumption (
Aπ
) so as to appeal to Fubini-Lebesgue theorem — as we did also above in (
18
). This proves (
16
). Now, let us show (
17
). First, applying the inequality (
16
) that we have just proved to
\gamma\leftarrow(1-t)\pi+t\gamma
, we have immediately that

\liminf_{t\downarrow 0}\frac{\mathscr{C}((1-t)\pi+t\gamma)-\mathscr{C}(\pi)}{t}\geq\int_{X\times Y}\nabla_{\rho}C(x,\pi_{x})(y)[\gamma-\pi](\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y).

Let us now show that the
supremum
limit is smaller or equal to the right-hand side, so that (
17
) will follow. We have

\frac{\mathscr{C}((1-t)\pi+t\gamma)-\mathscr{C}(\pi)}{t}=\int_{X}\phi_{t}(x)\mu(\mathrm{d}x)

where we define
\phi_{t}:X\to\mathbb{R}
as

\phi_{t}(x):=\frac{C(x,\pi_{x}+t(\gamma_{x}-\pi_{x}))-C(x,\pi_{x})}{t}.

The function
\phi_{t}
is measurable. Furthermore, for each fixed
x\in X
, thanks to the convexity of
C
with respect to its second variable, it is monotone in the variable
t\in[0,1]
. In particular, we have
\phi_{t}(x)\leq\phi_{1}(x)
for all
x\in X
. We claim that
\phi_{1}
is integrable with respect to
\mu
. Indeed,
\phi_{1}(x)=C(x,\gamma_{x})-C(x,\pi_{x})
, and both of these terms once integrating against
\mu
yield respectively the weak costs
\mathscr{C}(\gamma)
and
\mathscr{C}(\pi)
, which are assumed to be finite. Therefore, we can appeal to Fatou’s lemma to obtain

\begin{split}\limsup_{t\downarrow 0^{+}}\frac{\mathscr{C}((1-t)\pi+t\gamma)-\mathscr{C}(\pi)}{t}&\leq\int_{X}\limsup_{t\downarrow 0}\phi_{t}(x)\,\mu(\mathrm{d}x)\\
&=\int_{X}\lim_{t\downarrow 0}\phi_{t}(x)\,\mu(\mathrm{d}x)\end{split}

Here, the
supremum limit
is a limit by convexity of
\mathcal{P}(Y)\ni\rho\mapsto C(x,\rho)
. But now, we recall by differentiability of
C
with respect to its second variable that

\lim_{t\to 0}\phi_{t}(x)=\int_{Y}\nabla_{\rho}C(x,\pi_{x})(y)[\gamma_{x}-\pi_{x}](\mathrm{d}y)

as shown in (
14
). We emphasise that the above limit holds for
\mu
almost-surely all
x\in X
since the assumption
\mathscr{C}(\gamma)<\infty
implies that
C(x,\gamma_{x})
is finite for
\mu
almost-surely all
x\in X
, which ensures that (
14
) holds. This terminates the proof of (
17
).
∎

We will show the following first-order optimality conditions for the weak OT problem:

Lemma 4
.

Let
C:X\times\mathcal{P}(Y)\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be such that for all
x\in X
the map
\mathcal{P}(Y)\ni\rho\mapsto C(x,\rho)
is convex and differentiable. Assume
\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
has finite cost
\mathscr{C}(\pi)
and the property (
Aπ
) holds. Then
\pi
is optimal for the weak optimal transport problem if and only if

(20)

\boxed{\int_{X\times Y}\nabla_{\rho}C(x,\pi_{x})(y)\eta^{+}(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y)\geq\int_{X\times Y}\nabla_{\rho}C(x,\pi_{x})(y)\eta^{-}(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y)}

for all
\eta\in\mathcal{R}_{\pi}
provided that
\mathscr{C}(\pi+t\eta)<\infty
for all
t>0
small enough.

We recall that
\mathcal{R}_{\pi}
is the radial cone to feasible set
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
at the point
\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
as defined in (
4
). We also recall that
\eta^{+}
(resp.
\eta^{-}
) denotes the positive (resp. negative) part of the signed and finite Radon measure
\eta\in\mathcal{M}(X\times Y)
. The inequality (
20
) is the first-order optimality condition analogue of the zeroth-order optimality condition of Proposition
1
. Here, we need to go to the first order because the objective of the WOT problem is no longer linear — see also Remark
5
below for a more general picture of the situation.

Proof of Lemma
4
.

We start with the sufficient part. Beware that throughout, we work under the technical assumption (
Aπ
). Let
\gamma\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
be such that
\mathscr{C}(\gamma)<\infty
. By Lemma 3, we have

\mathscr{C}(\gamma)\geq\mathscr{C}(\pi)+\int_{X\times Y}\nabla_{\rho}C(x,\pi_{x})(y)\,[\gamma-\pi](\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y).

We now prove that the last integral is non-negative. Let us set
\eta:=\gamma-\pi
, so that
\eta
is an admissible perturbation at
\pi
, that is
\eta\in R_{\pi}
. Indeed, we have

\pi+t\eta=(1-t)\pi+t\gamma\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)\qquad\text{for all }t\in[0,1].

Moreover, by convexity of
\mathscr{C}
,

\mathscr{C}(\pi+t\eta)=\mathscr{C}((1-t)\pi+t\gamma)\leq(1-t)\mathscr{C}(\pi)+t\mathscr{C}(\gamma)<\infty

for all
t\in[0,1]
. Hence, we can rightfully appeal to the condition (
20
). Writing
\eta=\eta^{+}-\eta^{-}
for the Jordan decomposition of the signed measure
\eta
, the condition
(
20
) yields

\int_{X\times Y}\nabla_{\rho}C(x,\pi_{x})(y)\,\eta^{+}(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y)\geq\int_{X\times Y}\nabla_{\rho}C(x,\pi_{x})(y)\,\eta^{-}(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y).

Consequently,

\int_{X\times Y}\nabla_{\rho}C(x,\pi_{x})(y)\,[\gamma-\pi](\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y)=\int_{X\times Y}\nabla_{\rho}C(x,\pi_{x})(y)\,\eta(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y)\geq 0.

Therefore
\mathscr{C}(\gamma)\geq\mathscr{C}(\pi)
. Since this holds for every
\gamma\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
with
\mathscr{C}(\gamma)<\infty
, the transport plan
\pi
is optimal.

Let us now deal with the necessary part of the lemma. Let
\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
be an optimal transport plan for the weak optimal transport problem that verifies the assumptions of the lemma. Given a perturbation
\eta\in\mathcal{R}_{\pi}
, let us show that (
20
) holds. By optimality of
\pi
– and the fact that
\mathscr{C}(\pi)<\infty
– we have

\mathscr{C}(\pi+t\eta)-\mathscr{C}(\pi)\geq 0
for all
t>0
small enough. Dividing by
t
this inequality, letting
t\to 0
and appealing to (
17
), we obtain that

\int_{X\times Y}\nabla_{\rho}C(x,\pi_{x})(y)\eta(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y)\geq 0.

We then obtain the claim that (
20
) holds by decomposing
\eta
into its positive and negative parts.
∎

Remark 5
.

As in Remark
1
, let us indicate that is again a much general situation to which the weak optimal transport problem is only a particular case. Let
X
be a normed vector space,
f:X\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be a (differentiable) convex function on
X
and
C\subset X
be a convex set. Let us consider the convex minimisation problem

(21)

\min_{x\in C}f(x).

Then,
x\in C
is optimal if and only if
\nabla f(x)[h]\geq 0
for all
h\in R_{C}(x)
— where
R_{C}(x)
is the radial cone defined in (
3
). Indeed, let us assume
x
is optimal, and let
h\in R_{C}(x)
. Then,
f(x+th)-f(x)\geq 0
for
t>0
small enough, so that standardly dividing by
t
and letting it tend to zero yields the claim. The other direction goes as follows: since
f
is convex, we have
f(x)+\nabla f(x)[y-x]\leq f(y)
for all
x,y\in C
. Thus, if
\nabla f(x)[h]\geq 0
for all
h\in R_{C}(x)
, it is then immediate that
f(x)\leq f(y)
for all
y
and thus
x
is optimal. In the case where
f
is not differentiable, then we can use subgradients. Then, if for all
h\in R_{C}(x)
there exists at least one
g\in\partial f(x)
— where
\partial f(x)
denotes the (non-empty) subdifferential of
f
at
x
– such that
\langle g,h\rangle\geq 0
, then
x
is optimal. Indeed, for all
y\in C
, letting
h:=y-x
, then
f(x)\leq f(x)+\langle g,h\rangle\leq f(x+h)=f(y)
such
x
is optimal. In the other direction, assume that
x
is optimal. Then, for all
h\in R_{C}(x)
and
t
small enough
f(x+th)\geq f(x)
. This entails that
f^{\prime}(x;h)\geq 0
for all
h\in R_{C}(x)
where we let
f^{\prime}(x;h)
be the directional derivative at
f
in the direction
h
(since
f
is convex, it exists for all
h
in
\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
). Then either
f^{\prime}(x;h)=0
for all
h
, then
f
is actually differentiable at
x
and
\nabla f(x)=0
, so the conclusion is trivial. Otherwise, there exists a direction
h_{0}\in R_{C}(x)
such that
f^{\prime}(x;h_{0})>0
. Using that
f^{\prime}(x,h_{0})=\sup_{g\in\partial f(x)}\langle g,h_{0}\rangle
this means that there exists a subgradient
g_{0}\in\partial f(x)
such that
\langle g_{0},h_{0}\rangle\geq 0
. Therefore, the exact statement is: if
x
is optimal, then for all
h\in R_{C}(x)
there exists
g\in\partial f(x)
such that
\langle g,h\rangle\geq 0
.

3.3.
C
-cyclical monotonicity characterises optimality

We have shown, in the case of the classical OT problem, that one can easily go from the validity of the zeroth-order optimality condition for the classical OT problem onto
\mathcal{R}_{\pi}
from its validity onto
\mathcal{F}_{\pi}
— yielding the
necessary part
of
c
-cyclical monotonicity for optimality — either by mere density if the cost functional
\pi\mapsto\int_{X\times Y}c\,d\pi
was continuous, see Proposition
4
, or under less stringent assumptions on the cost of transport
c
using the ergodic theorem,
cf.
Theorem
2
.
Vice-versa
, we went from the validity of the zeroth-order optimality condition from
\mathcal{F}_{\pi}
onto
\mathcal{R}_{\pi}
— yielding the
sufficient part
of
c
-cyclical monotonicity for optimality — either by mere density again, appealing to Proposition
3
, if again the cost functional is continuous, cf. Proposition
4
.

Now, to prove that
C
-cyclical monotonicity as defined in Definition
5
is equivalent to optimality for the weak optimal transport problem (
WOT
) is in some sense completely “free”, thanks to the work done previously for the classical optimal transport problem.

First, let us remark that
C
-cyclical monotonicity as in Definition
5
is trivially equivalent to the veracity of the
first-order optimality condition
(
20
) when evaluated at
cyclical perturbations

\eta\in\mathcal{C}_{\pi}
. Just as in the classical optimal transport case, we can define
finite optimality
here, which simply consists in the validity of first-order optimality condition (
20
) when evaluated at
finite perturbation

\eta\in\mathcal{F}_{\pi}
. We emphasise that finite optimality and
C
-cyclical monotonicity are again equivalent for the weak optimal transport. Indeed, it suffices to apply Remark
2
with the linear map
L:\mathcal{M}(X\times Y)\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
defined as

L(\eta):=\int_{X\times Y}\nabla_{\rho}C(x,\pi_{x})(y)\eta(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y).

— provided that the map
X\times Y\ni(x,y)\mapsto\nabla_{\rho}C(x,\pi_{x})(y)
is Borel measurable, as will be assumed in our theorems.

Second, we also know by Lemma
4
above that optimality in (
WOT
) is completely equivalent to the first-order optimality condition (
20

3.4.
Removing differentiability: the case of the barycentric cost

In this section, we explore briefly if what precedes can be extended to the case where the weak cost of transport

3.4.1.
Removing differentiability

A natural question is if whether or not what precedes can be extended to the case where the weak cost of transport

3.4.2.
The case of the barycentric cost

and therefore

(38)

(\|y_{i+1}-y_{i}\|-2\varepsilon)\frac{1}{\pi(B_{i}^{\varepsilon})}\int_{B_{i}^{\varepsilon}}\mathds{1}_{B_{\gamma^{\varepsilon}}}(x)\pi(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y)\\
\leq\int_{B_{\gamma^{\varepsilon}}\cap B_{\varepsilon}(x_{i})}\left\|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}z[\xi^{\varepsilon}_{x}-\pi^{\varepsilon}_{x}](\mathrm{d}z)\right\|\mu(\mathrm{d}x)\\
\leq(\|y_{i+1}-y_{i}\|+2\varepsilon)\frac{1}{\pi(B_{i}^{\varepsilon})}\int_{B_{i}^{\varepsilon}}\mathds{1}_{B_{\gamma^{\varepsilon}}}(x)\pi(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y)

We now claim that there exists
\overline{\varepsilon}>0
small enough so that we have the set inclusion
B_{\gamma^{\varepsilon}}\subset B_{\gamma^{\overline{\varepsilon}}}
for all
\varepsilon<\overline{\varepsilon}
. Indeed, let us remark that the set
B_{\gamma^{\varepsilon}}
rewrites as the set of points
x\in X
so that
x=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}z\pi_{x}(\mathrm{d}z)
and so that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}z\xi^{\varepsilon}_{x}(\mathrm{d}z)\neq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}z\sigma^{\varepsilon}_{x}(\mathrm{d}z)
. This last condition rewrites, using the disintegrations, as

(39)

\frac{1}{\pi_{x}(B_{\varepsilon}(y_{i}))}\int_{B_{\varepsilon}(y_{i})}z\pi_{x}(\mathrm{d}z)\neq\frac{1}{\pi(B_{i+1}^{\varepsilon})}\int_{B_{i+1}^{\varepsilon}}y\pi(\mathrm{d}x^{\prime},\mathrm{d}y)

We note that, as
\varepsilon\to 0
, this condition reads (formally for the moment)
y_{i}\neq y_{i+1}
. Of course, we may assume without loss of generality that
y_{i}\neq y_{i+1}
. Indeed, if it were that
y_{i}=y_{i+1}
, then

(40)

\int_{B_{\gamma^{\varepsilon}}\cap B_{\varepsilon}(x_{i})}\left\|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}z[\xi^{\varepsilon}_{x}-\pi^{\varepsilon}_{x}](\mathrm{d}z)\right\|\mu(\mathrm{d}x)\leq\|y_{i+1}-y_{i}\|+2\varepsilon=2\varepsilon

and thus, in the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
, this integral does not contribute to the sum since it vanishes to zero. Therefore, only the case where
y_{i+1}\neq y_{i}
is of importance. We note passing by that the previous result implies trivially that, in the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
, the inequality (
31
) implies

(41)

\sum_{i|x_{i}\neq b_{i}}\frac{(y_{i+1}-y_{i})^{\intercal}(b_{i}-x_{i})}{\|b_{i}-x_{i}\|}\geq-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|y_{i+1}-y_{i}\|.

But the sought-for inequality (
28
) is finer since only the indices
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
where
x_{i}=b_{i}
are consider in the sum on the right-hand side. Let us therefore assume that
y_{i}\neq y_{i+1}
for all
i=1,\dots,n
. If
x\in B_{\gamma^{\varepsilon}}
, and using that
y_{i+1}\neq y_{i}
, then there exists a small enough
\overline{\varepsilon}>0
such that (
39
) holds for all
0<\varepsilon<\overline{\varepsilon}
. Indeed, this follows from the obvious facts that

\left\|\frac{1}{\pi_{x}(B_{\varepsilon}(y_{i}))}\int_{B_{\varepsilon}(y_{i})}z\pi_{x}(\mathrm{d}z)-y_{i}\right\|\leq\varepsilon

and

\left\|\frac{1}{\pi(B_{i+1}^{\varepsilon})}\int_{B_{i+1}^{\varepsilon}}y\pi(\mathrm{d}x^{\prime},\mathrm{d}y)-y_{i+1}\right\|\leq\varepsilon

for all
\varepsilon>0
. Therefore, letting for instance
\overline{\varepsilon}<\frac{\|y_{i+1}-y_{i}\|}{2}
, we have the set inclusion
B_{\gamma^{\varepsilon}}\subset B_{\gamma^{\overline{\varepsilon}}}
for all
0<\varepsilon<\overline{\varepsilon}
.

Using this fact, we obtain

(42)

\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{B_{\gamma^{\varepsilon}}\cap B_{\varepsilon}(x_{i})}\left\|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}z[\xi^{\varepsilon}_{x}-\pi^{\varepsilon}_{x}](\mathrm{d}z)\right\|\mu(\mathrm{d}x)\\
\leq\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\|y_{i+1}-y_{i}\|+2\varepsilon}{\pi(B_{i}^{\varepsilon})}\int_{B_{i}^{\varepsilon}}\mathds{1}_{B_{\gamma^{\overline{\varepsilon}}}}(x)\pi(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y)

Now, according to the Lebesgue differentiation theorem, we know that there exists a set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
so that
\mu(E)=1
and so that

\frac{1}{B_{\varepsilon}(x)}\int_{B_{\varepsilon}(x)}\mathds{1}_{B_{\gamma^{\overline{\varepsilon}}}}(x^{\prime})\mu(\mathrm{d}x^{\prime})\xrightarrow[\varepsilon\to 0]{}\mathds{1}_{B_{\gamma^{\overline{\varepsilon}}}}(x)

for all
x\in E
.
If we could ensure that
x_{i}\in E
for all
i=1,\dots,n
, then we would obtain

\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{B_{\gamma^{\varepsilon}}\cap B_{\varepsilon}(x_{i})}\left\|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}z[\xi^{\varepsilon}_{x}-\pi^{\varepsilon}_{x}](\mathrm{d}z)\right\|\mu(\mathrm{d}x)\leq\|y_{i+1}-y_{i}\|\mathds{1}_{B_{\gamma^{\overline{\varepsilon}}}}(x_{i})

and thus the claimed inequality (
28
) would immediately follows. Let us discuss how to ensure that this happens.

First, beware that the measurable set
E\equiv E[n,\mathcal{X},\overline{\varepsilon}]
actually depends on the function
\mathds{1}_{B_{\gamma^{\overline{\varepsilon}}}}
which itself depends on
n
, the family of points
\mathcal{X}:=\{(x_{i},y_{i}):i=1,\dots,n\}
under consideration, and
\overline{\varepsilon}>0
. To simplify the presentation, let us write
B_{\gamma^{\overline{\varepsilon}}}=B[n,\mathcal{X},\overline{\varepsilon}]
as well. The idea is then: since any subspace of a metrisable separable space is itself separable, the support
\textsf{supp}(\pi)
of the transport plan
\pi
is separable. Let us then consider
S\subset\textsf{supp}(\pi)
to be a dense and countable subset of
\textsf{supp}(\pi)
. For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the set
\mathbb{X}_{n}=\{\mathcal{X}\}
of all families
\mathcal{X}=\{(x_{1},y_{1}),\dots,(x_{n},y_{n})\}\subset S
of
n
elements inside
S
is therefore itself a countable set as a finite product of countable sets. Then, for each
\overline{\varepsilon}>0
, consider the set

E^{\overline{\varepsilon}}:=\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\bigcap_{\mathcal{X}\in\mathbb{X}_{n}}E[n,\mathcal{X},\overline{\varepsilon}]

The set
E^{\overline{\varepsilon}}
is measurable and
\mu(E^{\overline{\varepsilon}})=1
. Then, we finally consider

E:=\bigcap_{m\in\mathbb{N}}E^{1/m}

The set
E
is again measurable and
\mu(E)=1
. We then consider
\Gamma:=(E\times\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap\Gamma_{0}
where
\Gamma_{0}
is the
\pi
-full measure set over which the convergence ensured by the Lebesgue differentiation theorem holds in (
34
). We claim that the sought-for inequality (
28
) holds over
\Gamma
. This follows from the fact, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and any family

\mathcal{X}=\{(x_{1},y_{1}),\dots,(x_{n},y_{n})\}\subset\Gamma\subset(E\times\mathbb{R}^{d}),

(where
a priori

\mathcal{X}\not\subset S
) and for all
\eta>0
, there exists a family
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{X}_{n}
which is
\eta
-close to
\mathcal{X}
, in the sense that, letting

\mathcal{X}^{\prime}=\{(x_{1}^{\prime},y_{1}^{\prime}),\dots,(x_{n}^{\prime},y_{n}^{\prime})\},

we have
\|(x_{i},y_{i})-(x_{i}^{\prime},y_{i}^{\prime})\|<\eta
for all
i=1,\dots,n
. Now, let us consider
\eta=\varepsilon/2
. Then, by similar arguments to that of preceding, we have that
B[n,\mathcal{X},\varepsilon]\subset B[n,\mathcal{X}^{\prime},\varepsilon/2]
. In particular, selecting
\overline{\varepsilon}=1/m
for
m\in\mathbb{N}
big enough, we obtain

(43)

\frac{1}{\pi(B_{i}^{\varepsilon})}\int_{B_{i}^{\varepsilon}}\mathds{1}_{B[n,\mathcal{X},\overline{\varepsilon}]}(x)\pi(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y)\leq\frac{1}{\pi(B_{i}^{\varepsilon})}\int_{B_{i}^{\varepsilon}}\mathds{1}_{B[n,\mathcal{X}^{\prime},\overline{\varepsilon}/2]}(x)\pi(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y).

Finally, the right-hand side converges to
\mathds{1}_{B[n,\mathcal{X}^{\prime},\overline{\varepsilon}/2]}(x_{i})\|y_{i+1}-y_{i}\|
since
x_{i}\in E
. This terminates the proof.

∎

The inequality (
28
) is to be thought as the analogue to the
c
-cyclical monotonicity property in the special case of the barycentric cost. Theorem
5
says if
\pi
is optimal, then it must verify this analogous property. A natural question is to go the other way around, that is to consider the following question:
Is this true that, if
\pi
verifies (
28
), then
\pi
is optimal for the weak optimal transport with barycentric cost ?
It is rather straightforward that:

Proposition 5
.

Let
\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
has finite, i.e.
\mathscr{C}(\pi)<\infty
. Assume that
\mu
almost-surely all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have
x\neq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}z\pi_{x}(\mathrm{d}z)
. Also assume that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|y\|\nu(\mathrm{d}y)<\infty
. Then, if there exists
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
so that
\pi(\Gamma)=1
and so that for all
n\geq 1
and all
(x_{1},y_{1}),\dots,(x_{n},y_{n})\in\Gamma
, we have

(44)

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{(y_{i+1}-y_{i})^{\top}(b_{i}-x_{i})}{\|b_{i}-x_{i}\|}\geq 0

where
b_{i}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}z\pi_{x_{i}}(\mathrm{d}z)
, then
\pi
is optimal for the weak optimal transport problem (
WOT
) with barycentric cost.

Proof.

Because for
\mu
almost-surely all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have
x\neq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}z\pi_{x}(\mathrm{d}z)
, then the weak cost of transport
C
is differentiable for
\mu
almost-surely all
x
with respect to its second variable and its gradient reads

\nabla_{\rho}C(x,\pi_{x})(y)=\dfrac{(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}z\pi_{x}(\mathrm{d}z)-x)^{\top}y}{\|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}z\pi_{x}(\mathrm{d}z)-x\|}=:c(x,y)

To show that
\pi
is optimal, we must show that the first-order optimality condition holds, that is

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}c(x,y)[\gamma-\pi](\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y)\geq 0

for all
\gamma\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
so that
\mathscr{C}(\gamma)<\infty
,
provided it holds for cyclical perturbations
\eta\in\mathcal{C}_{\pi}
— as is the essence of (
44
).
This will follow by applying (the proof of) Theorem
4
. We therefore only need to check that the “cost”
c
defined above verifies the assumptions of this theorem. This function is real-valued and clearly Borel measurable — by measurability of
x\mapsto\pi_{x}
, as already discussed. A bit of discussion is to be made regarding the range of values that the function
c
above takes. We stated Theorem
4
under the assumption that
c
be non-negative. But this was mainly for convenience, and the theorem accommodates to slightly weaker assumptions, as already discussed above — see the
footnote
at p.
2
. In particular, we have

c(x,y)\geq-\|y\|:=g(y)

and
g
is integrable with respect to
\nu
since
\nu
is assumed to have finite first-moment. This terminates the proof.
∎

In the case where there are points
x\in X
so that
x=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}z\pi_{x}(\mathrm{d}z)
(with positive
\mu
-measure) the situation is slighty more involved, since to obtain optimality we need to be able to select a family of (extended) subgradients so that (
22
) holds. Altogether, there is
a priori
no reasons to obtain (
28
) by some limiting arguments as a sufficient criterion for optimality (which was obtained using a specific kind of subgradients,
i.e.
the one that realises the
supremum
in (
23
)). We could certainly explore more in detail this approach, but since this section is mainly intended to explain briefly what could be said in the non-differentiable case without going to much in details, we do not comment further on this point.

3.5.
A comment on the entropy-regularised problem

When the weak cost of transport
C:X\times\mathcal{P}(Y)\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is (strictly) convex with respect to its second-argument, this may push the (unique) solution of (
WOT
) inside the relative interior of
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
compared to the (
OT
) problem whose solution lives on the boundary of the constraint set (in fact, on extreme points).
In this case, the defining inequality in the
C
-cyclical monotonicity introduced in Definition
5
becomes an equality
. Indeed, the relative interior of the set of transport plan is made of those plans with full support,
i.e.

\textsf{supp}(\pi)=X\times Y
. This means that if
\eta\in\mathcal{R}_{\pi}
is an admissible perturbation, then so is its opposite
-\eta
. This is for instance the case for the
entropy-regularised optimal transport
problem, which we recall to be an instance of the weak optimal transport problem with

C(x,\rho):=\varepsilon\int_{Y}\ln\rho\,d\rho+\int_{Y}c(x,y)\rho(\mathrm{d}y)

where
\varepsilon>0
is the regularisation parameter. The weak cost of transport defined as such is differentiable in its second variable and its gradient (in the sense of Section
3.1.1
) reads

\nabla_{\rho}C(x,\rho)(y)=c(x,y)+\varepsilon\ln\rho(y)+\varepsilon.

As it is well-known, the unique solution to the entropic optimal transport lives in the relative interior of
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
. But then, we obtain that for all
(x_{1},y_{1}),\dots,(x_{n},y_{n})\in\Gamma
where
\Gamma\subset X\times Y
is some set over which
\pi
concentrates, we have

(45)

\boxed{\sum_{i=1}^{n}c(x_{i},y_{i})+\varepsilon\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ln\pi_{x_{i}}(y_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c(x_{i},y_{i+1})+\varepsilon\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ln\pi_{x_{i}}(y_{i+1})}

where as usual
y_{n+1}:=y_{1}
.
The equality (
45
) is then the analogue of the cyclical monotonicity property for the entropic optimal transport problem
. This equality is not at all shocking, since using the well-known duality theory for the entropic optimal transport problem, we know that the optimiser
\pi^{*}
is unique and given by the Gibbs measure

\pi^{*}(x,y)=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}(c(x,y)-f(x)-g(y))\right)

for some entropic Kantorovich potentials
f:X\to\mathbb{R}
and
g:Y\to\mathbb{R}
. In particular, the equality (
45
) is in fact immediately verified.

Appendix A
Proof of Lemma
2

In this appendix, we prove Lemma
2
whose statement is repeated below for convenience of the readers. It is not unlikely that this result is known and common knowledge to some audiences, but as it is we were not able to find any reference for it, so that we prove it here.

Lemma 5
.

Let
\gamma\in\mathcal{P}(Z)
where
Z
is a Polish space (or in fact only second countable) equipped with its Borel
\sigma
-algebra. Then, for all
z\in Z
, there exists a sequence
(E_{k}(z))_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
of Borel sets — all containing
z
— so that
\gamma(E_{k}(z))>0
for
\gamma
almost-surely all
z\in Z
and all
k\in\mathbb{N}
and so that for any measurable function
\phi:Z\to\mathbb{R}
which is integrable with respect to
\gamma
, it holds that

(46)

\frac{1}{\gamma(E_{k}(z))}\int_{E_{k}(z)}\phi(w)\gamma(\mathrm{d}w)\xrightarrow[k\to\infty]{}\phi(z)

for
\gamma
-a.s. all point
z\in Z
.

Proof of Lemma
2
.

As a Polish space,
Z
is second countable, meaning that its topology has a countable base
(U_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
. Let us define
\mathcal{F}_{k}:=\sigma(U_{1},\dots,U_{k})
the
\sigma-
algebra generated by
U_{1},\dots,U_{k}
, so that
\mathcal{B}(Z)=\sigma(\bigcup_{k\geq 1}\mathcal{F}_{k})
where
\mathcal{B}(Z)
is the Borel
\sigma
-algebra on
Z
. Since
\mathcal{F}_{k}
is finitely generated by
k
elements, it has at most
2^{k}
atoms
e_{1},e_{2},\dots,e_{n_{k}}\in\mathcal{F}_{k}
. Then, let us define
E_{k}(z)
for all
z\in Z
to be the atom in
\mathcal{F}_{k}
that contains
z
. We remark that for
\gamma
almost-surely all
z\in Z
, we have
\gamma(E_{k}(z))>0
. Indeed, if
A_{k}:=\{z\in Z:\gamma(E_{k}(z))=0\}
, then
A_{k}=\cup_{i\in I_{k}}e_{i}
where
I_{k}\subset\{1,\dots,n_{k}\}
is the set of indices so that
i\in I_{k}
if
\gamma(e_{i})=0
. Since the atoms are disjoint by definition, we have
\gamma(A_{k})=\sum_{i\in I_{k}}\gamma(e_{i})=0
. By considering the measurable set
A=\cup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}A_{k}\in\mathcal{B}(Z)
, we obtain that for
\gamma
almost-surely all
z\in Z
and for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
we have
\gamma(E_{k}(z))>0
. Then, for any function
\phi:Z\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
which is integrable with respect to
\gamma
, we have that
\mathbb{E}[\phi|\mathcal{F}_{k}]
is constant and precisely given by

(47)

\mathbb{E}[\phi|\mathcal{F}_{k}]=\frac{1}{\gamma(E_{k}(z))}\int_{E_{k}(z)}\phi(w)\,\gamma(\mathrm{d}w)

Now, it is a known fact that
\mathbb{E}[\phi|\mathcal{F}_{k}](z)\to\phi(z)
as
k\to\infty
for
\gamma
-a.s. all point
z\in Z
. Indeed, this follows from
Levy upwards theorem
— which is a peculiar case of the celebrated Doob’s martingale convergence theorems, see
e.g.

[
11
]
.
∎

Remark 7
.

We remark that the exact same construction yields that for all
\Phi\in L^{1}(\gamma\otimes\gamma)
, it holds that

(48)

\frac{1}{\gamma\otimes\gamma(E_{k}(z)\times E_{k}(t))}\int_{E_{k}(z)\times E_{k}(t)}\Phi d\gamma\otimes\gamma\xrightarrow[k\to\infty]{}\Phi(z,t)

for
\gamma\otimes\gamma
-a.s. all couples
(z,t)\in Z\times Z
. Here, we emphasise that we can consider the same
E_{k}
’s are constructed above. Indeed, one replaces in the above proof
\mathcal{F}_{k}
by the finitely-generated
\sigma
-algebra

\mathcal{G}_{k}:=\sigma(\{U_{i}\times U_{j}\,|\,i,j=1,\dots,k\})

(which in fact is the product
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{F}_{k}\otimes\mathcal{F}_{k}
). The atoms of
\mathcal{G}_{k}
are the product of the atoms of
\mathcal{F}_{k}
, and the claim follows.
