Magnitude homology of real hyperplane arrangements
Junnosuke Koizumi, Ye Liu

Abstract
. We define and study the magnitude and magnitude homology of a real hyperplane arrangement by regarding its tope graph as a metric space. We prove several structural results for the magnitude of arrangements, including a symmetry formula, palindromicity of the numerator and denominator, a face decomposition formula, and results on the sign pattern of the magnitude power series. For the magnitude homology of arrangements, we obtain combinatorial formulas for small lengths and show that it detects Boolean arrangements. We also lift the face decomposition formula to a homological decomposition and derive explicit formulas for the diagonal magnitude Betti numbers. Another notable feature is that the magnitude Euler characteristic satisfies a reciprocity theorem analogous to Ehrhart–Macdonald reciprocity. We conclude by presenting several conjectures. In particular, we conjecture that the magnitude homology of an arrangement is torsion-free and is determined by the intersection lattice.

1.
Introduction

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an arrangement of hyperplanes passing through the origin in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
The hyperplanes of
\mathcal{A}
divide
\mathbb{R}^{d}
into regions called chambers.
We denote the set of chambers by
\mathrm{Ch}(\mathcal{A})
.
The
tope graph

\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A})
of
\mathcal{A}
is the graph with vertex set
\mathrm{Ch}(\mathcal{A})
and with edges joining two chambers separated by a single hyperplane.
More generally, the tope graph is defined for an oriented matroid.
Although its definition is elementary, the tope graph contains essentially all combinatorial information about a hyperplane arrangement:

Theorem 1.1

(Theorem 4.2.14 of
[
4
]
)
.

A simple oriented matroid is uniquely determined (up to reorientation) by its unlabeled tope graph.

We refer to Figure 1 of
[
34
]
for a detailed diagram of implications. In particular, if two arrangements have isomorphic tope graphs, then they have homeomorphic complexified complements, isomorphic intersection lattices, and isomorphic filtered Varchenko–Gelfand algebras. Hence we may study
\mathcal{A}
via
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A})
.

In this paper, by applying the emerging theory of magnitude to tope graphs, we define the magnitude and magnitude homology of arrangements and initiate the study of these invariants.
Magnitude is a cardinality-like invariant of enriched categories and metric spaces, defined by Leinster
[
24
,
21
]
; for metric spaces, it can be interpreted as measuring their “effective size.”
The magnitude of a graph is defined by regarding the graph as a metric space via the shortest-path metric
[
25
]
.
The magnitude of a graph is a rational function defined using the inverse of the
q
-distance matrix; in the case of hyperplane arrangements, this matrix coincides with the classical Varchenko matrix.
The notion of magnitude homology, a categorification of magnitude, was introduced for graphs by Hepworth and Willerton
[
17
]
, and for enriched categories and metric spaces by Leinster and Shulman
[
23
]
.
It is a bigraded homology theory that reflects deep metric properties.

Our motivation is twofold.
First, magnitude and magnitude homology give new invariants of real hyperplane arrangements.
What information about an arrangement is reflected by these new invariants remains mysterious and is worth exploring.
Second, the tope graphs of arrangements form a class of graphs with many distinctive features, such as antipodal symmetry and decompositions into faces, and may therefore serve as a source of interesting examples in the magnitude theory of graphs.

At the decategorified level, we show that the magnitude of an arrangement,
\mathrm{Mag}(\mathcal{A},q)
, satisfies remarkable structural properties.
We prove a symmetry relating
\mathrm{Mag}(\mathcal{A},q)
and
\mathrm{Mag}(\mathcal{A},q^{-1})
, which reflects the antipodal symmetry of the tope graph.
The reduced rational form of
\mathrm{Mag}(\mathcal{A},q)
has palindromic numerator and denominator, and all its poles are roots of unity.
Moreover, if we define the
interior magnitude
by
\mathrm{Mag}^{\circ}(\mathcal{A},q)=(-1)^{\rank\mathcal{A}}q^{\#\mathcal{A}}\mathrm{Mag}(\mathcal{A},q)
, then the following
face decomposition formula
holds:

\mathrm{Mag}(\mathcal{A},q)=\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{A})}\mathrm{Mag}^{\circ}(\mathcal{A}_{F},q).

Here
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{A})
denotes the set of faces (covectors) of
\mathcal{A}
, and
\mathcal{A}_{F}=\{H\in\mathcal{A}\mid F\subset H\}
.
This serves as a discrete
q
-analogue of the classical decomposition of the Euler characteristic of a zonotope.
We then investigate the sign pattern of the magnitude power series. In particular, we show that for arrangements of rank at most
3
, the signs of the magnitude coefficients eventually alternate. We also present an example with non-alternating magnitude.

The magnitude homology
MH_{k,\ell}(\mathcal{A})
of an arrangement
\mathcal{A}
is a more refined invariant.
By analyzing the structure of gated subgraphs arising from the faces of an arrangement, we establish the basic properties of this invariant.
First, we describe all magnitude homology groups of length
\ell\leq 2
in terms of the combinatorics of the arrangement.
Next, we show that magnitude homology detects Boolean arrangements: an arrangement is Boolean if and only if its magnitude homology is concentrated on the diagonal.
Furthermore, we define the
interior magnitude homology

MH^{\circ}_{k,\ell}(\mathcal{A})
, which is a categorification of the interior magnitude
\mathrm{Mag}^{\circ}(\mathcal{A})
, and lift the face decomposition formula for magnitude to the homological level.
In particular, we obtain the
Euler characteristic reciprocity

\chi^{\circ}_{\ell+\#\mathcal{A}}(\mathcal{A})=(-1)^{\mathrm{rank}\mathcal{A}}\chi_{\ell}(\mathcal{A}),

where
\chi_{\ell}(\mathcal{A})
(resp.
\chi^{\circ}_{\ell}(\mathcal{A})
) is the Euler characteristic of
MH_{*,\ell}(\mathcal{A})
(resp.
MH^{\circ}_{*,\ell}(\mathcal{A})
).
This can be regarded as a new combinatorial reciprocity theorem analogous to the Ehrhart–Macdonald reciprocity theorem for lattice polytopes
[
11
,
26
]
and Stanley’s reciprocity theorem for posets
[
30
]
.
As an application of the homological face decomposition, we give a complete description of the diagonal magnitude Betti numbers in terms of the intersection lattice.

The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 recalls the necessary material from metric graph theory and the theory of hyperplane arrangements, establishing the foundational link between gated subgraphs and covector composition. Section 3 investigates the basic properties and structure of the magnitude rational function. Section 4 focuses on the sign pattern of magnitude power series. Section 5 explores magnitude homology, presenting the Boolean diagonality theorem and the face decomposition theorem. Finally, Section 6 poses several open problems and conjectures for future study. The appendix contains results from numerous SageMath computer experiments.

Recollections

2.1.
Metric graph theory

For a graph
G=(V,E)
, a
walk
(or
gallery
) of length
k
from a vertex
x\in V
to a vertex
y\in V
is a sequence of vertices
(x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
such that
x_{0}=x
,
x_{k}=y
, and
\{x_{i},x_{i+1}\}\in E
for
i=0,1,\ldots,k-1
. We define a distance function
d:V\times V\to\mathbb{Z}\cup\{\infty\}
by declaring
d(x,y)
to be the minimum length among walks from
x
to
y
. If there is no walk from
x
to
y
, we set
d(x,y)=\infty
. Then
(G,d)
is a generalized metric space. In particular, when
G
is connected, that is, when there is a walk between any pair of vertices,
(G,d)
is a metric space. We call the maximum value of
d
the
diameter
of
G
, denoted by
\diam(G)
.

A subgraph
H
of
G
can be equipped with a metric in two ways. One is the restriction of
d_{G}
to
H
, and the other is the graph metric
d_{H}
on
H
itself. We say that
H
is an
isometric subgraph
of
G
if the two metrics coincide, that is, if
d_{H}(x,y)=d_{G}(x,y)
for
x,y\in V(H)
.

In a graph
G
, a walk from
x
to
y
of minimal length
d(x,y)
is called a
minimal walk
. For
x,y\in V
, define the
closed interval

[x,y]_{G}
as the set of all vertices traversed by a minimal walk from
x
to
y
. We equip it with the partial order
u\prec_{G}v
if there is a minimal walk from
x
to
y
that traverses
u
before
v
. Note that
[x,y]_{G}
has a unique minimal element
x
and a unique maximal element
y
. A vertex
u
belongs to
[x,y]_{G}
if and only if
d(x,y)=d(x,u)+d(u,y)
. More generally, vertices
u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}
form a strictly linearly ordered chain in
[x,y]_{G}
, that is,
x\prec_{G}u_{1}\prec_{G}\cdots\prec_{G}u_{k}\prec_{G}y
, if and only if

(2.1)

d(x,y)=d(x,u_{1})+d(u_{1},u_{2})+\cdots+d(u_{k-1},u_{k})+d(u_{k},y).

We define the
open interval

(x,y)_{G}
as the subposet of
[x,y]_{G}
obtained by excluding
x
and
y
.

We say that a subgraph
H
of
G
is
convex
in
G
if, for any
x,y\in V(H)
, every minimal walk from
x
to
y
traverses only vertices in
H
; equivalently,
[x,y]_{G}\subseteq V(H)
for
x,y\in V(H)
. Every convex subgraph is an isometric subgraph, but the converse is false.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
G
be a connected graph, let
H
be an isometric subgraph of
G
, and let
x\in V(G)
. We say that
H
is
gated with respect to

x
if there is
u\in V(H)
such that, for any
v\in V(H)
,

d(x,v)=d(x,u)+d(u,v).

Such a vertex
u
must be unique, since the above equation says that
u
is the closest vertex to
x
in
H
. We call
u
the
gate
of
H
with respect to
x
. Intuitively, every minimal walk from
x
to a vertex of
H
enters
H
at the gate. We say that
H
is a
gated subgraph
of
G
if it is gated with respect to every vertex of
G
. In this case, we call the map
p:G\to H
taking any vertex of
G
to its gate in
H
the
gate projection
. Note that the restriction of
p
to
H
is the identity map
\text{id}_{H}
.

Proposition 2.2

(
[
2
, Proposition A.1]
)
.

A gated subgraph is necessarily convex.

Proof.

Suppose
H
is gated in
G
. Choose a minimal walk in
G
from
x
to
y
for
x,y\in V(H)
, and let
z
be an arbitrary vertex in this walk. We need to show that
z\in V(H)
. Since
H
is gated, we have

d(z,x)=d(z,p(z))+d(p(z),x),~d(z,y)=d(z,p(z))+d(p(z),y).

Since
z
belongs to
[x,y]_{G}
, adding these two equations yields

d(x,y)=d(x,z)+d(z,y)=d(x,p(z))+d(p(z),y)+2d(z,p(z))\geq d(x,y)+2d(z,p(z)).

This forces
d(z,p(z))=0
, i.e.
z=p(z)\in V(H)
.
∎

In this paper, the graph homomorphisms we use are
distance non-increasing
maps (1-Lipschitz maps)
f:G\to H
, that is, maps
f:V(G)\to V(H)
such that
d_{H}(f(x),f(y))\leq d_{G}(x,y)
for
x,y\in V(G)
. Note that gate projections are distance non-increasing.

2.2.
Graph magnitude

See Leinster
[
25
]
for details.
For a finite simple graph
G=(V,E)
, define the Zeta matrix
Z_{G}=Z_{G}(q)=[q^{d(x,y)}]_{x,y\in V}
, where
q^{\infty}=0
by convention.
Note that
Z_{G}(0)
is the identity matrix and hence
\det Z_{G}(q)\in\mathbb{Z}[q]
is a polynomial with constant term
1
.
The rational function
1/\det Z_{G}(q)\in\mathbb{Q}(q)
can then be expressed as a power series in
\mathbb{Z}[\![q]\!]
.
In particular,
Z_{G}(q)
is invertible over
\mathbb{Q}(q)
, with inverse
Z_{G}(q)^{-1}=\frac{1}{\det Z_{G}(q)}\mathrm{adj}~Z_{G}(q)
, whose entries can be expressed as power series in
\mathbb{Z}[\![q]\!]
.

The
magnitude
of
G
is defined by the sum of all entries of
Z_{G}(q)^{-1}
.

\mathrm{Mag}(G)=\mathrm{Mag}(G,q):=\sum_{x,y\in V}Z_{G}(q)^{-1}_{x,y}\in\mathbb{Q}(q)~(\mathrm{or}~\mathbb{Z}[\![q]\!]).

In general, the sum of all entries of a matrix
A
is equal to
\mathbf{1}^{T}A\mathbf{1}
, where
\mathbf{1}
is the column vector of appropriate size with all entries equal to
1
.
If
B
is invertible, then the sum of all entries of
B^{-1}
can be computed from
\mathbf{1}^{T}B^{-1}\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{1}^{T}\mathbf{x}
, where
\mathbf{x}
is the unique solution of
B\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{1}
. For a graph
G
, define the weight vector
w_{G}:=Z_{G}^{-1}\mathbf{1}
as the unique solution to
Z_{G}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{1}
. In other words, as a function
w_{G}:V\to\mathbb{Q}(q)
,
w_{G}(x)=\sum_{y\in V}Z_{G}(q)^{-1}_{x,y}
is the
x
-row sum of
Z_{G}^{-1}
.
Then the magnitude is given by

\mathrm{Mag}(G)=\mathbf{1}^{T}w_{G}=\sum_{x\in V}w_{G}(x).

The magnitude of a vertex-transitive graph can be computed explicitly.

Proposition 2.3

(
[
25
, Lemma 3.2]
)
.

If
G
is vertex-transitive (i.e.
\mathrm{Aut}(G)
acts transitively on
V
), then

\mathrm{Mag}(G)=\frac{\#V}{\sum_{x\in V}q^{d(g,x)}}

for any
g\in V
.

Proof.

The denominator
s(g)=\sum_{x\in V}q^{d(g,x)}
is independent of
g\in V
by the assumption that
G
is vertex-transitive, and we denote it by
s=s(g)
.
It is then easy to see that
w_{G}=[1/s\cdots 1/s]^{T}
is the solution of
Z_{G}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{1}
.
We conclude
\mathrm{Mag}(G)=\#V/s
.
∎

Example 2.4
.

Let
K_{n}
be the complete graph on
n
vertices and let
C_{n}
be the cycle graph on
n
vertices. They are vertex-transitive.
Their magnitudes are given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{Mag}(K_{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{1+(n-1)q},

\displaystyle\mathrm{Mag}(C_{2n})

\displaystyle=\frac{2n(q-1)}{(q^{n}-1)(q+1)}=\frac{2n}{(1+q)(1+q+q^{2}+\cdots+q^{n-1})},

\displaystyle\mathrm{Mag}(C_{2n-1})

\displaystyle=\frac{(2n-1)(q-1)}{2q^{n}-q-1}=\frac{2n-1}{1+2q+2q^{2}+\cdots+2q^{n-1}}.

Magnitude behaves like cardinality in the following sense.

Proposition 2.5

(
[
25
, Lemmas 3.5 and 3.6]
)
.

Let
G,H
be graphs. Write
G\sqcup H
and
G\square H
for the disjoint union and Cartesian product of
G
and
H
, respectively. Then

\mathrm{Mag}(G\sqcup H)=\mathrm{Mag}(G)+\mathrm{Mag}(H),\quad\mathrm{Mag}(G\square H)=\mathrm{Mag}(G)\mathrm{Mag}(H).

Under certain conditions, magnitude satisfies inclusion-exclusion.

Theorem 2.6

(
[
25
, Theorem 4.9]
)
.

If a graph
X
has a decomposition into subgraphs
X=G\cup H
such that
G\cap H
is convex in
X
and gated in
H
, then

\mathrm{Mag}(X)=\mathrm{Mag}(G)+\mathrm{Mag}(H)-\mathrm{Mag}(G\cap H).

2.3.
Magnitude homology of graphs

For a graph
G
, we call a
(k+1)
-tuple
\vec{x}=(x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})\in V^{k+1}
of vertices of
G
a
k
-chain of
G
and define its length by
\ell(\vec{x})=d(x_{0},x_{1})+d(x_{1},x_{2})+\cdots+d(x_{k-1},x_{k})
. We say that a
k
-chain
\vec{x}
is
proper
if adjacent nodes are distinct, that is, if
x_{i}\neq x_{i+1}
for all
i
. Let
P_{k}(G)
be the set of proper
k
-chains of
G
, and let
P_{k,\ell}(G)
be the set of proper
k
-chains of length
\ell
. The following result of Leinster is the starting point of the categorification of graph magnitude introduced by Hepworth–Willerton
[
17
]
.

Proposition 2.7

(
[
25
, Proposition 3.9]
)
.

For a graph
G
, we have

\mathrm{Mag}(G)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{k}\sum_{\vec{x}\in P_{k}(G)}q^{\ell(\vec{x})}\in\mathbb{Z}[\![q]\!].

In other words, if
\mathrm{Mag}(G)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}c_{\ell}q^{\ell}\in\mathbb{Z}[\![q]\!]
, then

c_{\ell}=\sum_{k=0}^{\ell}(-1)^{k}\#P_{k,\ell}(G).

In particular, we have
c_{0}=\#V
and
c_{1}=-2\#E
.

Let
MC_{k,\ell}(G)
be the free abelian group generated by
P_{k,\ell}(G)
. We define the boundary operator
\partial:MC_{k,\ell}(G)\to MC_{k-1,\ell}(G)
by
\partial=\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}(-1)^{i-1}\partial_{i}
, where
\partial_{i}:MC_{k,\ell}(G)\to MC_{k-1,\ell}(G)
is defined by

\partial_{i}(x_{0},\ldots,x_{k})=\begin{cases}(x_{0},\ldots,\widehat{x_{i}},\ldots,x_{k}),&\text{ if }d(x_{i-1},x_{i+1})=d(x_{i-1},x_{i})+d(x_{i},x_{i+1});\\
0,&\text{ otherwise},\end{cases}

where
\widehat{x_{i}}
denotes deletion of
x_{i}
. One can verify that
\partial^{2}=0
.
We call the homology of the chain complex
(MC_{*,\ell}(G),\partial)
the length
\ell

magnitude homology
of
G
, denoted by
MH_{*,\ell}(G)
. Since the boundary map preserves endpoints, we have a decomposition

MC_{k,\ell}(G)=\bigoplus_{u,v\in V(G)}MC_{k,\ell}(G;u,v),

where
MC_{k,\ell}(G;u,v)
is the subgroup generated by chains
P_{k,\ell}(G;u,v)=\{\vec{x}\in P_{k,\ell}(G)\mid x_{0}=u,\ x_{k}=v\}
. These subgroups form subcomplexes and induce a decomposition of homology groups

(2.2)

MH_{k,\ell}(G)=\bigoplus_{u,v\in V(G)}MH_{k,\ell}(G;u,v).

We define the magnitude Betti number by
\beta_{k,\ell}(G)=\rank MH_{k,\ell}(G)
and the length
\ell
magnitude Euler characteristic by
\chi_{\ell}(G)=\sum_{k}(-1)^{k}\beta_{k,\ell}(G)
.

Proposition 2.8

(
[
17
, Propositions 2.9 and 2.10]
)
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph.

(1)

MH_{0,0}(G)
is free abelian of rank
\beta_{0,0}(G)=\#V
.

(2)

MH_{1,1}(G)
is free abelian of rank
\beta_{1,1}(G)=2\#E
.

(3)

If
MH_{k,\ell}(G)\neq 0
, then
\ell/\diam(G)\leq k\leq\ell
, and the inequality
\ell/\diam(G)\leq k
is strict if
\diam(G)>1
and
\ell>0
.

The following proposition is a straightforward consequence of Proposition
2.7
and says that magnitude can be recovered from magnitude homology.

Proposition 2.9

(
[
17
, Theorem 2.8]
)
.

Let
G
be a graph, and
\mathrm{Mag}(G)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}c_{\ell}q^{\ell}\in\mathbb{Z}[\![q]\!]

c_{\ell}=\chi_{\ell}(G)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{k}\beta_{k,\ell}(G)=\sum_{k=\ell/\diam(G)}^{\ell}(-1)^{k}\beta_{k,\ell}(G).

Proposition 2.10

(
[
17
, Proposition 3.3]
)

Magnitude homology is a functor from the category of finite graphs with distance non-increasing maps to the category of bigraded abelian groups.

A direct application of functoriality is as follows. Let
G

If

Definition 2.17
.

We define the length
\ell

geodesic magnitude complex
by

MC_{*,\ell}^{\mathrm{geod}}(G):=\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}u,v\in V(G)\\
d(u,v)=\ell\end{subarray}}MC_{*,\ell}(G;u,v).

Its homology is called the length
\ell

geodesic magnitude homology
.
The geodesic magnitude homology
MH^{\mathrm{geod}}_{k,\ell}(G)
is a direct summand of
MH_{k,\ell}(G)
.
It admits a decomposition

MH_{k,\ell}^{\mathrm{geod}}(G)=\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}u,v\in V(G)\\
d(u,v)=\ell\end{subarray}}MH_{k,\ell}(G;u,v).

Note that
MC_{*,\ell}^{\mathrm{geod}}(G)
and
MH_{k,\ell}^{\mathrm{geod}}(G)
are trivial if
\ell>\diam(G)
.

We define the geodesic magnitude Betti number by
\beta_{k,\ell}^{\mathrm{geod}}(G)=\rank MH_{k,\ell}^{\mathrm{geod}}(G)
and the length
\ell
geodesic magnitude Euler characteristic by
\chi_{\ell}^{\mathrm{geod}}(G)=\sum_{k}(-1)^{k}\beta_{k,\ell}^{\mathrm{geod}}(G)
. Observe that
\beta_{k,\ell}(G)\geq\beta_{k,\ell}^{\mathrm{geod}}(G)
.

Geodesic magnitude homology is a coarse approximation of magnitude homology. As a more refined approximation, Kaneta–Yoshinaga
[
20
]
introduced
geodesically simple magnitude homology
.
For a proper chain
\vec{x}=(x_{0},\ldots,x_{k})\in P_{k}(G)
, an internal node
x_{i}
(
i=1,2,\ldots,k-1
) is called a
smooth

\vec{x}
if
d(x_{i-1},x_{i+1})=d(x_{i-1},x_{i})+d(x_{i},x_{i+1})
. A node that is not smooth is called a
singular
node. Note that endpoints are singular. We write
\varphi(\vec{x})
for the subchain of
\vec{x}
consisting of all singular nodes and call it the frame of
\vec{x}
. Note that
\varphi(\vec{x})
is not necessarily proper.

Definition 2.18
.

A proper chain
\vec{x}
is
geodesically simple
if
\ell(\varphi(\vec{x}))=\ell(\vec{x})
.

Definition 2.19
.

We define
MC_{k,\ell}^{\mathrm{gs}}(G)
to be the subgroup of
MC_{k,\ell}(G)
generated by geodesically simple chains.
Then
MC_{*,\ell}^{\mathrm{gs}}(G)
forms a subcomplex of
MC_{k,\ell}(G)

[
20
, Propositions 3.6 and 3.7]
.
Its homology is called the
geodesically simple magnitude homology
of
G
, denoted by

.

How well geodesically simple magnitude homology approximates magnitude homology is controlled by the notion of a
4
-cut.

Definition 2.20
.

A proper
3
-chain
(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in P_{3}(G)
is called a
4
-cut
of

if
(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2})
and
(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})
are geodesic but
(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})
is not geodesic. Let
m_{G}
be the minimum length of
4
-cuts of
G
. We set

if

has no
4
-cuts.
Note that
m_{G}\geq 3
.
Any proper chain with length smaller than

is geodesically simple.

Lemma 2.21
.

If
\vec{x}=(x_{0},\ldots,x_{k})\in P_{k}(G)
satisfies
\varphi(\vec{x})=(x_{0},x_{k})
and
\vec{x}
is not geodesically simple, i.e.
(x_{i-1},x_{i},x_{i+1})
is geodesic for
i=1,\ldots,k-1
, but
\vec{x}
is not geodesic, then
\vec{x}
has a subchain that is a
4
-cut.

Proof.

Let
\vec{y}=(y_{0},\ldots,y_{p})
be a contiguous subchain of
\vec{x}
, that is,
y_{0}=x_{i},y_{1}=x_{i+1},\ldots,y_{p}=x_{i+p}
for some
i
, such that
\vec{y}
is not geodesic and
p
is as small as possible. This means
(y_{0},\ldots,y_{p-1})
and
(y_{1},\ldots,y_{p})
are geodesic. Then

. We claim
\vec{z}:=(y_{0},y_{1},y_{p-1},y_{p})
is a
4
-cut. First note that
y_{p-1}\neq y_{1}
as they are connected by a geodesic
(y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{p-1})
. That
(y_{0},y_{1},y_{p-1})
and
(y_{1},y_{p-1},y_{p})
are geodesic follows from the assumption that
(y_{0},\ldots,y_{p-1})
and
(y_{1},\ldots,y_{p})
are geodesic. Moreover,
\ell(\vec{z})=d(y_{0},y_{1})+d(y_{1},y_{p-1})+d(y_{p-1},y_{p})=\sum_{i=1}^{p}d(y_{i-1},y_{i})>d(y_{0},y_{p})
, since

is not geodesic. This shows

is not geodesic and hence is a

∎

Let
\vec{a}\in P_{m,\ell}(G)
and

-framed magnitude homology
of

. Kaneta–Yoshinaga proved that the chain complex

decomposes as

(2.3)

MC_{k,\ell}^{\mathrm{gs}}(G)=\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\leq k\\
\vec{a}\in P_{m,\ell}(G)\end{subarray}}MC^{\mathrm{gs}}_{k,\vec{a}}(G),

and the same is true for the homology groups.

Theorem 2.22

(
[
20
, Theorem 3.12]
)
.

Let

Proposition 2.23

(
[

For

Any proper chain
\vec{x}=(a,x_{1},\ldots,x_{k-1},b)\in P_{k,\ell}(G;a,b)

Mapping

to the linearly ordered chain

∎

Corollary 2.24
.

The geodesic magnitude homology admits a decomposition

MH_{k,\ell}^{\mathrm{geod}}(G)\cong\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}a,b\in V(G)\\

2.4.
Hyperplane arrangements

The standard references are
[
28
,
31

. Let
\mathcal{A}
be an arrangement in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
For two chambers
C,C^{\prime}\in\mathrm{Ch}(\mathcal{A})

for the set of hyperplanes in
\mathcal{A}
that separate
C
and
C^{\prime}
.
The
tope graph

\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A})
of

is the graph with vertex set
\mathrm{Ch}(\mathcal{A})
in which two vertices
C
and

are connected by an edge if

consists of a single hyperplane.
The graph metric

satisfies

.
Note that
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A})
is a
partial cube
, that is, it is isomorphic to an isometric subgraph of the hypercube graph
Q_{\mathcal{A}}=\{+,-\}^{\mathcal{A}}
.

We denote by

the
intersection lattice
of
\mathcal{A}
, ordered by reverse inclusion. An element of
L(\mathcal{A})
is called a

. Note that the maximal flat is
\hat{1}=\cap_{H\in\mathcal{A}}H=:\cap\mathcal{A}

\mathcal{A}
, and the minimal flat

is the ambient space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The arrangement is called

if the center is the origin. The rank of a flat

\rank X=\codim X

\mathcal{A}
is defined as

for the set of flats of rank

characteristic polynomial
of
\mathcal{A}
by
\chi(\mathcal{A},t)=\sum_{X\in L(\mathcal{A})}\mu(\hat{0},X)t^{\dim X}

[
35
]

The arrangement
\mathcal{A}
determines a stratification of

(covectors). Let
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{A})
be the poset of faces, ordered by closure inclusion. The unique minimal face is the center

\mathrm{Ch}(\mathcal{A})

always exists, while their join

taking a face

For a face

2.5.
Tope graphs and graph-theoretic properties

For an arrangement
\mathcal{A}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, note that the tope graph
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A})
is isomorphic to the
1
-skeleton of
Z(\mathcal{A})
. For a face
F\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{A})
, we let
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A})_{F}
be the induced subgraph of
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A})
with vertices
\mathrm{Ch}(\mathcal{A})_{F}=\{C\in\mathrm{Ch}(\mathcal{A})\mid F\leq C\}
; equivalently,
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A})_{F}
is the
1
-skeleton of the face
z_{F}
of
Z(\mathcal{A})
. We call
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A})_{F}
the
F
-subgraph of
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A})
. We first prove a useful lemma.

Lemma 2.29
.

For
F\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{A})
, the subgraph
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A})_{F}
of
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A})
is gated with gate projection
D\mapsto FD
.

Proof.

We must show that the equality

(2.4)

d(D,C)=d(D,FD)+d(FD,C)

holds for
D\in\mathrm{Ch}(\mathcal{A})
and
C\in\mathrm{Ch}(\mathcal{A})_{F}
. In terms of sign vectors, the distance
d(X,Y)=\sum_{H\in\mathcal{A}}d(X,Y)_{H}
is the Hamming distance (the number of unequal signs), where

d(X,Y)_{H}=\begin{cases}1,\text{ if }X_{H}\neq Y_{H};\\
0,\text{ if }X_{H}=Y_{H}.\end{cases}

We prove that both sides of equation (
2.4
) have the same contribution on each
H\in\mathcal{A}
. If
H\notin\mathcal{A}_{F}
, then
F_{H}\neq 0
and
(FD)_{H}=F_{H}
; also, since
C\in\mathrm{Ch}(\mathcal{A})_{F}
, we have
C_{H}=F_{H}
. Therefore (
2.4
) evaluated at
H
is
d(D,C)_{H}=d(D,FD)_{H}+0
, which is true since both sides equal
1
if
D_{H}\neq F_{H}
and
0
if
D_{H}=F_{H}
. Next suppose
H\in\mathcal{A}_{F}
. Then
F_{H}=0
and
d(D,FD)_{H}=0
. The equation (
2.4
) evaluated at
H
is
d(D,C)_{H}=0+d(FD,C)_{H}
, which is true since
D_{H}=(FD)_{H}
.
∎

Let

n
hyperplanes in
\mathbb{R}^{r}
. For example, consider a generic arrangement
\mathcal{A}=U_{r,r+1}
. The tope graph
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A})
is isomorphic to the induced subgraph of
Q_{\mathcal{A}}=\{+,-\}^{\mathcal{A}}\cong Q_{r+1}
obtained by removing the all-
+
vector and the all-
-
vector.

Example 2.32
.

Consider a finite Coxeter system
(W,S)
and the associated Coxeter arrangement
\mathcal{A}_{W}
. The zonotope
Z(\mathcal{A}_{W})
is the
W
-permutahedron, and the tope graph
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A}_{W})
is isomorphic to the Cayley graph of
(W,S)
.

Tope graphs have the following graph-theoretic properties. Recall that a chamber
C
is
simplicial
if it is a simplicial cone. An arrangement is
simplicial
if all its chambers are simplicial.

Proposition 2.33

(
[
4
, Propositions 4.2.15 and 4.4.8]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an arrangement of
\rank\mathcal{A}=r
. The tope graph
\mathcal{T}=\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A})
has the following properties as an unlabeled graph.

(1)

For each vertex
C
, there is a unique vertex
-C
such that
d(C,-C)=\diam(\mathcal{T})
.

(2)

The mapping
C\mapsto-C
is a fixed-point-free automorphism of
\mathcal{T}
.

(3)

\mathcal{T}
is bipartite.

(4)

For each vertex
C
, we have
\deg(C)\geq r
, where equality holds if and only if
C
is simplicial.

(5)

\mathcal{T}
is
r
-connected.

2.6.
Varchenko matrix

Varchenko introduced a bilinear form associated with an arrangement and computed its determinant
[
33
]
. Generalizations to oriented matroids can be found in
[
18
]
. See
[
8
]
and
[
2
, Sections 8.4–8.5]
for details.

Here we only consider the equal-weight case. For a real hyperplane arrangement
\mathcal{A}
, we define the
q
-Varchenko matrix
by

\mathcal{V}_{q}(\mathcal{A})=[q^{d(C,D)}]_{C,D\in\mathrm{Ch}(\mathcal{A})}.

In other words,
\mathcal{V}_{q}(\mathcal{A})
is the Zeta matrix of the tope graph
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A})
(see Section
2.2
). This matrix can also be defined for oriented matroids.

Theorem 2.34

(
[
33
]
, see also
[
2
, Theorem 8.23]
)
.

The determinant of the
q
-Varchenko matrix is

\det\mathcal{V}_{q}(\mathcal{A})=\prod_{X\in L(\mathcal{A})\setminus\{\hat{0}\}}(1-q^{2|\mathcal{A}_{X}|})^{c^{X}\beta_{X}}.

2.7.
Tope poset and generating function

Fix a chamber
B\in\mathrm{Ch}(\mathcal{A})
and define a partial order on
\mathrm{Ch}(\mathcal{A})
by
C\preceq C^{\prime}
if
S(B,C)\subseteq S(B,C^{\prime})
. This poset is called the
tope poset
of
\mathcal{A}
based at
B
, denoted by
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A},B)
. The poset
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A},B)
has bottom element
B
and top element
-B
, graded by the rank function
\rho_{B}(C)=d(B,C)
. In the notation of Section
2.1
,
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A},B)=[B,-B]_{\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A})}
.

Proposition 2.35

(
[
9
, Theorem 2.2]
, see also
[
4
, Theorem 4.4.2]
)
.

An open interval
(C_{1},C_{2})
in
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A},B)
is homotopy equivalent to a sphere
S^{\rank\mathcal{A}-\dim F-2}
if
[C_{1},C_{2}]=\mathrm{Ch}(\mathcal{A})_{F}
for some
F\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{A})
, and is contractible otherwise.

Corollary 2.36

(
[
10
, Theorem 1.11]
, see also
[
4
, Corollary 4.4.3]
)
.

The Möbius function on
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A},B)
is given by

\mu(C_{1},C_{2})=\begin{cases}(-1)^{\rank\mathcal{A}-\dim F},&\text{ if }[C_{1},C_{2}]=\mathrm{Ch}(\mathcal{A})_{F}\text{ for some }F\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{A});\\
0,&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

Proposition 2.37

(
[
3
, Theorem 3.4]
)
.

If
\mathcal{A}
is simplicial, then
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A},B)
is a lattice for any
B\in\mathrm{Ch}(\mathcal{A})
.

Let
D_{\mathcal{A},B}(q)=\sum_{C\in\mathrm{Ch}(\mathcal{A})}q^{d(B,C)}
be the rank-generating function of
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A},B)
. Note that it is the sum of the entries in the
B
-row/column of the
q
-Varchenko matrix
\mathcal{V}_{q}(\mathcal{A})
.

Proposition 2.38

(
[
29
]
)
.

If
\mathcal{A}
is a Coxeter arrangement with exponents
e_{1},\ldots,e_{\ell}
, then we have

D_{\mathcal{A},B}(q)=\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}[1+e_{i}]_{q}

for every
B\in\mathrm{Ch}(\mathcal{A})
, where
[n]_{q}=1+q+q^{2}+\cdots+q^{n-1}
is the
q
-number.

The generating-function decomposition formula has been generalized to supersolvable arrangements
[
3
]
and inductively factored arrangements
[
19
]
. See also
[
1
,
27
,
12
]
.

3.
Magnitude of arrangements

For a real central hyperplane arrangement
\mathcal{A}
, we define the magnitude
\mathrm{Mag}(\mathcal{A})=\mathrm{Mag}(\mathcal{A},q)
of
\mathcal{A}
as the magnitude of its tope graph
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A})
.

3.1.
Basic properties

We first prove the symmetry of the magnitude with respect to
q\leftrightarrow q^{-1}
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an arrangement with
N=\#\mathcal{A}
hyperplanes. Then

\mathrm{Mag}(\mathcal{A},q^{-1})=q^{N}\mathrm{Mag}(\mathcal{A},q).

Proof.

Recall that there is an involution
\tau:\mathrm{Ch}(\mathcal{A})\to\mathrm{Ch}(\mathcal{A})
sending
D
to
-D
.
For any two chambers
C,D\in\mathrm{Ch}(\mathcal{A})
, we have
d(C,D)+d(C,-D)=N
. Thus the
q
-Varchenko matrix
\mathcal{V}_{q}=\mathcal{V}_{q}(\mathcal{A})=[q^{d(C,D)}]
satisfies

q^{N}\mathcal{V}_{q^{-1}}=[q^{N-d(C,D)}]=[q^{d(C,-D)}]=\mathcal{V}_{q}P_{\tau},

where
P_{\tau}
is the permutation matrix induced by
\tau
. By the definition of magnitude, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Mag}(\mathcal{A},q^{-1})=\mathbf{1}^{T}(\mathcal{V}_{q^{-1}})^{-1}\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{1}^{T}(q^{-N}\mathcal{V}_{q}P_{\tau})^{-1}\mathbf{1}=q^{N}\mathbf{1}^{T}P_{\tau}^{-1}\mathcal{V}_{q}^{-1}\mathbf{1}=q^{N}\mathbf{1}^{T}\mathcal{V}_{q}^{-1}\mathbf{1}=q^{N}\mathrm{Mag}(\mathcal{A},q),

where we used
\mathbf{1}^{T}P_{\tau}^{-1}=\mathbf{1}^{T}
.
∎

Since tope graphs are partial cubes, the magnitude
\mathrm{Mag}(\mathcal{A})
satisfies the one-point property established in
[
22
]
.

Proposition 3.2
.

If
\mathcal{A}
is an arrangement, then
\mathrm{Mag}(\mathcal{A},1)=1
.

Next we prove some structural results for
\mathrm{Mag}(\mathcal{A})
.

Theorem 3.3
.

For an arrangement
\mathcal{A}
, let
\mathrm{Mag}(\mathcal{A},q)=P(q)/Q(q)
be the reduced expression of the magnitude, with the leading coefficient of
Q(q)
positive. Then

(1)

\#\mathcal{A}=\deg Q-\deg P
.

(2)

P,Q
are palindromic.

(3)

All roots of
Q
are roots of unity other than
1
.

Proof.

Let
P(q)=a_{m}q^{m}+\cdots+a_{0}
and
Q(q)=b_{n}q^{n}+\cdots+b_{0}
where
a_{m}\neq 0
and
b_{n}\neq 0
. Then
m=\deg P
and
n=\deg Q
. Since
\mathrm{Mag}(\mathcal{A},0)=a_{0}/b_{0}=\#\mathrm{Ch}(\mathcal{A})
(Proposition
2.7
), we have
a_{0}\neq 0
and
b_{0}\neq 0
. By the symmetry formula (Proposition
3.1
), we have

q^{N}=\frac{\mathrm{Mag}(\mathcal{A},q^{-1})}{\mathrm{Mag}(\mathcal{A},q)}=\frac{P(q^{-1})Q(q)}{P(q)Q(q^{-1})}.

Multiplying both sides by
q^{m-n}
, we obtain

q^{N+m-n}=\frac{q^{m}P(q^{-1})Q(q)}{P(q)q^{n}Q(q^{-1})}=\frac{(a_{m}+\cdots+a_{0}q^{m})(b_{n}q^{n}+\cdots+b_{0})}{(a_{m}q^{m}+\cdots+a_{0})(b_{n}+\cdots+b_{0}q^{n})}.

Comparing degrees, we obtain
N+m-n=0
, and (1) is proved.

Write
P^{*}(q)=q^{m}P(q^{-1})=a_{m}+\cdots+a_{0}q^{m}
and
Q^{*}(q)=q^{n}Q(q^{-1})=b_{n}+\cdots+b_{0}q^{n}
for the reverse polynomials. We have proved

\mathrm{Mag}(\mathcal{A},q)=\frac{P(q)}{Q(q)}=\frac{P^{*}(q)}{Q^{*}(q)}.

Note that the fraction
P^{*}(q)/Q^{*}(q)
is reduced, since if
P^{*}
and
Q^{*}
share a common root
\alpha\neq 0
, then
P
and
Q
would share the common root
1/\alpha
, contradicting the assumption that
P/Q
is reduced. Hence we must have
P^{*}(q)=rP(q)
and
Q^{*}(q)=rQ(q)
for some constant
r\neq 0
. Substituting
q=1
into the equations
Q^{*}(q)=q^{n}Q(1/q)
and
Q^{*}(q)=rQ(q)
, we obtain

Q^{*}(1)=Q(1)=rQ(1).

Since
\mathrm{Mag}(\mathcal{A},1)=P(1)/Q(1)=1
(Proposition
3.2
) and
P/Q
is reduced, we have
Q(1)\neq 0
, so
r=1
. Then
P^{*}=P
and
Q^{*}=Q
, and (2) is proved.

Since
\mathcal{V}_{q}^{-1}=\frac{1}{\det\mathcal{V}_{q}}\mathrm{adj~}\mathcal{V}_{q}
, the roots of
Q(q)
are roots of
\det\mathcal{V}_{q}
, which are roots of unity by Theorem
2.34
. We have already seen that
Q(1)\neq 0
. Thus (3) is proved.
∎

If an arrangement
\mathcal{A}
decomposes as a direct sum
\mathcal{A}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{A}_{2}
, then the tope graph decomposes as the Cartesian product
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A})=\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A}_{1})\square\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A}_{2})
. Therefore, by Proposition
2.5
, we have a decomposition of magnitude.

Proposition 3.4
.

\mathrm{Mag}(\mathcal{A}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{A}_{2})=\mathrm{Mag}(\mathcal{A}_{1})\mathrm{Mag}(\mathcal{A}_{2})
.

Coxeter arrangements carry a natural action of the corresponding Coxeter groups on the chambers, which makes their magnitudes easy to compute.

Proposition 3.5
.

For a Coxeter arrangement
\mathcal{A}_{W}
with exponents
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{\ell}\}
, we have

\mathrm{Mag}(\mathcal{A}_{W})=\frac{\#W}{\prod_{i}[1+e_{i}]_{q}}.

Proof.

The tope graph
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A}_{W})
is the
1
-skeleton of the
W
-permutahedron, or equivalently the Cayley graph of
W
with respect to the standard generating set (Example
2.32
), and hence is vertex-transitive. The result follows from Proposition
2.3
and Proposition
2.38
.
∎

3.2.
Face decomposition formula

For reciprocity purposes, let us define the following.

Definition 3.6
.

We define the
interior magnitude
of
\mathcal{A}
by

\mathrm{Mag}^{\circ}(\mathcal{A},q):=(-1)^{\rank\mathcal{A}}\mathrm{Mag}(\mathcal{A},q^{-1})=(-1)^{\rank\mathcal{A}}q^{\#\mathcal{A}}\mathrm{Mag}(\mathcal{A},q),

where the latter equality follows from Proposition
3.1
.

We prove the following “face decomposition formula” for the magnitude of an arrangement.

Theorem 3.7
.

For any arrangement
\mathcal{A}
, we have

\mathrm{Mag}(\mathcal{A})=\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{A})}\mathrm{Mag}^{\circ}(\mathcal{A}_{F})=\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{A})}(-1)^{\codim F}q^{\#\mathcal{A}_{F}}\mathrm{Mag}(\mathcal{A}_{F}).

Remark 3.8
.

Theorem
3.7
can be reformulated as

\mathrm{Mag}(\mathcal{A})=\sum_{X\in L(\mathcal{A})}c^{X}\mathrm{Mag}^{\circ}(\mathcal{A}_{X})=\sum_{X\in L(\mathcal{A})}(-1)^{\codim X}c^{X}q^{\#\mathcal{A}_{X}}\mathrm{Mag}(\mathcal{A}_{X}),

where
c^{X}=\#\mathrm{Ch}(\mathcal{A}^{X})
.
It can also be written as

\mathrm{Mag}(\mathcal{A})=\frac{\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{A})\setminus\{\cap\mathcal{A}\}}\mathrm{Mag}^{\circ}(\mathcal{A}_{F})}{1-(-1)^{\rank\mathcal{A}}q^{\#\mathcal{A}}}=\frac{\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{A})\setminus\{\cap\mathcal{A}\}}(-1)^{\codim F}q^{\#\mathcal{A}_{F}}\mathrm{Mag}(\mathcal{A}_{F})}{1-(-1)^{\rank\mathcal{A}}q^{\#\mathcal{A}}}.

This can be regarded as a recursive formula for computing
\mathrm{Mag}(\mathcal{A})
.

Remark 3.9
.

Taking the limit
q\to 1
gives
\mathrm{Mag}(\mathcal{A},1)=1
and
\mathrm{Mag}^{\circ}(\mathcal{A},1)=(-1)^{\rank\mathcal{A}}
by Proposition
3.2
.
Therefore, Theorem
3.7
can be regarded as a
q
-analogue of the decomposition of the Euler characteristic of the zonotope into its open faces.

Corollary 3.10
.

The magnitude
\mathrm{Mag}(\mathcal{A})
of an arrangement
\mathcal{A}
is determined by
L(\mathcal{A})
.

Lemma 3.11
.

For any arrangement
\mathcal{A}
and
D\in\mathrm{Ch}(\mathcal{A})
, we have

\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{A})}(-1)^{\codim F}q^{\#\mathcal{A}_{F}+d(D,FD)}=1.

Proof.

If
\rank\mathcal{A}=0
, then
\mathcal{A}
is the empty arrangement and there is nothing to prove, so let us assume
r:=\rank\mathcal{A}\geq 1
. Passing to the essentialization does not change the chamber set, the tope graph, or the quantities
\#\mathcal{A}_{F}
and
\codim F=\rank(\mathcal{A}_{F})
, so we may work in an essential arrangement of rank
r
.

Choose defining forms
\alpha_{H}
so that
D_{H}=+
for every
H\in\mathcal{A}
.
For a face
F
, define
M_{D}(F)\subset\mathcal{A}
by

M_{D}(F):=\{H\in\mathcal{A}:F_{H}\neq+\}.

Since
F_{H}=0
exactly when
H\in\mathcal{A}_{F}
, while
d(D,FD)
counts the hyperplanes on which
F_{H}=-
, we have
\#M_{D}(F)=\#\mathcal{A}_{F}+d(D,FD)
.
Therefore it is enough to prove

S_{D}(q):=\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{A})}(-1)^{\codim F}q^{\#M_{D}(F)}=1.

Expand each power as

q^{\#M_{D}(F)}=(1+(q-1))^{\#M_{D}(F)}=\sum_{T\subseteq M_{D}(F)}(q-1)^{\#T}.

After exchanging the order of summation we obtain

S_{D}(q)=\sum_{T\subseteq\mathcal{A}}(q-1)^{\#T}E_{T},\qquad E_{T}:=\sum_{F:\,T\subseteq M_{D}(F)}(-1)^{\codim F}.

Hence the desired identity will follow once we show
E_{T}=\mathbf{1}_{T=\varnothing}
, i.e.
E_{T}=0
if
T\neq\varnothing
and
E_{\varnothing}=1
.
We interpret
E_{T}
as a reduced Euler characteristic. Let
S^{r-1}
be the unit sphere in the ambient space
V=\mathbb{R}^{r}
. For each hyperplane
H\in\mathcal{A}
, let

H_{D}^{-}:=\{x\in S^{r-1}:\alpha_{H}(x)\leq 0\}

be the closed hemisphere opposite to
D
, and for
T\subseteq\mathcal{A}
define

\Sigma_{T}:=\bigcap_{H\in T}H_{D}^{-}\subseteq S^{r-1},\qquad\Sigma_{\varnothing}:=S^{r-1}.

The arrangement induces a regular CW decomposition of
S^{r-1}
where the open cells are
F\cap S^{r-1}
for non-minimal faces
F\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{A})\setminus\{\cap\mathcal{A}\}
.
An open cell
F\cap S^{r-1}
lies in
\Sigma_{T}
if and only if
T\subseteq M_{D}(F)
. Therefore

\chi(\Sigma_{T})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}F\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{A})\setminus\{\cap\mathcal{A}\}\\
T\subseteq M_{D}(F)\end{subarray}}(-1)^{\dim F-1}.

Since
\codim F=r-\dim F
and the minimal face
\cap\mathcal{A}
contributes
(-1)^{r}
to
E_{T}
, we obtain

\displaystyle E_{T}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{r}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}F\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{A})\setminus\{\cap\mathcal{A}\}\\
T\subseteq M_{D}(F)\end{subarray}}(-1)^{r-\dim F}=(-1)^{r-1}\left(\chi(\Sigma_{T})-1\right).

If
T=\varnothing
, then
\Sigma_{\varnothing}=S^{r-1}
, so
E_{\varnothing}=(-1)^{r-1}(\chi(S^{r-1})-1)=1
.
If
T\neq\varnothing
, then
\Sigma_{T}
is the intersection of a nonempty collection of closed hemispheres, so
\Sigma_{T}
is homeomorphic to
D^{r-1}
.
Therefore

E_{T}=(-1)^{r-1}(\chi(D^{r-1})-1)=0
.
This shows
E_{T}=\mathbf{1}_{T=\varnothing}
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.7
.

Fix a face
F\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{A})
.
Let
w_{F}\colon\mathrm{Ch}(\mathcal{A})_{F}\to\mathbb{Q}(q)
be the weight vector of
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A})_{F}\cong\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A}_{F})
, so that

\sum_{C\in\mathrm{Ch}(\mathcal{A})_{F}}q^{d(E,C)}w_{F}(C)=1

for every
E\in\mathrm{Ch}(\mathcal{A})_{F}
.
Extend
w_{F}
by zero outside
\mathrm{Ch}(\mathcal{A})_{F}
.
Now define a function
\widetilde{w}_{\mathcal{A}}\colon\mathrm{Ch}(\mathcal{A})\to\mathbb{Q}(q)
by

\widetilde{w}_{\mathcal{A}}(C):=\sum_{F\leq C}(-1)^{\codim F}q^{\#\mathcal{A}_{F}}w_{F}(C).

Taking the sum over all chambers gives

\displaystyle\sum_{C\in\mathrm{Ch}(\mathcal{A})}\widetilde{w}_{\mathcal{A}}(C)

\displaystyle=\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{A})}(-1)^{\codim F}q^{\#\mathcal{A}_{F}}\sum_{C\in\mathrm{Ch}(\mathcal{A})_{F}}w_{F}(C)

\displaystyle=\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{A})}(-1)^{\codim F}q^{\#\mathcal{A}_{F}}\mathrm{Mag}(\mathcal{A}_{F},q)

\displaystyle=\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{A})}\mathrm{Mag}^{\circ}(\mathcal{A}_{F},q).

Therefore Theorem
3.7
will follow once we prove that
\widetilde{w}_{\mathcal{A}}
is the actual weight vector of
\mathcal{A}
.
Fix a chamber
D\in\mathrm{Ch}(\mathcal{A})
. We compute the
D
-th coordinate of
\mathcal{V}_{q}(\mathcal{A})\widetilde{w}_{\mathcal{A}}
:

\displaystyle\sum_{C\in\mathrm{Ch}(\mathcal{A})}q^{d(D,C)}\widetilde{w}_{\mathcal{A}}(C)

\displaystyle=\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{A})}(-1)^{\codim F}q^{\#\mathcal{A}_{F}}\sum_{C\in\mathrm{Ch}(\mathcal{A})_{F}}q^{d(D,C)}w_{F}(C).

Since
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A})_{F}
is gated with gate projection
D\mapsto FD
(Lemma
2.29
), we have

d(D,C)=d(D,FD)+d(FD,C)

for every
C\in\mathrm{Ch}(\mathcal{A})_{F}
.
Using this identity and the defining equation for the local weight vector
w_{F}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{C\in\mathrm{Ch}(\mathcal{A})}q^{d(D,C)}\widetilde{w}_{\mathcal{A}}(C)

\displaystyle=\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{A})}(-1)^{\codim F}q^{\#\mathcal{A}_{F}+d(D,FD)}\sum_{C\in\mathrm{Ch}(\mathcal{A})_{F}}q^{d(FD,C)}w_{F}(C)

\displaystyle=\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{A})}(-1)^{\codim F}q^{\#\mathcal{A}_{F}+d(D,FD)}.

By Lemma
3.11
, this equals
1
for every
D\in\mathrm{Ch}(\mathcal{A})
.
∎

4.
Computations for rank 3 arrangements

4.1.
Magnitude of rank 3 arrangements

Using the face decomposition formula (Theorem
3.7
), we can explicitly compute the magnitude of rank
3
arrangements.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an arrangement of rank
3
with
n=\#\mathcal{A}
.
For
k\geq 2
, we write
n_{k}
for the number of faces
F\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{A})
with
\codim F=2
and
\#\mathcal{A}_{F}=k
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an arrangement of rank
3
with
\#\mathcal{A}=n
.
Then we have

\mathrm{Mag}(\mathcal{A})=\dfrac{1}{1+q^{n}}\biggl(\#\mathrm{Ch}(\mathcal{A})-\sum_{k\geq 2}\dfrac{2kn_{k}q(1-q^{k-1})}{(1+q)(1-q^{k})}\biggr).

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{F}_{d}(\mathcal{A})
denote the set of faces of codimension
d
of
\mathcal{A}
.
By Theorem
3.7
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Mag}(\mathcal{A})

\displaystyle{}=\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{0}(\mathcal{A})}1-\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{1}(\mathcal{A})}\dfrac{2q}{1+q}+\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{2}(\mathcal{A})}\dfrac{2\#\mathcal{A}_{F}q^{\#\mathcal{A}_{F}}(1-q)}{(1+q)(1-q^{\#\mathcal{A}_{F}})}-q^{n}\mathrm{Mag}(\mathcal{A},q)

\displaystyle{}=\#\mathrm{Ch}(\mathcal{A})-\#\mathcal{F}_{1}(\mathcal{A})\cdot\dfrac{2q}{1+q}+\sum_{k\geq 2}\dfrac{2kn_{k}q^{k}(1-q)}{(1+q)(1-q^{k})}-q^{n}\mathrm{Mag}(\mathcal{A},q).

For each
F\in\mathcal{F}_{2}(\mathcal{A})
, the number of faces
G\in\mathcal{F}_{1}(\mathcal{A})
of codimension
1
adjacent to
F
is exactly
2\#\mathcal{A}_{F}
.
Conversely, for each
G\in\mathcal{F}_{1}(\mathcal{A})
, there are exactly two faces
F\in\mathcal{F}_{2}(\mathcal{A})
of codimension
2
adjacent to
G
.
Therefore we have

\#\mathcal{F}_{1}(\mathcal{A})=\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{2}(\mathcal{A})}2\#\mathcal{A}_{F}=\sum_{k\geq 2}kn_{k}.

Using this identity, we obtain

\displaystyle(1+q^{n})\mathrm{Mag}(\mathcal{A})

\displaystyle{}=\#\mathrm{Ch}(\mathcal{A})-\sum_{k\geq 2}\dfrac{2kn_{k}q}{1+q}+\sum_{k\geq 2}\dfrac{2kn_{k}q^{k}(1-q)}{(1+q)(1-q^{k})}

\displaystyle{}=\#\mathrm{Ch}(\mathcal{A})-\sum_{k\geq 2}\dfrac{2kn_{k}q(1-q^{k-1})}{(1+q)(1-q^{k})}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Example 4.2
.

The arrangement
\mathcal{A}(6,1)
from Grünbaum’s catalogue
[
15
]
satisfies

\#\mathrm{Ch}(\mathcal{A}(6,1))=24\quad\text{and}\quad n_{k}=\begin{cases}6,&\text{ if }k=2;\\
8,&\text{ if }k=3;\\
0,&\text{ if }k\geq 4.\end{cases}

By Theorem
4.1
, its magnitude is given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{Mag}(\mathcal{A}(6,1))

\displaystyle{}=\dfrac{1}{1+q^{6}}\biggl(24-\dfrac{24q}{(1+q)^{2}}-\dfrac{48q}{1+q+q^{2}}\biggr)

\displaystyle{}=\frac{24}{(1+q)^{2}(1+q+q^{2})(1+q^{2})}.

This coincides with the formula given in Example
3.13
.

4.2.
Alternating sign property

In all the examples shown in Appendix
A
, the coefficients in the series expansion of the magnitude of each arrangement seem to have alternating signs. That is, if we write

\mathrm{Mag}(\mathcal{A})=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}c_{\ell}q^{\ell},

then
(-1)^{\ell}c_{\ell}\geq 0
appears to hold.
Here we prove that if
\rank\mathcal{A}\leq 3
, then
(-1)^{\ell}c_{\ell}>0
holds for all sufficiently large
\ell
.
On the other hand, in the next subsection, we construct an example for which the inequality

(-1)^{\ell}c_{\ell}\geq 0

fails for small
\ell
.

Definition 4.3
.

For each
k\geq 2
, we define a rational function
G_{k}(q)
and a sequence
(g_{k,\ell})_{\ell\geq 0}
by

G_{k}(q)=\dfrac{q(1-(-q)^{k-1})}{(1-q)(1-(-q)^{k})}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}g_{k,\ell}q^{\ell}.

Theorem
4.1
implies that, for an arrangement
\mathcal{A}
of rank
3
with
\#\mathcal{A}=n
, we have

(4.1)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Mag}(\mathcal{A},-q)=\dfrac{1}{1+(-q)^{n}}\biggl(\#\mathrm{Ch}(\mathcal{A})+\sum_{k\geq 2}2kn_{k}G_{k}(q)\biggr).

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an arrangement of rank
3
.
Let
n_{k}
be the number of faces
F\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{A})
with
\codim F=2
and
\#\mathcal{A}_{F}=k
.
Then we have
n_{2}>0
.

Proof.

We may assume that
\mathcal{A}
is essential.
Consider the zonotope
Z(\mathcal{A})
of
\mathcal{A}
, which is a polytope in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
The facets of
Z(\mathcal{A})
are polygons with an even number of vertices.
The number of facets with
2k
vertices is exactly
n_{k}
.
If
n_{2}=0
, then every facet of
Z(\mathcal{A})
must have at least
6
vertices.
However, Euler’s polyhedral formula shows that this is impossible.
Therefore we have
n_{2}>0
.
∎

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an arrangement with
\rank\mathcal{A}\leq 3
.
Let
c_{\ell}
denote the coefficient of
q^{\ell}
in the series expansion of
\mathrm{Mag}(\mathcal{A})
:

\mathrm{Mag}(\mathcal{A})=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}c_{\ell}q^{\ell}.

Then we have
(-1)^{\ell}c_{\ell}>0
for all sufficiently large
\ell
.

Proof.

When
\rank\mathcal{A}\leq 2
, the claim can be checked directly.
Therefore, we may assume that
\rank\mathcal{A}=3
.
Let
\#\mathcal{A}=n
.
Write
n_{k}
for the number of faces
F\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{A})
with
\codim F=2
and
\#\mathcal{A}_{F}=k
.
Define
(e_{\ell})_{\ell\geq 0}
by

\mathrm{Mag}(\mathcal{A},-q)=\dfrac{1}{1+(-q)^{n}}\biggl(\#\mathrm{Ch}(\mathcal{A})+\sum_{k\geq 2}2kn_{k}G_{k}(q)\biggr)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}e_{\ell}q^{\ell}.

It suffices to show that
e_{\ell}>0
for all sufficiently large
\ell
.
Put

H(q):=\#\mathrm{Ch}(\mathcal{A})+\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}2kn_{k}G_{k}(q),\qquad H(q)=\sum_{\ell\geq 0}a_{\ell}q^{\ell}.

We first record two elementary facts about the coefficients
g_{k,\ell}
of
G_{k}
.
If
k
is even, then

G_{k}(q)=\frac{q(1+q^{k-1})}{(1-q)(1-q^{k})}=\frac{q}{1-q}\sum_{j\geq 0}\left(q^{kj}+q^{kj+k-1}\right).

Hence
g_{k,\ell}
is non-negative and non-decreasing in
\ell
. In particular,
for
k=2
we have
g_{2,\ell}=\ell
.
If
k
is odd, then

G_{k}(q)=\frac{q(1-q^{k-1})}{(1-q)(1+q^{k})}=\frac{q(1+q+\cdots+q^{k-2})}{1+q^{k}}.

Thus the coefficient sequence
(g_{k,\ell})_{\ell\geq 0}
is periodic and bounded.
Since
n_{2}>0
by Lemma
4.4
, there is a constant

C>0
such that
a_{\ell}\geq 4n_{2}\ell-C
for all
\ell\geq 0
.

We now distinguish the parity of
n
.
First suppose that
n
is odd. Then
1+(-q)^{n}=1-q^{n}
, so

\mathrm{Mag}(\mathcal{A},-q)=\frac{H(q)}{1-q^{n}}=H(q)\sum_{j\geq 0}q^{nj}.

Therefore, with
N=\lfloor\ell/n\rfloor
,

e_{\ell}=\sum_{j=0}^{N}a_{\ell-nj}\geq\sum_{j=0}^{N}\bigl(4n_{2}(\ell-nj)-C\bigr).

The right-hand side is a polynomial of degree
1
in
\ell
with leading coefficient
4n_{2}(N+1)>0
, so it tends to
+\infty
as
\ell\to\infty
.
Hence
e_{\ell}>0
for all
sufficiently large
\ell
in this case.

Next suppose that
n
is even. Then
1+(-q)^{n}=1+q^{n}
and

\frac{1}{1+q^{n}}=\sum_{j\geq 0}(-1)^{j}q^{nj}.

For even
k
, let
b_{k,\ell}
be the coefficient of
q^{\ell}
in the series expansion of
\frac{G_{k}(q)}{1+q^{n}}
.
Then we have

b_{k,\ell}=\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor\ell/n\rfloor}(-1)^{j}g_{k,\ell-nj}.

Since
(g_{k,\ell})_{\ell}
is non-negative and non-decreasing, pairing adjacent
terms gives
b_{k,\ell}\geq 0
for every
\ell
.
For
k=2
, using
g_{2,m}=m
, we also get
b_{2,\ell}\to+\infty
.
We claim that the odd
k
terms, after division by
1+q^{n}
, are
bounded. Indeed, for odd
k
and even
n
,

\frac{G_{k}(q)}{1+q^{n}}=\frac{q(1+q+\cdots+q^{k-2})}{(1+q^{k})(1+q^{n})}.

Moreover
\gcd(1+q^{k},1+q^{n})=1
, because
k
is odd and
n
is even. Hence the
denominator has only simple roots of unity, and the coefficient sequence is
bounded. The coefficient sequence of
\#\mathrm{Ch}(\mathcal{A})/(1+q^{n})
is also bounded. Thus there
is a constant
D>0
such that the total contribution of
\#\mathrm{Ch}(\mathcal{A})
and all odd
k

terms to
e_{\ell}
is at least
-D
for every
\ell
.
Combining these observations, and using Lemma
4.4
, we obtain

e_{\ell}\geq 4n_{2}b_{2,\ell}-D.

Since
b_{2,\ell}\to+\infty
, the right-hand side is positive for all
sufficiently large
\ell
. This proves the theorem.
∎

4.3.
Arrangements with non-alternating magnitude

In this section, we use the construction of Burr–Grünbaum–Sloane
[
5
]
for the orchard problem to construct an example of an arrangement whose magnitude does not have the alternating-sign property.

Recall the formula (
4.1
) for
\mathrm{Mag}(\mathcal{A},-q)
.
The series expansion of
G_{3}(q)
is given by

G_{3}(q)=\dfrac{q}{1-q+q^{2}}=q+q^{2}-q^{4}-q^{5}+q^{7}+q^{8}-\cdots.

This suggests that for an arrangement with large
n_{3}
, the series expansion of
\mathrm{Mag}(\mathcal{A},-q)
might have negative coefficients.
This motivates the construction given below.

Definition 4.6
.

Let
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a finite set of points in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
For each
k\geq 2
, define
m_{k}
to be the number of lines that contain exactly
k
points from
S
.
Define an arrangement
\mathcal{A}(S)
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
by

\mathcal{A}(S)=\{(x,y,1)^{\perp}\mid(x,y)\in S\}.

The arrangement
\mathcal{A}(S)
is essential unless
S
is contained in a single line.
It satisfies
n_{k}=2m_{k}
for
k\geq 2
.

We use the following result to construct an
S
with large
m_{3}
.

Theorem 4.7

(
[
5
, Theorem 1]
, see also
[
14
, Proposition 2.6]
)
.

For any
N\geq 3
, there exists a finite set
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
with
\#S=N
such that

m_{k}=\begin{cases}N-1-2\cdot\mathbf{1}_{3\mid N},&\text{ if }k=2;\\
\biggl\lfloor\dfrac{N(N-3)}{6}+1\biggr\rfloor,&\text{ if }k=3;\\
0,&\text{ if }k\geq 4,\end{cases}

where
\mathbf{1}_{3\mid N}
is equal to
1
when
3
divides
N
and
0
otherwise.

Example 4.8
.

As an example of Theorem
4.7
, we construct a set of points
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
with
\#S=18
such that

m_{k}=\begin{cases}15,&\text{ if }k=2;\\
46,&\text{ if }k=3;\\
0,&\text{ if }k\geq 4.\end{cases}

Let
X
be the set of non-singular points of the real projective algebraic curve

Y^{2}=X^{2}(X-Z).

Then the points of
X
naturally form a group isomorphic to
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
.
An explicit parametrization is given by

\phi(t)=\bigl[\cot^{2}(\pi t)+1:\cot(\pi t)(\cot^{2}(\pi t)+1):1\bigr]\quad(t\in\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}).

Let
P_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
be the image of
\phi(j/18)
under the projective transformation

[X:Y:Z]\mapsto[X:Y:X+Y+Z]
.
Then
P_{i},P_{j},P_{k}
lie on a single line if and only if
i+j+k\equiv 0\pmod{18}
.
Define
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
by

S=\{P_{j}\mid j=0,1,\dots,17\}.

There are exactly
46
triples
\{i,j,k\}\subset\mathbb{Z}/18\mathbb{Z}
such that
i+j+k\equiv 0\pmod{18}
, so we have
m_{3}=46
.
Also,
m_{k}=0
for
k\geq 4
.
Since
m_{2}+3m_{3}=\binom{18}{2}
by double-counting, we have
m_{2}=15
.

Example 4.9
.

We give an explicit construction of an arrangement
\mathcal{A}
such that the series expansion of
\mathrm{Mag}(\mathcal{A})
does not have alternating signs.
Let
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a finite set as in Example
4.8
.
Then the associated arrangement
\mathcal{A}(S)
satisfies

\#\mathcal{A}(S)=18,\quad n_{k}=\begin{cases}30,&\text{ if }k=2;\\
92,&\text{ if }k=3;\\
0,&\text{ if }k\geq 4,\end{cases}\quad\text{and}\quad\#\mathrm{Ch}(\mathcal{A})=216.

Figure
2
shows the projectivization of
\mathcal{A}(S)
.
By Theorem
4.1
, the magnitude of
\mathcal{A}(S)
is given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{Mag}(\mathcal{A})

\displaystyle{}=\dfrac{1}{1+q^{18}}\biggl(216-\dfrac{120q}{(1+q)^{2}}-\dfrac{552q}{1+q+q^{2}}\biggr)

\displaystyle{}=216-672q+792q^{2}-360q^{3}-72q^{4}-48q^{5}+720q^{6}-1392q^{7}+1512q^{8}-\cdots.

In particular, the coefficient of
q^{4}
is
-72<0
.

5.
Magnitude homology of arrangements

We continue to denote invariants of
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A})
simply by the corresponding invariants of
\mathcal{A}
, such as
MH_{k,\ell}(\mathcal{A}):=MH_{k,\ell}(\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A}))
,
\beta_{k,\ell}(\mathcal{A}):=\rank MH_{k,\ell}(\mathcal{A})
, and
\chi_{\ell}(\mathcal{A})=\sum_{k}(-1)^{k}\beta_{k,\ell}(\mathcal{A})
. Also recall the notation
c^{X}=\#\mathrm{Ch}(\mathcal{A}^{X})
for
X\in L(\mathcal{A})
.

5.1.
Small lengths

We begin our study of
MH_{k,\ell}(\mathcal{A})
by investigating small lengths
\ell\leq 2
.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an arrangement. Then the only nontrivial
MH_{k,\ell}(\mathcal{A})
for
\ell\leq 2
are as follows.

(1)

MH_{0,0}(\mathcal{A})
is free abelian of rank
\beta_{0,0}(\mathcal{A})=\#\mathrm{Ch}(\mathcal{A})
.

(2)

MH_{1,1}(\mathcal{A})
is free abelian of rank
\beta_{1,1}(\mathcal{A})=2\sum_{H\in\mathcal{A}}c^{H}
.

(3)

MH_{2,2}(\mathcal{A})
is free abelian of rank
\beta_{2,2}(\mathcal{A})=\beta_{1,1}(\mathcal{A})+4\sum_{X\in L_{2}(\mathcal{A}),\#\mathcal{A}_{X}=2}c^{X}
.

Proof.

(1) and (2) follow from (1) and (2) of Proposition
2.8
, together with the observation that an edge in
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A})
corresponds to a chamber of the restriction
\mathcal{A}^{H}
for some
H\in\mathcal{A}
.

For (3), since
m_{\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A})}\geq 3
, we may apply Theorem
2.22
for
\ell=2<m_{\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A})}
:

MH_{2,2}(\mathcal{A})\cong\bigoplus_{\vec{a}\in P_{1,2}(\mathcal{A})\cup P_{2,2}(\mathcal{A})}MH^{\mathrm{gs}}_{2,\vec{a}}(\mathcal{A}).

By Theorem
2.25
, the framed magnitude homology of
\mathcal{A}
can be computed from interval posets. For
\vec{a}=(a_{0},a_{1})\in P_{1,2}(\mathcal{A})
, where
a_{0},a_{1}
are chambers at distance
2
, we may suppose
S(a_{0},a_{1})=\{H_{1},H_{2}\}
and let
X=H_{1}\cap H_{2}\in L_{2}(\mathcal{A})
. If
\#\mathcal{A}_{X}=2
, then the interval poset
(a_{0},a_{1})_{\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A})}
consists of two incomparable chambers between
a_{0}
and
a_{1}
. If
\#\mathcal{A}_{X}>2
, then
(a_{0},a_{1})_{\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A})}
is a singleton. Since
MH^{\mathrm{gs}}_{2,(a_{0},a_{1})}(\mathcal{A})\cong\widetilde{H}_{0}(C(a_{0},a_{1})_{\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A})})
, taking reduced homology shows that only the first type of
(a_{0},a_{1})
, with
\#\mathcal{A}_{X}=2
, contributes a copy of
\mathbb{Z}
to the magnitude homology. Hence the total contribution to
MH_{2,2}(\mathcal{A})
from
P_{1,2}(\mathcal{A})
is
4\sum_{X\in L_{2}(\mathcal{A}),\#\mathcal{A}_{X}=2}c^{X}
copies of
\mathbb{Z}
.

Similarly, for
\vec{a}=(a_{0},a_{1},a_{2})\in P_{2,2}(\mathcal{A})
, if
a_{0}\neq a_{2}
, then
\vec{a}
is geodesic and hence
\varphi(\vec{a})\neq\vec{a}
, making the
\vec{a}
-framed magnitude homology trivial. Thus we only need to consider
\vec{a}=(a_{0},a_{1},a_{0})\in P_{2,2}(\mathcal{A})
, where
a_{0}
and
a_{1}
are adjacent. The interval posets
(a_{0},a_{1})_{\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A})}
and
(a_{1},a_{0})_{\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A})}
are empty. Then
MH^{\mathrm{gs}}_{2,(a_{0},a_{1},a_{0})}(\mathcal{A})\cong\widetilde{H}_{-2}(C(a_{0},a_{1})_{\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A})}\otimes C(a_{1},a_{0})_{\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A})})\cong\mathbb{Z}
. Each such
(a_{0},a_{1},a_{0})
corresponds to a directed edge of
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A})
. Hence the total contribution to
MH_{2,2}(\mathcal{A})
from
P_{2,2}(\mathcal{A})
is
\beta_{1,1}(\mathcal{A})
copies of
\mathbb{Z}
. This proves (3).

To finish the proof, we need to show that all other
MH_{k,\ell}(\mathcal{A})
for
\ell\leq 2
are trivial. By Proposition
2.8
(3), we only need to prove
MH_{1,2}(\mathcal{A})=0
. We use Theorem
2.22
again:

MH_{1,2}(\mathcal{A})\cong\bigoplus_{\vec{a}\in P_{1,2}(\mathcal{A})}MH^{\mathrm{gs}}_{1,\vec{a}}(\mathcal{A}).

Consider
\vec{a}=(a_{0},a_{1})\in P_{1,2}(\mathcal{A})
. The interval poset
(a_{0},a_{1})_{\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A})}
cannot be empty. Hence
MH^{\mathrm{gs}}_{1,(a_{0},a_{1})}(\mathcal{A})\cong\widetilde{H}_{-1}(C(a_{0},a_{1})_{\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A})})=0
.
∎

5.2.
Geodesic magnitude homology and diagonal tope graphs

We now determine the geodesic magnitude homology of arrangements.
Recall that
c^{X}=\#\mathrm{Ch}(\mathcal{A}^{X})
.
Let
c_{X}=\#\mathrm{Ch}(\mathcal{A}_{X})
.
Both
c^{X}
and
c_{X}
are determined by the intersection lattice
L(\mathcal{A})
.

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an arrangement. Then its geodesic magnitude homology is given as follows:

MH_{k,\ell}^{\mathrm{geod}}(\mathcal{A})\cong\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}F\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{A})\\
\rank\mathcal{A}_{F}=k,\ \#\mathcal{A}_{F}=\ell\end{subarray}}\mathbb{Z}^{\#\mathrm{Ch}(\mathcal{A}_{F})}\cong\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}X\in L_{k}(\mathcal{A})\\
\#\mathcal{A}_{X}=\ell\end{subarray}}\mathbb{Z}^{c^{X}c_{X}}.

In particular,
MH_{k,\ell}^{\mathrm{geod}}(\mathcal{A})
is determined by
L(\mathcal{A})
.

Proof.

By Corollary
2.24
, we have

MH_{k,\ell}^{\mathrm{geod}}(\mathcal{A})\cong\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}a,b\in\mathrm{Ch}(\mathcal{A})\\
d(a,b)=\ell\end{subarray}}\widetilde{H}_{k-2}(C_{*}(a,b)_{\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A})}).

By Proposition
2.35
, the interval
(a,b)_{\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A})}
is homotopy equivalent to
S^{\rank\mathcal{A}_{F}-2}
if
[a,b]_{\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A})}=\mathrm{Ch}(\mathcal{A})_{F}
for some
F\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{A})
, and is contractible otherwise.
Therefore each
F\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{A})
such that
k=\rank\mathcal{A}_{F}
and
\ell=\#\mathcal{A}_{F}
contributes a copy of
\mathbb{Z}^{\#\mathrm{Ch}(\mathcal{A})_{F}}
to
MH_{k,\ell}^{\mathrm{geod}}(\mathcal{A})
, and there is no other summand.
∎

Corollary 5.3
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an arrangement. Then its geodesic magnitude Betti numbers are given by

\beta_{k,\ell}^{\mathrm{geod}}(\mathcal{A})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}X\in L_{k}(\mathcal{A})\\
\#\mathcal{A}_{X}=\ell\end{subarray}}c^{X}c_{X}.

As an application, we give a characterization of diagonal tope graphs.

Corollary 5.4
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an essential arrangement. Then
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A})
is diagonal if and only if
\mathcal{A}
is Boolean.

Proof.

We know that the Boolean arrangement
Bl_{d}
has tope graph
\mathcal{T}(Bl_{d})=Q_{d}
, which is diagonal (see Example
2.14
). Suppose
\mathcal{A}
is not Boolean. Then
n=\#\mathcal{A}>r=\rank\mathcal{A}
. We have

\beta_{r,n}(\mathcal{A})\geq\beta_{r,n}^{\mathrm{geod}}(\mathcal{A})=\#\mathrm{Ch}(\mathcal{A}),

where the equality follows from Corollary
5.3
. This means that
\mathcal{A}
is not diagonal.
∎

5.3.
Magnitude homology with support

Consider a proper chain
\vec{x}=(x_{0},\ldots,x_{k})\in P_{k,\ell}(\mathcal{A})
, and define
S(\vec{x})=S(x_{0},x_{1})\cup\cdots\cup S(x_{k-1},x_{k})
as the set of hyperplanes crossed by
\vec{x}
. Observe that
\#S(\vec{x})\leq\ell(\vec{x})
. One verifies that
x_{i}
is smooth in
\vec{x}
, i.e.
d(x_{i-1},x_{i+1})=d(x_{i-1},x_{i})+d(x_{i},x_{i+1})
, if and only if
S(x_{i-1},x_{i+1})=S(x_{i-1},x_{i})\sqcup S(x_{i},x_{i+1})
. Thus, if a proper chain
\vec{y}
appears in the boundary of
\vec{x}
, then
S(\vec{x})=S(\vec{y})
. In other words, the boundary operator
\partial:MC_{k,\ell}(\mathcal{A})\to MC_{k-1,\ell}(\mathcal{A})
preserves crossed hyperplanes. This motivates the following definition.

Definition 5.5
.

For an arrangement
\mathcal{A}
and a subarrangement
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
, we define the
\mathcal{B}
-supported magnitude chain group

MC_{k,\ell}(\mathcal{A};\mathcal{B})
as the subgroup of
MC_{k,\ell}(\mathcal{A})
generated by
P_{k,\ell}(\mathcal{A};\mathcal{B})=\{\vec{x}\in P_{k,\ell}(\mathcal{A})\mid S(\vec{x})=\mathcal{B}\}
. The above discussion shows that
MC_{*,\ell}(\mathcal{A};\mathcal{B})
forms a subcomplex of
MC_{*,\ell}(\mathcal{A})
, called the
\mathcal{B}
-supported magnitude complex
of
\mathcal{A}
. Its homology
MH_{*,\ell}(\mathcal{A};\mathcal{B})
is called the
\mathcal{B}
-supported magnitude homology
of
\mathcal{A}
.

The following result is a direct consequence of the above observation that the magnitude boundary operator preserves crossed hyperplanes.

Proposition 5.6
.

For an arrangement
\mathcal{A}
, we have the following decomposition of magnitude chain groups:

MC_{k,\ell}(\mathcal{A})=\bigoplus_{\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{A}}MC_{k,\ell}(\mathcal{A};\mathcal{B}),

which induces a decomposition of homology groups

MH_{k,\ell}(\mathcal{A})=\bigoplus_{\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{A}}MH_{k,\ell}(\mathcal{A};\mathcal{B}).

Furthermore,
MC_{k,\ell}(\mathcal{A};\mathcal{B})=MH_{k,\ell}(\mathcal{A};\mathcal{B})=0
if
\ell<\#\mathcal{B}
.

Using the notion of magnitude homology with support, we define interior magnitude homology. Later, we show that this is a categorification of the interior magnitude defined in Definition
3.6
.

Definition 5.7
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an arrangement and let
X\in L(\mathcal{A})
be a flat. We define the
X
-supported magnitude complex
of
\mathcal{A}
by

MC_{*,\ell}(\mathcal{A};X):=\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{A}\\
\cap\mathcal{B}=X\end{subarray}}MC_{*,\ell}(\mathcal{A};\mathcal{B}).

Its homology
MH_{*,\ell}(\mathcal{A};X)
is called the
X
-supported magnitude homology
of
\mathcal{A}
. In particular, we define

MC_{*,\ell}^{\circ}(\mathcal{A}):=MC_{*,\ell}(\mathcal{A};\cap\mathcal{A}),

and call it the length
\ell

interior magnitude complex
of
\mathcal{A}
. We call
MH_{*,\ell}^{\circ}(\mathcal{A}):=H_{*}(MC_{*,\ell}^{\circ}(\mathcal{A}))
the length
\ell

interior magnitude homology
of
\mathcal{A}
. We also define the
interior magnitude Betti number

\beta_{k,\ell}^{\circ}(\mathcal{A}):=\rank MH_{k,\ell}^{\circ}(\mathcal{A})
and the length
\ell

interior magnitude Euler characteristic

\chi_{\ell}^{\circ}(\mathcal{A}):=\sum_{k}(-1)^{k}\beta_{k,\ell}^{\circ}(\mathcal{A})
.

5.4.
Face decomposition for magnitude homology

Here we prove a homological version of the face decomposition formula established in Theorem
3.7
.
To this end, we introduce another notation.

Definition 5.8
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an arrangement and let
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
be a subarrangement. Consider an induced subgraph
G
of
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A})
. We define the
localized
\mathcal{B}
-supported magnitude chain group

MC_{k,\ell}(G;\mathcal{B})
as the subgroup of
MC_{k,\ell}(\mathcal{A};\mathcal{B})
generated by chains entirely contained in
G
:

P_{k,\ell}(G;\mathcal{B})=\{(x_{0},\ldots,x_{k})\in P_{k,\ell}(\mathcal{A};\mathcal{B})\mid x_{0},\ldots,x_{k}\in V(G)\}.

Lemma 5.9
.

For
F\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{A})
, the chain groups
MC_{*,\ell}(\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A})_{F};\mathcal{B})
form a subcomplex of
MC_{*,\ell}(\mathcal{A};\mathcal{B})
.

Proof.

We must show that the boundary operator
\partial
stabilizes the subgroup. By Proposition
5.6
,
\partial
strictly preserves the set
\mathcal{B}
of crossed hyperplanes. Furthermore, by Lemma
2.29
, the
F
-subgraph
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A})_{F}
is gated, and therefore convex (Proposition
2.2
). Because the boundary operator
\partial_{i}
only deletes the chamber
x_{i}
from the sequence, all remaining chambers in the chain
\partial_{i}\vec{x}
are still contained in the vertex set of
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A})_{F}
. Thus,
\partial(MC_{k,\ell}(\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A})_{F};\mathcal{B}))\subseteq MC_{k-1,\ell}(\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A})_{F};\mathcal{B})
.
∎

Proposition 5.10
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an arrangement and let
X\in L(\mathcal{A})
be a flat. Then the
X
-supported magnitude complex can be decomposed as follows:

MC_{*,\ell}(\mathcal{A};X)\cong\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}F\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{A})\\
s(F)=X\end{subarray}}MC_{*,\ell}^{\circ}(\mathcal{A}_{F}).

Proof.

By Definition
5.7
,
MC_{*,\ell}(\mathcal{A};X)=\bigoplus_{\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{A},\cap\mathcal{B}=X}MC_{*,\ell}(\mathcal{A};\mathcal{B})
. For a subarrangement
\mathcal{B}
with
\cap\mathcal{B}=X
, consider a chain
\vec{x}\in P_{k,\ell}(\mathcal{A};\mathcal{B})
. By Definition
5.5
,
S(\vec{x})=\mathcal{B}
. The chain
\vec{x}
never crosses any hyperplane in
\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{A}_{X}
. Therefore, every chamber in
\vec{x}
has a constant nonzero sign on each hyperplane
H\in\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{A}_{X}
. These signs determine a chamber
F\in\mathrm{Ch}(\mathcal{A}^{X})
, or equivalently a face
F\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{A})
with
s(F)=X
. Therefore the chain
\vec{x}
lies in the
F
-subgraph
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A})_{F}
. By Lemma
2.29
,
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A})_{F}
is a gated subgraph of
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A})
isometrically isomorphic to
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A}_{F})
. This induces a direct-sum decomposition of the
\mathcal{B}
-supported chains over the faces supported on
X
:

MC_{k,\ell}(\mathcal{A};\mathcal{B})\cong\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}F\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{A})\\
s(F)=X\end{subarray}}MC_{k,\ell}(\mathcal{A}_{F};\mathcal{B}).

Summing both sides over
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
such that
\cap\mathcal{B}=X
, we obtain

MC_{*,\ell}(\mathcal{A};X)\cong\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}F\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{A})\\
s(F)=X\end{subarray}}\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{A}_{F}\\
\cap\mathcal{B}=X\end{subarray}}MC_{*,\ell}(\mathcal{A}_{F};\mathcal{B}).

Since we are summing over
F
supported on
X
, we have
\mathcal{A}_{F}=\mathcal{A}_{X}
and
\cap\mathcal{A}_{F}=X
, so the inner sum is exactly
MC_{*,\ell}^{\circ}(\mathcal{A}_{F})
. This proves the desired formula.
∎

We are now ready to state the main structural result for the magnitude homology of an arrangement.

Theorem 5.11
.

For an arrangement
\mathcal{A}
, we have the following face decomposition of its magnitude complex:

MC_{k,\ell}(\mathcal{A})\cong\bigoplus_{F\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{A})}MC_{k,\ell}^{\circ}(\mathcal{A}_{F}),

which induces the following face decomposition of magnitude homology:

MH_{k,\ell}(\mathcal{A})\cong\bigoplus_{F\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{A})}MH_{k,\ell}^{\circ}(\mathcal{A}_{F}).

Proof.

By Proposition
5.6
, the magnitude chain complex decomposes into a direct sum over all subarrangements
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
,

MC_{k,\ell}(\mathcal{A})=\bigoplus_{\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{A}}MC_{k,\ell}(\mathcal{A};\mathcal{B}).

Grouping the
\mathcal{B}
-summands according to
\cap\mathcal{B}
, we have

MC_{k,\ell}(\mathcal{A})=\bigoplus_{X\in L(\mathcal{A})}\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{A}\\
\cap\mathcal{B}=X\end{subarray}}MC_{k,\ell}(\mathcal{A};\mathcal{B})=\bigoplus_{X\in L(\mathcal{A})}MC_{k,\ell}(\mathcal{A};X).

By Proposition
5.10
, the
X
-supported complex decomposes into interior complexes

MC_{k,\ell}(\mathcal{A})\cong\bigoplus_{X\in L(\mathcal{A})}\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}F\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{A})\\
s(F)=X\end{subarray}}MC_{k,\ell}^{\circ}(\mathcal{A}_{F})=\bigoplus_{F\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{A})}MC_{k,\ell}^{\circ}(\mathcal{A}_{F}).

The decomposition of homology follows immediately.
∎

Corollary 5.12
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an arrangement with
\rank\mathcal{A}=r
and
\#\mathcal{A}=n
.
Then we have

\mathrm{Mag}^{\circ}(\mathcal{A})=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\chi_{\ell}^{\circ}(\mathcal{A})q^{\ell}.

Moreover, we have
\chi_{\ell+n}^{\circ}(\mathcal{A})=(-1)^{r}\chi_{\ell}(\mathcal{A})
for
\ell\geq 0
, and
\chi_{\ell}^{\circ}(\mathcal{A})=0
for
\ell<n
.

Proof.

Define
\Delta(\mathcal{A})
to be the difference of the two expressions:

\Delta(\mathcal{A})=\mathrm{Mag}^{\circ}(\mathcal{A})-\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\chi_{\ell}^{\circ}(\mathcal{A})q^{\ell}.

We prove
\Delta(\mathcal{A})=0
by induction on the rank of
\mathcal{A}
.
By Theorem
3.7
and Theorem
5.11
, we have

\mathrm{Mag}(\mathcal{A})=\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{A})}\mathrm{Mag}^{\circ}(\mathcal{A}_{F}),\quad\chi_{\ell}(\mathcal{A})=\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{A})}\chi^{\circ}_{\ell}(\mathcal{A}_{F}).

Since
\mathrm{Mag}(\mathcal{A})
can be written as
\sum_{\ell}\chi_{\ell}(\mathcal{A})q^{\ell}
, we obtain

\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{A})}\Delta(\mathcal{A}_{F})=0.

The induction hypothesis shows that
\Delta(\mathcal{A}_{F})=0
for every face
F
other than
\bigcap\mathcal{A}
.
Thus
\Delta(\mathcal{A})=0
.
The final assertion follows from the identity
\mathrm{Mag}^{\circ}(\mathcal{A})=(-1)^{r}q^{n}\mathrm{Mag}(\mathcal{A})
.
∎

Remark 5.13
.

The identity
\chi_{\ell+n}^{\circ}(\mathcal{A})=(-1)^{r}\chi_{\ell}(\mathcal{A})
in Corollary
5.12
is analogous to the Ehrhart–Macdonald reciprocity theorem for lattice polytopes
[
11
,
26
]
and Stanley’s reciprocity theorem for posets
[
30
]
.

Remark 5.14
.

In light of Corollary
5.12
, it is natural to expect that there exists an isomorphism of homology groups

MH_{k,\ell}(\mathcal{A})\cong MH_{k+r,\ell+n}^{\circ}(\mathcal{A})

for all
k,\ell\geq 0
, and that the interior magnitude homology
MH_{k,\ell}^{\circ}(\mathcal{A})
vanishes for
0\leq\ell<n
.

5.5.
Magnitude homology on the diagonal

In this subsection, we give a combinatorial formula for the diagonal magnitude homology
MH_{\ell,\ell}(\mathcal{A})
of an arrangement
\mathcal{A}
.
First, we determine the interior magnitude homology of Boolean arrangements using Theorem
5.11
.

Corollary 5.15
.

For the Boolean arrangement
Bl_{d}
, the interior magnitude homology
MH_{k,\ell}^{\circ}(Bl_{d})
is concentrated on the diagonal
k=\ell
, and
MH_{\ell,\ell}^{\circ}(Bl_{d})
is free abelian of rank

\beta_{\ell,\ell}^{\circ}(Bl_{d})=2^{d}\binom{\ell-1}{d-1}.

Proof.

We know from Corollary
5.4
and Example
2.14
that
\mathcal{T}(Bl_{d})
is diagonal and
MH_{\ell,\ell}(Bl_{d})
is free abelian of rank
\beta_{\ell,\ell}(Bl_{d})=2^{d}\binom{\ell+d-1}{d-1}
. By Theorem
5.11
, the interior magnitude homology
MH_{k,\ell}^{\circ}(Bl_{d})
is a direct summand of
MH_{k,\ell}(Bl_{d})
, and hence is concentrated on the diagonal
k=\ell
. Theorem
5.11
also gives

\beta_{\ell,\ell}(Bl_{d})=\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}(Bl_{d})}\beta_{\ell,\ell}^{\circ}((Bl_{d})_{F}).

If
F\in\mathcal{F}(Bl_{d})
has
\codim F=j
, then the localization
(Bl_{d})_{F}
has essentialization
Bl_{j}
. Applying Möbius inversion on
\mathcal{F}(Bl_{d})
yields the desired formula.
∎

Next we prove the following vanishing result for diagonal interior magnitude homology of non-Boolean arrangements.

Theorem 5.16
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an arrangement of
\rank\mathcal{A}=r
and
\#\mathcal{A}=n
. Suppose
\mathcal{A}
is not Boolean, so that
r<n
. Then the diagonal interior magnitude homology of
\mathcal{A}
vanishes in all lengths:

MH_{\ell,\ell}^{\circ}(\mathcal{A})=0,\quad\forall\ell\geq 0.

Proof.

By Definition
5.7
, we have

MH_{\ell,\ell}^{\circ}(\mathcal{A})=\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{A}\\
\cap\mathcal{B}=\cap\mathcal{A}\end{subarray}}MH_{\ell,\ell}(\mathcal{A};\mathcal{B}).

We shall prove that each summand vanishes. In what follows, we suppose that
\mathcal{A}
is essential without loss of generality, because passing to the essentialization does not alter the tope graph or the intersection lattice.

For
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
such that
\cap\mathcal{B}=\cap\mathcal{A}=\{0\}
, let
m=\#\mathcal{B}
. Then
m\geq r
. Let
\phi:\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A})\hookrightarrow Q_{\mathcal{A}}=\{+,-\}^{\mathcal{A}}
be the embedding of
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A})
into the hypercube
Q_{\mathcal{A}}
. Since
\phi
is distance preserving, it induces an injective chain map
\phi_{\#}:MC_{*,\ell}(\mathcal{A})\hookrightarrow MC_{*,\ell}(Q_{\mathcal{A}})
.

Let
c\in MC_{\ell,\ell}(\mathcal{A};\mathcal{B})
be a cycle. It maps to a cycle
\phi_{\#}(c)\in MC_{\ell,\ell}(Q_{\mathcal{A}})
. By the endpoint decomposition (
2.2
), we may suppose without loss of generality that
c
is a linear combination of
\mathcal{B}
-supported walks with a common starting vertex and a common terminal vertex. This means that the sequence of chambers in each walk of
c
flips exactly the
m
coordinates corresponding to
\mathcal{B}
, while the signs on the remaining
n-m
coordinates corresponding to
\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{B}
remain equal to those of the starting vertex. Geometrically, this means that the entire cycle
\phi_{\#}(c)
is confined to an
m
-dimensional face of
Q_{\mathcal{A}}
, denoted by
Q_{\mathcal{B}}
by abuse of notation.

We claim that this subcube
Q_{\mathcal{B}}
contains a vertex
v_{0}
that is not a vertex of
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A})
. Indeed, in the case
m>r
, the hyperplanes of
\mathcal{B}
contain linear dependencies. Consequently, the total number of geometrically realizable sign patterns on
\mathcal{B}
is less than
2^{m}
. However, the subcube
Q_{\mathcal{B}}
contains all
2^{m}
sign patterns. Therefore, at least one sign pattern in this subcube is not geometrically realizable, so at least one vertex
v_{0}
inside the subcube
Q_{\mathcal{B}}
does not correspond to a chamber in
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A})
. In the case
m=r
,
\mathcal{B}
is a Boolean subarrangement. Its
2^{r}
chambers form full-dimensional orthants. Because
\mathcal{A}
is not Boolean, there exists a hyperplane
H\in\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{B}
, and we must have
r\geq 2
. This hyperplane
H
cannot intersect the interior of every orthant. Thus, there exists a pair of antipodal chambers
C_{v},C_{-v}\in\mathrm{Ch}(\mathcal{B})
separated by
H
(that is,
H
does not cut their interiors), where
v\in\{+,-\}^{\mathcal{B}}
is the sign vector of
C_{v}
. We may assume that the extension of
v
to
\tilde{v}\in\{+,-\}^{\mathcal{A}}
using the signs of the starting vertex of
c
, regarded as a vertex of
Q_{\mathcal{A}}
, is geometrically realizable, i.e. is a vertex of
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A})
. Then the extension of
-v
to
\widetilde{-v}\in\{+,-\}^{\mathcal{A}}
is not geometrically realizable, since by definition
\widetilde{-v}_{H}=\widetilde{v}_{H}
, contradicting the geometric condition that
C_{v}
and
C_{-v}
are separated by
H
. Thus, we have shown the existence of such a forbidden vertex
v_{0}
in the subcube
Q_{\mathcal{B}}
.

For a fixed
\ell
,
MC_{\ell+1,\ell}(Q_{\mathcal{A}})=0
; by abuse of notation, we regard
\phi_{\#}(c)
as a homology class in
MH_{\ell,\ell}(Q_{\mathcal{A}})
. Recall that the isomorphism given in Example
2.14

\nabla:\bigoplus_{\ell_{1}+\cdots+\ell_{n}=\ell}MH_{\ell_{1},\ell_{1}}(Q_{1})\otimes\cdots\otimes MH_{\ell_{n},\ell_{n}}(Q_{1})\xrightarrow{\cong}MH_{\ell,\ell}(Q_{\mathcal{A}}),

is induced by the Eilenberg–Zilber chain map
[
17
]
. We may assume that there are basis elements
b^{\lambda}
of the left-hand side, one for each summand, indexed by a composition
\lambda=(\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{n})
of
\ell
, such that
\phi_{\#}(c)=\sum_{\lambda}d_{\lambda}\nabla(b^{\lambda})
for some scalars
d_{\lambda}
. We must show that
d_{\lambda}=0
.

Since
c
crosses all hyperplanes in
\mathcal{B}
, its image
\phi_{\#}(c)
in
MC_{\ell,\ell}(Q_{\mathcal{A}})
is a linear combination of walks in
Q_{\mathcal{A}}
along which every
\mathcal{B}
-coordinate is flipped at least once. This means that the basis elements
b^{\lambda}
involved are indexed by compositions
\lambda=(\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{n})
with all
\ell_{i}\geq 1
for
i\in\mathcal{B}
. We write such an element as
b^{\lambda}=b_{1}^{\lambda}\otimes\cdots\otimes b_{n}^{\lambda}
, where
b_{i}^{\lambda}\in MH_{\ell_{i},\ell_{i}}(Q_{1})
is an
\ell_{i}
-step walk in the
i
-th
Q_{1}
. Thus
b^{\lambda}
consists of
\ell
steps in total in these
n
dimensions with distribution
\lambda
.

By definition of the Eilenberg–Zilber map,
\nabla(b^{\lambda})
is the signed sum of all possible shuffles of all these
\ell
steps. It is a linear combination of walks in
Q_{\mathcal{A}}
from the vertex
(s(b_{1}^{\lambda}),\ldots,s(b_{n}^{\lambda}))
to the vertex
(t(b_{1}^{\lambda}),\ldots,t(b_{n}^{\lambda}))
, where
s(b_{i}^{\lambda})
and
t(b_{i}^{\lambda})
represent the starting vertex and terminal vertex in
Q_{1}
, respectively. Since all
\ell_{i}\geq 1
for
i\in\mathcal{B}
, any vertex in the subcube
Q_{\mathcal{B}}
must be visited by at least one walk in
\nabla(b^{\lambda})
. The next key observation is that if
\lambda\neq\mu
, then
\nabla(b^{\lambda})
and
\nabla(b^{\mu})
share no common walk because their step distributions differ.

Remark 5.17
.

By Corollary
5.15
and Theorem
5.16
, we see that
\mathcal{A}
is Boolean if and only if
\beta_{\ell,\ell}^{\circ}(\mathcal{A})\neq 0
for some
\ell\geq 1
.

Corollary 5.18
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an arrangement. The diagonal magnitude homology
MH_{\ell,\ell}(\mathcal{A})
is free abelian of rank
\beta_{\ell,\ell}(\mathcal{A})
, which is determined by the Boolean flats of the intersection lattice
L(\mathcal{A})
. More precisely, for
\ell\geq 1
,

\beta_{\ell,\ell}(\mathcal{A})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}X\in L(\mathcal{A})\\
\rank X=\#\mathcal{A}_{X}\end{subarray}}c^{X}\cdot 2^{\rank X}\binom{\ell-1}{\rank X-1}.

Consequently,
\beta_{\ell,\ell}(\mathcal{A})
grows as a polynomial in
\ell
of degree
d_{\max}-1
, where

d_{\max}=\max\{\rank X\mid X\in L(\mathcal{A}),\ \rank X=\#\mathcal{A}_{X}\}.

Proof.

Since
MC_{\ell+1,\ell}(\mathcal{A})=0
, the diagonal magnitude homology
MH_{\ell,\ell}(\mathcal{A})
is free abelian. By Theorem
5.11
, the global magnitude homology admits a face decomposition. Restricting this decomposition to the diagonal (
k=\ell
) and taking Betti numbers, we obtain

\beta_{\ell,\ell}(\mathcal{A})=\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{A})}\beta^{\circ}_{\ell,\ell}(\mathcal{A}_{F}).

By Theorem
5.16
, the interior Betti number
\beta^{\circ}_{\ell,\ell}(\mathcal{A}_{F})
vanishes unless the localization
\mathcal{A}_{F}
is Boolean, i.e.
\#\mathcal{A}_{F}=\rank\mathcal{A}_{F}
. Therefore, the sum collapses to the faces whose support flat
X=s(F)
satisfies
\rank X=\#\mathcal{A}_{X}
. For these faces,
\mathcal{A}_{F}\cong Bl_{\rank X}
. We can group the nonzero terms of the summation by their support flats
X\in L(\mathcal{A})
. For each valid flat
X
, the number of faces in
\mathcal{A}
with support
X
is exactly
c^{X}
. Substituting the known formula for the interior diagonal Betti numbers of the Boolean arrangement (Corollary
5.15
), namely
\beta^{\circ}_{\ell,\ell}(Bl_{d})=2^{d}\binom{\ell-1}{d-1}
where
d=\rank X
, we obtain

\beta_{\ell,\ell}(\mathcal{A})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}X\in L(\mathcal{A})\\
\rank X=\#\mathcal{A}_{X}\end{subarray}}c^{X}\cdot 2^{\rank X}\binom{\ell-1}{\operatorname{rank}X-1}.

For
\ell\geq\rank X
, the binomial
\binom{\ell-1}{\rank X-1}
is a polynomial in
\ell
of degree
\rank X-1
. Therefore, for
\ell
sufficiently large,
\beta_{\ell,\ell}(\mathcal{A})
grows as a polynomial in
\ell
of degree
d_{\max}-1
.
∎

Remark 5.19
.

Example
2.14
and Proposition
5.1
(2)(3) are special cases of the formula in Corollary
5.18
.
