1. PROJECT ABSTRACT
This project aimed to develop and implement a new framework for improving bioproduction under harsh
conditions, resulting in economic advantages for industrial processes. Our approach addresses the failure
of reactions required for bio-production, due to inhibition, denaturation, mis-folding or disorder of
individual enzymes, using E. coli as a model system. We have developed and implemented theoretical and
experimental frameworks for bioproduction at low intracellular pH, and updated and synthesized
frameworks for bioproduction at high temperature. Our approach is complementary to efforts to improve
microbial robustness by engineering the cell membrane and has advantages relative to evolutionary-based
organism improvement by prioritizing bio-production.

3. SUMMARY OF OBJECTIVES AND DISCOVERIES
The original goal of this project was to improve bioproduction at high temperature or at low pH by
identifying poorly-functioning enzymes and then replacing them with more robust versions. We have
made substantial progress in developing and applying experimental and theoretical frameworks relevant
to low intracellular pH. Ongoing experimental work is addressing protein function at high temperature.
3.1 Representative stressor: low intracellular pH

It has been previously established that the E. coli product profile during anaerobic fermentation changes
as the extracellular pH decreases. Specifically, there is a decrease in the production of formate, acetate
and ethanol and a greater than 10-fold increase in production of lactate (Stokes, 1949). Reproduction of
this experimental result is only possible when the model accounts for the effect of pH (Figure 1). Having
demonstrated the necessity for accounting for the effect of intracellular acidification, the PET-FBA model
allowed us to explore the associated metabolic burdens and cellular coping mechanisms. The shift
towards lactate production can be explained as a means of maintaining sufficient NAD regeneration
despite the reduced protein efficiency of the acetyl-CoA branch used at neutral pH. The PET-FBA model
demonstrates the importance of accounting for seemingly small changes in the intracellular environment
and could be modified to account for other stressors.

3.1.2 Quantitative assessment of protein pH sensitivity: The breadth of pre-existing quantitative
(literature) data regarding protein sensitivity to low pH was much smaller than we had anticipated. We
have performed a proteome-wide quantitative analysis of the E. coli proteome, resulting in the estimation
of a pH50 for 1,512 proteins (Figure X). An additional 148 proteins were determined to be stable at pH
values as low as 2.5. This omics-wide analysis is consistent with previously-published and our own assay-
based assessment of purified proteins.
The experimental procedure used here was inspired by proteomic studies of temperature stability, such as
Leuenberger et al and Jarzab et al. This work was not part of our original project description, but was
identified as needed based on the low availability of pH stability data. This method is not restricted to low
pH and could be applied to, for example, high product concentration. A manuscript describing these
results is in the final stages of preparation for submission to Molecular Systems Biology and is attached to
this report. It is important to note that these studies reflect protein stability – the ability of the protein to
remain soluble – and not protein activity.

3.1.3 Intracellular acidification as a stressor during succinate production
Our original project objectives included enzyme replacement in succinate-producing E. coli as a means of
mitigating the burden of intracellular acidification. We obtained strain FZ591 (Zhu et al 2017) and
performed some baseline characterization. The main prediction of the PET-FBA for mitigating
intracellular acidification is to increase abundance of lactate dehydrogenase as a means of maintaining
NAD. This was judged to be an ineffective strategy to implement in FZ591, as it would divert flux away
from succinate production.
3.1.4 Building a predictive model for enzyme pH stability
Given the substantial amount of data regarding protein temperature tolerance, there are many models for
predicting enzyme thermostability as a function of sequence / structure. There is much less data available
for pH, and thus there is an absence of models. One reason for the discrepancy in data availability
between temperature and pH is that the temperature inside of a cell is (generally) the same as the
temperature outside of the cell. If an organism can, for example, grow at 50°C, it is assumed that all
proteins encoded by this organism can tolerate temperatures of 50°C. Intracellular pH is more

complicated, as cells maintain an intracellular pH that differs from the extracellular pH. Intracellular pH
is also more difficult to measure, and these measurements have been performed for a limited number of
organisms. Thus, pH sensitivity data is only available for studies that have been performed with purified
proteins. The subtle distinction between enzyme activity (the ability to convert the substrate to product)
and enzyme stability (the ability to remain soluble) is also a confounding factor, as these terms are
sometimes used interchangeably.
Our team has been working to curate the available data from hundreds of papers – not restricted to E. coli
– in order to build a predictive model for enzyme activity. As with 3.1.2, this model was not part of our
proposed work but was judged to be impactful and needed. We have also developed a transfer learning
approach using organism-specific data (the pH of optimal growth, minimum growth and maximum
growth) and amino acid pKa data. Our model also focuses on prediction of a range of pH values over
which the enzyme will be active, instead of a single maximum value. This model has been presented at
scientific conferences and a manuscript is in preparation.

3.2.2 Experimental identification of temperature-limitation of the methionine biosynthesis pathway.
It is well-established that methionine biosynthesis enzyme MetA (homoserine o-succinyltransferase)
serves as a ‘metabolic fuse’ for E. coli. Mordukhova et al’s 2008 demonstration that evolution of MetA
for improved temperature stability (I229T N267D) increased the specific growth rate of E. coli at high
temperatures, with direct media supplementation enabling growth at even higher temperatures (46°C).
However, neither Chang et al’s 2013 genome-scale modeling or our own updated model described above
identified methionine biosynthesis as a potential bottleneck at high temperatures. Therefore, we also used
an experimental approach to complement the use of the genome-scale model.
We first reproduced Mordukhova’s replacement of the wild-type MetA with MetAI229T,N267D and confirmed
its ability to enable E. coli growth at 45°C (Figure 4). We expanded upon the ability of methionine to
rescue growth at 46°C, by observing this same rescue effect for homocysteine and for cystathionine (both
downstream of MetB) and no rescue effect for homocysteine or O-succinyl-L-homoserine plus cysteine.

This suggests that improving the thermostability of MetA is not sufficient to restore the methionine
biosynthesis pathway function, as MetB apparently fails at 46°C. Surprisingly, we have observed that
purified MetB is stable at temperatures up to 55°C. Assessment of activity as a function of temperature is
ongoing.

3.2.3 Metabolomics approach to identification of temperature-limiting enzymes. Sevin et al (2017)
demonstrated that a metabolomics approach could detect the failure of individual enzymes. Specifically,
they prepared a metabolite extract and collected protein lysate from a wild-type strain and individual
deletion mutants. The metabolite extract was subjected to metabolomics analysis (LC-MS and GC-MS)
before and after incubation with the protein lysates. The altered metabolite profile was used to infer the
metabolic function of the deleted genes. Here, we modified this approach but had a single protein lysate
(wild-type) that was incubated at various temperatures (Figure 5). We envisioned this approach as
complementary to the pH stability analysis described in 3.1.2 – the proteomic analysis quantifies stability
while the metabolomics analysis quantifies activity. In this manner, the effect of any stressor on protein
activity and stability could be quantified in a high-throughput manner.
This approach identified several metabolic pathways that were perturbed due to incubation of the cell
lysate at 50°C. Some of these are consistent with the genome-scale modeling results described above,
such as the pathways for biosynthesis of cofactors (biotin). And consistent with the results described in
3.2.2, we observed perturbation of the methionine biosynthesis pathway. Complementary to these findings
was the identification of perturbations to the isoleucine production pathway (data not shown).
We confirmed the temperature sensitivity of the isoleucine pathway through an amino acid dropout
experiment. We observed that supplementation of MOPS defined glucose media with all twenty amino
acids enables growth at 48°C. As expected, elimination of methionine from this mixture completely
abrogates the benefit of the amino acid supplementation and no growth is observed at 48°C. Consistent
with the metabolomics analysis, elimination of isoleucine from the mixture is nearly as detrimental. The
fact that metabolites downstream of IlvD are able to successfully replace isoleucine in this mixture
suggests that either IlvC or IlvD are the key bottleneck enzymes. IlvD activity assays indicate that it is not
the problem, leading us to focus our ongoing efforts on IlvC.

Long unbeknownst to us, a 2018 paper (Gumulya et al, Nature Catalysis) had reconstructed IlvC (referred
to only as ketol-acid reductase KARI in their work) from its ancestral sequence and demonstrated its
increased activity at 50°C. Our own experiments with this ancestral version of IlvC showed promise at
48°C when expressed on a plasmid, but not when genomically integrated. We are still troubleshooting
these final experiments prior to preparation of a manuscript.

3.3 Computational model of protein structure and sequence. This project has provided the opportunity
to develop methods to extract dynamics from experimentally-derived protein structures, datamine protein
structures to obtain protein entropies, improve sequence matching by including structural information and
improve protein annotation and phylogenies. A publication is planned for submission in April 2026
(“Improved Predictions of Protein Stability by Using Large Protein Language Models”).
3.4 Demonstration of the shortcomings of evolutionary approaches. One of the inspirations for this
project is the failure of traditional organism evolution to substantially improve growth at high
temperature. Catoiu et al’s 2023 compilation of the E. coli ‘alleleome’ provided the opportunity to
demonstrate this shortcoming. Specifically, 20% of the 31,000 non-synonymous amino acid shifts
documented in this dataset consist of interconversion of alanine with valine and threonine. Of the 380
possible non-synonymous amino acid substitutions, 225 (59%) are not observed. Thus, adaptive
laboratory evolution is doing a very poor job of sampling the available amino acid space. These results
were presented at the 2024 DOE PI meeting and the attached letter was submitted to Science, but not
accepted. We have struggled to identify an appropriate venue for dissemination of this summary.
Concluding remarks: We expect to publish our temperature related results, though the perceived impact
and novelty is decreased relative to our original expectations. Our approach to theoretical an experimental
characterization of inhibition due to low intracellular pH is viewed as impactful and we intend to pursue
the proteomic-based approach with other inhibitors. This project has provided training opportunities for
undergraduate researchers, graduate students, postdoctoral researchers and research scientists.

 Evolution may be clever, but its reach is short

Laura R. Jarboe, Cargill Professor of Chemical and Biological Engineering, Iowa State University,
ljarboe@iastate.edu

Summary:
Compilation of evolved E. coli genomes highlights the failure of adaptive laboratory evolution to sample
the available biological space.

Main text:
Adaptive laboratory evolution (ALE) is an invaluable tool for understanding biological systems (1, 2) and
improving biomanufacturing platforms (3, 4). ALE can compensate for our imperfect knowledge of
biological systems, as summarized in Orgel’s 2nd rule “evolution is cleverer than you are” (5). Reverse
engineering and retrospective analysis of mutations acquired during ALE provide learning opportunities
and support the goal of being able to ‘predictably program biology in the same way in which we write
software’ (6).

There is a substantial amount of accumulated ALE data for the model organism Escherichia coli. Analysis
of the E. coli alleleome (7) included a compilation of data from ALEdb (8) and long-term evolutionary
experiments (LTEE). These mutations were compared to naturally-occurring sequence variation, with the
conclusion that laboratory-acquired mutations are more likely to impact protein properties. This compiled
data also provides an excellent opportunity to assess the effectiveness of ALE in sampling the possible
biological space. While there are 380 possible non-synonymous substitutions (excluding stops), only 167
(44%) of these are observed among the 33,033 unique non-synonymous amino acid substitutions
documented in the ALEdb and LTEE dataset (Figure 1). When two samples with more than 4,000 coding-
region mutations each are excluded, only 155 (41%) of the possible substitutions are observed.

The observed sparsity of this sampling is consistent with the underlying nature of the biological code.
Some amino acid substitutions require only a single nucleotide change (‘hop’), such as the purine
transition mutation of ACC to GCC, leading to replacement of a threonine with an alanine. Alternatively,
it requires three transversion mutations to substitute a cysteine (UGC or UGU) in place of a methionine
(AUG). At the codon level, the data aligns with the expected trends: the vast majority (>99%) of the
observed substitutions were acquired with a single nucleotide change. Intriguingly, the arginine 
threonine substitution is not observed, even though there are two single-transversion routes to this change
(AGA  ACA, AGG  ACG). However, AGA and AGG are the two least-frequent amino acid codons.

Catoiu et al used the Grantham Score to quantify the severity of amino acid substitutions. This is one of
several scores based on amino acid physicochemical properties. Alternatively, Yampolsky and Stolfzfus
estimated amino acid exchangeability scores from experimental data (9). Several of the high
exchangeability substitutions require two or three mutations and are not observed in the ALE/LTEE
dataset, such as lysine  alanine and glutamine  valine. And yet there are reports of such substitutions
resulting in improved enzyme function. A lysine  alanine substitution increased the half-life of β-1,3-
1,4-glucanase at 70°C by more than 5-fold (10). A glutamine  valine substitution in ketol-acid
reductoisomerase (IlvC) increased NADH-dependent catalytic efficiency by more than 80-fold (11).

The poor sampling of these promising substitutions with current ALE methods is problematic both in
terms of our understanding of biological systems and our ability to improve biomanufacturing. The
existing framework for analyzing and describing evolved genomes places a high priority on identifying
single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) relative to the two- or three-nucleotide substitutions (SUBs) that
would enable some of the under-sampled amino acid substitutions. Care is needed to ensure that multiple
mutations within a single codon are interpreted together, not individually.

There have been advances in directed evolution of individual enzyme targets (12-14). However, these
negate the ‘clever’ nature of evolution by requiring the selection of enzyme targets, as opposed to the
organism-wide scope of ALE. A strategy for refactoring of the E. coli genetic code in order to increase
access to these under-sampled substitutions has been modeled (15). The optimized design involves
recoding 60 of the 64 codons. Refactoring of a even a single codon has been demonstrated (3), but with
resulting ‘severe’ growth impairment.

The dramatic increase in accessibility of genome sequencing and analysis has enabled this data collection
and analysis. And yet if less than half of the available biological space is being sampled, what
opportunities are being missed? This issue of sparse sampling within the natural set of amino acids needs
more attention and discussion. For example, the rarely-sampled substitutions need to be recognized and
given a high priority for further study.

 ABSTRACT

Protein stability is a fundamental determinant of cellular function, robustness, and adaptability, yet
quantitative knowledge of protein stability remains sparse and largely limited to low-throughput,
enzyme-specific assays. While proteome-wide approaches have recently enabled systematic analysis of
thermal stability, the impact of pH on protein stability at the systems level remains poorly characterized.
Here, we present a proteomics-based framework to map acid stability across the Escherichia coli
proteome. We quantified protein solubility following incubation of cell lysates across a range of pH
conditions, exploiting the insolubility of denatured proteins to enrich for stable species prior to mass
spectrometric analysis.

Using this approach, we assessed the acid stability of 1,660 unique E. coli proteins and estimated pH₅₀
values for over 90% of them. Proteome-wide pH50 values ranged from 1.73 to 6.33, with a median of
5.14, revealing widespread heterogeneity in protein acid sensitivity. Approximately 9% of detected
proteins exhibited sustained stability across all tested pH values and were classified as acid stable.
Comparisons with available biochemical literature data and targeted biochemical characterization of
citrate synthase (GltA) confirmed agreement between proteomic and purified-enzyme stability
measurements.

This study provides the first comprehensive map of protein acid stability in E. coli and establishes a
scalable framework for proteome-wide pH stability analysis. Our study also offers a foundation for
identifying unstable proteins that can be engineered to improve robustness of microbial systems for
biotechnology and fermentation applications.

 INTRODUCTION
Proteins are the principal functional molecules of living systems, directly executing nearly all biological
processes. These biomolecules are however sensitive to environment conditions like pH, temperature
and solvent toxicity.
A central property governing protein function is stability. Protein stability is the ability of a protein to
maintain its folded structure and functionality after exposure to stress such as increased temperature,
highly acidic, alkaline or toxic chemicals. Loss of stability can lead to unfolding, aggregation, and
increased susceptibility to proteolysis, with immediate consequences for cellular homeostasis.1,2
Understanding protein stability can help improve optimization of cell-free biosynthesis platforms,
storage of protein products, and inform enzyme engineering strategies.
Despite its importance, quantitative knowledge of protein stability remains sparse. Traditional enzyme
stability assays typically rely on enzyme-specific activity measurements performed under
heterogeneous experimental conditions, often requiring bespoke substrates and protocols. While these
assays provide valuable insight into individual enzymes, their low throughput and lack of
standardization limit comparability across proteins and studies. As a result, organism-wide conclusions
about protein stability cannot be readily drawn from existing data. This limitation is underscored by the
scarcity of published stability measurements: among the approximately 4,200 proteins encoded by the
Escherichia coli genome, pH stability assay data are available for only a few dozen proteins,
highlighting a substantial gap in proteome-level understanding of acid stability.
Proteomics-based approaches offer a powerful alternative by enabling the simultaneous analysis of
thousands of proteins under uniform perturbation conditions. In particular, thermal proteome profiling
and related methods have provided valuable insights into protein thermostability and proteome-wide
melting behavior3–6. However, most proteome-wide stability analyses to date have focused on
temperature, leaving other physiologically relevant stressors comparatively unexplored.
Acidity represents a fundamentally distinct and biologically important challenge to protein stability.
Unlike temperature, which affects intracellular and extracellular environments uniformly, bacteria
actively regulate their cytoplasmic pH through. Mechanisms of ion transport and metabolism within a
narrow range despite fluctuations in extracellular pH.7–11 This decoupling complicates the relationship
between environmental acidity and intracellular protein stability, making acid stress a nontrivial and
underexplored dimension of proteome behavior. Moreover, pH-dependent stability is directly relevant
to protein storage, downstream processing, and large-scale fermentation, where acidification can
negatively impact enzyme function, cellular viability, and bioprocess productivity.
Here, we present a proteomics-based framework for the systematic analysis of protein acid stability in
E. coli (Figure 1). We establish and validate standardized protocols for sample preparation and data
analysis that enable comparative, proteome-wide assessment of protein stability under acidic
conditions. By extending stability profiling beyond temperature to acidity, this study expands the
current understanding of enzyme stability at the systems level and provides a foundation for identifying
unstable protein targets for rational protein and strain engineering.

 MATERIALS AND METHODS
The protocol used in this study was adapted from previous thermostability work3–5, with the
modification that protein lysate was incubated at different pH values instead of different temperatures.
Proteomic analysis was performed at the Iowa State University Protein Facility.
Organism growth and preparation of cell lysate. All experiments used E. coli K12 MG1655. Cells
were grown in filter-sterilized BD DifcoTM Luria-Bertani broth (LB) medium (initial pH 7.0) in 250 mL
baffled flasks filled to 50 mL at 37°C with rotary shaking at 250 rpm to an OD600 of ~0.8. Cells were
harvested by centrifugation at 4000 x g for 10 min at 4°C and washed with ice-cold 1X phosphate
buffered saline (PBS) buffer (pH 7.0), pelleted again, and then resuspended in the lysis buffer. The lysis
buffer contained one tablet of Complete Protease Inhibitor (Roche #65726900) in 50 mL 1X PBS (pH
7.0). The cells were then sonicated at 35% amplitude at 4°C, with cycles of pulse on for 5 seconds,
pulse off for 30 seconds, for approximately 18 minutes. The resulting lysates were then centrifuged at
4000 x g for 20 min at 4°C to remove cellular debris. The supernatants were transferred to a fresh tube,
and the protein concentration was determined using the Modified Lowry Protein Assay Kit (Thermo
Scientific). Protein concentration was then adjusted to approximately 4 mg/mL using the lysis buffer,
generating a stock proteome solution.
Acid treatment of cell lysate. Ice-cold lysis buffer was prepared as above, but with varying pH values,
adjusted using HCl. The stock proteome solution was added to a target concentration of 1 mg/mL and
the pH of the mixture was measured (Figure 2). Samples were held for 48 hours at 4°C. Samples were
then filtered through a 0.45-µm low protein-binding syringe filter (Millipore, ref: SLHVR13SL), and
the concentration of remaining soluble proteins was estimated using the Lowry assay.
SDS-PAGE. Each of the soluble fractions were denatured with Laemmli sample buffer (Bio-Rad
#1610737) containing 5% v/v β-mercaptoethanol and heated for 8 minutes at 98°C and separated on
SDS‐polyacrylamide precast gels (Bio-Rad #4561083) by electrophoresis at 120V. The gels were
stained with Bio-Rad Coomassie Brilliant Blue and de-stained according to standard protocols (Figure
2).
Protein digestion. Soluble fractions were reduced at 37°C for 30 minutes with 5 mM dithiothreitol and
alkylated with 15 mM iodoacetamide for 30 minutes at room temperature in the dark. The solution was
then digested overnight at 37°C with trypsin/Lys-C at a ratio of 1 μg trypsin/Lys-C:25 μg sample.
Formic acid was added to a 1% v/v concentration to stop digestion, and the digestion was dried in a
SpeedVac. Samples were then desalted using C18 columns (Nest Group BioPureSPN Mini, HUM
S18V) before drying again in a SpeedVac. Pierce Peptide Retention Time Calibration Mixture (PRTC,
#88320) was spiked into the sample at 25 fmol/μL to serve as an internal control.
LC-MS/MS analysis. Peptides were separated using a Thermo Scientific DNV PepMap Neo (75 μm
× 50 cm, double nano viper, PN DNV75500PN) with a stainless-steel emitter. A linear gradient from
Buffer A (0.1% v/v formic acid in water) and Buffer B (80% v/v acetonitrile, 20% water, 0.1% v/v
formic acid) was applied over 120 minutes at a flow rate of 0.2 μl/min. The peptide mixture was run
on a Thermo Scientific EASY nLC-1200 coupled to a Thermo Scientific Nanospray FlexIon source
and MS/MS was run on a Thermo Scientific Q Exactive Hybrid Quadrupole-Orbitrap Mass
Spectrometer with a Higher-Energy Collisional Dissociation (HCD) fragmentation cell.
The analysis, protein, and peptide quantification were performed on Proteome Discoverer v.3.0. The
data were searched using Mascot and Sequest HT against the Uniprot E. coli and PRTC databases.
Proteome Discoverer’s Minora Feature Detection node was used for the label-free quantification. The
mass tolerance for chromatographic alignment was set to ±10 ppm, and results were adjusted to a 1%
protein false discovery rate. Carbamylation (Cys) was set as a fixed modification, with dynamic
modifications of oxidation (Met) and deamidation (Asn, Gln).

Estimation of the denaturation point (pH50) and confidence interval. Normalized soluble protein
concentration was assumed to trend with treatment pH and protein denaturation point (pH50) as

Accounting for experimental noise. We tested 10 distinct pH values. Proteins that retained at least
some stability across this pH range would be expected to have 10 associated normalized concentration
values. However, proteins that have a complete loss of stability would be detected in fewer than 10
conditions. These expectations are tempered by experimental noise and the stochastic nature of finite
sampling. For example, some proteins were detected in the near-neutral pH values of 6.8 and 6.0, not
detected at moderate pH values, such as 5.8 and 5.6, but then were detected again at low pH values such
as 3.5 and 2.8. Therefore, we implemented a sliding-window strategy of the sigmoidal model presented
above to decrease the influence of this noise while preserving information.

For each protein, we required concentration data for at least three sequential pH values. As a first pass,
we considered only the four least acidic pH treatments: 6.8, 6.0, 5.8 and 5.4, estimating a 95%
confidence interval for the pH50 for each protein. The dataset was then expanded to include the next two
values, 5.2 and 4.3 and a new 95% confidence interval was estimated. A similar analysis was performed
with eight pH values and then ten. The optimal sliding window for each protein was selected based on
a combined criterion of minimal sum of squared errors (SSE) and maximal data coverage. There were
40 proteins that were not detected at pH 6.8 and yet were quantified at at least pH 6.0, 5.8 and 5.4. For
these proteins, the concentrations were normalized relative to pH 6.0 and the sliding window strategy
still utilized four, six and eight data points, with the final window consisting of nine data points instead
of ten.

 RESULTS
To study the effect of pH on the E. coli MG1655 proteome, we adapted an approach developed to assess
proteome thermostability.3–5 This approach exploits the insolubility of denatured proteins, resulting in
their elimination from the sample prior to protein quantification and identification. Here, instead of
treating the harvested proteome at various temperatures, we treated the harvested proteome at various
pH values. Proteins that were not stable at the incubation pH became insoluble and were removed by
filtration prior to mass spectrometric analysis (Figure 1).
Increased acidity reduces protein solubility
Cells were grown to mid-exponential phase in rich media at pH 7.0 and lysed in the presence of a
protease inhibitor cocktail. Lysates were then held at the indicated pH for 48 hours at 4°C. This
treatment resulted in the expected loss of protein solubility as a function of the treatment pH, evidenced
by visible precipitation (Fig 2A). Similarly, the bulk concentration of soluble proteins decreased to less
than 20% of the initial value at pH values lower than 4 (Fig 2B). Similar trends were apparent when
treated lysates were visualized on a gel (Fig 2C). These results demonstrate the impact of pH on the
bulk protein solubility.
The soluble protein fraction for each treatment pH was subjected to proteomic analysis (Table S1). The
intention of our pH treatment was to enrich the soluble protein population based on the pH sensitivity
of individual proteins. Of the proteins detected in solution at both pH 6.8 and 2.5, we observed a median
concentration decrease of 84%. Also, of the 1,661 unique proteins identified in lysate treated at pH 6.8,
475 (28.5%) decreased in concentration to such a degree that they were no longer detectable in the
lysate treated at pH 2.5.

Quantitative analysis
Having demonstrated that the ‘meltome’ approach can be effectively modified for assessment of protein
pH sensitivity, we normalized all the measurements of protein concentration. Specifically, the
concentration of each protein in solution at each treatment pH was normalized relative to the baseline
condition of pH 6.8. In a few cases (n=40) where protein concentration was estimated at at least three
pH values, but the protein was not detected at pH 6.8, concentrations were normalized relative to pH
6.0. Under ideal conditions, all normalized concentrations should be less than or equal to one. However,
the inherent noise of this type of experimental procedure inevitably results in some values outside of
this expected maximum. Specifically, our set of 13,430 normalized concentrations includes 20% that
are greater than one, <1% of which exceed five. The Jarzab meltome dataset is similar, with 14% of the
abundance ratios being greater than one. Thus, the noise in our dataset is consistent with other similar
studies.

We detected 1,661 unique proteins (Fig. 3). The growth conditions used here were similar to those used
in Schmidt et al’s 17 comprehensive proteomics study and Jarzab et al’s harvesting of proteins for
assessment of thermal stability5, which detected 1,733 and 1,550 respectively. Thus, the degree of
coverage of the E. coli proteome is consistent with other studies. More than half of the proteins detected
in each of these three studies were also detected in the other two.

We then fit each of the normalized concentration profiles to a sigmoidal equation (Eq. 1) and
estimated the pH50 and the associated 95% confidence interval. We were able to estimate a pH50 value
for 1,512 unique E. coli proteins (Supplementary Table 3), ranging from 1.73 to 6.33 with a median of
5.14 (Figure 4A). We also calculated a 95% confidence interval for each estimated pH50 value. Across
all proteins, the median size of this confidence interval is 0.5 pH units.

Data for three representative enzymes is shown in Figure 4B, with pH50 values above and below the
median value. For example, MetK having a pH50 of 5.55+0.09 means that it is more acid sensitive

than KatG (pH50 4.3+0.1), with GadA having a pH50 that is intermediate to these two other proteins
(pH50 4.9+0.1).

Surprisingly, some proteins showed evidence of sustained stability even in highly acidic treatment
conditions. For example, RpsE, RpiP and YibT all had concentration ratios greater than 0.5 in all queried
pH values (Figure 4C). Any protein that was detected in at least four treatments and had a concentration
of of at least 0.5 in all of these measurements was classified as “stable” and removed from the data
fitting pipeline. In total, 148 proteins were given this designation, representing 8.8% of the total proteins
detected here. Note that these proteins are not represented in Fig 4B and do not contribute to the
estimated median pH50 value. Recognition of the presumed structural stability of these enzymes at such
low pH values is useful information for future studies.
Our workflow queried the acid stability of 1,660 unique E. coli proteins. For more than 90% of these,
we calculated a pH50 value and the associated 95% confidence interval. We have classified roughly 9%
of the proteins as stable. The remaining proteins are discussed below.
Estimated proteome stability aligns with existing literature data on protein stability
As described above, many studies of protein pH tolerance assess activity as a function of pH and do not
explicitly consider structural stability. Thus, there is only a small set of E. coli proteins previously
characterized for pH stability. These assays typically involve incubation of purified protein at some pH
value and temperature for some length of time, and then assessment of the remaining protein activity in
a standard assay condition, typically near neutral pH. These stability assays lack a standard approach,
with widely varying incubation times and temperatures. Therefore, our approach not only explicitly
addresses protein stability, it also provides uniform assessment of proteome-wide stability.

We collected literature data describing the pH stability of 42 proteins from E. coli and other enteric
bacteria (Supplementary Table 4). These literature pH50 values had a median of 4.65 with 73% of the
pH50 values between pH 4 and 6 in contrast to a median of 5.14 and 83%, respectively, for the results
presented here. The distribution of the literature pH50 values does not significantly differ from the
distribution of the E. coli proteome pH50 values reported here (unpaired t-test, p = 0.40).

We identified 18 E. coli enzymes with quantitative assessment of pH stability data available both in
the literature18–31 and from our study (Table 1). For these literature studies, there are often only a few
pH values tested. For example, the studies of GlpK measured the stability at pH 5.0, 6.0, 7.0 and 8.0
and while it is apparent that the pH50 lies between 5.0 and 6.0, a more precise estimate is not possible.
Thus, the data is consistent with our estimate of the GlpK pH50 95% confidence interval of 5.65 –
5.70. Similarly, the pH₅₀ values obtained here are consistent with the data from half of these previous
reports (Table 1). Specifically, the literature reports for AroG, GabD, GlpK, GshB, NanA, PanB,
ProA, TesA and TnaA are consistent with our proteomic-based assessments. Since four of the
remaining proteins have a pH50 that is lower than the literature reports and five have a pH50 that is
higher, there is no evidence of a systematic discrepancy between this proteomic-based study and the
literature.

Previous characterization of GabD had been performed at pH values as low as 5.0, with minimal
observed decrease in stability22. However, our approach was able to query a larger range of pH values
and estimate a pH50 of 4.3. Previous characterization of purified TesA involved incubation at pH values
as low as 2.0, with the observed maintenance of at least 60% stability relative to incubation at pH 7.029.
This is consistent with our classification of TesA as stable due to its maintenance of normalized
concentrations greater than 0.50 across all pH values.

Within the literature reports, the inconsistent treatment time of purified proteins is apparent.
Specifically, incubation times range from 5 minutes to 24 hours and incubation temperatures from 4 to
75°C. Surprisingly, the degree of similarity between the literature incubation condition and our own

treatment of lysates at 4°C for 24 hours does not appear to correspond to the degree of similarity
between the observed pH50 values.

Thus, though there are only a few data points available for comparison, our proteomic approach appears
to have reliably reproduced qualitative and quantitative assessments of pH stability gathered in
traditional, laborious biochemical assays. The scarcity of data available for comparison highlights the
need for this type of high-throughput analysis of protein pH sensitivity.

Characterization of purified GltA is consistent with proteomic pH50
We selected citrate synthase (GltA) for further validation of the proteomic approach and for side-by-
side comparison of stability and activity data. This essential protein catalyzes the synthesis of citrate
in the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle and is already known to be subject to allosteric inhibition by
NADH and the downstream metabolite 2-oxoglutarate14. To the best of our knowledge, the pH
sensitivity of GltA has not previously been characterized, either on an activity basis or a structural
basis.

GltA was cloned and purified as described above. For consistency with the proteomic data, the stability
was assessed by incubating the purified enzyme at the indicated pH at 4°C for 24 hours. Then the
insoluble proteins were removed via precipitation. The amount of GltA protein remaining soluble was
then quantified by measuring the activity in the supernatant at pH 6.9 according to a well-established
assay14 (Figure 6). The observed enzyme stability was relatively highat treatment pH values of 5.5 or
higher, but at pH values of 4.5 or below, there was a complete loss of stability. Thus, the experimental
assessment of the stability of purified GltA (pH50 5.3 ± 0.1) is consistent with the proteomic-based
estimate (5.34 ± 0.03), providing further support for our method.

This experimental assessment of GltA activity also provides the opportunity to directly contrast the pH
dependence of enzyme stability to the pH dependence of enzyme activity. The impact of pH on enzyme
activity can be assessed by performing the activity assay at various pH values (Figure 6). Contrastingly,
as demonstrated above, the assessment of stability uses a single assay pH to query the retention of
enzyme stability following treatment. The difference between GltA stability and activity dependence
on pH is apparent. Specifically, while the enzyme mainly retains its soluble form at pH values as low
as 5.5, its activity is halved (relative to pH 7.0) at a pH value of 6.5. This demonstrates the importance
of explicitly defining which protein characteristics are being measured and described.

The greater pH sensitivity of GltA activity relative to stability is like as a result of the dependence of
activity on the protonation states of a small number of active-site residues and local electrostatic
networks, whereas overall protein stability is buffered by distributed interactions across the entire
protein structure32,33

pH sensitivity differs according subcellular location
The data obtained here provides potential insight into general trends of protein pH sensitivity, such as
potential variation according to sub-cellular location. Proteins were flagged according to their known
subcellular compartment, as specified by EcoCyc, and binned according to their estimated pH50 values
(Table X). Significant differences in distribution according to location were observed.

Cytoplasmic proteins were enriched in the moderately acidic range (pH50 = 4.5 – 5.0, 5.0 – 5.5) relative
to those from the inner membrane and periplasm. Specifically, 53% of cytoplasmic proteins had a pH50
in the range of 4.5–5.5, compared to 35% and 34%, respectively. Inner membrane proteins were
enriched at higher pH50 values, particularly in the pH50 5.5–6.0 bin (34%), relative to less than 15% for
the cytoplasmic and periplasmic proteins. Finally, the periplasmic proteins had higher representation in
the bins representing acid tolerance: the categories for pH50<4.0 and ‘stable’. Note that no significant
differences were observed for proteins found in the outer membrane, presumably due to the relatively
small number of proteins in this group (n=45).

The apparent acid stability of the periplasmic proteins is consistent with Liu et al’s study where
periplasmic proteins were shown to be stable under acid and other harsh conditions34. Specifically,
proteins in periplasmic extracts retained solubility, evidenced by visualization on 2D-gel after treatment
for 15 minutes with 0.5M HCl, while non-periplasmic extracts were highly aggregated.

Acid enrichment of specific proteins
As described above, we were able to estimate a pH50 value for more than 90% of the proteins detected
in our experimental condition. Approximately 9% of these proteins were deemed ‘stable’, such as TesA.
Some proteins showed fluctuations in the normalized concentration that prevented either their
classification as stable or the assignment of a pH50 value. Thirteen of these proteins had sparse or noisy
data and were classified as ‘other’. Surprisingly, there were two proteins that showed increased
abundance/concentration as the treatment increased in acidity.

Specifically, ModA, the periplasmic binding protein of the molybdate ABC transporter, became
increasingly abundant with decreasing pH. Tus also exhibited a distinct stability profile, with abundance
peaking near pH 4.0. These results suggest that some proteins may not only possess enhanced stability
under acidic conditions but also are enriched relative to the baseline treatment. One possible explanation
for this enrichment is increased accessibility of the soluble protein fraction to the proteolytic treatment
preceding LC/MS.

Correlation of pH50 with isoelectric point, melting temperature and molecular weight
The large amount of data regarding protein temperature sensitivity enables comprehensive analyses,
such as those presented by Pinney et al (ref). However, there are (relatively few data) regarding protein
pH stability. Here, we performed a cursory comparison of the estimate pH50 values with other available
protein properties. Specifically, we examined possible correlations between pH50 values and isoelectric
point (pI) and molecular weight (MW), both obtained from UniProt and mainly predictable based on
protein sequence. Linear regression analysis revealed no meaningful associations (Figure S1).

We also compared our set of estimated pH50 values to the estimated Tm for each protein. Three studies
have recently queried the temperature stability of the E. coli proteome12–14; our analysis considered each
of these independently (Fig S2). However, again no meaningful trends were observed.

 DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSIONS

We provide a proteome-wide map of acid stability in E. coli. Our analysis revealed that acid treatment
induces widespread protein denaturation, yet individual proteins differ in their stability.

Stability assays done in previous studies as well as the enzyme assay of GltA we performed in our lab
demonstrate that proteomic estimates of stability can capture physiologically relevant effects.

GltA is only one of the enzymes identified as unstable. To expand on these findings, stability assays
for additional key metabolic enzymes to confirm loss of function could open up multiple engineering
targets that, when collectively stabilized, make the entire cell more tolerant. This idea resonates with
“weakest link” concept that an organism’s growth or productivity may be limited by just a few
exceptionally sensitive enzymes. Through a rational, data‐driven approach, we can address these
bottlenecks step by step.

An important future direction is using our data for predictive modeling. Our dataset provides a
foundation for machine learning approaches that relate acid stability to protein sequence and structure.
Such models could streamline enzyme engineering by identifying unstable proteins that could be
modified to improve the robustness or even predict protein sequences and structures that are associated
with highly stable proteins.
Finally, this approach is broadly applicable. It can be extended to other stressors such as solvents or
high salt, and to diverse organisms, providing a versatile framework for mapping proteome stability
under suboptimal conditions.
