U-HRCT and MRI-Based Indicators for Limited Mouth Opening in Patients With Temporomandibular Disorders
Authors: Xiaoqing Yuan, Yanbo Liang, Wen Chen, Ning Zhang, Xinghong Zhou, Pengfei Zhao, Hongxia Yin, Peng Zhang, Wenmin Guan, Ruisen Fu, Xiaofeng Huang, Ruowei Tang, Zhenchang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLimited mouth opening (LMO), a manifestation of temporomandibular disorders (TMD), severely impairs masticatory function, speech, and quality of life. This study aimed to identify ultra-high-resolution computed tomography (U-HRCT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)-based indicators of LMO, and to develop a diagnostic model for LMO.MethodsA total of 226 TMD patients (median age 25.00 years; 81.4% females) with 452 temporomandibular joints (TMJs) who underwent U-HRCT and MRI scanning were retrospectively enrolled. Clinical features (age, sex, maximal mouth opening, and parafunctional oral habits) and imaging features (condylar bone morphology, morphological parameters, and disc position) were analysed by univariate and multivariate generalized estimating equation analyses to identify independent indicators of LMO. All patients were randomly assigned to training and testing sets at a ratio of 7:3. Receiver operating characteristic curve with area under the curve (AUC), calibration curve, and decision curve analysis (DCA) were used to evaluate the performance of the model.ResultsAge, condylar flattening, condylar head height, and anterior disc displacement without reduction were chosen as indicators for LMO and used to construct the model. The training set showed 0.764 of AUC (95% CI, 0.697-0.832) and testing set showed 0.744 of AUC (95% CI, 0.648-0.840) in diagnosing LMO. Calibration curves showed good consistency, and DCA confirmed positive net benefit across clinically relevant thresholds.ConclusionsThis U-HRCT and MRI-integrated model showed potential for individualised LMO risk stratification in TMD patients. The model underscores the synergistic value of multimodal imaging biomarkers and may facilitate a personalised management strategy.

Introduction

LMO, defined as maximal mouth opening (MMO) less than 35 mm, is a common clinical symptom of oromaxillofacial diseases,1 which may be accompanied by pain in the joint and surrounding tissues. Even in the absence of pain, LMO can substantially impair basic physiological functions such as mastication and verbal communication.2 Long-term illness is likely to make patients fall into social isolation, thereby triggering negative psychological problems such as anxiety and depression, and significantly reducing quality of life.3 In addition, patients with LMO often face difficulty in intubation, which poses a major challenge to airway management during anaesthesia and emergency surgery.4

Multiple diseases can contribute to LMO, and many affected patients are diagnosed with temporomandibular joint disorders (TMD),5 with the prevalence of LMO in TMD patients reaching 8.1%.6 The etiology of LMO is multifactorial,7, 8, 9 among which structural abnormalities of the temporomandibular joint (TMJ) are one of the important pathogenic factors. The TMJ is composed of both soft tissue and bony structure, and due to its complex anatomical structure and biomechanical characteristic, it is prone to various lesions.10 Accumulating evidence indicates that both osseous morphological alterations and disc displacement are key pathogenic factors of LMO.11 Changes in condylar morphology and articular disc displacement disrupt the normal anatomical and kinematic relationship between the condyle and articular fossa, creating mechanical impediment to TMJ movement that result in LMO.12,13 Studies have shown that bone morphology is related to LMO, and disc displacement can also lead to mechanical overload and joint deformities, thereby affecting mouth opening movement.14, 15, 16

Due to the concealment of structural abnormalities of the TMJ, clinical physical examination is difficult to accurately identify lesion location and severity, making imaging examination an irreplaceable tool for providing key evidence in the etiological diagnosis and condition assessment of LMO.17 Ultra-high-resolution computed tomography (U-HRCT), a reliable modality for fine bone structure imaging, enables precise assessment of subtle condylar morphological changes and joint space narrowing with up to 50 μm spatial resolution and reduced radiation exposure.18,19 Simultaneously, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) can display changes in TMJ soft tissue and is currently the gold standard for evaluating disc position and morphology.20 Due to coexistence of bone changes and disc displacement in TMD process, comprehensive analysis integrating both CT and MRI data can help improve management strategy for patients with LMO. These structural abnormalities are interrelated and serve as quantifiable imaging biomarker, underscoring the necessity of precise anatomical assessment for understanding LMO pathophysiology and guiding therapeutic decision-making.

Therefore, this study aimed to identify U-HRCT and MRI-based indicators of LMO, and to develop a diagnostic model for LMO. The objective was to enable early identification of high-risk individual and facilitate personalised management strategy in clinical practice.

Methods

Study design and participants

This retrospective study included consecutive patients who presented to the oral and maxillofacial surgery at a tertiary referral centre from November 2021 to June 2024, with a clinical diagnosis of TMD based on the Diagnostic Criteria for Temporomandibular Disorders (DC/TMD).21 The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) clinical diagnosis of TMD per DC/TMD criteria; (2) complete clinical record of active mouth opening measurement; and (3) completion of both U-HRCT and MRI examinations. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) TMJ pathology secondary to trauma, neoplasm, infection, surgery, or other organic lesions, and LMO caused by acute masticatory muscle spasm or non-TMJ disorders (n = 25); (2) suboptimal MRI image quality precluding diagnostic evaluation (n = 28); and (3) incomplete clinical or imaging data (n = 78). Finally, a total of 226 patients were enrolled, comprising 452 TMJs for analysis.

This retrospective study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (No. 2022-P2-366-01 and 2024-P2-061-02) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The informed consent was obtained from the participants.

Definition of LMO

LMO was assessed through MMO measurement. Patients were positioned seated upright with the head and neck in neutral alignment. Dentists measured the vertical distance between incisal edges of upper and lower central incisors with a Vernier caliper, and recorded the maximal painless opening, accurate to millimeter. Based on previous studies, the included patients were divided into two groups: MMO < 35 mm as the LMO group and MMO ≥ 35 mm as the non-LMO group.22,23

U-HRCT image acquisition

A U-HRCT scanner (Ultra3D, LargeV, Beijing) was performed for TMJs unilaterally. The scanning parameters were: tube voltage of 100-110 kVp, tube current-time product of 120-180 mAs, field of view (FOV) of 65 mm, collimation of 35 mm, slice thickness of 0.1 mm, slice interval of 0.1 mm, and voxel size of 0.1 mm × 0.1 mm × 0.1 mm. The radiation dose was 82.99-98.11 µGy per scan.24

MRI image acquisition

MRI examination was performed on a 3.0T MR750 superconducting scanner (GE Healthcare, USA) using a head coil. The scanning sequences included axial T1-weighted imaging, oblique coronal T2-weighted imaging, and closed and open mouth oblique sagittal proton density-weighted imaging. The oblique sagittal plane was prescribed perpendicular to the condylar long axis (line connecting the medial and lateral poles), while the oblique coronal plane was prescribed parallel to this axis. The condylar plane demonstrating maximal cross-sectional area and optimal anatomic delineation on axial images served as the localisation reference. Patients were instructed to achieve maximal intercuspation for closed-mouth sequences and to utilise a mouth-opening device to maintain maximal opening for open-mouth acquisitions.

The scanning parameters were: axial T1-weighted imaging (repetition time [TR] of 692 ms, echo time [TE] of 18 ms, FOV of 20 cm × 20 cm, matrix of 320 × 224, slice thickness of 3.0 mm, slice interval of 0.3 mm, slices of 20); oblique coronal T2-weighted imaging (TR of 4353 ms, TE of 70 ms, FOV of 12 cm × 12 cm, matrix of 320 × 192, slice thickness of 2.0 mm, slice interval of 0.2 mm, slices of 20); and closed and open mouth oblique sagittal proton density-weighted imaging (TR of 1500 ms, TE of 25 ms, FOV of 12 cm × 12 cm, matrix of 320 × 192, slice thickness of 2.0 mm, slice interval of 0 mm, slices of 24).

Standardisation of imaging evaluation and repeatability analysis

All imaging evaluations were performed on a standardised Picture Archiving and Communication System workstation with identical monitor specification and uniform window width/level setting. Observers were fully blinded to clinical information, symptom severity, and study outcome throughout the entire evaluation process. All images were evaluated by a junior radiologist with 5 years of experience (reader 1, X.Y.) under the supervision of a senior radiologist with 10 years of experience (reader 2, R.T.). After one month, reader 1 (X.Y.) repeated the measurement to assess intra-observer reproducibility. The differences between the two observers and the two measurements by reader 1 were analysed to evaluate intra- and inter-observer repeatability, respectively. Intra- and inter-observer repeatability were calculated for quantitative variables using intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) and for qualitative variables using Kappa coefficient.

Imaging evaluation

U-HRCT was used to evaluate the condylar bone morphology and morphological parameters (Figure 1). Condylar bone morphology was categorised as normal or abnormal, with abnormalities including flattening, erosion, osteophyte, sclerosis, or subcortical cyst as follows25,26: (1) flattening was defined as loss of the round natural contour of the condylar head, (2) erosion was defined as damage or absence of the cortical border, (3) osteophyte was defined as hypertrophic marginal bone formation, (4) sclerosis was defined as subchondral bone abnormalities visualised as dense and thickened cortical bone, and (5) subcortical cyst was defined as the cystic structure beneath the cortical bone. The morphological parameters were also measured.10 The horizontal plane at the lowest point of the sigmoid notch is defined as the standard horizontal plane. (1) The distance between the apex of the condyle and the horizontal plane through the articular tubercle was defined as the condylar head height. (2) The distance between the highest point of the articular tubercle and the horizontal plane through the joint nodule was defined as condylar fossa depth. (3) The shortest distance between the anterior slope of condyle and the anterior slope of fossa was defined as the anterior joint space. (4) The shortest distance between the posterior side of condyle and the posterior side of articular fossa was defined as the posterior joint space. (5) The shortest distance from the uppermost point of the condyle to the articular fossa was defined as the superior joint space.Fig. 1Schematic diagram of U-HRCT (A-H) and MRI (I-M) feature measurement methods. MRI was used to evaluate the disc position18 (Figure 1). (1) The normal disc position was defined as the posterior band locating above the apex of the condylar process in the closed-mouth position, and the intermediate band positioning between the condylar process and articular eminence in the open-mouth position. (2) Anterior disc displacement with reduction (ADDwR) was defined as the posterior band was locating anterior to the condylar process in closed-mouth position, but the relationship between the disc and condyle positioning normal in the open-mouth position. (3) Anterior disc displacement without reduction (ADDwoR) was defined as the posterior band locating anterior to the condylar process in closed-mouth position, and keeping its anterior position in the open-mouth position.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 27.0 and R 4.3.2. All analyses were conducted at the TMJ side level, except for Table 1 which summarises patient-level demographic characteristics. Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was employed to verify whether the quantitative data conformed to a normal distribution. Continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range [IQR]), and categorical variables were reported as number (percentage). Intra- and inter-observer repeatability were calculated for quantitative variables using ICC and for qualitative variables using Kappa coefficient. Comparisons between the LMO group and non-LMO group were performed by Mann-Whitney U test or chi-square test. Candidate indicators of LMO were initially screened using univariable generalized estimating equation (GEE) logistic regression with patient ID as the clustering variable and an exchangeable working correlation structure. To avoid potential data leakage caused by assigning bilateral TMJs from the same patient to different datasets, patients were randomly allocated to a training set and testing set in a 7:3 ratio. Both TMJs from the same patient were kept in the same dataset. A diagnostic model was constructed based on the selected independent factors, and its performance was evaluated in the testing set. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were drawn. The area under the curve (AUC) was calculated, as well as the corresponding sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy were calculated based on the Youden index to evaluate the performance. The calibration curve was used to evaluate the calibration of the model. Clinical utility was evaluated through decision curve analysis (DCA) by calculating net benefit across a range of threshold probabilities. A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristic

A total of 226 patients (452 TMJs) were included in this study. The median age was 25.00 years (IQR 17.00-31.00), with 184 females (81.4%) and 42 males (18.6%). The median MMO for the entire cohort was 42.00 mm (IQR 35.00-48.00) (Table 1).

Among the 452 TMJs, 96 (21.2%) sides met the criterion for LMO group, while 356 (78.8%) sides constituted the non-LMO group. For U-HRCT, compared to the non-LMO group, the LMO group exhibited a significantly lower frequency of condylar flattening (19.8% vs 37.4%, P = .001), and lower condylar head height (median 6.05 mm [IQR 4.63-7.50] vs 7.50 mm [IQR 6.20-8.60], P < .001). For MRI, the prevalence of ADDwoR was markedly higher in the LMO group (59.4% vs 31.2%, P < 0.001). Intra- and inter-observer agreement

For U-HRCT and MRI, the intra-observer consistency showed good agreement (ICCs range: 0.78 to 0.88, Kappa coefficients range: 0.79 to 0.88), and inter-observer consistency showed good agreement (ICCs range: 0.75 to 0.83, Kappa coefficients range: 0.79 to 0.88).

Discussion

This study integrated U-HRCT-derived osseous parameters and MRI-based disc position to diagnose the risk of LMO in TMD patients. The model incorporating age, condylar flattening, condylar head height, and ADDwoR demonstrated moderate discriminatory performance in both training and testing sets. These findings underscore the synergistic value of combining high-resolution osseous assessment with soft tissue evaluation for risk stratification in TMD. The model could be regarded as an adjunctive risk stratification tool to help identify patient who may in need of closer clinical monitoring, more comprehensive imaging evaluation, or early individualised management.

Recent studies have provided useful context for imaging-based TMJ assessment. Maita et al.27 showed that panoramic radiograph may serve as an opportunistic screening tool for TMJ degenerative joint disease, but their sensitivity for detecting osseous findings was insufficient for standalone diagnosis. Osama et al.28 further demonstrated that cone-beam CT (CBCT)-derived morphological features were associated with TMJ disc displacement, supporting the value of imaging for assessing TMJ structural alterations. Building on these findings, the present study integrated U-HRCT-derived osseous parameters with MRI-based disc position, thereby combining high-resolution assessment of subtle condylar morphology with disc abnormalities for LMO risk stratification in TMD patients.

In this study, the OR of LMO in ADDwoR is 2.72, indicating that even after adjusting for age and osseous factors, patients with this condition have over 2.7-fold higher odds of developing LMO compared to those without disc displacement. This robust association confirms previous reports,12,14,15 emphasising the critical role of disc-condyle incoordination in LMO pathogenesis while providing strong quantification of its diagnostic weight within a multimodal context. This finding aligns with established biomechanical principles.29 Disc displacement without reduction fundamentally alters joint kinematics by obstructing condylar translation along the articular eminence during mouth opening, thereby creating a mechanical barrier that physically restricts maximal interincisal distance.30 Clinically, this finding requires high vigilance for impending LMO in ADDwoR patients and early implementation of interventions before irreversible changes occur. Notably, this aligns with the study by Xu et al.,31 which also identified ADDwoR as an independent LMO indicator. However, Xu et al.’s model integrated MRI-derived soft tissue features (abnormal disc signal, abnormal joint space) whereas this study incorporates U-HRCT-based osseous metrics, highlighting the complementary value of osseous and soft tissue assessment.

Except for ADDwoR, condylar flattening and reduced condylar head height were negatively associated with LMO in the present study. One possible explanation is that flattening likely represents an adaptive remodelling response to chronic mechanical loading.32 This morphological adaptation may increase the contact surface area between articulating components, thereby distributing masticatory forces more evenly and preserving functional capacity.33,34 Reduced condylar height likely reflects adaptive remodelling in this context. This anatomical configuration could minimise mechanical resistance from periarticular constraints and optimise the efficiency of rotational movement, thereby preserving functional capacity despite structural changes.35 Besides, from a kinematic perspective, excessive condylar height may predispose to impingement against the articular eminence or posterior discal attachments during translation, particularly when disc displacement is present.36 Alternative explanations should also be considered, including selection bias, differences in disease duration or chronicity, heterogeneous stages of TMJ remodelling, and unmeasured clinical factors such as muscle dysfunction. However, it is crucial to recognise that moderate decreases may represent beneficial adaptation, whereas severe resorption likely constitutes pathological degeneration that would correlate with worse outcomes. Therefore, longitudinal studies are needed to determine whether these osseous features represent adaptive remodelling, compensatory structural changes, or pathological degeneration at different stages of TMD.

In addition to imaging examinations, the positive association between age and LMO risk (OR = 1.03) is consistent with the progressive nature of TMJ degeneration. Advanced age correlates with cumulative biomechanical stress, biochemical changes in cartilage composition, and diminished tissue repair capacity, collectively contributing to joint dysfunction.37, 38, 39, 40 Notably, the magnitude of this effect remained significant even after adjusting for structural parameters, suggesting age-related factors beyond morphological alterations influence mouth opening capacity.41 In addition, although sex was not significantly associated with LMO in the analysis, this finding should be interpreted with caution. The predominance of female patients in this study is consistent with the known epidemiological profile of TMD, with a female proportion of over 65% in clinical TMD cohort.42,43 The relatively small number of male patients may have limited the statistical power to detect sex-related effect. Therefore, the absence of a statistically significant sex effect does not necessarily indicate that sex has no influence on LMO risk.

Muscle-related factors, including lateral pterygoid abnormality, masticatory muscle tenderness, and muscle dysfunction, may contribute to mouth opening.13 Psychological factors, such as anxiety and depression, may influence symptom perception and functional limitation.44 Because these variables were not systematically available in this cohort, they were not included in the present model. The absence may partly explain the moderate discriminative ability of the model and may limit its generalisability, particularly in patients whose LMO is mainly driven by muscular or psychosocial mechanisms.

A diagnostic model was constructed through logistic regression by integrating age, condylar flattening, condylar head height, and ADDwoR. The model showed moderate discriminative performance, with AUCs of 0.764 in the training set and 0.744 in the testing set, and corresponding sensitivity and specificity of 0.750 / 0.724 and 0.643 / 0.698, respectively. The trade-off between false-positive and false-negative diagnosis remains clinically important. In practice, positive diagnosis should prompt closer monitoring and further clinical evaluation, rather than directly guiding treatment decision. Clinicians can estimate individualised LMO probability, facilitating risk communication and shared decision-making. Nevertheless, given its moderate discriminative ability, the model should be interpreted as an adjunctive risk stratification tool, rather than a replacement for comprehensive clinical evaluation. The calibration curves and DCA results suggest potential clinical utility across clinically relevant threshold probabilities. The constructed model supports individualised risk estimation, early identification of high-risk patients, and personalised therapeutic strategy, thereby addressing an unmet need for objective, imaging-based tools to facilitate clinical decision-making in routine LMO risk evaluation.

There were several limitations to this study. First, the retrospective single-centre design may introduce selection bias and limit generalisability. Although the predominance of young female patients reflects the epidemiology of TMD, it may restrict applicability to older or male populations. The reliance on U-HRCT-derived osseous parameters may also limit use in clinical settings without U-HRCT. Second, this study only included patients with TMD, and the findings may not be generalisable to LMO caused by non-TMD conditions. Third, the cross-sectional nature precludes causal inferences regarding the temporal relationship between imaging features and LMO development. Longitudinal studies are needed to determine whether these predictors are causal factors or simply associated disease markers. Fourth, muscle parameters and psychosocial factors were not systematically evaluated, which may have introduced confounding and limited the model’s applicability to patients whose LMO is primarily driven by muscular or psychosocial mechanisms. Future studies should integrate these variables to develop a more comprehensive diagnostic model for LMO. Finally, the fixed MMO < 35 lt; 35 mm cut off may introduce classification bias because normal mouth opening varies according to age, sex, body size, and ethnicity.45,46 Future studies should validate the model using alternative thresholds and demographic-adjusted normative references.

Conclusions

The model based on imaging features showed potential as an adjunctive risk-stratification tool to help identify patients with TMD who require closer monitoring or further evaluation.
