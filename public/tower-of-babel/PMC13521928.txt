Combined impact of diabetes mellitus and substance use on injury risk: a nationwide population-based cohort study in Taiwan
Authors: Chun-Liang Wu, Yao-Ching Huang, Ren-Jei Chung, Shi-Hao Huang, Tsu-Hsuan Weng, Bing‑Long Wang, Chun-Teng Tsai, Chi-Hsiang Chung, Pi-Ching Yu, Chao-Hsi Huang, Wu-Chien Chien
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to examine the independent and combined impacts of diabetes mellitus (DM), and substance use on the risk of accidental injuries in a nationwide population-based cohort in Taiwan. Using Taiwan’s National Health Insurance Research Database (NHIRD), two retrospective cohorts were analyzed. The DM cohort (2000–2013) included 31,424 patients with DM and 94,272 age-, sex-, and index-year-matched controls. The substance uses cohort (2013–2022) included 324,648 substance users and an equal number of matched controls. Injury outcomes were classified as traffic accidents, falls, burns and fires, brain injuries, suicide, and drowning. Cox proportional hazards models were applied to estimate adjusted hazard ratios (aHRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and Kaplan–Meier analyses were used for descriptive visualization of cumulative injury risk, and interaction was formally assessed using Cox regression models. Patients with DM exhibited a 1.702-fold higher risk of injury compared to non-DM individuals, while substance users demonstrated a 1.444-fold higher risk compared to non-users. Among substance users, the highest risks were found for suicide (aHR = 10.287), intentional injury (aHR = 2.453), and falls (aHR = 1.742). Female sex, low income, major illness, and comorbidities (stroke, hypertension, hyperlipidemia) further amplified injury risk. Both DM and substance use were independently associated with increased injury risk. In joint-effects analyses, individuals with both DM and substance use had the highest injury risk among the four exposure groups (non-DM/non-user, DM only, substance use only, and DM + substance use). The multiplicative interaction between DM and substance use was statistically significant. Results were consistent in sensitivity analyses restricted to unintentional injuries. DM and substance use independently elevate the risk of accidental and intentional injuries; their coexistence is associated with the highest injury risk that markedly increases injury susceptibility. Integrating chronic disease management with substance use prevention and behavioral interventions may reduce injury-related morbidity and mortality in high-risk populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-56758-1.

Introduction

Injuries remain a major public health threat worldwide, contributing substantially to mortality and long-term disability across the life course. According to the most recent syntheses of Global Burden of Disease (GBD) estimates, unintentional injuries continue to account for a large share of years lived with disability and health loss globally, underscoring the need to identify modifiable and high-impact risk factors to guide prevention strategies1–3. In Taiwan, official vital statistics consistently list “accidents and unintentional injuries” among the leading causes of death, highlighting a persistent national prevention priority4,5.

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is highly prevalent and rising, with recent global assessments suggesting the burden is considerably higher than earlier estimates and continues to grow, especially in low- and middle-income settings6–8. Beyond its well-known chronic complications, DM has been increasingly implicated in elevated injury susceptibility through multiple mechanisms, including peripheral neuropathy, visual impairment, postural instability, and episodes of hypoglycemia that can acutely impair cognition and psychomotor performance6,9–12. Contemporary cohort evidence indicates that individuals with DM, particularly those using insulin, face higher hospitalization risk for injuries relative to the general population, while clinical practice statements emphasize driving safety and management of hypoglycemia to mitigate crash risk10–12.

A growing literature also links DM to fall risk in older adults. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses report consistently higher odds of falls among people with diabetes-especially those with diabetic peripheral neuropathy (DPN)-and identify balance and gait disturbances, reduced proprioception, and muscle weakness as key proximal pathways9,13–15. These impairments may interact with environmental hazards to increase the likelihood of fractures, head injuries, and other severe outcomes.

In parallel, substance use-including alcohol, illicit drugs, and tobacco—has long been recognized as a driver of both intentional and unintentional injuries via impaired judgment, delayed reaction time, increased risk-taking, and co-occurring mental health conditions. Recent syntheses reaffirm strong associations between substance use and a spectrum of injuries (e.g., suicide and self-harm, violence-related injury, motor vehicle crashes, and falls), with alcohol use contributing prominently to both acute and chronic harms3,16–18.

Therefore, using Taiwan’s National Health Insurance Research Database (NHIRD), we examined the independent and combined effects of diabetes mellitus and substance use on subsequent injury risk.

Therefore, leveraging nationwide claims data from NHIRD, this study investigates the independent and combined impacts of DM and substance use on subsequent injury risk in Taiwan. We hypothesized that (1) DM and substance use each independently increase the risk of injuries after adjustment for sociodemographic and clinical factors; and (2) individuals with concurrent DM and substance use exhibit synergistically higher injury risks than those with either exposure alone, with heterogeneity across injury subtypes (e.g., falls, traffic injuries, intentional injuries). Addressing this gap will inform integrated, cross-disciplinary strategies that align chronic disease management with substance use prevention to reduce injury-related morbidity and mortality.

Materials and methods

Data source

We used Taiwan’s NHIRD, a nationwide claims database derived from the single-payer National Health Insurance (NHI) program that covers > 99% of Taiwan’s ~ 23 million residents since 1995. The NHIRD contains de-identified demographics, outpatient and inpatient encounters, diagnostic/procedure codes, and prescription records19–21. Diagnoses were coded using ICD-9-CM before the national transition and ICD-10-CM thereafter. Access to the NHIRD is regulated; all direct identifiers are encrypted prior to release. The study protocol was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Tri-Service General Hospital (TSGHIRB E202516061) and adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki9,19,22–26.

To address differences in coding validity across eras, we constructed two time-period-specific cohorts. DM was analyzed using the 2000–2013 NHIRD, during which ICD-9-CM coding for chronic metabolic diseases has been extensively validated. In contrast, substance use disorders were primarily analyzed using the 2013–2022 NHIRD after Taiwan’s nationwide transition to ICD-10-CM, which substantially improved diagnostic specificity for alcohol—and drug-related conditions. This design allowed maximization of exposure validity within each coding era while preserving long-term follow-up for injury outcomes.

All injury outcomes and comorbidities were harmonized across coding systems using established ICD-9-CM to ICD-10-CM crosswalks, following published NHIRD methodological guidance. Broad, validated injury categories were applied to minimize differential misclassification during the transition period.

Vital status was ascertained through linkage with Taiwan’s National Death Registry, which is routinely linked to the NHIRD and provides complete and validated mortality information. Death was treated as a censoring event in all time-to-event analyses.

Study design and population

This retrospective population-based cohort study was conducted using data from Taiwan’s NHIRD. Three analytic cohorts were constructed for different analytic purposes: (1) a DM cohort to evaluate the independent effect of DM, (2) a substance-use cohort to evaluate the independent effect of substance use, and (3) an interaction analytic cohort to evaluate the combined and interactive effects of DM and substance use on injury risk. The construction of all cohorts is summarized in Fig. 1. Flowchart of cohort construction and analytic framework for main-effect and interaction analyses

Two time-period cohorts were constructed for distinct analytic purposes. The DM cohort (2000–2013) was used to evaluate the independent association between diabetes mellitus and injury risk while maximizing follow-up duration in the ICD-9-CM era. The substance-use cohort (2013–2022) was defined in the ICD-10-CM era, when substance-use diagnoses were more completely captured, and coding specificity improved. To ensure temporal and coding consistency, interaction and joint-effect analyses of DM and substance use were restricted to a unified analytic cohort from 2013 to 2022.

DM cohort (2000–2013)

The DM cohort was constructed from NHIRD beneficiaries aged ≥ 18 years between January 1, 2000, and December 31, 2013. Individuals with newly diagnosed DM were identified using validated claims-based criteria. The index date was defined as the first qualifying DM diagnosis. Individuals with any injury diagnosis during the 1-year look-back period prior to the index date or with missing demographic information were excluded. Each individual with DM was matched to three non-DM controls (1:3 matching) on age, sex, and index year. The matched DM cohort was followed from the index date until the first incident injury, death, or the end of follow-up, whichever occurred first.

Substance-use cohort (2013–2022)

The substance-use cohort was constructed from NHIRD beneficiaries aged ≥ 18 years between January 1, 2013, and December 31, 2022. Incident substance use was identified using claims-based diagnostic codes. The index date was defined as the first qualifying substance-use diagnosis. Individuals with any injury diagnosis during the 1-year look-back period prior to the index date or with missing demographic data were excluded. Each substance user was matched 1:1 to a non-user on age, sex, and index year. The matched substance-use cohort was followed from the index date until the first incident injury, death, or the end of follow-up, whichever occurred first.

Interaction analytic cohort (2013–2022)

To evaluate the interaction and combined effects of DM and substance use, we constructed a dedicated interaction analytic cohort within a single calendar period to ensure temporal consistency. This cohort was derived from the substance-use matched cohort between 2013 and 2022. All participants were free of injury at baseline.

Baseline DM status was ascertained prior to the index date using claims recorded during the 1-year look-back period. Participants were then classified at baseline into four mutually exclusive exposure groups: (1) neither DM nor substance use, (2) DM only, (3) substance use only, and (4) both DM and substance use. The index date for all four groups was defined as the substance-use index date (or the corresponding matched date for non-users). Participants were followed from the index date until the first incident injury, death, or the end of follow-up, whichever occurred first. This design ensured that all exposure groups shared the same baseline, calendar period, and follow-up window, enabling valid estimation of interaction and combined effects. Follow-up started on the index date (date of first substance-use diagnosis for users; the corresponding matched index date for non-users) and continued until the first incident injury event, death, or 31 December 2022, whichever occurred first.

For the interaction model, we fitted Cox proportional hazards regression including main effects (DM and substance use) and an interaction term (DM × substance use), adjusting for all covariates described in Section "Covariates".

Different calendar periods were used for the DM and substance-use cohorts because the study aimed to address two distinct objectives: estimating independent effects using the longest available follow-up for each exposure and estimating interaction effects within a single unified cohort. Accordingly, interaction analyses were restricted to the 2013–2022 period, during which both exposures could be consistently ascertained and jointly evaluated.

Intentional injuries, including self-harm and assault-related injuries, were included in the primary outcome because they constitute a recognized component of the overall injury burden in population-based injury epidemiology. Global injury surveillance systems and burden-of-disease frameworks routinely classify intentional and unintentional injuries together when estimating total injury risk associated with chronic diseases and behavioral exposures3,36. Excluding intentional injuries could therefore underestimate the total injury burden observed in individuals with DM and substance use.

To evaluate whether the observed associations were driven predominantly by intentional injuries, we conducted a prespecified sensitivity analysis restricted to unintentional injuries only. The consistency of results in this analysis supports the robustness of the main findings.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Baseline characteristics of study participants are summarized in Table 1. Because the DM cohort (2000–2013) and the substance-use cohort (2013–2022) were constructed in different calendar periods using separate matching strategies, baseline comparisons were performed within each matched cohort separately. SMDs were used as the primary measure of covariate balance, with values < 0.1 indicating adequate balance.

In the DM cohort, 31,424 individuals with DM were matched to 94,272 non-DM controls (1:3 matching). Age and sex distributions were well balanced between groups (SMDs < 0.05). Compared with non-DM controls, individuals with DM had a higher prevalence of cardiometabolic comorbidities, including hypertension, hyperlipidemia, coronary artery disease (CAD), heart failure, and stroke, as well as chronic kidney disease (CKD) and catastrophic illness certification. Several comorbidities showed residual imbalance (SMDs > 0.1), reflecting the underlying clinical burden associated with DM rather than incomplete matching.

In the substance-use cohort, 324,648 substance users were matched 1:1 to an equal number of non-users. Age and sex were well balanced (SMDs < 0.02). Substance users were more likely than non-users to have low-income status and a higher prevalence of cardiometabolic diseases, chronic liver disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and psychiatric comorbidities, including depressive, anxiety, and psychotic disorders. Although matching achieved good balance for demographic variables, several clinical and psychiatric covariates remained imbalanced (SMDs > 0.1), supporting the need for multivariable adjustment in subsequent analyses.

No statistical comparisons were made across cohorts, as the DM cohort and the substance-use cohort were derived from different calendar periods and served distinct analytic purposes. All covariates listed in Table 1 were included in the adjusted Cox regression models.

Independent effects of diabetes mellitus and substance use on injury risk

Discussion

Principal findings

Across two nationwide NHIRD cohorts, DM and substance use were each independently associated with an increased risk of subsequent injury. When evaluated jointly within a unified analytic cohort, individuals with both DM and substance use consistently exhibited the highest injury risk among the four exposure groups. These findings indicate that the coexistence of metabolic dysfunction and substance-related behaviors is associated with substantially elevated injury risk, particularly for falls and intentional injuries. Injury risk was also socially patterned, with higher risks concentrated among socioeconomically disadvantaged groups and individuals with greater comorbidity burden, consistent with prior epidemiologic literature39,12,16,27–32,40–42.

Relation to prior work

Our findings regarding DM are consistent with prior evidence demonstrating excess injury hospitalizations among people with diabetes, particularly those treated with insulin. Proposed mechanisms include peripheral neuropathy, visual impairment, balance and gait disturbances, and hypoglycemia-related neurocognitive effects9,12,27,28,37,40. Clinical guidelines emphasize driving safety, structured counseling, and hypoglycemia prevention for individuals at elevated risk27,28,40. Observational and mechanistic studies linking impaired awareness of hypoglycemia to increased motor-vehicle accident risk further support our findings for traffic-related injuries among individuals with DM12,38,40.

For substance use, contemporary global syntheses and surveillance reports consistently demonstrate strong associations with transport injuries, violence, self-harm, and falls. Alcohol and psychoactive drugs impair attention, reaction time, and executive control, while psychiatric comorbidity further increases the risk of intentional injuries. These associations have been observed across diverse populations and are reflected in large-scale surveillance systems, including the U.S. National Survey on Drug Use and Health (NSDUH), as well as meta-analyses of alcohol-attributable injury burden16,29–35,41,42. By evaluating diabetes mellitus and substance use within a single analytic framework, our study extends prior single-exposure research and highlights a population in whom these two common conditions co-occur. Rather than suggesting novel mechanisms, our findings emphasize the importance of recognizing coexisting metabolic and behavioral risk factors when assessing injury susceptibility in routine clinical practice. This population-level perspective complements existing etiologic studies and supports the need for integrated prevention strategies in high-risk individuals9,12,16,27–32,37,40,41,42,43.

Plausible mechanisms

Several mechanisms may underlie the observed associations. First, neurocognitive and sensorimotor impairment may play a central role. Peripheral neuropathy and microvascular or retinal changes in DM reduce proprioception and visual acuity, while psychoactive substances further impair attention and motor coordination, increasing susceptibility to falls and traffic injuries9,16,34. Second, autonomic dysregulation and hypoglycemia may contribute to injury risk. Both DM and alcohol disrupt autonomic balance, and superimposed hypoglycemia can precipitate confusion, loss of consciousness, or delayed reaction time during high-hazard activities such as driving or working at heights12,27,31,38,40,45. Third, mental health comorbidity is common in both DM and substance use disorders and may increase the risk of self-harm and interpersonal violence32,40,46. Finally, social determinants likely amplify risk, as injury burden is disproportionately concentrated among low-income populations exposed to environmental hazards, occupational risks, and limited access to preventive resources 16,29,30,33–35,40,41.

Heterogeneity and equity

Prior epidemiologic and global injury surveillance studies have reported that injury burden is socially and clinically patterned, with higher rates often observed among women in certain injury categories and among individuals with greater multimorbidity or socioeconomic vulnerability3,30,36,40,41.

In the present study, formal sex- or comorbidity-stratified interaction analyses were not conducted. Therefore, we do not interpret our findings as evidence of subgroup-specific effect modification. Future studies with prespecified stratified analyses are warranted to evaluate potential heterogeneity of effects.

Clinical and public-health implications

At the clinical level, integrating substance-use screening and brief intervention into routine diabetes care-alongside hypoglycemia education, vision care, and neuropathy rehabilitation-may help mitigate injury risk. The American Diabetes Association (ADA) Standards of Care (2024) and driving safety statements provide actionable recommendations for counseling, glucose monitoring access, and assessment of driver fitness27,28,40. At the system level, NHIRD-based risk stratification could support targeted outreach, such as automated alerts for dual exposure and referral to addiction services, falls clinics, or mental health care41,42,43. At the policy level, converging global evidence supports population-level interventions, including alcohol warning labels, pricing policies, and outlet density regulation, to reduce alcohol-attributable injuries29,39,44,45,46.

Methodological considerations and robustness

The contribution of this study lies in identifying a readily recognizable high-risk population in routine care rather than in establishing a novel etiologic mechanism. By evaluating diabetes mellitus and substance use within a unified analytic framework and formally testing their interaction, this study provides population-level evidence that individuals with both conditions may warrant integrated injury-prevention strategies.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, because this study used administrative claims data, misclassification of exposures and outcomes is possible, particularly across the transition from ICD-9-CM to ICD-10-CM coding. To mitigate this issue, we applied validated code sets, used published crosswalks, and conducted primary analyses within coding-era–specific cohorts, with interaction analyses restricted to a single ICD-10-CM period. Any residual misclassification is likely to be non-differential and would therefore bias estimates toward the null.

Second, residual confounding cannot be fully excluded. Although matching and multivariable adjustment were applied, information on disease severity, functional status, lifestyle factors, and detailed substance-use patterns was not available. Accordingly, observed associations should be interpreted as non-causal and population-level.

Third, some injury subtypes (e.g., burns, drowning, fire-related injuries) were rare, resulting in limited precision and wider confidence intervals. These estimates should therefore be interpreted cautiously, with emphasis on direction rather than magnitude.

Fourth, analyses were restricted to the first incident injury event, which may underestimate the total burden of recurrent injuries and limit interpretation of subtype-specific findings.

Future research

Future research should prioritize the development and external validation of predictive models for individuals with dual exposure, pragmatic trials of integrated intervention bundles (e.g., glucose optimization, fall prevention, and substance-use brief intervention), and policy evaluations using NHIRD-linked outcomes. Surveillance updates and national vital statistics should be leveraged to monitor equity impacts and temporal trends in injury risk among high-risk populations29,30,32,36. Although individuals with both DM and substance use consistently showed the highest injury risk, subgroup-specific interaction effects were not formally evaluated and should be examined in future studies.

Conclusion

This nationwide population-based study demonstrates that DM and substance use are each independently associated with an increased risk of injury in the Taiwanese population. When evaluated jointly, individuals with both conditions consistently exhibited the highest overall injury risk. These findings highlight the importance of considering metabolic and behavioral risk factors together when assessing injury susceptibility. Future studies should evaluate whether integrated clinical and public health interventions can effectively reduce injury risk in this high-risk population.
