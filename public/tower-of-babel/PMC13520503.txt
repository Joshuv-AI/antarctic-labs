Comparison of CDC and ECDC reverse sequence algorithms for syphilis diagnosis: clinical implications of discordant serological results
Authors: Tuncer Karpuz, Gozde Ongut, Ozlem Koyuncu Ozyurt, Hatice Yazisiz, Mustafa Ata Turkoglu, Rasih Felek, Dilara Ogunc, Derya Mutlu, Levent Donmez, Dilara Inan, Dilek Colak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundReverse sequence algorithms are increasingly used for the serological diagnosis of syphilis in clinical practice; however, differences between the approaches recommended by the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) and the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control (ECDC) may influence test interpretation.MethodsWe performed a retrospective laboratory-based study including 82 serum samples with reactive Treponema pallidum electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (ECLIA) results collected at a tertiary-care university hospital. Samples were evaluated according to CDC and ECDC reverse sequence algorithms using rapid plasma reagin (RPR) and Treponema pallidum hemagglutination assay (TPHA) in the respective testing sequence. Agreement between algorithms was assessed using percentage concordance and Cohen’s κ statistic, and discordant results were further evaluated using available clinical and follow-up data.ResultsDiscordant serological patterns were identified between the two algorithms. Although overall agreement between the algorithms was high (96.3%; κ = 0.709), the wide confidence interval of Cohen’s κ suggested statistical uncertainty, likely related to the limited number of discordant cases. Using the ECDC algorithm, three of 82 (3.7%) ECLIA-reactive samples with reactive RPR but nonreactive TPHA results did not fulfill the serological criteria of the testing sequence at the time of evaluation; follow-up serological findings in two of these cases were considered compatible with recent or early active syphilis.ConclusionsAlthough overall agreement between the algorithms was high, interpretation of discordant serological patterns may vary depending on testing sequence and assay selection. Introduction

Syphilis remains a significant global public health concern, and accurate laboratory diagnosis is critical for effective treatment and prevention of transmission [1]. Because Treponema pallidum (T. pallidum) cannot be routinely cultured in clinical laboratories, the diagnosis of syphilis relies primarily on serological testing. In recent years, reverse sequence screening algorithms that begin with automated treponemal assays have increasingly been adopted in clinical microbiology laboratories due to their suitability for high-throughput workflows [2, 3].

Several reverse sequence screening algorithms have been described in laboratory and clinical guidelines. The algorithm outlined by the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) uses an initial treponemal screening assay followed by a quantitative nontreponemal test to assess disease activity and treatment response, with a second treponemal assay reserved for resolving discordant results. In contrast, the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control (ECDC) algorithm incorporates a second, different treponemal assay as a confirmatory test, while quantitative nontreponemal testing is subsequently used to support clinical interpretation and assessment of disease activity [4, 5]. Although TPPA is commonly recommended in reverse sequence algorithms, TPHA remains widely used in many clinical microbiology laboratories and was the secondary treponemal assay available in our institution during the study period.

Although both algorithms are widely implemented in clinical microbiology laboratories, they differ in testing sequence, interpretation strategy, and laboratory workflow [6, 7]. While comparisons between traditional and reverse screening strategies are well described, direct comparisons between the CDC- and ECDC-recommended reverse sequence algorithms remain limited [8–10]. From a laboratory perspective, differences in test sequence may influence workflow and the interpretation of serological results, particularly in early infection.

In this study, we compared the CDC and ECDC reverse sequence algorithms using routinely collected clinical and laboratory data to evaluate their performance in routine laboratory practice.

Methods

Study design and population

This retrospective laboratory-based study was conducted at the Central Laboratory of Akdeniz University Hospital between December 2020 and July 2021. During the study period, a total of 2344 serum samples were screened for syphilis using an automated treponemal assay, of which 162 yielded reactive results.

In routine clinical practice, clinicians could request treponemal screening using T. pallidum electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (ECLIA) together with additional treponemal testing using T. pallidum hemagglutination assay (TPHA). In the laboratory workflow, quantitative nontreponemal testing with rapid plasma reagin (RPR) was routinely performed for all samples with reactive ECLIA results, whereas TPHA testing was generally recommended for samples with nonreactive RPR results. Only samples with complete serological data—including ECLIA, quantitative RPR, and TPHA—were eligible for inclusion. Laboratory information system records were reviewed to identify ECLIA-reactive samples with available RPR and TPHA results.

Among the included samples, 40 patients already had complete serological data (ECLIA, RPR, and TPHA) obtained from the same serum sample during routine clinical evaluation. For 31 patients, additional TPHA results obtained before or after the study period were available in the hospital information system and were evaluated together with follow-up serological findings. For the remaining 11 patients, TPHA testing was performed retrospectively on stored serum samples to allow standardized comparison of the CDC- and ECDC-recommended reverse algorithms. These samples had been stored at −20 °C in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations before analysis.

Of the 162 reactive samples, 100 had complete serological data. When multiple samples were available from the same patient, only the first available sample from each patient was included to avoid duplication bias. After exclusion of duplicate samples, 82 patients with unique samples were included in the final analysis. The study population comprised outpatients, hospitalized patients, and individuals undergoing routine medical evaluations.

Serological testing and diagnostic algorithms

Treponemal screening was performed using the Elecsys Syphilis electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (ECLIA; Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany). According to the manufacturer’s instructions, results with a cut-off index (COI) ≥1.0 were interpreted as reactive.

Quantitative nontreponemal testing was performed using the rapid plasma reagin (RPR) test (RPR Carbon Antigen Kit, Atlas Medical, UK). TPHA testing was conducted using the Treponema pallidum hemagglutination assay (Plasmatec Laboratory Products Ltd., Lab21 Healthcare Ltd., UK).

The CDC and ECDC reverse sequence algorithms were retrospectively applied using available serological data. In the CDC algorithm, ECLIA-reactive samples were first evaluated with quantitative RPR testing, and TPHA was considered as the second treponemal test in samples with discordant ECLIA and RPR results. In the ECDC algorithm, ECLIA-reactive samples were evaluated with TPHA as the second treponemal assay, while RPR results were subsequently used for clinical interpretation and assessment of disease activity.

For cases with discordant results between algorithms, available clinical records and follow-up serological data were systematically reviewed to aid interpretation.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 23.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Agreement between the CDC and ECDC reverse algorithms was assessed using overall percentage agreement and Cohen’s kappa (κ) coefficient, interpreted as follows: very good (0.81–1.00), good (0.61–0.80), moderate (0.41–0.60), fair (0.21–0.40), and poor (≤0.20) [11].

Ethical approval

Ethical approval was obtained from the Akdeniz University Clinical Research Ethics Committee (22.12.2021, KAEK-927), and the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Approval was granted retrospectively, and only anonymized laboratory and clinical data were analyzed. The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study.

Results

Patient characteristics and indications for testing

Among the 82 patients included in the study, 67 (81.7%) were male and 15 (18.3%) were female, with a mean age of 40.9 years. Of these, 49 (59.8%) were individuals living with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV). Among individuals living with HIV, 44 were receiving antiretroviral therapy, four had been newly diagnosed with HIV at the time the syphilis tests included in this study were requested and had not yet started treatment, and one patient was diagnosed with HIV after the syphilis test results evaluated in this study.

The indication for syphilis testing could be determined for 28 patients, including nine individuals living with HIV. Among individuals living with HIV, annual screening for sexually transmitted infections is routinely performed in the infectious diseases clinic. Among the 28 patients with identifiable testing indications, 22 had a prior history of syphilis and were tested for follow-up. In one HIV-negative patient hospitalized for evaluation of fever and cough, syphilis serology was incidentally found to be positive (RPR 1:32, TPHA 1:2560). In another patient, testing was requested because of clinical suspicion of secondary syphilis. In one case, screening was performed because the patient’s spouse had been hospitalized with neurosyphilis. Another patient had previously received treatment for syphilis concurrently with their spouse who had been diagnosed earlier, and the current tests were requested for follow-up. In two additional patients living with HIV, syphilis testing appeared to be performed during routine infectious diseases follow-up visits. The specific indication for testing could not be determined from the available medical records for the remaining 54 patients.

Comparison of CDC and ECDC algorithms

According to the CDC algorithm, 54 (65.9%) of the ECLIA-reactive samples were RPR-reactive and considered serologically compatible with syphilis. TPHA results were available for all samples, and 51 (94.4%) were TPHA-reactive, while three samples were TPHA-negative.

Among the 28 ECLIA-reactive/RPR-negative samples, 24 (85.7%) showed reactive TPHA results and were considered algorithm-positive according to the CDC algorithm. Overall, TPHA was reactive in 75 (91.5%) of the 82 ECLIA-reactive samples evaluated according to the ECDC algorithm.

In total, 78 (95.1%) and 75 (91.5%) patients were classified as algorithm-positive according to the CDC and ECDC algorithms, respectively (Fig. 1). Agreement between the two algorithms was high (96.3%), with a Cohen’s κ coefficient of 0.709 (95% CI, 0.16–1.00) (Table 1). The wide confidence interval suggested statistical uncertainty related to the limited number of discordant cases.

Four patients showed ECLIA-reactive/RPR-negative/TPHA-negative results. Three were men living with HIV who had a prior diagnosis of syphilis (Patients 1 and 2) or a history of treated neurosyphilis (Patient 3). The fourth patient, a kidney transplant donor candidate, had no clinical evidence of syphilis and a low ECLIA cutoff index (COI, 1.23), suggesting a possible biological false-positive result (Patient 4).

Three additional patients had TPHA-negative but RPR-reactive results. These cases corresponded to the RPR-reactive but TPHA-negative samples observed in the CDC algorithm. One of these patients was a female nephrology patient undergoing pre-transplant evaluation with a low-titer RPR (1:1); due to insufficient clinical data, her infection status could not be determined (Patient 5).

The remaining two patients were a couple living with HIV who had previously tested negative for syphilis during routine screenings. In the samples included in this study, both patients had reactive ECLIA and RPR results, while contemporaneous TPHA results were not available at the time of evaluation. During follow-up, both patients subsequently demonstrated TPHA seroconversion (≥1:2560 titer), accompanied by progressive decline in RPR titers and eventual seroreversion to nonreactive results. Both patients were clinically evaluated and received benzathine penicillin treatment because of reactive syphilis serology. These longitudinal serological and clinical findings were considered compatible with recent or early active syphilis at the time of the initial discordant serological pattern (Patients 6 and 7).

Discussion

This study compared the CDC- and ECDC-recommended reverse syphilis screening algorithms using routinely collected laboratory data from a real-world clinical cohort. Overall, both algorithms demonstrated high diagnostic yield and substantial agreement, consistent with prior large-scale studies reporting near-equivalent performance across different reverse screening strategies [8, 9].

Previous investigations have primarily compared traditional and reverse screening algorithms, showing improved detection of latent and previously treated syphilis, particularly in high-risk populations, but with an increased frequency of discordant serological results requiring careful interpretation [6, 7, 12]. Our findings extend this literature by directly comparing two widely used reverse algorithms and demonstrating how differences in test sequencing and confirmatory strategies may influence the interpretation of discordant serological patterns.

Notably, when applying the ECDC-recommended algorithm, several ECLIA-reactive/RPR-reactive cases did not fulfill the serological criteria based on TPHA results available at the time of evaluation. Follow-up serological testing in two of these cases subsequently demonstrated TPHA seroconversion (≥1:2560 titer), accompanied by progressive decline in RPR titers and eventual seroreversion to nonreactive results. These longitudinal serological findings were considered compatible with recent or evolving syphilis infection. Both patients subsequently underwent clinical evaluation and received benzathine penicillin treatment because of reactive syphilis serology. Although contemporaneous TPHA results were not available for these cases, the observed serological evolution highlights the potential complexity of interpreting discordant reverse-sequence serological patterns. It is possible that differences in assay sensitivity and the timing of antibody detection between treponemal and nontreponemal tests may contribute to such discordant findings [3]. These observations emphasize the importance of cautious interpretation of discordant serological results within reverse sequence algorithms.

Among individuals living with HIV, the implications of these differences were particularly pronounced. Previous work has shown that, in this population, reverse algorithms demonstrate very good agreement, while traditional approaches may miss a substantial number of cases, leading to underestimation of syphilis prevalence [13]. In our cohort, several discordant ECLIA-reactive/RPR-negative/TPHA-negative cases occurred in individuals living with HIV with a prior diagnosis of syphilis, including one with treated neurosyphilis. These findings highlight the complexity of serological interpretation in this population, in whom treponemal responses may be atypical and negative results do not reliably exclude prior infection.

One additional ECLIA-reactive/RPR-negative/TPHA-negative case had no history of syphilis and a low ECLIA COI (1.23), suggesting a possible biological false-positive result. This interpretation is consistent with findings by Lee et al., who reported that an Elecsys Syphilis COI threshold of 3.6 optimally predicted T. pallidum particle agglutination assay (TPPA) COI results, with high sensitivity and specificity [10]. However, several TPHA-negative samples in our cohort showed higher COI values, suggesting that COI thresholds may not be directly applicable across different treponemal assays. These findings emphasize that discordant serological patterns should be interpreted cautiously and in conjunction with clinical information and follow-up testing.

Several studies have documented that treponemal assays such as TPHA and fluorescent treponemal antibody absorption (FTA-ABS) may decline or revert to nonreactive over time in people living with HIV [14, 15]. In contrast, treponemal immunoassays tend to remain reactive more consistently. Park et al. demonstrated that treponemal immunoassays are more likely to remain reactive than FTA-ABS in patients with a history of syphilis [16]. We observed a similar pattern, likely reflecting the high analytical sensitivity of the treponemal immunoassay (ECLIA) used as the initial screening test.

It is also important to consider assay-related factors. In our implementation of the ECDC algorithm, TPHA was used as the second treponemal test, whereas many prior studies employed TPPA. Although both are agglutination-based assays, TPPA has been reported to have slightly higher sensitivity in primary syphilis [3, 17]. This difference in assay performance may partially explain the presence of RPR-reactive but TPHA-negative cases, suggesting that some discordant results may reflect assay sensitivity in early infection rather than limitations of the algorithm itself.

From a laboratory perspective, the ECDC algorithm may reduce unnecessary reflex nontreponemal testing following a reactive treponemal screen [9]. However, earlier incorporation of nontreponemal testing, as recommended in the CDC algorithm, may facilitate more timely recognition of active syphilis, particularly in populations undergoing frequent screening or with ongoing transmission. Although diagnostic efficiency rather than cost-effectiveness was the primary focus of this study, previous analyses suggest that reverse algorithms may not always be cost-effective in individuals living with HIV [18].

This study has several limitations. Its retrospective, single-center design and relatively small sample size may limit generalizability. Incomplete clinical histories and follow-up data for some patients may have contributed to uncertainty in serological interpretation. In addition, the absence of a definitive gold standard for syphilis diagnosis introduces inherent limitations, particularly in cases with discordant serological results. Although overall agreement between the CDC and ECDC algorithms was high, the wide confidence interval of Cohen’s κ indicates statistical uncertainty, likely related to the limited number of discordant cases and small cell counts. Furthermore, contemporaneous second treponemal test results were not available for all discordant cases, which may have influenced interpretation of evolving serological patterns.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the CDC and ECDC reverse sequence algorithms demonstrated high overall agreement in routine laboratory practice; however, interpretation of discordant serological patterns may vary depending on testing sequence and assay selection. Follow-up findings in selected discordant cases highlighted the potential complexity of serological interpretation in evolving infection. These findings suggest that earlier incorporation of nontreponemal testing within reverse sequence algorithms may aid interpretation of discordant serological patterns in routine clinical practice and may be relevant for clinical microbiology laboratories using reverse sequence screening approaches.
