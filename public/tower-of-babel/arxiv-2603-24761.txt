Rafture: Erasure-coded Raft with Post-Dissemination Pruning
Rithwik Kerur, Divyakant Agrawal, Michael K. Reiter, Dahlia Malkhi

Abstract
. Spreading and storing erasure-coded data in distributed systems effectively is challenging in real settings. Practical deployments must contend with unpredictable network latencies, particularly when information dispersal is integrated into consensus protocols, a prominent and latency-sensitive use case. Existing approaches address this challenge through timeout-based dissemination and adaptive communication or storage decisions driven by acknowledgments during dissemination. However, these designs focus almost exclusively on dissemination-time efficiency, complicate recovery with reconstruction procedures that require metadata that can differ per consensus value, and rely on a centralized leader to make storage decisions for all nodes. This paper introduces Rafture , a novel information dispersal algorithm, and its integration in a consensus protocol, that overcomes these limitations. Rafture is the first information dispersal solution to incorporate post-dissemination pruning, allowing systems to adapt storage costs after dissemination completes. It employs a simple, fixed-threshold erasure code while varying distinct fragment assignment along a second dimension. This ensures that reconstruction is always possible from F + 1 F+1 fragments using the same interpolation method and no additional metadata. Rafture further enables nodes to adapt autonomously based on locally observed information, eliminating the need for global coordination. We evaluate Rafture in highly dynamic network settings and show that it simplifies recovery while significantly improving long-term storage consumption under variable network conditions.



1.
Introduction

The challenge of reliably storing and disseminating information in distributed systems is often a trade-off between fault tolerance and resource efficiency. While replication provides high availability, it incurs significant storage and bandwidth overheads. Information dispersal algorithms, pioneered by Rabin’s IDA
(
34
)
, offer a compelling alternative by splitting data into fragments using Erasure Codes (EC), allowing for reconstruction from a subset of fragments. In theory, IDA provides optimal redundancy. In practice, however, deploying it within critical infrastructure such as consensus protocols presents significant operational challenges.

The primary difficulty lies not in the coding theory, as robust EC schemes are well-established
(
23
;
28
;
35
)
, but in handling unpredictable network latencies. We specifically focus on log-replication systems where a leader needs to disseminate a stream of log entries to
N
followers and ensure log consistency and availability against up to
F<N/2
failures.
To tolerate
F
node failures, the leader must wait for all nodes to acknowledge they have received encoded fragments of each entry. The problem is that the leader cannot easily distinguish between a slow node and a crashed node.

To address this challenge, recent advancements such as CRaft
(
39
)
, HRaft
(
18
)
, and FlexRaft
(
40
)
have introduced adaptive dissemination strategies. These protocols adopt different strategies to address a lack of responses from follower nodes.
While these approaches successfully improve dissemination in dynamic network conditions, they suffer from three key limitations. First, if the network experiences temporary transmission delays, they increase data redundancy levels, leaving nodes with redundant data long after network conditions have stabilized. Second, to support adaptive coding, these systems maintain metadata for each log entry and apply different reconstruction methods and parameters per entry. Third, they rely on centralized coordination, requiring the leader to make storage decisions for all nodes, which can become a bottleneck.

In this paper, we introduce
Rafture

, a novel information dispersal algorithm, and its integration in a consensus protocol, that fundamentally rethinks how erasure coding is managed in dynamic networks. Unlike prior works that focus exclusively on the dissemination phase, Rafture is the first solution to incorporate
post-dissemination pruning
.

From previous approaches, Rafture borrows the encoded information dispersal with adaptiveness, while improving storage utilization, simplifying reconstruction, and introducing post-dissemination pruning. Briefly, it works as follows:

A Rafture leader starts the dissemination with a
(F+1,(F+1)\times(N-1))
coding scheme, a much larger number of fragments than the other schemes mentioned before. Under faultless conditions, every node needs to store only one fragment. However, if the leader estimates that
f

\lceil\frac{(F+1)}{(F+1-f)}\rceil

(F+1)
fragments will survive. A precise calculation of the number of fragments to assign per node as a function of
f
is given in Section
4
.

Importantly, Rafture nodes can prune fragments post-dissemination once additional acknowledgments are collected. Therefore, uniquely to Rafture, any storage increase caused by temporary network disruptions is quickly purged.

An additional benefit of post-dissemination pruning is that it opens a continuum of possible leader dissemination strategies. An adaptive leader dissemination strategy is based on the leader estimating the number of failed nodes via both heartbeats and previous responses. A simple strategy is for the leader to adjust the number of fragments it sends each node precisely according to this estimate. This saves communication bandwidth, but if the leader receives insufficient responses, it may incur a retransmission round to send additional fragments.
Rather than using this simple strategy, Rafture avoids retransmissions and achieves faster completion by letting the leader overestimate the number of failures and initially send extra distinct fragments. These extra fragments are pruned immediately when responses are collected. Specific adaptive strategies are discussed in Section
4
. Trading fast turnaround for a temporary hold on extra fragments has significant benefits, particularly in the context of mission-critical data center and cloud systems
(
9
)
.

Finally, the reconstruction algorithm in Rafture notably always recovers every log entry from exactly
F+1
fragments using the same coding parameters, regardless of how the log entry was disseminated. This simplifies system design and avoids the need to keep separate meta-information with each replicated log entry.

In summary, the Rafture architecture decouples the latency-critical dissemination path from the background storage optimization. During dissemination, the system can afford to be slightly over-conservative to ensure low latency in the face of network jitter. Once the temporary disruptions are repaired, Rafture autonomously prunes redundant fragments. Last, reconstruction remains the same for all historical information the system stores.

We evaluate Rafture under highly dynamic network settings and demonstrate significant improvements in both storage and latency. Our results show that Rafture matches the low-latency performance of state-of-the-art protocols like FlexRaft by quickly adapting to communication disruptions. Uniquely, however, Rafture significantly saves long-term storage costs by dynamically reducing redundant storage as network health improves. By optimizing for the critical path first and storage second, Rafture combines low latency with optimized storage with only minimal and temporary storage increase against network disruptions.
The evaluation substantiates our original motivation for creating a robust and efficient information dissemination regime for distributed consensus.

2.1.
Information Dispersal

The information dispersal problem, originally formulated in Rabin’s seminal IDA method
(
34
)
, considers the following challenge: a leader has a message
M
of size
B
that it intends to disseminate among a set of
N
nodes, while providing both data availability and storage efficiency. Specifically, the dispersal must guarantee that despite up to
F<N/2
nodes crashing, the message
M
can be successfully reconstructed from the information stored on nodes that haven’t failed.

Rather than sending each of the
N
nodes a full replica of
M
, a key technique for storage efficiency is employing erasure codes.
An erasure code is parameterized using two values,
(k,m)
: it encodes a message
M
into
k+m
code pieces referred to as
fragments
, such that any
k
fragments suffice to reconstruct
M
.
In the encoded information dispersal approach, the leader encodes
M
via a
(k,m)
erasure code and disseminates fragment(s) to nodes.
With a Reed Solomon code
(
35
)
,
each fragment size is
B/k
, the total storage is
\frac{B}{k}\times(k+m)
, and the
storage blowup
over
B
is
\frac{(k+m)}{k}
.
Other practical codes, such as Luby-Transform
(
23
)
or Fountain codes
(
28
)
have similar blowup characteristics.

In Rabin’s IDA, the parameters
(k,m)
of the erasure code directly mirror the system parameters
N=2F+1
, with
k=F+1,m=F
. With this parameterization, there are
k+m=2F+1=N
fragments, and a leader assigns exactly one fragment to each node. This assignment maintains data availability against up to
F
node failures with
(2F+1)/(F+1)\approx 2
storage blowup. However, it poses operational difficulties against network delays, discussed next.

Given these inherent challenges in asynchrony, a key question arises: how can we effectively integrate encoded information dispersal into consensus protocols while maintaining both storage efficiency and system resilience?

We define the difference between a
responsive
,
unresponsive
, and
failed
node as follows: a responsive node is a node that responds to messages in a timely manner, i.e., before the leader’s timeout expires. An unresponsive node is a node that is still alive, but due to bad network conditions does not respond before the timeout expires. Notably, an unresponsive node can later become responsive if network conditions improve. Lastly, a failed node is a node that has crashed and will not respond to any incoming messages. The difficulty in asynchrony is that a leader cannot distinguish between an unresponsive and failed node as from its perspective they both behave the same.

Several previous approaches attempt to integrate information dispersal into
F
-resilient Raft-consensus.
In particular, CRaft
(
39
)
, HRaft
(
18
)
and FlexRaft
(
40
)
all employ an
(F+1,F)
coding scheme
and differ in how they handle dynamic responsiveness.
More specifically, these three methods work as follows:

CRaft:

In CRaft, a leader starts the replication of every log entry by broadcasting a single encoded fragment to each node. If there are any unresponsive nodes, i.e., if the leader misses even a single acknowledgment,
the leader switches to full-copy replication. Figure
1
(top row) demonstrates such a scenario where CRaft moves from disseminating and storing coded fragments to full replication, following a single node failure. Note that in this case, CRaft requires a second round of retransmission and resorts to storing the entire log entry at each node.
Thus, with even a single unresponsive node, CRaft’s latency doubles and the storage degenerates to full replication with
O(N)
storage blowup.

HRaft:

HRaft starts the same way as CRaft and addresses the abrupt performance degradation of CRaft by reactively replenishing fragments across responsive nodes. Rather than defaulting immediately to full-copy replication,
the HRaft leader replicates fragments assigned to unresponsive nodes on available nodes. More specifically, if the leader misses acknowledgments from
f
nodes, it sends copies of the fragments intended for the
f
missing nodes to
F
responsive nodes. Consequently, some nodes hold
f
extra fragments.
Figure
1
(second row) illustrates HRaft’s execution under the same scenario as CRaft, namely, a single unresponsive node. In reaction to an unresponsive S3, HRaft sends a copy of fragment F3 to
F=2
extra nodes.
Like CRaft, HRaft requires a second round of retransmission to address unresponsiveness. In many scenarios, HRaft improves the storage required compared with CRaft: rather than immediately degrading to full replication, it increases storage gradually, replicating only the fragments for which it misses acknowledgments.
However, if many nodes are unresponsive, the responsive nodes must store all the fragments designated for them. For example, if
N/4
of the nodes are unresponsive, HRaft incurs
O(N)
storage blowup.

FlexRaft:

FlexRaft improves upon both CRaft and HRaft by proactively estimating the number of responsive nodes and adjusting the coding parameters based on this estimate.
The FlexRaft leader uses both heartbeats at a fixed interval and responses to dissemination to form an estimate.
The leader adjusts the erasure code parameters and the assignment of fragments to nodes based on its current estimate.
For example, if the leader estimates that one node may have failed, it adjusts the encoding scheme to
((F+1)-1,F)
. More generally, if the leader estimates that there are
f
failures, it uses a
(F+1-f,F)
encoding scheme.
Accordingly, each fragment size increases from
\frac{B}{(F+1)}
to
\frac{B}{(F+1-f)}
, and only
(F+1-f)
fragments are needed to recover the encoded log entry.
After re-encoding, the leader disperses the slightly larger fragment to responsive nodes.
As shown in Figure
1
(third row), FlexRaft may require a retransmission at the exact time a failure happens. Subsequent rounds do not require retransmissions as they make use of the existing coding parameters until the leader learns that the failure has been repaired. In general FlexRaft only requires retransmission if it receives fewer responses than it estimated using previous heartbeats and responses to dissemination. Although FlexRaft reduces
storage overhead, it ends up with a replicated log in which each entry may use a different coding scheme, complicating meta information maintenance and recovery.

In summary, all the above approaches incur a retransmission round if they encounter unresponsive nodes; they complicate the reconstruction process because different log entry’s may use different encodings; and they do not address post-dissemination storage maintenance. In the rest of the paper, we detail how Rafture overcomes these shortcomings and present an evaluation of its performance.

3.
System Model

Partial synchrony.

We will consider the classical
partial synchrony
model with
F<N/2
benign failures. More specifically, the system consists of
N
nodes interconnected via point-to-point message passing, such that
F<N/2
nodes can crash. We will assume static membership such that once the protocol has started, no nodes will join or leave the system, besides the nodes that fail. Once a node fails, it will not recover and start participating in the protocol again. Furthermore, if a node fails, we make no assumptions about the messages it sent prior to failing. We will assume that links between non-faulty nodes are reliable, and that messages sent between non-faulty nodes eventually arrive. There is an unknown Global Stabilization Time (GST), after which all message transmissions have a known bound.

Performance measures.

With these assumptions as our starting point, we shift our focus to the metrics we aim to optimize. Our primary goal is to enhance asynchronous performance by optimizing for two metrics:
storage cost
and
dispersal latency
.

We define
storage cost
as the aggregate size of storage used across all nodes to persist the fragments associated with a message. For a baseline, full replication where a message of size
B
is sent to all
N
nodes incurs a total storage cost of
O(N\cdot B)
. We also define
per-node storage
as the storage required on a responsive node in the system.

We define
dispersal latency
as the number of network hops between the leader initiating dissemination for a message and the moment the leader is certain that the message has been persisted on a sufficient number of nodes for guaranteed recovery. We focus on this metric to isolate the performance of the information dispersal protocol, explicitly decoupling it from the baseline overhead of the Raft consensus protocol and client round-trip times.

Solution

Below, we will describe adaptive strategies both for fragment assignment during dissemination and for post-dissemination fragment pruning:

(1)

In Section
4.1
, a leader maintains an estimate of the number of responsive nodes in the system. The leader disseminates fragments to all nodes based on this estimate.

(2)

In Section
4.2
, all nodes (leaders and followers) continually update the observed responsive-quorum and autonomously adjust down the number of fragments they store.

In section
4.3
we provide a proof of data availability for our approach and in Section
5

4.1.
Dissemination and Reconstruction

A Rafture leader starts every dissemination attempt by encoding
M
into a
very large
number of fragments using a
(F+1,(N-1)(F+1))
erasure coding scheme: creating
(F+1)\times N
unique fragments such that any
F+1
can reconstruct
M
. The leader employs an adaptive dissemination strategy, where it varies the number of fragments it sends to each node, from 1 to
F+1
.
The Rafture leader uses both heartbeats and previous consensus responses to estimate a responsive-quorum, similar to FlexRaft.
Based on this estimate, the leader determines how many fragments to assign to each node.

For example, in steady state the leader may receive heartbeat responses from all
N
nodes and send only one fragment to each node, similar to IDA. If the leader observes up to heartbeat/response omissions by
F/2
, it may increase the number of fragments per node by one, and send at least two fragments per node. More generally, if the leader estimates the responsive-quorum to be
F+t
, it sends at least
\lceil\frac{(F+1)}{t}\rceil
distinct fragments to each node. If the leader collects responses from
F+t
nodes acknowledging that they received
\lceil\frac{(F+1)}{t}\rceil
fragments each, there will always be
F+1
fragments present in the system to reconstruct the original message
M
. If the leader does not collect
F+t
acknowledgments, then it needs to retransmit additional fragments.

However, uniquely to Rafture, the leader may prevent retransmission almost always if it initially sends each node a slightly higher number of fragments, say
\lceil\frac{(F+1)}{t^{\prime}}\rceil
where
t^{\prime}<t
.
There are two advantages in sending a higher number of fragments initially:

(1)

Lower the risk of retransmission, e.g., zero risk when sending
F+1
fragments per node.

(2)

Faster completion of the dissemination phase, since a leader must wait for a lower number of responses, e.g.,
F+1
if it sends each node
F+1
fragments.

If a higher number of nodes
do
respond, e.g.,
F+t
nodes acknowledge that they store say
\lceil\frac{(F+1)}{t^{\prime}}\rceil
fragments each, then the extra fragments will immediately pruned (see the pruning section below).
Bandwidth is typically abundant, while dispersal latency may be critical; hence, the leader can trade off a slightly higher communication cost at the outset to avoid a latency penalty later.

4.2.
Post Dissemination Pruning

Rafture’s primary departure from prior works is that every node can prune the extra fragments post-dissemination by itself. Pruning does not require coordination or re-coding of information and can be carried autonomously for every node.

(1)

Asynchronous Collection:
After waiting for a certain number of responses in the initial dissemination round, the leader continues to collect responses for
M
in the background.

(2)

Responsive-quorum Updates
As the leader gathers more responses post commit, it updates its estimate of the number of nodes that have stored fragments of
M
. It piggybacks this information in subsequent heartbeats or log entries.

(3)

Dynamic Storage Optimization:
Updates about the responsive-quorum permit nodes to dynamically discard their fragments. For instance, if a node learns that
\frac{3N}{4}
are storing fragments of
M
, it may safely discard all but two of its local fragments. In general, if a node determines that
F+t
nodes are storing fragments, it only needs to retain
\lceil\frac{(F+1)}{t}\rceil
fragments. Since there are no overlaps between the fragments that nodes store, it does not matter which fragments the node drops.

Through a combination of these two approaches, Rafture uniquely adapts to varying network conditions by:

(1)

Only varying the number of fragments assigned per node;

(2)

Allowing different numbers of fragments to be stored by different nodes: so long as
F+1
fragments can be collected in the entire system,
M
will remain available;

(3)

Not requiring re-encoding of
M
;

(4)

Not impacting the recovery computation, which always takes
F+1
fragments and reconstruct
M
in the same manner, oblivious to the assignment of fragments to nodes; and

(5)

Not requiring maintaining meta-information per message
M
;

(6)

Almost always avoiding retransmission rounds.

Beyond quantifiable storage and latency gains, a primary design objective is protocol simplicity. Existing solutions often compromise this goal, suffering from either storage inefficiency or significant metadata overhead. By using a static encoding scheme with uniformly distributed fragments, reconstruction is reduced to a simple
k
-out-of-
N
collection of any
F+1
fragments, eliminating the need to track fragment placement or manage evolving encoding states.

Safety and Liveness

Lemma 0.

For a system of
N
nodes tolerating
F
failures (where
N\geq 2F+1
), sufficient fragments persist to reconstruct a log entry despite
F
failures.

Proof.

Let
Q
be the set of nodes that have stored the fragments, and
N_{\mathit{surv}}=|Q|-F
be the number of nodes in
Q
surviving
F
failures. We first show that Rafture employing leader-based coordination guarantees the total number
S
of surviving fragments satisfies
S\geq F+1
, ensuring reconstructability. Recall the in Rafture, the leader instructs nodes to prune fragments at three levels:

(1)

(
|Q|\geq F+1
):
if a node receives acknowledgements from only
F+1
nodes including itself that are storing the log entry, it stores
F+1
fragments per node.
N_{\mathit{surv}}=|Q|-F\geq(F+1)-F=1
. Even if only one node survives, it retains
F+1
fragments, so
S=1\times(F+1)=F+1
.

(2)

(
|Q|\geq\frac{3}{4}N
):
When a node receives acknowledgments from
\frac{3}{4}N
, storage is pruned to two fragments.
N_{\mathit{surv}}\geq\frac{3}{4}N-F
. The total fragments are
S=2N_{\mathit{surv}}\geq 2(\frac{3}{4}N-F)=\frac{3}{2}N-2F
. Substituting
N
, we get
S\geq\frac{3}{2}(2F+1)-2F=3F+1.5-2F=F+1.5
. Since
S
must be an integer,
S\geq F+1
.

(3)

(
|Q|=N
):
When a node receives acknowledgements from all
N
nodes, storage is pruned to one fragment.
N_{\mathit{surv}}=N-F\geq(2F+1)-F=F+1
. Since each node holds one fragment,
S=1\times(F+1)=F+1
.

We now address the case where nodes individually adjust the number of fragments they keep based on the number of acknowledgments known to them locally.
Denote by
|Q_{\mathit{hi}}|
the highest acknowledgment count known to any node (not necessarily the leader). Therefore, every surviving node stores at least
\lceil(F+1)/(|Q_{\mathit{hi}}|-F)\rceil

N_{\mathit{surv}}\geq(|Q_{\mathit{hi}}|-F)

.

5.
Implementation

We implemented Rafture in C++ building upon the existing FlexRaft implementation
(
1
)
which contains approximately 6,000 lines of code. We implemented Rafture using the ISA-L library
(
17
)
for erasure coding and RCF 3.0
(

for interprocess communication. To facilitate evaluation, we constructed a distributed key-value store backed by RocksDB v7.3.1
(

)
. Architecturally, each node hosts a Rafture consensus module alongside the RocksDB state machine, utilizing a background thread to asynchronously apply committed entries. We assume familiarity with the original Raft consensus algorithm
(

)

LogEntry Modifications:
We extend the
LogEntry
structure to include two new fields:
Threshold 1
and
Threshold 2
. Both are tuples in the form
(\mathit{Term},\mathit{Index})
.
Threshold 1
represents the highest
(\mathit{Term},\mathit{Index})
for which the leader has received responses from
3N/4
nodes. Similarly,
Threshold 2
represents the highest
(\mathit{Term},\mathit{Index})
for which the leader has received responses from all
N
nodes.

Log Replication and Commitment:
In Rafture, log replication follows the standard Raft structure but utilizes erasure coding as described in Section
4
.

Follower Processing and Storage Pruning:
Upon receiving an
AppendEntries
message, the follower node initially persists all associated fragments. To optimize storage usage, it subsequently prunes fragments based on the global resilience indicated by the leader’s
Threshold
values. The pruning strategy maintains a safety invariant: the cluster must always retain sufficient fragments to reconstruct a log entry despite
F<N/2
failures. As shown in Section
4.3
, it is safe for a follower to retain two fragments for all entries indexed until
Threshold 1
, and a single fragment for all entries indexed until
Threshold 2
.

Our implementation utilizes a leader-driven dissemination model, where the leader broadcasts network response information to all followers. These metrics are derived from data gathered via periodic heartbeats and standard message exchanges in Raft. While we focus on this leader-centric approach, alternative mechanisms such as all-to-all peer communication could be employed to allow nodes to independently assess network state. Such decentralized state acquisition would enable nodes to perform local storage optimizations without relying on the leader’s view, a direction we reserve for future work.

6.
Evaluation

We provide two sets of evaluations: one analytical and one experimental. The analytical results provide predicted storage of CRaft, HRaft, FlexRaft, and Rafture against unstable network conditions that can be hard to emulate in live settings. The experimental evaluation compares an implementation of Rafture forked from FlexRaft against FlexRaft itself, thus eliminating any side-effects of different communication libraries or erasure coding implementations. We selected FlexRaft as our baseline because it represents the current state of the art in integrating erasure coding with consensus and offers a robust, open-source codebase. Both sets of evaluations assume a log entry of size 4000 bytes.

Through our evaluation we aim to demonstrate the following claims:
C1
Rafture maintains the same storage utilization as FlexRaft under normal-case network conditions;
C2
Rafture maintains better storage utilization than FlexRaft under network partitions;
C3
Rafture maintains better network latency than all previous approaches under unstable network conditions.

6.1.
Experimental Setup

We conducted our experiments on a high-performance DigitalOcean Droplet equipped with 16 vCPUs and 64GB of RAM. We deployed the cluster within a single physical machine to isolate the storage metrics from external network variance. Since our primary evaluation metric is storage utilization efficiency—which is a function of the consensus state rather than packet latency—colocating the nodes allows us to deterministically reproduce partition scenarios without the noise of non-deterministic network jitter.

6.2.
Storage Benchmarks

Figure
2(a)
depicts storage utilization when two nodes are partitioned from the network for an increasing number of nodes in the cluster. The two nodes are partitioned until 2000 entries and don’t respond to any incoming messages. Once the partition ends the nodes start responding to messages. Under these conditions, Rafture performs as well as or slightly worse than FlexRaft until the partition ends. Once the partition ends, Rafture has better performance as it is able to adapt and reduce the higher storage cost required during the partition. This performance gap stems from how they interpret an unresponsive node: FlexRaft interprets the lack of response as a node crash, allowing it to immediately adjust its encoding strategy. In contrast, Rafture assumes the node is simply unresponsive (asynchrony). Consequently, Rafture continues to operate without modifying its encoding scheme to exclude the missing node, incurring a higher storage overhead to maintain safety assumptions while waiting for the node to rejoin the network.

Appendix A
Raft Consensus

Figure 6
.
Raft Pseudocode

We start by describing the basics of Raft consensus
[
32
]
. At a high level, the main goal of Raft consensus is to ensure a fault
-
tolerant, consistent replication of a log across a cluster of nodes. A Raft cluster consists of N=2F+1 nodes that can tolerate up to F node failures. At any point of execution, each node in a Raft cluster is categorized as a
leader
,
follower
, or
candidate
. In an ideal point of execution, there is one leader and
N-1
followers. The leader is in charge of handling all client requests and replicating them to the followers. From each node’s perspective, there should be only one viable leader at any point in time. A follower is passive: it only responds to incoming Remote Procedure Calls (RPCs) and does not issue any of its own. Candidates are used to elect a new leader if a majority of nodes believe the current leader has failed.

Logical time in Raft is divided into
terms
such that there is at most one leader per term and
indices
such that there can be multiple indices per term. Terms are used to identify obsolete information such as log entries from a prior leader that haven’t been replicated to a majority of nodes. Indices are used to uniquely identify information within a term such that a (Term, Index) tuple can uniquely identify information in the log. If a follower determines that there is no leader, it changes its state to candidate and sends RequestVote RPCs to all other nodes in the system. The candidate will repeat this until it receives votes from a majority of nodes, in which case it will become the leader. If it receives a response from a valid leader, it will revert to a follower. Lastly, if nobody wins the election, the term is incremented and a new election begins.

Upon becoming a leader, the node can start responding to client requests. As it receives requests from clients, the leader compiles all of the requests into a LogEntry that also stores meta information such as the term and index. The leader then appends the entry to its log, increments the current index, and sends AppendEntries RPCs to followers. Upon hearing back from a majority, the leader commits the entry, applies the entry to its state machine, and sends a response back to the client. The leader will then notify other nodes of a commit in subsequent RPCs.

The key component that guarantees Safety in Raft is the log and the rules for electing a leader and adding an entry. For example, a follower will only vote for a candidate if the candidate has a more up-to-date log than it does. Additionally, when the leader sends out AppendEntries, they must include the prior (term, entry) tuple so each follower can verify that its log matches the leader’s. Thus if a follower appends a log entry, it will agree on the log with the leader up to and including that entry.
