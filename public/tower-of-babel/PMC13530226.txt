Classification and cross-site prediction of PM2.5 chemical composition patterns using a machine learning framework
Authors: Hyemin Hwang, Jaeseok Heo, Mu Hyun Jung, So Yeon Kim, Jae Young Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study presents an integrated machine learning framework for classifying PM2.5 composition-based chemical regimes using the air quality management offices (AQMOs) database. By employing self-organizing maps (SOM) and K-means clustering, patterns in PM2.5 composition were visualized by grouping observations with similar chemical characteristics. The identified regimes showed distinct composition-based features: urban and industrialized areas were associated with higher frequencies of industrial- and traffic-related chemical patterns, whereas coastal or island regions showed higher frequencies of sea-salt-related and background-like chemical regimes. Subsequently, the identified chemical regime labels were classified using a Random Forest model, achieving an overall accuracy of 0.921 and a macro F1-score of 0.843. The trained model was further applied to independent measurement sites (OS, HS, and GC) to evaluate cross-site applicability. The proposed framework provides a practical approach for characterizing PM2.5 chemical regimes using multi-site observational data and may serve as a complementary preliminary diagnostic tool for small-scale studies or regions where detailed receptor-model results are not yet available.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-56487-5.Keywords: Self-organizing map (SOM), Random forest, PM2.5, Source characterization, Chemical regimeSubject terms: Environmental sciences, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Fine particulate matter (PM2.5) has a significant impact on human health, including respiratory and cardiovascular diseases and increased mortality rates1–3. The World Health Organization (WHO) reports that approximately 7 million people worldwide die annually from exposure to air pollution4. PM2.5 pollution is particularly severe in South Korea. In particular, high population density areas, such as metropolitan regions and regions with dense industrial facilities, have been found to experience more severe impacts from PM2.55–8. Because PM2.5 is composed of diverse chemical species, its formation pathways, toxicity, and source-related characteristics can vary by chemical composition and emission environment9. Accordingly, chemical composition-based characterization is important for interpreting PM2.5 pollution regimes and supporting air-quality management.

Source characterization and source apportionment of PM2.5 have commonly been conducted using receptor models such as positive matrix factorization (PMF) and chemical mass balance (CMB), while chemical transport models such as community multiscale air quality (CMAQ) have been used to simulate the formation, transport, and contributions of PM2.510,11. Among these, the receptor model is one of the most widely used methods in source apportionment research. However, these methodologies depend on the quality and quantity of the input data11,12. Moreover, receptor-model-based analyses are generally site-specific and often require repeated model construction for each monitoring location, which may limit rapid characterization of PM2.5 composition patterns at new or less-characterized sites13.

In recent years, machine learning techniques have been increasingly used as complementary tools to receptor models and multivariate statistical approaches for identifying nonlinear patterns in environmental datasets. The self-organizing map (SOM) algorithm has been widely used as an unsupervised learning method for exploratory data analysis, pattern recognition, and visualization of complex environmental and atmospheric datasets14–17. Zheng et al.18 and Guo et al.19 used the SOM algorithm to identify heavy metal pollution patterns in soil and then applied the PMF and gradient boosting algorithms to identify the sources of pollution and evaluate the importance of input variables. In addition, Zhang et al.20, Du et al.21, and Xu et al.22 combined PMF-derived source contributions with Random Forest or related machine-learning models to evaluate the impact of various factors on PM2.5 pollution. However, these receptor-model-based contribution analyses are typically performed on a site-specific basis, often requiring independent analyses for each monitoring location23. Moreover, these studies generally aim to quantify source contributions or identify driving factors after source apportionment, whereas fewer studies have focused on predicting composition-based PM2.5 chemical regimes across monitoring sites primarily based on chemical fingerprints. A multi-site machine-learning framework may help screen composition-based PM2.5 regimes at additional monitoring sites, particularly where site-specific receptor-model results are not yet available. Therefore, this study focuses on multi-site classification of PM2.5 chemical composition patterns and evaluates its cross-site applicability using independent field measurements.

This study aimed to systematically classify PM2.5 chemical composition patterns observed at the air quality management offices (AQMOs) in South Korea and to evaluate whether the identified patterns could be applied to independent monitoring datasets. To achieve this, a machine learning framework was developed by combining SOM-based pattern identification, K-means clustering of SOM nodes, and a Random Forest classifier for predicting composition-based chemical regime labels. The proposed framework is intended to provide a complementary screening tool for interpreting PM2.5 chemical regimes at additional monitoring sites where site-specific source-apportionment results are not yet available.

Methods

Station data collection and measurement

This study was conducted based on air quality data collected from ten AQMOs between 2019 and 2023. For the AQMO dataset, hourly measurements included PM2.5 mass concentration and chemical PM2.5 components (SO42−, NO3−, Cl−, Na+, NH4+, K+, Mg2+, Ca2+, OC, EC, Si, S, Ti, V, Cr, Mn, Fe, Ni, Cu, Zn, As, Ba and Pb) provided by the national monitoring network. The raw data collected on an hourly basis were converted into daily averages for analysis. Detailed information on the locations of the AQMOs and the data collection periods are summarized in Table S1.

Furthermore, to evaluate the cross-site applicability of the proposed model, supplementary field measurements were conducted in Osan (OS), Hwaseong (HS), and Gwacheon (GC) in Gyeonggi-do, South Korea from May 2023 to April 2024. Unlike the continuous hourly monitoring, these supplementary samples for chemical characterization were collected on a daily basis. PM2.5 samples were collected using sequential PM2.5 samplers (LAS-16, EMA Engineering, South Korea). Specific coordinates and sampling dates are listed in Table S2. PM2.5 was simultaneously collected onto polytetrafluoroethylene filters and quartz filters. The PM2.5 mass concentrations were determined gravimetrically by measuring the pre- and post-sampling weights of the filters using a robotic weighing system. Following the mass measurement, the chemical components were analyzed. The ionic components (SO42−, NO3−, Cl−, Na+, NH4+, K+, Mg2+, and Ca2+) were determined using ion chromatography (Dionex ICS-3000, Thermo Scientific, USA). A comprehensive suite of elemental components (Si, S, Ti, V, Cr, Mn, Fe, Ni, Cu, Zn, As, Ba and Pb) was analyzed using energy dispersive X-ray fluorescence (Epsilon 4, Malvern Panalytical, UK). Finally, the carbonaceous components, organic carbon (OC) and elemental carbon (EC), were analyzed using a carbon analyzer (Model 5 L, Sunset Laboratory Inc., USA). Prior to statistical analysis, rows containing missing or invalid values (NaN or infinite values) were removed to ensure data quality. No additional outlier removal was performed, because high-concentration values may represent actual pollution episodes.

Self-organizing map (SOM)

The SOM algorithm was used to analyze nonlinear patterns in the collected data. It is an unsupervised learning technique that preserves the topological structure of high-dimensional representation while converting complex multivariate data into low-dimensional data24. SOM has been widely used for exploratory data analysis, pattern recognition, and visualization of high-dimensional environmental datasets17,25–27. The SOM input matrix consisted of 10,486 daily observations and 24 chemical variables. The variables included PM2.5 mass concentrations, ionic components (SO₄²⁻, NO₃⁻, Cl⁻, Na⁺, NH₄⁺, K⁺, Mg²⁺, Ca²⁺), carbonaceous components (OC and EC), and trace elements (Si, S, Ti, V, Cr, Mn, Fe, Ni, Cu, Zn, As, Ba, and Pb). All variables were standardized using z-score normalization prior to SOM training. The SOM was trained using a grid search over multiple hyperparameters. The explored hyperparameter space and the corresponding optimal values selected based on model performance are summarized in Table S3. Model performance was evaluated using quantization error (QE), topographic error (TE), and distribution matching error (DME)26,27. Lower QE, TE, and DME values were interpreted as indicating better representation of the input data distribution and topology preservation. Specifically, QE, TE, and DME were min–max normalized across all tested configurations and transformed into scores as , since lower values indicate better performance. The optimal SOM configuration was then selected based on a combined score, which was calculated as the average of the three transformed scores with equal weighting. Each node of the trained SOM was visualized to identify the spatial and pattern distributions of major pollutants, and a U-matrix was calculated to visually express the distance between nodes.

Subsequently, K-means clustering was applied to the SOM nodes to further group similar patterns, following the common practice of clustering SOM outputs to support the interpretation of map structures28,29. The clustering features included SOM node prototype vectors, node hit counts, U-matrix values, and neighboring-node distance metrics derived from the U-matrix. These clustering features were standardized before K-means clustering. The optimal number of clusters was determined using multiple evaluation metrics, including silhouette score, connectivity, inertia (Elbow method), and the Davies–Bouldin index (Figure S1)29–31. To integrate these metrics into a unified criterion, each metric was normalized and transformed such that higher values indicate better clustering performance. The overall clustering performance was then assessed by averaging the normalized scores with equal weighting, enabling a balanced evaluation of cluster compactness, separation, structural continuity, and model stability. Considering both the quantitative evaluation results and the interpretability of the resulting chemical regimes, the optimal number of clusters was determined to be seven. Each cluster can be interpreted as a distinct chemical regime characterized by the relative dominance of certain pollutants. These cluster labels were used as the target variable for the random forest-based classification model. The SOM algorithm was implemented using the “MiniSom” library, and K-means clustering was performed using the “scikit-learn” library (version 1.5.2) in Python (version 3.12.7).

Random Forest

A Random Forest classifier was developed to predict the chemical regime labels identified by the SOM-K-means framework for additional datasets. Random Forest is an ensemble learning technique based on multiple decision trees which is robust to overfitting32. The input features included the same chemical composition variables used in the SOM analysis, along with spatial variables (longitude and latitude) and a temporal variable (date). The dataset was first divided into training and test sets using an 80:20 stratified split. Hyperparameter tuning was performed on the training set using grid search with 5-fold stratified cross-validation, with the macro-averaged F1-score used as the optimization metric. The explored hyperparameter space and the corresponding optimal values are summarized in Table S4. The final model was then evaluated on the held-out test set. Class imbalance was addressed using class weighting during model training, and model performance was evaluated using accuracy, balanced accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score. To assess cross-site applicability, a leave-one-group-out (LOGO) cross-validation strategy was employed, with monitoring site used as the grouping variable. Because the available data periods differed among AQMOs, LOGO validation was conducted using all valid daily observations available for each site after preprocessing. In this approach, each of the ten AQMO sites was iteratively excluded from the training set and used as an independent test set, while the model was trained on data from the remaining sites. To further evaluate cross-site applicability, the trained model was applied to independent field measurement datasets collected from Osan (OS), Hwaseong (HS), and Gwacheon (GC), which were not included in the model training or validation process. The Random Forest modeling and data processing were implemented in Python (version 3.12.7) using the scikit-learn (version 1.5.2), pandas (version 2.2.3), and numpy (version 1.26.4) libraries.

Results

General characteristics of PM2.5 mass concentration and chemical components

Figure 1 shows the distribution of PM2.5 mass concentration and its components for the AQMO and measurement site data. For most components, the concentration distributions between the AQMO data (South Korean average for 2019–2023) and the measurement data (OS, HS, and GC regions for 2023–2024) showed comparable distribution patterns. However, some differences were observed; SO42−, Na+, K+, Mg2+, OC, and Mn concentrations were higher at the measurement sites, whereas EC concentrations were higher in the AQMO data. This variation in the concentration distribution can be attributed to the temporal and spatial differences between the two datasets. The AQMO dataset consisted of multi-site observations from ten AQMOs across South Korea during 2019–2023, whereas the supplementary measurement data were localized to the OS, HS, and GC regions from 2023 to 2024.

Figure 3 presents the clustering results obtained by applying the k-means algorithm to the SOM output. Seven clusters were identified based on the optimal cluster number determined using multiple evaluation metrics (Figure S1 and S2). Each cell represents the cluster label assigned to the corresponding SOM node (map unit). The spatial arrangement of clusters on the SOM grid reflects the similarity of pollutant compositions, with neighboring cells representing more similar chemical patterns. Clusters that occupy adjacent regions on the SOM indicate gradual transitions in chemical composition, suggesting continuity between related pollution regimes rather than abrupt changes.

The clustering results revealed seven distinct chemical regimes characterized by differences in pollutant composition and concentration levels (Figs. 2 and 3; Table 1). Ryou et al.33 reported that in South Korea, PM2.5 source contributions were significantly attributed to secondary aerosols, motor vehicles, combustion/industry, biomass/field burning, soil dust, and natural sources. The pollution characteristics in each cluster appeared with multiple overlapping factors, and Table 1 summarizes the dominant pollutants and inferred source-related chemical patterns for Clusters 0 to 6 based on these established profiles. Specifically, Cluster 2 is characterized by elevated secondary inorganic aerosols, which consist primarily of ionic substances such as NO3−, SO42−, and NH4+ [34–36, mixed with OC, EC, Zn, and Pb. This suggests possible traffic-related influences that include OC and EC emissions from gasoline and diesel fuel combustion in vehicle exhaust, as well as non-exhaust emission materials (e.g., Zn and Pb) from tires and brake wear37,38. Cluster 0 is closely associated with mixed combustion and biomass/field burning, which is characterized by K+ and Cl− as primary indicators, showing high prevalence in rural areas39,40, along with OC, EC, and Pb. Clusters 1 and 6 both exhibit crustal dust characteristics driven by Mg2+ and Ca2+. However, Cluster 6 suggests possible industrial or combustion-related influence which is characterized by heavy metals, such as Ti, Cr, Mn, and Fe, as its main components41,42. Similarly, Cluster 5 was suggestive of heavy oil combustion, prominently marked by S and V. Cluster 4 was characterized by sea-salt-related components, including Na+, Mg2+, and Cl− [43. Finally, Cluster 3 is categorized as ‘Others’, representing mixed or background-like chemical signals without a singular dominant source signature.

Figure 4 illustrates the spatial distribution of clusters across AQMOs, highlighting the regional variations in PM2.5 chemical regimes. The urban and industrial regions demonstrated distinct cluster profiles reflecting differences in local emission environments and geographical characteristics. Ansan and Ulsan showed relatively distinct cluster distributions associated with industrialized environments. Ansan is notable for its high proportion of Cluster 2, which was characterized by secondary inorganic aerosol markers together with traffic-related components such as OC, EC, and Zn. This pattern is consistent with the presence of the Shihwa and Banwol national industrial complexes and heavy traffic activity in the region7. In contrast, Ulsan showed a high frequency of Cluster 5 (heavy oil combustion), characterized by S and V. This profile is consistent with Ulsan’s large-scale petrochemical complexes and maritime activities in its major industrial port44,45. The distribution of Cluster 4 (marine aerosols) provided significant geographical insights. Baengnyeong Island, located in the northernmost part of the Yellow Sea, showed a high proportion of Cluster 4 consistent with its coastal location and exposure to sea spray46. In Seosan, however, this marine signal appeared to be relatively masked by the high prevalence of Cluster 0 (mixed combustion), possibly reflecting the influence of nearby petrochemical facilities and agricultural activities47. In major metropolitan areas such as Seoul, Gwangju, and Daejeon, Clusters 0 and 3 were generally dominant, suggesting mixed combustion-related and background-like chemical characteristics48,49. Jeju Island was characterized by the highest proportion of Cluster 3 (Others/Background). This suggests that Jeju samples were frequently associated with background-like chemical characteristics or low dominance of specific source markers. This distribution suggests that while inland cities share common urban signatures, regional topography and industrial infrastructure may contribute to localized differences in PM2.5 chemical regimes across South Korea50.

Random Forest classification and regional cluster predictions

Table 2 presents the results of classifying the seven clusters using the random forest model. The model’s performance was summarized using the precision, recall, F1-score, and support values for each cluster, along with overall evaluation metrics, such as the macro average. The model achieved an overall accuracy of 0.921 and a macro-average F1-score of 0.843. Among the individual regimes, Cluster 3, which had the largest sample size, demonstrated the highest F1-score (0.965), indicating stable classification performance. Conversely, Clusters 5 and 6 yielded relatively lower F1-scores (0.750), which was primarily attributed to their small sample sizes and the resulting class imbalance. The confusion matrix results (Table S5) showed that most samples were correctly classified, with misclassifications mainly limited to clusters with partially overlapping chemical characteristics, particularly Cluster 0–Cluster 3 and Cluster 0–Cluster 2. This suggests that mixed-combustion-related regimes were occasionally difficult to distinguish from background-like or secondary-aerosol-related regimes. In contrast, Cluster 3 showed relatively stable classification performance with minimal confusion, likely due to its larger sample size and broader background-like characteristics. Furthermore, the Leave-One-Group-Out (LOGO) cross-validation results, summarized in Table S6, provided additional evidence for the model’s cross-site applicability. When each AQMO site was excluded from training and used as an independent test site, the model achieved a mean accuracy of 0.899. Regionally, the model showed relatively high performance in areas with distinct background-like or relatively uniform profiles, such as Chuncheon and Jeju. Conversely, classification performance was relatively lower in industrial cities with highly complex and mixed emission environments, such as Ansan and Ulsan.

Figure 5 shows the application of the trained model to the independent datasets from OS, HS, and GC to predict their chemical regime labels. For OS, Cluster 3 was the most frequently predicted regime at 42.5%, followed by Cluster 0 and Cluster 4 (both at 26.4%), and Cluster 2 at 4.6%. Similarly, GC exhibited a comparable pattern dominated by Cluster 3 (40.4%), followed by Cluster 4 (31.3%), Cluster 0 (22.2%), and Cluster 2 (6.1%). In contrast, HS showed a distinct distribution. While Clusters 4 (34.3%), 3 (28.8%), and 0 (23.3%) were also prevalent, HS exhibited a notably higher proportion of Cluster 2 (13.7%) compared to OS and GC. OS and GC exhibited a tendency for the background cluster to appear more frequently, whereas the elevated representation of Cluster 2 in HS suggests a relatively greater influence of chemical patterns associated with secondary inorganic aerosols and mixed vehicle/industrial emissions. This finding is consistent with the proximity of industrial complexes near the HS monitoring station51, suggesting that the model can capture source-related chemical patterns in independent datasets.

Discussion

This study presents an integrated machine-learning framework for identifying PM2.5 composition-based chemical regimes as a complementary screening approach to conventional receptor models. By combining Self-Organizing Maps (SOM), K-means clustering, and a Random Forest (RF) classifier, the proposed framework provides a data-driven approach to classify pollution patterns based on their chemical fingerprints. While Choi et al.52 employed k-means clustering to classify temporal pollution patterns within a single urban center, this study differs in that it applies a model trained on multi-site AQMO data to evaluate cross-site applicability using Leave-One-Group-Out cross-validation. Furthermore, application of the trained model to independent field measurements from OS, HS, and GC suggests that the proposed framework may serve as a cost-effective preliminary diagnostic tool for sites where detailed receptor-model results are not yet available.

The results indicated localized differences in PM2.5 chemical regimes across South Korea, which were associated with regional geographical and industrial characteristics. Industrialized and urbanized areas, such as Ansan and Hwaseong, showed relatively higher contributions of Cluster 2, characterized by secondary inorganic aerosol markers and traffic-related components. This pattern is consistent with the presence of industrial complexes, high population density, and heavy traffic activity in these regions53,54. In addition, Ulsan, which has large petrochemical complexes and port facilities, showed a relatively high frequency of Cluster 5, characterized by S and V, consistent with heavy-oil-combustion-related signatures reported in previous studies55,56. In contrast, coastal areas showed different chemical characteristics. Baengnyeong Island showed a high proportion of Cluster 4, consistent with the influence of sea-salt-related components and possible marine aerosol transport46. Jeju Island was frequently associated with Cluster 3, suggesting background-like chemical characteristics or low dominance of specific source markers.

Although the proposed framework provides useful diagnostic information on PM2.5 chemical regimes, it does not derive quantitative mass contributions typically obtained from receptor models such as PMF. Therefore, this machine learning approach should be viewed as a complementary screening tool that helps identify sites or periods requiring detailed quantitative analysis. The proposed framework has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. First, because the framework is based on unsupervised clustering of chemical composition data, the identified clusters should be interpreted as data-driven chemical regimes rather than definitive source apportionment factors. Second, independent receptor-model-based source apportionment results were not available for OS, HS, and GC; therefore, the application to these sites should be regarded as a qualitative assessment of cross-site applicability rather than definitive external validation. Third, meteorological drivers, atmospheric transport, and chemical transformation processes were not explicitly incorporated into the current framework, which may influence the interpretation of some regimes, particularly marine-related or secondary-aerosol-related patterns. Fourth, because the model was trained using site-specific observational datasets from AQMOs, some degree of site-dependent bias may remain in the learned chemical patterns. Finally, because the model was developed using monitoring data from South Korea, caution is required when applying these chemical regimes to regions with substantially different climatic conditions, emission structures, or atmospheric environments. Future research should focus on developing hybrid models that integrate meteorological variables and trajectory analysis to account for long-range transport, thereby improving the interpretation of chemical regimes. Additionally, expanding the training dataset to include seasonal variations and meteorological conditions would further enhance the model’s predictive accuracy and cross-site applicability.

Conclusions

This study presents an integrated machine-learning framework utilizing the multi-site AQMO database to support the classification of PM2.5 chemical regimes. The SOM algorithm and the Random Forest model were integrated to systematically identify composition-based chemical regimes in South Korea. In particular, this methodology showed potential cross-site applicability, serving as a practical diagnostic tool for independent monitoring sites or areas with limited data. However, this study focused on qualitative pattern recognition and did not estimate quantitative mass contributions or explicitly incorporate meteorological conditions and long-range transport processes. To address these limitations, future studies should develop hybrid models that integrate meteorological factors and trajectory analysis to improve the interpretation of chemical regimes.
