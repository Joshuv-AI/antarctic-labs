Boltzmann-Like Occupation of Nonequilibrium Steady States on Dense Networks
Jacob Calvert

Abstract
A central problem in statistical physics is to extend the Boltzmann distribution to nonequilibrium steady states (NESS). We prove that NESS on large dense networks have Boltzmann-like occupation despite extensive entropy production. We further show that the active-matter heuristic of “low rattling” is asymptotically exact. Intuitively, these NESS spend a greater fraction of their time in states they leave more slowly. This explanation extends to the broader class of “equiaccessible” steady states, which play a role in our analysis akin to that of equilibrium in linear response.

This Letter extends the Boltzmann distribution to NESS on large dense networks. Our results imply that the steady-state distributions of such NESS can be inferred through local measurements made away from stationarity. They further establish that the active-matter heuristic of “low rattling”

denotes covariance with respect to the latter. According to the fluctuation–dissipation theorem (FDT), equilibrium steady states satisfy
\partial_{\eta}\langle\bm{O}\rangle_{\mathrm{eq}}=\beta\Cov_{\mathrm{eq}}(\bm{O},\bm{V})
. Equation (
28
) therefore predicts that experimental verification of the value of the static FDT does not suffice to conclude that a steady state is in thermal equilibrium, strengthening a result of
Owen et al. 2020
.

Discussion—
We showed that, despite being far from equilibrium, NESS on large dense networks have Boltzmann-like occupation (
Fig.

1
). We proved this as a consequence of the exit rates
w_{i}
concentrating around their expected values and the stationary probabilities
\widehat{\pi}_{i}
of the embedded process becoming increasingly uniform. The latter motivated the definition of equiaccessible steady states as those with uniform
\widehat{\bm{\pi}}
, whose occupation is therefore determined by rattling
\mathcal{R}_{i}
. We then used these observations to evaluate existing claims and make new predictions about NESS. First, we established that the active-matter heuristic of low rattling is asymptotically exact for NESS on dense networks
Chvykov et al. 2021
;
Calvert and Randall 2024
(
Fig.

2
). Second, we argued that such NESS cannot satisfy a principle of near-minimal EP, as was recently conjectured
Ray and Boyd 2025
(
Fig.

3
a). Third, we derived a nonequilibrium FDT, which implies that static response to energy perturbations is insufficient to determine whether a steady state is in thermal equilibrium.

Although we have emphasized what our results mean for steady states in general, one can use the ideas of this Letter to establish many other interesting properties of NESS on dense networks. For example, one can use the elements of the full proof of
Eq.

6
to show that the response of their occupation to perturbations of transition rates localizes. As a consequence, they asymptotically saturate the fundamental sensitivity bounds of Aslyamov and Esposito
(
Aslyamov and Esposito 2024
, Eq. 16)
, which were previously known to be saturated only in the absence of current. One can further use
Eq.

6
to specialize the Hatano–Sasa equality
Hatano and Sasa 2001
to versions of the nonequilibrium work relation and Clausius inequality
Jarzynski 1997a
;
Jarzynski 1997b
;
Crooks 1998
, much in the same way that we obtained
Eq.

27
. Trepagnier et al. raised the possibility of a nonequilibrium analogue of the Boltzmann distribution for precisely this reason
Trepagnier et al. 2004
.

The virtue of our results about NESS on large dense networks, and equiaccessible steady states more broadly, is that they relate simple properties of states to global occupation. We focused on the “forward” direction of this relationship, which entails explaining occupation, as we did for the principle of low rattling. Future studies could explore the reverse direction, which entails designing or modifying dynamics to produce desirable occupation. This direction is inspired by the analogous use of the Boltzmann distribution to “program” the collective behavior of robots
Li et al. 2021
and microparticles
Yang et al. 2022
through their individual properties, and parallels recent work on frenetic steering
Lefebvre and Maes 2023
;
Lefebvre and Maes 2024
.

Proof of main result
—The preceding proof of
Eq.

6
made additional assumptions to prove that

\max_{i}|N\pi_{i}-1|\to 0,

(A1)

as
N\to\infty
. The proof was otherwise complete. We now establish
Eq.

A1
in full—that is, assuming only that the
(X_{ij},X_{ji})
are independent copies of
(X,X^{\prime})
, which has the same distribution as
(X^{\prime},X)
, and that
\Var(X)\in(0,\infty)
.

The key idea is to analyze the three-jump transition probability matrix
\bm{P}=\widehat{\bm{W}}^{\,3}
. We will show that there is a constant
\alpha\in(0,1)
such that, for all sufficiently large
N
,
\bm{P}
satisfies

\min_{i,j}P_{ij}\geq\frac{\alpha}{N},

(A2)

and that the row sums of
\bm{P}
satisfy

\delta=\max_{i}\left|\sum_{j}P_{ij}-1\right|\to 0.

(A3)

The following argument explains why these two facts suffice to prove
Eq.

A1
.

Due to
Eq.

A2
, we can express
\bm{P}
in terms of the uniform matrix
\bm{U}=\bm{1}\bm{1}^{\top}/N
and a second column-stochastic matrix
\bm{K}
as

\bm{P}=\alpha\bm{U}+(1-\alpha)\bm{K}.

(A4)

Using the fact that
\widehat{\bm{\pi}}
is also stationary for
\bm{P}
, as well as the fact that
\bm{U}\bm{p}=\bm{u}
for any probability (column) vector
\bm{p}
, it is easy to verify the identity

(\bm{I}-(1-\alpha)\bm{K})(\widehat{\bm{\pi}}-\bm{u})=\bm{P}\bm{u}-\bm{u}.

(A5)

The spectral radius of
(1-\alpha)\bm{K}
is bounded above by its maximum row sum, which by
Eq.

A3
is at most
1-\alpha+\delta
. Since
\delta<\alpha
for all sufficiently large
N
, the Neumann series
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}((1-\alpha)\bm{K})^{n}
converges for such
N
, and multiplying it on the left of
Eq.

A5
yields

\widehat{\bm{\pi}}-\bm{u}=\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}((1-\alpha)\bm{K})^{n}\right)(\bm{P}\bm{u}-\bm{u}).

(A6)

For any
N\times N
nonnegative matrix
\bm{M}
and
N\times 1
column vector
\bm{v}
, we have the bound

\|\bm{M}\bm{v}\|_{\infty}\leq\|\bm{v}\|_{\infty}\max_{i}\sum_{j}M_{ij},

(A7)

where
\|\bm{v}\|_{\infty}=\max_{i}|v_{i}|
. In particular,

\|(1-\alpha)\bm{K}\bm{v}\|_{\infty}\leq\|\bm{v}\|_{\infty}(1-\alpha+\delta).

(A8)

By iteratively applying this bound to
Eq.

A6
, we find that

\|\widehat{\bm{\pi}}-\bm{u}\|_{\infty}\leq\|\bm{P}\bm{u}-\bm{u}\|_{\infty}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(1-\alpha+\delta)^{n}\leq\frac{\|\bm{P}\bm{u}-\bm{u}\|_{\infty}}{\alpha-\delta}.

(A9)

Equation (
A1
) then follows from
Eq.

A3
:

N\|\widehat{\bm{\pi}}-\bm{u}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{N\|\bm{P}\bm{u}-\bm{u}\|_{\infty}}{\alpha-\delta}\leq\frac{\delta}{\alpha-\delta}\to 0.

(A10)

To complete the proof, it remains to explain why
\bm{P}
satisfies
Eqs.

A2
and
A3
.

First, the column sum estimate in
Eq.

13
implies that

P_{il}=\sum_{j,k}\frac{X_{ij}}{C_{j}}\frac{X_{jk}}{C_{k}}\frac{X_{kl}}{C_{l}}\geq\frac{\sum_{j,k}X_{ij}X_{jk}X_{kl}}{\mu^{3}N^{3}(1+o(1))}.

(A11)

Since
\Var(X)
is strictly positive, there exist positive constants
x
and
p
such that
\mathbb{P}(X\geq x)\geq p
. Because
X_{ij}
and
X_{kl}
are independent whenever
(k,l)\notin\{(i,j),(j,i)\}
, a standard counting argument shows that, for all sufficiently large
N
, for every
i
and
l
, there are at least
p^{3}N^{2}/2
directed paths from
i
to
l
along which every edge has a weight of at least
x
. In particular, the sum on the right-hand side of
Eq.

A11
satisfies

\sum_{j,k}X_{ij}X_{jk}X_{kl}\geq\frac{p^{3}N^{2}}{2}x^{3},

(A12)

hence
Eq.

A2
holds with, e.g.,
\alpha=p^{3}x^{3}/4\mu^{3}
.

Second, note that the argument leading to
Eq.

13
also shows that the row sums
R_{i}=\sum_{j}X_{ij}
of
\bm{X}
uniformly satisfy

R_{i}=\mu N(1+o(1)).

(A13)

The estimates of
C_{i}
and
R_{i}
together imply that

\sum_{j}\widehat{W}_{ij}=\sum_{j}\frac{X_{ij}}{C_{j}}=\frac{R_{i}}{\mu N(1+o(1))}=1+o(1).

(A14)

In other words, the vector
\bm{\varepsilon}=\widehat{\bm{W}}\bm{1}-\bm{1}
of row sum excesses has
\|\bm{\varepsilon}\|_{\infty}=o(1)
. We apply this fact to

\bm{P}\bm{1}-\bm{1}=\bm{\varepsilon}+\widehat{\bm{W}}\bm{\varepsilon}+\widehat{\bm{W}}^{2}\bm{\varepsilon}.

(A15)

Combining
Eqs.

A7
and
A15
, we find that

\delta=\|\bm{P}\bm{1}-\bm{1}\|_{\infty}\leq\left(1+(1+\|\bm{\varepsilon}\|_{\infty})+(1+\|\bm{\varepsilon}\|_{\infty})^{2}\right)\|\bm{\varepsilon}\|_{\infty}.

(A16)

This establishes
Eq.

A3
because
\|\bm{\varepsilon}\|_{\infty}=o(1)
, completing the proof of
Eq.

6
. ∎

\bm{P}=\widehat{\bm{W}}^{\,3}

\widehat{\bm{W}}

\widehat{\bm{W}}^{2}

Entropy production formulas
—First, we prove that
\sigma_{\mathrm{NESS}}=\sigma(\bm{\pi}^{\mathrm{eq}})(1+o(1))
, which we used to obtain
Eq.

21
. We start by expanding the logarithm in
\sigma_{\mathrm{NESS}}
:

\sigma_{\mathrm{NESS}}=\sum_{i\neq j}W_{ij}\pi_{j}\left(\log\frac{X_{ij}}{X_{ji}}+(E_{j}-E_{i})+\log\frac{\pi_{j}}{\pi_{i}}\right).

(B1)

Note that, for any real-valued function
\bm{f}
on the states, the stationarity of
\bm{\pi}
implies that

\sum_{i\neq j}W_{ij}\pi_{j}(f_{j}-f_{i})=0.

(B2)

We apply this fact with
f_{j}=E_{j}
and
f_{j}=\log\pi_{j}
to find that

\sigma_{\mathrm{NESS}}=\sum_{i\neq j}W_{ij}\pi_{j}\log\frac{X_{ij}}{X_{ji}}.

(B3)

Note that the probability fluxes under
\bm{\pi}
and
\bm{\pi}^{\mathrm{eq}}
differ by
W_{ij}(\pi_{j}-\pi_{j}^{\mathrm{eq}})=X_{ij}\delta_{j}/Z
, where
\delta_{j}=\pi_{j}/\pi_{j}^{\mathrm{eq}}-1
is the relative error in
Eq.

6
. The absolute difference in their EPs is then

|\sigma_{\mathrm{NESS}}-\sigma(\bm{\pi}^{\mathrm{eq}})|\leq\|\bm{\delta}\|_{\infty}\frac{1}{Z}\sum_{i\neq j}X_{ij}\,|\log(X_{ij}/X_{ji})|.

(B4)

As in
Eq.

20
, the standard SLLN implies that the sum on the right-hand side equals
\tilde{a}N^{2}(1+o(1))/Z
, where
\tilde{a}=\mathbb{E}[X|\log(X/X^{\prime})|]\in(0,\infty)
. Consequently,
\sigma_{\mathrm{NESS}}
satisfies

\sigma_{\mathrm{NESS}}=\sigma(\bm{\pi}^{\mathrm{eq}})\left(1+\|\bm{\delta}\|_{\infty}\frac{\tilde{a}}{a}(1+o(1))\right),

(B5)

which verifies the claim because
\|\bm{\delta}\|_{\infty}=o(1)
by
Eq.

6
.

We now derive
Eq.

22
. We start by substituting
W_{ij}=e^{E_{j}}X_{ij}
into the definition of the EP from

8
. Expanding the logarithm, we find that

(B6)

where

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\sigma(\bm{p})]

\displaystyle=a\sum_{i\neq j}r_{j}+\mu\sum_{i\neq j}r_{j}\log\frac{r_{j}}{r_{i}}

(B7)

\displaystyle=a(N-1)S(\bm{q})+\mu S(\bm{q})\sum_{i\neq j}q_{j}\log\frac{q_{j}}{q_{i}},

(B8)

in terms of the related probability distribution

with normalization constant

22

Derivation of nonequilibrium FDT
—We now derive
Eq.

28

27

to a perturbation of the vertex parameters through

\partial_{\eta}\langle\bm{O}\rangle_{\bm{\pi}}=\sum_{k}O_{k}\partial_{\eta}\pi_{k}=\sum_{k}O_{k}\sum_{i}\partial_{E_{i}}\pi_{k}\,d_{\eta}E_{i}.

(C1)

Applying

27

\partial_{E_{i}}\pi_{k}

\sum_{i}\partial_{E_{i}}\pi_{k}\,d_{\eta}E_{i}=-\beta\pi^{\mathrm{eq}}_{k}(\langle\bm{V}\rangle_{\mathrm{eq}}-V_{k})(1+o(1)).

(C2)

Substituting this into
Eq.

C1

28

\displaystyle\partial_{\eta}\langle\bm{O}\rangle_{\bm{\pi}}

\displaystyle=-\beta\sum_{k}O_{k}\pi^{\mathrm{eq}}_{k}(\langle\bm{V}\rangle_{\mathrm{eq}}-V_{k})(1+o(1))

(C3)

\displaystyle=\beta\Cov_{\mathrm{eq}}(\bm{O},\bm{V})(1+o(1)).

(C4)
