Diet-Dependent Variations in the Gut Microbiota and Metabolic Pathways of the land slug Deroceras laeve
Authors: Sergio Cornelio-Martínez, María Daniela Frade-Pérez, María Laura González-Dávalos, Alfredo Varela-Echavarría, Ofelia Mora-Izaguirre
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe slug Deroceras laeve has emerged as a valuable model for biological research, but limited knowledge exists of the microbiota that inhabit the digestive tract of this species. This study assessed the bacterial microbiota of the stomach and intestine of D. laeve fed either a diet formulated for rodents (RD) or a diet of fresh vegetables (VD). Pseudomonadota was the most abundant phylum in both digestive compartments, although it decreased in abundance with the VD diet. The genus Rahnella was the most abundant in both regions with a decrease caused by the VD diet and a concomitant increase in richness. Predicted metabolic pathways indicated that fatty acid biosynthesis predominated in the stomach of slugs fed the RD, whereas pyruvate fermentation and amino acid biosynthesis were enriched in VD animals. In the intestine, aerobic respiration, pyruvate fermentation, fatty acid and amino acid biosynthesis were identified as conserved pathways. Predictive functional profiling revealed that signaling and cellular processes, genetic information processing, and metabolism were predominant functions across all groups. These results reveal a more diverse microbiome in slugs fed VD, paralleling the findings in other animals and providing a good grounding for the study of ecological adaptations of this species to its environment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00248-026-02811-5.Keywords: Deroceras laeve, Diet, Microbiota, Metabolic pathways, Stomach, Intestine

Introduction

The phylum Mollusca is the second largest among metazoans, and gastropods are the most diverse class, comprising over 62,000 species worldwide. Gastropods live in marine, freshwater, and terrestrial environments. They contribute to nutrient cycling, soil formation, and decomposition of organic matter [1]. In recent years, they have gained attention as model organisms for studies of physiological, cellular, and molecular processes [2–4]. In our laboratory, the terrestrial slug Deroceras laeve has been used as a model organism for diverse studies, especially regeneration and transcriptomic analysis [5, 6]. D. laeve is a valuable model due to its anatomical complexity, high reproductive capacity, resistance to starvation, and strong regeneration ability [5]. Understanding its environment and basic physiological processes is important for accurately interpreting experimental responses and regenerative mechanisms.

It is well established that the digestive system microbiota plays essential roles in physiological processes of the host, including those of gastropods. These functions include growth and development through the digestion of ingested food, pathogen resistance, immune modulation, maintenance of the internal homeostasis, and regulation of behavior [7–9]. In recent years, it has been observed that microbiota modulates nutritional outcomes and, reciprocally, diet affects the composition and function of the microbiota. Moreover, recent studies on the gut microbiome of gastropods have revealed that it performs roles that are analogous to those observed in mammals [7, 10]. Understanding the composition and functional roles of the gut microbiota in D. laeve is essential for a deeper understanding of its biology. Furthermore, determining the changes in the gut microbiota composition induced by laboratory diet is crucial to elucidate the host-microbiome interactions and their influence on the host physiology. In the present study we investigated the composition and diversity of the bacterial component of the microbiota of the digestive tract of D. laeve and assessed its functional potential and changes induced by the standard laboratory diet compared to a plant-based diet.

Experimental Procedures

Animals

Specimens of D. laeve of the UNAM-INB strain [5] were kept at 18 °C ± 2 °C in an incubator under a 12 h–12 h light-dark cycle in plastic containers with plant soil. Animals were divided into two containers, each containing 45 slugs. One group was fed ground Laboratory Rodent Diet 5001 (LabDiet) (23% protein, 4.5% fat, and 6% fiber) (RD) and the other a fresh vegetable diet (VD) consisting of equal proportions of carrot, grass, chard, and lettuce washed with distilled water, cut into small pieces, and provided weekly for three months. Thereafter, animals from each dietary treatment were divided into three groups, each consisting of a pool of fifteen individuals from which the stomach and the intestine were obtained. Hence, for each dietary condition and digestive tract region, we obtained three samples composed each of a pool of 15 individuals (n = 3). The NOM-062-ZOO-1999 which contains the regulations for the use of laboratory animals of the Mexican government and the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of the National Institutes of Health (U.S.A.) do not explicitly regulate research with the species used in this study and, hence, no animal research ethics committee approval was necessary. However, the work was performed keeping the stress and the number of animals to the minimum as necessary.

Isolation of the Contents of the Stomach and Intestine

Slugs were completely immobilized by anesthesia in 5% ethanol for one hour and then sacrificed. The stomach and intestine were obtained from slugs fed with diet formulated for rodents (SRD and IRD) or a diet of fresh vegetables (SVD and IVD) using micro-scissors made in-house by modifying Dumont No. 5 tweezers. Stomach and intestine samples were paired as both tissues were isolated from the same pools of 15 animals each. Thereafter, the stomach and intestine were sliced open and rinsed in sterile PBS (Sigma-Aldrich, Saint Louis, MO, USA) supplemented with 0.15 mM of sodium chloride to obtain their contents, the tissue was removed, and the remaining liquid samples were frozen at -20 °C until they were used.

DNA Extraction and 16 S rRNA Gene Amplicon Sequencing

DNA extraction was performed according to the RBB + C method [11] as follows. An aliquot of 1 mL from each sample was homogenized in lysis buffer with zirconium beads to disrupt bacteria. Lysates were centrifuged at 4 °C for 5 min at 16,000xg, and the supernatants were collected. Nucleic acids were precipitated sequentially with 10 mM ammonium acetate, 98% isopropanol, and 70% ethanol. Finally, residual proteins were removed, and DNA was purified using QIAamp DNA Mini Kit spin columns (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) according to the protocol provided by the manufacturer. DNA concentration was measured with a Qubit 3.0 spectrophotometer (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and its integrity was determined by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. The V3-V4 region of the 16 rRNA gene was amplified using the universal primers 341 F (5′-CCTAYGGGRBGCASCAG-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACNNGGGTATCTAAT-3′) by Research Services NOVOGEN on the Illumina MiSeq platform using paired-end 2 × 250 bp chemistry. PCR reactions were performed with 15 µL of Phusion High-Fidelity PCR Master Mix; 0.2 µM of forward and reverse primers, and 10 ng of template DNA. Thermal cycling consisted of initial denaturation at 98℃ for 1 min, followed by 30 cycles of denaturation at 98℃ for 10 s, annealing at 50℃ for 30 s, and elongation at 72℃ for 30 s, and an additional final step at 72℃ for 5 min. Raw paired-end reads (250 bp) were received as FASTQ files using Phred + 33 quality encoding (Table S1).

Bacterial Diversity and Statistical Analysis

The bioinformatic analysis was carried out using the high-performance computing cluster of the Laboratorio Nacional de Visualización Científica Avanzada (LAVIS-SECIHTI, UNAM, https://www.lavis.unam.mx). Raw 16 S rDNA gene sequences were processed in QIIME 2 using the DADA2 pipeline to denoise reads, remove chimeras, and generate high-quality amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). Raw paired-end reads (250 bp) were quality filtered and trimmed using the DADA2 pipeline with truncLen = 240, maxN = 0, maxEE = 2, and truncQ = 2. PhiX sequences were removed during filtering. Sequence denoising was performed following error-rate learning, paired-end merging, and chimera removal using the consensus method. SILVA ribosomal RNA gene database was employed to annotate taxonomic information for each ASV using QIIME 2 software. ASVs were taxonomically classified from the phylum to genus level, and unclassified taxa were retained to preserve information regarding community structure. The updated bacterial nomenclature of Oren and Garrity was used [12]. Alpha diversity indices, Shannon, Simpson, and Chao1 indices were calculated to evaluate microbial richness and evenness within each sample. Statistical differences between the experimental groups (SRD/SV, IRD/IVD, SRD/IRD, and SVD/IVD) were evaluated using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, with adjusted p-values applied for multiple comparisons. Beta diversity was evaluated using Jaccard distance matrix to determine differences in microbial community compositions between samples. Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) on Jaccard distances was performed to visualize clustering patterns among the experimental groups. Statistical differences in beta diversity were analyzed using PERMANOVA.

Functional Predictions of the Microbiome of Deroceras Laeve

We analyzed 16 S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing data using a structured workflow. The ASV count table (seqtab.nochim.csv) was used as the abundance matrix, with samples annotated according to their experimental groups (SRD, SVD, IRD, IVD) from metadata.csv. Taxonomic assignments for each ASV were obtained from taxonomy.complete.csv or taxonomy.tsv, providing hierarchical classification from Domain to Species. The abundance matrix was reshaped to a long format to allow integration with taxonomy and metadata.

Statistical Identification of Differential Abundance at the Genus Level

Differential abundance analyses were performed in ALDEx2 using filtered ASV count tables after excluding taxa with total counts of less than 10 or present in fewer than two samples. CLR transformation was applied with 128 Monte Carlo instances (mc.samples = 128) and denom = “all”. Significant taxa were identified using wi.eBH < 0.05 and absolute effect size ≥ 0.5. Significant ASVs were collapsed at the genus level, summarizing adjusted p-values, effect sizes, prevalence, and abundance metrics.

Predictive Metabolic Pathways and Metagenomic Functional Profiles

Functional profile and metabolic pathways of microbiota were performed using PICRUSt2 (https://github.com/picrust/picrust2) (i.e., KEGG ortholog (KO) abundances). ASV abundances were normalized by predicted 16 S rRNA gene copy number. The sum of KO abundances within a pathway was used to calculate KEGG pathway abundances and metagenomic function. Heatmap visualization of aggregated metabolic pathways and functional profile abundances across all groups was generated to highlight the most prevalent features.

Results

Characterization of the Bacterial Communities of the Stomach and Intestine of Deroceras Laeve

The 16 S rDNA sequencing yielded 2,232,687 high-quality sequences, clustered into 3,045 distinct amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). The number of identified phyla varied across the digestive tract segments and diets. A total of 27 and 32 phyla were detected in the stomach under a diet formulated for rodents (RD) and a fresh vegetable diet (VD), respectively, whereas 41 and 35 phyla were identified in the intestine (Fig. 1A). However, the percentage of ASVs assigned to phyla was very high and those unassigned were negligible (0.09% and 0.19% in the stomach and 0.26% and 0.19% in the intestine, for RD and VD, respectively) (Fig. 1B). Additionally, the number of families, genera, and species varied between the digestive tract segment and diet, showing a considerable number of taxa at these three levels (Fig. 1A). The ASVs assigned to family and genus represented most of the identified sequences per sample group (Fig. 1B). In contrast, at the species level, most of the sequences remained unassigned (Fig. 1B). Based on these results, we used the genus level to describe the composition of the microbiota, as this provided an optimal balance between taxonomic resolution and classification reliability.

Microbiota at the Phylum Level

Pseudomonadota was the most abundant phylum across all groups (Fig. 2). In the stomach, it represented 88.2% and 80.8% of the total sequences in the RD and VD groups, respectively (Fig. 2A). This was followed by Actinomycetota which accounted for 4.54% and 12%, and Bacteroidota which represented 3.25% and 3.41% in RD and VD diets, respectively. Bacillota and Verrucomicrobiota were the fourth and fifth most abundant phyla in the stomach, with low relative abundance. In the intestine Pseudomonadota represented 79.18% and 69.88% in RD and VD diets, respectively (Fig. 2B). Meanwhile Actinomycetota accounted for 11% and 19.4%, followed by Bacteroidota which represented 3.94% and 4.73% in RD and VD, respectively. Bacillota and Cyanobacteriota ranked as the fourth and fifth phyla in the intestine, accounting for small fractions of sequences. These results also revealed that the relative abundance of Pseudomonadota was higher in the stomach compared to the intestine with both diets, and highest in both regions for VD. Moreover, these changes were accompanied by increase in Actinomycetota in intestine and its decrease in both regions in VD, compared to RD.

Microbiota at the Genus Level

Regarding genera with a relative abundance above 1%, differences associated with diet were observed, with a larger number of detected genera in VD-fed slugs in both the stomach and intestine compared to RD (Fig. 3). Specifically, the number of genera between RD and VD increased from 8 to 16 in the stomach and from 10 to 20 in the intestine.

Most abundant bacterial genera in the stomach and intestine of Deroceras laeve. (A) Genera with a relative abundance above 1% in the stomach of slugs fed with RD or VD. (B) Genera with a relative abundance above 1% in the intestine of RD- and VD-fed slugs

In the stomach, Rahnella was the most abundant genus at 83.5% in RD-fed slugs and it declined to 51.1% in VD-fed slugs (Fig. 3A). Stenotrophomonas ranked as the second most abundant genus in RD-fed slugs, while the Rhizobium complex (Allorhizobium-Neorhizobium-Pararhizobium) occupied this position in VD-fed slugs. Only the genera Raoultella, Sphingobacterium, and Pseudomonas were shared between the two dietary groups, but the three were more abundant in VD-fed slugs, accompanied by a decrease in the abundance of Rahnella.

In the intestine, Rahnella remained the most abundant genus in slugs fed with RD, but in VD-fed animals the Rhizobium complex became dominant, Rahnella being displaced to third in abundance (Fig. 3B). The second most abundant genus was Stenotrophomonas in RD-fed slugs, and Brevibacterium in VD-fed slugs. The genera Pseudomonas, Stenotrophomonas, Devosia, Sphingobacterium, Raoultella, and Enterobacter were detected in both dietary groups in the intestine, showing variable relative abundance except for Sphingobacterium which exhibited the same abundance in both diets.

Interestingly, Amaricoccus was found in the stomach and intestine of RD-fed slugs with similar abundance, but was absent in VD-fed slugs.

Diversity of Microbial Communities

To evaluate the effects of diet on microbial richness and diversity across digestive compartments, we analyzed alpha and beta diversity metrics. The analysis of alpha diversity showed no significant differences between RD and VD in the stomach across Chao1 (p = 0.31), Shannon (p = 0.09), and Simpson (p = 0.25) indices, although the mean tended to be higher for the three in VD-fed slugs (Fig. 4A). Similarly, in the intestine, alpha diversity analysis showed no significant differences in Chao1 (p = 0.57) and Shannon (p = 0.23) indices between RD and VD diet, whereas the Simpson index was significantly higher in VD (p = 0.028) (Fig. 4B), consistent with lower evenness and greater species dominance under RD, and high uniformity in species diversity with little dominance of any under VD. The analysis of beta diversity revealed compositional differences between the stomach and intestine of D. laeve. The principal coordinate analysis based on the Jaccard index, which measures the similarity between groups, showed partial clustering of samples corresponding to the anatomical segments, with the Principal Coordinate 1 (PCoA1) and the Principal Coordinate 2 (PCoA2) explaining 15.8% and 14.9% of the total variation, respectively. Although some dispersion was observed within each cluster, samples from different diets did not show a consistent pattern of separation, suggesting that anatomical compartment rather than diet was the main factor associated with beta diversity (PERMANOVA, R2 = 0.1846, F = 2.2638, p = 0.032) (Fig. 4C). This confirms a statistically significant effect of anatomical segment on community composition. Finally, rarefaction curves suggested some variations in richness between samples (Fig. 4D). All curves approached clear plateaus, demonstrating adequate sequencing depth and high coverage of the microbial communities in both anatomical compartments, thus providing confidence in the results obtained.

Diet-Dependent Changes of Bacterial Genera in the Digestive System

Although alpha diversity did not reveal significant differences between diets across digestive compartments, differential abundance analysis was performed at the genus level to identify taxa affected by dietary treatment. In the stomach, we identified 26 genera and 5 unclassified ASVs showing significant differences in abundance between RD- and VD-fed slugs (Fig. 5A; Table 3 of supplementary information). Additionally, the most differentially abundant genera detected belong to the phylum Pseudomonadota followed by Actinomycetota, and Bacteroidota. Among the first two phyla, the most abundant differentiated genera included the Pseudoclavibacter, Rahnella1 and Dietzia, followed by Xanthomonas, Stenotrophomonas, Rhizobium complex, and Aliihoeflea in that order (Fig. 5A). In the intestine, we observed 53 genera and 9 non-assigned ASVs showing significant differences in abundance between RD- and VD-fed slugs (Fig. 5B; Table 4 of supplementary information). Similar to the stomach, the intestine exhibited a predominance of differentially abundant genera belonging to the phylum Pseudomonadota followed by Actinomycetota, Bacteroidota, and Verrucomicrobiota. The most abundant differential genera observed in the first two phyla, were Pseudoclavibacter and Stenotrophomonas, followed by Xanthomonas, Aliihoeflea, Demaquina and Rhizobium complex, respectively (Fig. 5B).

Inferred Signaling Pathways Across Digestive System Segments and Dietary Treatments

The analysis of the inferred metabolic pathways revealed 442 shared across all groups with varying contributions (mean NSTI = 0.224) (Fig. 6).

Each row represents a specific metabolic pathway, and each column corresponds to an experimental group. The results for the three samples of each group are shown. In the stomach of RD-fed slugs, the pathways for fatty acid biosynthesis, gondoate biosynthesis (anaerobic), superpathway of fatty acid biosynthesis initiation, and mycolate biosynthesis were the most abundant, which correspond to the biosynthesis of fatty acids. In RD-fed slugs the pentose phosphate pathway (non-oxidative branch) I and fatty acid β-oxidation I (generic) were also among the most abundant pathways predicted. Importantly, aerobic respiration (cytochrome I) did not appear among the top 50, while pyruvate fermentation to branched-chain alcohols ranked eighth. Furthermore, the inferred functional analysis predicted a higher relative abundance of pathways associated with sucrose degradation and mixed acid fermentation in RD-fed slugs but were not among the most abundant pathway in VD-fed slugs (Table S2).

By contrast, the functional analysis predicted a strong association of the stomach microbiota of VD-fed slugs with oxidative metabolism, with secondary contributions from reductive pathways and fatty acid biosynthesis. Pathways related to aerobic respiration mediated by Cytochrome C and pyruvate fermentation to branched-chain alcohols were among the predominant inferred functions. High contribution of the pathways for gondoate biosynthesis (anaerobic), cis-vaccenate biosynthesis, and mycolate biosynthesis corresponding to fatty acid biosynthesis was also observed. Additionally, slugs fed with VD showed an enrichment of predicted pathways associated with amino acid biosynthesis, which revealed a lower predicted abundance in RD-fed slugs.

In the intestine, in contrast, the two most abundant inferred metabolic pathways under both diets were aerobic respiration I (cytochrome c) and pyruvate fermentation to branched-chain alcohols (Fig. 6). However, in RD-fed slugs, the pentose phosphate pathway (non-oxidative branch) I was the third predicted metabolic pathway, while in VD-fed slugs it ranked sixteenth. Subsequent high-contributing pathways were fatty acid biosynthesis, followed by amino acid biosynthesis, with similar rankings in RD- and VD-fed slugs.

Collectively, these findings suggest that the intestinal microbiome of RD- and VD-fed slugs is involved in largely conserved metabolic pathways, contrasting with the divergent results in stomach.

Comparative Analysis of Predicted Microbial Functions in the Stomach and Intestine under Different Diets

Predicted functional profiling of the bacterial communities in RD- and VD-fed slugs at the KEGG level 1, revealed BRITE hierarchies (biological relationship and information transmission environment) as the most abundant category in all groups, followed by metabolism and environmental information processing (Fig. 7). At level 2, signaling and cellular processes, together with genetic information processing were among the most prevalent predicted functions in all groups. Within the top 10 functional categories, carbohydrate metabolism, amino acid metabolism, cofactor and vitamin metabolism, and energy metabolism were consistently represented in both the stomach and intestine of RD and VD fed slugs.

Discussion

Phylum Composition in the Stomach and Intestine

In the present study, we characterized the stomach and intestinal microbiota of the land slug D. laeve fed rodent or fresh vegetable diets. The predominant phyla detected in both digestive compartments were, in descending order of abundance, Pseudomonadota, Actinomycetota, Bacteroidota, Bacillota, Verrucomicrobiota, Cyanobacteriota, and Chloroflexota. This taxonomic profile is consistent with previous reports conducted in the digestive tract of other mollusks, including the fresh water snail Planorbella trivolvis, the sea snail Coralliophila meyendorffii and the Korean mussel Mytilus coruscus, in which Pseudomonadota was the most abundant phylum [13–15]. Also the freshwater gastropods Biomphalaria straminea, Bellamya aeruginosa, and Physella acuta, among others, show similar microbiota composition with Pseudomonadota as the predominant phylum, followed by Bacteroidota, Bacillota, Actinomycetota, Verrucomicrobiota, and Cyanobacteriota [16]. The widespread dominance of Pseudomonadota and the conserved intestinal composition of the microbiota across mollusks reflect an evolutionary and functional consistency within the phylum and even in some insects [17, 18].

Our results also revealed a higher relative abundance of Pseudomonadota in slugs fed the fermentable carbohydrate- and protein-rich RD than in those fed VD. In other gastropods such as Planorbella trivolvis, Pseudomonadota predominates in non-herbivorous diets with low cellulose content, whereas in herbivore snails fed high cellulose diets the gut microbiota additionally had an important contribution of Bacteroidota [14]. Similarly, an increase in the abundance of Pseudomonadota has been reported in the abalone Haliotis midae fed formulated laboratory diets rich in protein and fermentable substrates [19]. In contrast, in Haliotis gigantea an asparagus-based diet produced a microbiota dominated by Tenericutes, whereas in kelp-fed animals, Pseudomonadota was the predominant phylum [20]. These observations highlight that Pseudomonadota respond dynamically to dietary composition, particularly to fermentable carbohydrates and protein-rich substrates, reflecting their metabolic adaptability within the gastropod gut. Overall, the predominance of Pseudomonadota observed in these studies strengthens the findings of our recent comparative metagenomic study of D. laeve and of thirteen other mollusk species including terrestrial, freshwater, and marine species [21]. In all of them, Pseudomonodota was the predominant bacterial phylum, albeit with diversity at the genus level in species from several habitats and even in different tissues.

Diversity in Bacterial Composition in the Stomach and Intestine

Diversity analysis revealed region- and diet-dependent differences in the digestive microbiota of D. laeve, with the intestine being more sensitive to dietary changes than the stomach. Rarefaction analysis revealed sufficient sequence depth for both regions, but a higher taxonomic richness in the intestine than in the stomach. This is consistent with a previous report which showed that the crop, intestine, and rectum of the snail Achatina fulica are highly specialized compartments with different digestive functions and microbial communities [22]. Overall, the Chao1, Shannon, and Simpson indices indicated that the intestine hosts more diverse and equitable communities, whereas the stomach exhibited lower diversity and minimal dietary effects, suggesting that acidic conditions and enzymatic activity limit bacterial growth [23]. In the intestine, the Simpson index showed significant differences between diets, with VD-fed slugs showing greater equitability and bacterial richness, consistent with findings in Planorbella trivolvis which demonstrated that cellulose-rich diets increase intestinal bacterial diversity [14]. Beta diversity analysis further supports these findings by showing a partial but consistent separation between the stomach and intestinal communities, suggesting that factors such as pH, oxygenation, and substrate availability influence community enrichment [24]. These observations suggest that the digestive compartment determines the organization of bacterial communities in the microbiota of D. laeve, while diet contributes to the taxa composition, particularly in the intestine.

Genus Composition in the Stomach and Intestine

Although alpha diversity did not differ significantly between diets, VD-fed slugs showed nearly twice the number of abundant genera. Furthermore, PCoA showed a partial separation associated with diet. However, the PERMANOVA analysis indicated that anatomical compartments were the principal factor of variations in bacterial communities. In contrast, the effect of diet was reflected in the differential abundance with 32 and 61 genera showing significant differences induced by diet in stomach and intestine, respectively. In the snail Planorbella trivolvis, the intestinal microbiota composition at the genus level differed markedly with diet, as cellulose-rich herbivorous diet exhibited 22 abundant genera, compared to 7 observed in the non-herbivore diet group [14]. Other studies have also described changes in the gut bacterial communities of gastropods influenced by diet [19, 25, 26]. These findings reveal that nutrient-rich diets such as RD are associated with lower microbial richness and diversity.

In D. laeve, Rahnella was the dominant genus in the stomach and intestine, with higher relative abundance in RD-fed slugs, showing statistically significant differences between diets in the stomach. Previous works have reported Rahnella in the microbiota of mollusks, particularly in the intestine of snails [27–29], and at lower abundances in bivalves [30]. Functionally, Rahnella degrades xylan-derived sugars via β-xylosidase and mediates lactose fermentation and nitrogen fixation, consistent with the elevated amino acid metabolism and fermentation pathways inferred by our bioinformatic metabolic analysis in RD-fed slugs [31, 32]. Our results also showed Stenotrophomonas as the second most abundant genus in the stomach and intestine of RD-fed slugs, with a significant decrease compared to VD. In the intestine of VD-fed slugs, the Rhizobium complex predominated, while ranking second in the stomach of VD-fed slugs. Diet-dependent enrichment of Stenotrophomonas has also been reported across mollusks, including Haliotis gigantea [20] and Mytilus coruscus [15], highlighting its sensitivity to dietary changes [33]. Moreover, Stenotrophomonas play key roles in amino acid transport and metabolism as well as energy production and conversion, such as in the pentose phosphate pathway, TCA cycle, fatty acid, and amino acid biosynthesis [34]. Consistent with these functions, our results revealed fatty acid and amino acid biosynthesis among the most enriched pathways, suggesting that Stenotrophomonas contributes substantially to the metabolic potential of the D. laeve microbioma. The Rhizobium complex previously reported in hepatopancreas and gut of various mollusks [35, 36], has been associated with biological nitrogen fixation [37, 38]. This raises the possibility that members of the Rhizobium complex are associated with metabolic processes related to amino acid biosynthesis, as previously reported in other systems. In our study, this complex was detected predominantly in VD-fed slugs. However, it cannot be ruled out that its presence derives from environmental and dietary exposure through fresh plant material. Therefore, its persistence as a stable bacteria associated with the digestive tract cannot be confirmed with the current experimental design. In addition, Pseudomonas, Serratia, Raoultella, Pantoea, and Enterobacter were detected as abundant in both the stomach and intestine of D. laeve. Their distribution, however, differed across compartments and diets. These genera have been associated to digestive, defense, and stress adaptative functions in other invertebrates and mollusks [16, 39, 40]. Interestingly, D. laeve shares only a limited number of Pseudomonadota taxa with freshwater gastropods, such as Pseudomonas and Bosea, indicating that few genera are conserved across mollusks of different habitats [14, 16]. Together, these findings suggest that the bacterial communities in the gut microbiota of D. leave varies according to its environment and diet, although some taxa may also present environmental or food-related microbial contributions.

Inferred Metabolic Pathways of the Stomach and Intestine Microbiome

The inferred metabolic pathways indicate strong influence on stomach microbial metabolism, with reductive metabolism and fatty acid biosynthesis predominating in RD and enhanced oxidative metabolism, pyruvate fermentation and amino acid biosynthesis in VD. In contrast, the intestinal microbiome of both RD- and VD-fed slugs exhibited a relatively conserved metabolic profile, dominated by aerobic respiration, pyruvate fermentation, and fatty acid and amino acid biosynthesis. The fatty acid biosynthesis has previously been inferred in the gut microbiota of the snails Campeloma decisum and Cipangopaludina japonica [41]. Also, in the gut of the hydrothermal snail Alviniconcha marisindica, genes related to lipid metabolism and amino acid biosynthesis were enriched [42], while the gut microbiota of Campeloma decisum and Cipangopaludina japonica was enriched in pyruvate fermentation and aerobic respiration via cytochrome c [41]. In mammals, microbiota-synthetized amino acids contribute 0.1%-10% of the circulating amino acid pool, and disruptions in their synthesis have been associated with various extraintestinal diseases [43]. Meanwhile, fatty acids synthetized by microbiota are associated with immunomodulatory effects, contribute as energy source for the epithelial cells of the digestive tract, and maintain intestinal structural integrity [44, 45]. These findings suggest a versatile microbial community able to contribute to both the biosynthetic and energetic demands of the host under different dietary conditions. However, specific biological functions of metabolites derived from the microbiota require further investigations.

Predictive function analysis suggests that signaling and cellular processes and genetic information processing, dominate microbial functions in both digestive compartments across diets. In the worm Enchytraeus albidus, proteins related to genetic information processing as well as signaling and cellular processes were detected among the most prevalent inferred functions of the microbiome [46]. Moreover, metabolism ranked third in both the stomach and intestine of RD- and VD-fed slugs, with carbohydrate and amino acid metabolism-related proteins among the top ten inferred functions. These results are consistent with reports in Cipangopaludina cathayensis, in which metabolism predominates at KEGG level 1, while carbohydrate and amino acid pathways dominate level 2 [47]. These findings suggest a functional profile in the microbiome of D. laeve dominated by proteins involved in signaling, cellular processes, genetic information, and metabolism, which is consistent with functional inferred patterns reported in other invertebrates.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that dietary composition exerts a clear influence on the microbiome of the digestive system in the land slug D. laeve, which drives changes in the metabolic pathways mediated by the microbiota. Although alpha diversity remains stable, beta diversity analysis revealed compositional changes in the microbiome of both the stomach and intestine between RD- and VD-fed slugs. At the metabolic level, our results indicate that diet-specific substrates could modulate microbial metabolism. RD-fed slugs exhibited a microbiota enriched in reductive and lipid biosynthesis pathways, particularly fatty acid biosynthesis, while VD-fed slugs displayed enhanced pyruvate fermentation and amino acid biosynthetic activity, indicating shifts in predicted metabolic pathway profiles toward oxidative and nutrient-assimilative functions. Despite these differences, core functional capacities such as aerobic respiration and amino acid metabolism remained conserved in the intestinal microbiota, revealing a functional stability in this compartment. Moreover, predictive analysis revealed that signaling and cellular processes, genetic information processing, and metabolism were the predominant functions mediated by the microbiota across all groups. Collectively, these results indicate that while diet alters the taxonomic and metabolic composition of the D. laeve gut microbiota, it does not appear to disrupt its essential cellular functions. This work provides a foundation for future studies using D. laeve as a model for investigating microbiota-driven physiological processes and dietary modulation of microbial function, both in relationship to the high ability to adapt to diverse environments reflected by the world-wide distribution of this species.

A limitation of this study, however, was the lack of environmental and extraction controls, which limits our ability to definitively state that the bacteria found are stable gut residents. This is particularly valid for the Rhizobium complex, Pantoea, Pseudomonas, and Sphingobacterium which have been associated with plants and soil. Therefore, future studies should incorporate methods for controlling these conditions.
