High-Dimensional Theory of LoRA Fine-Tuning in a Solvable Attention Model
Duranthon, Fabrizio Boncoraglio, Lenka Zdeborová

Abstract
We develop a high-dimensional statistical theory of low-rank adaptation (LoRA) in attention models, capturing the interplay between pre-training and fine-tuning. We introduce a solvable framework in which a single-head attention layer is first pre-trained on a data-abundant task and subsequently adapted via a rank-one LoRA update on limited data. In the high-dimensional limit, both stages admit a sharp asymptotic characterization in terms of a finite set of order parameters, yielding explicit predictions for test errors and representation alignment. Our analysis shows that the impact of pre-training on LoRA is summarized by an effective noise term, from which we derive prescriptions for the optimal pre-training procedure. We also demonstrate a regime with a mismatch between the value of the test error and representation quality, and propose an application of our theory to active fine-tuning.

Introduction

Large pre-trained attention models are rarely retrained from scratch for each new task.
Instead, they are adapted to downstream data through parameter-efficient fine-tuning methods, which keep most pre-trained weights frozen and optimize only a small number of task-specific parameters. Among these methods, low-rank adaptation (LoRA) has become a standard approach: it injects trainable low-rank updates into the weight matrices of a frozen transformer, often matching or approaching full fine-tuning performance while substantially reducing memory, storage, and optimization costs
[
1
]
. This empirical success is part of a broader line of parameter-efficient adaptation methods, including adapters
[
2
]
, prefix tuning
[
3
]
and QLoRA
[
4
]
. A recurring empirical observation is that fine-tuned adaptation appears to live in a much lower-dimensional space than pre-training, as also suggested by intrinsic-dimension studies of language-model fine-tuning
[
5
]
.

Recent theoretical work has begun to clarify some aspects of LoRA. Existing results mainly address its expressivity, optimization geometry or dynamics
[
6
,
7
,
8
,

,

,
11
,

]
.
Closer to our work,
[
14
]
derives generalization bounds for asymmetric LoRA, where one factor of the LoRA is frozen at random and only the other is trained, while
[
15
]
provides an NTK-based analysis that characterizes optimization and generalization in a lazy regime. These works shed light on important facets of LoRA, but leave open the question of a quantitative statistical theory for adaptation after pre-training. In particular, a characterization of how fine-tuning performance depends on both (large) pre-training and (small) fine-tuning sample sizes and pre-training quality is still lacking.

In this work we develop such a theory in a solvable high-dimensional model of LoRA fine-tuning for attention. We consider a single-head tied-attention layer that is first pre-trained on an extensive-rank attention-indexed (AIM) target
[
16
,
17
]
with a large number of samples, and is then adapted to a downstream/fine-tuning task by only learning a rank-one LoRA component from a much smaller dataset. This yields a minimal setting in which pre-training a large model, freezing, and low-rank adaptation coexist in a form that remains analytically tractable. Our main result is a sharp asymptotic characterization of the LoRA-fine-tuned estimator. In the high-dimensional limit, both the pre-training stage and the fine-tuning stage are described by a finite set of scalar order parameters, from which we obtain explicit predictions for the pre-training test error, the fine-tuning test error, and the overlaps with the ground truth. This provides, to our knowledge, the first tight high-dimensional learning-curve theory for LoRA fine-tuning after attention pre-training.

Contributions.

Our main contributions are:

1.

We introduce a solvable high-dimensional setting of LoRA fine-tuning in attention, with a full-rank pre-training stage on a large dataset and a rank-one fine-tuning on a much more limited dataset.

2.

We derive a sharp asymptotic characterization of the LoRA test error and reconstruction overlap in terms of finite-dimensional order parameters. Technically, we rely on the versatility of the high-dimensional analysis to merge independent results for the pre-training and the fine-tuning task into a joint characterization.

3.

We identify an effective-noise mechanism which quantifies how the quality of pre-training objective controls fine-tuning performance. This single parameter allows us to derive quantitative prescriptions for the optimal pre-training procedure and an idealized active-learning rule for fine-tuning.

4.

We identify regimes in which there is a mismatch at the fine-tuning stage between good test error and good reconstruction overlap thanks to memorization of the pre-training data.

A two-stage model for pre-training and LoRA fine-tuning

We consider sequences of
T
tokens of dimension
D
for a sequence-to-sequence task. We have access to a large dataset of pre-training samples and to a smaller dataset for fine-tuning. More precisely, we consider a set of
N
pre-training input and output sequences
\mathcal{S}=(X_{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times D},y_{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times D})_{\mu\in[N]}
and a set of
N^{\prime}
fine-tuning input and output sequences
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}=(X^{\prime}_{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times D},y^{\prime}_{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times D})_{\mu\in[N^{\prime}]}
.

Model Architecture:
we consider an attention layer with tied keys and queries and identity values, parameterized by the weights
W\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times P}
, with
P
the rank of
W
, and
w\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
.
W
is the large-rank part of the weights that are pre-trained, while
w
is the low-rank part that is fine-tuned. Given a sample
X,y
from
\mathcal{S}
or
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
, the attention predicts the sequence

\displaystyle\hat{y}(X;W,w)=\sigma(A-\mathbb{E}_{X}A)X\,,\quad\quad A=D^{-1}X\left(P^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}WW^{\top}+ww^{\top}\right)X^{\top}\,,

(1)

where
\sigma:\mathbb{R}^{T\times T}\to\mathbb{R}^{T\times T}
is the activation function. In the following we will consider it to be the row-wise softmax or the rescaled identity
\sigma:x\mapsto x/T
.
A
is the pre-activated attention matrix.
\mathbb{E}_{X}
is the empirical expectation over the sequences of the set
\mathcal{S}
or
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
and the term
-\mathbb{E}_{X}A
is a batch normalization. The scaling with respect to
D
and
P
is taken so that all the elements of
A
are of order one, and
W
and
w
contribute comparably to
A
.

Pre-training:
we first pre-train the attention by minimizing the following empirical loss

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(W)=\sum_{\mu}^{N}D^{-1}||\hat{y}(X_{\mu};W,0)-y_{\mu}||_{F}^{2}\ .

(2)

We fix
\hat{W}\in\argmin_{W}(\mathcal{L}+\mathscr{R})
a minimizer of the loss, with
\mathscr{R}(W)=\lambda||W||_{F}^{2}
a regularization.

LoRA fine-tuning:
we then fine-tune the attention on the loss

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\prime}(w)=\sum_{\mu}^{N^{\prime}}D^{-1}||\hat{y}(X^{\prime}_{\mu};\hat{W},w)-y^{\prime}_{\mu}||_{F}^{2}

(3)

taking
\hat{w}\in\argmin_{w}(\mathcal{L}^{\prime}+\mathscr{R}^{\prime})
, with
\mathscr{R}^{\prime}(w)=\lambda^{\prime}||w||_{2}^{2}
.
\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}>0
are the regularization strengths.

Measured performances:
we are interested in the test errors of the attention with weights
\hat{W},\hat{w}
on test pre-training and fine-tuning samples

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\hat{W})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{(X,y)\sim\mathcal{P}}\left[D^{-1}||\hat{y}(X;\hat{W},0)-y||_{F}^{2}\,\big|\,\mathcal{S}\right]

(4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(\hat{W},\hat{w})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{(X^{\prime},y^{\prime})\sim\mathcal{P}^{\prime}}\left[D^{-1}||\hat{y}(X^{\prime};\hat{W},\hat{w})-y^{\prime}||_{F}^{2}\,\big|\,\mathcal{S},\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\right]

(5)

We will also consider the reconstruction error of the LoRA task measured as the cosine similarity (or
overlap
) between the learned weights and the ground truth
o_{w}=\nicefrac{{|\hat{w}^{\top}w^{*}|}}{{\|\hat{w}\|_{2}\|w^{*}\|_{2}}}
.

For a generic pre-training and fine-tuning dataset, the two-stage optimization above is too complex to admit a sharp analytical description. To make the problem tractable while preserving the key ingredients of pre-training followed by low-rank adaptation, we consider the data to be synthetic, generated by a target of the same type of the analyzed architecture.

Probabilistic model for synthetic data:

let
P_{0}
be the rank of the target and take
W^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times P_{0}}
,
w^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
common to all samples, with
W^{*}_{i,j}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
and
w^{*}_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
for all
i\in[D],j\in[P_{0}]
. We consider two possible scenarios, that we distinguish by the index
\mathrm{e}\in\{0,1\}
:

–
i) fine-tuning on different data:
in this case the sequences
X
and
X^{\prime}
of the pre-training and fine-tuning train sets are different and we draw them independently. We set
\mathrm{e}=0
.

–
ii) fine-tuning on reused sequences:
in this case the input sequences are shared between the pre-training and fine-tuning, while the output sequences correspond to different tasks. We set
\mathrm{e}=1
.

We set
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
to be the pre-training and fine-tuning distributions of the samples. They are jointly defined by

\displaystyle(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{P}^{\prime}):\left\{\begin{matrix}X\sim\mathcal{P}_{0}\\
\xi\sim\mathcal{P}_{\Delta}\\
A^{*}=D^{-1}P_{0}^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}XW^{*}W^{*\top}X^{\top}\\
y=\sigma(A^{*}-\mathbb{E}_{X}A^{*}+\xi)X\end{matrix}\right.\ ,\left\{\begin{matrix}X^{\prime}\sim\mathrm{e}\delta_{X}+(1-\mathrm{e})\mathcal{P}_{0}\\
\xi^{\prime}\sim\mathrm{e}\delta_{\xi}+(1-\mathrm{e})\mathcal{P}_{\Delta}\\
{A^{\prime}}^{*}=D^{-1}X^{\prime}\left(P_{0}^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}W^{*}W^{*\top}+w^{*}w^{*\top}\right){X^{\prime}}^{\top}\\
y^{\prime}=\sigma({A^{\prime}}^{*}-\mathbb{E}_{X^{\prime}}{A^{\prime}}^{*}+\xi^{\prime})X^{\prime}\end{matrix}\right.

(6)

where
(\mathcal{P}_{0})_{a,i}=\mathcal{N}(0,1)
for all
a\in[T],i\in[D]
;
\xi,\xi^{\prime}\in\mathscr{S}(T)
are symmetric noise with strength
\Delta\geq 0
and law
(\mathcal{P}_{\Delta})_{a,b}=\mathcal{N}(0,\Delta/(2-\delta_{a,b}))
for all
a\leq b
. The factor
1/(2-\delta_{a,b})
is to take into account the symmetrization.
\mathbb{E}_{X}
and
\mathbb{E}_{X^{\prime}}
are expectations over the sequences. For
\mathrm{e}=0
the two distributions factorize over the samples, while for
\mathrm{e}=1
we match all the randomness.
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
are then constructed by independently drawing
N^{\prime}
samples from
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{P}^{\prime})
and the remaining
N-N^{\prime}
from
\mathcal{P}
.

Asymptotic limit:
we consider a high-dimensional limit
D\to\infty
together with

\displaystyle\frac{P_{0}}{D}\to\kappa_{0}=\Theta(1)\ ,\quad\frac{P}{D}\to\kappa=\Theta(1)\ ,\quad\frac{N}{D^{2}}\to\alpha=\Theta(1)\ ,\quad\frac{N^{\prime}}{D}\to\alpha^{\prime}=\Theta(1)

(7)

with
\kappa\geq 1
and a finite number of tokens
T=\Theta(1)
. This means that, for the pre-training, we consider
W
and
W^{*}
to have extensive rank and we have access to a quadratically large number of samples; while for the fine-tuning we consider
w
and
w^{*}
low-rank (rank one) and we only have access to a limited linear number of samples. We emphasize that
N\gg N^{\prime}
, which corresponds to the common case where the fine-tuning samples are limited compared to the pre-training samples.

Comments and limitations:

the pre-training and fine-tuning tasks share a common extensive-rank component, which must first be learned during pre-training and then transferred to the downstream/fine-tuning task. The additive noise
\xi
models residual interactions and task variability that are not captured by the attention architecture, and therefore allows for a controlled mismatch between the target and the student. We focus on Gaussian input sequences for analytical tractability, but, as in related high-dimensional models
[
27
,
28
,
29
,
30
]
, we expect the resulting characterization to be robust beyond the strictly Gaussian setting. Finally, we restrict here to tied keys and queries and to a rank-one LoRA update; these choices yield the simplest setting in which the interplay between pre-training and low-rank adaptation can be analyzed exactly. Extensions to untied attention and higher-rank updates can be directly obtained following
[
17
]
and
[
22
]
. The assumption
\kappa\geq 1
avoids a finite-width bottleneck in the pre-training stage. The case
\kappa<1
can be treated by including the corresponding rank constraint, but we do not consider it here in order to focus on the transfer from the frozen extensive-rank component to the rank-one LoRA update.

Asymptotic characterization of the LoRA

Order parameters:

\mathcal{L}^{\prime}

\displaystyle Q_{0}

\displaystyle=D^{-2}\tr S^{*}S^{*}

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle=D^{-2}\tr\hat{S}\hat{S}

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle=D^{-2}\tr\hat{S}S^{*}

(8)

\displaystyle q_{0}

\displaystyle=D^{-1}w^{*\top}w^{*}

\displaystyle q

\displaystyle=D^{-1}\hat{w}^{\top}\hat{w}

\displaystyle m

\displaystyle=D^{-1}\hat{w}^{\top}w^{*}

3.1
Effective low-dimensional pre-activations

A standard consequence of high-dimensional concentration in teacher–student models is that the behavior of the predictor can be reduced to a finite-dimensional effective problem. In the present setting, all observables of interest can be expressed in terms of the joint distribution of a small number of scalar pre-activations associated with the target and the trained attention. We therefore introduce these effective variables first.
For a sequence
X
let the following be the pre-activations of the target and the trained attention.

\displaystyle z^{*}

\displaystyle=A^{*}-\mathbb{E}_{X}A^{*}+\xi\qquad\mathrm{where}\;A^{*}=D^{-1}P_{0}^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}XW^{*}W^{*\top}X^{\top}

(10)

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle=A-\mathbb{E}_{X}A\qquad\mathrm{where}\;A=D^{-1}P^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}X\hat{W}\hat{W}^{\top}X^{\top}

(11)

\displaystyle\chi^{*}

\displaystyle=D^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}Xw^{*}\qquad\mathrm{and}\qquad\chi=D^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}X\hat{w}

(12)

We characterize the distribution of these pre-activations firstly for
X
being a test sequence.

(Test samples)

Consider

\displaystyle\left(\begin{smallmatrix}z^{*}_{a,b}\\

(13)

Consider

\displaystyle\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\chi^{*}_{a}\\

We then turn to train samples. Because of the correlations between the trained weights and the train sequences, the distribution of the pre-activations is more involved. We introduce the proximal operators, for the pre-training and the fine-tuning. For symmetric

\displaystyle\Psi(z;z^{*},\tilde{z})

\displaystyle=-||\sigma(z)-\sigma(z^{*})||_{F}^{2}-\sum_{a\leq b}^{T}\frac{1}{2V(1+\delta_{a,b})}(\tilde{z}_{a,b}-z_{a,b})^{2}

\displaystyle\psi(\chi;\chi^{*},\tilde{\chi},z^{*},z)

\displaystyle=-||\sigma(z+\chi\chi^{\top}-qI_{T})-\sigma(z^{*}+\chi^{*}\chi^{*\top}-q_{0}I_{T})||_{F}^{2}-\sum_{a}^{T}\frac{1}{2v}(\tilde{\chi}_{a}-\chi_{a})^{2}

\displaystyle\mathrm{Prox}(z^{*},\tilde{z})=\argmax_{z\in\mathscr{S}(T)}\Psi(z;z^{*},\tilde{z})\ ,\qquad\mathrm{prox}(\chi^{*},\tilde{\chi},z^{*},z)=\argmax_{\chi\in\mathbb{R}^{T}}\psi(\chi;\chi^{*},\tilde{\chi},z^{*},z)\ .

Result 2

(Train samples)
.

Consider

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}:(z^{*},\tilde{z})\sim\mathcal{Q}\quad\mathrm{and}\quad z=\mathrm{Prox}(z^{*},\tilde{z})\ .

3.2
Determination of the order parameters

The various laws

We introduce the spectral distribution of the pre-trained weights. Let

\mu_{\mathrm{sc},\eta}(x)=\sqrt{4\eta^{2}-x^{2}}/(2\pi\eta^{2})

To give some insights, the limit

4.2
Reused sequences

We now consider the case
\mathrm{e}=1
when the fine-tuning sequences are sampled from the pre-training set. In this case, the best pre-training procedure faces a trade-off between good fine-tuning test error
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
and good fine-tuning reconstruction
o_{w}
. This trade-off arises from the possibility to overfit and memorize the pre-train data, thus having
z_{\mu}\approx z^{*}_{\mu}
for all sequences from both
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
. This reduces the effective noise during the fine-tuning to
\Delta_{\mathrm{eff}}(\mathrm{e}=1)\approx 0
and leads to a good reconstruction of
w^{*}
. Because of the overfitting, the test errors
\mathcal{E},\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
are not optimal, the gains in reconstruction being not enough to compensate. This is depicted in Fig.
3
, which shows that, for the considered value of the model parameters, overfitting the pre-train data by taking
\lambda\to 0
maximizes the fine-tuning overlap
o_{w}
; while there is a finite
\lambda\approx 0.3
that minimizes
\mathcal{E}
and
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
. We can provide a precise description of the reduction of the noise obtained by reusing sequences. As detailed in App.
C
, for a linear
\sigma
, we have

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathrm{eff}}(\mathrm{e}=1)=\frac{1}{(1+4VT^{-2})^{2}}\Delta_{\mathrm{eff}}(\mathrm{e}=0)

(27)

where
V\geq 0
is given by Result
4
. Consequently the effective noise is always smaller when reusing sequences, compared to training on new sequences at
\mathrm{e}=0
. When
V\to\infty
, the pre-training fits data and the effective noise goes to zero.

Our model highlights that there can be a mismatch between good test error and good reconstruction, and raises the question of what is a relevant evaluation metric. More precisely, the task defined by the fine-tuning train loss
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
can be seen as a proxy for retrieving the ground truth structure
w^{*}
of the data, which can be used e.g. for further downstream tasks.
w^{*}
does not depend on the training procedure and it may not matter whether the model has a good test error
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
. A similar mismatch has been proven for unsupervised settings in
[
35
,
36
]
; we extend these observations to a supervised setting.

5
Conclusions

We introduced a solvable high-dimensional model for LoRA fine-tuning after attention pre-training, in which an extensive-rank component is learned from
N=\Theta(D^{2})
samples and a rank-one adaptation is learned from
N^{\prime}=\Theta(D)
samples. Within this setting, we obtained a sharp asymptotic characterization of the resulting predictor. Our results also exhibit regimes where test error and representation quality are misaligned, extending to supervised settings observations previously made in unsupervised learning.

The single-head, rank-one analysis presented here serves as a first step toward more general settings. A natural extension is to higher-rank LoRA updates and multi-head attention, where multiple directions or heads may compete for the information contained in the frozen representation, potentially leading to a richer structure of effective noise across tasks.

More broadly, it will be important to understand how robust this effective-noise picture is beyond the present setting. This includes extending the analysis beyond Gaussian inputs to establish universality, as well as studying learning dynamics, where the interaction between pre-training and fine-tuning unfolds over time rather than at the level of the empirical risk minimizer. The flexibility of the high-dimensional framework also makes it possible to investigate related phenomena such as catastrophic forgetting (see App.
A.8
).

Appendix A
Derivation of the main results

In this section we provide the derivation of the results on the asymptotic characterization of the trained attention of Section
3
. This derivation mainly relies on merging results established by previous work for the pre-training task
[
17
]
, and results established for ERM of rank-one attention in
[
22
]
, with an additional frozen field inherited from the pre-training. These results are based on well established techniques such as approximate message passing (AMP), Gaussian equivalence theory and replica theory from statistical physics. These techniques have been rigorously proven for similar settings in
[
37
,
31
]
and conjectured to extend to
[
17
,
22
]
.
In the present description of the results we thus focus on how these the two descriptions are merged in order to obtained the joint characterization of the pre-training and fine-tuning stages.

A.1
Reduction to an attention-output problem

We first reduce the sequence-to-sequence loss to a loss on the attention matrices. For a fixed matrix
B\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times T}
and
X\sim\mathcal{P}_{0}
, one has

D^{-1}\|BX\|_{F}^{2}=\operatorname{Tr}\left(B\frac{XX^{\top}}{D}B^{\top}\right)\xrightarrow[D\to\infty]{}\|B\|_{F}^{2},

(29)

since
XX^{\top}/D\to I_{T}
for fixed
T
. Hence the sequence-to-sequence square loss is asymptotically equivalent to the square loss between the corresponding attention matrices. For instance,

\displaystyle D^{-1}\left\|\sigma(A-\mathbb{E}_{X}A)X-\sigma(A^{*}-\mathbb{E}_{X}A^{*}+\xi)X\right\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt=\left\|\sigma(A-\mathbb{E}_{X}A)-\sigma(A^{*}-\mathbb{E}_{X}A^{*}+\xi)\right\|_{F}^{2}+o_{D}(1).

(30)

We use this reduction throughout the derivation.

A.2
Reduction to a Gaussian test channel, test samples

We show that the pre-activations of the attention are Gaussian over the test samples.
Let

S^{*}=P_{0}^{-1/2}W^{*}W^{*\top},\qquad\hat{S}=P^{-1/2}\hat{W}\hat{W}^{\top}.

(31)

For a fresh test sequence
X
, independent of the training sets and of
\hat{W},\hat{w}
, define

z^{*}=A^{*}-\mathbb{E}_{X}A^{*}+\xi,\qquad z=A-\mathbb{E}_{X}A,\qquad\chi^{*}=D^{-1/2}Xw^{*},\qquad\chi=D^{-1/2}X\hat{w},

(32)

with

A^{*}=D^{-1}XS^{*}X^{\top},\qquad A=D^{-1}X\hat{S}X^{\top}.

(33)

Since
\mathbb{E}_{X}X_{a}^{\top}BX_{b}=\delta_{ab}\operatorname{Tr}B
for any deterministic matrix
B
, the centering term is

(\mathbb{E}_{X}A^{*})_{ab}=\delta_{ab}D^{-1}\operatorname{Tr}S^{*},\qquad(\mathbb{E}_{X}A)_{ab}=\delta_{ab}D^{-1}\operatorname{Tr}\hat{S}.

(34)

Thus

z^{*}_{ab}=D^{-1}X_{a}^{\top}S^{*}X_{b}-\delta_{ab}D^{-1}\operatorname{Tr}S^{*}+\xi_{ab},\qquad z_{ab}=D^{-1}X_{a}^{\top}\hat{S}X_{b}-\delta_{ab}D^{-1}\operatorname{Tr}\hat{S}.

(35)

The vector pre-activations are Gaussian by construction:

\begin{pmatrix}\chi^{*}_{a}\\
\chi_{a}\end{pmatrix}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\begin{pmatrix}q_{0}&m\\
m&q\end{pmatrix}\right),\qquad q_{0}=D^{-1}w^{*\top}w^{*},\quad q=D^{-1}\hat{w}^{\top}\hat{w},\quad m=D^{-1}\hat{w}^{\top}w^{*}.

(36)

For the quadratic pre-activations, we use the Gaussian equivalence principle for centered quadratic measurements of Gaussian data, as in
[
17
,
37
]
. In the present finite-
T
setting, this amounts to replacing the finite collection of centered matrices

X_{a}X_{b}^{\top}-\delta_{ab}I_{D},\qquad 1\leq a\leq b\leq T,

(37)

by jointly Gaussian Wigner measurements with matching covariance, up to errors vanishing as
D\to\infty
. Equivalently, for fixed
T
, the finite collection of scalar quadratic measurements is asymptotically Gaussian and its covariance is obtained by Wick contractions. In particular,

\displaystyle D^{-2}\operatorname{Cov}_{X}\left(\operatorname{Tr}S^{*}(X_{a}X_{b}^{\top}-\delta_{ab}I_{D}),\operatorname{Tr}S^{*}(X_{a^{\prime}}X_{b^{\prime}}^{\top}-\delta_{a^{\prime}b^{\prime}}I_{D})\right)

\displaystyle=\delta_{aa^{\prime}}\delta_{bb^{\prime}}(1+\delta_{ab})D^{-2}\operatorname{Tr}(S^{*})^{2}+o_{D}(1),

(38)

and similarly for
\hat{S}
and the cross-covariance between
S^{*}
and
\hat{S}
. Including the additive noise gives, for
1\leq a\leq b\leq T
,

\begin{pmatrix}z^{*}_{ab}\\
z_{ab}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}z^{*}_{ba}\\
z_{ba}\end{pmatrix}\sim\mathcal{Q}_{ab}=\mathcal{N}\left(0,(1+\delta_{ab})\begin{pmatrix}\Delta/2+Q_{0}&M\\
M&Q\end{pmatrix}\right),

(39)

where

Q_{0}=D^{-2}\operatorname{Tr}(S^{*})^{2},\qquad Q=D^{-2}\operatorname{Tr}\hat{S}^{2},\qquad M=D^{-2}\operatorname{Tr}\hat{S}S^{*}.

(40)

Equations (
36
) and (
39
) give Result
1
. Combining them with the reduction (
30
) gives the test-error part of Result
3
.

(53)

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle=\frac{\hat{M}}{2\hat{V}}\left[2J(\eta,\epsilon)-\eta J_{1}-\epsilon J_{2}\right].

(54)

Equations (
49
)–(
54
) are the pre-training state-evolution equations used in the simulations. At convergence, the pre-training test error is

\mathcal{E}(\hat{W})=\mathbb{E}_{(z^{*},z)\sim\mathcal{Q}}\|\sigma(z)-\sigma(z^{*})\|_{F}^{2}.

(55)

A.4
Decoupling of the extensive-rank and rank-one components

We now explain why the LoRA vector
w
learned during fine-tuning is described, in the high-dimensional limit, only by its overlap with the rank-one teacher
w^{*}
, and not by additional overlaps with the extensive-rank matrices
S^{*}
and
\hat{S}
.

Conditionally on the pre-training stage,
S^{*}
and
\hat{S}
are frozen matrices with normalized traces of order one. In the Marchenko–Pastur setting considered here, their operator norms are of order
\sqrt{D}
and their squared Frobenius norms are of order
D^{2}
. Let
B
denote either of the centered matrices

S^{*}-\frac{\operatorname{Tr}S^{*}}{D}I_{D},\qquad\hat{S}-\frac{\operatorname{Tr}\hat{S}}{D}I_{D}.

(56)

Let
u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
be deterministic vectors with
\|u\|_{2},\|v\|_{2}=O(\sqrt{D})
, independent of a fresh Gaussian sequence
X
. A direct Wick contraction over
X
gives, for any token indices
a,b,c,d
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Cov}_{X}\left(D^{-1}X_{a}^{\top}BX_{b},\,D^{-1/2}X_{c}^{\top}u\,D^{-1/2}X_{d}^{\top}v\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt=D^{-2}\left[\delta_{ac}\delta_{bd}\,u^{\top}Bv+\delta_{ad}\delta_{bc}\,v^{\top}Bu\right].

(57)

Since
\|B\|_{\rm op}=O(\sqrt{D})
and
\|u\|_{2},\|v\|_{2}=O(\sqrt{D})
,

D^{-2}|u^{\top}Bv|\leq D^{-2}\|B\|_{\rm op}\|u\|_{2}\|v\|_{2}=O(D^{-1/2}).

(58)

Therefore the mixed covariance (
57
) vanishes as
D\to\infty
. In words, the extensive-rank quadratic fields
(z^{*},z)
and the rank-one fields
(\chi^{*},\chi)
are asymptotically independent at the level of the finite-dimensional effective channel, except for the internal covariances within each block already encoded by
(Q_{0},Q,M)
and
(q_{0},q,m)
.

This also rules out additional order parameters involving both
w
and the extensive-rank matrices. For example, for any vector
w
with
\|w\|_{2}=O(\sqrt{D})
,

D^{-2}|w^{\top}S^{*}w|\leq D^{-2}\|S^{*}\|_{\rm op}\|w\|_{2}^{2}=O(D^{-1/2}),

(59)

and similarly for
\hat{S}
. Hence quantities such as

D^{-2}w^{\top}S^{*}w,\qquad D^{-2}w^{\top}\hat{S}w,\qquad D^{-2}w^{*\top}\hat{S}w

(60)

vanish in the normalization relevant for the extensive-rank pre-activations. The rank-one LoRA component can therefore learn the direction
w^{*}
at the scale
N^{\prime}=\Theta(D)
, but it cannot learn the extensive-rank component, which remains a frozen Gaussian side field.

Consequently, conditionally on the pre-training order parameters
(Q,M,V)
, the fine-tuning problem reduces to a rank-one generalized linear model with teacher vector
w^{*}
, trainable vector
w
, and sample-wise side information
(z^{*},z)
. For fresh fine-tuning samples,
(z^{*},z)
has the test law
\mathcal{Q}
. For fine-tuning samples reused from the pre-training set, the same decoupling holds, but
(z^{*},z)
has the train law
\mathcal{R}
inherited from the pre-training stage.

A.5
Fine-tuning free entropy and state evolution

We now turn to the rank-one fine-tuning stage. Without the frozen fields
(z^{*},z)
, this is a rank-one sequence multi-index ERM problem. We use the corresponding high-dimensional characterization of
[
23
,
22
,
26
]
, with the modification that the output channel is conditioned on the pre-training fields
(z^{*},z)
. By the decoupling argument of Appendix
A.4
, these fields enter as side information and no additional overlaps with
S^{*}
or
\hat{S}
are needed.

Conditionally on the pre-training stage,

(z^{*},z)\sim\begin{cases}\mathcal{Q},&\mathrm{e}=0,\\
\mathcal{R},&\mathrm{e}=1.\end{cases}

(61)

The rank-one channel is parametrized by

\chi^{*}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{T}),\qquad\zeta\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{T}),\qquad\tilde{\chi}=m\chi^{*}+\sqrt{q-m^{2}}\,\zeta,

(62)

with
\chi^{*}
and
\zeta
independent. For fixed
(\chi^{*},\tilde{\chi},z^{*},z)
, define

\displaystyle\psi(\chi;\chi^{*},\tilde{\chi},z^{*},z)

\displaystyle=-\left\|\sigma\left(z+\chi\chi^{\top}-qI_{T}\right)-\sigma\left(z^{*}+\chi^{*}\chi^{*\top}-I_{T}\right)\right\|_{F}^{2}-\sum_{a=1}^{T}\frac{(\tilde{\chi}_{a}-\chi_{a})^{2}}{2v},

(63)

and

\chi^{\rm prox}=\operatorname{prox}(\chi^{*},\tilde{\chi},z^{*},z)=\argmax_{\chi\in\mathbb{R}^{T}}\psi(\chi;\chi^{*},\tilde{\chi},z^{*},z).

(64)

The fine-tuning free entropy is

\displaystyle\phi(m,q,v;\hat{m},\hat{q},\hat{v})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\alpha^{\prime}}m\hat{m}+\frac{1}{2\alpha^{\prime}}(q\hat{v}-v\hat{q})+\frac{\hat{m}^{2}+\hat{q}}{2\alpha^{\prime}(2\lambda^{\prime}+\hat{v})}

\displaystyle\hskip 34.14322pt+\mathbb{E}_{\chi^{*},\zeta,z^{*},z}\max_{\chi\in\mathbb{R}^{T}}\psi(\chi;\chi^{*},\tilde{\chi},z^{*},z),

(65)

which is the formula stated in Result
5
. The three terms in the free entropy above have the standard generalized-linear-model interpretation. The first two terms enforce the order parameters
q,m,v
, the rational term comes from integrating out the microscopic vector
w
under the quadratic regularization
\lambda^{\prime}\|w\|_{2}^{2}
, and the last term is the effective output-channel contribution. The only difference with a standard rank-one GLM is that the output channel depends on the frozen pre-training field
(z^{*},z)
.

State-evolution equations.

Extremizing (
65
) with respect to
(\hat{m},\hat{q},\hat{v})
gives the following stationarity equations used in the numerical implementation. Let

C(\chi^{*},\tilde{\chi},z^{*},z)=-\left[\nabla_{\chi}^{2}\psi(\chi^{\rm prox};\chi^{*},\tilde{\chi},z^{*},z)\right]^{-1}.

(66)

Then the fixed point is

\displaystyle m

\displaystyle=\hat{m}v,

(67)

\displaystyle q

\displaystyle=(\hat{m}^{2}+\hat{q})v^{2},

(68)

\displaystyle v

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\lambda^{\prime}+\hat{v}},

(69)

\displaystyle\hat{m}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha^{\prime}}{v}\mathbb{E}\left[\chi^{*\top}\chi^{\rm prox}-\frac{m}{v}\operatorname{Tr}C(\chi^{*},\tilde{\chi},z^{*},z)\right],

(70)

\displaystyle\hat{q}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha^{\prime}}{v^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\chi^{\rm prox}-\tilde{\chi}\|_{2}^{2}\right],

(71)

\displaystyle\hat{v}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha^{\prime}T}{v}-\frac{\alpha^{\prime}}{v^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\operatorname{Tr}C(\chi^{*},\tilde{\chi},z^{*},z)\right]-2\alpha^{\prime}\mathbb{E}\left[\partial_{q}\psi(\chi^{\rm prox};\chi^{*},\tilde{\chi},z^{*},z)\right].

(72)

All expectations are taken over (

) and (

-qI_{T}

At a fixed point, the fine-tuning test error is

\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(\hat{W},\hat{w})=\mathbb{E}_{(z^{*},z)\sim\mathcal{Q},\;(\chi^{*},\chi)\sim\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}}\left\|\sigma\left(z+\chi\chi^{\top}-qI_{T}\right)-\sigma\left(z^{*}+\chi^{*}\chi^{*\top}-I_{T}\right)\right\|_{F}^{2}

(73)

and the overlap is given by
o_{w}=\frac{m}{\sqrt{q}}
.
For softmax activation, the proximal map (

A.6
Replicon stability conditions

We collect here the stability conditions associated with the two state evolutions used above. They are local stability conditions for the replica-symmetric effective description around the extremizer selected by the algorithms. We verified them numerically in all parameter regimes shown in the figures.

For the extensive-rank pre-training stage, the condition is the one derived in
[
17
,
38
]
. Let
(z^{*},\tilde{z})\sim\mathcal{Q}
, let
\nabla^{2}
denote the Hessian of
\Psi
with respect to its first argument, of dimension
L\times L
with
L=T(T+1)/2
, and write

\mathrm{Prox}=\mathrm{Prox}(z^{*},\tilde{z}).

(74)

Then the pre-training replicon condition reads

\displaystyle 2\alpha\,\mathbb{E}\left\|\left(V\nabla^{2}\Psi(\mathrm{Prox};z^{*},\tilde{z})\right)^{-1}+I_{L}\right\|_{F}^{2}\int\mu_{\hat{Q}^{1/2}/\hat{M}}({\rm d}x)\mu_{\hat{Q}^{1/2}/\hat{M}}({\rm d}y)\frac{(\zeta(x)-\zeta(y))^{2}}{\hat{V}^{2}(x-y)^{2}}<1,

(75)

where

\zeta(x)=\left(x-\frac{2\sqrt{\kappa}\lambda}{\hat{M}}\right)_{+}.

(76)

The rank-one fine-tuning replicon condition is the one stated in main text. We recall it. Let
(z^{*},z)\sim\mathcal{Q}
if
\mathrm{e}=0
and
(z^{*},z)\sim\mathcal{R}
if
\mathrm{e}=1
, let
(\chi^{*},\tilde{\chi})
be distributed as in (
62
), and write

\mathrm{prox}=\mathrm{prox}(\chi^{*},\tilde{\chi},z^{*},z).

(77)

Then the fine-tuning replicon condition is

\displaystyle\alpha^{\prime}\,\mathbb{E}\,\left\|(v\nabla^{2}\psi(\mathrm{prox};\chi^{*},\tilde{\chi},z^{*},z))^{-1}+I_{T}\right\|_{F}^{2}<1\ .

(78)

The derivation is done by considering the approximate message passing (AMP) algorithm associated to the distribution on
w
induced by the fine-tuning loss
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
. The AMP is stated in
[
22
]
, for a generic class of sequence multi-index models. We specialize it to our case for the indices
\chi,\chi^{*}
and take in account the supplementary noise
z,z^{*}
from the pre-training. The replicon condition is then obtained by considering a linear perturbation of the AMP around its fixed-point and checking whether it is amplified or not. The computation follows steps similar to
[
38

.

A.8
Catastrophic forgetting: performances of the fine-tuned model on the pre-training data

One can evaluate the performances of the fine-tuned model on the original pre-training loss. Considering a new test pre-training sample
X,y
, it reads

\mathcal{E}^{\prime\prime}(\hat{W},\hat{w})=\mathbb{E}_{(X,y)\sim\mathcal{P}}\left[D^{-1}||\hat{y}(X;\hat{W},\hat{w})-y||_{F}^{2}\,\big|\,\mathcal{S},\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\right]

(83)

In the frame of our theory, this quantity admits an asymptotic characterization, similarly to Result
3
. Taking
(z^{*},z)\sim\mathcal{Q}
and
(\chi^{*},\chi)\sim\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
we have

\mathcal{E}^{\prime\prime}(\hat{W},\hat{w})=\mathbb{E}\,||\sigma(z+\chi\chi^{\top}-qI_{T})-\sigma(z^{*})||_{F}^{2}\ .

(84)

We expect this error
\mathcal{E}^{\prime\prime}
to be higher than the pre-training test error
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
because of the supplementary learned term
\chi\chi^{\top}-qI_{T}
that acts like noise. This is reminiscent of the catastrophic forgetting phenomenon.

Finite-dimensional recalibration protocol.

We consider the regime
\Delta=0.5,\alpha=0.1,\alpha^{\prime}=3,T=3,\kappa_{0}=\kappa=1
,

\lambda

For each value of
\lambda

\hat{W}
at finite dimension using LBFGS and compute the recalibration factor
\beta^{\star}=\frac{M}{Q}
,
where
(Q,M)
are the corresponding pre-training state-evolution order parameters. The frozen representation is then rescaled according to
\hat{W}\mapsto\sqrt{\beta}\,\hat{W}
,
with

\beta\in\left\{0.7\beta^{\star},\;0.85\beta^{\star},\;\beta^{\star},\;1.15\beta^{\star},\;1.30\beta^{\star}\right\}.

10

250

30
