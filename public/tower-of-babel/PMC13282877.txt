Commenting on “a randomized trial of continuous positive airway pressure for the asthma of obesity” by Anne E. Dixon and colleagues
Authors: Lisha Kuang, Pantao Shu
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We read with interest the article entitled: “A randomized trial of continuous positive airway pressure for the asthma of obesity" [1] by Anne E. Dixon and colleagues. The authors evaluated the clinical useful of Continuous Positive Airway Pressure (CPAP) for patients with comorbid obesity and asthma (obese asthma).

The correlation between obesity and asthma has been one of the research hotspots in recent years [2]. Both clinical studies [3] and genetic research [4] have confirmed that obesity is closely associated with asthma exacerbation. However, there remains a long way to go in translating these findings into clinical practice. It appears that only the treatment of obesity itself (weight loss) constitutes an effective strategy for obese asthma [5].

Previous studies suggest that obesity may exacerbate asthma through the following mechanisms: ① mechanical effects including restriction of the thoracic cavity by adipose tissue, central airway expiratory collapse, and reduced lung capacity; ② non-mechanical factors including oxidative stress, nitric oxide, inflammation, and infection [3]. Regarding the mechanical effects of obesity on asthma, this study [1] proposed a compelling hypothesis: the reduction in lung capacity and central airway collapse caused by obesity maybe closely associated with the tendency for exacerbation of asthma. Therefore, CPAP may alleviate obese asthma by increasing lung capacity and supporting the airways. The application of CPAP represents a potentially valuable interventions in clinical practice for obese asthma.

However, the study failed to draw a definitive conclusion. It found that CPAP was beneficial only for obesity alone but showed no significant improvement in obese asthma. We propose the following analysis related to this result.

First, improvement in patients with obesity alone by CPAP was consistent with theoretical predictions and previous research findings. CPAP provides positive airway pressure throughout the entire respiratory cycle, thereby supporting the airways and alleviating the upper airway obstruction and decreased lung volume caused by obesity. Previous studies have also demonstrated that CPAP has definitive therapeutic effects on obesity-related hypoventilation syndrome and sleep apnea syndrome [6].

Secondly, regarding the poor efficacy of CPAP in obese asthma, the authors propose the following potential reasons: small airway collapse (mainly), limited case numbers, inconsistent baseline data across groups, insufficient CPAP duration, and the need for further exploration of CPAP pressure. We agree that the pathophysiological characteristics of asthma itself may be the primary cause of the unsatisfactory outcome. Patients with obesity alone may also experience small airway collapse and dysfunction, but the severity and pathogenesis differ from those with obesity and asthma [3]. Asthma is primarily characterized by chronic nonspecific airway inflammation. This inflammation leads to widespread small airway narrowing, resulting in expiratory dyspnea, alveolar hyperinflation, and endogenous PEEP [7]. Considered from lung function data, the study from Dixon et al. also indicated greater air-trapping at baseline in obese asthma. Under these conditions, CPAP may fail to reduce AHR effectively.

We propose that the following improvements for future studies.

Besides CPAP, Biphasic Positive Airway Pressure (BiPAP) should be considered in obese asthma populations.

Besides CPAP, Biphasic Positive Airway Pressure (BiPAP) should be considered in obese asthma populations.

Firstly, BiPAP has the same advantage of CPAP. The pathophysiological basis of asthma is the abnormal contraction of airway smooth muscle triggered by various irritants. Through maintaining the airway smooth muscle in a continuously stretched state, CPAP may improve the excitation-contraction coupling characteristics of airway smooth muscle, while the BiPAP mode, which also employs positive pressure ventilation, may confer similar benefits. Additionally, like CPAP, BiPAP has the same effect to counteract intrinsic positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEPi).

Moreover, BiPAP may be more efficient at reducing the work of breathing compared to CPAP, and that maybe beneficial for AHR in asthma.

CPAP only provides a ‘bracing’ effect, maintaining airway patency throughout the respiratory cycle, and its role is passive. BiPAP, on the other hand, is an active assisted ventilation mode that offers both ‘bracing’ and ‘assisted breathing.’ While maintaining airway patency, it helps patients complete inhalation with less effort. Since it better aligns with respiratory physiology, BiPAP may restore smooth muscle to its normal physiological state and achieve the desired effect of relaxing airway smooth muscles [8, 9].

Although not in the acute exacerbation phase, the asthma patients enrolled in the study exhibited poorer pulmonary function compared to the control group, characterized by lower FEV1 and higher residual volume. That suggests potentially increased respiratory work and heavier load on the respiratory muscles; Furthermore, it is undeniable that patients with obesity-associated asthma bear a heavier respiratory burden compared to those with pure asthma or obesity. Under these circumstances, the application of BiPAP may yield more benefits.

There has another indirect evidence suggest that reducing respiratory muscle load may be beneficial in alleviating AHR in asthma: compare with CPAP, BiPAP is more efficient in alleviating asthma exacerbations and prognosis [7]. That imply that BiPAP may be beneficial in restoring of ASM function, and also may alleviate AHR in asthma.

Besides work of breathing, contraction-relaxation rhythm of ASM should also be considered. The loss of the inherent contraction-relaxation rhythm in respiratory smooth muscle, leading to excessive contractions or prolonged contraction duration, is a key factor in the onset or exacerbation of asthma [10]. BiPAP maintains ASM relaxation through positive airway pressure while inducing a more physiological breathing rhythm, potentially enabling ASM to gradually restore its normal contraction-relaxation rhythm under lower load. This may facilitate the recovery of airway hyperresponsiveness (AHR).

Based on the above analysis, BiPAP appears to be more suitable for the treatment of obesity-related asthma.

Given that most asthma patients can achieve effective symptom relief with conventional treatment without NPPV, we recommend that study subjects should meet the following criteria: severe obesity affecting respiratory function, unsatisfactory response to conventional asthma therapy, and significant comorbidities associated with obesity (e.g., Asthma-OSA overlap syndrome [11, 12].

Given that most asthma patients can achieve effective symptom relief with conventional treatment without NPPV, we recommend that study subjects should meet the following criteria: severe obesity affecting respiratory function, unsatisfactory response to conventional asthma therapy, and significant comorbidities associated with obesity (e.g., Asthma-OSA overlap syndrome [11, 12].

Current clinical studies have demonstrated that NPPV can provide short-term benefits for severe asthma, including symptom improvement and enhanced lung function. However, the precise application protocols, optimal timing of implementation, and long-term efficacy of NPPV remain uncertain, requiring larger sample sizes and more rigorous clinical trials [13].

Current clinical studies have demonstrated that NPPV can provide short-term benefits for severe asthma, including symptom improvement and enhanced lung function. However, the precise application protocols, optimal timing of implementation, and long-term efficacy of NPPV remain uncertain, requiring larger sample sizes and more rigorous clinical trials [13].

In summary, the efficacy of CPAP as a treatment for obesity-asthma syndrome cannot be conclusively determined based on current findings. Further validation through more stringent and persuasive experimental designs is warranted. Future research should focus on selection of study subjects, design of groupings, and the choice of non-invasive ventilation modes.
