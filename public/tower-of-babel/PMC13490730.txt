MicroRNA profiling in serum and correlation with tumor tissue and clinicopathological parameters in periampullary carcinoma
Authors: Vaibhav Kumar Varshney, Shailja Sharma, Sudha Anjali, Muna Afroz Shaikh, Balakrishnan Selvakumar, Peeyush Varshney, Lokesh Agarwal, Subhash Soni, Shilpi Gupta Dixit, Sundeep Singh Saluja
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Backgrounds/AimsMicroRNAs (miRNAs) have emerged as biomarkers for cancer diagnosis and progression. This pilot study evaluated whether circulating miRNAs correlate with tumor tissue expression and clinicopathological parameters in periampullary carcinoma (PACA) patients.MethodsThirty-nine PACA patients who underwent pancreatoduodenectomy (April 2021–March 2024) were enrolled. Total RNA from preoperative serum and tumor tissue was isolated using the miRNeasy kit (Qiagen). Quantitative reverse-transcription polymerase chain reaction was performed for 14 PACA-related miRNAs, with U6 small nuclear RNA as a reference (ΔCt = CtmiRNA – CtU6 for normalization); relative expression was calculated as ΔΔCt = ΔCtcases – ΔCtcontrols.ResultsThe study comprised 28 male patients with median age of 56 years (interquartile range 51–63). The predominant tumor location was ampulla (43.6%), followed by distal common bile duct (23.1%). Serum ΔCt analysis showed significant differences for miR-199, miR-31, miR-329, and miR-19, with downregulation of miR-195 and miR-375; after false discovery rate adjustment, miR-199, miR-31, miR-329, miR-195 and miR-375 remained significant, whereas the serum miR-19 difference was only nominal (p = 0.050). In tumor tissue, miR-199, miR-329, and miR-19 were upregulated (ΔΔCt −2.75, −0.87, and −1.82, respectively), while miR-31 was downregulated (ΔΔCt 2.27) compared with controls. Serum miR-19 ΔCt showed a weak correlation with CA 19-9 (Spearman’s ρ = 0.31, p = 0.049). Subgroup analyses revealed no significant differences in miRNA expression across histological subtypes or tumor stages.ConclusionsSerum miR-199 and miR-19 showed exploratory concordance between circulating and tumor tissue expression in PACA, but their clinical utility remains limited and requires validation in a larger cohort.

INTRODUCTION

Periampullary carcinomas (PACAs) are a heterogeneous group of neoplasms that arise within 2 cm of the ampulla of Vater, originating from the ampulla, distal common bile duct (CBD), duodenum, or pancreatic duct. The prognosis of PACA depends on its histological subtype and tumor origin. Given that these anatomic sites differ in histology, molecular alterations, and clinical behavior, it is unlikely that a single biomarker will perform uniformly across all periampullary primary tumors [1]. Conventional serum tumor markers, particularly carbohydrate antigen 19-9 (CA 19-9) and carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), have limited utility for prognostication in PACA due to their low sensitivity and specificity, especially in cases of biliary obstruction [2]. This limitation highlights the need for alternative biomarkers that can predict prognosis and survival outcomes in patients with PACA.

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are small, non-coding RNAs, 18–24 nucleotides in length, that post-transcriptionally regulate gene expression by binding to messenger RNA molecules via nucleotide complementarity [3]. These molecules regulate critical cellular processes, including differentiation, proliferation, apoptosis, and metastasis [4]. Importantly, miRNAs remain stable and analyzable in formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissues and circulating blood, making them suitable for both tissue- and serum-based analysis [3].

Recent studies have demonstrated differential miRNA expression patterns in PACA tissues compared with normal tissues. Kalluri et al. [5] identified upregulated expression of miR-17, 18a, 18b, 19, 20a, 31, 99, 154, 192, 194, 196a, 196b, 199, 203, 329, 375, 378, 548, and 625 in PACA tissues. Subsequently, Murali Manohar et al. [6] demonstrated increased expression of plasma miRNAs-31, 192, 194, 196a, and 375. They noted a positive correlation between miRNA 192 and tumor tissue expression, as well as CA19-9, suggesting improved sensitivity and specificity for predicting tumor characteristics. Similarly, Sandhu et al. [7] reported upregulation of miR-95 and miR-592 and downregulation of miR-196b and miR-497 in the pancreatobiliary type. In contrast, miR-127 and miR-377 were associated with the intestinal type and linked to improved overall survival and recurrence-free survival. However, the comprehensive correlation between circulating miRNAs, tumor tissue expression, and clinicopathological parameters in PACA remains unclear.

Furthermore, in this pilot study, we evaluated PACA as a pooled disease spectrum to explore whether selected circulating miRNAs show overall serum–tissue concordance. This exploration could justify more granular, site-stratified analyses in larger cohorts. We aimed to evaluate the expression of circulating miRNAs differentially expressed in the serum of patients with PACA, correlate them with tumor tissue expression and clinicopathological parameters, and assess their potential as non-invasive biomarkers for PACA evaluation.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This prospective observational study was conducted at the Department of Surgical Gastroenterology in collaboration with the Department of Biochemistry at the All India Institute of Medical Sciences, Jodhpur (Rajasthan), India from April 2021 to March 2024. Consecutive patients who underwent pylorus-resecting pancreatoduodenectomy with histopathologically confirmed PACA and R0 resection were included. Patients with palliative or R2 resection, concomitant acute or chronic pancreatitis, or those who received neoadjuvant therapy were excluded. As this was a pilot study, a formal sample size calculation was not performed; instead, sequential patients who fulfilled the inclusion criteria were recruited. The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee at AIIMS, Jodhpur (reference number: AIIMS/IEC/2020/3219) and conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to inclusion. The study was designed and reported in accordance with the STROBE guidelines.

Preoperative evaluation included a detailed clinical history and physical examination. Routine laboratory investigations, including hemograms, liver function tests, and coagulation profiles, were performed. Imaging studies included contrast-enhanced computed tomography in the pancreatic protocol, and endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography with tissue biopsy, if possible, for diagnosis. Serum tumor markers (CA 19-9 and CEA) were measured preoperatively in all patients after biliary drainage.

Histopathological examination of pancreatoduodenectomy specimens was performed according to standard protocols. Tumor classification and staging followed the 8th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer classification [8]. Histological subtype assessment (intestinal vs. pancreatobiliary) was based on the World Health Organization classification and an immunohistochemistry panel using CDX2, CK20, and MUC2 for the intestinal variant, and CK7, MUC1, and MUC4 for the pancreatobiliary variant. Furthermore, all periampullary tumor sites were analyzed as a pooled cohort because the numbers in each subgroup were too small for adequately powered origin-specific comparisons.

Venous blood samples were collected in plain vacutainer tubes from both PACA patients and controls during their preoperative workup. After clotting at room temperature, samples were processed using a standardized two-step centrifugation protocol. The clarified serum was then aliquoted and stored at –80°C until analysis. Serum samples were visually inspected, and grossly hemolyzed samples were discarded before RNA extraction; no dedicated biochemical or miRNA-based hemolysis index was applied.

Fresh tumor tissue was collected from the resected specimen immediately after surgery and stored at –80°C. The control group comprised 30 patients undergoing abdominal surgery for benign or non-periampullary malignant indications, specifically: symptomatic cholelithiasis (n = 14), chronic pancreatitis (n = 9), choledochal cyst (n = 5), and choledocholithiasis (n = 2). Macroscopically normal pancreatic, duodenal, or bile duct tissue and preoperative serum from these individuals served as tissue and serum controls, respectively. Although not individually matched, controls were frequency-matched for age (median 52 years, interquartile range [IQR] 47–61) and sex (21 male patients). Total RNA was isolated from the tumor tissue and serum using the miRNeasy miRNA isolation kit (QIAGEN) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA quality and quantity were assessed using a NanoDrop One spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Only samples with acceptable spectrophotometric purity (A260/280 between approximately 1.8 and 2.1) and sufficient RNA yield were included in downstream quantitative reverse-transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) assays.

The isolated miRNAs were reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using the Exiqon LNA RT kit (QIAGEN). Real-time qRT-PCR was performed using Qiagen/Exiqon-based miRNA assays targeting 14 miRNAs previously reported to be differentially expressed in PACA: miR-17, miR-18, miR-19, miR-31, miR-99, miR-127-3p, miR-192, miR-194, miR-195, miR-196a, miR-199, miR-329, miR-375, and miR-548 [6,7]. All qRT-PCR reactions were run in technical duplicates. Assays were repeated if replicate Ct values differed markedly or if amplification curves were atypical. Median Ct values from concordant duplicates were used for analysis.

U6 snRNA was used as the endogenous reference control because prior work demonstrated its suitability for circulating miRNA normalization. In our cohort, U6 Ct values did not differ significantly between PACA cases and controls and showed low inter-sample variability in both serum and tissue [9].

For each sample, ΔCt was calculated as the difference between the Ct of the target miRNA and that of U6 (ΔCt = CtmiRNA – CtU6). Lower ΔCt values indicated higher miRNA expression, whereas higher ΔCt values suggested lower expression. Relative expression between groups was calculated as ΔΔCt = ΔCtcases – ΔCtcontrols; thus, a negative ΔΔCt value indicates higher miRNA expression in cases compared with controls, and fold change was derived as 2-ΔΔCt.

Postoperative follow-up included clinical evaluation, imaging (contrast-enhanced CT), and tumor marker assessment every 3 months for the first year. The median follow-up period was 18 months (range: 6–34 months).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as medians with IQRs, and categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Comparisons between groups were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test. Spearman correlation coefficients were calculated using ΔCt values to assess the relationship between serum miRNA expression and tumor-related factors such as serum CA 19-9 levels, bilirubin levels, and tumor stage. Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied to the 14 serum miRNAs and for correlations with CA 19-9. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were constructed using serum ΔCt values for each miRNA to discriminate PACA cases (n = 39) from controls (n = 30). Areas under the curve (AUCs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), optimal ΔCt cut-offs (maximizing the Youden index), and corresponding sensitivities and specificities were calculated using SPSS version 21.0 (IBM Corp.). Because lower ΔCt values indicate higher miRNA expression, values below the ΔCt cut-off were classified as PACA, and values above the cut-off as controls for miRNAs that were upregulated in PACA. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

RESULTS

The study cohort comprised 39 patients (28 males and 11 females) with a median age of 56 years (IQR 51–63). Tumor locations included the ampulla of vater (43.6%), distal CBD (23.1%), pancreatic head (17.9%), and duodenum (15.4%). Histological subtypes included intestinal-type (33.3%) and pancreatobiliary-type (66.7%). Tumor staging demonstrated T1–T2 in 20 patients (51.3%) and T3 in 19 (48.7%), and lymph node involvement (N1–N2) was observed in 29 patients (74.4%). The differentiation grade distribution included well differentiated (n = 4, 10.3%), moderately differentiated (n = 28, 71.8%), and poorly differentiated (n = 7, 17.9%). The median tumor size was 2.2 cm (IQR 1.8–2.8), and the median serum CA 19-9 was 72 U/mL (IQR 28.7–299.5). Demographic and perioperative parameters are presented in Table 1.

Out of 14 miRNAs analyzed in serum samples, significant upregulation was observed for miR-199 (median ΔCt 9.17 vs. 12.17 controls), miR-31 (median ΔCt 5.78 vs. 7.59 controls), miR-329 (median ΔCt 10.16 vs. 11.18 controls), and miR-19 (median ΔCt 9.75 vs. 10.89 controls), with significant downregulation of miR-195 (median ΔCt 7.16 vs. 5.25 controls) and miR-375 (median ΔCt 10.16 vs. 6.12 controls). After FDR adjustment, miR-199, miR-31, miR-329, miR-195, and miR-375 remained statistically significant, whereas the serum miR-19 difference showed a borderline adjusted p-value of 0.050 (Table 2). In tumor tissues, miR-199, miR-329, and miR-19 demonstrated clear upregulation, with ΔΔCt values of –2.75, –0.87, and –1.82, respectively, indicating higher tumor-associated expression. Conversely, miR-31 showed a ΔΔCt value of 2.27, confirming its downregulation in tissues relative to the controls (Table 3). Similarly, comparison of serum and tissue miRNA ΔΔCt showed strong upregulation of miR-19 and miR-199 at both sites (Table 4).

Spearman’s rho correlation analysis revealed a statistically significant positive correlation between serum miR-19 and serum CA 19-9 levels (ρ = 0.31, p = 0.049). However, this positive correlation between ΔCt (miR-19) and CA 19-9 indicates a weak inverse association between miR-19 expression and CA 19-9 levels. Further, after FDR adjustment, the correlation between miR-19 and CA 19-9 did not reach the adjusted significance threshold. The other three miRNAs (miR-199, miR-329, and miR-31) showed no statistically significant correlation with CA 19-9 (Table 5).

Diagnostic performance analysis using ROC curves showed that serum miR-19 and miR-199 had a modest ability to discriminate PACA patients from controls, with AUCs of 0.74 (95% CI 0.55–0.81) and 0.72 (95% CI 0.50–0.78), respectively. At the optimal ΔCt cut-offs of 4.50 for miR-19 and 5.20 for miR-199, sensitivities were 72.2% and 69.2%, and specificities were 63.8% and 60.3%, respectively. In contrast, miR-31 and miR-329 yielded lower AUCs of 0.55 (95% CI 0.40–0.70) and 0.65 (95% CI 0.37–0.67), with sensitivities of 58.9% and 61.5% and specificities of 55.3% and 50.6%, respectively, indicating limited standalone diagnostic value (Fig. 1; Supplementary Table 1).

miRNA expression by histological subtype, tumor stage, and lymph node status

Exploratory subgroup analyses are summarized in Supplementary Table 2–4. miR-199 showed numerically higher expression in intestinal-type tumors; however, the small differences in miR-329, miR-19, and miR-31 between the intestinal and pancreatobiliary types were not statistically significant. Similarly, miR-19 showed slightly higher expression in T3–T4 tumors than in T1–T2 tumors, but this trend was not significant (p = 0.142), and miR-329 and miR-199 were not associated with the T stage. Finally, none of the assessed miRNAs demonstrated significant differences between node-negative and node-positive disease, indicating no association with nodal involvement in this cohort.

DISCUSSION

This study evaluated 14 circulating miRNAs in a pooled cohort of patients with PACA, correlating their serum expression with tumor tissue and clinicopathological features. miR-199 and, to a lesser extent, miR-19, showed concordant upregulation in both tissue and serum. In contrast, miR-31 was upregulated only in serum, while miR-329 exhibited modest tissue-serum concordance. These patterns suggest a tumor-related origin for miR-19 and miR-199, whereas circulating miR-31 may partly reflect stromal or systemic responses rather than direct tumor cell expression.

The parallel upregulation of miR-19, miR-199, and miR-329 in both circulating serum and tumor tissue suggests that at least a portion of the circulating signal for these miRNAs is tumorderived. This consistent serum–tissue pattern, particularly for miR-199, aligns with previous studies indicating that circulating miRNA levels often mirror tumor tissue expression and can serve as surrogate markers of tumor burden [5-7,10]. Therefore, the elevated levels of miR-199 and miR-329, along with a modest serum signal for miR-19 in preoperative serum, support a biologically plausible link between circulating and tumor tissue levels. However, the observed AUC values (all ≤ 0.68) indicate only modest discrimination, suggesting these miRNAs are not suitable as standalone diagnostic tests.

miR-19 is a key component of the oncogenic miR-17-92 cluster, which has been extensively characterized as promoting cellular proliferation and inhibiting apoptosis, thereby reinforcing its role in tumor aggressiveness [11,12]. Previous work has also implicated miR-19, miR-199, miR-329, and miR-31 in oncogenic and microenvironmental pathways across gastrointestinal malignancies [13-18]. Our pilot dataset, however, was not designed to test specific mechanisms, and the present analyses cannot establish causal roles for these miRNAs in periampullary carcinogenesis. Instead, our findings should be interpreted primarily as evidence of serum-tissue concordance for miR-199 and miR-19 and of only modest discriminatory ability on ROC analysis (AUCs ≤ 0.74). These findings provide hypothesis-generating signals that warrant confirmation in larger, mechanistically focused studies.

In this cohort, serum miR-19 showed a weak correlation with CA 19-9 (ρ = 0.31, p = 0.049), suggesting that these markers may capture partly overlapping but not identical information. Because lower ΔCt values reflect higher miRNA expression, the positive correlation between ΔCt (miR-19) and CA 19-9 corresponds to a weak inverse association between miR-19 expression and CA 19-9 levels. After FDR correction, this association was no longer statistically significant, indicating that the correlation should be interpreted cautiously and requires validation in larger cohorts with prespecified adjustment for multiple comparisons. Although previous work by Murali Manohar et al. [6] suggested that combining specific miRNAs with CA 19-9 may improve diagnostic performance in PACA, our study did not demonstrate any clear improvement in discrimination over CA 19-9 alone, and any added value of incorporating miR-19 into a multimarker panel remains purely exploratory.

Finally, we did not observe a significant correlation between the studied miRNAs and bilirubin levels, which may suggest their relative independence from cholestasis and potential utility in patients with obstructive jaundice, where the performance of CA 19-9 is known to be limited. However, given the small sample size, modest AUCs, and lack of external validation, these observations should be regarded as preliminary and require confirmation in larger, prospectively designed studies. The trend toward a correlation between miR-199 and CA 19-9 (ρ = 0.25, p = 0.128), although not statistically significant, may reflect insufficient statistical power in the current cohort or different kinetics of miRNA and traditional tumor marker release into circulation. The lack of a significant correlation between miR-329 and CA 19-9 suggests that these markers may reflect distinct biological phenomena and provide independent biological or clinical information.

Analysis stratified by histological subtype (intestinal vs. pancreatobiliary type) revealed minimal differences in miRNA expression between subtypes, in contrast to previous observations by Sandhu et al. [7], who reported distinct miRNA signatures associated with different PACA histological types. This discordance may reflect cohort-specific factors, differences in tissue sampling methodologies, or the complex relationship between tissue levels and circulating miRNA expression. Larger prospective studies designed to investigate subtype-specific miRNA signatures are required.

The trend of higher miR-19 expression in advanced tumor stages (T3–T4) suggests its potential as a stage-related biomarker. However, the absence of a statistically significant link between other miRNAs and the T stage may be due to the limited sample size and requires validation in a larger group. Additionally, monitoring circulating miRNA levels over time during neoadjuvant therapy and after resection may offer insights into their potential to detect disease recurrence [10,14].

The identification of upregulated serum miRNAs provides preliminary evidence for their potential inclusion in multimodal biomarker panels for PACA detection alongside conventional tumor markers. The non-invasive nature of serum-based miRNA assessment makes it especially appealing for preoperative risk stratification, predicting chemotherapy response, and postoperative surveillance for disease recurrence [14]. However, the technique and cost involved in their analysis currently limit their role. Nonetheless, with technological advancements and increased use, the speed and costs of the process are expected to decrease in the near future.

This study has several important limitations. First, the small cohort size precluded the construction of multivariable models and the inclusion of an independent validation set, thereby limiting statistical power and generalizability. Second, pooling and analyzing all periampullary sites, while reflecting real-world case-mix, may obscure site-specific miRNA signatures. Third, the absence of patient-matched normal tissue prevented direct paired comparisons of PACA tissue miRNA expression with normal tissue. Furthermore, paired postoperative or longitudinal serum samples were not collected, which prevented the assessment of perioperative kinetics or recurrence detection. Future studies should incorporate serial sampling to clarify whether miR-199 and miR-19 are true drivers of tumor behavior or merely correlate with tumor burden. Despite these limitations, our findings provide proof-of-concept evidence for circulating miRNAs as complementary biomarkers in PACA.

In conclusion, this pilot study demonstrates that miR-199 and miR-19 are concordantly upregulated in both the serum and tumor tissue of patients with PACA. This supports a biologically plausible serum-tissue link, rather than establishing definitive biomarkers. However, the diagnostic performance of these individual miRNAs is modest, and the association between serum miR-19 and CA 19-9 is weak. Therefore, these findings should be regarded as exploratory, hypothesis-generating data that may inform the design of future multimodal biomarker panels. Larger, site-stratified longitudinal studies with independent validation and functional experiments are needed before miR-199 or miR-19 can be incorporated into routine clinical decision-making.
