Mean and Cut-Off Values of the Corrected QT Interval From Childhood to Adolescence
Authors: Hiroshi Suzuki, Junichi Ozawa, Yoko M. Nakao, Koji Kawakami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe corrected QT interval (QTc) is essential for long QT syndrome (LQTS) screening. QTc values change with growth, and sex differences emerge at puberty. However, few longitudinal studies have assessed QTc changes across childhood and adolescence, and age- and sex-specific QTc cut-offs using Bazett’s and Fridericia’s formulas (QTcB and QTcF, respectively) near the onset of puberty have not been established.Methods and ResultsWe retrospectively analyzed electrocardiograms from school-based screening. QT and RR intervals were measured manually, and QTcB and QTcF were calculated in 4th graders (n=2,753); subsets were additionally assessed in 1st graders (n=1,289) and 7th graders (n=1,351). Longitudinal changes in QTc were evaluated. QTc cut-offs for LQTS screening were determined using both the 99.5th percentile and histogram assessment. No significant sex difference in mean QTcB and QTcF values was found in 1st graders. These values in 4th graders were significantly shorter in males than in females (QTcB: 374±20 vs. 378±21 ms; QTcF: 389±23 vs. 395±24 ms; P<0.001). QTcB cut-offs (males/females) for 1st, 4th, and 7th graders were 453/448, 449/462, and 463/462 ms, respectively. The QTcF cut-off values (males/females) for 1st, 4th, and 7th graders were 425/436, 438/438, and 440/443 ms, respectively.ConclusionsSex differences in QTc emerge in the 4th grade. Age- and sex-specific QTc cut-offs are required for screening in children and adolescents.

Central Figure

Long QT syndrome (LQTS) is a major cause of sudden cardiac death in young individuals, primarily due to ventricular arrhythmias. LQTS is characterized by the prolongation of the heart rate (HR)-corrected QT interval (QTc), which serves as a key diagnostic marker.

QTc values change with growth in healthy individuals and patients with LQTS,1 and sex differences emerge at puberty.2 However, there have been few longitudinal studies on QTc changes across childhood and adolescence. Puberty generally begins at approximately 9–10 years of age, which is a critical developmental period,3 but standard values of QTc for this age group are limited. Although an electrocardiogram (ECG) is performed in 4th graders (aged 9–10 years) as part of the Japanese school-based screening programs for identifying cardiovascular disease, no clear QTc cut-off values have been established for detecting QTc prolongation at this age.4

Although there is no universally accepted method for calculating QTc, Bazett’s and Fridericia’s formulas are the most widely used in children and adolescents. Bazett’s formula is incorporated into the diagnostic criteria for LQTS in guidelines5,6 and a consensus statement.7 Fridericia’s formula is used in primary screening for QTc prolongation in the Japanese school-based screening programs8 and is also recommended by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration.9

In this study, we aimed to determine QTc changes from childhood to adolescence and determine age- and sex-specific QTc cut-off values during those periods. To achieve these objectives, we longitudinally evaluated QTc values corrected using Bazett’s and Fridericia’s formulas (QTcB and QTcF, respectively) in participants of our school-based screening programs to identify cardiovascular disease in children in the 4th grade and adjacent grades (1st and 7th grades).

Methods

Study Design

A retrospective, population-based longitudinal study using ECGs and questionnaires was conducted on our school-based screening program to identify cardiovascular disease in Minamiuonuma City, Japan. In the screening program, all 1st, 4th, and 7th graders, aged 6–7, 9–10, and 12–13 years, respectively, underwent 12-lead resting ECG, and their guardians were asked to complete self-administered questionnaires. These questionnaires included information on episodes of palpitation, chest pain, and loss of consciousness, a history of cardiac disease or Kawasaki disease, and a family history of sudden cardiac death or unknown origin of sudden death at <40 years of age.

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Niigata University Medical and Dental Hospital (reference nos. 2017-0276 and 2023-0088) and conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. The requirement for informed consent was waived because of the study’s retrospective nature. However, information regarding this study was disclosed on the Niigata University website to allow participants to opt out of the study.

Participants

A total of 2,787 4th graders (1,417 males and 1,370 females) underwent 12-lead resting ECG as part of our school-based screening program in Minamiuonuma City, Japan, between April 2018 and March 2023. We included 2,753 participants (1,400 males and 1,353 females) in the study after exclusion for the following reasons: those who declined to participate (n=0); those who had a history of heart disease other than LQTS (n=25); those who showed arrhythmias on ECG (e.g., rhythm disturbances of the P wave, such as wandering pacemaker or junctional rhythm, second- or third-degree atrioventricular block, intraventricular conduction disturbances, such as complete bundle branch block or Wolff–Parkinson–White syndrome, or premature atrial or ventricular beats; n=5); or those who had ECGs that were difficult to interpret (n=4). Of these, 1,289 participants (663 males and 626 females) who were in the 4th grade between 2021 and 2023 were additionally assessed in the 1st grade between 2018 and 2020. Furthermore, 1,351 participants (691 males and 660 females) who were in the 4th grade between 2019 and 2021 were additionally assessed in the 7th grade between 2022 and 2024.

Of the entire cohort, 5 participants were genetically diagnosed with LQTS (Table 1).10 In this study, 3 females with LQT2 were assessed only in the 4th grade in 2018, whereas 1 female with LQT1 and 1 male with LQT8 were additionally evaluated in the 1st and 7th grades, respectively. All 5 participants remained asymptomatic. Three females with LQT2 and 1 male with LQT8 were first identified as having a prolonged QTc during the school-based screening program in the 7th and 4th grades, respectively, and this was subsequently confirmed by genetic testing. The female with LQT1 was diagnosed during infancy through family screening. QT Interval Measurements and QTc Calculations

All ECGs were recorded at each school using a portable PC-based system (Fukuda Denshi, Tokyo, Japan) with a paper speed of 25 mm/s, an amplitude of 0.1 mV/mm, a sampling rate of 500 Hz, and a bandwidth of 0.25–35 Hz. The same electrocardiograph was used throughout the study period, and recordings were obtained annually between April and May. All ECGs were recorded by certified technicians. QT and RR intervals (QT and RR, respectively) were manually measured by a pediatric cardiologist (H.S.) at 200% magnification using on-screen digital calipers in publicly available software (ImageJ version 1.54k). QT and RR from 3 consecutive beats in lead II or lead V5 were measured. When neither lead II nor V5 was suitable, lead I was chosen. QT was measured using the tangent method. QTcB and QTcF values averaged over 3 consecutive beats were used for analysis.

Determination of QTc Cut-Off Values for Detecting QT Prolongation

Based on prior reports,4,11 QTc cut-off values were determined using both percentile-based methods and histogram assessment. The prevalence of LQTS has been reported to range from approximately 1 in 1,000 to 1 in 3,000.12–16 Accordingly, the 99.5th percentile (1/200) was calculated and adopted as the primary cut-off to reduce false-negative results in screening. Because extreme percentiles may be influenced by outliers, we additionally inspected QTc histograms and assessed for a ‘hiatus’, defined as a gap between adjacent QTc values without intervening subjects, within the range of approximately 1/100 to 1/500 of the population. This approach was used to support the final selection of cut-off values.

Statistical Analysis

To assess interobserver variability, 2 pediatric cardiologists (H.S. and J.O.) independently performed the same measurements on a random subset of 100 ECGs, and agreement between the 2 observers was evaluated using a Bland–Altman plot (Supplementary Figure). Sex differences within the same grade were analyzed using the unpaired Student’s t-test. Longitudinal comparisons between grades within the same cohort were assessed using the paired t-test. A 2-tailed P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were conducted using SPSS version 30.0 for Windows (IBM SPSS Inc., Armonk, NY, USA).

QTc Cut-Off Values for Detecting QTc Prolongation

The 99.5th percentile (1/200) values are presented in Table 2. Histograms of QTcB and QTcF for 4th-grade males and females are shown in Figures 2 and 3. The 99.5th percentile values were 449 ms for QTcB and 438 ms for QTcF in 4th-grade males, and 462 ms for QTcB and 438 ms for QTcF in 4th-grade females. Visual inspection of these distributions supported the appropriateness of the 99.5th percentile as the cut-off value. Histograms for 1st- and 7th-grade males and females are shown in Figures 4 and 5. In these groups, the 99.5th percentile was generally considered appropriate as a cut-off. However, in 1st-grade females (QTcB 466 ms) and 7th-grade males (QTcF 458 ms), the 99.5th percentile appeared to be influenced by outliers and was considered excessively high as a cut-off (Figures 4B-b,5A-d). In these subgroups, alternative thresholds corresponding to the presence of a hiatus within the 1/100–1/500 range were identified (QTcB 448 ms for 1st-grade females and QTcF 440 ms for 7th-grade males) and were considered more appropriate. Patients With LQTS

In the present cohort, 5 participants had a genetic diagnosis of LQTS. Using the cut-off values defined in this study, 1 male patient with LQT8 and 1 female patient with LQT1 were identified as having QTc prolongation by both correction methods (Table 1). In contrast, 3 patients with LQT2, who were evaluated in the 4th grade but not in the 7th grade in this study, were not identified as having QTc prolongation by either QTcB or QTcF in the 4th grade.

Discussion

Cut-Off Values for Detecting QTc Prolongation

The QTcB cut-off values of 4th graders identified in this study were close to the sex-specific values in the Schwartz score.17 The QTcF cut-off values of 4th graders were closer to those currently used for 7th graders than to those for 1st graders in the Japanese school-based screening programs in both sexes.8 In this study, we determined cut-off values for QTc among more than 1,000 participants from the general population of 4th graders (aged 9–10 years) using manual tangent measurements stratified by sex. To the best of our knowledge, no previous reports are comparable with this study. Most previous reports of reference QTc values in children and adolescents relied on automated measurements.18

Reports based on manual QTc measurements in children are limited. Vink et al. reported QTc values for genotype-negative family members identified through family screening.19 However, detailed age stratification was not provided in their report. Hazeki et al. studied a relatively large number of participants in the Japanese school-based screening program, but their cohort did not include 4th graders (aged 9–10 years).4

Although the number of participants in the 1st and 7th grades was smaller than that in the 4th grade, we also examined QTc cut-off values for these grades. Hazeki et al.4 reported QTcF values in 1st and 7th graders using manual measurements. On the basis of their findings, the Japanese school-based screening program adopted QTcF cut-off values of 430 ms for 1st graders and 440 ms for 7th graders in both sexes.8 The QTcF cut-off values identified in this study were largely consistent with these established values.

However, there are methodological differences in the determination of percentile values between the 2 studies. In our study, a percentile based on the actual distribution was adopted. In contrast, Hazeki et al. defined the QTc cut-off value using the estimated 99.975th percentile derived from the standard deviation.4 Because the distribution of QTc is skewed, cut-off values are recommended to be determined using the actual percentile distribution. Indeed, the percentile value used by Hazeki et al. to establish their QTc cut-off was lower than the reported prevalence of LQTS.4

We were unable to identify any studies that evaluated age- and sex-specific QTcB cut-off values in children and adolescents using manual measurements. Based on our findings, QTcB cut-off values of 450 ms for both sexes in 1st graders, and 450 ms for males and 460 ms for females in the 7th grade may be reasonable for practical use.

The standard deviation of QTc increased with age in both males and females, suggesting greater inter-individual variability with growth. Although LQTS was not specifically evaluated, QTc variability in patients with LQTS might also increase with age. Consequently, overlap in QTc values between healthy individuals and those with LQTS may become more pronounced during growth, potentially complicating differentiation.

In this study, QTc cut-off values were derived primarily from percentile-based analysis and were further refined by histogram assessment. Although percentile-based thresholds are commonly used to define cut-offs, extreme percentiles are more vulnerable to instability in smaller samples and to the influence of outliers. In our cohort, this issue was apparent in the smaller 1st- and 7th-grade subgroups where histogram assessment, including identification of a hiatus in the QTc distribution, helped refine the final cut-off values. Further validation of these cut-off values in larger and in independent cohorts is warranted.

Sex Differences in QTc

Sex differences in QTc values have been reported to emerge during puberty and are mainly affected by sex hormones.2 In this study, sex differences were already present from 4th grade (aged 9–10 years), suggesting that such differences could emerge as early as the onset of puberty. A study using automated QTcB measurements reported that sex differences occurred at 10–11 years of age, which is consistent with our findings.18 Similarly, a report on the Japanese school-based screening programs for 1st, 7th, and 10th graders demonstrated the development of sex differences in QTcF with growth.4 In that study, QT values showed little sex difference within each grade, whereas differences in RR emerged with growth, which influenced QTcF. This pattern is consistent with our results.

Longitudinal Changes in QTc

We longitudinally evaluated the mean QTc from 1st to 4th grades and from 4th to 7th grades. Mean values of QTcB remained unchanged in males but increased in females, whereas QTcF increased in both sexes, with a greater increment observed in females. Longitudinal studies of QTc during childhood and adolescence remain limited. A study that followed 1,473 preadolescents engaged in sports (aged 7–15 years) over 3 years showed no significant longitudinal changes in QTcB or QTcF in both sexes,20 which differs from our findings. This discrepancy between studies may be explained by the wide age range included in their cohort and the potential effect of exercise.

Although we were unable to identify any cross-sectional studies that evaluated age- and sex-specific QTcB values in children and adolescents using manual measurements, 2 relatively large studies reported mean QTcB values obtained through automated measurements.11,18 In one study, mean QTcB values did not differ between 1st- and 7th-grade females, whereas in males, mean QTcB values in 7th grade were 5 ms shorter than those in 1st grade.11 In another study that compared children aged 6–7 years with those aged 12–13 years, the mean QTcB values were 4 ms longer in older females and 3 ms shorter in older males.18 The finding that the increase in mean QTcB values from childhood to adolescence was greater in females than in males was consistent across both studies and aligns with our results. However, in both previous studies, the age- and sex-specific mean QTcB values were approximately 30 ms longer than those observed in this study. We speculate that this discrepancy between studies may be attributable to differences in measurement methodology.

In a previous cross-sectional study using manual tangent measurements, mean QTcF values were longer in 7th graders than in 1st graders in both sexes, with a more pronounced grade-related difference in females.4 The QTcF values reported in that study are generally consistent with those observed in this study.

Patients With LQTS

In this study, 3 female patients with LQT2 had QTc values below the cut-off in the 4th grade but were identified as having QTc prolongation at the 7th grade evaluation, which led to their diagnosis. QTc values are known to change with growth in healthy individuals and patients with LQTS, highlighting the limitations of relying on a single QTc measurement for LQTS screening. Repeated screenings and careful assessment of T-wave morphology are important for accurate detection of LQTS.

QT Measurements and QT Correction Formula

In this study, QT intervals were measured manually using the tangent method. Although the accuracy of automated measurements has improved in recent years, manual measurement remains the standard approach for diagnosing LQTS.19 QT values obtained using automated algorithms based on derivative methods tend to be longer than those measured using the tangent method, and their reliability may be reduced, particularly when T-wave morphology is difficult to delineate.21

We proposed screening cut-off values for both QTcB and QTcF. A previous study in general populations across childhood and adolescence has shown that QTcF provides a QT correction that is less dependent on HR than QTcB.4 Accordingly, QTcF may be advantageous for early screening in 4th graders and adjacent age groups. In contrast, QTcB has been extensively validated for the diagnosis and clinical evaluation of LQTS,22,23 and its use remains recommended.

Study Limitations

This study has some limitations. First, for the longitudinal assessment, the technicians performing ECG recordings differed from year to year. In addition, ECGs were obtained at individual schools, and variations in environmental conditions (e.g., annual differences in climate) indicate that recordings were not obtained under fully standardized conditions. Second, this study was conducted in a single municipality in Japan, and the majority of participants were Asian children from the same region. Therefore, the generalizability of our findings is limited, and caution is warranted when applying the proposed cut-off and mean QTc values to other ethnic populations.

Conclusions

Using longitudinal data from a large general population and manual QT measurements, we demonstrated that QTc values change with growth in a sex-specific manner during childhood and adolescence, with greater increases observed in females. Sex differences in QTc emerged as early as the 4th grade. Our findings indicate that age- and sex-specific QTc cut-offs are necessary for accurate QTc assessment in children and adolescents. Notably, the QTcF cut-off values identified for 4th graders were closer to those for 7th graders than to those for 1st graders in both sexes, providing clinically relevant reference values that may improve the accuracy of LQTS detection in children and adolescents.
