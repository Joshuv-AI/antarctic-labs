RNAlater-compatible protocol for nuclei isolation from radical prostatectomy prostate cancer resections, enabling single-nucleus resolution RNA-seq
Authors: Magdalena Julita Krystkiewicz-Orzechowska, Dorota Anusewicz, Katarzyna Kośla, Izabela Baryła, Agata Sakowicz, Michał Olczak, Mateusz Mroczek, Marek Lipiński, Andrzej K Bednarek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe present an optimised protocol for single-nucleus isolation from RNAlater-preserved prostate cancer (PC) resections that enables transcriptomically robust snRNA-seq. Our streamlined procedure employs bead-mill homogenization and high-salt lysis to generate nuclei suspensions, eliminating the need for FACS or density-gradient purification. The protocol yielded high-quality libraries (Q30 ≥ 90%) with minimal ambient RNA. Analysis of 3,365 nuclei identified eight distinct cellular compartments, preserving the full cellular repertoire, including luminal, stromal, and immune lineages. These findings demonstrate that snRNA-seq is feasible in RNAlater-preserved PC specimens enabling routine analysis of clinical samples and expanding the applicability of single-cell genomics in translational research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-57993-2.Keywords: snRNA-seq, Sequencing, Prostate cancer, Protocol, 10x Genomics, RNAlater, Single nuclei isolation, Transcriptomics, Heterogeneity, ProtocolSubject terms: Biological techniques, Cancer, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Oncology, Urology

Introduction

The ability to analyse gene expression at the single-cell level has revolutionised molecular research over the past decade. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has provided unprecedented insights into cellular heterogeneity within human tissues and has enhanced our understanding of complex diseases. scRNA-seq enables detailed exploration of the intricate transcriptomic landscapes of individual cells, revealing cellular dynamics that were previously inaccessible through traditional bulk RNA sequencing. However, this technique requires tissues to be processed into viable single-cell suspensions of limited size and shape, which is particularly challenging in clinical and surgical settings. Moreover, it relies on rapid enzymatic dissociation of fresh tissue, a process that can induce acute stress-response transcripts and systematically underrepresent fragile or highly adherent epithelial populations1. Single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) has emerged as a promising alternative to overcome these limitations. By profiling nuclear transcripts from frozen or chemically preserved material, it bypasses the aforementioned bottlenecks. In matched tumours, snRNA-seq recovers all major cell classes while preferentially retaining adherent epithelial and stromal states that are often lost with scRNA-seq2,3. snRNA-seq is therefore particularly advantageous for processing fixed or partially dissociated tissues, especially clinical samples that may not be immediately accessible for fresh processing.

Prostate cancer (PC) is the most commonly diagnosed malignancy in men across 118 countries4. The Lancet Commission reports that its annual incidence is projected to almost double, from 1.4 million cases in 2020 to 2.9 million by 2040, with a concomitant 85% increase in mortality over the same period5. PC research has advanced substantially with the advent of high-resolution genomic technologies, including scRNA-seq and snRNA-seq. PC is characterized by profound intratumoral heterogeneity arising from multifocal lesions and a complex interplay of diverse cell types and states. This cellular diversity underlies the disparate clinical trajectories observed among patients. Consequently, elucidating both intra- and inter-patient heterogeneity remains a key priority for precision oncology6. This includes dissecting shifts in androgen-receptor (AR) signalling, metabolic reprogramming and the emergence of neuroendocrine or therapy-persistent cell states. Capturing such complexity is though as crucial as the ability to isolate and analyse nuclear content from fixed tumor samples collected during radical prostatectomy.

To bridge the logistical gap between surgical sample collection and downstream genomic applications, RNAlater has become widely adopted in clinical biobanking. RNAlater is an aqueous, non-toxic tissue stabilization reagent that rapidly permeates tissues and precipitates RNases, thereby preserving RNA integrity at ambient temperatures7. Its primary clinical advantage is that it eliminates the immediate need for snap-freezing in liquid nitrogen or ultra-low-temperature freezers during tissue procurement. Consequently, it enables routine collection, transport, and long-term storage of high-quality biospecimens across standard pathology departments and multicentre clinical trials8. However, although RNAlater preservation is highly effective for bulk transcriptomics, its high-salt mechanism significantly hardens the extracellular matrix, making tissue dissociation into single-cell or single-nucleus suspensions technically challenging. Thus, despite the strong potential of snRNA-seq, protocols for processing RNAlater-preserved samples remain underdeveloped, and empirical evidence for their suitability and technical optimization is limited.

The number of protocols for processing PC resections for scRNA-seq or snRNA-seq is currently limited, and only a few snRNA-seq workflows accommodate RNAlater-preserved tissue. The fastest protocol to date, a 90-minute kidney biopsy workflow from Rousselle et al., recovers 16 renal cell types without fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) or ultracentrifugation, but has not been benchmarked in other tissues such as PC8. Collectively, these observations highlight a lack of standardised methods for processing PC samples, particularly those preserved in RNAlater, which is common in routine clinical practice.

To address these gaps, we have developed a high-salt, bead-based nuclei isolation protocol adapted for RNAlater-fixed prostate resections. This workflow processes sample in 150 min and omits FACS and density-gradient steps. In downstream snRNA-seq, we achieve over 90% Q30 bases, with a median of 490 genes detected per nucleus, comparable to published fresh-tissue standards. Our protocol faithfully preserves luminal, basal, transit-amplifying and stromal compartments, including metabolically reprogrammed AR-high nuclei. Thus, our results provide evidence for the suitability of snRNA-seq on RNAlater-stored PC resections and eliminate the need for extensive debris-removal procedures, as validated by downstream performance on the 10x Chromium platform.

This streamlined nuclei-isolation approach expands the feasibility of snRNA-seq in clinical samples that were previously difficult to process, thereby broadening the scope of single-cell genomics in biomedical research and clinical applications, particularly in PC studies.

Results

Nuclei isolation and yield, sequencing performance and QC metrics

The protocol presented herein describes a procedure for nuclei isolation from RNAlater-preserved human prostate tumor resections for 10x Chromium snRNA-seq. Although the use of RNAlater-preserved tissues in 10x single-cell assays has been considered questionable, the overall performance, challenges, and outcomes of this protocol are herein demonstrated. In-house modifications addressed tissue stiffness, and consequently its limited accessibility to reagents, thereby resolving low procedural efficiency, excessive nuclei aggregation and nuclei damage. This approach overcomes a major limitation of 10x single-cell technology, namely the requirement to maintain high levels of sample freshness and cell viability at high level until processing, and demonstrates that sample fixation is feasible, albeitnot without limitations.

Optimisation of nuclei isolation from RNAlater-preserved radical prostatectomy PC resections identified single-tube bead-mill homogenisation combined with high-salt lysis as the shortest and most efficient protocol among those tested (see Methods). This approach preserved nuclear integrity and yielded downstream snRNA-seq data with Illumina Q30 scores ≥ 90%. Sequencing libraries were generated from 5.4 ± 0.7 × 10⁶nuclei per 700 mg of tissue, comparable to outputs reported for high-throughput kidney and muscle workflows8,9.

Initial bioinformatic processing included removal of ambient RNA, which reduced the median contamination fraction to 2.8% without decreasing raw counts (Table 1). To define an appropriate threshold for mitochondrial content, we applied dynamic quality filtering (per sample, using 95th percentile of percent.mt and the 5th percentile of log10(genes ∕ UMI)). This retained 816 of 896 nuclei (28% of the 3,000 targeted GEMs) from the low-grade, pure peripheral zone Gleason 6 (3 + 3; GS6) sample and 2,549 of 2,693 nuclei (85% of the 3,000 targeted GEMs) from the high-grade, multi-zone Gleason 8 (4 + 4; GS8) sample (Table 1). In total, 3,365 nuclei isolated from resections of two treatment-naïve PC patients, preserved in RNAlater and processed using protocol were further profiled (Fig. 1).

A panel of violin plots (Figs. 2 and 3) summarises quality-relevant variables, including number of features (nFeature_RNA), RNA counts (nCount_RNA), and mitochondrial content (percent.mt) as well as biological markers of quality and cellular stress such as MALAT1 expression, oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) pathway activity, and three new modules (hereinafter referred to as ARset, NucQC, and TAC). Together, these modules capture molecular depth, potential technical artifacts and the biological state of nuclei. Scoring the OXPHOS pathway helps resolve ambiguity between true biological activity and contamination by focusing on active transcription. While high levels of mitochondria-encoded transcripts (e.g., MT-) in snRNA-seq often reflect ambient cytoplasmic RNA adhering to the nuclear membrane, the OXPHOS signature consists almost entirely of nucleus-encoded electron transport chain (ETC) subunits (e.g., NDUFA1, COX5A, SDHB). Detection of these nucleus-encoded transcripts within isolated nuclei represents nascent pre-mRNAs and mRNAs captured during active transcription before their physiological export to the cytoplasm. Thus, a robust nucleus-encoded OXPHOS score accompanying a high mitochondrial load supports the presence of a genuine, highly active metabolic transcriptional program at the time of fixation, rather than mere cytoplasmic contamination from a damaged specimen. ARset in turn confirms tumour-specific transcriptional activity, NucQC assesses physical nuclear integrity, and TAC ensures that QC does not bias against cycling cells collectively validating that the observed metabolic and proliferative differences represent biological signal, rather than noise. In addition, because mechanical dissociation of samples is known to induce an immediate stress response in single-cell datasets through genes such as HSPA1A, DNAJB1, FOS, and JUN, a Stress module score was calculated as a further quality indicator.

Median Stress scores remained around zero point in every cluster, with overlapping IQRs (Fig. 3). Despite a three-fold difference in total nuclei yield between the high-grade GS8 and low-grade GS6 samples (2,549 vs. 896 nuclei, respectively), both datasets exhibited highly consistent median gene detection (508 vs. 505 genes per nucleus). This metric, which reflects a combination of sequencing depth, library complexity, and technical stochasticity, indicates that the downstream droplet encapsulation and sequencing performance were stable and comparable across both specimens, irrespective of the initial nuclei input. GS8 nuclei displayed a broader distribution of mitochondrial RNA (median = 35%, IQR: 29–41%) than GS6 (median = 8%, IQR: 6–10%), yet remained below their respective 95th percentile thresholds and were retained for downstream analysis, hence confirming that inclusion of mitochondria-rich nuclei does not compromise overall data quality. Because elevated percent.mt may indicate either biological mitochondrial activity, or ambient contamination, such nuclei were explicitly retained and examined for their functional content in downstream pathway analysis. Importantly, this approach preserves critical metabolic signals that would otherwise be lost under a rigid 5–10% mitochondrial filter, often applied in sc/snRNA-seq pipelines (e.g10. Furthermore, elevated OXPHOS, ARset, and TAC scores in GS8 suggest metabolic reprogramming and an AR-driven, high-grade PC phenotype, rather than simple leakage of mitochondrial transcripts. NucQC scores were comparable between samples, providing evidence that GS8 nuclei are intact despite their higher mitochondrial content and OXPHOS signal. To explicitly validate our dynamic quality filtering, we examined nuclei that would be excluded under conventional rigid mitochondrial thresholds (> 10% percent.mt) (Supplementary Fig. 1). These nuclei did not show elevated apoptosis module scores, nor did they exhibit increased ambient RNA contamination as estimated by DecontX (Supplementary Fig. 1D-E). Instead, they displayed the highest expression of the nucleus-encoded OXPHOS module (Supplementary Fig. 1C). Together, these findings demonstrate that retaining mitochondria-rich compartments does not introduce technical artifacts or dying cells, but instead preserves highly viable, transcriptionally active tumor states.

Collectively, these observations demonstrate that bead-assisted homogenisation and high-ionic strength lysis can generate snRNA-seq libraries from RNAlater-preserved prostate tissue resections within < 150 min. Although a direct head-to-head comparison with freshly processed tissue was not performed in this study, the obtained sequencing quality metrics, such as library complexity (median > 500 genes per nucleus) and low ambient RNA contamination, closely align with established quality standards for fresh and flash-frozen prostate single-cell and single-nucleus RNA-seq datasets reported in the literature and further cross-validated in later analyses (tuong, chen). Unsupervised clustering, transcriptional programs and cross-dataset analysis position RNAlater nuclei alongside public scRNA-seq atlases

To validate the biological fidelity of nuclei isolated from RNAlater-preserved PC resections, we performed integrated transcriptomic clustering, reference-based cell type annotation, and molecular signature analysis. The eight transcriptomic clusters delineated from 3,365 nuclei recapitulate a pathologically coherent portrait of the two PC specimens (GS6, GS8) while highlighting grade-dependent shifts in epithelial, stromal and immune composition (Fig. 1). These clusters were initially assigned labels based on the ten most discriminative differentially expressed (DE) marker genes (Supplementary Table 1), then refined by anchor-based alignment with public PC datasets (Tuong et al.11, Chen et al.12 mapped against the Prostate Cell Atlas labels and ultimately corroborated with pathway enrichment (Supplementary Table 2) as well as commonly referenced markers from prostate atlases13–15 (Tables 2 and 3).

To rigorously assess cross-dataset concordance and refine these identities, we integrated our snRNA-seq data with public scRNA-seq prostate atlases (Tuong et al.11 and Chen et al.12. Post-integration UMAP analysis demonstrated sufficient structural alignment to map broad lineages across datasets, revealing a compartmentalized mixing dynamic (Fig. 4). Specifically, split UMAP projections and broad lineage annotations showed that the normal and malignant epithelial compartments exhibited partial spatial separation. As is commonly observed in single-cell cancer integrations, this reflects expected patient-specific tumor heterogeneity as well as intrinsic biological batch effects arising from cross-modality comparisons (isolated nuclei in our study versus reference whole cells). Conversely, where mutually present (e.g., within the Tuong et al. integration), the tumor microenvironment (TME) components, comprising stromal and immune cells, aligned much more cohesively across datasets without modality-driven spatial distinctness. Dataset composition analysis (Fig. 4, bottom row) quantitatively confirmed these differential mixing dynamics. Ultimately, despite the inherent modality differences and spatial shifts observed in the primary scRNA-seq comparison, automated reference label transfer using SingleR successfully assigned 94% of our nuclei to robust atlas-defined identities.

Clusters c0 and c1 (48% of nuclei) spanned the luminal-differentiation continuum (Supplementary Fig. 2). Cluster c0 exhibited androgen-regulated transcripts (KLK3, TMPRSS2, ERG), matching the LE-KLK3 lineage of the Prostate Cell Atlas and thus representing fully differentiated secretory epithelium. Cluster c1 retained AR activity but was additionally enriched for neurodevelopmental genes (ROBO2, NPAS3), consistent with an “early-secretory” state reported in fresh-tissue atlases15. Notably, the high-grade GS8 tumour contributed the great majority of LE-KLK3 nuclei, whereas the lower-grade GS6 sample retained a larger proportion of early-luminal and basal-skewed cells, mirroring its more differentiated histology and lower PSA density in situ. Cluster c2, defined by prostate-specific lncRNAs (PCA3, PCAT14) and peripheral-zone markers (CACNA1D, CPNE4), represented a PCA3⁺ malignant luminal compartment typical of aggressive disease. Cluster c3 was almost exclusively identified among GS8 nuclei, with an upregulated nuclear fraction of OXPHOS genes and MYC targets, together with elevated MALAT1, indicating a mitochondria-rich, oxidative, proliferative phenotype that persists despite stringent mitochondrial filtering. Cluster c5 combined mitochondrial translation factors (UQCRQ, NDUFA4) with a dense ribosomal and G2/M-checkpoint signature, reproducing the “ribo-high cycling” luminal state15 and underscoring a second proliferative axis.

Stromal and neural compartments were recognized among clusters c4, c6, and c7. Cluster c6 expressed contractile apparatus genes (ACTA2, MYH11, CNN1, TAGLN) characteristic of myofibroblasts, although automated mapping against the Prostate Cell Atlas occasionally misassigned them to endothelial or Hillock epithelial identities. Cluster c7, dominated by GS8, displayed a canonical CAF matrix program (COL6A3, COL1A2, DCN, CTGF, AXL), consistent with the histological impression of a reactive stroma in high-grade tumours. Cluster c4 was enriched for adhesion molecules (CNTNAP2, NRXN3, LRRC4C) typical of peripheral nerve fibres, reflecting the inclusion of adjacent neural tissue in the sampled dissection. Importantly, these nuclei lacked canonical neuroendocrine prostate cancer (NEPC) markers such as CHGA and SYP (mean normalized CHGA expression in c4: 0.005, mean normalized SYP expression in c4: 0.05). Given that our samples were derived from treatment-naïve patients, the presence of late-stage, castrate-resistant NEPC cells is clinically improbable, confirming their peripheral nerve identity. The endothelial fraction doubled in GS8, supporting microscopic evidence of enhanced angiogenesis, while the CAF-rich c7 cluster was likewise GS8-biased. In contrast, virtually all B-, T-, NK- and mast-cell nuclei resided in GS6, illustrating the well-described transition to an immune “cold” microenvironment with tumour progression.

Pathway enrichment analysis using fgsea aligned with these identities: androgen-response and epithelial differentiation signatures dominated in c0 and c1; keratinisation and cytoskeletal remodelling were elevated in the basal-skewed compartment; OXPHOS and MYC programs peaked in c3 and c5; and stromal clusters were enriched for extracellular-matrix (ECM) organisation and vascular development (Supplementary Table 2).

Across all clusters, pseudobulk profiles correlated strongly with their Prostate Cell Atlas counterparts (median r = 0.88), supporting the notion that RNAlater preservation and the bead-mill/high-salt lysis workflow maintained core transcriptional identities, including metabolic extremes. While cross-dataset correlation cannot definitively exclude all potential preservation-related shifts, these results suggest that our workflow largely minimizes heat-shock or dissociation artefacts.

Overall, the present data demonstrate that the nuclei isolation protocol for snRNA-seq is able to capture the full epithelial, stromal, and immune repertoire of PC specimens, preserving rare but clinically relevant states such as PCA3⁺ malignant cells, mitochondria-rich OXPHOS-high nuclei, and ribosome-high cycling progenitors, and providing a metabolic and immunological reflection of tumour grade.

Discussion

Single-nucleus RNA sequencing provides a pragmatic approach to interrogate cell-type–resolved transcriptional programs and the tumour microenvironments in PC while minimising dissociation-induced transcriptional artefacts that confound scRNA-seq. Stress responses triggered by warm enzymatic dissociation, including immediate-early gene activation, are well documented, and even cold-active proteases only partially mitigate these effects1,7. By profiling nuclei rather than whole cells and enabling analysis of banked clinical material, snRNA-seq has proven particularly useful in solid tumours3,16.

Within this context, a streamlined, sorter-free protocol tailored to RNAlater-preserved prostate resections yielded morphologically intact nuclei suitable for droplet-based snRNA-seq. Bead-mill homogenisation coupled with high-salt lysis shortened the time from tissue to nuclei suspension to approximately 150 min, avoided FACS and density gradients, and still produced high-quality libraries (Q30 ≥ 90%) with low ambient RNA after in silico decontamination. The dataset recovered 3,365 nuclei, which resolved into eight coherent compartments spanning epithelial (including AR-responsive luminal), stromal, endothelial, neural, and immune lineages, reflecting the expected complexity of PC tissue. To fully assess the robustness and reproducibility of the proposed workflow, the marked difference in nuclei recovery between the GS6 (approximately 30% of the targeted 3,000 nuclei) and GS8 (approximately 90%) samples warrants detailed consideration. This variability highlights a crucial interplay between tissue biology and technical processing constraints. Biologically, the difference is primarily driven by the intrinsic histo-architectural features defined by the Gleason grading system. Low-grade tumors (GS6) are well-differentiated, characterized by distinct glands with large luminal spaces separated by an abundant, dense fibromuscular stroma, which inherently results in lower overall cellularity17. In contrast, high-grade tumors (GS8) are poorly differentiated, comprising fused cribriform glands or solid epithelial sheets with a high nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio and significantly diminished intervening stroma17. Consequently, an equivalent mass of GS8 tissue contains a substantially higher baseline number of nuclei. Technically, these biological differences are exacerbated by the mechanism of RNAlater preservation. RNAlater stabilizes nucleic acids through high-salt protein precipitation and tissue dehydration. This process renders the collagen-rich extracellular matrix and smooth muscle, predominate in low-grade prostate specimens, highly rubbery, stiff, and exceptionally resistant to mechanical dissociation7,8. Conversely, the highly cellular, epithelium-dense regions of high-grade tumors are structurally more brittle and thus more susceptible to bead-mill homogenization, facilitating efficient nuclei release. Therefore, while our workflow is robust in capturing the full cellular repertoire and maintaining data quality across different grades, variation in absolute nuclei yield is a predictable outcome. It directly reflects the varying histopathological composition, stromal content, and initial cellularity of the clinical specimen rather than technical inconsistency of the protocol.

Although our protocol captured the broad spectrum of prostate lineages, we observed relatively low recovery of tumor microenvironment (TME) cells, particularly immune populations (e.g., lymphocytes and mononuclear phagocytes). Biologically, PC is classically characterized as an immunologically “cold” malignancy with inherently sparse immune infiltration11,18. However, technical biases intrinsic to our isolation strategy undoubtedly contribute to this underrepresentation. The combination of RNAlater-induced tissue stiffening, aggressive bead-mill homogenization, and high-salt lysis imposes severe mechanical and osmotic stress on the sample. Fragile nuclei, such as those of lymphocytes, are disproportionately susceptible to rupture under these conditions compared with the more robust nuclei of luminal epithelial cells and fibroblasts3,7. Thus, while aggressive mechanical disruption is necessary to extract intact nuclei from a challenging, dehydrated fibrotic matrix, it inadvertently leads to preferential lysis and depletion of delicate immune TME components.

Regarding existing nuclei isolation strategies, the present minimalist approach aligns conceptually with RNAlater-compatible clinical workflows that intentionally omit sorting and ultracentrifugation while retaining data quality. In kidney biopsies, a 90-minute protocol without FACS or gradients successfully recovered diverse cell types with acceptable QC metrics, establishing feasibility for RNAlater-preserved specimens8. By contrast, many tumour and brain pipelines pair detergent or mechanical lysis with FACS to reduce debris and doublets, improving background at the expense of time and instrumentation3,19. Classic density-cushion or gradient methods provide very clean suspensions but demand more equipment and higher sample input20. Our results indicate that, for RNAlater-preserved prostate resections, a gradient-free, sorter-free workflow can retain biological signal while reducing handling.

However, careful attention to analytical artefacts remains essential. Ambient RNA is a recurrent challenge in droplet-based methods, for which Bayesian and empirical decontamination tools are now well validated and were used here without compromising counts21. Rigid mitochondrial-read thresholds (e.g., 5–10%, as commonly suggested) can inadvertently discard valid biological signals and should be adjusted to the tissue context; therefore adaptive filtering strategies are recommended10,22. The need for adaptive filtering is further underscored by intrinsic biological differences between tumor grades observed in our study. The high-grade GS8 sample showed both a higher median mitochondrial fraction (35.3%) and a lower proportion of intronic reads (7.8%) than the low-grade GS6 sample (8.2% mt, 22.7% intronic). Because morphological QC confirmed effective tissue lysis and successful isolation of intact nuclei in both samples, these metrics likely reflect peri-nuclear retention of the endoplasmic reticulum and adjoined mitochondria, a known effect of mechanically dissociating highly metabolically active, rough ER-rich secretory tumor cells7,23,24. DecontX analysis confirmed that this signal is endogenous to the droplets rather than ambient contamination. Consequently, applying rigid filtering protocols optimized for healthy tissue or whole-cell pipelines would disproportionately remove these biologically active, aggressive tumor subpopulations. Context-dependent QC metrics are therefore essential for solid tumor snRNA-seq. As orthogonal evidence of nuclear integrity, enrichment of nuclear-retained MALAT1 supported the nuclear origin of the transcriptomes, consistent with foundational studies on MALAT1 localisation and function and comparative analyses demonstrating MALAT1 elevation in nuclei versus whole cells25,26. Notably, MALAT1 also has disease relevance in PC; experimental depletion induces a BRCAness-like state and enhances sensitivity to PARP inhibition, reinforcing its utility as both a nuclear QC anchor and a biologically interpretable signal in differential expression analyses27.

Biological fidelity was further supported by the recovery of canonical prostate lineages including KLK3 + AR-responsive luminal cells, early-luminal and basal surrogates, cancer-associated fibroblasts, endothelium, and nerve fibres, and by concordance with independent single-cell atlases of healthy and malignant prostate11,15. Transcriptomic clusters delineated from isolated nuclei recapitulated a pathologically coherent portrait of the PC specimens while highlighting grade-dependent shifts in epithelial, stromal and immune composition. Furthermore, our cross-dataset integration demonstrated that while the stromal and immune states generally integrate seamlessly across protocols when present, the malignant epithelial transcriptomes are far more sensitive to patient-specific heterogeneity and the technical differences between nuclear and whole-cell RNA pools (i.e., scRNA-seq vs. snRNA-seq). The distinct spatial clustering of epithelial nuclei versus whole cells emphasizes that modality acts as a primary technical driver of separation. Nevertheless, the cohesive structural alignment of the TME components confirms that our RNAlater and bead-mill/high-salt lysis workflow largely minimizes heat-shock or dissociation artefacts, faithfully preserving the underlying biological architectures. Together, these findings position an RNAlater-compatible, sorter-free workflow as an efficient option for generating high-quality snRNA-seq data from routine urologic specimens, while acknowledging context-dependent recovery that should be reported alongside clinical and histologic metadata.

Several avenues could further strengthen and extend these observations. Because our study inferred protocol efficacy by benchmarking against published literature rather than parallel processing of fresh tissue, prospective, matched comparisons of RNAlater-preserved versus fresh or flash-frozen tissue, as well as comparisons with FACS-assisted and gradient-based nuclei preparations across tumour grades and anatomic zones would quantify trade-offs among yield, doublets, and background and refine guidance for workflow selection in clinical settings. Complementary long-read or isoform-aware single-nucleus methods, such as SnISOr-Seq, could address the intronic content of nuclear libraries and enhance isoform-level inference28.

To fully realize the potential of snRNA-seq in routine clinical practice and translational oncology, proposed workflows must be logistically and economically feasible in real-world hospital environments. Classic nuclei isolation methods relying on FACS or ultracentrifugation demand expensive, specialized core facilities and highly trained operators8. Moreover, FACS introduces a significant processing bottleneck, as samples must be sorted sequentially, increasing processing time and the risk of sample degradation. In contrast, our developed workflow addresses these translational barriers by relying exclusively on widely available, low-maintenance benchtop equipment, such as a standard centrifuge and a bead-mill homogenizer. This sorter-free approach substantially reduces per-sample cost and hands-on time. Furthermore, it provides considerable scalability: bead-mill devices can process multiple tissue samples in parallel, readily accommodating the throughput required for large-scale hospital biobanking, and multicentre clinical trials3. Collectively, evidence from streamlined RNAlater workflows in other tissues and the present results in PC converge on an approach that lowers infrastructure barriers without forfeiting cell-state resolution, supporting broader integration of single-nucleus transcriptomics into translational studies and clinical research pipelines3,8.

Materials and methods

Patients, collection of samples, and processing of prostate cancer tissues

Ten RNAlater-preserved samples of prostate cancer tissue derived from consenting, treatment-naïve patients whounderwent radical prostatectomy (II Clinic of Urology, Medical University of Łódź) were used to optimize a nuclei isolation procedure for downstream snRNA-seq. The post-surgical material was examined microscopically, and lesions identified preoperatively as a tumor (prostate MRI and TRUS biopsy) were sampled using scissors in a way that preserved glandular architecture. After resection, specimens of approximately 5 cm3 were immediately transferred to a RNAlater buffer and stored at 4℃ until further processing. Nuclei isolation was usually performed within 1 month of storage, consistent with the preserving capacity of RNAlater.

Reagents

The tissue homogenization procedure utilized 1x PBS (pH 7.4) and 3.5 mm stainless steel UFO beads (SSUFO35, Next Advance, Inc.) in conjunction with a Bullet Blender® 5E Pro tissue homogenizer (Next Advance, Inc.). The nuclei lysis buffer was prepared using nuclease-free water and consisted of Tris-HCl (10 mM, pH 7.4), NaCl (250 mM), MgCl2 (21 mM), CaCl2 (1 mM), NP-40 (1% v/v), EGTA (1 mM), EDTA (5 mM), and an RNase inhibitor cocktail (0.2 U/µL; Millipore-Sigma, cat. no. 3335399001). The wash and resuspension buffers comprised 1x PBS (pH 7.4) supplemented with bovine serum albumin (2% w/v) and the RNase inhibitor cocktail (0.2 U/µL). For the morphological assessment of nuclei via Pappenheim’s method, May-Grünwald solution and Giemsa stock solution were used.

Nuclei isolation procedure

Initially we tested several nuclei isolation techniques based on ready-to-use reagents (Minute™ Single Nucleus Isolation Kit for Tissues/Cells, Invent Biotechnologies, Inc.), sucrose gradient7, and the 10x Genomics protocol for nuclei isolation with in-house modifications (10x Genomics demonstrated protocol CG000124 Rev F). The detailed protocols that we tested, together with experience-based comments and observations, are summarized in Table 4. During this initial ptimization phase, the evaluation of these alternative methods was strictly qualitative. Because RNAlater preservation makes the specimen rubber-like, hard, and heavily cross-linked, conventional manual homogenization and standard dissociation methods (including collagenase IV digestion) failed to adequately disintegrate the tissue. This resulted in macroscopic tissue remnants and severe microscopic clumping. Consequently, the release of intact nuclei was either entirely absent or far below the reliable limit of detection. Severe aggregation and excessive debris precluded meaningful quantitative assessment or progression to downstream single-nucleus sequencing for these failed conditions.

Our optimization was therefore driven by the need to overcome this fundamental mechanical barrier. We ultimately employed the Bullet Blender® 5E Pro tissue homogenizer with a dedicated bead kit (3.5 mm stainless steel UFO beads, SSUFO35) following the manufacturer’s protocol for carcinoma tissue (Next Advance, Inc.). Briefly, tissue samples were minced into approximately 0.5 mm fragments with a sterile scalpel blade and transferred to 5 mL tubes containing 2 mL of PBS and 4 UFO beads. Homogenization was performed at speed 20 for 4 min at 4℃, followed by centrifugation of the disintegrated sample (1500 rpm, 10 min, 4℃). After removal of the supernatant, the lysis was carried out in 5 mL of lysis buffer for 50 min on ice, with intermittent gentle pipetting using a Pasteur or wide-bore pipette tip to minimize nuclear shearing. The sample was then centrifuged through a sterile cell strainer (40 μm) in lysis buffer (500 rpm, 10 min, 4℃). The resulting nuclei pellet of nuclei was resuspended in 500 µL of washing buffer containing RNase inhibitors cocktail (0.2 U/µL) and 10% DMSO for freezing and long-term storage at -80℃ for downstream applications. In parallel, an additional 25 µL aliquot was collected for morphological assessment of nuclei quality (Pappenheim method) and counting with trypan blue (using a Countess™ II FL Automated Cell Counter). Our optimized isolation protocol yielded approximately 5–6 × 106 nuclei from 700 mg of tissue. Ultimately, for snRNA-seq, two nuclei aliquots isolated from PC samples with the most distinct clinicopathological characteristics, intact morphology and a concentration of at least 1 × 106 nuclei were selected.

A detailed, step-by-step interactive version of the optimized nuclei isolation protocol used in this study has been deposited and is publicly available on protocols.io (DOI: https://doi.org/10.17504/protocols.io.q26g77r93gwz/v1).

Assessment of nuclei morphology and sample quality

Morphological assessment of isolated nuclei and evaluation of their integrity were performed using the Pappenheim method with May-Grünwald-Giemsa staining. Approximately 10 µL of the nuclei suspension was smeared onto a glass slide for light microscopy. The dried smear was stained with May-Grünwald reagent for 7 min, followed by gentle removal of the stain and a 10 s rinse with distilled water. The specimen was then stained with Giemsa reagent (1:10) for 5 min, gently washed with distilled water, and air-dried. Microscopic slides with stained nuclei content were examined under a light microscope using an oil immersion objective at a total magnification of 1000x (Fig. 5).

snRNA-seq with 10x platform and downstream data processing

snRNA-seq (n = 2) was outsourced to GENEWIZ Germany GmbH (a part of AZENTA Life Sciences). Libraries were prepared according to the 10x Chromium 3’ Gene Expression bundle workflow, targeting 3,000 nuclei per sample. Samples were sequenced on a Illumina NovaSeq 6000 flow cell with a 100 bp read length, targeting 100,000 reads per nucleus. FASTQ files generated from the 10x snRNA-seq workflow were processed using the `cellranger count` pipeline from the Cell Ranger v7.0.1 suite (10x Genomics). Reads were aligned to the 10x Genomics pre-built human reference genome GRCh38-3.0.0. Because the libraries were generated from isolated nuclei, intronic reads were included in the gene expression quantification (which is the default in Cell Ranger v7). This pipeline was used for alignment, filtering, barcode counting, and UMI counting to generate the raw feature-barcode matrices for downstream QC.

In-house downstream processing and analyses were conducted in the R environment (v 4.5.2). Gene–barcode matrices were then imported with the Seurat package (v 5.3.0) package and decontaminated for ambient RNA using the DecontX package (v 1.8.0)21. Per-sample quality filtering used data-driven thresholds. Specifically, nuclei above the 95th percentile of mitochondrial RNA content (percent.mt) or below the 5th percentile of library complexity (log10_genes_per_umi = log10(nFeature_RNA/nCount_RNA)) were removed. Post-QC objects were merged and Seurat v5 layers were unified with the `JoinLayers` function. Data were then normalized and scaled. The top 2,000 highly variable genes (“features”) were identified and used for dimensionality reduction (Principal Component Analysis, dimensions 1:30 followed by Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection, dimensions 1:20 ). A kNN graph was constructed in PCA space, and Louvain clustering was performed at a resolution of 0.5.

Cluster markers were identified using `FindAllMarkers` function. Biological and quality-related programmes were quantified with `AddModuleScore()` using the following gene sets: OXPHOS (HALLMARK_OXIDATIVE_PHOSPHORYLATION gene set via MSigDB), ARset (AR, KLK3, KLK2, TMPRSS2, NKX3-1, ACPP, FKBP5, NKAIN1), NucQC (RPLP0, RPL3, RPS3, RPL13A, NPM1, BOP1, NCL, FBL), TAC (TOP2A, MKI67, MCM5, PCNA, BIRC5, CCNB1, AURKB, CENPF), and a heat-shock/stress panel (HSPA1A, HSPA1B, DNAJB1, HSP90AA1, HSPB1) as well as MALAT1 expression. Between-sample comparisons (GS8 vs. GS6) were performed using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. Reference mapping was carried out with SingleR using the Human Primary Cell Atlas to obtain predicted labels per nucleus. For prostate-specific benchmarking, a public prostate datasets from Tuong et al.11 and Chen et al.12 were converted to Seurat objects, normalized, and integrated with our data using anchors (`FindIntegrationAnchors(dims = 1:30)`, and `IntegrateData(dims = 1:30)`) followed by scaling, PCA (dims = 1:30), and UMAP (dims = 1:30). Lineage concordance was quantified by pseudobulk correlations using AverageExpression per cluster (our data) and per reference cell type (atlas) on intersected genes, followed by Pearson correlation. Sample-level differential expression analysis (GS8 vs. GS6 across all nuclei) produced a ranked vector (average log2FC) for `fgsea` against MSigDB Hallmarks (minSize = 15, maxSize = 500, nperm = 10000). Normalized enrichment scores (NES) and FDR values were used for interpretation. All plots were generated with Seurat/ggplot2. To quantitatively evaluate cross-dataset mixing, the original high-resolution cell type annotations from the reference atlases were aggregated into three broad biological compartments: “Epithelial / Tumor”, “Stroma (TME)’”(encompassing fibroblasts, smooth muscle, and endothelial cells), and “Immune (TME)” (encompassing T, B, NK, mast cells, and mononuclear phagocytes). Dataset mixing dynamics were visualized using split-UMAP projections faceted by dataset origin. Additionally, integration efficiency was quantified using stacked bar charts, which calculated the relative proportion of nuclei (current study) versus whole cells (reference atlases) within each broad biological compartment.

Default settings were used unless specified otherwise above.
