Risk factors for stoma-site incisional hernia after ileostomy reversal:
impact of fascial suture material
Taiki Kajiwara1,2 · Shinobu Ohnuma1 · Hideaki Karasawa1 · Hideyuki Suzuki1 · Gumpei Yoshimatsu1 · Yoshihiro Sato1 ·
Megumi Murakami1 · Ichiro Ise1 · Michiaki Unno1
Received: 9 May 2026 / Accepted: 4 September 2026
© The Author(s) 2026

Abstract
Purpose To determine the incidence of stoma-site incisional hernia (SSIH) after ileostomy reversal and identify the associated risk factors, focusing on fascial suture materials.
Methods This single-center retrospective study included 194 patients who underwent ileostomy reversal for colorectal neoplasms (2010–2023) with ≥ 330 days of postoperative computed tomography follow-up. All fascial closures used interrupted
absorbable sutures. SSIH was defined on computed tomography. Risk factors were evaluated using logistic regression, and
inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) was applied to assess the association between suture materials and SSIH.
Results SSIH occurred in 21 patients (10.8%) at a median of 282 days, and five patients (23.8%) underwent hernia repair.
In a multivariable analysis, higher body mass index (odds ratio [OR]: 1.24 per kg/m², p = 0.008) and use of braided absorbable sutures (OR: 2.90, p = 0.042) were associated with SSIH. IPTW analysis showed a consistent association with braided
absorbable sutures (OR: 3.24, p = 0.013). In an exploratory model that included the calendar period, this association was
attenuated.
Conclusions SSIH occurred in approximately 10% of patients after ileostomy reversal. A higher body mass index was
consistently associated with SSIH. Fascial suture material may be a clinically relevant component of stoma-site closure;
however, its independent effect requires further evaluation.
Keywords Stoma-site incisional hernia · Ileostomy · Suture material · Risk factors

Introduction
Diverting loop ileostomy is widely used to protect high-risk
anastomoses following rectal resection for rectal tumors and
to reduce the clinical severity of anastomotic leakage. However, stoma-site incisional hernia (SSIH) after ileostomy
reversal is a common complication, with a reported incidence of approximately 10–30% [1–5]. Beyond cosmetic
concerns, SSIH may lead to clinically significant complications, such as incarceration or bowel obstruction, and a

Patient-related factors, including obesity, have been
consistently associated with increased SSIH risk [1–7]. In
addition, the stoma closure site is often exposed to a potentially contaminated environment and it may be subjected
to increased tension during abdominal wall reconstruction, which may contribute to a higher incidence of SSIH
compared with general abdominal incisional hernia [2, 8].
As patient-related risk factors are largely non-modifiable,
optimizing the surgical technique represents a practical
approach to prevention. In particular, fascial closure methods—including suture material and technique—may play a
key role. The Current European Hernia Society guidelines
emphasize the importance of the closure strategy, including the small-bites closure technique and the use of slowly
absorbable monofilament sutures [6, 7]. However, clinical
evidence specifically evaluating the impact of fascial suture

material (braided absorbable versus monofilament absorbable) on SSIH is limited. Although purse-string skin closure
has been shown to reduce surgical site infection (SSI) after
stoma reversal [9], its relevance to long-term outcomes such
as SSIH is uncertain.
In this study, we aimed to determine the incidence and
repair rate of SSIH after ileostomy reversal and identify
associated risk factors, with a particular focus on fascial
suture material.

Methods
Study design and patients
This single-center retrospective observational study included
patients who underwent ileostomy reversal between January
2010 and October 2023 at our institution. Of 374 consecutive
patients, the study population was restricted to those with
colorectal neoplasms, as postoperative computed tomography (CT) follow-up was routinely performed in this group.
Patients with benign diseases were excluded (n = 147) due to
the lack of comparable CT surveillance.
Additional exclusions included patients with < 330 days
of CT follow-up after reversal (n = 25), those whose ileostomies were constructed at other hospitals (n = 7), and those
who underwent concurrent procedures at the time of reversal (n = 1). The final cohort comprised 194 patients. A flowchart of the patient selection process is shown in Fig. 1. The

330-day threshold was selected because SSIH was defined
on the basis of CT findings, as described later, and postoperative CT at approximately 1 year after ileostomy reversal
was performed at 12 ± 1 months.
The study protocol was approved by the Institutional
Review Board of Tohoku University Hospital (Approval
No. 2025-1-967). Owing to the retrospective study design,
informed consent was waived, and an opt-out procedure
was implemented in this study.

Outcomes and definitions
The primary endpoint was the occurrence of SSIH. No
patient was diagnosed with SSIH based on clinical symptoms alone. Therefore, SSIH was defined on postoperative
CT as a fascial defect at the stoma-closure site with protrusion of intra-abdominal contents (such as preperitoneal fat
or the bowel) [10]. The event date was defined as the first
CT detection, and the time to diagnosis was calculated from
the date of the ileostomy reversal. Clinical findings alone
were not used to diagnose SSIH. The need for hernia repair
was also evaluated.
Data collected included patient characteristics (age, sex,
diagnosis, body mass index [BMI], American Society of
Anesthesiologists [ASA] class, and comorbidities), perioperative factors (approach, operative time, blood loss, postoperative length of stay, complications, wound infection,
and ileus), closure-related factors (wound closure procedure
and suture material for fascial closure), surgical procedure
for primary tumors, timing of stoma construction, chemotherapy before reversal, and interval to reversal. Fascial
closure materials were limited to monofilament absorbable
sutures (Polydioxanone; PDS®) and braided absorbable
sutures (Polyglactin 910; Vicryl®).
Missing data were handled using complete-case analysis.
Fascial suture material was not documented in a subset of
cases and was the primary source of the missing data. Baseline characteristics were compared between patients with
and without documented fascial suture material to assess
any potential biases.

Statistical analysis
Patients with and without SSIH were compared. Continuous
variables are presented as medians (interquartile range) and
were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test, whereas
categorical variables were compared using Fisher’s exact
test.
Risk factor analysis was performed using a logistic
regression analysis. In the univariable analyses, the associations between each variable and SSIH were evaluated
and reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence
intervals (CIs). In the multivariable analysis, the number
of variables was limited to three to avoid overfitting, given
the small number of events (n = 21). BMI and blood loss
were selected based on clinical relevance and univariable
associations, with fascial suture material being the primary
exposure of interest. Continuous variables were scaled to
improve interpretability (age per 5 years, operative time per
10 min, blood loss per 10 mL, and BMI per 1 kg/m²).
As a sensitivity analysis, inverse probability of treatment
weighting (IPTW) based on propensity scores for the use
of braided versus monofilament absorbable sutures was
applied. Propensity scores were estimated using logistic
regression, including age, sex, BMI, ASA class, comorbidities, operative time, blood loss, chemotherapy before reversal, and the interval to reversal.
As monofilament absorbable sutures were standardized
in 2019, the cohort was divided into earlier (2010–2018) and
later (2019–2023) periods. Follow-up duration and selected
characteristics were compared by suture material and calendar period. In addition, an exploratory multivariable logistic
regression analysis, including the calendar period, was performed to assess potential period-related confounding.
Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05, and
0.05 ≤ p < 0.10 was considered a trend. All analyses were
conducted using the JMP Student Edition version 18.2.0
(JMP Statistical Discovery LLC, Cary, NC, USA).

Results
Patient characteristics and SSIH occurrence
The patient characteristics are summarized in Table 1.
Among 194 patients, the median age was 60 years, and
126 were male. The median follow-up duration was 1292
days. Cancer was the most common diagnosis, and the
median BMI was 22.0 kg/m². The median operative time
was 104 min and the median blood loss was 29 mL. Wound
closure was performed using the purse-string technique in
64.9% of patients and primary closure in 28.4%. Fascial
closure was performed using monofilament absorbable

sutures in 53.6% of cases and braided absorbable sutures
in 36.6%; in 9.8% of cases, the fascial suture material was
not documented in the surgical records. SSIH occurred in
21 patients (10.8%), with a median time to diagnosis of 282
days. Among them, five patients (23.8%) underwent hernia
repair.
Baseline characteristics were generally comparable
between patients with and without documented fascial
suture material; however, ASA class and wound closure
method differed between groups, the incidence of SSIH was
similar (Supplementary Table S1).

Two-group comparisons
Table 2 summarizes the comparisons between patients with
and without SSIH. Patients with SSIH had a significantly
higher BMI (p = 0.0015). Blood loss and age showed a trend
toward being higher in the SSIH group (p = 0.0622), as did
age (p = 0.0987, respectively). No significant differences
were observed in sex, comorbidities, postoperative complications (Clavien–Dindo grade ≥ 2), wound infection, ileus,
chemotherapy exposure, or the interval to stoma reversal. Regarding closure-related factors, the wound closure
method did not differ between the groups; however, braided
absorbable sutures were used more frequently in the SSIH
group (p = 0.0125).

Logistic regression analysis

IPTW analysis

Table 3 summarizes the logistic regression analysis. In the
univariable analysis, BMI (per 1 kg/m²; OR: 1.26, 95% CI:
1.08–1.48, p = 0.002), blood loss (per 10 mL; OR: 1.14, 95%
CI: 1.03–1.25, p = 0.012), and braided absorbable sutures
(OR: 3.66, 95% CI: 1.37–10.91, p = 0.009) were associated
with SSIH.
In the multivariable model including the three prespecified factors, BMI (per 1 kg/m²; OR: 1.24, 95% CI: 1.06–
1.48, p = 0.008) and braided absorbable sutures (OR: 2.90,
95% CI: 1.04–8.94, p = 0.042) remained independently
associated with SSIH. Blood loss showed a non-significant
trend (per 10 mL; OR: 1.10, 95% CI: 0.99–1.23, p = 0.085).

To assess robustness, IPTW-weighted logistic regression
was performed (Table 4). Covariate balance improved after
weighting, with a maximum absolute standardized mean difference of 0.102 for blood loss (Supplementary Table S2).
In the IPTW-weighted marginal model, braided absorbable sutures remained significantly associated with SSIH
(OR, 3.24; 95% CI, 1.28–8.74; p = 0.013). This association
persisted in the doubly robust model (OR: 3.53, 95% CI:
1.35–10.01; p = 0.010). Furthermore, BMI was independently associated with SSIH (per 1 kg/m²; OR: 1.21, 95%
CI: 1.03–1.44; p = 0.019), whereas blood loss was not (per
10 mL; OR: 1.03, 95% CI: 0.92–1.14; p = 0.574).

Additional analyses according to suture material
and calendar period
Additional analyses according to fascial suture material and
calendar period are presented in Supplementary Tables S3
and S4. As the suture material was closely related to the
calendar period and follow-up duration, an exploratory
multivariable model including the calendar period was performed. In this model, braided absorbable sutures showed
higher estimated odds of SSIH than monofilament absorbable sutures; however, this association was no longer statistically significant (Supplementary Table S5).
Among the 25 patients excluded because of CT followup < 330 days, 11 had available postoperative CT within
330 days, and SSIH was detected in one patient. No patients
were diagnosed with SSIH based on clinical symptoms
alone (Supplementary Table S6).

Discussion
In this study, SSIH occurred in 10.8% of patients after ileostomy reversal, and 23.8% of those with SSIH underwent
hernia repair. Most cases were diagnosed within 1 year,
suggesting that abdominal wall stability during the early
healing phase may be important for the development of
SSIH. Consistent with previous reports, a higher BMI was
independently associated with SSIH. In the primary analysis, braided absorbable sutures were associated with SSIH;
this association was observed in the IPTW analysis after
adjustment for patient and perioperative characteristics. In
the exploratory analysis, including the calendar period, the
direction of the association between suture material and
SSIH remained consistent; however, the association was no
longer statistically significant. Therefore, although fascial
suture material may be relevant to SSIH development, its
independent effect cannot be definitively established.
Differences in tensile strength retention may partly explain
the association between braided sutures and SSIH. Monofilament absorbable polydioxanone sutures (PDS®) retain
tensile strength longer (approximately 74% at 2 weeks, 50%
at 4 weeks, and 25% at 6 weeks), whereas braided absorbable polyglactin 910 sutures (Vicryl®) lose strength more
rapidly, with reported retention declining from 79% on day
14 to 5% on day 35 [11, 12]. Given that SSIH often develops
early after stoma reversal, insufficient tensile support during
the initial healing phase may increase the susceptibility to
SSIH. Infection-related mechanisms may contribute to this
phenomenon. Braided sutures may facilitate capillary action
and fluid migration, potentially enabling suture-dependent
bacterial transport [13, 14]. In addition, bacterial adherence
to suture materials has been implicated in the development

13

of infections [13]. As stoma closure wounds are more likely
to be contaminated than typical surgical wounds, subclinical infection and chronic inflammation may persist even in
the absence of overt SSI. As SSI is a recognized risk factor
for incisional hernia, prolonged inflammation may impair
fascial healing through extracellular matrix degradation and
abnormal collagen remodeling, ultimately promoting hernia formation [6, 7, 15, 16]. Accordingly, the use of braided
sutures in this setting may increase the risk of SSIH. Nevertheless, these mechanistic explanations remain speculative
owing to the absence of microbiological and detailed wound
healing data.
As monofilament absorbable sutures were adopted as
the standard material in 2019, the calendar period was
closely linked to the suture material. Therefore, adjusting
for the calendar period may have attenuated the association
between suture materials and SSIH. Although the association between braided absorbable sutures and SSIH was no
longer statistically significant in this model, the direction
of association remained consistent. Nonetheless, the exploratory model including the calendar period should be interpreted cautiously because it included four covariates for 21
SSIH events. Thus, the present findings do not exclude the
potential benefits of monofilament absorbable sutures; however, the independent effect of suture material could not be
definitively established. This attenuation after adjustment
for the calendar period should be interpreted as an important neutral finding and suggests that changes in perioperative management over the long enrollment period may have
influenced the study results. During this 13-year period,
aspects of perioperative care other than fascial suture materials may have changed, including the standardization of
surgical techniques and wound management. Therefore, the
observed difference in SSIH risk should be interpreted in the
context of these unmeasured temporal changes. Although
monofilament absorbable sutures may be a reasonable component of standardized stoma-site closure, SSIH prevention
is likely multifactorial and it may require the comprehensive standardization of perioperative management and closure techniques.
In addition, a higher BMI was an independent risk factor,
consistent with previous studies [1–5]. Obesity may increase
wound tension and intra-abdominal pressure by increasing
the abdominal wall radius. Furthermore, impaired perfusion
and chronic inflammation in the adipose tissue may delay
wound healing, leading to a delayed recovery of fascial
strength and subsequent incisional hernia formation [5, 15,
16]. Blood loss was associated with SSIH in the univariable analysis, but it was not independently significant in the
multivariable analysis, showing only a trend. A higher blood
loss may reflect surgical complexity or tissue fragility, but
it appears to be confounded by other factors. As BMI is not

Surgery Today

readily modifiable in the perioperative period, standardizing
fascial closure with monofilament absorbable sutures may
be a practical preventive strategy, particularly for patients at
higher risk. Their prolonged tensile strength may better support early wound healing than that of sutures. In addition,
prophylactic mesh placement at the time of stoma reversal
has been suggested to be an effective preventive strategy
[17] and it may be considered in selected high-risk patients.
However, because mesh placement may increase costs and
cause complications, standardizing fascial suture materials
may be a more practical first step.
This study has some limitations. First, its retrospective single-center design introduces some potential selection bias and limits generalizability. As only patients with
colorectal neoplasms who were undergoing routine CT
surveillance were included, the observed incidence should
not be interpreted as the overall incidence following ileostomy reversal. Second, because SSIH was defined on CT,
patients without sufficient CT follow-up duration could not
be reliably evaluated in this study. Among the 25 patients
excluded because of CT follow-up < 330 days, 11 had available CT scans, and SSIH was detected in one patient. These
findings suggest that many early SSIH events were unlikely
to have been missed, although selection bias related to CT
follow-up duration cannot be excluded. Third, the effects
of detailed technical components of abdominal wall closure
and missing data could not be fully adjusted. Technical factors such as bite size and pitch are important [18], but they
were not routinely recorded. In addition, missing data on
fascial suture material may not have been completely random. Fourth, significant imbalances were observed among
the sutured groups. Suture material was closely related to the
calendar period, follow-up duration, and changes in the skin
closure method. Although the IPTW analysis and an exploratory model including the calendar period were performed,
the possibility of residual confounding or overadjustment
could not be excluded. Moreover, the limited number of
SSIH events restricted the multivariable analyses. Future
prospective multicenter studies using standardized closure
protocols are required to provide more robust evidence for
clinical practice.

Conclusions
SSIH is a clinically important complication that occurs in
approximately one in ten patients after ileostomy reversal. A higher BMI was consistently associated with SSIH.
Braided absorbable sutures were associated with SSIH in
the primary and IPTW analyses; however, this association
was attenuated after adjustment for the calendar period.
Although the independent effect of fascial suture material

remains uncertain, the use of monofilament absorbable
sutures for fascial closure may be a simple and practical
component of standardized closure aimed at reducing the
risk of SSIH.
