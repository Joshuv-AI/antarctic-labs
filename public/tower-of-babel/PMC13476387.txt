Cost-Effectiveness of Routine Infant Meningococcal B Vaccination in the Netherlands
Authors: Joost J. M. Simons, Florian Zeevat, Tjalke A. Westra, Jan C. Wilschut, Nina M. van Sorge, Cornelis Boersma, Maarten J. Postma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionInvasive meningococcal disease (IMD) is a severe infectious disease that can cause mortality and debilitating sequelae. IMD is typically vaccine-preventable. In the Netherlands, meningococcal serogroup B (MenB) causes almost all IMD cases. The objective of this study was to assess the cost-effectiveness of routine infant MenB vaccination in the Netherlands, using the latest incidence data, real-world evidence and vaccine data.MethodsWe used the dynamic transmission cost-effectiveness (DyCE) model to compare routine infant vaccination with three doses of the four-component meningococcal B vaccine (4CMenB) versus no MenB vaccination, from a societal perspective. The model followed 100 cohorts over lifetime, was updated with the most recent Dutch epidemiological evidence, and was adapted in accordance with the latest Dutch health economic guidelines. Additionally, IMD incidence was varied in scenario analyses to model the unpredictable incidence of MenB IMD and potential outbreaks of this disease.ResultsOver lifetime, 2367 MenB IMD cases, 1077 long-term sequelae cases and 116 deaths were prevented with 4CMenB vaccination in the base case. 4CMenB vaccination saved 14,703 quality-adjusted life years (QALYs), predominantly by preventing long-term sequelae (45.5%) and deaths (52.2%). The incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) was €99,752 per QALY gained in the base case and decreased below the willingness-to-pay threshold of 80,000 if the IMD incidence increased by 24%, or if the vaccine acquisition costs decreased by 22% (from €78.5 to €61.45). The ICER was most sensitive to variations in vaccine effectiveness, the incidence of MenB IMD and vaccine acquisition costs.ConclusionsIn the Netherlands, 4CMenB vaccination is likely to be cost-effective if MenB incidence increases by 24%. This comprehensive analysis provides decision-makers with new and updated information on the cost-effectiveness of infant vaccination with 4CMenB in the Netherlands.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40121-026-01375-8.

Introduction

The coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic dramatically illustrated the threat that infectious diseases pose to society and the key role vaccines play in managing this threat. In addition to the benefits of vaccination during a pandemic, vaccines can also prevent infectious disease outbreaks.

Invasive meningococcal disease (IMD) is a severe infectious disease with an unpredictable incidence and risk of outbreaks [1]. IMD typically presents as meningitis and/or sepsis. It has a high case fatality rate, even with appropriate and timely treatment, and a range of long-term complications of varying severity, such as hearing loss, mental health problems and amputations in many survivors [2, 3].

IMD is caused by Neisseria meningitidis, a diplococcal bacterium that resides in the human nasopharynx. While many N. meningitidis serogroups have been identified, > 95% of IMD cases are caused by serogroups A, B, C, W, X or Y, with repartitions varying on the basis of geography and local vaccination programmes [4]. In European countries such as in the Netherlands, most IMD cases are caused by N. meningitidis serogroup B (MenB) [5, 6]. The incidence of IMD caused by different serogroups is difficult to predict (Fig. 1) [7]. In the Netherlands, the burden of disease due to serogroup A has typically been low. A vaccination programme against serogroup C (MenC) disease, initiated in 2001 following an outbreak, successfully reduced MenC IMD incidence. An outbreak of serogroup W (MenW) disease that began in 2016 was addressed by switching vaccines in children from the monovalent MenC vaccine to the quadrivalent MenACWY, introducing MenACWY vaccination for adolescents (aged 14 years) in 2018, including a catch-up vaccination for 15–18-year-olds [5, 7]. The incidence of MenB IMD was relatively high in the Netherlands during the 1990s and has declined since then. As a result of social distancing measures introduced to slow the spread of COVID-19, the incidence of MenB IMD, along with that of many other infectious diseases, decreased during the pandemic [7, 8]. However, since the pandemic, the incidence of MenB IMD has been rapidly increasing and now exceeds the levels observed prior to the COVID-19 pandemic. These data highlight the unpredictable nature of IMD and the potential risk of a future increase in incidence driven by natural fluctuations and the ending of social distancing measures. The risk is particularly high for MenB IMD, for which no vaccination programme is currently in place in the Netherlands, although there are vaccines available.

Currently, two MenB vaccines are available. One of them, the four-component MenB vaccine (4CMenB), can be used in infants. This vaccine was developed combining multiple subcapsular protein antigens, overcoming the limitations of traditional capsule-based approaches. Polysaccharide meningococcal vaccines are ineffective against MenB because its capsular polysialic acid mimics human neural cell structures, rendering them poorly immunogenic and potentially causing autoimmunity [9].

In 2015, the UK became the first country to include 4CMenB in its National Immunisation Programme (NIP) for infants [10, 11]. Currently, 4CMenB has been approved in more than 50 countries and included in 19 NIPs, including those of the Czech Republic, France, Germany, Italy, Luxembourg, Portugal, Spain, Switzerland and the UK [12]. However, the Netherlands has not yet added 4CMenB to its routine vaccination programme, partly due to concerns about its cost-effectiveness [5, 13].

The low incidence of IMD, as well as the lack of consideration of the full burden of the disease in earlier analyses, are key factors underlying the unfavourable cost-effectiveness results [14]. An early cost-effectiveness analysis by Pouwels et al. (2013) [13] concluded that 4CMenB was unlikely to be cost-effective in the Netherlands, primarily due to the declining incidence of MenB IMD and the requirement for a fourth dose. The base-case cost per quality-adjusted life year (QALY) gained, €243,778, was estimated using the lower incidence rates observed between 2005 and 2009. However, scenario analyses using data from 1990 to 1993, when the incidence was higher, showed a considerably lower incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) of €85,931 per QALY gained [13]. This illustrates how sensitive the Pouwels model is to changes in MenB incidence.

Since the analysis by Pouwels et al. was published [13], additional evidence has emerged that strengthens the potential impact of MenB vaccination. This includes a broader range of vaccine-related benefits for both patients and caregivers, which could greatly affect cost-effectiveness outcomes [14]. Moreover, recent long-term data on the effectiveness and safety of 4CMenB have become available from England, Italy and Portugal [15–18]. These findings provide a better understanding of the long-term burden and costs of IMD caused by MenB, both to patients and from a societal perspective. Reflecting the severity of IMD, the cost-effectiveness threshold applied in the Netherlands is €80,000 per QALY gained [19, 20]. A recent cost-of-illness study in the Netherlands further assessed the economic burden of IMD during the acute phase and for a range of IMD sequelae, such as hearing loss, neurological deficits, limb amputation, epilepsy, skin scarring, renal disease, blindness/severe visual impairment and psychological impairment [21].

As the number of cases of MenB IMD has been increasing in the past few years, and given the recent availability of data on the long-term effectiveness of MenB vaccination, updated burden estimates and a broader recognition of vaccine-related benefits for both patients and caregivers, there is a need to reassess the value of MenB vaccination. Therefore, the objective of this study was to evaluate the cost-effectiveness of routine infant vaccination with 4CMenB in the Netherlands.

Methods

Model and Inputs

The dynamic transmission-based cost-effectiveness (DyCE) model was updated to compare IMD-related outcomes in infants vaccinated with 4CMenB versus infants not vaccinated with 4CMenB, using the most recent evidence and health economic guidelines pertaining to the Dutch context. Unvaccinated infants were chosen as the comparator because no vaccine programme against MenB is currently in place in the Netherlands. The DyCE model has been used previously to assess the cost-effectiveness of implementing an infant 4CMenB vaccination programme in England, France and Germany [14, 22, 23]. This model includes two components: (i) a dynamic transmission model, commonly used in infectious disease modelling to estimate the number of cases by simulating transmission of the disease and in this case also the nasopharyngeal carriage, and (ii) an economic decision-tree model, applying QALYs and costs for each health state to estimate the long-term effects of the disease and its sequelae. As per Dutch health economic guidelines [24], the cost–utility analysis was conducted from the societal perspective over a lifetime, including costs to the patient, their family, the healthcare system and other sectors, as well as indirect productivity loss costs.

The study by Pouwels et al. [13] served as a starting point for adapting the DyCE model to the Dutch setting, with several important updates. Consistent with previous cost-effectiveness analyses, Pouwels et al. [13] modelled a single cohort. However, multi-cohort models may yield more accurate estimates of vaccination impact, as they more closely reflect the reality of immunisation programmes, in which a new cohort is vaccinated each year. Therefore, in the present study, the model simulated the vaccination and follow-up of 100 birth cohorts over a lifetime horizon (100 years) and aggregated the results.

Input values and their sources are provided in Supplementary Table S1. Incidence was based on the most recent data on IMD infections in 2024 (147 cases of which 128 IMD-B cases and 38 IMD-B cases in infants and young children). Data were obtained from the Netherlands Reference Laboratory for Bacterial Meningitis (NRLBM) based on isolates submitted by Dutch hospitals (Fig. 1) [7].

When Pouwels and colleagues conducted their analysis in 2013 [13], data on MenB resource use and costs were scarce, with no evidence available on the costs and quality-of-life impact that MenB infection had on patients’ families or caregivers (spillover effects). Pouwels et al. [13] also modelled a 4CMenB schedule requiring four doses at a hypothetical price of €40 per dose. The DyCE model was populated with updated resource use and costs for the broad-ranging disease burden on patients. Sixteen potential physical, neurological and mental health sequelae were considered in this analysis [21, 25], as well as health spillover effects on parents of affected children for the QALY estimates, based on models for England, France and Germany [14, 22, 23]. A discount rate of 3% for costs and savings and 1.5% for QALYs was applied according to Dutch guidelines [24]. All costs were adjusted to their 2025 equivalents in euros by correcting for inflation rates using the Dutch consumer price index [26].

A three-dose 4CMenB vaccination schedule (at 2, 4 and 12 months of age), according to the label, was used in the model, with an acquisition cost per dose of €78.50, reflecting the current list price. The DyCE model included estimates of vaccine effectiveness (33.5%, 78.7% and 80.1% after the first, second and third doses, respectively) and exponential waning, with an average persistence of protection of 48 months, based on the latest evidence [15–17].

Herd immunity effects were excluded from the base-case analysis, as there is currently no evidence that 4CMenB provides protection for non-vaccinated individuals [27]. Additionally, cross-reactive protection was excluded in the base-case analysis, but included in the scenario analyses, as the 4CMenB vaccine demonstrates potent bactericidal activity against serogroups C, W and Y, suggesting cross-protection [28]. This is supported by real-world evidence from England that shows protection against MenW IMD in infants and toddlers [14, 29]. The overall impact of cross-protection due to the existing MenACWY vaccination programme was expected to be negligible.

Outcomes

The total number of IMD cases (reported as MenB IMD cases and MenACWY cases), the number of IMD cases with sequelae and the number of deaths due to IMD were calculated over the selected time horizon, with or without vaccination with 4CMenB. Vaccine acquisition and administration costs and the costs due to treatment of vaccine-related adverse events were considered. The direct medical and non-medical costs due to IMD, as well as indirect IMD costs, were modelled in the vaccinated and unvaccinated cohorts. The total number of QALYs gained, and the ICER of vaccination versus no vaccination, were calculated from the third-party payer and societal perspectives, i.e., including productivity losses.

Sensitivity Analyses

Scenario analyses were conducted to explore the potential for increased incidence and future outbreaks if the annual increases in MenB cases continue. In addition to the base case, three scenarios with increased overall IMD incidence were considered. The first scenario examined the incidence level at which the ICER would fall just below the currently accepted willingness-to-pay threshold of €80,000 per QALY. The second scenario assumed an annual overall IMD incidence of 500 cases, reflecting the IMD incidence observed in the late 1990s. The third scenario considered an annual incidence of 300 cases, representing an intermediate level between the higher incidence observed in the 1990s and the relatively lower incidence of the past decade [7]. In both of the increased cases scenarios the proportional distribution of cases across all ages and serotypes was used and total incidence was inflated to 300 or 500 cases. Other scenarios explored the impact of indirect protection from 4CMenB vaccination on carriage and transmission, including cross-protection against MenACWY disease; an alternative discounting value (1.5% for costs); modelling 5 or 50 cohorts; and, in combination with the incidence scenarios, the effect of reduced vaccine prices.

Univariate deterministic sensitivity analyses (DSA) were conducted to assess the sensitivity of the model results to variations in input parameters. To assess structural and parameter uncertainty, probabilistic sensitivity analyses (PSA) with n = 500 replications were conducted (Table S1 for all input, DSA and PSA parameters).

Ethical approval

This analysis used data from previously conducted studies and did not include any data from new studies involving human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

Results

Base Case

The model showed that introducing an infant 4CMenB programme in the Netherlands would result in a decline in MenB cases among infants and young children (0‒4 years old). Based on 100 cohorts, the model predicted that an infant 4CMenB programme in the Netherlands would prevent 2367 MenB cases, 1077 long-term sequelae cases and 116 deaths in the base case (Table 1). Per cohort this would translate to 23.67 MenB cases, 10.77 long-term sequelae cases and approximately 1.16 deaths. Most of the 14,703 total QALYs gained were due to long-term sequelae and deaths prevented (45.5% and 52.2%, respectively). The model predicted total medical cost savings of €7.5 million and €13.2 million for acute care and sequelae management, respectively, as well as savings of €8.0 million for long-term caregiving, €4.6 million for special education and €1.6 million for productivity losses. With a three-dose schedule, the model predicted a lifetime cost of €1.5 billion for implementing the programme for 100 cohorts (Table 1). Scenario Analyses

An overview of the scenario analyses is presented in Table 3. 4CMenB vaccination became cost-effective at the Dutch willingness-to-pay (WTP) threshold for severe diseases of €80,000 per QALY gained when MenB IMD number of cases increased by 24% (nine cases in infants and young children) compared with the base case (scenario 6) or the vaccine acquisition costs decreased by 22% (from €78.50 to €61.45). Additionally, a number of cases of 300 (scenario 7) or 500 (scenario 8) MenB IMD cases per year reduced the ICER to €47,666 and €27,649 per QALY gained, respectively. When the effect of vaccination on carriage was considered (scenario 2), the ICER improved to €45,319 per QALY gained. The vaccine price also had a significant impact on the ICER (Fig. 2). Using fewer cohort cycles (scenarios 4 and 5) considerably increased the ICER. The ICER was less impacted when cross-protection was included (scenario 1, €96,082). Discussion

Based on the most recent IMD data from 2024 (147 cases per year, of which 38 MenB IMD cases in infants and young children), the current 4CMenB list price (€78.50), vaccination benefits in preventing physical, neurological and mental health sequelae and vaccination modelled in 100 cohorts, we estimated the impact of introducing an infant 4CMenB programme in the Netherlands. The results indicated substantial health gains, with 2367 MenB IMD cases avoided, 1077 long-term sequelae cases avoided, 116 deaths avoided and 14,703 QALYs gained over a lifetime, assuming a vaccination coverage of 90% for the third dose, comparable with that of other infant vaccines. From a societal perspective, this translates into an ICER of €99,752 per QALY gained. If number of IMD cases in the Netherlands increased by 24% (nine IMD-B cases in infants and young children), the ICER would be €79,985 per QALY gained, making the intervention cost-effective at the Dutch WTP threshold of €80,000 per QALY gained. The intervention’s cost-effectiveness would also be achieved if the vaccine acquisition cost decreased by 22%.

An earlier cost-effectiveness analysis by Pouwels et al. (2013) [13] found that vaccination was not cost-effective with an assumed MenB incidence 1.07 cases per 100,000 person-years. However, vaccination with 4CMenB was closer to the willingness-to-pay threshold of €80,000 per QALY gained in scenario analyses using data observed during 1990‒1993 (€85,931/QALY gained). Important differences with our analysis included the number of cohorts modelled (a single cohort by Pouwels et al. [13] versus 100 in our case), the number of doses needed to complete the vaccination schedule (four versus three), updated estimates for vaccine waning and duration of protection and base-case incidence (Dutch MenB incidence from 2005 to 2009 versus more recent incidence rates). Nevertheless, studies from other countries using similar modelling approaches and assumptions have reported outcomes consistent with those of our present study. Modelling has suggested that 4CMenB vaccination could have considerable health benefits in other countries, including France, Germany and the UK [14, 22, 23]. In Germany, 4CMenB could prevent 3303 IMD cases, 304 deaths and 1435 long-term sequelae due to IMD of all serotypes. Furthermore, 4CMenB would save 25,878 QALYs, at a cost of €188,762 per QALY gained [22]. The ICER was most sensitive to the discount rate used for health outcomes. It was estimated that infant vaccination with 4CMenB would avoid 6845 IMD cases across all age groups in France [23]. The estimated ICER was €316,272 per QALY gained, and this was highly sensitive to the types of sequelae included, number of MenB IMD cases, 4CMenB effectiveness and the impact of IMD on the quality of life of a patient’s parents. When comprehensively considering the MenB IMD burden, including long-term sequelae, the impact beyond the patients themselves and societal preferences for the prevention of rare but devastating diseases, 4CMenB infant vaccination was also found to be cost-effective in the UK [14]. The ICER was most sensitive to the number of IMD cases.

To date, 4CMenB vaccination has been incorporated into NIPs for infants in 13 European countries [12], including France, Germany, Luxembourg, Switzerland and the UK [12, 30]. The most important reason for these countries to include 4CMenB was the severity of the disease and the risk of future increases in incidence or outbreaks [31, 32]. Because its incidence fluctuates and the disease is unpredictable, early implementation of vaccination would help prevent future cases. In the Netherlands, MenB incidence has historically fluctuated, and the recent post-COVID-19 increase warrants caution. Previous economic evaluations concluded that MenB vaccination was not cost-effective [13, 33]. Our study, using up-to-date and local model inputs, shows that if MenB incidence increases, 4CMenB vaccination could become a cost-effective intervention. Our results provide valuable insights for policymakers and health authorities. Decreased vaccine acquisition costs, potentially reflecting large-scale procurement, would also make the intervention more cost-effective at the current incidence level.

Limitations

As with any cost-effectiveness analysis, this study has some limitations. Whenever possible, recent data from the Netherlands were used to populate the model. However, when such information was lacking, data from other high-income countries were used, notably for disutility and the probability of sequelae. In addition, the findings of this analysis are specific to the Dutch context and may not be generalisable to other settings. Our base-case scenario used the latest national epidemiological data for MenB available from the NRLBM up to 2024 [7], with scenarios exploring various incidences. A 24% increase in the number of MenB cases would make implementing 4CMenB cost-effective. Given that MenB number of cases has been increasing since the COVID-19 pandemic, a 24% increase in number of IMD cases is plausible. Dutch guidelines for performing cost-effectiveness analyses recommend that costs are discounted more than effects [24]. This can lead to more favourable outcomes over a longer term, when more cohorts are modelled, because future costs lose value more rapidly than future health benefits. In our analysis, a 100-year time horizon was considered. To mitigate the effect of this differential discounting that accumulates over the longer term, we conducted scenario analyses to explore the impact of modelling fewer cycles or changing to non-differential discounting and found that both substantially increased the ICER. No underreporting was considered, as we assumed that all IMD cases were captured through the national reporting system [34].

In addition to the advances incorporated in the present analysis, including the use of a dynamic transmission model and a societal perspective in line with Dutch guidelines, our findings also highlight potential limitations of conventional health technology assessment frameworks when evaluating rare but devastating diseases such as invasive meningococcal disease. Even at relatively low incidence, the burden of MenB IMD is substantial from a patient and family perspective, given the high mortality risk, lifelong sequelae and profound impact on quality of life. Standard QALY-based approaches may insufficiently capture these effects, particularly spillover disutility among family members and caregivers, long-term productivity losses and broader societal consequences. Recent developments in HTA methodology increasingly emphasise the need to account for such broader elements of value, including caregiver and family QALY losses, human capital effects across the life course and the societal value of preventing severe, unpredictable health outcomes. Incorporating these broader societal benefits into economic evaluations could provide a more comprehensive assessment of the value of meningococcal vaccination and support more informed decision-making, particularly in settings where traditional cost-effectiveness thresholds alone may underestimate the true societal impact of prevention.

Conclusions

Our findings indicate that MenB vaccination in the Netherlands may be cost-effective if the number of IMD cases continues to increase compared with previous years, particularly under a projected increase of 24%, or if the vaccine acquisition costs decreases by 22%. The severe clinical manifestations of IMD, its potential for causing unpredictable outbreaks, and the current predominance of serogroup B in the Netherlands collectively warrant consideration of MenB prevention strategies. Historically, meningococcal vaccine implementation in the Netherlands has adopted a reactive approach: vaccines targeting serogroups C and A, C, W and Y were introduced only after outbreaks had already resulted in substantial disease burden. In contrast, early implementation of a new intervention could mitigate future disease burden, reduce long-term healthcare costs and avoid the adverse consequences associated with delayed policy response.
