Lung Parenchymal and Airway Injuries: Differential Diagnosis and Severity Grading
Authors: You Na Kim, Seulgi You, Joo Sung Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Lung parenchymal and airway injuries are common and potentially life-threatening complications of thoracic trauma, requiring rapid and accurate radiological evaluation for optimal patient management. Chest CT remains an important diagnostic tool, because imaging findings-such as the extent of pulmonary injury and precise localization of tracheobronchial tears, often directly guide critical management decisions. This review provides a comprehensive overview of the key imaging features of pulmonary contusion, laceration, and tracheobronchial injury, with emphasis on clinically relevant findings that guide treatment. We also propose a clinical checklist and structured report to facilitate timely communication between radiologists and clinicians.

INTRODUCTION

Thoracic trauma is a major cause of trauma-related morbidity and mortality, occurring in approximately 60% of patients with polytrauma (123). Among the various thoracic injuries, lung parenchymal and airway injuries are particularly critical, as they can rapidly progress to life-threatening respiratory compromise if not promptly identified and managed. Imaging is crucial for diagnosing thoracic trauma. While radiography is typically used for the initial investigation, CT offers a more rapid and precise approach to detecting and characterizing these injuries (34). Accurate radiological assessment not only guides immediate clinical decision-making but also informs the need for intensive care monitoring or surgical intervention.

This review provides a comprehensive overview of the radiologic features and severity grading of lung and airway injuries. Furthermore, we propose a structured reporting template to facilitate timely and effective communication between radiologists and referring clinicians.

CONTUSION

Pulmonary contusion is the most common type of lung injury, occurring in up to 75% of blunt chest trauma cases (567). It represents a non-penetrating injury characterized by alveolar hemorrhage and interstitial edema without disruption of the pulmonary parenchyma. Contusions typically manifest as ground-glass opacities or areas of consolidation with a nonsegmental distribution, often corresponding to the site of impact or to dependent portions of the lung. Subpleural sparing of 1–3 mm has been observed (568). Contusions may be initially undetectable on radiography within the first 6 hours after trauma but typically peak in extent and conspicuity within 48–72 hours (5689). In contrast, CT can readily visualize contusions within minutes of injury. Resolution of uncomplicated contusions typically begins within 24–48 hours, with complete resolution occurring within approximately 7 days (10).

The time course and spatial distribution of pulmonary opacities are useful in differentiating pulmonary contusions from other causes of parenchymal abnormalities after trauma. Pulmonary contusions can develop at the time of injury or within several hours. Therefore, opacities that newly appear 24 hours or more after trauma, increase beyond 2–3 days, or persist for more than 1–2 weeks are atypical and suggest an alternative diagnosis, such as aspiration, infection, or acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS). Unlike aspiration or infectious pneumonia, which follow an airway-centric, lobar, or segmental distribution, pulmonary contusions demonstrate a nonsegmental pattern reflecting transmitted kinetic energy (35891112).

More extensive contusion is associated with impaired alveolar gas exchange and reduced lung compliance, resulting in hypoxemia and increased respiratory effort (6812). Previous studies have shown that the extent of contusion on CT correlates with the need for mechanical ventilation and the development of respiratory complications. In particular, contusions involving more than approximately 20%–30% of total lung volume have been associated with a higher risk of ARDS and prolonged ventilatory support (131415). Early recognition and quantification of the extent of contusion may therefore guide clinical decision-making regarding respiratory support and intensive care unit monitoring. LACERATION

Pulmonary laceration occurs when the lung parenchyma is disrupted or torn due to blunt or penetrating trauma. Pulmonary lacerations typically manifest as spherical or elliptical cavities (1116). This is attributed to the elastic recoil of the surrounding lung tissue, which pulls back from the laceration itself. On initial chest radiographs, lacerations are frequently obscured by surrounding pulmonary contusions. However, CT can readily detect even small cavities in the acute setting (Fig. 3) (317). These cavities may be solitary or multiple, often described as “Swiss cheese” appearance (1118). The laceration cavities can be filled with air (pneumatocele), blood (hematocele or hematoma), or both (hematopneumatocele).

Pulmonary lacerations also cause air leakage, which commonly leads to pneumothorax after blunt trauma (19). Hemothorax and pneumothorax may develop if the laceration extends into the pleural cavity (20). Furthermore, blood may extravasate into the airway and thoracic cavity, potentially resulting in hemorrhagic shock (21).

Pulmonary hematoma manifests as a well-circumscribed, homogeneous mass on CT. As the internal hemorrhage is absorbed over time, it usually evolves into an air-filled pneumatocele (2223). While the majority resolve gradually within five weeks, a pulmonary nodule that persists beyond four weeks may necessitate careful differential diagnosis to exclude malignancy.

Based on the mechanism of injury, pulmonary lacerations are classified into four types (322). Type 1 laceration (compression rupture) is the most common type, caused by a compressive force, and typically results in a central lung cavity. Type 2 laceration (compression-shear) is located in the paraspinal region and occurs when a sudden blow creates a shearing force of the lower lungs across the spine. Type 3 laceration (rib penetration tear) is a peripherally located cavity near fractured ribs, frequently associated with pneumothorax. Type 4 laceration (adhesion tear) occurs adjacent to preexisting pleuropulmonary adhesions and is generally identified during surgery or autopsy.

Compared with pulmonary contusions, lacerations require more time to resolve, often taking weeks to months, and may result in parenchymal scarring (2425). They can occasionally be complicated by a lung abscess, expansion of the laceration cavity through a ball-valve mechanism, bronchopleural fistula, or pseudoaneurysm (111726).

TRACHEOBRONCHIAL INJURY

Tracheobronchial injuries are uncommon but potentially life-threatening complications of severe blunt or penetrating trauma, given the central location and important functions of the central airways (27). Traumatic airway injuries can be categorized into upper and lower airway injuries. Upper airway injuries involve structures superior to the cervical trachea, whereas lower airway injuries include those distal to it (27). Bronchial injuries occur more frequently than tracheal injuries and are usually located within 2.5 cm of the carina, predominantly on the right side (328). Tracheal lacerations commonly occur 2 cm above the carina and frequently involve the posterior membranous wall (529). Bronchial lacerations are typically oriented parallel to the cartilage rings, whereas tracheal lacerations are more often oriented perpendicular to the rings.

Trauma patients presenting with dysphonia, hoarseness, or stridor in conjunction with hemoptysis should be evaluated to identify the site of injury. Furthermore, refractory hypoxemia persisting after endotracheal intubation, or persistent air bubbling from the underwater seal drain, should raise immediate concern for major airway injury (27).

Common imaging findings of tracheobronchial injuries include pneumomediastinum, pneumothorax, and cervical subcutaneous emphysema (311). Persistent pneumothorax or lung collapse despite adequate chest tube drainage should raise suspicion of tracheobronchial injury. In rare cases of complete bronchial transection, the “fallen lung sign” may be observed, in which the affected lung falls away from the hilum—inferiorly on erect radiographs and posteriorly on supine CT scans (30). In intubated patients, herniation or overdistension of an endotracheal tube balloon at the level of a tracheal laceration may also be observed (31). Cuff overdistension in this setting may represent either a contributing factor or a consequence of tracheal wall disruption, as decreased wall resistance at the site of rupture can facilitate balloon overdistension or herniation through the defect (Fig. 4) (32). Direct CT findings include complete cut-off or discontinuity of the tracheobronchial wall, focal intimal flap projection into the lumen, and extraluminal air adjacent to the airway (Fig. 5) (527).

Management was determined based on CT findings and bronchoscopic results. Although bronchoscopy represents the gold standard for the diagnosis of tracheobronchial injury, CT is a rapid and noninvasive method for evaluating the site and extent of injury (29). Surgical management is generally indicated for lacerations measuring greater than 2 cm, particularly those exceeding 4 cm or involving cartilage, demonstrating significant and persistent air leaks despite chest tube placement, or those associated with esophageal or vascular damage (2933). In patients with partial tears or those at high surgical risk, nonoperative management with metallic or silicone stent placement may be considered (29).

PNEUMOMEDIASTINUM: COMMON ASSOCIATED FINDING AND DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSIS

Pneumomediastinum is the most common imaging manifestation of blunt tracheobronchial injury. However, the air in the mediastinum may originate from other sources. The Macklin effect is a phenomenon caused by alveolar rupture and pulmonary interstitial emphysema, with air dissecting along the bronchovascular sheaths toward the mediastinum (34). On CT, it is characterized by linear streaks of air tracking along the bronchovascular bundles.

Esophageal injury may also present with pneumomediastinum. Associated findings, such as periesophageal fluid, mediastinitis, hydropneumothorax, or leakage of orally administered contrast medium into the mediastinum or pleural space, are helpful in differentiating esophageal injury from tracheobronchial injury. Water-soluble contrast esophagography can confirm the site of injury (335).

Additionally, subcutaneous emphysema caused by rib fracture may extend along the fascial planes and spread into the mediastinum (34). An adjacent rib fracture on CT, along with extrapleural air, helps distinguish this source from alveolar or airway-related pneumomediastinum.

CLINICAL CONSIDERATIONS UNDER MECHANICAL VENTILATION

Although intubation and mechanical ventilation are necessary in patients with chest trauma and respiratory failure (333637), they can paradoxically exacerbate lung and airway injuries through barotrauma and air leakage. When positive pressure ventilation (PPV) is applied to patients with simple pneumothorax, it may rapidly progress to tension pneumothorax because of the check-valve mechanism at the pleural defect (3839). Therefore, radiologists should alert clinicians when observing a mediastinal shift, diaphragmatic depression, or deep sulcus sign on supine radiographs. Pneumatoceles associated with pulmonary lacerations can expand rapidly under PPV, resulting in a mass effect on the surrounding lung parenchyma and leading to respiratory compromise (3340). Furthermore, PPV may induce the Macklin effect (3441), and this finding must be carefully differentiated from tracheobronchial or esophageal injuries, as management strategies differ substantially. In rare cases, PPV may contribute to systemic air embolism, which can lead to severe cerebrovascular and circulatory events (3342).

PROPOSAL OF STRUCTURED REPORTING FOR PULMONARY AND AIRWAY TRAUMA

Radiological reports are essential for effective communication between radiologists and referring clinicians. In a trauma setting, structured reports have demonstrated significant advantages, as they are more time-efficient, concise, and provide a clearer diagnostic framework than narrative reports (43). Accordingly, prompt communication through structured reporting directly affects patient management and clinical outcomes. Therefore, we propose a structured reporting template for lung parenchyma and airway trauma (Table 2). This framework integrates established evidence-based classifications with expert opinion-based refinements. The individual grading categories in Table 2 were adapted from established references, including the modified American Association for the Surgery of Trauma (AAST) lung injury scale (1144) and published tracheobronchial injury literature (2729), while the overall structure of the reporting template represents the authors’ expert opinion-based proposal to facilitate systematic reporting in clinical practice.

Grading pulmonary contusion based on these lobar extents is clinically important, as a higher AAST grade reflects a more extensive contusion, which is associated with an increased risk of ARDS and the need for prolonged mechanical ventilation. Classification of pulmonary laceration subtypes is helpful for predicting injury mechanisms and identifying associated injuries (22). Specifically, a persistent air leak from the distal airway lasting more than 72 hours may reflect ongoing communication with the peripheral bronchiole. Major lacerations involving a segment or lobe with imaging findings suggestive of an air leak, such as a persistent or expanding pneumothorax despite chest tube drainage, may necessitate surgical repair or resection (45). Furthermore, the reporting of pulmonary hematomas should distinguish between stable and expanding collections to identify patients requiring immediate intervention for uncontrolled bleeding.

For airway injuries, differentiation between upper and lower airway involvement is essential for determining treatment strategies and surgical planning (2729). The initial priority is to secure the airway, most often through careful intubation, to ensure ventilation (33). Upper airway injuries are usually clinically evident, whereas most lower airway injuries are occult on the primary survey and require a high index of clinical suspicion. The surgical approach depends primarily on the site of the injury. Upper airway injuries are typically accessed via a cervical approach, whereas lower airway injuries are approached through thoracotomy or sternotomy. Small lacerations may be managed conservatively with endotracheal tube cuff inflation distal to the injury site and supplemental oxygenation, or by direct suture. Surgery remains the preferred treatment for patients with large tears. Lung resection may be necessary when the bronchial injury is accompanied by concomitant pulmonary vascular injury or irreversible parenchymal damage (4647). Nonsurgical approaches, such as endobronchial stent placement, have also been proposed for patients with high surgical risk or significant comorbidities.

CONCLUSION

Lung parenchymal and airway injuries require prompt radiological recognition and accurate characterization to guide timely clinical management. Imaging plays an important role in injury detection, severity grading, and the identification of associated complications. Radiologists should provide systematic and clinically relevant reports to communicate effectively with the trauma team. This article proposes a structured reporting template to standardize the assessment of pulmonary and airway trauma, thereby contributing to improved patient outcomes.
