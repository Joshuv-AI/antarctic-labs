Radiographic Analysis of Bone Graft Changes and Anatomical Factors Influencing Maxillary Sinus Floor Augmentation
Authors: Xiao Wei, Fei Yang, Qianrong Zhou, Ruixue Li, Yang Sun, Youcheng Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study aimed to investigate the role of anatomical factors on the outcomes of lateral sinus floor augmentation with simultaneous implant placement using cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) images.MethodsThis retrospective study evaluated 80 patients who underwent a sinus lift procedure with simultaneous implant placement and Bio-Oss particles using the lateral window approach. The patients underwent CBCT scans preoperatively, immediately, and 6 months after surgery. CBCT images were used to measure bone graft changes and anatomical features, including sinus width (SW), sinus angle, Schneiderian membrane thickness (Tm), and sinus floor morphology. Descriptive statistics and multiple linear regression models were used to investigate the correlations between these features.ResultsThe linear changes in apical bone grafts are shown below: apical bone grafts height loss of the implant (aBGHL, median [interquartile range, IQR]: 1.00 [1.53] mm), bone and bone grafts height loss 2 mm away from the implant root horizontally (BGHL, median [IQR]: 1.14 [1.61] mm), apical bone grafts width loss of the implant (aBGWL, median [IQR]: 1.60 [4.15] mm), and bone grafts width loss 2 mm below the implant root (BGWL, median [IQR]: 0.80 [2.00] mm). Both aBGHL and BGHL were positively correlated with SW and Tm, while aBGWL and BGWL were primarily correlated with SW in a series of sequentially adjusted multivariable models.ConclusionDuring the early bone remodelling phase, SW plays a pivotal role in the linear resorption of bone grafts, while Tm primarily influences the loss of bone graft height.Clinical relevanceThe results suggest that sinuses with a wide bucco-palatal bony wall distance and a thick Schneiderian membrane are more likely to experience greater bone substitute loss after sinus floor augmentation. These findings will aid clinicians in the selection of implant sites, as well as determining the type and quantity of bone grafting materials.Key words: Maxillary sinus, Bone remodelling, Sinus floor augmentation, Cone-beam computed tomography

Introduction

The maxillary posterior region, due to pathological conditions such as periodontitis or chronic apical periodontitis, along with physiological factors such as sinus pneumatisation,1 often lacks sufficient residual bone height (RBH) for the placement of dental implants after tooth loss. It is universally acknowledged that maxillary sinus floor augmentation (MSFA), which involves the lateral window approach and transcrestal technique, plays a significant role in handling this challenge.

First proposed by Tatum in 19762 and published by Boyne and James in 1980,3 lateral sinus floor augmentation (LSFA) provides an insusceptible space filled with apical bone grafts where implants can be inserted either simultaneously or gradually. Compared to the transcrestal procedure, the lateral approach allows for precise and direct control of sinus elevation height; however, it may be accompanied by postoperative reactions, complicated operative manipulations, and relatively high bone graft costs. In cases of severe shortage of RBH at the planned implant site, a sinus lift operation using the lateral approach has been found to be the most suitable treatment option.4

Bone substitute materials, utilised universally for implant placement, can achieve similar clinical outcomes compared with autogenous bone,5 and their stability influences the long-term success rate of dental implants.6,7 Additionally, the anatomical morphology of the posterior maxilla affects the osteogenesis and resorption of bone grafting materials. The sinus width (SW) is the most commonly discussed research factor and has been found to be positively associated with graft bone resorption8,9 during transcrestal sinus floor elevation. Several studies have revealed that SW is inversely proportional to new bone formation after either transcrestal10 or lateral sinus floor elevation,11,12 contradicting the findings of another study.13 In addition to SW, the sinus angle (SA) was also strongly associated with bone graft resorption in transcrestal sinus floor elevation, whereas Schneiderian membrane thickness (Tm) exerted no significant influence.9 Additionally, RBH reportedly failed to influence the outcome of LSFA,11, 12, 13, 14, 15 similar to sinus contours.11 While previous studies have reported associations between maxillary sinus anatomical factors (such as SW, SA, and Tm) and bone graft resorption after MSFA, the majority focused on one or only a few variables using univariable analysis, without controlling for interactions and confounding biases. Furthermore, few studies have simultaneously evaluated the influence of multiple anatomical factors on bone graft resorption in both height and width dimensions.

Cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) is an indispensable tool for diagnosis and treatment planning, as it provides relatively lower radiation doses and three-dimensional information.16 To a large extent, CBCT can be considered the gold standard for measuring the maxillary sinus.17 Scholars speculate that pre-surgical evaluations of sinus floor elevation using CBCT should be conducted using a classification grade based on 13 anatomical factors, such as RBH, SW, and Tm.18 Hence, using CBCT measurements, this study aimed to investigated the independent effects of multiple anatomical variables — including RBH, residual bone width (RBW), SW, SA, Tm, thickness of the buccal bony wall of the sinus (Tw), and sinus floor morphology (SFM) — on bone graft height and width resorption in LSFA.

Surgical procedure

All surgeries were performed by the same expert clinician. First, patients rinsed their mouths with 0.12% chlorhexidine for 3 min. After disinfection and administration of local anaesthesia, the lateral wall of the sinus was exposed by incising and reflecting the full-thick flaps. Subsequently, by tapping into the piezoelectric instruments, an osteotomy window was prepared to carefully elevate the Schneiderian membrane. The implant site was prepared according to a preoperative plan. Under the sinus membrane, 0.5 g Bio-Oss particles (Bio-Oss, 1-2 mm, Geistlich, Princeton, NJ, USA) were inserted into each elevated site with a collagen membrane (Bio-Gide, 13ⅹ25 mm, Geistlich, Princeton, NJ, USA) covering the osteotomy window, and the implant was placed simultaneously. The insertion torques of the implants were > 15 N·cm. The flaps were then repositioned and sutured (Figure 1).

Process of lateral maxillary sinus floor augmentation. (A) The images show the operation area pre-surgery, (B-C) elevation of the sinus membrane, (D-E) socket preparation, (F-H) implantation of bone grafts and collagen membrane with simultaneous implant placement, and (I) suturing for hemostasis.

After surgery, all patients received antimicrobials (amoxicillin 500 mg TID for 7 days or azithromycin for 5 days with a loading dose of 500 mg on day 1 and 250 mg thereafter), and analgesics (ibuprofen 400 mg every 6 h) when needed. Oral hygiene instructions, such as using chlorhexidine mouthwash thrice daily for 2 weeks, were also provided to the patients. The sutures were removed 2 weeks postoperatively.

Radiographic evaluation

CBCT images were obtained preoperatively, immediately, and 6 months after surgery using a CBCT machine (KaVo Dental, Biberach, Germany). The parameters were as follows: slice thickness 0.2 mm, voxel size 0.25 mm3, tube voltage 150 kV, tube current 5 mA, and acquisition period 7s. The CBCT images were transferred to the software (Mimics 15.0, Materialise NV, Leuven, Belgium) in the form of “DICOM” for further measurements. Using Mimics 15.0, 3D models of the surgical area were reconstructed based on grey‑value thresholds followed by manual editing. Using specific dental morphological markers (incisal corner, cusp-fossa morphology or the apex of a dental defect), the models, which were obtained from each patient at different time points, were superimposed, and the measurement site was then localised corresponding to the implant-placement site (Figure 2). All measurements were performed on the original CBCT images by two independent and previously calibrated examiners unaware of the clinical data. The examiners underwent a calibration training with the principal investigator to align with measurement criteria prior to the formal study. Subsequently, the inter-examiner reliability was assessed using a data set of 20 randomly selected CBCT scans independent of the study. The calibration process continued until the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of continuous variables exceeded 0.90 and the Kappa value (κ) of categorical variables exceeded 0.80 respectively in the assessment. In the study, for continuous variables, the mean of the two examiners’ measurements was used for the formal analysis. Meanwhile, for categorical variables, in case of disagreement between the two examiners, a third senior investigator was consulted for the final decision. Statistical analysis

All data analyses were performed using statistical software (IBM SPSS Statistics, Version 27.0, IBM Corp., USA). Descriptive statistics were calculated as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range [IQR]) depending on whether the data was normally distributed. The inter-examiner reliability of continuous variables was assessed by ICC.20 κ statistics were used to evaluate the agreement between examiners for categorical variables.21 Univariable and multivariable linear regression models were used to evaluate the relationships between linear graft bone resorption and anatomical variables, including RBH, RBW, SW, SA, Tm, Tw, and SFM. Anatomical factors were included as explanatory variables in multivariable models only if they reached a P-value < .1 in the univariable linear regression analysis. Three models were used in the multivariable linear regression analysis of each anatomical variable on the basis of the stepwise regression method: (1) unadjusted, (2) number of implants in unilateral LSFA and implant system adjusted, and (3) multivariable adjusted, including variables in model 2 plus gender and age. Statistical significance was achieved when the P-value was < .05.

Results

Study population

A total of eighty implants from 80 patients (47 males and 33 females) were analysed in this study (Figure 5). The average age of patients was 59.23 ± 11.95 years, ranging from 23 to 85 years. One implant per patient was evaluated, with 7 implants in the premolar region and 73 in the molar region. Fourteen patients had a single implant placed during unilateral LSFA, and 66 patients had multiple consecutive implants placed. Three types of implants were used: Nobel (Sweden), Bego (Germany), and Straumann (Switzerland). The basic characteristics of the patients and implants are shown in detail in Table 1.

Radiographic evaluations of baseline characteristics

The anatomical characteristics of the implants were measured at baseline. The inter-examiner reliability was classified as excellent for the measurement of RBH, RBW, SW, SA, Tm and Tw (RBH: ICC = 0.97 [95%CI = 0.94-0.98]; RBW: ICC=0.96 [95%CI = 0.93-0.98]; SW: ICC = 0.98 [95%CI = 0.96-0.99]; SA: ICC = 0.98 [95%CI = 0.96-0.99]; Tm: ICC = 0.95 [95%CI = 0.91-0.97]; Tw: ICC = 0.98 [95%CI = 0.97-0.99]), and the inter-examiner agreement was good for the evaluation of SFM (κ = 0.85, [95%CI = 0.74-0.96]). The RBH of implants ranged from 1.0 to 5.0 mm, with a mean value of 3.53 ± 0.95 mm, while the mean RBW was 9.10 ± 3.19 mm, ranging from 3.0 mm to 18.0 mm. The average values of SW and SA were 14.83 ± 3.04 mm (range, 8.60−25.60 mm) and 87.03 ± 9.87° (range, 67.00−114.00°), respectively. The median [IQR] Tw was 1.60 [0.80] mm; the median [IQR] Tm was 1.20 [1.75] mm. Regarding the contour of the sinus floor at the implant sites, 17.50% were tapered, 58.75% were ovoid, and 23.75% were square. SW was positively correlated with SA (r = 0.748, P < .001), RBW (r = 0.370, P < .001) (Figure 6), and SFM (r = 0.710, P < .001) (Table 2). No statistically significant correlation was found between SW and Tw (r = -0.213, P = .057) (Table 2).

CBCT measurements of bone grafts of the implant and influencing factors

The ICC of aBGH, BGH, aBGW and BGW immediately after surgery were 0.93 [95%CI = 0.89-0.95], 0.92 [95%CI = 0.87-0.95], 0.92 [95%CI = 0.87-0.94] and 0.90 [95%CI = 0.87-0.93] respectively, while the ICC of aBGH, BGH, aBGW and BGW 6 months after surgery were 0.94 [95%CI = 0.89-0.97], 0.93 [95%CI = 0.90-0.96], 0.93 [95%CI = 0.89-0.96] and 0.92 [95%CI = 0.88-0.94]. The ICC of IPL was 0.93 [95%CI = 0.90-0.94]. They all demonstrated high inter-observer reliability. The mean SEH was 9.89 ± 1.73 mm (range, 6.70−14.80 mm) immediately after LSFA with simultaneous implant placement. Meanwhile, the mean IPL was 6.50 ± 0.99 mm (range, 4.30−8.95 mm). The median aBGH was 3.08 mm (IQR: 2.39 mm) immediately after surgery, decreasing to 1.98 mm (IQR: 1.58 mm) 6 months after surgery; BGH averaged 14.04 ± 1.61 mm immediately after surgery, decreasing to 12.64 ± 1.37 mm 6 months after surgery. Similarly, aBGW was 15.53 ± 4.06 mm immediately after surgery, decreasing to 12.33 ± 4.08 mm 6 months after surgery; BGW was 15.09 ± 2.93 mm and 13.60 ± 2.74 mm, respectively, at the same time points. Furthermore, the aBGHL (median [IQR]: 1.00 [1.53] mm), BGHL (median [IQR]: 1.14 [1.61] mm), aBGWL (median [IQR]: 1.60 [4.15] mm), and BGWL (median [IQR]: 0.80 [2.00] mm) of 80 implants were measured.

Discussion

Lateral maxillary sinus augmentation is a reliable, safe, and efficient approach for achieving vertical bone gain, particularly in patients with insufficient RBH.4 Allowing functional remodelling, bone substitutes function as scaffolds for blood clots and are gradually replaced with new bone, the stability of which plays a pivotal role in osteogenesis in the sinus.7,22 Additionally, bone grafts apical to the implant apex alleviate bone strain around the collar of supporting implants, with a slight increase in strain in the sinus floor region.23 Although there are successful reports of non-grafting lateral sinus floor elevation,22,24,25 the classical grafting protocol can achieve more stable and predictable outcomes, as bone grafts provide physical support for space maintenance to facilitate new bone formation26,27 as well as decrease the stress around the implant neck.

After LSFA, the bone repair process begins with the aggregation of osteogenic progenitor cells and the exposure of the bone matrix of the sinus bony walls.9 Apical bone grafts of the implant are in direct contact with the sinus bony walls, sinus floor, and Schneiderian membrane, and the main nutrients come from the blood supply of the maxillary sinus, rather than the alveolar process. Consequently, unlike sinus-related variables, RBH showed no direct impact on postoperative bone resorption in LSFA in this study, a finding consistent with several previous reports.11,12,14,15

Although composed of cortical bone, the lateral wall of the maxillary sinus possesses significant osteogenic potential.28 The aBGHL, BGHL, aBGWL, and BGWL were positively correlated with SW, as demonstrated by their stable and statistically significant effect estimates across multivariable models with increasing adjustments. The consistent direction and significance of these associations across crude, partially, and fully adjusted models validated our findings. The wider the sinus, the greater the graft bone resorption around the apex of the implant. Compared with spacious maxillary sinuses, narrow maxillary sinuses show shorter bucco-palatal bony wall distance while elevating the equivalent sinus membrane from the floor, with better access to osteogenic progenitor cells and blood supply, which facilitates cell colonisation and new bone formation in view of the centripetal gradient of bone formation,29 not only in transcrestal sinus floor elevation8,10,30 but also in LSFA.11,12,31 Furthermore, the tented space, supported by the implant apex and bone substitutes, is subjected to increased tension in the Schneiderian membrane in wider sinuses,8 impairing the osteogenic and angiogenic processes. In this study, SW was measured 5 mm above the sinus floor, a level consistent with the height of bone grafts around the implant apex, considering that the RBH averaged 3.53 ± 0.95 mm and simultaneous-inserted implants were all 10 mm in length.

In addition to SW, a positive correlation was observed between Tm and bone graft height loss (aBGHL and BGHL) in a series of sequentially adjusted models. The regression coefficients of Tm remained positive and statistically significant in all three models, indicating the potential osteogenic role of the Schneiderian membrane. Several studies have demonstrated that the sinus mucosa contains pluripotent mesenchymal stem cells, which are useful for osteogenesis and bone remodelling during dental implant placement.32, 33, 34 Nevertheless, compared to the surrounding sinus walls and septa, the osteogenic role of the mucosa is weaker,35 especially in the early stages of healing.36 Kevin et al.37 observed a pathological response of the paranasal sinuses to sinusitis when the mucosal thickness exceeded 2 mm. This may increase the number of bacteria or other organisms, which is linked to greater bone graft height loss,38 in line with the findings of this study. Nonetheless, some scholars posit that Schneiderian membrane thickness and antral pseudocysts do not significantly influence graft bone remodelling and new bone formation after transcrestal sinus floor elevation.9,39 This difference can be explained by possible variations in surgical approach (lateral window versus transcrestal), graft material, inclusion criteria, methodologies for graft resorption evaluation, as well as statistical analyses. Unlike the conventional univariable analysis, multivariable linear regression with sequential adjustment for confounders can better investigate the role of these anatomical variables on the outcomes of LSFA. Despite reports that implants can succeed even with sinus mucosal thickening or pseudocysts,40, 41, 42 mucosal thickness > 5 mm may cause ostial obstruction and sinusitis after MSFA.43,44 Hence, only patients with a Schneiderian membrane thickness ≤ 5 mm were included.

According to the univariable analysis, RBW, SA, and SFM appeared to correlate with bone graft resorption, which aligns with the findings of a previous study.9 Nevertheless, given the potential multicollinearity and confounding factors, SW and Tm may be more useful and reliable for predicting graft height loss 6 months after surgery based on multivariable linear regression analysis, especially considering that SW was strongly correlated with SA (r = 0.748, P < .001), RBW (r = 0.370, P < .001), and SFM (r = 0.710, P < .001). Consequently, SW and Tm remained independently correlated with graft height loss after adjustment for relevant confounders among these variables, whereas the apparent effects of RBW, SA, and SFM were attenuated in multivariable analysis.

Rather than focusing on only one or two factors, this study provides relatively comprehensive evaluations of the independent effects of 7 anatomical variables on graft resorption by sequentially adjusted multivariable linear regression models. SW was consistently associated with graft resorption in both height and width dimensions, while Tm was primarily related to graft height loss. In cases with a wide sinus, graft height and width stability are both compromised. Considering the strong influence of SW and Tm on bone graft loss, clinicians should tailor the grafting material categories and quantities in terms of SW and Tm at the planned implant sites before surgery. Autologous bone, which is osteogenic, osteoconductive, and osteoinductive, remains the gold standard for the sinus lift procedure.45 Also, anorganic bovine bone such as Bio-Oss primarily serves as osteoconductive scaffolds and typically exhibit a slower resorption rate.46 However, due to the limitations of autogenous bone harvesting, such as donor site morbidity and limited availability, bone substitutes are often combined with autogenous bone in clinical practice for MSFA to optimise osteogenic efficacy and surgical safety.47,48 Consequently, for sinuses with greater widths and thicker membranes, more bone substitutes or those combined with autografts should be used.45,30 In clinical studies, the role of SW has been elaborated on more frequently in graft bone maturation and remodelling after LSFA.11,12,31,49 However, researchers tend to overlook the effects of Schneiderian membrane thickness, which may introduce bias to the results.

Despite these findings, this study had some limitations. In this study, all patients underwent a comprehensive periodontal assessment (including probing and periapical radiographs) prior to surgery. Included patients had no untreated periodontitis. Any periodontitis present was no more severe than Stage I (localised) following treatment and re-evaluation, and no local signs of periodontal or periapical inflammation were observed. The multivariable linear regression models adjusted for the number of implants in unilateral LSFA and implant system, and the fully adjusted model further included age and sex. Although several important confounders were adjusted, residual confounding from unmeasured factors cannot be ruled out. Nevertheless, the consistency across models alleviates this concern to some extent. Additionally, this study focused mainly on the changes in bone grafts during the early healing period. Long-term prospective cohort studies with larger sample sizes are required to avoid potential biases. However, findings from long-term studies suggest that most graft bone resorption occurred during the initial healing phase,7,50,51 especially in the first 6 months.52,53 Consequently, the loss of bone graft material in the early postoperative phase may represent total bone graft resorption to a certain extent, indicating that the present research method is feasible. Moreover, due to the limitations of CBCT, differences still exist between CBCT images and gold standards such as micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) and histologic assessment.54, 55, 56 Notwithstanding, CBCT images remain highly valuable for clinical reference, given the modality's accessibility and prevalence.57, 58, 59

Conclusion

Among these anatomical variables, SW and Tm serve as independent determinants of early bone graft resorption after LSFA. SW was positively correlated with both height and width resorption of graft bone materials, while Tm was primarily associated with height resorption. Sinuses with wide bucco-palatal bony wall distances and thick Schneiderian membranes are more likely to experience greater bone substitute loss after LSFA, which requires more bone grafts or those combined with autografts during surgery. Clinicians should evaluate the SW and Tm of the planned implant site before the sinus lift procedure in order to guide the selection of implant sites, along with the type and quantity of bone grafting materials.
