Analysis of the Gas-Bearing Structure and Carbon Isotope Fractionation Characteristics in Deep Coal: Insights from In Situ Gas Desorption and Molecular Simulations
Authors: Yu Qiao, Shuangbiao Han, Ye Wang, Jie Huang, Yu Liu, Yongping Wan, Zhenchuan Wang, Liang Qiao, Yurun Rui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Carbon isotope fractionation plays a key role in understanding the occurrence state, migration mechanism, and resource potential assessment of deep coalbed methane (CBM). The gas-bearing characteristics and carbon isotope fractionation behaviors of deep coal in the eastern margin of the Ordos Basin were systematically investigated using in situ gas desorption and molecular simulations. In situ gas desorption experiments were conducted on coal samples to determine the gas content, gas components, and isotopes. Molecular simulations were conducted by constructing a coal molecular model to simulate methane adsorption and diffusion in pores under varying nanoscales, temperatures, and pressures, thereby revealing the microscopic mechanisms governing gas-bearing properties of deep coal. The results show that the free/adsorbed gas ratio in the desorption process of deep coal ranges from 0.94 to 2.22. The measured δ13CH4 values are generally heavy and exhibit a gradual enrichment trend at different temperature stages. The microscopic theoretical model of methane molecular adsorption and diffusion is innovatively proposed, which reveals that methane in deep coal mainly exists in the adsorbed state, and the free state content is gradually increasing. Meanwhile, during methane migration, there are differences in the diffusion rates of isotopes. The macroscopic experiments reflect the overall trend of methane desorption under field conditions, while the microscopic simulations reveal the microscopic mechanism of methane adsorption/desorption at the pore scale. The two complement each other from different perspectives and jointly support the research conclusion, providing a new perspective for the study of the enrichment laws of deep coalbed methane.

Introduction

In the context of global low-carbon energy structural transformation, coalbed methane has gained substantially enhanced strategic value as a clean alternative energy source. , High-yield breakthroughs of gas flow have been achieved at depths of 1000–3000 m in the Ordos Basin, Qinshui Basin, Junggar Basin, and the southeastern part of the Sichuan Basin. , Thus, deep coalbed methane has attracted increasing attention. Energy exploration is undergoing a revolutionary transformation to deeper strata. The eastern margin of the Ordos Basin is a critical area for unconventional natural gas enrichment in China. , The exploration and development of deep coalbed methane (buried depth >1500m) has become a trending topic in academic circles. Recent advancements in reservoir fine characterization technologies for this key region have facilitated the effective exploitation of proven coalbed methane reserves, providing solid and critical technical support for the optimization and upgrading of China’s energy structure. ,

Currently, the development strategies for deep coalbed methane (CBM) generally borrow from mature shale gas development paradigms. However, substantial discrepancies in reservoir properties and production behaviors exist between the two resources, and systematic research targeting deep CBM remains in its preliminary stage. Deep coal reservoirs contain adsorbed and free gas. ,− Adsorbed methane is dominant in deep coalbed methane reservoirs. ,, Adsorption and free methane content are the key parameters affecting the mining process of deep coalbed methane. Nevertheless, the dynamic conversion between adsorbed and free gas during CBM production exhibits highly complex coupled behaviors, leading to an incomplete understanding of the in situ production evolution mechanism. , The adsorption capacity of coal seams determines the content of coalbed methane. , The pore structure of deep coal seams is influenced by high stress, temperatures, and pressure, affecting the gas state of coalbed methane. , Coal is a heterogeneous porous material with a dual-pore structure, and the pore structure of deep coal is influenced by high stress, temperatures, and pressure. − The pore and fracture structure significantly affects the gas state of coalbed methane, , as well as methane adsorption, desorption, diffusion, and seepage. −

Methane carbon isotopes serve as critical geochemical tracers for gas source identification and migration mechanism interpretation. Since Qi(1985) established a research system for coalbed methane isotopes in China, this indicator has been widely applied to CBM source discrimination, geological condition evaluation, and production dynamic monitoring. Nevertheless, isotope fractionation remains a core scientific bottleneck restricting the mechanistic understanding of deep CBM systems. Compared with shallow CBM reservoirs, deep high-temperature and high-pressure environments induce considerably more complex isotopic fractionation effects during methane adsorption, desorption, diffusion, and migration. This complexity is primarily governed by the unique nanoscale pore systems of deep coal seams and their coupled interactions with multiphase gas components. Although previous studies have achieved substantial advances in revealing deep CBM reservoir formation dynamics, characterizing reservoir heterogeneity, and developing high-efficiency exploitation technologies, microscale controlling mechanisms of gas occurrence remain poorly understood. In recent years, different scholars have found that there are multistage fractionation characteristics in the process of methane migration through core desorption experiments, seepage-diffusion experiments, wellhead gas detection, molecular simulation and other studies, which may be related to gas production, mineral composition, gas content, and gas phase state. Although on-site measurements provide in situ geological parameters, it is limited by the complexity of underground conditions, and its test conditions and costs are high. Molecular simulation can be used to assess the interaction between gas and the coal matrix at the atomic level. Relevant studies have adopted carbon nanotube-based molecular dynamics (MD) models to reveal methane molecular diffusion characteristics − and have further clarified the influence of variable nanopore structures on gas transport behaviors and resultant methane isotopic evolution via diverse shale pore simulation systems.

We propose a research framework that combines the macroscopic observation with molecular simulation. Through high-precision coal analysis experiments, starting from macroscopic geological phenomena and combining microscopic simulations at the pore scale, we studied the structural characteristics and isotope fractionation process of deep coalbed methane in the Ordos Basin from different perspectives. This has injected new scientific connotations into the theoretical system for the accumulation of deep coalbed methane.

Geological Setting

The Ordos Basin, a giant petroliferous and coal-bearing sedimentary basin in northwestern China, represents a vital energy resource base with profound geological research significance. Benefiting from its superior resource endowments and typical tectonic-sedimentary evolution characteristics, this basin has long been a core focus of national energy exploration and geological research. The northern boundary of the Ordos Basin is the Yimeng Uplift at the great bend of the Yellow River and the southern boundary is the Weibei Plateau. It extends to the Yellow River Valley at the junction of Qin and Jin in the east and includes the Shizuishan-Yinchuan-Guyuan syncline in the west, spanning Shaanxi, Gansu, Ningxia, Inner Mongolia, and Shanxi provinces. As a large-scale synclinal sedimentary basin, the basin center is dominated by fully developed Paleozoic and Mesozoic stratigraphic sequences. In terms of scale, it stretches approximately 640–700 km from north to south and about 400 km from east to west, covering an area ranging from 275,000 to 370,000 km2.

During the Late Carboniferous to Early Permian, the study area experienced a sedimentary facies transition from barrier coastal settings to fluvial-deltaic systems. Thick coal seams are widely distributed in the Carboniferous-Permian strata of the basin, among which No. 5 coal seams of the Shanxi Formation and the No. 8 coal seams of the Benxi Formation serve as the primary coal-bearing units. They have a high hydrocarbon generation capacity and a thermal evolution degree.

Distinct sedimentary differences control the heterogeneous development of the two coal seams. The No. 5 coal seam in the second member of the Shanxi Formation was deposited in a continental fluvial-deltaic environment, where widespread swamp facies prevailed during deposition and resulted in relatively thin coal thickness. In comparison, the No. 8 coal reservoirs of the Benxi Formation originated in a lagoonal sedimentary environment and are currently covered by forest swamps. , This coal seam features a greater thickness, and its coal type is classified as bright to semibright coal. In Situ Gas Desorption and Laboratory Testing

Coal core specimens were acquired via conventional drilling and coring techniques. Field desorption experiments were conducted to obtain in situ gas content, gas samples, and core specimens for subsequent laboratory testing. Prior to on-site coring operations, the desorption device was fully assembled and calibrated in advance. Immediately after core retrieval, the surface of each coal sample was thoroughly cleaned to remove the drilling contamination. Basic sample parameters, including core length and weight, were precisely measured, and the corresponding sample numbers were recorded in detail. Subsequently, the sample was placed into the desorption tank, which was filled with saturated saltwater and sealed to facilitate natural desorption. The volume of desorbed gas was separately quantified under ambient temperature and in situ formation temperature conditions, while the residual gas volume was measured through high-temperature heating treatment. The lost gas volume was determined by performing a linear regression of the square root of the measured desorption gas volume and the desorption time. The cumulative desorption amount in the standard state was plotted on the ordinate axis, and the square root of the sum of the lost gas and desorption time was plotted on the abscissa. The intercept of the fitted curve on the vertical axis corresponds to the lost gas volume of the coal sample (Figure ).

Relationship between coal petrographic desorption and gas occurrence. Adapted with permission from ref . Copyright 2021, Oil & Gas Geology.

The experimental temperature settings were determined based on measured in situ geothermal temperatures (65–75 °C) of target coal seams within the study area. This selection precisely restores the actual geothermal environment of the reservoir, while also meeting the requirements for the stable operation of the experimental device. Traditionally, coal crushing-desorption approaches have been widely adopted for residual gas testing in field practices. Alternatively, temperature heating methods have achieved favorable performance for residual gas quantification in shale gas research in recent years. In this work, the residual gas was obtained by heating the high-temperature water bath to 90 °C. This closed-system measurement protocol avoids damage to the original pore structure and gas occurrence state of coal samples, ensuring the accuracy of test results. A three-stage temperature progression, namely, normal temperature, ground temperature, and elevated high temperature, was implemented to simulate the dynamic desorption processes of deep CBM. The desorption, residual, and lost gas volumes were recorded in real time to calculate the total gas content and the ratio of adsorbed to free gas. Gas samples were obtained at specific times, and the environmental temperature, pressure, and gas volume were recorded. This multistage experimental design possesses rigorous geological and engineering scientific rationality: simulating the natural desorption behavior of reservoir sample collection and ground storage and transportation at the normal temperature stage, characterizing the basic desorption characteristics of samples at normal temperature and pressure ; the 70 °C geothermal condition simulates the natural desorption under the original geothermal of the reservoir and characterizes the core ability of in situ desorption. The high-temperature stage (90 °C) further reveals the ultimate desorption potential of coal rocks under thermal stimulation, providing reliable engineering references for evaluating reservoir modification potential during deep CBM development. Coal samples (Shanxi Formation and Benxi Formation) were selected based on the gas content and the coal seam’s roof and floor conditions. The basic parameters of the rock samples were obtained. A gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS) isotope analyzer was used to calculate the relative abundance of 13C and 12C in the compound by detecting the phase ion peak and calculating the δ13C value. The equation is as follows: 1 δ13C(‰)=(RSARST−1)×1000 Here, R SA is the ratio of 13CH4 to 12CH4 in the sample and R ST is the ratio of 13C to 12C in the reference material. We used the South Carolina PEE DEE arrowhead fossil (PDB) as an international standard. The international standard value of 13C/12C is 0.0112372.

A series of basic experimental tests have been conducted on the coal samples, including total organic carbon (TOC) determination, vitrinite reflectance measurement, low-temperature carbon dioxide adsorption test, low-temperature nitrogen adsorption test, high-pressure mercury intrusion test, and mineral composition analysis. TOC is an indicator of the potential of shale gas and coalbed methane resources, reflecting the organic matter content of rocks. The samples were acidified and injected into the analyzer, and the difference between total carbon and inorganic carbon-total organic carbon (TOC) was obtained by carbon dioxide detection. The pore structure was analyzed to clarify the occurrence and distribution of the coalbed methane. We conducted low-temperature carbon dioxide adsorption, low-temperature nitrogen adsorption, and high-pressure mercury injection tests of coal samples to obtain experimental parameters for subsequent molecular simulation. X-ray diffraction (XRD) was used to determine the mineral composition and relative content of the samples.

Molecular Model

Molecular Model Establishment

Molecular simulations serve as powerful tools for elucidating the microscale mechanisms of carbon isotope fractionation. Complementary high-precision gas desorption experiments and carbon isotope testing were performed to acquire reliable experimental data for the mechanism analysis. The results reveal that distinct fractionation behaviors of the 12CH4/13CH4 isotope pair occur during the methane phase transition. This finding provides fundamental theoretical support for the molecular-scale interpretation and application of methane isotope geochemistry in deep CBM research. Future work will focus on establishing a constrained molecular dynamics (MD) model for porous coal media to further clarify the kinetic mechanisms of isotope fractionation under nanoconfinement.

This study innovatively proposes a coupled computing framework consisting of the Large-Scale Atomic-Molecular Massively Parallel Simulator (LAMMPS) and Materials Studio software programs. The scheme is based on the first-principles calculation, that is, the density functional theory (DFT), to establish the electron cloud distribution model. Based on this, a large-scale molecular dynamics (MD) simulation was carried out to characterize the microscale desorption kinetics behavior of CBM. A refined potential energy surface parameter database is established to trace the migration trajectories and energy exchange processes of isotope molecules on the picosecond (10–12 s) time scale. This multiscale computational strategy provides a quantitative theoretical basis for unraveling isotope fractionation mechanisms within nanometer pore systems of deep coal reservoirs.

The physical and chemical characteristics of the coal samples are required to construct a molecular model. The TOC content (Table ) and pore structure are crucial factors (Table and Figure ). On the basis of solid-state 13C nuclear magnetic resonance experiments, the specific macromolecular structures of deep coals were obtained. The average TOC content of the coal in this study area is 73.71%, and the average vitrinite reflectance is 2.81%. It belongs to high-rank coal, and its properties are close to those of highly carbonized graphene plates. Coal is mainly filled with micropores and macropores, and there are some mesopores. Among them, micropores mainly provide pore volume for pores smaller than 1.5 nm, while mesopores are concentrated around 3 nm pores. Therefore, a composite model of organic matter pores at different nanoscales was constructed, as shown in Figure . These models can truly reflect the microstructure characteristics inside coal samples, providing accurate physical scenarios for the subsequent simulation studies.

Molecular trajectory information, energy evolution characteristics, spatial distribution, and diffusion coefficients were quantitatively extracted from simulation outputs. By comparing carbon isotope fractionation behaviors under varying temperature, pressure, and pore structure conditions, the dominant influencing factors, including pore geometry, surface properties, and thermodynamic conditions, were systematically clarified. The integrated macroscale and microscale analysis framework established in this study effectively reveals the intrinsic fractionation mechanisms of deep coalbed methane. Furthermore, the improved carbon isotope fractionation model overcomes the inherent limitations of traditional empirical models, enabling more accurate characterization of in situ gas occurrence states and multiscale transport behaviors within coal nanopore systems.

Results and Discussion

Characteristics of Deep Coal Reservoirs

The coal lithotypes developed in the study area primarily include bright coal, semi-bright coal, and semi-dull coal. The macroscopic coal characteristics are described as follows (Figure ): bright coal (represented by the Y1711 well sample): this lithotype exhibits intense and homogeneous surface luster with distinct, well-developed stratification. The coal matrix is predominantly composed of clarain and vitrain with a high compositional purity. The coal body is dense, the fracture is flat, the whole is delicate and continuous layered structure, and the characteristics of the primary sedimentary structure are remarkable. Semi-bright coal (represented by the Y2085 well sample): This lithotype shows moderate luster and continuous bedding development. The coal is composed of clarain and vitrain (or a small amount of fusain). The fracture smoothness is inferior to that of bright coal, and the texture compactness is slightly lower. It can be seen that the mixed structural characteristics of multiple macerals are present. Semi-dull coal: This lithotype is characterized by weak luster and poorly developed bedding with irregular fracture morphologies.

In general, coals within the study area are dominated by primary sedimentary structures and contain an abundance of natural cleats and fractures. Such macroscopic structural features lay a favorable geological foundation for the superior physical properties of deep coal reservoirs and provide essential geological conditions for the subsequent CBM exploration and development.

The vitrinite reflectance (R o) of coal samples ranged from 2.08% to 3.16%, with an average value of 2.78% (Table ), indicating high-rank coal. It is generally in the overmature and dry gas generation stage. The coalbed methane potential was high, suggesting that it is a high-yield gas reservoir. The TOC of the coal samples was high, ranging from 57.13% to 85.75%, with an average content of 75.89% (Table ). The organic carbon content was high, indicating high adsorption capacity. The average moisture (M ad) content of the coal is 0.76%; the average volatile matter (V ad) content is 6.51%; the average ash (A ad) content is 11.15%; and the average fixed carbon (F Cd) content is 74.46% (Table ).

The organic components were dominated by vitrinite (42.5%–71.6%, with an average of 61.37%), followed by inertinite (2.4%–28.8%, with an average of 16.53%), and lipids (0.3%–3.0%, with an average of 1.60%). The inorganic component content was 2.5%–41.7%, with an average of 19.47% (Figure ). The dominant minerals were clay minerals, accounting for an average of 61.8%. The coal contained large amounts of quartz (average of 8.13%), calcite (average of 5.22%), and pyrite (average of 3.53%) and some carbonate minerals (Figure ).

Components of coal.

Mineral composition of coal.

The coal samples obtained from the study area are characterized by well-developed microporous systems. The adsorption capacity continuously increases with increasing relative pressure, demonstrating favorable gas storage potential. Pore volume exhibits an obvious peak distribution, which is predominantly contributed by pores with diameters of 0.4–0.7 nm. Notably, the directional shrinkage of local gelatinous components during coalification facilitates pore homogenization within the coal matrix (Figures a,b). Based on the classification of gas adsorption curves by IUPAC, the morphology of the low-temperature nitrogen adsorption–desorption curve in Figure b can be classified as Type II and Type III. The mesopores had a small proportion and consisted of slit-shaped H3 and H4 (Figure b,e). It shows that the pore connectivity is crossed, which is not conducive to gas seepage. Furthermore, mesopore structural heterogeneity is jointly regulated by the pore compression effect under in situ stress and the pore-filling behavior of clay minerals.

Pore characteristics of the coal samples. (a) Microporous adsorption curve. (b) Mesoporous adsorption–desorption curve. (c) Mercury withdrawal curve shape. (d) Micropore volume contribution curve. (e) Mesoporous pore volume contribution curve. (f) Macropore volume contribution curve.

Mercury intrusion porosimetry (MIP) results indicate that the macropore diameters of the tested coals are mainly concentrated within 10–100 μm. Mercury saturation presents an approximately linear growth trend with increasing injection pressure. In the high-pressure region, mercury could fill most of the pore space, and this characteristic indirectly confirmed the existence of a large number of micropores in the coal samples (Figure c). It is worth noting that there were significant differences in the mercury intrusion–extrusion curves among different coal samples: the curves of some coal samples tended to be flat in the high-pressure section, indicating that their pores were basically filled with mercury and the pore connectivity was good. Good connectivity is conducive to the desorption of adsorbed gas. However, the mercury injection volume of other coal samples increased rapidly in the high-pressure section. This phenomenon implies the presence of abundant unfilled tiny pores, into which mercury can further intrude with elevated external pressure (Figure f).

A full-scale pore characterization of the coal was carried out by combining low-temperature CO2 (less than 2 nm), low-temperature N2 adsorption (2–50 nm), and high-pressure mercury injection (50–100000 nm) experiments. The results show that the total pore volume of the coal was 0.0547–0.1275 cm3/g, with an average of 0.084 cm3/g (Table ). The main pore volume was provided by micropores (average pore volume of 0.0615 cm3/g, average proportion of 77.23%), followed by macropores (average pore volume of 0.0200 cm3/g, average proportion of 20.57%), and mesopores (average pore volume of 0.0022 cm3/g, average proportion of 2.20%) (Figure ). The micropores were dominant, increasing the specific surface area of the coal and improving the methane adsorption capacity. The macropores provide space for free methane, which is consistent with the high gas content and large proportion of free gas.

The Gas Content of Coal and the Variation Characteristics of Methane Carbon Isotopes

Figure a shows the field desorption test results of 20 coal seam core samples from 3 wells in the Yanchang block. The gas content was 13.59–28.51 m3/t, with an average of 20.78 m3/t. The content of the No. 5 coal was 16.35–28.51 m3/t, with an average of 22.96 m3/t. The content of the No. 8 coal was 13.59–23.86 m3/t, with an average of 18.60 m3/t, which is mainly due to the No. 5 coal’s stronger gas adsorption capacity from more developed micropores and higher vitrinite content. The lost gas content was 0.54–8.35 m3/t, with an average of 3.80 m3/t, and the content of the residual gas was 0.55–4.22 m3/t, with an average of 1.95 m3/t. The No. 5 coal seam exhibits a larger volume of lost gas relative to residual gas, whereas the No. 8 coal seams present an opposite gas distribution pattern. This distinct discrepancy is primarily attributed to the inherent differences in the pore porosity and permeability between the two target coal seams. Furthermore, coal structural characteristics serve as a vital factor controlling the lost gas content. Broken coal has more fracture surfaces, damaging adsorption sites and forming unobstructed gas migration channels, leading to higher gas loss than intact coal with a complete matrix and fewer fractures. The ratio of adsorbed gas to free gas and the gas production efficiency are critical factors in deep coalbed methane exploration and development. This ratio was obtained based on the measured gas volume at room temperature, ground temperature, and high temperature. Because of the large difference in the lost gas in the gas-bearing structure, the desorption gas is selected for calculation.

Shale gas and coalbed methane both belong to self-generated and self-stored natural gas reservoirs. They share commonalities in many aspects such as rich formation conditions, depositional environment conditions, and engineering mechanical conditions for development. Current studies commonly employ indoor isothermal adsorption experiments to measure coal’s gas adsorption capacity and estimate free gas volume based on pore volume theory. This method, however, has notable limitations. Crushing coal samples for testing triggers massive loss of free gas in primary fractures. Additionally, laboratory setups cannot reconstruct formation effective stress, in situ water saturation, or multicomponent competitive adsorption environments. The method only statically calculates theoretical adsorbed gas volume under isolated temperature and pressure conditions, lacking a basis to describe the dynamic two-phase gas desorption process and leading to large errors in adsorbed free gas differentiation.

In contrast, the in situ sealed staged desorption method tests intact coal on site, completely retaining coal’s original pore-fracture structure and subsurface geological conditions. It quantitatively separates adsorbed and free gas by tracking the inflection point of desorption rate variation and accurately reflects underground real gas occurrence, making it better suited for quantifying in situ two-phase gas distribution in coal seams. For this reason, this paper obtains total gas content via field desorption tests and calculates adsorbed and free gas quantities using a two-phase evaluation model optimized for staged desorption data. Desorption Gas Volume in Three Stages

Statistical analysis of coal samples with distinct adsorbed and free gas storage characteristics reveals that free gas content gradually increases with increasing burial depth (Figure ). The adsorbed gas volume exhibits a significant positive correlation with micropore specific surface area and micropore abundance (Figure a,b). This correlation demonstrates that expanded micropore specific surface areas can substantially enrich effective adsorption sites and enlarge storage space for methane molecules, thereby enhancing the gas adsorption capacity of coal matrices. As the predominant contributor to coal pore volume, micropore development directly governs the methane adsorption potential of deep coal reservoirs. This fundamental mechanism explains why the present study prioritizes micropore pore modeling to investigate gas isotope fractionation and gas production behaviors. In addition, clay mineral components exert a non-negligible regulatory effect on methane adsorption and occurrence (Figure c). The studied coal samples contain abundant clay minerals, primarily consisting of illite–montmorillonite mixed layers and kaolinite. Benefiting from their lamellar crystal morphology and unique structural characteristics, these clay minerals provide extensive internal and external specific surface areas, which further supplement effective adsorption sites for methane molecules.

Micropore characteristics of coal samples, the clay minerals, and the adsorption gas capacity relationship.

Different from the regulation of micropores and clay minerals on adsorbed gas, macropores and fracture systems are the core channels and reservoir spaces for CBM migration and enrichment, and their development characteristics directly affect the occurrence efficiency and content of free gas. When the formation pressure rises to the critical threshold, the promotion effect of pressure on adsorption will be weakened by temperature-dominated desorption, which will promote the adsorption gas to reach saturation and transform to the free state, and the methane adsorbed in the pores of clay minerals will also gradually release with this process. The size expansion and connectivity improvement of macropores and fissures in deep coal reservoirs can significantly improve the capacity of free gas storage. Collectively, the micropore–clay mineral dominated adsorption system and the macropore–fracture controlled free gas system jointly determine the comprehensive reservoir physical properties and gas-bearing performance of deep coal seams.

The maturity, exchange rate of methane and carbon dioxide, water dissolution rate, and desorption-diffusion rate significantly affect the methane isotopes of coalbed methane. , The coal samples were obtained from the formation in situ and immediately sealed and analyzed. Thus, they were only affected by the formation pressure, temperature, and desorption-diffusion process. The gas composition analysis showed that methane was the primary component. Accordingly, this study focuses on clarifying the differential adsorption and diffusion behaviors of methane molecules during multistage desorption.

The carbon isotope value of the coal samples in Y2085 well was -32.09–-22.10‰, the average value was -27.29‰, and the fractionation difference was 9.99‰ (Figure a,b). At the later stage of the normal temperature stage, the carbon isotope value becomes heavier, and the rapid desorption of 12CH4 leads to a lower concentration, and the larger concentration difference makes the free 13CH4 begin to diffuse out. As the temperature rises, the kinetic energy of methane molecules increases, while the binding force and adsorption force of pores on molecules weaken. 13CH4 begins to desorb rapidly and shows a gradually heavier trend during the ground temperature stage. The Mechanism of Methane Occurrence and Migration at the Microscopic Scale

Macroscopic experiments often have difficulty in describing the microscopic interaction mechanism between pore diameters and methane molecules. In contrast, molecular simulation techniques provide an effective microscopic approach and novel mechanistic insight to elucidate CBM occurrence behaviors, enabling intuitive observation of coal–methane interfacial interactions within pores of different diameters. , Pore structure characterization confirms that the tested coal samples (Y2085–S2–8, Y2085–B1–5, Y1711–S2–2, and Y1711–B1–2) develop a full-scale pore system consisting of micropores, mesopores, and macropores. Quantitative pore proportion statistics show that micropores dominate the total pore volume, accounting for 87.61%–93.5%, while mesopores and macropores occupy only 0.23%–1.06% and 6.23%–11.99%, respectively.

Based on molecular simulation algorithms, multicondition adsorption–desorption simulations with variable temperatures, pressures, and pore sizes were performed on the four coal specimens collected from two typical CBM wells. Combined with the full-scale pore characterization of coal, the gas-bearing structure was depicted. The simulation procedures and force field parameter settings adopted in this study are consistent with the methodological descriptions in the preceding section. Near the pore surface, the adsorption potential energy of 13CH4 is slightly lower than that of 12CH4. Due to the large physical mass, 13CH4 can occupy more adsorption sites on the pore surface and preferentially adsorb on the pore surface, which leads to the desorption of 13CH4 molecules from the pore surface requiring higher energy contribution. The molecular weight of 13CH4 is 1.0833 times that of 12CH4, and the acceleration of 12CH4 is greater when subjected to similar forces. The intricate nanoscale pore network imposes significant blocking effects on methane fluid migration. The molecular interaction between methane molecules is also an important factor affecting molecular transport. Together, these lead to different adsorption behaviors and dynamic migration processes of 12CH4 and 13CH4 molecules on the pore wall. The existence states of molecules are classified based on their positions and diffusion distances, and they are divided into adsorbed state and free state. In the constructed composite pore model, molecules migrating freely through pore throats and channels were defined as free gas, whereas molecules stably attached to pore walls or trapped within fracture voids were classified as adsorbed gas. This study adopts molecular dynamics (MD) simulations to systematically investigate the desorption and diffusion behaviors of deep coalbed methane under variable temperatures, pressures, pore sizes, and coal molecular structural conditions. From a microscopic perspective, it clarifies the mechanism of the methane carbon isotope fractionation. The mean square displacement (MSD) serves as a critical quantitative indicator for evaluating the random motion range and diffusion capability of gas particles. By characterization of the temporal evolution of molecular displacement, MSD can effectively reflect the diffusion kinetics of methane atoms and molecules. Based on the difference of the movement of methane molecules in the migration process of different temperatures, pressures, and pore conditions, this paper explains the measured isotope fractionation effect from a microscopic point of view and provides a new paradigm for its mechanism research.

Figure shows the mean-square displacements of four coal samples at normal temperature, ground temperature, and high temperature. During the CBM desorption process, both 12CH4 and 13CH4 exhibit universal two-stage kinetic behavior consisting of rapid desorption and slow desorption. The time nodes of the two desorption stages are basically the same, but the corresponding diffusion coefficients (calculated based on MSD) are significantly different. Such kinetic discrepancies are predominantly governed by the intrinsic pore structure, formation temperature, and reservoir pressure of the deep coal reservoirs. The adsorption of methane in the micropores of the coal matrix is dominated by intermolecular van der Waals force. Because of the larger molecular weight of 13CH4, the lower zero-point vibration energy, and the stronger adsorption between 13CH4 and the coal matrix, the adsorption stability of 13CH4 is higher, and it is more sensitive to the changes of external conditions such as temperature and pressure. Thus, it is more affected by temperature and other factors. The 12CH4 and 13CH4 concentrations increased rapidly and fluctuated, but their diffusion intensities differed significantly. The mean-square displacement of 12CH4 in the 1.5 nm pores did not change considerably as the temperature increased. The mean-square displacement increased slightly with the temperature, which may be due to an increase in molecular kinetic energy (Figure a). 12CH4 has a higher diffusion capacity because of its lower molecular weight. As the pore size increased to 3 nm, the mean-square displacement of 12CH4 exhibited stratification. As shown in Figure c, the mean-square displacement is the smallest in the normal temperature stage. It increases with the temperature, indicating that the pore structure is the main factor affecting the diffusion of 12CH4. The 13CH4 molecules in the 1.5 and 3 nm pores showed a zigzag trend. Since the methane molecule (13CH4) has a higher adsorption energy, it is more affected by the adsorption–desorption effect. However, the temperature and pore structure are not the only control factors (Figure ,d). Together, multiple coupled geological conditions coregulate the diffusion capacity of 13CH4, and such differential responses become increasingly prominent with prolonged desorption time.

Diffusion Coefficients of Coal Core

Controlled by the coupled effects of the coal macromolecular structure, temperature, pressure, and pore geometric confinement, methane diffusion behaviors exhibit significant discrepancies among the four tested coal samples (Figure ). Given that two methane molecular isotopologues (12CH4 and 13CH4) were adopted in this study, their diffusion coefficients were characterized independently to reveal isotopic fractionation during diffusion. For the Y2085–S2–2 sample, the diffusion coefficient of 12CH4 was higher than that of 13CH4 at the initial stage under both normal temperature and ground temperature conditions; yet, with the extension of diffusion time, the 12CH4 diffusion coefficient declined and was eventually lower than that of 13CH4. In the high-temperature stage, the diffusion coefficient of 13CH4 remained consistently higher than that of 12CH4 for this sample (Figure a). As for the Y2085–B1–5 sample, the dominant molecular species in terms of diffusion coefficient alternated between 12CH4 and 13CH4 across the three temperature stages, while a notable phenomenon was that 12CH4 always exhibited a higher diffusion coefficient at the early stage of each temperature transition (Figure b).

Distribution maps of diffusion coefficients of coal samples.

For the Y1711–S2–2 sample, 13CH4 exhibits a higher diffusion rate under normal temperature conditions. In contrast, 12CH4 dominates the initial diffusion stage under ground temperature and high-temperature conditions, followed by accelerated 13CH4 diffusion in the late desorption stage (Figure c). The Y1711–B1–2 sample displayed a consistent variation trend in the diffusion coefficients of 12CH4 and 13CH4 throughout all the three temperature stages (Figure d). By comparing the diffusion characteristics of molecular simulation with the measured carbon isotope fractionation patterns, it was found that the changing trends of the two were highly consistent. This consistency was primarily embodied in the direct correspondence between the temporal variation of δ13CH4 values and the relative changes in the diffusion coefficients of 12CH4 and 13CH4: the δ13CH4 value became heavier when 13CH4 dominated the diffusion process with a faster rate, whereas it became lighter when 12CH4 was the dominant diffusing species with a higher diffusion coefficient.

Isotopic fractionation intensity can be quantitatively characterized by the diffusion coefficient ratio of 13CH4 to 12CH4, defined as the fractionation coefficient α. In this definition, α > 1 represents heavy isotope enrichment (V13C > V12C), while α < 1 indicates light isotope enrichment (V13C < V12C). The coal fractionation coefficient is 0.09–16.33, with an average of 3.05 (Table ).

During the desorption process of coal No. 5 in the Y2085 well, the 12CH4 first underwent rapid desorption. Subsequently, the diffusion rate of 13CH4 was faster than that of 12CH4 in the later stage of normal temperature. This phenomenon is attributed to the progressive release of residual adsorbed gas dominated by 13CH4, driven by continuous pore-pressure depletion in the late desorption period. Because of the increase in molecular kinetic energy, the diffusion rate of 12CH4 is higher than that of 13CH4. As the desorption stage progresses, 13CH4 gradually detaches from the surface of the pores and takes a dominant position in the desorption process. The high-temperature stage showed a similar trend as the normal-temperature stage. No. 8 coal samples in the Y2085 well exhibited consistency during the desorption processes at three different temperatures. 12CH4 was desorbed first, followed by 13CH4, which began to desorb rapidly.

By comparison, the two coal samples from the Y1711 well display alternating fractionation behaviors under formation temperature and high-temperature conditions, with sequential stages of V13C < V12C and V13C > V12C. This alteration is closely associated with the preferential release of free-phase 12CH4 from cleats and fractures in the early desorption stage. The adsorbed 13CH4 was dissolved as the free 12CH4 concentration decreased. At the normal temperature stage, there is only a tendency for 13CH4 to diffuse relatively quickly.

The trend of the fractionation coefficient was consistent with that of the field-measured isotope values, i.e., an enrichment in heavy isotopes. α decreased during the conversion at different stages (Figure ). This study has shown that the fractionation coefficient reflects the diffusion process, desorption rate, and phase transition of methane molecules, making it indispensable for investigating gas-bearing structures.

Gas-Bearing Capacity of Deep Coal

Gas in the coal occurs as free gas in the cracks and matrix pores, as adsorbed gas on the surface of the matrix pores, and as dissolved gas in kerogen pores. − The methane carbon isotope fractionation process showed a ‘down-up’ phenomenon. Combined with the desorption process of this study, the gas occurrence state was determined and the influence of pore conditions was evaluated. It is clear that deep coalbed methane has the characteristics of giving priority to the production of free gas and supplying adsorbed gas for a long time.

The carbon isotope fractionation mechanism and general model of deep coal gas production process clock are as follows: In the early stage of desorption, the value of δ13CH4 is basically stable and the fluctuation is small. The difference in migration speed caused by the mass of 12CH4 and 13CH4 can be ignored. The two migrate synchronously under high pressure difference, mainly in cleats and fractures. Methane migration within the coal matrix follows Darcy seepage behavior, and the seepage efficiency is jointly controlled by molecular diffusion kinetics and fracture development degree. At this stage, adsorbed gas and free gas in the matrix and pore systems maintain their original in situ proportional distribution.

The δ13CH4 value decreases, and the gas in the fracture and matrix pores is produced together. The matrix gas is mainly free gas, which diffuses from the matrix to the fracture. Due to the different diffusion, adsorption and desorption rates of isotopes in different media, their distribution, and output are also different. Due to the weak polarity of 12CH4 gas, it is preferentially dissolved. At the same time, as the matrix gas is mixed, the adsorption capacity of 12CH4 is weakened. During the precipitation process, 12CH4 is relatively enriched, resulting in a smaller isotope value.

The value of δ13CH4 increases, the produced gas is mainly the adsorbed gas, and a small amount of free gas in the matrix pores. Based on the early rapid desorption, the concentration of 12CH4 in coal decreases and the desorption rate slows down. The concentration gradient caused by leads to the desorption of methane molecules in mesopores and micropores into free gas and migration into cracks and pores, resulting in the enrichment of 13CH4 and the increase of δ13CH4. Overall, deep CBM production is dominated by free gas yield in the early exploitation stage. As desorption persists, adsorbed gas gradually becomes the dominant gas source via continuous desorption and phase conversion, sustaining long-term gas production (Figure ).
