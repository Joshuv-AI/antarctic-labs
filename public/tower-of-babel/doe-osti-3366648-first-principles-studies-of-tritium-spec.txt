 1 INTODUCTION
1.1 Background

 Tritium (³H) production and retention in a nuclear reactor depends on several factors. Two of the key

factors are the physical and chemical properties of materials under radiation. Tritium production and

retention requires optimal performance of materials integrated into tritium-producing burnable absorber

rods (TPBARs).1 These materials usually have exceptional physical and chemical stability against

damaging radiation and oxidation at high temperature in a reactor.2 Zirconium (Zr) and its alloys are used

in nuclear reactors as tritium (³H) capturing getter materials.3 These Zr based materials have low thermal

neutron absorption cross-section and excellent mechanical and thermal properties. In Watts Bar Nuclear

reactors, lithium aluminate pellets produce tritium during irradiation. The tritium diffuses from the pellets

to the surface of the getter, through a thin nickel (Ni) layer to the getter substrate, where it reacts with Zr

to form zirconium hydride (ZrHx).4, 5 The precipitation of these hydrides causes volumetric expansion and

embrittlement, which could negatively affect the performance and lifespan of Zr-based getter materials.

Computational modeling using realistic atomic-scale interfaces can provide valuable insight into tritium

diffusion and hydride formation mechanisms across the complex alloy interfaces, ultimately leading to a

better understanding of getter performance and lifetime.5

 In the past, we studied γ-LiAlO₂ and Li₂ZrO₃ pellet materials’ chemical properties and diffusivity and

solubility behavior for ³H.6 These studies also focused on surface characteristics, diffusion of ³H and O³H

species on defective (100) and (101) surfaces, and the formation, adsorption, and desorption of ³H₂O on

γ-LiAlO₂ and LiAl₅O₈ surfaces in the presence of impurities such as Helium (He) and Carbon (C).7 We

found that ³H release strongly depends on the number of lithium vacancies. Elemental tritium (3H2) was

found to be the dominant species desorbed from the pellets at low vacancy concentrations, while ³H₂O

desorption was more prevalent as the vacancy concentration increases. Thus, both 3H2 and 3H2O can be

 6

expected to diffuse to the Ni surface of the getter. Because the diffusion barrier for O is higher than ³H,

the Ni coating effectively allows selective ³H diffusion. In FY23 and FY24, we performed intensive

investigations of ³H₂ and ³H₂O binding and dissociation on the Ni(111) surface and interface.⁸ We

modelled the Ni/Zr interface by optimizing the individual Ni and Zr slab geometry. While we explored

tritium diffusion in various zirconium hydride phases,¹˒⁶˒⁷ several questions remain unanswered including

the mechanisms of ³H diffusion across the Ni/Zircaloy-4 interface, and how do impurities such as Sn and

O influence ³H diffusion through complex interfaces with varying Ni and Zr concentrations. In the getter

substrate, 3H is absorbed by Zr, forming Zr hydrides. Tin (Sn) impurities were found to have a strong

repulsive influence on the tritium diffusion. ¹˒⁷ In FY25, we first constructed complex Ni/NiZr and NiZr/Zr

interfaces by optimizing Ni, NiZr and Zr slabs separately. Second, we calculated the solubility and

diffusion barriers for ³H across the optimized interfaces. Third, we introduced impurities such as Sn in the

interface and calculated the impact of impurity on the diffusion barriers.

1.2 Project Technical Scope
 In FY25, based on the ideal interface model of Ni/Zircaloy-4 generated in FY24, we explored the

diffusion pathways of 3H across Ni-NiZr and NiZr/Zr alloys interfaces under different conditions,

including with oxide/hydroxide clusters on the Ni layer, and impurities (e.g., Sn, O and defects) located

in Ni/NiZr and NiZr/Zr interfaces. Due to the size limitation under DFT simulation, we separated the 3-

layer system into 3 sub-systems: Ni/NiZr interface, NiZr alloy layer, NiZr-alloy/Zircaloy-4 interface. We

then introduced O and OH impurities in Ni, and other impurities (Sn, C, defects) in NiZr alloy and

Zircaloy-4 layers. We conducted diffusion pathway calculations for 3H from Ni to the NiZr alloy layer,

into the NiZr layer, and across the NiZr-alloy/Zr interface to get into the Zircaloy-4 getter. The study used

NETL supercomputer resources and did not require the use of classified data or hardware. To achieve our

objectives, we conducted the investigation by using spin-polarized density functional theory (DFT)

 7

methods to capture the magnetic features of Ni and its impact on 3H diffusion. The project was divided

into four subtasks:

• Task 1: Build NiZr-alloy layer model. Based on the literature data, we built a NiZr alloy with fixed

 Ni concentration. This model was used for the following tasks.

• Task 2: Exploring 3H diffusion across Ni- NiZr-alloy interface. Based on our FY24 Ni-Zr interface

 model, we built an interface model of Ni-NiZr-alloy, then performed 3H diffusion pathway with and

 without O/OH impurity on the Ni-side and Sn or defect impurity on the NiZr alloy side.

• Task 3: Calculating the 3H diffusion pathways in NiZr alloy. Based on the NiZr -alloy model from

 Task 1, we calculated the 3H diffusion pathways and barriers within the NiZr alloy with and without

 Sn or defect impurity.

• Task 4: Exploring the 3H diffusion pathways across NiZr-alloy – Zr interface with and without

 impurity present on both sides. By building an interface of NiZr-alloy and Zircaloy-4, we performed

 similar calculations to obtain the 3H diffusion pathways. Combining results from tasks 2-4, a complete
 3
 H diffusion mechanism from the Ni-coating into the Zircalory-4 getter was revealed.

 2 THEORITICAL AND COMPUTATIONAL METHOD

2.1 Background
 In the TPBAR, Ni is a coating on the α-Zr getter alloy (Zircaloy-4) and the coating helps to prevent

an oxide tritium barrier from forming on the surface of the Zircaloy-4 due to the presence of 3H2O vapor

in the gas phase of the TPBAR. The diffusion barrier for O₂ in Ni is several times higher than that for

³H.8, 9 This makes oxygen species significantly less likely to diffuse through the Ni layer to the Zircaloy-

4 getter.10 The ³H absorbed by the getter into the Ni coating diffuses through the complex NiZr-Ni and

NiZr-Zr interfaces. Modeling these interfaces is particularly challenging due to low-symmetric atomic

sites, atomic rearrangements, and intermixing of Ni and Zr atoms at the interfacial region. Nevertheless,

simplified slab models can provide valuable insights on the interface geometry, as well as tritium solubility

and diffusivity across the interface. Computationally, this task becomes even more complex at the density

functional theory (DFT) level of calculation because of the presence of low-symmetry impurity and defect

sites, and the size of the interface to perform diffusion calculation.

 10

 In this chapter, we will provide our computational and theoretical details about the approach for the

study of interface models, solubility, and diffusivity for 3H with and without vacancy defects, Sn and O

impurities at the interface region.

2.2 Computational Method

 The calculations were carried out using density functional theory (DFT) with a plane-wave basis set,

as implemented in the Vienna ab initio simulation package (VASP).11 The electron–ion interactions were

treated using the projector augmented wave (PAW) method. The potential for the tritium isotope (³H) was

generated by modifying the atomic mass in the VASP reported standard hydrogen (¹H) potential.

Exchange–correlation interactions were described within the generalized gradient approximation (GGA)

using the spin-polarized Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) functional. A plane-wave cutoff energy of 400

eV was used to ensure energy convergence. The self-consistent calculations were considered converged

when the total energy change was below 10⁻⁵ eV and the residual atomic forces were less than 0.01 eV/Å.

We modelled the NiZr/Ni interface by combining the Ni (111) and Zr (0001) surfaces. To simplify the

model, we took the NiZr (0001) surface and it was sandwiched with Ni (111) and Zr (0001) surfaces to

create NiZr/Ni and NiZr/Zr interfaces. The Ni and Zr surfaces were modeled as a slab containing 100

atoms arranged in four layers, each with 25 atoms.¹⁴ A vacuum spacing of 12 Å was applied along the z-

axis to prevent interactions between periodic images. Ni systems of varying slab sizes and layer numbers

were generated from fully relaxed bulk Ni structures. For the Ni (111) surface, a three-layer 5 × 5 supercell

slab was used with a 12 Å vacuum along the z-axis. The Brillouin zone was sampled using a 3 × 3 × 1

Monkhorst–Pack k-point grid.

 The interface structures of NiZr/Ni and NiZr/Zr were optimized by taking individually optimized Ni,

Zr and NiZr surfaces. A ³H atom was introduced at the interface region of these interfaces, and ³H

 11

formation energy was calculated. The diffusion barriers and pathways for ³H were evaluated in the pristine

interface and in systems containing a Zr vacancy and Sn impurities. The climbing-image nudged elastic

band (c-NEB) method was employed to determine the diffusion pathways using intermediate images for

each trajectory to accurately map the potential energy barriers.

 3 RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
3.1 Introduction

 To model the NiZr/Ni and NiZr/Zr interfaces, the individual Ni and Zr surfaces were first optimized

separately and then combined to form the NiZr structure. Then the NiZr interface was sandwiched with

Ni and Zr surfaces. The resulting interfaces were further relaxed to obtain the most stable configuration,

which was subsequently used to evaluate tritium (³H) solubility and diffusivity. We calculated the relative

stability of Ni5Zr, Ni7Zr2, Ni10Zr7, NiZr, and NiZr2 solid compounds. We modelled the NiZr surface with

high relative stability and created an interface with Ni (111) and Zr (0001) surfaces. We optimized the

complex interfaces in order to achieve stable structures.12 The formation energies of ³H on these surfaces

were calculated, along with the surface diffusion barriers. Several possible surface-to-bulk diffusion

pathways were identified. Our results reproduced both the dissociation process of ³H₂ on Zr surfaces and

the energy profiles for ³H transfer within bulk Zr.

 We found that the Ni1Zr3 I4-mcm and Ni1Zr2 I4-mcm were two relatively stable alloy systems.

Around 500 K, the Ni/Zr ratio was reported to be ¼. We proceeded with Ni1Zr3 I4-mcm to calculate the

diffusivity and solubility of ³H in order to model the system around TPBAR operating temperatures. The

two layers of Ni1Zr3 alloy and three layers of Zr crystal were interfaced and optimized. The bond length

between NiZr and Zr in the interface was calculated to be 2.58 Å and the bond-length between Ni1Zr3 and

Ni in the interface was 2.19 Å. Diffusion from a pristine surface into a Sn-doped subsurface exhibited
 12

high energy barriers for both forward and reverse processes, indicating that Sn impurities in the subsurface

region hinder tritium desorption. In this chapter, we provide detail about the Ni/NiZr and NiZr/Zr interface

constructions starting from the NiZr alloy systems and provide diffusion mechanisms for ³H in pure and

Sn-doped interfaces.

3.2.1 NiZr Alloys Optimization
 Experimental observation shows that the interface of Ni and Zr is highly complex, consisting of

several intermetallic layers formed during heat treatment after depositing the Ni coating. Microscopy

reveals that there are intermetallic compounds, such as Ni5Zr, Ni7Zr2, Ni10Zr7, NiZr, and NiZr2 in the

interdiffusion zone between the Ni coating and the Zircaloy-4 substrate.7, 14 Ordered Ni0.5Zr0.5 and

Ni0.33Zr0.67 intermetallic compounds indicated that amorphization may be initiated at high temperature

when the long-range order is weakened by antisite defects.15, 16 The accumulation of antisite defects where

atoms occupy incorrect lattice sites can cause sufficient chemical disorder, large atomic displacement, and

drop in shear modulus, triggering initialization of a solid-to-amorphous transition at elevated temperature.

Here, we modelled Ni and Zr intermetallic compounds with varying concentrations of each element and

optimized them to create an individual interface.

 14

 We conducted extensive literature reviews to get inferences on the possible chemical alloy systems

for Ni and Zr. In our relative stability calculations, we studied the following compounds: Ni1Zr1, Ni1Zr2

I4-mcm, Ni1Zr1 I4-mmm, Ni1Zr3, Ni2Zr1 and Ni3Zr1.17 The crystallization temperature, heat of

crystallization and activation energy of NiZr were experimentally measured using calibrated differential

scanning calorimetry (DSC) for varying concentrations of Ni and Zr.16 It was found that as the NiZr alloy

is cooled down after heating above 800K, the Zr concentration gradually increases and around 550 K,

Ni/Zr ratio is found to be ~ ¼.

4 SUMMARY OF REPORT AND FURTHER WORK

 The zircaloy is an excellent getter material that captures 3H in the TPBAR. It is coated with a thin

layer of Ni, where Ni hinders oxide formation and allows only 3H to diffuse freely to the getter substrate.

Modelling the NiZr alloy interface is crucial to understand 3H trapping, diffusion and retention. Previous

experiments demonstrated the complex nature of the Ni-Zr interdiffusion zone with several intermetallic

layers with varying Ni and Zr concentration. Our FY24 study on the Ni/Zr interface revealed that there

are trapping sites in the interface regions that hinder diffusion dynamics. The presence of impurities such

as O and Sn increased diffusion barriers. Based on those results, in FY25 we explored the diffusion

pathways of 3H across Ni/NiZr-alloy layer/Zircaloy-4 interfaces under different conditions. We included

impurities such as Sn and defects such as Zr vacancy in the Zircaloy-4 substrate. We separated the

interdiffusion zone into three sub-systems: Ni/NiZr interface, NiZr alloy layer, NiZr-alloy/Zr interface

and optimized them individually. Basically, (a) we built Ni1Zr3/Zr and Ni/Ni1Zr3 interfaces by taking Ni

(111), Ni1Zr3 (0001) and Zr (0001), (b) we explored 3H diffusion across both the interface layer Ni1Zr3/Zr

and Ni/Ni1Zr3 in pure interface model from (a), and (c) we introduced Sn impurity and Zr vacancy defects

at the zircaloy layer and calculated diffusion barriers in order to analyze the impact due to the presence of

the impurity and vacancy.

 We thoroughly reviewed the literature on the Ni and Zr alloy phases and modelled the relative

stability of five different intermetallic compounds. These were Ni1Zr1, Ni1Zr2 I4-mcm, Ni1Zr1 I4-mmm,

Ni1Zr3, Ni2Zr1 and Ni3Zr1. We found that Ni1Zr3 is relatively more stable than other alloys examined in

our study. The shortest bond length between Ni1Zr3 (0001) and Zr (0001) surfaces was found to be 2.58

Å. The energy barrier in a pure system was found to be 0.37 eV and it also had negative diffusion barriers.

We concluded that the pathways with these negative energy barriers have trapping sites. With a Zr

 25

vacancy, we found the energy barrier to be 0.4 eV. The highest energy barrier was found with the Sn

impurity, which was 0.6 eV.

 We also optimized the Ni/Ni1Zr3 interface, and we found the shortest bond length between Ni

(111) and Ni1Zr3 (0001) surfaces was 2.19 Å. By taking a total of six layers (three from each surface type)

in the optimized interface, we investigated the diffusion pathways for 3H from Ni to Ni1Zr3. While 3H

diffusion from the Ni layer showed no barrier, we found a small barrier of 0.2 eV in Ni1Zr3. Overall, we

found no significant barrier for 3H diffusion in the Ni/Ni1Zr3 interface.

 Introduction of different impurities such as C, CO, CO2 and NiOx and Ni(OH)x clusters in the

interface models, and their impacts on the diffusion barriers would provide further insight on the tritium

retention. Going forward, we will introduce this impurity in the Ni region and Sn and Zr vacancy in NiZr

alloy region. This will provide understanding of the change in the diffusion barriers due to the presence

of carbon related impurities at the interface region.
