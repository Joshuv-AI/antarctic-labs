INTRODUCTION

The Memorandum of Agreement (MOA), by which the efforts of the Wind Turbine Radar Interference
Mitigation Working Group (WTRIM WG) are organized, requires the Executive Steering Group (ESG) to
provide a yearly progress report at the end of each calendar year. This document includes a brief
description of the major projects undertaken by the WTRIM WG for fiscal year 2025 categorized under
the three strategic themes as detailed in the updated Federal Interagency WTRIM Strategy. These themes
are:
 1. Improving the capacity of government and industry to evaluate the impacts of wind energy
 installations on radar systems
 2. Developing the hardware and software mitigation measures to increase the resilience of radar
 systems to wind turbines
 3. Encouraging the development of next-generation radar systems that are resistant to wind turbine
 interference.

2025 was a year of uncertainty for the wind energy market. In the first half of the year, wind energy
installations surged, more than doubling compared to the same period in 2024. However, this momentum
was tempered in the second half, as wind turbine orders fell to their lowest levels since 2020, as reported
by the American Clean Power Association (ACP)1. Additionally, an Executive Order temporarily withdrew
all areas on the outer continental shelf from offshore wind leasing.

Amid these challenges, the Wind Turbine Radar Interference Mitigation Working Group remained
steadfast in its mission to evaluate and mitigate the impacts of wind turbines on federally owned radar
systems. For example, the U.S. Wind Turbine Database (USWTDB) played a pivotal role by providing
essential data on wind turbine locations and specifications. This information is critical for ongoing
cumulative impact analyses and wind impact assessments, particularly in understanding the spatial
overlap between wind energy potential and radar line-of-sight. A notable initiative in 2025 is the
performance study of the Aircraft Detection Lighting System (ADLS) to investigate the suitability of ADLS
for military aviation given that these systems were originally designed for civilian aircraft. The study
addresses concerns regarding the performance of ADLS radars for modern low-observable (stealthy)
military aircraft, examining the required warning distances for safe turbine avoidance.

A significant achievement in 2025 was the amendment of the Memorandum of Agreement to include
the Department of Homeland Security. This addition, along with several interagency agreements among
WTRIM members to pursue further mitigation techniques for long-range radar systems and weather
radar systems, underscores the Working Group’s commitment to solving these challenges. These
collaborative efforts are crucial for ensuring the coexistence of wind energy development and radar
functionality.

WTRIM Memorandum of Agreement Amendment
 Funding organization: NA
 Background: The purpose of the amendment is to add the Department of Homeland Security as
 a member to the Wind Turbine Radar Interference Mitigation (WTRIM) Interagency

 WTRIM Working Group was signed and executed by all WTRIM members as of April 15, 2025.

U.S. Wind Turbine Database
 Funding organization: Department of Energy (DOE), U.S. Geological Survey (USGS), and American
 Clean Power Association (ACP)
 Background: The U.S. Wind Turbine Database (USWTDB; https://eerscmap.usgs.gov/uswtdb)
 provides the locations of land-based and offshore wind turbines in the United States,
 corresponding wind project information, and turbine technical specifications. It is a continuously
 updated geo-rectified set of coordinates and characteristics of the wind turbine fleet of installed
 onshore and offshore wind turbines. Further, the USWTDB team prepares custom datasets
 exclusively for WTRIM members of turbines under construction or in advanced development for
 both onshore and offshore locations. These sets of data are used to determine likely future
 cumulative impacts of turbines on radar systems in certain geographies.
 Status: The USWTDB viewer surpassed 22 million views in 2025 with a steady rate over time of
 roughly 300,000 views per month. 8% of the 2023–2024 views are from .gov including the
 National Oceanic & Atmospheric Administration (NOAA), DOE, U.S. Department of Agriculture
 (USDA), FAA, and national labs. Roughly 0.5% are from .mil origins including the US Armed Forces
 and the Secretary of War. Other large users include: .edu users including major colleges and
 universities and K–12 schools across the US; .st users including state governments; and, finally,
 developers, equipment manufacturers, utilities, and members of the public. The USWTDB
 interface allows online users to perform data summary calculations, screening and year-by-year
 changes of key turbine and project features, which are viewed in graphical format based on
 whichever turbines are showing in the viewer. In 2025, the team released a dataset of
 decommissioned turbines to USWTDB users and will continuously update this going forward.

Geographic Areas of Concern Update
 Funding organization: Department of War (DOW)
 Background: The Institute for Defense Analyses (IDA) was tasked to develop definitions for
 geographic areas of concern (GACs) that could have an adverse impact on military operations and
 readiness from potential wind turbine projects. Additionally, IDA was tasked to develop draft GAC
 maps for three military installations as a pilot program. The installations chosen were Sheppard
 AFB, Fort Indiantown Gap and Naval Weapons Systems Training Facility Boardman.

 5

 Status: IDA developed definitions for the GACs in three tiers. These definitions were vetted
 through the Clearinghouse and the Services. The definitions were then used to develop GACs for
 the three pilot sites. The definitions are:
 • Area of Concern (AoC) 1: AoC1 is a geographic area that will result in an adverse impact*
 on military operations or readiness, and there are no feasible mitigation options.
 • Area of Concern (AoC) 2: AoC2 is a geographic area that will result in an adverse impact*
 on military operations or readiness where Mitigation Resolution Team (MRT) will be
 required to determine if there is a feasible mitigation option. Mitigation will depend upon
 the impact of each proposed project on current and anticipated DOW activities.
 • Area of Concern (AoC) 3: AoC3 is a geographic area of low to medium risk to military
 operations and readiness. DOW will monitor all new energy project proposals to
 determine risk to DOW activities.
 * As defined by Section 312 of Title 10, United States Code, (10 U.S. § 183a (h)(1)) “Adverse impact on military operations and readiness
 means any adverse impact upon military operations and readiness, including flight operations, research, development, testing, and
 evaluation, and training, that is demonstratable and is likely to impair or degrade the ability of the armed forces to perform their warfighting
 missions.”

 The GACs delineate areas of concern where physical obstruction and/or electromagnetic
 interference may occur and ultimately affect military operations and readiness.

NORAD Areas-of-Concern List Update
 Funding organization: North American Aerospace Defense Command (NORAD)
 Background: NORAD maintains a Radar Area-of-Concern list that tracks the impact of existing and
 proposed wind turbines on individual radars. This effort is high-level analysis to identify and
 quantify the impact of existing and proposed wind energy projects on the National Airspace
 System (NAS) and the NORAD and USNORTHCOM missions. This product ranks areas of concern
 providing data for plans and operations. Additionally, it informs options for radar interference
 mitigation and prioritization of radar platforms and locations.
 Status: NORAD works closely with DOW entities and the U.S. Wind Turbine Database partners to
 integrate both onshore and offshore existing and proposed wind turbine data into NORAD and
 USNORTHCOM analysis. Additionally, NORAD works closely with MIT Lincoln Laboratory to
 compare analysis of wind energy projects.
 NORAD completed an update to the Areas-of-Concern list that has been expanded to 47 radars
 and reordered to reflect recent wind energy growth trends.

Wind Impact Assessment to Radar

 Background: Emerging offshore wind development in coastal United States waters will impact
 DOW and the U.S. Coast Guard (USCG) operating areas in various ways. The Wind Impact
 Assessment to Radar (WIAR) is focused specifically on understanding and mitigating potential
 offshore wind interference effects to U.S. Navy and USCG radar systems. The objective of the
 WIAR project is to conduct a baseline assessment of impacts to currently fielded airborne and
 shipboard systems from offshore wind farms on a scale that currently exists only in other parts of
 the world but is anticipated to be in place in the next few years along the East Coast of the United
 States.

 Status: The study team is analyzing data collected by a U.S. Navy Destroyer operating in the North
 Sea and is working to coordinate future collection events in the vicinity of the Coastal Virginia
 Offshore Wind and other offshore energy projects.

Offshore Wind Impacts on Surveillance Radar

 Background: The Naval Air Warfare Center Aircraft Division (NAWCAD) received funding to
 evaluate the impact of wind farms on commercial airborne surveillance radars. Phase I validated
 and quantified the extent of clutter caused by wind turbines on radars currently used for range
 clearance operations. Phase II utilized trade studies and High-Performance Computing (HPC) to
 develop mitigation strategies aimed at addressing predicted radar performance limitations and
 improve surface vessel traffic awareness in wind farm-affected areas.
 Status: Phase III is underway, focusing on model verification and validation through a planned test
 event involving operational wind turbines in Coastal Virginia. The technical mitigations developed
 during Phase II, combined with the results of Phase III testing, will inform future safety risk
 assessments and guide decision-making for range clearance precautions and procedures at the
 Atlantic Test Range (with potential applicability at other locations that use similar surveillance
 radars).

Underwater Acoustic Impacts of Offshore Wind

 Background: Wind turbines create noise as they operate. Offshore wind turbines transfer
 mechanical noise into the water during construction, normal operations, and periodic
 maintenance. Literature suggests that offshore turbines generally produce noise in the low
 frequencies during operations, and such frequencies are of interest to naval operations.
 Additional effects could include less noise in the medium frequencies because the turbine slows
 down the wind. These changes in the ocean noise could affect Navy undersea training, testing,
 and operations when conducted in proximity to the turbine farms.
 Status: CNA completed an assessment of potential underwater acoustic impacts on undersea
 operations and training from offshore wind turbines, as requested by the DOW’s Military Aviation
 and Installation Assurance Siting Clearinghouse. CNA provided a government-only report to the
 DOW and Navy elements.

Offshore Wind Impacts Modeling via AI & Machine Learning

 Background: Georgia Tech Research Institute (GTRI) seeks to assess the impact of wind turbine
 installations on the Air Surveillance Radar (ASR)-11 and, subsequently, investigate approaches for
 mitigating any anticipated performance degradation. Initial efforts have focused on developing a
 database of ASR-11 data, both simulated and operational. These data sets will be used to support
 establishing baseline ASR-11 target detection performance (with and without wind turbines
 present) and for the development and assessment of any wind turbine mitigation algorithms.
 Status: GTRI has developed a software environment for simulating ASR-11 returns from ground
 clutter, moving targets, thermal noise, and wind turbines. A large simulation database has been
 generated to support performance assessments and algorithm development. Additionally, GTRI
 has obtained and processed FAA-provided ASR-11 radar data and is currently performing the task
 of labeling that data. Once this is completed, GTRI will being to test a set of selected potential
 approaches for mitigating the impact of wind turbines on the ASR-11.

MIT LL ASR/ARSR Multi-static Project

 Background: This project seeks to enhance the capabilities of existing air surveillance radar
 systems by deploying multiple receivers around regions of high-priority airspace to improve
 sensitivity for small targets, to increase resolution, and to reject ground clutter from wind turbines
 and other sources.
 Status: MIT Lincoln Laboratory (MIT LL) has completed Phase I and II of the project and is currently
 in Phase III, algorithm development and multi-sensor analysis. This includes RF equipment
 performance optimization and antenna analysis and redesign. MIT LL tested the system in
 Burlington, VT, showing promising results. Additional testing of the system is being planned.

Wind Turbine Interference Mitigation via Adaptive Beam Steering (“Sidekick”) – Phase II

 Background: The goal of this project is to develop and provide a technical mitigation solution
 using higher radar sample rates and multiple beam processing to minimize impacts on Airport
 Surveillance Radar (ASR) systems from wind farm installations.
 Status: Space Dynamic Laboratory (SDL) has developed a passive radar receiver that uses the
 primary DASR signal as the transmitter. The receiver is an 8-element antenna array that uses
 elevation to discriminate targets from ground as well as an adaptive beam forming process to
 suppress energy coming from the direction of the ground. SDL has collected data from Travis Air
 Force Base with an instrumented Cessna aircraft flying spirals over the wind resource area
 demonstrating promising results.

Machine Learning for Clutter Suppression and Characterization
 Funding organization: FAA
 Background: MIT Lincoln Laboratory has developed a real-time machine learning inference model
 that can significantly reduce clutter from wind turbines and other sources for radar systems.
 Status: In FY25 MIT LL continued to train the model based on nearly a year of data from multiple
 radar sites. In FY26 MIT LL will work to transfer the software source code and training process to
 agency stakeholders, as well as test and evaluate the model based on radar performance
 requirements. For more information, requests can be made to the Long Range Radar Joint

Long Range Radar Adaptive Nulling Feasibility Study

 Background: This project analyzed the potential and value of replacing Long Range Radar
 components and employing adaptive elevation nulling to detect targets above wind turbines.
 Status: The project is completed. The study leveraged data from the IFT&E program. Study noted
 that adaptive nulling would require changes to two hardware boards within the radar. For more
 information, request can be made to the Long Range Radar Joint Program Office.

FAA ARSR-4 Upgrades
 Funding organization: FAA, DOW, DOE
 Background: False targets increase near wind turbines that are located within the operating range
 of long-range radar systems. Increases in false targets decrease the radar’s probability of
 detection of true targets, thus reducing the fidelity of the search data provided to stakeholders.
 The AJW-142, part of the NAS Defense Programs (NDP), developed the Stationary Doppler Target
 Suppressor (SDTS) that successfully met 80% probability of detection with an acceptable false
 alarm rate. As part of deploying the SDTS and clutter map segregation, the ARSR-4’s Constant
 False Alarm Rate (CFAR) circuit board and Detection Maps circuit board will be modernized. The
 current wire-wrapped CFAR and Detection Maps boards in the ARSR-4 will be replaced with
 custom designed Printed Circuit Boards (PCBs) with Field Programmable Gate Arrays (FPGAs) and
 synthesized firmware that correlates to most of the Integrated Circuit (IC) logic currently on the
 wire-wrapped versions. This modernization will provide the necessary memory and processing
 capability to implement Clutter Map Segregation and SDTS.
 Status: Two Interagency Agreements have been put in place- one between the DOE and FAA, and
 one between the DOW and FAA- to fund upgrades and testing to the ARSR-4 radar at critical radar
 sites. The agreements will support hardware and software upgrades and performance assessment

 9

 for several ARSR-4s critically saturated by wind farms, as identified by NORAD and supported by
 an analysis completed by MIT-LL.

Passive Radar CRADA
 Funding organization: FAA CRADA
 Background: The FAA executed a CRADA (cooperative research and development agreement)
 with Hensoldt to explore the feasibility of a passive radar for air surveillance. This past summer
 Hensoldt collected data with their Twinvis passive radar for targets of opportunity over wind
 resource areas near Corpus Christi, Texas. Twinvis tracked various aircraft over the wind turbines,

 Status: Hensoldt and the FAA are completing an appendix to their main CRADA report to

Air Force Flight Standards Agency Infill Radar Program
 Funding organization: U.S. Air Force
 Background: The Air Force seeks to demonstrate an infill radar capable of non-cooperative2 target
 detection in and around wind resource areas. Recently, the Air Force experimented with a rotating
 antenna infill radar for this purpose and is now looking to a phased array antenna infill radar. The
 Air Force published a Request for Information (RFI) in January 2025 resulting in seven vendor
 responses. Subsequently, the Air Force published a Request for Proposals (RFP) in June 2025
 resulting in five responses for a phased array antenna infill radar. Expert evaluations of the
 proposals yielded two viable contenders for support and demonstration of wind turbine
 mitigation performance.
 Status: The two vendors are under contract to deploy their infill radar at Travis Air Force Base in
 2026. In addition to targets of opportunity, civil air patrol flights will provide targets with complex
 flight paths to stress the effectiveness of each radar over the wind resource area.

Wind Turbine Clutter Mitigation for the NEXRAD Network
 Funding organization: DOE
 Background: The proposed complete wind turbine clutter (WTC) mitigation solution includes
 signal-processing-based detection and filtering techniques. An effective WTC detection technique
 must be able to respond to dynamic changes in the atmosphere and new wind farm
 developments. Changes in spatial variations in temperature, pressure, and humidity cause radar
 beam propagation changes, referred to as anomalous propagation, that result in apparent
 changes in the radar line-of-sight. That is, the radar beam “bends” toward (away from) the ground
 resulting in wind turbines causing more (less) impact on radar performance. Also, storm dynamics
 near wind farms determine how much WTC impacts radar performance. In this project, scientists
 at the National Severe Storms Laboratory and the Cooperative Institute for Severe and High
 Impact Weather Research and Operations will continue the evaluation of the two WTC detection
 techniques and advance them.
 Status: A proof-of-concept WTC filtering technique was developed, and its performance was
 preliminarily demonstrated using one data set collected with a NEXRAD radar. An important

2 Non-cooperative refers to targets without the use of on-board avionics, using radio signal reflections to cooperate with the

radar.

 10

 finding from this initial demonstration is that the WTC filter must be applied only to locations that
 have dominant WTC contamination. When the filter is applied to locations with uncontaminated
 weather signals or weak WTC contamination, it may lead to significant weather signal losses that
 can negatively impact the performance of the radar. Additionally, because the WTC filter is
 computationally expensive, its application only on WTC-contaminated locations would help keep
 the overall computational complexity compatible with current NEXRAD signal-processing
 capabilities. The change is performance based on the content of the location which motivates the
 need for a WTC detection technique that can effectively control the application of the WTC filter,
 analogously to how the NEXRAD network uses a ground-clutter detection technique to limit the
 application of the ground-clutter filter to locations with ground-clutter contamination.
 An Interagency Agreement (IAA) was signed between the DOE and the National Oceanic and
 Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) to fund this effort.

NOAA IOOS – BOEM Memorandum of Understanding & Offshore Wind Farm
 Agreements
 Funding organization: NOAA Integrated Ocean Observing System (IOOS), Bureau of Ocean Energy
 Management (BOEM)
 Background: BOEM issues commercial wind energy leases that give lessee the exclusive right to
 submit a Site Assessment Plan and Construction and Operations Plan, and to conduct activities
 described in those plans as approved by BOEM. NOAA’s IOOS Office’s mission is to manage the
 Integrated Ocean Observing System to produce, integrate, and communicate high quality ocean,
 coastal, and Great Lakes information to meet the safety, economic, and stewardship needs of the
 Nation. Offshore wind turbines have the potential to interfere with the High-Frequency (HF) radar
 systems if appropriate mitigation measures are not in place.
 Status: The MOU Annex establishes a mechanism for NOAA and BOEM to ensure that offshore
 wind lessees are responsible for mitigation potential HF-radar interference pursuant to
 Construction and Operations Plan terms and conditions for any offshore wind project authorized
 by BOEM that may cause HF-radar interference.

OTT: Software Tools for the Mitigation of Wind Turbine Interference in the U.S. IOOS
 Network
 Funding organization: NOAA Integrated Ocean Observing System (IOOS)
 Background: Building on initial mitigation research efforts by CODAR Ocean Sensors, funded by
 the Bureau of Ocean Energy Management, this project will conduct the necessary system
 integration, testing, validation, and verification needed for operational capacity. Offshore wind
 turbines create interference with HF-radar measurements that can lead to blind spots in the
 national HF-radar network. As the implementation of offshore wind-based energy grows, the
 potential for interference with HF-radar operations will also grow, creating sizable gaps in high-
 quality data and coverage.
 Status: Recent research has been able to characterize this interference, creating the opportunity
 to implement software-based solutions that mitigate this interference and close those gaps. This
 project will: collect wind turbine interference data using a range of HF-radar systems; develop a
 focused dataset of simulated, hybrid, and observed wind turbine interference to test mitigation;
 document operational changes of the networked IOOS HF-radar systems that will increase the
 accuracy of mitigation methods; and conduct a full scale, in situ validation of mitigation methods
 at the first major U.S. offshore wind farm. HF-radar is a critical component of the IOOS mission,

 11

 capable of making both high spatial and temporal resolution observations of surface currents over
 the continental shelf. The plan outlined here, paired with the recommendations of an IOOS-tasked
 community working group, will result in validated software operating on HF-radar site computers,
 enabling radars to observe ocean currents in and around operating offshore wind farms.

RCS Characterization of Wind Turbine Utilizing Carbon Fiber in Blades
 Funding organization: DOE
 Background: Wind turbine blades manufactured with carbon fiber have been shown to weigh
 25% less than ones made from traditional fiberglass materials. That means blades that utilize
 carbon fiber in their spar-cap are capable of being longer than traditional fiberglass blades. This
 may pose additional impacts to radar systems already stressed with increased number of wind
 turbines constructed within the line-of-sight of the radar. Sandia National Laboratories (Sandia)
 will conduct initial research into the potential increased impact that carbon fiber may have on
 radar systems when wind turbine blades are manufactured with carbon fiber.
 Status: Sandia selected the IEA 15 MW offshore reference wind turbine as the model for this effort
 due to its open-source design, which facilitates collaboration between private industry and public
 research organizations. The team analyzed the electromagnetic scattering behavior of wind
 turbine systems using computational electromagnetic (CEM) methods. Comparisons between
 perfect electric conductor (PEC) and fiberglass boundary conditions highlighted limitations in
 layered impedance boundary conditions for modeling dielectric materials. A working high-
 frequency structure simulation model was established, providing a foundation for future research
 into advanced materials and Doppler-related studies.

Aircraft Detection Lighting System Radar Performance Study

 Background: Aircraft Detection Lighting System (ADLS) (most notably their aircraft detection
 radars) were designed to protect the safety of civilian aircraft. It is not known how they will
 perform to protect the safety of military aircraft, which differ from typical civilian air traffic in
 terms of speed, flight profile, and radar detectability. ADLS radar performance for modern low-
 observable (stealthy) military aircraft is a particular concern. This study examines the suitability
 of ADLS performance to protect the safety of military aviation, by examining the required warning
 distances to allow a military aircraft to safely avoid turbines, and whether current ADLS radars
 can technically and operationally provide appropriate warning distances.
 Status: This study is in progress. A technical analysis is being prepared by the Center for Naval
 Analyses and will be delivered to the DOW’s Military Aviation and Installation Assurance Siting
 Clearinghouse once operational field test data can be incorporated to validate the study findings.

2026 WTRIM WORKING GROUP OBJECTIVES
In fiscal year 2026, the WTRIM WG will continue to cooperate and coordinate to advance near- (5 years),
mid- (10 years), and long-term (20 years) mitigations. Consistent with the activities specified in the
Federal Interagency WTRIM Strategy and the MOA, the WTRIM WG will focus on the following list of
objectives:

 • Continue to support development and execution of technical analyses to identify, characterize,
 and mitigate impacts of wind turbines on member Agency capabilities and mission areas.
 o Increase focus of technical analyses to potential offshore wind impacts to Federal
 agencies and the public equities they support.
 o Support testing and evaluation activities related to WTRIM and Federally-funded
 studies.
 • Continue to support the efforts to mature and deploy mitigation capabilities through field
 testing and analysis.
 • Continue to address concerns associated with impacts to oceanographic and meteorological
 weather radars.
Continue to support the development of next-generation radar systems that are resistant to wind turbine
interference in coordination with efforts such as the Golden Dome and the Brand-New Air Traffic Control
System (BNATC).
