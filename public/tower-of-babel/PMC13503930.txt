Performance evaluation of steel frames with a novel replaceable pipe damper system
Authors: Farshad Taiyari, Jamal Roshani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This paper investigates the seismic behavior of steel moment-resisting frames equipped with a newly developed brace-type damper system, comprising replaceable energy-dissipating pipe elements and supporting cruciform and box elements, and proposes appropriate seismic design factors for implementing such systems within code-based design frameworks. For this purpose, structural models with and without the damper system are developed in OpenSees. The study also evaluates two distinct patterns for allocating pipe elements within the braces in order to estimate the required quantity and to examine their impact on structural performance. An iterative design method, integrated with incremental dynamic analysis (IDA), is employed to quantify the seismic design factors of the system, such as reduction factor due to ductility, overstrength, response modification, and displacement amplification. Fragility curves are subsequently developed to evaluate the likelihood of damage in the analyzed models. The findings indicate the excellent performance of the dual systems, with the first-mode-based distribution of pipe dampers increasing the response modification factor by about 87% compared to the moment-resisting frame. For intermediate steel moment-resisting frames equipped with dampers arranged according to the first mode shape, the overstrength, response modification, and displacement amplification factors are approximately 2.28, 8.57, and 5.42, respectively. The analysis also shows that the first-mode-based distribution performs more effectively than a uniform allocation, achieving comparable structural performance while reducing the total required length of pipe elements.

Introduction

Passive energy dissipation systems are being used more frequently in buildings to mitigate the effects of seismic vibrations. These systems can improve a structure’s seismic performance by supplying the necessary energy dissipation and limiting damage to primary structural components. Among various categories of this type, metallic yielding dampers (MYDs) are more popular due to their simple application, stable hysteresis behavior, and cost-effectiveness. The dissipation of energy in MYDs occurs via plastic deformation of their metallic substances1.

The initial concept of using MYDs in structural systems dates back to the research of Kelly et al.2. Added damping and stiffness (ADAS) dampers3, welded steel triangular-plate added damping and stiffness (TADAS) devices4, shear panel dampers5, and slit dampers6 represent some of the most widely recognized devices of this category. Numerous studies have been carried out over the past few decades to improve the practical limitations of existing MYDs7,8. In this context, various MYD configurations have been suggested, and their seismic behavior has been examined using both experimental tests and numerical analyses. Pipe dampers with shear and bending behavior9–11, U-shaped dampers12–15, accordion metallic dampers16, S-shaped and C-shaped damper devices17,18, and multi-slit dampers19 are some recent MYD proposals.

MYDs can also be classified based on their configuration and placement within the structural framing system. A common approach involves integrating the dampers into chevron bracing systems, where the devices are installed in a predefined gap between the chevron braces and the beam. A key limitation of this approach is the restricted space available, which limits the number of dampers that can be placed within the system14. An alternative configuration involves introducing damper devices within diagonal braces, as recommended by several previous studies10–12,14. This second installation strategy combines the energy dissipation capabilities of MYDs with the buckling-restrained braces (BRBs), which provide enhanced stability by preventing buckling under compressive loads. As a result, it offers effective seismic energy dissipation while minimizing the number of required supporting elements14. In this context, Mazzolani and Taiyari20 examined the seismic response of steel structures incorporating U-shaped metallic dampers. The study considered two damper placement strategies of conventional chevron bracing and a diagonal layout incorporating dampers inside the braces. Their study demonstrated the superiority of the diagonal configuration in terms of both structural performance and cost-effectiveness.

More recently, Roshani and Taiyari11 introduced a novel brace-type damper that incorporates replaceable pipes within a diagonal brace and evaluated its seismic performance through experimental tests and numerical simulations. The results demonstrate that the damper system provides sufficient energy dissipation while maintaining stable hysteretic behavior. Depending on the particular configuration of the damper assembly, a wide range of energy dissipation capacities can be achieved based on structural requirements, resulting in an optimal use of the damper system throughout the full height of the structure. Despite favorable performance of this system, its effects on the structural performance have not yet been investigated and it seems to be a crucial step to provide useful insights for further applications in structures.

The design process of MYDs for application in a structure is another challenging issue that has been addressed by a number of researchers21–25. Zhang et al.22 proposed a simplified design methodology for structures with MYDs, based on maintaining uniform damping ratios. In their study, a bilinear elastoplastic behavior was considered for the damper system. A similar method based on uniform inter-story drifts was also introduced in23. Cheng et al.24 proved the efficiency of the performance-based plastic design approach for steel structures using double-yielding metallic dampers in providing the predefined performance targets at both Design Basis Earthquake and Maximum Considered Earthquake levels. Javidan and Kim25 proposed a simplified ductility-based design procedure for the seismic retrofit of structures using MYDs. Their approach allows the devices to be distributed along the structural height based on predefined performance limit states, and they demonstrated its effectiveness in enhancing structural ductility and energy dissipation. Apart from introduced design approaches, the use of simple and well-known equivalent lateral force method is also permitted for the design of buildings with damper systems in the US code26. It is clear that in this method, appropriate seismic factors for the applied damper system should be estimated. In recent years, numerous studies have been carried out to identify the seismic behavioral factors of building structures equipped with various damper systems27–31.

The primary aim of this study is to evaluate the seismic design factors of steel moment-resisting structures incorporating a new brace-type metallic yielding damper with replaceable pipe elements, which was proposed by the authors in their previous work11. To achieve this, a set of structural models with different number of stories with and without damper systems was selected. Two different distribution patterns of the damper system based on the structural first mode shape and uniform distribution along the height of the structure were considered. Then, incremental dynamic analyses (IDA) were performed to estimate seismic design factors (i.e., reduction factor due to ductility, overstrength, response modification, and displacement amplification factors). In the end, the seismic fragility curves of the structures were obtained and compared with each other to evaluate the effects of the damper system as well as its distribution pattern along the structural height.

Brace-type damper with replaceable pipes

General description

The new brace-type damper system proposed by the authors is based on replaceable pipe elements placed inside a diagonal brace11. These pipe elements are bolted to the cruciform element on one side and to plates of the outer box element on the other side, which are then welded together to form the complete system. Figure 1 illustrates the overall view of the system.

The system is installed to the building frame via connection plates attached to the box and cruciform elements. During lateral deformation of the structure, relative displacement occurs between the box and the cruciform elements, inducing shear forces in the pipe components. These forces activate yielding in the pipes, thereby enabling significant energy dissipation. The deformed and undeformed shapes of the system as well as the pipe elements are presented in Fig. 1 for comparative purposes.

The hysteretic behavior of the damper system has been thoroughly studied under experimental tests and numerical simulations, and the stable hysteresis along with the excellent energy dissipation capacity of the damper system has been confirmed in the previous study11. The overall force-displacement behavior of the system, including three distinct behavioral mechanisms: flexural, flexural-tensile and solo tensile, is illustrated in Fig. 2. This behavior was approximated by a multi-linear curve fitted to the monotonic pushover response, with key parameters calibrated to match brace stiffness and energy dissipation at each stage11. This behavior is characterized by four key points that delineate the transitions between different mechanisms. The corresponding forces and displacements at these transition points are functions of the pipe geometry and can be estimated using the relationships provided in11.

Design considerations

This section presents a simplified design method for key components of the proposed damper system, derived from insights obtained through prior experimental and numerical studies. The design process is outlined through a step-by-step flowchart in Fig. 3, and additional explanations are provided in the subsequent text to guide practical implementation.

Design of energy-dissipating elements

Experimental investigations have shown that the displacement, strength and stiffness capacities of the proposed damper system are highly sensitive to the geometry of the pipe elements11. Specifically, the displacement capacity is predominantly influenced by the pipe’s thickness and outer diameter, whereas the stiffness and strength capacities are more significantly affected by the number and width of the pipe elements. Accordingly, the first step in the design procedure involves selecting appropriate values for the pipe thickness and outer diameter to ensure that the required displacement capacity is achieved for the target design limit state.

Design and modeling of structures

General assumptions

A series of 5, 10, and 15-story buildings, each with a story height of 3.2 m and three bays of 6 m are selected as case studies and designed based on AISC360-2232 to assess the structural performance of dual systems incorporating intermediate moment-resisting frames and the proposed brace-type damper systems11. Three structural configurations are investigated: (1) a system with steel moment-resisting frames only, and (2) two dual systems combining steel moment-resisting frames with the damper system. The dual systems differ in the vertical distribution of the pipe elements, one uses a uniform distribution, where all stories have the same damper properties as the first story, and the other is based on the first mode shape, where the damper properties in each story are designed proportionally to the seismic force estimated from the first mode shape of the structure. These three groups are hereafter referred to as SMRF, SMRFD(m1), and SMRFD(u), respectively, for the sake of simplification, where “D” indicates the inclusion of dampers, and “m1” and “u” denote the distribution pattern of the dampers. Plan and elevation views of the studied frames are illustrated in Fig. 5.

For all floors, dead and live gravity loads are considered as 5, and 2 kN/m2, respectively. The buildings are assumed to be constructed in a site with high seismicity, where the design spectral accelerations at 0.2 s and 1.0 s are 1.5 g and 0.94 g, respectively. The seismic design of the models was done according to the ASCE7-2226 and the following assumptions are considered: the importance factor Ie = 1, site Class D, and seismic design category D. The response modification factor R, and the displacement amplification factor Cd of the intermediate SMRF are taken to be 4.5, and 4, respectively, as given in ASCE7-22. While they are obtained iteratively for SMRFD(m1) and SMRFD(u) models, starting from initial values of 8 and 5 as given for the BRBs in this code, respectively.

A two-dimensional finite element model of each structure is created in OpenSees software to conduct dynamic analyses. Column and beam elements are defined using the dispBeamColumn element, with wide flange sections (W-sections) represented by fiber sections. Grade S300 steel, characterized by a 200 GPa elastic modulus, 300 MPa yield strength, and 460 MPa ultimate strength, is assigned to the fiber sections through uniaxialMaterial Steel02. The material’s inelastic deformation is limited to 15 times its yield strain using the uniaxialMaterial MinMax option. The P-delta effect is included to capture geometric nonlinearity in all analyzed models.

In this study, the steel moment-resisting frames are modeled with idealized rigid beam-to-column connections, and panel zone deformations are neglected, reflecting common practical assumptions in seismic design. Composite slab effects are not considered; while composite action can significantly influence seismic performance, previous studies have shown that its impact is secondary to the global structural response of the frame33–36. The current study focuses on evaluating the contribution of the proposed replaceable pipe damper system to the overall seismic performance of the structures.

The iterative design process

According to ASCE7-2226, the moment frame of a dual system equipped with a damper system was designed to independently resist at least 75% of the prescribed lateral seismic forces. In addition, the damper system was separately designed to provide resistance corresponding to 50% of the prescribed lateral seismic forces. The complete structural system was subsequently verified to satisfy the required inter-story drift limits under the total design lateral loading. The main design challenge in these building frames is to correctly estimate their seismic factors, such as reduction factor due to ductility, overstrength, response modification, and displacement amplification factors. These factors can be extracted through the structural capacity curve. Herein, the calculation of shear forces for the structures equipped with the proposed system is performed iteratively, starting with the response modification and displacement amplification of 8, and 5, as recommended for dual systems equipped with BRB in the US code. The moment frames and the damper system are designed separately for 75% and 50% of the resulting shear force, as mentioned earlier, and the moment-resisting frame together with the damper system is checked to meet the design code criteria for the assumed performance level. The width and quantity of pipe elements for each model are subsequently established according to their selected distribution pattern along the building height (either uniform or first-mode-based), following the design procedure described in Sect. 2.2. Afterwards, Incremental dynamic analyses are conducted on the models using a series of earthquake records (as will be discussed in Sect. 4), and the new seismic factors are re-calculated based on the IDA results through the Eqs. Earthquake records and IDA method

The FEMA-P69538 earthquake records are used here for nonlinear dynamic analyses. The records as well as their main characteristics including magnitude, peak ground acceleration (PGA), site class, and fault type are presented in Table 2. All records are from locations equal to or greater than 10 km from the epicenter. Owing to the 2D characteristics of the considered models, only the largest component of each record in term of PGA is adopted for the analyses.

Herein, the evaluation of seismic behavioral factors of the structural models is done through incremental dynamic analysis (IDA). IDA is a parametric analysis method that is able to estimate the performance of a structure under a series of ground motion records, each scaled at different intensity levels39,40. In the current study, PGA is opted as the earthquake intensity measure, as the derived capacity curves of base shear versus roof displacement are independent of the chosen intensity measure. The scaling process begins with a PGA of 0.1 g and is increased in increments of 0.1 g until a predefined damage limit state is reached. The capacity curves obtained from IDA in terms of base shear and maximum roof displacement are then used to calculate seismic factors, as mentioned in Sect. 3.3. This approach, which derives seismic factors from capacity curves generated by IDA, has been successfully applied in previous studies41–43.

The limit state of failure is defined based on both global and local criteria: global limit states include a maximum inter-story drift ratio of 2%, and global dynamic instability of the structure, while local limit states include the steel material’s ultimate strain and the damper system’s maximum displacement capacity. The occurrence of any global limit state is considered as the termination criterion, whereas local limit states alone do not define collapse but may contribute to the onset of global failure.

Results and discussion

A series of IDA analyses are conducted for the considered structural models in order to assess the efficiency of the proposed brace-type damper system in enhancing the structural performance and to provide appropriate seismic factors required for their code-based design process. As previously discussed in Sect. 2.2, the stiffness and strength capacities of the brace are mostly affected by the width of the pipe as well as their number within the brace. Therefore, they are considered as the main design variable to achieve optimum solutions. Herein, the optimal values of the width and the number of pipes is obtained during the structural design process, and their corresponding values at the final stage of the iterative design process are reported in Table 3 for SMRFD(m1), and SMRFD(u) models. Due to the specific configuration of the pipes inside the brace (see Fig. 1), their total number must be a multiple of four to prevent undesirable torsional movements of the cruciform element during the operation of the system. Therefore, further optimization can be achieved by changing the pipe width. Besides, the distribution pattern of pipe elements along the structural height is another important issue to consider for optimal design, as the required number of pipes in each story depends on the assumed lateral load pattern distribution. According to Table 3, the total pipe length increases by about 62%, 33%, and 40% in the 5-, 10-, and 15-story models, respectively, when a uniform distribution of pipe elements along the structural height is preferred to a distribution pattern similar to their first mode shape.

It is observed that both the nonlinearity of the IDA curves and the corresponding PGA levels increase in structures equipped with the proposed bracing system compared with the conventional SMRF, indicating an improvement in seismic performance. For the 5-story models, the maximum PGA levels are nearly identical, with values of 1.20 g, 1.18 g, and 1.20 g for SMRF, SMRFD(m1), and SMRFD(u), respectively. In contrast, for taller structures, the presence of the damper system leads to noticeably higher PGA capacities. For the 10-story models, the maximum PGA values are 1.09 g, 1.37 g, and 1.27 g, while for the 15-story models they are 1.22 g, 1.54 g, and 1.53 g for SMRF, SMRFD(m1), and SMRFD(u), respectively. These results indicate that the seismic capacity improvement becomes more pronounced as the building height increases, while the influence of the damper distribution pattern along the structural height remains relatively small.

As observed from the median curves, the yield point of the SMRF models is higher than that of the SMRFD models, resulting in a larger elastic region for the conventional moment-resisting frames. In contrast, the nonlinear region and the associated ductility of the SMRFD models are significantly greater, reflecting the enhanced energy dissipation capacity provided by the proposed bracing system. Furthermore, the comparison of the curves in Fig. 10 indicates the larger initial stiffness and lower ultimate strength of the SMRFD models than the SMRFs. Considering the average values of 5-, 10-, and 15-story buildings, the ultimate strength of SMRFD(m1) models is about 50%, 33%, 16% lower than the corresponding SMRFs, respectively. The reduction percentages are 46%, 34%, and 14%, respectively, considering the SMRFD(u) models.

Seismic factors including reduction factor due to ductility, overstrength factor, response modification factor, and displacement amplification factor are determined for all considered models through the repetitive procedure presented in Sect. 3.3, and the average results of the 22 records are presented in Fig. 11 for each structural model. The numerical values of seismic factors are also given in Table 4.

According to Fig. 11, the highest overstrength factor and also the lowest ductility factor belongs to SMRF models; however, the opposite finding is observed with respect to SMRFD(u) models. This can be also obtained from the results of Fig. 10 by comparing the yield and maximum displacement and strength of the models. The SMRFD(m1) models show slightly higher and smaller overstrength and ductility reduction factors, respectively, with respect to the corresponding values of SMRFD(u) models. It is also obtained that as the number of stories increases, the overstrength factor decreases. The trend is almost reversed according to the results of ductility reduction factor. The average values of overstrength factors for SMRF, SMRFD(m1), and SMRFD(u) configurations are 2.70, 2.28, and 2.20, respectively; while considering the results of ductility reduction factor, they are 1.70, 3.80, and 3.98, respectively.

The response modification factor shows a descending trends with respect to the number of stories. A similar trend is also seen in the results of displacement amplification factors. The average values of R for the SMRF, SMRFD(m1), and SMRFD(u) models are calculated to be about 4.59, 8.57, and 8.62, respectively. The corresponding results of Cd are also 2.65, 5.42, and 5.70, respectively. It can be obtained that although the SMRF models exhibit higher ultimate strength, their response modification and displacement amplification factors are lower compared with the SMRFD models. This indicates that these factors are primarily governed by the ductility and energy dissipation capacity of the structures rather than the ultimate strength.

Figure 12 illustrates the fragility curves of the considered structural models, obtained based on their IDA results. These curves represent the cumulative distribution of earthquake intensity with respect to the collapse probability or other limit states. The fragility curves can be estimated by both analytical or empirical methods depending on the type of analysis and data source. In the IDA method, the response of structures under a set of ground motions is used to obtain the data required for fragility estimation. The fragility is defined as the fraction of the number of records that satisfy the assumed limit state, to the total number of records. Fragility curves are generated by fitting the collected data to a log-normal cumulative distribution function.

Comparison of the fragility curves between the different structural configurations indicates a similar damage potential for the 5-story models. This observation is also supported by the statistical parameters of the fragility functions, where the median collapse capacities are 1.22 g, 1.18 g, and 1.26 g for the SMRF, SMRFD(m1), and SMRFD(u) models, respectively, with corresponding logarithmic standard deviations of 0.37, 0.40, and 0.41. As the number of stories increases, the probability of damage in the SMRFD models decreases compared to the conventional SMRF system. For the 10-story structures, the median collapse capacities increase from 1.09 g in the SMRF to 1.38 g and 1.34 g in the SMRFD(m1) and SMRFD(u) configurations, respectively, while the associated dispersions are 0.46, 0.41, and 0.44. A similar trend is observed in the 15-story models, where the median capacities of the damped systems reach 1.56 g and 1.57 g, compared with 1.17 g for the SMRF, with dispersion values ranging between 0.42 and 0.47. These results confirm that the proposed damper system significantly improves the collapse capacity of mid- and high-rise frames. It is also observed that the damage potential of SMRFD(m1) and SMRFD(u) systems remains very similar, indicating that the excessive use of pipe elements in the uniform distribution pattern does not lead to a noticeable improvement in the fragility performance of the structure.

Conclusions

The seismic performance of dual systems with steel moment-resisting frames and a novel brace-type damper system with replaceable pipes is investigated in this study. A step-by-step instruction for the design of the damper system is provided, and proper seismic factors required for code-based design are recommended. Different structural models with and without damper systems are considered for the analyses, and two different distribution patterns are used to estimate the required number of pipes along the height of structure to better understand its impact on the performance of structures. Seismic factors of the assumed structures including reduction factor due to ductility, overstrength, response modification, and displacement amplification factors are calculated through the results of incremental dynamic analyses. The effect of the damper system on the damage probability has also been investigated based on their fragilities. The main results are presented as follows:
