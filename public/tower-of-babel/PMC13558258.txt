Longitudinal Wire Bias Pattern on Intravascular Ultrasound as an Independent Predictor of Coronary Artery Injury During Orbital Atherectomy for Calcified Nodules
Authors: Nobuhiro Watanabe, Yoshiro Tsukiyama, Tomohiro Inoue, Taishi Miyata, Hiroto Kinutani, Nobuyuki Takahashi, Hiroyuki Yamamoto, Shinsuke Nakano, Koki Matsuo, Keisuke Miwa, Mitsuaki Itoh, Hiroya Kawai, Tomofumi Takaya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCalcified nodules (CN) are a challenging coronary lesion subset often treated with orbital atherectomy (OA), which, although effective, carries a risk of coronary artery injury (CAI), including hematoma and perforation. In this study we assessed the relationship between intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) findings, particularly the wire bias pattern, and CAI during OA for CN.Methods and ResultsWe retrospectively analyzed 67 patients with CN who underwent IVUS-guided percutaneous coronary intervention with OA between August 2020 and August 2023. The primary endpoint was CAI, defined as hematoma or perforation. IVUS findings, including tenting sign, undesirable wire position, and a newly classified wire bias pattern (Types A–D), were evaluated. 9 patients (13.4%) developed CAI: 8 hematomas (11.9%) and 1 perforation (1.5%). Type D wire bias showed the highest incidence of CAI (47.1%, P<0.001). Multivariate logistic regression identified Type D as the sole independent predictor of CAI (odds ratio 22.43; 95% confidence interval 2.61–192.9; P=0.005). Neither tenting sign nor undesirable wire position was significantly associated.ConclusionsA Type D wire bias pattern on IVUS (defined as the guidewire tracking along the healthy vessel wall proximal and distal to the CN) was an independent predictor of CAI during OA. Preprocedural identification of this pattern may improve risk stratification and procedural strategies to minimize complications.

Calcified nodules (CNs) are a challenging subset of coronary lesions characterized by calcium deposits protruding into the vessel lumen, creating a convex luminal surface.1 CNs are associated with increased procedural complexity and higher complication rates during percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI).2 Their irregular morphology and hardness often necessitate specialized atherectomy techniques to achieve adequate lesion preparation and successful stent deployment or use of a drug-coated balloon (DCB).

Recent studies from Japanese centers have provided important insights into the clinical characteristics and outcomes of CN interventions. Nozoe et al. demonstrated that the use of rotational atherectomy (RA) was significantly associated with a lower incidence of restenosis in CNs.3 Khalifa et al. showed that stent implantation without debulking devices for CNs may result in suboptimal stent expansion in the setting of acute myocardial infarction.4 Furthermore, recent studies have demonstrated that CNs remain associated with unfavorable long-term clinical outcomes despite the use of contemporary PCI strategies, including debulking devices and DCB therapy.5 Taken together, these findings suggest that adequate lesion preparation using debulking devices may play an important role in the treatment of CNs. However, CNs are typically eccentric, making debulking devices more likely to contact the healthy vessel wall than with a superficial calcific sheet, thereby potentially increasing the risk of coronary artery injury (CAI). Previous reports have also suggested that CNs may represent a risk factor for debulking device-related complications.6

Orbital atherectomy (OA) has emerged as an effective treatment modality for heavily calcified coronary lesions, including CN.7 The recent ECLIPSE trial demonstrated the efficacy of OA vs. balloon angioplasty for severely calcified lesions, establishing OA as a viable treatment option for appropriately selected patients.8 The device utilizes a diamond-coated crown that orbits at high speeds to modify calcified plaque, facilitating subsequent balloon dilatation and stent implantation.9 Although OA has demonstrated efficacy in improving procedural success rates, it is associated with potential complications, including CAI manifesting as hematoma formation or coronary artery perforation.10 Therefore, optimal patient selection criteria to minimize complications is an area of active investigation.

Intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) guidance has become the standard of care for complex PCI, providing detailed vessel morphology assessment and enabling optimization of procedural planning.11 IVUS can identify specific features that may predispose complications during atherectomy procedures, including vessel wall characteristics, calcium distribution, and guidewire positioning relative to the lesion.12 Recent expert consensus documents have further emphasized the importance of imaging-guided device selection, lesion morphology assessment, and procedural planning for heavily calcified and anatomically complex coronary lesions.13,14

Previous studies have reported various predictors of complications during atherectomy procedures, but the specific IVUS parameters that predict CAI during OA for CN remain unclear.15 Traditionally, vessel injury risk has been considered to be assessable by single cross-sectional IVUS evaluation. However, CNs frequently exhibit eccentric calcium distribution and longitudinal morphological irregularity.16 Because OA involves rotational sanding along the vessel axis, vessel injury may be influenced not only by the guidewire position at the minimal lumen area (MLA) but also by the longitudinal pattern of sustained guidewire–vessel wall interaction throughout the treatment segment. Furthermore, a guidewire inherently tends to straighten within a tortuous vessel segment, generating directional force vectors against the vessel wall. Such longitudinal mechanical forces cannot be adequately assessed by a single cross-sectional image, which may not fully capture the spatial and mechanical relationship between the guidewire and the vessel wall along the lesion length.

We hypothesized that longitudinal guidewire bias patterns might better reflect the mechanical environment predisposing to CAI. Accordingly, the objective of this study was to clarify the relationship between IVUS findings and the occurrence of CAI following OA during PCI for CN, with particular emphasis on establishing and evaluating a novel longitudinal wire bias pattern classification.

Methods

Study Design and Population

This retrospective, single-center observational study was conducted at Hyogo Prefectural Harima-Himeji General Medical Center in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. We analyzed the data for consecutive patients with CN who underwent IVUS-guided PCI using OA between August 2020 and August 2023. The primary endpoint was defined as CAI, the occurrence of hematoma or coronary artery perforation during or immediately after OA. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Hyogo Prefectural Harima-Himeji General Medical Center (Approval No. [R07-33]), and the requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the analysis.

Patient Selection

Inclusion criteria were: (1) presence of CN confirmed by IVUS, (2) treatment with OA during PCI, (3) availability of complete IVUS imaging data, and (4) complete procedural and outcome data. Exclusion criteria included emergency procedures without adequate imaging assessment and patients with previous stent implantation at the target site.

PCI Procedure

The use of OA was allowed at the operator’s discretion. OA was primarily performed using a pullback technique after IVUS. When the OA catheter could not cross the lesion in the delivery mode, predilation with a small-diameter balloon was performed to facilitate passage of the OA catheter in the delivery mode. Antegrade debulking was undertaken only when the device could not be advanced despite these measures, and this approach was required in 6.0% of cases. Debulking was initially performed 3 times at low speed, followed by IVUS to confirm procedural safety and effectiveness. Escalation to the high-speed mode was considered only when IVUS assessment indicated that size-up was both feasible and necessary. IVUS was also used to qualitatively assess procedural efficacy, including plaque modification, lumen gain, and stent expansion before and after OA and PCI.

IVUS Analysis

In the present study, IVUS images for the assessment of OA safety were derived from the initial IVUS examination performed prior to OA. When the IVUS catheter could not cross the lesion before OA, the first IVUS examination obtained after antegrade OA was used for analysis.

All IVUS examinations were performed using commercially available systems. Images were acquired during automated pullback at 1 mm/s. Two blinded investigators reviewed images independently; discrepancies were resolved by consensus. Interobserver agreement was assessed, and disagreements were resolved by consensus.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean±standard deviation or median (interquartile range) as appropriate. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. Comparisons between groups were performed using Student’s t-test or the Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables and chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. Univariate logistic regression analysis was performed to identify potential predictors of CAI. Variables with P<0.20 in the univariate analysis were included in the multivariate model. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed using backward elimination to identify independent predictors of CAI. Results are presented as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). A two-sided P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using SPSS (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Patients’ Characteristics

A total of 67 consecutive patients with CN treated with OA were included in the analysis (Table 1). The mean age was 76.0±8.5 years, and 70.1% were male. Diabetes mellitus was present in 49.3% of patients, hypertension in 79.1%, and dyslipidemia in 83.5%. Chronic renal failure requiring hemodialysis was present in 25.3% of patients. Acute coronary syndrome was the presenting condition in 29.9% of cases. Lesion distribution included left anterior descending artery in 24 patients (35.8%), right coronary artery in 23 patients (34.4%), left circumflex artery in 19 patients (28.4%), and left main trunk in 1 patient (1.5%). Predictors of CAI

The univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses are presented in Table 4. Univariate logistic regression analysis identified the wire bias pattern as significantly associated with CAI (OR 23.20; 95% CI 2.35–228.83; P=0.007). The tenting sign (OR 2.26; 95% CI 0.33–15.57; P=0.41) and undesirable wire position (OR 3.90; 95% CI 0.54–28.17; P=0.18) did not reach statistical significance. In the multivariate logistic regression analysis adjusting for potential confounders, the wire bias pattern remained the only independent predictor of CAI (OR 22.43; 95% CI 2.61–192.9; P=0.005). Discussion

This study represents the first comprehensive analysis of IVUS predictors of CAI during OA for CN. Our key finding was that the wire bias pattern, particularly the Type D configuration in which the guidewire is positioned near the healthy vessel wall both distal and proximal to the CN, is a strong and independent predictor of CAI with an OR=22.43. The principal novelty of the present study is the introduction of a longitudinal wire bias pattern classification. Our findings suggest that longitudinal assessment provides additional mechanistic insight beyond single cross-sectional evaluation and may improve preprocedural risk stratification in CNs.

Clinical Significance of the Wire Bias Pattern

The concept of a wire bias pattern has not been previously described as a predictor of complications during OA for CN. Our classification system, based on guidewire position relative to the CN and healthy vessel wall, is a novel risk stratification tool. The dramatically increased CAI risk with the Type D pattern (47.1% vs. 0–7.1% for the other patterns) suggests that this finding should be considered a strong contraindication to OA or should prompt alternative treatment strategies.

Even among calcified lesions, the specific morphology and distribution pattern of calcification may substantially influence procedural outcomes and complication risk during PCI.17 The mechanism underlying this association likely relates to the eccentric nature of CNs and the orbital motion of the atherectomy device. When the guidewire and catheter are biased toward the healthy vessel wall, the OA crown may preferentially engage and traumatize the non-calcified portion of the vessel, leading to hematoma formation or vessel perforation.18

Comparison With Previous Studies

Lee et al. recently reported that, in optical coherence tomography imaging, a catheter contact angle >92° and guidewire contact with the normal coronary artery were associated with CAI after OA.19 A catheter contact angle >92° and guidewire contact are considered to correspond to the tenting sign and undesirable wire position in our study. Importantly, our investigation exclusively focused on CNs, a particularly high-risk subset of coronary lesions. Although both the tenting sign and undesirable wire position were identified as potential risk factors, the wire bias pattern proved to be an even stronger predictor. Thus, our IVUS-based wire bias pattern classification provides a complementary approach that can be assessed preprocedurally, offering practical guidance for risk stratification.

Previous studies have reported varying complication rates with OA, with perforation rates typically ranging from 0.5% to 3.0%.20 Our overall CAI rate of 13.4% is higher than some reports, but includes both hematoma and perforation, with the latter occurring in only 1.5% of cases, consistent with the published literature. The higher overall rate may reflect our specific focus on CNs, which represent a particularly challenging lesion subset.

Yamamoto et al. emphasized the importance of combined atherectomy approaches for severe nodular calcification, particularly in curved arteries where wire bias becomes critical.21 Previous reports have also highlighted that vessel tortuosity and CNs may increase the risk of device-related complications during OAS procedures.6 Our study extends these concepts by identifying specific IVUS features that predict complications, enabling more precise risk assessment.

Enhanced Clinical Implications

The identification of the wire bias pattern as a strong predictor of CAI has important practical clinical implications. Preprocedural IVUS assessment should include systematic evaluation of wire position at the MLA and adjacent segments. When the Type D wire bias pattern is identified, operators should consider: wire repositioning techniques using specialized guidewires or support devices to move away from the healthy wall; alternative lesion preparation strategies such as intravascular lithotripsy or RA; modified OA technique with lower speeds, shorter treatment duration or smaller crown size; enhanced procedural vigilance with frequent angiographic assessment for early signs of vessel injury; DCB therapy as the preferred final treatment strategy to avoid further vessel trauma, consistent with the concept of the “leave nothing behind” strategy proposed in recent expert consensus documents;22 consideration of postponing the procedure to optimize wire position or selecting alternative treatment approaches.

The tenting sign and undesirable wire position, although not independently predictive in our multivariate analysis, showed trends toward increased CAI risk and may serve as additional warning signs when present in conjunction with unfavorable wire bias patterns. Real-time IVUS guidance should be used to confirm guidewire position throughout the target lesion before initiating OA. Furthermore, recent OCT-based analyses have suggested that even after apparently adequate calcific debulking with RA or OA, residual lipid-rich plaque morphology may still adversely affect stent expansion and procedural outcomes.23 These findings further underscore the inherent complexity and therapeutic challenges of CNs. Although debulking devices may play an important role in the treatment of CNs, careful preprocedural assessment of CAI risk remains essential in determining whether atherectomy-based PCI should be performed.

Study Limitations

First, this was a single-center retrospective study with a relatively small sample size (n=67), which may limit the generalizability of our findings and statistical power for detecting smaller effect sizes. Given the 13.4% event rate, our study had adequate power (>80%) to detect large effect sizes (OR >3.0) but limited power for smaller effects. Second, the definition and classification of the wire bias pattern is novel and requires validation in larger, multicenter studies with standardized assessment protocols. Third, because the aim of this study was to evaluate the safety of OA based on the initial IVUS assessment, dynamic changes in guidewire bias pattern before and after OA debulking were not evaluated. Fourth, the study did not assess debulking effects, focusing primarily on acute procedural complications. Additionally, our patient population was specifically selected for CN morphology, and these findings may not apply to other types of calcified lesions treated with OA. Finally, the retrospective nature precludes assessment of whether alternative treatment strategies guided by IVUS findings might have prevented complications. The wide CI for our primary finding (OR 2.61–192.9) reflect the relatively small sample size and suggest that larger studies are needed to provide more precise estimates.

Future Directions

Future research should focus on prospective validation of the wire bias pattern classification system and development of standardized assessment protocols. Investigation of wire repositioning techniques and alternative treatment strategies for high-risk patterns is warranted. Additionally, development of automated or semi-automated IVUS analysis tools to identify wire bias patterns could facilitate widespread clinical adoption. Multicenter studies incorporating the extensive Japanese experience with atherectomy procedures would provide valuable validation of our findings.

Conclusions

The longitudinal wire bias pattern on IVUS, especially the Type D configuration, was a strong independent predictor of CAI during OA for CN. This novel finding provides clinicians with a valuable risk stratification tool for procedural planning and complication avoidance. Preprocedural IVUS assessment should include systematic evaluation of the wire bias pattern to optimize patient safety and procedural outcomes. Further validation of these findings in larger, prospective studies is warranted to establish standardized assessment protocols and alternative treatment strategies for high-risk patterns.
