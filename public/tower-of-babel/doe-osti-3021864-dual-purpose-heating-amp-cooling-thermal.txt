 Executive Summary
The integration of heat pumps with thermal energy storage (HP-TES) systems is gaining
attention as a viable solution for managing peak building demand driven by immense
cooling and heating loads. With growing reliance on renewable energy sources, thermal
energy storage offers an excellent opportunity to mitigate mismatches in thermal load
between energy supply and demand. The use of phase-change material (PCM) TES is
especially promising, as PCMs offer significant latent energy storage capacity with
smaller temperature glides in smaller volumes compared to other TES technologies.
However, challenges arise because current HP-TES architectures can load-shift only
cooling or heating, not both, requiring two systems and thus doubling cost, weight, and
footprint. Furthermore, current research efforts lack specific tools and techniques to
advance integrated systems from concept design to end-user application, focusing on
only discharge performance.
To address these challenges, this research proposes a dual-mode (heating and cooling)
integrated HP-TES system that uses room-temperature PCM-TES as a high-temperature
heat source in heating mode and a low-temperature heat sink in cooling mode, thereby
reducing temperature lifts and compressor power. Design criteria for PCM-TES heat
exchangers were developed, balancing thermal-hydraulic performance with practical
constraints such as available building space and weight requirements along with PCM
selection considerations such as shipping conditions, moisture exposure, and number of
available cycles.
A detailed transient model for HP-TES systems was developed to enable rapid annual
performance assessments in any US climate zone. Detailed comparisons of HP-TES
performance versus a state-of-the-art base heat pump were conducted for all US
ASHRAE climate zones. In cooling mode, overall cooling demand reductions in California
were around 20%, while Honolulu's tropical climate led to 14% reductions. Heating mode
demand reductions were notably higher, especially in very cold-climate regions, ranging
from 40% to 65% for Chicago, IL, to Fairbanks, AK, primarily because the new HP-TES
system does not require additional backup heating during peak hours.
A laboratory-scale HP-TES system was prototyped and tested using a novel test matrix
designed to cover a wide range of possible operating modes, e.g., continuous and cyclic
heating and cooling modes. Experimental testing showed demand reductions of 0.6%-
30% in cooling mode and 40%-60% in heating mode, with the larger reductions observed
under more extreme ambient conditions. The experimental data were utilized to validate
the transient HP-TES model, which demonstrated excellent agreement in capacity (<4%),
power draw (<1%), and compressor suction / discharge conditions. The highest
deviations were reported during startup conditions.
Finally, a commercialization plan for HP-TES was presented, highlighting key insights on
capital expenses and expected location-specific operating cost savings, along with
opportunities for California-specific markets. The work presented here provides design
guides and procedures to turn such a system from concept to an off-the-shelf product for
consumers.

 Background
Motivation
According to the International Energy Agency (IEA), the building sector (residential,
public, and commercial) currently accounts for 30% of the global energy consumption and
28% of the worldwide carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions (Figure 1(a)) [1]. Space heating
accounts for 45% of building emissions, whilst space cooling consumes 20% of global
electrical demand [1], [2]. As urban development expands and decarbonization efforts
continue, the demand for electrical space heating and cooling systems is expected to
increase. This will put more pressure on the electrical grid, especially during peak
demands, and relying partially on wind and solar energy may lead to unstable supply and
higher costs to meet those peak needs, e.g., the duck curve in California (Figure 1(b)).
Thus, exploring avenues such as energy storage systems and improving the grid
reliability and interactivity can stabilize the grid's electrical supply and provide the
asynchronous balance of supply and demand requirements [3], [4].

 Figure 1: (a) global mean surface temperatures [5], and (b) daily curve energy duck-curve in
 California [6]

Energy storage systems can be classified into electrochemical, mechanical, or thermal
types [7]. Electrochemical energy storage typically uses batteries that consist of an
electrolyte, an anode, and a cathode, where electrical energy is released or gained by the
movement of ions from the anode to the cathode through the electrolyte during discharge
and, when connected to a circuit, during recharge [8], typically used renewable energy
sources such as wind or solar photovoltaics [9], [10]. Mechanical energy storage systems
usually include liquid displacement systems, such as dams, which are beyond the scope
of this study [7]. Thermal energy storage (TES) systems have significant potential in the
US, particularly for space heating and cooling applications, given their substantial share
in the built environment and the increasing adoption of dual-purpose (heating and cooling)
heat pumps (HPs). In the US, space heating and cooling account for almost 50% of the
energy use in residential and commercial buildings (Figure 2) [11].

                                          Page 7 of 127

 Figure 2: Energy use breakdown in the US (a) residential buildings [11], and (b) commercial
 buildings [12]

While TES systems have high potential, significant challenges arise in implementing them
to meet space, peak energy, and recharge energy requirements. In general, integrated
space conditioning TES systems consume more annual energy, despite reducing peak
energy requirements, due to higher recharge energy demands, striking the need for
designs which improve overall system round-trip efficiency. That being said, there is no
standardized method for designing an integrated heat pump thermal energy storage (HP-
TES) system, as numerous system architectures and design considerations exist [13],
[14]. Such different system architectures can yield varying control strategies, TES
considerations (materials and designs), and operational approaches [15], [16].
Additionally, throughout the literature, HP-TES systems are often modeled with simplified
assumptions at specific design conditions and then extrapolated annually, which may lead
to optimistic scenarios [17]. Furthermore, the US has diverse climate zones, varying
heating and cooling demand profiles, and peak hours; thus, there is no single HP-TES
system design applicable to all locations [18].
Literature Survey: Available HP-TES Technologies and Controls
Thermal Energy Storage Technologies
Thermal energy storage systems can be categorized into three types: sensible, latent,
and thermochemical (Table 1). In sensible TES systems, the main modes of storage rely
on the material's specific heat capacity, where the driving force for heat transfer is the
material's thermal conductivity and the temperature difference of the TES system medium
[19].

The use of smart TES system integrations was reported in the literature, especially in
heating applications [22], [23]. Nonetheless, the literature indicates that sensible TES
requires a large volume of material (e.g., water), which can lead to stratification within
TES tanks [15], [20], [24]. One study used water temperature variation in a heat pump
water heater (HPWH), with the bottom side serving as a heat source and the top side as
a heat sink, thereby further increasing the temperature variation within a sensible storage
tank [25].
Given the substantial volume of sensible TES requirements and the lower specific heat
capacity, a possible alternative is a latent TES system. In these systems, energy is stored
through the solid-liquid and/or liquid-vapor phase transition of a material. Such materials,
referred to as phase-change materials (PCMs), have a specific storage capacity during
the phase transition process, during which energy is added or released as the material
melts or solidifies, or boils or condenses. In general, PCMs are typically those with solid-
to-liquid phase transitions [17], [20], [21], [26]. The reduction in volume compared to the
sensible TES system primarily stems from the higher thermal energy stored in the two-
phase region than the specific heat capacity of sensible TES [27]. This is evident in Figure
3, where the required energy to change phase at the phase-change temperature or within
the temperature range is substantial compared to the single-phase sensible regions.

HP-TES Integration Strategies
TES integrations can be either active or passive. Active TES enables a complete
intentional control of melting and solidification through a mechanical system, whereas
passive systems rely solely on ambient temperatures or conditions to discharge and
charge [29]. Examples of passive TES integrations include the pre-cooling of outdoor air
around the condenser (Figure 4), as described by Said & Hassan [30], to lower the
temperature lift during the vapor compression cycle during high-temperature hours. In this
case, the PCM is discharged and melted using outdoor hot air, and then recharged in
cooler nocturnal ambient conditions. Another example of a passive system was proposed
by Chaiyat & Kiatsiriroat [31], in which the recirculated room air was pre-cooled before

                                        Page 9 of 127

mixing with the ventilation air. In this case, warm room-temperature return air melts the
PCM during peak hours, and cooled off-coil supply air is used to solidify the PCM. In both
examples, PCM integration techniques were passively implemented to limit HP operating
hours during high-demand periods, thereby reducing grid power draw.

 Figure 4: Passive HP-TES systems: precooling condenser air (left, Said & Hassan [30]), and pre-
 cooling mixed air (right, Chaiyat & Kiatsiriroat [31])

As passive systems are limited in their applications and potential, there is a greater focus
on active systems [15], [32] due to their flexibility and higher peak-shaving potential.
Active HP-TES systems utilize mechanical components and controls to operate the TES,
achieving higher peak energy reductions due to more flexible system architectures [17],
[33] and more robust control strategies [34]. Active HP-TES system concepts exist in
several configurations, depending on the location and the application. Cooling-specific
HP-TES applications usually use a low-temperature PCM (e.g., ice) as a heat sink or
directly to cool a space (Figure 5(a)). On the other hand, heating-specific utilizes a high-
temperature heat source (Figure 5(b)).

Figure 5: Examples of (a) space cooling only HP-TES chiller-ice storage system [35] and (b)space
 heating-based HP-TES system [14]

To leverage HP-TES load-shifting capabilities in both cooling and heating modes, one
option is to use two TES tanks, one hot and one cold, to reduce peak loads, as
experimentally demonstrated by Moreno et al.[36]. In this study, a water-to-water HP was
combined with two PCM-TES tanks for charging purposes: one connected to the
evaporator (PCM temperature: 10°C) and the other to the condenser (PCM temperature:
                                         Page 10 of 127

46°C). Both tanks utilize salt-hydrate PCMs, which are macro-encapsulated in modules
within a PCM heat-transfer fluid tank. In discharge mode, the tanks are directly connected
to a prototype building through hydronic loops and three-way valves. Comparisons were
made between the PCM tanks, and only sensible water storage was conducted, showing
that the PCM tank can store 33.5% more energy per volume, maintaining indoor setpoint
conditions by 21% longer. Nonetheless, the recharge time for the PCM tanks was 4.6
times higher. Challenges occurred in charging the TES due to PCM supercooling and the
low PCM thermal conductivities.
While the literature has shown the need to reduce peak demand as much as possible, it
is important to investigate the conditions under which demand reduction can yield energy
savings. To this end, Hirschey et al. [37] conducted an exploratory study on appropriate
TES temperatures, employing a Carnot COP approach to assess both demand reduction
and energy-saving potential. In line with the literature, lower TES temperatures in cooling
mode provided greater cooling demand reduction, and vice versa in heating (i.e., higher
TES temperatures yield greater heating demand reductions). However, this increases
overall energy requirements, as more energy is needed to recharge the TES. Therefore,
it is important to keep the TES temperature as close as possible to the application's
temperature to achieve energy savings.
This leads to the option of integrating a single TES which utilizes near-room-temperature
PCMs (20–30°C) with HP systems to load-shift in both cooling and heating modes (Figure
6). These room-temperature PCM-TES minimize compressor power by reducing the
temperature lifts, i.e., increasing heating-mode evaporating temperatures and reducing
cooling-mode condensing temperatures [38]. A techno-economic study was conducted
using simplified models and rule-based controls to assess the peak savings in different
locations. Results indicate that higher peak reductions can be achieved in extreme
climatic locations. Furthermore, PCMs around 20°C favored cooling-dominated regions,
while PCMs around 30°C favored heating-dominated areas, consistent with the
temperature-lift dependence.

HP-TES Controls Strategies
HP-TES system architecture is not the only contributing factor to peak load reduction and
annual energy consumption; system controls play a significant role in determining the
peak load reduction and shifting capabilities. While most control strategies come from
chiller-ice systems, it is important to understand: (1) the control hierarchy, (2) control
algorithms and approaches, and (3) their impact on demand reduction and energy
consumption. Thus, in HP-TES system controls, three categories need to be considered:
(1) discharge control, (2) recharge control, and (3) building loads.
Controls can be classified into two levels: (1) traditional component-level controls, and (2)
advanced system-level controls [15]. Traditional controls are the most straightforward,
including on/off and PID controls. “On/Off controls”, also known as “bang-bang controls”
[1], are used when a setpoint temperature is specified. For example, Lin et al. [47], [48]
modeled and experimentally tested an underfloor PCM integrated with an electric heater.
The electric heater was turned on when indoor conditions were below 18°C, and off when
above 20°C.
However, PID and on/off-based controls are simplistic and cannot control complex HP-
TES architectures with several operating modes, especially during daily or hourly
operations. Thus, a higher level of controls is required to operate the appropriate mode
at the necessary period (i.e., peak hours for TES discharge, off-peak hours for recharging,
etc.), while maintaining indoor setpoint conditions for thermal comfort. This level of
control, supervisory control, can be categorized into several classes: simplified static
control strategies and advanced dynamic controls [15], [34], [49]. In simplified static
control strategies, the main control strategies were identified from the literature: (i) TES
priori, (ii) chiller priori, (iii) constant proportion (partial chiller & TES), and (iv) rule-based
controls. In TES priori systems, the ice/PCM tank is fully utilized during peak hours for
space cooling when the chiller is off, or it can be activated to meet any additional building
loads that the TES cannot cover. On the other hand, chiller priori controls correspond to
the chiller running at its nominal capacity, and the TES is used to cover any excess
building loads. In constant proportion (partial chiller/partial TES), the chiller and TES are
set to operate with a fraction of their capacities to meet the required building load (e.g.,
40% chiller capacity, 60% TES capacity). For each of these control techniques, the sizing
of the TES and chiller may differ based on the required building loads, recharge loads,
and the peak load ratios that both the TES and chiller must cover [34], [50], [51], [52].
                                                              Page 13 of 127

  Figure 7: Chiller-TES control strategies (left) TES prior, (middle and right) constant proportion
 controls [51]

Rule-based controls (RBCs) are classified as heuristic controls, where a set of rules is
hard-coded into the controller: “if Condition X occurs, then take Action Y”, depending on
surrounding conditions [15], [34], [53]. In fact, one may argue that the previous control
approaches (i.e., chiller priori, TES priori, and constant proportion) may be a subcategory
of RBCs [52]. Nonetheless, the development of RBCs requires prior knowledge of how
the system will perform under different conditions [26], [35].
Advanced control strategies include optimal controllers and model predictive controllers
(MPCs). MPCs are more sophisticated, as they are equipped with precise models to
forecast future behavior over the next time horizons. These prediction models are then
fed into an optimizer to determine the most suitable decision for the next time step. Such
controls typically involve an optimizer that uses an objective function to read inputs such
as ambient conditions, cost ratios, and plant system feedback, then determines how to
operate the system to meet the objective function within the second requirement of the
system's defined constraints. The objective function is a time-based function, and its
simplest form is represented in Eq.(1), which is a minimization cost function [35]. Other
forms of functions may exist, such as minimizing energy consumption [54] or maximizing
COP. This objective function varies based on the system's user requirements [34].

reduced-order models rather than in field tests, due to their complex applications.

Research Gaps
Based on the current literature review, the research gaps are as follows:
 • Dual-Mode HP-TES System Design Techniques
 o No standardized design methodology for designing HP-TES systems for
 residential and commercial building space conditioning
 o Limited performance assessment of HP-TES systems across different
 climate zones, mainly focusing on operating cost reductions without
 considering annual climatic conditions
 • HP-TES System Transient Modeling & Experimental Analysis
 o Most model-based approaches combine map-based or steady-state HP
 models coupled with TES transient models, highlighting TES transient
 behaviors only
 o Complete HP-TES transient modeling analysis was not found, but is crucial
 to identify how this system's performance would be impacted at startups,
 shutdowns, and mode switches
 o Complete HP-TES transient model validations tend to focus on single
 (cooling or heating) mode only, and solely on the TES system rather than
 the entire HP system
 o The literature lacks general testing guidelines for integrated HP-TES
 systems
 • HP-TES Prototyping and Field Testing
 o The literature lacks essential insights on how to size the TES system for
 field applications, such as handling corrosive PCMs with aluminum heat
 exchangers, and constraints on size and weight.
 o Practical HP-TES integration constraints, such as acceptable refrigerant
 charge increases and applicable suitable control strategies, are not
 discussed, while advanced control techniques are usually presented in
 simulation environments only.
 o The literature lacks demonstrations of dual-mode HP-TES using single
 room-temperature TES, specifically taking this concept from the design
 stage to the field test implementation stage
Consequently, this research sheds light on the design, integration, modeling, control,
experimentation, and field testing of a dual-mode single-room-temperature HP-TES. A
framework for HP-TES system design was presented, and a dedicated supervisory
controller was implemented.

 Project Objectives
Statement of Project Objectives
The objective of the project is to develop and validate an integrated 4-Ton (14.1 kW) heat
pump-thermal storage system that can operate in both cooling and heating modes,
capable of ≥50% peak demand reduction for 2 hours and ≥20% total energy efficiency
improvement for all modes with the thermal battery at a cost of ≤$15/kWhthermal.
The technology proposed herein consists of a dual-purpose (heating and cooling) thermal
battery with room temperature storage integrated with heat pump(s) (HPs) for commercial
applications. The battery serves as the heat reservoir, absorbing heat when operating as
a condenser in cooling mode or rejecting heat when operating as an evaporator in heating

 • Load Shifting – The thermal storage enables load shifting by leveraging the higher

System Feasibility Assessment (Milestone 1.1 – 1.2)
Original System Targets
The original system and load-shifting targets called for a 5-Ton (17.6 kW) HP system and
load shifting for four peak utility hours, directly integrated into the vapor-compression
cycle (Figure 8). The proposed technology consists of a dual-purpose (heating and
cooling) thermal battery with room temperature thermal energy storage integrated with
heat pump(s) (HPs) for residential and commercial applications. The battery serves as
the heat reservoir, absorbing heat when operating as a condenser in cooling mode or
rejecting heat when operating as an evaporator in heating mode. The main components
include one compressor, two heat exchangers (HX), multiple expansion devices, and one
thermal battery.

To achieve a 50% demand reduction for a base COP of 3.2 at AHRI A-test conditions,
quasi-steady state simulation results indicate that the TES battery UA requirements need
to be between 6000 and 9000 Btu/(hr-°F) (Figure 11). Balancing between heating and
cooling demand reductions of 50%, it was found that selecting the higher temperature
PCM (25°C) would increase heating demand reductions given the lower temperature lifts
(i.e., higher evaporating pressures & temperatures), and the lower temperature PCM
(18.5°C) in cooling mode, corresponding to lower discharge pressures and temperatures.
Consequently, the selection of the PCM temperature should balance both heating and
cooling, and a 22°C PCM temperature would provide a suitable demand reduction for the
design day in both modes.

HP-TES Operation
The dual-mode R410A HP-TES system, with a 4-Ton (14.1 kW) capacity investigated
herein, utilizes a single 22°C temperature salt hydrate PCM-TES [77]. In heating mode,
the PCM-TES operates as a high-temperature heat source and a low-temperature heat
sink in cooling mode, thereby reducing the system's temperature lift and improving its
performance during peak hours. The HP-TES system consists of three main sub-systems:
(a) a fixed-speed air-source R410A base HP (Figure 15(a)); (b) the HP-TES operating in
discharge mode, where the outdoor coil is disconnected (Figure 15(b)); and (c) the HP-
TES operating in recharging mode, where the indoor coil is disconnected (Figure 15(c)).
Each sub-system can operate in heating or cooling mode, depending on the building
space conditioning requirements. More information on the HP-TES transient modeling
can be found in Milestone 1.4.

TES Component Design and Manufacturing (Milestone 1.3 & 1.5)
Design Criteria
The thermal battery design was conducted considering the following criteria:
• Heat exchanger performance: the TES exchanges heat between water and the PCM
 material through a secondary loop. The required design target is 14-17 kW for 2 hours.
 The extra capacity includes the compressor power during cooling discharge mode.
• Weight: especially for packaged RTUs, weight is a constraint. Designing a lighter TES
 will be necessary; thus, reducing the weight of either the heat exchanger or the PCM
 should be considered.
• Material compatibility: Corrosion tests were conducted for uncoated and coated
 aluminum alloy specimens
• TES cost: aluminum off-the-shelf HXs were a good option to bring down the cost.
 Furthermore, off-the-shelf bins were used to reduce overall manufacturing costs,
 rather than having a custom-built TES tank.

TES PCM-HX Design & Packaging
To design the PCM-HX, a first-order assessment was conducted comparing several off-
the-shelf HX designs for PCM storage availability, compactness, and material utilization.
The PCM storage availability refers to the amount of available “free volume” where the
PCM can be placed. The remaining two metrics, compactness and material utilization,
are HX geometry metrics related to the HX envelope and material (tubes & fins) volumes,
respectively. The first-order assessment shows that the microchannel HXs had the
highest compactness and material utilization, making them an excellent design candidate
for PCM-HX TES [79].

 Figure 23: Comparison of PCM-HX geometries' compactness and material utilization [79]

However, the challenges associated with selecting “off-the-shelf-HXs” must be
addressed. Initially, header-and-serpentine microchannel HXs were the first candidates.
However, both candidates (Figure 24) present their own challenges. The issue with the
header MCHX is that its overall header and frame widths are 30-40% greater than the
                                       Page 29 of 127

microchannel tube width. This presents a challenge when placing multiple HXs together
to meet the required heat transfer surface area requirement, as the amount of PCM will
increase, raising the thermal battery weight to ~1200 kg. Furthermore, this additional PCM
will make only a minimal contribution to the thermal battery capacity and performance, as
it will be located far from the primary heat transfer surfaces. Therefore, this weight
increase defies the purpose of adjusting the thermal battery operating hours and capacity
targets to meet a pre-determined constraint that the thermal battery can be no more than
three times larger than the HP mass (~780 kg).

 Figure 24: Commercially available (a) header and (b) serpentine MCHXs [20]

On the other hand, commercially-available serpentine MCHXs are quite small, requiring
at least 500 units to meet the heat transfer surface area and PCM volume requirements.
This becomes a problem because the available inlet tubes are perpendicular to the
serpentine tube (Figure 24(b)), requiring excessive labor to cut, braze, and assemble the
thermal battery for the 500+ serpentine MCHXs.
Given these challenges, it was suggested that all-aluminum tube-fin coils be used. Rheem
provided two tube-fin HXs, with different fin densities, 13-FPI and 16-FPI. To meet the
required capacity of 17 kW (cooling mode) for 2 hours, the number of required HXs was
calculated. The results show that the 13-FPI tube-fin HX is more convenient, as only 32
are required, whereas 112 are needed for the 16-FPI HX to accommodate the required
PCM volume.

complete TES system comprises two bins, each composed of 16 HXs and one-half the
PCM volume. All HXs and bins are connected in parallel.

HP-TES System Operation
The HP-TES is designed to provide at least 4 Tons (14.1 kW) of space conditioning for at
least two continuous hours during utility peak hours in all US climate zones. To meet this
target, simulations were conducted using TMY3 [95] data and building loads from the
Spawn of EnergyPlusTM [60], [61] for a DOE prototype small-office building [75]. In these
simulations, the building “operating period” was 6 AM to 8 PM, during which continuous
space conditioning was required. Table 11 summarizes the utility peak hours for eight
representative U.S. cities, one per ASHRAE climate zone. Since the utility peak periods
extend beyond the TES 2-hour operating window, a subset of the utility peak hours was
selected for discharge operation, ensuring the cooling (heating) discharge operating
window occurs at the most extreme (highest in cooling, lowest in heating) ambient
temperatures, which is expected to result in the largest possible demand reduction. To
minimize the recharge energy requirement, the coolest (warmest) nighttime hours were
selected in cooling (heating) modes [37], [96]. Of the selected locations, two require
additional discussion: (i) Honolulu, HI, representing all-year-round cooling due to its
tropical climate, and (ii) Fairbanks, AK, a location without a daily utility peak period,
thereby providing the freedom to select the most suitable hours “thermodynamically”, i.e.,
based on ambient temperatures alone, to discharge and recharge the TES.

Hot, Warm, and Moderate Climate Zones (CZs 1 – 4)
 Riverside, CA (Climate Zone 3B)
Because this location is the closest to the field test location (Rancho Cucamonga, CA),
special focus will be placed on Riverside, CA (CZ 3B), followed by a discussion of special
cases in other climate zones. To assess the system potential in Riverside, CA, the
baseline HP and HP-TES simulated performances were compared at peak hours. Figure
36 shows the COP improvements for the HP-TES in cooling (discharge) mode compared
with baseline HP operating at different outdoor temperatures in July. As the HP-TES
disconnects the cooling cycle from the outdoor HX, the heat sink temperature drops,
considerably reducing the temperature and pressure lifts and increasing the COP. This is
most effective when the outdoor temperatures are significantly higher than PCM
temperatures, resulting in maximum savings in extreme ambient conditions. The profile
of the HP-TES curves in Figure 36 changes from a steep-to-gentle slope during melting
as the PCM transitions from sensible heating in the solidus state to two-phase melting.
Although the COP decreases from 6.5 to 4.5 as the PCM melts, this performance is still
50% higher than the baseline HP operation at an outdoor temperature of 35°C.

Another option is to use the thermal battery during the thermodynamic peak and combine

the single-speed pump in the secondary loop was replaced with a variable-speed pump

and a PI controller was used to control the pump speed (i.e., the heat transfer from the
plate heat exchanger to the thermal battery).

Comparing the single-speed and variable-speed pumps (Figure 43), the variable-speed
pump can have a greater effect at lower outdoor temperatures. As compressor power is
dominant, at lower outdoor temperatures, the compressor efficiency is highest, as lower
cycle temperatures are achieved and heat can be more easily rejected to a colder heat
sink. The variable-speed pump enhances the recharge by maximizing the water-side heat
transfer in the thermal battery. At higher outdoor temperatures, the variable-speed pump's
effects are minimal, as the compressor efficiency begins to degrade due to higher
temperature lifts required, and the outdoor heat exchanger's heat transfer effectiveness
decreases. In Honolulu, HI, nighttime temperatures range from 14 – 20°C, corresponding
to reductions in recharge energy of 10 – 33%. This shows there is potential to use this
system in Honolulu, HI, without creating a nighttime peak during recharge, keeping the
recharge energy close to the discharge energy. Nonetheless, additional mitigation
through further component improvements and optimal control strategies is required to
maximize cost savings and achieve some energy savings in this location.

HP-TES System Controls and Shakedown Tests
In HP-TES systems, there are two levels of controls: (i) the component level controls (i.e.,
PID-EXVs, blowers), and (ii) supervisory controls (rule-based, optimal control, model-
predictive controls). To tune the component controls, shakedown tests were conducted
to ensure stable operation of the HP-TES for the six operating modes listed:
 • Cooling HP operation (no TES)
 • Cooling HP-TES discharge
 • Cooling HP-TES charge
 • Heating HP operation (no TES)
 • Heating HP-TES discharge
 • Heating HP-TES charge
The control objective is to maintain the subcooling of the outdoor EXV (or brazed PHX
EXV for discharge modes) by controlling the opening degree of the corresponding EXV.

mode shake-down test.

The prototype was installed within a pair of controlled psychrometric chambers, with the

through code tester with humidity and pressure sensors in the ducts. ID and OD chamber
conditions were controlled and recorded throughout each experiment. Cooling or heating
capacity was calculated using the air-side enthalpy method, and total power was
measured by a power meter. Table 14 summarizes measurement uncertainties, and
calculated uncertainties of both COP and capacity are less than 5%.
The TES-VCS system can operate year-round, supporting space heating and cooling.
The six operating modes are illustrated in Figure 57: cooling normal (C-N), cooling TES
charge (C-Ch), cooling TES discharge (C-D), heating normal (H-N), heating TES charge
(H-Ch), and heating TES discharge (H-D). In each mode, two of the three heat
exchangers are active while the third remains idle. The two reversing valves determine
which heat exchangers are active and the refrigerant flow direction, allowing six distinct
                                               Page 57 of 127

operational configurations. Two check valves located upstream of the compressor
prevent backflow during mode switching, ensuring system stability. Since the PHX has a
smaller internal volume than the OD coil, refrigerant imbalance can occur, leading to
overcharging or undercharging of the active loop. To address this, the OD-EXV is
designed to regulate subcooling, using the OD coil as a refrigerant buffer. Table 2
summarizes the functional roles of each HX and EXV. The ID-EXV and PHX-EXV control
superheat and could be replaced with TXVs in practical systems. At the same time, the
OD-EXV operates under proportional-integral-derivative (PID) control of subcooling to
stabilize refrigerant charge during TES discharge modes. This coordinated EXV control
ensures smooth transitions and reliable operation across all six operating conditions.

The operating conditions considered in the laboratory assessment framework were
selected to best capture HP-TES system performance across a wide range of outdoor
temperatures in both heating and cooling operating modes. In this section, we provide
justifications for selecting each test point and explain why it is crucial for evaluating HP-
TES system performance.
Base HP Space Conditioning without TES. Two cases each for heating and cooling mode
(Tests #1 and #6) corresponded to the base HP providing space conditioning without
TES. These cases are of significant importance, given that the TES is sized for a finite
operating period (at least two continuous hours). Thus, the base HP mode will provide
space conditioning for a vast majority of the day (e.g., up to 83% (10 hours) of a 12-hour
working day). Therefore, the HP-TES system operating in steady-state base HP mode
should not have degraded performance compared to a traditional HP (i.e., without TES).
One “favorable” ambient condition (Test #1H = H1Full condition; Test #6C = FFull condition),
and one “unfavorable” ambient condition (Test #6H = H3Full condition; Test #1C = AFull
condition) are considered for base HP mode operating without TES.

                                                          Page 62 of 127

Continuous TES Discharge Mode. Two cases each for heating and cooling mode (Tests
#3 and #8) corresponded to continuous unit operation in TES discharge mode. The key
difference between the respective heating and cooling cases is the ambient temperature
(Test 3H = H1Full; Test 8H = H3Full; Test 3C = AFull; Test 8C = FFull), which is relevant for
estimating the heat losses from the TES and may impact the usable TES operating time.
Continuous TES Charge Mode. Similar to the continuous TES discharge mode discussed
above, Tests #2, #7, and #9 corresponded to continuous unit operation in TES charging
mode. The key difference again is the ambient temperature (Test 2H = H1Full; Test 7H =
H3Full; Test 9H = H2Full; Test 2C = AFull; Test 7C = FFull; Test 9C = BFull), where any TES
heat losses may impact the time (and energy input) required to fully recharge the TES
units for use during the next day.
Cyclic TES Discharge Mode. One case each for heating and cooling mode (Test #5)
corresponded to unit-on/off cycling, where the TES discharges for 15 minutes, followed
by 15 minutes with the unit off. This operating mode mimics an on/off cycling pattern for
a thermostatically controlled heat pump. It is thus one of the most critical tests of system
performance for real-world integration.
Continuous TES Charge Mode at Extreme Ambient Temperatures. One case each for
heating and cooling mode (Test #10) corresponded to continuous unit operation in TES
charging mode at extreme ambient temperatures, i.e., heating mode charging at cold-
climate heat pump conditions when winter-peaking utilities may most require demand
reduction (H4Full, DBT = 5°F/-15°C) and cooling mode charging at a high ambient
temperature (DBT = 105°F/40.6°C) that may occur during times of day when excess solar
power generation results in low-cost electricity. In both cases, the TES heat losses are
expected to significantly impact the time and energy required to fully recharge the TES
units.
Cyclic TES Charge Mode with Intermittent Base HP Mode Space Conditioning. One case
each for heating and cooling modes (Test #4) corresponded to a cyclic operation in which
the TES is charged for 15 minutes, followed by 15 minutes of base HP space conditioning.
This operating mode could occur when a thermostat calls for space conditioning occurs
during off-peak hours when TES charging is scheduled. As human comfort is the primary
objective of any heat pump system, such a test is critical to demonstrate that the HP-TES
can switch between passive TES charging and active space conditioning.
Intermittent TES Charge / Discharge Mode. The final test case (Test #11) corresponded
to a cyclic unit operation in which the unit was cycled between 15 minutes of TES charging
and 15 minutes of TES discharging for at least 2 hours, or until the TES was completely
discharged. Such a test allows for exploring the benefits of TES heat transfer from
repeated transient operation and may inform future control architectures to improve
overall HP-TES system performance.

 o Off-peak time = 9:00pm – 6:00am, TES recharging whenever the

 only; thermostat temperature setback to minimize space conditioning
 energy and increase likelihood of TES recharge

HP-TES Field Test: Expected Peak Demand Reduction
While the HP-TES field controller did not operate as required, base HP operation was
conducted and data recorded during Summer 2025, in Rancho Cucamonga, CA. Using
the validated HP-TES complete transient model from Milestone 2.2, base HP validations
were conducted, primarily focusing on power demand throughout a selected summer day
(July 2nd 2025). The transient model was simulated in base HP mode only, with all check
valves surrounding the plate heat exchanger closed. Results show excellent agreement
throughout the day in power demand, providing confidence in utilizing the experimentally
validated models for estimating the actual expected performance at the field location.

                                          Page 95 of 127

 Figure 94: HP-TES model validation using field test data

To demonstrate the HP-TES load-shifting capability, the validated model was used by
varying the required control inputs. On the component level, the check valves associated
with base HP, discharge, and recharge modes were opened and closed as necessary.
The compressor input for on and off was also time-based, using the field-test data power
requirements as the input. A time-based control was used for this simulated
demonstration, with recharge hours set to 2 – 5 AM and discharge hours to 4 – 6 PM. At
the beginning of the day, it is assumed that the PCM is fully discharged and has an initial
temperature of 28°C. Figure 95 shows that for the simulated day, the peak power demand
reduction ranges from 23% to 32%, with recharge power requirements 3 – 6% below the
peak-hour power requirement. To improve the performance of the HP-TES during peak
and recharge hours, it is important to consider reducing the hydronic loop pressure drops,
and selecting pumps that are optimally sized for that secondary loop.

 Figure 95: HP-TES simulated field test day

However, there are key differences between the simulated model and the system that
was installed at the field test location. Firstly, the model utilizes water as the secondary
hydronic loop heat transfer fluid, while the installed system used a 30% propylene glycol
solution. Glycol solutions tend to have slightly lower specific heat capacities and thermal
                                       Page 96 of 127

conductivities, requiring higher pumping power. Secondly, the simulated pump was based
on that which was utilized for the laboratory-scale testing at ORNL as opposed to the
field-tested installed pump, which could slightly alter the peak demand savings based on
actual pump power consumption. Finally, the long piping lengths between the ground
(TES location) and the roof (HP unit) were neglected in the HP-TES models, again
changing the required pumping power. Nonetheless, given that the designed pump power
ranged from 745 W to 800 W, the currently modelled pump power was tuned by adjusting
the pump efficiency to around 780 W. Thus, it is expected that the peak demand
reductions and recharge requirements will not change significantly.

HP-TES Modifications
The modified heat pump (HP) prototype was derived from a commercially available 4-ton
(14.1 kW) rooftop packaged unit to enable six operating modes that integrate a hydronic
thermal energy storage (TES) loop. The primary design modifications required to support
this multi-functional operation include hardware additions, refrigerant circuit
reconfiguration, and advanced control implementation. Key modifications and
components are listed as follows.
 • Dual reversing-valve configuration: ORNL developed a two-reversing-valve
 arrangement to enable smooth switching among normal, TES charge, and TES
 discharge modes in both heating and cooling seasons. This change allows the
 same compressors and heat exchangers to operate under multiple refrigerant flow
 paths.
 • Addition of a brazed plate heat exchanger (PHX): A compact refrigerant-to-water
 PHX was integrated in parallel with the original air coils to serve as either an
 evaporator or condenser during TES charge and discharge modes.
 • Hydronic loop and TES coupling: A closed-loop water-glycol circuit, pump, and
 associated piping were added to connect the PHX to the TES tank containing
 phase-change material (PCM at 22°C).
 • Electronic expansion valves (EXVs) and check valves: Three EXVs (ID-EXV, PHX-
 EXV, OD-EXV) can achieve dynamic refrigerant flow and charge control. The OD-
 EXV also functions as a subcooling-control device and refrigerant buffer
 management valve when the outdoor coil is inactive. Parallel check valves allow
 automatic bypass in each mode, simplifying operation.
 • Control hardware and software integration: ORNL developed and validated a
 subcooling-controlled EXV strategy and coordinated compressor and valve
 sequencing logic. The control boards interface with existing rooftop unit electronics
 and require only modest firmware adaptation for commercial manufacturing.

                                       Page 98 of 127

 • Manufacturing and assembly implications: Most mechanical changes, such as
 PHX addition, tubing adjustments, and control wiring, can be implemented on
 existing rooftop HP production lines with limited disruption. The additional costs,
 relative to the baseline 4-ton rooftop HP, are primarily due to the PHX, additional
 EXVs, and control board upgrades. Labor requirements mainly stem from hydronic
 loop installation and refrigerant circuit modifications.
Factors Affecting System Cost
To make the design practical and scalable in real manufacturing and market, beyond the
core technical modifications, other contributing factors include:
 • Standardization potential: The modular TES connection (two-port hydronic
 interface) allows the same modified rooftop HP chassis to serve multiple TES
 sizes, supporting product scalability.
 • Component supply chain: All new components (EXVs, PHX, valves, pump) are
 commercially available and already used in HVAC manufacturing, facilitating
 near-term integration.
 • Testing and quality assurance: ORNL’s prototype test data confirm that the
 modified configuration maintains system safety and reliability, ensuring
 compatibility with standard factory test procedures.
Overall, the manufacturing adjustments required for the TES-integrated rooftop HP are
modest, leveraging existing production capabilities while adding control flexibility and TES
connectivity. This makes large-scale commercialization feasible once performance and
cost optimization are validated through field demonstrations.
Operating Expenses
Operating costs that reflect annual and hourly energy consumption are primarily
influenced by dynamic utility rates and HP-TES performance during discharge and
recharge hours. The possible dynamic (non-flat) utility rates can be sub-categorized into
four categories: (1) static TOU pricing, (2) dynamic TOU (DP) pricing, (3) real-time pricing
(RTP), and (4) critical peak pricing (CPP). For static TOU, the price varies between two
flat rates during set peak utility hours. In contrast, DP TOU pricing is fixed, but the time of
higher pricing can vary daily depending on the end-user's power usage. RTP has various
non-static rates per hour depending on electricity costs, while CPP has mostly flat electric
rates, except for occasional high-price events [107]. In the US, the non-flat utility rate
programs mostly follow the static TOU pricing [108].

Demand reduction and recharge energy requirements vary by operating mode, set peak
utility time-of-use hours (TOU), and location. Using annual transient-system simulations
with the Spawn of EnergyPlus [109], [110], expected cost reductions were calculated for
several cost-pricing scenarios from Southern California Edison (SCE) [99]. The electricity
rates or tariffs offered to commercial customers typically include a demand component
(per kW of peak demand) and an energy component (per kWh of energy consumed),
depending on the customer's size. Customers with peak demand under a certain
threshold may be eligible for flat rates (energy costs only) like residential customers. Both
demand and energy components can vary by season: summer, winter, and shoulder
(spring/summer). In addition, some utilities, including those in California, now offer time-
of-use (TOU) energy rates that reflect the differences in generation costs throughout the
year. Moreover, an emerging class of utility rates now includes a real-time pricing (RTP)
component to reflect market dynamics.

Market Barriers
While the HP-TES system demonstrates strong technical performance and
manufacturing feasibility, several market barriers must be addressed to enable large-
scale commercialization and deployment across the U.S. building sector. These barriers
can be grouped into several primary categories: economic, technical, manufacturing, and
behavioral.
 • Economic barriers: Although the incremental hardware cost of $600–700 per unit
 is modest, many residential and small-commercial customers are susceptible to
 the first cost. Without visible incentives or rebates, the added expense may
 discourage early adoption. The payback period for HP-TES depends strongly on
 local utility tariff structures and load profiles. In regions with flat electricity rates,
 ROI may exceed 7-10 years unless dynamic pricing or demand-response
 programs are available.
 • Technical and control barriers: Current rooftop and split-system controllers are
 designed for simple on/off or thermostatic control. TES-integrated operation
 requires multi-mode coordination (normal, charge, discharge) based on ambient
 conditions and utility signals. Development of low-cost, high-fidelity smart
 controllers is therefore essential. Existing HVAC rating standards (e.g., AHRI
 210/240) do not yet include testing protocols for hybrid TES-HP systems, which
 complicates performance comparisons and certification.

                                        Page 107 of 127

 • Manufacturing and supply chain barriers: Prototype production at ORNL and
 Rheem indicates that incremental cost could be reduced by 30-40% with high-
 volume production. However, early-stage market volumes may remain too small
 to justify large-scale factory investments. TES modules containing PCM are
 relatively heavy and may require specialized handling during shipping and
 installation. Localized assembly or modular prefabrication could mitigate these
 costs.
 • Market awareness and adoption barriers: HVAC contractors will need training on
 system installation, TES connection, and commissioning procedures. Simplified
 plug-and-play designs with pre-charged hydronic loops can reduce installation
 barriers. Despite demonstrated reliability at ORNL’s test facilities, early adopters
 may remain cautious. Field demonstration data and warranty guarantees will be
 critical to establishing market confidence.
Incentives for TES Capital Costs (CapEx)
From a building owner’s perspective, the purchase of a TES system and its associated
equipment and controls are an added cost when compared to a non-storage system
alternate. The cost associated with addition of a TES system should be offset by reduction
in the size of cooling or heating equipment that would otherwise be required to meet an
application’s peak load directly. This is usually not the case; therefore, incentives need to
be provided to the building operator for installing such a system.
Incentives can come from federal and/or state tax credits and rebates. However, such
rebates tend to expire after a certain period of time. One large opportunity for such
incentive is from the utility that is providing electricity to the building. If such peak load
reduction in the building is coincidental with the utility peak load reduction, then it may
make sense for the utility to offer incentives.
From a utility perspective, meeting the marginal electric demand requires additional
capital investment for generation, transmission, and distribution in order to retain required
capacity margins. An alternative to the installation of more generation and T&D equipment
is the installation of a TES system at the point of electric use that reduces the peak
electrical demand; thereby, eliminating the need for electrical infrastructure. The use of a
thermal storage system for shifting the end-use demand for electricity from on-peak to
off-peak periods has two impacts – demand shifting not only reduces the peak demand
but it also improves the load factor for the utility equipment. Peak demand reduction
means that a given existing power grid (power plant, transmission lines, etc.) can serve
more end-use customers without having to build another more infrastructure to meet
future electricity demands that may appear due to growth.
Assuming persistent demand reduction for several TES systems in its service territory,
the utility could conceivably, fund a portion of the TES unit capital cost and still realize
savings over the alternative of added infrastructure. Usually, utilities develop
“workpapers” to determine the level of incentives that could be provided.

It is to be noted that utilities have several alternatives technologies available at their
disposal for system-wide peak load reductions. Some technologies that are gaining
attention are: 1) Front of the meter batteries – Li-ion and other technologies; these are
very large systems, designed for utility grade application. 2) Demand reduction for large,
controllable loads – data centers, EV charging fleets, buildings, etc.
Incentives for TES Operating Costs (OpEx)
From a building owner’s perspective, TES should be operated during high peak utility
tariff periods. As discussed earlier, utilities offer “time of use” tariffs, which consider high
prices at peak periods. As long as the “delta” between low price and high price is high,
TES offers a cost-effective solution. Operating costs for the building owner will come
down, and there is a financial advantage for the owner to install TES system.
Currently, in many parts of the U.S., the price differential between peak period and non-
peak period is not high enough on its own to encourage building owners to procure and
install small and dedicated TES systems. There is an opportunity in some US markets
(Ex: NYC, Southern California, Arizona, Florida) for ice storage systems in large buildings.
These primarily have ROI’s from peak demand reduction for large systems. New tariffs,
specifically designed for TES systems, need to be further developed in the country (Note:
Few tariffs were developed in the past). Rate making is a complicated process; Utilities
and Public Utility Commissions need to be convinced that such new tariffs will spur
adoption of TES technologies, and reduce peak demand across the country’s electrical
grid. Hopefully, demonstrations such as this project, will encourage new tariffs to be
developed and introduced into the market.
It is to be noted that TES technologies do not play a significant part in traditional “demand
response” programs. Such programs are designed to reduce peak demand (or shift load)
during specific periods of grid vulnerability. TES technologies are designed for permanent
peak load shift, and not during specific periods of the year.

 Significant Accomplishments and Conclusions
HP-TES Performance Assessment
  • System-level transient models of an HP-TES system with a hydronic loop were
 developed, and annual simulations were conducted for peak utility hour discharge
 and off-peak nocturnal recharge across representative cities in all eight US climate
 zones.
 o The primary performance metrics for assessing HP-TES performance
 across the different climate zones are demand reduction and recharge
 energy increase
 o Cycle temperature lift is the key indicator of system behaves; thus, it is
 essential to select location-appropriate PCM temperatures, depending on
 the ambient conditions.
 o Simulation results for warm climate zones (e.g., Riverside, CA) show that
 the peak cooling COP improvement was between 50%-100%,with greater
 improvements occurring at higher outdoor temperatures.
 o Tropical locations (i.e., Honolulu, HI) only required cooling mode operation
 and had lower annual demand reductions, given the moderate ambient
 temperatures during utility peak hours throughout the year.
 o In cold climate regions (i.e., Chicago, IL), the HP-TES heating mode energy
 savings were primarily due to eliminating the need for backup heating during
 peak hours.
 o Heating demand reductions were generally greater than cooling demand
 reductions, as backup heating was not required when using the PCM-TES
 as the heat source, rather than cold ambient air
  • Comprehensive assessment of the required design conditions to size HP-TES
 systems and other considerations needed to ensure sustainable operations
 o Using ASHRAE “1%” design conditions would lead to higher (20 – 30%)
 anticipated demand reductions compared to TMY3 weather data with hourly
 simulations.
 o It is vital to use ASHRAE design conditions along with location-specific
 weather data (e.g., TMY3, actual weather data), as they are more
 representative of peak hour conditions and thus yield more realistic annual
 demand reductions.
 o Selection of PCM temperatures should not be based only on maximizing
 demand reductions, but also minimizing recharge energy requirements to
 eliminate artificial nighttime demand peaks if all HP-TES systems are
 recharging at the same time on a mass adoption scale
HP-TES System Experimentation & Transient Model Validation
  • Prototyped and experimentally tested a dual-mode HP-TES setup at ORNL
 laboratories in controlled psychrometric chambers
 o Successfully assembled and controlled the HP-TES operation, both
 continuous and intermittent operations, demonstrating tested peak demand

 reductions between 10 – 40% in cooling mode, and 25 – 60% heating mode.
 The more extreme the ambient conditions, the higher the demand
 reductions.
 o Developed a novel experimental test matrix based on AHRI 210/240 test
 standard, considering a wide range of operating modes (i.e., cooling,
 heating, discharge, recharge, base-modes, cyclic vs. continuous

 o Base dual-mode HP-TES system demonstrated no degradation in system

• Conducted complete system-level transient simulation validations using

 o The models were able to predict the refrigerant and hydronic loop pressures
 and temperatures, boosting confidence in the annual simulations

 Path Forward
Multiple potential avenues for future work have resulted from this work. These are
summarized for all three research thrusts (field test studies, system optimization, and
considerations of market penetration) as follows.
Field Testing
 • Extensive field testing should be conducted, including:
 o Full annual testing across several climate zones, identifying potential
 system demand reductions and reductions in carbon dioxide emissions both
 seasonally and during the peak periods
 o Demand reduction and recharge requirements analysis for different nominal
 HP capacities and load shifting hours across different climate zones. This
 is because every location is unique; thus, different load-shifting
 opportunities exist at each location, e.g., building loads, peak period
 duration, ambient conditions, etc.
 o Exploration of additional PCM temperatures, targeting the most suitable
 PCM temperature in each climate zone
HP-TES System Optimizations
 • Improvement of HP-TES system performance by:
 o Optimizing the PCM-HX designs to meet required operating conditions,
 while minimizing TES weights, and electrical power draws.
 o Optimizing base HP cycle components to improve HX effectiveness and
 compressor efficiencies at HPTES-specific system pressure lifts
 o Utilize the current HP-TES transient models to target location-specific
 optimal control strategies, maximizing both energy and cost savings.
Considerations for Market Penetration
 • Assess the requirements for the transition of such a technology from a Technology
 Readiness Level 7 to 9 by:
 o Assessment of HP-TES with competing technologies, e.g., electrochemical
 storage, variable-speed HP systems, district heating/cooling plants, and
 how the HP-TES concept can be leveraged and/or combined with such
 technologies to achieve further demand reductions
 o Assess the challenges in PCM-HX design and manufacturing, namely TES
 size reduction, material compatibility, and corrosion resistance
 o Assess the HP-TES potential utilizing future low-GWP refrigerants by
 looking at a complete life-cycle assessment, refrigerant lower flammability
 limits, and safety considerations
 o Address the lack of community and public awareness of such technology
 and its potential
