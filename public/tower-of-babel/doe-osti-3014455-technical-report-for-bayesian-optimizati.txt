Executive Summary
Preserving and enhancing beam polarization in the hadron injector chain at Brookhaven National
Laboratory (BNL) is a critical requirement for the scientific success of the Relativistic Heavy Ion Collider
(RHIC) and the future Electron-Ion Collider (EIC) [1]. Beam polarization directly enters the experimental
figure of merit to high order, and even modest polarization losses during acceleration can significantly
reduce physics output [2]-[5]. Achieving optimal polarization performance is challenging due to the
complexity of the Linac-Booster-AGS accelerator chain, the high dimensionality of controllable parameters,
sparse and noisy diagnostics, and the reliance on manual, operator-intensive tuning procedures that are
difficult to maintain over time.
This project explored how physics-informed Bayesian learning and machine learning (ML)-based
optimization can augment traditional accelerator modeling and control strategies to improve polarization
preservation in BNL’s hadron injectors. Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute (RPI) contributed methodological
advances in Bayesian calibration, uncertainty quantification (UQ), digital twins, and ML-enabled
optimization, working in close collaboration with BNL, Cornell University, SLAC, and Thomas Jefferson
National Accelerator Facility (TJNAF).
A central contribution from RPI was the development and application of Bayesian calibration and
discrepancy modeling techniques to accelerator digital twins. High-fidelity physics models, while essential,
exhibit systematic mismatches with real machine behavior due to unmeasured or poorly characterized
effects such as magnet misalignments, transfer-function uncertainties, and instrumentation noise. By
treating these effects probabilistically, Bayesian inference was used to calibrate simulation models to beam-
based measurements and to quantify residual uncertainty. The resulting calibrated digital twins provide not
only improved predictive accuracy but also principled uncertainty estimates, which are essential for safe
and robust downstream optimization.
Building on these calibrated models, the project demonstrated ML-based optimization under uncertainty
for several polarization-critical processes. Bayesian optimization was applied to the Linac-to-Booster
injection problem, a notoriously complex and weakly instrumented process involving foil stripping, multi-
turn injection, scraping, and early acceleration. A model-independent Bayesian optimization strategy was
shown to efficiently tune corrector and quadrupole settings to maximize transmitted beam intensity after
scraping, effectively minimizing transverse emittance and improving downstream polarization preservation.
Importantly, this approach converged rapidly using limited experimental data, highlighting its suitability
for online accelerator operation [6].
In parallel, RPI co-developed and co-authored work on reinforcement learning (RL)-based optimization of
AGS RF bunch merging, a key step in reducing space-charge-driven emittance growth. Using a physics-
based simulator, RL agents were trained to control time-dependent RF voltages and phases to minimize
longitudinal emittance growth during bunch merging. These results demonstrated the feasibility of learning
stable, high-performance RF control policies that can adapt to drift and perturbations, complementing
Bayesian optimization approaches used elsewhere in the injector chain [7].
Collectively, these contributions advance the state of the art in AI-enabled accelerator control by tightly
integrating physical models, probabilistic inference, and data-efficient machine learning. The methods
developed in this project directly support improved polarization performance for RHIC operations and
provide a methodological foundation for the EIC, where even greater system complexity and performance

demands are anticipated. More broadly, this work serves as a proof of principle for how physics-informed
Bayesian learning and ML-enabled digital twins can be deployed in mature accelerator facilities to enhance
performance, robustness, and operational sustainability.
1. Project Background and Objectives
1.1 Scientific Context
The RHIC at BNL is the world’s only facility capable of delivering high-energy polarized proton beams,
enabling unique studies of the spin structure of nucleons and nuclei. Its successor, the EIC, will rely on the
same hadron injector chain and will place even more stringent demands on beam polarization. For
polarized-beam colliders, experimental performance is commonly quantified by a figure of merit that scales
as a high power of the beam polarization, often proportional to 𝑃! . As a result, relatively small polarization
losses during acceleration can lead to disproportionately large reductions in usable physics data, while
modest improvements in polarization can yield substantial gains in experimental sensitivity.
Preserving polarization through the Linac-Booster-AGS accelerator chain is technically challenging.
Polarization degradation arises from a combination of emittance growth, imperfect timing of fast control
elements, and depolarizing spin-orbit resonances that must be crossed during acceleration. Mitigating these
effects requires coordinated tuning of many accelerator components, including steering magnets,
quadrupoles, radiofrequency (RF) systems, tune-jump quadrupoles, and skew quadrupoles. The resulting
control problem is inherently high-dimensional, with strong nonlinear couplings between control
parameters and beam observables.
Compounding this complexity, accelerator diagnostics relevant to polarization optimization are often
sparse and noisy. Key quantities such as polarization itself are measured infrequently and with nontrivial
statistical and systematic uncertainties, while many intermediate observables are only indirectly related to
polarization loss. Furthermore, accelerator performance is subject to drift over time due to thermal effects,
hysteresis, power-supply variations, and slow changes in machine conditions. Consequently, optimization
strategies that rely solely on static models or one-time expert tuning are difficult to sustain in routine
operation. These challenges motivate the use of data-efficient, uncertainty-aware machine learning methods
that can integrate physical models with experimental data, adapt to changing conditions, and support robust
decision-making under uncertainty.
1.2 Project-Level Objectives
The overarching objective of this project is to achieve a 5% absolute increase in beam polarization at AGS
extraction energy, thereby significantly enhancing the physics reach of RHIC and establishing operational
methodologies relevant to the EIC. Achieving this goal requires coordinated progress across three tightly
coupled technical areas:
 1) Emittance reduction, particularly during injection into the Booster and AGS and during RF
 manipulations such as bunch splitting and merging, to mitigate emittance-dependent depolarization
 mechanisms.
 2) Improved timing accuracy of fast control elements, enabled by more precise and robust energy
 determination throughout the acceleration cycle.
 3) Suppression of depolarizing resonances, especially the horizontal intrinsic resonances in the AGS,
 through optimized use of skew quadrupoles and related control strategies.
A defining feature of the project is the emphasis on automation through machine learning, with explicit
treatment of measurement uncertainty and model limitations. Rather than replacing physical understanding,
the project integrates machine learning with established accelerator physics models to improve reliability,
efficiency, and long-term stability of polarization optimization.

3. Physics-Informed Bayesian Modeling Framework
A central contribution of this project is the development and application of a physics-informed Bayesian
modeling framework for accelerator digital twins, designed to support reliable optimization and control
under realistic uncertainty. Rather than treating accelerator simulations as deterministic predictors, this
framework explicitly accounts for model inadequacy, measurement noise, and unobserved system
parameters. The resulting probabilistic digital twins form the methodological foundation for the machine-
learning–based optimization strategies described in subsequent sections.
3.1 Bayesian Calibration of Accelerator Models
Accurate accelerator models are indispensable for beam optimization and control, yet long-standing
discrepancies between simulations and real machine behavior limit the effectiveness of purely model-based
approaches. In this project, Bayesian calibration was applied to Bmad-based accelerator models [8] of the
AGS Booster and associated injector components to systematically reconcile simulations with beam-based
measurements while preserving physical interpretability.
3.1.1 LtB line and its beam properties
For polarized proton operation, polarized H- beam is accelerated to 200 MeV in the Linac and transported
to the Booster through the Linac to Booster (LtB) transfer line. There are 13 quadrupoles and 16 correctors
in the LtB line used to optically match the incoming beam into the Booster ring . We constructed a physics
simulation of the LtB line in Bmad to study beam behavior at injection. To obtain the beam’s phase space
distribution, we performed quadrupole scans in the LtB line and the results showed discrepancies between
simulation and measurement, as shown in Fig. 1. Beam size variations observed at two multi-wires for the
same quadrupole return different emittance fitting results. We considered the following reasons for this
discrepancy: (a) dispersion, (b) x-y coupling, and (c) non-Gaussian beam distributions. Polarized H- beam
is created in a solenoid field of the OPPIS source, which produces transverse phase space coupling. This
has not been quantified by measurement yet. Obtaining this coupling by quadrupole scans requires either
tilted multi-wires or skew quadrupoles, which have not been installed in the LtB line.

3.1.2 Calibration framework
The starting point is a physics simulation model that maps controllable inputs (e.g., corrector settings) and
physical parameters to predicted beam observables. While such models encode first-principles accelerator
physics, they inevitably depend on parameters that are latent or imperfectly known, including mechanical
alignment errors, magnet field response characteristics, and other machine-specific effects. Rather than
fixing these quantities at nominal values, the Bayesian approach treats them as random variables with prior
distributions that reflect engineering knowledge and measurement uncertainty.
Given beam measurements, such as orbit responses to corrector kicks, the calibration problem is formulated
as a statistical inference task. The objective is to infer the posterior distribution of uncertain model
parameters conditioned on observed data, thereby updating prior beliefs in a principled way. This approach
replaces traditional point fitting with posterior inference, yielding a family of calibrated models consistent
with both physics and measurements.
3.1.3 Treatment of latent and nuisance parameters
Two classes of latent parameters were a particular focus of this work:
 1) Magnet misalignments, which introduce orbit distortions and coupling effects that are difficult to
 characterize directly through survey data alone. Misalignments were modeled as random
 perturbations about design positions, allowing their impact on orbit responses to be inferred
 statistically from beam measurements.
 2) Magnet transfer functions, which describe the relationship between power-supply settings and
 effective magnetic fields. While nominal transfer functions are derived from magnet
 characterization during construction, operational conditions and aging can lead to deviations that
 are not captured in static models. These deviations were parameterized and included as nuisance
 parameters in the calibration process.
By explicitly including these effects in the Bayesian model, calibration does not attempt to force agreement
by over-tuning a small number of parameters. Instead, it distributes discrepancies across physically
meaningful latent variables, reducing the risk of compensating errors and improving model robustness.
3.1.4 Discrepancy modeling and posterior inference
Even after accounting for latent parameters, residual differences between simulation and experiment remain
due to unmodeled physics, numerical approximations, and diagnostic limitations. To address this, a model-
discrepancy term was introduced, typically represented by a statistical surrogate such as a Gaussian process.
This term captures systematic deviations that cannot be eliminated through parameter calibration alone.
Posterior inference over both physical parameters and discrepancy terms yields a calibrated digital twin that
reproduces observed beam behavior within quantified uncertainty bounds. Importantly, the inference
process revealed that multiple parameter combinations can produce similar observable responses,

 5

highlighting the need to reason in terms of distributions rather than single “best-fit” values. This
probabilistic view is essential for downstream decision-making and distinguishes the Bayesian calibration
approach from deterministic fitting methods traditionally used in accelerator modeling.
3.2 Uncertainty Quantification
Beyond improving nominal model fidelity, the Bayesian framework provides a systematic approach to
uncertainty quantification (UQ), which is critical for safe and effective accelerator optimization. In this
project, UQ was not treated as an afterthought but as an integral component of the digital-twin methodology.
3.2.1 Posterior distributions and correlation structure
The outcome of Bayesian calibration is a joint posterior distribution over uncertain model parameters, rather
than independent point estimates. Analysis of these posteriors revealed strong correlations between certain
parameters, particularly among those associated with magnet transfer functions and alignment effects. Such
correlations indicate identifiability limits imposed by available measurements: different parameter
combinations can produce indistinguishable beam responses within measurement error. Recognizing these
limits is essential. Attempting to independently estimate highly correlated parameters can lead to unstable
or misleading results, whereas the Bayesian framework naturally encodes these dependencies in the
posterior covariance structure. This information can be used to guide future measurement campaigns,
prioritize diagnostic improvements, and constrain optimization algorithms to directions that are well
supported by data.
The comparison between measured and simulated orbit response data (Fig 2, left) shows small but
statistically significant discrepancies. We are conducting a systematic Bayesian uncertainty quantification
analysis, which infers a posterior probability distribution over the parameter space (Fig. 2, right), providing
a full characterization of the uncertainty in the Digital Twin parameters rather than a point estimate.

3.2.2 Implications for accelerator optimization and control
Uncertainty quantification plays a pivotal role in accelerator control decisions for several reasons. First, it
enables risk-aware optimization, in which control actions are evaluated not only by their expected
performance but also by their sensitivity to modeling uncertainty. Second, UQ helps prevent over-
confidence in calibrated models, reducing the likelihood that optimization algorithms exploit spurious
features arising from model error. Third, uncertainty estimates can be propagated through surrogate models
used in Bayesian optimization or reinforcement learning, ensuring that exploration and exploitation are
balanced appropriately under limited data.

In the context of the AGS Booster digital twin, UQ analysis showed that some sources of model discrepancy,
such as variations in power-supply transfer functions, cannot be uniquely identified from orbit response
data alone. This insight informed subsequent methodological choices, including the use of model
discrepancy terms and the emphasis on data-efficient learning strategies that remain robust in the presence
of irreducible uncertainty.
3.2.3 Role within the broader project
The Bayesian UQ framework developed at RPI underpins multiple downstream efforts in this project,
including ML-based injection optimization and RF control strategies. By providing calibrated models with
quantified uncertainty, it establishes a principled foundation for learning-based optimization methods that
interact safely with complex accelerator systems. More broadly, this work demonstrates how uncertainty-
aware digital twins can support the transition from expert-driven tuning to automated, ML-enabled
operation in existing and future DOE accelerator facilities.
4. ML-Based Optimization Contributions
Building on the calibrated, uncertainty-aware digital twins described in Section 3, this project investigated
ML-based optimization strategies for polarization-critical processes in the BNL hadron injector chain. Two
complementary classes of methods were explored: Bayesian optimization (BO) for low-dimensional,
weakly instrumented tuning problems, and reinforcement learning (RL) for sequential, time-dependent
control tasks. Together, these approaches address distinct but interconnected challenges in injector
optimization.
4.1 Bayesian Optimization of Booster Injection
Injection from the Linac into the Booster is a critical determinant of downstream beam quality and
polarization preservation. The process involves H⁻ stripping on a foil, multi-turn injection with repeated
foil traversal, intentional scraping to control emittance, and early acceleration. Due to the complexity of
these coupled effects and the limited availability of direct diagnostics, first principles modeling alone is
insufficient to reliably predict optimal settings.
To address this challenge, a model-independent Bayesian optimization framework was developed and
tested for tuning the Linac-to-Booster (LtB) transfer line and Booster injection parameters. Rather than
relying on a fully predictive simulation model, the BO approach treated the accelerator as a black-box
system, learning the relationship between selected control variables, such as corrector and quadrupole
strengths, and measurable performance metrics. The optimization objective was defined as maximizing
beam intensity downstream of the fixed scrapers following Booster injection and early acceleration.
Because the scrapers impose a hard aperture constraint, maximizing transmitted intensity is equivalent to
minimizing the effective transverse beam size and emittance at this stage. Reducing emittance at injection
is a key lever for improving polarization preservation through the AGS and RHIC, where emittance-
dependent depolarization mechanisms dominate.
Using a small number of carefully selected control variables, the BO algorithm, implemented with the Xopt
framework, demonstrated rapid convergence to optimal or near-optimal injection settings. Beam intensities
and beam sizes, monitored in the Booster to AGS (BtA) transfer line, at least as good as the established
optimum were quickly achieved, enabling us to automatically maintain optimal Booster cycles without
human intervention. The ability to converge with few evaluations highlights the suitability of Bayesian
optimization for online accelerator applications, where data acquisition is costly and operational constraints
limit exploratory scans.
4.2 Reinforcement Learning for AGS Bunch Merging
While Bayesian optimization is well-suited to static or low-dimensional tuning problems, other
polarization-critical processes involve time-dependent, sequential control that is difficult to express as a
static optimization problem. One such process is RF bunch merging in the AGS, which is used to combine

 7

multiple lower-intensity bunches into a single bunch with suitable intensity for transfer to RHIC. Bunch
merging requires coordinated control of RF cavity voltages and phases at multiple harmonic numbers over
time. Suboptimal settings lead to longitudinal emittance growth and intensity loss, which in turn exacerbate
depolarization in subsequent acceleration stages. Traditionally, this process is tuned manually by
experienced operators using heuristic interpretation of wall-current-monitor diagnostics, and performance
can drift over time.
To address this challenge, the project developed and evaluated a RL-based control strategy using a physics-
based AGS simulator. In this framework, an RL agent interacts with the simulated accelerator environment,
adjusting time-dependent RF voltage and phase ramps to optimize a reward function tied to longitudinal
emittance preservation and bunch quality. RL tends to be extremely data-intensive, often requiring a large
amount of measured data, which for particle accelerators can be prohibitive. However, the better the Digital
Twin of an accelerator, the lower the number of required measurements. In our test case of bunch merging
in the AGS, the Julia simulator is extremely accurate, because RF oscillations can be modeled very
accurately. The RL implementation, therefore, converged extremely quickly. This is shown in Fig. 4, where
the longitudinal bunch profile was intentionally made bad (top) and was quickly optimized to a much shorter
merged bunch (bottom). By learning policies rather than static parameter settings, the RL approach
naturally accommodates the sequential and dynamical nature of the control problem. The results provide a
proof of principle that RL can be used to stabilize and potentially automate complex RF gymnastics that
are directly relevant to polarization preservation.

4.3 Complementarity of BO and RL
The application of both Bayesian optimization and reinforcement learning within this project highlights
their complementary strengths for accelerator optimization: (1) BO is particularly effective for problems
with a small number of control variables, expensive evaluations, and weak or indirect diagnostics. Its data
efficiency and built-in uncertainty handling make it well-suited for online tuning tasks such as Booster
injection optimization. (2) RL excels in sequential, time-dependent control problems, where performance
depends on the evolution of system states over time. RL enables learning of control policies for processes
such as RF bunch merging, where static optimization is insufficient.
Experience from this project suggests that future ML-enabled accelerator control systems will benefit from
hybrid strategies, in which BO is used for coarse tuning and parameter selection, while RL is applied to
fine-grained, dynamical control. Importantly, both approaches benefit from the uncertainty-aware digital

 8

twins described in Section 3, which provide realistic environments for training, validation, and risk
assessment. Together, these ML-based optimization contributions demonstrate a pathway toward more
automated, robust, and sustainable control of polarization-critical accelerator processes, with direct
relevance to ongoing RHIC operations and future EIC readiness.
5. Outcomes and Technical Contributions
5.1 Scientific and Technical Outcomes
A major outcome of this work is the validation of a physics-informed Bayesian calibration pipeline for
accelerator digital twins. By combining Bmad-based physics models with Bayesian inference and explicit
discrepancy modeling, the project demonstrated that long-standing mismatches between simulation and
measured beam behavior can be addressed in a principled and interpretable manner. Rather than relying on
deterministic tuning or ad hoc parameter adjustments, the calibrated digital twins produce posterior
distributions over uncertain model parameters that are consistent with beam-based measurements. This
represents a shift from point-fit modeling toward probabilistic representations that better reflect the realities
of complex, aging accelerator systems.
Closely related to this is an improved understanding of uncertainty limits and identifiability in accelerator
modeling. Posterior analyses revealed strong correlations among certain latent parameters, particularly
those associated with magnet transfer functions and alignment effects. These correlations indicate intrinsic
limits on what can be inferred from available diagnostics alone. Recognizing these limits is itself a
significant technical contribution, as it informs both model usage and future diagnostic design, and prevents
overconfidence in inferred model parameters. The project thus established uncertainty quantification not
merely as a numerical add-on, but as a decision-relevant component of accelerator modeling.
On the optimization side, the project demonstrated ML-enabled injection optimization for the Linac-to-
Booster interface using BO. A model-independent BO framework was shown to efficiently tune corrector
and quadrupole settings to maximize beam intensity after scraping, which is equivalent to minimizing
transverse emittance at injection. The optimizer converged rapidly using a small number of control variables
and limited experimental evaluations, recovering beam quality comparable to expert-tuned configurations
even when starting from degraded conditions. This result confirms that data-efficient ML methods can
meaningfully assist or partially automate injector tuning tasks that are otherwise labor-intensive and
sensitive to drift.
In parallel, the project produced RL-based RF control strategies for AGS bunch merging. Using a physics-
based simulator, RL agents learned time-dependent RF voltage and phase ramps that minimize longitudinal
emittance growth during merging. These results demonstrate that RL can handle sequential, dynamical
control problems that are difficult to cast as static optimization tasks. Importantly, the learned policies were
robust to perturbations included in the simulator, highlighting the potential of RL for stabilizing complex
RF gymnastics relevant to polarization preservation.
Taken together, these outcomes establish a coherent methodological framework in which calibrated digital
twins, uncertainty quantification, Bayesian optimization, and reinforcement learning work in concert. The
results show that ML-based approaches can be both physically grounded and operationally relevant,
addressing concrete performance bottlenecks in the hadron injector chain.
5.2 Integration with BNL Operations
An important aspect of this project is that the developed methods were tested and evaluated in settings
closely aligned with real accelerator operations, rather than being confined to abstract or idealized studies.
BO of Booster injection was applied using realistic control variables, diagnostics, and operational
constraints, demonstrating feasibility for online or near-online use. Similarly, the digital-twin calibration
and uncertainty analyses were grounded in beam-based measurements collected under routine operating
conditions, ensuring that inferred uncertainties reflect actual machine behavior. The RL studies for AGS

system, providing a credible pathway toward experimental deployment. While full operational integration

Beyond immediate demonstrations, the outcomes of this project have provided direct inputs to ongoing
BNL and EIC-Beam AI efforts. The calibrated digital twins, uncertainty-aware modeling practices, and ML
optimization strategies developed here inform current work on AI-ready accelerator infrastructure, digital
twin deployment, and autonomous tuning for both RHIC operations and EIC preparation. The project’s
results have been disseminated through IPAC conference papers, quarterly progress reports, and regular
technical coordination meetings, ensuring that lessons learned are shared across institutional boundaries. In
summary, the project delivered validated methods, demonstrated performance improvements, and
actionable insights that extend beyond the specific use cases studied. These contributions support a
transition toward more automated, uncertainty-aware accelerator operation and provide a foundation for
future ML-enabled control strategies at DOE nuclear physics facilities.

preparing for next-generation accelerator systems. At the operational level, the project addresses critical
challenges in RHIC injector performance, where beam polarization is a dominant factor in experimental
reach. By developing and validating physics-informed, uncertainty-aware modeling and optimization
methods, the work supports improved preservation of polarization through the Linac-Booster-AGS chain.
The demonstrated capability to calibrate accelerator models to beam-based measurements, quantify
uncertainty, and apply data-efficient machine learning for injector tuning directly informs strategies for
maintaining high polarization during RHIC operations, reducing sensitivity to drift and decreasing reliance
on manual, expert-only tuning procedures.
Beyond near-term RHIC impact, the project establishes a methodological foundation for the Electron–Ion
Collider (EIC). The EIC will reuse the hadron injector chain while operating at unprecedented levels of
complexity and performance. Manual tuning approaches that have historically been effective at RHIC will
be increasingly difficult to scale to EIC requirements. The physics-informed Bayesian learning framework,
ML-enabled digital twins, and hybrid optimization strategies developed in this project provide a blueprint
for automated, robust, and interpretable control of EIC injectors. In particular, explicit treatment of
uncertainty and model discrepancy aligns with the need for safe and reliable optimization in a facility that
will operate continuously at high performance.
More broadly, this work contributes to DOE Nuclear Physics goals by advancing the concept of AI-ready
accelerators. The project demonstrates how digital twins calibrated with uncertainty, combined with
Bayesian optimization and reinforcement learning, can be integrated with existing accelerator infrastructure.
These capabilities are not limited to polarization optimization but are applicable to a wide range of
accelerator tuning, commissioning, and control tasks across DOE user facilities. As such, the outcomes of
this project support a long-term transition toward intelligent, adaptive accelerator systems that enhance
scientific productivity, operational resilience, and workforce efficiency. In summary, the project delivers
both immediate operational benefits for RHIC and durable methodological advances that align with DOE
Nuclear Physics priorities for the EIC and future accelerator-based research programs.
9. Conclusions and Lessons Learned
This project demonstrated that physics-informed Bayesian learning combined with modern machine-
learning techniques provides a powerful and practical framework for addressing complex optimization
problems in accelerator systems. Integrating physical models with probabilistic inference enabled the
development of calibrated digital twins that more accurately represent real machine behavior than
deterministic simulations alone. When coupled with data-efficient optimization methods such as Bayesian
optimization and reinforcement learning, these models supported meaningful improvements in injector
tuning strategies relevant to polarization preservation.

 12

Several aspects of the approach proved particularly effective. Bayesian calibration and uncertainty
quantification allowed model discrepancies and measurement noise to be treated explicitly, reducing
overconfidence in inferred parameters and improving the robustness of downstream optimization. Bayesian
optimization was shown to be well suited for low-dimensional tuning problems with limited diagnostics,
while reinforcement learning provided a viable pathway for sequential, time-dependent control tasks such
as RF bunch merging. Together, these methods reduced reliance on manual, operator-intensive tuning and
demonstrated a scalable approach to maintaining performance in the presence of drift and operational
variability.
At the same time, the project highlighted important challenges that remain. Chief among these are
identifiability limits imposed by existing diagnostics: certain latent parameters cannot be uniquely inferred
from available measurements, leading to strong correlations in posterior distributions. Instrumentation
constraints and measurement sparsity continue to bound the fidelity of digital twins and the confidence with
which optimization decisions can be made. These findings underscore the need to jointly consider advances
in diagnostics, experimental design, and learning algorithms, rather than relying on algorithmic
sophistication alone.
The lessons learned from this work have direct implications for future DOE accelerator facilities, including
the EIC. As accelerators grow in scale and complexity, reliance on expert-only tuning will become
increasingly unsustainable. The methodologies developed here provide a transferable blueprint for AI-
enabled accelerator operation that emphasizes physical interpretability, uncertainty awareness, and data
efficiency. Continued investment in digital twins, AI-ready infrastructure, and workforce training will be
essential to realizing the full potential of these approaches across DOE Nuclear Physics user facilities.
