Sampling from multi-modal distributions on Riemannian manifolds with training-free stochastic interpolants
Alain Durmus, Maxence Noble, Thibaut Pellerin

Abstract
In this paper, we propose a general methodology for sampling from un-normalized densities defined on Riemannian manifolds, with a particular focus on multi-modal targets that remain challenging for existing sampling methods. Inspired by the framework of diffusion models developed for generative modeling, we introduce a sampling algorithm based on the simulation of a non-equilibrium deterministic dynamics that transports an easy-to-sample noise distribution toward the target. At the marginal level, the induced density path follows a prescribed stochastic interpolant between the noise and target distributions, specifically constructed to respect the underlying Riemannian geometry. In contrast to related generative modeling approaches that rely on machine learning, our method is entirely training-free. It instead builds on iterative posterior sampling procedures using only standard Monte Carlo techniques, thereby extending recent diffusion-based sampling methodologies beyond the Euclidean setting. We complement our approach with a rigorous theoretical analysis and demonstrate its effectiveness on a range of multi-modal sampling problems, including high-dimensional and heavy-tailed examples.

Introduction

In this paper, we study the problem of sampling from a
target
probability distribution supported on a state space
\mathsf{M}
, assuming that its density is available up to a normalization constant. This problem can be viewed as the counterpart of generative modeling: while the objective in both cases is to generate samples from the target, generative modeling typically assumes access to a finite dataset of available samples.

While many classical examples focus on discrete state spaces or Euclidean domains, sampling problems defined on Riemannian manifolds arise frequently. A canonical example is when
\mathsf{M}
is the hypersphere of
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
for some dimension
d
, denoted by
\mathsf{S}^{d}
, which appears naturally in applications such as imaging
[
38
]
or constrained probabilistic models
[
31
]
. Somewhat counterintuitively, sampling on
\mathsf{S}^{d}
may even be preferable to sampling directly on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
: for instance, when the target has heavy tails or a large effective support, mapping the distribution to the sphere via stereographic projection can provide substantial practical benefits
[
50
]
. Further examples include Bayesian inference problems in which parameters inherently lie on non-Euclidean manifolds
[
21
,
42
]
, as well as settings where
\mathsf{M}
is defined through constraints that endow it with a Riemannian structure, such as polytopes
[
40
]
.

In Euclidean spaces, Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods constitute the dominant class of sampling algorithms, and many of them admit principled extensions to Riemannian manifolds; see, for example,
[
11
,
15
]
. Under suitable assumptions on the target distribution, most notably log-concavity, strong theoretical guarantees can be established, including non-asymptotic convergence rates
[
17
,
18
]
. Nonetheless, the practical performance of these methods often deteriorates when the target is complex. A well-known difficulty arises for multi-modal targets, whose probability mass concentrates around several isolated regions, commonly referred to as “modes”. This issue is further exacerbated in high-dimensional settings and affects both Euclidean and Riemannian sampling schemes.

In contrast, the challenge of multi-modality has been largely mitigated in generative modeling through the widespread success of score-based diffusion models
[
46
]
. These models simulate a non-equilibrium
stochastic
dynamics that gradually transforms a simple noise distribution, e.g., Gaussian, into the data distribution, thereby naturally accommodating highly multi-modal structures. The evolution of this dynamics is designed to follow, at the marginal level, a path of intermediate distributions obtained by applying progressively decreasing Gaussian convolutions to the target. A central ingredient of these models is the score function, namely the gradient of the logarithm of the intermediate densities, which is generally intractable and therefore learned from data using machine learning techniques. Closely related to diffusion models, flow matching methods
[
35
]
propose an alternative formulation based on
deterministic
dynamics that similarly transport the noise distribution to the target. Empirically, such deterministic flows are often less sensitive to time discretization errors than their stochastic counterparts. Both diffusion and flow matching models rely on score-based quantities and can in fact be unified within the framework of stochastic interpolants
[
2
]
, which provides a principled way to navigate between stochastic and deterministic dynamics that share the same prescribed marginal path. In the paper, we adopt this unifying perspective and terminology.

The remarkable empirical performance of diffusion models in Euclidean generative modeling has recently motivated their adaptation to sampling problems; see, for instance,
[
24
,
29
,
22
]
. In this context, the absence of data samples makes score estimation the central challenge. Existing approaches typically adapt classical MCMC techniques to estimate these scores on the fly,
i.e.
, during the simulation of the dynamics, yielding encouraging practical results. In parallel, diffusion-based models on Riemannian manifolds have been the subject of early and extensive investigation
[
14
,
27
]
. More recently, the most efficient approaches have relied on flow matching, notably the work of
[
10
]
, which leverages tractable geodesics to construct geometry-aware dynamics while avoiding the direct simulation of manifold-valued diffusion processes. A natural question is whether such method can be adapted to the sampling setting. Despite its appeal, this question has not been addressed so far, and closing this gap is the main objective of the present work. Our main contributions are summarized as follows:

•

Inspired by
[
10
]
, we develop a rigorous stochastic interpolant framework on a broad class of Riemannian manifolds to construct non-equilibrium dynamics transporting a noise distribution to the target. Tailored to the sampling setting, we establish theoretical results that justify the approach and provide explicit expressions for its core components.

•

Building on ideas from diffusion-based sampling
[
22
]
, we introduce a practical methodology to simulate the resulting deterministic Riemannian dynamics. The approach relies on estimating the vector field of the dynamics on the fly, expressed as conditional expectations, using standard MCMC tools. These tools are employed in a setting where they are significantly more effective than when directly used to sample from the target. We refer to the resulting method as
Flow-based Riemannian Iterative Posterior Sampling
(FRIPS). We further detail the FRIPS algorithm for two representative manifolds, namely
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and the
d
-sphere
\mathsf{S}^{d}
, for which we derive efficient simplifications.

•

Finally, we demonstrate the effectiveness of FRIPS on a range of controlled Riemannian sampling problems, where pronounced multi-modality and high dimensionality present substantial challenges. Our results show that FRIPS consistently outperforms existing Riemannian sampling methods under comparable computational budgets, highlighting its potential for real-world applications.

Riemmanian stochastic interpolant and its Markov projection

In this section, we provide assumptions on the state space
where the measure we wish to sample from is defined. We also introduce the theoretical foundations underlying our sampler and in particular the Riemmanian Markovian projection. More precisely, we begin with a brief overview of Riemannian geometry to formalize our setting. We denote the

\nabla_{\dot{\upgamma}}\dot{\upgamma}=0

. In charts, this corresponds to a second order Ordinary Differential Equation (ODE). Any such path is called a

For any

, there exists a

,

between

For any

.

.

Any function

. If not specified otherwise, any vector field will be assumed smooth.
We write
\mathrm{div}(\mathbf{u})

of a continuously differentiable vector field

A
.

Geodesic curves induce the

on
\mathsf{M}
, defined for
x\in\mathsf{M}
and

\mathrm{Exp}_{x}(v)=\upgamma_{x,v}(1)
, where
\upgamma_{x,v}
is the geodesic with initial value
\upgamma_{x,v}(0)=x
and velocity
\dot{\upgamma}_{x,v}(0)=v
. Given

, the map

onto
\mathsf{M}\setminus\mathrm{Cut}(x)

\mathrm{ID}(x)=\{v\in T_{x}\mathsf{M}\,:\,\|v\|_{x}<\mathbf{c}_{x}(v/\|v\|_{x})\}\;,

(4)

)

[

Consider

\mathsf{M}

\mathbf{C}
1)

The vector field
\mathbf{v}
is continuous with respect to

\mathbf{C}
2)

It holds

3
FRIPS methodology

Relying on the theoretical concepts elaborated in
Section

2
, we present a generic sampling algorithm that outputs approximate samples from a target distribution
\pi_{1}
of the form (
3
), by simulating the Markovian process
(X^{\mathrm{M}}_{t})_{t\in\left[0,1\right]}
described in (
15
). Since the velocity field
\mathbf{u}
driving the underlying ODE is not tractable, we propose to adopt a training-free strategy previously used in Euclidean space
[
28
,
24
,
48
,
22
]
: we estimate
\mathbf{u}_{t}(X_{t}^{\mathrm{M}})

on-the-fly
by (i) sampling from the intermediate denoising posterior distributions evoked in
Section

2.4
and (ii) computing an empirical approximation of the expectation in (
17
). We coin this methodology as
Flow-based Riemannian Iterative Posterior Sampling
(FRIPS). We specify its main generic features in
Section

3.1
, before describing versions that can be implemented in practice in
Section

3.2
. For the rest of the paper, we assume that
\mathsf{M}
satisfies
1
(illustrated by the running example of the sphere), that
\pi_{0,1}
is the independent coupling and
\pi_{0},\pi_{1}
are absolutely continuous with respect to
\rho_{\mathbf{g}}
, thus satisfying
2
. We assume that the
\mathrm{Exp}
and
\mathrm{Log}
maps are available in closed-form, as well as the Jacobian factor (
26
).

3.1
Generic version of FRIPS

In principle, one can get a sample from
\pi_{1}
by following the probability flow ODE (
15
), starting from a sample
x_{0}\sim\pi_{0}
. Unfortunately, even if the vector field
\mathbf{u}
was perfectly known, integrating exactly this ODE is not feasible; henceforth, we need to rely on some discretization schemes. For ease of presentation, we restrict our methodology to the use of the Euler scheme, but generalization to the Exponential Integration scheme
[
16
]
or higher-order methods could also be applied; see e.g.
[
39
,
13
]
regarding numerical integration on manifolds. Given a sequence of
K\geqslant 1
increasing timesteps
0\leqslant t_{0}\leqslant\cdots\leqslant t_{K}\leqslant 1
, with
t_{K}
being close to
1
, applying the Euler scheme to the ODE (
15
) amounts to define the sequence of iterates
\{X_{k}\}_{k=0}^{K}
with
X_{0}\sim\pi_{t_{0}}
and for any
k\in\{0,...,K-1\}

X_{k+1}=\mathrm{Exp}_{X_{k}}(h_{k+1}\mathbf{u}_{t_{k}}(X_{k}))\;,

(27)

where
h_{k+1}=t_{k+1}-t_{k}

Estimation of the velocity field

Consider
t\in\left[0,1\right)
and
x_{t}\in\mathsf{M}
.
In generative modeling applications, the vector field
\mathbf{u}_{t}
is learned by a neural network as the solution of a regression problem, by leveraging available samples from
\pi_{1}
, thus enabling a straightforward estimation of
\mathbf{u}_{t}(x_{t})
. In contrast, here we do not suppose to have access to samples from
\pi_{1}
but only to its un-normalized density
q_{1}
, see (
3
). Nonetheless, by remarking that
\mathbf{u}_{t}(x_{t})
is the integral of the tractable function
x_{1}\mapsto\mathrm{Log}_{x_{t}}x_{1}/(1-t)
against the measure
\pi_{1|t}(\mathrm{d}x_{1}|x_{t})
, whose density
p_{1|t}(\cdot|x_{t})
is known up to a normalization constant by (
19
), we can estimate it by using Monte-Carlo methods similarly to previous Euclidean diffusion-based sampling methods
[
28
,
24
,
48
,
22
]
. We denote by
\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{t}(x_{t})
the obtained estimation of
\mathbf{u}_{t}(x_{t})
, of the form

\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{t}(x_{t})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}w^{i}\frac{\mathrm{Log}_{x_{t}}X_{1}^{i}}{1-t}

(28)

where
\{X^{i}_{1}\}_{i=1}^{N}
are
N\geqslant 1
particles with corresponding normalized weights
\{w^{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{N}w^{i}\updelta_{X^{i}_{1}}\approx\pi_{1|t}(\cdot|x_{t})
. We refer to
Algorithm

2
for the generic version of this algorithmic step, and to
Section

3.2
for its concrete implementation via several Monte Carlo methods.

Why carefully choosing
t_{0}
matters

Overall, FRIPS methodology amounts to replace the original sampling problem targeting
\pi_{1}
(potentially multi-modal) with a sequence of
K
sampling sub-problems, associated to the distributions
\{\pi_{1|t_{k}}(\cdot|X_{k})\}_{k=0}^{K-1}
, where
X_{k}
corresponds to the
k
-th iterate of the sequence defined in (
27
), which are expected to be easier than the original one. Regarding their complexity, we can make the following key observations:

•

In the case where
t_{0}=0
(as done in generative modeling), getting a sample
X_{0}
from
\pi_{t_{0}}=\pi_{0}
does not pose any difficulty. However, the very first step of the main loop in
Algorithm

1
requires to be able to sample from the posterior
\pi_{1|0}(\cdot|X_{0})
, which is strictly equal to the target
\pi_{1}
by choice of the independent coupling
\pi_{0,1}
. In particular, this first sub-problem is as difficult as the original one.

•

On the other hand, we note that
\pi_{1|t}(\cdot|x_{t})
converges to the Dirac measure at
x_{t}
as
t
is closer to
1
. Thus, we expect
\pi_{1|t}(\cdot|x_{t})
to resemble to a unimodal distribution centered around
x_{t}
for
t
close to
1
, a scenario for which existing Monte Carlo methods can be efficient. Hence, setting
t_{0}>0
close enough to
1
is a guarantee that the simplicity of the subsequent sampling sub-problems is thus appealing. Nevertheless, if
t_{0}
is too close from 1, sampling accurately from
\pi_{t_{0}}
to get the initial state
X_{0}
is challenging, as
\pi_{t_{0}}
resembles more and more to
\pi_{1}
.

At the light of this discussion, the starting time
t_{0}
in
[0,1)
should be carefully chosen such that estimating the velocity field
\mathbf{u}
and computing the initial state
X_{0}
are both of relative difficulty. In practical applications, the choice of
t_{0}
can significantly impact the overall performance of FRIPS. We discuss heuristic approaches to tune
t_{0}
in
Section

4.1

without access to target samples
, by leveraging supplementary information on the target distribution
\pi_{1}
. For now, we assume that
t_{0}
is arbitrarily fixed and explain below how to sample the initial state.

Computation of the initial state

To sample the initial state
X_{0}
distributed according to
\pi_{t_{0}}
, we may adopt the Langevin-based initialization strategy from
[
22
,
28
]
. In Euclidean spaces, this approach aims at running the
Unadjusted Langevin Algorithm
(ULA)
[
43
]
on
\pi_{t_{0}}
by leveraging the expression of its score
\mathrm{grad}\log p_{t_{0}}
(as such, intractable) given by the Tweedie’s formula

\mathrm{grad}\log p_{t_{0}}(x_{t_{0}})=\int_{\mathsf{M}}\mathrm{grad}\log p_{t_{0}|1}(x_{t_{0}}|x_{1})\pi_{1|t_{0}}(\mathrm{d}x_{1}|x_{t_{0}})\;,

(29)

where the conditional score
\mathrm{grad}\log p_{t_{0}|1}(x_{t_{0}}|x_{1})
(with respect to the first variable
x_{t_{0}}
) is tractable, computed from (
30
). In our Riemannian setting, for any
x_{t_{0}}\in\mathsf{M}
, we can estimate
\mathrm{grad}\log p_{t_{0}}(x_{t_{0}})
in the exact same fashion as
\mathbf{u}_{t_{0}}(x_{t_{0}})
(see
Algorithm

2
), using Monte Carlo approximation of the posterior distribution
\pi_{1|t_{0}}(\cdot|x_{t_{0}})
. The generic version of this estimation is summarized in
Algorithm

3
, while the full Langevin-based initialization step, relying on the Riemannian version of ULA
[
11
,
19
]
denoted by RLA, is presented in
Algorithm

4
.

To be theoretically well grounded, this Langevin-based strategy requires sufficient regularity of the terms in (
29
) to justify exchanging the Riemannian gradient and the integral. However, this condition may fail to hold in some situations; see
Section

5.3
for an illustrative example. In such cases, we instead propose a zero-th order analog of this initialization strategy. Observing that
p_{t_{0}}(x_{t_{0}})
can be interpreted as the normalizing constant of the posterior distribution
x_{1}\mapsto q_{1}(x_{1})p_{1|t_{0}}(x_{1}|x_{t_{0}})
for a fixed state
x_{t_{0}}
, we resort to an instance of
pseudo-marginal MCMC

[
5
]
to sample from
\pi_{t_{0}}
. Specifically, we instantiate a Metropolis-Hastings (MH) scheme based on a prescribed proposal Markov kernel
\mathcal{Q}
, in which each evaluation of
p_{t_{0}}
is replaced by an unbiased Importance Sampling estimator, using the technique described in paragraph (c) in
Section

3.2
. For compact manifolds, with
\pi_{0}
the uniform distribution, a natural choice is
\mathcal{Q}=\pi_{0}
, which yields an Independent MH (IMH) algorithm summarized in
Algorithm

5
.

3.2
Posterior sampling with Monte Carlo methods

A key aspect of FRIPS algorithm is the ability to efficiently and accurately sample from the posterior distributions
\{\pi_{1|t_{k}}(\cdot|X_{k})\}_{k=0}^{K-1}
to perform velocity field estimation (
Algorithm

2
), and if needed score estimation (
Algorithm

3
). By taking inspiration from previous diffusion-based sampling methods in Euclidean space
[
22
,
24
,
28
,
48
]
, we describe below three Riemannian-based Monte Carlo methods that solve this task: (a) Markov Chain simulation, (b) rejection sampling and (c) importance sampling - each one being a version of the generic step called
PosteriorSampling
used in
Algorithms

2
and
3
. In the rest of this section, we consider an arbitrary posterior
\pi_{1|t}(\cdot|x_{t})
, where
t\in[0,1)
and
x_{t}\in\mathsf{M}
, which we aim to sample from. Based on the result of
Proposition

4
, we recall that this distribution admits a density
x_{1}\mapsto p_{1|t}(x_{1}|x_{t})
satisfying

p_{1|t}(x_{1}|x_{t})\propto p_{0|1}(\psi_{t}^{-1}(x_{t};x_{1})|x_{1})\operatorname{Jac}_{t}(x_{t};x_{1})\mathbbm{1}_{\mathsf{O}_{t}(x_{1})}(x_{t})q_{1}(x_{1})\;.

(30)

(a) Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC)

As widely done in general sampling tasks, we first propose to get samples from
\pi_{1|t}(\cdot|x_{t})
by building a Markov chain whose invariant distribution is precisely
\pi_{1|t}(\cdot|x_{t})
, as presented by
[
22
]
. Depending on the regularity of the target distribution
\pi_{1}
, one may either use (i)
zeroth-order
schemes, that only involve the density
q_{1}
(for instance geodesic slice sampling
[
15
]
), or (ii)
first-order
approaches, such as RLA, assuming the existence and access to the score
\mathrm{grad}\log q_{1}
on
\mathsf{M}
. In the experiments of
Section

5
, we employ a Riemannian analogue of the Metropolis-Adjusted Langevin Algorithm (MALA)
[
43
]
, which can be viewed as an extension to general Riemannian manifolds of
[
20
]
. The RLA proposal is followed by a Metropolis-Hasting correction step, relying on the posterior density (
30
) and the (typically approximate) proposal density
f
given in (
63
), see
Section

B.1
.

While MCMC methods are proven to have a large mixing time when targeting highly multi-modal distributions (see e.g.
[
37
]
), we expect to use them in a more favorable setting, by targeting posterior distributions that are less complex than the target distribution
\pi_{1}
and increasingly simpler (
i.e.
, more unimodal) as
t
increases. This can be ensured by carefully setting
t_{0}
as discussed in the previous section. In particular, if
t_{0}
is not well tuned, the intermediate MCMC runs could have a prohibitive mixing time, thus producing biased posterior samples under a limited computational budget. In this case, errors could accumulate at each global step
k
of
Algorithm

1
.

One key advantage of using MCMC methods for posterior sampling is the possibility of wisely warm-starting Markov chains from one global step of
Algorithm

1
to another. Indeed, for a given
k\in\{0,K-1\}
, the posterior distributions
\pi_{1|t_{k}}(\cdot|X_{k})
and
\pi_{1|t_{k+1}}(\cdot|X_{{k+1}})
are likely to be close to each another if
K
is set large enough. Hence, using the last state of the Markov chains built at time
t_{k}
as initialization of the chains built at time
t_{k+1}
(granted this last state lies in the support of
\pi_{1|t_{k+1}}(\cdot|X_{{k+1}})
, which may depends on
(t_{k+1},X_{k+1})
, see (
30
)) could drastically reduce the number of MCMC iterations needed for convergence. Simulating a long MCMC chain could then only be necessary for the first sampling sub-problem (when the posterior distribution is more complex) at the initialization of FRIPS, see
Algorithms

4
and
5
. We discuss the initialization of the corresponding Markov chains in
Section

B
.

(b) Rejection Sampling (RS)

Interestingly, we remark that, in all Riemannian settings considered in this paper, we are always able to sample without any bias from the distribution with un-normalized density
x_{1}\mapsto p_{t|1}(x_{t}|x_{1})
, denoted by
\nu_{1|t}(\cdot|x_{t})
; see
Section

5
for applications. For instance, in the particular Euclidean case where
\pi_{0}
is set to be Gaussian (with
\pi_{0,1}
still being the independent coupling),
\nu_{1|t}(\cdot|x_{t})
is a tractable Gaussian distribution; see
Section

B.5
. In the case of the
d
-sphere or Grassmann manifold with
\pi_{0}
the uniform distribution, we verify in
Sections

B.2
and
B.4
that
\nu_{1|t}(\cdot|x_{t})
is a simple pushforward of
\pi_{0}
, enabling unbiased sampling as well. Note however that, for general Riemannian manifolds or base distribution
\pi_{0}
, sampling exactly from
\nu_{1|t}(\cdot|x_{t})
is not necessarily straightforward. In this case, only approximate samples from
x_{1}\mapsto p_{t|1}(x_{t}|x_{1})
may be obtained, for instance by applying MCMC routines.
Assume now that the target un-normalized density
q_{1}
is upper bounded by some
q_{1}^{\star}>0
; in this case,
x_{1}\mapsto p_{t|1}(x_{t}|x_{1})q_{1}^{\star}
is still a valid un-normalized density for
\nu_{1|t}(\cdot|x_{t})
. Then, recalling that
p_{1|t}(x_{1}|x_{t})\propto p_{t|1}(x_{t}|x_{1})q(x_{1})
holds for any
x_{1}\in\mathsf{M}
, we may perform rejection sampling to target
\pi_{1|t}(\cdot|x_{1})
by (i) sampling a proposal state
\tilde{x}_{1}\sim\nu_{1|t}(\cdot|x_{t})
and (ii) applying the acceptance-rejection step defined by the acceptance rate

\alpha^{\text{RS}}(\tilde{x}_{1})=\frac{p_{t|1}(x_{t}|\tilde{x}_{1})q_{1}(\tilde{x}_{1})}{p_{t|1}(x_{t}|\tilde{x}_{1})q_{1}^{\star}}=\frac{q_{1}(\tilde{x}_{1})}{q_{1}^{\star}}\;.

(31)

We summarize this procedure in
Algorithm

7
. Note that this method has previously been used in Euclidean state spaces by
[
24
]
. In particular, it requires knowledge of an upper bound
q_{1}^{\star}
on
q_{1}
. If not provided, the sequential nature of
Algorithm

1
allows for iterative updates of the bound
q_{1}^{\star}
used in
Algorithm

7
every time
q_{1}
is evaluated, as suggested in
[
24
]
.

Although using the RS method for posterior sampling is appealing, as we are guaranteed to obtain unbiased samples from
\pi_{1|t}(\cdot|x_{t})
, granted unbiased samples from the proposal
\nu_{1|t}(\cdot|x_{t})
, we expect the acceptance rate
\alpha^{\text{RS}}
to be low in average due to the potential mismatch between
\pi_{1|t}
and
\nu_{1|t}
, especially in high dimension. In the case where this rate is prohibitively low during the execution of FRIPS, RS may be replaced by one of the other posterior sampling methods.

(c) Importance Sampling (IS)

Taking inspiration from
[
28
,
48
]
, we propose a third posterior sampling approach based on IS
[
1
]
that relies on the same reasoning as the RS method. To be more precise, we propose to set the distribution
\nu_{1|t}(\cdot|x_{t})
, defined in the previous paragraph, as the IS proposal to target
\pi_{1|t}(\cdot|x_{t})
; in this case, the corresponding un-normalized IS weights are single evaluations of the target density
q_{1}
, similarly to (
31

Algorithm

Although the overall approach incurs bias due to the use of self-normalizing weights, it has the advantage of requiring a fixed number of target density evaluations given by the number of samples
N

N
target evaluations to obtain
N
samples. Finally, we emphasize that IS suffers from the same fundamental drawback as RS related to the mismatch between the proposal
\nu_{1|t}
and
\pi_{1|t}
; we refer to
[
8
]
for a more in-depth discussion.

4
FRIPS in practice

After describing the general methodology of FRIPS in
Section

, we provide here several heuristics
leveraging partial knowledge about

to correctly set FRIPS hyper-parameters in practical settings.

4.1
Choice of

As explained in the previous section, the most critical hyper-parameter of FRIPS is without a doubt the starting time
t_{0}\in[0,1)
, especially in the case where
\pi_{1}
is multi-modal. In this section, we provide a heuristic to tune it based on prior knowledge about
\pi_{1}
within a realistic setting,
i.e.
, without access to ground truth samples. This strategy relies on the intrinsic dynamics of the Markovian projection described hereafter.

Markovian dynamics in presence of multi-modality

In
[
7
]
, the authors study a Euclidean stochastic interpolant derived from a diffusion model (
i.e.
, almost-independent coupling
\pi_{0,1}
with
\pi_{0}
being Gaussian) in the case where
\pi_{1}
is multi-modal. In particular, they highlight three regimes of the associated Markovian projection directly related to the multi-modality of
\pi_{1}
, that are identified by
transition times

t_{S}
(speciation time) and
t_{C}
(collapse time) such that
0<t_{S}<t_{C}<1
. They show that, when
t>t_{C}
, the particle
X_{t}^{M}
has already committed to one of the modes of
\pi_{1}
, which strongly determines its evolution up to the final state
X_{1}^{M}
, while it is mostly mode-blind when
t<t_{S}
. In particular, this can be analyzed equivalently through the lens of the so-called
duality of log-concavity

[
22
]
for the posterior distributions
\{\pi_{1|t}\}_{t\in[0,1]}
: when
t>t_{C}
,
\pi_{1|t}
resembles a uni-modal distribution (thus corroborating the “collapsing” behavior of the dynamics), while it is multi-modal for
t<t_{S}
. Both works come to the conclusion that the key to obtain accurate sampling in a diffusion-based formulation in multi-modal scenarios is to carefully handle the in-between time window
[t_{S},t_{C}]
. Such regimes are by definition inherent to FRIPS (independently of the Riemannian nature of the state space). Following the arguments developed in
Section

3.1
, an appropriate starting time
t_{0}
should hence belong to the critical intermediary phase
(t_{S},t_{C})
. We emphasize that the initialization of
t_{0}
is deeply discussed in
[
22
]
, albeit in an Euclidean setting. The authors demonstrate theoretically and empirically the existence of a sweet spot for
t_{0}
(similar to the interval
[t_{S},t_{C}]
), at which both the initialization targeting
\pi_{t_{0}}
and the subsequent iterative sampling sub-problems are relatively simple. While this analysis requires additional assumptions on the target and relies on a different interpolation process, it suggests the existence of an optimal starting time for other stochastic interpolation methods such as FRIPS.

Heuristic estimation of
t_{0}

Based on this characterization of the Markovian dynamics, we propose a heuristic to estimate the transition times
t_{S}
and
t_{C}
(and therefore, set
t_{0}\in(t_{S},t_{C})
), granted prior knowledge about the target distribution. In the rest of this paragraph, we consider a multi-modal distribution
\pi_{1}
defined as a mixture of
J
uni-modal distributions
\{\pi_{1,j}\}_{j=1}^{J}
, each with their normalized density
q_{1,j}
supported on
\mathsf{M}
, such that

\pi_{1}(\mathrm{d}x)=\sum_{j=1}^{J}w_{j}\pi_{1,j}(\mathrm{d}x)\;,

(32)

where
\{w_{j}\}_{j=1}^{J}
are
unknown
positive weights with
\sum_{j=1}^{J}w_{j}=1
. We assume that we have access to “mode-blind” samples from
\pi_{1}
,
i.e.
, a collection of (possibly approximate)
N_{j}
samples from each
\pi_{1,j}
, with knowledge of their mode label, such that the whole dataset is not representative of the ground truth relative weights
\{w_{j}\}_{j=1}^{J}
. In practical applications, “mode-blind” samples may be available if we have access to the location of the modes, e.g. in the context of drug design
[
30
]
. Still, estimating the true weights remains a challenging task
[
23
]
.

To empirically observe the speciation and collapse phenomenons evoked above, we propose to leverage those samples as follows. Given
j\in\{1,\ldots,J\}
, a time
t\in[0,1)
and samples
\{x_{1}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{j}}
drawn from
\pi_{1,j}
, we run FRIPS initialized at
t_{0}=t
with
X_{0}\sim\pi_{t_{0}|1}(\cdot|x_{1}^{i})
for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,N_{j}\}
, and observe if the resulting sample
\hat{x}_{1}
is likely to have been drawn from
\pi_{1,j}
; to do so, we compute the posterior estimator associated to the empirical distribution of the available samples, defined by

\text{Mode}(\hat{x}_{1})=\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{i=1,\ldots,J}\frac{N_{i}q_{1,i}(\hat{x}_{1})}{\sum_{j=1}^{J}N_{j}q_{1,j}(\hat{x}_{1})}\;,

(33)

and verify if it is equal to
j
. The resulting “Return Accuracy”, computed by averaging the outputs of this procedure for all samples
\{x_{1}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{j}}
, is denoted by
\tau_{j}(t)=\texttt{ReturnAccuracy}(t,j)
, see
Algorithm

9
. Regarding the value of
\tau_{j}(t)
, we expect the following behavior.

•

If
t<t_{S}
, then
X_{0}
is mode-blind and the performance of Monte Carlo posterior sampling is limited by the multi-modality of
\pi_{1|t}
. For these two reasons, we expect to have
\tau_{j}(t)\approx 1/J
, which corresponds to the regime where simulated particles return to the modes of
\pi_{1}
with a random assignment.

•

If
t>t_{C}
, we expect to have
\tau_{j}(t)\approx 1
, as the initialization
X_{0}
is well informed about the origin mode, and Monte Carlo posterior sampling methods are likely to perform well.

Hence, by varying the time input
t
, we expect to have an increasing profile for
t\mapsto\tau_{j}(t)
, from
1/J
to 1. As such, we may identify the transition times
t_{S}
and
t_{C}
by averaging the obtained information for all distributions
\{\pi_{j}\}_{j=1}^{J}
, and therefore, accordingly set
t_{0}
, see
Section

5.1
for an illustration.

4.2
Very first initialization of FRIPS

The initialization of FRIPS with RLA strategy, see
Algorithm

4
, or Pseudo-IMH procedure, see
Algorithm

5
, requires an initial sample
{X}_{0}^{0}\in\mathsf{M}
, before running the MCMC refinement steps. While setting
{X}_{0}^{0}\sim\pi_{0}
appears to be a natural choice, one should keep in mind that the next step of the algorithm involves Monte-Carlo estimation of an integral against
\pi_{1|t_{0}}(\cdot|{X}_{0}^{0})
, which may be ill-conditioned if
p_{t_{0}}({X}_{0}^{0})
is too close to
0
. Unfortunately, without any knowledge about the target distribution, choosing a candidate state
{X}_{0}^{0}
is not straightforward. Assuming that we have access to “mode-blind” samples from
\pi_{1}
, see
Section

4.1
for more details, we propose to set
{X}_{0}^{0}=\psi_{t_{0}}(X_{0},\hat{X}_{1})
where
X_{0}\sim\pi_{0}
and
\hat{X}_{1}
is precisely a “mode-blind” sample, hence providing a ”plausible” sample from
\pi_{t_{0}}
to start FRIPS. Furthermore, by generating several such plausible samples at time
t_{0}
, we may compute a rough estimate of the dispersion of
\pi_{t_{0}}
, and tune the hyperparameters of
Algorithms

4
and
5
accordingly.

4.3
General noise schedule and time discretization

In this work, we define the interpolation process
(X_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
as a minimizing geodesic, that is, a minimizing interpolation path with constant speed. While this choice appears the most natural and simple, we may consider, for any
(x_{0},x_{1})\in\mathsf{D}
, the interpolation function

t\in\left[0,1\right]\mapsto\psi^{\alpha}_{t}(x_{0};x_{1})=(\upgamma_{x_{0}}^{x_{1}}\circ\alpha)(t)=\mathrm{Exp}_{x_{1}}((1-\alpha(t))\mathrm{Log}_{x_{1}}x_{0})\;,

(34)

where
\alpha:\left[0,1\right]\rightarrow\left[0,1\right]
is an arbitrary increasing, differentiable function such that
\alpha(0)=0
and
\alpha(1)=1
. As a consequence, by setting the stochastic interpolant as
X^{\alpha}_{t}=\psi^{\alpha}_{t}(X_{0};X_{1})
almost surely for any
t\in\left[0,1\right]
, we would obtain the time derivative
\dot{X}^{\alpha}_{t}=\dot{\alpha}(t)\dot{\upgamma}_{X_{0}}^{X_{1}}(\alpha(t))
. Hence, the velocity field associated to the Markovian projection of
(X^{\alpha}_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
would be given by

\mathbf{u}^{\alpha}_{t}(x_{t})=\mathbb{E}[\dot{X}^{\alpha}_{t}|X^{\alpha}_{t}=x_{t}]=\int_{\mathsf{M}}\frac{\dot{\alpha}(t)\mathrm{Log}_{x_{t}}x_{1}}{1-\alpha(t)}\pi^{\alpha}_{1|t}(\mathrm{d}x_{1}|x_{t})\;,

(35)

where
\pi^{\alpha}_{1|t}(\cdot|x_{t})
denotes the posterior distribution of
X_{1}^{\alpha}
given
X^{\alpha}_{t}=x_{t}
, thus generalizing (
17
). Adopting the nomenclature of diffusion models, we refer to
\alpha
as the noise schedule. In the Euclidean setting, various noise schedules are for instance studied by
[
22
]
, and are proved to have an influence on the sampling performance. Specifically, the authors design noise schedules that match pre-defined profiles of the Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR), which informs on the increasing recovery of information about
\pi_{1}
with respect to
\pi_{0}
as
t
increases. In the case of the Euclidean linear interpolant
X_{t}=(1-t)X_{0}+tX_{1}
, the SNR is for instance defined by

\operatorname{SNR}(t)=\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\|tX_{1}\|^{2}\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[\|(1-t)X_{0}\|^{2}\right]}\propto\frac{t^{2}}{(1-t)^{2}}

(36)

Additionally, they propose to set the time discretization
\{t_{k}\}_{k=0}^{K}
so that the log-SNR gap between each step is kept constant, instead of using the canonical uniform discretization
t_{k}=t_{0}+k(t_{K}-t_{0})/K
. Empirically, this choice allows for a drastic reduction in the number of global steps
K
while maintaining sampling quality.

For simplicity, we adopt the standard noise schedule
t\mapsto\alpha(t)=t
together with a uniform time discretization in our numerical experiments (
Section

5
), as the choice of
K
has only a limited impact on performance in the settings considered. We defer the practical implementation of FRIPS for general noise schedules and non-uniform time discretization schemes to future work.

5
Numerical experiments

In this section, we quantitatively assess the performance of FRIPS in comparison with classical Monte Carlo samplers on a collection of challenging multi-modal target distributions defined on Riemannian manifolds. Following the recommendations of
[
23
]
, we focus on controlled
bi-modal
targets for which the exact mode weights are known, and evaluate sampling performance through the ability of the generated samples to recover these proportions.

Concretely, we mainly report the relative statistical error of the Monte Carlo estimate of the weight of the dominant mode, systematically denoted by
w\in(0,1)
and associated with mode
j=1
. Given
N
generated samples
\{\hat{x}_{1}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, this estimator is defined as

\displaystyle\hat{w}=(1/N)\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}_{\text{Mode}(\hat{x}_{1}^{i})=1}

(37)

where the posterior mode assignment
\text{Mode}(\cdot)
is specified in (
33
). In all experiments, we set
N=4096
and repeat each sampling procedure four times to obtain confidence intervals.

Overall, this mode weight metric provides a clear and interpretable measure of multi-modal sampling performance. For completeness, we may additionally report complementary sample-based metrics. To ensure a fair comparison across methods, we systematically match the total number of evaluations of
q_{1}
, which constitutes the main computational bottleneck in practice, within each experiment. Further details on the experimental setup and the choice of hyperparameters are deferred to
Section

B
.

5.2
Mixture of Student’s distributions via stereographic projection

To further assess the performance of FRIPS in challenging multi-modal sampling scenarios, we consider a bi-modal target distribution
\pi_{1}
with heavy tails, defined on Euclidean space,
i.e.
,
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
d\geqslant 1
. Such targets are notoriously difficult for standard Euclidean sampling methods, including diffusion-based approaches that rely on Gaussian noising schemes, which are poorly adapted to heavy-tailed distributions; see, for instance,
[
45
]
in the context of generative modeling. In contrast with the previous experiment in
Section

5.1
,
\pi_{1}
is not originally defined on a Riemannian manifold, a choice made deliberately. To demonstrate the effectiveness of FRIPS in this setting, we adopt the strategy proposed in
[
50
]
and rather aim to sample from the push-forward of
\pi_{1}
under the inverse stereographic projection (SP). This yields a bi-modal distribution, denoted
\pi_{1}^{S}
, supported on the
d
-sphere
\mathsf{S}^{d}
, where FRIPS can be naturally applied. The underlying rationale is that sampling from the resulting Riemannian distribution using geometry-aware tools is expected to be more easier in practice than sampling directly from its Euclidean counterpart. Samples generated on the sphere are subsequently mapped back to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
to recover samples from
\pi_{1}
.

Concretely, we consider an
unbalanced
mixture of two multivariate Student’s
t
-distributions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with dominant mode weight
w=2/3
. The un-normalized target density
q_{1}
is given by

x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto(2/3)\left(1+\frac{1}{\nu}\left\|\frac{x-\mu_{1}}{\tau}\right\|^{2}\right)^{-\frac{\nu+d}{2}}+(1/3)\left(1+\frac{1}{\nu}\left\|\frac{x-\mu_{2}}{\tau}\right\|^{2}\right)^{-\frac{\nu+d}{2}}\;,

(39)

where
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are the mode locations,
\tau=0.05
is the scale parameter, and
\nu=1
denotes the degree of freedom, taken identical for both modes for simplicity. In
Section

B.3
, we derive the corresponding un-normalized density
q_{1}^{S}
associated with
\pi_{1}^{S}
, along with details on the stereographic projection operator. Importantly, the projected distribution remains bi-modal with dominant mode weight unchanged at
w=2/3
.

To isolate the combined effect of heavy tails and multi-modality, we fix the dimension to
d=4
and consider two regimes of increasing difficulty, taking inspiration from
[
23
]
: a
low mode separation
regime with
\mu_{1}=(3/2,\ldots,3/2)
and
\mu_{2}=(-3/4,\ldots,-3/4)
, and a
high mode separation
regime with
\mu_{1}=(2,\ldots,2)
and
\mu_{2}=(-1,\ldots,-1)
. To highlight the benefit of combining FRIPS with SP, we compare its performance to a naive Euclidean counterpart that directly targets
\pi_{1}
using FRIPS in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with a Gaussian initial distribution
\pi_{0}
, which corresponds to the deterministic variant of the algorithm proposed in
[
22
]
. Both approaches use MALA for posterior sampling (
Algorithm

6
).
We also include standard Monte Carlo baselines, namely MALA on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, as well as MALA combined with SP, which can be viewed as a first-order instance of the approach in
[
50
]
, who originally consider a Random-Walk Metropolis-Hastings scheme.

Beyond assessing multi-modality through mode weight estimation, we additionally evaluate the ability of the samplers to capture heavy tails using a metric specifically designed for this purpose: the mean squared logarithmic error (MSLE), computed with respect to ground-truth samples; see, e.g.,
[
3
, Section 3.2]
. This metric quantifies the accuracy of tail sampling through a hyperparameter
\xi\in(0,1)
, corresponding to the recovery of the
\xi
th percentile of the distribution. In practice, we set
\xi=0.99
. All metrics are systematically evaluated in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Main results

The results are reported in
Figure

6
, which displays the performance of FRIPS with stereographic projection (orange) and its Euclidean counterpart (blue) as a function of
t_{0}\in[0,0.9]
. The MALA baselines, shown as dashed lines, are independent of
t_{0}
and use the same color coding. Two main observations emerge.

First, within each setting—Euclidean or Riemannian—there exists a value of
t_{0}
for which FRIPS outperforms MALA simultaneously in terms of multi-modality and heavy-tail recovery. These optimal values differ markedly across settings: they correspond to relatively small
t_{0}
values in the Euclidean case (around
t_{0}\approx 0.2
) and much larger values in the Riemannian case (around
t_{0}\approx 0.8
), highlighted by vertical dashed lines.

This discrepancy reflects fundamentally different behaviors of the two algorithms. As expected, increasing the distance between modes exacerbates the difficulty of multi-modal sampling, as evidenced by the degradation of MALA’s mode weight estimates. More importantly, while the Euclidean and Riemannian versions of FRIPS exhibit comparable performance in recovering mode proportions, the Euclidean variant performs significantly worse in capturing heavy tails, as measured by MSLE. In contrast, FRIPS combined with SP achieves consistently strong tail performance, largely invariant to the degree of mode separation. These results underscore the advantage of the Riemannian formulation of FRIPS for sampling from distributions that are both multi-modal and heavy-tailed.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we studied the problem of sampling from multi-modal distributions defined on Riemannian manifolds when only an un-normalized density is available. Our approach draws inspiration from generative modeling, where stochastic interpolants
[
2
]
have emerged as an effective framework for handling multi-modality. Leveraging recent extensions of these ideas to Riemannian manifolds
[
10
]
, we introduced a novel sampling methodology, termed Flow-based Riemannian Iterative Posterior Sampling (FRIPS). The proposed method is entirely training-free, relying solely on Monte Carlo techniques, and can be viewed as a Riemannian analogue of the Euclidean approach developed in
[
22
]
. To support our methodology, we developed a rigorous theoretical framework for Riemannian stochastic interpolants tailored to the sampling setting and provided practical guidelines for instantiating FRIPS. We demonstrated the effectiveness of the approach on a range of challenging multi-modal targets defined on the
d
-sphere and Grassmann manifolds, where traditional Monte Carlo samplers fail, highlighting FRIPS robustness to pronounced multi-modality and increasing dimensionality. One remaining limitation concerns the choice of the initial time of the denoising dynamics that underlies FRIPS, which currently requires a grid search to achieve optimal performance. Nevertheless, our empirical findings suggest a structured dependence of this hyperparameter with respect to the target distribution, opening the door to principled tuning strategies. Developing such automated and practical selection procedures based on theoretical investigation is an important direction for future work.

Background on Riemannian geometry

To complete the brief introduction to Riemannian geometry of
Section

2.1
,
we properly introduce some notions used in the paper. We refer to
[
32
,
44
]
for basics on this topic.

A.1
Riemannian structure and geodesics

Assume
\mathsf{M}
is a
d
-dimensional smooth, connected manifold.

Smooth manifold structure

By definition, there exist a (maximal)
atlas
on
\mathsf{M}
,
i.e.
a collection of compatible
coordinate charts
whose domains cover the whole manifold. A coordinate chart at
x\in\mathsf{M}
is a homeomorphism
\varphi:\mathsf{U}\rightarrow\mathsf{U}^{\prime}
, where
\mathsf{U}
is an open subset of
\mathsf{M}
that contains
x
and
\mathsf{U}^{\prime}
is an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. By compatible, we mean that the compositions
\varphi\circ\psi^{-1},\psi\circ\varphi^{-1}
are diffeomorphisms (in the usual
\mathbb{R}^{d}
sense), where
\varphi,\psi
are two charts with overlapping domains. A function
f
between two smooth manifolds is called smooth if its composition with the appropriate coordinate charts is, in the usual
\mathbb{R}^{d}
sense.

We call
local coordinates
the components of
\varphi=(x^{1},...,x^{d})
. Given a point
z\in\mathsf{M}
, the
tangent space at
z
is a
d
-dimensional vector space spanned by the
coordinate vectors
in any local coordinate system. For every
1\leqslant i\leqslant d
and every smooth function
f:\mathsf{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, coordinate vectors are defined by

\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}}\right|_{z}f=\partial_{i}\left[f\circ\varphi^{-1}\right]_{|\varphi(z)}\;,

(40)

where
\partial_{i}
denotes the usual partial derivative in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The
differential of
f
at
z
, denoted
\mathrm{d}f_{z}:T_{z}\mathsf{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, is the linear form defined by
\mathrm{d}f_{z}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}}\right)=\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}}\right|_{z}f,1\leqslant i\leqslant d
. The
Riemannian gradient

\mathrm{grad}f(z)\in T_{z}\mathsf{M}
is the only tangent vector at
z
satisfying
\mathrm{d}f_{z}=\langle\mathrm{grad}f(z),\,\cdot\,\rangle_{z}
.

Metric and vector fields

A
Riemannian metric

z\in\mathsf{M}\mapsto\mathbf{g}_{z}
defines an inner product in
T_{z}\mathsf{M}
for every
z\in\mathsf{M}
, varying smoothly in
z
, and denoted
\left\langle v,w\right\rangle_{z}
for every
v,w\in T_{z}\mathsf{M}
.

A (smooth)
vector field

X
is a (smooth) map from
\mathsf{M}
to the tangent bundle
T\mathsf{M}
,
i.e.
the disjoint union of all tangent spaces. In local coordinates, a vector field can be written as
X_{z}=X^{i}_{z}\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}}\right|_{z}
for every
z\in\mathsf{M}
. The
Riemannian divergence

\text{div}X
is defined in local coordinates by

\text{div}\left(X^{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}}\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det g}}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}}\left(X^{i}\sqrt{\det g}\right)\;,

(41)

where
\det g
denotes the determinant of the Gram matrix
g
of
\mathbf{g}
for these coordinate vectors, whose elements are denoted
g_{i,j}=\left\langle\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}},\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{j}}\right\rangle
.

The
Levi-Civita connection

\nabla

[
32
, Chapter 5]
associated to
\mathbf{g}
defines the differentiation of a vector field with respect to another: given two vector fields
X,Y
,
\nabla_{X}Y
outputs a vector field. Given a smooth curve
t\in I\subset\mathbb{R}\mapsto\upgamma(t)\in\mathsf{M}
, a vector field
X
along
\upgamma
is
parallel
if
\nabla_{\dot{\upgamma}}X=0
, where for every
t\in I,\dot{\upgamma}(t)\in T_{\upgamma(t)}\mathsf{M}
is the velocity field of
\upgamma
. Given
a\in I
and a tangent vector
v\in T_{\upgamma(a)}
, a vector field
X
along
\upgamma
satisfying
X_{\upgamma(a)}=v
and
\nabla_{\dot{\upgamma}}X=0
is called the
parallel transport of
v
along
\upgamma
.

Geodesics and completeness

Geodesics
are smooth functions
t\in I\subset\mathbb{R}\mapsto\upgamma(t)\in\mathsf{M}
satisfying
\nabla_{\dot{\upgamma}}\dot{\upgamma}=0
, which results in the geodesic equation in local coordinates
[
32
, Chapter 6]
. Given
a\in I,v\in T_{\upgamma(a)}\mathsf{M}
,
t\in I\mapsto\tau_{\upgamma(a),v}(t)\in T_{\upgamma(t)}\mathsf{M}
denotes the parallel transport of
v
along
\upgamma
at
\upgamma(t)
.

In
1
, we suppose that
\mathsf{M}
is complete, that is, metric complete for the Riemannian distance associated with
\mathbf{g}
. According to the Hopf-Rinow theorem
[
32
, Theorem 6.19]
, metric completeness is actually equivalent to geodesic completeness for a connected manifold: every geodesic can be extended to
\mathbb{R}
, and every pair of points in
\mathsf{M}
can be connected by at least one minimizing geodesic.

A.2
Jacobi fields and spherical coordinates

Jacobi fields

Fix
z\in\mathsf{M}
and
v\in T_{z}\mathsf{M}
, and let
\upgamma_{z,v}
denote the geodesic starting at
z
with initial velocity
v
,
i.e.
,
\upgamma_{z,v}(0)=z
and
\dot{\upgamma}_{z,v}(0)=v
, so that
\upgamma_{z,v}(t)=\mathrm{Exp}_{z}(tv)
(the exponential map has been defined in
Section

2.1
). A
Jacobi field
along
\upgamma_{z,v}
(see
[
9
, Section II.5]
,
[
34
, Chapter 10]
) is a smooth vector field
t\in\left[0,+\infty\right)\mapsto J(t)\in T_{\upgamma_{z,v}(t)}\mathsf{M}
satisfying the Jacobi equation

\nabla_{t}^{2}J+\mathbf{R}(\dot{\upgamma}_{z,v},J)\dot{\upgamma}_{z,v}=0\;,

(42)

with
\mathbf{R}
the curvature tensor
[
9
, Section I.4]
and
\nabla_{t}
the covariant derivative. Since
\nabla_{t}\dot{\upgamma}_{z,v}=0=\mathbf{R}(\dot{\upgamma}_{z,v},\dot{\upgamma}_{z,v})
,
\dot{\upgamma}_{z,v}
is a Jacobi field, as well as
t\mapsto t\dot{\upgamma}_{z,v}(t)
. The corresponding trivial solutions, which lie in the subspace
\mathbb{R}\dot{\upgamma}_{z,v}(t)\subset T_{\upgamma_{z,v}(t)}\mathsf{M}
at each
t
, are called
tangential
Jacobi fields. The other solutions, orthogonal to
\dot{\upgamma}_{z,v}(t)
for any
t\geqslant 0
, are called
normal
Jacobi fields.

Jacobi fields relate to the differential of the exponential map: from
[
33
, Proposition 10.10]
, the Jacobi field along
\upgamma
with initial condition
J(0)=0,\nabla_{t}J(0)=w
reads

J(t)=\mathrm{d}{\mathrm{Exp}_{z}}_{|tv}tw\;.

(43)

If
\mathsf{M}
has constant sectional curvature
\kappa

[
33
, Proposition 8.29]
, Jacobi fields admit a closed-form expression
[
33
, Proposition 10.12]
. This condition holds e.g. for the Euclidean space (
\kappa=0
), and the unit sphere
\mathsf{S}^{d}
(
\kappa=1
).
Normal Jacobi fields are of the form

J(t)\propto s_{\kappa}(t)\mathbf{e}(t)\;,

(44)

with
\mathbf{e}
a parallel unit normal vector field along
\upgamma_{z,v}
,
i.e.
obtained as the parallel transport along
\upgamma
of a unit tangent vector of
T_{z}\mathsf{M}
, orthogonal to the initial velocity vector
\dot{\upgamma}_{z,v}(0)=v
. The function
s_{\kappa}
is defined as
t\mapsto\sin(\sqrt{\kappa}t)/\sqrt{\kappa}
if
\kappa>0
,
t\mapsto\sinh(\sqrt{-\kappa}t)/\sqrt{-\kappa}
if
\kappa<0
, and
t\mapsto t
if
\kappa=0
.

Spherical coordinates and Riemannian measure

Following
[
9
, Chapter 3]
, we provide a complete construction of the spherical coordinates. These coordinates allow us to write a practical expression of the
Riemannian measure

\rho_{\mathbf{g}}
, proving (
24
).
We repeat the corresponding paragraph of
Section

2.4
, with additional details.

By definition, the Riemannian measure
\rho_{\mathbf{g}}
is constructed as follows; see also
[
9
, Section III.3]
. Given a chart
\varphi=(x^{1},...,x^{d})\,:\,\mathsf{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
\mathsf{U}
is an open subset of
\mathsf{M}
, and a function
f\,:\,\mathsf{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that
f\circ x^{-1}
is measurable, the expression

\int_{\varphi(\mathsf{U})}\left(f\sqrt{\det g}\right)\circ\varphi^{-1}\mathrm{d}\lambda\;,

(45)

with
\lambda
the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, does not depend on the specific chart but only on
\mathsf{U}
and
f
. Consequently, it defines
\int_{\mathsf{U}}f\mathrm{d}\rho_{\mathbf{g}}
.
Then, by picking any atlas on
\mathsf{M}
, along with a partition of unity, the measure
\rho_{\mathbf{g}}
is extended to the whole manifold.

Although this construction of
\rho_{\mathbf{g}}
is valid for any chart, spherical coordinates yield a particularly convenient form for functions expressed along geodesics.
Fix
z\in\mathsf{M}
. Recall that, under
1
, the map
\mathrm{Exp}_{z}
is a diffeomorphism from
\mathrm{ID}(z)\subset T_{z}\mathsf{M}
, defined in (
4
) onto
\mathsf{M}\setminus\mathrm{Cut}(z)
. By restriction to
\mathrm{ID}^{*}(z)=\mathrm{ID}(z)\setminus\{0\}
, we obtain a diffeomorphism onto
\mathsf{M}^{z}=\mathsf{M}\setminus\{\mathrm{Cut}(z)\cup\{z\}\}
. Denote
S_{z}\mathsf{M}\subset T_{z}\mathsf{M}
the unit sphere of
T_{z}\mathsf{M}
.
Then, it holds that
\mathrm{ID}^{*}(z)=\{t\xi\,:\,\xi\in S_{z}\mathsf{M},0<t<\mathbf{c}_{z}(\xi)\}
.

Let
D_{z}\mathsf{M}\subset S_{z}\mathsf{M}\times\left(0,+\infty\right)
denotes the set of pairs
(\xi,t)
such that
t\xi\in\mathrm{ID}^{*}(z)
.
The map
(\xi,t)\mapsto\varphi_{z}(\xi,t)=\mathrm{Exp}_{z}t\xi
is a diffeomorphism from
D_{z}\mathsf{M}
onto
\mathsf{M}^{z}
,
whose inverse writes

x\in\mathsf{M}^{z}\mapsto\varphi^{-1}_{z}(x)=\left(\frac{\mathrm{Log}_{z}x}{\operatorname{dist}(z,x)},\operatorname{dist}(z,x)\right)\;.

(46)

Since
\rho_{\mathbf{g}}(\mathrm{Cut}(z)\cup\{z\})=0
, almost every point in
\mathsf{M}
can be parameterized in this way.

To construct an atlas on

\mathsf{M}^{z}
, we pick a chart
u=(u^{1},...,u^{d-1})
mapping an open subset
\mathsf{O}\subset S_{z}\mathsf{M}
to
\mathsf{U}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
, and denote
\xi=u^{-1}:\mathsf{U}\rightarrow\mathsf{O}
.
Then,
(v,t)\mapsto\mathrm{Exp}_{z}t\xi(v)
is a diffeomorphism from
\mathsf{U}^{\prime}\coloneq(u\times\operatorname{Id})(D_{z}\mathsf{M}\cap\mathsf{O}\times\left(0,+\infty\right))
onto
\mathsf{O}^{\prime}\coloneq\varphi_{z}(D_{z}\mathsf{M}\cap\mathsf{O}\times\left(0,+\infty\right))
.
The inverse map provides a chart
x=(x^{1},...,x^{d}):\mathsf{O}^{\prime}\rightarrow\mathsf{U}^{\prime}
on
\mathsf{M}^{z}
, namely

x^{i}=u^{i}\circ\left(\frac{\mathrm{Log}_{z}}{\operatorname{dist}(z,\cdot)}\right),1\leqslant i\leqslant d-1;\qquad x^{d}=\operatorname{dist}(z,\cdot)\;.

(47)

Then, by definition of
\rho_{\mathbf{g}}
, an integral on
\mathsf{O}^{\prime}\subset\mathsf{M}^{z}
against
\rho_{\mathbf{g}}
can be computed as an integral on
\mathsf{U}^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, against the Lebesgue measure.
For every
\rho_{\mathbf{g}}
-integrable function
f
,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathsf{O}^{\prime}}f\mathrm{d}\rho_{\mathbf{g}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathsf{U}^{\prime}}\left(f\sqrt{\det g}\right)\circ x^{-1}(v,t)\mathrm{d}t\mathrm{d}v

(48)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathsf{U}}\int_{0}^{\mathbf{c}_{z}(\xi(v))}f(\mathrm{Exp}_{z}t\xi(v))\sqrt{\det\left\langle\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}},\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{j}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{Exp}_{z}t\xi(v)}}\mathrm{d}t\mathrm{d}v\;.

(49)

To integrate over the entire manifold
\mathsf{M}
(equivalently over
\mathsf{M}^{z}
), one selects an atlas on
S_{z}\mathsf{M}
and constructs a corresponding atlas on
\mathsf{M}^{z}
using the procedure described for a single chart
u
. Using a partition of unity, we can write the change of measure formula on
\mathsf{M}^{z}
using the expression (
49
) for a specific chart: see
[
32
, Chapter 16]
for details regarding integration on manifolds.

Now, still following
[
9
, Chapter III]
, we make explicit the determinant factor of (
49
) using Jacobi fields.
Consider the coordinate vector fields
\frac{\partial}{\partial u^{i}},\frac{\partial}{\partial t}
on
D_{z}\mathsf{M}
, and write
\partial_{i}\xi\coloneqq\frac{\partial}{\partial u^{i}}
for every
1\leqslant i\leqslant d-1
.

It follows from (
59
) that for every
1\leqslant i\leqslant d-1
,

\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}}\right|_{\mathrm{Exp}_{z}t\xi}=J_{i}(t,\xi)\;,

(50)

with
J_{i}
the Jacobi field along
\upgamma_{z,\xi}
satisfying the initial condition
J_{i}(0,\xi)=0
,
\nabla_{t}J_{i}(0,\xi)=\partial_{i}\xi
. Furthermore,
\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{d}}\right|_{\mathrm{Exp}_{z}t\xi}=\dot{\upgamma}_{z,\xi}
. Hence, in these coordinates, the metric coefficient
g_{i,j}
along
\upgamma_{z,\xi}
is computed as

\left\langle\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}},\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{j}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{Exp}_{z}t\xi}=\langle J_{i}(t,\xi),J_{j}(t,\xi)\rangle_{\mathrm{Exp}_{z}t\xi}\;,

(51)

for
1\leqslant i,j\leqslant d-1
. As for
\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{d}}
, since it corresponds to the tangential Jacobi field
\dot{\upgamma}_{z,\xi}
, we simply have
\|\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{d}}\|=\|\xi\|=1
by definition, and
\langle\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{d}},\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}}\rangle=0
by
[
9
, Equation II.5.7]
. In contrast, the Jacobi fields
J_{i}
,
1\leqslant i\leqslant d-1
, are normal Jacobi fields.

The Jacobi equation can be restated as a differential equation in the tangent space
T_{z}\mathsf{M}
, by introducing the linear map
\mathcal{R}_{z,\xi}(t)\colon T_{z}\mathsf{M}\to T_{z}\mathsf{M}
defined as

\mathcal{R}_{z,\xi}(t)v=\tau_{z,\xi}(t)^{-1}\left[\mathbf{R}\left(\dot{\upgamma}_{z,\xi}(t),\tau_{z,\xi}(t)v\right)\,\dot{\upgamma}_{z,\xi}(t)\right]\;,

(52)

for any
v\in T_{z}\mathsf{M}
.
We consider the associated linear second-order ODE

\ddot{\mathscr{A}}_{z,\xi}+\mathcal{R}_{z,\xi}(t)\mathscr{A}_{z,\xi}=0\;,

(53)

with initial conditions
\mathscr{A}_{z,\xi}(0)=0
and
\dot{\mathscr{A}}_{z,\xi}(0)=\operatorname{Id}
.

Using the symmetries of the curvature tensor (see
[
9
, Equation I.4.4]
), the operator
\mathcal{R}_{z,\xi}(t)
vanishes on
\mathbb{R}\xi
for all
t\geqslant 0
. Consequently, any solution
\mathscr{A}_{z,\xi}
of (
53
) can be viewed as an endomorphism of
\xi^{\perp}\subset T_{z}\mathsf{M}
, the orthogonal complement of
\mathbb{R}\xi
. Denote
(t,\xi)\in(0,+\infty)\times S_{z}\mathsf{M}\mapsto\mathscr{A}_{z,\xi}(t)
the solution of (
23
) associated with
\xi
.

We then have, for every
1\leqslant i\leqslant d-1
,

\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}}\right|_{\mathrm{Exp}_{z}t\xi}=J_{i}(t,\xi)=\tau_{t,\xi}\mathscr{A}_{z,\xi}(t)\partial_{i}\xi\;.

(54)

It follows that the square root of the determinant of (
51
), that appears in the change of measure formula (
49
), is equal to
\det\mathscr{A}(t,\xi(v))

[
9
, Theorem III.3.1]
. Rather than integrating using charts on
S_{z}\mathsf{M}
, we can integrate directly over
S_{z}\mathsf{M}
using the measure
\mu_{z}
induced from the Euclidean Lebesgue measure. We have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathsf{U}^{\prime}}f\mathrm{d}\rho_{\mathbf{g}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathsf{U}}\int_{0}^{\mathbf{c}_{z}(\xi(v))}f(\mathrm{Exp}_{z}t\xi(v))\det\mathscr{A}_{z,\xi(v)}(t)\mathrm{d}t\mathrm{d}v

(55)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathsf{O}}\int_{0}^{\mathbf{c}_{x_{1}}(\xi)}f(\mathrm{Exp}_{z}t\xi)\det\mathscr{A}_{z,\xi}(t)\mathrm{d}t\mathrm{d}\mu_{z}(\xi)\;,

(56)

which is then extended to the whole manifold as

\int_{\mathsf{M}}f\mathrm{d}\rho_{\mathbf{g}}=\int_{S_{z}\mathsf{M}}\int_{0}^{\mathbf{c}_{x_{1}}(\xi)}f(\mathrm{Exp}_{z}t\xi)\det\mathscr{A}_{z,\xi}(t)\mathrm{d}t\mathrm{d}\mu_{z}(\xi)\;.

(57)

Computing
\det\mathscr{A}

If
\mathsf{M}
has constant sectional curvature
\kappa
, normal Jacobi fields are given by (
44
): it follows that
\mathscr{A}_{z,\xi}(t)=s_{\kappa}(t)I_{d-1}
, with
I_{d-1}
the identity matrix in dimension
d-1
, hence
\det\mathscr{A}_{z,\xi}(t)=s_{\kappa}(t)^{d-1}
for any
(t,\xi)\in S_{z}\mathsf{M}
.

If
\mathsf{M}
is a locally symmetric space (see
[
33
, Chapter 10]
),
\det\mathscr{A}
is available in closed form as well, through the Jacobian of the exponential map
[
12
]
. Given
v\in T_{z}\mathsf{M}
a non-zero vector, it holds

\operatorname{Jac}_{z,v}\coloneqq\det\left[\tau_{z,v}(1)^{-1}\circ\mathrm{d}{\mathrm{Exp}_{z}}_{|v}\right]=\frac{1}{\|v\|^{d-1}}\det\mathscr{A}_{z,v/\|v\|}\left(\|v\|\right)\;,

(58)

where the composition with the isometry
\tau_{z,v}(1)^{-1}
allows for a non-ambiguous definition of the determinant. On locally symmetric spaces, the left-hand term is explicit (
[
12
, Theorem 4.1]
):

\operatorname{Jac}_{z,v}=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\left(\frac{\sinh(\sqrt{-\lambda_{i}(R_{u})})}{\sqrt{-\lambda_{i}(R_{u})}}\right)^{n_{i}}\;,

(59)

with
\lambda_{i}(R_{u})
the
i
-th complex eigenvalue (and
n_{i}
its algebraic multiplicity) of the linear map
v\in T_{z}\mathsf{M}\mapsto R_{u}(v)=\mathbf{R}(v,u)u\in T_{z}\mathsf{M}
.

B.1
Algorithmic details

Initializing the MCMC chains

When using an MCMC method for posterior sampling, e.g. MALA, the iterative nature of FRIPS allows for some simplifications. As already mentioned in
Section

3.2
, once the first MCMC chain is run, we can reuse it to initialize the next one. However, we must keep in mind that the posterior density may have a constrained support, due to the expression of
p_{t|1}
(
25
).
If, given a state
(t_{k},X_{k})
of
Algorithm

1
, the final MCMC sample
x_{1}
obtained from the previous chain at time
t_{k-1}
does not satisfy the support constraint
X_{k}\in\mathsf{O}_{t_{k}}(x_{1})
, due to the update of
t_{k},X_{k}
, we may project it within the appropriate domain. From
Proposition

1
, the condition
X_{k}\in\mathsf{O}_{t_{k}}(x_{1})
rewrites as

\operatorname{dist}(X_{k},x_{1})<(1-t_{k})\mathbf{c}_{x_{1}}\left(\frac{\mathrm{Log}_{x_{1}}X_{k}}{\operatorname{dist}(x_{1},X_{k})}\right)\;.

(60)

Thus, replacing
x_{1}
by

\upgamma_{X_{k}}^{x_{1}}(s)=\mathrm{Exp}_{x_{1}}\left((1-s)\mathrm{Log}_{x_{1}}X_{k}\right)\;,

(61)

with
s
slightly smaller than
(1-t_{k})\mathbf{c}_{x_{1}}\left(\frac{\mathrm{Log}_{x_{1}}X_{k}}{\operatorname{dist}(x_{1},X_{k})}\right)/\operatorname{dist}(x_{1},X_{k})
provides a plausible point to initialize the next chain. Similarly, we use this projection procedure to reuse the MCMC samples when running the RLA initialization (
Algorithm

4
).

The question of initializing the very first MCMC chain remains. Given a starting point
X_{0}
, if we initialize the MCMC chain targeting
\pi_{1|t_{0}}(\cdot|X_{0})
arbitrarily (for instance, distributed as
\pi_{0}
), it might lie outside of the support. One solution is to resort to the projection procedure we just described. Alternatively, we can sample a random vector
v
within an appropriate disk in
T_{X_{0}}\mathsf{M}
, and set
x_{1}=\mathrm{Exp}_{X_{0}}v
. If
\mathsf{M}=\mathsf{S}^{d}
, we can pick
\mathrm{Exp}_{X_{0}}v
, with
\|v\|<(1-t_{0})\pi
chosen uniformly, as an initial state, which is what we do in practice.

MALA on manifold

In our numerical experiments, we include a Metropolis-Hasting step after the usual RLA proposal (
Algorithm

6
). While this step usually induces a small bias on non-trivial manifold, we find it empirically improves the sampling performance. Furthermore, it allows for geometrically increase/decrease the step-size
\delta
to maintain a given acceptance rate. Since the posterior densities (
30
) support gradually shrink as
t
approaches
1
, the automatic tuning of the step-size is an important feature.

Given a target density
p
on a
d
-dimensional Riemannian manifold
\mathsf{M}
and a current point
x\in\mathsf{M}
, the RLA proposal is
[
19
]

x^{*}=\mathrm{Exp}_{y}(\sqrt{2\delta}Z),\quad y=\mathrm{Exp}_{x}\bigl(\delta\mathrm{grad}\log p(x)\bigr)\;,

(62)

where
Z\in T_{y}\mathsf{M}\simeq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is drawn from
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.

The corresponding proposal distribution given
x
is therefore
({\mathrm{Exp}_{y}})_{\sharp}\mathcal{N}(0,2\delta)
. On a general Riemannian manifold,
\mathrm{Exp}_{y}
usually fails to be a global diffeomorphism. As a result, the exact proposal density should account for all points
z\in\mathsf{M}
satisfying
\mathrm{Exp}_{y}(\sqrt{2\delta}\mathrm{Log}_{y}z)=x^{*}
. Assuming a small step size
\delta
, we neglect these contributions and approximate the proposal density as

f(x^{*}|x)=\frac{\mathcal{N}(\mathrm{Log}_{y}x^{*};0,2\delta)}{\operatorname{Jac}_{y,\mathrm{Log}_{y}x^{*}}}\;,

(63)

where the denominator denotes the Jacobian determinant of
\mathrm{d}\mathrm{Exp}_{y}
at
\mathrm{Log}_{y}x^{*}
, see (
58
). Empirically, we find that omitting this determinant has little impact on the algorithm.
The Metropolis–Hastings acceptance probability is then computed as

\alpha=\min\Bigl(1,\frac{p(x^{*})}{p(x)}\frac{f(x|x^{*})}{f(x^{*}|x)}\Bigr)\;.

(64)

Note that this procedure is biased due to the approximation on
f
.

\displaystyle=\rho_{\mathbf{g}}(\mathsf{S}^{d})^{-1}\int_{S_{x_{t}}\mathsf{S}^{d}}\int_{0}^{\pi}\sin^{d-1}(s)p_{1}(\mathrm{Exp}_{x_{t}}s(1-t)\xi)\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}\mu_{x_{t}}(\xi)

(68)

(69)

We check that

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho_{\mathbf{g}}(\mathsf{S}^{d})^{-1}}{p_{t}(x_{t})}\int_{S_{x_{t}}\mathsf{S}^{d}}\int_{0}^{\pi}s\xi\sin^{d-1}(s)p_{1}(\mathrm{Exp}_{x_{t}}(1-t)s\xi)\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}\mu_{x_{t}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho_{\mathbf{g}}(\mathsf{S}^{d})^{-1}}{p_{t}(x_{t})}\int_{\mathsf{S}^{d}}\mathrm{Log}_{x_{t}}x_{1}\times p_{1}(\psi_{t}(x_{1};x_{t}))\rho_{\mathbf{g}}(\mathrm{d}x_{1})\;.

For almost every

Stereographic projection

We detail below how to compute the target density and its score once projected to
\mathsf{S}^{d}

Density and score

Given a scale parameter
R>0
, the stereographic projection operator
\mathrm{SP}:\mathsf{S}^{d}\setminus{N}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
N
denotes the north pole
(0,...,0,1)\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
, is defined by

\mathrm{SP}(z)=R\frac{z-z_{d+1}e_{d+1}}{1-z_{d+1}}\;,

(82)

where
z_{d+1}
is the
d
-th component of
z
in the canonical basis
(e_{i})_{i=1}^{d+1}
of
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
.
The projected target distribution is defined as
\pi_{1}^{S}={\mathrm{SP}^{-1}}_{\sharp}\pi_{1}
, and the associated unnormalized density is given by

\displaystyle z\in\mathsf{S}^{d}\setminus{N}

\displaystyle\mapsto q_{1}^{S}(z)=q_{1}(\mathrm{SP}(z))\left(\frac{\|\mathrm{SP}(z)\|^{2}+R^{2}}{2R}\right)^{d}\;,

(83)

where the multiplicative factor is the Jacobian determinant of the transformation.

To apply first-order sampling schemes such as MALA (
Algorithm

6
) within FRIPS, we compute the gradient log of this density. With
\cdot^{T}
denoting the transpose, it is given by

\mathrm{grad}\log q_{1}^{S}(z)=\left[\mathrm{grad}\log q_{1}(\text{SP}(z))\right]^{T}\mathrm{d}\text{SP}_{z}+d\times\frac{e_{d+1}-z_{d+1}z}{1-z_{d+1}}\;.

(84)

The differential
\mathrm{d}\text{SP}_{z}
admits the explicit expression

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathrm{d}\text{SP}_{z}=\frac{R}{1-z_{d+1}}\left[(z-z_{d+1}e_{d+1})\left(\frac{e-z_{d+1}z}{1-z_{d+1}^{2}}\right)^{T}\right.\\
&\hskip 113.81102pt\left.+I_{d+1}-zz^{T}-(e-z_{d+1}z)\left(\frac{e-z_{d+1}z}{1-z_{d+1}^{2}}\right)^{T}\right]\;.\end{split}

This expression is obtained by computing the differential in the ambient space
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
and subsequently projecting onto the tangent space of
\mathsf{S}^{d}
.
The posterior density
p_{1|t}
and its score are then derived similarly to
Section

B.2
.

Heavy-tail metric

The heavy-tail metric reported on
Figure

6
is defined in e.g.
[
3
]
. We include it here for completeness.
Given
n
exact samples from
\pi_{1}
,
(x_{i})_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}
, for every
1\leqslant j\leqslant d
, order the
j
-th components of the samples, so we have
x_{1}^{j}\leqslant...\leqslant x_{n}^{j}
. Then, if
(\tilde{x}_{i})_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}
are approximate samples, set for any
\xi\in\left[0,1\right]
:

\text{MSLE}(\xi)=\frac{1}{d\lceil(1-\xi)n\rceil}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{i=1}^{\lceil(1-\xi)n\rceil}\left(\log x_{n-i+1}^{j}-\log\tilde{x}_{n-i+1}^{j}\right)^{2}\;,

(85)

granted every
x_{n-i+1}^{j},\tilde{x}_{n-i+1}^{j},1\leqslant j\leqslant d,1\leqslant i\leqslant\lceil(1-\xi)n\rceil
is non-negative. By setting
\xi
close to

, this metric measures the discrepancy between tail samples of the target distribution and approximate ones. In our numerical experiments, we set

.

B.4
Grassmann manifold

We introduce the properties of the Grassmann manifold required to run FRIPS, and specify the hyperparameters used in the corresponding experiment (
Section

5.3
).

Definition and basic properties

We refer to
[
6
]
for a comprehensive introduction.
The Grassmann manifold
\mathrm{Gr}(n,p)
is the set of all
p
-dimensional linear subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
It can be identified with the set of rank-
p
orthogonal projectors
P\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, satisfying
P^{T}=P
and
P^{2}=P
.
An alternative and useful viewpoint is to see
\mathrm{Gr}(n,p)
as a quotient of the orthogonal group
\mathsf{O}(n)
, the group of
n\times n
matrices
Q
such that
Q^{T}Q=I_{n}
(the identity matrix of
\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
Since
\mathsf{O}(n)
is a compact Lie group, its quotients naturally inherit a smooth manifold structure.

To make this construction explicit, consider the
Stiefel manifold

\mathrm{St}(n,p)
, defined as the set of
n\times p
matrices
U
satisfying
U^{T}U=I_{p}
.
Such matrices are obtained by retaining the first
p
columns of an element of
\mathsf{O}(n)
.
Each point
P\in\mathrm{Gr}(n,p)
then corresponds to an equivalence class of matrices
U\in\mathrm{St}(n,p)
through the projection map
\pi(U)\coloneqq UU^{T}=P
.

The Grassmann manifold
\mathrm{Gr}(n,p)
is a compact, embedded submanifold of
\mathrm{Sym}(n)
, the space of
n\times n
symmetric matrices, with dimension
d=p(n-p)
.
Equipped with its canonical Riemannian structure,
\mathrm{Gr}(n,p)
is complete and connected
[
6
, Section 4.1]
, and therefore satisfies
1
.

In practice, it is computationally advantageous to work with
n\times p
Stiefel representatives rather than
n\times n
projectors.
Importantly, both the exponential and logarithm maps on
\mathrm{Gr}(n,p)
can be computed entirely using Stiefel representatives and their tangent spaces; see
[
6
, Proposition 3.3 and Algorithm 5.3]
.

Let
U\in\mathrm{St}(n,p)
.
The tangent space
T_{U}\mathrm{St}(n,p)
admits an orthogonal decomposition into a vertical space
\mathsf{Ver}_{U}\mathrm{St}(n,p)
, defined as the kernel of
\mathrm{d}\pi_{U}
, and a horizontal space
\mathsf{Hor}_{U}\mathrm{St}(n,p)
, given by its orthogonal complement with respect to the Riemannian metric on
\mathrm{St}(n,p)
.
The tangent space at
P=UU^{T}\in\mathrm{Gr}(n,p)
can then be identified with
\mathsf{Hor}_{U}\mathrm{St}(n,p)
.
As a consequence, any tangent vector
\Delta\in T_{P}\mathrm{Gr}(n,p)
admits a
horizontal lift

\Delta_{U}^{\mathsf{hor}}\in\mathsf{Hor}_{U}\mathrm{St}(n,p)
, which can be written as

\Delta_{U}^{\mathsf{hor}}=U_{\perp}\Delta\;,

(86)

where
U_{\perp}
denotes any orthogonal completion of
U
.

Posterior density

Following
[
6
, Section 5.1]
, the cut time at
P
in direction
\Delta\in T_{P}\mathrm{Gr}(n,p)
is

\mathbf{c}_{P}(\Delta)=\frac{\pi}{2\sigma_{1}}\;,

(87)

with
\sigma_{1}
the largest the singular value of
\Delta_{U}^{h}
, where
U\in\mathrm{St}(n,p)
is any Stiefel representative of
P
. From
Proposition

4
, the cut time defines the support
\mathsf{O}_{t}(x_{1})
of the density
p_{t|1}(\cdot|x_{1})
for every
t\left[0,1\right),\,x_{1}\in\mathrm{Gr}(n,p)
.

Then, relying on the symmetric space structure of
\mathrm{Gr}(n,p)
, the Jacobian determinant of
\mathrm{Exp}_{P}
at
\Delta
can be computed from the singular values of
\Delta_{U}^{h}
. From (
59
), we can derive
[
12
, Section 4.2.1]
:

\operatorname{Jac}_{P,\Delta}=\prod_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant q}\mathrm{sinc}(\sigma_{i}+\sigma_{j})\mathrm{sinc}(\sigma_{i}-\sigma_{j})\prod_{i=1}^{q}\mathrm{sinc}(\sigma_{i})^{|n-2p|}\;,

(88)

where
x\mapsto\mathrm{sinc}(x)=\sin(x)/x
and
\sigma_{i},\,1\leqslant i\leqslant q\coloneqq\min(p,n-p)
, denote the singular values of
\Delta_{U}^{\mathsf{hor}}
counted with multiplicity one. A closed-form expression of
\det\mathscr{A}
then follows from (
58
), and as a consequence, we obtain a closed-form expression of the density
p_{t|1}
of
Proposition

4
as well.

Notice that the Jacobian determinant (
88
)
does not
vanish on the boundary of the injectivity domain, which corresponds to tangent vectors
\Delta
such that
\sigma_{1}=\pi/2
. This differs from the
\mathsf{S}^{d}
case, where we easily check that the Jacobian term
does vanish
: as a result, the density
p_{t|1}
from
Proposition

4
is continuous on
\mathsf{S}^{d}
, but not on
\mathrm{Gr}(n,p)
, where it only continuous on its support.

Score of the posterior density

To use a first-order posterior sampling scheme like MALA within FRIPS, we need to compute the score
x_{1}\mapsto\mathrm{grad}\log p_{t|1}(x_{t}|x_{1})
,
x_{t}\in\mathrm{Gr}(n,p),t\in\left[0,1\right)
being fixed. We suggest using automatic differentiation, using the closed-form expression of
p_{t|1}
derived above. Since we are working with Stiefel representatives, we actually compute the usual Euclidean gradient of
\log p_{t|1}(x_{t}|\cdot)\circ\pi
, with
\pi
the projector
\mathrm{St}(n,p)\subset\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}\rightarrow\mathrm{Gr}(n,p)
, using automatic differentiation. Then, we project the result to
\mathsf{Hor}_{\tilde{x}_{1}}\mathrm{St}(n,p)
, where
\tilde{x}_{1}
is a Stiefel representative of
x_{1}
. We refer to
[
6
, Section 3.3]
for a precise justification.

Sampling from
\nu_{1|t}

Similar to the sphere case, the distribution
\nu_{1|t}(\cdot|x_{t})
, with density
x_{1}\mapsto p_{t|1}(x_{t}|x_{1})
for a fixed
x_{t}\in\mathrm{Gr}(n,p)
, is actually the pushforward measure
\psi_{t}(\cdot;x_{t})_{\sharp}\pi_{0}
, when
\pi_{0}
is the uniform distribution on
\mathrm{Gr}(n,p)
. The justification is similar (see
Section

B.2
), noticing that the horizontal lifts of
\mathrm{Log}_{x_{1}}x_{t}
and
\mathrm{Log}_{x_{t}}x_{1}
share the same singular values: this follows directly from the computation of the logarithm
[
6
, Algorithm 5.3]
. As a consequence, it holds
\det\mathscr{A}_{x_{1},\xi_{t}}=\det\mathscr{A}_{x_{t},\tilde{\xi}_{t}}
, with
\xi_{t}=\mathrm{Log}_{x_{1}}x_{t}
and
\tilde{\xi}_{t}=\mathrm{Log}_{x_{t}}x_{1}
.

Mixture of Gaussian distributions on
\mathrm{Gr}(n,p)
: hyperparameters

We set
\sigma=0.25
, and a mode distance
\operatorname{dist}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})=2.18
and
\operatorname{dist}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})=2.45
for the low and high mode-sepration regimes, respectively.
We use
K=128
uniform timesteps ranging from
t_{0}
to
0.99
, with one MALA chain of length
M=32
at each posterior sampling step. The IMH initialization (
Algorithm

5
) uses
M=256
steps, where each evaluation of the posterior
p_{t_{0}}
is estimated with a batch of
512
samples from
\nu_{1|t_{0}}
.

As explained in
Section

5.3
, Tweedie’s formula (
29
) cannot be applied here due to the discontinuity exhibited above, which is why we settle for a zero-th order scheme for the initialization at
t_{0}
.

B.5
Euclidean space

In
Section

5.2
, we run FRIPS in the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which obviously satisfies
1
. This particular setting is closely related to e.g.
[
22
]
, and not the main object of this paper. Still, for completeness, we provide some details below.

Posterior density

The interpolation process (
11
) is simply
X_{t}=tX_{1}+(1-t)X_{0}
, with
X_{0}\sim\pi_{0}=\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I_{d}),\,\sigma>0
. A simple change of variable shows that for every
t,x_{t}
,

p_{t|1}(x_{t}|x_{1})=\mathcal{N}(x_{t};tx_{1},(1-t)^{2}\sigma^{2}I_{d})\;,

(89)

which is consistent with
Proposition

4
.

Validity of
3

We can check the technical assumption
3
, assuming the target density
p_{1}
has a finite first moment, which ensures
3
-
(ii)
, and that it has regularity
C^{1}
.

Let
t\in\left[0,1\right)
. For every
x_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, notice that
p_{t}(x_{t})=\int p_{t|1}(x_{t}|x_{1})p_{1}(x_{1})\mathrm{d}x_{1}
is non-negative and
C^{1}
using the Leibniz rule and the fact that for every
x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},x_{t}\mapsto p_{t|1}(x_{t}|x_{1})
is
C^{1}
and bounded. Then,

x_{t}\mapsto\mathbf{u}_{t}(x_{t})=\frac{1}{p_{t}(x_{t})}\int\frac{x_{1}-x_{t}}{1-t}p_{t|1}(x_{t}|x_{1})p_{1}(x_{1})\mathrm{d}x_{1}

(90)

is
C^{1}
using the Leibniz rule and the first moment assumption on
p_{1}
. Similarly, it is continuous in the
t
variable,
x_{t}
being fixed. Thus,
3
-
(i)
holds.

C.1
Proof of
Proposition

1

Proof.

(i)

Under
1
,
[
33
, Theorem 16.34]
ensures that
\rho_{\mathbf{g}}(\mathrm{Cut}(x_{1}))=0
which implies by the Fubini theorem that
\rho_{\mathbf{g}}\otimes\rho_{\mathbf{g}}(\mathsf{D}^{\complement})=\int\mathrm{d}\rho_{\mathbf{g}}(x_{1})\rho_{\mathbf{g}}(\mathrm{Cut}(x_{1}))=0
. Then for
\rho_{\mathbf{g}}\otimes\rho_{\mathbf{g}}
-almost all
(x_{0},x_{1})
,
t\in\left[0,1\right]\mapsto\psi_{t}(x_{0};x_{1})
is the unique minimizing geodesic between
x_{0}
and
x_{1}
, hence smooth by definition of geodesics
[
33
, Chapter 4]
.

(ii)

Let
t\in\left[0,1\right)
and
x_{1}\in\mathsf{M}
. Under
1
, the map
v\in\mathrm{ID}(x_{1})\mapsto\mathrm{Exp}_{x_{1}}v
is a smooth diffeomorphism onto its image
\mathsf{M}\setminus\mathrm{Cut}(x_{1})

[
33
, Proposition 5.19, Theorem 10.34]
, and its inverse
x\mapsto\mathrm{Log}_{x_{1}}x
defines a diffeomorphism from
\mathsf{M}\setminus\mathrm{Cut}(x_{1})
to
\mathrm{ID}(x_{1})
. Furthermore
v\in\mathrm{ID}(x_{1})\mapsto(1-t)v
is a diffeomorphism valued in
\mathrm{ID}(x_{1})
by definition of
\mathrm{ID}(x_{1})
and since
t\neq 1
. We conclude that
\psi_{t}(\cdot;x_{1})
is a diffeomorphism from
\mathsf{M}\setminus\mathrm{Cut}(x_{1})
into its image. The map defined in (
9
) is actually well defined for every
x_{t}\in\mathsf{M}\setminus\mathrm{Cut}(x_{1})
, and we easily verify using the properties of the exponential and logarithm maps that it is the desired inverse map when restricted to
\mathsf{O}_{t}(x_{1})
.

(iii)

The fact that
\mathsf{O}_{t}(x_{1})
is open is an immediate consequence of the fact that
\psi_{t}(\cdot;x_{1})
is a diffeomorphism and
\mathsf{M}\setminus\mathrm{Cut}(x_{1})
is open
[
33
, Theorem 16.34]
. Regarding the last point, any point
x_{t}\in\mathsf{M}\setminus\mathrm{Cut}(x_{1})
satisfies
\operatorname{dist}(x_{1},x_{t})<(1-t)\mathbf{c}_{x_{1}}\left(\frac{\mathrm{Log}_{x_{1}}x_{t}}{\operatorname{dist}(x_{1},x_{t})}\right)
if and only if
\mathrm{Log}_{x_{1}}x_{t}/(1-t)\in\mathrm{ID}(x_{1})
by definition of the cut time, and this statement is equivalent to

\mathrm{Log}_{x_{1}}\mathrm{Exp}_{x_{1}}(\mathrm{Log}_{x_{1}}x_{t}/(1-t))=\mathrm{Log}_{x_{1}}x_{t}/(1-t)\;,

(91)

which rewrites as
x_{t}=\psi_{t}(\psi_{t}^{-1}(x_{t};x_{1});x_{1})\in\mathsf{O}_{t}(x_{1})
.

∎

C.2
Proof of
Lemma

1

Proof.

By
Proposition

1
,
t\in\left[0,1\right]\mapsto\psi_{t}(x_{0};x_{1})
is smooth for almost every
(x_{0},x_{1})\in\mathsf{M}\times\mathsf{M}
; hence the conditional vector field
t\in\left[0,1\right]\mapsto\dot{X}_{t}=\dot{\psi_{t}}(X_{0};X_{1})\in T_{X_{t}}\mathsf{M}
is
\pi_{0,1}
-almost surely well-defined.

Recall that, by (
7
), for every
(x_{0},x_{1})\in\mathsf{D},\,t\in\left[0,1\right)
,
\dot{\psi}_{t}(x_{0};x_{1})
is the velocity vector at time
t
of the minimizing geodesic
\dot{\upgamma}_{x_{0}}^{x_{1}}
. Denote
x_{t}=\upgamma_{x_{0}}^{x_{1}}(t)
and notice that the time-shift
s\in\left[0,1\right]\mapsto\upgamma_{x_{0}}^{x_{1}}(s(1-t)+t)
coincides with the minimizing geodesic
s\in\left[0,1\right]\mapsto\upgamma_{x_{t}}^{x_{1}}(s)
, as
0\leqslant s(1-t)+t\leqslant 1
for every
s\in\left[0,1\right]
, by uniqueness of
\upgamma_{x_{0}}^{x_{1}}
. Therefore, since
\dot{\upgamma}_{x_{t}}^{x_{1}}(0)=\mathrm{Log}_{x_{t}}{x_{1}}
by definition of minimizing geodesics and the logarithm map, we have
(1-t)\dot{\upgamma}_{x_{0}}^{x_{1}}(t)=\mathrm{Log}_{x_{t}}x_{1}
. Thus, almost surely, (
16
) holds.
∎

C.3
Proof of
Proposition

2

Proof.

By
Lemma

1
, for any smooth function with compact support
f:\left(0,1\right)\times\mathsf{M}\to\mathbb{R}
, almost surely, we have for every
t\in\left(0,1\right)
,

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}f(t,X_{t})=\partial_{t}f(t,X_{t})+\left\langle\mathrm{grad}_{x}f(t,X_{t}),\dot{X}_{t}\right\rangle_{X_{t}}\;,

(92)

hence

\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}f(t,X_{t})\right]=\mathbb{E}[\partial_{t}f(t,X_{t})]+\mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle\mathrm{grad}_{x}f(t,X_{t}),\mathbb{E}[\dot{X}_{t}|X_{t}]\right\rangle_{X_{t}}\right]\;.

(93)

Integrating over
\left(0,1\right)
, the left-hand term vanishes, yielding

\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathsf{M}}\partial_{t}f(t,x)\mathrm{d}\pi_{t}(x)\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathsf{M}}\left\langle\mathrm{grad}_{x}f(t,x),\mathbf{u}_{t}(x)\right\rangle_{x}\mathrm{d}\pi_{t}(x)\mathrm{d}t=0\;,

(94)

completing the proof.

∎

C.4
Proof of
Proposition

4

Proof.

Let
t\in\left[0,1\right)
and
x_{1}\in\mathsf{M}
.
Let
f:\mathsf{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a bounded continuous function. Since
\pi_{t|1}(\cdot|x_{1})=\psi_{t}(\cdot;x_{1})_{\sharp}\pi_{0|1}(\cdot|x_{1})
,
\psi_{t}(\cdot;x_{1})
being well-defined almost everywhere, we have

\int_{\mathsf{M}}f(x_{t})\pi_{t|1}(x_{t}|x_{1})=\int_{\mathsf{M}}f\circ\psi_{t}(x_{0};x_{1})p_{0|1}(x_{0}|x_{1})\rho_{\mathbf{g}}(\mathrm{d}x_{0})\;.

(95)

We write this integral using spherical coordinates centred at
x_{1}
: see
Equation

24
.

\int_{S_{x_{1}}\mathsf{M}}\int_{0}^{\mathbf{c}_{x_{1}}(\xi)}f\left(\psi_{t}(\mathrm{Exp}_{x_{1}}s\xi;x_{1})\right)p_{0|1}\left(\mathrm{Exp}_{x_{1}}s\xi|x_{1}\right)\det\mathscr{A}_{x_{1},\xi}(s)\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}\mu_{x_{1}}(\xi)\;.

(96)

Now, we can use the expression of
\psi_{t}
to simplify the Riemannian exponential and logarithm maps, yielding

\displaystyle\int_{S_{x_{1}}\mathsf{M}}\int_{0}^{\mathbf{c}_{x_{1}}(\xi)}f\left(\mathrm{Exp}_{x_{1}}s(1-t)\xi\right)p_{0|1}\left(\mathrm{Exp}_{x_{1}}s\xi|x_{1}\right)\det\mathscr{A}_{x_{1},\xi}(s)\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}\mu_{x_{1}}(\xi)

(97)

\displaystyle=\int_{S_{x_{1}}\mathsf{M}}\int_{0}^{\mathbf{c}_{x_{1}}(\xi)(1-t)}f\left(\mathrm{Exp}_{x_{1}}s\xi\right)p_{0|1}\left(\mathrm{Exp}_{x_{1}}\frac{s}{1-t}\xi|x_{1}\right)\det\mathscr{A}_{x_{1},\xi}\left(\frac{s}{1-t}\right)\frac{\mathrm{d}s}{1-t}\mathrm{d}\mu_{x_{1}}(\xi)

(98)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&=\int_{S_{x_{1}}\mathsf{M}}\int_{0}^{\mathbf{c}_{x_{1}}(\xi)}\mathbbm{1}\left(\frac{s}{1-t}\xi\in\mathrm{ID}(x_{1})\right)f\left(\mathrm{Exp}_{x_{1}}s\xi\right)p_{0|1}\left(\mathrm{Exp}_{x_{1}}\frac{s}{1-t}\xi|x_{1}\right)\\
&\hskip 227.62204pt\det\mathscr{A}_{x_{1},\xi}\left(\frac{s}{1-t}\right)\frac{\mathrm{d}s}{1-t}\mathrm{d}\mu_{x_{1}}(\xi)\;,\end{split}

where we first applied a linear change of variable
s\leftarrow s(1-t)
, and then used that for a given
\xi\in S_{x_{1}}\mathsf{M}
,
s\xi\in\mathrm{ID}(x_{1})
if and only if
s<\mathbf{c}_{x_{1}}(\xi)
. Finally, we recognize the expression of
\psi_{t}^{-1}(\cdot;x_{1})
, and note that
\det\mathscr{A}_{x_{1},\xi}\neq 0
when
s\xi\in\mathrm{ID}(x_{1})

[
9
, Section III.2]
, to go back to an integral on
\mathsf{M}
. Using the variable
x_{t}=\mathrm{Exp}_{x_{1}}s\xi
and denoting
\mathrm{Log}_{x_{1}}x_{t}/\operatorname{dist}(x_{1},x_{t})=\xi_{t}
, we get

\displaystyle\begin{split}&=\int_{S_{x_{1}}\mathsf{M}}\int_{0}^{\mathbf{c}_{x_{1}}(\xi)}\mathbbm{1}\left(\frac{s}{1-t}\xi\in\mathrm{ID}(x_{1})\right)f\left(\mathrm{Exp}_{x_{1}}s\xi\right)p_{0|1}\left(\mathrm{Exp}_{x_{1}}\frac{1}{1-t}\mathrm{Log}_{x_{1}}\mathrm{Exp}_{x_{1}}s\xi|x_{1}\right)\\
&\hskip 227.62204pt\frac{\mathscr{A}_{x_{1},\xi}\left(\frac{s}{1-t}\right)}{\mathscr{A}_{x_{1},\xi}(s)}\frac{\mathrm{d}s}{1-t}\mathrm{d}\mu_{x_{1}}(\xi)\end{split}

\displaystyle\begin{split}&=\int_{\mathsf{M}}\mathbbm{1}\left(\frac{\mathrm{Log}_{x_{1}}x_{t}}{1-t}\in\mathrm{ID}(x_{1})\right)f(x_{t})p_{0|1}\left(\mathrm{Exp}_{x_{1}}\frac{1}{1-t}\mathrm{Log}_{x_{1}}x_{t}|x_{1}\right)\\
&\hskip 227.62204pt\frac{\det\mathscr{A}_{x_{1},\xi_{t}}\left(\frac{\operatorname{dist}(x_{t},x_{1})}{1-t}\right)}{(1-t)\det\mathscr{A}_{x_{1},\xi_{t}}(\operatorname{dist}(x_{t},x_{1}))}\rho_{\mathbf{g}}(\mathrm{d}x_{t})\end{split}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathsf{M}}f(x_{t})\mathbbm{1}_{\mathsf{O}_{t}(x_{1})}(x_{t})p_{0|1}\left(\psi_{t}^{-1}(x_{t};x_{1})|x_{1}\right)\operatorname{Jac}_{t}(x_{t};x_{1})\rho_{\mathbf{g}}(\mathrm{d}x_{t})\;,

(99)

where
\operatorname{Jac}_{t}(x_{t};x_{1})
is defined for every
x_{t}\in\mathsf{O}_{t}(x_{1})
as

\operatorname{Jac}_{t}(x_{t};x_{1})=\frac{\det\mathscr{A}_{x_{1},\xi_{t}}\left(\frac{\operatorname{dist}(x_{t},x_{1})}{1-t}\right)}{(1-t)\det\mathscr{A}_{x_{1},\xi_{t}}\left(\operatorname{dist}(x_{t},x_{1})\right)}\;.

(100)

This proves that
\pi_{t|1}(\cdot|x_{1})
has a density with respect to
\rho_{\mathbf{g}}
, given by

p_{t|1}(x_{t}|x_{1})=\mathbbm{1}(x_{t}\in\mathsf{O}_{t}(x_{1}))p_{0|1}(\psi_{t}^{-1}(x_{t};x_{1})|x_{1})\operatorname{Jac}_{t}(x_{t};x_{1})\;.

(101)

Since
x_{t}\in\mathsf{O}_{t}(x_{1})\mapsto\psi_{t}^{-1}(x_{t};x_{1})
is smooth, as well as
(s,\xi)\mapsto\mathscr{A}_{x_{1},\xi}(s)
(from
[
9
, Equation III.1.4]
, it is defined as the solution of a second-order ODE with smooth coefficients), this density is smooth on
\mathsf{O}_{t}(x_{1})
granted smoothness of
p_{0|1}(\cdot|x_{1}).

∎
