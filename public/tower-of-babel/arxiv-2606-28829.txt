On Carmichael numbers of the form 2 n ​ p m + 1 2^{n}p^{m}+1
Florian Luca

Abstract
Here, we show that if m ≥ 5 m\geq 5 is fixed and odd, then there are only finitely many Carmichael numbers of the form 2 n ​ p m + 1 2^{n}p^{m}+1 for positive integers n n and prime p p .

Introduction

A positive integer
N
is Carmichael if it composite and
a^{N}\equiv a\pmod{N}
holds for all integers
a
. It is known that there are infinitely many Carmichael numbers. They are all odd, squarefree and are characterized by the property that
p-1\mid N-1
for all prime factors
p
of
N
. Every such number is of the form
2^{n}k+1
for some positive integer
n
and odd integer
k
. Let

{\mathcal{K}}:=\{k~{\text{\rm odd}}:2^{n}k+1~{\text{\rm is~Carmichael~for~some~positive~integer}}~n\},

The set
{\mathcal{K}}
has been investigated in a few papers. For example, in
[
3
]
, it is shown that the smallest element in
{\mathcal{K}}
is
27
. It comes from the Carmichael number
1729=2^{6}\times 3^{3}+1
which also happens to be the famous Ramanujan taxicab number. In
[
1
]
, it was shown that
{\mathcal{K}}
does not contain primes, in
[
6
]
it was shown that
{\mathcal{K}}
does not contain squares of primes and in
[
5
]

it was shown that the only prime
p
such that
p^{3}\in{\mathcal{K}}
is
p=3
.
This time we are studying numbers of the form
p^{m}
in
{\mathcal{K}}
for some
m\geq 4
and prove the following theorem.

Theorem 1
.

If
m\geq 5
is odd then there only finitely many primes
p
such that
p^{m}\in{\mathcal{K}}
.

Our proof uses a result of Corvaja and Zannier from
[
4
]
whose proof is based on the Schmidt’s Subspace Theorem and therefore the proof is not effective.

The proof of Theorem
1

In Theorem 1 in
[
3
]
, it was shown that if
k\geq 3
is odd and is such that
N=2^{n}k+1
is Carmichael, then

n<2^{2\times 10^{7}\tau(k)^{2}(\log k)^{2}\omega(k)},

where
\omega(k),~\tau(k)
are the number of distinct prime factors of
k
and the total number of positive divisors of
k
, respectively. In particular, if
k=p^{m}
and
m
is fixed, then

n<2^{2\times 10^{7}(m+1)^{2}m^{2}(\log p)^{2}}.

Thus, if we fix
m
and show that
p
can take only finitely many values it will follow that there are only finitely many Carmichael numbers of the form
2^{n}p^{m}+1
for some prime
p
. This is what we will prove when
m
is odd.

2.1
Bounding
N

We follow the method of proof of Theorem 1 in
[
3
]
. We assume
m\geq 5
and odd,
p>m
is prime and let
N:=2^{n}p^{m}+1
. Assume that
N
is Carmichael and write

N=\prod_{i=1}^{k}(2^{a_{i}}p^{b_{i}}+1),\qquad 1\leq a_{i}\leq n,~0\leq b_{i}\leq m,

(1)

where

q_{i}:=2^{a_{i}}p^{b_{i}}+1

.
The primes

for which

1
) of

[
3
]
shows that

N_{0}:=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i\leq k\\
b_{i}=0\end{subarray}}q_{i}\leq\prod_{j=0}^{\alpha}(2^{2^{j}}-1)=2^{2^{\alpha+1}}-1<(p^{2m})^{2}=p^{4m}.

(2)

Next we show that
N/N_{0}>1
. Indeed, for if not, we have

N=N_{0}=\prod_{i=1}^{k}(2^{a_{i}}+1),

where we may assume that
1\leq a_{1}<a_{2}<\cdots<a_{k}

a_{i}
are powers of
2

, we have
a_{1}+1\leq a_{2}\leq n

which is a contradiction. Thus, putting

N_{1}:=N/N_{0},

(3)

we have that
N_{1}>1
. Next let
i
be such that

q_{i}=2^{a_{i}}p^{b_{i}}+1

. Assume first that

and
2^{a_{i}}p^{b_{i}}
are multiplicatively dependent. It is easy to see that this happens if and only if there exists an integer

2^{n}p^{m}=\rho^{t}\qquad{\text{\rm and}}\qquad 2^{a_{i}}p^{b_{i}}=\rho^{\lambda}\qquad{\text{\rm for~some}}\qquad 1\leq\lambda<t.

The condition that
q_{i}\mid N
implies

\rho^{\lambda}+1\mid\rho^{t}+1\quad{\text{\rm which leads to}}\quad\lambda\mid t\quad{\text{\rm and}}\quad t/\lambda\equiv 1\pmod{2},

and the condition that

is prime implies that

is a power of

in the factorization of

, we get that

is the largest power of
2
dividing

.
Since

is odd, we get that

, so

. Thus,

for some integer

2^{n}p^{m}+1=(2^{u}p+1)((2^{u}p)^{m-1}-(2^{u}p)^{m-2}+\cdots-2^{u}p+1).

Let
q
be any prime factor of the second factor above. We then get that
(2^{u}p)^{m}\equiv-1\pmod{q}
, so the order of
2^{u}p
modulo

is even and a divisor of
2m
. However, since

is one of the

’s it follows that this order is a divisor of
q_{i}-1\mid 2^{n}p^{m}
. Since

, this order must be exactly

divides

. Thus,

q

2^{u}p+1

((2^{u}p)^{m}+1)/(2^{u}p+1)

, a contradiction.

Lemma 2

If

(4)

If

. If

and

If

, otherwise divisibilities (

) and (

\displaystyle 2^{a_{i}}p^{b_{i}}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle-1\pmod{q_{i}},

\displaystyle 2^{n}p^{m}

\displaystyle-1\pmod{q_{i}}.

Note that

We now follow the argument of Lemma 3 in
[

(7)

Look at the numbers

\displaystyle\frac{(2m+1)nX^{1/2}}{X-1}\leq\frac{(2m+2)n}{X^{1/2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(2m+2){\sqrt{n\log p}}.

Raising the first congruence in (

\displaystyle N_{1,s}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2^{(2m+2){\sqrt{n\log p}}}e^{m{\sqrt{n\log p}}}+1=2^{(2m+2+m/\log 2){\sqrt{n\log p}}}+1

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle 2^{(3.5m+2){\sqrt{n\log p}}+1}<2^{4m{\sqrt{n\log p}}}.

Hence,

2^{n}p^{m}<2^{n}p^{m}+1=N=N_{0}N_{1}<2^{4m(m+1){\sqrt{n\log p}}}p^{4m},

Recalling that

This gives
