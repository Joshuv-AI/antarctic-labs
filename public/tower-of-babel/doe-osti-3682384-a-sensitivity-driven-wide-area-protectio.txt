1 Executive Summary
Traditionally, power system generation sources have been composed of synchronous
generators, of which the fault current behavior is understood with minimal differences
between generation size and types due to the physics of their construction. Present
protection schemes and modeling methods are based upon these understood
characteristics. Most renewable generation is composed of inverter-based resources
(IBR), in which fault current is determined by switching control software and hardware
limitations, each of which can vary between manufacturers and even between models of
the same manufacturer. The resulting fault current is low in magnitude, low in negative-
sequence current, unpredictable phase angles, and is a challenge to model. These
characteristics also result in a challenge to traditional protection schemes and fault
simulation software. To address several of these concerns, the project has the following
goals:
1. Improve IBR models: Improve IBR models used in short circuit (SC) programs to
 accurately capture the response of IBRs at the bulk power system (BPS) level for fault
 and protection studies.
2. Develop automation tool: Develop an automation tool that allows engineers to
 identify protection coordination and sensitivity issues by performing SC and protection
 coordination studies in a high IBR-penetrated grid by applying variations to the IBR
 models, faults, contingencies, etc.
3. Develop schemes: Develop new protection mitigation solution schemes that
 complement the existing protection systems to ensure safe operation of the BPS with
 higher IBR penetration levels. The project team did not achieve this final goal, as the
 Department of Energy (DOE) stopped the project early due to changes in DOE funding
 priorities. The termination notice came at the beginning of the final project phase, while
 the team was identifying and beginning to investigate protection issues. It should be
 noted that the team discussed a 100% penetration scenario. However, this scenario
 would require the use of grid-forming IBR models that are not presently available.
 Since developing these models requires additional effort, the 100% penetration
 scenario was not pursued during this project. In the future, developing the
 methodology and models for the 100% scenario could benefit the industry.
Completion of the goals would result in accurate IBR models for use in commercial fault
simulation software and a protection tool with automated coordination capabilities, which
resolve protection issues relating to high penetration IBRs. The inclusion of protection
tools within existing commercial fault simulation software would provide a familiarity and
relatively easy incorporation with existing practices without having to learn a new or use
a separate simulation platform.
The project divided work into the following six tasks, of which the first four were
completed:
1. Utility Perspective on IBR Protection Challenges and Impacts: Gathered data
 related to protection challenges posed by IBRs.

 Page 6 of 7

2. IBR Model Improvement: Developed an accurate IBR model for SC simulation
 software.
3. IBR Model Validation: Validated the proposed IBR SC model against a PSCAD
 model and the results from inverter power testing in a laboratory hardware-in-the-loop
 (HIL) test setup.
4. Sensitivity-driven Wide-area Protection (SWAP) Coordination Tool: Developed
 the tool to perform protection sensitivity analysis for high-penetration scenarios.
5. Protection Scheme Development: Develop the protection mitigation schemes that
 overcome the protection gaps associated with IBRs.
6. Solution Validation and Verifications using HIL Testing: Tested HIL using the
 protection mitigation options.
Task 1 included obtaining protective relay short circuit oscillography data and sending out
questionnaires to various utilities and industry entities, such as the North American
Transmission Forum (NATF). The project obtained protective relay oscillography data for
over 40 events over the life of the project, detailing various protection issues related to
IBRs. The protection questionnaire was sent to several entities to determine the
protection issues needing focus. The major issues highlighted were a lack of accurate
steady state IBR models, low IBR fault current, a lack of negative-sequence current
injection, incorrect faulted phase identification, and inconsistent phase relationship
between ground and negative-sequence currents. The highlighted issues result in
misoperation of phase and ground distance elements, along with directional phase and
ground overcurrent elements.
Task 2 included IBR model development and improvement. Four inverter manufacturers
were contacted to provide model diversity (Tesla, EPC, Power Electronics, and Hitachi).
Non-disclosure agreements (NDA) were signed with three of the manufacturers, allowing
the use of their Power Systems Computer-aided Design (PSCAD) “black box” models to
assist with the development of the new steady state IBR model (one of the manufacturers
did not require an NDA). The new IBR model consists of a dynamic link library (DLL)
format, which is compatible with commercial fault simulation software such as ASPEN
OneLiner. A total of three IBR models were developed.
Task 3 consisted of IBR model validation, which included comparisons against PSCAD
models and inverter power testing in a laboratory HIL test setup. Comparison of the
results from these tests showed a close match to the PSCAD and inverter power testing.
Task 4 developed the SWAP Coordination software tool, using the IBR model developed
above, and determined the protection gaps on an actual utility system model.
Development of the tool was substantially complete, and user testing was underway to
gather feedback on output behavior and usability. Various simulations were performed.
However, the project was terminated prior to the completion of user testing and a
comprehensive evaluation of the protection gaps. Tasks 5 and 6 were not performed due
to the project being terminated.
Overall, the project resulted in the identification of several protection issues related to
IBRs and the development of accurate steady-state DLL-based IBR models for use in

 Page 7 of 8

ASPEN OneLiner. The model was validated against PSCAD electromagnetic transient
(EMT) models and inverter power testing. An accurate IBR steady-state model that can
be applied with commercial fault simulation software results in more accurate fault data
that is used to determine equipment ratings, relay settings, and relay coordination. While
the SWAP tool was exercised using various simulation cases, additional refinements
identified through user feedback were not implemented due to early project termination.

2 Background
The following reports are applicable to this project:

• Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers (IEEE) Power System Relaying and
 Control Committee (PSRC), Technical Report by Working Group (WG) C32
 “Protection Challenges and Practices for Interconnected IBRs to Utility Transmission
    Systems,” 2021 [1]
• IEEE Power & Energy Society (PES) Technical Report PES-TR 78 “Modification of
 Commercial Fault Calculation Programs for Wind Turbine Generators,” 2020 [2]
• IEEE PES Technical Report PES TR-68 “Impact of Inverter-based Generation on Bulk
 Power System Dynamics and SC Performance,” 2018 [3]
• SAND2024-13494 Technical Report “Assessing IBR Modeling Gaps in Commonly
 Used SC Programs,” 2024 [4]
• SAND2020-0265 Technical Report “Impact of IBR Negative-sequence Current
 Injection on Transmission System Protection,” 2019 [5]
The PES TR-78 report [2] introduced the Voltage-controlled Current Source (VCCS)
method that is now commonly used in commercial software. However, this methodology
does not account for negative-sequence current injection capable IBRs. As a result, IBR
manufacturers must create detailed tables specifying voltages, currents, and phase
angles across various per-unit values and fault types over different timeframes—a
process that is often cumbersome and potentially impractical (refer to Figure 1 for VCCS
table examples).

 Figure 1. VCCS Tables

This project advances this method by introducing Windows DLL-based modeling
developed from the IBR manufacturer’s black box PSCAD file. This approach eliminates
the need for tables, supports negative-sequence current injection, and more accurately
represents the specific IBR. ASPEN OneLiner DLL modeling examples are provided in
Figure 2.

 Page 9 of 10

 Figure 2. ASPEN OneLiner DLL Model

The SAND2024-13494 report [4] presents a comparison of the modeling methods
employed in ASPEN, and related software such as CAPE, and CYME, specifically
examining the VCCS and current injection relay (CIR) models. The recommendation was
to adopt the VCCS model due to its adaptability for representing IBR non-linear
characteristics. However, it is unsuitable for IBRs with negative-sequence current
injection. The report also identified the DLL modeling method as a promising approach,
which aligns with the direction of this project.
PSRC C32 [1], PES TR-68 [3], and SAND2020-0265 [5] have collectively identified
challenges related to modeling and protection of IBRs, notably concerning phase
distance, phase overcurrent, and directional supervision using negative-sequence
quantities. These reports observed instances where phase distance elements either failed
to operate or operated incorrectly, negative-sequence elements reversed direction, and
insufficient fault current hindered phase overcurrent element operation. This project
employed DLL IBR models and developed a SWAP software tool to improve SC
protection simulations and help predict these identified issues. The project was
terminated prior to completion of the assessment of protection alternatives that could
effectively address these issues.

3 Project Objectives
Traditional protection schemes for BPS and fault simulation tools are designed around
the familiar behavior of synchronous generators. However, as renewable energy, and
battery storage sources that use IBRs become more common, these conventional
approaches will face new challenges, since IBRs have distinctly different fault current and
transient characteristics. This project seeks to address these issues, enabling greater
integration of renewables at higher levels while ensuring reliable grid protection.
Several problems arise with IBR generation, including the absence of suitable SC models
for commercial simulation software, the low fault currents produced by IBRs, and fault
current characteristics that are difficult to simulate accurately. These complications can
lead to incorrect settings for relays, causing them to mis-operate—either disconnecting
transmission components unnecessarily or leading to loss of IBR generation. While such
events may be minor individually, they can result in serious consequences if large
amounts of generation or essential equipment are affected, potentially triggering
widespread cascading outages.
The project’s main goals are:
1. To enhance IBR models in SC analysis programs so they better represent how IBRs
 behave on the BPS during faults and protection studies.
2. To create an automation tool that helps engineers pinpoint coordination and sensitivity
 issues in protection schemes, using comprehensive SC and protection studies with
 varying IBR models, faults, and contingencies.
3. To develop new protection solutions that work alongside existing systems, allowing
 safe operation of the BPS even at high IBR penetration rates. The protection solutions
 will cover line, bus, and transformer protection methods.
Upon achieving these objectives, the project will deliver accurate IBR models for fault
analysis, automated tools for protection coordination, and alternative mitigation
strategies. These advancements will facilitate more efficient, accurate, and grid-friendly
integration of IBR installations. Consequently, higher levels of IBR penetration can be
achieved, reducing outages caused by inadequate modeling or protection gaps, and
resulting in a more stable grid with greater renewable energy adoption.
The project is structured into seven tasks, with the final task depending on sufficient time
and budget.

4 Project Results and Discussion
4.1 Utilities’ Perspective on Protection Challenges with High Inverter-
 based Resource Penetration
The team completed the report on the utilities’ perspective on protection challenges
associated with higher IBR penetration. A paper on the subject was completed and
accepted by the Western Protective Relaying Conference (WPRC). Additionally, this topic
was published as a Sandia National Laboratories (SNL) publication, where 40+ IBR field
records from fault events were evaluated. The events are from Pacific Gas and Electric
Company (PG&E), Oncor, and Southern Company. These events were used to identify
protection issues associated with IBR generation and could be used to validate the IBR
models developed by this project.
The SNL report summarizes utilities’ experiences as they see a rapid increase in solar,
wind, and battery energy storage resources that interconnect to their electric system
through inverters. IBRs have fault current characteristics that are unlike the fault current
response of traditional rotating-machine-based generators, which are well known and
repeatable. IBR’s non-traditional fault current behavior is due to the IBR control scheme,
which is configured to provide a clean AC output but also protect the inverter’s sensitive
power electronics devices from damage, one source of which is overcurrent. This results
in low fault current magnitude, low or no negative-sequence current injection, the
variability of sequence component currents, the variability of voltage with respect to
current angles, and the lack of inertia. The control scheme also results in a fault current
response that can vary between manufacturers and between models of the same
manufacturer.
High penetration of IBRs can adversely affect the protection schemes applied in areas
with high penetration of IBRs. With the proliferation of IBRs, utilities are finding out that
conventional protection schemes need to evolve to continue efficiently and effectively
protecting electric power systems. This is mainly because the existing protection
elements and practices have been designed based on the fault current response of
conventional rotating machines. In several cases, the available literature does not provide
any clear solution for the issues when the protection scheme does not operate properly
near IBRs.
This report identified various protection challenges due to IBRs that the electric industry
is facing, from the utility perspective. Instead of focusing on one issue, the report looked
broadly at all the challenges that system protection has experienced with high penetration
of IBRs. Based on the IBR response from various utilities during real fault events and
gathering perspectives from different utility SMEs via questionnaire, the report
summarizes gathered data and internal experiences.
Challenges identified in the report are listed below, and most of them are backed by real
field events:

• Modeling challenges.

                                                                             Page 15 of 16

• Low fault current contribution.
• Negative-sequence quantities unavailable or unreliable for IBRs.
• Challenges with rapid frequency changes.
• Memory polarization issues.
• Distance protection performance.
• Faulted phase identification issues.
• Directional element performance issues.
• Apparent impedance oscillations.
• Wind turbine generator (WTG) challenges.
• Uncontrolled response challenges.
• Momentary cessation or zero-power mode challenges.
• Inadvertent tripping of inverters.
• Fault ride-through (FRT) issues.
• Issues with permissive overreaching transfer trip (POTT) schemes.
• Issues with blocking schemes.
• Challenges with power swing protection schemes.
• Interactions of IBRs with series-compensated transmission lines.
• Unintentional islanding issues.
• Issues with a large amount of IBRs on distribution affecting transmission.

4.2 Utilities’ Response to Questionnaire
A questionnaire was developed to gather information from SMEs of six different utilities
about protection challenges, modeling approaches, and fault responses, and to discuss
ideas on how they foresee solving the problem that higher penetrations of IBRs will pose
in the future. Based on the responses received a WPRC paper was developed and
shared.
Utilities are modeling the IBRs as synchronous machines and adjusting the R, X, and/or
current limits of the synchronous machine models. Other utilities have tried newer
modeling methods introduced by fault simulation software vendors (like ASPEN and
CAPE) but have moved away from using the new methods because these models are
still evolving. One of the issues with newer model types, like voltage-controlled current
sources, is that these newer models are removed when reducing the network or providing
a Thevenin equivalent. For the neighboring utilities, it is common to exchange the
Thevenin equivalent with each other, and utility companies need the ability to reduce the
network. One utility complained that Type 4 wind generator models shut down in software
(due to convergence issues).
Some utilities are adding tags to generators and distributed sources to identify what type
of generation is being modeled. This allows the generator of a particular type to be toggled
off/on in the future.

                                                                            Page 16 of 17

One international utility is using EMT analysis tools for modeling IBRs and plant-level
studies.
Utilities are beginning to establish guidelines for IBR modeling and are adopting evolving
IBR models. One utility is now modeling wind turbines as sources. There are also
questions about modeling the generation source as both PV and BESS.
On the protection side, utilities are also flagging the limited fault current from IBRs on
radial feeders as a major challenge. To address this, they are increasingly using current
differential protection or direct transfer trip schemes to ensure faults are cleared reliably.
There is also a concern that in case of a fault on a transmission with multiple IBRs, once
the transmission circuit breakers open, the multiple IBR anti-islanding detection
relays/elements may interfere with each other.
IBRs can continue to generate power during grid outages. This poses an anti-islanding
risk, where the IBR unintentionally operates as an unintended island.
Further, during disturbances, IBRs may experience momentary cessation or trip offline if
voltage or frequency deviates significantly (IBRs are sensitive to grid voltage and
frequency variations).
IBR controls often suppress the injection of unbalanced currents during faults, which
renders negative-sequence components used for fault detection unreliable. With IBRs
interconnecting to the transmission system, some utilities are relying on transfer trip-
based anti-islanding protection, which has been challenging to implement, especially
when there is a series of ring buses between two network sources.
Utilities are concerned that with high penetration, the system fault current will decrease,
and conventional protection schemes may be degraded or, in the worst case, may not
operate. SMEs think that synchronous generation will still be required for the existing
protection philosophy that relies on impedance-based protection or overcurrent
protection.
Utilities will have to employ the current differential protection scheme across the system
on all lines. However, there is concern about redundancy and the reliability of
communication paths.
IBRs may not be capable of generating sufficient negative sequence fault current with
guaranteed angles. Consequently, this desensitizes protection functions reliant on
negative-sequence components, such as polarization units in distance relays, which carry
over to communication-aided protection schemes, such as POTT and directional
comparison blocking (DCB) schemes.
High penetration of IBRs in power systems creates weak systems prone to fast power
swings. Conventional distance relays may be susceptible to these fast power swings,
potentially causing overtrips. They can generate off-nominal fault currents, which are

                                                                              Page 17 of 18

filtered out in the relays. These off-nominal currents or voltages can be dangerous to the
system but will not be detected by relays due to filtering.
Commercialized SC software, such as CAPE and ASPEN, commonly used in utilities for
protection coordination and breaker rating studies, lacks a comprehensive IBR model.
This deficiency introduces several uncertainties in grid operation and planning.
Utilities see protection challenges with higher IBR penetration, but they cannot quantify
them due to modeling challenges.
Utilities are not analyzing IBR responses for all the fault events. It has to do with the
timeliness of the event retrieval process and the effort and coordination required to get
the event from the IBR generation owner. There is also a lack of industry standards for
fault event data retrieval for IBRs.
One utility observed that upon request, IBR generation owners will provide records.
However, record retrieval may take a long time. PRC-030 [6] should address the fault
record issues with the timeliness of records.
Utilities are not receiving events from the IBR facilities and have a hard time capturing
anything of significance with their digital fault recorder (DFR) and phasor measurement
unit (PMU) infrastructure.
Model information comes very late from the manufacturers. This causes delays with SC
studies and relay settings.
Inverter manufacturers are reluctant to share the model with utilities. It is difficult to obtain
time domain models (PSCAD models) or phasor domain models unless an NDA is signed.
This can take a long time. Time domain models are needed to validate phase domain
model data.
Inverter manufacturers should collaborate with ASPEN and CAPE to develop a
comprehensive and reliable IBR model for SC and protection coordination studies.
Additionally, they should provide the necessary data required to model IBRs in these
software platforms accurately.

5 Inverter-based Resource Modeling Based on a New
  Approach Developed for Three Manufacturers
Lack of good inverter models for steady state fault simulation is a big industry issue, and
one of the major tasks for this project was to improve SC models for IBRs that can be
used for wide-area protection coordination (WAPC).
The SC behavior of IBRs is dependent on proprietary vendor-specific control schemes.
Although standards provide recommendations for IBR’s fault behavior during SCs,
manufacturers retain flexibility in how they design and implement fault-current control
strategies. As a result, different vendors’ inverters can exhibit markedly different
responses during SC events. This variability is a primary reason why the generic IBR
models commonly embedded in commercial SC analysis tools often fail to accurately
reproduce the fault behavior of real-world inverters from diverse manufacturers. To
address this limitation, a “tabular data-based fault model” was previously introduced by
PSRC Working Group ￼ ￼￼. This approach enables the representation of vendor-
specific behavior through manufacturer-provided lookup tables. While valuable in
concept, its practical implementation is challenging. A single vendor may use many
different fault-setting configurations, and creating comprehensive tables that cover all
possible settings is both complex and time-consuming. In addition, the negative-
sequence fault response is sometimes linked to the positive-sequence response, making
it even more difficult to produce separate tables for positive- and negative-sequence
quantities. Consequently, existing modeling frameworks still lack a robust, consistent
method for representing the accurate fault behavior of modern IBRs.
One possible solution to bridge this gap is the development of a “black box” DLL–based
SC model which is manufacturer-specific. This architecture enables IBR vendors to
embed their own fault-current control logic derived from a standardized DLL interface,
developed by SC software programs, without disclosing proprietary information. The SC
programs access the vendor-supplied DLL package and interface with it during iterative
fault calculations. The DLL model receives inputs such as the inverter’s terminal voltage,
active and reactive power, and pre-fault system data. Using these inputs, the model
executes the vendor-implemented control logic to compute the inverter’s fault current
contribution in a manner that accurately reflects its real-world behavior under faulted
conditions.
While the industry is still in the exploration phase of evaluating black box DLL-based SC
modeling, the project team has taken a leading role by developing and implementing the
first model of its kind in North America. Beyond conceptual development, the model has
undergone rigorous validation using a combination of offline time-domain simulations and
HIL testing, providing a high level of confidence in its accuracy, robustness, and practical
applicability for future protection and planning studies.
Under the project, the EMT models were acquired from three distinct IBR vendors (Power
Electronics, EPC, and Tesla) under an NDA. These models were black box EMT models,
meaning they did not disclose the detailed control schemes of the inverters. However,
they provided access to certain key information, such as basic FRT settings, which

                                                                            Page 19 of 20

allowed fault simulations under various conditions. While these fundamental FRT settings
were helpful for understanding the general fault characteristics, a more comprehensive
fault study was conducted using EMT simulations to confirm the FRT settings and further
analyze the fault response. Guided by this study, the project team developed a dynamic
phasor-based model, which serves as an essential bridge to allow the development of the
steady-state SC model from the EMT model or field data.

6 Dynamic Model Development
Since the EMT black box models acquired from the vendors did not disclose the details
about the control block diagrams or transfer functions, the dynamic model development
project used the WECC second-generation IBR control block model, which was adapted
to incorporate the specific FRT settings implemented by the three vendors. These settings
were determined through fault studies conducted under steady-state conditions using the
EMT models for each vendor. The WECC model was modified in several key ways to
reflect the unique fault detection methods, FRT curves, current injection logic, and
current-limiting strategies used by each vendor. Additionally, to simulate the vendors’
behavior during both close-in faults (which occur near the source of the fault) and remote
faults (which occur farther from the fault location), the WECC model was enhanced by
introducing concepts such as freezing the PLL and adding hysteresis (which will be
explained in detail later).
Beyond these modifications, the project also involved fine-tuning the WECC model’s
control block to match the time delays observed in the EMT responses from the individual
vendor models.
The result of these enhancements was the creation of dynamic black box models for each
vendor in the form of DLLs. Through dynamic modeling, valuable insights were gained
regarding which aspects of the control blocks influence the dynamic response and which
aspects affect steady-state performance. It is important to note that the dynamic black
box models are different than SC black box models.
A key finding was that the steady-state fault behavior of the IBR, especially under remote
fault conditions that result in higher terminal voltages, depends on the dynamic response
of the system. For instance, one of the vendors employs a method in which, once an FRT
condition is detected, the system locks into FRT mode using a timer. The dynamic
response at the end of the timer then determines whether the IBR will continue operating
in FRT mode or return to normal mode during steady state while the fault persists.
Dynamic modeling, therefore, plays an essential role in understanding how steady-state
behavior can be influenced by dynamic response. Additionally, in the case of close-in
faults in a weak system, the rotation of the phase angle was found to cause convergence
issues in the SC solution. Simulation of these scenarios in dynamic modeling showed that
the rotation of the phase angle was due to system instability rather than a numerical issue.
This finding highlights the importance of dynamic studies in distinguishing between
numerical difficulties and actual system stability issues, especially when convergence
problems arise in SC programs.
The Dual DLL approach, as shown in Figure 3, is employed to develop and study IBRs
dynamic behavior during a fault. This approach minimizes the effort of the IBR vendor to
maintain only one core logic model while developing simple wrapper DLLs for different
software programs to interface with.

7 Steady-state Short Circuit Model Development
The phasor-domain, steady-state SC DLL interface structure was originally developed by
ASPEN and subsequently provided to ETAP. Building upon this foundation, ETAP
enhanced its SC solver to seamlessly interface with the DLL framework. The
implementation was validated using the IEEE PSRC C24 modeling example [7], which
confirmed that models developed through the DLL framework are fully compatible with
both ASPEN and ETAP, producing consistent and matching results across the two
platforms.
ETAP further expanded the DLL framework by introducing standardized template
functions that allow IBR vendors to implement their proprietary fault-current calculation
logic. Leveraging insights gained from EMT studies performed for each vendor, ETAP
developed three representative DLL models, each designed to emulate the SC behavior
of a commercial inverter. These models were used to evaluate the performance of DLL-
based representations relative to actual vendor implementations.
The methodology and findings were documented and submitted to the IEEE PSRC WG
C45 on “Protection and SC Modeling of Systems with High Penetration of IBRs” [8]. As
the industry continues to actively explore the SC DLL modeling approach, this project
represents the first known effort in North America to evaluate, implement, and
demonstrate its practical application.

The dynamic DLL model developed for three vendors was integrated with ETAP software
to conduct dynamic simulations. The results from these simulations were compared to

replicate EMT transient behavior, but to reliably capture the system’s long-term and

11 Lessons Learned from the Black Box Dynamic Link
 Library Development
FRT settings play a critical role in shaping the fault response of IBRs. As such, power
system engineers need to understand the FRT configurations adopted by different IBR
vendors, including the range of available settings and their impact on system behavior.
While the industry generally recognizes a set of fundamental FRT parameters—such as
reactive current injection slopes (K-factors) and voltage threshold levels—this study,
conducted during the development of the black-box models, revealed significant
variability in how these parameters are implemented across vendors. Analysis of three
different vendor models showed that each employs a distinct and often extensive set of
FRT parameters within its inverter controls.
The development of the black box DLL models provided valuable insights into these
vendor-specific FRT characteristics. Notable differences were observed in fault detection
methods, active and reactive current injection strategies, techniques for limiting positive-
and negative-sequence currents under current saturation, and inverter behavior during
both close-in and remote fault conditions. A concise overview of these IBR system
characteristics is provided below.

11.1 Fault Ride-through Detection Method
It is important to know when and how an inverter transitions from normal operation mode
to the FRT mode. Inverters typically compare their terminal voltage to a threshold value
to detect faults, but the specific voltage used for this comparison can vary. Some inverters
use positive-sequence voltage for fault detection, while others rely on the minimum line-
to-line voltage. Additionally, some inverters also show the dependency on negative-
sequence voltage for detecting faults. Some vendors hard-code a specific method, while
others offer flexibility by allowing the selection of different options.

11.2 Reactive Current Injection Method
Although some of the existing grid codes provide recommendations about the reactive
current injection methods to support the grid during low voltage conditions, the vendors
have the flexibility of implementation of reactive current injection methods. This is why the
inverters from different manufacturers vary in the way they inject/absorb reactive power.
The amount of reactive current injections not only depends on the K-factors but also
depends on whether active or reactive power is given priority. Since all three vendors
under study have been found to have implemented reactive power priority, this section
focuses on that control aspect only.

11.4 Current-limiting Method
The methods used by inverters to generate positive-sequence active current, positive-
sequence reactive current, and negative-sequence current were described in the
preceding subsections. When the combined available current exceeds the inverter’s
current limit, one or more of these components must be curtailed based on the priority
assigned by the inverter control strategy.
For inverters capable of injecting negative-sequence current, the current-limiting
approach can be broadly classified into three categories: negative-sequence priority,
positive-sequence priority, and proportional allocation. In the negative-sequence priority
mode, the inverter delivers the maximum available negative-sequence current. In
contrast, the positive-sequence active and reactive currents are reduced as necessary to
remain within the current limit. In contrast, the positive-sequence priority mode prioritizes
the delivery of positive-sequence current, with the negative-sequence current curtailed
accordingly. Under the proportional method, both positive- and negative-sequence
currents are reduced proportionally to satisfy the current constraint.
While some vendors implement a single, fixed current-limiting strategy, others provide
configurable options that allow users to select among these methods.

11.5 Behavior during Close-in Faults: Angle Rotation
When a three-phase bolted fault occurs near the terminal of the inverter, the phase angle
difference between the voltage and current is determined by the impedance of the
element between the inverter and the fault point. If this impedance is different from what
the inverter’s control sets, conflict occurs between the inverter’s control and the physical
characteristics of the circuit, which leads to the rotation of the phase angle of voltage and
current. In SC programs, it is essential to understand how a specific vendor’s inverter
behaves in the time domain under such conditions, as angle rotation in the time domain
corresponds to the divergence in the iterative SC solution. In such cases, SC programs
typically use IBR’s pre-fault positive-sequence voltage as a reference instead of the
calculated IBR’s terminal voltage in each iteration to ensure solution convergence. The
study of three vendors in this paper reveals that some allow angle rotations, while one
somehow prevents angle rotation.
Figure 18 illustrates the rotation, in time, of the phase angles of the current and voltage
measured at the inverter terminal of a vendor during a bolted three-phase fault occurring
nearby. This scenario is not insignificant for fault studies because, while angle rotation
may seem minimal for a few cycles, the frequency change during this time can impact
measurements and relay operation, potentially leading to malfunctions in protective
devices.

11.6 Behavior during Remote Faults: Hysteresis
As discussed in Section 11.1, FRT detection relies on threshold voltages. It was observed
that the IBR vendors under study typically use a hysteresis approach with two distinct
thresholds: a lower threshold (e.g., 85%) to transition the inverter into the FRT mode and
a higher threshold (e.g., 90%) to reset it to the normal operating mode. Between these
two thresholds, the inverter stays locked in the FRT mode. This concept is depicted on
Figure 19.
The importance of hysteresis lies in preventing the inverter from rapidly switching
between FRT and normal modes during remote faults. For example, if the terminal voltage
of the inverter nears the lower threshold during a fault, there is a risk that the inverter
might reset to normal mode, only to immediately switch back to FRT mode. This constant
toggling can cause fluctuating current injections. By having an upper voltage threshold in
place, the inverter remains locked in FRT mode until it surpasses the upper threshold,
thus avoiding the toggling issue.
Two vendors studied in this paper employ a hysteresis approach in their FRT curves,
while one vendor uses a timer-based approach instead. In the timer-based method, the
inverter switches to FRT mode once the voltage falls below a preset threshold. If the
voltage rises above the threshold, the inverter only switches to normal mode after a preset
time delay. FRT detection and rest methods based on hysteresis can be accurately
modeled in the iterative SC program, while for a timer-based approach, SC programs may
lock the FRT detection permanently after a preset number of iterations if the voltage falls
below the FRT threshold.

beyond just the K-factors and encompass a wide variety of settings. These settings are
implemented in different ways, with each vendor adopting distinct approaches for
configuring and managing these fault attributes.

13 Load Impact on Inverter-based Resource Modeling
 Simulation Results Evaluation
Automating changes in IBR penetration levels impacts the pre-fault voltage profile and
IBR pre-fault current, which ultimately impacts SC simulation results. ASPEN OneLiner
provides options to use a power flow pre-fault solution or a linear network pre-fault
solution approximation for a system containing IBRs. Choosing between them involves a
tradeoff between accuracy and simplicity. Including a power flow pre-fault solution
significantly adds to the SWAP tool complexity, impacting design and end users. To
assess whether the SWAP tool should support a power flow pre-fault solution, testing was
conducted to quantify the relative differences in IBR SC simulation results between a
linear network pre-fault solution approximation and a power flow pre-fault solution.
Additionally, synchronous generator simulation results were recorded under the same
scenarios to establish a baseline for the comparisons. For the synchronous generator
scenario, a commonly accepted flat pre-fault voltage assumption was also simulated to
establish precedent. The results were used to guide SWAP tool design considerations
and to identify any requirements related to managing load under varying penetration
scenarios.
The tests used the same modified IEEE 14 bus system ASPEN model that was developed
for DOE DE-EE0010655 [9] and documented in SNL report SAND2024-13494,
“Assessing Inverter-based Resources Modeling Gaps in Commonly Used SC Programs”
[4]. The following modifications were made to the original IEEE 14 bus system to make it
more suitable for the SC study:

• All generators are assumed to have j0.3 per unit source impedance and are connected
 to the system through a Wye-Delta transformer with 13.8 kV at the generator side.
• The voltage bases for the 2 voltage levels in the original IEEE 14 bus system are not
 specified. For this assessment, the high voltage side was set to 138 kV, and the low
 voltage side was set to 34.5 kV.
• Bus 7, which originally connected Buses 4, 8, and 9, is removed, Bus 8 is removed,
 and the generator from Bus 8 is moved to Bus 4.
• Bus 13 is added along with a smaller generator that can be used to represent low IBR
 penetration scenarios.
• Zero-sequence impedance of lines is set to be 3 times larger than the positive-
 sequence impedance.
• Relays were added in 21 locations to measure voltages and currents.
The simulation results were recorded for three-phase, line-to-line, and single-line-to-
ground faults at each bus and measured at each relay location (represented as a yellow
circle in Figure 24) that is within two branches from the fault location.

within 2 branches of the fault location and for total fault current following simulation of

13.2 Observations
13.2.1 Phase and Positive-sequence Current
Phase and positive-sequence current magnitudes are most affected, particularly where
branch fault currents are low with respect to load current. Branch fault current magnitudes
with load flow are reported to be larger due to the inclusion of load current.

• Higher IBR penetration tends to reduce fault currents relative to load, resulting in a
 greater impact on SC simulation results when load is included versus excluded.
• From a sensitivity perspective, it is more conservative to omit load from the SC
 protection assessment since protection operation should not be dependent on load.
• From a coordination perspective, phase and positive sequence elements should be
 set to operate well above expected load. Therefore, when fault currents with load flow
 are nearly the same or less than load currents, omitting the load would not be expected
 to change the protection response.
The same applies to the positive sequence current angle with respect to voltage. These
are most affected as the fault current magnitude decreases below load levels.
13.2.2 Negative- and Zero-sequence Current
Negative-sequence current magnitudes are affected by pre-fault conditions in a similar
manner to positive-sequence. Protection elements based on negative-sequence could be
impacted more since these elements can typically be set more sensitively, especially
when the positive-sequence fault current magnitude is at or below load levels.
Zero-sequence current magnitude tends to increase slightly when using power flow pre-
fault conditions. The effect is more in IBR cases when compared to the traditional
synchronous generator case. The differences between fault current levels are observed
in the total zero-sequence fault current magnitude when considering pre-fault conditions.
The difference can be up to 10% in the 70% IBR penetration case vs. 6% in the traditional

                                                                                 Page 48 of 49

synchronous generator case. These differences are not that large and could probably be
accounted for by setting and simulation margins.

13.2.3 Considerations for Excluding Load
Based on the extra complexity required to build and use the SWAP tool, considering
power flow pre-fault conditions, it was decided not to support simulations using power
flow pre-fault conditions for this project. It’s realized that with more complex systems, this
approach may not apply, and expansion of the tool to support simulations using power
flow pre-fault conditions should be evaluated by another project. An additional rationale
for not including the load is summarized below:

• The assessment that was done under this project merely compared results from
 ASPEN OneLiner under different pre-fault conditions.
  There was no external control to validate simulation results. This leaves
 uncertainty that including load is more accurate.
  More testing would be required to determine if it is worthwhile.
• Significant additional effort is required to maintain a power flow base case in the SC
 model, and to set up IBR penetration scenarios for the SWAP tool.
• Protection sensitivity is more of a focus for the SWAP tool than coordination, in which
 relay setting margins can be increased.
  Sensitivity tends to be assessed more conservatively under minimum current
 conditions, which occurs when load is excluded from simulations.
• Designing the SWAP tool to support power flow pre-fault conditions significantly
 increases the complexity of the tool and the associated risks to the project budget and
 schedule related to the tool development.

14 Sensitivity-driven Wide Area Protection Tool Development
SWAP provides a stochastic sensitivity-driven method that can define many potential
scenarios with the inclusion of various decisive factors to perform wide-area protection
coordination studies. This tool (which will be developed for commercial use and can tie to
the utility SC software) is a valuable application for utility engineers to analyze how
different IBR penetration scenarios will impact their system efficiently and reliably.
The SWAP tool was designed/configured to work specifically with the ASPEN OneLiner
program. To minimize the integration effort with other commercial SC analysis
programs, the tool architecture was split into SC analysis program-independent and
dependent functions. The independent function is one that is vendor-agnostic, while the
dependent function may need customization for applications with other platforms. The

platforms.

14.3.2 Inverter-based Resource Operating Modes and Parameters
The following are IBR resource operating modes and parameters:

• Industry experience in North America is that most of the IBR settings that are impactful

 fixed range.
• The LVRT reactive current slope (i.e., K-factor) is the most likely flexible parameter to
 vary and impact the inverter fault response.
  This is the focus of the IBR operating mode and parameter variation.
• The user can scale the slope of the K-factors for positive sequence LVRT and negative
 sequence current injection separately.
  The user can choose a multiplier of 0.1–5, and the resulting K-factor range is
        limited to 0.5–6.
  If the resulting K-factor is 4 or above, then the SWAP tool will adjust the dVmode to
 1 (current injection curve is continuous at the LVRT threshold).
• Scaling is implemented temporarily (for the scenarios of concern) across the network
 model for all IBRs.

16.4 Slow Phase Overcurrent Relays
IBRs have a significant impact on phase overcurrent relays at higher IBR penetrations.
Fault contributions are lower at higher IBR levels, and phase overcurrent relays operate
slowly or, in some cases, do not operate at all. This leads to protection coordination
problems or the inability to detect and clear the faults.
For a phase instantaneous overcurrent element, the relay element will not see the fault
further into the line or will not see the fault at all at high IBR penetrations. This leads to
slower fault clearing and possible coordination problems.

16.5 Effect on Ground Overcurrent Relays
Ground overcurrent relays showed fewer issues compared to phase overcurrent relays.
Reasons that contributed to the lesser impact on ground relays are as follows:

• Pickup of ground overcurrent relays is lower since these are less sensitive to loading.
• Delta tertiary of transformers contributes to the ground overcurrent, and there is less
 effect on ground currents due to transformer configurations.
However, there are slow ground overcurrent relay operations in some cases at higher
penetrations. In the case shown below (Table 23 and Table 24), ground overcurrent time
increased from 82 seconds to 131 seconds when going from SWAP1 (39% IBR
penetration) to SWAP4 (88% IBR penetration).

17 Significant Accomplishments and Conclusions
This project developed a questionnaire to gather information from other utilities to identify
their IBR protection challenges and IBR fault responses. Information was gathered from
six different utilities. Additionally, sensitive relay triggers were set on transmission lines
connected to IBRs to collect IBR relay events for internal and external faults. Several relay
events (more than 40) were collected and analyzed. Some events were also shared by
other utilities. The information gathered by questionnaire responses, and relay event
reports, were used to help identify the study areas. The results were also used to write a
paper accepted and presented at WPRC (West Protective Relaying Conference) and was
also published as a Sandia National Laboratories publication.

Advanced and accurate IBR DLL based models using three different manufacturers were
developed for use in commercial fault software. These models were verified against
PSCAD models and HIL testing, where the positive and negative voltage and current
values differed by less than 3% and the phase angle differences were less than 2.1
degrees. While the industry is still in the exploration phase of evaluating black box DLL-
based SC modeling, the project team has taken a leading role by developing and
implementing the first model of its kind in North America.

The SWAP tool was developed and showed promise for running protection studies in high
penetration IBR situations. Over five hundred and seventy simulations were performed
on a large section of an actual utility system under various IBR penetration levels. As
expected, as penetration levels exceeded 70% there were some protection issues such
as, slowing of ground time overcurrent elements, in a couple of instances, the phase
distance element did not operate for the last 5% of the line, one of the lines had phase
and ground non-operation for the last 10% of the line. Further investigation and mitigation
was not performed due to termination of the project.

18 Path Forward
This project was a three-year effort terminated at the completion of the second year.
Therefore, the protection scheme mitigations could not be developed and tested.
Additionally, there were delays in the development of the tool, which in turn delayed the
evaluation of the protection schemes. The first two years resulted in the utility
questionnaire with responses from several utilities, obtaining over 40 IBR events, which
also included relay misoperations. The utility questionnaire indicated several areas of
concern related to IBRs, specifically related to low fault current, low or no negative-
sequence current, and inadequate IBR models. New DLL models were developed and
incorporated into ASPEN OneLiner. The SWAP tool was constructed and showed early
positive results. Of these, the DLL model concept should be further developed such that
manufacturers can easily produce IBR DLL models. Additionally, fault simulation software
companies should resolve the non-convergence and simulation speed issues resulting
from many DLL models in the modeling area. Finally, completing the SWAP tool
development, identifying protection issues, and mitigation should be further investigated.

18.1 Future Research Potential
A couple of future potential uses of the SWAP tool could be explored:

 • The SWAP tool or some of its elements could be used to tune IBR fault response
 or optimize relay settings based upon shifting system conditions, generation mix,
 time of day generation deployment or IBR penetration levels.

 • Use of the tool or some of its elements to create a platform for validating inverter
 SC models against their response to future faults. This could be an automated
 collection of DFR records from relays and inverters, compared automatically
 against the predicted response to deliver a model performance assessment
 which could feed back to a refined model. This could effectively create a real-
 world test bed and provide accurate real-code models without having to depend
 on manufacturer simulation and testing capabilities and facilities.

 • While the SWAP tool was designed to focus on the impacts of power-electronic
 interfaced generation, future work could include applying similar techniques to study the
 protection system under power-electronic interfaced loads, such as variable frequency
 drives (VFD), uninterruptible power supplies (UPS) and data centers. Similar to IBR
 generation, these loads would not respond in a linear fashion, such as fixed impedance
 loads, during faults, so they would require new modeling practices. There is potential for
 future power-electronic interfaced loads to provide grid support, such as reactive power
 during faults. Whatever the controls are, short circuit DLL models could be developed
 for VFD and data centers to integrate into commercial software such as ASPEN
 OneLiner. As discussed in Chapter 13, most utilities do not include load in their
 protection studies, so this would require a major change for both utilities and the short
 circuit software vendors. As the number of large load interconnections increases, there
 could be significant benefits to accurately capturing their impact on protection
 coordination in the SWAP tool.
