Schrödinger Bridges via the Hacking of Bayesian Priors in Classical and Quantum Regimes Preprint: APS/123-QED
Clive Cenxin Aw, Peter Sidajaya

Abstract
Bayes’ rule is widely regarded as the canonical prescription for belief updating. We show, however, that one can arbitrarily preserve pre-specified beliefs while appearing to perform Bayesian updates via “prior hacking”: engineering a reference prior distribution such that, for a fixed channel and evidence, the update matches a chosen target distribution. We prove that this is generically possible in both classical and quantum settings whenever Bayesian inversions are well-defined (with the Petz recovery map as the quantum analogue to Bayes’ rule), and provide constructive algorithms for doing so. We further establish a duality between prior hacking and Schrödinger bridge problems (a key object in statistical physics with applications in generative modelling), yielding in the quantum setting a unique, inference-consistent selection among candidate bridges. This formally establishes the Bayes-like updating that Schrödinger bridges are performing with respect to the process as opposed to the reference prior, both in classical and quantum settings.

Classical Setting

II.1
The Classical Formalism

Before introducing what we mean by prior hacking, we delineate the formalism used—namely, that of stochastic matrices and Bayesian updating. We work with a discrete probability space
\Omega
with
d
elements where probability distributions
p:\Omega\longrightarrow[0,1]
live. These can be represented by column probability vectors, which we also denote as
p
to avoid notational encumbrance
1
1

1

While we have overloaded the symbol
p
by assigning it to a function and a vector, in equations, the function
p(\cdot)
will always appear with brackets, while the vector
p
would not have any. This also applies to the other objects that we are going to introduce next.
. These exist in
\mathds{R}^{d}
with entries
p(x)
, such that
\forall p:\sum_{x}p(x)=1
and
\forall x:p(x)\geq 0
. The set of all probability vectors in
d
dimensions form a probability simplex
\Delta^{d-1}
in
d-1
dimensions.

Consider two probability spaces
\Omega
and
\Omega^{\prime}
with dimensions
d
and
d^{\prime}
and simplexes
\Delta^{d-1}
and
\Delta^{d^{\prime}-1}
. A stochastic process
\mathcal{E}(\cdot|x)
is a conditional probability function that assigns a probability to
\Omega^{\prime}
given that
x\in\Omega
has been observed. We can transform a probability function
p
in
\Delta^{d-1}
to another probability function
q
in
\Delta^{d^{\prime}-1}
by the equation

[\mathcal{E}p](y)=\sum_{x}\mathcal{E}(y|x)p(x).

(1)

which simply formalizes the forward propagation from the input distribution
p
to its output distribution
\mathcal{E}p
. Thus, the stochastic process may be represented as a left-stochastic (column-stochastic) matrix
\mathcal{E}
in
\mathds{R}^{d^{\prime}\times d}
with
\mathcal{E}_{y,x}:=\mathcal{E}(y|x)
.
Finally, the joint probability function
P
which lives in
\Omega^{\prime}\times\Omega
is given by
P(x,y):=\mathcal{E}(y|x)p(x)
.

Bayesian Updating

Central to this work is the process of updating one’s description of an input given some output evidence (sometimes called retrodiction when
\mathcal{E}
is a forward process in time). That is, given an evidence distribution
q(y)
, which is some output sample of a characterized process
\mathcal{E}(y|x)
, what can one infer (or update on earlier inferences) about the input distribution of that process?

One might propose to simply mathematically invert the process and update one’s description of the input to
\mathcal{E}^{-1}q
. But for various reasons, this approach is neither mathematically general, physically sound nor practically robust
2
2

2

\mathcal{E}^{-1}
, in general, is not a physical stochastic process. If
q
lies outside the image of
\mathcal{E}
,
\mathcal{E}^{-1}q
could lie outside the probability simplex of
\Omega
. Such an approach is also not robust to making inferences when the process is singular, nor does it adhere to the strong practical principle of not neglecting prior information we had about the overall population for which the sample was obtained.
. Rather, the statistically normative approach centres around Bayes’ rule:

\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{\gamma}(x|y):=\frac{\mathcal{E}(y|x)\gamma(x)}{[\mathcal{E}\gamma](y)}

(2)

where
\gamma
is referred to as the reference prior (or simply the prior), a probability distribution which captures our initial information or belief on the input, and
\mathcal{E}\gamma
is its forward propagation. It is important to emphasize that, generally, different reference priors lead to different Bayes maps
42
;
43
, as this feature is pertinent to the results in this work. Unlike
\mathcal{E}^{-1}
,
\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{\gamma}
always exists as a stochastic process as long as singularities are avoided in the denominator (which can always be done by adding small perturbations of the process
5
). In matrix form, we can write Eq. (
2
) as

\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{\gamma}=D_{\gamma}\mathcal{E}^{\textsf{T}}D_{\mathcal{E}\gamma}^{-1},

(3)

where
D_{a}
is the diagonal matrix with entries
(D_{a})_{i,i}=a_{i}
for a given vector
a
. Now, with this Bayes map, one updates their prior
\gamma
to a new conclusion
p
:

\gamma\quad\mapsto\quad p=\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{\gamma}\,q.

(4)

This is how a genuine Bayesian or Jeffrey’s update works
38
;
71
. With that, we introduce a reverse-engineering of this: prior hacking.

II.2
Classical Prior Hacking

As mentioned, for proper Bayesian updates, a genuine reference prior
\gamma_{g}
induces a genuine conclusion
p_{g}=\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{\gamma_{g}}\,q
via Bayes’ rule on
\mathcal{E}
and an evidence
q
. Now, one can invert this by starting with a
fixed
conclusion
p_{h}
which induces a frankly conceptually perverse “prior”
\gamma_{h}
instead. Consider Figure
1
for an illustration of this.

Notice that, doxastically or sequentially speaking,
p_{h}
and
\gamma_{g}
are the same thing: some initial belief. But while
\gamma_{g}
gets updated through Bayes’ rule upon evidence
q
,
p_{h}
is a fixed conclusion that we do not want to change our minds about, and yet also want to make it seem like a conclusion that emerges from rational Bayesian inference upon receiving some evidence. This inversion of Bayesian update prompts some
\gamma_{h}
, which we call the hacking (or hacked) prior, for which
\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{\gamma_{h}}q=p_{h}
. Prior hacking refers to the process of finding
\gamma_{h}
that solves this relation for some tuple
(\mathcal{E},p_{h},q)
of that process and some evidence and fixed conclusion.

With that, we drop the subscripts to avoid encumbrance and state the problem of prior hacking concisely:
given
a tuple
(\mathcal{E},p,q)
, under what conditions do we have
\gamma
such that

\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{\gamma}\,q=p?

(5)

From here, unless stated otherwise,
\gamma
is taken to be the hacking prior that solves Eq. (
5
).

First, note that we can rewrite Eq. (
5
) to

p\oslash[\mathcal{E}^{\textsf{T}}(q\oslash[\mathcal{E}\gamma])]=\gamma.

(6)

In this work,
\odot
and
\oslash
are the Hadamard or elementwise product and division, respectively. We also remark in passing that
\oslash
might not be always be well-defined due to singularities. This becomes relevant when discussing when prior hacking is possible. The details of the Hadamard product and the derivation of Eq. (
6
) can be also found in Appendix
A
.

Key Theorems for Classical Prior Hacking

We move to some key results that emerge from this problem. As it turns out, Eq. (
6
) is equivalent to a known problem in Statistics known as the Sinkhorn problem, the Iterative Proportional Fitting (IPF), or matrix scaling, among other names
62
;
63
;
61
;
30
;
35
;
45
;
14
;
44
. The problem is such: Given an initial matrix
X
, we wish to find
a
and
b
, such that the matrix
M=D_{a}XD_{b}
,
has a row sum
u
and column sum
v
. That is,

\displaystyle Me

\displaystyle=u

(7)

\displaystyle M^{\textsf{T}}e

\displaystyle=v,

(8)

where
e=(1,1,\cdots,1)^{\textsf{T}}
. The details of this problem can be found in Appendix
B
. Suffice to say, the problems are mathematically equivalent with the substitutions
(X,u,v,a,b)\longrightarrow(\mathcal{E},p,q,\Gamma,\gamma)
, where

\Gamma=q\oslash\mathcal{E}\gamma.

(9)

One may see
\Gamma
as an expression of divergence between the actual result (captured by the evidence received as
q
) and the postulated result (that is,
\mathcal{E}\gamma
the propagation of ones’ reference prior). With this, we can take a known theorem and apply it to the question of classical prior hacking.

Theorem 1
.

(the Sinkhorn problem
35
;
45
;
14
;
44
)
Given a tuple
(\mathcal{E},p,q)
, where
\mathcal{E}
is a
d\times d^{\prime}
transition matrix and probability distributions
p\in\Delta^{d-1}
and
q\in\Delta^{d^{\prime}-1}
, the following are equivalent:

1.

There exists
\gamma
such that

\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{\gamma}\,q=p,

(10)

where
\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{\gamma}
is the Bayes map of
\mathcal{E}
, based on
\gamma
.

2.

There exists positive vectors
\Gamma
and
\gamma
such that
D_{\Gamma}\mathcal{E}D_{\gamma}
has row sums
q
and column sums
p
.

3.

There exists another matrix
B
with row sums
q
and column sums
p
, i.e.
Be=q
and
B^{\textsf{T}}e=p
, with the condition

B_{y,x}>0\quad\forall(y,x)\text{ s.t }\mathcal{E}_{y,x}>0.

(11)

4.

For every
Y\subset\{1,\dots,d^{\prime}\}
and
X\subset\{1,\dots,d\}
, such that
\mathcal{E}_{Y^{\bot}X}=0
,

\sum_{y\in Y}q_{y}\geq\sum_{x\in X}p_{x},

(12)

with equality holding for
\mathcal{E}_{YX^{\bot}}=0
and
S^{\bot}
denotes the complement of the set
S
.

Proof.

As mentioned, in Appendix
B
we show that the first and second points are equivalent. A modern review of the proofs of the other points can be found in
35
. The only difference here is that we require
\gamma
to be a normalised probability distribution. Since the two diagonal matrices
D_{\Gamma},D_{\gamma}
are related by a scalar constant, we can always normalise
\gamma
into a probability distribution.
∎

While the third statement is probably the most mathematically straightforward, the fourth statement may provide better physical intuitions. For every subset
Y
and
X
such that
\mathcal{E}_{Y^{\bot}X}=0
means that all transitions coming out from
x\in X
must go to
Y
. Because every
p(x)
must go to
X
, the total mass of all the
q(y)
must be at least the same as the total mass of all the
p(x)
. Additionally, when
\mathcal{E}_{YX^{\bot}}=0
, this means that
(Y,X)
and
(Y^{\bot},X^{\bot})
are decoupled from each other, and thus the total mass in each partition must be conserved. Finally, of note is that when all the entries of
\mathcal{E}
are positive, then the matrix
B_{i,j}:=\frac{q(i)\,p(j)}{S}
where
S=\Sigma_{i}\,q(i)=\Sigma_{j}\,p(j)=1
will always satisfies the second existence condition, meaning that it is prior hackable
3
3

3

Additionally,
\gamma
is unique if and only if
\mathcal{E}
is fully indecomposable. For more information on full indecomposability, we refer the readers to
35
;
13
.
.

Now, Theorem
1
deals with the prior-hacking with respect to a given tuple of
(\mathcal{E},p,q)
. A natural follow-up is to consider, for a given
\mathcal{E}
, what conditions make it such that it is prior-hackable
for all

p,q
. This brings us to our next theorem,

Theorem 2
.

For transition matrices
\mathcal{E}
, the following statements are equivalent:

1.

\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{\gamma}
is well defined for all
\gamma
,

2.

\forall(y,x):\mathcal{E}_{y,x}>0
,

3.

\mathcal{E}
is prior-hackable for all probability inputs
p
and outputs
q
.

Proof.

To avoid encumbering the main text, the proof can be found in Appendix
C.1
.
∎

With this, one might say that there is a vast embedded pluralism available to Bayesian reasoning. The set of transformations that omit prior hacking for some
p,q
are measure-zero and are precisely those for which a Bayesian inversion is not always well defined.

We note in passing that additional corollaries pertaining to concatenations of channels are included in Appendix
C.2
. Corollary
1
is a simple upshot that any concatenation of always prior-hackable channels is also always (i.e. for any
p,q
) prior-hackable (though the converse does not hold—prior-hackable concatenations may be decomposed into channels that are individually not always prior-hackable), while Corollary
2
gives a definition of primitivity of Markov matrices

in explicitly Bayesian notions.

Obtaining the Hacked Prior

While the existence of a hacking prior can be determined by Theorem
1
, we have not yet described any method of finding a solution. To our knowledge,
\gamma
in Eq. (
6
) cannot be written in a closed form solution. Fortunately, a corollary of Theorem
1
is that an algorithm known as the RAS algorithm will converge to the solution of the problem given its existence
61
. One expression of the algorithm can be obtained by simply taking Eq. (
6

Algorithm 1
.

(RAS algorithm)
The solution to Eq. (
6
) can be obtained by randomly picking any initializing
\gamma_{0}\in\Delta^{d-1}

\gamma_{i+1}=p\oslash[\mathcal{E}^{\emph{\textsf{T}}}(q\oslash(\mathcal{E}\gamma_{i}))].

(13)

As
i\rightarrow\infty
,
\gamma_{i}
will converge to the solution, if it exists.

Classical Schrödinger Bridges & Prior Hacking

The Sinkhorn problem has been rediscovered or found to be equivalent to other problems in various fields, such as transportation planning
23

59
;
22

25
;
56

57
;

7

50

20

26
;
41
;
17
;

31
. This brings us to the primary connection of this work, between prior hacking and these objects in statistical physics. We begin first by reviewing key features of the Schrödinger bridge problem.

The Schrödinger Bridge Problem

SB problems consist of an observation of initial and final distributions of particles in a system,
p(x)
and
q(y)
, as well as a prior joint distribution
A(x,y)
. However, at least for non-trivial cases,
\sum_{y}A(x,y)\neq p(x)
and
\sum_{x}A(x,y)\neq q(y)
. The task is to find a distribution
B
, the Schrödinger bridge, that is as close as
P
whose marginals still satisfy both distributions
p,q
. The closeness here is defined by the KL-divergence.

\begin{array}[]{r c l}B=&\displaystyle\argmin_{B^{\prime}}&\textsf{D}(B^{\prime}\|A)\\
=&\displaystyle\argmin_{B^{\prime}}&\displaystyle\sum_{x,y}B^{\prime}(x,y)\log\left(\frac{B^{\prime}(x,y)}{A(x,y)}\right).\\
&\text{subject to}&\displaystyle\sum_{x}B^{\prime}(x,y)=q(y)\\
&&\displaystyle\sum_{y}B^{\prime}(x,y)=p(x)\end{array}

(15)

The solution to this problem, it turns out, can be found by a method equivalent to the Sinkhorn problem. First, we have to find
\Gamma
and
\gamma
such that the following equations are satisfied:

\displaystyle[\mathcal{E}^{\textsf{T}}\Gamma](x)\,\gamma(x)=p(x)

(16)

\displaystyle[\mathcal{E}\gamma](y)\,\Gamma(y)=q(y),

(17)

where

\displaystyle[\mathcal{E}^{\textsf{T}}\Gamma](x)=\sum_{y}\mathcal{E}(y|x)\Gamma(y)

(18)

\displaystyle[\mathcal{E}\gamma](y)=\sum_{x}\mathcal{E}(y|x)\gamma(x).

(19)

Here
\mathcal{E}(y|x)=A(x,y)/\sum_{y}A(x,y)
is the transition probability induced by the prior distribution. Note that, firstly, in this setting neither
\Gamma
nor
\gamma
has to be a probability distribution. Instead,
\gamma
is called the forward scaling potential and
\Gamma
the backward scaling potential. However, they are still related by Eq. (
9
) that was defined for the prior hacking problem, and are thus not independent of each other. Secondly, the marginal
\sum_{y}A(x,y)
does not matter in this problem (see Appendix
E
).

The solution (i.e. the SB) would then be given by

B(x,y)=\gamma(x)\mathcal{E}(y|x)\Gamma(y),

(20)

and the optimum transition probability
\mathcal{F}(y|x)=B(x,y)/p(x)
is given by

\mathcal{F}(y|x)=\mathcal{E}(y|x)\frac{\Gamma(y)}{\mathcal{E}^{\textsf{T}}\Gamma(x)}.

(21)

Connection to Prior Hacking

One immediately notices Eq. (
21
)’s resemblance to Bayes’ rule as per Eq. (
2
). Indeed, as alluded to already, the SB problem has a surprisingly close relation to the prior hacking problem. To see this, we first write both Eqs. (
16
) and (
17
) in matrix notation as

\displaystyle(\mathcal{E}^{\textsf{T}}\Gamma)\odot\gamma=p,

(22)

\displaystyle\Gamma\odot(\mathcal{E}\gamma)=q.

(23)

If the objects are nonzero, these give nothing but Eq. (
6

With this we note some key connections between what has been discussed. Firstly, solving the classical, single-step discrete SB problem is mathematically equivalent to solving the Sinkhorn problem and the prior hacking problem. In fact, the Eqs. (

This naturally leads to our third point. While the Sinkhorn and the SB problem invokes

q
and the postulated result
\mathcal{E}\gamma

Finally, we note that via Eq. (

\mathcal{F}=D_{\Gamma}\mathcal{E}D_{\mathcal{E}^{\textsf{T}}\Gamma}^{-1}.

(24)

As alluded, there is much resemblance to Bayes maps as written in Eq. (

based on the hacked prior
\gamma

\hat{\mathcal{F}}_{p}=\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{\gamma}

(25)

Proof.

Applying Bayesian inversion to the adjusted forward channel
\mathcal{F}
with the prior

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{F}}_{p}

\displaystyle=D_{p}D_{\mathcal{E}^{\textsf{T}}\Gamma}^{-1}\mathcal{E}^{\textsf{T}}D_{\Gamma}D^{-1}_{\mathcal{F}p}

\displaystyle=D_{\gamma}\mathcal{E}^{\textsf{T}}D_{\mathcal{E}\gamma}^{-1},

(26)

where we have applied Eqs. (

III.1
Quantum Prior Hacking

With the classical setting discussed, we move to the prior hacking in the quantum regime. Here, probability distributions
p,q
are upgraded to quantum states
\rho,\omega
. These are operators in the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}^{d}
represented by density matrices in
\mathds{C}^{d\times d}
, with the constraints that
\text{Tr}\rho=1
and
\rho\succeq 0
. Moreover, the forward channel
\mathcal{E}
is upgraded to a quantum channel, which is a map
\mathcal{E}[\cdot]:\mathcal{H}^{d}\longrightarrow\mathcal{H}^{d^{\prime}}
that is completely positive and trace preserving (CPTP). Our choice of the quantum analogue to Bayesian inversion Eq. (
2
) is the Petz recovery map
55
;
54
;
8
;
51
;
43
;
5
, given by:

\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{\gamma}[\omega]=\sqrt{\gamma}\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathcal{E}[\gamma]}}\omega\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathcal{E}[\gamma]}}\right]\sqrt{\gamma}.

(28)

The task of prior hacking is still operationally the same: given an evidence state
\omega
and a conclusion
\rho
, is it possible to find a reference prior state
\gamma
(now a density operator) such that
\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{\gamma}[\omega]=\rho
? To this, we state the quantum analogue of Theorem
2
:

Theorem 4
.

For any pair of states
\rho,\omega\in\mathcal{H}^{d}
and any channel
\mathcal{E}:\mathcal{H}^{d}\longrightarrow\mathcal{H}^{d}
that always maps to full rank states, i.e.,
\forall\rho^{\prime}\;\text{rank}(\mathcal{E}[\rho^{\prime}])=d
, prior hacking is always possible, i.e.,
\exists\gamma\;\text{s.t.}\;\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{\gamma}[\omega]=\rho
.

Proof.

To avoid encumbering the main text, the proof can be found in Appendix
C.3
.
∎

The condition that
\mathcal{E}[\cdot]
is always full rank is akin to
\mathcal{E}
having strictly positive entries in the classical setting. In both cases, these conditions are needed to make sure that the reverse map
\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{\gamma}
is always well defined regardless of the choice of the reference prior
\gamma
. We will discuss some example of quantum channels that do not satisfy this in Sec.
III.2
.

Obtaining the Hacked Prior

Though the quantum solution to prior hacking is guaranteed to exist due to Theorem
4
, finding the solution is not straightforward. One possible way to do it is to derive a fixed point iteration similar to Algorithm
1
. First, however, we have to define
\Gamma
for the quantum scenario:

\Gamma=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathcal{E}[\gamma]}}\omega\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathcal{E}[\gamma]}}.

(29)

Algorithm 2
.

The solution to quantum prior hacking for a channel
\mathcal{E}
, evidence
\omega
, and target
\rho
can be obtained by randomly picking a full-rank
\gamma_{0}
from the space of states and iterating the following equation:

\gamma_{i+1}=\left[\sqrt{\rho}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\rho}}(\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}\left[\Gamma_{i}\right])^{-1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\rho}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\sqrt{\rho}\right]^{2},

(30)

where

\Gamma_{i}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathcal{E}[\gamma_{i}]}}\omega\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathcal{E}[\gamma_{i}]}}.

(31)

As
i\rightarrow\infty
,
\gamma_{i}
will converge to the solution.

The derivation of the iteration can be found in Appendix
D
. As with the classical version (Algorithm
1

), this algorithm still alternates between propagation and counter propagation steps by the channel (
\mathcal{E}[\cdot]
and
\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}[\cdot]
) and scaling steps with the output and input states (represented by the operations
\mathcal{D}_{\omega}(\cdot)
and
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{\rho}(\cdot)
).

Step-by-step, it can be written as:

\gamma_{i}
\mathcal{E}[\gamma_{i}]
\Gamma_{i}
\mathcal{E}^{\textsf{T}}\Gamma_{i}
\gamma_{i+1}
\mathcal{E}[\cdot]
\mathcal{D}_{\omega}(\cdot)
\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}[\cdot]
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{\rho}(\cdot)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\omega}(\cdot)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\cdot}}\,\omega\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{\cdot}}

(32)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{\rho}(\cdot)

\displaystyle=\left[\sqrt{\rho}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\rho}\,\cdot\,\sqrt{\rho}}\right)^{1/2}\sqrt{\rho}\right]^{2}.

(33)

Notably, unlike in the classical case,
\mathcal{D}_{\omega}
and
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{\rho}
are not symmetric with each other.

III.2
Notable Qubit Channel Cases

As with the classical setting, we briefly run through some illustrative examples of quantum prior hacking. Since this work’s focus is not on specific channels, we will only briefly state some notable observations, leaving more specific theorems for future exploration.

•

Unitaries
are analogous to permutation matrices in the classical regime, being reversible. Expectedly, non-trivial prior hacking is always impossible in this case since
\forall\gamma:\hat{\mathcal{U}}_{\gamma}=\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}

3
;
42
.

•

As with classical erasures,
quantum erasures
send all input density operators to the same density operator. Applying the Petz recovery map on such discard-and-prepare channels also yield quantum erasures, but to the reference state
3
;
42
. Thus, quantum prior hacking here is the same as classical prior hacking—since all information is erased, hacking is always trivial with
\gamma=\rho
.

•

Amplitude damping
channels for qubit systems are akin to absorbing maps for classical stochastic matrices. Since some choice of vacuum state
\ket{0}\!\bra{0}
is sent to itself, these channels do not satisfy the conditions of Theorem
4
. This excludes prior hacking for classical evidence toward conclusions of greater weights in
\ket{0}\!\bra{0}
. Consider Figure
6
for additional geometric intuition.

•

The depolarising channel,

\mathcal{D}[\rho]=\lambda\frac{\mathds{I}}{2}+(1-\lambda)\rho,

interpolates between an unitary (identity) channel (
\lambda=0
), where non-trivial prior hacking is impossible, and an erasure channel (
\lambda=1
), where it is trivial. For intermediate
\lambda
, the Petz map depends continuously on both the channel and the reference state. As the reference state varies, its image deforms continuously from a single point to the full Bloch ball. By continuity, no region can remain inaccessible, implying surjectivity and hence the possibility of prior hacking (Theorem
4
). For details, see Appendix
G
.

•

The dephasing channel
\mathcal{Z}
is defined by the Kraus operators

Z_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&\sqrt{1-\lambda}\end{pmatrix},\quad Z_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&\sqrt{\lambda}\end{pmatrix}.

(34)

It suppresses off-diagonal terms and, unlike the depolarising channel, fails the full-rank condition of Theorem
4
. Specifically, computational basis states are fixed points and remain rank-deficient, rendering the Petz map ill-defined for such references and preventing prior hacking to pure basis states. Nevertheless, prior hacking can still occur in restricted cases. This is most apparent in the completely dephasing limit (
\lambda=1
), where the channel projects onto diagonal states. Notably, for any evidence
\omega
and toward a decoherent conclusion
\rho
, prior hacking is possible if and only if
\rho,\omega
shares the same weights in the computational basis. For details, consider Figure
7
and Theorem
7
in Appendix
G
.

IV
Concluding Discussion

We conclude by summarizing our findings alongside their conceptual upshots, before mentioning possible avenues for future work.

Bayes’ Rule, Schrödinger Bridges & Belief Kinematics

We have showed that pathological closed-mindedness can be spoofed as sound Bayesian updating. Theorem
2
demonstrates the broad susceptibility of Bayesian inference to such prior hacking. Remarkably, this vulnerability is categorically parallel to the problem of constructing Schrödinger bridges (Theorem
3
).

This correspondence has notable implications for belief kinematics and philosophical discussions of epistemic pathologies
9
;
15
. We emphasize that in statistical physics, the Schrödinger bridge is far from pathological: it represents a principled update of a reference process to the most reasonable neighbouring dynamics consistent with the empirical constraints
p,q
(Eq.
15
). However, when interpreted through the lens of belief dynamics, the mathematical structure admits a pathological reading. If prior hacking corresponds to retroactively altering one’s prior
\gamma
to protect a desired belief, then Schrödinger bridging corresponds to revising one’s model of the evidence-generating process
\mathcal{E}
itself in order to preserve that belief.

In informal terms, while prior hacking resembles the claim: “I forgot to mention that I have hidden evidence that I was never sick,” Schrödinger bridging corresponds to: “I realize now that doctors cannot be trusted after all.” Theorem
3
shows that these two forms of epistemic inertia are mathematically equivalent. Altering one’s model of evidence propagation after the fact is formally indistinguishable from manipulating the prior so that Bayesian updating yields the desired conclusion. But once again, this pathological reading is only possible if
p
had
not
been a genuine observation but just some pre-selected belief we do not wish to update on.

Returning to the role of Schrödinger bridges in statistical physics and its applications, our link to prior hacking (Theorem
3
) demonstrates that these constructions are really doing something characteristically Bayesian in nature. In effect, they implement some dual of Bayes’ rule, not on prior distributions themselves, but on the underlying process, given genuine observations on the input
p
and output
q
. Given Bayes’ rule’s epistemic significance, this perspective elucidates the same significance embedded in Schrödinger bridges.

The Petz Recovery Map & Quantum Schrödinger Bridges

Our quantum extension for the Petz recovery reveals a similar susceptibility to prior hacking as in the classical setting (Theorem
4
). Theorem
6
is particularly noteworthy as it shows that the Petz map obeys a quantum analogue to Theorem
3
defined for QSBs as constructed in the existing literature
31
. Furthermore, the result
selects
a unique inference-consistent candidate for QSBs among the current continuum of generic candidates.

Avenues for Future Work

First, unlike in the classical setting, quantum Schrödinger bridges are not yet known to arise from a well-defined optimization problem. Appendix
E
discusses possible reasons for this gap. Identifying such an optimization principle would be a natural extension of current work, particularly in light of the inference-consistent formulation of QSBs introduced here.

Second, our analysis has focused primarily on existence results. A natural follow-up is to understand how “easy prior hacking is” for given tuples
(\mathcal{E},p,q)
. Our simulations and figures suggest that highly dissipative process are easier to prior hack. Erasure channels are trivially hackable in both classical and quantum settings while channels with high absolute determinants require more fine-graining. Studying robustness under constrained prior adjustments may provide a new operational yet belief-kinematic perspective on the irreversibility of a channel
\mathcal{E}
with respect to states
p,q
.

Finally, given the strong structural correspondence established here between Bayesian inference and Schrödinger bridges, it may be worthwhile to examine whether existing applications of Schrödinger bridge methods in statistical physics and machine learning can be helpfully conceptualized through the lens of prior hacking, and whether such a perspective offers any advantages for associated computational tasks.

as the inputs. For completeness, we will both prove the theorem for both non-Hermitian (which has already been shown in
[
28
]
) and Hermitian
\alpha
and
\beta
.

First, consider the case that both
\alpha
and
\beta
are Hermitian, which means
U_{0}=U_{T}=\mathds{1}
. The proof of Theorem
4
(see Section
C.3
) only requires continuity of
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathcal{E},\rho}
and the fact that
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathcal{E},\rho}(\Xi)=\Xi
when
\Xi
is pure, both of which are true, given that
\mathcal{E}
is positivity improving. Thus, there will always be a
\Xi
such that
\mathcal{F_{\mathsf{Herm}}}[\rho]=\omega
. In the case that both
\alpha
and
\beta
are not Hermitian, we can fix
U_{0}
and
U_{T}
to any values. This does not affect continuity, and the action on the surface is now given by
\mathcal{F}[\rho]=U_{T}\Xi U_{T}^{\dagger}
, which is simply a rigid rotation on the surface of the set, which can be topologically deformed into the identity still. Therefore, by the same logic, using the decomposition
\alpha=U_{0}\sqrt{\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}[\Xi]}
and
\beta=U_{t}\sqrt{\Xi}
, there will always be a
\Xi
such that
\mathcal{F_{\mathsf{gen}}}[\rho]=\omega
, for every fixed value of
U_{0},U_{T}
.

Note that, while we have proven the existence of the solution, the convergence of both Algorithms
2
and
3
is still an open question.

Appendix D
Fixed Point Iteration for the Quantum Scenario

Given a channel
\mathcal{E}
, an evidence
\omega
, and a target state
\rho
, we want to solve the equation

\rho=\sqrt{\gamma}\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}\left[\Gamma\right]\sqrt{\gamma}.

(66)

First, note that for the matrix equation
MNM=L
, one solution for
M
is

M=L^{1/2}(L^{-1/2}N^{-1}L^{-1/2})^{1/2}L^{1/2}.

(67)

Taking the square, we can obtain

\gamma=\left[\sqrt{\rho}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\rho}}(\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}\left[\Gamma\right])^{-1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\rho}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\sqrt{\rho}\right]^{2},

(68)

which we can use as an update rule, similar to the classical scenario. Note that Eq. (
67
) can be rewritten in a different manner, for example with
L
in the middle instead of
N
, which will lead to equivalent update rules.

Appendix E
Optimisation problem for the quantum Schrödinger bridge

Unlike the classical scenario, the standard QSB is formulated without starting from a minimisation problem. The question of the distance measure to be minimised to obtain the quantum Schödinger problem was left open in
[
31
]
. On the other hand, the Petz map can be obtained as a solution for the quantum version of the Minimum Change Principle (MCP)
[
6
]
. Thus, it is natural to ask whether the optimisation problem for the QSB can be obtained by quantising the MCP.

The quantisation can be done by substituting probability distributions with quantum states, stochastic matrices with CPTP maps, and joint probability distributions with Quantum States Over Time (QSOT). In
[
6
]
, the QSOT is defined from a channel and an input state:

\mathcal{E}\star\rho=\left(\mathds{I}_{B}\otimes\sqrt{\rho^{\textsf{T}}}\right)C_{\mathcal{E}}\left(\mathds{I}_{B}\otimes\sqrt{\rho^{\textsf{T}}}\right),

(69)

where
C_{\mathcal{E}}
is the Choi–Jamiołkowski isomorphism of the channel
\mathcal{E}
. The marginals of the QSOT would give the input and output states:

\text{Tr}_{B}[\mathcal{E}\star\rho]=\rho,\quad\quad\text{Tr}_{A}[\mathcal{E}\star\rho]=\mathcal{E}[\rho].

(70)

Thus, a straightforward quantisation of Eq. (
15
) is given by

\begin{array}[]{r c l}\mathcal{F}=&\displaystyle\argmin_{\mathcal{F}^{\prime}}&\displaystyle\textsf{D}(\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\star\rho\|\mathcal{E}\star\eta).\\
&\text{subject to}&\displaystyle\text{Tr}_{B}[\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\star\rho]=\rho\\
&&\displaystyle\text{Tr}_{A}[\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\star\rho]=\omega\end{array}

(71)

Here
\eta
is the input marginal distribution that is in the initial prior process. It is the quantum analogue of
\sum_{y}A(x,y)
in the classical version of the problem. However, here we quickly encounter a problem: the solution of this optimisation depends on
\eta
, whereas in the classical problem it does not. In the classical version, we have

\displaystyle\textsf{D}(B\|A)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y}B(x,y)\log\left(\frac{B(x,y)}{\mathcal{E}(y|x)\eta(x)}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y}B(x,y)\log B(x,y)-\sum_{x,y}B(x,y)\log\mathcal{E}(y|x)-\sum_{x}p(x)\log\eta(x).

Here, the last term is a constant that is fixed by the setup of the problem, thus the solution will not depend on
\eta
. However, the same cannot be said for the quantised version of the problem, regardless of whether one uses the Umegaki relative entropy
[
68
]
, the Belavkin-Staszewski relative entropy
[
10
]
, or the quantum fidelity. Thus, the QSB, either in its generic form
\mathcal{F_{\mathsf{gen}}}
in Eq. (
36
) or the inference-consistent form
\mathcal{F}
in Eq. (
47
) will not be a solution of Eq. (
71
) as it is currently written, as they both do not depend on
\eta
. With these notes, we leave the problem of formalising an optimisation problem for the Schrödinger bridge open for future exploration.

Appendix F
\mathcal{F_{\mathsf{Herm}}}
does not meet the conditions for Theorem
6

Setting
\Xi\mapsto\Gamma
for Eq. (
41
), alongside Eq. (
44
), we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{\mathsf{\Xi\mapsto\Gamma}}_{\mathsf{Herm}}[\rho]=\sqrt{\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathcal{E}[\gamma]}}\omega\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathcal{E}[\gamma]}}}\mathcal{E}\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma}}\rho\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma}}}}\rho\frac{1}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma}}\rho\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma}}}}\right]\sqrt{\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathcal{E}[\gamma]}}\omega\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathcal{E}[\gamma]}}}\neq\omega.

Of course, if it so happens that
[\omega,\mathcal{E}[\gamma]]=0
and
[\rho,\gamma]=0
, then we do have a valid QSB. But this is generally not the case, and so
\mathcal{F_{\mathsf{Herm}}}
does not feature inference consistency as per Theorem

.

One final remark here is that the symmetry between the Hermitian QSB
\mathcal{F_{\mathsf{Herm}}}
and quantum prior hacking may be traced to the classical regime. There, we have the two coupled equations

\displaystyle\Gamma

\displaystyle=q\oslash(\mathcal{E}\gamma)

(72)

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle=p\oslash(\mathcal{E}^{\textsf{T}}\Gamma).

(73)

When the problem is quantised, the prior hacking approach leads naturally to the relations

\displaystyle\Gamma

\displaystyle=\mathcal{D}_{\omega}(\mathcal{E}[\gamma])

(74)

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle=\mathcal{D}_{\rho}^{\prime}(\mathcal{E}[\Gamma]),

(75)

where
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{D}
are defined in Eqs (

\displaystyle\Xi

\displaystyle=\mathcal{D}_{\omega}^{\prime}(\mathcal{E}[\xi])

(76)

\displaystyle\xi

\displaystyle=\mathcal{D}_{\rho}(\mathcal{E}[\Xi]).

(77)

Note the slight difference in the choice of the operations. Therefore, these opposing transformations leads to them being symmetrical but inequivalent problems. As previously alluded to, it is clear that if everything commutes (that is, if everything is classical) these two problems do in fact collapse to being the same.

Appendix G
More Details on Prior hacking for Illustrative Qubit Channels

G.1
Depolarising channels

The depolarising channel is perhaps the most common type of channel in which the image is full rank over the whole domain. It is defined as

\mathcal{D}[\rho_{0}]=\lambda\frac{\mathds{I}}{2}+(1-\lambda)\rho_{0},

(78)

with some parameter
\lambda
that determines the strength of the channel. Simply put, it is a mixing of the original input state with white noise.

Here we will first summarise the behaviour of the Petz map for the depolarising channel, while the full mathematical expressions can be found in the next sections. First, setting
\lambda=1
, we get
\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{\gamma}[\omega]\big\rvert_{\lambda=1}=\gamma
, which recovers the erasure channel result of making prior hacking trivial. Conversely, setting
\lambda=0
yields the unitary channel result with
\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{\gamma}[\omega]\big\rvert_{\lambda=0}=\vec{\omega}
, i.e., no dependence on
\gamma
and making prior hacking impossible.

Next, we denote the length of the Bloch vector
\gamma
as
|\vec{r}|
, which is simply a measure of the purity of
\gamma
. If we evaluate it for a fully mixed state prior (
|\vec{r}|=0
), we get
\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{\gamma}[\omega]\big\rvert_{|\vec{r}|=0}=(1-\lambda)\omega+\lambda\frac{\mathds{1}}{2}=\omega^{\prime}
. On the other hand, for a pure state prior (
|\vec{r}|=1
) we have
\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{\gamma}[\omega]\big\rvert_{|\vec{r}|=1}=\gamma
. For a fixed purity value
|\vec{r}|
in between the two extremes, the set of output states would form a deformed sphere inside the Bloch sphere that is shifted in the
\hat{z}
direction. In other words, as we shift from
|\vec{r}|=0
to
|\vec{r}|=1
, this deformed sphere will continuously go from covering just a single point
\omega^{\prime}
to the whole Bloch sphere. Because
\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{\gamma}[\omega]
is continuous everywhere, there cannot be any ‘tearing’, ‘puncturing’, or sudden ‘jumps’ in this transition. This means that every point in the Bloch sphere must be hit at some point by some value of
\gamma
, thus making prior hacking possible. This is the intuition behind Theorem
4
.

G.1.1
Deriving the Petz map

The channel is defined by the operation

\mathcal{D}(\rho)=\lambda\frac{\mathds{I}}{2}+(1-\lambda)\rho,

(79)

with
0\leq\lambda\leq 1
, with the extreme cases being a unitary and an erasure channel. The Kraus operators are given by

\displaystyle K_{0}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1-\frac{3}{4}\lambda}\mathds{I},

\displaystyle K_{i}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\lambda}{4}}\sigma_{i},\qquad i\in\{x,y,z\}.

Let
\gamma
be a state with the Bloch vector
\vec{r}=(r_{x},r_{y},r_{z})
, with length
|\vec{r}|
. Let
\hat{n}=\frac{\vec{r}}{|\vec{r}|}
. The eigenvectors of
\gamma
are then given by
\ket{\hat{+n}}
and
\ket{\hat{-n}}
, with the associated eigenvalues
\mu_{\pm}=\frac{1\pm|\vec{r|}}{2}
. Moreover, the depolarising channel does not change the eigenbasis of the state, thus the eigenvectors of
\mathcal{D}[\gamma]
remain the same, while its associated eigenvalues, denoted by
a_{i}
, are given by

a_{\pm}=\frac{\lambda}{2}+(1-\lambda)\mu_{\pm}=\frac{1\pm|\vec{r}|(1-\lambda)}{2}.

(80)

Finally, define
P_{\pm}=\ket{\pm\hat{n}}\bra{\pm\hat{n}}
.

The depolarising map is self-adjoint, and thus the Kraus operators of the Petz recovery map are given by

M_{\alpha}=\gamma^{\frac{1}{2}}K_{\alpha}\mathcal{D}[\gamma]^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

(81)

For the identity term, we have
M_{0}=\sqrt{1-\frac{3}{4}\lambda}\sum_{j=\{+,-\}}\sqrt{\frac{\mu_{j}}{a_{j}}}P_{j}
. Meanwhile, for the other terms, we have
M_{i}=\sqrt{\frac{\lambda}{4}}\sum_{j,k=\{+,-\}}\sqrt{\frac{\mu_{j}}{a_{k}}}P_{j}\sigma_{i}P_{k}
. Applying the Kraus operators to a state
\omega
, we get

M_{0}\omega M_{0}^{\dagger}=\left(1-\frac{3}{4}\lambda\right)\sum_{j,k}\sqrt{\frac{\mu_{j}\mu_{k}}{a_{j}a_{k}}}P_{j}\omega P_{k},

(82)

for the first operator, and

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{3}M_{i}\omega M_{i}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda}{4}\gamma^{\frac{1}{2}}\sigma_{i}\mathcal{D}[\gamma]^{-\frac{1}{2}}\omega\mathcal{D}[\gamma]^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sigma_{i}\gamma^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda}{4}\gamma^{\frac{1}{2}}\left[2\text{Tr}\left(\mathcal{D}[\gamma]^{-\frac{1}{2}}\omega\mathcal{D}[\gamma]^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)-\mathcal{D}[\gamma]^{-\frac{1}{2}}\omega\mathcal{D}[\gamma]^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right]\gamma^{\frac{1}{2}},

(83)

for the other operators. The second equality is obtained by applying the Pauli matrix identity
\sum_{i=0}^{3}\sigma_{i}A\sigma_{i}=2\text{Tr}(A)\mathds{1}-A
. The second term is identical to the
M_{0}\omega M_{0}^{\dagger}
term except for the
\lambda
coefficient, which allows them to be summed up together to
1-\lambda
. The first term can be written as

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda}{2}\gamma\text{Tr}(\omega\mathcal{D}[\gamma]^{-1})=\frac{\lambda}{2}\gamma\text{Tr}\left[\frac{\omega P_{+}}{a_{+}}+\frac{\omega P_{-}}{a_{-}}\right].

(84)

Finally, since the channel is spherically symmetric, without loss of generality, we can fix
\omega
to the Bloch vector
\vec{s}=(0,0,s)
. This will allow us to simplify the first term further into

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda}{2}\gamma\text{Tr}\left[\frac{\omega P_{+}}{a_{+}}+\frac{\omega P_{-}}{a_{-}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda}{4}\gamma\text{Tr}\left[\frac{1+n_{z}s}{a_{+}}+\frac{1-n_{z}s}{a_{-}}\right]

\displaystyle=\lambda\gamma\left[\frac{1-|\vec{r}|n_{z}s(1-\lambda)}{1-(1-\lambda)^{2}|\vec{r}|^{2}}\right].

Finally, the map is then given by

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{\gamma}(\omega)=\frac{1-\lambda}{2}\sum_{j,k}\sqrt{\frac{\mu_{j}\mu_{k}}{a_{j}a_{k}}}P_{j}(\mathds{1}+s\sigma_{z})P_{k}+\lambda\gamma\left[\frac{1-|\vec{r}|n_{z}s(1-\lambda)}{1-(1-\lambda)^{2}|\vec{r}|^{2}}\right].

(85)

Let us expand the first term. The terms that are diagonal in the eigenbasis of
\gamma
, given by
j=k
give

\frac{\mu_{\pm}}{a_{\pm}}P_{\pm}(\mathds{1}+s\sigma_{z})P_{\pm}=\frac{\mu_{\pm}}{a_{\pm}}(1\pm sn_{z})P_{\pm},

(86)

while the off-diagonal terms with
j\neq k
would yield
\sqrt{\frac{\mu_{+}\mu_{-}}{a_{+}a_{-}}}P_{\pm}(\mathds{1}+s\sigma_{z})P_{\mp}=s\sqrt{\frac{\mu_{+}\mu_{-}}{a_{+}a_{-}}}P_{\pm}\sigma_{z}P_{\mp}
. We then apply the identity
P_{+}\sigma_{z}P_{-}+P_{-}\sigma_{z}P_{+}=\sigma_{z}-n_{z}(\hat{n}\cdot\vec{\sigma})
to simplify it to

s\sqrt{\frac{\mu_{+}\mu_{-}}{a_{+}a_{-}}}(\sigma_{z}-n_{z}(\hat{n}\cdot\vec{\sigma})).

(87)

The Bloch vector of the final state is given by
\text{Tr}(\vec{\sigma}\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{\gamma}(\omega))
. The
\gamma
-diagonal terms give
\frac{1-\lambda}{2}\left[\left(\frac{\mu_{+}}{a_{+}}-\frac{\mu_{-}}{a_{-}}\right)+sn_{z}\left(\frac{\mu_{+}}{a_{+}}+\frac{\mu_{-}}{a_{-}}\right)\right]\hat{n}
, the off-diagonal terms give
(1-\lambda)s\sqrt{\frac{\mu_{+}\mu_{-}}{a_{+}a_{-}}}(\hat{z}-n_{z}\hat{n})
, while the final term give
\lambda\left[\frac{1-|\vec{r}|n_{z}s(1-\lambda)}{1-(1-\lambda)^{2}|\vec{r}|^{2}}\right]\vec{r}
. Denote the output vector to be
\vec{b}_{\mathcal{D},\gamma,\omega}
. Combining the three terms, we have

\displaystyle\vec{b}_{\mathcal{D},\gamma,\omega}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\lambda}{2}\left[\left(\frac{\mu_{+}}{a_{+}}-\frac{\mu_{-}}{a_{-}}\right)+sn_{z}\left(\frac{\mu_{+}}{a_{+}}+\frac{\mu_{-}}{a_{-}}\right)\right]\hat{n}+(1-\lambda)s\sqrt{\frac{\mu_{+}\mu_{-}}{a_{+}a_{-}}}(\hat{z}-n_{z}\hat{n})+\lambda\left[\frac{1-|\vec{r}|n_{z}s(1-\lambda)}{1-(1-\lambda)^{2}|\vec{r}|^{2}}\right]\vec{r}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\lambda}{2}\left[\left(\frac{\mu_{+}}{a_{+}}-\frac{\mu_{-}}{a_{-}}\right)+sn_{z}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\mu_{+}}{a_{+}}}-\sqrt{\frac{\mu_{-}}{a_{-}}}\right)^{2}\right]\hat{n}+(1-\lambda)s\sqrt{\frac{\mu_{+}\mu_{-}}{a_{+}a_{-}}}\hat{z}+\lambda\left[\frac{1-|\vec{r}|n_{z}s(1-\lambda)}{1-(1-\lambda)^{2}|\vec{r}|^{2}}\right]\vec{r}.

(88)

G.1.2
Finding the limits

Now we will derive the limits of the map as outlined. First, setting
\lambda=1
recovers the erasure channel result with

\vec{b}_{\mathcal{D},\gamma,\omega}\bigg\rvert_{\lambda=1}=\vec{r},

(89)

meaning that to obtain any evidence
\rho
we just need to set
\gamma=\rho
. Meanwhile, the other extreme of
\lambda=0
would completely erase the dependence on
\gamma
:

\vec{b}_{\mathcal{D},\gamma,\omega}\bigg\rvert_{\lambda=0}=s\hat{z}=\vec{s},

(90)

which makes prior hacking impossible. Next, when we evaluate it at
|\vec{r}|=0
, we get
\mu_{\pm}=a_{\pm}=\frac{1}{2}
and the first and third term go to zero, leaving only

\vec{b}_{\mathcal{D},\gamma,\omega}\bigg\rvert_{|\vec{r}|=0}=(1-\lambda)s\hat{z}.

(91)

This is simply the Bloch vector associated with the state
\omega^{\prime}=(1-\lambda)\omega+\lambda\frac{\mathds{1}}{2}
. On the other hand, when we evaluate
|\vec{r}|=1
, we get

\vec{b}_{\mathcal{D},\gamma,\omega}\bigg\rvert_{|\vec{r}|=1}=\vec{r}.

(92)

G.2
Dephasing channels

The dephasing channel
\mathcal{Z}
is given by Kraus operators

Z_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&\sqrt{1-\lambda}\end{pmatrix},\quad Z_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&\sqrt{\lambda}\end{pmatrix}.

(93)

Intuitively, it reduces the off-diagonal terms in the input state, bringing it closer to a decoherent state. It is an example of a channel that does not fulfil the requirement of Theorem
4
. For
\rho_{0}=\ket{i}\bra{i}
,
\mathcal{Z}[\ket{i}\bra{i}]=\ket{i}\bra{i}
and is thus not full rank. Because of this reason, it is quite straightforward to see that prior hacking is impossible if the target conclusion is either one of the pure computational state
\rho=\ket{i}\bra{i}
. Normally a pure choice conclusion is prior-hackable by setting
\gamma
to be the same pure state. However, in the case for this channel, the Petz map is undefined for
\gamma=\ket{i}\bra{i}
due to its rank deficiency. However, there are other examples of a combination of
(\rho,\omega)
that is prior-hackable still. This is most easily seen in the
completely dephasing channel
, given by taking
\lambda=1
.

Theorem 7
.

For a completely dephasing channel
\mathcal{Z}
, an evidence
\omega
, and a decoherent conclusion
\rho
, prior hacking is possible if and only if both
\omega
and
\rho
have the same diagonals in the
Z
-basis, i.e.,
\rho=\mathcal{Z}[\omega]
.

Proof.

The prior hacking equation to be solved is given by

\rho=\sqrt{\gamma}\mathcal{Z}\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathcal{Z}[\gamma]}}\omega\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathcal{Z}[\gamma]}}\right]\sqrt{\gamma}.

(94)

Note that the dephasing channel is self-adjoint. We can further simplify it by:

\displaystyle\rho

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\gamma}\mathcal{Z}\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathcal{Z}[\gamma]}}\omega\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathcal{Z}[\gamma]}}\right]\sqrt{\gamma}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\gamma}\mathcal{Z}[\gamma])^{-1/2}\mathcal{Z}[\omega](\mathcal{Z}[\gamma])^{-1/2}\sqrt{\gamma}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\gamma}(\mathcal{Z}[\gamma])^{-1}\mathcal{Z}[\omega]\sqrt{\gamma}.

(95)

Now, define

D=(\mathcal{Z}[\gamma])^{-1}\mathcal{Z}[\omega]:=\begin{pmatrix}d_{0}&0\\
0&d_{1}\end{pmatrix},

(96)

with
d_{0},d_{1}\geq 0
. Moreover, they cannot be both zero, as that requires
\omega
to have zero trace. Now, write

\sqrt{\gamma}:=\begin{pmatrix}x&y\\
y^{*}&z\end{pmatrix},

(97)

with
x,z\geq 0
and
xz\geq|y|^{2}
. Then, we can get

\rho=\sqrt{\gamma}D\sqrt{\gamma}=\begin{pmatrix}x^{2}d_{0}+|y|^{2}d_{1}&y(xd_{0}+zd_{1})\\
y^{*}(xd_{0}+zd_{1})&|y|^{2}d_{0}+z^{2}d_{1}\end{pmatrix}.

(98)

If
\gamma
has any initial coherence, then
|y|>0
, which also implies
x,z>0
. However, since
\rho
is decoherent, this means that
y(xd_{0}+zd_{1})=0
. Since it is impossible for both
d_{0}
and
d_{1}
to be zero, this presents a contradiction. Thus,
\gamma
must be decoherent too. However, if
\gamma
is decoherent, then
\mathcal{Z}[\omega]
commutes with
\gamma
and Eq. (
G.2
) can be written as

\rho=\mathcal{Z}[\omega].

(99)

Thus, prior hacking is possible if and only if both
\rho
and
\omega
are in the same
Z
-coordinate in the Bloch sphere.
∎
