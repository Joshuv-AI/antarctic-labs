Statistical Inference for Homogenization Limits Driven by Wiener or Hermite Processes
Pablo Ramses Alonso-Martin

Abstract
We study the effective estimation of the diffusivity and Hurst parameter for the homogenized limit of a class of slow/fast systems. Depending on the system parameters, this limit solves a stochastic differential equation driven by either a Wiener process or a Hermite process. In the class of models we consider, the fast variable is a fractional Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process. We show that estimators constructed from the homogenized limit remain consistent when applied to appropriately subsampled data generated by the original slow/fast system. A key tool in our analysis is the consistency of renormalized quadratic variations for a family of additive functionals of the fast process. Using Wiener chaos expansions, we obtain an L 2 L^{2} -orthogonal decomposition of these renormalized quadratic variations. This allows us to show that, under appropriate subsampling conditions, the consistency properties of the estimators are preserved even when the data is generated by the slow/fast system rather than the homogenized limit. We also show that, under stricter subsampling conditions, a non-central limit theorem is preserved in the case where the fluctuations of the estimator around the true value are non-Gaussian. As a direct consequence of convergence in L 2 L^{2} , we obtain consistency of an estimator for the limiting self-similarity that does not require knowledge of the limiting diffusivity. Finally, we show that our results apply to a class of one-dimensional fluctuation models.



1
Introduction

The primary goal of this article is to study the behaviour of the quantity

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:estimators}C_{\varepsilon,\delta}=\begin{cases}N^{2H^{*}(m)-1}\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\bigl(X^{\varepsilon}_{(i+1)\delta}-X^{\varepsilon}_{i\delta}\bigr)^{2}\quad&H^{*}(m)>1/2\\
\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\bigl(X^{\varepsilon}_{(i+1)\delta}-X^{\varepsilon}_{i\delta}\bigr)^{2}\quad&H^{*}(m)\leq 1/2\end{cases}

(1.1)

where
X^{\varepsilon}_{t}
is the first component of the solution to the system of equations

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:multiscalesystem}\begin{cases}dX^{\varepsilon}_{t}=\alpha(\varepsilon,H^{*}(m))G(Y^{\varepsilon}_{t})dt\\
dY^{\varepsilon}_{t}=-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}Y_{t}^{\varepsilon}dt+\frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon^{H}}dB^{H}_{t}\end{cases}

(1.2)

for some
G\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\gamma)
with Hermite rank
m\geq 1
. Here
B^{H}_{t}
is a fractional Brownian motion with Hurst parameter

H\in(0,1)
and
\gamma=\mathcal{N}(0,1)
is the invariant measure of the fast process
Y^{\varepsilon}_{t}
. The quantity
H^{*}(m)=(H-1)m+1

determines the qualitative behaviour of the limiting process and the scaling factor
\alpha(\varepsilon,H^{*}(m))
is defined as

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:scalingfactor}\alpha(\varepsilon,H^{*}(m))=\begin{cases}\varepsilon^{H^{*}(m)-1}\quad&H^{*}(m)>1/2\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}|\log(\varepsilon)|}\quad&H^{*}(m)=1/2\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\quad&H^{*}(m)<1/2\end{cases}

(1.3)

As shown in
[
4
,
8
]
under suitable assumptions on
G
the slow variable
X^{\varepsilon}_{t}
converges weakly in appropriate path spaces to an effective process
X_{t}
which, depending on the regularity of the fast process and the Hermite rank of
G
, is either a rescaled Wiener process or a rescaled Hermite process of order
m
:

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:effectivelimit}X_{t}\;=\;\begin{cases}\sqrt{C}\,W_{t},&H^{*}(m)\leq 1/2,\\[3.99994pt]
\sqrt{C}\,Z_{t}^{H^{*}(m),m},&H^{*}(m)>1/2,\end{cases}

(1.4)

where the scaling constant
C=\mathbb{E}[X_{1}^{2}]
encodes the size of the fluctuations of the limit.

The interest in such quantities (
1.1
) stems from their use as statistical estimators for the fluctuation size and Hurst parameter in the limiting equation of the slow process
X^{\varepsilon}_{t}
as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
.
Such systems arise naturally in the context of rough homogenization for random ODEs of the form

\dot{x}^{\varepsilon}_{t}=h(x^{\varepsilon}_{t})g(y^{\varepsilon}_{t})+\alpha(\varepsilon)G(y^{\varepsilon}_{t})

where the solution to the slow equation in (
1.2
) acts as a driver but also plays the role of a second order term in the perturbation expansion of the vector field that determines the evolution of interest,
allowing us to study the fluctuations of the slow process around its averaged limit.

The weak convergence stated above makes the pair
(H^{\prime},C)
, with
H^{\prime}=H^{*}(m)\vee 1/2
the self-similarity index of the limiting process, the natural target of statistical inference: together they determine the second-order fluctuation structure of
X^{\varepsilon}
. A finer inferential question would be to characterize the limiting process uniquely, which in the regime
H^{*}(m)>1/2
requires also the Hermite rank
m
of
G
. Estimating
m
directly would, however, demand either observing the fast process
Y^{\varepsilon}
or knowing
G
explicitly—both of which defeat one of the main motivations for multiscale inference—and we therefore leave it outside the scope of this work. For most applications it is in any case sufficient to distinguish a Wiener limit (
H^{*}(m)\leq 1/2
) from a long-range dependent one (
H^{*}(m)>1/2
), and, within the latter regime, the Gaussian case
m=1
(fractional Brownian motion) from the non-Gaussian cases
m\geq 2
, the first of which is the Rosenblatt process
[
17
]
. Beyond
m=2
little is known about the distribution of Hermite processes and, to the best of our knowledge, their simulation has only been addressed very recently
[
1
]
, so resolving
m
past the Gaussian/non-Gaussian dichotomy is of limited practical interest.

We follow a
plug-in
inference strategy: we take estimators that are known to be consistent for the parameters of the effective equation and apply them to discrete observations of the multiscale process
X^{\varepsilon}
, asking under what joint regimes of the sampling step
\delta
and the scale separation
\varepsilon
the classical guarantees survive. The quantity
C_{\varepsilon,\delta}
defined in (
1.1
) is precisely the estimator we use for
C
: when
H^{*}(m)\leq 1/2
the limit is a Wiener process and
C_{\varepsilon,\delta}
reduces to its standard quadratic variation; when
H^{*}(m)>1/2
the limit exhibits long-range dependence and consistency of
C_{\varepsilon,\delta}
on the effective process is known from
[
15
]
for the fractional Brownian case
m=1
, from
[
16
]
for the Rosenblatt case
m=2
, and from
[
6
]
for higher orders. To establish consistency of
C_{\varepsilon,\delta}
on the multiscale process we follow a strategy similar to
[
16
,
6
]
, with the additional complication of having to control the multiscale nature of the problem.

Because the normalization of
C_{\varepsilon,\delta}
depends explicitly on
H^{\prime}
, estimating
C
presupposes access to the self-similarity index, so we also need an estimator for
H^{\prime}
. We consider the ratio estimator

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:estimatorofH}\hat{H}_{\varepsilon,\delta}=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2\log 2}\log\!{\left(\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{2N-1}\bigl(X^{\varepsilon}_{(i+1)\delta/2}-X^{\varepsilon}_{i\delta/2}\bigr)^{2}}{\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\bigl(X^{\varepsilon}_{(i+1)\delta}-X^{\varepsilon}_{i\delta}\bigr)^{2}}\right)},

(1.5)

which compares quadratic variations on two nested partitions. A more orthodox transformation of a single quadratic variation would require the knowledge of
C
to estimate

; the ratio is instead scale-invariant, and once

-consistency of
C_{\varepsilon,\delta}
is established, consistency of
\hat{H}_{\varepsilon,\delta}
follows by the continuous mapping theorem. This motivates the two-step procedure we analyze throughout the article: first estimate
H^{\prime}
via
\hat{H}_{\varepsilon,\delta}
, then plug the estimate into the normalization of
C_{\varepsilon,\delta}
to recover
C
.

1.1
Organization of the article and Notation

The rest of the article is organized as follows. In Section 2 we introduce all the preliminary concepts required for the rest of the manuscript. In Section 3 we motivate the need for a subsampling scheme when observing the slow process of a multiscale system like (
1.2
) by
showing that the quadratic variation of the slow process is asymptotically biased when the process is observed at an unconditional high-frequency. In Section 4 we show that under an appropriate subsampling rate the renormalized quadratic variation is asymptotically unbiased and we
provide explicit rates. Finally, specializing to the case where the estimator belongs to a finite Wiener chaos we show in Section 5 that the renormalized quadratic variation is consistent in all cases and in Section 6 we prove a non-Central Limit Theorem that resembles the classical results
for Hermite processes but using multiscale observations.

We will use the notation
A(x)\sim B(x)
to denote
\lim_{x}\frac{A(x)}{B(x)}=1
where
x
will either go to
\infty
or to
0
depending on the context. In other words, it denotes asymptotic equivalence not just up to the order of magnitude but
also to proportionality constants. We will also use the notation
A(x)\asymp B(x)
to denote asymptotic equivalence up to a constant. That means
\lim_{x}\frac{A(x)}{B(x)}=C<\infty
for some unspecified constant
|C|>0
.
We will also use
A(x)\lesssim B(x)
to denote that
\lim\frac{A(x)}{B(x)}=0
where
x
may vanish or blow up depending on the context. This is equivalent to being asymptotically bounded up to an unspecified constant.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Analysis on the Wiener space

We work on an irreducible Gaussian probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
carrying an isonormal Gaussian family
\{W(h):h\in H\}
indexed by the separable Hilbert space
H=L^{2}(\mathbb{R},dt)
; that is,
h\mapsto W(h)
is linear and
\mathbb{E}[W(h)W(g)]=\langle h,g\rangle_{H}
. In our setting
W(h)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}h(s)\,dW_{s}
is the Wiener integral against a standard Brownian motion
W_{t}
.

The Hermite polynomials

H_{n}(x)=(-1)^{n}e^{x^{2}/2}\frac{d^{n}}{dx^{n}}e^{-x^{2}/2}

form an orthogonal basis of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\gamma)
with
\gamma=\mathcal{N}(0,1)
: every
G\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\gamma)
admits an expansion
G(x)=\sum_{n\geq m}c_{n}H_{n}(x)
, and the smallest
m
with
c_{m}\neq 0
is the
Hermite rank
of
G
. Their behaviour under jointly Gaussian arguments is characterised by the following classical identity, which we will invoke throughout.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
(X,Y)
be a jointly Gaussian vector with
\mathbb{E}[X]=\mathbb{E}[Y]=0
and
V[X]=V[Y]=1
. Then

\mathbb{E}[H_{m}(X)H_{n}(Y)]=\begin{cases}n!\,\mathbb{E}[XY]^{n},&m=n,\\
0,&m\neq n.\end{cases}

Denote by
\mathcal{H}_{n}
the
L^{2}(\Omega)
-closure of
\mathrm{Span}\{H_{n}(W(h)):\|h\|_{H}=1\}
. We then have the orthogonal decomposition
L^{2}(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})=\bigoplus_{n\geq 0}\mathcal{H}_{n}
.

For
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
the multiple Wiener–Itô integral

I_{n}(f)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\,dW(x_{1})\cdots dW(x_{n})

is the unique linear isometry
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to L^{2}(\Omega)
extending the natural definition on product indicators. Writing
\mathrm{Sym}(f)
for the symmetrisation of
f
one has
I_{n}(f)=I_{n}(\mathrm{Sym}(f))
and

\mathbb{E}[I_{p}(f)I_{q}(g)]=\delta_{pq}\,p!\,\langle\mathrm{Sym}(f),\mathrm{Sym}(g)\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{p})}.

For symmetric
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{p})
,
g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{q})
and
0\leq r\leq p\wedge q
, the
r
-contraction

(f\otimes_{r}g)(x_{1},\dots,x_{p-r},y_{1},\dots,y_{q-r})=\\
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{r}}f(x_{1},\dots,x_{p-r},s_{1},\dots,s_{r})\,g(y_{1},\dots,y_{q-r},s_{1},\dots,s_{r})\,ds_{1}\cdots ds_{r}

is an element of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{p+q-2r})
; we write
f\tilde{\otimes}_{r}g:=\mathrm{Sym}(f\otimes_{r}g)
for its symmetrisation and
f^{\otimes n}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=f(x_{1})\cdots f(x_{n})
for the
n
-th tensor power of
f
. Multiple Wiener–Itô integrals obey the product formula

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:productstructure}I_{p}(f)\,I_{q}(g)=\sum_{r=0}^{p\wedge q}r!\binom{p}{r}\binom{q}{r}\,I_{p+q-2r}(f\tilde{\otimes}_{r}g),

(2.1)

which is the main computational tool in the chaos decomposition of
C_{\varepsilon,\delta}
. Finally,
I_{n}
realises an isometric isomorphism between symmetric
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\mathcal{H}_{n}
via

H_{n}(W(h))=I_{n}(h^{\otimes n}),\qquad\|h\|_{H}=1.

2.2
Fractional Brownian Motion

A fractional Brownian motion (fBM) with Hurst parameter
H\in(0,1)
is the centred continuous Gaussian process
(B^{H}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
with covariance

\mathbb{E}[B^{H}_{t}B^{H}_{s}]=\frac{1}{2}(t^{2H}+s^{2H}-|t-s|^{2H}).

The Mandelbrot–Van Ness representation
[
10
]
expresses it as a Wiener integral against a standard Brownian motion,

B^{H}_{t}=\tilde{c}_{H}^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}}K^{H}(t,s)\,dW_{s},

with an explicit kernel
K^{H}
whose precise form we will not need. As a consequence, integration against
B^{H}
reduces to integration against
W
: it is shown in
[
11
,
12
]
that there exists a linear operator
T_{H}
, given by fractional integration when
H>1/2
and by fractional differentiation when
H<1/2
, such that

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:fBMWienerintegralrelation}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(s)\,dB^{H}_{s}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}T_{H}f(s)\,dW_{s}

(2.2)

for every
f
with
T_{H}f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, the identity holding in
L^{2}(\Omega)
.

2.3
Fast component: time-rescaled fractional Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process

We take
Y^{\varepsilon}_{t}
to be the unique stationary solution to

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:fOUSDE}dY_{t}=-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}Y_{t}\,dt+\frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon^{H}}dB^{H}_{t},

(2.3)

with
\sigma^{2}=\frac{2}{\Gamma(2H+1)}
chosen so that
\mathbb{E}[(Y^{\varepsilon}_{t})^{2}]=1
. It admits the closed-form representation

Y^{\varepsilon}_{t}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}P_{t,\varepsilon}(s)\,dB^{H}_{s},\qquad P_{t,\varepsilon}(s)=\frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon^{H}}e^{-(t-s)/\varepsilon}{\mathbf{1}}_{(-\infty,t]}(s),

so (
2.2
) identifies
Y^{\varepsilon}_{t}
as an element of the first Wiener chaos of
W
:

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:fOUasWienerintegral}Y^{\varepsilon}_{t}=p\int_{\mathbb{R}}K_{t,\varepsilon}(s)\,dW_{s},\qquad K_{t,\varepsilon}:=T_{H}P_{t,\varepsilon}.

(2.4)

Since
P_{t,\varepsilon}(s)=P_{0,\varepsilon}(s-t)
and
T_{H}
commutes with translations, the kernel
K_{t,\varepsilon}
is a time-shift of its value at
t=0
: setting
K^{\varepsilon}:=K_{0,\varepsilon}
and writing
(\tau_{t}f)(u):=f(u-t)
for the time-shift operator, we have
K_{t,\varepsilon}=\tau_{t}K^{\varepsilon}
. This shorthand will be used throughout the chaos decomposition of
C_{\varepsilon,\delta}
as it will prove very useful when dealing with inner products. For
H>1/2
, the kernel
K^{\varepsilon}
admits the explicit representation

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:fOUkernelrepresentation}K^{\varepsilon}(s)=\varepsilon^{-1/2}e^{-s/\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{s/\varepsilon}e^{v}\,v^{H-3/2}\,dv\qquad(s>0),

(2.5)

and carries a normalisation constant
p>0
given by

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:pconstant}p^{2}=\frac{1}{\beta(H-1/2,\,2H-2)\,\Gamma(2H-1)},

(2.6)

which we record here because it will play an important role in the kernel computations of Section
B
.

The covariance
\varrho(s):=\mathbb{E}[Y^{1}_{0}Y^{1}_{s}]
, extended symmetrically by
\varrho(-s)=\varrho(s)
, and its rescaled version
\varrho^{\varepsilon}(s):=\mathbb{E}[Y^{\varepsilon}_{0}Y^{\varepsilon}_{s}]=\varrho(s/\varepsilon)
, exhibit the long-range decay

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:rhoasymptotic}\varrho(s)\sim\sigma^{2}H(2H-1)\,s^{2H-2}=:\kappa_{H}\,s^{2H-2}\qquad(s\to\infty),

(2.7)

which follows from taking
N=1
in Theorem 2.3 of
[
5
]
. The leading constant
\kappa_{H}
is negative for
H<1/2
and equals
1/\Gamma(2H-1)
for
H>1/2
; it appears as the leading prefactor of the chaos-asymptotic estimates of Section
A
, where the relation
\varrho(s)^{q}\sim\kappa_{H}^{q}\,|s|^{q(2H-2)}
for
q\geq 1
will be used repeatedly.

2.4
Hermite Processes

For
H\in(1/2,1)
and
m\in\mathbb{N}
the
Hermite process of order
m
and self-similarity
H
is the element of the
m
-th Wiener chaos of
W
defined by

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:gehringerhermiteprocess}Z_{t}^{H,m}=\frac{K(H,m)}{m!}\,I_{m}(f_{t}^{H,m}),\qquad f_{t}^{H,m}(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})=\int_{0}^{t}\prod_{j=1}^{m}(s-x_{j})_{+}^{-{\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1-H}{m}\right)}}\,ds,

(2.8)

where the normalising constant is given explicitly by

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:hermitenormalisation}K(H,m)={\left(\frac{m!\,H(2H-1)}{\beta\!{\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{H-1}{m},\,2\frac{1-H}{m}\right)}^{m}}\right)}^{1/2},

(2.9)

so that
\mathbb{E}[(Z_{1}^{H,m})^{2}]=1
; see
[
14
,
7
]
for a derivation. The process
Z^{H,m}
is self-similar with parameter
H
, has stationary increments, and covariance

\mathbb{E}[Z_{t}^{H,m}Z_{s}^{H,m}]=\frac{1}{2}\bigl(t^{2H}+s^{2H}-|t-s|^{2H}\bigr).

For
m=1
, (
2.8
) reduces to the Mandelbrot–Van Ness representation of a fBM; for
m=2
it is the Rosenblatt process, and for
m\geq 2
the process is non-Gaussian.

3
Sampling without scale separation

The two results below highlight what goes wrong if the sampling scheme does not take the scale-separation parameter
\varepsilon
into account, and together motivate the rest of the article. Proposition
3.1
shows that if one samples on scales finer than
\varepsilon
— that is,
\delta/\varepsilon\to 0
— the quadratic-variation functional
C_{\varepsilon,\delta}
collapses to zero. Proposition
3.2
shows that sampling arbitrarily fast at a fixed
\varepsilon
(the natural setting where one ignores the multiscale structure altogether) leads the Hurst-parameter estimator to
1
, regardless of the true asymptotic Hurst exponent
H^{*}(m)
. The common thread is that on scales smaller than
\varepsilon
the slow process is effectively smooth and carries no information about its limiting self-similarity exponent: any reasonable estimator must therefore probe it on scales larger than
\varepsilon
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
X^{\varepsilon}
be the solution of (
1.2
) with
0<H<1
,
G\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\gamma)
with Hermite rank
m
. Let
T=N\delta
for any time horizon
T
. If
\delta(\varepsilon)>0
is such that
\delta(\varepsilon)\xrightarrow{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}0
and
\delta(\varepsilon)/\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
then

\mathbb{E}{\left[C_{\delta,\varepsilon}\right]}\xrightarrow{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}0

Proof.

We can again assume
T=1
for simplicity. We first derive the crude estimate

\mathbb{E}[(X^{\varepsilon}_{\delta}-X^{\varepsilon}_{0})^{2}]=\alpha(H^{*}(m),\varepsilon)^{2}\int_{0}^{\delta}\int_{0}^{\delta}\mathbb{E}[G(Y^{\varepsilon}_{s})G(Y^{\varepsilon}_{u})]dsdu\leq\alpha(H^{*}(m),\varepsilon)^{2}\delta^{2}\mathbb{E}[G(Y_{0})^{2}]

from which, if
H^{*}(m)\neq 1/2

0\leq\mathbb{E}[C_{\delta,\varepsilon}]\leq\alpha(H^{*}(m),\varepsilon)^{2}\delta^{(2-2H^{*}(m))\wedge 1}\mathbb{E}[G(Y_{0})^{2}]={\left(\frac{\delta}{\varepsilon}\right)}^{(2-2H^{*}(m))\wedge 1}\mathbb{E}[G(Y_{0})^{2}]

and if
H^{*}(m)=1/2
then

\mathbb{E}[C_{\delta,\varepsilon}]=\frac{\delta}{\varepsilon|\log(\varepsilon)|}\mathbb{E}[G(Y_{0})^{2}]

and the result follows.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
X^{\varepsilon}
be the solution of (
1.2
) and fix
T=N\delta
for any time horizon
T
,
0<H<1
and
G\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R},\gamma)
for some
p>2

such that
\{x\in\mathbb{R}:G(x)=0\}
has measure zero under
\gamma
. Then for any
\varepsilon>0
fixed

\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0}H^{*}_{\varepsilon,\delta}=1

in probability.

Proof.

We assume without loss of generality that
T=1
. The idea here is to re-scale the quantity of interest and define

A(\varepsilon,\delta)=\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}(X^{\varepsilon}_{(i+1)\delta}-X^{\varepsilon}_{i\delta})^{2}}{\alpha(H^{*}(m),\varepsilon)^{2}\delta}

to then show that it converges to the constant
\mathbb{E}(G(Y_{0})^{2})
in two steps. In step 1 we show that

{\left|A(\varepsilon,\delta)-\delta\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}G(Y_{i\delta/\varepsilon})^{2}\right|}\xrightarrow{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}0\quad\text{ in }L^{1}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})

and then in step 2 we prove that

{\left|\delta\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}G(Y_{i\delta/\varepsilon})^{2}-\int_{0}^{1}G(Y_{s/\varepsilon})^{2}ds\right|}\xrightarrow{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}0\quad\text{ in }L^{1}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})

and in a step 3 we conclude the result using these.

Step 1
. Note that

A(\varepsilon,\delta)=\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}(X^{\varepsilon}_{(i+1)\delta}-X^{\varepsilon}_{i\delta})^{2}}{\alpha(H^{*}(m),\varepsilon)^{2}\delta}=\delta^{-1}\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\int_{i\delta}^{(i+1)\delta}\int_{i\delta}^{(i+1)\delta}G(Y^{\varepsilon}_{t})G(Y^{\varepsilon}_{s})dsdt

therefore,

\displaystyle A(\varepsilon,\delta)-\delta\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}G(Y_{i\delta}^{\varepsilon})^{2}=

\displaystyle\delta^{-1}\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\int_{i\delta}^{(i+1)\delta}\int_{i\delta}^{(i+1)\delta}(G(Y^{\varepsilon}_{u})G(Y^{\varepsilon}_{v})-G(Y^{\varepsilon}_{i\delta})^{2})dudv

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\frac{\int_{i\delta}^{(i+1)\delta}\int_{i\delta}^{(i+1)\delta}(G(Y^{\varepsilon}_{u})G(Y^{\varepsilon}_{v})-G(Y^{\varepsilon}_{i\delta})^{2})dudv}{(\delta)^{2}}.

Taking modulus and expectation on both sides we deduce that, by stationarity

\mathbb{E}{\left[{\left|A(\varepsilon,\delta)-\delta^{-1}\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}G(Y^{\varepsilon}_{i\delta})^{2}\right|}\right]}\leq\frac{\int_{0}^{\delta/\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{\delta/\varepsilon}\mathbb{E}[|G(Y_{u})G(Y_{v})-G(Y_{0})^{2}|]dudv}{(\delta/\varepsilon)^{2}}

after applying Fubini on the right-hand side and using the fact that
Y_{t}
is stationary and so is
G(Y_{t})
when
G
is measurable.

If
F(u,v)=\mathbb{E}[|G(Y_{u})G(Y_{v})-G(Y_{0})^{2}|]
is continuous at
(0,0)
then we can conclude the result taking limits on both sides and using the fact that

\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{t^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{t}F(u,v)dudv=F(0,0)

We thus only need to show that

\lim_{(u,v)\rightarrow(0,0)}F(u,v)=0

for which we only need that

G(Y_{t})\xrightarrow{t\rightarrow 0}G(Y_{0})

in
L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})
since

\mathbb{E}[|G(Y_{u})G(Y_{v})-G(Y_{0})^{2}|]\leq\\
||G(Y_{u})||_{L^{2}(\Omega)}||G(Y_{v})-G(Y_{0})||_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+||G(Y_{0})||_{L^{2}(\Omega)}||G(Y_{u})-G(Y_{0})||_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

We first note that, since
G\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\gamma)
then
G(Y_{t})\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})
as

\int_{\Omega}G(Y_{t}(\omega))^{2}d\mathbb{P}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}G(x)^{2}d\gamma<\infty.

We will proceed by a standard approximation argument, showing the result for a continuous function
G
and relying on continuous functions being dense on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\gamma)
. Let
G
be a continuous function, as
Y_{t}
has continuous paths almost surely we have pointwise convergence in
\Omega
in the sense of

\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}G(Y_{t}(\omega))=G(Y_{0}(\omega))

for almost all
\omega\in\Omega
. Using stationarity and the integrability properties mentioned earlier it also holds that

\sup_{t\geq 0}\mathbb{E}[|G(Y_{t})|^{q}]=\mathbb{E}[|G(Y_{0})|^{q}]<\infty

for all
2<q\leq p
. This implies that the family
(G(Y_{t})^{2})_{t\geq 0}
is uniformly integrable in
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
and this immediately ensures convergence in
L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})
using Vitali’s Theorem
[
2
]
. The argument to extend to not necessarily continuous functions
G
is very standard.

Step 2
. We now show that

\delta\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}G(Y_{i\delta}^{\varepsilon})^{2}\xrightarrow[\delta\rightarrow 0]{L^{1}}\int_{0}^{1}G(Y^{\varepsilon}_{s})^{2}ds

(3.1)

The result is almost straightforward for a continuous function
G
given that

\delta\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}G(Y^{\varepsilon}_{i\delta})^{2}\xrightarrow[\delta\rightarrow 0]{\text{a.s.}}\int_{0}^{1}G(Y^{\varepsilon}_{s})^{2}ds

via a Riemann sum argument. Now simply noting that

\mathbb{E}{\left[{\left(\delta\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}G(Y^{\varepsilon}_{i\delta})^{2}\right)}^{2}\right]}=\delta^{2}\sum_{i,j}\mathbb{E}{\left[G(Y^{\varepsilon}_{i\delta})^{2}G(Y^{\varepsilon}_{j\delta})^{2}\right]}\leq\mathbb{E}[(G(Y_{0})^{4})]<\infty

(3.2)

we get that the sequence is Uniformly Integrable and thus concluding the convergence in
L^{1}
. Now for a general
G\in L^{4}
we simply proceed again by density choosing a sequence of continuous functions
(G^{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

such that for any given
\eta>0
there exists
n_{\eta}
such that for any
n\geq n_{\eta}
it holds that
||G_{n}^{2}-G^{2}||_{L^{1}}\leq||G_{n}^{2}-G^{2}||_{L^{2}}<\eta
. Then

{\left|\delta\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}G(Y^{\varepsilon}_{i\delta})^{2}-\int_{0}^{1}G(Y^{\varepsilon}_{s})^{2}ds\right|}\leq\\
\delta\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}|G(Y_{i\delta}^{\varepsilon})^{2}-G_{n}(Y_{i\delta}^{\varepsilon})^{2}|+|\delta\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}G_{n}(Y^{\varepsilon}_{i\delta})^{2}-\int_{0}^{1}G(Y^{\varepsilon}_{s})^{2}ds|

(3.3)

Taking now expectation on both sides and limits as
\delta\rightarrow 0
we get that

\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0}\mathbb{E}{\left|\delta\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}G(Y^{\varepsilon}_{i\delta})^{2}-\int_{0}^{1}G(Y^{\varepsilon}_{s})^{2}ds\right|}<\eta+\mathbb{E}{\left[{\left|\int_{0}^{1}G_{n}(Y^{\varepsilon}_{s})^{2}-G(Y^{\varepsilon}_{s})^{2}ds\right|}\right]}<2\eta

from which the result follows as
\eta
is arbitrarily small.

Step 3
. The ratio inside the logarithm in (
1.5
) may be written as

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{2N-1}{\left(X^{\varepsilon}_{(i+1)\delta/2}-X^{\varepsilon}_{i\delta/2}\right)}^{2}}{\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}{\left(X^{\varepsilon}_{(i+1)\delta}-X^{\varepsilon}_{i\delta}\right)}^{2}}=\frac{\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{2N-1}{\left(X^{\varepsilon}_{(i+1)\delta/2}-X^{\varepsilon}_{i\delta/2}\right)}^{2}}{\alpha(H^{*}(m),\varepsilon)^{2}\delta/2}\alpha(H^{*}(m),\varepsilon)^{2}\delta/2}{\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}{\left(X^{\varepsilon}_{(i+1)\delta}-X^{\varepsilon}_{i\delta}\right)}^{2}}{\alpha(H^{*}(m),\varepsilon)^{2}\delta}\alpha(H^{*}(m),\varepsilon)^{2}\delta}=\frac{A(\varepsilon,\delta/2)}{A(\varepsilon,\delta)2}

(3.4)

and we know that
A(\varepsilon,\delta)
converges in probability to
\int_{0}^{1}G(Y^{\varepsilon}_{s})^{2}ds
as
\delta\rightarrow 0
under the assumptions in the statement of the proposition. Using the Continuous Mapping Theorem for convergence in
probability
[
3
]
the ratio then converges to
\frac{1}{2}
and the result follows from applying the logarithm (once again under the umbrella of the Continuous Mapping Theorem for convergence in probability). Note that the assumption
\mathbb{P}(G(Y_{t})=0)=0
is here necessary to apply the Continuous Mapping Theorem.

4
Asymptotic unbiasedness under subsampling

The goal of this section is a first step towards the appropriateness of
C_{\varepsilon,\delta}
as an estimator: we prove that
\mathbb{E}[C_{\varepsilon,\delta}]\rightarrow C^{2}
as
\delta/\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
. This is the least we may expect from an estimator and will be a building block for the consistency and distributional results of §§
5
–
6
. The behaviour splits naturally into the two generic regimes
H^{*}(m)<1/2
and
H^{*}(m)>1/2
, which we treat together in Theorem
4.1
, and the borderline regime
H^{*}(m)=1/2
, which requires an additional logarithmic condition on the subsampling rate and is collected at the end of the section in Theorem
4.2
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
X^{\varepsilon}
be the solution of (
1.2
) with
0<H<1
and
G\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\gamma)
with Hermite rank
m
such that
H^{*}(m)\neq 1/2
. Let
T=N\delta
for any time horizon
T
. If
\delta(\varepsilon)>0
is such that
\delta(\varepsilon)\xrightarrow{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}0
and
\varepsilon/\delta(\varepsilon)\rightarrow 0
then

\mathbb{E}[C_{\varepsilon,\delta}]\rightarrow C^{2},

where

C^{2}=\mathbb{E}[X_{1}^{2}]=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sum_{q=m}^{\infty}c_{q}^{2}q!\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varrho(s)^{q}\,ds,&H^{*}(m)<1/2,\\[10.00002pt]
\displaystyle\frac{c_{m}^{2}m!\,(\sigma^{2}H(2H-1))^{m}}{H^{*}(m)(2H^{*}(m)-1)},&H^{*}(m)>1/2.\end{cases}

Quantitatively,

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:quantitativeestimateofexpectation}\mathbb{E}[C_{\varepsilon,\delta}]=C^{2}+\begin{cases}K\,(\varepsilon/\delta)^{1-(2H^{*}(m)\vee 0)},&H^{*}(m)<1/2,\\[6.00006pt]
K_{1}(\varepsilon/\delta)^{2H^{*}(m)-1}+K_{2}(\varepsilon/\delta)^{2H^{*}(m)},&H^{*}(m)>1/2,\end{cases}

(4.1)

for finite constants
K,K_{1},K_{2}
depending only on
H,m,G
.

Proof.

The two cases share the common starting point

\mathbb{E}[(X^{\varepsilon}_{\delta})^{2}]=\alpha(H^{*}(m),\varepsilon)^{2}\int_{-\delta/\varepsilon}^{\delta/\varepsilon}{\left(\delta/\varepsilon-|s|\right)}R(|s|)\,ds,

where
R(s)=\mathbb{E}[G(Y_{0})G(Y_{s})]=\sum_{q\geq m}c_{q}^{2}q!\varrho(s)^{q}
; the regimes diverge in how the resulting tail integrals behave.

Case
H^{*}(m)<1/2
.

We first prove the following estimate on the second moment of
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\int_{0}^{\delta}G(Y^{\varepsilon}_{t})dt
.
Note that the estimate is very similar to (4.12) in
[
8
]
as it is essentially finding the gap in the inequality for the case
p=2
.

Let

R(s)=\mathbb{E}[G(Y_{0})G(Y_{s})]=\sum_{q=m}^{\infty}c_{q}^{2}q!\varrho(s)^{q}

and note that on the one hand

|R(s)|\leq R(0)=K\sum_{q=m}^{\infty}c_{q}^{2}q!=K||G||_{L^{2}(\gamma)}^{2}<\infty.

On the other hand, since
\varrho(s)^{q}\sim\kappa_{H}^{q}|s|^{(2H-2)q}
when
|s|
is large, we also have that
R(s)\sim\kappa_{H}^{m}|s|^{m(2H-2)}
for large
|s|
. Loosely speaking,
R(s)
behaves like a constant close to zero and decays at algebraic rate
m(2H-2)
away from zero. We now claim that

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:orderofsecondmoment}\mathbb{E}[(X^{\varepsilon}_{\delta}-X^{\varepsilon}_{0})^{2}]=C^{2}\delta+R(\varepsilon,\delta)

(4.2)

where the remainder is of order
|R(\varepsilon,\delta)|\sim\varepsilon(\delta/\varepsilon)^{2H^{*}(m)\vee 0}
.
We note that the remainder is in fact negative (as it may seem as if (
4.2
) contradicts (4.12) in
[
8
]
otherwise)
but we are just interested in the order. To prove that this is the case we first write it as follows

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(X^{\varepsilon}_{\delta}-X^{\varepsilon}_{0})^{2}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{\delta}\int_{0}^{\delta}\mathbb{E}[G(Y^{\varepsilon}_{t})G(Y^{\varepsilon}_{s})]dsdt

(4.3)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\int_{0}^{\delta/\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{\delta/\varepsilon}\mathbb{E}[G(Y_{t})G(Y_{s})]dsdt

(4.4)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\int_{0}^{\delta/\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{\delta/\varepsilon}R(|s-t|)dsdt

(4.5)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\int_{-\delta/\varepsilon}^{\delta/\varepsilon}(\delta/\varepsilon-|s|)R(|s|)ds

(4.6)

\displaystyle=\delta\int_{-\delta/\varepsilon}^{\delta/\varepsilon}R(|s|)ds-\varepsilon\int_{-\delta/\varepsilon}^{\delta/\varepsilon}|s|R(|s|)ds

(4.7)

where the fourth equality follows from a change of variables taking advantage of the symmetry of the covariance function. This allows to split the second moment in three terms as follows

\mathbb{E}[(X^{\varepsilon}_{\delta}-X^{\varepsilon}_{0})^{2}]=\delta\int_{\mathbb{R}}R(s)ds-\varepsilon\int_{-\delta/\varepsilon}^{\delta/\varepsilon}|s|R(|s|)ds-\delta\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus(-\frac{\delta}{\varepsilon},\frac{\delta}{\varepsilon})}R(s)

(4.8)

We now prove that the second and third term vanish under appropriate subsampling and determine its order. Using the symmetry of the integrand the second term may be written as

\varepsilon\int_{|y|<\delta/\varepsilon}|s|\sum_{q=m}^{\infty}c_{q}^{2}q!\varrho(|s|)^{q}ds=2\varepsilon\int_{0}^{\delta/\varepsilon}s\sum_{q=m}^{\infty}c_{q}^{2}q!\varrho(s)^{q}ds

(4.9)

recall that
\varrho(s)\leq 1\wedge|s|^{2H-2}
hence, assuming
\delta/\varepsilon>1
we can split the integral as

2\varepsilon+2\varepsilon\int_{1}^{\delta/\varepsilon}s^{(2H-2)m+1}ds=2\varepsilon+2\varepsilon\int_{1}^{\delta/\varepsilon}s^{2H^{*}(m)-1}ds

now note that when
H^{*}(m)<0
,
s^{2H^{*}(m)-1}
is integrable on the half axis hence the integral in the second term becomes constant and the order is just
\varepsilon
. When this is not the case we get

\int_{1}^{\delta/\varepsilon}s^{2H^{*}(m)-1}ds=\frac{1}{2H^{*}(m)}{\left((\delta/\varepsilon)^{2H^{*}(m)}-1\right)}

and therefore the order of the second term is
\varepsilon(\delta/\varepsilon)^{2H^{*}(m)\vee 0}

For the third term we simply use that the first term in the sum dominates and hence determines the order and

2\delta\int_{\delta/\varepsilon}^{\infty}s^{(2H-2)m}=2\delta\int_{\delta/\varepsilon}^{\infty}s^{2H^{*}(m)-2}=\frac{2\delta}{2H^{*}(m)-1}(\delta/\varepsilon)^{2H^{*}(m)-1}\propto\varepsilon(\delta/\varepsilon)^{2H^{*}(m)}

Finally, taking expectation and accounting for the extra term
\delta^{-1}
after taking sums we get

\mathbb{E}[C_{\varepsilon,\delta}]=C^{2}+(\varepsilon/\delta)^{1-2H^{*}(m)\vee 0}

and hence

\mathbb{E}[C_{\varepsilon,\delta}]\rightarrow C^{2}

whenever
\delta/\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
.

Case
H^{*}(m)>1/2
.

In this case
X^{\varepsilon}_{t}=\varepsilon^{H^{*}(m)-1}\int_{0}^{t}G(Y^{\varepsilon}_{s})ds=\varepsilon^{H^{*}(m)}\int_{0}^{t/\varepsilon}G(Y_{s})ds
.
Let again
R(s)=\mathbb{E}[G(Y_{s})G(Y_{0})]
we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(X^{\varepsilon}_{\delta})^{2}]

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{2H^{*}(m)}\int_{0}^{\delta/\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{\delta/\varepsilon}R(|s-t|)dsdt

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{2H^{*}(m)}\int_{-\delta/\varepsilon}^{\delta/\varepsilon}(\delta/\varepsilon-|s|)R(|s|)ds

\displaystyle=2\varepsilon^{2H^{*}(m)-1}\delta\int_{0}^{\delta/\varepsilon}R(s)ds-2\varepsilon^{2H^{*}(m)}\int_{0}^{\delta/\varepsilon}sR(s)

(4.10)

Recall that
R(s)=\sum_{q=m}^{\infty}(c_{m})^{2}m!\varrho(s)^{q}
where
\varrho(s)\sim\sigma^{2}H(2H-1)|s|^{2H-2}+o(|s|^{2H-4})
for large
|s|
and
\varrho(|s|)\leq 1
. Therefore
R(s)\sim(c_{m})^{2}m!(\sigma^{2}H(2H-1)|s|^{2H-2})^{m}
and

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\delta/\varepsilon}R(s)ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}R(s)ds+\int_{1}^{\delta/\varepsilon}R(s)ds

\displaystyle\sim\frac{c_{m}^{2}m!(\sigma^{2}H(2H-1))^{m}}{2H^{*}(m)-1}(\delta/\varepsilon)^{2H^{*}(m)-1}+K

where
K
is a finite constant. Going back to the first integral in (
4
) we have that

2\varepsilon^{2H^{*}(m)-1}\delta\int_{0}^{\delta/\varepsilon}R(s)ds\sim\delta^{2H^{*}(m)}\frac{2c_{m}^{2}m!(\sigma^{2}H(2H-1))^{m}}{2H^{*}(m)-1}+\varepsilon^{2H^{*}(m)-1}\delta K

Going now to the second term in (
4
) following a similar strategy we get

\displaystyle 2\varepsilon^{2H^{*}(m)}\int_{0}^{\delta/\varepsilon}sR(s)ds

\displaystyle\sim 2\int_{0}^{s_{0}}sds+2c_{m}^{2}m!(\sigma^{2}H(2H-1))^{m}\int_{s_{0}}^{\delta/\varepsilon}s^{(2H-2)m+1}ds

\displaystyle=\frac{2c_{m}^{2}m!(\sigma^{2}H(2H-1))^{m}}{2H^{*}(m)}\delta^{2H^{*}(m)}+\varepsilon^{2H^{*}(m)}K

Finally, we just need to note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[C_{\varepsilon,\delta}]

\displaystyle=\delta^{-2H^{*}(m)}\mathbb{E}[(X^{\varepsilon}_{\delta})^{2}]

\displaystyle=\begin{multlined}\frac{2c_{m}^{2}m!(\sigma^{2}H(2H-1))^{m}}{2H^{*}(m)-1}-\frac{2c_{m}^{2}m!(\sigma^{2}H(2H-1))^{m}}{2H^{*}(m)}+(\varepsilon/\delta)^{2H^{*}(m)-1}K_{1}\\
+(\varepsilon/\delta)^{2H^{*}(m)}K_{2}\end{multlined}

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{m}^{2}m!(\sigma^{2}H(2H-1))^{m}}{H^{*}(m)(2H^{*}(m)-1)}+(\varepsilon/\delta)^{2H^{*}(m)-1}K_{1}+(\varepsilon/\delta)^{2H^{*}(m)}K_{2}

\displaystyle=C+(\varepsilon/\delta)^{2H^{*}(m)-1}K_{1}+(\varepsilon/\delta)^{2H^{*}(m)}K_{2}

which concludes that, when
\varepsilon/\delta\rightarrow 0
,
\mathbb{E}[C_{\varepsilon,\delta}]\rightarrow C
.

The borderline case
H^{*}(m)=1/2
requires a logarithmic restriction on the subsampling rate; we record it here for completeness, although it does not feature in the consistency or distributional analysis that follows.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
X^{\varepsilon}
be the solution of (
1.2
) with
0<H<1
,
G\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\gamma)
with Hermite rank
m
such that
H^{*}(m)=1/2
. Let
T=N\delta
for any time horizon
T
. If
\delta(\varepsilon)>0
is such that
\delta(\varepsilon)\xrightarrow{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}0
,
\varepsilon/\delta(\varepsilon)\rightarrow 0
and

\log(\delta)/\log(\varepsilon)\rightarrow 0
then

\mathbb{E}[C_{\varepsilon,\delta}]\rightarrow C

where

C^{2}=\mathbb{E}[X_{1}^{2}]=\frac{c_{m}^{2}m!(\sigma^{2}H(2H-1))^{m}}{H^{*}(m)(2H^{*}(m)-1)}.

In particular,

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:quantitativeestimateofexpectationH=1/2}\mathbb{E}[C_{\varepsilon,\delta}]=C^{2}{\left|\frac{\log(\delta/\varepsilon)}{\log(\varepsilon)}\right|}+K_{1}\frac{1}{|\log(\varepsilon)|}+K_{2}\frac{\varepsilon/\delta}{|\log(\varepsilon)|}

(4.11)

for some finite constants
K_{1},K_{2}
.

Proof.

In this case

X^{\varepsilon}_{t}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon|\log(\varepsilon)|}}\int_{0}^{t}G(Y^{\varepsilon}_{s})ds=\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon}{|\log(\varepsilon)|}}\int_{0}^{t/\varepsilon}G(Y_{s})ds.

Let again
R(s)=\mathbb{E}[G(Y_{s})G(Y_{0})]
and through the same calculation we get that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(X^{\varepsilon}_{\delta})^{2}]=2\frac{1}{|\log(\varepsilon)|}\delta\int_{0}^{\delta/\varepsilon}R(s)ds-2\frac{\varepsilon}{|\log(\varepsilon)|}\int_{0}^{\delta/\varepsilon}sR(s)

(4.12)

The particularity of this case is that

R(s)\sim(c_{m})^{2}m!(\sigma^{2}H(2H-1)|s|^{2H-2})^{m}=(c_{m})^{2}m!(\sigma^{2}H(2H-1))^{m}|s|^{-1}

hence the order of the integrals changes

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\delta/\varepsilon}R(s)ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}R(s)ds+\int_{1}^{\delta/\varepsilon}R(s)ds

\displaystyle\sim c_{m}^{2}m!(\sigma^{2}H(2H-1))^{m}|\log(\delta/\varepsilon)|+K

where
K
is a finite constant. Going back to the first integral in (
4.12
) we have that

2\frac{1}{|\log(\varepsilon)|}\delta\int_{0}^{\delta/\varepsilon}R(s)ds\sim 2c_{m}^{2}m!(\sigma^{2}H(2H-1))^{m}\frac{|\log(\delta/\varepsilon)|}{|\log(\varepsilon)|}\delta+\frac{1}{|\log(\varepsilon)|}\delta K

Going now to the second term in (
4.12
) following a similar strategy we get

\displaystyle 2\frac{\varepsilon}{|\log(\varepsilon)|}\int_{0}^{\delta/\varepsilon}sR(s)ds

\displaystyle\sim 2\int_{0}^{s_{0}}sds+2c_{m}^{2}m!(\sigma^{2}H(2H-1))^{m}\int_{s_{0}}^{\delta/\varepsilon}1ds

\displaystyle=2c^{2}_{m}m!(\sigma^{2}H(2H-1))^{m}\frac{\delta}{\log(\varepsilon)}+\frac{\varepsilon}{|\log(\varepsilon)|}K

Finally, we just need to note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[C_{\varepsilon,\delta}]

\displaystyle=\delta^{-1}\mathbb{E}[(X^{\varepsilon}_{\delta})^{2}]

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\sim 2c_{m}^{2}m!(\sigma^{2}H(2H-1))^{m}\frac{|\log(\delta/\varepsilon)|}{|\log(\varepsilon)|}+\frac{1}{|\log(\varepsilon)|}K\\
-2c^{2}_{m}m!(\sigma^{2}H(2H-1))^{m}\frac{1}{|\log(\varepsilon)|}-\frac{\varepsilon/\delta}{|\log(\varepsilon)|}K\end{multlined}

\displaystyle=2c_{m}^{2}m!(\sigma^{2}H(2H-1))^{m}\frac{|\log(\delta/\varepsilon)|}{|\log(\varepsilon)|}+K_{1}\frac{1}{|\log(\varepsilon)|}+K_{2}\frac{\varepsilon/\delta}{|\log(\varepsilon)|}

which concludes that, when
\varepsilon/\delta\rightarrow 0
,
\log(\delta)/\log(\varepsilon)\rightarrow 0
, then
\mathbb{E}[C_{\varepsilon,\delta}]\rightarrow C
.

Remark 4.3
.

Note that the standard choice of subsampling rate is
\delta=\varepsilon^{\alpha}
for some
\alpha\in(0,1)
. This choice is not valid for this case and would in fact introduce bias by a factor of
1-\alpha
. The prototypical choice
of a subsampling rate in this case would be
\delta=\exp(-|\log(\varepsilon)|^{\alpha})
for some
\alpha\in(0,1)
.

This case is not particularly interesting from the inferential point of view. On the one hand, it is very hard (if not impossible) to diagnose whether or not we are in the borderline case purely from a discrete set of observations. This is because
the parameter
H^{*}(m)
is only ”observable” when taking values in
(1/2,1)
. For this reason we exclude this case from the rest of the paper.

5
Consistency on a finite Wiener Chaos

Now that we have found an observational regime for which the estimator is asymptotically unbiased we want to strengthen this convergence to some mode of consistency. This is much more desirable for our purposes since the setting we want to work in
is that of only one string of data being observable at a time.

For this, we will take great advantage of the
L^{2}
structure the estimator has. In particular, in the simple case that
G(x)=c_{m}H_{m}(x)
the
estimator
C_{\varepsilon,\delta}
admits an explicit decomposition into orthogonal Wiener chaoses which allows to determine the asymptotic behaviour of its variance and hence to conclude consistency in
L^{2}
.

Chaos decomposition of the estimator

Proposition 5.1
.

The estimator
C_{\varepsilon,\delta}
admits the following decomposition into orthogonal Wiener chaoses

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:chaosdecompositionfinite}C_{\varepsilon,\delta}=c_{m}^{2}T_{0}+\sum_{r=0}^{m-1}{\left(\frac{c_{m}m!}{(m-r)!\sqrt{r!}}\right)}^{2}T_{2m-2r}

(5.1)

where the index
2m-2r
records the order of the Wiener chaos to which each term
T_{2m-2r}
belongs, and

T_{2m-2r}=\\
N^{2H^{\prime}-1}\alpha(\varepsilon,H^{*}(m))^{2}I_{2m-2r}{\left(\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\int_{k\delta}^{(k+1)\delta}(\tau_{s}K^{\varepsilon})^{\otimes m}ds\otimes_{r}\int_{k\delta}^{(k+1)\delta}(\tau_{s}K^{\varepsilon})^{\otimes m}ds\right)},

(5.2)

and

c_{m}^{2}T_{0}=\mathbb{E}[C_{\varepsilon,\delta}].

(5.3)

The combinatorial weights
\bigl(\tfrac{c_{m}m!}{(m-r)!\sqrt{r!}}\bigr)^{2}
are the normalisation constants from the product formula for multiple Wiener–Itô integrals.

Proof.

The decomposition relies on the product formula for multiple Wiener–Itô integrals (
2.1
) together with a stochastic Fubini theorem (see
[
13
, Theorem 2.1]
) which allows us to interchange the deterministic time integral with the multiple Wiener–Itô integral. We start by rewriting
C_{\varepsilon,\delta}
as

\displaystyle C_{\varepsilon,\delta}

\displaystyle=N^{2H^{\prime}-1}\alpha(\varepsilon,H^{*}(m))^{2}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}{\left(\int_{k\delta}^{(k+1)\delta}c_{m}H_{m}(Y^{\varepsilon}_{s})ds\right)}^{2}

(5.4)

\displaystyle=N^{2H^{\prime}-1}\alpha(\varepsilon,H^{*}(m))^{2}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}{\left(\int_{k\delta}^{(k+1)\delta}c_{m}I_{m}((\tau_{s}K^{\varepsilon})^{\otimes m})ds\right)}^{2}

(5.5)

\displaystyle=N^{2H^{\prime}-1}\alpha(\varepsilon,H^{*}(m))^{2}c_{m}^{2}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}I_{m}{\left(\int_{k\delta}^{(k+1)\delta}(\tau_{s}K^{\varepsilon})^{\otimes m}ds\right)}^{2}

(5.6)

\displaystyle=\begin{multlined}N^{2H^{\prime}-1}\alpha(\varepsilon,H^{*}(m))^{2}c_{m}^{2}\\
\sum_{r=0}^{m}r!\binom{m}{r}^{2}I_{2m-2r}{\left(\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\int_{k\delta}^{(k+1)\delta}(\tau_{s}K^{\varepsilon})^{\otimes m}ds\otimes_{r}\int_{k\delta}^{(k+1)\delta}(\tau_{s}K^{\varepsilon})^{\otimes m}ds\right)}\end{multlined}

We will denote the kernel of the multiple Wiener-Itô integral above as

g_{k}^{m,r,\varepsilon}=\int_{k\delta}^{(k+1)\delta}(\tau_{s}K^{\varepsilon})^{\otimes m}ds\otimes_{r}\int_{k\delta}^{(k+1)\delta}(\tau_{s}K^{\varepsilon})^{\otimes m}ds

(5.9)

Corollary 5.2
(Variance decomposition).

The variance of the estimator
C_{\varepsilon,\delta}
may be expressed as

\mathbb{E}[(C_{\varepsilon,\delta}-\mathbb{E}[C_{\varepsilon,\delta}])^{2}]=\sum_{r=0}^{m-1}{\left(\frac{c_{m}m!}{(m-r)!\sqrt{r!}}\right)}^{4}\mathbb{E}[T_{2m-2r}^{2}]

(5.10)

and the second moment of the chaos terms may be bounded as

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:secondmomentofchaosterm}\mathbb{E}[T_{2m-2r}^{2}]\leq(2m-2r)!N^{2(2H^{\prime}-1)}\alpha(\varepsilon,H^{*}(m))^{4}(E_{2m-2r}^{\text{Diag}}+E_{2m-2r}^{\text{Off}})

(5.11)

with the inequality being an equality if
r=m-1
due to the kernels being symmetric. The terms
E_{2m-2r}^{\text{Diag}}
and
E_{2m-2r}^{\text{Off}}

may be expressed explicitly as

Variance asymptotics

Theorem 5.3
(Variance bound on a finite chaos).

Let
\delta(\varepsilon)>0
be such that
\delta(\varepsilon)\xrightarrow{\varepsilon\to 0}0
and
\varepsilon/\delta\to 0

Proposition 5.4

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[T_{2m-2r}^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq\bigl(D^{\mathrm{Diag}}_{H,m,r}+D^{\mathrm{Off}}_{H,m,r}\bigr)\delta^{2(m-r)(2-2H)\wedge 1}\quad 1\leq r<m-1,

When

\displaystyle=\min\{0\leq r\leq m:r(2H-2)<-1\}

\displaystyle=\min\{\lfloor m/2\rfloor\leq r\leq m:2(m-r)(2H-2)>-1\}

These two values do not always appear:

6
Asymptotic Distribution

The fluctuating behaviour of the estimator
C_{\varepsilon,\delta}
around the true value has two distinct regimes depending on the amount of correlation present in the underlying model. If it decays fast enough, then we see
a Central Limit Theorem behaviour and the fluctuations, appropriately renormalized, are Gaussian. If the correlation decays too slowly, then these are not any longer Gaussian but, as in the limiting setting
[
6
]
,
we observe a Rosenblatt distribution. This resembles very well the classical dichotomy in the Breuer-Major Theorem. Note that these two different regimes can already be deduced from the asymptotic behaviour of the second chaos in the expansion of the estimator. In
the case where the order is
\delta
that means we need to rescale the difference by
\delta^{-1/2}=\sqrt{N}
to see non-trivial fluctuations which is a good indicator that we should expect a Central Limit Theorem. The proof of this Central Limit Theorem is current work in progress.

6.1
Non-Central Limit Case

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
H>3/4
and
H^{*}(m)>1/2

is the estimator defined in (

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:constantnoncentrallimit}c_{H}={\left(\frac{c_{m}m!}{\sqrt{(m-1)!}}\right)}^{2}\frac{(1/\Gamma(2H-1))^{m}}{((m-1)(2H-2)+2)((m-1)(2H-2)+1)}\\

\displaystyle\delta^{2H-2}|\mathbb{E}[C_{\varepsilon,\delta}]-C|

\displaystyle\sim K\delta^{2H-2}(\varepsilon/\delta)^{2H^{*}(m)-1}

and the right-hand side vanishes by the assumption on

7
Application to 1d fluctuation problems

An application to have in mind of the previous results (
1.2
) is to perform statistical inference on the effective diffusivity of random ODEs of the form

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:randomodes}\dot{x}_{t}^{\varepsilon}=h(x_{t}^{\varepsilon})g(y_{t}^{\varepsilon})+\alpha(\varepsilon)G(y_{t}^{\varepsilon})

(7.1)

where
y_{t}^{\varepsilon}
is the fractional Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process defined in (
2.3
),
G
is a measurable function that is square-integrable
with respect to the standard Gaussian measure just as in previous sections and
h\in\mathcal{C}_{b}^{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R})
,
g\in\mathcal{C}_{b}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R})
,
H\in(1/3,1)
.
As shown in
[
8
]
these equations have effective dynamics as
\varepsilon\to 0
described as solutions to stochastic differential equations
driven by either Brownian motion or Hermite processes of the form

d\bar{x}_{t}=\bar{g}h(\bar{x}_{t})dt+C\circ\ dW_{t}

(7.2)

or

d\bar{x}_{t}=\bar{g}h(\bar{x}_{t})dt+CdZ_{t}^{H,m}

(7.3)

where
\bar{g}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}g(y)d\mu(y)
and the stochastic integrals are to be understood in the Stratonovich and Young sense respectively (clearly it does not matter in this case of additive noise but the noise could be
multiplicative in which case the interpretation matters, see
[
8
]
).

We show that the estimator
C_{\varepsilon,\delta}
defined in (
1.1
) may be used to consistently estimate the constant
C
appearing in the limiting equations above. In other words, that bounded drifts do not alter the
L^{2}
-consistency
of the estimator.

We first derive the estimate

Proposition 7.1
.

Let
x^{\varepsilon}_{t}
be the solution to the random ODE (
7.1
) and
y_{t}^{\varepsilon}
be the fractional Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process defined in (
2.3
). Then for any
G\in L^{2}(\mu)
we have that

{\left\|\alpha(\varepsilon,H^{*}(m))\int_{0}^{\delta}G(Y^{\varepsilon}_{s})ds\right\|}_{L^{2}}\sim K\delta^{1/2\vee H^{*}(m)}

(7.4)

Proof.

Recall that
\alpha(\varepsilon,H^{*}(m))=\varepsilon^{(H^{*}(m)-1)\vee-1/2}
and therefore

\alpha(\varepsilon,H^{*}(m))\int_{0}^{\delta}G(Y^{\varepsilon}_{s})ds=\varepsilon^{(H^{*}(m))\vee 1/2}\int_{0}^{\delta/\varepsilon}G(Y_{s})ds

where the equality is in law. Using now the same procedure as in the proofs of Section 4 and the estimates from Lemma
C.1

we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}{\left[{\left(\int_{0}^{\delta/\varepsilon}G(Y_{s})ds\right)}^{2}\right]}

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,\delta/\varepsilon]^{2}}\mathbb{E}[G(Y_{s})G(Y_{t})]dsdt

(7.5)

\displaystyle\sim K(\delta/\varepsilon)^{(m(2H-2)+2)\vee 1}

(7.6)

\displaystyle=K(\delta/\varepsilon)^{2H^{*}(m)\vee 1}

(7.7)

concluding that

{\left|{\left|\alpha(\varepsilon)\int_{0}^{\delta}G(Y^{\varepsilon}_{s})ds\right|}\right|}_{L^{2}}\sim K\varepsilon^{H^{*}(m)\vee 1/2}(\delta/\varepsilon)^{H^{*}(m)\wedge 1}=K\delta^{H^{*}(m)\vee 1/2}

(7.8)

Theorem 7.2
.

Let
x^{\varepsilon}_{t}
be the solution to the random ODE (
7.1
) and

be the fractional Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process defined in (

). Then the estimator

\displaystyle f(N)\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}(x^{\varepsilon}_{k\delta,(k+1)\delta})^{2}

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:changeofvariables}\int_{[a,a+h]^{2}}\beta(s-t)^{k}dsdt=2\int_{0}^{h}(h-s)\beta(s)^{k}ds

(C.4)

Case 1
:
We treat each of the terms arising in (
C.4
) separately
for the first term we have that, for
h>1
:

\displaystyle 2h\int_{0}^{h}\beta(s)^{k}ds

\displaystyle=2h\int_{0}^{1}\beta(s)^{k}ds+2h\int_{0}^{h}\beta(s)^{k}ds

(C.5)

\displaystyle\sim\frac{2K^{k}h^{\alpha k+1}}{\alpha k+1}+2Ch

(C.6)

\displaystyle\sim\frac{2K^{k}h^{\alpha k+1}}{\alpha k+1}

(C.7)

where
C
is some constant we are not specific about since the first term dominates as
h\to\infty
whenever
\alpha k>-1
. A similar technique on the second term leads to

\displaystyle 2\int_{0}^{h}s\beta(s)^{k}ds

\displaystyle\sim\frac{2K^{k}h^{\alpha k+2}}{\alpha k+2}+2C

(C.8)

\displaystyle\sim\frac{2K^{k}h^{\alpha k+2}}{\alpha k+2}

(C.9)

Now putting the two together through (
C.4
) we get

\displaystyle\int_{[a,a+h]^{2}}\beta(s-t)^{k}dsdt

\displaystyle\sim\frac{2K^{k}h^{\alpha k+1}}{\alpha k+1}-\frac{2K^{k}h^{\alpha k+2}}{\alpha k+2}

(C.10)

\displaystyle=\frac{2K^{k}h^{\alpha k+2}}{(\alpha k+2)(\alpha k+1)}

(C.11)

Case 2:
in this case we can write (
C.4
) as

2h\int_{0}^{\infty}\beta(s)^{k}ds-2h\int_{h}^{\infty}\beta(s)ds-2\int_{0}^{h}s\beta(s)^{k}ds

(C.12)

and we only need to show that the first term is the dominant as
h
grows. For the second term we have the asymptotics

\displaystyle 2h\int_{h}^{\infty}\beta(s)^{k}ds\sim-\frac{2h^{\alpha k+2}}{\alpha k+1}

(C.13)

and due to the integrability condition here
\alpha k+2<1
. Similarly for the third term

\displaystyle 2\int_{0}^{h}s\beta(s)^{k}\sim\frac{2h^{\alpha k+2}}{\alpha k+2}

(C.14)

which concludes this case.

Case 3:
we use again (
C.4
) and now the first term behaves as

\int_{0}^{h}\beta(s)^{k}ds\sim\int_{0}^{1}cds+\int_{1}^{h}Ks^{-1}ds\sim K\log(h)+c

(C.15)

whereas the second one

\int_{0}^{h}s\beta(s)^{k}ds\sim\int_{0}^{1}csds+\int_{1}^{h}Ks^{0}ds\sim Kh+c

(C.16)

putting it all together in (
C.4
) we get

\int_{[a,a+h]^{2}}\beta(s-t)^{k}dsdt\sim 2Kh\log(h)

(C.17)

Lemma C.2
.

The following asymptotics hold as
N
grows

1.

If
\alpha\in(-1,0)
:

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}(1-k/N)(k/N)^{\alpha}\sim\frac{1}{\alpha+1}-\frac{1}{\alpha+2}

(C.18)

2.

If
\alpha=-1
:

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}(1-k/N)(k/N)^{-1}=\log(N)+\gamma-1+\frac{1}{2N}+o(N^{-2})

(C.19)

3.

If
\alpha<-1
:

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}(1-k/N)(k/N)^{\alpha}\sim\zeta(-\alpha)(1/N)^{\alpha+1}

(C.20)

where
\zeta(\cdot)
is the Riemann zeta function.

Proof.

Case 1:
in this case the summability of
f(x)=(1-x)x^{\alpha}
on
(0,1)
leads to a Riemann sum type of approximation argument

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}(1-k/N)(k/N)^{\alpha}=\int_{0}^{1}f(x)dx=\frac{1}{\alpha+1}-\frac{1}{\alpha+2}

(C.21)

Case 2:
in this case we use the well-known asymptotics of the harmonic numbers
H_{N}
which are defined as
H_{N}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}1/k
and satisfy the following asymptotics as
N\to\infty
:

H_{N}=\log(N)+\gamma+\frac{1}{2N}+o(N^{-2})

(C.22)

where
\gamma
is the Euler-Mascheroni constant. Using this we can write

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}(1-k/N)(k/N)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\frac{1}{k}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}1

(C.23)

\displaystyle=H_{N-1}-1=\log(N)+\gamma-1+\frac{1}{2N}+o(N^{-2})

(C.24)

Case 3:
Let
\alpha\neq-1
. To proceed with this case we need to introduce the Hurwitz zeta function

\zeta(-\alpha,a)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(n+a)^{\alpha}

where
\zeta(-\alpha,1)=\zeta(-\alpha)
is just the Riemann zeta function. This function is useful as it allows to write

\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}k^{\alpha}=\zeta(-\alpha)-\zeta(-\alpha,N)

and it satisfies the following asymptotics for large
s
, and
|\arg(s)|<\pi
(see
[
9
]
p.25 where a first order approximation is
enough for our purposes, we also disregard the constants as they will not appear in the leading term of our quantity of interest).

\zeta(-z,s)\sim\frac{1}{2}s^{z}+\frac{1}{-(z+1)}s^{1+z}+o(s^{z-1}).

Let
S_{N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}(1-k/N)(k/N)^{\alpha}
then we can write

S_{N}=(1/N)^{\alpha+1}(\zeta(-\alpha)-\zeta(-\alpha,N))-(1/N)^{\alpha+2}(\zeta(-(\alpha+1))-\zeta(-(\alpha+1),N))

The goal now is to use the asymptotics above to show that
\zeta(-\alpha,N)\xrightarrow{N\rightarrow\infty}0
and the leading term is
\zeta(-\alpha)(1/N)^{\alpha+1}
.
Note that

\displaystyle N^{-(\alpha+1)}\zeta(-\alpha,N)

\displaystyle\sim\frac{1}{2}N^{-1}+\frac{1}{-(\alpha+1)}+o(N^{-2})

(C.25)

\displaystyle N^{-(\alpha+2)}\zeta(-(\alpha+1),N)

\displaystyle\sim\frac{1}{2}N^{-1}+\frac{1}{-(\alpha+2)}+o(N^{-2})

(C.26)

Therefore

S_{N}\sim\zeta(-\alpha)(1/N)^{\alpha+1}-\zeta(-(\alpha+1))(1/N)^{\alpha+2}+\frac{1}{(\alpha+1)(\alpha+2)}+o(N^{-2})

which concludes the result.

In the case
\alpha=-2
we need to be slightly more careful as for the second term zeta functions are not defined but the dominant term remains unchanged. Note that

\displaystyle(1/N)\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}(1-k/N)(k/N)^{-2}

\displaystyle=N\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}k^{-2}-\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}k^{-1}

(C.27)

\displaystyle\sim N\zeta(2)-H_{N-1}

(C.28)

\displaystyle\sim N\zeta(2)-\log(N)-\gamma+1+\frac{1}{2N}+o(N^{-2})

(C.29)

and therefore the leading term is still
\zeta(2)N
as claimed.

Lemma C.3
.

Let
\nu(s)
be such that
|\nu(s)|\leq C
for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
and
|\nu(s)|\sim K|s|^{\gamma}
as
s\to\infty
for some
\gamma<0
. Let
\alpha,\beta
and

m
be such that
\alpha+\beta+(m-1)\gamma>-2
. Then as
h\to\infty
we have that

\int_{[0,h]^{3}}(s-u)_{+}^{\alpha}(s-v)_{+}^{\beta}\nu(u-v)^{m-1}dsdudv\asymp h^{\alpha+\beta+(m-1)\gamma+2}

(C.30)

Proof.

\displaystyle\int_{[0,h]^{3}}(s-u)_{+}^{\alpha}(s-v)_{+}^{\beta}\nu(u-v)^{m-1}dsdudv

\displaystyle=\begin{multlined}\int_{0}^{h}\int_{0}^{s}\int_{0}^{s}(s-u)^{\alpha}(s-v)^{\beta}\\
\nu(u-v)^{m-1}dudvds\end{multlined}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{h}\int_{[0,s]^{2}}x^{\alpha}y^{\beta}\nu(|x-y|)^{m-1}dxdyds

(C.33)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{h}F(s)ds

(C.34)

and we study now the behaviour of
F(s)
in the different regimes. Around the diagonal we have that

\displaystyle\int_{\{(x,y)\in[0,s]^{2}:|x-y|\leq 1\}}x^{\alpha}y^{\beta}

\displaystyle\nu(|x-y|)^{m-1}dxdy

(C.35)

\displaystyle\asymp\int_{0}^{s}\int_{(x-1)\vee 0}^{x+1}x^{\alpha}y^{\beta}dxdy

(C.36)

\displaystyle\asymp\int_{0}^{s}x^{\alpha}{\left((x+1)^{\beta+1}-((x-1)_{+})^{\beta+1}\right)}dx

(C.37)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}x^{\alpha}(x+1)^{\beta+1}dx+\int_{1}^{s}x^{\alpha}{\left((x+1)^{\beta+1}-(x-1)^{\beta+1}\right)}dx

(C.38)

\displaystyle\asymp\begin{cases}s^{\alpha+\beta+1}\quad&\alpha+\beta>-1,s>1\\
\log(s)\quad&\alpha+\beta=-1,s>1\\
1\quad&s\leq 1\end{cases}

(C.39)

and away from the diagonal we have that

\displaystyle\int_{\{(x,y)\in[0,s]^{2}:|x-y|>1\}}x^{\alpha}y^{\beta}

\displaystyle\nu(|x-y|)^{m-1}dxdy

(C.40)

\displaystyle=\int_{\{(x,y)\in[0,s]^{2}:|x-y|>1\}}x^{\alpha}y^{\beta}|x-y|^{\gamma}dxdy

(C.41)

\displaystyle=s^{\alpha+\beta+\gamma+2}\int_{\{(x,y)\in[0,1]^{2}:|x-y|>1/s\}}x^{\alpha}y^{\beta}|x-y|^{\gamma}dxdy

(C.42)

\displaystyle\asymp s^{\alpha+\beta+\gamma+2}\int_{[0,1]^{2}}x^{\alpha}y^{\beta}|x-y|^{\gamma}dxdy

(C.43)

provided that the latter integral is finite, which we now show is the case if
\alpha+\beta+\gamma>-2
.

\displaystyle\int_{[0,1]^{2}}x^{\alpha}y^{\beta}|x-y|^{\gamma}dxdy

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{x}x^{\alpha}y^{\beta}(x-y)^{\gamma}dydx+\int_{0}^{1}\int_{x}^{1}x^{\alpha}y^{\beta}(y-x)^{\gamma}dydx

(C.44)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{x}x^{\alpha}y^{\beta}(x-y)^{\gamma}dydx+\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{y}x^{\alpha}y^{\beta}(y-x)^{\gamma}dydx

(C.45)

For the first term we can do

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}x^{\alpha}\int_{0}^{x}y^{\beta}(x-y)^{\gamma}dydx

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}x^{\alpha+1}\int_{0}^{1}(xt)^{\beta}(x-xt)^{\gamma}dtdx

(C.46)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}x^{\alpha+\beta+\gamma+1}\int_{0}^{1}t^{\beta}(1-t)^{\gamma}dtdx

(C.47)

\displaystyle=\beta(\beta+1,\gamma+1)\int_{0}^{1}x^{\alpha+\beta+\gamma+1}dx

(C.48)

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta(\beta+1,\gamma+1)}{\alpha+\beta+\gamma+2}

(C.49)

and for the second one we can get through the exact same argument that it is equal to
\frac{\beta(\alpha+1,\gamma+1)}{\alpha+\beta+\gamma+2}
. We can thus conclude that
the behaviour away from the diagonal dominates since

F(s)\asymp s^{\alpha+\beta+\gamma+2}+G(s)+1

(C.50)

where

G(s)\asymp\begin{cases}s^{\alpha+\beta+1}\quad&\alpha+\beta>-1,s>1\\
\log(s)\quad&\alpha+\beta=-1,s>1\end{cases}

(C.51)

and thus in any case it is dominated by the first term as
s\to\infty
since
\gamma>-1
. We thus conclude the proof by going back to the original integral and writing

\int_{0}^{h}F(s)ds\asymp\int_{0}^{1}1ds+\int_{1}^{h}s^{\alpha+\beta+\gamma+2}ds\asymp h^{\alpha+\beta+\gamma+3}

(C.52)

Auxiliary Integrals

For the sake of completeness we include here some auxiliary integrals that are used in the main proofs. These are all based on the properties of the Beta function and known results.

Lemma D.1
.

For any
1/2<H<1
we have that

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:willsee}\int_{-\infty}^{u\vee v}(u-x)^{H-3/2}(v-x)^{H-3/2}dx=\beta(H-1/2,2-2H)|u-v|^{2H-2}

Proof.

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{u\vee v}(u-x)^{H-3/2}

\displaystyle(v-x)^{H-3/2}dx=\int_{0}^{\infty}(s+|u-v|)^{H-3/2}s^{H-3/2}ds

(D.2)

\displaystyle=|u-v|^{2H-3}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\left(\frac{s}{|u-v|}\right)}^{H-3/2}{\left(\frac{s}{|u-v|}\right)}^{H-3/2}ds

(D.3)

\displaystyle=|u-v|^{2H-2}\int_{0}^{\infty}(1+t)^{H-3/2}t^{H-3/2}dt

(D.4)

\displaystyle=\beta(H-1/2,2-2H)|u-v|^{2H-2}

(D.5)

Lemma D.2
.

For any
1/2<H<1
we have that

\int_{u\vee v}^{1}(s-u)^{H-3/2}(s-v)^{H-3/2}ds<|u-v|^{2H-2}\beta(H-1/2,2-2H).

(D.6)

Proof.

\displaystyle\int_{u\vee v}^{1}(s-u)^{H-3/2}

\displaystyle(s-v)^{H-3/2}ds=\int_{0}^{1-u\vee v}(s+|u-v|)^{H-3/2}s^{H-3/2}ds

(D.7)

\displaystyle=|u-v|^{2H-3}\int_{0}^{1-u\vee v}{\left(\frac{s}{|u-v|}\right)}^{H-3/2}{\left(\frac{s}{|u-v|}+1\right)}^{H-3/2}ds

(D.8)

\displaystyle=|u-v|^{2H-2}\int_{0}^{(1-u\vee v)/|u-v|}t^{H-3/2}(t+1)^{H-3/2}dt

(D.9)

\displaystyle<|u-v|^{2H-2}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{H-3/2}(t+1)^{H-3/2}dt

(D.10)

\displaystyle=|u-v|^{2H-2}\beta(H-1/2,2-2H)

(D.11)

Lemma D.3
.

For any
3/4<H<5/6
we have that, if

\int_{u}^{1}(s-u)^{H-3/2}(s-v)^{2H-2}ds<|u-v|^{3H-5/2}\beta(H-1/2,5/2-3H)

(D.12)

whereas if

\int_{v}^{1}(s-u)^{H-3/2}(s-v)^{2H-2}ds<|u-v|^{3H-5/2}\beta(2H-1,5/2-3H)

Proof.

In the first case we have

\displaystyle\int_{u}^{1}(s-u)^{H-3/2}(s-v)^{2H-2}ds

\displaystyle=(u-v)^{3H-5/2}\int_{0}^{(1-u)/(u-v)}s^{H-3/2}(1+s)^{2H-2}ds

(D.14)

\displaystyle<(u-v)^{3H-5/2}\int_{0}^{\infty}s^{H-3/2}(1+s)^{2H-2}ds

(D.15)

and in the second case we have

\displaystyle\int_{v}^{1}(s-u)^{H-3/2}(s-v)^{2H-2}ds

\displaystyle=(v-u)^{3H-5/2}\int_{0}^{(1-v)/(v-u)}s^{2H-2}(1+s)^{H-3/2}ds

\displaystyle=(u-v)^{3H-5/2}\int_{0}^{1}s^{H-3/2}(s+1)^{2H-2}ds

Appendix E
The fOU kernel

We note that the fastened fractional OU process we are interested in admits the following kernel representation when
H>1/2
:

Y^{\varepsilon}_{t}=p\,\varepsilon^{-1/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-\frac{t-s}{\varepsilon}}\int_{0}^{\frac{t-s}{\varepsilon}}e^{v}(v)_{+}^{H-3/2}dvdW_{s}

(E.1)

let for now
g(s)=e^{-s}\int_{0}^{s}e^{v}v^{H-3/2}dv
so that the kernel representation can be written as

Y^{\varepsilon}_{t}=p\,\varepsilon^{-1/2}\int_{-\infty}^{t}g{\left(\frac{t-s}{\varepsilon}\right)}dW_{s}

(E.2)

we study now the asymptotics of this function
g(s)
. The function
g
is continuous and bounded on
\mathbb{R}
and it satisfies
g(0)=0
and

g(s)\sim s^{H-1/2}
as
s\to 0
and
g(s)\sim s^{H-3/2}
as
s\to\infty
. The asymptotic behaviour near
0
is pretty much irrelevant but the
important bit is that there exists a finite value
s^{*}
such that
g(s^{*})=\sup_{s\in\mathbb{R}}g(s)
and before that value we can bound the kernel by
a constant and after that value we can use its asymptotic behaviour. Note that

\lim_{s\to\infty}g(s)s^{3/2-H}=1

in the sense that there is no constant missing there. Let now
\tau_{t}K^{\varepsilon}(x)=\varepsilon^{-1/2}g{\left(\frac{t-x}{\varepsilon}\right)}
. We are interested
in the quantity:

\int_{-\infty}^{t\wedge s}\tau_{t}K^{\varepsilon}(x)(s-x)^{H-3/2}dx

(E.3)

In the case when
s<t
then
t-x>t-s
and therefore we can use the asymptotic behaviour of the kernel to get that, as
\varepsilon\to 0
:

\int_{-\infty}^{t\wedge s}\tau_{t}K^{\varepsilon}(x)(s-x)^{H-3/2}dx\sim\varepsilon^{1-H}(t-s)^{2H-2}\beta(H-1/2,2-2H)

(E.4)

The case when
s>t
is more delicate as we need to filter out the constant behaviour. We can write

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{t}\tau_{t}K^{\varepsilon}(x)(s-x)^{H-3/2}dx

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{t-s^{*}\varepsilon}\tau_{t}K^{\varepsilon}(x)(s-x)^{H-3/2}dx+

(E.5)

\displaystyle\int_{t-s^{*}\varepsilon}^{t}\tau_{t}K^{\varepsilon}(x)(s-x)^{H-3/2}dx

(E.6)

\displaystyle=A_{t}^{<}+A_{t}^{\varepsilon}

(E.7)

In the first term we can use the asymptotic behaviour of the kernel to get that, as
\varepsilon\to 0
:

\displaystyle A_{t}^{<}

\displaystyle\sim\varepsilon^{1-H}\int_{-\infty}^{t-s^{*}\varepsilon}(t-x)^{H-3/2}(s-x)^{H-3/2}dx

(E.8)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{1-H}\int_{-\infty}^{-s^{*}\varepsilon}(-x)^{H-3/2}(-x+(s-t))^{H-3/2}dx

(E.9)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{1-H}(s-t)^{2H-3}\int_{s^{*}\varepsilon}^{\infty}{\left(\frac{x}{s-t}\right)}^{H-3/2}{\left(\frac{x}{s-t}+1\right)}^{H-3/2}dx

(E.10)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{1-H}(s-t)^{2H-2}\int_{s^{*}\varepsilon/(s-t)}^{\infty}t^{H-3/2}(t+1)^{H-3/2}dt

(E.11)

\displaystyle\sim\varepsilon^{1-H}(s-t)^{2H-2}\beta(H-1/2,2-2H)

(E.12)

Instead the second term is controlled by

\displaystyle A_{t}^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\lesssim\varepsilon^{-1/2}\int_{t-s^{*}\varepsilon}^{t}(s-x)^{H-3/2}dx

(E.13)

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon^{-1/2}\int_{t-s^{*}\varepsilon}^{t}(s-t)^{H-3/2}dx=\varepsilon^{1/2}(s-t)^{H-3/2}

(E.14)

where we simply use the boundedness of the kernel as that dominates the behaviour there. We sum up this behaviour in the following lemma.

Lemma E.1
.

Let
\tau_{t}K^{\varepsilon}(x)=\varepsilon^{-1/2}g{\left(\frac{t-x}{\varepsilon}\right)}
where
g(s)=e^{-s}\int_{0}^{s}e^{v}v^{H-3/2}dv
be the kernel of the fastened fractional OU process for
H>1/2
. Then as
\varepsilon\to 0
we have
that

•

If
s<t
then as
\varepsilon\to 0
:

\int_{-\infty}^{t\wedge s}\tau_{t}K^{\varepsilon}(x)(s-x)^{H-3/2}dx\sim\varepsilon^{1-H}(t-s)^{2H-2}\beta(H-1/2,2-2H)

(E.15)

•

If
s>t
then as
\varepsilon\to 0
we have the following decomposition

\int_{-\infty}^{t\wedge s}\tau_{t}K^{\varepsilon}(x)(s-x)^{H-3/2}dx=A_{t}^{<}+A_{t}^{\varepsilon}

(E.16)

where the first term behaves as

A_{t}^{<}\sim\varepsilon^{1-H}(s-t)^{2H-2}\beta(H-1/2,2-2H)

(E.17)

and the second term is controlled by

A_{t}^{\varepsilon}\lesssim\varepsilon^{1/2}(s-t)^{H-3/2}

(E.18)
