CRISPR Screen Reveals Pathways and Factors Driving Tyrosine Kinase Inhibitor Resistance in Hepatocellular Carcinoma
Authors: Si‐Yu Chen, Li‐Hua Yang, Zi‐Qian Liang, Jian‐Di Li, Shi‐de Li, Yu‐Long Deng, Guo‐Qiang Chen, Jing‐Wen Ling, Sheng‐Sheng Zhou, Gang Chen, Rong‐Quan He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) resistance severely limits clinical outcomes in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), highlighting the urgent need to elucidate its underlying molecular mechanisms. In this study, an unbiased genome‐wide CRISPR/Cas9 screening identified novel key factors related to the therapeutic responsiveness of TKI in HCC. By integrating data from 20 datasets encompassing 322 samples, a comprehensive TKI therapeutic response landscape for HCC was constructed. GO and Reactome enrichment analyses revealed that dysregulated RNA splicing, ubiquitination, endocytosis/exocytosis, and cell cycle pathways modulate TKI sensitivity, with close links to antitumor immunity. This study identified GPATCH4, CCT3, C19orf53, UACA, PPM1M, and LIN37 as key genes mediating TKI resistance in HCC. These six genes were found to be highly expressed in HCC and significantly associated with HCC patient prognosis. Drug sensitivity assays identified a significant association between their expression and responsiveness to TKI agents. In‐house quantitative real‐time PCR validated their differential expression levels in normal hepatocytes, parental HCC cells, and TKI‐resistant HCC sublines. ssGSEA, TIMER2, and ESTIMATE analysis revealed that their expression modulates HCC immune infiltration. Bibliometric analysis revealed a growing focus on immunotherapy‐based combination regimens to overcome TKI resistance. Ferroptosis, epithelial‐mesenchymal transition and hypoxia were new research directions, which were closely related to the pathways investigated in this study. In conclusion, this study identified RNA splicing, ubiquitination, endocytosis/exocytosis, and cell cycle pathways, as well as GPATCH4, CCT3, C19orf53, UACA, PPM1M, and LIN37, as novel directions and targets for TKI–immunotherapy combination strategies, providing new insights for overcoming TKI resistance in HCC.Keywords: CRISPR‐associated protein 9, drug resistance, hepatocellular carcinoma, tumor microenvironment, tyrosine kinase inhibitors

Introduction

Tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) is the cornerstone of the first‐line treatment of advanced hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [1]. However, the inevitable drug resistance of TKI significantly reduces the long‐term effect of clinical treatment [2, 3]. Therefore, a comprehensive investigation of the mechanisms underlying TKI resistance, and identifying reliable predictive biomarkers for TKI treatment response are crucial for addressing TKI resistance in HCC and improving the long‐term survival benefits of HCC patients [4]. At present, it is believed that the mechanism of TKI resistance in HCC involves epigenetic regulation, substance transport between cancer cells, regulatory cell death, tumor environment, and so on [5]. However, these studies are only aimed at specific genes or genomes, including only a limited number of targets, and there is no accurate and high‐dimensional panoramic molecular map to provide guidance for the study of HCC TKI drug resistance [6, 7, 8].

The simplicity and accuracy of the Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats (CRISPR)/CRISPR‐associated protein 9 (CRISPR/Cas9) gene editing system have brought a new era of gene editing. In tumor cells, gene editing based on CRISPR/Cas9 can effectively target the driving genes, including the key genes that affect tumor drug resistance [9, 10]. Compared with RNA interference, which selectively inactivates target gene mRNA, CRISPR/Cas9 library screening has lower noise, minimum off‐target effect, and higher data repeatability [11]. Gene expression omnibus, the cancer genome atlas, and other databases have amassed extensive datasets that require comprehensive analysis and exploration. At present, only a few studies have combined basic transcriptomic data with higher‐dimensional CRISPR screening data to investigate the mechanisms of TKI resistance in HCC [9, 12], and they relied solely on data from a single database. To construct a TKI drug response spectrum, we combined multidimensional database data integration with unbiased whole‐genome CRISPR/Cas9 knockout screening. This approach may facilitate research into TKI resistance mechanisms in HCC and provide a potential theoretical basis for improving clinical outcomes in HCC patients.

In this study, we conducted genome‐wide knockout screening in HCC cells with or without TKI treatment by using a genome‐scale CRISPR knockout (GeCKO v2) library [13] to roundly plumb the potential mechanism of TKI resistance. We integrated multiple databases to probe the mechanism of the occurrence and development of HCC TKI resistance, constructed a brand‐new molecular map of HCC TKI resistance, explored a brand‐new functional pathway, and obtained six new core genes of HCC TKI treatment response. These results may provide new insights into the clinical response and prognosis of HCC related to TKI treatment.

Materials and Methods

Screening Potential TKI Resistance‐Related Genes by CRISPR/Cas9 Knockout System

The GeCKO v2 Library is divided into two sub‐libraries, A and B, containing 65,383 single‐guide RNAs (sgRNAs) in Library A and 58,028 sgRNAs in Library B. Each sub‐library includes three unique sgRNAs targeting every gene, and each is independently capable of genome‐wide coverage. Only Library A contains four sgRNAs designed to target each of the 1864 miRNAs. Human GeCKO v2 Library A was selected to transfect the Huh7 cell line with a virus concentrated solution. After successful transfection and random knockout of the target genes, TKI drug (anlotinib) was used for 21 days, and then the library cell DNA was extracted from the surviving HCC cells. PCR amplification was carried out using a 2 × Rapid Taq Master Mix kit; the amplified fragments were subjected to library construction, and the flow chart of library construction was shown in Figure S1, followed by high‐throughput sequencing. Model‐based analysis of genome‐wide CRISPR/Cas9 knockout (MAGeCK) was used to analyze the sequencing results.

Data Mining of HCC TKI Therapeutic Transcriptome

Based on a comprehensive database search utilizing specific search strategies and strict inclusion/exclusion criteria, the relevant datasets of HCC TKI were screened. Detailed information can be found in Data S2. Finally, the datasets of TKI‐treated HCC cells or tissues from 7 research centers (including 103 TKI‐treated non‑resistant cells or patients' samples and 113 placebo‐treated non‑resistant cells or patients' tissues) were included to construct HCC TKI‐treated maps. Additionally, 13 datasets, including 56 drug‐resistant samples and 50 normal cell or nonresistant tissue samples, were included to establish the HCC TKI resistance‐sensitivity spectrum. In addition, we also found a set of CRISPR screen dataset GSE182185 [14] of HCC cell lines treated by Sorafenib, Lenvatinib, and Regorafenib. After obtaining each independent data‐set, we used the R package “sva” to correct the batch effect and complete the combination of transcriptome expression profiles. The R package “meta” was used to obtain differentially expressed genes (DEGs), and the standard mean differences (SMD) is the mean difference between the two groups of estimated values divided by the mean standard deviation (MD/SD).

Enrichment Analysis

Using R's clusterProfiler and ReactomePA packages, the GO annotation analysis and Reactome analysis of DEGs were carried out, and the false discovery rate was < 0.05, which was statistically significant.

The Core Genes responsive to TKI Treatment Obtained by Multidimensional Data Integration

We collected the multicenter datasets of HCC before October 2024 and calculated all DEGs in HCC [15]. By combining the DEGs in HCC, the transcript spectrum of HCC TKI treatment response and the integrated data of internal and external TKI CRISPR screen, the DEGs of the above groups of data were intersected to obtain the core genes responsive to TKI treatment.

Differential Expression and Prognostic Value Analysis of the Core Genes Responsive to TKI Treatment in HCC

The mRNA expression data of the core genes came from the multicenter datasets of HCC. We used R v4.2 to draw SMD forest plot to analyze the expression of six core genes in the following comparisons: HCC versus normal liver tissue and metastatic samples versus samples in situ. In addition, a summary receiver operating characteristic curve was made to analyze the ability of target genes to distinguish cancerous and noncancerous tissues through Stata software and the R package pROC. The protein expression data came from The Human Protein Atlas. Kaplan–Meier (K–M) survival analysis was used to evaluate the influence of core genes on the overall survival of HCC patients. Based on the stage information of samples in the TCGA dataset, the t‐test was used to calculate whether the expression level of the core genes differed across different stages.

Drug Sensitivity Analysis of the Core Genes Responsive to TKI Treatment and Their Expression in Drug‐Resistant Samples

We downloaded gene‐drug sensitivity data from CellMiner database 2.4.2. R packages encompassing “limma,” “ggplot2,” and “ggpubr” were operated to process data and visualize the results. p value was calculated by the pearson test, and p < 0.05 was statistically significant. R v4.2 was used to draw SMD forest plot to analyze the expression of six core genes in TKI‐resistant tissues versus nonresistant tissues.

Validation of the Expression of Core Genes Responsive to TKI Treatment in HCC Using Quantitative Real‐Time PCR (qRT‐PCR)

THLE‐2 cells derived from primary normal human liver cells were supplied by Shanghai Zhong Qiao Xin Zhou Biotechnology. HCC cell lines LM3 and PLC/PRF/5 were purchased from the National Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures. Sorafenib‐resistant cell lines LM3‐SR and PLC/PRF/5‐SR were successfully established by continuous exposure of the two parental cell lines to gradually increasing low concentrations of sorafenib. The mRNA expression of the core genes in each cell line was detected by qRT‐PCR. The detailed procedures of qRT‐PCR were described in Data S2. The primer sequences of the reference gene GAPDH and the candidate genes were listed in Table S1.

Analysis of Immune Heterogeneity of the Core Genes Responsive to TKI Treatment

The TIMER2 method was used to explore the correlation between the six core genes and immune infiltration. We focused on CD4+ and CD8+ T lymphocytes in the analysis. By using the ssGSEA method and lollipop chart, we statistically analyzed the infiltration levels of 23 immune cells and 13 immune functions between the high and low expression groups of the six core genes. Using the ESTIMATE software package, we calculated the stromal score and immune score of HCC samples, which reflected the relative content of stromal cells and immune cells respectively. The higher the score, the higher the content, and the combination of the two scores could further obtain the Estimate score. We estimated the situation of these three scores in the high and low expression groups of the six core genes [16].

Bibliometric Analysis of TKI Resistance in HCC

We selected the Web of Science Core Collection to search for articles related to HCC TKI resistance. The search period was from January 1976 to October 2024 (Detailed search strategy can be found in Data S2). In total, 2588 articles were included in the study, and Figure S2 provided an overview of the detailed screening process. The FieldByYear function was used to analyze keywords' evolution at key time points. ThematicMap function was used to explore the thematic characteristics of different periods and to investigate the evolution of themes over time.

Results

Whole‐Genome CRISPR/Cas9 Screening Identifies Novel Key Factors Involved in HCC TKI Resistance

In this study, we used the GeCKO v2 library A, which contains 65,383 sgRNAs, targeting 19,050 genes to screen potential drug targets. After targeted knockout, the cell genome DNA was subjected to library construction, high‐throughput sequencing, and the resulting raw reads contained low‐quality reads with connectors. To ensure the quality of the information analysis, raw reads need to be filtered to obtain clean reads, which are used for subsequent analysis. After filtering the raw sequencing reads, the proportion of clean reads in the library construction was all greater than 99.7%, and the proportion of clean reads after the administration of TKI drugs was all > 99.6%. At the same time, according to the sequencing characteristics of Illumina, using paired‐end sequencing data, the proportion of Phred values greater than 30 (Q30) in the total nucleotides was all above 90% on average, and the average error rate of all nucleotides was below 0.1% (Table 1). Therefore, the obtained data met the conditions for subsequent analysis. The qualified expression data of CRISPR screen sequencing were analyzed by the MAGeCK algorithm. Both positive and negative screening approaches were utilized to uncover genes associated with TKI response in HCC. In CRISPR negative screening, each sgRNA was designed to knock out a specific target gene in HCC cells. After transduction with the sgRNA library, cells were exposed to TKI treatment. Genes that intrinsically mediated TKI resistance enabled HCC cells to survive drug stress; accordingly, knockout of such resistance genes via targeting sgRNAs abolished the endogenous drug‐resistant phenotype and rendered cells susceptible to TKI treatment. Consequently, cells carrying these sgRNAs underwent massive cell death, leading to a marked depletion of corresponding sgRNAs in the final surviving cell population. By comparing sgRNA abundance between drug‐treated and control groups through high‐throughput sequencing, genes targeted by significantly depleted sgRNAs were identified as candidate TKI resistance genes. Conversely, an increased sgRNA abundance in the treatment group indicated that the presence of its target gene maintained cellular sensitivity to the drug, identifying it as a candidate TKI sensitivity gene. This corresponded to the principle of positive screening. The entire workflow of CRISPR screening and the identification of TKI resistance/sensitivity genes was illustrated in Figure S3. We obtained differentially expressed sgRNAs by DNA‐seq of the surviving cells after TKI treatment. With fold change (FC) > 1.2 or FC < 0.8 and corrected p value < 0.05 as inclusion conditions, a total of 1962 candidate genes were analyzed. Among these, 959 negative selection genes meeting “p < 0.05 and fold change (FC) < 0.8” criteria were defined as TKI resistance genes. Meanwhile, 1007 positive selection genes meeting “p < 0.05 and FC > 1.2” criteria were identified as TKI‐sensitive genes (Figure 1A). In addition, the sgRNA knockout efficiency of DEGs was visually analyzed by the MAGeCK analysis tool (Figure 1B).

Integrating Genome‐Wide CRISPR‐Cas9 Screening and GSE182185 Dataset to Explore Mechanisms of HCC TKI Resistance

We obtained CRISPR screen data (GSE182185) [14], which performed a drug treatment experiment with three TKI drugs (e.g., sorafenib, regorafenib, lenvatinib) and included baseline and DMSO‐treated controls as negative experiments. We processed it using the Toronto human knockout pooled library (TKOv3) amplification protocol, following statistical analysis, the number of negatively screened DEGs obtained after TKI treatment in Hep3B‐Cas9 and SNU‐398‐Cas9 cells was 920 and 891, while the number of positively screened DEGs was 492 and 646. Based on cell line differences, we integrated our in‐house CRISPR‐Cas9 screening results with GSE182185 data to identify 2560 potential drug‐resistant genes and 2074 drug‐sensitive genes. To systematically investigate molecular mechanisms associated with HCC TKI resistance, we performed GO and Reactome pathway analysis. The results demonstrated that drug‐resistant genes were mainly corresponded to RNA splicing‐related pathways. GO results included: ncRNA processing, rRNA metabolic process, and rRNA processing (Figure 2A,B). In the results of Reactome analysis, the following pathways were significantly enriched: Processing of Capped Intron‐Containing Pre‐mRNA, rRNA processing in the nucleus and cytosol, and rRNA processing. No significant enrichment was observed for sensitive genes.

Construction of a Transcriptome‐Based Response Gene Atlas to TKI Therapy in HCC and Exploration of Underlying Mechanisms

By analyzing the datasets of HCC tissues or cells treated with TKI, we identified 807 DEGs with high expression after TKI treatment and 1133 DEGs with low expression after TKI treatment. Enrichment analysis showed that highly expressed DEGs were enriched in ubiquitination‐related pathways, such as the proteasome‐mediated ubiquitin‐dependent protein catabolic process (biological process, BP), SCF ubiquitin ligase complex, cullin‐RING ubiquitin ligase complex, ubiquitin ligase complex (cellular component, CC), etc. (Figure 2C). The Reactome pathway showed the enrichment in the Response of EIF2AK1 (HRI) to heme deficiency (Figure 2D). Lowly expressed DEGs in HCC tissues treated by TKI were significantly enriched in the related pathways of RNA synthesis and processing during protein synthesis, such as ribonucleoprotein complex biogenesis, ribosome biogenesis, spliceosomal complex, and catalytic activity, acting on RNA (Figure 2E). The Reactome pathway also revealed related pathway enrichment, such as processing of capped intron‐containing pre‐mRNA, mRNA splicing‐major pathway, and mRNA splicing (Figure 2F).

Based on the TKI‐resistant HCC sample datasets, we obtained 800 DEGs with up‐regulated expression and 512 DEGs with down‐regulated expression. The highly expressed DEGs were found to overlap with the pathways related to endocytosis and exocytosis, such as positive regulation of exocytosis, regulation of exocytosis, and early endosome membrane (Figure 3A). The pathways of Reactome enrichment analysis were as follows: caspase activation via death receptors in the presence of ligand, suppression of phage maturation, and insertion of tail‐anchored proteins into the endoplasmic reticulum membrane (Figure 3B). There were 822 low‐expression genes, and the enrichment analysis of GO and Reactome pathway showed that they were significantly enriched in cell cycle‐related pathways. In GO analysis, BP: DNA‐templated DNA replication, DNA replication, DNA replication checkpoint signaling; CC: mitochondrial inner membrane, chromosomal region, condensed chromosome, centromeric region (Figure 3C). Among the top 10 Reactome pathways, cell cycle‐related pathways were significantly enriched (Figure 3D).

The Whole Genome CRISPR/Cas9 Screening and Multiple Databases Analysis Were Integrated to Reveal the Key Genes Related to TKI Resistance

To identify reliable genes associated with HCC TKI treatment, this project integrated multiple transcriptome data of HCC [15], HCC TKI treatment spectrum, resistance/sensitivity spectrum, as well as internal and external TKI CRISPR screening data (Figure 4A,B). All lists of DEGs used in this study were presented in Data S1. As a result, six core genes related to HCC TKI treatment response were identified, including three HCC TKI‐sensitive genes: G‐patch domain containing 4 (GPATCH4), Chaperonin‐containing TCP1 subunit 3 (CCT3). Chromosome 19 Open Reading Frame 53(C19orf53), and three drug‐resistant genes: uveal autoantigen with coiled‐coil domains and ankyrin repeats (UACA), Protein phosphatase, Mg2+/Mn2+ dependent 1M (PPM1M) and Lin‐37 DREAM MuvB core complex component (LIN37).

The Core Genes Responsive to TKI Treatment in HCC Were Highly Expressed and Correlated With Patient Prognosis in HCC

Based on the RNA‐seq and tissue microarray data of multiple datasets, the mRNA of GPATCH4, CCT3, C19orf53, UACA, PPM1M, and LIN37 were all highly expressed in HCC (SMD for GPATCH4, CCT3, C19orf53, UACA, PPM1M, and LIN37 were 0.88, 1.39, 0.55, 0.24, 0.22, 0.67, p < 0.01, Figure S4), and the area under the curve value indicated that all six genes had strong discriminating ability to distinguish HCC from noncancerous tissues (Figure S5). Compared to normal liver tissue specimens, the expression levels of protein for GPATCH4, CCT3, C19orf53, UACA, PPM1M, and LIN37 were all increased in HCC tissue specimens (Figure S6). K‐M curve survival analysis showed that HCC patients with high expression of GPATCH4 and CCT3 had a lower survival probability, while HCC patients with high expression of UACA had a higher survival probability, HCC patients with high expression of C19orf53, PPM1M, and LIN37 had no significant difference in death risk (Figure S7A–F). At the same time, we found that the expression levels of six core genes were all increased in HCC patients with later clinical stages (CCT3, C19orf53 were statistically significant, while other genes were not statistically significant, Figure S7G). By calculating the SMD value, we found that the expression levels of GPATCH4, CCT3, C19orf53, and LIN37 in HCC metastatic foci were all higher than those in situ foci, while there was no statistical significance of UACA and PPM1M (Figure S8).

The Core Genes Responsive to TKI Treatment Were Related to TKI Drug Sensitivity

We obtained the RNA‐seq spectrum of 263 kinds of anti‐tumor drugs and core genes from the CellMiner database, and analyzed the underlying relationship between drug activity and gene expression. The higher the IC50 (half maximum inhibitory concentration) value, the lower the drug sensitivity. GPATCH4, CCT3, C19orf53, UACA, PPM1M, and LIN37 were all significantly related to the sensitivity of TKI‐targeted drugs such as Fostamatinib, Amuvatinib, and Selumetinib (Figure S9). Significant differential expression of these key genes was observed in TKI‐resistant HCC samples relative to their nonresistant counterparts (SMD for GPATCH4, CCT3, C19orf53, UACA, PPM1M, and LIN37 were −0.92, −0.47, −0.66, 0.56, 0.60, 1.09, p < 0.01, Figure S10).

Validation of the Expression of Core Genes in Response to TKI Treatment in HCC Using qRT‐PCR

Compared with THLE‐2 cells, the mRNA levels of GPATCH4, CCT3, C19orf53, UACA, and LIN37 all showed an upregulated trend in both LM3 and PLC/PRF/5 cells (with no statistical significance for C19orf53 in LM3 cells, and for C19orf53, UACA, and LIN37 in PLC/PRF/5 cells), while PPM1M was significantly downregulated in both cell lines (Figure 4C). Compared with the parental cells, the mRNA levels of GPATCH4, CCT3, and C19orf53 were significantly downregulated in the sorafenib‐resistant LM3‐SR subline, while those of UACA and LIN37 were significantly upregulated (PPM1M expression showed no statistical significance). In the sorafenib‐resistant PLC/PRF/5‐SR subline, the mRNA levels of GPATCH4, CCT3, and C19orf53 were significantly downregulated, and LIN37 was significantly upregulated (UACA and PPM1M expression showed no statistical significance, Figure 4D).

The Core Genes Responding to TKI Treatment Were Closely Associated With Tumor Immune Infiltration in HCC

We estimated the link between the six core genes responding to TKI treatment and the abundance of CD4+ T cells and CD8+ T cells in HCC. The overall results noted that the six core genes were positively correlated with the abundance of CD4+ T cells and CD8+ T cells, which are closely related to immunotherapy in the microenvironment (Figure 4E,F). The results of ssGSEA demonstrated that the infiltration levels of various immune cells were different between the high‐expression group and the low‐expression group of the core genes (Figure 5A–F). For example, Activated.CD4.T.cell and Type 2. T. Helper. Cell both showed higher infiltration levels in the high‐expression group of the six core genes, while the infiltration of other types of immune cells varied across different genes. We investigated the connection between the expression of the six core genes and 13 kinds of immune function scores by ssGSEA analysis (Figure S11). The results indicated that, compared to the PPM1M low‐expression group, except for Type II IFN Response, all immune function scores in the PPM1M high‐expression group were higher. There was no significant relevance between the other five genes and immune function. Furthermore, the outcomes of ESTIMATE suggested that compared with the low expression group, the high expression groups of CCT3, C19orf53, and LIN37 had lower stromal cell scores, the high expression group of UACA had higher stromal cell scores, and the high expression group of PPM1M had higher immune scores and ESTIMATE scores (Figure 6).

Bibliometric Analysis Reveals Future Research Directions in TKI Resistance in HCC

We analyzed the bibliometric landscape of all eligible publications regarding HCC TKI resistance retrieved from Web of Science spanning 1976 to 2024. A total of 2588 articles and 73,222 literature references in 635 academic journals were obtained. Keyword analysis revealed the core of TKI resistance research in HCC. We analyzed the evolution of keywords in the field of TKI resistance of early HCC from 2010 to 2024 in order to obtain more microscopic knowledge distribution and structural characteristics (Figure 7A). In the nearly 5 years of research from 2020 to 2024, the keywords focused on such potential new targets for tumor treatment as MTOR, EGFR, and GPX4, and such mechanisms as hypoxia, autophagy, metabolism, and ferroptosis. Furthermore, terms including combination therapy, microenvironment, and immunotherapy suggested that immune‐based combination therapies had demonstrated superior efficacy in overcoming TKI resistance in clinical HCC. Thematic map is a visualization based on keyword clustering that illustrates the relationships among themes formed by different keywords. As shown in Figure 7B, in the upper left corner of niche themes, keywords such as immune, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and combination therapy indicated that the combination treatment of targeted therapy, immunotherapy, and chemotherapy had provided an important clinical approach to overcome the TKI resistance. Emerging or declining themes suggested that such targets as AKT, PI3K, and MTOR were emerging themes that might disappear soon and did not need to be used as the focus of in‐depth studies. The basic themes shown in the lower right corner might be the hot spot trend for future research, focusing on ferroptosis, epithelial‐mesenchymal transition (EMT), and hypoxia.

Discussion

Over the years, systemic therapy has remained the primary treatment approach for advanced HCC. However, the objective response rate of sorafenib was only 9.2%, and even the recently emerging lenvatinib, a TKI drug offering a favorable cost–benefit ratio in the treatment of HCC [17], was only 24.1% [18]. The primary reason for this limitation was the inevitable development of treatment resistance, which hindered effective HCC therapy. Therefore, it is imperative to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying TKI resistance to develop novel therapeutic strategies that maximize their clinical benefits. However, the molecular landscape underlying TKI resistance in HCC remains incompletely characterized, particularly through systematic integration of multiomics data.

To address this challenge, our study employed a CRISPR/Cas9 knockout screen to identify potential genes mediating TKI resistance in HCC. We performed genome‐wide knockout in HCC cells using the GeCKO v2 library, followed by treatment with TKI for 21 days. To enhance the level of evidence and clinical translational value of the present study, anlotinib was selected as the targeted therapeutic agent for CRISPR screening. Recent clinical evidence has demonstrated that the combination of “anlotinib + penpulimab” yields superior progression‐free and overall survival benefits compared with the standard sorafenib regimen in patients with unresectable HCC [19]. In April of this year, the updated Immune Checkpoint Inhibitor Clinical Practice Guidelines by CSCO Guidelines in 2025 included “anlotinib + penpulimab” as a Class I recommendation for first‐line treatment of advanced HCC. Despite expanding clinical usage, studies focusing on anlotinib resistance‐related genes are still limited. Existing studies have abundantly explored resistance genes of sorafenib and lenvatinib. We adopted anlotinib for genome‐wide CRISPR knockout screening, which may have helped address the insufficient knowledge of its resistance mechanisms. Furthermore, we integrated our identified candidate TKI resistance genes with published CRISPR screening datasets of other TKIs from public databases and performed intersection analysis with multidimensional HCC TKI‐related omics data. This multisource data consolidation substantially strengthened the reliability and evidence strength of our screened resistance gene signatures. Overall, anlotinib is among the representative agents for advanced HCC therapy. Finally, through systematic bioinformatic analysis, we identified 959 negatively selected genes, representing potential mediators of TKI resistance in HCC, and 1007 positively selected genes, indicative of potential TKI sensitivity. These findings may provide a potential theoretical basis for understanding the mechanisms underlying TKI resistance in HCC.

In this study, we established a comprehensive, high‐dimensional atlas of TKI treatment response in HCC. Pathway enrichment analysis revealed multiple functionally distinct pathways associated with TKI resistance mechanisms. Candidate TKI resistance‐related genes were enriched in pathways related to RNA splicing, ubiquitination, and endocytosis/exocytosis. These pathways have been widely implicated in modulating tumor drug response [20, 21, 22]. Notably, crosstalk between protein ubiquitination and mRNA alternative splicing has been reported, with both processes regulating cell migration [23]. Ubiquitination also contributes to tumorigenesis and progression via regulating endocytosis, thereby affecting therapeutic drug responsiveness [24]. Similarly, TKI‐sensitive genes were enriched in RNA splicing and cell cycle‐related pathways. Accumulating evidence supports crosstalk between mRNA splicing and cell cycle checkpoint regulation, which jointly shapes tumor plasticity and drug response [25, 26]. Currently, there are no reported studies elucidating how HCC develops resistance to TKI drugs through the crosstalk among RNA splicing, ubiquitination, endocytosis/exocytosis, and cell cycle‐related pathways. These findings may offer novel insights into TKI resistance mechanisms in HCC and expand the current knowledge.

The enriched pathways overlapping with TKI response‐associated DEGs appear to regulate the HCC immune microenvironment. Immunotherapy is increasingly important in the treatment of HCC. The HCC tumor microenvironment is highly heterogeneous, and immune dysfunction is common in patients. Immune checkpoint inhibitors and combination immunotherapies have shown promising efficacy in preclinical and clinical HCC studies [27, 28]. Accumulating evidence has established critical roles for ubiquitination, RNA splicing, endocytosis, and cell cycle regulation in modulating tumor responses to immunotherapy [29, 30, 31, 32]. Investigating the crosstalk among these pathways in HCC and their cotargeting might provide a new idea for the systematic drug combination therapy of HCC.

In addition to identifying the aforementioned potential pathways for addressing HCC TKI resistance, we also identified six core genes associated with HCC TKI response. These six potential HCC TKI‐related genes were identified by integrating unbiased whole‐genome CRISPR/Cas9 screening and global database analysis: GPATCH4, CCT3, C19orf53, UACA, PPM1M, and LIN37. Our results revealed that all six genes were upregulated in HCC. Some of these genes were significantly correlated with patients' overall survival, clinical stage and tumor metastasis, implying that they could serve as promising biomarkers for HCC. Using in‐house qRT‐PCR assays, we validated the upregulated expression of GPATCH4, CCT3, C19orf53, UACA, and LIN37 in HCC cell lines. In contrast, PPM1M was significantly downregulated, showing an expression pattern inconsistent with our bioinformatic predictions. We speculated that this discrepancy arose from two major aspects. First, biological heterogeneity existed between public datasets used for bioinformatics analysis and the HCC cell lines adopted in our in vitro experiments. Public omics data were generally derived from clinical tumor tissues containing mixed cell populations, whereas cell lines represented a relatively simple in vitro cell model, which led to divergent expression profiles of individual genes. Second, the biological function of PPM1M might be mainly regulated at the posttranslational level. It was possible that the transcriptional level of PPM1M did not fully reflect its actual protein activity and biological function in HCC cells. In this case, posttranslational modifications such as phosphorylation and ubiquitination could play a more decisive role than mRNA abundance. Collectively, the discordance between transcriptional results from cell line experiments and bioinformatic analyses was a common phenomenon in cancer research. Subsequent studies would be performed to explore the protein level and posttranslational regulation of PPM1M, so as to clarify its exact role in HCC.

GPATCH4 is a key nucleolar protein; previous studies have shown that GPATCH4 knockdown can inhibit cell proliferation and increase tumor cell susceptibility to apoptosis following treatment with the high‐dose chemotherapeutic agent etoposide [33]. Dysregulation of the CCT3‐LINC00326 regulatory network reduces cellular lipid accumulation, increases lipid degradation, and impairs liver cancer growth [34]. The cotranscription of C19orf53 and HSF1 can be enhanced through the activation of mTORC1, breaking the metabolic balance and leading to cell proliferation and increased amino acid consumption [35]. Knockdown of UACA in HCC cell lines inhibits cell proliferation and invasion while promoting cellular senescence [36]. UACA has also been implicated in chemotherapy resistance in breast cancer [37]. PPM1M belongs to the metal‐dependent protein phosphatase family. Its expression is strongly associated with immune cell infiltration in the tumor microenvironment [38] and is involved in the regulation of multiple immune‐related pathways. LIN37 is a key component of the DREAM complex, which regulates the cell cycle. Loss of LIN37 impairs p53‐dependent G1/S cell cycle arrest in the colorectal cancer cell line HCT116, thereby affecting cell proliferation [39]. Drug sensitivity assays and multicenter database analyses revealed that these genes were associated with TKI treatment response in HCC. In‐house qRT‐PCR validation further confirmed the differential expression of these genes between parental HCC cell lines and their matched TKI‐resistant sublines, collectively corroborating their critical functional roles in mediating HCC TKI resistance. These genes were also associated with several pathways identified in this study—including ubiquitination, RNA splicing, endocytosis/exocytosis, and dysregulated cell cycle control—that might modulate TKI responsiveness in HCC. GPATCH4 binds to small nucleolar RNAs and small Cajal body‐associated RNAs, which mediate modifications of ribosomal RNAs and small nuclear RNAs, thereby influencing ribosome function and pre‐mRNA splicing [40]. CCT3 interacts with ACTN4, inhibiting iron uptake by HCC cells via endocytosis and thereby modulating sorafenib sensitivity in HCC [41]. LIN37 itself is a key gene involved in cell cycle regulation. To our knowledge, only one study has explored the association between these six genes and sorafenib resistance in HCC [41]. No studies have yet explored the mechanisms by which GPATCH4, C19orf53, UACA, PPM1M, or LIN37 regulate HCC TKI response. These core genes may serve as candidate targets for the management of TKI resistance in HCC.

Unexpectedly, our results suggested that GPATCH4, CCT3, C19orf53, UACA, PPM1M, and LIN37 might play a role in predicting immunotherapy response in HCC patients. Current studies have shown that these genes can influence the cellular immune microenvironment through multiple mechanisms. For example, C19orf53 modulates cellular amino acid consumption; alterations in amino acid metabolism are closely associated with tumor growth, metastasis, and drug resistance by regulating the fate of various immune cells [35, 42]. The presence of UACA‐specific autoantibodies is associated with immunotherapy resistance in patients with metastatic urothelial carcinoma [43]. Across multiple cancer types, PPM1M is closely associated with immune cell infiltration into the tumor microenvironment and is involved in regulating multiple immune‐related pathways [38]. However, no studies have explored the relationship between these six genes and immunotherapy in HCC to date. We identified six candidate factors linked to TKI sensitivity. These factors correlated with tumor immune infiltration and may modulate cellular responses to both TKI treatment and immunotherapy in HCC.

To further corroborate the potential guiding significance of the key pathways and core genes identified in this study for addressing TKI resistance in HCC, we performed a systematic bibliometric analysis to delineate the research trends and developmental landscape of this field. At a macro level, bibliometric data revealed that angiogenesis remained the core foundational theme in the field, while combination therapy and immunotherapy have emerged as the dominant areas of intense investigation for overcoming drug resistance over the past 5 years. At the mechanistic level, our analysis identified ferroptosis, EMT, and hypoxia as well‐validated, key biological processes mediating TKI resistance in HCC. Critically, these processes showed strong concordance with the key TKI resistance‐associated pathways identified in our work, including ubiquitination, RNA splicing, endocytosis/exocytosis, and dysregulated cell cycle control. For instance, elevated activity of the E3 ubiquitin ligase MARCHF6 has been shown to suppress cellular ferroptosis [44]. EMT promotes stem‐like properties and attenuates cell proliferation via inducing cell cycle arrest [45]. Dysregulated alternative splicing under hypoxia has been reported to reduce tumor cell drug sensitivity [46]. These published observations cross‐validated our findings, indicating that the pathways uncovered in our study—most notably ubiquitination and RNA splicing—likely represent upstream molecular events that underpin well‐characterized resistance phenotypes including ferroptosis and EMT. Taken together, our bibliometric analysis not only strengthened the clinical and translational potential of our study findings but also suggested that, when addressing the clinical bottleneck of drug resistance in HCC, efforts should not be confined to improving single‐agent regimens. Instead, investigating the shared, convergent pathways and core targets that mediate multidrug resistance may represent a promising theoretical direction.

This research had its limitations. While our analysis has pinpointed potential therapeutic targets and underlying mechanisms related to HCC TKI therapy—providing possible avenues for overcoming resistance to both TKI and immunotherapy—the current study was constrained by the scope of in vitro validation. Additional in vitro and in vivo studies will be essential to more robustly confirm the reliability of these results. In our future research, we will design relevant experimental to strengthen the conclusions drawn from our findings.
