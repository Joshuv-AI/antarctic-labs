Effects of household washing methods and post-ripening on the residue dissipation of the systemic fungicides mandestrobin and metconazole in Persimmons
Authors: Se-Min Kang, Jun-Yeong Hwang, Jang-Hun Kim, Hoon Choi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStudies evaluating the efficiencies of removing mandestrobin (MAND) and metconazole (MEZ) residues from persimmons using household washing methods are lacking. Therefore, in this study, the efficiencies of household washing processes in removing the systemic fungicides MAND and MEZ from persimmons (Diospyros kaki Thunb) and their dissipation characteristics during postharvest ripening were investigated. Following immersion, the initial residues were mainly distributed in the calyx (64.2–79.9%), followed by the peel (17.3–22.7%) and flesh (2.8–13.1%). Among the various washing methods, hand rubbing under running water yielded the highest MAND removal efficiency (78.9%) from the whole fruits, particularly from the peel (74.6%). Whereas MEZ removal was independent of the washing method (except for washing under running tap water; removal rate of 33.7%), with reductions of 51.9–58.8% within the whole fruits, baking soda was the most effective for the peel. Overall, hand rubbing under running tap water was an accessible, green approach. The residue levels exhibited no significant reductions over 21 d of ripening, and the respective biological half-lives of MAND and MEZ based on the amounts of the residues were 132.7 and 27.4 d, indicating extremely slow dissipation. These findings provide scientific guidance for effective washing practices and the improved assessment of pesticide exposure when using persimmons.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-58472-4.Keywords: Mandestrobin, Metconazole, Persimmon, Ripening, Washing treatmentSubject terms: Chemistry, Environmental sciences

Introduction

The persimmon (Diospyros kaki Thunb) is a fleshy, fibrous fruit with a thin peel and soft flesh. It is primarily cultivated in East Asia, and is categorized as a pome fruit under the CODEX food classification system1,2. According to the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO, Rome, Italy), global persimmon production reached 5.1 million metric tons in 2024, highlighting its status as one of the major fruits consumed worldwide. China accounts for approximately 77.6% of global production, while the Republic of Korea is also a significant producer, contributing approximately 4.4% of the total output3. Persimmons contain abundant polyphenols, carotenoids, vitamins, organic acids, and dietary fiber, which are associated with beneficial metabolic and cardiovascular effects, particularly in relation to diabetes, obesity, and hypertension2,4.

Persimmons are cultivated in > 1,000 varieties worldwide, and they are generally classified as astringent or non-astringent, depending on the soluble tannins responsible for the astringency5,6. Non-astringent varieties such as “Fuyu”—known as “Dangam” in the Republic of Korea—are peeled and eaten immediately after harvesting, and they display short shelf lives due to the rapid softening of their flesh6,7. Conversely, astringent varieties, such as “Dungsi” and “Bansi” in the Republic of Korea, are rich in functional compounds, including phenolic compounds and carotenoids2, but their astringent tastes render their consumption immediately after harvest challenging. Consequently, these varieties are consumed only after their astringency is removed via softening treatment (e.g., KOH and ethephon) or natural ripening. They are also processed into dried products denoted “Gotgam” in the Republic of Korea or used as ingredients in various processed foods, such as beverages, syrups, purees, vinegar, and wine4,8. In addition, the calyx of the persimmon is traditionally used as a herbal medicine, and it exhibits bioactive properties, including anti-inflammatory and gastrointestinal regulatory effects, highlighting its potential as a functional food by-product9,10.

Persimmons are susceptible to various pests and diseases during cultivation, including circular leaf spot and anthracnose caused by Plurivorosphaerella nawae and Colletotrichum gloeosporioides, respectively11,12. Effectively controlling these diseases is essential to maintain and enhance the yield and fruit quality, necessitating the use of pesticides. Among the 48 pesticides registered for disease control in persimmons in the Republic of Korea, mandestrobin (MAND) and metconazole (MEZ) are the most commonly used fungicides in persimmon orchards13. MAND and MEZ act by inhibiting mitochondrial electron transport and ergosterol biosynthesis, respectively14. The maximum residue limits (MRLs) for MAND and MEZ in persimmons in the Republic of Korea are 0.7 and 0.5 mg/kg, respectively, whereas that for MAND in Japan is 3 mg/kg15,16.

Although pesticide residues in agricultural products are regulated and managed by national authorities via MRLs and good agricultural practices (GAPs), consumers express significant concern due to the inherent toxicities of pesticides. They often attempt to remove pesticide residues from produce using various household washing practices. Although washing is successful in reducing their levels, the efficiency varies depending on the type of product, physicochemical properties of the pesticides, and washing conditions. NaHCO3, which is known as baking soda, is effective in removing thiabendazole and phosmet from apples17. Furthermore, soaking in a mixture of NaHCO3 and corn starch displays high efficacies in reducing the thiabendazole levels of fruits, such as apples, grapes, lemons, and strawberries18. Meanwhile, 3% vinegar or salt effectively removes chlorfenapyr, indoxacarb, and spirotetramat from strawberries19, whereas distilled H2O is the most effective in removing malathion, boscalid, and pyraclostrobin from sweet cherries20. Combining conventional washing with advanced technologies, such as O3 or ultrasonic treatment, enhances the efficiency of residue removal from food matrices21,22. Research regarding the washing of persimmons is limited, but chlorfenapyr can be successfully removed using 0.1% alcohol or 0.2% surfactant23. Furthermore, pesticide residues within fruits can also degrade during postharvest storage and ripening, the kinetics of which are driven by biological processes and the environmental conditions, such as the temperature and humidity24.

To the best of our knowledge, no studies have evaluated the efficiencies of removing MAND and MEZ residues from persimmons using household washing methods. Therefore, this study quantified the residue distributions in and removal efficiencies from the flesh, peel, and calyx of the persimmon upon using several washing methods, e.g., running tap water (with and without hand rubbing) and chemical treatment with acetic acid, a washing agent, or baking soda, which are readily available at home. In addition, this study assessed the dissipation characteristics of the MAND and MEZ residues during the ripening period, as astringent persimmons are typically consumed after natural ripening. The findings should provide scientific data for consumers to support the management of pesticide exposure and improve the safe consumption of persimmons.

Methods

Reagents and materials

Analytical standards of MAND (purity: 99.38%) and MEZ (purity: 98.85%) were purchased from Dr. Ehrenstorfer (Augsburg, Germany) and Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA), respectively. Commercial pesticide products used for immersion in a spray solution included a 40% suspension concentrate (SC) of MAND (Mandes, FarmHannong, Seoul, Republic of Korea) and 20% SC of MEZ (Sallimkkun, Dongbang Agro, Seoul, Republic of Korea), which were obtained from a local pesticide supplier. Astringent persimmons of the “Dungsi” cultivar, which were uniform in size, color, and maturity, were purchased from a local food market and used in the studies after confirming the absence of pesticide residues. Acetonitrile, methanol, and H2O (liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) grade) were obtained from J.T. Baker (Avantor, Radnor, PA, USA), whereas formic acid (purity: 99.5%) was purchased from FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical (Osaka, Japan), and QuEChERS extraction kits (EN 15662: disodium citrate sesquihydrate (0.5 g), sodium citrate (1 g), NaCl (1 g), and MgSO4 (4 g)) and dispersive solid-phase extraction (d-SPE) cleanup kits for fruits and vegetables (MgSO4 (150 mg) and primary-secondary amine (25 mg)) were purchased from Agilent Technologies (Santa Clara, CA, USA). The deionized H2O (18.2 MΩ·cm) for application in washing and the pesticide spray solution was prepared using a Milli-Q water purification system (Direct-Q3UV, MilliporeSigma, Burlington, MA, USA), and individual stock solutions (100 mg/L) were prepared using analytical standards in acetonitrile and stored at − 20 °C. Working solutions for use in matrix-matched calibration were freshly prepared for each analysis via the serial dilution of the stock solutions with blank sample extracts.

Fruit immersion in the pesticide solutions

The pesticides were applied via immersion, which is the simplest method and closest to field application. For use in pesticide treatment, the commercial formulations were diluted with H2O in respective ratios of 1:2,000 and 1:3,000 for MAND and MEZ, in accordance with the GAPs registered in the Republic of Korea. The MAND (7.5 mL) or MEZ (5.0 mL) formulation was added to H2O (15 L). Persimmons (50 ea) were immersed in the diluted pesticide solution (15 L) containing MAND (200 mg/L) or MEZ (67 mg/L) for 5 min. They were then air-dried under shaded conditions at 25 °C for 24 h to remove the excess solution from the fruit surfaces25. The treated persimmons were subsequently used in the evaluation of washing treatments and the residue dissipation during postharvest ripening.

Washing

Washing was conducted using the following methods commonly utilized in households. The detailed procedure was modified to suit persimmons based on the method of Yang and Choi26: washing under running tap water, hand rubbing under running tap water, or washing with acetic acid, a washing agent, or baking soda, followed by rinsing under running water. Each washing procedure was performed using three replicates, with five persimmons per replicate. The persimmons were washed under running tap water (pH 7, 20 °C) at a flow rate of 0.1 L/s for 12 s, with or without hand rubbing. For use in chemical treatment, solutions were prepared by diluting acetic acid (50 mL; Glacial acetic acid ≥ 99%; Yoorim Corporation, Busan, Republic of Korea)27, the washing agent (10 mL; Polyethylene glycol (PEG); Shinyo Pure Chemicals Co., Ltd., Osaka, Japan)23, or baking soda (500 g; 100% sodium bicarbonate; Aekyung, Seoul, Republic of Korea)27 in H2O (5 L) to yield final concentrations of 1.0% (v/v) acetic acid, 0.2% (v/v) PEG washing agent, and 10% (w/v) baking soda, respectively. The persimmons were immersed in the prepared chemical solution (5 L) for 1 min and then rinsed under tap water running at a flow rate of 0.1 L/s for 12 s. After washing, the samples were air-dried at 25 °C for 4 h. The samples were peeled, and the masses of the flesh, peel, and calyx were measured to determine the distributions of the pesticide residues in each fraction on an individual-sample basis.

Ripening procedure

The dissipation patterns of the pesticide residues within persimmons during postharvest ripening were investigated. The treated persimmons were stored under shaded conditions at 25 °C and 80% relative humidity for 21 d, and composite samples of five persimmons per replicate were collected in triplicate at 1, 3, 7, 14, and 21 d after treatment. At each sampling time, all samples were weighed to monitor changes in fruit quality during ripening.

Sample preparation

Sample preparation was conducted according to the analytical method outlined in the Food Code of the Republic of Korea15. The collected samples (flesh and peel) were homogenized with dry ice using a blender (Blixer 2, Robot Coupe, Vincennes, France) and stored at − 20 °C until analysis. A homogenized sample (10 g) was transferred to a conical tube and extracted with acetonitrile (10 mL) via vigorous shaking for 2 min at 1,300 rpm using a high-speed shaker (2010 Geno/Grinder, SPEX SamplePrep, Metuchen, NJ, USA). The extract was then combined with QuEChERS extraction salts, shaken for 2 min at 1,300 rpm, and centrifuged for 5 min at 3,515 × g using a centrifuge (Combi 408, Hanil Scientific, Gimpo, Republic of Korea). An aliquot (1 mL) of the supernatant was transferred to a d-SPE cleanup tube, vortexed for 1 min, and centrifuged for 5 min at 15,928 × g using a microcentrifuge (M15R, Hanil Scientific). The resulting supernatant was diluted with acetonitrile (1:1, v/v) and subsequently analyzed using a liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) system.

Analysis of the residue using LC-QTOF MS

The MAND and MEZ residues within the persimmons were analyzed using an ExionLC AD HPLC system coupled to an X500R QTOF mass spectrometer equipped with a Turbo V source (SCIEX, Marlborough, MA, USA). Chromatographic separation was realized using a Kinetex PS-C18 column (100 × 3 mm, i.d. 2.6 μm, Phenomenex, Torrance, CA, USA) maintained at 40 °C, with a flow rate of 0.2 mL/min. The mobile phases comprised H2O (A) and methanol (B), both containing 0.1% (v/v) formic acid and delivered using the following gradient program: 75% B from 0.0 to 0.5 min; 75–95% B from 0.5 to 4.5 min; 95% B from 4.5 to 7.5 min, followed by 3.5 min of column equilibration. The injection volume was 2 µL, and the retention times of MAND and MEZ were 4.80 and 5.06 min, respectively. The mass spectrometer was operated in positive electrospray ionization (ESI) mode, and the high-resolution multiple-reaction-monitoring conditions were optimized with the following source parameters: spray voltage, 5.0 kV; desolvation line temperature, 550 °C; ion source gas 1 (neutralizing gas), 50 psi; ion source gas 2 (heating gas), 50 psi; curtain gas, 25 psi; and collision-activated dissociation gas, 7 psi. The N2 gas used in nebulization, heating, and collision was produced using a N2 generator (Euroscience, Seongnam, Republic of Korea). The respective MAND and MEZ precursor ions m/z 314.16 and 320.15 were selected based on time-of-flight–mass spectrometry full-scan analysis over the m/z range 100–400. Two product ions were monitored for each compound as quantifier and qualifier transitions: m/z 192.10 and 160.07 for MAND and m/z 70.04 and 125.01 for MEZ, respectively. The declustering potentials (DPs) and collision energies (CEs) were optimized at a DP of 80 V with CEs of 10 and 20 V for MAND and a DP of 30 V with CEs of 30 and 50 V for MEZ. The data were acquired and processed using the SCIEX OS software (ver. 1.2).

Method validation for analytical quality

The analytical method used in pesticide residue determination was validated in accordance with the European SANTE/11312/2021 guidelines28 in terms of the following parameters: limits of quantification (LOQs), linearity, matrix effects (MEs), accuracy, precision, and consistency of the ion ratio. The LOQs for MAND and MEZ were established based on the instrumental LOQs (signal-to-noise ratio ≥ 10), injection volume (2 µL), sample mass (10 g), volume of the extraction solvent (10 mL), and a dilution factor of 2. The linearities of the matrix-matched calibration curves for both pesticides were assessed using the coefficients of determination (R2) in the concentration range 5–100 µg/L. The MEs were evaluated by comparing the slopes of the calibration curves prepared using blank sample extracts to those of the calibration curves prepared using acetonitrile. The accuracy and precision of the method were assessed via recovery studies performed in triplicate at multiple fortification levels: LOQ and 10 × LOQ for all matrices, with additional levels of 100 × LOQ for whole and peel samples, and 10,000 × LOQ and 3,000 × LOQ for MAND and MEZ, respectively, in calyx samples. The storage stabilities of MAND and MEZ in each component of the persimmon were evaluated over approximately two months between homogenization and analysis at a fortification level of 10 × LOQ. The consistencies of the ion ratios of the quantifier and qualifier transitions of the sample extracts were also evaluated and compared to those of matrix-matched calibration standards for MAND and MEZ.

Results and discussion

Performance and analytical reliability of the method

Chromatographic detection was performed at respective retention times of 4.80 ± 0.01 and 5.06 ± 0.03 min for MAND and MEZ (Figs. S1–S2), with a relative standard deviation (RSD) of < 0.7%, indicating reproducible separation per injection under LC-MS/MS conditions. No interference peaks were identified around the retention times of the target compounds using blank samples, confirming the selectivity of the method. The LOQs of both pesticides in all matrices were 0.01 mg/kg, and the linearity was good, with R2 > 0.9987 in each case, satisfying the criterion of R2 > 0.98 (Table S1). The respective calculated MEs of 1.5–3.0% and 0.3–10.7% for MAND and MEZ were minimal (< 20%), indicating a slight signal enhancement but a negligible influence of the matrix on quantification accuracy29. The deviations in the transition ion ratios of the sample extracts relative to those of the calibration standards satisfied the ± 30% requirements specified in the guidance (Table S1). As shown in Table 1, the mean recovery ranges from 81.1 to 115.5%, with an RSD of 0.2–10.1%, satisfying the acceptance criteria (mean recovery and RSD of 70–120% and < 20%, respectively). The storage stability study reveals a recovery rate of 92.9–108.1%, indicating no significant degradation of the pesticides over two months of storage. The established analytical method was fully validated in accordance with the SANTE/11312/2021 guidelines28, and thus, the method was found suitable for use in accurately and reliably quantifying the MAND and MEZ residues in each component of the persimmon.

Initial residue distributions after immersion

The distributions of the pesticide residues among the different components of the persimmon were evaluated to understand the residue patterns following application. The mass of each persimmon used in the study was approximately 230 g. The flesh, peel, and calyx respectively account for 87.6%, 11.2%, and 1.2% of the total individual mass (Table 2). The distribution ratio of the residue was determined based on the amount of the residue (µg) calculated by multiplying the concentration (mg/kg) within and mass (g) of each component of the persimmon (Fig. 1). The residue distributions are highly heterogeneous across the fruit components (Table 3). The calyx exhibits the highest respective residue concentrations of 34.4 and 24.9 mg/kg for MAND and MEZ, which represent approximately 64.2% and 79.9% of the total amounts of the residues, respectively. The calyx, which is morphologically similar to a leaf, displays a relatively large surface area per unit mass owing to its rough, fibrous structural properties, thus contributing to the adsorption and retention of the pesticide residues10. The peel exhibits the second-highest respective concentrations of 1.24 and 0.642 mg/kg of MAND and MEZ, accounting for 22.7% and 17.3% of the total amounts of the residues, respectively. The edible flesh respectively accounts for only 13.1% and 2.8% of the total amounts of the residues of MAND and MEZ. This is owing to the edible flesh having lower residue concentrations and higher fraction of the total mass, in addition to the flesh being in indirect contact with the pesticide solution. As both fungicides are systemic, moderately-to-highly lipophilic compounds (logP values of MAND and MEZ of 3.51 and 3.85, respectively), they should easily penetrate the waxy cuticular layers14, but most of the residues accumulate on the surface rather than migrating into the flesh. Therefore, inward diffusion is limited under the immersion conditions applied in this study, and thus, the fruit peel is the predominant site of residue accumulation.

As shown in Fig. 1, the flesh-to-peel residue proportion of MAND is 57.7%, which is approximately 3.6-fold higher than that of MEZ (16.2%). Hence, a higher distribution into the flesh is observed for MAND, which is attributed to its translaminar activity30. The cuticular membrane of the persimmon comprises cutin (48.4%) and wax (34.7%), and the wax comprises pentacyclic triterpenoids, such as ursolic acid and oleanolic acid31. MAND is less hydrophobic than MEZ, and thus, MAND penetrates and translocates more easily into the flesh owing to its weaker interactions with the cutin monomers and its higher tendency to diffuse through the lipid-rich matrix. This enhanced partitioning into the cuticular lipid phase may contribute to its higher proportion in the flesh compared to that of MEZ.

Efficiency of residue removal via washing

The levels of effectiveness of various household washing practices in removing the MAND and MEZ residues from persimmons were systematically evaluated (Table 3; Fig. 2). The physicochemical properties of MAND and MEZ–including their molecular weights (313.4 and 319.8 for MAND and MEZ, respectively), vapor pressures (9.15 × 10− 5 and 2.1 × 10–5 mPa for MAND and MEZ, respectively), and polarities (logP values of 3.51 and 3.85 for MAND and MEZ, respectively)–are generally similar14.

However, the efficiencies of MAND and MEZ removal, in addition to their flesh-to-peel residue proportions, clearly differ (Fig. 1), and thus, evaluating the residue characteristics of pesticide based solely on lipophilicity and water solubility is limited. Instead, the results suggest a higher potential for peel-to-flesh penetration or redistribution into the flesh using MAND. These differences may be attributed to the variations in affinity for the cuticle-wax layer caused by the subtle differences in the polarities and chemical structures of MAND and MEZ. Furthermore, adjuvants used in formulations can affect the wettability, adsorption, and permeability of the spray solution on the fruit surface. This is supported by differences in the rates of dermal penetration in rats using the technical grade and formulated products of furathiocarb32. Therefore, the permeability and partitioning characteristics of pesticides in persimmons are governed by the complex interplay between the physicochemical properties of the active ingredients and their formulations, which can lead to significant differences within the fruit.

In whole fruits, the highest level of MAND removal (78.9%) is realized via hand rubbing under running tap water, followed by treatment with the PEG washing agent (67.4%), acetic acid (62.3%), baking soda (54.2%), and simple washing under running tap water (44.3%, Fig. 2A). Hand rubbing likely disrupts or removes the cuticular wax layer, thus improving the desorption of the residues. This action removes a significant portion of the MAND adsorbed onto and absorbed into the peel, which is consistent with the removal of 74.6% of MAND from the peel (Fig. 2A). Conversely, the efficiencies of MEZ removal from whole fruits using the rubbing and chemical treatments are 51.9–58.8%, but simple washing under running tap water results in the lowest removal rate of 33.7% (Fig. 2B). As < 15% of the MEZ in the flesh and peel is removed, hand rubbing or chemical treatment is essential for effectively removing MEZ residues.

The respective levels of the MAND and MEZ residues in the flesh are 14–41- and 35–102-fold lower than those in the peel, and the differences in concentration after applying the washing methods are relatively minor (Table 3). The MAND and MEZ residues in the flesh respectively decrease by 55.0–81.4% and 14.5–68.7% after washing, with a higher removal efficiency generally observed for MAND (Fig. 2). As the flesh does not directly encounter the washing solutions, these reductions are attributed to incomplete separation during peeling. The higher removal of MAND indicates that the distribution of MAND, with its lower lipophilicity, is increased within the inner peel and peel-flesh interface.

Hand rubbing under running tap water significantly decreases the levels of the MAND and MEZ residues within the peel from 1.24 to 0.314 and 0.642 to 0.350 mg/kg, respectively. In contrast, simple washing under running tap water results in no significant differences in the residual concentrations (Table 3). MEZ is most effectively removed (54.1%) using baking soda (Fig. 2B). Although alkalinity promotes the degradation of several pesticides, MAND and MEZ are stable in alkaline environments, and thus, the potential for the chemical degradation of MEZ is considered low14,17. Meanwhile, components containing COOH groups–such as triterpenoids and other acidic components within the peel–may partially ionize, increasing the polarity and altering the physicochemical properties of the peel31. Consequently, the hydrophobic interaction between MEZ and the cuticular wax layer is likely weakened, facilitating the desorption and removal of MEZ during alkaline washing.

The removal efficiency of MAND from the calyx is 0.7–20.2%, with hand rubbing under running tap water and treatment with the PEG washing agent showing significantly higher effectiveness in residue removal (Fig. 2A). In contrast, MEZ is effectively removed from the calyx, with removal efficiencies of 28.3–60.0% and no significant differences between the washing methods except for washing under running tap water (Fig. 2B). These results suggest that the adsorption and distribution rates of MEZ on the rough, fibrous surface of the calyx exceed those of MAND. This is consistent with the higher potential of MAND for penetration and diffusion from the peel into the flesh. Despite the significantly reduced level of the MEZ residue within the calyx, it remains as high as 9.9–17.8 mg/kg (Table 3), and thus, significant safety concerns persist if the calyx is consumed or used.

Residue dissipation during the ripening of the persimmon

The dissipation characteristics of the MAND and MEZ residues within persimmons during natural ripening were investigated. No significant differences in the levels of the MAND residues in the whole fruit (including the flesh and peel) are observed over time. However, the mean level of the MAND residue at 21 d of ripening is approximately 120% of the initial mean level (Fig. 3A). In contrast, MEZ residues are decreased by approximately 8% of the initial level (Fig. 3B). The mass of the fruit decreases continuously during the ripening period–by approximately 27.2 and 34.0% for the MAND- and MEZ-treated persimmons, respectively–which is attributed to H2O loss (Fig. 3A and B). Analysis was performed based on the absolute amount of the residue to realistically evaluate the dissipation pattern that accounts for the concentration effect caused by H2O loss (Fig. 4). The absolute amount of the residue is calculated by multiplying the residual concentration by the mass of each individual fruit. Based on the calculated amounts of the residues, the respective estimated λ values are − 0.0052 (95% CI, − 0.0135 to − 0.0031 d–1) and − 0.0253 d–1 (95% CI, − 0.0306 to − 0.0200 d–1) for MAND and MEZ, which correspond to respective DT50 values of 132.7 (95% CI, 51.3–226.2 d) and 27.4 d (95% CI, 22.7–34.6 d) for MAND and MEZ. The persimmons ripen within 21 d in this study, and an extended ripening period would likely result in quality deterioration or rot. Consequently, the potential for pesticide reduction during the ripening period is limited. A previous study reported that broflanilide residues in persimmons decreased from 0.05 mg/kg to 0.04 mg/kg over a 21 d ripening period, reflecting the low dissipation rate observed in the present study27. The DT50 of chlorfenapyr in persimmons stored at 4 °C for 21 d was reported to be 23.9 d, whereas storage at 20 °C yielded a DT50 of 11.0 d, indicating an approximately 2-fold decrease in the dissipation rate under cold storage conditions23. An approximately 10-fold increase in the half-lives of pesticide residues in apples and lemons has also been reported under cold storage compared to ambient temperature storage24. However, since cold storage delays the ripening process, ambient temperature storage is preferable to ensure adequate ripening of persimmons. Meanwhile, given that the half-life of chlorfenapyr in on-tree fruit was 8.8 d23, the dissipation rate of pesticide residues during post-harvest storage appears to be lower than that observed in on-tree fruit.

Fruit ripening is an irreversible process involving physicochemical and sensory changes, which are regulated by various plant hormones, such as ethylene, abscisic acid, and indole-3-acetic acid33. These hormones activate pectinase and cell wall-modifying enzymes, causing the structures of the cell walls to loosen and degrade, ultimately softening the flesh. Concurrently, the levels of tannins decrease, resulting in the loss of astringency, and furthermore, the color changes from yellow to red due to the biosynthesis and modification of pigments, such as anthocyanins, carotenoids, and chlorophyll34,35. The levels of β-galactosidase, endo-1,4-β-glucanase, pectate lyase, pectinesterase, and polygalacturonase increase during this stage33. However, considering the limited pesticide dissipation observed in this study, pesticide degradation via biochemical metabolism exhibits a low correlation with the enzymatic activity associated with fruit ripening.

Similar to the dissipation patterns of the whole fruits, the levels of the MAND and MEZ residues within the calyces display no significant differences during the ripening period of 21 d, remaining at 65.6–84.1 and 23.4–32.1 mg/kg, respectively (Fig. 3C and D). Additionally, as no significant differences in the masses of the calyces are observed, these results suggest that no pesticide degradation occurs during ripening. This is likely because of the fibrous structure, low moisture content, and consequent low enzymatic activity within the calyx. Considering the high initial levels of the residues and low potential for degradation, additional precautions are required prior to preparing medicinal or functional products using the calyx.

Conclusions

This study evaluated the residue distributions and dissipation kinetics of the systemic fungicides MAND and MEZ in persimmons, along with the effectiveness of household washing methods in removing their residues. Although both pesticides displayed similar physicochemical properties, they exhibited differences in their distributions within the fruit and the effects of washing on their removal. The results suggested that the residue characteristics of pesticides were influenced by their intrinsic properties–such as lipophilicity and water solubility–in addition to their specific interactions with the formulation and cuticular wax layers. Hand rubbing achieved more effective removal than that achieved by chemical treatment. Further, hand rubbing represented a greener, more low-cost approach with reduced H2O usage and washing time compared to chemical treatment, which requires an additional step to rinse off the washing agent. During ripening, the residue levels were significantly affected by H2O loss rather than biochemical degradation, causing them to persist over time. The calyx, in particular, consistently exhibited high residue levels, with low degrees of removal via washing and limited degradation, and thus, it is a potential source of pesticide exposure if consumed or used as a functional ingredient. The findings of this study provide comprehensive insights into the characteristics of residues within persimmons, and they are expected to contribute to mitigating consumer exposure to these residues via the proposed viable washing strategies. Although pesticide residues were analyzed 24 h after immersion in this study, the residue levels observed under controlled experimental conditions may not fully represent those occurring in actual cultivation environments, indicating limitations in accurately assessing pesticide exposure reduction. Therefore, future research is necessary to establish the precise magnitude of pesticide exposure mitigation achievable through washing practices.
