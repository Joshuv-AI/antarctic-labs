A. Structure design and manufacturing
A.1 Conventional Brazed, disk-type NCRF Accelerating Structures
Fabrication of conventional, brazed NRCF accelerating structures is proceed via the following steps:
1. Procurement of accelerator grade copper1
2. Machining:
 1.1. Rough machining of components from stock material,
 1.2. (Optional) Stress relieving of components (500C/1hr in vacuum),
 1.3. Precision machining of RF and positioning features.
2. Mechanical inspection for form and finish.
3. (Optional) Clamped cold testing.
4. Chemical cleaning of components.
5. Brazing of components into sub-assembly at T1[oC] : Example: copper waveguide onto stainless steel
 flange; cells into cell stack.
 5.1. Inspection of sub-assemblies: mechanical, hermeticity, visual.
6. Post-braze machining of sub-assembly: Example: water cooling channels, waveguide to cell stack
 interface.
7. Brazing of sub-assembly components at temperature T2 < T1: Example: cooling channels onto cell stack.
8. Final auxiliary joining: аttached target/gun/vacuum flanges and water cooling via TIG welding and/or
 mechanical fastening.
It is important to mention that step 2: machining, often involves multiple different machining setups as well as
the potential for using different machining equipment for each setup, with an example being rough machining
on a low-precision milling machining and precision machining on a high-precision hybrid lathe with milling
capabilities machine. Another item to note is the degree of mechanical inspection needed depends significantly
on the complexity of the part, as well as whether or not it is a first article.
In terms of chemical cleaning needed for NCRF AS, there are some differences in processes used, with some
manufacturers only performing ultrasonic cleaning in a mild detergent, while others perform a combination of
ultrasonic cleaning as well as 1-2µm of material removal via isotropic chemical etching. This difference is
based on the target gradients and the machining technologies utilized, as low gradient AS do not require as
pristine surface quality and ultra-precise machining technologies typically do not impart high levels of foreign
material contamination.
Brazing is done either in a hydrogen or vacuum atmosphere, again with some differences in technology used
depending on provider and application. Roughly speaking, hydrogen brazing is historically more common and
there are arguments to be made regarding improved cleanliness. Unfortunately, due to the safety challenges
associated with hydrogen gas, it is an increasingly difficult to source process technology. Alternatively,
vacuum furnace technology has continuously improved over the decades and the resulting high gradient
performance between vacuum and hydrogen brazed components is often indistinguishable.
Regardless of the process used, brazing has a few significant advantages compared to fusion-based joining
methods such as electron beam welding. Firstly: there is very little thermal deformation of the final assembly,
as the parts are heated and cooled uniformly and thus are less susceptible to distort. During welding, there is
significant localized heating, which leads to localized thermal expansion and contraction, resulting in the
increased potential for component deformation. Often, this thermal deformation can be minimized through a
combination of proper weld joint design, appropriate welding technology and optimized welding sequences.
During brazing, a multimeter long AS can be stacked vertically with self-locating registration features such
that the central cell axis can be very precisely aligned. For reference, the straightness of the beam axis for 1.8m
long C-band structure designs for an FEL application are ±50 µm while the X-band straightness would require

Besides issues of thermal deformation, brazing also has the advantage that multiple hermitic joints can be
formed during a single brazing operation and economies of scale can be realized when brazing multiple
structures in the same furnace. For example, the roughly 100+ cells used in the Spring8, SwissFEL and ELI-

welding is possible, it is often more complicated than brazing.

Another useful capability of brazing is the so-called ‘step-brazing’ process, where sub-assemblies are joined
using a braze alloy of melting temperature T1. Critical mating surfaces of the sub-assembly are then machined
then brazed to other sub-assemblies using a braze alloy with melting temperature T2. Because the melting
temperature of braze alloy used in step 2 is less than the alloy used in step one, the prior joints remain
untouched. As an example, a waveguide input coupler is brazed at T1 along with the primary cell stack. The
mating feature between the waveguide coupler and cover plate is machined to a precise dimension so that it
mates to the cell stack. The cell stack is then brazed the waveguide feed in Step 2.
Despite these numerous advantages, the striking disadvantage of brazed accelerator structures in the significant
reduction of material hardness, generally decreasing the yield strength from 350 MPa to 60 MPa and reducing
the surface hardness from 100 HV to 40 HV. It has been shown that hard copper alloys with high electrical
conductivity have high gradient performance which exceeds copper which has been softened by the thermal
cycle of the brazing process.

From an RF perspective, a key feature of split-cell structures is that surface currents do not flow across any
bonding junctions.
While conventional AS are typically composed of individual discs which are turned, split-cell AS are
manufactured using only multi-axis milling. Split-cell structures significantly reduce the AS part count and
greatly simplify the assembly process, offering potentially significant cost savings. Issues such as longitudinal
alignment between parts, as well as straightness of beamline bore, can be significant.
Another advantage of split-cell structures is the ability to implement so-called ‘distributed coupling’ [4]. In this
topology, internal waveguides run alongside the accelerator cavities, and power is fed to each cell directly. In
comparison to conventional side-coupled or aperture coupled AS, the distributed coupling approach enables
cavity shapes to be optimized solely for high gradients, maximized shunt impedance and permits more
localized power reflection during breakdown events compared to traditionally coupled structures.
The DARPA GRIT program aims to develop technology related to high flux, hard X-ray compact inverse
Compton Scattering sources for medical and inspection applications. Recently, split-cell, vacuum brazed, 1 m
long, distributed coupling C-band accelerating structures have recently been demonstrated by SLAC and
RadiaBeam [5], with examples of the components, finished accelerating structure and GRIT beamline shown
below.

Figure 3: Distributed coupling C-band accelerating structure developed by SLAC and RadiaBeam [6] (Left)
machined components including waveguide feed network (Center) Low-power RF testing of the finished structure
(Right). Two accelerating structures installed on the GRIT beamline at RadiaBeam.

A.3 Welded NCRF Structures
Because no RF currents cross the any bonding junctions; split-cell structures are well suited for welding, which
could include TIG, laser or electron beam welding. As discussed below, the exact details of the design and
manufacturing of welded NCRF structures is not mature with significant mechanical and RF challenges which
have not yet been addressed.

Fundamentally, the challenge with welded NCRF structures is that the welding source joins the parts together
by only accessing the outer surface of the structure. Furthermore, small weld features with minimal contact
between faying surfaces is desirable because this limits the necessary heat input for hermetic joints as well as
minimal thermal deformation. In comparison, braze alloy can be liberally used between large area faying
surface with no impact on the final AS geometry.
A fairly mature X-band, split-cell design fabricated from quadrants was demonstrated by KEK, including
integrated cooling ports, with an exploded view of the design and the welded cell stack shown is below [7].

Figure 4: KEK electron beam welded quadrant X-band accelerating structure (Left) Conceptual CAD design with
exploded view of quadrants and waveguide feeds (Right) Quadrants welded together without waveguides.

One important feature of this particular structure design is the use of waveguide couplers attached to the ends
of the structure. Presumably, these waveguide couplers are vacuum brazed using conventional approaches,
then welded onto the ends of the structure, resulting in a hybrid welded/brazed accelerating structure. It does
not seem like the structure matured past the welded cell stack shown in Figure 4-right and was never high
power tested.
Similarly, researchers at SLAC proposed a welding manufacturing strategy of X-band split-cell structures
based on a combination of electron beam welding, brazing and mechanical fastening, with the structure design
and weld joint geometry shown below, including numerous stainless steel, knife edge vacuum flanges.

Like the KEK structure design, the SLAC design utilizes a series of linear welds to join the structure halves
together, as shown in Figure 5-center. Attaching the vacuum flanges is more complex, as it requires a separate
setup for the radial welds of the beam port flanges as well as XY CNC-controlled plug welds of the side-
mounted vacuum pumping ports. Because the electron beam must “see” the weld joint, the vacuum flanges

need to be welded onto the stems after joining the stem to the body, otherwise the flange will shadow the weld
joint. These additional welds require multiple pump down cycles and setups, incurring significant costs and
increasing the likelihood of structure contamination with vacuum pump hydrocarbons or weld vapor.
Furthermore, the four waveguide WR90 vacuum flanges are mechanically fastened to the AS body. The
technical feasibility of this approach is unknown, as the waveguide stainless steel bolts must fasten into the

copper knife edge to annealed copper gasket to stainless steel knife edge vacuum seal is unknown.
Additional design compromises of this SLAC structure include clamp-on cooling circuits and push-only tuning
pins. Weld joint design and process development is also not extensively discussed, although insufficient weld
penetration create a risk for virtual vacuum leaks formed by two tightly pressed surfaces, while over powering
the welds can result in spatter on the inside RF surface.
Although not optimized, both SLAC and KEK EBW welded structure design address two fundamental
challenge of welding compared to brazing: the beam must be able to ‘see’ the weld joint and welded joints
must be formed serially. Risk for virtual vacuum leaks between two pressed surfaces are also a challenge for
welded NCRF structures, limiting potential design choices compared brazed NCFC components.

A.4 Baseline RF & Mechanical design
While a complete mature design for welded, split-cell NCRF accelerating structures has yet to be
demonstrated; we will utilize a C-band, quadrant type, distributed-coupling cell stack proposed as part of the
C3 initiative [9]. The C3 concept utilizes liquid nitrogen cooling of conventional copper to improve surface
conductivity, and thus increase the structure shut impedance by nearly a factor of 3 [10].
In its current baseline design reiteration, the C3 accelerating structure is 1.1m long device composed of 4

The manufacturing strategy is based on a a series of copper EB welds which are listed in Figure 6-top right.
Namely EB weld #1 is four, length-wise linear welds which join the quadrants together while two EB weld #2
close out front and back of the quadrant structure. Next EB weld #3 is composed of four plug-type welds
joining the brazed copper/stainless steel waveguide stem to the upper section of the structure. Finally, ten plug-

with serve either as beam or vacuum ports. In the following process, a series of manual stainless-steel to
stainless TIG welds are performed, joining the waveguide flange to the waveguide stem (TIG#1) or the
vacuum flange to the vacuum stem (TIG#2).
This two-step EB then TIG welding approach addresses a significant geometric challenge of the structure,
which is that it is difficult for the electron beam to weld beneath the shadow of a flange. This approach
addresses this issue by EB welding brazed copper/stainless steel stems to various ports on the structure. The
flanges can then be TIG welded using stainless steel to stainless steel welding. The EB welding process will
take a full 8 hrs, with a pumpdown beginning the day before. Special consideration regarding handling,
assembly and EB welding setup will be needed to account for the weight of the structure (250kg) and to
consolidate the number of setups within the EBW machine. With the device fixtured onto an axially rotating
remotely controllable fixture, it may be possible to reduce the number of setups to 3 total, one for all 4x
longitudinal welds, 4x coupler welds and 8x side stems and a setup each for the end joints and stems. However
designing and shaking down such a fixture have not be performed and would encompass a significant, but
potentially highly valuable engineering effort.
In terms of structure design, we assume that the external cooling channels are mechanically fastened to the
structure, similar to idea proposed by Dolgashev et al and shown in Figure 5, although it may be possible to
mill cooling channels into the structure which could subsequently be sealed with EBW’ed covers.
In comparison, a TESLA-style 9-cell SRF cavity is composed of 62 EB welds. Many of these welds each

B.1 Machining and inspection
The quality of the RF surfaces has a tremendous effect on the high gradient performance of an NCRF
accelerating construction. Precise mating surfaces and registrations features are also critically important since
these determine the alignment between the RF quads as well as the final straightness of the AS. Precision
machine tools, RF-specific machining expertise and state-of-the-art computer aided manufacturing(CAM)
software are critical to achieving these requirements.

B.1.1 Feedstock material considerations
The properties of the feedstock material have a significant effect on the quality of the finished part. The
materials standard for high power RF devices is ASTM F68: Standard Specification for Oxygen-Free Copper
in Wrought Forms for Electron Devices, which contains specifications for chemical purity, surface defects,
sub-surface microporosity, grain size as well hydrogen embrittlement testing. A key requirement of F68-grade
copper is low oxygen content, with a maximum 5 ppm of O permitted. This would be considered Oxygen Free
Electronic grade copper. Furthermore, most accelerator applications additionally require the use of F68 Class 2
or better copper, which is determined through special macro and microscopic investigation.
During the development of the CLIC AS technology, CERN established standard for high gradient copper,
EDMS No: 790779: Oxygen-Free Electronic copper Bars/blanks/ingots. This standard further builds upon
ASTM F68 by including specifications for multi-directional 3D forging, half-hard temper (yield strength: 200-
240 MPa minimum, tensile strength: 240-280 MPa) and fine (max. 90 μm) and homogeneous grain size
(ASTM E112, grain size 4 or greater). Including requirements on forging and temper limits risks of voids
during machining. The homogeneity of the material is 100% ultrasonically inspected to detect any potential
continuity faults, following CERN specifications [13].
One fundamental challenge concerning precision manufacturing of welded accelerating structures is the
internal residual stress within the stock material. Cold work process such as rolling or forging hardens copper
by creating residual compressive and tensile stress throughout the material. When material is removed during
machining, the equilibrium between compressive and tensile stress can cause the part to warp and deform,
resulting in loss of geometric part accuracy. During fabrication of brazed NCRF structures, some workpieces
will undergo a stress-relieving heat treatment at 500oC. While beneficial for geometric accuracy, this stress-
relieve process will soften the hardened copper, losing any high gradient advantages. In the following, we
assume this issue has already been address via strict source of feedstock material as well as detailed machining
strategies developed by the National Labs working in conjunction with industry.

B.1.2 Machining considerations
Historically, due to their topology, choice of diffusion bonding and finish and tolerance requirements, critical
high gradient NCRF surfaces were turned on a lathe with single-point diamond tooling to extremely tight
tolerances and fine finishes. Notably X-band CLIC structures required a flatness of <1 μm, roughness of
Ra<25 nm and form accuracy of ±2.5 μm. Turning has the distinct advantage that the cutting surface has a
singular, well defined edge and the cutting tool itself is stationary, thereby limiting vibrations. From a design
perspective, turning has a distinct disadvantage in that the geometry being turned should be rotationally
symmetric. Advanced machine tool configurations are possible, including synchronized tool/chuck motion as
well as the use of ‘mill-turn’ type lathes, which combines both milling and turning within a single machine but
stop start motion and tool engagement / disengagement must be controlled carefully in order to not spoil the
pristine surfaces.
During the fabrication of the SwissFEL linacs, PSI developed a lathe-based turning process in-house before
transferring technology to industry for series production [14]. The primary requirements were a
environmentally-controlled machining facility (18.50°C +/- 0.1°C, <50% relative humidity), pneumatically
stabilized slanted bed lathe and poly and mono-crystalline diamond (PCD, MCD) cutting tools. The result was
a form tolerances of ± 2 μm with a surface finish Ra <25 nm and concentricity of less than 50 μm on the disk
structures [15]. When combined with the automated robotic assembly, the SwissFEL linac did not require any
post braze tuning.
Compared to turning, milling involves a rotating cutting tool with multiple cutting surfaces and a quasi-
stationary workpiece. The lever arm of the rotating cutting tool, as well as asymmetry in the cutting tool
surfaces can lead to issues regarding form and finish accuracy. But unlike turning, milling enables the
formation of complex and precise shapes with a single part setup especially when 5-axis machining technology
is employed.
Milling and turning are complementary technologies which can often be used in conjunction, as shown in the
ELI-NP cell design demonstrated by INFN. The best in class demonstration of vertical milling was achieved at
KEK using 5-axis milling of a X-band accelerating structure, achieving a maximum peak roughness of Ry ≈ 1
μm and a profile accuracy of ± 2.5 μm, with some reports indicating that electropolishing was used [3,7]

Some of the known current challenges to realizing a welded structure shown are shown in Figure 8.
Many of these issues are routinely solved through tooling and manufacturing planning and RF design
compromises. For example, undercut features in the cell noses are reduced to sizes that permit manufacturing
in conventional 3 axis mills with lollipop undercutting tools and piece part flatness requirements are
maintained through clever implementation of tooling to constraint components through the bonding processes.
However, the required use of tooling and more involved setups typically results in additional personnel touch
time and thus cost increases. This highlights the importance of involving a full engineering and manufacturing
team before concluding the RF design process in order to ensure that a production optimized design is realized.
Precise machine tools such as vertical milling centers are characterized by high thermal stability and
compensation feedback, hydrostatic-dampening, high stiffness, wear-free guideways and drives. Strict
adherence to the specified precision also involves the use of controlled waviness of the cutting tools, where the
waviness is defined by the amount of deviation from a true circle and is measured from peak to valley (typical
range of waviness values is between 0.1 μm and 0.05 μm). [18] Further, embedded part probing and tool
setting features available on most modern CNC equipment are highly useful to ensure part location/orientation
and cutting tool location and form are known prior cutting the workpiece.
For large piece parts exceeding 1.5m or so, large format gantry machines can be utilized such as the GM-2-
5AX. The GM-2-5AX is a full 5-axis “gimbal head” machine, where both rotary axes are mounted on the Z-
axis ram, and the spindle/cutting tool assembly rotates, while the workpiece is stationary on the machine table.
This allows the cutting tool to point at any angle within the rotary-axis travel limits. This style of machine
requires more facility particulate and temperature environmental controls due to its open topology, along with
stiffer foundations to manage the higher loads and lower resonant frequencies. These factors will significantly
add to the infrastructure and capex costs during factory setup and thus the choice to constrain the OAL to ~1m

B.1.3 Metrology
A critical requirement in all precision manufacturing is the ability to verify that the components were
manufactured to the required specifications. Therefore, following critical machining operations it is important
to perform measurements of the mechanical features generated and to ensure that other critical dimensions
were not affected by fixturing or cutting forces. Although some features may be inspected through the use of
conventional manual inspection tools such as micrometers, gauge pins and various indicator setups, complex
geometries and numerous geometries are best inspected through the use of coordinate measuring machines
(CMMs). The most frequently utilized types of CMMs within the accelerator manufacturing industry are
Bridge CMMs, Cantilever CMMs and laser trackers.

Figure 11: Bridge CMM (left), Cantilever CMM (center) and laser tracker CMM (right).

The most precise and accurate CMMs tend to be in the bridge CMM category due to their rigid platforms,
thermal compensation systems and sensitive probing options. For example in the fabrication of CLIC
structures at CERN, bridge CMMs were utilized including a Leitz® PMM-C Infinity with a measuring error of
MPEE [μm] = 0.3 + L/1000 (L in [mm]), a probing error MPEP [μm] = 0.4 and a scanning probing error
MPETHP [μm] = 1.2 / 59 [s] according to ISO 10360-2 and ISO 10360-4 respectively [18]. This study also
considered the effects of various probe options particularly to ensure damage to cells were minimized. At the
time of this study the probe utilized was a LSP S4 capable of 0.02N-0.16N of contact trigger force required
depending on the required measurement settings. Unfortunately this low force probe still imparted marks on
the order of 50-80nm on soft copper surfaces. Leitz was also developing an even lower force capable LSP S4
ANF version to decrease this probing force down to 0.005N, although this product seems to have not yet made
it market.
For in-process inspection of less precise features, such as during conventional braze stack up or just prior to
RF cold test measurements, a laser tracker may be the best option and can be used to validate full assembly
form. Laser trackers are also the device of choice for final AS fiducialization where precision geometries of the
AS are related to fiducial cup locations to be utilized during the beamline installation survey group alignment
process.

B.2 Chemical cleaning
RF cavities used in high gradient accelerators are susceptible to a phenomenon known as RF breakdown. RF
breakdown is a major obstacle to higher gradient structures, limits the working power, and produces
irreversible surface damage in RF components.19 As described by Pande and Singh, “During a breakdown
event, the stored energy in the electromagnetic field suddenly pierces through the walls, usually in localized
places, causing local melting and contamination of the surrounding surface. This results in a very fast and
localized dissipation of stored energy. Durations involved in such incidence varies from tens to thousands of
nanosecond.”20
The physics leading to the onset of this phenomenon are not fully understood but it is generally known that RF
breakdown occurs when field emission in localized areas becomes enhanced and is sufficient to overcome the
dielectric breakdown potential of the vacuum in the accelerator cavity. The sites for enhanced field emission is
either an intrinsic surface feature (grain boundaries, cracks, inclusions, slip lines) or extrinstic surface feature

                                                                                                      Page 14 of 55

 “Manufacturing full-scale high gradient copper accelerators: Electron beam welding and allied processes”

(contamination, oxides, organic residues).21 Given the role of these surface defects in RF breakdown events,
surface preparation is a critical factor in preventing breakdown.
Achieving adequate surface preparation to reduce RF breakdown prior to the use of accelerator components is
done through cleaning, baking, and conditioning. Baking and conditioning are done in-situ and after surface
polishing and cleaning. Vacuum baking is performed to induce outgassing of dissolved sub-surface gases
(hydrogen) that can create plasmas that lead to RF breakdown. Conditioning (or processing) involves a series
of increasing pulse lengths and energies that “season” the structure prior to full-energy operation by removing
particle contaminants, desorbing gases, and vaporizing asperities. However, even with proper baking and
conditioning, polishing and cleaning of copper cavity components after fabrication and machining are still very
important steps in the process of manufacturing full-scale accelerators. Clean fabrication and assembly
processes are known to speed up RF conditioning [22], which can often be a very lengthy and costly part of the
installation process.
Another concern related to the surface finish and cleanliness of high-gradient structures is the presence of
“dark current” induced by high voltages. Dark current is a low-level current that arises under high voltage
fields prior to breakdown. Dark current can degrade the performance of high-gradient structures as well initiate
breakdown events. A study by Suzuki et al. showed that surface preparation has also been shown to affect dark
current emissions [23]. Their conclusion was that distilled water cleaning offered the best protection against
dark current emissions, however, more research is likely needed to clarify the connection between specific
finish, surface preparation techniques and dark current. Regardless, it can be stated that a cleaner surface will
minimize dark current.
Given the need for adequate surface smoothness and cleanliness, surface preparation is a critical need for the
production of high-gradient structures. This section investigates the requirements and best practices for
achieving surface preparation and cleanliness in high-gradient structures. Necessary capital expenditures,
equipment, materials, personnel, and laboratory space will be considered. Some conventional chemical and
electrochemical cleaning methods will also be discussed.

Electron beam welding is a specialized process critical to advanced manufacturing applications across many
sectors of importance to the US economy and science including nuclear, aerospace, defense, semi-conductors,

superconducting magnets, and accelerators. Electron beam welding is predominately used to manufacture
niobium superconducting accelerators. The DOE has a long history of researching and developing electron
beam welding technologies [29, 30, 31, 32, 33]. A comprehensive review of the process and its history is
provided by Patterson et al. [34]. The considerations and challenges of implementing this critical technology
for copper accelerator manufacturing are discussed in the following section.

B.3.1 State-of-the-Art Welding of High-Gradient Structures
When considering which processes may be best suited for joining copper in accelerator applications both the
weldability of the material and the requirements of the application must be considered. Accelerator
performance is impacted by microstructure, material purity, geometric tolerances and surface cleanliness
especially on the interior surface of the RF structure. Joining processes suitable for completing long-
continuous seams will be compared. Solid state processes such as friction stir welding are well suited to work
with face-centered-cubic and soft materials like copper. They require a high load perpendicular to the joint,
with a high risk deforming the internal RF geometry. In addition, the underbead may have burs or other defects
unacceptable for the application, and the process can have limitations with respect to geometry, tool access,
and post-weld machining requirements. Friction stir welding has many advantages, including minimal
oxidation and ability to join thick-section components, and has been used with great economical success for
spent nuclear fuel canisters made from copper [35].
While successful for welding low thermal conductivity metals such as steel, tungsten inert gas (TIG) and metal
inert gas (MIG) arc welding processes encounter difficulty when trying to maintain a stable weld pool in high
conductivity metals like copper. Welds made from these processes can result in high distortion of the
components from shrinkage and thermal gradients as well as significant oxidation. High power density laser
beam processes can overcome the limitations encountered by arc processes but the high reflectivity of copper
at standard laser wavelengths (1µm or 10µm) results in less than 2% of the laser power being absorbed in the
solid state. The absorption of the laser dramatically increases once liquid is formed and increases again once a
keyhole vapor column is formed in the liquid. These dramatic fluctuations in power absorption result in
instability in laser welding. In contrast, over 90% of electron beam power is absorbed by copper. While the
high conductivity of copper makes welding copper challenging, the electron beam process undoubtedly
produces the highest quality welds. Significant progress on welding copper using highly absorbing green and
blue lasers is being driven by electric mobility applications such as electric vehicles and merits attention in
coming years [36]. In 2024, Trumpf consigned a TruDisk 2021 2kW green laser systems to EWI-Ohio for use
on research and development projects, specifically focusing on motor and power electronics[37].
While there has been limited development on EBW of high gradient accelerating structures, CERN has a long
history of EBW of copper RF structures for both conventional and Nb thin-film coatings applications, with

Brazing uses a braze alloy to bond sections of an accelerator. A hydrogen brazing process uses a back purge of
hydrogen as a fluxing agent, reducing oxides and removing hydrocarbon contamination from the surface of the
copper. While the vacuum environment of electron beam welding inhibits oxidation, it does not have the
beneficial cleaning of the high temperature hydrogen or vacuum environment. The brazing process requires
600°C to 1000°C temperatures sustained for more than 1 hour, which significantly alters the copper
microstructure. The electron beam process limits alteration of microstructure to within millimeters of the joint
centerline and maintains a consistent chemistry with no use of braze alloy. Brazing often depends on capillary
forces controlled by geometry and surface conditions for the braze alloy to flow and fill a joint. In contrast
electron beam welding requires tight geometric tolerances but advanced control such as beam oscillation can
accommodate joint fit-up issues.

B.3.2 Equipment Considerations
Internal vs External Electron Beam Gun
There are two major configurations of electron beam guns: internally mounted on a motion control system
(Figure 17), and externally mounted in a fixed position (Figure 18). The internal gun is limited to low voltage
but the external gun can be either low or high accelerating voltage. The internal gun is also limited in size,
whereas the external gun has no limit to column length. The longer column length enables integration of more
focusing lenses and advanced electro-optical controls for beam manipulation. The smaller size of the internal
gun also limits the additional vacuum that can be added to the column thus increasing its risk for
contamination of the gun which can lead to defects in the welds. The major benefit of the internal gun is

High Voltage vs Low Voltage Electron Beam Gun
High accelerating voltage guns (>60kV) have smaller focus diameters and longer focal distances than low
accelerating voltage guns. High voltage systems can also generate greater net power with greater weld
penetration. The smaller focus diameter is not necessarily beneficial for most joint types on copper
accelerators, unless an advanced beam manipulation is used. The longer focal distance has three major
benefits: longer depth of focus which increases the allowable position tolerance, joint access, and reduced
contamination from materials vaporizing off the weld pool. The latter is an issue in internal guns, which can
cause the tungsten emitter to short circuit via an arc event. This type of failure can cause significant damage to
the welder as well as part being welded; however, with proper preventative maintenance and a skilled operator
and engineering team, this can be avoided. The higher voltage gun is able to generate much deeper weld
penetration than the low voltage gun, but both are able to fabricate all welds needed in a copper accelerator.
High voltage welders are generally more expensive than low voltage systems because of the increase power
supply costs, more complex electrical isolation needs within the gun as well as thicker- chamber walls to shield
process-generated x-rays. A high power, high voltage gun can be used for complex beam manipulation which

Sensing and Monitoring Capabilities
Process monitoring equipment that can be used in electron beam welding includes in-situ measurements (e.g.
beam measurement) that occur before welding, and in-operando measurements (e.g. optical or thermal imaging)
that occur during welding. Electron beam processes operate in a vacuum, which can damage or decrease the
effectiveness of equipment through pressure differentials, lack of convective cooling, or vapor deposition. In-
addition, the x-rays produced during the process can damage or decrease the effectiveness of sensors. This
requires custom modifications of sensors to operate with electron beam welding.

Some vendors have a well-established process development protocol that it has integrated with an advanced
qualification approach which is graphically represented in Figure 30. This protocol is currently used to develop
the wire-fed electron beam additive manufacture (WEBAM) components but can also be used for welding
processes. In-situ measurement with beam characterization equipment enables automated measurement,
tracking, and calibration of the electron beam properties (e.g. power, profile, diameter, focus). There are several
models of beam characterization equipment available on the market. A fully computerized numerical control
(CNC) system enables an automatic control of all relevant parameters for all the mechanical drives, and the
electro-magnetic operations of the electron beam. It also provides the collection of this data leading to a
repeatable, high quality welds and documentation of the production process.

Vacuum Pump Types
Vacuum is a significant consideration in EBW costs as it influences both the capital costs of the system through
the pumps used, as well as the operational expenses of the system via time lost to pump down, i.e. cycle time.
Minimizing the time needed to reach operating vacuum weld vacuum is a significant consideration, and estimates
put the utiliziation time (welding time) of EBW at roughly 10% due to vacuum pump down times.
EBW systems are composed of 2 vacuum systems for rough and high vacuum operation and includes the
associated valves for switching between processes. A roughing pump skid such as a wet rotary vane pump or
lubricant-free ‘dry’ scroll pump is used to achieve the pressure level required for the high vacuum pumps to
operate. High vacuum pumps are generally one of three types: diffusion pumps, cryo pumps, and turbo pumps.
Turbo pumps are generally used on small chambers as well as the gun itself, and quickly become
uneconomical for large pumping speeds, which can often reach 10,000 L/s. Diffusion pumps are most
commonly found on large volume chambers and require a high-quality silicon-based oil to avoid hydrocarbons
contamination in the vacuum chamber and weld via backstreaming. Specialized diffusion pump oils such as
Santovac 5 have been successfully used to weld SRF cavities with minimal contamination. Since cryopumps
are dry, they provide cleanest vacuum (ie no backstraeming hydrocarbons) as well as very high pump speeds,
but are generally costly and complex to maintain. Furthermore maintaining an ultrahigh vacuum during
electron beam welding is not practical due to the challenges of performing a bakeout, as well as the intrinsic
vaporization that occurs from the weld pool which limits vacuum levels to the 10-4 to 10-6 Torr range. Added
vacuum components such as Roots blower or cold traps can also assist in reducing pumpdown time.
The vacuum valves found on an EBW system can be expensive, especially the large diameter gate valves
associated with diffusion or cryopumps. Most EBW systems include a gate valve between the chamber volume
and the gun volume, allowing for rapid turn-around of parts by not venting the gun volume. The cost and
maintenance of the valves, as well as the manually operated chamber doors, requires detailed maintenance
protocols.

and iterative problem solving to complete which expands non-weld time and reduces profitability.

The design team and fabrication team both need expertise in specifics of working with electron beam welding.
The physics and materials science of how this process functions is drastically different from traditional arc
welding processes, and as such subject-specific training is needed. Without this training, the team involved
will at best be operating inefficiently, at worst ineffectively, and will have major issues with scaling production

EH&S
The major safety concerns related to electron beam welding include high voltage electricity, heavy equipment
and robotics, vacuum environment, and x-rays. Training on the dangers of each of these factors is required for
safe design with and operation of the EBW equipment. The vacuum chamber is designed to contain the x-rays
and the electron beam gun does not operate with the chamber open so this reduces potential safety concerns.
Heavy equipment and robotics safety training are no different than standard trainings; the main variation of
these systems from standard robotic or motions systems is the requirement that they are effectively shielded so
that the EMF does not interfere with the beam, and that they are designed to operate in the convective cooling
absent vacuum environment. High voltage electricity has particular dangers than need specific training to
understand so that safe operation can be achieved.
Maintenance and OEM Support
The basic function of an electron beam welding system is similar to a scanning electron microscope (SEM).
The complex electromagnetic systems that make up the beam column require highly specific training to
maintain and repair. A major difference between EBW and SEM is that and EBW system have much higher
power and also generate vapor. The associated power management system for EBW requires additional subject
matter expertise to maintain and repair. The vacuum system is another technology that requires highly specific
technical expertise for maintenance and repair. In addition to these core components of the EBW system, the
CNC controls, sensors, and motion control systems all require advanced training to ensure effective
maintenance and repair. Due to the very broad technical expertise required to maintain and repair this
equipment, rapid EBW OEM support is critical. When comparing EBW technologies world leaders include the
Pro-Beam Group, Sciaky Inc., and the Global Beam Technologies Group (GBT) which owns both the US
company PTR, and the German company Steigerwald Strahltechnik GmbH. All of these companies have major
customer positions in the USA working across industry, the Department of Defense, and the Department of
Energy. Support is particularly important in the case of a catastrophic failure where diagnostics and
replacement components are needed immediately, and any delay would lead to loss of business and potential
bankruptcy. Repair and maintenance costs are commonly between $30-100k and sometimes can have 2 months
of lead time, so relationships with OEMs are very important. Due to these costs and lead times it is important
to invest in having personnel who are trained to perform the repairs and multiple machines form the same
OEM.

Economics of an Electron Beam Welding Job Shop
Due to the very high capital investment required for electron beam welding systems and the required support
equipment, businesses need to ensure steady flow of work or risk going bankrupt. Due to being 50 to 300 times
the cost of an arc welder, the economics of an electron beam welding job shop are drastically different than an
arc welding job shop. These very difficult financial headwinds incline electron beam welding job shops
towards long term contracts and relationships, and away from short term high-engineering cost piecemeal
work. A private non-OEM job shop needs to build a catalogue of qualified welds that have a good work flow
(i.e high weld time to non-weld time). This ensures long-term relationships with government and private
entities to manufacture and repair current products and develop new products for similar long-term
manufacture and repair contracts. Proximity to relevant customers is beneficial to building these relationships.
Due to the high capital cost of electron beam welding equipment and supporting facilities, job shops have their
equipment running near capacity. Accommodating development or one-off jobs requires significant
engineering investment without repeat of the same job and as such, there is little economic drive for a shop to
prioritize this kind of high mix/low volume work compared to low mix/high volume work with clear revenue
projection.
Job shops also hesitate to take large contracts (e.g. 15days/month) that will result in neglecting or turning away
work from their long-term customers. There is also a major concern related to past performance of requested
jobs where the customers significantly overestimated their ability to produce parts for welding. Past customers
have entered contracts with job shops then only met 50% of the promised production and eventually fell to 0%.
These changes can be due to market conditions (down turn in aircraft engine demands) as well as
governmental policy changes (reduction in defense spending). If the contract is large, this could mean serious
financial problems for the jobs shop, and is a reason that it is preferred to spread the revenue generation across
multiple projects and customers. In terms of spreading revenue generation, 6 days/month of welding is
considered a large contract and an agreed upon contingency if the number of days was not met would be
prudent. Furthermore, qualification may be needed on multiple machines in case issues were encountered.
When approaching large contracts, it is suggested that a cost and materials first article development effort be
completed before providing a detailed quote for series production. Sometimes the rough order of magnitude
quotes can include a do not exceed clause, but the more mature the product and process is, the more accurate
the quote can be. A job shop can operate on 1- or 2-part production with the caveat that the long-term customer
and the job shop have a mutual understanding of the final product and timeline for production. These low and
high production runs cannot sustain a business if the job shop is waiting for engineering decisions from
customer; weld time is critical and if a machine is sitting idle, the shop is losing money. A series production of
30-40 parts would be considered a big run, with a specific example being nuclear applications that often
require a run of 20 pieces.
Following prior facility models, the DOE prefers to purchase finished copper accelerators from industry.
Currently there is no vendor with all of the required technology in-house required to manufacture a finished
EBW copper accelerator. RadiaBeam Technologies has developed in-house capabilities and a network of
partners for production of brazed copper accelerating structures utilizing a combination of internal capabilities
and external vacuum brazing service providers. A significant portion of this infrastructure could be adapted to
production of copper EBW accelerating structure but significant development and equipment would be needed

to meet the needs of a 100 structure production run. The current DOE model is that the private industry should
bear the risk of the investment and amortize the cost of investing in facilities over 10 years. Both DOD and
DOE primes have discussed this cost-share framework for equipment and facilities with job shops. The major
concern is which party owns the risk and the lack of guarantee of funding over the period of amortization. In
fact, contracts are seldom guaranteed for 1 year: and previous US accelerator companies went bankrupt when
they invested in the required equipment for manufacturing accelerators and the contract was then awarded to a
foreign entity. A similar issue was encountered with a job shop investing in manufacturing accelerators for a
DOE National Lab. The job shop invested in the internal forming, machining, pre-weld etch and welding
equipment and developed an external partnership for heavy etching, electropolishing and clean room assembly.
The National Lab customer would not accept having dependency on external Lab so the contract was not
completed at a loss for the job shop. Fortunately, the job shop properly budgeted and planned for this
possibility and the loss of business was not fatal to the company.
With the potential risk and the evidence of past bad faith relationships, it is unlikely private industry would
make the significant investment for copper accelerator manufacturing via electron beam welding. Because of
the high risk of ownership, the DOE could purchase and own the equipment for the private company with a
agreed upon buy-back with the OEM if the venture did not work economically. While this distributes the risk
between DOE and private company, it can become a complex accounting practice. Even with this agreement in
place, a private company would need to significantly invest in install costs, engineering time, project
management, and other factors so would be hesitant to take on the risk without guarantee of funding for a
specific number of years.
These risks are demonstrate why large companies such as General Atomics are well suited for short-term
projects with rapid ramp up periods. They have the critical mass of employees needed for rapid reassignment
to make a project successful, as well as the cashflow and facilities that can be rapidly repurposed, and they
have the lineage of know how required to complete these complex engineering projects. Even with these
immense resources, a large company such as General Atomics are reluctant to take on the risk of these capital
and personnel investments, as failures of prior large scale projects such as the Superconducting Super Collider
demonstrate macro-economics as well as politics can end even the largest projects.
With equipment such as electron beam welding, there is a potential to reduce risk by serving multiple
industries. This may not be possible if there are concerns of cross contamination between materials. The clean
room and electron beam welding facilities may be able to support normal conducting, superconducting, and
medical applications. Welding could also be performed for nuclear components, pressure vessels, and other
scientific devices including waveguides, gyrotron components, and klystron components. Zanon is an example
of an Italian company that started as and maintains a major pressure vessel division but has successfully
transitioned that capability to SRF niobium cavity production. Conversely, Babcock and Wilcox is a US
pressure vessel manufacturer best known for their nuclear reactor fabrication capabilities that attempted to
enter the SRF cavity production market but lost out on large contracts to European competitors during the
1980s.[66]
However, the success of the SRF venture depended on international funding from countries across the world.
Without even the DOE guaranteeing funding for a domestic accelerator facility, there would be little to no
chance that a facility could operate let alone compete with international manufacturers.
Access to Electron Beam Welding Job Shop and Potential Alternatives: When comparing potential job shop

high mix/low volume components. Engineers are examining a redesign of certain HFIR sub-systems to TIG

B.4 High Power RF testing/conditioning
In order to minimize the burden on facility staff, perform immediate and rigorous feedback testing on
manufacturing quality and improve device acceptance throughput, it would be prudent to also consider the
requirements for high power testing and qualification of the AS. High gradient AS require more time than
those with conventional gradients to condition up to operating power levels. In addition, the process must be
carefully performed in order to ensure no permanent damage is imparted upon the structures. A typical
processing methodology will start with short pulses while gradually increasing the power levels while
maintaining a threshold breakdown rate (BDR) and vacuum level. Once a target peak power level is achieved
at a particular pulse length, the power is dropped back down, pulse length increased and power ramped up,
again with the same progress criteria. Due to the advanced nature of modern RF power control systems, this
process can be automated and ran with minimal operator intervention. This later point is important from a
labor cost perspective as conditioning a high gradient structure can take hundreds of millions of pulses to
accomplish and routinely will encompass durations of weeks[80].
For high power RF testing, the linac is assembled into a test stand and connected to the RF drive train, cooling
water supply, and mechanical supports. At this point, RF conditioning can begin. Short pulses of low-power
RF are coupled to the accelerating structure at low repetition rate. The repetition rate, pulse length and power
levels are systematically increased, while maintaining the vacuum below a certain safe threshold, until all
parameters reach their maximum values. This process can take several hours to several days depending on the
condition of the system.
Several examples of world class high power RF testing facilities exist worldwide including the SLAC ASTA
test facility, the CERN Xbox facilities, KEK Nextef and in more recent times, the INFN TEX, LANL CERF-
NM and RadiaBeam facilities although the later two operate at C-Band.
The SLAC ASTA test facility (Figure 37) consists of a XL-4 klystron-based RF source capable of providing
up to 60 MW of RF power with variable pulse widths and repletion rate to the structure/component under test.
The structure are vacuum isolated from the rest of the system using high power ceramic-based RF windows.
The setup ensures that the system is tolerant to high reflected powers and can accept cavities with a wide range
of coupling beta without putting an undue stress on the RF power source. Typical interlocks monitoring
reflected power, water flow, temperature and vacuum provide protection.

 • Ability to vary pulse width, repetition rate and power level to an arbitrary number (true asynchronous

appropriate criteria the whole conditioning process can also be automated to significantly reduce the amount of
human interaction needed during the conditioning process. By keeping the breakdown rate (BDR) low, chances
of damaging the structure can be greatly reduced. Operating parameters that tend to have high BDR can be easily
identified and can be actively avoided when operating the system.

B.4.1   CAPEX
Captial Expenditures associated with standing up a high power test stand are significant. A single C-Band RF
power klystron capable of 50MW peak powers and the associated modulator will cost approximately $1.5M.
The ancillary equipment required to operate and control the system is approximately $0.5M of off the shelf
components which includes controls racks, power meters, chillers and various personal and machine protection
system hardware. Unfortunately, a key cost driver and technical development required for this test stand is the
development of reliable key high power RF components such as windows, isolators, directional couplers and
loads. Many of these items are not commercially available or have not been productized. Although RadiaBeam
in collaboration with SLAC through the GRIT program, has manufactured many high power directional
couplers and loads for their internal needs, these items are not available on the open market. Some
development has also been undertaken for 50MW RF windows and is still in progress at the time of the
writing, but have not been matured and fully high power tested. Finally isolators to handle 50MW power levels
are still in development, with 20MW version from Microwave Techniques currently representing the state of
the art for modest 2KW average power capacities. If the development of these C-band components can
progress and be commercially available when such a high power test stand is required, the costs can be modest
and in the range of $0.3M per station, however in the current state, it is expected to require approximately $1M
of further developmental investment. Further, a very specialized and robust control system built on a PLC
backbone and capable of safe automated conditioning is expected to require approximately $1.5M of
developmental effort. Finally, another large expense will involve the construction of testing bunkers to manage
the significant high energy radiation emitting from the structures during conditioning. Foundation
reinforcements and bunker construction is expected to total approximately $1M fully permitted while facility
infrastructure expenses will amount to another $1M for facility water, heat exchangers, power, climate control
and control room buildout. It is expected that at a rate of conditioning one structure per month, two power
stations and associated equipment will be required with many spares on hand to minimize schedule risk.
Included in the spares list would be another full RF power station. This will result in a total of roughly ~$12M
for the high power testing facility required.

B.4.2 Personnel
Staffing of a high power test facility will likely require highly trained personnel with advanced degrees to
oversee the day to day operations, results interpretations and be on-call in the case of system malfunctions and
trouble shooting. Two Senior Scientists and two junior scientists are expected for these roles and can also
service the needs of other RF based duties such as cold testing. 4 trained technical staff for system hookup and
preparation and exchanging, along with infrastructure maintenance are envisioned. Additionally 1 dedicated
operator per shift for 24 hour operation is expected although human factors may suggest that two would be
better. All in, the staffing levels are not expected to be high in this facility and should be shared if possible
with other departments. Although the average wages would be higher, due to the lower levels a monthly
expense of ~$250k including OH/GA/benefits seems reasonable. If administrative, IT and facilities
maintenance staff are required due to a stand alone facility, this could add another $50-60k per month.

B.4.3 ES&H
Environmental, Safety and Health consideration for this facility will primarily revolve around radiation safety
monitoring and compliance requirements. Requirements for shielding, access barriers, dose monitoring and
record keeping at the facility and individual level, establishment of procedures and training and active
interlock systems are well known and important to adhere to. High voltage and currents, along with heavy
lifting and material handling dangers will exist within this facility and extensive training, interlocks, lockout
tagout and safety equipment will be required. Further processes for removing and storing potentially activated
materials from within the bunkers will also be required to ensure staff and public safety.
