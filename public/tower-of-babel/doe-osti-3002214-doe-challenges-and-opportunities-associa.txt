 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Fusion energy represents a transformative opportunity to deliver a safe, plentiful, and carbon-free source
of reliable primary power. In recent years, fusion research and development have accelerated
significantly, particularly within the US, driven by decades of foundational public investment. Notably, in
2024 the US Department of Energy (DOE) established a comprehensive Fusion Energy Strategy aimed at
collaborating with industry partners to enable the deployment of fusion power plants and grid integration
by the 2030s. This strategy is chiefly implemented through the DOE Office of Science (SC) Fusion
Energy Sciences program.

This project was initiated to identify potential approaches for the Office of Environment, Safety, and
Health (NA-ESH-12) within DOE’s National Nuclear Security Administration to begin engagement with
the fusion community on future accountable material needs. The goal of the project is to inform and
influence the supply of and demand for accountable nuclear materials as fusion energy is developed and
commercialized. NA-ESH-12 must proactively engage with the fusion community regarding the
production and management of accountable nuclear materials. Given the complexity and scale of
materials required for research, pilot projects, and eventual commercial reactors, early coordination is
vital. The project’s objective is to provide insights that will shape the supply and demand landscape for
critical nuclear materials, ensuring that DOE is prepared to effectively support fusion energy development
and commercialization.

In FY 2025, an initial limited review was conducted to identify the status of the fusion energy
community’s progress toward full-scale energy production and the need for accountable nuclear material.
This included communications with SC, NA-ESH-12, Savannah River National Laboratory, and Oak
Ridge National Laboratory, and attending the Rutgers University–sponsored Supply Chain Workshop:
Scaling the Fusion Industry and the International Atomic Energy Agency’s Ninth DEMO Programme
Workshop.

The review to date indicates that the amounts of tritium, lithium-6, and deuterium required by the fusion
industry will be dependent on fuel type, breeding technology, blankets, and R&D improvements. One
concern is that the commercial sector does not have a sufficient supply chain to meet the demand for
development and commercialization for fusion energy production. The supply and demand estimates for
these materials should be routinely reviewed as fusion technologies mature.

 1. BACKGROUND

Fusion can potentially provide a safe, abundant, zero-carbon-emitting source of reliable primary energy.
Over the past decade, the fusion energy research and development landscape has evolved significantly,
especially in the US. Building on decades of public investments in fusion science and technology (S&T),
major advances are being achieved domestically and globally by public- and private-sector entities. In
2024, the US Department of Energy (DOE) developed a Fusion Energy Strategy to work with industry to
support the deployment of fusion energy and its supply to the grid by the mid-to-late 2030s (Figure 1).

 Figure 1. Timeline for demonstration and deployment of fusion energy.

The strategy includes participation by the DOE organizations shown in Figure 2 as well as public- and
private-sector entities [DOE 2024].

 Figure 2. DOE equities with representation on the Fusion Crosscut Team.

Even before fusion energy is commercialized, significant quantities of accountable nuclear materials will
be needed to support R&D and technology demonstration facilities. The scale-up of the production of
these materials is so significant that years of advanced planning is needed to ensure that materials are
available to support the commercialization of the technology. Understanding the consumption,
production, and material interactions during R&D, pilot plant, and full-scale operations will be essential
to managing accountable material inventories in the major streams in a full fusion nuclear environment.
The goal of this project is to identify potential approaches for the Office of Environment, Safety, and

Health (NA-ESH-12) within DOE’s National Nuclear Security Administration (NNSA) to begin
engagement with the fusion community on future accountable material needs.

The goal of the project is to inform and influence the supply of and demand for accountable nuclear
materials as fusion energy is developed and commercialized. The scope of the project includes the
following:

 Develop an initial assessment of the accountable materials needed to support fusion development and
 implementation.

 Identify any potential effects on DOE demand and supply for these materials.

 Assess the international supply of these materials.

 Identify potential approaches for NA-ESH-12 to use in engaging with the fusion community.

 Provide NA-ESH-12 with contact information for initiating engagement with the fusion community
 to consider DOE Order 410.2 requirements in a full fusion environment.

The approach for accomplishing these goals is to begin engaging with the fusion energy community
through direct communications with organizations within DOE that participate in fusion energy programs
and through workshops and conferences to engage the private sector and international communities. This
report summarizes the information gained in FY 2025 through communications with DOE Office of
Science (SC), DOE/NNSA NA-ESH-12, Savannah River National Laboratory, and Oak Ridge National
Laboratory and through attending the Rutgers University–sponsored Supply Chain Workshop: Scaling the
Fusion Industry and the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) Ninth DEMO Programme
Workshop.

 2. STATUS OF FUSION NUCLEAR ENERGY DEVELOPMENT EFFORTS

Recent global fusion industry surveys indicate that at least 45 companies in 14 countries (including the
US, Canada, UK, Japan, EU, and China) are working to commercialize fusion energy and invested in
the range of $7B to $10B in the development and deployment of fusion energy [DOE 2024; Fusion
Industry Association]. A list of investors is provided in Appendix A, and company profiles are provided
in the Fusion Industry Association’s 2024 report. Of these companies, 56% are located in the US, and
approximately 80% of private sector investment is in US fusion companies. Approximately 60% of the
companies that responded projected the same timeline as DOE for fusion plants to be delivering
electricity to the grid. Eight companies have developed contracts with DOE for implementation of pilot
plants under the Milestone-Based Fusion Development Program.

Although several fuel sources are being developed for production of fusion energy, the majority of the
companies (65%) are developing deuterium–tritium (D-T) fuel sources. The roles that tritium, deuterium,
and lithium play in the fusion reaction are shown in Figure 3 [Pearson 2022].

Although a recognized need exists for tritium, deuterium, and lithium-6 to meet the demands of this
fusion technology, much of the fusion community’s near-term focus is on systems designs and
construction of demonstration and pilot plants. The Supply Chain Workshop: Scaling the Fusion Industry
focused primarily on developing partnerships between companies deploying systems and companies
providing construction materials. However, the Fusion Industry Association’s 2024 survey of the global
fusion industry indicated that tritium self-sufficiency is the second greatest major challenge between now
and 2030. After 2030, it is projected to become the greatest major challenge along with full life cycle
issues such as waste management.

 3. ACCOUNTABLE NUCLEAR MATERIAL NEEDS

Fusion uses light isotopes as its fuel, such as deuterium and tritium (isotopes of hydrogen), helium-3, and
lithium-6. Because tritium is not naturally available in meaningful quantities, sustainable commercial
fusion energy based on deuterium and tritium (D-T) requires generating (i.e., “breeding”) tritium from
lithium-6, which is therefore also required for D-T fusion plants. Although each D-T fusion plant is
envisioned to breed tritium continuously to sustain its own tritium supply, each plant also requires a
certain amount of startup tritium and lithium-6. A sustainable supply of helium-3 would be required for
fusion approaches using deuterium and helium-3 [DOE 2024].

In 2023, SC, through its Fusion Energy Sciences (FES) program, completed an initial high-level
assessment of the potential demand for enriched light isotopes at the demo and deployment scale-up
phases (e.g., up to 10% of the US electricity market). The DOE strategy is to collaborate with partners to
ensure viable supplies of fusion fuel isotopes, including domestic sources, by the mid-to-late 2030s to
support potential fusion deployment scale-up per the expected timeline for deployment of fusion energy
[DOE 2024]. Several other organizations, primarily researchers and potential feed stock suppliers, are

also estimating the amount of accountable nuclear material that will be needed to support the fusion
industry. Examples are given in the following sections.

3.1 TRITIUM

Significant quantities of tritium will be needed to support fusion R&D and technology demonstration
facilities. In this phase of development, facilities are not expected to produce the tritium needed. In a
commercial fusion plant, the plant will produce the tritium needed to fuel the reactor, so tritium will not
be needed from outside sources after the fusion energy industry reliably adds tritium to the market.

Current inventory and DOE production will meet DOE national security needs [DOE 2021], but DOE
tritium production/inventories are not expected to be able to meet the fusion demand.

Essentially all commercially available tritium is produced as a by-product in Canada deuterium uranium
(CANDU) fission reactors. Estimates of the availability of tritium to meet the fusion energy demand vary.
In 2022, Kyoto Fusioneering models indicated that fusion demand is likely to begin to deplete the
available stockpile as early as 2035 [Pearson 2018]. Beyond 2050, tritium is unlikely to be produced by
CANDU (end of life), so supply is highly uncertain [Pearson 2022]. This assessment is in general
agreement with a 2022 article in Science that noted that several ongoing research efforts were focused on
the tritium supply issue [Clery 2022]

Recent information indicates that Canada is extending reactor operations for the next 20–40 years through
refurbishments and is exploring opportunities for new, larger reactors, which could extend the supply of
tritium to well beyond 2057 [Suppiah 2025].

3.2 LITHIUM

Enriched lithium-6 is used in the fusion reactor blanket to produce tritium to fuel the fusion reaction.
Significant quantities of enriched lithium-6 will continue to be needed when fusion plants are
commercialized. A breeding blanket for a fusion pilot plant will likely require enriched lithium-6 in
quantities on the order of tens of tonnes [Pearson 2022]. Current available inventories and production
capabilities for enriched lithium-6 are not projected to meet the future fusion demand.

The column exchange (COLEX) process is the only historically proven way to produce lithium-6 at scale.
It is expensive, energy intensive, and environmentally damaging. Production by DOE ended in 1963. The
process is now banned under the Minamata treaty (ban on new use of large quantities of mercury for
industrial purposes). The existing DOE inventory will not be available for commercial use [Pearson
2022]. Russia and China produce the current supply of lithium-6, but they use most of it for their own
needs [Power Technology 2025].

The fusion community is beginning to look for methods for enriching lithium. US vendors include
Hexium in Texas using atomic vapor laser isotope separation (AVLIS) and Molten Salt Solutions in New
Mexico using a proprietary process [Power Technology 2025; Molten Salt Solutions 2024].

3.3 DEUTERIUM/HEAVY WATER

Deuterium supply has been less of a concern within the fusion community than supplies of tritium and
lithium-6 because of deuterium’s current commercial availability.

DOE no longer produces heavy water, and the present inventory is being retained for DOE defense
programs [DOE 2021]. Estimates indicate that non-defense requirements could be met by procuring

 4

heavy water on the international market because restrictions on international suppliers would not apply to
non-weapons-related users such as the fusion industry. Canada and India, which rely on heavy water for
nuclear power plants (NPPs) for electricity, make the most heavy water [Ragheb 2021]. Canada is also
partnering with Kyoto Fusioneering to support the deuterium needs of the D-T fusion system it is
pursuing. Canada has historically produced heavy water for commercial applications and has the
capability to produce deuterium. Other vendors also exist in the supply chain; however, production may
need to be scaled up to meet the growing need.

Other countries with heavy water production facilities include Argentina, Iran, Romania, and Russia
[Ragheb 2021]

 4. ISSUES TO BE CONSIDERED

4.1 SUPPLY AND DEMAND

The fusion industry’s demand for accountable nuclear materials will depend on the technologies used by
the implementing companies.

The international nature of fusion commercialization must also be considered when engaging with the
fusion community. Research and development facilities and pilot plants will be developed in several
countries. Key suppliers of accountable nuclear materials to support fusion are expected to be outside the
US. Disparities exist between US accountability practices and international practices.

4.2 NONPROLIFERATION AND SAFEGUARDS

Fusion plants are expected to pose a lower nuclear proliferation risk than nuclear fission reactors. Fusion
plants do not use any special nuclear material used in nuclear weapons. However, D-T fusion plants and
the high-energy neutrons they produce will have the potential to breed plutonium from natural uranium or
uranium-233 from thorium. In addition, D-T fusion plants will include significant inventories of tritium
and lithium-6, which are used in advanced nuclear weapons.

A question has arisen as to the role of the Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons (NPT)
with respect to fusion. Because fusion does not use source material, special fissionable material, or the
fission nuclear fuel cycle, commercial fusion applications fall outside the current NPT regime. However,
consideration is being given to whether the nuclear nonproliferation regime should be extended to include
future fusion plants [Hua 2022]). The DOE and NNSA are supporting the path toward an appropriate
nonproliferation approach in collaboration with the US fusion industry and other stakeholders
[DOE 2024].

4.3 WASTE MANAGEMENT

Because the generation of high-energy neutrons that is inherent to fusion reactions, especially for D-T
fusion systems and, to a lesser extent, for other fusion fuel cycles, fusion will produce activated and
tritiated waste. Fortunately, these wastes are not anticipated to require long-term geological storage.
Nevertheless, R&D is needed to minimize waste and develop cost-effective waste separation, recycling,
and disposal pathways. [DOE 2024].

In the absence of official standards and guidelines for nuclear fusion plants, fusion designers have
adopted, as far as possible, well-established standards for fission-based NPPs. Fusion waste is
significantly different from fission NPP waste; that is, the quantity of fusion waste is much larger.
However, it mostly comprises low-level waste (LLW). Notably, the waste does not contain many long-

 5

lived isotopes; it mainly contains tritium and other activation isotopes, but no transuranic elements. An
important benefit of fusion employing reduced-activation materials is the lower demand for decay heat
removal and overall rapid radioactivity decay. The dominant fusion wastes are primarily composed of
structural materials. The fusion industry should be able to avoid creating high-level waste, but the volume
of fusion LLW will be significant, both in terms of pure volume and volume per unit of electricity
produced [Gonzalez de Vicente 2022].

The annual Waste Management Symposium has a session dedicated to management of radioactive waste
from fusion and small modular reactors.

 5. PATH FORWARD

The amounts of tritium, lithium-6, and deuterium required by the fusion industry will be dependent on
fuel type, breeding technology, blankets, and R&D improvements. The supply and demand estimates for
these materials must be routinely reviewed as fusion technologies mature.

Ways to engage with the fusion industry include, but are not limited to, interactions with other DOE
agencies, attending workshops and conferences such as those coordinated by the IAEA, and interactions
with private sector companies through organizations such as the Fusion Industry Association. For
example, SC, through its FES program, is developing a new national fusion science and technology
roadmap that will be released soon. The annual Waste Management Symposium has a session dedicated
to management of radioactive waste from fusion and small modular reactors. The 2024 Supply Chain
Workshop: Scaling the Fusion Industry was hosted by the Rutgers Business School; future workshops are
being planned. Other examples include the DOE Tritium Focus Group, IAEA’s DEMO Programme
Workshop, the International Conference on Tritium Science and Technology, and International
Symposium on Fusion Nuclear Technology.

During the R&D and technology demonstration phases, DOE could contribute accountable materials to
advance fusion power development. Over the long term, the potential exists for DOE to obtain
accountable materials from fusion suppliers to support DOE needs. The potential effect on DOE
management of accountable materials warrants NA-ESH-12’s continued engagement with the fusion
energy community.
