Full genome sequencing, evolutionary dynamics, and pathogenicity evaluation of chicken infectious anemia virus with emphasis on Upper Egypt reveals genetic variability linked to vaccinal strains
Authors: Eman Abd Elmenum Shosha, Ibrahim Eldaghayes, Ali Mahmoud Zanaty, Mohammed A. Gamaleldin, Mervat Masoud Mohamed, A. N. Gamal Maha, Doha Abd Alrahman Ahmed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Despite intensive vaccination efforts, Chicken Infectious Anemia Virus (CIAV) remains a formidable threat to the Egyptian poultry industry, primarily through the emergence of vaccine-escape variants. While most studies have focused on Northern Egypt, there is a critical knowledge gap regarding the viral landscape in the Southern provinces. This study provides the most extensive surveillance to date, spanning 10 diverse Egyptian governorates with a strategic focus on Upper Egypt (2024–2025). Out of 400 collected samples, TaqMan-based qPCR revealed a high prevalence of 30% (120/400). Five representative isolates were selected for whole-genome sequencing and molecular characterization. The isolates were classified as Genotype II by phylogenetic analysis, showing a notable genetic divergence (85–87% amino acid similarity) from traditional Genotype I vaccine strains. Notably, comprehensive recombination analysis identified isolate PX96998 as a mosaic variant, with breakpoints indicating intra-genotypic recombination between closely related genotype II lineages, highlighting complex evolutionary dynamics in the field. Furthermore, 3D homology modeling of the VP1 protein identified critical structural alterations in functional loop regions, potentially hindering the binding affinity of neutralizing antibodies and facilitating immune evasion. In an experimental setting, the genotype II isolate demonstrated hypervirulence in SPF chickens, causing severe development retardation and extensive atrophy of lymphoid organs (p < 0.0001). Pathognomonic pale, fatty bone marrow with a sharp drop in PCV% to 18 ± 0.58% indicated severe aplastic anemia. By the fifth week after infection, there was a complete humoral collapse with undetectable ELISA titers due to the huge systemic replication revealed by viral load measurement, which peaked in the thymus (9.50 ± 0.25 log₁₀) and bone marrow (9.00 ± 0.22 log₁₀). These results were supported by histopathology, which revealed intranuclear inclusion bodies and systemic lymphocyte depletion. These findings support the highly pathogenic genotype II CIAV’s dominance and aggressive evolution in Egypt. The emergence of recombinant variants with altered structural epitopes underscores the urgent need to replace outdated vaccination plans with genotype-matched immunogens in order to lessen the severe consequences on Egypt’s poultry industry.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12985-026-03257-4.

Introduction

The poultry industry in Egypt faces a continuous challenge from immunosuppressive pathogens, with chicken infectious anemia virus (CIAV) remaining at the forefront of these threats. As a major poultry pathogen, CIAV induces a state of severe immunopathology by targeting the primary lymphoid and hematopoietic tissues, leading to a cascade of economic losses due to secondary infections and compromised vaccine efficacy [1, 2]. According to the current International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses (ICTV) classification system, CIAV is taxonomically classified under the realm Floreoviria, kingdom Shotokuvirae, phylum Commensaviricota, class Cardeaviricetes, order Sanitavirales, family Anelloviridae, genus Gyrovirus, and species Gyrovirus chicken anemia [3]. The virus possesses a single-stranded, closed circular DNA genome of approximately 2.3 kb [3]. The three mains partially overlapping open reading frames (ORFs) in the CAV genome are called VP1, VP2, and VP3. Their nucleotide sequence lengths are 1350, 651, and 366, respectively. VP1 (51.6 KDa), VP2 (211 KDa), and VP3 (13.6 KDa) are three viral proteins with varying molecular weights that are encoded by these three overlapping ORFs. The primary structural protein of the virus is called VP1, the scaffolding protein is called VP2, and the non-structural protein called apoptin is called VP3 [4, 5]. Neutralizing antibodies are induced by the protective proteins VP1 and VP2 [6]. Since the VP1 exhibits the greatest nucleotide diversity, it is typically employed for molecular research and genetic characterization of CAV [3–6].

Phylogenetic study based on the VP1 gene has identified three genetically distinct genotypes of CAV (I, II, and III). CAV isolates from various regions of the world have shown only slight differences in expressed amino acid sequences [3]. Previous investigations had only found a single serotype [7] until a possible serotype 2 strain (strain CAV-7) from the United States emerged [8]. Affected birds may exhibit clinical or subclinical symptoms, with young chicks under four weeks being the most susceptible. Key manifestations include increased mortality, slowed growth, and severe anemia, alongside pathognomonic lesions such as thymus and bone marrow atrophy, subcutaneous hemorrhages, and hemorrhagic syndrome commonly known as Blue Wing Disease [3, 6, 9]. At the cellular level, the virus targets lymphoid cells, inducing apoptosis triggered by VP3 expression. This subsequent lymphocyte depletion leads to significant immunosuppression, making the birds more vulnerable to secondary bacterial and viral infections [3]. The virus primarily targets hematopoietic cells in the bone marrow and T lymphoblasts within the thymus. This pathogenesis results in significant clinical impact among susceptible young chicks, with morbidity and mortality rates potentially climbing to 55% and 80%, respectively [2–9].

CAV exhibits a specific tropism, replicating in embryonated eggs and lymphoid cell lines but not in primary chicken cells like kidney cells or fibroblasts [10]. While MDCC-MSB1 suspension cultures are commonly employed for viral growth in molecular research [9], the labor-intensive nature of traditional isolation has shifted the focus toward rapid diagnostics. Consequently, PCR and hybridization are now routinely used to identify viral DNA directly from infected tissues [3–6]. Epidemiologically, being non-enveloped, CIAV is remarkably resistant to common disinfectants and environmental stressors, necessitating the use of potent, specialized disinfection protocols [11]. Furthermore, the epidemiology of the virus is complicated by its dual transmission routes: horizontal and vertical. Vertical transmission via the eggs of infected breeders is especially critical, as it leads to early-age infections and peak mortality in young chicks. Consequently, vaccinating breeder flocks is of paramount importance to provide progeny with protective maternal antibodies [6].

The chronological detection of CAV in Egypt began in 1990 in a commercial chicken farm. Historically, Egyptian poultry populations were primarily affected by Genotype I strains. Field isolates shared high genetic similarity with the CUX-1 vaccine strain, despite the presence of specific mutations associated with pathogenicity [3, 12]. However, the epidemiological landscape has recently shifted; [6, 13] have highlighted the emergence and rising dominance of Genotype II variants. This evolutionary transition suggests that the traditional Genotype I-based vaccines, such as Cux-1, may offer inadequate cross-protection. The genetic drift observed in the VP1 capsid protein’s hypervariable regions likely facilitates immune evasion, resulting in the clinical breakthroughs currently seen in Egyptian flocks.

Crucially, while several studies have characterized CIAV in Northern Egypt, there is a profound lack of molecular and pathogenic data concerning the strains circulating in Upper Egypt. This regional data scarcity has critically masked an ongoing, unmonitored viral evolution driven by distinct field conditions, environmental stressors, and unique localized vaccination pressures. It is highly hypothesized that these regional factors have accelerated the selection of distinct genetic variants capable of escaping standard vaccine-induced immunity, thereby directly explaining the recurrent vaccination failures observed in the field. The present study was therefore conducted to bridge this knowledge gap, providing the first comprehensive investigation into the prevalence, genetic evolution, and pathogenicity of CIAV in this geographically distinct and high-density poultry sector.

Materials and methods

The present study was conducted in strict compliance with the established guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals. All experimental procedures received prior approval from the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the Faculty of Veterinary Medicine, Assiut University (Approval No. 02–3-10–2025). To alleviate pain and distress, birds were humanely anesthetized with a ketamine/xylazine mixture (25/5 mg/kg BW) before invasive procedures. Upon study completion, birds were humanely euthanized via cervical dislocation under deep general anesthesia, adhering to international animal welfare protocols.

Epidemiological investigation and clinical characterization of suspected CIAV cases in egyptian poultry flocks

A comprehensive epidemiological survey was conducted over a 20-month period, from April 2024 to November 2025. A total of 400 clinical tissue samples, including the bursa of Fabricius, thymus, spleen, and liver, were collected from 80 different poultry flocks. The sampling strategy encompassed both commercial poultry farms and backyard (household) rearing systems across 10 Egyptian governorates, providing wide geographical coverage from Upper to Lower Egypt, including Assiut, Sohag, Aswan, New Valley, Qena, Minya, Fayoum, Cairo, Kafr El-Sheikh, and Matrouh (Table S1, Supplementary Material; Fig. 1). The study evaluated various poultry production sectors, including breeders, commercial layers, and broilers, with bird ages ranging between 2 and 27 weeks. Vaccination protocols differed across sectors: breeders and commercial layers received a single dose of the live-attenuated Cux-1 strain via drinking water (administered at 13–15 weeks), while broilers and backyard flocks remained unvaccinated. Clinically, the investigated birds showed signs indicative of CIAV, such as generalized weakness, pale combs, and stunted growth, with field mortality fluctuating between 4 and 15%. Tissue processing and virus preparation

The collected pooled tissues (bursa of Fabricius, thymus, spleen, and liver) from each flock were processed immediately under aseptic conditions. Approximately 1 g of pooled tissues was finely minced and homogenized in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.2) to prepare a 20% (w/v) suspension. To maintain viral stability and prevent bacterial or fungal contamination, the suspension was supplemented with an antibiotic–antimycotic mixture containing 100 U/mL penicillin, 100 µg/mL streptomycin, and 0.25 µg/mL amphotericin B. The homogenates were subjected to three freeze–thaw cycles to enhance cell lysis and maximize viral release. Subsequently, the suspensions were clarified by centrifugation at 4,000 × g for 15 min at 4°C. The supernatants were carefully collected, aliquoted into sterile cryovials, and stored at − 80°C until further use for DNA extraction and molecular characterization [3]. The clarified tissue homogenate was molecularly pre-screened and confirmed to be negative for major avian immunosuppressive viruses, including IBDV, MDV, and ALV, to eliminate the possibility of co-infection.

Molecular detection and screening of CIAV by real-time PCR (qPCR)

Following the manufacturer’s guidelines, the FAVORGEN Viral Nucleic Acid Extraction Kit (FAVORGEN Biotech Corp., Cat. No. FAVNK002) was employed to extract total viral DNA from 5 mg of each tissue sample. A mechanical homogenizer (PRO 200, Pro Scientific, USA) was used to process the specimens at 4°C prior to the extraction procedure to maintain sample integrity. The purified DNA was eluted in 50 µL of elution buffer and stored at − 20°C until further analysis. Detection and quantification of the CIAV genome were performed using a highly sensitive TaqMan-based real-time PCR assay as previously described by [14]. The qPCR assay utilized a specific set of primers and a probe designed to target a conserved region of the CAV genome. This set included the forward primer CAV1F (5′-CTC AAG CGA CTT CGA CGA AGA-3′), the reverse primer CAV1R (5′-AGC CTC ACA CTA TAC GTA CCG-3′), and a TaqMan probe (CAV-probe: 5′-FAM-ACA GCG GTA TCG TAG ACG AG-TAMRA-3′) labeled with FAM and TAMRA. The qPCR was performed in a total volume of 20 µL per reaction. Each mixture contained 10 µL of 2 × QuantiNova Probe Master Mix (Qiagen, Germany), 0.5 µL of each primer (forward and reverse), 0.25 µL of the specific probe, and 5 µL of template DNA. Amplification was performed using an Applied Biosystems real-time PCR system (USA) under the following cycling conditions: initial denaturation at 95°C for 2 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95°C for 10 s and annealing/extension at 60°C for 30 s. Following the initial screening, samples with the lowest Ct values were utilized for further characterization, including full-genome sequencing and analysis of the partial VP2 gene region.

Conventional PCR and full-genome and partial VP2 gene amplification

Following the initial screening, 16 representative positive samples, selected based on the lowest cycle threshold (Ct) values, were subjected to conventional PCR for amplification of the full circular genome (n = 5) and the partial VP2 gene (n = 10). To amplify the full circular genome (approximately 2.3 kb), three sets of specific overlapping primers (F1/R1, F2/R2, and F3/R3) were used according to the methodology described by [3]. In parallel, the partial VP2 gene was amplified using a specific primer pair targeting a conserved region, as previously described by [15]. All primers used in this study are listed in Table 1. PCR amplification was conducted in a final volume of 25 µL, utilizing 12.5 µL of EmeraldAmp® GT PCR Master Mix (Takara Bio, Shiga, Japan). The reaction included 1 µL (10 pmol/µL) of each forward and reverse primer, 5 µL of template DNA, and 5.5 µL of nuclease-free water. Thermal cycling was performed on a T100™ Thermal Cycler (Bio-Rad, USA) with the following parameters: an initial 5-min denaturation at 95°C, followed by 35 cycles of 95°C for 30 s (denaturation), 56°C for 45 s (annealing), and 72°C for 90 s (extension), ending with a 10-min final extension at 72°C. The specificity of PCR products was confirmed by electrophoresis on a 1.5% (w/v) agarose gel. Purification, sequencing, and phylogenetic analysis of CIAV isolates

The products for the full-genome fragments (850, 900, and 750 bp) and the partial VP2 segments (~ 600 bp) were electrophoresed on 1% low-melting-point agarose gels. Target bands were excised and purified using the QIAquick® Gel Extraction Kit (Cat. No. 28704, Qiagen, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Purified amplicons were eluted in 30 µL of elution buffer and stored at -20 °C until sequencing. The 16 representative isolates underwent bi-directional cycle sequencing utilizing an ABI PRISM® 3730XL Analyzer. This process incorporated BigDye™ Terminator Cycle Sequencing Kits and AmpliTaq DNA polymerase within an MJ Research PTC-225 Peltier Thermal Cycler (Applied Biosystems, USA). To achieve optimal sequence coverage and accuracy, the original PCR primers were employed for sequencing. The resulting sequences were initially screened for identity using the NCBI BLAST tool. Subsequently, Lasergene (DNAStar, version 10) and BioEdit v7.2 [16] were used for the assembly, editing, and curation of the raw sequence data. Sequence alignment at both nucleotide and deduced amino acid levels was performed using the Clustal W module [17]. Evolutionary relationships were evaluated by constructing phylogenetic trees using MEGA X software [17]. The neighbor-Joining method was applied in conjunction with the Tamura-Nei substitution model, and branch reliability was validated through 1000 bootstrap replicates. Upon completion of the analysis, the sequences were deposited in the NCBI GenBank database, and unique accession numbers were obtained for each isolate.

Recombination analysis

To identify putative recombination events in the sequences of the CAV isolates, RDP v.4.97 software was used to assess the presence of such recombination events using the analytical method of BootScan, and 3Seq. A p-value (less than or equal to 0.05) was employed to validate the detection of recombination regions. In addition, upon detection of recombination regions, the evolutionary relationships among the identified parental sequences associated with each recombination event were investigated. The identified putative recombination events were subsequently validated and analysed using SimPlot software [18, 19].

VP1 protein structure modeling

To further characterize the structural features of VP1 protein in the CIAV isolates, a three-dimensional structure (3D) of the VP1 protein with the reference strain of SD24, was generated using the homology-modelling pipeline via the automatic mode of SWISS-MODEL (Swiss Institute of Bioinformatics; https://swissmodel.expasy.org/ accessed on 01 October 2026). Consequently, the resulting structure for current isolates was detected using PyMOL software (Version 1.7.4, LLC; http://www.pymol.org/) [20–22]. Model quality was evaluated using the Global Model Quality Estimation (GMQE) and QMEAN scoring systems provided by SWISS-MODEL. Structural visualization and superimposition analyses were performed using PyMOL version 1.7.4.

In Vivo pathogenicity assessment of egyptian CIAV strains

To evaluate the virulence of CIAV in previously unstudied regions, the Assiut isolate (GenBank Accession Nos. PX596994 and PX597000) was used for in vivo trials. This represents the first pathogenic assessment of an isolate from Upper Egypt, offering a critical contrast to existing literature, which has concentrated exclusively on viral strains recovered from northern Egyptian provinces. A total of 100 one-day-old Specific-Pathogen-Free (SPF) chicks (Kom Osheem SPF project, Fayoum, Egypt) were randomly divided into two groups (n = 50 per group). All experimental procedures were conducted under strict biosecurity measures within a high-standard, properly controlled isolation facility equipped with strictly monitored negative-pressure isolators. The chicks were housed under hygienic conditions with filtered air supply and restricted personnel access to entirely prevent any horizontal transmission or external contamination. To rigorously confirm the SPF and CIAV-negative status prior to challenge, random meconium and embryonic tissue samples from the same hatch were molecularly screened using the TaqMan quantitative qPCR assay. Furthermore, serological testing using a commercial ELISA kit (IDEXX Laboratories, Inc, USA) verified the absence of maternally derived anti-CIAV antibodies, ensuring zero baseline contamination. Birds in the challenged group were administered an intramuscular (I/M) injection of 0.1 mL, containing 4 × 103 viral DNA copies. Conversely, the control group was injected with an identical volume of sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Birds were monitored daily for 35 days post-infection for clinical signs and mortality. Daily clinical scoring was performed based on a standardized 4-point scale evaluating depression, feather ruffling, and paleness of the comb/wattle: Grade 0 (Normal); Grade 1 (Mild depression and slight feather ruffling); Grade 2 (Moderate depression, visible paleness, and reluctance to move); and Grade 3 (Severe depression, severe paleness, or death). Individual body weights (BWs) were recorded weekly (7, 14, 21, 28, and 35 dpi). To assess lymphoid atrophy and growth retardation, three chickens from each group were randomly euthanized at each of the five weekly time points (totaling 15 birds per group for destructive sampling). The remaining 35 birds per group served as the baseline population to ensure a robust and statistically sound sample size (n = 15 remaining birds at the final timelines) for computing longitudinal parameters like weekly Packed Cell Volume (PCV%), antibody kinetics, and cumulative mortality rates while strictly balancing animal welfare and ethical constraints. The relative organ-to-body weight ratios (organ index) for the thymus, spleen, and bursa of Fabricius were calculated using the standard formula according to [23]. Heparinized blood samples were collected weekly to determine packed cell volume (PCV%) using the microhematocrit method and hemoglobin (Hb) levels. Birds with PCV < 25% were considered anemic, according to [24]. Serum specimens were analyzed for the presence of anti-CIAV antibodies utilizing the BioChek CIAV Antibody ELISA kit (BioChek B.V., Netherlands) according to the manufacturer’s protocols [25].

Viral load dynamics in lymphoid organs, bone marrow, and the liver were tracked using qPCR, with results expressed in terms of mean log10 DNA copies/g [26]. Representative tissues (the thymus, bone marrow, and spleen) were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin, processed routinely, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Sections were examined microscopically for characteristic lesions, including lymphoid depletion and bone marrow aplasia [1].

Standard curve-based absolute qPCR for viral DNA

To evaluate viral load dynamics and tissue tropism during the pathogenicity trial, absolute CIAV DNA quantification was performed on the liver and lymphoid tissues (spleen, thymus, and bone marrow), according to [26]. Total DNA was isolated from 5 mg of each tissue specimen. The AriaMx qPCR System (Agilent Technologies, USA) was utilized for quantification. Absolute quantification was achieved using a standard curve generated from ten-fold serial dilutions of a certified CIAV vaccine with a known genomic concentration. The resulting standard curve demonstrated high linearity (R2 = 0.994) and a slope of -3.077, yielding an amplification efficiency of 111.33% and a y-intercept of 12.81. The assay exhibited high diagnostic sensitivity, with a limit of detection (LoD) of 101 viral DNA copies per reaction and a limit of quantification (LoQ) of 102 viral DNA copies per reaction. To maintain analytical standardization across varying samples, the final viral concentrations were normalized and expressed as log10 DNA copies per milligram of tissue (log10 copies/mg tissue), calculated based on the initial tissue weight (5 mg) utilized for total DNA extraction. All reactions were performed in triplicate, and viral concentrations were reported as log10 DNA copies per µL of template DNA. Results

Clinical and macroscopic findings

Clinical surveillance conducted across ten Egyptian governorates (2024–2025) identified a disease profile closely linked to host age. Acute Chicken Infectious Anemia (CIA) was the primary finding in young broilers (2–3 weeks old), manifesting as profound depression, growth retardation, and characteristic pallor of the skin and combs. Mortality in these cohorts varied between 3 and 10%, whereas infections in older breeder and layer flocks (22–27 weeks) remained largely subclinical. A post-mortem examination of 400 specimens revealed systemic lesions, characterized by marked atrophy of the bursa of Fabricius and thymus. The bone marrow exhibited a distinct yellowish-white discoloration, signifying hematopoietic failure. Furthermore, petechial hemorrhages in the proventriculus and ecchymoses on the wings (blue wing disease) were prevalent, often accompanied by hepatomegaly and splenomegaly.

Molecular screening and DNA amplification

Among the 400 tissue specimens collected, 120 (30%) were identified as positive for CIAV DNA via TaqMan-based qPCR screening. From these positive cases, sixteen representative isolates were randomly selected for further genetic characterization via conventional PCR. These included five isolates for full-genome amplification (~ 2.3 kb) and ten (10) isolates for partial VP2 gene amplification (~ 600 bp). Notably, agarose gel electrophoresis confirmed the successful amplification of all 16 isolates, yielding clear bands at the expected molecular sizes with high primer specificity. No amplification was detected in the negative controls. The resulting high-quality amplicons were purified and subjected to bi-directional sequencing. This was performed to characterize their molecular profiles and evaluate the evolutionary dynamics of the circulating strains.

Mutation and homology modeling analyses

A comparative analysis of amino acid sequence (~ 588 amino acids “aa”) demonstrated that our Egyptian CIAV isolates (n = 5) exhibited a significant degree of similarity with previously reported Egyptian genotype II strains, especially in comparison to the reference strain (CAV/2015/Egypt; MG827098). These sequences showed conserved regions with different amino acid substitutions in the relevant regions. In the N-terminal region (aa 1–130), all isolates exhibited a singular alanine (A) substitution at position 16, a change also noted in multiple global reference strains. In the hypervariable region of VP1 (aa 116–126) (Figure S1, Supplementary Materials), isolates PX596996-PX596998 displayed a serine (S) substitution, and PX596998 also demonstrated a leucine (L) substitution at position 126. In the central region (aa 220–240), several mutations were identified, such as isoleucine (I) substitutions in PX596994 and PX596998.

Also, variations were found in the motif that spans positions 235–245. These changes include proline (P), serine (S), leucine (L), and histidine/glycine (H/G) [P/S/L/HG pattern], which is a sign of genotype III strains among all isolates. All five isolates had a substitution in methionine (M) at position 254, which is what was found in previously documented Egyptian strains. Moreover, PX596998 displayed a distinctive (L) substitution at position 283. Three different mutations were identified in the central region of VP1 (aa 250–320) (Figure S1, Supplementary Materials), All Egyptian field isolates showed a substitution to lysine (K) at position 296 compared to the reference sequence. At position 301, a substitution to glutamic acid (E) was observed. The isolates also had a substitution to methionine (M) at position 306. In the VP2 region at aa 467, a cysteine (C) substitution was currently detected in PX596998, while the other isolates showed no changes. Importantly, the recorded isolates did not show any amino acid substitutions in the hypervariable region of VP1 (aa 139–151). In the C-terminal region, all isolates also showed an isoleucine (I) substitution at position 533.

Hematological profile and bone marrow suppression

The hematological analysis at the peak of infection revealed severe pancytopenia (Table S4, supplementary materials; Fig. 7). The infected group exhibited a profound drop in hematocrit (PCV%) to 18 ± 0.58%, falling significantly below the normal threshold (> 25%) and the control group (32.33 ± 0.88%; P < 0.001). This was accompanied by a significant reduction in RBC count (1.67 ± 0.09 × 10⁶/µL) and hemoglobin levels (7 ± 0.58 g/dL). Furthermore, a significant leukopenia and lymphopenia were recorded, alongside a compensatory increase in heterophils (45.67 ± 1.73%), confirming the virus’s destructive effect on the hematopoietic cells in the bone marrow.

Gross pathological lesions (In Vivo Trial)

Experimental infection with the Assiut Genotype II isolate induced characteristic pathognomonic lesions in SPF chicks (Figure S2, supplementary materials). The most prominent clinical hallmark was "blue-wing disease," presenting as severe cyanosis and subcutaneous hemorrhages on the wing tips. Systemic hemorrhages were consistently observed, including extensive petechiae and ecchymoses in the breast and thigh muscles, as well as severe mucosal hemorrhages at the proventriculus-gizzard junction. The post-mortem examination further revealed a profound impact on the hematopoietic and lymphoid systems. The bone marrow in the femurs was pale and fatty, replacing the normal deep red hematopoietic tissue. Severe atrophy of the lymphoid organs was a defining feature, with the thymus reduced to translucent remnants and marked involution of the Bursa of Fabricius. Additionally, the kidneys were enlarged and pale with visible urate deposition (nephrosis) in several acute cases.

Discussion

Chicken infectious anemia virus continues to be a formidable challenge to the poultry industry worldwide, primarily due to its profound immunosuppressive effects, which trigger vaccination failures and heighten susceptibility to co-infections [6, 11, 42]. In Egypt, particularly in the southern (Upper Egypt) governorates, the poultry industry relies heavily on intensive vaccination programs to mitigate the impact of immunosuppressive viruses. Despite the widespread implementation of these protocols, field outbreaks of chicken infectious anemia (CIA) continue to be reported, manifesting as significant economic losses and recurrent vaccination failures. Crucially, while several studies have characterized CIAV in Northern Egypt, there is a profound lack of molecular and pathogenic data concerning the strains circulating in Upper Egypt. The present study was therefore conducted to bridge this knowledge gap, providing the first comprehensive investigation into the prevalence, genetic evolution, and pathogenicity of CIAV in this geographically distinct and high-density poultry sector.

The present study reveals a high prevalence rate of 30% (120/400) via TaqMan-based qPCR across Upper Egyptian governorates. Interestingly, this prevalence is notably higher than the 23% we recently reported for the same period (2024–2025) in other Egyptian provinces [6]. This discrepancy suggests that CIAV may be circulating more intensively in the southern regions (Upper Egypt), potentially due to differences in environmental stressors, flock management practices, or the unique evolutionary dynamics of the strains localized in these governorates. Crucially, while previous research has extensively focused on Northern Egypt, this study represents the first comprehensive investigation into the molecular and pathogenic landscape of CIAV specifically in Upper Egypt. The recorded higher prevalence in this region, despite the concurrent vaccination efforts, underscores a critical "geographical gap" in vaccine efficacy and highlights the urgent need for regional-specific surveillance and the potential development of immunogens that match the highly prevalent genotype II strains identified in our study.

Whole-genome phylogenetic analysis identified four major CIAV genotypes (I–IV), each supported by higher bootstrap values. Specifically, the phylogenetic analysis based on complete genome sequencing demonstrated that five investigated CIAV isolates clustered tightly within genotype II. These isolates shared high nucleotide and amino acid similarities (97–98%) with previous CIAV Egyptian strains isolated in 2015–2016. This observation is consistent with earlier reports mentioned by [3, 15, 43, 44], confirming the continued dominance of genotype II in Egypt. Moreover, there is close genetic relatedness of current Egyptian CIAV isolates and those reported worldwide, including those from China, Australia, Malaysia, the USA, and Brazil, with nucleotide and amino acid similarities ranging from 96 to 98%. These findings are in agreement with previous global studies by [5, 20, 45, 46]. Recently, several commercial vaccines, including Del-Ros, Nobilis P4, and Cux-1, are widely used in breeder flocks to induce maternal antibodies that protect young chicks during their first two to three weeks of life [5, 46]. The phylogenetic analysis and amino acid analyses revealed that these field isolates were distinctly apparent from the vaccines’ strains (CAV 26P4/Netherland-2007), showing 85%–87% amino acid similarity. These previous observations also align with those findings recorded by [15, 47]. This divergence likely contributes to the CIAV infection occurrence in vaccinated flocks; these findings agree practically with studies reported by [3, 15]. Further studies are required to determine the vaccine efficacy against these recent isolates. In contrast, the mentioned isolates exhibited higher similarity (95–98%) to other CIAV vaccine-related strains, including CAV/Del-Ros, CAV Cuxhaven-1, and CAV vaccine 3711.

However, it is critical to interpret this genetic similarity with caution. While a high percentage of nucleotide identity is often observed, it does not provide definitive evidence of direct vaccine derivation or vaccine-derived evolution. Instead, this high similarity reflects a shared evolutionary ancestry or the continuous co-circulation of phylogenetically related ancestral strains within the region, rather than a direct transmission or reversion to virulence of commercial vaccine viruses. Finally, these promising findings confirm continuous circulation of genotype II of CIAV in Egyptian poultry flocks with various mutations and multiple evolutions, particularly within the VP1. Moreover, nucleotide and amino acid sequence analyses demonstrated a high similarity and strong genetic relatedness between these sequences (99%), suggesting the circulation of a single CIAV strain in chicken populations and a common ancestral source. Collectively, these results demonstrate that our current strains circulating in various Egyptian governorates are phylogenetically related to global strains and previously reported Egyptian ones, indicating substantial local circulation alongside broader intercontinental connectivity.

Regarding our available results, the Egyptian CIAV isolate analysis demonstrates an amino acid variation level. The high level of sequence conservation in the N-terminal region aligns with previous studies that suggest this domain is structurally limited and essential for viral integrity [48–50]. Commonly, the substitution at position ~ 16 is detected between global CIAV strains. The presence of leucine substitutions at position 126 in PX596998 may affect capsid shape and antigenic properties. These changes could influence how the host’s immune system recognises the virus and may aid in immune evasion, and these results came nearly in accordance with [51, 52]. The central region of VP1 (aa 220–240), which exhibited variability among the isolates, is under positive selection pressure [53–55]. The isoleucine substitutions and variations within the P/S/L/HG motif may indicate adaptive evolution driven by host immune responses, particularly in vaccinated poultry. The partial similarity of these motifs to those in genotype III strains suggests some convergent evolution. The conserved methionine substitution at position 254 has been previously noted in Egyptian and international strains and is not considered a major virulence factor, parallel to our results [56]. However, the additional leucine substitution in PX596998 may affect protein folding and stability.

Subsequently, the unique cysteine substitution identified in PX596998 is significant, as this region overlaps with VP2-associated functional domains. VP2 plays a crucial role in viral replication and proper VP1 folding. The introduction of a cysteine residue may change protein–protein interaction, potentially impacting the efficiency of viral replication [49, 57]. Concerning previous studies, these mutations occur outside the classic virulence-associated residues of VP1 (positions 75, 89, 125, 139, and 144), indicating that their role is related to structural adaptation. This region, known for its critical virulence-associated residues, was completely conserved compared to the reference strain. The isoleucine substitution in the C-terminal region (~ aa 540) seems to be a lineage-specific marker. Our subsequent results came in accordance with several recent CIAV strains [48, 55]. Additionally, this mutation analysis of these Egyptian isolates supports the ongoing evolution of genotype II CIAV strains circulating in Egypt. Among the examined isolates, PX596998 exhibited the most divergence, containing multiple unique substitutions across different functional regions. This isolate may be an emerging variant with potentially altered biological characteristics.

Genetic recombination significantly influenced virus evolution, playing a key role in generating and maintaining viral genetic diversity [58, 59]. Recombination analysis of the CIAV genotype II isolate PS96998 (Matrouh_Sample5) revealed high nucleotide similarity to both parental strains: CIAV strain 12-GD2018101 and CAV-EG-14. This high sequence identity suggests recombination occurred between closely related genotype II lineages, consistent with recent reports highlighting frequent intra-genotypic recombination in CIAV. Two recombination breakpoints were detected, reflecting the circular structure of the CIAV genome. Importantly, this configuration implies a mosaic genomic structure where the central region originates from one parental lineage, whereas the other regions are from the alternate lineage. This observation is also stated in CIAV recombination by [49–52, 56]. Furthermore, the close relationship between the parental strains and geographically linked isolates may indicate the co-circulation of multiple CIAV lineages in poultry. Such conditions facilitate the emergence of novel variants and promote their recombination. Similar mechanisms have been mentioned in recent CIAV studies from Asia and South America [49, 51, 59].

Regarding homology modelling, the structural features observed in this study align with the role of VP1 as the major capsid protein responsible for virion assembly and antigenicity. Notably, VP1 is the only structural protein forming the capsid of CIAV and is essential for inducing neutralising antibodies [60, 61]. The compact structure observed in this model supports its function in forming a stable icosahedral capsid; these results were consistent with previous structural and functional analyses [61]. The combination of structured (α-helical) and unstructured (loop) regions reflects a typical viral capsid design. The rigid domains provide structural integrity, and flexible loops mediate host interactions and immune evasion. This is particularly important for CIAV, where minor amino acid variations in VP1 significantly influence virulence and antigenicity [60, 62, 63]. Although genomic and phylogenetic analyses demonstrated a close evolutionary relationship between several Egyptian isolates and vaccine-derived strains, functional antigenic characterization was not performed. Future investigations, employing virus-neutralization assay, are warranted to evaluate the extent of immunological cross-reactivity and protective efficacy of currently used genotype I vaccines against the circulating genotype II Egyptian CIAV isolates.

The in vivo pathogenicity trial further underscored the "hypervirulent" nature of the Assiut Genotype II isolate. The manifestation of severe clinical symptoms and the pathognomonic pale, fatty bone marrow were quantitatively corroborated by a drastic decline in packed cell volume (PCV%), which fell to 18 ± 0.58%. This correlation underscores the profound haematological impact of the viral infection. This reduction is notably more pronounced than the 19.33% decline documented in our previous studies [6, 23, 64] and falls well below the established clinical anaemia threshold [65]. Such acute haematopoietic failure is directly linked to the exceptionally high viral titres identified in the bone marrow (9.00 ± 0.22 log₁₀) and thymus (9.50 ± 0.25 log₁₀), where CIAV aggressively targets erythroid and myeloid progenitor cells [64].

Furthermore, the experimental infection resulted in a "total humoral collapse", evidenced by the rapid decline of ELISA antibody titres to undetectable levels by the 4th and 5th weeks post-infection. This immunological exhaustion, which aligns with the patterns observed in our previous study [2, 6, 42], suggests that these emerging Egyptian genotype II variants create a persistent "immunological void". Such a state not only hinders the host’s ability to clear CIAV but also leaves the birds highly vulnerable to secondary opportunistic pathogens, explaining the high mortality and production losses observed in the dense poultry sectors of Upper Egypt.

The dynamics of viral shedding are critical for the horizontal transmission and endemic persistence of CIAV in poultry environments. In the present study, the high viral loads quantified by qPCR across multiple organs, peaking at 9.50 ± 0.25 log₁₀ in the thymus and 9.00 ± 0.22 log₁₀ in the bone marrow, suggest a massive systemic replication that facilitates prolonged viral shedding.

Consistent with our previous observations in [6], the high viral concentration in the lymphoid organs and digestive tract (evidenced by proventricular lesions) indicates that these genotype II strains are shed in high titres through faeces and feather dander. This extensive shedding, coupled with the virus’s well-known environmental stability, explains the rapid spread within flocks and the difficulty of decontamination in Upper Egyptian farms. Furthermore, the persistent high viral load detected in the bone marrow up to 21 dpi suggests that infected birds remain active "shedders" for an extended period, creating a continuous source of infection that can bypass maternally derived antibodies (MDA) in subsequent cycles. This "prolonged shedding profile" is a key factor in the economic impact of genotype II, as it ensures the maintenance of a high viral pressure in the environment, even in the presence of vaccination.

The macroscopic lesions observed in this study provide a clear visual confirmation of the high virulence of the Upper Egyptian CIAV genotype II isolate. The appearance of "blue-wing disease" (Fig. 2A) and extensive systemic haemorrhages in the skeletal muscles and proventriculus (Fig. 2B, C) are indicative of severe vascular damage and thrombocytopenia. These findings align with our recent report [6], suggesting that genotype II strains circulating in Egypt have a high tropism for vascular endothelial cells and haematopoietic tissues, leading to the clinical manifestation of the haemorrhagic syndrome. A pivotal diagnostic feature was the transformation of the bone marrow from its normal deep red colour to a pale, fatty, and yellowish appearance (Fig. 2D). This gross change is a direct consequence of bone marrow aplasia, where the virus destroys the haemocytoblasts. This finding is quantitatively supported by the profound drop in PCV% (18 ± 0.58%) recorded in our results. Consistent with [6, 23], the severity of bone marrow lesions in genotype II infections appears to be more aggressive than those typically associated with the classical genotype I strain, explaining the higher mortality and morbidity rates observed in the field.

The irreversible damage caused by emerging Egyptian genotype II variants is evidenced by systemic lymphoid exhaustion and the hallmark presence of intranuclear inclusion bodies [6, 66, 67]. Furthermore, the direct correlation between maximum viral titres and the development of severe tissue aplasia highlights the enhanced virulence and rapid replication dynamics that characterise these native strains. To mitigate the devastating effects of CIAV in Egypt, developing genotype-matched vaccines based on local isolates is crucial. This approach addresses the limitations of current genotype I-based programmes, particularly the risk of vaccine escape and suboptimal cross-protection. Additionally, the widespread use of Genotype I vaccines may be driving selective pressure that favours the dominance of Genotype II strains, which possess superior environmental stability and fitness in the local field. Such evolutionary shifts necessitate an immediate update to regional vaccination strategies.

Collectively, the scarcity of molecular, and epidemiological data from Upper Egypt has limited the characterization of locally circulating CIAV strains. The present study demonstrates that several Upper Egyptian isolates exhibit notable genetic divergence from vaccine-related strains, particularly within genomic regions associated with viral evolution. While the current data do not directly demonstrate reduced vaccine efficacy, these findings suggest a hypothesis that ongoing viral evolution may influence antigenic similarity between field and vaccine strains. Consequently, continuous molecular surveillance in Upper Egypt is essential not only for recording viral diversity but also for identifying emerging variants that warrant further evaluation through cross-neutralization and vaccine-challenge studies to determine their potential impact on vaccine-mediated protection.

Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive pathogenic and genetic characterization of CIAV genotype II strains circulating in commercial broiler flocks across various Egyptian governorates. Genetic detection through PCR and quantitative PCR confirmed the high viral prevalence, while complete genome sequencing of representative isolates revealed 97%–98% and 96%–98% nucleotide and amino acid similarities to reference Egyptian strains (2015–2016) and contemporary global isolates from different countries, respectively. Conversely, a lower genetic similarity (85%–87%) was observed with the commercial vaccine strain ‘CAV 26P4/Netherland-2007’. A major strength of this study is its integrated approach, combining clinical pathogenicity, molecular characterization, and phylogenetic analysis to provide novel genetic insights into the viral epidemiology of Upper Egypt, which has remained poorly investigated. While whole-genome characterization was focused on a selected representative subset of high-viral-load strains, which may limit absolute population-wide genetic representativity, the high sequence identity underscores a synchronized local evolution. These promising findings provide empirical evidence of the continuous circulation, active mutations, and ongoing evolution of genotype II variants in Egyptian poultry. Future investigations should focus on experimental vaccine-matching trials to update national prevention and enhance regional control strategies.
