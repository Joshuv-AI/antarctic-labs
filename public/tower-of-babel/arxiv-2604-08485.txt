Formalizing building-up constructions of self-dual codes through isotropic lines in Lean
Jae-Hyun Baek, Jon-Lark Kim

Abstract
The purpose of this paper is two-fold. First, we show that, after a specified form isometry, the two-coordinate reduction in the binary Hilbert-symbol realization of Chinburg and Zhang is inverse to Kim’s building-up construction, up to permutation equivalence. Second, for q ≡ 1 ( mod 4 ) q\equiv 1\pmod{4} , we develop a q q -ary analogue of this reduction-and-extension mechanism. The identity c 2 = − 1 c^{2}=-1 yields the isotropic line governing the split construction. For every fixed ordered pairing of the coordinates, we obtain a universal rank- r r boxed normal form, where r r is the dimension of the intersection with the product of these isotropic lines. Applications include optimal self-dual [ 6 , 3 , 4 ] [6,3,4] and [ 8 , 4 , 4 ] [8,4,4] codes over 𝔽 5 \mathbb{F}_{5} , optimal self-dual [ 8 , 4 , 5 ] [8,4,5] and [ 10 , 5 , 6 ] [10,5,6] codes over 𝔽 13 \mathbb{F}_{13} , and a self-dual [ 12 , 6 , 6 ] [12,6,6] code over 𝔽 13 \mathbb{F}_{13} . We also give an exact repeated boxed realization of self-dual [ 18 , 9 , 8 ] [18,9,8] and [ 20 , 10 , 10 ] [20,10,10] codes over 𝔽 13 \mathbb{F}_{13} , in which the split-boxed parent and its building-up child occur in one complete generator matrix. The algebraic core is formalized in Lean 4.

Introduction

Coding theory is the study of reliable communication. Its modern foundations
were laid by Shannon’s seminal paper
[
19
]
. Since then, many classes of
linear codes have been studied. In particular, the extended binary Hamming
code of length 8, the binary Golay code of length 24, and the ternary Golay
code of length 12 are self-dual, that is,

\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}^{\perp}
.
Thus the classification or construction of self-dual codes has been one of the most active research problems
[
17
]
.
Self-dual codes have wide connections with several mathematical areas including
t
-design theory
[
1
]
, group theory
[
5
]
, number theory
[
2
]
,
[
5
]
,
[
20
]
. Furthermore, they have applications in quantum error-correcting codes
[
3
]
.

Kim
[
8
]
introduced “building-up construction” which proved that all binary self-dual codes can be constructed in this way. This construction starts from a binary self-dual code of length
2n
and then adds a vector of length
2n
of odd weight to construct a binary self-dual code of length
2n+2
. Kim–Lee
[
11
]
,
[
10
]
generalized this method to construct self-dual codes over finite fields
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, where
q
is even or
q\equiv 1\pmod{4}
.
In practice, this is a principal method for constructing examples from a
split bilinear space. However, the formula for the new generator rows is
often presented in terms of an auxiliary vector whose inner product with
itself is
-1
. One of the central points of the present paper is that the
corresponding correction coordinates are naturally organized by an
isotropic line in a hyperbolic plane, and that the forward construction
admits a precise row-wise and family-wise characterization in those terms.

On the other hand, Kreck and Puppe
[
12
]
constructed binary
self-dual codes from the cohomology of three-manifolds. Chinburg and
Zhang
[
4
]
developed the arithmetic analogue for rings of

S
-integers. For a suitable set
S
of places of a number field, the image
of the global-to-local map in first étale cohomology is Lagrangian for
the sum of the local Hilbert pairings. Their Theorem 1.5 shows that, for

K=\mathbb{Q}
, every binary self-dual code of length at least four occurs up
to permutation equivalence. We use precisely this realization theorem and
its boxed reduction; no further étale-cohomological machinery is needed
below.

In this paper, we focus on
q\equiv 1\pmod{4}
. Over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, self-dual codes are maximal totally isotropic subspaces for the standard Euclidean bilinear form on
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2n}
. In this split case the elementary identity
c^{2}=-1
is the basic algebraic input behind both the classical building-up construction and the structural description of the ambient quadratic space: it produces an isotropic line in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2}
, identifies the Euclidean plane with a hyperbolic plane, and makes the norm form
x^{2}+y^{2}
split.

We establish that each generator row of the building-up admits the isotropic line decomposition
r_{i}=-y_{i}\cdot(1,c,0,\ldots,0)+(0,0,g_{i})
, where
(1,c)
spans an isotropic line in the hyperbolic plane. This decomposition provides a precise geometric interpretation of the correction terms in the building-up construction. We also give a constructive norm-form: once
c^{2}=-1
and
2\neq 0
, every
a\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
can be written explicitly as
a=x^{2}+y^{2}
, which in turn yields extension vectors of prescribed norm.

The odd-characteristic and binary inputs are distinct. For

q\equiv 1\pmod{4}
, the element
-1
is a square in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
, so
the Euclidean plane is hyperbolic: the vectors

h_{1}=(1,c),\qquad h_{2}=\frac{1}{2}(1,-c)

satisfy
h_{1}\cdot h_{1}=h_{2}\cdot h_{2}=0
and

h_{1}\cdot h_{2}=1
. In the binary theorem of
Chinburg–Zhang
[
4
]
, the arithmetic image is Lagrangian for
a cohomological pairing; selected orthonormal coordinates identify that
pairing with the Euclidean dot product. The coordinate plane deleted in the
resulting binary reduction has Gram matrix
I_{2}
and is non-alternating.

In the present paper, this distinction organizes the binary comparison, the
split
q
-ary construction, its universal normal form, and selected
applications over
\mathbb{F}_{5}
and
\mathbb{F}_{13}
.

Our main contributions are as follows.

(1)

In the binary case, we distinguish the cohomological pairing from the
Euclidean dot product by a specified form isometry. In the resulting
Euclidean coordinates, boxed reduction deletes a two-coordinate block with
Gram matrix
I_{2}
, the tail rows form a self-dual predecessor, and the
remaining coefficients satisfy Kim’s correction formula
(Theorem
3.4
).

(2)

In the
q
-ary case, we prove the adapted reduction and extension
theorem, a forward split boxed construction, and its reverse statement under
an explicit adapted-presentation hypothesis
(Theorems
3.9
–
3.13
).

(3)

For each fixed ordered coordinate pairing, every Euclidean self-dual
code over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
,
q\equiv 1\pmod{4}
, has the universal rank-
r
boxed
normal form of Theorem
3.15
, with

r=\dim(\mathcal{C}\cap U_{c})
.

(4)

The applications retain the explicit small-length results over

\mathbb{F}_{5}
and
\mathbb{F}_{13}
from the submitted manuscript and add one complete

[18,9,8]
and
[20,10,10]

The modular Lean 4 development formalizes the paper-facing algebraic

Preliminaries

We refer to
[
7
]
for basic concepts and facts and to
[
17
]
for self-dual codes.

Throughout the paper,
\mathbb{F}_{q}
denotes a finite field. We equip
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
with the Euclidean bilinear form

\langle u,v\rangle:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}v_{i}.

Here “Euclidean” refers to the coding-theoretic dot product, not to
positive definiteness. For
u,v\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
, their distance and the weight
of
u
are, respectively,

d(u,v):=\bigl|\{i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}:u_{i}\neq v_{i}\}\bigr|,\qquad\mathrm{wt}(u):=\bigl|\{i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}:u_{i}\neq 0\}\bigr|=d(u,0).

For a code
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
with at least two codewords, its
minimum distance is

d(\mathcal{C}):=\min\{d(u,v):u,v\in\mathcal{C},\ u\neq v\},

and, when
\mathcal{C}\neq\{0\}
, its minimum weight is

\mathrm{wt}(\mathcal{C}):=\min\{\mathrm{wt}(u):u\in\mathcal{C},\ u\neq 0\}.

Since
d(u,v)=\mathrm{wt}(u-v)
, the minimum distance of a nonzero linear code
equals the minimum weight of its nonzero codewords:

d(\mathcal{C})=\mathrm{wt}(\mathcal{C})
. For a linear code

\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
, we write

\mathcal{C}^{\perp}:=\{v\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}:\langle v,c\rangle=0\text{ for all }c\in\mathcal{C}\}.

For subspaces
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\subseteq V
, put

\mathcal{A}\cap\mathcal{B}:=\{v\in V:v\in\mathcal{A}\text{ and }v\in\mathcal{B}\}
. For a matrix
G
over
K
,

\langle G\rangle_{K}
denotes the code spanned by its rows.

Definition 2.1

(self-dual code)
.

A linear code
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
is
self-dual
if

\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}^{\perp}

[
16
]
.

Definition 2.2

(Lagrangian subspace)
.

Let
V
be a finite-dimensional vector space over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, equipped
with a nondegenerate symmetric bilinear form. A subspace
L\subseteq V

is called
Lagrangian

[
14
,
18
]
if
L=L^{\perp}
.

For a nondegenerate form,

\dim L^{\perp}=\dim V-\dim L
. Hence
L=L^{\perp}
if and only if

L
is totally isotropic and
2\dim L=\dim V
. In particular, a
bilinear space admitting a Lagrangian subspace is even-dimensional.
Applied to the standard Euclidean form, this says that

\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
is self-dual if and only if it is totally isotropic
and
2\dim\mathcal{C}=n
. Thus a self-dual code is precisely a Lagrangian
subspace of
(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
, and its length is even.

Definition 2.3

(Permutation equivalence)
.

Writing codewords as row vectors, two codes

\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{2}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
are

permutation equivalent
if there is a permutation
\sigma\in S_{n}

such that

\mathcal{C}_{2}=\mathcal{C}_{1}P_{\sigma},

where

is the permutation matrix associated with
\sigma

In the Lean notation below,

. Coordinate permutations are Euclidean

\langle uP_{\sigma},vP_{\sigma}\rangle=\langle u,v\rangle.

Consequently, permutation equivalence preserves Euclidean self-duality.
A general monomial transformation also permits independent nonzero
coordinate scalings; it need not preserve the Euclidean form or
self-duality. It is therefore not included in our use of “equivalence”
below. Over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
, permutation and monomial equivalence coincide because

\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\times}=\{1\}

Definition 2.4

(hyperbolic plane)
.

A hyperbolic plane
[
18
]
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
is a
2
-dimensional symmetric bilinear space with basis
\{e_{1},e_{2}\}
satisfying

\langle e_{1},e_{1}\rangle=\langle e_{2},e_{2}\rangle=0,\qquad\langle e_{1},e_{2}\rangle=1.

Such a basis
\{e_{1},e_{2}\}
is called a hyperbolic pair. Any
1
-dimensional totally isotropic subspace is called an isotropic line.

In even dimension and odd characteristic, a nondegenerate symmetric
bilinear space is called
split
if it is an orthogonal direct sum of
hyperbolic planes. Thus splitting is a property of the bilinear space, not
of the underlying finite field.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
q
be odd and let
c\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
satisfy
c^{2}=-1
. Then:

(i)

The element
c
has multiplicative order
4
.

(ii)

For every
k\geq 1
, the matrix equation
AA^{T}=-I_{k}
has the explicit solution
A=cI_{k}
.

(iii)

The binary quadratic form
x^{2}+y^{2}
splits as

x^{2}+y^{2}=(x-cy)(x+cy),

hence it represents every element of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.

Proof.

For (i), since
q
is odd,
-1\neq 1
. Thus
c^{4}=1
and

c^{2}\neq 1
, so
c
has order
4
.

For (ii), take
A=cI_{k}
. Then

AA^{T}=c^{2}I_{k}=-I_{k}
.

For (iii),

(x-cy)(x+cy)=x^{2}-c^{2}y^{2}=x^{2}+y^{2}.

Given
a\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
, take

x=\frac{1+a}{2},\qquad y=\frac{1-a}{2}c.

Then
x-cy=1
,
x+cy=a
, and consequently

x^{2}+y^{2}=a
.
∎

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
q
be odd and let
c\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
satisfy

. Then the vectors

form a hyperbolic basis of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2}
. Equivalently, the line spanned by
(1,c)
is isotropic and the Euclidean plane is split.

Proof.

Direct calculation gives

\langle e_{1},e_{1}\rangle=1+c^{2}=0,\qquad\langle e_{2},e_{2}\rangle=2^{-2}(1+c^{2})=0,\qquad\langle e_{1},e_{2}\rangle=2^{-1}(1-c^{2})=1.

If

, pairing successively with

and

gives

. Hence

\mathbb{F}_{q}(1,c)
is isotropic and the Euclidean plane is

∎

Building-Up Theory

3.1
Binary Case

In 2001, Kim
[

Theorem 3.1
.

(
[
8

Let
G_{0}
be a generator matrix of a binary self-dual code

\mathcal{C}_{0}
of length
2n

\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2n}

{\bf x}\cdot{\bf x}=1

G=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc!{\vrule width 1.2pt}c}1&0\hfil\lx@intercol\vrule width=&\mathbf{x}\\
\hrule height=1.2pt\cr y_{1}&y_{1}\hfil\lx@intercol\vrule width=&g_{1}\\
\vdots&\vdots\hfil\lx@intercol\vrule width=&\vdots\\
y_{n}&y_{n}\hfil\lx@intercol\vrule width=&g_{n}\end{array}\right]

generates a binary self-dual code
\mathcal{C}
of length
2n+2
.

Then the converse of Theorem

Theorem 3.2

(

At real places the local groups use Tate cohomology. The sum of the local

on

,

. Under their local

\iota_{E}:(W,B_{\rm coh})\xrightarrow{\sim}(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2n},\langle\ ,\ \rangle_{\rm E}).

Only after fixing

Theorem 3.4

(Euclidean realization and binary boxed reduction)
.

Let

, let
L\subset W
be the Chinburg–Zhang arithmetic image,
and suppose that

. Fix the form isometry

\iota_{E}
above and put
\mathcal{C}=\iota_{E}(L)
. Suppose that the coordinate
permutation furnishing the Chinburg–Zhang boxed representative, followed by
the indicated elementary row operation, gives

G^{\prime}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc!{\vrule width 1.2pt}c}1&0\hfil\lx@intercol\vrule width=&x\\
\hrule height=1.2pt\cr y_{1}&y_{1}\hfil\lx@intercol\vrule width=&g_{1}\\
\vdots&\vdots\hfil\lx@intercol\vrule width=&\vdots\\
y_{n-1}&y_{n-1}\hfil\lx@intercol\vrule width=&g_{n-1}\end{array}\right],\qquad x,g_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2n-2}.

Let

, and put

. Then:

(1)

, while

and the boxed

(2)

(3)

(4)

Consequently,

exactly. After restoring the

Proof.

Remark 3.7
.

Then the converse of the above theorem was also stated and proved in

Theorem 3.8
.

(
[

, Proposition 2.2]
)
Let

be a finite field with

. Any Euclidean self-dual code

over

Theorem 3.9

(Adapted split reduction and building-up extension over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
)
.

Let
q
be a prime power with
q\equiv 1\pmod{4}
, and let
K=\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
Fix
c\in K^{\times}
such that
c^{2}=-1
.
Let
W=K^{2n}
be equipped with the standard Euclidean inner product

\langle u,v\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{2n}u_{i}v_{i}.

Let
L\subset W
be a self-dual code, i.e.,
L=L^{\perp}
. Assume that,
after a permutation of coordinates and row operations,
L
admits a
generator matrix of the form

M=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc!{\vrule width 1.2pt}c}1&0\hfil\lx@intercol\vrule width=&x\\
\hrule height=1.2pt\cr-y_{1}&-cy_{1}\hfil\lx@intercol\vrule width=&g_{1}\\
\vdots&\vdots\hfil\lx@intercol\vrule width=&\vdots\\
-y_{n-1}&-cy_{n-1}\hfil\lx@intercol\vrule width=&g_{n-1}\end{array}\right],

where
x\in K^{2n-2}
and
g_{i}\in K^{2n-2}
for
1\leq i\leq n-1
.

Let
W_{0}=K^{2n-2}
and define
L_{0}:=\langle g_{1},\dots,g_{n-1}\rangle\subset W_{0}
.
Then the following statements hold:

(1)

Let

denote the first coordinate plane in the displayed
decomposition of

.
Then
W=E\perp W_{0}
in the displayed coordinates, and
E
is
isometric to a hyperbolic plane. This is an abstract change of basis in

E
; no coordinate change is applied to the displayed matrix
M
.

(2)

L_{0}
is self-dual in

.

(3)

One has

\langle x,x\rangle=-1,\qquad y_{i}=\langle x,g_{i}\rangle\quad(1\leq i\leq n-1).

(4)

Consequently,

so that

is obtained from

by a

-ary building-up construction.

(5)

Conversely, two reverse statements hold:

(5a)

Let

be self-dual, let

be a basis of

, and let

satisfy

then the row family generated by

is linearly independent and generates a self-dual code of length

.

(5b)

More generally, if one starts from any generating family of a self-dual parent, the same formula always produces a self-orthogonal child family, and that child is self-dual as soon as its row span has dimension

.

Proof.

The decomposition

, with

, is

has Gram matrix

. Since

, the

and

. Thus

is abstractly hyperbolic,
proving (1), while

remains written in the standard coordinate basis.

Write

gives pairwise orthogonality of these rows. Hence

The code

has dimension
n
, and the displayed

They are therefore linearly independent.
Suppose that

\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}g_{i}=0

\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}y_{i}=\left\langle x,\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}g_{i}\right\rangle=0,

and therefore

The independence of the rows of
M
implies

for every

. Thus

are linearly independent. Their span

is self-orthogonal and has dimension

, so

. This proves (2) and (3),
and substitution of the coefficient identity into the rows proves (4).

Conversely, let

be self-dual, put

\langle r_{0},r_{0}\rangle=1+\langle x,x\rangle=0,\quad\langle r_{0},r_{i}\rangle=-y_{i}+\langle x,g_{i}\rangle=0,

and

\langle r_{i},r_{j}\rangle=(1+c^{2})y_{i}y_{j}+\langle g_{i},g_{j}\rangle=0.

is a basis, projection onto the last

. Hence

is not in their span. The child therefore has dimension

and
is self-dual. For an arbitrary generating family, the same Gram calculation
and the stated half-dimension hypothesis give self-duality.
∎

3.3

The preceding adapted results do not apply directly to an arbitrary

Theorem 3.15

(Universal rank-
r
boxed normal form over

\mathbb{F}_{q}
)
.

Let
K=\mathbb{F}_{q}
, where
q\equiv 1\pmod{4}
, fix
c\in K^{\times}
with

c^{2}=-1
, and fix an ordered pairing
K^{2n}=(K^{2})^{n}
. For every
Euclidean self-dual code
\mathcal{C}\subseteq K^{2n}
, put

U_{c}=\bigoplus_{j=1}^{n}K(1,c),\qquad r=\dim(\mathcal{C}\cap U_{c}),\qquad k=n-r.

After a permutation of the
n
two-coordinate blocks and elementary row
operations,
\mathcal{C}
has a generator matrix

G_{r}(c;Q,\ell,D)=\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c}\bigl(Q_{ij}(1,c)+\delta_{ij}(0,1)\bigr)_{k\times k}&\bigl(\ell_{it}\bigr)_{k\times r}\\
\hline\cr\bigl(-(D\partial_{c}\ell_{j})_{s}(1,c)\bigr)_{r\times k}&\bigl(D_{st}(1,c)\bigr)_{r\times r}\end{array}\right],

(4)

where
1\leq i,j\leq k
,
1\leq s,t\leq r
,

\ell_{it}=(u_{it},v_{it})\in K^{2}
,

(\partial_{c}\ell_{i})_{t}=v_{it}-cu_{it},\qquad Q_{ii}=\frac{c}{2}\bigl(1+\ell_{i}\cdot\ell_{i}\bigr),

D\in M_{r}(K)
is nonsingular, and the remaining coefficients satisfy

c(Q_{ij}+Q_{ji})+\ell_{i}\cdot\ell_{j}=0\qquad(i\neq j).

(5)

The last
r
rows span
\mathcal{C}\cap U_{c}
. Conversely, every matrix
(
4
) with
\det D\neq 0
and
(
5
) has
k+r
independent, pairwise
orthogonal rows and generates a Euclidean self-dual code. Moreover,
retaining any subset of the first
k
rows together with the corresponding
block columns and all terminal rows and columns gives a self-dual code of
the same form with the same
D
.

Proof.

For
v=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n})\in(K^{2})^{n}
, write each block uniquely as

v_{j}=(\alpha_{j}(v),c\alpha_{j}(v)+\beta_{j}(v)),\qquad\beta_{j}(v)=v_{j,2}-cv_{j,1},

and define
\partial:\mathcal{C}\to K^{n}
by

\partial(v)=(\beta_{1}(v),\ldots,\beta_{n}(v))
. Since

\ker\partial=\mathcal{C}\cap U_{c}
, rank–nullity gives

\mathrm{rank}\partial=k
. Choose
k
coordinates on which

\operatorname{im}\partial
projects isomorphically, permute them to the
first
k
positions, and choose rows
g_{1},\ldots,g_{k}\in\mathcal{C}
whose defects
restrict there to the standard basis. Complete them by a basis

S_{1},\ldots,S_{r}
of
\ker\partial
. Applying
\partial
to a linear
relation first determines the coefficients of the
g_{i}
; independence of
the
S_{s}
then shows that these
n
rows form a basis of
\mathcal{C}
.

Let
Q_{ij}
be the first coordinate of the
j
-th pivot block of

g_{i}
, and write its terminal block-row as
\ell_{i}
. Thus its

j
-th pivot block is

Q_{ij}(1,c)+\delta_{ij}(0,1)
. Since every
S_{s}
lies in

\ker\partial
, write its pivot and terminal blocks as

A_{sj}(1,c)
and
D_{st}(1,c)
, respectively. The identity

(\alpha,c\alpha+\beta)\cdot(\gamma,c\gamma+\delta)=c(\alpha\delta+\beta\gamma)+\beta\delta

and the orthogonality of the chosen basis give

A_{si}+(D\partial_{c}\ell_{i})_{s}=0,\qquad\delta_{ij}+c(Q_{ij}+Q_{ji})+\ell_{i}\cdot\ell_{j}=0.

The first equation gives the lower-left block in
(
4
); the second gives
(
5
), and its diagonal part gives the stated
formula for
Q_{ii}
. It remains to prove that
D
is nonsingular.
Let
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{r})\in K^{r}
satisfy

\lambda D=0
. For every
i
, the first identity yields

(\lambda A)_{i}=\sum_{s=1}^{r}\lambda_{s}A_{si}=-\sum_{t=1}^{r}(\partial_{c}\ell_{i})_{t}\sum_{s=1}^{r}\lambda_{s}D_{st}=0.

Consequently, every pivot and terminal block of

\sum_{s}\lambda_{s}S_{s}
is zero. Since the
S_{s}
are linearly
independent,
\lambda=0
. Thus
\ker D=0
, and hence

\det D\neq 0
.

Conversely, suppose that
D
is nonsingular and that
(
5
) holds. Denote the first
k
rows of
(
4
) by
p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}
, put

q_{it}=(\partial_{c}\ell_{i})_{t}
, and retain the notation

S_{1},\ldots,S_{r}
for the terminal rows. Since

(1,c)\cdot(1,c)=0
, one has

S_{s}\cdot S_{u}=0,\qquad p_{i}\cdot S_{s}=c\left(A_{si}+\sum_{t=1}^{r}q_{it}D_{st}\right)=0.

Moreover,

p_{i}\cdot p_{j}=\begin{cases}1+2cQ_{ii}+\ell_{i}\cdot\ell_{i}=0,&i=j,\\
c(Q_{ij}+Q_{ji})+\ell_{i}\cdot\ell_{j}=0,&i\neq j.\end{cases}

Thus the displayed rows are pairwise orthogonal.

Suppose now that

\sum_{i=1}^{k}\xi_{i}p_{i}+\sum_{s=1}^{r}\mu_{s}S_{s}=0.

Applying
(x,y)\mapsto y-cx
to the
j
-th pivot block gives

\xi_{j}=0
, because the defect of that block is
\delta_{ij}
in

p_{i}
and zero in every
S_{s}
. Hence
\xi_{1}=\cdots=\xi_{k}=0
.
The first coordinates of the terminal blocks then give

0=\sum_{s=1}^{r}\mu_{s}D_{st}=(\mu D)_{t}\qquad(1\leq t\leq r).

Since
D
is nonsingular,
\mu=0
. The
k+r
rows are therefore
linearly independent and pairwise orthogonal in
K^{2(k+r)}
; their row
space is consequently self-dual. If

x=\sum_{i}\xi_{i}p_{i}+\sum_{s}\mu_{s}S_{s}
belongs to
U_{c}
, then the defect of
its
j
-th pivot block is
\xi_{j}
, so
\xi_{j}=0
for every
j
.
Conversely, every linear combination of the
S_{s}
lies in
U_{c}
.
Therefore, writing

\mathcal{C}_{r}:=\langle G_{r}(c;Q,\ell,D)\rangle_{K}
,

\mathcal{C}_{r}\cap U_{c}=\langle S_{1},\ldots,S_{r}\rangle_{K}.

Finally, let
I\subseteq\{1,\ldots,k\}
. Retaining the rows
p_{i}

with
i\in I
, their pivot block columns, and all terminal rows and block
columns replaces
Q
by
Q|_{I\times I}
and
(\ell_{i})_{i=1}^{k}
by

(\ell_{i})_{i\in I}
, while leaving
D
unchanged. The two Gram
identities above restrict to indices in
I
. Hence its rows are pairwise
orthogonal; the pivot-block defects determine the coefficients of the
retained
p_{i}
, and the nonsingularity of
D
determines those of the

S_{s}
. Thus the restricted matrix has
|I|+r
independent rows in

K^{2(|I|+r)}
and generates a self-dual code. For

I=\varnothing
, this row space is

\{(z_{1}(1,c),\ldots,z_{r}(1,c)):z\in K^{r}\},

the full terminal isotropic space in
K^{2r}
, because
D
is
nonsingular. This proves the final assertion.
∎
