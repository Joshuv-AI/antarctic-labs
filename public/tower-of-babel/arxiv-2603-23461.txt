End-to-End Efficient RL for Linear Bellman Complete MDPs with Deterministic Transitions
Zakaria Mhammedi, Alexander Rakhlin, Nneka Okolo

Abstract
We study reinforcement learning (RL) with linear function approximation in Markov Decision Processes (MDPs) satisfying linear Bellman completeness —a fundamental setting where the Bellman backup of any linear value function remains linear. While statistically tractable, prior computationally efficient algorithms are either limited to small action spaces or require strong oracle assumptions over the feature space. We provide a computationally efficient algorithm for linear Bellman complete MDPs with deterministic transitions , stochastic initial states, and stochastic rewards. For finite action spaces, our algorithm is end-to-end efficient; for large or infinite action spaces, we require only a standard argmax oracle over actions. Our algorithm learns an ε \varepsilon -optimal policy with sample and computational complexity polynomial in the horizon, feature dimension, and 1 / ε 1/\varepsilon .



1
Introduction

Reinforcement Learning (RL) is a standard framework for sequential decision making
(
Sutton and Barto 2018
)
, with applications ranging from game playing
(
Mnih et al. 2015
;
Silver et al. 2016
)
and robotics
(
Andrychowicz et al. 2020
)
to training and fine-tuning large language models
(
Ouyang et al. 2022
;
Schulman et al. 2017
)
.
In many of these applications, the state and action spaces are large or infinite, making function approximation essential to generalize across states and actions
(
Bertsekas and Tsitsiklis 1996
;
Sutton and Barto 2018
)
.
A classical and widely studied approach is
linear value function approximation
, where one represents value functions as linear functions of pre-specified feature vectors.
This approach dates back decades
(
Bradtke and Barto 1996
;
Sutton and Barto 2018
)
and continues to underpin many modern empirical methods
(
Mnih et al. 2015
;
Schulman et al. 2017
)
.

Linear Bellman completeness.

This paper focuses on
linear Bellman complete
MDPs, a fundamental setting in RL with function approximation.
In this setting, we are given feature maps
\phi_{h}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
for each step
h
in the horizon (where
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{A}
are the state and action spaces, respectively), and we assume that the
Bellman backup
of any linear function (in these features) remains linear. The Bellman backup at step
h
maps a function
f_{h+1}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}
to the function
(x,a)\mapsto\mathbb{E}[\bm{r}_{h}+\max_{a^{\prime}}f_{h+1}(\bm{x}_{h+1},a^{\prime})\mid\bm{x}_{h}=x,\bm{a}_{h}=a]
; it is the core operator underlying value iteration.
Formally, linear Bellman completeness means that for any linear function
\langle\phi_{h+1}(x,a),\theta\rangle
, its Bellman backup at step
h
can be expressed as
\langle\phi_{h}(x,a),\theta^{\prime}\rangle
for some coefficient vector
\theta^{\prime}
.
This structural assumption is attractive for several reasons:

•

Generality:
Linear Bellman completeness strictly generalizes several well-studied RL models, including linear MDPs
(
Jin et al. 2020
;
Yang and Wang 2019
)
, and also captures the Linear Quadratic Regulator (LQR), a cornerstone model in control theory
(
Bertsekas and Tsitsiklis 1996
;
Wu et al. 2024
)
.

•

Necessity for value iteration:
It is a necessary condition for the success of least-squares value iteration (LSVI), one of the most classical algorithms in RL
(
Bradtke and Barto 1996
)
. Without Bellman completeness, LSVI can diverge arbitrarily
(
Tsitsiklis and Van Roy 1996
)
.

•

Statistical tractability:
When the learner has access to sufficiently exploratory data, linear Bellman completeness is sufficient for statistically efficient learning
(
Munos 2005
;
Munos and Szepesvári 2008
;
Zanette et al. 2020b
)
.

The computational challenge.

Despite its simplicity and statistical tractability, linear Bellman completeness appears to exhibit a
statistical-computational gap
.
While we know that polynomial sample complexity is achievable, the proposed algorithms that attain it are computationally intractable
(
Zanette et al. 2020b
;
Jin et al. 2021
)
.
This gap is particularly notable given the centrality of linear Bellman completeness to the theory of value iteration.

The core difficulty is
exploration
: algorithms must collect data with sufficient coverage to learn a near-optimal policy.
Classical approaches that rely on data from a fixed exploratory policy can fail if that policy does not provide adequate coverage of the state-action space, leading to poor sample efficiency or suboptimal policies
(
Munos 2005
)
.
Overcoming this requires careful exploration, but the two principal exploration paradigms that have proven successful in simpler RL settings both break down for linear Bellman complete MDPs:

•

Optimistic bonuses.
In tabular MDPs and linear MDPs, algorithms such as UCBVI
(
Azar et al. 2017
;
Jin et al. 2018
)
and LSVI-UCB
(
Jin et al. 2020
)
add exploration bonuses to the reward that scale inversely with the frequency of visits to each state (or feature direction). This “local optimism” approach is computationally efficient but requires that the value function class be complete with respect to these exploration bonuses. Unfortunately, for linear Bellman completeness, standard bonus functions (e.g., quadratic functions of the features) do not preserve linearity under Bellman backups, breaking this approach
(
Zanette et al. 2020b
)
.

•

Global optimism and version spaces.
An alternative approach is to maintain a version space of plausible value functions and construct policies that are optimistic with respect to this set
(
Jiang et al. 2017
;
Zanette et al. 2020b
;
Jin et al. 2021
;
Du et al. 2021
)
. While statistically efficient, these methods require solving complex nonconvex optimization problems at each step, making them computationally intractable even in the linear case.

As a result, the following fundamental question has remained open:

Is there an algorithm that learns a near-optimal policy in a linear Bellman complete MDP using
\mathrm{poly}(d,H,1/\varepsilon)
samples and computation time?

Even the special case where the action space is a small constant was only recently resolved
(
Golowich and Moitra 2024a
)
, but their approach has complexity exponential in
|\mathcal{A}|
. Moreover, their algorithm requires a bounded
Q
-function parameter assumption. As shown in
(
Wu et al. 2024
, Section 3.1)
, this assumption significantly limits generality: it excludes linear MDPs as a special case, since Bellman backups can amplify parameter norms arbitrarily (we discuss this limitation in more detail in
Appendix

I
).
Prior to this,
Zanette et al. 2020c
gave the first computationally efficient algorithm (FRANCIS), but under a strong reachability assumption requiring that every feature direction be reachable.

Our setting and contributions.

We study the setting where the
state transition dynamics are deterministic
but the initial state and rewards are stochastic
(
Wen and Van Roy 2017
;
Du et al. 2020
;
Wu et al. 2024
)
.
This setting is practically relevant (e.g., in simulators, game environments, or robotic systems with known physics but stochastic rewards), and it remains theoretically challenging: the learner must still explore to estimate value functions from noisy reward feedback and handle stochastic initial states.
Importantly, even this restricted setting has remained open for large action spaces. While
Wu et al. 2024
studied this setting, their approach is not end-to-end computationally efficient: it relies on a linear optimization oracle over the feature space (one that solves problems of the form
\sup_{(x,a)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}}\phi_{h}(x,a)^{\top}\theta
for various
\theta
). Such an oracle is not needed in, e.g., the linear MDP setting, and its requirement represents a significant gap. Resolving this discrepancy is an important step toward understanding the general problem.

Our main contribution is a computationally efficient algorithm for linear Bellman complete MDPs with deterministic transitions, large action spaces, and stochastic rewards and initial states. For finite action spaces, our algorithm is end-to-end computationally efficient and requires no oracle access. For infinite action spaces, we require only an
\operatorname{argmax}
oracle over actions (i.e.,
\operatorname{argmax}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\phi_{h}(x,a)^{\top}\theta
for a given
x
and
\theta
), which is the standard oracle assumption used even in computationally efficient linear MDP algorithms
(
Jin et al. 2020
)
.

At a high level, the algorithm proceeds layer-by-layer, constructing a small set of policies that induce feature vectors spanning the relevant subspaces at each layer. The key insight is that deterministic dynamics enable efficient discovery of all relevant feature directions without requiring the linear optimization oracle that prior work relies upon.

While the paradigm of first learning a policy cover and then planning is well-established
(
Du et al. 2019
;
Mhammedi et al. 2023a
;
Mhammedi et al. 2023b
)
, adapting it to linear Bellman completeness requires new ideas. The novelty in our approach lies in: (i) exploiting deterministic dynamics to efficiently construct the cover without solving complex optimization problems, and (ii) reducing stochastic-reward planning to deterministic-reward planning by first estimating the instantaneous reward parameters—this reduction allows us to bypass the bounded
Q
-function parameter assumption required by prior work.

Preliminaries

We model the environment as a Markov Decision Process (MDP), formally given by a tuple
\mathcal{M}=(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{A},H,P,r^{\star})
. Here
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{A}
denote the state and action spaces, both of which may be large or infinite; when
\mathcal{A}
is finite, we write
A\coloneqq|\mathcal{A}|
. The horizon is
H\in\mathbb{N}
. The reward function is specified by
r^{\star}=\{r_{h}^{\star}\}_{h=1}^{H}
where each
r_{h}^{\star}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}\to[0,1]
. The transitions are governed by
P=\{P_{h}\}_{h=0}^{H}
where
P_{h}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}\to\Delta(\mathcal{X})
for
h\geq 1
, and
P_{0}(\cdot\mid\varnothing)
specifies the initial state distribution.

A deterministic policy is a sequence
\pi=\{\pi_{h}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{A}\}_{h=1}^{H}
. We denote by
\Pi
the set of all deterministic policies and focus on such policies throughout. Under policy
\pi\in\Pi
, a trajectory
(\bm{x}_{1},\bm{a}_{1},\bm{r}_{1}),\ldots,(\bm{x}_{H},\bm{a}_{H},\bm{r}_{H})
is generated via
\bm{a}_{h}=\pi_{h}(\bm{x}_{h})
,
\bm{r}_{h}\sim r_{h}^{\star}(\bm{x}_{h},\bm{a}_{h})
,
\bm{x}_{h+1}\sim P_{h}(\cdot\mid\bm{x}_{h},\bm{a}_{h})
, starting from
\bm{x}_{1}\sim P_{0}(\cdot\mid\varnothing)
. We use
\mathbb{P}^{\pi}[\cdot]
and
\mathbb{E}^{\pi}[\cdot]
to denote probability and expectation with respect to this sampling process.

The state-action value function (
Q
-function) and state value function are defined as
Q_{h}^{\pi}(\cdot,\cdot)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}^{\pi}\bigl[\sum_{\tau=h}^{H}\bm{r}_{\tau}\mid\bm{x}_{h}=\cdot,\bm{a}_{h}=\cdot\bigr]
and
V_{h}^{\pi}(\cdot)\coloneqq Q^{\pi}_{h}(\cdot,\pi_{h}(\cdot))
, respectively. We write
V_{h}^{\star}(\cdot)\coloneqq\sup_{\pi}V_{h}^{\pi}(\cdot)
for the optimal value.
More generally, for an arbitrary collection of reward functions
r_{1:H}
(where each
r_{h}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}
), we define the value functions under
r_{1:H}
as

Q_{h}^{\pi}(x,a;r_{1:H})\coloneqq\mathbb{E}^{\pi}\left[\sum_{\tau=h}^{H}r_{\tau}(\bm{x}_{\tau},\bm{a}_{\tau})\mid\bm{x}_{h}=x,\bm{a}_{h}=a\right],\quad V_{h}^{\pi}(x;r_{1:H})\coloneqq Q_{h}^{\pi}(x,\pi_{h}(x);r_{1:H}).

When
r_{1:H}=r^{\star}_{1:H}
(the MDP’s reward functions), this recovers the standard notation:
V_{h}^{\pi}(\cdot)=V_{h}^{\pi}(\cdot;r^{\star}_{1:H})
.

Definition 2.1
(Linear Bellman completeness).

For each layer
h\in[H]
, let
\phi_{h}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a feature map and define
\mathcal{B}_{h}\coloneqq\left\{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:|\langle\phi_{h}(x,a),\theta\rangle|\leq 1\ \forall(x,a)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}\right\}.

The MDP
\mathcal{M}
is defined to be
linear Bellman complete
with respect to the feature mappings
(\phi_{h})_{h\in[H]}
if, for each
h\in[H]
, there is a mapping
\mathcal{T}_{h}:\mathcal{B}_{h+1}\to\mathcal{B}_{h}
so that,

\forall\theta\in\mathcal{B}_{h+1},\forall(x,a)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A},\quad\langle\phi_{h}(x,a),\mathcal{T}_{h}\theta\rangle=\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\langle\phi_{h+1}(\bm{x}_{h+1},a^{\prime}),\theta\rangle\mid\bm{x}_{h}=x,\bm{a}_{h}=a\right].

Assumption 2.1
(Deterministic transitions and Linear Bellman completeness).

We assume:

1.

Deterministic transitions:
For each
h\in[H]
, the transition
P_{h}(\cdot\mid x,a)
is supported on a single state for all
(x,a)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}
. (The initial state
\bm{x}_{1}
and rewards
\bm{r}_{h}
remain random.)

2.

Linear Bellman completeness:
The MDP
\mathcal{M}
satisfies
Definition

2.1
with respect to
\phi_{1:H}
.

Assumption 2.2
(Boundedness and linearity).

We assume the feature maps
\phi_{1:H}
are known and that:

1.

Boundedness:
For all
h\in[H]
and
(x,a)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}
,
\|\phi_{h}(x,a)\|_{2}\leq 1
.

2.

Reward linearity:
For all
h\in[H]
,
\mathbb{E}[\bm{r}_{h}\mid\bm{x}_{h}=x,\bm{a}_{h}=a]=\langle w^{\star}_{h},\phi_{h}(x,a)\rangle
for some unknown
w^{\star}_{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\|w^{\star}_{h}\|\leq\sqrt{d}
.

Boundedness assumptions.

Several related definitions of linear Bellman completeness have been considered in the literature, some of which impose additional
\ell_{2}
-norm constraints that can be overly restrictive.
We briefly discuss two such assumptions and explain why they are problematic:

1.

Bounded
Q
-function parameters.
Some works
(
Golowich and Moitra 2024a
;
Zanette et al. 2020b
;
Zanette et al. 2020c
)
assume that the parameter vector
\theta
of any
Q
-function satisfies
\|\theta\|_{2}\leq R
for some polynomial
R
in
d
. This additional assumption weakens the generality of linear Bellman completeness: as shown in
(
Wu et al. 2024
, Section 3.1)
, it excludes linear MDPs as a special case.

2.

Non-expansiveness of Bellman backup.

Song et al. 2022
assume that Bellman backups do not increase the
\ell_{2}
-norm of parameters. This is even more restrictive and fails in simple tabular MDPs
(
Wu et al. 2024
)
.

In contrast,
Item

2
of
2.2
only assumes bounded parameters
\|w^{\star}_{h}\|\leq\sqrt{d}
for the
instantaneous reward
functions.
This is a standard assumption in the linear MDP literature
(
Jin et al. 2020
)
and does not constrain the
Q
-function parameters.

Importantly, even with bounded reward parameters and Bellman completeness, the
Q
-function parameters can still grow unboundedly—but this does not pose a problem for our approach, which circumvents the need to bound
Q
-function parameters (see
Sections

3
and
4
).

Action argmax oracle.

Throughout, we assume that ties in
\operatorname{argmax}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}
operations are broken in an arbitrary but fixed manner.
For large or infinite action spaces, we assume access to an oracle that returns this argmax, i.e., computes
\operatorname{argmax}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\phi_{h}(x,a)^{\top}\theta
for any given state
x
and vector
\theta
.

Goal.

Our goal is to derive an algorithm that returns a policy

\widehat{\pi}
with
\mathbb{E}[V_{1}^{\star}(\bm{x}_{1})-V_{1}^{\widehat{\pi}}(\bm{x}_{1})]\leq\varepsilon
with probability
1-\delta
.
For finite action spaces, we aim for an end-to-end computationally efficient algorithm with sample complexity polynomial in
(d,H,1/\varepsilon,\log(1/\delta))
.
For large or infinite action spaces, we aim for the same complexity, with an additional polynomial number of calls to the action
\operatorname{argmax}
oracle.

Additional notation.

Since transitions and policies are deterministic, the trajectory from any
\bm{x}_{1}=x
under
\pi
is unique. We write
\phi^{\pi}_{h}(x)
for the feature
\phi_{h}(\bm{x}_{h},\bm{a}_{h})
along this trajectory.

3
Warm Up: An Efficient Algorithm Under Finite Actions

In this section, we present a simpler algorithm and analysis for finding a near-optimal policy in linear Bellman complete MDPs (under
2.1
and
2.2
) that runs in polynomial time, albeit with polynomial dependence on the number of actions
A
.
Our approach combines existing algorithmic building blocks: the
Optimistic Constraint Propagation
(
OCP
) algorithm of
Wen and Van Roy 2017
and the
robust barycentric spanner
construction from
Mhammedi et al. 2023a
.

Prior work and context.

Wu et al. 2024
observed that
OCP

(
Wen and Van Roy 2017
)
extends to linear Bellman completeness when rewards and transitions are both deterministic, but noted it may not apply when rewards are stochastic.
Here we show that
OCP
, combined with additional machinery, can tackle stochastic rewards—with complexity polynomial in
A
.
In
Section

4
, we present our main algorithm which removes dependence on
A
.

High-level approach.

Our approach adopts the two-phase exploration-exploitation principle used in prior work
(
Du et al. 2019
;
Misra et al. 2020
;
Mhammedi et al. 2023b
)
, with
Mhammedi et al. 2023a
being closest to ours:

•

Phase I (Exploration):
Build a
policy cover
layer-by-layer. A policy cover is a collection of spanning policies
\Gamma_{1:H}
such that for each layer
h
, the expected feature vectors
\{\mathbb{E}^{\pi}[\phi_{h}(\bm{x}_{h},\bm{a}_{h})]:\pi\in\Gamma_{h}\}
approximately span the space of all reachable expected features. This is achieved via barycentric spanner computation.

•

Phase II (Exploitation):
Estimate reward parameters via regression on rollouts from the policy cover, then use policy optimization. By estimating rewards
first
, we bypass the bounded
Q
-function parameter assumption (
Section

2
).

Phase I uses only deterministic intrinsic rewards (linear in features by construction), for which
OCP
succeeds.
Phase II estimates stochastic reward parameters first, reducing to a deterministic setting where
OCP
applies.

Barycentric spanner.

Our exploration phase relies on
barycentric spanners

(
Awerbuch and Kleinberg 2008
)
: a
C
-barycentric spanner for
\mathcal{W}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a subset
\{w_{1},\ldots,w_{d}\}\subseteq\mathcal{W}
such that every
w\in\mathcal{W}
can be written as
w=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\beta_{i}w_{i}
with
|\beta_{i}|\leq C
.
In our setting,
\mathcal{W}=\{\mathbb{E}^{\pi}[\phi_{h}(\bm{x}_{h},\bm{a}_{h})]:\pi\in\Pi\}
.
We use the relaxed
(C,\varepsilon)
-
approximate
spanner
(
Mhammedi et al. 2023a
)
, allowing additive error:
\|w-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\beta_{i}w_{i}\|\leq\varepsilon
with
|\beta_{i}|\leq C
.
Such a spanner provides a basis for exploration: rollouts from spanning policies yield features approximately covering all “reachable” directions.

To compute a spanner, we use
RobustSpanner
from
(
Mhammedi et al. 2023a
)
, which tolerates approximate oracles (unlike
(
Awerbuch and Kleinberg 2008
)
) at the cost of additive error
\varepsilon
.
The algorithm requires two oracles:

1.

Approximate linear optimization
(
LinOpt
): Given
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, return
\widehat{\pi}
approximately maximizing the functional
\pi\mapsto\theta^{\top}\mathbb{E}^{\pi}[\phi_{h}(\bm{x}_{h},\bm{a}_{h})]
over
\pi\in\Pi
.

2.

Vector estimation
(
Vec
): Given
\pi
, estimate
\mathbb{E}^{\pi}[\phi_{h}(\bm{x}_{h},\bm{a}_{h})]=\mathbb{E}[\phi_{h}^{\pi}(\bm{x}_{1})]
.

Tolerance to approximation errors is critical since errors arise from estimation.
We implement
LinOpt
via
OCP
and
Vec
via Monte Carlo (
Algorithm

9
); see
Appendix

N
.

Optimistic Constraint Propagation (
OCP
).

The
OCP
algorithm was introduced by
Wen and Van Roy 2017
for efficient reinforcement learning in deterministic systems with general function approximation.
Given a hypothesis class
\mathcal{Q}
of candidate
Q
-functions,
OCP
acts optimistically by maximizing over
\mathcal{Q}
, and refines
\mathcal{Q}
via Bellman consistency constraints after each transition.
The main assumptions are “
Q^{\star}
-realizability” (optimal
Q
-function in
\mathcal{Q}
) and deterministic rewards and transitions; the regret bound scales with the eluder dimension of
\mathcal{Q}
. The former assumption holds under linear Bellman completeness with linear
\mathcal{Q}
. Our presentation (
Algorithm

1
) takes reward functions
r_{1:H}
as input, allowing OCP to be invoked with rewards different from the true MDP rewards.

For finite actions,
OCP
is efficient: the optimization in
8
(supremum over
Q
-functions then actions) can be solved by enumerating actions and solving a linear program for each. This becomes intractable for large/infinite action spaces or with only an action
\operatorname{argmax}
oracle. The original guarantee of
Wen and Van Roy 2017
is a regret bound.
To adapt to our PAC setting (stochastic initial states, single near-optimal policy), we add evaluation (Lines
15
–
19
) that returns the best-performing policy among the first
T
episodes.
The PAC guarantee follows via standard online-to-batch conversion.

Lemma 3.1
(Guarantee of

Fix
\varepsilon,\delta\in(0,1)

(

Phase II: Policy optimization with stochastic rewards.

Having obtained a policy cover
\Gamma_{1:H}
from Phase I, we now turn to Phase II: using this cover to optimize the (stochastic) reward functions.
A natural approach would be to follow the Phase II procedure of
Mhammedi et al. 2023a
, which uses
Policy Search by Dynamic Programming
(PSDP)
(
Bagnell et al. 2003
)
.
PSDP proceeds via a backward loop over layers
\ell=H,\ldots,1
: at each layer, one rolls in with policies from the cover to reach layer
\ell
, then rolls out with the partially constructed policy
\widehat{\pi}_{\ell+1:H}
, regressing onto the sum of future rewards to learn a
Q
-function for layer
\ell
.
The policy
\widehat{\pi}_{\ell}
is then constructed as the argmax of this
Q
-function, and the procedure continues to layer
\ell-1
.

The challenge: unbounded
Q
-function parameters.

For PSDP to succeed, we need the
Q
-functions of learned policies to remain linear;
Golowich and Moitra 2022
showed this holds for argmax policies (with a specific tie-breaking rule).
However, obtaining tractable guarantees for the regression problems requires bounded
Q
-function parameters—a substantial limitation (
Section

2
).

Our solution.

We reduce to the deterministic setting where
OCP
applies (
Algorithm

3
):

1.

Reward estimation:
Since
2.2
bounds reward parameters
w^{\star}_{h}
(by
\sqrt{d}
), we estimate them efficiently via ridge regression on rollouts from the cover
\Gamma_{1:H}
. The spanner property (
Lemma

3.2
) ensures accurate estimates
\widehat{w}_{h}
in all relevant directions.

2.

Planning with deterministic rewards:
Construct proxy rewards
\widehat{r}_{h}(x,a)=\phi_{h}(x,a)^{\top}\widehat{w}_{h}
and invoke
OCP
to find a near-optimal policy.

Guarantee of Phase II.

Since the proxy rewards are deterministic and linear,
OCP
optimizes them efficiently. For any policy cover
\Gamma_{1:H}
satisfying the spanner property (
Lemma

3.2
),
Algorithm

3
outputs a near-optimal policy—the proof combines accurate reward estimation with
OCP
’s guarantee (see
Appendix

B
).

An Efficient Algorithm for Large Action Spaces

In this section, we address the significantly more challenging setting where the action space
\mathcal{A}
is large or infinite, making
OCP
computationally intractable.
We assume access only to an
\operatorname{argmax}
oracle over actions as described in
Section

2
.

Why not use PSDP or OCP?

Since OCP’s computational cost scales polynomially with
|\mathcal{A}|
(see
Section

3
), we must consider alternative approaches.
A natural idea is to use policy optimization algorithms such as PSDP to build the policy cover layer-by-layer.
However, PSDP requires bounded
Q
-function parameters to obtain tractable guarantees—an assumption that may exclude even linear MDPs as remarked in
Section

2
.
Furthermore, algorithms like PSDP require roll-in policies with good state coverage.
One could imagine using the approach of
Algorithm

2
: building approximate spanners layer-by-layer and feeding the spanner from previous layers to the PSDP routine.
This is, for example, the approach taken by
Mhammedi et al. 2023a
in the low-rank MDP setting.
However, in the linear Bellman complete setting, coverage errors compound across layers: if the spanner at layer
h
fails to span some relevant feature directions, these gaps propagate and amplify at subsequent layers. Since
RobustSpanner
can only compute
approximate
spanners, this compounding is unavoidable.
Thus, we need a fundamentally different approach.

Our approach: FQI with exact linear optimization.

Instead of using OCP or PSDP, we use Fitted Q-Iteration (
FQI
)
(
Munos and Szepesvári 2008
)
and perform linear optimization
exactly
within each layer.
To enable this exact linear optimization, we build an additional set of policies
\Psi_{h}
at each layer
h
that satisfy a
coverage property
: for i.i.d. initial states
\bm{x}_{1},\bm{x}_{1}^{{\scriptscriptstyle 1}},\dots,\bm{x}_{1}^{{\scriptscriptstyle n_{\texttt{cover}}}}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\mathcal{S}_{h}\subseteq\operatorname{Span}\left\{\phi^{\pi}_{h}(\bm{x}_{1}^{{\scriptscriptstyle m}}):m\in[n_{\texttt{cover}}],\pi\in\Psi_{h}\right\}\right]\geq 1-\delta/H^{2},

(3)

where, with high probability over the initial state,
\mathcal{S}_{h}
is a subspace spanned by
\phi_{h}^{\pi}(\bm{x}_{1})
for all
\pi\in\Pi
.
Such a coverage property ensures that sampling enough initial states and rolling in with policies in
\Psi_{h}
covers all relevant feature directions.
Thanks to deterministic transitions (
2.1
), the regression problems in
FQI
can be solved
exactly
via minimum-norm least-squares; coverage then guarantees these solutions are correct for most sampled initial states.
Unlike
Wu et al. 2024
, our least-squares problems are
unconstrained
, avoiding the need for a linear optimization oracle over features.
At the same time, we also compute an approximate spanner
\Gamma_{h}
via
RobustSpanner
.
The reason we need both
\Psi_{h}
and
\Gamma_{h}
is that while the coverage policies
\Psi_{h}
suffice for deterministic regression in
FQI
, they lack the spanner property needed for stochastic regression.
In Phase II, estimating the reward parameters
w^{\star}_{h}
via ridge regression requires the spanner property of
\Gamma_{h}
for accurate estimation across relevant feature directions.

The
SubspaceCover
subroutine.

The subroutine that computes the coverage policies
\Psi_{h}
(along with the subspace
\mathcal{S}_{h}
) is
SubspaceCover
(
Algorithm

4
).
Unlike
RobustSpanner
or the original barycentric spanner algorithm,
SubspaceCover
does not attempt to maximize the determinant (which would yield a true spanner).
Instead, its goal is simply to: (i) identify the subspace
\mathcal{S}_{h}
where feature maps land with high probability, and (ii) find a set of policies
\Psi_{h}
that satisfy the coverage property
(
3
)
.
The formal guarantee (
Lemma

E.3
, proved in
Appendix

E
) shows that given coverage policies for layers
1,\dots,h-1
,
SubspaceCover
outputs a subspace
\mathcal{S}_{h}
containing the span of all reachable feature vectors at layer
h
with high probability, along with policies
\Psi_{h}
that provide coverage for
\mathcal{S}_{h}
.

SubspaceCover
identifies the subspace
\mathcal{S}_{h}
in a greedy fashion.
It runs an iterative process that searches for policies leading to features that fall
outside
the current subspace
\mathcal{S}^{{\scriptscriptstyle t}}_{h}
.
Once such a policy is identified (detected by the test in
21
), it is added to
\Psi_{h}
.
Next, this policy is used to identify a single “persistent” outlier direction
v
via the subroutine
ComputeOutlierDirection
in
25
.
Once this direction is found, the subspace is updated:
\mathcal{S}^{{\scriptscriptstyle t+1}}_{h}\leftarrow\operatorname{Span}(\mathcal{S}^{{\scriptscriptstyle t}}_{h}\cup\{v\})
; see
26
.
Since the ambient dimension is
d
, this process terminates after at most
d

The relevant distribution

via ridge regression on rollouts from the spanner policies

The key difference is that, instead of using

The coverage property of

ensures that

can solve the value function regression problems exactly (in closed form), while the spanner property of

ensures accurate reward parameter estimation.
Together, these properties guarantee that

6
outputs a near-optimal policy for the original stochastic reward MDP; see
Appendix

G
for the formal statement and proof.

Combining Phase I (
Algorithm

5
) and Phase II (
Algorithm

6
), we obtain the following end-to-end guarantee.
The structure parallels

for the OCP-based approach, but with the key difference that the complexity is independent of the size of
\mathcal{A}
(the proof is in
Appendix

Theorem 4.1
(End-to-end suboptimality guarantee).

Fix
\varepsilon,\delta\in(0,1)
and horizon
H\in\mathbb{N}
. Suppose
2.2
and
2.1
hold.
Define
L\coloneqq\max\{1,\log(dH/(\delta\varepsilon))\}

Consider the following procedure:

1.

Run
Algorithm

5
with inputs
(\varepsilon^{\prime},\delta/4)
where
\varepsilon^{\prime}\coloneqq\varepsilon/(c_{0}d^{2}H^{2}L)
for a sufficiently large universal constant

(\mathcal{S}_{1:H},\Psi_{1:H},\Gamma_{1:H})

2.

Run
\textup{{PolicyOpt}}_{\texttt{fqi}}(\Psi_{1:H},\Gamma_{1:H},n,1)
(

6

n\coloneqq\max\left\{n_{\texttt{cover}},\,c_{2}\cdot\frac{d^{4}H^{2}L^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right\},\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\hbox to20.74pt{\crtrefnumber{eq:n-policyopt}\hfil}eq:n-policyopt}

(7)

where

is defined in
(

)
instantiated with
\varepsilon^{\prime}
, and

is a sufficiently large universal constant, obtaining output policy

.

Then, with probability at least

5
Conclusion

We have presented computationally efficient algorithms for reinforcement learning in linear Bellman complete MDPs with deterministic transitions.
This brings us a step closer to resolving the general case with stochastic transitions, which remains an important open problem.

Our techniques may also be applicable to large language model (LLM) fine-tuning via reinforcement learning.
In this setting, the “environment” is a sequence of tokens, and the transition dynamics are deterministic: given a prompt and a sequence of generated tokens, the next state (the concatenated sequence) is fully determined.
A natural setting to investigate is the
linear softmax
parameterization, studied recently by
Foster et al. 2025
;
Xiong et al. 2023
;
Cen et al. 2024
.
This parameterization arises naturally when fine-tuning only the final layer of a pretrained autoregressive language model such as GPT-2
(
Radford et al. 2019
)
, while keeping all earlier layers fixed.
Due to the linearity of the policy class and the determinism of the transitions, this setting shares key structural similarities with ours, making it a promising direction for future work.

Step 4: Combining the events..

By the chain rule for conditional probabilities,

(\theta^{{\scriptscriptstyle t,j}})_{j\in[K^{{\scriptscriptstyle t}}]}
are an orthogonal basis of
(\mathcal{S}^{{\scriptscriptstyle t}}_{h})^{\perp}
, we have that for all
\pi\in\Pi
,

\displaystyle\phi_{h}^{\pi}(x)\notin\mathcal{S}^{{\scriptscriptstyle t}}_{h}\iff\max_{i\in[K^{{\scriptscriptstyle t}}]}\left|\phi_{h}^{\pi}(x)^{\top}\theta^{{\scriptscriptstyle t,i}}\right|\neq 0.

(41)

Now, by
(
25
)
, we have that for all
x\in\mathcal{X}^{{\scriptscriptstyle h}}
and
\pi\in\Pi
,

\displaystyle\max_{i\in[K^{{\scriptscriptstyle t}}]}\left|\phi_{h}^{\pi}(x)^{\top}\theta^{{\scriptscriptstyle t,i}}\right|\neq 0\implies\left(\min_{i\in[K^{{\scriptscriptstyle t}}]}\phi_{h}^{\widehat{\pi}^{{\scriptscriptstyle t,i,-}}}(x)^{\top}\theta^{{\scriptscriptstyle t,i}}<0\text{ or }\max_{i\in[K^{{\scriptscriptstyle t}}]}\phi_{h}^{\widehat{\pi}^{{\scriptscriptstyle t,i,+}}}(x)^{\top}\theta^{{\scriptscriptstyle t,i}}>0\right).

(42)

Therefore, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\operatorname{Span}\{\phi_{h}^{\pi}(\bm{x}_{1}):\pi\in\Pi\}\not\subseteq\mathcal{S}^{{\scriptscriptstyle t}}_{h}\text{ and }\bm{x}_{1}\in\mathcal{X}^{{\scriptscriptstyle h}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left[\max_{i\in[K^{{\scriptscriptstyle t}}]}\sup_{\pi\in\Pi}\left|\phi_{h}^{\pi}(\bm{x}_{1})^{\top}\theta^{{\scriptscriptstyle t,i}}\right|\neq 0\text{ and }\bm{x}_{1}\in\mathcal{X}^{{\scriptscriptstyle h}}\right],

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left[\max_{i\in[K^{{\scriptscriptstyle t}}]}\max\left(\phi_{h}^{\widehat{\pi}^{{\scriptscriptstyle t,i,+}}}(\bm{x}_{1})^{\top}\theta^{{\scriptscriptstyle t,i}},-\phi_{h}^{\widehat{\pi}^{{\scriptscriptstyle t,i,-}}}(\bm{x}_{1})^{\top}\theta^{{\scriptscriptstyle t,i}}\right)>0\text{ and }\bm{x}_{1}\in\mathcal{X}^{{\scriptscriptstyle h}}\right],

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\in[K^{{\scriptscriptstyle t}}]}\mathbb{P}\left[\phi_{h}^{\widehat{\pi}^{{\scriptscriptstyle t,i,+}}}(\bm{x}_{1})^{\top}\theta^{{\scriptscriptstyle t,i}}>0\text{ and }\bm{x}_{1}\in\mathcal{X}^{{\scriptscriptstyle h}}\right]+\sum_{i\in[K^{{\scriptscriptstyle t}}]}\mathbb{P}\left[\phi_{h}^{\widehat{\pi}^{{\scriptscriptstyle t,i,-}}}(\bm{x}_{1})^{\top}\theta^{{\scriptscriptstyle t,i}}<0\text{ and }\bm{x}_{1}\in\mathcal{X}^{{\scriptscriptstyle h}}\right],

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\in[K^{{\scriptscriptstyle t}}]}\mathbb{P}\left[\phi_{h}^{\widehat{\pi}^{{\scriptscriptstyle t,i,+}}}(\bm{x}_{1})\notin\mathcal{S}^{{\scriptscriptstyle t}}_{h}\text{ and }\bm{x}_{1}\in\mathcal{X}^{{\scriptscriptstyle h}}\right]+\sum_{i\in[K^{{\scriptscriptstyle t}}]}\mathbb{P}\left[\phi_{h}^{\widehat{\pi}^{{\scriptscriptstyle t,i,-}}}(\bm{x}_{1})\notin\mathcal{S}^{{\scriptscriptstyle t}}_{h}\text{ and }\bm{x}_{1}\in\mathcal{X}^{{\scriptscriptstyle h}}\right],

and using that
K^{{\scriptscriptstyle t}}\leq d
, we get

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2H}+2d\sqrt{\frac{2\log(4/\delta^{\prime})}{n_{\texttt{test}}}},

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{H},

(43)

where the last inequality follows from the choice of
n_{\texttt{test}}
in
(
17
)
.
Therefore, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\operatorname{Span}\{\phi_{h}^{\pi}(\bm{x}_{1}):\pi\in\Pi\}\subseteq\mathcal{S}^{{\scriptscriptstyle t}}_{h}\text{ and }\bm{x}_{1}\in\mathcal{X}^{{\scriptscriptstyle h}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}[\bm{x}_{1}\in\mathcal{X}^{{\scriptscriptstyle h}}]-\mathbb{P}\left[\operatorname{Span}\{\phi_{h}^{\pi}(\bm{x}_{1}):\pi\in\Pi\}\not\subseteq\mathcal{S}^{{\scriptscriptstyle t}}_{h}\text{ and }\bm{x}_{1}\in\mathcal{X}^{{\scriptscriptstyle h}}\right],

\displaystyle\geq 1-(h-1)\frac{\varepsilon}{H}-\frac{\varepsilon}{H},

(44)

where the last inequality follows from
(
43
)
. This completes the proof.
∎

.

for
c_{2}
sufficiently large.

•

Span error term:

H\cdot(\sqrt{d}+C)\cdot 25d\varepsilon^{\prime}
.

With
\varepsilon^{\prime}=\varepsilon/(c_{0}d^{2}H^{2}L)
and
\sqrt{d}+C=O(\sqrt{dL})

sufficiently large (using

•

.

This is

for
c_{0}
sufficiently large (using

).

Summing all four contributions yields
\mathbb{E}[V_{1}^{\pi}(\bm{x}_{1})-V_{1}^{\widehat{\pi}}(\bm{x}_{1})]\leq\varepsilon
for all

.

Step 4: Combining the events.

By the chain rule for conditional probabilities,

\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{E}^{\texttt{cov}}\cap\mathcal{E}^{\texttt{opt}}]=\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{E}^{\texttt{cov}}]\cdot\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{E}^{\texttt{opt}}\mid\mathcal{E}^{\texttt{cov}}]\geq(1-\delta/4)(1-3\delta/4)\geq 1-\delta.

)

Part III
Auxiliary Results and Additional Related Work

Appendix I
Related Works

Tabular and linear MDPs.

The simplest RL setting is the
tabular
case, where both the state and action spaces are finite and small enough that every state-action pair can be visited multiple times.
Here, computationally efficient and near-optimal algorithms have been known for decades, including classical methods such as
Q
-learning
[
Watkins and Dayan 1992
]
and value iteration
[
Bertsekas and Tsitsiklis 1996
]
, as well as modern optimistic exploration algorithms such as UCBVI
[
Azar et al. 2017
]
,
Q
-learning-UCB
[
Jin et al. 2018
]
, and variants achieving optimal sample complexity
[
Zhang et al. 2020
]
.

The
linear MDP
setting
[
Jin et al. 2020
,
Yang and Wang 2019
]
extends the tabular case to large state and action spaces by assuming that the transition dynamics and rewards are linear in known feature maps
\phi_{h}(x,a)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Specifically, there exist an unknown signed measure
\mu_{h}
and an unknown vector
\theta_{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that for all bounded measurable functions
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
and all
(x,a)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}
,
\mathbb{E}[f(\bm{x}_{h+1})\mid\bm{x}_{h}=x,\bm{a}_{h}=a]=\langle\phi_{h}(x,a),\mu_{h}(f)\rangle
, where
\mu_{h}(f)\coloneqq\int f(x^{\prime})d\mu_{h}(x^{\prime})
, and
r_{h}(x,a)=\langle\phi_{h}(x,a),\theta_{h}\rangle,
with
\|\theta_{h}\|\leq\sqrt{d}
.
This structure allows efficient exploration via optimistic bonuses, leading to computationally efficient algorithms such as LSVI-UCB
[
Jin et al. 2020
]
, with recent refinements achieving optimal or near-optimal rates
[
Agarwal et al. 2022
,
He et al. 2023
,
Zanette et al. 2020a
]
.

Statistically efficient approaches.

Linear Bellman completeness strictly generalizes linear MDPs but does not impose any structure on the transition function—it only requires that Bellman backups of linear value functions remain linear
[
Zanette et al. 2020b
]
.
This makes the setting significantly more challenging computationally, since techniques that work for linear MDPs (e.g., adding quadratic exploration bonuses) no longer apply: quadratic functions do not remain linear under Bellman backups in this setting.

Early work established that linear Bellman completeness is
statistically
tractable.

Munos 2005
,
Munos and Szepesvári 2008
showed that with sufficiently exploratory data, fitted
Q
-iteration succeeds under Bellman completeness.

Zanette et al. 2020b
proposed ELEANOR, an algorithm that achieves polynomial sample complexity in the online setting by maintaining a version space and performing global optimistic planning.
Similarly,
Jin et al. 2021
proposed GOLF using Bellman eluder dimension, and
Du et al. 2021
studied bilinear classes.
However, all these algorithms are
computationally intractable
: they require solving nonconvex optimization problems to implement optimistic planning over complex version spaces, even when the value function class is linear.

Reachability and constant actions.

The first computationally efficient algorithm for linear Bellman completeness was FRANCIS
[
Zanette et al. 2020c
]
, which works under a strong
reachability
assumption that for every direction in feature space, there exists a policy whose expected features have non-trivial projection onto that direction.
The question of computationally efficient learning without reachability remained open until very recently.

Golowich and Moitra 2024a
made a breakthrough by introducing a
local optimism
approach that constructs exploration bonuses preserving Bellman linearity.
This was the first polynomial-time algorithm for the general setting.
However, their approach has a critical limitation: the sample and computational complexity scale as
\mathrm{poly}(d,H,1/\varepsilon)^{O(|\mathcal{A}|)}
, exponential in the number of actions.
As they note, this restricts their method to settings where
|\mathcal{A}|
is a small constant (e.g.,
|\mathcal{A}|=2
or
3
). Additionally, their algorithm requires a bounded
Q
-function parameter assumption: that the parameter
\theta
satisfying
Q_{h}(\cdot,\cdot)=\phi_{h}(\cdot,\cdot)^{\top}\theta
has
\|\theta\|_{2}\leq R
for some polynomial
R
in
d
. As shown by
Wu et al. 2024
, this assumption limits generality: it excludes linear MDPs as a special case, since Bellman backups can amplify parameter norms arbitrarily in that setting.

Deterministic dynamics setting.

The special case of deterministic transition dynamics has been studied as a stepping stone toward the general problem, and is practically relevant for applications such as game environments and robotic systems with known kinematics.

Wen and Van Roy 2017
provided a polynomial-time algorithm when both transitions and rewards are deterministic. For finite action spaces, our algorithm builds on their work, using their subroutines for constructing approximate spanners and for policy optimization (see
Section

3
). For large action spaces, we develop a different approach based on exact identification of relevant feature directions (see
Section

4
).

Du et al. 2020
extended this to stochastic rewards under a
positive suboptimality gap
assumption, meaning that suboptimal policies have value strictly bounded away from optimal.
Most recently,
Wu et al. 2024
studied the same setting as ours: deterministic transitions with stochastic rewards and initial states, without assuming a suboptimality gap.
Their algorithm uses a randomization-based approach called “null space randomization,” which adds exploration noise only in the null space of previously collected data.
However, their algorithm requires solving constrained regression problems that rely on a
linear optimization oracle
over the feature space, specifically the ability to compute
\sup_{(x,a)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}}\phi_{h}(x,a)^{\top}\theta
for any
\theta
.
Our work removes this oracle assumption for finite action spaces, achieving end-to-end computational efficiency using only standard regression and value function evaluation. For infinite action spaces, we require only an
\operatorname{argmax}
oracle over actions (i.e.,
\operatorname{argmax}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\phi_{h}(x,a)^{\top}\theta
for a given
x
and
\theta
), which optimizes only over actions rather than state-action pairs. This is a strictly weaker requirement and is the standard oracle assumption in computationally efficient algorithms for linear MDPs
[
Jin et al. 2020
,
Yang and Wang 2019
]
.

Policy covers and spanning arguments.

Our algorithm is inspired by the policy cover framework
[
Du et al. 2019
,
Misra et al. 2020
]
, which constructs a small set of policies that collectively “cover” the state space.
This approach has been successful in related problems such as representation learning in RL
[
Mhammedi et al. 2023b
,
Mhammedi et al. 2023a
,
Golowich and Moitra 2023
]
and learning in POMDPs
[
Golowich and Moitra 2022
]
.
A key challenge in applying policy covers to linear Bellman completeness is that coverage errors compound across layers: if the policy cover at layer
h
fails to span some relevant feature directions, these gaps propagate and amplify at subsequent layers, causing the cover to miss increasingly more of the feature space as the horizon grows.
Our contribution is showing how deterministic dynamics help address this challenge. For finite action spaces, we show that approximate spanners suffice (see
Section

3
). For large action spaces, we develop machinery (
SubspaceCover
) that exactly identifies all relevant feature directions at each layer, avoiding the error compounding entirely (see
Section

4
).

Exploration via randomization.

Randomization has proven to be a powerful alternative to bonus-based exploration in RL.
A prominent example is
Randomized Least-Squares Value Iteration
(RLSVI)
[
Osband et al. 2016
]
, which adds Gaussian noise to least-squares estimates and has been shown to achieve near-optimal worst-case regret for linear MDPs
[
Agrawal et al. 2021
,
Zanette et al. 2020a
]
.

Ishfaq et al. 2021
developed randomization algorithms for general function approximation under bounded eluder dimension and Bellman completeness.
In preference-based RL, randomization has led to the first computationally efficient algorithm with near-optimal regret for linear MDPs
[
Wu et al. 2023
]
.

However, these randomization approaches face challenges when applied to linear Bellman completeness:
many inject noise larger than the estimation error to ensure optimism, which can cause exponential growth of parameter values over the horizon.
Truncating these values works for low-rank MDPs but is problematic under linear Bellman completeness, since truncation may break the linearity property.
Our work and the concurrent work of
Wu et al. 2024
address this by restricting noise to the null space of collected data, though we differ in our approach to ensuring computational efficiency.

Broader structural conditions.

Linear Bellman completeness is captured by several broader structural conditions studied in the RL literature, including
Bellman rank

[
Jiang et al. 2017
]
,
witness rank

[
Sun et al. 2019
]
,
Bellman eluder dimension

[
Jin et al. 2021
]
, the
decision-estimation coefficient

[
Foster et al. 2021
]
, and
bilinear classes

[
Du et al. 2021
]
.
These frameworks provide different lenses for understanding when RL is statistically tractable.
While statistically efficient algorithms exist for these settings, computationally efficient algorithms remain unknown in general.

Offline setting

In the offline setting,
Golowich and Moitra 2024b
recently established a computationally efficient algorithm for linear Bellman completeness, leveraging different techniques suitable for the batch setting.

Lemma J.2
.

Let
P
be an arbitrary probability distribution over
\mathbb{R}^{d}

\bm{u}_{1},\dots,\bm{u}_{n+1}
be i.i.d. random vectors drawn from

\mathbb{P}\left[\bm{u}_{n+1}\notin\operatorname{Span}(\bm{u}_{1},\dots,\bm{u}_{n})\right]\leq\frac{d}{n+1}.

Proof.
Let
S=\{\bm{u}_{1},\dots,\bm{u}_{n+1}\}
be the set of
n+1
i.i.d. samples. We utilize a symmetry (exchangeability) argument based on the concept of “critical” samples.

Critical samples.

For a fixed set of samples
S
, we say that the sample
\bm{u}_{i}\in S
is critical with respect to

\bm{u}_{i}\notin\operatorname{Span}(S\setminus\{\bm{u}_{i}\}).

If
\bm{u}_{i}
is critical, it implies that removing
\bm{u}_{i}
strictly decreases the dimension of the subspace spanned by the set. That is:

\dim(\operatorname{Span}(S\setminus\{\bm{u}_{i}\}))<\dim(\operatorname{Span}(S)).

Bounding the number of critical samples.

Let
C\subseteq S
be the set of all critical samples in
S
. We claim that the vectors in
C
are linearly independent.

•

Suppose for the sake of contradiction that the vectors in
C
are linearly dependent. Then, there exists a non-trivial linear combination:

\sum_{\bm{u}_{j}\in C}\alpha_{j}\bm{u}_{j}=0,

where not all
\alpha_{j}
are zero.

•

Let
\bm{u}_{k}\in C
be a vector with a non-zero coefficient
\alpha_{k}\neq 0
. We can rewrite the equation as:

\bm{u}_{k}=-\sum_{\bm{u}_{j}\in C,j\neq k}\frac{\alpha_{j}}{\alpha_{k}}\bm{u}_{j}.

•

This equation expresses
\bm{u}_{k}
as a linear combination of other vectors in
C
. Since
C\subseteq S
, the other vectors are in
S\setminus\{\bm{u}_{k}\}
. Thus,
\bm{u}_{k}\in\operatorname{Span}(S\setminus\{\bm{u}_{k}\})
.

•

This contradicts the definition of
\bm{u}_{k}
being a critical sample.

Therefore, the set of critical samples
C
must be linearly independent. Since the vectors lie in
\mathbb{R}^{d}

d

|C|\leq d.

Applying exchangeability.

Define the indicator variable
\bm{I}_{i}
for the event that
\bm{u}_{i}

\bm{I}_{i}=\mathbb{I}\{\bm{u}_{i}\notin\operatorname{Span}(\{\bm{u}_{1},\dots,\bm{u}_{n+1}\}\setminus\{\bm{u}_{i}\})\}.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\text{number of critical samples}]

Lemma M.1
(Transfer under finite-budget rejection sampling on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
).

Let
Q
be a probability distribution on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and let
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be measurable with
p\coloneqq Q(A)\in(0,1].

Define the conditional distribution
P\coloneqq Q(\cdot\mid A)
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Consider a (possibly randomized) algorithm
\mathtt{Alg}
which, when run with access to i.i.d. samples from a distribution
D
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
draws exactly
N\in\mathbb{N}
samples from
D
(possibly adaptively) and outputs
\mathrm{Out}(D)
.
Assume that when
\mathtt{Alg}
is run with i.i.d. samples from
P
, its output satisfies an event
\mathcal{E}
with probability at least
1-\delta
:

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\hbox to20.74pt{\crtrefnumber{eq:assumption-P-Rd}\hfil}eq:assumption-P-Rd}\mathbb{P}\big[\mathcal{E}\text{ holds for }\mathrm{Out}(P)\big]\;\geq\;1-\delta,

(85)

where the probability is over the i.i.d.

-samples used by
\mathtt{Alg}
and any internal randomness of
\mathtt{Alg}
.

Define
\mathtt{Alg}^{\texttt{RS}}
as the implementation of
\mathtt{Alg}
in which each time
\mathtt{Alg}
requests a sample from
P
,
we instead invoke
\textup{{RejectionSampling}}(Q,A,K)
; the value returned by
\textup{{RejectionSampling}}(Q,A,K)
is provided to
\mathtt{Alg}
as the requested sample
Let
\mathrm{Out}^{\texttt{RS}}
denote the resulting output. Then the following statements hold:

\mathbb{P}\big[\mathcal{E}\text{ holds for }\mathrm{Out}^{\texttt{RS}}\big]\;\geq\;1-\delta-N(1-p)^{K}\;\geq\;1-\delta-Ne^{-pK}.

Proof. Step 1:
Fix one invocation of
\textup{{RejectionSampling}}(Q,A,K)
and let
X_{1},\dots,X_{K}\sim Q
be the i.i.d. samples drawn within that invocation.
Let
J
be the (random) index of the first accepted sample, with the convention
J=\infty
if no accept occurs.
On the event
\{J<\infty\}
, the returned value is
X_{J}
, while on
\{J=\infty\}
the algorithm returns
\mathbf{0}
.

Fix any measurable
B\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and any
1\leq j\leq K
. By independence,

\mathbb{P}[X_{J}\in B,\ J=j]=\mathbb{P}\big[X_{1}\notin A,\dots,X_{j-1}\notin A,\ X_{j}\in(B\cap A)\big]=(1-p)^{j-1}Q(B\cap A),

where
p=Q(A)
. Summing over

yields

\mathbb{P}[X_{J}\in B,\ J<\infty]=\sum_{j=1}^{K}(1-p)^{j-1}Q(B\cap A)=Q(B\cap A)\cdot\frac{1-(1-p)^{K}}{p}.

Also,
\mathbb{P}[J<\infty]=1-(1-p)^{K}
. Therefore,

\mathbb{P}[X_{J}\in B\mid J<\infty]=\frac{\mathbb{P}[X_{J}\in B,\ J<\infty]}{\mathbb{P}[J<\infty]}=\frac{Q(B\cap A)}{p}=\frac{Q(B\cap A)}{Q(A)}=Q(B\mid A)=P(B).

Thus, conditional on not returning
\mathbf{0}
, a single call to
\textup{{RejectionSampling}}(Q,A,K)
returns an exact draw from
P
.

Step 2: coupling for
N
calls (including adaptive requests).

Run
\mathtt{Alg}^{\texttt{RS}}
and let
G
be the event that all
N
calls to
\textup{{RejectionSampling}}(Q,A,K)
return a nonzero vector.
On
G
, each call returns an exact draw from
P
by Step 1, and the internal randomness used across calls
is independent because each call uses fresh i.i.d. draws from
Q
.
Therefore, the sequence of
N
values provided to
\mathtt{Alg}
on
G
is i.i.d. with law
P
, even if
\mathtt{Alg}
requests them adaptively.
It follows that the conditional distribution of the entire execution of
\mathtt{Alg}^{\texttt{RS}}
given
G

matches that of
\mathtt{Alg}
run with true i.i.d.
P
-samples, which implies

\Law(\mathrm{Out}^{\texttt{RS}}\mid G)=\Law(\mathrm{Out}(P)).

Step 3: lower bound on
\mathbb{P}[G]
.

Let
F_{i}
be the event that the
i
-th call to
\textup{{RejectionSampling}}(Q,A,K)
returns
\mathbf{0}
.
Then
F_{i}
occurs exactly when
K
consecutive draws from
Q
miss
A
, hence

\mathbb{P}[F_{i}]=(1-p)^{K}.

By the union bound,

\mathbb{P}[G^{c}]=\mathbb{P}\left[\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}F_{i}\right]\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{P}[F_{i}]=N(1-p)^{K},

so
\mathbb{P}[G]\geq 1-N(1-p)^{K}
.
Using
1-p\leq e^{-p}
yields
(1-p)^{K}\leq e^{-pK}
, hence also
\mathbb{P}[G]\geq 1-Ne^{-pK}
.

Step 4: transferring the guarantee.

By Step 2 and the assumption
(
85
)
,

\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{E}\mid G]=\mathbb{P}\left[\mathcal{E}\text{ holds for }\mathrm{Out}(P)\right]\geq 1-\delta.

Therefore,

\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{E}]\geq\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{E}\cap G]=\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{E}\mid G]\cdot\mathbb{P}[G]\geq(1-\delta)\mathbb{P}[G]\geq 1-\delta-\mathbb{P}[G^{c}]\geq 1-\delta-N(1-p)^{K},

which gives the first inequality in the last paragraph of the lemma.
The second inequality follows from
(1-p)^{K}\leq e^{-pK}
.
∎

Choosing

.

In

M.1
we obtained

\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{E}\text{ holds for }\mathrm{Out}^{\texttt{RS}}]\;\geq\;1-\delta-N(1-p)^{K},

where

. To ensure

\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{E}\text{ holds for }\mathrm{Out}^{\texttt{RS}}]\;\geq\;1-2\delta,

it suffices to make the rejection-sampling failure term at most

Equivalently,

(1-p)^{K}\leq\frac{\delta}{N}.

Taking logs (noting

) gives the exact requirement

Convenient sufficient choice.

Using

(valid for

which guarantees
(1-p)^{K}\leq e^{-pK}\leq\delta/N
and hence
N(1-p)^{K}\leq\delta
.

Lemma O.1
(Uniform amplification for a subspace target).

Let
\delta\in(0,1)
and let
\bm{u}_{1},\bm{u}_{2},\dots
be i.i.d. random vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Fix a linear subspace
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and an integer
n\geq 1
.
Let
r\coloneqq\dim(S)
and assume
r\geq 1
.
Assume that for every
v\in S
,

\mathbb{P}\left[v\in\operatorname{Span}(\bm{u}_{1},\dots,\bm{u}_{n})\right]\geq\frac{1}{2}.

Let
k\coloneqq\left\lceil\log_{2}\left(\frac{r}{\delta}\right)\right\rceil
and
m\coloneqq kn
.
Then

\mathbb{P}\left[S\subseteq\operatorname{Span}(\bm{u}_{1},\dots,\bm{u}_{m})\right]\geq 1-\delta.

Proof.
Fix a basis
b_{1},\dots,b_{r}
of
S
.
For each
i\in[r]
and each
j\in\{1,\dots,k\}
define the block event

A_{j}(b_{i})\coloneqq\left\{b_{i}\in\operatorname{Span}\left\{\bm{u}_{(j-1)n+1},\dots,\bm{u}_{jn}\right\}\right\}.

By the i.i.d. assumption, for every fixed
i\in[r]
, the events
A_{1}(b_{i}),\dots,A_{k}(b_{i})
are independent and identically distributed, and

\mathbb{P}\left[A_{j}(b_{i})\right]=\mathbb{P}\left[b_{i}\in\operatorname{Span}\left\{\bm{u}_{1},\dots,\bm{u}_{n}\right\}\right]\geq\frac{1}{2}\qquad\text{for all }j\in\{1,\dots,k\}.

Let
W\coloneqq\operatorname{Span}\left\{\bm{u}_{1},\dots,\bm{u}_{m}\right\}
. If
A_{j}(b_{i})
occurs for some
j
, then

b_{i}\in\operatorname{Span}\left\{\bm{u}_{(j-1)n+1},\dots,\bm{u}_{jn}\right\}\subseteq W
, hence

\bigcup_{j=1}^{k}A_{j}(b_{i})\subseteq\{b_{i}\in W\}.

Therefore,

\mathbb{P}[b_{i}\notin W]\leq\mathbb{P}\left[\bigcap_{j=1}^{k}A_{j}(b_{i})^{c}\right]=\prod_{j=1}^{k}\mathbb{P}\left[A_{j}(b_{i})^{c}\right]\leq\left(1-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{k}=2^{-k}.

By the choice
k=\left\lceil\log_{2}\left(r/\delta\right)\right\rceil
, we have
2^{-k}\leq\delta/r
, hence

\mathbb{P}[b_{i}\in W]\geq 1-\frac{\delta}{r}.

Using a union bound over
i\in[r]
, we obtain

\mathbb{P}\left[\forall i\in[r],\ b_{i}\in W\right]\geq 1-\sum_{i=1}^{r}\mathbb{P}[b_{i}\notin W]\geq 1-\delta.

On the event
\{\forall i\in[r],\ b_{i}\in W\}

Lemma O.3
.

Lemma O.4
(Sequential union bound with random stopping).

Let
T\in\mathbb{N}
and
\delta\in(0,1)
. Consider an algorithm
\mathcal{A}
with state space
\mathsf{S}
.
During its execution,
\mathcal{A}
may call a subroutine
\mathcal{B}
repeatedly, for at most
T
calls in total.
Index these
potential
calls by
i\in\{1,2,\dots,T\}
, and let
\tau\in\{0,1,\dots,T\}
be the (random) total number of calls that are actually executed.

For each
i\in\{1,\dots,T\}
, let
\mathbf{S}_{i,-}\in\mathsf{S}
be the random state of
\mathcal{A}
immediately
before
the
i
-th call to
\mathcal{B}
,
and let
\mathbf{S}_{i,+}\in\mathsf{S}
be the random state of
\mathcal{A}
immediately
after
that call (when executed).

Let
\mathcal{F}_{i-1}
be the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the history of
\mathcal{A}
up to (and including)
\mathbf{S}_{i,-}
,
and assume that
\tau
is a stopping time with respect to
(\mathcal{F}_{i})_{i\geq 0}
.

Assume further that for each
i
and each
s\in\mathsf{S}
, there exists a measurable set
\mathcal{E}_{i}(s)\subseteq\mathsf{S}

such that the event
\{\mathbf{S}_{i,+}\in\mathcal{E}_{i}(\mathbf{S}_{i,-})\}
is measurable and

\mathbb{P}\left[\mathbf{S}_{i,+}\in\mathcal{E}_{i}(s)\mid\mathbf{S}_{i,-}=s\right]\geq 1-\delta.

Then

\mathbb{P}\left[\forall i\in\{1,\dots,\tau\}:\ \mathbf{S}_{i,+}\in\mathcal{E}_{i}(\mathbf{S}_{i,-})\right]\geq 1-\delta\,\mathbb{E}[\tau].

In particular, since
\tau\leq T
almost surely, the right-hand side is at least
1-T\delta
.

Proof.
For each
i\in\{1,\dots,T\}
define the failure event

F_{i}\coloneqq\left\{\mathbf{S}_{i,+}\notin\mathcal{E}_{i}(\mathbf{S}_{i,-})\right\}.

We use the pointwise bound

\mathbb{I}\left\{\bigcup_{i=1}^{\tau}F_{i}\right\}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}\mathbb{I}\{F_{i}\}.

Taking expectations and expanding the random sum,

\mathbb{P}\left[\bigcup_{i=1}^{\tau}F_{i}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}\mathbb{I}\{F_{i}\}\right]=\sum_{i=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{I}\{i\leq\tau\}\cdot\mathbb{I}\{F_{i}\}\right].

By the tower property,

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{I}\{i\leq\tau\}\cdot\mathbb{I}\{F_{i}\}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{I}\{i\leq\tau\}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{I}\{F_{i}\}\mid\mathcal{F}_{i-1}\right]\right].

Since
\tau
is a stopping time, the event
\{i\leq\tau\}
is
\mathcal{F}_{i-1}
-measurable. Moreover,

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{I}\{F_{i}\}\mid\mathcal{F}_{i-1}\right]=\mathbb{P}\left[F_{i}\mid\mathcal{F}_{i-1}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{P}\left[F_{i}\mid\mathbf{S}_{i,-}\right]\mid\mathcal{F}_{i-1}\right].

By assumption, for every realization
s\in\mathsf{S}
we have

\mathbb{P}\left[F_{i}\mid\mathbf{S}_{i,-}=s\right]=1-\mathbb{P}\left[\mathbf{S}_{i,+}\in\mathcal{E}_{i}(s)\mid\mathbf{S}_{i,-}=s\right]\leq\delta,

so
\mathbb{P}[F_{i}\mid\mathcal{F}_{i-1}]\leq\delta
almost surely. Hence

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{I}\{i\leq\tau\}\cdot\mathbb{I}\{F_{i}\}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{I}\{i\leq\tau\}\cdot\delta\right]=\delta\,\mathbb{P}[i\leq\tau].

Summing over
i
gives

\mathbb{P}\left[\bigcup_{i=1}^{\tau}F_{i}\right]\leq\delta\sum_{i=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}[i\leq\tau]=\delta\,\mathbb{E}[\tau],

where the last identity uses
\mathbb{E}[\tau]=\sum_{i=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}[i\leq\tau]
for
\tau\in\{0,1,\dots,T\}
.
Taking complements yields the stated success bound.
∎

Lemma O.5
(Boosting a conditional spanning guarantee by oversampling).

Let
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a linear subspace, and let
\bm{x}
be an
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued random variable with

p\coloneqq\mathbb{P}[\bm{x}\notin S]\geq\varepsilon.

Let
Q
denote the conditional law
Q\coloneqq\Law(\bm{x}\mid\bm{x}\notin S)
, and let
u\in S^{\perp}
be fixed.
Assume that for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\delta\in(0,1)
,

Q^{\otimes n}\left[u\in\operatorname{Span}\left\{\bm{y}_{1}^{\perp},\dots,\bm{y}_{n}^{\perp}\right\}\right]\geq 1-\delta,

where
\bm{y}_{1},\dots,\bm{y}_{n}
are i.i.d. from
Q
and
(\cdot)^{\perp}
denotes orthogonal projection onto
S^{\perp}
.

Set
m\coloneqq\left\lceil\frac{n}{\varepsilon}+\frac{2\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)n}}{\varepsilon}+\frac{4\log(1/\delta)}{\varepsilon}\right\rceil
and let
\bm{x}_{1},\dots,\bm{x}_{m}
be i.i.d. copies of
\bm{x}
. Then

\mathbb{P}\left[u\in\operatorname{Span}\left\{\bm{x}_{1}^{\perp},\dots,\bm{x}_{m}^{\perp}\right\}\right]\geq 1-2\delta.

Proof.
Define the indicators
I_{i}\coloneqq\mathbb{I}\{\bm{x}_{i}\notin S\}
and the count

N\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{m}I_{i}.

Then
N\sim\mathrm{Bin}(m,p)
with mean
\mu\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[N]=mp
. Since
p\geq\varepsilon
and
m=\left\lceil(1+c)n/\varepsilon\right\rceil
,

\mu=mp\geq m\varepsilon\geq(1+c)n.

Let
J\coloneqq\{i\in\{1,\dots,m\}:I_{i}=1\}
be the random index set of samples that fall outside
S
.
On the event
\{N\geq n\}
, define
i_{1}<\dots<i_{n}
to be the first
n
indices in
J
.

We first relate the target event to these
n
selected indices. Since

\operatorname{Span}\left\{\bm{x}_{i_{1}}^{\perp},\dots,\bm{x}_{i_{n}}^{\perp}\right\}\subseteq\operatorname{Span}\left\{\bm{x}_{1}^{\perp},\dots,\bm{x}_{m}^{\perp}\right\},

we have

\mathbb{P}\left[u\in\operatorname{Span}\left\{\bm{x}_{1}^{\perp},\dots,\bm{x}_{m}^{\perp}\right\}\right]\geq\mathbb{P}\left[\{N\geq n\}\cap\left\{u\in\operatorname{Span}\left\{\bm{x}_{i_{1}}^{\perp},\dots,\bm{x}_{i_{n}}^{\perp}\right\}\right\}\right].

By conditioning on
(I_{1},\dots,I_{m})
,

\mathbb{P}\left[\{N\geq n\}\cap\left\{u\in\operatorname{Span}\left\{\bm{x}_{i_{1}}^{\perp},\dots,\bm{x}_{i_{n}}^{\perp}\right\}\right\}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{I}\{N\geq n\}\,\mathbb{P}\left[u\in\operatorname{Span}\left\{\bm{x}_{i_{1}}^{\perp},\dots,\bm{x}_{i_{n}}^{\perp}\right\}\mid I_{1},\dots,I_{m}\right]\right].

We now analyze the conditional probability inside the expectation.
Fix any realization of
(I_{1},\dots,I_{m})
with
N\geq n
. Conditional on
(I_{1},\dots,I_{m})
,
the variables
\{\bm{x}_{i}:I_{i}=1\}
are independent and each has law
Q
.
Therefore the selected tuple
(\bm{x}_{i_{1}},\dots,\bm{x}_{i_{n}})
is i.i.d. from
Q
, and hence

\mathbb{P}\left[u\in\operatorname{Span}\left\{\bm{x}_{i_{1}}^{\perp},\dots,\bm{x}_{i_{n}}^{\perp}\right\}\mid I_{1},\dots,I_{m}\right]=Q^{\otimes n}\left[u\in\operatorname{Span}\left\{\bm{y}_{1}^{\perp},\dots,\bm{y}_{n}^{\perp}\right\}\right]\geq 1-\delta.

Plugging this back gives

\mathbb{P}\left[u\in\operatorname{Span}\left\{\bm{x}_{1}^{\perp},\dots,\bm{x}_{m}^{\perp}\right\}\right]\geq(1-\delta)\mathbb{P}[N\geq n].

It remains to lower bound
\mathbb{P}[N\geq n]
.
Since
\mu\geq(1+c)n
, we have
n\leq\mu/(1+c)
, so

\{N<n\}\subseteq\left\{N\leq\left(1-\alpha\right)\mu\right\}\quad\text{where}\quad\alpha\coloneqq 1-\frac{1}{1+c}=\frac{c}{1+c}.

By the multiplicative Chernoff bound for a binomial random variable,

\mathbb{P}\left[N\leq\left(1-\alpha\right)\mu\right]\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\mu\right).

Therefore,

\mathbb{P}[N<n]\leq\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{c}{1+c}\right)^{2}\mu\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{c}{1+c}\right)^{2}(1+c)n\right)=\exp\left(-\frac{c^{2}}{2(1+c)}\,n\right),

and hence

\mathbb{P}[N\geq n]\geq 1-\exp\left(-\frac{c^{2}}{2(1+c)}\,n\right).

Combining the bounds and setting
c\geq\max\left\{2\sqrt{\frac{L}{n}},\,\frac{4L}{n}\right\}
completes the proof.
∎
