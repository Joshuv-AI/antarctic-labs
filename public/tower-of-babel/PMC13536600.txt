Safety, tolerability, pharmacokinetics, and pharmacodynamics of SHR8554, a biased µ-opioid receptor agonist: a phase I trial in healthy volunteers
Authors: Lifang Zhang, Ge Luo, Haiying Wang, Honggang Lou, Min Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis randomized, placebo-controlled, dose-escalation phase I trial evaluated the safety, tolerability, pharmacokinetic (PK), and pharmacodynamic (PD) profiles of SHR8554, a biased µ-opioid receptor agonist, in healthy male volunteers under different infusion durations. The study included three cohorts: a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled single-infusion cohort (0.25–3 mg administered over 30 min), an open-label infusion-duration cohort evaluating 0.75 mg administered over 15, 5, or 2 min, and a CYP2D6 poor metabolizer cohort receiving a single 1.5 mg dose over 30 min. A total of 56 participants were enrolled. The maximum tolerated dose (MTD) of SHR8554 was 3 mg. The most common treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs) were dizziness, nausea, and vomiting, with higher incidence at increased doses and shorter infusion durations. PK analyses showed that systemic exposure (Cmax and AUC) increased in a slightly less than dose-proportional manner across the 0.75–3 mg dose range, with a mean elimination half-life of approximately 6 h. Infusion duration had minimal impact on overall drug exposure. A measurable analgesic effect was first observed at 1.5 mg, with onset within 10 min after administration and duration of approximately 4 h. SHR8554 increased pain tolerance and reduced numerical rating scale (NRS) scores in an exploratory cold pain model, although no clear dose-dependent trend was observed. SHR8554 was generally well tolerated within the tested dose range and demonstrated preliminary analgesic activity in healthy volunteers.Keywords: biased μ-opiod receptor agonist, SHR 8554, phase I trail, pharmacokinetics, pharmacodynamics

Introduction

Postoperative pain remains a major challenge in perioperative care despite advances in multimodal analgesic strategies [1, 2]. Inadequate postoperative pain control may delay recovery, prolong hospitalization, and increase the risk of chronic postsurgical pain [3]. Opioid analgesics remain widely used for postoperative pain management because of their potent analgesic efficacy; however, their clinical utility is often limited by adverse effects such as respiratory depression, nausea, vomiting, and gastrointestinal dysfunction. Therefore, the development of safer opioid analgesics with improved tolerability remains an important goal in perioperative pain management.

Most clinically used opioids, including morphine and fentanyl, exert their analgesic effects primarily through activation of the µ-opioid receptor (MOR), a G protein–coupled receptor [4]. Conventional MOR agonists activate both G protein signaling and β-arrestin-2 recruitment pathways [5]. While G protein signaling mediates opioid analgesia, β-arrestin-2 recruitment has been associated with several opioid-related adverse effects, particularly respiratory depression and gastrointestinal dysfunction [6]. Oliceridine (TRV130), a G protein–biased MOR agonist approved by the United States Food and Drug Administration in 2020 for intravenous treatment of moderate-to-severe acute pain, preferentially activate G protein signaling while reducing β-arrestin recruitment [7]. Clinical studies have shown that oliceridine provides effective analgesia with a potentially improved respiratory safety profile compared with conventional opioids [8]. These findings have supported continued interest in biased MOR agonists as a strategy for improving opioid tolerability.

SHR8554 is another G protein–biased MOR agonist currently under clinical development. Preclinical pharmacology and toxicology studies showed that SHR8554 produced analgesic effects comparable to morphine and oliceridine in both in vitro and in vivo models. In addition, SHR8554 demonstrated lower β-arrestin-2 recruitment and reduced gastrointestinal and respiratory inhibitory effects than morphine, while exhibiting a safety profile similar to that of oliceridine. These preclinical findings suggest that SHR8554 may provide effective analgesia with improved tolerability.

Interindividual variability in opioid response remains an important clinical concern and may be influenced by genetic polymorphisms in drug-metabolizing enzymes. CYP2D6 plays a major role in the metabolism of several commonly used opioids, including codeine and tramadol, and CYP2D6 polymorphisms can substantially affect analgesic efficacy and adverse event risk [9–11].

Given the potential impact of CYP2D6 variability on opioid pharmacology, this phase I study evaluated the safety, tolerability, pharmacokinetics (PK), and pharmacodynamics (PD) of single-dose intravenous SHR8554 in healthy adult participants, including the potential influence of CYP2D6 genotype on PK and analgesic responses.

Methods

Source of drug

The investigational study drug, SHR8554, was provided by Jiangsu Hengrui Pharmaceuticals Co., Ltd. (Lianyungang, China).

Ethics

This randomized, dose-increasing, placebo-controlled phase I clinical trial was conducted at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine. Participants were enrolled from March 26, 2018, to November 22, 2018. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Code for Quality Management of Drug Clinical Trials and the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol, all protocol amendments, and the informed consent form and its updates were approved by the Ethics Committee (2017 − 498). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. This study was registered (Registration No. CTR20180587; First Public Release Date: 2 May 2018) in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registration Platform (http://www.chinadrugtrials.org.cn), a registry recognized by the Chinese National Medical Products Administration (NMPA).

Participants

Inclusion criteria: males aged 18–45 years; body weight ≥ 50 kg, kg and body mass index (BMI) 18–26 kg/m²; ²; non-dominant hand cold pain test immersion time of 10–120 s; good health as confirmed by physical examination, laboratory tests, and other relevant assessments; provision of written informed consent; and agreement to use appropriate contraceptive measures during the study period.

Exclusion criteria: known allergy or hypersensitivity to SHR8554 or its excipients; history of cardiovascular, hepatic, renal, or other significant systemic diseases; recent use of prescription drugs, over-the-counter medications, or health supplements; history of drug abuse, heavy smoking, or excessive alcohol consumption; and participation in another clinical trial within a specified period before screening.

CYP2D6 poor metabolizer were identified based on the metabolic ratio of dextromethorphan to dextrorphan. Participants received oral dextromethorphan followed by 250 mL of water 3 h later, and urine samples were collected 4 h after administration for analysis. Participants with a dextromethorphan/dextrorphan ratio > 0.3 were classified as CYP2D6 poor metabolizers.

Dosage and timing of administration

Selection of the administered dose

Dose selection was based on the Guidelines for Estimation of the Maximum Recommended Starting Dose for Initial Clinical Trials in Healthy Adult Volunteers. According to preclinical toxicology studies, the no-observed-adverse-effect level (NOAEL) was 2 mg/kg in rats and 1 mg/kg in cynomolgus monkeys, corresponding to a human equivalent dose (HED) of 0.323 mg/kg. Using a safety factor of 10, the maximum recommended starting dose (MRSD) was calculated to be 1.94 mg.

Considering the starting dose of TRV130 (0.15 mg), formulation specifications, and operational feasibility, the starting dose of SHR8554 was set at 0.25 mg. The initially planned dose groups were 0.25, 0.75, 1.5, 3, 4.5, and 6 mg. Dose escalation was discontinued if four or more participants (≥ 50%) within the same dose group experienced moderate or more severe adverse events. Because four participants in the 3 mg group experienced moderate vomiting, dose escalation was stopped at 3 mg, and the 4.5 mg and 6 mg dose groups were discontinued. Additional 2 mg and 2.5 mg dose groups were subsequently added. The final dose groups were 0.25, 0.75, 1.5, 2, 2.5, and 3 mg.

Timing of drug administration

The study drug was administered by intravenous infusion by trained study personnel on the morning of dosing. The observation period was 1–2 days after dosing for Group A and from the dosing day to Day 6 for Group B. During the observation period, participants remained in the phase I clinical ward and and received standardized meals. Strenuous exercise, caffeine-containing beverages, alcoholic consumption, and smoking were prohibited.

Dosing regimens

All test drugs were diluted with normal saline to a total volume of 10 mL and administered using an intravenous infusion pump:

Group A (including the poor metabolizer subgroup): infusion rate of 20 mL/h for 30 min.

Group B: infusion rates adjusted according to the dosing cycle as follows: 40 mL/h for 15 min, 120 mL/h for 5 min, and 300 mL/h for 2 min.

Randomization and masking

Participants were enrolled according to the order of screening eligibility. The 0.25 mg dose group was predefined as a sentinel cohort without placebo allocation. Beginning with the 0.75 mg dose group, the study adopted a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled design. The randomization schedule was generated using SAS JMP Clinical software. The investigational drug and placebo were packaged according to drug identification numbers and differed only in numerical codes within the same dose group. After screening, participant-specific randomization and drug numbers were assigned through the Interactive Web Response System (IWRS). Group A remained double-blinded throughout the study. Emergency unblinding was permitted only in cases of serious adverse events, severe drug-related adverse events, or emergency resuscitation. Formal unblinding was performed after database lock and completion of blinding verification.

Outcome indicators

Primary outcomes

Safety assessments included adverse events (AEs), physical examination findings, laboratory tests (hematology, blood biochemistry, urinalysis, thyroid function, and coagulation function), vital signs (heart rate, respiration, blood pressure, and body temperature), blood oxygen saturation (SpO2), 12-lead electrocardiogram, and drug dependence assessment throughout the study.

Secondary outcomes

Pharmacokinetic (PK) parameters of SHR8554 after single-dose administration included the area under the concentration–time curve (AUC0~t, AUC0~∞), time to maximum plasma concentration (Tmax), maximum plasma concentration (Cmax), elimination half-life (t1/2), apparent clearance rate (CL), and apparent distribution volume (Vd).

Pharmacodynamic (PD) assessments included changes from baseline in the cold pain test, measured as the immersion time of the non-dominant hand in circulating water maintained at 2.0 ± 0.1 °C, and changes from baseline in the numeric rating scale (NRS) pain score after administration.

Sample size

According to the Technical Guidelines for Clinical Pharmacokinetic Studies of Chemical Drugs issued by the Chinese National Medical Products Administration (NMPA), single-dose studies in healthy participants generally require 8–12 participants per dose group. In this study, except for the lowest dose group (0.25 mg), each dose group included 10 participants, of whom 8 received SHR8554 and 2 received placebo. The 0.25 mg dose group included 2 participants who received SHR8554 only, without placebo control. A total of 58 participants were planned for enrollment across Groups A and B.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized by descriptive statistics, including mean, standard deviation, median, minimum, and maximum values. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. For PK data, the coefficient of variation, geometric mean, geometric standard deviation, and geometric coefficient of variation were additionally calculated where appropriate. PK parameters were estimated using non-compartmental analysis in WinNonlin version 7.0. Safety analyses were performed using the safety analysis set. PD data were analyzed descriptively. Dose proportionality was evaluated using a power model, and the estimated slope (β1) with corresponding 90% confidence intervals was calculated.

Results

Participants

A total of 285 underwent screening, of whom 226 failed to meet the eligibility criteria. Overall, 56 (94.2%) participants received study treatment and completed the study, all of whom were included in the safety, PK, and PD analysis datasets.

In Group A, participants were randomly assigned to the SHR8554 dose groups (0.25, 0.75, 1.5, 2, 2.5 mg, and 3 mg) and the placebo group. Among these, 49 participants (94.2%) received the study drug and completed the study, while 3 participants withdrew prematurely (Fig. 1). All participants in Group B (N = 6, 0.75 mg) received three study doses as planned and completed the study. One participant identified as a CYP2D6 poor metabolizer (1.5 mg) also received the study drug and completed the study. All 56 participants who received SHR8554 were male and of Han ethnicity. Safety results

The most common treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs) in Group A were dizziness (9/49, 18.4%), vomiting (7/49, 14.3%), and nausea (6/49, 12.2%). The incidence of TEAEs generally increased with increasing SHR8554 dose. In the highest-dose group (3 mg), 4 of 5 participants (80.0%) experienced moderate TEAEs, compared with 1 of 8 participants (12.5%) in the second-highest-dose group.

At the 0.75 mg dose level, shortening the infusion duration from 30 min to 15, 5, or 2 min resulted in an increased incidence of drug-related TEAEs, predominantly dizziness. The incidence of dizziness was higher with 5-minute and 2-minute infusions than with 15-minute infusion, whereas no apparent difference was observed between the 5-minute and 2-minute infusion groups.

Pharmacokinetic results

Mean plasma concentration–time profiles of SHR8554 following single-dose administration in Group A (0.75, 1.5, 2, 2.5, and 3 mg) are shown in Fig. 2A and B. As summarized in Table 3, median Tmax ranged from 0.33 to 0.50 h, and mean t1/2 ranged from 6.08 to 6.96 h across dose groups. Mean Cmax values ranged from 7.41 to 23.92 ng/mL, whereas mean AUC₀–t and AUC₀–∞values ranged from 15.98 to 56.82 ng·h/mL and 16.90 to 60.30 ng·h/mL, respectively. Dose proportionality was evaluated using a power model based on logarithmically transformed PK parameters (Cmax, AUC₀–t, and AUC₀–∞ over the dose range of 0.75–3 mg (Table 4). Following single 30-minute intravenous infusion, the estimated slope (β1) values for Cmax, AUC₀–t, and AUC₀–∞ were all < 1, and the corresponding 90% confidence intervals did not include 1, indicating a slightly less than dose-proportional increase in systemic exposure over the evaluated dose range.

Pharmacodynamics results

Pharmacodynamic effects were assessed using pain tolerance, defined as the duration of non-dominant hand immersion in circulating water maintained at 2 °C, and subjective pain intensity measured by pain scores. The 0.25 mg dose group in Group A included only two participants and therefore served as a sentinel cohort; accordingly, it was excluded from the pain tolerance analysis. The CYP2D6 poor metabolizer group (N = 1) was also excluded because of the limited sample size.

Pain tolerance

Following a single 30-minute intravenous infusion in Group A, pain tolerance generally increased in the SHR8554 groups compared with placebo. Specifically, the AUC of change from baseline in pain tolerance increased, and a greater proportion of participants achieved at least a twofold increase in immersion duration relative to baseline (Table 6). However, no clear dose-dependent effect was observed across the SHR8554 dose groups (Table 7). Within the 0.75 mg dose group, mean pain tolerance following the 30-minute infusion appeared higher than that observed with the 5-minute and 2-minute infusions, except for the 15-minute infusion group. Median values were generally comparable across infusion duration groups (Table 7).

Pain scores

Following a single 30-minute intravenous infusion in Group A, baseline-adjusted pain score AUC values were lower in all SHR8554 dose groups except the 0.75 mg group compared with placebo. However, no clear dose-dependent reduction was observed across the SHR8554 dose groups. Within the 0.75 mg dose group, median pain scores following 15-, 5-, and 2-minute infusions were numerically lower than those observed with the 30-minute infusion. The lowest median pain score was observed in the 15-minute infusion group, although the differences were not statistically significant.

In Group A, no improvement relative to baseline was observed in the placebo group (N = 10) at any post-dose time point. In contrast, participants receiving SHR8554 showed reductions in pain scores during the early post-dose period beginning at the 1.5 mg dose level. Sustained improvement across all post-dose time points was observed only in the 2.5 mg group (Fig. 4A). In Group B (N = 6), mean pain scores were reduced relative to baseline at multiple time points (Fig. 4B). In the CYP2D6 poor metabolizer group (N = 1), no sustained reduction in pain scores was observed during the post-dose observation period (Fig. 4C).

Discussion

Postoperative pain remains an important clinical challenge, and inadequate control of acute postoperative pain may contribute to the development of chronic postsurgical pain and impaired postoperative recovery [3, 12]. Although conventional µ-opioid receptor (MOR) agonists provide effective analgesia, their clinical utility is often limited by adverse effects associated with β-arrestin-2 recruitment, including respiratory depression and gastrointestinal dysfunction. Biased MOR agonists such as oliceridine were developed to improve opioid tolerability while preserving analgesic efficacy, and the clinical approval of oliceridine has further supported biased MOR agonism as a promising strategy for acute pain management [13]. In the present phase I study, SHR8554 demonstrated acceptable tolerability, predictable pharmacokinetic characteristics, and measurable analgesic activity in healthy participants, supporting further clinical evaluation in acute pain settings. Some pharmacokinetic results from this study were previously included as supplementary information in Huang et al. [15]; however, the present study represents the dedicated and comprehensive report of the safety, tolerability, pharmacokinetics, and pharmacodynamics of a single dose of SHR8554 based on the complete original study dataset.

The adverse events observed in this study, primarily dizziness, nausea, and vomiting, were generally consistent with the known pharmacological effects of MOR agonists. Notably, tolerability appeared to be influenced not only by dose but also by infusion duration. Although overall systemic exposure remained comparable across different infusion regimens, shorter infusion durations were associated with more frequent adverse events, suggesting that transient peak exposure may be more closely associated with acute tolerability than overall systemic exposure. This observation may have practical implications for perioperative analgesic administration, where infusion duration could potentially be adjusted to balance rapid analgesic onset against tolerability. Importantly, dose escalation was terminated at 3 mg because four participants experienced moderate vomiting at this dose level, whereas the 2–2.5 mg dose range appeared to maintain acceptable tolerability together with measurable pharmacodynamic activity. Although these findings should be interpreted cautiously given the limited sample size, they may provide preliminary guidance for dose selection in subsequent studies.

Previous studies have shown that biased MOR agonists such as oliceridine may produce less opioid-induced hyperalgesia and reduced reward-related behavior compared with morphine at comparable analgesic doses [14]. Nevertheless, these agents may still retain risks associated with opioid exposure, including dependence, abuse, and overdose. Therefore, although SHR8554 demonstrated an overall favorable tolerability profile within the evaluated dose range, further studies remain necessary to determine whether biased MOR agonism ultimately translates into clinically meaningful improvements in long-term opioid safety relative to conventional opioids.

Within the evaluated dose range, systemic exposure to SHR8554 increased in a slightly less than dose-proportional manner, whereas the elimination half-life remained relatively stable across dose groups at approximately 6 h. The stable elimination profile suggests predictable in vivo disposition and may reduce the likelihood of unintended accumulation during perioperative analgesic administration. Interestingly, despite increasing exposure, no clear dose-dependent improvement in pain tolerance or pain scores was observed. This finding may reflect the intrinsic limitations of experimental pain models in healthy participants, including substantial interindividual variability and limited sensitivity for distinguishing analgesic effects at higher opioid exposures. It is also possible that the present findings suggest limited incremental pharmacodynamic effects at higher exposure levels within the evaluated dose range, whereas tolerability limitations became more apparent with increasing exposure. The relatively rapid onset of analgesic activity together with an apparent duration of approximately 4 h may support further evaluation of SHR8554 in perioperative intravenous analgesia and short-term acute pain management.

In vitro studies have shown that SHR8554 is metabolized primarily by CYP2D6, CYP3A4, and CYP3A5, and previous studies demonstrated that coadministration with itraconazole significantly increased SHR8554 exposure [15]. CYP2D6 is also known to play an important role in the metabolism of several commonly used opioids, including codeine and tramadol [16–18]. In the present study, however, only one CYP2D6 poor metabolizer was included, precluding meaningful evaluation of the influence of CYP2D6 polymorphisms on SHR8554 pharmacokinetics or pharmacodynamics. Nevertheless, these findings highlight the importance of further investigating the effects of genetic variability, organ dysfunction, and concomitant medications on SHR8554 disposition in future clinical studies.

This study has several limitations. First, the study population consisted exclusively of healthy male participants aged 18–45 years, and the overall sample size was relatively small. Therefore, the present findings may not fully reflect the pharmacological responses observed in postoperative patients or other clinically relevant populations. Second, pharmacodynamic assessment was based on a cold pain model rather than clinical postoperative pain, and the relationship between the observed analgesic signal and clinical analgesic efficacy remains uncertain. Third, this was a single-dose study, and the safety, tolerability, pharmacokinetic characteristics, and potential accumulation associated with repeated administration were not evaluated. Further studies in patient populations will therefore be required to better define the clinical efficacy, optimal dosing strategy, and long-term safety profile of SHR8554.

Conclusion

This phase I study demonstrated that the novel biased MOR agonist SHR8554 was generally well tolerated in healthy male participants and exhibited predictable pharmacokinetic characteristics with measurable analgesic activity in an experimental cold pain model. These findings support further clinical evaluation of SHR8554 for acute pain management. However, whether SHR8554 offers clinically meaningful advantages over conventional opioids remains to be established in future studies.
