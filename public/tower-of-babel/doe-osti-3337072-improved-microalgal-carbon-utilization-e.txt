1. Executive Summary
1.1 Project Objectives
The project aims to develop a process for turning carbon dioxide (CO2) emitted from power plants or
industrial facilities into useful products. In detail, first, an electrolyzer will be used to convert the CO 2 into
liquid form (e.g., formic acid or formate). The formic acid/formate will then be used as a carbon and
energy source (i.e., food) for genetically engineered algae growth. Each of the steps in the process has
already been demonstrated on a lab scale, but in the proposed work, we will scale them to the bench
scale. The size of the electrolyzer will be increased from 5 cm 2 to 1000 cm2. The productivity of the algae
strains will be enhanced so that the algae convert at least 75% of the carbon in the formate feed. The
growth will move from a beaker in the lab to a 1000 L outdoor algae pond. And the algae will be blended
with other materials to produce a salable product. If we succeed, the algae pond's carbon utilization
efficiency and productivity will be enhanced, and we will have a useful route to lower CO 2 emissions from
a power plant or industrial facility.

1.2 Summary of Major Accomplishments and Findings
Dioxide Materials has successfully scaled up the CO2 to formic acid electrolyzer from 100 cm² to 1000 cm²
and demonstrated industrially relevant current densities with the scaled-up electrolyzers using a CO 2
source that simulates industrial CO2 waste. The National Laboratory of Rockies (NLR) has successfully
engineered P. renovo strains to significantly improve their performance. AzCATI has demonstrated the
feasibility of outdoor cultivation and deployment, successfully producing biomass paste for product
development. Algix has successfully converted biomass into bioplastics and conducted materials analysis
and property evaluations. Overall, the project results have met all the success criteria. Specific findings
from the project include:

2.1.2 100 cm2 electrolyzer modification and performance improvements
The 100 cm2 formic acid electrolyzer underwent significant modifications to its components to overcome
challenges associated with scaling up from the 5 cm2 laboratory-size electrolyzer. These modifications
primarily focused on the flow field design, center compartment configuration, and ion exchange medium
selection and modification.
• Ion Exchange Materials Development
For 5 cm2 formic acid electrolyzers, the ion exchange material used in the center compartment is in the
form of beads, which brings difficulty in cell assembling and limits the minimum thickness of the center
compartment. In this project, we planned to develop new ion exchange materials or modify the beads so
that they can be easily applied to the scale-up electrolyzer assembly. We developed/tested three ion

prepared by the sulfonation of the PPS fiber paper and PEEK mesh in a 4M H 2SO4 solution at certain

electrolyzer performance. We didn’t run formic acid product analysis due to the low and unstable current

• Center Compartment Development

  Figure 4. Different designs of the center compartment and the manufactured V4 center compartment.
  a) V2, b) V3, c) V4, d) manufactured V4 center compartment.
The center compartment plays a very important role in the formic acid electrolyzer with the current
configuration. To scale the electrolyzer size from 5 cm2 to 100 cm2 and 1000 cm2, we need to modify the
original design of the 5 cm2 center compartment. We planned to work on the modification of the center
compartment design of the 100 cm2 formic acid electrolyzer first, then scale up to 1000 cm2 size, and do
any modifications if needed. Figure 4 shows all the designs of the center compartment in this work (V2,
V3, V4) based on an original V1 design, which is the first version of the center compartment, like the one
used in 5 cm2 formic acid electrolyzers. We realized that the flow distribution in the center compartment
with the V1 design was poor. We then came up with a V2 design for the center compartment, which was
used in the 100 cm2 formic acid electrolyzer to evaluate the effect of this design on the electrolyzer's
performance. With the V2 design, the 100 cm2 formic acid electrolyzer could produce >5wt% formic acid
at ≥100 mA/cm² current density from simulated flue gas for over 100 hrs. However, we noticed that the
flow distribution was not uniform, especially at the corners. We decided to increase the total number of
distribution channels, as shown in V3. However, we found that it was very challenging to manufacture the
V3 center compartment due to the limited capability of our CNC machine. We then modified the design
to get the V4 center compartment. Compared with the V2 design, the V4 design has the same number of
distribution channels but longer splitters and narrower channels. We also ran a simulation of the V4 design,
and the results indicated a uniform flow distribution. The center compartment was then manufactured
(Figure 4d) and was tested in the 100 cm2 formic acid cell to evaluate the electrolyzer performance. If it
works with improved cell performance, it will provide a solid starting point for the design of the center
compartment for the 1000 cm2 formic acid electrolyzer.

• 100 cm2 Electrolyzer Performance Improvement

In the initial phase of the 100 cm² scale-up effort, we conducted two primary 100-hour performance trials
using a simulated blast furnace gas mixture (approximately 30% CO₂, 25% CO, and 1% H₂). While these
trials successfully demonstrated that the electrolyzer could meet the project milestone of producing ≥5%
formic acid at current densities of ≥100 mA/cm², the overall efficiency and stability were initially
insufficient for commercial viability. During the first 100-hour test, the Faradaic efficiency (FE) was
measured at only 20%, a significant reduction from the 70–80% typically observed in 5 cm² laboratory-
scale cells. A subsequent second run showed improvement, with the FE increasing to 40%, though it
remained below established performance benchmarks.

To evaluate long-term durability, a 250-hour stability test (Run 3) was performed, which ultimately
extended to 300 total hours of operation at a current density exceeding 100 mA/cm². Throughout this
extended duration, the electrolyzer consistently produced a formic acid product with a concentration
above 6 wt%. However, despite this stable production, the Faradaic efficiency plateaued between 35%
and 40%, confirming that while the system could maintain output, the internal electrochemical efficiency
required further optimization to reach the desired >70% targets.

Technical analysis of these first three runs identified several critical design limitations that hindered
performance at the 100 cm² scale. The V2 center compartment design utilized in these tests resulted in
non-uniform fluid distribution, particularly evidenced by poor flow at the corners of the cell. Additionally,
the reliance on loose ion-exchange resin beads in the center compartment introduced significant
assembly challenges and operational risks; as the cell size increased, uniform loading became difficult, and
the beads were prone to shifting, which led to flow blockages and reduced ion transport efficiency. These

2.1.4 Reducing Oxygen in the Flow Gas Feed
One important project task is to reduce the O2 level in the flue gas to minimize the deteriorating effect of
O2 on formic acid electrolyzer performance, as demonstrated previously. We focused on developing
methods to remove O2 in the flue gas from a coal-fired power plant. The first strategy we explored was
utilizing a proton exchange membrane fuel cell (PEMFC) to remove O 2 (Figure 9a). Specifically, the flue gas
with 6-10% O2 was fed into the cathode side of the PEMFC where O2 was consumed. Accordingly, H2 was
consumed on the anode side. Since the formic acid electrolyzer produced H2 as a byproduct, we proposed

to provide H2 for the PEMFC from the formic acid electrolyzer. However, in the present work, an H2 tank
was used as an H2 source for simplicity. The focus of the work was the evaluation of PEMFC performance
in removing O2 from the flue gas. We started with a 5 cm2 PEMFC with a Pt cathode, a PtRu anode, and a
Nafion HP15 membrane. The flow rate of the simulated flue gas with 8% O2 was calculated to meet the
flow rate requirement of the formic acid electrolyzer. The flow rate of H2 on the anode side was set to
match the flow rate of the flue gas on the cathode side. The operation temperatures and other conditions
were investigated and set up to achieve the best PEMFC performance. O 2 concentration was analyzed
with gas chromatography (GC). The results showed that the 5 cm 2 PEMFC removed around half of O2 (~4%
O2 remained in the flue gas) from the flue gas under the optimized conditions. We then decided to use a
25 cm2 PEMFC to improve the O2 removal performance. All the other conditions were the same as the 5
cm2 PEMFC. Figure 9b shows the polarization curve of the 25 cm2 PEMFC. The maximum current was 1.8A,
limited by the amount of O2 in the flue gas fed into the cathode side of the fuel cell. The relation between
fuel cell current and O2 concentration was also investigated, and the result is shown in Figure 9c. The O2
concentration decreased with the increase in current. At the maximum current of 1.8A, an O 2
concentration of 0.66% was obtained. Lower than 1% O2 concentration could also be obtained at 1.7A fuel
current. We then ran the 25 cm2 PEMFC for 150 hours and investigated the O2 removal performance. The
O2 removal results are shown in Figure 9d. As can be seen, using a 25 cm2 PEMFC could significantly
remove O2 from the flue gas. For 150 hours, the remaining O2 concentration in the flue gas after 25 cm 2
PEMFC was mostly under 1%, which meets the O2 concentration limit requirement for the formic acid
electrolyzer operation. These results demonstrated the feasibility of using PEMFCs to remove O 2 in the
flue gas from the coal-fired power plant. The results have met Milestone 1.3.1 where oxygen
concentration was reduced from 8-10% to <5% in the flue gas from coal-powered power plants. Originally,
we proposed to feed H2 produced from the formic acid electrolyzer to the PEMFC. One disadvantage of
this method is that a trace amount of CO produced in the formic acid electrolyzer will also be fed into the
PEMFC. CO is a well-known poisoning species for PEMFC, which is also proved by the rapidly declining
PEMFC performance when feeding H2/CO into the anode side of the PEMFC in this work. This has been

 17

proved by the drastic fuel cell performance decline with a trace amount of CO in the feeding gas. Therefore,
a separated H2 source is preferred if the PEMFC method is used for O 2 removal.

We then explored the second method with an O2 pump for O2 removal. We have demonstrated the
feasibility of this method for O2 removal in a previous project funded by NETL. Specifically, a MEA
electrolyzer with PtRu cathode, an IrO2 anode, and a Nafion HP15 membrane were used for this purpose.
As shown in Figure 10a, the flue gas was fed into the cathode side, and O2 was reduced to water on the
cathode side, then the flow gas with a lower O 2 concentration was fed into the formic acid electrolyzer.
Figure 10b is the performance of a 25 cm2 O2 pump for O2 removal for 100 hours of operation. The 25 cm2
O2 pump was operated under room temperature and 1.8 A cell current. The cell voltage was around 1.2 V
and stable during operation. The flue gas was fed into the cathode side and DI water was circulated on
the anode side. O2 concentration was analyzed by GC. As shown in Figure 10b, the O2 pump could
significantly reduce O2 concentration to less than 1% in the flue gas and shows stable performance. The
advantage of O2 pump compared with PEMFCs is the simpler system and operation requirements,
specifically, without H2 involved. Although O2 concentration can be reduced to under 1%, an O2 scavenger
is still desired to ensure a stable minimum O2 concentration in the flue gas. We built an O2 scavenger
column and tested the O2 removal performance of the integrated O2 pump and O2 scavenger system.
Figure 10c shows the O2 concentrations with only O2 pump and O2 pump + O2 scavenger. It is clear that
the O2 concentration decreased from 8.6% to 0.6% if an O2 pump was applied. We then run a long-term
O2 removal test using an O2 pump + scavenger system. The O2 concentration was around 0.2% for 70 hours,
then increased to above 0.5% and stabilized, as shown in Figure 10d. The O2 scavenger uses an iron-based
material to react with O2, the performance drops once all the active materials are reacted. Therefore,
scavenger replacement is needed to keep the O2 removal performance if a lower O2 concentration (~0.2%
vs. ~0.6%) is required. These results demonstrate that we have met Milestone 1.3.1: Reduction of the O2
concentration in the flue gas from 8-10% seen in the flue gas from a coal-fired power plant to <5%.

2.2 Engineering algae strains
Traditional algal cultivation relies on gaseous CO2 delivery, which suffers from low carbon utilization
efficiency (CUE) due to gas-to-liquid mass transfer limitations and significant off-gassing. To overcome
these hurdles, this project employs a paradigm-shifting approach: using a CO2 electrolyzer to produce
water-soluble formic acid as a stable intermediate carbon source. Formic acid is completely miscible in
water, enabling nearly 100% carbon transfer efficiency. The project utilizes Picochlorum renovo, a highly
productive, halophilic, and thermotolerant microalga. While wild-type (WT) P. renovo cannot natively
utilize formate, preliminary work demonstrated that heterologous expression of a formate
dehydrogenase (FDH) enzyme enables growth on formate. The project focused on genetically and
evolutionarily optimizing the formate utilization to support economic viability.

2.2.1 Genetic Engineering and FDH Screening

Figure 11. FDH Screening for Optimal Bioconversion. A series of diverse FDH were incorporated into P. renovo and

The objective is to genetically modify a recombinant P. renovo strain to improve formate utilization rate
and efficiency. To this end, the NLR team first initiated the construction of a series of formate
dehydrogenase (FDH) expression cassettes for incorporation into P. renovo leveraging previously
established genetic tools. We have successfully designed, synthesized, and assembled FDH integration
cassettes from 16 species of bacteria with known formate utilization capacity.

The NLR team incorporated the FDH cassettes into P. renovo and initiated high-throughput screening
thereof in 24-well cultivation plates. Strains were screened at increasing concentration of formate in the
presence of 2% CO2 to identify strains with improved formate tolerance. Notably, wild-type P. renovo
cannot survive in the presence of 10 mM, but our team has identified engineered strains of P. renovo that
can survive in 75 mM formate (Figure 11).

We next measured the growth of the top-candidate modified strains of P. renovo using formate as a
carbon and energy source in the presence of ambient CO2. (Figure 12). Wild type grows poorly on ambient

 19

CO2 and does not grow in the presence of formate. Conversely, several engineered strains demonstrated
robust growth capacity using formate as a primary carbon and energy source. These strains were then
further characterized to enable down-selection to the final chassis. We also performed Ribosomal Binding
Site (RBS) tuning to optimize expression levels. Five unique RBSs were tested to span five orders of
magnitude of translation rates, identifying the minimum rate needed to maximize formate uptake.

Additionally, we assessed the impact of pH upon formate transport, finding that low pH cultivation
dramatically enhances formate utilization (Figure 13). Engineered P. renovo consumes formic acid, causing
the pH of the growth media to increase upon consumption. To this end, we designed a pH control system

integrated with the photobioreactor to maintain constant pH using formic acid as a buffering agent. To
mitigate this pH increase, additional formic acid can be added to lower the pH. To automate this process,
we have integrated pH controllers, interfacing with a peristaltic pump, and appropriate process controllers
to maintain a setpoint pH in photobioreactors (Figure 14). As formic acid is consumed, it is automatically
replenished in this system, enabling continued algal cultivation on formic acid. The resultant system leads
to near-static pH and co-optimized substrate delivery and consumption rates. The resultant data will
inform planned cultivation regimes at AzCATI testbed in outdoor deployment.

Strain engineering efforts then focused on the Adaptive Laboratory Evolution (ALE) of P. renovo to

The ALE process yielded significant physiological improvements in the engineered variants compared to

  Growth Rate: Observations indicated a 30–50% enhancement in growth rates over the original

  Carbon Utilization: The evolution process dramatically improved the carbon utilization rate,

  Comparative Analysis: While the WT strain displayed no capacity for growth or formate utilization

To complement the ALE results, we integrated top-candidate formate transporters into the evolved P.
renovo strains. This dual approach aimed to concurrently maximize 1) Formate Transport: Improving the
uptake of the substrate into the cell, and 2) Formate Conversion: Enhancing the enzymatic oxidation of
formate to CO2 via the optimized FDH. This work involved the isolation and genomic resequencing of these
evolved strains to identify specific mutations, providing hypothesis-driven targets for further efficiency
improvements.

The ALE results successfully fulfilled the requirements of Milestone 2.1.2, achieving >75% formate
utilization efficiency through the cultivation of the FDH variant (utilizing an optimized ribosomal binding
site) under increasing formate concentrations for a duration exceeding 30 days. Following the successful
completion of the ALE phase at NLR, the top-performing candidate strains were transferred to the AzCATI
deployment testbed to initiate large-scale cultivation trials and evaluate performance in a simulated
production environment.

Algix completed the compositional analysis of the algal samples received from AzCATI. A complete
compositional review of the P. Renovo samples received was conducted to identify what concerns if any
may exist with the processing of the P. Renovo biomass from the novel growth conditions exhibited in this
project work. The results were listed in Table 2. The biomass was first dried to assess moisture content
and identify how much free moisture was available.

The moisture of the material was already quite low at around 3.7% to 3.85% so the drying that took place
was limited and took some time to accomplish. The low moisture content received is of great benefit to
BLOOM commercial interests and makes this very promising material for large scale production. Though
the low moisture content is beneficial, its largest generates benefits to processing and compound
manufacturing and does not translate to material specifications of the finished compound.

So other more critical compositional information was tested including protein content and ash content.
Protein content is the main driver for plastic-like behavior, and ash contributes to filler like behavior. So
desirable materials tend to have a higher protein content than ash and have sufficient protein and ash
contents such that any remaining content is not likely to be attributed to non-structural carbohydrates
such as starch, alginates, glycogen, and others. These non-structural carbohydrates tend to break down
at lower temperatures and produce sugars as breakdown products which then undergo Maillard or
Caramelization reactions that make protein-rich portions of the biomass behave less plastic-like. These
sugar byproducts can form agglomerates or other destructive domains within plastics that can deposit in
plastic processing equipment, diminish plastic properties, or even prevent plastic processing in some
applications. As such a favorable algae biomass for plastic processing will be dominated by protein and
ash with a ratio of protein and ash that strongly favors protein.

 25

Protein content for the samples received ranged from 43.5% to 46% of the composition a very promising
result. Protein contents in algae can reach as high as 50 to 60% but these results often only occur under
very controlled conditions. Most algae we have sourced ranges from 35 to 45% and this exists on the high
end of that range making it very favorable.

Ash content of the samples received ranged from 19.5 to 23.5% ash. These results fall within the middle
of the typical range of algae ash contents, which ranges from 15 to 30% ash. These results are not good
from the general expectations of available algae supplies, but are quite good given that this is a saline
solution grown algae. These results indicate that we can expect that much of the expected salt has been
removed through the processing of the biomass prior to our receipt of the material, such that problems
associated with salt are likely to be diminished. Salt can contribute to property loss via beta-scission of
polymers and can contribute to increases in moisture uptake of the plastic parts, which can contribute to
property loss and processing challenges. Other salt-related problems may also develop, such as machine
corrosion and others, but the previously described problems are the most likely when working with salt-
rich biomasses.

Outside of compositional info directed at material property performance, other categories of algae
qualities exist that impact performance such as odor and plasticization potential. Both of these qualities
may be in some cases related to material properties but also impact other facets of the material such as
processing characteristics, and consumer experience.

A good algae biomass will have as little odor as possible with minimal breakdown products associated
with thermal and biological degradation. This is to ensure not only that the material has been properly
handled to ensure natural polymer integrity is maintained, but also to ensure malodorous compounds do
not develop that are difficult to remove and make algae plastics unacceptable in commercial applications.
The human olfactory response is especially sensitive to odors related to anaerobic breakdown as it triggers
instinctual aversions to food rot. So if these odors are created the removal of them to extremely low levels
is necessary to avoid consumer aversion and is impractical in most if not all applications to achieve. So
odor is a key criterion for the suitability of an algae biomass in bioplastic composite usage. Odor is tested
via GCMS analysis to qualitatively identify the compounds present and determine the total count and
offensiveness of compounds present. Samples tested were found to contain 20 to 40 positively identified
odor compounds with around 3 of them being malodorous. This is well below typical threshold cutoffs
and indicates the algae was properly harvested and dried to prevent the generation of odorous
compounds and does not represent a significant odor formation risk.

Algae plasticization is also an important characteristic of algae predicting its behavior as either a polymer
or a filler. The plasticization also predicts the potential for the material to be able to change dimensions
while being processed allowing it to be better suited for more profitable markets such as fibers and films.
Algae was pretreated to allow for thermal denaturation of algal proteins and then was exposed to heat
and pressure on a compression molder to assess the materials suitability for plasticization. If the material
is readily able to be plasticized in these conditions it will form a uniform piece with no indication of layers
or layering and will have some plastic like behavior on break allowing for some bend without immediate
breaking. If it is not readily plasticized it will be simply a compressed layered structure of biomass which
doesn’t form a consistent substrate and which upon bending or even handling will immediately fracture.

 26

The P. Renovo samples tested are shown below (Figure 20) which have formed very consistent substrates
indicating at least a minimum desired quality of plasticization has occurred, but the fracturing of the parts

The algae biomass to products conversion process shown in Figure 25 was simulated in Aspen Plus v14

2.5.3 Techno-economic analysis
Both the algae cultivation and conversion models yield the outputs needed to populate the economic
model in Microsoft Excel, such as product flow rates as well as material and energy usages. This work
employed a discounted cash flow analysis to calculate two main economic metrics: the minimum biomass
selling price (MBSP) obtained in the algae farm and the net present value (NPV) obtained in the biorefining
portion. The NPV reported herein valorizes the FAME-rich stream obtained after transesterification of
lipids and separation of PUFA components for NIPU production being sold at an assumed price of
$2.25/GGE, alongside the NIPU coproduct sold at a price of $5.93/kg for full price polyurethane and
$2.97/kg for scrap polyurethane (accounting for around 4% of NIPU production), while the solid
thermoplastic coproduct is assumed to sell at a market value of approximately $0.90/kg. Standard
economic assumptions include an assumed 10% internal rate of return (IRR), 40% equity rate with a 10-
year loan, 21% income tax rate, a 30-year plant lifetime, and uses 2020 dollars to report results. In the

conventional algae farm, algae biomass is the only product of the facility; in formate-integrated systems,
the coproducts from the electrolyzer stack are sold at $0.23/kg (CO), $1.57/kg (H2), and $0.04/kg (O2) [6].

1) Algae cultivation
The MBSPs estimated with the baseline framework (i.e., with the direct use of CO 2 as the carbon source
for algae growth, as depicted in Figure 24A) are $719/ton AFDW and $775/ton AFDW for HC and HP
compositional profiles, respectively. Since the two cases consider the same values for the most critical
algae farm parameters, such as average biomass productivity and algae farm size, the differences between
these MBSPs are strictly a function of biomass composition: the cost component associated with nutrients
in HP biomass production is larger than in HC algae farms due to a higher need for nitrogen and
phosphorus compounds in the former rather than in the latter.

The use of an electrolysis unit integrated with the algae farm (Figure 24B) increases capital costs by
roughly $620M, with the electrolyzer stack accounting for around 65% of this total and ancillary
equipment and PSA unit responsible for 18% and 17% of the costs, respectively. Electricity requirements
also increase substantially in this algae farm configuration, moving from around 12 MW in the baseline
framework to nearly 300 MW in the integrated approach. Electricity demand in the electrolysis unit
amounts to between 220-245 MW, while the PSA unit required for coproduct separation requires an
additional 43-48 MW (depending on the biomass compositional profile). Besides converting CO 2 to
formate, the electrolysis section is also able to yield CO, H2, and O2 as coproducts, which are sold to the
market to generate coproduct credits in the analysis. This credit amounts to $80-90/ton AFDW based on
the biomass compositional profile of interest. Under this approach, the formate-based MBSPs determined
for the integrated framework are $1,654/ton AFDW and $1,623/ton AFDW for HC and HP compositional
profiles, respectively.

The calculated increase of around $900/ton AFDW for the formate-integrated case is a direct result of the
significant increase in capital costs and operational expenses (namely electricity) brought by the
electrolysis operation as previously detailed. This gap can be further reduced with potential changes in
the technological landscape, such as lower electricity prices, improvements in the performance of the
electrolysis unit, or a combination thereof. Figure 26 quantifies the impact of such technological
developments on the MBSP of both conventional and formate-integrated cases. While numbers vary
slightly depending on the chosen compositional profile, the trend is the same independently of this
parameter. Reducing electricity prices from $0.07/kWh to $0.03/kWh would lower MBSPs for the
formate-integrated case by around $450/ton AFDW, from $1,654-1623 to $1,164-1179/ton AFDW. If
electricity rates are kept at $0.07/kWh, then considering improved parameters for the electrolysis of CO 2
instead reduces MBSPs by around $210-230/ton AFDW, thus highlighting that electricity requirements
(and prices) are the most impactful parameter in this approach. Finally, combining both improvements
together would yield MBSPs close to $1,000/ton AFDW for the formate-integrated farm, more closely
approaching MBSP parity with the conventional approach (i.e., using CO2 directly as the carbon source for
algae growth) of around $700/ton AFDW.

More economically competitive results could also potentially be obtained if a biorefinery that effectively
adds value to algae-derived carbohydrates were employed. For example, fermentable carbohydrates
present in the HC profile could be selectively deconstructed and bioconverted to high-value products in
the biorefinery, such as carboxylic acids (adipic acid or acrylic acid, for example), thus adding an additional
revenue strain to the processing plant; however, this would not alter the economic trends between
formate versus CO2 utilization as the cultivation carbon source. However, the formate-based approach
could also become more competitive if it could be shown to increase algae biomass productivity arising
from the uptake of formate instead of CO2. While this has not been identified yet, it could be a strategy
for effectively reducing MBSPs/improving NPVs as areal biomass productivity is the single-largest metric
affecting the economics of algae farms [4].
