Context-dependent roles of lncRNA JPX in human cancers
Authors: Yongzhong Li, Tongtong Li, Jiayi Li, Yirong Su, Yupu Liu, Lihan Chen, Yining Pan, Jiayi Ma, Ting Gong, Huaming Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundJPX is a long non-coding RNA involved in X-chromosome inactivation through regulation of XIST. In cancer research, its reported role varies across tumor types and is often discussed in separate mechanistic axes. This review re-examines the current literature with emphasis on context dependence, the JPX–XIST relationship, evidence strength, and translational caution.MethodsPubMed was searched up to November 2025 using combinations of “JPX” or “Jpx” with “lncRNA,” “XIST,” “cancer,” “tumor,” “carcinoma,” and tumor-specific terms. Peer-reviewed studies that evaluated JPX expression, function, or clinical relevance in cancer were included.ResultsAvailable evidence indicates that JPX has context-dependent roles in human cancers. In lung cancer, esophageal squamous cell carcinoma, oral squamous cell carcinoma, osteosarcoma, cervical cancer, gastric cancer, ovarian cancer, glioblastoma, head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, and endometrial cancer, JPX has mainly been reported in oncogenic settings. Proposed ceRNA-like mechanisms were common in these tumors. In hepatocellular carcinoma, breast cancer, and uveal melanoma, the published findings were more consistent with XIST-related tumor-restraining programs. The evidence remains uneven. Many reported mechanisms came from single studies, limited model systems, or lacked independent validation.ConclusionJPX should not be regarded as a uniformly oncogenic lncRNA. Its role appears to depend on tumor context, the dominant downstream program, and, in some settings, the status of the JPX–XIST axis. JPX is better viewed as a candidate biomarker and an experimental target than as a clinically validated one.

Introduction

Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) are transcripts longer than 200 nucleotides. They are not translated into proteins, but they can regulate chromatin state, transcription, RNA processing, and signaling networks [1–3]. Many lncRNAs show tissue-specific expression and participate in cancer-related pathways. Because of this, they are widely discussed as candidate biomarkers and experimental therapeutic targets. JPX is of particular interest because it lies upstream of XIST and has been implicated in XIST activation during X-chromosome inactivation [4, 5].

JPX has been reported to be dysregulated in at least 14 cancer contexts, including lung cancer, esophageal squamous cell carcinoma, oral squamous cell carcinoma, osteosarcoma, cervical cancer, gastric cancer, ovarian cancer, glioblastoma, colorectal cancer, head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, endometrial cancer, hepatocellular carcinoma, breast cancer, and uveal melanoma [6–29]. The current literature is heterogeneous. Some studies included perturbation and rescue experiments. Others were limited to expression analysis, risk modeling, or pathway prediction. The reported functional direction of JPX is also not uniform across tumor types.

This narrative review focused on peer-reviewed studies that directly examined JPX expression, function, or clinical relevance in human cancers. Greater attention was given to studies with mechanistic validation, especially gain- and loss-of-function assays, rescue experiments, in vivo support, and direct clinical analyses. Studies based mainly on expression profiling, prognostic modeling, or in silico prediction were also included when they helped define the overall landscape, but their conclusions were interpreted more cautiously.

The available literature on JPX remains fragmented and is largely organized by tumor type. For clarity, this review follows the same structure. The purpose is not simple listing. Each subsection considers four points: the reported functional direction of JPX, the representative mechanistic model, the status of XIST involvement, and the main limitations affecting interpretation. On this basis, the review further discusses cross-cancer patterns, differences in evidence strength, and the context-dependent relevance of the JPX–XIST axis (Fig. 1; Table 2).

Mechanisms and functional roles of JPX in human malignancies

The available literature on JPX remains highly fragmented across tumor types. For this reason, the following sections are organized by cancer context. This structure is not used simply to summarize individual studies. It is meant to compare how JPX behaves in different biological settings, how strongly the proposed mechanisms are supported, whether XIST was directly examined, and how far the current data can be interpreted in translational terms. In many cancers, the reported pathways came from single studies or limited model systems. They are therefore described here as reported or proposed mechanisms unless broader validation is available.

Lung cancer (LC)

Lung cancer (LC) remains a major cause of cancer-related death worldwide [30–32]. In this setting, JPX has mainly been reported in an oncogenic context. Several JPX-centered pathways have been described, including JPX/miR-33a-5p/Twist1 with Wnt/β-catenin activation, a JPX-associated miR-378a-3p ceRNA network in lung adenocarcinoma, JPX/miR-5195-3p/VEGFA, and JPX/miR-145-5p/CCND2 [6–9]. These reported mechanisms are not identical, but the phenotypic findings are broadly similar. JPX was linked to proliferation, migration, invasion, metastasis, and aggressive clinicopathologic features.

These pathways are better viewed as representative models reported by individual studies rather than as a uniformly validated regulatory network. The overall phenotypic trend is more consistent than the pathway hierarchy. XIST was not directly examined in the cited LC studies (Fig. 2).

Esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC)

Esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) is a common malignancy worldwide and has a high incidence in East Asia [33–36]. In the available study, JPX was upregulated in ESCC tissues relative to adjacent normal tissues and was associated with T4 or N1 disease. Mechanistic analysis suggested that JPX may promote proliferation, migration, invasion, angiogenesis, and EMT-related changes through the miR-516b-5p/VEGFA axis [10]. The current evidence remains limited. It is based mainly on a single mechanistic study. Survival analysis was not developed in depth, and independent validation is lacking. XIST was not directly examined in the cited ESCC study. ESCC is better viewed as a functionally supported but clinically early context for JPX.

Oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC)

Oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) is a common malignant tumor of the oral cavity [37]. The available evidence supports an oncogenic association of JPX in OSCC, but the data came from different study types. Functional experiments supported a proposed JPX/miR-944/CDH2 axis and were consistent with a more invasive phenotype. In a separate line of work, JPX was included in a pyroptosis-related lncRNA prognostic signature, in which higher JPX expression was associated with poorer overall survival and higher pathological grade [11, 12].

These findings should not be treated as the same level of evidence, because the mechanistic study and the prognostic model were developed independently. In this setting, the evidence for JPX may be regarded as moderate. XIST was not directly assessed in the cited OSCC studies.

Osteosarcoma (OS)

Osteosarcoma (OS) is a malignant bone tumor that mainly affects children and young adults [38, 39]. In OS, JPX has mainly been reported in an oncogenic setting. The available literature described a proposed JPX/miR-33a-5p/PNMA1 axis and, in a separate study, a JPX-associated Wnt/β-catenin signaling context that may also be linked to the response to melatonin [13, 14].

These studies point to similar phenotypic effects, as JPX was associated with proliferation, migration, and invasion. The pathway hierarchy, however, remains unclear. JPX in OS is better regarded as a biologically relevant but incompletely defined factor rather than a well-established therapeutic target. Its prognostic value is also unclear. Broader in vivo confirmation is needed, and XIST was not directly examined in the cited OS studies.

Cervical cancer (CC)

Cervical cancer (CC) remains a major malignancy in women [40, 41]. The reported data are consistent with an oncogenic role of JPX in CC. The reported study identified a JPX/miR-25-3p/SOX4 axis and linked JPX to increased proliferation, migration, and invasion in CC cells. When JPX was silenced, xenograft growth and Ki-67 expression were reduced. These effects were partly reversed by miR-25-3p inhibition [15].

Compared with several other tumor types, the mechanistic support in CC is relatively stronger, because functional assays, rescue experiments, and in vivo validation were included. The clinical evidence is less developed. Survival data were not established in depth, and the reported clinical relevance was inferred mainly from tissue expression and clinicopathologic association. XIST was not directly assessed in the cited CC study.

Gastric cancer (GC)

Gastric cancer (GC) remains an important cause of cancer mortality worldwide [42–44]. In GC, JPX has mainly been reported in an oncogenic context. The available study suggested that high JPX expression and low miR-197 expression were associated with poorer survival. Mechanistic assays also supported a proposed JPX/miR-197/CXCR6 axis with autophagy-related effects [16]. In GC cells, JPX knockdown reduced cell viability, migration, and invasion, and these effects were partly reversed by miR-197 inhibition.

This model remains preliminary. Most of the evidence came from in vitro experiments, and the broader biological relevance of the JPX–CXCR6–autophagy link has not been independently validated. XIST was not directly examined in the cited GC study. GC is better viewed as a functionally suggestive but intermediate-evidence context for JPX.

Ovarian cancer (OV)

Ovarian cancer (OV) ranks as one of the most aggressive and deadliest forms in gynecologic malignancies [45, 46]. In OV, JPX has been reported to be associated with increased proliferation and migration, reduced apoptosis, and activation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway [17]. Consistent changes were also described in apoptosis-related markers, including increased Bcl-2, decreased Bax, and weaker caspase-associated signals. These findings suggest that JPX biology in OV may extend beyond a simple ceRNA-like model.

The upstream molecular link between JPX and this kinase cascade remains unclear, and the clinical evidence is limited. The available OV study is consistent with an oncogenic association of JPX, but the mechanism is not yet fully resolved. XIST was not assessed in the cited OV study.

Glioblastoma multiforme (GBM)

Glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) is an aggressive brain tumor with poor prognosis, and temozolomide resistance remains a major therapeutic challenge [47, 48]. JPX was reported to be upregulated in GBM tissues and cell lines, and higher JPX expression was associated with poorer prognosis. Functional studies linked JPX to increased proliferation, glycolysis, temozolomide resistance, and DNA repair, while apoptosis was reduced. Mechanistically, JPX was reported to interact with the m6A demethylase FTO and to stabilize PDK1 mRNA, thereby forming a proposed JPX/FTO/PDK1 axis [18].

Compared with many ceRNA-centered reports, this study offers relatively deeper mechanistic support because it links JPX with RNA stability, metabolic reprogramming, and treatment resistance. The translational relevance remains preliminary. Broader validation is needed, and the value of JPX or the JPX/FTO/PDK1 module in patient stratification has not been defined. XIST was not directly examined in the cited GBM study.

Colorectal cancer (CRC)

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a common malignancy and remains a major public health problem [49, 50]. In CRC, JPX was reported to be upregulated relative to adenomatous colon polyps, and ROC analysis suggested moderate discrimination between these two groups, whereas the distinction between tumor and marginal tissues was weaker [19]. No significant association was found between JPX expression and clinicopathologic features. In silico analysis predicted interactions between JPX and several miRNAs, with enrichment in pathways related to Wnt signaling, apoptosis regulation, protein phosphorylation, and FOXO and ErbB signaling.

These findings remain mainly descriptive and prediction-based. CRC is better regarded as a hypothesis-generating rather than a mechanistically established context for JPX. XIST was not directly assessed in the cited CRC study.

Head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC)

Head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) is a common malignant tumor. It remains a major clinical challenge [51, 52]. In this cancer type, JPX was reported to be upregulated and to be associated with a proposed miR-193b-3p/PLAU axis [20]. Functional assays showed that JPX overexpression promoted proliferation, migration, and invasion, whereas JPX knockdown suppressed these malignant phenotypes. The study also suggested that JPX may contribute to epithelial–immune crosstalk through PLAU–PLAUR signaling and may therefore have relevance to the tumor microenvironment.

This model is of interest, but the current evidence still relies mainly on a single study. Independent validation is needed. The reported survival association is suggestive, yet the biomarker value of JPX remains uncertain. XIST was not directly examined in the cited HNSCC study.

Endometrial cancer (EC)

Endometrial cancer (EC) is a common gynecologic malignancy, and dysregulated proliferation contributes to its progression [53]. Available data are consistent with an oncogenic role of JPX in EC. JPX was reported to be elevated in EC tissues, with higher expression in late-stage disease. Functional assays showed that JPX silencing reduced cell viability, colony formation, and migration, while apoptosis increased and cell cycle arrest was induced. Mechanistic experiments supported a proposed JPX/miR-140-3p/PIK3CA axis with downstream JAK2/STAT3 activation, and partial rescue effects were observed after miR-140-3p inhibition or PIK3CA overexpression [21].

Compared with many other tumor types, EC provides relatively stronger functional support for JPX. The clinical evidence remains preliminary and requires validation in larger independent cohorts. XIST was not directly assessed in the cited EC study.

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC)

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is a major type of liver cancer [54]. AFP remains widely used for HCC detection, although its sensitivity and specificity are limited, especially in early disease [55]. Unlike many JPX-high tumors, HCC is more consistent with a tumor-restraining JPX–XIST context. In tumor tissues, JPX and XIST were both reported to be downregulated relative to matched non-cancerous tissues, and lower expression was associated with higher histological grade, more advanced stage, and poorer overall survival [24]. Mechanistic findings suggested that JPX increased XIST expression in HCC cells, whereas XIST suppressed HepG2 proliferation through the miR-155-5p/SOX6/PTEN pathway [22]. A separate study further proposed that exosomal JPX released from HCC cells may promote XIST transcription in circulating blood cells by relieving CTCF-mediated repression [23].

HCC represents one of the clearest XIST-supported contexts for JPX. The biological meaning of this axis, however, appears to differ by sampling compartment. Tissue-based and blood-based findings should therefore be interpreted separately.

Breast cancer (BC)

Breast cancer (BC) is the most commonly diagnosed cancer in women worldwide and remains a major cause of cancer-related death [56–60]. In BC, the available data are more consistent with a tumor-restraining JPX–XIST program than with a ceRNA-like oncogenic model. JPX has been reported to regulate XIST transcription through interaction with CTCF and enhancement of XIST promoter activity [61]. In line with this upstream relationship, reduced XIST expression was associated with increased AKT phosphorylation, and a positive correlation between JPX and XIST expression was also observed [62]. Mechanistic analysis further suggested that reduced XIST promoted HDAC3 recruitment to the PHLPP1 promoter, which lowered PHLPP1 expression and enhanced AKT activation [25].

These findings are consistent with a functionally relevant JPX–XIST link in BC. The overall dataset remains limited, and broader validation across molecular subtypes is still needed.

Uveal melanoma (UM)

Uveal melanoma (UM) is an intraocular tumor derived from uveal melanocytes and is associated with a high risk of metastasis [63]. In the reported study, JPX was placed within a higher-order lncRNA regulatory cascade rather than a simple ceRNA-like model. CANT1 acted as a tumor-suppressive lncRNA, and its overexpression inhibited migration, metastasis, and colony formation. These effects were linked to activation of a CANT1–JPX/FTX–XIST cascade, because silencing JPX or FTX blocked XIST reactivation and partially reversed the inhibitory phenotype [26].

These findings place UM within another XIST-related context for JPX. The current evidence remains limited in breadth, and the clinical significance of this pathway has not been established.

Cross-cancer patterns and critical appraisal of the evidence

A ceRNA-dominant pattern has been reported in several JPX-high tumors. Different downstream miRNAs were reported across cancer types, but this does not necessarily indicate contradiction. A more cautious interpretation is that JPX may act as a context-dependent regulatory hub, with molecular partners that vary across cellular settings. In this respect, the recurrent association between high JPX expression and pro-tumor phenotypes appears more consistent than the identity of any single downstream axis.

For a more consistent interpretation, the current literature may be viewed along three dimensions: mechanistic depth, in vivo support, and clinical maturity. Mechanistic support may be regarded as limited when the evidence is mainly descriptive, prediction-based, or confined to a narrow in vitro setting. It may be regarded as moderate when functional assays support a proposed pathway but external validation is lacking. Support becomes relatively stronger when rescue experiments, in vivo data, or more integrated mechanistic analyses are available. Under this framework, CC and glioblastoma provide relatively stronger mechanistic contexts, whereas CRC remains largely descriptive and hypothesis-generating. Many other tumor types lie between these two ends.

Clinical maturity remains limited in most cancers discussed here. Associations with survival, stage, or diagnostic performance often came from single cohorts and rarely included external validation or multivariable adjustment. For this reason, such findings are better interpreted as preliminary rather than confirmatory.

Several limitations recur across the JPX literature. Many studies were based on relatively small cohorts, often from a single center, and independent validation was uncommon. Multivariable analyses were limited in studies that proposed prognostic or diagnostic relevance. Mechanistic conclusions also relied heavily on in vitro systems, while in vivo confirmation and clinically relevant validation were insufficient in many settings. These issues should be kept in mind when JPX is discussed as a biomarker candidate or experimental target.

Therapeutic interpretation also requires caution. A pathway identified in one or two cell lines does not establish targetability by itself. This point is important for lncRNAs, because their functions may depend on subcellular localization, molecular partners, and lineage-specific chromatin context. The current JPX literature is therefore more informative for biological interpretation and hypothesis generation than for immediate clinical translation.

The JPX–XIST axis as a context-dependent central theme

JPX was originally characterized in the context of X-chromosome inactivation and XIST regulation, rather than as a broadly defined cancer-associated lncRNA [4, 64]. This background makes JPX different from many other cancer-related lncRNAs. Its role in cancer appears to depend not only on JPX expression itself, but also on whether the JPX–XIST relationship remains biologically active in a given tumor setting (Fig. 3).

The current literature does not support the JPX–XIST relationship at the same level across cancers. In HCC and UM, experimental findings directly support an XIST-centered regulatory program. In BC, the available data are also consistent with a functionally relevant JPX–XIST link, although the overall evidence base remains limited. In several JPX-high cancers, including LC, ESCC, OSCC, OS, GC, OV, glioblastoma, CRC, HNSCC, and EC, XIST was not directly examined in the cited mechanistic studies. In these settings, the relevance of the JPX–XIST axis should be viewed as unresolved or not assessed rather than experimentally established.

This distinction improves conceptual clarity. In HCC, BC, and UM, the available findings are more compatible with an upstream or cooperative role of JPX in an XIST-centered tumor-restraining network. In many JPX-high epithelial tumors, the published work mainly described proposed ceRNA-like oncogenic mechanisms and did not clarify the status of XIST. Lack of direct evidence for XIST involvement should not be taken as evidence of absence, but it does limit how strongly the JPX–XIST axis can be invoked in those cancers.

Several factors may contribute to the divergent outcomes of the JPX–XIST axis across cancers. These include lineage-specific downstream effectors of XIST, sex-related biology, tumor stage, subcellular localization of JPX, and sampling compartment [65–68]. The discordance between blood and tissue findings in HCC is especially informative, because it suggests that JPX-related biomarkers may carry different biological meanings in tumor tissue, exosomes, and circulating blood cells. Future studies need to clarify where JPX changes occur, in which patient groups they appear, and whether the relevant downstream program is XIST-dependent, XIST-related but indirect, or XIST-unresolved.

Therapeutic potential and translational challenges

JPX is more appropriately viewed as an experimental target than as a clinically actionable one. In principle, lncRNAs may be targeted by antisense oligonucleotides, siRNA-based approaches, CRISPR interference, or strategies that disrupt key RNA–protein interactions [69–71]. The most suitable strategy for JPX is likely to depend on disease context. In tumors with high JPX expression and apparent oncogenic output, inhibitory approaches may be conceptually relevant. In tumors with low JPX expression and XIST-related tumor-restraining features, restoration of JPX activity may be biologically meaningful in principle. These possibilities remain hypothetical.

Several translational barriers remain substantial. Many reported JPX mechanisms were based on single downstream axes in limited model systems, so target selection may become overcommitted to pathways that do not generalize well. lncRNA function is also often localization-dependent. An expression change alone does not guarantee that a therapeutic intervention will reproduce the intended biological effect. Delivery is another practical challenge, especially in solid tumors and in central nervous system disease such as GBM.

Safety also deserves careful attention. Because JPX is linked to XIST and X-chromosome biology, on-target effects outside the tumor need to be considered, especially in sex-specific or tissue-specific settings. This does not exclude therapeutic development, but it does require mechanism-matched pharmacodynamic biomarkers, toxicology evaluation, and compartment-aware study design. In most cancers discussed here, the more immediate value of JPX is likely to lie in stratification and biology-guided hypothesis testing rather than near-term drug development.

Conclusion

Across tumor types, the available evidence does not support a uniform oncogenic role for JPX. Its biological effect appears to depend on the dominant downstream program in a given setting.

Interpretation of JPX remains incomplete without considering XIST. In HCC, BC, and UM, the available data are more consistent with a tumor-restraining or context-restrained JPX–XIST program. In many other cancers, the published literature focused on ceRNA-like oncogenic behavior and did not directly examine XIST. This gap needs closer attention in future work.

From a translational perspective, the clinical evidence is limited and preliminary. In most tumor types, JPX is closer to a candidate biomarker than to a validated one. The same is true for therapeutic targeting.
