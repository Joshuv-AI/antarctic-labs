The successful use of rituximab in children with IgA nephropathy and minimal change-like lesions: a case series
Authors: Yibo Cheng, Qian Fu, Xueying Cui, Manman Hu, Menglin Chang, Yeping Jiang, Hui Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIgA nephropathy with minimal change disease (IgAN-MCD) is a rare glomerulopathy combining features of both entities, typically presenting as steroid-sensitive nephrotic syndrome (NS) with frequent relapses. While rituximab (RTX) is commonly used in pediatric glomerular diseases, its efficacy in IgAN-MCD remains unclear. Our retrospective study evaluated the efficacy and safety of RTX in eight children with IgAN-MCD who remained steroid-dependent or frequently relapsed despite treatment with glucocorticoids and second-line immunosuppressants before RTX. Over a follow-up period of 12–41 months, RTX significantly reduced the annual relapse rate by 1.1 episodes per year, prolonged remission by 3.5 months, and extended the steroid-free interval by 7.4 months. Overall, 87.5% of patients successfully discontinued glucocorticoids, and 50% stopped immunosuppressants. Three patients relapsed at 9, 16, and 19 months, likely due to insufficient CD19+ B-cell monitoring and lack of consolidation dosing, yet all achieved sustained remission by the final follow-up. No serious RTX-related adverse events were observed. These findings suggest that RTX effectively reduces relapse, prolongs remission, and decreases dependence on steroids and immunosuppressants in pediatric IgAN-MCD, with a favorable safety profile for refractory cases.

Introduction

IgA nephropathy (IgAN) is the most common primary glomerulonephritis worldwide. Approximately, 15–20% of patients progress to end-stage renal disease within 10 years, and 30–40% within 20 years. The pathogenesis of IgAN is primarily associated with IgA deposition in the glomerular mesangium. A subset of IgAN patients presents with pathological features resembling minimal change disease (MCD), characterized by normal or minimally altered glomerular morphology under light microscopy (LM) and widespread foot process effacement under electron microscopy (EM). This distinct subtype is referred to as IgAN with MCD (IgAN-MCD) [1].

Compared with typical IgAN, IgAN-MCD more closely resembles MCD in its clinical presentation, often manifesting as nephrotic syndrome (NS) with sensitivity to glucocorticoids, with a tendency toward frequent relapses. According to the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) 2021 Clinical Practice Guideline for the Management of Glomerular Diseases, IgAN patients with a kidney biopsy demonstrating mesangial IgA deposition and light and EM features otherwise consistent with MCD should be treated in accordance with the guidelines for MCD [2]. However, these patients still face the challenge of steroid dependence or frequent relapses during immunosuppressive therapy, requiring long-term maintenance treatment with immunosuppressants [3,4].

In recent years, rituximab (RTX) has demonstrated therapeutic potential in various glomerular diseases. Previous studies have shown that RTX can reduce proteinuria in certain patients with IgAN [5]. In pediatric NS, RTX has been effective in maintaining long-term steroid-free remission [6–9]. Moreover, B-cell depletion therapy has been proposed as a promising alternative treatment strategy for patients with IgAN complicated by NS [10,11]. In adults, RTX has been successfully applied in IgAN-MCD, leading to reductions in proteinuria, fewer relapses, and facilitation of steroid tapering [12]. Emerging evidence suggests that the presence of circulating anti-nephrin antibodies in most children with IgAN-MCD suggests a podocyte injury mechanism similar to MCD, thereby supporting the rational use of RTX in this subgroup [13].

However, data on the use of RTX in pediatric patients with IgAN-MCD remain scarce, and no systematic studies have been conducted to evaluate its efficacy and safety in this specific population. Therefore, our study retrospectively analyzed the clinical course and outcomes of eight children with biopsy-confirmed IgAN-MCD who received RTX therapy. The objective was to preliminarily assess the treatment response and safety profile of RTX in this unique subgroup and provide evidence to guide individualized therapeutic strategies.

Materials and methods

Study population

This was a single-center retrospective cohort study. A total of eight pediatric patients diagnosed with IgAN-MCD and treated with RTX at our hospital. Inclusion criteria were as follows: age ≤18 years; pathologically confirmed IgAN-MCD based on renal biopsy; received at least one dose of RTX with complete follow-up data available; meets the definitions of frequent relapses and steroid dependence in the KDIGO 2025 Clinical Practice Guideline for the Management of Nephrotic Syndrome in Children [14]. The exclusion criteria were as follows: patients with secondary IgAN caused by systemic diseases such as systemic lupus erythematosus or Henoch–Schönlein purpura nephritis; those with active chronic infections (e.g., tuberculosis, hepatitis B/C); and patients who were lost to follow-up. Our study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Children’s Hospital, Capital Medical University, with informed consent waived (Approval No. 2025-E-120-R).

Diagnostic criteria

IgAN was diagnosed based on the presence of dominant or co-dominant mesangial IgA deposits with ≥1+ intensity on immunofluorescence (IF), electron-dense deposits in the mesangium under EM, and exclusion of secondary causes of IgAN.

IgAN-MCD was defined as meeting the diagnostic criteria for IgAN, with basically normal glomerular structure or mild mesangial hyperplasia observed under LM, accompanied by >80% foot process effacement on EM, consistent with MCD features [15,16].

Treatment protocol

Before RTX, routine inactivated vaccines (including influenza and pneumococcal vaccines) were recommended to be completed at least four weeks in advance, while live-attenuated vaccines were avoided during treatment. RTX was administered at a dose of 375 mg/m2 (maximum 500 mg), given one to four times as induction therapy until peripheral B-cell depletion was achieved, after which treatment was paused. During maintenance therapy, additional RTX doses were administered upon B-cell reconstitution, defined as a B-lymphocyte proportion >1% or an absolute count >5 cells/μL. Throughout RTX treatment, children received oral glucocorticoids in combination with mycophenolate mofetil (MMF). Oral trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole was routinely prescribed for prophylaxis against Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia. The specific treatment regimen was adjusted according to the children’s clinical response and lymphocyte subset counts.

Data collection

General information: age at onset, sex, disease duration before RTX, and renal pathological; laboratory parameters: serum albumin, serum creatinine, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), peripheral blood CD19+ B-cell levels, and 24-h urinary protein excretion before RTX treatment; treatment information: glucocorticoids and immunosuppressants use before and after RTX therapy, as well as the total dose and number of RTX infusions; efficacy evaluation: remission status, relapse frequency, duration of glucocorticoids discontinuation, and longest relapse-free interval; adverse events: including infections, allergy, and neutropenia.

Definitions

Steroid dependence: two consecutive relapses during recommended prednisone or prednisolone therapy for first presentation or relapse (either at full-dose or during tapering) or within 14 days of prednisone or prednisolone discontinuation;

Frequent relapses: ≥2 relapses in the first 6 months following remission of the initial episode or ≥3 relapses per 12 months in any subsequent 12-month period;

Complete remission: first morning urine or 24-h urine protein-to-creatinine ratio (uPCR) ≤200 mg/g (0.2 g/g or 20 mg/mmol or negative or trace dipstick or <100 mg/m2 per day) on three or more consecutive days.

Partial remission: first morning urine or 24-h uPCR >200 mg/g (0.2 g/g) but <2 g/g (or >20 and <200 mg/mmol).

Relapse: recurrence of nephrotic-range proteinuria in a child who had previously achieved complete remission, or urine dipstick ≥3+ for 3 consecutive days [14].

Follow-up

After RTX treatment, eight children were followed up for ≥12 months. Follow-up included at least the following items: Complete blood count and C-reactive protein; liver and kidney function; 24-h urine protein quantification; lymphocyte subset analysis; and records of relapse, use of glucocorticoids and immunosuppressants, and adverse events.

Renal histopathology

Renal biopsy specimens were evaluated using IF, LM, and EM. Staining methods included hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), periodic acid–Schiff (PAS), periodic acid–silver methenamine (PASM), and Masson’s trichrome. IF was used to assess IgG, IgM, IgA, C3, C4, C1q, and fibrin-related antigen deposition. EM was used to evaluate electron-dense deposits and foot process effacement.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using SPSS version 27.0 (Chicago, IL). Categorical variables were expressed as counts (n) or percentages (%). Continuous variables with a normal distribution were presented as mean ± standard deviation (x ± s), while non-normally distributed data were expressed as median and interquartile range (IQR) [M (P25, P75)]. Comparisons between pre- and post-treatment data were performed using paired t-tests or Wilcoxon’s signed-rank tests, as appropriate. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline clinical characteristics

Eight children with IgAN-MCD were enrolled, including six boys and two girls, with a median age at onset of 5.4 years (IQR, 2.9–7.1). At diagnosis, all presented with NS; five had frequent relapses and three had steroid dependence/frequent relapses.

Before RTX, the median disease duration was 59.1 months (IQR, 54.5–77.3). The median follow-up after RTX was 24.8 months (IQR, 16.1–34.8). All children had received glucocorticoids and at least one second-line immunosuppressant (tacrolimus, MMF, or cyclosporine, and so on).

Children received a median of four RTX infusions (IQR, 4–6), with a cumulative dose of 1,910 mg (IQR, 1,703–2,586). The median interval between doses was 5.4 months (IQR, 0.4–8.4). One patient received three doses, one 5 doses, one 6 doses, and one 8 doses; four patients each received four doses. To gain further insight into the clinical and pathological characteristics of refractory IgAN-MCD, we identified six children who achieved remission with initial immunosuppressive therapy and compared them with eight children in the RTX group. The findings suggest that, compared with the control group, the RTX group had a younger age at disease onset, a lower prevalence of hematuria, and less frequent M1 lesions according to the Oxford classification (Supplementary Tables 2 and 3).

Glucocorticoids and immunosuppressant use

After RTX therapy, seven children (87.5%) discontinued glucocorticoids; two of them restarted glucocorticoids after relapse, and one other patient was unable to stop glucocorticoids during the RTX treatment period. The median steroid-free period increased from 6.8 months (IQR, 1.0–11.9) before RTX to 13.4 months (IQR, 9.8–19.7) after RTX (p < 0.05), the average steroid-free duration was extended by 8.3 months (Table 3, Supplementary Table 4, Figure 2).

Relapse and remission

Following RTX treatment, three patients (37.5%) experienced relapse: patients 5 and 6 relapsed twice each, while patient 7 relapsed once. Importantly, none of these relapses met the criteria for steroid dependence or frequent relapses. The annual relapse number was significantly reduced, from 1.5 (1.0–1.5) episodes per year before RTX to 0 (0–0.7) episodes per year after RTX, the annual recurrence rate decreased by an average of 1.1 episodes per year (p < 0.05).

The longest relapse-free remission period was prolonged from 13.4 months (IQR, 10.4–17.5 months) before RTX to 17.5 months (IQR, 12.4–24.9 months) after RTX. Although the difference did not reach statistical significance (p > 0.05), seven out of eight patients (87.5%) exhibited a prolongation of the relapse-free interval, with a mean prolongation of 5.5 months (Figure 2).

Adverse events

None of the eight children experienced infusion reactions or serious adverse events during RTX infusion, and no serious complications such as hypogammaglobulinemia, neutropenia, and severe infection were observed during follow-up.

Discussion

Our study is the first to demonstrate the potential therapeutic value of RTX in pediatric patients with IgAN-MCD. A self-controlled comparison before and after RTX treatment revealed that RTX prolonged relapse-free intervals and steroid-free durations, reduced relapse frequency, and was safe. These findings suggest that RTX may represent a novel and effective therapeutic option for this rare and challenging pediatric population.

IgAN-MCD is a rare pathological subtype of IgAN characterized by overlapping features of both MCD and IgAN, first reported by Mustonen et al. [17]. It is more commonly observed in Asian populations than in European cohorts [18], with a reported incidence ranging from 0.6% to 7% among primary IgAN cases [4,15–19], and approximately 2.8% in children [20]. Clinically, IgAN-MCD resembles MCD, presenting predominantly with NS, initially steroid-sensitive but frequently relapsing or becoming steroid-dependent, thus often requiring combination immunosuppressive therapy. Despite these challenges, the long-term prognosis of IgAN-MCD appears more favorable than that of classic IgAN [16,21,22].

The pathogenesis of IgAN-MCD remains uncertain. One prevailing hypothesis considers it a podocytopathy subtype of IgAN [23], whereas another posits it as a dual glomerulopathy – an overlap of IgAN and MCD [1]. Both IgAN-MCD and typical IgAN patients exhibit aberrant glycosylation of IgA1 [15], yet studies suggest IgAN-MCD patients have lower plasma levels of galactose-deficient IgA1 (Gd-IgA1) and its corresponding antibodies, with weaker glomerular IgA1 and Gd-IgA1 staining and reduced inflammatory potential of poly-IgA1 complexes in mesangial cells [24]. These findings imply that the IgAN component in IgAN-MCD may be milder, supporting the dual-pathology hypothesis rather than coincidental IgA deposition in MCD. Recent studies have shown that most children with IgAN-MCD exhibit positive circulating anti-nephrin antibodies in plasma, suggesting that the mechanism of podocyte injury in these patients is similar to that of MCD [13].

Notably, IgAN-MCD may represent a pathological subtype that gradually progresses along with disease course and evolving immunological background. In the present study, case 1 was initially diagnosed with mild mesangial proliferative glomerulonephritis (IF: IgA negative), but repeated renal biopsy five years later confirmed IgAN-MCD. Similar progression patterns have been reported in previous pediatric studies. For example, among 83 children with IgAN, two were initially diagnosed with MCD and one with diffuse mesangial proliferation, all later confirmed as IgAN-MCD upon repeated renal biopsy due to disease recurrence [25]. These observations support the notion that IgAN-MCD may not represent a stable pathological state at a specific time point, but rather a sequential superimposed state where IgAN and MCD occur independently and successively.

Recent advances in single-cell RNA sequencing have shed light on the unique immunopathological features of IgAN-MCD. Chen et al. analyzed circulating and renal immune cells from three IgAN-MCD children and identified abnormal activation of intermediate monocytes, C-C chemokine receptor 2-mediated renal injury, and the involvement of regulatory T cells in tissue repair. Furthermore, the downregulation of PTGDS (encoding prostaglandin D2 synthase) in injured podocytes was proposed as a potential biomarker and therapeutic target in IgAN-MCD [26].

According to the KDIGO 2021 Clinical Practice Guideline for the Management of Glomerular Diseases, IgAN patients with a kidney biopsy demonstrating mesangial IgA deposition and light and EM features otherwise consistent with MCD should be treated in accordance with the guidelines for MCD [2]. While previous studies have demonstrated that glucocorticoids or their combination with immunosuppressants can induce rapid remission, frequent relapses, and steroid dependence remain common [3,4]. However, there is no consensus on whether treatment strategies for frequently relapsing or steroid-dependent NS in MCD should be extrapolated to IgAN-MCD, and the evidence supporting the use of RTX in pediatric IgAN-MCD remains scarce.

RTX, an anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody, depletes peripheral B cells and inhibits autoantibody production and immune complex deposition. It has been widely used and proven effective and safe in treating pediatric NS [6–9]. Observational studies in adults with IgAN-MCD suggest RTX may reduce proteinuria, relapse frequency, and steroid dependency [12]. Our study adds new pediatric data supporting its clinical benefit in IgAN-MCD.

However, the efficacy of RTX in classic IgAN is still controversial. A randomized controlled trial by Lafayette et al. on RTX in patients with IgAN with proteinuria and renal impairment found no significant reduction in proteinuria after one year of RTX treatment and reported a higher incidence of adverse events [5]. Other case reports have shown that RTX may be beneficial in refractory or active IgAN [10,27], but due to a lack of large-scale clinical evidence, current KDIGO guidelines do not recommend RTX for routine IgAN management [2].

RTX may exert dual therapeutic effects in IgAN-MCD: it may reduce Gd-IgA1 production and mesangial immune injury by targeting abnormal B cells, and simultaneously improve podocyte integrity and glomerular filtration barrier function, thereby alleviating proteinuria [28]. Recently, Xu and coworkers developed a kinetic–pharmacodynamic model to predict NS relapse, aiming to identify factors influencing both disease recurrence and B-cell repletion, thereby providing a basis for tailored treatment. This model confirms the association between CD19+ B-cell repletion and relapse [29]. In our study, three children’s relapses predominantly occurred during B-cell reconstitution, suggesting an association between B-cell dynamics and disease control. This highlights the potential utility of B-cell-guided sequential RTX dosing strategies for long-term management of IgAN-MCD.

Despite previous reports characterizing the clinical features and outcomes of IgAN-MCD, considerable limitations remain overall. First, studies specifically addressing treatment strategies are limited, particularly in pediatric populations where high-quality evidence on RTX efficacy and safety is lacking. Second, the pathogenesis of IgAN-MCD is not fully understood, and there is no standardized therapeutic protocol. Clinical decision-making must balance principles of IgAN management with individualized approaches for NS, tailored according to disease course, prior treatment responses, and immunological profile.

Our study provides clinical evidence supporting RTX as an effective and safe individualized treatment for pediatric patients with refractory IgAN-MCD, achieving better disease control and fewer relapses compared with conventional immunosuppressive regimens. However, this single-center retrospective study with a small sample size offers only preliminary evidence. Larger, multicenter prospective studies are needed to further define the role of RTX in this population.

In conclusion, our study provides the first clinical evidence supporting the efficacy and safety of RTX in pediatric IgAN-MCD, offering a promising treatment option for refractory cases.
