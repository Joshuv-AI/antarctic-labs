Managing TRAP Sequence Conservatively: A Case Report of Full‐Term Pregnancy With Favorable Outcome
Authors: Bishal Khaniya, Ayush Shrestha, Anish Sah, Rita Adhikari, Sujata Bhandari, Aashish Bhandari, Ram Aryal, Sameep Sapkota, Puspa Raj Bham, Madhur Bhattarai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAcardiac twinning, also known as Twin reversed arterial perfusion (TRAP) sequence, is a rare anomaly seen exclusively in monochorionic twin pregnancies. It occurs due to abnormal placental vascular anastomoses, allowing retrograde perfusion from healthy “pump” twin to severely malformed twin lacking a functional heart. The prognosis is poor; mortality is 100% in the acardiac twin and 50%–55% in the pump twin. We present a case of 30‐year‐old gravida 3 parity 1 with previous 1 abortion (G3 P1 A1) having an acephalus acardiac twin diagnosed during routine second trimester ultrasound scan. Because there were no signs of hemodynamic compromise in the normal twin and pregnancy was continued up to 38 weeks of gestation when elective lower segment cesarean section was done. This case emphasizes conservative management of TRAP sequence if there is no risk of hydrops or cardiac failure in the pump twin.Keywords: acardiac twinning, conservative management, monochorionic twin pregnancy, pump twin, TRAP sequence

Introduction

Twin Reversed Arterial Perfusion (TRAP) sequence, also known as acardiac malformation, is a rare complication of monochorionic multiple pregnancies. It involves one twin lacking a functional heart (acardiac twin), with the morphologically normal co‐twin (pump twin) providing circulation for both fetuses [1]. According to van Gemert et al., due to a better ultrasound diagnosis and the spread of assisted reproductive technologies in recent years, TRAP incidence occurs in 2.6% of monozygotic twins and 1 every 9500 to 11,000 pregnancies, with almost 75% in diamniotic as in our case and 25% in monoamniotic twin pairs [1, 2]. TRAP sequence results from abnormal placental vascular anastomoses with consequent increase in arterial pressure in one twin leading to reversal of blood flow in the other.

Although early prenatal detection of TRAP sequence is essential for optimizing the clinical outcome of the pump twin [2], this paper presents a TRAP sequence diagnosed during routine second trimester antenatal USG scan, delivered by elective lower segment cesarean section (LSCS) with survival of the pump twin.

Case History/Examination

A 30‐year‐old patient gravida 3 parity 1 with previous 1 abortion (G3 P1 A1) came to OPD for her routine second trimester checkup. The Pregnancy was planned and spontaneous and was diagnosed via urine pregnancy test after 1.5 months of missed period. Her first trimester was uneventful. Pregnancy was continued to 2nd trimester and on her routine ultrasound scan in 2nd trimester a normal fetus corresponding to 23 weeks and 2 days of gestation was seen. In addition to it, an amorphous mass measuring (6.8 × 3.4) cm was present in anterior superior aspect of uterine cavity which later, at 25 week and 1 days of gestation developed into approximately (8.4 × 4.8) elongated mass in upper uterine cavity, representing a case of fetus papyraceus, with a thin separating membrane visible and thus the diagnosis of monochorionic diamniotic twin pregnancy was made. Routine Anomaly scan was also done during her routine second trimester routine, which was normal. Pregnancy was continued till the 3rd trimester. Umbilical artery doppler at 34 + 4 weeks was normal. On USG, 14.1 × 6.9 cm fetus like structure seen in upper uterine cavity separated from thin septa with vascularity. Due to limited resources test for vascular prognosis indicators were not done.

Her obstetric history included one full‐term pregnancy with normal vaginal delivery of a healthy male child weighing 3.5 kg eight years ago and a first‐trimester medical termination six years ago. She had hemithyroidectomy 14 years ago and had a drug history of 4 doses of injectable depo followed by oral contraceptive pill for 1 year and discontinued all contraceptive methods for the past two years.

Her physical examination showed fair general condition with no pallor, edema, or dehydration. Pulse rate was 90 beats per minute, and blood pressure was 100/60 mmHg bilaterally. On per abdominal examination, the fundal height corresponded to 36 weeks, the uterus relaxed with a longitudinal lie and cephalic presentation, the presenting part was free and fetal heart rate was 130 beats per minute. Per speculum and per vaginal examination were not done.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations & Treatment

USG was done at 37 weeks and 6 days of gestation that showed twin pregnancy with one fetus corresponding to approximately 14 weeks gestation located in the right upper part of uterus, suggestive of fetus papyraceus, and the other fetus at 37 weeks' gestation, measuring 14.36 cm and weighing approximately 3099 g in cephalic presentation with placenta upper uterine segment so elective lower segment cesarean section (LSCS) was planned and performed.

Conclusion and Results

By Lower Segment Cesarean Section, twins were delivered, Twin 1 was delivered as live male infant weighing 3.04 kg in cephalic presentation with clear and adequate liquor, and Apgar scores of 7/10 and 8/10 (Figure 1). Twin 2 was an acardiac twin weighing 335 g, with clear and adequate liquor, and Apgar score of 0/10 and 0/10 (Figures 1 and 2). The placenta was monochorionic and diamniotic. The post‐partum period was uneventful and both the mother and twin 1 were discharged after 2 days and was advised for follow up for child vaccination.

Discussion

This case highlights a rare monochorionic diamniotic twin pregnancy complicated by fetus papyraceus with an acardiac twin, successfully managed to term, resulting in survival of the pump twin without antenatal complications despite limited access to advanced fetal vascular prognostic investigations.

Although the first case was described by Benedetti in 1953, the first case has been reported in international literature in the 1950s and the first description of prenatal diagnosis of an acardiac twin was reported by Lehr and Dire in 1978 [1]. Although several theories have been postulated the concrete pathogenesis is still unknown [3]. However, its mechanism and reason can be understood by its anatomy of blood circulation which is different from circulation in normal fetus. In Twin Reversed Arterial Perfusion (TRAP) sequence, the deoxygenated blood from the pump twin doesn't fully reach the placenta. Instead, a portion of it flows through arterio‐arterial anastomoses in a reverse direction, entering one or both umbilical arteries and supplying the systemic circulation of the acardiac twin. This abnormal blood flow pattern gives rise to the TRAP sequence [4].

The blood flow stolen by acardiac twin usually is oxygen‐poor and prevents its upper body formation, with the development of only the lower extremity. In the case of the TRAP acardiac twin, we had to deal with a parasite fetus whose head, arms, and heart were not developed.

Acardiac twinning (TRAP sequence) is classified by cardiac development and cephalic–truncal formation. Based on the heart, it is divided into hemiacardius (partially formed heart) and holoacardius (complete absence of the heart). Morphologically, four types are described: acardius ancephalus (most common, absent head and thorax with developed lower limbs), acardius anceps (poorly formed head with extremities), acardius amorphus (shapeless mass without recognizable structures), and acardius acormus (rarest, isolated head without a body). One of the limitations of our case is we did not classify the acardiac twin then.

TRAP sequence can be diagnosed prenatally by ultrasonography in monochorionic multiple pregnancies. Characteristic findings include an acardiac or rudimentary heart in one fetus and retrograde arterial blood flow to the acardiac twin, demonstrated on Doppler ultrasound [3, 4]. Arterial blood flow towards the affected fetus in color doppler is diagnostic of TRAP sequence [3, 5]. In our case, USG done for routine prenatal checkup in the second trimester led to the finding and diagnosis of the acardiac twin.

The diagnosis can be made as early as 11–12 weeks of gestation through transvaginal ultrasound and 3D ultrasound [2]. As in our case most of diagnosis is made in 2nd and 3rd trimester, while in some case remain unknown till delivery when the childbirth is preceded or followed by ambiguous amorph mass expulsion [3].

The prognosis of such conditions depends upon factors like abnormal umbilical vein diameter ratio, Doppler velocimetry indices of the pump twin such as systolic/diastolic ratio, resistance index, pulsatility index, resistance index, and the ratio of the weight of the cardiac twin to the weight of the pump twin [4].

According to moore, the incidence of preterm delivery is seen in 90%, polyhydroamnios in 40%, and congestive heart failure in 30% when the ratio is above 70:100 (0.7) [3, 6]. The prognosis is poor with mortality is 100% in the acardiac twin because of severe malformation and the mortality risk of pump twin increases in the presence of hydrops or high‐output heart failure, polyhydramnios and fetal brain injury in the pump twin. In our case the ratio was approximately 0.1 so there was birth of normal pump twin by elective LSCS.

Optimal management of TRAP sequence is controversial due to its rarity, with ongoing discussion around conservative vs. interventional approaches and timing. The main goal is to ensure the pump twin's survival and achieve term delivery. According to the condition of pumping twin TRAP sequence can be treated in different ways, conservative management is selected if there is no risk of hydrops of cardiac failure in pumping twin [2], while fetus between 18 and 27 weeks of pregnancy, which are prone to poor prognosis are the main candidates for the surgery [7]. The intervention that can be performed are laser ablation, bipolar cord coagulation and radiofrequency ablation. While most common technique reported in the literature for the treatment of the TRAP Sequence is radiofrequency ablation (RFA) under ultrasound or fetoscopic guidance, several studies show laser ablation is a technically feasible treatment for the TRAP in the first or early second trimester (before 16 weeks of gestation) [3]. And finally, some authors suggested that a conservative approach should be the first choice, with the possibility of shifting to intervention to stop the reversed perfusion if signs of fetal impairment develops [3]. The main goal of treatment should be to safely stop blood flow to the acardiac twin, protecting the pump twin from heart failure or death. In our case none of the above‐mentioned interventional approaches were performed. Since in our case the pump twin was hemodynamically stable, conservative approach for management was preferred with favorable outcome of live birth of male baby of 3.04 kg. This prevented unnecessary intervention in pregnancy state and had economic benefit in a poor resource setting such as in under‐developed countries.

Conclusion

TRAP Sequence is a rare but severe condition, which should be suspected in cases of monozygous twin pregnancies when one twin has no cardiac activity while the other is normal. Diagnosis of acardiac twin should be made in early days of pregnancy most preferably in first trimester and can be done by USG and doppler to improve survival of pump twin. If treatment is needed, the best timing of intervention is still debatable; conservative management can be done if there is no risk to the pump twin, though the latest studies encourage intervention in the first trimester. The main goal of management should be to deliver a healthy near pump twin without heart failure or hydrops.
