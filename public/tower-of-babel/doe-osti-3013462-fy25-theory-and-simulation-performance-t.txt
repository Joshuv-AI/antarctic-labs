1. Background & Context
The urgency to deliver fusion power is growing now more than ever, with increasing pressure for
both public programs and private companies to meet milestones timelines and overcome
significant remaining technical challenges to ensure growth of a nascent fusion industry in time to
meet rapidly growing clean energy demands. With incredible advancements in computation and
years of investment in fusion model development and validation, integrated modeling is poised to
fill a key role in accelerating the timeline to a fusion pilot plant (FPP).
Future fusion pilot plants will operate in regimes far beyond current experience, and device design
will rely on physics-based prediction and extrapolation. Many concepts will also rely on simulation
to assess safety (shielding, tritium management, materials activation and lifetimes), economics and
scalability before the decision to build. Importantly, integrated simulation can be used to reveal
and solve the complexities of system integration that may otherwise not be apparent in physical
components or models developed in isolation. New experimental test facilities that produce
relevant conditions to validate and resolve key technical challenges for various subsystems
(materials, blankets, fuel cycle, etc.) have been repeatedly called for by the fusion community but
are not yet realized. Integrated modeling has an important role in identifying realistic load
conditions (thermal, electromagnetic, plasma, neutron and photon loads, etc.) and defining the
components and experiments for these test facilities in order to ensure meaningful validation that
sufficiently reduces modeling uncertainties and technical risk for the full integrated reactor.
The Fusion REactor Design and Assessment (FREDA) SciDAC project is building a component-
based integrated modeling framework & data structure to enable self-consistent, multi-fidelity,
iterative optimization workflows for the fusion reactor design process. FREDA aims to shorten the
time to viable designs by providing a set of flexible workflows to support the various stages of the
design process using an integrated model hierarchy, ranging from the simple analytic descriptions
to the highest fidelity, theory-based plasma and engineering modeling developed by the fusion and
fission communities. These tools are expected to be needed for timely support of FPP design in
the milestone program and in the FIRE collaboratives.
The plasma simulation backbone of FREDA is IPS-FASTRAN [IPS 2023] with newly developed
coupled Core-Edge Pedestal-SOL (CESOL) workflows, which is being extended to the far-SOL
region up to the plasma facing components. FREDA incorporates the FERMI [Badalassi 2023]
engineering modeling suite and will enable self-consistent evaluation of the thermal shields,
limiters, blanket, magnets, and other surrounding structures with predictions of temperatures,
erosion, dpa, activation, tritium generation and transport, creep, corrosion, material degradation,
etc. Parametric generation of 3D CAD enables rapid iteration of component geometry in response
to plasma and loading specifications.
The overall FREDA project goals are to 1) Complete the framework and workflows to fill the
fusion-plasma physics modeling gaps needed for timely support of FPP design, 2) Implement and
advance fusion-engineering modeling for coupled, multi-fidelity plasma+engineering assessment
and rapid design optimization, 3) Demonstrate targeted plasma+engineering simulations and
evaluate the robustness and achievability of a reactor design point, and 4) Utilize advanced HPC

framework and workflow management to efficiently couple and execute physics and engineering
components.
2. Goals for FY25 Theory and Simulation Performance Target
The FY25 TSPT will report on progress in completing necessary FREDA workflows connecting
the plasma to the first wall, as well as a demonstration of basic plasma+engineering
parametrization and evaluation of an example reactor concept as an initial step towards the goal of
integrated assessment of reactor designs using the FREDA framework.
A major gap for plasma+engineering coupling is predicting heat and particle flux to and from the
wall. Even if full edge physics predictions for an FPP regime are not yet validated, model coupling
allows progress in assessing feasibility of engineering requirements and tolerances. First,
components for the SOL plasma and neutral transport solvers will be added to IPS to enable self-
consistent prediction of plasma profiles across the regions. The SOL model mesh will be extended
to the far-SOL up to PFC. Workflows will be enabled to assess the particle and heat loads to the
surrounding structures.
The targeted plasma+engineering evaluation will not be comprehensive: rather, we will
demonstrate subsets of analysis based on FY25 FREDA workflow developments for first wall
loading, neutronics/tritium tracking, and magnet stress analysis. Since there are not readily
available or complete fusion pilot plant reference designs, tool development and demonstration is
being performed on ARC-like or FNSF type devices that have been used in previous studies. FY25
tasks will include generating a net electric compact advanced tokamak (CAT) plasma with a Dual
Coolant Lead Lithium (DCLL) blanket concept. The CAT pilot plant design study [Buttery 2021]
utilized IPS-FASTRAN and currently serves as the basic starting example for FREDA
development. Some of the tokamak radial geometry build will be based on existing
PROCESS/ARIES system code parameterization used in FNSF studies [Kessel 2018, Kessel 2021]
that specifies the location and dimensions of the various surrounding components such as the
structural ring, vacuum vessel, low-temperature shield, and magnetic field coils. For the blanket,
various public and private concepts include solid lithium ceramics, liquid PbLi, pure liquid lithium,
and molten salt. However, the recent 2023 fusion blanket workshop did not prioritize any blanket
concept. Therefore, we will utilize the DCLL blanket concept, which has been developed and
studied in the public program for ARIES and FNSF, and the team has access to the CAD, material
list, and published simulation results. FREDA parametrization tools will be developed and tested
to 1) generate parametrized CAD components from user defined modules called within the
geometry generation, 2) take an existing high fidelity CAD and defeature for lower fidelity/faster
analysis, or 3) take an existing CAD and create parametrization for optimization.

1. Demonstrate workflow for plasma + first wall and divertor loading prediction

Self-consistent modeling of the entire cross-section of tokamak plasmas is an essential element of
whole device modeling (WDM). One of the key challenges for future reactors such as ITER and a
Fusion Pilot Plant (FPP) is simultaneous optimization of core fusion performance and boundary
techniques to maintain acceptable heat loads to the plasma facing components (PFCs). This
requires improved understanding of interaction between core, edge pedestal, and scrape-off layer
(SOL).
IPS-FASTRAN is the plasma backbone integrated modeling framework of FREDA, providing a
range of efficient and robust integrated modeling workflows to predict a steady-state solution (d/dt
~ 0) and time evolution of core transport on a resistive transport time scale. This approach couples
external heating/current drive (H&CD), MHD equilibrium, ideal MHD stability, and edge pedestal
calculations, built upon a modern HPC-integrated modeling framework, the Integrated Plasma
Simulator (IPS). An important recent advance (Fig. 1) was to extend IPS-FASTRAN to include
the pedestal and scrape-off-layer (SOL) regions by coupling the predictive pedestal model EPED1
and 2-D SOL transport solvers such as C2/GTNEUT and SOLPS-ITER.
The primary goal of Q1 is to establish a workflow to predict the reactor profiles from the core to
wall self-consistently using a Compact Advanced Tokamak (CAT) reference case as a
demonstration example. This workflow will be employed throughout the FREDA project for the
reactor design optimization and multi-fidelity evaluation of design concept.

A range of efficient coupling methods has been developed to improve consistency of the profiles
among the regions and speed up the CESOL modeling for a reactor design, which requires large
ensemble simulation. The core+pedestal profiles inside the separatrix are predicted using TGLF
and EPED with ne,sep, Te,sep, Ti,sep as boundary conditions. Then, the pedestal profile is prescribed
in the SOLPS-ITER modeling, solving the 2D transport equations only in the SOL region. This is
equivalent to SOLPS-ITER solution with Dirichlet boundary condition at the separatrix combined
with a power balance analysis inside of the separatrix.
The coupled system is closed by the global particle and energy balance at the separatrix (Fig. 2).
This specific coupling aims to determine the density and temperature at the separatrix, which are
used to update the input to EPED1 and efficiently close the strong non-linear dependency among
the regions. The total fluxes though the separatrix can be calculated by the volume integration of
source terms in the core and edge pedestal, which must match the 2-D transport solution in the
SOL:

1.1. Divertor parametrization and generation of SOLPS grids
A new parametric tool was developed that generates an approximate first-wall and divertor contour
based on the magnetic equilibrium geometry and a small number of input parameters. For a future
parametric geometric build-out tool, physics and engineering constraints that determine the
divertor shape will be used to set a cost function that can be used in conjunction with other
parametric tools to holistically optimize the overall design. For now, the divertor tool simply takes
as input minimum and maximum values of R and Z, defining a toroidal ‘box’ where the divertor
is to be drawn. Along with these limits, the primary input parameters are the desired angle of
incidence of the magnetic field with the surface normal; the distance from the x-point to the inner
and outer strike point; and the radial extent of the divertor. These parameters largely determine the
deposited power from incident plasma (for a given Psol), the volume available for dissipation from
impurity radiation, and the overall size of the divertor, respectively. While these parameters are
currently user-specified, they can be computed from physics and engineering considerations. For
example, the upstream heat flux density and decay length (determined from the core scenario and
parameters such as the magnetic field strength) can be used to set the desired incident angle, with
a minimum value set by practical limits on alignment and tile-fishscaling.

1.3. Mapping heat flux to first wall and divertor
Heat fluxes to the first wall and divertor of tokamak fusion reactors are expected to be extreme, of
the order of 10 MW/m2 in the divertor, close to material limits. Reliably predicting and managing
these heat loads is therefore critical to the economic viability of a fusion reactor design. Heat fluxes

 10

consist of (a) photons radiated from the plasma; (b) hot plasma electrons; (c) plasma ions, both
thermal and surface recombination energy; and (d) neutral gas, particularly charge-exchanged fast
neutrals. In a fusion reactor the photon radiation due to deliberately injected (“seeded”) impurity
species will typically account for a significant fraction of the heat load.
Here, IPS workflows have been developed to map the heat flux to the first wall and divertor using
both lower fidelity (HEAT) and higher fidelity (BOUT++ and UEDGE) models. In the future, an
extended-grid version of SOLPS-ITER will be employed to cover the far-SOL region, extending
up to the outer wall.

2. Multiphysics magnet analysis
It is often expected that the overall cost of a fusion power plant will be reduced at higher magnetic
field and reduced device size. However, physical space limitations (inboard blanket thickness for
sufficient nuclear shielding and magnet lifetime/heating), structural limitations (mechanical and
cyclic stresses), etc. ultimately limit the compactness. High reliability of the magnets is one of the
most important aspects for an economical magnetic confinement fusion power plant; thus
evaluation of magnet structural and nuclear limitations is required for assessment of operating
point feasibility and robustness. Here, we focus on validation of the electromagnetic (EM) stress
calculation by the Elmer FEM (open-source, [Malinen 2013]) and Diablo (export-controlled,
[Solberg 2015]) codes for mock toroidal field (TF) coils. The use of the open-source software is
often preferred especially for the frameworks designed to serve wider scientific community.
However, the use of more mature codes such as commercial and export control codes brings more
confidence in the results. Therefore, to use open-source codes with confidence, a validation study
is needed for this specific application.
Note that we are treating the magnet analysis at medium fidelity. Therefore, we have no distinction
between different magnet types (resistive, low-T superconductors, high-T superconductors), other
than via material properties. We are not accounting (at least right now) for the difference in the
internal structure of different magnet types. This will require modeling the details of the magnet
geometry. Apart from modeling the detailed geometry, the main difference in resistive vs.

 12

superconducting magnets would be the heat deposition and resultant thermal expansion induced
by resistive coils, which we can model with existing tools. In our analysis, we will assume
superconducting magnets, though for computational purposes they have a non-zero resistance (in
order to solve for the gradient of the potential).

The simulation of the toroidal field (TF) coil was performed at a “medium fidelity” level and the
internal structures were not modeled. The lack of detail in the internal structure is appropriate for
reasonably accurate and fast-running engineering modeling of constraints early in the design
process, such as in iterative optimization or large parameter space scans over device geometry.
The representation of the CAT TF coil geometry is presented in Fig. 10. The parametric CAD

extended domain is needed for electromagnetic simulations to assess the magnetic field
distribution. The fundamental material properties used to describe how materials deform under
stress are the same for both Diablo and Elmer analysis. The Case was stainless steel alloy with
Young’s Modulus of 190 GPA and a Poisson’s ratio of 0.265. The Coil used reduced properties

2.2. Elmer results
The Elmer FEM code is a finite element code capable of solving coupled partial differential
equations. It is an open-source software composed of modules that when coupled can solve a multi-
physics problem. The TF analysis workflow utilized the following Elmer FEM models:
 (1) CoilSolver – determines current distribution inside the coils.
 (2) WhitneyAVSolver – solves Maxwell's equation given coil current.
 (3) StressSolver – Evaluates stress on the coil given electro-magnetic forces.
In addition, the post-processing and output modules were used.

The coil solver results of the current density inside the coil are presented in Fig. 12a. As expected,
the current density is higher on the inside of the coil since it follows the shortest path. The current
density is passed to the Maxwell solver to determine the magnetic flux density (Fig. 12b), nodal
forces and Maxwell stresses. The Maxwell stresses due to magnetic field are then used to evaluate
principal stresses acting on the TF coil and displacements (Fig 13). Figure 14 presents the direction
of the displacement. The inboard surface of the TF coil is constrained to zero displacement and
forces are balanced on the central pillar. Therefore, there is almost no displacement on the inboard.
The forces on a TF coil point outward and decrease with the B field magnitude change. At the top
and bottom of the TF coil, we observe the stretching effect. To compensate there is a radial
displacement at the centerline of the outboard coil.

2.4. Conclusion and future plans for multiphysics magnetic analysis
Our experience using the Elmer code has shown that it is difficult to use and can easily provide
spurious results if not in the hands of an experienced user due to lack of detailed documentation.
In our case, it took many iterations to provide a solution which was physically realistic, and it
required investigating numerous different techniques before we finally settled on the direct use of
the Maxwell Stress. For these analyses, it was important to have a second code to help guide the
analysis, for which the Diablo code was invaluable, even if it was somewhat hamstrung by the
need to map the magnetic field results and use the Magnetic Pressure. In particular, it remains easy
for the magnetic solve to produce results which have problems such as lack of symmetry or local
mesh-driven artifacts. In our case it took many iterations until we found a solution which had
sufficient radial symmetry.
To that end, we have been working to implement Elmer-like methodology into the MFEM
(www.mfem.org) open-source finite element library. Like Elmer, MFEM is open source, but
MFEM has a number of advantages versus Elmer:
 (a) MFEM is written in modern C++, and hence tools such as RAJA can (and have been)
 used to implement MFEM-based applications on GPU’s with a high degree of
 portability
 (b) MFEM has a built-in capability for higher-order elements (both hexahedral and
 tetrahedral)
 (c) MFEM has built-in capabilities for adaptive mesh refinement, for both hexahedral
 (including both isotropic and anisotropic) and tetrahedral (isotropic only) using
 hanging nodes and various error estimators.

The first wall engineering model has a large number of elements, 25077. Performing ray-tracing
and plasma flux mapping for each of these faces would be inefficient, so instead a 2-stage
procedure is used for ray-tracing and mapping of plasma fluxes that is described below.

Ray-tracing of plasma emission is first performed using a relatively coarse boundary mesh that is
taken from the GEQDSK file that was used to generate the plasma mesh. This enables a larger
number of samples to be used for each face, reducing sampling noise. The intersection of the face
normals in the engineering mesh with elements of this GEQDSK mesh are then calculated,
mapping the heat flux density on the GEQDSK onto the engineering mesh.

Plasma heat fluxes due to ion and electron fluxes occur primarily at the plasma sheath, since the
far-SOL up to the wall is not simulated in the present analysis. To identify which faces in the
engineering model these fluxes will impinge on, we first trace along magnetic flux surfaces from
the edges of the plasma domain to find where these intersect the engineering mesh. Using the fact
that the engineering mesh elements are ordered, this process identifies ranges of faces to consider.
For each face in these ranges, we trace back along magnetic flux surfaces to the sheath boundary
location in the plasma simulation. The heat flux at the plasma boundary due to charged species is
mapped along magnetic fields, taking into account changes in the magnetic field and the
orientation of the engineering mesh face. This procedure is fully automated using Hermes-3
outputs and OpenFOAM face and point files, as is the photon ray-tracing analysis.

The sum of photon, electron and ion heat fluxes, mapped to the engineering model faces and then
integrated over the wall, totals 49MW. This is in good agreement with the power input to the
plasma simulation (51MW), providing a check on the power accounting from injection into the
plasma simulation to the input of the engineering analysis. This is one of several checks that will
be automated in Q3, so that the results of an IPS-automated analysis can be cross-checked for
convergence and accuracy.

Thermal analysis of the first wall is a key step in fusion power plant design. Within the first wall
and blanket structure, materials compatibility issues such as corrosion and thermal creep impose
constraints on the maximum allowable temperature of different materials and solid-liquid
interfaces. In this analysis, we consider the DCLL blanket concept with a reduced activation ferritic
martensitic (RAFM) first wall and Helium cooling channels. As a primary analysis, only the
RAFM first wall is modeled in detail with a reduced order model for the heat removal by Helium
cooling. In the future, detailed modeling of the Helium cooling channels will be performed and
the reduced model assessed for accuracy.

The first wall domain with a discretized mesh is shown in Figure 3.1. This is obtained from the
first wall region of the FNSF class design as shown in Figure 2.1. The mesh shown is created using
the Tracer tool described above. The heat conduction in the RAFM first wall is solved on this mesh
with considerations of the fusion environment. The neutronics volumetric heat deposition (shown
in Figure 3.2) is mapped using the FERMI tool and included as a source term. The total heat
deposited by neutronics on the 20 degree sector studied here is 2.95 MW. A sink term for the
Helium cooling model is used to account for the cooling of the first wall without explicitly
modeling the Helium cooling channels. The model is based on the Helium cooling profile
described in [Smolentsev 2024] where Helium enters the first wall from the bottom at a

bottom of the first wall and minimum at the top is considered as shown in Figure 3.2. It should be
noted that Helium cooling is only applied to the first wall and not to the divertor. The mass
flux of Helium is varied to study its effect on the temperature distribution in the first wall. In these
simulations, the blanket side of the first wall is maintained at a constant temperature of 470 C due
to corrosion considerations as mentioned in [Smolentsev 2024]. The temperatures due to this
cooling model with a Helium mass flow rate of 1 kg/s is shown in Figure 3.3. The simplistic nature
of this model also leads to some over predictions since the Helium sink model cools the entire first

volume shown here. This fraction is estimated to be about 50-60% from recent literature. If we

3. Demonstrate multiphysics magnet analysis

Magnet design should be an iterative cycle between plasma and engineering analysis. Plasma
targets (Bₜ, shaping, q-profile, ripple <~0.5%, pulse length, stability control) directly drive
requirements for coil currents, geometry and structural support, cooling and protection. Magnet
engineering sets the reality for feasible plasma operating space. But a one-way (plasma à
magnets) pass could create an over-engineered, needlessly costly coil set or a design that later
proves unbuildable or have low-lifetime once real stresses, nuclear loads, cooling, or maintenance
constraints are included. Thus, iteration should look like (1) propose plasma performance targets
(2) generate coil set & structural model (3) compute coupled EM loads, stresses, temperatures,
fatigue margins, ripple (4) update allowable plasma requests & engineering constraints (5) re-solve
equilibrium and control requirements.

Early plasma targets should be negotiable, as increased fidelity analysis will reveal realistic
primary + cyclic stresses, joint / splice limits, allowable current density vs. temperature margin,
shielding thickness, and cost scaling with B (volume of high-grade structure + superconductor).
As noted in [Federici 2024], while HTS can simplify the magnet cooling scheme, high yield
strength steels can only provide structural support up to a certain point; beyond that, structural
concepts with different scalability behavior would need to be developed.

The full magnet analysis should consider Lorentz loads from the combined TF + PF + CS field
set; a good example is that for DEMO [Giannini 2022]. An initial workflow for TF magnet analysis
has been implemented within the FREDA framework. The TF coil geometry is informed by the
blanket dimensions to ensure a consistent and integrated geometric representation, and the TF
current is taken directly from the state file. An engineering module,
TokDesigner/Engineering/Elmer, was developed to enable stress analysis in Elmer to be initiated
directly from the interface. The analysis process is divided into two main stages: (1) geometry

 50

generation and (2) execution of the Elmer simulation. The workflow utilizes template input files
that are populated with data from the state file, ensuring streamlined analysis.

The coolant considered for cooling the magnets from the neutron heating is supercritical Helium.
Supercritical liquid Helium at 4.5K and 0.3 MPa has a viscosity of 3.3E-06 Pa-s, density of 145
kg/m3, specific heat capacity of 5200 J/kg-K, and thermal conductivity of 0.1 W/m-K, and is still
Newtonian down to the lambda point (2.17K). This provides a lower bound on the temperature of
He. Also, reference mass flow rates of Helium are obtained from previous ITER analyses and
expected to vary between 1.8 - 2.5 kg/s with very low pressure losses (about 1 bar). The operating
temperature of TF coils are quoted at 5K while the temperature difference between He outlet and
inlet should not exceed 0.3 K [Vaghela 2014] to maintain safe margins of operation and prevent
hot spots. Using this information, an analysis has been performed where we use cooling model for
the He coolant spread volumetrically throughout the domain, i.e., the cooling is also homogenized.
Although this is expected to lead to conservative results, it gives good estimates for an initial
scoping analysis.

applicability of the model developed here. In practice we want to stay away from this temperature

maximum temperature in the entire domain is to the mass flow rate of He.

domain while keeping the heat removal rate constant, the He flow rate required for cooling
decreases. With this decrease, comes an increase in temperature in the domain. This dependency

lower 𝛿 values but linear at larger 𝛿 values.

close to the value for 𝑄E% = 100 W/𝑚B , 𝛿 = 0, which was 𝑚E% = 0.627 kg/s. However, we see
that for the 𝛿 = 0.3 case, the min/max temperatures are 28.56/40.08 K while for the 𝛿 = 0 case,
these were 32.43/37.3 K. Thus, even with the same He flow rate, if cooling is applied only in

internal regions away from the wall (which is how magnet coils are designed presently), the

4.2 Tritium transport in solids
We have already developed models for tritium transport in fluid regions of the fusion blanket.
Here, we focus on developing the models for transport in solids, and later we will combine methods
for solid and fluid tritium transport for full fusion blanket analyses. There are a few tools which
can already handle the transport of tritium within solids. Our objective is to identify the tool which
is most suited for integration into FREDA, test it out (especially for coupling with fluid tritium
transport modules already developed) and deploy for use in blanket analysis and design. The
available tools include COMSOL multiphysics, TMAP8 [Lindsay 2021] which is a part of the
MOOSE framework, and FESTIM (Finite Element Simulation of Tritium in Materials) [Mathurin
2024]. While COMSOL is a useful tool, it is a paid software hence not appropriate for the present
SciDAC project where our goal is to develop a suite of open-source tools. TMAP8 and FESTIM
are both open-source but TMAP8 seems to have some restrictions for usage in high-fidelity 3D
simulations which we will explore through the following discussions. Hence, our choice of
software for coupling with OpenFOAM and OpenMC to enable integrated fluid-solid tritium
transport in fusion blankets and vacuum vessels is FESTIM. FESTIM is an open-source finite

element code developed in Python for simulation hydrogen transport in materials leveraging the
open-source FEniCS library [Alnaes 2015]. FESTIM has capabilities for heat transfer, handling
material interfaces, options for multidimensional and multi-material simulations as required in
fusion blankets. Furthermore, FESTIM is used in universities, fusion companies, as well as
national labs.

1.2. Reducing peak heat flux by increasing plasma radiation (Q4 effort beyond milestones)
Reducing peak heat fluxes to plasma-facing surfaces in the divertor requires reducing plasma
temperature at the wall to approximately < 5eV so that the plasma is in a semi- or fully-detached
state. Two actuators to achieve this are (a) injection of impurities such as krypton and neon that
radiate strongly in the temperature range of the Scrape-Off Layer (SOL) and divertor; and (b) gas
puffs in the divertor that increase plasma density and reduce temperature. The impurity model used
in these simulations is fixed fraction, in which impurity ions are assumed to be everywhere a
constant fraction of the ion density. Gas puffs therefore introduce both hydrogenic and impurity
species.
Low plasma temperatures in the divertor (<5 eV) must be simultaneously achieved with high
plasma temperatures at the edge of the confined plasma (referred to here as “upstream”): A plasma
that is cold everywhere will likely have poor fusion performance and may be unstable (e.g. a
MARFE). Achieving this dual objective is the essence of the Integrated Tokamak Exhaust &
Performance (ITEP) gap. We visualize this tradeoff in Figure 1.4, in which we take the first SOL

1.3.3. Development of IPS-TGLF-EP module
Coupling of TGLF-EP into the IPS-FASTRAN framework allows rapid assessment of AE
transport risk across scenarios. Within an IPS run, a Python script generates plasma state inputs in
TGLF-EP’s native “input.gacode” format from equilibrium and kinetic profile data stored within
IPS’s state variables. These profiles have been manually confirmed to agree in test cases. Once the
input.gacode is generated, TGLF-EP is launched to generate the critical gradient, then the 1D
transport code Alpha is run to calculate the predicted EP flux and final profile. The critical gradient
and predicted EP flux are then written back to the IPS instate file for use in successive iterations
of FASTRAN.
In standalone TGLF-EP+Alpha, nodes and cores are set in the submitting batch script. All other
user inputs (designation of which species is the driving EP, simulation resolutions, various control
and mode-selection flags) are stored in the input.TGLFEP and Alpha_input namelist files. Within
the IPS-FASTRAN framework, total required nodes are set within the IPS batch script (dictated
by the needs of the code with the highest core count, usually TGLF-EP if TGLF-EP is included),
and the number of cores for the TGLF-EP case is set within the fastran_scenario.config file as for
all other modules called by FASTRAN. The input.TGLFEP and Alpha_input namelists are
preserved, per the IPS-FASTRAN paradigm applied to other modules.

1.4. Use multi-fidelity uncertainty quantification to investigate edge-physics models
Studying how design decisions impact critical performance metrics is essential for efficient
optimization and risk assessment. Traditional one-at-a-time parameter scans are inadequate as they
fail to capture nonlinear parameter interactions, while comprehensive brute-force exploration is
computationally prohibitive. This work introduces a rigorous, automated methodology to map the
relationship between design parameters and performance metrics at a fraction of the conventional
computational cost. Our goal is to enhance reactor design robustness and reliability through
systematic uncertainty quantification (UQ). The framework integrates multi-fidelity UQ methods
within the Integrated Plasma Simulator (IPS). By combining models of varying physical fidelity
and cost, our approach reduces the number of expensive, high-fidelity simulations while
preserving accuracy. This enables efficient downstream analyses and ensures computational
resources are strategically allocated.
Ultimately, this framework provides a structured pathway to bound predictions of key performance
and materials metrics for engineering specifications, identify dominant sources of uncertainty, and
quantify their impact. Examples of uncertainties are input uncertainties (e.g., material properties,
level of detail in CAD), model uncertainties (e.g., assumptions, missing physics), numerical
uncertainties (e.g., grid resolution, interpolation), and uncertainties in model coupling (e.g.,
boundary conditions). By strategically using computations to achieve robust design goals, tracking
uncertainty reduction over time, and providing clear evidence that confidence in extrapolation is
increasing, this framework positions UQ as a cornerstone of predictive reactor design.
1.4.1. Framework implementation within the Integrated Plasma Simulator
The framework leverages the Dakota toolkit, a powerful open-source software package that serves
as the underlying engine for the analysis. Our workflow abstracts Dakota’s complex syntax,
replacing it with a declarative JSON configuration file. A Python script, ips_multifidelity.py,
orchestrates the analysis by translating the JSON into precise Dakota inputs and launching parallel
execution through IPS. This improves reproducibility, reduces manual errors, and lowers the
barrier to entry for these techniques. It allows domain physicists to robustly estimate parameter
sensitivities without requiring deep expertise in the underlying statistical methods. An entire
analysis is launched with a single command: ips_multifidelity.py --config=dakota_config.json.
While the current focus is on forward UQ, which includes tasks such as Monte Carlo-based
estimation of moments and sensitivity analysis, this capability can be extended to integrate other
Dakota features. Future work could introduce new capabilities for optimal design under
uncertainty, or calibration workflows to match model parameters with experimental data.
1.4.2. Methodology
A direct study of performance metrics via standard Monte Carlo methods requires thousands of
model evaluations, which is infeasible for computationally expensive fusion codes. Our multi-
fidelity Monte Carlo (MFMC) approach constructs a statistical estimate that achieves a target
accuracy at a reduced computational cost by integrating information from a less accurate but
computationally cheaper model. The strategy proceeds as follows:

 71

 1. Pilot study and correlation analysis: A limited number of low- and high-fidelity
 simulations are performed initially. These are used not to calculate the final estimate, but
 rather to establish the statistical relationship between the two models.
 2. Optimal budget allocation: The algorithm determines the optimal allocation of the
 computational budget between the low- and high-fidelity models by solving an
 optimization problem. It seeks to minimize the variance of the final MFMC estimator for
 a fixed computational budget. This allocation is a function of the relative computational
 cost of the two models and the Pearson correlation coefficient between their outputs.
 3. Variance reduction via control variate: The MFMC estimator uses the numerous low-
 fidelity results as a "control variate" to correct the high-fidelity estimate. Because the
 statistical properties of the LF model can be determined with high precision at a low cost,
 this information is used to reduce the statistical uncertainty in the sparse HF data.

1.4.3. Application: analysis of a multi-fidelity family of edge-physics models
To demonstrate the framework’s efficacy, we analyzed the performance of the Scrape-Off Layer
(SOL) in a Fusion Pilot Plant design. This study leverages two SOL simulation models:

 • Low-fidelity (LF) model: C1, an analytical model for rapid calculation.
 • High-fidelity (HF) model: SOLPS, a computationally intensive transport code.

This section details the computational resource allocation, the resulting statistical estimator
performance, and a quantitative assessment of the method's efficiency compared to a conventional
single-fidelity Monte Carlo simulation.
The study employed an MFMC approach to estimate the divertor electron heat flux (qe_div, in
MW/m²) as a response to the injection power (Pinj, in MW). The online adaptive algorithm
dynamically allocated computational resources based on statistical feedback. The LF model was
evaluated 82 times, providing a broad, low-cost exploration of the quantity of interest's behavior.
In contrast, the HF model was evaluated a total of 22 times. The algorithm initially populated the
design with 16 HF samples and subsequently allocated 6 additional HF samples adaptively to
locations that optimally reduce the variance of the control variate estimator. To quantify the total
computational expenditure, the cost of the entire campaign was normalized to the cost of the HF
model. The combined 82 LF and 22 HF evaluations amounted to a total computational cost
equivalent to 32.3 HF evaluations. This figure serves as the computational budget for assessing
the method's overall efficiency.
The primary objective of the multi-fidelity approach is to achieve a reduction in the variance of
the statistical estimator for a given computational budget, thereby increasing the precision of the
final result. The results, summarized in Table 1, show the computational advantages of the
employed MFMC strategy.

estimate.

correlation between the models. The analysis revealed a coefficient of determination (R²) of 0.4,

to improve the linear correlation between the high- and low-fidelity models. A stronger correlation
would, in turn, enhance the computational efficiency of the multi-fidelity methodology by yielding
greater variance reduction.
While the LF model is biased, systematically underpredicting the HF results as all points lie above
the identity line, it reliably tracks the output of the HF model. The linear correction equation,
SOLPS = 0.5 + 2.6 C1, quantifies this relationship. This strong correlation is why the multi-fidelity
strategy succeeded, ultimately delivering a more precise statistical estimate of the quantity of
interest for a fixed computational budget.
1.4.4. Discussion
This work has successfully demonstrated an automated framework for performing multi-fidelity
uncertainty quantification within IPS. The application to a SOL physics problem showed that the
MFMC method yields a more precise estimate of the divertor heat flux compared to a standard
Monte Carlo simulation of equivalent computational cost. This efficiency gain is rooted in the
synergistic use of a computationally inexpensive low-fidelity model to explore the parameter space
broadly, with a strategic number of high-fidelity simulations to correct for bias and ensure
accuracy. The integration of this capability into the IPS via a user-friendly interface lowers the
barrier for incorporating rigorous UQ into the fusion design workflow. It enables designers to
quantify uncertainties more efficiently, leading to more robust and reliable system designs. The
successful implementation of this forward UQ method lays the groundwork for extending the
framework to include other Dakota capabilities, such as design optimization and model calibration
studies, further accelerating the fusion energy development cycle.

3.3. Remaining gaps in materials modeling for engineering analysis of PFCs
The frontier in integrated modeling is incorporating fusion materials data, and modeling where
data is absent, to predict when and where plasma-facing and structural components might fail
under fusion-relevant conditions. Fusion materials face multiple, often overlapping extremes
including high heat and particle fluxes, large thermal gradients, atomic displacements, nuclear
transmutation, and complex chemical environments. These challenges span across the full facility,
ranging from PFCs (divertor, first wall, armor, actuators) to blanket & cooling channels, the
vacuum vessel, structural materials, and even magnets.
Our goal is to identify and incorporate results from ongoing research efforts that address critical
gaps in the engineering analysis of PFCs (e.g. evolution of thermal properties, cracking, tritium
retention) and to shortlist the codes/models/methods to couple into FREDA. As we prioritize, we
should consider: What problem are we trying to solve? Why is this problem a priority compared
to others? What conditions must be met for it to be solved?
Example questions are: When will degradation of first wall or divertor materials begin to affect
plasma performance? At what point will slag or co-deposits become problematic for tritium
retention, or increase the likelihood of UFOs that can trigger disruptions or radiative collapse?
What is the realistic lifetime of PFCs, and how often must they be replaced?
To answer these questions, integrated modeling must span scales and processes, moving from
PFCs to core, to calculate 1) Component heat removal 2) Material surface evolution and overall
thermomechanical degradation 3) Device-scale particle migration 4) Impurity transport (wall to
SOL to core) and 5) Cyclic failure prediction and risk quantification.
While high-fidelity, long-running codes are not well suited for self-consistent iteration within
integrated modeling workflows, it remains important to interface with them where possible. If

4.1 Multiphysics magnet analysis – Stress
The magnet stress analysis was originally performed in Elmer and then validated using a Diablo
analysis as part of the Q1 report. Here, we repeat the analysis using a new module we are
developing, MFEM-FE-M, which is built on the open-source MFEM finite element library
(www.mfem.org). MFEM-FE-M is designed for magneto-structural analysis of magnets. This
duplicates, in some sense, the Elmer capability, but has the additional benefits of being built on
the MFEM library, which is written in modern C++ and naturally accommodates higher-order
elements, automatic mesh refinement, GPU computation, and forthcoming features such as
differentiable solvers. In practice, this means that derivatives of quantities of interest (e.g. peak
magnet stress) will be directly available, enabling design iterations without the need for expensive
(and in many cases inaccurate) suite of reduced-order surrogate fits.
MFEM-FE-M calculates the magnetic field using a version of the “Tesla” min-app included in the
MFEM library example set, adapted for our geometry. We utilize a “wedge” mesh representing
the top-half of a single coil and surrounding air (Fig. 4.1a), corresponding to a 22.5 degree wedge,
representing 1/16 of the full toroid (16 coils). Voltage boundary conditions are applied at the
symmetry plane, using an automatic algorithm to adjust the voltage to achieve the pre-defined
desired current of 5,468,750 A. This approach could also be adapted to instead prescribe a desired
mangetic field strength (either peak or averaged over a specified volume). The wedge mesh was
generated in Cubit and exported to a file in Exodus format which was then read into MFEM-FE-
M via the native Exodus/Netcdf readers. Boundary conditions are defined in Cubit as sidesets
corresponding to the planes of the wedge, the bottom symmetry plane, and the coil voltage planes
(subsets of the symmetry plane), and utilized in the subsequent calculations.
MFEM-FE-M first calculates the current by solving for the voltage in the coil, setting the coil to
have a nominal electrical conductivity of 1.0, and modifying the voltage so that the gradient of the

4.2 Multiphysics magnet analysis – Thermal/Cooling
Here we set out to estimate, under characteristic neutron loading, how much toroidal field magnet
cooling is required to keep a REBCO magnet coil < 25 K? The heating experienced by the magnet
coil and magnet case has been calculated by MCNP on a separate, relatively coarse, tetrahedral
meshes for the coil and case, where the coil has been homogenized. The results represent a rather
non-smooth representation of the heating due to neutron flux. In order to use this for thermal
analysis, a number of steps have been taken.

 1. The data has been analyzed for extreme values and those values above a threshold have
 been capped at the threshold value, see Figures 4.4. It is expected that a longer run of
 MCNP, perhaps on a finer mesh, and calculating more statistics will eliminate the need for
 this operation.
 2. The Engineering mesh, which is a conforming tetrahedral mesh for a single coil created
 using Cubit, has been exported to an Exodus file and then transformed into the VTK format
 using tools available within the Visit visualization framework, though it should be noted
 that these tools exist in many places, for example in the VTK toolset. This was done
 manually but these tools are python scriptable and can easily be automated.
 3. The data has then been mapped to the (much finer) engineering mesh for a single coil,
 using tools in the VTK Toolkit via python scripting.
 4. The data has then been exported to a text file, as part of the same python script, and this
 file utilized within the Diablo input deck using an “include” statement.
 5. A thermal analysis incorporating a convective heat boundary condition, representing
 external insulation, and temperature-dependent sink term representing the effect of cooling,
 was then performed.

In the Diablo analysis, the mesh as mentioned consisted of a single coil+case assembly. The entire
coil assembly is approximately 12 m tall, consisting of 846,000 linear tetrahedral elements and
118,500 nodes, with an average element edge length of about 5 cm. We applied a Neumann
boundary condition to the outside of the case, calculated as having an efficient 1.0e4 times that of
standard ¾" Fiberglass insulation, having an R-value of 120 K/(W/m^2), which corresponds to a
heat transfer coefficient of 8.33e-03 W/(m^2 K), representing the effect of thermal insulation.
Ambient temperature (outside) was set to 300K.

5. Demonstrate nuclear analysis
This section demonstrates the nuclear analysis workflow developed in FREDA. The workflow
involves:
 1. Monte Carlo neutron-photon coupled transport to calculate tritium breeding ratio and heat
 deposition. The tritium breeding and heat deposition values are volume-normalized and
 assessed on an unstructured volumetric mesh using OpenMC.
 2. Computational fluid dynamics to model heat transfer, fluid flow, and tritium transport,

Two demonstrations are presented. The first is a two-way coupling workflow applied to an ARC-

 Summary and Takeaways of FY25 Theory and Simulation
 Performance Target: Development of an integrated modeling
 framework for fusion reactor design and assessment
This report documents the FY25 Theory and Simulation Performance Target (TSPT) of developing
an integrated modeling framework for fusion reactor design and assessment (FREDA). Over Q1-
Q4, new capabilities were developed across both plasma and engineering domains and
demonstrated on an example representation of a Compact Advanced Tokamak with a Dual Cooled
Lead Lithium blanket. This represents a first-of-a-kind demonstration of coupled core-to-wall-to-
engineering for a reactor. Self-consistent CESOL workflows were applied to provide core,
pedestal, and SOL prediction; new modules were developed for energetic particle stability
(FAR3D) and transport (TGLF-EP) analysis; and boundary plasma modeling (SOLPS-ITER,
BOUT++/Hermes-3) was expanded to evaluate wall and divertor heat fluxes and interface with
engineering thermal analysis. A parameterized CAD tool, TRACER, was expanded to generate
medium-fidelity divertor, blanket, and coil geometries; OpenFOAM and Diablo workflows were
applied for first-wall and divertor thermal analyses with helium cooling; and reduced-order models
were created for high-mass-flux divertor cooling. Magnet multiphysics capabilities were verified
between Elmer, Diablo, and a new MFEM-based solver, and workflows enable stress, thermal,
and neutron-fluence analysis of TF coils with neutronics-driven heating. Nuclear and blanket
analysis workflows were demonstrated, including tritium breeding, transport, and CFD-informed
thermo-mechanical assessment. Preliminary multi-fidelity uncertainty quantification workflows
were applied to boundary modeling codes and shown to achieve variance reductions with fewer
high-fidelity boundary simulations.
Key findings highlight the challenges of resolving the ITEP gap to find suitable balance between
wall and divertor loads, neutron heating, and practical limits of PFC cooling. Next step priorities
are to develop automated workflows to check boundary code convergence and detachment,
implement tighter physics-engineering CAD provenance tracking, and inclusion of plasma-
material interface models for SLAG and tungsten cracking behavior. Collectively, these
developments establish sophisticated capabilities for predictive, multi-fidelity, whole-device
modeling that integrates plasma physics, materials, magnets, and nuclear engineering to guide
pathways to viable Fusion Pilot Plant design points.

 • Plasma boundary modeling
 o Self-consistent core-edge-SOL prediction matters: In the case of CAT, the
 separatrix density predicted by CESOL is higher than the assumption of 1/4 neped

 o Initial first wall and divertor heat fluxes were compared between two mapping

• Nuclear analysis
 o Nuclear analysis was performed assuming a homogenized DCLL blanket model,
 and the total TBR in the blanket obtained by OpenMC was 1.2139 +- 0.0004.
 o A higher fidelity analysis of tritium transport in the inboard DCLL blanket was
 performed. Tritium generation rates from OpenMC (in W/m3-s), along with the
 velocity and temperature fields from HiMAG, were mapped to OpenFOAM to
 calculate the evolution of tritium concentration in the blanket as a function time.
 The inboard blanket showed ~0.7 mg tritium lost from blanket after 1000 seconds
 when the Soret effect was included (compared to ~0.08 mg with Fickian diffusion
 alone), indicating that temperature gradients can cause enhanced diffusion at the
 outlet and from liquid into solid at the wall.
 o Explicit vs. homogenized temperature distributions in neutronics lead to 3–4%
 differences in local flux and tritium breeding, but only ~1 K difference in CFD
 results.
 o We evaluated FESTIM (open-source, FEM-based) for tritium transport in solids
 and prioritized FESTIM for FREDA integration (over TMAP8) due to 3D
 capability, faster runs, more trapping-site flexibility, and GPU acceleration
 potential. We demonstrated mapping of OpenMC and OpenFOAM fields into
 FESTIM for ARC-class blanket, but highlighted high Peclet number regions
 (stability issues in cooling channels) and stabilization techniques required.

• Magnets
 o A FREDA-FreeGS interface was used to reduce coil currents and electromagnetic
 forces by adjusting PF coil positions within the (R,Z) space, and the current and
 forces were checked in both flattop and current ramp. The results revealed an
 asymmetry in the forces acting on coils located above and below the midplane, both
 before and after optimization. Although the currents are equivalent in the coils
 above and below the midplane, the discrepancy in the electromagnetic forces on
 these coils may be attributed to the inherent asymmetry in the plasma shape with
 respect to the midplane; however, further investigation is required. It was also
 found that the shape and number of filaments seems to have negligible effect on the
 current density and electromechanical stresses, perhaps because the self-force
 calculation assumes a circular cross-section in any case, which requires further
 investigation in the code.
 o Magnet displacement and stress predictions are largely comparable between Elmer
 and Diablo, with small differences which can attributed to the differences in the use
 of the magnetic pressure in Diablo versus the full Maxwell stress in Elmer.
 However, it took many Elmer mesh refinement iterations to find a solution with
 sufficient radial symmetry and avoid mesh-driven artifacts. This was why we
 pursued the new MFEM-FE-M module, which produced simulation results that
 were in general agreement with the Elmer/Diablo results, and in addition have the
 ability to use higher-order representations of magnetic fields and stresses.
 o Magnet cooling analysis was performed using homogenized coil representation and
 OpenFOAM models with helium coolant. For the nominal CAT case, achieving
 <25 K coil temperatures under neutron loading requires substantial heat extraction
 (~200 W per coil) and highly efficient insulation. In Q3, reducing insulation from
 99% à 90% drives coil temperatures >200 K, causing superconductivity loss.
 Subsequent simulations were performed using Diablo in Q4 which confirmed these
 simulation results. It is important to have high-fidelity thermal deposition outputs
 from the neutronics solution in order to achieve thermal results which do not suffer
 from spurious hotspots – in the Diablo Q4 analyses, a filter was utilized to eliminate
 spurious hot spots in the provided heat deposition, but it is intended that with greater
 resolution on the neutronics side this procedure will not be necessary.
• UQ application
 o Multi-fidelity approach worked well even with moderate correlation (R²=0.4)
 between SOLPS and C1. Achieving full SOLPS convergence is a clear pathway to
 boost the correlation and further improve model performance. Multi-fidelity
 demonstrated significant efficiency gains (23% standard error reduction, 8% better
 than cost-equivalent high-fidelity-only MC) for SOL heat flux problem.
• Version control
 o During these development stages we have experienced first-hand the challenges of
 geometry version control, where plasma simulated a new divertor geometry without
 communicating the update to engineering, causing a mapping error between plasma
 and engineering surfaces. Provenance tracking methods must be implemented.
