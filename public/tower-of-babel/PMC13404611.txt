Blood-based cell-free DNA for addressing colorectal cancer screening disparities: a study protocol for a randomised trial
Authors: Nicole P Mirabadi, Job G Godino, Ernesto Macias Martinez, Jane Samaniego, Yi Le, Lin Liu, Samir Gupta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionColorectal cancer (CRC) screening reduces cancer incidence and mortality, but participation rates are suboptimal in the USA and associated with inequities in CRC outcomes for lower income and many racial/ethnic minoritised groups. A novel, US Food and Drug Administration-approved cell-free tumour DNA (cfDNA) blood test has the potential to improve screening rates, but the impact of offering this test on participation, including among populations at disproportionately increased risk for inequities in CRC outcomes, has not been studied. We designed a small effectiveness randomised controlled trial (RCT) comparing a strategy of offering expanded options for CRC screening including a cfDNA test to a strategy of standard usual care options for screening.Methods and analysisThis study is a two-arm, parallel-group RCT, with 1:1 randomisation of 340 patients to an intervention group versus a usual care group for CRC screening. Participants will include patients who are aged 45–75, not up-to-date with CRC screening and attending a routine primary care visit at Family Health Centers of San Diego, a Federally Qualified Health Center (FQHC) system serving a racial and ethnically diverse, low-income patient population. The intervention group will be offered choice of colonoscopy, at home faecal immunochemical test (FIT) or a combination of in clinic cfDNA and at home FIT. The usual care group will be offered a choice of colonoscopy or at home FIT. Both groups will receive education on the importance of screening and reminders to complete testing. Primary outcome is CRC screening completion within 60 days of enrolment, summarised as the proportion of all patients randomised by group, compared via a χ2 test of proportions. Secondary outcomes will include: number of abnormal results for each test modality, test results, number of patients with abnormal tests referred for abnormal test follow-up, and number of patients who completed colonoscopy within 90 days of an abnormal non-invasive test result.Ethics and disseminationThis study was approved by the University of California, San Diego Office of Institutional Review Board Administration (811489). A waiver of documented (signed) consent was approved by and will allow for a pragmatic design that mimics usual care, in which all patients will be offered all options for CRC screening. Main results will be disseminated to trial participants via reporting of test results and study publication.Trial registration numberNCT06929481.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) screening saves lives, but participation rates are suboptimal. In the USA, national screening rates are 59% among age-eligible adults, and lower by racial/ethnic group (59% for black, 51% for Hispanic, 52% for Indigenous/Native American and 48% for Asian individuals; compared with 60% for non-Hispanic white individuals), residency (30% for recent immigrants), insurance status (53% for Medicaid) and income (46% for <100% of the federal poverty level, which represents income below the poverty threshold determined by the US government).1

Non-invasive options for initial screening, such as a blood-based test, may help to improve screening participation. A test evaluating circulating cell-free DNA (cfDNA) methylation and fragmentation patterns, as well as for somatic pathogenic variants in the Kirsten rat sarcoma viral oncogene homolog (KRAS) and adenomatous polyposis coli (APC) genes has recently been approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) as an option for CRC screening.2 3 Approval was based on a seminal study reporting the test had 83% sensitivity and 90% specificity for CRC detection, using colonoscopy as the reference standard.2 3 Given these results and the convenience of blood-based testing, the cfDNA test is postulated to have great potential for optimising CRC screening participation.2–5

The impact of implementing the cfDNA test on key screening outcomes, including screening disparities, is uncertain because of key evidence gaps. Offering cfDNA to non-responders to mailed faecal immunochemical test (FIT) outreach resulted in a 17.5 percentage point increase in participation over usual care in one study, but the study was set in a well insured integrated healthcare system, and included 80% non-Hispanic white individuals making generalisability to other health settings and populations uncertain.6 Importantly, cfDNA participation has not been studied in populations at highest risk for non-participation in screening, particularly low-income and minoritised racial/ethnic groups. Because cfDNA provides lower sensitivity for advanced polyp detection, statistical modelling studies have suggested population benefits of cfDNA will be most favourable if implementation results in a net increase in screening participation with limited substitution of participation with established tests with higher sensitivity for advanced neoplasia, such as colonoscopy or FIT.7 8 Colonoscopy follow-up rates with non-invasive tests such as FIT are suboptimal, averaging 50% or lower across the USA,9–12 but follow-up rates have not been well studied after abnormal cfDNA, with one underpowered study suggesting numerically lower completion after abnormal cfDNA versus FIT.6 Thus, while success of cfDNA as a strategy for screening likely depends on achieving high rates of screening and abnormal test follow-up, and avoiding significant rates of substitution of established tests with cfDNA, these issues have not been widely studied. Studying these evidence gaps within settings serving populations with traditionally low screening rates, such as Federally Qualified Health Centers (FQHCs), is particularly salient.

The overarching aim of our research is to establish whether a cfDNA blood-based strategy can reduce disparities by boosting CRC screening participation. This study will provide early evidence of the potential impact of making a cfDNA test available to populations with traditionally low rates of screening. Results will inform guideline and policymakers on whether the cfDNA test should be supported as a screening option as well as support planning for a large-scale study examining the impact of offering cfDNA on screening participation, CRC and advanced neoplasia detection and screening disparities.

Methods and analysis

Objectives and hypotheses

Primary objective

Our primary objective is to compare the impact of offering standard usual care versus expanded options, including a cfDNA test on screening participation among 340 patients age-eligible, not up-to-date with CRC screening.

Hypothesis

Screening participation, as defined by completion of any screening test within 60 days of randomisation, will be >15 percentage points higher in the intervention group compared with usual care. A screening rate difference of >15% was defined as the minimal clinically important difference.

Secondary objective

Among individuals with abnormal test results, compare rates of colonoscopy referral, scheduling and completion in the intervention versus usual care group. Among patients completing screening, the relative distribution of screening by test type (FIT, colonoscopy, cfDNA or both cfDNA and FIT) will be summarised.

Hypothesis (exploratory)

Numerically more patients will have colonoscopy referral, scheduling, and completion in the intervention group.

Study design

We will conduct a two-arm, randomised controlled trial of 340 patients at the Family Health Centers of San Diego (FHCSD). Patients will be randomly assigned to a usual care control group offered the standard options of at home FIT or colonoscopy, or an intervention group offered the expanded options of at home FIT, colonoscopy or in clinical cfDNA test plus at home FIT (see figure 1). Duration of participation and follow-up is 180 days and the entire study will take place over the span of 1 year. The full trial protocol is provided in online supplemental data 1, and follows the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) reporting guideline.13

Randomisation and intervention schema. cfDNA, cell-free DNA; FIT, faecal immunochemical test.

Recruitment and study population

Using convenience sampling of patients presenting for routine primary care visits, we will enroll 340 participants age-eligible for CRC screening (45–75 years old), not up-to-date with screening and attending primary care visits during the recruitment window (see Sample size section for power calculation). According to routine practice, eligibility for average risk screening will be confirmed by primary care clinicians.

Study setting

The study will be conducted at FHCSD, the fourth largest FQHC system in the nation. FQHCs are community-based healthcare organisations that receive federal funding to provide comprehensive primary care services to underserved populations. To qualify as an FQHC, centres must meet specific federal requirements such as providing care regardless of patients’ ability to pay and offering services on a sliding fee scale based on income. FQHCs are mandated to deliver accessible, culturally competent care and often serve patients with higher rates of socioeconomic disadvantage and limited access to healthcare resources.

FHCSD operates a network of 27 primary care clinics serving >160 000 unique patients through >1 000 000 visits across San Diego County. FHCSD patients are vulnerable to health disparities: 89% live at <200% and 75% <100% of the Federal Poverty Level, which respectively represent a percentage of income below the poverty threshold determined by the US government; 39.6% are Hispanic individuals (approaching 90% for a number of individual clinics); 8% are black individuals, 3% are Asian individuals; 4% report as more than one race (32% did not report race). In contrast, the racial composition across the USA consists of 60% white individuals, 18% Hispanic individuals, 12% black individuals, 7% Asian individuals and 2% reported more than one race.14 In 2023, 27% (42 523 individuals) of patients were medically uninsured, making FHCSD the largest healthcare provider to uninsured patients in California and the second largest in the nation. In addition, 70.2% of these patients were identified as racial/ethnic minorities (48.3% Hispanic, 12.3% black, 4.7% Asian and 0.7% Indigenous/Native American). In 2023, there were 45 457 patients aged 45–75 years old who were seen at FHCSD and due for CRC screening across 27 sites. Of those, 20 717 (45.57%) completed screening by the end of the calendar year.

Sample size

Sample size required was estimated based on having >80% power to detect a >15 percentage point difference in proportion up-to-date, assuming two-sided alpha=0.05 and an expected 30% proportion of individuals in the usual care group becoming up-to-date on follow-up, with 1:1 allocation. Usual care screening participation assumptions were based on a prior randomised trial.13 We empirically selected a >15% difference because we judged this as the minimal clinically relevant change that would warrant implementation of a novel test such as cfDNA into usual practice. Based on these assumptions, we estimated a need for n=163 individuals per group (326 individuals total) for adequate power for the planned comparisons. Ultimately a sample of 170 per group randomised and meeting inclusion and exclusion criteria (340 total) was selected to account for potential problems with missing data or other follow-up issues.

Assignment of interventions: allocation, randomisation and blinding

Participants will be randomly assigned to the intervention or usual care groups with 1:1 allocation, with block permuted randomisation with alternating block sizes of four and six to ensure balance and concealment. The randomisation sequence will be generated by a study statistician (YL) under supervision of a senior statistician (LL) using the blockrand package in R, with randomly generated study IDs for each participant. A study team member not involved in patient recruitment will create sealed envelopes containing group assignment and study ID for each participant. Envelopes will be prepared in the exact order of the randomised list, without sorting by study ID. Each card will be printed such that the study name and participant ID are visible through the bottom left window of a standard envelope, while treatment assignment will be concealed inside the folded section of the card. In approaching a potential study patient, the research coordinator involved will be blind to group assignment until just prior to the encounter. At that point, the envelope will be opened to determine group assignment, and the research coordinator will deliver either usual care or intervention-based recruitment to the candidate participant.

Informed consent

Our study will use assumed consent with an ‘opt out’ option based on the provision of an informed consent form to each participant. A waiver of documented (signed) consent was approved by the University of California, San Diego (UCSD) Institutional Review Board (IRB) (online supplemental data 2) and will allow for a pragmatic design that mimics usual care, in which all patients will be offered all options for CRC screening. Overall, this approach represents the optimal balance of autonomy together with knowledge to be gained with minimal risk to participants.

Intervention

Patients assigned to the usual care group will be offered options for screening using a standardised script outlining: (1) importance of screening; (2) option of screening with at home FIT or colonoscopy, including key characteristics of each test (online supplemental data 3). Description of key characteristics will include (1) test completion steps; (2) test performance, stating that colonoscopy picks up 9 out of 10 CRCs and important growths called polyps and that FIT picks up 3 in 4 cancers and 1 in 4 important polyps; we define ‘important polyps’ as adenomas 1 cm or larger in size or containing villous/tubulovillous histology or high-grade dysplasia. The script will note abnormal FIT requires a colonoscopy to check for CRC and important growths. Patients assigned to the intervention group will receive an identical offer, but with additional option of screening with an in clinical cfDNA (Shield, Guardant Health) plus an at home FIT. Shield is a methylation partitioning cfDNA blood-based test FDA approved for average risk CRC screening. Rationale for offering FIT with cfDNA is that cfDNA is commercially available as a laboratory-developed test, but has not yet been widely guideline endorsed as a stand-alone test. The script will state cfDNA is a blood test meant to be complementary to current screening with ability to detect 8 out of 10 CRCs and 1 in 10 important polyps, and offers the advantage of having a same day test for CRC detection to complement the FIT that they will do at home (online supplemental data 4). The script will also state colonoscopy is required if either the cfDNA or FIT is abnormal. For patients who opt for the cfDNA plus at home FIT option, the primary clinician will request an on-site research coordinator to perform a same day blood draw for the cfDNA test following manufacturer instructions (online supplemental data 5).

Potential benefits from intervention

Participants may participate in screening that helps with early detection and prevention of CRC. Results may inform clinician and policymaker decision-making surrounding the value of cfDNA as a strategy for increasing CRC screening, and its tradeoffs relative to other established tests.

Potential risks from intervention

Probability and magnitude of harm or discomfort anticipated in the proposed interventions are not greater than those ordinarily encountered by patients in routine primary care. However, a patient electing for FIT or cfDNA plus FIT has a higher chance of having a missed CRC or an advanced polyp compared with if they elect for colonoscopy. Participants who select the cfDNA test will undergo venipuncture, which carries risks similar to any clinical-based blood draw.

Participant timeline

Each participant will have a clinical visit where they will be either offered the usual care or expanded options for CRC screening. If the participant chooses the cfDNA test, they will have their blood drawn that same day in clinic and sent to the cfDNA test manufacturer reference laboratory (Guardant Health) for testing. FHCSD usual care procedures will be followed to encourage FIT and colonoscopy completion. Results of the cfDNA test will be reported at days 15–30 to avoid having the results influence the patient’s decision to complete the at-home FIT. Passive follow-up with manual chart review will be conducted to abstract documentation of screening test completion that occurred between day 1 and day 60. Chart reviews to assess for colonoscopy follow-up on an abnormal test result will be done to capture colonoscopy exposure within 180 days post randomisation. Discontinuation of intervention

If the patient declines discussion of CRC screening or declines screening, no further study interventions will occur. Discontinuation from the study intervention does not mean discontinuation from the study. We will collect data regarding subsequent CRC screening participation and outcomes within the study follow-up window for all patients randomly assigned unless they ‘opt out’ of study participation.

Outcomes and measures

Primary outcome is the proportion of participants up-to-date within 60 days of study inclusion, with up-to-date being defined as completion of an FIT, colonoscopy or cfDNA test, as summarised in table 3. A 60-day time frame was selected because the trial is being conducted with limited resources and with the premise that results from this study could inform a much larger longitudinal trial with more rigorous (eg, 6 months, 12 months or even longer) screening participation outcomes. A priori, we will count completion of the cfDNA test alone as being up-to-date, though we recognise that current screening quality metrics do not yet count cfDNA as being up-to-date. Further, though patients offered cfDNA are recommended to also complete an at home FIT, we will not require that patients electing for the cfDNA option complete both tests to be counted as up-to-date for primary outcome assessment. This approach was chosen to achieve a pragmatic balance between setting the analysis to reflect a potential future state, where cfDNA alone could be counted as up-to-date and current clinical practice which does not yet recognise completion of cfDNA alone as guideline concordant care. Secondary outcomes will include: (1) Screening completion at 60 days, requiring, for the intervention group, completion for patients choosing a combination of cfDNA and FIT be defined by completing both tests; (2) proportion with a screening test ordered at baseline; (3) screening test selection at baseline; (4) among those with an abnormal FIT or cfDNA, number with colonoscopy referral, colonoscopy scheduling and colonoscopy completion, as well as number and type of colonoscopy findings within 90 days of the result (table 3).

Data collection

Demographic characteristics, assigned primary care clinician and outcomes will be collected through a combination of electronic health record queries and manual chart review. All patients randomised will be considered to have complete follow-up so long as procedures for collecting data on screening test completion and colonoscopy completion after an abnormal test were followed.

Data management and confidentiality

The study participant’s contact information will be securely stored at the clinical site for internal use during the study. Individual participants and their research data will be identified by a unique study identification number. Only limited protected health information will be transmitted to and stored at UCSD for analysis: date of birth, dates of medical visits, dates of medical screening and zip codes. The study data entry and study management systems used by FHCSD and UCSD research staff will be secured and password protected. At the end of the study, all study databases will be de-identified and archived at the FHCSD.

Data monitoring

Principal investigators, in collaboration with the research coordinator and data manager, will conduct on-site and central monitoring.

Statistical analysis plan

The intervention and usual care groups will be compared with respect to baseline demographic characteristics, including age, sex, race/ethnicity and clinical provider assigned. Identity of the clinical provider will be coded.

Analysis of the primary outcome

We will compare the proportion up-to-date with screening within 60 days of study inclusion with a χ2 test of proportions following the intent-to-screen principle. A modified intent-to-screen analysis, where all patients randomised who are confirmed to meet inclusion/exclusion criteria will be retained in the analytic sample. Reasons for non-inclusion of a randomised subject in the analytical sample will be documented. A two-sided p value of <0.05 will be used to indicate statistical significance. Multivariable logistic regression will be used to assess the difference in intervention group adjusting for baseline characteristics as appropriate. For these analyses, a nominal p value of <0.05 will be considered as indicative of statistical significance. Effect sizes (risk difference, relative risk) will be reported with their 95% CIs. The absolute risk difference and the number needed to treat (invite) to achieve one additional screening will be reported with respective 95% CIs. Secondary analysis of the primary outcome stratified by key subgroups (sex, race and ethnicity, income level, preferred language) will be conducted. For subgroup analyses, effects will be presented using a forest plot and supplemented with sensitivity analyses (eg, assuming lost-to-follow-up participants as ‘completed’ and ‘not completed’ screening, respectively).

Analysis of the secondary outcome

We will compare, on an exploratory basis, among those with an abnormal FIT, cfDNA or both, the number referred for and completing colonoscopy, and their findings within 90 days of the abnormal test. Descriptive methods will be used to quantify differences, and no formal power calculations were done for this exploratory analysis, which will serve mainly as preliminary data to support a larger study.

Patient and public involvement

Patients were directly involved in recruitment and will continue to be involved in study conduct and dissemination/implementation. As this is an open study, all study assessments performed on a study participant will be shared in their entirety with the patient and their clinical care teams. At the end of the study, the final study results will be published and shared with all study participants.

Ethics and dissemination

The trial protocol was approved by the UCSD Office of IRB Administration (811489). Protocol modifications are communicated to the UCSD IRB and the trial funder. No specialised safety assessments are planned for the usual care group since patients will receive usual care offers for CRC screening. In the intervention group, participants who choose to complete screening with cfDNA and FIT will be monitored for any adverse events related to the blood draw. Assessment will include reports of any problems with the blood draw occurring during the procedure, immediately afterwards or within 10 days. No other adverse events are anticipated, but if the study team becomes aware of any adverse events during interactions with study subjects or through medical chart reviews for outcome assessment, these will be recorded, characterised and reported to the UCSD IRB. The trial results will be submitted for peer-review publication and the ClinicalTrials.gov registration will be updated with these results. A limited, de-identified dataset for replication of analyses will be made available on request to the corresponding author, and with execution of a data use agreement, if permitted by the human subjects protection programmes at UCSD and FHCSD.

Discussion

We propose one of the first evaluations of offering a novel cfDNA screening test on screening participation among individuals at high risk for experiencing screening inequities. Offering cfDNA with an at home FIT allows for early investigation of a screening test that is commercially and clinically available as a laboratory developed test, but not yet widely guideline recommended early in the test’s life cycle, generating data from a high priority population that can help inform future guidelines and eventual expanded implementation of the test to the groups most likely to benefit from this medical innovation if proven effective for increasing screening. Although the cfDNA test has a higher cost compared with FIT, this trial will establish whether cfDNA substantially increases screening and justifies the cost.

This study is poised to be impactful, whether or not our hypothesis is true. If cfDNA increases participation, results will support larger, more definitive studies to confirm the role of this test for alleviating inequities in CRC outcomes. If offering cfDNA has minimal effect on screening, results can be used to guide future studies to understand which populations might be most likely to complete the test, and to inform decisions by guideline and policymakers regarding whether the test should be supported as a screening option.

Limitations

We considered waiting until the cfDNA is more widely guideline recommended, such as by the US Preventive Services Task Force, prior to doing this study. However, this process is expected to take years, and we believe the advantages of getting early evidence of cfDNA implementation impact from populations most likely to benefit from this innovation outweigh challenges to generalisability based on our offer strategy. Further, minimal impact is expected regarding the option of offering FIT plus cfDNA, since we postulate convenience of immediately getting up-to-date with a blood test will outweigh perceived challenge of being requested to do an in-clinic blood test and a subsequent at home FIT. We employ a single-centre design with a short 60-day follow-up window for assessment of screening completion, have not proposed assessment of participant attitudes and motivations for screening test choice, and do not include assessment of key long-term clinical outcomes like cancer detection rate and mortality due to the limited resources available for this early assessment of offering cfDNA as a screening test. A follow-up study with longer follow-up, including qualitative data collection from participants, will be required to understand the long-term impact of implementing cfDNA as a screening option on participation rates, their implications for patients, and how offering cfDNA may be considered for implementation at a larger scale within health systems.

Trial status

This clinical trial successfully launched in April 2025, with over 483 individuals enrolled to date, with 360 confirmed to meet eligibility criteria. The anticipated projected end date is July 2026.
