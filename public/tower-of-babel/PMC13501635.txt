Operationalizing a minimal-metric school screening pathway: using non-cycloplegic spherical equivalent as a first-stage triage metric for hyperopia-reserve insufficiency in Chinese schoolchildren
Authors: Wensi Chen, Feng Chen, Li Xie, Chunyan Wu, Siyin Ding, Daoman Xiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Background

Myopia has reached epidemic proportions globally, with particularly high prevalence in East Asia [1]. Early identification of children at increased risk of myopia is important for implementing timely risk-reduction strategies [2]. A key physiological concept in this preventive approach is hyperopia reserve (HR), the age-dependent physiological hyperopic refractive status in children that gradually diminishes through emmetropization [3, 4]. Premature depletion of this reserve, referred to in this study as hyperopia reserve insufficiency (HRI), has been associated with increased risk of future myopia in longitudinal research [5].

In large-scale school vision screening, cycloplegic refraction remains the reference standard for accurately assessing refractive status, but it is difficult to implement universally because of time, staffing, parental acceptance, and safety-monitoring requirements. Non-cycloplegic refraction is easier to perform and is widely used in school screening, but it is affected by accommodation and may produce myopic readings that are not confirmed after cycloplegia [6–9]. Such findings have traditionally been labelled as pseudomyopia.

The interpretation of non-cycloplegic myopic readings is clinically important. In some children, a negative NC-SE may represent true myopia; in others, it may reflect accommodative influence superimposed on low or borderline hyperopic reserve. This study therefore evaluated a practical screening hypothesis: whether NC-SE, although not a diagnostic substitute for cycloplegic refraction, may serve as a simple first-stage triage metric to identify children who warrant cycloplegic assessment for possible HRI.

The aim of this study was to evaluate the relationship between NC-SE and Cyclo-SE in a school-based cohort of Chinese children and to assess the screening performance of NC-SE for operationally defined HRI. The study also sought to frame a scalable school-screening pathway in which NC-SE is used as an initial triage gate, followed by cycloplegic confirmation when needed in real-world implementation settings.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This was a cross-sectional, school-based refractive assessment study conducted between May 2025 and July 2025 in Zengcheng District, Guangzhou, Guangdong Province, China. Zengcheng is an administrative district of Guangzhou. A total of 1,650 students from participating primary and secondary schools were included through cluster sampling from schools that agreed to participate in the district-based child eye-health screening program. The analytic dataset available for this revision did not contain school-level identifiers; therefore, the exact number of participating schools and the distribution of primary versus secondary schools should be verified from the original screening records before final submission. The study was reported in accordance with the STROBE statement for observational studies.

Participants

A total of 1,650 schoolchildren aged 6–15 years were enrolled. Age groups were predefined for analysis as 6–8 years, 9–11 years, and 12–15 years to reflect age-related changes in refractive development and school-stage differences. The final age-group distribution was 1,076 children aged 6–8 years, 446 aged 9–11 years, and 128 aged 12–15 years.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Children were eligible if they were aged 6–15 years, enrolled in participating schools, had written informed consent from parents or guardians, and completed non-cycloplegic autorefraction, UCVA assessment, and cycloplegic autorefraction. Children were excluded if they had a history of ocular surgery, known ocular disease affecting refraction or visual acuity, active ocular inflammation, neurological or systemic disease affecting visual function, inability to cooperate with examination, or incomplete key data.

Measurements and procedures

All enrolled children underwent the same standardized examination sequence. First, non-cycloplegic autorefraction and UCVA assessment were performed. Second, cycloplegic autorefraction was performed in all 1,650 participants according to a standardized protocol. Therefore, Cyclo-SE was available for the full cohort and was used as the reference standard in all analyses involving HRI, cycloplegic myopia, pseudomyopia, and ROC performance.

Uncorrected visual acuity (UCVA) was measured at 5 m using a standard logarithmic visual acuity chart and recorded in decimal notation. In this manuscript, visual acuity refers to UCVA unless otherwise specified.

Non-cycloplegic autorefraction was performed using a Topcon KR-800 autorefractor (Topcon Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). Based on the study protocol, the instrument was not classified as a closed-field autorefractor. Spherical equivalent (SE) was calculated as sphere plus one-half cylinder.

Cycloplegia was induced using 0.5% tropicamide eye drops according to a standardized protocol. One drop was instilled into each eye five times, with a 5-minute interval between instillations. Cycloplegic autorefraction was performed 30 min after the fifth instillation. The same cycloplegic protocol was applied to all 1,650 participants.

Eye selection and data handling

The manuscript reports child-level screening classifications. The predefined eye-selection or child-level classification rule used in the original statistical analysis should be verified from the statistical analysis file before final submission. Because two eyes from the same child are correlated, the final manuscript should explicitly state whether the right eye, the more myopic eye, or an either-eye child-level rule was used. Missing data were reviewed before analysis; children with missing key refractive or visual acuity data were excluded from relevant analyses.

Definitions of key outcomes

Hyperopia reserve insufficiency (HRI) was defined operationally for school-screening triage rather than as a universally standardized diagnostic entity. In this study, HRI referred to preserved UCVA accompanied by insufficient cycloplegic hyperopic reserve. For children aged 6–8 years, HRI was defined as Cyclo-SE from 0 to + 1.25 D with UCVA > = 1.0. For children aged 9–15 years, HRI was defined as Cyclo-SE from 0 to + 1.00 D with UCVA > = 1.0.

These age-specific thresholds were selected to reflect the expected decline of physiological hyperopia with age and to identify children whose hyperopic reserve was low despite preserved presenting visual function. The thresholds were intended for screening triage and risk stratification, not for definitive diagnosis or universal normative classification.

The visual acuity criterion was included to identify children whose UCVA remained normal but whose cycloplegic hyperopic reserve was already low. This subgroup may be missed by UCVA-only school screening. Children with reduced visual acuity require separate clinical evaluation and were not the primary target of this reserve-oriented screening definition.

Pseudomyopia was defined operationally as a myopic non-cycloplegic refraction result (NC-SE <= -0.50 D) that was not confirmed after cycloplegia (Cyclo-SE > -0.50 D). This definition was used for screening reclassification and should not be interpreted as a direct clinical diagnosis of accommodative spasm. Cycloplegic myopia was defined as Cyclo-SE <= -0.50 D.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as medians and interquartile ranges because refractive measures were not normally distributed. Categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages. Age-group comparisons were performed using the Kruskal-Wallis test for continuous variables and chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables.

Correlation between NC-SE and Cyclo-SE was assessed using Spearman’s correlation coefficient. Because correlation does not establish agreement, the term agreement was avoided unless formal agreement analyses such as Bland-Altman plots were performed.

ROC analysis was used to evaluate the ability of NC-SE to identify operationally defined HRI. Because cycloplegic autorefraction was performed in all participants, ROC analysis was based on the full cohort of 1,650 children. AUC, sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, positive likelihood ratio, negative likelihood ratio, and diagnostic odds ratio were reported where available. Age-specific ROC analyses and alternative cutoffs such as -0.25 D were not recalculated in the current revision because the subgroup ROC output was not available; these analyses should be added if the individual-level dataset is reanalyzed. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Corp.).

Ethical considerations

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The research protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Guangzhou Women and Children’s Medical Center (Approval No. 572A01). Informed consent was obtained from all individual participants included in the study.

Contextual reference to external evidence

To contextualize the findings, we provided narrative comparisons with previously published datasets, including the Anyang Childhood Eye Study and a prospective Shandong cohort study. No formal pooling or meta-analysis was performed. These external studies were used only to support interpretation and not to infer longitudinal outcomes from the present cross-sectional dataset.

Correlation between non-cycloplegic and cycloplegic SE

Prevalence of pseudomyopia and HRI

The overall prevalence of pseudomyopia was 22.9% (378/1,650; 95% CI: 20.9%-25.0%), declining with age from 25.9% in children aged 6–8 years to 12.5% in those aged 12–15 years (Table 3). Among children with normal visual acuity, pseudomyopia prevalence was 24.1% (77/320; 95% CI: 19.7%-29.0%).

Diagnostic performance of NC-SE for detecting HRI

For detecting operationally defined HRI, NC-SE achieved an AUC of 0.661 (95% CI: 0.618–0.704; Fig. 2). The Youden-index-optimized cutoff was approximately − 0.56 D. A pragmatic cutoff of -0.50 D yielded sensitivity of 0.824 (95% CI: 0.765–0.870), specificity of 0.550 (95% CI: 0.524–0.575), PPV of 20.5% (95% CI: 17.9%-23.4%), and NPV of 95.7% (95% CI: 94.1%-96.9%). The positive likelihood ratio was 1.83 (95% CI: 1.68–1.99), the negative likelihood ratio was 0.32 (95% CI: 0.24–0.43), and the diagnostic odds ratio was 5.70 (95% CI: 3.92–8.29).

These values indicate moderate discrimination and support the use of NC-SE as a first-stage triage metric rather than as a stand-alone diagnostic test for HRI.

Cycloplegic reclassification of non-cycloplegic myopic findings

Because pseudomyopia was defined using both NC-SE and Cyclo-SE, Cyclo-SE was not modelled as an independent diagnostic test for pseudomyopia. The previously included supplementary ROC analysis using Cyclo-SE to identify pseudomyopia was removed to avoid circular interpretation. Pseudomyopia is instead presented as a cycloplegic reclassification outcome among children with myopic non-cycloplegic refraction.

Discussion

In this school-based cross-sectional study of 1,650 Chinese children, NC-SE showed moderate discriminative ability for operationally defined HRI. Because cycloplegic autorefraction was performed in all participants, the prevalence of HRI and pseudomyopia and the ROC performance of NC-SE were estimated against a full-cohort cycloplegic reference standard. The findings suggest that NC-SE may be useful as a simple first-stage triage metric in school screening. However, the AUC of 0.661 and PPV of 20.5% indicate that NC-SE should not be regarded as a stand-alone diagnostic test for HRI. Its main value lies in identifying children who may warrant cycloplegic assessment of hyperopic reserve.

A negative NC-SE finding in school screening may reflect true myopia in some children, but in others it may represent accommodative influence superimposed on low or borderline hyperopic reserve. This interpretation is physiologically plausible and consistent with external longitudinal evidence [10, 11]. but it was not directly proven by the present cross-sectional study. Therefore, pseudomyopia should be interpreted as an operational reclassification category rather than a direct diagnosis of accommodative spasm or a definitive prognostic marker [12, 13].

The low PPV deserves careful consideration. At the − 0.50 D cutoff, the PPV was 20.5%, indicating that many children flagged by NC-SE would not meet the operational HRI definition after cycloplegic confirmation. This is not unexpected in a sensitivity-oriented screening strategy, but it implies a substantial false-positive referral burden. In real-world implementation, this may increase healthcare utilization, parental anxiety, and follow-up workload. Therefore, local calibration of the cutoff, monitoring of referral yield, and consideration of healthcare capacity are necessary before broad implementation.

The HRI definition used in this study should be interpreted as an operational screening criterion. It was not intended to establish a universal diagnostic standard [14]. The rationale for using relatively higher thresholds in younger children is that physiological hyperopia normally declines with age, and a low reserve at earlier ages may indicate reduced buffering capacity before the onset of manifest myopia [15, 16]. Nevertheless, the optimal thresholds for HRI remain uncertain and may vary across populations, educational environments, cycloplegic protocols, and baseline myopia prevalence [17]. External validation and longitudinal outcome-based calibration are therefore required.

Comparison with existing screening strategies

Visual-acuity-based screening is simple and widely used, but it may miss children with preserved UCVA and insufficient hyperopic reserve. Cycloplegic refraction remains the reference standard for assessing refractive status, but universal cycloplegia is difficult to implement in many school-screening settings because of time, staffing, parental acceptance, and safety-monitoring requirements. Axial length and AL/CR-based biometric models provide important structural information and may improve risk stratification, but they require additional equipment, examiner training, and quality-control procedures. Photoscreening and AI-assisted screening approaches may further improve efficiency, but their performance depends on device availability, algorithm validation, and local implementation capacity.

The NC-SE single-gate approach proposed here is not intended to replace these multimodal strategies. Rather, it provides a low-burden first-stage triage option that may be useful where universal cycloplegia or biometric testing is not feasible. Children flagged by NC-SE should undergo confirmatory cycloplegic assessment before clinical classification or management decisions.

Strengths

A key strength of this study is that cycloplegic autorefraction was performed in all 1,650 participants. This allowed full-cohort estimation of HRI prevalence, pseudomyopia prevalence, and NC-SE screening performance against a cycloplegic reference standard, thereby avoiding partial verification bias due to selective cycloplegic testing. The study also used age-stratified analyses and focused on an operationally relevant school-screening question.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, although cycloplegic refraction was performed in all participants, the cross-sectional design precludes direct assessment of incident myopia or longitudinal predictive validity. Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted as evidence of screening performance for operationally defined HRI, rather than proof of future myopia progression.

Second, axial length and corneal biometric data were not collected. Because hyperopia reserve and myopia risk are closely related to ocular growth and axial elongation, the absence of biometric data limited our ability to explore the structural basis of low hyperopic reserve and pseudomyopia.

Third, accommodative function was not directly measured. Therefore, the operational classification of pseudomyopia in this study should not be interpreted as a direct clinical diagnosis of accommodative spasm or accommodative dysfunction [12]. In addition, although a uniform 0.5% tropicamide protocol was applied, residual accommodation after tropicamide cycloplegia cannot be completely excluded.

Fourth, the HRI thresholds used in this study were operational screening criteria rather than universally standardized diagnostic cutoffs [14]. Their reproducibility and generalizability require validation in different regions, ethnic groups, educational environments, and screening systems.

Fifth, although the sample size was relatively large, the study was conducted in a single district of Guangzhou, and the oldest age group was relatively small. This may limit the precision of age-stratified estimates and the external generalizability of the findings.

Sixth, the study did not include formal cost-effectiveness analysis, decision-curve analysis, or referral-burden modelling [18]. Because the PPV of the proposed NC-SE cutoff was low, future implementation studies should evaluate referral yield, healthcare utilization, parental anxiety, and net benefit under different screening thresholds.

Finally, although cluster sampling was used, school-level identifiers were not available in the analytic dataset used for this revision, so clustering effects could not be formally adjusted. Failure to account for school-level intraclass correlation may affect the precision of standard errors and confidence intervals. Future analyses should retrieve school identifiers from the original screening records and incorporate school-level clustering, external validation, and longitudinal follow-up.

Suggestions for future studies

Future studies should validate this screening pathway in prospective cohorts with complete cycloplegic refraction, axial length, corneal curvature, and accommodative measurements. Longitudinal follow-up is needed to determine whether children flagged by NC-SE and confirmed as having low hyperopic reserve have a higher incidence of myopia. Future work should also compare NC-SE alone with combined models incorporating visual acuity, axial length, AL/CR ratio, parental myopia, near-work exposure, outdoor time, andAI-assisted risk prediction [19, 20]. Cost-effectiveness analysis and decision-curve analysis will be important to determine context-specificreferral thresholds and implementation strategies.

Conclusion

NC-SE showed moderate ability to identify operationally defined HRI in this school-based cohort of Chinese children. Because all participants underwent cycloplegic autorefraction, the performance estimates were based on a full-cohort cycloplegic reference standard.

These findings support the potential use of NC-SE as a low-burden first-stage triage metric in school screening, particularly for identifying children who may warrant cycloplegic assessment of hyperopic reserve. However, NC-SE should not be interpreted as a stand-alone diagnostic test for HRI. Its modest AUC, low specificity, and low PPV indicate the need for confirmatory cycloplegic refraction and careful consideration of referral burden.

Future longitudinal studies incorporating axial length, accommodative measurements, cost-effectiveness analysis, and external validation are needed to determine the prognostic value and implementation feasibility of this screening pathway.
