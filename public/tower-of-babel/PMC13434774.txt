Engineered halohydrin dehalogenase mediates remote enantiocontrolled dehalogenative hydroxylation via an unconventional mechanism
Authors: Xiao Jin, Hai-Xia Zhang, Kui-De Lu, Zhang-Yang Wang, Hui-Hui Wang, Yong-Zheng Chen, Nan-Wei Wan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Expanding enzyme catalytic diversity is essential to unlocking the full potential of biocatalysis for green chemistry. Halohydrin dehalogenases are promiscuous biocatalysts that typically mediate the intramolecular dehalogenative cyclization of haloalcohols to form cyclic ethers, as well as the ring-opening of these O-heterocycles with various anionic nucleophiles. Here we report that engineered variants of the halohydrin dehalogenase HheC instead catalyze an intermolecular dehalogenative hydroxylation of ε-haloalcohols with remote enantiocontrol. Mechanistic investigations establish that an Asp-Arg dyad serves as the key catalytic motif responsible for this unconventional dehalogenation pathway. Through directed evolution, we further improve both the remote stereoselectivity and catalytic efficiency of the enzyme and systematically profile its substrate scope. The biocatalytic process is also successfully scaled to preparative and gram scales for the synthesis of chiral ε-haloalcohols and ε-diols. Our work uncovers an unusual dehalogenation mechanism and a practical platform for enantiocontrolled synthesis of chiral ε-substituted alcohols.

Introduction

Biocatalysis has established itself as an indispensable approach in synthetic chemistry, facilitating reactions with high chemo-, regio-, and stereoselectivity under environmentally benign conditions1. Advances in enzyme discovery, protein engineering, and mechanistic understanding over the past decades have significantly broadened the scope of biocatalytic reactions, enabling efficient and precise synthesis of many valuable molecules in pharmaceuticals, agrochemicals, and materials science2,3. The integration of rational design with computational methods has recently unlocked unprecedented enzymatic reactivity and substrate flexibility4,5, while artificial cascade reactions offer innovative paradigms for sustainable manufacturing6. Nevertheless, the narrow scope of accessible enzymatic reactions poses a fundamental constraint on the broader utility of biocatalytic technology. Emerging strategies such as merge biocatalysis with photoredox or electrocatalysis are beginning to transcend these barriers, diversifying reaction portfolios and expanding the functional scope of enzyme-mediated synthesis7,8. Despite significant advances, the deliberate expansion of enzyme catalytic diversity remains a critical challenge and a frontier research direction9,10.

Organic halides serve as ubiquitous precursors and pivotal intermediates in numerous organic transformations. Although chemical catalysis has established diverse strategies for transforming organic halides11, dehalogenase-mediated processes offers a considerably milder alternative, playing a pivotal role in both environmental remediation and selective synthesis12,13. Mechanistically, these enzymes employ distinct strategies for carbon–halogen bond cleavage, which are broadly classified into three categories: reductive, oxidative, and hydrolytic dehalogenation13. Reductive dehalogenases catalyze net two-electron reductive elimination reactions via either α-elimination to cleave a C–X bond, forming a C–H bond and a halide ion, or β-elimination, which breaks two vicinal C–X bonds to yield an unsaturated product and two halide ions. In contrast, oxidative dehalogenation of haloaromatics proceeds through enzymatic hydroxylation, replacing a halogen atom with a hydroxyl group employing molecular oxygen or hydrogen peroxide as the oxygen source. Alternatively, enzymatic hydrolytic dehalogenation employs a redox-neutral mechanism wherein the halogen is replaced by a hydroxyl group sourced from a water molecule, thereby distinguishing it from oxidative counterparts. Hydrolytic dehalogenases encompass a range of types defined by their substrate specificity. Well-characterized examples include haloalkane dehalogenases (HLDs; EC 3.8.1.5), haloacid dehalogenases (HADs; EC 3.8.1.2), and 4-chlorobenzoyl-CoA dehalogenases (CCDHs; EC 3.8.1.6), which facilitate the enzymatic hydrolysis of halogenated alkanes, α-halogenated carboxylic acids, and 4-chlorobenzoate, respectively. Notably, halohydrin dehalogenases (HHDHs; EC 4.5.1.X) represent a unique class of dehalogenases that are functionally classified as lyases16,17. Although phylogenetically associated with the short-chain dehydrogenase/reductase (SDR) superfamily, they have lost the canonical dinucleotide-binding motif and evolved a specialized halide-binding site18,19. In contrast to typical hydrolytic dehalogenases, HHDHs catalyze an intramolecular dehalogenative cyclization of β-halohydrins to epoxides with concomitant release of HCl, mediated by a conserved Ser-Tyr-Arg triad and proceeding without the involvement of water (Fig. 1b)18,19. Initially known for epoxide formation, the catalytic scope of HHDHs has been expanded recently by our group and other researchers to include the cyclization of longer-chain halohydrins (γ and δ), yielding oxetanes and tetrahydrofurans (Fig. 1b)20–23. Interestingly, these enzymes exhibit remarkable versatility by catalyzing the ring-opening of epoxides and oxetanes with a range of anionic nucleophiles such as azide, nitrite, and cyanide20,24. This catalytic promiscuity renders them highly attractive biocatalysts for the asymmetric synthesis of diverse valuable synthons, encompassing β-, γ-, and δ-substituted alcohols (e.g., halo-, azido-, cyano-, nitro-) and heterocyclic scaffolds (e.g., epoxides, oxetanes, tetrahydrofurans, oxazolidinones, thiiranes)20,25–38.

Recognizing remote stereogenic centers poses a scientific task in asymmetric synthesis and is of growing interest to the pharmaceutical and fine chemical industry, as these compounds or their intermediates contain a stereocenter separated from the reactive functional group by an extended achiral spacer39. Exerting control from a distant position requires overcoming molecular conformational flexibility, which can lead to multiple transition states and erode stereoselectivity. Designing a catalytic system capable of selectively recognizing a specific molecular conformation and reliably dictating the stereochemical outcome at a remote carbon center is thus exceptionally challenging. While pioneering chemical methods have been explored to address this challenge40–43, the intrinsic and complex chiral scaffolds of enzymes endow position as a highly promising solution for such demanding asymmetric transformations 39,44–46.

Inspired by the successful extension of HHDH-catalyzed stereoselective dehalogenation to remote δ-halohydrins, we ventured into the asymmetric transformation of the even more distant ε-haloalcohols (Fig. 1c), an endeavor that presented two major enzymatic challenges. First, the increased length and size of the substrate require a more spacious active site to ensure proper binding and catalytic conformation. Second, the four-atom separation between the halogen functional group and the chiral carbon center complicates stereochemical recognition at a distance. In this study, we successfully created engineered HHDHs that enable the remote enantiocontrolled dehalogenation of ε-haloalcohols via an unexpected intermolecular dehalogenative hydroxylation pathway, a departure from the canonical intramolecular dehalogenative cyclization mechanism (Fig. 1c). Strikingly, mechanistic investigation elucidated that an aspartate-arginine dyad, rather than the native catalytic triad (NCT), mediates this catalytic transformation. Harnessing this unusual enzymatic dehalogenation, we established an efficient biocatalytic method with broad substrate scope, good enantioselectivity, and desirable scalability on both preparative and gram scales.

Results

Reaction discovery and mechanistic elucidation

Our investigation began with a systematic evaluation of our HHDH enzyme library using racemic ε-haloalcohol 1a (5-chloro-1-phenylpentan-1-ol) as the model substrate. Mechanistically, we hypothesized an intramolecular dehalogenative cyclization to yield 2-phenyltetrahydro-2H-pyran (1c), analogous to the established ring-closure paradigm from β-, γ-, and δ-halohydrins catalyzed by HHDHs (Fig. 2a). Strikingly, comprehensive screening of 41 wild-type HHDHs (spanning subtypes A-G) revealed complete catalytic inactivity (Supplementary Table 1). Subsequent assessment of 32 engineered mutants derived from HheG and HheC revealed no detectable activity toward formation of the target product 1c, underscoring the inherent challenges of enzymatic six-membered ring construction (Supplementary Tables 2–3). Intriguingly, we observed that three HheC mutants, SM5 (P84T), SM7 (W249F), and SM9 (L142A), consistently generated unidentified HPLC peaks, suggesting the potential emergence of an alternative reaction pathway. As the HheC-SM9 variant yielded the highest titer of the unknown product, we scaled up its biotransformation. Subsequent isolation and structural characterization using NMR and HRMS analyses unambiguously indicated the product as the ε-diol 1b (Fig. 2a). Structural assignment was further confirmed by comparison with chemically synthesized racemic 1b standard, showing identical chromatographic retention times (Fig. 2b). Additional quantitative reactions showed that mutants SM5 and SM7 produced 1b with <5% HPLC yield, whereas SM9 demonstrated a substantially higher yield of 19% (Fig. 2a). Chiral HPLC analysis using validated method confirms S-enantioselectivity in SM9-catalyzed unconventional dehalogenation (Fig. 2b)27,28, affording (S)-1b (59% ee) and (R)-1a (15% ee). Subsequently, we sought to decipher the unusual dehalogenation mechanism of the engineered SM9 mutant, which appears to be divergent from the native HHDH pathway. Currently, dehalogenative hydroxylation is mediated by two mechanistically distinct enzyme classes: cofactor-dependent oxidative dehalogenases (e.g., flavin-dependent oxygenases, cytochrome P450s) that use O₂ or H₂O₂, and cofactor-independent hydrolytic dehalogenases (e.g., haloalkane dehalogenases, haloacid dehalogenases) that utilize H₂O and typically feature a nucleophilic aspartate13,14. Given the cofactor-independent nature of HHDHs18,19, the SM9-mediated dehalogenative hydroxylation likely proceeds via a mechanism analogous to that of hydrolytic dehalogenases.

Structural analysis of wild-type HheC (HheC-WT, PDB ID: 1PWZ)47 revealed eight aspartate residues per monomer, with D80 and D182 positioned in the active site (Fig. 2c). The D80 adjacent to R149 of the NCT (S132-Y145-R149), while D182 is situated within the halide-binding site (HBS). To probe the functional roles of the two potential catalytic aspartate residues, we performed site-directed mutagenesis (SDM) on the SM9 mutant. As shown in Fig. 2d (Supplementary Table 4 for details), the D80A and D80E mutations abolished the enzymatic dehalogenation activity. In contrast, while D182A and D182E mutations impaired enzymatic activity, the variants retained partial dehalogenation functionality. These results strongly indicated that D80 is the catalytic residue in this dehalogenation process.

Unexpectedly, in the crystal structure of HheC-WT, the side chain of D80 is oriented away from the catalytic center and forms two hydrogen bond with R149 via its backbone NH group, making direct side-chain involvement in catalysis unlikely (Fig. 2e). Since D80 resides on a flexible loop, we propose that conformational dynamics may reposition its side chain toward the active site during catalysis, consistent with its putative functional role. To test our hypothesis, we first generated a structural model of the SM9 variant by introducing the L142A mutation into chain A of the wild-type HheC structure (PDB: 1PWZ) using the mutagenesis tool in PyMOL software48. This model was then subjected to a 600-ns all-atom molecular dynamics (MD) simulation performed with the AMBER 22 software package49. The system reached equilibrium after approximately 30 ns, as indicated by the RMSD analysis of Cα atoms (Supplementary Fig. 1). We monitored the interatomic distances between S132-O and Y145-O, as well as between Y145-O and the N1/N2 atoms of R149, which indicated that the NCT residues maintained close spatial proximity throughout the simulation (Supplementary Fig. 2). Strikingly, analysis of the distances between D80-O1/O2 and R149-N1/N2 revealed that the D80 side chain underwent a significant conformational shift, moving ~3 Å closer to R149 (from ~6 Å to ~3 Å) during the simulation (Supplementary Fig. 3). MD trajectory analysis reveals that the three NCT residues maintain relatively stable spatial positions (Fig. 2f). Notably, following system equilibration, the carboxyl group of D80 side chain reorients toward the active site and forms two hydrogen bonds with the guanidinium group of R149 (Fig. 2g). This configuration supports the role of D80 as a key catalytic residue in the dehalogenative hydroxylation reaction.

Both haloalkane dehalogenases and haloacid dehalogenases catalyze substrate-specific dehalogenative hydroxylation through an intermolecular SN2 substitution mechanism using H2O as the hydroxyl donor50. These enzymes universally employ an aspartate carboxylate as the catalytic nucleophile, which attacks the halogen-bearing carbon to form a covalent enzyme-substrate ester intermediate while releasing the halide ion (Fig. 3a). The ester intermediate is then hydrolyzed by an activated water molecule to the corresponding primary alcohol product and restores the carboxylate group of the enzyme. Notably, several DL-2-haloacid dehalogenases have been demonstrated to catalyze hydrolytic dehalogenation through an exceptional single-step mechanism that bypasses ester intermediate formation (Fig. 3b)51–53. To elucidate whether the atypical dehalogenation catalyzed by SM9 proceeds via a two-step (ester intermediate) or a one-step (direct displacement) mechanism, we then undertook isotopic labeling studies using H216O and H218O (Fig. 3c, see Supplementary Figs. 4–6 for details). All reactions were performed using stoichiometric substrate 1a and the lyophilized form of the purified SM9 enzyme, with both components at 10 mM concentration in 50 μL H2O. The observed exclusive incorporation of 16O into products when using H216O, and reciprocally 18O when using H218O, along with perfect isotopic conservation (1:1 16O:18O product ratio from 1:1 mixed solvent). These results thereby establish a water-mediated direct displacement mechanism for the dehalogenation.

We subsequently evaluated the dehalogenation cyclization activity of HheC-WT, mutant SM9 (containing mutation L142A), and mutant SM9/D80A (containing mutations L142A and D80A) against β-, γ-, and δ-haloalcohols (1a1, 1a2, 1a3), which yield the corresponding epoxide (1c1), oxetane (1c2), and tetrahydrofuran (1c3), respectively (Fig. 3d, see Supplementary Tables 5–7 for details). The results indicated that while HheC-WT exhibited activity toward the three tested substrates, it was inactive toward the longer-chain ε-haloalcohol substrate 1a in our initial screening. Additionally, the SM9 mutant maintained dehalogenation activity across all three substrates, albeit with notably diminished efficiency observed specifically for γ-halohydrin 1a2. These results suggest that the L142A mutation confers upon HheC the ability to convert ε-haloalcohols without losing its original dehalogenative cyclization activity. However, the SM9/D80A mutant was completely inactive toward all tested substrates. Residue D80, previously shown to be essential for proton delivery in the dehalogenative cyclization reaction by shuttling a proton to the solvent after Y145 deprotonation19, was found to be equally indispensable for the dehalogenative hydroxylation pathway.

We next systematically evaluated the functional contribution of the NCT residues (S132, Y145, R149) and HBS residues (N176–Y187) to the dehalogenative hydroxylation activity through comprehensive SDM strategy. Mutagenesis of NCT (S132A/T/C, Y145A/F/W, R149A/K/D/E) universally abolished dehalogenation activity. In addtion, alanine mutagenesis of the 12 HBS residues showed that only E181A and D182A retained trace activity; all other mutants were inactive (Fig. 3e, see Supplementary Tables 8–9 for details). Furthermore, SM9 showed no activity toward haloketone 1a4, underscoring the critical positioning of the hydroxyl group for productive substrate binding and catalysis (Fig. 3f). These data collectively establish the foundational importance of both NCT and HBS structural elements for the dehalogenative hydroxylation reaction.

Integrating these findings, we suggest a reaction mechanism for the unconventional dehalogenative hydroxylation of ε-haloalcohol (Fig. 3g). Substrate binding orients the ε-haloalcohol hydroxyl group to form hydrogen bonds with S132 and Y145, while positioning the halogen atom in the HBS region. Although Y145 could be deprotonated by R149 under alkaline conditions to act as a catalytic base, the constrained architecture of the active site likely prevents the resulting phenolate from engaging in nucleophilic attack on the distal halogenated carbon, thereby suppressing intramolecular cyclization of the ε-haloalcohol. On the other hand, although Baldwin’s rules predict that 3-exo-tet, 4-exo-tet, 5-exo-tet, and 6-exo-tet ring closures are all favorable54, our background cyclization reactions in alkaline aqueous solution (pH 8.5) show that ε- and γ-haloalcohols (forming even-membered cyclic ethers) undergo dehalogenative cyclization significantly less readily than β- and δ-haloalcohols (forming odd-membered cyclic ethers). cyclization The inherent challenge of ring formation in ε-haloalcohols poses a barrier to tetrahydro-2H-pyran production, although enzymatic cyclization reactions can exhibit unique specificities55. In light of these constraints, we propose that R149 assists D80 in polarizing its carboxylate to activate a water molecule, thereby facilitating a single concerted-step dehalogenation to yield the ε-diol. Here, D80 serves as the general base for water deprotonation, while R149 plays a dual role in positioning the D80 carboxylate and providing charge stabilization through two hydrogen bonds.

The proposed mechanism necessitates a basic environment to promote R149 deprotonation, which in turn enhances D80-mediated water activation. However, the dehalogenation reaction concomitantly releases H+ and Cl-, thereby acidifying the reaction medium. Additional experiments indicated the conversion exhibited a strong positive correlation with buffer capacity, decreasing from 21% to 7% when the buffer concentration was reduced from 50 mM to 25 mM, and rising strikingly to 41% at 100 mM (Fig. 3h, see Supplementary Table 10 for details). This dependence conclusively demonstrates that an alkaline condition is essential to sustain catalytic activity, providing key evidence for the proposed reaction mechanism.

Notably, comprehensive sequence alignment of HheC with 70 classified HHDHs across subtypes A–G reveals that D80 in HheC is not a universally conserved residue (Supplementary Fig. 7). Its conservation is strictly limited to subtype A enzymes, with leucine occupying the equivalent position in other subfamilies. This phylogenetic distribution indicates that D80 is dispensable for the canonical dehalogenation cyclization activity in most HHDHs and represents a distinctive molecular signature of HheC and its subtype A relatives. Consequently, the observed unusual activity likely constitutes a lineage-specific functional innovation rather than a general catalytic property across HHDH family.

Moreover, our proposed mechanism exhibits parallels to limonene-1,2-epoxide hydrolases (LEHs). Unlike most α/β hydrolase-fold epoxide hydrolases, which employ a highly conserved Asp–His–Asp triad to hydrolyze epoxides via a covalent ester intermediate, LEHs utilize an Asp–Arg–Asp triad to activate a water molecule directly for epoxide hydrolysis. In LEHs, the arginine residue positions the catalytic aspartates and stabilizes charge, thereby assisting one aspartate in activating water by abstracting a proton (Supplementary Fig. 8)56,57. Therefore, despite catalyzing distinct SN2 reactions, SM9 and LEHs share a similar strategy for the direct activation of a water molecule. Intriguingly, SM9 operates through a distinct catalytic strategy featuring an aspartate-arginine dyad (D80-R149), which diverges from the aspartate-asparagine (D189-N114) pair used in DL-2-haloacid dehalogenases (Supplementary Fig. 9)52,53.

Enzyme engineering and reaction optimization

Subsequently, we pursued the engineering of HheC-SM9 (hereafter designated HheC-εM1) via structure-guided directed evolution to enhance both catalytic efficiency and enantiocontrol in the remote dehalogenation reaction, thereby enabling the asymmetric synthesis of chiral ε-haloalcohols and ε-diols. Guided by our earlier mechanistic insights, we initially performed molecular docking of substrate (S)-1a into the active site of mutant εM1 to select a plausible binding conformation (Fig. 4a). In this docked conformation, the substrate’s hydroxyl group formed dual hydrogen bonds with S132 and Y145, while the terminal chlorine atom was positioned within the HBS region. Guided by this structural model, we strategically selected ten residues for mutagenesis studies, categorizing them into two functional groups: Group I (P84, T134, A142, and W139), which likely interact with the substrate’s phenyl ring, and Group II (P175, N176, Y177, L178, F186, and Y187), located within the HBS region (Fig. 4a). Motivated by the finding that the L142A mutation in HheC-WT conferred significant dehalogenation activity toward 1a, we first conducted site-saturation mutagenesis (SSM) at the corresponding position A142 in εM1 but failed to identify additional active variants. The L142A variant remained the only active hit, indicating its unique role. This mutation replaces a bulky residue with a smaller alanine, presumably remodels the substrate’s phenyl ring binding region through steric release and thereby enables productive substrate binding. Turning to residue W249, subsequent SSM of εM1 successfully identified the double mutant εM2 (L142A/W249P). This variant exhibited a substantial enhancement in catalytic efficiency, increasing the conversion yield from 30% to 53% (Fig. 4b, entry 2 vs entry 1). Notably, εM2 also showed a marked improvement in enantioselectivity (E = 75), yielding product (S)-1b with 87% ee while retaining the (R)-1a substrate at 99% ee. Using εM2 as the parent enzyme, we performed another round of SSM targeting the remaining Group I residues (P84, T134, W139). This mutagenesis led to the identification of two improved variants: εM3 (L142A/W249P/P84G) and εM4 (L142A/W249P/T134L), which exhibited enhanced enantioselectivities with the E values of 96 (46% conv.) and 87 (37% conv.), respectively (Fig. 4b, entries 3-4 vs entry 2). We therefore integrated the two beneficial mutations (P84G and T134L) through SDM strategy to generate combinational variant εM5 (L142A/W249P/P84G/T134L). The result indicated this quadruple mutant showed reduced catalytic efficiency (25% conv.) without enhancement in enantioselectivity compared to the parent εM3 (Fig. 4b, entry 5 vs entry 3). Additional screening of the εM3 mutant library targeting position N176 yielded variant εM6 (L142A/W249P/P84G/N176Q), which exhibited enhanced enantioselectivity while maintaining catalytic efficiency (47% conv., E = 102; Fig. 4b, entry 6). Remarkably, SSM mutagenesis of εM3 at residue F186 yielded variant εM7 (L142A/W249P/P84G/F186Y) with an E value of 149 at 44% conversion (Fig. 4b, entry 7). However, incorporating the N176Q mutation into εM7 (to generate εM8) diminished both activity and enantioselectivity (42% conv., E = 124; Fig. 4b, entry 8). Moreover, comprehensive SSM screening of residue N176 and its adjacent positions (P175, Y177, L178, Y187) failed to yield any variants that surpassed the performance of the εM7 mutant.

Capitalizing on the pronounced pH dependence of the dehalogenation reaction, we performed further optimization using the top-performing variant HheC-εM7. Conversion increased sharply from 3% at pH 6.5 to 48% at pH 9.5, albeit with a slight compromise in enantioselectivity (Fig. 4c, see Supplementary Table 11 for details). This strong pH-dependence is in line with the mechanistic requirement for alkaline conditions. Subsequently, all eight engineered mutants were re-evaluated at pH 9.5 in Gly-NaOH buffer (8h, Fig. 4d, see Supplementary Table 12 for details). The standout variants (εM6, εM7, and εM8) afforded (S)-1b with 94-96% ee while leaving (R)-1a enriched to 91-94% ee, marking a substantial enhancement in kinetic resolution efficiency over the initial performance at pH 8.5 in PB buffer (10h, Fig. 4b).

Substrate scope of the biocatalytic approach

We next assessed the substrate scope of the engineered variants under optimized conditions against a series of ε-haloalcohols (2a–22a) at the analytical scale. A comparative summary of three representative reactions for each substrate is presented in Fig. 5. The meta- and para-substituted ε-chloroalcohols (2a–8a), bearing either electron-donating or electron-withdrawing groups, underwent smooth reaction (22–60% conv.), affording chiral (S)-ε-diols (2b–8b) with 51– > 99% ee and leaving unreacted (R)-ε-chloroalcohols with 28– > 99% ee. Notably, the introduction of two substituents on the phenyl ring (9a) did not significantly impede catalytic activity (22–61% conv.), albeit with reduced enantioselectivity (E < 15). Remarkably, the system also accommodated thiophene-containing substrate 10a with high enantioselectivity (E = 96–128), achieving 19–46% conversions and delivering the corresponding ε-diol (S)-10b with 96-98% ee. Additionally, the dehalogenation of cyclohexane-substituted alkyl ε-chloroalcohol 11a proceeded with moderate enantioselectivity, affording ε-diol (S)-11b with 77-91% ee and unreacted (R)-11a with 51- > 99% ee. Likewise, ε-bromoalcohol 12a was also efficiently converted to the corresponding ε-diol with excellent enantioselectivity (47–51% conv., E > 100). Synthetic scalability of the biocatalytic approach

Subsequently, the synthetic utility of this biocatalytic strategy was showcased via preparative-scale reaction of five representative ε-haloalcohols using our engineered enzymes. As shown in Fig. 6a, all biotransformations were highly efficient, furnishing the chiral ε-diols in 42–49% isolated yield and 88–96% ee. Concurrently, the unreacted ε-haloalcohol starting materials were recovered in 45–49% yield and 90–98% ee. To demonstrate practical scalability, a large-scale biotransformation of (rac)-12a (12 mmol, 2.9 g) was then conducted at an elevated substrate concentration of 120 mM (Fig. 6b). After 57 h, the process delivered (S)-1b (1.05 g, 47% yield, 95% ee) and recovered (R)-12a (1.36 g, 47% yield, 93% ee). Both ε-haloalcohols and ε-diols are privileged synthetic building blocks owing to their versatile handles for structural diversification, and their synthesis has therefore attracted considerable interest58–60. These chiral scaffolds that werecobtained via our biocatalytic approach, were further diversified through a series of downstream transformations (Fig. 6b). Chiral ε-diol (S)-1b was efficiently converted to the chiral lactone (S)-1ba61. In addition, the absolute configuration of (S)-1ba was unambiguously assigned by single-crystal X-ray diffraction analysis (Supplementary Table 35), thereby confirming the S-enantioselectivity of the enzymatic transformation. Moreover, benzylic fluorination of (S)-1b proceeded with complete stereoretention to furnish ε-fluoroalcohol (S)-1bb in 95% ee62. Importantly, (S)-1b has been established as a strategic intermediate in medicinal chemistry58, serving as the linchpin for a four-step synthesis of a NK1 receptor antagonist63. On the other hand, chiral ε-haloalcohol (R)-12a readily underwent cyclization to form (R)-12aa64, containing a useful tetrahydropyran scaffold—a privileged structural motif prevalent in bioactive compounds65,66. Furthermore, (R)-12a was successfully transformed into ε-azido alcohol (R)-12ab, installing a versatile azido handle for further functionalization67–69. These useful transformations proceeded in good yields with full retention of stereochemistry, underscoring the synthetic versatility of our biocatalytic platform for providing diverse chiral building blocks.

Discussion

HHDHs canonically catalyze an intramolecular SN2 cyclization of β-halohydrins to form cyclic ethers via a conserved Ser-Tyr-Arg catalytic triad. In this work, we have engineered an unconventional catalytic mechanism into HheC that mediates the intermolecular SN2-type dehalogenative hydroxylation of ε-halohydrins through a single L142A mutation. Mechanistic studies reveal that this transformation follows a single-step pathway that bypasses ester-intermediate formation—a feature shared with DL-2-haloacid dehalogenases. Notably, however, the reaction is enabled by an aspartate-arginine dyad in HHDH, distinct from the aspartate-asparagine pair employed by DL-2-haloacid dehalogenases, thus representing a unique catalytic strategy. We further demonstrate that hydrogen-bonding interactions between the native catalytic triad residues (S132 and Y145) and the substrate hydroxyl group are essential for substrate binding, and that the HBS residues are indispensable for maintaining the unusual dehalogenation activity.

Moreover, through directed evolution and optimization of reaction conditions, we have established an efficient biocatalytic platform for remote enantiocontrolled dehalogenative hydroxylation, with significantly improved enantioselectivity and catalytic efficiency. The preparative utility of this approach is demonstrated by the production of ε-haloalcohols and ε-diols with 90–98% and 88–96% ee, respectively. We also validated the practicality of this method through gram-scale synthesis at a substrate concentration up to 29 g/L. In addition, downstream derivatization experiments with the chiral products to useful molecules further confirmed the synthetic versatility of this biocatalytic strategy.

In summary, this work not only unveils an unusual intermolecular dehalogenation mechanism that expands the functional repertoire of dehalogenases but also successfully develops a platform for remote enantiocontrolled biotransformation. We expect that these findings will advance the understanding of enzyme functional evolution and promote applications in green synthesis.

Methods

Directed evolution of HheC-SM9 (HheC-εM1)

Site-saturation mutagenesis of HheC-εM1 was performed via QuickChange PCR using degenerate codons. The amplified products were purified, digested with Dpn I, and transformed into E. coli BL21(DE3) competent cells by heat shock. Single colonies were picked and grown in 2-mL 96-deep-well plates containing 300 μL TB-Kan medium (50 μg/mL kanamycin) at 37 °C for 6 h. Subsequently, 900 μL of fresh TB-Kan medium supplemented with 0.2 mM IPTG was added to each well, and the plates were incubated at 28 °C and 900 rpm for 12 h. Cells were harvested by centrifugation (1600 × g, 15 min, 4 °C) and used directly for biotransformations.

For dehalogenation performance screening, harvested cells were resuspended in 500 μL of PB buffer (100 mM, pH 8.5) containing 10 mM (rac)-1a (with 1% v/v DMSO as co-solvent). The plates were shaken at 30 °C and 900 rpm for 10 h. Reactions were extracted with 700 μL ethyl acetate, and the organic phases were separated, dried over anhydrous Na2SO4, and analyzed by chiral HPLC. Mutants showing improved catalytic activity and/or enantioselectivity were further validated in 5-mL biotransformation reactions (PB buffer, pH 8.5, 100 mM; 10 mM (rac)-1a; 10 g dcw/L E. coli cells) at 30 °C for 10 h. Conversions and ee values (1a and 1b) were determined by chiral HPLC. Positive variants were preserved for sequencing by Sangon Biotechnology Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The site-directed mutagenesis was performed following the same protocol as used for site‑saturation mutagenesis. Three colonies were selected and sequenced to confirm the presence of the desired mutation.

Preparative-scale biocatalytic enantioselective dehalogenation of ε-haloalcohols

A resting cell suspension of E. coli (HheC-εM) was prepared at a concentration of 10 g dcw/L in PB buffer (100 mM, pH 9.5, 100 mL) in a 200 mL round-bottom flask. To this suspension was added ε-haloalcohol (rac)-a (1–2 mmol, with 1% v/v DMSO as co-solvent), yielding a final substrate concentration of 10–20 mM. The reaction mixture was stirred at 30 °C for 1–12 h. Upon completion, the mixture was extracted with ethyl acetate (3 × 100 mL), and the organic layers were separated by centrifugation (8800 × g, 3 min), combined, dried over anhydrous Na2SO4, and concentrated under reduced pressure. Flash chromatography on silica gel (petroleum ether/ethyl acetate = 10:1 to 1:1) afforded chiral ε-diols (S)-b and ε-haloalcohols (R)-a.
