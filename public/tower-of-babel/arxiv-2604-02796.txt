A polynomial bound for the minimal excluded minors for a surface
Sarah Houdaigoui, Ken-ichi Kawarabayashi

Abstract
As part of the graph minor project, Robertson and Seymour showed in 1990 that the class of graphs that can be embedded in a given surface can be characterized by a finite set of minimal excluded minors [ 30 ] . However, their proof, because existential, provides no explicit information about these excluded minors. In 1993, Seymour established the first upper bound on the order of such minimal excluded minors [ 33 ] . Very recently, Houdaigoui and Kawarabayashi improved this result by deriving a quasi-polynomial upper bound [ 15 ] . Despite this progress, the gap between this bound and the known linear lower bound Ω ⁡ ( g ) \Omega(g) (where g g denotes the genus) remains substantial. In particular, they conjectured that a polynomial upper bound should hold. In this paper, we confirm this conjecture by showing that the order of the minimal excluded minors for a surface of genus g g is O ⁡ ( g 8 + ε ) O(g^{8+\varepsilon}) for every ε > 0 \varepsilon>0 . This result significantly narrows the gap between the known lower and upper bounds, bringing the asymptotic behavior much closer to the conjectured optimum. Our approach introduces a new forbidden structure of minimal excluded minors. Let G G be a minimal excluded minor for a surface of Euler genus g g . Houdaigoui and Kawarabayashi [ 15 ] showed that G G contains O ⁡ ( log ⁡ g ) O(\log g) pairwise disjoint cycles that are contractible and nested in some embedding of G G . We strengthen this result by proving a separator-based variant: for any contractible subgraph H ⊆ G H\subseteq G with a separator of size s s (with H H completely contained in one side), the subgraph H H contains O ⁡ ( log ⁡ s ) O(\log s) disjoint cycles that are contractible and nested in some embedding of G G . This allows us to replace a genus-dependent bound with a separator-dependent one, which is the main new ingredient in deriving our polynomial bound.



1
Introduction

The graph minor theorem
[
32
]
, proved by Robertson and Seymour in 2004, states that every class of graphs closed under minors can be characterized by a finite list of minimal excluded minors (which we will denote by
excluded minors
from now on). Understanding the nature of the excluded minors for various classes of graphs closed under minor operations is a very active area of research. In particular, exploring the link between minor-monotone graph parameters and the presence of specific graphs as minors has been a very fruitful area of research.
The most iconic example of such work is the grid minor theorem
[
29
]
, which states that there exists a function
f
such that, for every grid
H
, every graph with treewidth at least
f(|V(H)|)
contains
H
as a minor. There has been extensive work on lower and upper bounds for the function
f
, see
[
5
,
6
]
for the current state of the art. There are also results analogous to the grid minor theorem for pathwidth
[
22
,
34
]
and treedepth
[
20
]
.
Another minor monotone graph parameter that has always aroused major interest is the genus. It was well studied before the graph minor project, but this project sparked a renewal in this field and a shift in the angle of approach.

In 1930, Kuratowski
[
24
]
showed that planar graphs are precisely those that do not contain a subdivision of
K_{5}
or
K_{3,3}
as a subgraph. Subsequently, in 1937, Wagner showed that planar graphs are precisely those that do not contain
K_{5}
or
K_{3,3}
as a minor. These two results can easily be shown to be equivalent.
Finding an analog to Kuratowski’s or Wagner’s theorems for higher genus surfaces was for a long time a central open problem: in 1978, Glover and Huneke showed that there is a finite number of minimal subgraphs excluded as subdivision (thereafter minimal excluded subgraphs) for the projective plane
[
11
]
; then Glover, Huneke and Wang presented in 1979 a list of 103 minimal excluded subgraphs
[
10
]
; finally, Archdeacon proved in 1980 that this list is complete
[
2
,
3
]
.
It is easy to show that a class of graphs is characterized by a finite set of excluded minors if and only if it is characterized by a finite set of minimal excluded subgraphs (see, e.g.,
[
25
, Proposition 6.1.1]
).
In 1989, Archdeacon and Huneke extended the result from the projective plane to every non-orientable surface by showing that every non-orientable surface admits only a finite number of minimal excluded subgraphs. Robertson and Seymour finally settled the question by showing that the number of excluded minors for a surface is always finite in a preliminary result of the celebrated graph minor theorem
[
30
]
.
Simpler proofs of the graph minor theorem for surfaces have been found by Mohar
[
26
]
and Thomassen
[
35
]
.

However, the graph minor theorem approach is purely existential and hence gives no clue on how to find excluded minors for a surface. Therefore, except for the plane and the projective plane, the exact list of excluded minors for each surface is unknown. Considerable efforts toward such a result for the torus have been made. Still, even though several classes of minimal excluded subgraphs for the torus are known (see
[
7
,
16
,
8
,
14
,
4
]
), no exhaustive list has been produced yet. Among all of these results, there are at least 2200 excluded minors for the torus. It is, therefore, unrealistic in the foreseeable future to hope that a characterization of excluded minors will be explicitly found for surfaces of the Euler genus higher than the torus.

Consequently, the research community has been trying to bound the number of excluded minors on a fixed surface by a function of the surface’s Euler genus. In 1993, Seymour gave the first and, until quite recently, only known upper bound on the order of an excluded minor for a surface
[
33
]
.

Theorem 1.1

(
[
33
]
)
.

Let
S
be a given surface of Euler genus
g
. Every excluded minor for
S
has at most
2^{2^{k}}
vertices where
k=(3g+9)^{9}
.

The only known lower bound on the maximum order of excluded minors for a surface is
\Omega(g)
, which is obtained by
g+1
copies of
K_{3,3}
.
Therefore, the gap between known lower and upper bounds is huge. Recently, Houdaigoui and Kawarabayashi
[
15
]
finally improved the bound to a quasi-polynomial in the Euler genus
g
of the surface:

Theorem 1.2

(
[
15
]
)
.

Let
S
be a given surface of Euler genus
g
. Every excluded minor for
S
is of order at most
g^{O(\log^{3}g)}
.

As mentioned in
[
15
]
, this bound is most likely far from the best possible. Indeed, they conjectured that a polynomial bound should be possible.
In this paper, we manage to confirm this conjecture.
The main result of this paper is the following:

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
S
be a given surface of Euler genus
g
. Every excluded minor for
S
is of order at most
U(g)=\tilde{O}(g^{8+\varepsilon})
for every
\varepsilon>0
.

Technical contributions over
[
15
]
.

Let us highlight the technical contributions of this paper.
Let
G
be an excluded minor for a surface of genus
g
.

Our approach introduces a new forbidden structure for a minimal excluded minor
G
for a surface of Euler genus
g
. Houdaigoui and Kawarabayashi
[
15
]
proved that
G
contains
O(\log g)
pairwise disjoint cycles that are contractible and nested in some embedding of
G
. We refine this result by establishing a separator-based variant: for any contractible subgraph
H
of
G
with a separator of size
s
(i.e., for any planar induced subgraph
H
that is separated from the rest of the graph
G
by a separator on its outer face boundary of size
s
),
H
contains
O(\log s)
disjoint cycles that are contractible and nested in some embedding of
G
. This allows us to shift from a genus-dependent bound to a separator-dependent bound.

At a high level, the proof of this result follows the argument established in
[
15
]
, showing that
G
contains
O(\log g)
pairwise disjoint cycles that are contractible and nested in some embedding of
G
. Nevertheless, the conceptual shift underlying our approach is essential to establishing our main result: it allows us to overcome a barrier that prevented so far from obtaining a polynomial bound. Indeed, by identifying a contractible subgraph of
G
that admits a separator of size asymptotically smaller than
g
, we can focus on a region of the graph in which the number of disjoint contractible nested cycles is smaller than the
O(\log g)
bound that applies to the entire graph and that is believed to be asymptotically tight, according to
[
15
]
. Indeed, in the rest of our proof, we try to isolate a contractible subgraph of
G
that admits a separator of size
O(\log g)
, which consequently contains
O(\log\log g)
pairwise disjoint contractible nested cycles. This way, we can “forbid”
O(\log\log g)
nested cycles, rather than
O(\log g)
nested cycles, which allow us to obtain the polynomial bound in terms of the genus
g
(indeed, we can bound the number of vertices of such a contractible subgraph, which is a key idea).
This constitutes the paper’s main new ingredient.

In the rest of the proof, we build on the techniques developed in
[
15
]
and use well-established methods for graphs of bounded treewidth (see, for example,
[
19
]
), to derive our polynomial bound.

Algorithmic implications.

Let us highlight the algorithmic implications of our results.
As a consequence of the graph minor theorem, Robertson and Seymour
[
31
]
showed in 1995 that, for every minor-closed class of graphs
\mathcal{G}
, there exists a cubic time algorithm that decides whether a graph belongs to
\mathcal{G}
(membership test). The running time was later improved to quadratic by Kawarabayashi, Kobayashi, and Reed
[
17
]
in 2012 and to quasi-linear by Korhonen, Pilipczuk, and Stamoulis
[
23
]
in 2024.
However, although the algorithm in itself is explicit and therefore constructive, it relies on the (finite) set of excluded minors that characterizes the class of graphs
\mathcal{G}
, which is, in general, unknown.
Furthermore, Fellows and Langston
[
9
]
observed in 1989 that there is no algorithm that, given a Turing machine that is a membership test for a minor-closed class of graphs
\mathcal{C}
, computes the excluded minors that characterize
\mathcal{C}
. Nevertheless, Fellows and Langston showed in the same paper that an explicit upper bound on the order of the excluded minors in a minor-closed class of graphs yields an algorithm to find them. Adler, Grohe, and Kreutzer
[
1
]
also gave such an algorithm.

Moreover, we take two examples to illustrate the importance of a bound for the order of the excluded minors for a surface. First, Kawarabayashi, Mohar, and Reed
[
18
]
gave in FOCS 2008 a linear time FPT-algorithm for embedding graphs into an arbitrary surface
S
, with parameter the genus of
S
, which relies on the bound on the order of the excluded minors for
S
. Second, Grohe, Kawarabayashi, and Reed
[
13
]
gave, in 2013, a quadratic-time algorithm for computing graph minor decompositions, which also uses the bound on the order of excluded minors for a surface.

Apart from the surface case, the treewidth bound for the excluded grid theorem is now polynomial
[
5
]
, and more recently, building on
[
21
]
, all the bounds for the excluded graph minor structure theorem are now polynomial in terms of the minor size to exclude as shown by Gorsky, Seweryn and Wiederrecht in FOCS 2025
[
12
]
.
These results are well motivated by the need to efficiently solve many optimization problems. Since our result is now polynomial in the genus
g
, we expect to contribute to solving various problems more efficiently for the bounded genus graphs.

Definitions and Notation

The definitions and notation presented here are the same as in

Basics

We consider simple graphs. Let
G

A

The

(or

if clear in the context). The

of
G
is the maximum degree of a vertex of

and is denoted
\Delta(G)
.

A

H
of

is a graph obtained from

by successive operations of deletion of a vertex, deletion of an edge, and contraction of an edge. A

of

is a minor of

different from

.
We denote by

(resp.

), and we denote

the graph obtained from

. We denote

. In that case,

A

.

The

To describe graphs on surfaces, we follow the definitions and notation of Mohar and Thomassen’s book
Graphs on Surfaces

[
25
]
, with the sole exception that we use the term genus to denote the Euler genus. Thereafter, we give a condensed summary of the definitions and notation used in this paper.

Surface and disk

We define a
surface
as a connected compact Hausdorff topological space
S
so that each point of
S
has an open neighborhood homeomorphic to the open unit disk in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. A
simple closed curve
in a surface
S
is a continuous 1-1 function
f:[0,1]\mapsto S
with
f(0)=f(1)
. A
disk
on
S
is a simple closed curve that can be continuously deformed into a single point.

Embedding and facial walk

Let
G
be a graph and
S
a surface, we define an
embedding

\Pi
of
G
in
S
to be a pair
\Pi=(\pi,\lambda)
where
\pi=\{\pi_{v}|v\in V(G)\}
associates to each vertex
v\in V(G)
a cyclic permutation of the edges incident to
v
and
\lambda:E(G)\mapsto\{-1,1\}
associates to each edge
e\in E(G)
a sign (either
-1
or
1
) called its
signature
.

A
local change
of an embedding
\Pi=(\pi,\lambda)
of
G
changes the clockwise ordering to anticlockwise at some vertex
v\in V(G)
, i.e.
\pi_{v}
is replaced by its inverse
\pi_{v}^{-1}
, and
\lambda(e)
is replaced by
-\lambda(e)
for every edge
e
that is incident with
v
. Two embeddings of
G
are
equivalent
if one can be obtained from the other by a sequence of local changes.

The
face traversal procedure
is the following: We start with a vertex
v\in V(G)
and an edge
e=vw\in E(G)
. Let’s traverse the edge from
v
to
w
, if the signature of
e
is
1
, then we continue the walk along the edge
e^{\prime}=\pi_{w}(e)
, otherwise (
\lambda(e)=-1
), we change
\pi_{u}
by
\pi_{u}^{-1}
for every
u\in V(G)
and we continue the walk along the edge
e^{\prime}=\pi_{w}(e)
. And so forth. The walk is completed when the initial edge
e
is encountered in the same direction (from
v
to
w
) and with the same orientation (
\pi
is the same as before we began the walk). A
\Pi
-facial walk
(or, if clear in the context,
face
) is a closed walk in
G
determined by the face traversal procedure. We define the
size
of a face to be the number of edges that the walk contains. We define the
maximum face degree
of
(G,\Pi)
to be
\Delta_{F}(G,\Pi)=\max\{|f|,f\text{ is a face in }(G,\Pi)\}
.

Let
C
be a cycle of
G
, we define the
signature
of
C
in
\Pi
to be
\lambda(C)=\prod_{e\in C}\lambda(e)
. We say that
C
is
two-sided
if
\lambda(C)=1
and
one-sided
otherwise. We say that an embedding
\Pi
of
G
is
orientable
if every cycle
C
of
G
is two-sided; otherwise, we say that it is
non orientable
.

Genus and embeddability

In this paper, the
genus
of a surface always refers to the Euler genus, that is to say, twice its orientable genus if the surface is orientable and its non orientable genus if the surface is non orientable. The sphere is the only surface of genus
0
, the projective plane is the only surface of genus

and is a non orientable surface.
Let

be the
\Pi
-facial walks of
(G,\Pi)
. We define the Euler characteristic of
\Pi
to be
\chi(\Pi)=|V(G)|-|E(G)|+|F(G,\Pi)|
.
For an embedding
\Pi
and a graph

g(\Pi)=2-\chi(\Pi)
denotes the

of
\Pi
, and

denotes the genus of an embedding of

with minimal genus. The formula

\chi(\Pi)=2-g(\Pi)

is called the

.
We say that
G
is
embeddable
in a surface

, if there exists an embedding
\Pi
of
G
so that
g(\Pi)=g(S)
and
\Pi
is
orientable

In this section, we prove three structural results on

These results are improvements over the results of Seymour
[

and, more recently, over the results in
[

[

The proofs in this section are largely inspired by the proofs in

.

This unification allows us to find a better bound for the structure formerly called isolated paths: in

]
, it is proved that

.

While

are direct extensions of results proven in
[

.
Moreover, it is easy to verify that

.

In

Let

Remark that, by construction, the set

for

Claim 6.3.2
.

For

Proof of the claim.

The trees in

Moreover, by minimality of

, there is no induced path of length

Finally,

7
Main proof

In this section, we present the main result of this paper: the order of
G
is bounded by a polynomial function
U(g)=ag^{8}\times(\log g)^{b(\log\log g)^{3}}
for some constant
a
and
b
.

The section is organized as follows:

•

First, we present a few results about balanced covers that directly build on the work on balanced separators from Robertson and Seymour
[
28
]
and tailor this tool to our needs.

•

Then, we present the main proof of the paper through
7.6
and
7.7
: in
7.6
, we first show that there is a
\Pi
-contractible subgraph
G_{0}
of
G
that is separated from the rest of
G
by a separator of size
O(g\log^{2}g)
. Moreover, the order of
G
is bounded by
|V(G_{0})|\times Q(g)
with

some polynomial function of

. In

, we then find a subgraph
H
of

that is separated from the rest of

by a separator of size

, and so that the order of

is bounded by

with

some polynomial function of

. Finally, we conclude the proof by applying

.

•

Finally, we prove

and therefore extend the bound on the order of

to the order of any excluded minor for the surface

7.1
Balanced tree separators

Using the celebrated balanced separator theorem from Robertson and Seymour
[
28
]
, we find a balanced cover of any graph, so that the intersection of a set of the cover with the other sets of the cover is small (at most the product of the treewidth of the graph and a function logarithmic in the size of the cover).

For a tree
T
, we define a
1
-separation sequence
of
T
to be a collection
(T_{1},...,T_{m})
of subtrees of
T
(
m\geq 2
) such that
T_{1}\cup...\cup T_{m}=T
and
|T_{i}\cap T_{j}|\leq 1
for all
i\neq j
.
In
[
28
]
, Robertson and Seymour proved the following theorem, known as the balanced separator theorem:

Theorem 7.1

(Balanced separator theorem,
[
28
, (2.6)]
)
.

Let
H
be a graph and let
(T,(V_{t})_{t\in T})
be a tree decomposition of
H
. Then, there is a
1
-separation
(T_{1},T_{2})
of
T
such that

\frac{1}{3}|V(H)-V_{t_{0}}|\leq|\bigcup_{t\in T_{1}}V_{t}-V_{t_{0}}|\leq\frac{2}{3}|V(H)-V_{t_{0}}|

and

\frac{1}{3}|V(H)-V_{t_{0}}|\leq|\bigcup_{t\in T_{2}}V_{t}-V_{t_{0}}|\leq\frac{2}{3}|V(H)-V_{t_{0}}|

with
T_{1}\cap T_{2}=\{t_{0}\}
.

We first prove the following variant of
7.1
:

Corollary 7.2
.

Let
H
be a graph and let
(T,(V_{t})_{t\in T})
be a tree decomposition of
H
. Suppose that
\max_{t\in T}|V_{t}|\leq\frac{1}{A}|V(H)|
for some constant
A>1
. Then, there is a
1
-separation
(T_{1},T_{2})
of
T
such that

\frac{1}{3}|V(H)|\leq|\bigcup_{t\in T_{1}}V_{t}|\leq(\frac{2}{3}+\frac{1}{3A})|V(H)|

and

\frac{1}{3}|V(H)|\leq|\bigcup_{t\in T_{2}}V_{t}|\leq(\frac{2}{3}+\frac{1}{3A})|V(H)|

Proof.

By
7.1
, we easily get that
|\bigcup_{t\in T_{i}}V_{t}|\geq\frac{1}{3}|V(H)|
for
i=1,2
. Indeed, for
i=1,2
and
T_{1}\cap T_{2}=\{t_{0}\}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{3}|V(H)-V_{t_{0}}|

\displaystyle\leq|\bigcup_{t\in T_{i}}V_{t}-V_{t_{0}}|

\displaystyle\frac{1}{3}(|V(H)|-|V_{t_{0}}|)

\displaystyle\leq|\bigcup_{t\in T_{i}}V_{t}|-|V_{t_{0}}|

\displaystyle\frac{1}{3}|V(H)|+\frac{2}{3}|V_{t_{0}}|

\displaystyle\leq|\bigcup_{t\in T_{i}}V_{t}|

\displaystyle\frac{1}{3}|V(H)|

\displaystyle\leq|\bigcup_{t\in T_{i}}V_{t}|

Moreover, by using again
7.1
, we get that
|\bigcup_{t\in T_{i}}V_{t}|\leq(\frac{2}{3}+\frac{1}{3A})|V(H)|
for
i=1,2
. Indeed, for
i=1,2
and
T_{1}\cap T_{2}=\{t_{0}\}
, we have

\displaystyle|\bigcup_{t\in T_{i}}V_{t}-V_{t_{0}}|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{3}|V(H)-V_{t_{0}}|

\displaystyle|\bigcup_{t\in T_{i}}V_{t}|-|V_{t_{0}}|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{3}(|V(H)|-|V_{t_{0}}|)

\displaystyle|\bigcup_{t\in T_{i}}V_{t}|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{3}|V(H)|+\frac{1}{3}|V_{t_{0}}|

\displaystyle|\bigcup_{t\in T_{i}}V_{t}|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{3}|V(H)|+\frac{1}{3A}|V(H)|

\displaystyle|\bigcup_{t\in T_{i}}V_{t}|

\displaystyle\leq(\frac{2}{3}+\frac{1}{3A})|V(H)|

∎

Finally, we extend
7.2
to a
1
-separation of
T
of arbitrary size:

Lemma 7.3
.

Let
H
be a graph and let
(T,(V_{t})_{t\in T})
be a tree decomposition of
H
. Suppose that
\max_{t\in T}|V_{t}|\leq\frac{1}{4k}|V(H)|
. For
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, there is a
1
-separation sequence
(T_{1},...,T_{k})
of
T
such that, for every
1\leq i,j\leq k
,

1.

|\bigcup_{t\in T_{i}}V_{t}|\leq 3|\bigcup_{t\in T_{j}}V_{t}|
,

2.

V(T_{i})\cap(\bigcup_{1\leq j\neq i\leq k}V(T_{j}))
has size at most
\lfloor\log_{\frac{4}{3}}3k\rfloor
.

Proof.

We prove the first property by induction on
k
.

For
k=1
, this is clear.

Now, suppose that the property is true for some
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, let’s prove that it is true for
k+1
.
Let
H
be a graph and let
(T,(V_{t})_{t\in T})
be a tree decomposition of
H
. By induction hypothesis, there is a
1
-separation sequence
(T_{1},...,T_{k})
of
T
such that, for every
1\leq i,j\leq k
,

|\bigcup_{t\in T_{i}}V_{t}|\leq 3|\bigcup_{t\in T_{j}}V_{t}|
and such that, for
1\leq i\leq k
,
V(T_{i})\cap(\bigcup_{1\leq j\neq i\leq k}V(T_{j}))
has size at most
\lfloor\log_{\frac{4}{3}}3k\rfloor
. Suppose that the order
T_{1},...,T_{k}
is chosen so that
|\bigcup_{t\in T_{i}}V_{t}|\leq|\bigcup_{t\in T_{j}}V_{t}|
if

.

Then, remark that
\max_{t\in T_{k}}|V_{t}|\leq\max_{t\in T}|V_{t}|\leq\frac{1}{4k}|V(H)|\leq\frac{1}{4}|\bigcup_{t\in T_{k}}V_{t}|
.
Consequently, by
7.2
, there is a
1
-separation
(T_{k}^{\prime},T^{\prime\prime}_{k})
of

such that

\frac{1}{3}|\bigcup_{t\in T_{k}}V_{t}|\leq|\bigcup_{t\in T_{k}^{\prime}}V_{t}|\leq\frac{3}{4}|\bigcup_{t\in T_{k}}V_{t}|\text{\quad and \quad}\frac{1}{3}|\bigcup_{t\in T_{k}}V_{t}|\leq|\bigcup_{t\in T_{k}^{\prime\prime}}V_{t}|\leq\frac{3}{4}|\bigcup_{t\in T_{k}}V_{t}|

Let’s show that the
1
-separation sequence
(T_{1},T_{2},...,T_{k-1},T^{\prime}_{k},T^{\prime\prime}_{k})
of
T
has the needed property. It suffices to verify it for
T_{i}
(
1\leq i\leq k-1
) and
T_{k}^{\prime}
without loss of generality.
We indeed have that

|\bigcup_{t\in T_{i}}V_{t}|\leq|\bigcup_{t\in T_{k}}V_{t}|\leq 3|\bigcup_{t\in T^{\prime}_{k}}V_{t}|\text{\quad and \quad}|\bigcup_{t\in T^{\prime}_{k}}V_{t}|\leq|\bigcup_{t\in T_{k}}V_{t}|\leq 3|\bigcup_{t\in T_{i}}V_{t}|

This concludes the proof of the first property.

Finally, we prove the second property. Suppose that there exists
1\leq i\leq k
such that

V(T_{i})\cap(\bigcup_{1\leq j\neq i\leq k}V(T_{j}))\text{ has size }n>\lfloor\log_{\frac{4}{3}}3k\rfloor

Remark that by construction,
T_{i}
is obtained after a sequence of
n

1
-separations:
T,U_{1},U_{2},U_{3},...,U_{n}=T_{i}
where, for all
1\leq i\leq n-1
,
U_{i+1}\subseteq U_{i}
and
|\bigcup_{t\in U_{i+1}}V_{t}|\leq\frac{3}{4}|\bigcup_{t\in U_{i}}V_{t}|
(according to

).

Therefore, we get that

|\bigcup_{t\in T_{i}}V_{t}|\leq\left(\frac{3}{4}\right)^{n}|\bigcup_{t\in T}V_{t}|=\left(\frac{3}{4}\right)^{n}|V(H)|.

Moreover, by the first property, we have that

|\bigcup_{t\in T_{i}}V_{t}|\geq\frac{1}{3}|\bigcup_{t\in T_{k}}V_{t}|\text{\quad and \quad}k\times|\bigcup_{t\in T_{k}}V_{t}|\geq\sum_{j=1}^{k}|\bigcup_{t\in T_{j}}V_{t}|\geq|V(H)|

Therefore,

|\bigcup_{t\in T_{i}}V_{t}|\geq\frac{|V(H)|}{3k}

Finally, we have
(\frac{3}{4})^{n}\geq\frac{1}{3k}
, which is a contradiction because

, and therefore
(\frac{3}{4})^{n}<\frac{1}{3k}
.
∎

of the genus

.

We first prove that there is a

We will use the following classical result for graphs on surfaces:

Let

[

]

Conclusion
