Background

The trabecular meshwork–Schlemm’s canal (TM–SC) complex is not a static structure [1]. Previous studies show this region may change dynamically with intraocular pressure (IOP) fluctuations and cardiac activity rhythm [2, 3]. Such pulsatile behavior has mainly been studied using imaging techniques such as phase-sensitive optical coherence tomography, which detects subtle motion of the TM–SC complex [4, 5]. However, direct visualization of rhythmic movement within Schlemm’s canal by routine gonioscopy is rarely reported.

We report a special case. At the 2-year follow-up after penetrating canaloplasty (PCP), gonioscopy revealed regular, rhythmic to-and-fro movement of a 10 − 0 suture in Schlemm’s canal (SC). This report aims to document this unusual gonioscopic finding and to cautiously discuss its possible observational significance.

Case presentation

A 53-year-old man was diagnosed with chronic angle-closure glaucoma. He had a history of old uveitis and macular edema in the left eye. The highest recorded IOP in the left eye was 48 mmHg. Two years earlier, he had undergone trabeculectomy in the left eye. Postoperatively, IOP remained inadequately controlled despite treatment with brimonidine tartrate, latanoprost, and brinzolamide/timolol.

At presentation, the best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) in the left eye was 0.4, and the IOP was 34 mmHg despite three topical antiglaucoma medications. The superior filtering bleb in the left eye was low and flat, with mixed conjunctival hyperemia and mild corneal edema. The anterior chamber was relatively shallow. The pupil measured approximately 1.5 mm, and lens clarity was poor. Fundus details could not be clearly visualized through the small pupil. Gonioscopy showed angle closure of more than 180 degrees in the left eye. To control IOP, the patient underwent PCP in the left eye at our hospital. The postoperative course was generally stable, and no additional glaucoma surgery was required. After surgery, the patient continued regular use of brinzolamide/timolol, and IOP remained stable.

At the 2-year follow-up, the BCVA in the left eye was 0.8, and the IOP was 18 mmHg. The superior conjunctival filtering bleb was low and flat; the cornea was clear; the peripheral iridectomy was patent; the pupil was about 2 mm; and the lens had mild opacity. Gonioscopy under slit-lamp using a standard lens was performed, with images and videos recorded (Figs. 1, 2 and 3). At roughly the 1 o’clock position in the superior angle, the previous trabecular incision and the 10 − 0 suture traversing Schlemm’s canal were visible. The suture showed rhythmic oscillatory movement within the local field of view, observed reproducibly during the same session (see Additional file 1: Video S1). The anterior chamber was quiet, with no signs of active inflammation.

Discussion

This case documents an incidental dynamic gonioscopic finding during long-term postoperative follow-up after PCP. At the 2-year postoperative visit, the retained 10 − 0 suture within the SC showed subtle, regular to-and-fro movement on gonioscopy. Previous studies suggest the aqueous outflow pathway isn’t entirely static, and the TM-SC complex may show subtle pressure- or pulse-related motion [6, 7]. Such dynamic changes have mainly been studied with imaging techniques like phase-sensitive optical coherence tomography [4, 5]. In contrast, direct visualization of rhythmic motion in the SC region during routine gonioscopy remains undocumented.

In the present case, the patient underwent PCP. PCP is a Schlemm’s canal–based reconstructive procedure that does not rely on subconjunctival filtering bleb [8, 9]. In the standard procedure, a conjunctival flap is created, then superficial and deep scleral flaps to expose SC. After identification of SC, trabeculectomy and peripheral iridectomy are performed. A microcatheter with a 10 − 0 suture is inserted into SC via the surgical opening and advanced around, with concurrent canal viscodilation. After circumferential catheterization, the 10 − 0 suture is tensioned and secured within SC as part of the PCP procedure. The scleral and conjunctival flaps are then closed. The patient previously had a trabeculectomy, and a trabecular opening was already at the surgical site. Therefore, during PCP, this pre-existing opening served as the entry site to access SC, enabling microcatheter-assisted circumferential canal viscodilation and placement of a 10 − 0 suture.

The procedure aims to improve the conventional aqueous outflow pathway through circumferential canal viscodilation and suture tensioning [8, 10]. Placement and retention of a 10 − 0 suture within the SC are part of the procedure. Therefore, the retained suture in this case may be regarded as a visible in vivo marker of local motion in the SC region. In this context, the rhythmic movement of the retained suture may be cautiously interpreted as a visible manifestation of local dynamic behavior in this region. However, the physiological significance of this observation remains unclear, and this single qualitative finding cannot demonstrate recovery of conventional aqueous outflow function.

Given that this finding was identified primarily through routine gonioscopic video observation, motion artifact should be considered. In the recorded sequence, the surrounding gonioscopic structures appeared relatively stable, making incidental motion artifact less likely. Nevertheless, subtle movements related to the ocular pulse, eye movement, or examination factors could not be completely ruled out. Another limitation is that this observation was not obtained under a predefined protocol designed to assess suture pulsation. Therefore, the movement was not quantified, and synchronized recording of cardiac rhythm, intraocular pressure fluctuations, or phase-sensitive optical coherence tomography was not performed. Future studies could prospectively examine rhythmic suture movement on gonioscopy after Schlemm’s canal procedures involving intraschlemm’s canal suture placement, and use gonioscopic video with quantitative imaging and physiological monitoring to clarify its mechanism and clinical relevance.

Conclusion

This case describes an incidental gonioscopic finding of subtle, rhythmic movement of a retained 10 − 0 suture within Schlemm’s canal 2 years after penetrating canaloplasty. The retained suture may serve as a visible in vivo marker of dynamic behavior in the Schlemm’s canal region. However, the physiological and clinical significance of this observation remains uncertain, and further studies using quantitative imaging and physiological monitoring are needed.
