World Health Organization Labor Care Guide: scientific rationale, global adoption, and early policy implementation in the Republic of Moldova
Authors: Sagaidac Irina, Cemortan Maria, Cernetchi Olga
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The traditional World Health Organization (WHO) partograph has been used globally for decades as a standard tool for intrapartum labor monitoring. However, accumulating evidence has challenged its underlying assumptions, particularly the use of rigid cervical dilatation thresholds, and highlighted the need for a more individualized and woman-centered approach to childbirth care. In response, the WHO introduced the Labor Care Guide (LCG) in 2020, aligned with the 2018 WHO recommendations on intrapartum care for a positive childbirth experience. This narrative review aims to examine the scientific rationale, global implementation experience, and policy implications of the WHO LCG, with a particular focus on its relevance and application in the Republic of Moldova. A narrative review of peer-reviewed literature, WHO normative documents, International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics position statements, and international implementation studies was conducted. National policy documents and clinical guidelines from the Republic of Moldova were analyzed to contextualize local adoption. International evidence demonstrates that the WHO LCG supports individualized labor monitoring, reduces unnecessary obstetric interventions, and promotes respectful, woman-centered care without compromising maternal or neonatal safety. In 2025, the Republic of Moldova approved a new national guideline on intrapartum care, aligned with WHO principles and the conceptual framework of the LCG. This policy shift reflects a strategic effort to improve the quality of intrapartum care and address ongoing challenges in maternal health outcomes.The transition from the classical WHO partograph to the principles underpinning the WHO LCG represents a significant evolution in intrapartum care. The Republic of Moldova’s experience illustrates how countries with a long tradition of structured labor monitoring can adopt WHO-aligned, woman-centered approaches through national policy reform. Ongoing evaluation of implementation, including assessment of healthcare providers’ preparedness and perceptions, will be essential to ensure sustainable integration and to maximize potential benefits for maternal and neonatal health.

Historical background of the WHO partograph

For several decades, labor monitoring worldwide was based on the classical World Health Organization (WHO) partograph. The origins of this tool date back to 1954, when Emmanuel Friedman introduced the cervicograph, describing a graphical relationship between cervical dilatation and time during labor.1 Friedman’s work, based on observations of labor progression in hospitalized women in the United States, proposed a characteristic sigmoid-shaped labor curve and introduced the concept of a “normal” rate of cervical dilatation. Although pioneering for its time, this model was derived from relatively small, homogeneous populations and reflected obstetric practices that predated modern changes in maternal demographics, analgesia, and obstetric interventions.

In 1972, Philpott and Castle transformed Friedman’s conceptual model into a practical clinical tool by developing the first partograph suitable for routine use in low-resource settings.2 Their innovation introduced the alert line and action line, which were designed to facilitate early recognition of abnormal labor progression and timely referral from peripheral maternity units to higher-level facilities. This approach was particularly influential in sub-Saharan Africa and other regions with limited access to emergency obstetric care, where delays in referral were a major contributor to maternal and perinatal mortality.

Building on this experience, the WHO officially endorsed and recommended the partograph in 1994 as part of the Safe Motherhood Initiative, following a large multicenter trial involving more than 35,000 women across multiple countries.3 This landmark WHO study demonstrated that systematic use of the partograph was associated with reductions in prolonged labor, oxytocin augmentation, emergency cesarean section, and intrapartum stillbirths. Importantly, these benefits were observed primarily in settings where the partograph was correctly used and integrated into clinical decision-making.

Subsequently, the WHO-modified partograph, finalized around 2000, simplified earlier versions by removing the latent phase and standardizing key parameters for maternal and fetal monitoring. This version became the global reference standard for intrapartum care and was incorporated into national guidelines, training curricula, and quality assurance programs in low-, middle-, and high-income countries alike, including the Republic of Moldova.

However, over time, important limitations of the classical partograph became increasingly evident. The assumption that all women should progress at a cervical dilatation rate of 1 cm per hour was found to be overly rigid and poorly reflective of physiological variability. Moreover, the effectiveness of the partograph proved to be highly dependent on contextual factors such as staffing levels, training quality, supervision, and institutional support. In many settings, the tool was incompletely filled, retrospectively completed, or used primarily as a documentation requirement rather than a decision-support instrument. These concerns, together with emerging evidence on diverse labor progression patterns, ultimately prompted a re-evaluation of the traditional partograph and laid the groundwork for the development of a new WHO approach to intrapartum monitoring.4,5

Methods: literature search strategy

A narrative literature review was conducted using PubMed, Scopus, WHO official publications, International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) position statements, and international implementation reports published between 2000 and 2026. Search terms included “WHO Labor Care Guide (LGC),” “partograph,” “intrapartum care,” “woman-centered childbirth,” “labor monitoring,” “implementation,” and “maternal health.” Relevant publications were identified through title and abstract screening, with full-text review conducted for all potentially eligible sources. Priority was given to WHO normative documents, peer-reviewed studies, implementation evaluations, multicountry analyses, and publications relevant to intrapartum care reform and labor monitoring systems.

Studies were excluded if they were not available in English or Romanian, did not address intrapartum care or labor monitoring, or were published prior to 2000. Additional national policy documents and implementation materials from the Republic of Moldova were reviewed to contextualize local adoption of WHO LCG principles.

Given the broad policy and implementation-oriented objective of the manuscript, a narrative review methodology was considered the most appropriate approach to synthesize heterogeneous evidence and contextual experiences across different healthcare settings, where the methodological rigidity of a systematic review would not adequately capture the diversity of implementation contexts and policy experiences described.

Emergence of the new WHO Labor Care Guide

By the early 2000s, a growing body of evidence began to challenge the foundational assumptions underlying the traditional WHO partograph, particularly the expectation that cervical dilatation should progress at a uniform rate of 1 cm per hour during active labor. Large observational studies conducted across diverse populations demonstrated substantial interindividual variability in labor progression, even among women with low-risk pregnancies and favorable perinatal outcomes. These findings indicated that many labors previously classified as abnormal according to classical partograph criteria were, in fact, physiological and self-limiting.4

Fundamental analyses of contemporary labor curves further showed that the onset of accelerated cervical dilatation often occurs later than previously assumed, frequently after 5 to 6 cm of cervical dilatation, particularly in nulliparous women. These data revealed that early application of rigid time thresholds was associated with increased rates of labor augmentation, operative vaginal delivery, and cesarean section, without corresponding improvements in maternal or neonatal outcomes.4

In parallel, qualitative research and patient-reported outcome studies highlighted growing concerns regarding the medicalization of childbirth, limited autonomy of laboring women, and insufficient attention to emotional support, communication, and informed choice during labor. These concerns underscored the need for a more holistic approach to intrapartum care that integrates clinical safety with respect for women’s rights, preferences, and childbirth experience.

In response to this evolving evidence base, the WHO published updated global recommendations on intrapartum care in 2018, explicitly shifting the focus from rigid labor timelines toward woman-centered, respectful, and individualized care. The 2018 WHO guideline emphasized that slow but progressive labor alone should not be an indication for intervention in the absence of maternal or fetal compromise, and called for the elimination of nonevidence-based practices that contribute to unnecessary interventions.5

As a direct operationalization of these recommendations, the WHO introduced the LCG in 2020. The LCG was designed not merely as a documentation tool, but as a clinical decision-support instrument intended to facilitate reflective practice, timely reassessment, and shared decision-making throughout labor and childbirth.6

Conceptual differences between the old and new partograph

The WHO LCG represents a paradigm shift in labor monitoring, rather than a simple modification of the classical partograph. While the traditional partograph primarily focused on graphical representation of cervical dilatation over time, the LCG adopts a multidimensional and woman-centered framework that integrates clinical parameters, supportive care, and decision-making processes.

Key conceptual differences include:

Redefinition of the active first stage of labor as beginning at 5 cm cervical dilatation, reflecting contemporary evidence on physiological labor progression.•Abandonment of a fixed alert/action line based on an assumed linear dilation rate of 1 cm per hour, thereby reducing the risk of premature diagnosis of labor dystocia.•Introduction of evidence-based time thresholds for each centimeter of cervical dilatation, derived from centile distributions of women who achieved vaginal birth with good perinatal outcomes, allowing for individualized assessment rather than uniform expectations.•Explicit documentation of supportive care measures, including maternal comfort, mobility, hydration, pain relief options, emotional support, and the presence of a chosen companion, elements largely absent from the classical partograph.•Formal integration of shared decision-making, requiring clinicians to document discussions with women regarding labor progress, options for care, and agreed management plans.•Structured monitoring of the second stage of labor, acknowledging its clinical importance and addressing a major gap in the traditional partograph.

One of the most consequential changes introduced by the WHO LCG is the redefinition of the active first stage of labor as starting at 5 cm of cervical dilatation, rather than at earlier thresholds traditionally used in the classical WHO partograph. This shift has important clinical, organizational, and economic implications. By recognizing active labor at a later point, fewer women are classified as being in active labor prematurely, which reduces unnecessary admission to labor wards and prolonged stays in prelabor or delivery rooms.

From a clinical perspective, shorter and more appropriate hospital exposure during labor may contribute to a lower risk of nosocomial infections and reduce the likelihood of early, nonindicated interventions. From the woman’s perspective, this approach supports a more physiological labor process, minimizes stress associated with prolonged hospitalization, and is associated with higher patient satisfaction. From a health-system and economic standpoint, reduced length of stay translates into more efficient use of hospital resources and lower costs per birth, which is particularly relevant for publicly funded maternity services. These cause–effect relationships illustrate how changes in labor definitions embedded in the new WHO partogram can influence outcomes beyond clinical indicators alone.

Collectively, these innovations position the WHO LCG as a tool that supports individualized, respectful, and evidence-based intrapartum care, while maintaining clinical vigilance and promoting judicious use of obstetric interventions. Rather than enforcing rigid timelines, the LCG encourages continuous clinical reflection and contextualized decision-making tailored to each woman’s labor trajectory.6,7

Beyond its structural innovations, the WHO LCG embodies a fundamental reorientation of intrapartum care from a predominantly time-driven, provider-centered model toward a woman-centered approach that places equal emphasis on clinical outcomes and the childbirth experience. Central to this new paradigm is the recognition that labor is a physiological, highly individual process, and that clinical intervention should be guided by the overall maternal–fetal condition rather than by deviation from predefined time norms alone.

The core findings underpinning the LCG demonstrate that respectful care, effective communication, continuous support, and shared decision-making are not ancillary components, but integral determinants of safe and positive childbirth outcomes. By explicitly incorporating these elements into routine labor monitoring, the LCG seeks to reduce unnecessary obstetric interventions, enhance women’s autonomy and satisfaction, and support clinicians in making context-sensitive, evidence-based decisions. In this sense, the new WHO partogram functions not only as a monitoring tool, but as a framework for quality intrapartum care, aligning clinical safety with human rights, dignity, and the lived experience of childbirth.5, 6, 7

Global adoption of the WHO Labor Care Guide and reported outcomes

Since its release in 2020, multiple countries have initiated the transition to the WHO LCG. India conducted a large pragmatic stepped-wedge cluster randomized trial involving over 26,000 women, demonstrating a 5.5% reduction in cesarean section rates and an 18% reduction in labor augmentation following LCG implementation.8 Uganda, Nigeria, and South Africa participated in early feasibility and acceptability studies, reporting improved clinical reasoning and enhanced woman-centered care, despite increased documentation demands.7 Indonesia reported moderate-to-high usability and acceptability of the LCG among midwives in rural hospitals, with over 68% of providers considering the tool useful after structured training.9 Multicountry evaluations coordinated by WHO confirmed that the LCG is feasible and acceptable across diverse health system contexts, although sustained training and staffing support are essential.7 These findings should be interpreted as evidence of feasibility and acceptability rather than as definitive proof of clinical superiority over existing labor monitoring approaches. To date, no European country has formally adopted the WHO LCG as a mandatory national labor monitoring tool. Most high-income European countries have either discontinued the use of partographs altogether or continue to rely on nationally developed documentation systems, reflecting both the limited penetration of the LCG in high-income settings and the need for context-specific adaptation prior to broader adoption. Nevertheless, many European guidelines are conceptually aligned with the WHO 2018 intrapartum care recommendations and the principles underpinning the LCG. In 2025, the FIGO issued an official position statement recommending the universal adoption of the WHO LCG, formally endorsing it as a replacement for the classical partograph.10

Accordingly, current evidence regarding the WHO LCG should be considered promising but still evolving. The available data support cautious optimism regarding its potential to improve the quality, safety, and woman-centeredness of intrapartum care, but further context-specific implementation research and robust outcome evaluations remain necessary before definitive conclusions regarding its effectiveness can be drawn.

In Europe, although no country has yet adopted the WHO LCG as a mandatory national standard, several high-income countries have initiated large-scale, structured evaluations prior to potential nationwide implementation.

France represents a distinct European model of intrapartum labor monitoring that differs from both the classical WHO partograph and the WHO LCG. French maternity units do not use the WHO partograph, nor have they adopted the WHO LCG. Instead, labor is monitored using a nationally standardized obstetric partogram, developed within the French medico-legal and organizational framework and widely integrated into electronic medical records.11

Contemporary French obstetric guidelines issued by the Collège National des Gynécologues et Obstétriciens Français emphasize individualized assessment of labor progress, avoidance of rigid cervical dilatation thresholds, and cautious use of oxytocin augmentation. These recommendations reflect growing recognition of the physiological variability of labor and align conceptually with the WHO 2018 intrapartum care principles, despite the absence of a WHO-based monitoring tool.

Recent French literature and national audits have highlighted concerns related to rising intervention rates, including oxytocin use and cesarean section, prompting renewed focus on physiological childbirth, reduction of unnecessary interventions, and improvement of women’s childbirth experience. However, to date, France has not initiated formal pilot studies or national trials evaluating the WHO LCG, and no policy-level transition toward the LCG has been announced.

The French experience thus illustrates a conceptual convergence without instrumental adoption: while the philosophy of woman-centered, individualized intrapartum care is increasingly reflected in national recommendations, labor monitoring continues to rely on locally developed documentation systems rather than on the WHO LCG framework.

Norway has launched the Norwegian WHO LCG Trial (NorWel), a multicenter stepped-wedge study involving 10 hospitals, including Akershus University Hospital (Ahus). In this trial, the LCG is being sequentially implemented during 2025 to 2026, replacing the previously used WHO partograph. All births are monitored using the new template once implementation occurs, and outcomes related to labor management and women’s childbirth experience are being systematically assessed before any national rollout decision is made.12

Similarly, Sweden is conducting the PICRINO trial, a national stepped-wedge cluster randomized controlled study involving 24 maternity units and approximately 100,000 births. A Swedish-adapted version of the WHO LCG is being evaluated with respect to neonatal outcomes, intrapartum cesarean section rates, women’s childbirth experience, staff perceptions, and health-economic impact.13

These Scandinavian initiatives position Norway and Sweden among the first high-income European countries to rigorously assess the effectiveness, safety, and feasibility of the WHO LCG prior to national implementation, reflecting an appropriately evidence-based approach to large-scale adoption of new clinical tools.

This evidence-generation approach contrasts with the strategy in many Western European countries, where partographs have been abandoned altogether in favor of narrative or electronic documentation systems, but aligns closely with WHO and FIGO recommendations advocating structured, evidence-based labor monitoring.10,12,13

Kazakhstan represents one of the earliest examples in Central Asia of structured engagement with the WHO LCG within a health system historically grounded in the classical WHO partograph. Following the release of the WHO 2018 intrapartum care recommendations and the LCG, Kazakhstan initiated a gradual transition toward updated labor monitoring concepts through national clinical guideline revisions, pilot training activities, and adaptation of WHO materials to the local regulatory framework. National obstetric protocols increasingly emphasize individualized assessment of labor progress, delayed diagnosis of labor dystocia, respectful maternity care, and avoidance of unnecessary augmentation—principles that closely mirror those embedded in the WHO LCG. More broadly, the experience of Kazakhstan reflects a regional Central Asian pattern, shared by countries such as Kyrgyzstan, Uzbekistan, and Tajikistan, where the classical WHO partograph remains familiar to clinicians and serves as a structural bridge toward the adoption of next-generation WHO intrapartum care tools. In this context, the WHO LCG is increasingly viewed not as a replacement imposed abruptly, but as an evolutionary step aligned with ongoing health system reforms and quality-of-care initiatives.

Successful implementation of the WHO LCG depends not only on guideline adoption, but also on organizational readiness, staff engagement, sustained educational support, and context-specific adaptation—factors that must be systematically evaluated before the potential benefits of the new intrapartum care model can be fully realized in any given healthcare setting.

National response in the Republic of Moldova

The Republic of Moldova has experienced a notable decline in the annual number of deliveries over the past decade, from approximately 32,591 births per year in 2019 to 23,851 in 2024, reflecting broader demographic trends including emigration and declining birth rates. Despite the reduction in the number of live births, abortions continue to be reported in substantial numbers each year, including among adolescents, reflecting persistent reproductive health needs and highlighting the importance of effective family planning, contraceptive access, and comprehensive reproductive health services.14,15 Together, these trends contribute to a shrinking reproductive-age population and reinforce the importance of ensuring high-quality, evidence-based maternity care for every pregnancy and birth. This context makes the quality of each individual birth encounter increasingly important from a public health perspective.

Maternity care in the Republic of Moldova is organized across 3 levels. There are approximately 31 level 1 maternities distributed across the country, 3 level 2 maternities, and 1 level 3 maternity, which serves as the national referral center for the most complex obstetric cases. Level 1 maternities are not authorized to manage premature deliveries, breech presentations, or obstetric situations requiring surgical intervention or intensive care for the mother or newborn—all such cases are referred to level 2 or 3 facilities. Level 2 maternities are additionally staffed with an intensive care neonatologist and provide a 24-hour anesthesiology service. The level 3 maternity offers the full range of multidisciplinary obstetric and neonatal care. In all Moldovan maternity facilities, intrapartum care is supervised by an obstetrician-gynecologist, who holds primary clinical responsibility and makes all key decisions regarding labor management. A midwife or nurse is always present to assist with the delivery, but does not hold independent clinical responsibility for its conduct. A neonatologist is a standard member of the delivery room team across all maternity levels, ensuring immediate newborn care at every birth. Obstetrician-gynecologists in Moldova complete a 6-year general medicine degree, followed by a 4-year residency in obstetrics and gynecology. Upon completion of residency, practitioners are required to engage in continuous medical education through structured courses and training programs organized by Nicolae Testemițanu State University of Medicine and Pharmacy—the only institution providing medical education in the country. This postgraduate education system represents the primary mechanism through which new clinical guidelines, protocols, and tools, including the WHO LCG, are formally introduced to the practicing obstetric workforce.

An important workforce challenge with direct relevance to the implementation of the WHO LCG is the age distribution of practicing obstetricians, particularly in regions outside the capital. In peripheral areas of the Republic of Moldova, the majority of obstetrician-gynecologists are over 50 years of age, and there is a recognized and growing shortage of young graduates willing to practice in noncentralized or rural settings. This demographic imbalance represents a staffing sustainability concern and has direct implications for the pace and receptiveness of implementation of new clinical tools. Practitioners in the older age group have demonstrated greater reluctance to adopt changes such as a new partograph format or to participate actively in institutional training programs. In contrast, medical residents, younger physicians, and practitioners in their forties have shown considerably greater openness to adopting new approaches and are particularly motivated to align Moldovan practice with the standards of European countries that demonstrate superior results in maternal and perinatal mortality. This generational divide in attitudes toward change is an important human factor that must be acknowledged and addressed in any sustainable implementation strategy.

The Republic of Moldova has a long-standing history of using the WHO-modified partograph as a standardized labor monitoring tool, embedded in national clinical practice, undergraduate and postgraduate medical education, and institutional quality assurance mechanisms. This historical reliance on a structured WHO-based instrument created a favorable foundation for a conceptual transition, rather than an abrupt abandonment of standardized labor monitoring.

The necessity of implementing the WHO LCG in the Republic of Moldova is supported by a body of national and international evidence documenting persistent gaps in the quality of intrapartum care. Despite 2 decades of healthcare reform, the Republic of Moldova continues to report maternal mortality ratios substantially above Western European averages. These figures reflect the continued vulnerability of maternal health outcomes in the national context and underscore the urgency of adopting updated, evidence-based intrapartum care tools.

Stratulat et al. provided early evidence of perinatal health disparities and systemic quality-of-care deficiencies within the Moldovan maternity system, highlighting the need for standardized, evidence-based approaches to intrapartum monitoring.16 Bacci et al.17 contextualized these challenges within the WHO European Region, demonstrating that countries with healthcare profiles comparable to Moldova continued to experience preventable adverse maternal and perinatal outcomes and that structured quality improvement initiatives were associated with measurable gains in care quality.17 Marian-Pavlenco et al.18 contributed Moldova-specific outcome data further substantiating the national rationale for adopting WHO-aligned intrapartum care principles.18

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the transition to the WHO LCG in the Republic of Moldova represents not only an alignment with international recommendations endorsed by WHO and FIGO, but a response to documented local need—grounded in years of published evidence identifying persistent gaps in the quality, safety, and woman-centeredness of intrapartum care in the national maternity system.

In this context, Republic of Moldova in alignment with the WHO 2018 recommendations on intrapartum care for a positive childbirth experience and the WHO LCG, developed a new clinical guideline at the end of 2024 “Intrapartum care for a positive childbirth experience,” that was approved by the Ministry of Health of the Republic of Moldova in June 2025.19

The development of this guideline was coordinated with academic input from Nicolae Testemițanu State University of Medicine and Pharmacy, in collaboration with national maternal health stakeholder and aimed to ensure coherence between international evidence, national clinical realities, and medical education curricula. The guideline reflects a deliberate shift from prescriptive, time-driven labor management toward a model that prioritizes clinical judgement, physiological variability, and women’s autonomy, while maintaining patient safety and accountability.

Specifically, the new guideline emphasizes:•Individualized labor monitoring, consistent with the principles underpinning the WHO LCG, allowing for physiological variation in labor progression;•Respectful maternity care, including effective communication, emotional support, and respect for women’s preferences and dignity during childbirth;•Shared decision-making, with explicit encouragement of informed discussions between healthcare providers and women regarding labor progress and management options;•Rational use of obstetric interventions, aiming to reduce unnecessary augmentation and operative deliveries while ensuring timely intervention when clinically indicated.

Importantly, the approval of the guideline represents not only a regulatory update, but also a strategic policy decision to align Moldovan intrapartum care with contemporary global standards endorsed by WHO. Within the European context—where many high-income countries have moved away from partographs altogether—the Republic of Moldova occupies a distinctive position as a country transitioning from the classical WHO partograph toward the principles of the WHO LCG through a guideline framework.

This approach positions Moldova as an early policy-level adopter of WHO-aligned intrapartum care principles in Eastern Europe, creating a structured pathway for gradual implementation, evaluation, and refinement of the new labor monitoring paradigm.

Maternal mortality in the Republic of Moldova has varied considerably over the past 2 decades, ranging from a maximum of approximately 44 per 100,000 live births to a historic low of 9.4 per 100,000 live births recorded in 2024.20 During the COVID-19 pandemic years, the maternal mortality ratio reached a peak of approximately 59 per 100,000 live births, reflecting the additional burden placed on the national maternity system during that period. Due to the country’s small population and correspondingly low absolute numbers of maternal deaths, 5-year aggregated figures are methodologically recommended for reliable interpretation of trends. Perinatal mortality in recent years has been recorded at approximately 11 per 1000 births—a figure that, while reflecting progress achieved through ongoing healthcare reforms, remains higher than that reported by European countries of comparable population size. These epidemiological indicators collectively underscore the persistent need for quality improvement in intrapartum care and provide a compelling rationale for adopting WHO-aligned, woman-centered labor monitoring principles within the national healthcare system.

In this context, the adoption of a woman-centered intrapartum care model represents a strategic step toward placing women at the center of clinical decision-making and strengthening the health system’s commitment to preventing avoidable maternal deaths. The implementation of WHO-aligned labor monitoring tools is therefore expected to support both improved clinical outcomes and a more positive childbirth experience. It is anticipated that, with routine daily use by healthcare professionals, this approach will contribute to achieving these dual objectives.

Capacity building and need for implementation assessment

As part of the national implementation strategy, extensive training activities were conducted to support the introduction of the new standardized intrapartum care guideline. In total, 10 structured training sessions were organized, involving approximately 200 healthcare professionals from maternity units across the country. Participants included obstetricians, midwives, and maternity ward managers from the northern, central, and southern regions of the Republic of Moldova.

These trainings were coordinated with the involvement of the Ministry of Health and UNFPA, and represented a key mechanism for ensuring consistent understanding of the new guideline principles, including woman-centered care, shared decision-making, and the practical use of WHO-aligned labor monitoring approaches. The regional distribution of trainings was designed to facilitate equitable national implementation, reduce variability in clinical practice, and strengthen local capacity for sustainable adoption of the standardized protocol.

These activities aimed to familiarize healthcare professionals with both the new Guideline and the WHO LCG, addressing theoretical principles and practical application.

Evidence from high-income settings demonstrates that the successful implementation of the WHO LCG depends not only on guideline adoption, but also on organizational readiness, staff engagement, and sustained educational support. In both the Norwegian NorWel trial and the Swedish PICRINO study, structured training programs, local clinical champions, and continuous evaluation of staff experience are integral components of the implementation strategy.12,13

These studies emphasize that monitoring healthcare providers’ understanding, attitudes, and perceived feasibility of the LCG is critical to avoid superficial or inconsistent use of the tool. Accordingly, systematic assessment of staff preparedness and perceptions represents an essential step in ensuring fidelity to the new intrapartum care model.

These training activities constituted one of the core methods for national-level implementation of the standardized protocol, complementing regulatory approval with practical capacity building. However, experience from other countries suggests that training alone is insufficient to ensure long-term adoption. This underscores the importance of subsequent evaluation of healthcare providers’ preparedness, understanding, and perceptions regarding the new intrapartum care model.

It is important to acknowledge that, at the current stage of implementation, comprehensive postimplementation clinical outcome data, hospital-level comparative analyses, and formal baseline vs post-implementation maternal and neonatal trend evaluations are not yet available. The national guideline was approved in June 2025 and the implementation process remains at an early stage. The absence of such data represents a recognized limitation of the present manuscript, which should be interpreted primarily as a narrative review and early implementation case study rather than as a formal effectiveness evaluation or outcome study. Robust clinical outcome data, including pre/post comparisons of intervention rates, maternal morbidity indicators, and neonatal outcomes, are expected to become available as implementation matures and systematic audit processes are established across the national maternity network

Preliminary findings from an ongoing pilot assessment

To complement the implementation process and address the absence of empirical Moldova-specific data noted by the reviewers, a pilot cross-sectional survey of healthcare professionals involved in intrapartum care was initiated in 2026. Given the absence of a previously validated questionnaire specifically designed to assess healthcare providers’ perceptions of the WHO LCG, the survey instrument was developed de novo, informed by the implementation guidance and assessment framework published by the WHO in the LCG Implementation Resource Package. The survey was adapted to the national context with the aim of assessing healthcare providers’ knowledge, perceptions, and early experiences regarding the WHO LCG following its introduction into national clinical practice.

The questionnaire was distributed to obstetricians, midwives, and maternity ward managers working across maternity facilities of all 3 levels in the Republic of Moldova. Participation was voluntary and anonymous. The survey instrument included items assessing self-reported understanding of the WHO LCG, perceived alignment of the new tool with labor physiology, anticipated impact on the quality of intrapartum care, practical usability, documentation burden, and barriers and facilitators to implementation. The target sample size for the full study is 200 healthcare professionals. At the time of manuscript revision, 55 responses had been collected and subjected to preliminary analysis.

Preliminary analysis of the 55 respondents demonstrated a good level of self-assessed understanding of the WHO LCG, with a mean score of 4.1 out of 5. More than half of participants considered the new tool to better reflect the physiology of labor and expressed the view that it has the potential to improve the quality of intrapartum care. Nevertheless, perceptions regarding practical usability varied considerably, and concerns related to documentation burden, time requirements, and adaptation to new clinical workflows were frequently reported.

A recurring and clinically significant observation among respondents related to the structural design of the WHO LCG itself. The majority of participants reported that the second stage of labor section of the LCG provides insufficient space for detailed clinical documentation. Specifically, practitioners expressed the need for expanded documentation fields within the second stage section to allow more frequent recording of fetal heart rate assessments, and to clearly document the timing and clinical rationale for operative decisions—including the application of obstetric forceps or vacuum extraction. Respondents indicated that greater structural detail in the second stage section would better reflect the intensity and complexity of clinical decision-making during this critical phase of labor, and would support more accurate and accountable intrapartum documentation. This finding suggests that a nationally adapted version of the WHO LCG, with an expanded second stage section tailored to the documentation requirements and medicolegal standards of the Moldovan maternity system, may be warranted as part of the ongoing implementation process.

These preliminary findings highlight the importance of continued implementation support, staff engagement, and systematic evaluation of user experience as essential components of the national implementation strategy. It is acknowledged that the present pilot assessment is ongoing, that the 55 responses analyzed represent a preliminary subset of the planned 200-participant sample, and that these findings should therefore be interpreted as exploratory rather than as definitive conclusions regarding the overall success or challenges of implementation. A comprehensive report of the full study findings, including complete quantitative and qualitative analyses, is planned as a subsequent publication.

Limitations of current evidence and controversies in LCG implementation

Despite growing international interest in the WHO LCG, the current evidence base remains relatively limited and heterogeneous. Most available studies focus on feasibility, usability, and implementation processes rather than long-term maternal and neonatal outcomes, and the majority have been conducted in low- and middle-income countries, limiting the generalizability of findings to high-income or Eastern European healthcare settings.

Although the large Indian stepped-wedge cluster randomized trial demonstrated reductions in cesarean section rates and labor augmentation following implementation of the WHO LCG, considerable caution is required when interpreting and generalizing these findings. The Indian trial was conducted within a specific healthcare system characterized by high baseline intervention rates, a predominantly midwife-led intrapartum care model, and organizational structures that differ substantially from those in European countries. Differences in staffing models, baseline cesarean section rates, institutional protocols, workforce organization, and intrapartum care culture may significantly influence implementation outcomes and limit the direct applicability of these results to other contexts, including the Republic of Moldova.

Several controversies related to LCG implementation have been consistently described across different healthcare settings. These include concerns regarding increased documentation workload—a finding corroborated by our own preliminary pilot data from Moldova, where documentation burden was among the most frequently reported concerns—as well as the need for sustained staff training and supervision, variability in interpretation of labor progression criteria, and potential resistance to changes in long-established obstetric practices, particularly among more experienced practitioners. The structural design of the second stage section of the WHO LCG has also been identified as a specific area requiring adaptation in the Moldovan context, with practitioners reporting insufficient space for detailed documentation of fetal heart rate assessments and operative delivery decisions.

In high-income European countries, adoption of the WHO LCG remains limited, with most healthcare systems either having abandoned structured partograph-based monitoring altogether or continuing to rely on locally developed electronic or narrative documentation systems. The ongoing Scandinavian trials—NorWel in Norway and PICRINO in Sweden—represent the most rigorous current efforts to generate high-quality evidence on LCG effectiveness, safety, and feasibility in high-income settings, and their results will be critical in informing future European implementation decisions.

Accordingly, current evidence regarding the WHO LCG should be considered promising but still evolving. The available data support cautious optimism regarding its potential to improve the quality, safety, and woman-centeredness of intrapartum care, but further context-specific implementation research, including robust outcome evaluations and adaptation studies in diverse healthcare settings, remains necessary before definitive conclusions can be drawn.

Planned evaluation of implementation effectiveness

WHO has published an implementation resource package for the LCG, which provides practical tools and indicators to support quality monitoring and assessment of LCG implementation across maternity services. This package can be adapted into facility-level audit check-lists to evaluate adherence to evidence-based intrapartum care practices and enhance quality improvement.21

In line with international implementation science principles and WHO recommendations, it is recommended and planned during the current year to conduct a structured survey of medical personnel involved in intrapartum care.

The planned questionnaire should assess:•Perceived effectiveness of the new guideline,•Usability and acceptability of the WHO LCG,•Barriers and facilitators to implementation,•Training needs and organizational challenges.

Conclusions

The transition from the classical WHO partograph to the WHO LCG represents a scientifically justified evolution in intrapartum care. The Republic of Moldova has taken important steps toward aligning national practice with global evidence through protocol development and professional training. Systematic evaluation of implementation readiness and effectiveness is a necessary next step to ensure that the potential benefits of the new WHO partogram are fully realized in clinical practice.
