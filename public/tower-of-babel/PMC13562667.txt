Reinforcement learning-assisted distributionally robust energy management for multi-microgrid networks
Authors: Huixuan Li, Yihan Zhang, Yongle Zheng, Zhongfu Tan, Xianyu Yue, Xiaoliang Jiang, Yijun Jiang, Shiqian Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This paper proposes a hybrid reinforcement learning–assisted distributionally robust optimization (RL–DRO) framework for robust and economically efficient energy management in interconnected multi-microgrid systems under renewable, demand, and price uncertainty. The framework integrates deep reinforcement learning to generate adaptive scheduling policies with a Wasserstein-metric distributionally robust optimization formulation that enhances robustness against probability distribution shifts and non-stationary uncertainty. The upper level maximizes cumulative rewards of reinforcement learning agents representing individual microgrids, while the lower level optimizes power dispatch and energy exchange decisions subject to operational and network constraints. A five-microgrid test system equipped with photovoltaic generation, battery storage, and flexible loads is evaluated using 300 stochastic scenarios derived from historical data. Simulation results demonstrate that the proposed RL–DRO framework achieves a superior trade-off between cost efficiency and operational robustness when compared with deterministic, stochastic, and standalone reinforcement learning benchmarks. Specifically, the framework reduces expected operational cost by 14.8%, improves operational feasibility and service continuity as reflected by a proxy-based resilience indicator from 84.5% to 96.1%, and decreases the loss-of-load probability from 4.8% to 2.1%. Furthermore, the proposed approach maintains near-optimal performance as the Wasserstein ambiguity radius increases to 0.25, highlighting its robustness to distributional shifts and adverse uncertainty realizations. Rather than modeling explicit physical disturbances or fault-driven contingencies, the proposed framework focuses on sustaining feasible, adaptive, and cost-effective operation under severe uncertainty and stressed operating conditions. The hybrid learning–optimization paradigm thus unifies data-driven adaptability with theoretical robustness, providing a scalable and uncertainty-aware pathway for autonomous operation of future distribution networks.

Introduction

The global power system is undergoing a profound transformation driven by the rapid proliferation of distributed energy resources (DERs)1,2, the decentralization of grid control architectures, and the increasing penetration of renewable energy at the distribution level. In this context, microgrids—localized clusters of photovoltaics, wind generation, energy storage systems, and flexible loads—have emerged as a cornerstone of next-generation smart grids3,4. By enabling localized energy management and coordinated operation at the network edge, microgrids offer significant potential for improving operational efficiency, supporting carbon reduction objectives, and enhancing robustness under uncertainty5,6. However, as microgrids become increasingly autonomous and interconnected, their operation is exposed to substantial challenges arising from renewable intermittency, stochastic demand patterns, and cyber–physical variability7,8. These uncertainties complicate coordinated scheduling and dispatch, particularly in multi-microgrid systems where operational decisions are tightly coupled through network constraints and energy exchange mechanisms.

Against this backdrop, a wide range of studies have investigated microgrid scheduling and economic operation using centralized optimization frameworks, often under assumptions of perfect information, full coordination, and synchronized decision-making9,10. While such approaches can be effective in small-scale or highly supervised environments, they face fundamental limitations in scalability, adaptability, and robustness when applied to systems comprising numerous interacting microgrids or agents11,12. Centralized solutions are prone to communication bottlenecks, lack flexibility in the presence of local uncertainties, and are poorly suited to decentralized settings with privacy constraints or asynchronous control. Moreover, scenario-based stochastic formulations are sensitive to modeling errors and distributional shifts in renewable generation and demand, particularly under deep renewable penetration13,14. Although these studies have laid important foundations for uncertainty-aware energy management, their architectural choices and uncertainty-handling mechanisms limit their applicability in highly decentralized and non-stationary operating environments.

The above limitations reveal a clear research gap in the existing literature. Specifically, there remains a lack of distributed, learning-enabled optimization frameworks that can support autonomous multi-microgrid operation while explicitly accounting for uncertainty and distributional ambiguity. Most prior works address uncertainty through predefined scenarios or probabilistic models, yet they do not incorporate adaptive mechanisms that continuously update decision policies as operating conditions evolve. Furthermore, the integration of learning-based decision-making with physically grounded and robustness-aware optimization models has not been sufficiently explored for interconnected multi-microgrid systems. Motivated by this gap, this paper focuses on developing an uncertainty-aware and scalable operational paradigm in which intelligent microgrid agents can adapt their local strategies through interaction with the environment, while preserving system-level feasibility and economic efficiency.

Within this context, the proposed framework adopts a multi-layered, optimization-oriented structure for coordinated multi-microgrid energy management under uncertainty. A high-resolution, multi-agent dispatch formulation is first developed to capture system-level operational interactions among distributed generation, energy storage evolution, flexible demand, and inter-microgrid energy exchange. Network behavior is represented through a LinDistFlow-based, AC-approximate model that preserves voltage magnitude variations, active and reactive power coupling, and loss-related effects in a computationally tractable manner. This formulation is embedded within a distributionally robust optimization (DRO) framework to explicitly address ambiguity in renewable generation and demand distributions. By constructing Wasserstein ambiguity sets from historical data, the DRO layer hedges against sampling errors and non-stationary uncertainty, ensuring that microgrid operations remain feasible and cost-effective even under adverse and worst-case distributional deviations. On top of this robust optimization layer, a deep reinforcement learning (RL) mechanism is integrated to enable autonomous decision-making among distributed agents. Each microgrid is modeled as an independent learning agent that iteratively updates its stochastic policy using the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm, guided by a multi-objective reward structure encoding operational cost, voltage deviation penalties, power imbalance indicators, and constraint violations. Learning is performed in a fully distributed manner through interaction with the environment, without centralized orchestration, while a consensus-driven regularization mechanism is introduced to mitigate policy divergence and encourage coordinated behavior across the network15.

The methodological novelty of this work lies in the tight coupling between the distributionally robust optimization formulation and the reinforcement learning process within a unified decision-making framework. Rather than employing reinforcement learning as a heuristic or high-level supervisory tool, robustified reward signals derived from the DRO layer are directly embedded into the RL optimization objective. This design enables agents to learn policies that are adaptive to data-driven uncertainty while remaining grounded in worst-case operational considerations. As a result, the proposed framework supports robust and economically efficient operation, scalability to large networked settings, and sustained feasibility under severe uncertainty and stressed operating conditions, without relying on explicit modeling of physical disturbances or fault-driven contingencies. Within this framework, robustness is reflected through feasibility preservation and bounded performance degradation under uncertainty, while reliability-related indicators are used in a proxy-based manner to assess service continuity. Resilience is therefore interpreted in an operational sense, capturing the system’s ability to maintain acceptable performance under adverse uncertainty rather than recovery from physical disruptions. Extensive simulations on benchmark IEEE distribution systems demonstrate that the proposed framework consistently outperforms deterministic, stochastic, and standalone reinforcement learning baselines in terms of economic efficiency, renewable integration, and operational robustness, thereby highlighting the effectiveness of the proposed RL–DRO paradigm for uncertainty-aware multi-microgrid energy management16.

In this work, the term resilience is adopted in an operational and optimization-oriented sense, rather than in the protection- or event-driven context commonly used in fault isolation, disturbance–recovery trajectory analysis, or islanding survivability studies. Specifically, resilience is interpreted as the ability of a multi-microgrid system to maintain operational feasibility, service continuity, and acceptable performance levels under stochastic disturbances, uncertainty, and adverse operating conditions. Accordingly, resilience is evaluated through proxy-based performance indicators that are consistent with the system-level modeling scope of the proposed framework, including loss-of-load probability, feasibility probability under uncertainty, and performance degradation reflected in reward or cost metrics. These indicators quantify the system’s capacity to absorb variability, adapt to uncertainty, and avoid infeasible or severely degraded operating states, rather than explicitly modeling post-disturbance recovery dynamics or protection actions. This definition aligns with a growing body of optimization- and data-driven energy management literature, where resilience is characterized through probabilistic robustness, constraint satisfaction, and sustained operational performance under uncertainty. Literature review

The rapid transformation of power systems driven by the large-scale deployment of distributed energy resources (DERs) has stimulated extensive research on modeling, optimization, and control of decentralized energy systems2,15. Early studies in this area primarily focused on generation scheduling and power flow optimization under deterministic assumptions, often formulated using linear or mixed-integer linear programming techniques16,17. These works established foundational models for microgrid dispatch and network-constrained operation, but their reliance on perfect forecasts and centralized solvers limited their adaptability in dynamic and uncertain environments. As renewable penetration and storage integration increased, more sophisticated formulations were developed to capture multi-timescale interactions, non-convex device dynamics, and real-time reconfigurability. Nevertheless, many of these approaches continue to assume strong model-based predictability and centralized coordination, making them less effective in coping with real-world uncertainty, variability, and decentralization.

To better reflect the decentralized nature of modern distribution systems, a substantial body of literature has investigated multi-agent and distributed architectures for microgrid scheduling and coordination18,19. Game-theoretic formulations, including Nash equilibrium models and Stackelberg leader–follower structures, have been employed to capture strategic interactions among multiple prosumers or interconnected microgrids20,21. These studies have provided valuable insights into coordination incentives, bounded rationality, and equilibrium behavior. However, equilibrium-based approaches typically assume that agents possess accurate knowledge of payoff functions, strategy spaces, and system responses, assumptions that are difficult to satisfy in practice due to limited observability, communication delays, and privacy constraints. To alleviate these issues, distributed optimization methods based on primal–dual decomposition, alternating direction methods, and consensus algorithms have been proposed to enable cooperative dispatch without full system observability. While such methods improve scalability and decentralization, they still depend on explicit uncertainty models and static objective formulations, limiting their ability to adapt to evolving operating conditions.

In parallel, robust optimization and stochastic programming techniques have been extensively explored to explicitly address uncertainty in DER-rich power systems22. Scenario-based stochastic optimization and chance-constrained programming have been used to model renewable intermittency and demand fluctuations by sampling from probabilistic distributions23,24. Although these approaches offer probabilistic guarantees, they often require large scenario sets to capture rare but impactful events, resulting in high computational complexity. More recently, distributionally robust optimization (DRO) has emerged as a theoretically grounded and computationally attractive alternative25,26. DRO constructs ambiguity sets of probability distributions around empirical data and optimizes the worst-case expected performance over these sets. In power system applications, DRO has been applied to model uncertainty in wind and solar generation, load demand, and electricity market prices27. In particular, Wasserstein-based DRO formulations provide favorable properties such as out-of-sample performance guarantees and finite-sample robustness. Despite these advantages, existing DRO-based approaches are predominantly embedded within static optimization frameworks and lack mechanisms for adaptive learning or behavioral evolution in decentralized multi-agent environments.

To introduce dynamic adaptability and sequential decision-making capabilities, reinforcement learning (RL) has attracted increasing attention in power and energy system applications28. RL enables agents to learn control policies directly through interaction with the environment, without requiring complete system models. Applications of RL span load scheduling, HVAC control, energy storage management, and electric vehicle charging, demonstrating promising performance in terms of cost reduction and operational flexibility. The advent of deep reinforcement learning (DRL) has further enhanced scalability by employing deep neural networks as function approximators for policies and value functions. Actor–critic methods such as DDPG, A3C, and PPO have been successfully applied to continuous control problems in microgrid and smart energy management scenarios. However, many RL-based approaches exhibit limited robustness under uncertainty, particularly when trained on finite or biased historical datasets. Moreover, standard RL formulations often struggle with rare but high-impact events, such as renewable ramping or unexpected system disturbances, due to their reliance on empirical reward signals29.

Recognizing these limitations, several recent studies have attempted to integrate robustness considerations into reinforcement learning frameworks through adversarial training, robust Markov decision processes, or risk-sensitive reward formulations. Some hybrid approaches incorporate scenario-based uncertainty into RL training or employ CVaR-type risk measures to mitigate downside risk. Nonetheless, most existing methods remain loosely connected to rigorous DRO theory grounded in ambiguity sets, and few explicitly exploit Wasserstein-based robustness within learning-based decision processes. In addition, many hybrid models treat reinforcement learning as a heuristic enhancement layered on top of traditional optimization solvers, rather than as an integral component of a unified decision-making framework.

At the intersection of distributed optimization, robust uncertainty modeling, and learning-based control, only a limited number of studies have explored the joint use of DRO for uncertainty representation, deep reinforcement learning for policy learning, and distributed coordination mechanisms for multi-agent systems. Most multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) frameworks in energy systems either neglect formal risk modeling or assume idealized cooperation among agents. Furthermore, explicit coordination mechanisms such as policy consensus constraints and the implications of robust reward formulations on learning stability remain insufficiently investigated. The mathematical formulation of the proposed framework establishes the analytical foundation for describing the multi-layered decision-making process in a multi-microgrid system operating under renewable, demand, and price uncertainties. The modeling structure integrates two fundamental dimensions: (1) the operational physics and constraints of distributed energy resources (DERs), including photovoltaic (PV) generation units, energy storage systems (ESS), and flexible loads; and (2) the learning-driven decision interactions between reinforcement learning (RL) agents and the optimization environment. Together, these dimensions allow the joint representation of stochastic, dynamic, and coupled decision processes within a unified mathematical framework.

The resulting formulation constitutes a two-level optimization problem. The upper level maximizes the cumulative reward of RL agents, representing the learned decision-making process, while the lower level minimizes the total operational cost of the system under all physical and economic constraints. The lower-level model includes nodal power balance constraints, ESS dynamics, SoC boundaries, line flow limits, voltage regulations, and temporal coupling among decision variables. The complete formulation thus integrates the physical operation of distributed energy resources with adaptive learning-based control under stochastic uncertainty. This hybrid structure enables the system to continuously learn from environmental interactions, adjust to uncertain conditions, and maintain robustness against distributional deviations. Unlike static optimization, the proposed model embeds adaptive intelligence within its mathematical core, resulting in a self-correcting energy management mechanism. The network model adopted in this work is a LinDistFlow-based, AC-approximate formulation designed for system-level optimization and learning integration. It preserves voltage magnitude variations, active and reactive power coupling, and loss-related effects in a linearized and computationally tractable manner, while avoiding the full nonlinearity of AC power flow equations. The proposed formulation is neither a DC power flow model nor a protection-grade AC representation; instead, it provides a consistent approximation regime tailored for large-scale, uncertainty-aware, reinforcement learning–enhanced energy management in distribution networks. Within this modeling scope, discrete contingency events, including line faults, protection-triggered trips, and inverter outages, are not explicitly modeled. The framework is intentionally focused on coordinated operational decision-making under uncertainty, rather than on event-driven fault analysis, protection coordination, or post-contingency recovery processes. Explicitly representing such contingencies would require detailed protection logic, switching dynamics, and device-level failure models, which lie outside the analytical objectives of the present study and would substantially increase model complexity without directly supporting learning-based operational optimization. Accordingly, all reported performance and resilience-related results should be interpreted as reflecting operational robustness and feasibility preservation under stochastic variability and adverse operating conditions, rather than system behavior under fault-induced contingencies or protection-driven islanding events. Solution approach

The methodology operationalizes the hybrid reinforcement learning–assisted optimization framework through a tightly coupled learning–optimization pipeline. Its main objective is to integrate the adaptability of reinforcement learning (RL) with the reliability of mathematical optimization, enabling each microgrid to autonomously discover and refine operational strategies under high uncertainty. The methodological structure comprises four interconnected stages: system state encoding, RL policy training, uncertainty propagation, and robust multi-agent coordination.

In the first stage, the physical and economic status of each microgrid is encoded into a normalized state vector that captures energy generation, storage levels, demand, and market price signals. This vector forms the input space for RL agents. Each agent is modeled as an autonomous decision-maker that observes the environment and learns an optimal control policy through a reward-driven exploration–exploitation process. The reward function incorporates multiple objectives including cost reduction, load satisfaction, and resilience enhancement. Over successive episodes, the agents update their policy parameters using gradient-based reinforcement learning algorithms until convergence toward stable policies is achieved.

In the second stage, the learned actions from RL agents are integrated into the optimization layer. The RL actions (such as storage charge/discharge decisions and energy exchange levels) serve as initial estimates for the optimization problem. The optimization layer then refines these actions using mathematical programming techniques that ensure physical feasibility and constraint satisfaction. This step effectively combines exploratory intelligence with deterministic reliability, balancing adaptability with robustness.

The third component of the methodology focuses on modeling and managing uncertainty propagation. The uncertain variables, including solar irradiance, load, and price, are sampled to generate stochastic scenarios. Instead of assuming a fixed probability distribution, the system constructs an ambiguity set using Wasserstein-based metrics, defining a bounded region around the empirical distribution. This quantifies uncertainty explicitly and provides a mechanism for worst-case protection, ensuring that the optimization remains feasible even when actual conditions deviate significantly from historical samples.

The fourth methodological stage addresses the coordination mechanism across multiple microgrids. A decentralized algorithmic scheme allows local RL agents to optimize their policies while periodically exchanging summarized information through a central coordinator. The algorithm alternates between local learning updates and centralized optimization refinements until convergence. This design maintains scalability for large-scale systems and preserves data privacy by avoiding full information exchange among agents. Computational implementation employs a Python-based environment using TensorFlow for RL training and Gurobi for robust optimization solving. Training processes utilize parallel stochastic gradient descent executed on NVIDIA A100 GPUs, while post-optimization analysis is conducted on Intel Xeon Gold processors.

Prior to presenting the reinforcement learning formulation, it is noted that electricity prices are modeled as exogenous stochastic state variables available to the learning agents. The framework does not explicitly account for strategic price-maker behavior or endogenous price formation mechanisms, as the primary objective is to study operational decision-making and robustness under uncertain price signals. Results

To evaluate the performance of the proposed reinforcement learning-assisted distributionally robust optimization framework for autonomous microgrid operation, we conduct extensive simulations based on a modified IEEE 33-bus radial distribution system. The test system is populated with five microgrids located at buses 7, 9, 13, 18, and 24, each equipped with hybrid distributed energy resources (DERs) including photovoltaic generation, lithium-ion battery storage, and flexible demand clusters. Each microgrid contains a 150–300 kW rooftop PV system modeled with real irradiance profiles from the California Energy Commission’s SolarAnywhere dataset, featuring temporal resolution of 15 minutes over a 24-hour horizon. Battery storage capacities vary from 250 kWh to 500 kWh with charge/discharge efficiency of 0.95 and degradation cost modeled quadratically. Load data are synthesized from residential and commercial demand traces derived from the Pecan Street dataset, scaled to maintain realistic daily peak loads ranging between 100 kW and 400 kW per microgrid. To reflect uncertainty in both renewable generation and consumption behavior, we construct a set of 1,000 historical disturbance scenarios spanning a variety of weather types and consumption patterns. These scenarios are clustered using a Wasserstein barycenter-based k-medoids algorithm into 20 representative profiles, which are used to form the empirical distribution Pin our distributionally robust optimization (DRO) formulation. The Wasserstein ambiguity set is defined with a radius \varepsilon = 0.1, tuned via cross-validation to balance out-of-sample robustness with computational tractability. Price signals for local flexibility markets are modeled using historical time-of-use tariffs from PG&E, with dynamic multipliers introduced to simulate real-time congestion and scarcity. Physical parameters including line impedances, thermal capacities, and voltage bounds are consistent with standard IEEE distribution test feeders. Reactive power is not neglected; rather, it is co-optimized under a linearized DistFlow formulation, with nodal voltage regulated within [0.95, 1.05] p.u.

Before presenting the numerical results, it is important to clarify the interpretation of extreme events within the scope of this study. Extreme events are represented here in a probabilistic and distributional sense, rather than through explicit modeling of physical disruptions or infrastructure damage. Specifically, the proposed framework characterizes extreme operating conditions as statistically adverse realizations of uncertainty, including rare but severe deviations in renewable generation, load demand, and market price signals. These scenarios impose significant stress on system feasibility and operational performance, thereby serving as a mechanism to evaluate robustness at the decision-making level. This representation of extreme events is intentionally aligned with the system-level, optimization-oriented objectives of the proposed framework. Modeling physical disruptions such as component damage, line failures, or disaster-induced outages would require detailed failure dynamics, protection actions, and restoration processes, which lie outside the analytical scope of reinforcement learning–enhanced operational scheduling. Instead, the use of distributional ambiguity enables the assessment of worst-case uncertainty realizations while maintaining computational tractability and scalability for large-scale multi-microgrid coordination. Accordingly, all results reported in this section should be interpreted as reflecting operational robustness and feasibility preservation under severe uncertainty and unfavorable operating conditions, rather than system behavior under fault-induced or disaster-driven physical disruptions. This interpretation ensures consistency between the adopted modeling assumptions and the performance conclusions drawn from the simulation results.

In interpreting the results, it is important to distinguish the notions of robustness, reliability, and resilience as they are used in this study. Robustness refers to the ability of the proposed framework to maintain feasibility and stable performance under uncertainty and adverse operating conditions, particularly with respect to worst-case realizations captured by the distributional ambiguity set. In this context, robustness is primarily associated with constraint satisfaction and bounded performance degradation under uncertainty, rather than with discrete failure events. Reliability is traditionally concerned with the continuity of service delivery and is often quantified through metrics such as loss-of-load probability. In this work, reliability-related indicators are employed in a limited and proxy-based manner to reflect service adequacy under uncertainty, without explicitly modeling component failure rates, outage durations, or repair processes. Reliability is therefore not treated as an independent modeling objective, but rather as a supporting performance indicator within the operational scope of the proposed framework. Resilience is adopted in an operational and system-level sense that builds upon robustness and reliability considerations, emphasizing the system’s ability to sustain acceptable performance under severe uncertainty and unfavorable operating conditions. Resilience is evaluated through proxy-based measures such as feasibility probability, loss-of-load probability, and performance degradation, rather than through disturbance–recovery trajectories, fault isolation dynamics, or post-event restoration processes. All resilience-related claims in this paper should be interpreted within this operational and proxy-based framework, ensuring consistency with the adopted modeling assumptions and analytical scope.

Implementation and deployment considerations

While the numerical results demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed RL–DRO framework in improving economic performance and operational robustness under uncertainty, it is important to discuss the computational aspects associated with practical deployment. The simulation results presented in this section are obtained through an offline training and evaluation process, in which reinforcement learning agents are trained using historical data and representative uncertainty realizations, and robustness calibration is supported by optimization solvers. This offline computation stage may involve GPU resources and solver execution, which is common in learning-enabled energy management studies and is primarily intended for policy development rather than real-time operation.

In contrast, once training is completed, online operation is dominated by decentralized policy inference at the individual microgrid level. This inference process typically requires only lightweight forward evaluations of trained neural networks and local decision updates, resulting in low computational overhead that can be handled by standard CPU-based controllers or edge devices. As a result, real-time deployment does not require continuous execution of computationally intensive optimization solvers, and the operational burden scales naturally with the number of microgrids through distributed execution. The numerical results therefore reflect a realistic deployment paradigm in which computational complexity is concentrated in the offline stage, while online control remains efficient and responsive. To further support scalability in large multi-microgrid systems, several practical acceleration and deployment strategies can be adopted without altering the core framework. These include parallel and distributed training across agents, solver warm-starting and reduced-order approximations for robustness calibration, as well as surrogate or hierarchical optimization schemes operating at slower timescales. Such strategies provide feasible pathways for mitigating computational burden while preserving the uncertainty-aware and coordination properties demonstrated in the numerical results. Overall, these considerations indicate that the proposed framework can be practically extended to larger systems by combining offline computation with efficient online inference, aligning the numerical findings with realistic engineering deployment scenarios.

Scalability and network topology considerations

The numerical evaluation in this work is conducted on a representative multi-microgrid system with a limited number of interconnected microgrids and a radial distribution structure, which serves to clearly illustrate the effectiveness of the proposed framework in coordinated and uncertainty-aware operation. It is acknowledged that larger-scale systems and non-radial or meshed network topologies introduce additional complexity in terms of power flow interactions, coupling strength, and coordination dynamics. However, the proposed framework itself is not inherently restricted to a specific network size or topology, as its decision-making and learning mechanisms are formulated at the agent level and coupled through general network constraints.

From a methodological perspective, the distributed and modular nature of the proposed approach provides a natural pathway for extension to larger systems with a greater number of microgrids. Each microgrid operates as an autonomous agent with localized decision-making, allowing scalability to be addressed through parallel execution and decentralized coordination rather than centralized control. Similarly, the underlying optimization and learning components can be combined with more general network models to accommodate non-radial or meshed topologies, where power flow interactions and loop constraints become more prominent. These extensions do not alter the core principles demonstrated in the numerical results, but rather expand the range of application scenarios in which the proposed framework can be evaluated.

Discussion on bilevel interaction and solver fidelity

The proposed framework adopts a bilevel interaction structure in which reinforcement learning operates at the upper level and optimization-based decision evaluation is performed at the lower level. While this structure is presented in an idealized form, it does not require the lower-level optimization problem to be solved to strict global optimality at every learning iteration. In practical implementations, the lower-level solver provides structured feasibility guidance and robustness-aware feedback, which may be subject to numerical tolerances, approximation errors, or delayed updates.

From a learning perspective, the reinforcement learning process naturally interacts with environments that exhibit stochasticity, noise, and imperfect feedback. As a result, bounded solver inaccuracies or solution latency are absorbed into the learning dynamics as part of the environment uncertainty, rather than causing instability or invalidating the learning process. The role of the lower-level optimization is therefore to shape the reward and constraint landscape in a consistent manner, rather than to act as an exact oracle at every iteration. In practical deployments, solver fidelity and computational latency can be managed through implementation-level strategies such as relaxed optimality tolerances, warm-starting, asynchronous solver updates, or slower-timescale optimization relative to policy learning. These considerations do not alter the conceptual validity of the bilevel framework, but rather reflect realistic engineering trade-offs when integrating learning and optimization. Conclusion

This study developed a hybrid reinforcement learning–assisted optimization framework for robust and economically efficient energy management in interconnected multi-microgrid systems under renewable, demand, and price uncertainty. By integrating adaptive learning with distributionally robust decision optimization, the proposed approach bridges data-driven intelligence with operational feasibility and cost effectiveness under adverse and non-stationary uncertainty. Reinforcement learning agents capture dynamic interactions among distributed energy resources and iteratively learn adaptive control policies, while the robust optimization layer refines these decisions to ensure feasibility preservation and bounded performance degradation under worst-case distributional deviations30.

Numerical experiments on a five-microgrid system demonstrate that the proposed framework achieves significant improvements in operational performance when compared with conventional deterministic, stochastic, and standalone reinforcement learning approaches. Specifically, the framework reduces expected operating cost by 14.8%, improves operational resilience in a proxy-based sense by 11.6%, and decreases the loss-of-load probability by more than half. Temporal dispatch results reveal coordinated utilization of photovoltaic generation and battery storage under uncertainty, while spatial power flow patterns highlight emergent cooperation among microgrids driven by learned policies. These results confirm that adaptive learning and structured robust optimization can jointly deliver scalable, reliable, and cost-efficient energy management without relying on explicit modeling of physical disturbances or fault-driven contingencies31.

Beyond the specific test system, the proposed framework provides a generalizable paradigm for embedding learning mechanisms into uncertainty-aware operational decision-making in decentralized energy systems. Rather than addressing event-driven fault recovery or infrastructure-level resilience, the framework focuses on sustaining feasible and efficient operation under severe uncertainty and stressed operating conditions. Future work will extend this approach to multi-agent reinforcement learning with continuous state–action representations, dynamic pricing feedback, and communication constraints, further enhancing its applicability to large-scale regional energy coordination and robust operational planning under increasing uncertainty.
