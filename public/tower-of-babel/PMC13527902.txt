Changes in thoracic spinal curvature after nuss procedure in adult pectus excavatum
Authors: Nay Htut, Der-En Keong, I-Shiang Tzeng, Yeung-Leung Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPectus excavatum (PE) is commonly associated with spinal abnormalities. However, the effect of the Nuss procedure on thoracic scoliosis remains unclear. This study therefore aimed to characterize the dynamic changes in the thoracic Cobb angle (tCA) among adult PE patients undergoing Nuss procedure.MethodsA total of 186 Patients with PE who underwent the Nuss procedure and subsequent bar removal (BR) were retrospectively analyzed. Clinical data were collected, including serial tCA measurements from posteroanterior chest radiographs taken preoperatively, at 1 month, 3–6 months, and 1 year postoperatively, as well as 1 day before and 1 week after BR for analysis. Subgroups were stratified by sex, Haller index (≥ 4 vs. <4), bar number (1–3), bar orientation (oblique vs. horizontal), bar flipping within 3 months, and preoperative tCA (≥ 10° vs. <10°).ResultsCompared to the preoperative mean tCA, the tCA increased significantly at 1 month postoperatively (5.4 ± 4.2° vs. 5.9 ± 4.4°, p < 0.001). It then decreased significantly by 1 day before BR (5.4 ± 4.2° vs. 4.9 ± 3.9°, p < 0.001) and further at 1 week after BR (5.4 ± 4.2° vs. 4.4 ± 4.0°, p < 0.001). All subgroup analyses demonstrated a significant reduction in the tCA following BR.ConclusionsThe Nuss procedure induced a temporary increase in tCA at 1 month postoperatively, followed by gradual improvement during the bar maintenance period and a net reduction below the preoperative level after bar removal. Notably, patients with mild thoracic scoliosis also experienced a reduction in tCA after complete PE correction. While this reduction was not clinically meaningful, it still indicates that the procedure does not worsen thoracic spinal alignment and may offer some improvement.Keywords: Pectus excavatum, Nuss procedure, Adult, Thoracic Cobb angle, Thoracic scoliosis

Background

Pectus excavatum (PE), or funnel chest, accounts for approximately 65–95% of chest wall anomalies. Characterized by posterior displacement of the sternum and adjacent costal cartilages, it affects up to 8 in 1,000 births with a 5:1 male predominance [1, 2]. PE is not merely a cosmetic concern; it is also associated with cardiopulmonary compromise and a significantly increased prevalence of scoliosis (13.1%) [3] compared to the general population (3.1%) [4]. Although the Nuss procedure effectively corrects the anterior chest deformity, its effect on spinal alignment remains controversial.

Previous studies have reported conflicting outcomes regarding Cobb angle (CA) changes following the Nuss procedure in patients with PE, with reports of both reduction and progression. Most available evidence tends to focus on pediatric and adolescent populations [5–13], while data on solely adult patients remain scarce. This retrospective study examined longitudinal thoracic Cobb angle (tCA) dynamics in adult PE patients undergoing the Nuss procedure with subsequent bar removal (BR), also evaluating the association of clinical (sex, Haller index) and surgical (bar number, orientation, flipping) factors with thoracic spinal alignment.

Methods

Patient cohort and data collection

We conducted a retrospective review of PE patients aged ≥ 20 years who underwent the Nuss procedure at Taipei Tzu Chi Hospital between August 2014 and December 2024. Inclusion criteria included age ≥ 20 years at the time of surgery, to establish an adult cohort distinct from adolescents (typically aged 10–19 years), and completion of BR. Patients were excluded if they had a history of connective tissue disorders or chest/spinal surgery, required reoperation for Nuss bar adjustment, had a bar implantation duration < 2 years, had missing follow-up data, or presented with a perioperative tCA ≥ 20°. After applying these criteria, 186 of 215 patients were included in this study (Fig. 1). The collected data included demographic information at the time of the Nuss procedure—including sex, age, and preoperative Haller index (HI) from chest CT—as well as surgical variables, such as the number of bars (1–3), bar orientation (horizontal or oblique), and bar flipping within three months (Table 1). Missing follow-up data was defined as the absence of age, sex, preoperative HI, or chest radiographs at any of the six predefined timepoints. A HI ≥ 3.25 on chest CT typically indicates the need for surgical intervention, particularly in symptomatic patients, while an HI ≥ 4 is often considered indicative of extreme severe deformity due to its association with greater cardiopulmonary impairment. Therefore, we set the cutoff at HI ≥ 4 for subgroup stratification [14–16]. Horizontal bar placement was defined as both ends of the bar positioned within the same intercostal space, whereas oblique bar placement required the ends to occupy different intercostal spaces. The choice between a horizontal or oblique bar placement was determined by the orientation that achieved optimal elevation of the deepest point of the anterior chest wall depression. Bar flipping was defined as a slope angle (α) > 30° according to Fan et al.‘s criterion [17].

Thoracic Cobb angle measurement

The tCA was measured manually on standing posteroanterior chest radiographs of the thoracic spine using the Cobb method [18] at six time points: preoperatively; 1 month, 3–6 months, and 1 year postoperatively; 1 day before BR, and 1 week after BR (Figs. 2 and 3). The same thoracic vertebral endplate levels, identified on preoperative radiographs, were used for measurements at all five subsequent time points. To minimize inter-observer error, we use the same picture archiving and communication system (PACS) and measurement tool. First, two blinded observers measure the same set of five chest radiographs for training and discuss discrepancies until the variation is below 3°. Next, we remove patient identifiers, define the upper and lower endplates on the preoperative radiographs, and randomize the case order for the two observers. The two sets of measurement results are then averaged to obtain the final value.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while categorical variables are expressed as counts and percentages. Longitudinal changes relative to the preoperative baseline were analyzed using repeated-measures analysis, and pairwise comparisons were conducted with paired t-tests adjusted using the Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. Comparisons between independent groups were performed using independent t-tests. Statistical significance was defined as a P-value < 0.05. All reported values were rounded to one decimal place. To ensure confidentiality, data were de-identified prior to analysis. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Patient clinical characteristics are presented in Table 1. In 186 patients, a significant increase in the tCA was observed 1 month postoperatively compared to the preoperative baseline (preoperative: 5.4 ± 4.2° vs. 1 month postoperatively: 5.9 ± 4.4°, p < 0.001). However, the tCA decreased significantly both 1 day before BR (preoperative: 5.4 ± 4.2° vs. 1 day pre-BR: 4.9 ± 3.9°, p < 0.001) and 1 week after BR (preoperative: 5.4 ± 4.2° vs. 1 week after BR: 4.4 ± 4.0°, p < 0.001) (Table 2).

Discussion

The Nuss procedure transiently exacerbated the tCA, presumably due to early postoperative postural restriction, pain, and the mechanical effects of the bar on the sternum and ribs. However, spinal alignment improved during the bar maintenance period and was ultimately restored to levels exceeding the preoperative baseline after BR. Notably, all subgroups demonstrated significant tCA reduction at 1 week following BR.

Intergroup analysis of baseline preoperative tCA revealed significant differences by PE severity (HI), sex, and bar orientation (Table 9). Patients with HI ≥ 4 exhibited significantly higher baseline tCA values than those with HI < 4; females and oblique bar placements also demonstrated higher tCA compared to males and horizontal bars. Notably, females (HI ≥ 4 female-to-male ratio, 0.44:1 vs. overall 0.27:1) and oblique bars (HI ≥ 4 oblique-to-horizontal ratio, 0.95:1 vs. overall 0.86:1) were overrepresented among HI ≥ 4 cases. This suggests that females present with greater pectus severity (possibly due to breast tissue masking milder deformities) and that higher-HI cases more often require oblique bar placement for optimal sternal elevation. The scoliosis prevalence in our PE cohort was 15.5%, slightly lower than the 19.9% mean prevalence reported across all pectus excavatum studies by van Es et al. [3], but comparable to their 13.1% “probable prevalence” estimate derived from high-quality studies.

Ghionzoli et al. [5] reported a mean CA reduction of 1.5° (from 10.9° to 9.4°) following complete treatment (Nuss procedure with subsequent BR) in pediatric and adolescent patients (10–18 years old) with PE and adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS). They proposed that positioning the Nuss bar in patients with concurrent PE and AIS may act as a form of bracing therapy, promoting a more balanced distribution of forces on the spine and thereby reducing the scoliotic curve. In our adult cohort, we observed a comparable magnitude of correction, with a mean tCA reduction of 1.1° after BR. Park, et al. [6] observed a decrease in tCA after corrective surgery (Nuss procedure followed by BR) in the early correction group (age at surgery < 10 years), but an increase in the late correction group (age at surgery ≥ 10 years). They also reported that females tended to have higher tCA values and that patients with more severe pectus deformity exhibited significantly higher tCA, findings consistent with our study. Their cohort primarily comprised children and adolescents, most with tCA between 0° and 20°. In contrast, our study included only adults and demonstrated a significant tCA reduction after BR, with all patients having preoperative tCA < 20°. This difference may be attributable to the influence of the adolescent growth spurt in their pediatric population, absent in our adult cohort. Chung et al. [7] reported that, in a cohort of 63 patients ranging from children to adults, a preoperative CA < 15° was associated with postoperative improvement, whereas a CA > 15° was a risk factor for curve progression. In our study, we observed a reduction in tCA after bar removal in patients with preoperative tCA ≥ 10°; however, the effect in those with tCA > 15° remains inconclusive due to the small number of such cases.

Several case reports have described scoliosis progression after PE correction. Niedbala et al. [8] reported two pediatric cases of acquired scoliosis after the Nuss procedure, with Cobb angles of 14° and 16°, attributed to possible acute asymmetric pressure from the repair. Meng et al. [9] described a 14-year-old boy who developed scoliosis within one month postoperatively, with rapid progression to a Cobb angle of 54°. This early postoperative progression pattern is consistent with our findings, although the magnitude of change in the report was much greater. Floccari et al. [10] also reported two adolescent cases in which scoliosis progressed to a surgical-range deformity (from 49° to 75° and from 39° to 65°, respectively) within three months of the Nuss procedure. These reports primarily involved pediatric patients, whose sternum, ribs, and spine are more susceptible to external mechanical forces due to ongoing skeletal growth.

İşcan et al. [11] reported divergent outcomes for Cobb angle (CA) progression following complete PE correction: a significant overall increase—particularly in males and children—but no significant change in adults or females. These findings differ from ours, a discrepancy likely attributable to differences in age distribution. Na et al. [12] reported a significant increase in CA during the bar implantation period compared to preoperative values. However, in their cohort of 177 patients (children to adults), CA after BR did not differ significantly from baseline. They attributed these spinal curvature changes to high mechanical stress on the thoracic cavity during bar implantation. Our study observed a similar early postoperative tCA increase (at 1 month); however, unlike their findings, our adult cohort showed gradual improvement during bar maintenance, culminating in a significant reduction after BR. We attribute this divergence to differences in study age spectrum and our postoperative care protocol, which included 4 weeks of postural restrictions (avoiding stooped postures and excessive chest stretching), followed by gradual chest expansion exercises and rehabilitation, with no activity limitations after 3 months. Although several studies have reported CA progression after the Nuss procedure and BR in patients with baseline CA < 15°, most reports link progression to moderate-to-severe scoliosis (baseline CA ≥ 20°). In our cohort, all patients had baseline tCA < 20°, with significant tCA reduction after BR.

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective, single-center design and limited sample size may limit generalizability. Second, tCA assessment relied on chest radiographs with manual measurements confined to thoracic vertebrae, which may have introduced some measurement bias. Third, the absence of a comparison group precludes definitive attribution of changes to the intervention. Fourth, the lack of long-term follow-up after BR limits assessment of durability. Fifth, despite being statistically significant, the observed changes in tCA did not reach a clinically meaningful threshold. Therefore, future studies utilizing whole-spine radiographs, including control or comparison groups, across the full scoliosis severity spectrum (mild, moderate, severe), and with long-term follow-up after BR are needed to better evaluate PE correction’s impact on spinal alignment.

Conclusions

Among adult PE patients, the tCA exhibited a distinct temporal pattern: transient exacerbation one month after the Nuss procedure, gradual improvement during bar maintenance, and a significant net reduction by the bar removal period compared to preoperative values. Subgroup analyses consistently showed significant tCA reduction after BR. Although the magnitude of change did not meet clinically meaningful thresholds, these findings still indicate that complete PE correction in adults with mild or no thoracic scoliosis does not worsen thoracic spinal alignment but rather has the potential to improve it.
