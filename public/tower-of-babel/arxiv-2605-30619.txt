Reward Learning from Best-of- N N Preference Data: Targets, Tradeoffs, and Design Principles
Rattana Pukdee rpukdee@cs.cmu.edu, Maria-Florina Balcan ninamf@cs.cmu.edu, Pradeep Ravikumar pkr@cs.cmu.edu

Abstract
Best-of- N N sampling is widely used to construct pairwise preference data: N N candidates are drawn from a base distribution, and the best is paired with a rejected response. Despite its widespread use, what Bradley–Terry (BT) reward learning extracts from such data, and how to choose N N and the base distribution, remain unclear. We specialize a recent analysis of preference data via its induced conditional distribution to Best-of- N N . For independent-reference variants, we derive closed-form reward targets as explicit functions of N N and the base distribution, and show that they preserve the latent reward ranking. For the practical Best-vs-Random and Best-vs-Worst variants, chosen and rejected responses are coupled through the same candidate set, so exact BT representability generally fails; nevertheless, bounded-class minimizers approach the reference targets as N N grows. Although margin and connectivity are known to govern sample efficiency in pairwise preference learning, Best-of- N N couples them through N N in opposing directions: larger N N widens pairwise margins but reduces connectivity. This trade-off yields two design principles: use larger N N when preference labels are the bottleneck, smaller N N when generation is the bottleneck; and shape the base distribution to place mass between the responses whose comparison matters most at test time. Experiments on synthetic and real preference data support the predicted dependence on sample size and base-distribution shape. 1 1 1 Code available at: https://github.com/rattaoup/bon-preference

Introduction

Pairwise preference data is a general and increasingly important supervision signal in machine learning. It has been extensively studied in the literature on learning to rank

In modern AI systems, pairwise preference data is often used to train reward models, typically with a Bradley-Terry objective, and these reward models may then be used to further optimize a language model through reinforcement learning
(
50
;
40
;
31
)
. Preference data is also used more directly in fine-tuning the model as well
(
36
;
28
)
. A common approach to construct the preference data is through Best-of-
N
sampling. For a given context,
N
responses are sampled from a base distribution, and an oracle identifies the best response as the chosen one. The rejected response is then selected either uniformly from the remaining
N-1
responses (Best-vs-Random) or as the worst response (Best-vs-Worst). The special case
N=2
recovers the standard pairwise-comparison setting
(
8
)
. The common intuition is that larger
N
improves this data, since the chosen response is better in absolute terms. We show this intuition is incomplete: what matters is not only the quality of the chosen response, but which comparisons the dataset makes available. We study reward learning as a clean lens on the role of data with two fundamental questions.

1.

When preference data is generated through Best-of-N sampling, what reward function is induced by the resulting dataset under Bradley-Terry objective?

2.

How should the candidate-set size N and the base distribution be chosen ?

Our analysis builds on a recent distribution-agnostic framework
(
34
)
that analyzes preference data through the conditional preference distribution (CPRD), characterizing when it is Bradley-Terry representable and showing that finite-sample learning is governed by margin and comparison connectivity. That framework leaves the induced reward targets, margins, and connectivity abstract, since they depend on the data-generation mechanism. We instantiate it for Best-of-
N
preference data, where the order-statistic structure makes these quantities concrete and reveals how they scale with
N
. Our contributions are summarized as follows:

Contributions.

1.

Targets:
We study a clean reference setting in which the rejected response is sampled independently. In this setting, we characterize the reward targets induced by Best-of-
N
preference data in closed form and show that they preserve the ranking of an underlying ground-truth reward. This identifies what signal reward learning is recovering from the data. For the practical Best-vs-Random and Best-vs-Worst settings, we show that the corresponding population minimizers do not admit the same clean characterization, but approach these reference targets as
N
grows.

2.

Tradeoffs:
We show that
N
controls a fundamental tradeoff between margin and comparison connectivity. Increasing
N
pushes chosen responses toward the top of the reward distribution, which amplifies pairwise margins and makes individual comparisons more informative. But this same concentration narrows the range of responses being compared, reducing comparison connectivity and leaving intermediate comparisons underrepresented. These two effects pull in opposite directions and govern sample efficiency.

3.

Design principles:
We derive two actionable guidelines. The candidate-set size
N
should be chosen based on the resource bottleneck: larger
N
is more helpful when preference labels are scarce, whereas smaller
N
is often preferable when preference labels are cheap and data is abundant. Beyond choosing
N
, the base distribution itself is also a design lever through what we call the between-mass principle: the induced margin between two responses grows with the amount of base-distribution mass placed between them in reward order. Shaping the base distribution to put mass between the response pairs that matter under the target evaluation distribution selectively amplifies those margins. We validate these predictions on both synthetic and real preference data.

Taken together, our results provide a learning-theoretic foundation for reward learning from Best-of-
N
preference data, while yielding concrete principles for data design in practice.

Learning from Best-of-
N
Preference Data.

Understanding how preference data affects the final model is an important question, since it can inform the design of better datasets. Recent work suggests that better preference data leads to better downstream policies

;

;

We study a related question from a different perspective: rather than analyzing the end-to-end policy outcome, we stop at the reward-learning stage and ask how the preference data shapes the learned reward function. This provides a cleaner view of the effect of the data itself, without conflating it with the base policy’s behavior.

In practice, preference data can be constructed in several ways, including by comparing responses from language models of different capacities

3
Problem Setup

In practice, Best-of-
N
(BoN) preference data pipelines may rely on human annotators, reward models, or LLM judges to identify preferred responses. We study a clean theoretical abstraction of this setting in which preferences are induced by an underlying latent reward function
r^{*}
, even though
r^{*}
itself is never observed. This abstraction allows us to ask what reward function is learned from the resulting comparison data.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a context space and
\mathcal{Y}
be a set of outcomes. Assume that
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
are finite but can be exponentially large. Let
r^{*}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
be a target latent reward function. The supervision signal is in the form of pairwise comparison data where triplets
(x,y^{+},y^{-})
indicate that a chosen outcome
y^{+}
is preferred over a rejected outcome
y^{-}
given a context
x
i.e. the latent reward
r^{*}(x,y^{+})>r^{*}(x,y^{-})
.

To construct the pairwise preference data, the chosen outcome
y^{+}
is sampled from the Best-of-
N
mechanism. First, we sample
N
outcomes from a base distribution
y_{1},\dots,y_{N}\sim P_{\text{base}}(\cdot\mid x)
, the outcome with the highest reward score is the chosen outcome,
y^{+}=\arg\max_{y_{i}}r^{*}(x,y_{i})
. This procedure only requires access to an oracle that can identify the best outcome among
N
candidates, rather than access to the reward scores
r^{*}(x,y_{i})
themselves. This is a common approach in practice to construct pairwise preference data. For the rejected outcome
y^{-}
, two dominant approaches are

•

Best-vs-Random

(
23
;
20
)
: we sample
y^{-}
from
\{y_{1},\dots,y_{N}\}\setminus\{y^{+}\}
uniformly at random.

•

Best-vs-Worst

(
32
;
48
)
: the outcome with the lowest reward score among
\{y_{1},\dots,y_{N}\}
is the rejected outcome,
y^{-}=\arg\min_{y_{i}}r^{*}(x,y_{i})
. This may require another oracle call to identify the worst outcome.

Whenever we observe a collision
y^{+}=y^{-}
, we discard the triplet. Best-vs-Random and Best-vs-Worst guarantee that the latent reward score
y^{+}
is always higher than
y^{-}
.
Let
P
be the probability distribution of the BoN preference data. Let
\mathcal{H}
be a hypothesis class of reward functions. A common learning objective for reward learning is Bradley-Terry (BT) objective given by

\mathcal{L}_{P}(r)=\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[-\log\sigma(r(x,y^{+})-r(x,y^{-}))\right]

(1)

when
\sigma(t)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-t}}
is the sigmoid function. Since the loss only depends on the difference between the reward scores of the chosen and rejected outcomes,
r(x,y^{+})-r(x,y^{-})
, the reward is identifiable only up to an additive constant. To fix this ambiguity, we restrict
\mathcal{H}
to normalized reward functions satisfying
\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mu}[r(x,y)]=0
for a fixed reference distribution
\mu
over
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
. For example, this can be a uniform distribution. Finally, we define some notations that will be useful for our analysis. First, score quantiles
F(x,y)
capture the probability that a random outcome from the base distribution
P_{\text{base}}(\cdot\mid x)
has a lower or strictly lower reward score than the reward of
y
.

3.1
Strict and Inclusive Score Quantiles.

For any outcome
y\in\mathcal{Y}
, define the
strict quantile

F^{-}(x,y):=\Pr_{y^{\prime}\sim P_{\text{base}}(\cdot\mid x)}[\,r^{*}(x,y^{\prime})<r^{*}(x,y)\,]

(2)

as the probability that a randomly drawn outcome from the base distribution has strictly lower reward than
y
. We also define the
inclusive quantile

F(x,y):=\Pr_{y^{\prime}\sim P_{\text{base}}(\cdot\mid x)}[\,r^{*}(x,y^{\prime})\leq r^{*}(x,y)\,]=F^{-}(x,y)+P_{\text{base}}(y\mid x).

(3)

We also define pairwise margin,

3.2
Margin.

For any reward function
r
and any context
x
and outcomes
y,y^{\prime}
, the pairwise margin of
r
is given by

\Delta_{r}(x;y,y^{\prime}):=r(x,y)-r(x,y^{\prime})

(4)

With these definitions in hand, we turn to our first question: what reward function does BoN preference data induce?

4
Characterizing the Learned Reward

We begin with the infinite data setting and analyze the minimizer of the population risk (equation
1
). For analysis, we first define reference variants
Best-vs-Independent
and
Best-vs-Worst-Independent
, where the negative sample is drawn independently from the base distribution and the Worst-of-N sampling respectively. These variants have a much cleaner analysis and we will later on connect it back to the practical settings of Best-vs-Random and Best-vs-Worst. We will use subscript
BR,BW,BI,BWI
to denote if the data distribution and the reward function corresponds to Best-vs-Random, Best-vs-Worst, Best-vs-Independent and Best-vs-Worst-Independent respectively.

4.1
Independent Rejected Outcome

We start with the Best-vs-Independent scenario. The rejected outcome is independent of the chosen one, we can factorize the probability mass of
P_{\text{BI}}
as

P_{\text{BI}}(x,y^{+},y^{-})=P(x)P_{\text{BoN}}(y^{+}\mid x)P_{\text{base}}(y^{-}\mid x)

(5)

where
P_{\text{BoN}}(y\mid x)
is the conditional distribution of the outcome sampled with the Best-of-N sampling. We can derive
P_{\text{BoN}}(y\mid x)
in terms of score quantiles.

4.1
.

Assuming that there is no tie in reward score, for any context
x
and outcome
y
, the probability mass of
P_{\text{BoN}}
is given by

P_{\text{BoN}}(y\mid x)=F(x,y)^{N}-F^{-}(x,y)^{N}

(6)

Positive-negative conditional independence under
P_{\text{BI}}
is useful because
34
showed that it is exactly the condition under which the induced pairwise preference admits a Bradley–Terry representation. In other words, the BT form is not merely a modeling assumption here, but the true functional form of
\Pr_{\text{BI}}(y\succ y^{\prime}\mid x)
, the conditional probability of preferring
y
over
y^{\prime}
given
x
:

\Pr_{\text{BI}}(y\succ y^{\prime}\mid x):=\frac{P_{\text{BI}}(x,y,y^{\prime})}{P_{\text{BI}}(x,y,y^{\prime})+P_{\text{BI}}(x,y^{\prime},y)}=\sigma(r^{*}_{\text{BI}}(x,y)-r^{*}_{\text{BI}}(x,y^{\prime}))

(7)

when
r^{*}_{\text{BI}}
is the population minimizer.
34
also showed that the minimizer is given by the log ratio between the distribution of the chosen and rejected responses. These results allow us to characterize the target reward function
r^{*}_{\text{BI}}
under
P_{\text{BI}}
.

4.2
Identification of
r^{*}_{\text{BI}}
.

Let
r^{*}_{\text{BI}}\in\arg\min_{r}\mathcal{L}_{P_{\text{BI}}}(r)
then there exists a function
c:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
such that

r^{*}_{\text{BI}}(x,y)=\log\frac{P_{\text{BoN}}(y\mid x)}{P_{\text{base}}(y\mid x)}+c(x)

(8)

for almost every pair
(x,y)
with respect to
P_{\text{BI}}
. Furthermore,
r^{*}_{\text{BI}}
preserves the ranking induced by
r^{*}
that is for any
x,y,y^{\prime}
with non-zero probability mass,

r^{*}(x,y)>r^{*}(x,y^{\prime})\implies r^{*}_{\text{BI}}(x,y)>r^{*}_{\text{BI}}(x,y^{\prime})

(9)

In other words, even though we never observe
r^{*}
, minimizing the BT loss on BoN data recovers a reward function that agrees with
r^{*}
on every pairwise comparison. Similarly, for the Best-vs-Worst-Independent, by conditional independence, we can also factorize the distribution
P_{\text{BWI}}
as

P_{\text{BWI}}(x,y^{+},y^{-})=P(x)P_{\text{BoN}}(y^{+}\mid x)P_{\text{WoN}}(y^{-}\mid x)

(10)

and derive a similar characterization for
r^{*}_{\text{BWI}}
.

4.3
.

Assume that there is no tie in reward score, for any context
x
and outcome
y
, the probability mass of
P_{\text{WoN}}
is given by

P_{\text{WoN}}(y\mid x)=(1-F^{-}(x,y))^{N}-(1-F(x,y))^{N}

(11)

4.4
Identification of
r^{*}_{\text{BWI}}
.

Let
r^{*}_{\text{BWI}}\in\arg\min_{r}\mathcal{L}_{P_{\text{BWI}}}(r)
then there exists a function
c:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
such that

r^{*}_{\text{BWI}}(x,y)=\log\frac{P_{\text{BoN}}(y\mid x)}{P_{\text{WoN}}(y\mid x)}+c(x)

(12)

for almost every pair
(x,y)
. Furthermore,
r^{*}_{\text{BWI}}
preserves the ranking induced by
r^{*}
that is for any
x,y,y^{\prime}
with non-zero probability mass,

r^{*}(x,y)>r^{*}(x,y^{\prime})\implies r^{*}_{\text{BWI}}(x,y)>r^{*}_{\text{BWI}}(x,y^{\prime})

(13)

4.2
Best-vs-Random and Best-vs-Worst

Next, we turn to the Best-vs-Random and Best-vs-Worst scenarios where the chosen outcome and rejected outcome are no longer independent. Without independence, we show that the population risk minimizer with respect to
P_{\text{BR}}
and
P_{\text{BW}}
does not have a closed-form minimizer anymore. We introduce a notion of comparison distribution to help with the analysis. This captures how often a pair of outcomes are compared together for a given context, regardless of which is preferred.

4.5
Comparison distribution.

For any distribution
P
over
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Y}
, the comparison distribution
\widetilde{P}
is a distribution over
x
and an unordered pair
\{y,y^{\prime}\}
with
y\neq y^{\prime}
with density

\widetilde{P}(x,\{y,y^{\prime}\})\propto P(x,y,y^{\prime})+P(x,y^{\prime},y)

(14)

4.6
Best-vs-Random and Best-vs-Worst perform pairwise margin maximization.

Assume that there is no tie in reward score.
For each context and pair of outcomes
(x,\{y,y^{\prime}\})
, define

y_{\max}\in\arg\max_{u\in\{y,y^{\prime}\}}r^{*}(x,u),\qquad y_{\min}\in\arg\min_{u\in\{y,y^{\prime}\}}r^{*}(x,u)

Then for any hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}
, the population risk minimizer
r^{*}_{\text{BR}}\in\arg\min_{r\in\mathcal{H}}\mathcal{L}_{P_{\text{BR}}}(r)
satisfies

r^{*}_{\text{BR}}\in\arg\min_{r\in\mathcal{H}}\;\mathbb{E}_{(x,\{y,y^{\prime}\})\sim\widetilde{P}_{\mathrm{BR}}}\Big[-\log\sigma\!\big(r(x,y_{\max})-r(x,y_{\min})\big)\Big]

The same result holds for
r^{*}_{\text{BW}}
by replacing the comparison distribution
\widetilde{P}_{\mathrm{BR}}
with
\widetilde{P}_{\mathrm{BW}}
.
In particular, both objectives are the same pairwise margin-maximization loss, differing only in the comparison distribution.

The proposition shows that for any pair of outcomes
(\{y,y^{\prime}\})
, the BT objective maximizes the margin between the outcomes with higher and lower true reward scores. As a result, if
\mathcal{H}
is unbounded, then we can decrease the BT objective by increasing the margin on any pair indefinitely,
\Delta_{r}(x,y_{\max},y_{\min})\to\infty
, which leads to an unbounded minimizer. As such, we do not have a nice closed-form minimizer for
r^{*}_{\text{BR}}
and
r^{*}_{\text{BW}}
, and the minimizer depends on properties of
\mathcal{H}
. Regardless, we can still show that
r^{*}_{\text{BR}}\to r^{*}_{\text{BI}}
and
r^{*}_{\text{BW}}\to r^{*}_{\text{BWI}}
in margin semi-norm as
N
grows.

4.7
Margin semi-norm.

For a preference distribution
P
, the margin semi-norm between two reward functions
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
is defined as

\lVert r_{1}-r_{2}\rVert_{\Delta,P}=\mathbb{E}_{(x,y,y^{\prime})\sim P}\left[(\Delta_{r_{1}}-\Delta_{r_{2}})^{2}\right]

(15)

4.8
r^{*}_{\text{BR}}
is close to
r^{*}_{\text{BI}}
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a convex hypothesis class that is bounded by some constant
B
. Let
r^{*}_{\text{BR}}\in\arg\min_{r\in\mathcal{H}}\mathcal{L}_{P_{\text{BR}}}(r)
then there exists a constant
c_{B}
such that for any
N\geq 2
,

\lVert r^{*}_{\text{BR}}-r^{*}_{\text{BI}}\rVert_{\Delta,P_{\text{BR}}}\leq\frac{c_{B}}{N-1}

(16)

4.9
r^{*}_{\text{BW}}
is close to
r^{*}_{\text{BWI}}
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a convex hypothesis class that is bounded by some constant
B
. Let
r^{*}_{\text{BW}}\in\arg\min_{r\in\mathcal{H}}\mathcal{L}_{P_{\text{BW}}}(r)
then there exists a constant
c_{B}
and
\rho<1
such that for any
N\geq 2
,

\lVert r^{*}_{\text{BW}}-r^{*}_{\text{BWI}}\rVert_{\Delta,P_{\text{BW}}}\leq c_{B}\rho^{N}

(17)

The key step in the proof is to show that
P_{\text{BR}}
and
P_{\text{BW}}
are close in total variation distance to
P_{\text{BI}}
and
P_{\text{BWI}}
, respectively. Establishing this requires deriving closed-form expressions for the probability mass functions of these distributions and then carrying out careful algebraic comparisons; this is the most technically involved part of the argument.
The convexity of
\mathcal{H}
is then used only to transfer this distributional closeness to closeness of the corresponding population minimizers, via a standard argument from learning theory. We defer the full proof to Appendix
A
.

These results imply that, as
N
grows, the margins induced by
r^{*}_{\text{BR}}
and
r^{*}_{\text{BW}}
become close to those induced by
r^{*}_{\text{BI}}
and
r^{*}_{\text{BWI}}
, respectively. Since
r^{*}_{\text{BI}}
and
r^{*}_{\text{BWI}}
achieve perfect accuracy, and since the ranking is determined by the sign of the margin, it follows that the Best-vs-Random and Best-vs-Worst settings also approximately recover the ranking induced by
r^{*}
. Having identified these as sensible population targets, we turn to the finite-sample setting, where a fundamental tradeoff emerges: increasing
N
helps in one way, but hurts in another.

Remark:
Unlike the independent variants, the pairwise preferences induced by
P_{\text{BR}}
and
P_{\text{BW}}
do not admit a BT form. However, the TV bounds above imply they approach those of the independent variants as
N
grows, making BT an increasingly reasonable model.

5
Sample Complexity

In practice, we only have access to a finite number of triplets, so even if the population risk minimizer is a good target, we must also ask how quickly we can learn it. We measure the performance of the learned reward in terms of the consistency of ordering with respect to the target reward function. We define the accuracy as follows.

5.1
Accuracy.

Let
Q
be a test distribution over
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Y}
. The accuracy of a reward function
r
with respect to
Q
and
r^{*}
is given by

\operatorname{Acc}_{Q}(r)=\Pr_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y,y^{\prime}\sim Q\end{subarray}}[\operatorname{sign}(\Delta_{r}(x,y,y^{\prime}))=\operatorname{sign}(\Delta_{r^{*}}(x,y,y^{\prime}))]

(18)

Here, we fix the test distribution
Q
to represent the downstream quantity of interest, while varying the training distribution
P
, which depends on
N
and the base distribution
P_{\text{base}}
. This lets us assess the impact of the BoN preference distribution. We will focus on the Best-vs-Independent and Best-vs-Worst-Independent scenarios since they have clean target reward functions under BoN preference data
(r^{*}_{\text{BI}},r^{*}_{\text{BWI}})
which preserve the ranking as
r^{*}
. With finite samples, the learned reward
\hat{r}
deviates from the target due to estimation error, and whether this error flips the ranking on a given pair depends on whether it exceeds the margin between them.
34
formalized this intuition and showed that sample complexity is governed by two quantities: the margin and the connectivity of the comparison distribution. A larger margin between two pairs of outcomes implies that the correct ordering can tolerate a larger estimation error, and a larger connectivity leads to a smaller estimation error. We analyze how the BoN preference data shapes both quantities.

5.1
Margin

5.2

r^{*}_{\text{BI}}

Assuming no ties in reward score, for any context

with non-zero probability mass, the margin of
r^{*}_{\text{BI}}
admits the decomposition

\Delta_{r^{*}_{\text{BI}}}(x;y,y^{\prime})~=~N\log\frac{F(x,y)}{F(x,y^{\prime})}~+~B_{N}(x;y,y^{\prime}),

(19)

where
B_{N}(x;y,y^{\prime})
is uniformly bounded in

.

5.3
Margin of
r^{*}_{\text{BWI}}
.

Assume no ties in
r^{*}(x,\cdot)
.
For any context
x
and pair of outcomes
y,y^{\prime}
with non-zero probability mass, the margin of
r^{*}_{\text{BWI}}
admits the decomposition

\Delta_{r^{*}_{\text{BWI}}}(x;y,y^{\prime})=N\left(\log\frac{F(x,y)}{F(x,y^{\prime})}+\log\frac{1-F^{-}(x,y^{\prime})}{1-F^{-}(x,y)}\right)+B_{N}(x;y,y^{\prime})

(20)

where
B_{N}(x;y,y^{\prime})
is uniformly bounded in

.

The most immediate implication is that increasing
N
amplifies all pairwise margins uniformly, making it a straightforward way to make the target reward easier to learn. But the slope at which the margin grows reveals a more informative and targeted lever. The slope increases when the base distribution places more mass between the two outcomes in the reward ranking (Figure
1
). We define this quantity as the between mass.

5.4
Between Mass.

For any context
x
and outcomes
y,y^{\prime}
where
r^{*}(x,y)>r^{*}(x,y^{\prime})
, the between mass between
y
and
y^{\prime}
is given by

\Pr_{\tilde{y}\sim P_{\text{base}}(\cdot\mid x)}(r^{*}(x,y^{\prime})<r^{*}(x,\tilde{y})<r^{*}(x,y))=F(x,y)-F(x,y^{\prime})

(21)

The higher the between mass between a pair of outcomes, the faster the margin between them grows with
N
.

So far, we have treated
P_{\text{base}}
as fixed and studied how
N
shapes the induced reward. The margin decomposition reveals a complementary lever: the base distribution itself enters the margin through the between-mass term. Unlike increasing
N
, which raises all margins indiscriminately, shaping
P_{\text{base}}
allows one to selectively increase margins on specific pairs. This lever is available whenever the practitioner has some control over the generation distribution. For example, by choosing which model to sample from or applying filtering before BoN. This selectivity is appealing because, in practice, not all pairwise comparisons are equally important: in safety, comparisons that matter most are between harmful and acceptable outputs, in reasoning, the crucial distinctions are between correct solutions and plausible near-misses. Each setting induces a different test distribution, and our result provides a principled guide for designing the base distribution to increase margins precisely where they are needed. Concretely, we provide examples of how to design the base distribution to increase margins for three different applications for reward learning.

Designing the Base Distribution to Increase Margins

Safety.

The key comparisons are those that separate harmful responses from acceptable ones. To enlarge these margins, the base distribution should place substantial mass in the mediocre region: responses that are flawed or suboptimal, but not obviously dangerous. This creates more separation between harmful and acceptable behavior.

Reasoning.

The critical distinction is between plausible-but-incorrect solutions and fully correct ones. If the base distribution produces too many clearly poor responses, then little mass lies between plausible and correct solutions. On the other hand, if it almost always produces perfect solutions, then this intermediate region again becomes small. Thus, the base distribution should be strong enough to reliably generate decent responses, while still concentrating substantial mass on near-correct but imperfect solutions.

General.

These tasks require distinguishing among responses across a broad mid-to-high quality range. Since the relevant comparisons are distributed over a wider band, the base distribution should spread its mass throughout this region in order to preserve sufficient between-mass across many comparisons.

5.2
Connectivity

Recall from
34
, the connectivity degree controls the estimation error of the reward learned from a training preference data distribution
P
on a test distribution
Q
in the worst-case scenario. It is defined as follows,

5.5
Connectivity.

The connectivity degree of a comparison distribution
P
with respect to a hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}
and a test distribution
Q
is defined as

\lambda_{\text{conn}}(P,Q;\mathcal{H})=\inf_{f,g\in\mathcal{H}}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{P}[(\Delta f-\Delta g)^{2}]}{\mathbb{E}_{Q}[(\Delta f-\Delta g)^{2}]}

(22)

The original notion of connectivity degree corresponds to the case where the test distribution
Q
ranges over all comparisons in the outcome space, so that it measures how well the training distribution
P
captures the pairwise variation over the entire comparison space. Here, we generalize this notion by allowing
Q
to be an arbitrary, possibly selective test distribution. In this sense,
\lambda_{\text{conn}}(P,Q;\mathcal{H})
can also be interpreted as worst-case measure of how well
P
covers the pairwise variation that matters under
Q
.

In contrast to the margin that grows linearly with
N
, we show that connectivity for
P_{\text{BI}}
operates on an exponentially small scale in
N
. The intuition is that as
N
increases, BoN sampling concentrates on outcomes with extremely high rewards while WoN sampling concentrates on outcomes with extremely low rewards. As a result, the observed comparisons become increasingly concentrated on a small subset of pairs, rather than covering a broad range of pairwise comparisons. Figure
2
illustrates this in a simple finite-outcome setting, where edge thickness represents the probability of observing each comparison. As
N
increases, the Best-vs-Independent graph converges to a star graph, while the Best-vs-Worst-Independent graph converges to a single edge between the best and worst outcomes. This loss of comparison diversity leaves intermediate comparisons underrepresented, which in turn degrades connectivity with respect to the test distribution
Q
.

5.6
Informal connectivity bounds for
P_{\text{BI}}
.

Let
P_{\text{base}}
be a base distribution. For any
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and define
A_{x,\alpha}=\{y\in\mathcal{Y}:r^{*}(x,y)\leq\alpha\}
as a set of outcomes which the reward is less than
\alpha
for a given context
x
. If
\mathcal{H}
is expressive enough and
Q
satisfies coverage and nondegeneracy conditions, there exist constants
C_{Q},D_{Q,\alpha}>0
such that for any
N
,

C_{Q}\Big(\min_{x,y}P_{\text{base}}(y\mid x)\Big)^{N}\leq\lambda_{\text{conn}}(P_{\text{BI}},Q;\mathcal{H})\leq D_{Q,\alpha}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P_{x}}\!\left[P_{\text{base}}(A_{x,\alpha}\mid x)^{N}+P_{\text{base}}(A_{x,\alpha}\mid x)\right].

Thus, the connectivity degree for
P_{\text{BI}}
is controlled by an exponentially small term in
N
, together with a residual term determined by the mass of the cut
A_{x,\alpha}
.

Overall, large
N
can reduce connectivity by concentrating probability mass on a narrower set of comparisons. The full formal statement and proof, along with a similar result for
P_{\text{BWI}}
, are given in Appendix
B
.

5.3
Accuracy Bound

From previous sections,
N
is a single knob that controls both margin and connectivity degree. However, there is a trade-off, increasing
N
leads to a larger margin but decreases the connectivity degree. To make this trade-off explicit, we combine the two results into an accuracy bound that yields a practical guideline for choosing
N
as a function of the sample size
n
.

5.7
Accuracy Bound.

Assume that
\mathcal{H}
is convex, bounded by some constant
B
, and realizable,
r^{*}_{\text{BI}}\in\mathcal{H}
. Assume also that for every triplet
(x,y,y^{\prime})
in the support of
Q
, both
P_{\text{base}}(y\mid x)
and
P_{\text{base}}(y^{\prime}\mid x)
are non-zero. Let
\hat{r}
be the empirical risk minimizer of the BT learning objective with respect to
P_{\text{BI}}
. Then there exist constants
D>0
and
M_{Q}>0
such that with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\operatorname{Acc}_{Q}(\hat{r})\geq\sup_{k>0}\Big[\Pr_{Q}\left(\left|\log\frac{F(x,y)}{F(x,y^{\prime})}\right|\geq k+\frac{M_{Q}}{N}\right)\;-\;D\frac{\operatorname{Comp}(n,\mathcal{H},\delta)}{N^{2}k^{2}\lambda_{\text{conn}}}\Big]

(23)

where
\lambda_{\text{conn}}:=\lambda_{\text{conn}}(P_{\text{BI}},Q;\mathcal{H})
.

The complexity term
\operatorname{Comp}(n,\mathcal{H},\delta)
is a standard Rademacher-complexity quantity which we define rigorously in Appendix
B
. The first term measures how often the absolute margin slope under
Q
exceeds a threshold, up to a vanishing
O(1/N)
correction. In contrast, the second term captures the estimation error and decays like
1/(N^{2}\lambda_{\text{conn}})
. The bound therefore preserves the same interpretation: increasing
N
helps by enlarging the target margin, but hurts through the connectivity degree. The optimal choice of
N
depends on the sample size
n
, which in turn depends on the resource bottleneck of preference label collection.

Principle for Selecting
N
Based on Resource Bottlenecks

Preference Label-Limited Regime.

In many practical settings, obtaining preference labels requires human annotators, which severely limits the number of triplets
n
that can be collected. In this small-
n
regime, the complexity term is large, so it is often advantageous to use a larger
N
. Although a larger
N
increases the cost of identifying the BoN sample, the overhead can be modest in some applications: in visual tasks, selecting the best item from
N
images may be relatively easy. In addition, selecting the best item among
N
candidates is substantially easier than producing a full ranking, and can be done with at most
\lceil\log_{2}N\rceil
pairwise comparisons via a simple tournament procedure. Thus, when human annotation is the dominant bottleneck, it is often worthwhile to spend more effort per comparison triplet in order to improve the quality of each comparison.

Generation-Limited Regime.

When preference annotations are cheap or can be automated, for example with LLM judges, it becomes possible to collect many triplets. In this large-
n
regime, increasing
N
provides diminishing returns because the complexity term is already small. It is therefore more compute-efficient to use a smaller
N
and spend the saved generation budget on collecting more triplets, rather than on generating many candidates for each triplet.

Design Principles.

1.

When preference labels are scarce, choose a larger
N
.

2.

When preference labels are abundant, choose a smaller
N
and use the saved generation budget to collect more triplets.

3.

When the base distribution is under the practitioner’s control, shape it to maximize between mass on the comparisons that matter most under the target application.

7
Conclusion and Future Work

We study preference data generated by Best-of-
N
sampling through the lens of reward learning. This perspective lets us characterize the reward targets induced by the data, identify the trade-off between margin and connectivity, and derive practical principles for data design. In particular, our analysis suggests choosing
N
based on the resource bottleneck, and shaping the base distribution to place more mass between the comparisons that matter under the target test distribution. Several directions remain open, including understanding the small-
N
regime, going beyond Bradley–Terry reward learning, and determining how these data-design effects propagate to downstream policy optimization and iterative training. More broadly, our results show that preference-data construction is not merely a collection detail: it shapes both what reward is learned and how efficiently it can be learned.

For the denominator, we have

\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}(1-F^{-}(x,y))^{N-1-j}\,(1-F(x,y))^{j}<\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}(1-F^{-}(x,y^{\prime}))^{N-1-j}\,(1-F(x,y^{\prime}))^{j}.

(46)

Putting everything together, we can conclude that

\frac{\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}F(x,y)^{N-1-j}\,F^{-}(x,y)^{j}}{\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}(1-F^{-}(x,y))^{N-1-j}\,(1-F(x,y))^{j}}>\frac{\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}F(x,y^{\prime})^{N-1-j}\,F^{-}(x,y^{\prime})^{j}}{\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}(1-F^{-}(x,y^{\prime}))^{N-1-j}\,(1-F(x,y^{\prime}))^{j}}.

(47)

Finally,

\frac{P_{\text{BoN}}(y\mid x)}{P_{\text{WoN}}(y\mid x)}>\frac{P_{\text{BoN}}(y^{\prime}\mid x)}{P_{\text{WoN}}(y^{\prime}\mid x)}.

(48)

This concludes our proof.

From Theorem 5.2 of

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{P}(r)=C-Z\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{(x,\{y,y^{\prime}\})\sim\widetilde{P}}\Big[\omega_{P}\log\sigma(\Delta_{r})+(1-\omega_{P})\log(\sigma(-\Delta_{r}))\Big]

(49)

where
\omega_{P}(y\succ y^{\prime}\mid x)
is the CPRD of
P
and
\Delta_{r}(x,y,y^{\prime})=r(x,y)-r(x,y^{\prime})
is the margin between two outcomes.
C,Z
are constants and
\widetilde{P}
is a comparison distribution induced by
P
.

We claim that the CPRD of the Best-vs-Random scenario is either 0 or 1. For any context and pairs of outcomes
(x,\{y,y^{\prime}\})
, let
y_{\max}\in\arg\max_{u\in\{y,y^{\prime}\}}r^{*}(x,u)
and
y_{\min}\in\arg\min_{u\in\{y,y^{\prime}\}}r^{*}(x,u)
be the outcome with a higher and lower reward score. By the definition of Best-vs-Random, the reward score of the positive sample is always higher than the reward score of the negative sample. Therefore, we would always have
y_{\max}
as a positive sample and
y_{\min}
as a negative sample. Therefore, the CPRD is given by

\omega_{P_{\text{BR}}}(y_{\max}\succ y_{\min}\mid x)=\frac{P(x,y_{\max},y_{\min})}{P(x,y_{\max},y_{\min})+P(x,y_{\min},y_{\max})}=1

(50)

and

\omega_{P_{\text{BR}}}(y_{\min}\succ y_{\max}\mid x)=\frac{P(x,y_{\min},y_{\max})}{P(x,y_{\min},y_{\max})+P(x,y_{\max},y_{\min})}=0.

(51)

Substitute this back, we have

\omega_{P}\log\sigma(\Delta_{r})+(1-\omega_{P})\log(\sigma(-\Delta_{r}))=\log\sigma(r(x,y_{\max})-r(x,y_{\min})).

(52)

With this result, we can conclude that the minimizer satisfies

r^{*}_{\text{BR}}\in\arg\min_{r\in\mathcal{H}}\mathbb{E}_{(x,\{y,y^{\prime}\})\sim\widetilde{P}_{\text{BR}}}\Big[-\log\sigma(r(x,y_{\max})-r(x,y_{\min}))\Big].

(53)

The result also holds for Best-vs-Worst, which also guarantees that the reward score of the positive sample is always higher than the reward score of the negative sample.

In the next part, we will show that the
r^{*}_{\text{BR}}
would converge to the
r^{*}_{\text{BI}}
as
N

P_{\text{BR}}
would be close to
P_{\text{BI}}
as

N

P_{\text{BR}}

A.2
Probability mass of
P_{\text{BR}}
.

Assume that there is no tie in reward score, for any context
x
and outcome
y,y^{\prime}
there exists a constant
c>0
for which the probability mass of
P_{\text{BR}}
is given by

P_{\text{BR}}(x,y,y^{\prime})=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }y=y^{\prime}\text{ or }r^{*}(x,y^{\prime})>r^{*}(x,y)\\
\frac{cN}{N-1}P(x)P_{\text{base}}(y^{\prime}\mid x)P_{\text{BoN},N-1}(y\mid x)&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(54)

where
P_{\text{BoN},N-1}
is the probability mass of
P_{\text{BoN}}
with
N-1
draws.

Proof.

The first case is quite simple. When we see a pair
y=y^{\prime}
, we do not add it to the comparison distribution. Therefore, the probability mass is 0. In addition, if
r^{*}(x,y^{\prime})>r^{*}(x,y)
, then it is not possible for
y
to be preferred over
y^{\prime}
given the context
x
. Therefore, the probability mass is 0 as well. For the second case, assume that
y,y^{\prime}
are two outcomes with
r^{*}(x,y)>r^{*}(x,y^{\prime})
and
y\neq y^{\prime}
. We can first factorize

P_{\text{BR}}(x,y,y^{\prime})=P(x)P_{\text{BR}}(y^{+}=y,y^{-}=y^{\prime}\mid x)

(55)

where
y^{+}
and
y^{-}
are the positive and negative samples. To compute the second conditional probability term, we use the fact that for a given sample
y_{1},\dots,y_{N}
, the probability of the positive sample being
y
and the negative sample being
y^{\prime}
is given by

P_{\text{BR}}(y^{+}=y,y^{-}=y^{\prime}\mid x,y_{1},\dots,y_{N})=1[\arg\max_{y_{i}}r^{*}(x,y_{i})=y]\cdot\frac{|\{i:y_{i}=y^{\prime}\}|}{N-1}.

(56)

The first term is an indicator function which ensures that the sample with the maximum reward score is
y
while the second term is the probability that the random sample that is drawn uniformly from the rest of outcomes is
y^{\prime}
which is basically the number of outcomes that is equal to
y^{\prime}
divided by the number of remaining outcomes,
N-1
. We can rewrite this as

P_{\text{BR}}(y^{+}=y,y^{-}=y^{\prime}\mid x,y_{1},\dots,y_{N})=\frac{1}{N-1}1[\arg\max_{y_{i}}r^{*}(x,y_{i})=y]\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{N}1[y_{i}=y^{\prime}].

(57)

Taking the expectation over all
y_{1},\dots,y_{N}
, we have

\displaystyle P_{\text{BR}}(y^{+}=y,y^{-}=y^{\prime}\mid x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{y_{1:N}}[P_{\text{BR}}(y^{+}=y,y^{-}=y^{\prime}\mid x,y_{1},\dots,y_{N})]

(58)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Pr_{y_{1:N}}(y_{i}=y^{\prime},\argmax_{y_{j}}r^{*}(x,y_{j})=y)

(59)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Pr_{y_{1:N}}(y_{i}=y^{\prime},\argmax_{y_{j},j\neq i}r^{*}(x,y_{j})=y)

\displaystyle(\text{since }r^{*}(x,y)>r^{*}(x,y^{\prime}))

(60)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Pr_{y_{i}}(y_{i}=y^{\prime})P_{y_{j},j\neq i}(\argmax_{y_{j}}r^{*}(x,y_{j})=y)

\displaystyle(\text{by independence})

(61)

\displaystyle=\frac{N}{N-1}P_{\text{base}}(y^{\prime}\mid x)P_{y_{2:N}}(\argmax_{y_{j}}r^{*}(x,y_{j})=y)

(62)

\displaystyle=\frac{N}{N-1}P_{\text{base}}(y^{\prime}\mid x)P_{\text{BoN},N-1}(y\mid x)

(63)

Finally, we note that the above result only holds when
y\neq y^{\prime}
. When
y=y^{\prime}
, we have to replace
\frac{|\{i:y_{i}=y^{\prime}\}|}{N-1}
with
\frac{|\{i:y_{i}=y^{\prime}\}|-1}{N-1}
since one sample is already chosen as
y
. Regardless, we don’t need to worry about such case since we will discard the pair with
y=y^{\prime}
and set the probability mass to 0. To ensure that the probability mass is valid, we need to scale up the probability mass of the rest by some factor
c
. This concludes the proof.

Before we prove that the total variation between

A.3
Normalization Preserves Closeness.

Let
\widetilde{P}
and
\widetilde{Q}
be two unnormalized probability mass functions over a finite set of outcomes
\Omega
. Let
Z_{p}:=\sum_{x\in\Omega}\widetilde{P}(x)
and
Z_{q}:=\sum_{x\in\Omega}\widetilde{Q}(x)
be the normalization constants of
\widetilde{P}
and
\widetilde{Q}
respectively with
P(x)=\frac{\widetilde{P}(x)}{Z_{p}}
and
Q(x)=\frac{\widetilde{Q}(x)}{Z_{q}}
as the normalized probability mass functions. If there exists a constant
c>0
such that
Z_{p}\geq c
and
Z_{q}\geq c
, then the total variation of
P,Q
is bounded by the total variation of
\widetilde{P},\widetilde{Q}
,

\operatorname{TV}(P,Q)\leq\frac{2}{c}\operatorname{TV}(\widetilde{P},\widetilde{Q})

(64)

Proof.

Without loss of generality, assume that
Z_{q}\geq Z_{p}
. The total variation of
P,Q
is given by

\displaystyle\operatorname{TV}(P,Q)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in\Omega}|P(x)-Q(x)|

(65)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in\Omega}|\frac{\widetilde{P}(x)}{Z_{p}}-\frac{\widetilde{Q}(x)}{Z_{q}}|

(66)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in\Omega}|\frac{\widetilde{P}(x)}{Z_{p}}-\frac{\widetilde{Q}(x)}{Z_{p}}|+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in\Omega}|\frac{\widetilde{Q}(x)}{Z_{q}}-\frac{\widetilde{Q}(x)}{Z_{p}}|

(67)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z_{p}}\operatorname{TV}(\widetilde{P},\widetilde{Q})+\frac{|Z_{p}-Z_{q}|}{2Z_{p}}

(68)

Since

\frac{1}{2}|Z_{p}-Z_{q}|=\frac{1}{2}|\sum_{x\in\Omega}\widetilde{P}(x)-\sum_{x\in\Omega}\widetilde{Q}(x)|\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in\Omega}|\widetilde{P}(x)-\widetilde{Q}(x)|=\operatorname{TV}(\widetilde{P},\widetilde{Q})

(69)

We have

\operatorname{TV}(P,Q)\leq\frac{2}{Z_{p}}\operatorname{TV}(\widetilde{P},\widetilde{Q})\leq\frac{2}{c}\operatorname{TV}(\widetilde{P},\widetilde{Q})

(70)

∎

Now, we have established that
P_{\text{BR}}
and
P_{\text{BI}}
are close as

grows, with this we can also show that the population risk minimizer would also be close to each other as well. Here we apply a standard technique in learning theory that first shows that a small TV distance means that the difference in the population risk would be small. Then, we use the strong convexity of the population risk to show that the difference in the population risk minimizer would be small as well. We will first prove some technical lemmas that will be useful for the proof and then will present the main proof accordingly.

The first lemma shows that we can bound the population risk difference in terms of the total variation between the two distributions.

A.5
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a hypothesis class that is bounded by some constant
B
. Let
P
and
Q
be two comparison distributions then there exists a constant
M_{B}
such that

\sup_{r\in\mathcal{H}}|\mathcal{L}_{P}(r)-\mathcal{L}_{Q}(r)|\leq 2M_{B}\operatorname{TV}(P,Q)

(112)

Proof.

First, we will show that if
\mathcal{H}
is bounded by
B
then the BT loss is also bounded as well. Recall that the loss on any
(x,y,y^{\prime})
is given by

\ell(\Delta_{r})=-\log\sigma(r(x,y^{+})-r(x,y^{-}))=\log(1+\exp(r(x,y^{-})-r(x,y^{+}))).

(113)

Since
r
is bounded by
B
, we have
|r(x,y^{-})-r(x,y^{+})|\leq 2B
so that

\ell(\Delta_{r})\leq\log(1+\exp(2B)):=M_{B}.

(114)

Next, we will show that the difference in the population risk is bounded by the total variation between the two distributions.

\displaystyle|\mathcal{L}_{P}(r)-\mathcal{L}_{Q}(r)|

\displaystyle=|\sum_{x,y,y^{\prime}}P(x,y,y^{\prime})\ell(\Delta_{r}(x,y,y^{\prime}))-\sum_{x,y,y^{\prime}}Q(x,y,y^{\prime})\ell(\Delta_{r}(x,y,y^{\prime}))|

(115)

\displaystyle=|\sum_{x,y,y^{\prime}}(P(x,y,y^{\prime})-Q(x,y,y^{\prime}))\ell(\Delta_{r}(x,y,y^{\prime}))|

(116)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{x,y,y^{\prime}}|P(x,y,y^{\prime})-Q(x,y,y^{\prime})|\ell(\Delta_{r}(x,y,y^{\prime}))|

(117)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{x,y,y^{\prime}}|P(x,y,y^{\prime})-Q(x,y,y^{\prime})|M_{B}

(118)

\displaystyle=2M_{B}\operatorname{TV}(P,Q)

(119)

Since this is true for any
r\in\mathcal{H}
, it would also hold for the supremum over all
r\in\mathcal{H}
. This concludes the proof.
∎

The second lemma shows that if the population risk between two distributions is small on all hypotheses in the hypothesis class, then the population risk minimizer of one distribution would also perform well on the other distribution.

A.6
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a hypothesis class and
P
,
Q
be two comparison distributions such that
\sup_{r\in\mathcal{H}}|\mathcal{L}_{P}(r)-\mathcal{L}_{Q}(r)|\leq\epsilon
. Let
r^{*}_{P}
and
r^{*}_{Q}
be the population risk minimizer of
\mathcal{L}_{P}
and
\mathcal{L}_{Q}
respectively. Then, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{P}(r^{*}_{Q})\leq\mathcal{L}_{P}(r^{*}_{P})+2\epsilon

(120)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{Q}(r^{*}_{P})\leq\mathcal{L}_{Q}(r^{*}_{Q})+2\epsilon

(121)

Proof.

We write the difference between the population risk of
r^{*}_{P}
and
r^{*}_{Q}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{P}(r^{*}_{Q})-\mathcal{L}_{P}(r^{*}_{P})

\displaystyle=(\mathcal{L}_{P}(r^{*}_{Q})-\mathcal{L}_{Q}(r^{*}_{Q}))+(\mathcal{L}_{Q}(r^{*}_{Q})-\mathcal{L}_{Q}(r^{*}_{P}))+(\mathcal{L}_{Q}(r^{*}_{P})-\mathcal{L}_{P}(r^{*}_{P}))

(122)

\displaystyle\leq|\mathcal{L}_{P}(r^{*}_{Q})-\mathcal{L}_{Q}(r^{*}_{Q})|+(\mathcal{L}_{Q}(r^{*}_{Q})-\mathcal{L}_{Q}(r^{*}_{P}))+|\mathcal{L}_{Q}(r^{*}_{P})-\mathcal{L}_{P}(r^{*}_{P})|

(123)

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon+0+\epsilon=2\epsilon

(124)

The first and third term follows from the assumption that
\sup_{r\in\mathcal{H}}|\mathcal{L}_{P}(r)-\mathcal{L}_{Q}(r)|\leq\epsilon
. The second term is at most 0 since
r^{*}_{Q}
is the population risk minimizer of
\mathcal{L}_{Q}
so that
\mathcal{L}_{Q}(r^{*}_{Q})\leq\mathcal{L}_{Q}(r)
for any
r\in\mathcal{H}
including
r^{*}_{P}
. We have the first result and the second result follows from the same argument.
∎

In this Lemma, we show that when the hypothesis class is convex, we can bound the difference between the margin of a reward function with the population risk minimizer with the difference in their population risk.

A.7
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a convex hypothesis class that is bounded by some constant
B
. Let
P
be a comparison distribution and
r^{*}_{P}
be the population risk minimizer of
\mathcal{L}_{P}
. There exists a constant
c_{B}
such that for any reward function
r\in\mathcal{H}
, we have

\lVert r-r^{*}_{P}\rVert_{\Delta,P}\leq\frac{2}{\sigma_{B}}(\mathcal{L}_{P}(r)-\mathcal{L}_{P}(r^{*}_{P}))

(125)

Proof.

First, we will show that when
\mathcal{H}
is bounded,
\mathcal{L}_{P}(r)
is strongly convex. This result follows from the proof of Theorem 6.7 in
34
. We provide a brief proof here for completeness. We can write the BT objective as

\mathcal{L}_{P}(r)=C+\mathbb{E}_{(x,\{y,y^{\prime}\})\sim\widetilde{P}}\left[g_{x,{y,y^{\prime}}}(\Delta_{r}(x,y,y^{\prime}))\right].

(126)

where
g_{x,{y,y^{\prime}}}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, is defined for each
(x,\{y,y^{\prime}\})
as

\displaystyle g_{x,{y,y^{\prime}}}(a)

\displaystyle=-\omega_{P}(y\succ y^{\prime}\mid x)\log\sigma(a)-(1-\omega_{P}(y\succ y^{\prime}\mid x))\log(1-\sigma(a))

(127)

We will write
\Delta_{r}
for
\Delta_{r}(x,y,y^{\prime})
for convenience from now. The first thing we will show is that
g_{x,{y,y^{\prime}}}
is strongly convex with respect to
a
. We can check this by taking the derivative.
Using the property that
\sigma^{\prime}(a)=\sigma(a)(1-\sigma(a))
, we have the first derivative

g_{x,{y,y^{\prime}}}^{\prime}(a)=\sigma(a)-\omega_{P}

(128)

and the second derivative

g_{x,{y,y^{\prime}}}^{\prime\prime}(a)=\sigma(a)(1-\sigma(a))

(129)

Since the score function
r
is bounded by a constant
B
, for any
(x,\{y,y^{\prime}\})
, we know that
|\Delta_{r}|=|r(x,y)-r(x,y^{\prime})|\leq 2B
.
Now, for
a\in[-2B,2B]
we know that

\sigma(a)(1-\sigma(a))\geq\sigma(2B)(1-\sigma(2B)):=\sigma_{B}>0

(130)

With this assumption, we can conclude that
g_{x,{y,y^{\prime}}}
is
\sigma_{B}
-strongly convex. Recall that if
f
is
\sigma_{B}
-strongly convex, then for any
x,y
, we have

f(x)-f(y)\geq f^{\prime}(y)(x-y)+\frac{\sigma_{B}}{2}(x-y)^{2}.

(131)

Apply this inequality on
g_{x,{y,y^{\prime}}}
with
\Delta_{r}
and
\Delta_{r^{*}_{P}}
and drop
(x,y,y^{\prime})
for convenience.

g_{x,{y,y^{\prime}}}(\Delta_{r})-g_{x,{y,y^{\prime}}}(\Delta_{r^{*}_{P}})\geq g^{\prime}_{x,{y,y^{\prime}}}(\Delta_{r^{*}_{P}})(\Delta_{r}-\Delta_{r^{*}_{P}})+\frac{\sigma_{B}}{2}(\Delta_{r}-\Delta_{r^{*}_{P}})^{2}.

(132)

Take the expectation over the distribution
\widetilde{P}
, we have

\mathbb{E}_{\widetilde{P}}[g_{x,{y,y^{\prime}}}(\Delta_{r})-g_{x,{y,y^{\prime}}}(\Delta_{r^{*}_{P}})]\geq\mathbb{E}_{\widetilde{P}}[g^{\prime}_{x,{y,y^{\prime}}}(\Delta_{r^{*}_{P}})(\Delta_{r}-\Delta_{r^{*}_{P}})]+\frac{\sigma_{B}}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\widetilde{P}}[(\Delta_{r}-\Delta_{r^{*}_{P}})^{2}].

(133)

The left hand side is just the difference between the population risk, while the second term on the right hand side is the margin semi-norm with respect to
P
; since
(\Delta_{r}-\Delta_{r^{*}_{P}})^{2}
is invariant under swapping
y
and
y^{\prime}
, its expectation under
\widetilde{P}
is equal to the expectation over
P
which is exactly
\lVert r-r^{*}_{P}\rVert_{\Delta,P}
.

\mathcal{L}_{P}(r)-\mathcal{L}_{P}(r^{*}_{P})\geq\mathbb{E}_{\widetilde{P}}[g^{\prime}_{x,{y,y^{\prime}}}(\Delta_{r^{*}_{P}})(\Delta_{r}-\Delta_{r^{*}_{P}})]+\frac{\sigma_{B}}{2}\lVert r-r^{*}_{P}\rVert_{\Delta,P}.

(134)

Now, the final part is to show that the first term on the right hand side is non-negative. We achieve this by using the property of
\mathcal{H}
being convex. From convexity, we know that for any
t\in[0,1]
, we have

r_{t}=(1-t)r^{*}_{P}+tr\in\mathcal{H}

(135)

Since
r^{*}_{P}
is the population risk minimizer, the right derivative of the population risk with respect to
t
at
t=0
must be non-negative.

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\mathcal{L}_{P}(r_{t})\bigg|_{t=0}\geq 0

(136)

where

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\mathcal{L}_{P}(r_{t})

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\mathbb{E}_{\widetilde{P}}[g_{x,{y,y^{\prime}}}(\Delta_{r_{t}})]

(137)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\widetilde{P}}[g^{\prime}_{x,{y,y^{\prime}}}(\Delta_{r_{t}})\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Delta_{r_{t}}]

(138)

We can derive

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Delta_{r_{t}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}((1-t)\Delta_{r^{*}_{P}}+t\Delta_{r})=\Delta_{r}-\Delta_{r^{*}_{P}}.

(139)

Therefore,

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\mathcal{L}_{P}(r_{t})=\mathbb{E}_{\widetilde{P}}[g^{\prime}_{x,{y,y^{\prime}}}(\Delta_{r_{t}})(\Delta_{r}-\Delta_{r^{*}_{P}})]

(140)

Setting
t=0
and substituting back, we would have that

\mathbb{E}_{\widetilde{P}}[g^{\prime}_{x,{y,y^{\prime}}}(\Delta_{r^{*}_{P}})(\Delta_{r}-\Delta_{r^{*}_{P}})]\geq 0

(141)

as desired. As a result, we can conclude that

\mathcal{L}_{P}(r)-\mathcal{L}_{P}(r^{*}_{P})\geq\frac{\sigma_{B}}{2}\lVert r-r^{*}_{P}\rVert_{\Delta,P}.

(142)

This concludes the proof.
∎

A.8
Total Variation between
P_{\text{BW}}
and
P_{\text{BWI}}
.

Assume that there is no tie in the reward score and we discard the pair with
y=y^{\prime}
. Then, there exists a constant
c>0
and
\rho<1
for which for any
N\geq 2
, the total variation between
P_{\text{BW}}
and
P_{\text{BWI}}
is given by

\operatorname{TV}(P_{\text{BW}},P_{\text{BWI}})\leq c\rho^{N}

(147)

Proof.

The main idea of the proof is to bound the total variation between
P_{\text{BW}}
and
P_{\text{BWI}}
on the pair of best and worst outcome. This is because we know that as
N
grows, we are more likely to see the outcome with the highest and lowest true reward score. For the first step, we will calculate the probability mass of
P_{\text{BW}}
and
P_{\text{BWI}}
.

Part 1: Calculate the probability mass of
P_{\text{BWI}}
and
P_{\text{BW}}
.

We start with the probability mass of
P_{\text{BWI}}

P_{\text{BWI}}(y,y^{\prime}\mid x)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }y=y^{\prime}\\
\frac{1}{c_{I}}P_{\text{BoN}}(y\mid x)P_{\text{WoN}}(y^{\prime}\mid x)&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(148)

Here
c_{I}
is a constant that ensures that
P_{\text{BWI}}
is a valid probability distribution. We need this constant because whenever
y=y^{\prime}
, we discard the triplet. We will bound this constant term. Without loss of generality, assume that the set of outcomes is given by
y_{1},y_{2},\dots,y_{m}
where
r^{*}(x,y_{1})<r^{*}(x,y_{2})<\dots<r^{*}(x,y_{m})
so that
y_{1}
is the worst outcome and
y_{m}
is the best outcome.
We also define the unnormalized probability mass of
P_{\text{BWI}}
as

\widetilde{P}_{\text{BWI}}(y,y^{\prime}\mid x)=P_{\text{BoN}}(y\mid x)P_{\text{WoN}}(y^{\prime}\mid x)

(149)

From Lemma
4.1
and
4.3
, we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{P}_{\text{BWI}}(y_{i},y_{j}\mid x)

\displaystyle=P_{\text{BoN}}(y_{i}\mid x)P_{\text{WoN}}(y_{j}\mid x)

(150)

\displaystyle=(F(x,y_{i})^{N}-F^{-}(x,y_{i})^{N})((1-F^{-}(x,y_{j}))^{N}-(1-F(x,y_{j}))^{N})

(151)

\displaystyle=(F(x,y_{i})^{N}-F(x,y_{i-1})^{N})((1-F(x,y_{j-1}))^{N}-(1-F(x,y_{j}))^{N})

(152)

\displaystyle=(F_{i}^{N}-F_{i-1}^{N})((1-F_{j-1})^{N}-(1-F_{j})^{N})

(153)

This follows from the fact that
F^{-}(x,y_{i})=F(x,y_{i-1})
. We also write
F_{i}=F(x,y_{i})
for convenience. It is clear that
P_{\text{BWI}}
is just
\widetilde{P}_{\text{BWI}}
normalized by some constant that takes care of the case when we discard the pair with
y=y^{\prime}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle c_{I}

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\neq y^{\prime}}\widetilde{P}_{\text{BWI}}(y,y^{\prime}\mid x)

(154)

\displaystyle=1-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\widetilde{P}_{\text{BWI}}(y_{i},y_{i}\mid x)

(155)

\displaystyle=1-\sum_{i=1}^{m}(F_{i}^{N}-F_{i-1}^{N})((1-F_{i-1})^{N}-(1-F_{i})^{N})

(156)

For each
i
between 1 and
m
, the term
(F_{i}^{N}-F_{i-1}^{N})((1-F_{i-1})^{N}-(1-F_{i})^{N})
would converge to
0
as
N
grows. Further, when
i=1
, we have

(F_{1}^{N}-F_{0}^{N})((1-F_{0})^{N}-(1-F_{1})^{N})=F_{1}^{N}(1-(1-F_{1})^{N})

(157)

and when
i=m
, we have

(F_{m}^{N}-F_{m-1}^{N})((1-F_{m-1})^{N}-(1-F_{m})^{N})=(1-F_{m-1}^{N})(1-F_{m-1})^{N}

(158)

Both terms also converge to
0
as
N
grows. As a result, we can conclude that
c_{I}\to 1
as
N
grows and there exists a constant
a>0
for which
c_{I}\geq a>0
for all
N
. Combining everything together, we have

P_{\text{BWI}}(y_{i},y_{j}\mid x)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }i=j\\
\frac{1}{c_{I}}(F_{i}^{N}-F_{i-1}^{N})((1-F_{j-1})^{N}-(1-F_{j})^{N})&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(159)

when
c_{I}\to 1
as
N
grows. Next, we will calculate the probability mass of
P_{\text{BW}}
. First, for any
i\leq j
we know that

P_{\text{BW}}(y_{i},y_{j}\mid x)=0

(160)

because
y_{j}
has a higher reward score than
y_{i}
so it’s impossible for
y_{i}
to be preferred over
y_{j}
. For the case when
i>j
, the probability is not zero anymore. Let
Y_{1},Y_{2},\dots,Y_{N}
be the
N
outcomes sampled from the base distribution and let
\widetilde{P}_{\text{BW}}
be the unnormalized probability mass of
P_{\text{BW}}
then

\displaystyle\widetilde{P}_{\text{BW}}(y_{i},y_{j}\mid x)

\displaystyle=\Pr(\arg\max_{Y_{i}}r^{*}(x,Y_{i})=y_{i},\arg\min_{Y_{i}}r^{*}(x,Y_{i})=y_{j})

(161)

\displaystyle=\Pr(\max r^{*}(x,Y_{i})=r^{*}(x,y_{i}),\min r^{*}(x,Y_{i})=r^{*}(x,y_{j}))

(162)

\displaystyle=\Pr(r^{*}(x,y_{j})\leq r^{*}(x,Y_{i})\leq r^{*}(x,y_{i}))-\Pr(r^{*}(x,y_{j})\leq r^{*}(x,Y_{i})<r^{*}(x,y_{i}))

(163)

\displaystyle\quad-\Pr(r^{*}(x,y_{j})<r^{*}(x,Y_{i})\leq r^{*}(x,y_{i}))+\Pr(r^{*}(x,y_{j})<r^{*}(x,Y_{i})<r^{*}(x,y_{i}))

(164)

\displaystyle=(F_{i}-F_{j-1})^{N}-(F_{i-1}-F_{j-1})^{N}-(F_{i}-F_{j})^{N}+(F_{i-1}-F_{j})^{N}

(165)

This follows from the assumption that there is no tie in the reward score and the second to last line holds from an inclusion-exclusion principle. We have

P_{\text{BW}}(y_{i},y_{j}\mid x)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }i\leq j\\
\frac{1}{c_{D}}\widetilde{P}_{\text{BW}}(y_{i},y_{j}\mid x)\end{cases}

(166)

Similarly
c_{D}
is a constant that ensures that
P_{\text{BW}}
is a valid probability distribution which handles the case when
i\leq j
. We know that

c_{D}=\sum_{i>j}\widetilde{P}_{\text{BW}}(y_{i},y_{j}\mid x)=\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}\sum_{i=j+1}^{m}\widetilde{P}_{\text{BW}}(y_{i},y_{j}\mid x)

(167)

Let
S_{j}=\sum_{i=j+1}^{m}\widetilde{P}_{\text{BW}}(y_{i},y_{j}\mid x)
then we can write

\displaystyle S_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=j+1}^{m}\widetilde{P}_{\text{BW}}(y_{i},y_{j}\mid x)

(168)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=j+1}^{m}(F_{i}-F_{j-1})^{N}-(F_{i-1}-F_{j-1})^{N}-(F_{i}-F_{j})^{N}+(F_{i-1}-F_{j})^{N}

(169)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=j+1}^{m}(F_{i}-F_{j-1})^{N}-\sum_{i=j}^{m-1}(F_{i}-F_{j-1})^{N}-\sum_{i=j+1}^{m}(F_{i}-F_{j})^{N}+\sum_{i=j}^{m-1}(F_{i}-F_{j})^{N}

(170)

\displaystyle=(F_{m}-F_{j-1})^{N}-(F_{j}-F_{j-1})^{N}-(F_{m}-F_{j})^{N}

(171)

\displaystyle=(1-F_{j-1})^{N}-(1-F_{j})^{N}-(F_{j}-F_{j-1})^{N}

(172)

Therefore,

\displaystyle c_{D}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}S_{j}

(173)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}((1-F_{j-1})^{N}-(1-F_{j})^{N}-(F_{j}-F_{j-1})^{N})

(174)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{m-2}(1-F_{j})^{N}-\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}(1-F_{j})^{N}-\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}(F_{j}-F_{j-1})^{N}

(175)

\displaystyle=1-(1-F_{m-1})^{N}-\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}(F_{j}-F_{j-1})^{N}

(176)

\displaystyle=1-\sum_{j=1}^{m}(F_{j}-F_{j-1})^{N}

(177)

Similar to
c_{I}
,
c_{D}\to 1
as
N
grows assuming that the base distribution does not concentrate all probability on a single outcome. There exists a constant
b>0
for which
c_{D}\geq b>0
for all
N
.

Part 2: Bound the total variation between
P_{\text{BWI}}
and
P_{\text{BW}}

We will start with the total variation between the conditional distributions
P_{\text{BWI}}(y,y^{\prime}\mid x)
and
P_{\text{BW}}(y,y^{\prime}\mid x)

\delta_{(m,1)}
be the probability distribution that assigns probability
1
to the pair
(y_{m},y_{1})

to all other pairs. By triangle inequality,

\displaystyle\operatorname{TV}(P_{\text{BWI}}(y,y^{\prime}\mid x),P_{\text{BW}}(y,y^{\prime}\mid x))

(178)

\displaystyle=(1-P_{\text{BWI}}(y_{m},y_{1}\mid x))+(1-P_{\text{BW}}(y_{m},y_{1}\mid x))

(179)

The final line follows from
\delta_{(m,1)}
put zero probability mass everywhere and assign probability
1

\displaystyle 1-P_{\text{BWI}}(y_{m},y_{1}\mid x)

\displaystyle=1-\frac{(F_{m}^{N}-F_{m-1}^{N})((1-F_{0})^{N}-(1-F_{1})^{N})}{c_{I}}

(180)

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{I}-(1-F_{m-1}^{N})(1-(1-F_{1})^{N})}{c_{I}}

(181)

(182)

(184)

when

p_{1}

\displaystyle 1-P_{\text{BW}}(y_{m},y_{1}\mid x)

(188)

(189)

\displaystyle\operatorname{TV}(P_{\text{BWI}}(y,y^{\prime}\mid x),P_{\text{BW}}(y,y^{\prime}\mid x))

\displaystyle\leq 2(\frac{1}{a}+\frac{1}{b})\max(1-p_{m},1-p_{1})^{N}

(191)

\displaystyle:=c_{x}\rho_{x}^{N}

(192)

\displaystyle\operatorname{TV}(P_{\text{BWI}},P_{\text{BW}})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{x}\operatorname{TV}(P_{\text{BWI}}(y,y^{\prime}\mid x),P_{\text{BW}}(y,y^{\prime}\mid x))P(x)

(193)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{x}P(x)c_{x}\rho_{x}^{N}

(194)

\displaystyle\leq(\sum_{x}P(x)c_{x})\max_{x}(\rho_{x})^{N}

(195)

\displaystyle:=c\rho^{N}

(196)

This concludes the proof.

Proof.

Recall the closed form for

\displaystyle\Delta_{r^{*}_{\text{BI}}}(x;y,y^{\prime})

(203)

Since
0\leq\alpha_{x}(y)<1

.

\{\log(1-\alpha_{x}(y)^{N})\}_{N\geq 1}

0
and below by
\log(1-\alpha_{x}(y))
.
Therefore their difference remains bounded, and the final term
-\log(P_{\text{base}}(y\mid x)/P_{\text{base}}(y^{\prime}\mid x))
is constant. The dominant term is
N(\log F(x,y)-\log F(x,y^{\prime}))
. If
r^{*}(x,y)>r^{*}(x,y^{\prime})
, then
F(x,y)>F(x,y^{\prime})
, so the margin diverges.
∎

See
5.3

Proof.

We use the result from Theorem
4.4
which provides a closed form for
r^{*}_{\text{BWI}}
,
we have

r^{*}_{\text{BWI}}(x,y)=\log\frac{P_{\text{BoN}}(y\mid x)}{P_{\text{WoN}}(y\mid x)}+c(x)

(204)

and

\displaystyle\Delta_{r^{*}_{\text{BWI}}}(x;y,y^{\prime})

\displaystyle=r^{*}_{\text{BWI}}(x,y)-r^{*}_{\text{BWI}}(x,y^{\prime})

(205)

\displaystyle=\log\frac{P_{\text{BoN}}(y\mid x)}{P_{\text{WoN}}(y\mid x)}-\log\frac{P_{\text{BoN}}(y^{\prime}\mid x)}{P_{\text{WoN}}(y^{\prime}\mid x)}

(206)

\displaystyle=\log\frac{F(x,y)^{N}-F^{-}(x,y)^{N}}{(1-F^{-}(x,y))^{N}-(1-F(x,y))^{N}}-\log\frac{F(x,y^{\prime})^{N}-F^{-}(x,y^{\prime})^{N}}{(1-F^{-}(x,y^{\prime}))^{N}-(1-F(x,y^{\prime}))^{N}}

(207)

\displaystyle=\log\frac{F(x,y)^{N}(1-(\frac{F^{-}(x,y)}{F(x,y)})^{N})}{(1-F^{-}(x,y))^{N}(1-(\frac{1-F(x,y)}{1-F^{-}(x,y)})^{N})}-\log\frac{F(x,y^{\prime})^{N}(1-(\frac{F^{-}(x,y^{\prime})}{F(x,y^{\prime})})^{N})}{(1-F^{-}(x,y^{\prime}))^{N}(1-(\frac{1-F(x,y^{\prime})}{1-F^{-}(x,y^{\prime})})^{N})}

(208)

We can see that the terms
(1-(\frac{F^{-}(x,y)}{F(x,y)})^{N})
and
1-(\frac{1-F(x,y)}{1-F^{-}(x,y)})^{N})
are increasing with
N
and converge to
1
as
N\to\infty
. This also holds for
y^{\prime}
. Therefore, we can write the margin as

\displaystyle\Delta_{r^{*}_{\text{BWI}}}(x;y,y^{\prime})

\displaystyle=N\log\frac{F(x,y)}{1-F^{-}(x,y)}-N\log\frac{F(x,y^{\prime})}{1-F^{-}(x,y^{\prime})}+B_{N}(x;y,y^{\prime})

(209)

\displaystyle=N\left(\log\frac{F(x,y)}{F(x,y^{\prime})}+\log\frac{1-F^{-}(x,y^{\prime})}{1-F^{-}(x,y)}\right)+B_{N}(x;y,y^{\prime})

(210)

where
B_{N}(x;y,y^{\prime})
is uniformly bounded in
N
. This concludes the proof.
∎

B.1
Formal connectivity bounds for
P_{\text{BI}}
.

Assume that every triplet in the support of
Q
has non-zero probability under
P_{\text{BI}}
. Then there exists a constant
C_{Q}>0
for which for any
N
,

\lambda_{\text{conn}}(P_{\text{BI}},Q;\mathcal{H})\geq C_{Q}(\min_{x,y}P_{\text{base}}(y\mid x))^{N}

(211)

Fix
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and define
A_{x,\alpha}=\{y\in\mathcal{Y}:r^{*}(x,y)\leq\alpha\}
. Assume that
\mathcal{H}
contains functions
f,g
such that
f(x,\cdot)-g(x,\cdot)
is constant on
A_{x,\alpha}
and on its complement for every
x
, and that

\gamma_{Q,\alpha}:=\Pr_{Q}(y\in A_{x,\alpha},y^{\prime}\notin A_{x,\alpha})+\Pr_{Q}(y\notin A_{x,\alpha},y^{\prime}\in A_{x,\alpha})>0.

If
\eta:=1-\max_{x,y}P_{\text{base}}(y\mid x)>0
, then there exists a constant
D_{Q,\alpha}>0
such that for any
N
,

\lambda_{\text{conn}}(P_{\text{BI}},Q;\mathcal{H})\leq D_{Q,\alpha}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P_{x}}[P_{\text{base}}(A_{x,\alpha}\mid x)^{N}+P_{\text{base}}(A_{x,\alpha}\mid x)]

(212)

Proof.

We start with the lower bound. Recall the definition of the connectivity degree,

\lambda_{\text{conn}}(P_{\text{BI}},Q;\mathcal{H})=\inf_{f,g\in\mathcal{H}}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{P_{\text{BI}}}[(\Delta f-\Delta g)^{2}]}{\mathbb{E}_{Q}[(\Delta f-\Delta g)^{2}]}.

(213)

If
\lambda_{\text{conn}}(P_{\text{BI}},Q;\mathcal{H})=0
, the lower bound is immediate. So assume that
\lambda_{\text{conn}}(P_{\text{BI}},Q;\mathcal{H})>0
. For any
f,g\in\mathcal{H}
, we can rewrite

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{P_{\text{BI}}}[(\Delta f-\Delta g)^{2}]

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{Q(x,y,y^{\prime})>0}P_{\text{BI}}(x,y,y^{\prime})(\Delta f-\Delta g)^{2}

(214)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{Q}[\frac{P_{\text{BI}}(x,y,y^{\prime})}{Q(x,y,y^{\prime})}(\Delta f-\Delta g)^{2}]

(215)

\displaystyle\geq\left(\min_{Q(x,y,y^{\prime})>0}\frac{P_{\text{BI}}(x,y,y^{\prime})}{Q(x,y,y^{\prime})}\right)\mathbb{E}_{Q}[(\Delta f-\Delta g)^{2}].

(216)

Therefore,

\lambda_{\text{conn}}(P_{\text{BI}},Q;\mathcal{H})\geq\min_{Q(x,y,y^{\prime})>0}\frac{P_{\text{BI}}(x,y,y^{\prime})}{Q(x,y,y^{\prime})}.

(217)

Let

Z_{I}(x)=1-\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}P_{\text{BoN}}(y\mid x)P_{\text{base}}(y\mid x)

(218)

be the normalization constant that removes the event
y=y^{\prime}
. Then, for any
y\neq y^{\prime}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{P_{\text{BI}}(x,y,y^{\prime})}{Q(x,y,y^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=\frac{P(x)P_{\text{BoN}}(y\mid x)P_{\text{base}}(y^{\prime}\mid x)}{Z_{I}(x)Q(x,y,y^{\prime})}

(219)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{P(x)P_{\text{BoN}}(y\mid x)P_{\text{base}}(y^{\prime}\mid x)}{Q(x,y,y^{\prime})}

(220)

since
Z_{I}(x)\leq 1
. By Lemma
4.1
,

P_{\text{BoN}}(y\mid x)=F(x,y)^{N}-F^{-}(x,y)^{N}\geq P_{\text{base}}(y\mid x)^{N}.

(221)

Hence, for every
(x,y,y^{\prime})
in the support of
Q
,

\frac{P_{\text{BI}}(x,y,y^{\prime})}{Q(x,y,y^{\prime})}\geq\frac{P(x)P_{\text{base}}(y^{\prime}\mid x)}{Q(x,y,y^{\prime})}P_{\text{base}}(y\mid x)^{N}\geq C_{Q}(\min_{x,y}P_{\text{base}}(y\mid x))^{N}

(222)

where

C_{Q}:=\min_{Q(x,y,y^{\prime})>0}\frac{P(x)P_{\text{base}}(y^{\prime}\mid x)}{Q(x,y,y^{\prime})}>0.

(223)

Substituting back, we obtain

\lambda_{\text{conn}}(P_{\text{BI}},Q;\mathcal{H})\geq C_{Q}(\min_{x,y}P_{\text{base}}(y\mid x))^{N}.

(224)

Now, we prove the upper bound. Fix
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and define

A_{x,\alpha}=\{y\in\mathcal{Y}:r^{*}(x,y)\leq\alpha\}

(225)

and write
p_{A}(x):=P_{\text{base}}(A_{x,\alpha}\mid x)
. Without loss of generality, let the set of outcomes be
\{y_{1},y_{2},\dots,y_{m}\}
such that
r^{*}(x,y_{1})<r^{*}(x,y_{2})<\dots<r^{*}(x,y_{m})
(assuming that there is no tie in reward score). Let
k
be the largest index for which
r^{*}(x,y_{k})\leq\alpha
. Then

p_{A}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}P_{\text{base}}(y_{i}\mid x)=F(x,y_{k}).

(226)

On the other hand, under the Best-of-
N
sampling we have

P_{\text{BoN}}(A_{x,\alpha}\mid x)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}P_{\text{BoN}}(y_{i}\mid x)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}(F(x,y_{i})^{N}-F^{-}(x,y_{i})^{N})=F(x,y_{k})^{N}=p_{A}(x)^{N}.

(227)

By assumption,
\mathcal{H}
contains
f,g\in\mathcal{H}
such that for any
x
,

f(x,y)-g(x,y)=\begin{cases}\gamma_{1}&\text{if }y\in A_{x,\alpha}\\
\gamma_{0}&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(228)

Denote
\phi=f-g
. For this choice,

\displaystyle\lambda_{\text{conn}}(P_{\text{BI}},Q;\mathcal{H})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}_{P_{\text{BI}}}[(\phi(x,y)-\phi(x,y^{\prime}))^{2}]}{\mathbb{E}_{Q}[(\phi(x,y)-\phi(x,y^{\prime}))^{2}]}

(229)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Pr_{P_{\text{BI}}}(y\in A_{x,\alpha},y^{\prime}\notin A_{x,\alpha})+\Pr_{P_{\text{BI}}}(y\notin A_{x,\alpha},y^{\prime}\in A_{x,\alpha})}{\Pr_{Q}(y\in A_{x,\alpha},y^{\prime}\notin A_{x,\alpha})+\Pr_{Q}(y\notin A_{x,\alpha},y^{\prime}\in A_{x,\alpha})}.

(230)

By assumption, the denominator is exactly
\gamma_{Q,\alpha}>0
. For the numerator, first note that

\displaystyle Z_{I}(x)

\displaystyle=1-\sum_{i=1}^{m}(F_{i}^{N}-F_{i-1}^{N})p_{i}

(231)

\displaystyle\geq 1-(\max_{i}p_{i})\sum_{i=1}^{m}(F_{i}^{N}-F_{i-1}^{N})

(232)

\displaystyle\geq 1-\max_{i}p_{i}

(233)

\displaystyle\geq 1-\max_{x,y}P_{\text{base}}(y\mid x)=\eta.

(234)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\Pr_{P_{\text{BI}}}(y\in A_{x,\alpha},y^{\prime}\notin A_{x,\alpha})+\Pr_{P_{\text{BI}}}(y\notin A_{x,\alpha},y^{\prime}\in A_{x,\alpha})

(235)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P_{x}}\left[\frac{1}{Z_{I}(x)}\left(P_{\text{BoN}}(A_{x,\alpha}\mid x)(1-p_{A}(x))+(1-P_{\text{BoN}}(A_{x,\alpha}\mid x))p_{A}(x)\right)\right]

(236)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\eta}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P_{x}}\left[p_{A}(x)^{N}(1-p_{A}(x))+(1-p_{A}(x)^{N})p_{A}(x)\right]

(237)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\eta}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P_{x}}\left[p_{A}(x)^{N}+p_{A}(x)\right].

(238)

Substituting back, we conclude that

\lambda_{\text{conn}}(P_{\text{BI}},Q;\mathcal{H})\leq\frac{1}{\eta\gamma_{Q,\alpha}}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P_{x}}[p_{A}(x)^{N}+p_{A}(x)].

(239)

This proves the upper bound with
D_{Q,\alpha}=\frac{1}{\eta\gamma_{Q,\alpha}}
.
∎

B.2
Connectivity bounds for
P_{\text{BWI}}
.

Let
P_{\text{base}}
be a base distribution. Then there exists a constant
C>0
such that for any
N
,

\lambda_{\text{conn}}(P_{\text{BWI}},Q;\mathcal{H})\geq C\Big(\min_{x,y\neq y^{\prime}}P_{\text{base}}(y\mid x)P_{\text{base}}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\Big)^{N}

(240)

On the other hand, for any real-valued
\alpha
, we define the set of outcomes for which the reward is less than
\alpha
for a given context
x
as
A_{x,\alpha}=\{y\in\mathcal{Y}:r^{*}(x,y)\leq\alpha\}
. For any
\alpha
, if
\mathcal{H}
is flexible enough, then there exists a constant
D>0
such that for any
N
,

\lambda_{\text{conn}}(P_{\text{BWI}},Q;\mathcal{H})\leq D\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P_{x}}[1+(P_{\text{base}}(A_{x,\alpha}\mid x))^{N}(1-P_{\text{base}}(A_{x,\alpha}\mid x))^{N}]

(241)

Proof.

Here we absorb the normalization constant of
P_{\text{BWI}}
into the constants. The proof is similar to the proof of Theorem
B.1
, except that we replace the calculation of the probability mass of
P_{\text{BI}}
with that of
P_{\text{BWI}}
. We start with the lower bound,

\displaystyle\frac{P_{\text{BWI}}(x,y,y^{\prime})}{Q(x,y,y^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=\frac{P(x)P_{\text{BoN}}(y\mid x)P_{\text{WoN}}(y^{\prime}\mid x)}{Q(x,y,y^{\prime})}

(242)

\displaystyle=\frac{P(x)(F(x,y)^{N}-F^{-}(x,y)^{N})((1-F^{-}(x,y^{\prime}))^{N}-(1-F(x,y^{\prime}))^{N})}{Q(x,y,y^{\prime})}

(243)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{P(x)P_{\text{base}}(y\mid x)^{N}P_{\text{base}}(y^{\prime}\mid x)^{N}}{Q(x,y,y^{\prime})}.

(244)

Again, the term that decays with
N
is
P_{\text{base}}(y\mid x)^{N}P_{\text{base}}(y^{\prime}\mid x)^{N}
. So there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\frac{P_{\text{BWI}}(x,y,y^{\prime})}{Q(x,y,y^{\prime})}\geq C\Big(\min_{x,y\neq y^{\prime}}P_{\text{base}}(y\mid x)P_{\text{base}}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\Big)^{N}.

(245)

For the upper bound, we first calculate the probability mass of
P_{\text{WoN}}(A_{x,\alpha}\mid x)
. We assume the same outcome ordering as in the proof of
P_{\text{BI}}
,

\displaystyle P_{\text{WoN}}(A_{x,\alpha}\mid x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}P_{\text{WoN}}(y_{i}\mid x)

(246)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}((1-F^{-}(x,y_{i}))^{N}-(1-F(x,y_{i}))^{N})

(247)

\displaystyle=1-(1-F(x,y_{k}))^{N}

(248)

\displaystyle=1-(1-P_{\text{base}}(A_{x,\alpha}))^{N}.

(249)

With this, we have

\displaystyle\Pr_{P_{\text{BWI}}}(y\in A_{x,\alpha},y^{\prime}\notin A_{x,\alpha})+\Pr_{P_{\text{BWI}}}(y\notin A_{x,\alpha},y^{\prime}\in A_{x,\alpha})

(250)

\displaystyle=(P_{\text{BoN}}(A_{x,\alpha}\mid x))(1-P_{\text{WoN}}(A_{x,\alpha}\mid x))+(1-P_{\text{BoN}}(A_{x,\alpha}\mid x))(P_{\text{WoN}}(A_{x,\alpha}\mid x))

(251)

\displaystyle=(P_{\text{base}}(A_{x,\alpha}))^{N}(1-P_{\text{base}}(A_{x,\alpha}))^{N}+(1-(P_{\text{base}}(A_{x,\alpha}))^{N})(1-(1-P_{\text{base}}(A_{x,\alpha}))^{N})

(252)

\displaystyle\leq 1+(P_{\text{base}}(A_{x,\alpha}))^{N}(1-P_{\text{base}}(A_{x,\alpha}))^{N}.

(253)

∎

B.3
General-
Q
estimation error.

Let
P
be a comparison distribution, let
\mathcal{H}
be a convex hypothesis class bounded by some constant
B
, and let
r^{*}_{P}\in\arg\min_{r\in\mathcal{H}}\mathcal{L}_{P}(r)
. Let
S
be a set of
n
i.i.d. samples drawn from
P
, and let
\hat{r}
be the empirical risk minimizer of the BT learning objective based on
S
. Then there exists a constant
D>0
such that with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\mathbb{E}_{Q}[(\Delta_{\hat{r}}-\Delta_{r^{*}_{P}})^{2}]\leq D\frac{\operatorname{Comp}(n,\mathcal{H},\delta)}{\lambda_{\text{conn}}(P,Q;\mathcal{H})}

(254)

where

\operatorname{Comp}(n,\mathcal{H},\delta):=\frac{1}{\sigma_{B}}\left(\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{S}(\mathcal{H}_{\text{pair}})+M_{B}\sqrt{\frac{\log(2/\delta)}{n}}\right),

(255)

with
M_{B}:=\log(1+\exp(2B))
and
\sigma_{B}:=\sigma(2B)(1-\sigma(2B))
.

Proof.

If
\lambda_{\text{conn}}(P,Q;\mathcal{H})=0
, the claim is vacuous. So assume that
\lambda_{\text{conn}}(P,Q;\mathcal{H})>0
. By the definition of the connectivity degree,

\mathbb{E}_{Q}[(\Delta_{\hat{r}}-\Delta_{r^{*}_{P}})^{2}]\leq\frac{1}{\lambda_{\text{conn}}(P,Q;\mathcal{H})}\mathbb{E}_{P}[(\Delta_{\hat{r}}-\Delta_{r^{*}_{P}})^{2}].

(256)

By Lemma
A.7
,

\mathbb{E}_{P}[(\Delta_{\hat{r}}-\Delta_{r^{*}_{P}})^{2}]=\lVert\hat{r}-r^{*}_{P}\rVert_{\Delta,P}\leq\frac{2}{\sigma_{B}}(\mathcal{L}_{P}(\hat{r})-\mathcal{L}_{P}(r^{*}_{P})).

(257)

Let
\widehat{\mathcal{L}_{P}}
denote the empirical BT risk on the sample
S
. Since
\hat{r}
is the empirical risk minimizer,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{P}(\hat{r})-\mathcal{L}_{P}(r^{*}_{P})

\displaystyle=(\mathcal{L}_{P}(\hat{r})-\widehat{\mathcal{L}_{P}}(\hat{r}))+(\widehat{\mathcal{L}_{P}}(\hat{r})-\widehat{\mathcal{L}_{P}}(r^{*}_{P}))+(\widehat{\mathcal{L}_{P}}(r^{*}_{P})-\mathcal{L}_{P}(r^{*}_{P}))

(258)

\displaystyle\leq 2\sup_{r\in\mathcal{H}}|\mathcal{L}_{P}(r)-\widehat{\mathcal{L}_{P}}(r)|.

(259)

Now let
\ell(a)=-\log\sigma(a)
. Since
\mathcal{H}
is bounded by
B
, we have
|r(x,y)-r(x,y^{\prime})|\leq 2B
, so

\ell(a)\leq\log(1+\exp(2B)):=M_{B}

(260)

for all relevant
a
. Moreover,
\ell
is
1
-Lipschitz. Therefore, by the standard Rademacher-complexity bound and the contraction lemma, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\sup_{r\in\mathcal{H}}|\mathcal{L}_{P}(r)-\widehat{\mathcal{L}_{P}}(r)|\leq 2\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{S}(\mathcal{H}_{\text{pair}})+3M_{B}\sqrt{\frac{\log(2/\delta)}{n}}.

(261)

Combining the last three displays, we obtain

\mathbb{E}_{Q}[(\Delta_{\hat{r}}-\Delta_{r^{*}_{P}})^{2}]\leq\frac{1}{\lambda_{\text{conn}}(P,Q;\mathcal{H})}\left(\frac{8}{\sigma_{B}}\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{S}(\mathcal{H}_{\text{pair}})+\frac{12M_{B}}{\sigma_{B}}\sqrt{\frac{\log(2/\delta)}{n}}\right).

(262)

Absorbing the numerical factors into a constant
D
proves the lemma.
∎

See
5.7

Proof.

Since
r^{*}_{\text{BI}}
preserves the ranking induced by
r^{*}
, the sign-based accuracy with respect to
r^{*}
is the same as the sign-based accuracy with respect to
r^{*}_{\text{BI}}
. By Lemma
B.3
applied with
P=P_{\text{BI}}
and
r^{*}_{P}=r^{*}_{\text{BI}}
, there exists a constant
D>0
such that with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\mathbb{E}_{Q}[(\Delta_{\hat{r}}-\Delta_{r^{*}_{\text{BI}}})^{2}]\leq D\frac{\operatorname{Comp}(n,\mathcal{H},\delta)}{\lambda_{\text{conn}}(P_{\text{BI}},Q;\mathcal{H})}.

(263)

For any threshold
u>0
, order preservation implies

\displaystyle\operatorname{Acc}_{Q}(\hat{r})

\displaystyle\geq\Pr_{Q}(|\Delta_{\hat{r}}-\Delta_{r^{*}_{\text{BI}}}|\leq|\Delta_{r^{*}_{\text{BI}}}|)

(264)

\displaystyle\geq\Pr_{Q}(|\Delta_{r^{*}_{\text{BI}}}|\geq u)-\Pr_{Q}(|\Delta_{\hat{r}}-\Delta_{r^{*}_{\text{BI}}}|>u)

(265)

\displaystyle\geq\Pr_{Q}(|\Delta_{r^{*}_{\text{BI}}}|\geq u)-\frac{\mathbb{E}_{Q}[(\Delta_{\hat{r}}-\Delta_{r^{*}_{\text{BI}}})^{2}]}{u^{2}}

(266)

(267)

5.2
. Because
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}

has non-zero probability mass under

M_{Q}:=\max_{(x,y,y^{\prime})\in\operatorname{supp}(Q)}\sup_{N\geq 1}|B_{N}(x;y,y^{\prime})|<\infty.

(268)

For every
(x,y,y^{\prime})
in the support of
Q
, Proposition
5.2
gives

\Delta_{r^{*}_{\text{BI}}}(x;y,y^{\prime})=N\log\frac{F(x,y)}{F(x,y^{\prime})}+B_{N}(x;y,y^{\prime}).

(269)

Therefore,

|\Delta_{r^{*}_{\text{BI}}}(x;y,y^{\prime})|\geq N\left|\log\frac{F(x,y)}{F(x,y^{\prime})}\right|-M_{Q}.

(270)

(273)

C.1
Synthetic Data Setup

Context and Response Space.

Let
\mathcal{S}=\{u_{1},u_{2},\dots,u_{m}\}
be a finite set of items with
u_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and let
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{Y}=\mathcal{S}
. We use the same space for contexts and responses to simplify the synthetic setting, so the score function can be interpreted as a similarity function between items. We set
m=16
and
d=128
for simplicity. To generate the dataset, we sample each
u_{i}
from
\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
.

Ground-truth Score Function.

The ground-truth score is defined via a two-layer neural network
f^{*}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{e}
with hidden dimension
h=32
and output embedding dimension
e=8
, using ReLU activations:

r^{*}(x,y)=\operatorname{cosine-similarity}(f^{*}(x),f^{*}(y)),

(274)

ensuring scores lie in
[-1,1]
.

Triplet Generation.
We generate
n
triplets
S=\{(x_{i},y_{i}^{+},y_{i}^{-})\}_{i=1}^{n}
by sampling
x_{i}
uniformly from
\mathcal{X}
and drawing
y_{i}^{+}
and
y_{i}^{-}
from a negative distribution
p^{-}(\cdot\mid x_{i})
and a positive distribution
p^{+}(\cdot\mid x_{i})
that is BT-consistent with
r^{*}
, i.e.,
\log(p^{+}(y\mid x)/p^{-}(y\mid x))=r^{*}(x,y)
. By default, we use a uniform negative distribution
p^{-}(\cdot\mid x)=\frac{1}{m}
. The validation set is also generated in the same way.

Training and Evaluation.
We train a two-layer neural network with the same architecture as
f^{*}
to minimize the empirical BT loss on
S
. We use the Adam optimizer with learning rate selected from
\{10^{-4},10^{-3},10^{-2}\}
. We train for 200 epochs and select hyperparameters via a validation set of 2048 triplets drawn from the same distribution based on validation loss. We report accuracy with respect to the uniform distribution
Q
over all pairs
(u_{i},u_{j})
, with standard error over 5 random seeds.

C.6
Details on distribution reshaping

To modify the shape of the base distribution in a standardized way, we use upsampling and downsampling. Each time we sample a response from the base distribution, we accept it with probability
1-p
. Otherwise, we evaluate its reward score. If the score lies in the percentile range
[c-w/2,c+w/2]
for the given context
x
, then we accept the response in the upsampling setting and reject it in the downsampling setting; outside this range, we do the opposite. We repeat this process until we obtain
N=4
responses, and then apply the Best-vs-Random mechanism to generate the training data. Equivalently, this procedure increases or decreases the probability mass in a specified score band, yielding the modified base distributions

P_{\text{base,up}}\propto(1-p)P_{\text{base}}+pP_{c,w}

(275)

P_{\text{base,down}}\propto(1-p)P_{\text{base}}-pP_{c,w}

(276)

where
P_{c,w}
denotes the base distribution with probability mass concentrated in the region whose reward score lies in the percentile range
[c-w/2,c+w/2]
. Here
c
is the percentile center and
w
is the width of the band. We use
c\in\{15,30,50,70,85\}
,
w=30
, and
p=0.8
. This up/downsampling procedure provides a standardized way to reshape the base distribution. For example, setting
c=15
targets lower-score responses, while setting
c=85
targets higher-score responses. We use
n=512
samples for each dataset and train a reward model with the same setup as in the previous experiments.

C.8
Additional details for GSM8K

GSM8K

(
9
)
is a question-answering dataset rather than a preference dataset, so we construct preference pairs from model-generated responses. For each prompt in the test set, we sample multiple responses from the base generator and score them with the reward model. Each response is labeled as correct or incorrect by extracting the final numeric answer and comparing it to the gold answer. We then pair a randomly selected correct response against the highest-scoring incorrect response (i.e., the most convincing wrong answer according to the reward model), and discard prompts that lack either a correct or incorrect response.

A complication is that the reward model is poorly calibrated for mathematical correctness on
GSM8K
: the score distributions for correct and incorrect responses overlap substantially, meaning a non-trivial fraction of correct responses receive lower reward scores than incorrect ones from the same prompt. To avoid contaminating the test set with pairs where the reward model effectively disagrees with the ground-truth labeling, we additionally require the chosen (correct) response to outscore the rejected (incorrect) response by a reward margin of at least 2. Figure
17(a)
shows the resulting score distributions: even after this filter, the chosen and rejected distributions remain heavily overlapping, indicating that the test set is far from trivial.
