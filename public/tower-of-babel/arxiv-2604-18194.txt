Attraction, Repulsion, and Friction: Introducing DMF, a Friction-Augmented Drifting Model
Arkadii Kazanskii, Tatiana Petrova, Konstantin Bagrianskii, Radu State

Abstract
Drifting Models [ 3 ] train a one-step generator by evolving samples under a kernel-based drift field, avoiding ODE integration at inference. The original analysis leaves two questions open. The drift-field iteration admits a locally repulsive regime in a two-particle surrogate, and vanishing of the drift ( V p , q ≡ 0 V_{p,q}\equiv 0 ) is not known to force the learned distribution q q to match the target p p . We derive a contraction threshold for the surrogate and show that a linearly-scheduled friction coefficient gives a finite-horizon bound on the error trajectory. Under a Gaussian kernel we prove that the drift-field equilibrium is identifiable: vanishing of V p , q V_{p,q} on any open set forces q = p q=p , closing the converse of Proposition 3.1 of Deng et al. 2026 . Our friction-augmented model, DMF ( Drifting Model with Friction ), matches or exceeds Optimal Flow Matching on FFHQ adult → \to child domain translation at 16 × 16\times lower training compute.



Keywords:
generative modeling; flow matching; drifting model; dissipation.

1
Introduction

Recent work on generative modelling has reduced sampling to progressively
fewer forward passes. The move from diffusion to flow matching was followed
by methods that aim at straight trajectories and single-step generation,
including Rectified Flow (RF)
[
11
]
,
OT-CFM
[
17
]
, and Optimal Flow Matching
(OFM)
[
7
]
. OFM recovers provably straight,
transport-optimal trajectories, at the cost of a strongly convex inner
optimisation per training step.

Drifting Models (DMs) of
Deng et al. 2026
dispense with ODE
integration altogether: a generator
f_{\theta}
is trained by pushing its
samples under a kernel-based drift field
V_{p,q}=V_{p}^{+}-V_{q}^{-}
, where
V_{p}^{+}

attracts samples to data and
V_{q}^{-}
pushes them apart within the current
batch. DMs attain single-step quality competitive with OFM at a fraction of
the training cost, but the original analysis leaves two questions open.
First, a simple two-particle surrogate of the iteration admits a locally
repulsive regime — at small inter-sample distance the repulsion can
dominate the attraction to the target, and the original paper does not
rule this out. Second, the equilibrium condition
V_{p,q}\equiv 0
is only
shown to be necessary for
q=p
; the converse – Proposition 3.1
of
Deng et al. 2026
– is an open question.

This paper addresses both. Our analysis of the unnormalised two-particle
surrogate (a deliberate simplification of the normalised
field (
1
)) yields a closed-form contraction threshold and
motivates DMF, a variant of DM in which the drift is scaled by

1-\gamma(i)
for a monotone schedule
\gamma(i)\in[0,1]
with

\gamma(0)=0
and
\gamma(T-1)=1
.
Proposition

1
bounds the
finite-horizon error trajectory of DMF in the surrogate in terms of the
positive part of the instability margin. Under a Gaussian kernel we prove that the equilibrium of the
unscaled drift is identifiable: vanishing of
V_{p,q}
on any open subset
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
forces
q=p
(
theorem

1
). Empirically, DMF
matches or exceeds OFM on FFHQ adult
\to
child domain translation at
roughly
16\times
lower training compute. The combination does not yet
constitute a quantitative convergence theorem;
section

5

returns to this point.

Related Work

Flow matching and straight-trajectory generators.

Flow matching
[
10
,
1
]
learns a
time-dependent velocity field whose flow transports a source distribution to
the data distribution. The learned trajectories are typically curved, so
inference requires multi-step ODE integration. Rectified
Flow
[
11
]
straightens trajectories by iterative reflow;
OT-CFM
[
17
]
conditions the target velocity on optimal
transport couplings and recovers straight paths in a single training pass.
These methods retain an ODE formulation and hence require either numerical
integration at inference or an auxiliary training procedure to eliminate it.

Optimal transport parametrisations.

A related line couples generative modelling with
OT
[
8
,
9
]
. Optimal Flow
Matching
[
7
]
, which we take as our principal baseline,
parametrises the velocity field as the gradient of an Input Convex Neural
Network
[
2
,
12
]
so that the minimiser of
the flow-matching objective coincides with the Brenier map for the quadratic
cost. A strongly convex inner optimisation is solved per sample at each
training step.

Drifting Models.

Deng et al. 2026
proposed Drifting Models, in which a one-shot
generator
f_{\theta}
is trained by evolving its samples under a drift field

V_{p,q}=V_{p}^{+}-V_{q}^{-}
. Attraction to the target distribution
p
and
repulsion from the current generator distribution
q
are expressed as
kernel-weighted expectations. DMs attain competitive single-step generation
quality without ODE simulation at inference, but their Proposition 3.1
establishes only one direction of the equivalence:
q=p
implies

V_{p,q}\equiv 0
, while the converse – necessary for an identification
guarantee – is left open. Dynamic stability of the training iteration is
also not analysed. The present work addresses both.

Dissipation in stochastic samplers.

Dissipation terms enter Langevin-based samplers
[
18
]

and score-based SDE models
[
16
]
as an intrinsic part
of the generative dynamics. Our use of dissipation is analogous in spirit
but acts on the ODE-free drift-field iteration of DM rather than on an SDE.

ALAE as a latent backbone.

For domain translation on FFHQ at
1024\!\times\!1024
we follow the protocol
of
Kornilov et al. 2024
and operate in the
W
latent space of
StyleALAE
[
13
]
.

3
Methodology

3.1
Revisiting the Drifting Field Dynamics

We recall the DM construction. Let
p
be the target data distribution and

q
the distribution of samples produced by the current generator. The
drift field used at training is

V_{p,q}(x)=V_{p}^{+}(x)-V_{q}^{-}(x),

(1)

with attraction term
V_{p}^{+}
pulling samples towards
p
and repulsion term

V_{q}^{-}
pushing samples apart in the current batch:

\displaystyle V_{p}^{+}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z_{p}}\,\mathbb{E}_{y^{+}\sim p}\bigl[k(x,y^{+})\,(y^{+}-x)\bigr],

(2)

\displaystyle V_{q}^{-}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z_{q}}\,\mathbb{E}_{y^{-}\sim q}\bigl[k(x,y^{-})\,(y^{-}-x)\bigr],

(3)

with normalisers
Z_{p}(x)=\mathbb{E}_{y^{+}}[k(x,y^{+})]
,
Z_{q}(x)=\mathbb{E}_{y^{-}}[k(x,y^{-})]

and the Laplace kernel

k(x,y)=\exp\!\left(-\frac{\lVert x-y\rVert}{\tau}\right),

(4)

parametrised by a bandwidth
\tau>0
. In
theorem

1
we
analyse the analogue of this construction under a Gaussian kernel; see also

remark

4
.

A local unnormalised surrogate.

To exhibit the attraction–repulsion trade-off in a tractable form we analyse
a local
unnormalised surrogate
of (
1
), obtained by
retaining the kernel-weighted numerator of (
2
)–(
3
)
and dropping the denominators
Z_{p},Z_{q}
. The surrogate is a
simplification of (
1
): the normalised dynamics carries an
extra dependence on
Z_{p}/Z_{q}
which requires a larger-batch or
continuous-density treatment that we do not attempt here. What the
surrogate does capture is the local competition between attraction and
repulsion that drives the instability, and it supports the
schedule-dependent bound of
proposition

1
below.

Consider a training step at which samples are within bandwidth of their
nearest targets (
\lVert x^{i}-y^{+}\rVert\leq\tau
) and localise attention to
one target
y^{+}
and two generated particles
x_{1}^{i}
,
x_{2}^{i}
placed
symmetrically across it at distance
a
:

y^{+}=0,\qquad x_{1}^{i}=+a,\qquad x_{2}^{i}=-a.

(5)

The unnormalised surrogate drift acting on
x_{1}
sums the raw kernel-weighted
attraction and repulsion contributions,

\widehat{V}(x_{1})\;=\;k(x_{1}^{i},y^{+})(y^{+}-x_{1}^{i})\;-\;k(x_{1}^{i},x_{2}^{i})(x_{2}^{i}-x_{1}^{i})\;=\;-k_{t}\,a\;+\;2k_{d}\,a,

(6)

with

\displaystyle k_{t}

\displaystyle=k(x_{1}^{i},y^{+})=e^{-a/\tau}

\displaystyle\text{(kernel to target)},

(7)

\displaystyle k_{d}

\displaystyle=k(x_{1}^{i},x_{2}^{i})=e^{-2a/\tau}=k_{t}^{2}

\displaystyle\text{(kernel between particles)}.

(8)

The corresponding update is

x_{1}^{i+1}\;=\;x_{1}^{i}+\widehat{V}(x_{1}^{i})\;=\;a\,(1-k_{t}+2k_{d}).

(9)

Contraction towards
y^{+}
,
\lvert x_{1}^{i+1}\rvert<\lvert x_{1}^{i}\rvert
,
holds precisely when

\boxed{\;k_{d}<\frac{k_{t}}{2}\;}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad k_{t}<\tfrac{1}{2}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\boxed{\;a\,>\,\tau\ln 2\;}.

(10)

For
a<\tau\ln 2
the (surrogate) repulsion from
x_{2}
overpowers the
attraction to
y^{+}
and the error
\varepsilon^{i}=x_{1}^{i}-y^{+}
obeys

\varepsilon^{i+1}\;=\;\lambda(a^{i})\,\varepsilon^{i},\qquad\lambda(a)\;=\;1-k_{t}(a)+2k_{d}(a),

(11)

with
\lambda(a)>1
iff
a<\tau\ln 2
. In this regime the trajectory is
locally pushed away from the target; the full nonlinear surrogate iteration
has a non-zero fixed point at
a=\tau\ln 2
(where
\lambda=1
), to which
trajectories on both sides asymptote (see
fig.

1
). The
surrogate therefore captures a locally divergent, globally bounded dynamics;
we refer to the regime
a<\tau\ln 2
as
locally repulsive
and
reserve “divergent” for the linearised behaviour near the target.

Remark 1

(Fixed-point structure of the surrogate)
.

The scalar iteration
a^{i+1}=f(a^{i})
with

f(a)=a\,(1-k_{t}(a)+2k_{d}(a))
,
k_{t}(a)=e^{-a/\tau}
,

k_{d}(a)=k_{t}(a)^{2}
, has two fixed points. The trivial one,
a=0
, is
locally expansive:
f^{\prime}(0)=2
. The non-trivial one,
a^{\ast}=\tau\ln 2

(solving
k_{t}=2k_{d}
, equivalently
k_{t}=\tfrac{1}{2}
), is locally
contracting:

f^{\prime}(a^{\ast})\;=\;1-\tfrac{\ln 2}{2}\;\approx\;0.653.

Monotone trapping follows from the factorisation

f(a)-a\;=\;a\,e^{-a/\tau}\bigl(2e^{-a/\tau}-1\bigr),

whose sign is positive on
(0,a^{\ast})
and negative on
(a^{\ast},\infty)
;
moreover
f
maps each of these intervals into itself, as can be seen from

f(a^{\ast})=a^{\ast}
and the continuity of
f
. Hence every trajectory
from
a_{0}>0
is monotone and converges to
a^{\ast}
; neither the target

y^{+}=0
nor the threshold
a^{\ast}
are themselves crossed.

Figure

1
(a), (b), (d) illustrate this on both sides of

a^{\ast}
.

In the full many-particle training dynamics, with a neural-network
parametrisation of
V
and stochastic batches, distances may alternate
between the locally repulsive and locally attractive regimes across steps.
Such alternation is outside the scope of the scalar
surrogate (
11
), whose monotone behaviour is described
above; the surrogate captures only the local mechanism.

3.2
Dissipation as a Stabilising Mechanism

The locally repulsive regime of (
11
) is analogous to
an underdamped mechanical oscillator, in which a restoring force alone is
insufficient for convergence and a dissipative term is required. Motivated
by this analogy, we introduce a scalar friction coefficient
\gamma\in[0,1]

and apply it to the DM drift at training:

V_{\gamma}(x)\;=\;(1-\gamma)\,V(x),

(12)

where
V
is the drift field of (
1
) (including the
normalisers). In the surrogate of
section

3.1
, the same scaling
applied to
\widehat{V}
yields the per-step update

x_{1}^{i+1}\;=\;a\,\lambda_{\gamma}(a),\qquad\lambda_{\gamma}(a)\;=\;1-(1-\gamma)\bigl(k_{t}(a)-2k_{d}(a)\bigr).

(13)

All statements of this subsection concern the surrogate dynamics; the
correspondence with the full normalised dynamics is empirical and is
discussed in
section

5
.

Per-step growth factor.

Let
\tilde{\eta}(a)\coloneqq 2k_{d}(a)-k_{t}(a)
denote the
signed
instability margin
of the linearised surrogate
dynamics (
11
):
\tilde{\eta}(a)>0
in the locally
repulsive regime
a<\tau\ln 2
and
\tilde{\eta}(a)\leq 0
otherwise.
Equation (
13
) is equivalent to

\varepsilon^{i+1}=(1+(1-\gamma)\tilde{\eta}(a^{i}))\,\varepsilon^{i}
; for any
fixed
\gamma
the deviation
\lvert\lambda_{\gamma}-1\rvert
is

(1-\gamma)\lvert\tilde{\eta}(a^{i})\rvert
, so at
\gamma=\tfrac{1}{2}
the
repulsive growth rate is halved relative to the frictionless case.

A finite-horizon cumulative bound.

Let
\tilde{\eta}^{i}\coloneqq\tilde{\eta}(a^{i})
and write

\eta^{+}_{i}\coloneqq\max(\tilde{\eta}^{i},0)
for its positive part.

Assumption 1

(Uniform upper bound on the positive margin)
.

There exists
\eta_{\max}\geq 0
such that
\eta^{+}_{i}\leq\eta_{\max}
for
every step
i\in\{0,\dots,T-1\}
. In the surrogate of
section

3.1

one has
k_{d}=k_{t}^{2}
with
k_{t}\in(0,1]
, so

\tilde{\eta}(a)=2k_{t}^{2}-k_{t}\leq 1
(with the bound attained as
k_{t}\to 1
);
hence
\eta_{\max}\leq 1
unconditionally. The assumption is made at this
level of generality to accommodate alternative surrogates (e.g. more
particles) in which the range of
\tilde{\eta}
differs.

Proposition 1

(Finite-horizon cumulative bound)
.

Let
T\geq 2
and let
\gamma:\{0,\dots,T-1\}\to[0,1]
be a non-decreasing
schedule with
\gamma(0)=0
and
\gamma(T-1)=1
. Suppose that

1
holds and that the multiplier is non-negative, i.e.
1+(1-\gamma(i))\,\tilde{\eta}^{i}\geq 0
for every
i\in\{0,\dots,T-1\}
— a
condition automatically satisfied in the two-particle surrogate of

section

3.1
, where
\tilde{\eta}^{i}\in[-1/8,1]
. Then the error

\varepsilon^{i}=x_{1}^{i}-y^{+}
of the surrogate
update (
13
) satisfies

\lvert\varepsilon^{T}\rvert\;\leq\;\lvert\varepsilon^{0}\rvert\,\prod_{i=0}^{T-1}\bigl(1+(1-\gamma(i))\,\eta_{\max}\bigr)\;\leq\;\lvert\varepsilon^{0}\rvert\,\exp\!\Bigl(\eta_{\max}\!\sum_{i=0}^{T-1}(1-\gamma(i))\Bigr).

(14)

For the linear schedule
\gamma(i)=i/(T-1)
one has

\sum_{i=0}^{T-1}(1-\gamma(i))=T/2
and the bound specialises to

\lvert\varepsilon^{T}\rvert\leq\lvert\varepsilon^{0}\rvert\,e^{\eta_{\max}T/2}
.

Proof.

The update (
13
) reads

\varepsilon^{i+1}=(1+(1-\gamma(i))\tilde{\eta}^{i})\,\varepsilon^{i}
. The
positivity hypothesis gives

\lvert\varepsilon^{i+1}\rvert\;=\;\bigl(1+(1-\gamma(i))\tilde{\eta}^{i}\bigr)\,\lvert\varepsilon^{i}\rvert\;\leq\;\bigl(1+(1-\gamma(i))\,\eta^{+}_{i}\bigr)\,\lvert\varepsilon^{i}\rvert\;\leq\;\bigl(1+(1-\gamma(i))\,\eta_{\max}\bigr)\,\lvert\varepsilon^{i}\rvert,

where the first inequality uses
\tilde{\eta}^{i}\leq\eta^{+}_{i}
and

(1-\gamma(i))\geq 0
, and the second uses
1
.
Telescoping yields the first bound of (
14
). The
second bound follows from
\ln(1+x)\leq x
for
x\geq 0
and summation of the
logarithms. The linear-schedule specialisation is a direct arithmetic
simplification.
∎

Remark 2

(Finite- vs infinite-horizon boundedness)
.

Proposition

1
is a
finite-horizon
bound: it controls

\lvert\varepsilon^{T}\rvert
at a fixed training horizon
T
. The bound
grows exponentially in
T
, so uniform boundedness as
T\to\infty
does not
follow from the statement above; it would require a schedule with

\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}(1-\gamma(i))<\infty
, which is not satisfied by the
linear schedule. In practice, training is run at a fixed
T
and the
nonlinear surrogate iteration is bounded globally by the non-zero fixed
point
a=\tau\ln 2
; the bound above describes the early-time growth within
this globally bounded envelope.

Remark 3

(Friction as drift-amplitude annealing)
.

Equation (
12
) is operationally equivalent to a
drift-amplitude annealing schedule applied to
V
; the term
friction

is used here by analogy with a damped oscillator rather than as a strict
mechanical descriptor. The contribution of this subsection is not the
annealing itself but its coupling to the instability margin of

section

3.1
: the schedule is selected to bound the quantity that the
surrogate dynamics causes to grow, not as a generic learning-rate
heuristic. A velocity-damping variant that corresponds more literally to
mechanical friction is analysed in
appendix

B
.

Proposition

1
does not expand the per-step stability region
of (
10
). It bounds the cumulative divergence
over
T
steps and forces the per-step update magnitude to zero as

\gamma\!\to\!1
. The empirical consequences of this mechanism are examined
in
section

4
. A velocity-damping variant that scales the
velocity rather than the drift force is analysed
in
appendix

B
; it does not enlarge the unstable regime and
yields a strictly looser cumulative bound.

3.3
Identifiability of the DM Equilibrium

Proposition

1
bounds the training trajectory but does not
identify the distribution that it approaches. The same gap was noted
by
Deng et al. 2026
in their Proposition 3.1: the equilibrium
condition
V_{p,q}\equiv 0
is necessary but not, in general, sufficient
for
q=p
. We close the gap under a Gaussian kernel.

Theorem 1

(Identifiability at equilibrium, Gaussian kernel)
.

Let
p,q
be Borel probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and let
V_{p,q}
be
the drift field of (
1
) computed with the Gaussian kernel

k_{\tau}(x,y)=\exp(-\lVert x-y\rVert^{2}/(2\tau^{2}))
,
\tau>0
. If there
exists a non-empty open set
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
on which
V_{p,q}(x)=0
for
every
x\in U
, then
p=q
as probability measures.

The proof is given in full in
appendix

A
. It uses four
ingredients specific to the Gaussian kernel:
(i) the log-gradient identity

V_{\mu}(x)=\tau^{2}\nabla_{x}\log Z_{\mu}(x)
with

Z_{\mu}=\mu*\psi_{\tau}
, which holds for the Gaussian and fails for the
Laplace kernel;
(ii) real-analyticity of
Z_{\mu}
, obtained by holomorphic extension and
Fubini;
(iii) the identity theorem for real-analytic functions, which propagates the
zero set of
V_{p,q}
from
U
to all of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
;
(iv) Fourier inversion using that
\hat{\psi}_{\tau}>0
.

Corollary 1

(Closing the converse of Deng et al., Proposition 3.1)
.

Let
p
be the target distribution and
q
any Borel probability measure on

\mathbb{R}^{d}
. If the unscaled Gaussian-kernel drift
V_{p,q}
of (
1
)
vanishes on any non-empty open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then
q=p
.

Corollary

1
restates
theorem

1
in equilibrium
form. The statement is about the
unscaled
drift
V_{p,q}
, not about
the friction-augmented
V_{\gamma}=(1-\gamma)V
: at
\gamma=1
the latter
vanishes trivially for every
q
, so the corollary does not by itself
characterise the fixed points of DMF. This point is revisited
in
section

5
.

Taken together, these two results isolate complementary mechanisms rather
than combining into a convergence theorem:

proposition

1
gives a finite-horizon bound for the
unnormalised surrogate, while
theorem

1
identifies the
unique zero of the unscaled Gaussian drift at the population level. No
quantitative rate of approach is established, and the experiments
of
section

4
use the Laplace kernel
of
Deng et al. 2026
, for which step (i) of the proof fails; an
analogous identifiability statement for that kernel remains open
(
remark

4
).

3.4
Practical Implementation

Friction schedule.

We use
\gamma
as a hyperparameter that increases monotonically over
training. The two-particle analysis of
section

3.1
underestimates the
actual instability for two reasons. First, each generated sample interacts
with many repulsors rather than one. Second, the drift is approximated by a
neural network, so inter-particle distances are perturbed at each step.
Under these conditions, a schedule that starts near
\gamma=0
(favouring
exploratory shifts towards the target) and reaches
\gamma=1
at the end of
training (damping residual divergence via (
14
)) is
a natural choice. In our experiments the linear schedule

\gamma(i)\;=\;\frac{i}{T-1},\qquad i\in\{0,\dots,T-1\},

(15)

satisfies
\gamma(0)=0
and
\gamma(T-1)=1
exactly, as required by

proposition

1
, and performed best among the variants we
considered.

Discussion

Mechanism in the surrogate.

By
remark

1
, the frictionless iteration has a non-trivial
stable fixed point at
a^{\ast}=\tau\ln 2
: every trajectory from
a_{0}>0

is attracted to this force-balance radius rather than to the target at

y^{+}=0
(
fig.

1
). The friction schedule

\gamma(i)\to 1
shrinks the per-step update to zero and halts the
iteration at a state
a^{T}<a^{\ast}
before it relaxes to the fixed
point (
fig.

1
c). DMF therefore
avoids
the
surrogate’s pathological attractor by not completing the approach to it.

Extrapolation to DMF training.

Empirically, DMF improves over DM on FID (
table

2
). Two
mechanisms are consistent with this improvement. At the sample level,
the surrogate suggests that friction prevents local force-balance
configurations of the form isolated by the two-particle analysis. At the
NN-parameter level, the schedule
\gamma(i)\to 1
acts as an effective
learning-rate annealing on the drift update, allowing the parameters to
settle. Disentangling these contributions and establishing a rigorous
link between the surrogate and the full batch-training outcome is left
to future work.

What the two results give, and what they do not.

The cumulative bound of
proposition

1
is a finite-horizon
statement on the surrogate dynamics of DMF; it does not expand the
per-step contraction region of the frictionless iteration, nor does it
imply uniform boundedness as
T\to\infty
. The identifiability result
(
theorem

1
) concerns the zeros of the
unscaled

Gaussian drift
V_{p,q}
rather than the schedule-dependent

V_{\gamma}=(1-\gamma)V
used in DMF; at
\gamma(T-1)=1
the latter
vanishes trivially for every
q
, so the combination of the two results
is not a convergence theorem for DMF. A quantitative rate of approach to
that distribution is left to future work.

Limitations.

The two-particle surrogate of
section

3.1
is a deliberate
simplification; the full dynamics involve many particles, a neural
approximation of the drift, and the normalisers
Z_{p},Z_{q}
that the
surrogate drops. The identifiability theorem is specific to the Gaussian
kernel — the analogous result for the Laplace kernel of our
implementation is open (
remark

4
).

Future work.

Four directions are natural. First, extending
theorem

1
to
the Laplace kernel, either by an analytic-continuation argument via
cumulant-generating functions or by recovering a log-gradient-type identity
for a different smoothed density. Second, a quantitative convergence rate
to the equilibrium, closing the gap between the current bounded-trajectory
statement and distributional convergence. Third, learned or data-dependent
schedules
\gamma(i,x)
. Fourth, scaling studies beyond FFHQ, including
class-conditional generation on ImageNet.

Broader impact.

DMF inherits the capabilities and risks of any high-fidelity face generator.
Because the experimental setting is unpaired translation between age groups,
standard misuse risks apply (non-consensual edits, identity manipulation).
We do not release trained checkpoints; our released code is intended only
for methodological reproduction on public benchmarks.

Appendix A
Proof of
Theorem

1
(Identifiability under Gaussian kernel)

We restate
theorem

1
for convenience and prove it in full.

Theorem 2

(Restatement of
theorem

1
)
.

Let
p,q
be Borel probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\geq 1
. Define the
Gaussian kernel
\psi_{\tau}(z)=\exp(-\lVert z\rVert^{2}/(2\tau^{2}))
for

\tau>0
, and, for any
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the kernel density estimator

Z_{\mu}(x)\;:=\;\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\psi_{\tau}(x-y)\,d\mu(y),

and the drift

V_{\mu}(x)\;:=\;\frac{1}{Z_{\mu}(x)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\psi_{\tau}(x-y)(y-x)\,d\mu(y).

Write
V_{p,q}:=V_{p}-V_{q}
. If there exists a non-empty open set

U\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
on which
V_{p,q}(x)=0
for every
x\in U
, then
p=q
.

Proof.

We proceed in four steps.

Step 1 (Well-posedness).

Since
0<\psi_{\tau}\leq 1
and
\mu
is a probability measure,
Z_{\mu}(x)

is a finite positive real number for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and every

\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. In particular
Z_{p},Z_{q}>0
pointwise on

\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Step 2 (Log-gradient identity).

The map
x\mapsto\psi_{\tau}(x-y)
is
C^{\infty}
for every
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and

\nabla_{x}\psi_{\tau}(x-y)\;=\;-\frac{x-y}{\tau^{2}}\,\psi_{\tau}(x-y)\;=\;\frac{y-x}{\tau^{2}}\,\psi_{\tau}(x-y).

The Gaussian factor bounds the integrand uniformly: the scalar map

r\mapsto r\,e^{-r^{2}/(2\tau^{2})}
attains its maximum
\tau/\sqrt{e}
on

[0,\infty)
, so for every
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\bigl\lvert\nabla_{x}\psi_{\tau}(x-y)\bigr\rvert\;=\;\frac{\lvert x-y\rvert}{\tau^{2}}\,\psi_{\tau}(x-y)\;\leq\;\frac{1}{\tau\sqrt{e}}.

This bound is independent of
y
and
x
, hence integrable against any
probability measure
\mu
; dominated convergence justifies differentiation
under the integral sign without any moment assumption on
\mu
. Hence

\nabla_{x}Z_{\mu}(x)\;=\;\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{y-x}{\tau^{2}}\,\psi_{\tau}(x-y)\,d\mu(y)\;=\;\frac{Z_{\mu}(x)\,V_{\mu}(x)}{\tau^{2}}.

(19)

Dividing by
Z_{\mu}(x)>0
gives the identity

V_{\mu}(x)\;=\;\tau^{2}\,\nabla_{x}\log Z_{\mu}(x),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(20)

Step 3 (Real-analytic extension).

We show that
Z_{\mu}
extends to an entire function on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
, so
that its restriction to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is real-analytic. For
z\in\mathbb{C}^{d}

define

Z_{\mu}(z)\;:=\;\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\exp\!\Bigl(-\tfrac{1}{2\tau^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{d}(z_{j}-y_{j})^{2}\Bigr)\,d\mu(y),

where the inner sum is the complex bilinear form, not
\lVert z-y\rVert^{2}
.
Write
z=x+i\xi
with
x,\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then

\sum_{j=1}^{d}(z_{j}-y_{j})^{2}\;=\;\lVert x-y\rVert^{2}-\lVert\xi\rVert^{2}+2\,i\,\xi\!\cdot\!(x-y),

so that the modulus of the integrand equals

\psi_{\tau}(x-y)\,\exp(\lVert\xi\rVert^{2}/(2\tau^{2}))
. On any compact set

K\subset\mathbb{C}^{d}
we have
\sup_{z\in K}\lVert\xi\rVert\leq R_{K}
for
some
R_{K}<\infty
, and
\psi_{\tau}(x-y)\leq 1
; hence the integrand is
uniformly bounded by
\exp(R_{K}^{2}/(2\tau^{2}))
and the integral converges
absolutely and uniformly on
K
. Since the integrand is holomorphic in
z

for each fixed
y
and is uniformly integrable on compact subsets of

\mathbb{C}^{d}
, Morera’s theorem combined with Fubini implies that

z\mapsto Z_{\mu}(z)
is holomorphic on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
. Its restriction to

\mathbb{R}^{d}
is therefore real-analytic; so is
\log Z_{\mu}
(since

Z_{\mu}>0
) and so is
\log Z_{p}-\log Z_{q}
.

Step 4 (Propagation and Fourier inversion).

By (
20
) and hypothesis,

\nabla_{x}\log(Z_{p}/Z_{q})(x)=V_{p,q}(x)/\tau^{2}=0
for every
x\in U
.
The function
\log Z_{p}-\log Z_{q}
is real-analytic (Step 3), so every
partial derivative of it is real-analytic. By the identity theorem for
real-analytic functions on the connected domain
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, a real-analytic
function that vanishes on the non-empty open set
U
vanishes everywhere.
Applying this to each component of
\nabla\log(Z_{p}/Z_{q})
, we conclude

\nabla_{x}\log(Z_{p}(x)/Z_{q}(x))=0
for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, hence there
exists a constant
c\in\mathbb{R}
with

Z_{p}(x)\;=\;e^{c}\,Z_{q}(x),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Integrating: by Fubini,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}Z_{\mu}(x)\,dx\;=\;\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\!\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\psi_{\tau}(x-y)\,d\mu(y)\,dx\;=\;\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\psi_{\tau}(z)\,dz\;=\;(2\pi\tau^{2})^{d/2},

so
\int Z_{p}=\int Z_{q}=(2\pi\tau^{2})^{d/2}
, forcing
e^{c}=1
. Therefore

Z_{p}\equiv Z_{q}
, i.e.
p*\psi_{\tau}=q*\psi_{\tau}
pointwise.

Both sides are non-negative, bounded, and integrable:
Z_{p},Z_{q}\leq 1

pointwise and
\int Z_{\mu}\,dx=(2\pi\tau^{2})^{d/2}
(Fubini), so

Z_{p},Z_{q}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. The
L^{1}
Fourier
transform is therefore well-defined; the convolution theorem for the
Fourier transform of a finite measure with an
L^{1}
function gives

\hat{p}(\omega)\,\hat{\psi_{\tau}}(\omega)\;=\;\hat{q}(\omega)\,\hat{\psi_{\tau}}(\omega),\qquad\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

The Gaussian’s Fourier transform

\hat{\psi_{\tau}}(\omega)=(2\pi\tau^{2})^{d/2}\exp(-\tau^{2}\lVert\omega\rVert^{2}/2)

is strictly positive everywhere, so we may divide:

\hat{p}(\omega)=\hat{q}(\omega)
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. By the uniqueness theorem for
characteristic functions of probability measures (Lévy’s theorem),
p=q
.
∎

Remark 4

(Laplace kernel)
.

The publicly released DM implementation and our code both use the Laplace
kernel
\psi^{L}_{\tau}(z)=\exp(-\lVert z\rVert/\tau)
. Two steps of the
proof above fail for this kernel. First, the log-gradient
identity (
19
) depends on

\nabla_{z}\psi_{\tau}(z)\propto z\,\psi_{\tau}(z)
, which holds for the
Gaussian but not for the Laplace kernel, whose gradient

\nabla_{z}\psi^{L}_{\tau}(z)=-(z/\tau\lVert z\rVert)\psi^{L}_{\tau}(z)

carries a non-polynomial factor
1/\lVert z\rVert
. Second,
\psi^{L}_{\tau}

is not real-analytic at
z=0
; consequently
Z_{\mu}=\mu*\psi^{L}_{\tau}

need not be real-analytic (take
\mu=\delta_{0}
to see that

Z_{\delta_{0}}=\psi^{L}_{\tau}
itself is not real-analytic at
x=0
), so
the identity-theorem argument of Step 3 does not apply either. What does
extend is
Fourier injectivity
: the Laplace kernel’s Fourier
transform
\hat{\psi^{L}_{\tau}}(\omega)\propto(1+\tau^{2}\lVert\omega\rVert^{2})^{-(d+1)/2}
is strictly positive on

\mathbb{R}^{d}
, so
p*\psi^{L}_{\tau}=q*\psi^{L}_{\tau}
implies
p=q
. Any route
that establishes
p*\psi^{L}_{\tau}=q*\psi^{L}_{\tau}
from

V_{p,q}\equiv 0
in the Laplace case therefore suffices; we leave a
rigorous treatment (e.g. via analytic continuation of cumulant-generating
functions of compactly supported
p,q
) as future work. For DM/DMF
implementations, switching the kernel from Laplace to Gaussian is a
three-line change and brings the population-level drift-equilibrium
analysis into alignment with the kernel used in training; it does not, by
itself, extend
theorem

1
to finite-batch neural
optimisation.

Appendix B
Second-order DMF: stability and cumulative bound

The main text uses the first-order friction update of
(
12
), which scales the drift
force
. This
appendix analyses a velocity-damping alternative that scales the

velocity
directly, equivalent to a heavy-ball iteration with
time-varying momentum coefficient
1-\gamma(i)

(
definition

1
below). We show that the variant does not
enlarge the unstable regime
\eta\geq 0
, and its cumulative bound is
strictly looser than
proposition

1
by a factor of

\exp((\eta_{\max}+1)T/2)
. The first-order form is retained throughout
the main text; the analysis below is included for completeness and as a
starting point for a momentum-based variant.

Definition 1

(Second-order DMF)
.

Given a friction schedule
\gamma:\{0,\dots,T-1\}\to[0,1]
and an initial
velocity
v^{0}=0
, the
second-order DMF
update is

v^{i+1}=(1-\gamma(i))\,v^{i}+V(x^{i}),\qquad x^{i+1}=x^{i}+v^{i+1}.

(21)

B.1
Heavy-ball equivalence

Proposition 2

(Heavy-ball form)
.

For the second-order DMF update (
21
) with
v^{0}=0
, the
position iterates satisfy, for every
i\geq 1
,

x^{i+1}\;=\;x^{i}\;+\;V(x^{i})\;+\;(1-\gamma(i))\bigl(x^{i}-x^{i-1}\bigr).

Proof.

From the update,
v^{i}=x^{i}-x^{i-1}
for
i\geq 1
. Substituting into

v^{i+1}=(1-\gamma(i))v^{i}+V(x^{i})
gives

x^{i+1}-x^{i}=(1-\gamma(i))(x^{i}-x^{i-1})+V(x^{i})
, which rearranges to
the stated identity.
∎

Proposition

2
identifies the second-order DMF update with the
classical Polyak heavy-ball method
[
14
]
applied to the
drift field
V
with a time-varying momentum coefficient

\beta(i):=1-\gamma(i)
.

B.2
Per-step spectral stability in the linearised two-particle regime

We linearise as in
section

3.1
: near the target
y^{+}=0
, the drift on
the particle
x_{1}
takes the form
V(x)=\eta\,x
with
\eta=2k_{d}-k_{t}\in\mathbb{R}

(positive in the unstable regime, non-positive in the stable regime). For a

fixed
momentum coefficient
\beta\in[0,1]
the augmented state

s^{i}:=(x^{i},v^{i})^{\top}
satisfies
s^{i+1}=M(\beta)\,s^{i}
with

M(\beta)\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}1+\eta&\beta\\
\eta&\beta\end{pmatrix}.

Proposition 3

(Spectrum of
M(\beta)
)
.

\det M(\beta)=\beta
,
\operatorname{tr}M(\beta)=1+\eta+\beta
, and the
characteristic polynomial is

\chi_{\beta}(\lambda)\;=\;\lambda^{2}-(1+\eta+\beta)\lambda+\beta.

The discrete spectral radius satisfies
\rho(M(\beta))<1
if and only if the
following three conditions jointly hold:

\displaystyle(\mathrm{i})

\displaystyle\quad\lvert\det M(\beta)\rvert=\beta<1,

\displaystyle(\mathrm{ii})

\displaystyle\quad 1+\operatorname{tr}M(\beta)+\det M(\beta)=2+\eta+2\beta>0,

\displaystyle(\mathrm{iii})

\displaystyle\quad 1-\operatorname{tr}M(\beta)+\det M(\beta)=-\eta>0.

For
\eta\geq 0
no
\beta\in[0,1)
makes
M(\beta)
stable; the second-order
update therefore does not enlarge the per-step stability region of the
first-order update.

Proof.

The determinant computation is direct:
(1+\eta)\beta-\eta\beta=\beta
. The
trace and characteristic polynomial are then immediate. Conditions (i)-(iii)
are the Jury/Schur-Cohn criterion for a real quadratic polynomial to have
both roots strictly inside the unit disc
[
15
, §4.3]
. For
\eta\geq 0
, condition (iii) fails, so at
least one eigenvalue of
M(\beta)
lies on or outside the unit circle.
∎

Remark 5
.

For
\eta>0
the spectral radius of
M(\beta)
exceeds
1
for every fixed

\beta\in[0,1)
. Adding Polyak momentum does not stabilise the first-order
linear system in the unstable regime.

B.3
Cumulative bound under a monotone friction schedule

Since per-step stability is unchanged by the addition of momentum, any
benefit of the second-order update must come from the non-stationary
schedule.

Proposition 4

(Cumulative bound for the second-order iteration)
.

Assume the per-step margin
\eta^{i}=2k_{d}(a^{i})-k_{t}(a^{i})
is
non-negative for every
i\in\{0,\dots,T-1\}
(locally repulsive regime)
and satisfies
\eta^{i}\leq\eta_{\max}
for some
\eta_{\max}\geq 0
. Under
the linear schedule
\gamma(i)=i/(T-1)
, the second-order surrogate
trajectory starting from
(x^{0},0)
satisfies

\max\!\bigl(\lvert x^{T}\rvert,\lvert v^{T}\rvert\bigr)\;\leq\;\lvert x^{0}\rvert\,\exp\!\bigl(\eta_{\max}\,T+T/2\bigr)\;=\;\lvert x^{0}\rvert\,\exp\!\bigl((2\eta_{\max}+1)\,T/2\bigr).

Proof.

Write
\beta(i)=1-\gamma(i)=1-i/(T-1)
and
M_{i}:=M(\beta(i))
with

\eta=\eta^{i}
. Under
\eta^{i}\geq 0
, every entry of
M_{i}
is
non-negative and

\lVert M_{i}\rVert_{\infty}\;=\;\max\!\bigl((1+\eta^{i})+\beta(i),\;\eta^{i}+\beta(i)\bigr)\;=\;1+\eta^{i}+\beta(i)\;\leq\;1+\eta_{\max}+\beta(i).

By sub-multiplicativity and induction (with
v^{0}=0
, so

\lVert s^{0}\rVert_{\infty}=\lvert x^{0}\rvert
),

\lVert s^{T}\rVert_{\infty}\;\leq\;\prod_{i=0}^{T-1}\bigl(1+\eta_{\max}+\beta(i)\bigr)\,\lvert x^{0}\rvert.

Taking logarithms and using
\ln(1+x)\leq x
for
x\geq 0
:

\sum_{i=0}^{T-1}\ln\!\bigl(1+\eta_{\max}+\beta(i)\bigr)\;\leq\;\eta_{\max}\,T+\sum_{i=0}^{T-1}\!\!\Bigl(1-\tfrac{i}{T-1}\Bigr)\;=\;\eta_{\max}\,T+T/2,

which gives
\lVert s^{T}\rVert_{\infty}\leq\lvert x^{0}\rvert\exp(\eta_{\max}T+T/2)

and rearranges to the stated form.
∎

Remark 6
.

Proposition

4
is stated under
\eta^{i}\geq 0
. In the
two-particle surrogate the per-step margin
\eta^{i}=2k_{d}(a^{i})-k_{t}(a^{i})

additionally satisfies
\eta^{i}\geq-1/8
, so the mixed regime (locally
repulsive and locally attractive steps interleaving) is bounded by the same
argument with
\eta^{i}
replaced by
\lvert\eta^{i}\rvert\leq\max(\eta_{\max},1/8)

inside
\lVert M_{i}\rVert_{\infty}
. The resulting bound replaces

\eta_{\max}
in the exponent by
\max(\eta_{\max},1/8)
and is therefore
only a constant worse than
proposition

4
for

\eta_{\max}\geq 1/8
.

Remark 7

(Comparison with first-order)
.

Proposition

1
gave
\lvert\varepsilon^{T}\rvert\leq\lvert\varepsilon^{0}\rvert\,\exp(\eta_{\max}T/2)
for the first-order DMF;
proposition

4
gives
\max(\lvert x^{T}\rvert,\lvert v^{T}\rvert)\leq\lvert x^{0}\rvert\,\exp(\eta_{\max}T+T/2)
for the second-order variant. The
second-order exponent exceeds the first-order exponent by

\eta_{\max}T/2+T/2=(\eta_{\max}+1)T/2
: half a
T
from momentum
accumulation and an additional
\eta_{\max}T/2
because momentum amplifies the
instability over time. The bounded-trajectory guarantee of the second-order
update is therefore strictly
weaker
in this non-stationary analysis.
Empirically, this upper-bound looseness need not reflect actual dynamics:
momentum can provide variance reduction on stochastic gradient estimates.
The analysis above establishes
that the second-order update is well-defined, that it reduces to the
baseline DM step as
\gamma\to 1
, and that its trajectory remains bounded
under the same assumption as the first-order variant.

Remark 8

(Late-training behaviour)
.

As
\gamma\to 1
,
\beta\to 0
, and the second-order update
v^{i+1}=V(x^{i})
,

x^{i+1}=x^{i}+v^{i+1}=x^{i}+V(x^{i})
coincides with the first-order update
at
\gamma=0
, i.e. with the
baseline
DM step. This is in contrast to
first-order DMF, where
\gamma\to 1
freezes the trajectory. The two variants
therefore implement different late-training regularisations: first-order DMF
anneals the step magnitude to zero, while second-order DMF anneals the
momentum to zero. A hybrid variant
v^{i+1}=\beta(i)v^{i}+(1-\gamma(i))V(x^{i})

that combines both mechanisms recovers freezing at
\gamma\to 1
and is an
obvious direction for further study.
