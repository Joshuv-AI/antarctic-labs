Design, Synthesis and Biological Evaluation of New Oxazolidinone Antimicrobial Agents with Low Monoamine Oxidase A Inhibition
Authors: Jie Zhang, Yuhua Hu, Hao Tian, Chuang Li, Zihe Liang, Yuhe Yuan, Fangfang Lai, Wenxuan Zhang, Song Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionLinezolid is an oxazolidinone antibacterial agent that acts by binding to the 23S rRNA of the bacterial 50S ribosomal subunit to inhibit protein synthesis and exhibits potent activity against Gram-positive pathogens and Mycobacterium tuberculosis. Although resistance to linezolid remains relatively low, its clinical use is limited by safety concerns arising from inhibition of monoamine oxidase A (MAO-A). To address this limitation, we designed and synthesized a series of novel oxazolidinone derivatives with reduced MAO-A inhibition and preserved antibacterial potency.MethodsWe applied a rational drug design strategy based on the distinct binding models of linezolid within human MAO-A and the ribosomal large subunit of Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus). Molecular docking was used to screen designed compounds for high affinity toward the S. aureus ribosome and low affinity toward MAO-A. Selected compounds were synthesized and evaluated for in vitro antibacterial activity against Gram-positive bacteria. Compounds with strong antibacterial activity were further assessed for MAO-A inhibition, anti-Mycobacterium tuberculosis (M. tuberculosis) activity, and cytotoxicity.Results & DiscussionThis strategy led to the identification of compound 3a as a highly promising oxazolidinone derivative. 3a exhibited superior antibacterial activity compared with linezolid against S. aureus, methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA), Staphylococcus epidermidis (S. epidermidis), and Enterococcus faecalis (E. faecalis), with minimum inhibitory concentrations (MIC) of approximately 0.5–1 μg/mL. Importantly, 3a showed significantly reduced inhibitory activity against human MAO-A. The compound also retained potent activity against M. tuberculosis H37Rv and displayed low cytotoxicity toward human cells. Notably, 3a possesses core potential as a lead compound for the development of next-generation of oxazolidinone antibiotics.ConclusionThese results closely validated our rational design strategy.

Introduction

The discovery of antibiotics with novel mechanisms of action has become increasingly difficult.1 At the same time, widespread and inappropriate antibiotic use has accelerated the emergence of bacterial resistance.2,3 Together, these factors have intensified the global antimicrobial resistance crisis, which now poses a serious threat to public health worldwide.4 In 2024, the World Health Organization classified antimicrobial resistance as a global public health emergency. Infections caused by methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) and vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus (VRE) remain particularly concerning.5 The O’Neill Report, commissioned by the UK government, further projected that drug-resistant infections could cause up to 10 million deaths annually by 2050 if effective interventions are not implemented.6

Against this background, linezolid has demonstrated substantial clinical value.7 Linezolid is the first fully synthetic oxazolidinone antibacterial agent and was approved in the United States in 2000.8 The drug exerts its antibacterial activity by binding to the 23S rRNA in the peptidyl transferase center (PTC) of the bacterial 50S ribosomal subunit. This interaction prevents the correct accommodation of aminoacyl‑tRNA into the ribosomal A site and thereby inhibits the elongation stage of bacterial protein synthesis.9–11 This mechanism enables linezolid to treat infections caused by a broad range of Gram-positive pathogens, including drug-resistant strains such as MRSA and VRE.12 Linezolid also serves as a key therapeutic agent for multidrug-resistant and rifampicin-resistant tuberculosis.13,14 Chinese studies have shown that the baseline resistance rate to linezolid is relatively low, being 0.8% among tuberculosis patients overall and only 2.2% among patients with multidrug-resistant tuberculosis.15 Therefore, linezolid demonstrates considerable clinical value.

Despite its efficacy, safety concerns significantly limit the long-term and widespread use of linezolid.16 Adverse reactions associated with bone marrow suppression and monoamine oxidase (MAO) inhibition present major clinical challenges.17 Clinical studies have reported high incidences of peripheral neuropathy, anemia, leukopenia, and optic neuritis in patients receiving linezolid therapy. The risk of these adverse effects increases substantially when the daily dose exceeds 12 mg/kg.18 Bone marrow suppression (myelosuppression) is a critical hematologic toxicity of linezolid. This toxicity has been closely linked to the finding that linezolid’s primary metabolite, PNU142586, directly targets DNA topoisomerase IIα (TOP2A) and IIβ (TOP2B). By disrupting DNA replication, transcription, and the ATP hydrolysis activity of these enzymes, this action triggers antiproliferative and cytotoxic effects in hematopoietic cells.19 These myelosuppressive effects typically manifest after more than 10 days of continuous linezolid treatment, consistent with the time‑dependent accumulation of toxic metabolites and progressive impairment of bone marrow cell function. In contrast, adverse reactions related to MAO inhibition may occur early during therapy.16

Linezolid is a weak, reversible, competitive, and nonspecific MAO inhibitor.20 MAO is a mitochondrial enzyme that is highly expressed in neuronal tissues and is responsible for the oxidative deamination of aromatic amines. Two MAO isoforms exist in humans. MAO-A preferentially metabolizes serotonin and norepinephrine, whereas MAO-B primarily degrades phenylethylamine and dopamine.21 Oxazolidinones can inhibit MAO, particularly MAO-A, due to their structural similarity to toloxatone, a known MAO-A inhibitor used as an antidepressant.22 Inhibition of MAO-A, combined with the intake of tyramine-rich foods or certain medications, may trigger severe hypertensive crises, known as the “cheese effect.”.23 These safety concerns highlight the urgent need to develop new oxazolidinone antibiotics with reduced MAO-A inhibition while maintaining potent antibacterial activity.

In this study, we designed a series of novel oxazolidinone derivatives by investigating the differential binding models of linezolid with MAO-A and the S. aureus ribosome. Virtual screenings were performed using molecular docking against the crystal structure of the S. aureus ribosomal large subunit in complex with linezolid (PDB: 4WFA)24 and the crystal structure of human MAO-A bound to harmine (PDB: 2Z5X).25 This approach enabled us to prioritize compounds with high ribosomal affinity and low MAO-A binding potential. We synthesized the selected compounds and evaluated their antibacterial activity against four Gram-positive pathogens: S. aureus, MRSA, S. epidermidis, and E. faecalis. We further assessed MAO-A inhibition and anti-M. tuberculosis activity for the most promising candidates.

Material and Methods

Molecular Docking

The Glide module of the Schrodinger programme was used to perform molecular docking. The Protein Data Bank (www.rcsb.org) was searched to retrieve the X-ray crystallographic structures of the large ribosomal subunit of S. aureus in complex with linezolid24 and the crystal structure of human MAO-A bound to harmine.25 The accession IDs of these structures are 4WFA and 2Z5X, respectively. Although there are documented sequence variations in ribosomal drug-binding residues among different bacterial species,26 the ribosomal drug-binding sites of S. aureus are highly conserved overall, with only a small number of lineage-specific natural variations, and this species does not belong to the highly divergent group. The 4WFA crystal structure, despite having a lower resolution compared to 7TTU27 and 6DDD,28 originates from S. aureus (including MRSA), unambiguously defines linezolid’s binding mode, and its binding pocket is highly conserved among the clinically relevant Gram-positive pathogens targeted in our antibacterial evaluation. It offers a dependable and representative framework for structure-based drug design. The preparation of the retrieved protein structures was carried out using the Schrodinger program’s preparation wizard panel. During the preparation steps, missing hydrogens were added, bond ordering was assigned, disulfide bonds were created and missing side chains and loops were replaced using Prime. In the final refinement step, full energetic optimization was achieved using the OPLS4 force field, with the RMSD of heavy atoms fixed at 0.3 Å. The LigPrep panel was used to prepare linezolid and its analogues, resulting in low 3D structures with acceptable chirality. The ionization state of each ligand structure was created at a physiological pH of 7.2 ± 0.2. The generation of the glide grid file was done through selection of the co-crystallized ligand in the receptor grid generating window. The calculation of a default center and size for the region for which grids will be produced is based on the position and size of the ligand. This calculation is carried out by Glide. The molecular docking was finally carried out using Schrodinger’s screening workflow. This involved loading the prepared ligands and receptor grid file into Maestro’s work area, and then using “standard precision (SP)” methodology to dock the ligands into the target protein’s binding pocket. However, molecular docking of small molecules onto structured RNAs such as the bacterial ribosome remains notoriously challenging,29–31 we visually inspected the predicted binding poses of every compound against the empirical binding modes of linezolid, tedizolid, and other oxazolidinones observed in crystal structures. This manual verification step allowed us to exclude poses with steric clashes, missing essential hydrogen bonds, or unrealistic tail orientations, and to retain only those that are consistent with the well‑established binding mode of the oxazolidinone class. While no shape bias was used when docking with MAO-A.

Chemistry

All materials were obtained from commercial suppliers and were used without further purification unless stated otherwise. Reactions were monitored by TLC (silica gelGF254) from Yantai Chemical Industry Research Institute. Column chromatography was run on silica gel (200–300 mesh) from Qingdao Ocean Chemicals (Qingdao, Shandong, China). Final compounds were determined via analysis by Waters UPLC/SQD system using a reversed-phase C18 column with 5%–95% CH3CN in water (0.1% HCOOH) for 10 min at a flow rate of 0.2 mL/min. All melting points were recorded on a Mettler Toledo MP80 automatic melting point system. Mass spectra (MS) were taken in ESI mode on Agilent 1100 LC-MS (Agilent, Palo Alto, CA, U.S.A). 1H NMR and 13C NMR spectra were recorded on Bruker AV-III, 400 and 500 MHz spectrometers (Bruker Bioscience, Billerica, MA, USA) with TMS as an internal standard.

Biological Assay

In vitro Antibacterial Activity

The antibacterial activity of all compounds was measured according to the broth dilution method prescribed by the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines. Four Gram-positive bacteria strains (S. aureus ATCC 29213, MRSA ATCC 43300, E. faecalis ATCC29212, and S. epidermidis ATCC 12228) and one Gram-negative bacteria strains (E. coli ATCC 25922) were purchased from American Type Culture Collection, Manassas, VA, USA. All strains were stored at −80°C in glycerol stock before culture and were revived by overnight incubation at 37°C with shaking at 220 rpm prior to use. 100 μL of each different concentration of the test sample solution was added to the sterilized 96-well polystyrene plate, and the final concentrations of the drug were 128, 64, 32, 16, 8, 4, 2, 1, 0.5, 0.25, 0.125, 0.06 μg/mL, respectively. 100 μL of the bacterial suspension was added to each well, and the final concentration of the solution was about 105 CFU/mL. The plates were sealed and incubated in an incubator at 35–37°C for 18–24 h to determine the results. The MIC was defined as the lowest concentration showing no visible bacterial growth, determined by visual inspection of broth clarity. MIC values were determined in six independent replicates and presented as descriptive endpoints.

Evaluation of Antibacterial Activity Against M. tuberculosis

MIC against replicating Mycobacterium tuberculosis (M. tuberculosis) was determined using the microplate Alamar blue assay (MABA). Linezolid was included as a positive control. M. tuberculosis H37Rv (ATCC 27294) was grown to the late log phase (70–100 Klett units) in Difco Middlebrook 7H9 Broth (catalog no. 271310), supplemented with 0.2% (vol/vol) glycerol, 0.05% Tween 80, and 10% (vol/vol) albumin-dextrose-catalase (BBL Middlebrook ADC Enrichment, catalog no. 212352) (designated as 7H9-ADC-TG). Cultures were harvested by centrifugation, washed twice, and suspended in phosphate-buffered saline. The suspension was filtered through an 8 µm pore-size filter to eliminate aggregates, aliquots for single use, and flash-frozen at −80°C. For each independent assay, a new aliquot was thawed to prepare the inoculum for genuine biological replication of H37Rv. Two-fold serial dilutions of test compounds and positive controls were prepared in 7H9-ADC-TG at a volume of 100 µL per well using 96-well black, clear-bottom microplates (BD Biosciences). A 100 µL aliquot of M. tuberculosis suspension (containing 2 × 105 CFU) was dispensed into each well, giving a total assay volume of 200 µL. Plates were incubated at 37°C; on day 7, 12.5 µL of 20% Tween 80 and 20 µL of Alamar blue reagent were added to all wells. After additional 37°C incubation for 16–24 h, fluorescence was recorded with excitation at 530 nm and emission at 590 nm. The MIC was defined as the lowest concentration effecting a reduction in fluorescence of ≥90% relative to the mean of replicate bacterium-only controls. MIC determinations were performed in six independent biological replicates, each conducted on a separate day using an independently thawed bacterial aliquot. All data from the six replicates were presented as descriptive endpoints.

MAO-A Inhibitory Activity Evaluation

All reagents required for the experiment, including the assay buffer, substrate solution, detection working solution, and MAO-A enzyme solution, were prepared in advance and kept on ice prior to use. The stock solutions of test compounds were diluted with assay buffer to appropriate concentrations. Microplate loading was performed, and blank control wells (containing only buffer, without enzyme, substrate, or compound, for background correction), negative control wells (containing enzyme, substrate, and an equal volume of buffer or 1% DMSO), positive control wells (containing enzyme, substrate, and a known concentration of positive drug such as safinamide/linezolid), and test compound wells (containing enzyme, substrate, and different concentrations of the test compound) were set up as required. After loading, the plate was incubated at 37°C in the dark for 30 minutes. Subsequently, 100 µL of the reaction solution (composed of assay buffer, Amplex Red, horseradish peroxidase (HRP), and the substrate tyramine) was added to each well. The reaction was allowed to proceed at 37°C in the dark for an additional 30 minutes. Fluorescence was measured using a microplate reader with excitation at 560 ± 10 nm and emission at 590 ± 10 nm. The inhibition rate (%) of the compounds against MAO was calculated according to the following formula:

Calculate the inhibition rate (%) of the compound against MAO enzyme: [1 − (Sample fluorescence intensity − Blank control fluorescence intensity)/(Negative control fluorescence intensity − Blank control fluorescence intensity)] * 100%.

Statistical analysis was undertaken only for groups with a size of n = 6, where group size is the number of independent values. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Prior to one-way ANOVA, the normality of data distribution was verified via Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was assessed using Levene’s test in GraphPad Prism 8.0.2. Since the data violated the normality assumption (Shapiro–Wilk test, p < 0.05) and the homogeneity of variances assumption (Levene’s test, p < 0.05), one-way ANOVA was replaced by nonparametric tests: the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn’s test for multiple comparisons with the linezolid group. Statistical significance was accepted at the level of P < 0.05.

In vitro Cytotoxicity

Compound 3a was screened on human normal hepatocyte LO2 cells, human embryonic kidney HEK293 cells, and human renal proximal tubule HK-2 cells using a standard Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay in vitro. Human normal hepatocytes LO2, human embryonic kidney cell HEK293 and human renal proximal tubule cells HK2 cells were all purchased from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% (v/v) penicillin-streptomycin. Approximately 104 cells were plated onto each well of a 96-well plate and incubated in 5% CO2 at 37°C for 24 h. After the cells adhered to the plate surface, the medium was carefully removed. Test compounds were serially diluted with medium (DMSO ≤ 0.1%), Fresh medium supplemented with the test compound at concentration gradients of 6.25 μM, 12.5 μM, 25 μM and 30 μM was added. In addition, blank control and solvent control groups were set up simultaneously. Paclitaxel (purity ≥ 97%, Sigma-Aldrich) was used as a positive control to validate the assay. The concentration range of paclitaxel against the three cell lines was set according to references.32–34 Stock solutions were prepared in DMSO and diluted in culture medium immediately before use to the following final concentration ranges: 0.01–10 μM for LO2 cells, 1–1000 nM for HEK293 cells, and 0.01–100 μM for HK2 cells. The final DMSO concentration in all wells, including vehicle controls, was kept below 0.1% (v/v). All groups were incubated continuously for 48 h under the same culture conditions. Then, fresh 10 μL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well and incubated with cells at 37°C for 4 h and the absorbance at 450 nm was measured. The cell viability was calculated using the following formula: Cell viability (%) = [(OD(drug) − OD(blank))/(OD(control) − OD(blank))] × 100%. Where OD(drug) is the absorbance value of the drug treatment group, OD(blank) is the absorbance value of the blank control group, and OD(control) is the absorbance value of the normal control group. Statistical analysis was undertaken only for groups with a size of n = 6, using GraphPad prism 8.0.2 software. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Results and Discussion

Compound Design and Virtual Screening

We first analyzed the structural differences between the binding pockets of linezolid in MAO-A and in the ribosomal large subunit of S. aureus (Figure 1). When bound to MAO-A, the morpholine ring of linezolid occupies a narrow and hydrophobic pocket. The interaction is primarily mediated by weak hydrophobic contacts with residues such as PHE208 and ILE335. Conversely, the morpholine ring binds within a larger and more open pocket in the S. aureus ribosome. This ribosomal site is surrounded mainly by nucleobases, including A2478 and U2533. These observations suggest that the ribosomal binding pocket can accommodate bulkier substituents than the MAO-A pocket. In addition, the known structure–activity relationship of linezolid35–41 also showed that the C-ring region is well tolerant to structural modification,42–45 making it an attractive site for further optimization. Based on the differences in the binding modes of linezolid to MAO-A and the S. aureus ribosome, we proposed increasing steric hindrance at the C-ring region to weaken MAO-A binding while preserving ribosomal affinity. To implement this strategy, we replaced the morpholine ring with a piperazine linker.46 We further incorporated thiazole47–50 and pyridazine moieties,51–54 which are privileged heterocycles frequently found in anti-infective agents. Moreover, the pyridazine nucleus has been utilized in oxazolidinone-based antibacterial design.55 Using this design strategy, we generated a series of oxazolidinone derivatives composed of the linezolid core, a piperazine linker, and substituted thiazole or pyridazine rings (Figure 2). Binding Modes of Compound 3a

We wished to better understand the binding mode of compounds 3a, which exhibited excellent antibacterial activity in vitro. A molecular docking study was conducted based on the crystallization of the large ribosomal subunit of S. aureus in complex with linezolid (PDB 4WFA). The conformation of compound 3a revealed an identical site-specific pattern and perfect overlap with linezolid, indicating that compound 3a may bind to the 50S ribosomal subunit in the same manner as linezolid (Figure 5). Conclusion

In this study, we applied a rational drug design strategy based on structural differences between the MAO-A binding pocket and the ribosomal binding site of S. aureus. This approach enabled the identification of compound 3a as a highly selective oxazolidinone derivative. Compound 3a exhibited the strongest antibacterial activity among the tested compounds and showed lower MIC values than linezolid against multiple Gram-positive pathogens. Importantly, compound 3a demonstrated significantly reduced MAO-A inhibition, consistent with our design hypothesis. The compound also retained potent activity against M. tuberculosis H37Rv and exhibited low cytotoxicity toward human cells. Ongoing studies are evaluating the in vivo antibacterial efficacy and safety profile of compound 3a in mouse models.
