Aberrometric and Visual Outcomes Following Planned Axis Flip versus No Axis Flip During Toric Intraocular Lens Implantation: A Comparative Observational Study
Authors: Alicia C Espinosa-Moreno, Leire Irusteta-Jiménez, Daniela Pulido-London, Azyadeh Camacho-Ordonez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo compare postoperative aberrometric and visual outcomes between eyes with and without a planned astigmatic axis flip after toric intraocular lens (IOL) implantation.SettingInstituto de Oftalmologia Conde de Valenciana, Mexico City, Mexico.DesignComparative observational study.MethodsThis study included patients who underwent uneventful cataract surgery with toric IOL implantation to minimize residual astigmatism, eyes were classified into undercorrection without an axis flip and an overcorrection with an axis flip. Postoperative evaluation included uncorrected and best-corrected visual acuity, spherical equivalent refraction, and aberrometry using the Pentacam AXL Wave (Oculus Optikgeräte GmbH). Aberrometric parameters included total higher-order aberrations (HOA), coma, spherical aberration (SA), and trefoil. Intergroup comparisons were performed using independent-samples t-tests with Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate correction for multiple comparisons. Multivariate linear regression analyses were performed adjusting for age, sex, preoperative astigmatism magnitude and type, IOL model, and axial length. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.ResultsEighty eyes of 80 patients were analyzed (mean age of 67.16 ± 11.49 years). There were no significant differences between planned axis flip and no-axis flip groups in uncorrected distance visual acuity (p = 0.628), best-corrected distance visual acuity (p = 0.685), or spherical equivalent refraction (p = 0.455). Total HOA, coma, and SA were comparable between groups (all p > 0.05). In contrast, the planned axis flip group demonstrated a higher trefoil aberration compared with the no-axis flip group (0.15 ± 0.06 µm vs 0.08 ± 0.09 µm; p < 0.001). Multivariate linear regression analysis confirmed that axis flip was independently associated with higher trefoil aberration (β = 0.075, 95% CI [0.038, 0.111]; p = 0.0001), with no significant associations found for any other aberrometric or visual outcome.ConclusionPlanned astigmatic axis flip during toric IOL implantation was not associated with differences in postoperative visual outcomes, despite a significant increase in trefoil aberration. These findings suggest that planned axis flip does not appear to compromise visual or aberrometric performance and may be considered among the available strategies to minimize residual astigmatism. Prospective studies incorporating functional vision metrics and longer follow-up are needed to confirm these findings.

Introduction

Astigmatism correction during cataract surgery plays a critical role in achieving optimal postoperative visual quality.1 Corneal astigmatism is a common optical aberration in which refractive power differs across meridians, preventing light rays from converging at a single focal point on the retina.1 Uncorrected astigmatism can result in reduced visual acuity, image distortion, and glare, with its functional impact influenced by both magnitude and axis orientation.2

Despite advances in toric intraocular lens (IOL) technology, residual refractive astigmatism remains a clinical challenge. Postoperative residual astigmatism ranging from 0.18 to 0.77 diopters has been reported following toric IOL implantation.2,3 Contemporary toric calculators allow surgeons to balance undercorrection and overcorrection strategies; however, sometimes overcorrection may lead to astigmatic axis flip, defined as a shift in the orientation of the residual refractive astigmatism to the meridian 90° away from the preoperative corneal astigmatism axis, resulting from the selection of an IOL cylinder power that overcorrects the existing astigmatism.4

The choice between undercorrection and overcorrection remains clinically relevant. Undercorrection avoids axis flipping but may leave residual astigmatism, whereas overcorrection can improve astigmatic correction at the expense of potential axis flip. Traditionally, axis flipping has been discouraged due to concerns about reduced visual quality, largely derived from spectacle optics, where meridional magnification may induce visual disturbances. However, this assumption may not be directly applicable to toric IOLs, as intraocular correction occurs near the nodal point of the eye. Consequently, the trade-off between residual astigmatism and axis flipping remains insufficiently defined.5

Evaluating the outcomes of an axis-flip strategy requires precise knowledge of two interdependent parameters: the postoperative refractive cylinder axis which confirms whether an axis flip has actually occurred, defined as a shift of 90° ± 22.5° from the preoperative corneal astigmatism axis, and the actual intraoperative toric IOL alignment.4 The postoperative refractive cylinder axis is the definitive indicator of whether residual astigmatism has flipped, while actual IOL alignment determines the effectiveness of the correction: even small degrees of misalignment can substantially reduce astigmatic correction, potentially nullifying the advantage of overcorrection.6 Distinguishing a true planned axis flip from an unintended IOL rotation is therefore essential to correctly attribute refractive outcomes to the correction strategy itself.

Traditional optometric principles have historically discouraged astigmatic overcorrection due to patient intolerance associated with meridional magnification and spatial distortion in spectacle correction.7 These limitations, however, are largely related to vertex distance and are not directly applicable to IOL implantation. From an optical standpoint, astigmatic magnitude may have a greater impact on visual performance than axis orientation.4,6

Recent studies have suggested that axis flip following toric IOL implantation does not adversely affect visual acuity or patient-reported outcomes.7,8 Nevertheless, these investigations have primarily relied on conventional visual acuity metrics; some have included limited patient satisfaction measures, though none have employed validated PRO instruments with a comprehensive assessment of visual disturbances.5 Furthermore, patient-reported outcomes (PROs) such as satisfaction, spectacle independence, and subjective perception of visual disturbances represent a complementary and increasingly essential dimension of postoperative assessment.9,10 The absence of validated PRO instruments in prior axis-flip studies limits the ability to conclude the patient’s functional experience, independent of measured optical parameters. Similarly, these investigations have primarily relied on conventional visual acuity metrics, without objective assessment of the aberrometric profile.

Wavefront aberrometry provides a comprehensive evaluation of optical performance by quantifying lower- and higher-order aberrations, offering insight beyond standard visual acuity measurements.11,12 Clinically, this distinction is relevant because eyes with identical Snellen acuity may differ in their aberrometric profile, with higher-order aberrations, particularly coma and spherical aberration, contributing to symptoms such as glare, halos, and reduced contrast sensitivity, especially under mesopic conditions.13 Standard visual acuity metrics may therefore fail to detect subtle but functionally significant differences in optical quality between correction strategies, underscoring the need for objective aberrometric assessment in comparative studies of toric IOL outcomes. The Pentacam AXL Wave integrates corneal tomography, optical biometry, and Hartmann–Shack wavefront analysis, enabling objective assessment of aberrations and postoperative visual quality.14,15 This device has demonstrated high repeatability and reliability in measuring ocular aberrations in both normal and post-surgical eyes.16 We hypothesized that planned axis flip following toric IOL implantation would result in comparable aberrometric and visual outcomes to eyes without axis flip, given that the optical effects of intraocular astigmatism correction differ fundamentally from those of spectacle-based correction. The purpose of this study was to compare postoperative aberrometric and visual outcomes between eyes with and without planned axis flip after toric IOL implantation and to determine whether this strategy affects aberrometric and visual outcomes.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

This comparative observational study included 80 eyes of 80 patients aged ≥18 years who underwent uneventful phacoemulsification cataract surgery with toric IOL implantation at the Instituto de Oftalmología Conde de Valenciana in Mexico City between August 2024 and July 2025. All participants were fully informed about the study and provided written informed consent prior to enrollment, as this was an essential requirement for inclusion.

Axis flip is defined as a shift in the orientation of the residual refractive astigmatism to the meridian 90° away from the preoperative corneal astigmatism axis, resulting from selection of an IOL cylinder power that overcorrects the existing astigmatism. Eyes were allocated preoperatively to one of two groups based on the manufacturer-specific toric IOL calculator output generated during the surgical planning visit, prior to any intervention. When the lowest-residual IOL option produced residual astigmatism on the same axis as the preoperative corneal astigmatism, the eye was allocated to the no-flip group (undercorrection); when it produced residual astigmatism on the opposite axis (90° away), the eye was allocated to the axis-flip group (overcorrection). In all cases, the IOL yielding the lowest predicted residual astigmatism was selected, consistent with the split-the-difference principle adopted by contemporary toric calculators.4,17 No surgeon’s discretion was applied in group assignment.

Exclusion criteria included previous ocular surgery, ocular trauma, corneal scarring, preexisting corneal disease (eg, keratoconus, corneal dystrophies), retinal pathology (eg, age-related macular degeneration, diabetic retinopathy), optic nerve disease (eg, glaucoma), and intraoperative or postoperative surgical complications (eg, posterior capsule rupture, IOL decentration, significant postoperative inflammation).

Ophthalmologic Assessment

All patients underwent a complete ophthalmologic examination 3 months postoperatively, a time point at which toric IOL rotational stability and refractive outcomes are known to be stable.18 The examination included measurement of uncorrected distance visual acuity (UDVA) and best corrected distance visual acuity (BCDVA) using standardized ETDRS charts at 4 meters, slit-lamp biomicroscopy, dilated fundus examination, and aberrometric assessment. Visual acuity was recorded in logMAR notation for statistical analysis.

Preoperative keratometry and biometry were performed using the IOLMaster 700 (Carl Zeiss Meditec AG, Jena, Germany). Toric IOL power calculation was performed using the manufacturer-specific online toric calculator for each platform: the Barrett Toric Calculator for ZCT lenses (Johnson & Johnson Vision), and the respective manufacturer calculators for ANKORIS (Hanita Lenses), MX60T (Bausch + Lomb), and SNA6AT (Alcon Laboratories). Posterior corneal astigmatism and surgically induced astigmatism were considered as incorporated in the manufacturer-specific calculator. All eyes were targeted for the lowest possible residual refractive astigmatism. Depending on the individual case and calculator recommendation, the refractive strategy consisted of either undercorrection without an axis flip or overcorrection with an axis flip.17,19

Postoperative manifest refraction was performed by a trained optometrist under photopic conditions at the 3-month visit. Residual refractive cylinder magnitude was recorded in diopters and spherical equivalent was used for group comparisons. Toric IOL axis position was assessed at the 3-month visit under pharmacological mydriasis (tropicamide 1%) using the slit-lamp method, in which a thin coaxial slit beam was aligned along the axis markings of the IOL, consistent with the standard technique described in the literature.20

Surgical Technique

All surgeries were performed by experienced surgeons using a standardized phacoemulsification technique through a 2.4-mm temporal clear corneal incision. After cortical cleanup and capsular polishing, the toric IOL was implanted in the capsular bag. Intraoperative axis alignment was performed using digital marking systems with the patient in the supine position. The IOL was rotated to the predetermined axis, and proper alignment was confirmed before surgery was completed. No adjunctive measures for rotational stability (eg, optic capture, capsular tension rings) were employed.

Aberrometry Measurement

Whole-eye wavefront aberrometry was assessed using the Pentacam ® AXL Wave (Oculus Optikgeräte GmbH, Wetzlar, Germany), which integrates a Hartmann–Shack aberrometer to obtain objective refraction and whole-eye aberration data.14,15

All eyes underwent a full-sequence examination performed by a trained and experienced technician before instillation of any topical drops and under scotopic lighting conditions (approximately 1 lux). For all measurements, the analysis zone for whole-eye aberrations was standardized at 4.0 mm, and the vertex distance was set at 13.75 mm.

Aberrometric parameters were recorded as root mean square (RMS) values in micrometers (µm) and included total higher-order aberrations (HOA), coma, spherical aberration (SA), and trefoil. Only measurements with quality scores ≥90% were included in the analysis. Each parameter was measured three times, and the average value was used for statistical analysis.

Sample Size Calculation

Sample size calculation was based on detecting a difference of 0.10 μm in RMS aberrations, assuming a standard deviation of 0.15 μm, with 80% power and a 95% confidence level. Using these parameters, a minimum of 36 eyes per group was required. Accounting for potential losses and ensuring adequate statistical power, a total sample size of 80 eyes (40 in each group) was determined.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 31.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Normality of distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages.

Comparisons between groups were conducted using independent-samples t-tests with Welch correction when variances were unequal. For categorical variables, Fisher’s exact test was used when expected cell counts were small. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Mean differences were reported with 95% confidence intervals (CI). To account for multiple comparisons, the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure was applied separately within two conceptually distinct outcomes domains: visual and refractive outcomes (UDVA, BCDVA, and spherical equivalent; k=3) and aberrometric outcomes (total HOA, coma, spherical aberration, and trefoil; k=4). FDR-adjusted p-values are reported alongside unadjusted values in the results tables.

Multivariate linear regression analyses were performed for the primary aberrometric outcomes (total HOA, trefoil, and coma) and for each visual and refractive outcome, with group assignment (axis flip vs no-flip) as the primary predictor. Covariates included age, sex, preoperative astigmatism magnitude and type (with-the-rule, against-the-rule, oblique), IOL model, and axial length. Standardized beta coefficients and 95% confidence intervals are reported.

An exploratory subgroup analysis was performed restricted to the two most frequently implanted IOL models (ZCT, n=39; ANKORIS, n=34), which together accounted for 91.3% of the sample, to evaluate whether aberrometric outcomes differed by IOL platform within each correction strategy group. Given the limited subgroup sample sizes, these analyses are considered hypothesis-generating only.

Results

Demographic and Clinical Characteristics

Eighty eyes of 80 patients were included, with a mean age of 67.16 ± 11.49 years. Forty-nine patients (61.3%) were female, and 31 (38.8%) were male. Four models of toric IOL were implanted. The ZCT model (Johnson & Johnson Vision, Santa Ana, CA, USA) was the most frequently used, implanted in 39 eyes (48.8%), followed by the ANKORIS model (Hanita Lenses, Kibbutz Hanita, Israel) in 34 eyes (42.5%). The MX60T model (Bausch + Lomb, Rochester, NY, USA) and SNA6AT model (Alcon Laboratories, Fort Worth, TX, USA) were used less frequently, implanted in 4 eyes (5.0%) and 3 eyes (3.8%), respectively.

Baseline Characteristics Between Groups

Each group included 40 eyes, with one eye per patient analyzed. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were comparable between the two groups. No statistically significant differences were observed in age (p = 0.361), sex (p = 0.251), or intraocular lens model implanted (p = 0.780) between eyes with planned axis flip and no-axis flip. Visual and Refractive Outcomes

Comparisons between eyes with planned axis flip and those with no-axis flip showed no significant differences in postoperative uncorrected distance visual acuity (0.17 ± 0.09 LogMAR vs 0.16 ± 0.10 LogMAR; p = 0.628), best-corrected distance visual acuity (0.12 ± 0.08 LogMAR vs 0.11 ± 0.08 LogMAR; p = 0.685), or spherical equivalent refraction (−0.02 ± 0.46 D vs −0.10 ± 0.51 D; p = 0.455).

Postoperative residual cylinder magnitude was comparable between groups (planned axis flip: 0.71 ± 0.60 D vs no-axis flip: 0.72 ± 0.46 D; mean difference −0.009 D; p = 0.945). The proportion of eyes achieving residual cylinder ≤ 0.50 D was similar between groups (51.4% vs 54.1%; p = 1.000), as was the proportion achieving residual cylinder ≤ 1.00 D (91.4% vs 89.2%).

Aberrometric Outcomes

Analysis of HOA revealed no statistically significant differences between groups in total HOA (0.22 ± 0.09 µm vs 0.22 ± 0.06 µm; p = 0.909), coma (0.07 ± 0.03 µm vs 0.08 ± 0.05 µm; p = 0.232), or spherical aberration (0.07 ± 0.15 µm vs 0.05 ± 0.03 µm; p = 0.412). However, the planned axis flip group had a higher trefoil aberration when compared with the no-axis flip group (0.16 ± 0.07 µm vs 0.09 ± 0.09 µm; mean difference −0.069 µm, 95% CI −0.105 to −0.034; p < 0.001). Although the difference in trefoil between groups reached statistical significance, the absolute values in both groups remained below 0.25 µm, the threshold above which trefoil has been associated with measurable degradation of visual acuity, and no corresponding differences were found in UDVA, BCDVA, or any other visual outcome measure.21

Multivariate linear regression analysis confirmed that planned axis flip was independently associated with higher trefoil aberration (β = 0.075, 95% CI [0.038, 0.111]; standardized β = 0.433; p = 0.0001). No significant associations were found between planned axis flip and total HOA (β = −0.003, 95% CI [−0.039, 0.032]; p = 0.851), coma (β = −0.011, 95% CI [−0.030, 0.008]; p = 0.261), spherical aberration (β = 0.009, 95% CI [−0.034, 0.052]; p = 0.668), UDVA (β = 0.008, 95% CI [−0.035, 0.050]; p = 0.721), BCDVA (β = 0.002, 95% CI [−0.037, 0.040]; p = 0.938), or spherical equivalent refraction (β = 0.113, 95% CI [−0.101, 0.326]; p = 0.298).

Subgroup Analysis by IOL Model

An exploratory subgroup analysis was performed restricted to the two most frequently implanted IOL models, which together accounted for 91.3% of the sample (ZCT, n=39; ANKORIS, n=34). Within the ZCT subgroup, axis-flip eyes exhibited significantly higher trefoil aberration compared to no-flip eyes (0.168 ± 0.093 µm vs 0.080 ± 0.043 µm; mean difference +0.088 µm; p=0.001; p_BH=0.004), consistent with the overall sample finding. No significant differences were observed in total HOA, coma, or spherical aberration within this subgroup (all p_BH >0.05). Within the ANKORIS subgroup, no significant trefoil difference was detected between correction strategies (0.139 ± 0.031 µm vs 0.097 ± 0.127 µm; p=0.190; p_BH=0.190); however, coma was significantly lower in axis-flip eyes (0.057 ± 0.026 µm vs 0.099 ± 0.052 µm; mean difference −0.042 µm; p=0.006; p_BH=0.023).

Discussion

The present study evaluated the impact on postoperative aberrometric and visual outcomes of planned axis flip during toric IOL implantation to reduce residual astigmatism. To our knowledge, this is the first study to objectively compare the whole-eye wavefront aberrometric profile between eyes with and without planned axis flip after toric IOL implantation. Our results demonstrate that planned axis flip does not affect uncorrected or best-corrected distance visual acuity, spherical equivalent refraction, or HOA, despite a statistically significant increase in trefoil aberration. These results suggest that planned axis flip does not appear to compromise aberrometric or visual outcomes when appropriately planned, supporting its consideration as a refractive strategy to minimize residual astigmatism; however, prospective studies with functional vision metrics and longer follow-up are needed to confirm its broader clinical applicability.

Studies have shown that toric IOL implantation is a successful approach to correcting preexisting corneal astigmatism during cataract surgery, leading to excellent visual outcomes and spectacle independence.22–24 Despite these advancements, the primary challenges in toric IOL use remain selecting the optimal IOL toricity and properly aligning the IOL to minimize residual astigmatism.25 In this context, when traditional alignment would produce undercorrection, alternative alignment techniques, such as planned axis flip, have been suggested to reduce residual refractive error.4,26

Previous investigations evaluating the optical consequences of flip axis in toric IOLs have suggested that this approach does not compromise visual acuity or patient-reported outcomes when appropriately selected.5,26 According to Beheregaray et al4 astigmatic overcorrection with planned axis flip can be a valid strategy for targeting minimal remaining refractive astigmatism. Similarly, Naderi et al5 Naderi describe that the axis flip of the refractive cylinder after toric IOL implantation did not significantly affect vision or patient-reported outcome measures. Our findings are consistent with these reports, as no significant differences were observed between groups in postoperative visual acuity or refractive outcomes. This supports the concept that axis inversion, when based on careful preoperative calculations, does not reduce optical performance.

From a clinical decision-making standpoint, our results support the use of axis flip when the calculator-recommended overcorrecting IOL yields a lower predicted residual astigmatism than the undercorrecting alternative, consistent with the “split-the-difference” principle adopted by several contemporary toric calculators.4 Clinicians should be aware, however, that the benefit of axis flip diminishes with increasing degrees of IOL misalignment: analytical models have demonstrated that beyond a critical misalignment threshold, the residual astigmatism of overcorrection may exceed that of undercorrection, nullifying the advantage of axis flip.6 Axis flip should therefore be favored when accurate intraoperative alignment can be ensured, for example, with digital marking systems or intraoperative aberrometry. In settings with greater risk of misalignment, such as with manual marking alone or anatomically challenging cases, a conservative undercorrection strategy may be preferable. It should be noted that these recommendations are derived from a non-randomized observational study without direct rotational stability data; definitive clinical guidance will require prospective randomized evidence incorporating postoperative cylinder axis recording, IOL misalignment angle measurement, and long-term follow-up.

An important consideration in the overall refractive strategy is the balance between residual astigmatism and spherical equivalent refraction. When an overcorrecting IOL is selected to achieve axis flip, the IOL sphere power may simultaneously introduce a hyperopic shift in the spherical equivalent, which can adversely affect patient satisfaction and uncorrected near vision even in the absence of significant residual astigmatism.4 In our study, mean spherical equivalent was comparable between groups (axis flip: −0.02 ± 0.46 D vs no-flip: −0.10 ± 0.51 D; p=0.455), suggesting that hyperopic shift was not a differential concern in this cohort; however, individual-level differences may exist that group-level comparisons cannot detect. Surgeons should verify the predicted spherical equivalent in the calculator output when selecting an overcorrecting IOL to avoid unintended hyperopia, particularly in patients with high expectations for spectacle independence.

From an aberrometric perspective, HOA are increasingly recognized as objective indicators of optical quality following cataract surgery.27–29 In the present study, total HOA, coma, and SA were comparable between groups, suggesting that axis flip does not disrupt the wavefront profile. These findings are consistent with comparative studies of different toric IOL platforms, which have reported no clinically significant differences in coma or total HOA between toric and non-toric IOLs.23,30

The higher trefoil aberration observed in the planned axis flip is important but should be interpreted cautiously. Trefoil is a third-order aberration characterized by a three-lobed pattern in the wavefront map and has been shown to have a relatively limited impact on high-contrast visual acuity compared with coma or spherical aberration.31,32 Its effect on subjective visual quality is often subtle, particularly at lower magnitudes.33 This may explain why the increase in trefoil observed in our study was not associated with measurable differences in visual acuity outcomes.

Importantly, the magnitude of trefoil aberration observed in the planned axis flip group remained within ranges previously reported after uncomplicated cataract surgery with toric IOL implantation.34 Scialdone et al30 reported trefoil values ranging from 0.08 to 0.15 µm in a comparative study of two aspheric toric IOL models, with no associated differences in visual performance. Similarly, Visser et al23 found no significant differences in trefoil between toric and non-toric IOLs and no correlation between small trefoil increases and patient-reported visual quality.

Moreover, the absence of differences in visual acuity in our study suggests that the higher trefoil may not translate into visual impairment.35,36 The threshold for clinically significant trefoil aberration has been estimated to be approximately 0.12 and 0.30 µm,37 well above the values observed in either group in our study.

The success of planned axis flip as a refractive strategy depends on accurate preoperative planning and precise intraoperative alignment. Studies have shown that even small degrees of toric IOL misalignment can significantly reduce the effective astigmatic correction.25,38 Oshika et al39 reported that the incidence of repositioning surgery for toric IOL misalignment ranges from 1.0% to 3.3%, with most cases occurring due to early postoperative rotation. Additionally, in our study, the 3-month postoperative assessment timepoint allowed for evaluation of outcomes after the period of highest risk for IOL rotation, which typically occurs within the first weeks after surgery.40,41 The comparable visual and refractive outcomes between groups suggest that rotational stability was achieved in both alignment strategies.

This study has several limitations. First, group allocation was determined by toric calculator output rather than randomization; although baseline characteristics were comparable and multivariate analysis was performed, residual confounding cannot be excluded, and a randomized controlled trial would be required to establish causality. Second, subjective quality-of-vision questionnaires and validated patient-reported outcome instruments were not included, which may have provided further insight into the perceptual relevance of the observed trefoil differences; even if below the threshold for visual acuity degradation, the trefoil difference could still be associated with subtle perceptual phenomena such as starburst or reduced mesopic performance that standard acuity metrics cannot detect. Future studies should incorporate PRO instruments to fully characterize the functional experience of patients undergoing axis flip versus standard correction strategies. Third, the study included multiple IOL models, which may have introduced heterogeneity in optical design and material properties. However, the distribution of IOL models was similar between groups, minimizing the potential for confounding. An exploratory subgroup analysis restricted to the ZCT and ANKORIS platforms did not reveal consistent aberrometric differences across correction strategies; larger, IOL-stratified studies are needed to determine whether the trefoil finding is platform-specific. Fourth, vector analysis of astigmatic correction using the Alpins method was not performed, precluding a comprehensive assessment of surgically induced astigmatism, target-induced astigmatism, and the difference vector between groups. Future studies should incorporate this methodology. Fifth, the 3-month postoperative follow-up, while sufficient to assess stable refractive and aberrometric outcomes, does not capture potential long-term changes in rotational stability or astigmatic shift, particularly in eyes with against-the-rule astigmatism, which may experience progressive ATR shift over years following toric IOL implantation.42 Long-term prospective studies are needed to evaluate the durability of the axis flip strategy. Sixth, the analysis was limited to a 4.0-mm pupil diameter, which may not fully represent visual performance under all lighting conditions. Future studies should consider analyzing aberrations at multiple pupil diameters. Seventh, the single-center design and specific patient population of a tertiary referral center limit the generalizability of these findings; external validation in multicenter prospective cohorts is warranted. Finally, the quantitative toric IOL misalignment angle was not recorded as a discrete variable, precluding a direct assessment of rotational stability; however, postoperative residual cylinder magnitude was comparable between groups (0.71 ± 0.60 D vs 0.72 ± 0.46 D; p = 0.945), suggesting comparable refractive efficacy.

Conclusion

Planned axis flip during toric IOL implantation was not associated with differences in postoperative visual acuity or refractive outcomes. Although a statistically significant increase in trefoil aberration was observed in the planned axis flip group, this finding remained below the clinically relevant threshold and did not translate into measurable differences in visual performance. These findings suggest that, when carefully planned preoperatively using a manufacturer-specific toric IOL calculator, planned axis flip does not appear to compromise visual or aberrometric outcomes and may be considered among the available strategies to minimize residual astigmatism. However, given the observational design and absence of direct rotational stability data and functional vision metrics, these conclusions should be interpreted cautiously. Prospective studies incorporating functional vision metrics and longer follow-up are needed to confirm these findings and further define the role of planned axis flip in optimizing refractive outcomes in patients undergoing cataract surgery with corneal astigmatism.
