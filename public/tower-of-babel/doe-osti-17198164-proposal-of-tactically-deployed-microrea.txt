EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Microreactors are a quickly growing technology with serious benefits, risks, and implications. They
promise to be a much-needed energy solution for some of the toughest tactical scenarios and
requirements. This technology is still in the developmental stage where engineering focus is given to
performance and reliability. However, the risks associated with this technology—both from an
operational perspective and defense perspective—necessitate additional focus on vulnerabilities.
Designing systems to proactively address vulnerabilities is more effective than attempting to
remediate them after deployment. This is no small task and requires a specialized team coming
from a wide range of disciplines to perform effectively and efficiently. The breadth of technical
engineering expertise and facilities available at Sandia National Laboratories (especially in energetics,
as is a focus of this proposal) lends itself well to this exact type of problem. The authors propose
the development of a system for tactical threat assessment and mitigation development for
microreactors to enable efficient, proactive threat mitigation.

1. PROPOSAL
As will be shown later in this proposal, microreactors (MRs) are a quickly growing technology with
serious benefits, risks, and implications. They promise to be a much-needed energy solution for
some of the toughest tactical scenarios and requirements. However, the risks associated with this
technology must be studied and mitigated to ensure the safety and security of those affected by it.
This is no small task and requires a specialized team coming from a wide range of disciplines to
perform effectively and efficiently.
Our proposal is that a system should be set up for tactical threat assessment and mitigation
development for MRs—similar to the Idaho National Laboratory (INL) Gateway for Accelerated
Innovation in Nuclear (GAIN) system. This proposed system would be facilitated by Sandia but
would work directly with external contacts, such as the Department of Defense Special Operations
Forces and various small businesses, to support development of tactically-focused MR technology.
These external contacts could also include Department of Energy Intelligence, NA-80, Defense
Intelligence Agency, Special Operations Command, and the Office of the Secretary of Defense—
among others. In this program, the technology would be analyzed for weaknesses—both from a
defensive and offensive perspective—and mitigations would be developed. Sandia would work with
external partners to assist with improving the designs as well as informing offensive teams expecting
to encounter these systems.

2. MR BACKGROUND
MRs are exactly what the name implies. It is a special category of nuclear reactor that utilizes newer,
more advanced technologies for generating nuclear power—albeit at a much smaller scale. MRs are
specifically unique in that they generally produce less than 20 MW, are highly modular, built in a
factory, and often delivered in a nearly ready-to-use state. By being built in a factory-style setting
and delivered to the intended use site, their physical size and modularity can quickly decrease site
construction costs and shorten setup timelines. By mass producing these reactors, overall costs can
decrease, thus increasing cost efficiencies of individual units which can be installed in multi-unit
configurations to fit needs. Additionally, due to the compact modular nature of these MRs, they can
also be mobile, thereby widening the range of possible use cases. Historically, nuclear reactors have
been large, fixed facilities that require massive amounts of resources and time to build and a
significant workforce to maintain and oversee operations and security—contradictory to MRs.

2.1. Commercial MRs
In the commercial world, MRs shine by promising to dramatically reduce construction and security
costs due to their modularity and factory construction. In this category, a major focus of the
manufacturer is to make the MR, in our own words, “walk away safe”. Undoubtedly, any nuclear
reactor has significant hazard and risk associated with it. Damage to the environment and personnel
are clearly unacceptable consequences, so designing the MR to fail in a safe method is critical.
However, because commercially-focused MRs tend to be designed for favorable environments that
are often already securely guarded sites and/or on friendly soil, limited effort is generally focused on
making the MR “walk away secure,” which is the focus of this discussion and the reasoning behind
this proposal.

2.2. Tactical MRs
A tactically deployed MR—as defined in this proposal—is a small, portable reactor that is used as
the primary or auxiliary power source for temporary, or short-term military or intelligence
applications in both domestic and foreign deployments. The fundamental characteristics of MRs
make them extremely attractive for tactical deployment in both remote and austere environments.
Because they are generally pre-fueled and expected to operate off that fuel source for multiple years,
MRs do not require the frequent refueling and the rigorous maintenance associated with the
currently used fossil fuel generators. This significantly reduces the manpower and fueling logistics—
and therefore, collateral risk—associated with producing power in tactical scenarios. Instead of
focusing attention on transporting fuel and manpower to the power generation site, the focus can be
redirected to other critical issues, such as protecting the perimeter and avoiding detection by
adversaries. Specifically, this aspect of MRs lends itself to forward operating locations, strategically
hardened deeply buried targets (SHDBT), and deployment in space. The key point is that any assets
(and associated support) that can be emplaced by helicopter can be powered by this technology
without the need for detectable infrastructure (roads, power lines, communications, etc.). However,
due to the complex nature of tactical MRs and the intended operating locations and requirements,
they must be designed as both “walk away safe” and “walk away secure.” Not only should the design
ensure limited damage to the environment and human population, but it must also be highly
secure—limiting manipulation/exploitation by adversaries in both standard operation and in the
event of attack or abandonment in place.

2.3. Major MR players
Effective development and application of MRs requires a multitude of capabilities, experience, and
knowledge. The manufacturers produce and build the designs with the end users in mind, generally
with R&D and testing assistance from external sources.

2.3.1. Manufacturers
In the world of MRs in the United States, there are currently several large names, and quite a few
smaller names. Some common players are Westinghouse Electric Company, Oklo, BWX
Technologies, and Antares Nuclear. Overall, there are currently around 10 US companies dedicating
resources to MR designs, with this number growing rapidly. These companies are split between
commercial and defense deployment.

2.3.2. R&D assistance
While the majority of the design and fabrication of these MRs is done in-house, there are certain
areas that benefit greatly from the involvement of outside involvement. These external networks
often have more robust test facilities, fabrication capabilities, and insider knowledge (in the case
specific to military application) that the manufacturers generally don’t possess. Some of the most
significant R&D providers include INL and Sandia National Laboratories (SNL). When not directly
funded by the manufacturer, funding for this work can travel via many channels, including
Department of Energy, Department of Defense, and the INL GAIN voucher system, which is
specifically aimed at commercial nuclear reactor work.

2.3.3.     End users
The most basic end user of an MR design is an entity with a need for power generation. This can
include cities, businesses, hospitals, and perhaps—at some point in the near future—homes.

Remote communities, mining operations, and disaster/emergency response could also benefit from
this technology. However, during this early developmental stage of MRs, larger well-funded entities
are prevalent. Commercial end users of most interest at the moment are technology corporations
looking to power vast data centers. All aspects of the military are also potential end users of MRs—
not just those in risky environments. Having robust backup power is extremely beneficial to all,
even on friendly soil.

2.4. Current status of MR technology
MRs—both commercial and tactical—are a quickly developing technology. As discussed previously,
a vast number of companies are currently developing MR concepts with very few in the construction
stage. Because MR development is generally in the early stages, important features can be integrated
into designs rather than modifying designs post-production in the vein of threat protection.
Of specific interest to the authors of this paper is protection methodologies for all MRs, but
especially from the tactical/operational perspective. Extensive research within the confines of
Sandia (discussions with more than 30 subject matter experts) and a few critical external experts has
shown that MR energetic threat protection efforts are frighteningly limited in both commercial and
tactical MRs. Companies occasionally give thought to such protection but are generally much more
concerned with the operation and function of their MR technology—and rightly so. Unfortunately,
this leaves a gaping hole in the technology for adversaries to take advantage of MRs and wreak
havoc.

3. SANDIA’S ENERGETICS CAPABILITIES
With more than 75 years of “exceptional service in the national interest,” SNL has an extreme
breadth of knowledge, expertise, and capabilities that make it an effective leader in the world of
technological research and development. Not only can new technology be conceptualized at Sandia,
but it can also be built, tested, and recorded by experts from a wide range of specialized fields.
Because of this wide range of capabilities all co-located in one cohesive organization, Sandia, in
simple terms, is a systems engineering laboratory perfectly suited for MR testing and evaluation.
Specifically, Sandia is a prime answer to the need for a MR tactical threat consultant from an
energetic/ballistics perspective—a resource that appears to be sorely lacking and deeply needed.
Sandia has a long history of developing energetics solutions to one-off problems, as well as
developing mitigation strategies for defensive posturing. Facility-wise, there are several explosive
research and development and test and evaluation organizations in which concepts can be evaluated,
including ballistics facilities capable of rounds as large as 50 caliber and 42mm and open detonation
ranges capable of over 100lb net explosive weight. These facilities are operated by a multitude of
energetics SMEs, including operators, engineers, and hydrocode modelers.
The Explosives Systems & Technologies Group (ESTG) at SNL has supported the nation in
systems engineering applied to energetics analysis, research and development, and test and
evaluation. This group is comprised of four remote sites on Kirtland Air Force Base with
specializations in: radiological/nuclear response (Site 9920); advanced warfighter tools and
techniques development (Thunder Range); SOCOM hands-on response and team training (site
9940); and high fidelity test and evaluation (Explosives Applications Lab, site 9930).
ESTG has a long history of solving one-of-a-kind problems associated with energetics. This group
has led, or participated in, many critical accident investigations including: the USS Iowa 16-inch gun
accident (providing an alternate source to the US Navy stance of insider threat); the Fukushima

reactor incident; the Oklahoma City Bombing of the Murrah Building (determining source term
size); the TWA flight 800 incident (concluding it was a true accidental explosion); the DeBruce grain
elevator explosion (at the time the largest grain silo in the country); the M98 distraction device
production accident; the Umatilla Rocket Motor fires (during incineration of nerve-agent-filled M55
Rockets), and many, many others.
This group responded to a call from DHS in 2006 on the plot to destroy a transatlantic airliner mid-
flight with liquid explosives. The quick turn-around analysis and testing laid the groundwork for the
TSA 3-1-1 rule. Engineers from the Explosives Applications Lab continued as the energetics subject
matter experts and experimenters for a five-year interagency effort to determine threat quantity
levels for commercial aircraft for all potential improvised (and commercially available) energetics
mixtures.
Several of the ETG labs have led exploitation efforts on foreign warheads and warhead energetics
subsystems, leading back to Soviet-era air-to-surface and surface-to-air warheads. In another
specific example, the Explosives Applications Lab led a collaborative effort to design an energetics
package to be placed in such a location as to affect a foreign adversary. This lab also developed a
rod-fragment warhead for potential replacement of the AMRAA warhead, concluding in multiple
Mach-5 sled experiments.
Recent work in ESTG has centered on ballistics research, directed energy for attack and
disablement, and advanced manufacturing techniques for shaped charge design and analysis. Much
work is also being conducted on novel drone usage for attack and disablement, leveraging Sandia’s
expertise in various DOE technologies. The Explosives Applications Lab team is the design entity
for the Explosives Destruction System for recovered Chemical Weapons Destruction – this program
has existed between Sandia and the US Army, Recovered Chemical Materiel Directorate for over 25
years, and represents the only transportable chemical weapons destruction system in the country.
And finally, with respect to the main interest of this proposal, representative cross sections of a
commercial MR were tested for ballistic and energetic vulnerabilities and provided much-needed
feedback to the system designers.
Clearly, the Explosives Systems & Technologies Group has a depth and breadth of energetics
knowledge and experience like no other in the nation. With world class test and evaluation facilities
and world class energetics experts, coupled with modeling and simulation expertise and a long
history of response and attack, team-based, hands-on training programs, it is capable of addressing
complex, rapid-response issues related to national security.

4. CONCLUSION
In conclusion, MRs are a new technology with certain benefits in both commercial and
defense/operational arenas. Especially in terms of defense scenarios, these MRs promise to be a
much-needed energy solution. Limited effort is being spent designing these systems to reduce
vulnerabilities to outside threats. The authors see a serious need for a system for tactical threat
assessment and mitigation development for MRs. With the wide range of capabilities and experience
in a multitude of disciplines, SNL is well-suited to contribute as part of this system.
