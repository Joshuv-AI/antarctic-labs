α \alpha -Fair Insurance Pricing: A Fairness Continuum
Tianhe Zhang, Xiguang Liu, Peng Shi

Abstract
Fairness in insurance pricing remains a long-standing and deeply debated puzzle. On one hand, insurers, driven by profitability considerations, set premiums that differentiate across individual risks to achieve actuarial fairness. On the other hand, insurance serves a critical societal function by pooling risks across a population, motivating cross-subsidization among groups to promote solidarity fairness. The tension between these two competing notions of fairness makes insurance pricing inherently complex, particularly in modern settings where granular data allow for increasingly fine risk differentiation and regulators face growing pressure to protect vulnerable groups. To address this challenge, we propose an α \alpha - F air I ndividual S olvent P remium ( α \alpha -FISP) framework for insurance pricing that explicitly captures the trade-off between actuarial and solidarity fairness while guaranteeing solvency, a fundamental requirement in insurance operations. We formulate the pricing problem as a constrained optimization task, where actuarially fair premiums are adjusted subject to budget constraints on cross-subsidization within each risk class. This formulation naturally yields a family of solutions parameterized by α \alpha , tracing a continuum between purely actuarial and purely solidarity-based pricing and enabling decision-makers to select an operating point along this fairness spectrum. We derive theoretical guarantees for the proposed framework. Numerical experiments show that α \alpha -FISP is computationally tractable and aligns well with the U.S. regulatory regimes featuring heterogeneous state-level fairness requirements.



1
Introduction

Fairness has become a central theme in the era of data-driven decision-making, particularly as machine learning (ML) algorithms are increasingly deployed in high-stakes domains such as credit scoring, criminal justice, and healthcare. In this paper, we focus on fairness in insurance pricing, a setting where algorithmic decisions have widespread financial and societal impact. The insurance industry is inherently data-driven, relying heavily on statistical and predictive models to derive insights from data and set premiums
(
17
)
. With the rapid growth of InsurTech and the availability of granular data, algorithmic pricing plays a central role in modern insurance operations
(
33
)
. Given the insurance market’s size and economic criticality, ensuring fair pricing is a practical and societal imperative.

Insurance operates in a profit-driven environment while simultaneously providing financial protection as a societal good. This dual role makes fairness in insurance pricing a long-standing and deeply debated puzzle. On one hand, insurers—driven by profitability, competition, and solvency considerations—set premiums that differentiate across individual risks to achieve actuarial fairness. A premium is actuarially fair if it equals or closely reflects the insured’s expected loss
(
21
)
. Traditionally, insurers have implemented actuarial fairness through risk classification, grouping individuals with similar attributes into homogeneous risk classes. Refining these classes improves alignment between premiums and underlying risk, which helps mitigate adverse selection and encourages loss-control behavior by making policyholders financially accountable for their risk exposure
(
34
;
32
)
. On the other hand, insurance fulfills a critical social function by pooling risks across the population, which motivates cross-subsidization between groups to promote solidarity fairness. A solidarity-oriented view of fairness, often grounded in principles of social justice
(
23
)
, emphasizes equality in sharing the burden of losses. Under this view, fairness may require some policyholders to pay more than their pure expected loss—or others to pay less—to maintain access and affordability for those at higher risk through no fault of their own.

The tension between these two competing notions of fairness—solidarity and actuarial—makes insurance pricing inherently complex. This debate is often conceptualized along a continuum, with a pure “moral” or solidarity-driven perspective at one end and a pure “technical” or actuarially driven perspective at the other
(
35
)
. While these notions are incompatible when taken to their extremes, empirical studies suggest that public preferences tend to occupy a nuanced middle ground, accepting some degree of cross-subsidization as long as pricing remains broadly risk-reflective
(
11
)
. This balancing act has become even more challenging in modern settings. Advances in data availability and predictive modeling have enabled insurers to differentiate risks with unprecedented granularity. While such refinement improves actuarial precision, it can also magnify premium differences between groups, raising concerns about affordability and social exclusion. In addition, because many rating variables are correlated with sensitive attributes such as gender, race, or socioeconomic status, data-driven pricing can unintentionally reproduce or reinforce existing inequities
(
16
)
. Consequently, regulators face growing pressure to protect vulnerable populations and restrict the extent of permissible risk differentiation. Recent regulatory interventions highlight this trend, including the EU Gender Directive (2004/113/EC), which prohibits the use of gender in life insurance pricing
(
10
)
, and Colorado’s SB 21-169 statute, which bars unfair discrimination across multiple protected characteristics
(
7
)
. Taken together, the tension between market-driven risk differentiation and regulation-mandated solidarity highlights the need for frameworks that balance these objectives, creating pricing systems that are both economically viable and socially acceptable.

Motivated by these observations, we introduce a novel insurance pricing framework, termed
\alpha
-fairness, to explicitly capture the trade-off between actuarial and solidarity fairness. This framework yields premiums parameterized by
\alpha\in[0,1]
, defining a continuum from purely actuarial to purely solidarity-based pricing and enabling decision-makers to select an operating point aligned with their strategic or regulatory priorities. First, we formally define the notion of
\alpha
-fairness. A premium structure is said to be
\alpha
-fair if, within any group of policyholders sharing the same non-sensitive attributes (a risk class), the ratio of the lowest to the highest premium is at least
\alpha
. At the extremes,
\alpha=0
corresponds to fully differentiated premiums based on sensitive attributes (pure actuarial fairness), while
\alpha=1
enforces uniform premiums within the group (pure solidarity fairness). Second, we formulate the computation of
\alpha
-fair premiums as a constrained optimization problem that adjusts actuarially fair premiums subject to budget constraints controlling the degree of cross-subsidization within each risk class. The resulting framework is computationally tractable, statistically well-grounded, and robust. Third, the proposed
\alpha
-fair premiums satisfy individual-level solvency requirements, a critical property for insurance pricing that is often overlooked in existing fair-pricing studies. More broadly, the framework is flexible and accommodates practical considerations such as portfolio-level profit targets and regulatory caps on rate changes.

We emphasize that, while our definition of
\alpha
-fairness is framed in terms of sensitive and non-sensitive attributes—reflecting one of its most pressing applications—the framework is broadly applicable. In this broader view, non-sensitive attributes correspond to rating variables used to define homogeneous risk classes, whereas sensitive attributes refer to variables for which regulators restrict or prohibit risk-based differentiation. These may include direct measures of socioeconomic status (e.g., gender, race) or proxy variables correlated with such characteristics.

Fairness in Insurance Pricing

The discussion of fairness in insurance pricing has a long history in actuarial science, well before the emergence of algorithmic fairness in machine learning. Classical insurance theory distinguishes between actuarial fairness and solidarity fairness (or social fairness)

. Much of the early actuarial literature treated this as a normative tension rather than a quantitative trade-off, with fairness operationalized through the design of rating structures that balance heterogeneity and pooling
(

Recent research in actuarial science has revived this debate in light of advances in data availability and machine learning. A growing body of work has sought to formalize fairness criteria and clarify how regulatory objectives translate into modeling constraints

14

9

A complementary line of research focuses on computing discrimination-free premiums. For example,

In contrast, our work adopts a broader and more structural view of fairness in insurance pricing. Rather than constraining the predictive model directly, we model fairness as an explicit allocation mechanism that governs how actuarially fair premiums are adjusted under cross-subsidization constraints. This perspective aligns more closely with the long-standing actuarial debate between solidarity and actuarial fairness, while introducing a formal and tunable parameter,
\alpha

\alpha
-fairness framework thus complements existing literature by connecting traditional actuarial fairness principles with mordern fairness optimization, providing both a normative interpretation and a practical computational procedure for balancing social and actuarial objectives.

2.3
Our Contribution

This paper advances the literature on fairness in both machine learning and insurance pricing in several important ways. First, beyond existing fairness notions and frameworks, we introduce a new paradigm, referred to as
\alpha
-fairness framework, that formalizes a continuum between actuarial and solidarity fairness. Unlike conventional fairness metrics that focus on prediction errors or demographic parity, the
\alpha
-fairness directly regulates premium differentials within risk classes, reflecting the inherently economic nature of fairness in insurance. Second, we formulate the computation of
\alpha
-fair premiums as a constrained optimization problem that adjusts actuarially fair premiums subject to group-specific cross-subsidization constraints. This formulation further guarantees individual-level solvency, a critical property that existing fairness-based pricing approaches often struggle to achieve. The resulting
\alpha
-Fair Individual Solvent Premium (
\alpha
-FISP) is supported by rigorous theoretical guarantees, including existence, boundedness, sample complexity preservation, and order preservation, providing a solid foundation for practical implementation. Third, the proposed framework yields a computationally tractable premium (
\alpha
-FISP) and is regulatorily adaptable: varying state-level fairness requirements can be achieved by choosing the interpretable fairness budget parameter
\alpha
, enabling a unified pricing pipeline that aligns with the state-governed structure of U.S. insurance regulation. Simulation studies and empirical case analyses demonstrate that
\alpha
-fair pricing aligns naturally with regulatory objectives. Finally, our work conceptually bridges machine learning and actuarial perspectives on fairness. By framing fairness as an allocation problem rather than solely a predictive constraint, the
\alpha
-fairness framework integrates ethical and regulatory considerations directly into insurance pricing, yielding a unified, interpretable, and practically implementable framework.

Problem Formulation

3.1
Preliminaries

Let
(X,D,Y)
be a random triplet with unknown joint distribution
\mathbb{P}_{X,D,Y}
. We observe
n
samples:

(x_{i},d_{i},y_{i})\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathbb{P}_{X,D,Y},\quad i=1,\ldots,n.

In the insurance pricing context,
x_{i}\in\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p}

denotes a
p
-dimensional vector of non-sensitive rating variables used for
risk classification,
d_{i}\in\mathcal{D}
denotes sensitive attributes, and

y_{i}\in\mathcal{Y}
represents the realized loss (or
risk outcome) of a policyholder. We assume that
Y
is a non-negative random variable with finite second moment, i.e.,

\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}]<\infty
, and that
D
takes values in a finite discrete set, i.e.,
|\mathcal{D}|<\infty
.

Definition 3.1

(Actuarial Fair)
.

The actuarial fair premium for
Y
w.r.t.
(X,D)
is defined as:

\mu(X,D):=\mathbb{E}[Y|X,D].

This premium fully reflects differences in expected losses attributable to both non-sensitive and sensitive attributes. For notational convenience, we denote
\mu(X,D)
by
\mu_{D}(X)
throughout the paper.

Definition 3.2

(Solidarity Fair)
.

The solidarity fair premium for
Y
w.r.t.
X
is defined as:

\mu(X):=\mathbb{E}[Y|X].

Under this pricing rule, individuals sharing the same non-sensitive attributes are charged a common premium, thereby pooling risks across sensitive attributes.

Definition 3.3

(Individual Fairness)
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{F}
be a pricing function
f:\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
defined on an input space
\mathcal{Z}
. Equip
\mathcal{Z}
with a metric
d_{\mathcal{Z}}
and the output space with metric
d_{\mathbb{R}}
. For
\epsilon\geq 0
and
\delta\geq 0
given,
f
is
individually fair
if for any
z,z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}
satisfying
d_{\mathcal{Z}}(z,z^{\prime})\leq\epsilon
, we have
d_{\mathbb{R}}(f(z),f(z^{\prime}))\leq\delta
.

This principle requires that individuals who are similar according to the chosen input-space metric receive similar outcomes. The thresholds
(\epsilon,\delta)
allow relaxation of strict fairness requirements when exact parity is not feasible.

3.2
\alpha
-Fairness and Individual Solvency

For fair insurance pricing, we introduce two purpose-built distance functions.

Definition 3.4

(Metric on Feature Space)
.

Let
dist_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{D}}:(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{D})\times(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{D})\to[0,\infty]

be defined for
(x,d),(x^{\prime},d^{\prime})\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{D}
as

dist_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{D}}\bigl((x,d),(x^{\prime},d^{\prime})\bigr)=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if }x=x^{\prime},\\
+\infty,&\text{if }x\neq x^{\prime}.\end{cases}

This extended pseudometric is characterized exclusively by non-sensitive attributes. It treats policyholders with identical non-sensitive attributes (
x=x^{\prime}
) as maximally similar regardless of sensitive attributes, while deeming those with differing attributes (
x\neq x^{\prime}
) incomparable.

Definition 3.5

(Metric on Outcome Space)
.

For strictly positive premiums
y,y^{\prime}>0
assigned by a pricing function
f
, define

dist_{\mathcal{Y}}(y,y^{\prime}):=1-\min\left(\frac{y}{y^{\prime}},\frac{y^{\prime}}{y}\right).

This scale-invariant metric satisfies all metric axioms for
y,y^{\prime}>0
. It quantifies proportional deviation between premiums, where
dist_{\mathcal{Y}}(y,y^{\prime})=0
if and only if
y=y^{\prime}
(exact/strict fairness constraint), and approaches
1
as the ratio between premiums becomes unbounded (no fairness constraint).

With the distance functions defined on the feature space and outcome space, we can now formalize a proportional notion of individual fairness for insurance premiums.

Definition 3.6

(
\alpha
-Fairness)
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{F}
be a pricing function
f:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
, and let

dist_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{D}}
and
dist_{\mathcal{Y}}
be the feature-space and outcome-space distance functions defined in Definitions
3.4
and
3.5
, respectively. Given thresholds
\epsilon\geq 0
and
\alpha\in[0,1]
,
f
is said to be
\alpha
-fair
if, for any pair of individuals
(x,d),(x^{\prime},d^{\prime})\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{D}
satisfying
dist_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{D}}\bigl((x,d),(x^{\prime},d^{\prime})\bigr)\leq\epsilon
, we have
dist_{\mathcal{Y}}\bigl(f(x,d),f(x^{\prime},d^{\prime})\bigr)\leq 1-\alpha
.

When
x=x^{\prime}
,
\alpha
-fairness requires
\min\left(\frac{f(x,d)}{f(x,d^{\prime})},\frac{f(x,d^{\prime})}{f(x,d)}\right)\geq\alpha,

ensuring that individuals with identical non-sensitive attributes receive proportionally similar premiums, with
\alpha
controlling the allowable deviation. When
x\neq x^{\prime}
, no fairness constraint is imposed, allowing full use of non-sensitive attributes for risk classification. The scale-invariant design of
dist_{\mathcal{Y}}
ensures this proportional constraint holds across all premium levels.

For notational clarity, let
\{d_{1},\ldots,d_{|\mathcal{D}|}\}
denote the support of
D
, and let
p_{k|x}:=\mathbb{P}(D=d_{k}|X=x)
.

Definition 3.7

(Individual Solvency)
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{F}
be a pricing function
f:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
. The function
f
is
individually solvent
if, for every fixed
x\in\mathcal{X}
,

\sum_{k=1}^{|\mathcal{D}|}\left(f(x,d_{k})-\mu_{k}(x)\right)p_{k|x}\geq 0,

where
\mu_{k}(x):=\mathbb{E}[Y|X=x,D=d_{k}]
denotes the actuarially fair premium.

Individual solvency enforces self-sufficiency by requiring premiums for identical non-sensitive attributes to cover their group’s expected loss. This precludes cross-subsidization across heterogeneous groups (
x\neq x^{\prime}
), confining financial liability to actuarially comparable pools.

3.3
Constrained Optimization

Our objective is to compute insurance premiums that satisfy both
\alpha
-fairness and individual solvency. We formulate this task as a constrained risk minimization problem. Specifically, for a pricing function
f:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
, we seek to minimize mean squared error for
Y
:

\min_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\;\mathbb{E}_{(X,D,Y)\sim\mathbb{P}_{X,D,Y}}\bigl[(f(X,D)-Y)^{2}\bigr],

(1)

subject to the constraints:

\displaystyle 1-dist_{\mathcal{Y}}\left(f(x,d_{k}),f(x,d_{k^{\prime}})\right)

\displaystyle\geq\alpha,\ \ \ \forall x\in\mathcal{X},\alpha\in(0,1],~k,k^{\prime}\in[|\mathcal{D}|].

(2)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{|\mathcal{D}|}\left(f(x,d_{k})-\mu_{k}(x)\right)\cdot p_{k|x}

\displaystyle\geq 0,\ \ \ \forall x\in\mathcal{X}.

(3)

The solution to this optimization problem is denoted the
\alpha
-Fair Individual Solvent Premium
(
\alpha
-FISP). To simplify, we express the nonlinear constraint (
2
) in the following equivalent linear inequalities:

\displaystyle f(x,d_{k})-\alpha\,f(x,d_{k^{\prime}})

\displaystyle\geq 0,

(4)

\displaystyle f(x,d_{k^{\prime}})-\alpha\,f(x,d_{k})

\displaystyle\geq 0,

(5)

for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
and all pairs
k<k^{\prime}
.

Taken together, individual solvency and
\alpha
-fairness yield a two-tier equity framework.
Individual solvency anchors premiums to legitimate risk differentiation based on non-sensitive
attributes, ensuring inter-group equity. Conditional on risk class,
\alpha
-fairness governs
intra-group treatment across sensitive attributes, enforcing proportional fairness. This
hierarchical separation resolves the tension between actuarial fairness and societal fairness
by decoupling risk classification from fairness enforcement.

We begin by establishing the existence and uniqueness of the optimal solution. This result is fundamental, as it guarantees that the proposed fairness and solvency requirements lead to a well-defined pricing rule rather than an ill-posed or degenerate optimization problem. We then argue that the problem is well-posed from an insurance operations perspective. In particular, we show that imposing

We also derive the out-of-sample constraint violation rate for completeness. Finally, we establish that for any fixed

, the probability of an out-of-sample solvency violation larger than
\tau
decays at rate
\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2})
.

We have established that the proposed constrained optimization problem is well-posed (Theorem
4.5
) and admits a consistent
\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2})
constraint violation rate (
4.7
). We now study the structural properties of
\alpha
-FISP that align naturally with insurance operations and economic intuition.

Proposition 4.8

(Boundedness of
\alpha
-FISP)
.

Let
f^{*}\in\mathcal{F}
be the optimal solution to the constrained optimization problem ((
1
), (
3
), (
4
), and (
5
)) for any fixed
\alpha\in(0,1]
. If there exists
\mu_{\min}>0
s.t.
\mu_{k}(x)\geq\mu_{\min}
and
p_{k|x}>0
for
\mathbb{P}_{X}
-a.e.
x\in\mathcal{X}
and all
k\in[|\mathcal{D}|]
.
Then, for
\mathbb{P}_{X}
-a.e.
x
, we have

\min_{k}\mu_{k}(x)\leq f^{*}(x,\cdot)\leq\max_{k}\mu_{k}(x).

Remark 4.9
.

Proposition
4.8
ensures that
\alpha
-FISP lies within the convex hull of the actuarially fair premiums for each risk class
x
. This property is critical both operationally and economically. Operationally, it guarantees that fairness and solvency constraints do not induce extreme pricing behavior: no premium is pushed below the minimum expected loss or inflated beyond the highest actuarially fair benchmark within the same risk class. Such bounds are consistent with underwriting practices and regulatory expectations, which prohibit loss-leading or excessively punitive pricing. Economically, the result shows that fairness-induced adjustments operate as a reallocation of premiums within an actuarially meaningful range, rather than as a distortion that breaks the link between price and risk. This property also ensures numerical stability of the optimization problem and precludes pathological solutions that could otherwise arise under
\alpha
-fairness constraints.

Proposition 4.10

(Order Preservation)
.

Let
f^{*}\in\mathcal{F}
be the optimal solution to the constrained optimization problem ((
1
), (
3
), (
4
), and (
5
)). Suppose there exists
\mu_{\min}>0
s.t.
\mu_{k}(x)\geq\mu_{\min}
and
p_{k|x}>0
for
\mathbb{P}_{X}
-a.e.
x\in\mathcal{X}
and all
k\in[|\mathcal{D}|]
.
Then, for
\mathbb{P}_{X}
-a.e.
x
, the following hold:

(i) If
\mu_{k}(x)\geq\mu(x)
, then
f^{*}(x,d_{k})\geq\mu(x),\quad\forall\alpha\in(0,1]
.

(ii) Suppose there exists a partition of
\mathcal{D}
into a low-risk cluster
C_{L}

and a high-risk cluster
C_{H}=\mathcal{D}\setminus C_{L}
, such that for some

M(\alpha,x)>0
,

\begin{cases}f^{*}(x,d_{k})=\alpha M(\alpha,x),&\forall k\in[|C_{L}|]\\
f^{*}(x,d_{k})=M(\alpha,x),&\forall k\in[|C_{H}|].\end{cases}

If
\sum_{k\in[|C_{L}|]}p_{k|x}\leq\frac{1}{1+\alpha}
, then for every
k\in[|C_{L}|]
,
f^{*}(x,d_{k})\leq\mu(x),\quad\forall\alpha\in(0,1].

(iii) For any
k,j\in[|\mathcal{D}|]
and any fixed
\alpha\in(0,1]
,

\mu_{k}(x)\geq\mu_{j}(x)\quad\implies\quad f^{*}(x,d_{k})\geq f^{*}(x,d_{j}).

Remark 4.11
.

Part (i) shows that if a risk class
(x,d_{k})
is priced above the solidarity fair premium under actuarial fairness (
\alpha=0
), then for any fairness budget
\alpha\in(0,1]
, the
\alpha
-FISP remains lower bounded by
\mu(x)
. Part (ii) provides a complementary upper-bound result under a structural assumption on the optimal solution and a sufficient condition on the mass of low-risk groups. This condition is economically intuitive: when a low-risk class has dominant probability mass, the solidarity premium
\mu(x)
approaches its actuarial benchmark, causing the feasible
\alpha
-fair premium band to shift upward in order to satisfy individual solvency while minimizing prediction error. An illustrative example is provided in Appendix
A.1
. While the structural assumption in part (ii) may not always hold in practice,
part (iii) guarantees a robust form of fairness: the relative ordering of premiums across sensitive attributes within a risk class is preserved under
\alpha
-FISP. Thus, actuarially riskier groups are never priced below less risky ones, ensuring monotonicity and interpretability of the resulting premium structure.

6
Conclusion

This paper introduces
\alpha
-fair insurance pricing, a principled framework that formalizes fairness in insurance as a continuum between actuarial and solidarity perspectives. By directly regulating premium dispersion within risk classes,
\alpha
-fairness captures the economic substance of fairness in pricing decisions, rather than relying on indirect predictive or demographic criteria. We formulate pricing as a constrained optimization problem, yielding the
\alpha
-FISP as a flexible and tractable solution. We establish theoretical guarantees and show through numerical experiments that the framework is computationally efficient and compatible with heterogeneous regulatory environments. More broadly, this work reframes fairness in insurance pricing as an allocation problem, offering a transparent and interpretable mechanism for navigating regulatory and ethical trade-offs.

Appendix A: Deferred Examples

A.1
Proposition
4.10
(ii) Example

We present a simple numeric example in this section. Consider a simplified setting with a single risk class (ignoring non-sensitive risk class
x
for brevity) and a binary sensitive attribute
D\in\{L,H\}
, representing Low-risk and High-risk groups. Assume actuarial fair premiums,
\mu_{L}=10
and
\mu_{H}=100
for each class, and a fairness budget
\alpha=0.5
. According to Proposition
4.10
(ii), we calculate the sufficient cutoff
p_{L}\leq\frac{1}{1+\alpha}=\frac{1}{1+0.5}
, which is approximately 0.67. We compare two scenarios varying the population mix
p_{L}=\mathbb{P}(D=L)
.

Case 1: Condition Satisfied (
p_{L}=0.5
)
. We assume low-risk mass equals to 0.5, which is below the threshold 0.67. Solidarity premium,
\mu
, is calculated through a weighted average:
0.5\cdot 10+(1-0.5)\cdot 100=55
. Recall optimal solution is defined as the minimizer of weighted loss function
L=p_{L}\cdot(f_{L}-\mu_{L})^{2}+p_{H}\cdot(f_{H}-\mu_{H})^{2}
. Under
\alpha
-FISP constraint, we have
f_{L}=\alpha\cdot f_{H}
. Then, re-formulate loss function to
L(f_{H})=p_{L}\cdot(\alpha\cdot f_{H}-\mu_{L})^{2}+p_{H}\cdot(f_{H}-\mu_{H})^{2}
, and take derivative respect to
f_{H}
, we have
f_{H}^{*}=\cfrac{\alpha p_{L}\mu_{L}+p_{H}\mu_{H}}{\alpha^{2}p_{L}+p_{H}}
. Plug-in numerical values, we have
f_{H}^{*}=84
, and
f_{L}^{*}=42
. We can see solvency constraint is naturally satisfied as
0.5\cdot 84+0.5\cdot 42=63\geq 55
. We also observe that
f_{L}^{*}\leq\mu
. The result is intuitive: the fair premium for the low-risk group is subsidized but remains below the population average.

Case 2: Condition Violated (
p_{L}=0.9
)
. Now the low-risk group dominates the population (
0.9>0.67
). Use similar formulation as in previous case,
\mu
equals to 19;
f_{H}^{*}
equals to approximately 44.62;
f_{L}^{*}
equals to approximately 22.31. Individual solvency is satisfied (
0.9\cdot 22.31+0.1\cdot 44.62\approx 24.54\geq 19
), but notice that
f_{L}^{*}>\mu
.
f_{L}^{*}<f_{H}^{*}
confirms result Proposition
4.10
(iii), but low-risk mass domination, under
\alpha
-fair constraint, leads to price paid by low-risk individuals exceed the solidarity benchmark.

Through this illustrative example, we demonstrate the practical implication of Proposition
4.10
. When the low-risk mass condition is satisfied, the
\alpha
-FISP framework performs ideally, yielding a premium structure that naturally interpolates between actuarial and solidarity fairness. When the low-risk mass condition is violated, rigid adherence to a proportional fairness in skewed populaitons, especially when ensure myopic fair forcing low-risk premium equivalent to high-risk premium, may unintentionally over-penalize low-risk individuals, creating a scenario where the "fair" price is paradoxically more expensive than a fully solidified price. Our
\alpha
-FISP flexibility prevents the over-penalization of low-risk individuals that often arises from binary notion of fairness.

Appendix C: Deferred Proofs

C.1
Proposition
4.1
Proof

Proof.

We first show that the feasible set is non-empty. For
x\in\mathcal{X}
, define the candidate function:

f_{0}(x,d_{k})=\mu(x),\ k=1,\ldots,|\mathcal{D}|.

Since
\mu(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{|\mathcal{D}|}\mu_{k}(x)\cdot p_{k|x}
. Therefore,
f_{0}
trivially satisfies IS and
\alpha
-fairness constraints. Thus,
f_{0}
is feasible. Moreover, we know
f_{0}\in\mathcal{F}
, since

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[(f_{0}(X,D)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left[\mu(X)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left[\left({\mathbb{E}}\left[Y\mid X\right]\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\mathbb{E}}\left[Y^{2}\mid X\right]\right]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left[Y^{2}\right]

\displaystyle<+\infty.

Therefore, we have
f_{0}\in\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,IS}
and
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,IS}\neq\emptyset
.

Next, we show that the feasible set
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,IS}
is convex. Let
f,g\in\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,IS}
and
t\in[0,1]
. Define
h:=tf+(1-t)g
, then we have for
a.e.

x
:

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{|\mathcal{D}|}\left(h(x,d_{k})-\mu_{k}(x)\right)\cdot p_{k|x}

\displaystyle=t\sum_{k=1}^{|\mathcal{D}|}\left(f(x,d_{k})-\mu_{k}(x)\right)\cdot p_{k|x}+(1-t)\sum_{k=1}^{|\mathcal{D}|}\left(g(x,d_{k})-\mu_{k}(x)\right)

\displaystyle\geq 0.

Also, for any
k<k^{\prime}
and
a.e.

x
, we have:

\displaystyle h(x,d_{k})-\alpha h(x,d_{k^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=t\left(f(x,d_{k})-\alpha f(x,d_{k^{\prime}})\right)+(1-t)\left(g(x,d_{k})-\alpha g(x,d_{k^{\prime}})\right)

\displaystyle\geq 0.

Therefore,
h\in\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,IS}
meaning
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,IS}
is convex.

Then, we show that
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,IS}
is closed in
L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X,D})
.

Let
\{f_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}\subset\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,IS}
s.t.
f_{n}\to f
in
\mathcal{F}=L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X,D})
. We know that
L^{P}
convergence implies convergence in probability. Thus, we can find a subsequence
\{f_{n_{j}}\}_{j\geq 1}
s.t.
f_{n_{j}}\overset{a.s.}{\to}f
w.r.t. the probability measure
\mathbb{P}_{X,D}
. That is, there is some
\Omega_{0}\subset\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{D}
and
\mathbb{P}_{X,D}(\Omega_{0})=1
s.t.

f_{n_{j}}(x,d)\to f(x,d),\forall(x,d)\in\Omega_{0}.

But, note that IS and
\alpha
-fairness constraints are evaluated pointwise. We need to leverage the
a.s.
convergence to ensure for
\mathbb{P}_{X}
-
a.e.

x
, the limit function
f
also satisfies the constraints.

Now, fix
k\in[|\mathcal{D}|]
. Define

A_{k}:=\left\{x\in\mathcal{X}\mid f_{n_{j}}(x,d_{k})\to f(x,d_{k})\right\}.

Since we know
f_{n_{j}}(x,d)\overset{a.s.}{\to}f(x,d)
, therefore, we have

\mathbb{P}_{X,D}\left(\left\{(x,d)\mid x\in A_{k}^{c}\right\}\right)=0.

By conditioning on
X
, we get:

\mathbb{P}_{X,D}\left(\left\{(x,d)\mid x\in A_{k}^{c}\right\}\right)=\int\mathbf{1}_{A_{k}^{c}}(x)\cdot p_{k|x}d\mathbb{P}_{X}(x)=0.

But, since
p_{k|x}>0
, then this forces

\mathbb{P}_{X}(A_{k}^{c})=0.

Therefore, for each
k\in[|\mathcal{D}|]
, we have

\mathbb{P}_{X}(A_{k})=1.

Now, define

A:=\bigcap_{k=1}^{|\mathcal{D}|}A_{k},

then, this gives

\mathbb{P}_{X}(A)=1,

as
A
is a finite intersection of probability-1 sets.

Thus, we have shown that
\forall x\in A
,

f_{n_{j}}(x,d_{k})\to f(x,d_{k}),\forall k\in[|\mathcal{D}|].

Next, fix a pair
k<k^{\prime}
. Then, for each subsequence index
j
, define

B_{j}^{k,k^{\prime}}:=\left\{x\mid f_{n_{j}}(x,d_{k})-\alpha f_{n_{j}}(x,d_{k^{\prime}})\geq 0\ \text{and}\ f_{n_{j}}(x,d_{k^{\prime}})-\alpha f_{n_{j}}(x,d_{k})\geq 0\right\}.

We know
f_{n_{j}}
is feasible, therefore, we have

\mathbb{P}_{X}\left(B_{j}^{k,k^{\prime}}\right)=1.

Now, define

B^{k,k^{\prime}}:=\bigcap_{j=1}^{\infty}B_{j}^{k,k^{\prime}},

then, this gives

\mathbb{P}_{X}\left(B^{k,k^{\prime}}\right)=1,

as
B^{k,k^{\prime}}
is a countable intersection of probability-1 sets.

Now, we further define

B^{\alpha-\text{fair}}:=\bigcap_{k<k^{\prime}}B^{k,k^{\prime}},

then, this again gives

\mathbb{P}_{X}\left(B^{\alpha-\text{fair}}\right)=1,

since
|\mathcal{D}|<\infty
, there are only finitely many pairs of
(k,k^{\prime})
.

Similarly, for each
j
, define

B_{j}^{IS}:=\left\{x\in\sum_{k=1}^{|\mathcal{D}|}\left(f_{n_{j}}(x,d_{k})-\mu_{k}(x)\right)\cdot p_{k|x}\geq 0\right\},

since
f_{n_{j}}
is feasible, we have

\mathbb{P}_{X}\left(B_{j}^{IS}\right)=1.

Define

B^{IS}:=\bigcap_{j=1}^{\infty}B_{j}^{IS},

we have

\mathbb{P}_{X}\left(B^{IS}\right)=1.

Finally, define

B:=B^{\alpha-\text{fair}}\cap B^{IS},

we get:

\mathbb{P}_{X}(B)=1.

Thus, we have shown that
\forall x\in B
, all constraints hold for each
f_{n_{j}}
.

Note that since
\mathbb{P}_{X}(A)=\mathbb{P}_{X}(B)=1
, thus, we have
\mathbb{P}_{X}(A\cap B)=1
. Now, we are ready to take the limit.

Pick any
x\in A\cap B
. For each
j
, we have:

f_{n_{j}}(x,d_{k})-\alpha f_{n_{j}}(x,d_{k})\geq 0,

take
j\to\infty
, we have:

f(x,d_{k})-\alpha f(x,d_{k})=\lim_{j\to\infty}\left(f_{n_{j}}(x,d_{k})-\alpha f_{n_{j}}(x,d_{k})\right)\geq 0.

The same argument will show that the
\alpha
-fairness constraint is satisfied for the reverse way.

Pick any
x\in A\cap B
. For each
j
, we have:

\sum_{k=1}^{|\mathcal{D}|}\left(f_{n_{j}}(x,d_{k})-\mu_{k}(x)\right)\cdot p_{k|x}\geq 0.

Since
|\mathcal{D}|<\infty
, and
f_{n_{j}}(x,d_{k})\to f(x,d_{k})
. By letting
j\to\infty
, we get:

\lim_{j\to\infty}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{|\mathcal{D}|}\left(f_{n_{j}}(x,d_{k})-\mu_{k}(x)\right)\cdot p_{k|x}\right)=\sum_{k=1}^{|\mathcal{D}|}\left(f(x,d_{k})-\mu_{k}(x)\right)\cdot p_{k|x}\geq 0.

Therefore, we have proved that
f\in\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,IS}
and this implies that
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,IS}
is closed in
\mathcal{F}
.
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,IS}
is also weakly closed since it is also convex.

Next, define the objective

\displaystyle\psi(f)

\displaystyle:={\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(f(X,D)-Y\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}{\mathbb{E}}\left[f(X,D)^{2}\right]-{\mathbb{E}}\left[Y^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\|f\|_{2}^{2}-{\mathbb{E}}\left[Y^{2}\right].

Since
{\mathbb{E}}\left[Y^{2}\right]<\infty
, we have
\psi(f)\to\infty
whenever
\|f\|_{2}^{2}\to\infty
. Thus,
\psi
is coercive.

To show
\psi
is weakly LSC, expand the square to get:

\displaystyle\psi(f)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(f(X,D)-Y\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left[f(X,D)^{2}\right]-2{\mathbb{E}}\left[f(X,D)Y\right]+{\mathbb{E}}\left[Y^{2}\right]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left[f(X,D)^{2}\right]-2{\mathbb{E}}\left[f(X,D){\mathbb{E}}\left[Y\mid X,D\right]\right]+{\mathbb{E}}\left[Y^{2}\right]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left[f(X,D)^{2}\right]-2{\mathbb{E}}\left[f(X,D)\mu_{D}(X)\right]+{\mathbb{E}}\left[Y^{2}\right].

But, note that, by Jensen’s inequality, we have

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mu_{D}(X)^{2}\right]\leq{\mathbb{E}}\left[Y^{2}\right]<\infty,

meaning
\mu_{D}(X)\in L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X,D})
. Thus,

\psi(f)=\|f\|_{2}^{2}-2\langle f,\mu_{D}\rangle+{\mathbb{E}}\left[Y^{2}\right].

Since
\|\cdot\|_{2}^{2}
is weakly LSC on
L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X,D})
and
\langle\cdot,\mu_{D}\rangle
is a bounded linear operator (inner product operator).
Therefore,
\psi
is also weakly LSC.

Now, let

m:=\inf_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,IS}}\psi(f).

Since
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,IS}\neq\emptyset
and
m<\infty
. Take
\{f_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}\subset\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,IS}
with
\psi(f_{n})\to m
. Then, by coercivity,
\{f_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
is bounded in
\mathcal{F}
. Therefore, we can find a weakly convergent subsequence
f_{n_{j}}\rightharpoonup f^{*}
in
\mathcal{F}
. But, we already proved that
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,IS}
is weakly closed. Thus,
f^{*}\in\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,IS}
. By weakly LSC, we have:

\psi(f^{*})\leq\liminf_{j\to\infty}\psi(f_{n_{j}})=m.

Therefore
\psi(f^{*})=m
, hence
f^{*}
is a minimizer.

To show
f^{*}
is the unique minimizer, let
f,g\in\mathcal{F}
and
t\in(0,1)
, then apply the identity with
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
,
t\in(0,1)
:

\left(ta+(1-t)b\right)^{2}=ta^{2}+(1-t)b^{2}-t(1-t)(a-b)^{2},

we get:

\psi(tf+(1-t)g)=t\psi(f)+(1-t)\psi(g)-t(1-t)\|f-g\|_{2}^{2}.

If
f\neq g
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X,D})
, then
\|f-g\|_{2}^{2}>0
, resulting in

\psi(tf+(1-t)g)<t\psi(f)+(1-t)\psi(g).

This implies that
\psi
is strictly convex.

Now, let
f^{*},g^{*}
be two distinct minimizers. Take
t=\frac{1}{2}
and define

h:=\frac{1}{2}f^{*}+\frac{1}{2}g^{*}\in\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,IS}.

Since we have proven that
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,IS}
is convex. Then, we have

\displaystyle\psi(h)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\psi(f^{*})+\frac{1}{2}\psi(g^{*})-\frac{1}{4}\|f^{*}-g^{*}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=m-\frac{1}{4}\|f^{*}-g^{*}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle<m,

contradicting the optimality of
f^{*},g^{*}
, and the infimum
m
over
F_{\alpha,IS}
.

Therefore, there exists a minimizer
f^{*}
up to
\mathbb{P}_{X,D}
-
a.s.
equality.
∎

Proof

Proof.

Step 1: upper bounding the individual solvency (IS) deviation

Note that we can write the population IS functional as follows

\displaystyle G(f,t)

\displaystyle:={\mathbb{E}}\left[(f(X,D)-Y)t(X)\right]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(\sum_{k=1}^{|\mathcal{D}|}(f(X,d_{k})-\mu_{k}(X))\cdot p_{k|x}\right)t(X)\right].

Consequently, we get the plug-in empirical IS functional as

\hat{G}_{n}(f,t):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{|\mathcal{D}|}(f(X_{i},d_{k})-\hat{\mu}_{k}(X_{i}))\cdot\hat{p}_{k|X_{i}}\right)t(X_{i}),

and the empirical IS functional with true
\mu_{k}
and
p_{k|\cdot}
as

G_{n}(f,t):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{|\mathcal{D}|}(f(X_{i},d_{k})-\mu_{k}(X_{i}))\cdot p_{k|X_{i}}\right)t(X_{i}),

For any
f\in\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}
, define

\displaystyle IS_{f}(x)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{k=1}^{|\mathcal{D}|}(f(x,d_{k})-\mu_{k}(x))\cdot p_{k|x},

\displaystyle\hat{IS}_{f}(x)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{k=1}^{|\mathcal{D}|}(f(x,d_{k})-\hat{\mu}_{k}(x))\cdot\hat{p}_{k|x}.

Then, we can decompose the error as follows:

G(f,t)-\hat{G}_{n}(f,t)=\left(G(f,t)-G_{n}(f,t)\right)+\left(G_{n}(f,t)-\hat{G}_{n}(f,t)\right).

Then, we will bound term by term. Starting with
|G(f,t)-G_{n}(f,t)|
.

Fix
x
. Then, we have

\hat{IS}_{f}(x)-IS_{f}(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{|\mathcal{D}|}\left(f(x,d_{k})-\mu_{k}(x)\right)\left(\hat{p}_{k|x}-p_{k|x}\right)+\sum_{k=1}^{|\mathcal{D}|}\left(\mu_{k}(x)-\hat{\mu}_{k}(x)\right)\cdot\hat{p}_{k|x}.

Taking absolute value, we get:

\displaystyle\left|\hat{IS}_{f}(x)-IS_{f}(x)\right|

\displaystyle\leq(B_{f}+B)\sum_{k=1}^{|\mathcal{D}|}\left|\hat{p}_{k|x}-p_{k|x}\right|+\sum_{k=1}^{|\mathcal{D}|}\left|\mu_{k}(x)-\hat{\mu}_{k}(x)\right|\cdot\hat{p}_{k|x}

\displaystyle\leq M\epsilon_{p}+\epsilon_{\mu},\forall x.

Since
|f(,d_{k})-\mu_{k}(x)|\leq B_{f}+B=M
and
\sum_{k=1}^{|\mathcal{D}|}\hat{p}_{k|x}=1
. Therefore, with
t(x)\in[0,1]
, we have:

\displaystyle\left|G(f,t)-G_{n}(f,t)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(IS_{f}(X)-\hat{IS}_{f}(X)\right)t(X)\right]\right|

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|IS_{f}(X)-\hat{IS}_{f}(X)\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq M\epsilon_{p}+\epsilon_{\mu}.

Thus, we get:

\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{\alpha},t\in\mathcal{T}}\left|G(f,t)-G_{n}(f,t)\right|\leq M\epsilon_{p}+\epsilon_{\mu}.

Next, we bound
\left|G_{n}(f,t)-\hat{G}_{n}(f,t)\right|
.

Define the plug-in constrained class:

\hat{\mathcal{H}}:=\left\{\hat{h}_{f,t}(x):=\hat{IS}_{f}(x)t(x)\mid f\in\mathcal{F}_{\alpha},t\in\mathcal{T}\right\}.

Then, we have:

\sup_{f,t}\left|G_{n}(f,t)-\hat{G}_{n}(f,t)\right|=\sup_{\hat{h}\in\hat{\mathcal{H}}}\left|P_{X}\hat{h}-P_{n,X}\hat{h}\right|,

where
P_{X}
is the expectation over
X
and
P_{n,X}
is the empirical average over
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
.

For any
x
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\hat{IS}_{f}(x)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{k=1}^{|\mathcal{D}|}\left(f(x,d_{k})-\hat{\mu}_{k}(x)\right)\cdot\hat{p}_{k|x}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{|\mathcal{D}|}\left(|f(x,d_{k})|+|\hat{\mu}_{k}(x)|\right)\cdot\hat{p}_{k|x}

\displaystyle\leq B_{f}+B

\displaystyle=M.

Therefore,

\left|\hat{h}_{f,t}(x)\right|\leq M.

Now, we bound the covering number of
\hat{\mathcal{H}}
.

Let
\{f_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N_{F}}
be an
\epsilon
-net of
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X,D})
and
\{t_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{N_{T}}
be an
\epsilon
-net of
\mathcal{T}
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X})
. For any
(f,t)
, pick
f_{j},t_{\ell}
close. Then, we can write:

\hat{h}_{f,t}-\hat{h}_{f_{j},t_{\ell}}=\left(\hat{IS}_{f}-\hat{IS}_{f_{j}}\right)t+(t-t_{\ell})\hat{IS}_{f_{j}}.

Squaring, we get:

\left(\hat{h}_{f,t}-\hat{h}_{f_{j},t_{\ell}}\right)^{2}\leq 2\left(\hat{IS}_{f}-\hat{IS}_{f_{j}}\right)^{2}+2(B_{f}+B)^{2}(t-t_{\ell})^{2}.

Note that

\hat{IS}_{f}(x)-\hat{IS}_{f_{j}}=\sum_{k=1}^{|\mathcal{D}|}(f(x,d_{k})-f_{j}(x,d_{k}))\cdot\hat{p}_{k|x}.

Let

0<p_{\min}:=\inf_{x\in\mathcal{X},k\in\mathcal{D}}p_{k|x},

and then, taking expectation over
X
, we get:

\displaystyle\left\|\hat{IS}_{f}-\hat{IS}_{f_{j}}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{E}}_{X}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{|\mathcal{D}|}(f(X,d_{k})-f_{j}(X,d_{k}))^{2}\cdot\hat{p}_{k|x}\right]

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{E}}_{X}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{|\mathcal{D}|}(f(X,d_{k})-f_{j}(X,d_{k}))^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{E}}_{X}\left[\frac{1}{p_{\min}}\sum_{k=1}^{|\mathcal{D}|}(f(X,d_{k})-f_{j}(X,d_{k}))^{2}\cdot p_{k|x}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p_{\min}}\left\|f-f_{j}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X,D})}^{2}.

Therefore,

\left(\hat{h}_{f,t}-\hat{h}_{f_{j},t_{\ell}}\right)^{2}\leq\frac{2\epsilon^{2}}{p_{\min}}+2M^{2}\epsilon^{2}=\epsilon^{2}\left(\frac{2}{p_{\min}}+2M^{2}\right),

which immediately implies that

\left\|\hat{h}_{f,t}-\hat{h}_{f_{j},t_{\ell}}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X})}\leq\epsilon\sqrt{\frac{2}{p_{\min}}+2M^{2}},

meaning that the finite set

\left\{\hat{h}_{f_{j},t_{\ell}}\mid j=1,\ldots,N_{F},\ell=1,\ldots,N_{T}\right\}

is an
\epsilon\sqrt{2/p_{\min}+2M^{2}}
-net of
\mathcal{H}
w.r.t.
L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X})
. Hence, we have:

N\left(\epsilon\sqrt{\frac{2}{p_{\min}}+2M^{2}},\mathcal{H},L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X})\right)\leq N\left(\epsilon,\mathcal{F}_{\alpha},\mathbb{P}_{X,D}\right)\times N\left(\epsilon,\mathcal{T},L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X})\right).

Let
\tilde{\epsilon}=\epsilon\sqrt{2/p_{\min}+2M^{2}}
and
C_{0}=\sqrt{2/p_{\min}+2M^{2}}
, we get:

N\left(\tilde{\epsilon},\mathcal{H},L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X})\right)\leq N\left(\frac{\tilde{\epsilon}}{C_{0}},\mathcal{F}_{\alpha},L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X,D})\right)\times N\left(\frac{\tilde{\epsilon}}{C_{0}},\mathcal{T},L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X})\right).

Then, by Assumption
4.2
, we get:

\displaystyle\log N(\tilde{\epsilon},\hat{\mathcal{H}},L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X}))

\displaystyle\leq V_{F}\log\frac{A_{F}C_{0}}{\tilde{\epsilon}}+V_{T}\log\frac{A_{T}C_{0}}{\tilde{\epsilon}}

\displaystyle\leq\left(V_{F}+V_{T}\right)\log\frac{A_{H}}{\tilde{\epsilon}},

where
A_{H}>0
, absorbing
A_{F},A_{T},C_{0}
.

Now, the standard symmetrization argument gives

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sup_{\hat{h}\in\hat{\mathcal{H}}}\left|P_{X}\hat{h}-P_{n,X}\hat{h}\right|\right]\leq 2{\mathbb{E}}\left[\hat{\Re}_{n}(\hat{\mathcal{H}})\right]

.
Then, by the Dudley’s inequality, we have:

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\hat{\Re}_{n}(\hat{\mathcal{H}})\right]\leq\frac{12}{\sqrt{n}}\int_{0}^{M}\sqrt{\log N(\epsilon,\hat{\mathcal{H}},L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X}))}\ d\epsilon.

Plug in
\log N(\tilde{\epsilon},\hat{\mathcal{H}},L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X}))\leq\left(V_{F}+V_{T}\right)\log\frac{A_{H}}{\tilde{\epsilon}}
, we get

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\hat{\Re}_{n}(\hat{\mathcal{H}})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{12}{\sqrt{n}}\int_{0}^{M}\sqrt{(V_{F}+V_{T})\log\frac{A_{H}}{\epsilon}}\ d\epsilon

\displaystyle=\frac{12\sqrt{V_{F}+V_{T}}}{\sqrt{n}}\int_{0}^{M}\sqrt{\log\frac{A_{H}}{\epsilon}}\ d\epsilon.

Let
I:=\int_{0}^{M}\sqrt{\log\frac{A_{H}}{\epsilon}}\ d\epsilon
and substitute
\epsilon=Mu
, then

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle=M\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{\log\frac{A_{H}}{Mu}}\ du

\displaystyle=M\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{\log\frac{A_{H}}{M}+\log\frac{1}{u}}\ du

\displaystyle\leq M\int_{0}^{1}\left(\sqrt{\log\frac{A_{H}}{M}}+\sqrt{\log\frac{1}{u}}\right)\ du

\displaystyle=M\left(\sqrt{\log\frac{A_{H}}{M}}+\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}M.

Therefore, with symmetrization, we get:

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sup_{\hat{h}\in\hat{\mathcal{H}}}\left|P_{X}\hat{h}-P_{n,X}\hat{h}\right|\right]\leq CM\sqrt{\frac{V_{F}+V_{T}}{n}}.

Define

\Phi(X_{1}^{n})=\sup_{\hat{h}\in\hat{\mathcal{H}}}\left|P_{X}\hat{h}-P_{n,X}\hat{h}\right|.

To apply McDiarmid’s inequality, we replace
X_{i}
with
X_{i}^{\prime}
. Then, for any fixed
\hat{h}
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\hat{h}(X_{j})-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j\neq i}\hat{h}(X_{j})-\frac{1}{n}\hat{h}(X_{i}^{\prime})\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\left|\hat{h}(X_{i})-\hat{h}(X_{i}^{\prime})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2M}{n}.

Hence, by McDiarmid’s inequality, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\Phi(X_{1}^{n})-{\mathbb{E}}[\Phi(X_{1}^{n})]\geq t\right)

\displaystyle\leq{\sf exp}\left(-\frac{2t^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle={\sf exp}\left(-\frac{nt^{2}}{2M^{2}}\right).

Since we want the event w.p. at least
1-\frac{\delta}{4}
, thus, set
{\sf exp}\left(-\frac{nt^{2}}{2M^{2}}\right)=\frac{\delta}{4}
, and get
t=M\sqrt{\frac{2\log(4/\delta)}{n}}
. Then, we have:

\mathbb{P}\left(\Phi(X_{1}^{n})-{\mathbb{E}}[\Phi(X_{1}^{n})]\geq M\sqrt{\frac{2\log(4/\delta)}{n}}\right)\leq\frac{\delta}{4}.

This implies that w.p. at least
1-\frac{\delta}{4}
,

\displaystyle\Phi(X_{1}^{n})

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{E}}[\Phi(X_{1}^{n})]+M\sqrt{\frac{2\log(4/\delta)}{n}}

\displaystyle\leq CM\sqrt{\frac{V_{F}+V_{T}}{n}}+M\sqrt{\frac{2\log(4/\delta)}{n}}.

Therefore, we conclude that w.p. at least
1-\frac{\delta}{4}

\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{\alpha},t\in\mathcal{T}}\left|G_{n}(f,t)-\hat{G}_{n}(f,t)\right|\leq CM\left(\sqrt{\frac{V_{F}+V_{T}}{n}}+\sqrt{\frac{\log(4/\delta)}{n}}\right).

Combining with the previous bound, we get:

\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F},t\in\mathcal{T}}\left|G(f,t)-\hat{G}_{n}(f,t)\right|\leq M\epsilon_{p}+\epsilon\mu+CM\left(\sqrt{\frac{V_{F}+V_{T}}{n}}+\sqrt{\frac{\log(4/\delta)}{n}}\right).

Now, choose

\eta_{n}=M\epsilon_{p}+\epsilon\mu+CM\left(\sqrt{\frac{V_{F}+V_{T}}{n}}+\sqrt{\frac{\log(4/\delta)}{n}}\right),

then on this good event, we have for every
f\in\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,IS}
and every
t\in\mathcal{T}
,

G(f,t)\geq 0\implies\hat{G}_{n}(f,t)\geq G(f,t)-\left|G(f,t)-\hat{G}_{n}(f,t)\right|\geq-\eta_{n}.

Therefore,

\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,IS}\subset\hat{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha,IS}^{(\eta_{n})},

in particular

f_{\alpha,IS}^{*}\in\hat{\mathcal{F}}_{\alpha,IS}^{(\eta_{n})}.

Now, we upper-bound the risk deviation.

Define the loss class

\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}:=\{\ell_{f}(x,d,y):=(f(x,d)-y)^{2}\mid f\in\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}\},

then, we know that

|\ell_{f}(x,d,y)|\leq(|f(x,d)|+|y|)^{2}\leq(B_{f}+B)^{2}=M^{2}.

Let

\Delta_{n}:=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}}|\mathcal{R}(f)-\mathcal{R}_{n}(f)|=\sup_{\ell\in\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}}|P\ell-P_{n}\ell|.

Then, using the standard Rademacher generalization bound, we have

\Delta_{n}\leq 2\Re_{n}(\mathcal{L}_{\alpha})+3M^{2}\sqrt{\frac{\log(4/\delta)}{2n}},

w.p. at least
1-\frac{\delta}{4}
.

Then, we relate
\Re_{n}(\mathcal{L}_{\alpha})
to
\Re_{n}(\mathcal{F}_{\alpha})
.

For
i=1,\ldots,n
, define

\phi_{i}(u):=(u-Y_{i})^{2},u\in[-B_{f},B_{f}].

Then, define

\tilde{\phi}_{i}(u):=\phi_{i}(u)-\phi_{i}(0)=(u-Y_{i})^{2}-Y_{i}^{2}.

Then, for
u,v\in[-B_{f},B_{f}]
,

\displaystyle|\tilde{\phi}(u)-\tilde{\phi}(v)|

\displaystyle=|\phi_{i}(u)-\phi_{i}(v)|

\displaystyle=|(u-Y_{i})^{2}-(v-Y_{i})^{2}|

\displaystyle=|u-v||u+v-2Y_{i}|

\displaystyle=|u-v|(|u|+|v|+2|Y_{i}|)

\displaystyle\leq 2(B_{f}+B)|u-v|

\displaystyle=2M|u-v|.

This implies that each

\alpha

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\tilde{g}_{\alpha}(X,D)-Y\mid X=x\right]

C.5.2
Proposition

(ii) Proof

Proof.

We first consider when
|\mathcal{D}|=2
.

Let one level in
\mathcal{D}
correspond to low risk (L) and the other to high risk (H).

Fix
x\in\mathcal{X}
. If
\mu_{L}(x)\geq\alpha\mu_{H}(x)
and
\mu_{H}(x)\geq\alpha\mu_{L}(x)
, then, we know
\left(f^{*}(x,d_{L}),f^{*}(x,d_{H})\right)=\left(\mu_{L}(x),\mu_{H}(x)\right)
is feasible (IS hits equality by definition of
\mu(x)
) and optimal with
\mu_{L}(x)\leq\mu(x)\implies f^{*}(x,d_{L})=\mu_{L}(x)\leq\mu(x)
, as desired.

Now, consider the case when
\mu_{L}(x)<\alpha\mu_{H}(x)
. Then
\left(\mu_{L}(x),\mu_{H}(x)\right)
is no longer feasible. Observe that for any fixed
f(x,d_{H})
, the objective

\psi(f)=\left(f(x,d_{L})-\mu_{L}(x)\right)^{2}\cdot p_{L|x}+\left(f(x,d_{H})-\mu_{H}(x)\right)^{2}\cdot p_{H|x},

is only a function of
f(x,d_{L})
and is minimized at the smallest feasible
f(x,d_{L})
. Since
\mu_{L}(x)<\alpha\mu_{H}(x)
, any feasible solution that keeps
f(x,d_{H})
close to
\mu_{H}(x)
must have
f(x,d_{L})>\mu_{L}(x)
. The objective is strictly increasing in
f(x,d_{L})
for
f(x,d_{L})>\mu_{L}(x)
. Therefore, we only need to consider the scenario where
f(x,d_{L})=\alpha f(x,d_{H})
.

Now, let
f(x,d_{L})=\alpha f(x,d_{H})
, we have:

\psi(f)=\left(\alpha f(x,d_{H})-\mu_{L}(x)\right)^{2}\cdot p_{L|x}+\left(f(x,d_{)}-\mu_{H}(x)\right)^{2}\cdot p_{H|x}.

Fairness constraint is automatically satisfied by the setup of
f(x,d_{L})=\alpha f(x,d_{H})
. For IS, we have:

\left(p_{H|x}+\alpha p_{L|X}\right)f(x,d_{H})\geq f(x,d_{L})\cdot p_{L|x}+f(x,d_{H})\cdot p_{H|X}=\mu(x).

This implies

f(x,d_{H})\geq\frac{\mu(x)}{p_{H|x}+\alpha p_{L|x}}.

Now, if we minimize
\psi
with
f(x,d_{L})=\alpha f(x,d_{H})
, we would obtain

\frac{\partial}{\partial f(x,d_{H})}\psi(f)=2\alpha\left(\alpha f(x,d_{H})-\mu_{L}(x)\right)\cdot p_{L|x}+2\left(f(x,d_{H})-\mu_{H}(x)\right)\cdot p_{H|x}=0.

This gives

\tilde{f}(x,d_{H})=\frac{\alpha\mu_{L}(x)\cdot p_{L|x}+\mu_{H}(x)\cdot p_{H|x}}{\alpha^{2}p_{L|x}+p_{H|x}},

consequently,

\tilde{f}(x,d_{L})=\alpha\tilde{f}(x,d_{H})=\frac{\alpha^{2}\mu_{L}(x)\cdot p_{L|x}+\alpha\mu_{H}(x)\cdot p_{H|x}}{\alpha^{2}p_{L|x}+p_{H|x}}.

Therefore, we have that

f^{*}(x,d_{H})=\max\left\{\tilde{f}(x,d_{H}),\frac{\mu(x)}{p_{H|x}+\alpha p_{L|x}}\right\},

and

f^{*}(x,d_{L})=\alpha f^{*}(x,d_{H}).

If the maximum is attained in order to satisfy IS, then

\displaystyle f^{*}(x,d_{L})

\displaystyle=\alpha f^{*}(x,d_{H})

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha\mu(x)}{p_{H|x}+\alpha p_{L|x}}.

Then, enough to verify that if

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha\mu(x)}{p_{H|x}+\alpha p_{L|x}}\leq\mu(x)\iff\alpha

\displaystyle\leq p_{H|x}+\alpha p_{L|x}

\displaystyle=1-p_{L|x}+\alpha p_{L|x}

\displaystyle=\alpha+(1-\alpha)(1-p_{L|x}),

as desired.

Now, suppose IS is slack, that is
f^{*}(x,d_{H})=\tilde{f}(x,d_{H})
, then, we have:

\displaystyle f^{*}(x,d_{L})

\displaystyle=\alpha f^{*}(x,d_{H})

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha^{2}\mu_{L}(x)\cdot p_{L|x}+\alpha\mu_{H}(x)\cdot p_{H|x}}{\alpha^{2}p_{L|x}+p_{H|x}}.

Then, enough to verify if

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha^{2}\mu_{L}(x)\cdot p_{L|x}+\alpha\mu_{H}(x)\cdot p_{H|x}}{\alpha^{2}p_{L|x}+p_{H|x}}\leq\mu(x)\iff\frac{\alpha^{2}\mu_{L}(x)\cdot p_{L|x}+\alpha\mu_{H}(x)\cdot p_{H|x}}{\alpha^{2}p_{L|x}+p_{H|x}}\leq\mu_{L}(x)\cdot p_{L|x}+\mu_{H}(x)\cdot p_{H|x}.

Then, simplify to get:

\alpha\tilde{f}(x,d_{H})-\mu(x)=\frac{(1-\alpha)(1-p_{L|x})\left((1+\alpha)(\mu_{H}(x)-\mu_{L}(x))\cdot p_{L|x}-\mu_{H}(x)\right)}{\alpha^{2}p_{L|x}+p_{H|x}},

since
(1+\alpha)(1-p_{L|x})\geq 0
and
\alpha^{2}p_{L|x}+p_{H|x}>0
, we get:

\alpha\tilde{f}(x,d_{H})\leq\mu\iff p_{L|x}\leq\frac{\mu_{H}(x)}{(1+\alpha)\left(\mu_{H}(x)-\mu_{L}(x)\right)}.

Take
\mu_{L}(x)\to 0
, we get the worst-case condition

p_{L|x}\leq\frac{1}{1+\alpha},

to have

f^{*}(x,d_{L})\leq\mu(x),

as desired.

To extend the result to
|\mathcal{D}|>2
, we need to impose an additional restriction on the optimal solution.

Suppose there exists a low-risk cluster
C_{L}\subset\mathcal{D}
and a high-risk cluster
C_{H}=\mathcal{D}\setminus C_{L}
s.t. for some
M(x)>0

\begin{cases}f^{*}(x,d_{k})=\alpha M(x),\ \forall k\in C_{L}\\
f^{*}(x,d_{k})=M(x)\ \ \ ,\ \forall k\in C_{H}.\end{cases}

Then, let

\displaystyle p_{L|x}:=\sum_{k\in C_{L}}p_{k|x},\quad\mu_{L}(x):=\frac{1}{p_{L|x}}\sum_{k\in C_{L}}\mu_{k}(x)\cdot p_{k|x},

\displaystyle p_{H|x}:=\sum_{k\in C_{H}}p_{k|x},\quad\mu_{H}(x):=\frac{1}{p_{H|x}}\sum_{k\in C_{H}}\mu_{k}(x)\cdot p_{k|x}.

Then, with the same argument used for
|\mathcal{D}|=2
, we have the worst-case sufficient condition

\sum_{k\in C_{L}}p_{k|x}\leq\frac{1}{1+\alpha},

then, for every
k\in C_{L}
,

f^{*}(x,d_{k})\leq\mu(x),\forall\alpha\in(0,1].

This completes the proof.

Proof.

Fix

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[(f(X,D)-Y)^{2}\mid X=x\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{|\mathcal{D}|}{\mathbb{E}}\left[(f(x,d_{k})-Y)^{2}\mid X=x,D=k\right]\cdot p_{k|x}

Then, since the variance term does not depend on

Let

\alpha M(x)\leq f(x,\cdot)\leq M(x).

On the other hand, if there is some

\alpha M(x)\leq f(x,\cdot)\leq M(x),

then

\alpha\leq\frac{f(x,d_{k})}{f(x,d_{j})}\leq\frac{1}{\alpha},

for all pairs
(k,j)

x

\alpha
-fairness constraints are precisely the existence of a scalar

satisfying the above inequalities.

Thus, for fixed
x
, the optimization becomes:

\min_{f\in\mathcal{F},M(x)\in\mathbb{R}}\sum_{k=1}^{|\mathcal{D}|}(f(x,d_{k})-\mu_{k}(x))^{2}\cdot p_{k|x},

subject to

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{|\mathcal{D}|}(f(x,d_{k})-\mu_{k}(x))\cdot p_{k|x}

\displaystyle\geq 0

\displaystyle\alpha(M(x)\leq f(x,d_{k})

\displaystyle\leq M(x),\forall k.

Note that this is convex with a strictly convex objective. Thus, we can leverage the KKT condition.

Now, define the Lagrange multipliers:

•

\lambda(x)\geq 0
, for
\mu(x)-\sum_{k=1}^{|\mathcal{D}|}f(x,d_{k})\cdot p_{k|x}\leq 0
.

•

u_{k}(x)\geq 0

.

•

, for
\alpha M(x)-f(x,d_{k})\leq 0

Then, by stationarity w.r.t.

1.

2.

If

\alpha<1
