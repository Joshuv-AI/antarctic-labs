Three-arm randomised controlled trial of text message reminders and financial incentives to improve antidepressant adherence among adults in primary care with depression: study protocol
Authors: Steven C Marcus, Cadence F Bowden, Kevin G Lynch, Mark Olfson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Almost three-quarters of patients who are treated for depression receive their treatment exclusively from primary care providers who have relatively short visits with their depressed patients and little capacity to monitor their treatment adherence. Introduction

Each year approximately 8.5% of adults in the USA will experience an episode of major depressive disorder, imposing a heavy burden on individuals, their families and their communities.13 Depression interferes with educational achievement, reduces marital stability and occupational productivity, increases suicide risk and cardiovascular disease and impairs daily function.49

Antidepressant medications are an effective treatment; their efficacy for adults with major depressive disorder has been confirmed in a meta-analysis of 522 randomised controlled trials (RCTs) involving more than 116 000 patients.1012 However, antidepressant discontinuation early in treatment is common, with more than one-third of outpatients starting antidepressants for depression not refilling their first prescription.101315 Further, only about half of adults in the USA who initiate antidepressants for new episodes of depression continue for 6 months.16 17 Clinical features of depression, including hopelessness about the potential benefits of treatment, loss of energy and motivation, social isolation and memory impairment may challenge establishing a regular medication-taking routine and increase the risk of antidepressant non-adherence.18 19 Another cause of inconsistent adherence and early discontinuation is that antidepressants frequently have a delayed onset of action. In a meta-analysis examining antidepressant response in the first 6 weeks of treatment, only about half of the response occurred in the first 2 weeks, with the remainder spread over the following 4 weeks.20 Therefore, for many people newly starting antidepressants, the behaviour of taking antidepressants each day is unlikely to be reinforced via symptom relief in the first few weeks. As a result, the risk of non-adherence may be greatest early in treatment, before antidepressants have reached full therapeutic effect21 22 and before consistent medication-taking routines are established.

Almost three-quarters of patients who are treated for depression are managed exclusively by primary care providers (PCPs) and most are prescribed an antidepressant.23 Although PCPs often try to connect their patients with mental health specialists, approximately two-thirds of PCPs report that they cannot secure a referral.24 Thus, for many patients, antidepressants prescribed by their PCP are a cornerstone of their depression care, making optimal delivery in this setting especially important. At the same time, PCPs have relatively short visits with their depressed patients and little capacity to monitor their treatment adherence and well-being. As a result, there is a critical need to develop effective strategies to support and promote antidepressant adherence for patients seen in primary care, without interfering with PCP workflow or requiring intensive clinical resources.

Text message reminders have been widely used to improve medication adherence across many medical conditions.2527 In addition to addressing the common problem of patients forgetting medication doses by serving as cues for medication use, daily messages can also be motivating and offer practical information.28 Text messages may be particularly effective because they are likely to be read shortly after being received29 and serve as a reminder temporally aligned with decision-making. In a meta-analysis of studies of several chronic medical conditions, mobile phone text messages were found to double the odds of medication adherence.26 In the USA, healthcare systems increasingly rely on text reminders to support patient engagement. For example, the Veterans Health Administration and other large health organisations routinely send automated text messages to patients’ mobile phones to support adherence to recommended care.30 31 In parallel, a growing number of smart phone applications, many of which use text reminders, have been developed to encourage medication adherence. A review identified 805 such applications, though only four had empirical support.32

Another approach to promoting health behaviours is contingent financial incentives. Grounded in operant conditioning theory, financial incentives have been found to increase adherence for myriad medications and conditions,3339 and there is evidence that financial incentives can improve long-term medication adherence.40 41 Contingent financial incentives may be particularly relevant to antidepressant adherence behaviour because antidepressants have delayed onsets of action and patients do not experience immediate symptom relief. Earning contingent financial incentives could serve as a bridge to strengthen medication adherence intentions and pill-taking habit formation, especially early in treatment.42 There is a growing interest in the use of financial incentives to improve health. As of 2018, 19 state Medicaid programmes and nearly all Medicaid managed care organisations had developed beneficiary incentive programmes for health behaviours.43 44 In addition, 86% of employers in 2018 offered financial incentives to improve employee health.32

The current study

To address treatment non-adherence, we are conducting a 3-arm RCT to compare the short- and intermediate-term effectiveness of two adherence support strategies to usual care for adult primary care patients with depression who have been newly prescribed antidepressant medications. We are examining whether daily text messages with and without financial incentives improve depression symptoms and antidepressant adherence, assessed via wireless pill bottle and survey administration at 6 and 12 weeks. Specifically, we aim to determine the relative effectiveness of (1) 12 weeks of text reminders with financial incentives provided during the first 6 weeks (reminders and incentives), (2) 12 weeks of daily text reminders without financial incentives (reminders alone), compared with (3) treatment as usual, and with each other.

The primary outcome will be symptom response on the Patient Health Questionnaire 9 (PHQ-9) depression rating scale at 12 weeks, defined as a 50% or greater reduction in symptom severity from baseline.45 Secondary analyses will examine symptom response at 6 weeks and antidepressant adherence at 6 and 12 weeks.

Methods and analysis

Patient involvement and intervention development

The current study builds on our pilot RCT (n=120) that tested escalating and de-escalating financial incentives on antidepressant adherence and depression symptoms.46 Findings from the pilot study demonstrated escalating incentives of $2 to $7 per day significantly improved daily antidepressant adherence compared with a control group during the first 6 weeks of antidepressant medication treatment.46 Starting with a small incentive and increasing it over time may help individuals build a new habit by reducing development of tolerance to a fixed incentive value in a manner that is analogous to how patients may develop tolerance to some medications and require progressively higher doses to maintain the same effects.47 Escalating incentives have the potential theoretical advantage of building momentum that might be needed to initiate a new behaviour such as starting antidepressant medications.48 Because the pilot demonstrated preliminary effectiveness, feasibility and acceptability,46 the proposed trial will incorporate escalating incentives contingent on daily adherence. In the pilot study, participants received alerts about earned adherence payments, which may have functioned as reminders.46 The proposed study will disentangle the effects of reminders and incentives by testing whether more sophisticated, behaviourally informed messages alone can achieve effects comparable to reminders paired with incentives.

Drawing on principles of behavioural science49 and behaviour change,50 our team developed 84 text message reminders designed to support antidepressant adherence. In prior work, we first identified modifiable barriers to antidepressant adherence, such as forgetting doses, social stigma, delayed medication benefits and competing demands on time or attention (eg, work or family responsibilities). Each barrier was paired with an evidence-based behaviour change technique. For example, to address the barrier of competing demands on time and attention, we applied the technique of conserving mental resources by encouraging simple routines or cues to support medication taking. Sample messages are provided in table 1. We conducted a pilot study to evaluate the perceived effectiveness51 and acceptability52 of these daily text reminders among 181 patients who had been diagnosed with depression and were taking antidepressant medication.53 Feedback from this study helped us to refine the text message reminders to be used in the current study.

Sample selection

Study participants will be recruited from 70 practices within Penn Medicine at the University of Pennsylvania, an integrated academic primary care network serving approximately 400 000 patients annually. The study sample will consist of adult primary care patients (n=525) initiating antidepressant treatment for major depressive disorder. Patients will be considered eligible if they are initiating a new antidepressant prescription from their PCP, defined as no prior antidepressant use in the preceding 90 days. Potentially eligible participants will be identified through daily queries of the electronic health record system, and relevant recruitment data will be stored securely in REDCap, a web-based software platform hosted at Penn Medicine.54 Research staff will use telephone outreach to invite patients to participate in the study, confirm eligibility and obtain informed consent. During recruitment, details of the intervention strategies will be withheld from all participants to avoid influencing expectations about randomisation or disappointment related to not being assigned to an active intervention, particularly those involving financial incentives. The University of Pennsylvania Institutional Review Board has reviewed all procedures and approved this study. The informed consent form is provided as online supplement 1.

Intervention description

Enrolled patients will receive an electronic pill bottle, which wirelessly transmits pill bottle opening, for use throughout the study.55 Telephone baseline assessments will be scheduled within 1 week of consent and no later than 10 days from the participant’s medication start date, allowing sufficient time for the pill bottle to arrive. During the baseline assessment, research staff will instruct participants on how to activate the pill bottle and place their antidepressant medication in the bottle. Participants will be instructed to use the electronic pill bottle to take their antidepressant medication as prescribed by their clinician for the duration of their study participation. Study day 1 is defined as the day following the baseline assessment.

On the day of baseline assessments, participants will be randomly assigned in equal proportions with block randomisation (block size=9) to one of three study arms using the Way to Health platform,56 which will also manage delivery of the text message reminders and administration of financial incentives. The study arms are (1) 12 weeks of daily text reminders paired with 6 weeks of escalating financial incentives (reminders and incentives), (2) 12 weeks of daily text reminders without financial incentives (reminders alone) or (3) treatment as usual. Personnel conducting baseline assessments do not have access to the randomisation sequence. Following randomisation, participants will be given information about their intervention assignment. Sample size calculation

The primary analyses will compare the two active interventions to treatment as usual for depression symptom response at week 12 and evaluate their relative effectiveness through a non-inferiority analysis. We will use two-sided tests, with a test-wise alpha level of 0.017 (0.05/3) to control type I error. We expect 47% of the treatment as usual group to have depression response at 12 weeks, consistent with results from the STAR*D trial.22 46 In our pilot study, retention was 94% at week 6 and 87% at week 12, and the within-person correlation in depression symptom scores was 0.4 between weeks 6 and 12.46 Power was estimated using simulation methods based on mixed-effects logistic regression models that incorporated expected response rates, dropout and within-person correlation. With a total sample of 525 patients, the study will have 80% power to detect a 17% risk difference (Relative Risk=1.36) when comparing the active interventions with treatment as usual. For the non-inferiority analysis, assuming a true 7% difference favouring the reminders and incentives group, we will have 80% power using a non-inferiority margin of 10%.

Outcome measurements and data analysis

The primary outcome will be symptom response at 12 weeks, defined as a ≥50% reduction from baseline depression symptom severity as measured by the PHQ-9. Secondary outcomes will include symptom response at 6 weeks and daily antidepressant adherence at 6 and 12 weeks. The PHQ-9 will be administered by trained assessors via telephone at screening and at 6 and 12 weeks post-enrolment. Assessors collecting follow-up data will be blinded by withholding participant assignment to study condition and are responsible for conducting both 6- and 12-week follow-up assessments with the same participant to help maintain study retention. To analyse the primary outcome, we will use a mixed effects logistic regression model with repeated PHQ-9 response measurements to compare each intervention group with the treatment as usual group and to conduct a non-inferiority test comparing the intervention conditions.57 The model will include terms for study group, time and a group by time interaction term. We will evaluate group differences at the 12-week (primary outcome) and 6-week (secondary outcome) timepoints using contrasts within each timepoint.58

Secondary analyses will assess antidepressant adherence using electronic pill bottles, defined as the number of days with a recorded opening. Adherence data will be wirelessly transmitted to a secure portal and evaluated at 6 and 12 weeks. Participants will be classified as adherent at each timepoint if they take their antidepressant on at least 80% of days during the assessment period. Differences between each intervention and treatment as usual will be evaluated using mixed effects logistic regression models as described for the primary outcome.

All analyses will use an intent-to-treat approach, with participants analysed in their randomised groups regardless of adherence to the intervention. With respect to missing data, based on our pilot study, we anticipate minimal missingness, with a dropout rate of less than 10%. Our proposed mixed-effects modelling approach will provide valid estimates under standard missing-data assumptions.

Current trial status

Recruitment began in March 2025 and a total of 177 patients have been randomised as of March 2026.

Ethics and dissemination

This study is registered on clinicaltrials.gov (NCT06496646) and has been approved by the University of Pennsylvania Institutional Review Board. The protocol aligns with guidance from the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) checklist,59 provided as online supplemental file 2. The study is closely monitored by the principal investigators and the project director and is overseen by an independent Data Safety and Monitoring Board (DSMB) comprised of two physician-scientists and a biostatistician. All safety concerns, adverse events and ethical considerations are reviewed by the team and reported, as applicable, to the DSMB and the Institutional Review Board. De-identified data from this study, including individual-level data and associated documentation, will be submitted on an ongoing basis to the National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH) National Data Archive and will be available to researchers in accordance with NIMH policies.

Discussion

Antidepressant non-adherence represents a major public health burden, and patient-centred interventions to improve adherence may offer a feasible, scalable solution. Although many implementation strategies have focused on targeting clinicians to improve the delivery of evidence-based care,60 there is growing recognition of the benefits of actively involving patients in their own healthcare.61 Our study’s strength is that it evaluates interventions that target patient behaviour, aiming to promote greater patient engagement in their own care and, in turn, improve depression symptoms and medication adherence.62 Importantly, this trial will examine whether low-cost text reminders alone can achieve adherence effects comparable to reminders paired with financial incentives, offering a scalable and cost-efficient approach to improve antidepressant adherence.

Our study has several limitations. First, we are recruiting from a single large regional academic primary care network, and therefore, our study findings may not generalise to patients receiving care in other practice settings. Second, adherence will be measured using electronic pill bottles which, while superior to self-reported measures of adherence,63 still may misclassify medication use. Third, depression outcomes will be assessed using the self-reported PHQ-9, which may not be as accurate as a clinician-administered scale.64 Fourth, the study focuses on antidepressant adherence for major depressive disorder and therefore may not generalise to antidepressant use for other psychiatric conditions. Finally, participants who elect to enrol in the study may not be representative of the broader population of patients prescribed antidepressants, although the randomised design helps mitigate selection bias.

Despite these limitations, the study proposes a promising set of interventions to address antidepressant non-adherence that can be readily adapted into primary care workflows.
