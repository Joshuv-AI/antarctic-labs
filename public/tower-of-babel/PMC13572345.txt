Redox-based dual-substrate fermentation enhances sustainable production of 3-hydroxypropionic acid by recombinant E. coli
Authors: Kala Baskar, Vishnu Prasad J, Guhan Jayaraman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract3-Hydroxypropionic acid (3-HP) is an important platform chemical for a circular bioeconomy since it is a precursor for the production of several high-value derivatives such as acrylic acid as well as biodegradable plastics. Escherichia coli is a promising host for 3-HP biosynthesis due to its well-characterized metabolic network and ease of genetic manipulation. In E. coli, 3-HP is produced from glycerol via a two-step pathway involving glycerol dehydratase and an NAD⁺-dependent aldehyde dehydrogenase. However, a major limitation in this process is redox imbalance, as both glycerol catabolism and 3-HP biosynthesis generate excess NADH, thereby restricting NAD⁺ availability and limiting product formation. In this study, we systematically evaluated the influence of cosubstrate selection to enhance 3-HP production by decoupling biomass formation from product synthesis while achieving redox balance. An engineered E. coli strain, with knockouts of glycerol kinase (glpK) and glycerol dehydrogenase (gldA) to prevent glycerol catabolism, was employed to redirect glycerol flux exclusively toward 3-HP biosynthesis. A secondary carbon source was supplied to support cell growth and promote NAD⁺ regeneration. Six different sugars were evaluated as growth substrates to assess their impact on redox balance and 3-HP production. Among the tested secondary substrates, sucrose and xylose enabled high 3-HP titers of 61 and 50 g/L, respectively, significantly surpassing the 30 g/L titer obtained using glycerol as the sole carbon source. The strategy was successfully extended to renewable feedstocks such as molasses and pretreated lignocellulosic liquor hydrolysate in combination with crude glycerol, achieving 3-HP titers in the range of ~ 35–40 g/L.Key points• Redox imbalance limits glycerol-based 3-HP biosynthesis in E. coli.• Redox-optimized dual-substrate feeding enhances 3-HP titer, yield and productivity.• Validation with renewable substrates provides feasibility for a circular bioeconomy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00253-026-13926-7.

Introduction

3-Hydroxypropionic acid (3-HP) is a versatile platform chemical that can be converted to numerous high-value derivates including acrylic acid, methyl acrylate, acrylamide, malonic acid, acrylonitrile, and 1,3-propanediol which have wide applications in adhesives, coatings, paints, and diaper industries (Borodina et al. 2015; Wang et al. 2023). Furthermore, 3-HP can be polymerized to poly-3-hydroxypropionate which is biodegradable and can replace petrochemical plastics (Sikazwe et al. 2025). Because of these wide commercial applications, 3-HP is estimated to have a production of about 3.6 million tonnes per year and a global market value in excess of 10 billion USD (Joshi et al. 2025). Various studies on microbial production of 3-HP have garnered much attention after this compound was listed by the US Department of Energy as one of the top three platform chemicals that can be produced by biological methods (Werpy and Petersen 2004). There are significant advantages for bio-based production due to low carbon footprint compared to conventional petroleum processes, as well as decreased reliance on petroleum.

Various microorganisms such as Klebsiella pneumoniae (Zhao et al. 2015, 2019; Li et al. 2016), Escherichia coli (Zhang et al. 2023b; Chen et al. 2024), Bacillus subtilis (Garg et al. 2023), and Lactobacillus reuteri (Zabed et al. 2019) have been genetically modified to produce 3-HP from glycerol, which is a by-product of the biodiesel industry. Among these, E. coli and K. pneumoniae have been widely used as production hosts. Although the highest reported 3-HP titer of 110.7 g/L was achieved using K. pneumoniae, its pathogenic nature limits its industrial applicability (Cao et al. 2025). E. coli has emerged as a safer and more tractable host due to its metabolic robustness, well-known genome information, and ability to grow on a broad range of carbon sources (Wang et al. 2023).

E. coli can be engineered to produce 3-HP from glycerol by incorporating a simple two-step pathway. In the first step, glycerol dehydratase converts glycerol into 3-hydroxypropionaldehyde (3-HPA), which is further converted into 3-HP by an NAD+-dependent aldehyde dehydrogenase (Lim et al. 2016). A key bottleneck in this process is the regeneration of NAD+, since 3-HP biosynthesis generates excess NADH. One common strategy to regenerate NAD+ is to couple 3-HP production with 1,3-propanediol (1,3-PDO) production, since propanediol oxidoreductase requires NADH for the conversion of 3-HPA to 1,3-PDO. For example, Zhang et al. (2023a) have maintained redox balance by coproducing 77.37 g/L of 3-HP and 63.16 g/L of 1,3-PDO by an engineered E. coli strain, using glycerol as the sole carbon substrate with a 3-HP yield of 0.43 mol/mol. The same strategy has been applied, albeit with lower titers, with K. pneumoniae (Ko et al. 2017) and with resting cells of L. reuteri (Zabed et al. 2019). However, due to the coproduction of 1,3-PDO, the yield of 3-HP from glycerol is quite low in these processes. Enhancement of 3-HP yield from glycerol requires minimal production of 1,3-PDO and therefore an alternate strategy to regenerate NAD+.

For 3-HP production processes from glycerol, other cofactor engineering strategies have been exploited to enhance NAD+, such as incorporation of NADH oxidase (Li et al. 2013), using a NAD+ independent aldehyde oxidase (Li et al. 2014), and overexpression of niacin-based NAD+ biosynthesis pathway (Wu et al. 2021). However, 3-HP titer remained low (< 40 g/L) in all these studies. Most studies have focused on genetic engineering approaches, with comparatively little attention paid to process-level strategies for redox control. An alternative process-level approach involves dual-substrate feeding, wherein glucose is supplied to support biomass formation and indirectly enhance NAD+ regeneration, while decoupling it from the glycerol supplied for the directed bioconversion to 3-HP. Although promising, prior studies using this method have achieved relatively low titers (~ 40 g/L) (Niu et al. 2017) or suboptimal specific product yield (0.63 g3-HP/gDCW) (Kim et al. 2014). These limitations highlight the need for novel redox-balanced process engineering strategies that can efficiently generate NAD+ and high 3-HP titer.

In this study, we have adopted a dual-substrate feeding strategy to enhance 3-HP production while maintaining redox balance in E. coli. Glycerol is a reductive substrate and glycerol catabolism through the glycolytic pathway generates excess NADH while 3-HP biosynthesis also produces NADH, leading to an imbalance in the cellular NAD+/NADH ratio. Hence, the carbon flux from glycerol is directed toward the production of 3-HP by blocking the flow toward glycolytic pathway by deletion of glpK and gldA genes (Fig. 1). A secondary carbon source is supplied for cell growth, to use the 3-HP-generated excess NADH for biomass production and thereby regenerate NAD+. In this study, six secondary substrates were tested for their ability to effectively regenerate NAD+ and enhance 3-HP titer and yield, with minimal by-product accumulation. By optimizing process parameters and feeding strategies, we successfully coupled biomass formation with 3-HP biosynthesis through redox balancing. Among the tested substrates, sucrose and xylose emerged as the most effective secondary carbon sources, enabling the production of 61.37 g/L and 50.08 g/L 3-HP, respectively. The strategy developed with synthetic substrates was validated with renewable feedstocks. Using molasses or pretreated lignocellulosic liquor (rich in xylose) in combination with pretreated crude glycerol, 3-HP titers of 34.77 g/L and 40.16 g/L were achieved, respectively. Materials and methods

Bacterial strains and plasmids

All the strains and plasmids used in this study are listed in Table S1.1, Supplementary File 1. The strains E. coli W (ATCC9637) and E. coli W ΔglpK ΔgldA and plasmids pDK7_dhaB_gdrAB and pUC19_kgsadh and pACYC were generous gifts from Professor Sunghoon Park, UNIST, Republic of Korea. pUC19 vector was purchased from New England Biolabs, USA.

Construction of recombinant plasmids for 3-HP production

The genes dhaB, gdrAB (from K. pneumoniae DSM 2026), and kgsadh (from Azospirillum brasilense) were codon optimized for E. coli and synthesized from GenScript, USA. The synthesized fragments of dhaB and gdrAB genes encoding glycerol dehydratase and its reactivation factor were cloned into pACYC vector using SalI and NotI restriction enzymes. The T7 promoter in pACYC vector was replaced by tac promoter using Gibson assembly with the corresponding primers (Table S1.2, Supplementary File 1). Codon-optimized kgsadh gene encoding aldehyde dehydrogenase was cloned into pUC19 vector using SalI and BamHI restriction enzymes.

Flask cultivation conditions

All shake-flask studies were carried out with 50-mL media in 250-mL Erlenmeyer flasks, at 30 or 37 ℃ and 250 rpm. For batch shake-flask studies, modified M9 media containing 100 mM glycerol (primary substrate for product formation), 150 C-mM (carbon millimolar) of a secondary carbon substrate, 1 g/L yeast extract, 1 g/L ammonium chloride, 1 g/L sodium chloride, and 0.25 g/L magnesium sulfate heptahydrate in 100 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7) was used. Various secondary carbon substrates such as glucose, xylose, fructose, galactose, sucrose, and lactose were used for supporting the growth of the culture in the modified M9 media. To maintain the equivalence of carbon utilization, 150 C-mM of secondary substrate was added to the flasks. Initial OD of the cells was kept as 0.05. When OD reached 0.6–0.8, the culture was induced with 0.5 mM IPTG. After 45 min of induction, 2 µM of coenzyme B12 was added every 3 h, until 12 h.

For fed-batch shake-flask studies with intermittent pH adjustment, modified M9 media with 5 g/L yeast extract, 2 g/L ammonium chloride, 2 g/L of sodium chloride, and 0.5 g/L magnesium sulfate heptahydrate in 100 mM potassium phosphate buffer was used. Initial concentration of carbon substrates in the media was kept as 100 mM glycerol and 150 C-mM of the secondary substrate. 100 mM glycerol alone was pulsed at 9 h and 15 h, while the secondary substrate was not added further. The culture was induced at 1 OD with 0.5 mM IPTG, and pH was adjusted to 7 every 3 h (until 15 h) using 2 M sodium hydroxide solution. 2 µM coenzyme B12 was added every 3 h (until 12 h), and the samples were taken at regular time intervals.

Bioreactor cultivation conditions

All the bioreactor studies were carried out in a 2.4-L bioreactor (Biojenik Engineering, India) with an initial working volume of 1 L. Modified M9 media was used with initial concentration of 100 mM glycerol, 5 g/L xylose or sucrose, 5 g/L yeast extract, 2 g/L ammonium chloride, 2 g/L of sodium chloride, and 2 g/L magnesium sulfate heptahydrate, buffered with 100 mM potassium phosphate. A single colony from the agar plate was inoculated in 5-mL Luria Broth (Miller) which is then subcultured overnight at 37 ℃ in 100-mL modified M9 media. This overnight inoculum was added to the bioreactor, such that the starting OD is 0.1. Cells were induced at 6 ± 0.5 OD with 0.5 mM IPTG. After 45 min of induction, 2 mM coenzyme B12 was added every 3 h until the end of fermentation. For constant aeration experiments, agitation was maintained at 450 rpm with an air flow rate of 2 vvm. In dissolved oxygen (DO)–controlled experiments, the initial conditions were set to 450 rpm and 2 vvm until the DO level dropped to 5–10%, and then, it was maintained at 5 or 10% using cascade control. The pH was maintained at 7.0 through automated addition of 8 M sodium hydroxide solution. For fed-batch bioreactor experiments, feeding was performed using a pH-stat strategy for glycerol (8 M) and a DO-stat strategy for sucrose or xylose (60% w/v). Additional media components were supplied at regular intervals as required. Polypropylene glycol 2000 was added as required during the fermentation to control foaming.

Analytical methods

Determination of biomass concentration

To estimate biomass concentration, 1 mL of sample was centrifuged at 10,000 rpm and the pellet was resuspended in 0.9% NaCl. Optical density was measured at 600 nm with appropriate dilutions using an UV-visible spectrophotometer (Jasco, Japan). Optical density was calibrated against dry cell weight (DCW) with 1 OD corresponding to 0.37 gDCW/L.

Determination of metabolite concentration

Extracellular metabolites such as sugars, glycerol, 3-HP, 1,3-PDO, and acetate were quantified by using HPLC equipped with RID and PDA detectors (Shimadzu, Japan). For this, the fermentation samples were centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 5 min and the supernatant was diluted and filtered using a 0.22-µm filter membrane. Xylose, glucose, galactose, fructose, glycerol, 3-HP, 1,3-PDO, and acetate were estimated using two columns connected in series—Phenomenex Luna C18 column (250 mm × 4.6 mm) followed by Phenomenex Rezex ROA column (300 mm × 7.8 mm), with mobile phase containing 2.5 mM sulfuric acid at 0.4 mL/min and 40 ℃. For sucrose estimation, the centrifuged supernatant was diluted and hydrolyzed with invertase enzyme (Sigma Aldrich) at 55 ℃ for 15 min, filtered, and then injected into the HPLC; the resultant glucose and fructose peaks were quantified to estimate sucrose. Lactose and lactate were estimated by Phenomenex Rezex ROA column (300 mm × 7.8 mm), using isocratic elution with 5 mM sulfuric acid at 50 ℃ and 0.6 mL/min.

Determination of NAD+/NADH ratio

NAD+ and NADH concentrations were determined using the Sigma-Aldrich NAD/NADH assay kit (MAK468) with slight modifications in the manufacturer’s protocol. Sample volumes collected at different time intervals were adjusted such that the harvested biomass corresponded to ~ 3 mg DCW. The cells were centrifuged at 6000 rpm and 4 ℃ for 10 min. The pellet was then washed with ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.5) and then resuspended in NAD or NADH extraction buffer followed by heat extraction and quantification according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Pretreatment of lignocellulosic biomass

Corncob granules (particle size 2–3 mm) were provided by Sparconn Life Sciences (Bengaluru, India). Pretreatment was performed based on the protocol described by Vishnu Prasad et al. (2020), with minor modifications. Corncob biomass (375 g) was soaked in distilled water for 18 h at room temperature, at a solid loading of 15% (w/v). It was then transferred to a 5-L high-pressure reactor (Amar Equipment Pvt. Ltd., Mumbai, India) and subjected to hydrothermal pretreatment at 175 ± 5 °C for 30 min, followed by rapid decompression to atmospheric pressure by opening the pressure release valve. This pretreatment process predominantly solubilizes hemicellulose with minimal hydrolysis of the cellulose and lignin fraction. Therefore, after pretreatment, we obtain a solid fraction that is rich in cellulose and lignin and a liquid fraction containing hemicellulose (~ 600-mL pretreated liquor). The pretreatment conditions were optimized to minimize the degradation of hemicellulose-derived sugars to furfural/hydroxymethylfurfural (HMF), thereby retaining the bulk of hemicellulose as xylo-oligosaccharides in the pretreated liquor. The pretreated liquor contained ~ 4 g/L of xylose as the predominant monosaccharide along with low concentrations of HMF and furfural (1.25–1.5 g/L).

Enzymatic hydrolysis of pretreated liquor

To facilitate the conversion of xylo-oligosaccharides present in the pretreated liquor, enzymatic hydrolysis was carried out using SacchariSEB C6L Plus, a commercial cellulase enzyme complex obtained from Advanced Enzymes Technologies Ltd. The pH of the pretreated liquor was adjusted to 4.8 and preincubated without enzyme addition at 50 °C and 150 rpm for 30 min. Enzyme solution was then added at 0.75 filter paper units (FPU) per mL of pretreated liquor and incubated at 50 °C and 150 rpm for 24 h. Since this pretreated liquor predominantly contained xylo-oligosaccharides, enzymatic hydrolysis of this liquor resulted in a xylose-rich fraction with minimal glucose content. The obtained pretreated liquor hydrolysate was found to contain ~ 42 g/L xylose and 5 g/L glucose, which was further concentrated (~ eightfold) by evaporation at 80 ℃ in a water bath to ~ 330 g/L xylose and 40 g/L glucose. This concentrated pretreated liquor hydrolysate (PLH) was autoclaved at 115 ℃ for 10 min and used for the production studies.

Pretreatment of crude glycerol

Crude glycerol was obtained from the Centre for Waste Management, Sathyabama Institute of Science and Technology, Chennai. This is a by-product of biodiesel produced from used cooking oil. Using crude glycerol directly resulted in precipitate formation in M9 media, thereby interfering with OD measurement. Therefore, crude glycerol was pretreated to remove the impurities and fatty acids. Initially, crude glycerol was heated at 70 ℃ to remove methanol. The residual oil fraction present in crude glycerol was extracted overnight with an equal volume of hexane. This results in the formation of three layers—the bottom layer containing glycerol, the middle layer containing free fatty acids as soap, and the top layer containing oil fraction. From this, the glycerol fraction was separated using a separating funnel and subjected to activated charcoal treatment (10% w/v). The resulting pretreated crude glycerol (PCG) has a concentration of approximately 8 M which was autoclaved and used for further studies.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0. All the fermentation experiments using synthetic substrates were carried out at least in duplicates, and the analyses for intracellular NAD(H) levels were carried out in triplicates. The results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. The statistical significance for the intracellular NAD(H) levels was determined using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for group comparisons.

Results

Strain engineering for dual-substrate consumption

To alleviate the redox limitations imposed by using glycerol as sole carbon source (see Section 1, Supplementary File 2), a pathway engineering strategy was employed to decouple 3-HP pathway from biomass formation and redirect glycerol exclusively toward 3-HP synthesis. To achieve this, we used the strain E. coli W ΔglpK ΔgldA, wherein the native glpK and gldA genes have been deleted to block glycerol entry into central carbon metabolism. This strain necessitates the provision of a secondary carbon source to support biomass formation (Fig. 1).

In this framework, glycerol serves primarily as a NADH-generating substrate for 3-HP production, while biomass formation from a secondary carbon substrate contributes to NAD⁺ regeneration. By balancing NADH production and its consumption (NAD+ regeneration), this dual-substrate feeding enables concomitant and sustained growth and product formation through redox coupling.

To execute the above strategy, we obtained the recombinant strain EC2_DUBGK from Prof. Sunghoon Park (Ulsan National Institute of Science and Technology, Republic of Korea), in which 3-HP production was engineered by overexpression of dhaB, gdrAB, and kgsadh genes in the E. coli W ΔglpK ΔgldA strain. The dhaB and gdrAB genes were expressed in a low-copy-number pDK7 vector. In contrast, the kgsadh gene was expressed in high-copy-number pUC19 vector to facilitate efficient downstream conversion of 3-HPA to 3-HP and prevent 3-HPA accumulation.

To assess 3-HP production by the recombinant EC2_DUBGK strain, both batch and fed-batch shake-flask studies were performed in modified M9 media supplemented with 25 mM glucose for biomass formation and 100 mM glycerol for 3-HP production. In batch-flask studies, the EC2_DUBGK strain produced 42 mM 3-HP and 28 mM of 1,3-PDO, whereas in fed-batch shake-flask studies, the strain produced 110 mM 3-HP and 43 mM 1,3-PDO (Table S1.3, Supplementary File 1).

To enhance 3-HP production, all the three pathway genes (dhaB, gdrAB, and kgsadh) were codon optimized for expression in E. coli. Since empty pDK7 vector was not commercially available, the codon-optimized dhaB and gdrAB genes were cloned into a modified pACYC vector. Since the original pDK7 vector used a tac promoter (more suitable for expression in E. coli W host), we replaced the native T7 promoter in the pACYC vector with the tac promoter via Gibson assembly. The kgsadh gene was cloned separately into pUC19 vector. Both these plasmids were transformed into E. coli W Δglpk ΔgldA thereby generating EC2_KG23 strain.

In batch shake-flask experiments, the EC2_KG23 strain produced 45.4 mM 3-HP, which was comparable to the EC2_DUBGK strain. However, 1,3-PDO formation was significantly reduced to 17.8 mM in the EC2_KG23 culture, indicating a shift in metabolic flux away from the reductive branch of glycerol metabolism (Table S1.3, Supplementary File 1). Under fed-batch shake-flask conditions, the EC2_KG23 strain achieved a 3-HP titer of 155 mM, representing approximately 40% increase compared to EC2_DUBGK—while 1,3-PDO level was marginally lower (37.3 mM). Consistent with the observed increase in product titer, the molar yield of 3-HP from glycerol improved from 0.72 mol/mol in EC2_DUBGK to 0.80 mol/mol in EC2_KG23. These results clearly indicate that codon optimization of all the three genes has resulted in enhanced 3-HP titer and yield. Based on these findings, the EC2_KG23 strain was selected for further studies.

In addition, the optimal condition for 3-HP production by the EC2_KG23 strain was found to be 30 ℃ and a pH window of 7–7.5 (see Section 2, Supplementary File 2).

Effect of various secondary carbon substrates on 3-HP production

Decoupling of the primary and secondary substrate utilization in the EC2_KG23 strain gave us the flexibility to evaluate the effects of different secondary carbon substrates on 3-HP production. Six sugar substrates, viz., glucose, xylose, galactose, fructose, sucrose, and lactose, were chosen for our studies. Initial concentration of all the substrates was normalized to equivalent carbon-moles (150 C-mM) to ensure direct comparison of yield coefficients and other metabolic parameters.

While biomass formation remained comparable across all conditions (Table 1), substantial differences were observed in product distribution in fed-batch shake-flask studies. Cultures grown on xylose, galactose, fructose, and sucrose achieved higher 3-HP titer (190–200 mM) than those grown on glucose or lactose (160–180 mM). Among these, xylose and sucrose favored the most efficient 3-HP production with minimal accumulation of acetate and 1,3-PDO. The suppression of acetate formation with xylose and sucrose as substrates indicates a more effective carbon utilization of the secondary substrate. Since 1,3-PDO formation serves as a major NADH-consuming pathway during glycerol metabolism, the reduced accumulation of 1,3-PDO suggests more effective NADH reoxidation through growth-associated pathways when the cells were grown on xylose and sucrose. In contrast, cultures grown on other sugars exhibited increased by-product accumulation, suggesting suboptimal redox balancing and carbon flux distribution. In particular, xylose and sucrose minimized carbon loss to undesired by-products, thereby representing optimal cosubstrates for high-yield 3-HP biosynthesis.

Fed-batch bioreactor studies

Fed-batch bioreactor experiments were carried out with the high-yield secondary substrates, sucrose and xylose, to further enhance the 3-HP titers. These experiments required feeding of dual substrates, viz., glycerol for product formation and a secondary substrate for growth. Although the consumption of the two substrates was decoupled from each other, growth and product formation occurred concomitantly, coupled by the necessity to maintain redox balance. Fed-batch bioreactor studies were initially carried out with sucrose and glycerol at constant aeration and agitation to evaluate the performance of the recombinant strain EC2_KG23. In these experiments, the cultivation medium initially contained 5 g/L sucrose and 50 mM glycerol. Upon reaching the mid-exponential phase of growth in the initial batch culture mode, 3-HP pathway genes were induced with 0.5 mM IPTG, after which the process was shifted to fed-batch mode. Glycerol was supplied continuously from a 10 M reservoir at a constant flow rate of 2.4 mL/h, from the point of induction until 24 h, after which it was reduced to 1.2 mL/h until the end of fermentation. In contrast, sucrose was pulse-fed in response to sharp increases in dissolved oxygen (DO), which indicated carbon limitation.

Under these conditions, the cells consumed 24.5 mM sucrose and reached a maximum OD of ~ 8.6 and produced 216.6 mM 3-HP (Fig. 2). Following induction, substantial accumulation of acetate and 1,3-PDO was observed, and cell growth ceased after approximately 15 h. Optimization of dissolved oxygen level for 3-HP production

The DhaB enzyme, which catalyzes the conversion of glycerol to 3-hydroxypropionaldehyde, is oxygen sensitive, whereas efficient 3-HP production requires aerobic conditions for NADH oxidation. Therefore, DO levels were controlled in subsequent experiments, at defined set points. DO level was controlled at either 5% or 10% using cascade control. Following induction, a DO-stat-based sucrose feeding strategy was manually implemented to support biomass formation and NAD⁺ regeneration, while glycerol feeding was regulated using a pH-stat approach. The culture pH was maintained at 7.0 through automated cofeeding of NaOH and glycerol. By coupling glycerol consumption (and 3-HP formation) with pH maintenance, the glycerol concentration in the reactor was kept reasonably constant (100 mM).

At 5% DO, this strategy resulted in the highest 3-HP production, yielding 557 mM (50.8 g/L) 3-HP in 30 h, with a molar yield of 0.93 mol/mol while limiting by-product formation to 26.6 mM acetate and 43 mM 1,3-PDO (Fig. 3a). Increasing the DO level set point to 10% did not substantially improve the 3-HP titer or yield but led to increased accumulation of acetate (57.4 mM) (Fig. S1.1, Supplementary File 1). Moreover, the volumetric productivity decreased from 2.12 g/L/h at 5% DO to 1.5 g/L/h at 10% DO, likely due to oxygen sensitivity of the DhaB enzyme responsible for converting glycerol to 3-HPA (Zhao et al. 2015; Kim et al. 2020). When the cells grow in sucrose, it is observed that the NAD+ concentration is high before induction, resulting in a NAD+/NADH ratio of 9.59 (Fig. 3c). After induction at 6 h, rapid 3-HP formation resulted in decreasing the NAD+ pool from 6.67 to 3.09 µmol/gDCW by 13 h. Effect of mixed sugars on 3-HP production

We wanted to investigate 3-HP production from renewable feedstocks, including crude glycerol, molasses, and PLH obtained from lignocellulosic biomass (LCB). Since both molasses and PLH contain mixed sugars, the influence of synthetic mixed sugars was investigated on cellular metabolism and 3-HP production through fed-batch shake-flask studies.

The individual and combined effects of glucose and fructose in the presence of sucrose (the sugar mix in molasses) were first investigated. Supplementing sucrose with glucose or fructose resulted in elevated 1,3-PDO levels indicating a shift in redox balance toward reductive by-product pathways (Table 2). To evaluate the combined effect of fructose and glucose present in molasses, these were provided along with sucrose in proportions mimicking the molasses composition (total sugar in molasses—630 g/L containing 53% sucrose, 27% fructose, and 20% glucose). Under these conditions, 3-HP titer decreased by 20.3% in comparison to media containing only sucrose and glycerol. This was accompanied by higher concentrations of 1,3-PDO while acetate levels and biomass formation remain largely unchanged (Table 2).

Evaluation of renewable feedstocks for dual-substrate 3-HP production

Following the assessment of the effects of mixed sugars in EC2_KG23 strain, renewable feedstocks were evaluated to determine the robustness of the dual-substrate strategy under industrially relevant conditions. When the culture was supplemented with molasses as a secondary substrate in combination with synthetic glycerol in fed-batch shake-flask experiments, 141 mM of 3-HP was produced. This was accompanied by a marginal increase in 1,3-PDO accumulation (48.3 mM), while acetate production and biomass formation remained comparable to those observed with corresponding sugar mixtures (Table 2). On the other hand, supplementation of PCG along with molasses resulted in 3-HP titers and by-product profiles which were comparable to those of molasses and synthetic glycerol. The dual-substrate feed of molasses and crude glycerol resulted in ~ 87% of the 3-HP titer obtained with synthetic mixture containing sucrose, glucose, and fructose.

In the case of LCB-derived substrates, supplementation with PLH in combination with crude glycerol produced metabolite profiles comparable to those observed with corresponding synthetic sugar mixtures (Table 3). This suggests that the pretreatment strategies for LCB and crude glycerol effectively minimized the presence of inhibitory compounds, enabling efficient substrate utilization by the EC2_KG23 strain.

These findings (summarized in Tables 2 and 3) highlight the critical role of sugar composition in determining carbon flux distribution and redox balance during 3-HP production. While renewable feedstocks such as molasses, PLH, and PCG are compatible with the dual-substrate strategy, the presence of mixed sugars in the renewable substrates can adversely impact 3-HP production efficiency by promoting reductive by-product formation.

Fed-batch bioreactor studies with renewable substrates

Since promising results were obtained in the preliminary fed-batch shake-flask studies using renewable feedstocks, we evaluated their efficacy for 3-HP production under fed-batch bioreactor conditions.

Implementation of the dual-substrate feeding strategy using molasses as the growth-supporting substrate and PCG for 3-HP synthesis in EC2_KG23 culture resulted in the production of 385 mM 3-HP (34.7 g/L), accompanied by 54 mM 1,3-PDO (Fig. 6a). During fermentation, approximately 120 mL of molasses feed corresponding to a total of 400 mM hexose equivalents was fed into the reactor along with ~ 66 mL of PCG. Since molasses contains a mixture of sucrose, glucose, and fructose, the total sugar fed during fermentation was expressed as hexose equivalents rather than as individual sugar concentrations. The molar yield of 3-HP from glycerol reached 0.87 mol/mol. This corresponds to 56% of the 3-HP titer and 94.5% of the yield achieved with defined synthetic sucrose–glycerol substrates. The mixed sugars present in molasses did not lead to a substantial increase in by-product accumulation unlike fed-batch shake-flask studies. However, 3-HP titer has reduced compared to synthetic feedstocks which may be attributed to the complex composition of molasses. Previous studies have reported that the presence of nonsugar components, including heavy metals, ash, and processing-derived contaminants, in molasses can affect microbial growth and product formation (Liu et al. 2008; Wang et al. 2017). However, since the molasses used in this study was not characterized for the impurities’ profile, the specific cause of the reduced titer remains unclear. Discussion

Despite extensive efforts toward microbial production of 3-HP, only a limited number of studies have reported titers exceeding 60 g/L while simultaneously achieving high productivity and yield. This is notable given that the glycerol to 3-HP pathway is among the simplest biosynthetic routes, involving only two enzymatic steps, and can be readily introduced into diverse microbial hosts. Nevertheless, several challenges still exist for achieving industrially relevant titers—including product toxicity, strain tolerance, and efficient NAD⁺ regeneration. Among these, redox imbalance remains a central bottleneck, as both glycerol metabolism and 3-HP biosynthesis impose a substantial burden on the intracellular NAD⁺/NADH pool. Although numerous genetic approaches to enhance NAD⁺ availability have been reported, the resulting 3-HP titers have generally remained modest. This indicates the need for complementary process strategies that enable sustained redox balance during 3-HP production (Li et al. 2013, 2014; Wu et al. 2021).

In the present study, we addressed the redox management problem through a bioprocess-oriented strategy rather than relying solely on strain-level cofactor engineering. We implemented a dual-substrate fermentation strategy in which glycerol was directed exclusively to 3-HP biosynthesis, while a secondary carbon source was provided to support biomass formation and NAD⁺ regeneration. This decoupling was genetically enforced by deleting glpK and gldA, thereby preventing the entry of glycerol into glycolysis.

Conventionally, glucose has been employed as a cosubstrate alongside glycerol to enhance growth and indirectly improve 3-HP production. However, previous studies using this approach have reported either relatively low titers or low specific yields. For example, although biomass concentrations as high as 91.4 g/L have been achieved, 3-HP titers were limited to approximately 57.3 g/L (636 mM), corresponding to a specific product yield of less than 0.7 g 3-HP per g biomass (Kim et al. 2014). Other reports have documented even lower titers, often below 45 g/L (Lim et al. 2016; Niu et al. 2017). These observations suggest that glucose-supported growth is ineffective for resolving the redox constraints inherent to glycerol-based 3-HP production.

In this study, we systematically evaluated six alternative sugar substrates as secondary carbon sources for biomass formation, with selection criteria based on enhanced 3-HP production and minimal accumulation of 1,3-PDO, a known redox sink for excess intracellular NADH. Among the tested substrates, sucrose and xylose consistently outperformed others, enabling 3-HP yields exceeding 90% and substantially reduced by-product formation. This is further highlighted by intracellular NAD+/NADH ratios during fed-batch fermentation. When glycerol was supplied as the sole carbon source for both growth and 3-HP production (in the EC1_KG23 strain), NADH levels were elevated even prior to induction, resulting in a low NAD⁺/NADH ratio of 2.57 (Section 1, Supplementary File 2). In contrast, sucrose- and xylose-grown cultures of EC2_KG23 exhibited substantially higher preinduction NAD⁺/NADH ratios of 9.59 and 4.68, respectively. Under glycerol-only conditions, the specific productivity of 3-HP declined progressively over time, indicating an intrinsic inability of the cells to simultaneously maintain redox balance and sustain 3-HP biosynthesis. In contrast, dual-substrate feeding maintained the NAD⁺/NADH ratio throughout fermentation, with minimal 1,3-PDO accumulation, confirming effective redirection of carbon flux toward 3-HP.

Nevertheless, at high product concentrations (> 400 mM), severe growth inhibition and reduced sugar uptake were observed, indicating product inhibition (Nguyen-Vo et al. 2019). At this stage, the addition of glucose transiently restored substrate uptake and enhanced 3-HP production without significantly increasing biomass. When the EC2_KG23 strain was subjected to grow in various substrates under various 3-HP concentrations, it has been observed that the growth rate of the sucrose or xylose-grown cells was drastically affected at 3-HP concentrations exceeding 400 mM (Fig S1.2, Supplementary File 1). On the other hand, glucose-grown cultures exhibited greater tolerance toward 3-HP and were able to grow at 3-HP concentrations of 500 mM. This response is likely linked to sugar transport energetics under acid stress. While detailed studies on acid stress effects on sucrose and xylose transporters are limited, the lactose permease LacY is known to depend on the proton motive force, which collapses under acidic conditions due to increased proton influx (West 1970). Similar impairment is plausible for permease-mediated sucrose uptake at high 3-HP concentrations (Vadyvaloo et al. 2006; Guan and Liu 2020). In contrast, glucose uptake via the phosphotransferase system relies on transfer of phosphate group rather than proton gradients, enabling continued uptake under stress conditions. Supporting this interpretation, previous studies have reported cessation of xylose uptake during late-stage 3-HP fermentation, while glucose uptake remains active (Jung et al. 2015; Heo et al. 2019). Moreover, the xylose transporter (XylFGH) incurs an energetic cost during xylose transport (Zhu et al. 2022), and stressed cells require ATP for tolerance mechanisms (Guan and Liu 2020). This further explains the beneficial effect of glucose pulsing in restoring metabolic activity.

Another critical parameter influencing process performance is DO level, given the oxygen sensitivity of glycerol dehydratase (DhaB) and the requirement for aerobic NADH oxidation. While high DO levels (20–30%) have been reported to enhance 3-HP production in alternative pathways (Batista et al. 2024; Chen et al. 2024), fine-tuning oxygen availability is particularly important for DhaB-dependent systems. Kim et al (2020) reported that their engineered E. coli strain resulted in enhanced 3-HP titer at 20% DO but with increased acetate accumulation compared to 10% DO. However, after optimizing the ribosomal binding site for ydcW gene that encodes for the conversion of 3-HPA to 3-HP, they reported that 3-HP titer dropped from 781 mM (70.4 g/L) at 10% DO to 646 mM (58.2 g/L) at 20% DO due to reduced glycerol uptake (Kim et al. 2020). Consistent with this report, we observed that increasing DO level from 5 to 10% led to elevated acetate accumulation and a marked decrease in volumetric productivity, likely due to a reduction in glycerol uptake caused by oxidative inactivation of DhaB. Under optimized conditions (5% DO), EC2_KG23 achieved 681 mM (61.37 g/L) 3-HP with sucrose–glycerol feeding (Fig. 3a) and 50.08 g/L with xylose–glycerol feeding (Fig. 4a).

In the final part of this study, the industrial relevance of the dual-substrate strategy was demonstrated by extending it to renewable feedstocks. To date, only a few studies have reported 3-HP production above 25 g/L from renewable substrates (Table S1.4, Supplementary File 1). Using PCG in combination with molasses or PLH, we achieved 3-HP titers of 385 mM (34.7 g/L) and 454 mM (40.9 g/L), respectively. Molasses-supported fermentations yielded lower titers than defined substrates, likely due to the presence of inhibitory components, suggesting that further pretreatment may be beneficial. The process with lignocellulosic liquor–based substrate achieved approximately 77% of the 3-HP titer and 96% of the yield obtained with synthetic xylose–glycerol feedstocks, demonstrating that the redox-coupled growth and production framework remains effective even in complex substrate environments. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study demonstrating the production of 3-HP from the pretreated liquor hydrolysate fraction, representing the effective utilization of xylose-rich stream from lignocellulosic biomass.

Recently, Zhang et al. (2023a) have demonstrated that alleviating redox imbalance by coproduction of 3-HP and 1,3-PDO through extensive strain engineering has effectively enhanced 3-HP titer to 855 mM (77 g/L) in E. coli. However, the carbon flux from glycerol is evenly distributed among both 3-HP and 1,3-PDO and thus 3-HP yield and productivity are moderate. Here, we demonstrate that a redox-based dual-substrate feeding with minimal strain engineering can alleviate the redox imbalance, thereby enhancing 3-HP production. Although the reported titer is lower than the highest literature value, this strategy offers a complementary approach with better product selectivity, conversion efficiency, and productivity than reported by Zhang et al. (2023a). Our feeding strategy was also extended to renewable feedstocks and moderately high titers were obtained. With appropriate pretreatment strategies, we expect to enhance 3-HP titers and yields similar to those obtained with defined substrates. Overall, these results establish the feasibility of dual-substrate fermentation for 3-HP production from renewable feedstocks and provide a foundation for future process optimization, feedstock integration, and technoeconomic evaluation toward sustainable biomanufacturing and a circular bioeconomy.
