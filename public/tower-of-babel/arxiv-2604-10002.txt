A generalization of the inverse mapping theorem in infinite dimensions
Sajjad Lakzian

Abstract
. We present a generalization of the inverse mapping theorem, where variations of a weaker non-expansiveness property (referred to as property 𝖠 {\sf A} ) replace the key 𝖢 1 \mathsf{C}^{1} condition. We also obtain inverse mapping theorems that can be applied to non-smooth maps. Also as a by-product of the generalized inverse mapping theorem, we prove generalizations of the implicit function theorem and existence and uniqueness theorem of abstract PDE systems as well.

1.
Introduction and Main Results

Let
\left(\mathcal{B},\|\cdot\|\right)
be a Banach space. The classical inverse mapping theorem (IMT) states that a differentiable
\mathsf{C}^{1}
map
f:U\subset\mathcal{B}\to\mathcal{B}
(where
U
is an open domain) with invertible (Frechét) derivatives everywhere, is a local diffeomorphism. It is a folklore fact that the optimal regularity requirements for the IMT to hold, must strictly lie somewhere between mere differentiability and being
\mathsf{C}^{1}
.

The continuity of the Fréchet derivative is a key assumption that can be removed when
\mathcal{B}
is finite dimensional; this is attributed to Černavskiĭ
[
3
]
and is known as the everywhere differentiable IMT. Alternative proofs were presented in
[
19
,
25
]
. All the proofs in the aforementioned literature heavily rely on the local compactness of (a finite dimensional)
\mathcal{B}
which characteristically fails in infinite dimensions. Yet as we will see, still local weak compactness can be utilized to get a generalized IMT.

There is an extensive literature concerning generalizations of IMT “beyond the
\mathsf{C}^{1}
realm in infinite dimensions” and/or “beyond the everywhere differentiable with non-degenrate derivative in finite dimensions”. Three major approaches are to either

•

assume the non-degeneracy of stronger notions of derivatives
: examples in this direction are abundant. We will mention a few important ones. Clarke’s IMT in finite dimensions that applies to Lipschitz maps and only requires the non-degeneracy of Clarke’s generalized derivatives
[
5
]
. In
[
12
]
, an IMT is proven that works for continuous self maps of reflexive Banach spaces yet it requires the non-degeneracy of a set-valued derivative. Using strong derivatives (or strict derivative), there is a host of IMTs for set-valued maps expressed in terms of strong and metric regularity including Bartle-Graves and Lyusternik-Graves theorems; see
[
9
]
.

•

to consider weak derivatives but imposing stronger non-degeneracy conditions
: This approach works best in finite dimensions and usually involves integrability assumptions on the distortion of maps. For example local inversion of Sobolev maps falls in this category. See e.g.
[
13
,
17
]
and the references therein.

•

to impose appropriate contraction assumptions that are weaker than being
\mathsf{C}^{1}
: generalizations that utilize this approach, typically require imposing some sort of a “contraction” assumption that will allow the use of the Banach fixed point theorem. For example, see
[
18
]
for an overview of the proof of a standard version of IMT for Lipschitz maps with this approach.

As previously mentioned, infinite dimensional Banach spaces are not locally compact but a large class of them consists of those that are locally weakly compact. Our aim in these notes, is to take the third approach by imposing a (quasi)-non-expansiveness property (unlike the previous works in which strict contraction is required) in combination with weak local compactness.

1.1.
Property A

Notation:
Throughout,
\mathcal{B}_{i}
s denote Banach spaces. Unless otherwise stated,
f:U\subset\mathcal{B}_{1}\to\mathcal{B}_{2}
will denote a map from an open subset of
\mathcal{B}_{1}
to
\mathcal{B}_{2}
. The domain of
f
will be denoted by
\mathsf{Dom}(f)
, mainly to emphasize the cases where the domain is not necessarily open.

It is easy to see that for a
\mathsf{C}^{1}
map
f
with an invertible derivative, the following two properties hold true:

•

For every
a
, the function
x\mapsto x-(D_{a}f)^{-1}f(x)
is
L_{a}
-Lipschitz for some
L_{a}<1
on a ball

for some sufficiently small

;

•

By making

smaller if necessary, for all

, and all

, the map

is a contracting self-map of

, see Remark
1.4
. The Lipschitz constant

Note the elementary fact that

Definition 1.1

(Property
{\sf A}
)
.

f:\mathsf{Dom}(f)\subset\mathcal{B}_{1}\to\mathcal{B}_{2}

{\sf A}
,
{\sf A}

is respectively

•

a contractive self map of

i.e. a map with a Lipschitz constant less than
1
.

•

a non-expansive self map of

i.e. a map with a Lipschitz constant
1
;

•

either without fixed points or is "quasi-nonexpansive" (see §

Convention:
At a general point

(not necessarily in the interior) of the domain, the aforementioned properties hold if

admits a local extension that satisfies the said property at

.

Terminology:
When the injective maps

can be taken to be linear, we refer to the property as (strong, weak)
{\sf LinA}
.

Based on the observation preceding to Definition

, the property

for a

map holds on all sufficiently small scales (determined locally uniformly in

Definition 1.2
.

Suppose

on a set

•

forms a basis for the topology of

.

•

We say

on

, (locally) uniformly on all scales when there exist (locally) uniform (in

\alpha_{a},\beta_{a},\eta_{a},\gamma_{a}>0

\eta_{a}\leq\gamma_{a}

\mathrm{dist}(a,\partial C_{a})\geq\beta_{a}\diam(C_{a}),\hskip 9.24994pts_{a,C_{a}}\geq\alpha_{a}\diam(C_{a}),\hskip 9.24994pt\text{and},\hskip 9.24994pt\eta_{a}r\leq\diam(C_{a})\leq\gamma_{a}r.

Convention:
For general domains (not necessarily open), we say

satisfies (strong, weak)
{\sf A}
on all scales when it admits local extensions that do so. Similarly one defines (strong, weak)
{\sf A}
locally uniformly on all scales for general domains.

Lemma 1.3

(pairing property)
.

Suppose
f^{1}:\mathcal{B}_{1}\to\mathcal{B}_{2}
satisfies (weak,strong)
A
and
f^{2}:\mathcal{B}_{3}\to\mathcal{B}_{4}
is
\mathsf{C}^{1}
then

(1)

the map
(f^{1},f^{2}):\mathcal{B}_{1}\times\mathcal{B}_{3}\to\mathcal{B}_{2}\times\mathcal{B}_{4}
satisfies (weak, strong)
A
;

(2)

if
f^{2}
satisfies (weak, strong)
A
everywhere on all scales then so does
(f^{1},f^{2})
.

Proof
.

Based on Remark
1.4
,
f^{2}
satisfies strong
A
everywhere on all scales. Let
\tau_{a}=\frac{1}{2}\diam(C_{a})
.

Let
a\in\mathcal{B}_{1}
and
b\in\mathcal{B}_{3}
.
Note that
C_{a}\times B_{\tau_{a}}(b)
is also a bounded and convex set with

\diam\left(C_{a}\times B_{\tau_{a}}(b)\right)=\sqrt{2}\diam\left(C_{a}\right).

It is straightforward to see that

\widetilde{(f^{1},f^{2})}_{(a,b),(y_{1},y_{2}),(A_{a},(D_{b}f^{2})^{-1}),C_{a}\times B_{\tau_{a}}(b)}=\left(\widetilde{f^{1}}_{a,y_{1},A_{a,C_{a}}},\widetilde{f^{2}}_{a_{2},y_{2},(D_{b}f^{2})^{-1}}\right),

for all
(y_{1},y_{2})\in B_{s}((a,b))
for sufficiently small
s
depending on
C_{b}
and
\diam(C_{b})
only.

Item (1) follows due to the following elementary facts:

•

The Lipschitz constant of
(\tilde{f^{1}},\tilde{f}^{2})
is the minimum of the Lipschitz constants of the constituent maps

•

The map
(\tilde{f^{1}},\tilde{f}^{2})
has a fixed point when both
\tilde{f^{1}}
and
\tilde{f^{2}}
have fixed points.

•

If
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
are respective fixed points then

\displaystyle\|(\tilde{f^{1}}(x_{1}),\tilde{f}^{2}(x_{2}))-(p_{1},p_{2})\|

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\|f^{1}(x_{1})-p_{1}\|^{2}+\|f^{2}(x_{2})-p_{2}\|^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\|x_{1}-p_{1}\|^{2}+\|x_{2}-p_{2}\|^{2}}

\displaystyle=\|(x_{1},x_{2})-(p_{1},p_{2})\|

Item (2) follows by considering the following facts:

•

Cartesian product of two topology bases is a topology basis for the product topology;

•

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}\left((a,b),\partial\left(C_{a}\times B_{\tau_{a}}(b)\right)\right)\geq\min\left\{\beta_{a},\beta_{b}=\frac{1}{2}\right\}\diam(C_{a})

•

\displaystyle s_{(a,b),C_{a}\times B_{\tau_{a}}(b)}\geq\min\left\{\alpha_{a},\frac{1}{2}\right\}\diam{C_{a}}

•

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\min\left\{\eta_{a},1\right\}r\leq\diam\left(C_{a}\times B_{\tau_{a}}(b)\right)\leq\sqrt{2}\max\left\{\gamma_{a},1\right\}r

Hence one can take

\displaystyle\beta_{(a,b)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\min\{\beta_{a},\beta_{b}=\frac{1}{2}\},\hskip 9.24994pt\alpha_{(a,b)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\min\left\{\alpha_{a},\frac{1}{2}\right\},

\displaystyle\eta_{(a,b)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\min\left\{\eta_{a},1\right\},\hskip 9.24994pt\hskip 9.24994pt\hskip 9.24994pt\;\hskip 1.0pt\gamma_{(a,b)}=\sqrt{2}\max\left\{\gamma_{a},1\right\}.

∎

Relations with other regularity conditions

Note that we do not require
f
to be continuous, however, (strong)
A
automatically implies continuity since for every
x_{0}
, continuity of

\tilde{f}_{a,y,A_{a,C_{a}}}

and
A_{a,C_{a}}
imply

A_{a,C_{a}}(f(x_{0}^{+})-y)=A_{a,C_{a}}(f(x_{-})-y)

and by injectivity of
A_{a,C_{a}}
, we deduce
f({x_{0}}^{+})=f({x_{0}}_{-})
.

Next let us note that the property weak
A
does not require

to even be continuous or have continuous inverse (let alone Lipschitz or metric regular) as the following example illustrates.

Example 1.
Let
c>0
. Let
h_{a}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
denote the function

h_{a}(x):=\begin{cases}x-a-\frac{c}{2}&x\leq a\\
x-a+\frac{c}{2}&x>a\end{cases}

Let
A(x)=x
. Then

\tilde{h}_{y}:x\mapsto x-A(f(x)-y)

is given by

\tilde{h}_{y}(x)=\begin{cases}a+\frac{c}{2}+y&x\leq a\\
a-\frac{c}{2}+y&x>a\end{cases}

hence for sufficiently small
y
(
|y|<\frac{c}{2}
),
\tilde{h}_{y}:[a-c,a+c]\to[a-c,a+c]
does not have fixed points. Hence
h_{a}
satisfies a weak
A
at
a
.

Note that in Example
1
,
h_{a}
is invertible with a discontinuous inverse thus
h
also fails to be metric regular at
a
.

The following example illustrates that a non-Lipschitz function can satisfy strong
A
.

Understanding a function that satisfies property (weak, strong)
A
on all scales and its regularity is a challenging question that we do not have a definite answer for. As far as the inverse function theorem in concerned, “
\mathsf{C}^{1}
+ invertible derivative” is stronger than property
A
as in argues in the following Remark.

Remark 1.4
.

A
\mathsf{C}^{1}
map
f
with an invertible derivative satisfies strong
{\sf LinA}
, locally uniformly on all scales. To see this, we first note that by differentiability, it holds

(1.1)

\displaystyle x-a-(D_{a}f)^{-1}(f(x)-y)=o(\|x-a\|)+(D_{a}f)^{-1}(y-f(a)),\hskip 9.24994pt\text{as}\hskip 9.24994ptx\to 0.

Thus, there exists an
r_{a}>0
such that for all
r\leq r_{a}
, and
y\in B_{\nicefrac{{r}}{{2}}}(f(a))
, the map

\tilde{f}_{a,y,(Df)^{-1}}

is a self map of
B_{r}(a)
and
\|D\tilde{f}_{a,y,(Df)^{-1}}\|\leq\tau_{a}<1
for some
\tau_{a}\in(0,1)
. This means
\tilde{f}_{a,y,(Df)^{-1}}
is a contractive map.

It is a well-known fact that continuous differentiability is equivalent to locally uniform differentiability (i.e. (
3.1
) holds locally uniformly in
a
); e.g. see
[
26
, Page 35]
. This implies that we can choose
r_{a}
locally uniformly in
a
.

Taking the convex sets
C_{a}
to be
B_{r}(a)
for all sufficiently small
r
and letting
\beta_{a}=\alpha_{a}=\frac{1}{2}
and
\eta_{a}=\gamma_{a}=1
, the claim follows.

Therefore, property
{\sf A}
locally uniformly on all scales is weaker than
\mathsf{C}^{1}
with invertible derivative. For Lipschitz maps, the satisfaction of
A
on a locally uniform scale is again an intricate issue that will not be explored in these notes.

Remark 1.5
.

Note that the first part of the argument in Remark
1.4
only relies on the differentiability at

is a Lipschitz map with a non-degenerate Clarke’s generalized Jacobian, then it satisfies property
A
at least at all of its differentiability points. By Rademacher’s theorem, this means that at almost every point when the Banach spaces
\mathcal{B}_{1}
and
\mathcal{B}_{2}
are finite dimensional. In the infinite dimensions, the latter implies that

satisfies
A
on a dense open subset of
\mathcal{B}_{1}
provided

is norm differentiable or Asplund; this is indeed according to the infinite dimensional generalization of the Rademacher’s theorem; see the seminal work
[

Main Results

Convention:

f

Definition 1.6

A Banach space

Theorem 1

(Generalized IMT)
.

Let

be an everywhere differentiable map with invertible derivative. Then

is a local diffeomorphism provided any of the following items hold

(1)

if

satisfies strong
{\sf A}
everywhere on all scales;

(2)

If
\mathcal{B}_{1}
is locally weakly compact and

is weak-weak continuous and satisfies
{\sf A}
everywhere on all scales;

(3)

If
\mathcal{B}_{1}
is locally weakly compact with

{\sf A}

(4)

\mathcal{B}_{1}

and

{\sf A}
everywhere on all scales.

Remark 1.7
.

A large class of Banach spaces

Corollary 1

In what follows we wish to provide criteria to ensure certain maps that are not everywhere differentiable, are still almost diffeomorphism in a certain suitable sense. Ideally one wishes to obtain an inverse mapping theorem that works for Lipschitz maps. But our analysis does not yet work in such generality. However this can be done for certain maps that are in a sense more regular than Lipschitz maps but less than everywhere differentiable with an invertible derivative.

First we need to introduce certain notion of large sets in terms of a convexity like property that works well with our tools.

Definition 1.8
.

A subset
S
of
\mathcal{B}_{1}
is said to be segmentally dense if for any line segment

S

Notation:
We denote the set of points at which

is differentiable by
\mathsf{FD}

The following notions are large scale analogues of a map having a non-degenerate derivative.

Definition 1.9
.

•

The map

Suppose

1.3.
Road map and Organization

In §
2
, we provide some necessary background material needed for better readability of these notes.

In §
3
, we provide the detailed proofs of the main and auxiliary results. In particular, the road map of the generalized IMT is as follows:

(1)

Variants of property
A
along with suitable hypotheses on the domain Banach space in each case, is utilized to prove the existence of fixed points of the maps
\tilde{f}
; this in turn results in local surjectivity;

(2)

Differentiability along with an invertible derivative is argued to provide discreteness of the map
f
i.e. the fact it has discrete fibers;

(3)

Invoking the convexity hypotheses, we show local injectivity, thus establishing
f
is a local homeomorphism;

(4)

Revisiting differentiability with an invertible derivative, we show local diffeomorphism.

In order to demonstrate the significance of these results, we have included an application to abstract ODE theory, of the generalized theorems we have established in §
4
where we show existence and uniqueness for a certain type of ODE system without continuity or Lipschitz condition on the RHS of the equation.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Weak topology and local weak compactness

The weak topology on a Banach space is one that topologizes weak convergence of sequences. In this paper, a weakly continuous map
f:\mathsf{Dom}(f)\subset\mathcal{B}_{1}\to\mathcal{B}_{2}
between Banach spaces, refers to a map that is weak-weak continuous.

A well-known consequence of Banach-Alaoglu’s theorem (known as Kakukati’s theorem) is that reflexive Banach spaces are locally weakly compact; see
[
6
, Theorem V.4.2]

By the Schauder-Tychonoff fixed point theorem, when
\mathcal{B}
is locally weakly compact, any weakly continuous self map
f:C\to C
admits a fixed point when
C
is a bounded closed convex set.

2.2.
Convexity of a Banach space

Terminology:
In some older literature it is customary to refer to convexity (mainly in the context of uniform ones) as rotundity.

Characteristically, in any normed linear space, any ball is convex. Many important fixed point results in nonlinear functional analysis often requires stronger notions of convexity.

Recall the modulus of convexity of a Banach space
\mathcal{B}
is the non-decreasing function
\mathsf{m_{c}}:[0,2]\to[0,1]
given by
[
21
]

\mathsf{m_{c}}(\epsilon):=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\|x\|=\|y\|=1\\
\|x-y\|\geq\epsilon\end{subarray}}\left(1-\frac{\|x+y\|}{2}\right).

It is known that
\mathsf{m_{c}}
is convex; see
[
14
]
.

The characteristic of convexity is defined as
[
14
]

\epsilon_{0}:=\sup\left\{\epsilon\;\;\text{\textbrokenbar}\;\;\mathsf{m_{c}}(\epsilon)=0\right\}.

2.2.1.
Strictly convex space

\mathcal{B}
is said to be strictly convex if the balls are strictly convex equivalently if
\mathsf{m_{c}}(2)=1
; see
[
4
]
.

2.2.2.
Uniformly convex space

Uniform convexity is stronger than strict convexity.

Definition 2.1

(uniform convexity)
.

\mathcal{B}
is said to be uniformly convex if

\forall\epsilon>0,\;\exists\delta>0,\;\;s.t.\;\;\|x\|=\|y\ =1\;\text{and}\;|\|x-y\|\geq\epsilon\Longrightarrow\|\frac{x+y}{2}\|\leq 1-\delta

equivalently
\mathcal{B}
is uniformly convex if and only if
\epsilon_{0}=0
. Uniformly convex spaces are reflexive (Milman-Pettis theorem).

2.2.3.
k
-uniformly convex spaces

The
k
-uniformly convex (originally called
k
-uniformly rotund or
k
-UR) spaces were introduced in
[
24
]
as generalizations of uniformly convex spaces. These spaces are reflexive (as uniformly convex Banach spaces are).

Definition 2.2

(k-uniformly rotund (convex)
[
24
]
)
.

\mathcal{B}
is called
k
-UR if

\forall\epsilon>0,\;\;\exists\delta>0\;\;s.t.\;\;\|x_{1}\|=\cdots=\|x_{k+1}\|=1\;\text{and}\;V(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{k+1})\Longrightarrow\left\|\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{k+1}x_{i}}{k+1}\right\|\leq 1-\delta

where
V(x_{1},\cdots,x_{k+1})
is the
k
-dim volume given by

V(x_{1},\cdots,x_{k+1}):=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}f_{i}\in\mathcal{B}^{*}\\
\|f_{i}\|\leq 1\end{subarray}}\det\begin{pmatrix}1&1&\cdots&1\\
f_{1}(x_{1})&f_{1}(x_{2})&\cdots&f_{1}(x_{k+1})\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
f_{k}(x_{1})&f_{k}(x_{2})&\cdots&f_{k}(x_{k+1})\end{pmatrix}.

2.3.
Fixed points of (quasi)-nonexpansive maps

2.3.1.
Fixed points of non-expansive maps

A nonexpansive map is simply one with Lipschitz constant 1 i.e. it does not increase distances.

According to the Browder-Göhde-Kirk fixed point theorem
[
11
]
, a non-expanding self map on a bounded closed convex set
C
in a uniformly convex
\mathcal{B}
, admits a fixed point. It is also well-known that if
\mathcal{B}
is merely strictly convex, then
f
has a convex set of fixed points (possibly empty).

A simple argument combined with the convexity results obtained in
[
2
]
for nonexpansive maps shows that, when
C
is a bounded closed convex subset of
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{B}
satisfies
{\sf FPP}
, then the set of fixed points of a non-expansive self map
f
on
C
is a closed convex subset of
C
, see Proposition
3.2
.

It was proven in
[
20
]
that
k
-uniformly rotund spaces satisfy the
{\sf FPP}
.

2.3.2.
Fixed points of quasi-nonexpansive maps

A (not necessarily continuous)
f:C\to C
(
C
closed and convex) is called quasi-nonexpansive if it has at least one fixed point and distances to the fixed point are not increased under it i.e. if for all such fixed points
p\in C
, it satisfies

\|f(x)-f(p)\|\leq\|x-p\|,\hskip 9.24994pt\forall x\in C.

This was introduced in
[
7
]
.

In
[
10
]
, it has been shown that if
\mathcal{B}
is strictly convex, then the set of fixed points of a quasi-nonexpansive map is a nonempty closed convex set on which
f
is continuous.

3.
Proofs

Note that openness, local surjectivity and local injectivity are inherited by maps restricted to smaller domains and targets (equipped with induced topologies). Since all the arguments are local and with the conventions previously made, in the proof of the fact that
f
is a local homeomorphism, we can without loss of generality, assume that the domain of
f
is open. So we will proceed with this assumption.

Let us note the simple yet key fact that, due to the injectivity of the maps
A_{a,C_{a}}
, one deduces

f^{-1}(y)\cap C_{a}=\text{the set of fixed points of $\tilde{f}_{a,y,A_{a,C_{a}}}$}.

When all the variables can be inferred form the context, with a slight abuse of notation, we will simply write
\tilde{f}_{a,y}
and suppress the other variables. Similarly we will write
s_{a}
and
A_{a}
.

3.1.
Key Lemmas

Lemma 3.1
.

If
f:U\subset\mathcal{B}_{1}\to\mathcal{B}_{2}
satisfies either of the hypotheses of the Main Theorem, then it is open and locally surjective.

Proof
.

For local surjectivity, it suffices to show that for every
a
, and
y\in B_{s_{a}}(f(a))
the map
\tilde{f}_{a,y}
has at least one fixed point. Indeed, this implies
B_{s_{a}}(f(a))\subset f(C_{a})
. The
{\sf FPP}
assumption in hypotheses 3 and 4, guarantees the existence of such a fixed point. In hypothesis 1, the Banach fixed point theorem can be applied. In hypothesis 2, the Leray-Schauder fixed point theorem can be invoked. Therefore in all cases, local surjectivity follows.

For openness, we need to show that the image of every open ball around some
a
contains an open ball around
f(a)
. Consider the open ball
B_{r}(a)
. Since we have assumed property
{\sf A}
on all scales, there exists a closed bounded convex set
C_{a}\in\mathscr{C}
with
C_{a}\subset B_{r}(a)
. By the first part,
f(C_{a})
contains
B_{s_{a}}(f(a))
which implies that
f(B_{r}(a))
contains the ball
B_{s_{a}}(f(a))
. Therefore,
f
is an open map.
∎

The following is by now a standard result. For the definition of normal structure, we will refer the reader to
[
1
]
.

Proposition 3.2
.

Suppose
\mathcal{B}
has a normal structure (in particular, if it is locally weakly compact (this contains reflexive spaces)) with
{\sf FPP}
. Let
g:C\to C
be a non-expanding self map of a bounded closed convex set
C
. Then the set of fixed points of
g
is a non-empty closed convex subset of
C
.

Proof
.

See
[
2
]
.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

If
f
is differentiable everywhere with an invertible derivative, then it is a discrete map i.e. for every
y
,
f^{-1}(y)
is a discrete set.

Proof
.

Since discreteness of a map is preserved under compositions with linear isomorphisms, we can assume, without loss of generality, that
a=0
,
y=f(0)=0
and
D_{0}f=\raisebox{0.0pt}{\scalebox{1.1}{$\mathbbm{1}$}}\hskip-1.0pt
.

The definition of differentiability implies that for each
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
r(\epsilon)>0
such that

(3.1)

\displaystyle\|f(x)-x\|\leq\varepsilon\|x\|,\hskip 9.24994pt0<\|x\|<r(\epsilon),

and thus one deduces
f^{-1}(0)\cap B_{r}(0)=\left\{0\right\}
. This means that every
x\in f^{-1}(y)
is an isolated point. Thus the inverse images of points are discrete sets.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

Suppose
f
is a discrete map and for every
C_{a}\in\mathscr{C}
with sufficiently small diameter, and for every fixed
y\in B_{s_{a}}(f(a))
, we know that

f^{-1}(y)\cap C_{a}

is a non-empty closed convex set. Therefore,
f
is locally injective at
a
.

Proof
.

Suppose, on the contrary that
f
is not locally injective at
a
. Take
y\in B_{s_{a}}(f(a))
and
z_{1},z_{2}\in C_{a}
with
f(z_{1})=f(z_{2})=y
where
z_{1}\neq z_{2}
. According to the convexity hypothesis, we have
[z_{1},z_{2}]\subset f^{-1}(y)
which contradicts the discreteness of
f
.
∎

The following is well-known, yet the proof is provided for the sake of thoroughness.

Lemma 3.5
.

Suppose
f
(with an open domain) is a local homeomorphism and is differentiable at
a
with an invertible derivative. Then, the local inverse
f^{-1}
is differentiable at
f(a)
and
D_{f(a)}f^{-1}=(D_{a}f)^{-1}
.

Proof
.

Without loss of generality, let us assume
a=f(a)=0
and
D_{0}f=\raisebox{0.0pt}{\scalebox{1.1}{$\mathbbm{1}$}}\hskip-1.0pt
. Then we have

\lim_{y=f(x)\to f(a)}\frac{\|f^{-1}(y)\|}{\|y\|}=\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{\|x\|}{\|f(x)\|}=1.

Hence,

(3.2)

\displaystyle\|x-f(x)\|=o(\|f(x)\|),\hskip 9.24994pt\text{as $\|f(x)\|\to 0$},

and the claim follows.
∎

3.2.
Proof of the main results

3.2.1.
Proof of Theorem
1

The openness (and consequently the local surjectivity) follows from Lemma
3.1
. We only need to argue the local injectivity of
f
; since this implies that
f
is a local homeomorphism. Thus, by Lemma
3.5
, it is a local diffeomorphism.

In
Statement (1)
, the local injectivity is guaranteed by the uniqueness of he fixed point in the Banach fixed point theorem.

In
Statements (2), (3)
, the local injectivity follows from the discreteness established in Lemma
3.3
together with Proposition
3.2
and Lemma
3.4
.

In
Statement (4)
, the local injectivity follows from Lemma
3.4
in combination with the fact that in strictly convex spaces, the set of fixed points of quasi-nonexpansive maps form a convex set, see §
2.3.2
.
∎

3.2.2.
Proof of the Corollary
1

Statement (1)

Properness and being a local homeomorphism ensure that the inverse image of each point is a finite set which in turn implies that such a point admits a neighborhood that is evenly covered. Consequently
f
has the unique lifting property, see the proof of
[
16
, Proposition 1.30]
.

A local homeomorphism with the unique lifting property with a path connected domain and locally simply connected target, is a covering map; e.g. see
[
8
, Section 5.6, Proposition 6]
. Applying the above argument to path connected components of
U
completes the proof of the claim.

Statement (2)

The claim directly follows from the topology fact that a local homeomorphism from a compact Hausdorff space to a Hausdorff space is a covering map.
∎

3.2.3.
Proof of the Corollary
2

This follows from the Main Theorem in combination with the results in
[
22
]
.
∎

3.2.4.
Proof of the Corollary
3

Let
x
be fixed. By the qualifier “locally uniform on all scales”, there exists a neighborhood
U_{x}
such that
\alpha_{a},\beta_{a}>0
can be taken to be the same constants for all
a\in U_{x}\cap\mathcal{R}
.

Since
\mathcal{R}
is dense, we can choose
a\in\mathcal{R}
arbitrarily close to
x
. Now for sufficiently small
r
, there exists
a\in\mathcal{R}
and
C_{a}
with
\eta_{a}r\leq\diam(C_{a})\leq\gamma_{a}r
such that
B_{2\gamma_{a}r}(x)
contains
C_{a}
. Consequently by the proof of Lemma
3.1
,
f(B_{2\gamma_{a}r}(x))
contains
B_{\alpha_{a}\eta_{a}r}(f(a))
which contains
B_{\frac{1}{2}\alpha_{a}\eta_{a}r}(f(x))
provided we had chosen
a
with
\|f(x)-f(a)\|\leq\frac{1}{2}\alpha_{a}\eta_{a}r
.

Thus openness (thus also local surjectivity) follows.
∎

3.2.5.
Proof of the Corollary
4

Again local surjectivity and openness follows by a similar argument as in the proof of Corollary
3
. The local injectivity is argued as follows. Suppose
f
is not locally injective. Thus there exists
a
and
C_{a}
and
y
such that the fixed points of
\tilde{f}_{a,y}
form a bounded closed convex set that is not a singleton. In particular, it includes a line segment. The directional derivative of
f
along any line segment and in the direction of the line segment exists and vanishes. This contradicts both “non-degenerate directional derivatives” and “being segmentally non-degenerate”.
∎

3.2.6.
Proof of the Theorem
2

Consider the map

\mathcal{F}:\mathcal{B}_{1}\times\mathcal{B}_{2}\to\mathcal{B}_{1}\times\mathcal{B}_{2},

given by

\mathcal{F}(t,x)=\left(t,g(t,x)\right).

Note

D_{(t,x)}\mathcal{F}=\begin{pmatrix}\raisebox{0.0pt}{\scalebox{1.1}{$\mathbbm{1}$}}\hskip-1.0pt&0\\
D_{t}g&D_{x}g\end{pmatrix}.

Since
D_{x}g
is invertible, we deduce
D_{(t,x)}\mathcal{F}
is invertible as well.

According to the key pairing Lemma (Lemma
1.3
),
\mathcal{F}
satisfies the same hypothesis of the Theorem
1
as
g
does. Thus by Theorem
1
, we deduce
\mathcal{F}
is a local diffeomorphism. Let
\mathcal{F}^{-1}
be the “unique” local inverse around

whose image contains a neighborhood of

It is straightforward to see that

for some

thus

. This means locally

is the graph of the unique function

∎

The abstract pde system (

g(t,u(t))=c=g(0,b).

(this is a pde since

we deduce

D_{x}g(t,u(t))\circ u^{\prime}(t)-D_{t}g(t,u(t))=0.

which by virtue of the chain rule, the latter is just

D_{t}\left(g(t,u(t))\right)=0.

Thus any possible solution lies on a the level set

By the IFT proven in Theorem

∎
