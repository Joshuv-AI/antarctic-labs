1. Introduction
There is growing interest by concentrating solar power (CSP) stakeholders worldwide in the development and
construction of “hybrid” CSP systems, e.g., CSP systems with thermal energy storage (TES) that integrate
directly or indirectly with other renewable or fossil-fired generation technologies. Of the 28 CSP projects being
built in China, 27 of them are combined with PV installations (5 of those also include wind installations). 1 While
CSP costs have dropped significantly over the past decade, the costs of CSP systems are still high relative to
the cost of PV systems. 2 However, CSP systems have the advantage of low-cost TES which enables solar
energy to be collected and stored during the solar day and dispatched, via a conventional power cycle,
throughout night hours or hours with low or no solar radiation. Li-ion batteries can be a cheaper solution for
storage capacities below approximately four hours whereas CSP with TES has benefits in longer storage
ranges. Additionally, geographical locations that have favorable conditions for CSP development (i.e., high
direct normal irradiance resource, relatively flat ground, near a population center) are also favorable for utility-
scale PV development, suggesting that these systems will most likely be located near each other.

Figure 1: CSP-TES-PV-Battery hybrid system configuration (Graphic by Al Hicks, National Laboratory of the
 Rockies).

CSP integrated with TES offers a lower cost dispatchable solution when considering current or future markets
requiring long-duration energy storage to meet reliability targets. However, during the solar day, PV generation
is the cheapest form of renewable electricity generation, making it difficult for CSP without TES to be cost-
competitive. As such, many CSP developers are proactively developing or considering hybrid configurations
and operation strategies to ensure sufficient plant revenues in certain global markets. In theory, CSP-PV
hybrid systems could operate behind a single grid interconnect that is rated for either the PV or the CSP cycle
capacity, whichever is greater. This could enable greater utilization of existing infrastructure and less variability
in generation. Additional revenues can be based on increased capacity values (higher reliability in meeting
peak demand) as well as various ancillary services to the grid system. To this end, the SolarPACES Market
Advisory Board (MAB) highlighted their interest in modeling and valuation of hybrid CSP systems as a high

Hybrid power systems can be broadly defined as systems involving multiple energy generation, storage,
and/or conversion technologies that are integrated—through an overarching control framework or physically—
to achieve cost savings and enhanced capabilities, value, efficiency, or environmental performance compared
to the independent alternatives. 3 For purposes of this work, we more narrowly define hybrid CSP systems as
those that include CSP with TES co-located with PV systems with or without electrochemical batteries and
with or without electric heaters (to provide an energy path between PV and TES). Hybrid system models have
been developed by each of the public institutions represented within this work, i.e., KTH Royal Institute of
Technology, the National Laboratory of the Rockies (in collaboration with Colorado School of Mines),
Politecnico di Milano, and the DLR Institute of Solar Research. Using these models, the overall objective of
this work is to conduct round-robin comparison case studies assessing the ability to model the performance
and techno-economics of hybrid CSP-TES/PV-BESS systems.

This report provides background information and detailed descriptions of four CSP-PV-BESS hybrid system
techno-economic models from four SolarPACES member country research institutions (Chapter 2). At the end
of Chapter 2, we draw comparisons between the four CSP hybrid system models. In Chapter 3, we present
the performance and financial assumptions used in the intercomparison case study including definitions for
key design indicators (KDIs) and key performance indicators (KPIs). In Chapter 4, we present the results from
the intercomparison case study using a single system design comparison across all the CSP hybrid models.
Additionally, we present the results of a design optimization study for which each research institution
generated a Pareto frontier of solar hybrid system net present value (NPV) subject to a percentage of load
met. In Chapter 5, we provide lessons learned from this model intercomparison and recommendations for
system modeling of CSP hybrid systems. We conclude and summarize our work in Chapter 6.

2. Hybrid PV-CSP Modeling Tools
This section presents CSP hybrid system modeling approaches that exist within and outside of our team of
collaborators. The various approaches used to model CSP hybrid systems, including the strengths and
weaknesses, as well as the functionality of each approach, has been reported.

2.1. MoSES – Modeling of Sustainable Energy Systems (KTH)

MoSES (Model of Sustainable Energy Systems) is a simulation tool developed at KTH Royal Institute of
Technology and designed for the pre-design, operation, and optimization of hybrid renewable technologies
with storage, including solar and wind, for both grid-connected and cogeneration industry applications. For
this project, MoSES has been used to estimate the techno-economic performance of hybrid CSP-PV power
plants, considering various costs, design assumptions, meteorological conditions, and electricity markets. 4,5
MoSES incorporates a CoolProp environment 6 and the National Laboratory of the Rockies’ (NLR)-PySAM
wrapper for System Advisor Model (SAM). 7 Within MoSES, a comprehensive range of models is employed,
encompassing both established subsystems and those that are currently in the developmental phase. The
performance of state-of-the-art components, including conventional steam Rankine cycles, molten salt
receivers, and TES, has been validated through a comparison with SAM estimates. Furthermore, ad hoc
models for components that are still undergoing development have been devised through a combination of
literature review and collaboration with industrial partners and equipment manufacturers. These models have
been verified against anticipated performance curves documented in existing literature. The techno-economic
indicators have been evaluated by coupling thermodynamics with an economic model based on a bottom-up
estimation method, as shown in Figure 2. The resulting system model is customizable in terms of location
(meteorological data, grid availability, electricity market, and economics of location), design assumptions, and
dispatch strategy.

 Figure 2: MoSES modeling methodology flowchart

The hybrid CSP-PV layouts presented in this study were designed through the resolution of a multi-objective
optimization problem. Objective functions such as levelized cost of energy (LCOE), hybrid capacity factor
(HCF), capital expenditure (CAPEX), annual energy yield (AEY), and maximum profit, as well as the design
variables, can be customized by the users. To solve the optimization problem, a Genetic Algorithm is
implemented, which utilizes principles of natural selection and genetics to obtain a set of tradeoff optimal

2.2. HOPP – Hybrid Optimization and Performance Platform (NLR/MINES)

The Hybrid Optimization and Performance Platform (HOPP) is an open-source Python tool developed by
NLR. 12 The tool assesses the operational effectiveness and economic feasibility of combined renewable
energy arrangements. Such hybrid systems integrate diverse generation, storage, and transformation
technologies. HOPP can model annual energy yield of various renewable energy technologies, including PV,
wind, and CSP (tower and trough), paired with storage technologies. As demonstrated through the capabilities
of HOPP, these hybrid systems, either coordinated through a central control or directly integrated, outperform
their isolated counterparts in cost-effectiveness, enhanced performance, efficiency, and environmental
benefits when compared with independent alternatives. 13

 Figure 6: Overview of the HOPP software framework.

HOPP enables the user to sample and optimize a plant’s configuration, tailoring it to specific performance
metrics reflective of the market demands and the developer’s objectives. The HOPP framework employs a
price-taker dispatch optimization to manage the operations of subsystems 14 and uses nonlinear optimization
techniques to ascertain the optimal values for critical design and sizing parameters, shown in Figure 6. 15
Currently, HOPP can only optimize subsystem sizing using one objective (i.e., does not support multi-objective
optimization).

Operational decisions are determined by a mixed integer linear program that can either maximize revenue or
minimize the operational costs necessary to follow a load depending on the targeted application. 16 HOPP’s
dispatch optimization uses a rolling time horizon approach and is implemented using the Python software

The design evaluation of HOPP fundamentally relies on the NLR’s System Advisor Model (SAM) technology
and financial modules. 18 HOPP utilizes PySAM, a Python API for SAM to interact with all technology
performance and financial modules, excluding the CSP modules, which uses PySSC. HOPP provides a
unified interface to modify and simulate SAM technology models. SAM’s integration into the HOPP framework
is illustrated in Figure 6. In recent work, several black-box solving methods were evaluated using fast-running
test problems, and a Bayesian-optimization algorithm was selected. 19 In this study, we use the Bayesian-
optimization algorithm to optimize the hybrid CSP system design sizing variables.

Although HOPP lacks a GUI, it is available as an open-source Python package on PyPI and the source code
is publicly available on GitHub. Designed with object-oriented programming, HOPP is built to incorporate
additional technologies as they are developed. However, HOPP has limited documentation and training,
making the learning curve steep for users without prior experience in Python and/or SAM. At the time of
writing this, HOPP does not support electric heaters within the molten storage loop which would enable the
molten salts to store surplus PV generation.

2.3. AESOPT – Aggregated Energy System Optimization Tool (POLIMI)

The Aggregated Energy System Optimization Tool (AESOPT) is an optimization tool for the design and
operation of hybrid energy systems, developed by Politecnico di Milano. The tool is specifically designed to
support the modelling of highly integrated energy systems characterized by the presence of renewable energy
sources and other energy generation, conversion and storage technologies. Recently, the tool has been
adapted and applied for the modelling of CSP plants coupled with other renewable technologies, such as PV 20
and wind. 21 This version of the tool has been used to perform the comparisons presented in this study. Thanks
to AESOPT capability of modelling the single subsystems, the CSP technology can be modelled by separately
considering its constitutive components as AESOPT subsystems: the solar field section, thermal energy
storage and power block. This approach enables the selection of different technologies for each subsystem,
resulting in multiple CSP configurations (e.g., power tower, parabolic trough, and linear Fresnel collectors).
The AESOPT optimizer is able to simultaneously optimize the main plant design and sizing parameters while
also providing a preliminary plant operation and the associated dispatch strategy throughout the
representative year. The inclusion of the preliminary plant dispatch strategy in the optimization tool is essential
to correctly estimate the operating costs and ensure the feasibility of the obtained solution. The advantage of
simultaneously optimizing the design and operation phases is that the mutual dependency between design
and operation decisions can be explicitly considered. The concurrent design and operation optimization
problem is formulated as a mixed-integer linear program (MILP), written in Python using Pyomo 22 as the
optimization modelling language.

AESOPT supports the modelling of components with slow inertia and specific dynamics, such as the CSP
power block. It is possible to limit sudden power fluctuations in power block output by imposing a maximum
ramp rate during online operation and to model different startup types (e.g., hot, warm and cold, depending
on the downtime) that are characterized by different startup trajectories. Then, a startup cost can also be
associated with each startup type to resemble realistic operation and to prevent excessive component wear.
In this specific case, such features were not enabled but only the hot startup type with no cost associated has
been considered for the sake of comparison.
To preserve the computational tractability of the MILP model, the optimization problem is formulated over a
set of representative periods identified by a clustering algorithm 25 while the non-linear cost functions and
performance curve related with each subsystem are linearized by means of piecewise linear approximations
or other linearization techniques, since a linear description of each plant component should be provided to the
optimizer. By doing so, the linearity of the optimization model is preserved and global optimality of the MILP
solution can be guaranteed. Moreover, the optimization problem can be solved using any mixed-integer linear
solver, whether open-source or commercial, such as Gurobi. 26 The solution time varies significantly depending
on the system configuration, the number of design optimization variables, and the representative periods
considered. It can range from a few minutes for relatively simple models to several hours for the most complex
ones.
The optimal design is carried out by seeking to minimize the total annual cost (TAC) or to maximize the plant
profits, depending on the specific user objective and owner requirements. Although AESOPT is primarily
designed to consider a single objective function, multi-objective optimization is possible as long as the
mathematical expression of the objective function remains linear. For example, to maximize the plant's
dispatchability in addition to minimizing the TAC, the annual energy generated by the plant can be included in
the objective function with a negative cost coefficient (assuming objective minimization).
Currently, AESOPT does not have a GUI but it is composed of multiple Python modules constituting the
programming interface used to create the model and visualize the results. However, the development of a GUI
to make the tool more user-friendly is planned. AESOPT is a proprietary software of Politecnico di Milano but
the release of an open-source version of the software is under consideration.

2.4. YACOP – Yield Assessment Calculation and Optimization Program (DLR)

The Yield Assessment Calculation and Optimization Program (YACOP) 27 provides a framework for the
modeling and techno-economic analysis of power and heat generation systems on utility scale. YACOP is
being developed at the DLR Institute of Solar Research in order to investigate and optimize the techno-
economic design of hybrid and therefore often complex energy generation systems. In order to also enable
the investigation of innovative system concepts, one focus of development is on being able to easily integrate
new system and component models at different levels of detail and still evaluate them effectively. YACOP is
therefore based on a modular structure in which the models for describing the operating behavior of the
components and the models for the techno-economic evaluation are regarded as black boxes by the
simulation and optimization routines.
The modular structure of the component and system models makes it possible to create any system
configuration from existing component models. Templates and predefined interfaces are available for creating
new component models. It is possible to define physical models using equations as well as to integrate
empirical models based on performance maps. A mixture of both approaches is also possible. The focus is on
thermodynamic component models, which are connected using interfaces for heat transfer fluid flows at a
specific temperature and pressure as well as interfaces for electrical power flows. Simplified mapping of start-
up and shut-down behavior as well as load point changes of individual components is also possible.

typical periods from the specified input data using various clustering methods. Next, an operating strategy is
defined for the system. This can either be defined by a rule-based heuristic or by an objective and constraints
to perform a dispatch optimization. Finally, the KPIs that are to be minimized or maximized as part of the
design optimization are defined. In addition, boundary values are defined for the design parameters of the
components to be optimized. Typical objectives for the design optimization are levelized cost of energy
(LCOE), capacity factor (CF), net present value (NPV) or total annualized cost (TAC). It is possible to define
multiple objectives for the design optimization.
Genetic algorithms are used to carry out the techno-economic optimization. These repeatedly call up the
annual yield calculation and the associated post-processing for various designs. The annual yield calculation
is carried out with time steps of 15 - 60 min and repeatedly calls the heuristic strategy or the dispatch
optimization. Both, the rule-based heuristics as well as the dispatch optimization can access a look-ahead of
input data for up to 72 h. In the scope of this project, only rule-based heuristics are used to define the operating
strategy. After the typical operating year for the given design has been calculated, the technical and economic
KPIs are determined in a post processing and returned to the design optimizer for evaluation. In order to
shorten the computing time, the design evaluations can be executed in parallel on several processor cores.
At the end of the techno-economic optimization, the parameters, KPIs and the results of the annual yield
calculation are available for each design examined. Depending on the definition of the optimization, the optimal
design or several pareto-optimal designs are also output.
YACOP is programmed in Python and offers a programming interface in the form of a Python package. All
Python packages and optimization solvers used are non-commercial and freely available.

2.5. CSP Hybrid Modeling Tools Outside of Working Group

In addition to the models included in this study, there has been additional work done in Hybrid CSP-PV
modeling. Here we highlight hybrid CSP-PV system modeling work done outside of our working group. This
summary of work provides the reader with an appreciation of other CSP-PV system modeling work, their
approaches, and their applications. However, this is not a comprehensive review of all work done within this
research space. Hybrid CSP-PV system design optimization is an active area of research for a wide range of
applications.

Mata-Torres et al. perform a multi-objective optimization to size a hybrid CSP-PV system that generates power
and desalinate salt water simultaneously. 28 The authors utilize TRNSYS to obtain the hour performance
results which are fed into a thermo-economic model that calculates the objective function which is first used
in a surrogate model that reduces the number of design variables to obtain the solution space for the genetic
algorithm used for the multi-objective optimization. Other models in literature use a similar approach;
Valenzuela et al. examine a hybrid plant for power and water cogeneration and Zurita et al. evaluate different
dispatch approaches. 29,30

Bousselamti et al. develop a hybrid CSP-PV model to evaluate optimal design under different dispatch
strategies by utilizing a genetic algorithm for a multi-objective problem. 31 Genetic algorithm approaches are
common; Zhai et al. use a genetic algorithm to evaluate operational strategies and Kong et al. use a genetic
algorithm to solve a design and dispatch problem. 32,33 Kong et al. use a staged approach in which the first
stage minimizes the violation of a power generation schedule to obtain an initial operational schedule and the
second stage minimizes operational costs to improve upon the schedule.

Sigarchian et al. sees to replace u-Grid, a microgrid simulation tool with exhaustive search optimization, with
a particle swarm optimization algorithm to solve the design problem for a hybrid CSP-PV plant with a backup
generator. 34 Bousselamti et al. use particle swarm to solve a single objective for the design problem while
Wang et al. utilize particle swarm for a multi-objective problem. 35,36

Petrollese et al. develop a model to simulate the expected performance of the solar field, TES, power block,
PV system and battery as a function of the design parameters. 37 A linear program was then used to find the
optimal design while minimizing LCOE. Richter et al. utilize a predictive storage strategy and linear program
to solve the design problem. 38

2.6. Comparison Between the CSP Hybrid Modeling Tools

All models considered in this study are used for the techno-economic optimization of CSP hybrid systems.
Power tower and parabolic trough CSP configurations are handled by all models, while the common
hybridization technologies include photovoltaics and wind. All models have the capability to store energy with
molten salt TES and electrochemical batteries. AESOPT, MoSES and YACOP models can include EHs to
provide an electric-to-thermal energy path. AESOPT can model EH in parallel of the solar receiver while
MoSES and YACOP can model EH either parallel or in series (booster) of the solar receiver. All models have
the ability to capture hourly time fidelity; HOPP, AESOPT and YACOP can use clustering algorithms to reduce
computational expense using a set of representative periods at hourly fidelity. MoSES, HOPP and AESOPT
use SAM’s PVWatts to provide performance estimates for PV generation. MoSES is the only tool within this
study to have a GUI. HOPP is the only tool that is publicly available.
 Table 1: Summary of design and dispatch modeling approaches

  Model Design optimization approach Dispatch optimization approach
  MoSES Black box genetic algorithm Classical optimization (mixed integer)
  HOPP Initial sampling to black box Bayesian optimizer Classical optimization (mixed integer)

  YACOP Black box genetic algorithm Heuristic or classical optimization

Table 1 presents a summary of modeling approaches for the CSP hybrid system design and dispatch for each
tool. AESOPT is the only tool within this study to use a mixed-integer linear program to simultaneously solve
the design and dispatch of the hybrid CSP system. MoSES, YACOP and HOPP use black-box optimization
algorithms to solve the design sizing problem. MoSES and YACOP use the same genetic algorithm whereas
HOPP implements a Bayesian optimizer. HOPP and MoSES both use a mixed-integer linear program to
optimize sub-system operations, whereas YACOP uses a heuristic approach (but also supports dispatch
optimization). MoSES, YACOP and HOPP use a rolling horizon or look-ahead approach for the dispatch
optimization.
Each of these modeling tools can represent various CSP and non-CSP hybrid configurations, including
different CSP technologies, different TES mediums, and locations of EHs (i.e., parallel or series to the CSP
receiver). The development of each configuration requires careful thought and time to implement which is
typically driven by specific project objectives. A common CSP hybrid configuration had to be established in
order conduct a comparison. The power tower CSP configuration is chosen for the analysis due to the ability
for the technology to be paired with TES and higher efficiency power cycles compared to parabolic troughs.
The TES medium is molten salt; the only model capable of modeling other storage mediums is MoSES. At the
time of writing, HOPP is the only model that cannot include an electric heater (EH), which restricts the
hybridization technologies to PV and BESS. The power cycle is steam Rankine. Three institutions can model
time fidelity from 5 minutes to hourly, but AESOPT restricts the time fidelity to hourly for this analysis.
Even though there are sufficient similarities for the different models to complete a comprehensive study, the
input data structures are different between the models. CSP system performance is very sensitive to the solar
field, receiver, and power cycle efficiency assumptions for design point and off-design behavior. To reduce the
discrepancies experienced within this comparison, the working group decided to fix the power cycle and solar
field performance by using look-up efficiencies tables. These inputs are detailed in the following sections.
In addition, all tools use similar approaches to model different aspects of CSP system performance but use
different underlying assumptions. For example, when modeling power cycle startup, all models incorporate
power block ramping limitations, resulting in reduced electricity production during ramp-up/down hours.

However, MoSES and AESOPT do not account for the energy required to warm cycle components, while
HOPP and YACOP do. HOPP and YACOP model parasitic power consumption of the molten salt pumps,
heliostats drives, and control center at the component level, whereas MoSES and AESOPT include them as
a fixed percentage. Other modeling assumptions for system losses are also different throughout the models.
While all groups use classical optimization for dispatch, the assumptions around the approach can lead to
different results based on what is valued in the model. For example, in a CSP-PV hybrid system, the choice
of curtailment can vary depending on how the model values each technology, i.e., curtailment PV over CSP
or vice versa. Additionally, YACOP has the ability to use both a heuristic or classical optimization for dispatch
optimization, whereas AESOPT, MoSES and HOPP only use classical optimization. Classical optimization is
deterministic and is more capable of following electrical prices, in particular, when time-of-use prices are high.
At the time of the case study execution, YACOP could only use the heuristic approach for operations.

3.3.3. Steam Rankine power block

The model of the PB provides the cost and performance maps of the steam Rankine PB as function of its
nominal capacity. For each nominal capacity, the performance map correlates the PB efficiency to its load and
to the ambient temperature to evaluate part-load and off-design performance.

The PB layout is designed to use hot solar salts as heat source for the re-heated steam Rankine cycle. The
set of heat exchangers that transfer heat from the HTF to water/steam is constituted by an economizer, an
evaporator, a superheater and a reheater in parallel with the superheater. The water preheating line includes
three low-pressure surface feedwater heaters, the deaerator and three high-pressure surface feedwater
heaters. The last high-pressure feedwater heater is fed with a controlled steam extraction to avoid low water
temperature in the economizer at part-load, which may cause undesired freezing of the solar salts. 52,53 The
PB heat rejection is performed via an air-cooled condenser to maximize the plant flexibility with respect to the
site selection. The plant layout is shown in Figure 12, while the key design assumptions for the PB model are

3.5.1. System Optimization

For the case studies in which the sub-system sizing was optimized, we maximize the system’s NPV while
achieving a specific HCF. Each institution outlines their specific design optimization modeling approach below.

MOSES Optimization Approach
To solve the optimization problem, MOSES employs a Genetic algorithm that leverages principles of natural
selection and genetics to generate a set of trade-off optimal solutions using evolutionary strategies. The
optimization problem has been implemented in Python using Pymoo and follows the general framework of the
NSGA-II algorithm with modified survival selection. The algorithm produces a Pareto front for the two
objectives, NPV and HCF. For each design, the optimizer performs an annual yield assessment with hourly
resolution, followed by post-processing, to calculate KPIs. During the annual yield assessment, dispatch
optimization is conducted using Pyomo to manage the system's decisions regarding when to store electricity,
when to store heat, and when to generate electricity, with the dispatch strategy tailored to maximize revenues.
HOPP Optimization Approach
Within this study, the HOPP modeling framework uses a two-step approach for sampling the design space
and optimizing the design variables. In the first step, HOPP uses centroidal Voroni tessellation sampling to
generate 1000 sample designs. Each sample is evaluated using an hourly annual simulation and sample
evaluation utilizes parallel processing. Once all samples are evaluated, the design variables and
corresponding objective function values are provided to a non-linear black-box optimization algorithm that
iterates over the design space further to improve on the best-known solution. For this study, HOPP uses skopt
gp_minimize, a black-box Bayesian optimizer, to optimize the design variables. This algorithm is terminated
after 50 iterations. To sweep HCF, we added an HCF constraint to the objective function to penalize any

To preserve the computational tractability of the problem, in the single-step optimization, the input profiles with

possible to assess accurately the plant performances under real operating conditions and compute the actual
values of KPIs in the post-processing phase.

YACOP Optimization Approach

YACOP uses the Non-Dominated Sorting Genetic Algorithm (NSGA II) to perform the design optimization runs.
The algorithm is parametrized with a population size of 96 and a maximum of 30 generations. It directly outputs
a Pareto front of the two objectives NPV and HCF. For each design, the optimizer evaluates an annual yield
assessment with hourly fidelity, followed by post processing, to calculate KPIs. During the annual yield
assessment, a rule-based heuristic defines the operating strategy. This heuristic always tries to send the
maximum possible electric power to the grid without incorporating the price signal; therefore, the dispatch is
not optimized. The component operation is defined using priorities: The PV power is primarily sent to the grid.
PV excess power is sent to the BESS. If the BESS is fully charged, PV excess power is sent to the TES via
the EH. If the TES is fully charged or no EH is available, PV excess power is curtailed. If the PV power is not
sufficient to reach the targeted system power output and the TES is not empty, the PB is used. The ST always
charges the TES. If the TES is fully charged and ST heat is available, the ST heat is curtailed.

The largest deviations observed were associated with OPEX and revenues which are both strongly dependent
on the system control strategy (i.e., dispatch optimization versus heuristic approaches). With hybrid systems,
there are always decisions to be made regarding when technologies should generate and when storage
should be charged or discharged. For most cases, these decisions are trivial e.g., PV generation is available
and equals or exceeds demand then PV power is dispatched. However, operating decisions become
particularly interesting when there is excess generation and storage is nearing capacity. Each system control
strategy will have biases built into the logic and assumptions. Even a mixed integer linear programming
approach (i.e., dispatch optimization) will be sensitive to model fidelity, cost assumptions, and estimated sub-
system performance.

For this case study, we optimized the system design by maximizing NPV for a given HCF. This case study

5. Recommendations for CSP Hybrid System Modeling
In this study, we investigated the techno-economic optimization of CSP-PV hybrid systems using four tools
with different modeling capabilities. This chapter presents recommendations for modeling such systems,
focusing on lessons learned from harmonizing and comparing different tools, covering three key aspects:
standardization, modeling and optimization, and tool development.

October 2026                                                                                  56

5.1. Standardization

We encountered significant challenges in harmonizing the inputs and outputs of the different tools. After
several iterations, we developed a set of technical parameters and component models, alongside financial
parameters, cost functions, and economic models. These were largely consistent across all tools, with
deviations of less than 5% in overall performance comparisons. While standardizing inputs for hybrid system
models may be beneficial, different modeling approaches and levels of detail are required depending on the
use case, which often necessitates varying input data. Therefore, the harmonized inputs and equations
provided are intended to serve as guidance for the techno-economic assessment of CSP-PV hybrid systems.
The provided use case enables users and tool developers to build and verify CSP-PV hybrid models.
Currently, there exists no standardized techno-economic model for CSP-PV hybrid systems. While the models
in this study showed general agreement, there were some significant differences that resulted in deviations
between models. Additionally, the agreement achieved within this study is not guaranteed for use cases and
configuration not explored.

We recommend standardizing model outputs to facilitate the comparison of hybrid configurations with each
other and with standalone solutions, independent of the modeling approach used. In Section 3.4, we defined
key design and performance indicators such as annual energy shares, PV shares, and HCF (Hybrid Capacity
Factor), which can be used to compare system structures and component utilization across different designs.
However, this is not a fully comprehensive list of KDIs and KPIs for all CSP hybrid configurations. More work
is needed to standardize these indicators and to determine methods for fair comparisons across technologies.

For economic assessments, we do not recommend specific metrics, as they depend on the case study,
required level of detail, and available information. However, we emphasize that LCOE cannot fully capture
technology dispatchability and market incentives such as NPV or benefit-to-cost ratio. 60 Additionally, we
recommend analyzing economic metrics in conjunction with the HCF. If economic metrics are evaluated
without considering HCF or the time of generation, standalone plants with low capacity factors are often
identified as the optimal solution. Since the benefits of hybrid systems typically emerge at high HCFs, we
advise always assessing economic KPIs over a range of HCFs.

5.2. Modeling and Optimization

The tools in this study employed different modeling approaches and optimization methods to perform the
techno-economic assessment of CSP-PV hybrid systems. These methods have distinct advantages and
limitations, which are discussed in this section.

For linear and mixed-integer programs, non-linear behavior must be represented through linearized equations,
which can introduce modeling errors. Increasing the model’s detail adds variables and equations, which can
lengthen solution time. However, convex problems guarantee a global optimum, and several solvers
(commercial and open-source) are available to effectively handle such problems. Black-box algorithms, on
the other hand, optimize by iteratively running the model without using internal mathematical information,
instead basing improvements on input-output relationships. This can be computationally intensive because
the algorithm must execute the model an unknown number of iterations to explore the solution space and find
a local optimum. Handling constraints and defining termination conditions are non-trivial tasks, and finding the
global solution is not guaranteed. However, black-box methods impose no restrictions on the underlying
mathematical formulation which enables increased model complexity and fidelity.

60 Hamilton, William, Martinek, Janna, Cox, John, and Newman, Alexandra. Integrating Concentrating Solar Power Technologies into the Hybrid
Optimization and Performance Platform (HOPP). United States: N. p., 2022. Web. doi:10.2172/1884793.

October 2026                                                                                                                 57

Different combinations of deterministic and black-box optimization are used to assess hybrid systems in the
tools. Regardless of the combination of techniques, two stages must be considered in the techno-economic
optimization of CSP-PV hybrid systems: optimal dispatch and optimal design. The primary differences in the
approaches used are summarized as follows:

  Single-stage deterministic models (e.g., AESOPT) are computationally efficient as they solve both
 dispatch and design optimization simultaneously. These models combine operational and design
 variables, capturing all coupling effects. However, linearized models should be used to characterize
 each plant component, and the time horizon spans an entire typical year, resulting in large
 optimization problems. Clustering time-dependent inputs into typical periods can reduce problem
 size and improve computational speed but at the cost of accuracy.
  Two-stage approaches (e.g., HOPP, MoSES) decouple design and dispatch optimization by using
 deterministic methods for dispatch and black-box algorithms for design. Dispatch problems are
 solved iteratively as the system is simulated through time, using techniques such as rolling horizons
 to forecast system performance in the near-future (e.g., 48 or 72 hours). This approach requires the
 development and maintenance of two models, i.e., a technology simulation model and a dispatch
 optimization model, as well as complex methods for communicating and executing dispatch signals
 with the technology simulation model. Deviations between these two models reduces overall solution
 quality, e.g., the dispatch problem predicts more or less energy production, and the simulation model
 must handle discrepancy. While this approach increases computational requirements, particularly
 with many designs, there are no modeling restrictions for KPIs, e.g., cash flow discounting for battery
 replacement. Parallelizing design evaluations can accelerate the process. However, some black-box
 algorithms do not support parallelization of evaluations, thereby requiring parallelization to occur
 during initial sampling.
  Heuristic approaches (e.g., YACOP) use a heuristic for dispatch and a black-box algorithm for
 design optimization. While dispatch results are suboptimal due to predefined decisions in the
 heuristic, there are no modeling restrictions for components or KPIs. Heuristic based dispatch can be
 easy to implement, fast to solve, and provide accurate solutions for simple operating scenarios.
 However, they are not as flexible when evaluating economically driven operations. This approach
 enables highly detailed models but can be computationally demanding, depending on the complexity
 of the design evaluations.

No single approach is universally recommended, as all produced qualitatively comparable results in this study.
However, key observations from tool comparisons include:

  Depending on the design algorithm, different methods can optimize economic KPIs considering the
 HCF. Multi-objective algorithms such as NSGA-II produce Pareto fronts showing trade-offs between
 objectives. Single-objective algorithms can use HCF constraints to compare economic KPIs for
 various HCF values.
  Mixed-integer linear program solvers are more efficient in terms of computation time and solution
 quality but struggle with complex correlations, such as nonlinearities or replacement costs.
  Black-box algorithms are easier to apply and free from modeling constraints, though they typically
 result in longer computational times.
  When time-varying electric prices are considered, omitting a dispatch optimizer can lead to
 suboptimal solutions, with lower NPV over HCF, as demonstrated in Chapter 4.3. The optimality gap
 depends on the heuristic strategy and its complexity. Additionally, omitting start-up and ramping
 costs within dispatch optimization can lead to optimistic revenue estimates.
  Differences in model fidelity can lead to moderate deviations in optimization results. For example,
 MoSES and AESOPT neglect CSP auxiliary power and power block start-up and shut-down energy

  Minor differences in economic models can cause significant deviations in KPIs. For instance, HOPP

5.3. Tool Development

While comparing tools, we identified two user groups: standard users and expert users, each with different
requirements. Both groups aim to perform techno-economic optimizations to investigate hybrid and stand-
alone solutions for specific use cases. The tool should enable multi-objective optimization of hybrid
configurations, where users define meteorological data, demand profiles, and market incentives. Component
behavior and an operational or dispatch optimization objective should also be defined. The outputs should
include the KPIs defined in this report.

Standard users require a user-friendly interface that allows them to easily create new projects and select
different technology configurations. They should be able to use default values for component inputs and
customize meteorological data, demand profiles, and market incentives. A graphical user interface (GUI) and
user documentation would facilitate easy setup, execution, and interpretation of results.

Expert users may prefer a more flexible programming interface to model complex use cases and explore
innovative solutions. Key features for expert users could include the ability to:

 - Adapt and add component models
 - Modify dispatch strategies
 - Adjust system configurations
 - Incorporate project-specific cost functions and KPIs

We suggest supporting the availability of tools that are easy to handle, enabling non-expert users to explore
the advantages of hybrid systems. For research on hybrid systems, expert interfaces are necessary with
higher flexibility to investigate innovative hybrid solutions and use cases. By providing tools that cater to
different user groups and requirements, we could promote the exploration of hybrid systems' benefits, support
market growth, and foster further research activities.

6. Conclusions
CSP-PV hybrid systems can provide dispatchable renewable energy generation at very high HCFs at a lower
cost than mono-technology solar solutions. Locations that are favorable for CSP development are also
favorable to PV development, meaning that these two technologies will inherently be deployed within the
same regions. During the solar day, PV is the lowest cost renewable energy source currently on the market;
however, electrochemical battery storage is cost-prohibitive for durations greater than 4 to 6 hours. CSP, when
paired with TES, is a dispatchable renewable energy source that enables storage durations from 10 to 18
hours; however, solar field and receiver costs constitute a large portion of CAPEX. CSP-PV hybrids systems
enable the exploitation of individual technological strengths while minimizing their weaknesses.

Within this study, we compared four CSP-PV hybrid modeling tools developed by four independent research
institutions. These models include MoSES, developed by KTH Royal Institute of Technology, HOPP,
developed by the National Laboratory of the Rockies (in collaboration with Colorado School of Mines),
AESOPT, developed by Politecnico di Milano, and YACOP developed by the DLR Institute of Solar Research.
We provided background on each of these modeling frameworks, including strengths and weaknesses, and
drew comparisons between them. As a team, we harmonized model inputs and outputs for the conducted
round-robin cases studies. These case studies include: i) fixed design variable values, ii) design optimization
with variable demand profile, and iii) fixed design variable values and design optimization with a flat demand
profile. In general, the models showed agreement; however, there were significant differences because of
underlining model methodologies and/or assumptions, some of which were not fully investigated within this
work.

With fixed design variables values, the four CSP hybrid models were able to predict KPIs within a deviation of
approximately ± 5%. The largest deviations observed were associated with OPEX and revenues which are
both strongly dependent on the system control strategy (i.e., dispatch optimization versus heuristic
approaches). Each model within this study uses different control strategies for the CSP hybrid system. These
control strategies have different assumptions, fidelity, and priorities which impact the scheduling of generation
and storage assets. Within this work, we did not fully explore each model’s formulation of hybrid control. The
second case study within this work conducted design optimization with variable demand profile to maximize
NPV for HCFs from 30% to 95% (with and without electric heaters). For our case study, the NPV was negative
for HCFs greater than 40%. We do not impose any energy storage incentives within this study. Positive-NPV
system designs with high-HCFs could be achieved through lower system costs and/or higher revenue
streams. This work presented the optimal technology combinations from HCFs of 30% to 95%; PV-only
systems below 40% HCF, PV-BESS systems from 40-60% HCF, CSP with TES, and PV systems for HCFs
greater than 60% where the BESS is reintroduced at very high HCFs (i.e., 95%). When EHs are included
within the solution space, they can significantly impact NPVs for HCFs above 90% by providing flexibility by
converting excess PV to TES. Lastly, we repeated the first two case studies with a flat demand profile of 50
MWe. Flat demand profiles provide the hybrid system with fewer operational constraints which enables more
favorable economic outcomes. This case study highlights that dispatch optimization outperforms heuristic
control approaches.

To finish this work, we create recommendations for improving the modeling and optimization of CSP-PV hybrid
systems. We highlight that standardization of inputs and outputs would enable a cross comparison of various
hybrid configurations and stand-alone systems. We highlighted a summary of the different modeling
approaches used for CSP hybrid systems. Lastly, we discussed that tool development should be catered to
two user groups: standard users requiring a user-friendly interface with default values and simple
customization options and expert users requiring more flexibility to modify models and control strategies.

Appendix C – Results of Fixed Design Comparison
The results of the comparison between the fixed design cases at varying HCF levels are presented below.
The aim was to compare predicted KPIs from each model using the optimal design solutions identified by two
of the four models (AESOPT and HOPP) across different HCF levels (30%, 50%, 70%, and 90). Specifically,
for each HCF, the optimal design determined by the selected institution (i.e., AESOPT or HOPP) was used as
a reference. The design variables of the other models were set to the same values, and the results were
compared by calculating the deviations of KPIs relative to the reference model.

Although an initial comparison between the models using a fixed design was discussed in Section 4.1, a
similar analysis was repeated for various HCF levels to assess the sensitivity of the model comparison to
changes in HCF, and consequently to variations in design configurations. This approach facilitates the
identification of discrepancies and deviations due to modelling difference in single subsystems (i.e., PV, CSP,
BESS), which may be difficult to detect when analyzing the hybrid configuration involving all plant components,
as outlined earlier in this report.

agreement regarding the AEY for the PV (deviation < 1.3%), YACOP exhibits a more substantial deviation (-

production starting from the weather data (AESOPT uses SAM, HOPP and MoSES use PySAM and YACOP

reasons. The large disparity in CAPEX of MoSES and AESOPT can be attributed to differences in the
assumed number of BESS replacements compared to YACOP and HOPP. MoSES predicts one replacement
due to low BESS utilization, AESOPT predicts two replacements due to higher lifetime assumed for the battery,
and YACOP and HOPP both assume three replacements. Additionally, different financial models have been
used to account for BESS replacements (see Section 3.4.2) which further contribute to CAPEX differences,
indirectly affecting NPV values. In terms of system performance, a reduction in AEY, HCF, and revenues is
observed across all tools when compared to the corresponding values achieved by HOPP for the same design
configuration.

At 70% HCF, the optimal configuration combines PV with CSP, eliminating the need for battery storage.
Without the presence of a BESS, deviations on CAPEX are visibly reduced, as illustrated in Figure 35.
However, a non-negligible deviation remains for AESOPT, primarily due to the linearization error introduced
by the piecewise linear approximation of the investment cost of the CSP section of the plant, including the
power tower, receiver, solar field, and power block. In terms of AEY and HCF, the positive deviations observed
for MoSES and AESOPT are a result of the simplified CSP model, which does not account for start-up energy
losses and parasitic loads for the CSP sub-system. This leads to an overestimation of the annual energy
production from the CSP sub-system. Even though YACOP shows similar performance to HOPP of the CSP
AEY due to the similarities in auxiliaries consumption modelling, it results in a lower AEY. This difference may
be attributed to the differences in estimated PV production, as previously noted for the 30% HCF. The
discussed deviations in plant HCF and AEY translate into positive (for MoSES and AESOPT) and negative
(for YACOP) deviations in plant revenues, and correspondingly opposite deviations in LCOE (-3.4% and -
5.7% for MoSES and AESOPT, +1.9% for YACOP). Overall, a good level of agreement is observed across
most KPIs, with the maximum deviation found in AESOPT's NPV (+9.9%). The configuration at 90% HCF
remains the same as at 70% HCF in terms of plant sub-systems, with the only notable difference being the
increased component sizes to meet the higher HCF target. As shown in Figure 35, the increase in component
size has a minimal impact on the trend of KPI deviations, which remains similar to those observed for 70%
HCF, therefore analogous conclusions can be drawn. In general, the KPIs for the solution combining PV and
CSP at both 70% and 90% HCF show a high degree of agreement, with limited maximum deviations in HCF,
AEY (<5%), and NPV (<10%).
