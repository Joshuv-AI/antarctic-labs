Association of tryptophan metabolites with cognitive impairment in chronic kidney disease

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundChronic kidney disease (CKD) leads to the accumulation of uremic toxins (UTs), including tryptophan-derived metabolites such as kynurenine (KYN), indoxyl sulfate (IS), and indole-3-acetic acid (IAA). IS has been identified as a potential contributor to cognitive impairment (CI), whereas less is known about the other tryptophan metabolites. We aimed to evaluate the association between these toxins related to tryptophan metabolism and CI in patients with CKD.MethodsThree thousand thirty-three patients with CKD stages 2–5 enrolled in CKD–Renal Epidemiology and Information Network (CKD-REIN) cohort. Patients with both UT measurement and cognitive function screening [with mini-mental state examination (MMSE)] were included. CI was defined as an MMSE score ≤ 26/30. Logistic regression was used to assess the association between the studied UTs and MMSE.ResultsOf the 2389 included patients [mean (SD) age: 66.5 (12.8) years, 66% males, mean (SD) estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) 34.8 (13.4) ml/min/1.73 m2], 858 patients (35.9%) had an MMSE score ≤26/30. After adjustment for sociodemographic factors, cardiovascular risk factors, cerebrovascular disease, current depression, eGFR, urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio, and UTs known to be associated with CI, no significant association was found between KYN or IAA and MMSE ≤26/30, whereas IS was significantly associated [Odds Ratio (OR), 95% confidence interval: 1.11 (1.00–1.21)].ConclusionAmong tryptophan-derived metabolites studied, IS was associated with CI in patients with CKD, in contrast to KYN and IAA. Despite sharing a common origin, the UTs studied follow distinct metabolic pathways with different effects, highlighting the need for further investigation of the tryptophan pathway on CI in CKD.

INTRODUCTION

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a common disease affecting around 9%–13% of the adult population worldwide [1], and is a global public health problem due to the progressive increase in its prevalence, frequent complications and high cost to society [2]. CKD is commonly comorbid with hypertension, diabetes, and cardiovascular (CV) disease, and neurological complications include stroke, cognitive impairment (Cl), and depression. Various pathophysiological hypotheses highlighted a significant link between CKD and CI [3–5]. However, the underlying pathophysiology and risk factors remain unclear.

In CKD, gut dysbiosis and declining kidney function lead to the accumulation of uremic toxins (UTs) [6] defined as solutes retained due to impaired renal excretion that exert deleterious biological effects. These toxins are mainly derived from amino acid metabolism, particularly tryptophan, and disrupt biological or biochemical functions; their potential toxicity has been demonstrated in several studies [7–10]. Tryptophan is an essential amino acid for protein synthesis, playing a vital role in various metabolic processes [11]. It is the precursor for several bioactive compounds through the kynurenine (KYN), indole, and serotonin pathways (Supplementary Fig. S1).

As tryptophan metabolites, KYN, indoxyl sulfate (IS), and indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) have emerged as significant UTs, particularly in CKD. We recently showed an association between high KYN levels and CV disease in a large cohort of non-dialyzed CKD patients [10]. Likewise, elevated IAA levels were associated with CV disease and higher mortality rates in CKD, including patients on dialysis [12]. Beyond vascular damage associated with tryptophan-derived UTs, several studies have shown an association between IS and CI in both dialysis and non-dialysis patients. Besides vascular CI, other metabolic pathways and toxic mechanisms appear to be involved, though not fully understood [13, 14]. However, there is a lack of studies on the neurotoxic effects of many tryptophan-derived metabolites, except IS. Other UTs, such as para-cresyl sulfate (pCS), phenylacetylglutamine (PAG), and trimethylamine oxide (TMAO), have also shown potential neurotoxicity and effect on cognition [15–20].

The complex interplay between UTs and CI in CKD warrants further research. Therefore, we aimed to investigate the association of tryptophan-derived metabolites with cognition, independent of UTs already known to affect cognitive function.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and population

We conducted a cross-sectional data analysis obtained from the prospective CKD–Renal Epidemiology and Information Network (CKD-REIN) cohort study (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT 03381950) [21]. The study protocol was approved by the institutional review board at the French National Institute of Health and Medical Research (Paris; IRB 00003888).

This cohort was conducted from 40 representative nephrology outpatient clinics in France between July 2013 and April 2016 and included 3033 patients with CKD stages 2–5, with no previous dialysis or transplantation, and who were followed by nephrologists for up to 5 years. The patients were above ≥18 years old and provided written informed consent. We included patients with available Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) scores and UT measurements, within a maximum 3-month interval.

Data collection

Data was collected by trained clinical research associates (CRAs) from medical records or patient interviews at baseline and then annually. All patients underwent standard urine and blood tests (recommended by the French health authorities in routine CKD care). GFR was estimated with the 2009 CKD-EPI equation using serum creatinine values [21, 22]. Urine albumin-creatinine ratio (uACR) was measured directly or estimated from proteinuria measurements. We used the sociodemographic data (age, sex, education level, living alone/not), history of cerebrovascular or CV disease, heart failure, current smoking, obesity, history of diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and systolic blood pressure. Depressive symptoms were evaluated with the short form Centre for Epidemiologic Studies Depression scale (CESD) [23]. Current depression at baseline was defined as a CESD score ≥ 10/30 or antidepressant use.

A detailed protocol for data collection was published elsewhere [24]. Supplementary Table S1 provides more information about the variables used in this study.

Uremic toxins measurement

Upon enrolment of the patients, serum samples were collected, immediately stored at 4°C and aliquoted within 6 h without additional processing. All CKD-REIN samples were stored frozen (−80°C) at the Biobanque de Picardie (BRIF number: BB-0033-00017) and shipped frozen for analysis to Garches. KYN, IS, IAA, pCS, TMAO, PAG, and tryptophan fractions were assayed in serum using a validated liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry technique [25]. Among these UTs, TMAO and PAG are small, water-soluble molecules; the others are protein-bound; thus, serum UT concentrations were reported as free and total (Supplementary Table S1).

Assessment of MMSE

The MMSE was administered by trained CRAs. This 30-item questionnaire (1 point/question) is frequently used to screen CI before further in-depth investigations. We analyzed the baseline MMSE score.

In our study, we used the MMSE score cut-off of ≤26/30 to define CI. The <24/30 cutoff is often used to detect major neurocognitive disorder (previously known as dementia); the ≤26/30 cutoff is used in epidemiological studies for detecting mild cognitive decline in patients lacking major neurocognitive disorder [26]. Additionally, since the ability to give informed consent was required for study participation, we did not expect to observe any individuals with a major neurocognitive disorder or a score of <24/30.

Statistical analyses

The patients' baseline characteristics were described according to CKD stages, categorized by estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) levels (≥45, 30–44, and <30 ml/min/1.73 m²). MMSE scores were also described according to the KYN, IS, and IAA tertiles.

Given evidence in the literature that pCS, PAG, and TMAO may be associated with CI [18–20], these UTs were also incorporated into our statistical models. Since KYN, IS, pCS, IAA, and tryptophan are albumin-bound UTs, the free fraction represents the biologically active component responsible for their effects. Therefore, we evaluated the free forms rather than the total concentrations. In contrast, analyses for TMAO and PAG were performed using total concentrations, as these compounds are not protein-bound.

Categorical variables were presented as percentages and compared by the Chi-square test; continuous variables were compared by ANOVA or the Kruskal-Wallis test (depending on the normality of distribution). Logistic regression was used to evaluate the association between KYN, IS, and IAA levels and MMSE ≤26/30. As the UT concentrations did not show a normal distribution, we used a log-transformed scale of these values. Consequently, the association with MMSE ≤26/30 should be interpreted as corresponding to a doubling of UT level. First model adjusted for the unmodifiable sociodemographic factors (age, sex, education level); second model adjusted for the additional CV risk factors (systolic blood pressure per 10 mmHg increase, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, obesity, current smoking), cerebrovascular disease, current depression and log2 transformation of UTs previously reported in the literature to be associated with cognition: log2[IS], log2 [pCS], log2[PAG] and log2[TMAO]; the third model additionally adjusted for kidney function parameters eGFR and uACR. Moreover, we tested for collinearity between variables by calculating the variance inflation factor (VIF, using the car package in R).

In sensitivity analysis, UT tertiles were considered categorical variables, with the lowest tertile as reference. Additional analyses evaluated the association of log2[tryptophan] (considered both as a continuous and categorical tertile variable) as a precursor in these pathways. The same adjustments were applied.

Multiple imputation of missing data with chained equations was applied, with the creation of 30 datasets of 30 iterations each [27]. All variables presented in the logistic regressions were included in the imputation procedure. Odds ratios (ORs) were quoted with their corresponding 95% confidence interval; the threshold for statistical significance was set to P < .05. Statistical analyses were performed using R software® with the technological platform France Cohortes (version 7.2.1).

RESULTS

Characteristics of the study population

We included 2389 patients in the analysis (Fig. 1, Table 1). The mean (SD) age was 66.5 (12.8) years, 66% males, and the mean (SD) eGFR was 34.8 (13.4) ml/min/1.73 m2. As eGFR declined, the percentage of patients who had CV risk factors such as hypertension and obesity increased significantly (P = .001). At later stages of the disease, patients were more likely to have an increased mean systolic blood pressure and polymedication (P<.001). A significant difference in uACR distribution and significantly lower MMSE scores were observed with reduced eGFR (P < .001).

The median [inter-quartile range (IQR)] KYN, IS, IAA, and tryptophan levels were 0.13 [0.09–0.18], 0.05 [0.03–0.09], 0.03 [0.02–0.05] and 0.91 [0.7–1.2] mg/l, respectively. A significant increase was observed in these UTs as eGFR declined. The median [IQR] MMSE score was 28 [25–29], and 858 patients (35.9%) had an MMSE ≤26/30 (Table 1).

The distribution of MMSE ≤26/30 across KYN tertiles differed significantly (P = .03). In the low, medium, and high tertiles of KYN, the percentages of patients with MMSE ≤26/30 were 32.6%, 36.3%, and 38.9%, respectively. Similarly, a significant difference was observed across the tertiles of IS (P < .001), with 40.7% of patients having MMSE ≤26/30 in the highest tertile compared to the low and medium tertiles, 31.1% and 36.1% respectively. In contrast, no significant difference was found across the tertiles of IAA (Fig. 2). The percentage of patients with lower MMSE scores was 38.8% in the highest tertile of tryptophan compared to the low and medium tertiles with 32.8% and 36.1%, respectively (P = .044) (Supplementary Fig. S2).

Association between UT concentrations and MMSE

Crude analysis demonstrated that a two-fold increase in UT concentrations was associated with MMSE ≤26/30 [OR 95% (confidence interval)] 1.17 [1.04–1.31] for KYN, 1.15 [1.05–1.25] for IAA, and 1.13 [1.06–1.19] for IS. For IS concentrations, the association remained significant after statistical adjustments in all three models (adjusted ORs 1.09 [1.03–1.17]; 1.10 [1.00–1.21]; 1.11 [1.00–1.21], respectively, for models 1, 2, and 3). The association was no longer significant for KYN in model 1 (OR 1.05 [0.93–1.18]), and for IAA in model 2 (OR 1.05 [0.94–1.17]) (Table 2). There were no interactions or collinearities between UTs or between UTs and eGFR.

DISCUSSION

In this large cohort study, we characterized tryptophan metabolites to investigate potential associations with lower cognitive performance in the CKD population without kidney replacement therapy. We observed a significant association between higher free serum IS levels and lower MMSE scores independently of factors associated with CI, such as age, sex, education level, CV risk factors, cerebrovascular disease, current depression, eGFR, uACR, and other UTs (pCS, TMAO, PAG) known to contribute to CI. However, we did not observe such an association for KYN and IAA. The results for IS are consistent with previously published data, supporting earlier studies conducted in smaller cohorts of CKD patients, including those on dialysis [13, 14]. To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate IS together with other tryptophan-derived UTs, while adjusting for the other UTs contributing to CI in a large cohort of patients with CKD.

The most studied UTs in CI are IS [13, 14], TMAO [15, 20], pCS [16, 18], and recently, PAG [19]. In CKD-associated neurological complications, tryptophan is frequently mentioned in depression research as a precursor of serotonin [28]. However, the role of tryptophan and its metabolites in cognitive processes has been less investigated. Tryptophan metabolism is one of the most altered metabolisms in kidney failure [29]. We examined different branches of the same metabolic pathway, which led to the production of various UTs. Besides IS, we selected KYN and IAA specifically because there is limited data regarding their relationship with CI in CKD. We also incorporated UTs originating from other pathways (pCS, TMAO, and PAG) into our adjustments. Our data showing a significant association between increased IS levels and CI, but not with KYN or IAA, suggest that different biological mechanisms may underlie the neurocognitive consequences of different tryptophan pathways.

Around 1% of tryptophan is metabolized into serotonin, whereas ∼95% of free tryptophan follows the KYN pathway [30] with two enzymes: indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO) and tryptophan-2,3-dioxygenase (Supplementary Fig. S1).

IS is a product of the indole pathway and induces inflammatory processes, oxidative stress by increasing reactive oxygen species (ROS), and endothelial dysfunction [31], all of which contribute to increased CV risk [8, 32, 33] and neurological complications. IS-induced aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) activation is linked to blood-brain barrier (BBB) disruption and CI [34, 35]. By activating AhR and nuclear factor-kB, IS induces inflammation, leading to neuronal death [36]. Since all IS, KYN, and IAA are AhR ligands, the association observed in our study with IS and CI, but not with KYN and IAA, suggests that IS may interfere with cognitive function through mechanisms other than only AhR. In addition to BBB disruption, IS also inhibits organic anion transporter-3, resulting in the accumulation of IS in the brain. In human astrocytes under oxidative stress conditions, IS promotes cell apoptosis by inhibiting the mitogen-activated protein kinase signaling pathways and glycolysis [36–38]. Moreover, increased IS may cause cerebrovascular lesions and possibly contribute to neuronal dysfunction or cell death [13, 14, 37].

Regarding IAA and the gut-brain axis, the recent results have been noteworthy. Previously, a study has reported that higher serum IAA concentrations were correlated with depression, anxiety and quality of sleep in CKD patients [31]. Consistently, another study involving 230 hemodialysis patients demonstrated that elevated IAA levels were associated with poor cognitive function [39]. In contrast, a recent mouse model study suggested that IAA supplementation may improve sleep deprivation-induced CI [40]. Another study in mice indicated that IAA attenuates oxidative stress and inflammatory responses via AhR in microglia, showing a protective effect against sepsis-associated encephalopathy, and also improving CI [41]. However, our analysis did not reveal an association between IAA and lower cognitive performance. Further research is needed to clarify the role of IAA in the relationship between gut dysbiosis and CKD-induced CI.

The inflammatory conditions (commonly observed in CKD) upregulate IDO activity, leading to increased KYN production [30]. IS can also induce IDO enzyme via AhR activation [42], generating KYN metabolites. Due to impaired BBB permeability, downstream toxic metabolites, such as quinolinic acid, cause neurotoxicity [29, 30]. Even if our results are not significant, it would be worthwhile studying the KYN pathway more specifically in patients with more pronounced CI. As stated previously, the higher serum KYN levels are associated with CV complications [10]. These findings suggest that multiple pathways appear to be involved, and that the independent association of KYN with non-atheromatous CV complications, but not with atheromatous disease, may not be reflected in cognitive function. The primary driver leading to vascular cognitive decline appears to be atherosclerotic-related. If KYN influences non-atheromatous CV complications rather than atheromatous disease and exerts its role in uremic toxicity, this may explain the lack of an observed association with cognitive dysfunction or indicate that such an effect has not yet become apparent at this stage of CKD-induced CI. Our analyses did not reveal an association between KYN and CI in CKD.

Overall, current evidence does not clearly define how the balance between indole, KYN, and serotonin pathways of tryptophan metabolism is influenced by CKD. In a CKD 5/6 nephrectomy rat model, the tryptophan metabolism was shifted to the KYN pathway instead of indole and serotonin due to inflammation, indicating an activation of IDO and tryptophan catabolism [43]. Similarly, a small study in CKD patients reported an increased tryptophan degradation via the KYN pathway due to the loss of precursor tryptophan, without evident elimination of tryptophan metabolism via the serotonin pathway [31]. The mechanisms maintaining the balance between KYN metabolites and neuroactive serotonin are not clearly known. It is plausible that CKD may alter this balance, leading to decreased serotonin formation. Reduced serotonin, chronic inflammation and accumulation of tryptophan-derived UTs, such as KYN and IS, represent shared mechanisms underlying both depression and CI. These factors exhibit a bidirectional relationship, with each potentially exacerbating the other [35]. In our study, we did not find an association between KYN and CI. However, the distribution of tryptophan metabolites warrants further investigation for both depression and CI in patients with CKD.

The free serum concentrations of KYN, IS, and IAA observed in our cohort were substantially higher than those reported in the healthy volunteers [44], consistent with the known accumulation of these compounds in CKD and our results. Future studies with matched healthy controls could better quantify the increase and clarify the pathophysiological significance of these metabolites.

Another possible explanation may be the different transporter systems of KYN [45], IAA [39], and IS [13, 14] which may influence the permeability and passage across the BBB, and therefore, their impacts on cognition. In addition, since these protein-bound UTs are cleared from the kidneys and accumulate in CKD, their elimination and distribution may also differ due to the transport system across the renal proximal tubule [46].

Taken together, these findings underscore the importance of developing strategies to reduce the excess production of IS or increase its excretion for the prevention or treatment of the CKD-induced CI.

Our study has several strengths. First, this study included a large sample size of patients who are nationally representative of nephrology practices in France. This allowed for high statistical power and permitted extensive adjustments for confounders, including other UTs known to be associated with CI. Second, UT assays were centralized using the validated, robust ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry technique. Third, MMSE is a widely accepted assessment method and was conducted by CRAs.

Study limitations include: first, a causal relationship cannot be established due to the study design, which is an epidemiological longitudinal cohort study. Second, regarding the results and the lack of data to evaluate the serotonergic pathway, our study design cannot address any mechanistic question. Third, although the MMSE measures global cognition, it is not an effective screening tool for executive dysfunction, which is the domain most early affected in CKD, potentially introducing an assessment bias. More sensitive and specific cognitive tests should be applied to evaluate the functional cognition in CKD patients. In our patient population, cognitive function was relatively preserved, which may be interpreted as a selection bias inherent in all cohorts [47]. Nevertheless, even in a population with relatively preserved cognitive function, a significant difference in MMSE scores across CKD stages and an independent association of IS with CI have been observed. In our cohort study, the 30-item MMSE was considered a discrete rather than a continuous variable. Therefore, instead of linear models, we performed logistic regression by converting the MMSE into a binary variable as being ≤26/30 or >26/30. Furthermore, the sensitivity analysis with an alternative threshold of MMSE <24/30 was not significant. The smaller number of patients with an MMSE <24/30 may have reduced the statistical power of the study, potentially limiting the detection of an association. The previous studies suggesting that IS contributes to CI, along with the inability to demonstrate this association in the present study, may also be considered as supporting evidence for this hypothesis. Lastly, tryptophan metabolism is influenced by gut and microbiota; however, our study did not include any parameters related to nutrition or diet.

In conclusion, we highlighted that among tryptophan circulating derivatives, higher IS levels are independently associated with CI in patients with CKD, in contrast to KYN and IAA. Further studies are needed to explore tryptophan metabolism in CKD and the interactions between pathways. Determining biological risk factors for neurological complications will help optimize therapeutic management and develop new treatment strategies.
