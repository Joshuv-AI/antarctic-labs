Effect of Medial Unicompartmental Knee Arthroplasty on Clinical Outcomes and Stress Distribution of Patellofemoral Joint
Authors: Yang Ding, Hua‐zhang Xiong, Kai‐bo Sun, Yi Zeng, Yong Nie, Yuan‐gang Wu, Bin Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate the clinical and biomechanical effect of medial fixed‐bearing unicompartmental knee arthroplasty (UKA) on the patellofemoral joint (PFJ). However, whether patellofemoral joint (PFJ) degeneration should be considered a contraindication for UKA remains controversial. In addition, the biomechanical effects of UKA on PFJ stress distribution have not been fully elucidated.MethodThirty‐two patients (35 knees) who underwent medial fixed‐bearing UKA were included with a mean of 4.5 years follow‐up. The clinical parameters including the Oxford Knee Score (OKS), Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index score (WOMAC), and Visual Analogue Scale (VAS), and radiographic parameters including lateral patellofemoral angle (LPFA), congruence angle (CA), lateral patellar tilt angle (LPTA), posterior tibial slope (PTS), and hip‐knee‐ankle (HKA) angle were compared. Finite element analysis (FEA) was performed for further investigating PFJ stress change after medial fixed‐bearing UKA.ResultsAt the final follow‐up, the clinical and radiographic parameters were significant improvement compared to the preoperative measurements (p < 0.05); there were not significantly different in the clinical parameters between patients with and without PFJ arthropathy (p > 0.05). As the knee varus angle was gradually corrected using UKA, the excessive stress in the medial PFJ was partially transferred to the lateral PFJ, and the stress in the medial and lateral PFJ tended to be balanced.ConclusionMedial fixed‐bearing UKA can improve clinical outcomes and optimize the PFJ alignment and stress distribution. The presence of PFJ arthropathy did not compromise the clinical outcomes. However, the presence of preoperative lateral PFJ arthropathy has a risk of inferior clinical outcomes due to increasing stress on the lateral PFJ after medial fixed‐bearing UKA.Keywords: biomechanical evaluation, clinical outcome, finite element analysis, patellofemoral joint, unicompartmental knee arthroplasty

Background

Unicompartmental knee arthroplasty (UKA) is a common operation with excellent results and high patient satisfaction for the treatment of medial compartmental knee osteoarthritis (OA) [1]. These favorable results are mainly attributed to the preservation of the patellofemoral joint (PFJ), lateral compartment, and cruciate ligaments [2]. However, there is still controversy about whether preoperative PFJ arthropathy affects clinical outcomes.

The progression of PFJ arthropathy has been reported as a cause for revision for UKA [3, 4], and historically, PFJ arthropathy was considered a contraindication for the procedure [5, 6].

Recently, several studies reported that neither clinical symptoms nor radiographic changes of the PFJ had been shown to affect clinical outcomes following medial UKA [7, 8, 9]. A few clinical papers have considered these satisfactory outcomes to result from the redistribution of stress and realignment of the PFJ after medial UKA [10, 11, 12]. For instance, Thein et al. [11] reported that Robotic‐Assisted (RA) medial fixed‐bearing UKA can improve PFJ alignment. While Kwon et al. [13] found that neither contact stress of PFJ nor quadriceps force significantly increased from knee extension to deep flexion after medial UKA. Nonetheless, drawing a conclusion for stress redistribution and realignment of the PFJ from which PFJ arthropathy does not affect clinical outcomes [4, 11, 14], may be premature with few patellar indices and without the objective biomechanical outcomes in the coronal plane alignment of PFJ.

Therefore, the purposes of this study were: to compare the clinical and radiographic outcomes between preoperative and postoperative in patients who underwent medial fixed‐bearing UKA; to assess whether final functional outcomes differ between patients with and without preoperative PFJ arthropathy; to further explore changes in PFJ stress after medial fixed‐bearing UKA through finite element analysis (FEA).

We hypothesized that medial fixed‐bearing UKA has a beneficial effect on improvement of the clinical and radiographic outcomes; the functional scores have not significant differences between patients with and without PFJ arthropathy at the final follow‐up, and medial fixed‐bearing UKA can optimize PFJ stress distribution.

Methods

The functional scores and radiographic data in all patients who underwent medial fixed‐bearing UKA performed by the Orthopedic Surgery of West China hospital of Sichuan University for treatment of medial compartmental terminal OA between March 2017 and February 2018 were collected. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of our hospital (No. 201302007), and all patients signed the informed consent form. The register was performed for this study in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR1900026934). All methods were performed in accordance with the Chinese Ethical Guidelines for Medical and Biological Research Involving Human Subjects, and each participant provided written informed consent.

The indications and contraindications for UKA were based on Deckard et al.'s criteria [2]. The PFJ was evaluated using standard Merchant's radiography [15] by two independent radiologists who were blinded to intraoperative PFJ grade and score outcomes. The Altman scoring and Ahlback grade system evaluated the preoperative and postoperative conditions of the PFJ [16]. The intraoperative Outerbridge classification was used to assess the cartilage of the PFJ by the surgeon, who also was blinded to radiographic Altman scoring and Ahlback grade outcomes [17]. The patients were divided into two groups according to the presence and absence of PFJ arthropathy. The normal group had no PFJ arthropathy (Outerbridge classification 0–2) and the OA group had a PFJ arthropathy (Outerbridge classification 3–4) [18]. When outcomes of Altman score and Ahlback grading for grouping were not consistent with the Outerbridge classification, grouping based on the Outerbridge classification, which is the gold standard for determining cartilage damage by direct visualization [19].

The standard medial fixed‐bearing UKA procedure [20] was performed in all patients using the Zimmer High Flex Unicompartmental Knee System (System Zimmer Inc. Warsaw, USA). The prosthesis components, including the femoral component, polyethylene insert, and tibial baseplate, were modeled based on manufacturer specifications. These components were established using standard computer‐aided design (CAD) models in IGES format, rather than reconstructed via reverse engineering. The implant geometries were directly imported into Geomagic Studio (version 12.0, 3D Systems, USA) and assembled with the anatomical knee model. Surface‐to‐surface, frictionless contact formulations were applied to the femorotibial, patellofemoral, and implant–bone interfaces. Patellar denervation was intraoperatively performed for each patient. All patients were followed up after surgery at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months, and annually thereafter, and they were radiographically and functionally evaluated before surgery and at each follow‐up visit.

Clinical Evaluation

A variety of patient‐reported outcome measures (PROMs) were evaluated by a senior author. Knee function was assessed using the Oxford Knee Score (OKS) with a total score from 0 to 48 (worst‐best) [21] and the Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index score (WOMAC) with a range from 0 to 96 (best‐worst) [22]. The pain was assessed by the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) with a scale from 0 to 10 (best‐worst) [23].

Radiographic Evaluation

The patellar indices were measured on the Merchant's views according to the method presented by Merchant et al. [24]. Preoperative and postoperative alignment of the PFJ were measured by applying the lateral patellofemoral angle (LPFA), congruence angle (CA), and lateral patellar tilt angle (LPTA) [10, 15]. The component alignment was evaluated on the standard weight‐bearing AP and lateral views of the knee based on the technique described by Gulati and Khow [25, 26]. Posterior tibial slope (PTS) was measured according to the method reported by Khow et al. [26]. The correction of genu varum was measured using hip‐knee‐ankle (HKA) angle based on preoperative and postoperative standing AP long‐leg alignment radiographs. Varus angles are designated by positive HKA angle values, while valgus angles are negative [27]. The radiographic evaluation was performed by two investigators. Inter‐observer reliability between the two observers was evaluated by applying interclass correlation coefficients (ICCs). The ICCs were interpreted using previously reported semi‐quantitative criteria [28].

FEA

The bony and soft tissue structures were extracted from computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) images of the left healthy knee of one volunteer (sex: male, age: 35 years, height: 175 cm, weight: 70 kg) according to previous methods [29]. All imaging data were imported in DICOM format and processed using Mimics software (version 10.0, Materialize, Belgium). Bone structures were segmented using a threshold‐based method, while cartilage and other soft tissues were segmented using a combination of thresholding and manual refinement. Multimodal image registration between CT and MRI datasets was performed within Mimics to ensure anatomical alignment between bony and soft tissue structures. The segmented models were exported as stereolithography (STL) files and imported into Geomagic Studio (version 12.0, 3D Systems, USA) for surface processing, including smoothing, noise reduction, and surface optimization. The processed geometries were further converted into nonuniform rational B‐spline (NURBS) surfaces and exported in IGES format for subsequent solid model generation and assembly. The three‐dimensional (3D) natural knee and neutral UKA FEA models were developed and validated at 30° of knee flexion by comparing them with models in previous studies [30]. Bone structures were meshed using 10‐node modified tetrahedral elements, whereas cartilage, menisci, and other soft tissues were meshed using eight‐node hexahedral elements, with local mesh refinement applied in the patellofemoral joint region to ensure numerical stability and convergence. Material properties were assigned according to previously published studies; in the present finite element model, the femur, tibia, patella, and fibula were defined as rigid bodies. Articular cartilage and menisci were modeled as isotropic linear elastic materials. Ligaments and tendons were defined as incompressible, transversely isotropic hyperelastic materials to better represent their nonlinear mechanical behavior. A detailed summary of the constitutive models and material classifications for all structures is provided in (Table S1), including rigid body assumptions for bones, isotropic linear elastic models for cartilage and menisci, and incompressible transversely isotropic hyperelastic formulations for ligaments and tendons. Different knee flexion angles (30°, 60°, and 90°) were generated by virtually rotating the femur relative to the tibia in postprocessing around a predefined flexion–extension axis derived from the femoral condyles. During flexion simulation, internal/external rotation and varus/valgus alignment of the tibia relative to the femur were kept constant to isolate the effect of sagittal‐plane flexion and ensure consistency across models. Then the neutral UKA and varus 10° knee models at 30°, 60°, and 90° of knee flexion, and the UKA models with different degrees of varus (0°, 2°, 4°, 6°, 8°, and 10°) at 30° of knee flexion were created. The relative position of the patella at each flexion angle was determined according to the corresponding femoral trochlear geometry; specifically, a geometry‐driven and contact‐constrained approach was adopted. At each flexion angle (30°, 60°, and 90°), the femur was rotated relative to the tibia, and the patella was positioned in alignment with the femoral trochlear groove based on the reconstructed anatomical geometry. Surface‐to‐surface contact interactions between the patellar and femoral cartilage were defined, allowing the patella to reach a stable equilibrium position under geometric constraints. No dynamic muscle‐driven patellar tracking or subject‐specific kinematic constraints were applied; therefore, patellar positioning was primarily governed by joint geometry and contact interactions in this static finite element framework.

The material properties of knee structures and components were set based on previous studies [12, 31, 32, 33, 34], respectively. Major knee ligaments were modeled using simplified tension‐only spring elements with predefined stiffness values obtained from the literature. Ligament properties were held constant across all simulated flexion angles, and ligament tensions were not individually calibrated for different flexion positions. This modeling strategy was intended to maintain joint stability under controlled conditions rather than reproduce in vivo ligament tension variations during knee flexion. A quadriceps force of 400 N was applied to the tendon of the quadriceps to all kinds of models [35]. All finite element analyzes were performed using ABAQUS software (version 2016; Dassault Systèmes Simulia Corp, Johnston, Rhode Island, USA), where boundary conditions, contact definitions, and loading scenarios were implemented.

Statistical Analysis

Sample size calculations were performed using PASS 2011 software (NCSS LLC, Kaysville, Utah, USA). As previously described by Pongcharoen and Reutiwarangkoon [36], the incidence of anterior knee pain exhibited a risk difference of 20% (with a power of 0.80 and a significance level of 0.05), and 25 patients were required. In the current study, the sample size was confirmed before analysis with the postoperative VAS scores (1.08 ± 0.9); therefore, to detect a difference of 0.8 in the VAS scores (with a power of 0.90 and a significance level of 0.05), a total of 26 patients were required.

Statistical tests were performed using SPSS software SPSS version 22 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, Illinois). All values were expressed as the mean values with standard deviations (SDs). The normally distributed data were tested using parametric independent t‐tests, and Wilcoxon tests were performed to test the nonnormally distributed data regarding the preoperative and postoperative outcomes. The scores between the normal and OA group were compared using the independent Student's t‐test or Mann–Whitney U test. The independent t‐test or Wilcoxon (W) test was performed for two continuous variables in the FEA data. The continuous variables of multiple groups were compared using ANOVA/Tukey's test or Kruskal–Wallis H(K)/Mann–Whitney U test. A value difference was considered statistical significance when the corresponding p‐value was < 0.05.

Results

Baseline Demographics

A total of 33 patients (36 knees) were recruited for this study, one patient lost follow‐up. A total of 32 patients (35 knees) were included in the final analysis, the total number of patients exceeds the calculated sample size (26 patients), and the sample size was valid. There were 1 male (2 knees) and 31 females (33 knees). The average age and body mass index (BMI) were 65.6 ± 8.7 years (range, Sc 49–79 years) and 24.9 ± 2.2 kg/m2 (21.7 to 30.2 kg/m2) at the time of the operation, respectively. The mean follow‐up duration, duration of operation, and length of hospital stay were 4.5 ± 0.3 years (4.0 to 5.0 years), 45.7 ± 2.8 min (38 to 54 min), and 5.9 ± 1.1 days (4 to 8 days), respectively. There were 14 and 21 knees in the OA and normal groups, respectively. Altman Scores, Ahlback grades and Outerbridge classifications of PFJ were shown in Table 1. No complications occurred.

Discussion

In this study, the main findings can be summarized as follows: medial fixed‐bearing UKA significantly improved clinical outcomes, including pain and functional scores; the presence of patellofemoral joint (PFJ) arthropathy did not adversely affect clinical outcomes; medial fixed‐bearing UKA improved PFJ alignment as demonstrated by radiographic parameters; and finite element analysis (FEA) revealed that medial PFJ stress was reduced and redistributed toward the lateral PFJ following correction of varus alignment.

Clinical Outcomes of Medial Fixed‐Bearing UKA and the Impact of PFJ Arthropathy

In this study, all patients showed significant improvements in both pain and functional scores from the preoperative assessment to the final follow‐up. Importantly, no progressive degeneration and pain of PFJ was observed postoperatively. Additionally, there were no significant differences between OA and normal groups in the functional scores at the final follow‐up. These findings suggest that medial fixed‐bearing UKA not only improves clinical outcomes but may also have a beneficial effect on PFJ alignment and stress distribution.

The results of this study were in consistent with the published studies. Carlo et al. [8] found that medial fixed‐bearing UKA can improve the quality of life and sports activity level in middle‐aged, active patients. PFJ OA showed no significant correlation with poorer outcomes at 5‐year follow‐up. Deckard et al. [2] found that the clinical outcomes of medial fixed‐bearing UKA were not adversely impacted by PFJ arthropathy. PFJ arthropathy did not affect clinical outcomes and may be due to the following factors. First, the contact region of the corroded surfaces in the PFJ might have been changed after medial fixed‐bearing UKA. Second, osteophytes of the patella and femoral condyle causing impingement and pain to the surfaces of the PFJ have been removed. Third, PFJ arthropathy have been symptomless preoperatively [4]. Lastly, patellar denervation can significantly alleviate pain and improve clinical results [37]. Those outcomes demonstrated that patients with isolated medial knee OA generally benefitted from medial fixed‐bearing UKA with excellent results, PFJ arthropathy can't negatively affect the clinical outcomes.

Radiographic Improvement and Optimization of PFJ Alignment

Furthermore, our outcomes revealed significant improvements in the CA, LPTA, and LPFA measured radiographically at the final follow‐up. The improvement of these outcomes demonstrated that medial UKA could optimize the PFJ alignment. In fact, medial UKA can cause a multiplanar realignment of the knee and the lower limb alignment. In this study, these alignments were improved by an average of 1.6° and 7.4° in the PTS and HKA angle, respectively. The external rotation of the tibia increases as the PTS increases, which can improve PFJ congruence [38]. Thein et al. [11] also found that medial UKA improves PFJ congruence. Those findings were consistent with our results. They also considered that the improved PFJ congruence suggests that medial fixed‐bearing UKA may secondarily redistribute contact pressures across the PFJ realignment, unload the degenerating PFJ to decrease wear, and help protect the PFJ against progressive degeneration. This mechanical interpretation indicates that PFJ arthropathy has a low effect on clinical results; this was also demonstrated in the clinical portion of this study where PFJ arthropathy did not affect the clinical outcomes of UKA. These outcomes could imply that medial fixed‐bearing UKA, through increasing PTS and correction of genu varum, can optimize the alignment of PFJ and lower limbs and secondarily redistribute contact pressures.

Biomechanical Insights From FEA of PFJ Stress Redistribution

To prove that the change of PFJ alignment after medial fixed‐bearing UKA may affect the stress distribution of the PFJ, we established FEA models to evaluate relative changes in PFJ stress distribution following coronal alignment correction. It should be emphasized that the FEA model was not intended to replicate absolute PFJ stress magnitudes in elderly patients with OA, but rather to demonstrate relative trends in stress redistribution under controlled conditions. We found a significant reduction in the mean von Mises stress value of the medial patellar facet and a significant increase on the lateral side in neutral UKA models compared with the genu varum models. The changes in the von Mises stress value were dependent on the degrees of valgus correction, which tended to be equal between the medial and lateral facets of the patella with valgus correction of genu varum to a neutral position. This may imply that the clinical outcomes were not reversed by medial fixed‐bearing UKA when the medial PFJ arthropathy was present, and the excessive load of the medial PFJ was partially transferred to the lateral side. Conversely, the observed increase in lateral PFJ stress in the FEA models raises the possibility that patients with advanced lateral PFJ degeneration may be more susceptible to symptom progression following medial UKA [39, 40]. However, given the simplified nature of the model and the use of a young, healthy subject, these observations should be interpreted as hypothesis‐generating rather than definitive evidence. This indicated that in patients with the lateral PFJ arthropathy medial fixed‐bearing UKA may have a risk of aggravation of lateral PFJ symptoms and OA progression due to the increased stress. This supported that medial UKA may be contraindicated when the lateral PFJ has grooved changes and severe bone damage [41].

Strengths of the Study

This study has several strengths. First, it integrates both clinical and biomechanical analyzes, providing a comprehensive evaluation of the effects of medial fixed‐bearing UKA on the patellofemoral joint (PFJ). By combining clinical outcomes with finite element analysis (FEA), this study offers mechanistic insights that extend beyond traditional clinical observations. Second, the study directly addresses an important and ongoing clinical controversy regarding the role of PFJ arthropathy in UKA and provides evidence that PFJ degeneration does not necessarily compromise postoperative outcomes. Third, detailed radiographic assessments were performed to evaluate changes in PFJ alignment, allowing for a more objective understanding of joint realignment following UKA. Fourth, the use of FEA enabled the investigation of stress redistribution within the PFJ, which cannot be directly measured in clinical studies, thereby enhancing the depth and interpretability of the findings. Finally, this study identifies a potential biomechanical explanation for the increased risk in patients with lateral PFJ arthropathy, which may have important implications for patient selection and surgical decision‐making.

Limitations and Future Directions

The present study also has some limitations. First, there was a relatively short follow‐up duration (4.5 years) and a small sample size (32 patients, 35 knees). Second, gender with a 1:31 male to female ratio may also influence the results of this study; this limits the generalizability of this study. Third, using healthy knees with no OA change in the medial compartment to develop FEA models and simulate a varus knee deformity may have introduced other variables. Fourth, normal single knee CT and MRI data were used to establish the models, and the individual‐specific features and diversity of knee structures and biomechanical properties were not considered. In addition, routine patellar denervation was performed in all patients, which may have contributed substantially to postoperative pain relief and could partially explain the favorable pain outcomes observed despite the presence of patellofemoral joint pathology. Finally, the FEA component was intended to illustrate relative trends in patellofemoral stress redistribution rather than absolute biomechanical values. In addition, the simulations were performed under static conditions, and therefore dynamic knee and patellofemoral joint behavior during gait could not be assessed. Further clinical and biomechanical studies with a larger sample size and longer follow‐up duration, as well as more radiological data of volunteers, are needed to develop models, acquire more data, confirm our findings, and further investigate the effect of PFJ realignment caused by medial fixed‐bearing UKA on the biomechanics of the PFJ.

Conclusions

Preexisting patellofemoral joint arthropathy did not negatively impact the mid‐term clinical outcomes of medial fixed‐bearing UKA. Biomechanical analysis suggests that stress redistribution following coronal alignment correction may be one explanatory mechanism. However, surgeons should remain cautious regarding severe isolated lateral PFJ compartment disease, as theoretical modeling indicates increased postoperative stress in this area, which may compromise outcomes.
