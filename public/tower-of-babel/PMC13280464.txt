Progressive Right‐Sided Heart Failure and Secondary Tricuspid Valve Disease in Pediatric Lutembacher Syndrome: A Case Report
Authors: Christopher Collin Hayes, Kelly Chodos, Bryan P. Stefek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLutembacher syndrome (LS) is a rare condition characterized by the coexistence of mitral stenosis (MS) and an atrial septal defect (ASD). Clinical manifestations vary according to the severity of MS and the size of the ASD.Case PresentationWe report a 13‐year‐old female with congenital MS and a secundum ASD who presented with decompensated right heart failure after being lost to follow‐up for 10 years. Progressive worsening of MS and enlargement of the ASD resulted in significant left‐to‐right shunting, severe right heart volume overload, pulmonary hypertension, tricuspid annular dilation, and severe tricuspid regurgitation. Cardiac catheterization demonstrated low cardiac output, pulmonary overcirculation, and postcapillary pulmonary hypertension. The patient underwent mechanical mitral valve replacement, ASD closure, and tricuspid annuloplasty.OutcomePostoperatively, the patient experienced transient left ventricular dysfunction that gradually improved with medical therapy. At 2‐year follow‐up, she demonstrated normal biventricular systolic function and complete resolution of heart failure symptoms.ConclusionThis case highlights how a large ASD may initially mask symptoms of severe MS while simultaneously promoting progressive right‐sided heart failure and secondary tricuspid valve disease. Early surveillance and timely intervention are essential to prevent irreversible cardiac remodeling in patients with LS.

1. Introduction

LS is a rare condition that describes MS with an associated ASD [1]. The clinical significance of LS is dependent on the size of the ASD and the severity of the MS. In the setting of a small restrictive ASD and severe MS, LS will favor the physiology of MS. However, in the setting of a large nonrestrictive ASD with mild MS, LS will favor the physiology of a large ASD [2]. We describe an unusual pediatric case of severe congenital MS with a large nonrestrictive ASD that progressed to secondary tricuspid valve disease following prolonged loss to follow‐up.

2. Case Report

A 13‐year‐old female with a history of congenital mild MS and a large ASD, lost to cardiology follow‐up for 10 years, presented to the emergency department with dyspnea, fatigue, and lower extremity edema. The patient was tachycardic and tachypneic with an oxygen saturation of 94%. Lung exam demonstrated bibasilar crackles, and cardiac exam was significant for an opening snap in diastole, a Grade 3/6 holosystolic murmur, and an S3 gallop. Abdominal exam revealed ascites and hepatomegaly. Lower extremities were edematous with delayed capillary refill. ECG showed sinus tachycardia, biatrial enlargement, and biventricular hypertrophy with left ventricular strain (Figure 1). Chest X‐ray showed cardiomegaly and bilateral pleural effusions (Figure 2). BNP was elevated to 37,505. Echocardiogram revealed severe MS, a large 12 mm ASD (increased from 8 mm), and severe right heart dilation with dilation of the tricuspid valve annulus (4.36 cm, Z score +4.96) and severe tricuspid regurgitation. Cardiac catheterization demonstrated low cardiac output (Qs = 1.3 L/min/m2), postcapillary pulmonary hypertension (PVR 3WU), and significantly increased pulmonary blood flow, as demonstrated by a Qp/Qs of 5.4:1 (Figure 3). The patient underwent open heart surgery with a 25 mm mechanical On‐X mitral valve replacement, primary ASD closure, and tricuspid valve annuloplasty. The patient developed intraoperative complete heart block requiring temporary epicardial atrial pacing, which resolved within 24 h. Postoperatively, the patient had a significant left bundle branch block with runs of accelerated idioventricular rhythm and severe LV dysfunction with an LV ejection fraction (EF) of 23%. Anticoagulation therapy with warfarin was initiated postoperatively. In addition, the patient was started on enalapril and metoprolol. At the patient′s 6‐month follow‐up, she had mild RV dysfunction, moderate LV dysfunction (EF 46%), and mild tricuspid stenosis and insufficiency. Function continued to improve with normal RV function and mild LV dysfunction (EF 55%) at the 1 year follow‐up, with persistent mild tricuspid stenosis and insufficiency. At the patient′s 2‐year follow‐up, she had normal biventricular systolic function with a LV EF of 57% and no symptoms of heart failure. She demonstrated stable mild tricuspid stenosis and insufficiency. Her On‐X valve was functioning appropriately. The patient is currently managed on enalapril, metoprolol, warfarin, and aspirin.

3. Discussion

We present a rare case of pediatric LS complicated by severe MS and a large ASD who presented in heart failure following a 10‐year loss to follow‐up. Unlike other reports, this case is unique as the patient′s ASD masked MS symptoms and led to significant tricuspid annular dilation resulting in a two‐valve disease that ultimately required surgical repair. The prolonged loss to follow‐up uniquely highlights the natural progression of this disease.

Hemodynamic catheterization showed our patient had severe MS and significant pulmonary overcirculation with an elevated Qp/Qs of 5.4, indicative of a large left‐to‐right shunt from her ASD. The ASD acted as a “pop‐off” mechanism for the left atrium, reducing left atrial pressure and delaying pulmonary congestion, while simultaneously increasing right‐sided volume overload. This shunting led to dilation of the tricuspid valve annulus and severe tricuspid regurgitation, necessitating surgical repair of a two‐valve disease. Overcirculation resulted in postcapillary pulmonary hypertension with a mean pulmonary artery pressure of 48 mmHg and pulmonary vascular resistance of 3WU (Figure 3). Ultimately, the combination of pulmonary overcirculation and severe MS led to heart failure and low cardiac output (Qs = 1.3 L/min/m2).

Historically, severe congenital MS has been repaired surgically by valvotomy or with mitral valve replacement [3]. However, over the past 20 years, balloon mitral valvuloplasty has been shown to be an effective adjunct to surgery in reducing the mitral valve gradient in MS. A large retrospective study comparing the results of balloon mitral valvuloplasty, surgical mitral valvuloplasty, and mitral valve replacement in 108 children treated for severe MS found that balloon mitral valvuloplasty successfully decreased the mean MS gradient by approximately 40%, nearly doubled the mitral valve orifice size, and delayed the timing for surgical intervention [4]. Further, there was no significant difference in outcome between percutaneous and surgical interventions. The authors found that balloon valvuloplasty was often the first‐line treatment for most forms of congenital MS, with surgery reserved for patients with other intracardiac lesions such as atrial or ventricular septal defects. Overall, the authors conclude that the type of interventional and surgical management is dependent on each patient′s unique anatomy. In a case report, Johny et al. describe a successful interventional balloon mitral valvuloplasty with ASD device closure in a 30‐year‐old with LS with severe MS and a large ASD after determining the patient had favorable mitral valve anatomy with a low Wilkins score and adequate atrial rims [5]. Conversely, in a different case report, an 18‐year‐old with LS had an irreparable mitral valve with thickened calcified leaflets requiring surgical replacement with ASD patch closure [6]. These cases highlight the need for careful evaluation of both the mitral valve and ASD anatomy when considering options for repair.

Routine echocardiogram imaging is the mainstay of outpatient surveillance for LS, with the option for further transcatheter hemodynamic evaluation and treatment as needed [7]. Growth during childhood presents an additional variable that may play a role in increasing MS gradients over time and further warrants close follow‐up with the goal of early intervention to prevent progression toward a two‐valve disease and irreversible ventricular remodeling.

4. Conclusion

This case demonstrates the complex hemodynamic interplay between severe congenital MS and a large ASD in pediatric Lutembacher syndrome. Although the ASD initially decompressed the left atrium and delayed pulmonary symptoms, prolonged left‐to‐right shunting ultimately resulted in severe right‐sided volume overload, pulmonary hypertension, and secondary tricuspid valve disease. Early surveillance and timely intervention are critical to prevent irreversible cardiac remodeling and progression to multivalvular disease.
