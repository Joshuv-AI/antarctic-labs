See and Switch: Vision-Based Branching for Interactive Robot-Skill Programming
Petr Vanc, Jan Kristof Behrens, Václav Hlaváč, and, Karla Stepanova

Abstract
Programming robots by demonstration (PbD) is an intuitive concept, but scaling it to real-world variability remains a challenge for most current teaching frameworks. Conditional task graphs are very expressive and can be defined incrementally, which fits very well with the PbD idea. However, acting using conditional task graphs requires reliable perception-grounded online branch selection. In this paper, we present See & Switch , an interactive teaching-and-execution framework that represents tasks as user-extendable graphs of skill parts connected via decision states (DS), enabling conditional branching during replay. Unlike prior approaches that rely on manual branching or low-dimensional signals (e.g., proprioception), our vision-based Switcher uses eye-in-hand images (high-dimensional) to select among competing successor skill parts and to detect out-of-distribution contexts that require new demonstrations. We integrate kinesthetic teaching, joystick control, and hand gestures via an input-modality-abstraction layer and demonstrate that our proposed method is teaching modality-independent, enabling efficient in-situ recovery demonstrations. The system is validated in experiments on three challenging dexterous manipulation tasks. We evaluate our method under diverse conditions and furthermore conduct user studies with 8 participants. We show that the proposed method reliably performs branch selection and anomaly detection for novice users, achieving 90.7 % \color[rgb]{0,0,0}90.7\% and 87.9 % \color[rgb]{0,0,0}87.9\% accuracy, respectively, across 576 \color[rgb]{0,0,0}576 real-robot rollouts. We provide all code and data required to reproduce our experiments at imitrob.ciirc.cvut.cz/publications/seeandswitch .



I
Introduction

Teaching robots new tasks from human guidance rather than code-based programming makes automation more accessible to non-experts. In
Learning from Demonstration
(LfD), a robot derives its control policy by imitating the observed human behavior (e.g., kinesthetic teaching) instead of relying on hand-crafted code or trajectories
[
6
,
2
,
7
]
. Building on this idea,
Programming by Demonstration
(PbD) enables end-users to teach robots interactively through demonstrations, without writing any code or tuning parameters
[
5
,
12
,
6
]
.

This demonstration-based approach is intuitive, but a key challenge is that a single demonstration’s validity is limited to the current state of the environment and its immediate neighborhood. In the face of significant changes, a fixed replay of the original demonstration may fail because of the demonstration’s implicit assumptions.
Consider the following example. The robot is taught to measure a voltage with a probe by demonstrating how to approach the contact point. It might succeed when the contact point is accessible, but if it is hidden behind a closed door (see Fig.
1
), the original behavior is inappropriate.
Instead, a different behavior (opening the doors first) is required to accomplish the task.
Traditional PbD systems lack such conditional adaptation; they repeat the learned motion and are brittle to changes in environmental conditions. In this paper, we focus on overcoming this limitation and enabling the robot to incrementally learn to handle the given task under different circumstances by leveraging iterative human feedback and grounding in the visual domain.

Our solution builds on the concept of
conditional skills
:
distinct
task variants
corresponding to alternative environmental states, such as various probe states.
Recent PbD frameworks
[
17
,
12
,
6
]
represent such behaviors using task-graph models with multiple
skill variants
, where a skill variant defines a complete task solution as an end-to-end execution path from task start to completion. Each skill variant is composed of individual
skill parts
(trajectory segments).
During execution, it is possible to switch at
decision states
(DS).
At each DS, a suitable branch (successor skill part) is selected online based on sensory feedback
[
6
]
, defining the executed variant. The Collaborative Incremental Programming (CIP) system by Eiband et al.
[
12
]
exemplifies this strategy: it incrementally constructs a task graph of individual skill parts from demonstrations, and an
anomaly detector
monitors the robot inputs to decide if execution is out-of-distribution (OOD) and if a new skill part is needed.

However, a central challenge remains: selecting the appropriate skill variant online given high-dimensional real-world observations. During incremental learning or unexpected failures, the robot may encounter previously unseen situations (anomalies) that must be detected, and the user prompted to provide a new demonstration. Existing systems typically rely on low-level internal signals, such as force sensing
[
12
]
.
Vision-based approaches for skill branching are increasingly more popular
[
19
]
, but many still require manually specified perceptual features. While proprioception reliably captures internal contact and motion states, it is inherently limited to what the robot can sense through its body, often only during interaction, whereas vision provides broader, contact-free access to environmental context across diverse scenarios.

In this work, we extend task-graph-based PbD with a vision-grounded decision mechanism and human feedback to enable incremental, open-ended refinement. We introduce an image-based
Switcher
(Sec.
III-D
) that uses an eye-in-hand camera to evaluate the scene at decision states, to (i) select the appropriate skill variant, and (ii) detect novel contexts outside the current task graph. When such contexts occur, the system can (iii) incorporate situation-specific corrective demonstrations to expand the task graph via conditional branching.
To support efficient refinement, we provide a modality-agnostic teaching interface that allows corrective demonstrations during execution via kinesthetic guidance, joystick control, or hand gestures. This enables users to add recovery behaviors online with minimal effort and without restructuring the existing task representation.
Building on the CIP-style framework
[
12
]
, our contributions are:

•

Automatic insertion of decision states (DS)
triggered by (visual) anomaly detection
[
11
]
or user intervention, enabling conditional branching for incremental refinement;

•

Online task-graph expansion via user-guided recovery demonstrations
, adding new skill variants without modifying the existing structure;

•

Modality-agnostic teaching
(kinesthetic, joystick, gestures) for efficient in-situ recovery demonstrations.

We evaluate the framework in a user study comparing teaching modalities in terms of feasibility and teaching efficiency.

II
Related Work

PbD has progressed from single-trajectory replay to interactive, graph-structured task models capturing conditional behavior. CIP learns task graphs with decision states and recovery branches from multiple demonstrations, using proprioceptive and force-based uncertainty for anomaly triggering and branch selection
[
11
,
12
]
, while Aayush et al. induce reactive behavior trees from one demonstration via a combination of learning and logic-based synthesis
[
15
]
. Neural Task Graph Networks infer latent graphs from a single video and execute unseen multi-step tasks
[
14
]
. We adopt the same high-level representation (user-editable task graph) but replace hand-crafted switching logic with a unified, image-based classifier that operates at decision-state context windows, enabling robust and automatic branch selection under ambiguous visual conditions.

Execution monitoring and recovery have been addressed by learning recovery motions and visual monitors. Wu et al. encode anomaly recovery skills with Gaussian Process regression over multiple demonstrations
[
27
]
. ConditionNET and ILeSiA learn, respectively, symbolic preconditions/effects and visual risk scores from language and camera supervision to monitor execution
[
24
,
26
]
, while symbolic failure-detection pipelines learn human-interpretable predicates from multimodal signals
[
13
]
. Diffusion-PbD instead uses visual foundation models to extract keyframes and transfer waypoints to novel scenes, focusing on generalization rather than explicit, user-editable branching
[
20
]
. In contrast, our Switcher is trained per decision state on eye-in-hand images to jointly (i) classify among competing skill parts and (ii) flag OOD views within the shared embedding space, tightly coupling branch selection and anomaly detection.

Vision foundation models are increasingly used as state encoders for manipulation and conditional control. DINOBot
[
22
]
leverages DINO
[
8
]
features for retrieval-based one-shot imitation and pixel-level alignment to novel objects.
Similarly, we use DINO features within a decision-state-specific classifier to support efficient branch selection and anomaly detection, aligning visual representation learning with the discrete, incremental structure of
task-graph execution.

III
Method

We present a modality-agnostic system for programming
conditional skills
(multi-variant tasks) from user demonstrations. The approach integrates online execution monitoring and mechanisms for teaching recovery behaviors, formalized in the robot policy described in Sec.
III-A
). Our design retains the CIP
[
12
]
decision-state (DS) logic and anomaly-triggered insertion rule (Sec.
III-B
).
We introduce (
i
) an
input-modality-abstraction layer
that unifies teaching interfaces (Sec.
III-C
), and (
ii
) a
vision-based
observation channel (eye-in-hand) capable of detecting anomalies and online selection of the most suitable skill continuation at each DS: the Switcher, Sec.
III-D
.
The core idea of our method is to enable incremental user-guided task definition and to simplify task-part selection and anomaly detection
by conditioning decisions on perceptions at the DS and only deciding among relevant task-part alternatives. This effectively avoids the need for global scene understanding.

III-D

Observation-based State Evaluator (The Switcher)

The core contribution of our system is the Switcher
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{S}
: It decides at each timestep based on the observed image (
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}z^{t}
), a target skill part or triggers an anomaly (
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}z^{t}
is not previously seen). As introduced in Sec.
III-A
, if the anomaly is triggered and the user approves, then we create a DS and demonstrate a new skill part by creating a new branch. The Switcher
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{S}
module is trained on images
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}z_{(i),r}^{t}
collected from all skill parts
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\text{Parts}^{T}
for the given task
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}T
that are available, see Fig.
2

The Switcher
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{S}
is composed of two parts: 1) Anomaly detector and 2) State Estimator.

III-D
1
Anomaly detector

We build on top of the IleSiA
[
26
]
anomaly detector, trained on a dataset of collected images. It estimates whether a new image is OOD relative to the training set.
We want the anomaly detector to detect changes such as: (1) a new object appears/disappears, (2) the pose, or state of the object changes, and (3) the camera view changes, meaning the robot is not respecting the predefined trajectory path. In practice, it should alert us when the probe is misplaced or an obstacle is blocking access.
We see the benefit of using the same model for anomaly detection and state estimation, because it enables the system to determine whether a detected anomaly corresponds to a known state or a novel context.However, when anomalies are user-triggered, visual cues may be insufficiently distinctive, preventing reliable association with existing states and transitions.

Context window

Let
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}t_{\mathrm{thresh}}
denote the first timestep at which an anomaly threshold is crossed. We define the decision-state (DS) context window as:
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}W=\{t_{\mathrm{thresh}},\dots,t_{\mathrm{thresh}}+e\}
, where
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}e
is the window length. Only observations with
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}t\in W
are used for branch selection and anomaly evaluation, i.e.
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s(W)=s(t_{\mathrm{thresh}},t_{\mathrm{thresh}}+e)
.

Permitted skill parts available for switching

At a DS, switching is allowed only from the root skill part
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s_{(\text{curr})}
to a selected subset of skill parts
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}M^{U}_{(\text{curr})}\subset Parts^{T}
, containing the permitted successor skill parts.
The subset is initialized as
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}M^{U}_{(\text{curr})}\leftarrow\left\{s_{(\text{curr})}\right\}
.
When a new skill part
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s_{(\mathrm{new})}
is demonstrated from this DS, the set is extended as

M^{U}_{(\mathrm{curr})}\leftarrow M^{U}_{(\mathrm{curr})}\cup\{s_{(\mathrm{new})}\}.

III-D
2
State Estimator

For each new DS created, we train a dedicated State Estimator model (Sec.
III-D
) using images from the corresponding context window
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}W
and the permitted skill parts
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}M^{U}_{(\text{curr})}
.
Restricting switching to
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}M^{U}_{(\mathrm{curr})}
prevents ambiguity arising from visually similar states that correspond to different, unobservable environmental conditions. For example, if the door is outside the camera view, we could switch to both door-opened/closed skill parts, not taking into the account the knowledge of the door state. We further constrain the switching only to skill parts whose start timestep
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}K_{i}
satisfies
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}t\geq K_{i}
, ensuring that only temporally valid successors are considered.
Each DS is associated with a branching timestep
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}t_{DS}
. All branching timesteps are clustered into larger temporal segments by merging overlapping context windows using a union-of-intervals procedure
[
10
]
(window size
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}e=10
).
A separate instance of the State Estimator model is trained on each resulting cluster using the image set
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Z_{M^{U}_{(i)}\subset M}^{W}
collected over the corresponding DS window
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}W
and the permitted skill parts
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}M^{U}_{(i)}
.

III-E

Proposed DINO-based Switcher for DS estimation

To implement DS-local state estimation in the Switcher, we use self-supervised DINO vision transformers
[
8
]
as frozen feature extractors. Given an eye-in-hand image
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}z
, the backbone outputs a set of patch embeddings
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\{x_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
,
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}x_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We convert these patch embeddings into a class prediction over competing skill parts using one of the following strategies:

Prototype inference (mean).

We form an image representation either by mean pooling (
mean
) or by concatenating patch embeddings (
concat
). For each class
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}c_{i}
, we compute a prototype by averaging the training representations of that class. At inference time, we assign the query image to the class with maximum cosine similarity to its prototype.

Multi-instance learning (MIL).

We treat patch embeddings as a set of instances and learn an attention-based MIL head that aggregates
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\{x_{k}\}
into a class prediction.

Attention-gated features (attn).

To emphasize informative regions, we use the last-layer
[CLS]
-to-patch self-attention weights as patch importance scores. We apply either
hard
gating (discard low-attention patches) or
soft
gating (down-weight them), and reduce multiple attention heads by mean or max before aggregation.

Anomaly detection.

We use the same DINO embedding space for novelty detection by computing an anomaly score from the query image’s similarity to the DS training set (e.g., based on the maximum cosine similarity to known classes) and flagging an anomaly when the score crosses a threshold (set via a training-score percentile
percentile_keep

\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\,\in(0,1]
).

The user demonstrations

IV-B
2
System-gated vs. user-gated anomaly detection

As described in Sec.
III-D
, the proposed framework supports both
automatic
DS insertion via visual anomaly detection and
manual
user triggering. Conceptually, it comprises two coupled components: (i) anomaly detection that proposes a new DS, and (ii) DS-local state estimation (the Switcher) that selects among competing successor skill parts.

In deployment, we allow both the system and the user to trigger an anomaly to maximize robustness. In the user study, however, we intentionally used
user-gated
DS timing. This choice isolates the evaluation of the Switcher and the teaching workflow from temporal errors in automatic triggering: early, late, or missed anomaly detections would otherwise confound branch-selection accuracy and obscure modality comparisons. User-gating also yields consistent DS alignment and reliable labels in the collected dataset, while improving study safety and execution stability by keeping the participant in control. Automatic anomaly detection is evaluated separately offline on the collected data (Sec.
V-B
).

VI
Conclusion and Discussion

We presented a conditional PbD framework that represents tasks as branching skill parts and supports incremental extension via decision states (DS).
A vision-based Switcher selects the appropriate branch at each DS or flags observations as outside the demonstrated contexts, i.e., an anomaly.

Switcher performance and practical implications

On the user-study DS windows, performance varies due to noisy and partially observable data: for branch selection, only about half of DS instances achieve high test accuracy (e.g., 31/78 DS models exceed 90% for
dinov2 small attn
).
By filtering out cases where, for example, the camera does not capture the correct features, the overall accuracy increases from 81.7% to 90.7%.
In contrast, anomaly detection is more stable (43/78 DS models exceed 90%). In a controlled label-growth experiment,
dinov2 small attn
remains robust as the number of competing classes increases (93.6% at 5 classes, and 90.8% at 6 classes), supporting use in larger DS branch sets.

User study findings

Non-expert participants successfully taught conditional tasks where kinesthetic teaching produced the shortest demonstrations (19.5–24.2s) and remained efficient even for
Cable wrap
(23.2s), while joystick and gesture teleoperation became much longer for the harder tasks (up to 61.4s and 73.5s, respectively).
Replay success was highest for
Peg pick
(up to 99.2% with joystick), while
Cable wrap
was the most challenging overall (48.8–58.1%), with kinesthetic teaching achieving the best success rate.
Taken together, the results support the practical use of kinesthetic teaching when available, while also showing that gesture-based teaching remains a viable alternative for modality-independent task programming.

Limitations and open problems

The main limitation is observability: switching fails when the discriminative scene information is not visible to the eye-in-hand camera, motivating multi-view sensing or viewpoint control. Performance can also degrade under strong visual distractors.
Finally, enabling re-joining of branches after the decisive variation could reduce graph growth and better support long tasks with local corrections.
