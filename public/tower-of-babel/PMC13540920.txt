CAGNet: a structure-aware clustering-alternated graph network for cell-cell interaction inference in spatial transcriptomics
Authors: Han Ma, Xin Zhang, Hang Chen, Yan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMotivationUnderstanding cell-cell interactions (CCIs) in spatial transcriptomics is crucial for uncovering the spatial organization and functional heterogeneity of tissues. However, existing graph-based models typically rely on static clustering or fixed adjacency structures, which limits their ability to capture dynamic cellular relationships.ResultsWe propose CAGNet, a two-stage framework for CCI inference from spatial transcriptomics data. In Stage 1, a Graph Attention Network encoder with joint feature and graph reconstruction learns structure-aware node embeddings from spatial gene expression profiles. In Stage 2, an alternating optimization mechanism iteratively updates cluster centers via KL-guided soft assignment and refines node embeddings through spatial graph reconstruction, establishing a closed-loop between representation learning and clustering. Experiments on three 10x Genomics Visium datasets demonstrate that CAGNet consistently outperforms six CCI inference baselines across ACC, AUC, AP, Precision, Recall, and F1. CAGNet also achieves the highest Adjusted Rand Index on all three datasets against six spatial domain identification methods, confirming that the learned embeddings capture biologically relevant spatial organization. Information-theoretic analysis further shows that CAGNet retains the highest mutual information between input features and learned embeddings among all compared methods. Ablation studies and 5-fold cross-validation confirm the contribution of each component and the reproducibility of the results.AvailabilityThe proposed method is implemented in the CAGNet package available at http://github.com/mahan1233333-maker/CAGNet.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12859-026-06534-9.Keywords: Spatial transcriptomics, Cell-cell interaction, Graph neural network, Clustering

Introduction

Multicellular life processes depend on the coordinated interactions among diverse cell types, and cell–cell interactions (CCIs) represent the fundamental basis of these processes [1, 2]. Elucidating CCI mechanisms is therefore biologically significant for understanding tissue development, immune responses, and disease pathogenesis [3]. With technological advancements, single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) technologies have enabled remarkable progress in understanding cellular mechanisms at an unprecedented resolution level [4, 5]. Complementarily, spatial transcriptomics (ST) provides valuable spatial information regarding cells or spots that comprise multiple or partial cells [6–10]. The integration of these technologies has empowered researchers to uncover the fundamental nature of CCIs within their spatial contexts [11].

Recent advances in deep learning have further enhanced the modeling of complex biological systems. In particular, representation learning techniques, such as contrastive learning and sequence-based embedding models, have demonstrated strong capability in capturing high-dimensional biological patterns [12]. Moreover, multi-component deep learning frameworks that jointly optimize multiple modules, such as cross-omics translation models with cycle consistency and adversarial training, have shown improved robustness and representation quality in complex prediction tasks [13]. Beyond biological applications, similar synergistic design principles have recently been explored in other domains: CFM-GP [14] learns cell-type-agnostic gene perturbation effects via conditional flow matching, and GTG-ACO [15] jointly learns heuristic and pheromone matrices using a Graph Transformer for combinatorial optimization. These studies further inspire our strategy of tightly coupling representation learning with an auxiliary clustering objective. These developments highlight the importance of jointly modeling multiple sources of biological information and optimizing them in a unified framework.

Current computational approaches for CCIs can be broadly categorized into two classes. The first class is ligand–receptor (L–R)-based methods, which depend on curated L–R databases to identify potential communication links by identifying co-expressed ligand and receptor pairs across different cell populations. Representative methods such as CellChat [16], CellPhoneDB [17], and iTalk [18] construct communication networks and quantify interaction strengths. Several extensions incorporate spatial proximity or correlation-based filtering to improve reliability, including SingleCellSignalR [19], SpotSC [20], and ProximID [21]. Although these methods provide strong interpretability, they heavily rely on prior knowledge and are limited in capturing higher-order structural patterns [22].

The second class of methods formulates CCI inference as a graph-based learning problem. Traditional approaches utilize node embedding techniques such as DeepWalk [23] and node2vec [24], while more recent methods employ graph neural networks (GNNs) to better capture structural dependencies. For example, SEGCECO [25] leverages subgraph-level embedding strategies to model higher-order structural patterns and functional relationships in gene expression data. Emerging studies in spatial transcriptomics have further explored advanced graph-based frameworks, including path-based heterogeneous graph learning for spatial data imputation [26], iterative multi-view graph neural networks for modeling intercellular regulation [27], and multi-slice integration frameworks for cross-sample spatial analysis [28]. In addition, dynamic cross-graph attention mechanisms have been proposed to model complex interactions across heterogeneous biological networks [29]. These approaches significantly improve the modeling of spatial structure and intercellular relationships.

Meanwhile, clustering-based methods have also been widely studied for spatial transcriptomics analysis. Recent works, such as multi-view adaptive fusion networks [30] and similarity-purified multi-omics clustering frameworks [31], focus on integrating spatial and molecular information to enhance clustering performance. However, in most existing approaches, clustering is treated as an independent preprocessing or downstream analysis step, rather than being tightly integrated into the representation learning process.

Most existing graph-based CCI inference methods treat node embedding and clustering as separate processes. Although some studies, such as ClusterLP [32], attempt to incorporate clustering structures into link prediction via alternating optimization, these methods remain limited in their ability to fully exploit deep representation learning. As a result, clustering information cannot effectively guide embedding learning throughout the training process.

To address these limitations, we propose a novel CCI inference framework named CAGNet (Clustering-Alternated Graph Network), which integrates clustering into the core representation learning process through an alternating optimization mechanism. Specifically, the framework iteratively updates node embeddings and cluster centers by fixing one component while optimizing the other. A graph attention network (GAT) is used to encode spatial transcriptomics data into structure-aware embeddings, followed by a soft clustering module to refine cluster assignments. The clustering structure serves as an intermediate guidance signal that improves the quality of node embeddings, which are subsequently used for CCI link prediction. Meanwhile, a reconstruction module is employed to update embeddings based on graph structure constraints. Through this mutual refinement process, clustering structures and node embeddings are jointly optimized, enabling the model to better capture both local and global patterns of cell–cell interactions.

Methods

In this work, we focus on cell–cell interaction inference based on spatial transcriptomics data, which integrates gene expression profiles with spatial coordinates, enabling the analysis of cellular interactions within their native spatial context.

The overall framework of CAGNet consists of two stages, as illustrated in Fig. 1. In the first stage, raw spatial transcriptomics data are preprocessed and then fed into a Graph Attention Network encoder, which learns structure-aware node embeddings by jointly modeling node features and spatial graph topology. A reconstruction decoder is simultaneously trained to reconstruct both node features and the adjacency matrix from the learned embeddings, with the feature reconstruction loss and graph reconstruction loss jointly supervising the training of the encoder. The refined node embeddings and the reconstructed sparse graph A' obtained from this stage serve as inputs to the second stage.

Overall framework of CAGNet

In the second stage, K-means clustering is first applied to to obtain initial cluster centers . Subsequently, an alternating optimization strategy is employed, iteratively fixing node embeddings to update cluster centers and fixing cluster centers to update node embeddings until convergence. After training converges, the final node embeddings NE and cluster centers CC are used for two downstream evaluations.

For cell-cell interaction inference, NE and CC are passed to the reconstruction_graph module, which computes a pairwise interaction score for each cell pair by combining direct cosine similarity between node embeddings and cluster-based soft-assignment similarity. The resulting continuous scores are normalized and thresholded at 0.5 to produce a binary N × N interaction matrix, where each entry indicates the presence or absence of an interaction between the corresponding cell pair. The quality of these predictions is assessed on held-out test edges using ACC, AUC, AP, Precision, Recall, and F1.

For spatial domain identification, each spot is assigned to its nearest cluster center in the NE embedding space according to Euclidean distance, yielding discrete cluster labels. These labels are compared against ground-truth spatial domain annotations to compute ARI, alongside evaluations with other baseline methods.

Data preprocessing

To mitigate biases arising from sequencing depth variations and technical noise, and to ensure the stability and reliability of downstream analyses, CAGNet adheres to the standard workflow for spatial transcriptomics data processing, performing rigorous quality control and feature selection on the raw data. The specific steps are as follows:

Data filtering: The gene expression matrix and spatial location information are integrated, retaining only spots located within the tissue region with matched barcodes. Cells expressing in fewer than 50 genes and genes expressed in fewer than 3 cells are removed to minimize the impact of low-quality cells and genes on downstream analyses. These thresholds are standard in spatial transcriptomics analysis (e.g., Scanpy defaults and 10x Genomics recommendations). Stage 1: GAT-based representation learning

Based on the preprocessed expression matrix and adjacency relationships A, we construct a joint framework of a GAT encoder and a reconstruction decoder. Dual reconstruction tasks are employed to ensure the biological interpretability of the embedding space.

GAT encoder

The GAT encoder introduces an attention mechanism to perform weighted aggregation of information from neighboring nodes. Unlike the linear compression of PCA, the GAT encoder learns topology-aware nonlinear embeddings, meaning that each cell’s representation not only preserves its own gene expression features but also integrates contextual information from nearby cells. Through this dual-decoding strategy, the model obtains constraints in both the expression and spatial dimensions, enabling comprehensive characterization of the cells.

CAGNet introduces an alternating update mechanism, transforming node representation learning and clustering from a conventional sequential training paradigm into a dynamic, co-optimized process. Within this framework, node embeddings and cluster centers continuously interact during the iterative process: updates to the cluster centers provide structurally discriminative constraints for node representations, while the optimization of node embeddings, in turn, enhances the robustness and accuracy of the clustering results. Through this alternating iteration, the model achieves a dynamic balance between local similarity and global network structure, effectively avoiding local optima and substantially improving the overall performance and generalization capability in modeling cell–cell interaction networks.

Results

Datasets

This study utilized three publicly available Visium spatial transcriptomics datasets provided by 10x Genomics [36], each representing distinct tissue types and experimental conditions:

Mouse brain section (Coronal, 10x Genomics): Coronal brain tissue sections were obtained from male C57BL/6 mice. The original gene expression matrix contained 2,702 spots and 32,285 genes. After filtering, feature extraction, and dimensionality reduction, it was reduced to 2,702 spots and 50 genes.

Human breast cancer (Visium Fresh Frozen, Whole Transcriptome): Tissue sections were obtained from BioIVT breast invasive ductal carcinoma samples. The original gene expression matrix contained 4,898 spots and 36,601 genes. After filtering, feature extraction, and dimensionality reduction, it was reduced to 4,898 spots and 50 genes.

Human spinal cord (Whole Transcriptome Analysis): Tissue sections were obtained from fresh-frozen human spinal cord samples provided by BioIVT. The original gene expression matrix contained 2,812 spots and 36,601 genes. After filtering, feature extraction, and dimensionality reduction, it was reduced to 2,812 spots and 50 genes.

Baselines

To comprehensively evaluate CAGNet, we compare against two categories of baselines. For CCI inference, six graph representation learning models are selected, covering autoencoding, variational inference, attention mechanisms, modularity-aware, alternating-update, and subgraph-based approaches. For spatial domain identification, six methods are included, ranging from classical clustering algorithms to state-of-the-art spatial transcriptomics methods. Together, these baselines ensure a thorough and representative evaluation of both the link prediction and clustering capabilities of CAGNet.

CCI inference baselines

GAE [37]: A graph autoencoder based on graph convolutional networks, capable of learning low-dimensional node embeddings in an unsupervised manner and performing link prediction via adjacency matrix reconstruction.

VGAE [37]: Extending GAE by introducing a variational inference mechanism to probabilistically model the latent representations, thereby enhancing the robustness and generalization ability of node embeddings.

GAT [38]: A graph neural network model based on attention mechanisms, which adaptively assigns importance weights to neighboring nodes to obtain more flexible and fine-grained node representations.

MGAE [39]: A modularity-aware graph autoencoder that incorporates community structure information into the encoding process, enabling joint optimization of node representations for both link prediction and community detection tasks.

ClusterLP [32]: A graph representation learning framework that alternately updates node embeddings and cluster centers to capture community structure, thereby enhancing link prediction performance. Unlike CAGNet, it focuses on iterative embedding–clustering refinement without incorporating deeper feature reconstruction or structural guidance.

SEGCECO [25]: A subgraph-based method that uses an attributed graph convolutional neural network to predict cell-cell communication from single-cell RNA-seq data. SEGCECO constructs a cell-cell similarity graph via SoptSC and captures both latent and explicit attributes of the resulting undirected graph for link prediction.

Spatial domain identification baselines

Leiden [40]: A community-detection algorithm that improves upon the Louvain method by guaranteeing well-connected communities, applied to the KNN graph constructed from gene expression features.

Agglomerative [41]: A hierarchical clustering method that iteratively merges nodes based on linkage criteria, applied to PCA-reduced features.

STAGATE [42]: A graph attention auto-encoder framework that integrates spatial information and gene expression profiles to identify spatial domains. STAGATE adopts an attention mechanism to adaptively learn the similarity of neighboring spots and optionally incorporates cell type-aware pre-clustering.

SpaGCN [43]: A graph convolutional network approach that integrates gene expression, spatial location, and histology for spatial domain identification. SpaGCN aggregates gene expression from neighboring spots via graph convolution to detect spatially coherent expression domains.

stGRL [44]: A multi-task graph neural network framework for spatial transcriptomics that adopts an encoder-decoder architecture and employs a zero-inflated negative binomial (ZINB) distribution to model over-dispersion and dropout events in gene expression data, while incorporating graph contrastive learning to enhance clustering performance.

CiCLoDS [45]: An unsupervised framework that jointly optimizes clustering and feature selection for spatial transcriptomics under a user-defined gene budget, incorporating spatial side information via positional encodings.

Experimental results

Dataset splitting and hyperparameter selection

In this study, the following edge-splitting procedure was applied. First, the raw spatial adjacency matrix A (constructed during preprocessing using the KNN algorithm on spatial coordinates, as described in Sect. "Data preprocessing") was used to extract positive edges(i.e., spatially neighboring cell pairs). Adopting spatial adjacency as a proxy label provides a stable and reproducible benchmark for the link prediction task without relying on external annotations, which is a common practice in spatial graph learning. The positive edges were then split into training, validation, and test sets using the train_test_split function from sklearn.model_selection. After splitting, an equal number of negative edges (non-neighboring cell pairs) were generated for the validation and test sets using the random selection function np.random.choice from NumPy. For instance, for the Human Breast Cancer dataset, this procedure resulted in a total of 16,371 positive edges. Subsequently, the edges were divided into training, validation, and test sets in an 85%: 5%: 10% ratio, and the corresponding negative edges were added for validation and testing.

Importantly, all splits are performed on the static KNN-based A before any model training. This protocol strictly prevents information leakage and follows standard link-prediction evaluation practices.

To further validate the robustness and sensitivity of the proposed model under different data splits, 5-fold cross-validation was additionally performed on all three datasets. Specifically, the positive edges were randomly shuffled and divided into five equal folds. In each round, four folds were used for training and the remaining fold for testing, with an equal number of randomly sampled negative edges added to the test set to ensure a balanced evaluation. This procedure was repeated five times so that every edge appeared in the test set exactly once. The detailed results of the two experimental approaches are presented in Sect. "Spatial clustering results evaluation".

The main hyperparameters of the model include the GAT encoder dimensions, AE encoder dimensions, the K value in the K-nearest neighbors algorithm, and the number of initial cluster centers n_clusters. Based on the comparative experiments described below, the optimal settings were determined as follows.

GAT encoder: The input dimension was set to 50, matching the feature dimension after PCA-based dimensionality reduction. The encoder consisted of a three-layer architecture with dimensions 64, 64, and 50, respectively, and incorporated a multi-head attention mechanism (heads=2) to enhance neighborhood feature aggregation. The first two layers were designed to capture local spatial relationships, while the final layer reduced the representation to 50 dimensions to obtain compact node embeddings. Dropout (p=0.2) was applied at each layer to mitigate overfitting and balance expressive power with computational efficiency. Using the Mouse Brain Section dataset as an example, after 500 training epochs, the total loss decreased from 9.1144 to 1.1951, indicating that the model effectively captured spatial structural features. The loss curve is shown in Fig. 2.

Loss curve illustration

AE encoder: The input dimension was set to 50, consistent with the dimension of the node embeddings produced by the GAT encoder in Stage 1. The hidden layer dimensions were set to 128, 256, and 128, forming a symmetric bottleneck structure that balanced feature representation capacity with model complexity. A dropout rate of 0.2 was applied to mitigate overfitting and enhance generalization. Using the Human Breast Cancer dataset as an example, after 100 training epochs, the reconstruction loss decreased from 0.028 to 0.016, indicating that the model effectively captured the main structural information of the low-dimensional features, and provided a stable initialization for subsequent graph representation learning and clustering tasks.

The clustering results are not an end goal but serve as an important complementary analysis. Within the CAGNet framework, the clustering module functions as an intermediate component that guides node embedding learning through alternating optimization. The clustering visualizations and metrics are provided solely to demonstrate that this intermediate component is functioning as intended, producing biologically meaningful cell groupings, which in turn supports the robustness of the final link prediction.

To quantitatively evaluate the improvement in clustering quality brought by alternating optimization, the Silhouette score was computed over 5 independent runs across all three datasets, as summarized in Table 6. The Silhouette score measures how well each data point fits its assigned cluster relative to other clusters, ranging from -1 to 1 with higher values indicating better-defined clusters. After model training, the final node embeddings consistently achieve higher Silhouette scores compared to the initial PCA-based features across all three datasets, with improvements of +0.044, +0.055, and +0.085 on the Mouse Brain Section, Human Breast Cancer, and Human Spinal Cord datasets, respectively. The small standard deviations across runs further confirm the stability and reproducibility of the results. These findings suggest that by introducing the alternating update mechanism between clustering and node embeddings, the model not only effectively captures cell-cell interaction relationships but also measurably improves the compactness and separability of the learned representations.

For fair comparison, all models were trained using the same input features, adjacency matrix, and data splitting strategy. where RI is the Rand Index computed as the proportion of spot pairs that are assigned consistently between the predicted and true clusterings, and is its expected value under random label assignment. For spatial domain identification experiments, the number of clusters was set to match the ground-truth annotation of each dataset: 7 for the Mouse Brain Section dataset, and 9 for both the Human Breast Cancer and Human Spinal Cord datasets.

As shown in Table 7, CAGNet achieves the highest ARI across all three datasets (0.5336 on Mouse Brain Section dataset, 0.3919 on Human Breast Cancer dataset and 0.3277 on Human Spinal Cord dataset), outperforming both classical baselines (Leiden, Agglomerative) and specialized spatial transcriptomics methods (STAGATE, SpaGCN, stGRL and CiCLoDS). Information-theoretic analysis

To validate the effectiveness of CAGNet from an information-theoretic perspective, we further evaluated the mutual information (MI) between the input features X and the learned latent embeddings Z, denoted as I(X; Z). A higher MI score indicates that the learned representations retain more intrinsic underlying biological structure from the raw data. Since estimating MI in high-dimensional spaces is non-trivial, we employed a non-parametric KNN-based estimator (k=5) implemented via mutual_info_regression in scikit-learn, approximating I(X; Z) by averaging the marginal mutual information between X and each dimension of Z. To ensure a fair comparison, all methods use the same reference input X, namely the 50-dimensional PCA features obtained after preprocessing.

As shown in Table 10, CAGNet consistently achieves the highest MI scores across all three datasets, demonstrating superior information preservation. On the Mouse Brain Section dataset, CAGNet obtains 0.1720, outperforming all baselines including ClusterLP (0.0877), GAE (0.0473), GAT (0.0478), VGAE (0.0236), MGAE (0.0302), and SEGCECO (0.0538). Consistent improvements are also observed on the Human Breast Cancer (0.1065 vs. next-best 0.0462 for ClusterLP) and Human Spinal Cord (0.0855 vs. next-best 0.0255 for ClusterLP) datasets, representing improvements of 130.5% and 235.3% over the second-best method respectively. These results indicate that by integrating spatial graph structure and iterative clustering guidance, the latent space generated by CAGNet retains more spatial information, validating its effectiveness in leveraging spatial context for representation learning.

Removing the GAT encoder causes the most substantial performance degradation across all datasets, with F1 dropping from 0.9855 to 0.6968 on the Human Spinal Cord dataset and from 0.9621 to 0.8626 on Mouse Brain Section, confirming that graph attention-based spatial encoding is the most critical component for capturing cell-cell interaction patterns. Removing the reconstruction decoder results in marginal performance changes across all datasets (e.g., F1 decreasing by only 0.0016 on Mouse Brain Section), suggesting that feature reconstruction provides auxiliary regularization rather than being the primary driver of prediction quality. Removing the clustering module leads to noticeable degradation on the Human Spinal Cord dataset (F1: 0.9855 → 0.9698), while its impact is smaller on the other two datasets. This indicates that the KL-based soft assignment provides more critical structural guidance on datasets with higher tissue complexity, where cluster boundaries are harder to recover without explicit clustering supervision. Removing the alternating update mechanism leads to consistent degradation across all six metrics on all three datasets. The most notable drops are observed on Mouse Brain Section (ACC: 0.9606 → 0.9523, Precision: 0.9329 → 0.9299, F1: 0.9621 → 0.9607) and Human Spinal Cord (ACC: 0.9753 → 0.9741, F1: 0.9855 → 0.9843), demonstrating that the joint iterative refinement of node embeddings and cluster centers consistently improves model stability and generalization across all evaluated metrics and datasets.

Conclusion

This study addresses several prevalent limitations in existing CCI inference methods, including the decoupled treatment of node representation learning and clustering, the absence of structure-aware spatial constraints, and the lack of dynamic feedback during embedding optimization. We propose CAGNet, a two-stage framework that integrates GAT-based spatial representation learning with an alternating optimization mechanism to jointly refine node embeddings and cluster centers for CCI prediction.

In Stage 1, a three-layer GAT encoder with joint feature and graph reconstruction captures structure-aware embeddings from spatial transcriptomics data, providing a high-quality initialization for Stage 2. In Stage 2, the alternating optimization iteratively refines node embeddings and cluster centers in a mutually reinforcing manner, establishing a closed-loop between representation learning and clustering that overcomes the unidirectional dependency of conventional sequential paradigms.

Extensive experiments on three spatial transcriptomics datasets (Mouse Brain Section, Human Breast Cancer, and Human Spinal Cord) demonstrate that CAGNet consistently outperforms six CCI inference baselines across ACC, AUC, AP, Precision, Recall, and F1, achieving particularly strong results on the Human Spinal Cord dataset (ACC=0.9917, F1=0.9917). Beyond CCI prediction,CAGNet also achieves the highest ARI on all three datasets (mb=0.5336, hb=0.3919, hsc=0.3277), outperforming specialized spatial transcriptomics methods including STAGATE and SpaGCN, demonstrating that the alternating optimization produces embeddings that are more consistent with ground-truth spatial domain organization.

To further validate model effectiveness, we conducted information-theoretic analysis measuring I(X; Z) between the input PCA features and learned embeddings. CAGNet consistently achieves the highest mutual information across all datasets and all compared methods, confirming that the integration of spatial graph structure and iterative clustering guidance produces embeddings that retain substantially more informative content from the original gene expression profiles. Ablation studies confirm the contribution of each component: removing any single module leads to measurable performance degradation, with the clustering module showing greater importance on structurally complex datasets (F1 drop of 0.0157 on Human Spinal Cord) and the alternating update mechanism consistently improving ACC and F1 across all three datasets. 5-fold cross-validation further confirms the stability and reproducibility of CAGNet, with F1 standard deviations below 0.004 across all datasets, demonstrating that the results are not attributable to favorable data splits.

Despite these promising results, certain limitations remain. The model performance is sensitive to the choice of cluster number K; inappropriate selection may lead to over- or under-clustering and affect prediction accuracy. Additionally, the current framework assumes a homogeneous graph structure, which may be insufficient to capture diverse interaction patterns in heterogeneous networks with multiple relationship types. Future work could explore adaptive cluster number estimation, extension to heterogeneous and multi-relational graph scenarios, and integration of ligand-receptor prior knowledge to further improve the biological interpretability of inferred cell-cell interactions.
