Exact mean-field phase diagram for self-avoiding active particles in a lattice
Felipe Hawthorne, Cristiano F. Woellner, José A. Freire

Abstract
We investigate motility-induced phase separation in a lattice gas of self-propelled particles with hard-core exclusion, where an internal director biases particle hopping along the lattice coordination directions while undergoing rotational diffusion, together with a thermal-like translational diffusion. Rather than employing stochastic simulations, we adopt a master-equation formalism within a general mean-field approximation. By linearizing the mean-field master equation around the homogeneous stationary state and applying Bloch’s theorem, the stability analysis is reduced to a z z -dimensional tight-binding eigenvalue problem. A perturbation expansion in the wavenumber near 𝐤 = 0 {\mathbf{k}}=0 then yields the spinodal surface in closed analytical form for six Bravais lattices: linear, square, hexagonal, simple cubic, body-centered cubic, and face-centered cubic. The influence of lattice geometry is shown to enter exclusively through a single coefficient 𝒜 \mathcal{A} which we evaluate exactly for each case. We further show that translational diffusion smooths the interface between the dense and dilute phases. Finally, we determine the rotational probability currents associated with the inhomogeneous stationary states, a distinctive signature of the broken detailed balance underlying active-system dynamics.



I
Introduction

Self-propelled particles with purely repulsive interactions can phase-separate into coexisting dense and dilute states via a kinematic mechanism independent of attraction or alignment: particles accumulate where they slow down, increasing local density and further reducing their speed. This instability was first identified by Tailleur and Cates in a one-dimensional run-and-tumble model with density-dependent tumbling rates
26
. Simulations of persistently moving and repulsively interacting particles later confirmed the same behavior
13
;
20
, now known as Motility-Induced Phase Separation (MIPS). Experimental evidence was reported in active colloids
4
, with comprehensive reviews in
18
;
6
;
15
.

MIPS on lattices have been studied in settings that include density-dependent tumbling rates
27
;
21
;
22
, and persistent motion plus hard-core exclusion
23
;
9
;
28
;
29
.

Although the bulk of MIPS theory has been developed through Langevin-type simulations and effective hydrodynamic field equations
5
;
24
, the complementary probabilistic approach, based on master equations or Fokker-Planck equations, has remained comparatively rare in the MIPS literature, see however
3
.

We consider the master equation for self-propelled particles on a generic lattice with hard-core exclusion. Each particle carries an internal director that biases its persistent hopping along one of the
z
lattice directions and undergoes rotational diffusion, alongside thermal-like translational diffusion.

We present in close analytical form, for six different lattices and at the mean-field level, the spinodal surface that encloses the region of parameter space where the homogeneous state (gas phase) becomes locally unstable.

The probabilistic description of MIPS provides a complementary perspective on clustering, distinct from the dynamical, Langevin-based approaches prevalent in the literature. Within this framework, the roles of active motion and thermal-like noise are captured explicitly and visualized directly, making their contributions to phase separation transparent.

A hallmark of active matter is the presence of a nonzero, purely rotational, steady-state probability current in configuration space, arising from the breakdown of detailed balance
7
;
10
;
11
;
14
;
19
and observed in biological systems
1
;
8
;
2
. Such currents are not directly accessible within Langevin or hydrodynamic descriptions, whereas they emerge here transparently.

II
defines the lattice model. Section
III

IV

II
The Active Lattice Gas Model

In this section we define the model in terms of its stochastic dynamics, and in the next section the corresponding master equation. Our work will be entirely focused on the later.

We consider
N
active particles moving by hopping between nearest-neighbor sites of a Bravais lattice (linear, square, hexagonal, simple cubic, body-centered cubic and face-centered cubic) . Steric exclusion is enforced: no two particles may occupy the same site simultaneously. Each particle carries an internal director that, at any given time, points toward one of the
z
(the lattice coordination number) directions.

Our model closely follows the one introduced in Ref.
9
. Both the particle positions and their internal directors evolve in discrete time steps via the following update rules:

1.

Active motion: a particle attempts to move in the direction of its current internal director with rate
w_{a}
. That is, if the target site is unoccupied, the hop occurs with probability
w_{a}\,dt
, where
dt
is an infinitesimal time increment.

2.

Translational diffusion: independently of its internal director, a particle attempts to move to a randomly chosen nearest-neighbor site with rate
w_{t}
. As before, the move is accepted only if the target site is empty.

3.

Rotational diffusion of the director: the internal director can rotate to any of its adjacent directions with rate
w_{r}
. Our adjacency criterium is of minimal angle between lattice directions. For instance, on a square lattice, if the current direction is “up”, the director may rotate to “left” or “right” with probability
w_{r}\,dt
each.

III
The Mean-Field Master Equation

At the mean-field level, the probability of observing a given global microstate at time
t
is expressed in terms of the local probabilities
p_{{\mathbf{r}},s}(t)
, which represent the likelihood that site
{\mathbf{r}}
is occupied by a particle whose director points in the
s
-th direction, with
s=\{1,\ldots,z\}
.

It follows that,

p_{{\mathbf{r}},\Sigma}\equiv\sum_{s=1}^{z}p_{{\mathbf{r}},s}

(1)

is the probability that site
{\mathbf{r}}
is occupied and

N=\sum_{{\mathbf{r}}}p_{{\mathbf{r}},\Sigma}

(2)

is the total number of particles.

The time evolution of these probabilities follows from the stochastic dynamics defined above.

\displaystyle\frac{dp_{{\mathbf{r}},s}}{dt}=

\displaystyle w_{a}\,\big[p_{{\mathbf{r}}-{\mathbf{a}}_{s},s}\,(1-p_{{\mathbf{r}},\Sigma})-p_{{\mathbf{r}},s}\,(1-p_{{\mathbf{r}}+{\mathbf{a}}_{s},\Sigma})\big]

(3)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle w_{t}\,\sum_{{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}\sim\,{\mathbf{r}}}\big[p_{{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},s}\,(1-p_{{\mathbf{r}},\Sigma})-p_{{\mathbf{r}},s}\,(1-p_{{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},\Sigma})\big]

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle w_{r}\,\sum_{s^{\prime}\sim\,s}(p_{{\mathbf{r}},s^{\prime}}-p_{{\mathbf{r}},s}).

Here,
{\mathbf{a}}_{s}
denotes the lattice vector in the coordinate direction
s
. The notation
{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}\sim\,{\mathbf{r}}
indicates that
{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}
is a nearest neighbor to
{\mathbf{r}}
, and
s^{\prime}\sim s
indicates that
s^{\prime}
is one of the coordinate directions adjacent to
s
according to a minimal angle criterion.

The first term on the right-hand side represents active motion along the director
{\mathbf{a}}_{s}
; the second term accounts for translational diffusion; and the third term describes the rotational diffusion of the director. The
(1-p)
factors enforce the steric interaction.

This set of equations conserves the total number of particles and has a trivial homogeneous stationary solution (for a general lattice with
M
sites and coordination number
z
)

p^{H}_{{\mathbf{r}},s}=\phi/z,

(4)

where
\phi
is the filling fraction

\phi\equiv N/M.

(5)

III.1
The local stability of the homogeneous solution

Here we obtain analytically the domain of parameter space
\{w_{a},w_{t},\phi\}
(
w_{a}
and
w_{t}
in units of
w_{r}
) where the homogeneous stationary solution is locally stable.

Linearizing the master equation about the homogeneous solution,
{\mathbf{p}}(t)={\mathbf{p}}^{H}+\delta{\mathbf{p}}(t)
, where the
Mz
probabilies are arranged as a single-indexed vector with the
z
internal degrees of freedom grouped together for each of the
M
lattice sites, i.e.,
{\mathbf{p}}=(p_{11},\ldots,p_{1z},\ldots,p_{M1},\ldots,p_{Mz})
,

\frac{d\delta{\mathbf{p}}}{dt}={\mathbb{J}}^{H}\cdot\delta{\mathbf{p}}.

(6)

The Jacobian matrix of the homogeneous solution, denoted
{\mathbb{J}}^{H}
, consists of
z\times z
blocks
{\mathbb{J}}^{H}_{{\mathbf{r}},\tilde{\mathbf{r}}}
that couple internal degrees of freedom at neighboring lattice sites
{\mathbf{r}}
and
{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}
. More precisely:

{\mathbb{J}}^{H}_{{\mathbf{r}},\tilde{\mathbf{r}}}=\begin{cases}-w_{t}z\,{\mathbb{D}}+w_{r}\,{\mathbb{R}}-w_{a}\sum\limits_{s=1}^{z}{\mathbb{A}}(s),&\text{if }\tilde{\mathbf{r}}={\mathbf{r}},\\
w_{t}\,{\mathbb{D}}+w_{a}\,{\mathbb{A}}(s),&\text{if }\tilde{\mathbf{r}}={\mathbf{r}}+{\mathbf{a}}_{s},\end{cases}

(7)

where the
z
-dimensional square matrices
{\mathbb{D}}
,
{\mathbb{R}}
, and
{\mathbb{A}}(s_{0})
are:

{\mathbb{D}}_{s,\tilde{s}}=(1-\phi)\,\delta_{s,\tilde{s}}+\tfrac{\phi}{z}

(8)

{\mathbb{R}}_{s,\tilde{s}}=-n_{z}\,\delta_{s,\tilde{s}}+\delta_{s\sim\tilde{s}}

(9)

[{\mathbb{A}}(s_{0})]_{s,\tilde{s}}=(1-\phi)\,\delta_{s,-s_{0}}\,\delta_{s,\tilde{s}}+\tfrac{\phi}{z}\,\delta_{s,s_{0}}

(10)

The
n_{z}
in the
{\mathbb{R}}
matrix is the number of adjacent lattice coordinate directions (e.g.,
n_{z}=2
in the square and hexagonal lattices).
\delta_{s,\tilde{s}}
is the usual Kronecker delta, and
\delta_{s\sim\tilde{s}}
is
1
if
s
and
\tilde{s}
are adjacent directions, and
0
otherwise. The matrix
{\mathbb{R}}
can be viewed as the Laplacian of the (undirected) connection graph of coordinate directions.

The eigenvalues of the Jacobian matrix determine the local stability of the homogeneous stationary state (
4
). The translational invariance of
{\mathbb{J}}^{H}
(assuming periodic boundary conditions (PBC)) implies that its eigenvectors may be searched in Bloch form

(\delta{\mathbf{p}}_{\mathbf{k}})_{{\mathbf{r}},s}=({\mathbf{u}}_{\mathbf{k}})_{s}\,e^{i{\mathbf{k}}\cdot{\mathbf{r}}},

(11)

where the
M
vectors
{\mathbf{k}}
(consistent with the PBC) are restricted to the first Brillouin zone (1BZ) of the Bravais lattice.
The
z
eigenvalues associated with each
{\mathbf{k}}
are obtained from the following
z
-dimensional eigenvalue problem:

\displaystyle{\mathbbm{j}}_{\mathbf{k}}\cdot{\mathbf{u}}_{\mathbf{k}}=

\displaystyle\lambda_{\mathbf{k}}\,{\mathbf{u}}_{\mathbf{k}}

(12)

\displaystyle{\mathbbm{j}}_{\mathbf{k}}\equiv\Big\{w_{r}\,{\mathbb{R}}+w_{t}\,F({\mathbf{k}})\,{\mathbb{D}}\,+

\displaystyle w_{a}\sum_{s}(e^{i{\mathbf{k}}\cdot{\mathbf{a}}_{s}}-1)\,{\mathbb{A}}(s)\Big\}

\displaystyle F({\mathbf{k}})\equiv

\displaystyle\sum_{s}(e^{i{\mathbf{k}}\cdot{\mathbf{a}}_{s}}-1)

The entire procedure is analogous to analyzing the spectrum of a tight-binding Hamiltonian with
z
orbitals per lattice site. In particular, the eigenvalue spectrum is invariant under lattice point symmetries,

\lambda_{S{\mathbf{k}}}=\lambda_{{\mathbf{k}}},

(13)

where
S
denotes a matrix representation of any of the elements of the lattice point symmetry group.

{\mathbbm{j}}_{{\mathbf{k}}=0}=w_{r}\,{\mathbb{R}}
and exhibits a trivial zero eigenvalue associated with a uniform (all-ones) eigenvector. For a generic wavevector
{\mathbf{k}}
, some of the eigenvalues of
{\mathbbm{j}}_{\mathbf{k}}
may be complex. The homogeneous stationary state is locally unstable when any of the
Mz
eigenvalues of the full matrix
{\mathbb{J}}^{H}
has a positive real part.

The full spectrum is naturally presented in
{\mathbf{k}}
space and is illustrated in the Supporting Information for the case of a square lattice. There it becomes apparent that the negative real part eigenvalues first appear in the immediate vicinity of
{\mathbf{k}}=0
. That is, the homogeneous state becomes unstable against large wave-length fluctuations.

III.2
The spectrum near
{\mathbf{k}}=0

Here we assume an infinite lattice, so that the wavevectors
{\mathbf{k}}
form a continuum within the first Brillouin zone.

The matrix
{\mathbbm{j}}_{\mathbf{k}}
can be expanded in powers of
k
near
{\mathbf{k}}=0
:

{\mathbbm{j}}_{{\mathbf{k}}\sim 0}=\,w_{r}\,{\mathbb{R}}+{\mathbbm{j}}_{1}+{\mathbbm{j}}_{2}+\ldots

(14)

where (
d
is the spatial dimension and
a=|{\mathbf{a}}_{s}|
is the lattice parameter)

\displaystyle{\mathbbm{j}}_{1}\equiv

\displaystyle w_{a}\,\sum_{s}i({\mathbf{k}}\cdot{\mathbf{a}}_{s})\,{\mathbb{A}}(s)

(15)

\displaystyle{\mathbbm{j}}_{2}\equiv

\displaystyle-\frac{w_{t}za^{2}{\mathbf{k}}^{2}}{2d}\,{\mathbb{D}}-\,\frac{w_{a}}{2}\sum_{s}({\mathbf{k}}\cdot{\mathbf{a}}_{s})^{2}\,{\mathbb{A}}(s)

Standard Rayleigh–Schrödinger perturbation theory, applied with the caveat that
{\mathbbm{j}}_{1}
and
{\mathbbm{j}}_{2}
are non-Hermitian matrices, provides the leading-order
k
-dependence of the zero eigenvalue.

In terms of the normalized eigenvectors of the
{\mathbb{R}}
matrix,

{\mathbb{R}}\cdot{\mathbf{v}}_{n}=\lambda_{n}{\mathbf{v}}_{n},\quad(n=0,...,z-1),

(16)

where, in particular,

{\mathbf{v}}_{0}=z^{-1/2}[1,1,\ldots,1]^{T}\qquad(\lambda_{0}=0),

(17)

we obtain near
{\mathbf{k}}=0
:

\begin{split}\lambda_{0}&={\mathbf{v}}_{0}^{\dagger}\cdot{\mathbbm{j}}_{1}\cdot{\mathbf{v}}_{0}+{\mathbf{v}}_{0}^{\dagger}\cdot{\mathbbm{j}}_{2}\cdot{\mathbf{v}}_{0}\\
-&\sum_{n\neq 0}\frac{({\mathbf{v}}_{0}^{\dagger}\cdot{\mathbbm{j}}_{1}\cdot{\mathbf{v}}_{n})({\mathbf{v}}_{n}^{\dagger}\cdot{\mathbbm{j}}_{1}\cdot{\mathbf{v}}_{0})}{w_{r}\lambda_{n}}+\mathcal{O}(k^{3})\end{split}

(18)

In the Supporting Information we computed (

\begin{split}\lambda_{0}&=\Bigg[-\bigg(\frac{w_{t}z+w_{a}}{2d}\bigg)+\frac{w_{a}^{2}(1-\phi)(1-2\phi)}{w_{r}\,z}\,{\cal{A}}\Bigg]\,{\mathbf{k}}^{2},\qquad\\
&({\mathbf{k}}\sim 0).\end{split}

(19)

III.3
The Spinodal region

Figure
2
illustrates two different inhomogeneous stationary solutions of the mean field master equation for a
20\times 20
square lattice, both obtained with the same set of model parameters where the homogeneous solution is unstable (checked numerically), i.e., within the spinodal region. We simply integrated Eq.(

\sum_{s}\hat{{\mathbf{a}}}_{s}\,p_{{\mathbf{r}},s}

These solutions are locally stable, i.e., the numerically computed Jacobian spectrum consists of a single zero eigenvalue, with all other eigenvalues having negative real parts.

Because the master equation is invariant under the lattice space group operations, any stationary state is mapped to another stationary state by an arbitrary lattice translation combined with any lattice point-group symmetry. Each such stationary solution possesses its own basin of attraction and can be reached from suitable initial conditions.

III.3.2
The rotational probability current

The master equation can be viewed as a discrete probability continuity equation, and its stationary states have divergenceless probability currents (see the lattice expression for the current in the SI). All inhomogeneous stationary solutions of the ME display a non-zero, hence purely rotational, probability current. This is to be contrasted with relaxation to equilibrium when the master equation obeys detailed balance (not the case with Eq. (
3
)). When detailed balance is present, the ME drives the system to free-energy minima, with zero probability current. See the theoretical discussion in Refs.
10
;
11
, and an experimental illustration in Ref.
2
.

Figure
4

2
. The concentration of the probability current in the gas phase region is the probabilistic manifestation of the continuous attachment/detachment of particles to the MIPS cluster commonly seen in Langevin type simulations, see for instance Ref.
16
.

III.3.3
Homogeneous state escape time

We consider the mean escape time of an initial condition in the vicinity of the unstable homogeneous steady state. Following this escape, trajectories evolve toward a stable inhomogeneous state. The escape time is governed by the number and magnitude of the eigenvalues of
{\mathbb{J}}^{H}
with positive real parts, as well as by the projection of the initial condition onto the corresponding unstable eigenspace. We adopt a specific average measure of this timescale and analyze its dependence on model parameters within the spinodal region.

We use as initial condition:

p_{{\mathbf{r}},s}(0)=\tfrac{\phi}{z}+\xi_{{\mathbf{r}},s},

(20)

with
\xi_{{\mathbf{r}},s}
uniformly distributed with zero average and
\sum_{{\mathbf{r}},s}\xi_{{\mathbf{r}},s}^{2}=\epsilon^{2}
. That is, the starting point is at an Euclidean distance of
\epsilon
from the homogeneous steady state.

We followed the time evolution of the decrease of the dimensionless entropy per particle,

\Delta S(t)\equiv-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{{\mathbf{r}},s}p_{{\mathbf{r}},s}(t)\log[p_{{\mathbf{r}},s}(t)]-S_{H},

(21)

where
S_{H}=-\log(\phi/z)
is the homogeneous state entropy per particle. Some representative entropy evolutions can be seen in the SI.

III.4
The Metastable Region

The metastable region is the portion of parameter space outside the spinodal surface and inside the binodal surface in which the homogeneous state remains linearly stable yet coexists with locally stable inhomogeneous stationary states. If an underlying free-energy function existed, this region would be characterized by multiple free-energy minima. Outside the binodal surface the ME has a single locally stable stationary state, the homogeneous state.

A systematic investigation of the
Mz
dimensional microstate space to locate the binodal surface is beyond reach, so that we simply illustrate one such inhomogeneous stationary state for a set of parameters outside the spinodal surface, see Fig.
6
. The local stability was checked by numerically computing the Jacobian spectrum.

It is worth noting that, in a one-dimensional model closely related to ours
17
, a hydrodynamic approach enabled the semi-analytical determination of the binodal line. More generally, Langevin-type simulations are commonly employed to locate the binodal line
20
;
12
;
25
;
16
.

IV
Conclusion

We used a master-equation formulation to study motility-induced phase separation in a lattice gas of persistent particles with hard-core exclusion. Bloch’s theorem reduced the linearized dynamics around the homogeneous stationary state to a
z
-dimensional eigenvalue problem of tight-binding type. A perturbation expansion near
{\mathbf{k}}=0
produced a closed-form spinodal surface that retains the same functional structure across the six Bravais lattices considered, with lattice geometry entering only through a single coefficient
\mathcal{A}
that we computed exactly. Inhomogeneous stationary solutions inside the spinodal region become progressively smoother at the borders with increasing translational diffusion, in agreement with continuous-space Langevin studies of hard active disks
16
, and sustain rotational probability currents that follow directly from the absence of detailed balance and are computed here without coarse-graining.

The six Bravais lattices treated here are illustrative. The same construction could, in principle, be extended to other Bravais lattices, with or without a basis, and to dynamics with hopping beyond nearest neighbors.
