Freebirth and professional responsibility: Respect for autonomy does not justify clinical neutrality on preventable fetal and neonatal harm
Authors: Amos Grünebaum, Frank A Chervenak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe freebirth movement, planned birth without any qualified professional attendant, is expanding internationally, including in Scandinavia, where rising rates and incomplete outcome reporting have raised clinical and ethical concerns. A recent editorial in this journal emphasizes respectful engagement with women considering out‐of‐hospital birth and the importance of rebuilding trust through less coercive models of care. We argue that insufficient distinction between professionally attended home birth and planned unassisted birth risks conflating two distinct professional obligations: respecting patient autonomy and maintaining clinical neutrality in the face of preventable fetal and neonatal harm. These are not equivalent obligations. Respect for autonomy requires accurate, complete, evidence‐based counseling and a clear professional recommendation when the evidence warrants one, while preserving the patient's right to decline. After viability, the fetus acquires patient status with corresponding beneficence‐based obligations that planned unassisted birth forecloses entirely. Directive counseling against a practice associated with increased risk of preventable fetal and neonatal death is not coercion. It is what informed consent requires of the clinician. Professional responsibility does not ask clinicians to choose between compassion and clarity. It requires both.Keywords: fetal patient, freebirth, home birth, informed consent, neonatal harm, patient autonomy, professional responsibility, unassisted birth

Key message

Respecting autonomy does not require clinical neutrality about freebirth. Clinicians should counsel respectfully, preserve the woman's right to decline care, and make clear recommendations against planned unassisted birth when preventable fetal or neonatal harm is foreseeable.

The freebirth movement, planned birth without any qualified professional attendant, is no longer a marginal phenomenon. In Norway and Iceland, as well as other countries, rates of unassisted and unplanned home births have risen measurably over the past decade. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4

The commercial infrastructure behind this growth is substantial and largely US‐based. The Free Birth Society, founded in 2017 by a Los Angeles‐based former doula with no medical training, operates a podcast downloaded more than five million times, a YouTube channel with nearly 25 million views, and a suite of paid courses estimated to have generated $13 million since 2018. 3 A 2025 Guardian investigation documented 48 cases of late‐term stillbirths, neonatal deaths, or serious harm across multiple countries linked to Free Birth Society content and instruction. 3

Most national registries do not capture planned unassisted births as a distinct category, so the true scale of the practice and its associated outcomes remain incompletely known, a gap that is itself clinically significant. Against this background, the question of how obstetricians and midwives should counsel women considering unassisted birth is not merely academic. It has direct implications for fetal and neonatal outcomes, and it implicates foundational principles of professional responsibility.

A recent editorial raises legitimate concerns about the medicalization of childbirth and the erosion of trust that drives some women toward unassisted birth. 4 We share these concerns. Coercive clinical practices, failed communication, and the closure of small midwifery‐led units have contributed to the conditions in which the freebirth movement has grown. These are real problems that warrant serious attention.

However, some readers may interpret the editorial as suggesting that clinicians should minimize directional counseling about planned unassisted birth in order to preserve trust and avoid alienating women who reject medicalized childbirth. 4 We believe such an interpretation would be difficult to reconcile fully with professional responsibility obligations.

Importantly, the editorial does not explicitly endorse planned unassisted birth, nor does it recommend withholding discussion of clinical risk. The authors appropriately emphasize respectful engagement with women considering out‐of‐hospital birth and the importance of rebuilding trust through improved communication and less coercive models of care. We share these goals. However, we are concerned that insufficient distinction between professionally attended home birth and planned unassisted birth may unintentionally obscure important ethical and clinical differences. Respect for patient autonomy and maintenance of professional neutrality in the face of preventable fetal and neonatal harm are not equivalent obligations.

A GROWING BUT UNDERCOUNTED RISK

Reports of adverse fetal and neonatal outcomes associated with planned unassisted births are accumulating, even as systematic registry data remain limited by underregistration of this practice in most countries. A 2025 investigative report documented neonatal deaths linked to the Free Birth Society across multiple countries, raising concern about the organized promotion of unassisted birth by commercial social media operations. 3 Bjellmo and Iversen reported a growing number of unassisted home births in Norway accompanied by serious safety concerns and called for improved registration and oversight. 2 In Iceland, planned unassisted births rose from virtually nonexistent to 2.1 per 1000 births by 2024, a trend that prompted national attention precisely because outcomes in this group were not being captured. 4 The absence of a robust evidence base on planned unassisted birth outcomes does not imply safety. Rather, the evidence gap itself is part of the safety concern: planned unassisted birth removes professional attendance, emergency response, and systematic outcome reporting, making both preventable harm and reliable risk estimation more likely to be missed. A practice that forecloses data collection is not one that clinicians can responsibly treat as equivalent to attended alternatives.

RESPECT FOR PATIENT AUTONOMY AND PROFESSIONAL NEUTRALITY ARE NOT SYNONYMOUS

The distinction between respecting a patient's autonomy and abandoning professional judgment is foundational. Respect for autonomy requires that clinicians empower patients with accurate, complete, evidence‐based information, including a clear recommendation when the evidence warrants one, while preserving the patient's right to decline. This is what informed consent means in practice. A clinician who declines to offer a directional recommendation in deference to expressed patient preferences has not honored autonomy. That clinician has withheld the professional judgment that the patient came to access. Failure to offer clear, evidence‐based counseling when risk is clinically serious represents a failure of professional responsibility. 5 , 6

THE FETAL PATIENT

This obligation is especially weighty in obstetrics because the pregnant woman is not the only patient. After viability, the fetus acquires patient status, with corresponding beneficence‐based obligations on the part of the clinician. 5 Planned unassisted birth denies the fetal patient professional oversight at the moment it is most needed. The clinician should not remain neutral about a birth setting that forecloses all such obligations.

The risk of adverse neonatal outcome associated with planned unassisted births is at least as high as that of unplanned out‐of‐hospital births, which carry substantially higher neonatal mortality and morbidity than planned attended births in any setting. 7 , 8 , 9 This places planned unassisted birth in a categorically higher‐risk position than any attended out‐of‐hospital setting. Professionally attended out‐of‐hospital birth in carefully selected low‐risk women within an integrated maternity system is ethically and clinically different from planned birth without any qualified attendant.

TRUST WITHOUT EQUIVOCATION

It has been argued that suggesting that unassisted birth is irrational will reduce trust and drive women further from professional care. We understand this concern. However, the proposed remedy, treating planned unassisted birth as one position along a legitimate continuum of birth choices, carries its own serious risk. Normalizing a practice associated with preventable fetal and neonatal injury and death does not build sustainable trust. It lends professional legitimacy to a choice that the evidence does not support. The appropriate response to eroded trust is to engage women's legitimate grievances directly, address failures in communication, and advocate for reforms that make professional care more accessible and less coercive. None of this requires professional silence about avoidable risks.

Directive counseling is not coercion. It is not paternalism. Clinicians who coerce patients, override refusals, or perform interventions without consent act unprofessionally. However, a clinician who provides a clear, compassionate, evidence‐based recommendation against a practice that carries an increased risk of fetal and neonatal death, while fully preserving the patient's right to decline, has done exactly what is required of a professional. 5 , 6 Making a recommendation when evidence is clear does not diminish autonomy. It fulfills the clinician's professional obligation in the informed consent process and empowers the patient to make a genuinely informed choice.

The freebirth movement, not unlike the home birth movement, has grown in part because professional medicine has sometimes failed to explain its reasoning clearly, engage patient concerns seriously, and adapt to women's preferences for less interventive care. 10 The path back to trust runs through better communication, reformed systems, and more accessible midwifery‐led models, not through professional equivocation about preventable harm. 10 We agree with the editorial authors that respectful communication, avoidance of coercion, and attention to women's concerns are essential components of ethical maternity care. Clinicians should listen carefully, address systemic failures honestly, and counsel directionally when the evidence is clear. All three are possible simultaneously. Professional responsibility does not ask clinicians to choose between compassion and clarity. It asks them to practice both. Respecting autonomy means preserving the woman's right to decline care, but it does not require clinicians to remain neutral when a preventable pathway to fetal or neonatal harm is being chosen.
