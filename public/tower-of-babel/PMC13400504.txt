Impact of millet-immobilized probiotic on oxidation and volatile profile of dry-fermented sausage
Authors: Marilia Silva Malvezzi Karwowski, Caroline Maria Andrade Cavalari, Bruno José Gonçalves Silva, Renata Ernlund Freitas Macedo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigated the impact of incorporating millet-immobilized probiotics on the oxidation and volatile profile of dry-fermented sausage. Four formulations were tested: FC (free-cell Lp. plantarum), NI4% (free-cell + 4% millet), I2% (2%-immobilized Lp. plantarum), and I4% (4% millet-immobilized Lp. plantarum). Analyses were carried out during ripening (0, 2, 7, 14, and 21 days) and storage (41, 61, 81, 100, and 121 days). Immobilized probiotics showed higher counts (> 8 log CFU/g) than free cells (P < 0.05) for up to 121 days of storage. Immobilization did not impact the pH, Aw and carbonyl contents of the product, however the millet addition demonstrated a significant antioxidant effect, inhibiting lipid oxidation. Forty-one volatile compounds were identified, with their complexity increasing significantly over time. While alcohols predominated at the onset of ripening (day 0), aldehydes and alcohols became the dominant chemical families during storage. Notably, the volatile profile of treatments with immobilized probiotics (I2% and I4%) remained consistent with the free-cell control (FC), indicating that the millet matrix successfully preserved the product’s characteristic aroma. PCA confirmed that sample differentiation was primarily driven by storage duration rather than the immobilization process. Although hexanal was the most abundant oxidation marker, its delayed predominance in millet-immobilized formulations suggests a protective effect against advanced oxidative deterioration. These findings demonstrate that millet is an effective carrier for maintaining probiotic viability and oxidative stability without compromising the volatile fingerprint of dry-fermented sausages.

Introduction

Fermentation is among the oldest food preservation technologies and remains fundamental to the production of meat products (Pilevar and Hosseini, 2017). Dry-fermented sausages are typically manufactured using meat (commonly pork), animal fat, salt, spices, and selected starter cultures under controlled conditions, resulting in products with characteristic texture, aroma, and flavor profiles (Sun et al., 2020).

Starter cultures, particularly combinations of Lactiplantibacillus plantarum and Staphylococcus xylosus, are key drivers of biochemical transformations in dry-fermented sausages. Through carbohydrate fermentation, proteolysis, and lipolysis, these microorganisms promote acidification, microbiological stability, and the formation of volatile compounds that define product aroma and sensory quality (Ba et al., 2018; Kaya & Kaban, 2024). In addition to their technological role, selected lactic acid bacteria (LAB) may exhibit probiotic properties, offering opportunities for the development of functional fermented meat products. However, maintaining probiotic viability during sausage processing and extended storage remains a major technological challenge, particularly when probiotic cells are added in free form.

Immobilization strategies have been proposed to enhance the survival and functional stability of starter and probiotic bacteria under adverse processing conditions. Protective matrices can physically entrap bacterial cells, reducing their exposure to environmental stresses such as dehydration, oxygen, and oxidative reactions. Previous studies have demonstrated that encapsulation systems based on protein–polysaccharide matrices effectively preserve probiotic viability in food systems during storage (Atta et al., 2024, 2025), supporting the application of immobilization approaches in fermented foods. Cereal grains have emerged as natural, food-grade carriers for probiotic immobilization in fermented sausages. Wheat grains have been successfully applied as immobilization supports in meat fermentation systems (Sidira et al., 2015, 2017, 2019). Nonetheless, gluten-free grains such as millet represent a promising alternative. Due to their small particle size and light color, millet can be incorporated at low levels without negatively affecting product appearance or formulation. When added in limited amounts, millet facilitates the physical entrapment of probiotic cells within its structure, providing mechanical protection and improving bacterial survival throughout fermentation, drying, and storage.

In this context, our previous work demonstrated the effective use of proso millet (Panicum miliaceum L.) as an immobilization matrix for Lp. plantarum, maintaining probiotic populations above 8 log CFU/g in dry-fermented sausages stored for > 90 days under non-refrigerated conditions (Karwowski et al., 2023). Beyond its technological functionality, millet contributes nutritional and functional value, as it contains proteins, carbohydrates, dietary fiber, and antioxidant compounds, including phenolic acids (notably ferulic acids), flavonoids, and tannins (Saha et al., 2016). These bioactive components may further influence oxidative processes in meat systems.

Lipid oxidation and volatile compound formation in fermented sausages are strongly influenced by microbial enzymatic activity, seasoning, and formulation components (Ferrocino et al., 2018). Consequently, evaluating how millet-based immobilization of probiotic LAB affects oxidative stability and aroma development is essential for assessing its technological feasibility. Despite growing interest in functional fermented meats, studies addressing the combined effects of immobilized probiotics on lipid and protein oxidation and volatile profiles during extended storage remain limited.

Therefore, the objective of this study was to evaluate the impact of incorporating Lp. plantarum immobilized in millet on oxidation and volatile compounds profile of dry-fermented sausages during processing and storage at room temperature. By monitoring these parameters over 121 days, this work provides new insight into the application of cereal-based immobilization systems as multifunctional tools to enhance probiotic viability while preserving the physicochemical quality of fermented meat products.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strain and culture conditions

Lactiplantibacillus plantarum was isolated from the native microbiota of traditional salami, with its probiotic attributes examined in prior investigations (Santa et al., 2015; Wolupeck et al., 2017). Staphylococcus xylosus, sourced from the commercial inoculum Bactoferm T-SPX (CHR Hansen, Denmark), served as another starter culture. Free cell inocula of Lp. plantarum and S. xylosus were cultivated by growing the strains in MRS broth (De Man Rogosa Sharpe, Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) and TSB broth (Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA), respectively, at 37 °C for 48 h. Subsequently, the broths underwent centrifugation (Eppendorf 5810R) at 5000 x g for 15 min at 21 °C. The supernatants were discarded, and the resulting pellets were reconstituted in sterile 0.9% saline solution for use in inoculating the meat mixture (Karwowski et al., 2023, 2024).

Preparation of support and cell immobilization

Millet were sterilized at 121 °C for 15 min, and immobilization of Lp. plantarum occurred using a cell suspension containing approximately 5 × 10¹⁰ CFU/mL and 500 g of millet in 2 L MRS broth (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany), followed by incubation at 37 °C for 72 h (Karwowski et al., 2023, 2024). Following immobilization, the liquid was removed, and millet underwent two washes with sterile water to eliminate any remaining free cells attached to their surfaces.

Production of probiotic dry-fermented sausages

Fermented sausages were crafted using a blend of pork meat (60% w/w), beef (20% w/w), pork fat (12% w/w), and a mix of seasonings including NaCl (1.8% w/w), sodium isoascorbate (1% w/w), glucose (0.5% w/w), sucrose (0.5% w/w), NaNO3 (0.005% w/w), NaNO2 (0.015% w/w), and spices such as white pepper, garlic, and nutmeg. Pork shoulder and lean beef underwent grinding using a conventional meat grinder (JAMAR®, Curitiba, Brazil) equipped with an 8 mm disc, while pork back fat was cubed (~ 0.5 cm3). Following ingredient blending, S. xylosus and Lp. plantarum (either free or millet-immobilized) were introduced to target concentrations of approximately 5 log CFU/g and 9 log CFU/g, respectively. To ensure experimental accuracy, microbiological analysis was performed directly on the meat batter after inoculation. These baseline counts revealed an actual initial concentration of approximately 8 log CFU/g for Lp. plantarum.

Four batches were created: FC—comprising free cells of Lp. plantarum; NI4%—consisting of free cells of Lp. plantarum with an additional 4% of sterile millet; I2%—containing 2% millet-immobilized Lp. plantarum; and I4%—incorporating 4% millet-immobilized Lp. plantarum. The mixtures were then stuffed into collagen casings (De Comércio de Tripas e Condimentos Ltda., Paraná, Brazil) with a diameter of 25 mm and superficially inoculated with a commercial suspension of Penicillium nalgiovense (Bactoferm MOLD-600 CHR Hansen, Denmark). The sausages were placed in a drying chamber at 25 °C and 91% relative humidity (RH) for 48 h. The temperature was gradually reduced by 1 °C every 24 h until reaching 18 °C. Similarly, the relative humidity was decreased by 1% every 24 h until it reached 85%. After a ripening period of 21 days, the fermented sausages were considered “ready to eat.” Subsequently, the sausages were vacuum-packed in polyethylene bags and stored at 21 ± 1 °C and 83 ± 2% RH for 100 days (Karwowski et al., 2023). Evaluation of pH, Aw, lipid oxidation (TBARS), protein oxidation (carbonyls), and count of Lp. plantarum were carried out during ripening (days 0 R, 2 R, 7 R, 14 R, and 21 R) and storage (days 41 S, 61 S, 81 S, 101 S, and 121 S). The count of S. xylosus was performed only during ripening. The experiment was conducted independently twice. pH, Aw, TBARS, and carbonyls were analyzed in triplicate (n = 6, 60 samples per treatment). Count of Lp. plantarum and S. xylosus were analyzed in duplicate (n = 4, 40 samples per treatment and n = 4, 20 samples per treatment), respectively. Analysis of volatile compounds was performed at 0 R, 21 R, 61 S, and 121 S, in duplicate (n = 4, 16 samples per treatment).

Count of Lp. plantarum and S. xylosus

Representative 25 g portions of fermented sausage samples were blended with 225 mL of sterilized peptone water 0.1% (Sigma-Aldrich, UK) and subjected to serial dilutions. Lp. plantarum 343 was enumerated on MRS agar (Himedia Laboratories Pvt. Ltd, Mumbai, India) at 37 °C for 48 h., while S. xylosus was enumerated on Baird Parker agar (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) at 37 °C for 48 h (APHA, 2015).

Physicochemical analyses

pH and Aw (water activity)

The pH was measured with a digital pH meter (HI253, Hanna Instruments, Dallas, Texas, USA), by inserting the electrode into three randomly selected points in the sausage, following the protocol by the Adolfo Lutz Institute Analysis Manual (IAL, 2008). The water activity (Aw) was assessed using an Aqualab CX2 device (Decagon Devices Inc., Canada).

Determination of carbonyls

Carbonyl content extraction followed the procedure outlined by Jongberg et al. (2013) with slight modifications. Initially, 3 g of fermented sausage was homogenized in 20 mL of isolation buffer (comprising 10 mM monosodium phosphate [NaH2PO4], 100 mM sodium chloride [NaCl], 2 mM magnesium chloride [MgCl2], and 1 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid [EDTA]) through four consecutive centrifugation cycles (at 3,000 g and 4 °C for 15 min each). Before the final centrifugation step, the pellet was resuspended in 10 mL of 100 mM NaCl, homogenized, and filtered through voile fabric. The resulting pellets were diluted in 5 mL of deionized water. Carbonyl compounds were determined using the colorimetric DNPH method (Colombo et al., 2016), which involves derivatization of protein carbonyls (PCO) with 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine (DNPH) followed by spectrophotometric analysis. The concentration of carbonyls was calculated using an absorption coefficient of 22,000 M/cm for the formed hydrazone at 370 nm, and expressed as nmol per mg of protein (nmol/mg protein).

Determination of TBARS

Lipid oxidation was assessed by determining the TBARS value, quantified in mg MDA/kg, employing a standard curve of malonaldehyde (MDA) following the method outlined by Vyncke (1970). Absorbance readings at 538 nm were obtained for each sample using a spectrophotometer (Spectrum, model SP-1105).

Determination of volatile compounds

Volatile organic compounds (VOCs) were analyzed using Solid Phase Microextraction (SPME) followed by Gas Chromatography-Mass Spectrometry (GC-MS/MS), according to Di Cagno et al. (2008), with minor modifications. Briefly, 4 g of each sample were sealed in a 20 mL headspace vial with a Teflon-lined septum. Extraction was performed at 40 °C for 30 min using a 2 cm fiber coated with 50/30 $\mu$m Divinylbenzene/Carboxen/Polydimethylsiloxane (DVB/CAR/PDMS) (Supelco, Bellefonte, PA, USA). The analytes were analyzed using a GC-2010 Plus coupled to a TQ8040 triple quadrupole mass spectrometer with an AOC-5000 Plus autosampler (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan). A SH-Rtx-5MS capillary column (30 m x 0.25 mm x 0.25 $\mu$m) was used, with Helium as the carrier gas (1.8 mL/min). The oven temperature began at 40 °C (2 min), increased to 200 °C at 10 °C/min, and then to 250 °C at 15 °C/min, holding for 5 min. Injector and detector temperatures were 280 °C and 250 °C, respectively, with a 1:5 split ratio.

VOCs were identified by comparing their mass spectra with the NIST (National Institute of Standards and Technology), considering only compounds with a similarity index higher than 85%. The relative abundance of each identified volatile compound was determined by calculating its peak area relative to the total area of all integrated peaks, expressed as a percentage (Area %).

Statistical analysis

Data of pH, Aw, carbonyls, TBARS and count of starter cultures were analyzed using the General Linear Model (GLM), incorporating treatment (FC, NI4%, I2%, and I4%), time (0 R, 2 R, 7 R, 14 R, 21 R, 41 S, 61 S, 81 S, 101 S, 121 S), as fixed effects, and repetition as a random effect. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed and means were compared using Tukey’s test (P < 0.05).

Qualitative analysis of volatile compounds was performed based on the built-in database of NIST (National Institute of Standards and Technology) Library Search. The total peak areas expressed as percentages were calculated by the normalization method. and presented as mean ± standard error. The analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s test (P < 0.05), was employed to compare treatments at 0 R, 21 R, 61 S, and 121 S. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was also conducted on identified volatile compounds to elucidate the aroma profile of the fermented sausages. Analyses were carried out using the Statgraphics Centurion Program (16.1.11, Statpoint Technologies, Warrenton, Virginia, USA).

Results and discussion

pH and Aw

pH values were affected by treatment and time (Table 1). All treatments showed a significant decrease in pH at the beginning of ripening. At 2 R, pH values had reached the lowest average, 4.99. The initial pH drop is consistent with the study by Seleshe and Kang (2021), who observed that rapid acidification is an important characteristic of lactic acid bacteria (starter cultures), with pH values ranging from 4.91 to 5.23 after ripening. At 7 R, a significant and progressive increase in pH was observed across all treatments until 21 R. This phenomenon in the later stages of dry sausage ripening is characteristic. This trend is primarily attributed to microbial-associated proteolytic activity, which becomes more prominent after the initial acidification phase. During this period, bacterial proteases and endogenous muscle enzymes degrade myofibrillar and sarcoplasmic proteins into peptides and free amino acids. The subsequent microbial deamination of these compounds leads to the accumulation of alkaline metabolites, such as ammonia and amines. These nitrogenous substances exert a buffering effect on the organic acids (primarily lactic acid) produced during fermentation, resulting in the gradual elevation of pH values until the end of the ripening process (Hu et al., 2023; Loffi et al., 2024). The lower pH observed in FC may be associated with the absence of millet in this treatment. In contrast, formulations containing 2% and 4% millet showed comparatively higher pH values during ripening, particularly between 7 and 21 days of maturation. The incorporation of plant-based ingredients and dietary fibers into meat matrices frequently modulates the physicochemical environment, particularly affecting the pH of the final product. According to a systematic review by Siladji et al. (2024), the addition of macro- and microalgae to various sausage formulations generally leads to a significant increase in pH values. Specifically, the inclusion of Kappaphycus alvarezii in mechanically deboned chicken meat sausages resulted in a significant rise in pH, with values ranging from 6.53 in control samples to 6.64 in formulations containing 6% seaweed (Pindi et al., 2017). Similarly, the use of Undaria pinnatifida (Wakame) in salt-reduced frankfurters (Choi et al., 2017) elevated the pH from 6.05 in the control to 6.15 in samples treated with 2.0% seaweed. The addition of Chlorella species to pork frankfurters (Bošković Cabrol et al., 2023) also promoted higher pH levels compared to control samples. This upward trend was also observed in fermented products, such as Spanish “chorizo” sausages reformulated with microalgae proteins (Thirumdas et al., 2018), where pH values increased significantly from 5.83 in soy-enriched control samples to 5.97 for Spirulina and 5.88 for Chlorella. These fluctuations are primarily attributed to the inherent mineral and polysaccharide composition of the added ingredients, which interact with the meat’s buffering capacity (Siladji et al., 2024). It can be hypothesized that millet, analogous to these algae species, contains a complex matrix rich in minerals and dietary fibers that may provide a comparable buffering or slightly alkalizing effect within the meat matrix, thereby explaining the observed pH trends in the fermented sausage formulation. Nevertheless, by 101 S and 121 S, these differences had leveled off, with no significant variation among treatments.

Carbonyl content

Protein oxidation promotes the fragmentation of the protein backbone, yielding smaller peptides, and the subsequent reactions of carbonyl groups may contribute to the formation and modulation of flavor compounds (Chen et al., 2017). In contrast to the observed impact on lipid oxidation, the addition of millet did not affect protein oxidation in fermented sausages (Table 3), however, maturation time had a significant effect on carbonyl content. A significant increase in carbonyls was observed in all treatments (P < 0.05) throughout ripening (0 R to 21 R), indicating heightened protein susceptibility to oxidation during this period. Notably, carbonyl content remained stable during storage (from 41 S to 121 S), an observation that aligns with the findings of Xiao et al. (2018) and Chen et al. (2015) in Cantonese dry sausages. This oxidative behavior is explained by the fact that protein oxidation in fermented meat products is a complex free-radical chain reaction involving initiation, transmission, and termination phases (Wang et al. 2022a, b; Estévez, 2011). The increase during ripening is primarily driven by reactive oxygen species (ROS) and metal-catalyzed reactions, where transition ions such as Fe2+ or Cu+ promote the formation of carbon-centered protein radicals (Wang et al. 2022a, b). Conversely, the stability during storage can be attributed to the regulatory role of LAB, which function as natural antioxidants by scavenging free radicals and chelating pro-oxidant metal ions (Wang et al. 2022a, b). This stability is further supported by the LAB’s intracellular enzymes, such as superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT), and the production of antioxidant metabolites like bioactive peptides (Wang et al. 2022a, b; Lund et al. 2011). While botanical ingredients may lead to an initial rise in carbonyls, they increase the matrix’s antioxidant activity, contributing to overall stability (Skwarek & Karwowska, 2025). Additionally, the accumulation of alkaline metabolites from protein degradation exerts a buffering effect that stabilizes the fermented matrix during prolonged storage (Wang et al. 2022a, b).

During the storage time, TBARS in FC increased from 1.94 to 2.34 MDA/kg (P < 0.05). According to Amini Sarteshnizi et al. (2015), TBARS value higher than 2.0 mg MDA/kg is the threshold for meat rancidity. In this study, the threshold was achieved at 61 S only in FC. In all other treatments, with the addition of millet, the threshold was achieved only at 100 S (I4%) and 121 S (NI4% and I2%). The lower lipid oxidation observed in sausages with millet (NI4%, I2%, and I4%) might be related to the amount of polyphenolic compounds in millet, that act as free radical scavengers against superoxide radicals (O2•−), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), hydroxyl radicals (HO•), and singlet oxygen (1O2), inhibiting the lipid oxidation (Grace et al., 2016). Millets are notable for their high content of phenolic compounds, mainly phenolic acids, flavonoids, and tannins. This profile confers strong antioxidant activity (Hassan et al., 2021). Other studies investigating the inclusion of seeds and plants rich in polyphenols in fermented sausages have demonstrated notable reductions in TBARS. For instance, Zhang et al. (2017) found a significant decrease in TBARS levels with the addition of rose polyphenols at a concentration of 3 mg/g. Similarly, Kurt (2016) reported a reduction in TBARS by incorporating grape seed at 1.5%, while Aquilani et al. (2018) found the same effect with grape seed and chestnut extracts. Therefore, the addition of millet might have contributed to the inhibition of lipid oxidation in dry-fermented sausages.

Count of Lp. plantarum and S. xylosus

Time and treatment significantly affected the viable counts of the probiotic strain (Table 5). Lp. plantarum has been widely considered for use in the production of fermented meat products due to its desirable technological and functional characteristics (Seleshe & Kang, 2021). In the present research, both time and treatment significantly affected the viability of the probiotic strain. The initial population of Lp. plantarum was approximately 8 log CFU/g across all experimental groups. During the ripening, cell counts peaked at day 21 in treatments I2% and I4%, reaching maximum levels of 10.18 and 10.30 log CFU/g, respectively. Following this peak, a gradual decline in viable cells was observed. However, during storage, samples I2% and I4% maintained significantly higher viability compared to the other non-immobilized groups, except at day 81. At 121 S, the fermented sausages with immobilized probiotic cells still presented elevated counts (> 8 log CFU/g), suggesting that the millet matrix provided protective effects for Lp. plantarum throughout storage. Immobilization of Lp. plantarum within millets enhances probiotic stability primarily through physical entrapment, which limits direct exposure to environmental stresses associated with processing and storage. Karwowski et al. (2023) demonstrated that millets were able to maintain high populations of immobilized Lp. plantarum (> 7 log CFU/g) and preserve its metabolic activity during up to 150 days of non-refrigerated storage, in addition to conferring protection during simulated oro-gastrointestinal digestion. Similarly, studies in fermented meat products have reported that immobilization or microencapsulation strategies improve probiotic survival by protecting bacterial cells against acidification, oxidative stress, dehydration, and other adverse conditions associated with fermentation and ripening (Sirini et al., 2022; Mani-López et al., 2024). In addition, the close association between immobilized cells and millet components, such as carbohydrates and phenolic compounds, may support bacterial metabolic activity and contribute to protection against oxidative stress. Rodríguez et al. (2024) also reported that Lp. plantarum is capable of metabolizing food phenolic compounds, generating bioactive metabolites with antioxidant activity that may improve bacterial adaptation and stability in plant-based matrices. These combined effects likely contribute to the greater stability and sustained activity of immobilized probiotics observed during extended storage, particularly after the ripening time.

The observed decline in Staphylococcus xylosus counts during the final stages of ripening is primarily attributed to dynamic physicochemical changes within the meat matrix, specifically the rapid reduction in pH and water activity (aw) (Wang et al. 2022a, b; Hu et al. 2023). Furthermore, the progressive dehydration of the product leads to a significant decrease in water activity. Once aw levels drop below approximately 0.81, most staphylococcal species cease to proliferate, resulting in the stabilization or gradual reduction of their populations toward the end of the ripening process (Hu et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2022a, b). S. xylosus is usually used as part of the starter culture in fermented meat products due to its dual role. Its antioxidant enzymes, such as catalase and superoxide dismutase, contribute to the early stabilization of lipid oxidation, while its esterase and lipolytic activities are essential for the formation of esters and branched aldehydes that define the ripened aroma of this type of product (Ba et al. 2018; Wang et al. 2022a, b). Since S. xylosus concentration and behavior remained consistent among all four formulations throughout the ripening process, the observed superior oxidative stability and aromatic complexity in the immobilized treatments (I2% and I4%) can be specifically attributed to the protective and antioxidant properties of the millet-immobilization matrix rather than the starter culture alone.

Volatile profile

The interest in the aromatic compound profile in the meat industry has been reported in several studies (Borrajo et al., 2022; Hu et al., 2020; Solomando et al., 2020), however, when functional starter cultures are immobilized for the production of probiotic fermented sausage, reports are scarce. Therefore, the strategy was adopted to assess the influence of immobilizing Lp. plantarum in millet on the volatile profile of the sausages. In this study, 41 different volatile flavor compounds were detected. The number of identified volatile compounds showed low variation throughout ripening and storage time: 0 R (n = 23), 21 R (n = 26), 61 S (n = 25), and 121 S (n = 24). The identified and quantified compounds were classified into the following chemical families: 14 alcohols, 9 aldehydes, 7 organic acids, 5 hydrocarbons, 4 esters, and, 2 ketones. Compounds originating from spices were also detected, including terpenes such as α-terpinene and limonene, sulfur compounds like diallyl disulfide from garlic, and thymol from oregano used in the formulation. As expected, these compounds were present in all treatments and were therefore excluded from the analysis.

Aroma perception is influenced not only by the concentration and odor thresholds of volatile compounds but also by their interactions with other food components. These compounds are generated through various chemical pathways that impart characteristic meat flavors and are classified according to their possible origins (Bleicher et al., 2022): lipid autooxidation, amino acid catabolism (Strecker degradation), carbohydrate fermentation, compounds from spices, staphylococci esterase activity, β oxidation, and unknown origin (derived from meat or food contaminants). At the onset of ripening (0 R), the aromatic profile was characterized by varying degrees of complexity among treatments: the FC group exhibited the highest initial diversity with 19 compounds, whereas the I4% treatment started with only 9 identified compounds. During this initial phase, alcohols were the predominant family in all formulations, showing the highest mean peak areas per compound, which ranged from 5.18% in FC to 7.81% in NI4%. As processing and storage progressed, a significant upward trend in the complexity of the aldehyde family was observed across all treatments. The number of identified aldehydes rose from 1 to 3 compounds at 0 R to a stable range of 7–8 compounds by 121 S for all groups, including the immobilized treatments (I2% and I4%). Regarding abundance, the mean area per aldehyde compound peaked at 21 R, with the FC treatment showing the highest area (6.24%), followed by NI4% (5.10%) and I2% (5.28%), while I4% maintained a more controlled level (4.10%). The alcohol family reached its maximum complexity at day 61 S, particularly in the immobilized treatments I2% and I4%, which identified 10 compounds each, compared to 9 compounds in FC and NI4%. However, despite this increase in variety, the mean area per alcohol compound significantly declined in all four treatments toward the end of storage, reaching values between 1.60% (I4%) and 1.72% (NI4%) at 121 S. Notably, the total number of volatile compounds followed distinct trajectories: while the FC group remained stable (19 compounds at both 0 R and 121 S), the treatments containing the millet matrix showed a marked increase in aromatic diversity over time. Specifically, the I4% treatment demonstrated the most significant evolution, more than doubling its complexity from 9 compounds at 0 R to 21 compounds at 121 S. This suggests that the immobilization of probiotics within the 4% millet matrix modulates the gradual release and formation of a wider array of volatile metabolites throughout the product’s shelf life.

The hydrocarbon was also present, especially at 21 R, when the immobilized treatments showed the highest mean peak area, which decreased over time. It is well known that aliphatic hydrocarbons have a low influence on the flavor of dry-cured meat products (Sidira et al., 2016). Organic acids constitute an important category of volatile compounds; however, in our study, this class was not predominant. Regarding the ester family, its formation is dependent on the presence of alcohols and acids, and the esterase enzyme produced by staphylococci in the starter culture. Esters are generated through esterification reactions involving carboxylic acids and ethanol and may be associated with the activity of specific microorganisms. The carboxylic acids participating in these reactions originate from the oxidation of aldehydes formed in the product during processing (Sha et al., 2017). Esters impart fruity and flowery notes to the flavor of dry-fermented sausage; some ethyl esters are responsible for this characteristic aroma that can mask rancid odors (Sun et al., 2010). Ketones are known to exert a significant influence on the aroma of meat products; however, they were more prevalent in the early stages of ripening.

Moreover provides the total sun for each class, considering the compounds identified across the different treatments throughout the time. Firstly, regarding the total sun of peak area of each class, there were some differences among treatments at the same time and, within the treatments over time (P < 0.05) for ketones and hydrocarbons. Alcohols originate from lipid oxidation, amino acid metabolism, and the reduction of methyl ketones (Zhou et al., 2021).

Alcohols are key contributors to the flavor of fermented meat products, as they significantly influence the overall aroma profile due to their low sensory perception thresholds (Dong et al., 2024). In particular, 1-hexanol (fatty, rancid, rotten fruit) (Montanari et al., 2018) was the predominant compound in the alcohol class, and its values decreased over time. From 61 S, the peak area of all treatments decreased, and at 121 S, I2% and I4% presented lower values (P < 0.05) compared with other treatments, indicating that the immobilization in millet could have a positive effect on reducing oxidation. 1-pentanol and 1-propanol (vegetal, green, pungent) remained stable over time, however, this last compound was only detected in I2% and I4% at 61 S. Several metabolites of bacterial origin, particularly from amino acid catabolism, were detected, including 3-methyl-1-butanol, 2-methyl-1-butanol, and 2-ethyl-1-hexanol. At 61 S, the concentrations of these compounds were significantly lower in I4% (P < 0.05). These volatile compounds are associated with a variety of sensory notes, including fatty, woody, and herbaceous characteristics (Lorenzo, 2014). Additionally, Bosse et al. (2017) linked the presence of alcohols to sweet, fruity, as well as onion- and mushroom-like aromas.

Aldehydes were the second most predominant family compound in fermented sausages, for which hexanal showed the highest content. This compound has been used as an indicator of quality and oxidative stability in meat and meat products. It originates from the secondary oxidation of ω−6 PUFA (Solomando et al., 2020), and it is the most abundant compound in meat products such as lacón (dry-cured shoulder) (Domínguez et al., 2016; Lorenzo, 2014), cecina (Gómez & Lorenzo, 2013), chorizo (Gómez & Lorenzo, 2013), and ham (Bermúdez et al., 2015). In low concentrations, it imparts pleasant green notes; however, in elevated amounts, it contributes to an undesirable rancid aroma. Despite the increase in hexanal concentration over time in all formulations, its evolution was significantly modulated by the treatment type. At the end of ripening (21 R), the FC group reached a hexanal content of 28.98%, whereas the I4% treatment exhibited a much lower intensity of 17.74%. This significant difference highlights that the immobilization of probiotics within the 4% millet matrix was more effective at delaying advanced oxidation than the free-cell control or the simple addition of millet in the NI4% group.

Other linear aldehydes associated with lipid auto-oxidation, such as nonanal (citrus, green), heptanal (cured, rancid), and octanal (citrus, floral), were also detected (Perea-Sanz et al., 2020). These compounds followed a trend similar to hexanal, increasing over time across all groups; however, at 61 S, their concentrations were significantly lower (P < 0.05) in all millet-containing treatments (NI4%, I2%, and I4%) compared to the FC group. This protective effect is likely linked to the antioxidant phenolic compounds present in millet, which act as free radical scavengers and mitigate the accumulation of secondary oxidation products. In terms of flavor development, branched aldehydes formed from amino acids such as leucine and isoleucine, specifically butanal, 2-methyl and butanal, 3-methyl, were identified as primary contributors to the characteristic ripened flavor of the dry-fermented sausages (Shi et al., 2019). The compound butanal, 2-methyl was detected throughout the storage period, with the exception of non-immobilized treatments at 121 S. On the other hand, butanal, 3-methyl was observed in all treatments starting from day 21 R, and no significant differences were identified among the groups during the majority of the study. This indicates that the millet matrix does not interfere with the essential microbial catabolism of amino acids required for traditional aroma formation. A notable exception occurred at 121 S, where the NI4% treatment presented a higher peak area (P < 0.05) compared to the other formulations.

A total of 4 ketones were detected in dry-fermented sausage from diverse sources. 2-heptanone, pentane, and heptane arise from the oxidation of free fatty acids (Narváez-Rivas et al., 2012), while other ketones, such as acetoin, are formed through Maillard reactions (Pérez-Santaescolástica et al., 2018). 2-heptatone remained stable over time, whereas acetoin was only detected at 0 R. A total of seven organic acids were identified in dry-fermented sausages. Acetic acid was the predominant acid, and the other acids showing high contents were butanoic acid, hexanoic acid, and 3-butynoic acid. Acetic acid has a vinegar note and contributes to the ripened aroma, while butanoic acid gives cheese notes (Domínguez et al., 2016). Additionally, other aromatic compounds were detected in low amounts, such as D-limonene, diallyl sulfide, alpha-pinene, and thymol, that could be derived from spices used in the preparation of the sausages, especially with the use of pepper, garlic, and nutmeg. Hydrocarbons are derived from the oxidative breakdown of lipids, possibly catalyzed by heme compounds like hemoglobin and myoglobin (Ruiz et al., 2006). With relatively high odor threshold values, they don’t significantly influence flavor (Lorenzo et al., 2013).

Figure 1 presents the loadings plots after Principal Component Analysis (PCA) for the volatile compounds at the start of ripening (0 R), where the first two principal components explained 90.0% (PC1) and 7.4% (PC2) of the total variability. The FC treatment exhibited a strong positive correlation with both PC1 and PC2, showing a specific association with hexanal, 1-hexanol, and hexane, which are compounds typically derived from lipid oxidation or associated with fatty and rancid notes. In contrast, the treatments containing higher levels of millets (NI4% and I4%) demonstrated a high positive correlation with PC1 but a negative correlation with PC2, being primarily related to acetoin and 2,3-butanediol. Due to its low odor threshold and distinctive buttery scent, acetoin plays a crucial role in shaping the initial aroma profile and can be produced through carbohydrate fermentation by lactic acid bacteria (Hu et al., 2020; Cesselin et al., 2021). At day 21 of ripening (Fig. 2), PC1 accounted for 96.1% and PC2 for 3.4% of the total variability. The FC treatment remained positively correlated with both components, with hexanal identified as the primary volatile compound contributing to the spatial differentiation of the control group at this stage. Conversely, the treatments containing millets (I2%, NI4%, and I4%) were negatively correlated with PC2. Specifically, 1-hexanol was linked to the NI4% and I2% groups, while hexane showed a specific association with the I4% treatment, indicating a more controlled oxidative profile in the immobilized formulations.Regarding the PCA plot at day 61 of storage (Fig. 3), the first two principal components explained 99.2% (PC1) and 0.8% (PC2) of the total variance. This indicates that the differentiation among treatments was almost entirely represented by PC1. Although hexanal was positively correlated with all treatments along PC1, it showed a notably stronger association with the FC, NI4%, and I2% groups than with the I4% formulation. Similarly, a broader range of alcohols and aldehydes, such as 1-hexanol, 1-pentanol, heptanal, and various methyl-butanols, remained more strongly associated with the FC and NI4% treatments, further supporting the protective role of the 4% immobilization matrix during storage. At 121 days of storage (Fig. 4), the PCA results showed that PC1 and PC2 explained 99.0% and 0.5% of the total variance, respectively. At this final stage, two distinct clusters emerged: one formed by FC and NI4%, and another by the immobilized treatments I2% and I4%. While hexanal remained the dominant compound correlated with all treatments along PC1, its association with most other alcohols and aldehydes was reduced, suggesting a stabilization of the volatile profile compared to the 61 S period.

Over time, the evolution of volatile compound profiles in the fermented sausages showed a clear transition from initial divergence to notable convergence and stabilization. Hexanal was consistently detected and showed a positive association with all treatments. However, at the beginning of processing (0 R), 1-hexanol was the predominant compound across treatments, while hexanal was present at lower intensity and more closely associated with FC. As processing progressed (21 R), the concentration of hexanal increased, particularly in the FC treatment. In contrast, treatments containing millets (NI4%, I2%, and I4%) were more closely associated with 1-hexanol, suggesting that the addition of millet contributed to a differentiated aroma profile with reduced hexanal content in the final product. During storage (S 61), hexanal levels intensified in all treatments. Additionally, FC, NI4%, and I2% showed stronger associations with a broader range of alcohols and aldehydes. Finally, at 121 S, the volatile profiles of all treatments converged, indicating the stabilization of aroma-related compounds. Hexanal remained the most prominent compound in all treatments, dominating the overall variance.

In the present study, differences in hexanal levels among treatments can be directly related to the form in which millet and probiotic bacteria were incorporated into the sausage matrix. Treatments containing immobilized Lp. plantarum (I2% and I4%) exhibited a more controlled evolution of hexanal during storage compared to FC and NI4%. Although NI4% contained the same amount of millet as I4%, the probiotic cells were added in free form, suggesting that immobilization, rather than millet inclusion alone, played a central role in modulating lipid oxidation pathways. The evolution of hexanal during shelf life is notable, as studies indicate that its levels tend to increase significantly during storage, especially in control groups that do not receive antioxidant ingredients. Similarly, the addition of byproducts like tomato pomace in fermented sausages has proven effective in reducing hexanal, acting as a natural antioxidant that preserves the product’s oxidative stability (Skwarek et al., 2024).

Although this study focused on instrumental and physicochemical characterization, the delay in reaching the TBARS threshold and the more controlled evolution of hexanal during storage in millet-containing treatments suggest a preservation of sensory quality. Historically, instrumental markers of lipid oxidation and volatile patterns have shown strong correlations with consumer perception of rancidity. However, since sensory evaluation was not performed in the present study, the direct impact of the physicochemical modifications on aroma, flavor, and consumer acceptance could not be established. Further studies incorporating descriptive and hedonic sensory analyses are necessary to determine whether these chemical changes translate into improved sensory quality and consumer preference, as well as to further support the industrial applicability of these probiotic formulations.

Millet proved to be a suitable and technologically feasible carrier for the immobilization of probiotic bacteria in dry-fermented sausages. This strategy preserved probiotic functionality while maintaining the physicochemical stability and the natural evolution of aroma-related compounds throughout ripening and storage time. Variations in volatile composition were primarily driven by processing and storage time rather than formulation differences, supporting the applicability of millet-based immobilization as a promising approach for the development of functional fermented meat products.

Future studies should focus on evaluating the applicability of this approach under industrial conditions, its long-term stability, and its impact on sensory acceptance. In addition, further research is needed to better elucidate the interactions between immobilized probiotics, millet-derived bioactive compounds, and the meat matrix during ripening and storage.
