Thyroid cancer and double-strand DNA break repair: The potential role of the MRN complex pathogenic variants☆
Authors: Sriram Gubbi, Vivek Moorthy, Ashna Grover, Deema Al-Souri, Khaled Bin-Satter, Padmasree Veeraraghavan, Craig Cochran, Joanna Klubo-Gwiezdzinska
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe molecular drivers of thyroid cancer are predominantly involved in the MAP kinase pathway, especially the BRAF and RAS pathogenic variants. The DNA repair pathways are rarely implicated in thyroid tumorigenesis. The MRE11-RAD50-NBS1 (MRN) complex is a key mediator of homologous recombination mode of double-strand break repair, and its role in thyroid cancer is poorly understood. We describe a patient with radioactive iodine-refractory, metastatic oncocytic thyroid carcinoma with an NBN gene (c.2166_2167delGCinsAT) pathogenic variant, who developed progressive disease despite receiving systemic therapy with lenvatinib and pembrolizumab. A gene query analysis for the three MRN complex genes (MRE11, RAD50, and NBN) across three thyroid cancer repositories on the cBioPortal database showed a prevalence of pathogenic variants in one or more MRN complex genes in 35/762 (4.6%) patients, and all the 35 patients had anaplastic thyroid cancer (ATC). Upon filtering the dataset to include only ATCs, the pathogenic variant prevalence increased to 22.2% (35/158 patients). The overall survival in ATC patients with MRN complex gene alterations was similar to ATC patients without the altered MRN complex genes [HR: 1.42 (95% CI: 0.93–2.17)]. A mutual exclusivity analysis demonstrated a tendency for pathogenic variants in one MRN complex genes to co-occur with pathogenic variants in the other two MRN complex genes. In conclusion, the MRN complex pathogenic variants could be potentially oncogenic in TCs and may be linked to aggressive forms of TC.Keywords: Thyroid cancer, oncocytic thyroid cancer, DNA repair, MRE11-RAD50-NBS1 complex, NBN, MRN complex

Introduction

The molecular landscape of differentiated thyroid cancer (DTC) has been extensively characterized over the past two decades. A landmark multiomics integrative analysis classified papillary thyroid cancer (PTC) into two distinct molecular phenotypes: BRAF-like, and RAS-like [1]. The BRAF-like tumors were found to demonstrate papillary architecture on histopathology and with high mitogen-activated pathway-kinase (MAPK) signaling, while the RAS-like tumors demonstrated a follicular architecture but with low MAPK signaling. BRAF-like tumors such as the classic PTC, mainly harbor the common BRAF V600E pathogenic variant or, rarely, RET-PTC fusions, while the RAS-like tumors, which include follicular variant of PTC, follicular adenoma, follicular thyroid cancer (FTC), oncocytic adenoma, and oncocytic carcinoma (OTC), harbor a variety of pathogenic variants, including variants in HRAS, KRAS, NRAS, DICER1, PPAR-fusions, NTRK-fusions, and pathogenic variants along the Akt-mTOR pathway [1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7]. A combination of these pathogenic variants with certain other pathogenic variants such as those of the TERT promoter, TP53, CDKN2A/2B, or global DNA hypomethylation can make the tumors more aggressive and/or dedifferentiated, and develop refractoriness to radioactive iodine treatment [5]. Other molecular drivers of DTC include those related to the cell-cycle (CDKN2A, CDKN2B, PPM1D, and CHEK2), protein translation (EIF1AX), or microRNA processing (DICER1) [1], [5].

OTC generally harbors pathogenic variants that belong to the RAS-like subtype [5]. However, OTC is characterized by certain distinct oncogenic drivers, including aneuploidy (generally, near-haploid genome), mitochondrial DNA mutations involving Complex 1, genes involved in transcriptional regulation and chromatin remodeling (DAXX, and ATRX), and several in-frame fusion genes, such as TMEM233-PRKAB1 and CHCHD10-VPREB3 [8]. Anaplastic thyroid cancer (ATC), being the most aggressive type of thyroid cancer (TC), carries a higher mutation burden and copy number alterations, frequent deletions of tumor suppressor genes, a higher prevalence of gene fusions, and a distinct tumor microenvironment compared to PTCs [9], and transcriptomic analyses suggest that poorly differentiated TCs and ATCs likely arise from DTCs through accrual of these additional mutations [10].

One of the lesser-known pathways implicated in the pathogenesis of TC is DNA repair. There are several modalities of DNA repair. These include base excision repair, nucleotide excision repair, mismatch repair (MMR), translesion synthesis, DNA interstrand crosslink repair, single-strand break repair, and double-strand break repair (DSBR), with each modality of DNA repair performed by a distinct set of genes and proteins [11], [12]. DSBR typically involves two different mechanisms, the non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) and homologous recombination (HRE) [11]. The NHEJ mode of DSBR may act independent of the cell-cycle status, but is more error prone and may increase the risk for chromosomal damage, while the HRE mode of DSBR is active during the S and G2 phases of the cell cycle, is highly error free, and plays a crucial role in maintaining the integrity of proliferating cells [13], [14]. One of the key mediators of HRE mode of DSBR is the MRE11-RAD50-NBS1 complex, or the MRN complex (MRE11: meiotic recombination 11 homolog 1, RAD50: ATP-binding cassette-ATPase, and NBS1: Nijmegen breakage syndrome protein 1 or nibrin), coded by the MRE11, RAD50, and NBN genes, respectively [15].

Defects in DNA repair pathways are implicated in a number of human disorders. Defects in nucleotide excision repair are seen in xeroderma pigmentosum, Cockayne syndrome, and trichothiodystrophy, defects in the ATM gene involved in DSBR results in ataxia telangiectasia, and defects in genes involved in DNA crosslink repair manifests as Fanconi anemia [11], [16], [17]. BRCA1 and BRCA2 are crucial genes involved in DSBR, and their pathogenic variants are seen in hereditary breast and ovarian cancers [13]. MMR gene defects result in microsatellite instability, and are associated with various solid tumors, as well as Lynch syndrome/hereditary nonpolyposis colon cancer (HNPCC) in cases of hereditary defects [18], [19]. The literature on the role of DSBR gene defects in TC is sparse due to the infrequent occurrence of these pathogenic variants in TCs. In this study, we focus on the potential role of MRN complex in TC. We first describe a patient with metastatic, radioactive iodine-refractory OTC with an NBN pathogenic variant as the primary oncogenic driver, and then we perform a gene query analysis of the 3 genes: MRE11, RAD50, and NBN (referred together as ‘MRN complex genes’ for the rest of this article), to provide certain clinical and prognostic insights.

Case presentation

A 53-year-old male was diagnosed with an incidental thyroid nodule on routine physical examination. He had no symptoms suggestive of thyroid dysfunction, dysphagia, airway compromise, or anterior neck discomfort. His family history was unremarkable for TC. He had no prior radiation exposure. Fine-needle aspiration cytology revealed a follicular neoplasm, (Bethesda IV). A total thyroidectomy with central compartment neck dissection was performed. Histopathology demonstrated a large (7 × 4 × 4 cm) OTC, with capsular and lymphovascular invasion (4 or more vessels involved), without extrathyroidal extension, and no lymph node involvement (pT3aN0Mx). He received adjuvant 165 mCi (6.11 GBq) 131I radioactive iodine. The post-therapy whole-body scan showed uptake only in the thyroid bed and central neck. A one-year post-therapy diagnostic whole-body scan was negative, with a serum thyroglobulin of 0.3 ng/ml (range: 0.1–40.9 ng/ml), and negative anti-thyroglobulin antibodies. About three years later, a rising serum thyroglobulin level of 6.5 ng/ml was noted on surveillance laboratory testing, raising suspicion for recurrence. Computed tomography (CT) of the chest revealed multiple new, bilateral pulmonary nodules. A lower lobe lung-wedge biopsy confirmed metastatic OTC (Fig. 1A). Immunohistochemistry (IHC) showed strong nuclear positivity for PAX8, and TTF-1, and some positivity for thyroglobulin, supporting thyroid primary origin, and a 95% positivity for PD-L1 (Figs. 1B-D). Tumor next generation sequencing (NGS), initially performed via Caris Life Sciences, showed a pathogenic variant in the MUTYH (G393D) gene. Germline pathogenic variants in MUTYH are associated with a cancer-predisposition syndrome (MUTYH-associated polyposis), but it only manifests in an autosomal recessive form [20]. The variant allele frequency of this pathogenic variant was 13% in this tumor, therefore, making it unlikely to be a germline pathogenic variant. A repeat NGS was performed via the Oncomine Comprehensive Assay v3, which identified a somatic pathogenic variant in NBN (c.2166_2167delGCinsAT; W722*), with no other pathogenic variants being identified, and was therefore deemed to be the driver pathogenic variant. Given progressive disease, with growing pulmonary and bone metastases involving the sternum, right scapula, and left acetabulum (Fig. 2A), as seen on 18F fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/computed tomography (FDG PET/CT), the patient was subsequently initiated on lenvatinib. He also received palliative external-beam radiation therapy (EBRT) to the sternum (2000 cGy in 5 fractions) and zoledronic acid for cancer-related bone pain, resulting in symptomatic improvement. Two years later, the patient developed hoarseness of voice and dysphagia. Imaging revealed progressive local invasion of the right thyroid cartilage and likely into the recurrent laryngeal nerve (Figs. 2C, D). At that time, metastatic disease elsewhere, including pulmonary and osseous sites, remained radiographically stable, with a serum thyroglobulin of 55.7 ng/ml. Given the risk for high surgical morbidity, re-operation of the tracheal tumor was deferred. Pembrolizumab was administered for 6 cycles as there was evidence of PD-L1 IHC positivity in the tumor, and lenvatinib was continued. Subsequent CT neck and FDG-PET/CT imaging 6 months after starting pembrolizumab demonstrated continued growth of the laryngeal mass, now involving adjacent thyroid cartilage and hyoid bone, with stable disease elsewhere. He received locoregional neck EBRT (1480 cGy in 4 fractions). Eight months after the EBRT, given disease progression, lenvatinib was switched to cabozantinib. The patient experienced nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, fatigue, and lightheadedness, requiring dose reduction of the drug. Most recent FDG-PET/CT imaging (Fig. 2B) performed 10 months after initiation of cabozantinib showed stable, but persistent, metastatic burden in the known regions and a serum thyroglobulin of 252 ng/ml and negative thyroglobulin-Abs. However, a magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the spine done earlier that year about 4 months after initiating cabozantinib) showed vertebral metastatic disease, most pronounced at T3, with a paratracheal soft tissue component extending into the spinal canal and emerging spinal cord compression, and a repeat MRI (Fig. 2E) performed along with the most recent FDG-PET/CT demonstrated slight worsening of cord impingement. Orthopedic intervention was deemed not to be the preferred choice, and therefore, the patient is currently being evaluated for spine-directed EBRT to prevent neurological complications.

Gene query analysis for the pathological variants in the MRN complex in thyroid cancer

A gene query was conducted for the MRE11, RAD50, and the NBN genes on the cBioPortal database (www.cbioportal.org) [21]. The cBioPortal for Cancer Genomics is an open-access, open-source database with clinical and molecular data on numerous human malignancies sourced from public databases and studies published on the respective malignancies and provides an interactive interface for users to compare and contrast variants in different malignancies. The list of genes, malignancies, and the type of variants can be customized to address a specific clinical or research question (https://docs.cbioportal.org/user-guide/faq/). A ‘gene query analysis’ involves extrapolation of details regarding variants (such as types of mutations or copy number alterations, number of patients/samples with mutated gene, survival trends in this population, and involved molecular pathways, among other data) in a specific gene (the MRN complex genes in case of our study) or a subset of genes of interest in a malignancy/a subset of malignancies (TC in case of our study). Our query included all types of TC from 3 different data sources listed on the cBioPortal website: 1. Thyroid Carcinoma’ (TCGA, PanCancer Atlas) (https://www.cell.com/pb-assets/consortium/pancanceratlas/pancani3/index.html). 2. ‘Anaplastic Thyroid Cancers' with data from the Global Anaplastic Thyroid Cancer Initiative (GATCI; Cell Reports 2024) [9], and 3. ‘Poorly-Differentiated and Anaplastic Thyroid Cancers' (MSK, JCI 2016) [10]. For all the analyses, we included only somatic mutations that were confirmed to be ‘drivers' (variants of uncertain significance were excluded) as annotated by OncoKB™ (https://www.oncokb.org/), and Cancer Hotspots (https://www.cancerhotspots.org/#/home), and only those tumors that were ‘profiled’ (sequenced) for these genes (Supplementary Fig. 1). The output data included: 1. An ‘oncoprint’, which is a pictorial depiction of the number of pathogenic variants per gene and the type of TC in the form of a color-coded bar per patient and the pathogenic variant in separate rows. The ‘oncoprint’ was generated in two separate sets, one with just the 3 MRN complex genes (Fig. 3A and B), and another with the 3 MRN complex genes along with 6 common thyroid cancer-related genes: BRAF, HRAS, KRAS, NRAS, TERT, and TP53 (Fig. 3C), 2. Kaplan-Meier curves for overall survival (OS) with hazard ratio (HR) and 95% confidence interval (95% CI) reports that were derived from a log-rank test, as well as median survival with 95% CI (Fig. 3D), and 3. A ‘mutual exclusivity analysis', which involved performing a pairwise comparison between the various pathogenic variants, with its results reported as log2 odds ratio (L2OR), and with a significant p-value set at <0.05 (Table 1). No separate statistical software was utilized, as all the statistical analyses were performed by the cBioPortal database. Only one sub-set of survival analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism Version 10.2.2 (Supplementary Fig. 2). Details regarding the individual studies are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Following the exclusion of 78 variants and copy number alterations of unknown significance and 12 patients whose tumors were not profiled for the 3 MRN complex genes (Supplementary Material Fig. 1), the resulting output was from 762 patients (794 samples), and one or more genes among the 3 MRN complex genes harbored pathogenic variants in 35/762 (4.6%) patients and in 38/794 (4.8%) samples (Fig. 3A). On individual gene level, MRE11 was altered in 25/762 (3.3%), RAD50 was altered in 16/762 (2.1%), and NBN was altered in 9/762 (1.2%) patients (Fig. 3A). All the pathogenic variants were copy number alterations and were identified as deep deletions (defined as a ‘deep’ loss/possible homozygous deletion per the cBioPortal website). All of these variants were exclusively noted in the ATC cohort from the GATCI 2024 study [9]. Therefore, the data was then filtered to exclude the other two studies and retain only the GATCI study, and the gene query analysis was repeated for the 3 MRN complex genes. Following exclusion of 73 variants and copy number alterations of unknown significance, a total of 158 patients (190 tumor samples) were selected (Supplementary Fig. 1), and 35 (22.2%) of patients and 38 (20%) tumor samples harbored MRN complex gene pathogenic variants. A total of 25/158 (15.8%) patients harbored MRE11, 16/158 (10.1%) patients harbored RAD50, and 9/158 (5.7%) patients harbored NBN pathogenic variants, with all the pathogenic variants again being deep deletions (Fig. 3B). As our patient had OTC, we looked further into the histopathological features of the ATC in all 35 patients with the MRN complex pathogenic variants. Interestingly, 2 out of these 35 patients had ATCs with OTC components on histopathology, and both patients had pathogenic variants in MRE11, and NBN (Supplementary Table 2). We also looked into patients with OTC or with OTC components inside a different subtype of thyroid cancer in the other two studies (TCGA PanCancer Atlas, and MSK, JCI 2016), and none of these patients harbored pathogenic variants in the 3 MRN complex genes (Supplementary Table 2).

The gene query analysis was repeated by including the 6 common thyroid cancer-related genes: BRAF, HRAS, KRAS, NRAS, TERT, and TP53, along with the 3 MRN complex genes on this ATC cohort (158 patients/190 samples) (Fig. 3C). Pathogenic variants in TP53 (65/158 patients, 41%) were most frequent, followed by BRAF (55/158 patients, 35%) and TERT (37/158 patients, 23%). The frequency of MRE11 pathogenic variants (25/158 patients, 16%) was similar to the frequency of KRAS pathogenic variants (25/158 patients, 16%), while more frequent than HRAS (22/158 patients, 14%) or NRAS (18/158 patients, 11%) pathogenic variants.

A mutual exclusivity analysis was performed comparing the 3 MRN complex genes with the 6 TC-related genes: BRAF, HRAS, KRAS, NRAS, TERT, and TP53, in patients from the GATCI study (Table 1). Pathogenic variants in any of these genes were found in 113 (72%) patients and 135 (71%) samples, and 159 variants and copy number alterations of unknown significance and samples which were not profiled for any of these genes were excluded (Supplementary Fig. 1). The highest magnitude of odds for co-occurrence was observed between MRE11 and NBN (L2OR: >3; p < 0.001, q < 0.001), followed by MRE11 and RAD50 (L2OR: 2.582; p = 0.003, q = 0.013). The rest of the associations were not significant for either co-occurrence or mutual exclusivity.

As all the tumors with MRN complex gene pathogenic variants were ATCs from the “GATCI, Cell Reports 2024” dataset, a survival analysis was conducted within this cohort by comparing the overall survival in ATC patients with MRN complex alterations with ATC patients without MRN complex alterations. There was no difference in the overall survival between the two groups [Median survival: 3.32 (95% CI: 2.46–6.93) months in pathogenic variant positive group versus 6.67 (95% CI: 4.47–10.58) months in the pathogenic variant negative group; HR: 1.42 (95% CI: 0.93–2.17)] (Fig. 3D). However, these alterations included non-driver variants. Therefore, we performed survival analysis on the raw data after filtering ATC patients with only driver changes in the MRN complex and comparing this group with the group without these variants, and the overall survival was not significantly different [HR: 1.29 (95% CI: 0.8–2.1)] (Supplementary Fig. 2). We then identified ATC patients from the MSK, JCI 2016 study and combined them with the GATCI cohort and then performed the survival analysis on the raw data. The overall survival between the two groups again was not significantly different [HR: 1.33 (95% CI: 0.82–2.1)] (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Discussion

We describe a patient with metastatic radioactive iodine-refractory OTC driven by a pathogenic variant in the NBN gene, a member of the MRN complex. Through a gene query analysis on a public database (cBioPortal), we demonstrate that pathogenic variants in the MRN complex are exclusively found in ATCs, and they are all copy number alterations (deep deletions). Through mutual exclusivity analysis, we then show that one or more of these MRN complex genes are likely to be concomitantly altered.

The thyroid gland harbors an environment with oxidative stress due to the generation of H2O2, thereby prone to DNA damage in the form of base oxidation, DNA strand breaks, abasic sites, gene rearrangements and fusions [22], [23]. Double-strand break (DSB) is the most severe form of DNA damage, often resulting from ionizing radiation, reactive oxygen species, cytotoxic agents, or DNA replication errors such as abortive topoisomerase activity and replication fork collapse [24], [25]. DSB also occurs under more physiological contexts, such as the V(D)J recombination in the immunoglobulin genes, T-cell receptor assembly within the lymphocytes, meiotic division, and telomere length maintenance [17]. Even low levels of DSB can trigger apoptosis, and therefore, DSBR is a crucial cellular process [26]. However, erroneous rejoining of DSB sites can give rise to mutations such as loss or amplification or translocation of gene segments [26]. In fact, DSB is a common form of DNA damage observed in ionizing radiation-induced TC, as noted in patients exposed to the Chernobyl nuclear fallout [27]. The exact function of the MRN complex is unclear, however, given its exonuclease and helicase activities, it may function as the initial sensor of DSB thereby activating the downstream components of the HRE mode of the DSBR pathway [26]. NBS1 in particular is activated through phosphorylation via the catalytically active ataxia-telangiectasia mutated (ATM) protein that is in turn activated by ionizing radiation-induced DSB, thereby facilitating molecular cascades leading to G1/S phase arrest in the cell cycle (Fig. 4) [28], [29], [30], [31], [32], [33], [34], [35]. Moreover, this MRN complex is involved in the mediation of the kinase activity of ATM towards its substrates such as p53, Chk2, and H2AX [36].

Mechanism of MRE11-RAD50-NBS1 (MRN) complex-mediated double-strand break repair (DSBR) through homologous recombination (HR). The MRN complex is a hetero-hexamer made up of dimers of each component (M2R2N2). First responders in DNA damage response, such as γH2AX and RAD17, recruit NBS1, a scaffolding protein, which aids in the nuclear translocation of MRE11-RAD50. RAD50 has ABC-ATPase domains which hydrolyze ATP, “opening” the configuration of its dimeric coils (containing a zinc hook in the loop). This allows DNA access to MRE11, generating 3′ single-stranded DNA overhangs by its endonuclease activity, necessary for HR, followed by its MRE11 exonuclease and so forth in course of HR. Interaction with the carboxy-terminal binding protein 1 interacting protein (CtIP), which associates with the N-terminus of NBS1 is needed for MRE11 functioning. In addition, the N-terminal region of the NBS1 protein contains a fork-head associated (FHA) domain, and two breast cancer associated 1C-terminus (BRCT) domains, among others. On the other hand, the C-terminal region is notable for the intrinsically disordered region, MRE11, and ATM-binding domains. NBS1 recruits the inactive ATM to its binding domain, and this binding activates ATM. The activated ATM then phosphorylates several downstream checkpoint proteins, including p53, H2AX, BRCA1, CHK2, and others, which are crucial to pause cell cycle progression and allow repair. ATM also phosphorylates NBS1, which in turn is required for the activation of the MRN complex. The mutation at amino acid residue 722 detected in the described patient falls between MRE11 and ATM-binding domains, and as the mutation is truncating, the ATM-binding domain is likely deleted, thereby preventing NBS1-ATM binding and ATM activation. Germline defects in the NBN gene results in Nijmegen breakage syndrome (NBS), an autosomal recessive disorder presenting with microcephaly, growth retardation with late catch up of growth, intellectual disability, distinctive facies, immune system hypofunction, and increased risk for certain malignancies, and premature ovarian insufficiency in women [37], [38]. However, TC is not conventionally associated with this condition. Germline MRE11 pathogenic variants results in ataxia telangiectasia-like disorder, characterized by increased sensitivity to ionizing radiation, immunodeficiency, and neuronal degeneration, while germline RAD50 pathogenic variants give rise to NBS-like disorder, with increased risk for malignancies [35]. Somatic pathogenic variants in the MRN complex have been identified in ovarian, endometroid, breast, nasopharyngeal, liver, and bladder cancers, lung small cell and adenocarcinomas, lymphoma, medulloblastoma, leukemia, cholangiocarcinoma, and others [35]. We could not find TC described in the literature with pathogenic variants in the MRN complex. A recent study evaluated cytotoxic agent-induced DSB repair capacity through flow cytometry in the lymphocytes of patients with papillary thyroid microcarcinoma and benign thyroid nodules. The DNA repair capacity was lower in the microcarcinoma group and on multivariable regression analysis, radiation exposure significantly correlated with reduced DNA repair capacity. NGS of a set of DNA repair genes revealed variants in RAD50 in four microcarcinoma patients (MRE11 and NBN were not sequenced), but pathogenicity of these variants was not analyzed [39].

The NBS1 protein in humans is comprised of 754 amino acids, and the NBN gene contains 16 exons [40]. The specific NBN pathogenic variant found in our patient (W722*) results in a protein truncated towards the C-terminal. While protein modeling predictions have mapped this amino acid position with high confidence (70% - 90% score in AlphaFold; https://alphafold.ebi.ac.uk/entry/AF-O60934-F1) within an alpha-helix motif, the exact function of this domain is not clear, and neither are there reports on mutations at this position per our knowledge. However, the C-terminal region beyond this amino acid residue is crucial for interaction with ATM as well as Skp2-mediated ubiquitination of NBS1 that is required for the NBS1-ATM interaction. Therefore, loss of this portion could lead to suboptimal functioning of ATM and unchecked G1/S phase transition, impaired apoptosis, and DNA damage accumulation [41], [42].

One of the better studied DNA repair pathways in TC is the MMR pathway. A study evaluating the expression profiles of the MMR genes (MSH2, MSH6, MLH1, PMS1, and PMS2) in various forms of thyroid tumors, including hyperplastic nodules, follicular adenomas, PTCs, and FTCs, identified higher expression of these genes in the malignant tumors compared to the benign tumors [43]. Moreover, point mutation analysis of the MLH1 and MSH2 genes on a subset of these tumors (benign and malignant) did not reveal any mutations. This higher expression of MMR genes in malignant tumors may suggest a compensatory upregulation to alleviate the DNA replication errors encountered in a rapidly proliferating neoplasm. Our group investigated the potential association between familial non-medullary thyroid cancer (FNTMC) and HNPCC/Lynch syndrome, a well-known condition with suboptimal MMR function [44]. One FNMTC family was identified satisfying the HNPCC diagnostic criteria, and 8 family members presented with HNPCC-associated tumors. A heterozygous MSH2 missense variant (rs373226409; C707Y) was identified in this family in 3 affected adults, 2 unaffected children of an affected adult, and was absent in unaffected adults. While the in silico analysis predicted pathogenicity, IHC staining of the thyroid tumors from 2 affected family members revealed intact MSH2 nuclear expression, and a stable MSI status. Another study evaluated a set of 94 genes in 48 probands from FNMTC families through targeted NGS, in silico analysis, structural modeling, and network analysis [45]. Likely pathogenic variants were identified in 15 FNMTC families, several of which were in genes of DNA repair, but none in the MRN complex. Structural modeling demonstrated a disruption of residue interaction, and functional network analyses demonstrated functional interaction among the different DNA repair genes, and across different pathways, with MAP kinase activation being the common event in tumor progression. These results suggested a putative role of germline pathogenic variants in the DNA repair genes in FNMTC.

Molecular testing for pathogenic variants in DTC is not routinely undertaken in all patients due to the low cost-benefit ratio, as the majority patients either demonstrate excellent response following surgery ± radioactive iodine, or demonstrate indeterminate/biochemical incomplete response, which can be managed through thyrotropin (TSH) suppression and through serial biochemical and imaging surveillance [5]. In fact, the ATA 2025 guidelines suggest that pre-operative genomic evaluation is not routinely recommended, and if genomic evaluation is undertaken, this data should be implemented under the context of clinical, histological, and radiological data to determine the extent of surgery. In clinical practice, the management of TC is predominantly driven by biochemical (serum thyroglobulin and thyrotropin), and imaging modalities, and molecular testing is only reserved for more aggressive, or radioactive iodine-refractory, metastatic TCs. Therefore, the true prevalence of DNA repair defects in TC is unknown, but by deducing from the known molecular landscape of TC, DNA repair defects seem to be uncommon drivers of thyroid oncogenesis.

The treatment of TCs driven by pathogenic variants in DNA repair genes is challenging as there are no targets directly amendable to pharmacotherapy. Multikinase inhibitors remain the initial option for systemic therapy in these patients [5]. In cases of high PD-L1 expression or high tumor mutational burden, immune checkpoint inhibitors are a feasible option [46]. The conventional cytotoxic DNA alkylating agents or DNA replication inhibitors (cisplatin, doxorubicin, etoposide, and others) ± immune checkpoint inhibitors may have therapeutic potential. Small molecule inhibitors of alternative DNA repair mechanisms (PARP inhibitors, ATR inhibitors, CHK1/CHK2 inhibitors, POLθ inhibitors, and DNA-PKcs inhibitors) by utilizing the ‘synthetic lethal’ effect on the neoplastic cells may hold potential in treating TC that progresses on standard-of-care therapy [47]. Meanwhile several new targets in the DNA-repair and related pathways are being actively investigated, along with efforts to repurpose clinically-approved drugs used for other indications [48]. There are no active clinical trials evaluating these drugs for TC at this time as per our knowledge but given the rarity of DNA repair defects in TC, these patients may have to be enrolled in basket trials or trials for unspecified solid tumors targeting DNA repair defects.

The strength of our study includes evaluation of pathogenic variant landscapes from a large, well-established public repository of TC generated using data from elegant studies performed on large patient populations with extensive molecular data, including point mutations, copy number variations, indels and gene fusions. However, there are limitations to this study. While our gene query analysis revealed certain intriguing data, the results must be interpreted with caution as they were directly extrapolated from the analysis performed by the cBioPortal database and these analyses were not performed first-hand on the raw data. Therefore, any additional adjustments for confounding factors could not be undertaken. The frequency differences among the various pathogenic variants that were evaluated may be subject to variations due to annotation differences or changes/upgrades in the bioinformatic workflows. Therefore, certain % differences of these variants (for example, MRE11 versus the RAS variants as in Fig. 3C) may be over- or underestimated. The studies utilize different human genome coordinate systems (GRCh38 in GATCI, and GRCh37 in the other 2 studies), but this does not preclude or hinder comparisons of pathogenic variant frequencies. Pathogenic variants in other molecular pathways may have contributed to clinical phenotypes but such pathway interactions were not evaluated in this study.

In conclusion, somatic MRN complex pathogenic variants are rarely associated with TC but are characterized by a relatively higher prevalence in ATCs, especially copy number alterations/deep deletions. Due to the rarity of TCs harboring pathogenic variants in the DSBR pathway, or DNA repair in general, their tumor biology and clinical attributes are poorly understood. Further studies are necessary to understand the role of MRN complex/DSBR defects in TC, and their effects on tumor histology, immune microenvironment, clinical behavior, interaction with other oncogenic driver pathways, and their response to therapies.
