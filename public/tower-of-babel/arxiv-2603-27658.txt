Entropy Production Rate in Stochastically Time-evolving Asymmetric Networks Preprint: APS/123-QED
Tuan Pham, Deepak Gupta

Abstract
Fluctuations in parameters that are typically treated as fixed play a crucial role in the behavior of complex systems. However, to date, we lack a general non-equilibrium thermodynamic treatment of such a complex system. In this Letter, to address this problem, we develop a framework in which fluctuating interactions between units of nonlinear network systems are modeled as uncorrelated colored noise (i.e., annealed disorder) with a correlation time. This approach enables us to quantify how the entropy production rate (EPR) depends on both the characteristic time-scale and the strength of the disorder. Using dynamical mean field theory we derive an exact expression for EPR at any transient time that is validated by simulations of the full non-linear dynamics. At stationarity, a relation between EPR and autocorrelation is established and then used to analytically study the particular case of linear systems.

SII
Derivation of Entropy production rate (5)

In this section, we compute the entropy production for the original dynamics (
1
) and compare it with that of the effective dynamics (
3
). In the original dynamics given by Eq. (
1
),
\zeta_{i}(t)
is the Gaussian thermal white noise coming from the environment. The strength of this noise
\sigma^{2}

is related to the environment’s temperature
T
by the Einstein fluctuation-dissipation relation as
\sigma^{2}=2k_{B}T
, where
k_{B}
is the Boltzmann’s constant. (Notice that we have set the dissipation constant
\gamma=1
.)
Since the elements

in Eq. (
1

coupled through a fully-connected network
\mathbf{J}
of

interactions, i.e.,
J_{ij}\neq J_{ji}
for every pair
(i,j)
, the system breaks down the detailed balance condition.
Therefore, the system constantly produces entropy, even in non-equilibrium steady states (NESS).

Following the decomposition of the total entropy production rate along a single stochastic trajectory
Seifert 2012a
, we have

\displaystyle\dot{S}_{\rm tot}=\dot{S}_{\rm res}+\dot{S}_{\rm sys}\ ,

(S78)

where
\dot{S}_{\rm res}(t)
is the rate at which entropy is dissipated into the environment and
\dot{S}_{\rm sys}(t)
is the change in the system’s entropy, respectively, along a single stochastic trajectory, and these are given by

\displaystyle\dot{S}_{\rm res}

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\frac{2}{\sigma^{2}}\sum_{i}\bigg(F\big[\sum_{j}J_{i,j}(t)x_{j}(t)\big]-x_{i}(t)\bigg)\circ\frac{{\rm d}x_{i}}{{\rm d}t}\ ,

(S79)

\displaystyle\dot{S}_{\rm sys}

\displaystyle=\displaystyle-k_{B}\sum_{i}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\ln p(\mathbf{x},t)\right)\circ\frac{{\rm d}x_{i}}{{\rm d}t}-k_{B}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\ln p(\mathbf{x},t)\ .

(S80)

Here “
\circ
” denotes the Stratonovich convention, and
p(\bm{x},t)
is the joint probability distribution of
\bm{x}
at time
t
.
In the non-equilibrium steady state, the average rate of system entropy production vanishes, i.e.,
\langle\dot{S}_{\rm sys}^{\rm(ss)}\rangle=0
, and hence the average rate of total entropy production becomes equal to the average rate of environmental entropy production, i.e.,

\big\langle\dot{S}_{\rm tot}^{\rm(ss)}\big\rangle=\big\langle\dot{S}_{\rm res}^{\rm(ss)}\big\rangle
, where the
\langle\cdot\rangle
represent the average taken over noise realizations.

Since the original dynamics in Eq. (
1
) can be represented by a one-dimensional effective process (
S44
)

S79
) can be shown to be equivalent to that of
the effective process (
S44
). Specifically, denoting
\dot{s}_{\rm res}(t):=\dot{S}_{\rm res}(t)/N

1
), we will prove that

\langle\dot{s}_{\rm res}(t)\rangle=\langle\dot{s}_{\rm res}(t)\rangle^{\rm(MF)}\ ,

(S81)

where the right-hand side is the average rate of environmental entropy production of the mean field (effective) dynamics (
S44
). Notice that the angular brackets on the right-hand side indicate the average with respect to the measure associated with the effective dynamics (
S44
). Furthermore, similar to the rate of environmental entropy production (
S79
) for the original dynamics, one can show that the stochastic rate of environmental entropy production of the effective dynamics is

M

in the stationary state for the linear system. In general, for a linear system, the DMFT equation (

\displaystyle\dot{x}(t)=-x(t)+\mu\langle x\rangle(t)+g\eta(t)+\zeta(t)\ ,

(S126)

\displaystyle\dot{M}(t)=-(1-\mu){M}(t)\ .

\displaystyle M(t)=M_{0}e^{-(1-\mu)t}\ .

\partial_{\tau}^{2}\bar{C}_{x}(\tau)=\big[1-g^{2}q_{\tau_{0}}(|\tau|)\big]\bar{C}_{x}(\tau)-\sigma^{2}\delta(\tau)\ ,

(S129)

SVIII.1
Quenched limit:
\tau_{0}\to\infty

It is instructive to check whether we can recover the known result for the quenched disorder such as
\tau_{0}\rightarrow\infty
, for which we have
q_{\tau_{0}}(|\tau|)=1
. Then, from Eq. (
S129
), we have

\partial_{\tau}^{2}\bar{C}_{x}(\tau)=(1-g^{2})\bar{C}_{x}(\tau)-\sigma^{2}\delta(\tau)\ ,

(S130)

with the initial condition
\dot{C}_{x}(\tau\rightarrow 0^{+})=-\sigma^{2}/2
. We Fourier transform the above equation (
S130
), and this gives

\displaystyle\hat{\bar{C}}_{x}(\omega)=\dfrac{\sigma^{2}}{\omega^{2}-(1-g^{2})}\ .

(S131)

The inverse Fourier transform of Eq. (
S131
) depends on whether
g^{2}>1
or
g^{2}<1
. Thus, we get

\displaystyle\bar{C}(\tau)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{\sigma^{2}}{2}~\dfrac{1}{\displaystyle\sqrt{1-g^{2}}}~\exp\left\{-|\tau|\sqrt{1-g^{2}}\right\}&\qquad g^{2}<1\\
\dfrac{\sigma^{2}}{2}~\dfrac{1}{\displaystyle\sqrt{g^{2}-1}}~{\rm sin}\left\{|\tau|\sqrt{g^{2}-1}\right\}&\qquad g^{2}>1\end{cases}\ .

(S132)

We can simply check that both of these solutions satisfy
\dot{C}_{x}(\tau\rightarrow 0^{+})=-\sigma^{2}/2
.
However, for case
g^{2}>1
, the correlation function is oscillatory, which is not a physical solution of Eq. (
S130
) as this equation is valid for the stationary state.
Then, substituting the expression of
\bar{C}(\tau)
into Eq. (
9
) for
g^{2}<1
, we arrive at the following expression for the entropy production rate:

\displaystyle\langle\dot{s}_{\rm res}(t)\rangle^{\rm(MF)}_{\rm NESS}=1-\sqrt{1-g^{2}}\ ,

(S133)

which agrees with the asymptotic analysis in the main text for the entropy production rate for the linear model.

SVIII.2
\tau_{0}\to 0
limit

In the limit
\tau_{0}\to 0
, we have
q_{\tau_{0}}(|\tau|)=\delta(\tau)
. From Eq. (
S129

\partial_{\tau}^{2}\bar{C}_{x}(\tau)=\big[1-g^{2}\delta(\tau)\big]\bar{C}_{x}(\tau)-\sigma^{2}\delta(\tau)\ ,

(S134)

The Fourier transform of above equation (
S134

\displaystyle\hat{\bar{C}}_{x}(\omega)=\dfrac{g^{2}\bar{C}_{x}(0)+\sigma^{2}}{1+\omega^{2}}\ ,

(S135)

and then inverting the Fourier transform, we obtain

\displaystyle\bar{C}_{x}(\tau)=[g^{2}\bar{C}_{x}(0)+\sigma^{2}]\dfrac{e^{-|\tau|}}{2}\ .

(S136)

Substituting
\tau=0
on both sides, we obtain the variance as follows

Q\equiv\bar{C}_{x}(0)=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2-g^{2}}\ .

(S137)

The above expression (
S137
) is in agreement with that observed in
Ferraro et al. 2025
, where the latter shows that the variance vanishes in the stationary state in the absence of external thermal noise. Furthermore, the positivity of
\bar{C}_{x}(0)
implies that
g^{2}<2
.
so that the DMFT
x

\displaystyle\bar{C}_{x}(\tau)=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2-g^{2}}e^{-|\tau|}\ ,

(S138)

while for

depends only on

Then, given the formula of the EPR (
9

\displaystyle\langle\dot{s}_{\rm res}(t)\rangle^{\rm(MF)}_{\rm NESS}=1-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\left(2-g^{2}\right)T}=\dfrac{g^{2}}{g^{2}-2}

(S139)

where we used
\sigma^{2}=2T

S95

\langle\dot{s}_{\rm res}\rangle^{\rm(MF)}=\beta\Big[\big\langle x^{2}(t)\big\rangle+\big\langle y(t)^{2}\big\rangle-2\big\langle x(t)\circ y(t)\big\rangle+\big\langle\zeta(t)\circ y(t)\big\rangle-\big\langle\zeta(t)\circ x(t)\big\rangle\Big]\ ,

(S140)

To evaluate the right-hand side of the above equation (
S140

\tau_{0}\rightarrow 0
and
\tau\rightarrow 0^{+}
. To this end, we remind ourself that

is actually our OU noise that represents the original term
\sum_{j}J_{ij}(t)x_{j}
in Eq. (
1

S140

and similarly,

y_{\tau_{0}}(t)

S140

\left\{\begin{aligned} \displaystyle\dot{x}_{\tau_{0}}&=-x_{\tau_{0}}(t)+y_{\tau_{0}}(t)+\zeta(t)\\
\displaystyle\dot{y}_{\tau_{0}}&=-\frac{y_{\tau_{0}}}{\tau_{0}}+\frac{1}{\tau_{0}}~g\sqrt{Q}\xi(t)\end{aligned}\right.\ ,

(S141)

where we have removed the time-dependence of
Q(t)

In this formulation, the NESS restriction of Eq. (

We have straightforwardly that

Since

From the regularized stochastic differential equations (

SIX
Comment on the difference between two dynamics

As discussed in
Nicoletti and Busiello 2026
, there are two distinct dynamics, both are commonly used in the neural network literature, though they correspond to different information-processing schemes: nonlinear integration and nonlinear summation. Our choice in Eq. (
1
) is the former:

\frac{{\rm d}x_{i}}{{\rm d}t}=-x_{i}(t)+F\left(\sum_{j\neq i}J_{ij}(t)x_{j}(t)\right)+\zeta_{i}(t)\,,\qquad\langle\zeta_{i}(t)\zeta_{j}(t^{\prime})\rangle=\sigma^{2}\delta_{ij}\delta(t-t^{\prime})\ .

(S149)

The latter is given by the following form

\frac{{\rm d}x_{i}}{{\rm d}t}=-x_{i}(t)+\sum_{j\neq i}J_{ij}(t)F\big(x_{j}(t)\big)+\zeta_{i}(t)\ .

(S150)

i.e., here, the signals generated by each node are first nonlinearly transformed, and then linearly projected by means of the interaction matrix
\mathbf{J}(t)
.

Before going to demonstrate the difference between the two processes Eq. (
S149
) and Eq. (
S150
) due to time-dependent
\mathbf{J}(t)
, let’s us recapitulate the exact equivalence between the two forms of non-linearity (see Appendix A in
Martorell et al. 2024
) for the
quenched limit
of
q_{\tau_{0}}\rightarrow 1
, i.e., time-independent
\mathbf{J}
once the noise strength is rescaled. Specifically, for
\langle\zeta_{i}(t)\zeta_{j}(t^{\prime})\rangle=\sigma^{2}\delta_{ij}\delta(t-t^{\prime})
, and as long as interactions
are uncorrelated and zero-averaged, the two below dynamics are equivalent:

\frac{{\rm d}x_{i}}{{\rm d}t}=-x_{i}(t)+F\left(\sum_{j\neq i}J_{ij}x_{j}(t)\right)+\zeta_{i}(t)\,,\qquad\Leftrightarrow\qquad\frac{{\rm d}x_{i}}{{\rm d}t}=-x_{i}(t)+\sum_{j\neq i}J_{ij}F\big(x_{j}(t)\big)+g^{2}\zeta_{i}(t)\ .

(S151)

However, these two dynamical processes become different in our annealed case. Let us demonstrate this point.
We introduce
y_{\ell}=\sum_{i}J_{\ell i}x_{i}
which satisfy the following equation:

\dot{y}_{\ell}(t)=\sum_{i}[\dot{J}_{\ell i}(t)x_{i}(t)+J_{\ell i}(t)\dot{x}_{i}(t)]\ .

(S152)

Notice that

\displaystyle\dot{J}_{\ell,i}(t)=\dfrac{\Sigma}{\sqrt{N}}\dot{Z}_{\ell,i}(t)=\dfrac{\Sigma}{\sqrt{N}}\bigg[-\dfrac{Z_{\ell,i}}{\tau_{0}}+\sqrt{\dfrac{1+2\tau_{0}}{\tau_{0}^{2}}}\xi_{\ell,i}(t)\bigg]\ .

(S153)

Multiplying both sides
x_{i}
and summing over index
\ell
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i}x_{i}\dot{J}_{\ell,i}(t)=\dfrac{\Sigma}{\sqrt{N}}\bigg[-\dfrac{1}{\tau_{0}}\sum_{i}x_{i}Z_{\ell,i}+\sqrt{\dfrac{1+2\tau_{0}}{\tau_{0}^{2}}}\sum_{i}x_{i}\xi_{\ell,i}(t)\bigg]\ .

(S154)

Similarly, we have

\sum_{i}J_{\ell,i}(t)\frac{{\rm d}x_{i}}{{\rm d}t}=-\sum_{i}J_{\ell,i}(t)x_{i}(t)+\sum_{i}J_{\ell,i}(t)F(y_{i})+\sum_{i}J_{\ell,i}(t)\zeta_{i}(t)\ .

(S155)

Adding the above two equations, we get

\displaystyle\dot{y}_{\ell}(t)=-y_{\ell}(t)+\sum_{i}J_{\ell,i}(t)F(y_{i})+\nu^{(1)}_{\ell}(t)+\dfrac{g}{\sqrt{N}}\bigg[-\dfrac{1}{\tau_{0}}\nu^{(2)}_{\ell}(t)+\sqrt{\dfrac{1+2\tau_{0}}{\tau_{0}^{2}}}\nu^{(3)}_{\ell}(t)\bigg]\ ,

(S156)

where we defined for convenience

\displaystyle\nu^{(1)}_{\ell}(t)

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{i}J_{\ell,i}(t)\zeta_{i}(t)\ ,

(S157a)

\displaystyle\nu^{(2)}_{\ell}(t)

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{i}x_{i}(t)Z_{\ell,i}(t)\ ,

(S157b)

\displaystyle\nu^{(3)}_{\ell}(t)

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{i}x_{i}(t)\xi_{\ell,i}(t)\ .

(S157c)

Just a remark that, by its definition, it is straightforward to show that
\nu^{(1)}_{\ell}(t)
has delta-correlated correlations:

\displaystyle\langle\nu^{(1)}_{\ell}(t)\nu^{(1)}_{\ell^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\langle J_{\ell i}J_{\ell^{\prime}j}\rangle_{\xi}\langle\zeta_{i}(t)\zeta_{j}(t^{\prime})\rangle_{\zeta}

(S158)

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}q_{\tau_{0}}(|t-t^{\prime}|)\frac{\Sigma^{2}}{N}~\sum_{i,j}\delta_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}\delta_{ij}\times[\delta_{ij}\delta(t-t^{\prime})]

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}q_{\tau_{0}}(0)g^{2}~\delta_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}\delta(t-t^{\prime})\ ,

The correlations
\big\langle\nu_{\ell}^{(\alpha)}\nu_{\ell^{\prime}}^{(\beta)}\big\rangle
, with
\alpha,\beta\in\{2,3\}
, of the other terms
\nu^{(2)}_{\ell}(t)
and
\nu^{(3)}_{\ell}(t)
involve four-point correlation functions, such as
\langle x_{i}(t)Z_{\ell,i}(t)x_{i}(t^{\prime})Z_{\ell,i}(t^{\prime})\rangle
; it does not illuminate how to factorize such a correlation function. Therefore,
\nu^{(2)}_{\ell}(t)
and
\nu^{(3)}_{\ell}(t)
generally are not delta-correlated.

Method of Numerical simulations

We solve the DMFT equation:

\displaystyle\dot{x}(t)=-x(t)+F[\mu M(t)+g\eta(t)]+\zeta(t)\ ,

(S159)

where we define
M(t)\equiv\langle x(t)\rangle^{\rm(MF)}
and the noise correlation is
\langle\eta(t)\eta(t^{\prime})\rangle=q_{\tau_{0}}(|t-t^{\prime}|)\langle x(t)x(t^{\prime})\rangle^{\rm(MF)}\equiv~q_{\tau_{0}}(|t-t^{\prime}|)C_{x}(t,t^{\prime})
.
In the following, we discuss the method for
q_{\tau_{0}}=1
(applicable for large
\tau_{0}
). However, the following calculations can be extended for
q_{\tau_{0}}\neq 1
.

SX.1
Noise
\eta(t)
generation

To solve the DMFT equation (
), we have to generate Gaussian noise that has the following correlation

\displaystyle\langle\eta(t)\eta(t^{\prime})\rangle=C_{x}(t,t^{\prime})\ ,

(S161)

where the correlation
C_{x}(t,t^{\prime})
itself is calculated using the trajectories of
x(t)
(

To obtain the noise trajectory
\eta(t)
, we start by guessing
C_{x}(t,t^{\prime})
, then we can compute
\eta(t)
for

, i.e. the correlated
\eta(t)
over all time.

We start by considering

\displaystyle\eta=LZ\ ,

(S162)

where
Z
is a column vector of normal random variables with zero mean and unit variance. Then, we have

\displaystyle\langle\eta\eta^{\top}\rangle=L\langle ZZ^{\top}\rangle L^{\top}=LL^{\top}\ .

(S163)

The left hand side is recognized as the correlation matrix
C_{x}=\langle xx^{\top}\rangle
correlation matrix. This implies:

\displaystyle C_{x}=LL^{\top}\ .

(S164)

Therefore, we find
L=C_{x}^{1/2}
, and the correlation matrix
C_{x}^{1/2}

\displaystyle L=SD^{1/2}S^{\top}\ ,

(S165)

where
D
is the diagonalize matrix of
C_{x}
and
S
is the similarity/orthogonal matrix. All of this gives the vector
\eta

\displaystyle\eta=SD^{1/2}S^{\top}Z\ .

(S166)

Since
S
is an orthonormal matrix and the distribution of
S^{\top}Z
is also a normal distribution with zero mean zero and unit variance. This can be seen mathematically as

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle\equiv S^{\top}Z\ ,

(S167)

\displaystyle\langle PP^{\top}\rangle

\displaystyle=S^{\top}\langle ZZ^{\top}\rangle S=S^{\top}S=I\ ,

(S168)

which means average over correlation of
P
or
Z
gives

as in the case of normal distribution. Therefore, for a given

we can compute the noise vector

\displaystyle\eta=SD^{1/2}Z\ ,

(S169)

using this vector
\eta_{\mathcal{T}/dt\times 1}
