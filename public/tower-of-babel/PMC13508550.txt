Transcranial direct current stimulation improves reduced global BOLD-CSF coupling in patients with insomnia disorder and comorbid anxiety: a resting-state functional MRI study
Authors: Yanan Zhang, Qingqing Sun, Xiaotong Zhang, Wanru Li, Yaru Wang, Lijia Cai, Zan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInsomnia disorder with comorbid anxiety is a prevalent clinical challenge. Although transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) is a promising noninvasive neuromodulatory approach, its effects on glymphatic-related neurovascular-cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) coupling in insomnia remain unclear. This study investigated the effects of tDCS on global blood oxygen level-dependent (gBOLD)-CSF coupling using resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) in patients with insomnia disorder and comorbid anxiety.MethodsWe conducted a 2-week, double-blind, randomized, sham-controlled trial. Patients with insomnia disorder and comorbid anxiety were randomized to receive either active or sham tDCS. The intervention consisted of 14 consecutive daily 20-min sessions delivered at 1.1 mA, targeting the left forehead and right dorsolateral prefrontal cortex. Healthy controls were frequency-matched to the overall patient cohort by age and sex. The Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), Insomnia Severity Index (ISI), 14-item Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale (HAMA-14), 17-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale, Epworth Sleepiness Scale, 20-item Multidimensional Fatigue Inventory, polysomnography (PSG), and rs-fMRI were assessed at baseline, and patients were reassessed after treatment. The primary outcome was the post-intervention between-group difference in gBOLD-CSF coupling; clinical scales and PSG parameters were secondary outcomes.ResultsSixty patients with insomnia disorder and comorbid anxiety and 30 healthy controls completed baseline assessments. Compared with healthy controls, patients showed significantly lower gBOLD-CSF coupling (P < 0.001, PFDR = 0.002). Coupling strength was negatively correlated with ISI, PSQI, HAMA-14 scores, and arousal index, and positively correlated with NREM stage 3 (N3) sleep duration in patients. Multivariate regression identified N3 sleep duration as an independent predictor of gBOLD-CSF coupling (β = 0.328, P = 0.005, PFDR = 0.040). After therapy, the active tDCS group showed a greater increase in gBOLD-CSF coupling than the sham tDCS group (effect size: 0.035, 95% CI: 0.011 to 0.058; Pinteraction = 0.005, PFDR = 0.014). Adverse events were mild and comparable between groups.ConclusionsPatients with insomnia disorder and comorbid anxiety showed reduced gBOLD-CSF coupling during wakeful rs-fMRI, which was associated with subjective sleep quality, insomnia severity, anxiety level, arousal index, and N3 sleep duration. Active tDCS enhanced gBOLD-CSF coupling compared with sham stimulation, suggesting that its therapeutic effects may involve modulation of neurovascular-CSF dynamics alongside improvements in sleep and anxiety symptoms.Clinical trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov ID NCT07340268, retrospectively registered on January, 13, 2026.Keywords: Insomnia disorder, Anxiety, Transcranial direct current stimulation, Resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging, Glymphatic-related neurofluid dynamics

Introduction

Insomnia disorder is a highly prevalent sleep disorder characterized by difficulty in initiating or maintaining sleep, early-morning awakening, and associated daytime dysfunction. Beyond sleep impairment, insomnia is associated with multiple physical and psychiatric conditions, particularly anxiety and other emotional disorders [1]. Anxiety frequently co-occurs with insomnia and is often accompanied by autonomic symptoms such as tension, dry mouth, sweating, and palpitations [2]. The bidirectional relationship between insomnia and anxiety may aggravate hyperarousal, sleep fragmentation, and daytime dysfunction [3].

The glymphatic system was originally described as a central nervous system clearance pathway that facilitates cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and interstitial fluid exchange, thereby promoting the elimination of metabolic waste products, including amyloid-β, tau proteins, and inflammatory mediators [4–6]. Glymphatic clearance is primarily active during sleep and depends on the water channel protein aquaporin-4(AQP4) polarized on perivascular astrocytic endfeet [5, 7, 8]. Evidence suggests that insomnia disorder and anxiety may be accompanied by reduced glymphatic-related neurovascular-CSF coupling or clearance, which has been linked to a range of adverse outcomes, including psychiatric comorbidity, cognitive dysfunction, and an increased risk of neurodegenerative diseases [9, 10].

Several imaging and experimental approaches have been applied or adapted to assess glymphatic-related physiology, including contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), positron emission tomography (PET) tracers, and diffusion-based MRI measures [11–13]. However, these methods are limited by invasiveness, low temporal resolution, species-specific applicability, or reliance on indirect structural proxies [14, 15]. Resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) studies have reported temporal coupling between global blood oxygen level-dependent (gBOLD) activity and CSF dynamics [16–19]. Specifically, low-frequency oscillations of the gBOLD signal exhibit a coupled relationship with CSF flow, reflecting both the influence of cerebral blood flow on CSF flow and the role of CSF flow on the removal of metabolic waste in the brain [20]. The gBOLD-CSF coupling approach uses standard rs-fMRI to noninvasively assess the temporal relationship between gBOLD fluctuations and CSF signal changes at a seconds-scale resolution. This measure is derived from post-acquisition analysis rather than real-time monitoring. Unlike static diffusion or PET-based methods, gBOLD-CSF coupling captures dynamic neurovascular-CSF interactions that may vary with physiological states [11, 20, 21]. It therefore serves as a clinically feasible indirect rs-fMRI marker of glymphatic-related neurofluid dynamics [12].

Current treatments for insomnia disorder with comorbid anxiety include cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I), pharmacological therapies, and neuromodulatory interventions [22–25]. CBT-I remains a first-line treatment but may be limited by time-intensive delivery and restricted accessibility. Pharmacological treatments include benzodiazepine receptor agonists, sedating antidepressants, melatonin-related agents, and dual orexin receptor antagonists (DORAs) [26]. DORAs provide a mechanistically distinct strategy by antagonizing orexin receptors and suppressing signaling that promotes wakefulness, and have shown efficacy and tolerability in the treatment of insomnia [27]. Nevertheless, pharmacotherapy may be limited by adverse effects, next-day impairment, drug interactions, or concerns about dependence with some medication classes [28]. These limitations highlight the need for alternative or adjunctive therapeutic strategies. Noninvasive brain stimulation techniques, including transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) and repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS), have emerged as promising interventions for insomnia and anxiety [29]. Compared with rTMS, tDCS offers advantages in ease of application and portability. tDCS modulates cortical excitability by delivering a weak direct current, typically 1 to 2 mA, through scalp electrodes, with anodal stimulation generally increasing and cathodal stimulation decreasing cortical excitability [30]. In particular, anodal stimulation of the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex has been implicated in mood regulation [31]. However, the impact of tDCS on gBOLD-CSF coupling in patients with insomnia disorder and comorbid anxiety remains unclear.

In this study, we compared gBOLD-CSF coupling between patients with insomnia disorder and comorbid anxiety and healthy controls using rs-fMRI, examined associations between coupling strength and clinical measures, and tested whether active tDCS modulated gBOLD-CSF coupling compared with sham stimulation. These analyses aimed to provide insights into neurophysiological processes that may accompany the therapeutic effects of tDCS in insomnia with anxiety.

Methods

Participants

We prospectively recruited patients with insomnia disorder and comorbid anxiety from the outpatient Department of Neurology, First Hospital of Jilin University, between February 2025 and November 2025. Diagnoses were based on the International Classification of Sleep Disorders, Third Edition, and the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition. Healthy controls were recruited during the same study period through advertisements posted in the health examination center. They were frequency-matched to the overall patient cohort at the group level according to age and sex. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians prior to study enrollment. The inclusion criteria for patients were as follows: (1) Insomnia Severity Index (ISI; range 0–28) score > 8; (2) 14-item Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale (HAMA-14; range 0–56) score ≥ 14; (3) 17-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD-17; range 0–52) score < 7; (4) Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE; range 0–30) score ≥ 27.

The exclusion criteria for patients and healthy controls were as follows: (1) other major psychiatric disorders, neurological diseases, or substance use disorders; (2) other sleep disorders, such as obstructive sleep apnea, rapid eye movement sleep behavior disorder, or restless legs syndrome; (3) insomnia or sleep disturbance secondary to organic diseases, such as epilepsy, diabetes, or renal failure; (4) shift work or abnormal circadian rhythm; (5) use of medications that could affect sleep, arousal, or central nervous system function within the past month, including central nervous system stimulants, sedative hypnotics, opioid analgesics, theophylline preparations, systemic corticosteroids, antipsychotics, antidepressants, or anxiolytics; (6) contraindications to MRI examination, such as claustrophobia, metal dentures, or metallic implants; (7) clinically significant organic or structural brain lesions detected on brain MRI; and (8) inability to complete questionnaires, monitoring procedures, or treatment protocols.

After baseline assessments, including sleep and mood questionnaires, portable polysomnography (PSG), and rs-fMRI for all participants, patients were randomly assigned to the active or sham tDCS group. Sleep questionnaires, mood questionnaires, PSG, and rs-fMRI were repeated within 2 days after tDCS treatment (Fig. 1).

Study design

This was a 2-week randomized, double-blind, sham-controlled trial. Baseline gBOLD-CSF coupling was first compared between patients with insomnia disorder and comorbid anxiety and healthy controls. Patients were then randomized 1:1 to active or sham tDCS using a stratified block design generated by an independent statistician. Group allocation was concealed in sealed envelopes and implemented by personnel not involved in enrollment or outcome assessment. No additional age or sex matching was performed between the two treatment groups. Baseline demographic variables, including age and sex, were compared between the treatment groups to assess the balance achieved by randomization. Following baseline evaluations, patients underwent once-daily tDCS for 2 weeks. The primary outcome was the post-intervention between-group difference in gBOLD-CSF coupling measured by rs-fMRI. Secondary outcomes included changes in sleep- and mood-related questionnaires and polysomnographic parameters. Safety outcomes included adverse events and their time of occurrence.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was calculated from pilot data in patients with insomnia disorder and comorbid anxiety. The expected between-group difference in the 2-week change in gBOLD-CSF coupling was 0.035, with a common standard deviation of 0.040. Because no established minimal clinically important difference is available for gBOLD-CSF coupling, this pilot-derived difference was used as the smallest effect of interest for the imaging endpoint. Although the expected direction was stronger coupling in healthy controls and greater improvement after active treatment, a two-tailed test was chosen a priori as a conservative approach that allowed for effects in either direction. With α = 0.05 and 80% power, 22 participants per group were required. To allow for 20% attrition, the target sample size was increased to 28 participants per group; therefore, 30 patients were enrolled in each treatment group. Thirty healthy controls were enrolled for the baseline case-control comparison according to available funding and scanner resources.

Clinical assessments

MRI examinations were performed in the morning to minimize potential circadian influences on glymphatic-related physiology. Demographic and clinical variables were collected within 48 h before MRI scans. The severity of insomnia symptoms and their impact on daytime functioning, as well as concerns and satisfaction regarding tDCS treatment efficacy, were assessed using the ISI [32]. Subjective sleep quality was assessed using Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) [33]. Anxiety severity was evaluated using the HAMA-14 [34], and depressive symptoms were assessed using the HAMD-17 [35]. Excessive daytime sleepiness was assessed using the Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS) [36], and fatigue was evaluated using the 20-item Multidimensional Fatigue Inventory (MFI-20) [37].

PSG

PSG was performed in participants’ homes using a standardized portable monitoring system (NEUROTRACE, PSM-A). Participants received standardized instructions before recording, and data quality was checked by trained technicians after acquisition. The recording montage included electroencephalography (F4-M1, F3-M2, C4-M1, C3-M2, O2-M1, and O1-M2), electrooculography (E1-M2 and E2-M2), chin electromyography (Chin1-Chin2), bilateral tibialis anterior muscle electromyography, electrocardiography, nasal pressure, finger oximetry, chest and abdominal respiratory effort belts. Sleep and related events were scored by certified PSG technologists according to the American Academy of Sleep Medicine Manual, version 3.0. Parameters included total sleep time, sleep efficiency, sleep onset latency, rapid eye movement (REM) sleep latency, wake after sleep onset, stage 1 non-REM (stage N1), stage 2 NREM (stage N2), stage 3 NREM (stage N3), REM sleep, arousal index, periodic limb movement index (PLMI), apnea and hypopnea index (AHI), mean arterial oxygen saturation, minimum arterial oxygen saturation, and end-tidal CO2.

TDCS intervention

Active or sham tDCS intervention (Nexalin Technology, Inc., CA, USA) was administered under identical instructions. Participants were instructed to turn off their mobile devices and remain awake and relaxed during treatment. The anode electrode (4.5 × 4.5 cm) was placed over the left frontopolar region (Fp1 in the international 10–20 system), and the cathode electrode (4.5 × 4.5 cm) was placed over the right dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (F4). Active stimulation was delivered at 1.1 mA for 20 min once daily for 2 weeks. At the beginning and end of treatment, there is a 30 s period of current fading in and out. Sham stimulation used the same 30-s ramp-up and ramp-down sensations at the beginning and end of the 20-minute session to mimic the cutaneous sensation of active stimulation, without receiving active current during the maintenance phase. To maintain the double-blind design, the tDCS devices were preprogrammed by an independent researcher using assigned codes, ensuring that neither the administering technician nor the patient knew whether stimulation was active or sham. The healthy controls did not receive this therapy. Participants could willingly withdraw from this study at any time and choose any other medical treatments.

Explanation on medication usage status

All participants followed standardized medication restrictions to reduce confounding and ensure safety. Medication-naïve participants were required to refrain from initiating sedative-hypnotic drugs, anxiolytics, antidepressants, antipsychotics, melatonin, central stimulants, opioid analgesics, corticosteroids, or theophylline preparations until the 2-week post-treatment endpoint. Participants already receiving medications for stable non-neurological conditions, such as antihypertensive or lipid-lowering therapy, were required to maintain the same dosage and frequency throughout the study. Compliance was verified at each study visit.

Imaging data acquisition and preprocessing

All participants underwent brain MRI scanning using a single 3.0 Tesla MRI scanner (Siemens, Erlangen, Germany) equipped with a 20-channel head and neck coil, with identical acquisition protocols applied across all participants. Noise-reducing earplugs were provided to attenuate scanner noise. During rs-fMRI, participants were instructed to keep their eyes closed, remain awake, and avoid focusing on specific thoughts. Foam padding was utilized to stabilize the head and reduce motion artifacts during scanning. A trained radiologist monitored the scanning process to ensure image quality. T1-weighted structural images were acquired using a magnetization-prepared rapid acquisition gradient-echo sequence with the following parameters: repetition time (TR) of 1800 ms, echo time (TE) of 2.30 ms, matrix dimensions of 256 × 256, a voxel size of 0.92 × 0.92 × 0.92 mm3, 192 slices. The rs-fMRI data were acquired using an echo-planar imaging sequence with the following parameters: TR of 1500 ms, TE of 30 ms, flip angle of 70°, matrix dimensions of 110 × 110, field of view of 220 × 220 mm2, slice thickness of 2.0 mm, 60 slices, and 320 volumes. After discarding the first 10 volumes during preprocessing, 310 time points were retained for subsequent analysis.

Preprocessing of rs-fMRI data was performed using the Data Processing Assistant for Resting-State fMRI (DPARSF, version 6.0, http://rfmri.org/dpabi) and Statistical Parametric Mapping software (SPM12, www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm) [38]. The first 10 volumes were discarded, followed by slice-timing correction and head-motion realignment. Scans were excluded from further analysis if maximum head motion exceeded 2.5 mm in translation or 2.5°in rotation in any direction; this criterion was applied at the scan-level, and individual frames were not scrubbed. Frame scrubbing was not applied to avoid disrupting the continuous temporal structure required for phase-based gBOLD-CSF coupling estimation. The remaining scans were spatially smoothed with a 4-mm full-width at half-maximum Gaussian kernel, linearly detrended, and temporally band-pass filtered at 0.01 to 0.1 Hz. CSF signals underwent the same preprocessing steps as gBOLD signals, except for spatial smoothing. The six rigid-body motion parameters derived from realignment were used for quality control and scan level exclusion but were not included as nuisance regressors in the primary gBOLD-CSF coupling analysis, because the analysis focused on the temporal and phase relationship between gBOLD fluctuations and CSF signal changes [39].

Definition of regions of interest (ROI)

The cortical gray matter region of interest (ROI) was defined using the Harvard-Oxford cortical structural atlas (https://neurovault.org/collections/262/) [40]. The gBOLD signal was derived by extracting and averaging BOLD signals from whole-brain cortical gray matter regions (Fig. 2A). Cortical gray matter ROIs were defined using atlas-based and subject-specific gray matter masks rather than manual segmentation. All fMRI signal analyses were conducted in individual gray matter space to minimize spatial blurring. Before averaging, voxelwise fMRI time series were z-score normalized to ensure comparable scaling across voxels. The resulting gBOLD signal primarily reflected synchronized global cortical fluctuations.

The CSF signal was extracted from the inferior slices of the rs-fMRI data, which are less affected by repeated radiofrequency pulses and are sensitive to CSF inflow near the inferior cerebellar and fourth ventricle regions [20]. The CSF mask was manually delineated on the inferior fMRI slice by two trained raters blinded to group allocation, and its anatomical location was confirmed using the corresponding T1-weighted structural image. Manual delineation was used because automated ventricular masks may not reliably capture the inferior CSF inflow signal targeted by this method and may instead emphasize ventricular CSF regions less specific to the inflow signal of interest. To minimize rater-dependent bias and assess reproducibility, CSF masks were independently delineated by the two raters, and inter-rater reliability was evaluated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) for gBOLD-CSF coupling estimates derived from the independently delineated CSF ROIs. Voxelwise fMRI signals within each CSF mask were z-score normalized before signal averaging to ensure consistency and comparability.

Quantification of gBOLD-CSF coupling strength

After extracting rs-fMRI signals from cortical gray matter and CSF regions, gBOLD-CSF coupling strength was quantified using lagged cross-correlation analysis. Operationally, one time series was shifted relative to the other across a predefined lag window from − 7.5 s to + 7.5 s, corresponding to -5 to + 5 TRs given the TR of 1.50 s, and the Pearson correlation coefficient between the shifted gBOLD and CSF time series was calculated at each lag. The resulting series of lag-specific Pearson correlation coefficients constituted the cross-correlation function. Based on the cross-correlation curve, the negative peak at a lag of + 4.5 s was used to quantify gBOLD-CSF coupling strength, corresponding to the inverse relationship between gBOLD fluctuations and subsequent CSF signal changes. This peak at a positive lag was selected because, under our lag convention, positive lags indicate CSF signal changes following gBOLD fluctuations, thereby preserving the physiologically interpretable temporal sequence of neurovascular-CSF coupling. The absolute value of this coupling index was used in subsequent statistical analyses to facilitate interpretation. We also estimated the cross-correlation function between the negative temporal derivative of the gBOLD signal and the CSF signal, as described previously [19]. To assess statistical significance, a permutation procedure was performed by randomly pairing gBOLD and CSF signals from different participants. This procedure was repeated 10,000 times to generate a null distribution and was implemented in MATLAB.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed variables are presented as means ± standard deviations and were compared using independent samples t tests. Non-normally distributed variables are presented as medians with interquartile ranges and were compared using Mann-Whitney U tests. Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages and were compared using chi-square tests. gBOLD-CSF coupling strength was derived from lagged cross correlation analysis of the processed gBOLD and CSF time series and was treated as an imaging outcome variable. A general linear model was used to compare gBOLD-CSF coupling strength between patients and healthy controls, adjusting for age, sex, and education. Partial correlation analyses adjusted for age, sex, and education were used to examine associations between gBOLD-CSF coupling strength and clinical indicators. Multiple linear regression analysis was further performed to identify independent clinical predictors of gBOLD-CSF coupling strength.Repeated measures analysis of covariance was used to compare changes in gBOLD-CSF coupling strength, questionnaire scores, and polysomnographic parameters before and after tDCS treatment between the active and sham groups, with age, sex, and education included as covariates. The group-by-time interaction was used to evaluate differential treatment effects between groups. Multiple comparisons were corrected using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate method. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

gBOLD signal couples with CSF signal changes

To investigate the coupling between gBOLD and CSF signals, we extracted fMRI time series from whole-brain cortical gray matter and CSF regions in all participants (Fig. 2A). Large CSF peaks were typically preceded by a positive gBOLD peak and followed by a negative gBOLD peak (Fig. 2B). Lagged cross correlation analysis revealed a significant positive peak between gBOLD and CSF signals at a lag of -3 s (r = 0.252, P < 0.001; permutation test, n = 10,000) and a significant negative peak at a lag of + 4.5 s (r = − 0.269, P < 0.001) (Fig. 2C). Furthermore, the cross-correlation between the CSF signal and the negative first-order derivative of the gBOLD signal showed a strong positive peak at zero lag (d/dt r = 0.369, P < 0.001) (Fig. 2D). These patterns indicate systematic coupling between global brain activity and CSF signal dynamics. Inter-rater reliability for gBOLD-CSF coupling estimates derived from independently delineated CSF ROIs was excellent, with average ICCs of 0.958 before treatment and 0.904 after treatment between the two researchers.

Comparison of gBOLD-CSF coupling between patients and healthy controls and its associations with clinical and sleep measures

After adjusting for age, sex and years of education, patients had significantly lower gBOLD-CSF coupling strength than healthy controls (P < 0.001, PFDR = 0.002, Fig. 3A), suggesting potential alterations in glymphatic-related neurofluid dynamics in patients with insomnia disorder and comorbid anxiety. Across all participants, gBOLD-CSF coupling was negatively correlated with age (r = − 0.360, P < 0.001, Fig. 3B). After adjusting for age, sex, and education, gBOLD-CSF coupling was negatively correlated with ISI score (r = -0.298, P = 0.021, Fig. 3C), PSQI score (r = -0.325, P = 0.011, Fig. 3D), HAMA-14 score (r = -0.264, P = 0.041, Fig. 3E), arousal index (r = -0.320, P = 0.015, Fig. 3F), and positively correlated with N3 sleep duration (r = 0.456, P < 0.001, Fig. 3G). Univariate and multivariate regression analyses showed that stage N3 duration (β = 0.328, P = 0.005, PFDR = 0.040) was independently associated with gBOLD-CSF coupling (Table 3).

Adverse events and safety outcomes

All patients who received active or sham tDCS were included in the safety analysis. No tinnitus, headache, dizziness, nausea, allergic dermatitis, phosphene perception, seizures, neurological complications, deaths, or other serious adverse events were reported in either group (Table 5).

Discussion

In this study, patients with insomnia disorder and comorbid anxiety showed reduced gBOLD-CSF coupling during wakeful rs-fMRI compared with healthy controls. Lower coupling was associated with poorer subjective sleep quality, greater insomnia severity, higher anxiety levels, higher arousal index, and shorter N3 sleep duration. Active tDCS increased gBOLD-CSF coupling relative to sham stimulation and improved several sleep and anxiety outcomes, with no significant between-group differences in adverse events. These findings suggest that insomnia with comorbid anxiety is associated with altered glymphatic-related neurofluid dynamics and that tDCS may modulate these dynamics alongside clinical improvement.

Recent studies support gBOLD-CSF coupling as a noninvasive rs-fMRI marker of coordinated neurovascular-CSF physiology [16, 19, 20]. This coupling reflects the temporal relationship between low-frequency global BOLD fluctuations and CSF signal changes and may be influenced by vasomotor activity and physiological state [6, 19, 41]. Accordingly, lagged cross-correlation analysis provides a quantitative measure of the coordination between global brain activity and CSF inflow-related signal fluctuations [16]. Our findings indicate altered gBOLD-CSF coupling in insomnia disorder with comorbid anxiety, but do not directly assess AQP4 polarization, interstitial solute transport, or metabolic waste clearance.

We found that gBOLD-CSF coupling strength was significantly reduced in patients with insomnia disorder and comorbid anxiety after adjustment for age, sex, and education. Imaging was intentionally performed during wakeful rest to obtain a standardized rs-fMRI measure that could be acquired consistently in both patients and healthy controls. Nevertheless, because glymphatic-related neurofluid dynamics are strongly influenced by sleep state, coupling measured during wakefulness may differ from that during NREM or REM sleep. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as reflecting wakeful resting-state gBOLD-CSF coupling rather than as a direct measure of sleep stage-specific glymphatic activity.

The relationship between lower gBOLD-CSF coupling and shorter stage N3 duration is biologically plausible. During slow-wave sleep, neural slow waves, hemodynamic oscillations, and CSF inflow can become strongly synchronized. Reduced stage N3 sleep may therefore shorten the temporal window during which sleep-related neurofluid dynamics are most pronounced. This interpretation is consistent with evidence that sleep disruption is associated with Alzheimer-related biomarker changes and that glymphatic and lymphatic fluid flow is regulated by circadian physiology [42, 43]. Conversely, an elevated arousal index reflects sleep fragmentation and instability, which may disrupt sustained physiological states that support coordinated neurovascular-CSF oscillations. These alterations may partly explain why lower coupling was associated with poorer subjective sleep quality, greater insomnia severity, and higher anxiety levels.

Furthermore, anxiety may contribute to altered gBOLD-CSF coupling through hyperarousal, autonomic dysregulation, and altered limbic-prefrontal control circuits, potentially preventing complete disengagement from hyperarousal states during sleep and interfering with the transition into slow-wave sleep [29]. Such disrupted sleep architecture may reduce the temporal windows that support glymphatic-related neurofluid physiology [44]. Altered clearance-related physiology may, in turn, be associated with neuroinflammatory mediators and metabolic waste products, potentially exacerbating sleep disturbances, anxiety symptoms, and neurodegenerative vulnerability [5, 45, 46]. Thus, attenuated gBOLD-CSF coupling may represent a potential nexus linking sleep microstructure, psychopathology, and coordinated neurovascular-CSF physiology. The age-related decline in coupling strength is consistent with prior evidence of age-dependent glymphatic alterations, including reduced slow-wave sleep, lower CSF turnover, and disrupted AQP4 polarization [47–49]. However, these mechanisms should be interpreted cautiously because rs-fMRI cannot directly assess AQP4 polarization, solute transport, or molecular clearance.

Our findings suggest that active tDCS has potential to improve sleep status and increase gBOLD-CSF coupling in patients with insomnia disorder and comorbid anxiety. Previous studies have reported that tDCS may alleviate insomnia symptoms and modulate anxiety- and mood-related neural networks [29–31]. These effects may be particularly relevant in patients with comorbid insomnia and anxiety, in whom sleep disturbance and emotional hyperarousal may mutually reinforce each other. A recent study from our institution also reported reduced gBOLD-CSF coupling in patients with insomnia disorder and increased coupling after tDCS treatment [50]. The present sham-controlled study extends these observations by focusing on patients with comorbid anxiety and by integrating PSG-derived sleep architecture with gBOLD-CSF coupling.

Building on these findings, we propose a cautious mechanistic hypothesis: tDCS may modulate cortical excitability, sleep-wake regulation, emotion-related fronto-limbic circuits, and arousal, thereby improving sleep architecture and indirectly enhancing coordinated neurovascular-CSF physiology [20]. This interpretation is consistent with evidence that global brain activity, hemodynamic oscillations, and CSF signal fluctuations are temporally coupled, particularly during sleep-related physiological states [51]. tDCS may also induce sustained and network-wide cerebral hemodynamic responses, providing a potential pathway through which neuromodulation could influence gBOLD-CSF coupling [52, 53]. However, whether changes in gBOLD-CSF coupling are driven directly by neuromodulation or indirectly through improvements in sleep and anxiety symptoms remains to be clarified in future studies.

The magnitude of symptom improvement was also clinically relevant. The active tDCS group showed a mean ISI reduction of 7.03 points, exceeding the reported 6-point minimal clinically important difference and approaching the commonly used 8-point threshold for treatment response. The mean PSQI reduction of 6.16 points also exceeded clinically meaningful change values reported in insomnia intervention studies, supporting both clinical and statistical improvement [32, 54, 55].

Several limitations should be emphasized. First, the sample size was relatively small and participants were recruited from a single center; larger multicenter studies are needed to confirm these findings. Second, the lack of long-term follow-up limits conclusions regarding the durability of the clinical and imaging effects of tDCS. Third, only one stimulation intensity, session duration, and electrode montage were tested; therefore, potential dose-response effects remain unclear. Fourth, gBOLD-CSF coupling is a putative indirect rs-fMRI marker of glymphatic-related neurofluid dynamics rather than a direct measure of glymphatic clearance. Fifth, objective vigilance monitoring with simultaneous EEG or eye tracking was not performed during rs-fMRI; although participants were instructed to remain awake, unrecognized drowsiness could have influenced coupling estimates. Sixth, nuisance regression was omitted to preserve the global BOLD and CSF signals of interest, but residual physiological noise or motion-related effects may remain. Future studies should incorporate physiological recordings, objective vigilance monitoring, alternative preprocessing pipelines, and sensitivity analyses to confirm the robustness of these findings.

Conclusions

This study showed that gBOLD-CSF coupling strength was significantly reduced in patients with insomnia disorder and comorbid anxiety and was associated with sleep architecture and anxiety severity. Active tDCS increased gBOLD-CSF coupling relative to sham stimulation and produced clinically meaningful improvements in subjective sleep outcomes. These findings suggest that tDCS may modulate glymphatic-related neurofluid dynamics and may represent a promising therapeutic strategy for insomnia disorder accompanied by anxiety, although further studies are needed to clarify the underlying mechanisms.
