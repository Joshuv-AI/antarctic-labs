Facilitating Tricuspid Edge-to-Edge Repair in Torrential Regurgitation Using Temporary Superior Vena Cava Balloon Occlusion
Authors: Firas Jaly, Holger Priebe-Brämer, Boris Dickmann, Jadwiga Klajn, Hans-Jörg Hippe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTranscatheter edge-to-edge repair (TEER) has emerged as the most widely adopted percutaneous therapy for severe tricuspid regurgitation (TR) in patients at high surgical risk. However, large coaptation gaps and severe leaflet tethering remain major anatomical challenges that may preclude successful leaflet grasping.Case SummaryWe describe 2 patients with torrential TR and large coaptation gaps (15 and 18 mm) who underwent successful tricuspid TEER facilitated by transient superior vena cava (SVC) balloon occlusion. A semicompliant 20-mm valvuloplasty balloon was temporarily inflated within the SVC to reduce venous return and right-sided preload during leaflet grasping. Balloon inflation resulted in marked annular reduction and near-complete elimination of the coaptation gap, enabling successful device implantation and substantial TR reduction in both cases.DiscussionTemporary SVC occlusion may represent a useful adjunctive technique to transiently reduce right-sided preload and tricuspid annular dimensions in challenging anatomies with large coaptation gaps, thereby facilitating successful TEER.Key Words: chronic heart failure, imaging, insufficiency, right ventricle, tricuspid valve, valve repair, valve replacement

Background

Over the past decade, increasing recognition of the prognostic impact of severe tricuspid regurgitation (TR) has driven rapid development of transcatheter therapies.1

Among these, transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (TEER) has become the most widely used percutaneous intervention for patients with symptomatic severe TR who are considered poor surgical candidates.2 The procedure is associated with excellent safety and meaningful symptomatic improvement in appropriately selected patients.3

However, anatomical suitability remains a key determinant of procedural success. Severe leaflet tethering, marked annular dilation, and large coaptation gaps are well-established predictors of failure.4 In particular, coaptation gaps exceeding 7 to 10 mm are associated with significantly reduced success rates.5

In such cases, alternative strategies—including transcatheter tricuspid valve replacement (TTVR)6 or heterotopic caval valve implantation7—may be considered. However, these approaches carry important limitations and may not be suitable in patients with impaired right ventricular (RV) function or pulmonary hypertension.

We report 2 cases demonstrating a novel intraprocedural strategy using temporary balloon occlusion of the superior vena cava (SVC) to transiently reduce right-sided preload and facilitate successful TEER in patients with torrential TR and very large coaptation gaps.

Case 1

Presentation and workup

An 85-year-old frail woman presented with progressive right-sided heart failure, including worsening peripheral edema, abdominal distension, and exertional dyspnea (NYHA functional class III).

Her medical history included coronary artery disease with recent right coronary artery percutaneous coronary intervention (6 months prior), long-standing atrial fibrillation, and heart failure with preserved ejection fraction with advanced (grade III) diastolic dysfunction. Comorbidities included chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (GOLD II) and refractory hypertension despite prior renal denervation.

Physical examination revealed elevated jugular venous pressure with prominent systolic pulsations (Lancisi sign), a holosystolic murmur along the lower left sternal border, bilateral pulmonary crackles, and peripheral edema.

Electrocardiography showed sinus rhythm with complete left bundle branch block (QRS 142 ms) and first-degree atrioventricular block.

Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated torrential TR with massive right atrial and RV dilation. Left ventricular function was preserved, while RV function was moderately reduced.

Transesophageal echocardiography revealed a complex quadricuspid tricuspid valve (Type IIIb, Hahn classification) with severe annular dilation and a maximal coaptation gap of 18 mm, along with marked septal leaflet tethering (Video 1). Transgastric imaging confirmed the large coaptation defect and torrential secondary tricuspid regurgitation (Video 2).

Laboratory testing showed elevated N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) (2,211 pg/mL), impaired renal function (creatinine 1.8 mg/dL), and mild hepatic enzyme elevation consistent with congestion.

Right heart catheterization confirmed severe TR with a prominent V-wave and mixed pulmonary hypertension. RV–pulmonary arterial coupling appeared borderline.

Management and procedure

Following multidisciplinary heart team discussion, surgery was deemed prohibitive (EuroSCORE II 5.61%, TriScore 48%).

TTVR was considered but rejected due to impaired RV function, pulmonary hypertension, and baseline conduction abnormalities with high risk of postprocedural pacemaker implantation.

Despite unfavorable anatomy, TEER was pursued given its superior safety profile.

The patient underwent preprocedural optimization with aggressive diuresis and levosimendan infusion; however, no meaningful reduction in coaptation gap was achieved.

The procedure was performed under general anesthesia with transesophageal echocardiographic and invasive hemodynamic monitoring. A TriClip G5 system (Abbott) was positioned at the tricuspid valve.

Initial attempts at leaflet grasping, including increased positive end-expiratory pressure (up to 20 cm H2O), were unsuccessful.

A novel preload-reduction strategy was therefore employed. Following careful intraprocedural sizing using echocardiographic and fluoroscopic guidance (Figure 1), a 20-mm semicompliant valvuloplasty balloon (Osypka VACS III, 40 mm in length) was advanced into the SVC via left femoral venous access and inflated proximal to the right atrium to achieve effective occlusion of the SVC while minimizing the risk of vascular injury.

Case 2

Echocardiographic assessment and sizing of the superior vena cava prior to balloon occlusion.

Balloon inflation transiently reduced caval venous return and RV preload, resulting in acute reduction of right atrial and tricuspid annular dimensions, and in turn marked reduction of the coaptation gap (from 1.8 cm to 0.6 cm). This enabled successful leaflet grasping (Video 3). A fluoroscopic assessment confirmed appropriate balloon positioning and complete SVC occlusion (Video 4).

Three XTW clips were implanted using a zipper technique (2 anteroseptal, 1 posteroseptal). Balloon inflation was maintained for ∼30 seconds during each grasp.

Only mild transient hypotension occurred, without hypoxia or instability.

Final imaging showed reduction of TR from torrential (grade V) to mild (grade I–II) (Video 5).

Outcome and follow-up

The postprocedural course was uneventful, and the patient experienced immediate symptomatic improvement. She was discharged home 2 days after the procedure.

At 3-month follow-up, transesophageal echocardiography demonstrated stable clip position with mild-to-moderate residual TR. Clinically, the patient showed marked improvement, with functional status improving from NYHA functional class III to I-II. NT-proBNP levels decreased from 2,211 pg/mL to 783 pg/mL. Renal function improved, with creatinine decreasing to 1.3 mg/dL, and liver enzymes normalized, consistent with relief of venous congestion.

Case 2

Presentation and workup

An 83-year-old woman with prior successful mitral TEER presented with progressive right-sided heart failure (NYHA functional class IIIb), including massive peripheral edema and dyspnea.

Evaluation revealed torrential secondary TR with a coaptation gap of ∼15 mm despite adequate medical therapy.

Coronary angiography excluded obstructive disease. Right heart catheterization showed moderately elevated pulmonary pressures.

Medical history included permanent atrial fibrillation, chronic kidney disease, and iron deficiency anemia.

Electrocardiography showed atrial fibrillation (heart rate 55 beats/min) with complete right bundle branch block.

Echocardiography demonstrated a quadricuspid valve (Type IIIb) with a large coaptation defect (>15 mm), refractory to aggressive diuresis (Video 6). Transgastric imaging further demonstrated the complex valve anatomy and large coaptation gap (Video 7). Left ventricular function was preserved, whereas RV function was mildly reduced with a tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion of 12 mm.

Management and procedure

As in case 1, TEER with a preload-limiting strategy was pursued.

After careful echocardiographic and fluoroscopic sizing, a 20-mm Osypka VACS III semi compliant valvuloplasty balloon was once again selected and fully inflated in the SVC, achieving complete vessel occlusion (Video 8).

Within a few cardiac cycles, near-complete elimination of the coaptation gap was observed (Video 9).

TEER was performed this time using the PASCAL system (Edwards Lifesciences). Three PASCAL ACE devices were implanted in a clover configuration (1 anteroseptal, 2 posteroseptal).

Leaflet grasping during balloon inflation was straightforward.

TR was reduced from grade V to grade I-II° (Video 10).

No hemodynamic compromise, oxygen desaturation, or instability occurred despite repeated balloon inflations.

Outcome and follow-up

The postprocedural course was uneventful, and the patient was discharged the following day.

At 3-month follow-up, the patient demonstrated sustained clinical improvement, with NYHA functional class improving from IIIb to II. Echocardiography confirmed stable device position with moderate residual TR. NT-proBNP levels decreased significantly from 4,274 pg/mL to 1,812 pg/mL. No further episodes of heart failure decompensation were reported (Table 1).

Ethical and Regulatory Considerations

Both procedures were performed as part of routine clinical care following multidisciplinary heart team discussion in patients deemed unsuitable for surgery and with limited alternative therapeutic options. The use of temporary SVC balloon occlusion as an adjunctive preload-reduction maneuver was performed off-label at the operator's discretion.

All patients provided written informed consent for the procedure and publication of anonymized clinical data and imaging.

Discussion

Severe TR is increasingly recognized as a major contributor to morbidity and mortality, particularly in older patients with multiple comorbidities. TEER has emerged as the most widely adopted therapy due to its favorable safety profile; however, large coaptation gaps (>7-10 mm), leaflet tethering, and annular dilation remain major limitations.8 In such anatomies, successful leaflet grasping may be extremely challenging.

Alternative Strategies

TTVR represents a promising option for anatomies unsuitable for repair. However, abrupt elimination of TR increases RV afterload, which may precipitate acute RV failure—particularly in patients with impaired RV function or pulmonary hypertension,9 as seen in our cases.

In addition, conduction disturbances requiring permanent pacing are common after TTVR, especially in patients with preexisting conduction disease.10

Heterotopic caval valve implantation may alleviate venous congestion but does not address the underlying valvular pathology and may result in persistent RV volume overload.

Given these considerations, TEER remained the preferred strategy.

Rationale for Preload Reduction

The effectiveness of TEER depends on adequate leaflet insertion and sufficient tissue bridge formation during grasping. In patients with torrential TR, severe annular dilation and leaflet tethering may result in coaptation gaps that exceed device capability. Dynamic reduction of right-sided preload may transiently decrease tricuspid annular dimensions and improve leaflet approximation, thereby facilitating successful grasping.

Several intraprocedural strategies have been proposed to transiently reduce right-sided preload and facilitate leaflet approximation during tricuspid TEER. These include aggressive preprocedural diuresis or ultrafiltration, ventilator-assisted maneuvers such as high positive end-expiratory pressure and Valsalva-like techniques, external chest compression, and patient positioning maneuvers designed to reduce venous return. More recently, temporary mechanical circulatory support systems for RV unloading, such as the Impella RP device, have also been proposed in selected patients with severe RV volume overload and large coaptation defects in order to improve leaflet approximation before TEER.

In both cases, high positive end-expiratory pressure levels (up to 20 cm H2O) were attempted without sufficient reduction of the coaptation gap. Reverse Trendelenburg positioning was considered but was not feasible due to technical limitations of the catheterization laboratory setup, which did not allow table tilting. Temporary RV mechanical support was considered disproportionate given the transient nature of the required preload reduction and the absence of overt RV failure or cardiogenic shock, and especially given the fact that, in case 1 for example, inotropic support using levosimendan had already been attempted. Given persistent unfavorable anatomy despite these measures, temporary SVC occlusion was ultimately employed as an adjunctive preload-reduction strategy.

Temporary occlusion of the SVC reduces venous return from the upper body, thereby decreasing right atrial filling and RV preload. This in turn potentially may lead to a reduction in tricuspid annular dimensions and improved leaflet coaptation.

In the present cases, this maneuver resulted in near-complete elimination of the coaptation gap, enabling successful device implantation.

Despite the favorable procedural outcomes observed in our cases, transient SVC occlusion may theoretically be associated with several important complications. Acute elevation of SVC pressure may result in cerebral venous congestion or transient neurologic symptoms. Additional potential risks include venous endothelial injury, thrombosis, vascular perforation or dissection, arrhythmias related to catheter manipulation, and hemodynamic instability resulting from abrupt preload reduction.

In the present series, balloon inflation was therefore limited to short intervals (∼30 seconds), performed with careful balloon sizing, and continuously monitored using invasive hemodynamic assessment and transesophageal echocardiography. No neurologic, vascular, arrhythmic, or sustained hemodynamic complications were observed. Nevertheless, larger studies are required to better define the safety profile and procedural limits of this technique.

Clinical Implications

These cases illustrate that dynamic manipulation of right-sided loading conditions can facilitate TEER in otherwise prohibitive anatomies.

Temporary SVC occlusion represents a simple, reproducible, and potentially widely applicable adjunctive technique that may expand the eligibility of patients for tricuspid TEER.

The concept of transient SVC occlusion as a preload-reduction strategy during tricuspid TEER has previously been proposed in an editorial by Tagliari et al.11 The present case series expands on this concept by demonstrating procedural feasibility and successful clinical implementation in patients with torrential TR and very large coaptation defects.

Further studies are needed to assess safety, reproducibility, and optimal patient selection.

Conclusions

Temporary balloon occlusion of the SVC can serve as an effective intraprocedural preload-limiting maneuver, enabling transient reduction of tricuspid annular dimensions and coaptation gap. This strategy may facilitate successful TEER in patients with otherwise unfavorable anatomy and represents a promising adjunctive technique in the treatment of severe TR.
