Agentic Video Generation: From Text to Executable Event Graphs via Tool-Constrained LLM Planning
Nicolae Cudlenco, Mihai Masala, Marius Leordeanu

Abstract
Existing multi-agent video generation systems use LLM agents to orchestrate neural video generators, producing visually impressive but semantically unreliable outputs with no ground truth annotations. We present an agentic system that inverts this paradigm: instead of generating pixels, the LLM constructs a formal Graph of Events in Space and Time (GEST) — a structured specification of actors, actions, objects, and temporal constraints — which is then executed deterministically in a 3D game engine. A staged LLM refinement pipeline fails entirely at this task (0 of 50 attempts produce an executable specification), motivating a fundamentally different architecture. The key architectural insight is a separation of concerns: the LLM handles narrative planning through natural language reasoning, while a programmatic state backend enforces all simulator constraints through validated tool calls, guaranteeing that every generated specification is executable by construction. Our system uses a hierarchical two-agent architecture — a Director that plans the story and a Scene Builder that constructs individual scenes through a round-based state machine — operating over exploration and building tools that provide paginated access to the simulation environment’s capabilities. Dedicated Relation Subagents further populate the logical and semantic edge types of the GEST formalism that procedural generation leaves empty, making this the first approach to exercise the full expressive capacity of the representation. The system can operate from an optional text prompt or autonomously — the Director Agent explores the simulation world, conceives a narrative, and produces both the executable GEST and a natural language story description. We evaluate in two stages: autonomous generation compared against procedural random baselines via a 3-model LLM jury, where agentic narratives win 79% of text comparisons and 74% of video comparisons against procedural baselines; and seeded generation where the same textual specification is given to the agentic system, VEO 3.1, and WAN 2.2, with human annotations confirming that engine-generated videos substantially outperform neural generators on physical validity (58% vs 25% and 20%) and semantic alignment (3.75/5 vs 2.33 and 1.50). The system produces multi-actor narrative videos with perfect spatiotemporal annotations (RGB, spatial relation graphs, event-frame mappings) at zero marginal cost — annotations that neural generators fundamentally cannot provide.

Introduction

Multi-agent AI systems for video generation have proliferated rapidly, with frameworks such as StoryAgent
7
, MAViS
17
, and DreamRunner
18
orchestrating specialized agents across scriptwriting, character design, and video rendering. These systems have demonstrated increasingly sophisticated narrative planning capabilities. However, they all share a fundamental limitation: every agent in the pipeline ultimately drives a
neural video generator
, producing pixels with no semantic guarantees. Objects spontaneously appear and disappear, actors morph between frames, and temporal ordering is routinely violated
3
;
16
.

We propose an inversion of this paradigm. Instead of using agents to generate pixels, we use agents to construct a
formal event graph specification
— a Graph of Events in Space and Time (GEST)
11
— that encodes actors, actions, objects, locations, and temporal constraints in an explicit, inspectable structure. This specification is then executed
deterministically
in a 3D game engine
4
, producing multi-actor narrative videos with perfect spatiotemporal annotations (RGB, per-frame spatial relation graphs, event-to-frame temporal mappings) as a byproduct of execution.

The central challenge is that GESTs must be both
narratively coherent
(telling a meaningful story) and
simulator-valid
(respecting the engine’s action chains, object lifecycles, POI capacities, and temporal constraint rules). LLMs excel at narrative coherence but cannot maintain the precise state tracking needed for simulator validity across the 50–200 events that a multi-actor story requires. We first built a staged LLM refinement pipeline — six specialized agents chained through LangGraph, each validating structured outputs via Pydantic — and found that it fails entirely at this task: across 50 generation attempts, zero produced a simulator-valid GEST. The failure modes compound across stages (hallucinated actions, invalid chains, object lifecycle violations, temporal cycles, context overflow), and post-hoc correction requires backtracking through dependent state at superlinear cost. Section
4
describes the pipeline and its failure modes in detail; this outcome motivated a fundamentally different architecture.

Our key insight is a
separation of concerns
: the LLM handles narrative decisions (what should happen), while a programmatic state backend handles constraint enforcement (what is allowed to happen). The LLM operates exclusively through tool calls — creating actors, starting action chains, adding temporal constraints — and every tool call is validated and executed by the same procedural generator
4
that guarantees executability for random generation. Invalid operations are rejected before they can affect the graph. This means every GEST produced by the system is executable by construction, regardless of what the LLM attempts.

Our contributions are:

•

The staged LLM pipeline and the analysis of its failure modes, which directly motivated the agentic architecture.

•

A hierarchical two-agent architecture for agentic video generation: a Director Agent that explores the simulation world and plans multi-scene stories, and a Scene Builder Subagent that constructs individual scenes through a round-based state machine with tool-enforced constraints, augmented by Relation Subagents that populate the logical and semantic edge types of the GEST formalism that procedural generation leaves empty, making this the first approach to exercise the full expressive capacity of the representation.

•

A tool-based constraint enforcement layer that repurposes a procedural GEST generator as a state backend, providing transaction-based chain construction, POI capacity tracking, object lifecycle management, temporal cycle detection, and interaction coordination.

•

A two-stage evaluation: (1) autonomous generation compared against procedural baselines via a 3-model LLM jury on narrative coherence, and (2) seeded generation where the same text is given to the agentic system, VEO 3.1, and WAN 2.2, with both LLM jury comparisons and human annotations showing that the engine substantially outperforms neural generators on physical validity and semantic alignment.

Prior work demonstrated text-to-GEST generation as a proof of concept
11
; we present the first end-to-end system that uses LLM agents to autonomously construct executable event graph specifications for deterministic 3D video generation — producing multi-actor narrative videos with dense spatiotemporal annotations (RGB, spatial relation graphs, event-frame mappings) and an architecture extensible to depth, pose, and instance segmentation
4
, rather than driving neural pixel generators. This transforms video generation from a programming task, where story structure is determined by the procedural generator’s code, into a specification task, where natural language shapes the story without any code changes. The system achieves 80% end-to-end success in seeded mode but remains at approximately 50% in fully autonomous generation, indicating that stability improvements are needed before unattended large-scale corpus production.

Background: GEST and the GEST-Engine

A GEST
11
is a directed graph
G=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
where nodes represent events and edges encode temporal, spatial, and semantic relationships. Each event node specifies an action, a performer, participating entities, a location, and properties. Special
Exists
nodes declare actors (with gender and name) and objects (with type). Temporal constraints include
before
,
after
,
same_time
, and
concurrent
, drawn from Allen’s interval algebra
1
.

The GEST-Engine
4
executes GESTs deterministically within GTA San Andreas via Multi Theft Auto (MTA). It implements a four-stage pipeline: (1) graph parsing and validation via set-cover episode selection, (2) entity and action grounding with chain-ID-based object identity, (3) temporal orchestration via Floyd-Warshall constraint satisfaction, and (4) execution with multi-modal artifact capture. The engine produces RGB video, per-frame pairwise spatial relation graphs, event-to-frame temporal mappings, and natural language descriptions at zero marginal annotation cost.

A procedural random generator
4
reads the engine’s capability registry—which catalogs all available episodes, POIs, action chains, object types, and actor skins—and produces random GESTs that are executable by construction by following valid next-action sequences at each POI. In this work, we significantly extend the procedural generator into a
state backend
for the agentic system. While the core state tracking (actors, events, temporal constraints, object lifecycles, POI capacities) originates from the random generator, we adapted it with a delegation interface that exposes validated operations to external agents, added transaction-based chain construction with rollback on failure, enriched the constraint enforcement with cycle detection and interaction coordination, and introduced scene-level state management for multi-scene stories. These extensions ensure that agentic generation benefits from the same executability guarantees as procedural generation while supporting substantially more complex narrative structures.

Procedural generation produces event-dense, structurally valid stories, but has no authored narrative intent: actors execute random sequences of available actions, and story-level structure — if present — is latent in the action sequence rather than explicitly authored. The agentic system described in Section
5
closes this gap by introducing an LLM Director that plans stories with explicit intent, motivation, and causal structure, while preserving the executability-by-construction guarantee of the underlying state backend.

4
The Staged LLM Approach

Before arriving at the agentic architecture, we built a staged LLM refinement pipeline: a LangGraph workflow chaining six specialized stages —
Concept
(abstract hierarchical GEST with parent/leaf scenes and actor archetypes),
Casting
(skin assignment from the engine’s catalog),
Episode Placement
(mapping scenes to valid simulation episodes),
Setup
(off-camera preparation actions and backstage positioning),
Screenplay
(translating abstract narrative into concrete action sequences), and
Scene Detail
(expanding each leaf scene into a complete executable GEST) — each implemented as a single prompt-and-parse step with structured output validation via Pydantic. The pipeline produced narratively coherent GESTs with logical structure and character motivation, but across 50 generation attempts
zero
produced an executable specification. The failure modes compounded across stages:
hallucinated actions
(e.g., “WalkTo” instead of “Move”),
invalid action chains
(“TypeOnKeyboard” without a prior “SitDown” and “OpenLaptop”),
object lifecycle violations
(putting down objects never picked up, two actors holding the same object),
temporal cycles
that the Floyd-Warshall solver cannot resolve, and
context overflow
from the 14,000-line capability registry. The fundamental problem is that LLMs cannot maintain precise state coherence across the 50–200 events that a multi-actor story requires; each stage makes locally reasonable decisions, but accumulated state — which actors are where, what they hold, which POIs are occupied, which temporal constraints are in effect — drifts from validity as the GEST grows, and post-hoc correction requires backtracking through dependent state at superlinear cost. The key lesson motivated the agentic architecture:
the LLM should handle narrative decisions (what should happen), while a programmatic backend should handle state-tracking and constraint enforcement (what is allowed to happen)
— operationalized in Section
5
through tool calls validated by the same procedural generator that guarantees executability for random generation.

5
Method: Agentic GEST Generation

5.1
Architecture Overview

The system uses a hierarchical two-agent architecture built on LangGraph for workflow orchestration and deepagents
1
1

1

https://github.com/langchain-ai/deepagents
for subagent delegation. A
Director Agent
handles high-level story planning: exploring the simulation world, selecting episodes and regions, picking actor skins, creating actors, and determining the sequence of scenes. For each scene, it delegates detailed event construction to a
Scene Builder Subagent
, which builds action chains, interactions, and temporal constraints through tool calls. After all scenes, the Director finalizes the GEST by linking scene boundaries with temporal constraints.

Both agents operate exclusively through function calling: every action—creating an actor, starting a chain, adding a constraint—is a tool call validated by the state backend (Section
5.5
). The agents never manipulate the GEST directly; they can only modify it through the tool interface, which rejects invalid operations before they affect the graph. Figure
1
shows the system architecture.

:

Both agents interact through Exploration Tools (read-only) and Building Tools (constraint-enforcing), which operate on the State Backend—the procedural generator repurposed as a tool layer.

5.2
The Director Agent

The Director receives a seed text (an optional story prompt) and a generation configuration (target scenes, actors). Its workflow has four phases:

Exploration.
The Director uses read-only, paginated exploration tools to discover the simulation world: browsing episodes, examining regions and their objects, inspecting POI action chains, and browsing character skins by gender (each with a visual description,
e.g
.
“a middle-aged white man with blonde hair in a black police uniform”). Pagination is essential—the capability registry is too large for a single prompt, so the agent must actively navigate the space.

Casting.
The Director creates the story structure via
create_story
and
create_actor
calls, specifying each actor’s name, gender, skin, and starting region. All actors are created before any scene is delegated.

Scene building.
Scenes are processed sequentially. For each, the Director calls
start_scene
with the episode, region, and participating actors, then delegates to the Scene Builder with a natural language task description (
e.g
.
“Build a scene where James and Sarah have lunch in the kitchen”). After the Scene Builder returns, the Director handles follow-up tasks and calls
move_actors
for location transitions.

Finalization.
The Director calls
finalize_gest
to link scene boundaries with cross-scene temporal constraints, producing the complete GEST.

5.3
The Scene Builder Subagent

The Scene Builder receives an isolated context: scene ID, episode, region, actors, and a narrative description. It does not see the full story—only its assigned scene—reducing context requirements and preventing accidental modification of other scenes.

Events are constructed through a round-based state machine:

1.

start_round()
: begins a temporal unit, returning the current state of all actors (posture, held objects, location).

2.

For each actor:
start_chain(actor, POI)
(which returns the valid next actions at the selected POI)
\to
one or more
continue_chain(actor, action)
(each advancing the chain by one action and returning the next valid actions)
\to

end_chain(actor)
to commit.

3.

do_interaction(actor1, actor2, type)
for synchronized two-actor actions (handshakes, hugs, conversations).

4.

start_recording
/
stop_recording
for camera control on committed events.

5.

add_temporal_dependency
or
add_starts_with
for cross-actor ordering.

6.

end_round()
: closes the round, adding cross-actor temporal ordering.

Multiple actors can have active chains simultaneously, and the subagent can interleave calls across actors. After all rounds,
end_scene()
returns a summary to the Director. The Scene Builder cannot create actors, move actors between regions, or finalize the GEST — these responsibilities belong exclusively to the Director, enforcing a clean separation between story-level and scene-level concerns.

5.4
Relation Subagents

After each scene is built and after the final GEST is assembled, the Director optionally delegates to two additional subagents for enriching the graph with higher-level annotations. A
Logical Relations Agent
adds causal and dependency relations between events (causes, enables, prevents, requires), capturing why events occur in relation to each other. A
Semantic Relations Agent
adds narrative coherence relations using free-text types (observes, interrupts, motivates, sets_context_for, contrasts_with), capturing how events relate at the story level rather than the temporal level. Both subagents receive a list of committed event IDs and operate through the same tool interface, ensuring that only valid event references are used. These relations are optional — the GEST is executable without them — but they populate the logical and semantic edge types defined in the GEST formalism that procedural generation never produces, bridging the gap between the representation’s expressive capacity and the generator’s output. To the best of our knowledge, this makes the agentic system the first approach to exercise the full expressive capacity of the GEST formalism in a single end-to-end pipeline.

5.5
Tool-Based Constraint Enforcement

The building tools implement a transaction-based state machine with four states (
IDLE
,
STORY_CREATED
,
IN_SCENE
,
IN_ROUND
). Each tool call verifies the correct state before executing. Key enforcement mechanisms include:

Actor state tracking.
Posture (standing/sitting/lying), held objects (with type and origin region), current location. Actors must be standing to start chains or interactions, and held objects can only be put down in their origin region.

Transactional chains.

continue_chain
creates events in temporary buffers; only
end_chain
commits them to the GEST. Failed chains are silently discarded.

POI capacity.
Exclusive-use objects (chairs, beds, gym equipment) are tracked per region, preventing two actors from using the same object.

Temporal cycle detection.
Before adding a
before
relation, the system walks the full dependency graph to check whether the new edge would create a cycle. Circular dependencies are rejected with an explanatory error.

Interaction rules.
Both actors must be standing, in the same region, not holding spawnables. No consecutive interactions without an intervening chain. The
Give
action requires an explicit receiver identifier and automatically creates a synchronized
INV-Give
event for the receiver.

Spawnable lifecycle.
Phone and cigarette actions are atomic sequences (
TakeOut
\to
Use
\to
Stash
) that lock the actor until completion.

5.6
Exploration Tools

Exploration tools provide read-only paginated access to the capability registry:

•

get_episodes
,
get_regions
,
get_pois
: browse environments.

•

get_poi_first_actions
,
get_next_actions
: discover valid action chains at specific POIs.

•

get_skins
: browse actor appearances by gender.

•

get_spawnable_types
,
get_interaction_types
: discover available object and interaction categories.

•

get_simulation_rules
: retrieve constraint documentation.

The explore-before-plan pattern prevents hallucination: the agent must verify through tool calls that a POI has a specific action before including it in a plan, addressing a primary failure mode of staged LLM approaches.

6.1
Data

Stage 1: Autonomous generation.
We generated stories using the agentic system without text prompts—the Director Agent autonomously explored the simulation world, conceived narratives, and built GESTs. From multiple generation batches, we obtained 5 unique story graphs, each simulated 5 times in the GEST-Engine, producing 25 agentic videos. Each agentic story carries a root narrative (the Director’s authored intent,
e.g
.
“Andrei receives an unexpected phone call while in his bedroom. He answers and exits to the hallway. Iulia arrives and knocks on the door, waiting for a response.”) and a Video Description from GEST (VDG) description—a post-simulation textual description generated by the proto-language pipeline
12
;
4
.

As baseline, we selected procedurally generated stories
4
matched 1:1 to the agentic groups by actor count. Procedural stories have VDG descriptions (same pipeline) but no authored narrative. To enable a fair narrative-level comparison, we generated
reconstituted narratives
for the procedural stories by prompting GPT-4o to infer a story-level intent from the VDG description, using one-shot prompting with an agentic example.

Stage 2: Seeded generation.
We selected 25 procedural stories from the GEST-Engine sample corpus
4
, diverse by scene type (classroom, gym, garden, house, mixed) and actor count (2–6). The procedural success rate for this subset was approximately 74% (roughly 34 generation attempts to obtain 25 successful simulations, computed post-hoc for comparison purposes). For each, we generated a VDG description and used it as a seed text for the agentic system—the Director received the procedural story’s description as narrative input and generated a new GEST re-interpreting that narrative. The same VDG description was also used as the prompt for VEO 3.1
6
and WAN 2.2
16
. This produced four video sources from the same textual specification: the original procedural video, the agentic re-interpretation, and the VEO and WAN neural generations. Of the 25 agentic generation attempts, 20 completed successfully (80%). We collected human annotations on the procedural, VEO, and WAN videos (34 annotations from 10 evaluators), evaluating physical validity (binary) and semantic alignment (1–5 Likert scale). Inter-annotator agreement was 66.7% for validity and 83.3% within
\pm
1 scale point for semantic alignment.

6.3
Evaluation Procedure

All evaluations use a 3-model LLM jury (GPT-5.2 Pro, Gemini 3.1 Pro, Claude Opus 4.6), following the LLM-as-a-judge paradigm
20
. Prior work on the same engine validated this approach through a human evaluation study
11
that established semantic quality rankings later confirmed by LLM jury evaluations, where the jury consistently preferred human references over machine conditions, demonstrating alignment with human judgment. Using three frontier models from different providers reduces single-model bias. Position bias is controlled by randomizing which text/video is assigned to position A vs B using a deterministic seed per pair.

Text jury.
Each pair of descriptions is presented anonymously as “Text A” / “Text B”. Judges score both 0–100 with no ties, evaluating narrative coherence, character motivation, temporal logic, and richness. We run three experiments: (1)
VDG vs VDG
—agentic VDG against procedural VDG, a fair comparison at the execution level; (2)
Narrative vs VDG
—agentic root narrative against procedural VDG, testing whether authored intent outperforms execution logs; (3)
Narrative vs Reconstituted
—agentic root narrative against procedural reconstituted narrative, a fair comparison when both sides have story-level intent.

Video jury.
Same 3-model jury. For each story, 10 frames are sampled using hybrid event-aware sampling (event midpoints from the event-frame mapping plus uniform 1 fps fill). Frames are labeled “Story A” / “Story B” via text overlay with randomized assignment. We run four experiments: (1)
Video only
—no text, judges assess purely from visual execution; (2)
VDG vs VDG
—videos paired with both VDG descriptions; (3)
Narrative vs VDG
—agentic narrative vs procedural VDG alongside videos; (4)
Narrative vs Reconstituted
—both narratives alongside videos.

Narrative fidelity.
Measures information loss through the generation pipeline. The jury receives the original root narrative and the VDG description of the same agentic story, scoring three dimensions on a 0–5 scale: event coverage (are the key events present?), character intent preservation (are character motivations maintained?), and causal structure (are cause-effect relationships preserved?).

Actor casting analysis.
Evaluates whether the Director Agent’s casting decisions produce appropriate visual appearances. For each actor, the jury receives the story narrative, the actor’s name, and the assigned skin description. Three dimensions are scored 0–2 (mismatch/neutral/match): gender, age, and attire appropriateness.

Narrative fidelity.
The jury scored three dimensions of fidelity between the Director’s narrative and the executed VDG description: event coverage (1.89/5), character intent preservation (0.68/5), and causal structure (0.68/5). Stage 2 shows substantially higher fidelity than Stage 1: event coverage improves from 1.89 to 3.12/5, character intent from 0.68 to 2.30/5, and causal structure from 0.68 to 2.14/5. 7
Discussion

7.1
Complementary Explicit and Implicit World Models

Our results suggest that explicit world models and neural generators address fundamentally different aspects of video generation. Neural models excel at visual realism but cannot maintain semantic consistency across complex multi-actor narratives. Formal specifications executed in 3D engines guarantee semantic correctness and produce dense spatiotemporal annotations as a byproduct, but at the cost of visual fidelity. The most promising direction is their combination: using GESTs as structured conditioning signals for neural video generators, preserving semantic reliability while achieving photorealistic output.

8
Limitations and Next Steps

8.1
Generation Stability

Autonomous generation succeeds about half the time. The dominant failure mode is repetitive planning — the agent exhausts its alternatives for expressing a scene within engine constraints and gives up. This is an engineering problem, not an architectural one: better variation strategies, explicit loop detection, and checkpoint-resume mechanisms should address it without changing the fundamental architecture.

8.2
The Fidelity Gap

Narrative fidelity (1.89/5 event coverage in autonomous mode, 3.12/5 when seeded) reflects a gap between what the Director narrates and what the engine can render, caused by expressive channels that the current platform does not expose.

Cinematography.
The engine captures from a fixed camera, with no tracking shots, zooms, or rack focus. Real storytelling is expressed through camera work: a tracking shot on a character walking away, a rack focus shifting attention between two speakers, a lingering wide shot of an empty room.

Temporal nuance.
The GEST temporal model draws from Allen’s interval algebra —
before
,
after
,
same_time
,
concurrent
— but cannot express frame-precise spatial triggers such as “as she enters, he looks away,” which demand synchronization of two actors relative to a spatial event.

Atmospheric expression.
Time of day, weather, lighting shifts — a sunset, rain starting, a room darkening — carry narrative weight at zero dialogue cost. The current engine does not modify environmental conditions during execution.

Action blending.
Real humans perform actions simultaneously and fluidly: smoking while talking, walking while looking at a phone. The MTA animation system plays atomic sequences — one action completes, then the next starts — producing the robotic quality that is the single largest contributor to the visual realism gap.

These are platform constraints, not architectural ones. The agentic system transfers directly to any engine that exposes these channels, and a GTA V port via FiveM or implementations in Unreal or Unity with animation blending and cinematic cameras would substantially close the fidelity gap without changing the pipeline.

8.3
Cost

Each agentic story currently costs approximately $0.25 in frontier-model API calls, with clear headroom through smaller fine-tuned models that internalize the simulation constraints.

9
Conclusion

We presented an agentic system that constructs formal event graph specifications (GESTs) for deterministic 3D video generation, inverting the dominant paradigm of using agents to drive neural pixel generators. The separation of concerns between narrative planning (LLM) and constraint enforcement (programmatic state backend) guarantees that every generated specification is executable by construction, achieving 80% end-to-end success rate with seeded generation versus 0% for a staged LLM pipeline.

Our two-stage evaluation reveals complementary findings. In both stages, the LLM jury consistently shows that procedural generation wins at the execution level (denser VDG descriptions), but agentic narratives carry genuine story structure that restores or reverses this advantage when used as context. Human annotations on seeded generation confirm that the same textual specification produces physically valid, semantically faithful videos when executed by the engine (58% validity, 3.75/5 alignment) but physically implausible outputs when rendered by VEO 3.1 (25%, 2.33) or WAN 2.2 (20%, 1.50). Seeding the Director with a concrete specification roughly doubles narrative fidelity compared to autonomous generation.

Beyond these individual results, this work brings together every direction the GEST formalism supports within a single system: the Director’s natural-language planning produces GESTs, the deterministic engine executes them into video with dense per-frame spatial and temporal annotations, and the proto-language pipeline describes what was rendered back into natural language. With the Relation Subagents populating the logical and semantic edge types that procedural generation never produces, the agentic system is the first to exercise the full expressive capacity of the GEST formalism in a single end-to-end pipeline — closing the loop from story to specification to video and back, with every component active.
