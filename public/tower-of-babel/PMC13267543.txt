Grynfeltt lumbar hernia mimicking recurrent renal colic in a young woman: A case report
Authors: Habib Bellamlih, Soufiane Belabbes, Brahim Zinoun, Taoufik Africha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLumbar hernias are a rare condition. These anomalies account for less than 2% of all abdominal hernias. They develop in areas of natural muscular weakness in the lumbar region, most often in the upper lumbar triangle (Grynfeltt-Les Shaft triangle). Lumbar hernias are often overlooked because their symptoms are nonspecific and can mimic more common conditions, such as renal colic or soft tissue masses, particularly lipomas. We report the case of a 32-year-old woman with recurrent episodes of left lumbar pain, initially suspected of being renal colic, but ultimately diagnosed with a Grynfeltt lumbar hernia after imaging. This case highlights the importance of considering lumbar hernia in the differential diagnosis of persistent or atypical flank pain and emphasizes the central role of CT imaging in establishing an early diagnosis and guiding management.Keywords: Grynfeltt lumbar hernia, Lumbar triangle, Renal colic, Strangulation, CT scan

Introduction

Lumbar hernias are relatively rare compared to other types of ventral abdominal wall hernias.

Fewer than 300 cases have been reported over the past 300 years, representing less than 2% of all abdominal hernias [1]. Despite the rarity of this condition, lumbar hernias can lead to certain complications, particularly strangulation and incarceration.

The main causes of lumbar hernias are acquired (spontaneous, postoperative, or post-traumatic). Clinical diagnosis is difficult, especially in obese individuals or those who have undergone surgery, due to the sometimes ambiguous nature of the symptoms. Imaging, particularly computed tomography (CT) scan and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), plays a crucial role in clarifying the diagnosis of the hernia and its complicated forms. The goal of surgery for this type of hernia is to eliminate the abdominal wall defect and construct a firm, elastic abdominal wall that will withstand the stresses of daily physical activity.

Case report

A 32-year-old woman presented to the emergency department with recurrent episodes of left-sided lumbar colic evolving over the preceding 15 days. The pain was described as intermittent, sharp, and colicky in nature, with radiation toward the ipsilateral groin. It was of moderate-to-severe intensity, occasionally associated with nausea but without vomiting, fever, dysuria, or macroscopic hematuria. There were no identifiable triggering or relieving factors.

Her past medical history was notable for a previous episode of right nephrolithiasis that had been treated medically. She had no history of recurrent urinary tract infections, metabolic disorders, or chronic illnesses such as diabetes mellitus or hypertension. She was not taking any regular medications. There was no prior history of abdominal or pelvic surgery and no history of trauma.

Regarding family history, there was no known familial predisposition to urolithiasis or renal disease. She denied any family history of metabolic or endocrine disorders.

On initial presentation, the patient was hemodynamically stable, with normal vital signs (blood pressure 120/75 mmHg, heart rate 78 bpm, temperature 36.8°C). Physical examination revealed mild tenderness in the left lumbar region without guarding or rebound tenderness. There was no palpable abdominal or flank mass. The remainder of the abdominal examination was unremarkable, and there was no costovertebral angle tenderness.

Laboratory investigations were performed to assess for infection, renal function impairment, and inflammatory response. The results were within normal limits, including hemoglobin (14 g/dL), white blood cell count (8200/mm³), CRP (4 mg/L), serum creatinine (0.9 mg/dL), and blood urea nitrogen (17 mg/dL). Urinalysis showed no leukocyturia, nitrites, or microscopic hematuria.

Given this nonspecific clinical presentation, an abdominal CT scan with iodinated contrast was performed, revealing a lumbar hernia in the left posterolateral abdominal wall, with protrusion of adipose tissue. The herniated material was limited to the retroperitoneal fat, without intestinal involvement. The intraherniated fatty contents were infiltrated and heterogeneous. The kidneys appeared normal, without focal lesions, hydronephrosis, or signs of pyelonephritis. No renal or ureteral stones were detected in the urinary tract or ureters (Fig. 1). For better characterization of the intraherniated contents, an abdominal MRI was performed with T1- and T2-weighted sequences, as well as a fat-suppressed sequence, in axial and sagittal planes. The MRI revealed a herniation of retroperitoneal fat in the left posterolateral abdominal wall. This herniation showed high signal intensity on T1- and T2-weighted images and low signal intensity on the fat-suppressed sequence. The herniated fat contained linear streaks that were hypointense on T1- and hyperintense on T2-weighted images and persisted after fat suppression, consistent with inflammatory changes in the intraherniated contents. No intestinal segments, blood vessels, or solid organs were present in the hernial sac (Fig. 2).

A diagnosis of incarcerated left lumbar hernia was made, and the patient was taken to the operating room the same day.

During surgery, the patient was placed in the right lateral decubitus position. The swelling was explored through an incision in the left flank. A globular retroperitoneal fatty mass, attached by a conical pedicle emerging from a narrow constrictor ring, was discovered herniated through the upper lumbar triangle. The constrictor ring was freed, and the pedicle, along with the entire herniated mass, was excised. An extraperitoneal polypropylene mesh was placed to reinforce the abdominal wall defect. The opening was repaired with sutures, and the remainder of the wound was closed layer by layer. The immediate postoperative course was uneventful. At the follow-up consultation 1 month later, the surgical scar had healed well and the patient had no symptoms.

Discussion

An abdominal hernia is defined as the protrusion of a portion of the abdominal cavity through a normal or aberrant opening, or from an area of weakness in the abdominal wall [2].

Lumbar hernia is a rare condition compared to other types of abdominal wall hernia, accounting for approximately 2% of all abdominal hernias [3].

This condition can be caused by a congenital or acquired abnormality of the posterolateral abdominal wall.

Lumbar hernias arise from defects in the lumbar musculature or posterior fascia, located between the 12th rib and the iliac crest . They are most often associated with prior surgery or trauma. The herniation can develop through the superior lumbar triangle (Grynfeltt’s triangle) or, less commonly, through the inferior lumbar triangle (Petit’s triangle). The upper lumbar triangle is anatomically delimited superiorly by the 12th rib, anteriorly by the internal oblique muscle, and posteriorly by the erector spinae muscle. The lower lumbar triangle is delimited anteriorly by the external oblique muscle, inferiorly by the iliac crest, and posteriorly by the latissimus dorsi muscle [4,5].

Clinical manifestations are often nonspecific and potentially misleading. They generally present as heaviness or pain in the flank. A palpable mass is rarely found.

Patients who have undergone a lumbar incision may experience intercostal pain or visible swelling due to neuralgia, muscle weakness related to intercostal nerve injury, or a herniated disc resulting from incomplete closure or a musculofascial diastasis. Distinguishing between these conditions is clinically complex and crucial because the treatment of these conditions differs significantly.

In patients without a history of lumbar surgery, lipoma, fibroma, or other soft tissue tumors are among the possible diagnoses. Lumbar hernias can often be mistaken, under certain circumstances, for hematomas or abscesses [1].

The present case is noteworthy because the clinical presentation closely mimicked recurrent renal colic, initially orienting the diagnostic workup toward a urological etiology. Similar diagnostic pitfalls have been described in previous reports, where lumbar hernias presented with flank pain in the absence of a palpable mass. This highlights the importance of considering lumbar hernia in patients with recurrent or atypical lumbar pain when routine urological investigations are negative.

CT is the gold standard for diagnosing lumbar hernia, enabling precise evaluation of the defect, its width, the involved musculoaponeurotic layers, and the contents of the hernia sac. These may include extraperitoneal fat alone, fat associated with retroperitoneal organs such as the kidney or colon, or intraperitoneal structures, most commonly small bowel, with variable volume between patients. Abdominal wall discontinuity, herniated adipose and/or visceral components, and associated tissue alterations can be accurately characterized.

Cross-sectional imaging also plays a key role in complicated presentations, particularly in cases of incarceration or strangulation, where hernia contents may appear thickened, heterogeneous, and suggestive of ischemia [6].

In practice, a noncontrast acquisition is usually sufficient when the sac contains only fat, whereas a portal venous phase with intravenous contrast is reserved for suspected complications—particularly strangulation—to evaluate bowel wall enhancement and ischemia; routine contrast administration is therefore not systematically required.

Dynamic acquisition with the Valsalva maneuver increases intra-abdominal pressure and enhances hernia conspicuity, improving visualization in 73%-82% of cases and revealing up to 14%-17% of otherwise occult defects . However, for lower lumbar (Petit’s triangle) hernias, which are typically large and clinically apparent, standard acquisition is generally sufficient and routine Valsalva imaging is not recommended [7].

A normal CT examination of the lumbar region effectively excludes lumbar hernia and redirects evaluation toward alternative causes. This is particularly important in postoperative patients, in whom imaging helps differentiate true herniation from postsurgical changes or pain syndromes.

MRI, particularly in patients with contraindication to CT, may be used as a complementary modality for the evaluation of lumbar hernia due to its excellent soft-tissue contrast, allowing precise depiction of the abdominal wall defect and its contents. However, it should generally be reserved for situations in which CT cannot be performed, as it is more time-consuming and costlier without providing superior diagnostic performance in routine assessment.

Management of lumbar hernia does not invariably require surgical intervention. Although operative repair is frequently recommended because of the potential risk of complications, including enlargement, obstruction, or strangulation, asymptomatic or clinically occult hernias may often be managed with careful clinical surveillance. Surgical treatment is generally reserved for patients who develop symptoms or complications, with the primary aim of closing the hernial defect and restoring the structural integrity and functional stability of the abdominal wall [8,9].

Several surgical techniques have been described for lumbar hernia repair, including primary anatomical closure, musculofascial flaps, open mesh repair, and laparoscopic approaches [10]. No single technique has been universally accepted as the gold standard because the optimal approach depends on hernia size, location, and surgeon expertise. However, extraperitoneal mesh repair is widely considered an effective option for isolated unilateral lumbar hernia because it provides tension-free reinforcement of the abdominal wall defect [11].

Transabdominal laparoscopic repair with a preperitoneal prosthesis for a lumbar hernia offers all the advantages of minimally invasive surgery to the patient. It is a tension-free repair. It follows the current principle of hernia surgery and is based on the sound physiological principle of the diffusion of total intra-abdominal pressure over each square inch of the implanted prosthesis [11].

Conclusion

This case illustrates an uncommon presentation of Grynfeltt lumbar hernia mimicking recurrent renal colic. Because clinical findings may be nonspecific and misleading, lumbar hernia should be considered in the differential diagnosis of persistent or atypical flank pain. CT imaging remains the cornerstone of diagnosis by accurately identifying the abdominal wall defect, characterizing hernia contents, and detecting potential complications. Early recognition allows appropriate surgical management and may prevent incarceration or strangulation.
