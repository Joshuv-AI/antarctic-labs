Rank One Completion for Higher Order Tensors
Linghao Zhang, Ioana Dumitriu, Jiawang Nie

Abstract
. We study the rank one completion problem for tensors of arbitrary orders. The notion of rank one determinable tensors is introduced. We explore its properties and propose a recursive algorithm for computing rank one tensor completion. This algorithm only requires solving linear systems and computing singular vectors. In the absence of noise, it produces a unique rank one completion under some assumptions. In the presence of noise, we show that the computed rank one tensor completion is close to the exact one when the noise is sufficiently small. Numerical experiments demonstrate the efficiency and accuracy of the proposed method.

1.
Introduction

A tensor
\mathcal{A}
of order
m
(a positive integer) and dimension
(n_{1},\ldots,n_{m})
can be viewed as the multidimensional array such that

\mathcal{A}\,=\,(\mathcal{A}_{i_{1}\cdots i_{m}})_{1\leq i_{1}\leq n_{1},\ldots,1\leq i_{m}\leq n_{m}}.

We denote by
\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times\cdots\times n_{m}}
the space of real tensors as above.
A
rank one
tensor is of the form

u_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes u_{m}
for vectors
u_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}},\ldots,u_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{m}}
such that

(u_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes u_{m})_{i_{1}\cdots i_{m}}\,=\,(u_{1})_{i_{1}}\cdots(u_{m})_{i_{m}}.

The
rank
of
\mathcal{A}
is the smallest number of rank one tensors whose sum equals
\mathcal{A}
.
This is referred to as the Candecomp-Parafac (CP) decomposition in the literature
[
12
,
13
,
21
]
.
There are various notions of tensor ranks (e.g., border rank, multilinear rank),
and tensor ranks depend on the ground field.
Moreover, determining tensor ranks is NP-hard
[
11
]
.
We refer to
[
6
,
10
,
12
,
13
,
21
,
25
,
26
]
for related work on tensor decompositions and tensor ranks.

The
tensor completion problem
(TCP)
is to fill in missing entries of a partially observed tensor
such that the completed tensor has some properties (e.g., it has a low rank).
TCPs appear in a broad range of applications,
including recommendation systems
[
9
,
16
]
,
computer vision
[
29
,
30
]
,
imaging and signal processing
[
22
,
23
,
39
]
;
see also
[
19
]
for more applications.
In contrast to matrix completion problems, TCP is substantially more challenging.
There exists a variety of methods for low rank tensor completions,
including sum-of-squares relaxations
[
3
]
,
tensor nuclear norm relaxations

[
24
,
27
,
34
,
35
,
37
]
,
approaches based on CP and Tucker decompositions

[
1
,
2
,
30
,
39
]
,
Riemannian-manifold optimization

[
8
,
20
,
32
,
33
]
,
and methods based on tubal rank and t-SVD

[
14
,
17
,
36
,
38
]
.

This paper focuses on the special but fundamental case of rank one tensor completion.
Solving this problem sheds light on more general low rank completion problems,
as rank one tensors exhibit rich algebraic and geometric problems.
We consider an
m
th order partially observed tensor (p.o.t.)

\mathcal{A}
with the observation label set

\Omega\subseteq[n_{1}]\times\cdots\times[n_{m}]\quad\text{where}\quad[n_{k}]\coloneqq\{1,\ldots,n_{k}\},

such that
\mathcal{A}_{i_{1}\cdots i_{m}}
is observed (given) for all
(i_{1},\ldots,i_{m})\in\Omega
.
For convenience, we denote this p.o.t. by
(\mathcal{A},\Omega)
.
The rank one tensor completion problem is to look for vectors

u_{1},\ldots,u_{m}
such that

(1.1)

\mathcal{A}_{i_{1}\cdots i_{m}}\,=\,(u_{1})_{i_{1}}\cdots(u_{m})_{i_{m}}\quad\text{for all}\quad(i_{1},\ldots,i_{m})\in\Omega.

If such
u_{1},\ldots,u_{m}
exist,
(\mathcal{A},\Omega)
is said to be
rank one completable
,
and
u_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes u_{m}
is called a
rank one completion
for
(\mathcal{A},\Omega)
.
Moreover,
(\mathcal{A},\Omega)
is said to have a
unique
rank one completion if
for any two rank one completions

u_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes u_{m}
and
v_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes v_{m}

of
(\mathcal{A},\Omega)
,
there exist nonzero scalars
\alpha_{k}
such that

u_{k}=\alpha_{k}v_{k}
for
k=1,\ldots,m
.

The rank one tensor completion problem has been studied from several perspectives in the literature.
Kahle et al.
[
15
]
investigate its algebraic and geometric properties.
Some computational methods are proposed in
[
4
,
28
,
31
,
40
]
.
The work
[
31
]
studies uniqueness for rank one tensor completion,
and
[
4
]
gives a semidefinite programming (SDP) approach.
The work
[
40
]
is motivated by the following observation for cubic order tensors:
the rank one tensor
a\otimes b\otimes c
is a completion for
(\mathcal{A},\Omega)

if and only if the vectors
a
and
b
satisfy

(1.2)

\det\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{A}_{ijk}&a_{i}b_{j}\\
\mathcal{A}_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}k}&a_{i^{\prime}}b_{j^{\prime}}\\
\end{bmatrix}=0

for all
(i,j,k),(i^{\prime},j^{\prime},k)\in\Omega
.
Equivalently, if we let
X=ab^{T}
, then

(1.3)

\mathcal{A}_{ijk}X_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}-\mathcal{A}_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}k}X_{ij}=0\quad\text{for all}\quad(i,j,k),(i^{\prime},j^{\prime},k)\in\Omega.

This gives a system of linear equations in

X_{\bar{\Omega}}\coloneqq(X_{ij})_{(i,j)\in\bar{\Omega}},

where
\bar{\Omega}
denotes the projection of
\Omega
onto the first two indices.
When the solution space of (
1.3
) is one-dimensional,
the partial matrix
X_{\bar{\Omega}}
can be determined uniquely up to scaling.
If
(\mathcal{A},\Omega)
is rank one completable,
then
X_{\bar{\Omega}}
can be completed to a full matrix
X
of rank one.
Then, we can obtain vectors
a,b
(up to scaling) from
X
,
and thereby
c
from
\mathcal{A}=a\otimes b\otimes c
on
\Omega
.
For such a case,
(\mathcal{A},\Omega)
is said to be strongly rank one completable in
[
40
]
.
However, this approach is specific to cubic order tensors
and does not extend naturally to the higher order case.
Moreover, it is not applicable in the presence of noise.
The follow-up work
[
28
]
considers noisy rank one completion for cubic order tensors.
It formulates the problem as biquadratic optimization with sphere constraints and solves it by an SDP relaxation.
This approach can be effective in practice,
but the biquadratic optimization may not always be solved globally by that relaxation.

Contributions

In this paper, we propose an efficient approach for completing rank one tensors of arbitrary orders.
Let
(\mathcal{A},\Omega)
be a p.o.t. of order
m
.
Suppose it admits a rank one completion

\mathcal{A}=u_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes u_{m}.

The tensor
\mathcal{A}
can be flattened into a matrix in various ways.
For convenience of description, we flatten
\mathcal{A}
along the first
m-1
indices,
so it becomes a rank one matrix

A=ab^{T},

where
a
is the vectorization of the tensor product
u_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes u_{m-1}
and
b=u_{m}
.
In this way, the p.o.t.
(\mathcal{A},\Omega)
induces a partially observed matrix
(A,\Omega)
.
As shown in Section
2
,
the vector
a
must satisfy a homogeneous linear system

(1.4)

Bx\,=\,0,

where
B
depends on
(\mathcal{A},\Omega)
but not on
b
.
Assume the solution space of (
1.4
) is one-dimensional,
then
a
can be determined uniquely up to scaling.
When the observed tensor entries are exact,
we can recover
a
from a nontrivial solution
x^{*}
to (
1.4
).
When these entries have noise, (
1.4
) may not have a nontrivial solution,
but we can instead select
x^{*}
as the right singular vector corresponding to the smallest singular value of
B
.
Once
x^{*}
is computed, the vector
b
can be recovered from the linear equation

A_{ij}=x^{*}_{i}b_{j}

on
\Omega
;
that is,
u_{m}
is obtained.
Next, we reshape
x^{*}
to a p.o.t. of order
m-1
, say,
(\mathcal{A}_{1},\Omega_{1})
.
We apply the above procedure to
(\mathcal{A}_{1},\Omega_{1})
,
and this yields the vector
u_{m-1}
.
Repeating this process recursively, we obtain vectors
u_{m-2},\ldots,u_{1}
,
which give a rank one completion.

Our new method has several advantages.
First, it applies to tensors of arbitrary orders.
Second, it remains robust in the presence of noise.
Third, our method only requires solving linear systems and computing singular vectors,
hence it is very efficient for large-scale problems.
Our main contributions are:

•

We introduce the notion of rank one
extractability
for partially observed matrices
and study its properties.

•

We extend the rank one matrix extractability to higher order tensors
by introducing the notion of rank one
determinability
.
The properties of rank one determinable tensors are studied.

•

Based on the above,
we propose a recursive algorithm (Algorithm
4.1
) for completing rank one tensors of order
m
.
It produces the chain

(\mathcal{A}_{0},\Omega_{0})\longrightarrow(\mathcal{A}_{1},\Omega_{1})\longrightarrow(\mathcal{A}_{2},\Omega_{2})\longrightarrow\cdots\longrightarrow(\mathcal{A}_{m-1},\Omega_{m-1}),

where each
\mathcal{A}_{j}
is a p.o.t. of order
m-j

(see Section
4
).

•

Our algorithm only requires solving linear systems and computing singular vectors,
and we prove that it is robust under noise.

The paper is organized as follows.
Section
2
introduces rank one extractable matrices
and studies the properties.
In Section
3
,
we define rank one determinability through tensor flattenings
and explore its connection with rank one completion.
Section
4
proposes a recursive algorithm for completing rank one tensors of arbitrary orders.
Section
5
gives perturbation analysis under noise.
Numerical experiments are presented in Section
6
.

2.
Rank one extractable matrices

For a partially observed matrix (p.o.m.)
A\coloneqq(A_{ij})\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}
,
let
\Omega\subseteq[n_{1}]\times[n_{2}]
be the set of indices
(i,j)

such that the entry
A_{ij}
is observed.
Denote

(2.1)

S_{1}=\{i:(i,j)\in\Omega\},\quad S_{2}=\{j:(i,j)\in\Omega\}.

We write
\Omega=\cup_{j\in S_{2}}\Theta_{j}
where

(2.2)

\Theta_{j}=\{(i,j):(i,j)\in\Omega\}=\{(i_{1},j),(i_{2},j),\ldots,(i_{m_{j}},j)\},

where
m_{j}=|\Theta_{j}|
is the cardinality of
\Theta_{j}
.
If
(A,\Omega)
is rank one completable, there exist
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}},y\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}}

such that
A_{ij}=x_{i}y_{j}
for all
(i,j)\in\Omega
.
Then,

\begin{bmatrix}A_{i_{1}j}\\
\vdots\\
A_{i_{m_{j}}j}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}x_{i_{1}}\\
\vdots\\
x_{i_{m_{j}}}\end{bmatrix}y_{j}\quad\implies\quad\mbox{rank}\begin{bmatrix}A_{i_{1}j}&x_{i_{1}}\\
\vdots&\vdots\\
A_{i_{m_{j}}j}&x_{i_{m_{j}}}\end{bmatrix}\leq 1.

This implies the 2-by-2 minor

A_{i_{s}j}x_{i_{t}}-A_{i_{t}j}x_{i_{s}}=0

for all
1\leq s<t\leq m_{j}
.
This gives

\binom{m_{j}}{2}

linear equations for each
j\in S_{2}
,
and there are

\sum_{j\in S_{2}}\binom{m_{j}}{2}

equations in total.
These equations are homogeneous in

x\,\coloneqq\,(x_{i})_{i\in S_{1}}.

The set of solutions to these linear equations is a subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{S_{1}}
.
We are interested in the case that the solution space is one-dimensional.

Definition 2.1
.

A p.o.m.
(A,\Omega)
is said to be rank one extractable if the solution space of
the following system in
x\in\mathbb{R}^{S_{1}}
is one-dimensional:

(2.3)

A_{ij}x_{i^{\prime}}-A_{i^{\prime}j}x_{i}=0,\quad(i,j),(i^{\prime},j)\in\Omega,\quad i<i^{\prime},\quad j\in S_{2}.

Observe that the system (
2.3
) can be equivalently written as

(2.4)

Bx\,=\,0

for some sparse matrix
B
,
each row of which has at most two nonzero entries.

Example 2.2
.

Consider the p.o.m.
A\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
with observed entries:

A_{11}=2,\quad A_{12}=-4,\quad A_{13}=6,\quad A_{21}=3,\quad A_{22}=-6,\quad A_{31}=-1.

We have
\Omega=\{(1,1),(1,2),(1,3),(2,1),(2,2),(3,1)\}
and

S_{1}=S_{2}=\{1,2,3\}
.
The nullspace of
B
as in (
2.4
) is one-dimensional,
so
(A,\Omega)
is rank one extractable.

For a subset
\Omega\subseteq[n_{1}]\times[n_{2}]
,
let
G(V_{1},V_{2},\Omega)
be the bipartite graph with vertex sets

V_{1}=\{1,\ldots,n_{1}\}
,
V_{2}=\{1,\ldots,n_{2}\}
and the edge set
\Omega
.
For convenience, we say a vector is
entirely nonzero
if all its entries are nonzero.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
(A,\Omega)
be a p.o.m. such that
A_{ij}\neq 0
for all
(i,j)\in\Omega
.

(i)

Suppose
(A,\Omega)
is rank one completable.
Then
(A,\Omega)
has a unique rank one completion if and only if the graph
G(V_{1},V_{2},\Omega)
is connected.

(ii)

If (
2.3
) has an entirely nonzero solution
\hat{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{S_{1}}
, then
there exists an extension
a\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}
of
\hat{x}
(i.e.,
\hat{x}
is a subvector of
a
) such that
A=ab^{T}
for some
b\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}}
, where all entries of
a,b
are nonzero.

(iii)

The p.o.m.
(A,\Omega)
is rank one completable if and only if (
2.3
) has an entirely nonzero solution.

(iv)

Suppose the graph
G(V_{1},V_{2},\Omega)
is connected.
Then every nontrivial solution to (
2.3
) is entirely nonzero.
If, in addition,
(A,\Omega)
is rank one completable, then
(A,\Omega)
is rank one extractable.

Proof.

(i) This conclusion can be found in
[
5
, Lemma 1]
and
[
18
]
.

(ii) Let
\hat{x}
be an entirely nonzero solution to (
2.3
).
For each
j\in S_{2}
, (
2.3
) implies

\mbox{rank}\begin{bmatrix}A_{i_{1}j}&\hat{x}_{i_{1}}\\
\vdots&\vdots\\
A_{i_{m_{j}}j}&\hat{x}_{i_{m_{j}}}\\
\end{bmatrix}=1\quad\implies\quad\begin{bmatrix}A_{i_{1}j}\\
\vdots\\
A_{i_{m_{j}}j}\\
\end{bmatrix}=\beta_{j}\begin{bmatrix}\hat{x}_{i_{1}}\\
\vdots\\
\hat{x}_{i_{m_{j}}}\\
\end{bmatrix},\,0\neq\beta_{j}\in\mathbb{R}.

Let
a\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}
and
b\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}}
be such that

\begin{gathered}a_{i}=\hat{x}_{i}\quad\text{for }i\in S_{1},\qquad b_{j}=\beta_{j}\quad\text{for }j\in S_{2},\\
a_{i}=1\quad\text{for }i\in[n_{1}]\setminus S_{1},\qquad b_{j}=1\quad\text{for }j\in[n_{2}]\setminus S_{2}.\end{gathered}

Then
A_{ij}=\hat{x}_{i}\beta_{j}=a_{i}b_{j}
on
\Omega
.
Hence,
A=ab^{T}
.

(iii) The “if” direction is shown in (ii).
Conversely,
if
(A,\Omega)
is rank one completable,
then there exist
a\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}
and
b\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}}
such that
A_{ij}=a_{i}b_{j}
on
\Omega
.
Let
\hat{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{S_{1}}
be a subvector of
a
satisfying
\hat{x}_{i}=a_{i}
for all
i\in S_{1}
.
Then, for each
j\in S_{2}
,
(i,j),(i^{\prime},j)\in\Omega
,
i<i^{\prime}
, we have

A_{ij}\hat{x}_{i^{\prime}}-A_{i^{\prime}j}\hat{x}_{i}=(a_{i}a_{i^{\prime}}-a_{i^{\prime}}a_{i})b_{j}=0.

So,
\hat{x}
solves (
2.3
) and is entirely nonzero since
A_{ij}=a_{i}b_{j}\neq 0
for all
(i,j)\in\Omega
.

(iv) Let
x
be a nontrivial solution to (
2.3
).
For each
j\in S_{2}
, (
2.3
) implies

\mbox{rank}\begin{bmatrix}A_{i_{1}j}&x_{i_{1}}\\
\vdots&\vdots\\
A_{i_{m_{j}}j}&x_{i_{m_{j}}}\\
\end{bmatrix}=1\quad\implies\quad\begin{bmatrix}x_{i_{1}}\\
\vdots\\
x_{i_{m_{j}}}\\
\end{bmatrix}=\alpha_{j}\begin{bmatrix}A_{i_{1}j}\\
\vdots\\
A_{i_{m_{j}}j}\\
\end{bmatrix},\,\alpha_{j}\in\mathbb{R}.

This means that for the above
j
,
x_{i}=\alpha_{j}A_{ij}
for all
i
with
(i,j)\in\Omega
.
Suppose otherwise
x
has a zero entry, say

x_{i_{1}}=0
for some
i_{1}\in S_{1}
.
Then, for every
j
with
(i_{1},j)\in\Omega
, we have

(2.5)

0=x_{i_{1}}=\alpha_{j}A_{i_{1}j}\implies\alpha_{j}=0

since
A_{i_{1}j}\neq 0
.
Since
G(V_{1},V_{2},\Omega)
is connected, there exists a path

i_{1}\to j_{1}\to i_{2}\to j_{2}\to i_{3}\to j_{3}\to\cdots\to j_{N-1}\to i_{N}

for some
N\geq|S_{1}|
such that
S_{1}\subseteq\{i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{N}\}
and

(i_{k},j_{k}),(i_{k+1},j_{k})\in\Omega,\quad k=1,\ldots,N-1.

We remark that
i_{k},j_{k}
may be repeated.
By (
2.5
), we have

0=x_{i_{1}}=\alpha_{j_{1}}A_{i_{1}j_{1}}
,
which implies
\alpha_{j_{1}}=0
.
Since
(i_{2},j_{1})\in\Omega
, we get
x_{i_{2}}=\alpha_{j_{1}}A_{i_{2}j_{1}}=0
.
Repeating this argument, we can similarly get

x_{i_{3}}=\cdots=x_{i_{N}}=0
.
Hence,
x=0
, which contradicts that
x
is a nontrivial solution to (
2.3
).
Therefore, every nontrivial solution to (
2.3
) is entirely nonzero.

Moreover, if
(A,\Omega)
is rank one completable, then by (iii),
(
2.3
) has an entirely nonzero solution
\hat{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{S_{1}}
.
Suppose there exists another nontrivial solution
\tilde{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{S_{1}}
to (
2.3
).
By the first part of (iv),
\tilde{x}
is entirely nonzero.
So by (ii), there exists an extension of
\hat{x}
(resp.,
\tilde{x}
),
say
\hat{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}
(resp.,
\tilde{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}
),
such that
A_{ij}=\hat{a}_{i}\hat{b}_{j}=\tilde{a}_{i}\tilde{b}_{j}
on
\Omega
,
for some
\hat{b},\tilde{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}}
.
Since
G(V_{1},V_{2},\Omega)
is connected, (i) implies that
(A,\Omega)
has a unique rank one completion.
Then there exists a scalar
\tau\neq 0
such that

\tilde{a}=\tau\hat{a}
and
\tilde{b}=\tau^{-1}\hat{b}
.
Hence,
\tilde{x}=\tau\hat{x}
.
This means (
2.3
) has a unique nontrivial solution up to scaling,
i.e.,
(A,\Omega)
is rank one extractable.
∎

We make the following remarks:

•

If
(A,\Omega)
is rank one extractable, then
(A,\Omega)
may not be rank one completable.
Consider the p.o.m.
A\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
with observed entries:

A_{11}=1
,
A_{21}=0
,
A_{22}=1
.
The solution space of (
2.3
) is one-dimensional,
so
(A,\Omega)
is rank one extractable,
but
(A,\Omega)
is not rank one completable.

•

If
(A,\Omega)
is rank one extractable, then
(A,\Omega)
may not have a unique rank one completion
even if it is rank one completable.
Consider the p.o.m.
A\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
with observed entries:

A_{11}=1
,
A_{21}=2
.
The solution space of (
2.3
) is one-dimensional.
This means
(A,\Omega)
is rank one extractable.
However,
(A,\Omega)
has infinitely many rank one completions.
For instance,
A=ab^{T}
with
a=(1,2)
and
b=(1,t)
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

•

The rank one extractability does not imply connectedness of
G(V_{1},V_{2},\Omega)

or vice versa.
As shown in the above item,
(A,\Omega)
is rank one extractable,
but
G(V_{1},V_{2},\Omega)
is not connected (the vertex
2\in V_{2}
is isolated).
Conversely, consider the p.o.m.
A\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
with observed entries:

\begin{array}[]{cccccc}A_{11}=1,&A_{12}=1,&A_{21}=2,&A_{22}=3,&A_{23}=4,&A_{32}=5.\end{array}

Then,
\Omega=\{(1,1),(1,2),(2,1),(2,2),(2,3),(3,2)\}

and
G(V_{1},V_{2},\Omega)
is connected.
However, one can check that the solution space of (
2.3
) is zero-dimensional,
i.e.,
(A,\Omega)
is not rank one extractable.

The above shows that there is no direct implication among rank one completability,
rank one extractability, and the connectedness of
G(V_{1},V_{2},\Omega)
.
However, they are equivalent under some assumptions.

Definition 2.4
.

For
\Omega\subseteq[n_{1}]\times[n_{2}]
, we say that
\Omega

is row-full if
S_{1}=[n_{1}]
, and
\Omega
is column-full if
S_{2}=[n_{2}]
.

We remark that

\Omega
is row-full (resp., column full) if and only if there is no isolated vertex in
V_{1}
(resp.,
V_{2}
)
in the bipartite graph
G(V_{1},V_{2},\Omega)
.

Theorem 2.5
.

Let
(A,\Omega)
be a p.o.m. such that
A_{ij}\neq 0
for all
(i,j)\in\Omega
.

(i)

Suppose the graph
G(V_{1},V_{2},\Omega)
is connected.
Then,
(A,\Omega)
is rank one extractable if and only if
(A,\Omega)
is rank one completable.

(ii)

Suppose
\Omega
is row-full and column-full, and

(A,\Omega)
is rank one completable.
Then, the following are equivalent:

(a)

(A,\Omega)
is rank one extractable;

(b)

(A,\Omega)
has a unique rank one completion;

(c)

the graph
G(V_{1},V_{2},\Omega)
is connected.

Proof.

(i) Suppose
G(V_{1},V_{2},\Omega)
is connected.
If
(A,\Omega)
is rank one extractable, then (
2.3
) has a unique nontrivial solution
(up to scaling),
say,
\hat{x}
.
Then Theorem
2.3
(iv) implies that
\hat{x}
is entirely nonzero.
By Theorem
2.3
(ii),
(A,\Omega)
is rank one completable.
Conversely, if
(A,\Omega)
is rank one completable, then
(A,\Omega)
is rank one extractable by Theorem
2.3
(iv).

(ii)
(a)\Rightarrow(b)
:
Let
A=ab^{T}
be a rank one completion.
Since
(A,\Omega)
is rank one extractable and
\Omega
is row-full,

a
is the unique (up to scaling) solution to (
2.3
).
Then,
b
is also unique since
\Omega
is column-full.
So,
(A,\Omega)
has a unique rank one completion.

(b)\Rightarrow(c)
:
Since
(A,\Omega)
has a unique rank one completion,

G(V_{1},V_{2},\Omega)
is connected by Theorem
2.3
(i).

(c)\Rightarrow(a)
: This is implied by item (i).
∎

We remark that in Definition
2.1
,
the rank one extractability of
(A,\Omega)
is defined by enumeration over the index
j
.
Similarly, one can also consider enumeration over the index
i
.
This is related to the rank one extractability of
(A^{T},\Omega^{\prime})
for

(2.6)

\Omega^{\prime}=\{(j,i):(i,j)\in\Omega\},\quad S_{1}^{\prime}=S_{2},\quad S_{2}^{\prime}=S_{1}.

However, the rank one extractability of
(A,\Omega)
does not imply
the rank one extractability of
(A^{T},\Omega^{\prime})
.
This is shown by the following example.

Example 2.6
.

Consider the p.o.m.
A\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
with observed entries:

A_{11}=A_{12}=1
,
A_{21}=A_{23}=A_{31}=0
.
Then
\Omega=\{(1,1),(1,2),(2,1),(2,3),(3,1)\}
.
The solution space of (
2.3
) is one-dimensional, so

(A,\Omega)
is rank one extractable.
However, the solution space of (
2.3
) for
(A^{T},\Omega^{\prime})

is two-dimensional, so
(A^{T},\Omega^{\prime})
is not rank one extractable.

Proposition 2.7
.

Let
(A,\Omega)
be a p.o.m. such that
A_{ij}\neq 0
for all
(i,j)\in\Omega
.

(i)

Suppose
(A,\Omega)
is rank one completable.
Then,
(A,\Omega)
is rank one extractable if and only if
(A^{T},\Omega^{\prime})
is rank one extractable.

(ii)

Suppose the graph
G(V_{1},V_{2},\Omega)
is connected.
Then,
(A,\Omega)
is rank one extractable if and only if
(A^{T},\Omega^{\prime})
is rank one extractable.

Proof.

(i) Without loss of generality, we can assume
\Omega
is row-full and column-full.
For the “only if” direction, we know the graph
G(V_{1},V_{2},\Omega)
is connected by Theorem
2.5
(ii).
This implies
G(V_{2},V_{1},\Omega^{\prime})
is also connected.
Since
(A,\Omega)
is rank one completable,
(A^{T},\Omega^{\prime})
is also rank one completable.
By Theorem
2.5
(i),
(A^{T},\Omega^{\prime})
is rank one extractable.
The proof for the “if” direction is the same.

(ii) Since
G(V_{1},V_{2},\Omega)
is connected,
by Theorem
2.5
(i),
we know

(A,\Omega)
is rank one extractable if and only if
(A,\Omega)
is rank one completable.
Note that
G(V_{2},V_{1},\Omega^{\prime})
is also connected,
and
(A,\Omega)
being rank one completable is equivalent to
(A^{T},\Omega^{\prime})
being rank one completable.
So, the conclusion follows from item (i).
∎

3.
Rank one determinable tensors

The notion of rank one extractable matrices in Section
2
can be extended to tensors.
For an integer
k\in[m]
, denote

(3.1)

\displaystyle\vec{i}

\displaystyle\coloneqq

\displaystyle\big(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k-1},i_{k+1},\ldots,i_{m}\big),

(3.2)

\displaystyle I_{k}

\displaystyle\coloneqq

\displaystyle\big\{\vec{i}:1\leq i_{l}\leq n_{l}\text{ for }l\neq k\big\}.

Denote by
\mathbb{R}^{I_{k}\times[n_{k}]}
the space of matrices with rows indexed by
\vec{i}\in I_{k}

and columns indexed by
j\in[n_{k}]
.
For
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times\cdots\times n_{m}}
,
let
\mbox{Flat}_{k}(\mathcal{A})\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{k}\times[n_{k}]}
be such that

(3.3)

\big(\mbox{Flat}_{k}(\mathcal{A})\big)_{\vec{i}j}\,\coloneqq\,\mathcal{A}_{i_{1}\cdots i_{k-1}ji_{k+1}\cdots i_{m}}

with
\vec{i}\in I_{k}
and
j\in[n_{k}]
.
The matrix
\mbox{Flat}_{k}(\mathcal{A})
is called the
k
th
flattening matrix
of
\mathcal{A}
.
We refer to
[
19
]
for tensor flattening (or matricization).

For
\Omega\subseteq[n_{1}]\times\cdots\times[n_{m}]
,
define the subset of
I_{k}\times[n_{k}]
:

(3.4)

\Omega^{(k)}\,\coloneqq\,\{(\vec{i},j):(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k-1},j,i_{k+1},\ldots,i_{m})\in\Omega\}.

When
\mathcal{A}
is partially observed on
\Omega
,
the matrix
\mbox{Flat}_{k}(\mathcal{A})
is partially observed on
\Omega^{(k)}
.
Denote the index sets

(3.5)

S^{(k)}_{1}=\big\{\vec{i}:(\vec{i},j)\in\Omega^{(k)}\big\},\quad S^{(k)}_{2}=\big\{j:(\vec{i},j)\in\Omega^{(k)}\big\}.

Denote the vector variable

x\,\coloneqq\,(x_{\vec{i}})_{\vec{i}\in S_{1}^{(k)}}.

Like (
2.3
),
we consider the linear system

(3.6)

\big(\mbox{Flat}_{k}(\mathcal{A})\big)_{\vec{i}j}\,x_{\vec{i^{\prime}}}-\big(\mbox{Flat}_{k}(\mathcal{A})\big)_{\vec{i^{\prime}}j}\,x_{\vec{i}}\,=\,0

for all

(\vec{i},j),(\vec{i^{\prime}},j)\in\Omega^{(k)}
,
\vec{i}<_{lex}\vec{i^{\prime}}
,
j\in S^{(k)}_{2}
.
Here,
<_{lex}
denotes the standard lexicographical ordering.
Equivalently, (
3.6
) reads as

\mathcal{A}_{i_{1}\cdots i_{k-1}ji_{k+1}\cdots i_{m}}\,x_{\vec{i^{\prime}}}-\mathcal{A}_{i^{\prime}_{1}\cdots i^{\prime}_{k-1}ji^{\prime}_{k+1}\cdots i^{\prime}_{m}}\,x_{\vec{i}}\,=\,0,

where
\vec{i^{\prime}}=(i^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,i^{\prime}_{k-1},i^{\prime}_{k+1},\ldots,i^{\prime}_{m})
.
The system (
3.6
) is equivalent to

(3.7)

B^{(k)}x\,=\,0

for some matrix
B^{(k)}
.
Denote the label set

(3.8)

\mathcal{P}_{k}\,\coloneqq\,[n_{1}]\times\cdots\times[n_{k-1}]\times[n_{k+1}]\times\cdots\times[n_{m}].

For
x\in\mathbb{R}^{S_{1}^{(k)}}
,
let
\mathcal{T}_{k}(x)
be the p.o.t. in

\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{P}_{k}}

such that

(3.9)

\mathcal{T}_{k}(x)_{i_{1}\cdots i_{k-1}i_{k+1}\cdots i_{m}}\,=\,x_{\vec{i}}

for all
\vec{i}\in S^{(k)}_{1}
.
Define the reshaping map

(3.10)

\Gamma_{k}:\mathbb{R}^{I_{k}}\to\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{P}_{k}},\qquad u\mapsto\Gamma_{k}(u)

such that for all
\vec{i}\in I_{k}
as in (
3.1
),

\Gamma_{k}(u)_{i_{1}\cdots i_{k-1}i_{k+1}\cdots i_{m}}\,=\,u_{\vec{i}}.

The above
\Gamma_{k}(u)
is a full tensor of order
m-1
.

Definition 3.1
.

The
m
th order p.o.t.
(\mathcal{A},\Omega)
is rank one determinable if:

(i)

When
m=2
,
(\mathcal{A},\Omega)
or
(\mathcal{A}^{T},\Omega^{\prime})
is rank one extractable,
where
\Omega^{\prime}
is defined as in (
2.6
).

(ii)

When
m\geq 3
, there exists
k\in[m]
such that

\big(\mbox{Flat}_{k}(\mathcal{A}),\Omega^{(k)}\big)
is rank one extractable,
and the unique (up to scaling) nontrivial solution
x^{*}
to (
3.6
) satisfies that

\big(\mathcal{T}_{k}(x^{*}),S_{1}^{(k)}\big)

In the above,
\mathcal{T}_{k}(x^{*})
is a p.o.t. of order
(m-1)
partially observed on
S_{1}^{(k)}
.
Now we give some examples of rank one determinable tensors.

Example 3.2
.

[

]

\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3\times 3}

\begin{array}[]{llll}\mathcal{A}_{111}=-1,&\mathcal{A}_{221}=-1,&\mathcal{A}_{311}=-1,&\mathcal{A}_{132}=1,\\

Definition 3.4
.

For
t\in[m]
, we say that
\Omega
is mod-
t
full if

\{i_{t}:(i_{1},\ldots,i_{m})\in\Omega\}\,=\,\{1,\ldots,n_{t}\}.

Theorem 3.5
.

Assume
\Omega
is mod-
t
full for every
t\in[m]
and

\mathcal{A}_{i_{1}\cdots i_{m}}\neq 0
for all
(i_{1},\ldots,i_{m})\in\Omega
.
If
(\mathcal{A},\Omega)
is rank one determinable,
then
(\mathcal{A},\Omega)
has a unique rank one completion when it is rank one completable.

Proof.

We prove by induction on
m
.
For the base step
m=2
, the conclusion is implied by Theorem
2.5
(ii).
Now assume the conclusion holds for the order
m-1
.
Suppose
\mathcal{A}
has two rank one completions:

u_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes u_{m}
,
v_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes v_{m}
.
We need to show that each
u_{k}
is a multiple of
v_{k}
.
Since
(\mathcal{A},\Omega)
is rank one determinable, there exists
k\in[m]
such that
(\mbox{Flat}_{k}(\mathcal{A}),\Omega^{(k)})
is rank one extractable.
The above implies
\mbox{Flat}_{k}(\mathcal{A})
has two rank one completions

(3.11)

\begin{array}[]{l}\Gamma_{k}^{-1}(u_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes u_{k-1}\otimes u_{k+1}\otimes\cdots\otimes u_{m})\cdot u_{k}^{T},\\
\Gamma_{k}^{-1}(v_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes v_{k-1}\otimes v_{k+1}\otimes\cdots\otimes v_{m})\cdot v_{k}^{T}.\end{array}

Observe that the two vectors

\begin{array}[]{l}U\,\coloneqq\,\big(\Gamma_{k}^{-1}(u_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes u_{k-1}\otimes u_{k+1}\otimes\cdots\otimes u_{m})\big)_{S_{1}^{(k)}},\\
V\,\coloneqq\,\big(\Gamma_{k}^{-1}(v_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes v_{k-1}\otimes v_{k+1}\otimes\cdots\otimes v_{m})\big)_{S_{1}^{(k)}}\end{array}

are both solutions to (
3.6
).
Since
\mathcal{A}_{i_{1}\cdots i_{m}}\neq 0
on
\Omega
,
it is clear that
U,V
are entirely nonzero.
Since
(\mbox{Flat}_{k}(\mathcal{A}),\Omega^{(k)})
is rank one extractable,
the solution space of (
3.6
) is one-dimensional.
So,
U=\alpha_{k}V
for some scalar
\alpha_{k}\neq 0
.
Note that (
3.11
) implies

U_{\vec{i}}\cdot(u_{k})_{j}\,=\,V_{\vec{i}}\cdot(v_{k})_{j}\quad\text{for all }\quad(\vec{i},j)\in\Omega^{(k)},

so
(u_{k})_{j}=\alpha_{k}^{-1}(v_{k})_{j}
for all
j\in S_{2}^{(k)}
.
Since
\Omega
is mod-
k
full,
S_{2}^{(k)}=[n_{k}]
,
hence
u_{k}=\alpha_{k}^{-1}v_{k}
.
Let
x^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{S_{1}^{(k)}}
be the unique (up to scaling) solution to (
3.6
), then

x^{*}=\beta_{1}U=\beta_{2}V

for nonzero scalars
\beta_{1},\beta_{2}
.
Observe that the p.o.t.

\mathcal{T}_{k}(x^{*})=\beta_{1}\mathcal{T}_{k}(U)=\beta_{2}\mathcal{T}_{k}(V)

has two rank one completions

\beta_{1}u_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes u_{k-1}\otimes u_{k+1}\otimes\cdots\otimes u_{m},\qquad\beta_{2}v_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes v_{k-1}\otimes v_{k+1}\otimes\cdots\otimes v_{m}.

Note that
(\mathcal{T}_{k}(x^{*}),S_{1}^{(k)})
is a p.o.t. of order
m-1
.
Since
S_{1}^{(k)}
is mod-
t
full for all
t\in[m]\setminus\{k\}

and
\mathcal{T}_{k}(x^{*})_{\vec{i}}\neq 0
for all
\vec{i}\in S_{1}^{(k)}
,
by the inductive hypothesis,
\mathcal{T}_{k}(x^{*})
has a unique rank one completion,
so there exist nonzero scalars
\alpha_{l}
such that

\beta_{1}u_{1}=\alpha_{1}\beta_{2}v_{1},\qquad u_{l}=\alpha_{l}v_{l}\quad\text{for}\quad l\in[m]\setminus\{1,k\}.

Also note
u_{k}=\alpha_{k}^{-1}v_{k}
.
This means the rank one completion for
(\mathcal{A},\Omega)
is unique.
∎

Under the assumption of Theorem
3.5
,
we prove that if
(\mathcal{A},\Omega)
is rank one determinable,
then it has a unique rank one completion.
However, the converse is not true, as shown in the following example.

Example 3.6
.

Consider the p.o.t.
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{3\times 3\times 4}
with observed entries:

\begin{array}[]{l}\mathcal{A}_{111}=\mathcal{A}_{221}=\mathcal{A}_{331}=\mathcal{A}_{132}=\mathcal{A}_{212}=\mathcal{A}_{322}=1,\\
\mathcal{A}_{123}=\mathcal{A}_{233}=\mathcal{A}_{313}=\mathcal{A}_{124}=\mathcal{A}_{214}=1.\end{array}

The solution space of (
3.6
) is three-dimensional for
k=1,2

and two-dimensional for
k=3
.
So,
(\mathcal{A},\Omega)
is not rank one determinable.
However, it has a unique rank one completion.
To see this, suppose (up to scaling)

\mathcal{A}\,=\,(1,a,b)\otimes(1,c,d)\otimes(e,f,g,h)

for nonzero
a,b,c,d,e,f,g,h\in\mathbb{C}
.
Comparing observed entries, one can see that

\begin{gathered}e=1,\quad ac=bd=df=af=bcf=cg=adg=bg=ch=ah=1,\\
df=af,\quad cg=bg,\quad ch=ah,\quad ac=af,\quad ac=ah,\end{gathered}

which implies
a=b=c=d=f=h
.
So,
bcf=adg
forces
f=g
.
Thus, all these parameters are equal.
Since
ac=bcf=1
, all of them must equal one.
Therefore,
(\mathcal{A},\Omega)
has the unique rank one completion

(1,1,1)\otimes(1,1,1)\otimes(1,1,1,1).

4.
A robust algorithm for rank one completion

Let
(\mathcal{A},\Omega)
be a p.o.t. of order
m
.
We aim to find a rank one tensor
u_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes u_{m}

in
\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times\cdots\times n_{m}}

such that

(4.1)

\mathcal{A}_{i_{1}\cdots i_{m}}\,=\,(u_{1})_{i_{1}}\cdots(u_{m})_{i_{m}}\quad\text{for all}\quad(i_{1},\ldots,i_{m})\in\Omega.

In this section, we propose a recursive method to solve (
4.1
).

For
k\in[m]
,
consider the flattening matrix
\mbox{Flat}_{k}(\mathcal{A})
.
Note
(\mbox{Flat}_{k}(\mathcal{A}),\Omega^{(k)})
is a p.o.m.
with the observation label set
\Omega^{(k)}
given in (
3.4
).
Assume it has the rank one completion

\mbox{Flat}_{k}(\mathcal{A})=ab^{T}.

Then, the subvector (
S_{1}^{(k)}
is defined in (
3.5
))

(a)_{S_{1}^{(k)}}\,\coloneqq\,(a_{\vec{i}})_{\vec{i}\in S_{1}^{(k)}}

must be a solution to (
3.6
) and thereby a solution to

(4.2)

B^{(k)}x\,=\,0,

where
B^{(k)}
is given in (
3.7
).
If
(\mbox{Flat}_{k}(\mathcal{A}),\Omega^{(k)})
is rank one extractable,
then
\text{null}(B^{(k)})
is one-dimensional.
That is, (
4.2
) has a unique (up to scaling) nontrivial solution, say,
x^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{S_{1}^{(k)}}
.
Then, the vector
b
can be determined from

\big(\mbox{Flat}_{k}(\mathcal{A})\big)_{\vec{i}j}\,=\,a_{\vec{i}}b_{j}\quad\text{for all}\quad(\vec{i},j)\in\Omega^{(k)}.

As in (
3.9
), the vector
x^{*}
can be reshaped to the
(m-1)
st order p.o.t.

\mathcal{T}_{k}(x^{*})\,\in\,\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{P}_{k}}

with the observation label set
S_{1}^{(k)}
.
We can repeat the above procedure for the p.o.t.
(\mathcal{T}_{k}(x^{*}),S_{1}^{(k)})
.
This gives a recursive method to solve (
4.1
),
in which the tensor order decreases by one after each recursion.
This process terminates when we reach the matrix case (i.e., the order equals two).

We illustrate the above recursive procedure for
m=4
.
Suppose
(\mbox{Flat}_{4}(\mathcal{A}),\Omega^{(4)})
is rank one extractable.
Let
x^{(0)}\in\mathbb{R}^{S_{1}^{(4)}}
be a nontrivial solution to (
3.6
).
Then we determine
u_{4}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{4}}
such that

x^{(0)}u_{4}^{T}
gives a rank one matrix completion for
\mbox{Flat}_{4}(\mathcal{A})
.
Next, reshape
x^{(0)}
to obtain the third order p.o.t.

\mathcal{A}_{1}\coloneqq\mathcal{T}_{4}(x^{(0)})\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}

with the observation label set
\Omega_{1}\coloneqq S_{1}^{(4)}
.
Assume the p.o.m.
\mbox{Flat}_{3}(\mathcal{A}_{1})
is rank one extractable.
Let
x^{(1)}
be a nontrivial solution to (
3.6
) for the p.o.t.
\mathcal{A}_{1}
.
Then, determine
u_{3}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{3}}
such that
x^{(1)}u_{3}^{T}
gives a rank one matrix completion for

\mbox{Flat}_{3}(\mathcal{A}_{1})
.
Next, reshape
x^{(1)}
to obtain the p.o.m.

\mathcal{A}_{2}\coloneqq\mathcal{T}_{3}(x^{(1)})\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}

with the observation label set
\Omega_{2}
.
Assume
(\mathcal{A}_{2},\Omega_{2})
is rank one extractable.
Let
x^{(2)}
be a nontrivial solution to (
3.6
) for the p.o.m.
\mathcal{A}_{2}
.
Then, determine
u_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}}
such that
x^{(2)}u_{2}^{T}
gives a rank one matrix completion for
\mathcal{A}_{2}
.
Finally, let
u_{1}=x^{(2)}
.
Then, under some general assumptions (see Theorem
4.4
),
we get the rank one tensor completion

u_{1}\otimes u_{2}\otimes u_{3}\otimes u_{4}
for
(\mathcal{A},\Omega)
.

In computational practice, how do we choose
k
such that
(\mbox{Flat}_{k}(\mathcal{A}),\Omega^{(k)})
is rank one extractable?
This requires the nullspace of
B^{(k)}
to be one-dimensional.
However, when observed tensor entries have noise,

B^{(k)}
may have only trivial nullspace.
For such a case, we can estimate the nullspace as follows
(let
B^{(k)}
be
\ell
-by-
n
):

•

If
\ell\geq n
, then
B^{(k)}
has singular values

(4.3)

\sigma_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\sigma_{n}\geq 0.

We compute the right singular vector
v_{n}
of
B^{(k)}
corresponding to
\sigma_{n}
.
Then,
\mbox{span}\{v_{n}\}
is an approximation for the nullspace of
B^{(k)}
.

•

If
\ell<n
, then
B^{(k)}
must have a nontrivial nullspace,
and we compute
\text{null}(B^{(k)})
directly.

Summarizing the above, we now present a recursive algorithm that solves (
4.1
).

Algorithm 4.1
.

Let
(\mathcal{A},\Omega)
be a p.o.t. of order
m\geq 2
.
Do:

Step 1

For each
k\in[m]
,
formulate
B^{(k)}
as in (
3.7
).
Compute the minimum singular value of
B^{(k)}
, denoted by
\sigma_{\min}(B^{(k)})
.
Choose
k
such that

(4.4)

\sigma_{\min}(B^{(k)})\,=\,\min_{i\in[m]}\sigma_{\min}(B^{(i)}).

If the above
k
is not unique, choose the
k
for which the gap

\sigma_{n-1}-\sigma_{n}
is maximum, where
\sigma_{n},\sigma_{n-1}
are as in (
4.3
).

Step 2

Compute the vector
x^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{S_{1}^{(k)}}
as follows
(let
B^{(k)}
be
\ell
-by-
n
):

i)

If
\ell\geq n
, compute the right singular vector

v_{n}
of
B^{(k)}
corresponding to
\sigma_{\min}(B^{(k)})
.
Let
x^{*}=v_{n}
.

ii)

If
\ell<n
, directly solve
B^{(k)}x=0
for a nontrivial solution
x^{*}
.
Normalize
x^{*}
so that
\|x^{*}\|=1
.

Step 3

As in (
3.9
), reshape
x^{*}
to obtain the p.o.t.
(\mathcal{T}_{k}(x^{*}),S_{1}^{(k)})
of order
m-1
.

Step 4

If
m>2
, compute a rank one tensor completion for the p.o.t.
\mathcal{T}_{k}(x^{*})
as

(4.5)

u_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes u_{k-1}\otimes u_{k+1}\otimes\cdots\otimes u_{m}.

If
m=2
, then
\mathcal{T}_{k}(x^{*})
has order one (i.e., is a vector),
and
\mathcal{P}_{k}
is one-dimensional, say,
\mathcal{P}_{k}=[n_{t}]
for some
t
.
If
S_{1}^{(k)}\neq[n_{t}]
,
we extend
\mathcal{T}_{k}(x^{*})
to a full dimensional vector in
\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{P}_{k}}
.
Otherwise,
\mathcal{T}_{k}(x^{*})
is automatically a rank one completion for itself.

Step 5

Obtain
u_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{k}}
by solving the linear least squares problem

(4.6)

\min_{u}\quad\|\mathcal{A}-u_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes u_{k-1}\otimes u\otimes u_{k+1}\otimes\cdots\otimes u_{m}\|_{\Omega}.

Step 6

Output the rank one decomposing tuple
(u_{1},\ldots,u_{m})
.

.

In Step 4, Algorithm

again to the
(m-1)
st order p.o.t.
(\mathcal{A}_{1},\Omega_{1})
.
Let

be the vector

obtained from

in Step 2.

to the
(m-2)

Example 4.3
.

Consider the p.o.t.
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2\times 2\times 2\times 2}
with observed entries:

\mathcal{A}_{11111}=\mathcal{A}_{11112}=\mathcal{A}_{11222}=\mathcal{A}_{12111}=\mathcal{A}_{21211}=\mathcal{A}_{21222}=\mathcal{A}_{22121}=1.

When Algorithm

is applied recursively,
the chain of p.o.t.’s as in (

) is

\begin{array}[]{l}(\mathcal{A}_{1})_{1111}=(\mathcal{A}_{1})_{1122}=(\mathcal{A}_{1})_{1211}=(\mathcal{A}_{1})_{2121}=(\mathcal{A}_{1})_{2122}=(\mathcal{A}_{1})_{2212}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}},\\
(\mathcal{A}_{2})_{111}=(\mathcal{A}_{2})_{112}=(\mathcal{A}_{2})_{121}=(\mathcal{A}_{2})_{212}=(\mathcal{A}_{2})_{221}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{5}},\\
(\mathcal{A}_{3})_{11}=(\mathcal{A}_{3})_{12}=(\mathcal{A}_{3})_{21}=(\mathcal{A}_{3})_{22}=\frac{1}{2},\qquad(\mathcal{A}_{4})_{1}=(\mathcal{A}_{4})_{2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}.\end{array}

The obtained rank one completion is

\mathcal{A}=u_{1}\otimes u_{2}\otimes u_{3}\otimes u_{4}\otimes u_{5}
with

\begin{array}[]{c}u_{1}=u_{2}=\big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}},\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\big),\,\,u_{3}=\big(\frac{2}{\sqrt{5}},\frac{2}{\sqrt{5}}\big),\,\,u_{4}=\big(\sqrt{\frac{5}{6}},\sqrt{\frac{5}{6}}\big),\,\,u_{5}=(\sqrt{6},\sqrt{6}).\end{array}

Theorem 4.4
.

Assume
\Omega
is mod-
t
full for every
t\in[m]
and

\mathcal{A}_{i_{1}\cdots i_{m}}\neq 0
for all
(i_{1},\ldots,i_{m})\in\Omega
.
Suppose
(\mathcal{A},\Omega)
is rank one completable and rank one determinable.
Let
(\mathcal{A}_{j},\Omega_{j},k_{j})
be as in (
4.7
).
Assume
(\mbox{Flat}_{k_{j}}(\mathcal{A}_{j}),\Omega_{j}^{(k_{j})})
is rank one extractable for all

.
Then, the tuple

produced by Algorithm

gives the unique rank one completion

\mathcal{A}=u_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes u_{m}.

Proof.

We prove by induction on

that Algorithm
4.1
gives a rank one completion.
The uniqueness is implied by Theorem
3.5
.

For the base step
m=2
,
A\coloneqq\mathcal{A}
is a matrix.
Without loss of generality, we can assume
k_{0}=2
in Step 1.
Then the matrix
B^{(2)}
as in (
3.7
) is the same as the matrix
B
in (
2.4
).
Since
(A,\Omega)
is rank one extractable,
\text{null}(B)
is one-dimensional.
Then, for the vector
x^{*}
produced in Step 2,
we have
\text{null}(B)=\mbox{span}\{x^{*}\}
,
and
x^{*}
is a solution to (
2.3
).
By Theorem
2.5
(ii),
the graph

is connected.
So,
x^{*}
must be entirely nonzero by Theorem
2.3
(iv).
Note that
\mathcal{T}_{k_{0}}(x^{*})
is a vector, so
Step 4 produces

, as
\Omega
is row-full.
By Theorem
2.3
(ii),
there must exist

such that

.
This means

is a minimizer of

(4.9)

\min_{u}\quad\|A-u_{1}\otimes u\|_{\Omega},

and the minimum value is zero.
Note that
u_{2}
produced in Step 5 is also a solution to (
4.9
).
Since
\Omega
is column-full,

u_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}}
can be uniquely determined by

(u_{2})_{j}\,=\,A_{ij}/(u_{1})_{i}\quad\text{for all }(i,j)\in\Omega.

This means
u_{2}=v_{2}
.
Hence,

is a rank one completion for
(A,\Omega)
.

For the inductive step, assume the conclusion holds for the order
m-1
.
Since
(\mathcal{A}_{0},\Omega_{0})
is rank one completable, there exists a rank one completion

(4.10)

\mathcal{A}_{0}\,=\,v_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes v_{m}.

Recall that
\Omega_{1}=S_{1}^{(k_{0})}
as in (
3.5
).
Then, the vector

(4.11)

\hat{x}\,\coloneqq\,\big(\Gamma_{k_{0}}^{-1}(v_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes v_{k_{0}-1}\otimes v_{k_{0}+1}\otimes\cdots\otimes v_{m})\big)_{\Omega_{1}}

is a solution to (

), i.e.,

.
Since
\mathcal{A}_{i_{1}\cdots i_{m}}\neq 0
on
\Omega
,
it is clear that
\hat{x}
is entirely nonzero.
Since
(\mbox{Flat}_{k_{0}}(\mathcal{A}_{0}),\Omega_{0}^{(k_{0})})
is rank one extractable,

\text{null}(B^{(k_{0})})
is one-dimensional.
Hence, the vector
x^{(0)}=x^{*}
obtained in Step 2 satisfies

for some scalar
\alpha_{k_{0}}\neq 0
.
Step 3 reshapes
x^{(0)}
to the
(m-1)
st order p.o.t.

(4.12)

\mathcal{A}_{1}\coloneqq\mathcal{T}_{k_{0}}(x^{(0)})=\alpha_{k_{0}}\mathcal{T}_{k_{0}}(\hat{x}).

By (
4.11
) and (

),
(\mathcal{A}_{1},\Omega_{1})
has the rank one completion:

\alpha_{k_{0}}v_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes v_{k_{0}-1}\otimes v_{k_{0}+1}\otimes\cdots\otimes v_{m}.

By the given assumption,
(\mathcal{A}_{1},\Omega_{1})
is rank one determinable,

\Omega_{1}
is mod-
t
full for all

,
and all observed entries of

are nonzero.
By the inductive hypothesis, the algorithm gives the unique rank one completion

u_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes u_{k_{0}-1}\otimes u_{k_{0}+1}\otimes\cdots\otimes u_{m}

for
(\mathcal{A}_{1},\Omega_{1})
.
Hence, there exist scalars

such that

u_{l}=\alpha_{l}v_{l}\quad\text{for all}\quad l\in[m]\setminus\{k_{0}\}.

By (
4.10
) and (
4.13
),

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle=v_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes v_{k_{0}-1}\otimes v_{k_{0}}\otimes v_{k_{0}+1}\otimes\cdots\otimes v_{m}

\displaystyle=u_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes u_{k_{0}-1}\otimes u^{*}\otimes u_{k_{0}+1}\otimes\cdots\otimes u_{m},

where

So,

is a minimizer of (

), and the minimum value is zero.
Note that

produced in Step 5 is also a solution to (

Since

is mod-

Hence,

.

(\mathcal{A},\Omega)
be a p.o.t. of order

.

denotes the noise tensor.
Suppose

is rank one completable,

to the perturbation

.

.
Let
\tilde{k}_{0}\in[m]
be the index chosen in Step 1,
and let
\tilde{x}^{(0)}
be the vector

obtained from
\widetilde{B}_{0}^{(\tilde{k}_{0})}
in Step 2.
Up to a sign, we can generally assume

.
Step 3 produces the
(m-1)
st order p.o.t.

, and let

Again, apply Algorithm
4.1
to

.

be the index chosen in Step 1,

be the vector obtained from

in Step 2.

.
Step 3 produces the p.o.t.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
(\mathcal{A},\Omega)
be an
m
th order p.o.t. such that

\mathcal{A}=u_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes u_{m}

is the rank one completion given by Algorithm

.
Assume
(\mathcal{A},\Omega)
is rank one determinable,

\Omega
is mod-
t
full for every

, and

\mathcal{A}_{i_{1}\cdots i_{m}}\neq 0

.
Consider a perturbation
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}\coloneqq\mathcal{A}+\delta\mathcal{A}
on

.
Let
(\mathcal{A}_{j},\Omega_{j},k_{j})
and

) and (

For

be the output of Algorithm

(\widetilde{\mathcal{A}},\widetilde{\Omega})

(ii)

, depending on
(u_{1},\ldots,u_{m})
, such that

\displaystyle\|\tilde{u}_{t}-u_{t}\|\,\leq\,C_{1}\|\delta\mathcal{A}\|_{\Omega}\quad\text{for}\quad t=1,\ldots,m,

(5.4)

\displaystyle\|\tilde{u}_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\tilde{u}_{m}-u_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes u_{m}\|\,\leq\,C_{2}\|\delta\mathcal{A}\|_{\Omega}.

Proof.

(i) For

,
it is clear that
\widetilde{\Omega}_{0}=\widetilde{\Omega}=\Omega=\Omega_{0}
.
For

,
let

,

, depending on

\max_{i\in[m]}\|\delta B_{0}^{(i)}\|\,\leq\,\frac{\mu_{0}}{3},

when

By Weyl’s theorem
[

, Corollary 5.1]
, for all

,

be the index chosen in Step 1 of Algorithm

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\sigma_{\min}(\widetilde{B}_{0}^{(i)})&\geq&\sigma_{\min}(B_{0}^{(i)})-\|\delta B_{0}^{(i)}\|\\

\displaystyle\sigma_{\min}(\widetilde{B}_{0}^{(i)})

\displaystyle\,\geq\,

\displaystyle\sigma_{\min}(\widetilde{B}_{0}^{(k_{0})})+\frac{\mu_{0}}{3},

\displaystyle\sigma_{\min}(\widetilde{B}_{0}^{(k_{0})})

\displaystyle\,<\,

\displaystyle\min\limits_{i\in[m]\setminus\{k_{0}\}}\sigma_{\min}(\widetilde{B}_{0}^{(i)}).

This forces

By assumption (a),
\text{null}(B_{0}^{(k_{0})})

(resp.,

.

, there exists

By inequality (

), we get

\|\tilde{x}^{(0)}-x^{(0)}\|\,\leq\,C_{0}D_{0}\|\delta\mathcal{A}_{0}\|_{\Omega_{0}}.

Note that

\mathcal{A}_{1}=\mathcal{T}_{k_{0}}(x^{(0)})
,

\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{1}=\mathcal{T}_{k_{0}}(\tilde{x}^{(0)})
,
and
\widetilde{\Omega}_{1}=\Omega_{1}
.
Observe that
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{1}-\mathcal{A}_{1}
is a reshaping of

\|\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{1}-\mathcal{A}_{1}\|_{\Omega_{1}}\,=\,\|\tilde{x}^{(0)}-x^{(0)}\|.

By (

), the above implies that when

is sufficiently small,

\|\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{1}-\mathcal{A}_{1}\|_{\Omega_{1}}\,\leq\,\rho_{0}\|\delta\mathcal{A}\|_{\Omega}\quad\text{for}\quad\rho_{0}\coloneqq C_{0}D_{0}.

Now we view

as
(\mathcal{A}_{0},\Omega_{0})

We can show that
\tilde{k}_{1}=k_{1}
(hence

) and (
5.2
) holds for
j=1
.
Similarly, one can show that the conclusion holds for
j=2,\ldots,m-2
.

(ii) We prove by induction on
m
.
For the base step
m=2
, we apply Algorithm
4.1
to

(\mathcal{A}_{0},\Omega_{0})=(\mathcal{A},\Omega)
.
Without loss of generality, we assume
k_{0}=2
in Step 1 of Algorithm
4.1
.
The matrix
B_{0}^{(2)}
formulated as in (
3.7
) is the same as the matrix
B
in (
2.4
).
By assumption (a),
\text{null}(B)
is one-dimensional.
Recall that
x^{(0)}
is the unit length vector computed from
B
in Step 2 of Algorithm
4.1
.
Since
\Omega
is row-full,
u_{1}=x^{(0)}
.
Similarly,
\tilde{x}^{(0)}
is the unit length vector computed from
\widetilde{B}
in Step 2 when Algorithm
4.1
is applied to
(\widetilde{\mathcal{A}},\widetilde{\Omega})
where
\widetilde{\Omega}=\Omega
.
Also since
\Omega
is row-full,
\tilde{u}_{1}=\tilde{x}^{(0)}
.
Let
\delta B\coloneqq\widetilde{B}-B
.
By (
5.9
),

(5.11)

\|\tilde{u}_{1}-u_{1}\|=\|\tilde{x}^{(0)}-x^{(0)}\|\leq C_{0}D_{0}\|\delta\mathcal{A}\|_{\Omega}.

In Step 5, Algorithm
4.1
computes
u_{2}
(resp.,
\tilde{u}_{2}
)
from
(\mathcal{A},\Omega)
(resp.,
(\widetilde{\mathcal{A}},\Omega)
) by solving
a linear least squares problem.
Fix an arbitrary
j\in[n_{2}]
,
we write

\Theta_{j}\,=\,\{(i_{1},j),(i_{2},j),\ldots,(i_{m_{j}},j)\},

where
\Theta_{j}
is defined as in (
2.2
).
Denote

\begin{array}[]{lcl}a\,\coloneqq\,\big[\mathcal{A}_{i_{1}j}\,\,\cdots\,\,\mathcal{A}_{i_{m_{j}}j}\big]^{T},&&\tilde{a}\,\coloneqq\,\big[\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{i_{1}j}\,\,\cdots\,\,\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{i_{m_{j}}j}\big]^{T},\\
w\,\coloneqq\,\big[(u_{1})_{i_{1}}\,\,\cdots\,\,(u_{1})_{i_{m_{j}}}\big]^{T},&&\tilde{w}\,\coloneqq\,\big[(\tilde{u}_{1})_{i_{1}}\,\,\cdots\,\,(\tilde{u}_{1})_{i_{m_{j}}}\big]^{T}.\end{array}

Then the entries
(u_{2})_{j}
and
(\tilde{u}_{2})_{j}
are, respectively, the minimizers for

(5.12)

\min_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{1}}\,\|a-\theta w\|,\qquad\min_{\tilde{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{1}}\,\|\tilde{a}-\tilde{\theta}\tilde{w}\|.

Since
\mathcal{A}_{ij}\neq 0
for all
(i,j)\in\Omega
and
\mathcal{A}=u_{1}u_{2}^{T}
,
we have
w\neq 0
and
(u_{2})_{j}\neq 0
.
Note that
a=(u_{2})_{j}w
, so

|w^{T}a|=|(u_{2})_{j}|\cdot\|w\|^{2}>0.

Let
\delta a\coloneqq\tilde{a}-a
and
\delta w\coloneqq\tilde{w}-w
.
Since
\delta a
is a sub-array of
\delta\mathcal{A}
, we have

(5.13)

\|\delta a\|\,\leq\,\|\delta\mathcal{A}\|_{\Omega}.

Since
\delta w
is a subvector of
\tilde{u}_{1}-u_{1}
, (
5.11
) implies

(5.14)

\|\delta w\|\leq\|\tilde{u}_{1}-u_{1}\|\leq C_{0}D_{0}\|\delta\mathcal{A}\|_{\Omega}.

The least squares solutions to (
5.12
) can be expressed as

(u_{2})_{j}=w^{T}a\big/w^{T}w,\quad(\tilde{u}_{2})_{j}=\tilde{w}^{T}\tilde{a}\big/\tilde{w}^{T}\tilde{w}.

Observe that

\displaystyle(\tilde{u}_{2})_{j}-(u_{2})_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{(w+\delta w)^{T}(a+\delta a)}{(w+\delta w)^{T}(w+\delta w)}-\frac{w^{T}a}{w^{T}w}=(u_{2})_{j}\cdot\frac{\tau-\gamma}{1+\gamma},

where

\tau\coloneqq\frac{w^{T}(\delta a)+(\delta w)^{T}a+(\delta w)^{T}(\delta a)}{w^{T}a},\qquad\gamma\coloneqq\frac{2w^{T}(\delta w)+(\delta w)^{T}(\delta w)}{w^{T}w}.

By the inequality
w^{T}(\delta w)\geq-\|w\|\|\delta w\|
, we get

\gamma\geq\frac{-2\|w\|\|\delta w\|+\|\delta w\|^{2}}{\|w\|^{2}}=-2\frac{\|\delta w\|}{\|w\|}+\Big(\frac{\|\delta w\|}{\|w\|}\Big)^{2}.

When
\|\delta\mathcal{A}\|_{\Omega}
is sufficiently small,
we have
1+\gamma\geq\frac{1}{2}
by (
5.14
), so

\frac{|(\tilde{u}_{2})_{j}-(u_{2})_{j}|}{|(u_{2})_{j}|}=\Big|\frac{\tau-\gamma}{1+\gamma}\Big|\leq 2|\tau-\gamma|\leq 2(|\tau|+|\gamma|).

Note that

|\tau|\leq\frac{\|w\|\|\delta a\|+\|\delta w\|\|a\|+\|\delta w\|\|\delta a\|}{|w^{T}a|},\qquad|\gamma|\leq\frac{2\|w\|\|\delta w\|+\|\delta w\|^{2}}{\|w\|^{2}}.

By (
5.13
) and (
5.14
), there exists a constant
L_{j}>0
such that

|(\tilde{u}_{2})_{j}-(u_{2})_{j}|\,\leq\,L_{j}\|\delta\mathcal{A}\|_{\Omega}.

Let
K\coloneqq\sqrt{n_{2}}\max_{j}L_{j}
, then

(5.15)

\|\tilde{u}_{2}-u_{2}\|=\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}|(\tilde{u}_{2})_{j}-(u_{2})_{j}|^{2}\Big)^{1/2}\leq K\|\delta\mathcal{A}\|_{\Omega}.

Hence, (
5.3
) holds with
C_{1}\coloneqq\max\{C_{0}D_{0},K\}
.
If
\|\delta\mathcal{A}\|_{\Omega}\leq\frac{1}{K}
, then (
5.15
) implies

\|\tilde{u}_{2}\|\leq\|\tilde{u}_{2}-u_{2}\|+\|u_{2}\|\leq K\|\delta\mathcal{A}\|_{\Omega}+\|u_{2}\|\leq 1+\|u_{2}\|.

Combining (
5.11
) and (
5.15
), we get

(5.16)

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\|\tilde{u}_{1}\otimes\tilde{u}_{2}-u_{1}\otimes u_{2}\|&=&\|\tilde{u}_{1}\otimes\tilde{u}_{2}-u_{1}\otimes\tilde{u}_{2}+u_{1}\otimes\tilde{u}_{2}-u_{1}\otimes u_{2}\|\\
&\leq&\|\tilde{u}_{1}-u_{1}\|\,\|\tilde{u}_{2}\|+\|u_{1}\|\,\|\tilde{u}_{2}-u_{2}\|\\
&\leq&C_{2}\|\delta\mathcal{A}\|_{\Omega}\end{array}

for the constant
C_{2}\coloneqq C_{0}D_{0}(1+\|u_{2}\|)+K\|u_{1}\|
, depending on
(u_{1},u_{2})
.

For the inductive step, assume the conclusion holds for the order
m-1
.
We apply Algorithm
4.1
to the
(m-1)
st order p.o.t.’s

(\mathcal{A}_{1},\Omega_{1})
and
(\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{1},\widetilde{\Omega}_{1})
.
By item (i),

\widetilde{\Omega}_{1}=\Omega_{1}
,
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{1}
and
\mathcal{A}_{1}
have the same dimension.
Let

\delta\mathcal{A}_{1}\coloneqq\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{1}-\mathcal{A}_{1}
.
Without loss of generality, we assume
k_{0}=m
.
By the inductive hypothesis, there exist constants
C_{1}^{\prime}>0
,
C_{2}^{\prime}>0
,
depending on
(u_{1},\ldots,u_{m-1})
,
such that

\begin{gathered}\|\tilde{u}_{t}-u_{t}\|\,\leq\,C_{1}^{\prime}\|\delta\mathcal{A}_{1}\|_{\Omega_{1}}\quad\text{for}\quad t=1,\ldots,m-1,\\
\|\tilde{u}_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\tilde{u}_{m-1}-u_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes u_{m-1}\|\,\leq\,C_{2}^{\prime}\|\delta\mathcal{A}_{1}\|_{\Omega_{1}}.\end{gathered}

By the inequality (
5.10
), the above becomes

\begin{gathered}\|\tilde{u}_{t}-u_{t}\|\,\leq\,C_{1}^{\prime}\rho_{0}\|\delta\mathcal{A}\|_{\Omega}\quad\text{for}\quad t=1,\ldots,m-1,\\
\|\tilde{u}_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\tilde{u}_{m-1}-u_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes u_{m-1}\|\,\leq\,C_{2}^{\prime}\rho_{0}\|\delta\mathcal{A}\|_{\Omega}.\end{gathered}

In Step 5, Algorithm
4.1
produces
u_{m}
and
\tilde{u}_{m}

by solving the linear least squares problems respectively (note that
\widetilde{\Omega}=\Omega
)

\min_{u}\,\|\mathcal{A}-u_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes u_{m-1}\otimes u\|_{\Omega},\qquad\min_{\tilde{u}}\,\|\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}-\tilde{u}_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\tilde{u}_{m-1}\otimes\tilde{u}\|_{\widetilde{\Omega}}.

Similar to (
5.15
), we can show that there exists
K^{\prime}>0
such that

\|\tilde{u}_{m}-u_{m}\|\,\leq\,K^{\prime}\|\delta\mathcal{A}\|_{\Omega}.

Hence, (
5.3
) holds with
C_{1}\coloneqq\max\{C_{1}^{\prime}\rho_{0},K^{\prime}\}
.
Observe that

\displaystyle\tilde{u}_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\tilde{u}_{m}-u_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes u_{m}=\,

\displaystyle(\tilde{u}_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\tilde{u}_{m-1}-u_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes u_{m-1})\otimes\tilde{u}_{m}

\displaystyle+u_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes u_{m-1}\otimes(\tilde{u}_{m}-u_{m}).

Similar to (
5.16
), we can show that there exists
C_{2}>0
,
depending on
(u_{1},\ldots,u_{m})
, such that (
5.4
) holds,
when
\|\delta\mathcal{A}\|_{\Omega}
is sufficiently small.
∎

\Phi_{1}\coloneqq[n_{1}]
.
For
t=1,\ldots,m-1

\Phi_{t+1}\subseteq[n_{1}]\times\cdots\times[n_{t+1}]

(6.1)

is the cardinality of

.

•

For the case

(i_{1},\ldots,i_{M})
of
(1,\ldots,M)

For the case

,

(i_{1},\ldots,i_{M})

(j_{1},\ldots,j_{n_{t+1}})
of
(1,\ldots,n_{t+1})
.

Conclusions

This paper studies the rank one completion problem for tensors of arbitrary orders.
We introduce rank one extractability for partially observed matrices
and study its properties.
We then define rank one determinable tensors recursively through flattenings.
Based on this, a recursive algorithm is proposed for rank one tensor completion.
Under some assumptions,
we prove that this algorithm produces a unique rank one completion.
In the presence of noise, we show that this algorithm is robust.
That is, when the noise is sufficiently small,
the rank one completion produced by the algorithm is close to the exact one.
Numerical experiments illustrate the efficiency and accuracy of our method.

Appendix A
Perturbation of matrix nullspace

Theorem A.1
.

Let
B\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times n}
be a matrix with
\mbox{rank}(B)=n-1
.
Let
v
be a solution to
Bx=0
with
\|v\|=1
.
Let
\widetilde{B}=B+\delta B
be a perturbation of
B
.

(i)

If
\ell\geq n
, let
\tilde{v}
be the right singular vector of
\widetilde{B}
corresponding to the minimum singular value
\tilde{\sigma}_{n}
such that
\|\tilde{v}\|=1
and
v^{T}\tilde{v}\geq 0
.

(ii)

If
\ell<n
, let
\tilde{v}
be a solution to
\widetilde{B}x=0
with
\|\tilde{v}\|=1
and
v^{T}\tilde{v}\geq 0
.

Let
\delta v\coloneqq\tilde{v}-v
.
Then there exists a constant
C_{0}>0
, depending on
B
, such that

(A.1)

\|\delta v\|\,\leq\,C_{0}\|\delta B\|,

when
\|\delta B\|
is sufficiently small.

Proof.

Let
B=U\Sigma V^{T}
be the SVD with

\Sigma=\begin{bmatrix}\Sigma_{1}&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times n},\quad\Sigma_{1}=\mbox{diag}(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{n-1}),

where
\sigma_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\sigma_{n-1}>\sigma_{n}=0
are singular values of
B
, and

U=\begin{bmatrix}u_{1}&u_{2}&\cdots&u_{\ell}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times\ell},\quad V=\begin{bmatrix}v_{1}&v_{2}&\cdots&v_{n}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}

are orthogonal matrices.
Since
\mbox{rank}(B)=n-1
, the nullspace of
B
is one-dimensional,
so the vector
v_{n}
is unique up to sign.
We can generally assume
v_{n}=v
.

(i) For the case that
\ell\geq n
,
up to orthogonal transformation, we can assume
B=\Sigma
and
v=e_{n}
.
Let
\tilde{u}
be the unit length left singular vector of
\widetilde{B}
, corresponding to its minimum singular value
\tilde{\sigma}_{n}
,
i.e.,
\widetilde{B}\tilde{v}=\tilde{\sigma}_{n}\tilde{u}
.
Note that

(A.2)

\widetilde{B}\tilde{v}=(B+\delta B)(v+\delta v)=Bv+B\cdot\delta v+\delta B\cdot v+\delta B\cdot\delta v.

Let
y\coloneqq B\cdot\delta v
.
Since
Bv=0
and
\widetilde{B}\tilde{v}=\tilde{\sigma}_{n}\tilde{u}
, the above implies

y=\tilde{\sigma}_{n}\tilde{u}-\delta B\cdot v-\delta B\cdot\delta v.

Write

y=[y_{1}\,\,y_{2}\,\,\cdots\,\,y_{\ell}]^{T}.

Since
\|\tilde{u}\|=\|v\|=\|\tilde{v}\|=1
and
\|\delta v\|=\|\tilde{v}-v\|\leq 2
,

\|y\|\leq\tilde{\sigma}_{n}\|\tilde{u}\|+\|\delta B\|\|v\|+\|\delta B\|\|\tilde{v}-v\|\leq\tilde{\sigma}_{n}+3\|\delta B\|.

Note
\tilde{\sigma}_{n}
(resp.,
\sigma_{n}
) is the minimum singular value of
\widetilde{B}
(resp.,
B
),
so

\displaystyle\tilde{\sigma}_{n}

\displaystyle=\min_{\|x\|=1}\|\widetilde{B}x\|\leq\min_{\|x\|=1}(\|Bx\|+\|\delta B\cdot x\|)

\displaystyle\leq\min_{\|x\|=1}\|Bx\|+\|\delta B\|=\sigma_{n}+\|\delta B\|.

Since
\mbox{rank}(B)=n-1
, we have
\sigma_{n}=0
.
This implies

\tilde{\sigma}_{n}\leq\|\delta B\|
,
so
\|y\|\leq 4\|\delta B\|
.
Denote

[z_{1}\,\,z_{2}\,\,\cdots\,\,z_{n}]=\delta v^{T}
.
Since

y=B\cdot\delta v=\Sigma\cdot\delta v,

we get
z_{i}=y_{i}/\sigma_{i}
for
i\in[n-1]
.
Let

\hat{z}=[z_{1}\,\,\cdots\,\,z_{n-1}]^{T},

then

\|\hat{z}\|^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\frac{y_{i}^{2}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{\sigma_{n-1}^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}y_{i}^{2}\leq\frac{\|y\|^{2}}{\sigma_{n-1}^{2}}.

Since

\tilde{v}=v+\delta v=e_{n}+\delta v=[z_{1}\,\,\cdots\,\,z_{n-1}\,\,1+z_{n}]^{T}
,
we have

1=\|\tilde{v}\|^{2}=\|\hat{z}\|^{2}+(1+z_{n})^{2},\quad 1+z_{n}=\tilde{v}^{T}e_{n}=\tilde{v}^{T}v\geq 0.

Hence,
z_{n}\leq 0
and

(A.3)

z_{n}=\sqrt{1-\|\hat{z}\|^{2}}-1=-\|\hat{z}\|^{2}\Big/\big(\sqrt{1-\|\hat{z}\|^{2}}+1\big).

This implies
|z_{n}|\leq\|\hat{z}\|^{2}
, so

\|\delta v\|^{2}=\|\hat{z}\|^{2}+z_{n}^{2}\leq\|\hat{z}\|^{2}+\|\hat{z}\|^{4}\leq\frac{\|y\|^{2}}{\sigma_{n-1}^{2}}\Big(1+\frac{\|y\|^{2}}{\sigma_{n-1}^{2}}\Big).

Recall that
\|y\|\leq 4\|\delta B\|
.
If
\|\delta B\|\leq\frac{\sigma_{n-1}}{4}
, then

\|y\|\,\leq\,\sigma_{n-1}\quad\implies\quad 1+\|y\|^{2}\big/\sigma_{n-1}^{2}\,\leq\,2,

so

\|\delta v\|\leq\frac{4\sqrt{2}}{\sigma_{n-1}}\|\delta B\|
.
Hence, (
A.1
) holds for

C_{0}=\frac{4\sqrt{2}}{\sigma_{n-1}}

if
\|\delta B\|\leq\frac{\sigma_{n-1}}{4}
.

(ii) For the case that
\ell<n
, we have
\ell=n-1
since
\mbox{rank}(B)=n-1
.
In the SVD
B=U\Sigma V^{T}
, we have

\Sigma=[\Sigma_{1}\,\,0]\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-1)\times n}.

Since
\widetilde{B}\tilde{v}=0
with
\|\tilde{v}\|=1
, (
A.2
) gives

U\Sigma V^{T}\delta v=B\cdot\delta v=-\delta B\cdot v-\delta B\cdot\delta v.

Multiplying
U^{T}
on both sides, we have

\Sigma V^{T}\delta v=-U^{T}\delta B\cdot v-U^{T}\delta B\cdot\delta v.

Let

y=[y_{1}\,\,y_{2}\,\,\cdots\,\,y_{n-1}]^{T}=\Sigma V^{T}\delta v.

Since
U
is orthogonal and
\|v\|=\|\tilde{v}\|=1
,

\|y\|\leq\|U^{T}\|\|\delta B\|\|v\|+\|U^{T}\|\|\delta B\|\|\tilde{v}-v\|\leq 3\|\delta B\|.

Let
z=[z_{1}\,\,z_{2}\,\,\cdots\,\,z_{n}]^{T}=V^{T}\delta v

and

\hat{z}=[z_{1}\,\,\cdots\,\,z_{n-1}]^{T},

then
y=\Sigma z
implies

\|\hat{z}\|^{2}\,\leq\,\|y\|^{2}\big/\sigma_{n-1}^{2}.

Since

V^{T}\tilde{v}=V^{T}(v+\delta v)=e_{n}+z=[z_{1}\,\,\cdots\,\,z_{n-1}\,\,1+z_{n}]^{T},

we have

1=\|\tilde{v}\|^{2}=\|V^{T}\tilde{v}\|^{2}=\|\hat{z}\|^{2}+(1+z_{n})^{2},

1+z_{n}=(V^{T}\tilde{v})^{T}e_{n}=\tilde{v}^{T}Ve_{n}=\tilde{v}^{T}v\geq 0.

Similar to (
A.3
), one can show
|z_{n}|\leq\|\hat{z}\|^{2}
and

\|\delta v\|^{2}=\|V^{T}\delta v\|^{2}=\|\hat{z}\|^{2}+z_{n}^{2}\leq\frac{\|y\|^{2}}{\sigma_{n-1}^{2}}\Big(1+\frac{\|y\|^{2}}{\sigma_{n-1}^{2}}\Big).

Recall that
\|y\|\leq 3\|\delta B\|
.
If
\|\delta B\|\leq\frac{\sigma_{n-1}}{3}
, then

\|\delta v\|\leq\frac{3\sqrt{2}}{\sigma_{n-1}}\|\delta B\|.

Hence, (
A.1
) holds for

C_{0}=\frac{3\sqrt{2}}{\sigma_{n-1}}

if
\|\delta B\|\leq\frac{\sigma_{n-1}}{3}
.
∎
