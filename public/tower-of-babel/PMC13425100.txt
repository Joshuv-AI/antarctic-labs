Separation from the CDC and its consequences for the public health workforce: findings from the CDC WISE Survey
Authors: Greta M Massetti, Ghenet Besera, Laura Chiang, Sherri Pals
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSince January 2025, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) has shed more than one-quarter of its workforce through layoffs, contract cancellations, and fellowship terminations, raising urgent questions about the consequences for affected individuals and for national public health capacity.MethodsWe surveyed 430 former CDC employees, contractors, and fellows to examine the economic, health, and psychological impacts of these separations.ResultsAmong respondents representing over 7300 combined years of public health experience, nearly 80% experienced involuntary separations, and more than half remained unemployed at the time of the survey. Three-quarters faced financial barriers to health care, nearly 80% reported moderate or severe sleep problems, and half experienced moderate or severe psychological distress. Mid-career and early-career professionals and parents with 2 or more children faced the greatest vulnerabilities; retirement, reemployment, and personal financial reserves were protective.ConclusionThese findings document the profound human costs of federal workforce instability and point to the need for targeted transition support, restoration of affected programs, and sustained investment in public health workforce infrastructure to prevent future disruptions.Keywords: public health workforce, job separation, public health infrastructure

Introduction

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), the nation's premier public health agency, has faced unprecedented disruption and organizational upheaval since January 2025, primarily driven by policy changes implemented under the current presidential administration, including the new Health and Human Services Secretary.1-5 CDC has shed nearly one-quarter of its staff since President Trump took office, with at least 3000 employees leaving the agency either involuntarily or of their own accord.1,3 The layoffs occurred in multiple waves, beginning in January with deferred resignation. In February, employees on probationary status were terminated. A major reduction in force (RIF) started on April 1 and resulted in the termination of approximately 1900 staff. Sweeping contract cancelations and terminations of fellowship programs have further impacted CDC's capacity. Many more CDC employees have left the agency through resignations and retirements.

The scale of disruptions has prompted concerns about the agency's capacity.1,6 Reductions and staff losses have disproportionately affected certain centers and offices, such as those working on injuries and chronic disease, the leading causes of morbidity and mortality in the United States.2,6 Significant questions remain about the implications of these disruptions for the agency and for the affected individuals. “Separation” is inclusive of all forms of separation from employment, including involuntary and voluntary.

Impacts of job loss and retirement

Employment transitions, whether sudden or anticipated, alter daily structure, professional identity, and life experiences.7 Work separation creates financial hardships due to loss of salary and benefits, and individuals face challenges securing comparable employment, particularly older workers.7,8 Research documents long-term earnings reductions following job loss, even after reemployment, with some earnings penalties persisting for decades.7 The psychological impact of unemployment extends beyond financial concerns; work provides structure, purpose, social connection, and identity, and separated individuals experience increased risk of depression and anxiety.9-12 Unemployment and involuntary retirement exert significant negative effects on health, with longer unemployment spells associated with worse outcomes.13,14 Separated adults are more likely to experience uninsurance, financial barriers to care, and unmet health needs.12,15,16

This study describes the separation experiences and loss of expertise among individuals voluntarily or involuntarily separated from CDC since January 2025, and examines the financial, health, and psychological impacts of those separations and their associated factors.

Data and methods

Data source

Data from the 2025 CDC Workforce Impact and Separation Experiences (CDC WISE) Survey, an anonymous, cross-sectional online survey of CDC employees, contractors, and fellows who separated from the agency since January 2025, were used for this analysis. The survey assessed the experiences and impacts associated with both voluntary and involuntary separation on former CDC employees’ economic, psychological, and health outcomes. Eligibility criteria included: separation from CDC employment on or after January 21, 2025, for any reason, age 18 years or older, and ability to complete an English-language online survey. Respondents were recruited through professional networks, social media, and direct outreach. This included direct email outreach to current and former CDC employees, distribution through newsletters for several organizations connecting and supporting former CDC employees (such as CDC Alumni and Friends, the National Public Health Coalition, and the CDC Mutual Aid Network), multiple LinkedIn posts throughout the study recruitment period, and messages in Signal chat groups and other messaging platforms. Participants did not receive any incentives for participation in the survey. The survey was administered online using Qualtrics from October 10, 2025, through January 25, 2026. Respondents provided informed consent electronically before accessing survey questions. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Georgia State University Institutional Review Board.

Measures

The CDC WISE Survey questionnaire was developed based on validated instruments and adapted questions relevant to CDC workforce separation. The survey included questions about participant demographics, household demographics, professional background, separation characteristics, and current employment status (see Table 1).17

Health status and health-related quality of life

Sleep quality was measured using 4 items from the Insomnia Severity Index,18 assessing problems falling asleep, staying asleep, and waking up too early (none, mild, moderate, severe) since separating from CDC, plus an item on sleep satisfaction (1 very dissatisfied to 5 very satisfied). Scores of 9 or more were categorized as moderate or severe sleep problems.18-20 Psychological distress was measured using the Patient Health Questionnaire 4 (PHQ-4),21 a validated 4-item instrument assessing depression and anxiety symptoms.22 Health-related quality of life (HRQOL)23 assesses general health status (fair or poor health; excellent, very good, or good health), physically unhealthy days (14 or more days; 13 or fewer days), mentally unhealthy days (14 or more days; 13 or fewer days), and activity limitations (14 or more days; 13 or fewer days).23-25 The 4 items were combined into an HRQOL index, with scores of 3 or 4 indicating poor health.

Health care access

Health care access limitations was defined as not having a dental visit in the past 12 months or not having a medical home. Four questions from the Medical Expenditure Panel Survey (MEPS)26 were used to examine financial barriers to health care: losing or anticipating losing health insurance, having delayed or foregone medical care due to cost, having delayed dental care due to cost, or being unable to afford to see a doctor.27-29 Respondents also reported whether they took prescription medication for anxiety or depression.

Financial hardship and financial buffer

Financial hardship was evaluated through 4 questions from MEPS26 assessing material hardship experiences: borrowing money or using savings to meet living expenses, not being able to pay rent or mortgage on time, not being able to pay utility expenses on time, and missing a credit card or loan payment. Scores of 3 or more indicated substantial financial hardship. Having a financial buffer was defined as being somewhat or very confident they could come up with $400 if an unexpected expense arose.

Complex grief from job loss

The Job Loss Grief Scale-Short Form includes 10 items that assess complicated grief following job loss. The scale has good psychometric properties.30,31 Items include “I feel bitter about my separation from my job,” “Separation from my job feels like a personal disaster,” and “I feel that my separation has smashed my view of the world” (never, sometimes, about half the time, most of the time, and always). Items were summed and retained as a continuous measure.30

Statistical analyses

Descriptive analyses characterized the demographic, separation, health, and professional characteristics of separated employees using frequencies and percentages for categorical and means and standard deviations for continuous variables. Bivariate models examined associations between demographic, separation, and outcome variables including moderate or severe sleep problems, psychological distress, 14 or more unhealthy days, overall poor health, health care access limitations, financial barriers to health care, and complex job loss grief. Variables with bivariate P values less than .25 were included in multiple regression models, fit separately to each outcome variable. We chose this approach, recommended by Hosmer and Lemeshow,32 to include relevant variables but build parsimonious models.33 We initially planned to also include financial hardship as an outcome in models, but cross-tabulations with predictor variables showed sparse or zero cell counts, which precluded analysis of associations.

Predictors of interest included marital status, years at CDC, current employment status, dependent children, sole or primary earner, health care access limitations, financial barriers to health care, and financial buffer. Predictors were selected based on previous literature on outcomes from job loss and hypothesized associations with outcomes;10,14 confounders were included to adjust for potential confounding but were not the focus of inference. Health care access limitations and financial barriers to health care were treated as outcomes in models assessing those constructs directly and as predictors in models for other outcomes. Confounders in analyses included gender, race/ethnicity, disability status, and education. Logistic regression was used for binary outcomes and linear regression for complex job loss grief.

To assess the impact of missing background variable data on our findings, we conducted a sensitivity analysis by performing multiple imputation by chained equations using 20 imputations, repeated the analyses for each outcome, combined parameter estimates according to Rubin's rules, and assessed models excluding participants missing demographic data (n = 48). All analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC).

Results

Demographic characteristics and professional background

Overall, 430 respondents completed the survey, with an average age of 46.4 years (SD = 10.8; Table 1). More than 80% of respondents identify as female. Respondents represented a combined 7348 years of experience working in public health, with 5911 of those years at CDC. Most respondents (91.2%) were federal employees at the time of separation; the largest group (42.1%) was in a scientist or epidemiologist role. Nearly two-thirds of respondents (64.6%) were terminated due to a RIF. Nearly 4 out of 5 (79.3%) had an involuntary separation, defined as a separation not due to regular or deferred resignation or retirement. More than half (52.0%) were unemployed and looking for work at the time of the survey; among those who were employed, 61.8% were earning less than they had earned at CDC, and 29.0% took part-time employment or gig work to make ends meet. Two-thirds of respondents (67.7%) were the sole or primary earner in the household. There were 264 children living in households among the 157 respondents (36.8%) who had children.

Health status and HRQOL

Three in 4 (78.8%) had moderate or severe sleep problems since separation, half (50.5%) experienced moderate or severe psychological distress, nearly 3 in 5 (58.5%) had 14 or more unhealthy days in the past month, nearly 1 in 5 (18.8%) experienced fair or poor health, and 1 in 4 (24.4%) had poor HRQOL (Table 2). Results of adjusted analyses (including adjusted ORs, 95% CI, and P values) are included in Table 3. Having financial barriers to health care was associated with significantly higher odds of experiencing sleep problems; having a financial buffer was associated with significantly lower odds of experiencing sleep problems. Compared with people who had 21 or more years of CDC experience, those with 11-20 years had higher odds of experiencing psychological distress. Being currently employed or retired and having a financial buffer were associated with significantly lower odds of experiencing psychological distress. Having financial barriers to health care was associated with significantly higher odds of having 14 or more unhealthy days in the past month, and having health care access limitations was associated with significantly higher odds of having poor HRQOL.

Health care access, financial barriers to health care, and job loss grief

Nearly all respondents (90.5%) received health insurance through CDC employment prior to separation, and 3 out of 5 (62.8%) lost or anticipated losing health insurance. About 1 in 10 (10.2%) experienced limitations in health care, either not having had a dental visit in the past year or not having a medical home. Among those who were taking medication for anxiety or depression, about 3 in 10 (29.5%) started medication since separating from CDC. About 1 in 10 respondents (10.9%) experienced substantial financial hardship. Results of adjusted analyses (including adjusted ORs, 95% CI, and P values) are included in Table 4. Being married was associated with significantly lower odds of having health care access limitations, while being at CDC for 10 years or fewer (compared with being at CDC for 21 years or more) was associated with significantly higher odds. Being at CDC for 10 years or fewer and 11-20 years (compared with 21 years or more) and having 2 or more dependent children were associated with significantly higher odds of having financial barriers to health care. Being employed or retired was associated with significantly lower odds of having financial barriers to health care.

Discussion

This study documents the profound consequences of mass workforce separations at CDC on 430 former employees, contractors, and fellows with an average of 17.3 years of public health experience. Nearly 8 in 10 separations were involuntary, a conservative estimate given that many resignations and retirements occurred earlier than planned due to organizational disruptions. The breadth of consequences, which span employment, financial security, physical health, and psychological well-being, reveals a workforce experiencing compounding hardship across every domain examined. The loss of critical specialized expertise in data science, surveillance, epidemiology, programming, and health communications threatens the nation's public health infrastructure. Behind these workforce losses are individuals: experienced scientists, epidemiologists, and public health practitioners. These individuals are experiencing significant challenges in navigating financial crisis, physical and psychological health issues, and grief, often without adequate support.

Mid-career professionals with 11-20 years of CDC experience faced significantly higher odds of psychological distress, financial barriers to healthcare, and complex grief from job loss. These individuals likely had substantial financial obligations, established career identities deeply tied to their CDC work, and limited time to rebuild retirement savings before a planned exit from the workforce. Early-career professionals with 10 years or fewer at CDC experienced significantly higher odds of both health care access limitations and financial barriers to care, suggesting they had less opportunity to build financial reserves or establish alternative professional networks. Together, these findings indicate that neither career stage provided protection from severe impacts, though the mechanisms differed. Parents with 2 or more dependent children faced 2.54 times the odds of financial barriers to healthcare, demonstrating how separation consequences cascade through families and threaten long-term economic security. The finding that more than three-quarters of respondents experienced financial barriers to health care is particularly striking, as this factor was significantly associated with sleep problems, increased unhealthy days, and heightened job loss grief. These associations suggest a cycle in which loss of health care access compounds both physical and psychological distress, creating interconnected vulnerabilities.

In contrast to these vulnerable groups, reemployment or retirement and having a financial buffer emerged as significant protective factors. These findings underscore that financial security and meaningful work provide crucial protection against the cascading consequences of job loss. However, the protective effect of reemployment offers limited reassurance given that more than half of respondents remained unemployed and actively seeking work months after separation, leaving this highly experienced workforce in a prolonged state of vulnerability.

Findings related to complex grief from job loss observed in this study warrant particular attention. The mean score of 30.3 is higher than what has been reported in other studies using this scale,30,31 and having a moderately long tenure at CDC (11-20 years) was a significant predictor of higher grief scores. For mission-driven public health professionals, this grief likely reflects not only loss of income but loss of professional identity, sense of purpose, and connection to a community united by shared commitment to protecting population health. Mid-career professionals, the unemployed, those without financial buffers, and those facing health care access barriers experienced significantly higher grief. This finding is consequential because complex grief can impair functioning, delay reemployment, and contribute to long-term mental health problems independent of depression or anxiety.31 The loss of these professionals represents not only a workforce capacity gap but the severing of individuals from work that gave their lives meaning and structure.7,9-12

Strength and limitations

Study strengths include the moderately large sample of directly affected individuals, comprehensive assessment across multiple impact domains, anonymous survey design, timely data collection capturing acute impacts, and inclusion of multiple employment types. Study limitations include the cross-sectional design, which limits causal inference, the self-selected convenience sample that may not represent all separated employees, and reliance on self-report for all measures. Recruitment through professional networks and social media may have introduced selection bias, as individuals with greater distress or stronger connections to CDC professional communities may have been more likely to participate. As with all anonymous online surveys, we cannot fully verify respondent identity or eligibility. However, the survey included an eligibility confirmation screen, the Qualtrics platform was configured to prevent duplicate submissions, and we conducted quality assurance processes to review all responses and flag implausible entries.

Complete information about the number of individuals who have been separated from CDC has not been made available, so it is not possible to accurately assess the representativeness of the present sample. Based on best-available estimates (https://www.cdcdataproject.org/chaos-at-cdc), approximately 2080 individuals were separated as of January 2026, suggesting that the present sample reflects about 21% of the population of separated employees. The current survey reflects a convenience sample rather than a representative sample of the population, and findings may be vulnerable to selection bias. The results may not be generalizable to the population of separated employees or to other sectors or public service employees more broadly.

Public health implications

The findings of this study document the profound individual-level consequences of workforce separations for highly experienced public health professionals navigating unemployment, loss of health care access, and psychological distress. Protective factor findings point to concrete intervention opportunities: Targeted transition support, mental health resources, and career placement assistance for separated federal workers could address documented gaps and mitigate the worst outcomes. Reemployment and financial reserves were the strongest protective factors identified, providing a clear evidence base for the design of such programs.

The mass workforce separations at CDC documented in this study had consequences not only for affected individuals but for the nation's public health infrastructure. The loss of 430 highly experienced professionals with over 7300 combined years of public health experience represents irreplaceable institutional knowledge and critical competency gaps in data modernization, surveillance infrastructure, emergency response, and health communications. Recovery from this disruption will require years of rebuilding given the specialized training and experience required for these roles. Investment in public health workforce stability is fundamentally an investment in public health infrastructure and population health protection.
