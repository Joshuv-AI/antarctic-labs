HA-330 hemoadsorption in septic shock requiring high-dose norepinephrine: a multicenter randomized controlled trial (CLEANSE)
Authors: Nattapat Wongtirawit, Watsamon Inyu, Ukrit Prajantasen, Piyarat Phairatwet, Ranistha Ratanarat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInflammatory cytokines play a pivotal role in septic shock, driving tissue injury and circulatory failure. Hemoadsorption has been proposed as an extracorporeal strategy that removes inflammatory mediators. Patient selection and treatment timing may be integral to clinical benefit.ObjectivesTo determine whether adjunctive hemoadsorption with the HA-330 cytokine adsorber reduces 28-day mortality in patients with septic shock requiring high-dose vasopressors, compared with standard treatment alone.MethodsThis multicenter, randomized controlled trial enrolled patients with septic shock requiring norepinephrine ≥ 0.2 mcg/kg/min at two tertiary care centers in Thailand. Participants were assigned 1:1 to standard treatment alone (ST group) or standard treatment with two 3-hour sessions of hemoadsorption using HA-330 (HA group). The primary outcome was 28-day mortality. The trial was terminated early at the second pre-planned interim analysis due to slow recruitment and funding constraints before reaching the planned sample size of 206 participants.ResultsA total of 128 participants were enrolled; 65 were assigned to the ST group and 63 to the HA group. The median age was 67 years, and baseline characteristics were largely comparable between groups. By day 28, 38 of 65 (58%) participants in the ST group and 28 of 63 (44%) in the HA group had died (relative risk, 0.76; 95% CI, 0.54–1.07; P = 0.16; hazard ratio, 0.68; 95% CI, 0.44–1.07; P = 0.09). No significant differences were observed in organ support-free days, shock reversal, vasopressor doses, or inflammatory markers. In a post-hoc Cox model adjusted for IL-6 (log-transformed) and VIS at hour 0, the hazard ratio for 28-day mortality was 0.62 (95% CI, 0.39–0.97; P = 0.037). No serious adverse events were reported in either group.ConclusionsIn this randomized trial which was terminated early, adjunctive hemoadsorption with HA-330 in patients with septic shock requiring high-dose vasopressors did not statistically reduce 28-day mortality.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05136183); registered November 29, 2021.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13054-026-06121-7.

Background

Septic shock remains a major cause of intensive care unit (ICU) admission worldwide, with 28-day mortality reaching 60% in patients requiring high-dose vasopressor support despite advances in resuscitation and organ support [1, 2]. The underlying pathophysiology involves a dysregulated host immune response that drives excessive inflammatory cytokine release, resulting in circulatory failure and multi-organ damage [3]. Immune-modulating therapies targeting this response have largely failed to improve clinical outcomes in randomized controlled trials, with only systemic corticosteroids remaining the only adjunct demonstrating consistent clinical benefit [4, 5].

Hemoadsorption has emerged as an extracorporeal strategy to mitigate inflammation-mediated injury by passing blood through an adsorbent cartridge that removes circulating inflammatory mediators [6–8]. The inflammatory cascade in septic shock involves two classes of target molecules: pathogen-derived endotoxin and host-derived inflammatory mediators. Mechanism-specific cartridges such as polymyxin B directly bind endotoxin, whereas broad-spectrum adsorbers such as the HA-330 disposable hemoadsorption cartridge (Jafron Biomedical, Guangdong, China) nonspecifically absorb molecules in the 10–60 kilodalton range, covering a broad spectrum of cytokines [7, 9]. In a randomized trial of HA-330 in patients with sepsis, significant reductions in circulating interleukin-6 and interleukin-8 were demonstrated in the hemoadsorption group, but 28-day mortality did not differ significantly between groups [10]. Notably, only half of the enrolled patients had septic shock, and the timing of hemoadsorption was not specified, limiting the potential for clinical benefit.

Patients with septic shock exhibit higher circulating cytokine levels than those with sepsis alone [11, 12], suggesting that a more severely ill, vasopressor-dependent population may derive greater benefit from cytokine removal. We further hypothesized that initiating hemoadsorption early before irreversible organ failure is established is critical to interrupting the inflammatory cascade. Based on these considerations, we conducted a multicenter randomized controlled trial to determine whether adjunctive hemoadsorption with the HA-330 reduces 28-day mortality in patients with septic shock norepinephrine requiring ≥ 0.2 mcg/kg/min, compared with standard treatment alone. Secondary objectives included ICU and hospital mortality, organ support-free days, vasopressor response, inflammatory markers, and safety.

Methods

Trial design and setting

This prospective, multicenter, randomized controlled trial was conducted in the ICUs of two hospitals in Bangkok, Thailand: Siriraj Hospital, Mahidol University (January 15, 2021–December 31, 2025), and Vajira Hospital, Navamindradhiraj University (September 5, 2024–December 31, 2025). The study protocol was approved by the Siriraj Institutional Review Board (COA no. Si 040/2021) and the Institutional Review Board of the Faculty of Medicine Vajira Hospital (COA 155/2567), and was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05136183). This study was supported by the Siriraj Research Fund, Faculty of Medicine Siriraj Hospital, Mahidol University; the funder played no role in study design, data analysis, or outcome assessment. The full study protocol has been published elsewhere [13], and this report is in accordance with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines [14].

Study participants

We screened all patients aged 18 years or older who were diagnosed with septic shock, as defined by the Sepsis-3 criteria [3]. Eligible patients were those receiving intravenous norepinephrine at ≥ 0.2 mcg/kg/min, or an equipotent dose of other vasopressors [15, 16]. Patients were excluded if (1) they had met the vasopressor threshold for more than 24 h, (2) had acute coronary syndrome, hemodynamically significant arrhythmias, acute ischemic stroke, or uncontrolled bleeding, (3) were in moribund conditions with expected death within 24 h, (4) had comorbidities with a life expectancy of less than 6 months, or (5) were pregnant. Written informed consent was obtained from each patient or from the next of kin or legal guardian when the patient was unable to consent.

Treatment allocation

After enrollment, participants were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to either the standard treatment (ST) group or the hemoadsorption (HA) group. The ST group received standard treatment alone, whereas the HA group received standard treatment with adjunctive HA-330 hemoadsorption. Randomization was performed using sealed envelopes with variable block sizes of 4, 6, and 8, stratified by study site.

Intervention

After randomization, participants in the ST group entered a fixed 6-hour period before the start of treatment. For participants in the HA group, this interval continued until the onset of the first hemoadsorption session, which was recommended to begin within 12 h of randomization to allow for vascular access placement and circuit preparation. The start of treatment—designated as hour 0 of day 1—was therefore fixed at 6 h after randomization in the ST group and variable in the HA group, determined by the time of first hemoadsorption initiation. This was prospectively defined based on pilot experience demonstrating that the time required to start hemoadsorption circuit resulted in meaningful physiological changes in the HA group between randomization and treatment initiation. Hour 0 serves as the reference point from which all outcomes are measured, as it more accurately reflects the onset of allocated treatment in both groups. The observed interval from randomization to hour 0 is reported in the Results.

All participants received standard treatment for septic shock in accordance with institutional guidelines derived from the Surviving Sepsis Campaign 2021 bundle [17], including appropriate antimicrobial therapy, source control, and fluid resuscitation. Vasopressor management followed a structured approach: norepinephrine was the first-line agent, and a second vasopressor was added when the norepinephrine dose exceeded 0.25–0.4 mcg/kg/min [17]. As vasopressin was not available in the country, epinephrine was generally the second vasopressor used. In accordance with the standard recommendations, initial mean arterial pressure (MAP) target of ≥ 65 mmHg was used in all participants, and individualized MAP target could be adjusted based on a MAP challenge in cases with refractory shock despite achieving the initial MAP target [18, 19]. No standard vasopressor weaning protocol was used, and weaning remained at the discretion of the treating clinicians. Life-support therapies, including renal replacement therapy (RRT), were administered according to clinical need.

For participants in the HA group, two hemoadsorption sessions were performed by trained ICU nurses using HA-330 disposable cartridges, beginning at hour 0 and hour 24. Hemoadsorption was delivered using available continuous RRT machines, with initial blood flow rate of 100 mL/min titrated up to 150 mL/min based on hemodynamic tolerance. Each session lasted 3 h. No systemic anticoagulation was administered during hemoadsorption; however, systemic anticoagulants for the prevention and treatment of thromboembolic diseases were permitted at the treating physicians’ discretion. After the second session, vascular access was removed unless required for ongoing RRT.

After the 48-hour treatment period, participants were followed until day 28 or hospital discharge, whichever came first. Patients discharged before day 28 were contacted by telephone to ascertain survival status.

Study outcomes

The primary outcome was 28-day mortality.

The secondary outcomes included ICU mortality, hospital mortality, and ICU-free, vasopressor-free, ventilator-free, and RRT-free days through day 28. Shock reversal at hour 6 was defined as a mean arterial pressure ≥ 65 mmHg with either a lactate reduction > 20% or an average urine output > 0.5 mL/kg/h. Vasoactive-inotropic score (VIS) [16], norepinephrine-equivalent dose of all vasopressors [2], Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) scores, serum C-reactive protein, and plasma IL-6 levels were assessed at hours 24 and 48.

Safety endpoints monitored through day 28 included hypotension or arrhythmias during hemoadsorption or other renal replacement therapy, systemic or dialysis catheter-related bleeding, and dialysis catheter-related infections. Participants were also monitored for new-onset leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, and septic shock. Serious adverse events were reported to regulatory authorities and monitored until resolution.

Sample size estimation

The 28-day mortality in the ST group was estimated at 60%, and an absolute reduction of 20% (to 40%) was anticipated in the HA group [2]. A combined sample of 206 participants was required to achieve 80% power at a two-sided type I error rate of 5%, with adjustment for two planned interim analyses.

Given the limited randomized evidence on hemoadsorption with HA-330 in this population, two prespecified interim analyses were performed on the primary outcome after 30% and 60% of enrolled participants had completed 28-day follow-up, using an asymmetric two-sided group sequential design with a Hwang–Shih–DeCani spending function (Supplementary Fig. 1).

At the second interim analysis, 128 participants had completed 28-day follow-up, corresponding to approximately 60% of the planned sample. The observed Z statistic was − 1.59 (one-sided P = 0.056), crossing neither the efficacy nor the futility boundary, and the trial was planned to continue per protocol. However, due to slower-than-anticipated enrollment over 55 months and exhaustion of available funding, the investigators and sponsor decided to terminate the study at this point.

Data analysis plan

All analyses were performed on the modified intention-to-treat (mITT) population, which included all participants who reached hour 0, comprising all participants in the ST group who survived for at least 6 h after randomization and those in the HA group who underwent the first hemoadsorption session. Ultimately, no participants were excluded under this criterion, and the predefined mITT population was therefore identical to the full intention-to-treat (ITT) population.

Baseline characteristics are reported as percentages, means (SD), and medians (IQR), as appropriate. The primary outcome of 28-day mortality was analyzed using the chi-square test and reported as relative risk and risk difference. Categorical outcomes were compared using the chi-square or Fisher exact test, and continuous variables were compared using the t test or Wilcoxon test, as appropriate. Kaplan–Meier methods and Cox proportional hazards regression were used to compare mortality endpoints, with time to death calculated from hour 0 to the date of death. Differences in organ support-free days were analyzed using competing-risk regression [20], with death as the competing event.

All analyses were performed using R, version 4.3.4 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Results

Participants

A total of 128 patients were enrolled and randomized to the ST group (n = 65) or the HA group (n = 63) (Fig. 1). The median age was 67 years, and 54% were male. Baseline characteristics were well balanced between groups at randomization (Table 1). The median interval from randomization to hour 0 was 6 h (fixed) in the ST group and 7.5 (IQR 5.0–13.0) hours in the HA group. Characteristics at hour 0 are shown in Table 2.

Primary outcome: 28-day mortality

The 28-day mortality was 58% in the ST group (38 of 65 participants) and 44% in the HA group (28 of 63 participants; relative risk of death in HA compared with ST, 0.76; 95% CI, 0.54–1.07; P = 0.16; Table 3). The hazard ratio for 28-day mortality in the HA group compared with the ST group was 0.68 (95% CI, 0.44–1.07; P = 0.09; Fig. 2).

In a post-hoc Cox proportional hazards model additionally adjusted for IL-6 (log-transformed) and VIS at hour 0, the hazard ratio for 28-day mortality in the HA group was 0.62 (95% CI, 0.39–0.97; P = 0.037). Full details of model derivation and diagnostics are provided in the Supplementary Material.

Mortality outcomes and organ support use

ICU mortality and hospital mortality were comparable between the two groups. Days alive and free of vasopressors, mechanical ventilation, RRT, and ICU through day 28 did not differ between groups (Table 3). Cumulative incidence analyses accounting for death as a competing event showed consistently comparable results (Fig. 3).

Vasopressor dose and other hemodynamic parameters

Shock reversal at hour 6 was achieved in 62% of participants in the ST group and 71% in the HA group (RR, 1.16; 95% CI, 0.91–1.49; P = 0.3).

Vasopressor dose, expressed as VIS, decreased progressively over the 48-hour treatment period in both groups (Fig. 4). No statistically significant between-group differences were demonstrated at any time point. MAP remained above the initial target of 65 mmHg throughout the treatment period in both groups.

Inflammatory markers

Serum CRP and plasma IL-6 levels at hour 0 were comparable between groups and did not differ significantly at hours 24 or 48 (Fig. 5). Both markers showed considerable variability within groups across all time points.

Discussion

In this multicenter randomized controlled trial that was terminated early, 128 patients with septic shock requiring high-dose vasopressors were randomly assigned to standard treatment alone or standard treatment with adjunctive hemoadsorption with HA-330. By day 28, 38 of 65 (58%) participants in the ST group and 28 of 63 (44%) in the HA group had died (RR 0.76; 95% CI 0.54–1.07; P = 0.16). The hazard ratio for death in the HA group was 0.68 (95% CI 0.44–1.07; P = 0.09). The confidence interval spans from a clinically meaningful reduction in mortality to no effect, and the primary result should be interpreted accordingly.

Two factors limited the precision of this primary outcome estimation. First, the trial was terminated before reaching its planned sample size of 206 after enrolling 128 participants over 55 months, due to slow recruitment and funding exhaustion. At both prespecified interim analyses, the observed Z statistics crossed neither the efficacy nor the futility boundary, although the treatment signal remained directionally consistent throughout (Z = − 1.21 and − 1.59). Second, the trial was powered for 20% absolute reduction in 28-day mortality, whereas the observed 14% absolute reduction was realized. The ambitious anticipated effect size was chosen to achieve a practical sample size for an invasive and resource-intensive intervention for which robust evidence was lacking. The available randomized trial on HA-330 showed no significant mortality benefit [10], and the most optimistic available RCT data came from EUPHAS (with 21% absolute reduction in 28-day mortality), which evaluated polymyxin B hemoperfusion, a mechanistically distinct adsorber [21].

A distinct design feature of this trial was the definition of hour 0 (the start of the treatment period) as the reference point of all outcomes. This was prespecified based on our pilot experience that variable preparation time before hemoadsorption introduced meaningful changes between the randomization and treatment initiation. Ultimately, the observed median time from randomization to hour 0 was 6 h in the ST group (fixed) and 7.5 (IQR 5.0–13.0) in the HA group, and parameters at hour 0 were comparable between the two groups. Although the predefined analysis was on the mITT population comprising all participants who reached hour 0, no participants were excluded on this criteria, making the mITT population identical to the full ITT population.

In a post-hoc Cox model additionally adjusted for IL-6 (log-transformed) and VIS at hour 0, the hazard ratio was 0.62 (95% CI 0.39–0.97; P = 0.037). Covariate selection was based on two considerations: biological rationale, as both are established independent predictors of mortality in septic shock, and study design considerations, as the variable interval from randomization to hour 0 introduced individual-level physiological changes occurring during the preparation period that are not reflected in characteristics recorded at randomization. Adjustment for IL-6 and VIS at hour 0 therefore accounts for this within-patient variation rather than any systematic between-group difference, which was not observed. This analysis was not prespecified and should be interpreted as exploratory, serving to contextualize the primary result rather than to establish an independent efficacy claim. The consistent direction across unadjusted and adjusted analyses supports the signal for treatment benefit despite the trial being underpowered.

Between-group comparisons of vasopressor dose, CRP and IL-6 across all time points were non-significant, raising a question about biological plausibility of this signal in mortality benefit in the adjusted model. Two considerations are relevant on the issue of inflammatory mediators. First, the absence of a systemic reduction of IL-6 at hours 24 and 48 does not signify that the adsorber was biologically inert. In a state of active hyperinflammation, ongoing cytokine production can substantially exceed extracorporeal clearance capacity, such that meaningful per-pass removal may not lead to detectable change in circulating levels [6, 22]. Second, HA-330 removes a broad molecular spectrum in the 10–60 kDa range, and the relevant biological activity of the adsorber may not be captured by IL-6 as a single surrogate [8].

A separate methodological explanation applies to the vasopressor dose outcome. The absence of a standardized weaning protocol may have contributed to the lack of between-group difference in vasopressor doses. In a cohort with severe, refractory shock and a 60% prevalence of pre-existing hypertension, individualized MAP targets above the guideline-recommended initial 65 mmHg threshold are routinely employed through MAP challenge protocols [5, 23], potentially prolonging vasopressor use in both groups independently of hemoadsorption effect. Furthermore, the comparison of vasopressor dose at hours 24 and 48 is additionally subject to informative dropout: participants who died earliest likely had the most refractory vasopressor requirements. With 11 and 6 early deaths in the ST and HA groups respectively within 48 h, more of the most severely ill participants in the ST group contributed no vasopressor data at later timepoints, creating a not-missing-at-random structure that can attenuate a true between-group difference.

Regarding adverse events, the most prominent was hypotension during hemoadsorption which occurred in 46% of the participants in the HA group. Most episodes were managed with brief interruptions of the procedure, and only 3 sessions (in 2 participants) were terminated early due to hemodynamic instability. New-onset thrombocytopenia occurred in 28% of HA participants compared with 18% of ST participants (P = 0.18). Thrombocytopenia during extracorporeal therapy is a recognized complication of contact activation between blood and the circuit surface and is usually transient [10, 24]. In our cohort, platelet counts at day 7 were similar between groups (81 [IQR 30–181] in ST vs. 77 [IQR 39–115] in HA; P = 0.4), aligning with the established data.

Across the current hemoadsorption literature, three design dimensions appear to be the drivers of heterogeneity in results: patient selection, treatment timing, and dosing.

Patient selection. The strongest evidence that enrichment strategy determines trial outcomes comes from endotoxin-targeted therapy with polymyxin B. A post-hoc analysis of EUPHRATES, which enrolled patients with an endotoxin activity assay (EAA) ≥ 0.60, suggested that patients with EAA > 0.90 may lie beyond the window of therapeutic modifiability [25, 26]. TIGRIS subsequently applied a narrowed EAA range of 0.60–0.89 and demonstrated a posterior probability of 28-day mortality benefit [27], directly supporting the rationale for biomarker-based enrichment.

For non-mechanism-specific adsorbers such as HA-330, the complexity of the inflammatory cascade makes targeting a specific biomarker more difficult, as circulating cytokine levels and their changes may capture only a portion of the cascade [8]. Although IL-6 level was measured at hour 0 in our trial, the turnaround time precluded its use as real-time enrollment criteria. We opted to use norepinephrine dose of ≥ 0.2 mcg/kg/min (a dose independently associated with mortality in our center [15]) as the enrichment criterion, reflecting the established correlation between vasopressor requirement and systemic cytokine burden [11, 12]. In an exploratory subgroup analysis stratified by VIS at enrollment on our cohort, participants with VIS ≥ 38 (above the median) had a relative risk of 28-day mortality of 0.66 (95% CI 0.42–1.04), compared with 0.87 (95% CI 0.52–1.46) in those with VIS < 38 (P for interaction = 0.358). The trial was not powered to detect effect modification, and this analysis should be interpreted accordingly. Nonetheless, the direction supports the hypothesis that patients with more severe vasopressor-dependent shock may attain greater benefit from hemoadsorption. This result aligns with that of TIGRIS, which demonstrated a larger mortality difference in patients receiving norepinephrine > 0.1 mcg/kg/min [27]. Taken together, these findings support vasopressor dose as a clinically available surrogate for enrichment in future hemoadsorption trials.

Treatment timing. The premise that earlier intervention has greater potential to interrupt an evolving inflammatory cascade draws support from data on corticosteroids use in septic shock, where earlier initiation has been associated with more rapid shock reversal [28, 29]. In the hemoadsorption literature, the trials with positive signals on clinical benefit all initiated treatment early after meeting septic shock criteria or the eligibility threshold [21, 25, 27], whereas trials without such signal enrolled patients over wider or less specified time windows [10, 22]. A proof-of-concept randomized trial of CytoSorb enrolling patients within 24 h of ICU admission or septic shock onset demonstrated significant reductions in norepinephrine requirements and procalcitonin [30], although the small sample precludes clinical inference. Whether timing in itself drives outcome heterogeneity across trials, or it is confounded by differences in enrichment strategy and adsorber mechanism cannot be determined from available data. Nonetheless, this evidence supports prospective evaluation of predefined time-to-treatment windows in future trials [31, 32].

Dosing. The recommended treatment duration for HA-330 is 2–2.5 h per session, with a manufacturer-specified maximum of 6 h [9]. We administered two 3-hour sessions at hour 0 and hour 24, a duration informed by pilot experience demonstrating higher circuit clotting rates with longer sessions. In contrast, current hemoadsorption protocols, particularly those using oXiris or CytoSorb, typically deliver hemoadsorption over extended periods during CRRT [33–35]. However, progressive slowing of elimination efficiency over a long treatment period, consistent with device saturation has been established in other type of adsorber [22, 36]. The underlying challenge is that in a state of active hyperinflammation, cytokine production may substantially exceed extracorporeal clearance capacity, and circulating concentrations may not reflect tissue cytokine dynamics [6]. Data on HA-330-specific cytokine kinetics are limited: a cohort study using HA-330 in combination with daily CVVHDF and daily cartridge changes showed non-significant reductions in inflammatory mediators over the treatment course [37], whereas results from studies using shorter, intermittent sessions similar to our protocol were conflicting [10, 24]. None of these studies demonstrated significant improvement in clinical outcomes, though their small sample sizes preclude direct comparison. The absence of significant IL-6 and CRP reductions in our study may reflect insufficient cumulative hemoadsorption dose, within-session adsorber saturation, or overproduction of cytokines described above [6]. The optimal dosing regimen for HA-330 and pharmacokinetic characterization is a prerequisite for the rational design of future trials.

This trial has several strengths. The multicenter, randomized design with a prespecified clinical enrichment strategy addressing a major deficiency in prior hemoadsorption literature. The prespecified group sequential design with documented interim Z statistics provides a transparent trajectory of the treatment effect prior to administrative termination.

The primary limitation is reduced power from early termination, resulting in wide confidence interval that span from clinically meaningful benefit to no treatment effect, and the study should be interpreted accordingly. Second, the open-label design, unavoidable for a procedure trial, can introduce biases in co-intervention, although the use of objective primary outcome of mortality helps mitigate this risk. Third, the restriction to two 3-hour sessions may have limited the adsorptive dose, and extended or repeated sessions to improve cytokine clearance remains untested. Finally, EAA was not measured, precluding biomarker-based subgroup analyses, and limiting direct comparison with endotoxin-targeted trials.

Conclusions

In this randomized trial that was terminated early due to slow recruitment and funding constraints, adjunctive hemoadsorption with HA-330 in patients with septic shock requiring high-dose vasopressors did not statistically reduce 28-day mortality compared with standard treatment alone. A post-hoc analysis adjusting for IL-6 (log-transformed) and VIS at the start of treatment was associated with a statistically significant reduction in the hazard of death in the hemoadsorption group. These findings support the plausibility of cytokine adsorption in patients with severe vasopressor-dependent septic shock but should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Patient enrichment, treatment timing, and dosing optimization should be considered as the critical design features for future definitive trials.
