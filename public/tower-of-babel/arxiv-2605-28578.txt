A Generalized Tikhonov Layer for Interpretable-by-design Graph Neural Networks
Nicolas Tremblay, Benjamin Ricaud, Filippo Maria Bianchi

Abstract
We propose the Tikhonov layer , a graph neural network layer that is interpretable by design: once trained, its learned parameters directly reveal which node features and which aspects of the graph topology were leveraged for prediction. In practice, the layer’s propagation matrix takes the closed-form 𝖱 = ( p ⁡ ( 𝖫 ) + 𝖰 ) − 1 ​ 𝖰 \mathsf{R}=(p(\mathsf{L})+\mathsf{Q})^{-1}\mathsf{Q} , where 𝖫 \mathsf{L} is the normalized graph Laplacian, 𝖰 = diag ⁡ ( q 1 , … , q n ) \mathsf{Q}=\mathrm{diag}(q_{1},\ldots,q_{n}) a learnable diagonal matrix of positive node-importance scores, and p ⁡ ( ⋅ ) p(\cdot) a learnable polynomial. For any input feature 𝒙 \bm{x} , the layer output 𝖱 ​ 𝒙 \mathsf{R}\bm{x} is the exact minimizer of a generalized graph Tikhonov problem that trades off node-level data fidelity against a topology-driven regularization penalty. The learned pair { { q i } , p } \{\{q_{i}\},p\} constitutes a built-in explanation: large q i q_{i} indicates that node i i ’s own features drive the prediction, while small q i q_{i} signals reliance on the local graph topology; the shape of p p reveals whether homophily, heterophily, or a band-pass response is exploited. Expressivity is preserved by routing complexity through a dedicated, arbitrarily deep 𝖰 \mathsf{Q} -network that produces the importance scores, while the Tikhonov layer itself remains transparent. We prove that distinct node-importance matrices yield distinct propagation operators, structurally coupling the explanation to the computation. Additionally, the Tikhonov layer provides, in a single layer, a global receptive field, mitigating both oversmoothing and oversquashing. Experiments on standard graph classification benchmarks confirm that the model matches (and sometimes outperforms) opaque baselines while producing interpretable and faithful explanations.

Introduction

Graph neural networks (GNNs) have seen rapid development over the last decade, advancing expressivity and performance on tasks such as graph and node classification. In graph machine learning, the data is twofold. On one hand is the structure, often represented as a graph

Research questions.

In the context of GNNs, interpretability is arguably even more complex than in classical neural networks: one must explain not only how the input features are progressively transformed and leveraged throughout the network, but also how the graph topology itself is exploited during the process. Are both the features and the topology used to solve the task? Or perhaps only the features or only the topology? Or perhaps only the features on some parts of the graph and the topology elsewhere? When the topology is used, how is it leveraged?

Additional properties.

Beyond interpretability, the Tikhonov layer naturally mitigates the effect of two notorious pathologies of deep
GNNs, namely
oversmoothing
and
oversquashing
.

Oversmoothing.
Stacking many message-passing layers causes all node representations to collapse
[
17
]
. Our architecture avoids involuntary oversmoothing in two ways. First, using a
single
Tikhonov layer avoids depth-induced accumulation. Second, since
p(\lambda)>0
on all of
[0,2]
, the operator
p(\mathsf{L})
is invertible and
\mathsf{R}=(p(\mathsf{L})+\mathsf{Q})^{-1}\mathsf{Q}\to\mathbf{0}
as all
q_{i}\to 0
: the only way to collapse node representations is to drive all importance scores to zero, which is a deliberate model choice rather than an unavoidable pathology.

Oversquashing.
In message-passing GNNs, long-range sensitivity
|\partial(\mathsf{R}\bm{x})_{i}/\partial x_{j}|
decays exponentially with the hop-distance
d_{\mathcal{G}}(i,j)
at a rate that is fixed by the architecture, regardless of model width
[
1
]
. In the Tikhonov layer the analogous quantity is
|\mathsf{R}_{ij}|
, which decays exponentially with
d_{\mathcal{G}}(i,j)
at rate
2/(K\sqrt{\kappa})
with
K
the degree of
p(\cdot)
and
\kappa=\operatorname{cond}_{2}(\mathsf{I}+\mathsf{Q}^{-1/2}p(\mathsf{L})\mathsf{Q}^{-1/2})
(Proposition
3.5
). Crucially,
\kappa
is
learnable
: when the model drives some
q_{i}
’s small,
\kappa
grows and
2/(K\sqrt{\kappa})\to 0
, making the decay negligible. The model thus learns to adjust its spatial reach end-to-end, trading local precision against long-range sensitivity through the
q_{i}
’s.

Note that oversquashing and oversmoothing can occur in the Q-network as it is a standard potentially deep GNN: mitigation strategies from the literature should be applied to address them as needed.

Spectral and Laplacian-based GNNs.

Early spectral GNNs
[

defined convolutions via the eigendecomposition of

All of these use polynomials of

Our

APPNP

Attention and node importance in GNNs.

Graph attention networks
[

Our

Unlike an attention coefficient, which only affects its two endpoints, a single

Self-explainable and interpretable GNNs.

Our model is a

GNN (SE-GNN): interpretability is built into the architecture, not recovered post-hoc via methods such as

Typical SE-GNNs such as

This factorization is critically vulnerable:

Our architecture avoids this two-module failure mode since there is no detached explanatory subgraph passed to a classifier as an arbitrary code.
The prediction pipeline is a

Also, rather than selecting a discrete subgraph, the Tikhonov layer assigns a continuous scalar importance

3
Methodology

In its general form, the multi-channel Tikhonov layer we propose can be written as a
J
-channel layer:
\sigma\left(\mathsf{R}_{1}\mathsf{X}\mathsf{W}|\cdots|\mathsf{R}_{J}\mathsf{X}\mathsf{W}\right)
,
where (i) the notation
\left(\mathsf{M}_{1}|\cdots|\mathsf{M}_{J}\right)
represents column-wise concatenation of the matrices
\{\mathsf{M}_{j}\}
; (ii)
\mathsf{R}_{j}
reads
\mathsf{R}_{j}=\left(p_{j}(\mathsf{L})+\mathsf{Q}_{j}\right)^{-1}\mathsf{Q}_{j}
where
p_{j}(\cdot)
are learnable polynomials verifying
0<p_{j}(\lambda)<1
on
[0,2]
. Each channel enjoys the variational interpretation of Eq. (
2
); (iii) The
\mathsf{Q}_{j}
’s are diagonal matrices of positive node-importance parameters. Each
\mathsf{Q}_{j}
is built via a dedicated
\mathsf{Q}
-network: there are thus
J
independent
\mathsf{Q}
-networks; (iv) there is only one learnable matrix weight
\mathsf{W}
. This uniqueness enables identifiability even when
J>1
(see Proposition
3.8
).

Each channel
j
learns a global graph filter
p_{j}(\lambda)
along with where on the graph and with what intensity it should be applied on the features, via the diagonal entries of
\mathsf{Q}_{j}
. If the data exhibits both homophily and heterophily at different locations and/or scales, the multi-channel design enables the model to learn different local behaviors.
This is similar to the “multi-head attention” in transformers, where each head can learn different patterns. In our experimental section, we will set
J=1
.

3.1
Theoretical properties

We establish the fundamental properties of the Tikhonov layer. Unless stated otherwise, all results are for the single-channel case (extensions to multi-channel are immediate); proofs are in appendix
A
. Let us recall our notation:
\mathsf{L}
is the normalized Laplacian with eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{k}\}
,
p(\cdot)
a polynomial of order
K>0

0<p(\lambda)<1
for
\lambda\in[0,2]
,
\mathsf{Q}
a diagonal matrix of strictly positive entries,
\mathsf{R}=(p(\mathsf{L})+\mathsf{Q})^{-1}\mathsf{Q}

Definition 3.1

(Complete
K
-hop support)
.

Let
\mathbf{P}=p(\mathbf{L})
. It is well-known that
\mathbf{P}
is
K
-localized:
d_{\mathcal{G}}(i,j)>K\implies P_{ij}=0
. We say that
\mathbf{P}
has
complete
K
-hop support
if the converse also holds:

P_{ij}\neq 0\iff d_{\mathcal{G}}(i,j)\leq K.

Assuming that
\mathsf{P}
has complete
K
-hop support is a very mild condition. For instance, if the coefficients of
p
are drawn at random from a continuous distribution—as is typically done during initialization before training—then
\mathsf{P}
has complete
K
-hop support almost surely; see Lemma
A.1
for a precise statement. Moreover, we only use this assumption to simplify claims (iii) of Propositions
3.1
and
3.4
.

Proposition 3.1

(Basic properties of
\mathsf{R}
)
.

(i) The inverse of
p(\mathsf{L})+\mathsf{Q}
exists:
\mathsf{R}
is well-defined. (ii) The spectrum of
\mathsf{R}
is contained in
(0,1)
; in particular,
\mathsf{R}
is injective. (iii) Under complete
K
-hop support,
\mathsf{R}
is symmetric if and only if the
q_{i}
’s are constant on each connected component of
\mathcal{G}
.

Point (iii) means that heterogeneous
q_{i}
’s make the influence of node
j
on node
i
differ from that of node
i
on node
j
: this asymmetric coupling lets the model represent direction-dependent influence.

Proposition 3.2

(Effect of
q_{i}
on feature preservation and local
p(L)
-harmonicity)
.

Let
p_{\text{max}}=\max_{k}p(\lambda_{k})
. Set
\bm{z}=\mathsf{R}\bm{x}
. For every
i\in\mathcal{V}
:

|z_{i}-x_{i}|\leq\frac{p_{\text{max}}}{q_{i}}\,\|\bm{x}\|_{2},\qquad|[p(\mathsf{L})\bm{z}]_{i}|\;\leq\;\sqrt{q_{i}}\,\sqrt{p_{\text{max}}}\,\left\|\bm{x}\right\|_{2}.

Thus, when
q_{i}\gg p_{\text{max}}
, node
i
’s features are preserved.
Conversely, when
q_{i}\ll p_{\text{max}}
, node
i
’s output is nearly
p(\mathsf{L})
-harmonic: its value
is dictated by the graph topology rather than by its own feature.

Proposition 3.3

(Spectral density and strict generalization)
.

The family of homogeneous Tikhonov operators
\{(p(\mathsf{L})+q\mathsf{I})^{-1}q\mathsf{I}\}
with
q>0
and
0<p(\lambda)<1
on
[0,2]
is dense in the uniform norm in the space of continuous spectral filters
\{g(\mathsf{L})\mid g:[0,2]\to(0,1)\}
. Furthermore,
\mathsf{R}=(p(\mathsf{L})+\mathsf{Q})^{-1}\mathsf{Q}
corresponds to a spectral filter
g(\mathsf{L})
if and only if
\mathsf{Q}
is homogeneous (
\mathsf{Q}=q\mathsf{I}
). For heterogeneous
\mathsf{Q}
,
\mathsf{R}
generically does not commute with
\mathsf{L}
, strictly extending the expressivity of spectral graph filters.

In the homogeneous case (
\mathsf{Q}=q\mathsf{I}
), the Tikhonov operator’s rational parameterization matches the expressivity of polynomial spectral GNNs. A heterogeneous
\mathsf{Q}
breaks the stationarity assumption of traditional spectral methods, enabling flexible, highly expressive
node-adaptive filtering
.

Proposition 3.4

(Global receptive field)
.

(i)
\mathsf{R}_{ij}=0
if
i,j
belong to different connected components. (ii) If
p(\lambda)=a+b\lambda
with
b>0
, then
\mathsf{R}_{ij}>0
for every
(i,j)
in
the same connected component. (iii) More generally for any complete
K
-hop support
p
: for almost every
\mathsf{Q}
(
i.e.
, for all
\mathsf{Q}
outside a measure-zero exceptional set):
R_{ij}\neq 0
for every
(i,j)
in the same connected component.

Thus, generically in
\mathsf{Q}
, the Tikhonov operator
\mathsf{R}
has a global receptive field.
Increasing the polynomial degree
K
enriches the set of representable spectral filters but does
not
enlarge the spatial reach, which is already global for
K=1
.
While non-zero entries are good news to mitigate oversquashing, they alone do not prevent it: the magnitude of
\mathsf{R}_{ij}
, quantified by the next proposition, is what truly matters.

Proposition 3.5

(Spatial decay of
\mathsf{R}
)
.

Let the graph be connected and
\kappa
be the condition number of
\,\mathsf{I}+\mathsf{Q}^{-1/2}p(\mathsf{L})\,\mathsf{Q}^{-1/2}
.
Then, for any pair
(i,j)
:

|\mathsf{R}_{ij}|\;\leq\;2\sqrt{\frac{q_{j}}{q_{i}}}\;\exp\!\left(-\frac{2}{K\sqrt{\kappa}}d_{\mathcal{G}}(i,j)\right).

This bound makes the oversquashing behavior precise. Both the prefactor

2\sqrt{q_{j}/q_{i}}
and the decay rate
2/(K\sqrt{\kappa})
depend on the learned
\mathsf{Q}
. When all
q_{i}
are large,
\kappa\approx 1
and the decay is rapid, making the layer act locally, similarly to a standard spectral filter. Conversely, when some
q_{i}
entries are small, long-range sensitivity is boosted by two coupled effects: (i) the prefactor
\sqrt{q_{j}/q_{i}}
amplifies distant contributions at destination nodes with small
q_{i}
; (ii)
\kappa
increases, weakening the exponential attenuation across the graph. Oversquashing is thus not absent by default, but
controllable by design
: the model learns the
q_{i}
’s end-to-end and can allocate long-range sensitivity where needed.

Proposition 3.6

(Structural coupling of
\mathsf{Q}
and
\mathsf{R}
)
.

The map

\mathsf{Q}\mapsto(p(\mathsf{L})+\mathsf{Q})^{-1}\mathsf{Q}
is injective on
the set of diagonal matrices with strictly positive entries. In particular,
two models sharing the same
p
,
\mathsf{W}
, and MLP head but differing in
their
\mathsf{Q}\neq\mathsf{Q}^{\prime}
produce distinct propagation
operators.

Unlike in two-module SE-GNN architectures, where a classifier can treat the extracted explanation as a separate label code, here
\mathsf{Q}
directly defines the propagation operator seen by the MLP head.
Thus, the explanation cannot be changed without changing the Tikhonov computation, which guarantees faithfulness of
\mathsf{Q}
to the induced propagation operator.

Proposition 3.7

(Joint identifiability of
(p,\mathsf{Q})
)
.

Let
(p_{1},\mathsf{Q}_{1})
and
(p_{2},\mathsf{Q}_{2})
be two pairs with diagonal
\mathsf{Q}_{j}\succ 0
and polynomials
0<p_{j}(\lambda)<1
on
[0,2]
.
If both pairs yield the same operator,

(p_{1}(\mathsf{L})+\mathsf{Q}_{1})^{-1}\mathsf{Q}_{1}=(p_{2}(\mathsf{L})+\mathsf{Q}_{2})^{-1}\mathsf{Q}_{2}
,
then there exists
\alpha>0
such that
\mathsf{Q}_{2}=\alpha\,\mathsf{Q}_{1}
and
p_{2}(\mathsf{L})=\alpha\,p_{1}(\mathsf{L})
.

The only operator-level non-identifiability is a global rescaling:
(p(\mathsf{L}),\mathsf{Q})
and
(\alpha p(\mathsf{L}),\alpha\mathsf{Q})
produce the same
\mathsf{R}
since the factor cancels in
(p(\mathsf{L})+\mathsf{Q})^{-1}\mathsf{Q}
.
This is benign: the
relative
pattern of the
q_{i}
’s, driving interpretation, is invariant to such rescaling.

Proposition 3.8

(Multi-channel identifiability)
.

Let
\mathsf{H}=\mathsf{X}\mathsf{W}
have rank
n
.
Consider the pre-activation
J
-channel layer output
\left(\mathsf{R}_{1}\mathsf{H}\mid\cdots\mid\mathsf{R}_{J}\mathsf{H}\right)
with
\mathsf{R}_{j}=(p_{j}(\mathsf{L})+\mathsf{Q}_{j})^{-1}\mathsf{Q}_{j}
.
If two parameter sets
\{(p_{j},\mathsf{Q}_{j})\}_{j=1}^{J}
and

\{(p^{\prime}_{j},\mathsf{Q}^{\prime}_{j})\}_{j=1}^{J}
yield the same output up to a permutation of the channel blocks, then there exists a permutation
\pi
of
\{1,\ldots,J\}
and
positive scalars
\alpha_{j}
such that, for every
j
,

p^{\prime}_{j}(\mathsf{L})=\alpha_{j}\,p_{\pi(j)}(\mathsf{L})
and

\mathsf{Q}^{\prime}_{j}=\alpha_{j}\,\mathsf{Q}_{\pi(j)}
.
In other words, up to the usual finite-spectrum ambiguity for polynomials, the only non-identifiabilities are per-channel rescaling
and channel permutation.

If
J
independent weight matrices
\{\mathsf{W}_{j}\}
are used instead of the unique
\mathsf{W}
above, a
cross-channel compensation becomes possible: one channel’s
\mathsf{W}_{j}
can
absorb work from another, breaking per-channel identifiability. Sharing

\mathsf{W}
across channels eliminates this issue at a modest cost in
expressivity, and is recommended in our framework, where interpretability is a priority.

3.2
Practical implementation details

Matrix inversion.
Computing
\mathsf{RX}=\left(p(\mathsf{L})+\mathsf{Q}\right)^{-1}\mathsf{Q}\mathsf{X}
for a feature matrix
\mathsf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
requires solving
d
linear systems with the symmetric positive definite matrix
\mathsf{M}=p(\mathsf{L})+\mathsf{Q}
. An exact sparse Cholesky factorization costs up to
\mathcal{O}\left(dn^{2}+(Km)^{3/2}\right)
, where
m
is the number of edges and
K=\deg(p)
. While cheaper than dense
\mathcal{O}(n^{3})
inversion, this is significantly more expensive than standard GNN layers (
e.g.
, GIN or GCN) operating in
\mathcal{O}(dm)
. We therefore use Preconditioned Conjugate Gradient (PCG).
PCG solves
\mathsf{M}\bm{z}=\mathsf{Q}\bm{x}
iteratively using matrix-vector products with
\mathsf{M}
at
O(Km)
per iteration. The number of iterations before convergence scales as
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\kappa(\mathsf{M})})
, where
\kappa(\mathsf{M})
is the condition number. With a diagonal (Jacobi) preconditioner
\mathsf{P}=\mathrm{diag}(\mathsf{M})
, which is trivial to compute, the condition number is significantly reduced, typically yielding convergence in a small number of iterations. For
d
right-hand sides, the total cost is
O(dTKm)
, where
T
is the number of PCG iterations, yielding much better scalability than sparse Cholesky on large graphs.

Remark 1

(Effective receptive field of PCG)
.

With no preconditioner or a diagonal preconditioner such as Jacobi, each solver iteration applies node-wise rescaling and multiplication by
\mathsf{M}
. Since
\mathsf{M}
mixes information across at most
K
hops, after
T
iterations the approximation’s effective reach is at most
(T-1)K
hops. Thus, while the exact Tikhonov operator is global (Proposition
3.4
), a truncated PCG solve acts as a local polynomial approximation unless
T
is large. This reflects a solver limitation, not a message-passing bottleneck: each iterate remains linear in
\bm{x}
without nonlinear compression. The true long-range sensitivity is governed by the learned
\mathsf{Q}
(Proposition
3.5
);
T
merely controls approximation accuracy. For tasks requiring long-range interactions,
T
should be set to large values (typically on the order of the graph diameter).

To eliminate memory overhead and numerical instability, we use implicit differentiation rather than backpropagating through the solver iterations.
Let
\bm{z}=\mathsf{M}^{-1}\mathsf{Q}\bm{x}
be the forward output and
\bar{\bm{z}}=\partial\ell/\partial\bm{z}
the incoming gradient from the loss
\ell
.
Differentiating
\mathsf{M}\bm{z}=\mathsf{Q}\bm{x}
with respect to
q_{i}
(for instance) gives
\partial_{q_{i}}\mathsf{M}\bm{z}+\mathsf{M}\partial_{q_{i}}\bm{z}=\partial_{q_{i}}\mathsf{Q}\bm{x}
,
i.e.
:
\partial_{q_{i}}\bm{z}=\mathsf{M}^{-1}\partial_{q_{i}}\mathsf{Q}(\bm{x}-\bm{z})
. Applying the chain rule:

\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial q_{i}}=\bar{\bm{z}}^{\top}\mathsf{M}^{-1}\partial_{q_{i}}\mathsf{Q}(\bm{x}-\bm{z})=u_{i}(x_{i}-z_{i})

where
\bm{u}=\mathsf{M}^{-1}\bar{\bm{z}}
is the adjoint variable. Computing
\bm{u}
is the only expensive step and requires one additional PCG solve with the same matrix
\mathsf{M}
.
Gradients with respect to
\bm{x}
and polynomial coefficients can also be expressed via
\bm{u}
.
Thus, we run PCG in the forward pass without storing the computational graph, and use this single adjoint solve for all gradients in the backward pass.

Bounding the
q_{i}
’s.
The
\mathsf{Q}
-network produces unconstrained scalar outputs
\tilde{q}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
for each node
i
. To map these to strictly positive values while ensuring numerical stability, we apply
q_{i}=\exp\!\big(\min(\tilde{q}_{i},\,\log q_{\max})\big)+q_{\min}

which clamps in log-space to prevent overflow in the exponential and adds a floor
q_{\min}
to guarantee strict positivity. Despite this clamping, the condition number can still be substantial, making preconditioning essential for efficient CG convergence.

Enforcing
0<p(\cdot)<1
on
[0,2]
.
This constraint is enforced via Bernstein polynomials, writing
p(\lambda)=\sum_{k=0}^{K}\operatorname{sigmoid}(\theta_{k})\,b_{k,K}\!\left(\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\right)
with
\{\theta_{k}\}
real learnable parameters and Bernstein basis functions
b_{k,K}(t)=\binom{K}{k}t^{k}(1-t)^{K-k}
that are all positive for
t\in[0,1]
. Since
\operatorname{sigmoid}(\theta_{k})\in(0,1)
and
\sum_{k}b_{k,K}(t)=1
on
t\in[0,1]
, this parametrization naturally enforces
0<p(\lambda)<1
for all
\lambda\in[0,2]
.

The CSBM

4.2
Resistance to oversquashing

We evaluate on the ECHO-diam task from the ECHO benchmark
[

Intuitively, the model identifies the endpoints of the longest path and uses their amplitude contrast with the interior as a proxy for the number of nodes lying between them.
The test MAE is
1.17

\sim 1.6
) and the best transformer (

), as reported in
[
21
]

5
Concluding remarks

We propose an original take on the problem of interpretability in GNNs. By severely constraining the form of the layer, we force the network to learn by-products (the node importance
q
-values and the graph filter
p(\lambda)
) that give us insight into how the network is solving the task. Interpretation is a difficult endeavour. Our method encounters two common drawbacks of the literature: i) some explanations can still be hard to understand for us humans (see, for instance, the second explanation for ECHO-diam discussed in Appendix
D
), ii) some tasks have many different explanations (see, for instance, the second explanation for Triangles discussed in Appendix
D
). Further research could be to add an auxiliary loss to favor some explanations rather than others. A limitation of the Tikhonov layer is its computational cost in cases where the task requires PCG to approximate long-distance interactions encoded in
\mathsf{R}
(as one needs to increase the number of PCG iterations). Promising solutions come from random numerical linear algebra that are able to obtain Monte-Carlo approximations of
\mathsf{R}\bm{x}
(for now only in the case where
p(\mathsf{L})=\mathsf{L}
), without computing
\mathsf{R}
, via carefully designed random walks on the graph
[
4
]
, for a total cost linear in
n
.

Density of the homogeneous family.

Choose any

[\mathsf{R},\mathsf{L}]=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad[\mathsf{R}^{-1},\mathsf{L}]=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad[\mathsf{Q}^{-1},\mathsf{L}]P=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad[\mathsf{Q},\mathsf{L}]=0.

where
[\mathsf{R},\mathsf{L}]=\mathsf{R}\mathsf{L}-\mathsf{L}\mathsf{R}
denotes the commutator.
For diagonal
\mathsf{Q}
,

([\mathsf{Q},\mathsf{L}])_{ij}=(q_{i}-q_{j})L_{ij},

so
\mathsf{Q}
commutes with
\mathsf{L}
iff
q_{i}=q_{j}
on every edge, i.e., iff
\mathsf{Q}
is constant on each connected component. Thus, on a connected graph, any nonconstant
\mathsf{Q}
gives an operator
\mathsf{R}
that does not commute with
\mathsf{L}
.

Finally, suppose that
\mathsf{R}=g(\mathsf{L})
for some scalar function
g
.
Then
\mathsf{R}
commutes with
\mathsf{L}
, so by the previous paragraph
\mathsf{Q}=q_{c}\mathsf{I}
on each connected component
G_{c}
.
On the zero-eigenvector supported on
G_{c}
,
\mathsf{R}
acts by the scalar

\frac{q_{c}}{p(0)+q_{c}}.

But
g(\mathsf{L})
acts as the same scalar
g(0)
on the whole nullspace of
\mathsf{L}
. Since
p(0)>0
, the map

q\mapsto\frac{q}{p(0)+q}

is strictly increasing, so all
q_{c}
must be equal.
Hence
\mathsf{Q}=q\mathsf{I}
.
Therefore,
\mathsf{R}
can be written as a scalar spectral filter
g(\mathsf{L})
only in the homogeneous case.
∎

Proof of Proposition
3.4
.

(i)
Define
\mathsf{M}=p(\mathsf{L})+\mathsf{Q}
. Since
\mathsf{R}_{ij}=(\mathsf{M}^{-1})_{ij}\,q_{j}
with
q_{j}>0
, the zero
pattern of
\mathsf{R}
is that of
\mathsf{M}^{-1}
.
Both
p(\mathsf{L})
and
\mathsf{Q}
are block-diagonal with respect to
connected components, hence so are
\mathsf{M}
and

\mathsf{M}^{-1}
, giving
\mathsf{R}_{ij}=0
between components.

(ii) Increasing degree-one case.

Write the increasing degree-one polynomial as
p(\lambda)=a+b\lambda
with
b>0
(and necessarily
a>0
as
0<p(\lambda)<1
must hold at
\lambda=0
), so that
\mathsf{M}=p(\mathsf{L})+\mathsf{Q}=a\mathsf{I}+b\mathsf{L}+\mathsf{Q}=b(\mathsf{L}+\mathsf{Q}^{\prime})
with
\mathsf{Q}^{\prime}=\frac{1}{b}\left(\mathsf{Q}+a\mathsf{I}\right)\succ 0
.
On each connected component,
\mathsf{L}+\mathsf{Q}^{\prime}
is a symmetric positive definite matrix with non-positive off-diagonal entries, and it is irreducible when the component has more than one node.
Hence, by the standard inverse-positivity of irreducible nonsingular
M
-matrices,

\left((\mathsf{L}+\mathsf{Q}^{\prime})^{-1}\right)_{ij}>0

for all
i,j
in the same connected component.
The singleton case is immediate.
Since:

\mathsf{R}=\mathsf{M}^{-1}\mathsf{Q}=\frac{1}{b}(\mathsf{L}+\mathsf{Q}^{\prime})^{-1}\mathsf{Q},

we get, for every pair
i,j
within the same connected component:

\mathsf{R}_{ij}=\frac{q_{j}}{b}\left((\mathsf{L}+\mathsf{Q}^{\prime})^{-1}\right)_{ij}>0.

(iii) Other cases.
Let us first treat the simple case
i=j
. One has:
\mathsf{R}_{ii}=\left(\mathsf{M}^{-1}\right)_{ii}q_{i}
with
\mathsf{M}=p(\mathsf{L})+\mathsf{Q}
. As
\mathsf{M}
is positive definite, so is
\mathsf{M}^{-1}
. A well-known fact of positive definite matrices is that their diagonal entries are always positive. As
q_{i}
is also positive, we have that
\forall i,\mathsf{R}_{ii}>0
.

Now, let
\mathcal{C}
be the set of all ordered pairs
(i,j)
of distinct vertices belonging to the same connected component of
\mathcal{G}
. Fix a pair
(i,j)\in\mathcal{C}
. Let the entries
q_{1},\dots,q_{n}
of the diagonal matrix
\mathsf{Q}
be treated as independent formal variables. By Cramer’s rule, the Tikhonov entry is given by

\mathsf{R}_{ij}=\bigl(\mathsf{M}^{-1}\bigr)_{ij}\,q_{j}=\frac{\mathsf{C}_{ji}(\mathsf{M})\,q_{j}}{\det(\mathsf{M})},

where
\mathsf{C}_{ji}(\mathsf{M})
is the
(j,i)
-th cofactor of
\mathsf{M}
. By the Leibniz formula for determinants, both
\det(\mathsf{M})
and the cofactors
\mathsf{C}_{ji}(\mathsf{M})
are multivariate polynomials in
\mathbb{R}[q_{1},\dots,q_{n}]
. Since
\mathsf{P}\succ 0
and
\mathsf{Q}\succ 0
,
\mathsf{M}
is positive definite, ensuring
\det(\mathsf{M})>0
for all positive diagonal
\mathsf{Q}
.

The cofactor is given by
\mathsf{C}_{ji}(\mathsf{M})=(-1)^{i+j}\det(\mathsf{M}_{\setminus j,\setminus i})
, where
\mathsf{M}_{\setminus j,\setminus i}
denotes the submatrix of
\mathsf{M}
obtained by deleting row
j
and column
i
. This submatrix is indexed by rows
\mathcal{V}\setminus\{j\}
and columns
\mathcal{V}\setminus\{i\}
, and each variable
q_{k}
(
k\neq i,j
) appears exactly once, in the entry corresponding to the original diagonal position
(k,k)
of
\mathsf{M}
, as part of the linear term
\mathsf{P}_{kk}+q_{k}
.

Since
i
and
j
lie in the same connected component, there exists a shortest path in the
K
-hop graph
(
i.e.
, the graph whose edges connect pairs of vertices at graph distance at most
K
) from
i
to
j
. Let
i=v_{0},v_{1},\dots,v_{m}=j
be such a shortest path, so that
d_{\mathcal{G}}(v_{r},v_{r+1})\leq K
for all
0\leq r<m
, and
m
is minimal. By the complete
K
-hop support hypothesis, each off-diagonal entry along this path satisfies
\mathsf{P}_{v_{r},v_{r+1}}\neq 0
.

Moreover, by minimality of the path, for any
r,s
with
|r-s|>1
we have
d_{\mathcal{G}}(v_{r},v_{s})>K
: otherwise, replacing the sub-path
v_{r},\dots,v_{s}
by the single hop
v_{r}\to v_{s}
would yield a strictly shorter path, contradicting minimality. By
K
-localization, it follows that
\mathsf{P}_{v_{r},v_{s}}=0
whenever
|r-s|>1
.

We now exhibit a monomial in the Leibniz expansion of
\det(\mathsf{M}_{\setminus j,\setminus i})
that is
not canceled
by any other term. Consider the permutation

\sigma^{*}:\quad v_{r}\longmapsto v_{r+1}\quad(0\leq r\leq m-1),\qquad k\longmapsto k\quad(k\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\{v_{0},\dots,v_{m}\}),

which is a valid bijection from
\mathcal{V}\setminus\{j\}=\mathcal{V}\setminus\{v_{m}\}
to
\mathcal{V}\setminus\{i\}=\mathcal{V}\setminus\{v_{0}\}
. This permutation selects: the off-diagonal constants
\mathsf{P}_{v_{r},v_{r+1}}
for the path vertices; and the linear diagonal terms
(\mathsf{P}_{kk}+q_{k})
for all
k\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\{v_{0},\dots,v_{m}\}
. Choosing the variable
q_{k}
from each such diagonal factor yields the monomial

\gamma_{ij}\cdot\prod_{k\,\notin\,\{v_{0},\dots,v_{m}\}}q_{k},\qquad\text{where}\quad\gamma_{ij}=\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma^{*})(-1)^{i+j}\prod_{r=0}^{m-1}\mathsf{P}_{v_{r},v_{r+1}}\neq 0.

This monomial involves precisely the variable set
\mathcal{S}=\{q_{k}\mid k\notin\{v_{0},\dots,v_{m}\}\}
and has degree
|\mathcal{S}|=n-(m+1)
.

We claim no other permutation
\tau\neq\sigma^{*}
contributes a term containing
\prod_{k\in\mathcal{S}}q_{k}
. Any such
\tau
must also fix all
k\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\{v_{0},\dots,v_{m}\}
(to select the diagonal factor
\mathsf{P}_{kk}+q_{k}
and thus include
q_{k}
), meaning
\tau
must act as a bijection from
\{v_{0},\dots,v_{m-1}\}
to
\{v_{1},\dots,v_{m}\}
on the path vertices. But since
P_{v_{r},v_{s}}=0
for
|r-s|>1
, the only bijection from
\{v_{0},\dots,v_{m-1}\}
to
\{v_{1},\dots,v_{m}\}
that yields a nonzero product
\prod_{r}\mathsf{M}_{v_{r},\tau(v_{r})}
is the forward shift
\sigma^{*}
itself. In other words,
\sigma^{*}
is the unique permutation contributing the monomial
\gamma_{ij}\prod_{k\in\mathcal{S}}q_{k}
: this term cannot be canceled and
C_{ji}(\mathsf{M})
is not identically zero as a polynomial in
\mathbb{R}[q_{1},\dots,q_{n}]
.

For each pair
(i,j)\in\mathcal{C}
, define the exceptional set

\mathfrak{E}_{ij}=\bigl\{\mathsf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{>0}\;\big|\;\mathsf{C}_{ji}(\mathsf{M})=0\bigr\},

which is the zero-set of a nonzero polynomial, hence an algebraic variety of Lebesgue measure zero in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Define the global exceptional set

\mathfrak{E}=\bigcup_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{C}}\mathfrak{E}_{ij}.

Since
\mathcal{G}
is finite,
|\mathcal{C}|\leq n(n-1)
, so
\mathfrak{E}
is a finite union of measure-zero sets and thus has Lebesgue measure zero.

For any positive diagonal matrix
\mathsf{Q}\notin\mathfrak{E}
, we have
\mathsf{C}_{ji}(\mathsf{M})\neq 0
for all
(i,j)\in\mathcal{C}
simultaneously. Since
q_{j}>0
and
\det(\mathsf{M})>0
, it follows that

\mathsf{R}_{ij}=\frac{\mathsf{C}_{ji}(\mathsf{M})\,q_{j}}{\det(\mathsf{M})}\neq 0

for every pair
(i,j)
in the same connected component, completing the proof.
∎

Proof of Proposition
3.5
.

Set
\mathsf{S}=\mathsf{I}+\mathsf{Q}^{-1/2}p(\mathsf{L})\,\mathsf{Q}^{-1/2}
such that

\mathsf{Q}^{1/2}\mathsf{S}\,\mathsf{Q}^{1/2}=p(\mathsf{L})+\mathsf{Q}
, giving

\mathsf{R}=\mathsf{Q}^{-1/2}\mathsf{S}^{-1}\mathsf{Q}^{1/2}
, hence

|\mathsf{R}_{ij}|=\sqrt{q_{j}/q_{i}}\;|(\mathsf{S}^{-1})_{ij}|
.
The matrix
\mathsf{S}
is SPD and is
K
-localized:
\mathsf{S}_{ij}=0
whenever

d_{\mathcal{G}}(i,j)>K
.
Write
d=d_{\mathcal{G}}(i,j)
.
By the Demko–Moss–Smith bound
[
11
]
,

|(\mathsf{S}^{-1})_{ij}|\leq 2\|\mathsf{S}^{-1}\|_{2}\,\rho_{0}^{d/K}

where
\rho_{0}=(\sqrt{\kappa}-1)/(\sqrt{\kappa}+1)
and
\kappa=\mathrm{cond}(\mathsf{S})
.
Using
\mathrm{arctanh}(x)\geq x
for
x\in(0,1)
,
\rho_{0}\leq e^{-2/\sqrt{\kappa}}
.
Finally, since
\mathsf{S}\succeq\mathsf{I}
,
\|\mathsf{S}^{-1}\|_{2}\leq 1
, yielding
|(\mathsf{S}^{-1})_{ij}|\leq 2e^{-\frac{2}{K\sqrt{\kappa}}d}
and the result.
∎

Proof of Proposition
3.6
.

Write
\mathsf{P}=p(\mathsf{L})
.
Since
p(\lambda)>0
on
[0,2]
, we have
P\succ 0
, hence
P
is invertible.
Moreover
\mathsf{R}^{-1}=\mathsf{Q}^{-1}(P+\mathsf{Q})=\mathsf{Q}^{-1}P+\mathsf{I}
.
If two positive diagonal matrices
\mathsf{Q}_{1},\mathsf{Q}_{2}
produce the same operator
\mathsf{R}
, then
\mathsf{Q}_{1}^{-1}P+\mathsf{I}=\mathsf{Q}_{2}^{-1}P+\mathsf{I}
, so
(\mathsf{Q}_{1}^{-1}-\mathsf{Q}_{2}^{-1})P=0
.
Since
P
is invertible,
\mathsf{Q}_{1}^{-1}=\mathsf{Q}_{2}^{-1}
, hence
\mathsf{Q}_{1}=\mathsf{Q}_{2}
.
∎

Proof of Proposition
3.7
.

Let
\mathsf{P}_{j}=p_{j}(\mathsf{L})
and
\mathsf{R}
be the common operator. Inverting
\mathsf{R}=(\mathsf{P}_{j}+\mathsf{Q}_{j})^{-1}\mathsf{Q}_{j}
we obtain
\mathsf{R}^{-1}=\mathsf{Q}_{j}^{-1}\mathsf{P}_{j}+\mathsf{I}
,
hence
\mathsf{Q}_{1}^{-1}\mathsf{P}_{1}=\mathsf{Q}_{2}^{-1}\mathsf{P}_{2}
.
Let

\mathsf{\Delta}=\mathsf{Q}_{2}\mathsf{Q}_{1}^{-1}=\mathrm{diag}(\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{n}),\quad\delta_{i}>0.

Then
\mathsf{P}_{2}=\mathsf{\Delta}\,\mathsf{P}_{1}.

Since
\mathsf{P}_{1}
is invertible (as
p_{1}(\lambda)>0
for
\lambda\in[0,2]
),
\mathsf{\Delta}=\mathsf{P}_{2}\mathsf{P}_{1}^{-1}
.
Both
\mathsf{P}_{2}
and
\mathsf{P}_{1}^{-1}
commute with
\mathsf{L}
, hence
\mathsf{\Delta}
commutes with
\mathsf{L}
. Since
\mathsf{\Delta}
is diagonal,
(\delta_{i}-\delta_{j})L_{ij}=0
for all
i,j
, so
\delta_{i}
is constant on each connected component.
Write this constant as
\alpha_{c}
.

Now let
\bm{u}_{c}\neq\bm{0}
be an eigenvector of
\mathsf{L}
associated to eigenvalue
0
, supported on component
\mathcal{G}_{c}
. Since
\mathsf{P}_{j}=p_{j}(\mathsf{L})
,

\mathsf{P}_{2}\bm{u}_{c}=p_{2}(0)\bm{u}_{c},\qquad\mathsf{P}_{1}\bm{u}_{c}=p_{1}(0)\bm{u}_{c}.

Using
\mathsf{P}_{2}=\mathsf{\Delta}\mathsf{P}_{1}
on this component gives
p_{2}(0)\bm{u}_{c}=\alpha_{c}p_{1}(0)\bm{u}_{c}
.
Since
p_{1}(0)>0
, all
\alpha_{c}=p_{2}(0)/p_{1}(0)
are equal.
Denoting the common value by
\alpha
, we obtain

\mathsf{Q}_{2}=\alpha\mathsf{Q}_{1},\qquad\mathsf{P}_{2}=\alpha\mathsf{P}_{1}.

∎

Proof of Proposition
3.8
.

Equal pre-activation outputs up to a permutation of channel blocks means

\mathsf{R}^{\prime}_{j}\mathsf{H}=\mathsf{R}_{\pi(j)}\mathsf{H}
for some
permutation
\pi
. Since

\mathsf{H}
has rank

, it has a right inverse.
Hence

\mathsf{R}^{\prime}_{j}=\mathsf{R}_{\pi(j)}
for each

.
Applying Proposition

to each channel

\mathsf{Q}^{\prime}_{j}=\alpha_{j}\,\mathsf{Q}_{\pi(j)}

Appendix C
Background on the CSBM

The Contextual Stochastic Block Model (CSBM) combines two well-studied random models: the stochastic block model (SBM) for graph structure, and a Gaussian covariate model for node features. We consider graphs on
n
nodes (even) split into two blocks of equal size
n/2
, with ground-truth labels
v_{i}\in\{-1,+1\}
.

Graph structure: SBM.

For each pair of nodes
(i,j)
, the edge is drawn independently as a Bernoulli with parameter
p_{\mathrm{in}}
if
i,j
belong to the same block and
p_{\mathrm{out}}
otherwise. Setting the average degree
\bar{d}
and the signal parameter
\lambda\in[0,\sqrt{\bar{d}}]
:

p_{\mathrm{in}}=\frac{1}{n}\bigl(\bar{d}+\lambda\sqrt{\bar{d}}\bigr),\qquad p_{\mathrm{out}}=\frac{1}{n}\bigl(\bar{d}-\lambda\sqrt{\bar{d}}\bigr).

The block structure becomes detectable when
\lambda
is large. The fundamental limit for recovering the communities (up to a constant fraction of misclassified nodes, with high probability as
n\to\infty
) is
\lambda=1
: weak recovery is possible if and only if
\lambda>1

[
9
,
20
]
.

Node features: covariate model.

With feature dimension
p=n/\gamma
, node
i
has feature vector
\bm{f}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
drawn as

\bm{f}_{i}=\sqrt{\tfrac{\mu}{n}}\,v_{i}\,\bm{u}+\bm{z}_{i},

where
\bm{u}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\mathsf{I}_{p}/p)
is a shared random direction,
\bm{z}_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\mathsf{I}_{p}/p)
is noise, and
\mu>0
is the feature signal strength. Weak recovery from features alone is possible if and only if
\mu/\sqrt{\gamma}>1

[
12
]
.

Combined CSBM threshold.

Jointly using both graph and features, the information-theoretic threshold for weak recovery is
[
12
]
:

\lambda^{2}+\frac{\mu^{2}}{\gamma}>1.

This curve in the
(\lambda,\,\mu/\sqrt{\gamma})
plane separates the recoverable from the unrecoverable regime, and is the red quarter-circle shown in Figure
6
.

Classification task.

The Tikhonov layer is trained to distinguish two distributions of graphs:

•

Class 1 (signal):
graphs drawn from CSBM
(\lambda,\bar{d},\gamma,\mu)
, with community-aligned features.

•

Class 0 (null):
features drawn with
\mu=0
(no community signal); graph drawn from SBM
(\lambda,\bar{d})
then randomly edge-rewired to preserve the degree distribution (removing any detectable community structure while keeping the degree sequence).

A model succeeding at this task must (i) jointly exploit structure and features, especially in the zone where
\lambda^{2}+\frac{\mu^{2}}{\gamma}>1
and
\lambda^{2}<1
and
\frac{\mu^{2}}{\gamma}<1
; (ii) automatically detect when
\mu
is small that the features are useless noise; (ii) automatically detect when
\lambda
is small that the topology is useless noise.
