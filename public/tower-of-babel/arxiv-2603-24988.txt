Pulse Breathing Dynamics in a Mode-Locked Laser measured via SHG autocorrelation Journal: opticajournal Publication type: Research Article
S. Kannan, S. Padmanaban, X. T. Yan, Y. S. Athreya, K. G. H. Baldwin, S. S. Hodgman, and A. G. Truscott *

Abstract
Pulse-to-pulse fluctuations in mode-locked lasers fundamentally limit applications from optical frequency combs to supercontinuum generation. While timing jitter has been extensively characterized, pulse amplitude and width fluctuations remain less accessible experimentally. We present a statistical autocorrelation method that demonstrates pulse breathing dynamics through Fano factor analysis of second-harmonic generation autocorrelation. This reveals a characteristic M-shaped Fano profile with maxima at the autocorrelation shoulders, a signature of pulse shape dynamics that is invisible to time-averaged diagnostics. Applying this method to a passively mode-locked oscillator, we measure a bound for the pulse width fluctuations of between 10.4 and 12.0 fs ( ∼ 5.0 − 5.7 % \sim 5.0-5.7\% of the 210 fs FWHM). This provides a theoretical projection of ∼ 3 % \sim 3\% spectral bandwidth fluctuations in photonic crystal fiber supercontinuum generation using this laser. This diagnostic capability opens the door to identifying and suppressing specific breathing mechanisms, paving the way for the design of ultra-stable oscillators required for precision frequency metrology.

Introduction

Passively mode-locked lasers capable of generating femtosecond pulse trains have become the corner stone of modern ultrafast photonics, with applications spanning optical frequency combs

,

]
, precision spectroscopy

, high-capacity optical communications
[

[

and high-harmonic generation of extreme-UV sources

Similarly, in optical frequency combs, pulse shape fluctuations couple directly to phase noise via the Kerr effect (amplitude-to-phase conversion), ultimately limiting the achievable carrier-envelope phase (CEP) stability and comb linewidth. While often assumed to be stationary, mode-locked pulses can exhibit complex transient dynamics, motivating the need for accessible techniques to characterize these pulse-to-pulse width fluctuations.

Standard pulse characterization techniques such as frequency-resolved optical gating (FROG)

,
7
,
37
]
and spectral phase interferometry (SPIDER)
[

can be used to retrieve the time averaged electric field and spectral phase. However, these integrating measurements average out rapid dynamics, obscuring pulse-to-pulse fluctuations. While timing jitter (stochastic variation in pulse arrival time) has been characterized to sub-femtosecond precision using balanced optical cross-correlation
[

,
31
]
and is well-understood theoretically
[

,
24

, they require pulse energies in the microjoule range and complex setups. Recently, Barbero et al.
[
2

demonstrated single-shot characterization of shot-to-shot pulse-shape fluctuations using birefringent wedges and an imaging spectrometer, enabling individual pulse retrieval from unstable sources. While such approaches provide complete temporal characterization of each pulse, they require high pulse energies and expensive imaging spectrometers, rendering them unsuitable for the routine characterization of nanojoule level oscillators. For stable oscillators operating at nanojoule pulse energies, direct statistical methods offer a more practical alternative. This diagnostic gap is particularly critical for soliton mode-locked lasers, where the soliton area theorem dictates a fundamental coupling between pulse energy and duration
[
1
]
. Consequently, even minor amplitude noise can drive correlated breathing in the pulse-width dynamics that remain invisible to conventional time-averaging diagnostics. Resolving these fluctuations provides a direct probe into the internal cavity physics, revealing how quantum noise and gain dynamics perturb the soliton attractor. This allows for the characterization of the laser’s specific noise transfer function, distinguishing between simple additive noise and coupled nonlinear breathing modes.

In this work, we demonstrate that statistical analysis of standard second-harmonic generation (SHG) autocorrelation signals provides a direct probe of pulse breathing dynamics. By acquiring thousands of single shot SHG intensity traces at each discrete delay position and computing the Fano factor (variance-to-mean ratio), we quantify a measure of pulse width fluctuations. This technique exploits the quadratic intensity dependence of the SHG process
[
3
]
, where the signal variance becomes highly sensitive to pulse width variations especially away from the pulse center. The signal levels were kept to
\sim 60\%
of saturation, but not lower because of signal to noise concerns. We apply this method to our mode-locked oscillator operating at 1030 nm. Finally, we utilize these retrieved fluctuation parameters to predict the coherence degradation in supercontinuum generation
[
12
]

2
Theoretical Framework

In a pulsed laser operating in the soliton mode-locking regime, pulse parameters are coupled through the interplay of dispersion, nonlinearity, gain, and loss
[
15
]
.
The resulting temporal intensity profile
I(t)
is well-described by a hyperbolic secant squared function:

I(t)=P_{0}\text{sech}^{2}\left(\frac{t}{\tau_{0}}\right),

(1)

where
P_{0}
is the peak power and
\tau_{0}
is the pulse duration parameter. The well known soliton area theorem relates energy
(E\propto P_{0}\tau_{0})
to the cavity parameters and states that the product
E\times\tau_{0}
is an invariant of the system
[
16
]
. Under first-order perturbations to the cavity parameters, the pulse must instantaneously adjust its shape to maintain the soliton condition.
This predicts a perfect anti-correlation (Pearson coefficient
\rho=-1
) between amplitude fluctuation and width fluctuation of the pulse. In experimental systems, however, uncorrelated noise sources partially decouple these parameters resulting in a measured correlation magnitude of
|\rho|<1
.

We now derive a metric to quantify pulse breathing magnitude using the statistical properties of the SHG autocorrelation trace. Since the autocorrelation of a sech
2
pulse is also a sech
2
function (with a width expansion factor), we model the time-averaged intensity of the autocorrelation signal
I_{ac}(\tau)
as:

I_{ac}(\tau;I_{0},w)=I_{0}\text{sech}^{2}(\tau/w),

(2)

where
I_{0}
represents the peak SHG intensity,
w
is the width parameter, and
\tau
is the delay. Assuming small perturbations, the variance of the intensity fluctuations
\langle\Delta I(\tau)^{2}\rangle
at any delay position can be expanded using first-order error propagation:

\langle\Delta I(\tau)^{2}\rangle\approx\left(\frac{\partial I}{\partial I_{0}}\right)^{2}\sigma_{I_{0}}^{2}+\left(\frac{\partial I}{\partial w}\right)^{2}\sigma_{w}^{2}+2\rho\sigma_{I_{0}}\sigma_{w}\left(\frac{\partial I}{\partial I_{0}}\right)\left(\frac{\partial I}{\partial w}\right),

(3)

where
\sigma_{I_{0}}
and
\sigma_{w}
denote the standard deviations of the peak intensity and width, respectively.

The exact analytical autocorrelation of a hyperbolic secant pulse is described by a transcendental function involving
\coth(\tau)
terms, although it is widely approximated by a
\text{sech}^{2}
profile due to the negligible deviation between the two forms (
<1\%
root-mean-square error). Under this approximation, the full-width at half-maximum (FWHM) of the autocorrelation trace is related to the pulse duration
\tau_{0}
by the deconvolution factor
\tau_{ac}\approx 1.543\tau_{0}
. Consequently, we treat the autocorrelation signal as a sech
2
function to derive closed-form expressions for the sensitivity derivatives.
A key property of self-similar pulse shapes, such as the sech
2
profile, is that the derivative with respect to width is directly proportional to the temporal derivative:
\frac{\partial I}{\partial w}=-\frac{\tau}{w}\frac{\partial I}{\partial\tau}
. Utilizing this property and defining the relative intensity noise (RIN) as
R
, the partial derivative terms evaluate to:

\left(\frac{\partial I}{\partial I_{0}}\right)^{2}\sigma_{I_{0}}^{2}=R^{2}\langle I(\tau)\rangle^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\left(\frac{\partial I}{\partial w}\right)^{2}\sigma_{w}^{2}=\left(\frac{\tau}{w}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{\partial I}{\partial\tau}\right)^{2}\sigma_{w}^{2}.

(4)

Equation. (
4
) reveals that the amplitude noise follows the intensity profile squared, whereas width fluctuations (breathing) vanish at zero delay (
\tau=0
) and
{\partial I}/{\partial w}=0
, and scale with the product of the delay and the temporal gradient
\tau\partial I/\partial\tau
.

The variance decomposition of Eq. (
3
) accounts for fluctuations
intrinsic to the laser pulse (amplitude and width). However, in a
scanning autocorrelator, the delay
\tau
is set by a mechanical
translation stage whose positioning accuracy is finite. Shot-to-shot
fluctuations
\delta\tau
in the effective delay contribute an
additional variance term:

\langle\Delta I(\tau)^{2}\rangle\approx\langle I(\tau)\rangle^{2}\Big[R^{2}+k^{2}\left(\frac{\sigma_{w}}{w}\right)^{2}+j^{2}\left(\frac{\sigma_{\tau}}{w}\right)^{2}+2\rho Rk\left(\frac{\sigma_{w}}{w}\right)\Big],

(5)

where
k
and
j
are the sensitivity factors (defined below) and
\sigma_{\tau}
is the RMS stage positioning noise assumed
uncorrelated with the laser parameters.
To quantify the magnitude of these fluctuations, we extract the root-mean-square (RMS) width fluctuation from the peaks of the variance profile — the delay-dependent variance
\langle\Delta I(\tau)^{2}\rangle
computed from an ensemble of single-shot SHG measurements at each delay step (see Fig.
4
(c) for the experimental trace). In the absence of pulse breathing (
\sigma_{w}=0
) and stage noise (
\sigma_{\tau}=0
, the variance reduces to a purely amplitude-noise-driven baseline
\langle\Delta I(\tau)^{2}\rangle=R^{2}\langle I(\tau)\rangle^{2}
, where
R
is the relative intensity noise measured independently from the single-arm SHG signal (see Section.
3
). By normalizing the excess variance above this baseline by the local mean intensity, we define a dimensionless breathing sensitivity factor
k
:

k(\tau)=\frac{1}{\langle I(\tau)\rangle}\frac{1}{w}\frac{\partial I}{\partial w}=2\Big(\frac{\tau}{w}\Big)\tanh\Big(\frac{\tau}{w}\Big).

(6)

The stage noise term is distinguished from pulse breathing by an analogous sensitivity function. A shot-to-shot stage position jitter
\delta\tau
produces an intensity fluctuation
\delta I=(\partial I/\partial\tau)\delta\tau
. Normalizing by the local mean intensity and factoring out
(\sigma_{\tau}/w)
in the same manner as
k
, we define
j(\tau)
:

j(\tau)=\Big(\frac{w}{\langle I(\tau)\rangle}\Big)\frac{\partial I}{\partial\tau}=2\tanh\left(\frac{\tau}{w}\right).

(7)

Since each variance contribution in Eq. (
5
) scales as its sensitivity squared multiplied by
\langle I(\tau)\rangle^{2}\propto
sech
{}^{4}(\tau/w)
, the delay-dependent shape of the stage noise contribution is proportional to
j^{2}
sech
4
, and that of the breathing contribution is proportional to
k^{2}
sech
4
. Because
k(\tau)=(\tau/w)\times j(\tau)
, the breathing term carries an additional factor of
(\tau/w)^{2}
, which shifts its peak to larger delays compared to the stage noise term. Specifically,
j^{2}
sech
4
is maximized at the inflection point of the autocorrelation (
\tau/w=\mathrm{atanh}(1/\sqrt{3})\approx 0.66
,
corresponding to
\tau\approx 119
fs), while
k^{2}
sech
4
peaks further from the centre at
\tau/w\approx 1.0

(
\tau\approx 180
fs), as illustrated in Fig.
4
. Crucially, this means the observed position of the variance peak in the experimental trace serves as a diagnostic: a peak near 119 fs indicates stage-noise-dominated variance, while a peak near 180 fs would indicate breathing-dominated variance.

To get a sensitive qualitative diagnostic of pulse breathing, we calculate the Fano factor. It is defined as the ratio of variance-to-mean
F(\tau)=\langle\Delta I(\tau)^{2}\rangle/\langle I(\tau)\rangle
:

F(\tau)\approx\langle I(\tau)\rangle\left[R^{2}+k^{2}\left(\frac{\sigma_{w}}{w}\right)^{2}+j^{2}\left(\frac{\sigma_{\tau}}{w}\right)^{2}+2\rho R\,k\,\frac{\sigma_{w}}{w}\right].

(8)

The structure of Eq. (
8
) predicts a characteristic shape for the Fano factor as a function of delay. In the absence of breathing (
\sigma_{w}=0
) and any external noise (
\sigma_{\tau}=0
), the Fano factor simplifies to
F_{0}(\tau)=R^{2}\langle I(\tau)\rangle
, which is simply a scaled replica of the mean autocorrelation profile. However, when pulse breathing or other sources of noise are present, the other terms introduces an excess noise contribution proportional to the square of the slope derivative. This creates a characteristic “M-shaped” deviation from the baseline amplitude noise profile. We quantify this deviation using the enhancement factor
\epsilon
:

\epsilon=\frac{F(\tau_{\text{shoulder}})-F(0)}{F(0)},

(9)

where
\tau_{\text{shoulder}}
denotes the delay corresponding to the local maxima of the Fano profile (the peaks of the M-shape, see Fig.
4
(c)). This metric
\epsilon
serves as a model-independent indicator of stability;
\epsilon\approx 0
implies stationary pulses dominated by amplitude noise, while
\epsilon>0
signifies the presence of extra noise dynamics.

While the Fano factor provides a sensitive qualitative diagnostic of pulse breathing through the M-shaped profile, we fit the variance profile directly to Eq. (
5
) to extract the noise parameters, as this avoids introducing additional sensitivity to uncertainties in the mean profile.

3
Experimental setup and Methods

The experiments were performed using a commercial, mode-locked Yb-doped oscillator (Menhir Photonics, MENHIR-1030). The laser is housed in a hermetically sealed enclosure, providing free-space output at a central wavelength of 1030 nm with a repetition rate of 160 MHz. It delivers an average power of 200 mW, corresponding to a pulse energy of
\sim
1.25 nJ, with a nominal pulse duration of
\sim 200
fs.

Initial pulse characterization was conducted using a home-built non-collinear Second Harmonic Generation Frequency-Resolved Optical Gating (SHG-FROG) apparatus (Fig.
2
)
[
7
]
. The output beam is divided by a 50:50 non-polarizing beamsplitter. One arm traverses a free space delay line (Thorlabs MAX312D, 1
\mu
m resolution) to introduce a variable time delay
\tau
, while the other beam path remains fixed. The two beams are then focused non-collinearly into a BBO crystal, generating a background-free sum-frequency generation (SFG) signal. This signal is spectrally resolved using a fiber-coupled spectrometer (Ocean Optics USB4000).
Unlike a simple intensity autocorrelation, the SHG-FROG technique spectrally resolves the autocorrelation signal at each delay step, producing a two-dimensional spectrogram (Fig.
3
(a)) that encodes both the amplitude and phase of the pulse. The pulse intensity and phase were jointly retrieved from this spectrogram using the common pulse retrieval algorithm (COPRA)
[
11
]
, an iterative algorithm that reconstructs the complex electric field by minimizing the difference between the measured and mathematically generated spectrograms (Fig.
3
(b)).
The retrieval converged to a FROG error (G-error) of
<0.5\%
, confirming the fidelity of the reconstructed pulse shape. The retrieved temporal profile fits a clean sech
2
shape with a full-width at half-maximum (FWHM) of 210 fs. The time-bandwidth product of
\sim 0.32
is within
\sim 2\%
of the transform limit (0.315) of sech
2
pulses, and the retrieved spectral phase is flat to within
\pm 0.1
rad across the pulse bandwidth (Fig.
3
). These results confirm soliton-like operation with balanced intracavity dispersion, validating the sech
2
assumption used in our variance model and establishing the mean pulse width
\tau_{0}=210
fs for subsequent analysis.

To quantify pulse breathing, we implemented a high-dynamic-range statistical autocorrelation
technique using the same non-collinear SHG apparatus described above (Fig.
2
), but
with the fiber-coupled spectrometer replaced by a high-speed avalanche photodiode (Menlo
Systems APD210) to enable single-pulse detection at the full 160 MHz repetition rate. The
delay
\tau
was scanned from
-400
to
+400
fs in
\sim
6 fs increments by changing the
mirror position in 1
\mu
m steps. At each delay step, the SHG signal was digitized by a Tektronix 5 Series MSO using FastFrame segmented memory mode to capture a statistical ensemble of 62,500 pulses. The APD signal, hardware-bandlimited to 1 GHz, was acquired in a 4 ns time window for each pulse. The acquisition was performed using a real-time sampling rate of 6.25 GS/s, yielding 25 independent samples per frame. This rate oversamples the 1 GHz waveform by a factor of
>3
relative to its Nyquist frequency (2 GHz). According to the Nyquist-Shannon sampling theorem, this ensures that the oscilloscope’s sinc interpolation can exactly reconstruct the peak amplitude of the continuous, bandlimited waveform from the discrete samples, making the measurement insensitive to sub-sample timing jitter between the trigger and the ADC clock.

Two calibration steps were performed to isolate laser dynamics from detection noise. First, the detector dark noise baseline (mean and variance) was characterized with the beam blocked. Second, the single-arm SHG signal (with one arm in Fig.
2
blocked) was recorded to determine the fundamental amplitude noise floor, independent of pulse overlap. After dark-noise subtraction, the coefficient of variation (
CV_{\text{SHG}}=\sigma/\mu
) of the single-arm signal was used to calculate the fundamental relative intensity noise
R
. Finally, the delay-dependent statistics— mean
\langle I(\tau)\rangle
, variance
\langle\Delta I(\tau)^{2}\rangle
, and Fano factor
F(\tau)-
were computed for the full autocorrelation trace.

Our statistical analysis separates the measured
variance into laser-intrinsic (amplitude noise and pulse breathing)
and instrument-induced (stage positioning) contributions.
Importantly, the variance peak position provides a diagnostic that
distinguishes these mechanisms: a peak near the inflection point
(
\tau/w\approx 0.66
) indicates stage-dominated noise, while a
peak near
\tau/w\approx 1
would indicate breathing-dominated
noise. In our measurement, the peak at
\sim 120\,
fs sits at the stage
noise position, indicating that stage vibrations contribute
significantly to the raw variance. After correction, the intrinsic
breathing is
\sigma_{w}/w\approx 5.0

To confirm that the extracted width fluctuations (
\sigma_{w}
) originate from intrinsic optical dynamics rather than uncompensated mechanical stage vibrations, we analyzed the radio-frequency (RF) spectra of the pulse-to-pulse fluctuations. Because our segmented memory acquisition captures 62,500 pulses, each in a discrete 4 ns window at the full 160 MHz repetition rate, spanning a total observation window of
\sim 0.4
ms, it enables computation of the PSD up to the 80 MHz Nyquist limit. While slow mechanical vibrations (
<5

5

broadband excess strongly indicates the presence of fast, intrinsic pulse shape fluctuations.

The spectral structure of this excess noise reveals two distinct driving mechanisms. First, the underlying flat, broadband continuum is characteristic of a stochastic process, consistent with the foundational Haus-Mecozzi noise model in which spontaneous emission exerts a white-noise driving force on the soliton parameters
[
14
]
. At sufficiently low frequencies, the soliton responds adiabatically, fully tracking the quantum fluctuations.
Second, emerging above this quantum noise floor is a distinct technical noise feature near
\sim 5
MHz. Because the fundamental relaxation oscillation frequency of a Yb-doped solid-state oscillator is restricted to the low tens of kilohertz by the
\sim 1
ms upper-state lifetime, this MHz-scale feature cannot originate from gain dynamics and must be an external technical noise source such as pump diode RIN. Crucially, a 5 MHz perturbation period (
\sim 32
cavity round trips) is much shorter than the soliton’s relaxation time in a low-loss solid-state cavity. The soliton therefore responds non-adiabatically, acting as a low-pass filter that dynamically damps high-frequency perturbations.
Quantitatively, this 5 MHz feature rises approximately 75% above the local broadband noise floor in the isolated breathing spectrum (Fig.
5
). The prominence of this peak, even after being strongly attenuated by the non-adiabatic soliton response, indicates that it originates from a powerful technical noise source driving the system.
The total measured variance,
\sigma_{w}^{2}
, receives contributions from both the strong adiabatic response to low-frequency noise and this damped non-adiabatic response to high-frequency noise. Our statistical technique is thus powerful enough to resolve the frequency-dependent nature of the cavity’s noise transfer function, successfully distinguishing between the fundamental quantum breathing limit and the non-adiabatic response to specific technical noise drivers.

This pulse breathing has significant implications for nonlinear applications, particularly supercontinuum generation in photonic crystal fibers (PCF). For a pump wavelength of 1030 nm launched into an anomalous dispersion PCF, the spectral broadening is governed by soliton fission
[
18
,
9
]
, a process critically dependent on the input soliton order
N

[
9
]
:

N=\sqrt{\frac{\gamma P_{0}\tau_{0}^{2}}{|\beta_{2}|}},

(10)

where
\gamma
is the nonlinear parameter of the fiber,
P_{0}
is the peak power of the laser,
\tau_{0}
is the pulse width and
\beta_{2}
is the group velocity dispersion parameter. Assuming the pulse energy is clamped by gain saturation (
E_{p}\approx\text{constant}
), the peak power scales inversely with width (
P_{0}\propto 1/\tau_{0}
). Substituting this into Eq. (
10
) reveals that the soliton order scales with the square root of the pulse duration:
N\propto\sqrt{\tau_{0}}
. Following established scaling laws for the soliton fission regime
[
1
]
, the bandwidth of the generated supercontinuum increases linearly with
N
. Consequently, our measured
\sim 5.0-5.7\%
breathing in pulse width provides a theoretical prediction of
\sim 2.5-2.9\%
fluctuation in supercontinuum bandwidth. While this percentage appears small, these fluctuations manifest as amplified relative intensity noise at the spectral edges of the continuum, where the intensity slope is steepest. This flickering of the bandwidth will degrade coherence and limits the signal-to-noise ratio in spectroscopic applications
[
5
,
6
]
. Unlike timing jitter, which can be actively stabilized, these breathing dynamics are intrinsic to the oscillator’s soliton shaping and often pass undetected by standard diagnostics. This suggests that for demanding SC applications, prioritizing low-breathing oscillators (which can be achieved by using a pump source with extremely low RIN or actively stabilizing the pump power before it enters the laser cavity and also by optimizing the cavity’s noise transfer function) is as critical as minimizing average intensity noise.

To place this in perspective, we compare the measured width fluctuations (
\sigma_{w}\approx 10.4-12.0
fs) to the laser’s timing jitter. Timing jitter, typically characterized by integrated phase noise, measures arrival time stability but provides no information about pulse shape. For context, the manufacturer specifies
\sim
7 fs of integrated timing jitter over a 1 kHz to 1 MHz analysis bandwidth (Menhir Photonics datasheet), capturing the dominant acoustic and mechanical noise sources. This corresponds to only
\sim 3\%
of the pulse duration. The breathing width fluctuations above are thus almost double the magnitude of the timing fluctuations. The unique value of statistical autocorrelation lies in identifying this
\sigma_{w}
component, offering a direct probe into the cavity noise transfer function that timing jitter measurements miss.

Conclusion

We have demonstrated that statistical analysis of SHG autocorrelation reveals pulse breathing dynamics in mode-locked lasers. Applied to our 1030 nm laser, we measured pulse width fluctuations of
\sim 10.4-12.0
fs (
\sim 5.0-5.7\%
). The method is inexpensive, fast, requires only standard apparatus, and provides information inaccessible to conventional FROG or spectral measurements. Future measurements employing piezoelectric stages with sub-nanometer positioning accuracy would
eliminate the stage contribution entirely, providing a cleaner extraction of
the breathing dynamics. The measured breathing amplitude enables theoretical prediction of the impact on supercontinuum spectral stability. This technique fills a gap between time-averaged pulse characterization and single-shot spectroscopy, offering practical access to cavity noise dynamics for oscillator development and application optimization.

This diagnostic capability will be important in our ongoing development of an optical frequency comb at 1030 nm. This will be used for precision spectroscopy of atomic transitions, using our ultracold metastable

The identification of pump diode RIN near 5 MHz as the dominant technical breathing mechanism provides a clear engineering target: active stabilization of the pump power at this frequency is expected to reduce the intrinsic breathing amplitude and improve carrier-envelope phase stability, bringing the oscillator closer to the quantum-limited breathing floor set by spontaneous emission
