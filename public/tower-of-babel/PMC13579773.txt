Discrimination of generalized anxiety and major depressive disorders by machine learning of resting-state functional MRI
Authors: Peng Fang, Yifei Li, Shukun Zhu, Kangsheng Bi, Yanjun Li, Jiakuai Yu, Jiajia Zhang, Mingming Zheng, Jiajia Zhu, Daomin Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo address the clinical difficulty of differentiating Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD) from Major Depressive Disorder (MDD), this study aimed to create a machine learning framework with strong interpretability, leveraging a comprehensive suite of resting-state functional MRI features to enhance potential relevance.MethodsA comprehensive set of functional measures, encompassing both local neural activity and global functional connectivity, was extracted as neuroimaging features from resting-state fMRI data. These data were obtained from 91 patients with Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD), 94 with Major Depressive Disorder (MDD), and 71 healthy controls (HCs). Five machine learning algorithms were used for classification, with performance estimated within a rigorous nested cross-validation framework. Then, partial correlations were conducted to assess the associations between top-ranked contributive neuroimaging features and symptom severity.ResultsFor the discrimination tasks, the machine learning models achieved area under the curve (AUC) values of 0.783 (GAD vs. MDD), 0.824 (GAD vs. HCs), and 0.867 (MDD vs. HCs), demonstrating moderate classification performance. Feature importance analysis revealed that precuneus function served as a prominently differential neurobiological signature and it showed opposing relationships with the severity of anxiety (positive correlation) and depression (negative correlation) across different diagnostic categories.ConclusionThese findings could guide the creation of a computational framework based on neuroimaging to effectively differentiate GAD from MDD. This is underscored by the pivotal role that the precuneus appears to play in the neurobiological processes associated with symptoms of both disorders.Trial registrationThis is a non-interventional study with a control group. Clinical trial registration is not applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12888-026-08326-0.

Introduction

Generalised Anxiety Disorder (GAD) and Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) are highly prevalent and disabling psychiatric conditions globally, and they exhibit significant comorbidity with each other [1]–3]. Differentiating between the two clinically presents a major challenge, a dilemma well-documented in epidemiological studies. Estimates indicate that about 60% of individuals with GAD also have comorbid MDD, while approximately 50% of MDD patients meet the criteria for GAD [4]. This high comorbidity is further complicated by substantial symptom overlap, including persistent worry, sleep disturbances, cognitive impairments, and fatigue [5]. Such clinical ambiguity has been demonstrated to result in elevated misdiagnosis rates, which in turn have been shown to cause a significant “treatment mismatch" [6]. The advancement of targeted neuromodulation therapies is further complicated by the diagnostic ambiguity between GAD and MDD, which prevents the identification of clear and specific neural targets for each disorder [7]. This diagnostic-therapeutic deadlock not only results in inefficient allocation of healthcare resources but also perpetuates a vicious cycle of persistent symptoms, accelerated functional decline, and increased suicide risk [8]. Thus, developing objective computational frameworks based on biological markers is critically needed to address this challenge.

Resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI) is an indispensable technique for investigating the neural bases of GAD and MDD [9]. A mounting body of evidence suggests overlapping and distinct neural correlates of GAD and MDD. Converging evidence indicates that GAD and MDD exhibit partially overlapping yet distinct neural underpinnings. Research has implicated dysregulated activity in the salience network (SN), with key hubs like the anterior insula (AI) and anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), as a primary feature of GAD [10, 11]. This phenomenon may reflect the neural correlates of heightened interoceptive awareness and abnormal threat vigilance. Conversely, MDD is primarily marked by dysfunction in the default mode network (DMN), involving altered connectivity and hyperactivity. Regions notably implicated include the posterior cingulate cortex and precuneus, which are central to rumination and self-referential processing. Concurrently, MDD involves prominent dysfunction in prefrontal-limbic circuits, which are essential for emotional regulation [12].

Nevertheless, the translation of these group-level neuroimaging findings into individual-level diagnostic prediction tools poses significant methodological challenges [13]. The field has become increasingly cautious due to what has been termed a reproducibility crisis [14], wherein promising initial findings from neuroimaging signatures frequently fail to generalize to independent cohorts. This issue often stems from methodological limitations that lead to inflated performance estimates. Several recurrent pitfalls have been identified as primary contributors [15]. A major limitation is the prevalent reliance on unimodal imaging features, such as isolated measures of functional connectivity or local activity, which capture only a limited facet of the complex pathophysiology underlying psychiatric disorders [16, 17]. This approach proves particularly inadequate for distinguishing between conditions with considerable neurobiological overlap, such as MDD and GAD. Meta-analytic evidence identifies a consistent pattern of network dysfunction in both disorders. MDD involves abnormalities across the prefrontal cortex (dorsolateral/ventromedial), ACC, amygdala, hippocampus, and striatum [18], GAD findings similarly involve dysregulation in regions like the amygdala, prefrontal cortex, ACC, and insula [19]. The substantial convergence of alterations in these prefrontal-limbic circuits undermines the diagnostic specificity of any signature derived from a single region or modality. Consequently, moving beyond unimodal features to a multidimensional framework that integrates information across distributed brain networks and complementary imaging modalities is imperative to capture the distinct and heterogeneous pathophysiological signatures of each disorder [20]. Furthermore, previous machine learning model development and evaluation are often conducted with limited sample sizes, which substantially increases the risk of overfitting and reduces generalizability [21]. Critically, validation schemes commonly fail to properly account for the entire model selection and optimization process, resulting in performance estimates that are optimistically biased [22]. Collectively, these factors present major obstacles to the clinical translation of neuroimaging-based diagnostic classifiers.

In the past decade, the application of machine learning in neuropsychiatry has gradually transitioned from exploratory proof-of-concept studies toward an emphasis on methodological precision and reliable performance evaluation. Concurrently, the integration of multi-dimensional imaging features has been shown to provide richer biological information and greater discriminatory power compared to single-modality approaches [23]. Furthermore, ensemble learning strategies, particularly those based on nested cross-validation, are increasingly regarded as the gold standard for obtaining realistic performance metrics when sample sizes are limited [24, 25]. While these methodologies do not replace the necessity for external validation, they provide a more cautious and statistically valid estimate of a model’s prospective generalizability [15, 25]. However, the comprehensive integration of multi-dimensional feature engineering with moderate validation frameworks in direct discrimination studies between GAD and MDD has remained relatively under-researched [26, 27].

To address these concerns, a comprehensive, multidimensional framework has been developed. This framework integrates complementary aspects of brain functional organization within a rigorously validated machine learning pipeline. The current analysis draws on resting-state fMRI data of high quality, acquired from a rigorously phenotyped cohort. A fine-grained brain atlas was leveraged to systematically extract a comprehensive set of features spanning two fundamental dimensions: local neural activity and global functional connectivity. Subsequently, this high-dimensional neuroimaging feature set was processed through a robust machine learning pipeline designed to maximize generalizability. To capture complex, non-linear relationships within the data, multiple machine learning algorithms were implemented. To obtain unbiased performance estimates and rigorously minimize overfitting, a strict nested cross-validation protocol was applied. Consequently, this research represents a methodological advance beyond previous unimodal or less rigorously validated studies. By combining multidimensional feature extraction with ensemble-based machine learning and stringent validation, this work demonstrates a viable framework for identifying resting-state fMRI signatures capable of accurately differentiating between the disorders. Furthermore, a specific subset of neurobiological features with notable discriminative power was identified and correlated with symptom severity. This provides a foundation for biologically informed diagnostic frameworks and paves the way for actionable clinical decision-support tools with the potential to substantially enhance patient outcomes [28].

Methods

Research subjects

The research was performed using a case-control design, comprising 256 participants. The study population comprised 91 patients diagnosed with GAD, 94 patients diagnosed with MDD and 71 HCs. Participants were recruited from the inpatient department of Hefei Fourth People’s Hospital (January 2022 to June 2025) who met the following criteria: aged 18–55 years, a primary ICD-10 diagnosis of GAD or MDD, and minimum symptom severity scores (HAMA-17 ≥ 14 for GAD; HAMD-24 ≥ 17 for MDD) [29, 30]. Symptom assessment was performed by trained clinicians using the Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale (HAMA-17) [31] and the Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD-24) [32].

Patients in the MDD group received a diagnosis based on ICD-10 criteria, which was confirmed by a consensus between two or more independent licensed psychiatrists using a structured clinical interview. To ensure diagnostic specificity, individuals with a comorbid anxiety disorder (including GAD) or any other psychiatric condition (e.g., schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, substance use disorders) were explicitly excluded. All patients were on a stable regimen of standard antidepressant pharmacotherapy (converted to fluoxetine equivalents) for a minimum of four weeks prior to participation. All patient diagnoses for both the GAD and MDD groups were established in accordance with ICD-10 criteria and confirmed by a structured clinical interview conducted independently by at least two psychiatrists. Common exclusion criteria applied to all participants included: (1) a comorbid diagnosis of other major psychiatric disorders; (2) a history of major systemic or neurological diseases; (3) a prior head injury with loss of consciousness; or (4) any contraindication to MRI or pregnancy. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Hefei Fourth People’s Hospital, and written informed consent was obtained from every participant. MRI data acquisition

All MRI data were obtained using a 3.0-T Discovery MR750w scanner (General Electric) fitted with a 24-channel head coil [33]. High-resolution T1-weighted anatomical images were acquired through a 3D brain volume sequence with the following parameters: repetition time (TR) = 8.5 ms, echo time (TE) = 3.2 ms, inversion time (TI) = 450 ms, flip angle = 12°, field of view = 256 × 256 mm², matrix size = 256 × 256, slice thickness = 1 mm, 188 sagittal slices, and acquisition time = 296 s [34, 35].

Resting-state fMRI data were collected using a gradient-echo echo-planar imaging sequence. The parameters of the magnetic resonance imaging scan were as follows: TR = 2000 ms, TE = 30 ms, flip angle = 90°, field of view = 220 × 220 mm², matrix size = 64 × 64, slice thickness = 3 mm, slice gap = 1 mm, 35 interleaved axial slices, 185 volumes, and acquisition time = 370 s [36–38]. Foam padding was employed to limit head motion, and earplugs were provided to reduce scanner noise [39]. Prior to the scan, participants were instructed to minimize movement, keep their eyes closed, maintain a relaxed posture, avoid focusing on specific thoughts, and refrain from falling asleep. Following the scan, participants were queried to confirm they had remained awake. All T1-weighted structural images were carefully inspected for artifacts, especially those from head motion, while functional images were checked to ensure complete cerebellar coverage. Importantly, no participants required exclusion based on imaging artifacts.

Image preprocessing

Image preprocessing was conducted utilising Statistical Parametric Mapping (SPM12) and Data Processing & Analysis for Brain Imaging (DPABI V8.3) [40, 41]. We discarded the initial 10 volumes to facilitate magnetic field stabilization. For subsequent volumes, we performed slice timing correction (with the reference set to the middle slice) followed by realignment to correct for head motion [42].

The nuisance regression model comprised several components: linear and quadratic drifts, 24 head motion parameters (Friston-24 model), and the mean signals from cerebrospinal fluid and white matter. Additionally, spike volumes (defined by a frame-wise displacement > 0.5 mm) were included as regressors [43]. We applied band-pass filtering (0.01–0.10 Hz) to mitigate the effects of low-frequency drift and high-frequency noise.

The T1-weighted images were co-registered to the mean functional image, segmented, and normalized to Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space via diffeomorphic anatomical registration through exponentiated Lie algebra (DARTEL). The resulting functional images were then subjected to normalisation using the deformation fields obtained above. The images were resampled to 3 mm isotropic voxels and spatially smoothed with a Gaussian kernel of 6 mm full-width at half maximum. The evaluation of image quality was conducted through the utilisation of two distinct methodologies: the measurement of mean frame-wise displacement and a comprehensive visual inspection.

Calculation of fMRI-derived measures and feature extraction

The features under consideration were extracted based on the Brainnetome Atlas 246 (BNA-246) parcellation [21]. The local neural activity features under scrutiny encompassed the amplitude of low-frequency fluctuation (ALFF) and fractional ALFF (fALFF) [44]. Specifically, the functional time course of each voxel was transformed to a frequency domain via a Fast Fourier Transform to obtain the power spectrum. The ALFF was defined as the averaged square root in a specific low-frequency band (0.01–0.1 Hz). The fALFF was defined as the ratio of the power spectrum in the low-frequency band (0.01–0.1 Hz) to that in the entire frequency range. The regional homogeneity (ReHo) was calculated as the Kendall’s concordance coefficient of the time course of a given voxel with those of its nearest neighbours (26 voxels) [45]. For a given voxel, the Degree centrality (DC) was defined as the sum of functional connections with correlation coefficients above a threshold of 0.25 between that voxel and all other voxels within the whole brain.

For the purpose of conducting a functional connectivity analysis, 30 seed regions were utilised. These regions encompassed key networks implicated in the pathophysiology of GAD and MDD, including the default mode network (precuneus, posterior cingulate cortex, medial prefrontal cortex), the salience network (anterior insula, anterior cingulate cortex), the central executive network (dorsolateral prefrontal cortex), and the limbic system (amygdala, hippocampus) [46]. Pearson correlation coefficients were then calculated between the mean functional time series of each seed region of interest (ROI) and all parcels defined in the BNA-246 atlas, providing a fine-grained whole-brain functional connectivity profile. The resulting correlation coefficients were converted to Fisher’s z-values to improve normality.The final feature set comprised 8634 features per participant.

Machine learning framework

Three binary classification tasks were conducted to evaluate the framework: distinguishing GAD from MDD (n = 185), GAD from HCs (n = 162), and MDD from HCs (n = 165). Five machine learning algorithms were employed to ensure robustness: a Support Vector Machine with an RBF kernel (C = 1.0, class_weight=’balanced’) [47]; a random forest classifier (200 estimators, max_depth = 10, min_samples_split = 5, class_weight=’balanced_subsample’); an XGBoost model (200 estimators, max_depth = 5, learning_rate = 0.1, with scale_pos_weight adjusted for class imbalance); a logistic regression model with L2 penalty (C = 0.5, solver=’liblinear’, class_weight=’balanced’) [48]; and a gradient boosting classifier (200 estimators, max_depth = 5, learning_rate = 0.1) [49]. The hyperparameters listed for each model represent the optimal values identified through Bayesian optimization (see Table S1 for complete search ranges and optimized values).

All algorithms were integrated into a standardized preprocessing and modeling pipeline. Features were scaled using RobustScaler. Dimensionality reduction was performed using SelectKBest, where the number of selected features (k) was determined dynamically based on sample size: k = 10 for n < 30, k = 20 for n < 50, k = 30 for n < 100, and k = 50 otherwise [50]. Feature selection for tree-based models (Random Forest, XGBoost, Gradient Boosting) was based on mutual information, calculated using the mutual_info_classif function. Specifically, this implementation employs a non-parametric estimator based on k-nearest neighbors (k = 3) to capture non-linear dependencies, while ANOVA F-value (f_classif) was used for linear models (SVM, Logistic Regression). The Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique was applied when the class imbalance ratio exceeded 2:1 [51].

To account for potential confounding factors, we explicitly controlled for motion, demographic, and medication variables within the machine learning pipeline. Prior to model training, we performed a nuisance regression to remove variance associated with head motion and other confounding variables. Specifically, we regressed out the mean framewise displacement (as a proxy for head motion), as well as age, sex, and medication types from the neuroimaging features. This residualization process ensured that the input data for the machine learning algorithms were purged of non-neural noise.

A nested cross-validation strategy was employed for model training and evaluation. The outer loop used 5-fold stratified cross-validation for final performance assessment. Within each fold, an inner 5-fold stratified cross-validation loop with Bayesian optimization (50 iterations) was used for hyperparameter tuning [52]. To prevent data leakage, key preprocessing steps such as scaling, feature selection, and oversampling were fit exclusively on the training folds.This rigorous nested validation framework, combined with L2 regularization in linear models, was specifically designed to mitigate overfitting risks associated with high-dimensional feature spaces. Regarding feature selection, the dynamic application of SelectKBest (mutual information for tree-based models, ANOVA for linear models) ensures that the most discriminative and stable features are retained for classification, effectively balancing model complexity with predictive power.

Model performance was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve as the primary metric [53], supplemented by accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, average precision, and a composite score (calculated as 0.4 × AUC-ROC + 0.3 × F1 + 0.3 × average precision) [53]. For model interpretation, feature importance was analyzed using permutation importance. Furthermore, SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values were utilized to generate summary plots, providing a detailed visualization of feature contributions and their impact on model predictions.

Statistical analysis

To compare groups, continuous variables were analyzed with one-way ANOVA (Tukey post-hoc) and categorical variables with the chi-square test. The analysis was conducted in a Python 3.8 environment with the following libraries: scikit-learn 1.0.2, xgboost 1.5.0, and imbalanced-learn 0.8.1 [54].

In order to explore the relationships between the top-ranked neuroimaging features identified by machine learning models and clinical symptom scores (total scores of HAMA-17 and HAMD-24), partial correlation analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics 26 with confounding factors such as body mass index, age, illness duration, sex, education level, medication status, and frame-wise displacement as nuisance covariates. False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction was applied for multiple comparisons. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05 (two-tailed), and effect sizes were reported for all analyses.

Power analysis performed with G Power 3.1 revealed that the sample size offered 90% power to detect medium effects (f = 0.25) at a significance level of α = 0.05.

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics

The demographic and clinical characteristics of the participants (91 GAD, 94 MDD, 71 HCs) are presented in Table 1. The three groups were comparable in age, gender, BMI, and years of education (all p > 0.05). As expected, considerable intergroup differences were found in the clinical assessments. Subsequent post-hoc analyses revealed that the MDD group exhibited the most elevated HAMD scores, with the GAD group and the HCs group following in a descending order (MDD > GAD > HCs, all p < 0.05). As expected, the GAD, MDD, and HC groups exhibited sequentially lower HAMA scores, with all pairwise differences being statistically significant (all p < 0.05). In contrast, no significant group differences were found in framewise displacement (FD) (p = 0.061) or medication dosage (P = 0.092) .

Classification performance of machine learning models

In order to address the clinical challenge of distinguishing GAD from MDD, a comprehensive machine learning framework was devised. The present framework utilised multiple resting-state fMRI features from 256 participants, including 91 GAD, 94 MDD, and 71 HCs. A three-level diagnostic system that gradually addresses clinical diagnostic challenges has been established through rigorous nested cross-validation with five different algorithms.

The models demonstrated moderate discriminative capabilities across all classification tasks (see Table 2; Fig. 2). For distinguishing GAD from MDD, the gradient-boosted model emerged as the optimal solution, achieving an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.783. Patient classification from healthy controls demonstrated greater efficacy. Specifically, the optimal model for distinguishing anxiety disorders from healthy controls was the Random Forest model, achieving an AUC of 0.824. For depression versus healthy controls, the optimal model was Random Forest, achieving an AUC of 0.867.

In the discrimination between GAD and MDD (Fig. 3A), the model relied most heavily on features involving the precuneus and its functional connectionsThe most discriminative features differed between classification tasks. For GAD vs. MDD, they included regional homogeneity (ReHo) in the left precuneus (dmPOS) and two functional connections: left dmPOS to right precentral gyrus, and left precuneus (A5m) to right thalamus. For distinguishing GAD from HCs (Fig. 3B), the model combined connectivity and local activity: functional connectivity between bilateral precuneus (A5m) and occipital pole regions (left-left and right-right), along with ALFF in the left insula.

The classification of MDD versus healthy controls (Fig. 3C) was characterized by a unique set of discriminative features. The top-ranked ones featured elevated DC in the left lingual gyrus, increased ALFF in the left precuneus (dmPOS), and stronger connectivity between the left precuneus (A31) and the right inferior occipital cortex.

These data highlight the precuneus as a pivotal node whose functional profiles distinguish both clinical conditions from each other and from health, underscoring its central role in the divergent neural circuitry of GAD and MDD.

Clinical relevance of important classification features

Within the GAD and MDD groups, we conducted partial correlation analyses to examine potential links between the key discriminative neuroimaging features and the severity of clinical symptoms. This analysis yielded a pivotal and interpretable finding regarding the model’s primary feature, that is, ALFF in the left precuneus (dmPOS). It is noteworthy that we found an opposite pattern of association between this neuroimaging feature and anxiety/depression severity (Fig. 5). In the cohort of individuals diagnosed with GAD, ALFF of the precuneus exhibited a significant positive correlation with the severity of anxiety (Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale [HAMA]: r = 0.277, p = 0.011). Across the entire sample, which included individuals diagnosed with GAD, MDD, and HCs, ALFF of the precuneus showed a significant negative correlation with the severity of depression (Hamilton Depression Rating Scale [HAMD]: r = -0.390, p = < 0.001). This finding indicated that increased activity in this region was associated with the severity of anxiety in GAD, while decreased activity was linked to the severity of depression across different diagnostic groups.

Discussion

A moderate and interpretable machine learning framework was developed in this study. Leveraging diverse resting-state functional MRI features, it achieved effective classification between Generalized Anxiety Disorder, Major Depressive Disorder, and healthy controls [16]. Aside from the good classification performance, another core finding was the identification of the precuneus, a pivotal hub of the DMN, as a critical neurobiological signature whose functional patterns exhibited contrasting clinical associations across disorders [55, 56]. Specifically, precuneus function exhibited a positive correlation with anxiety in GAD but a negative correlation with depression scores across diagnostic categories.

To determine whether classification performance was primarily driven by demographic differences rather than neuroimaging features, we conducted a demographic-only baseline analysis using age, sex, and education. During re-evaluation of this baseline model, we found that the originally reported below-chance AUC reflected the probability direction used in ROC calculation. After explicitly specifying the positive class and correcting the probability direction, the demographic baseline showed a corrected pooled AUC of 0.624. This performance remained lower than that of the full neuroimaging-based model, which achieved an AUC of 0.783 for GAD versus MDD. These findings support the incremental predictive value of resting-state fMRI features beyond basic demographic information.

Our feature importance analysis pinpointed the precuneus as central for differentiating GAD from MDD. The observed dichotomy, characterized by a positive association with anxiety in GAD and a negative association with depression, can be understood by examining the region’s functional heterogeneity, neurochemical profile, and circuit-specific interactions. Anatomically and functionally segregated, the precuneus contains subregions arranged along an anterior-posterior axis. This topographical organization underlies distinct connectivity profiles, with anterior portions more involved in self-referential cognition and posterior portions in visuospatial integration [57]. While our study did not directly measure neurochemistry, this anatomical and functional profile may be interpreted through a theoretical neurochemical lens. The precuneus is anatomically characterized as a glutamatergic cortical region, with its activity putatively modulated by local GABAergic interneurons and influenced by long-range neuromodulatory inputs. Notably, it receives dense serotonergic (5-HT) and noradrenergic (NA) projections from the raphe nuclei and locus coeruleus, respectively—systems that are profoundly implicated in arousal, mood, and anxiety [58]. Its downstream projections heavily target prefrontal, cingulate, and medial temporal areas, positioning it as a key integrator for assigning personal significance to stimuli and mental events.

A critical question in differentiating GAD from MDD is whether the identified neurobiological signatures reflect disorder-specific pathophysiology or merely track general symptom severity. Our findings provide strong evidence for the former. We observed a dissociation in the relationship between the key discriminative feature—the precuneus activity—and clinical symptoms. Specifically, ALFF in the precuneus showed a significant positive correlation with anxiety severity in GAD patients, while exhibiting a significant negative correlation with depression severity across the sample. This diametric pattern indicates that our classifier is not merely detecting a unidimensional ‘severity’ gradient; instead, it captures distinct neurobiological mechanisms where the same neural substrate is engaged in opposite directions by the two disorders.

Conceptually, our findings can be framed within the context of maladaptive self-projection and future-oriented simulation. This represents a hypothetical model rather than a confirmed mechanism. Pathological worry, a hallmark of GAD, involves excessive engagement with self-generated, negative future scenarios, may be linked to the DMN’s medial temporal lobe subsystem, of which the precuneus is a part [59]. It is possible that the anterior precuneus, crucial for episodic future thinking and scene construction, may exhibit glutamatergic hyperfunction or dysregulated engagement within a hyperactive DMN [60]. This potentially aligns with our finding of enhanced precuneus-occipital connectivity in GAD, suggesting a mechanism where self-referential thought is coupled with heightened visual imagery, potentially intensifying the affective impact of worry. This state may be sustained by altered balances in 5-HT or NA systems, which modulate the signal-to-noise ratio and gain of cortical networks involved in threat vigilance [61, 62].

Conversely, the transdiagnostic negative correlation with depression severity may theoretically reflect the precuneus’s role in motivation, hedonic processing. Theoretically, the posterior precuneus—with its strong connections to visuospatial and attention systems—is part of a network that assigns motivational salience, a function which is hypothesized to depend on intact mesocorticolimbic dopaminergic (DA) signaling [63]. Therefore, the observed hypoconnectivity or reduced activity in MDD could potentially stem from a disruption in the dopaminergic reward circuit, particularly the signaling from the ventral tegmental area to medial prefrontal and parietal regions [64]. If this theoretical model holds, such an impairment in a circuit critical for reward anticipation might manifest as anhedonia and apathy. Furthermore, dysfunction in the self-referential processing anchored in the precuneus may contribute to the excessive negative self-focus characteristic of depression [65]. The aberrant precuneus-thalamus connectivity observed in our GAD vs. MDD model suggests distinct pathophysiological mechanisms. As a relay hub for sensory and limbic information, the thalamus may engage in differential interaction patterns that speculatively align with disorder-specific symptomology: in GAD, hyperconnectivity with the precuneus could hypothetically underlie anxious rumination and hypervigilance, as supported by the positive correlation with symptom severity. whereas in MDD, altered thalamocortical rhythms could potentially contribute to psychomotor slowing and emotional blunting (negative correlation) [66].

In response to concerns regarding feature stability in high-dimensional models, our findings demonstrate robust replicability. The consistent identification of the precuneus and its connectivity profiles as top-ranking features across diverse algorithms—including both linear (SVM, Logistic Regression) and non-linear (Random Forest, XGBoost) classifiers—highlights the stability of these neurobiological signatures. Furthermore, the strict nested cross-validation protocol ensures that feature selection occurred independently within each training fold, minimizing the risk of overfitting and confirming that these features are reliable discriminators of GAD and MDD pathology.

Our data-driven findings converge with and refine classical models of DMN dysfunction in psychopathology [67]. While both GAD and MDD have been linked to DMN alterations, our results specify a diametric pattern at the precuneus hub. This clarifies prior literature: reports of DMN “hyperconnectivity” in anxiety disorders may specifically reflect the precuneus-driven, self-focused worry mechanism [19], whereas DMN “hypoconnectivity” in depression may index the precuneus’s failure to integrate internal states with external reward cues [18]. Our identification of features involving the posterior cingulate cortex and visual areas in MDD vs. HC classification further supports well-documented disturbances in the DMN and its interplay with sensory processing networks [68], potentially underlying rumination and negative perceptual biases.

Beyond the neurobiological insights, this study underscores the methodological power of a carefully constructed machine learning framework for clinical neuroimaging, moving towards building moderate neurobiological signatures from brain data. Our pipeline, which employs multi-algorithm ensembles and is anchored by a strict nested cross-validation protocol, was designed to maximize generalizability and deliver interpretable, biologically plausible features, thereby addressing key methodological pitfalls in the field [69]. By computationally integrating the opposing symptom associations of the same brain region, our model demonstrates its capacity to reveal complex, non-linear brain-symptom relationships that are often missed in conventional group-level analyses [70].

Compared to prior machine learning studies in MDD or GAD classification, our work demonstrates distinct methodological advantages that enhance the reliability and translational potential of our findings. First, the use of a rigorous nested cross-validation approach provides a more realistic and unbiased estimate of out-of-sample performance than simpler validation methods, directly addressing pervasive overfitting concerns in high-dimensional neuroimaging-based predictive modeling [71]. Second, our comprehensive feature engineering strategy, which involved systematically extracting and combining multidimensional metrics from a finely parcellated atlas, enabled a more complete utilization of the rich information in rs-fMRI data. This approach moves beyond the limitations of studies reliant on single metric or seed based analyses [72, 73]. Finally, the potential relevance of our framework is highlighted by achieving competitive classification performance using rs-fMRI alone, suggesting a more straightforward and cost-effective path for potential clinical translation compared to models that require complex, multi-modal data acquisition protocols [74]. The reliability of the learned neurosignatures is further supported by the well-phenotyped nature of our cohort, underscoring the importance of data quality alongside analytical rigor in neuroimaging machine learning.

In interpreting these results, it is necessary to consider the study’s limitations, which both contextualize the present findings and point to important avenues for further investigation. First, a critical limitation is the potential confounding influence of antidepressant medication. As all patients were receiving pharmacotherapy, the observed connectivity patterns may reflect both disease-state pathology and treatment-related neural adaptations. Specifically, antidepressants are known to modulate glutamatergic and monoaminergic systems that directly influence the default mode and salience networks identified in our model. Although we controlled for medication load as a covariate, we acknowledge that the specific impact of these agents on our classification accuracy remains difficult to disentangle. It is plausible that our model has learned features that are resilient to or representative of the ‘treated state’ of these disorders, which may limit the generalizability to medication-naïve populations. Future longitudinal studies tracking neural changes before and after medication initiation are essential to isolate the specific contributions of pharmacotherapy from the intrinsic pathophysiology of GAD and MDD.

Furthermore, to isolate the distinct neurobiological signatures of GAD and MDD, we employed strict exclusion criteria for comorbid psychiatric conditions. While this approach enhances diagnostic purity, it results in a sample that is less representative of the complex, comorbid presentations typically encountered in real-world clinical settings. Consequently, the performance of our classifier may be less robust in differentiating these disorders when they co-occur with other conditions. Crucially, the generalizability of the identified precuneus-centric neurosignature requires external validation in independent, multi-site cohorts. [75]. Our findings cannot delineate whether the neural patterns are enduring traits or transient states, given the cross-sectional design. To resolve this, longitudinal studies must examine the stability of these features and their utility in predicting illness course and treatment response.

In conclusion, this study makes a substantive advance by deploying a rigorous computational framework to delineate a dissociable role for the precuneus in GAD versus MDD, anchored in its functional connectivity and local dynamics. We move beyond broad characterizations of DMN dysregulation to specify a diametric pattern at this key hub, linking its opposing functional signatures to distinct clinical dimensions: the excessive engagement driven by anxiety in GAD, and the disconnection associated with motivation in MDD. Methodologically, the work demonstrates that highly validated machine learning applied to high-dimensional functional features can yield both clinically informative classification and interpretable neurobiological insights. By translating complex brain organization patterns into a viable differential computational framework, this research provides a validated framework and a set of candidate neurobiological signatures that pave the way toward actionable, biology-informed clinical decision-support systems in psychiatry.
