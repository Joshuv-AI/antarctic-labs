Ascorbate-driven quinone redox cycling as a pro-oxidant anticancer strategy
Authors: Christophe Glorieux, Cinthya Enriquez-Lara, Jaime A Valderrama, Julio Benites, Pedro Buc Calderon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractQuinones are natural compounds widely distributed in animals, plants, and microorganisms, where they play essential roles in cellular energy metabolism. Ascorbate (vitamin C) can exert antioxidant or pro-oxidant effects depending on concentration and cellular context. Studies have shown that quinones can undergo redox cycling, leading to the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), a process that is further enhanced in the presence of ascorbate.Due to the elevated production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and compromised antioxidant defenses in cancer cells, redox imbalance has been proposed as a potential “Achilles’ heel” of tumors. In this context, the combination of ascorbate and quinones represents a promising strategy for selectively targeting cancer cells through enhanced ROS generation.This review discusses the mechanisms and biological effects of natural and synthetic quinoid compounds, particularly their interactions with ascorbate in cancer cells.Keywords: Quinones, Ascorbate, Vitamin C, ROS, Redox cycling, Cancer

Introduction

Cancer remains a significant global health challenge with substantial incidence and mortality rates [1]. Cancer is characterized by uncontrolled growth, resistance to cell death, limitless replication, angiogenesis, invasion and metastasis, immune evasion, and genomic instability [2]. There is a consensus in the scientific literature that the alteration of redox homeostasis within tumor cells is another important hallmark of cancer, and current anticancer strategies increasingly recognize redox imbalance as a targetable vulnerability, particularly in tumors operating at elevated oxidative set points [3]. Cancer cells often exhibit increased levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS) due to altered levels of antioxidant molecules [4,5], mitochondrial dysfunction [6,7], and the activation of pro-oxidant enzymes such as NADPH oxidases [8]. Activation of these redox mechanisms causes an oxidative stress, which favors tumor growth by promoting cell proliferation and genetic instability [3,4]. However, tumors frequently exhibit reprogrammed rather than merely weakened antioxidant systems. Instead of a simple loss of redox defense capacity, cancer cells actively adapt their antioxidant networks to maintain redox balance under conditions of persistent oxidative stress. This metabolic rewiring includes altered glutathione metabolism, increased thioredoxin activity, and changes in NADPH production, allowing tumor cells to tolerate elevated reactive oxygen species while preserving signals that support proliferation, survival, and therapy resistance [3,7]. It should be noted, however, that low to moderate levels of ROS may promote tumor progression through redox signaling, whereas excessive ROS accumulation surpasses a critical threshold and becomes incompatible with cancer cell survival [3,7].

Therapeutic strategies based on redox modulation therefore aim to shift ROS levels from a pro-tumorigenic signaling range to a cytotoxic domain. Owing to the higher generation of ROS and impaired antioxidant defenses in cancer cells, the redox dysregulation thus appears as an ‘‘Achilles’ heel’’ of tumors and a vulnerability that could be exploited to selectively kill cancer cells [3,7]. Therefore, any increase in ROS levels would make cancer cells vulnerable to an additional oxidative stress due to their redox imbalance (Fig. 1). Importantly, this concept does not imply a nonspecific increase in oxidative damage, but rather a targeted exacerbation of an already dysregulated redox state that is characteristic of tumor cells. In this context, redox-active compounds may act as pro-oxidants selectively in cancer cells while remaining relatively innocuous in normal tissues endowed with intact antioxidant defenses.

Based on these observations, anticancer strategies have been developed to disrupt the precarious tumor redox homeostasis. Indeed, such biochemical property can be exploited by using redox-modulating compounds, which represent an interesting approach to induce cancer cell death. For this purpose, numerous redox-based agents, as single agents or in combination with chemotherapy, have been included in clinical trials [3,7]. In this context, our lab and others have demonstrated that ascorbate-driven redox cycling triggers selective tumor cell death. Therefore, a new strategy based on the use of pharmacologic concentrations of ascorbate and redox-active quinones has been developed [[9], [10], [11], [12], [13], [14], [15], [16]]. We postulate that ascorbate-driven quinone redox cycling functions as a controllable ROS amplifier that selectively pushes cancer cells beyond their redox viability threshold while overwhelming mitochondrial and proteostatic buffering systems. Briefly, when ROS are formed, they induce oxidative stress that preferentially kills cancer cells and spares healthy tissues. ROS primarily include superoxide anion (O2•-), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), hydroxyl radical (•OH), and, under specific photochemical or enzymatic conditions, singlet oxygen (1O2). These species differ markedly in reactivity, lifetime, diffusion capacity, and biological impact. Superoxide is typically generated through the one-electron reduction of molecular oxygen and is frequently, although not invariably, converted into H2O2 either spontaneously or through superoxide dismutase (SOD)-catalyzed dismutation. H2O2 is comparatively more stable and diffusible, and can function both as a redox signaling molecule and as a precursor of more reactive oxidants. By contrast, •OH is not usually produced as a primary ROS in biological systems, but is mainly formed from H2O2 through metal-catalyzed reactions, such as Fenton chemistry, which require redox-active iron. Therefore, the relative abundance and biological relevance of each ROS depend on the local redox environment, antioxidant enzyme systems, compartmentalization, and metal availability. In quinone redox cycling, particularly in quinone–ascorbate systems, H2O2 is often the most functionally relevant oxidant, as it can arise from superoxide dismutation and may accumulate in extra- and/or intracellular compartments depending on quinone properties and experimental conditions [[17], [18], [19], [20]]. In accordance with the experimental data reported in the literature, we distinguished the role of different oxidant species and their subcellular localizations when discussing specific mechanisms and redox modulation, and used the general term “ROS” when the types of oxidant species were not determined.

The present review aims to critically examine the major findings on the antitumor activity of combinations of ascorbate with both naturally occurring and synthetically designed quinones, and to delineate shared as well as compound-specific mechanisms of action that may inform novel anticancer strategies. We begin by introducing the first component of this association: sodium ascorbate, more commonly known as vitamin C.

Vitamin C (ascorbate), a molecule with biological activities beyond its antioxidant properties

Vitamin C has long been controversial in cancer therapy [21], largely because oral administration cannot reproduce the pharmacologic plasma concentrations achieved by intravenous delivery. This pharmacokinetic distinction is central to understanding its pro-oxidant anticancer activity. In this context, pharmacokinetic data suggested that pharmacologic concentrations of vitamin C (which can reach up to 20 mM in blood) can be achieved by intravenous and intraperitoneal administration, while plasma concentrations achieved orally remained physiological (50-100 μM) [22,23]. Since these concentrations exhibit anticancer activities in vitro (EC50 ranging from 3 to 7 mM), this raised the controversial question of the re-evaluation of vitamin C in cancer treatment. According to the pharmacokinetic data, parenteral administration of vitamin C decreased the tumor volumes in a mouse model of liver cancer, whereas oral administration of the same dosage did not affect tumor growth [23]. Taken together, the method of administration is crucial for achieving pharmacologic concentrations of vitamin C and providing potent anticancer effects.

Vitamin C was previously considered to have antioxidant activities by scavenging a variety of reactive oxygen and nitrogen species, including superoxide, hydroxyl radicals, singlet oxygen, lipid peroxyl radicals, nitric oxide, and peroxynitrite [24,25], and thereby protecting normal cells from oxidative damage. Conversely, vitamin C also displays a strong ability to induce an increased production of hydrogen peroxide when used at higher concentrations and in the presence of metal ions like iron or copper [26,27]. This dual role makes vitamin C particularly valuable for specifically targeting tumor cells while avoiding toxicity in normal tissues. Since pro-oxidants may affect both normal and tumor cells, the extremely low toxicity of vitamin C represents another major advantage, and supports the safe inclusion of vitamin C in clinical protocols for treating cancer patients. Multiple studies have demonstrated that the main reactive species generated by vitamin C is hydrogen peroxide, which can further produce several toxic molecules such as the hydroxyl radical, and induce subsequent various biological effects in cancer cells [[28], [29], [30]].

A key mechanistic question in pharmacologic ascorbate biology is the cellular compartment in which H2O2 and other ROS are generated and accumulate. Depending on the model system, H2O2 can arise predominantly in the extracellular milieu (followed by diffusion across cellular membranes) or be produced in intracellular compartments [31], where redox cycling is favored. This distinction is not trivial, as it is influenced by cell density, medium composition, and the presence of H2O2 scavengers (including pyruvate), as well as by membrane permeability to H2O2 (e.g., AQP) and extracellular antioxidant enzymes. Accordingly, comparisons across in vitro studies require careful consideration of these variables, and mechanistic claims of “intracellular delivery” of H2O2 should ideally be supported by appropriate controls (e.g., extracellular catalase protection assays, compartment-specific probes, and standardized culture conditions).

The vitamin C-mediated generation of H2O2 leads to oxidative stress, DNA damage, and selective killing of cancer cells [32]. The additional anticancer effects of vitamin C include enhanced collagen synthesis [33], reduced angiogenesis [34], stimulation of immune function [35], and altered epigenetic regulation [36]. For further details on the mechanisms of vitamin C-mediated H2O2 generation and cytotoxicity against cancer cells, the reader can refer to our previous review article [32].

Since vitamin C does not display strong antitumor activity as a single agent in clinical trials, the identification of optimal combinations with vitamin C is still needed. In this context, multiple chemotherapies [[37], [38], [39], [40], [41]], radiotherapy [42] and immunotherapy [43] can synergize with vitamin C by enhancing ROS generation and targeting other cancer vulnerabilities.

We will now focus on the second partner of the association with vitamin C, namely the quinone compounds.

The world of bioactive quinones

Chemical reactivity of quinoid compounds

Quinones are natural compounds widely distributed in animals, plants and microorganisms. They are involved in the biochemistry of energy production associated with the respiratory chain of living cells [44,45]. The structure of benzo- and naphthoquinones exhibits electrophilic double bonds, making them important chemical agents used in redox dehydrogenation reactions and as useful synthetic precursors of substituted analogues [46]. One of the most notable chemical properties of quinones is their facile and reversible electron transfer reactions [47,48] that proceed through the formation of semiquinone radical intermediates. It is worth noting that the magnitude of the redox potentials of quinones, a measure of their electronic capability, depends on the location of substituents around the quinoid scaffold and the surrounding environment (e.g., pH) [49]. In biological and chemical systems, quinones can undergo redox cycling, leading to the generation of reactive species including semiquinone radicals, superoxides, hydrogen peroxide and hydroxyl radicals [50]. This property is relevant in toxicology as shown by quinones like 1,4-benzoquinone that can cause oxidative stress in cells [51,52]. In therapeutics, such a property is exploited in anticancer and antimicrobial agents where ROS-induced damage is desired [[53], [54], [55]].

In this context, the 1,4-benzoquinones undergo reversible two-electron reductions to give the corresponding hydroquinones (1,4-dihydroxybenzenes) in protic media. These quinone/hydroquinone redox couples play crucial roles in biological systems as electron carriers in processes like photosynthesis and respiration (e.g., ubiquinone in the electron transport chain), as well as in analytical chemistry, such as their involvement in electrochemical reactions for sensing and detection applications. Since quinones exhibit strong UV-visible absorption due to their conjugated π-systems, this property is exploited in spectroscopic analysis and photocatalytic reactions [56,57].

Their synthetic potential arises from their electron-acceptor capacities, which allow the modification of their scaffolds by the insertion of new substituents into the respective quinone nucleus. This behavior is determined by the electron-acceptor quinone carbonyl groups that facilitate the attack of diverse nucleophiles at the electroactive ring. Based on the structure of the quinoid precursors, substitution products can be generated. Such are the cases of the insertion of alkylthio and arylamino groups into 1,4-benzo- and 1,4-naphthoquinones, which provide substitution products utilized in protein modification and medical applications [58,59].

Mechanism of action explaining the biological activities of quinones

Quinones are known for their distinctive chemical reactivity and significant biological activities. Their mechanisms of action are intricately linked to their redox properties: the ability to generate ROS and their interactions with biological macromolecules. These characteristics underlie their roles in physiological and pathological processes, as well as their applications in medicine and toxicology [44].

Several quinoid compounds are efficient electron carriers, which are able to mediate, in a reversible manner, the transfer between reducing agents and dioxygen by the so-called redox cycling. Under aerobic conditions, the one-electron reduction of the quinone by a suitable reducing agent generates a semiquinone intermediate, which in turn transfers one electron to dioxygen and reconverts itself into the original quinone yielding ROS such as superoxide anion and hydrogen peroxide [60]. Traditionally, the single electron reduction is usually performed by reductase enzymes. A very appealing candidate as a reducing agent to mimic the enzymatic quinone redox cycling is vitamin C. Indeed, although enzymatic one-electron reduction of quinones is classically mediated by flavoprotein reductases, pharmacological concentrations of ascorbate can efficiently substitute for these enzymes and sustain redox cycling [32,41,[61], [62], [63], [64]]. This non-enzymatic electron donation becomes particularly relevant under conditions of high intracellular ascorbate, as achieved through parenteral administration. This process consists of successive reduction–oxidation steps. Quinones accept electrons from biological reductants (e.g., NADH, NADPH), generating semiquinone radicals. These radicals can then transfer a single electron to molecular oxygen, regenerating the quinone precursor and producing ROS (such as superoxides and hydrogen peroxide), thereby causing damage to DNA, lipids, and proteins. While this activity is toxic to cells, it is also exploited in cancer therapy, where quinones selectively target rapidly dividing cancer cells [11,53].

Quinones act as redox cycling and electrophilic stressors by covalently modifying nucleophilic residues in proteins, particularly cysteines (Fig. 3). Such electrophilic reactivity may cooperate with oxidative stress to disrupt redox-sensitive signaling pathways and protein homeostasis in cancer cells [53,65,66]. Indeed, quinones frequently operate through a continuum of mechanisms rather than a single pathway, and the mere presence of quinoid structures does not necessarily confer toxicity, which instead depends on factors such as structural features, reactivity, and cellular context [67]. For some scaffolds, cytotoxicity is primarily driven by accelerated redox cycling with consequent superoxide/H2O2 generation. For others - particularly strongly electrophilic quinones - covalent adduction to nucleophilic thiols (e.g., glutathione and reactive cysteines in proteins) can be a major determinant of stress signaling and toxicity, sometimes preceding or amplifying ROS formation by depleting antioxidant reserves [53,68,69].

Schematic representation of the dual reactivity of quinones as redox cyclers and electrophilic agents. The two principal biochemical properties that determine quinone cytotoxicity are illustrated. At the top, one-electron reduction of the quinone by cellular reductants (e.g., NAD(P)H or ascorbate) generates semiquinone radicals that transfer electrons to molecular oxygen, leading to superoxide and hydrogen peroxide formation and the propagation of oxidative stress. Intracellular concentration of ascorbate is achieved through GLUT1 or SVCT, the transporters for dehydroascorbic acid (DHA) and ascorbate, respectively. This redox cycling process amplifies reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and perturbs mitochondrial function and cellular bioenergetics. At the bottom, the electrophilic character of the quinone scaffold enables Michael-type addition reactions with nucleophilic residues (–SH, –NH, –OH) in proteins and other macromolecules, resulting in covalent modification, enzyme inactivation, and disruption of redox-sensitive signaling pathways. The balance between ROS generation and electrophilic reactivity ultimately determines the intensity and modality of quinone-induced cytotoxicity.

It is noteworthy that quinones can also undergo substitution reactions with nucleophilic fragments, such as –SH and –NH, bonded to proteins and DNA. These structural changes in biological macromolecules can alter enzyme activities, disrupt signaling pathways, and induce apoptosis. For example, mitomycin C, a quinone-based anticancer drug, alkylates DNA to inhibit replication and transcription [70,71].

Regarding quinones such as coenzyme Q10, they have predominantly protective effects through their antioxidant properties, including scavenging of superoxide and lipid peroxyl radicals, and the maintenance of mitochondrial function. Coenzyme Q10 plays a central role in cellular respiration and has been implicated in protection against neurodegenerative and cardiovascular disorders [72]. As a physiological quinone, its behavior is primarily determined by its function as an electron carrier and redox buffer; however, under conditions of redox imbalance, it may exhibit pro-oxidant activity through semiquinone formation and subsequent generation of superoxide radicals and hydrogen peroxide. Structurally, coenzyme Q10 is characterized by full substitution of the quinone ring with alkyl and alkoxy groups, which effectively prevents Michael addition reactions and limits electrophilic reactivity [60]. Consequently, in contrast to cytotoxic quinones, its low electrophilicity and tight biological regulation restrict its capacity to induce direct molecular damage, positioning it primarily as a modulator of redox homeostasis rather than as a therapeutic pro-oxidant agent.

Ascorbate is transported into cells primarily through the sodium-dependent vitamin C transporters SVCT1 and SVCT2, whereas its oxidized form, dehydroascorbate (DHA), is taken up via facilitative glucose transporters (GLUTs) through a Na + -independent mechanism [73,74]. Although SVCT-mediated transport represents the major physiological route for vitamin C uptake, alternative mechanisms also contribute under specific conditions. For example, human and murine erythrocytes lack functional SVCT transporters yet contain significant intracellular ascorbate concentrations. Furthermore, intestinal ascorbate absorption remains largely preserved in SVCT1-knockout mice, suggesting the existence of compensatory transport pathways. Studies from Levine and colleagues have shown that intestinal DHA uptake may involve GLUT2 and GLUT8 transporters [75]. Owing to its structural similarity to glucose, DHA is transported through GLUT family members and is rapidly reduced intracellularly to ascorbate by glutathione- and NADPH-dependent mechanisms [76,77]. Notably, erythrocytes efficiently accumulate DHA even under glucose-depleted conditions, followed by its rapid intracellular reduction to ascorbate [76].

In the context of ascorbate combinations, the redox and electrophilic axes may converge: ascorbate can promote repeated quinone reduction/oxidation cycles, while electrophilic reactivity may simultaneously accelerate glutathione depletion, thereby lowering the threshold for ROS-mediated injury [62]. We therefore interpret “synergy” between ascorbate and quinones as potentially arising from both increased oxidant flux and enhanced electrophilic stress, depending on the scaffold and cellular context.

As shown in Fig. 4, quinones can influence cellular signaling and, by consequence, regulate cell death pathways by interacting with redox-sensitive proteins. For instance, quinones may modulate the Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (Keap1)/nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) signaling pathway. Indeed, tert-butylhydroquinone (tBHQ) modifies cysteine residues in Keap1, stabilizing Nrf2 and activating antioxidant response elements (ARE). This upregulates cytoprotective enzymes and reduces oxidative damage [78,79]. They can also inhibit nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) activation, notably reducing inflammation processes [80,81]. As previously mentioned, one typical example of apoptosis induction is β-lapachone that induces cancer cell death through NAD(P)H: quinone oxidoreductase 1 (NQO1) activity, causing lethal oxidative stress [82]. Regarding NQO1, it should be stressed that beyond its classical role in futile redox cycling and PARP-mediated cell death, NQO1 has emerged as a broader regulator of tumor redox biology. Recent studies indicate that NQO1 activity contributes to mitochondrial redox imbalance, metabolic vulnerability, and can influence ferroptotic sensitivity as well as antitumor immune responses, particularly in KEAP1-deficient tumors [83,84]. Importantly, not all quinones are substrates for NQO1, and a clear distinction must be made between NQO1-bioactivatable quinones and compounds that undergo non-enzymatic or one-electron redox cycling [11]. Key NQO1-bioactivatable quinones include β-lapachone, menadione, plumbagin, while shikonin and mitoxantrone are primarily activated by other enzymatic systems (Table 1). This distinction is essential for mechanistic clarity and for the rational development of ascorbate–quinone combinations with translational potential.

Compartmentalized ROS generation and oxidative stress–driven cell death modalities. The subcellular sources and propagation of ROS and their impact on distinct regulated cell death pathways are illustrated. Quinones (Q) undergo redox cycling in the presence of ascorbate (AscH−), generating H2O2. Additional ROS sources include NADPH oxidases (NOX) at the plasma membrane and mitochondrial electron transport chain leakage, producing superoxide (O2•-), which is dismutated by superoxide dismutases (SOD) to H2O2. Intracellular antioxidant systems — including glutathione (GSH), glutathione peroxidases (GPx), peroxiredoxins (PRX), thioredoxin (Trx), and NADPH-dependent reductases — buffer ROS to maintain redox homeostasis. When ROS production exceeds the cellular buffering capacity, redox imbalance ensues. H2O2 can undergo Fenton chemistry in the presence of Fe2+ to generate hydroxyl radicals (•OH), amplifying oxidative damage and leading to cell death such as apoptosis, necrosis, ferroptosis, necroptosis and pyroptosis. Nuclear Nrf2 activation represents a compensatory antioxidant response that attempts to restore redox balance; however, sustained oxidative stress shifts the cellular outcome toward distinct death modalities depending on ROS intensity, localization, and bioenergetic status. This scheme should be interpreted as a conceptual framework rather than as evidence that all depicted pathways are universally activated by ascorbate–quinone systems. The relative contribution of apoptosis, necroptosis, ferroptosis, pyroptosis, or bioenergetic collapse depends on the quinone scaffold, cellular context, antioxidant buffering capacity, and the experimental model used.

Enhanced ROS levels can activate multiple regulated cell death pathways, including apoptosis, necroptosis, and ferroptosis [3]; however, the specific pathways engaged in ascorbate–quinone systems remain incompletely defined and appear to depend on both ROS intensity and cellular context. Thus, oxidative stress operates not as a uniform trigger but as a spatially organized signaling network whose magnitude, persistence, and subcellular origin dictate the transition from redox adaptation to apoptosis, necroptosis, ferroptosis, pyroptosis, or bioenergetic collapse.

Although a wide range of quinones has been reported to synergize with ascorbate, their behavior is not uniform. Key properties such as redox potential, mode of electron transfer, electrophilicity, and interaction with cellular reductases critically determine their activity. These factors govern ROS generation and mechanistic outcomes will be discussed in the next section, thereby defining which quinone scaffolds are most suitable for ascorbate-based strategies.

Key characteristics of common quinoid compounds

While a broad diversity of quinones has been reported to synergize with ascorbate, not all quinone scaffolds are expected to behave similarly. A central question that remains to be addressed is which properties define an optimal quinone partner for pharmacological ascorbate. The magnitude and selectivity of ascorbate-driven cytotoxicity do not depend on a single variable but rather on a combination of physicochemical and biological determinants that can be used as rational criteria for quinone selection. These include redox behavior, electrophilic reactivity, cellular uptake, subcellular localization, and the capacity to sustain redox cycling under biologically relevant conditions. Table 1 summarizes the principal molecular descriptors and functional parameters considered essential for this purpose. The quinones listed therein (thymoquinone, tanespimycin (17-AAG), β-lapachone, plumbagin, shikonin and mitoxantrone) exert anticancer effects in various types of cancers [88,112,132,[134], [135], [136], [137], [138], [139], [140], [141], [142], [143], [144], [145], [146]], and were selected as representative model compounds to illustrate and comparatively discuss these determinants.

To move beyond descriptive comparisons, quinone–ascorbate systems can be analyzed within a structured framework that considers key mechanistic determinants. These include: (i) the dominant activation pathway (NQO1-dependent two-electron reduction versus one-electron redox cycling), (ii) the relative contribution of redox cycling versus electrophilic thiol reactivity, (iii) the spatial origin of ROS generation (intracellular versus extracellular), and (iv) dependence on cellular antioxidant systems such as catalase and glutathione. Applying this framework allows a more systematic comparison of quinone classes and helps identify those with the highest translational potential.

While redox potentials are frequently cited as key physicochemical descriptors of quinones [49], direct comparison of absolute values across different compounds requires caution. The variability in experimental conditions used to determine these parameters — including differences in pH, solvent composition, supporting electrolytes, working electrode materials, and reference systems — precludes strict quantitative comparison between studies. Accordingly, the redox potentials presented in Table 1 should be interpreted as contextual indicators of electron-accepting capacity rather than as strictly comparable thermodynamic constants. In cellular environments, redox behavior is further modulated by enzymatic reduction kinetics, intracellular localization, semiquinone stability, and the availability of molecular oxygen and transition metals. Thus, cytotoxic outcomes cannot be inferred solely from reported electrochemical potentials but instead arise from the interplay between physicochemical properties and biological context.

The quinones depicted in Table 1 exhibit substantial mechanistic heterogeneity that directly influences their cytotoxic behavior, reflecting differences in their ability to generate ROS, to act as electrophiles, or to combine both properties. While some compounds primarily function as redox cyclers, promoting sustained ROS production and oxidative stress, others exert their effects mainly through covalent modification of nucleophilic residues in proteins, particularly cysteines, thereby disrupting antioxidant systems and survival signaling pathways.

For instance, β-lapachone exemplifies a quinone whose cytotoxicity is largely driven by enzymatically fueled redox cycling, resulting in excessive production of superoxide radicals and hydrogen peroxide, NAD+ depletion, mitochondrial dysfunction and, depending on bioenergetic status, either apoptotic or caspase-independent cell death [11,[101], [102], [103], [104], [105], [106],137,140]. In contrast, plumbagin [66,81,108,[110], [111], [112], [113], [114], [115], [116], [117], [118],142] and shikonin [[119], [120], [121], [122], [123], [124], [125], [126], [127],139,145,146] exhibit dual reactivity, combining redox cycling with electrophilic activity. Through one-electron reduction, they form semiquinone intermediates that generate superoxide and, subsequently, hydrogen peroxide; under metal-catalyzed conditions, hydroxyl radicals may arise secondarily. In parallel, their quinone moiety acts as a Michael acceptor toward cellular thiols, leading to glutathione depletion and modification of thiol-dependent proteins. These coupled processes collectively enhance oxidative stress and contribute to their cytotoxic effects. Other compounds, such as thymoquinone [[85], [86], [87], [88], [89], [90], [91],134,138,147,148] and tanespimycin [10,[92], [93], [94],96,98,99], rely more strongly on selective protein targeting and proteostasis disruption than on explosive ROS production mainly involving moderate accumulation of superoxide and hydrogen peroxide, illustrating that quinoid scaffolds do not uniformly translate into redox-dominant mechanisms. Mitoxantrone further underscores this diversity, as its cytotoxicity is largely mediated through DNA intercalation and topoisomerase inhibition rather than oxidative amplification [129,[131], [132], [133],141]. Mitoxantrone is included here primarily as a structural reference rather than as a prototypical redox-cycling quinone, as its cytotoxic activity is largely independent of ascorbate-driven ROS amplification. Overall, cytotoxic efficacy correlates less with a single physicochemical parameter than with the capacity of a compound to integrate ROS generation, antioxidant system impairment, mitochondrial vulnerability and proteostasis disruption. These findings support a model in which quinone-induced cell death emerges from the convergence of redox imbalance and protein damage, with the specific death modality determined by metabolic buffering capacity, organelle sensitivity and the intensity of oxidative stress.

Briefly, key parameters may be recapitulated as follows: (i) lipophilicity, which modulates where ROS are generated (ii) dependence on cellular reductases (e.g., NQO1, P450 oxidoreductase, and related flavoproteins); that catalyze one- or two-electron reductions; (iii) electrophilic reactivity (Michael acceptor propensity), which can drive thiol adduction and glutathione depletion independently of ROS; and (iv) the redox potential and semiquinone stability, which influence the rate and persistence of redox cycling.

Emerging evidence indicates that cancer cells operate at an elevated redox set point that supports proliferation but simultaneously narrows their tolerance window to additional oxidative insults [4]. In this context, pharmacologic ascorbate does not merely increase ROS levels but may function as a redox amplifier when coupled to quinone redox cycling [62]. This amplification can exceed mitochondrial buffering capacity, destabilize proteostasis networks and disrupt iron-lipid homeostasis, thereby determining the modality of cell death. Framing ascorbate-quinone interactions within this redox threshold model provides a unifying mechanistic rationale for their selective anticancer activity.

A central challenge in the field is to distinguish mechanisms that are intrinsic to ascorbate–quinone systems from those that reflect general properties of quinones or ROS biology. In this review, we therefore adopt a hierarchical framework that explicitly separates: (i) experimentally demonstrated mechanisms in defined ascorbate–quinone combinations, (ii) mechanisms inferred from quinone-only or ascorbate-only systems, and (iii) broader conceptual models derived from ROS biology. This distinction is essential to avoid overgeneralization and to identify the specific chemical and biological features that govern the activity of ascorbate-driven redox cycling.

In the following sections, we discuss the association of ascorbate with quinone families through this lens and highlight where evidence supports redox cycling-dominant contributions to anticancer activity.

Ascorbate and its association with quinones

Ascorbate-driven quinone redox cycling represents a powerful strategy to generate substantial H2O2 fluxes in tumor systems, potentially exceeding the antioxidant buffering capacity, depending on cellular context and compartmentalization. In quinone–ascorbate systems, the relative contributions of extracellular and intracellular H2O2 generation are expected to be influenced by quinone lipophilicity, membrane permeability, and the localization of reductive enzymes. Lipophilic quinones may favor intracellular redox cycling, potentially within mitochondria or other membrane-rich compartments, whereas more hydrophilic quinones are more likely to promote extracellular H2O2 formation, which can subsequently diffuse into tumor cells through aquaporins (AQPs) [62,149]. Importantly, this compartmental balance is highly context-dependent and influenced by experimental variables such as cell density, culture conditions, and extracellular antioxidant buffering, and may differentially impact tumor selectivity and cytotoxic outcomes [63,[150], [151], [152]].

Responses to ascorbate-quinone combinations are expected to vary markedly across tumors, and several biomarkers may help to stratify sensitivity. These include: (i) antioxidant buffering capacity (catalase, glutathione, and peroxiredoxins/glutathione peroxidases systems); (ii) NQO1 expression/activity and redox enzyme networks that govern quinone reduction; (iii) Nrf2/Keap1 status, which shapes transcriptional antioxidant responses and electrophile sensing; (iv) mitochondrial function and metabolic phenotype (oxidative phosphorylation reliance versus glycolytic dependence), which influence susceptibility to ATP depletion and mitochondrial ROS; and (v) labile iron pools that can amplify oxidative damage through Fenton chemistry. With regard to this latter point, it should be noted that in the reducing cytosolic environment, ascorbate is particularly effective at mobilizing iron from ferritin, even at low physiological concentrations. This process can be further modulated by quinones acting as electron mediators, with their effects depending on their structure and redox potential, thereby influencing iron release and redox-driven cellular processes [153].

When available, we highlight these determinants alongside each quinone family and propose their use as practical biomarkers for future translational studies.

In this review, we summarize and critically analyze the experimental findings on the therapeutic potential of diverse classes of carbo- and heterocyclic quinones in combination with ascorbate. We focus on how this association modulates their biological activity and enhances antitumor effects. Particular attention is given to shared and compound-specific mechanisms of action underlying this synergy. By integrating these results, we aim to highlight key structure–activity relationships and identify general principles that may guide future drug development strategies. The association of ascorbate and euryfuryl-containing 1,4-benzo- and 1,4-naphthoquinones

A series of synthetic euryfuryl-substituted quinones 10–14, outlined in Fig. 5A, have been tested in combination with vitamin C to counteract cancer cell proliferation and induce cell death. The main findings are summarized below:

The combination of vitamin C (2 mM) with 2-euryfuryl-1,4-benzoquinone (10) induced marked cell death in TLT hepatoma cells, associated with decreased GSH and ATP levels. The association with compound 2-euryfuryl-3-nitro-1,4-benzoquinone (11) did not affect cell survival but induced decreases in both GSH and ATP levels [154]. 2-euryfuryl-1,4-naphthoquinone (12) and their hydroxy analogues, namely 2-euryfuryl-5-hydroxy-1,4-naphthoquinone (13) and 2-euryfuryl-5,8-dihydroxy-1,4-naphthoquinone (14) were further evaluated: while compounds 12 and 14 were non-toxic (0–10 μg/mL), compound 13 induced about 50% necrotic cell death. Vitamin C did not enhance cytolysis by compound 13 but potentiated depletion of ATP and GSH for all compounds [155]. It was hypothesized that the insertion of hydroxy groups in naphthoquinones (12–14), could increase the electron-withdrawing effect of the quinoid nucleus compared with the parent quinone compound 12. This effect was attributed to intramolecular hydrogen bond interactions, making them function as internal Brønsted acid, thus modifying the electron transfer from ascorbate to the electroactive quinoid nucleus [155].

The association of ascorbate and mono- and disubstituted 1,4-naphthoquinones

A group of the substituted 1,4-naphthoquinones (15–18), previously prepared in our laboratory (Fig. 5B), were examined in order to evaluate their potential synergism induced by ascorbate. We will first discuss the results reported for the association between ascorbate and menadione (2-methyl-1,4-naphthoquinone: vitamin K3, 16) including results obtained from in vitro, ex-vivo, in vivo analyses and clinical trials, when possible. We then focus our discussion on the effects obtained from the association of ascorbate and juglone (5-hydroxy-1,4-naphthoquinone 15). We also discuss the synergistic effects observed with ascorbate and 3-acyl-2-arylamino-1,4-naphthoquinones (17) and arylamino-1,4-naphthoquinones (18).

The association of ascorbate and menadione

Recent studies support the anticancer activity of vitamin C combined with menadione (16). In vitro, vitamin C (300 μM) plus menadione (3 μM) reduced leukemia lymphocyte viability (∼20%) without affecting normal lymphocytes, and enhanced the cytotoxicity of several anticancer drugs in Jurkat cells. Notably, combinations with barasertib and everolimus showed increased effects over 72 h, associated with apoptosis induction, decreased hydroperoxides, and increased protein carbonylation. Leukemic cells were more sensitive to these combinations than normal lymphocytes [61].

Another in vitro study showed that the ascorbate–menadione combination (100:1) inhibits ribonucleotide reductase (RNR), mainly targeting its R2 subunit. This was accompanied by oxidation of both Trx and GSH systems, leading to GSH depletion, increased protein glutathionylation, and oxidized Trx1. The resulting inhibition of glutathione peroxidase increased lipid peroxidation and triggered apoptosis-inducing factor (AIF)-mediated cell death. Thus, the combination induces both replicative and oxidative stress in cancer cells [156].

The role of ascorbate plus menadione in targeting cancer cell mitochondria was also investigated. This combination exhibited a highly specific suppression of cancer cell growth and viability, an effect that was accompanied by an extremely high production of mitochondrial superoxide in cancer cells, but not in normal cells. In addition, a significant dose-dependent depolarization of the mitochondrial membrane and depletion of oncometabolite succinate were observed in cancer cells [64].

In vitro studies in glioblastoma and non-transformed glial cells showed that ascorbate/menadione induces greater toxicity in cancer cells through an H2O2- and iron-dependent mechanism, suggesting a contribution of ferroptosis. Selenium supplementation reduced this cytotoxicity by enhancing antioxidant selenoproteins such as thioredoxin reductases and glutathione peroxidases, while inhibition of thioredoxin or glutathione-dependent systems further increased such toxicity. Ascorbate/menadione compete with thioredoxin for electrons, thereby impairing peroxiredoxin 1 function and disrupting redox homeostasis [157]. Interestingly, peroxiredoxin 1 is overexpressed in triple-negative breast cancer and it is associated with favorable prognosis: functional targeting of peroxiredoxin 1, either by genetic silencing or chemical inhibition with auranofin (a TrxR inhibitor), reduces cancer cell growth in vitro. Notably, these effects are further enhanced when combined with ascorbate and menadione, indicating a synergistic cytotoxic response [158].

Xenograft studies using androgen-independent DU145 prostate tumors in nude mice showed a significant survival benefit with ascorbate–menadione treatment [159]. Different administration routes led to tumor regression through oxidative stress–induced damage to nuclear components, ER, and mitochondria, associated with necrotic atrophy and features described as autoschizis [13,[160], [161], [162], [163], [164]]. Although this phenotype reflects extreme oxidative fragmentation, its distinction from severe ROS-driven necrosis remains unclear and is not formally recognized by current cell death classifications [165]. Notably, these effects occurred without apparent damage to normal tissues, supporting further evaluation of this combination in cancer therapy [159]. Nevertheless, despite previous in vitro and in vivo findings, oral supplementation with vitamin C did not enhance the effects of menadione in the RapidCaP model. This is an approach based on somatic gene transfer of Cre recombinase and luciferase into the prostate of genetically engineered mice, leading to focal tumor initiation followed by highly penetrant metastatic progression in a native microenvironment [166]. These results suggest that high-dose parenteral administration of vitamin C is required to assess its oxidative anticancer efficacy in vivo.

The combination of vitamin C, menadione, and α-tocopheryl succinate induced multiple cell death pathways in carcinoma cells, depending on concentration, with similar effects in melanoma cells, although α-tocopheryl succinate showed antagonistic behavior. Sublethal doses upregulated natural killer (NK) cell-activating ligands such as MICA (the stress-signaling ligand of the NKG2D receptor) and receptors (NKp30, NKp44, NKp46), enhancing tumor cell lysis by NK cells. These findings indicate both direct cytotoxic and immune-mediated mechanisms of action [167].

The association of ascorbate and juglone

Juglone (15), is a secondary metabolite produced naturally in walnut-type trees and exhibits allelopathic features in its native environment. In this context, it has been shown that 15 inhibits cell proliferation and induces ROS-mediated mitochondrial apoptosis. Regarding the association of ascorbate and juglone, several in vitro and in vivo studies have been conducted.

A strong decrease in T24 cell survival was induced by the association of ascorbate and juglone. The EC50 value at 24 h for juglone alone decreased from 28.5 μM to 6.3 μM in the presence of ascorbate, demonstrating a synergistic effect. The absence of caspase-3 activation and PARP cleavage, together with intense cell lysis morphology, suggests that juglone–ascorbate treatment may induce a non-apoptotic, necrosis-like phenotype rather than canonical apoptosis. In addition, DNA damage and a mild ER stress caused by juglone were both enhanced by ascorbate. Juglone slightly restricted motility, but cells lost their ability to migrate more markedly in the presence of ascorbate. Juglone causes a concentration-dependent decrease in cell proliferation. Ascorbate did not impair cell proliferation but its association with juglone led to a clonogenic death state [168].

The anticancer effects of juglone in combination with ascorbate were also evaluated in PANC-1 and BxPC-3 human pancreatic cancer cells. This combination decreased the expressions of antiapoptotic genes such as BCL2 and Birc5 (survivin) while the expression of the proapoptotic gene Bax was increased. In addition, apoptosis was increased, and Trx2 enzyme levels were decreased with all treatments in both cell lines [169].

The cytotoxic effects of both juglone and tamoxifen in the absence and presence of ascorbate, were investigated in MCF-7 cancer cells. Juglone and tamoxifen induced growth-inhibitory effects which were associated with lipid peroxidation and enhanced levels of ROS. Such effects were markedly increased in the presence of ascorbate. GSH, catalase, superoxide dismutase, and glutathione peroxidase significantly declined in cells exposed to combination treatments compared to cells incubated solely to tamoxifen, juglone, and the untreated control cells. In addition, the ascorbate-containing combination induced more apoptotic and necrotic, or necrotic-like, cell death than cells treated with each drug alone. Additionally, ascorbate activated ROS-responsive factors including Forkhead box O (FoxO) family members [170].

Ascorbate was used to investigate the antitumor effects of juglone, 2-(4-hydroxyaniline)-1,4-naphthoquinone (18a) and 2-(4-methoxyaniline)-1,4-naphthoquinone (18b) on DNA and cancer cells. These compounds intercalated into CT-DNA and induced oxidative cleavage, enhanced by ascorbate (1 mM). In MCF-7 cells, they caused DNA damage, reduced viability, increased ROS levels, phosphorylated H2AX, and inhibited protein kinase B (Akt/PKB) signaling, with stronger effects in combination with ascorbate. In vivo, juglone and compound 18a combined with ascorbate significantly inhibited Ehrlich tumor growth (up to 60%) and improved survival, whereas compound 18b showed no such potentiation [171].

The association of ascorbate and arylamino 1,4-naphthoquinones

Ascorbate-driven redox cycling of menadione (16) inhibits Hsp90 by inducing N-terminal cleavage [9,10,172]. Since Hsp90 stabilizes proteins essential for cancer cell survival, its disruption promotes proteasome degradation of client proteins [173]. 3-acyl-2-arylamino-1,4-naphthoquinones (17) were therefore evaluated with ascorbate, showing that their activity depends on redox properties and lipophilicity, influencing cytotoxicity in K562 cells. The protein cleavage was blocked by N-terminal Hsp90 inhibitors, suggesting that the availability or occupancy of the nucleotide-binding site plays a critical role. In addition, the survival of cancer cells and their metabolic and redox homeostasis were strongly impaired by the presence of ascorbate [174].

The effects induced by compounds 18a and 18b were further investigated with respect to cancer cell growth in the presence of ascorbate. These arylaminonaphthoquinones strongly inhibit the proliferation of T24 cells, but compound 18b was a better redox cycler than compound 18a, due to marked stabilization of the semiquinone radical species generated by its reduction via ascorbate. Compound 18b combined with ascorbate impairs the cell cycle, causing a decrease in the number of cells in the G2/M phase without involving other key cell cycle-regulating proteins. In addition, MAPK signaling pathway was affected, provoking the appearance of a senescent cancer cell phenotype and ultimately leading to necrotic-like cell death [175].

The association of ascorbate and miscellaneous quinones

Recent studies have explored redox-cycling “mitocans” targeting cancer cell mitochondria and disrupting energy homeostasis. Quinone/ascorbate pairs, particularly ascorbate/coenzyme Q0, showed strong anticancer effects across multiple cell lines and in vivo experiments using glioblastoma and colon cancer xenografts [178], as well as U87MG glioblastoma-bearing BALB/c nude mice [179]. These effects are attributed to enhanced mitochondrial superoxide production, accelerated redox cycling in dysfunctional mitochondria, and NOX downregulation. Interestingly, it has been shown that docosahexaenoic acid enhances the anticancer activity of the redox couple menadione/ascorbate, which also belongs to the “mitocans” family. The cytotoxicity and antiproliferative effects in Jurkat leukemic cells, were associated with increased mitochondrial superoxide production and accelerated ATP depletion [180]. Additionally, liposomal formulations combining thymoquinone and vitamin C demonstrated synergistic cytotoxicity in colorectal (HT29) and lung (A549) cancer cells [181].

The biological effects of mitoxantrone (compound 8), alone or combined with ascorbate, were investigated in MDA-MB231 and MCF7 human breast cancer cell lines. Mitoxantrone is a synthetic anti-cancer drug with significant clinical effectiveness in the treatment of human malignancies but with severe side effects. The combination of ascorbate and mitoxantrone induces apoptotic cell death, a mild G2/M elongation, H2AX phosphorylation, and mild PI3K activation [182].

A putative role of ascorbate and quercetin (a catechol-containing flavonoid) was investigated on Nrf2 activation, a pleiotropic transcription factor that may act as a sensor for electrophilic stress, thus regulating the intracellular antioxidant response [183]. A significant reduction in Nrf2 mRNA expression and protein levels, in addition to a significant reduction of glutathione peroxidases, glutathione reductase, and NQO1 enzymatic activity, was observed when prostatic PC3 cancer cells were exposed to ascorbate and quercetin [184].

Ascorbate-driven quinone redox cycling should not be viewed solely as a pro-oxidant cytotoxic strategy. Rather, it represents a tunable redox perturbation capable of shifting cancer cells across distinct death thresholds; depending on mitochondrial integrity, antioxidant capacity, iron availability, and proteostasis robustness. The emerging challenge is therefore not simply to increase ROS, but to understand the spatial and metabolic determinants that define whether oxidative stress culminates in apoptosis, ferroptosis, necrosis-like collapse, or mixed phenotypes.

It should be stressed that euryfuryl-substituted benzo- and naphthoquinones (10–14) were not included because, in contrast to classical redox-cycling quinones, they exhibit a less defined mechanistic profile. Although these compounds can induce significant metabolic perturbations, including ATP and glutathione depletion, their cytotoxicity is not consistently enhanced by ascorbate. Translational ranking of quinone partners for pharmacologic ascorbate

Based on the mechanistic considerations discussed above (Table 2), quinone scaffolds can be tentatively ranked according to their suitability as partners for pharmacologic ascorbate. Quinones with strong and well-characterized redox cycling capacity, particularly those efficiently bioactivated by NQO1, such as β-lapachone and menadione, emerge as the most promising candidates, as they sustain robust H2O2 generation and display selectivity toward cancer cells with altered antioxidant defenses. A second group includes dual-activity quinones such as plumbagin and shikonin, which combine redox cycling with electrophilic thiol reactivity; while highly potent, their broader chemical reactivity may lead to less predictable biological outcomes and a narrower therapeutic window. In contrast, structurally complex or heterocyclic quinones, such as arylamino and fused derivatives, tend to exhibit variable and context-dependent activities, often lacking a clearly predominant activation pathway or consistent redox behavior, thereby constraining their current translational potential.

Importantly, this classification should be viewed as context-dependent rather than definitive, as tumor-specific factors — including NQO1 expression, antioxidant capacity, iron availability, and metabolic status — are likely to critically modulate therapeutic outcomes. Nevertheless, this classification provides a rational basis for guiding the selection and further development of quinone partners in ascorbate-based anticancer strategies.

Clinical evidence and translational perspective of ascorbate–quinone systems

The clinical translation of ascorbate–quinone strategies remains at an early stage, and available evidence must be interpreted within a structured framework that distinguishes between direct, indirect, and historical data sources. To avoid conceptual ambiguity, clinical evidence can be organized into three categories: (i) studies directly evaluating defined quinone–ascorbate combinations, (ii) trials investigating pharmacologic ascorbate in oncology, and (iii) historical antioxidant mixture studies, which do not specifically address pro-oxidant redox cycling mechanisms.

Direct clinical evidence for ascorbate–quinone combinations

To date, clinical studies explicitly evaluating defined quinone–ascorbate combinations remain extremely limited. While several preclinical studies support the concept that redox-active quinones can amplify ascorbate-driven H2O2 production and induce tumor-selective cytotoxicity, these findings have not yet been systematically translated into controlled clinical trials.

In this context, existing reports involving quinone-containing regimens are often confounded by multicomponent formulations or lack appropriate mechanistic endpoints, making it difficult to attribute observed effects to redox cycling processes per se. For instance, some years ago two small pilot studies explored the combination of vitamin C and menadione in prostate cancer. In a first trial (n=20), patients received two cycles of 7-day oral courses of menadione (50 mg/m2/day) and ascorbic acid (5 g/m2/day), showing early changes in prostate-specific antigen (PSA) and homocysteine levels, including a transient PSA increase followed by a decrease [185]. In a second study, the formulation Apatone® (vitamin C - 5 g + menadione - 50 mg daily) was evaluated in a 12-week open-label phase I/IIa trial in cancer patients refractory to standard therapies. The reported changes in PSA kinetics should be interpreted cautiously, given the small sample size, open-label design, oral route of administration, and absence of randomized controls [186].

On the other hand, although it is not the main focus of this review, it should be noted that other combinations involving menadione, but not ascorbate, have also been evaluated in antitumor clinical trials [187,188]. This example may be regarded as a form of “proof of concept” supporting the pro-oxidant approach to anticancer therapy.

Therefore, these findings should be considered preliminary pharmacological observations rather than evidence of clinical efficacy. These data underscore the need for rigorously designed clinical studies incorporating pharmacodynamic biomarkers of oxidative and electrophilic stress.

Indirect clinical evidence from pharmacologic ascorbate trials

In contrast, a growing body of clinical research has evaluated high-dose intravenous (pharmacologic) ascorbate in cancer therapy. Pharmacokinetic studies have demonstrated that intravenous administration can achieve plasma concentrations in the millimolar range (up to ∼20 mM), whereas oral dosing remains limited to micromolar levels [189,190]. These pharmacologic concentrations are associated with extracellular hydrogen peroxide generation and have been proposed to exert selective cytotoxic effects in tumor tissues.

Recent randomized and controlled clinical studies provide a more accurate picture of the therapeutic potential of pharmacologic ascorbate. A randomized clinical trial (2024) in patients with metastatic pancreatic cancer reported that the addition of intravenous ascorbate to gemcitabine and nab-paclitaxel improved both overall survival and progression-free survival without increasing treatment-related toxicity [191]. In contrast, a phase II clinical trial (2024) in metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer found no significant clinical benefit when pharmacologic ascorbate was combined with docetaxel [192].

These recent findings are discussed in the PDQ® Cancer Information Summaries, and indicate that the clinical efficacy of pharmacologic ascorbate is highly context-dependent, likely influenced by tumor type, redox phenotype, metabolic state, and treatment combinations [[193], [194], [195]]. Importantly, these studies highlight that ascorbate alone does not uniformly produce antitumor effects and that rational combination strategies remain essential.

Historical antioxidant mixture studies

Earlier clinical studies combining vitamin C with antioxidants such as coenzyme Q10, selenium, and β-carotene have frequently been cited in the context of cancer therapy. However, these studies were typically non-randomized, heterogeneous, and not designed to test pro-oxidant mechanisms. For example, small pilot studies in end-stage cancer patients suggested improved survival outcomes with antioxidant mixtures [[196], [197], [198], [199]], but these interventions involved multiple compounds and lacked appropriate controls. Similarly, the Adjuvant Nutritional Intervention in Cancer (ANICA) study combined high-dose vitamins, fatty acids, and coenzyme Q10, making it impossible to attribute any observed effects to specific redox interactions [200]. An additional study evaluated the toxicity and activity of mitoxantrone plus ketoconazole and ascorbic acid at a dose of 250 mg in a cohort of 40 patients with hormone-refractory prostate cancer. The study concluded that mitoxantrone plus ketoconazole is well tolerated and showed promising antitumor effects, but no discussion about a putative role of ascorbic acid was done [201].

These historical studies should therefore be considered background context rather than mechanistic or translational evidence for ascorbate-driven quinone redox cycling. Importantly, antioxidant-based interventions may operate through mechanisms fundamentally distinct from the pro-oxidant strategy discussed in this review.

Translational considerations and future directions

Taken together, current clinical evidence suggests that pharmacologic ascorbate represents a viable platform for redox-based cancer therapy, but that its efficacy depends on appropriate biological context and rational combination strategies. The translation of ascorbate–quinone systems will require careful consideration of several unresolved variables, including:

•Tumor-specific redox phenotype and antioxidant capacity (e.g., catalase, GPx, thioredoxin systems)

Future clinical studies should therefore incorporate biomarker-driven patient stratification, standardized pharmacologic dosing of ascorbate, and mechanistically defined quinone partners. In addition, pharmacodynamic endpoints reflecting oxidative stress, thiol modification, and redox imbalance should be included to directly test the proposed mechanism of action.

Overall, while the clinical landscape remains preliminary, the combination of pharmacologic ascorbate with selected redox-active quinones represents a promising but still underexplored therapeutic strategy, requiring rigorous validation through well-controlled translational studies. Conclusions and perspectives

Data analyzed and discussed in this review indicate that combining pharmacologic concentrations of ascorbate with selected quinones represents a promising redox-based strategy to target cancer cells. Across diverse families of carbo- and heterocyclic quinones, a common mechanistic denominator emerges: ascorbate-driven quinone redox cycling amplifies intracellular H2O2 and ROS production beyond the buffering capacity of tumor cells, thereby exploiting their intrinsic redox imbalance.

Mechanistically, three major, often interconnected, processes can be distinguished:

(i)Induction of oxidative stress and cell death: Ascorbate/quinone pairs markedly increase ROS levels, overwhelming antioxidant defenses such as catalase, GSH, and superoxide dismutases. This oxidative imbalance causes mitochondrial dysfunction, lipid peroxidation, and DNA damage, thereby activating programmed cell death pathways, including apoptosis or apoptosis-like mechanisms. In cases where antioxidant capacity is profoundly impaired, necrotic cell death may also occur.

(ii)Disruption of redox-sensitive proteins and survival pathways: The quinone–ascorbate association impairs cancer cell growth by targeting and damaging redox-sensitive proteins and key survival enzymes, including thioredoxin reductase and crucial metabolic regulators. In certain cases, these effects extend to intracellular targets, for instance: disruption of the chaperone activity of Hsp90, leading to degradation of its client proteins; interference with critical signaling pathways such as PI3K/mTOR and Keap1/Nrf2; impairment of mitochondrial energy production; and, in some models, may engage nucleases and non-classical forms of cell death. Although some studies have described such morphological patterns as autoschizis, this entity remains mechanistically debated and is not formally recognized by the NCCD. These observations are therefore more appropriately interpreted as non-canonical, oxidative stress-driven cell death phenotypes rather than a distinct regulated cell death modality.

We acknowledge that several of the more recent publications included in this field primarily employed relatively basic assessment methods, such as viability assays (such as MTT) and general ROS measurements, which may partly explain the variability and limited mechanistic insight across studies. In that context, future work should aim to refine the selection of quinone partners (taking into account redox potential, lipophilicity, and enzyme selectivity), identify both the source and specific types of ROS generated, decipher the mechanisms underlying ascorbate/quinone-induced cell death, better define biomarkers predictive of response (e.g., NQO1/Nrf2 status, antioxidant capacity, mitochondrial function) and optimize dosing and delivery strategies, including tumor-targeted formulations. Importantly, the favorable safety profile of high-dose ascorbate and the chemical versatility of quinones provide a promising framework for translational development, provided that patient stratification and redox-related biomarkers are adequately considered. Well-designed clinical trials will be essential to determine whether the robust preclinical efficacy of ascorbate-driven quinone redox cycling can be translated into safe and effective therapeutic regimens for cancer patients.
