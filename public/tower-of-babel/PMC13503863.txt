Tunable yielding and emergent rheology in amorphous solids with active particle doping
Authors: Rashmi Priya, Jürgen Horbach, Smarajit Karmakar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mechanically stable glasses, notably well-annealed and ultrastable ones, typically exhibit brittle failure through shear-band formation. Whether this localization is an inevitable consequence of annealing or can be dynamically controlled remains unknown. We address this by doping amorphous solids with self-propelled particles that perform run-and-tumble or active Brownian dynamics. Through extensive simulations of a polydisperse model, we show that shear-band formation and propagation are governed by a competition among three timescales: the imposed shear time \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1/\dot{\gamma }$$\end{document}1/γ°, the activity persistence time τp, and the intrinsic time for a shear band to span the system. At low persistence time, active doping progressively converts a single system-spanning shear band into a network of shear bands, yielding continuously with increased yield stress and yield strain, and inter-band spacing that decreases as a power law with active force. Interestingly, we uncover a universal compensatory relationship between active forces and global shear rates: a rise in one offsets a decline in the other, arising from isomorphic-like behavior across force–strain-rate combinations. We further identify a non-monotonic relationship between yielding and persistence time: while the yield stress increases at small τp with increasing active forces, it tends to decrease at large τp. Under creep, increasing active force reduces the steady-state strain rate and delays fluidization, extending the compensation to the stress-controlled regime. These results establish that shear localization in brittle glasses is not dictated solely by preparation history but can be dynamically altered through internal activity, with broad implications for yielding in living and synthetic active matter.

Introduction

Glasses are nonequilibrium solids whose mechanical response remains a central problem in condensed-matter physics because it depends sensitively on preparation history and loading conditions1–6. Annealing protocol, system size and confining geometry, equilibration (or parent) temperature, interparticle interactions, shear temperature, and strain rate all influence the failure mode in glasses7–11. Well-annealed, larger systems typically fail in a brittle manner at low strain rates, whereas poorly annealed, smaller systems often exhibit a more ductile response. The same brittle response can cross over to more homogeneous flow at sufficiently high strain rates11,12, suggesting that brittleness is not a fixed material property but reflects a dynamical competition between the rate of strain localization and the rate at which strain is imposed.

Ultrastable glasses, typically prepared in silico via swap Monte Carlo13–16 or experimentally via physical vapor deposition17,18, are extreme realizations of stable glasses: they reside exceptionally deep in the energy landscape and fail in an especially brittle manner19–22. They therefore serve as a stringent test case for any proposed mechanism of localization control. Additional strategies to approach well-annealed/ultrastable states include cyclic shear, activity-induced annealing, replica-exchange Monte Carlo, random particle bonding and related methods4,23–29. Recent findings of close similarity in the responses of amorphous solids to oscillatory shear deformation and active forcing24,25 naturally raise the question of how activity microscopically affects the yielding process itself under other deformation protocols, such as simple shear.

Active particles are self-propelled particles (SPPs) and are driven by internal energy that keeps the system out of equilibrium30–36. They have been shown to fluidize dense glassy states by breaking cages above a critical force37, and subsequent work has established that activity and shear can produce equivalent mechanical responses through shared microscopic mechanisms24,38–40. Active doping has also been shown to control the brittle-to-ductile transition in dense colloidal gels41. A recent study42 on steady-state rheology addresses the importance of active fraction in a mixture of active-passive Brownian particles. These results collectively suggest that activity is not merely an effective temperature but a qualitatively distinct control parameter whose interplay with shear deserves direct investigation. However, prior studies have primarily examined shear and activity separately; research on the regime where both drives act simultaneously remains scarce. The key open problem is to understand how the coupling of active driving and shear deformation influences the yielding mechanism and the associated rheological properties across glasses with different annealing histories.

More broadly, yielding and shear-band formation are also known to depend on modifications of the internal properties of glassy states10,43–50, including point-like or rod-like inclusions, random particle bonding. Related work has characterized delayed yielding and stress overshoot in these systems27,29,51,52. Our work is distinct: rather than altering the static structure, we introduce a dynamic drive whose effect on yielding depends on the interplay of timescales.

Brittleness is widespread; we therefore first focus on ultrastable glasses, which are especially brittle and typically fail catastrophically via a single, system-spanning shear band. To test generality, we then repeat the analyses for well-annealed glasses prepared at higher parent temperatures. We perform the primary tests using run-and-tumble particles (RTPs) and subsequently reproduce the key results with active Brownian particles (ABPs), demonstrating robustness to the choice of self-propulsion model. Our results reveal that active dopants can enhance the mechanical stability of brittle glasses, as reflected in an increased overshoot in the stress response at yield without compromising the shear modulus. In contrast to the passive case, where yielding produces a sharp stress drop and a single system-spanning shear band, active doping leads to continuous yielding and a network of shear bands whose spacing decreases with f0.

The major conceptual developments made in this work are two-fold. The first one is to show that shear-band formation and propagation should be understood as a dynamical process governed by competing timescales: the imposed shear rate, the persistence time of the active drive, and the intrinsic timescale for shear localization to traverse the system, rather than as a purely structural or preparation-controlled phenomenon. The second is to discover a universal coupling between strain rate and active driving, resulting in a single parameter that controls the rheological responses of active solids in a unified manner. This unique control parameter describes the physics of shear deformation in active amorphous solids, irrespective of their preparation protocols or the nature of the imposed deformation. Thus, the rheological properties of a wide variety of amorphous solids, ranging from colloidal and molecular glasses to biological solids, in the presence of activity can be understood within a single framework.

In the low-τp regime, active forces and strain rate jointly redistribute plastic strain into a network of multiple shear bands before any single band can span the system. A compensatory relationship emerges: strain rate and active force enter the yield stress, steady-state stress, and inter-band spacing through a multiplicative combination \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\dot{\gamma }{f}_{0}^{m}$$\end{document}γ°f0m, and matched pairs produce identical inherent-structure energies, suggesting that the two drives access the same region of the energy landscape. This competing-timescale picture extends beyond constant-strain-rate loading: under creep at fixed applied stress53–56, increasing active force reduces the steady-state strain rate and delays fluidization, with the same parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\dot{\gamma }{f}_{0}^{m}$$\end{document}γ°f0m governing the flow regime universally. This confirms that the compensation is a general feature of the yielding dynamics rather than an artifact due to strain-controlled deformation. Contrary to the effective-temperature expectation, we find that at low τp activity shows non-temperature-like behavior, strengthening the system rather than fluidizing it, while the expected fluidization recovers at higher τp. Taken together, these results establish shear-band formation as a kinetic phenomenon whose morphology is controlled by the relative timing of shear, activity, and intrinsic relaxation, and demonstrate that preparation history sets only the baseline brittleness on which this kinetic competition acts.

The subsequent stress drop is no longer abrupt, unlike in the passive case at the same shear rate. A more gradual stress drop indicates a progressive failure with multiple stress relaxation events occurring simultaneously, resulting in more ductile-like behavior. The increase in yield stress occurs alongside a slight increase in the slope of the linear regime of the stress-strain curve, suggesting that adding activity raises the overall stress of the system. The steady-state stress at a fixed strain rate also rises. This is a counterintuitive observation, given that activity is often considered analogous to an effective temperature37,40. It is well-documented that increasing the temperature in a thermal passive system reduces both the peak stress and the steady-state stress. However, with increasing activity, the yield stress increases when the persistence time is sufficiently small. At a larger persistence time, the concept of an effective temperature seems to be more appropriate, as discussed later.

The formation of multiple shear bands occurs because high strain rates inhibit the propagation of a single system-spanning shear band, as the deformation occurs faster than the shear band propagation time, leading to independent shear bands forming at different sites11. When active particles are added to such a system, they enhance this effect, creating additional hindrance to shear band propagation and promoting the formation of multiple shear bands in response to external stress.

Robustness to Propulsion Statistics via Active Brownian Particle Doping

Many synthetic active-matter systems exhibit active Brownian particle (ABP) dynamics, providing an experimental testbed for our simulations. We therefore perform Brownian-dynamics simulations with 5% of the particles active, each with self-propulsion force f0 and persistence time τp. Unlike run-and-tumble propulsion (RTP), ABP’s orientations decorrelate gradually via rotational diffusion, which motivates testing whether our conclusions persist under these propulsion statistics. We shear the system as earlier and observe the stress-strain response. Figure 5e, f show the stress-strain response for glasses prepared at Tp = 0.026 (N = 10, 000) and Tp = 0.07 (N = 64, 000), with damping chosen to maintain the target temperature under shear (curves are averaged over 16 ensembles). At low persistence (τp = 5), increasing f0 produces a clear stress overshoot relative to the passive reference with a rounded, more continuous yielding. Thus, to harness enhanced mechanical stability while retaining continuous yielding, the low-persistence regime is optimal.

The shear-band fields in Fig. 5e, f, shown at γ = 0.20, mirror the RTP trends, where the passive system forms a single, system-spanning band, whereas ABP-doped ones yield multiple networked shear bands at τp = 5 and f0 = 2.0. Reproducing the effect with ABPs confirms that the suppression of brittle-like failure is not model-specific but reflects physics independent of the self-propulsion model, strengthening the possibility of experimental verification of our results in synthetic colloidal glasses.

Dependence of Shear Band Length Scale on Active Forces

The observation of multiple shear bands with increasing f0 for Tp = 0.026 suggests the emergence of an underlying length scale (ξ) that is related to the finite inter-shear band distance due to active forces. In ref. 11, an interesting relationship between such a lengthscale and the shear rate has been proposed for passive systems. Here, we show that in the presence of active forces, a similar relationship holds. To quantify this relationship, we first create a binary image of the shear-banded system following the same procedure as above for the calculations of the volume fraction of shear-banded regions. As in refs. 11,61, we draw horizontal and vertical lines to measure chord lengths between consecutive shear bands (see Supplementary Fig. 7 for details). After averaging the first moment of distance data across all ensembles, the evolution of ξ/L with strain rate for both passive and active cases is shown in Fig. 6g. Note that a scaling regime for ξ/L can be only expected if two conditions are met. On the one hand, the length scale ξ cannot grow more than the linear dimension L of the system, which implies ξ/L ≪ 1. On the other hand, ξ cannot decrease below a microscopic scale a which is of the order of the nearest-neighbor distance between particles. Thus, the second condition is ξ/a ≫ 1. As can be inferred from Fig. 6(g), for L = 100, the range of a possible scaling regime is roughly given by 0.3 > ξ/L > 0.05. In this range, ξ as a function of strain rate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\dot{\gamma }$$\end{document}γ° seems to decay in a power-law manner for the considered values of f0, albeit the regime of this decay covers less than one order of magnitude. To significantly increase this regime, one would have to consider much larger system sizes, which is beyond the scope of this work.

However, drawing on prior work on passive amorphous solids62–64, we can estimate the expected power-law exponent. In ref. 11 it was argued that the separation between shear bands decreases with increasing strain rate. When the time required for a shear band to span a system of length (L) exceeds the shear-rate timescale (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1/\dot{\gamma }$$\end{document}1/γ°), multiple shear bands appear. This explains the single, system-spanning band in AQS and the emergence of multiple bands at finite (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\dot{\gamma }$$\end{document}γ°). With active doping, an analogous criterion holds, but the control variable is (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\dot{\gamma }{f}_{0}^{m}$$\end{document}γ°f0m): activity introduces an additional timescale via the persistence time, which inhibits system-spanning propagation and promotes a multi-band network. The separation length ξ/L provides insight into the system’s ductility. At a very large separation length, the system can be interpreted as being brittle, while a decrease in separation length indicates increasing ductility. This supports the conclusion that activity enhances the ductility of the system. This macroscopic decrease in inter-band spacing is consistent with the reduction in the microscopic correlation length ξc extracted from C(r) (Fig. 2d), confirming that activity suppresses spatial cooperativity at both scales. Note that this equivalence between strain rate and activity is valid only in the small τp regime, and an effective temperature-like description starts to emerge at larger τp. Thus, the observed phenomenon of multiple shear-band formation cannot simply be explained as a higher-strain-rate effect, as the same effect is not found in the larger-τp regime.

This scaling and data collapse reveals a fundamental description of the mechanical response of the system where the product \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\dot{\gamma }{f}_{0}^{m}$$\end{document}γ°f0m serves as a scaling parameter. This implies that the system’s mechanical response to deformation is inherently linked to the activity.

This is a significant result, as it demonstrates that the compensatory effect of global shear and activity can be effectively utilized to rejuvenate well-annealed glass to the same parent temperature. For instance, in scenarios where it is difficult to control external shear (e.g., confined geometries or in certain experimental setups), one can tune internal activity to achieve the same rejuvenation and mechanical response. Conversely, if controlling activity is challenging (e.g., in systems with limited active particle concentration), adjusting the external shear rate can produce equivalent effects. In addition, the isomorphic behavior at small τp indicates a much more intricate coupling between globally imposed shear deformation and the local active forces. The origin of these matching responses down to the inherent structure throughout the entire strain evolution, remains to be explained theoretically.

Compensatory behavior under creep loading

We have demonstrated how activity introduces a timescale that competes with the timescales relevant for shear-band formation. Now, we analyze whether a similar scenario also holds for creep failure in 2D, where a constant stress is imposed. Unlike the constant shear-rate protocol, the system selects its own fluidization timescale τss under constant-stress shear53. The preparation of samples and the details of the creep protocol are described in the Methods section.

In the constant-stress protocol, the strain γ is tracked as a function of time t. This results in more homogeneous plastic drops, facilitating easier flow and lower yield stresses, which ultimately lead to fluidization.

Discussion

This study advances and adds another dimension to our understanding of a fundamentally challenging problem of heterogeneous, shear-band-mediated failure in brittle glasses, including ultrastable ones by investigating yielding behavior and shear band formation in the presence of active forces. We demonstrate that incorporating SPPs at finite temperatures and strain rates can enhance the mechanical stability of brittle glasses under homogeneous shear conditions. This enhancement is quantitatively characterized by a significant increase in yield stress and yield strain of the system, and persists down to low dopant concentrations, with the stress response growing systematically with doping fraction (Supplementary Fig. 14). We also report multiple shear band formations with an increase in the magnitude of active forces at a fixed shear rate whose inter-band spacing decreases with f0, and their gradual development accounts for the continuous yielding behavior observed in the stress-strain curve. The distinct roles of activity and shear in sustaining this band network are further disentangled by a switching protocol (Supplementary Fig. 15).

A recent study on a mechanical metamaterial66 demonstrates an increase in both stiffness and stretchability, along with substantial energy dissipation through progressive fracture under large deformation. This is achieved by distributing defects in a checkerboard pattern that can be seen as an analog to the emerging shear band network in our system with a doping of active particles. Analogous responses are seen in biological tissues, where epithelial and connective tissues can tune their activity to withstand loads. In particular, cells increase their contractility and reorganize their cytoskeletons, thereby increasing resistance to deformation and strengthening the tissue67,68. The observation of the progressive failure mechanism post-yield point has significant implications for future research.

Furthermore, our analysis shows that strain rate and active forces exhibit a complementary relationship, implying that a faster strain rate with low f0 is equivalent to a slower strain rate with high f0. This compensation arises from the isomorphic nature of a pair of combinations of strain rate and active force that rejuvenate the brittle glass to the same inherent state. In situations where external shear is challenging to control, internal activity can be adjusted instead; conversely, when tuning activity is constrained, modifying the external shear rate can achieve the same effect.

The compensatory picture extends beyond strain-rate control loading: under creep at fixed applied stress, increasing the active force reduces the steady-state strain rate and delays fluidization, with the fluidization timescale τss diverging as the applied stress approaches σY from above and shifting to higher stresses with increasing f0. The flow regime collapses under the same multiplicative variable \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\dot{\gamma }{f}_{0}^{m}$$\end{document}γ°f0m, and the scaled steady-state stress from creep falls on the same trend as the scaled strain-driven yield stress, confirming that the applied stress alone does not uniquely determine the flow state; rather, the system admits a family of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(\dot{\gamma },{f}_{0})$$\end{document}(γ°,f0) pairs that access the same basin of the energy landscape and yield identical mechanical responses. The fact that this degeneracy persists under imposed stress, where the system freely selects its own strain rate, rules out the possibility that the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\dot{\gamma }$$\end{document}γ°–f0 compensation is an artifact of externally imposed kinematics, suggesting that the isomorphism should be directly testable in creep experiments on active-doped colloidal glasses. Moreover, the emergent length scale in the system in the presence of activity and strain rate can be very well understood using the concepts of passive amorphous solids with a simple replacement of the control parameter from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\dot{\gamma }$$\end{document}γ° to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\dot{\gamma }{f}_{0}^{m}$$\end{document}γ°f0m. This indicates that the emergent rheological properties of active solids can be described by a single parameter, thereby simplifying the interpretation of experimental results in active matter, including biological systems.

In biological scenarios, it may be more feasible for a system to regulate its internal activity rather than control external forces acting on it. Cytoskeletal networks show that myosin-generated prestress can produce the same network stiffness as externally imposed shear69,70, providing a direct biological analog of the compensatory relationship identified here. The complementary relationship between shear rate and active forces thus suggests new directions for exploring yielding in biological systems under mechanical load. We believe that our findings, especially this strong coupling of strain-rate and activity, will have important implications for designing smart materials in the near future.

The interplay between persistence time and the system’s intrinsic timescale emerges as another critical factor, with particles moving freely before experiencing caging effects. Shorter persistence times lead to an earlier onset of the caged regime, which results in a longer caged time and enhanced mechanical stability, as evident from pronounced stress peaks. Although this enhancement of mechanical stability does not lead to stronger strain localization, it initiates multiple shear localization zones, forming multiple shear bands as strain increases. The distance between shear bands decreases with increasing active forces, indicating that the system becomes more ductile by introducing active dopants at lower persistence times. At higher persistence times, on the other hand, the particles escape their cages, and as the active forces increase, this cage-breaking event is enhanced, leading the system to liquefy as f0 increases further. This behavior is evident in both stress-strain curves and shear band structures with increasing active forces. The two τp regimes thus exhibit opposing behaviors, demarcating the operating window in which activity acts as a kinetic competitor to shear rather than as an effective temperature.

Our analysis of the SOP aimed to identify soft zones (high-SOP regions) within elastically stable zones shows that before yielding, the SOP field is spatially uniform in both passive and active-doped systems. However, as the yield point approaches in passive systems, the soft (high-SOP) regions transition from homogeneous to heterogeneous, concentrating along the shear band. In contrast, the active-doped systems display a soft zone pattern that facilitates the development of a branched shear band network after yielding. Previous studies71,72 have demonstrated that introducing a soft zone in an ultra-stable glass matrix can nucleate a shear band at that specific location, allowing for controlled shear band formation. Even though the active particles are evenly distributed throughout the sample, they create a soft zone pattern that encourages the formation of a branched shear band network.

Finally, regarding experimental relevance, our results hold for both RTPs and ABPs, indicating that the effect is robust to propulsion statistics and reflects a general physical mechanism. Active–passive mixtures with Janus particles in dense colloidal systems exhibit activity-controlled rheology73–76. We highlight recent work on colloidal gels76, in which photosensitive particles are used as dopants. These particles become active under external light and undergo run-and-tumble motion. These experimental results highlight that the incorporation of photoactive particles significantly influences the mechanical properties of active gels, particularly during annealing. Photoisomerization of azobenzene inside vapor-deposited molecular glasses, used to modulate photostability/annealing, further shows that light-driven molecular reconfiguration can occur within PVD matrices77. Whether the competing-timescale mechanism identified here can be exploited in practice, for example, through light-activated dopants in sheared molecular glasses, remains an open question, but for dense colloidal glasses, the experiments already proved its feasibility. Progress will require systems in which internal activity can be introduced and controlled under mechanical loading within dense amorphous matrices.

This study demonstrates that strain localization in brittle glasses is not an inevitable outcome of deep annealing but is instead a dynamic process governed by competing internal and external factors. In addition, the findings reveal a complex coupling between the imposed shear rate and activity, which gives rise to emergent rheological properties in active solids. These properties are regulated by a unique parameter that integrates both strain rate and activity.

Sample preparation

The system was equilibrated at a constant temperature of Tp = 0.06 for the 3D model and Tp = 0.026 for the 2D model. In this method, a swap attempt was made every 25 MD steps with N trial SMC moves, ensuring that the probability of swap attempts remains independent of system size. Simulations were performed under periodic boundary conditions with a time step of dt = 0.005 and a number density of ρ = 1.0. This density corresponds to a simulation box length of L = 100 in 2D for N = 10, 000 and approximately L ≈ 46.42 in 3D for N = 100, 000. Unless otherwise specified, all 3D results are reported for systems with N = 100, 000 particles (averaged over eight ensembles) and N = 2000 particles (averaged over 32 ensembles), while 2D results are reported for N = 10, 000 particles (averaged over 16 ensembles). We prepared additional samples of well-annealed glasses for RTP and ABP simulations. Systems of size N = 64, 000 were equilibrated at Tp = 0.035, 0.05, and 0.07 using the hybrid MD–SMC protocol described above. For each state, we generated 16 independent samples and report ensemble-averaged results.

The volume fraction is defined as the ratio of the volume or area (in 3D or 2D, respectively) occupied by the shear band to the total volume or total area. Calculation of structure order parameter (SOP)

SOP is a measure of how much the arrangement of local particles deviates from their closest-packed configuration, indicating how fluid or solid-like the system is ref. 83. When the value of SOP is large, it means the arrangement deviates significantly from the close-packed state, suggesting a more fluid-like behavior. To calculate the SOP for a particle o, its neighbors are calculated using Voronoi tessellation. For each neighboring pair of particles i, j, a triangular structure oij is formed.
