Balloon tamponade for central venous catheter–related perforations: A minimally invasive, vessel-sparing case series
Authors: Javad Jalili, Sarah Vaseghi, Mahdiyeh Baastani Khajeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveIatrogenic central venous injuries are rare but potentially catastrophic complications of catheter-based interventions. Management options remain limited, with surgical repair often infeasible in high-risk patients and covered stents associated with infection risk and permanent loss of access. Although balloon tamponade has been reported anecdotally in peripheral and arterial injuries, its role in central venous catheter–related perforations has not been systematically studied. This case series addresses this gap by evaluating balloon tamponade as a minimally invasive, vessel-preserving strategy for central venous injuries.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed 7 consecutive cases of superior vena cava, brachiocephalic vein, or common iliac vein perforations managed with fluoroscopy-guided balloon tamponade (2021-2025). Procedural details, immediate hemostasis, complications, and long-term outcomes were assessed over 6 to 12 months of follow-up.ResultsBalloon tamponade achieved complete hemostasis in 6 of 7 patients, avoiding surgical intervention and preserving vascular access. One case was managed conservatively without balloon inflation. No re-bleeding or long-term complications occurred during follow-up. All patients maintained stable vascular access and experienced full clinical recovery.ConclusionsThis case series represents one of the first structured evaluations of balloon tamponade for central venous catheter–related perforations, demonstrating its feasibility, safety, and durable outcomes. By providing vessel-sparing hemostasis without the need for surgical or stent-based intervention, balloon tamponade offers a promising minimally invasive strategy for high-risk patients. These findings highlight its potential role in future management algorithms and underscore the need for prospective studies to standardize the technique and validate its broader application.

Central venous catheterization is a cornerstone of care for patients requiring hemodialysis, chemotherapy, long-term parenteral nutrition, or critical care access.1 Despite advances in procedural guidance and ultrasound-assisted techniques, iatrogenic vascular injuries remain a serious and sometimes under-recognized complication, particularly in patients with multiple prior central venous interventions. These injuries, including venous wall perforations and contrast extravasation, can result in catastrophic outcomes such as hemothorax, mediastinal hematoma, cardiac tamponade, and death. The challenge is especially acute in patients with end-stage renal disease (ESRD), whose limited vascular access options and fragile vasculature significantly elevate procedural risks.1,2

Although open surgical repair historically has been the standard approach for central venous perforations, it is often not feasible in critically ill or dialysis-dependent patients.3 Endovascular alternatives such as covered stent placement may offer less invasive solutions but pose challenges in terms of anatomic suitability, infection risk, and future access preservation.4,5 In this context, balloon tamponade, a technique involving temporary inflation of a balloon catheter to compress the injury site and achieve hemostasis, has emerged as a promising intervention.6, 7, 8 Although prior literature has demonstrated its efficacy in arterial and peripheral venous injuries, its application in central venous structures such as the superior vena cava (SVC), brachiocephalic vein (BCV), and common iliac vein (CIV) remains limited to isolated case reports.9 No structured series to date has systematically evaluated the technical performance, safety profile, and follow-up outcomes of balloon tamponade in this setting.

This case series provides preliminary insight by presenting 7 cases of iatrogenic central venous catheter–related injury successfully managed with balloon tamponade. We detail procedural techniques, decision-making rationales, and both short- and long-term outcomes, demonstrating the feasibility and durability of this approach in a variety of complex clinical contexts. We hypothesize that balloon tamponade can achieve rapid hemostasis and preserve vascular integrity while avoiding the need for surgical intervention or permanent vascular exclusion. By sharing practical observations that may inform future frameworks for the endovascular management of venous perforations, this study aims to inform interventional practice, advocate for vessel-preserving strategies in high-risk patients, and lay the foundation for future guideline development in managing catheter-related vascular trauma.

Case Series

This retrospective descriptive case series includes 7 consecutive patients with iatrogenic central venous catheter–related perforations managed with balloon tamponade at Imam Reza Hospital, Tabriz, Iran, between March 2021 and February 2025. The initial application of balloon tamponade was performed in Case 1 based on emergency clinical judgment, guided by the interventional team's expertise and familiarity with similar techniques. Because of the favorable outcome, a similar procedural approach was adopted for subsequent patients. At the time of treatment, there were no institutional, national, or international guidelines specifically recommending balloon tamponade for central venous injuries.

No eligible cases were excluded during the study period, and no unsuccessful attempts at balloon tamponade were recorded.

All patients underwent diagnostic venography after suspected vascular injury. Balloon tamponade was reserved for patients demonstrating active contrast extravasation, suggesting ongoing venous bleeding. The decision to proceed with balloon tamponade was made by the interventional radiology team based on venographic findings, anatomic feasibility, procedural accessibility, and the overall clinical condition of the patient. In contrast, patients without active extravasation and without clinical evidence of ongoing hemorrhage were considered for conservative management with close monitoring. One patient in the present series was managed conservatively on this basis. In every case, the cardiothoracic surgery team was consulted preprocedure to ensure readiness for surgical repair, and covered stents were available as backup. This multidisciplinary approach was applied uniformly after the first successful case.

All procedures were performed in the Vascular and Interventional Radiology suite under fluoroscopic guidance by an experienced interventional radiologist (J.J.). Access was obtained through the right or left common femoral veins (R/L CFVs), depending on anatomic feasibility. After contrast venography was used to localize the site of perforation or penetration, an appropriately sized balloon dilation catheter (eg, Conquest 40, Atlas Gold; 8-14-mm diameter, 40-60 mm length) was advanced across the injury site. Although no predefined protocol guided the initial interventions, balloon sizing and inflation parameters were refined iteratively based on early experience. Balloon selection was based on preprocedural computed tomography (CT) or venographic measurements, with oversizing limited to approximately 10% of the target vessel diameter. Balloons were typically inflated to pressures between 6 and 10 atm for 15 minutes (exceptionally 30 minutes in Case 2), depending on the extent of extravasation and hemodynamic stability. Continuous intraprocedural hemodynamic monitoring was performed during balloon inflation in all cases. All inflations were performed under fluoroscopic monitoring to ensure proper positioning and avoid overexpansion. Hemostasis was assessed via control venography at 15 and 30 minutes postdeflation.

Systemic anticoagulation was not routinely administered postprocedure due to the acute vascular injury context and concern for re-bleeding in the setting of central venous perforation.

All patients underwent structured follow-up for 6 to 12 months at intervals of 1 to 3 months. Follow-up included clinical assessment, vascular access evaluation, and imaging (eg, duplex ultrasound) when indicated. Advanced imaging with CT venography was not routinely performed and was reserved for complex cases requiring further anatomic evaluation; Case 2 underwent CT venography during follow-up due to the severity of the initial SVC injury and associated complications.•Primary outcome: immediate technical success, defined as cessation of extravasation without need for surgery or stent•Secondary outcomes: complication-free survival (no re-bleeding, vascular access loss, thrombosis, or repeat intervention) and long-term vascular access preservation

Case 1

A 35-year-old female patient with right breast cancer underwent port catheter placement for chemotherapy. Four days after the catheter placement, she underwent chemotherapy. However, shortly after the initiation of the chemotherapy, the patient developed severe dyspnea, chest pain, and a decrease in oxygen saturation to 80%. Initial evaluation with CT imaging revealed that the catheter had penetrated the wall of the right brachiocephalic vein (RBCV) and entered the right pleural space. Therefore, the patient was promptly transferred to the Interventional Radiology Unit. As an emergency measure, a right pleural catheter was placed. Under fluoroscopic guidance, access was obtained via the right common femoral vein (RCFV). After reaching the perforation site in the RBCV, the tip of the port catheter was removed, and the CONQUEST 40 balloon dilation catheter (11 × 40 mm) was immediately inflated at the site. After 15 minutes of inflation, the balloon was deflated and venography revealed no contrast media extravasation. After 30 minutes, a control venography was performed, which revealed no evidence of bleeding. A new port catheter was inserted under ultrasound and fluoroscopic guidance 2 days later, and the patient received a successful session of chemotherapy (Figure 1). Case 3

An 85-year-old female patient with a history of hypertension, ESRD, and kidney stones, who has been undergoing dialysis 3 times a week for the past 10 years, was admitted for emergency dialysis due to elevated creatinine levels up to 18, metabolic acidosis, and dialysis catheter dysfunction. A permanent dialysis catheter was placed via the right internal jugular vein (RIJV) by the surgical team. After 3 sessions of hemodialysis (6 days after catheter insertion), the catheter lost its function. A CT scan was performed to assess the patency of the SVC, and the RBCV showed that the catheter was located within the pleura. Additionally, a moderate right pleural effusion with collapse of the right lower lung was reported. The patient underwent venography of the SVC, BCVs, SCVs, and the RIJV. The venography revealed that the catheter had entered the right pleural space through the posterior wall of the proximal part of the RBCV. Initially, the puncture site was packed by inflating an ATLAS GOLD (14 × 40 mm) balloon catheter from BARD. After that, the position of the initial catheter was corrected. Three days later, due to pleural effusion, a pleural catheter was placed under ultrasound guidance, which had a bloody output (Figure 3). After 2 days, further evaluation under ultrasound guidance revealed that the pleural effusion had clearly resolved, and the pleural catheter was removed. Case 5

A 33-year-old male patient, who had undergone bilateral ureteroscopy, placement of a left double-J stent, and insertion of a temporary dialysis catheter via the RIJV 2 weeks before this admission due to bilateral hydronephrosis, was referred to the Interventional service for catheter removal due to the atypical location of the dialysis catheter. Case 6

A 42-year-old male patient with no prior medical history was admitted to the emergency department with generalized tonic-clonic seizures. He presented with disorientation and decreased level of consciousness after flu-like symptoms. Initial laboratory evaluation revealed elevated creatine kinase (2525 U/L), elevated serum creatinine (6.97 mg/dL), and impaired liver function test results. With a presumptive diagnosis of acute tubular necrosis secondary to rhabdomyolysis, the patient was scheduled for temporary right femoral vein catheter placement and subsequently underwent an emergency hemodialysis session. However, 2 days after catheter insertion, the patient developed bilateral flank ecchymosis. Abdominal ultrasonography was performed to rule out retroperitoneal hematoma, which demonstrated a small amount of free fluid in Morrison's pouch and right perinephric space. Consequently, a surgical consultation was requested, and a RIJV catheter was placed. The surgical team recommended daily evaluation of the catheter insertion site for 7 days and further assessment regarding catheter removal.

After 5 days, a CT scan revealed that the tip of the right femoral catheter had migrated outside the right common iliac vein (RCIV) and into the retroperitoneal space. The patient was referred to the interventional radiology service and scheduled for venography of the RCIV. On day 13 postinsertion, venography of the RCIV and inferior vena cava was performed, which demonstrated extravasation from the posterior wall of the RCIV due to a malpositioned catheter. Thus, an ATLAS GOLD (14 × 60 mm) balloon catheter from BARD was inflated at the site where the catheter entered the wall of the RCIV. Immediate control of the bleeding and tamponading of the perforation site in the RCIV was successfully performed after balloon inflation, and the RCFV dialysis catheter was removed. After 30 minutes, control venography revealed no signs of contrast media extravasation (Figure 6). The patient was discharged on the second postprocedure day. Case 7

A 51-year-old female patient with a history of hypertension, seizures, ESRD, and leukocytoclastic vasculitis has been undergoing dialysis 3 times a week for the past 2 years. The patient has a history of multiple previous catheter dysfunctions and the placement of several catheters in central veins. The patient had a temporary LIJV catheter placed, which became dysfunctional 8 days after placement. A venography of the LIJV indicated that the dialysis catheter had shifted into the azygos vein, entering the venous wall. After reaching the perforation site through RCFV access, the tip of the catheter was gently removed from the vessel wall. No bleeding was seen immediately after the correction of the malposition of the tip of the catheter; thus, there was no need for balloon inflation at the perforation site, and the patient was stable (Figure E1). Thereafter, she underwent hemodialysis according to her treatment protocol and was discharged on the sixth day postprocedure. During the 1-year follow-up, the patient remained symptom-free and underwent regular thrice-weekly dialysis without specific complaints.

Discussion

This case series demonstrates that balloon tamponade can achieve rapid hemostasis and preserve central venous integrity in patients with central catheter-related perforations involving the SVC, BCVs, and CIVs. Technical success was achieved in all 7 patients, with hemostasis confirmed in 6 cases, whereas 1 patient was managed conservatively after venography showed no active extravasation. No patient required surgical repair or stent-graft placement. Although 2 patients experienced complications—cardiac tamponade with pericardial effusion and pleural effusion—these were successfully managed. All patients survived with favorable clinical follow-up extending to 12 months, without evidence of recurrent bleeding, loss of vascular access, or need for reintervention. These findings suggest balloon tamponade is a feasible, reproducible, and vessel-sparing alternative in the management of central venous catheter–related perforations.10

Although isolated case reports have described the use of balloon tamponade or balloon occlusion in managing vascular injuries such as pulmonary artery rupture11 or cardiac perforation,12 these instances remain limited to adjacent vascular structures and specific procedural contexts. Prior literature on central venous injuries has focused on complications of angioplasty or stent placement rather than balloon tamponade as a therapeutic strategy.13 Covered stents, although effective, carry risks of infection, restenosis, and permanent loss of venous access, with reported success rates of 80% to 95%.14,15 Surgical repair, although definitive, is often not feasible in dialysis-dependent patients.4 In this context, our series adds to the evolving evidence base by presenting one of the first systematically documented experiences of balloon tamponade in central catheter–related venous perforations, achieving 100% procedural success with no reinterventions. Distinctive features include the use of tailored balloon sizing and inflation protocols under fluoroscopic guidance, a focus on patients with limited access options (eg, dialysis dependence), and extended follow-up.

These results highlight balloon tamponade as a potential addition to modern interventional practice. It may represent more than a temporary measure and could serve as a viable option in carefully selected patients where surgical or stent-based strategies are undesirable.9 In our practice, balloon tamponade was reserved for patients with angiographically confirmed active contrast extravasation, whereas hemodynamically stable patients without evidence of ongoing venous leakage were considered for conservative management. Particularly in dialysis-dependent populations, where vascular access preservation is critical, balloon tamponade may serve as an intermediate option between conservative management and definitive repair.16 Our proposed algorithm (Figure 7) provides a possible framework for integrating balloon tamponade into stepwise decision-making, although prospective validation will be required before widespread adoption.Figure 7Proposed algorithm for the endovascular management of iatrogenic central venous catheter–related injuries. From a practical standpoint, balloon tamponade is not without risks. Overinflation may cause vessel rupture, thrombus formation can occur at the site of injury, and delayed re-bleeding after balloon deflation is possible.9 These risks can be mitigated by careful balloon sizing, real-time fluoroscopic monitoring, and limiting inflation times to 10 to 15 minutes. In our series, no clinically significant hemodynamic instability occurred during temporary balloon occlusion of the SVC, BCV, or CIV, and no patient required premature balloon deflation or repeated inflation cycles. Continuous intraprocedural monitoring was performed in all cases. Tamponade may also provide only temporary control in cases of large or actively bleeding perforations, underscoring the need for surgical or stent-based backup when attempting this strategy.17 Contraindications may include suspected catheter-related infection or uncorrected coagulopathy.18 Adoption of structured procedural checklists, preintervention imaging review, and postprocedural surveillance may improve reproducibility and safety.

Strengths and Limitations

This series has several strengths. It represents one of the first systematically reported experiences with balloon tamponade in central venous injuries, addressing a critical gap in the literature. All eligible cases during the study period were included, minimizing selection bias. Procedures were performed within a standardized multidisciplinary framework, with surgical backup and stent graft availability, ensuring patient safety. Finally, structured follow-up for up to 12 months enabled assessment of durable outcomes, including sustained vascular access function and absence of clinically evident recurrent complications.

Nonetheless, limitations must be acknowledged. The retrospective design, single-center setting, and small cohort size restrict generalizability. Outcomes may reflect institutional expertise, and procedural refinements evolved over time without a predefined protocol. The absence of a comparator group precludes direct evaluation against surgery or stent placement, and follow-up was limited to 6 to 12 months, leaving long-term vessel patency uncertain. In addition, systematic follow-up with CT venography was not routinely performed in all patients, limiting objective assessment of long-term central venous patency and subtle structural abnormalities.

Moving forward, multicenter prospective studies are needed to validate the efficacy and safety of balloon tamponade compared with covered stents or surgical repair. Randomized trials could help define optimal balloon size, inflation pressure, and dwell time to standardize technique and minimize risk. Cost-effectiveness analyses may also clarify its role relative to other interventions. Finally, integration of balloon tamponade into Interventional Radiology and Vascular Surgery training curricula will ensure that operators are prepared to manage central venous injuries with precision and confidence. By establishing balloon tamponade as a reliable, minimally invasive option, this work contributes to the broader effort to improve vascular complication management and preserve access in high-risk patient populations.

Conclusions

This case series highlights balloon tamponade as a safe, effective, and minimally invasive approach for managing selected central venous catheter–related perforations involving the SVC, BCV, and IJV. In patients with limited access options, this technique offers rapid hemostasis while preserving critical venous structures. The durability of outcomes in 1-year follow-up further supports its use as more than a temporary measure, positioning it as a reliable tool in the modern interventional armamentarium.

Given the absence of formal guidelines from major vascular and interventional societies, these findings suggest balloon tamponade may have a role in managing selected central venous perforations, warranting further prospective study before integration into clinical protocols. To optimize its broader adoption, future research should focus on prospective validation, comparative studies with covered stents or surgery, and standardized training in procedural technique. Ultimately, balloon tamponade represents not only a therapeutic option but a strategic advance in the safe and effective management of complex vascular access complications.
