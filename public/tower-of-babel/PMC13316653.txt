Mechanisms of dual-frequency focused ultrasound regulation of acoustic droplet vaporization-enhanced thermal lesion formation
Authors: Yubo Zhao, Yi Feng, Liang Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDual-frequency focused ultrasound combined with phase-change nanodroplets offers a promising strategy for improving thermal ablation; however, the mechanisms by which dual-frequency excitation reshapes acoustic droplet vaporization (ADV) and lesion formation remain unclear. Here, we developed a coupled numerical model to investigate how dual-frequency ultrasound governs ADV-enhanced thermal lesion formation through ADV nucleation, bubble-cloud evolution, acoustic-field redistribution, and acoustic energy deposition. The model incorporated nonlinear acoustic propagation, a modified classical nucleation theory for ADV, iterative bubble-cloud expansion, gas-volume-fraction-dependent effective acoustic properties, and bioheat transfer, and was qualitatively assessed against in vitro phantom experiments using a confocal 1 MHz + 5 MHz transducer system. In the presence of phase-change nanodroplets, single-frequency exposure produced delayed ADV after thermal build-up and generated a teardrop-shaped lesion, whereas dual-frequency exposure triggered immediate multisite ADV, accelerated bubble-cloud growth, and yielded a more triangular lesion. Increasing low-frequency acoustic power promoted a transition from teardrop-like to triangular lesions, while increasing nanodroplet concentration enhanced acoustic shielding and shifted lesion formation toward the proximal side of the bubble cloud. These results suggest that dual-frequency ultrasound regulates ADV-enhanced thermal ablation primarily by controlling where ADV is initiated, how the bubble cloud evolves, and where acoustic energy is deposited. The proposed model provides a mechanistic framework for optimizing dual-frequency ADV-mediated ultrasound therapy and engineering controllable lesion geometries.Keywords: Dual-frequency focused ultrasound, Acoustic droplet vaporization, Cavitation bubble clouds, Thermal lesion formation

Introduction

High-intensity focused ultrasound (HIFU) is a noninvasive and radiation-free therapeutic modality capable of depositing acoustic energy deep within tissue with high spatial precision and compatibility with real-time imaging [1], [2], [3]. However, conventional thermal HIFU ablation is still limited by modest heating efficiency, long exposure times, and the risk of collateral thermal injury [4], [5], [6]. Under single-frequency exposure, lesion formation is governed mainly by focal energy deposition and subsequent thermal diffusion, which provides limited flexibility for controlling both treatment efficiency and lesion geometry.

Phase-change nanodroplets offer a potential route to overcome these limitations. Compared with preformed microbubbles, perfluoropentane (PFP) nanodroplets are smaller, more stable, and more suitable for extravasation and functionalization [7], [8]. After acoustic droplet vaporization (ADV), they transform into microbubbles that can substantially enhance acoustic scattering, cavitation activity, and local energy absorption [9], [10]. As a result, ADV-enhanced ultrasound treatment can produce lesion morphologies that differ markedly from the regular ellipsoidal lesions typically generated by conventional HIFU, including teardrop-shaped, rhomboidal, and even proximally shifted coagulation zones [11], [12]. These observations suggest that nanodroplet vaporization does not merely increase heating efficiency, but can fundamentally reorganize the local acoustic and thermal environment.

Dual- and multiple-frequency ultrasound has emerged as an effective strategy for enhancing cavitation-related bioeffects compared with single-frequency excitation. Previous studies have shown that dual-frequency exposure can lower cavitation thresholds and improve therapeutic performance in applications such as thermal ablation [13], thrombolysis [14], and sonodynamic therapy [15]. In ADV-mediated treatment, dual-frequency ultrasound is particularly attractive because it may regulate not only whether vaporization occurs, but also where it starts and how the vaporization region evolves [16]. Although amplitude-modulated single-frequency excitation may also be used to increase the instantaneous pressure for activating droplet nucleation, this strategy relies mainly on power escalation at a single carrier frequency and therefore places high demands on the excitation system. By contrast, in a dual-frequency configuration, the high-frequency component can contribute to achieving the instantaneous negative pressure required for ADV initiation with a relatively lower power requirement, thereby reducing the burden on the driving system. More importantly, the frequency-combination strategy provides an additional degree of freedom for regulating vaporization occurrence, initiation site, bubble-cloud evolution, and ultimately the morphology and localization of the thermal lesion. Nevertheless, the mechanistic link between dual-frequency excitation, ADV initiation, bubble-cloud development, and the final thermal lesion remains insufficiently understood. In particular, it is still unclear how dual-frequency excitation reshapes the spatiotemporal pattern of ADV and how this altered vaporization pattern is translated into lesion morphology and localization. Although existing experimental studies have provided important observations, related modeling and theoretical analyses remain limited, and the underlying principles governing dual-frequency ADV-enhanced ultrasound therapy have yet to be clarified.

Several modeling studies have attempted to elucidate the relevant physical processes, but important limitations remain. Earlier bubble-enhanced HIFU models mainly focused on pre-existing microbubbles and their effects on attenuation and heating, without accounting for the phase-transition process unique to ADV [17]. More recent ADV-related models have improved understanding of bubble formation and temperature rise, but they often rely on simplified assumptions such as linear propagation, spatially homogeneous bubble distributions, or weak coupling among acoustic, bubble, and thermal fields [18]. These simplifications restrict their ability to describe scenarios in which strong nonlinearity, heterogeneous bubble generation, dynamic acoustic shielding, and lesion-shape evolution occur simultaneously. In our model, the initially distributed agents are phase-change nanodroplets, not pre-existing microbubbles. Before vaporization, these nanodroplets are assumed to have negligible influence on ultrasound propagation because of their small size. Under ultrasound excitation, only droplets that satisfy the local pressure- and temperature-dependent ADV nucleation condition are converted into microbubbles. Therefore, the generated microbubble cloud is activated on demand under specific acoustic and thermal conditions, rather than being prescribed as a spatially homogeneous bubble distribution.

In this study, we developed a two-stage coupled numerical model to examine how dual-frequency ultrasound reshapes ADV initiation and thereby governs bubble-cloud evolution, acoustic-field redistribution, and thermal lesion formation. In the first stage, local ADV initiation and bubble-cloud expansion are evaluated iteratively. In the second stage, after the bubble-cloud distribution becomes stable, the redistributed acoustic field is used to calculate heat deposition, temperature rise, and thermal lesion formation according to the gas-volume-fraction-dependent effective acoustic properties of the vaporized region. Thus, the present framework links ADV initiation, bubble-cloud evolution, acoustic-field redistribution, and thermal accumulation within one modeling sequence. In vitro phantom experiments performed under corresponding exposure conditions were used for qualitative comparison. Rather than seeking strict quantitative agreement, our goal was to determine whether the model could reproduce the observed trends in lesion morphology, temporal evolution, and spatial localization, and to use the combined simulation–experiment framework to identify the dominant mechanisms linking dual-frequency excitation to lesion shaping.

Numerical methods

Overview of the coupled numerical model

To clarify how dual-frequency excitation regulates lesion formation through ADV, a coupled numerical model was established to describe the sequence of events from nonlinear acoustic propagation to droplet vaporization, microbubble-cloud expansion, acoustic-field redistribution, and thermal lesion formation. The model was designed to capture the dominant physical mechanisms responsible for lesion shaping rather than to achieve strict quantitative agreement with the experiments.

At each stage of the calculation, the local acoustic pressure and temperature fields were used to evaluate whether ADV occurred. Once vaporization was triggered, the generated bubbles modified the local acoustic field through scattering and shielding, which in turn influenced subsequent vaporization and heat deposition. In this way, lesion geometry emerged from the coupled evolution of vaporization, bubble-cloud growth, acoustic-field redistribution, and thermal accumulation.

For computational efficiency, the simulations were performed in a two-dimensional domain. In addition, the vaporized region was represented using effective acoustic properties determined by the local gas volume fraction, and explicit temperature-dependent changes in tissue properties were not included. Accordingly, the numerical model was used primarily to investigate mechanisms and relative trends, whereas the comparison with experiments was intended as qualitative validation of lesion morphology, temporal evolution, and spatial localization.

Therefore, ΔV should be interpreted as a sub-grid representative observation volume for evaluating nucleation probability, rather than as the volume of a computational grid cell. In the two-dimensional implementation, the local pressure and temperature at each grid point were used to calculate NJ within this representative control volume. In the present model, nucleation of individual droplets was assumed to occur independently. Therefore, changing the initial droplet concentration does not alter the CNT-based nucleation process or the nucleation threshold of a single droplet under a given local pressure–temperature condition.

ADV vaporization includes several processes, including nucleation, post-nucleation bubble growth, and subsequent bubble oscillation. In the present model, vaporization was assumed to be complete once nucleation occurred, such that a nucleated droplet was considered to transform directly into a microbubble. During the growth stage, some nucleated droplets may fail to grow into microbubbles and may recondense back into the liquid state. In our model, complete vaporization means that once the nucleation criterion is satisfied, the nucleated droplet is assumed to undergo successful post-nucleation growth and is represented as a microbubble, while failed growth and recondensation into the liquid state are neglected. This assumption may slightly overestimate the predicted bubble-cloud extent, especially near the boundary of the vaporized region, where the acoustic and thermal conditions are closer to the activation threshold. If partial vaporization or finite vaporization kinetics were included, a fraction of nucleated droplets might fail to complete the post-nucleation growth stage or might recondense into the liquid phase. As a result, the effective bubble-cloud region would become smaller, particularly at the cloud periphery. Although this is a simplification, it is consistent with our previous findings [26]. Under the dual-frequency excitation conditions considered in this study, post-nucleation recondensation was rarely observed. Instead, most nucleated droplets underwent sustained bubble growth, and the resulting microbubble size was generally comparable to the grid size used in the simulations. Therefore, treating nucleated droplets as successfully converted microbubbles provides a practical approximation for incorporating ADV-induced bubble generation into the coupled acoustic and thermal model.

In addition, the bubble radius within each grid cell was not updated in real time. Rather, the collective dynamical effects of the bubble population were represented through their influence on the effective acoustic properties of the medium and on heat deposition. This effective-medium treatment allows the impact of bubble-cloud evolution on the overall acoustic and thermal fields to be captured at an acceptable computational cost, and similar strategies have been adopted in related modeling studies [27], [28].

It should be noted that the present model was designed to capture the cumulative redistribution of the acoustic field caused by the vaporized bubble cloud, rather than the sub-cycle hydrodynamic motion associated with individual bubble nucleation and expansion. Therefore, local flow motion induced by rapid bubble growth and short-lived pressure disturbances generated during individual vaporization events were not solved as independent dynamic variables. Instead, their dominant influence on the subsequent acoustic field was represented in an averaged manner through the iterative update of the bubble-containing region, including bubble-induced scattering, shielding, and gas-volume-fraction-dependent effective acoustic properties. This treatment is consistent with the effective-medium framework adopted in the present study and allows the model to focus on the coupled evolution of ADV initiation, bubble-cloud growth, acoustic-field redistribution, and thermal lesion formation.

Simulation setup and model assumptions

The coupled simulations were implemented using the open-source MATLAB toolbox k -Wave (version 1.4) [35]. To reduce computational cost, a two-dimensional cross-sectional model was adopted. The computational domain measured 60 mm × 60 mm and was discretized into 211 × 211 grid points. The Courant-Friedrichs-Lewy (CFL) number was set to 0.05, yielding a temporal step of 0.996 ns and a spatial step of 29.98 μm in both the axial and lateral directions. Under these settings, the maximum supported frequency in the simulation was 25.1 MHz.

The thermal parameters are listed only for the tissue-mimicking phantom because the bioheat-transfer calculation was performed in the phantom region where thermal lesion formation was evaluated. The surrounding water served only as the acoustic coupling medium, and heat diffusion into the water was not considered in the lesion calculation. In addition, during the acoustic-field updating stage associated with ADV and bubble-cloud evolution, the iterative calculation lasted only several tens of acoustic cycles, which was too short to produce appreciable thermal accumulation. Therefore, the specific heat capacity and thermal conductivity of water and isolated bubbles were not required as independent thermal inputs. In the subsequent thermal lesion formation stage, after the bubble-cloud distribution became stable, the vaporized region was treated as a homogeneous bubble–tissue mixture, and its effective thermal properties were assigned as described below.

Before vaporization, the nanodroplets were assumed to have a negligible influence on ultrasound propagation because of their small size and low volumetric fraction. After vaporization, the exposed region was treated as a bubble-containing effective medium whose acoustic properties depended on the local gas volume fraction fG.

The local gas volume fraction fG was determined from the initial droplet volume fraction fD in the phantom and the assumed volume expansion after vaporization. In this study, fD was controlled experimentally to regulate fG. The initial droplet radius was approximately 100 nm based on our previously published measurements [11]. To promote a uniform droplet distribution within the phantom, the droplet emulsion was thoroughly stirred and mixed with the liquid phantom material before gelation. After vaporization, the generated microbubble was assumed to have a diameter equal to one computational grid size, namely 29.98 μm, corresponding to a bubble radius of approximately 15 μm. Droplets reaching the vaporization threshold were assumed to be converted into microbubbles. Under this assumption, for simplicity, the volume expansion factor from the initial droplet to the vaporized microbubble was approximated as 1 × 106. Thus, fG in the vaporized tissue–microbubble mixture was indirectly controlled by adjusting the initial fD. For example, to obtain a final gas volume fraction of fG = 1 × 10−7, the initial droplet volume fraction was set to fD =1 × 10−13.

The effective absorption coefficient of the bubble-tissue mixture cannot be described by a simple analytical expression because it depends not only on fG, but also on acoustic frequency and pressure amplitude through bubble dynamics. In bubble-laden media, bubbles act as acoustic scatterers and secondary radiation sources, while viscous damping during cavitation converts a substantial fraction of acoustic energy into heat. Accordingly, the attenuation behavior reflects the combined effects of gas volume fraction, excitation frequency, and acoustic pressure amplitude.

To account for this effect, we adopted a global attenuation coefficient approach based on the methodology reported by Chavrier et al. [42] This coefficient was obtained from experimental measurements and fitting of the absorption behavior of a microbubble–tissue mixture. Therefore, within this effective-medium framework, the influence of bubble dynamics, including bubble oscillation and possible resonance-related absorption, is implicitly included in the fitted global attenuation coefficient. The attenuation coefficient in the vaporized region was adapted from the effective attenuation treatment reported by Chavrier et al. for tissue containing microbubbles. Although the microbubbles in Chavrier et al. were pre-existing, whereas the microbubbles in the present study were generated by ADV, the vaporized region after ADV can similarly be regarded as a bubble–tissue mixture. Therefore, the same type of effective attenuation treatment was used locally to describe the increased acoustic absorption/attenuation caused by the presence of microbubbles. It should be noted that this microbubble-enhanced attenuation was applied only in regions where the ADV criterion was satisfied and vaporized microbubbles were generated. In regions where droplets were not activated, the intrinsic acoustic properties of the phantom/tissue were retained. Thus, the model does not assume that microbubbles are initially or uniformly present throughout the phantom; rather, the enhanced attenuation is introduced only within the ADV-generated microbubble cloud [17]. Therefore, the attenuation coefficient used in the present simulations was treated as an effective function of gas volume fraction, ultrasound frequency, and acoustic pressure amplitude, with the relationships among these variables established with reference to Chavrier et al.

Computational workflow

The coupled simulation workflow can be summarized as follows. First, in the numerical calculation, nanodroplets were assumed to be uniformly distributed at the computational grid points according to the prescribed droplet concentration. Because the initial droplets were nanoscale, their effect on ultrasound propagation before vaporization was neglected. To calculate the acoustic field in the initial medium before vaporization, the k-Wave function kspaceFirstOrder2DG was applied after defining the acoustic and thermal properties of the computational domain.

Second, the local peak negative pressure and temperature were used to evaluate the ADV nucleation rate and nucleation number at each grid point. For grid points where vaporization occurred (i.e. NJ > 1), the local medium parameters were changed from the tissue values to the bubble values listed in Table 1. The large acoustic impedance difference between tissue and bubbles results in strong scattering at the tissue–bubble interface. This scattering modifies the local acoustic field and may generate larger local negative pressures, which can activate further vaporization of nearby droplets. Therefore, the ultrasound propagation, droplet vaporization, and bubble scattering processes were calculated iteratively. After each iteration, the bubble distribution was updated and the acoustic field was recalculated using kspaceFirstOrder2DG. This update-evaluation procedure was repeated until no additional grid points satisfied the vaporization criterion.

After the vaporized region became stable, the simulation proceeded to the thermal lesion formation stage. During the vaporization stage, the generated bubbles were treated as discrete scattering sites. During the subsequent thermal calculation, the bubble-containing region was treated as an effective tissue–microbubble mixture. The effective acoustic properties of this region were calculated using Eqs. (9), (10), (11) and the results of Chavrier et al. The simulated pressure waveform was used to calculate the acoustic intensity and the acoustic heat source term. The resulting heat source distribution was then used as the input for the k-Wave function kWaveDiffusion, which was employed to solve the transient temperature field and the corresponding CEM43 distribution. Thermal lesion formation was evaluated based on the criterion CEM43 > 240 min. The CEM43 value was calculated at each spatial grid point. Grid points with CEM43 > 240 min were classified as thermally damaged. The onset time of thermal lesion formation was defined as the first time point at which at least one grid point in the computational domain exceeded this threshold. The spatial distribution of the thermal lesion was obtained by thresholding the final CEM43 map, with all grid points exceeding 240 min included in the predicted lesion region. In this way, the final lesion geometry was obtained as the outcome of a coupled sequence linking ADV initiation, bubble-cloud evolution, acoustic-field redistribution, and thermal accumulation.

Experimental validation

Experimental setup for dual-frequency ultrasound ablation

To provide qualitative validation of the proposed numerical model, in vitro experiments were performed under exposure conditions corresponding to those used in the simulations. This section first describes the dual-frequency ultrasound experimental setup and then compares the baseline lesion morphology obtained experimentally and in the simulations in the absence of nanodroplets.

A schematic of the dual-frequency ultrasound ablation platform is shown in Fig. 1. A custom-designed focused ultrasound transducer system (Chongqing Haifu Medical Tech. Co., Ltd., Chongqing, China), comprising two confocal spherical annular elements, was used. The outer annular element operated at 1 MHz with a diameter of 94 mm, whereas the inner annular element operated at 5 MHz with a diameter of 50 mm. Both elements shared a common geometric focus at a focal distance of 60 mm. The electro-acoustic conversion efficiencies were approximately 75% for the 1 MHz element and 45% for the 5 MHz element.

Schematic of the dual-frequency ultrasound experimental platform.

Excitation signals for the two channels were generated by a dual-channel arbitrary waveform generator (DG5072, RIGOL, Beijing, China) and amplified independently using two power amplifiers (AG1016, T&G Power Conversion, Inc., Rochester, NY, USA). The 1 MHz channel was operated at a duty cycle of 60% to provide the primary acoustic heating, whereas the 5 MHz channel delivered 100 μs tone bursts at a pulse repetition frequency of 100 Hz, corresponding to a duty cycle of 1%. The total sonication duration was 10 s. This configuration provided low-frequency-dominated heating while allowing the high-frequency component to trigger ADV and cavitation-related effects.

During the in vitro experiments, the sample was placed at the common focal region of the transducer in a degassed water tank for acoustic coupling. The focal position was adjusted using a laser-assisted alignment system and a three-dimensional positioning device. The preparation methods for the gel phantoms and nanodroplets have been described previously in Refs. [43] and [44], respectively.

Overall, the experimental and simulated results were qualitatively consistent in the absence of nanodroplets, with both showing elliptical lesions. However, substantially different power levels were required to produce comparable lesion geometries in the experiments and simulations, precluding direct quantitative comparison. This discrepancy is mainly attributed to the dimensional simplification of the numerical model. The simulations were performed using a two-dimensional model, whereas the actual acoustic propagation, focusing, heat deposition, and thermal diffusion in the experiments occurred in three dimensions. Such a 2D approximation can lead to quantitative differences in the spatial distribution of acoustic energy and temperature, and therefore in the power required to generate similar lesion geometries. Nevertheless, this 2D simplification has been adopted in previous related study [28] to capture the main physical behavior while reducing computational cost. Accordingly, the subsequent experiment-simulation comparisons in this study are intended only to assess qualitative agreement in lesion morphology, temporal evolution, and spatial localization, rather than to establish a one-to-one quantitative correspondence in input power.

It should also be noted that the final temperature rise and thermal lesion formation predicted in this study were mainly caused by the ADV-induced formation of vaporized microbubbles. The presence of microbubbles increased the effective acoustic absorption and attenuation of the bubble–tissue mixture, thereby enhancing local acoustic energy deposition. In contrast, although the fundamental and low-order harmonics contributed to the heat source calculation, the additional heating associated with harmonic components was relatively small compared with the dominant effect of bubble-induced absorption enhancement. Therefore, the enhanced thermal response observed after ADV should be attributed primarily to the increased effective absorption caused by the vaporized microbubble cloud, rather than to harmonic heating alone.

Results

Microbubble-cloud formation and thermal lesion enhancement under single-frequency excitation

After establishing qualitative experiment-simulation agreement in the absence of nanodroplets, we next examined how nanodroplet vaporization, bubble-cloud evolution, and dual-frequency excitation regulate thermal lesion formation. We first investigated nanodroplet-mediated enhancement under single-frequency excitation. Fig. 4 shows the simulated evolution of nanodroplet vaporization and microbubble-cloud formation at 1 MHz and an acoustic power of 40 W, assuming a uniform initial distribution of PFP nanodroplets in the tissue region.

Dynamic process of nanodroplet phase transition into microbubbles.

During the early stage of insonation, the peak negative pressure at the focus remained approximately 8.9 MPa, which was below the nucleation threshold of PFP nanodroplets at the initial temperature [24]. As a result, no vaporization occurred. After approximately 1.4 s of thermal accumulation, the local temperature increased sufficiently to trigger nucleation of the first microbubble, as indicated by the isolated blue-violet point in Fig. 4. Once nucleated, this bubble immediately interacted with the subsequent incident acoustic wave and generated pressure amplification on the proximal side facing the transducer [28]. This effect promoted vaporization of neighboring droplets and initiated a cascade of bubble generation. In the first iteration, three adjacent droplets were activated by the scattering-enhanced field. In the next iteration, the newly formed bubble cluster triggered vaporization of seven additional droplets, followed by further expansion in subsequent iterations.

As the bubble cloud developed, the local acoustic field became increasingly distorted. The locations of peak positive and peak negative pressure progressively separated, and the corresponding waveforms became increasingly asymmetric, indicating that bubble-cloud formation substantially altered local wave propagation. Despite the higher negative pressure observed in later iterations, bubble-cloud expansion eventually ceased, suggesting that pressure amplification alone was insufficient once the local temperature at the displaced bubble locations fell below the threshold required for further nucleation [23].

The influence of this process on thermal lesion formation is shown in Fig. 5, where the lesion contour was determined using the criterion CEM43 > 240 min under single-frequency exposure at 1 MHz and 50 W. In the experiments, the 1 MHz transducer was operated at a duty cycle of 60%, whereas continuous-wave excitation was adopted in the simulations as an effective approximation to the experimental heating condition; accordingly, the simulated exposure time was set to 6 s to partially compensate for the difference in time-averaged acoustic energy. As the bubble cloud progressively enhanced local acoustic absorption, the lesion region expanded over time and underwent a distinct morphological transition. At 3 s, the lesion appeared as a narrow elongated strip. At 4 s, the contour began to broaden and develop a teardrop-like profile. At 5 s, the lesion pattern increased markedly and the teardrop morphology became more pronounced. By 6 s, the lesion had further matured, although the proximal tip appeared somewhat blunted because of thermal diffusion in the simulated field.

Both simulations and experiments consistently showed a progressive transition in lesion morphology from an elongated strip to a teardrop-shaped lesion. A minor discrepancy was observed at the proximal lesion tip, which appeared sharper in the experiments than in the simulations. This difference may reflect the use of constant material properties in the simulations, whereas temperature-dependent changes in the coagulated region could have limited heat diffusion during the experiments [45]. Nevertheless, the overall agreement in lesion morphology and temporal evolution supports the ability of the proposed model to capture the main features of single-frequency ADV-enhanced thermal lesion formation.

Microbubble-cloud formation and thermal lesion enhancement under dual-frequency excitation

We then examined the vaporization dynamics and lesion formation under dual-frequency excitation. Fig. 7 shows the simulated phase-transition process from nanodroplets to microbubbles under dual-frequency exposure with a low frequency of 1 MHz and a low-frequency acoustic power of 40 W, together with a high frequency of 5 MHz and a high-frequency acoustic power of 10 W.

Dynamic process of nanodroplet phase transition into microbubbles under dual-frequency ultrasound excitation.

Compared with single-frequency excitation, dual-frequency exposure produced a vaporization pattern distinct from that under single-frequency excitation. At the onset of insonation, the peak negative pressure in the focal region exceeded 12 MPa, which was sufficient to trigger ADV directly under the present modeling conditions [24]. As a result, vaporization occurred immediately, without the latency period observed under single-frequency excitation. In addition, multiple droplets within the focal volume satisfied the nucleation criterion simultaneously, leading to multi-site initiation of the bubble population rather than single-site nucleation followed by delayed expansion. This difference is illustrated in the second row of Fig. 7.

During subsequent iterations, the larger number of initially generated microbubbles produced stronger scattering feedback and broader pressure amplification, thereby accelerating cloud expansion. As shown in the left panel of Fig. 7, the vaporization region expanded from an initially slender structure into a much broader triangular region. The number of effective vaporization-update iterations was also greater than that observed under single-frequency excitation. The corresponding waveforms, shown in the right panel of Fig. 7, displayed increasingly pronounced separation between the locations of peak positive and peak negative pressure during rapid cloud growth, confirming stronger local field distortion under dual-frequency conditions.

After the microbubble cloud stabilized, the system entered a secondary stage dominated by thermal accumulation. At this stage, heating was still governed primarily by the 1 MHz component, and the overall progression remained broadly similar to that observed under single-frequency excitation. However, because the initial bubble-cloud distribution under dual-frequency excitation differed substantially from that under single-frequency excitation, the resulting lesion morphology was correspondingly altered.

The thermal consequences of dual-frequency ADV-enhanced exposure are summarized in Fig. 8. Compared with the single-frequency case, dual-frequency excitation produced a higher focal temperature and a more spatially concentrated temperature field after 6 s of insonation, as shown in Fig. 8(a). The temperature-rise curve in Fig. 8(b) was also smoother and more monotonic, consistent with immediate ADV initiation at the onset of exposure.

Simulation and experimental results of dual-frequency ultrasound with ADV-enhanced therapy. (a) Simulated temperature field distribution in the focal region. (b) Simulated temperature variation at the focal point. (c) Simulated lesion pattern. (d) Experimental lesion pattern.

The lesion contour predicted using the criterion CEM43 > 240 min is shown in Fig. 8(c). In contrast to the teardrop-shaped lesion produced under single-frequency excitation, the lesion obtained under dual-frequency excitation exhibited a more triangular morphology, closely corresponding to the spatial configuration of the bubble cloud shown in Fig. 7. This finding indicates that thermal lesion geometry was strongly influenced by the spatial organization of the ADV-generated microbubble cloud.

The corresponding in vitro result is shown in Fig. 8(d), obtained under a low frequency of 1 MHz with an input electrical power of 70 W and a high frequency of 5 MHz with an input electrical power of 20 W. The experimentally observed lesion also displayed a triangular morphology, in qualitative agreement with the simulated lesion pattern. Although differences remained in the absolute aspect ratio and extent of lesion elongation, the simulations and experiments consistently showed that dual-frequency excitation produced a lesion morphology distinct from that observed under single-frequency exposure. Together with the simulated vaporization dynamics, these findings support the interpretation that dual-frequency excitation promoted immediate multi-site vaporization.

Effects of low-frequency acoustic power and frequency combination on thermal lesion formation

The influence of excitation parameters on lesion formation was further investigated under dual-frequency excitation. Fig. 9 shows the effect of low-frequency acoustic power on the resulting lesion morphology. In the simulations shown in Fig. 9(a)–(c), the high-frequency component was fixed at 5 MHz and 25 W, whereas the low-frequency acoustic power was set to 20, 30, and 50 W. The corresponding experimental results are shown in Fig. 9(d), where the low-frequency input electrical power increased from 50 W to 70 W, while the high-frequency input electrical power was maintained at 25 W.

Influence of low-frequency acoustic power on thermal lesion formation. The acoustic power used in the simulations in (a), (b), and (c) was 20, 30, and 50 W, respectively. The corresponding input electrical powers used in the experiments in (d), from left to right, were 50, 60, and 70 W.

As the low-frequency power increased, the lesion morphology gradually transitioned from a teardrop-like pattern to a more pronounced triangular shape, accompanied by a clear increase in lesion size. At lower low-frequency power, the 5 MHz component was still able to trigger formation of an initial bubble population, but the comparatively limited time-averaged heating provided by the 1 MHz channel led to a final lesion morphology that remained similar to the teardrop-shaped lesion observed under high-power single-frequency exposure. At intermediate power levels, the lesion contour broadened and evolved toward a triangular form. At the highest power level, the lesion became larger and more distinctly triangular. A similar overall trend was observed in the experimental results shown in Fig. 9(d). Because the simulations and experiments used different power definitions and were not intended for direct quantitative comparison, the agreement discussed here should be interpreted primarily in terms of qualitative trends in lesion morphology.

As the low-frequency power increased, the corresponding acoustic pressure increased, causing more droplets to satisfy the vaporization criterion. Consequently, the ADV-activated region expanded and a larger microbubble cloud was generated. However, the basic morphology of the bubble cloud remained similar. As shown in Fig. 7, the vaporized region maintained a triangular distribution, while the size of this triangular region increased with increasing low-frequency power. In addition, the enhanced low-frequency power strengthened the thermal effect, leading to an enlarged triangular thermal lesion region. Therefore, the more pronounced triangular morphology observed at higher low-frequency power can be attributed to the expansion of the triangular ADV-activated bubble-cloud region and the corresponding enlargement of the thermal lesion area.

In addition, the low-frequency component affected not only the thermal response but also the droplet nucleation process. In this study, droplet vaporization was mainly governed by the local negative pressure. Under dual-frequency excitation, the instantaneous acoustic pressure consisted of contributions from both the low-frequency and high-frequency components. Therefore, the peak negative pressure used to determine whether droplets satisfied the vaporization criterion was influenced by both components. As the low-frequency power increased, the total negative pressure increased, allowing more droplets to meet the vaporization threshold. Consequently, the ADV-activated region expanded and a larger bubble cloud was generated, which further contributed to the enhanced thermal effect.

Due to the limitations of the experimental setup in our laboratory, experiments were only performed using the 1 MHz + 5 MHz dual-frequency combination. However, the frequency combination may have a considerable influence on the morphology of the thermal lesion region. To further illustrate the influence of frequency combination on the spatial distribution of nucleation, an additional acoustic-field simulation was performed for a fundamental-harmonic pair of 1.1 MHz + 2.2 MHz and qualitatively compared with the high-speed observations reported by Li et al. [46]. The 1.1 MHz + 2.2 MHz combination was selected because this 1:2 frequency ratio is commonly used in dual-frequency ultrasound studies and facilitates comparison with previous work. Fig. 10(a) shows the experimentally observed bubble distribution after 5.0 ms of insonation, in which the cloud exhibited a characteristic dual-cluster aggregation pattern. The corresponding simulated negative-pressure field is shown in Fig. 10(b). Under this frequency combination, the focal region split into two subsidiary negative-pressure foci of slightly different sizes, qualitatively consistent with the observed bubble pattern.

Acoustic field distribution and nucleation region under dual-frequency ultrasound at 1.1 MHz + 2.2 MHz. (a) High-speed photographic image of the nucleation region (reprinted with permission from Li et al. [46]; Copyright 2020 Elsevier). (b) Simulated acoustic-pressure field.

These results suggest that, in addition to acoustic power, the choice of frequency combination provides another degree of freedom for controlling the spatial organization of vaporization and, consequently, the resulting lesion geometry [24]. In the 1 MHz + 5 MHz configuration used in the main experiments, the acoustic field remained relatively similar to that under single-frequency 1 MHz exposure, and the lesion morphology therefore retained some similarity to that observed under high-power single-frequency excitation. By contrast, alternative frequency combinations, particularly fundamental-harmonic pairs, may generate more complex pressure-field structures and thereby offer greater flexibility for shaping the bubble cloud and the final lesion contour.

Effect of nanodroplet concentration on acoustic shielding and thermal lesion localization

In addition to excitation parameters, nanodroplet concentration also markedly influenced lesion formation. To investigate this effect, the droplet number density was increased such that the final gas-phase volume fraction in the bubble-containing region reached a relatively high level. For this analysis, a relatively low dual-frequency power setting was used to facilitate visualization of the subsequent cloud expansion, with the low-frequency acoustic power set to 20 W and the high-frequency acoustic power set to 5 W under the 1 MHz + 5 MHz configuration.

The resulting bubble-cloud formation process is shown in Fig. 11. Under these conditions, only a single bubble was activated during the first iteration, as indicated by the arrow in the panel labeled “1st”. As the iterative process proceeded, the vaporized region expanded into a compact bubble cloud with a comparatively high local gas volume fraction, as shown in the panel labeled “final”. Under these high-concentration conditions, the vaporized region could be approximated as a bulk effective medium in the subsequent acoustic analysis.

Dynamic formation of the vaporized microbubble cloud under elevated nanodroplet concentration conditions.

The associated thermal lesion patterns are shown in Fig. 12. Both simulations and experiments revealed a clear spatial separation between the bubble cloud and the thermal lesion. Specifically, lesion formation occurred predominantly in the proximal region ahead of the bubble-cloud front rather than within the interior of the cloud. This observation is consistent with a pronounced acoustic shielding effect [9]: when the local bubble concentration becomes sufficiently high, the strong acoustic impedance mismatch at the bubble-tissue interface enhances reflection and scattering, thereby reducing penetration of the incident acoustic energy into the cloud interior and shifting energy deposition toward the proximal boundary of the cloud.

Thermal lesion formation under elevated nanodroplet concentration conditions. Comparison between (a) simulation and (b) experiment.

Although the simulations reproduced the overall spatial trend, some differences remained. In the simulations, the lesion contour was relatively rounded and extended partially into the bubble-cloud region, whereas in the experiments the lesion was more clearly confined to the proximal side of the cloud. This difference likely arises because only the acoustic properties of the bubble-tissue mixture were updated in the present model, whereas the thermal properties of the bubble-rich region were assumed to remain identical to those of the surrounding tissue [45]. Even so, both simulations and experiments consistently showed that increasing nanodroplet concentration can fundamentally alter the spatial relationship between the bubble cloud and the thermal lesion through acoustic shielding.

The effect of microbubble concentration is expected to involve a concentration-dependent transition from heating enhancement to shielding-dominated behavior. At relatively low concentrations, increasing the concentration provides more available nucleation sites and promotes ADV-induced bubble-cloud formation, thereby enhancing thermal deposition. When the concentration becomes sufficiently high, the generated bubbles may form a dense bubble cloud, which can scatter and attenuate the incident ultrasound and reduce downstream acoustic energy deposition. Under this condition, acoustic shielding becomes increasingly important. However, this transition does not correspond to a universal fixed critical concentration, because it depends on acoustic pressure, frequency, droplet size, spatial distribution, and the density of the vaporized bubble cloud. Moreover, since the experiments were performed in a three-dimensional field whereas the simulations were conducted in a two-dimensional domain, the effective transition range may differ between the experimental and simulated results. Therefore, a microbubble concentration of 1 × 10−5 was selected in this study to facilitate comparison between experiment and simulation, while the specific transition range requires further investigation.

Discussion

The central finding of this study is that dual-frequency ultrasound regulates ADV-enhanced thermal lesion formation primarily by reshaping the spatiotemporal pattern of ADV initiation and subsequent bubble-cloud evolution. In the absence of nanodroplets, the addition of the high-frequency component produced only limited changes in lesion formation, indicating that dual-frequency excitation alone was insufficient to substantially alter the thermal outcome under the present exposure conditions. In contrast, once phase-change nanodroplets were introduced, lesion formation became strongly dependent on how ADV was initiated and how the resulting bubble cloud evolved. These results indicate that the key role of dual-frequency excitation in this system is not simply to increase vaporization efficiency, but to determine where vaporization starts, how the bubble cloud expands, and where acoustic energy is ultimately deposited.

A major mechanistic difference identified in this study is that single-frequency exposure produced delayed and localized ADV initiation, whereas dual-frequency exposure triggered immediate multi-site ADV. This difference in the initial vaporization pattern governed the subsequent bubble-cloud architecture and ultimately translated into distinct lesion geometries. Although a similar effect might in principle be achieved by further increasing the acoustic power under single-frequency exposure, such a strategy may raise safety concerns because it relies on substantially stronger insonation [5]. In the dual-frequency mode used here, however, the high-frequency component was delivered with a very low duty cycle and relatively low power, so its role was primarily to facilitate ADV initiation and early bubble-cloud formation rather than to introduce a substantial additional thermal burden. This feature may provide a more practical route to lesion reshaping while reducing the need for further power escalation under single-frequency exposure [16].

Taken together, these findings show that lesion geometry in dual-frequency ADV-enhanced ultrasound is determined by the coupled effects of initial vaporization patterning, subsequent thermal growth, and concentration-dependent acoustic shielding. The effect of low-frequency acoustic power indicates that lesion shaping in this system is governed by two coupled stages: initial ADV patterning under dual-frequency excitation and subsequent low-frequency-dominated thermal growth. Increasing low-frequency power mainly strengthens the translation of the initial bubble-cloud pattern into the final lesion geometry. The analysis of the 1.1 MHz + 2.2 MHz frequency pair further suggests that frequency combination itself can reshape the topology of the negative-pressure field, thereby providing an additional degree of freedom for controlling vaporization architecture and lesion geometry. Increasing nanodroplet concentration shifted the system toward a shielding-dominated regime, indicating that increasing droplet concentration does not simply enhance treatment but can also redirect energy deposition and alter lesion localization [47].

Although the ADV-induced bubble cloud and the thermal lesion are spatially related, a direct quantitative relationship between the bubble-cloud growth rate and the lesion expansion rate is difficult to define. The bubble-cloud growth mainly represents the expansion of the ADV-activated region, which is primarily driven by the local acoustic pressure field. This process occurs over a very short time scale, generally within tens of ultrasound cycles, after which the bubble-cloud region becomes approximately stable. In contrast, lesion expansion is determined by cumulative thermal dose and heat diffusion, which occur over a much longer time scale. In the present simulations, heating was modeled over 6 s, and the lesion boundary continued to evolve during this period, as shown in Fig. 5, Fig. 6. Therefore, the bubble cloud provides the region of enhanced attenuation and heat deposition, while the subsequent lesion expansion reflects the delayed accumulation of thermal dose. For this reason, the bubble-cloud growth rate and lesion expansion rate were not treated as having a simple linear or one-to-one quantitative relationship in this study. Several additional limitations should be acknowledged. First, to improve computational efficiency, a two-dimensional model was used, which precluded direct quantitative comparison with the experiments; accordingly, the experiment-simulation comparisons in this study were limited to qualitative agreement. Second, the material parameters were assumed to remain constant throughout the simulations, without accounting for temperature-dependent changes in tissue properties. This simplification may partly explain why the proximal portion of the simulated lesion was less thick and less sharp than that observed experimentally. It may also underlie the absence of an obvious thermal shielding effect in the simulations under high nanodroplet concentration conditions, under which only acoustic shielding was captured.

It should be noted that the nanodroplet concentrations used in this study were selected mainly for mechanistic investigation and for comparison with the controlled experimental conditions. These concentrations should be regarded as local effective concentrations in the insonated region rather than direct representations of systemic concentrations achievable in vivo. In practical in vivo applications, the local droplet concentration would be affected by the administration route, blood dilution, vascular permeability, tissue accumulation, targeting efficiency, and biological clearance. Therefore, the actual in vivo concentration distribution may be more heterogeneous and may differ from the simplified concentration assumptions used in the present simulation. Further studies that incorporate three-dimensional tissue geometry, perfusion, biodistribution, and pharmacokinetic effects are required to evaluate clinically realistic nanodroplet concentrations.

Although the present study was developed in the context of HIFU thermal ablation, the underlying mechanism is more general and may also be relevant to other ultrasound applications involving cavitation-cloud formation. In essence, the key process identified here is that the initial vaporization or bubble-generation pattern reorganizes the subsequent acoustic field, which then governs how the bubble cloud expands and where energy is ultimately concentrated. This type of coupling is not unique to ADV-enhanced thermal ablation. Similar field-bubble-cloud interactions are also important in cavitation-dominated modalities such as boiling histotripsy [48], where the formation and propagation of cavitation clouds are strongly influenced by bubble-mediated scattering and local pressure redistribution. Therefore, the present model and the mechanistic insights derived from it may provide a useful reference for understanding and potentially controlling cavitation-cloud localization, growth, and treatment morphology in ultrasound therapies beyond purely thermal ablation.

Overall, the present results suggest that dual-frequency ultrasound provides a practical means of regulating lesion geometry and localization in ADV-mediated thermal therapy by controlling ADV initiation, bubble-cloud evolution, and acoustic energy deposition. The proposed model may therefore help guide treatment-parameter optimization and lesion-shape engineering in future therapeutic ultrasound applications.

Conclusions

In this study, we developed a coupled numerical model and used in vitro experiments for qualitative validation to investigate how dual-frequency ultrasound regulates ADV-enhanced thermal lesion formation. In the absence of nanodroplets, dual-frequency excitation produced only limited changes in lesion formation. In contrast, when phase-change nanodroplets were present, single-frequency exposure led to delayed ADV initiation after thermal buildup and generated a teardrop-shaped lesion, whereas dual-frequency exposure triggered immediate multi-site ADV initiation, accelerated bubble-cloud evolution, and produced a more triangular lesion. Increasing low-frequency acoustic power promoted the transition from teardrop-like to triangular lesions, while increasing nanodroplet concentration enhanced acoustic shielding and shifted lesion formation toward the proximal side of the bubble cloud. These findings show that dual-frequency ultrasound regulates ADV-enhanced thermal ablation by controlling ADV initiation, bubble-cloud evolution, and acoustic energy deposition, thereby determining lesion geometry and localization. The proposed model provides a mechanistic basis for optimizing dual-frequency ADV-enhanced ultrasound treatment and designing controllable lesion geometries in future therapeutic applications.
