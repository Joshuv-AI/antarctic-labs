Temporary Interruption versus Continuation of Non-aspirin, Non-P2Y12 Antiplatelet Monotherapy for Delayed Post-polypectomy Bleeding
Authors: Yutaka Okagawa, Masanori Sato, Masahiro Hirakawa, Keita Seto, Ryo Ito, Ginji Oomori, Saki Saeki, Shutaro Oiwa, Naotaka Hayasaka, Shogo Miura, Tomoyuki Abe, Shinya Minami, Tetsuya Sumiyoshi, Yasuhiro Nagaoka, Hiroyuki Hisai, Shinichi Katsuki, Hirofumi Ohnishi, Kohichi Takada
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background/Study aim The Japan Gastrointestinal Endoscopy Society guidelines indicate that antiplatelet agents (APAs) other than aspirin and P2Y12 receptor antagonists (other APAs) may be interrupted the day before high-bleeding-risk procedures. However, this recommendation is based on low-quality evidence, and no comparative data exist. We aimed to evaluate whether 1-day interruption of other APAs reduced delayed post-polypectomy bleeding (DPPB) in patients undergoing hot snare polypectomy (HSP) or endoscopic mucosal resection (EMR). Patient and methods This retrospective multicenter cohort study included consecutive patients who received monotherapy with other APAs and underwent colorectal HSP/EMR between April 2020 and March 2024. The patients were categorized into the interruption and continuation groups. We compared the patient backgrounds, polyp findings, use of prophylactic clipping, intraprocedural bleeding, and DPPB between the two groups. Propensity score matching (PSM) was performed to reduce confounding factors. Results In total, 506 patients received monotherapy with other APAs, and 192 matched pairs were evaluated. The baseline and procedural characteristics were well balanced after PSM. DPPB occurred in 2 patients (1.0%) in both the interruption and continuation groups ( p = 1; absolute risk difference, 0.0% [95% confidence interval: −2.0 to 2.0]). Thromboembolic events occurred in 1 and 0 patients in the interruption and continuation groups, respectively. Conclusions One-day interruption of other APAs was not associated with a clear reduction in DPPB risk during HSP/EMR. Given the risk of thromboembolism after interruption, routine discontinuation may be unnecessary. Prospective studies are needed to reassess the current guideline recommendations.

Introduction

Endoscopic resection of colorectal adenomatous polyps significantly reduces both the incidence of colorectal cancer and colorectal cancer-related mortality 1 2 and has thus been widely adopted in clinical practice. With the ongoing aging of the population in Japan, an increasing number of patients undergoing colonoscopy are receiving antithrombotic therapy for coronary artery disease and cerebrovascular diseases. Therefore, balancing the risks of procedural bleeding and thromboembolism remains a major clinical challenge. To address this issue, the Japan Gastroenterological Endoscopy Society (JGES) developed guidelines in 2012, 3 and similar recommendations were published by the American Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ASGE) 4 and European Society of Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ESGE). 5 Hot snare polypectomy (HSP) and endoscopic mucosal resection (EMR) are classified as high-bleeding-risk procedures, and each guideline provides recommendations on whether antithrombotic agents should be continued, temporarily interrupted, or substituted during these procedures.

For aspirin, the JGES guidelines recommend temporary interruption, whereas the ASGE and ESGE guidelines recommend continuation. For thienopyridine derivatives, all three guidelines suggest temporary interruption for 5–7 days or substitution with aspirin. However, the protocol for antiplatelet agents (APAs) other than aspirin and P2Y12 receptor antagonists (P2Y12RA) (other APAs), such as cilostazol, sarpogrelate hydrochloride, dipyridamole, beraprost sodium, and limaprost alfadex, remains poorly defined. The JGES guidelines suggest that these agents may be discontinued the day before high-risk procedures; however, this recommendation is based on low-quality evidence and has not been addressed in the ASGE and ESGE guidelines. Cilostazol has only a mild effect on platelet aggregation and is associated with a low risk of increased bleeding events even when administered continuously. 6 However, some studies have raised concerns about the risk of delayed post-polypectomy bleeding (DPPB) when cilostazol is continued during colorectal polypectomy. 7

DPPB is a major adverse event associated with colorectal HSP/EMR. Although it occurs in only approximately 1% of patients, 8 the risk increases with the use of antithrombotic agents. Conversely, the temporary interruption of antithrombotic therapy is associated with thromboembolic events, 9 raising uncertainty about whether routine discontinuation is appropriate. Therefore, the clinical necessity of disrupting other APAs before colorectal polypectomy remains unclear. Hence, this study aimed to investigate whether 1-day interruption of other APAs reduced the incidence of DPPB in patients undergoing HSP/EMR. Toward this goal, we compared the incidence of DPPB between patients with continuation and temporary interruption of these agents during HSP/EMR. To minimize confounding factors, propensity score matching (PSM) was used.

Patients and Methods

Study Design and Patients

This retrospective multicenter cohort study was conducted in accordance with the tenets of the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol for the overall cohort was approved by the institutional review board (IRB) of Sapporo Medical University (Approval No. 362-126) and subsequently by the IRBs of all participating institutions. The present analysis was conducted using a subset of this approved cohort. The need for written informed consent was waived owing to the retrospective design of the study. However, all patients were offered the opportunity to opt out through institutional websites.

This study included consecutive patients who underwent HSP or EMR between April 2020 and March 2024. Eligible patients were those aged ≥20 years who were receiving antiplatelet monotherapy with other APAs and whose clinical course following the procedure could be confirmed. Patients whose clinical background, endoscopic findings, and post-polypectomy outcomes could not be evaluated; who refused the use of their clinical data; and on multiple antiplatelet therapies or taking other antithrombotic agents were excluded. Cold snare polypectomy (CSP) has recently been widely performed for diminutive polyps with a very low incidence of DPPB. 10 To avoid underestimation of the DPPB incidence in HSP/EMR, CSP was excluded from the analysis.

Endoscopic Procedures

All colonoscopies were performed using high-definition instruments (CF-H260AZI, CF-H290I, CF-HQ290Z, or PCF-H290ZI [Olympus Medical Systems, Tokyo, Japan]; EC-590ZW, EC-600ZP, EC-760ZP, or EI-580BT [Fujifilm, Tokyo, Japan]) with carbon dioxide insufflation. Conscious sedation was administered as needed via intravenous administration of midazolam or diazepam. Polypectomy was performed using a SnareMaster Plus (Olympus Medical Systems); Captivator, Captivator II (Boston Scientific, Marlborough, MA, USA), B-Wave, or DRAGONARE (Xemex, Tokyo, Japan).

Outcome Measures

The patients were categorized into two groups: those who continued with other APAs (continuation group) and those who discontinued these agents the day before the procedure (interruption group). Other APAs included in this study were cilostazol, eicosapentaenoic acid, sarpogrelate hydrochloride, beraprost sodium, limaprost alfadex, omega-3-acid ethyl esters, dilazep hydrochloride hydrate, and dipyridamole. Background patient characteristics, polyp findings, use of prophylactic clipping, intraprocedural bleeding, and DPPB were compared between the two groups. PSM was performed to reduce confounding factors. The incidence of thromboembolism was also investigated. Thromboembolic events were assessed within 28 days after polypectomy based on the in-hospital clinical course and postdischarge follow-up records, and were defined as clinically diagnosed cerebrovascular, cardiovascular, or venous thromboembolic events. An expert endoscopist was defined as a certified JGES fellow. DPPB was defined as the need for endoscopic hemostasis or hemoglobin decrease ≥2 g/dL due to hematochezia within 28 days postpolypectomy. For the calculation of DPPB onset, the day of polypectomy was considered as day 0. Intraprocedural bleeding was defined as active bleeding during the procedure that required endoscopic hemostasis. Polyp size and number were set at cutoff of 10 mm and 1 versus ≥2, respectively, based on previously reported DPPB risks. 11 12

Subgroup Analysis

All subgroup analyses were conducted using the full cohort prior to matching to preserve a sufficient sample size within each category because PSM inevitably reduced the number of analyzable cases. The subgroup factors examined included the type of APA (cilostazol or non-cilostazol), number of lesions (1 or ≥2), and the maximum lesion size (≥10 or <10 mm). Within each factor, DPPB rates were compared between the interruption and continuation groups.

Statistical Analysis

Quantitative variables are expressed as medians, whereas categorical variables are presented as total numbers and percentages. Pearson’s chi-squared test and Mann–Whitney U -test were used, as appropriate. The 95% confidence interval (CI) for the absolute risk difference was calculated using the Newcombe method. Propensity scores were estimated using logistic regression, incorporating the following 13 clinically relevant covariates: age, sex, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, liver cirrhosis, dialysis, inpatient versus outpatient setting, endoscopist experience, type of other APAs (cilostazol or others), number of resected polyps, polyp size, polyp location, and polyp morphology. Procedure-related variables that could occur after exposure assignment, such as prophylactic clipping and intraprocedural bleeding, were not included in the primary propensity score model. Covariate balance was assessed using standardized mean differences. Matching was performed using 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching with a caliper width of 0.2 standard deviations of the logit of the propensity score. All statistical analyses were performed using EZR (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University, Saitama, Japan), a graphical user interface for R version 2.13.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). 13 Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Patient Characteristics

Among the 763 patients identified, 257 patients were excluded because of a lack of detailed background or endoscopic data ( n = 10) and use of multiple antiplatelet therapies or use of other antithrombotic agents ( n = 247). Therefore, 506 patients were included in the analysis ( Fig. 1 ). The baseline patient characteristics are presented in Table 1 . There were 298 male and 208 female patients, and the median patient age was 75 years. A total of 57.1% and 23.5% patients had hypertension and diabetes mellitus, respectively. The reasons for taking other APAs were cerebral infarction, occlusive arterial disease, and others. Cilostazol and non-cilostazol agents were administered to 148 and 358 patients, respectively.

Table 2 shows the patients’ characteristics, polyp findings, and procedural findings before and after PSM in the two groups. Before PSM, there were significant differences in endoscopist experience, polyp size, and morphology (pedunculated or sessile/flat). These differences were adjusted after matching, and 384 patients were included (192 patients in each group). After PSM, there were no significant differences in age, sex, comorbidities, outpatient or inpatient status, experience of endoscopist, use of cilostazol or other agents, presence of prophylactic clips, number of resected polyps, and polyp findings. Subgroup Analysis

Subgroup analyses performed using the full cohort prior to matching did not reveal any meaningful differences in DPPB risk between the interruption and continuation groups across the examined clinical factors ( Table 3 ). When the cohort was stratified by APA type, the bleeding rates were similarly low in both the cilostazol and non-cilostazol subgroups. A comparable pattern was observed when patients were stratified by the number of lesions; neither 1 nor ≥2 lesions demonstrated a difference in bleeding risk according to the periprocedural management of other APAs. Although the risk of DPPB was slightly higher in the continuation group for those with polyp size ≥10 mm, the difference was not significant.

Discussion

Despite JGES guideline recommendations regarding the discontinuation of other APAs 1 day before high-risk procedures, the difference in DPPB rate between the interruption and continuation of other APAs has not been clarified to date. This multicenter PSM study demonstrated that compared to continuation, an interruption of other APAs was not associated with a lower risk of DPPB during colorectal HSP/EMR. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first multicenter study to directly compare interruption and continuation of these agents. Importantly, our findings represent the outcomes of electrocautery-assisted procedures (HSP/EMR), as we excluded CSP results with very low DPPB rates. 10 14 Our study provides evidence-based guidance for the peri-procedural use of other APAs in HSP/EMR.

A large case–control study of colorectal polypectomy reported no increased risk of DPPB even in patients taking aspirin. 5 In contrast, a prospective study of large colorectal lesions showed that the odds ratio of DPPB was 6.3 times higher in aspirin users than in non-antithrombotic agent users. 15 For P2Y12RA, a meta-analysis showed a significantly higher rate of DPPB in clopidogrel users than in non-antithrombotic agent users (2.7% vs. 0.6%). 16 Although several studies have evaluated the risk of DPPB in patients taking aspirin or P2Y12RA, few have examined the risk associated with other APAs. Cilostazol has been reported to be associated with a significantly shorter bleeding time after gastric biopsy compared to aspirin, ticlopidine, and clopidogrel 17 ; therefore, it is not associated with a high DPPB risk. However, a previous study has reported a higher DPPB rate in patients who continued cilostazol treatment during colorectal polypectomy than in those who did not receive antithrombotic agents. 7 The risk of bleeding associated with other APAs other than cilostazol remains unknown. Dipyridamole does not increase the risk of upper gastrointestinal bleeding. 18 Although only a small number of patients were evaluated, Watanabe et al. reported that the DPPB rate for agents other than cilostazol was the same as that for non-antithrombotic agents. 7 In this study, the continuation of other APAs, including cilostazol, did not result in a significant increase in the DPPB rate. We also performed a subgroup analysis stratified by cilostazol and non-cilostazol agents, and the bleeding rates were similarly low in both subgroups. However, the non-cilostazol group included pharmacologically heterogeneous agents with different mechanisms of action and potentially different effects on bleeding risk. Therefore, the results for the non-cilostazol group should be interpreted cautiously. Given the lack of a clear reduction in DPPB with 1-day interruption and the potential risk of thromboembolism, routine interruption of monotherapy with other APAs should be considered cautiously.

Most patients in this study underwent prophylactic clipping after colorectal polypectomy. While several reports have shown that prophylactic clipping is not effective for all endoscopic polypectomies, some have shown effectiveness depending on the polyp size, polyp morphology, and location. 19 20 21 Although the effectiveness of prophylactic clipping for DPPB remains controversial, its use in most of our patients may have led to the low incidence of DPPB. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted with caution, particularly in clinical settings where prophylactic clipping is not routinely performed. This may limit the generalizability of our results to centers with different clipping practices.

This study has some limitations. Importantly, the incidence of DPPB was very low in both groups, particularly after PSM, which resulted in only four observed events. This limited number of events suggests that this study was likely underpowered to detect small differences in bleeding risk between groups, and a type II error cannot be excluded. In addition, the 95% CI for the absolute risk difference was wide and included both clinically relevant benefit and harm, indicating substantial uncertainty in the estimated effect size and the need for cautious interpretation of the findings. Furthermore, this was a retrospective study with a relatively small sample size, which may have limited the ability to detect clinically meaningful differences in DPPB between the groups. Although no statistically significant differences were observed, these findings should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of no effect. In addition, we could not evaluate the bowel preparation quality, type of devices used for each patient, or the settings of the high-frequency electrosurgical unit. Because this was a multicenter study, procedural factors—including the possible use of both monopolar and bipolar snares and variation in electrosurgical systems across centers—may have influenced bleeding risk and contributed to heterogeneity in the results. Moreover, this study included only patients receiving monotherapy with other APAs; therefore, the applicability of these findings to patients receiving multiple antiplatelet therapies remains uncertain. This analysis was also limited to colorectal polypectomy and may not be generalizable to other high-risk endoscopic procedures. Another limitation is that we did not assess baseline bleeding risk using an established prediction model. Although several risk models for delayed bleeding after colorectal EMR, such as GSEED-RE2, 22 have been reported, these models were developed primarily for EMR of large colorectal lesions and may not be directly applicable to our retrospective multicenter cohort, which included both HSP and EMR.

In conclusion, among patients undergoing HSP/EMR, 1-day interruption of other APA monotherapy was not associated with a clear reduction in DPPB compared with continuation. Given the potential risk of thromboembolism with interruption, further prospective studies are needed to determine the optimal periprocedural management of these agents.
