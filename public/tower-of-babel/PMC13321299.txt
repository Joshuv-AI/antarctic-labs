Semiconducting Mixed-Valence Metal–Organic Framework with CuI/CuIII Based on Diethyldithiocarbamate Ligands
Authors: Tomoki Nishiyama, Ryo Matsushima, Shogo Nakajima, Kaho Hattori, Yuki Nakahashi, Takeshi Shimizu, Hirotaka Kitoh-Nishioka, Senku Tanaka, Wataru Kosaka, Hitoshi Miyasaka, Hirofumi Yoshikawa, Takashi Okubo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The unprecedented mixed-valent metal–organic framework (MOF) [CuI 4CuIIIBr5(Et-dtc)2]·CH2Cl2 (CuBrEt-3D; Et-dtc– = diethyldithiocarbamate), which facilitates solvent desorption, was characterized using single-crystal X-ray diffraction. CuBrEt-3D forms a three-dimensional framework featuring a planar CuIII center coordinated by diethyldithiocarbamate ligands, bridged by CuIBr units. This mixed-valent characteristic was confirmed through solid-state adsorption spectra, revealing a broad absorption extending to 2500 nm, attributed to intervalence charge transfer from CuI to CuIII. Remarkably, this compound exhibits significant air stability despite its mixed-valent nature. SQUID measurements verified its diamagnetic properties. CuBrEt-3D incorporates dichloromethane as a crystallization solvent within its pores, yet the solvent can be removed under mild conditions while maintaining porosity. Impedance spectroscopy demonstrated semiconducting behavior, and band-structure calculations clarified the carrier-transport pathways. CuBrEt-3D was employed as a cathode material for lithium-ion batteries, delivering capacities comparable to those of commonly used LiCoO2.

Introduction

In recent years, metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) have garnered increasing attention as inorganic–organic hybrid materials exhibiting high electrical conductivity, structural scalability, and compatibility with thin-film fabrication. Emphasizing these attributes, MOFs have also gained recognition as molecular electrode materials and are employed in secondary batteries, , sensing materials, , and organic field-effect transistors. Moreover, MOFs have demonstrated host–guest conductive responses to ubiquitous gases by leveraging their porosity and voids, highlighting their strong potential for future development as porous molecular materials with functionalities unattainable in conventional inorganic materials. Despite the vast number of MOFs reported to date, only a limited number exhibit electrical conductivity. , It is primarily because most cases have large energy gaps between metal ions and ligands with minimal orbital overlap, resulting in charge localization and hindering charge transfer. Additionally, the presence of pores or voids often disrupts carrier mobility within the framework.

Incorporation of mixed-valence states represents a promising strategy for realizing the transport of free charges, such as semiconducting , and metallic conduction, through dynamic electronic properties. According to the Robin–Day classification (Class I–III), a variety of mixed-valence coordination polymers (CPs) exhibiting semiconducting or metallic behavior have been reported. , In our previous work, we reported a series of CuI/CuII mixed-valence CPs that exhibit semiconducting behavior with narrow band gaps. These were synthesized by reacting CuII mononuclear complexes, which bear dithiocarbamate (dtc) ligands, with copper halides. The resulting CPs formed diverse frameworks in one-dimensional (1D), − two-dimensional (2D), , and three-dimensional (3D) structures, but all were limited to dense configurations without voids. Mononuclear Cu complexes with dtc ligands are known for enabling the stable isolation of the CuIII oxidation state, , which is typically considered highly reactive and thermodynamically unstable. This highlights the effectiveness of dtc ligands in stabilizing high-valent copper centers. However, all dtc-based CPs reported so far are confined to CuI/CuII mixed valence systems, with no extended structures incorporating CuIII species described. Dtc derivatives are ideal building blocks for porous, conductive mixed-valence MOFs, as metal ions can be bridged by sulfur atoms with large, diffuse atomic orbitals, facilitating the construction of multidimensional frameworks. If the CuIII oxidation state could be incorporated into extended frameworks using dtc ligands, it could lead to conductive MOFs with both high oxidative activity and multistep redox behavior. Nonetheless, incorporating the highly reactive CuIII into MOFs and achieving its stable immobilization remains challenging. Notably, reports of MOFs containing CuIII are exceedingly rare, with only a single mixed-valence 3D MOF based on 1,4-dicyano-2,3,5,6-tetrahydroxybenzene, which has strong covalent bonds with metal ions and high electron acceptor properties, as reported by Chen et al.

In this report, we demonstrate an exceptionally rare CuI/CuIII mixed-valence MOFs [CuI 4CuIIIBr5(Et-dtc)2]·CH2Cl2 (CuBrEt-3D; Et-dtc– = diethyldithiocarbamate), exhibiting semiconducting behavior. The presence of CuIII enables intervalence charge transfer (IVCT) from CuI to CuIII, resulting in a narrower band gap compared to CuI/CuII mixed-valence CPs. CuBrEt-3D is applicable to lithium-ion secondary batteries (LIBs) because the incorporated dichloromethane (CH2Cl2) can be removed while preserving the pore structure. Although interaction with the electrolyte induces partial framework degradation, leading to limited cycling stability, the coexistence of CuI and CuIII in CuBrEt-3D supports additional multistep redox processes, yielding a high discharge capacity comparable to that of commonly used LiCoO2 (170 mAh/g). These results highlight CuBrEt-3D as a novel CuI/CuIII mixed-valence MOF that uniquely combines porosity with semiconducting properties.

Results and Discussion

A mononuclear CuII complex bearing Et-dtc ligands [CuII(Et-dtc)2] used as a template unit for assembly reaction were prepared according to previously reported procedure. CuBrEt-3D was obtained as needle like crystals from a dichloromethane/methanol mixed solution of CuBr2 and the template-type dtc-based CuII mononuclear complex at −20 °C. The structure was determined by single-crystal X-ray diffraction, and the phase purity of the compounds was confirmed by PXRD, which agrees well with the calculated patterns (Figure S2b). Figure shows the single-crystal structure of CuBrEt-3D. The formula unit of CuBrEt-3D ([CuI 4CuIIIBr5(Et-dtc)2]·CH2Cl2) is composed of one [CuIII(Et-dtc)2]+ moiety and four CuI ions and five Br– ions, together with CH2Cl2 as a crystallization solvent. The valence of Cu2 ion of [CuIII(Et-dtc)2]+ was assigned to be CuIII from its geometry and bond lengths. The average S–CuIII bond length in the [CuIII(Et-dtc)2]+ unit (2.246 Å) is shorter than that observed previously reported CuI/CuII coordination polymers (average S–CuII: 2.305 Å), while is longer than those reported for [CuI 6CuIICuIIIBr7(Hm-dtc)4]·3CHCl3, [CuIII(Me2dtc)2]­(ClO4), and [CuIII(Et-dtc)2]­(FeCl4) (average S–CuIII: 2.208 Å). The central CuIII ion in CuBrEt-3D adopts a planar coordination geometry with a d8 electronic configuration, destabilizing the dx2–y2 orbital and resulting in diamagnetic behavior (vide infra). Each CuI ion coordinates to the S atom of [CuIII(Et-dtc)2]+ moiety and is further cross-linked by Br– ions to form S···CuI···Br chains along the c-axis (blue- and light-green-colored chains in Figure c and d). Cu1, Cu3, Cu4, and Cu5 are tetrahedrally coordinated, respectively. Also, the Cu···Br distance (average 2.48 Å) corresponds to the bond length typically observed for CuI···Br bonds, thus Cu1, Cu3, Cu4, and Cu5 were assigned as CuI valence. Adjacent S···CuI···Br chains are interconnected by bridging Br– 4 ions, generating a lattice in the a and b directions and giving rise to a porous framework (Figure c). The resulting pores encapsulate one CH2Cl2 molecule per formula unit introduced during synthesis (Figure a and b).

Magnetic property of CuBrEt-3D revealed it is diamagnetic, clearly proving the composition of the CuI/CuIII mixed-valence state in CuBrEt-3D (Figure S6). And also, the Cu 2p and S 2p XPS spectra are shown in Figure S7. The proportion of CuIII is lower than that of CuI, and its signal overlaps with the intense and sharp CuI peaks, making direct spectral deconvolution difficult. Nevertheless, the S 2p signal of the dithiocarbamate ligand exhibits a shift to higher binding energy compared to those in CuI/CuII coordination polymers and mononuclear complexes, supporting the assignment of CuIII. Figure (a) shows diffusion absorption spectra of solid samples of CuBrEt-3D (0.01 mmol; red), together with those of the mononuclear complex [CuII(Et-dtc)2] (0.01 mmol; black) as a reference, dispersed with MgO (80 mg). The absorbance is expressed using the Kubelka–Munk function f(R) = (1 – R 2)/2R, where R is the absolute reflectance of the sample. CuBrEt-3D exhibited a broad absorption band extending beyond 2000 nm, in which an unusual absorption band centered at approximately 1200 nm is not observed in CuI/CuII CPs or dtc-based mononuclear complexes reported previously. , This band in CuBrEt-3D can be attributed to IVCT from CuI to CuIII. By contrast, in an octanuclear CuI/CuII/CuIII cluster, the observation of IVCT from CuII to CuIII mediated by weak Cu–S interactions at around 1400 nm has been reported; however, CuBrEt-3D shows a blue-shifted band at nearly 1200 nm. This absorption band may be therefore assigned to an IVCT transition to CuIII2, mediated either by S, whose HOMO lies close in energy to that of CuI.(Figure S3) However, CuI–S bond distances are nearly identical, it is difficult to unambiguously identify from which CuI ion the charge-transfer transition originates. Furthermore, the bandgap (E g) of CuBrEt-3D, estimated from the absorption edge (Figure (b)), is approximately 0.67 eV, indicating an extremely narrow bandgap. This value is about half that reported for previously described mixed-valence CPs with 1D, 2D, and 3D frameworks, − suggesting potential for enhanced electrical conductivity through increased carrier density. The conduction band (CB) and valence band (VB) positions of CuBrEt-3D were evaluated using photoluminescence spectroscopy and UV–vis–NIR spectroscopy. The photoluminescence yield spectrum of CuBrEt-3D is shown in Figure (c). The VB energy level of CuBrEt-3D was estimated to be −5.30 eV, which is slightly lower than that reported for a 1D CuI/CuII CP. The CB energy level was determined to be −4.63 eV, consistent with replacement of CuII by CuIII. Most CuI/CuII mixed-valence CPs bearing dtc ligands exhibit semiconducting behavior. This is attributed to orbital overlap between the d orbitals of CuI and CuII and the HOMO and/or LUMO of the dtc ligands, which induces a narrow bandgap, and leads to antiferromagnetic interactions between CuII centers as well as electrical conductivity.

The electrical conductivity of CuBrEt-3D was evaluated using impedance spectroscopy because its narrow bandgap suggests potential charge-transport properties (Figure ). Impedance spectroscopy enables separation of frequency-dependent responses through equivalent-circuit fitting, allowing determination of the bulk resistance after excluding contributions from electrode contact and grain boundaries. Powdered microcrystalline CuBrEt-3D was sandwiched between brass electrodes with an area of 1.3 cm2, and pressure was applied to adjust the sample thickness to 521 μm. The frequency (f) response of the resulting cell was measured using an impedance analyzer under vacuum over the temperature range of 300–325 K. As indicated by the thermogravimetric analysis (TGA; Figure S2a), the impedance spectroscopy measurements were performed within the temperature range 300–325 K where CH2Cl2 could not be removed. Generally, the complex modulus spectrum (M* = M′ + jM″ = 1/ε* = jωC 0 Z*, where ε* is the complex permittivity, ω = 2πf, and C 0 is the vacuum capacitance of the circuit element) exhibits pronounced changes in shape and magnitude with increasing temperature. Accordingly, Cole–Cole plots of the modulus provide valuable insight into the electronic response of the material to an electric field. In particular, the diameter of the semicircle in the M′–M″ plot corresponds to the inverse capacitance, 1/C. The M′–M″ plot in Figure (a) exhibits two distinct features: a semicircle in the high-frequency region (right arc) corresponding to the bulk response, and a semicircle in the low-frequency region (left arc) associated with electrode contact resistance. The diameters of these semicircles remain unchanged with increasing temperature, indicating temperature-independent capacitance and an impedance response governed by the conductivity relaxation of CuBrEt-3D. Figure (b) shows the frequency dependence of the imaginary component (M″) of M* at different temperatures. With increasing temperature, the M″max peak shifts toward higher frequencies, indicating a reduction in the relaxation time (τ). This behavior supports the presence of correlated motion of mobile charge carriers. Accordingly, the bulk properties of CuBrEt-3D remain essentially unchanged, while the temperature dependence is primarily governed by variations in charge-carrier mobility. The peak frequency allowed straightforward evaluation of τ using the relation ωmaxτ = 1. The activation energy (E a) extracted from the relaxation process of CuBrEt-3D was 0.21 eV (Figure S4a), in good agreement with the value obtained from fitting (E a = 0.20 eV). Complex impedance spectroscopy (Z* = Z′ – jZ″, j = (−1)1/2) provides insight into the conductive properties of materials as a function of temperature and frequency. The resulting plots exhibit a partial semicircular arcs whose diameters decrease with increasing temperature, indicating typical semiconducting behavior. To quantitatively analyze the impedance response, the experimental data in the frequency range of 200 Hz to 10 kHz were fitted using the equivalent circuit model shown in the inset of Figure c with ZView software (Figure c). The equivalent circuit consists of two resistors (R 1 and R 2) and two constant phase elements (CPE 1 and CPE 2) connected in parallel. The bulk dc conductivity (σdc) was calculated from the R 1 value, corresponding to the bulk resistance in the high-frequency region, and plotted as a function of temperature in Figure (d). Consequently, the conductivity of CuBrEt-3D at 300 K was determined to be σ300 K = 3.31 × 10–8 S/cm. The activation energy (E a) for bulk electrical conductivity, could not be determined from the slope of the Arrhenius plots (ln σ versus 1000/T) because CH2Cl2 desolvation occurred above 340 K. CuBrEt-3D exhibits a narrower bandgap than the previously reported 3D CuI/CuII mixed-valence CP based on the dtc ligand; [CuI 4CuII 2Br4(Pyr-dtc)4]·CHCl3, suggesting a higher carrier density. However, the electrical conductivity of CuBrEt-3D is 1 order of magnitude lower than that of this CP (σ300K = 5.2 × 10–7 S/cm), indicating reduced carrier mobility. This reduced mobility may originate from highly anisotropic charge transport in CuBrEt-3D.

To clarify this behavior, band structure calculations were therefore performed for CuBrEt-3D. The band energy dispersion is shown in Figure . The band gap is estimated to be 0.239 eV, with both the valence-band maximum and the conduction-band minimum, separated by the Fermi level (red dashed line), located at the Γ point, indicating a direct-bandgap semiconductor. Calculations using the GGA-PBE functional with Grimme’s dispersion corrections in three dimensions do not significantly affect the band structure, while GGA functionals are known to underestimate the band gap. The largest band dispersion occurs between Γ and M2, implying the highest hole mobility along the crystal c-axis (the bright green and blue 1D direction in Figure ). This behavior is attributed to the large orbital overlap among [CuIII(Et-dtc)2]+, CuI ions, and Br– anions, which supports IVCT from CuI to CuIII and results in an electrical conductivity of ∼10–8 S/cm. As revealed by single-crystal X-ray structure, the ab plane is bridged exclusively by Br– 4 anions; however, the limited orbital overlap indicates that these linkages mainly serve to construct the framework, resulting in pronounced anisotropy along the one-dimensional direction. While single-crystal conductivity measurements would be ideal to confirm this anisotropy, crystals of adequate size for such measurements have not yet been obtained.

We tried to remove incorporated CH2Cl2 molecules from the pores. As shown in the TGA in Figure S2a, the CH2Cl2 can be removed without decomposition at around 345 K. However, vacuum heat treatment at around 345 K leads to a loss of crystallinity. This is attributed to the locally constricted nature of the 1D channels in CuBrEt-3D, as well as to the treatment temperature being significantly higher than the boiling point of CH2Cl2. In contrast, complete removal of CH2Cl2 while preserving single-crystal quality was achieved by applying a vacuum at room temperature for 1 week, as confirmed by TGA and elemental analysis of CuBrEt-3D after CH2Cl2 removal (Figure S2a). SCXRD measurements were also attempted; although the resulting data set was insufficient for full structural analysis, overall feature of CH2Cl2-free form (CuBrEt-3D′) was identical to that of CuBrEt-3D (Figure S1). The PXRD pattern of CuBrEt-3D′ agreed well with that of CuBrEt-3D, indicating no change in the framework structure (Figure S2b). Furthermore, magnetic susceptibility measurements of CuBrEt-3D′ revealed diamagnetic behavior over the temperature range of 1.8–300 K (Figure S6). These results suggest that CuBrEt-3D′ has basically the same 3D framework structure as a CuI/CuIII mixed-valence MOF. CuBrEt-3D, as well as CuBrEt-3D′, retains highly pure CuIII over the entire temperature range, regardless of the presence of CH2Cl2. The highly stable CuIII valence in this framework is clearly distinct from the formally mixed-valence CuI/CuIII 3D MOF reported by Chen et al., in which CuII electronic states emerge below 270 K due to valence tautomerism mediated by noninnocent ligands.

CuBrEt-3D′ was subjected to temperature-dependent impedance spectroscopy measurements under the same conditions as CuBrEt-3D. For a pelletized cell with a thickness of 260 μm, frequency (f) responses were recorded using an impedance analyzer under vacuum over the temperature range of 300–350 K. As in CuBrEt-3D′, two distinct features were observed. With increasing temperature, the peak of M″max shifted toward higher frequencies, indicating a decrease in the relaxation time (τ). In addition, the complex impedance plots exhibited incomplete semicircles, whose diameters decreased with increasing temperature, consistent with typical semiconducting behavior (Figure (a)). The experimental data in the frequency range of 200 Hz to 10 kHz were fitted using the equivalent circuit model shown in the inset of Figure a. The electrical conductivity of CuBrEt-3D′ at 300 K, corresponding to R 1, was determined to be σ300K = 1.31 × 10–7 S cm–1. The slightly higher conductivity compared with CuBrEt-3D may be attributed to the removal of CH2Cl2 guest molecules, which reduces guest–framework interactions and consequently decreases carrier trapping. In addition, a reduction in pore size may suppress carrier scattering, further contributing to the enhanced electrical conductivity. Furthermore, since CH2Cl2 desorption does not occur in the 325–350 K range, the activation energy could be reliably estimated, yielding E a = 0.48 eV, which is approximately half of the optical band gap.

CuBrEt-3D′ is a stable porous material (Figure S8) that exhibits semiconductive behavior and contains redox-active Cu ions. These characteristics satisfy the requirements for use as a cathode material of LIB. So, we put this material into an LIB cell. An electrolyte consisting of LiBF4 in combination with the low-coordinating ionic liquid 1-ethyl-3-methylimidazole BF4 was used. This electrolyte-solvent system prevents dissolution of active materials of CuBrEt-3D′. Figure (a) and (b) show the charge–discharge profiles, cycle performance of the discharge capacity, and Coulombic efficiency of the LIBs measured at a current density of 35 mA/g. In this study, the charging process was performed first. Although the initial charge was low (45 mAh/g), the initial discharge capacity reached 170 mAh/g, comparable to that of LiCoO2. Although capacity gradually decreased after 20 charge–discharge cycles, the Coulombic efficiency remained stable at approximately 100%. Furthermore, the two distinct plateaus observed in the discharge profile around 4–3 V and 3–2.5 V are likely indicative of multistep reduction processes associated with the central CuIII sites and the bridged CuI clusters, respectively. These assignments are also supported by cyclic voltammetry measurements of the LIB system (Figure S10). However, the PXRD patterns obtained after the first and second charge–discharge cycles exhibit overall broadened peaks (Figure S11a). This may be attributed to a loss of crystallinity induced by the intercalation of Li+ and BF4 –, or to the possible deposition of Li salts at the interface with CuBrEt-3D′. Therefore, the contribution of non-Faradaic processes, such as interfacial capacitance, cannot be neglected. On the other hand, no noticeable color change of the ionic liquid was observed after cycling, suggesting that decomposition products of CuBrEt-3D′ are unlikely to be the dominant origin of the observed capacity (Figure S11b). Furthermore, a control LIB consisting of carbon material with 10 wt % PVDF exhibited negligible capacity within the voltage range of 2–4 V (Figure S12). Taken together, these results indicate that the observed capacity cannot be solely attributed to side reactions or interfacial effects, but is predominantly derived from the mixed-valence CuI/CuIII MOF with multistep redox activity.

Conclusion

A CuI/CuIII mixed-valence MOF CuBrEt-3D was successfully synthesized using diethyldithiocarbamate as a ligand. This diamagnetic material exhibits distinct absorption bands originating from CuI–CuIII IVCT. Impedance spectroscopy revealed semiconductor behavior, and band structure calculations supported the existence of viable conduction pathways. Removal of the crystallization solvent from the pores under mild conditions enabled activation without compromising crystal quality, preserving the pore structure in CuBrEt-3D′. CuBrEt-3D′ demonstrated potential applicability in LIBs. These results suggest that integrating mixed-valence states with porosity may reconcile the conflicting requirements of structural space and electrical conductivity.

Experimental Section

The mononuclear metal complex Cu­(Et-dtc)2 was prepared following the procedure described in the literature. Reagents were purchased from Tokyo Chemical Industry Co., Ltd. and FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Co. All chemicals were used as received without further purification.

Synthesis of [CuI 4CuIIIBr5(Et-dtc)2]·CH2Cl2 (CuBrEt-3D)

A 150 mL MeOH solution of CuBr2 (1.34 g, 6.0 mmol) was added to a 150 mL CH2Cl2 solution of Cu­(Et-dtc)2 (0.74 g, 2.0 mmol). After stirring the reaction mixture for 5 min, it was filtered. Crystals of CuBrEt-3D suitable for X-ray analysis were obtained from the filtrate after 1 week at −20 °C in the freezer. Yield: 18%. Anal. X-ray Structure Determination

X-ray diffraction measurement of a single crystal for CuBrEt-3D was carried out using a CCD diffractometer (Rigaku Saturn724M+HyPix-6000HE) with multilayer mirror monochromated Mo Kα radiation (λ = 0.71073 Å). Single crystals were mounted on a thin Kapton film using Nujol and the data were collected at 113 K up to a maximum 2θ value of 59.28°. Among the 15581 reflections that were collected, 6233 were unique (R int = 0.04); equivalent reflections were merged. The linear absorption coefficient μ for Mo–Kα radiation is 11.459 cm–1. Transmission factors ranged from 0.128 to 1.000. The data were corrected for Lorentz and polarization effects. All crystallographic calculations were performed using Olex2. The crystal structure was solved by the SHELXT program using the intrinsic phasing method and refined by full-matrix least-squares minimization on F 2 with SHELXL. Non-hydrogen atoms were refined anisotropically, while hydrogen atoms were placed in calculated positions and refined using a riding model with fixed isotropic displacement parameters. The crystal parameters and experimental details of data collection are summarized in Table S2.

Physical Measurements

The magnetic property was investigated using a SQUID magnetometer (Quantum Design). UV–vis–NIR spectra were monitored using a U-4100 UV/vis/NIR Spectrophotometer (HITACHI). The impedance measurement of a powder-pressed pellet sample sandwiched by brass electrodes (diameter, 13 mm) was carried out using a 6440B (Wayne Kerr Electronics) series precision component analyzer in the frequency range of 100 Hz–3 MHz. The transient current for the photocarrier generation yield was monitored using a Tektronix TDS 2022B digitizing oscilloscope (terminate resistance ranging from 300 kW) and Keithley R6487 current integrator.

Methods for Band Calculation

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed using the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) functional based on the crystal structure of CuBrEt-3D after removal of the CH2Cl2 molecules. A single-point self-consistent field (SCF) calculation was first carried out using a 6 × 6 × 6 Monkhorst–Pack k-point mesh for Brillouin-zone integration. Subsequently, the total density of states (DOS) and projected DOS (PDOS) were calculated using a denser 12 × 12 × 12 Monkhorst–Pack k-point mesh and are shown on the right side of Figure . The band structure of CuBrEt-3D was then calculated along the high-symmetry points in the Brillouin zone determined using Seek-path software and is presented on the left side of Figure . In the DFT calculations, the kinetic energy cutoff was set to 60 Ry and electron–ion interactions were described using the Kresse–Joubert projected-augmented waves (KJPAW) pseudopotentials as implemented in the Quantum ESPRESSO package.

Fabrication and Evaluation of LIBs

CuBrEt-3D′ and conductive carbon TOKABLACK #5500 (Tokai Carbon) were ground for 30 min using an agate mortar and pestle. Polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) as a binder was added, and the mixture was ground for an additional 30 min. To enhance electronic conductivity, the weight ratio of CuBrEt-3D′, TOKABLACK #5500, and PVDF was set to 2:7:1. Decalin was then added to the ground mixture to form a slurry. This slurry was coated onto aluminum foil (20 μm thick) using the doctor blade method and dried overnight under vacuum at ambient temperature. Subsequently, the coated foil was cut into disc shapes (diameter: φ = 15.95 mm) and used as the cathode active material. CR2032 coin-type batteries were assembled in an argon-filled glovebox. The prepared cathode material was used as the anode, and lithium foil (0.2 mm thick, φ = 15.50 mm) was used as the cathode. For LIBs, a polypropylene separator (CS Tech, φ = 17.00 mm) was used. The LIB electrolyte was 1.0 M LiBF4 in 1-butyl-3-methylimidazole BF4 (Wako), an ionic liquid. Constant current charge–discharge tests at 35 mA/g were performed using the manufactured CR2032 coin-type batteries.
