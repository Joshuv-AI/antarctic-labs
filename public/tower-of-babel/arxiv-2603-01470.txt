Randomized Kriging Believer for Parallel Bayesian Optimization with Regret Bounds
Shuhei Sugiura, Ichiro Takeuchi, Shion Takeno

Abstract
We consider an optimization problem of an expensive-to-evaluate black-box function, in which we can obtain noisy function values in parallel. For this problem, parallel Bayesian optimization (PBO) is a promising approach, which aims to optimize with fewer function evaluations by selecting a diverse input set for parallel evaluation. However, existing PBO methods suffer from poor practical performance or lack theoretical guarantees. In this study, we propose a PBO method, called randomized kriging believer (KB), based on a well-known KB heuristic and inheriting the advantages of the original KB: low computational complexity, a simple implementation, versatility across various BO methods, and applicability to asynchronous parallelization. Furthermore, we show that our randomized KB achieves Bayesian expected regret guarantees. We demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed method through experiments on synthetic and benchmark functions and emulators of real-world data.

Introduction

Bayesian optimization (BO)
(
26
;
30
)
is a promising approach for optimization of expensive-to-evaluate black-box functions with a smaller number of function evaluations.
For this purpose, BO sequentially queries the input that maximizes the acquisition function (AF) based on a Bayesian model.
BO has been applied to a wide range of applications, such as AutoML
(
39
)
, robotics
(
3
)
, and materials informatics
(
49
)
.
However, in many real-world applications, observations can be obtained in parallel.
For example, if the objective function involves computer simulation and multiple computational resources are available, parallel execution of simulations is important to minimize wall-clock time for optimization.
Naïvely selecting parallel evaluation points using vanilla BO methods, which are originally designed for sequential evaluation, can result in wasting the querying cost since the input points often concentrate and provide redundant information.

Parallel BO (PBO)
(
38
)
aims to improve optimization efficiency by selecting diverse input points that effectively leverage parallel evaluations.
AF-independent approaches that extend vanilla BO methods to PBO by promoting diversity
(
12
;
1
;
13
)
are widely used due to their advantages, such as simple implementation, low computational complexity, and applicability to asynchronous parallelization.
A representative heuristic is kriging believer (KB)
(
12
)
, which promotes diversity by sequentially imputing predictive values as fictitious observations at input points currently under evaluation.
While these heuristics have been shown to perform well empirically, they generally lack theoretical guarantees.

In contrast to heuristic approaches, several PBO methods with theoretical guarantees have been proposed, including parallel Thompson sampling (PTS)
(
22
;
31
)
and batched upper confidence bound (BUCB)
(
8
)
.
Under standard regularity assumptions, these methods have guarantees regarding regret
(
40
)
.
However, such methods typically suffer from poor practical performance, lack theoretical guarantees for practical tuning of the confidence width parameter, or involve complex implementation.
This gap between strong theoretical guarantees and practical effectiveness motivates the development of PBO methods that are both theoretically principled and empirically competitive.

For efficient optimization, it is necessary to evaluate a diverse input set while avoiding redundant evaluation.

In these cases, RKB generates fantasized data from the predictive distribution at the inputs under evaluation.
Then, the next input is selected by maximizing an AF based on the model trained on both the observed (real) data and the fantasized data.
As a result, BO avoids redundant evaluation and ensures diversity among the evaluated inputs.

This paper proposes a randomized variant of the KB heuristic, referred to as randomized KB (RKB), shown in Fig.
1
.
Unlike the original KB, which imputes point estimates, that is, posterior means, as fantasized observations, the RKB instead uses one posterior sample for the ongoing evaluation points.
By construction, RKB retains the main practical advantages of KB, including ease of implementation, low computational complexity, applicability to asynchronous parallelization, and versatility to a wide range of BO methods.
Moreover, we establish upper bounds on both Bayesian cumulative regret (BCR) and Bayesian simple regret (BSR) for RKB when combined with several BO methods that admit regret guarantees
(
40
;
45
;
43
;
44
;
46
)
.
In particular, we derived the BSR upper bound independent of the number of parallel workers, as with
(
31
)
.
Furthermore, we show the effectiveness of RKB through experiments on synthetic and benchmark functions and emulators of real-world data.

Our contributions are summarized as follows:

1.

We propose a PBO method, RKB, that selects a diverse input set by conditioning on one random posterior realization for the ongoing evaluation points, inheriting the practical advantages of the original KB.

2.

We show Bayesian expected regret upper bounds for the proposed method in Theorems
4.1
,
4.2
, and
4.3
, which especially provide the BSR upper bound without dependence on the number of parallel workers.

3.

We demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed method via a wide range of experiments, including emulators of real-world data.

Joint Selection Methods.

Several studies have proposed PBO methods that select a set of inputs either by jointly optimizing a utility-based AF that explicitly quantifies a batch’s utility, or by sampling from a joint distribution designed to encourage diversity.

The empirical effectiveness of these heuristics has been demonstrated in multiple studies shown above, but lacks theoretical guarantees.

Distributed Selection Methods.

A third line of work is fully distributed PBO, especially for the case that even linear computational dependence on

Regarding PBO, several works have established regret guarantees.

Preliminaries

This section provides the background knowledge.

2.1
Problem Statement

We consider the optimization of a black-box and expensive-to-evaluate objective function
f
defined on a domain
\mathcal{X}
:

\bm{x}^{*}={\rm argmax}_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}}f(\bm{x}).

For this problem, we sequentially query
\bm{x}_{t}
for all iterations
t\in\mathbb{N}
and obtain an observation
y_{t}
, which can be contaminated, aiming for sample-efficient optimization.
As the generalization of parallel optimization, we consider the case that up to
Q
queries can be unobserved.
This setting recovers the vanilla BO with
Q=0
and includes synchronous and asynchronous parallelization with
Q+1
workers.

2.2
Gaussian Process Regression

We define the training dataset that is available at the beginning of the
t
-th iteration as
\mathcal{D}_{N_{t-1}}=\{(\bm{x}_{i},y_{i})\}_{i\in N_{t-1}}
, where
N_{t-1}\subset[t-1]\coloneqq\{1,\dots,t\}
is the index set of available data.
We denote
n_{t-1}\coloneqq|N_{t-1}|\geq t-Q-1
.
Furthermore, for notational simplicity, we define
\mathcal{D}_{t-1}=\mathcal{D}_{[t-1]}
.
We assume the following regularity assumption for the GP regression model
(
34
)
:

Assumption 2.1
.

For
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
r>0
, let
k
be a kernel on
\mathcal{X}\subset\left[0,r\right]^{d}
that satisfies
\max_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}}k{\left(\bm{x},\bm{x}\right)}\leq 1
.
Then, function
f
follows
\mathcal{GP}{\left(0,k\right)}
, and its input-output data is defined as

\begin{split}\left(\bm{x}_{t},y_{t}\right)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathbb{R},\quad y_{t}=f{\left(\bm{x}_{t}\right)}+\varepsilon_{t}\qquad\left(t\in\mathbb{N}\right),\end{split}

where
\varepsilon_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}{\left(0,\sigma_{\rm noise}^{2}\right)}
with
\sigma_{\rm noise}>0
.

Under Assumption
2.1
, the posterior distribution is also a GP.
For any
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}
, the posterior mean and variance of
f{\left(\bm{x}\right)}
conditioned on
\mathcal{D}_{N_{t}}
are given by

\begin{split}&\mu{\left(\bm{x};{\mathcal{D}}_{N_{t}}\right)}=\bm{k}_{N_{t}}{\left(\bm{x}\right)}^{\top}\left(\bm{K}_{N_{t}}+\sigma_{\rm noise}^{2}\bm{I}_{n_{t}}\right)^{-1}\bm{y}_{N_{t}},\\
&\sigma^{2}{\left(\bm{x};{\mathcal{D}}_{N_{t}}\right)}=k{\left(\bm{x},\bm{x}\right)}-\bm{k}_{N_{t}}{\left(\bm{x}\right)}^{\top}\!\!\left(\bm{K}_{N_{t}}+\sigma_{\rm noise}^{2}\bm{I}_{n_{t}}\right)^{-1}\!\bm{k}_{N_{t}}{\left(\bm{x}\right)},\\
\end{split}

where

I_{n_{t}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{t}\times n_{t}}
denotes the identity matrix,

\bm{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{t}\times n_{t}}
is a kernel matrix whose
\left(i,j\right)
-th element is
k{\left(\bm{x}_{[N_{t}]_{i}},\bm{x}_{[N_{t}]_{j}}\right)}
, where
[N_{t}]_{i}
is the
i
-th element of
N_{t}
,

\bm{y}_{N_{t}}=\left[y_{i}\right]_{i\in N_{t}}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{t}}
,
and
k_{N_{t}}{\left(\bm{x}\right)}=\left[k{\left(\bm{x},\bm{x}_{i}\right)}\right]_{i\in N_{t}}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{t}}
.

For continuous input domains, we leverage the following common regularity assumption of GPs
(
40
;
22
;
31
)
:

Assumption 2.2
.

Assume that
\mathcal{X}
is compact and convex, and
f\sim\mathcal{GP}{\left(0,k\right)}
satisfies

\exists a,b>0,\forall L\geq 0,\forall j\in\left[d\right],{\rm Pr}\left(\sup_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\left|\frac{\partial f{\left(\bm{x}\right)}}{\partial x_{j}}\right|>L\right)\leq a\exp{\left(-\frac{L^{2}}{b^{2}}\right)},

where
\left[x_{1}\;\cdots\;x_{d}\right]^{\top}=\bm{x}
.

2.3
Acquisition Functions for BO

BO algorithm
\mathcal{A}
selects an input to evaluate by maximizing the AF
\alpha:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
.
That is,
\mathcal{A}{\left(\mathcal{D}_{t-1}\right)}={\rm argmax}_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\alpha{\left(\bm{x};\mathcal{D}_{t-1}\right)}
.
Here, we describe three AFs used in our numerical experiment.

The first is UCB
40
defined as

\alpha_{\rm UCB}{\left(\bm{x};\mathcal{D}_{t-1}\right)}=\mu{\left(\bm{x};\mathcal{D}_{t-1}\right)}+\beta_{t}^{\frac{1}{2}}\sigma{\left(\bm{x};\mathcal{D}_{t-1}\right)}\quad\left(\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}\right),

where
\beta_{t}>0
is the confidence width parameter.
The second is EI
30
defined as

\alpha_{\rm EI}{\left(\bm{x};\mathcal{D}_{t-1}\right)}=\sigma{\left(\bm{x};{\mathcal{D}}_{t-1}\right)}\left(s\left(\bm{x};{\mathcal{D}}_{t-1}\right)\Phi{\left(s\left(\bm{x};{\mathcal{D}}_{t-1}\right)\right)}+\phi{\left(s\left(\bm{x};{\mathcal{D}}_{t-1}\right)\right)}\right)\quad\left(\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}\right),

where
\Phi
and
\phi
denote the cumulative distribution function and probability density function of the standard normal distribution, respectively, and
s\left(\bm{x};{\mathcal{D}}_{t-1}\right)
is defined as

s\left(\bm{x};{\mathcal{D}}_{t-1}\right)=\frac{\mu{\left(\bm{x};{\mathcal{D}}_{t-1}\right)}-\max_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}\mu{\left(\bm{x}^{\prime};{\mathcal{D}}_{t-1}\right)}}{\sigma{\left(\bm{x};{\mathcal{D}}_{t-1}\right)}}.

The third is PI from the maximum of a sample path (PIMS)
43
defined as

\alpha_{\rm PIMS}{\left(\bm{x};\mathcal{D}_{t-1}\right)}=1-\Phi{\left(\frac{g_{t}^{*}-\mu{\left(\bm{x};{\mathcal{D}}_{t-1}\right)}}{\sigma{\left(\bm{x};{\mathcal{D}}_{t-1}\right)}}\right)}\quad\left(\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}\right),

where
g_{t}^{*}
is the maximum of a sample path
g_{t}\sim p{{\left(f\mid\mathcal{D}_{t-1}\right)}}
. Hence, PIMS has randomness even if the data is fixed.

2.4
Kriging Believer for PBO

Here, we describe the KB
12
, which serves as the basis of our approach.
KB selects
\bm{x}_{t}
, incorporating the diversity of selected inputs, as

\bm{x}_{t}=\mathcal{A}{\left(\mathcal{D}^{\rm KB}_{t-1}\right)},\quad\mathcal{D}^{\rm KB}_{t-1}=\left\{\left(\bm{x}_{i},y^{\left(t\right)}_{i}\right)\right\}_{i=1}^{t-1},\quad y^{\left(t\right)}_{i}=\begin{cases}y_{i}&\left(i\in N_{t-1}\right)\\
\mu{\left(\bm{x}_{i};{\mathcal{D}}_{N_{t-1}}\right)}&\left(i\in\left[t-1\right]\backslash N_{t-1}\right)\end{cases},

(1)

where
\mathcal{A}
is an arbitrary vanilla BO algorithm.
As shown in Eq. (
1

f
in place of real data that have not yet been obtained.
Such conditioning on fantasized data prevents redundant evaluation.

2.5
Bayesian Regret and Maximum Information Gain

As a criterion of the performance of BO methods, we employ Bayesian regret

22

The BSR and BCR are defined as

{\rm BCR}_{T}=\mathbb{E}{\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}f^{*}-f{\left(\bm{x}_{t}\right)}\right]},\quad{\rm BSR}_{T}=\mathbb{E}{\left[f^{*}-f{\left(\hat{\bm{x}}_{T}\right)}\right]}\qquad\left(T\in\mathbb{N}\right),

(2)

where
f^{*}=f(\bm{x}^{*})
and
\hat{\bm{x}}_{T}={\rm argmax}_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\mu{\left(\bm{x};\mathcal{D}_{T}\right)}

In Eq. (
2

f
,
\varepsilon_{t}
, and
\mathcal{A}
. The bounds of BSR and BCR are represented using maximum information gain (MIG)
40
, defined as follows:

Definition 2.1
.

Let Assumption
2.1
hold. Then, for any
T\in\mathbb{N}
, MIG
\gamma_{T}
is defined as

\gamma_{T}=\max_{\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{T}\in\mathcal{X}}I{\left(\bm{y}_{T};\bm{f}_{T}\right)},

where
I
denotes Shannon mutual information,
\bm{y}_{T}=\left[y_{1}\;\cdots\;y_{T}\right]^{\top}
, and
\bm{f}_{T}=\left[f{\left(\bm{x}_{1}\right)}\;\cdots\;f{\left(\bm{x}_{T}\right)}\right]^{\top}
.

MIG
\gamma_{T}
is sublinear for commonly used kernels
40
;
50
;
20

\gamma_{T}=O{\left(d\log T\right)}
for linear kernel
k_{\rm Lin}{\left(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime}\right)}=\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{x}^{\prime}
;

\gamma_{T}=O{\left({\left(\log T\right)}^{d+1}\right)}
for Gaussian kernel
k_{\rm Gauss}{\left(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime}\right)}=\exp{\left(-\frac{1}{2l^{2}}\left\|\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime}\right\|^{2}\right)}
;
and
\gamma_{T}=O{\left(T^{\frac{d}{2\nu+d}}{\left(\log T\right)}^{\frac{4\nu+d}{2\nu+d}}\right)}
for Matérn-
\nu

k{\left(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime}\right)}=\frac{2^{1-\nu}}{\Gamma{\left(\nu\right)}}\left(\frac{\sqrt{2\nu}}{l}\left\|\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime}\right\|\right)^{\nu}J_{\nu}{\left(\frac{\sqrt{2\nu}}{l}\left\|\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime}\right\|\right)}
,
where
l>0
and
\nu>0
are lengthscale and smoothness parameters, respectively, and
\Gamma
and

are Gamma and modified Bessel functions of the second kind, respectively.

Algorithm
1

\mathcal{D}^{\rm RKB}_{t-1}
is defined as

\mathcal{D}^{\rm RKB}_{t-1}=\left\{\left(\bm{x}_{i},y^{\left(t\right)}_{i}\right)\right\}_{i=1}^{t-1},\quad y^{\left(t\right)}_{i}=\begin{cases}y_{i}&\left(i\in N_{t-1}\right)\\
g_{t}{\left(\bm{x}_{i}\right)}+\varepsilon^{\left(t\right)}_{i}&\left(i\in\left[t-1\right]\backslash N_{t-1}\right)\end{cases},\quad g_{t}\sim p{\left(f\mid{\mathcal{D}}_{N_{t-1}}\right)},\quad\varepsilon^{\left(t\right)}_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}{\left(0,\sigma_{\rm noise}^{2}\right)}.

(3)

Thus, dataset
\mathcal{D}^{\rm RKB}_{t-1}
contains values of a posterior sample path
g_{t}
of
f
in place of real data that have not yet been obtained.

As a result of this difference, we obtain the following theoretical and practical benefits.
First, from the theoretical perspective,
\mathcal{D}^{\rm RKB}_{t-1}
has the same distribution as
\mathcal{D}_{t-1}
conditioned on
D_{N_{t-1}}
by the construction.
This property is essential for the regret analysis in the next section.
Second, KB can be overconfident in the sense that the algorithm believes the point estimate, the posterior mean.
In contrast, RKB incorporates posterior uncertainty in a randomized manner, as in TS, thereby controlling the exploration-exploitation trade-off based on both posterior mean and uncertainty in practice.

Our RKB algorithm is similar to the hallucination believer (HB)
(
47
)
for preferential BO
(
4
)
.
HB leverages a posterior sample defined over the training inputs, which remains random because only preference data are observed, by which HB avoids a time-consuming MCMC method for the GP preference model.
The algorithms of HB and RKB are closely related, as both rely on posterior sampling and conditioning on hallucinated observations.
However, the motivation for using posterior samples differs fundamentally between the two approaches.

4.1
Theoretical Conditions for Base BO Algorithms

Our RKB can parallelize any BO algorithm
\mathcal{A}

To obtain regret bounds, we need the following condition of
\mathcal{A}

Condition 4.1
.

Let

(4)

where

Moreover, there exist
\zeta_{t}
and

that satisfy the following for all
\mathcal{D}_{t-1}\in 2^{\left(\mathcal{X}\times\mathbb{R}\right)}

\mathbb{E}{\left[u_{t}^{2}{\left(\mathcal{D}_{t-1}\right)}\right]}\leq\zeta_{t},\quad\mathbb{E}{\left[v_{t}{\left(\mathcal{D}_{t-1}\right)}\right]}\leq\xi_{t},\quad\sum_{t=1}^{T}\zeta_{t}=\tilde{O}(T),\quad\ \sum_{t=1}^{T}\xi_{t}=O(1),

(5)

where

Condition
4.1

(
40
)
, randomized UCB
(
45
;
46
)
, PIMS
(

)
, and EI from the maximum of a sample path (EIMS)

Note that, although Condition

(

4.2
Regret Bounds

First, we present the BCR bounds for finite and continuous input domains:

Theorem 4.1

(BCR bound for finite input domains)

Suppose that Assumption
2.1
and Condition
4.1
hold.

\left|\mathcal{X}\right|<\infty

, and
B_{T}
is the same as that of Lemma

Theorem 4.2

(BCR bound for continuous input domains)

Suppose that Assumptions

and
2.2
and Condition

Assume that the kernel is a linear kernel, a Gaussian kernel, or a Matérn-

,
C_{Q}

4.1

(

The key step in the proof is decomposing the regret into the regret incurred in the fantasized sample path
g_{t}
and the error between the fantasized sample path
g_{t}
and the objective function

as follows:

\begin{split}{\rm BCR}_{T}=&\underbrace{\mathbb{E}{\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}g_{t}^{*}-g_{t}{\left(\bm{x}_{t}\right)}\right]}}_{A_{1}}+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}{\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}g_{t}{\left(\bm{x}_{t}\right)}-f{\left(\bm{x}_{t}\right)}\right]}}_{A_{2}},\end{split}

where we leverage the fact that

and
\mathcal{D}^{\rm RKB}_{t-1}
are identically distributed.
By this decomposition, we can obtain the upper bound
A_{1}\leq B_{T}
, which can be seen as regret incurred by sequential optimization.
Furthermore,
A_{2}
can be bounded from above by
\tilde{O}\bigl(\sqrt{C_{Q}\gamma_{T}T}\bigr)

C_{Q}
incurred by imputing the posterior samples.
For the proof of Theorem
4.2

\frac{\pi^{2}}{3}+\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}s_{T}
.

Theorem 4.3

(BSR bound)
.

Suppose that Assumption
2.1
and Condition
4.1
hold.
Let
\bm{x}_{t}=\mathcal{A}{\left(\mathcal{D}^{\rm RKB}_{t-1}\right)}
.
Then, the following holds:

{\rm BSR}_{T}\leq\frac{B_{T}}{T}\qquad\left(T\in\mathbb{N}\right),

where
B_{T}
is the same as Lemma
4.1
.

See Appendix
D
for the proof.

Importantly, Theorem
4.3
achieves the BSR upper bound without dependence on
Q
.
This is the same rate as PTS
(
31
)
, DPP-TS
(
31
)
, and most sequential BO methods
(
35
;
45
;
43
;
46
)
except for the tighter high-probability cumulative regret bound recently shown by
20
.
This result implies that the theoretical performance in terms of the BSR may not be degraded by massive parallelization.
This property has been shown only for PTS and DPP-TS, which are fully distributed and joint selection methods, respectively, as discussed in Section

.
Thus, RKB is the first greedy PBO method that explicitly encourages diversity and achieves a guarantee comparable to that of PTS and DPP-TS.

The key observation for the proof is the monotonically decreasing property of the BSR, that is,

{\rm BSR}_{t}\geq{\rm BSR}_{T},

for all

From this fact, we can obtain the upper bound of the BSR by the average of fantasized regret as follows:

{\rm BSR}_{T}\leq\frac{1}{T}\mathbb{E}{\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}g_{t}^{*}-g_{t}{\left(\bm{x}_{t}\right)}\right]}.

As the objective function, we used emulators provided by Olympus

Consistent with the earlier experiments, RS and US exhibit inferior performance.
Moreover, the RKB and KB families, as well as PTS, are among the top-performing approaches across the experiments, whereas the LP family and BUCB perform less favorably, particularly on the fullerenes and hplc emulators.
These results indicate that RKB also demonstrates stable performance on real-world datasets.

6
Conclusion

We proposed the general-purpose PBO method, called randomized kriging believer (RKB), based on the widely used KB heuristic, inheriting the practical advantages of KB.
Furthermore, we showed the BCR and BSR upper bounds for RKB comparable to TS-based PBO methods
(
31
)
.
Finally, we demonstrated the effectiveness of the proposed method via extensive experiments.

There are several directions for future work.
First, since our RKB is a general-purpose algorithm for parallelization, extensions to various problem settings, such as multi-fidelity
(
41
;
42
)
, multi-objective
(
32
;
17
)
, and constrained BO
(
48
)
, are promising.
Second, extending our regret analysis is of interest; for example, this includes analysis in the frequentist setting and its application to the recent tighter regret bound in
(
20
)
.
Finally, as in prior work, our BCR analysis requires uncertainty sampling in the initial phase to avoid an additional
O(\sqrt{Q})
term.
Addressing this common limitation in PBO analysis remains an important open problem.

Lemma E.3
.

The following inequality holds:

\left(1+a\right)^{b}\leq 1+ab\qquad\left(a>0,b\in\left[0,1\right]\right).

(50)

Proof of Lemma
E.3
.

Let
a
be an arbitrary positive number.
We define

f{\left(x\right)}=\left(1+a\right)^{x}\qquad\left(x\in\left[0,1\right]\right).

(51)

Then,
f
is convex because the second derivative

of
f
satisfies

f^{\prime\prime}{\left(x\right)}=\left(\log{\left(1+a\right)}\right)^{2}\left(1+a\right)^{x}>0\qquad\left(x\in\left[0,1\right]\right).

(52)

Therefore, we have

f{\left(b\right)}=\left(1+a\right)^{b}\leq\left(1-b\right)f{\left(0\right)}+bf{\left(1\right)}=1+ab\qquad\left(b\in\left[0,1\right]\right),

(53)

which proves Lemma
E.3
.

Lemma E.4
.

The survival function of the standard normal distribution can be bounded above as

1-\Phi{\left(c\right)}\leq\frac{1}{2}\exp{\left(-\frac{c^{2}}{2}\right)}\qquad\left(c>0\right),

(54)

where
\Phi
is the cumulative distribution function of the standard normal distribution.

Proof of Lemma
E.4
.

The proof is the following:

\begin{split}1-\Phi{\left(c\right)}=&\int_{c}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp{\left(-\frac{r^{2}}{2}\right)}dr\\
=&\exp{\left(-\frac{c^{2}}{2}\right)}\int_{c}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp{\left(-\frac{r^{2}-c^{2}}{2}\right)}dr\\
=&\exp{\left(-\frac{c^{2}}{2}\right)}\int_{c}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp{\left(-\frac{\left(r-c\right)^{2}}{2}-c\left(r-c\right)\right)}dr\\
\leq&\exp{\left(-\frac{c^{2}}{2}\right)}\int_{c}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp{\left(-\frac{\left(r-c\right)^{2}}{2}\right)}dr\\
=&\exp{\left(-\frac{c^{2}}{2}\right)}.\end{split}

(55)

∎
