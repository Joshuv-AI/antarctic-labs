Boosting Inference with Guided Reasoning: Stochastic Exploration for Recursive Models
Andrew Corbett, Archit Sood, Anna Tzatzopoulou, Sai-Aakash Ramesh, Tim Dodwell

Abstract
Recent work on recursive architectures has shown that tiny neural networks can be surprisingly powerful on structured reasoning tasks. The trick is to model reasoning trajectories with a latent dynamical system. We argue that the inference-time behaviour of these architectures is best understood as approximate inference over latent reasoning trajectories, with deterministic recursion as the one-particle, zero-noise limit. We make this view operational through guided stochastic exploration : stochastic perturbations of the reasoning dynamics propose neighbouring trajectories, and the model’s existing early-stopping head reweights them online. The framework yields three label-free diagnostics: local stability, guide alignment, and cloud-token entropy. These predict, from inference traces alone, whether the procedure will help and which of its outputs to trust. On Sudoku-Extreme it lifts exact-solve accuracy from 85.9 % 85.9\% to 98.0 % 98.0\% without retraining; on Maze-Hard the diagnostics flag a misaligned guide, as validation performance later confirms. The same machinery thus characterises both when recursive reasoning has room to improve at the trajectory level and when the model’s internal guide can recover it.



1
Introduction

While large, compute-intensive models have dominated recent progress on structured reasoning tasks, recursive architectures
(
31
;
16
)

have shown breakthrough performance, matching large language models, with neural backbones of as little as
7M
parameters.
This combination of compactness and performance makes them well-positioned for AI deployment in settings with strict latency, privacy, and auditability constraints, where they can offer practical advantages over hyper-scaled alternatives.
We argue that what these architectures do at inference time is best understood as approximate inference over latent reasoning trajectories, with deterministic recursion as the one-particle, zero-noise limit.

Prototypical architectures, such as the Tiny Recursive Model (TRM) of
16
, already contain ingredients that suggest a richer inference procedure at test time, including learned early-stopping
Q
-heads and mixed-frequency latent updates.
These features have emerged predominantly through experimental iteration
(
31
;
16
;
22
)
, leaving open how they should be interpreted or used once training is complete.
We study this question through inference-time adaptation: given a frozen pre-trained backbone, how should additional computation be allocated across alternative latent reasoning trajectories, and when can we predict that this allocation will succeed?

We make this view operational without retraining. Stochastic perturbations of the inner recursion induce a proposal over neighbouring latent trajectories; the model’s
Q
-head, repurposed as a learned guide, reweights them online via a Feynman–Kac tilt. The deterministic recursion of
16
sits inside this framework as the one-particle, zero-noise limit, allowing direct comparison with the original model.

Background on recursive models

TRM-style architectures
(
31
;
16
)
apply a shared backbone
f_{\theta}
in two nested loops. An inner loop refines a latent state
z\in\mathcal{Z}
and an outer loop reads out an answer
y\in\mathcal{Y}
, both in
\mathcal{Y}=\mathcal{Z}:=\mathbb{R}^{L\times D}
(
L
sequence length,
D
feature dimension). We pair them as the joint state
h_{n}:=(y_{n},z_{n})\in\mathcal{H}:=\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Z}
at outer step
n
. In this work we consider the backbone parameters
\theta
fixed, and
f_{\theta}
as a pre-trained deterministic function.
For a fixed embedded input
x\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times D}
, the inner recursion, for
m=1,\ldots,M
, is given by the recursive formula

\displaystyle z_{n,1}

\displaystyle=f_{\theta}(x+y_{n}+z_{n}),

\displaystyle z_{n,m+1}

\displaystyle=f_{\theta}(x+y_{n}+z_{n,m}),

(1)

and the outer step, through (
1
), reads out the next answer from the final inner latent step
z_{n,M}
:

h_{n}=(y_{n},z_{n})\longmapsto\bigl(f_{\theta}(y_{n}+z_{n,M}),\,z_{n,M}\bigr),

(2)

Iterating (
1
)–(
2
) from a learned initial state
h_{0}=(y_{0},z_{0})
produces a deterministic trajectory
h_{0},h_{1},\ldots,h_{N}
, with decoded answers
a_{n}:=\mathrm{Decode}(y_{n})
at each outer step
n=1,\ldots,N
.

An early-stopping utility.

The architecture additionally carries a learned scalar
Q
-head
\tilde{q}_{\theta}:\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
, trained under detached BCE supervision as a binary classifier for whether
a_{n}
matches the ground-truth solution.
16
use it to trigger Adaptive Computation Time
(
15
)
. We read this as the model’s internal logit-scale estimate of halting success, and §
4
uses it as the natural score on which to tilt the predictive law.

Notation.

We identify tensors in
\mathbb{R}^{L\times D}
with their vectorisations in
\mathbb{R}^{LD}
and write
\|\cdot\|
for the induced Euclidean / Frobenius norm; the same symbol is used throughout §
4
–§
5
. For isotropic Gaussian noise
\xi_{m}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{LD})
the second-moment bound in Proposition
5.1
satisfies
\nu^{2}=\mathbb{E}\|\xi_{m}\|^{2}=LD
. We write
\sigmoid(y):=(1+e^{-y})^{-1}
for the sigmoid link function.

4
Guided Stochastic Exploration

We build on the deterministic recursion of §
3
to construct a path measure over latent reasoning trajectories: a stochastic kernel, generalising the inner-recursion (
1
), proposes neighbouring trajectories (§
4.1
); the
Q
-head reweighting tilts the predictive law toward correct solutions (§
4.2
); and a bootstrap particle approximation makes the resulting Feynman–Kac flow tractable (§
4.3
).

4.1
Exploration: the stochastic proposal

To widen the inner TRM recursion for exploration, let
\xi_{n,0:M}
be independent mean-zero noise variables and
\sigma\geq 0
the exploration scale. Define

\displaystyle\zeta_{n,1}

\displaystyle=z_{n,1}+\sigma\xi_{n,0},

\displaystyle\zeta_{n,m+1}

\displaystyle=f_{\theta}(x+y_{n}+\zeta_{n,m})+\sigma\xi_{n,m},

(3)

for
m=1,\ldots,M-1
, and the stochastic outer update

h_{n}=(y_{n},z_{n})\longmapsto\bigl(f_{\theta}(y_{n}+\zeta_{n,M})+\sigma\xi_{n,M},\,\zeta_{n,M}\bigr).

(4)

The proposal kernel is then the distribution induced by this random transition:

K_{f_{\theta},x}(A\mid h_{n}):=\\
\mathbb{P}\bigl(\bigl(f_{\theta}(y_{n}+\zeta_{n,M})+\sigma\xi_{n,M},\,\zeta_{n,M}\bigr)\in A\bigr),

(5)

for measurable
A\subseteq\mathcal{H}
, with implicit dependence on the inner depth
M
and noise scale
\sigma
through (
3
).
At
\sigma=0
the kernel collapses to the Dirac measure on (
2
), recovering the deterministic TRM update. For
\sigma>0
it widens the inner trajectory locally without changing backbone weights.

4.2
Exploitation: the
Q
-head tilt

Local exploration alone defines the proposal kernel (
5
); we select among proposals by reweighting with the
Q
-head
\tilde{q}_{\theta}
, instantiated as a Feynman–Kac tilt
(
9
)
of the predictive law.

Let
\pi_{n}
denote the law of the guided state after
n
outer steps. Propagation through the proposal kernel gives the predictive law

\pi_{n+1\mid n}(\mathrm{d}h^{\prime})=\int_{\mathcal{H}}K_{f_{\theta},x}(\mathrm{d}h^{\prime}\mid h)\,\pi_{n}(\mathrm{d}h),

(6)

which we tilt by the
Q
-head. Working on the log-probability scale,

q_{\theta}(h):=\log\sigmoid\bigl(\tilde{q}_{\theta}(y)\bigr),

(7)

where
h=(y,z)
, so that
G_{\beta}(h):=\exp(\beta q_{\theta}(h))\in(0,1]
is a proper potential for every inverse temperature
\beta\geq 0
. The guided update is the exponentially tilted
\beta
-law

\pi_{n+1,\beta}(\mathrm{d}h)\propto G_{\beta}(h)\,\pi_{n+1\mid n}(\mathrm{d}h),

(8)

a single mutation–selection step of the corresponding Feynman–Kac flow.

4.3
Finite-particle implementation

We approximate (
6
) by an empirical measure
\pi_{n}(\mathrm{d}h)\approx\sum_{s=1}^{S}w_{n}^{(s)}\delta_{h_{n}^{(s)}}(\mathrm{d}h)
. Propagating each particle through the proposal kernel gives the predictive mixture

\pi_{n+1\mid n}(h)\approx\sum_{s=1}^{S}w_{n}^{(s)}\,K_{f_{\theta},x}(h\mid h_{n}^{(s)}),

(9)

and the update therefore decomposes into exploration (§
4.1
) and guide-based exploitation (
8
),

Exploration:

\displaystyle\quad h_{n+1}^{(s)}\sim K_{f_{\theta},x}(\cdot\mid h_{n}^{(s)}),

(10)

Exploitation:

\displaystyle\quad w_{n+1}^{(s)}\propto w_{n}^{(s)}\,G_{\beta}\bigl(h_{n+1}^{(s)}\bigr).

(11)

Operating theory and diagnostics

5.1
Local tube stability of stochastic trajectories

We analyse the local properties of the inner recursion (
3
). The resulting predictions are measured in Figure
2
.

Fix
x\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times D}
and
h_{n}\in\mathcal{H}
, and suppress these, as well as subscripts
n
, from the notation. Let
z_{1:M}
denote the deterministic inner trajectory and
\zeta_{1:M}
the stochastic rollout of (
3
). Write
e_{m}:=\zeta_{m}-z_{m}
and

\tau_{r}:=\inf\{m\in\{1,\dots,M\}:\|e_{m}\|>r\}

(13)

for the deviation at step
m
and the first-exit step from the
r
-tube around the deterministic path, respectively, with the convention
\tau_{r}=M+1
when the set is empty.

Proposition 5.1

(Tube stability)
.

Assume there exist
r>0
and
\rho_{r}\in[0,1)
such that
z\mapsto f_{\theta}(x+y_{n}+z)
is
\rho_{r}
-Lipschitz on the
r
-tube
\bigcup_{m=1}^{M}\{z:\|z-z_{m}\|\leq r\}
, and that the noise variables are independent, mean-zero, with
\mathbb{E}\|\xi_{m}\|^{2}\leq\nu^{2}
. Then for
m=1,\ldots,M
the average in-tube deviations conform to the geometric decay bound

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|e_{m}\|^{2}\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{r}>m\}}\bigr]\;\leq\;\sigma^{2}\nu^{2}\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\rho_{r}^{2j}\;\leq\;\frac{\sigma^{2}\nu^{2}}{1-\rho_{r}^{2}}.

(14)

Corollary 5.2

(Finite-horizon tube-exit probability)
.

Under the hypotheses of Proposition
5.1
we have

\mathbb{P}(\tau_{r}\leq M)\;\leq\;\frac{M}{r^{2}}\frac{\sigma^{2}\nu^{2}}{1-\rho_{r}^{2}}.

(15)

Proposition
5.1
controls the deviation
e_{m}
while the rollout stays in the
r
-tube; Corollary
5.2
then quantifies how often that control applies. Together (
14
)–(
15
) identify the regime in which stochastic widening remains controlled. Fixed
M
, larger
r
, smaller
\sigma
, and stronger local contraction all tighten both bounds.
We evaluate these bounds empirically in Figure
2
, showing that the deviation bound is not exceeded and that the probability of remaining within the tube is high at moderate noise levels.

5.2
Alignment of the
Q
-head guide

Here we show that the BCE-trained
Q
-head is aligned with its training label in the integrated-covariance sense of (
16
) below.
The family
\{\pi_{n+1,t}\}_{t\in[0,\beta]}
in (
8
) is a one-parameter exponential tilt, such that bounded
\varphi:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies the tilt-derivative identity
(
9
)

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{n+1,t}}[\varphi(h)]=\operatorname{Cov}_{\pi_{n+1,t}}\!\bigl(\varphi(h),\,q_{\theta}(h)\bigr).

(16)

We call a guide
q_{\theta}

aligned
with
\varphi
on
[0,\beta]
if and only if
\operatorname{Cov}_{\pi_{n+1,t}}(\varphi,q_{\theta})\geq 0
throughout. Equivalently,
\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{n+1,t}}[\varphi(h)]
is non-decreasing in
t
on the tilting path
[0,\beta]
. Integrating yields an improvement measure against an ‘unguided’ baseline.

Lemma 5.4

(BCE alignment)
.

Let
Y\in\{0,1\}
and let
0<\eta<1
be a random variable with
\mathbb{E}[Y\mid\eta]=\eta
. For any strictly increasing
\psi
, the guide
q_{\theta}=\psi(\eta)
satisfies

\operatorname{Cov}_{\pi_{n+1,t}}\!\bigl(Y,\,q_{\theta}\bigr)\;\geq\;0\qquad\text{for all }t\in[0,\beta].

In particular, for
q_{\theta}=\log\eta
, the population BCE optimum among guides on which
\eta
is the conditional expectation of
Y
, is aligned with
Y
.

TRM’s
Q
-head is trained under BCE, so Lemma
5.4
applies with
\eta=\sigmoid(\tilde{q}_{\theta}(y))
. While Lemma
5.4
establishes alignment in principle (cf. Appendix
G.1
), in practice we want to test it from inference traces, both with labels (post-hoc) and label-free. For
H\sim\pi_{n+1,t}
, write

p_{n}(t):=\mathbb{P}_{\pi_{n+1,t}}(H\in A_{n})

(17)

for the probability mass on the success set
A_{n}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
under the tilted law.
We then introduce two scalar quantities derived from (
16
) that test alignment at outer step
n
(Figure
3
).

Class-conditional gap.

Here we define a post-hoc diagnostic to measure (
16
) in the binary-outcome case; see Appendix
F
for derivation and discussion.
For
\varphi=\mathbf{1}_{A_{n}}
, the covariance (
16
) factorises as
p_{n}(t)\bigl(1-p_{n}(t)\bigr)\,\Delta_{n}(t)
with

\Delta_{n}(t)\;:=\;\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{n+1,t}}[q_{\theta}\mid A_{n}]-\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{n+1,t}}[q_{\theta}\mid A_{n}^{c}].

(18)

Alignment on
[0,\beta]
is therefore equivalent to the scalar condition
\Delta_{n}(t)\geq 0
for all
t\in[0,\beta]
.
The sample diagnostic
\hat{\Delta}_{n}
requires success labels and is therefore a post-hoc score.
Figure
3
tracks (

) directly on saved particle clouds and shows the predicted mass shift emerging as soon as the

-head separates successful from unsuccessful trajectories.
Intuitively,
\hat{\Delta}_{n}
is the gap between the mean
Q
-head logit on solving particles and on failing ones. It accounts for the ‘space’ the guide has to discriminate.

yields the integrated bound

\bigl|\,p_{n}(\beta)-p_{n}(0)\,\bigr|\;\leq\;\tfrac{\beta}{2}\sup_{t\in[0,\beta]}\widehat{\std}_{s}\!\bigl[q_{\theta}(h_{n}^{(s)})\bigr],

(19)

where
\widehat{\std}_{s}
denotes the empirical standard deviation over particles and the supremum is taken over
h_{n+1}^{(s)}\sim\pi_{n+1,t}
.
A non-negligible
\widehat{\std}_{s}
is therefore necessary (but not sufficient) for a meaningful tempering improvement at outer step
n
. Indeed, the spread bounds the rate of mass shift
|p_{n}(\beta)-p_{n}(0)|
in either direction, so a small spread can rule out gains a priori.

In Figure
3
(bottom), the Maze-Hard
Q
-spread satisfies
\sup_{t\leq\beta}\widehat{\std}_{s}[q_{\theta}]\leq 0.04
. By (
19
), this caps the achievable mass shift
|\Delta p_{n}|
at
2\%
for
\beta=1
and
0.5\%
for
\beta=0.25
, well below the
5\%
oracle headroom on Maze-Hard. On Sudoku-Extreme, by contrast, the spread is large enough that (
19
) permits at least a
12.5\%
mass shift at
\beta=0.25
, consistent with the observed improvement (Figure
1
).

5.3
Token entropy as an uncertainty diagnostic

Modelling a particle distribution (
6
) also yields in-cloud uncertainty diagnostics at the token level. Since (
16
) holds for any bounded
\varphi:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}
, token-class marginals are obtained without modification to Algorithm
1
. Let
\mathrm{Dec}:\mathcal{H}\to\{1,\dots,C\}^{L}
be the deterministic decoder. For each token–class pair
(\ell,k)
, define
\varphi_{\ell,k}(h):=\mathbf{1}\{\mathrm{Dec}(h)_{\ell}=k\}
and
p_{\ell,k}(\beta;n):=\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{n+1,\beta}}[\varphi_{\ell,k}]
; this is the one-dimensional Feynman–Kac projection of (
6
) onto token-class
(\ell,k)
, with evolution governed by (
16
) under tempering.

Writing the marginal for each token
\ell
as
p_{\ell}(\beta;n):=\bigl(p_{\ell,1}(\beta;n),\ldots,p_{\ell,C}(\beta;n)\bigr)
, we summarise its dispersion by the normalised Shannon entropy, both at the terminal step and aggregated over outer steps
n_{1},\ldots,n_{J}
:

Terminal:

\displaystyle\quad H_{\ell}:=\frac{1}{\log C}\,H\bigl(p_{\ell}(\beta;N)\bigr),

(20)

Aggregated:

\displaystyle\quad\bar{H}_{\ell}:=\frac{1}{J\log C}\sum_{j=1}^{J}H\bigl(p_{\ell}(\beta;n_{j})\bigr).

(21)

We treat
H_{\ell}
and
\bar{H}_{\ell}
as risk-ranking diagnostics over token-level errors; empirical AUROCs and selective-prediction curves are in Figure
4
.

7
Discussion and broader impact

The original motivation for this work was to explore what an approximate Bayesian interpretation of recursive reasoning
(
16
)
might reveal about the elusive mechanics of the architecture. The investigation produced a Feynman–Kac-inspired theory that elevates the
Q
-head from afterthought to central guide for the selection of latent reasoning trajectories. The same machinery characterises both when recursive reasoning has trajectory-level headroom and when the model’s internal guide can recover it, a thesis we believe extends to recursive reasoning more broadly.

What surprised us was the immediate success of both ingredients on Sudoku-Extreme: modest stochastic exploration produced successful trajectories, and the
Q
-head correctly weighted particles towards these (Figure
1
). Maze-Hard was a step into the unknown as no prior work reports improvements there
(
22
, cf.)
. Stochastic exploration again exposed oracle-level headroom, but, as the theory predicted, the guide could not identify it.
The difference in character between these two benchmarks comes down to the guide. Improvement is “in there”, but the interesting question is: “how do we build the guide that finds it?”

We therefore propose three directions, each expanding the role of the guide.
(i) the guide as a first-class design object to handle structural constraints, custom-training objectives, or multi-guide compositions, rather than just the arbitrary choice of the
Q
-head;
(ii) train-time ramifications of the theory (Appendix
E
) and the inference-time compute efficiency of the particle-based method, including across-particle parallelism (Remark
4.1
) and scaling beyond the
S
-sweep of Figure
6
; and (iii) broader evaluation across reasoning benchmarks, including graph-based reasoning
(
26
)
, constraint-based language tasks
(
8
;
29
)
, and the more computationally intensive ARC-AGI benchmarks
(
2
)
.

More broadly, we propose generalisations of the framework beyond recursive reasoning. In AI safety, steering
(
30
;
24
)
has emerged as a promising approach for online adaptation of foundation-model behaviour. A guided variant of this approach brings a new, explicit mechanism for control. Rather than rotating activation vectors directly, a bespoke guide could identify sampled trajectories aligned with pre-trained constraints.

A natural next question is the transfer-learning capacity of small recursive models. If a guide
q_{A}
is used up to step
n
to produce
(y_{n},z_{n})
, can
z_{n}
seed another task, or can a second guide
q_{B}
redirect the same state towards a related solution? This question is especially natural for graph-based reasoning
(
23
;
26
)
and rule-governed language tasks
(
8
;
29
)
, where success is determined by consistent relational or logical constraints. If such transfer is possible, small recursive models would be more than compact solvers: they would become an example of a foundational reusable reasoning device, whose capabilities are generalist and, crucially, specialised online with a guide.

Appendix D
Trajectory contraction in token entropy

To understand the capacity for calibrated uncertainty quantification a little further, we ask the question: ‘how does accumulated entropy measure up against final-prediction entropy?’ The answer, we propose, sheds light on the evolution of uncertainty along the reasoning trajectories.

Specifically, we introduce a blunt measure of the relationship between the two statistics introduced in §
5.3
: the terminal
H_{\ell}
(
20
) and the path-integrated
\bar{H}_{\ell}
(
21

(22)

the per-token

between the trajectory mean and the terminal snapshot. Positive

therefore implies that the token marginal sharpened towards the final prediction.

Figure
7
reports the per-puzzle average of
C_{\ell}
. The histograms (
left
), separated by solve outcome, reinforce the utility of the diagnostic. Correctly solved puzzles, i.e. every token is correct, admit a clear positive contraction, as expected.

The more remarkable feature is the negative skew of the unsolved-puzzle histogram. With mean contraction near zero, the long left tail represents deepening entropy with the token marginal becoming less concentrated towards the terminal step. This implies that the trajectories continue to explore rather than settle on a stable incorrect solution.

The scatter plot (

) gives a bird’s-eye view of the same data, exposing the magnitudes of
\bar{H}_{\ell}
and
H_{\ell}
rather than only their difference. As expected from Figure
4
(

(0.1,0.1)
: below it, every prediction in the cloud is correct. Could these entropy statistics serve as calibrated guides in their own right?

Appendix F
Analysis of the class-conditional gap

In this section we dig deeper into the guide alignment diagnostic of §
5.2
.
We specialise the Gibbs covariance identity (
16
) to the binary-success case, yielding a log-odds derivative that directly links the class-conditional gap
\Delta_{n}
to the mass shift on the success set.

Fix an outer depth
n
and a measurable success set
A_{n}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
; in our experiments,
A_{n}
is the set of states that solve the puzzle by the terminal horizon
N
. Write
\varphi:=\mathbf{1}_{A_{n}}
and
p_{n}(\beta):=\mathbb{P}_{\pi_{n+1,\beta}}(H\in A_{n})
. Applying (
16
) with
\varphi=\mathbf{1}_{A_{n}}
and expanding the covariance gives

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\beta}\,p_{n}(\beta)&=&\operatorname{Cov}_{\pi_{n+1,\beta}}\!\bigl(\mathbf{1}_{A_{n}},\,q_{\theta}\bigr)\\
&=&p_{n}(\beta)\bigl(1-p_{n}(\beta)\bigr)\,\Delta_{n}(\beta),\end{array}

(23)

where (cf. (
18
))

\Delta_{n}(\beta)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{n+1,\beta}}\!\bigl[q_{\theta}(H)\mid H\in A_{n}\bigr]\\
-\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{n+1,\beta}}\!\bigl[q_{\theta}(H)\mid H\in A_{n}^{c}\bigr]

(24)

is the class-conditional mean gap in guide score between success and failure states. For
0<p_{n}(\beta)<1
, applying the logit chain rule

\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\beta}\log\tfrac{p}{1-p}=\tfrac{1}{p(1-p)}\tfrac{\mathrm{d}p}{\mathrm{d}\beta}

to (
23
) cancels the variance factor and yields

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\beta}\log\frac{p_{n}(\beta)}{1-p_{n}(\beta)}=\Delta_{n}(\beta).

(25)

Equation (
25
) is the labelled form of the one-number test in §
5.2
: tempering strictly improves the log-odds of success whenever
\Delta_{n}(\beta)>0
, and cannot help when the two classes are indistinguishable under
q_{\theta}
. The general alignment notion of §
5.2
reduces here to
\Delta_{n}(\beta)\geq 0
along the tilting path. The binary-success case is the natural setting for future custom guides; for example, classifiers identifying answers that lie outside a pre-trained constraint set (§
7
), or those that forecast future success (via a delayed training reward).

G.1
Proof of Lemma
5.4

By the tower property,
\mathbb{E}[Y\mid\eta]=\eta
gives
\mathbb{E}[Y\,\psi(\eta)]=\mathbb{E}[\eta\,\psi(\eta)]
and
\mathbb{E}[Y]=\mathbb{E}[\eta]
, hence

\operatorname{Cov}_{\pi_{n+1,t}}\!\bigl(Y,\,\psi(\eta)\bigr)\;=\;\operatorname{Cov}_{\pi_{n+1,t}}\!\bigl(\eta,\,\psi(\eta)\bigr).

(26)

Strict monotonicity of
\psi
gives
(\eta-\eta^{\prime})(\psi(\eta)-\psi(\eta^{\prime}))\geq 0
pointwise for an i.i.d. pair
(\eta,\eta^{\prime})
, and the standard identity
2\operatorname{Cov}(U,V)=\mathbb{E}[(U-U^{\prime})(V-V^{\prime})]
for i.i.d. copies gives

2\operatorname{Cov}\bigl(\eta,\,\psi(\eta)\bigr)=\mathbb{E}\bigl[(\eta-\eta^{\prime})(\psi(\eta)-\psi(\eta^{\prime}))\bigr]\;\geq\;0,

(27)

with equality iff
\eta
is constant on the support of
\pi_{n+1,t}
. The case
\psi=\log
gives the BCE-population statement. ∎

h_{n}=(y_{n},z_{n})

h_{n}

F_{\theta}(z):=f_{\theta}(x+y_{n}+z).

(28)

Then the deterministic and stochastic inner trajectories satisfy
z_{1}:=z_{n,1}=f_{\theta}(x+y_{n}+z_{n})
,
z_{m+1}=F_{\theta}(z_{m})
, as well as
\zeta_{1}=z_{1}+\sigma\xi_{0}

\zeta_{m+1}=F_{\theta}(\zeta_{m})+\sigma\xi_{m}
for

Write

E_{m}:=\{\|e_{1}\|\leq r,\dots,\|e_{m}\|\leq r\},

(29)

with the convention

Base case.

(30)

Fix

On

\zeta_{m}

\|D_{m}\|\leq\rho_{r}\|e_{m}\|
on

. Substituting into (

\mathbb{E}\left[\|e_{m+1}\|^{2}\mathbf{1}_{E_{m}}\right]\leq\rho_{r}^{2}\,\mathbb{E}\left[\|e_{m}\|^{2}\mathbf{1}_{E_{m}}\right]+\sigma^{2}\nu^{2}.

(33)

Since

, in particular

\mathbb{E}\left[\|e_{m+1}\|^{2}\mathbf{1}_{E_{m+1}}\right]\leq\rho_{r}^{2}\,\mathbb{E}\left[\|e_{m}\|^{2}\mathbf{1}_{E_{m}}\right]+\sigma^{2}\nu^{2}.

(34)

Iterating the recursion.
Define
a_{m}:=\mathbb{E}\left[\|e_{m}\|^{2}\mathbf{1}_{E_{m}}\right]
for
m=1,\dots,M
