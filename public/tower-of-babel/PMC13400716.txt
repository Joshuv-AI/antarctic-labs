OnabotulinumtoxinA in refractory postherpetic neuralgia: a case report
Authors: Dominic Bertram, Georg Haber, Dina von Rohrscheidt, Thomas R Tölle
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPostherpetic neuralgia (PHN) is a chronic neuropathic pain condition following herpes zoster, occurring particularly in elderly or immunosuppressed individuals. It is often difficult to treat and may significantly impair quality of life.Case ReportWe report the single-blinded treatment of refractory thoracic PHN with onabotulinumtoxinA (BoNT/A). A total of 200 IU was injected into the affected area across 40 injection points (5 IU per site), while one corner of the affected area was injected with saline as an intra-individual blinded comparator. A remaining painful area was treated with a further 100 IU BoNT/A 24 days later.ResultsPain intensity decreased progressively from severe pain with attacks up to 10/10 on the numeric rating scale (NRS) to 5/10 in the BoNT/A-treated area, while the saline-injected area showed no noteworthy improvement. Following the second BoNT/A injection, the remaining painful area reduced further to a coin-sized spot. The clinical effect lasted approximately 3 months and repeated treatment stabilised substantial pain reduction for almost 1 year.ConclusionBoNT/A may be an effective treatment option in selected patients with refractory focal PHN, particularly when established first- and second-line treatments are ineffective, poorly tolerated or contraindicated. Further controlled studies are required to clarify efficacy, duration of benefit and repeat-treatment strategies.Keywords: postherpetic neuralgia, onabotulinumtoxinA, neuropathic pain, pain management, case report

Background

Varicella-zoster virus (VZV) is the underlying cause of both varicella (chickenpox) and herpes zoster. In most cases, acute herpes zoster follows a mild and self-limiting course. 1 However, particularly in elderly and immunosuppressed patients, herpes zoster can progress to a chronic neuropathic pain syndrome that persists for months or even years after healing of the rash. This condition is known as postherpetic neuralgia (PHN). PHN is characterised by burning or shooting pain, ranging from mild to excruciating, and may be associated with allodynia. The condition frequently leads to reduced quality of life and withdrawal from social activities. 2 Importantly, pain in PHN can be difficult to manage and, in some cases, may be resistant to treatment.3–5

Botulinum toxin A (BoNT/A) has been investigated as a local treatment option for focal peripheral neuropathic pain, including PHN, with effects that appear to extend beyond muscle relaxation. Previous clinical studies and systematic reviews suggest possible analgesic effects in selected neuropathic pain conditions, while updated neuropathic pain treatment reviews continue to place established systemic treatments as first-line options.6–9 In this context, BoNT/A may be considered a later-line or adjunctive option for carefully selected patients with focal refractory neuropathic pain, particularly where systemic drugs are ineffective or poorly tolerated.

Case presentation

We report an elderly patient treated with onabotulinumtoxinA (BoNT/A) for refractory postherpetic neuralgia. The patient had spinocerebellar ataxia, with slowly progressing ataxia and dysarthria. He required a wheelchair and had cerebellar speech impairment, which complicated spoken conversation, although he had no cognitive impairment. The patient had no other acute or chronic conditions when he was first diagnosed with thoracic herpes zoster (Th2-3, Image 1). He was treated with oral acyclovir according to the standard of care and received pain medication following the WHO analgesic ladder from his primary care physician. The patient reported constant burning pain and itching with superimposed sharp, stabbing, agonising pain attacks reaching 10 out of 10 on the NRS (numeric rating scale). His quality of life, including daily routines, sleep and mood, was severely impaired by this pain syndrome, which failed to cease after remission of the rash.

Herpes Zoster eruptions during the initial manifestation.

Pain management was approached consecutively or in various combinations with the following substances: Ibuprofen (max. 1200 mg per day), metamizole (max. 1500 mg per day), pregabalin (max. 450 mg per day), gabapentin (max. 2400 mg per day), tramadol (max. 300 mg per day), oxycodone (20 mg per day) and eventually L-Polamidone (max. 22 mg per day). Also, a co-analgesic trial of amitriptyline (max. 50 mg per day) followed by duloxetine (max. 60 mg per day) was conducted with good effects on sleep and mood but negligible effects on pain. Dose limits were dictated by side effects (dizziness, drowsiness, nausea, obstipation) or the recommended upper dose limits.

Topical applications included local anaesthetics (lidocaine 4% gel, patch and subcutaneous infiltration), capsaicin patches (capsaicin led to worsening of the pain), menthol, propylene glycol, carbomer and ambroxol. An attempt at invasive dorsal-root-ganglion stimulation and CT-guided thoracic nerve-root infiltration was also conducted, with no substantial effect. Additionally, a multimodal pain therapy approach was followed, although this was limited by the physical handicap, speech impairment related to spinocerebellar ataxia, and language barriers.

Living through numerous unsatisfactory systemic drug trials, local therapies and interventions, the patient grew increasingly frustrated and spent a large amount of time on internet research about his condition. He got increasingly preoccupied with whether Botulinum toxin could be the treatment of choice for his long-lasting chronic pain disorder. He showed to be educated about the nature of chronic pain as well as the psychological and behavioural factors that might confound therapy refractoriness. In this context we also discussed with him the role of placebo effects in clinical trials. To account for this we proposed to conduct an injection therapy in terms of a compassionate-use trial for which we had to rely on a total of 200 IU BoNT/A.

Instead of reducing units per injection site, as 5 IU per injection site had been used in previous placebo-controlled trials, we decided to preserve the active dose and inject one corner of the affected area with saline. This was intended as an intra-individual blinded comparator area within a compassionate-use treatment, rather than as a validated substitute for a randomised controlled trial. The approach was chosen because available BoNT/A units were limited and because reducing the active dose per site could have risked a false-negative result. We recognise that such an in-situ comparator has important limitations, including possible regional variation in pain intensity and the inability to draw definitive conclusions from a single patient.

Treatment protocol

We administered a total of 200 IU BoNT/A intracutaneously across 40 injection points (5 IU per injection point) throughout the affected area (Image 2). The upper corner of the affected area was spared from BoNT/A treatment and injected with 0.1 ml saline per injection point. The patient was blinded to the contents of the syringe. BoNT/A and saline injections were well tolerated apart from injection-related pain, and no local or systemic side effects were observed or reported.

Results

The baseline pain syndrome was characterised by constant burning pain and itching, with superimposed stabbing attacks up to NRS 10/10 and substantial impairment of daily routines, sleep and mood. Starting 5 days after the initial injection, the patient reported a progressive decline in pain intensity. Ten days after injection, pain intensity dropped to 5/10 on the NRS in the area labelled by the patient as “area 2” (Image 3), which was the lowest pain rating since the beginning of PHN.

Illustration by the patient. (1) mild decline of pain intensity to 7–8/10 NRS, (2) decline of pain intensity to 5 out of 10 NRS, (Red dashed area) no change in pain intensity or pain quality. This area roughly corresponds with the sham-injected area (see Image 2).

Less pain control (7–8/10 NRS) was reported for “area 1”, and no noteworthy effect was reported for the red dashed area. This area roughly corresponded with the saline-injected region. The remaining area of tactile allodynia (Image 4) was therefore injected with an additional 100 IU BoNT/A 24 days after the initial treatment.

Remaining affected area as detected by tactile allodynia after the initial treatment. This area was then re-injected with BoNT/A (now open-label) 24 days after the initial treatment.

Following this second, open-label injection, the patient documented a sequential decline in pain intensity beginning approximately 1 week after treatment and a reduction of the affected painful area to a coin-sized spot (Image 5 and Figure 1). BoNT/A treatment was repeated around month 3 and 7 during the follow-up period. Although pain scores were not prospectively documented with the same degree of detail as during the initial treatment phase, review of medical records allows reconstruction of an approximate pain intensity, suggesting an undulation around NRS 5–6/10 throughout this period. The analgesic effect was sustained beyond the third treatment before gradually subsiding. Pain scores eventually returned to above 8/10 NRS, with concurrent re-expansion of the affected area (Image 6), prompting the patient to request discontinuation of BoNT/A treatment.

Discussion

The patient-reported decline in NRS rating reflected the spatial division between areas treated with BoNT/A and the saline-injected comparator area. Although this observation is consistent with a local treatment effect, it must be interpreted cautiously because this was a single case and the comparator area was not part of a validated randomised trial design.

According to several studies and meta-analyses BoNT/A seems to be a promising option in neuropathic pain conditions, where other pharmacological treatment options show no effect.6–8 BoNT/A’s mechanism of action in pain has been shown to unfold beyond the inhibition of SNAP-25 through peripheral and central desensitisation and the suppression of neurogenic inflammation. It has been shown to block the release of CGRP, substance-P, neuropeptide Y, COX-2 and IL-6 and might exhibit a direct anti-neuropathic effect by binding to calcium channels and the TRPV-1 receptor.7,10–15

The use of BoNT/A in pain conditions is continuously expanding and its antinociceptive effect has been widely recognised.6,8,10,11,16,17 However, at present the only approved indication in pain medicine is chronic migraine, and international guidance and reviews still position BoNT/A as a later-line treatment option for neuropathic pain rather than as a first-line therapy.9,18 Accordingly, as in our case, financing of compassionate-use treatment can be challenging. Nevertheless, for elderly and frail patients in whom anticonvulsants, opioids and NSAIDs may be poorly tolerated or contraindicated, BoNT/A may represent a well-tolerated option because of limited systemic side effects and drug interactions. It should therefore be considered as a possible treatment option in selected cases of refractory focal neuropathic pain such as PHN. In our case, repeated BoNT/A injections were associated with pain reduction for almost 1 year, yet further options in handling a waning BoNT/A effect (treatment pause, repeat treatment, dose adjustment or discontinuation) remain unclear. There is little evidence about the efficacy and limitations of BoNT/A treatment beyond a follow-up period of 24 weeks 19 Immunological mechanisms, including anti-BoNT/A neutralising antibodies, might be operational, as described as a complication of long-term use. 20 Continuous deafferentation has been demonstrated for PHN, which might block retrograde axonal transport and prevent the emergence of central effects of BoNT/A.21,22 It is not well understood whether dose adjustments can foster long-term effects of BoNT/A in these cases.

Conclusion

This single-case clinical observation suggests that BoNT/A may reduce pain in refractory postherpetic neuralgia. In this severe case of chronic pain disorder with somatic and psychological factors, the patient was strongly oriented towards a BoNT/A trial, so placebo confounding had to be anticipated. Shortage of available BoNT/A units and the patient’s willingness to undergo compassionate-use treatment allowed us to include an intra-individual saline-injected comparator area. The spatially differentiated response is consistent with a treatment effect, but conclusions remain limited by the single-case design and the non-validated comparator approach. Whilst BoNT/A remains a later-line treatment option for neuropathic pain in current international guidance, its potential in refractory focal cases such as the one described here merits further investigation.5,9,18

We conclude that further controlled studies of BoNT/A in refractory focal PHN are warranted to define efficacy, duration of benefit, repeat-treatment strategies and long-term limitations.

Ethical considerations

This report describes an individual compassionate-use/off-label treatment decision made in routine clinical care for a patient with refractory PHN after established treatments had failed or were poorly tolerated. A prospective research protocol was not submitted to the local ethics committee and a formal ethics committee statement was therefore not obtained. The patient gave informed consent to the proposed BoNT/A treatment, including the blinded saline-injected comparator area, and gave consent for publication of the anonymised clinical information and patient-provided images. No identifiable patient information is included in this report.
