1.0 INTRODUCTION
This technical basis update focuses on the design life of the SAVY-4000® (hereafter “SAVY”) series
of nuclear material storage containers―used for storing, transporting, and handling special nuclear
material outside of a glovebox―previously approved under DOE Manual 441.1-1, Nuclear Material
Packaging Manual. The SAVY is approved for storing all authorized contents for a period of 15 years.
This lifetime extension technical basis is established on compiled technical data from on-going SAVY
and Hagan surveillance programs, destructive analysis results, accelerated aging studies, and
performance testing, in line with the regulatory rigor of DOE M441.1-1 and will recommend an
additional design life extension of ten years, for a total system design life of 25 years. This applies to
Los Alamos National Laboratory with the option for additional sites to adopt this extension with the
approval of their local field element managers.
With design life performance as the primary focus of this addendum, it is recommended that the SAVY
SAR [1] and the previous lifetime extension technical basis [2] be reviewed in conjunction with this
addendum to gain a complete understanding of the documented safety analysis to date against all other
container performance criteria previously reviewed and approved. Relevant to this life extension
request are those containment boundary components that have the potential to be directly in contact
with nuclear material and include the 316L stainless steel deep drawn body with welded collar, the
316L lid with aluminosilicate Fiberfrax® filter assembly, and the Viton®-based O-ring. As with the
10-year life extension approval, the life-limiting components (LLCs) of concern are the 316L
components directly exposed to corrosive gases causing corrosion, with the mechanisms and severity
of corrosion requiring evaluation to support an additional ten-year extension. Aspects of container
performance that impact design life, such as corrosion resistance, are folded into the discussion as
required, to provide evidence supporting an additional ten-year extension. Also addressed are
validation initiatives on other LLCs such as the O-ring and filter that provide assurance over time of
the predicted 40-year life and Non-LLCs such as the components outside of the containment boundary
(e.g., the locking ring).

2.0 DESIGN LIFE
This section provides an update to the technical basis of the SAVY design life, focusing on the
components and associated performance requirements that limit overall service life. Design life for the
SAVY containment system is evaluated for the system as a whole and is limited by the most restrictive
LLCs, while the ability of the various LLCs to maintain integrity over time and meet the performance
basis is also considered. Design life, as a performance measure, is the primary factor addressed in this
technical basis.
All other performance requirements have been determined by analysis or tests in previous
documentation and, if not discussed, continue to be validated by current surveillance results. This point
is summarized in Section 2.1, Surveillance Program Results to Date, with additional detail available in
the annual surveillance reports referenced herein. Consolidated mapping of performance requirements
and surveillance acceptance criteria is contained in Table 1 below.
Additionally, design life recommendations within this technical basis document are identified on a
component basis. This component-level approach serves as an administrative convenience and does not
imply any operational maintenance requirement (i.e., if a component is replaced it is not further
increasing the lifespan of a container). That is, less restrictive LLC and non-LLC lifetime justification
will continue in annual surveillance reports if no trend indicates accelerated degradation relative to
original lifetime predictions. If a trend indicates accelerated degradation, then an LLC or non-LLC
would be re-evaluated for a reduced design life in subsequent lifetime extension reports and
reclassified as the most restrictive LLC if the new design life falls below the previously most
restrictive LLC. However, this does not imply component-level tracking, rather system-level tracking
via the SAVY serial number which defines the start of the design life in the nuclear material
accountability database.

2.1 Surveillance Program Results to Date
The DOE-approved surveillance program for the SAVY at LANL includes the development of an
annual surveillance plan, execution of planned surveillance activities, and documentation of
surveillance findings in a report for continued assessment of in-service nuclear material storage
containers. Each year, the surveillance plan is updated based on the previous year’s surveillance
results, laboratory results, and lifetime implications to continuously improve the surveillance program
as a whole. Surveillance opportunities include randomly sampled items (newly added since the last
technical basis update), engineering judgement items, trending items, and items of opportunity (i.e.,
identified abnormal conditions that were unpacked for evaluation). Another opportunity was the
surveilling of containers identified with a higher, but compliant leak rate due to lids being
manufactured from stainless steel bar stock which included impurity stringers. This was resolved in
fiscal year (FY)26 with the inspection and disposition of the final impurity stringer container, which
will be reported in the forthcoming FY26 surveillance report. All containers are subjected to a rigorous
array of tests, including container integrity evaluations (i.e., visual inspections and photographs),
helium leak rate testing, O-ring durometer and compression set testing, filter efficiency, pressure drop
testing, and filter water penetration testing. An additional enhancement since the previous lifetime
extension document included better recording of dose rate information through radiation tag imaging
and/or unpackaging form documentation when practical [2, 3]. This was done in lieu of previously
committed to direct filter measurements given that the filter membrane was proven to be unnecessary
for water ingress performance requirements (see Section 2.2.2). Destructive evaluation is utilized as
needed to explore LLCs further, validating integrity and also supporting life-extension efforts. At its
core, the goal of the surveillance program is to validate the design life over time by targeting the items
believed to create the greatest challenge to the integrity of the container and targeting items of specific
character to advance understanding of container performance.
A total of nine surveillance plans and reports have been completed to date [4–21]. In more recent
surveillance reports, a trending analysis of all test data is included. These trending analyses have
shown no statistically significant change in container performance (helium leak rate, O-ring durometer
and compression set, and filter performance) relative to non-destructive testing results (i.e., non-
corrosion related tests) and are consistent with the accelerated aging studies that originally calculated a
40-year design life [22]. Additionally, container integrity inspections have shown no O-ring durability
issues with the container, which continues to be consistent with the original O-ring durability study
[23]. Corrosion continues to be observed on surveillance containers containing a variety of nuclear
materials. Identification and non-destructive measurement of corrosion in a quantitative manner
continues to be a challenge for the surveillance program, though development is in progress for
multiple capabilities.
Until these non-destructive techniques are implemented, containers are selected for destructive
examination and analyzed for the severity of general corrosion, presence of stress corrosion cracking
(SCC), as well as the density, size (diameter), and depth of any corrosion pits. Additional discussion on
general corrosion, SCC, and pitting may be found in Section 2.4 below.

2.2 Degradation by Radiation and Thermal Exposure of the O-ring and Filter
 Components
Previous lifetime extension work similarly supports a conservative 40-year service life for O-rings and
filters [22]. However, new techniques and a deeper knowledge of stored content interaction with the
container necessitate a review of the validity of the accelerated aging studies. Continued surveillance
and additional characterization studies have been performed to validate these conclusions, including an
in-service O-ring chemical characterization effort and degraded polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) water-
ingress performance testing.

2.2.1 O-ring Changes in Service
Although non-destructive measurement techniques for the O-ring, namely compression set and
durometer measurements, provide invaluable data for assessing sealing performance over time, they do
not fully describe possible chemical degradation effects within the O-ring that could lead to
performance degradation in the future. To further ongoing research in this area, O-ring chemical
characterization through destructive examination techniques were pursued for an initial group of six
in-service O-rings alongside a pristine O-ring as a reference. These six O-rings, with varying levels of
radiation exposure from an approximate minimum and maximum dose of 1.58E2 rad and 7.79E3 rad
respectively, and the reference, were analyzed using attenuated total reflection Fourier transform
infrared spectroscopy, gel fraction measurements, nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy, size
exclusion chromatography, thermogravimetry analysis, differential scanning calorimetry, and swelling
ratio measurements to develop a comprehensive chemical characterization of aged, in-service O-rings
[24]. Results from all analysis techniques showed effectively no change between aged, in-service
O-rings and unaged, pristine O-rings, similar to that of the non-destructive surveillance results [21].
Chemical characterization provides additional information to the mechanical testing conducted that, in
turn, enables a more complete derivation of O-ring health. Inclusion of this characterization into future
surveillance efforts for a sampling of in-service O-rings is planned. This effort will ensure the ability of
the surveillance program to continue to validate the predicted life of 40 years.

2.2.2 Filter Evaluation
Concerns regarding the radiation resistance of PTFE remain relevant in nuclear facility operations due
to historical experiences with accelerated degradation and were voiced in the last design life extension
technical basis request [2]. Separate from these concerns, a facility-related event on crediting the
performance of the SAVY PTFE membrane for water-resistance against criticality, motivated further
investigation as to its performance when subjected to more challenging environments and a more
progressed state of degradation.

2.2.2 Filter Evaluation (continued)
Aside from its water-shedding function, the PTFE membrane is the credited feature that limits water
ingress to levels acceptable under facility requirements, thereby preventing interaction with the nuclear
material that could pose a criticality concern [26]. Testing performed against this event involved the
mechanical removal of the PTFE membrane from the external filter holes of a set of SAVY containers
without excessively damaging the Fiberfrax® filter material below it [26]. These modified containers,
representing a worst-case bounding condition, were then subjected to the criticality safety water
ingress test conditions involving full water submersion for the required period of time [27]. After the
specified testing interval, all containers were shown to fall below, and therefore comply with, the
criticality safety water ingress limit criteria without the influence of the PTFE membrane [28].
Therefore, the PTFE membrane has been shown to not contribute to the SAVY design’s ability to
satisfy performance requirements at this time and will no longer be considered relevant to design life
considerations.
The aluminosilicate filter material has not exhibited changes that impact filter performance across the
surveilled container population. A filter test system to evaluate filter efficiency is employed to
benchmark efficiency of the entire filter assembly (aluminosilicate media with the PTFE membrane)
during each surveillance activity. No statistically significant changes were observed during testing over
several years of container surveillance [21]. Evaluations made on this material using literature review
and dose estimates at the outset of the SAVY’s implementation provide additional support to the
current findings [1].

2.3 Recommendation on O-ring and Filter Component Design Life
The O-ring design life is recommended to be extended to at least 40 years based on continued
surveillance trending observations showing no degradation in performance relative to the lifetime
established in the SAVY-4000 O-Ring and Filter Lifetime Extension Report, as well as new chemical-
characterization results demonstrating no detectable differences between pristine and in-service
O-rings. O-rings from surveillance will continue to be subjected to chemical characterization year-
over-year as a new addition to the annual surveillance program to ensure continued performance
assurance. Surveillance rigor and trending analysis will continue at least at this level, to validate the
design life up to 40 years, and any adverse results will prompt additional investigation and possible
design life re-evaluation, if necessary. Additional surveillances may be included on an opportunistic
basis when containers are selected for evaluation against other performance criteria, such as container
integrity and corrosion.
The filter design life is recommended to be extended to at least 40 years based on continued
surveillance trending results showing no degradation in filter performance relative to the lifetime
established in the SAVY-4000 O-Ring and Filter Lifetime Extension Report, as well as a recent facility-
driven study demonstrating that the PTFE membrane does not contribute to meeting the container’s
performance requirements. Therefore, the PTFE membrane should no longer be considered as a vital
component to the SAVY design. Surveillance activities will continue for the filter to validate the design
life of the filter media itself, and any adverse results will prompt additional investigation and possible
design life re-evaluation, if necessary.

2.4 Continued Evaluations of the Corrosion Resistance of 316L Metal Components
The SAVY container is comprised of several stainless steel components, of which only three, the
container lid, collar, and body, face the interior storage environment. Other components, such as the
locking ring, handle, set screws, springs, and pins that form part of the locking mechanism, face the
external storage environment. To date, no corrosion of these externally facing components has been
observed as a direct result of the internal nor external storage environment.
The following discussion focuses primarily on the container body. All stainless steel components that
comprise the containment boundary are fabricated from 316L. However, the container body has the
smallest nominal thickness compared to the collar and lid and establishes the life-limiting threshold for
containment performance of the SAVY. It is also important to note that the container body is subject to
a harsher set of corrosion conditions as the main sources of corrosive species in the container (i.e., the
bag-out bag and stored material) are in contact with or are in close proximity to the container body.

2.4.1 General Corrosion
General corrosion is most apparent during visual inspection of containers. Macroscopically, the
appearance of this form of corrosion is observed by the resulting continuous or semi-continuous
patches of rust-like iron oxide features on the interior surface of SAVY containers. Direct
measurements of the wall thickness loss due to general corrosion using ultrasound thickness
measurements, structured light stereomicroscopy, and laser confocal microscopy have not shown a
statistically significant change in wall thickness arising from general corrosion when compared to
general container wall variability found in the manufacturing process [29]. General corrosion, observed
to date, falls within the “error band” of the general container wall variability. However, this form of
corrosion, while less severe than pitting corrosion, may have an additive effect on the severity of
pitting and will therefore continue to be tracked and measured moving forward in the context of
continuing to evaluate the growth and propagation of pitting.

2.4.2 Intragranular SCC
Visual inspection of surveillance containers and destructive examination, though limited, have not
identified any cracking in compliant storage to date. Only a non-compliant liquid item, which exposed
the outer container to 3M HCl vapor for over a year in storage, developed small intragranular stress
corrosion cracks in the weld region of the SAVY at the bottom of corrosion-induced pits [30]. This
environment is atypical of longer-term storage conditions but serves as key evidence for the potential
of intragranular SCC to appear in SAVY containers. Moreover, the development of cracks may not
only be facilitated by surface chemistry and stress state but also through co-evolving defects in the
container (i.e., pitting). There are two factors that must be assessed and these include the container
stress state and the storage environment.
First, a net tensile stress is required to provide the necessary conditions for SCC to appear. Previous
examinations of the container using both hole drilling and x-ray diffraction techniques showed that a
net tensile stress is present only in the weld region and decreases monotonically to a depth up to 30%
of the wall thickness relative to the inner surface [31, 32]. Beyond this depth, the stress state transitions
to compressive stress that is less favorable to the formation and propagation of cracks. U-bend
specimens with a similar variation in residual stress have also demonstrated similar behavior with
cracking primarily appearing on surfaces with a tensile residual stress [33].

2.4.2 Intragranular SCC (continued)
Stress corrosion cracking is a critical consideration for the container both in nominal storage conditions
and under accident scenarios. During a drop event, the container may experience a large, transient
tensile stress, potentially exceeding the yield strength, that could initiate cracking on either a native or
corroded surface. Moreover, prior observations of cracking at the bottom of pits suggests that these
defects may facilitate the formation and growth of cracks arising from their role as a stress
concentrator. Drop testing of containers subject to corrosion to emulate pit growth showed that drops
do not induce observable cracking in both pitted and unpitted regions of the material in the impact
region [34]. The current findings indicate that corrosion-facilitated crack formation during a drop
test/accident scenario does not occur on a container with a significantly large pit (i.e., 82% through
wall). This implies that crack formation on containers with similar pitting is not a concern, however
this result may be refined through further accelerated experimentation..
Second, the environment established in the container must support the development of SCC. There are
three principal environmental factors that contribute to SCC including relative humidity, temperature,
and concentration of corrosive species [35]. It is postulated that moisture condensation conditions are
necessary for corrosion to initiate and continue, as the water acts as the transport mechanism for
aggressive ions to the surface of the 316L [36]. Literature suggests one of the main corrosion
mechanisms of the SAVY is through the adsorption of gaseous hydrochloric acid on the container walls
under condensed moisture conditions, radiolyzed from either the PVC bag-out bag or the material itself
[37]. This is further substantiated by the observation of pitting corrosion on all surfaces of the
container for those where general corrosion has been observed [29, 38–40].
SCC is often observed at elevated temperatures that are not within approved SAVY storage conditions.
However, low or room temperature SCC can occur and is facilitated by solutions with both a low pH
(i.e., high HCl concentration) and a high concentration of Cl-containing species [41]. The remaining
factor critical to SCC, corrosive species concentration, is less deterministic. The radiolysis of the bag-
out bag and radioactive decay of stored material result in an evolving internal storage environment
where radiation flux, heat generation, and chemistry are changing over the course of storage. As a
result, corrosion may vary between different modalities [41, 42]. While the conditions for SCC to
occur may exist within a SAVY, the maturing package may not support SCC for the duration of
storage. For containers in compliant storage surveilled to date, no cracking has been observed. This is
strongly caveated by the fact that cracks that have been observed are sub-surface and on the order of
10s of micrometers or less. Current surveillance tools are unable to resolve such features, except for
those used as part of the destructive examination process. These destructive examination techniques
include optical examinations as well as laser confocal microscopy [43]. Moreover, the cracks observed
are on the order of the surface roughness and superficial to the structural integrity of the container.
Pitting reports generated to date from this guide do not include cracks because none have been
observed. Additionally, in FY26 work is underway to measure the stress states within the container
walls of the SAVY container, which may ultimately rule out the potential for SCC to be a lifetime
limiting phenomena.
In short, conditions for through-wall intragranular SCC directly challenging the design release rate
performance of the SAVY have not been observed to exist in allowed package configurations. Both the
necessary stress state in the container and a conducive environment for SCC to form and grow through
the thickness of the container have not yet been identified.

2.4.3 Corrosion Pitting
Pitting has been observed during container surveillance and currently represents the worst-case
corrosion mechanism with respect to potential containment failure. Given the wide range of material
forms and types, and environmental conditions encountered, the approach has been to bound the
maximum expected pitting growth rate using a conservative growth model and to compare this against
a critical pit depth at which failure could occur under a design-qualification release-rate assessment
scenario (e.g., a drop test). Hagans serve as a leading indicator through Hagan and SAVY surveillance
observations. Surveillance containers have shown to have comparable material types, forms, and
internal packaging configurations between the two containers [44, 45]. The 304L used in the body of
the Hagans, all else being equal, is less corrosion resistant than the 316L in SAVYs owing to the lower
chromium content and lack of molybdenum. Additionally, laboratory studies have also shown that
Hagan wall material subject to the same environment as SAVY wall material will exhibit more severe
corrosion pitting [46]. No SAVY surveilled to date has approached a 25-year service life (maximum
14), therefore no measurements of SAVY performance to 25 years can be made directly. Hagan
containers have been in use at LANL for over 25 years and the pitting behavior in the oldest of these
containers can be used to derive a bounding set of corrosion defect growth rates. The information from
the corrosion behavior from Hagan containers may be used to contextualize that observed in SAVY
containers.
A set of accelerated atmospheric and aqueous pitting corrosion growth experiments was performed to
create test samples for design qualification release rate performance evaluations via drop testing. These
samples, with varying pit depths and densities, achieved passing results with pit depths up to 82%
through wall thickness, corresponding to pits up to 490 μm deep [34]. Prior testing on containers
subject to static aqueous solution exposures developed pits between 35%–55% through wall thickness
and also passed post-drop He leak test criteria [47]. Further granularity on the pit threshold is not
available at this time but is currently the focus of future efforts. For the purpose of establishing a
threshold for this technical basis, pit depths up to 490 μm deep with the distributions noted in [34] are
used as end-of-life conditions for containers.
In SAVY containers, destructive examination on four containers has been performed to date. The
deepest pits include those with an ~80 μm depth after 3.5 years in storage, ~42 μm depth after 6.6
years, ~75 μm after 2.8 years, and ~57 μm after 6 years [29, 40]. In Hagan containers, surveillance
destructive analyses have observed the maximum pit depths of 139 μm (+/- 22.8 μm) after 17.5 years
and ~130 μm after 9 years [38, 39]. A defect growth rate coupled with these observations can be used
to estimate the design life of the container. While a linear growth rate was previously used to define a
bounding corrosion rate in containers, subsequent studies have found this to be overly conservative [2].

2.4.4 Intergranular Stress Corrosion Cracking
Intergranular SCC has not been observed in SAVY containers to date. Different from intragranular
SCC, intergranular SCC in austenitic steels is commonly caused by chemical changes to the host
material and can arise from many sources including sensitization from welding processes and hydride
formation from exposure to hydrogen-rich environments for extended durations. The most common
cause of intergranular SCC in austenitic steels is weld sensitization where chromium locally depletes
on the interior of grains and forms carbides on grain boundaries. The resulting material is less
corrosion resistant and suffers from a degradation of mechanical properties owing to the brittle
carbides on boundaries. This is effectively mitigated in the SAVY through the use of a low carbon 316
alloy [51]. Moreover, weld micrographs have not shown the formation of chromium-rich carbides or
precipitation of other compounds [32].
Hydrogen is liberated during the corrosion process for hydrogen-rich corrosive species, such as HCl.
The presence of hydrogen in the environment may result in their dissolution into the stainless steel. At
sufficiently high concentrations, metal hydrides can form on boundaries or in grain interiors both of
which result in the development of a local tensile stress state. The more imminently relevant of the
two, hydrogen dissolved into solution, is applicable to the SAVY under nominal storage conditions.
Localized deformation of the material may occur through several mechanisms, all of which serve to
reduce the macroscopic ductility of the 316L material [52–54]. The SAVY container vents to the
external environment and has maximum normal operating pressures of 1 kPa and 2 kPa for quart and
gallon sizes, respectively, with measured values significantly smaller [1]. The environment necessary
for the accumulation of low concentrations of hydrogen in the material (i.e., ppm) are not sustained by
the SAVY design.

2.4.4 Intergranular Stress Corrosion Cracking (continued)
Other causes of intergranular SCC are unfavorable at the nominal storage conditions SAVYs are
subject to. Many of these require extreme conditions (i.e., high temperature) or highly corrosive
compounds that have not been identified or are not allowed for storage. Therefore, intergranular stress
corrosion-cracking is not considered, at this time, to be a life-limiting phenomenon affecting either the
design release rate or design qualification release rate performance of the SAVY.
2.4.5 Galvanic Corrosion
The SAVY containers are comprised of, principally, two metal constituents: aluminum and stainless
steel alloys. The two aluminum components are the locking ring and handle. The locking ring is
fabricated using 7075 (T6) or T651 following guidance in ASTM B209. The locking ring is subject to
anodization following specifications outlined in MIL-A-8625 for type III class 1 coatings. The addition
of a thick aluminum oxide in this process provides a barrier to galvanic corrosion between the locking
ring and the 316L locking collar. The handle, made from alloy 6061 or 7075, does not undergo
anodization but is not a first or second order component to containerization of material. All stainless
steel components of the lid plate, filter cup, locking collar, and container body are fabricated from
316L and do not exhibit galvanic corrosion.

2.4.6 Inner Packaging Configurations (Nested Ancillary Containers)
Corrosion assumptions and resistance performance of the storage container rely on and are assured by
the presence of a proceduralized packaging configuration that consists of at least one inner ancillary
container and in most cases (depending on the material stored), a bag-out bag. Ancillary containers are
in direct contact with the nuclear material and, along with the bag-out bag, provide corrosion
mitigation from any excessively severe conditions caused by the stored material as long as integrity is
maintained. Their continued integrity, however, is not considered credited for the containment of the
nuclear material. The inclusion of these additional packaging layers has been proceduralized since the
introduction of the SAVY container at LANL and are therefore present in every compliantly stored
package at LANL since the SAVY’s inception [55]. Corrosion conditions, therefore, are taken as those
present outside of the inner packaging layers and are assessed accordingly. Because the inner
packaging configuration is so important, a new effort is underway to develop additional information
systems. These systems aim to provide more detailed data on the inner packaging layers of each
storage container. The effort also includes evaluating materials based on their process flow, rather than
relying solely on item description codes (IDC) and material types (MT) to better understand how
specific processing steps may influence material corrosivity, which is not captured by IDC alone.
These actions come in response to two inner ancillary container corrosion failures, one after
approximately seven years of storage due to what appeared to be an abnormal, semi-aqueous item
whose water content could not be accurately captured in the IDC, and the second being a “normal”
molten salt extraction salt (one of the most aggressive compliant materials, in terms of corrosivity) that
caused an inner ancillary container failure after approximately 14 years. Neither of these materials
penetrated the bag-out bag nor came in direct contact with the SAVY outer container, therefore
significantly mitigating the effective corrosivity seen by the SAVY. Additionally, in both cases,
warning signs made themselves visible on the exterior of the container via either white residue or
corrosion products surrounding the filter of the SAVY, which did not affect filter performance when
tested.

2.4.7 Administrative Control Structure for Identifying Potential Issues
In addition to proceduralized inner packaging layers, the procedures and operator training includes an
administrative control structure for identifying and reporting potential packaging issues as they emerge
or are discovered. These procedures give examples of potential packaging issues as well as direct
operators on how to report these issues for investigation and possible remediation. This is vital as the
surveillance program samples a targeted fraction of the container population. In order to ensure
confidence that the surveilled containers constitute the worst-case conditions, comprehensive inclusion
of as many possible container and content configurations is needed. Additionally, all of the worst-case
corroded outer containers exhibit obvious signs of degradation visually on the exterior of the container
near the filter, with examples shown in Fig. 1 of [56]. This indicator allows continued surveillance and
investigation into the corrosion response of the SAVY as it approaches the new recommended lifetime
with assurance that the worst-case items prior to any possible failure are captured.

2.5 Recommendation on the Metal Components Design Life
Based on new surveillance data and ongoing evaluations of the corrosion resistance of the Hagan
(304L SS) and SAVY (316L SS) components, the design life of the 316L stainless steel containment-
boundary components is recommended for extension to 25 years. The added assurance of
proceduralized inner packaging layers (i.e., nested ancillary containers) and the administrative control
structure for identifying potential worst-case corrosion packaging issues prior to corrosion-induced
failure provide additional support to this design life assessment. Complementary laboratory studies to
assess the performance boundary of the container relative to corrosion are underway. Efforts to assess
corrosion growth rates in storage environments are also planned, with an emphasis on those storage
conditions that result in extensive corrosion. Multiple surveillance and system enhancements
(discussed below) will also be implemented to assist in further understanding, characterizing, and
assessing the corrosion resistance of the 316L components. These components will continue to be
surveilled at the current rigor to validate the 25-year design life and any adverse results will prompt
additional investigation and possible design life re-evaluation as necessary.
The design life of the aluminum components (i.e., the locking ring and handle) and all of the metal
components outside of the containment boundary, are recommended to be extended up to 40 years
based on the results of the surveillance program―indicating no change in performance (i.e., ability to
open/close the container), no adverse galvanic corrosion effects, and continued confirmation that no
contact with nuclear material is possible for these components under normal conditions for the
proposed design life. These components will continue to be surveilled at the current rigor to validate
the 40-year design life and any adverse results will prompt additional investigation and possible design
life re-evaluation, if necessary. There is currently no identified degradation threshold for these
components, but a threshold will be defined in future Surveillance Plans. This threshold will
incorporate probability of degradation on a handling frequency basis.

2.6 Future Surveillance Program
Updates to the current surveillance program followed those recommendations from the first technical
basis update to the SAVY’s design life, namely the inclusion of container random sampling, the pursuit
of older, more severely challenged Hagans, and continued data collection including radiation dose
when available.

2.6 Future Surveillance Program (continued)
Given the results to date that show no statistically significant trend for any surveillance test and the
increasing severity of corrosion with age, the surveillance program will include new characterization
capabilities and a greater number of surveillances each year to continue populating the growing
corrosion characterization database. These changes are reflected in the FY25 surveillance plan,
including 20 surveillances scheduled (up from 15 the previous years) with additional high severity
items, integration of destructive examination of containers, more O-ring chemical characterization
efforts focused on a sampling of surveillance O-rings with known approximate dose rates, and
continued environmental evaluation.
2.7 Additional Enhancements and Future Work
In addition to the surveillance updates, enhancements are in-progress outside of the surveillance
program that aim to further an understanding of material characteristics, container storage history,
inner container configuration, and the environment within a container induced by nuclear material.
Material assessment and evaluation relied heavily on the use of MT codes for a description of the
nuclear material constituent and IDC for a partial understanding of the chemical composition and
physical state.
These combinations were then evaluated by subject matter experts (SMEs) to determine their relative
severity to the containment boundary and administratively controlled by limiting approved contents to
those combinations that did not present an overly challenging environment. This method presents
limitations in that the MT/IDC does not and cannot fully describe every nuclear material item. A more
reliable and descriptive method lies in the use of the item name to associate its origin with the correct
process monitoring flow diagram (PMFD). These diagrams describe the feed material and processing
steps to generate any given item name, and therefore provide more detailed information, when paired
with item history reports, of what chemical and physical changes the nuclear material underwent prior
to storage. Better understanding the material’s chemical composition and physical state allows for a
more accurate assessment of container response performance and storage risk, making surveillance
selection and test execution of greater value to continued container evaluation and validation.
Therefore, a review of and application of findings from these PMFDs to achieve the aforementioned
benefits will be pursued.
Improving non-destructive evaluation remains a key goal of the surveillance program in assessing
corrosion-related degradation as it has the potential to improve both the quality and quantity of
surveillance. Corrosion-induced wall loss cannot be assessed in surveillance containers without
destructive examination. For example, the worst-case containers (i.e., the oldest with the most
challenging contents) may not permit a thorough destructive evaluation and would benefit greatly from
techniques that enable an evaluation of the container wall without destructive approaches. At the very
least, a technique to aid in the down-selection, if destructive examination must be used, should be
established to ensure adequate coverage of the worst corrosion defects.
In addition to deepening the team’s understanding of the stored material characteristics, efforts are also
underway to gather container storage history and inner container configuration information. Storage
history is a matter of intelligently compiling already collected accountability records as well as
estimating conditions each surveillance container is exposed to and analyzed through the application of
software. Gathering inner container configuration information also involves the application of
software, but as a means to increase compliance in recording this information rather than compiling it.

2.7 Additional Enhancements and Future Work (continued)
Finally, comprehensive material studies are underway utilizing an array of SAVY containers loaded
with controlled amounts of material and carefully monitored environmental variables. Periodic data
collection from these test samples will provide a greater understanding of material chemical
characteristics, SAVY container response, and the container's and material's corrosion susceptibility to
environmental influence [571].
Integration of the aforementioned enhancements seeks to comprehensively increase knowledge
concerning the influence of what the container is storing, where the container is stored, what
configuration the container is stored in, and for how long. The successful development and
implementation of tools that incorporate these four factors will significantly enhance the team’s ability
to identify and understand the material and mechanisms that present the greatest challenge to the
SAVY over its design life.

2.8 Conclusion
Surveillance, destructive evaluation, accelerated aging, and corrosion evaluation results continue to
validate the calculated design life of the SAVY beyond 15 years. For the polymer components,
specifically the O-ring, continued non-destructive surveillance analysis and recent destructive chemical
characterization show no statistically significant change in performance relative to pristine O-rings,
maintaining consistency with the original lifetime prediction conclusions of 40 years from
comprehensive accelerated aging studies. For the PTFE membrane, recent destructive performance
testing in response to a facility event indicates compliance with facility performance criteria in absence
of the PTFE membrane and therefore suggests that it is no longer needed to maintain the performance
basis of the SAVY container. Other components analyzed specifically by the surveillance program,
such as the filter and components external to the containment boundary (e.g., the locking ring, locking
pin, bolts), align similarly with the above findings and show no statistically significant change relative
to containment performance objectives. These components (O-ring, PTFE membrane, filter, all
external metal components relative to the containment boundary) are recommended to have a design
life of 40 years as they continue to be validated by the surveillance program against this originally
calculated design life.
The remaining components, including the stainless steel collar, body, and lid, continue to present
challenges to the design life of the container due to corrosion. Addressing this challenge, accelerated
aging studies, post aging testing, and corrosion evaluations to understand the type and severity of
corrosion failure mechanisms as well as characterizing some of the worst-case corrosion observed in
service have been completed. These actions, along with the continued service and analysis of the less
corrosion resistant Hagan container as a leading indicator, provide confidence in the SAVY container’s
ongoing ability to safely and compliantly store nuclear material and resist corrosion in actual storage
environments and postulated accident scenarios, while further understanding and knowledge are gained
analyzing container performance in the coming years. A 25-year lifetime for the containment boundary
316L stainless steel components, which conservatively allows for additional evaluations in the next ten
years while providing assurance of continued safe and compliant storage during this time, is
recommended.
