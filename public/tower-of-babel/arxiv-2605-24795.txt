Abstract
. We study stochastic density control between Gaussian-mixture endpoint distributions under Brownian prior dynamics. Since the direct Schrödinger bridge between Gaussian mixtures is generally not available in closed form, we introduce a lifted path-space construction in which each trajectory is augmented with a source–target component label. Consequently, the problem decomposes into Gaussian component-to-component Schrödinger bridges with explicit marginal, drift, and cost formulas, while the mixture-level assignment reduces to a finite-dimensional entropic coupling problem with a Sinkhorn scaling form. We then analyze the projection obtained by discarding or forgetting the label. By construction, the projected law satisfies the original Gaussian-mixture endpoint constraints, but its relative entropy generally differs from the lifted relative entropy by a nonnegative conditional label-information gap. This gap reveals a path-space obstruction : the lifted optimizer cannot, in general, be identified with the direct unlabeled Schrödinger bridge after projection. We also derive the posterior-averaged Markov drift associated with the projected marginal flow, prove a kinetic-energy upper bound, and identify a common path-potential condition under which the projection gap vanishes. Several numerical illustrations showing density and shape control are recorded for a self-contained exposition.

These questions brings us to the main conceptual contribution of this manuscript, namely, recast Gaussian-mixture Schrödinger bridge constructions from a lifted path-space perspective.
Rather than treating a Gaussian-mixture bridge only as a computational superposition of pairwise Gaussian bridges, we formulate an augmented entropy-minimization problem on a label–trajectory space, in which the source–target component assignment is itself a probabilistic object.
In this formulation, the prior component coupling
\eta
specifies the reference assignment structure in the lifted space, while the optimized coupling
\pi
balances this prior assignment against the pairwise Gaussian bridge costs.
This viewpoint separates three issues that are often conflated: the exact solution of the lifted labeled problem, the feasibility of its projection onto the original unlabeled path space, and the information gap that prevents the projected law from being identified, in general, with the direct unlabeled Schrödinger bridge.
The detailed contributions are as follows (a schematic is given in Figure
1
).

•

Lifted formulation for Gaussian-mixture endpoints.

We address
(

Q

-a)
by augmenting each trajectory with an auxiliary source–target component label. For a given label
(i,j)
, the problem becomes a Gaussian Schrödinger bridge from the initial component
p_{i}^{0}
to the terminal component
p_{j}^{1}
.
Thus, the Gaussian-mixture representation is used as a structural decomposition device. This is an exact construction on the augmented label–trajectory space; it is not, by itself, an assertion that the resulting projected law is the optimizer of the original unlabeled Schrödinger bridge problem.

•

Finite-dimensional entropic assignment of component mass.

We address
(

Q

-b)
by showing that, after the pairwise Gaussian bridge costs have been computed, the remaining lifted problem reduces to a finite-dimensional entropic coupling problem over the mixture weights.
When the prior coupling
\eta
has full support, the optimizer has a Sinkhorn scaling form. This separates the componentwise continuous bridge calculations from the discrete component-assignment problem.

•

Projection gap between the lifted and unlabeled problems.

We address
(

Q

-c)
by projecting the lifted law back to the original path space, i.e., by forgetting the auxiliary label.
The projected law is feasible for the prescribed Gaussian-mixture endpoints. However, its relative entropy with respect to the original unlabeled Brownian reference generally differs from the lifted relative entropy.
We prove a projection identity showing that the lifted entropy decomposes into the projected path-space entropy plus a nonnegative conditional label-information gap. This gap identifies precisely the obstruction to interpreting the lifted optimizer as the optimizer of the direct unlabeled Schrödinger bridge problem.
We also give a structural common path-potential condition under which this projection gap vanishes, showing that the gap is not an unavoidable artifact of lifting.

•

Posterior-averaged Markov density control.

We address
(

Q

-d)
by constructing a state-only Markov drift obtained by posterior averaging of the pairwise Gaussian bridge drifts.
The resulting density–drift pair satisfies the Fokker–Planck equation, matches the prescribed endpoint densities, and obeys a kinetic-energy upper bound.
Posterior-averaged Gaussian-mixture bridge feedback laws have appeared in GMMflow-type constructions
[
49
]
; our contribution is to identify such feedback as the Eulerian Markov projection of a lifted labeled Schrödinger bridge and to separate
three distinct objects
:
optimality in the augmented labeled space, feasibility after projection to the original state space, and the information gap between the lifted and unlabeled path-space laws.
Numerical examples illustrate the lifted construction and projected Markov feedback for Gaussian-mixture steering.

3.
Main results

Section

2

They only encode a chosen decomposition of the endpoint densities. In this section, we use these component labels as auxiliary variables to construct a lifted path-space formulation.
This lifted formulation decomposes the mixture-to-mixture problem into pairwise component Schrödinger bridges coupled through a finite-dimensional transport plan.

We first recall the path-space relative-entropy representation of the stochastic control cost, then define the pairwise component bridges, and finally show that the lifted problem reduces exactly to a finite-dimensional entropic coupling problem over the mixture weights.
This construction should be interpreted as an exact reduction of a labeled, lifted problem. Its relation to the original unlabeled density-control problem is addressed afterward through a projection identity.

Proposition

(

,

3.4.
Lifting the mixture problem

We first define a few objects needed in the sequel.
A matrix
\pi\coloneqq(\pi_{ij})_{i\in\mathcal{I},j\in\mathcal{J}}
is a
component coupling
for the mixture weights
(\alpha^{0},\alpha^{1})
if

((9))

\pi_{ij}\geqslant 0,\quad\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\pi_{ij}=\alpha_{i}^{0}\;\;\text{for all }\,i\in\mathcal{I}\text{ and }\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\pi_{ij}=\alpha_{j}^{1}\;\;\text{for all }\,j\in\mathcal{J}.

We denote the set of all such couplings by
\Pi(\alpha^{0},\alpha^{1})
. Note that,
\pi_{ij}
is the fraction of total mass assigned to start from initial component
i
and end at terminal component
j
.
The constraints (
(9)
) ensure that, aggregating over all terminal components, the total mass leaving component
i
equals
\alpha_{i}^{0}
, and aggregating over all initial components, the total mass arriving at component
j
equals
\alpha_{j}^{1}
.

Define a discrete label space
\mathcal{Z}\coloneqq\mathcal{I}\times\mathcal{J}
. Let
Z
be a
\mathcal{Z}
-valued random variable with
\mathsf{P}\bigl(Z=(i,j)\bigr)=\pi_{ij}.

We lift the state space from trajectories
\Omega
to the product space

\displaystyle\bar{\Omega}\coloneqq\mathcal{Z}\times\Omega=\bigl(\{1,\ldots,N_{1}\}\times\{1,\ldots,N_{2}\}\bigr)\times\mathcal{C}\bigl([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{d}\bigr),

and equip
\bar{\Omega}
with
\mathcal{B}(\bar{\Omega})
.
Observe that, a point in
\bar{\Omega}
is a pair
(z,\omega)
with
z=(i,j)
and
[0,1]\ni t\mapsto\omega(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
a continuous trajectory. We now define one of the chief objects: For
\pi\in\Pi(\alpha^{0},\alpha^{1})
, define a probability measure on
\bar{\Omega}
by

((10))

\bar{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}\coloneqq\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\pi_{ij}\,\delta_{(i,j)}\otimes\mathsf{P}^{ij}\in\mathcal{P}(\bar{\Omega}).

In (
(10)
),
\delta_{(i,j)}
denotes the Dirac measure on the discrete label space
\mathcal{Z}\coloneqq\mathcal{I}\times\mathcal{J}
concentrated at the label
(i,j)
. Thus,
\delta_{(i,j)}\otimes\mathsf{P}^{ij}
is the product probability measure on
\bigl(\mathcal{Z}\times\Omega,\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{Z})\otimes\mathcal{B}(\Omega)\bigr)
satisfying

\displaystyle A\times B\mapsto(\delta_{(i,j)}\otimes\mathsf{P}^{ij})(A\times B)\coloneqq\delta_{(i,j)}(A)\mathsf{P}^{ij}(B)\quad\text{for all }A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{Z})\text{ and }B\in\mathcal{B}(\Omega),

where
\delta_{(i,j)}(A)=1
if
(i,j)\in A
, and
\delta_{(i,j)}(A)=0
otherwise.
Consequently, if
x_{0:1}\coloneqq\{t\mapsto x_{t}\;|\;t\in[0,1]\}
, to sample
(Z,x_{0:1})\sim\bar{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}
one needs to follow the two-step procedure: (i) draw
Z=(i,j)
with probability
\pi_{ij}
; (ii) conditional on
Z=(i,j)
, draw an entire trajectory
x_{0:1}
from the pairwise bridge law
\mathsf{P}^{ij}
.
Our first observation is that the endpoint constraints are satisfied by construction.

Proposition 3
.

Let
\pi\in\Pi(\alpha^{0},\alpha^{1})
and let
\rho_{t}^{\pi}(\cdot)
denote the time-
t
marginal density of
x_{t}
under
\bar{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}
.
Then,

((11))

x\mapsto\rho_{t}^{\pi}(x)\coloneqq\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\pi_{ij}\,\rho_{t}^{ij}(x),

and, in particular,
\rho_{0}^{\pi}=\rho_{0}
and
\rho_{1}^{\pi}=\rho_{1}.

Proof.

By Definition
(10)
, conditioning on
Z=(i,j)
yields the pairwise law
\mathsf{P}^{ij}
.
Therefore, the law of
x_{t}
under
\bar{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}
is the mixture of the laws under
\mathsf{P}^{ij}
with weights
\pi_{ij}
, giving
(11)
.
At
t=0
,
\rho_{0}^{ij}=p_{i}^{0}
, hence

\displaystyle\rho_{0}^{\pi}=\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{I}\times\mathcal{J}}\pi_{ij}p_{i}^{0}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\Big(\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\pi_{ij}\Big)p_{i}^{0}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\alpha_{i}^{0}p_{i}^{0}=\rho_{0}.

Similarly, at
t=1
,
\rho_{1}^{ij}=p_{j}^{1}
, hence,

\displaystyle\rho_{1}^{\pi}=\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{I}\times\mathcal{J}}\pi_{ij}p_{j}^{1}=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\Big(\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\pi_{ij}\Big)p_{j}^{1}=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\alpha_{j}^{1}p_{j}^{1}=\rho_{1}.

Thus, the lifted law
\bar{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}
satisfies the endpoints by construction.
∎

We now endow the lifted construction with an entropic optimality criterion, which selects the component coupling
\pi
by comparing
\bar{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}
against a labeled reference law on
\bar{\Omega}
.

3.4.1.
Augmented entropic formulation

Let
\pi,\eta\in\Pi(\alpha^{0},\alpha^{1})
with
\eta_{ij}>0
. Define the standard KL divergence
\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\pi\|\eta)\coloneqq\sum_{i,j}\pi_{ij}\log\!\left(\frac{\pi_{ij}}{\eta_{ij}}\right).

We now construct an augmented reference law in the lifted space
\bar{\Omega}
. Fix a strictly positive
prior coupling

\eta\in\Pi(\alpha^{0},\alpha^{1})
with
\eta_{ij}>0
.
Define the augmented (uncontrolled) measure

((12))

\bar{\mathsf{R}}^{\eta}\coloneqq\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\eta_{ij}\,\delta_{(i,j)}\otimes\mathsf{R}^{i}\in\mathcal{P}(\bar{\Omega}).

The coupling
\eta
plays the role of a prior component assignment before accounting for the pairwise bridge costs. We now come to one of our main results.

Define the admissible lifted class

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{lift}}\coloneqq\left\{\bar{\mathsf{Q}}\coloneqq\sum_{i,j}\pi_{ij}\delta_{(i,j)}\otimes\mathsf{Q}^{ij}\in\mathcal{P}(\bar{\Omega})\;\middle|\;\begin{array}[]{@{}l@{}}\pi\in\Pi(\alpha^{0},\alpha^{1}),\,\mathsf{Q}^{ij}\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega),\\
\mathrm{Law}_{\mathsf{Q}^{ij}}(x_{0})=p_{i}^{0},\;\mathrm{Law}_{\mathsf{Q}^{ij}}(x_{1})=p_{j}^{1}.\end{array}\right\}.

Theorem 1

(An exact lifted reduction)
.

Let
\eta\in\Pi(\alpha^{0},\alpha^{1})
satisfy
\eta_{ij}>0
for all

(i,j)\in\mathcal{I}\times\mathcal{J}
, and define
\bar{\mathsf{R}}^{\eta}

by (
(12)
).
Consider the lifted problem

((15))

\displaystyle\inf_{\bar{\mathsf{Q}}\in\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{lift}}}\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}\bigl(\bar{\mathsf{Q}}\|\bar{\mathsf{R}}^{\eta}\bigr).

Then, we have the following finite-dimensional form

((16))

\displaystyle\inf_{\bar{\mathsf{Q}}\in\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{lift}}}\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}\bigl(\bar{\mathsf{Q}}\|\bar{\mathsf{R}}^{\eta}\bigr)=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\alpha^{0},\alpha^{1})}\Big\{\sum_{i,j}\pi_{ij}\kappa_{ij}+\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\pi\|\eta)\Big\}.

Equivalently, in the kinetic-energy scaling representation

((17))

\displaystyle\inf_{\bar{\mathsf{Q}}\in\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{lift}}}\varepsilon\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}\bigl(\bar{\mathsf{Q}}\|\bar{\mathsf{R}}^{\eta}\bigr)=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\alpha^{0},\alpha^{1})}\Big\{\sum_{i,j}\pi_{ij}C_{ij}+\varepsilon\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\pi\|\eta)\Big\}.

Moreover, for every fixed
\pi\in\Pi(\alpha^{0},\alpha^{1})
, the optimal
conditional laws on every active pair
(i,j)
with
\pi_{ij}>0
are the pairwise bridges
\mathsf{Q}^{ij}=\mathsf{P}^{ij}
. Hence, for any optimal coupling
\pi^{\star}
, an optimal lifted law is given by

\displaystyle\bar{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi^{\star}}=\sum_{i,j}\pi^{\star}_{ij}\delta_{(i,j)}\otimes\mathsf{P}^{ij}.

If, in addition,
\pi^{\star}_{ij}>0
for all
(i,j)
, then the optimal
conditional laws are unique for all component pairs.

Proof.

Let

\bar{\mathsf{Q}}=\sum_{i,j}\pi_{ij}\delta_{(i,j)}\otimes\mathsf{Q}^{ij}\in\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{lift}}

be arbitrary.
We first prove the inequality “
\geqslant
” in (
(16)
).
If
\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}\bigl(\bar{\mathsf{Q}}\|\bar{\mathsf{R}}^{\eta}\bigr)=+\infty,

then the desired lower bound is immediate.
Hence, assume that
\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}\bigl(\bar{\mathsf{Q}}\|\bar{\mathsf{R}}^{\eta}\bigr)<+\infty.

In particular,
\bar{\mathsf{Q}}\ll\bar{\mathsf{R}}^{\eta}
.
Since
\bar{\mathsf{R}}^{\eta}=\sum_{i,j}\eta_{ij}\delta_{(i,j)}\otimes\mathsf{R}^{i}
and
\eta_{ij}>0
for every
(i,j)
, it follows that, for every active pair
(i,j)
with
\pi_{ij}>0
, one has

\mathsf{Q}^{ij}\ll\mathsf{R}^{i}.
Indeed, if
B\in\mathcal{B}(\Omega)
satisfies
\mathsf{R}^{i}(B)=0
, then
\bar{\mathsf{R}}^{\eta}\bigl(\{(i,j)\}\times B\bigr)=\eta_{ij}\mathsf{R}^{i}(B)=0.

Since
\bar{\mathsf{Q}}\ll\bar{\mathsf{R}}^{\eta}
, this implies
0=\bar{\mathsf{Q}}\bigl(\{(i,j)\}\times B\bigr)=\pi_{ij}\mathsf{Q}^{ij}(B).
Thus, if
\pi_{ij}>0
, then
\mathsf{Q}^{ij}(B)=0
, proving
\mathsf{Q}^{ij}\ll\mathsf{R}^{i}
.

Consequently, the standard chain rule for relative
entropy
[
20
, Theorem B.2.1]
applies on the disjoint label-slice decomposition of
\bar{\Omega}=\mathcal{Z}\times\Omega
, and gives

((18))

\displaystyle\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}\bigl(\bar{\mathsf{Q}}\|\bar{\mathsf{R}}^{\eta}\bigr)=\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\pi\|\eta)+\sum_{i,j}\pi_{ij}\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\mathsf{Q}^{ij}\|\mathsf{R}^{i}).

For each pair
(i,j)
, admissibility gives
\Law_{\mathsf{Q}^{ij}}(x_{0})=p_{i}^{0}
and
\Law_{\mathsf{Q}^{ij}}(x_{1})=p_{j}^{1}.
Hence, by the definition of the pairwise Schrödinger bridge
\mathsf{P}^{ij}
,
\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\mathsf{Q}^{ij}\|\mathsf{R}^{i})\geqslant\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\mathsf{P}^{ij}\|\mathsf{R}^{i})=\kappa_{ij}.

Therefore, using (
(18)
), we have

\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}\bigl(\bar{\mathsf{Q}}\|\bar{\mathsf{R}}^{\eta}\bigr)\geqslant\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\pi\|\eta)+\sum_{i,j}\pi_{ij}\kappa_{ij}.

Since this holds for every
\bar{\mathsf{Q}}\in\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{lift}}
, we obtain

((19))

\displaystyle\inf_{\bar{\mathsf{Q}}\in\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{lift}}}\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}\bigl(\bar{\mathsf{Q}}\|\bar{\mathsf{R}}^{\eta}\bigr)\geqslant\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\alpha^{0},\alpha^{1})}\Big\{\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\pi\|\eta)+\sum_{i,j}\pi_{ij}\kappa_{ij}\Big\}.

We now prove the reverse inequality.
Let
\pi\in\Pi(\alpha^{0},\alpha^{1})
be arbitrary, and recall the definition of
\bar{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}
from (
(10)
). Since each
\mathsf{P}^{ij}
satisfies
\Law_{\mathsf{P}^{ij}}(x_{0})=p_{i}^{0}
and
\Law_{\mathsf{P}^{ij}}(x_{1})=p_{j}^{1},
we have
\bar{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}\in\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{lift}}
.
Applying the preceding KL decomposition (
(18)
) with
\mathsf{Q}^{ij}=\mathsf{P}^{ij}
gives
\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}\bigl(\bar{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}\|\bar{\mathsf{R}}^{\eta}\bigr)=\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\pi\|\eta)+\sum_{i,j}\pi_{ij}\kappa_{ij}.

Taking the infimum over
\pi\in\Pi(\alpha^{0},\alpha^{1})
, we obtain

((20))

\displaystyle\inf_{\bar{\mathsf{Q}}\in\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{lift}}}\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}\bigl(\bar{\mathsf{Q}}\|\bar{\mathsf{R}}^{\eta}\bigr)\leqslant\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\alpha^{0},\alpha^{1})}\Big\{\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\pi\|\eta)+\sum_{i,j}\pi_{ij}\kappa_{ij}\Big\}.

Combining inequalities (
(19)
) and (
(20)
) yields

\inf_{\bar{\mathsf{Q}}\in\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{lift}}}\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}\bigl(\bar{\mathsf{Q}}\|\bar{\mathsf{R}}^{\eta}\bigr)=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\alpha^{0},\alpha^{1})}\Big\{\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\pi\|\eta)+\sum_{i,j}\pi_{ij}\kappa_{ij}\Big\}.

Multiplying both sides by
\varepsilon
, and using
C_{ij}=\varepsilon\kappa_{ij}
, yields (
(17)
).

Finally, fix
\pi\in\Pi(\alpha^{0},\alpha^{1})
.
The term
\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\pi\|\eta)
is fixed, and the only part depending on the conditional path laws is
\sum_{i,j}\pi_{ij}\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\mathsf{Q}^{ij}\|\mathsf{R}^{i}).

For every active pair
(i,j)
with
\pi_{ij}>0
, the conditional path law
\mathsf{Q}^{ij}
must minimize the pairwise problem

\inf\big\{\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\mathsf{Q}\|\mathsf{R}^{i})\;\big|\;\mathsf{Q}\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega),\;\Law_{\mathsf{Q}}(x_{0})=p_{i}^{0},\;\Law_{\mathsf{Q}}(x_{1})=p_{j}^{1}\big\},

where
\mathsf{Q}
is a generic path-space optimization variable
2
2

2

By the definition of the pairwise component Schrödinger bridge in (
(7)
), the minimizer of this problem is precisely
\mathsf{P}^{ij}
.
Therefore, on every active pair, the optimal conditional law is
\mathsf{Q}^{ij}=\mathsf{P}^{ij}
.

This pairwise minimization problem has a unique solution. Indeed, its admissible set is convex, and
\mathsf{Q}\mapsto\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\mathsf{Q}\|\mathsf{R}^{i})
is strictly convex on the set of measures absolutely continuous with respect to
\mathsf{R}^{i}
.
Since the Gaussian bridge
\mathsf{P}^{ij}
has finite entropy, every minimizer has finite entropy and hence is absolutely continuous with respect to
\mathsf{R}^{i}
.
Therefore, two distinct minimizers cannot exist. The unique minimizer is precisely the pairwise Schrödinger bridge
\mathsf{P}^{ij}
. Hence, for any optimal coupling
\pi^{\star}
, the optimal lifted law is

\bar{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi^{\star}}=\sum_{i,j}\pi^{\star}_{ij}\delta_{(i,j)}\otimes\mathsf{P}^{ij}.

If, in addition,
\pi^{\star}_{ij}>0
for all
(i,j)
, then every conditional law is active, and therefore the optimal conditional laws are unique for all component pairs. The proof is complete.
∎

Consequently, minimizing the lifted KL cost over
\pi\in\Pi(\alpha^{0},\alpha^{1})
is equivalently written, in the kinetic-energy scaling of Theorem
1
, as the finite-dimensional strictly convex program

((21))

\pi^{\star}\in\argmin_{\pi\in\Pi(\alpha^{0},\alpha^{1})}\left\{\sum_{i,j}\pi_{ij}\,C_{ij}+\varepsilon\,\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\pi\|\eta)\right\}=\argmin_{\pi\in\Pi(\alpha^{0},\alpha^{1})}\left\{\sum_{i,j}\pi_{ij}\,\kappa_{ij}+\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\pi\|\eta)\right\}.

Theorem
1
reduces the lifted entropy minimization problem to a finite-dimensional entropic transport problem over
\Pi(\alpha^{0},\alpha^{1})
; we now record its optimality conditions and the corresponding Sinkhorn scaling form.

3.4.2.
Optimality conditions and Sinkhorn form

We can solve for
\pi
in (
(21)
) using Sinkhorn-type scaling.
Recall that
\eta\in\Pi(\alpha^{0},\alpha^{1})
is the fixed strictly positive prior coupling used to define the lifted reference law
\bar{\mathsf{R}}^{\eta}
in (
(12)
).
Define the Gibbs kernel
[
44
]

((22))

K_{ij}\coloneqq\eta_{ij}\exp\!\left(-\frac{C_{ij}}{\varepsilon}\right).

Since
C_{ij}=\varepsilon\kappa_{ij}
, one has
K_{ij}=\eta_{ij}\exp(-\kappa_{ij})
. We have the following scaling form.

Proposition 4
.

The unique optimizer
\pi^{\star}
of (
(21)
) has the form

((23))

\pi_{ij}^{\star}=a_{i}\,K_{ij}\,b_{j},

for some positive vectors
a\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{1}}
,
b\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{2}}
chosen so that
\sum_{j}\pi_{ij}^{\star}=\alpha_{i}^{0},
and
\sum_{i}\pi_{ij}^{\star}=\alpha_{j}^{1}.

Proof.

Consider the Lagrangian for Problem (
(21)
)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\pi,\lambda,\mu)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\pi_{ij}C_{ij}+\varepsilon\sum_{i,j}\pi_{ij}\log\!\Big(\frac{\pi_{ij}}{\eta_{ij}}\Big)+\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\Big(\alpha_{i}^{0}-\sum_{j}\pi_{ij}\Big)+\sum_{j}\mu_{j}\Big(\alpha_{j}^{1}-\sum_{i}\pi_{ij}\Big).

Since
\eta_{ij}>0
and the retained mixture weights are strictly positive, the minimizer is strictly positive.
Hence, the first-order stationarity condition gives

\displaystyle 0=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\pi_{ij}}=C_{ij}+\varepsilon\Big(\log(\pi_{ij}/\eta_{ij})+1\Big)-\lambda_{i}-\mu_{j}.

Rearranging, we get
\log(\pi_{ij}/\eta_{ij})=(\lambda_{i}+\mu_{j}-C_{ij})/{\varepsilon}-1.

Exponentiating and absorbing constants into
a_{i}
and
b_{j}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\pi_{ij}=\eta_{ij}\exp\!\left(-\frac{C_{ij}}{\varepsilon}\right)\exp\!\left(\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\varepsilon}-\frac{1}{2}\right)\exp\!\left(\frac{\mu_{j}}{\varepsilon}-\frac{1}{2}\right)=a_{i}K_{ij}b_{j}.

This yields (
(23)
).
The marginal constraints determine the scaling vectors
a,b
, up to the usual reciprocal scaling ambiguity
a\mapsto ca
,
b\mapsto c^{-1}b
.
∎

Note that the scaling vectors
(a,b)
can be computed by alternating normalization:

\displaystyle a_{i}\leftarrow\frac{\alpha_{i}^{0}}{\sum_{j}K_{ij}b_{j}},\qquad b_{j}\leftarrow\frac{\alpha_{j}^{1}}{\sum_{i}K_{ij}a_{i}},

iterated to convergence. See
[
44
, Chapter 3]
for more details.

The preceding results solve the lifted labeled problem, but the original SB/density-control problem is posed on the unlabeled path space; we therefore next project the lifted law by discarding/forgetting the component label and quantify the resulting entropy discrepancy.

3.5.
Quantifying the gap

Theorem
1
is exact for the lifted problem.
However, the original problem is unlabeled and is posed on the path space
\Omega
, not on
\bar{\Omega}
. To this end, let us define

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\Omega}:\mathcal{Z}\times\Omega\to\Omega\quad\text{with }\mathcal{Z}\times\Omega\ni(z,\omega)\mapsto\mathcal{P}_{\Omega}(z,\omega)\coloneqq\omega\in\Omega,

which is the map that projects label-trajectory pairs onto the original path space.
Define the path-space marginal of the lifted law associated with
\pi
by

((24))

\displaystyle\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}\coloneqq(\mathcal{P}_{\Omega})_{\#}\bar{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)\quad\text{where }\,(\mathcal{P}_{\Omega})_{\#}:\mathcal{P}(\bar{\Omega})\longrightarrow\mathcal{P}(\Omega).

Since
\bar{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}=\sum_{i,j}\pi_{ij}\delta_{(i,j)}\otimes\mathsf{P}^{ij}
, we have
\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}=\sum_{i,j}\pi_{ij}\mathsf{P}^{ij}.

Indeed, for any
B\in\mathcal{B}(\Omega)
, the definition
\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}=(\mathcal{P}_{\Omega})_{\#}\bar{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}
in (
(24)
) gives

\displaystyle\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}(B)=\bar{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}(Z\times B)=\sum_{i,j}\pi_{ij}\bigl(\delta_{(i,j)}\otimes\mathsf{P}^{ij}\bigr)(Z\times B)=\sum_{i,j}\pi_{ij}\mathsf{P}^{ij}(B).

Similarly, for any
B\in\mathcal{B}(\Omega)
, the path-space marginal of the augmented reference law is

((25))

\displaystyle(\mathcal{P}_{\Omega})_{\#}\bar{\mathsf{R}}^{\eta}(B)=\bar{\mathsf{R}}^{\eta}(Z\times B)=\sum_{i,j}\eta_{ij}\mathsf{R}^{i}(B)=\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}^{0}\mathsf{R}^{i}(B)\eqqcolon\mathsf{R}^{\rho_{0}}\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega),

where we used
\sum_{j}\eta_{ij}=\alpha_{i}^{0}
.

Thus, projecting
\bar{\mathsf{R}}^{\eta}
onto the original path space removes the auxiliary label and recovers the uncontrolled Brownian reference law initialized from the mixture
\rho_{0}
.
Similarly, the projected law
\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}\coloneqq(\mathcal{P}_{\Omega})_{\#}\bar{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}
has endpoint marginals
\rho_{0}
and
\rho_{1}
by Proposition
3
.
Hence,
\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}
is a feasible path-space law for the original unlabeled endpoint problem. However, feasibility after projection does not imply equality of the corresponding relative entropies.
The lifted law retains the auxiliary label
Z=(i,j)
, whereas
\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}
is obtained after forgetting this label.
Consequently, in general

\displaystyle\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\bar{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}\|\bar{\mathsf{R}}^{\eta})\neq\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}\|\mathsf{R}^{\rho_{0}}).

The discrepancy between these two quantities is precisely the conditional label-information term quantified below.

Theorem 2

(Projection-gap identity for the lifted bridge)
.

Let
\pi,\eta\in\Pi(\alpha^{0},\alpha^{1})
and
\supp\pi\subseteq\supp\eta
.
Recall the definitions
\bar{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}\coloneqq\sum_{i,j}\pi_{ij}\delta_{(i,j)}\otimes\mathsf{P}^{ij}
and
\bar{\mathsf{R}}^{\eta}\coloneqq\sum_{i,j}\eta_{ij}\delta_{(i,j)}\otimes\mathsf{R}^{i}.

Let
\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}\coloneqq(\mathcal{P}_{\Omega})_{\#}\bar{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}
and
\mathsf{R}^{\rho_{0}}\coloneqq(\mathcal{P}_{\Omega})_{\#}\bar{\mathsf{R}}^{\eta}
be defined as in (
(24)
) and
(25)
.
Define the conditional label-information gap

((26))

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(\pi,\eta)\coloneqq\mathsf{E}^{\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}}\left[\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}\bigl(\bar{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}(Z\mid x_{0:1})\|\bar{\mathsf{R}}^{\eta}(Z\mid x_{0:1})\bigr)\right].

Then,
\mathcal{G}(\pi,\eta)\geqslant 0
, and
\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\bar{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}\|\bar{\mathsf{R}}^{\eta})=\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}\|\mathsf{R}^{\rho_{0}})+\mathcal{G}(\pi,\eta).

Consequently,

((27))

\displaystyle\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}\|\mathsf{R}^{\rho_{0}})=\sum_{i,j}\pi_{ij}\kappa_{ij}+\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\pi\|\eta)-\mathcal{G}(\pi,\eta),

which, since
C_{ij}=\varepsilon\kappa_{ij}
, is equivalent to

((28))

\displaystyle\varepsilon\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}\|\mathsf{R}^{\rho_{0}})=\sum_{i,j}\pi_{ij}C_{ij}+\varepsilon\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\pi\|\eta)-\varepsilon\mathcal{G}(\pi,\eta).

Proof.

Since each pairwise bridge satisfies
\mathsf{P}^{ij}\ll\mathsf{R}^{i}
on every active label pair and
\supp\pi\subseteq\supp\eta
, we have
\bar{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}\ll\bar{\mathsf{R}}^{\eta}.
Applying the standard relative-entropy chain rule
[
20
, Theorem B.2.1]
under the projection
\mathcal{P}_{\Omega}:\mathcal{Z}\times\Omega\to\Omega
gives

\displaystyle\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\bar{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}\|\bar{\mathsf{R}}^{\eta})=\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}((\mathcal{P}_{\Omega})_{\#}\bar{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}\|(\mathcal{P}_{\Omega})_{\#}\bar{\mathsf{R}}^{\eta})+\mathsf{E}^{\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}}\left[\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}\bigl(\bar{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}(Z\mid x_{0:1})\|\bar{\mathsf{R}}^{\eta}(Z\mid x_{0:1})\bigr)\right].

By definition,
(\mathcal{P}_{\Omega})_{\#}\bar{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}=\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}
and
(\mathcal{P}_{\Omega})_{\#}\bar{\mathsf{R}}^{\eta}=\mathsf{R}^{\rho_{0}},
and hence

\displaystyle\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\bar{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}\|\bar{\mathsf{R}}^{\eta})=\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}\|\mathsf{R}^{\rho_{0}})+\mathcal{G}(\pi,\eta).

The nonnegativity of
\mathcal{G}(\pi,\eta)
follows from the nonnegativity of the relative entropy.

Finally, applying the label-sliced KL decomposition from (
(18)
) in Theorem
1
, with
\mathsf{Q}^{ij}=\mathsf{P}^{ij}
, we obtain
\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\bar{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}\|\bar{\mathsf{R}}^{\eta})=\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\pi\|\eta)+\sum_{i,j}\pi_{ij}\kappa_{ij}.
Substituting this into the projection identity gives

((29))

\displaystyle\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}\|\mathsf{R}^{\rho_{0}})=\sum_{i,j}\pi_{ij}\kappa_{ij}+\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\pi\|\eta)-\mathcal{G}(\pi,\eta).

Multiplying by
\varepsilon
, and using
C_{ij}=\varepsilon\kappa_{ij}
, gives the kinetic-energy-scaled identity.
∎

Thus, the lifted objective equals the unlabeled path-space objective only up to the nonnegative gap term
\mathcal{G}(\pi,\eta)
.
In general, this term does not vanish, and therefore, one cannot identify the lifted labeled problem with the original unlabeled problem without additional structure.

Remark 3
.

Since
\mathcal{G}(\pi,\eta)
is an expected conditional relative entropy, it is nonnegative.
Moreover,
\mathcal{G}(\pi,\eta)=0
if and only if the two posterior distributions of the label
Z=(i,j)
, conditioned on the full path
x_{0:1}
(recall that
x_{0:1}\coloneqq\{t\mapsto x_{t}\;|\;t\in[0,1]\}
), coincide
\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}
-almost surely. More precisely,
\mathcal{G}(\pi,\eta)=0
if and only if, for
\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}
-a.e. path
\omega\in\Omega
,

\displaystyle\bar{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}\!\left(Z=(i,j)\mid x_{0:1}=\omega\right)=\bar{\mathsf{R}}^{\eta}\!\left(Z=(i,j)\mid x_{0:1}=\omega\right)

for every
(i,j)\in\mathcal{I}\times\mathcal{J}
. This translates, after choosing Radon–Nikodym versions of the posterior densities, to

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}(\pi_{ij}\mathsf{P}^{ij})}{\mathrm{d}\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}}(\omega)=\frac{\mathrm{d}(\eta_{ij}\mathsf{R}^{i})}{\mathrm{d}\mathsf{R}^{\rho_{0}}}(\omega)\quad\text{for all }(i,j)\text{ and for }\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}\text{-a.e. }\omega.

Under
\bar{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}
, the label
(i,j)
determines both the initial component and the terminal component of the pairwise bridge. Under
\bar{\mathsf{R}}^{\eta}
, the path is generated by the uncontrolled law
\mathsf{R}^{i}
, which depends on
i
but not dynamically on
j
.
Therefore, the posterior label distribution inferred from a full controlled bridge path need not agree with the posterior label distribution inferred from an uncontrolled reference path.
Thus, except for specially aligned cases (see Proposition
5
), one expects
\mathcal{G}(\pi,\eta)>0.
Consequently, one should be careful while identifying the lifted labeled objective with the original unlabeled path-space objective.

Proposition 5

(A common path-potential condition for zero projection gap)
.

Let
\pi,\eta\in\Pi(\alpha^{0},\alpha^{1})
, and suppose that they have the same support
\mathcal{A}\coloneqq\supp\pi=\supp\eta=\{(i,j)\in\mathcal{I}\times\mathcal{J}\;|\;\pi_{ij}>0\}=\{(i,j)\in\mathcal{I}\times\mathcal{J}\;|\;\eta_{ij}>0\}.

Assume that, for every
(i,j)\in\mathcal{A}
, one has
\mathsf{P}^{ij}\ll\mathsf{R}^{i}
and that there exist a nonnegative measurable function
h:\Omega\to[0,+\infty)
and constants
c_{ij}>0
,
(i,j)\in\mathcal{A}
, such that

((30))

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathsf{P}^{ij}}{\mathrm{d}\mathsf{R}^{i}}(\omega)=c_{ij}h(\omega)\quad\text{for }\,\mathsf{R}^{i}\text{-a.e. }\omega\,\text{ with }(i,j)\in\mathcal{A}.

Assume, moreover, that the constants are compatible with the lifted prior in the sense that there exists
\lambda>0
such that
\pi_{ij}c_{ij}=\lambda\eta_{ij}
for
(i,j)\in\mathcal{A}
.
Then, the projection gap vanishes, i.e.,
\mathcal{G}(\pi,\eta)=0.

Proof.

Recall Definitions (
(10)
) and (
(12)
).
Since
\supp\pi=\supp\eta=\mathcal{A}
and

\mathsf{P}^{ij}\ll\mathsf{R}^{i}
for every
(i,j)\in\mathcal{A}
, we have
\bar{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}\ll\bar{\mathsf{R}}^{\eta}
.
Also recall the projections (
(24)
) and (
(25)
).
From the projection-gap identity, we have that

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(\pi,\eta)=\mathsf{E}^{\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}}\left[\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}\left(\bar{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}(Z\mid x_{0:1})\|\bar{\mathsf{R}}^{\eta}(Z\mid x_{0:1})\right)\right].

It is therefore enough to show that the two posterior label distributions coincide
\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}
-a.e. Let
M\coloneqq\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{A}}\eta_{ij}\mathsf{R}^{i}
. For each
i
, write
r_{i}\coloneqq\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathsf{R}^{i}}{\mathrm{d}M}.

Using
\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathsf{P}^{ij}}{\mathrm{d}\mathsf{R}^{i}}=c_{ij}h,

the posterior label distribution under the lifted controlled law is, for
\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}
-a.e.
\omega
,

\displaystyle\bar{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}(Z=(i,j)\mid\omega)=\frac{\pi_{ij}c_{ij}h(\omega)r_{i}(\omega)}{\sum_{(k,\ell)\in\mathcal{A}}\pi_{k\ell}c_{k\ell}h(\omega)r_{k}(\omega)}.

Similarly, the posterior label distribution under the lifted reference law is

\displaystyle\bar{\mathsf{R}}^{\eta}(Z=(i,j)\mid\omega)=\frac{\eta_{ij}r_{i}(\omega)}{\sum_{(k,\ell)\in\mathcal{A}}\eta_{k\ell}r_{k}(\omega)}.

By the compatibility condition
\pi_{ij}c_{ij}=\lambda\eta_{ij}
for
(i,j)\in\mathcal{A}
, the numerator and denominator in the first expression are both multiplied by the same factor
\lambda h(\omega)
.
Hence,

\displaystyle\bar{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}\left(Z=(i,j)\mid x_{0:1}=\omega\right)=\bar{\mathsf{R}}^{\eta}\left(Z=(i,j)\mid x_{0:1}=\omega\right),

for every
(i,j)\in\mathcal{A}
,
\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}
-a.e.
\omega
.
Therefore,
the conditional relative entropy inside the definition of
\mathcal{G}(\pi,\eta)
is zero
\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}
-a.e., and hence
\mathcal{G}(\pi,\eta)=0
.
∎

Remark 4

(Interpretation and a non-vacuous zero-gap class)
.

Proposition
5
is a path-space statement.
Assumption
(30)
is on the likelihood ratios of the pairwise Schrödinger bridge laws.
The reason the projection gap vanishes in that case is that, after observing the projected path
\omega=x_{0:1}
, the same factor

h(\omega)
appears in all active posterior label weights and cancels during normalization.
The compatibility condition
\pi_{ij}c_{ij}=\lambda\eta_{ij}
for
(i,j)\in\mathcal{A}
,
then makes the posterior label distribution under the lifted controlled law coincide with the posterior label distribution under the lifted reference law.
Hence, the conditional label-information term is zero.

The condition (
(30)
), together with the compatibility condition
\pi_{ij}c_{ij}=\lambda\eta_{ij}
for
(i,j)\in\mathcal{A}
, is not vacuous.
A simple Gaussian-mixture class satisfying this condition is obtained from a common translation drift.
For example,
let
\varepsilon>0
and
N_{1}=N_{2}=N
, let the active assignment graph be diagonal,
\mathcal{A}=\{(i,i)\;|\;i=1,\ldots,N\},
and take
\pi_{ii}=\eta_{ii}=\alpha_{i}^{0}
for all
i=1,\ldots,N.

Fix
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and let
p_{i}^{0}=\mathcal{N}(m_{i}^{0},\Sigma_{i}^{0}),
and
p_{i}^{1}=\mathcal{N}(m_{i}^{0}+v,\Sigma_{i}^{0}+\varepsilon I).

Consider the controlled Brownian dynamics

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x_{t}=v\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,\mathrm{d}w_{t}\quad\text{with }\,x_{0}\sim p_{i}^{0}.

Its path law will be denoted by
\mathsf{P}^{ii}
.
Since the drift is constant, Girsanov’s formula gives, with respect to the uncontrolled reference law
\mathsf{R}^{i}
,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathsf{P}^{ii}}{\mathrm{d}\mathsf{R}^{i}}(\omega)=\exp\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}v^{\top}(x_{1}(\omega)-x_{0}(\omega))-\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\|v\|^{2}\right).

Therefore, Proposition
5
applies with

((31))

\displaystyle\omega\mapsto h(\omega)\coloneqq\exp\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}v^{\top}(x_{1}(\omega)-x_{0}(\omega))-\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\|v\|^{2}\right)\quad\text{with }\,c_{ii}=1.

Since
\pi_{ii}=\eta_{ii}
, the compatibility condition holds with
\lambda=1
.
Note that
\mathsf{P}^{ii}
is indeed the pairwise Schrödinger bridge from
p_{i}^{0}
to
p_{i}^{1}
.
Under the above controlled dynamics,
x_{1}\sim\mathcal{N}(m_{i}^{0}+v,\Sigma_{i}^{0}+\varepsilon I)=p_{i}^{1}.

Moreover, for any admissible drift
u
steering
p_{i}^{0}
to
p_{i}^{1}
, the mean displacement satisfies
\mathsf{E}\left[\int_{0}^{1}u_{t}(x_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t\right]=v.

Hence, Jensen’s inequality gives

\displaystyle\mathsf{E}\left[\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{2}\|u_{t}(x_{t})\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\right]\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\left\|\mathsf{E}\left[\int_{0}^{1}u_{t}(x_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t\right]\right\|^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\|v\|^{2}.

The constant drift
u_{t}\equiv v
attains this lower bound.
Therefore, it is optimal, and the corresponding path law is the pairwise Schrödinger bridge
\mathsf{P}^{ii}
.
Consequently, for this prescribed-assignment Gaussian-mixture transformation,
\mathcal{G}(\pi,\eta)=0.
This example does not collapse the mixture to a single Gaussian: the source means
m_{i}^{0}
, and covariances
\Sigma_{i}^{0}
may still vary with
i
.
In this example, Assumption (
(30)
) simply says that all active subpopulations undergo the same Brownian translation mechanism, so their pairwise bridge likelihood ratios share the common path-dependent factor
h
given in (
(31)
).

Remark 5

(A canonical choice of the prior coupling)
.

The choice of the prior coupling
\eta
should respect the prescribed
marginals
\alpha^{0}
and
\alpha^{1}
.
In particular, the entrywise uniform matrix
\eta_{ij}={1}/{N_{1}N_{2}}
belongs to
\Pi(\alpha^{0},\alpha^{1})
only in the special case where both
mixture weights are uniform, i.e.,
\alpha_{i}^{0}={1}/{N_{1}},
and
\alpha_{j}^{1}={1}/{N_{2}}.
For general mixture weights, a natural neutral choice is, instead, the product coupling

\eta_{ij}\coloneqq\alpha_{i}^{0}\alpha_{j}^{1}.

This corresponds to an independent prior between the source label

I\in\mathcal{I}
and the target label
J\in\mathcal{J}
.
This choice has a useful
information-theoretic interpretation
.
Since

\pi\in\Pi(\alpha^{0},\alpha^{1})
, its marginals are fixed and equal to

\alpha^{0}
and
\alpha^{1}
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\pi\|\alpha^{0}\otimes\alpha^{1})=\sum_{i,j}\pi_{ij}\log\left(\frac{\pi_{ij}}{\alpha_{i}^{0}\alpha_{j}^{1}}\right)\eqqcolon\mathsf{MI}_{\pi}(I;J),

where
\mathsf{MI}_{\pi}(I;J)
denotes the mutual information between the initial component label
I
and the terminal component label
J
under the joint law
\pi
.
Hence, for the independent prior
\eta=\alpha^{0}\otimes\alpha^{1}
, the finite-dimensional problem becomes

\displaystyle\min_{\pi\in\Pi(\alpha^{0},\alpha^{1})}\left\{\sum_{i,j}\pi_{ij}C_{ij}+\varepsilon\,\mathsf{MI}_{\pi}(I;J)\right\}.

Thus, the first term favors low-energy component-to-component bridges, while the second term penalizes excessive dependence between source and target labels.
The optimizer creates a structured assignment only when the reduction in pairwise bridge cost justifies the associated information cost.
In this sense,
\eta=\alpha^{0}\otimes\alpha^{1}
is a canonical default choice when no prior component-assignment information is available.
Moreover, the lifted formulation is not restricted to this neutral product prior — it is a natural benefit of our approach.
This feature is illustrated numerically in §
4.2.3
,
Figure
7
.

The identity (
(29)
) above quantifies the loss incurred when the label coordinate is removed from the lifted law.
It compares the labeled law
\bar{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}
on
\mathcal{Z}\times\Omega
with the unlabeled path law
\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}
on
\Omega
.
However,
\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}
is still a path-space object and, in general, retains
hidden memory
of the source–target label through the observed trajectory.
Thus,
\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}
should not automatically be identified with the law of a Markov diffusion driven by a state-feedback drift.
Following
[
49
]
, we next construct such a state-feedback drift at the Eulerian level and later quantify the additional passage from the generally non-Markov path law
\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}
to the Markov law generated by this feedback.

3.6.
Projecting back: regularity and feasibility

We now show that the standard posterior-averaged mixture feedback
[
49
]
arises canonically from the lifted labeled law.
Thus, the following construction should not be viewed as an independent ansatz; it is the Eulerian Markov projection of
\bar{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}
.
Fix
\pi\in\Pi(\alpha^{0},\alpha^{1})
, and consider the lifted law
\bar{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}
.
For every Borel set
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the joint law of the label
Z
and the state
x_{t}
satisfies

\displaystyle\bar{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}\bigl(Z=(i,j),\,x_{t}\in A\bigr)=\pi_{ij}\int_{A}\rho_{t}^{ij}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x.

Consequently, after forgetting the label, the time-
t
state density is

((32))

\displaystyle x\mapsto\rho_{t}^{\pi}(x)\coloneqq\sum_{i,j}\pi_{ij}\,\rho_{t}^{ij}(x).

Moreover, the conditional probability of the hidden label given the current state is

((33))

\gamma_{ij}(t,x)\coloneqq\bar{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}\bigl(Z=(i,j)\mid x_{t}=x\bigr)=\frac{\pi_{ij}\rho_{t}^{ij}(x)}{\rho_{t}^{\pi}(x)}.

Since the drift under the conditional bridge
\mathsf{P}^{ij}
is
u_{t}^{ij}
, the state-only Markov drift induced by the lifted construction is obtained by posterior averaging over the unobserved label

((34))

x\mapsto\bar{u}_{t}^{\pi}(x)\coloneqq\sum_{i,j}\gamma_{ij}(t,x)\,u_{t}^{ij}(x)=\frac{\sum_{i,j}\pi_{ij}\rho_{t}^{ij}(x)u_{t}^{ij}(x)}{\rho_{t}^{\pi}(x)}.

Equivalently, we have

\rho_{t}^{\pi}(x)\bar{u}_{t}^{\pi}(x)=\sum_{i,j}\pi_{ij}\rho_{t}^{ij}(x)u_{t}^{ij}(x).

This identity is the key reason why the pair
(\rho_{t}^{\pi},\bar{u}_{t}^{\pi})
satisfies the Fokker–Planck equation after summing the componentwise equations, which we will show later in Proposition
6
.
We also note that the objects in (
(32)
)–(
(34)
) are algebraically identical to those employed in the Gaussian-mixture bridge constructions in
[
49
]
.
The contribution here is not the formulas themselves, but their derivation from the lifted path-space law and its role in the projection analysis developed above.

Lemma 1

(Regularity of the projected drift)
.

Let
\pi\in\Pi(\alpha^{0},\alpha^{1})
, and recall the expressions (
(32)
), (
(33)
), and (
(34)
).
Then,
\bar{u}^{\pi}\in\mathcal{U}_{\rm reg}
.
Specifically, for every
R>0
, there exists
L_{R}(\cdot)\in L^{1}(0,1)
such that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\bar{u}_{t}^{\pi}(x)-\bar{u}_{t}^{\pi}(y)\right\rVert\leqslant L_{R}(t)\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert\qquad\text{with }\left\lVert x\right\rVert,\left\lVert y\right\rVert\leqslant R,

for a.e.
t\in[0,1]
, and
a_{\pi}(\cdot)\in L^{2}(0,1)
such that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\bar{u}_{t}^{\pi}(x)\right\rVert\leqslant a_{\pi}(t)(1+\left\lVert x\right\rVert)\qquad\text{for }x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

for a.e.
t\in[0,1]
.

Proof.

Recall from Proposition
2
that, for each
(i,j)\in\mathcal{I}\times\mathcal{J}
, the density
x\mapsto\rho_{t}^{ij}(x)
is smooth and strictly positive, while the drift has the affine form

\displaystyle x\mapsto u_{t}^{ij}(x)=A_{t}^{ij}(x-m_{t}^{ij})+c^{ij}\quad\text{for }t\in[0,1).

Moreover, by the explicit formulas for the pairwise Gaussian bridge in Section
3.3
,
the maps

t\mapsto m_{t}^{ij}
,
t\mapsto S_{t}^{ij}
, and
t\mapsto\Sigma_{t}^{ij}
are continuous on
[0,1]
, with
\Sigma_{t}^{ij}\in\mathbb{S}_{++}^{d}
for every
t\in[0,1]
.
Hence,
t\mapsto(\Sigma_{t}^{ij})^{-1}
is continuous and uniformly bounded on
[0,1]
. Since
A_{t}^{ij}=S_{t}^{ij}(\Sigma_{t}^{ij})^{-1}
, it follows that
t\mapsto A_{t}^{ij}
is uniformly bounded on
[0,1]
.

We first prove the linear-growth estimate.
For each
(i,j)
, the affine representation gives

\displaystyle\|{u_{t}^{ij}(x)}\|\leqslant\|A_{t}^{ij}\|\left\lVert x\right\rVert+\|A_{t}^{ij}m_{t}^{ij}\|+\|c^{ij}\|.

Thus, setting
a_{ij}(t)\coloneqq\|A_{t}^{ij}\|+\|A_{t}^{ij}m_{t}^{ij}\|+\|c^{ij}\|,
we have

\displaystyle\|u_{t}^{ij}(x)\|\leqslant a_{ij}(t)(1+\left\lVert x\right\rVert).

By the boundedness of
A_{t}^{ij}
,
m_{t}^{ij}
, and
c^{ij}
, each
a_{ij}
belongs to

L^{\infty}(0,1)
, hence also to
L^{2}(0,1)
. Since there are only finitely many component pairs,
a_{\pi}(t)\coloneqq\max_{i,j}a_{ij}(t)
is bounded and thus belongs to
L^{2}(0,1)
.
Using the facts that
\gamma_{ij}^{\pi}(t,x)\geqslant 0
and
\sum_{i,j}\gamma_{ij}^{\pi}(t,x)=1
, we obtain

\displaystyle\|\bar{u}_{t}^{\pi}(x)\|\leqslant\sum_{i,j}\gamma_{ij}^{\pi}(t,x)\|u_{t}^{ij}(x)\|\leqslant a_{\pi}(t)(1+\left\lVert x\right\rVert).

This proves the linear-growth condition.

It remains to prove the local Lipschitz condition. For a fixed
R>0
, we denote by
\mathsf{B}_{R}\coloneqq\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\;|\;\left\lVert x\right\rVert\leqslant R\}
, the closed Euclidean ball of radius
R
centered at the origin.
Observe that:

\circ

Since each
\rho_{t}^{ij}
is a smooth positive Gaussian density and
\rho_{t}^{\pi}=\sum_{i,j}\pi_{ij}\rho_{t}^{ij}
, the function
(t,x)\mapsto\rho_{t}^{\pi}(x)
is continuous and strictly positive on the compact set
[0,1]\times\mathsf{B}_{R}
.
Therefore,
\rho_{t}^{\pi}
has a positive minimum on this compact set
[
55
, Chapter 1]
.
Consequently, each posterior weight
x\mapsto\gamma_{ij}^{\pi}(t,x)
is continuously differentiable in
x
, and
\nabla_{x}\gamma_{ij}^{\pi}
is uniformly bounded on
[0,1]\times\mathsf{B}_{R}
.

\circ

Since
u_{t}^{ij}
is affine with uniformly bounded affine coefficients, both
u_{t}^{ij}
and
\nabla_{x}u_{t}^{ij}=A_{t}^{ij}
are uniformly bounded on
[0,1]\times\mathsf{B}_{R}
.
Differentiating
\bar{u}_{t}^{\pi}
with respect to
x
, we get

\nabla_{x}\bar{u}_{t}^{\pi}(x)=\sum_{i,j}\left[u_{t}^{ij}(x)\otimes\nabla_{x}\gamma_{ij}^{\pi}(t,x)+\gamma_{ij}^{\pi}(t,x)A_{t}^{ij}\right].

The right-hand side is uniformly bounded on
[0,1]\times\mathsf{B}_{R}
.
Hence, there exists a constant
C_{R}<+\infty
such that

\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\sup_{x\in\mathsf{B}_{R}}\left\lVert\nabla_{x}\bar{u}_{t}^{\pi}(x)\right\rVert\leqslant C_{R}.

From the mean-value theorem
[
52
, Chapter 5]

\displaystyle\left\lVert\bar{u}_{t}^{\pi}(x)-\bar{u}_{t}^{\pi}(y)\right\rVert\leqslant C_{R}\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert\quad\text{with }x,y\in\mathsf{B}_{R}.

Thus, we may choose
t\mapsto L_{R}(t)\coloneqq C_{R}
.
Therefore,
\bar{u}^{\pi}
satisfies the local Lipschitz condition required in
\mathcal{U}_{\rm reg}
. Combining the preceding linear-growth estimate and the local-Lipschitz estimate, we conclude that
\bar{u}^{\pi}\in\mathcal{U}_{\rm reg}
. The proof is complete.
∎

3.6.1.
Feasibility of the projected drift

Having constructed the posterior-averaged drift
\bar{u}^{\pi}
, we state the corresponding feasibility and energy properties.
These are direct consequences of the componentwise Fokker–Planck equations and the convexity of the kinetic energy, and are included to make the connection with Problem
2
explicit.

Proposition 6

(Projected feasibility and energy bound)
.

Let
\pi\in\Pi(\alpha^{0},\alpha^{1})
, and suppose that the posterior-averaged drift

\bar{u}^{\pi}
belongs to
\mathcal{U}_{\rm reg}
.
Then, the pair

(\rho_{t}^{\pi},\bar{u}_{t}^{\pi})
satisfies

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho_{t}^{\pi}+\nabla\cdot(\rho_{t}^{\pi}\bar{u}_{t}^{\pi})-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\Delta\rho_{t}^{\pi}=0,

on
(0,1)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Moreover,
\rho_{0}^{\pi}=\rho_{0}
,
\rho_{1}^{\pi}=\rho_{1}.
Futhermore, the projected Markov feedback satisfies the kinetic-energy bound

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\rm proj}(\pi)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{2}\rho_{t}^{\pi}(x)\|\bar{u}_{t}^{\pi}(x)\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\leqslant\mathcal{J}_{\rm lift}(\pi)\coloneqq\sum_{i,j}\pi_{ij}C_{ij}.

Proof.

By definition,
for each
(i,j)
, the pairwise bridge density
\rho_{t}^{ij}
and corresponding drift
u_{t}^{ij}
satisfy

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho_{t}^{ij}+\nabla\cdot(\rho_{t}^{ij}u_{t}^{ij})-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\Delta\rho_{t}^{ij}=0.

Multiplying by
\pi_{ij}
and summing over
(i,j)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\Big(\sum_{i,j}\pi_{ij}\rho_{t}^{ij}\Big)+\nabla\cdot\Big(\sum_{i,j}\pi_{ij}\rho_{t}^{ij}u_{t}^{ij}\Big)-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\Delta\Big(\sum_{i,j}\pi_{ij}\rho_{t}^{ij}\Big)=0.

By definition,
\rho_{t}^{\pi}=\sum_{i,j}\pi_{ij}\rho_{t}^{ij}
.
Moreover, from (
(34)
),
\rho_{t}^{\pi}\bar{u}_{t}^{\pi}=\sum_{i,j}\pi_{ij}\rho_{t}^{ij}u_{t}^{ij}.

Substituting these identities yields

\partial_{t}\rho_{t}^{\pi}+\nabla\cdot(\rho_{t}^{\pi}\bar{u}_{t}^{\pi})-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\Delta\rho_{t}^{\pi}=0.

The endpoint identities follow from Proposition
3
. It remains to prove the energy bound. Applying Jensen’s inequality on
\bar{u}_{t}^{\pi}(x)
gives

\displaystyle\left\lVert\bar{u}_{t}^{\pi}(x)\right\rVert^{2}\leqslant\sum_{i,j}\gamma_{ij}^{\pi}(t,x)\|u_{t}^{ij}(x)\|^{2}.

Multiplying by
\rho_{t}^{\pi}(x)
and using
\rho_{t}^{\pi}(x)\gamma_{ij}^{\pi}(t,x)=\pi_{ij}\rho_{t}^{ij}(x),
we obtain

\displaystyle\rho_{t}^{\pi}(x)\left\lVert\bar{u}_{t}^{\pi}(x)\right\rVert^{2}\leqslant\sum_{i,j}\pi_{ij}\rho_{t}^{ij}(x)\|u_{t}^{ij}(x)\|^{2}.

Finally, integrating over
[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
gives
\mathcal{J}_{\rm proj}(\pi)\leqslant\mathcal{J}_{\rm lift}(\pi)
.
∎

We have the following immediate corollary.

Corollary 1

(Admissibility for Problem
2
)
.

For every
\pi\in\Pi(\alpha^{0},\alpha^{1})
, the posterior-averaged drift
\bar{u}^{\pi}
belongs to
\mathcal{U}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})
.
In particular, the admissible set in Problem 1 is nonempty.

Proof.

By Lemma
1
,
\bar{u}^{\pi}\in\mathcal{U}_{\rm reg}
, so the SDE
\mathrm{d}x_{t}=\bar{u}_{t}^{\pi}(x_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,\mathrm{d}w_{t},
with
x_{0}\sim\rho_{0}
admits a unique strong solution.
Its one-time marginals solve the Fokker–Planck equation associated with
\bar{u}^{\pi}
.
By Proposition
6
, the explicitly constructed density
\rho_{t}^{\pi}
solves the same Fokker–Planck equation and satisfies
\rho_{0}^{\pi}=\rho_{0}
.
By uniqueness of the marginal/Fokker–Planck flow for drifts in
\mathcal{U}_{\rm reg}
, the law of
x_{t}
has density
\rho_{t}^{\pi}
.
In particular,
x_{1}\sim\rho_{1}
.
Finally, Proposition
6
gives

\displaystyle\mathsf{E}\left[\int_{0}^{1}\left\lVert\bar{u}_{t}^{\pi}(x_{t})\right\rVert^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right]=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho_{t}^{\pi}(x)\left\lVert\bar{u}_{t}^{\pi}(x)\right\rVert^{2}\mathrm{d}x\ \mathrm{d}t<+\infty.

Hence,
\bar{u}^{\pi}\in\mathcal{U}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})
.
∎

Consequently, Problem
2
is feasible, and the projected construction gives an explicit finite-energy admissible feedback.

3.7.
Mean-Field Extension

The lifted construction also provides a natural way to formulate the mean-field Schrödinger bridge problem
[
50
,
22
]
with Gaussian-mixture endpoints.
To this end, consider the linear McKean–Vlasov dynamics

\mathrm{d}x_{t}=A_{t}x_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t+\bar{A}_{t}\bar{x}_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t+B_{t}u_{t}(x_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+D_{t}\,\mathrm{d}w_{t},\quad\text{with }\bar{x}_{t}\coloneqq\mathsf{E}[x_{t}],

with the same prescribed Gaussian-mixture boundary distributions
\rho_{0}
and
\rho_{1}
.
Let

\displaystyle\hat{x}_{0}\coloneqq\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\alpha_{i}^{0}m_{i}^{0},\quad\hat{x}_{1}\coloneqq\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\alpha_{j}^{1}m_{j}^{1},

denote the endpoint means.
In the unconstrained case, the mean-field term can be separated by writing
x_{t}=\bar{x}_{t}+\tilde{x}_{t},
and
u_{t}=\bar{u}_{t}+\tilde{u}_{t},
where
\bar{x}_{t}\coloneqq\mathsf{E}[x_{t}]
,
\bar{u}_{t}\coloneqq\mathsf{E}[u_{t}(x_{t})]
, and
\mathsf{E}[\tilde{x}_{t}]=\mathsf{E}[\tilde{u}_{t}]=0
.
This yields the deterministic mean-steering problem

\dot{\bar{x}}_{t}=(A_{t}+\bar{A}_{t})\bar{x}_{t}+B_{t}\bar{u}_{t}\qquad\,\bar{x}_{0}=\hat{x}_{0},\,\,\bar{x}_{1}=\hat{x}_{1},

together with the centered linear Schrödinger bridge

((35))

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\tilde{x}_{t}=A_{t}\tilde{x}_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t+B_{t}\tilde{u}_{t}(\tilde{x}_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+D_{t}\,\mathrm{d}w_{t},

whose boundary distributions are the shifted Gaussian mixtures

\tilde{\rho}_{0}\coloneqq\sum_{i\in I}\alpha_{i}^{0}\mathcal{N}(m_{i}^{0}-\hat{x}_{0},\Sigma_{i}^{0}),\qquad\tilde{\rho}_{1}\coloneqq\sum_{j\in J}\alpha_{j}^{1}\mathcal{N}(m_{j}^{1}-\hat{x}_{1},\Sigma_{j}^{1}).

The lifted construction can then be applied directly to this centered problem. Namely, for each source–target component pair
(i,j)
, one solves the Gaussian Schrödinger bridge, equivalently, the linear covariance-steering problem,

\mathcal{N}(m_{i}^{0}-\hat{x}_{0},\Sigma_{i}^{0})\longrightarrow\mathcal{N}(m_{j}^{1}-\hat{x}_{1},\Sigma_{j}^{1}),

under the centered linear dynamics (
(35)
).
Let the resulting pairwise law, density, drift, and kinetic cost be denoted by
\tilde{\mathsf{P}}^{ij}
,
\tilde{\rho}_{t}^{ij}
,
\tilde{u}_{t}^{ij}
, and
C_{ij}
, respectively.
Given a component coupling
\pi\in\Pi(\alpha^{0},\alpha^{1})
, define the lifted centered law
\bar{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}_{\mathsf{MF}}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\pi_{ij}\,\delta_{(i,j)}\otimes\tilde{\mathsf{P}}^{ij}.

Applying the same posterior-averaging construction used in (
(32)
)–(
(34)
), now to the centered variables, define the quantities

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}_{t}^{\pi}(z)\coloneqq\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\pi_{ij}\tilde{\rho}_{t}^{ij}(z),\,\,\tilde{u}_{t}^{\pi}(z)\coloneqq\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\tilde{\gamma}_{ij}(t,z)\tilde{u}_{t}^{ij}(z),\,\,\tilde{\gamma}_{ij}(t,z)\coloneqq\frac{\pi_{ij}\tilde{\rho}_{t}^{ij}(z)}{\tilde{\rho}_{t}^{\pi}(z)}.

The corresponding mean-field feedback in the original coordinates is then

u_{t}^{\pi}(x)=\bar{u}_{t}+\tilde{u}_{t}^{\pi}(x-\bar{x}_{t}),
and the induced density is
\rho_{t}^{\pi}(x)=\tilde{\rho}_{t}^{\pi}(x-\bar{x}_{t}).

Consequently, the unconstrained mean-field problem can be viewed as the composition of a deterministic mean-steering problem and a lifted centered Gaussian-mixture Schrödinger bridge.
In this sense, the mixture-policy construction used for unconstrained mean-field Schrödinger bridges with Gaussian-mixture endpoints is precisely the projected Markov feedback associated with a component-labeled lifted path law.

3.8.
Hidden-label memory and Markovian projection

In this section, we distinguish the projected path law
\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}
in (
(24)
) and the Markov law generated by the posterior-averaged feedback
\bar{u}^{\pi}
in
(34)
.
The former is obtained by forgetting the auxiliary label in the lifted law, whereas the latter is obtained by driving the Brownian diffusion with the state-feedback drift
\bar{u}^{\pi}
.
We denote by
\widehat{\mathsf{Q}}^{\pi}\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
the path law induced by

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x_{t}=\bar{u}_{t}^{\pi}(x_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,\mathrm{d}w_{t},\qquad x_{0}\sim\rho_{0}.

By Corollary
1
, this law is well defined, has finite kinetic energy, and satisfies
\operatorname{Law}_{\widehat{\mathsf{Q}}^{\pi}}(x_{t})=\rho_{t}^{\pi}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x
for
t\in[0,1]
.
On the other hand,
\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}
is generally a mixture of bridge laws with a hidden source–target label fixed for the whole trajectory.
Therefore, even though each
\mathsf{P}^{ij}
is Markov, the mixture
\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}
need not be Markov with respect to the observed state process. To this end, let

((36))

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{t}^{x}\coloneqq\sigma(x_{s}:0\leqslant s\leqslant t),

denote the observed state filtration.
Under the lifted law
\bar{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}
, define the pathwise posterior label probabilities
\Gamma_{ij}^{\pi}(t)\coloneqq\bar{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}\bigl(Z=(i,j)\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}^{x}\bigr).

This should be distinguished from
\gamma_{ij}^{\pi}
in (
(33)
). The former conditions on the whole observed trajectory up to time
t
, whereas the latter conditions only on the current state.
Define the
\mathcal{F}_{t}^{x}
-adapted
[
48
, Chapter I, §1]
hidden-label drift
b_{t}^{\pi}\coloneqq\sum_{i,j}\Gamma_{ij}^{\pi}(t)u_{t}^{ij}(x_{t}).

This drift is generally non-Markovian, because it may depend on the observed past through the posterior
\Gamma_{ij}^{\pi}(t)
.

Proposition 7

(Hidden-label drift and Markovian projection)
.

For every
\pi\in\Pi(\alpha^{0},\alpha^{1})
, under the projected path law
\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}=(\mathcal{P}_{\Omega})_{\#}\bar{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}
, there exists an
(\mathcal{F}_{t}^{x})_{t\in[0,1]}
-Brownian motion
\widetilde{w}
such that we have the semimartingale representation

\displaystyle x_{t}=x_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}b_{s}^{\pi}\,\mathrm{d}s+\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,\widetilde{w}_{t}\quad\text{for }\,t\in[0,1].

Moreover, for a.e.
t\in[0,1]
, the posterior-averaged drift is a version of the conditional expectation

\displaystyle\bar{u}_{t}^{\pi}(x)=\mathsf{E}^{\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}}\bigl[b_{t}^{\pi}\mid x_{t}=x\bigr],

for
\rho_{t}^{\pi}
-a.e.
x
. Consequently,
\bar{u}^{\pi}
is the
L^{2}(\mathrm{d}t\otimes\mathsf{Q}^{\pi})
-orthogonal projection
of the hidden-label drift
b^{\pi}
onto the class of feedbacks depending only on the current state, i.e.,

((37))

\displaystyle\bar{u}^{\pi}\in\arg\min_{v}\mathsf{E}^{\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}}\left[\int_{0}^{1}\|b_{t}^{\pi}-v_{t}(x_{t})\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\right],

where the minimization is over measurable feedbacks
(t,x)\mapsto v_{t}(x)
for which the expression is finite.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{t}\coloneqq\sigma(Z)\vee\mathcal{F}_{t}^{x}
be the smallest sigma algebra containing both
\sigma(Z)
and
\mathcal{F}_{t}^{x}
, where
F_{t}^{x}
as in
(
(36)
).
For notational convenience, define the labeled drift
u_{t}^{Z}(x_{t})\coloneqq u_{t}^{ij}(x_{t})
on the event
\{Z=(i,j)\}.
Under
\bar{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}
, conditional on
Z=(i,j)
, the canonical process has law

\mathsf{P}^{ij}
.
Hence, in the enlarged filtration
\mathcal{G}_{t}
, the process
M_{t}\coloneqq x_{t}-x_{0}-\int_{0}^{t}u_{s}^{Z}(x_{s})\,\mathrm{d}s

is a continuous
\bar{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}
-local martingale with quadratic variation
\langle M\rangle_{t}=\varepsilon tI_{d}.

This is the standard semimartingale decomposition of a diffusion in its natural enlarged filtration; see
[
48
, Chapter II, §1 and §6]
and
[
30
, Chapter 3, §2–4]
.

Define the optional projection
[
48
, Chapter 7]
,
[
34
, Chapter 10]
,
[
4
, Chapter 2]
of the labeled drift onto the observed filtration by

\displaystyle b_{t}^{\pi}\coloneqq\mathsf{E}^{\bar{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}}\bigl[u_{t}^{Z}(x_{t})\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}^{x}\bigr]=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\bar{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}\bigl(Z=(i,j)\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}^{X}\bigr)u_{t}^{ij}(x_{t}).

We next verify that
b_{t}^{\pi}
is indeed the drift of the projected process with respect to
(\mathcal{F}_{t}^{x})
.
Define

\displaystyle\widetilde{M}_{t}\coloneqq x_{t}-x_{0}-\int_{0}^{t}b_{s}^{\pi}\,\mathrm{d}s.

Let
0\leqslant s\leqslant t
and
A\in\mathcal{F}_{s}^{x}
. Since
A\in\mathcal{G}_{s}
, the
(\mathcal{G}_{t})
-local martingale property of
M
, after localization if necessary, yields

\displaystyle\mathsf{E}^{\bar{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}}\left[\mathbf{1}_{A}\left(x_{t}-x_{s}-\int_{s}^{t}u_{r}^{Z}(x_{r})\,\mathrm{d}r\right)\right]=0.

Moreover, by the definition of
b_{r}^{\pi}
as the conditional expectation of
u_{r}^{Z}(x_{r})
given
\mathcal{F}_{r}^{x}
, and since

A\in\mathcal{F}_{s}^{x}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{r}^{x}
for
r\in[s,t]
, Fubini’s theorem
[
25
, Theorem 2.37]
and the tower property of expectation give

\displaystyle\mathsf{E}^{\bar{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}}\left[\mathbf{1}_{A}\int_{s}^{t}u_{r}^{Z}(x_{r})\,\mathrm{d}r\right]=\mathsf{E}^{\bar{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}}\left[\mathbf{1}_{A}\int_{s}^{t}b_{r}^{\pi}\,\mathrm{d}r\right].

Therefore,
\mathsf{E}^{\bar{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}}\bigl[\mathbf{1}_{A}\bigl(\widetilde{M}_{t}-\widetilde{M}_{s}\bigr)\bigr]=0.

Hence,
\widetilde{M}
is an
(\mathcal{F}_{t}^{x})
-local martingale under the projected law
\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}
.
Consequently,

\displaystyle x_{t}=x_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}b_{s}^{\pi}\,\mathrm{d}s+\widetilde{M}_{t},

is the
(\mathcal{F}_{t}^{x})
-semimartingale decomposition of the projected process.

Since
\widetilde{M}
differs from
x
only by a finite-variation process, subtracting the drift term does not change quadratic variation.
Moreover, under each conditional
law
\mathsf{P}^{ij}
, the coordinate process has diffusion coefficient
\sqrt{\varepsilon}I_{d}
.
Hence, under the projected law
\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}
, the matrix-valued quadratic variation of the continuous local martingale
\widetilde{M}
is
\langle\widetilde{M}\rangle_{t}=\varepsilon tI_{d}
for all
t\in[0,1]
; see
[
48
, Chapter II, §6]
.
Therefore, by the multidimensional Lévy characterization of Brownian motion, there exists an
(\mathcal{F}_{t}^{x})
-Brownian motion
(\widetilde{w}_{t})_{t\in[0,1]]}
under

\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}
such that
\widetilde{M}_{t}=\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,\widetilde{w}_{t};

see
[
30
, Chapter 3, §3]
or

[
51
, Chapter IV, §3]
.
Thus,

((38))

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x_{t}=b_{t}^{\pi}\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,\mathrm{d}\widetilde{w}_{t},

with respect to the observed filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{t}^{x})
.

We now identify the Markov projection of the adapted hidden-label drift
b_{t}^{\pi}
appearing in (
(38)
).
Since
\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}
is the path-space marginal of
\bar{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}
, conditional expectations of path-measurable quantities may be computed under
\bar{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}
. Using the tower property:

\displaystyle\mathsf{E}^{\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}}\bigl[b_{t}^{\pi}\mid x_{t}=x\bigr]=\mathsf{E}^{\bar{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}}\left[\mathsf{E}^{\bar{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}}\bigl[u_{t}^{Z}(x_{t})\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}^{x}\bigr]\mid x_{t}=x\right]=\mathsf{E}^{\bar{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}}\bigl[u_{t}^{Z}(x_{t})\mid x_{t}=x\bigr].

By the definition of the posterior weights,
\bar{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}\bigl(Z=(i,j)\mid x_{t}=x\bigr)=\gamma_{ij}^{\pi}(t,x).
Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathsf{E}^{\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}}\bigl[b_{t}^{\pi}\mid x_{t}=x\bigr]=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\gamma_{ij}^{\pi}(t,x)u_{t}^{ij}(x)=\bar{u}_{t}^{\pi}(x).

Finally, the variational characterization (
(37)
)
follows from the conditional-expectation projection theorem in
L^{2}
: among all square-integrable feedbacks
v_{t}(x_{t})
, the unique
L^{2}
-orthogonal projection of
b_{t}^{\pi}
onto the closed subspace of functions measurable with respect to
x_{t}
is
\mathsf{E}^{\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}}\bigl[b_{t}^{\pi}\mid x_{t}\bigr]=\bar{u}_{t}^{\pi}(x_{t}).

See, for example,
[
30
, Ch. 1, Sec. 2]
,
[
3
,
5
]
. This proves the result.
∎

Proposition
7
clarifies the status of the posterior-averaged feedback.
As emphasized before, it is
not
introduced as an independent ansatz.
Rather, it is the
Markovian projection
, in the conditional-expectation sense, of the non-Markov hidden-label drift associated with
\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}
.
Thus, the state-feedback
\bar{u}^{\pi}
is obtained by replacing the full path-posterior label information
\Gamma_{ij}^{\pi}(t)
with the current-state posterior
\gamma_{ij}^{\pi}(t,x_{t})
. We now state the final result of this article.

Theorem 3

(Entropy decomposition through Markovization)
.

Recall that
\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}=(\mathcal{P}_{\Omega})_{\#}\bar{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}
, and let
\widehat{\mathsf{Q}}^{\pi}
be the Markov path law generated by
\bar{u}^{\pi}
.
Then,

((39))

\displaystyle\varepsilon\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}\|\mathsf{R}^{\rho_{0}})=\varepsilon\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\widehat{\mathsf{Q}}^{\pi}\|\mathsf{R}^{\rho_{0}})+\varepsilon\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}\|\widehat{\mathsf{Q}}^{\pi}).

Moreover,

\displaystyle\varepsilon\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}\|\widehat{\mathsf{Q}}^{\pi})=\frac{1}{2}\mathsf{E}^{\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}}\left[\int_{0}^{1}\|b_{t}^{\pi}-\bar{u}_{t}^{\pi}(x_{t})\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\right].

Combining this with the projection-gap identity from §
3.5
gives the three-level decomposition

((40))

\displaystyle\varepsilon\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\bar{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}\|\bar{\mathsf{R}}^{\eta})=\varepsilon\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\widehat{\mathsf{Q}}^{\pi}\|\mathsf{R}^{\rho_{0}})+\varepsilon\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}\|\widehat{\mathsf{Q}}^{\pi})+\varepsilon\mathcal{G}(\pi,\eta).

Proof.

We first state the absolute continuity and integrability facts needed for the applications of Girsanov’s theorem
[
30
, Chapter 3, §3.5]
.
Since
\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\pi_{ij}\mathsf{P}^{ij}
and
\mathsf{R}^{\rho_{0}}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\alpha_{i}^{0}\mathsf{R}^{i},

and
\alpha_{i}^{0}>0
for all
i\in\mathcal{I}
, every
\mathsf{R}^{\rho_{0}}
-null set is an
\mathsf{R}^{i}
-null set for each
i\in\mathcal{I}
. Since
\mathsf{P}^{ij}\ll\mathsf{R}^{i}
, it follows that
\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}\ll\mathsf{R}^{\rho_{0}}.

Furthermore, under the lifted law
\bar{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}
, conditioned on
Z=(i,j)
, the path law is
\mathsf{P}^{ij}
.
Hence,

((41))

\displaystyle\mathsf{E}^{\bar{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}}\left[\int_{0}^{1}\|u_{t}^{Z}(x_{t})\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\right]=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\pi_{ij}\mathsf{E}^{\mathsf{P}^{ij}}\left[\int_{0}^{1}\|u_{t}^{ij}(x_{t})\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\right]=2\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\pi_{ij}C_{ij}<+\infty.

By Proposition
7
,
b_{t}^{\pi}=\mathsf{E}^{\bar{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}}\left[u_{t}^{Z}(x_{t})\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}^{x}\right].
Therefore, Jensen’s inequality gives

((42))

\displaystyle\mathsf{E}^{\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}}\left[\int_{0}^{1}\|b_{t}^{\pi}\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\right]\leqslant\mathsf{E}^{\bar{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}}\left[\int_{0}^{1}\|u_{t}^{Z}(x_{t})\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\right]<+\infty.

Thus,
\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}
has adapted drift
b_{t}^{\pi}
relative to the Brownian reference
\mathsf{R}^{\rho_{0}}
, with finite energy.
From the finite-energy form of Girsanov’s theorem, we have that

((43))

\displaystyle\varepsilon\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}\|\mathsf{R}^{\rho_{0}})=\frac{1}{2}\mathsf{E}^{\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}}\left[\int_{0}^{1}\|b_{t}^{\pi}\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\right].

Similarly, since
\widehat{\mathsf{Q}}^{\pi}
is the law of the Markov diffusion
\mathrm{d}x_{t}=\bar{u}_{t}^{\pi}(x_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,\mathrm{d}w_{t}
with
x_{0}\sim\rho_{0},
and the corresponding Markov feedback has finite quadratic energy, another application of the finite-energy form of Girsanov’s theorem gives

((44))

\displaystyle\varepsilon\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\widehat{\mathsf{Q}}^{\pi}\|\mathsf{R}^{\rho_{0}})=\frac{1}{2}\mathsf{E}^{\widehat{\mathsf{Q}}^{\pi}}\left[\int_{0}^{1}\|\bar{u}_{t}^{\pi}(x_{t})\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\right].

Since both
\widehat{\mathsf{Q}}^{\pi}
and
\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}
have one-time marginals
\rho_{t}^{\pi}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\varepsilon\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\widehat{\mathsf{Q}}^{\pi}\|\mathsf{R}^{\rho_{0}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathsf{E}^{\widehat{\mathsf{Q}}^{\pi}}\left[\int_{0}^{1}\|\bar{u}_{t}^{\pi}(x_{t})\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\right]=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{2}\rho_{t}^{\pi}(x)\|\bar{u}_{t}^{\pi}(x)\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

((45))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathsf{E}^{\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}}\left[\int_{0}^{1}\|\bar{u}_{t}^{\pi}(x_{t})\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\right].

We now compare
\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}
and
\widehat{\mathsf{Q}}^{\pi}
.
Under
\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}
, the adapted drift is
b_{t}^{\pi}
, while under
\widehat{\mathsf{Q}}^{\pi}
, the adapted drift is
\bar{u}_{t}^{\pi}(x_{t})
.
The two laws have the same initial law and the same nondegenerate diffusion coefficient. Moreover, the energy identities (
(44)
) and (
3.8
) imply

((46))

\displaystyle\mathsf{E}^{\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}}\left[\int_{0}^{1}\|b_{t}^{\pi}-\bar{u}_{t}^{\pi}(x_{t})\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\right]<+\infty,

because
\|b_{t}^{\pi}-\bar{u}_{t}^{\pi}(x_{t})\|^{2}\leqslant 2\|b_{t}^{\pi}\|^{2}+2\|\bar{u}_{t}^{\pi}(x_{t})\|^{2},
and the two terms on the right-hand side are integrable under
\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}
by (
(44)
) and (
3.8
), respectively.
The finite-energy estimate (
(46)
) implies, by Girsanov’s theorem, that
\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}\ll\widehat{\mathsf{Q}}^{\pi}
.
Therefore, the standard finite-energy
relative-entropy formula
[
30
, Chapter 3, Section 5]

for two diffusion laws with the same initial law and the same nondegenerate diffusion coefficient gives

((47))

\displaystyle\varepsilon\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}\|\widehat{\mathsf{Q}}^{\pi})=\frac{1}{2}\mathsf{E}^{\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}}\left[\int_{0}^{1}\|b_{t}^{\pi}-\bar{u}_{t}^{\pi}(x_{t})\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\right].

Finally, we show the first decomposition (
(39)
).
By Proposition
7
,
\bar{u}_{t}^{\pi}(x_{t})=\mathsf{E}^{\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}}\bigl[b_{t}^{\pi}\mid x_{t}\bigr].
Hence, the orthogonality property of conditional expectation gives, for a.e.
t\in[0,1]
,

((48))

\displaystyle\mathsf{E}^{\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}}\left[\bigl(b_{t}^{\pi}-\bar{u}_{t}^{\pi}(x_{t})\bigr)\cdot\bar{u}_{t}^{\pi}(x_{t})\right]=0.

Consequently, we have
\mathsf{E}^{\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}}\|b_{t}^{\pi}\|^{2}=\mathsf{E}^{\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}}\|\bar{u}_{t}^{\pi}(x_{t})\|^{2}+\mathsf{E}^{\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}}\|b_{t}^{\pi}-\bar{u}_{t}^{\pi}(x_{t})\|^{2},
for a.e.
t\in[0,1]
.
Integrating with respect to time and using the three relative-entropy identities (
(44)
)–(
(46)
)
gives

((49))

\displaystyle\varepsilon\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}\|\mathsf{R}^{\rho_{0}})=\varepsilon\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\widehat{\mathsf{Q}}^{\pi}\|\mathsf{R}^{\rho_{0}})+\varepsilon\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}\|\widehat{\mathsf{Q}}^{\pi}).

Remark 6

(When does the Markovization loss vanish?)
.

The term
\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}\|\widehat{\mathsf{Q}}^{\pi})
vanishes if and only if
\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}=\widehat{\mathsf{Q}}^{\pi}
.
By the Girsanov representation above, this is equivalent to
b_{t}^{\pi}=\bar{u}_{t}^{\pi}(x_{t})
for
\mathrm{d}t\otimes\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}
-a.e.
(t,\omega)
.
In other words, the hidden-label drift must already be determined by the current state, i.e., the additional label information contained in the observed past path must not improve the prediction of the current drift beyond what is already known from
x_{t}
.
The condition
b_{t}^{\pi}=\bar{u}_{t}^{\pi}(x_{t})
for
\mathrm{d}t\otimes\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}
-a.e.
(t,\omega)
,
fails, in general, because the past trajectory may contain information about which component pair
(i,j)
was selected, and this information is not usually contained in the current state alone.
Thus,
\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}
is generally non-Markov, whereas
\widehat{\mathsf{Q}}^{\pi}
is Markov by construction.
The condition can, nevertheless, hold in useful cases.
For example, if all active pairwise bridges have the same feedback drift, say
x\mapsto u_{t}^{ij}(x)\coloneqq u_{t}^{\circ}(x)
for all
(i,j)\in\supp\pi,
then both the hidden-label drift
b_{t}^{\pi}
and the posterior-averaged drift
\bar{u}_{t}^{\pi}(x_{t})
reduce to
u_{t}^{\circ}(x_{t})
, independently of the posterior label probabilities, and hence
\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\mathsf{Q}^{\pi}\|\widehat{\mathsf{Q}}^{\pi})=0
.
This situation includes, for instance, a common open-loop feedforward steering law
x\mapsto u_{t}^{ij}(x)\coloneqq v_{t}
for all active pairs, identical affine covariance-steering feedbacks
x\mapsto u_{t}^{ij}(x)\coloneqq A_{t}x+c_{t}
, a rigid translation of all active Gaussian components by the same displacement vector, or a symmetric multi-agent formation-control setting in which several label assignments induce the same instantaneous averaged velocity field.

5.
Concluding remarks

In this article, we developed a lifted Schrödinger bridge construction for Gaussian-mixture endpoint distributions by augmenting trajectories with source–target component labels, thereby reducing the mixture-to-mixture problem to explicit Gaussian component bridges and a finite-dimensional entropic coupling problem.
Projecting the lifted law back to the original state space yields a feasible feedback construction that matches the prescribed endpoints, admits a certified kinetic-energy upper bound, and is numerically shown to achieve performance very close to direct grid-based Schrödinger bridge solvers, while being numerically faster and retaining a much more interpretable and low-dimensional component-level structure.

Several extensions of this work are natural. First, the Brownian Gaussian component bridges can be replaced by linear-system covariance-steering bridges, or, more generally, by numerically computed nonlinear-prior Schrödinger bridges.
In that case, the lifted architecture remains the same, but the component quantities
(\rho_{t}^{ij},u_{t}^{ij},C_{ij})
are computed numerically rather than in closed form. Second, state constraints can be incorporated at the component level by replacing each pairwise bridge with a constrained Schrödinger bridge or chance-constrained covariance-steering subproblem; the finite-dimensional assignment layer is then preserved, while the costs
C_{ij}
encode constrained transfer geometry.
Third, unconstrained linear mean-field Schrödinger bridges with Gaussian-mixture endpoints can be handled by combining deterministic mean steering with a lifted centered Gaussian-mixture bridge. Stochastic maximum principle
[
18
,
17
,
19
,
21
]
, unbalanced optimal transport
[
14
,
43
]
, and optimal stopping
[
58
,
23
]
based viewpoints would also be interesting. Finally, a sharper analysis of the projection gap between the lifted and unlabeled path laws would further clarify when the lifted construction is close to the true unlabeled optimum.

Proof of Proposition
2
.

Let
(x_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
denote the canonical process under
\mathsf{P}^{ij}
.
Since
\mathsf{P}^{ij}
is a Brownian reciprocal process with Gaussian endpoint law, conditional on
(x_{0},x_{1})
, the path is a Brownian bridge. Therefore, for every
t\in[0,1]
,
x_{t}=(1-t)x_{0}+tx_{1}+\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,B_{t}^{\mathrm{br}},
where
(B_{t}^{\mathrm{br}})_{t\in[0,1]}
is a standard Brownian bridge, independent of
(x_{0},x_{1})
, satisfying
\mathsf{E}[B_{t}^{\mathrm{br}}]=0,
and
\operatorname{Cov}(B_{t}^{\mathrm{br}})=t(1-t)I.

Taking expectations gives

\mathsf{E}[x_{t}]=(1-t)\mathsf{E}[x_{0}]+t\,\mathsf{E}[x_{1}]=(1-t)m_{i}^{0}+t\,m_{j}^{1},

which proves the formula for
m_{t}^{ij}
.
Using independence of
B_{t}^{\mathrm{br}}
from
(x_{0},x_{1})
and
\operatorname{Cov}(x_{0},x_{1})=\Sigma_{01}^{ij}
, we obtain
\operatorname{Cov}(x_{t})=\operatorname{Cov}\big((1-t)x_{0}+tx_{1}\big)+\varepsilon\,\operatorname{Cov}(B_{t}^{\mathrm{br}}).

Hence,

\Sigma_{t}^{ij}=(1-t)^{2}\Sigma_{i}^{0}+t^{2}\Sigma_{j}^{1}+t(1-t)\big(\Sigma_{01}^{ij}+(\Sigma_{01}^{ij})^{\top}\big)+\varepsilon\,t(1-t)I,

as desired. In particular,
\Sigma_{t}^{ij}\in\mathbb{S}_{++}^{d}
for every
t\in[0,1]
, so
(\Sigma_{t}^{ij})^{-1}
is well defined.
Next, we derive the drift.
For a Brownian bridge with random endpoints, the drift is
[
30
, Chapter 5, Section 6]

u_{t}^{ij}(x)=\frac{1}{1-t}\big(\mathsf{E}[x_{1}\mid x_{t}=x]-x\big)\quad\text{for }t\in[0,1).

Since
(x_{t},x_{1})
is jointly Gaussian, the conditional expectation is affine, and thus, we get
\mathsf{E}[x_{1}\mid x_{t}=x]=m_{j}^{1}+\operatorname{Cov}(x_{1},x_{t})(\Sigma_{t}^{ij})^{-1}(x-m_{t}^{ij}).

Moreover,
\operatorname{Cov}(x_{1},x_{t})=(1-t)(\Sigma_{01}^{ij})^{\top}+t\,\Sigma_{j}^{1}.

Therefore,

u_{t}^{ij}(x)=\frac{m_{j}^{1}-m_{t}^{ij}}{1-t}+\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(x_{1},x_{t})-\Sigma_{t}^{ij}}{1-t}(\Sigma_{t}^{ij})^{-1}(x-m_{t}^{ij}).

Since
m_{j}^{1}-m_{t}^{ij}=(1-t)(m_{j}^{1}-m_{i}^{0}),
the constant term becomes
c_{ij}=m_{j}^{1}-m_{i}^{0}.

Also,

\operatorname{Cov}(x_{1},x_{t})-\Sigma_{t}^{ij}=(1-t)\Big((1-t)\big((\Sigma_{01}^{ij})^{\top}-\Sigma_{i}^{0}\big)+t\big(\Sigma_{j}^{1}-\Sigma_{01}^{ij}-\varepsilon I\big)\Big),

and hence,

\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(x_{1},x_{t})-\Sigma_{t}^{ij}}{1-t}=(1-t)\big((\Sigma_{01}^{ij})^{\top}-\Sigma_{i}^{0}\big)+t\big(\Sigma_{j}^{1}-\Sigma_{01}^{ij}-\varepsilon I\big)=S_{t}^{ij}.

Thus, we have
u_{t}^{ij}(x)=S_{t}^{ij}(\Sigma_{t}^{ij})^{-1}(x-m_{t}^{ij})+c_{ij},
which proves the affine drift formula in Proposition
(-b)
.

Finally, by definition of the pairwise cost and the Girsanov identity, we have that

C_{ij}=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{2}\,\rho_{t}^{ij}(x)\,\|u_{t}^{ij}(x)\|^{2}\ \mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t.

Since under
\rho_{t}^{ij}
one has
x_{t}-m_{t}^{ij}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{t}^{ij})
, and
u_{t}^{ij}(x_{t})=A_{t}^{ij}(x_{t}-m_{t}^{ij})+c_{ij},
the cross term vanishes after expectation, because
\mathsf{E}[x_{t}-m_{t}^{ij}]=0
.
Hence,
\mathsf{E}\big[\|u_{t}^{ij}(x_{t})\|^{2}\big]=\operatorname{tr}\!\Big(A_{t}^{ij}\Sigma_{t}^{ij}(A_{t}^{ij})^{\top}\Big)+\|c_{ij}\|^{2}.

Therefore,

C_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\left[\operatorname{tr}\!\Big(A_{t}^{ij}\Sigma_{t}^{ij}(A_{t}^{ij})^{\top}\Big)+\|c_{ij}\|^{2}\right]\mathrm{d}t.

Since
c_{ij}=m_{j}^{1}-m_{i}^{0}
is constant in time, this becomes

C_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}\|m_{j}^{1}-m_{i}^{0}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\operatorname{tr}\!\Big(A_{t}^{ij}\Sigma_{t}^{ij}(A_{t}^{ij})^{\top}\Big)\,\mathrm{d}t,

thus completing the proof.
∎
