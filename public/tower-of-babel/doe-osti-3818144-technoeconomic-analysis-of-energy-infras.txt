Disclaimer and Limitations

REopt is an NLR tool used to help evaluate the technoeconomic parameters associated with potential energy projects. REopt
analyses provide valuable technoeconomic insights into energy projects for behind-the-meter applications. This can help users
assess cost-optimal photovoltaic (PV) array sizes, whether or not it is cost-effective over the lifespan of the project to include battery
storage, or help predict the cost of meeting certain energy goals. As an energy model, REopt has inherent limitations. REopt results
assume perfect prediction of solar irradiance and electrical loads. Actual savings may be lower than REopt reports based on the
ability to accurately predict solar irradiance and load, and the control strategies used in the system. Therefore, investment
decisions should not be made off of REopt analyses alone.

 Introduction

Objective and Summary: This presentation provides an overview of three REopt analyses completed for the
Communities LEAP, Cohort 2 program in 2025. The objective of these analyses was to evaluate the potential of solar PV
to reduce long-term energy costs on three buildings in Alachua County, Florida, under a variety of constraints.

Buildings Overview:
• Facilities Management Office: Target pilot building, existing construction.
 Initial analysis presented to the coalition: January 31, 2025
• Civil Courthouse: New construction, operations currently housed in another building.
 Initial analysis presented to the coalition: May 12, 2025
• Environmental Protection Department (EPD) Lands Building: New construction, new operations.
 Initial analysis presented to the coalition: October 10, 2025

 Methodology: Analysis Overview

The community coalition requested NLR analyze the following three economic conditions, under two distinct sizing
scenarios of PV and BESS:
Economic Conditions:
• Base Case ITC: Includes a 30% ITC in Year 1.
• Without ITC: Evaluates project savings assuming 0% ITC, to assess project viability without tax incentive eligibility.
• Fully Grant-Funded: 100% of capital costs are covered by external grants, with no ITC applied.
Sizing Conditions:
• Installer Sizing: Given the limited space on the building's roof and high loads expected, a local energy developer
 external to NLR modeled high-wattage panels on the building's roof. This sizing aims to maximize the production of
 solar energy on the given roof space. This scenario will analyze the economic impact of this sizing.
• Cost-Optimal Sizing: In this scenario, REopt will be used to determine the cost-optimal sizing of PV in the absence of
 space constraints. This scenario estimates the PV size that will minimize lifecycle costs over the panel's assumed
 lifespan of 25 years.

                                                                                                     National Laboratory of the Rockies   |   21

 Methodology: Load Determination

Baseline Load Data:
Existing Civil Courthouse (101,000 s.f.) used as a
proxy for estimating energy demand. New
Courthouse Complex is slightly smaller (95,506 s.f.).
The five-year average of monthly load data for the
existing building was scaled down by ~5%
accordingly.
Hourly Load Profile:
Modeled using the DOE Commercial Reference
Building for a Medium Office. Hourly profile
adjusted to match the five-year average of monthly
load data provided by the site. Average simulated
loads are 123 kW, with peaks of 358 kW occurring in
August.
Demand Charge Consideration:
Demand charges are highly sensitive to the timing
of peak usage. If actual peak demand at the new
facility differs significantly from the modeled profile,
projected demand charge savings may be over or
underestimated.

 Key Considerations: Mounting

Ballasted Mounting
• Often used for flat roofs—no roof penetration required
• Relies on weight (ballast) to secure the system
• Alternatives include mechanically attached systems—secured with
 fasteners that penetrate roof structure
Landscape (Horizontal) Panel Orientation
• Reduces wind uplift forces due to lower panel profile
 ◦ Helps prevent panel displacement and roof damage during high winds
 ◦ Reduces ballast requirements
• May limit the density of panels compared to portrait (vertical)
Considerations for Ballasted Systems
• Additional Structural Load: Roof must support the weight of ballasts
 (typically concrete blocks)
• Higher Material Costs: Ballasts add to total system cost
• Roof Maintenance Access: Must allow space for access pathways

 Battery Energy Storage Analysis

Objective:
• Assess the economic impact of adding battery energy storage to the Courthouse complex. While previous scenarios
 considered only solar PV, this analysis considers both unconstrained PV and battery storage.

Courthouse Cost Considerations
• The courthouse complex has electrical loads comparable to a small commercial building.
• The peak loads of the Courthouse Complex are expected to be 358 kW, and average loads around 123 kW, which
 would likely require multiple battery units to cover.

Cost Considerations
• Modeled battery prices are derived from the cost of a Tesla Powerwall 3 in May 2025. The Powerwall is used as an
 example, representing a scalable, commercially-available, residential battery system.

  Battery Energy Storage
 Analysis
The REopt model suggests that the least-cost PV+BESS solution includes 76.68 kW PV paired with a 16 kWh (12 kW
AC) battery at the current battery pricing assumptions. The inclusion of battery storage reduces expected exports to utility
by 26% (from $86 in Year One down to $64) by storing more energy for on-site use. This improves system value by
avoiding low export credits (4¢/kWh) and offsetting higher utility rates (11.42¢/kWh). A battery system of this size cannot
meet peak load but could contribute to increased utility savings.

 Discussion

PV-Only Installer-Sized Scenario (76.68 kW):
• Models a larger system designed to maximize PV generation.
• System is estimated to pay itself back in under 10 years when owner is ITC-eligible.
• NPV:
      ◦   $48,886 with ITC
 ◦ $805 without ITC
 ◦ $171,188 with grant funding

Least-Cost Solution, PV-Only:
• At the expected price point, the least-cost solution is the largest amount of PV that can fit, which is the Installer-Sized Scenario.
Impact of Incentives and Funding:
• In both PV-only installation scenarios, the absence of ITC leads to a lower NPV, and ITC eligibility results in higher NPVs.
Battery Energy Storage:
• At prices similar to the Tesla Powerwall 3, residential-scale battery energy storage systems contribute to higher NPVs for the
 courthouse complex by reducing utility purchases, and batteries of approximately 16 kWh become part of a least-cost PV+BESS
 solution.

 Methodology: Uncertainty of Sensitive Parameters

This analysis is designed to support informed decision making, even as future conditions change. Factors like available
incentives, PV equipment and installation costs, and building energy loads are likely to shift over time or differ from
our present assumptions. This “sensitivity” analysis adjusts key assumptions to see how they influence model results.
Key sensitive parameters analyzed in this report include:
1. ITC eligibility

 The One Big Beautiful Bill Act accelerates ITC eligibility phaseout for both commercial and residential PV projects, and it
 subjects these projects to additional Foreign Entity of Concern provisions that may affect entities' abilities to procure PV panels
 in compliance with ITC eligibility requirements.

2. PV equipment costs

 PV equipment costs are subject to change and may be difficult to predict without upfront installer quotes and site visits to an
 existing building. This analysis will identify several potential unit costs, such that project economics can be assessed at different
 capital costs.

3. Load profile

 This building is not yet constructed or hosting operations elsewhere (office staff are not operating in another existing building
 that could be used as a proxy). This analysis will consider what happens to optimal PV sizes if the actual loads of the building
 are higher or lower than expected from the DOE commercial reference building estimation.

 Methodology: Load Determination

Load Profile:
Considering this is a new construction with no direct proxy for load within
the county, the load is modeled using the DOE Commercial Reference
Building for a Small Office, scaled to the size of the new construction.

The annual estimated load for a small office near Orlando, Florida, of
8,700 s.f. is 155,821 kWh/year. We will use this as our baseline load and
adjust this value ± 15% in our sensitivity analysis to account for the
possibility that the actual load is higher or lower than this baseline, Low Load Profile
affecting the optimal PV size and economics. The upper range of the load
estimates considers some HVAC and cooling load via a heat pump, but
the building load should ultimately be validated once the office is built and
receiving bills from the utility.

Demand Charge Consideration:
Demand charges are highly sensitive to the timing of peak usage. If actual
peak demand at the new facility differs significantly from the modeled
profile, projected demand charge savings may be over or underestimated.

 Summary and Discussion

The modeling conducted as part of this analysis indicates that:
• If the ITC is available, 28–60 kW of PV, with no battery, is cost-optimal, resulting in estimated NPV of
  $7,200 to $24,400 for PV prices ranging from $1,900 to $2,300, depending on building loads.
• If ITC is not available, cost-optimal PV sizes are on the residential scale [0–12 kW], which could
  increase the per-kW panel cost. Models were rerun with costs aligning with residential-scale solar
  ($3,618/kW) to determine optimal system sizes. However, at these higher prices without ITC, 0 kW of
  PV was chosen. Validate PV prices with a local installer.
• At the current assumed PV prices, this analysis predicts that it is more cost-effective to pursue smaller
  PV system sizes as opposed to maximizing roof space.
• For this building, batteries were not selected as part of a cost-optimal solution at the default prices.
  This indicates that batteries do not contribute to overall lower utility costs for this building, even when
  PV potentially over-generates, because of their high upfront costs. Batteries could provide a resilience
  benefit, but that was not included in this analysis.
