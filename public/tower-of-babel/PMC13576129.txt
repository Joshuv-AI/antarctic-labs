Laser hemorrhoidoplasty versus LigaSure hemorrhoidectomy for grade II-IV hemorrhoidal disease: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Ali Zamlout, Mohamed Ahmed Kasem, Omar Helmy, Omar Allam, Ahmed Refaat Abdellah, Khalid Allam, Yousef El-Ayman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMany surgical procedures have been developed to treat hemorrhoidal disease. Although conventional excisional techniques are effective, they are also associated with significant postoperative pain and longer recovery times. Consequently, newer techniques have been introduced to address these issues, including laser hemorrhoidoplasty (LHP) and LigaSure hemorrhoidectomy (LigH). This systematic review and meta-analysis aims to compare these two approaches in terms of perioperative outcomes and recurrence.MethodsA comprehensive search was conducted across PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, ClinicalTrials.gov, and Google Scholar, up to December 2025. We included all eligible studies involving adults with grade II–IV hemorrhoids. Primary outcomes included postoperative pain, time to return to routine activities, and recurrence. Secondary outcomes were operative time, length of hospital stay, and complications. The risk of bias was assessed using the RoB2 and ROBINS-I V2 tools. Meta-analyses were performed with a random-effects model. The certainty of evidence was evaluated using the GRADE approach.ResultsData from 461 patients (213 LHP, 248 LigH) across five studies were analyzed. Patients in the LHP group reported significantly lower pain scores on postoperative day 1 (MD: − 1.61 points; p = 0.025), earlier return to routine activities (MD: -5.75 days; p = 0.0002), shorter operative time (MD: -6.5 min; p = 0.002), and shorter hospital stay (MD: -0.3 days; p < 0.0001) compared to the LigH group. Conversely, LigH demonstrated a significantly lower risk of recurrence (RD: 0.12; 95% CI: [0.05, 0.19]). No significant differences were observed regarding postoperative bleeding, wound infection, urinary retention, or thrombosis.ConclusionLHP yields better perioperative outcomes, including less pain and faster recovery, making it a favorable option for patient comfort. However, LigH has lower recurrence rates. The certainty of evidence is very low. Further high-quality randomized trials are needed to confirm these results.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12893-026-03982-1.Keywords: Hemorrhoids, Laser Hemorrhoidoplasty, LigaSure, Systematic Review, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Hemorrhoids are pathologically enlarged anal vascular cushions located at the 3, 7, and 11 o’clock positions. They are believed to assist in continence, maintain resting anal pressure, and provide sensory feedback on the nature of anal canal contents [1, 2]. With an estimated prevalence of 4.4% in the United States, hemorrhoid disease (piles) remains one of the most common anorectal pathologies, with peak incidence between ages 45 and 65 [3, 4]. Hemorrhoids are classified as internal or external based on their relation to the dentate line. Clinically, hemorrhoids are graded according to the Goligher classification: Grade I involves only internal hemorrhoids with no prolapse; Grade II prolapses during straining but reduces spontaneously; Grade III requires manual reduction; and Grade IV indicates persistent, irreducible prolapse [4, 5]. Patients with Grades I and II often respond well to medical treatment, dietary modifications, and outpatient procedures (e.g., rubber band ligation); whereas patients with Grades III and IV, or those who do not respond to conservative treatment, usually require surgery [6, 7]. Common patient complaints include painless rectal bleeding, itching, discharge, painful defecation, and a mass protruding from the anal canal [8]. Risk factors for hemorrhoid disease include low-fiber diets, constipation, diarrhea, pregnancy, aging, and prolonged straining [2, 9].

Conventional hemorrhoidectomy procedures, such as Milligan-Morgan (open) and Ferguson (closed), have been associated with high postoperative pain and clinical complications [10]. However, both remained the predominant procedures for many years [9] because they provided favorable long-term outcomes, including low recurrence rates [11]. Recently, many minimally invasive techniques have been developed to address these drawbacks, such as laser, infrared photocoagulation, rubber band ligation, and sclerotherapy [10].

Laser hemorrhoidoplasty (LHP) is one of the most recent techniques, first introduced by Karahaliloğlu in 2007 [12]. A laser probe is inserted submucosally to induce coagulation and shrinkage of the hemorrhoidal cushions with controlled damage to surrounding tissue, resulting in less postoperative pain and fewer complications [13].

LigaSure™ is an advanced bipolar electrocautery sealing device capable of coagulating blood vessels up to 7 mm in diameter. It coagulates, shrinks, and excises enlarged hemorrhoidal cushions with minimal bleeding and thermal spread, leading to minimal tissue damage and less postoperative pain [14].

To date, there is no consensus on a superior surgical approach to treat all hemorrhoid patients, and the decision is tailored to balance effectiveness, cost, and complications. To our knowledge, this is the first systematic review and meta-analysis to directly compare the short- and long-term complications of laser hemorrhoidoplasty (LHP) and Ligasure hemorrhoidectomy (LigH) in the treatment of symptomatic hemorrhoids (Fig. 1).

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) statement [15, 16] and the Assessing the Methodological Quality of Systematic Reviews (AMSTAR-2) [17]. The study protocol was prospectively registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO ID: CRD420251266373) [18].

Literature search

A comprehensive search was performed on December 5, 2025, across the following electronic databases from the inception of each database, with no start date filter applied: MEDLINE (PubMed), Scopus, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, ClinicalTrials.gov, and Google Scholar. We used a combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text keywords in the following search strategy: (Piles OR hemorrhoids OR hemorrhoidectomy OR hemorrhoidoplasty OR “Hemorrhoids“[MeSH]) AND (Laser OR LHP OR “Lasers“[MeSH]) AND (LigaSure OR LigaSureTM). A complete version of the applied search strategy is available in the registered protocol [18]. No restrictions were applied regarding language, publication date, or study design. Additionally, the reference lists of all included studies and relevant review articles were manually screened (“backward snowballing”) to identify any eligible studies that might have been missed during the electronic search.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

All studies directly comparing Ligasure hemorrhoidectomy with Laser hemorrhoidoplasty in adults with grade II-IV hemorrhoidal disease were included. The primary outcomes of interest were: postoperative pain, time to return to routine activities, and recurrence. Secondary outcomes included: operative time, length of hospital stay, early postoperative complications (bleeding, urinary retention, edema, infection, and thrombosed hemorrhoid), late postoperative complications (anal stenosis, incontinence, persistent discharge), and patient satisfaction. The adult population was defined as patients aged ≥ 18 years. Articles were excluded if any of the following criteria were met: the study sample included pediatric and adult participants without age-based stratification; single-arm studies; animal studies; or study designs such as case reports, case series, commentaries, letters to the editor, and reviews.

Study screening and selection

Search results were imported into EndNote (version X9.3.3) for initial organization and duplicate removal, then uploaded to the Rayyan web platform for screening. All reviewers independently screened the titles and abstracts against the eligibility criteria. Following that, the full texts of relevant articles were retrieved and evaluated for inclusion by all reviewers. Disagreements were resolved through discussion and consensus. The selection process is documented in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 2).

PRISMA flow diagram of the study selection process

Data extraction

Data extraction was performed independently by all reviewers using a standardized, pre-defined electronic spreadsheet (Microsoft Excel 2021). The following information was extracted from each study: study characteristics (title, first author, year of publication, and geographic location), methodology (study design, inclusion/exclusion criteria, and follow-up duration), participant demographics (sample size, age, sex, and hemorrhoid grade), and clinical outcomes (operative parameters, pain scores, postoperative complications, and patient satisfaction). Medians and interquartile ranges were converted to means and standard deviations using Wan’s method [19].

Risk of bias assessment

Two experienced authors assessed the risk of bias in the randomized clinical trial (RCT) using the Cochrane Risk of Bias (RoB 2) tool [20]. For non-randomized studies, we employed the Risk Of Bias In Non-Randomized Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I) version 2 [21, 22]. Outcomes were categorized based on the risk-of-bias levels established by these tools. Any disagreements in quality assessments were resolved through discussion among the reviewers.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses and forest plots were performed using the “meta” package in R software [23, 24]. Due to anticipated clinical and methodological diversity (e.g., variations in surgical techniques, comorbidities, and follow-up durations), we adopted the random-effects model to calculate pooled effect sizes. For dichotomous outcomes, effect estimates were reported as Risk Ratios (RR) and Risk Differences (RD). For continuous outcomes, effect estimates were presented as Mean Differences (MD). All results were reported with 95% confidence intervals. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

We assessed statistical heterogeneity using the Cochrane’s Q test and the I² statistic. Heterogeneity was classified as low, moderate, substantial, or considerable according to the Cochrane Handbook classifications; a p-value < 0.10 was considered significant [25]. To provide a more clinically relevant assessment of heterogeneity, we also calculated and interpreted the 95% prediction interval (PI) for each pooled effect estimate [26].

Sensitivity analysis

To assess the possible impact of study design, we subgrouped the meta‑analyses by design (RCT vs. non‑RCT) for each outcome. We also performed a risk‑of‑bias (RoB)‑based sensitivity analysis by excluding studies judged to be at high risk of bias from the pooled analyses.

Given the variation in hemorrhoidal grades across the included studies (II–IV), we performed an additional leave‑one‑out (LOO) sensitivity analysis to assess whether any single study significantly influenced the pooled estimates.

Assessment of publication bias was not performed due to the limited number of studies included in the analysis (n < 10) [25].

Certainty of evidence

The certainty of evidence for each outcome was evaluated using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach, categorizing the confidence in the combined evidence into four levels: high, moderate, low, and very low [25, 27]. We summarized our results for each outcome and its certainty of evidence, using the GRADEpro web app to create the summary of findings table (Table 3).

Results

Literature search results

Our database search identified 711 publications. After removing 232 duplicates, we excluded an additional 420 studies during title and abstract screening. 59 articles advanced to full-text assessment, of which 54 were excluded for various reasons. Only five studies met the eligibility criteria for our review [28–32]. The detailed steps during this phase are presented in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 2).

Description of identified studies

Five studies were included in our systematic review and meta-analysis, involving a total of 461 patients (248 underwent LigH and 213 underwent LHP). Three of them were retrospective cohort studies [28–30], and two were clinical trials: one randomized [31] and the other non-randomized [32]. Three studies were conducted in Turkey, and two in Egypt. All studies included patients with grade II-III hemorrhoids, with one study including patients with grade III-IV [32]. A diode laser was used in the LHP group across all studies, with wavelengths of 1470 nm [28–30], 980 nm [31], or both [32]. The mean age of participants ranged from 34.3 (± 4.2) to 44.1 (± 2.7) years, with a male predominance (57.7–80.6% in the LigH group and 55.3–83.8% in the LHP group). The characteristics of the included studies are presented in Table 1, and the baseline characteristics of patients are shown in Table 2.

Risk of bias assessment

We summarized the risk-of-bias assessment for the five included studies in Fig. 3. Using ROBINS-I V2 for outcome-level assessment among the four non-randomized studies, only one study (Tümer 2023) [28] was judged to have a serious risk due to concerns about confounding bias, as the use of LHP was determined by the time of acquisition of the laser device (confounding by indication) and a possible lack of experience with the device, with no statistical adjustment for this bias. The remaining three studies had a moderate overall risk of bias, with issues related to confounding, outcome measurement, and bias in the selection of reported results. The main reasons for this judgment were their retrospective design and the absence of pre-published study protocols for comparison. All other domains were judged to be at low risk of bias.

Risk-of-bias summary using the ROB 2 tool for one randomized controlled trial and the ROBINS-I version 2 tool for four non-randomized studies

Based on the RoB 2 assessment, the single randomized controlled trial by Ibrahim et al. [31] had a high risk of bias, mainly due to selective outcome reporting and the lack of a previously registered, published protocol.

Detailed summaries of risk-of-bias assessments at the outcome level are provided in supplementary S2.

Results of the studies

Primary Outcomes

Postoperative pain

All five studies assessed pain using the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS). On postoperative day 1, LHP showed lower VAS scores (MD − 1.61 points; 95% CI: [–3.01, − 0.20]; p = 0.025; Fig. 4), with high heterogeneity (I²=98.4%; 95% PI: [–6.66, 3.44]). A design‑based sensitivity analysis was not possible because the only included RCT did not report pain. The effect became non-significant after excluding the high-risk-of-bias (RoB) study, Tümer et al. 2023 [28] (Figure S3.1). Furthermore, the leave‑one‑out (LOO) sensitivity showed that significance was also lost when any single study was omitted, except for Khadr (2024) [32] – the only study that included grade‑IV hemorrhoids (Figure S4.1).

Two forest plots compare postoperative pain on day 1 and day 7 between LHP and LigH groups using the visual analogue scale (VAS)

On postoperative day 7, the difference was not significant (MD − 1.23 points; 95% CI: [–2.47, 0.00]; p = 0.051; Fig. 4), with high heterogeneity (I²=97.1%; 95% PI: [–6.58, 4.12]). A RoB-based sensitivity analysis, by omitting Tümer (2023) [28], yielded similar results (MD -1.48; p = 0.192; Figure S3.2). The LOO analysis showed a significant difference favoring LHP over LigH (MD − 0.66; p = 0.0002; Figure S4.2) after removing Durgun (2023) [30].

Khadr et al. (2024) [32] also assessed pain at 2 and 4 weeks postoperatively, reporting a median VAS score of 0 in both groups at those time points.

Time to return to routine activities

The pooled analysis favors LHP (MD − 5.75 days, 95% CI [–8.79, − 2.70], p = 0.0002; Fig. 5), with high heterogeneity (I²=98.6%; 95% PI [–16.71, 5.21]). A design‑based sensitivity analysis was not feasible because the only RCT did not report this outcome [31]. After excluding the high RoB study Tümer (2023) [28], the effect remained significant (MD − 5.28 days; p = 0.003), with a wider prediction interval (95% PI [–20.48, 9.92]; Figure S3.3). Leave‑one‑out sensitivity analysis showed that the significance was robust to the omission of any single study (Figure S4.3).

Forest plot comparing the time to return to routine activities (days) between LHP and LigH groups

Recurrence

LHP had a significantly higher risk of recurrence compared to LigH (RR 4.39, 95% CI [1.95, 9.88], p = 0.0004, I²=0%; RD 0.12, 95% CI [0.05, 0.19], p = 0.001, I²=27.9%; Fig. 6). The prediction intervals were [1.17, 16.4] for RR and [–0.05, 0.28] for RD. A design‑based sensitivity analysis showed that the non‑RCT subgroup remained significant; the only included RCT showed a significant RD but a non‑significant RR (Fig. 6). After excluding the two high RoB studies (Tümer 2023 [28] and Ibrahim 2023 [31]), the pooled estimates remained stable, favoring LigH over LHP (RR 4.45, p = 0.009; RD 0.14, p = 0.001; Figure S3.4). The LOO sensitivity analysis showed that significance was robust to the omission of any single study (Figures S4.4, S4.5).

Forest plots comparing recurrence rates between LHP and LigH using risk ratio (RR) and risk difference (RD). Recurrence was assessed at 6 months (Ibrahim 2023, Taşkin 2023) and at 12 months (Durgun 2023, Tümer 2023)

Secondary Outcomes

Operative Time

LHP procedures were significantly shorter than LigH (MD − 6.47 min, 95% CI [–10.64, − 2.29], p = 0.002; Figure S1.1), with high heterogeneity (I²=98.4%; 95% PI [–20.37, 7.43]). A design‑based sensitivity analysis showed a consistent and significant MD across both RCT and non‑RCT subgroups. After excluding the two high RoB studies (Tümer 2023 [28] and Ibrahim 2023 [31]), the effect remained significant (MD − 8.54 min, 95% CI [–12.8, − 4.28], p < 0.0001; Figure S3.5). LOO sensitivity analysis showed that the effect was robust, except that omitting Taşkin (2023) shifted the pooled estimate toward borderline significance (MD − 4.19, p = 0.053; Figure S4.6).

Length of hospital stay

LHP had a significantly shorter hospital stay (MD − 0.32 days, 95% CI [–0.43, − 0.21], p < 0.0001; Figure S1.2), with moderate heterogeneity (I²=42%; 95% PI [–0.69, 0.04]). A design‑based sensitivity analysis showed a significant MD in both RCT and non‑RCT subgroups. After excluding the high RoB study (Ibrahim 2023 [31]), the effect remained significant (MD − 0.27 days, 95% CI [–0.43, − 0.10]; Figure S3.6). LOO sensitivity analysis showed that the effect was robust to the omission of any single study (Figure S4.7).

Bleeding

No significant difference was observed between LHP and LigH for either the risk ratio (RR 0.84, 95% CI [0.14, 5.07], p = 0.85, I²=75.8%) or the risk difference (RD − 0.03, 95% CI [–0.13, 0.08], p = 0.61, I²=88.1%). The prediction interval for RR was very wide [0.00, > 1000], and for RD it was [–0.34, 0.29] (Figure S1.3). A design‑based sensitivity analysis showed contrasting findings: the only RCT favored LigH (RR 7.0, p = 0.053; RD 0.4, p = 0.006), whereas the non‑RCT subgroup showed a trend toward less bleeding with LHP (RR 0.4, p = 0.0005; RD − 0.07, p = 0.234). After excluding the two high RoB studies (Tümer 2023 [28] and Ibrahim 2023 [31]), the RD remained non‑significant (RD − 0.11, 95% CI [–0.36, 0.14], p = 0.389; Figure S3.8). LOO sensitivity analysis showed that the RD remained insignificant when any study was omitted (Figure S4.8).

No statistically significant differences were found between LHP and LigH in terms of wound infection, urinary retention, and hemorrhoid thrombosis. For wound infection: RR 1.21 (95% CI [0.26, 5.65]), RD 0.00 (95% CI [–0.02, 0.03]); for urinary retention: RR 0.62 (95% CI [0.16, 2.35]), RD − 0.01 (95% CI [–0.06, 0.04]); and for hemorrhoid thrombosis: RR 1.20 (95% CI [0.36, 3.94]), RD 0.01 (95% CI [–0.02, 0.05]); See Supplementary Figures S1.4–S1.6. Design‑based, RoB‑based, and LOO sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of these findings (Figures S3.7, S3.8, S4.9-S4.11).

Edema

Based on data from two studies, event rates were 5% (6/119) in the LHP group versus 11.3% (17/150) in the LigH group (Taşkin 2023 [29] and Tümer 2023 [28]). Formal meta-analysis was not performed.

Anal Stenosis & Fecal Incontinence: Data from the included studies show no cases of anal stenosis or fecal incontinence in either study group, indicating no difference in these complications.

We did not incorporate satisfaction data into the meta-analysis because of the different reporting methods from only two studies. Khadr et al. [32] used a 7-point numeric scale and reported a median score of 6 for both groups: LHP (range: 2–6) and LigH (range: 3–7). Taşkin et al. [29] used a 5-point Likert scale, with all patients in the LHP group reporting either very satisfied (19/32, 59.4%) or satisfied (13/32, 40.6%). However, patients in the LigH group reported being very satisfied (6/34, 17.6%), satisfied (22/34, 64.7%), neutral (1/34, 2.9%), or unsatisfied (5/34, 14.7%).

Sero-mucous discharge was reported in only one study by Taskin et al. [29], indicating a low event rate in both groups (1/34, 2.9% in LHP group; and 2/32, 6.3% in LigH group).

Certainty of evidence

We evaluated the certainty of evidence using the GRADE approach, rating each outcome as high, moderate, low, or very low. We initially assigned low certainty because most of the included studies are retrospective cohorts. Ultimately, all outcomes were judged to have very low certainty.

Postoperative pain on days 1 and 7, operative time, and time to return to routine activities were downgraded due to risk-of-bias and inconsistency — indicated by significant heterogeneity (moderate to high) and/or sensitivity to leave-one-out analysis. Postoperative bleeding was also downgraded because of imprecision, as evidenced by the confidence interval crossing the no-effect line. Postoperative wound infection, urinary retention, and hemorrhoid thrombosis were downgraded for both risk-of-bias and imprecision. Recurrence and length of hospital stay were downgraded only for risk of bias.

Discussion

LHP, a minimally invasive technique, offers several advantages over conventional (excisional) surgical techniques, especially in reducing postoperative pain, decreasing intraoperative and postoperative bleeding, and shortening recovery time. It holds promise for high-risk patients, such as those receiving antiplatelet or anticoagulant therapy, in whom bleeding is a major concern. However, it still has limitations and concerns, including higher costs, limited efficacy in grade III cases with significant prolapse or grade IV cases, and higher reported recurrence rates, particularly in studies with longer follow-up periods. LHP has been recommended for grades II and III hemorrhoids. For more advanced cases or marked prolapse, combining LHP with mucopexy and other adjunctive procedures, such as hemorrhoidal artery ligation, has been recommended [33].

Our study demonstrates lower pain scores (VAS) on the first postoperative day (MD: -1.61 points, p = 0.025) for LHP compared to LigH, aligning with previous studies [34]. However, the results showed heterogeneity, and the difference at one week was borderline significant (p = 0.05, 95% CI: [0.00, 2.47]). The evidence was not robust because statistical significance was lost in both RoB-based and LOO sensitivity analyses. In addition, the subjective nature of VAS scoring may limit the precision of pain assessment.

Regarding recurrence rates, LigH showed a significant advantage over LHP (RR: 4.39; RD: 0.12). Because the baseline recurrence risk was low and the number of events was small across relatively small samples, the RR can be disproportionately large and may overstate the clinical difference. The RD, in contrast, shows that the absolute increase in recurrence with LHP is approximately 12% points. Therefore, the RD (0.12, p = 0.001) may provide a more stable and clinically interpretable estimate than RR. The 95% PI [-0.05, 0.28] indicates that in future settings, the predicted risk difference between LHP and LigH ranges from − 5% to 28%. Recurrence has long been a concern about the effectiveness of LHP. However, these rates have varied significantly since the initial LHP studies [29], with more recent studies reporting lower rates [28, 30]. This variation may be explained by advances in laser technology; modern studies utilize 1470 nm diode lasers with refined energy protocols and added mucopexy, which has been shown to improve outcomes. Other possible factors include differences in hemorrhoid grade and inconsistent follow-up durations across studies, which ranged from 6 to 18 months.

The time to return to normal activity, operative time, and length of hospital stay were also significantly shorter in LHP, which aligns with the previous literature and its minimally invasive approach [30, 31, 35]. However, significant heterogeneity was observed. Regarding the length of hospital stay, many institutions perform LHP as a day-case procedure, whereas others routinely admit patients overnight. The included studies did not explicitly report a pre-planned protocol for hospital admission. Therefore, this practice-related variation should be considered when interpreting the pooled analysis of this outcome.

The findings showed no significant differences in the remaining complications, including postoperative bleeding, wound infection, urinary retention, or thrombosis. However, not all studies reported every complication. Only two studies [28, 29] reported infection rates, and three studies provided data on postoperative bleeding and thrombosis. Additionally, data on edema, fecal incontinence, or anal stenosis were insufficient for quantitative analysis. The timeline of complication assessment also varied across studies. Most studies reported assessing outcomes at multiple follow-up visits during the initial months, but none clearly specified the exact timing window for each outcome assessment. Only Durgun et al. [30] specified the assessment of bleeding on the 1st and 7th postoperative days. Because the included studies did not predefine uniform assessment timelines, we defined a pragmatic time window for each complication based on the available information and follow-up visits to address this inconsistency and harmonize the analysis: urinary retention within 24–48 h; bleeding, wound infection, and hemorrhoid thrombosis within 30 days.

Tools for assessing quality of life and treatment effectiveness for hemorrhoids, such as the Hemorrhoidal Disease Symptom Score (HDSS) and the Short Health Scale for Hemorrhoidal Disease (SHS-HD), have been developed and validated [36]. However, only Khadr et al. [32] reported these measures in their results. Taskin et al. [29] reported quality-of-life outcomes using a Likert-type scale; therefore, these data were not suitable for quantitative analysis.

To our knowledge, this is the first systematic review and meta-analysis to directly compare these two techniques. The observed heterogeneity in the results likely reflects the small number of directly comparative trials, their small sample sizes, differences in hemorrhoid grade, and variation in study designs. Another source of inconsistency is the lack of standardized outcome definitions. For instance, Taşkin et al. [29] considered any visible internal hemorrhoid to be a recurrence, whereas other studies did not provide a definition. A further critical difference is the wide variation in follow‑up duration: Durgun et al. [30] and Tümer et al. [28] had mean follow‑up periods of 12 and 13 months, respectively; Ibrahim et al. [31] and Taşkin et al. [29] followed patients for 6 months; and Khadr et al. [32] followed patients for only 3 months.

Given the small sample sizes and low event rates in the included studies, the relative effect estimates (RRs) may be unstable and susceptible to overestimation. Therefore, absolute risk differences (RDs) provide a more clinically interpretable measure of effect in this context. In addition, several outcomes (e.g., postoperative pain, operative time) showed wide prediction intervals and high I2 values, indicating substantial heterogeneity. These findings should therefore be interpreted with caution, as the true effect in future studies may range from a clear benefit to little or no difference. Such heterogeneity reduces confidence in a single pooled estimate and supports a more individualized approach to clinical decision-making. Overall, the findings should be considered in light of these limitations and the low certainty of the evidence.

Additional notable gaps in the available studies are the lack of cost comparisons and quality-of-life (QoL) assessment data between the two techniques. These factors are crucial when discussing treatment options and guiding shared decision-making between patients and clinicians.

This meta-analysis emphasizes the need for future RCTs with standardized protocols, clearly defined target outcomes, longer follow-up periods, cost assessments, and quality-of-life measures.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis of five studies demonstrates that LHP yields superior short-term outcomes compared with LigH, including shorter operative time, less postoperative pain, shorter hospital stay, and faster return to daily activities. Conversely, LigH is associated with lower recurrence rates. Postoperative complications such as bleeding, wound infection, urinary retention, and hemorrhoid thrombosis were comparable between the two techniques. However, the certainty of evidence was very low, and the sensitivity analyses altered the statistical significance of certain variables, such as postoperative pain. Although LHP offers a quicker recovery, the potential for higher recurrence should be discussed with patients during counseling. Further well-designed multicenter clinical trials with larger sample sizes are required to derive more robust and definitive comparisons between these techniques.
