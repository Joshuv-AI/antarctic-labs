Executive Summary
The Dielectric Disk Accelerator (DDA) is a novel accelerating structure design for accelerating
electrons that use dielectric disks to improve efficiency. Compared to conventional metallic
accelerating structures, DDAs have larger shunt impedance, resulting in improved power
efficiency, and higher group velocity, reducing the time needed to build up the field in the
structure allowing for shorter input power pulse lengths. The shorter pulses decrease
breakdown probability making it possible to achieve a large accelerating gradient. The
structures studied during this project were a part of a development program primarily focused
on high-power, short pulse operation of DDAs for the first time. Two travelling-wave DDA
structures were designed, built, and tested: both a single-cell and a multicell 11.7 GHz
traveling wave structure powered by a 9 ns pulse-length wakefield power extraction-based RF
source at the Argonne Wakefield Accelerator. The multicell structure ran up to the available
RF power of 222 MW peak power for an accelerating gradient of about 80 MV/m with no signs
of breakdown.
As a complement to the important and successful short-pulse regime work with DDAs, an
11.4 GHz single-cell standing wave structure was also modeled, designed, built and tested,
powered by a klystron with variable pulse lengths from 10 ns to 200 ns at the Nextef2 test
stand at KEK This single-cell structure was tested with over 20 MW of input power before
suffering significant damage on a ceramic disk.
 Based on the results, the DDA travelling wave structure operating in short-pulse regime
demonstrated a high technological feasibility for practical application in future compact
accelerators. The grant awarded was used to support the graduate student researcher who
performed the research for her PhD in accelerator physics. [Ref 1]

Background of the project
The project, "Enabling High-Gradient, Efficient Wakefield Accelerators with High-Quality
Shaped Electron Bunches" is a multi-institution collaborative effort focused on the topic of
Beam-driven structure wakefield accelerators (SWFAs). These Advanced Accelerator
structures rely on high-charge “drive” bunches [O(10–100 nC)] passing through slow-wave
structures (SWSs) to excite electromagnetic wakefields. The produced wakefields can be
directly used to accelerate a trailing “main” bunch (CWA) or be extracted through a coupler
and waveguide into an optimized accelerating structure that accelerates the main bunch
(TBA) in a parallel beamline. The project aims to perform critical research relevant to the
Energy Frontier, as specified in the strategy report of the Advanced Accelerator Concept
workshop (AAC), and in the report on the community vision of Accelerator & Beam Physics
(ABP) thrusts of the general accelerator R&D (GARD) program. The project addresses four
research objectives theoretically and via numerical modeling complemented by laboratory
experiments at the Argonne Wakefield Accelerator (AWA) facility. These are:
I. Bright and high-charge beams generation and transport
II. High-precision phase-space control
III. Designing efficient high-gradient slow-wave structures
IV. Maximizing efficiency and conserving brightness during acceleration.

Ultimately, progress on the four objectives of the proposal culminate with the experimental
demonstrations of efficient high-gradient acceleration of bright beams in an SWFA (either in
the CWA or TBA configuration). NIU is focused on the CWA option, IIT on the TBA. This
project would result in significant advances over the research performed to date where either
high-gradient or high-efficiency was independently demonstrated.

Through this funding opportunity IIT has received support for the effort of one graduate

project, started working within the Chicagoland Accelerator Science Traineeship program

techniques with invaluable hands-on experience gained at Euclid and AWA.

Methodology
Description of the methods used
The research methods followed the following format: Simulations and material tests which
informed structure physics design. The chosen physics design was incorporated into
engineering design. Engineering design was followed by fabrication and hardware selection.
Once the fabrication was complete, RF low-power tests were performed and compared to
simulations which were refined to match any modifications that occurred during fabrication.
Next was installation into the high-power test chamber followed by high-power testing. Finally
data analysis and comparison to the simulations.

Data collection techniques: MATLAB script computer based data acquisition. The data collected
is primarily RF signals from oscilloscopes which are analyzed to determine the transmitted and
received RF power.The data set also included charge measurements from the integrating current
transformers and breakdown monitor signals (cameras, Faraday cup) illustrated in the block diagrams
below. The first one is for the data collection at AWA and the second is for the tests at KEK.

The high-power test results for the standing wave structure include measurements of the Q
Factor, the input power level, and the accelerating gradient. The performance was seriously
compromised by the damage that occurred in the structure early in the experiment. The peak
recorded accelerating gradient is 21.73 MV/m. This is about 7% of the expected gradient for
the maximum power level reached. Imperfections in fabrication and coupling of the structure
as well as damage sustained in the structure during testing all contributed to the poor
performance of this structure at KEK.

Conclusion

Summary of the main findings
The short-pulse travelling-wave dielectric disk accelerator DDA proved to handle high power
extremely well with virtually no breakdown or dark current problems. This was true in both the
simpler single-cell proof of concept proto-type as well as in the multi-cell version.

The single cell standing wave version designed for longer RF pulse operation (up to 200 ns)
did not fare so well and suffered damage during testing which made it unusable.

Recommendations
It is recommended that AWA continue and extend the DDA studies for the short-pulse regime
in particular. The effort to develop practical structures will continue at AWA when funding can
be secured. Under the current budget climate there is no additional money at AWA to directly
support student researchers so grants like this at the university level are important. Also, it is
necessary and important that a reasonable funding level is required that includes money for
structure fabrication, supporting materials and equipment and design as well as for graduate
research student support through the PhD. This important program shows much potential for
technological advancement of the RF accelerating structures.
