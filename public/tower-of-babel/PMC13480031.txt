Multi-omic analysis reveals Wharton’s jelly mesenchymal stem cells shift the pro-inflammatory phenotype of fibroblast subpopulation to induce skin rejuvenation
Authors: Penghong Chen, Yiqun Qiu, Jiyao Li, Dezhi Wang, Kailun Hu, Tianyang Lan, Xuefei Wang, Xiaosong Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSkin aging is an increasingly serious clinical problem with limited treatment options. Wharton’s jelly mesenchymal stem cells (WJMSCs) represent a promising regenerative strategy. However, their mechanisms of action remain to be fully elucidated through integrated multi-omics approaches.MethodsWe established D-galactose-induced aging models using human dermal fibroblasts (HDFs) and nude mice. The effects of WJMSC transplantation were evaluated through functional assays, histology, bulk RNA-seq, microarray, and single-cell transcriptomics. Key pathways and cellular subpopulations were further investigated via bioinformatic integration and experimental validation.ResultsIn vitro, WJMSC conditioned medium restored cellular functions—enhancing proliferation, migration, and reducing ROS and SA-β-gal activity. Systemic administration of WJMSCs reversed skin thinning, increased collagen deposition, rebalanced collagen I/III ratios, and reduced cell senescence. Additionally, WJMSCs promoted M2 macrophage polarization, angiogenesis, and Lgr6+ stem cells activation. The combined analysis of bulk RNA-seq and cytokine microarrays suggested that the IL-17 signaling pathway might be one of the key pathways mediated by WJMSCs. Single-cell transcriptomics revealed that WJMSCs activated the synthesis of newly synthesized RNA. Fibroblasts have been identified as the central communication hub in the skin microenvironment, mediating extensive crosstalk among various cell types. Further analysis highlighted that pro-inflammatory fibroblasts and secretory reticular fibroblasts are key drivers of skin aging. It was also found that WJMSCs downregulated the circadian rhythm-associated gene Dbp, which is specifically abundant in pro-inflammatory fibroblasts.ConclusionsHerein, we have provided comprehensive multi-omics evidence demonstrating that WJMSCs counteract skin aging through functional restoration, immunomodulation, and by altering the gene expression profiles of fibroblast subpopulations. These data have revealed potential therapeutic targets influenced by WJMSCs for remodeling fibroblast subpopulations in skin aging, and have provided new insights for precise cell-based interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13578-026-01594-z.Keywords: Wharton’s jelly mesenchymal stem cells, Skin aging, Fibroblast heterogeneity, Single-cell RNA sequencing, Dbp

Introduction

With the global population aging at an accelerating pace, anti-aging research has become a major focus of scientific and clinical interest. As the largest organ and the first-line barrier against external aggressors, the skin exhibits not only visible signs of aging such as wrinkles, loss of elasticity, and hyperpigmentation, but also undergoes functional decline and disruption of its microenvironment. This dysregulation leads to increased susceptibility to infections, impaired wound healing, and a higher risk of cutaneous malignancies [1, 2]. Intrinsic aging, driven primarily by genetic factors, represents an inevitable degenerative process. It is characterized by reduced cell proliferative capacity, degradation of the extracellular matrix (ECM), elevated oxidative stress, and accumulation of senescent cells [3]. Importantly, emerging evidence suggests that skin aging may serve as a reliable indicator of systemic aging, reflecting functional decline in other organs [4, 5]. This connection underscores the significance of studying skin aging to better understand overall organismal aging.

Fibroblasts play a central role in maintaining the structural integrity of connective tissues and are involved in organ morphogenesis, wound repair, and various pathological conditions. However, traditional studies have often treated dermal fibroblasts as a homogeneous population, thereby overlooking their inherent heterogeneity. This oversight has limited our understanding of their diverse functions. Recent advances in single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) have revealed remarkable heterogeneity among human dermal fibroblasts, which can be categorized into functionally distinct subpopulations [6]. These subtypes differ significantly in cytokine secretion, metabolic activity, and modulation of the microenvironment. Michael T. Longaker and colleagues identified ten major fibroblast subtypes in human skin; each exhibits distinct functional profiles related to ECM synthesis, immune regulation, and contractility [7]. These findings suggest their differential roles in shaping the skin microenvironment. In wound healing, FSP1-positive fibroblast subsets demonstrate pro-angiogenic properties by secreting factors such as VEGF-A, whereas α-SMA-positive myofibroblasts predominantly secrete pro-fibrotic mediators that drive collagen deposition and scar formation [8]. In the context of skin fibrosis, targeted inhibition of a pro-fibrotic fibroblast subpopulation expressing Cyp26b1 has been shown to attenuate fibrosis and promote regeneration [9]. These findings highlight the differential responses of fibroblast subpopulations to therapeutic interventions. Such heterogeneity likely influences skin aging progression, tissue repair quality, and the development of age-related pathologies.

Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) hold great promise in the field of regenerative medicine due to their multipotency, immunomodulatory properties, and paracrine activities, which collectively contribute to tissue repair and functional recovery [10]. Specifically, Wharton’s jelly-derived MSCs (WJMSCs) have attracted attention for their accessibility, robust proliferative capacity, and low immunogenicity, underscoring their potential for clinical applications [11, 12]. Although previous studies have reported that WJMSC transplantation can ameliorate skin aging [13], a systematic understanding of fibroblast heterogeneity and its relevance to treatment response remains lacking. Elucidating the dynamic changes of fibroblast subpopulations during aging and their crosstalk with MSCs is essential for developing precision anti-aging strategies that target specific fibroblast subtypes.

Here, WJMSCs are utilized to counteract D-galactose-induced senescence in both human dermal fibroblasts (HDFs) and a nude mouse skin aging model. Single-cell transcriptomics has revealed that fibroblasts serve as key cellular hubs, playing central roles in skin tissue function and communication. WJMSCs enhance the activity of nascent RNA synthesis in fibroblasts and reshape the intercellular communication network within the skin. Importantly, the study identified a type of pro-inflammatory fibroblast (PF) and secretory reticular fibroblasts (SRF) subpopulation, which exhibited significant aging characteristics and activation of the IL-17 signaling pathway, and was determined to be an important driver of skin aging. Among them, the gene Dbp was identified as a core target, which is highly expressed in this pro-inflammatory fibroblast subpopulation. This study not only interprets the dynamic cellular and molecular landscape of skin regeneration at multiple omics layers, but also provides a theoretical basis and potential intervention targets for developing precise anti-aging strategies targeting the heterogeneity of fibroblasts.

Materials and methods

WJMSCs culture

Passage 3 WJMSCs were purchased from Qilu Cell Therapy Technology Co., Ltd., China and were cultured in Mesenchymal Stem Cell Basal Medium (without FBS) (MSCYF01-500, YINFENG BIOLOGICAL, China) supplemented with Mesenchymal Stem Cell Supplement (MSCYF02-20, YINFENG BIOLOGICAL, China) and penicillin/streptomycin (P-S) (100 U/mL) (HyClone) under standard cell culture conditions (37℃, 5% CO2). The cells were passaged once they reached 80% confluence and WJMSCs at passages 5–7 were used in subsequent experiments. The conditioned medium of WJMSCs (CM) was concentrated 20 fold and used for in vitro experiments.

Construction of a senescent Human Dermal Fibroblasts (HDFs)

HDFs (CP-H103, Procell, Wuhan, China) were cultivated in cell-specific complete medium (CM-H103, Procell, Wuhan, China) at 37 °C under 5% CO2 until reaching 90% confluence [14]. To evaluate the therapeutic effects of CM, experiments were performed using three independent biological replicates (n = 3). The cells were assigned to three experimental conditions: a Normal group, cultured in complete medium free of D-galactose; a Model group, induced to senescence by incubation for 48 h in complete medium supplemented with 20 g/L D-galactose; and a CM treatment group, which underwent the same D-galactose induction followed by incubation with a mixture of CM and complete medium combined at a 1:10 ratio.

Scratch closure test

The HDFs were seeded at 5 × 104 cells/well in 12-well plates. The cells were incubated with D-gal for 2 days and then artificially scratched to generate a wound line using a sterilized 200-μL pipette tip, followed by treatment of CM. Wound size were imaged at 0 and 24 h under microscope, and measured by Image J 1.47 software. The cell migration ability was expressed as the migrated area ratio, calculated as the percentage of the closed area relative to the initial wound area at 0 h. Each experiment was conducted in triplicate.

Cell proliferation assay

A CCK8 assay (Beyotime) kit was used to quantify the proliferation of HDF. Briefly, 5 × 103 HDF cells were cultured in 96-well plate in 100 μL of cell culture medium at 37 ℃ for 24, 48 and 72 h. The normal group and the model group were added with complete medium (10% FBS high glucose DMEM), while the treatment group was treated with the intervention medium mixed with CM and complete medium in a ratio of 1:10. Then, 10 μL CCK-8 solution was added to each well and incubated at 37 ℃ for another 2 h. Optical density values at 450 nm were read using a microplate analyzer.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) detection assay

ROS Fluorometric Assay Kit (E-BC-K138-F, Elabscience Biotechnology Co.,Ltd) was used to measured ROS generation levels in HDFs. The cell culture medium was removed, and 500 μL of diluted DCFH-DA was added to the cells in one well of the 12-well plate and incubated at 37 ℃ for 30 min. The cells were washed three times with a serum-free cell culture medium to completely remove DCFH-DA, which did not enter the cells. The fluorescence microscope was used to detect cellular ROS in the different groups. For quantification, the mean fluorescence intensity of the cells was measured using Image J 1.47 software. The results were calculated and expressed as relative fluorescence intensity, normalized to the Normal group.

Senescence-associated-beta-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) staining

HDFs were plated in 12-well plates (5 × 104/ well), modeled with D-gal and followed by CM treatment for 48 h. The HDFs were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and then stained for SA-β-gal (Servicebio, G1073-100 T). Positive cells characterized by blue cytoplasmic staining were imaged using light microscopy. To quantify cellular senescence, the percentage of SA-β-gal-positive cells was analyzed using Image J 1.47 software by counting the blue-stained cells relative to the total cell number in multiple random fields.

Animal experimental procedures

A total of 45 healthy SPF grade BALB/c nude male mice (6-week-old), weighing 18–22 g, were provided by Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). No previous experimental procedures were performed on the animals prior to this study. The mice were individually reared in a light- and temperature-controlled environment for 1 week where they had free access to sterile food and water. The bedding in the rearing cages was regularly changed once every 3 days to keep it dry. To minimise potential confounders, animals were housed under identical conditions, treatments were administered at the same anatomical location and at consistent time points, and experimental procedures and outcome assessments were conducted in the same order across groups. Cage locations were rotated regularly during the experiment to reduce potential environmental bias. The mice were randomly divided into three groups of 15 per group: normal group (Normal), skin aging model group (Model) and WJMSCs group (WJMSCs), randomisation was performed using a computer-generated random number sequence. The total sample size of 15 animals per group was determined based on the cumulative requirements for downstream analyses, ensuring sufficient material was available for distinct tissue processing methods while minimizing inter-batch variation. No exclusion criteria were established a priori. All 45 animals were included in the study, and no data points were excluded from the final analysis. D-galactose dissolved in 0.9% normal saline (0.2 mL) was injected subcutaneously at a dose of 1500 mg/kg/day for 6 weeks in the Model and WJMSCs groups. An equivalent volume of 0.9% normal saline was injected instead of D-galactose into Normal group in the same manner for comparison. After the model was constructed, 2 × 106 WJMSCs resuspended in 200 μL PBS were subcutaneously injected into the dorsal skin of mice. Meanwhile, the same volume of 0.9% normal saline was injected into the Normal group and Model group. The placement of the injection were consistent in all animals, and treatments were applied once per week for 4 weeks. The subcutaneous injection of cells was a minimally invasive procedure performed swiftly by trained personnel. In accordance with established animal welfare guidelines, chemical anesthesia was not required for this technique. At the predetermined experimental endpoint, mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation. This method was performed exclusively by well-trained and experienced researchers to ensure immediate loss of consciousness and a humane death. Group allocation was concealed during the outcome assessment and data analysis. Investigators responsible for histological evaluation, image quantification, and data analysis were blinded to group assignment. The personnel involved in animal allocation and treatment administration were aware of the group allocation due to the nature of the experimental procedures but were not involved in outcome assessment or statistical analysis. The work has been reported in line with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0.

Transmission Electron Microscope (TEM) analysis

The skin was cut into small tissue pieces of 1mm3 in the TEM fixation solution (Servicebio, G1102), and then wash the tissues using 0.1 M PB (pH 7.4) for 3 times, 15 min each. 1% OsO4 in 0.1 M PB (pH 7.4) was used to fix tissue at room temperature for 2 h away from light. Following dehydration, tissues were infiltrated and embedded overnight, polymerized, and sectioned at 1.5 μm. Thin sections were stained with toluidine blue for light microscope. Ultra-thin sections (about 60–80 nm) were obtained using microtome (Leica UC7, Germany), collected on 150 meshes cuprum grids, stained with 2.6% lead citrate and then examined under TEM (HITACHI, HT7800, Japan).

Histopathological and immunofluorescence analysis

After sacrificing the mice, the skin tissue were collected, and then fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and paraffin embedded. Next, all paraffin embedded skin tissue was sectioned at 4 μm, followed by HE (hematoxylin and eosin) staining, Masson staining and sirius staining. The sections were examined microscopically under a microscope and the resulting images were analyzed. Epidermal and dermal thicknesses were determined from H&E-stained sections by averaging measurements from multiple vertical cross-sections per field. Collagen deposition was evaluated in Masson-stained sections by calculating the collagen volume fraction.For immunohistochemical (IHC) staining, the sections were separately incubated with antibodies against CD163 (Proteintech Group, Inc, 1:200) and IL-1β (Proteintech Group, Inc, 1:200). Subsequently, the fluorescent signals of these markers were visualized and captured using a fluorescent inverted microscope. For immunohistochemistry staining, the sections were incubated with primary antibodies against CD86 (Abcam, 1:100), p16 (Proteintech Group, Inc, 1:600), LGR6 (Proteintech Group, Inc, 1:100) overnight at 4℃, followed by incubation with the corresponding secondary antibodies and DAB detection.To quantify protein expression, images were analyzed using ImageJ 1.47 software. The results were expressed as the relative expression percentage.

SA-β-gal staining on skin tissue

For SA-β-gal staining, unfixed frozen skin section was stained with the β-galactosidase fixative solution for 20 min after drying naturally, then the fixing solution was poured off and the slice was washed 3 times for 5 min each with PBS. Excess moisture was removed from the slides, and the tissue was incubated with the SA-β-gal staining working solution at 37 °C for 16 h. Subsequently, the staining solution was discarded, and sections were washed twice with PBS followed by two rinses with distilled water. The sections were then counterstained with Nuclear Fast Red, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, and immediately photographed. For quantification, the SA-β-gal positive area percentage was analyzed using ImageJ software, calculated as the ratio of the blue-stained area to the total tissue area in each field.

Bulk RNA-seq

Total RNA was isolated using the Trizol Reagent (Invitrogen Life Technologies), with the concentration, quality and integrity assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific). Three micrograms of RNA were processed for library preparation. mRNA was purified from total RNA using poly-T oligo-attached magnetic beads. Fragmentation was carried out using divalent cations under elevated temperature in an Illumina proprietary fragmentation buffer. First-strand cDNA was synthesized with random primers and Super Script II, followed by second-strand synthesis using DNA Polymerase I/RNase H. Blunt ends were generated via exonuclease/polymerase treatment. After adenylation of the 3′ ends of the DNA fragments, Illumina PE adapter oligonucleotides were ligated to prepare for hybridization. After adenylation, Illumina PE adapters were ligated. Libraries were size-selected (400–500 bp) using AMPure XP system (Beckman Coulter, Beverly, CA, USA), then enriched through 15-cycle PCR with Illumina primers. Purified products were quantified using the Agilent high sensitivity DNA assay on a Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent). The sequencing library was then sequenced on NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina) Shanghai Personal Biotechnology Cp. Ltd. Using FPKM to standardize the expression of each gene, then difference expression of genes (DEGs) was analyzed by DESeq (v1.38.3) with screened conditions as follows: expression difference multiple |log2FoldChange|> 1, significant P-value < 0.05. Furthermore, R script (version 4.2.3) were used to generate heatmaps and volcano plots in the. Finally, functional enrichment analysis was performed using Metascape (metascape.org).

Semiquantitative cytokine microarray

The skin tissues were lysed with cell lysis buffer, and the supernatant was used for antibody assay using glass slide-based antibody cytokine microarray (RayBiotech, GSM-CAA-4000, Guangzhou, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The fluorescein-labeled array was visualized using an InnoScan 300 Microarray Scanner. Data were analyzed with RayBiotech Q-Analyzer software.

Single-cell RNA sequencing

6 h before sample collection, RNA metabolic marker reagents were administered via intraperitoneal injection into randomly selected nude mice from the three groups. After euthanasia, skin samples from the backs of the nude mice were collected immediately and stored on ice in tissue preservation solution for up to 30 min. The specimens were washed three times with Hanks balanced salt solution, chopped into small pieces, and then digested for 15 min at 37 ℃ using a tissue dissociation system with 3 mL of tissue dissociation solution. The cell suspension was collected and filtered through a 40-µm sterile filter. Red blood cell lysis buffer was added, and the mixture was incubated at room temperature for 5–8 min to lyse red blood cells. The mixture was then centrifuged at 300 × g at 4 ℃ for 5 min; the supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was gently resuspended in PBS. Finally, the samples were stained with trypan blue and cell viability was assessed under a microscope. Single-cell suspensions with a concentration of 2 × 105 cells/mL in PBS were loaded onto microfluidic devices, and scRNA-seq libraries were constructed according to the Singleron GEXSCOPE® protocol with GEXSCOPE® Single-Cell RNA Library Kit (Singleron Biotechnologies, Jiangsu, China). The process included cell lysis, mRNA trapping, labelling of cells (barcodes) and mRNA (UMIs), reverse transcription of mRNA into cDNA, amplification, and fragmentation of cDNA. The individual library of each sample was diluted to 4 nmol/L and pooled for sequencing on the platform of the Illumina HiSeq X Ten platform.

Data processing and analysis

The raw data were subjected to base identification and UMI correction by CeleScope software (v1.9.0) to generate the gene-cell expression matrix. For each sample dataset, we filtered expression matrix by the following criteria: 1) cells with gene count less than 200 or with top 2% gene count were excluded; 2) cells with top 2% UMI count were excluded; 3) cells with mitochondrial content 15% were excluded; 4) genes expressed in less than 5 cells were excluded. Subsequently, data standardization, hypervariable gene screening and principal component analysis were carried out through the Seurat package (v3.1.2). Cell clusters were visualized through Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) dimensionality reduction. To identify DEGs, we used the Seurat FindMarkers() function based on Wilcoxon rank sum test with default parameters, and selected the genes expressed in more than 10% of the cells in both of the compared groups of cells and with an average log(Fold Change) value greater than 0.25 as DEGs. p value was calculated by Bonferroni Correction and the value 0.05 was used as the criterion to evaluate the statistical significance. The clusterProfiler package (v3.16.1) was utilized to conduct gene ontology/Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathway (GO/KEGG) enrichment analysis. Pathways with p value less than 0.05 were considered as significantly enriched. Meanwhile, the CellPhoneDB tool (v2.1.0) was used to systematically analyze the cell interaction network. Permutation number for calculating the null distribution of average ligand-receptor pair expression in randomized cell identities was set to 1000. Individual ligand or receptor expression was thresholded by a cutoff based on the average log gene expression distribution for all genes across each cell type. Predicted interaction pairs with p value < 0.05 and of average log expression > 0.1 were considered as significant and visualized by heatmap_plot and dot_plot in CellphoneDB.

Statistical analysis

Data were expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical analysis and plotting of data were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 software (GraphPad Software Inc., Boston, MA, CA). The Shapiro–Wilk test (P > 0.05) and Q-Q plot were used to verify that the data from each group conformed to a normal distribution. For comparisons among multiple groups, statistical significance was determined using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. If assumptions were not met, the non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn’s post hoc test was utilized. A p-value < 0.05 indicates that the difference is statistically significant. In the chart, statistical significance is indicated as follows: * for P < 0.05, ** for P < 0.01, and *** for P < 0.001.

Results

WJMSCs restore the HDFs function lost with aging and mitigate cellular senescence

As the core cell type of skin tissue, the functional changes of fibroblasts directly participate in regulating the pathological process of skin aging. First, we evaluated the effect of CM on the proliferation activity of aging HDFs induced by D-galactose. As shown in Fig. 1B, D-galactose induction significantly reduced HDF viability in the Model group versus the Normal group at 24, 48, and 72 h (P < 0.05, P < 0.01, and P < 0.0001, respectively; n = 6), confirming effective proliferation inhibition. Conversely, WJMSC-CM treatment restored proliferation capacity in Model group cells, with the most pronounced effect observed at 72 h (P < 0.001 vs. Model group). To assess cell migration ability, scratch assay was used. Baseline scratch width was basically consistent among the Normal, Model, and CM groups. Comparatively, the migration in the CM group was higher compared to the Model group at 24 h (P < 0.01). The scratch healing area in the CM group was not significantly different from that of the Normal group (P > 0.05) (Fig. 1A, C).

Oxidative stress caused by abnormal accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) drives cellular aging by damaging the macromolecules—such as proteins, lipids, and DNA—and compromising the integrity of subcellular organelles. This process is one of the core mechanisms mediating skin aging [15]. Further results showed that the ROS level in the Model group was significantly increased compared with the Normal group (P < 0.0001), indicating elevated accumulation of reactive oxygen species in senescent cells. The ROS level in the CM group was significantly lower than that in the Model group (P < 0.01) but still did not reach the ROS level observed in the Normal group (P < 0.05), indicating that CM reduced ROS accumulation in senescent cells to a certain extent (Fig. 1D, E).

To evaluate the effect of CM on cellular senescence, we used the SA-β-GAL staining method to detect the positive rate of aging-related β-galactosidase in cells of each experimental group. The results showed a significant difference in the percentage of SA-β-GAL positive cells between the senescence induction group and the CM treatment group. Compared with the normal group, the Model group exhibited a significantly higher percentage of SA-β-GAL positive cells (P < 0.0001), confirming the successful establishment of the cellular senescence model. After CM treatment, the percentage of SA-β-GAL positive cells decreased significantly compared with the Model group (P < 0.01), indicating that CM treatment could partially alleviate the cellular senescence phenotype (Fig. 1F, G). Overall, WJMSCs can rejuvenate the functions of HDFs that deteriorate with age, and effectively alleviate cellular senescence (Fig. 1H).

Ameliorative effects of WJMSCs against D-galactose-induced skin aging in nude mice

Next, we explored the practical value of WJMSCs on reversing skin aging in a nude mouse. We first established a skin aging model by injecting D-galactose into the backs of mice. Then, we treated them with WJMSCs once weekly for four consecutive weeks and subsequent analysis of skin tissue morphology and composition (Fig. 2A).

The ultrastructures of skin in each group were compared by using TEM. The Normal group displayed regularly arranged collagen fibers, and the extended cytoplasm was close to the abundant collagen fibers (Fig. 2B, red five-pointed star). Model group exhibits characteristic features, such as disturbance and break of the alignment of collagen fibers (Fig. 2B, yellow box), which is considered as an important aspect of the skin aging process [16]. In addition, cells in aging skin showing apparent abnormal nucleus (Fig. 2B, red arrowhead). Nevertheless, these pathological features improved after WJMSCs treatment. The images result showed that the collagen fibers were arranged in a more orderly manner in WJMSCs group than in the Model group and was similar to that in the Normal group.

Histological analysis was used to evaluate the impact of WJMSC administration on the dorsal skin structure and collagen deposition (Fig. 2C, D). The Normal group showed intact epidermis and tightly connected dermis, with sebaceous glands closely arranged and hair follicle cells densely packed. The tissue exhibited intact structure and normal morphology, along with dense and abundant collagen fibers. The Model group exhibited signs of degeneration, including thickened epidermis, thinner dermis, indistinct hair follicle cell structure, reduced number of hair follicle cells, irregular shape, atrophic cell nuclei, and decreased collagen fiber content. In contrast, the tissue structure of mice injected with WJMSCs showed significant improvement. These mice had thinner epidermis (Fig. 2E), thicker dermis (Fig. 2F), and abundant collagen fibers arranged in bundles in the dermal layer of the skin tissue, along with an increased proportion of collagen distribution (Fig. 2G).

Compared to Masson staining, Sirius red staining allows for intuitive discrimination between type I collagen fibers (COL-I) and type III collagen fibers (COL-III). Under Sirius red staining, COL-I and COL-III appear red and yellow-green, respectively. Specific Sirius red staining combined with polarized light microscopy revealed that skin tissues in the Normal group exhibited significant yellow-green birefringence under orthogonal polarized light, indicating that type I and type III collagen fibers maintained their physiological ratio. In the Model group, the birefringence color distinctly shifted to orange-yellow, reflecting abnormal deposition of type I collagen fibers. Their thick, bundle-like arrangement contributed to dermal stiffness, which is a key symptom of skin photoaging and endogenous aging [17]. Following the WJMSCs intervention, the birefringence color shifted to an intermediate range between yellow-green and orange-yellow, suggesting a decrease in the proportion of type I collagen fibers and a tendency toward balanced synthesis of type III collagen fibers. This indicates that stem cell therapy reverses ECM dysregulation by modulating the metabolic balance between type I and type III collagen fibers (Fig. 2J).

WJMSCs attenuate SA-β-gal activity induced by senescence

The detection of SA-β-gal activity remains a key indicator for identifying senescent cells in both in vitro experiments and histological analyses [18]. The results of SA-β-gal staining in skin tissues revealed that the area positive for SA-β-gal staining in the Normal group accounted for 9.93% ± 0.19% of the total tissue area. In contrast, the Model group exhibited a significant increase in the blue-stained area compared to the Normal group (P < 0.0001), suggesting that SA-β-gal activity remains low under physiological conditions, but senescence induction markedly increases SA-β-gal expression. Following treatment with WJMSCs, the percentage of blue-stained area was significantly reduced compared to the Model group (P < 0.001). Importantly, no statistically significant difference was observed between the WJMSCs-treated group and the Normal group (P = 0.9655), suggesting that the treatment effectively suppresses overall senescence markers in the skin tissue (Fig. 2H, I). These findings demonstrate that WJMSCs intervention can notably attenuate SA-β-gal activity induced by senescence, further supporting its potential as a therapeutic strategy for mitigating skin aging.

WJMSCs restore skin homeostasis by attenuating inflammation, promoting angiogenesis, and activating stem cells

Skin aging is characterized by an imbalance in macrophage phenotypes and elevated inflammatory factors, which promote ECM degradation, tissue remodeling, and increased cellular senescence and death [19]. To investigate whether WJMSCs modulate this process, we performed immunohistochemical and immunofluorescence analyses on skin tissues. The expression levels of CD163 (an M2 macrophage marker), CD86 (an M1 macrophage marker), and IL-1β were assessed to reflect inflammatory status. Compared to the Model group, the WJMSCs group showed a significant reduction in CD86 expression (P < 0.0001) and an increase in CD163 expression (P < 0.01), indicating a shift from pro-inflammatory M1 to anti-inflammatory M2 macrophage polarization following WJMSCs treatment. Additionally, IL-1β levels were significantly lower in the WJMSCs group than in the Model group (P < 0.0001), suggesting attenuated inflammation in skin tissues (Fig. 3A–E).

We also observed a higher number of p16⁺ senescent cells in the Model group, which were significantly reduced after WJMSCs treatment (P < 0.01) (Fig. 3D, F). This implies a potential correlation between macrophage polarization imbalance and cellular senescence. Additionally, aging is associated with reduced dermal vascular density, leading to impaired nutrient supply and accelerated fibroblast dysfunction [20]. The results showed that expression of CD31, an endothelial cell marker, was significantly lower in the Model group compared to the Normal group (P < 0.0001). In contrast, the WJMSCs group exhibited upregulation of CD31 compared to the Model group (P < 0.0001) (Fig. 3D, G).

Another mechanism of skin aging involves the depletion of endogenous stem cells [21]. Among them, Lgr6⁺ epidermal stem cells have attracted attention in regenerative dermatology due to their unique capacity for epidermal and sebaceous gland regeneration [22]. We found that Lgr6 expression was significantly lower in the Model group than in the Normal group (P < 0.0001). WJMSCs treatment activated Lgr6 expression, resulting in a significant increase compared to the Model group (P < 0.0001) (Fig. 3D, H).

WJMSCs rejuvenate the aging-associated transcriptome and proteomic profile and modulate key signaling pathways

Subsequently, the integrated analysis of transcriptomics and cytokine microarrays were used to explore the mechanisms underlying the anti-aging effects of WJMSCs on skin (Fig. 4A). At the transcriptomic level, a total of 12 skin samples (4 in Normal group, 4 in Model group and 4 in WJMSCs group) were collected for bulk RNA-seq. Principal component analysis (PCA) of transcriptional profiling reveals distinct signature between groups, and showed that Normal group and WJMSC group were similar (Fig. 4B). We found that aging and cellular senescence-related genes significantly altered by WJMSCs treatment, such as Ccl8, Il16, Cxcl15 and Il2, while the expression of proliferation-related genes were increased, such as Mki67, Pcna and Ccne1 (Fig. 4C), which herald important aging improvements. The volcano map further exhibited the distribution of gene expression difference (Supplementary Table 1), WJMSCs treatment caused 61 genes to be up-regulated and 37 genes to be down-regulated (Fig. 4E). GESA analysis results proposed that the transcriptional alterations caused by WJMSCs treatment were correlate with immune-related and inflammation-related biological process (Fig. 4D). In addition, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis highlighted that estrogen signaling pathway, glucagon signaling pathway, and IL-17 signaling pathway were activated (Fig. 4F).

Furthermore, in order to validate transcriptomic findings, Raybiotech mouse semi-quantitative protein chip was further used to detect 200 related proteins. PCA clearly separated the samples from the MSCs-treated group and the Model group (Supplementary Fig. 1A). GO analysis also showed that these differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) were enriched in “regulation of leukocyte proliferation”, “regulation of leukocyte migration” and “cell chemotaxis” (Supplementary Fig. 1B–D). Consistent with observations from RNA-seq data, KEGG analysis showed that pathways enriched from DEPs were clustered in multiple inflammation-related and aging-related pathway, such as “NF-kappa B signaling pathway”, “p53 signaling pathway”, and “IL-17 signaling pathway” (Fig. 4G). Subsequently, through Venn analysis, the identical signaling pathway, IL-17 signaling pathway, were found at the transcriptomic and proteomic levels (Fig. 4H). Semi-quantitative cytokine array further revealed that the cytokine expression profile after MSCs treatment was close to that of Normal group, but different from that of Model group, and confirmed the widely reduction of various cytokines (IL-17E, IL-17B R, IL-4, CXCL-16, and pro-MMP-9), as compared with Model group (Fig. 4I). The above results implying that IL-17 signaling pathway might be a critical target for anti-aging therapy [23]. Taken together, all these transcriptome and proteomic features of cell senescence got reversed, leading to healthy tissue homeostasis after WJMSCs treatment.

Single-cell dynamic transcriptomics unveils WJMSCs-induced remodeling of the tissue microenvironment

To investigate the mechanisms underlying D-galactose-induced skin aging and the anti-aging effects of WJMSCs, we performed single-cell dynamic transcriptomic sequencing on skin samples from both the WJMSCs-treated and Model groups (Fig. 5A). This technology integrates metabolic RNA labeling, biochemical nucleotide conversion, and scRNA-seq to distinguish between “new” and “old” RNAs within individual cells, enabling assessment of nascent RNA levels, RNA synthesis rates, degradation kinetics, dynamic transcriptome expression, and infers transcriptional regulatory mechanisms. After quality control, a total of 22,687 cells were retained and classified into 13 distinct cell types (Fig. 5B): epithelial cells; sebaceous gland cells; Schwann cells; melanocytes; fibroblasts; endothelial cells; mural cells; hair follicle dermal papilla cells; T/NK cells; neutrophils; mast cells; Langerhans cells; and monocytes. Characteristic marker genes for each cell type are visualized via heatmaps and feature plots (Figs. 5D and E). Epithelial cells, fibroblasts, and monocytes were the most abundant populations (Fig. 5C), suggesting their potential central role in counteracting skin aging. Differential expression analysis between the WJMSCs-treated and Model groups was conducted using thresholds of |logFC|≥ 0.25 and an P-value ≤ 0.05. Figure 5F and G illustrate the number and expression patterns of DEGs across cell types after WJMSCs treatment (Supplementary Table 2). Collectively, these results characterize the cellular alterations in the skin microenvironment following aging and subsequent WJMSCs therapy.

We utilized CellphoneDB to infer cell–cell communication networks within the skin tissue. Fibroblasts exhibited the most extensive interactions, engaging strongly with mural cells, dermal papilla cells, epithelial cells, macrophages, endothelial cells, and others (Fig. 6D–F), underscoring their pivotal role in skin aging and rejuvenation. Further analysis of ligand–receptor pairs highlighted specific interactions, including Cxcl12_Cxcr4, App_Trem2, C3_C3ar1, and Ccl7_Ccr5, particularly between fibroblasts and monocytes (Fig. 6G). We also confirmed this conclusion from the perspective of gene expression levels. Figure 6H shows that C3 and Ccl7 are highly expressed in fibroblasts, while C3ar1 and Ccr5 are highly expressed in monocytes. Previous reports indicate that Ccl7 promotes TGF-β downstream signaling activation. This leads to increased type I collagen production and accelerated skin aging [24]. These results position fibroblasts as central hubs connecting epidermal, vascular, and immune cells. Fibroblast-immune crosstalk plays a crucial role in regulating dermal and epidermal immune balance, thereby maintaining skin homeostasis.

Pro-inflammatory and secretory-reticular fibroblasts are key drivers of skin aging

These findings collectively underscore the pivotal role of fibroblasts in maintaining skin microenvironment homeostasis, prompting a detailed investigation into the characteristics and biological functions of fibroblast subpopulations. We compared transcriptional profiles between fibroblasts from the WJMSCs and Model groups (Fig. 7A) and found that differentially expressed genes were predominantly enriched in immune response regulation, cellular senescence, antigen processing and presentation, and the IL-17 signaling pathway (Fig. 7B). To further elucidate fibroblast heterogeneity, we performed unsupervised reclustering analysis, identifying six distinct subpopulations (Fig. 7C) based on established markers and previous literature [6]. A heatmap illustrates the unique gene expression patterns of each subpopulation (Fig. 7D). The six fibroblast subtypes were characterized as follows (Fig. 7E): (1) Mesenchymal fibroblasts (MF), expressing mesenchymal progenitor markers such as Col11a1 and Postn, were primarily associated with extracellular matrix organization; wound healing; skeletal system development; and muscle cell differentiation. (2) Pro-inflammatory fibroblasts (PF), expressing Mmp3 and Efemp1, were mainly involved in leukocyte migration, chemotaxis, and cellular response to IL-1. (3) Secretory-papillary fibroblasts (SPF), expressing Apcdd1, Wif1, and Col23a1, participated in Wnt signaling pathway modulation, positive regulation of cell projection organization, and neurogenesis. (4) Secretory-reticular fibroblasts (SRF), expressing Slpi and Cthrc1, were implicated in ECM synthesis, collagen fibril assembly, and tissue remodeling. (5) SRF_MF hybrid fibroblasts, co-expressing genes including Gpx3, Gsn, and Ogn, were involved in responses to external stimuli and activation of defense mechanisms. (6) An undefined subpopulation, expressing Malat1, Meg3, and Dlc1, was associated with regulation of synaptic receptor localization, response to nerve growth factor, and neutrophil chemotaxis.

Research shows that one of the characteristics of cellular senescence is the prolonged stagnation of the cell cycle. Our results reveal that the G1/S transition of the cell cycle in SRF and PF in the Model group was significantly delayed, but this transition was improved after treatment with WJMSCs (Fig. 7F), suggesting that these two fibroblast subsets may have a closer connection with skin aging. Senescent cells secrete a series of pro-inflammatory factors and chemokines, collectively known as the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP). We observed differential expression profiles of SASP-related genes in different fibroblast subsets (Fig. 7G), and found the expression scores of SRF and PF were the highest (Fig. 7H). The activation status of each fibroblast subpopulation in aging-related pathways was determined by Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) enrichment scores. SRF and PF were identified as the two subpopulations with the highest activation levels and were significantly enriched in the apoptosis pathway, p53 signaling pathway, and insulin signaling pathway (Fig. 7I). In conclusion, these results suggest that SRF and PF are key cellular targets driving skin aging. Moreover, WJMSCs can effectively modulate these two types of cells to delay skin aging.

Multi-omics collaboration reveals anti-aging targets of WJMSCs

Single-omics approaches have certain limitations in presenting comprehensive results. Integrating bulk RNA-seq with scRNA-seq allows for a global assessment of changes in target genes within specific cell subpopulations. We intersected the DEGs obtained from bulk RNA-seq with those from the SRF and PF subpopulations (WJMSCs group versus Model group). The results showed three overlapping genes (Hspa1b, Zbtb16, Dbp) with PF-DEGs, and one overlapping gene (Dbp) with SRF-DEGs (Fig. 8A, B). Therefore, Dbp is hypothesized to be a key target gene, through which WJMSCs exert their therapeutic effects. Further comparison revealed that the expression level of Dbp was significantly downregulated in the WJMSCs group compared to the Model group (Fig. 8C). It has been reported that Dbp is a gene involved in regulating circadian rhythm, and the aging process leads to dysregulation of circadian rhythm-related genes [25]. The expression pattern of Dbp in the single-cell transcriptome was found to be primarily expressed in fibroblasts (Fig. 8D), particularly within the PF subpopulation (Fig. 8E–F). We also confirmed the differential expression of Dbp between the Model and WJMSCs groups using immunofluorescence (Fig. 8G).

This study further explored the transcriptional changes in the PF subpopulation after WJMSCs treatment. Enrichment analysis of downregulated DEGs in the WJMSCs group compared to the Model group indicated that PF in aged skin is involved in biological processes such as senescence-related pathways, cellular response to IL-4, and endoplasmic reticulum stress. These results reveal dysregulated signaling pathways in aged PF, including the estrogen signaling pathway and the IL-17 signaling pathway (Fig. 8H). Enrichment analysis of upregulated genes also indicated involvement of the IL-17 signaling pathway, suggesting that WJMSCs may regulate PF function through this pathway (Fig. 8I). Therefore, both bulk and single-cell transcriptomic analyses highlight the importance of the IL-17 signaling pathway in skin aging.

Discussion

Skin aging represents a multifaceted biological process driven by the gradual decline of cellular functions, the disruption of ECM homeostasis, and the presence of chronic inflammatory microenvironments. In this study, we provide multidimensional evidence demonstrating that WJMSCs significantly counteract both cellular and tissue-level aging phenotypes. More importantly, by integrating bulk transcriptomics, single-cell RNA sequencing, and proteomic profiling, we reveal WJMSCs exert rejuvenating effects by reprogramming fibroblast subpopulations and regulating related signaling networks.

It is important to clarify the source of the observed therapeutic effects. In our in vitro experiments, we used CM from WJMSCs, indicating that the rejuvenating effects on senescent HDFs are mediated by secreted factors (e.g., cytokines, growth factors, and extracellular vesicles) rather than by direct cell–cell contact [26]. In contrast, the in vivo experiments involved subcutaneous injection of live WJMSCs. While paracrine actions likely contribute substantially to the observed improvements, we cannot exclude the possibility that direct interactions between transplanted live WJMSCs and host cells (e.g., fibroblasts, macrophages) also play a role. Indeed, MSCs can interact with immune cells and other host cells through direct cell-to-cell contact mechanisms [27, 28]. Therefore, the anti-aging effects reported in this study are attributable primarily to WJMSC-derived secretory factors in the in vitro setting, whereas both paracrine mechanisms and potential direct cellular crosstalk may underlie the in vivo efficacy.

Macrophages play a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of skin aging. Studies have indicated that the aged skin microenvironment drives the polarization of skin macrophages toward the M1 phenotype. This pro-inflammatory M1-like phenotype, in turn, negatively impacts the expression of ECM proteins, by fibroblasts, further disrupting the skin ECM [29]. In our aged skin model, we observed consistent results, namely the high expression of M1 macrophage markers and the inflammatory mediator IL-1β. We also confirmed that WJMSCs can modulate macrophage phenotypic switching, manifested by an increase in the level of the M2 macrophage marker (CD163) and a concurrent decrease in the expression of M1 macrophage markers (CD86) and IL-1β. This suppresses the inflammatory cascade, reduces the expression of the senescence-associated biomarkers (SA-β-gal and p16), and effectively interrupts the vicious cycle of "inflammation-tissue senescence." Another mechanism of skin aging is the depletion of endogenous stem cells [30]; among these, Lgr6+ stem cells are among the most important. Lgr6 has been identified as a specific marker for a subpopulation of skin stem cells with broad lineage potential. Adult Lgr6+ cells possess long-term wound repair capabilities. They include the ability to form new hair follicles and are crucial for skin regeneration [22]. This study observed downregulated Lgr6 expression in the aged skin model group, while WJMSC treatment reactivated its expression. This suggests that WJMSCs may promote skin regeneration, including hair follicle regeneration, by activating skin stem cells. This finding aligns with the results from HE staining in this study, which showed restored hair follicle density and reestablished tissue polarity. It is important to note that the characterization of macrophage M1/M2 phenotypic switching and skin stem cell molecular markers in this study primarily relied on semi-quantitative analyses. These detection methods have limitations in terms of quantitative accuracy and validating molecular interactions, necessitating more in-depth follow-up studies.

Fibroblasts actively coordinate immune responses and tissue architecture within tissues [31], and their heterogeneity and plasticity in both health and disease highlight the necessity for precision medicine approaches. As the primary cellular component of the dermis, fibroblasts maintain skin homeostasis through the synthesis and organization of the ECM [32, 33]. Recent single-cell transcriptomic studies have revealed the functional diversity of skin fibroblasts, identifying distinct subpopulations with specific roles in inflammation, wound healing, and fibrotic responses [34–36]. Through scRNA-seq analysis, we classified fibroblasts into six subsets, among which, PF and SRF fibroblasts were closely associated with aging. These subsets showed high expression of genes involved in IL-1 response, leukocyte migration, and ECM synthesis. This is consistent with previous reports on inflammatory fibroblasts contributing to tissue fibrosis and aging in other organs [37–39]. Importantly, after WJMSC treatment, we observed a global increase in RNA metabolism, including both RNA synthesis and degradation, as well as enhanced de novo transcription in fibroblasts. These findings suggest that WJMSCs not only ameliorate aging-related secretory phenotypes but also enhance transcriptional activity and promote cellular regeneration. Furthermore, analysis of cell–cell communication identified fibroblasts as communication hubs that extensively interact with immune, endothelial, and epithelial cells through ligand-receptor pairs such as Cxcl12-Cxcr4 and Ccl7-Ccr5. This finding aligns with the emerging concept of fibroblasts as coordinators of immune-stromal crosstalk in the skin [40] and extends it to the context of stem cell-based rejuvenation therapies.

Our study reveals an additional layer of regulation involving the critical participation of the IL-17 signaling pathway. We found that this pathway is significantly enriched at both transcriptional and protein levels in aged skin and is modulated following treatment with WJMSCs. IL-17 is widely implicated in chronic inflammatory skin diseases such as psoriasis, which promotes keratinocyte proliferation and the release of inflammatory mediators [41]. Our results position IL-17 as a key player in natural skin aging, contributing to persistent low-grade inflammation, ECM degradation, and fibroblast senescence. Previous studies have suggested that IL-17 can induce skin aging [23], and our data demonstrate that WJMSC treatment suppresses IL-17 pathway activity and reduces inflammatory cytokine levels. Therefore, targeting the IL-17 pathway may represent a promising strategy for ameliorating skin aging. Building on this, by integrating bulk RNA-seq and single-cell RNA-seq data, we identified DBP (D-site-binding protein), a circadian output regulator, as a key gene downregulated upon WJMSC treatment. DBP is increasingly linked to aging and cellular homeostasis. Circadian rhythm disruption accelerates aging in multiple tissues. Additionally, core clock genes influence lifespan in model organisms [42]. Our study shows that DBP is predominantly expressed in pro-inflammatory fibroblasts and is upregulated in aged skin. Immunofluorescence validation confirmed reduced DBP protein expression in the WJMSC-treated group. This suggests that ameliorating circadian dysregulation may be part of the restorative mechanism of WJMSCs. Interestingly, recent evidence indicates that inflammatory stimuli can disrupt cellular circadian rhythms [43], and clock genes regulate NF-κB-dependent inflammation [44].Thus, DBP may lie at the intersection of aging, inflammation, and circadian biology, making DBP a novel candidate for therapeutic intervention.

The present study has some limitations. First, the use of immunodeficient nude mouse models has limited the investigation of the full spectrum of immune interactions that may affect human skin aging and the efficacy of WJMSCs—particularly adaptive immune responses. The absence of functional T and B cells could oversimplify the inflammatory landscape and potentially exaggerate or mask the activation of certain pathways, such as IL-17 signaling. Second, although we identified candidate genes regulated by Dbp through comprehensive transcriptomic analyses and confirmed their expression patterns by immunofluorescence, functional validation using knockout or overexpression models was lacking. Therefore, the causal relationship between the downregulation of Dbp and the improvement of cellular senescence cannot be confirmed. Finally, scRNA-seq provided rich cellular insights but do not incorporate spatial context. The absence of spatial transcriptomics or multiplex immunohistochemistry limits our ability to address the geographical specificity of fibroblast subtypes and their interactions within the native tissue environment.

Conclusions

WJMSCs significantly alleviate skin aging phenotypes through multi-pathway synergistic effects such as restoring collagen homeostasis, inhibiting inflammatory responses, reducing cellular senescence, and promoting the regenerative microenvironment. This study not only deepens the understanding of fibroblast heterogeneity but also provides a theoretical basis and potential intervention targets for anti-aging treatment strategies based on targeting fibroblast subpopulations or key signaling pathways.
