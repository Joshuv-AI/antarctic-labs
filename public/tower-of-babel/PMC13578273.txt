Qualitative gas chromatography–mass spectrometry and liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry fingerprinting of tissue-specific phytochemicals in finnish Quercus robur L. acorns
Authors: Inga-Liina Järvinen, Rahim Ullah, Kari Hartonen, Susanne K Wiedmer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcorns of Quercus robur L. are abundant in Finland but remain underutilized, and their chemical composition in high-latitude regions is not well documented. In this study, Finnish acorns were analyzed using gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS) and liquid chromatography–high resolution mass spectrometry (LC–HRMS) to provide a qualitative overview of their nonpolar and polar compounds. We examined fresh whole acorns as well as dried acorn nuts and dried pericarp fractions. GC–MS analysis indicated that fresh whole acorns were characterized by signals corresponding to phytosterols and tocopherol-related compounds, whereas dried acorn nuts presented increased relative abundances of long-chain n-alkanes. Similarly, dried pericarp analysis showed sterol-related signals and long-chain aldehydes. LC–MS analysis suggested that polar fractions were characterized by ellagitannins, phenolic acids, flavonoids, and organic acids. Although the study is qualitative, it provides the first combined GC–MS and LC–MS fingerprint of the chemical profiles of Finnish Quercus robur L. acorns. Introduction

The genus Quercus belongs to the family Fagaceae, which comprises several hundred species and is one of the most widely distributed tree genera worldwide. Quercus occurs across many temperate and subtropical regions, including Europe, Asia, North Africa, and the Americas. Different groups within the genus are dominant in different regions, reflecting their broad ecological adaptability and importance in forest ecosystems1. These species produce a well-known fruit called the acorn. From a morphological perspective, the acorn is a single-seeded nut that lacks endosperm and contains an achlorophyllous embryo2. Acorns from Quercus robur L., also known as English oak, have significant nutritional and bioactive potential. These acorns are rich in diverse phytochemicals, which contribute to their notable antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antimicrobial properties1,3,4. In addition to their physiological roles, acorns have attracted attention for their nutritional value and phytochemical composition2. Studies have shown considerable variation in these traits among different species and, in some cases, even within the same species. These differences are commonly attributed to environmental factors such as soil type and climate, as well as to processes involved in regeneration. Even so, recent research has confirmed several beneficial properties of acorns, suggesting that their potential use as a natural resource is likely to expand across a range of applications in the future2. However, reported differences in chemical composition across studies are likely associated with the geographical origin of the acorns5.

Finland is one of the densest forested countries in Europe, with forests covering more than 75% of its land area. These forests harbor a wide range of biodiversity and provide numerous ecosystem services6,7. Among Finland’s broadleaf tree species, one is Quercus robur L., which occurs mainly in various parts of the country where climatic conditions allow its growth. In Finland, Quercus robur L. plays valuable ecological and economic roles8,9. Quercus robur L. produces acorns, hard and nut-like fruits, that serve as a major means of reproduction and a source of nutrition for wildlife. Large quantities of acorns fall to the ground and remain largely unutilized, eventually decomposing as natural waste. Despite extensive studies on Quercus acorns from Central and Southern Europe, the chemical composition of Quercus robur L. acorns from high-latitude regions such as Finland remains largely undocumented, particularly with respect to tissue-specific profiles and combined GC–MS and LC–MS characterization. These fallen acorns, particularly their seeds, represent an underexplored bioresource that may contain a wide range of valuable chemical compounds. For example, acorns could be developed into a coffee substitute10 and as a source for bioethanol11. Therefore, in this study, we first used ultrasound-assisted extraction (UAE) because its reported effectiveness enhances extraction efficiency while minimizing thermal degradation12,13. Extracts were analyzed with GC–MS and LC‒MS for comprehensive profiling of the bioactive constituents for the valorization of acorns beyond their traditional use as animal feed.

Materials and chemicals

Chemicals and reagents

High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC)-grade methanol ≥ 99.8% and ethanol ≥ 99.8% were purchased from Fisher Scientific (United Kingdom). HPLC grade n-hexane 97+% was obtained from Thermo Scientific (Germany), Ultra HPLC grade acetonitrile (USA), formic acid 99–100% was obtained from VWR Chemicals (France), and the C7-C40 saturated alkanes standard 49,452-U was obtained from Supelco (USA). Instrumental helium (99.996% for GC) was purchased from Linde Gas AB (Finland). Hydrophobic PTFE syringe filters (0.45 μm; Part No. AF8-7102-12, Clarify), filter paper (grade 597, Schleicher & Schuell, Germany), and Millex hydrophilic PTFE syringe filters (0.45 μm; ref. SLCR013NK, Merck Millipore Ltd., Ireland) were used. Ultrapure water was prepared via a Millipore Direct-Q 3 instrument.

Collection and identification

Fully mature acorns of Quercus robur L. were collected in October 2025 during the natural ripening season from naturally growing trees in Espoo, Finland (60°14’40.8"N, 24°45’21.6"E). The acorns were gathered from the forest floor without causing damage to trees or surrounding vegetation. Collection was conducted in accordance with Finnish legislation, including Everyman’s Rights (Jokamiehenoikeus) and the Finnish Nature Conservation Act. Quercus robur L. is a common and non-protected species in Finland; therefore, no specific permit was required for the collection of fallen acorns for research purposes. Identification of Quercus robur L. was performed by Dr. Alexander Sennikov, plant taxonomist at the Botanical Museum, Finnish Museum of Natural History (LUOMUS), University of Helsinki (Helsinki, Finland). A voucher specimen has been deposited in the LUOMUS herbarium under accession identifier NE.3672 (Box 5887) and can be accessed at http://id.luomus.fi/NE.3672. The study complied with relevant institutional, national, and international guidelines, including the policy principles of the International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN) and the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora (CITES). Following collection, the acorns were rinsed with distilled water and divided into two groups: fresh and dried. One portion was air-dried at room temperature for 16 days. The dried acorns were subsequently separated into nuts (including seed coat) and dried pericarp. All samples were ground into a coarse powder prior to extraction.

Extraction and fractionation

For the extraction of acorn from Quercus robur L., different parts of the nut were used: whole fresh acorn (undried pericarp and nut), dried acorn nut, and dried acorn pericarp. A 3.0 g portion was extracted with EtOH/H₂O (70:30, v/v) with a solid-solvent ratio of 1:10 (w/v) via ultrasound-assisted extraction (UAE) at room temperature for 35 min (Fig. 1). For each sample, extraction was repeated eight times and the solvent with the extract was vacuum filtered and dried under a nitrogen stream at 30 °C. A total of 100 ml of methanol: water mixture (10:90, v/v) was prepared for fractionation. For liquid-liquid extraction, the dried crude extract was reconstituted in 20 mL of MeOH/H₂O and sonicated for 5 min to completely dissolve the crude extract. After that, 20 ml of hexane was added for separation of nonpolar compounds, and the process was repeated four times to isolate nonpolar compounds completely. From the resulting hexane fraction containing nonpolar compounds, the solvent (hexane) was evaporated under a nitrogen stream. Similarly, the remaining polar fraction (MeOH/H₂O) was also dried under a stream of nitrogen and weighed to establish the fraction yield. The same extraction protocol was used for the dried acorn nut and pericarp but with the initial sample mass reduced to 1.0 g. Gas chromatography‒mass spectrometry analysis

The dried hexane fractions of the fresh whole acorn, the dried acorn nut, and the pericarp were reconstituted in 4, 2 and 1 mL of hexane, respectively, and filtered through a hydrophobic 0.45 μm syringe filter into a stock solution prior to analysis. From this stock solution, diluted samples were prepared with concentrations of 1.19 mg/mL for fresh whole acorns, 0.45 mg/mL for dried acorns, and 1.14 mg/mL for dried acorn pericarp. GC–MS analysis was carried out via an Agilent 6890 N Network GC System coupled to an Agilent 5973 N system equipped with a quadrupole mass detector. Separation was achieved with a DB-5MS capillary column (29.5 m×0.25 mm i.d., 0.25 μm phase thickness) fitted to a 3 m (0.53 mm i.d.) deactivated precolumn. The samples were injected manually at 280 °C in splitless mode with an injection volume of 2 µL. Helium was used as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. The oven temperature program started at 50 °C with a 2 min hold, followed by a ramp of 8 °C/min to 300 °C and a final hold of 10 min, resulting in a total run time of 43 min. A saturated alkane C7–C40 standard was prepared in n-hexane at a concentration of 5 µg/mL and used for determination of alkane retention indices. Solvent blanks were analyzed under identical GC–MS conditions to monitor background contamination. Compound annotation was performed using the NIST 2020 mass spectral library, Agilent MassHunter software (version Qualitative analysis B.07.00, 2014), and retention index comparison where available. As no derivatization or authentic standards were used, non-alkane compounds are reported as tentatively identified.

Liquid chromatography‒mass spectrometry analysis

The dried MeOH/H₂O fractions of fresh whole acorn, dried acorn nut, and dried acorn pericarp were redissolved in 10 mL of water, resulting in a stock solution, and filtered through 0.45 μm hydrophilic PTFE syringe filters prior to LC‒MS. For the preparation of the LC‒MS samples, the stock solution was further diluted with water at a 1:2 (v/v) ratio. Analysis was performed via an Agilent 1260 Infinity II HPLC system coupled to a Thermo Scientific Q Exactive HF-X Hybrid Quadrupole–Orbitrap mass spectrometer. The HPLC system was equipped with an autosampler, a thermostated column compartment, and a quaternary pump. Separation was carried out on an XBridge Premier C18 reversed-phase column (Waters, USA; 50 × 2.1 mm, 2.5 μm). The mobile phase consisted of (A) 0.1% formic acid in water and (B) acetonitrile. The LC gradient program was as follows: 10% B was held from 0.0 to 1.0 min, increased linearly from 10% to 90% B from 1.0 to 7.5 min, held at 90% B from 7.5 to 9.0 min, returned to 10% B from 9.0 to 10.0 min, and then maintained at 10% B until 24.0 min for column re-equilibration. The total LC–MS run time was 24 min. Mass spectrometric data were acquired over a scan range of m/z 150–2000. Data were acquired in negative electrospray ionization mode (ESI−). Full-scan MS and data-dependent MS/MS spectra were acquired using Orbitrap detection. Data processing was performed using MZmine 4.8.5 with the Orbitrap data-dependent acquisition workflow for feature detection, deconvolution, and spectral matching. Compound annotation was putative and based on accurate mass, isotope pattern, MS/MS fragmentation, retention behavior, and database matching against publicly available spectral libraries, including GNPS14, corresponding to MSI level 2 annotation.

Results and discussion

Ultrasound-assisted extraction (UAE) is a more efficient and sustainable technique than traditional extraction methods, such as maceration. Studies have demonstrated that UAE significantly enhances extraction while reducing extraction time and solvent usage15. The crude extract yield of the dried acorn nut was the highest at 26.4%, followed by that of fresh whole acorn, with a crude yield of 17%. In addition, the crude yield for the dried acorn pericarp was the lowest at 3.4%. The highest crude yield of 26.4% from dried acorn nuts indicates that drying concentrates extractable compounds, likely by reducing moisture and facilitating solvent penetration16,17. In contrast, the yield from the dried pericarp is substantially lower at 3.4%, indicating that the outer layer contains fewer extractable compounds or is less amenable to extraction. Fresh whole acorns have an intermediate yield of 17.0%, reflecting the presence of moisture, which may hinder solvent efficiency.

The crude extracts of every sample contained significantly more water- and methanol-soluble compounds than hexane-soluble compounds, which can be observed from the fraction yields in Table 1. Notably, none of them reached a total of 100% recovery from the crude extract. A plausible explanation is that some compounds were lost during sample handling, whereas volatile constituents may evaporate during the drying steps. These differences highlight the importance of selecting the appropriate part and sample preparation for optimizing extraction yields for industrial or research purposes. Similar studies emphasize that acorn species and extraction methods influence yield and bioactive compound content, with drying often increasing the extract concentration and quality18,19. Understanding these factors is important for maximizing the value of acorn extracts for further applications.

Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry analysis

GC–MS analysis of the n-hexane fraction of Finnish whole acorns (fresh) revealed different classes of lipophilic compounds, characterized by signals corresponding to sterol and tocopherol. A total of seven major compounds were tentatively identified. The most prominent signal corresponded to sitosterol isomer, accounting for 57.06% of the total peak area, followed by tocopherol isomer (relative peak area 10.53%). Similarly, other compounds included the triterpenes squalene (3.53%), stigmasterol (2.65%) and campesterol (2.35%). Our results of fresh whole acorns revealed a high content of sitosterol isomer in Finnish acorns, which is in accordance with those found at lower latitudes. In Mediterranean and Central European studies of Quercus species, β-sitosterol is typically reported as the primary sterol, often comprising more than 60–80% of the total sterol fraction20,21 and with a total tocopherol content ranging from 973 to 1486 mg/kg of acorn oil22. Research has reported that phytosterols (especially β-sitosterol), tocopherols, squalene, and fatty acids are the major constituents of acorn oils, with some variation in relative abundance depending on the species, extraction method, and geographic origin23,24. Here, Finnish acorns showed relatively strong sterol-related signals; however, specific sterol isomers cannot be unambiguously distinguished without authentic standards. Regardless, previous works have reported high concentrations of γ-tocopherol in Quercus robur L25. The β-homolog has previously been reported more frequently in Quercus rubra25. The high presence of sterols such as γ-sitosterol, campesterol, and stigmasterol suggests potential bioactive properties, as these compounds are known for their antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and cholesterol-lowering effects26,27. Squalene (3.53%) was detected in moderate proportions. While squalene has been identified in specific plant oils such as olive oil, its presence in acorn lipophilic extracts aligns with observations that nuts can contain measurable amounts of squalene, although typically at lower absolute concentrations than other nuts do28.

GC–MS analysis of the n-hexane fraction of dried acorn nuts identified 15 major compounds. The profile is dominated by long-chain n-alkanes ranging from tetradecane to tetratriacontane with minor amounts of tocopherol and sitosterol isomers. The identities of the n-alkanes were confirmed with a standard solution containing C7-C40 alkanes (Fig. S2). The most abundant alkanes were docosane (9.60%) and eicosane (9.50%). Sitosterol isomer (2.01%) and tocopherol isomer (1.05%) remained present but at lower relative abundances than those in the fresh whole-acorn sample. The GC-MS results for the dried nuts are summarized in Table 2, and a representative chromatogram is shown in Fig. S3.

Previous studies of dried acorn nuts of Quercus robur L. are limited. Nevertheless, the fatty acid and hydrocarbon compositions of other species, including Quercus ilex, Quercus suber, Quercus rotundifolia and Quercus faginea, have been analyzed20,23,24. Most GC‒MS profiles of acorn oil are dominated by phytosterols and fatty acids, with alkanes present only in trace amounts20,24. Fatty acids were not detected in the GC‒MS analysis, most likely due to lack of derivatization. A previous study analyzing the alkane composition of Quercus suber and Quercus ilex acorns reported a greater abundance of longer alkanes (C27–C30) than the major alkanes C18–C26 identified in Quercus robur29. Although only even-numbered hydrocarbons were detected in the present study, odd-numbered carbon chains have been shown to be more abundant in other acorns29. The n-alkane assignments were supported by retention behavior and comparison with the C7–C40 alkane standard series. Free fatty acids were not clearly detected under the non-derivatized GC–MS conditions, highlighting the need for derivatization in future targeted lipid profiling. The increased relative abundance of alkanes in the dried acorn nut sample may reflect changes in extractability or relative enrichment of non-polar compounds following drying. Previous studies reported alkanes from the oils of Quercus species such as Quercus ilex and Quercus suber29. Here, the chain length distribution, from C14 to C34, reflects a complex wax and hydrocarbon mixture that likely originated from cuticular layers and stored lipid transformations. Despite the drying process, both tocopherol and sitosterol were still detected in the samples. However, the tocopherol content decreased to 1.05% after drying, which likely reflects its susceptibility to degradation during heat treatment and light and air exposure30. Even so, its presence indicates a certain level of stability under the applied drying and extraction conditions25. Similarly, a high level of long-chain alkanes in Finnish dried acorn nuts is likely the result of the combined effects of drying and the selectivity of 70% EtOH extraction.

GC-MS analysis of the dried acorn pericarp revealed that sterols and long-chain aldehydes were the predominant compounds. The strongest sterol-related signal corresponded to a tentatively assigned sitosterol isomer, accounting for 38.45% of the total peak area. Aldehydes such as hexacosanal and octacosanal were present at 14.74% and 5.66%, respectively. Among the alkanes, eicosane (C20), docosane (C22), tetracosane (C24), and hexacosane (C26) were detected, with relative abundances ranging from 2.15% to 3.03%. The results of the analysis of the dried acorn pericarp are summarized in Table 2, and a representative chromatogram is given in Fig. S4.

The detection of long-chain aldehydes, particularly hexacosanal and octacosanal, in this study is noteworthy. Many compounds, such as aldehydes, become relatively more abundant because they are less likely to be lost during sample pretreatment, as they are resistant to various drying conditions31. Aldehydes can form from the oxidative cleavage of fatty acids during drying or storage and represent generative products rather than primary metabolites32. In addition, when the pericarp and nut extracts were compared, the pericarp had more intact sterols than the nut fraction, where hydrocarbons dominated. The pericarp’s structural role and exposure to abiotic stress during drying may increase its susceptibility to lipid oxidation, explaining the relatively high abundance of long-chain aldehydes. Therefore, the drying process results in the production of hydrophobic compounds via the oxidative and thermal transformation of lipids33, which may lead to increased relative abundances of very‑long‑chain aldehydes and alkanes not observed in fresh whole-acorn extracts. Importantly, to the best of our knowledge, GC–MS profiling of lipophilic constituents of the acorn pericarp has not been previously reported. However, existing extraction studies on acorn pericarp polysaccharides have focused primarily on monosaccharide compositions and cell wall–derived components, whereas the profile of lipophilic compounds has not been investigated via GC–MS34. Consequently, the profiling of our n-hexane fraction represents a novel contribution in this context.

A comparative assessment of the three anatomical fractions of acorn, i.e., fresh whole acorn, dried nut, and dried pericarp, revealed differences in the relative distribution of tentatively assigned lipophilic compounds. Fresh whole acorns showed relatively stronger signals corresponding to sterol- and tocopherol-related compounds, whereas the dried nut fraction showed a shift toward a homologous series of long-chain n-alkanes (C14–C34), with docosane (C22) and eicosane (C20) as the major detected hydrocarbons. These differences may reflect changes in extractability or relative enrichment of non-polar compounds following drying rather than definitive tissue-specific accumulation. In contrast, the dried pericarp fraction showed a relatively strong sterol-related signal together with long-chain aldehydes, particularly hexacosanal (14.74%) and octacosanal (5.66%). The presence of these aldehydes may be associated with lipid oxidation during drying or storage. Compared with previous studies of Mediterranean Quercus species, where sterols and fatty acids are commonly reported as major lipid markers20,35, the present non-derivatized GC–MS analysis mainly revealed sterol-related features, long-chain aldehydes, and n-alkanes. Therefore, these observations should be interpreted as qualitative trends rather than definitive compositional differences. Because the GC–MS analysis was performed without derivatization and without authentic standards for non-alkane compounds, future targeted lipid profiling using saponification, derivatization, and standard-based confirmation will be required to verify the identity and relative importance of these lipophilic constituents.

Liquid chromatography‒mass spectrometry analysis

LC-MS in negative mode was used to determine the compound composition of fresh whole acorns (Fig. S5). Several highly polar metabolites, particularly organic acids and carbohydrates, eluted at early retention times under the reversed-phase C18 conditions, which may reflect limited retention on the stationary phase. Therefore, these early-eluting compounds were annotated cautiously, mainly based on accurate mass, isotope pattern, and MS/MS fragmentation. Their assignments should be considered putative, especially for structurally related isomers, for which confirmation would require authentic reference standards. Organic acids, including citric acid, D-quinic acid, and gluconic acid, presented high relative signal intensities. Similarly, polyphenols and hydrolyzable tannins, including ellagic acid, ellagic acid pentoside, galloyl glucose, 1,6-digalloyl-β-D-glucopyranose, 1,2,3-tri-O-galloyl-β-D-glucose, 1,2,4,6-tetra-O-galloyl-β-D-glucose, brevifolin-carboxylic acid, and ethyl gallate, are prominent. These findings indicate a rich composition of bioactive secondary metabolites in fresh whole acorn. Oxylipins, specifically 12-hydroxyjasmonic acid and its glucoside and sulfate derivatives, were also observed, suggesting the presence of jasmonate-related signaling compounds. In addition, carbohydrates such as isomaltulose and raffinose were identified, reflecting the sugar content and storage compounds. Polyphenols constituted the dominant class, followed by organic acids, oxylipins, and carbohydrates, highlighting the complex metabolic composition of fresh acorns. Our findings align with previous metabolomic studies of Quercus species, which reported that high phenolic contents, particularly ellagitannins and organic acids, have antioxidant potential and nutritional value3,36–38. Our results are similar to those of other studies where quinic acid has been reported as the most abundant metabolite in acorns, although previous studies have focused less on organic acids5. Here, the presence of jasmonic acid derivatives, such as 12-hydroxyjasmonic acid glucoside isomers, indicates active plant defense and signaling compounds. This finding is consistent with the known roles of jasmonates in stress responses, and prior studies have identified jasmonates in the acorns of Quercus serrata var. brevipetiolata39. 12‑Hydroxyjasmonic acid and its glucosides/sulfate have not been commonly reported in published LC–MS profiles of acorns. In addition, the detection of galloyl glucose derivatives and raffinose family oligosaccharides revealed complex carbohydrate and polyphenol profiles typical of fresh acorns, which are reportedly associated with their functional food properties40–42.

LC-MS analysis of the MeOH/H₂O fraction of the dried Finnish acorn nut revealed that polyphenols and hydrolyzable tannins, including ellagic acid, ellagic acid pentoside, ethyl gallate, gallic acid, digalloyl-β-D-glucose, vescalagin, castalagin, and pedunculagin, were the predominant metabolites. Carbohydrates and oligosaccharides, including a disaccharide putatively assigned as isomaltulose, sucrose, and raffinose, were also present, whereas organic acids (citric acid, D-quinic acid) and oxylipin derivatives (12-hydroxyjasmonic acid glucoside and 12-hydroxyjasmonate sulfate) occurred at lower relative levels. Our results indicate that polyphenolic compounds constitute the major class in dried acorn nuts, highlighting their rich secondary metabolite composition. The results of the LC-MS analysis of dried acorn nuts are summarized in Table 3, and a representative chromatogram is given in Fig. S6.

LC‒MS analysis of dried Finnish acorn nuts revealed high amounts of hydrolyzable tannins, such as vescalagin, castalagin, and pedunculagin. This reflects the persistence of bioactive polyphenols, known for their antioxidant and enzyme inhibitory activities, toward drying3,4,43. Our results support the nutraceutical potential of dried acorn nuts, which is consistent with reports of their use in functional foods and their role in modulating digestive enzymes relevant to glycemic control41,43. Previous studies have identified ellagic acid, gallic acid, and their derivatives as the major phenolic constituents in acorn flour extracts via LC–MS and HPLC–MS/MS38. Similarly, sugars and sugar derivatives, such as raffinose and sucrose, are typical seed metabolites, as shown by broad metabolomics profiling of acorns. A more recent study noted abundant carbohydrates in Quercus robur L. acorns via untargeted MS‑based approaches37. Although many of the metabolites we identified in the dried Finnish acorn nut extract have also been reported in other Quercus robur L. acorn studies, the overall profile and relative abundances differ from those in these previous reports3,44. However, the differences between our results and those of previous studies are likely due to variations in geographical origin, species, drying treatment, extraction conditions, and LC–MS methodologies (HPLC-HRMS used here), all of which may influence the acorn metabolite profile and relative abundances. Overall, the metabolite profile of dried acorn nuts is dominated by polyphenolic compounds, with substantial contributions from carbohydrates and a minor representation of organic acids and oxylipins, highlighting the rich chemical diversity and potential bioactive properties of acorns.

LC-MS profiling of the MeOH/H₂O fraction of the Finnish dried acorn pericarp revealed metabolites belonging mainly to polyphenols, organic acids, and lipid-related compounds. Polyphenols constitute the dominant class and include hydrolyzable tannins (ellagic acid, castalagin, and vescalagin), flavonoids and their glycosides (tiliroside, isoquercetin, taxifolin-3-glucoside, and quercitrin), flavan-3-ols and proanthocyanidins (catechin and procyanidin B1), and phenolic acids (gallic acid). Organic acids and sugar acids, such as citric acid, D-quinic acid, mucic acid, and gluconic acid, represent primary metabolic components. Lipid-related and fatty acid-derived metabolites, including 9,12,13-trihydroxyoctadeca-10,15-dienoic acid and 9,12,13-Trihydroxy-10E-octadecenoic acid, together with triterpenoids such as trachelosperogenin A1 and barrinic acid, were also detected. The results of the LC-MS analysis of the dried acorn pericarp are summarized in Table 3, and a representative chromatogram is given in Fig. S7.

A prior study reported the presence of barrinic acid in a Quercus robur L. heartwood extract45. Similarly, the detection of castalagin and vescalagin confirmed the abundance of ellagitannins in the pericarp, which aligns with literature reports that highlight the high phenolic content of acorn cups and shells, which serve as rich sources of antioxidants and bioactive compounds3,40. The detection of (epi)catechin and procyanidin B1 suggests that the pericarp contains flavan-3-ol-type compounds, although quantitative analysis would be required to confirm tissue-specific enrichment. These compounds are claimed to have health benefits, including anti-inflammatory and cardioprotective effects36,37.

The metabolite profiles across fresh whole acorn, dried nut, and pericarp fractions revealed a complex mixture of organic acids, sugars, ellagitannins, and flavonoids, which is consistent with the known phytochemical richness of Quercus species. Variations in relative abundance reflect processing effects (fresh vs. dried) and tissue-specific metabolite distributions, which influence the nutritional and functional properties of acorns. These findings corroborate recent metabolomic analyses that highlight the potential of acorns as sustainable, antioxidant-rich food sources with applications in functional foods and nutraceuticals3,36,37,41. Among our acorn extracts, ellagic acid and gallic acid were the only compounds that directly overlapped with those reported in Polish Quercus robur L. acorn flour44. In addition, our samples contained ellagitannins (vescalagin, castalagin, and pedunculagin isomers), sugars (isomaltulose, sucrose, and raffinose), organic acids (citric and D‑quinic acids), and jasmonates (12‑hydroxyjasmonic acid and its derivatives), which were either minor or not reported in their study. These differences likely reflect species- or tissue-specific compositions, extraction methods, and the inclusion of pericarp versus kernel, highlighting the diverse metabolite profiles of acorns beyond those of common phenolics.

Conclusions

This study provides a preliminary qualitative chemical overview of Finnish Quercus robur L. acorns using GC–MS and LC–MS. The observed chemical profiles differed among fresh whole acorns, dried nuts, and dried pericarp fractions. Fresh whole acorns showed relatively strong signals corresponding to sterol- and tocopherol-related compounds, dried nuts showed increased relative signals for long-chain n-alkanes, and the pericarp contained sterol-related signals together with long-chain aldehydes. The observed polar compounds were different among the three fractions. Fresh whole acorns showed relatively strong signals corresponding to citric acid, D-quinic acid and ellagic acids. Dried nuts were characterized by high relative signals of hydrolysable tannins such as vescalagin and castalagin. In contrast, pericarp was characterized by ellagic acid, flavonoids and flavonoid glycosides, including catechin, isoquercetin, quercitrin, and tiliroside.

While the current study was qualitative and relied on relative signal intensities rather than absolute quantification, these findings should be interpreted as compositional trends rather than definitive quantitative differences. Future studies should include quantitative analysis, controlled drying experiments, targeted lipid profiling with derivatization and authentic standards, and biological activity testing to better evaluate the potential use of acorns as sustainable natural resources.
