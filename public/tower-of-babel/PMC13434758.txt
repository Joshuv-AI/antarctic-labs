A continental-scale Eurasian ice sheet 2.4 million years ago
Authors: Kaleb Wagner, Lotta Ylä-Mella, Martin Margold, Mads F Knudsen, Aske L Sørensen, Nicolaj K Larsen, Robert S Anderson, Birte L Eriksen, Jesper Olsen, Jane L Andersen, Jiří Sláma, John D Jansen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnderstanding when ice sheets first developed over the continents is essential for attributing ice volume to marine records of global sea-level change. We resolve a long-standing terrestrial–marine mismatch in the timing of the first Eurasian Ice Sheet (EIS) expansion by dating the earliest glaciogenic unit in northwest Europe, the Hattem Bed Complex (HBC). Paired cosmogenic 26Al-10Be burial dating and detrital zircon fingerprinting indicate that glacially-derived Fennoscandian gravels reached the Netherlands 2.41−0.29+0.51 Ma, while ages from the overlying Appelscha Formation record a second EIS advance and attendant shutdown of the Baltic River System 1.72−0.25+0.25 Ma. Cyclic stratification of the HBC could reflect freshwater discharge to the North Atlantic associated with episodic disruption of meridional overturning circulation. The timing of the first trans-Baltic EIS overlaps the Laurentide Ice Sheet maximum (2.42−0.14+0.14Ma), underscoring substantial Northern Hemisphere ice growth more than a million years prior to the Mid-Pleistocene Transition.Subject terms: Cryospheric science, Cryospheric science, Palaeoclimate

Introduction

The intensification of Northern Hemisphere glaciation (iNHG) at the turn of the Pliocene is marked by the strengthening of glacial stages and outflux of ice-rafted debris (IRD) to the high-latitude seas1. By the earliest Pleistocene (~2.4 Ma), a proto-Laurentide Ice Sheet (LIS) had attained its maximum extent across mid-continental North America2—in contrast to northwest Europe, where fragmented onshore archives3 imply proto-Eurasian Ice Sheets (EIS) did not transit the Baltic Sea Basin until the Mid-Pleistocene Transition (~1.2–0.7 Ma), when glacial–interglacial cycles lengthened from ~41 to ~100 kyr4. And yet, offshore reflection seismic records on the mid-Norwegian margin point to a marine-terminating EIS, with glaciogenic progradation aligned to a prominent IRD spike at ~2.75 Ma5. This offshore–onshore mismatch is likely to be rooted in some combination of stratigraphic miscorrelation and the challenges of dating sediments >1 Ma.

Globally distributed ice-sheet chronologies are required to test whether threshold reductions6,7 in atmospheric CO2 drove cryosphere expansion at the Pleistocene onset. Transient sea-levels provide an incisive constraint, as prominent lowstands in recent reconstructions8,9 indicate substantial continental ice volumes during glacial stages of the Early Pleistocene. Ice sheets of this magnitude would have delivered coordinated freshwater fluxes to the North Atlantic, with important consequences for the stability of the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC) and deep-ocean carbon storage on orbital–millennial timescales. However, without reliable constraints on the terrestrial footprints of continental ice sheets during the iNHG, estimates of global ice volume and meltwater runoff inferred from deep-sea records remain equivocal.

Meltwater and sediment from the EIS were routed to the North Atlantic from its marine-terminating western margin and also via the North Sea Basin, where fragmented (glacio)fluvial archives intersect with more continuous records of shallow marine sedimentation (Fig. 1C)10,11. During the iNHG, the central and southern North Sea sub-basins formed major depocentres for the extensive Baltic (Eridanos) River System (BRS; Fig. 2A), which drained a ~1.1 million km2 catchment spanning the Baltic Platform and eastern Fennoscandian Shield12. The BRS delta front prograded westward across the North European Plain during the Middle Miocene to Late Pliocene, and from the Early Pleistocene the system progressively captured southern inputs from the Elbe, Weser, and Rhine–Meuse rivers (Figs. 1C, 2A–B)13. Sedimentation rates increased markedly across the iNHG; aggradation outpaced basin subsidence, yielding fluvial–deltaic successions in the northern Netherlands up to 200 m thick that prograde basinward into >1 km thick clinothem sets in the southern North Sea14.

The sedimentary record of the BRS is characterised by a distinctive gravel petrography known as the Baltic Gravel Assemblage (Fig. 2A, B)15. In the Netherlands, gravelly sands of this makeup are assigned to the fluvial–deltaic Peize Formation (Fm), including the Hattem Bed Complex (HBC) at its top, and overlain by braidplain sands of the Appelscha Fm16. The Hattem Beds comprise metres-thick bouldery to cobbly gravels rich in Fennoscandian erratics and are widely considered the earliest terrestrial evidence of glaciation in the North Sea Basin, marking the first trans-Baltic advance of the EIS17,18. Disproportionate weathering of these erratics (relative to admixed central European lithologies) reflects deep saprolitic alteration at their source, while preserved weathering rinds, clay coatings, and angular clay intraclasts indicate negligible fluvial abrasion owing to their entrainment under frozen conditions (e.g., via ice rafting)3,17,18. In contrast, the overlying Appelscha Fm retains a Fennoscandian component but is dominated by southern-sourced lithologies from the lower Rhine and Central European Uplands. This compositional shift brackets the Baltic Gravel Assemblage, signalling a waning BRS that was terminated upon the EIS first overriding its catchment (Fig. 2C, D)3,12. Within the Appelscha Fm, Fennoscandian lithologies are confined mostly to the capping Weerdinge Member (Mbr)—interpreted as the earliest sensu stricto glaciofluvial unit in the Netherlands. Deposition of the Weerdinge Mbr required a southern EIS margin extending into northern Germany at least (Fig. 2C, D)14, whereas the underlying HBC—representing transient injections of glacial materials into the BRS—registers an EIS margin beyond the Scandinavian Peninsula (Fig. 2B).

The HBC has not been dated directly; its putative Menapian age (~1.1 Ma)16,19 rests on a set of incompletely tested assumptions. Throughout the southern Netherlands, the Peize Fm is topped by the Rhine-sourced Sterksel Fm16,19, which is tied magnetostratigraphically at its base to the Jaramillo Subchron (1.07–0.99 Ma)16, thereby imparting a minimum-limiting ~1.1 Ma age for the HBC. The association of the HBC with the first trans-Baltic EIS is based on this (minimum) age matching that of the Fedje till, which is dated independently to ~1.1 Ma via 87Sr/86Sr and amino acid racemization chronologies20. The Fedje till floors the Norwegian Channel off southwest Norway, and direct dating of this deposit underpins arguments for Early Pleistocene onset of the Norwegian Channel Ice Stream (Fig. 1C)20. Establishment of the latter implies the EIS had attained sufficient thickness and spatial extent to sustain a fast-flowing, marine-terminating outlet along the Norwegian margin. However, recent dating and seismic-stratigraphic investigations question its chronology21–23 and motivate divergent (~1.8 Ma24 versus ~1.1 Ma25) views on the timing of the first grounded ice in the central North Sea Basin.

Determining the age of the HBC is key to clarifying whether large Northern Hemisphere ice sheets were initiated in response to uniform (global, hemispheric) or asymmetric (sub-hemispheric) forcing. While the iNHG marks a shift toward stronger orbital pacing1,26, interpretations based predominantly on globally integrated proxies can obscure the influence of regional processes (e.g., tectonic and ocean-atmospheric circulation changes) that may have also shaped Early Pleistocene ice-sheet growth. Moreover, cyclic stratification within the HBC suggests recurrent fluvial responses to a fluctuating ice margin and potentially preserves onshore evidence of episodic EIS meltwater discharges to the North Atlantic. The latter may provide a mechanistic link to coupled orbital- and millennial-scale climate variability recorded in Early Pleistocene marine archives27,28.

Here, we evaluate the chronology of the first trans-Baltic advance of the EIS by applying cosmogenic 26Al-10Be burial-dating to the HBC. Using samples recovered from the type-section18 drill core (B27E0116) at the base of the Molenweg quarry (52.4386°N, 6.0497°E) in the northeastern Netherlands (Fig. 2, 3C), we confirm a distinct Fennoscandian origin for the HBC via U-Pb analysis of detrital zircons, and we construct a 26Al-10Be burial age-depth model29 that attests to an earliest Pleistocene trans-Baltic EIS, predating other estimates by more than a million years (Myr). Viewed as an indicator of meltwater discharge to the North Atlantic, the HBC may coincide with periods of suppressed AMOC, marking attendant reductions in poleward heat transfer that could have favoured enhanced CO2 storage within the oceanic abyss.

Results

26Al-10Be burial chronology

Our 26Al-10Be burial-dating model places deposition of the HBC (HB3–HB1) between 2.93 and 2.10 Ma, spanning the 68% confidence intervals (CI) of samples 22-04 and 22-02 (Fig. 3D, F). The three individual HBC layers are dated to 2.42−0.29+0.51 Ma (HB3), 2.41−0.29+0.51 (HB2), and 2.39−0.29+0.51 (HB1) (Fig. 3F), yielding a mean age of 2.41−0.29+0.51Ma inside the interval’s marginal uncertainty. After applying a prior mean and variance of 750 ± 330 m Myr–1, we find (within error) that HBC burial ages are largely insensitive to sedimentation rate, with most change accommodated by hiatus durations (Supplementary Fig. 6–7; Supplementary Table 8).

Sample 22-05 from the lower Peize Fm, underlying the HBC, yields 3.17−0.37+0.38 Ma, and sample 22-01 from the overlying Appelscha Fm yields 1.80−0.40+0.34 Ma. Additional burial dates for the Appelscha Fm (Weerdinge Mbr) were measured from cores B17F0053 (near Weerdinge ~75 km northeast of B27E0116) and B06D0205 (near Tytsjerksteradiel ~85 km north of B27E0116; Fig. 2A), returning respective ages of 1.75−0.51+0.51 Ma and 1.63−0.42+0.42 Ma—all three overlapping within error and providing a mean age of 1.72−0.25+0.25 Ma. Unconformities (un3–un1) documented18 in the original B27E0116 core descriptions and observed in the wall exposures of the Molenweg quarry are also synthesised and incorporated within the age-depth profile (Fig. 3F). Probabilistic modelling of these hiatuses quantifies three erosional gaps: 0.59−0.38+0.58 Myr (un1) at the upper Peize Fm–Appelscha Fm contact; 0.61−0.41+0.49 Myr (un2) in the middle Peize Fm; and 0.29−0.21+0.36 Myr (un3) within the Balk Mbr of the Peize Fm (Fig. 3E, F). Omitting hiatuses—an approach not supported by the stratigraphy—yields models with narrower uncertainties but considerably poorer fits while returning a ~0.08 Ma younger mean age for the HBC (Supplementary Fig. 3; Supplementary Table 8). All uncertainties cited here are 68% CI, and all chronometric data are provided in Supplementary Notes, Supplementary Figs. 3–7, and Supplementary Table 8. Mean ages for the HBC and the Appelscha Fm are calculated as described in Supplementary Table 8.

U-Pb provenance analysis

We apply U-Pb geochronology to detrital zircons as a sediment fingerprinting tool to identify distinct proto-sources and signs of mixing during transport. Our analysis of zircon U-Pb ages from HB3 provides: (1) a robust comparison of sediment provenance between the fluvial sands of the uppermost Peize Fm hosting the HBC and previous interpretations of these units, which rely primarily on qualitative categorisation of macroscopic indicator lithologies, and; (2) an evaluation of reworking of the HBC sediments. Clarifying this potential reworking history is essential because the incorporation of older buried materials can distort modelled burial chronologies.

Zircons concentrated within the 63–150 μm grain-size fraction of the HB3 layer yield 120 concordant ages (Supplementary Fig. 9; Supplementary Data 1) with dominant Mesoproterozoic and Late Palaeoproterozoic modal peaks at ~1.6 and ~1.2 Ga, comprising ~29% and ~33% of the total distribution, respectively (Fig. 4A). These are separated by a minor ~1.4 Ga component, accounting for ~4% of grains. A mode at ~1.9 Ga represents ~11% of the distribution, while Archaean grains are rare and centred over a diffuse age component at ~2.8 Ga, totalling 6%. Palaeozoic and Late Neoproterozoic populations form shallow peaks at 440 and 640 Ma, together constituting ~17% of the measured dates (Fig. 4A).

Discussion

The first trans-Baltic advance of the Eurasian ice sheet

Our 2.41−0.29+0.51Ma burial age for the HBC establishes the earliest terrestrial evidence of an extensive EIS beyond the Baltic Basin. This timing accords with offshore evidence for a large EIS terminating in shallow waters of the southern North Sea Basin where multiple generations of iceberg scours atop 3D seismic horizons are assigned ages between 2.53 and 1.78 Ma (Figs. 2B, 5G)24. To the north, around the same time (~2.4 Ma), offshore reflection seismic evidence indicates the first shelf-edge glaciation in the Barents Sea, driven by coalescence of the Barents–Kara and Fennoscandian sectors of the EIS (Fig. 1A)5,33.

The revised ages we propose for the HBC and the overlying Appelscha Fm represent a major departure from estimates based on pollen and magnetostratigraphic data that, since the 1960s, have provided the temporal benchmarks for the Late Pliocene–Early Pleistocene of western Europe. And yet, we note that recent reassessments of key type-localities have cast doubt on the robustness of chronostratigraphic boundaries defined using these long-held schemes10,34,35.

The reliability of our 26Al-10Be chronology rests primarily on the assumption that the analysed samples were exposed and buried only once, and that minimal mixing with stored older sediments has occurred. Several lines of evidence support this interpretation: (1) 26Al/10Be ratios in our five samples decline overall with increasing depth, consistent with an expected down-core increase in age (Fig. 3D, F); (2) our dating of the Appelscha Fm is also matched by results from two peripheral locations (Fig. 2A), yielding a concordant age apparently unaffected by spurious reworking; (3) below the Appelscha, three samples from the HBC (22-04 to 22-02; Fig. 3D) yield very similar 26Al-10Be concentrations, despite their separation by several metres of fluvial sand; (4) these concentrations are notably distinct from the underlying lower Peize Fm—the most plausible source of recycled grains, and lastly; (5) the combination of high 10Be concentrations (7.9–9.7 ×105 at g–1) paired with low 26Al/10Be ratios (2.5–2.8; Fig. 3D) in our samples is compatible with partial radioactive decay from prolonged sediment burial following surface exposure29,36. This contrasts with a signal produced by repeated brief transport events and long intermittent storage, which tends towards low 10Be abundances and elevated 26Al/10Be ratios37.

The distinct Fennoscandian basement signature revealed by the U-Pb age distribution of detrital zircons in the HB3 unit (Fig. 4A–B) provides additional confidence in our 26Al-10Be-based chronology. These provenance analyses are consistent with previous work based on clast indicator counts17,18, underscoring a common northern source for both sand-sized and coarser-grained clastic components of the HBC (Fig. 4B). Further, the Fennoscandian signature measured in HB3 aligns closely with the Miocene sand succession in Denmark, which contains zircons sourced exclusively from Fennoscandian proto-sources32. Phanerozoic and Late Neoproterozoic (<0.8 Ga) zircon counts in HB3 are slightly elevated compared to the Danish Miocene succession (Fig. 4C)32; however, we note that these relatively young components are still less pronounced in HB3 than in fluvial sediments of the present-day Glomma River in Norway (Fig. 4C). The HB3 zircon distribution is also distinctive when compared to the underlying Pliocene marine sands of the Oosterhout Fm (Supplementary Fig. 1C), which contains both a higher proportion of pre-late Neoproterozoic zircons and a Variscan/post-Variscan peak (<380 Ma) that is notably absent in HB3 (Fig. 4C). In sum, we detect no sign of older buried materials being incorporated into our HBC samples in such a way that would bias the 26Al-10Be chronology significantly.

The intense pre-depositional weathering observed in the Nordic gravels of units HB3–1 reflects chemical alteration of the Fennoscandian Shield when Baltica occupied low (~30°N) Mesozoic latitudes38,39; and moreover, tallies with the first Pleistocene ice sheets quarrying a deeply-weathered regolith enriched in ultra-stable minerals. Evidence of the progressive stripping of this regolith through time can be seen in the dilution of unstable components of the heavy mineral assemblage spanning HB3–1 in core B27E0116 (Fig. 3B)18. Widespread removal of a ‘slippery’ regolith substrate has been proposed to explain ice-sheet profile changes across the Mid-Pleistocene Transition4, potentially reconciling40 evidence for extensive Early Pleistocene ice sheets2,24 with the limited global ice volume traditionally inferred from δ18Obenthic records.

Evolution of the Baltic River System and Baltic Sea Basin

The HBC has long been associated with the collapse of the BRS based on the expectation of large-scale drainage disruption when the EIS first overrode the Baltic Basin3,15. While the coarse-grained, high-energy HBC gravels do mark the terminal remnants of the Baltic Gravel Assemblage, the first indication of BRS shutdown is indicated by east–west to south–north reorientation of delta/shelf-edge progradation in the southern North Sea, signalling a shift from Baltic- to Rhine–Meuse-sourced sediment supply12,41,42. Onshore in the Netherlands, this transition is recorded by deposition of the Appelscha Fm (Fig. 2C, D), which we register (within uncertainty) up to 1.45 Myr after the first trans-Baltic EIS. According to our age model, emplacement of the Appelscha postdates a 0.59−0.38+0.58 Myr erosional hiatus at the Peize–Appelscha contact (un1 in Fig. 3E–F)—consistent with the regional offshore unconformity truncating the Markhams Hole Fm43. Previously assigned to the terrestrial Cromerian Glacial C stage (~Marine Isotope Stage [MIS] 16; 0.6 Ma) based on loose correlations19, we provide three new numerical ages for the Appelscha Fm: 1.80−0.40+0.34 Ma (core B27E0116), 1.75−0.51+0.51 Ma (core B17F0053), and 1.63−0.42+0.42 (core B06D0205). The mean 1.72−0.25+0.25 Ma timing coincides with a major regression and enhanced progradation rates in the central North Sea at ~1.75 Ma, followed by inferred ‘switching off’ (~1.71 Ma) of the BRS delta, as indicated by seismic stratigraphic evidence42.

A second implication of the revised HBC chronology pertains to the evolution of the Baltic Sea Basin. We propose that glacial erosion of the upper BRS catchment began in the Early Gelasian (~2.4 Ma) and had obliterated the river system by the Early Calabrian (~1.6 Ma). This scenario prompts re-evaluation of the Hall and van Boeckl44 model, which advocates a Middle to Late Pleistocene overdeepening of the Baltic by attributing much younger (<0.9 Ma) ages to the volumes of glacial deposits across the North European Plain.

First grounded ice in the central North Sea Basin

An Early Calabrian age for the Appelscha Fm (1.72−0.25+0.25 Ma) is supported by evidence for a basin-wide ice advance into the North Sea, but the timing of grounded ice development within the central basin remains contentious5. Competing interpretations assign ages of ~1.8 Ma24 and ~1.1 Ma25 to the so-called ‘crenulate’ seismic reflector and associated landforms that mark this grounding event (Fig. 2C, D). The younger 1.1 Ma timing relies partly on 87Sr/86Sr dating of foraminifera recovered from interpreted subglacial sediments near the base of borehole LN–BH3/445 and adjacent 16-1/8 (Fig. 2A)22. However, the reliability of this approach is limited by the low gradient of the Plio-Pleistocene seawater 87Sr/86Sr curve, meaning even minor analytical and diagenetic uncertainties can translate into significant apparent age offsets46. These uncertainties are further exacerbated in the North Sea Basin, where contemporaneous freshwater influx likely depressed the local isotopic baseline47. The limitations of 87Sr/86Sr dating in this setting are highlighted by conflicting age estimates for the Fedje till (Norwegian Channel). Initially constrained to ~1.1 Ma20, subsequent recalibration yielded ~1.3–1.2 Ma23 followed by ages ranging between 1.04 and 0.85 Ma22, whereas a detailed investigation based on 3D seismic ties indicates a much younger ~0.35 Ma timing21. Clarifying the chronology of this important marker horizon is therefore long overdue.

If deposition of the Appelscha Fm, termination of the BRS, and development of the crenulate reflector record a single EIS advance, then our 26Al-10Be chronology supports the earlier ~1.8 Ma timing proposed24 for the onset of grounded ice in the central North Sea Basin. Critically, however, if advances from ~1.8 Ma also involved coalescence of the British–Irish and Fennoscandian sectors of the EIS, as suggested by Rea et al. (Fig. 2C)24, progressive infill of the southern North Sea Basin by ~1.6 Ma42, alongside grounded ice within the central sub-basin, would have redirected continental runoff to the Iberian margin via the Fleuve Manche palaeoriver, with potential implications for North Atlantic overturning and breaching of the Weld-Artois structural barrier48. A well-positioned record from the Bay of Biscay spanning the last 1.2 Myr (core MD01-2448; Fig. 1A) indicates that such rerouting first occurred ~450 ka (MIS 12)49. Nevertheless, sedimentation and erosion rates along the mid-Norwegian margin and within the North Sea suggest that episodic shelf-edge glaciations—and potential British–Irish–Fennoscandian sector coalescence—may predate 1.5 Ma50. Records further south along the continental slope demonstrate enhanced terrigenous inputs from MIS G6 (~2.7 Ma), with millennial-scale variability onset from MIS 100 (~2.5 Ma) (Fig. 6H)27. Provenance tracing of these inputs, and further examination of North Sea and Iberian margin records across the ~1.8–1.2 Ma interval will be crucial for unravelling northwest European palaeodrainage and ice sheet configurations during the Early Pleistocene.

Climate–ice sheet evolution during the iNHG

Our revised Early Pleistocene chronology for the first trans-Baltic advance of the EIS accords with multiple independent proxies, establishing a coherent link to the broader reorganisation of global climate during the iNHG. A transition1 at MIS G6 (~2.7 Ma) is marked by the first significant IRD concentrations in the Norwegian Sea and adjacent North Atlantic (Fig. 5J)51,52. Lipid biomarkers and geochemical tracers identify North America as the source of glaciogenic dust spikes in the central North Atlantic, capturing the onset of major LIS growth from this time (Fig. 5I)53. And yet, Pb isotopes exclude the LIS as a possible IRD contributor to this sector until MIS G2 (~2.6 Ma)54. Ice sheets supplying IRD to the central North Atlantic were thus confined to the circum-Nordic Sea during MIS G6–G2 (~2.7–2.6 Ma) (Fig. 5J)54, and possibly until MIS 100 (2.54–2.51 Ma) when the Gulf of St Lawrence first emerged as a candidate source55.

Thereafter, δ18Obenthic records56 and δ18O-forced simulations of Eurasian ice volume57 register the onset of higher-amplitude glacial cycles around the Plio-Pleistocene boundary, with pronounced peaks at MIS 100, 98 and 96 (2.54–2.43 Ma) (Fig. 5A, M). These maxima (Fig. 6A) may stem from a favourable combination of orbital parameters, involving subdued precession modulated by decreasing eccentricity and high-amplitude obliquity variations (Fig. 6B), which together curb high-latitude summer insolation (Fig. 6C)28,58. The earliest Pleistocene witnessed increasingly severe glacial climates, attested by a strengthened East Asian Winter Monsoon (Fig. 5K)59 and a sharp decline in High Arctic summer temperatures (Fig. 5B)60. In northwest Europe, this timing is traced by the transition from Reuverian to Praetiglian (Pliocene to Pleistocene) pollen stages and the turnover of dinoflagellate cyst assemblages in the southern North Sea Basin19,61. Existing atmospheric CO2 proxies (Fig. 5L)62 indicate minimal secular change across this boundary—and perhaps even throughout the past 3 Myr63—although they lack sufficient resolution to capture orbital periodicities observed in recent models64.

While continuous high-resolution records can boast of precise dating control, reconstructions of pre-Late Pleistocene ice sheets must contend with more fragmented and loosely constrained archives. In this respect, persistent uncertainties obscure whether Early Pleistocene ice sheets responded asynchronously or in unison to orbital- and suborbital-scale forcings. Nonetheless, available terrestrial evidence does provide a crucial anchor for these dynamics; 26Al-10Be burial-dating of the Atlanta Fm till in central Missouri (USA) constrains the southernmost extent (39°N) of the LIS to 2.42−0.14+0.14 Ma2 (Fig. 5D), placing the North American glacial maximum in the earliest Pleistocene. This advance coincides with the first of several seawater δ18O (δ18Osw) anomalies that record episodic meltwater routing to the Gulf of Mexico (Fig. 5F)65. Our 26Al-10Be chronology for the initial trans-Baltic advance of the EIS (2.41−0.29+0.51Ma) overlaps the LIS maximum, suggesting that large ice sheets may have been present simultaneously over North America and Europe during the iNHG, between 2.56 (MIS 102) and 2.28 Ma (MIS 88). A more conservative framing, gained by differencing uncertainty ranges for the HBC and Atlanta Fm chronologies, allows a maximum potential lag of ~0.44–0.64 Myr between the first recorded continental-scale LIS and EIS. This interval spans 16 (or 11) glacial cycles between MIS G16–88 (2.92–2.28 Ma; if the EIS predates the LIS) or MIS 102–80 (2.56–2.12 Ma; if the EIS postdates the LIS) and procures a relatively narrow window for future coupled climate–ice-sheet models to resolve.

Quasi-synchronous expansion of large Northern Hemisphere ice sheets would induce pronounced glacio-eustatic lowstands, yet such signals are not captured in most δ18Obenthic-based reconstructions (Fig. 7B)66,67. This discrepancy also extends to a backstripped shallow-marine record derived from grain-size palaeobathymetry that reproduces strong 41-kyr orbital pacing but only muted glacial–interglacial sea-level amplitudes across the iNHG (Fig. 7A)68,69. On the other hand, state-of-the-art reconstructions that correct for temperature9,70 and δ18O mass balance8 infer deep lowstands during iNHG glacials, comparable in magnitude to the Last Glacial Maximum (LGM; e.g., –130 m) (Fig. 7B). Yet these estimates are complicated by noble gas ‘snapshots’ from Antarctic ice71, which challenge the sea-surface-to-mean-ocean temperature scaling used by Clark et al.72 to deconvolve δ18Obenthic8.

Despite this uncertainty, such LGM-like lowstands suggest that extensive Early Pleistocene ice sheets could be accommodated without invoking the ‘low-slung’ profiles allied to the regolith hypothesis4,40, even if widespread regolith removal from continental interiors preceded the Mid-Pleistocene Transition, as indicated by credible geochemical evidence73–75. Support for this view comes from a geomorphic cast of the LIS forebulge preserved in the buried upper Mississippi River valley, with its incision during Early Pleistocene drainage integration implying marginal ice thicknesses of ~1 km and a broadly similar ice-sheet geometry to that of the LGM76.

Did the EIS drive millennial-scale climate variability in the earliest Pleistocene?

Changes in ocean circulation are central to interpretations of the iNHG1, yet direct links to ice-sheet freshwater forcing remain elusive. Meltwater discharge to the North Atlantic is thought to have paced millennial-scale Heinrich and Dansgaard–Oeschger (D–O) climate oscillations during the Late Pleistocene77,78, but whether analogous scenarios arose during the iNHG—when ice sheets were of commensurate size8—remains unresolved. Because D–O variability occurred also during relatively moderate glacial and interglacial states79,80, rapid climate variability likely depended not only on global ice volume but also on factors such as the routing of freshwater to North Atlantic deepwater-formation regions. Consistent with this view, combined 231Pa/230Th, δ13C, and εNd records link Heinrich stadials with reduced AMOC vigour77,78, implying sensitivity to direct LIS meltwater input to the Labrador Sea. Although Heinrich events are commonly attributed to LIS discharge through the Hudson Strait81, growing evidence suggests that EIS instability may have acted as an important precursor or contributor82–87. Given its proximity to deepwater-formation regions in the Irminger and Iceland basins (Fig. 1B), the EIS may have therefore exerted disproportionate influence on ocean circulation (relative to its size) via freshwater delivery to the North Atlantic28; an argument supported by modelling88 and proxy-based evidence89 for AMOC suppression during intervals of rapid EIS decay.

Early Pleistocene records show pronounced millennial-scale variability in North Atlantic sediment geochemistry27 and IRD40,90–94 above a coeval ~3.7–3.9‰ δ18Obenthic threshold27,40—a condition met during nearly all glacial cycles of the iNHG (Fig. 6A), and up to 0.45‰ lower than a similar threshold proposed for the last five glacial cycles95. Notably, diagnostic Hudson Strait carbonate signatures do not appear in these IRD records until MIS 16 (~0.6 Ma)93, implicating the EIS and/or Greenland Ice Sheet as the primary sources of catastrophic iceberg discharge before the Mid-Pleistocene Transition. Although this threshold implies a link between ice volume and millennial-scale ice-sheet instability during the iNHG, the broader climatic significance of these events is unclear. Nonetheless, suborbitally resolved SST records53,55 do indicate ‘D–O-like’ variability on comparable timescales, including at the Gelasian Global Boundary Stratotype Section and Point (GSSP) in the central Mediterranean96. There, an alkenone-based reconstruction documents abrupt ~7 °C cooling at the onset of MIS 100 (2.53 Ma), followed by a ~22 kyr cold interval that is punctuated by six suborbital oscillations (Figs. 6G, 8A)97.

Water-mass tracers provide additional evidence for major and potentially rapid reorganisations of ocean circulation during the iNHG; however, the interpretation of these data has proved challenging. Radiogenic excursions in εNd at IODP Site U1313 in the central North Atlantic (Fig. 1A) are argued to reflect incursions of southern-component water that signify periods of AMOC suppression (Fig. 6F)98. Although the assumption of quasi-conservative εNd behaviour in this region has been questioned99, Kirby et al.100 use a multiproxy framework to demonstrate that water-mass properties at Site U1313 during MIS 100 (2.54–2.51 Ma) closely resembled those of the Last Glacial, maintaining the view98 that cold stages of the Early Pleistocene were accompanied by a weakened AMOC. These findings are replicated by an Atlantic–Pacific bottom-water temperature differencing method9 that circumvents the end-member uncertainties of isotopic water-mass tracers (Fig. 6F). The latter approach is unique in resolving pronounced, high-magnitude suborbital variability in water-mass exchange on timescales comparable to co-located ‘Heinrich-like’ IRD events during severe glacials (e.g., MIS 100, 98, and 96; 2.54–2.51 Ma; Fig. 8A). Poleward expansion of cool, nutrient-rich waters during these reorganisations likely enhanced deep-ocean carbon storage capacity, and induced positive feedback on Northern Hemisphere ice-sheet growth98,100.

While the origin of the HBC remains uncertain, we interpret these deposits as products of high-magnitude EIS meltwater discharge to the North Atlantic. The emplacement of metres-thick beds of ice-rafted bouldery gravels onto a low-gradient fluvial plain is most compatible with episodic glacial outburst flooding (e.g., owing to the overtopping and incision of ice dams and ice-cored moraines)101. Although the broad uncertainties of our HBC chronology preclude precise resolution of this flood-pulse tempo, the age model (Fig. 3F) cannot exclude the possibility that the three gravel units fall within a single ~41 kyr glacial–interglacial cycle. The correspondence between this inferred spacing and pronounced suborbital variability in marine records of IRD93, geochemistry27, and SST97 leaves us with the tantalising prospect that episodic meltwater pulsing from the EIS was a key factor driving rapid climate oscillations during the iNHG.

Methods

Sampling

B27E0116 was drilled to a depth of 115.5 m in 1969, intersecting a series of fine- to very-coarse-grained gravelly sands and clays assigned to Formation rank in Zandstra18. Archived materials were collected from the Core Store of the Geological Survey of the Netherlands, including three 1-m intervals (48.5–49.5 m, 56.5–57.5 m, 63.5–64.5 m) within the upper Peize Fm corresponding to the discrete HBC layers identified in Zandstra18. Bracketing samples were also obtained from over- and underlying stratigraphic units that we identify, respectively, as the base Appelscha (36.5–37.5 m) and lower Peize Fms (80.5–81.5 m; Fig. 3). Additionally, two samples of the Applescha Fm were retrieved each from cores B17F0053 (49.5–51.7 m depth) and B06D0205 (70.2–73.7 m depth). Lithostratigraphic reinterpretations are introduced here and draw upon the updated scheme for the Netherlands16 in accordance with our new dating (Supplementary Fig. 2).

26Al-10Be analysis

Sediments were dry sieved to isolate the 250–500 μm grain-size fraction for quartz concentration. Samples were processed to determine 26Al and 10Be abundances at the Aarhus University Cosmogenic Nuclide Laboratory following standard procedures for quartz purification and 26Al-10Be extraction (see Supplementary Notes). Measurement of nuclide concentrations was performed by an accelerator mass spectrometer (AMS) at Aarhus AMS Centre (AARAMS).

Burial age-depth modelling

Burial ages were estimated from 26Al and 10Be measured in quartz. During surface exposure, both nuclides are produced at a known ratio (26Al/10Be ≈ 7). Following burial, production is largely suppressed, and the ratio declines by differential radioactive decay at a rate dictated by each individual half-life (26Al = 0.705 Myr, 10Be = 1.387 Myr; Supplementary Table 7). Conventional methods for calculating burial ages exploit this decay clock (i.e., the ‘simple’ and isochron approaches102); however, they rely on assumptions that are ill-suited to glaciogenic sediments. Specifically, such methods presume: (1) steady bedrock erosion within the source area, and (2) continuous cosmic-ray exposure before burial36. In landscapes subject to repeated ice cover, where glacial plucking and landsliding are pervasive, these conditions are unlikely to hold. Moreover, ‘simple’ models require rapid, deep burial because they neglect post-burial nuclide production and, given that our samples do not derive from a single depth-stratigraphic horizon, the isochron method is not applicable.

We estimated burial ages for the lower, undisturbed 80 m of core B27E0116 using CosmoChron29, a probabilistic inverse age-depth framework that exploits vertical coupling among samples and enforces stratigraphic younging towards the top of the profile. A forward model simulates source–sink 26Al-10Be production and decay within a parameter space constrained by a prior containing a wide range of pre- and post-burial exposure histories. To accommodate complex, non-steady erosion and exposure, the prior is populated with a large, physically-based ensemble of uncorrelated pre-burial 26Al-10Be pairs generated by random sampling of the P-PINI source model described in Nørgaard et al.36. P-PINI was also employed to calculate Appelscha Fm ages in cores B17F0053 and B06D0205.

The CosmoChron forward model allows manual seeding of hiatuses, which we inserted at three depths in the B27E0116 age-depth model (40.5 m, 71.5 m, 105.7 m; Fig. 3F), corresponding to erosional unconformities identified in the original core and Molenweg quarry-wall descriptions18. Hiatus durations are treated as explicit time-gap parameters and are jointly sampled alongside accumulation rates, top age, and pre-burial nuclide ratios in a Markov Chain Monte-Carlo (MCMC) Bayesian inversion to generate posterior ensembles. Each hiatus is represented as a period of zero accumulation, segmenting the profile such that burial age at any depth is the sum of depositional ages plus the durations of any overlying hiatuses. Estimated hiatus durations (Fig. 3E) are represented by their posterior distributions and, together with the posterior distributions of sedimentation rates, define the overall down-core age-depth relationships. Uncertainties on hiatus durations are controlled by their length and the density and precision of bracketing chronological information.

The prior mean and variance of sink accumulation rate in the B27E0116 age-depth model are set to 750 ± 330 m Myr–1. This constraint is based on sedimentation rates observed across the Early Pleistocene marine–paralic transition in southern North Sea Basin cores A15-361 and B30F047011 (Fig. 2A), together with basin-wide averaged estimates from Lamb et al.103, Ottesen et al.41, and Kuhlmann et al.61. While acknowledging that coeval pro-delta BRS sedimentation rates derived from central parts of the basin are not strictly comparable to B27E0116, such continuous records are the most realistic available approximations. Complete modelling details are provided in Supplementary Notes and Supplementary Tables 5–7.

Zircon U-Pb geochronology

The lowermost HBC sample (HB3; 22-04; Fig. 3D) was dry sieved to isolate the 63–150 μm fraction for zircon selection. Zircons were randomly hand-picked following heavy mineral concentration and analysed (n = 126) by laser ablation inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (LA ICP-MS) at the Institute of Geology, Czech Academy of Science. A Thermo Scientific Element 2 sector field ICP-MS coupled to a 193 nm ArF excimer laser (Teledyne Cetac Analyte Excite laser) was used to measure Pb/U and Pb isotopic ratios from grain rims identified on cathodoluminescence images (Supplementary Fig. 8). The laser was fired at a repetition rate of 5 Hz and a fluence of 3.6 J cm–2 with 28 μm spot size, depending on grain size. Typical acquisitions consisted of 15 s of blank measurement followed by measurement of U, Th and Pb signals from the ablated zircon for an additional 35 s. Full details of sample preparation for zircon U-Pb dating are included in Supplementary Notes. LA ICP-MS measurement data for sample 22-04 are provided in Supplementary Data 1.
