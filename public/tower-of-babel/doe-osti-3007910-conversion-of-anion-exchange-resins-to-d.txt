 ABSTRACT

While addressing the need to dispose used anion exchange resins in a nitrate form, the question of their
conversion to a citrate form as described in a procedure underwent some scrutiny. It was established that
the term “citrate” actually referred to the monodeprotonated form of citric acid at a pH of 3.6 in order to
effectively displace the nitrate ion and replace it with the dihydrogen citrate. A complete study of various
conditions to reach this conclusion is presented along with a comparison with the performance of sodium
salicylate as an alternative to citrate.

 vi

 1. INTRODUCTION

Anion exchange resins are polymeric beads functionalized with functional groups bearing a positive charge.
Commonly, quaternary ammonium groups are used for strong base anion exchange resins. The positive
charge is balanced by various anions, such as chloride or nitrate, and the relative retentions are generally
consistent with the anion free energy of partitioning from water into a solvent. For radioisotope production,
anion exchange resins are commonly used to separate and purify higher-valence actinides that can form
polychloro- or polynitrato- anionic complexes with different affinities. Gradient elution of loaded columns
can yield excellent separations, such as those encountered when using high concentrations of LiCl. Dowex-
1 resin has different affinities for americium/curium chloro- complexes and transcurium chloro- complexes.
This separation is at the heart of californium campaigns, affording the rejection of lanthanide fission
products and separation of the americium/curium product from transcurium elements. Similarly,
purification of plutonium or neptunium from fission products can be achieved via anion exchange of
plutonium(IV) or neptunium(IV) hexanitrato- complexes using MP-1 resin (macroporous version of
Dowex-1 resin). Because of purity requirements or radiation damages, the resin must be replaced on a
regular basis and eventually dispositioned. The presence of nitrate can be a limiting factor; thus, a procedure
was established several years ago to convert the resin from the nitrate form to a less-constraining citrate
form [1]. In the multiple revisions over the years, users of the procedure noticed that the distinction between
citric acid and sodium citrate as the agent used for the conversion was no longer present. Additionally,
while studying the effect of citric acid’s presence on extracting actinides, procedure users noted that the
subtle color change that was expected when switching from a nitrate form to a citrate form was not
occurring. Studies were undertaken to assess the validity of the procedure and refine the conditions, if
necessary.

 2. EXPERIMENTAL

2.1 REAGENTS AND MATERIALS

All anion exchange resin tests were performed using Bio-Rad MP-1 resin. This resin was originally received
in the chloride form. Resin was either used as-received after initially removing fines, or converted to a
nitrate form using a series of 4 M NaNO3 and 0.5 M HNO3 until a water flush did not exhibit more than 5
ppm of chloride. The citric acid, sodium citrate, and sodium salicylate (ACS grade) were received from
Sigma-Aldrich. They were dissolved using 18 M·cm deionized water and further pH-adjusted, if needed.
The NaOH was ACS-grade, obtained from EM Science, and used as received. All the columns were
prepared with either 1 or 2 mL of resin fully settled overnight.

2.2 INSTRUMENTATION

Nitrate and chloride analyses were carried out via ion chromatography using a Thermo Fisher Scientific
Dionex Integrion high-pressure ion chromatograph system with a Dionex AS-11HC anion analytical
column (250 mm length, 4 mm in diameter.). All dilutions were carried out in helium-sparged deionized
water (>18.3 MΩ·cm) at 500× dilutions to quantify nitrate and chloride within the instrument linear
calibration range, with alternate dilutions used for outlier results. The calibration range for all analytes was
typically 50 ppb to 25 ppm. Fisher Scientific ACS-grade KOH was used as the eluent. Acceptance criteria
for data were ±10% on all calibration verifications, <MDL (method detection limit) or <10× lowest sample
detection for blanks, spike recoveries of ±25% and 20% Relative Percent Difference (RPD) or samples
<10× MDL, and 10% RPD for samples >10× MDL.

All pH measurements were carried out using a bench meter Thermo Scientific Orion Dual-Star 2115001
Dual Star pH/ISE benchtop meter with a 8102BNUWP ROSS Ultra glass refillable pH electrode.

 3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSIONS

3.1 CONVERSION USING 1 M CITRIC ACID

The first test, which led to this study, was carried out to confirm that anion exchange resin in the nitrate
form could be quantitatively converted to a citrate form using 3 BV (bed volumes) of 1 M citric acid. This
study was based on a procedure [1], but the studies supporting that procedure could not be found. Moreover,
based on previous experience when converting anion exchange resin from a chloride to a nitrate form, the
high nitrate concentration in contact with the resin in a chloride form led to a slight change in resin color.
When the resin in the nitrate form was put in contact with 1 M citric acid, the resin stayed the same color.
The solution from the elution was collected in 1/2 BV and analyzed for nitrate. After 6 mL of 1 M citric
acid (HCit bars in Figure 1), the column was rinsed with 2 mL (1 BV) of water (green bars), followed by
another 6 mL of citric acid adjusted to pH 12 with NaOH (shown as Cit3- bars in Figure 1). The results are
presented below.

 Figure 1. Nitrate elution on a 2 mL MP-1 resin column.

Based on these results, 3 BV of citric acid is not sufficient to quantitatively convert the resin from the nitrate
to the citrate form. Further investigation is needed to assess the best conditions for full conversion and
provide a solid basis for the conversion procedure.

3.3 CONVERSION USING 1 M SODIUM CITRATE

The previous tests were done using a solution of 1 M citric acid that was subsequently adjusted to pH 12
using NaOH. The addition of NaOH to the solution to adjust the pH of the solution reduced the total
concentration of citrate, which could reduce the effectiveness to displace the nitrate from the resin. Tests
were repeated using citric acid and NaOH before the volume was adjusted to ensure that the total citrate
concentration was precisely 1 M.

the citrate form should be more straightforward than with nitrate. A comparison can be made between the

3.5 CONVERSION USING CITRIC ACID IN DEPROTONATED FORMS

As mentioned in Section 3.2, the terms citric acid and sodium citrate are sometimes used interchangeably
for any of the form of this chemical, which can lead to confusion when the actual charge matters. Citric
acid is a triacid (H3Cit), and its pKa values are 3.13, 4.76, and 6.40 (Figure 7). The speciation diagram
corresponding to these pKa values is presented in Figure 8.

 Figure 7. pKa values of citric acid.

 pH

 Figure 8. Speciation diagram of citric acid as a function of pH.

In this study, tests were carried out with either citric acid (H3Cit) or fully deprotonated sodium citrate
because the pH was set at 12. The mono- and bis-protonated forms can only be controlled through pH
adjustment, and this aspect was not mentioned in the original procedure. Different citrate solutions at three
different pH values were tested.

To maintain the total citrate concentration at 1 M, solutions of 1 M citric acid and 1 M sodium citrate (triply
deprotonated) were mixed to obtain the desired pH. The pH was subsequently measured using a pH meter.
Alternatively, a more concentrated citric acid solution was adjusted with NaOH and brought to the final
volume in a volumetric flask with water.

3.6 PREVIOUS HOT CELL CONVERSIONS

Based on older records, resin conversion was done with resin present in the hot cell in 2015. That conversion
happened with 3 and 6 L batches of IONAC A580 resin. In both cases, the conversion of the resin was
monitored by taking samples after each bed volume of citric acid and measuring the concentration of nitrate.
The only difference between the two batches was that for the first one, the conversion was done with 0.5
M citric acid dissolved in water, but the second was done with a 0.5 M citric acid solution adjusted to pH
4.

The theoretical capacity of the IONAC resin reported by the manufacturer is 1.4 meq/mL, which
corresponds to 260.5 g of nitrate for the first batch and 521 g for the second one. Figures 12 and 13 show
the nitrate displacement profiles by bed volume. The bed volumes obtained by flowing citric acid are shown
in blue while those in which water was used are shown in green.

 Figure 12. Nitrate-to-citrate conversion of 3 L of IONAC resin using 0.5 M citric acid and water.

 Figure 13. Nitrate-to-citrate conversion of 6 L of IONAC resin using 0.5 M citric acid at pH 4.

Based on the nitrate concentrations measured in each bed volume of citric acid solution, the total amount
of nitrate displaced for the first batch was only 46.9 g, or 18%, but the second batch had a total displaced

These results confirm the necessity to operate the nitrate-to-citrate conversion using a sodium citrate
solution adjusted to a pH corresponding to a domain in which the main species is the monodeprotonated
citrate, H2Cit−.

3.7 CONVERSION USING 1 M SODIUM SALICYLATE

The use of sodium salicylate to convert anion exchange resin from the nitrate form to the salicylate form
has been demonstrated in the past [2]. Salicylic acid is a diproticacid (pKa 3.0 and 13.6); thus, the issue
with producing dianionic species with a greater charge density when neutralizing salicylic acid is alleviated
due to low dissociation of the phenolic proton at acidic pHs. The speciation diagram for salicylic acid us
presented in Figure 14. In terms of anion selectivity normalized to 1 for hydroxide, the selectivity of Dowex-
1 (MP-1) resin for citrate is 220 while the selectivity of salicylate is 450 [3].

3.7.1 Conversion from Chloride to Salicylate

The purpose of converting the anion exchange resin from the chloride form to the salicylate form is to have
a good determination of the capacity of the resin. The affinity of MP-1 resin for salicylate is orders of
magnitude greater than that for chloride, which should provide a complete conversion and release the
chloride to the mobile phase.

Table 9 presents the parts per million of chloride in the fractions and the cumulative percentages of chloride

 4. SUMMARY

The results presented in this report give clear evidence that a large charge-diffuse anion is the optimum to
convert anion exchange resins from the nitrate form to the desired anionic form. Although salicylate is a
monoacid and is present in that form at any pH, such is not the case for citric acid. As the pH increases,
citric acid can lose up to three protons, producing anions that are decreasingly charge-diffuse. The graph
presented in Figure 17 summarizes the results obtained for the nitrate conversion with either salicylate or
citrate at various pH values.

  Figure 17. Summary of the nitrate conversions of 1 mL MP-1 resin using 1 M of citrate or 1 M salicylate.

The only pH value in this study providing a quantitative conversion after a reasonable number of bed
volumes was the citrate solution at pH 3.6, which is in line with the results obtained in the hot cell. This
solution can ensure that the resin is converted quantitatively to a citrate form. The recommendation is to
use 5 BV.

 5. CONCLUSIONS

This systematic study stemmed from the need to gain a deeper understanding of the anion exchange
mechanisms when aiming for a nitrate-to-citrate conversion. With citric acid being a triacid, the pH has a

 18

direct effect on the anion present in solution. This study showed that only the monodeprotonated citrate
anion can displace nitrate quantitatively in a reasonable number of bed volumes. Two actual conversions
done years ago in the hot cell appear to support this conclusion. Salicylate was also tested as a comparison
and is also a very good candidate for such displacement. The results presented in this report provide a solid
basis when choosing a conversion agent, and the performance can be considered with other factors, such as
ease of downstream disposal or complexation competition with actinides. Notably, using citrate or salicylate
in the Radioisotope Engineering Development Center Building 7920 hot cell will require further studies on
the potential effect of these anions on the CLEANEX process used to remove transuranic material from
waste solutions.
