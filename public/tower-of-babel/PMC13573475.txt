Acute kidney injury following the use of chemical hair straightener: a case report
Authors: Mohammed Sadeq AlKhalaf, Abdullah Mohammed AlKhudair, Eliane Moussa El Tawil
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcute kidney injury (AKI) is defined by an increase in the serum creatinine according to the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes criteria. Known causes of AKI include nephrotoxic medications, dehydration, and obstructive uropathy, among others. Chemical hair products are commonly used to enhance hair appearance and health but often contain formaldehyde and its derivatives, which may pose significant health risks. Exposure to formaldehyde can occur through scalp absorption or heat-induced transformation. In this article, we describe a noteworthy case in which a widely used hair-straightening product, typically perceived as safe for everyday use, was associated with the development of Acute Kidney Injury (AKI).Case presentationA previously healthy 39-year-old woman presented with vomiting, diarrhea, and back pain after using chemical hair products. She was diagnosed with Acute Kidney Injury (AKI), as she had an elevated creatinine level of 624 micromoles/L and urinalysis showing pyuria. Kidney biopsy indicated interstitial eosinophilic infiltration and acute tubular injury with oxalate crystals. After receiving steroids, her kidney function improved gradually, and she achieved complete recovery without the need for renal replacement therapy.ConclusionThis study highlights that acute kidney injury from hair straightening can fully resolve if detected and treated promptly. It emphasizes the need for the medical community to advocate for reduced use of chemical hair products due to their nephrotoxic potential. The findings suggest that the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) should assess these products before they enter the market to evaluate the concentrations of harmful chemicals that may cause kidney damage or systemic toxicity upon use. This case report calls for increased awareness and regulatory action to safeguard public health.

Background

Chemical hair products are widely used worldwide to improve hair appearance and strength1. Many of these products contain formaldehyde or formaldehyde-releasing compounds, which are associated with respiratory, neurological, and potential carcinogenic effects [1–3]. Formaldehyde derivatives include Methanal, Methyl Aldehyde, Methylene Oxide, Formalin, Oxymethylene, Formic Aldehyde, Methylene Glycol, Paraformaldehyde, and Oxymethane [1]. These compounds are present in various household and cosmetic products at concentrations ranging from 0.5% to 10% [4, 5]. Formaldehyde-based compounds can be metabolized into oxalate, leading to calcium oxalate nephropathy and acute kidney injury (AKI) [4, 6, 7].

The risk of AKI following the use of hair-straightening products is increased in individuals with comorbidities such as diabetes mellitus, hypertension, or pre-existing kidney disease [5]. Renal injury may occur shortly after exposure through scalp absorption or heat-induced chemical transformation, even with products labeled as formaldehyde free.1 This case adds to the growing scientific literature on cosmetic product-associated nephrotoxicity and highlights the importance of clinician awareness, stricter safety regulations, and further research into the renal effects of formaldehyde-containing hair treatments.

Case presentation

A 39-year-old woman who had previously been in good health and had no known medical conditions, presented to the Emergency Department on February 25, 2025 at night, with complaints of vomiting and diarrhea, occurring seven times within a single day, accompanied by back pain. Vitals were as follows: temperature 36.6 °C, pulse 98 beats/min, blood pressure 138/89 mmHg, and respiratory rate of 18 breath/min. She weighed 64 kg and her height was 144 cm. Physical exam was unremarkable, no lower limb edema, stigmata of kidney or liver disease, or autoimmune diseases. Upon auscultation, she had a vesicular breathing with no added sounds, and S1S2 Cardiac sounds with no murmurs.

Basic metabolic panel revealed a creatinine level of 624 micromoles/L, indicating Acute Kidney Injury (AKI) stage 3, as classified by the kidney disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) criteria, with a urine output of 750 ml per day. Notably, she had used chemical hair-straightening products just three days prior to her presentation. Discussion

This case study illustrates AKI with Acute Interstitial Nephritis (AIN) following the use of chemical hair straightening; a kidney biopsy revealed interstitial eosinophilic infiltration and edema, along with the presence of scattered oxalate crystals, suggesting systemic Glycolic toxicity caused by the hair-straightening product. Multiple studies report AKI after hair-straightening treatments and attribute kidney injury to Glycolic acid derivatives, such as Glyoxylic acid, which readily converts to oxalate, leading to calcium oxalate nephropathy via Lactate Dehydrogenase (LDH) [3, 6, 8]. Majority of cases present with skin irritation, back pain, and gastrointestinal symptoms [1, 6, 9]. The majority of kidney biopsies reveal calcium oxalate nephropathy.

In our case, as well as in other studies, Acute Kidney Injury (AKI) has been observed following exposure to chemical hair products, regardless of whether they contain formaldehyde or are labeled as formaldehyde free, while other potential causes of AKI have been excluded. The tubules are primarily affected, leading to AIN. A kidney biopsy confirms the diagnosis by revealing oxalate crystal deposits, indicating oxalate nephropathy [7, 8, 10]. Some cases were managed with a short course of steroids only, similar to our case [9], or conservatively [8], while another case required dialysis [2].

Glyoxylic acid is more likely to cause kidney injury than Glycolic acid because it penetrates the skin more easily and converts to oxalate via Lactate Dehydrogenase (LDH) present in various organs [3, 5, 8]. In contrast, glycolate needs to be metabolized first in the liver by the enzyme glycolate Oxidase [4, 8]. The risk of acute kidney injury (AKI) increases with glycolate when the pH is low, the concentration of the product is high, and the duration of exposure is prolonged [3].

Limitations

This case report has several limitations. A notable limitation of this case report is that the exact chemical composition of the hair-straightening product could not be determined. The exposure occurred in a hair salon where multiple similar products were available and routinely used, making it impossible to identify the specific product applied at the time of exposure. An additional limitation was the restricted availability of follow-up investigations due to insurance-related constraints. Repeat laboratory testing, including spot urine analysis, complete blood count (CBC), and liver function tests, could not be consistently obtained throughout the treatment and recovery period. Consequently, monitoring of the patient’s clinical course and recovery was based primarily on serial serum creatinine and urea levels.

Conclusion

Acute kidney injury resulting from hair straightening has been reported to achieve complete recovery if identified early and treated appropriately. We recommend minimizing the use of chemical hair products due to their potential to cause kidney damage. It is advisable to avoid products containing high levels of formaldehyde derivatives, such as Glyoxylic acid. While no specific concentration limit has been established, multiple reported cases of toxicity have occurred when formaldehyde levels were close to 10%.
