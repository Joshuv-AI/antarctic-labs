Korean Soybean Nested Association Mapping (K-SoyNAM) population: genetic linkage map, genetic mapping, and genotype imputation
Authors: Ji-Min Kim, Jung-Kyung Moon, Soo-Kwon Park, Man-Soo Choi, Bo-Keun Ha, Sungwoo Lee, Kyung Do Kim, Soon-Chun Jeong, Ju Seok Lee, Sungtaeg Kang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe nested association mapping (NAM) population, first developed in maize, have been widely adopted across diverse crop species including barley, sorghum, rice, and soybean. These populations enable high-resolution mapping of complex traits and have been extensively utilized worldwide to dissect the genetic architecture of agronomically important traits such as yield, disease resistance, and seed composition.ResultsThe parents were selected from a Korean soybean collection widely used in the Korean soybean breeding program, along with a soybean reference genotype. The K-SoyNAM population was initially developed using 27 donor parents crossed with ‘Daepung’, generating 2,831 F5-derived recombinant inbred lines (RILs) from 27 families. After quality control filtering, 2,669 RILs from 24 families were retained for downstream analyses. The parental lines were deeply sequenced using whole genome reference sequencing, and the RILs were genotyped using the Axiom 180 K SoyaSNP array. Genetic linkage maps were constructed for each family, and a composite linkage map was developed across families. Linkage analysis of three agronomic traits, flowering time, flower color, and seed protein content, the utility of the K-SoyNAM population for quantitative trait locus (QTL) mapping. Genotype imputation based on a composite genetic linkage map generated 4,057,789 high-confidence single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) for the RILs. Comparison of genome-wide association study (GWAS) results before and after imputation demonstrated improved detection of small significant peaks in the imputed GWAS dataset.ConclusionsThe genotypic datasets for the RILs and parents are publicly available and are expected to be helpful in dissecting the genetic architecture and mapping QTLs controlling important complex traits in soybean.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-13030-0.

Background

Understanding the genetic basis of complex agronomic traits remains a central objective in crop improvement programs. Traits such as flowering time, seed composition, and yield are typically controlled by multiple loci and are strongly influenced by environmental conditions, making their genetic dissection challenging [1–4]. Traditionally, bi-parental linkage mapping has been widely used to identify quantitative trait loci (QTLs). Although this approach is effective for detecting loci segregating within a specific cross, its resolution is inherently limited by the small number of recombination events and the restricted allelic diversity of the parental lines [4, 5]. Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have emerged as a complementary strategy, offering higher mapping resolution by leveraging historical recombination events within diverse populations. However, GWAS often suffers from reduced statistical power in detecting rare alleles and can be confounded by population structure, which may lead to false-positive or false-negative associations [6].

To address these limitations, multi-parent populations integrating controlled recombination with broader allelic diversity have been developed. Among these, nested association mapping (NAM) designs enable simultaneous exploitation of linkage information within families and historical recombination across families, thereby improving both mapping power and resolution [5]. NAM populations are typically constructed by crossing multiple diverse founder lines with a common parent, followed by the development of recombinant inbred lines (RILs). This design enables the capture of broader allelic diversity while maintaining a structured population suitable for both linkage and association analyses [5, 7–9]. The maize US-NAM design has been used successfully to fine-map QTLs controlling several complex traits, including kernel composition, southern and northern leaf blight, leaf architecture, stalk strength, and height [9–14]. NAM populations have also been developed in other crop species, including barley (Hordeum vulgare L.) [15–18], sorghum [Sorghum bicolor (L.) Moench] [19–21], and rice (Oryza sativa L.) [22]. In soybean [Glycine max (L.) Merr.], the US-SoyNAM population have been successfully utilized to investigate the genetic architecture of important agronomic traits, including yield, seed composition, and plant architecture. In particular, the integration of high-density genotyping data and genotype imputation approaches has significantly enhanced mapping resolution and facilitated the identification of candidate genes underlying complex traits [23–30].

Genotype imputation has further expanded the utility of these populations by enabling the prediction of unobserved genotypes using reference datasets. By increasing marker density without additional genotyping costs, imputation enhances both the statistical power and resolution of genetic analyses [31]. In the US-SoyNAM population, imputation was successfully performed and confirmed to significantly reduce the interval of genomic regions controlling traits, thus greatly improving the efficiency of candidate gene identification [32].

Despite these advances, there remains a need to develop NAM populations that reflect locally adapted germplasm and breeding objectives. In Korea, soybean breeding programs have focused on diverse end-use types, including tofu, soy sprouts, and rice-cooking varieties. However, a comprehensive NAM population representing this diversity has not been fully characterized. In this study, we developed and characterized a Korean soybean NAM (K-SoyNAM) population to provide a valuable resource for genetic and genomic studies. Specifically, we aimed to (1) construct a high-resolution genetic linkage map, (2) evaluate the genetic architecture of the population, (3) assess the effectiveness of the population for QTL detection, and (4) investigate the impact of genotype imputation on the detection of causal loci through GWAS.

Method

Parent selection and population development

The K-SoyNAM population was constructed following the protocol of the original NAM design for maize [5, 33] (Fig. 1). The cultivar ‘Daepung’ was selected as the common parent due to its high adaptation to the environmental conditions in South Korea and its superior yield [34]. Twenty-seven donor parents were selected from the Korean soybean collection, which is commonly used in Korean soybean breeding programs [35] (Table S1, Figure S1). In the Korean soybean breeding program, soybean varieties are usually classified into three categories based on their use: tofu & soy sauce, soy sprouts, and cooking with rice. The diverse donor parents comprised 9 varieties for tofu & soy sauce, 6 for soy sprouts, 10 for rice cooking, and 2 for oil and soymeal purposes. In addition, the US soybean cultivar ‘Williams 82’ [36], which was used for the first soybean draft genome sequencing, was added to the K-SoyNAM parents (Table 1).

In 2012, each of the 27 donor parents was crossed with the hub parent, ‘Daepung’, in a greenhouse and experimental field at Dankook University (36°43′26.9″N 127°15′36.2″E) in Cheonan, Korea, resulting in approximately 500 F1 hybrid seeds. The F1 plants were self-pollinated to generate a total F2 populations consisting of approximately 5,000 individuals across all families. The F2 plants were subsequently advanced to the F5 generation by single-seed descent. All generations of self-pollination were grown in a greenhouse to minimize outcrossing and inadvertent selection. The last generation of greenhouse inbreeding was grown in the winter of 2014, and all F5 RILs were grown in an experimental field at Dankook University from the summer of 2015 to the winter of 2022 for seed multiplication and phenotypic data collection. The RILs were harvested from the experimental field, and approximately 200 g of seeds were stored as long-term seed stocks for future experiments at the National Agrobiodiversity Center in Jeonju, Korea (https://genebank.rda.go.kr/eng/uat/uia/actionMain.do) in 2022. In total, a population of 2,831 F5-derived RILs within 27 families was generated (Fig. 1).

Agronomic trait evaluation

From the K-SoyNAM population, two quantitative traits (flowering time and seed protein content) and one qualitative trait (flower color) were investigated and used for further genetic and genomic analysis. The K-SoyNAM population evaluated under a single-replication field design over two years at the experimental field at Dankook University (36°43′26.9″N 127°15′36.2″E) in Cheonan. Each line was planted in single 1.5 m row with a spacing of 70 cm between rows and 15 cm between plants within rows. Flowering time (reproductive stage 1) was recorded as the number of days from planting to the beginning of flowering, defined as the stage when the first flower opened in approximately 40–50% of the plants within a plot, during the 2015 and 2016 growing seasons. Flower color was visually scored as either white or purple during flowering over the same two years. To evaluate seed protein content, experimental plots were harvested after pod maturity and plant drying. Seed protein content was measured using near-infrared reflectance spectroscopy analysis [54] on a SpectraStar 2500XL Near-Infrared Analyzer (Unity Scientific, Brookfield, CT, US) and converted to a 13% moisture basis during the 2016 and 2017 growing seasons. The mean phenotypic values of flowering time and seed protein content obtained across years were used for subsequent analyses. Broad-sense heritability (H²) was estimated using variance components obtained from analysis of variance across years according to the following formula: H² = σ²G / (σ²G + σ²E / n), where σ²G is the genetic variance, σ²E is the environmental variance, and n is the number of years.

SNP genotyping and outlier identification

An Axiom 180 K SoyaSNP array (Affymetrix) genotyping platform was applied to the 2,831 F5-derived F6 RILs from each of the 27 families in the K-SoyNAM population, as well as to the parents [55]. Genomic DNA from each line and the parents was extracted from young trifoliate leaves using the cetyltrimethylammonium bromide method, and all DNA was quantified using an ND-1000 Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and diluted to 100 ng µL− 1 for further study. Genomic DNA from the lines and parents was hybridized into the Affymetrix GeneTitan array system and then scanned using a GeneTitan Scanner (Affymetrix) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. SNP genotyping analysis was performed based on the Axiom Genotyping Solution Data Analysis User Guide (http://www.affymetrix.com). Of the 180,961 SNPs, 170,223 were selected based on the development and validation study.

Outliers were identified using the 170,223 SNP data from the K-SoyNAM population through phylogenetic tree analysis using TASSLE5.0 software [56]. Genetic distances between each pair of RILs were calculated, and the Archaeopteryx tree was visualized using FigTree v1.4.4 (http://tree.bio.ed.ac.uk/software/figtree/) (Fig. 2A). If an RIL was not included in its corresponding family, the line was considered contaminated during the population development process (Fig. 2B, Table S2). After removing outliers and relocating some RILs to the appropriate families, SNPs with missing data points were imputed using BEAGLE 5.4 with default settings [31]. Of the 170,223 SNPs, 169,028 were selected after removing unanchored scaffolds and anchoring the chloroplast genome based on the reference soybean genome Glyma.Wm82.a2.v1 (available online at https://soybase.org/, accessed May 1, 2026) [57].

Genetic architecture of K-SoyNAM population

Before the genomic and genetic analyses, the genetic characteristics and population structure of the K-SoyNAM population were characterized using the genotype dataset. The 169,028 SNP genotype dataset was analyzed to detect polymorphic ratio and segregation distortion regions and to calculate the average fixation index using F-statistics (FST) [58] and principal component analysis (PCA). Polymorphic ratio and segregation distortion in the progeny were estimated by calculating allele frequencies for each SNP after converting alleles from the ATGC format to homozygous reference (2), homozygous alternative (0), and heterozygous (1) genotypes using customized R scripts. The FST value, which indicates the degree of population differentiation between the K-SoyNAM donor parents and their population, was estimated based on the genetic variation associated with SNP markers distributed among subpopulations using the R package ‘NAM’ version 1.7.3 [59]. PCA was performed to determine the genetic structure and variation among the subpopulations of the K-SoyNAM population. The additive relationship matrix was calculated using the ‘A.mat’ function within ‘rrBLUP’ version 4.6.3 [60]. The influence of each component was analyzed using the ‘prcomp’ function based on the relationship matrix. PCA results for PC1 and PC2 were visualized using the R package ‘ggplot2’ version 3.5.1 [61].

Out of 169,028 SNPs, SNPs with a minor allele frequency (MAF) < 0.01 were excluded, and the remaining 86,933 SNPs were used for structure, kinship, and LD analyses. To stratify covariates (Q matrix) from the population structure of the K-SoyNAM population, fastSTRUCTURE, which is widely used to compute posterior inference based on the Bayesian framework, was used [62]. An 87 K SNP genotyping dataset was converted to the variant call format (VCF) using the TASSEL 5 standalone command line interface [56], and fastSTRUCTURE was run for various numbers of populations (K = 1,.,30) to define the number of subpopulations using the marginal likelihood function. The marginal likelihood values as a function of the number of K were plotted using the R package ‘ggplot2’ version 3.5.1 [61]. To select the appropriate number of model components explaining the structure in the dataset, the chooseK.py python script from fastSTRUCTURE was used.

To determine the relationship between individuals, the kinship matrix (K) for the K-SoyNAM population was generated by calculating the proportion of shared alleles between individuals using the TASSEL 5 standalone command line interface via the centered identity by state method [56, 63]. The kinship matrix was visualized using the ‘kinship.heatmap’ function in the R package ‘ASRgenomics’ version 1.1.4 [64]. LD was calculated as r2 between all pairs of SNPs using the parameters (--r2 –ld-window-kb 1000 –ld-window 99999) in PLINK v1.90b [65]. Considering the different patterns of LD decay in heterochromatin and euchromatin, genomic regions identified as pericentromeric regions in SoyBase [57] (available online at http://soybase.org/, accessed May 11, 2024) were downloaded and applied. A customized R script was used to calculate the average r2 for non-overlapping windows of 10 kbp to estimate LD decay and plot physical distance versus r2 to determine LD decay. The rate of LD decay was measured in physical distance, where the average pairwise r2 value between alleles dropped to half of its maximum value.

Genetic linkage map construction and linkage analysis

Two types of genetic linkage maps can be generated: one is a composite genetic linkage map, considering all polymorphisms and recombination events from the 24 RIL populations, and the other is a single genetic linkage map, considering polymorphisms and recombination events from each RIL population. To construct the composite genetic linkage map, a union genotype dataset, including homozygous and polymorphic SNPs in the parental pair from the 24 populations, was considered. Among the filtered segregating SNPs, the co-segregating SNP sites were clustered into recombination bins, defined as classes of marker sites with identical genotypes across populations using the “binning” option in QTL IciMapping (v4.1) [66] with a threshold P-value of 0.001 for segregation distortion and allowing for missing values. Subsequently, a grouping function with thresholds of > 30 cM and > 5 logarithm of odds (LOD) was used to remove unanchored markers, and the genetic distances between markers were estimated using the Kosambi function. The order of markers was adjusted using the nearest neighbor method with a two-option function in QTL IciMapping (v4.1) [66].

To validate the genetic map, markers within the same recombination bin that differed by more than 10 Mbp in physical distance were identified when comparing genetic and physical distances. Outliers were removed, and the genetic distances and order were recalculated. In addition, the marker order derived from the genetic distances was compared with the physical distances from the reference soybean genome Glyma.Wm82.a2.v1 (available online at https://soybase.org/, accessed on May 11, 2024) [57], and then manually adjusted by repeated use of the ‘ordering’ function. For single genetic linkage map construction, a genotype dataset containing homozygous and polymorphic SNPs in the parental pair from their populations was considered. The same procedure (binning, grouping, ordering, and validation) was employed to construct a single genetic linkage map from each of the 24 RIL populations.

Inclusive composite interval mapping of the additive mapping method [67] was used for linkage analysis. The mean values of flowering time, seed protein content, and flower color were used as the phenotype to identify QTLs. A 1,000-permutation test (P < 0.05) was performed to determine the LOD threshold for significance [68]. To compare the QTLs identified in this study, previously reported QTL information was obtained from the SoyBase QTL database (available online at https://www.soybase.org/search/qtllist_by_symbol.php, accessed on May 11, 2024) [57]. Graphical representations of the genetic maps and QTL plots were visualized using MapChart 2.32 [69].

Genotype imputation and GWAS

Genotype imputation requires three types of datasets: a high-density genotype dataset as the reference panel, a high-quality recombination map, and a low-density genotype dataset for imputation [70]. Haplotype data from 25 parents of the K-SoyNAM population, extracted from 781 soybean accessions [71], were used as the reference panel. The composite genetic linkage map generated in this study, which represents the polymorphism and recombination events in the K-SoyNAM population, was used as a high-quality recombination map. The 86,933 SNP genotype dataset of the K-SoyNAM population, filtered from the Axiom 180 K SoyaSNP, was used as the low-density genotype dataset to be imputed.

BEAGLE v5.4 was used to impute the 86,933 SNP genotype dataset using 4,589,869 SNP variants from the reference panel considering the recombination rate from a recombination map with the following parameters: ne = 10, window = 200, err = 0.0005, imp-segment = 30, ap = true, gp = true [32]. Genotype imputation accuracy was assessed using the r2 between the true allele dosage and the imputed posterior allele probability implemented in the BEAGLE v5.4 program [31]. After genotype imputation, poorly imputed SNPs (r² < 0.5) and those with low MAF (< 0.01) were filtered out, resulting in 4,057,789 SNPs available for GWAS.

An MLM [72] including only K as a random effect was implemented for association analysis of the three agronomic traits using the TASSEL 5 standalone command line interface [56]. Both unimputed and imputed genotype datasets were used for association analysis to confirm whether genotype imputation methods improve the precision of GWAS results in detecting causal mutations. LD blocks containing significant SNPs were searched using the soybean reference genome browser SoyBase [57] (available online at https://www.soybase.org/tools/browsers/gbrowse.html?iframe_pathname_suffix=gmax2.0, accessed on April 1, 2026) to narrow down candidate genes responsible for the three agronomic traits.

Results

Genotype and phenotype dataset of K-SoyNAM population

The K-SoyNAM population, consisting of 27 families and 2,831 RILs, was developed, and all RILs were genotyped using an Axiom 180 K SoyaSNP genotyping platform (Affymetrix, Santa Clara, CA, USA) (Fig. 1, Figure S1, Table 1, Table S1). Phylogenetic analysis was performed to identify outliers generated during the development process, and these outliers were either reassigned to their original family or removed. Three RIL populations (NAM22, NAM26, and NAM27) with less than 60 RILs in each family were excluded from further genetic and genomic analysis, resulting in a final dataset of 24 populations with 2,669 RILs (Fig. 2A, Table S2).

Phenotypic data for the K-SoyNAM population, comprising three agronomic traits (flowering time, seed protein content, and flower color), are described in Table S3 and Figure S2. Flowering time was evaluated in the K-SoyNAM population due to its importance in local adaptation and to characterize the usefulness of the population for linkage or association analysis. The broad-sense heritability on a line-mean basis for flowering time in the K-SoyNAM population was 0.71. The donor parents of the K-SoyNAM population flowered between 44 and 66 days after planting, with a mean of 52.3 days (Table S3). All subpopulations were normally distributed, and transgressive segregation beyond the variation of their parents was confirmed in each subpopulation (Table S3, Figure S2A). In addition, the distribution of flowering time varied among the subpopulations (Figure S2A).

Seed protein content in K-SoyNAM was also evaluated, and its broad-sense heritability was 0.804. The donor parents of the K-SoyNAM population had seed protein contents ranging from 34.57 to 45.59%, with a mean of 38.9% (Table S3). The results varied among the subpopulations, especially in two populations (NAM11 and NAM15), where high seed protein content was observed. This variation was influenced by the parents of these two populations, ‘Danbaek’ (Kim et al., 1996) and ‘Saedanbaek’ (Kim et al., 2014), which have high seed protein content (Table S3, Figure S2B).

Flower color is one of the important qualitative traits of soybean. Among the parents of the K-SoyNAM population, 10 parents had white flowers, and 14 had purple flowers (Table S3). Since ‘Daepung’, the hub parent, had white flowers, segregation of flower color occurred only in the 14 populations crossed with parents having purple flowers (Table S3). In these populations, a segregation ratio of 1:1 was observed (except for NAM11), confirming that the K-SoyNAM population were successfully constructed and suitable for genetic and genomic analysis.

Genetic architecture of K-SoyNAM population

The polymorphic ratio and segregation distortion were represented by calculating allele frequencies across the entire genome in the K-SoyNAM population (Figure S3). These results showed that polymorphism was detected across the entire soybean genome (Figure S3A), and while most genomic regions segregated well, segregation distortion was observed in some genomic regions (Figure S3B). However, segregation distortion varied among subpopulations and occurred nonrandomly within the euchromatic and heterochromatic regions (data not shown).

The average FST of the whole chromosome in K-SoyNAM was 0.189, and those for each chromosome ranged from 0.154 (Chromosome 12) to 0.223 (Chromosome 18), indicating moderate genetic differentiation among subpopulations of the K-SoyNAM population (Figure S4A). From the PCA results, PC1 and PC2, which explain 17.86 and 11.52% of the variance, respectively, and revealed clustering patterns among founder-derived families within the K-SoyNAM population (Figure S4B). Although the clusters were partially overlapping because of the shared hub parent, the PCA results indicated detectable family-related structure inherent to the NAM design. The marginal likelihood estimated by fastSTRUCTURE increased with increasing K values and approached a plateau around K = 22–23 (Figure S4C), suggesting substantial population structure corresponding to the founder-derived families. The kinship matrix represented the degree of allele sharing among individuals in the K-SoyNAM population, with yellow colors indicating higher kinship and blue colors indicating lower kinship (Figure S4D). To further evaluate the appropriateness of the GWAS model, MLM models incorporating kinship only (MLM + K) and kinship plus population structure covariates (MLM + K+Q23) were compared using QQ plots and genomic inflation factors. The results showed highly similar inflation patterns between the two models, suggesting that the kinship matrix alone sufficiently accounted for family-related structure within the K-SoyNAM population (Figure S5, Table S4). LD decay analysis using PLINK v1.90b indicated that the pairwise squared correlation coefficient (r²) decreased to half of its maximum value at approximately 380–390 kb across the whole genome (Figure S4E). LD decayed more rapidly in euchromatic regions than in heterochromatic regions, whereas heterochromatic regions maintained higher LD over longer physical distances, consistent with reduced recombination in pericentromeric regions.

Genetic linkage map construction

From 169,029 SNPs, numerous SNPs with monomorphism, low-quality, and a high proportion of missing values were filtered out. A composite genetic linkage map with a total length of 1,458.24 cM and average marker intervals of 0.129 cM was constructed using 11,265 SNP markers. Detailed information on the maps is summarized in Table 2. The number of mapped markers and the length of the genetic linkage map varied among the chromosomes, ranging from 397 to 763 markers, with a mean of 563.25 markers, and from 45.67 to 108.43 cM, with a mean of 72.91 cM (Table 2).

Composite genetic map information of K-SoyNAM population

The 24 genetic linkage maps for each RIL population in K-SoyNAM were constructed using the same procedure as that of the composite genetic linkage map. Detailed information on these maps is summarized in the Supplementary Data. The number of mapped SNPs across all chromosomes ranged from 4,056 (NAM15) to 11,718 (NAM24), with an average of 7,349.58, and the total lengths of the genetic linkage maps ranged from 1,252.7 (NAM15) to 2,957.8 cM (NAM05), with an average of 2,094.3 cM (Supplementary Data A). The observed variation in map length among families may reflect multiple factors, including differences in parental genetic diversity, uneven chromosomal distribution of informative polymorphic markers, segregation distortion, residual heterozygosity remaining in F5-derived RILs, and family-specific recombination patterns. For example, the NAM15 population, which exhibited the shortest map length, retained relatively few informative mapped markers on several chromosomes after binning and filtering procedures, potentially reducing recombination resolution in those regions (Supplementary Data A.15). In addition, linkage groups from 24 chromosomes across 11 populations were split into two or more groups because no polymorphic markers or recombination events were detected between adjacent linkage groups (Supplementary Data A).

Most of the genetic linkage map of the 24 subpopulations was validated by confirming collinearity between the genetic (cM) and physical (bp) positions of the integrated SNPs, and linear correlations were plotted for the whole genome across all chromosomes (Fig. 3). Some chromosomes of a subpopulation did not show collinearity between the genetic (cM) and physical (bp) positions. This discrepancy can be explained by structural variations against the reference genome, Glyma.Wm82.a2.v1, or between the parents within their population (Supplementary Data B). The order of the markers was largely consistent across the subpopulations, and the order of the loci in the composite genetic linkage map was generally consistent with their physical positions in Williams 82, Glyma.Wm82.a2.v1, whether based on each family individually or on all families collectively (Supplementary Data C).

Genotype imputation

The 4,589,869 SNPs from the re-sequenced data of the 25 K-SoyNAM parents were imputed to 86,933 SNPs from the Axiom 180 K SoyaSNP data of 2,669 RILs from the K-SoyNAM population (Figure S7 and 8, Table S8). To assess the imputation accuracy of imputed SNPs, the distribution of accuracy values (squared correlation r²) across MAF bins of size 0.01 was examined (Figure S7). At the lower end of the MAF spectrum (MAF < 0.1), median accuracy values were generally below 0.3 and showed broad distributions. In contrast, m ost MAF bins above 0.1 showed median accuracy values greater than 0.5 with narrower distributions (Figure S7A). Because BEAGLE infers missing genotypes based on local LD structure, the distribution patterns of imputation accuracy across the MAF spectrum were further compared between euchromatic and heterochromatic regions (Figure S7B, C). The observed differences in imputation performance likely reflect multiple genomic features, including recombination rate, LD structure, haplotype diversity, repetitive sequence composition, and MAF distribution, rather than marker density alone. In particular, heterochromatic regions exhibited greater variability in imputation accuracy across several MAF bins, which may be associated with reduced recombination and complex haplotype structure in these regions. Finally, the imputed set of 4,546,027 SNPs was further filtered to remove poorly imputed (BEAGLE r² < 0.5) and low-frequency variants (MAF < 0.01). The resulting 4,057,789 high-quality SNPs were used for subsequent GWAS analyses (Figure S7D, Figure S8B).

Comparison of GWAS with different datasets

The usefulness of the dataset generated for GWAS by imputing SNP genotypes to an existing Axiom 180 K SoyaSNP genotype and phenotype dataset was evaluated for dissecting the genetic architecture of flowering time, seed protein content, and flower color. The GWAS results of the three agronomic traits, obtained by running a mixed linear model (MLM) with the imputed SNP dataset on the 2,669 RILs, were largely consistent with the results based on the existing Axiom 180 K SoyaSNP array genotype and phenotype dataset (Tables 4 and 5). The GWAS results for flowering time and flower color were consistent with previously reported gene regions, including E1, E2, PRR3b, E3, and W1 (Table 4, Fig. 6, Table S9, Figure S9). Focusing on chromosome 19 in the flowering time results, before imputation, only two SNPs were located above the threshold. However, after imputation, the surrounding SNPs were filled in, leading to more reliable results (Fig. 7). Among the four genomic regions identified in the GWAS results for seed protein content, only two regions contained the previously reported genes GmSWEET10a and POWR1. The other two genomic regions did not contain any reported genes (Fig. 8). Nevertheless, the reported QTL regions for seed protein content, qtl-chr6_prot and Seed protein 41 − 8, were identified to overlap with the genomic region on chromosomes 6 and 19, respectively (Figures S10 and S11). In addition, GmSWEET47 and GmSWEET48, a group of efflux transporters that play an indispensable role in sugar efflux, were identified on chromosome 19 in the GWAS results as promising candidate genes associated with seed protein content (Table 5; Fig. 9).

As expected, major peaks were identified for the three agronomic traits, and several minor significant peaks showed significance, such as those on chromosome 19 for flowering time and on chromosomes 6, 15, and 19 for seed protein content (Tables 5 and S6; Figs. 6, 7, 8 and 9). Although changes in p-values were clearly observed in these minor peaks (two increases and two decreases), all these peaks were compensated for by adjacent SNPs, which had slightly lower p-values and were involved in the same LD (Figs. 7 and 9). This implies that GWAS analysis using the imputed dataset can more precisely detect minor signals that have very weak effects on quantitative traits.

To further evaluate the reliability of genotype imputation, allelic effect sizes between unimputed and imputed GWAS datasets were compared for flowering time and seed protein content (Figure S12). Most significant loci showed consistent effect directions between the two datasets, and major association signals formed strong diagonal patterns, indicating that genotype imputation generally preserved the biological association signals detected in the unimputed dataset.

Discussion

Publicly available soybean NAM resources, particularly those developed in the United States, have provided valuable platforms for genetic studies; however, their genetic backgrounds and breeding objectives differ from those of Korean soybean germplasm. The whole genome sequence between the parental lines of the US [115] and Korean [71] SoyNAM populations reveals that they form two distinct groups, although some parents are intermixed (Table S10, Figure S13A). Two K-SoyNAM parents are part of the US group: K14 corresponds to Williams 82 K, while K08, ‘Pureun’ [37], is one of the parental lines of L78-379 [116], an isoline derived from Williams. Among the US-SoyNAM parents classified into the Korean group, four out of five originated from Asia [27]. One of the parental lines of US31 (LG90-2550), PI68658, originates from China [117], and K20 corresponds to ‘Socheong 2’, which belongs to the same group and has a parental line derived from Peking [48] (Figure S13A). The different genetic backgrounds between US and Korean SoyNAM parents are derived from not only geographical and environmental distances but also the breeding objectives of the two countries. In the United States, soybean is regarded as an oil crop, with major breeding targets being yield, seed composition, such as protein and oil, and soymeal protein [118]. Conversely, in Korea, soybean is considered a food crop, with breeding goals focusing on seed protein content and processing characteristics for various uses [119–122] (Figure S13B). Therefore, to explore the agronomic traits required for Korea’s breeding programs, developing a NAM population comprising Korean soybean germplasm is necessary.

An investigation of segregation distortion in the K-SoyNAM population revealed the regions that significantly deviated from the expected 1:1 segregation ratio (Figure S3). However, no consistent pattern was found among the populations. This is consistent with the US-SoyNAM population, suggesting that excluding pollen during selfing or the effect of unknown genetic factors may play a role [26]. Gametophyte factor 1-strong allele (Ga1-S), gametophyte factor (ga2), and sugar 1 (su1) from maize [33] and several sterility genes such as ga2, ga3, and S34 from rice [22] are related to pollen fertilization or seed germination and reportedly distort segregation.

Since the NAM population can also be used as an association panel, population structure was inferred to characterize genetic relationships among founder-derived families within the K-SoyNAM population (Figure S4). In an ideal NAM population, the number of subpopulations generally correspond to the number of founder-derived families, and fine-mapping of QTLs can be achieved through joint linkage mapping [123] or association analysis using MLM approaches incorporating family information [59]. The K-SoyNAM population exhibited detectable genetic differentiation among founder-derived families, as supported by the FST results (average FST = 0.189), PCA clustering patterns, and fastSTRUCTURE analysis (Figure S4). The marginal likelihood estimated by fastSTRUCTURE increased with increasing K values and approached a plateau around K = 22–23, suggesting substantial family-related structure within the K-SoyNAM population. Similarly, PCA based on the additive relationship matrix revealed partially overlapping but distinguishable clustering patterns along PC1 and PC2, reflecting the shared hub parent and family structure inherent to the NAM design (Figure S4B). Therefore, the K-SoyNAM population should be interpreted as a structured multi-family population sharing a common hub parent rather than a completely unstructured population. To further evaluate the appropriateness of the GWAS model, MLM models incorporating kinship only (MLM + K) and kinship plus population structure covariates derived from fastSTRUCTURE (MLM + K+Q23) were compared using flowering time and seed protein content traits. QQ plots and genomic inflation factors (λGC; Table S7 and Figure S5) showed highly similar patterns between the two models. The λGC values ranged from 0.82 to 0.98, indicating adequate control of population and family structure in the GWAS analyses and suggesting that the kinship matrix alone sufficiently accounted for family-related structure within the K-SoyNAM population. LD (r²) decay in the K-SoyNAM population was substantially slower than that observed in domesticated soybean germplasm collections (Figure S4E), decreasing to half of its maximum value at approximately 1,000 kb, which is more than tenfold greater than previously reported values in soybean germplasm collections (~ 97 kb) [71]. In addition, LD decayed more rapidly in euchromatic regions than in heterochromatic regions, whereas heterochromatic regions maintained higher LD over longer physical distances, consistent with reduced recombination in pericentromeric regions. Compared with diverse soybean germplasm collections, the relatively slow LD decay observed in the K-SoyNAM population likely reflects the limited number of historical recombination events and the family-based structure of the NAM design.

Residual heterozygosity was additionally evaluated across the K-SoyNAM population because the RILs were derived from the F5 generation. Although F5-derived RIL populations may retain residual heterozygosity compared with more advanced F7/F8-derived NAM populations, the observed heterozygosity levels in the K-SoyNAM population were generally within the expected range for genetic analyses. The average residual heterozygosity across families was 4.47%, which was generally within or below the theoretical expectation for F5-derived RIL populations (6.25%) (Table S2). Most families exhibited residual heterozygosity levels below 6%, although the Taekwang family showed a relatively higher value of 6.84%. Markers showing heterozygosity in parental lines were removed prior to genetic map construction, whereas heterozygous genotype calls within RILs were retained during linkage analysis according to the original genotyping pipeline. Because the observed heterozygosity levels were generally consistent with expectations for F5-derived populations, no additional filtering based on residual heterozygosity was applied before downstream analyses, including GWAS and genotype imputation.

High-resolution genetic linkage maps provide clues for genetic studies, such as detecting natural chromosomal rearrangements and recombination hotspots [124, 125]. A composite genetic linkage map includes all the recombination events occurring in multiple populations, which is one of the major advantages and strengths of the NAM population. Although the correlation plot of the composite genetic linkage map between genetic (cM) and physical (bp) position is more saturated, the results are similar to those of US-SoyNAM. Numerous redundant markers were eliminated to construct a genetic map that is collinear between genetic (cM) and physical (Mbp) positions while considering recombination events occurring in diverse populations (Table 2, Fig. 3). Two possible reasons for this are: first, all genotype information is based on the Williams 82 reference genome, implying that structural variations between Williams 82 and the parents of the K-SoyNAM population are not reflected. Second, Williams 82 genome assembly version 2 was used; therefore, it is expected that remapping using the current Williams 82 genome assembly version 6 [126] will result in a more accurate genetic map. A genetic linkage map comprising 24 maps from a single K-SoyNAM population was constructed, with an average of 367.83 markers and a length of 102.56 cM. Although the average number of individuals per population was 111, which is much lower than the 922 individuals in the Essex × Williams 82 (EW) population [127] and 240 individuals in the Williams 82 K × Hwangkeum (WH) population [124], the average distance between markers was 0.29 cM, which is slightly higher than the 0.269 cM in the EW population and 0.117 cM in the WH population. Unlike the EW and WH populations, the 24 genetic maps in the present study shared a hub parent, ‘Daepung’, which is expected to provide more valuable results for future genetic research.

The linkage mapping results for the three agronomic traits confirmed that the previously reported genes were well-identified and provided fine-mapped results for several loci (Table 3; Figs. 4 and 5). In the flowering time QTL results, saturated loci were detected at previously reported loci: chromosome 11 (First_flower_11 − 2), chromosome 15 (First_flower_12 − 3), and chromosome 19 (First_flower_5 − 3, First_flower_8 − 3) (Fig. 4D–F). In the seed protein content QTL results, saturated loci were detected at previously reported loci: chromosome 3 (Seed_protein_27 − 4, Seed_protein_36 − 35), chromosome 4 (Seed_protein_10-g3), chromosome 6 (Seed_protein_24 − 1), chromosome 7 (Seed_protein_33 − 5), chromosome 9 (Seed_protein_34 − 6), chromosome 10 (Seed_protein_41 − 11), chromosome 11 (Seed_protein_36 − 2), and chromosome 16 (Seed_protein_4–7) (Fig. 5A–D, F–H, L). In addition, 13 and 9 novel QTLs were identified for flowering time and seed protein, respectively (Tables S4 and S5).

Since several successful genotype imputation cases using BEAGLE 5.0 have been reported [71], the same strategy was applied to the K-SoyNAM population in the current study. In the BEAGLE 5.0 manual, imputation using genetic map distance is highly recommended. We constructed a composite genetic linkage map and used it to perform linear interpolation for estimating genetic positions between map positions [31]. Almost all genomic regions that were not genotyped using the Axiom 180 K SoyaSNP array were covered by genotype imputation, resulting in improved GWAS results (Figure S8). The enhanced GWAS system detected small significant peaks that were not clearly identified in the unimputed GWAS system [71]. Finally, it was demonstrated that the high-quality large mapping population in soybean could be used as an association panel with genotype imputation to improve GWAS of complex traits. The imputation results suggest that the genetic linkage map and methods developed here can be directly applied to accelerate the identification of genetic variation in soybean. In addition, the comparison of allelic effect sizes between unimputed and imputed GWAS datasets demonstrated that most major loci retained consistent effect directions after imputation. These results support the robustness of the imputation-based GWAS framework developed in this study while also suggesting that minor association peaks detected only after imputation should be interpreted cautiously until further biological validation is performed.

The results of this study are highly valuable as foundational materials widely applicable in genetic and genomic research of complex traits for the Korean soybean breeding program. The K-SoyNAM population enables QTL mapping that was previously unattainable in traditional bi-parental populations and allows for future research on recombination that can be applied to breeding. Additionally, the Korean breeding program can extend beyond conventional breeding and molecular breeding to incorporate genomic selection. To achieve more precise genetic mapping, two future studies are necessary. First, the current genotypic data is based on the Wm82.a2 reference genome, but transitioning to the recently reported Wm82.a6 reference genome [126] would enhance mapping accuracy. Second, performing de novo assembly of the parental lines of the K-SoyNAM population using long-read sequencing to construct a pan-genome would facilitate more efficient genetic analysis [128]. Overall, the K-SoyNAM population represents a valuable platform for advancing soybean genetics and breeding, particularly in the context of region-specific breeding objectives and food-grade trait improvement.
