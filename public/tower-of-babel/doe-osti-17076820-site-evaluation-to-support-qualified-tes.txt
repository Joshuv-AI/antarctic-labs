 SUMMARY

This study provides an assessment of the siting suitability of advanced microreactors identified by the
Reactor Pilot Program at both selected DOE and non-DOE sites. The assessment was conducted using
Oak Ridge Siting Analysis for Power Generation Expansion (ORSAGE) after reviewing existing
licensing frameworks, development of siting criteria, and evaluation of demonstration sites for
microreactors and drawing the prior INL's reactor-agnostic siting methodology, EPRI’s siting guidance,
DOE policies on reactor sites, and ORSAGE-based geospatial analyses.

Early-stage siting is often challenged by incomplete, restricted, or low-fidelity siting criteria. As a result,
preliminary evaluations rely on publicly available information and broad screening criteria, while detailed
environmental, geotechnical, seismic, and licensing analyses are reserved for future detailed studies.

INL's reactor-agnostic siting methodology demonstrated the value of using "Must" and "Want" criteria to
progressively eliminate unsuitable sites and rank viable alternatives. This approach emphasizes regulatory
compliance, environmental protection, infrastructure availability, and deployment practicality.

ORSAGE was employed as the primary geospatial screening platform. The database was updated to a 30-
meter spatial resolution to better reflect the smaller footprints of modern microreactor designs and was
applied to five reactor concepts across four demonstration clusters.

This study focused on four project clusters identified through a review of publicly available information
regarding current construction and site development efforts associated with pilot reactor projects. The
cluster in this context is a 10-mile study area. These clusters will host eight of the 11 announced projects;
however, this study analyzed seven of those eight projects. The selected clusters are:
 • Cluster 1: Idaho National Laboratory (INL)
 • Cluster 2: Texas A&M University’s RELLIS Campus
 • Cluster 3: Abilene Christian University (ACU)
 • Cluster 4: Orangeville, Utah

Key Takeaways
 § ORSAGE provides a transparent and repeatable framework for integrating environmental,
 regulatory, infrastructure, and safety constraints into a single siting analysis.
 § Microreactor siting requirements are currently assumed to be driven primarily by reactor
 technology rather than power output, with design-specific parameters incorporated as they
 become available.
 § Idaho National Laboratory site exhibited the highest overall suitability, with approximately 97%
 of the evaluated area meeting all siting criteria.
 § Texas A&M RELLIS Campus site exhibited the greatest siting challenges, with population
 setbacks and floodplains reducing suitable land to approximately 46% for the Last Energy PWR
 and 36% for the Aalo Pod SFR.
 § Abilene Christian University site demonstrated approximately 65% suitable land availability,
 with population centers and nearby hazardous facilities representing the primary constraints.
 § Orangeville site in Utah exhibited approximately 38% suitable land, with protected lands and
 rugged terrain serving as the dominant barriers to deployment. The majority of land designated as
 "unsuitable" is not necessarily unavailable for consideration; rather, its use would likely require
 additional investment, regulatory accommodations, and executive-level permitting.
 § Population-based setbacks, floodplains, protected lands, hazardous facilities, and topography
 emerged as the most influential siting constraints across the demonstration clusters.

Overall Conclusion
While the siting criteria for microreactor technology are not finalized yet, prior experience at INL with
other demonstration reactors is a valuable lesson for this study. ORSAGE provides a robust foundation
for identifying these opportunities and supporting risk-informed, defensible siting decisions for future
reactor demonstrations and commercial deployments. All the selected demonstration sites have sufficient
suitable land area within a 10-mile radius for different microreactor technologies.

1. INTRODUCTION
On May 23, 2025, Executive Order 14301, Reforming Nuclear Reactor Testing at the Department of
Energy, was issued to accelerate advanced reactor development by directing the Department of Energy
(DOE) to establish a Reactor Pilot Program and streamline the approval, construction, and operation of
Qualified Test Reactors (QTRs) and other advanced reactor demonstration projects at both DOE facilities
and selected non-DOE sites outside the national laboratory system. By establishing pathways for QTRs
and pilot reactor deployments both within DOE reservations and beyond national laboratories, the federal
government aims to create a modern testing ecosystem capable of reducing commercialization risk,
shortening deployment timelines, and strengthening U.S. leadership in advanced nuclear technology.
In 2025, DOE initially identified 11 advanced reactor projects to move their technologies towards
deployment, and the identified pilot reactor projects are listed in Table 1-1. DOE will work with industry

1.1 Contexts
Qualified Test Reactors (QTRs) are intended to serve as specialized experimental and prototype reactor
platforms for testing, validating, and maturing advanced nuclear technologies. Their primary purpose is to
support activities such as technology verification, fuel and materials qualification, operational
performance assessment, and the generation of data needed to inform future regulatory and licensing
decisions. Unlike commercial power reactors, which are designed primarily for reliable electricity
production, QTRs are focused on reducing technical uncertainties, demonstrating key performance
characteristics, and accelerating the path from innovation to deployment.
The growing policy emphasis on QTR development reflects a convergence of national priorities,
including energy security, technological competitiveness, national defense, and the expansion of nuclear
energy infrastructure. A longstanding challenge facing advanced reactor developers is the "demonstration
gap" that exists between laboratory-scale research and commercial deployment. Moving a reactor concept
from design and testing to a first-of-a-kind operating facility often involves significant technical,
financial, and regulatory risks. QTRs help bridge this gap by providing an intermediate demonstration
platform where reactor technologies can be evaluated under representative operating conditions while
producing the technical evidence and operational experience needed to support future licensing and
commercialization efforts.
Many DOE sites are well positioned to host QTRs and pilot reactors due to their established nuclear
infrastructure, security programs, environmental monitoring capabilities, emergency preparedness
systems, experienced workforce, and existing regulatory frameworks. These attributes can significantly
reduce deployment risks and shorten project timelines. However, while DOE sites will continue to play a
critical role in advancing reactor demonstrations, recent federal initiatives increasingly recognize that
widespread commercialization ultimately requires deployment in operating environments beyond national
laboratories. Demonstrating pilot reactors at non-DOE locations provides opportunities to validate
operational integration, assess market viability, evaluate business cases, and generate performance data
under realistic end-user conditions.
As efforts to accelerate QTR and pilot reactor deployment continue, there is an increasing need for robust,
transparent, and risk-informed siting methodologies. Although recent Executive Orders seek to streamline
permitting and reduce unnecessary barriers to deployment, site selection must continue to account for
critical considerations such as environmental stewardship, public safety, security requirements,
infrastructure readiness, emergency planning, and long-term operational needs. In this context, ORSAGE
serves as a valuable decision-support platform by providing advanced geospatial screening and suitability
assessment capabilities that can rapidly identify and evaluate candidate sites across both federal and non-
federal lands. By integrating environmental, infrastructure, demographic, regulatory, and security
considerations into a consistent analytical framework, ORSAGE can help support informed siting

2. ASSESSING LICENSING FRAMEWORKS FOR MICROREACTORS
The primary goal of this report is to evaluate the suitability of potential sites of the advanced reactors
identified by the Reactor Pilot Program using the siting assessment tool, ORSAGE. As explained in Table
1-1, most pilot program reactors are microreactors, which are different from the large commercial light
water reactors in terms of the source terms, fuel and coolant materials, required site space, etc. Thus, this
section focuses on microreactors as well as test and pilot reactors by discussing historical background,
existing licensing pathways, and the implications of this background for recent DOE missions.

2.1 Historical Background
Historically, the Atomic Energy Commission (AEC)—and subsequently divided into the Department of
Energy (DOE) and Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC) —has played a central role in advancing
nuclear energy technologies by providing funding, research infrastructure, technical expertise, and
regulatory oversight to support reactor development, testing, and demonstration activities. Through the
national laboratory system, DOE has enabled the maturation of innovative nuclear technologies by
offering access to specialized facilities, scientific expertise, and unique research capabilities.
More recently, a series of Executive Orders issued in May 2026b signaled an expanded federal
commitment to accelerating the commercialization and deployment of both Generation III+ and advanced
reactor technologies. In response, DOE has adopted a more proactive role in fostering public-private
partnerships reducing barriers to reactor demonstration and deployment. This approach includes
leveraging DOE authorization pathways for demonstration reactor operations, making selected federal
lands available for reactor development, and providing industry with access to DOE’s specialized
facilities, technical capabilities, and research expertise.
To support the growing demand for demonstration opportunities, DOE, through the National Reactor
Innovation Center (NRIC), is coordinating activities across multiple reactor development programs to
establish a cohesive, predictable, and efficient pathway for developers seeking access to DOE resources.
These efforts span both national laboratory sites and non-laboratory locations, creating a more
streamlined framework for reactor testing, licensing support, and deployment readiness.
One outcome of this initiative has been the establishment of the Nuclear Energy Launch Padc, which
designates selected parcels within the Idaho National Laboratory (INL) reservation for potential reactor
demonstrations and advanced nuclear energy projects (see Figure 2-1 for the site boundary of the Launch
Pad program). Complementing this effort, DOE and NRIC have developed a suite of technical guidance
documents, siting resources, and deployment frameworks intended to assist reactor developers in
navigating the demonstration process.
Where applicable and relevant, this report draws upon these guidance documents (e.g., [1]) and associated
DOE resources to inform the siting considerations, evaluation methodologies, and recommendations
presented herein.

This section examines the factors that influence both the availability and suitability of potential
microreactor sites, including land-use constraints, environmental and regulatory considerations,
infrastructure availability, and interactions with existing or planned facilities. Particular attention is given
to the status of datasets available to support preliminary siting decisions, the identification of critical data
gaps, and the additional information that may be required to support licensing, environmental review, and
final site qualification activities.
The data required to support microreactor siting decisions evolve significantly in both specificity and
quality assurance pedigree as the siting process progresses. During the early screening stages, publicly
available and lower-resolution datasets are often sufficient to identify broad areas of opportunity and
eliminate locations that are clearly unsuitable. This approach enables efficient evaluation of large
geographic areas and numerous candidate sites while minimizing the time and resources expended on
detailed characterization efforts.
As candidate sites advance through the evaluation process, however, increasingly detailed and site-
specific information becomes necessary. Final determinations of site suitability typically require extensive
data collection and analysis encompassing environmental baseline conditions, meteorological
observations, hydrologic studies, geological and geotechnical investigations, seismic hazard
characterization, infrastructure assessments, and other licensing-related evaluations. These data are often

required to satisfy regulatory expectations and support safety analyses associated with reactor

Equally important is the quality assurance pedigree associated with the data. Information used to support
licensing, safety-related analyses, and regulatory decisions must generally be collected, managed, and
documented in accordance with an approved nuclear quality assurance program to ensure traceability,
reproducibility, and technical defensibility throughout the project lifecycle. Consequently, datasets vary
not only in technical content but also in their suitability for regulatory use.
This graduated approach to data acquisition and qualification is particularly relevant because both
publicly available and non-public datasets exist across a broad spectrum of quality, resolution, and
applicability. Some datasets are sufficiently robust to support early-stage exclusion and screening
decisions, while others require significant refinement, validation, or supplemental collection efforts before
they can be used in final siting determinations. Furthermore, the spatial applicability of individual datasets
varies considerably. Certain data types, such as regional meteorological observations or broad geologic
characterizations, may be transferable across relatively large areas, whereas others, including geotechnical
properties, subsurface conditions, contamination profiles, and localized hazard assessments, can vary
significantly over short distances and therefore require site-specific investigation.
Understanding the availability, limitations, and intended use of these data sources is essential for
developing an efficient, risk-informed, and technically defensible approach to microreactor siting at DOE
laboratories and other potential deployment locations.

2.2 Learning from Preliminary Site Screenings for Advanced
Reactor Demonstration at INL
This section summarizes prior site suitability assessments conducted at INL. The analysis presented
herein is based largely on the findings, methodologies, and recommendations documented by Conner et
al. [2], which provide the technical foundation for many of the siting considerations discussed in this
section.
2.2.1 On Restricted and Unavailable Datasets
As stated earlier in Section 2.1, obtaining comprehensive datasets to evaluate candidate sites is often
challenging. Several factors contribute to data limitations, including:
 § Sensitivity of Data and Information Restrictions: Certain datasets contain sensitive
 information, such as cultural resources, archaeological sites, or security-related infrastructure.
 Access to these data may be restricted due to confidentiality, regulatory requirements, cultural
 preservation concerns, or national security considerations.
 § Limited Availability of High-Fidelity Data: High-resolution or site-specific datasets are often
 generated or collected during later phases of the siting process, such as detailed site
 characterization and final site assessments. Consequently, these data are typically unavailable
 during the initial screening stages. Furthermore, even when high-fidelity datasets are available
 and not subject to access restrictions, incorporating them into early-stage screening analyses may
 be impractical due to the associated costs, time requirements, and computational burden.
As a result, preliminary site screening efforts frequently rely on publicly available, lower-resolution
datasets that provide sufficient information for broad comparative assessments while reserving detailed
analyses for subsequent evaluation phases.

2.2.2 Imperative of Bounding Assumptions
As discussed in Section 2.2.1, comprehensive site-specific datasets and detailed technical specifications
are generally unavailable during the early stages of the siting process. This limitation is further
compounded by the evolving nature of microreactor technologies, as many reactor designs remain under
development and have not yet reached final design maturity. Given the diversity of vendor concepts and
the absence of finalized design specifications, early-stage siting evaluations must necessarily rely on a set
of bounding assumptions and technology-neutral criteria.
To address these challenges, the INL study [2] established a set of bounding requirements for evaluating
candidate DOE sites. Recognizing that NRIC is expected to host and test multiple microreactor
technologies from diverse vendors, the study adopted a plant parameter envelope. This approach enables
the identification of sites capable of accommodating a broad spectrum of reactor concepts while
maintaining flexibility for future design-specific evaluations.
For preliminary site screening, the methodology incorporated several fundamental siting considerations,
including:
 § Approximate land requirements and facility footprint;
 § Cooling system needs, including both air-cooled and water-cooled configurations;
 § Transportation and logistical requirements, such as access to rail, roadway, and heavy-haul
 infrastructure for delivery of large components and equipment; and
 § Modularity and factory-fabrication characteristics that influence construction, deployment, and
 operational requirements.
Accordingly, a series of bounding assumptions and screening constraints were applied to ensure broad
applicability across potential reactor technologies. These assumptions included restricting evaluations to
areas located within the INL boundary, considering sites suitable for both fast-spectrum and thermal-
spectrum reactor concepts, and limiting reactor capacities to less than 300 MWe for SMRs and less than
20 MWe for microreactors. In addition, candidate greenfield sites were required to contain a minimum
contiguous parcel of approximately 100 acres to accommodate construction laydown areas, operational
facilities, security infrastructure, and safety setback requirements.
For existing facilities and structures being considered as potential demonstration sites, additional
engineering criteria were applied. Specifically, buildings were required to possess a minimum floor
loading capacity of 750 pounds per square foot to support equipment handling, material movement, and
forklift operations associated with reactor deployment and testing activities.
Collectively, these assumptions provided a consistent and technology-inclusive framework for
preliminary site screening while preserving sufficient flexibility to accommodate future advances in
microreactor design and deployment strategies.

2.2.3 First Stage Site Screening via Elimination
Building upon the bounding assumptions established for microreactor deployment and
incorporating considerations related to NEPA, NRC licensing expectations, and applicable DOE
siting requirements, INL developed a six-step methodology for the preliminary screening and
evaluation of 32 potential sites within the INL reservation. The objective of their methodology is
to systematically reduce an initial set of plausible candidate locations to a smaller subset of
technically feasible and suitable sites for further investigation. The six-step screening process is
summarized as follows:

 § Step 1: Establish Screening Criteria
 o Define siting criteria under two broad categories: “Must” criteria and “Want” criteria.
 These criteria, summarized in Figure 2-2, form the basis for the screening and ranking
 process.
 § Step 2: Identify Candidate Sites
 o Compile a comprehensive inventory of potential sites to be considered in the evaluation
 process.
 § Step 3: Apply Mandatory (“Must”) Criteria
 o Evaluate each candidate site against the set of mandatory criteria established in Step 1.
 Sites that fail to satisfy any of the “Must” requirements are removed from further
 consideration. These criteria represent non-negotiable conditions necessary for site
 viability and therefore serve as hard exclusionary filters during the initial screening
 phase.
 § Step 4: Assess Desirable (“Want”) Criteria
 o Evaluate the remaining candidate sites against the set of desirable criteria. Unlike the
 mandatory requirements, these criteria are used to differentiate among sites that have
 successfully passed the initial screening and provide a basis for assessing relative
 suitability.
 § Step 5: Rank Candidate Sites
 o Develop a comparative ranking of the remaining sites based on their performance against
 the “Want” criteria. This step facilitates the identification of sites that offer the greatest
 overall potential for successful deployment.
 § Step 6: Select Sites for Detailed Evaluation
 o Identify and recommend the highest-performing sites for subsequent detailed siting
 studies, engineering assessments, environmental reviews, and licensing evaluations.
Through this structured and progressively selective process, INL’s methodology provides a
transparent, defensible, and technology-neutral framework for identifying the most promising
candidate sites for microreactor demonstration and deployment.

Consider the existing INL facilities shown in Figure 2-3. Within and around these facilities, as well as at
locations identified for potential future development, the INL study [2] evaluated a total of 32 candidate
sites for potential microreactor deployment. The assessment considered three broad categories of sites: (a)
existing INL buildings, (b) previously developed or disturbed areas, and (c) undeveloped land.
 § The first category—existing INL buildings—offers the opportunity to repurpose vacant or
 underutilized facilities for microreactor demonstrations. An important advantage of this approach
 is the ability to leverage existing site characterization studies, infrastructure assessments, and
 environmental analyses, thereby reducing the scope and uncertainty of preliminary siting
 activities.
 § The second category consists of previously developed or disturbed areas, including sites located
 within or adjacent to existing facilities. These locations are generally preferred because prior
 development activities often provide access to historical site data and supporting analyses. In
 addition, these areas have already experienced some degree of land disturbance, reducing the
 likelihood of encountering previously unidentified cultural or archaeological resources. Their
 proximity to existing INL infrastructure including roads, utilities, security systems, and support
 facilities can further reduce siting uncertainty, development costs, and project schedules.
 § The third category encompasses undeveloped or greenfield sites. While these locations offer
 maximum flexibility in facility design, layout, and future expansion, they typically lack detailed
 site-specific information. Consequently, evaluating their environmental sensitivities, permitting
 requirements, and overall suitability involves greater uncertainty and often requires additional
 characterization efforts.

into a functional demonstration platform for NRIC.

schedule, and licensing risks.

2.3 CERCLA and its Exclusion from Site Screening
The Comprehensive Environmental Response, Compensation, and Liability Act (CERCLA) provides the
federal framework for the identification, remediation, and productive reuse of contaminated properties,
including former nuclear research and development sites such as those located at INL. Commonly
referred to as the Superfund Program, CERCLA grants the federal government authority to investigate
hazardous waste sites, establish cleanup requirements, and oversee remediation activities necessary to
protect human health and the environment.
The Superfund Amendments and Reauthorization Act (SARA) of 1986 strengthened CERCLA by
emphasizing permanent, health-protective remediation strategies and enhancing the Environmental
Protection Agency’s (EPA) hazard-ranking and cleanup processes. Subsequently, the Superfund
Redevelopment Program (SRP), established in 1999, further expanded the framework by promoting the
safe and beneficial reuse of remediated properties. Collectively, these programs support:

 • Short-term removal actions to address immediate threats to human health and the environment.

 • Long-term remedial actions for sites listed on the EPA National Priorities List (NPL), with
 federal agencies such as DOE supporting technical evaluations and coordination with EPA
 throughout the cleanup process.
Prior to considering redevelopment or reuse of a CERCLA-managed site, several key activities are
typically required:
 a) Collection and review of site information, including assessment of current cleanup status and
 remaining environmental liabilities.
 b) Clarification of legal and liability considerations, including eligibility for protections such as the
 Bona Fide Prospective Purchaser (BFPP) provisions.
 c) Evaluation of future land-use objectives, redevelopment constraints, financing considerations, and
 stakeholder or community involvement requirements.
 d) Utilization of redevelopment tools and guidance, including reuse assessments, Ready-for-Reuse
 determinations, institutional controls, and future land-use planning documents.

June 29, 2026                                                                                              15

From a siting perspective, CERCLA-managed properties can offer several advantages. These may include
access to existing infrastructure, reduced land acquisition costs, well-defined contamination boundaries,
and established regulatory relationships that can facilitate redevelopment planning and coordination with
EPA.
However, when rapid site selection and deployment are priorities, several important considerations must
also be considered:
 § Land-Use Restrictions: Redevelopment activities may be constrained by institutional controls,
 engineering controls, or land-use restrictions imposed as part of the remedy. Furthermore, EPA is
 required to conduct periodic five-year reviews when residual contamination remains above levels
 that permit unrestricted use.
 § Liability Considerations: While BFPP status can provide protection from historical cleanup
 liabilities under certain conditions, prospective developers may still encounter EPA cost-recovery
 liens, windfall liens, or other legal obligations that require careful evaluation.
 § Cleanup Participation Requirements: Voluntary participation in cleanup activities may
 accelerate redevelopment and site reuse; however, such activities generally require coordination
 with and oversight by EPA and other regulatory authorities.
 § Local and Regional Coordination: Successful redevelopment often depends on active
 participation by local governments and stakeholders. Responsibilities may include land-use
 planning, infrastructure development, property management, tax administration, permitting
 support, and community engagement.
Consequently, while CERCLA sites can represent attractive redevelopment opportunities due to their
existing infrastructure and prior characterization efforts, their suitability for accelerated deployment
projects must be evaluated within the broader context of regulatory obligations, land-use restrictions,
liability considerations, and long-term stewardship requirements.

2.3.1 CERCLA a ‘Must-not-use’ Criterion for Siting at INL
As noted in Figure 2-2, sites subject to CERCLA restrictions were treated as exclusionary criteria during
the early-stage INL siting assessment. Accordingly, these locations were screened out during the initial
filtering process to minimize regulatory, environmental, and schedule-related risks. The primary rationale
for excluding CERCLA sites was to avoid several potential challenges:
 § Early Elimination Through Hazard Screening: CERCLA-designated areas were incorporated
 into the siting hazard maps and were systematically removed from consideration during the
 mandatory screening phase (Step 3) of the siting methodology.
 § Increased Regulatory Complexity: Development activities at or near CERCLA sites could
 significantly expand the scope of environmental review and oversight requirements. In particular,
 additional coordination under NEPA, CERCLA, and DOE regulatory frameworks could introduce
 substantial complexity, increase documentation requirements, and extend project schedules.
 § Subsurface Contamination Concerns: The presence of known or potential subsurface
 contamination beneath or adjacent to CERCLA sites could trigger additional characterization,
 remediation, monitoring, and regulatory review requirements. These activities would likely
 increase project costs, complicate licensing and permitting efforts, and potentially delay site
 development and reactor deployment.

Consequently, the exclusion of CERCLA-managed areas during the preliminary screening process
enabled INL to focus on candidate sites with fewer regulatory constraints, lower environmental
uncertainties, and a greater likelihood of supporting accelerated deployment schedules.

3. SITING ANALYSIS
In this section, a siting analysis is completed using a geographic information system (GIS) siting tool
known as Oak Ridge Siting Analysis for power Generation Expansion (ORSAGE). The analysis
completed as part of this work extends the capabilities of ORSAGE.

3.1 ORSAGE Background
The ORSAGE tool was originally developed to support the siting of large nuclear power plants and,
subsequently, small modular reactors (SMRs). Drawing upon relevant provisions of the Code of Federal
Regulations (as discussed in Section 2), guidance from the Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC), and
siting methodologies developed by organizations such as EPRI and INL, the ORSAGE team assembled a
comprehensive geospatial database designed to evaluate site suitability against a wide range of siting
criteria. See Reference [6] for the detailed methodology that informed the ORSAGE tool. These criteria
encompass geological, environmental, logistical, economic, regulatory, security, and safety considerations
that may influence the viability of a candidate site.
The specific configuration of siting constraints applied within ORSAGE often depends on the
characteristics of the technology being evaluated, including reactor design parameters, cooling

18                                                                                      June 29, 2026

requirements, land-use needs, and operational considerations. In addition, the screening criteria can be
tailored to reflect the objectives, priorities, and risk tolerances of the organization commissioning the
analysis. Thus, despite the use of the term ‘exclusion’ that would imply a go/no-go status for all criteria, it
should be understood that an exclusion is, more often than not, a barrier to siting rather than a rigid
declaration of non-suitability. Some layers, for instance, present primarily financial constraints. Examples
of such layers include slope and distance to appropriate streamflow. With additional capital expense for
site grading, additional water pumping infrastructure, among others, these challenges can be overcome.
Other restrictions such as population setback, proximity to active faults, and proximity to protected lands,
would require substantive changes to existing regulatory guidance and/or additional permitting processes
that could significantly extend project development time. Others still, including seismic activity, landslide
risk, and the presence of open water/wetlands, would require fundamental changes such as the selection
of a different reactor design as a possible work around.
Within ORSAGE, geospatial information is represented primarily as raster datasets, most commonly in
the GeoTIFF format. For siting applications, these datasets are referred to as decision layers. Each
decision layer represents a specific siting criterion and consists of a gridded spatial dataset composed of
100-meter by 100-meter cells. Individual cells are assigned binary values indicating whether the
corresponding location satisfies or fails the criterion being evaluated. Typically, a value of 1 denotes a
location that meets the criterion, while a value of 0 denotes a location that is excluded or unsuitable with
respect to that criterion.
This standardized framework enables multiple decision layers to be combined through overlay and
aggregation operations, allowing analysts to evaluate the cumulative impact of numerous siting
constraints simultaneously. By summing or intersecting the decision layers, ORSAGE can identify
locations that satisfy all applicable criteria, quantify the number of barriers affecting each grid cell, and
systematically narrow large geographic regions to a smaller set of potentially suitable candidate sites.
Locations that satisfy all exclusionary and suitability criteria are ultimately identified as the most
favorable areas for further evaluation and detailed site characterization.
The focus of the present work on siting for microreactor technologies requires a significant increase in
granularity to match the smaller footprint of microreactor sites. To this end, the ORSAGE database has
been updated and resampled to a 30-meter resolution. Where possible, updated data vintages have been
procured to model any changes in land cover, land use, or underlying methodology. Where updated
vintages could not be found or, in some cases, have been restricted in their availability, the original raw
data has been used to regenerate the corresponding decision layer at the increased 30-meter resolution.

3.2 Assumptions
At present, ORSAGE contains well-established siting criteria for a variety of commercial and advanced
reactor technologies. However, the same level of maturity does not yet exist for emerging reactor
concepts such as microreactors. Consequently, the current ORSAGE methodology assumes that siting
requirements are driven primarily by the underlying reactor technology and design configuration rather
than by power output alone.
Under this assumption, a microreactor employing a particular reactor technology is expected to be subject
to substantially the same siting considerations as its larger counterpart. For example, a pressurized water
microreactor would generally be evaluated using the same siting framework as a conventional pressurized
water reactor, with adjustments made only for resource-dependent criteria that scale with generating
capacity. Such criteria may include cooling-water requirements, land-use needs, transmission
interconnection requirements, and other infrastructure considerations directly influenced by reactor power
output.
An important exception to this assumption may arise for criteria associated with accident consequences
and emergency planning considerations. For example, setback distances from population centers and
other demographic constraints are often influenced by projected radiological consequences under

20                                                                                             June 29, 2026

postulated accident scenarios. While microreactors may ultimately demonstrate reduced source terms or
lower offsite consequences compared with larger reactor designs, sufficient regulatory and operational
experience is not yet available to establish alternative population-based screening thresholds for all
microreactor concepts. Therefore, unless supported by validated technical analyses or regulatory
guidance, existing population setback criteria are retained within ORSAGE for microreactor evaluations
as a conservative assumption.
Furthermore, ORSAGE is designed to incorporate technology-specific information whenever credible
design parameters become available from reactor developers. In cases where a developer provides
technical specifications that differ from the default assumptions embedded within the ORSAGE
framework, those parameters can be incorporated directly into the siting analysis. For example, molten
salt reactors (MSRs) are often assumed to tolerate a peak ground acceleration (PGA) of approximately
0.3g. If a reactor developer demonstrates through design analyses that its commercial-scale MSR can
safely withstand a 0.5g PGA seismic event, ORSAGE may apply the same seismic tolerance to the
developer’s corresponding micro-MSR design unless alternative design-specific information is provided.
This approach enables the siting analysis to remain flexible, technology-informed, and responsive to
evolving reactor designs while maintaining a consistent and defensible evaluation framework.

3.5 Summary
To better accommodate the relatively small physical footprint of modern microreactor designs,
the ORSAGE database was updated and resampled to a spatial resolution of 30 meters. Using
this enhanced database, siting suitability was evaluated for five distinct microreactor
technologies across four demonstration clusters.
The results of this preliminary feasibility assessment indicate that the Idaho National Laboratory
cluster contains the highest percentage of suitable land within a 10-mile radius of the evaluated
site center, making it the most favorable location among the clusters considered. In contrast, the
Texas A&M RELLIS Campus cluster exhibits the greatest siting challenges, particularly for
sodium-cooled fast microreactor concepts.
The primary constraints affecting the RELLIS cluster are the extensive floodplain areas within
the study region and the population-based setback requirements associated with the nearby
campus and surrounding communities. Collectively, these factors eliminate approximately 64%
of the land within the 10-mile-radius study area, leaving only about 36% of the cluster suitable
for deployment of the evaluated sodium-cooled fast reactor technology. These findings highlight
the significant influence of local geographic and demographic conditions on microreactor siting
outcomes, even when evaluating technologies with relatively small land and infrastructure
requirements.

4. CONCLUSIONS AND RECOMMENDATIONS

4.1 Conclusions
This study assesses the siting suitability of advanced microreactors proposed under the Reactor Pilot
Program at selected DOE and non-DOE sites using the Oak Ridge Siting Analysis for Power Generation
Expansion (ORSAGE). To perform this analysis, the ORSAGE database of siting criteria is updated to
reflect land use/land cover changes, and to increase the granularity of the analysis to match site sizes for
microreactors. The assessment integrates existing licensing frameworks, microreactor siting criteria,
demonstration site evaluations, INL's reactor-agnostic siting methodology, EPRI siting guidance, DOE
policies, and ORSAGE-based geospatial analyses.
The analysis examined seven pilot reactor projects within four demonstration clusters, each defined by a
10-mile radius around a designated center point and representing eight of the 11 announced projects.
The results demonstrate significant variability in site suitability across the demonstration clusters. The
Idaho National Laboratory cluster exhibited the highest percentage of suitable land, with approximately
97% of the area meeting all siting criteria. Exclusions were limited primarily to protected lands, wetlands,
and localized terrain constraints. In contrast, the Texas A&M RELLIS cluster exhibited the greatest siting
challenges. Population-based setbacks and extensive floodplain coverage reduced the amount of suitable
land to approximately 46% for the Last Energy PWR and 36% for the Aalo Pod SFR. The larger
population setback requirement associated with the sodium-cooled fast reactor accounted for most of the
difference between the two technologies.
The Abilene Christian University cluster demonstrated approximately 65% suitable land availability for
the Natura molten salt microreactor. Population constraints associated with the city of Abilene and
hazardous-facility setbacks associated with Dyess Air Force Base represented the dominant exclusionary

30                                                                                     June 29, 2026

factors, while floodplains contributed additional localized constraints. Finally, the Orangeville cluster
exhibited the lowest overall suitability among the western sites, with approximately 38% of the study area
remaining suitable. The primary barriers were the proximity of Manti-La Sal National Forest and the
rugged topography extending beyond the forest boundaries.
Across all demonstration clusters, the most influential siting constraints were population setbacks,
floodplains, protected lands, hazardous facilities, and topography.
Overall, the analysis demonstrates that microreactor siting is strongly influenced by local environmental,
demographic, infrastructure, and regulatory conditions, even for technologies with relatively small
physical footprints and modest resource requirements. As microreactor technologies continue to mature
and additional design-specific information becomes available, tools such as ORSAGE will provide an
increasingly powerful capability for supporting risk-informed, transparent, and defensible siting decisions
across both DOE-managed and commercial deployment environments.

4.2 Recommended Next Steps
To build on the findings of this report and help address the identified challenges, the following next steps
are proposed:
 § Expand siting studies: Identify and process additional data layers such as soil classification,
 geological formation, hydrogeology, among others that could provide additional site screening
 analysis.
 § Extend analyses across all sites: Implement additional analyses across the sites using additional
 datasets and models.
 § Engage with stakeholders for authoritative information: Engage with stakeholders in the
 industry to gather additional and authoritative information about microreactors that could inform
 robust siting analyses.
