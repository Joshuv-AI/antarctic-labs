PolarNet: Single-Minima Neural Network for Modeling Lyapunov Functions
Yuan Zhong, Jiaxin Cheng, Hefu Ye, Yicong Zhou

Abstract
Learning control strategies with provable stability guarantees continues to be a challenging problem. In this work, we examine a family of training-time behaviors exhibited by existing neural Lyapunov control methods under specific conditions, which can hinder the synthesis of a provably stable controller. We identify the root cause as the lack of neural network architectural guarantees on the learned Lyapunov function, and propose PolarNet, a network architecture that provably addresses these issues by structurally guarantee to have a single critical point. We provide theoretical guarantee regarding the properness and universality of PolarNet for modeling Lyapunov functions, and show that using it as a drop-in replacement in existing neural Lyapunov control methods can effectively circumvent particular difficulties in training. We conduct a set of numerical experiments to verify that PolarNet consistently maintains a single critical point and, when used as a drop-in replacement in existing neural Lyapunov control methods, successfully avoids training failures caused by the lack of architectural guarantees. The code of this paper is available at https://github.com/23-zy/PolarNet .

Introduction

Lyapunov functions are a cornerstone of modern control theory, serving as the primary tool for analyzing the stability of dynamic systems modeled in state space. Despite their near-dominant role, the search for a Lyapunov function has, for over a century, relied heavily on the physical insights and mathematical ingenuity of domain experts. Constructing such functions remains a longstanding open problem in both control theory and mathematics
[
1
]
. Although various analytical methods have been developed within the control community to assist in constructing Lyapunov functions, such as solving the algebraic Riccati equation
[
13
]
and sum-of-squares programming
[
17
]
, none of these approaches are universally applicable. Even for relatively simple systems, these methods may fail to produce a valid Lyapunov function or may incorrectly conclude that no such function exists.
Recent research (e.g.,
[
4
,
21
,
22
,
24
,
23
,
14
]
) has explored an intriguing learning-based alternative that automatically synthesizes provably stable controllers by jointly learning a control strategy and a Lyapunov function, with the latter serving as the stability certificate. Specifically, by embedding the Lyapunov conditions into the learning objective, these methods, collectively known as neural Lyapunov control, are applicable to complex uncertain nonlinear systems without sacrificing a theoretical stability guarantee. This line of work has achieved remarkable empirical success on a wide range of challenging control tasks, demonstrating a promising direction beyond conventional analytical approaches.

However, the reliance on flexible neural networks to learn Lyapunov functions introduces a new challenge that is absent in traditional analytical methods. We identify a family of training-time behaviors that can arise under specific conditions in existing neural Lyapunov control methods, that can potentially prevent the learning process from properly converging and thus blocking the synthesis of a provably stable controller. We trace this issue to the absence of a suitable structural guarantee in the neural networks used to learn Lyapunov functions. Specifically, we show that for a certain class of general nonlinear systems, any valid Lyapunov function must possess exactly one critical point, yet the network architectures commonly adopted in existing methods do not enforce this property structurally. This issue is specific to learning-based paradigms and has therefore been overlooked in the recent shift from analytical methods to learning-based controller synthesis.

To fill this gap, we propose PolarNet, a neural network architecture that provides a rigorous structural guarantee by exploiting an interesting mathematical fact. A key theoretical result is that PolarNet always possesses exactly one critical point, thereby circumventing the training difficulty described above. Furthermore, we show that under a mild condition, PolarNet is capable of constructing Lyapunov functions for any asymptotically stable equilibrium of general dynamic systems. We validate the effectiveness of PolarNet through numerical experiments on both synthetic function approximation tasks and a challenging nonlinear control problem. The results demonstrate that PolarNet consistently maintains a single critical point and, when used as a drop-in replacement in existing neural Lyapunov control methods, successfully avoids training failures caused by the lack of architectural guarantees. To the best of our knowledge, PolarNet is the first architecture that structurally enforces the single-critical-point property for Lyapunov function learning, offering a principled and practical solution to a previously overlooked failure mode in neural Lyapunov control.

2.2
Limitation of existing methods

For a proper Lyapunov function

\dot{V}_{\theta}(x)<0,\forall x\neq 0

{\dot{V}_{\theta}(x)}=\langle\nabla{V_{\theta}(x)},\dot{x}\rangle,\qquad\forall x\in\mathcal{X},

\nabla{V_{\theta}(x)}\neq 0,\qquad\forall x\neq 0.

Recent works of neural Lyapunov control (e.g.,

Method

3.1
Network architecture design

To address the issue described in
Section

2.2
, we propose PolarNet, a network architecture that guarantees a single-critical-point shape in addition to positive-definiteness. By construction, this architecture strictly enforces
eq.

4

Definition 0

(Single pole function)
.

A single pole function is a function
V(x):\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R},\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m},0\in\mathcal{X}
, such that it:

i)~
is
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
smooth
\forall x\in\mathcal{X}
;

ii)~
is positive definite:
V(0)=0
;
V(x)>0,\forall x\neq 0
;

iii)~
has exactly one critical point at
x=0
:
\nabla{V}\neq 0,\forall x\neq 0
;

iv)~
is proper on
\mathcal{X}
, i.e., every sublevel set
\{x\in\mathcal{X}|V(x)\leq c\}
of
V
is compact.
1
1

1

When
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
V
is continuous,
V(x)
being proper is equivalent to
V(x)
being radially unbounded.

We additionally show in

that, there exists a Lyapunov function, which is also a single pole function, for the equilibrium of the general dynamic system

1
.

3.2
Theoretical guarantee

Theorem

2
has shown that all single pole functions

can be formulated as
V(x)=\|\Psi(x)\|^{2},\forall x\in\mathcal{X}\setminus\{0\}

Theorem 4
.

If
\Psi:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}

i)~

\|\cdot\|^{2}

\Psi

ii)~

\Psi(x)\neq\Psi(0)=0,\forall x\neq 0

\|\Psi(x)\|^{2}\neq 0,\forall x\neq 0

\|\cdot\|^{2}\geq 0

\|\Psi(x)\|^{2}

iii)~
Since
\Psi

\Psi(x)=0

iv)~

N(y)=\|y\|^{2}

(with codomain

5
Limitations and future directions

The theoretical guarantee provided by our work assumes a locally Lipschitz dynamic system. When the system is not Lipschitz, PolarNet may still be applied, but does not guarantee in general that it can always represent a valid Lyapunov function for the system (i.e.,
Theorem

5
does not hold in this case). Future works may seek to generalize PolarNet to work with a broader continuum of dynamic systems.

Our method also only focus on continuous time dynamic system and stability in the Lyapunov sense, while discrete systems and other stability definitions are also of interest in many cases. Our work does not consider the case in discrete systems and stability definitions other than the Lyapunov stability.

It should also be noted that PolarNet is specifically designed for controller synthesis task with a single desired equilibrium. For out-of-scope tasks such as finding stability certificate of existing dynamic systems
[
3
]
, PolarNet does not have clear advantages over existing architectures, and may even be less expressive if the intention is to find multiple stable equilibriums. Care should be taken when trying to apply PolarNet in any of these out-of-scope tasks.

6
Related works

Neural Lyapunov control methods follow a common scheme: they simultaneously learn a controller
u_{\theta}
and its Lyapunov function
V_{\theta}
, thereby synthesizing a controller with provable stability guarantees. Several works (
[
4
,
24
,
23
]
) adopt a learner-falsifier architecture, where a falsifier actively searches the state space for points where Lyapunov stability criteria are violated. These points, termed counterexamples, are added to the training set for learning
u_{\theta}
and
V_{\theta}
. The learner then minimizes the empirical risk of Lyapunov stability criteria violation on this training set by using gradient descent to update
u_{\theta}
and
V_{\theta}
. When no counterexample can be found, the synthesized controller
u_{\theta}
is considered stable, and
V_{\theta}
is a valid Lyapunov function certifying its stability.
Among these methods,
[
4
,
24
]
formulate the counterexample generation problem as a
satisfiability modulo theories
(SMT)
[
2
]
problem and solve it using numerical SMT solvers.
In contrast,
[
23
]
propose a
projected gradient descent
(PGD) algorithm to approximately find counterexamples, improving the training efficiency.

Some other methods, such as
[
21
,
14
]
, deviate from this learner-falsifier architecture. They iteratively evaluate the region of attraction (RoA) of
u_{\theta}
during training and construct training sets that cover the current RoA and a surrounding region, thereby maintaining a contiguous RoA and gradually expanding it. Among these,
[
14
]
determines the RoA solely by checking the Lyapunov stability criteria with the current
u_{\theta}
and
V_{\theta}
, whereas
[
21
]
additionally collects numerically simulated state trajectories to assess the stability of initial states more reliably. Most of these methods target continuous-time dynamical systems, while
[
23
]
apply neural Lyapunov control to discrete-time systems.
[
22
]
propose a different approach that trains
u_{\theta}
and
V_{\theta}
on a finite grid over the state space without constructing more sophisticated training sets, and report empirical success.
[
21
,
22
,
6
]
use neural networks that are positive definite by construction to model
V_{\theta}
, eliminating the need for an additional loss term that enforces positive definiteness. To the best of our knowledge, PolarNet is the first architecture that structurally enforces the single-critical-point property for Lyapunov function learning.

7
Conclusion

In this work, we analyzed the cause of a family of training-time difficulties that may present in existing neural Lyapunov control methods, identifying their root cause as lack of neural network architectural guarantees, and propose a new network architecture, PolarNet, that addresses this issue by construction. We derived theoretical guarantees that PolarNet possess the desired mathematical properties by construction, and can be applied to all asymptotically stable equilibrium of locally Lipschitz dynamic systems. We then verified the effectiveness of PolarNet with numerical experiments, demonstrating that PolarNet indeed possesses the desired properties in practice, and can circumvent certain difficulties encountered in the training process of existing neural Lyapunov control method, as we have analyzed. We also noted on the theoretical assumptions we made. Future works may seek to generalize PolarNet to be provably effective in a wider range of scenarios.

Appendix A
Proof of
Theorem

2

The proof goes by first showing that we can smoothly identify different regular level sets (i.e., level sets that do not contain the critical point 0) of
V
, and construct a diffeomorphism
\Phi:L_{c}\times(-c,\infty)\to\mathcal{X}\setminus\{0\}
. We then smoothly map a regular level set to the standard sphere using Morse lemma, thus obtaining
\Psi_{0}
such that
V(x)=\|\Psi_{0}(x)\|^{2},\forall x\in\mathcal{X}\setminus\{0\}
. Finally we extend
\Psi_{0}
smoothly to 0 to obtain a suitable
\Psi
.

A.1
Gradient flow and a global product structure

Since
V
is
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
and has no critical points in
\mathcal{X}\setminus\{0\}
, its gradient
\nabla V
is also
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
and never zero on this set.
Hence the
normalised gradient vector field

X_{\mathrm{grad}}(x)=\frac{\nabla V(x)}{\|\nabla V(x)\|^{2}},\qquad x\in\mathcal{X}\setminus\{0\},

(11)

is well defined and of class
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
.
Let
\phi_{t}(x)
denote the
flow
of
X_{\mathrm{grad}}
, i.e. the unique maximal solution of the ordinary differential equation
\frac{d}{dt}\phi_{t}(x)=X_{\mathrm{grad}}(\phi_{t}(x))
with
\phi_{0}(x)=x
. For each
x
, the flow is a function of
t
defined on a maximal open interval

(T_{-}(x),\,T_{+}(x))
containing
0
(by the
Fundamental Theorem on Flows
in
[
12
]
).
If
T_{+}(x)<\infty
, then the trajectory must leave every compact subset of
\mathcal{X}
as
t\to T_{+}(x)
(by the
Escape Lemma
in
[
12
]
).

Now compute the derivative of
V
along the flow:

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\,V(\phi_{t}(x))=\bigl\langle\nabla V(\phi_{t}(x)),\,\dot{\phi}_{t}(x)\bigr\rangle=\bigl\langle\nabla V(\phi_{t}(x)),\,\frac{\nabla V(\phi_{t}(x))}{\|\nabla V(\phi_{t}(x))\|^{2}}\bigr\rangle=1.

(12)

Therefore,
V(\phi_{t}(x))=V(x)+t
as long as the flow is defined.
Because
V
is proper on
\mathcal{X}
and vanishes only at the origin,
by definition of properness, the sublevel set
S_{c}=\{x\in\mathcal{X}:V(x)\leq c\}
is compact for every
c\geq 0
.

For any
T>0
and any starting point
x\neq 0
, the forward segment

\{\phi_{t}(x):0\leq t\leq T\}
is contained in the compact sublevel set
S_{V(x)+T}
,
which lies inside
\mathcal{X}\setminus\{0\}
because
V\geq V(x)>0
there.
The Escape Lemma then implies that the flow cannot grow to infinity in finite forward time, hence

T_{+}(x)=+\infty
.
Similarly, moving backwards,
V
decreases linearly until it reaches
0
. The trajectory approaches the origin but never escapes
\mathcal{X}
because
V
is proper. Consequently the lower endpoint is exactly
T_{-}(x)=-V(x)
, and the flow exists for all

t\in(-V(x),\,+\infty)
.

For a regular value
c>0
, consider the level set

L_{c}=V^{-1}(c)
, which is a smooth
(m-1)
-dimensional manifold (by the
Regular Level Set Theorem
in
[
12
]
).
The map

\Phi:L_{c}\times(-c,\infty)\to\mathcal{X}\setminus\{0\},\qquad\Phi(p,t)=\phi_{t}(p),\qquad\forall p\in L_{c},t\in(-c,\infty),

(13)

is a bijection, because every
x\neq 0
can be written uniquely as

\phi_{t}(p)
with
p=\phi_{-(V(x)-c)}(x)\in L_{c}
and
t=V(x)-c
.
The inverse is

x\mapsto\bigl(\phi_{-(V(x)-c)}(x),V(x)-c\bigr)
.
Both
\Phi
and its inverse are
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
(the flow of a
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
vector field is
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
), so
\Phi
is a
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
diffeomorphism.
Thus we obtain a global product structure: every level set of
V
is diffeomorphic to
L_{c}
, and the punctured domain
\mathcal{X}\setminus\{0\}
is diffeomorphic to
L_{c}\times(0,\infty)
(with diffeomorphism
\Phi
).

Morse coordinates near the origin

Lemma 0

(Morse Lemma
[
15
]
)
.

Let
p
be a non-degenerate critical point for
f
(where

(y^{1},\cdots,y^{n})
in a neighbourhood
\mathcal{U}
of

y^{i}(p)=0
for all

f=f(p)-(y^{1})^{2}-\cdots-(y^{\lambda})^{2}+(y^{\lambda+1})^{2}+\cdots+(y^{n})^{2}
holds throughout
\mathcal{U}
, where
\lambda
is the index of
f
at
p

Corollary 0

There exists an open neighbourhood
\mathcal{U}_{0}\subset\mathcal{X}
of
0
and a
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
diffeomorphism

\varphi:\mathcal{U}_{0}\to B_{\delta}(0),\qquad B_{\delta}(0)=\{x|\|x\|<\delta\},

(14)

such that

for all

. And
\mathcal{U}_{0}
can be chosen such that
\mathcal{U}_{0}\subset\mathcal{X}
.

Since 0 is a non-degenerate critical point, its index
\lambda
is 0. Let
p=0
and
\lambda=0

1
, we can conclude that there exists a coordinate system
(y^{1},\cdots,y^{n})

\mathcal{U}

\mathcal{U}

\mathcal{U}_{0}

.

In other words, we use

to diffeomorphically map every level set

to

\phi_{a-c}

A.5
Extension to the origin

To obtain a global diffeomorphism (that additionally includes
x=0
), we modify the normalized gradient field
X(x)
near the origin so that its flow coincides with the radial flow coming from
\varphi
. Choose numbers
r_{1},r_{2}
with
\delta/2<r_{1}<r_{2}<\delta
and a smooth function
\eta:[0,\infty)\to[0,1]
such that

\eta(s)=0\;\;(s\leq r_{1}),\qquad\eta(s)=1\;\;(s\geq r_{2}).

(19)

On
\mathcal{U}_{0}\setminus\{0\}
, define:

X_{\mathrm{rad}}(x)=(D\varphi(x))^{-1}\varphi(x),

(20)

By the chain rule,

\langle\nabla V(x),\,X_{\mathrm{rad}}(x)\rangle=\bigl\langle\nabla(\|\varphi(\cdot)\|^{2})(x),\;(D\varphi(x))^{-1}\varphi(x)\bigr\rangle=2\|\varphi(x)\|^{2}=2V(x).

(21)

Normalize it to

Y_{\mathrm{rad}}(x)=X_{\mathrm{rad}}(x)/(2V(x))
, then
\langle\nabla V(x),Y_{\mathrm{rad}}\rangle=1
and
Y_{\mathrm{rad}}
is
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
on
\mathcal{U}_{0}\setminus\{0\}
.
Now we define the blended vector field as:

\widetilde{X}(x)=\begin{cases}\bigl(1-\eta(\|\varphi(x)\|)\bigr)\,Y_{\mathrm{rad}}(x)+\eta(\|\varphi(x)\|)\,X_{\mathrm{grad}}(x),&x\in\mathcal{U}_{0}\setminus\{0\},\\[4.0pt]
X_{\mathrm{grad}}(x),&x\in\mathcal{X}\setminus\mathcal{U}_{0}.\end{cases}

(22)

Since
\langle\nabla V(x),Y_{\mathrm{rad}}\rangle=\langle\nabla V(x),X_{\mathrm{grad}}\rangle=1
, and
\widetilde{X}
is a linear blend of
Y_{\mathrm{rad}}
and
X_{\mathrm{grad}}
, we have:

\langle\nabla V(x),\widetilde{X}(x)\rangle=1,\qquad\forall x\neq 0.

(23)

\widetilde{X}
is
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
on
\mathcal{X}\setminus\{0\}
and coincides with
Y_{\mathrm{rad}}
on the region

\{0<\|\varphi(x)\|\leq r_{1}\}
.

Let
\widetilde{\phi}_{t}
be the flow of
\widetilde{X}
. By the same properness argument as in
Section

A.1
,

\widetilde{\phi}_{t}(x)
exists for all

and stays inside
\mathcal{X}\setminus\{0\}
, and
V(\widetilde{\phi}_{t}(x))=V(x)+t
.
Because
\widetilde{X}=Y_{\mathrm{rad}}
near the origin, the flow there is exactly the radial flow of the Morse chart
\varphi
. In other words, for

0<\|\varphi(x)\|\leq r_{1}
, let

. Using
\dot{\widetilde{\phi}}_{t}=\widetilde{X}(\widetilde{\phi}_{t})
(by definition) and the equality
\widetilde{X}=Y_{\text{rad}}
on the region in question, we obtain

\dot{y}(t)=\frac{y(t)}{2\|y(t)\|^{2}},\qquad y(0)=\varphi(x).

(24)

The solution to

24

y(t)=\frac{\sqrt{\|\varphi(x)\|^{2}+t}}{\|\varphi(x)\|}\,\varphi(x).

(25)

Setting

and using

gives

(26)

hence

\widetilde{\phi}_{a-V(x)}(x)=\varphi^{-1}\!\left(\sqrt{a}\,\frac{\varphi(x)}{\|\varphi(x)\|}\right).

(27)

Now repeat the construction of
\Psi_{0}
using the new flow. Let

\Psi(x)=\sqrt{V(x)}\;h\bigl(\widetilde{\phi}_{a-V(x)}(x)\bigr),\qquad\forall x\neq 0,\;\Psi(0)=0.

(28)

For

with
\|\varphi(x)\|\leq r_{1}
, by

, we have:

\Psi(x)=\|\varphi(x)\|\;\frac{\varphi(x)}{\|\varphi(x)\|}=\varphi(x),\qquad\forall x\in\{x\in\mathcal{X}|V(x)\leq a\}.

(29)

Thus
\Psi
coincides with the
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
diffeomorphism
\varphi
on the whole ball

\{x:\|\varphi(x)\|<r_{1}\}

\Psi
is
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
on that neighbourhood,
\Psi(0)=0

\mathcal{C}^{\infty}

\|\Psi(x)\|^{2}=V(x),\qquad\forall\,x\in\mathcal{X}.

(30)

This completes the proof.

We consider a simplified case where the nominal system dynamics is accurate by setting the nominal system to be exactly the same as the true system.

It should also be noted that this method starts by first synthesizing an LQR
[
13
]
controller for linearization of the full nonlinear system dynamics at
x=0
. For this, we also give the linearization of the system described in
eq.

10
as:

\begin{cases}\dot{x_{1}}=x_{2}\\
\\
\dot{x_{2}}=\dfrac{\pi}{2}x_{1}+10u_{1}-\dfrac{1}{2}u_{2}.\end{cases}

(31)

Note that this method is also effective for uncertain nonlinear systems, this linearization is only used as part of the method, see
[
21
]
for further details.

This experiment is performed on a laptop with Intel i9-13900HX CPU, 32G RAM for around 40 minutes. This experiment is performed on CPU without using CUDA.
