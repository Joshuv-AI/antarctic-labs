Redefining preclinical testing: human-relevant alternatives beyond animal models
Authors: Jieun Jang, Wonjun Yang, Hanseul Jeong, Nam-Kyung Lee, Jong-Gil Park, Wantae Kim, Boksik Cha, Sung-Jin Yoon, Ji-Yoon Noh, Jangwook Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBoth ethical imperatives and scientific limitations increasingly challenge the traditional reliance on animal models for toxicity testing and drug evaluation, particularly in the era of precision medicine. In response, a paradigm shift is underway, marked by the development of advanced in vitro and in silico technologies that can offer human-relevant and mechanistically informed alternatives. This review examines well-established alternatives, such as receptor binding assays, the monocyte activation test, and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays, highlighting their applications, mechanisms, and limitations. We further explore emerging human-relevant technologies that include organoids, organ-on-a-chip systems, microphysiological systems, and artificial intelligence-powered modeling platforms. Special emphasis is placed on immune-integrated microphysiological systems as next-generation platforms to evaluate immunotherapy, vaccine responses, and immune toxicities. These models recapitulate dynamic human physiological processes, such as hematopoiesis and germinal center reactions, beyond the capabilities of traditional animal systems. Collectively, these technologies represent scientifically superior and ethically progressive trajectories for preclinical testing. Their integration into regulatory and industrial workflows requires continued refinement, cross-sector collaboration, and standardization.Keywords: AI-powered modeling, Animal testing alternatives, Microphysiological systems, Organoids, Organ-on-a-Chip

INTRODUCTION

Over the past few decades, biomedical research has relied heavily on animal models to evaluate the toxicity and efficacy of new drugs and chemicals. However, the scientific limitations and ethical concerns of such models have placed this traditional paradigm under increasing scrutiny. A new testing ecosystem is now emerging, driven by advances in cell-based platforms, microphysiological systems, and computational tools.

Alternative animal testing has evolved significantly over the years, and its history is driven by scientific, ethical, and regulatory motivations. Initially, animal testing was the cornerstone of toxicological and biomedical research. However, concerns over animal welfare and the reproducibility of results have led to the development of the 3Rs principle: Reduction, Refinement, and the Replacement of animal use in research (1). This movement has been reinforced by growing regulatory support. For example, the European Union has promoted the gradual expansion of non-animal testing approaches through REACH legislation and cosmetics safety regulations, reflecting a policy-level commitment to scientific validity and industrial sustainability. The FDA Modernization Act 2.0, passed in the United States, officially enables the use of non-animal data to demonstrate drug safety and efficacy. Such legal shifts signify a critical transition toward New Approach Methodologies (NAMs).

A pivotal moment occurred during the COVID−19 pandemic. The pandemic underscored the importance of NAMs for rapid drug development, as traditional animal testing proved too slow, and often ineffective, for species-specific pathogens (2). This urgency has accelerated investments in innovative testing platforms that accurately reflect human biology. Alternative in vivo models, such as zebrafish and Caenorhabditis elegans, have gained popularity because of their reliability and cost-effectiveness in toxicity screening (3). By complementing these models, in vitro platforms, such as cell cultures and tissue-engineered systems, have become increasingly viable to assess nanotoxicity and drug efficacy, thereby offering cost-effective and ethical solutions (1, 4).

Regulatory frameworks, such as the Lautenberg Chemical Safety Act in the U.S. and the EU Chemicals Regulation (REACH), have also encouraged the development and acceptance of non-animal testing methods. However, practical implementation remains a challenge (5). These developments catalyze regulatory changes, while also providing mechanistic insights into human-specific drug responses that traditional models often fail to capture. Taken together, these developments represent more than just methodological updates—they also reflect a paradigm shift. The transition from animal models is both ethically desirable and scientifically necessary, especially as we move toward processes that are more personalized, immunologically complex, and involve high-throughput drug discovery. This review explores the limitations of traditional models, and the emerging innovations poised to transform preclinical testing (Fig. 1).

LIMITATIONS OF ANIMAL MODELS

Animal models have long been the foundation of preclinical research, and are valued for their ability to mimic certain aspects of human biology. However, significant gaps persist in their ability to recapitulate complex human pathophysiology, especially in the context of modern precision medicine. By providing a comparative framework for functional studies and variant interpretation, genetic similarities between animals and humans significantly enhance the accuracy of research findings in understanding human diseases. However, despite these similarities, evolutionary divergence in transcriptional regulation and immune system complexity often limit the translatability of the findings. Although animal models are reliable to predict the effectiveness of treatments in human clinical trials, methodological flaws and differences between animal models and human conditions result in many promising animal studies failing to translate to human success (6, 7).

This issue is particularly pronounced in chronic and multifactorial diseases, such as Alzheimer’s disease, diabetic cardiomyopathy, or autoimmune disorders, where no single model can fully replicate the complexity of human pathology, including genetic background, aging, or environmental exposure. For example, models for diabetic kidney disease and Alzheimer’s disease often fail to incorporate critical disease-modifying variables, such as insulin resistance duration, or age-related factors (8). Similarly, models for pheochromocytoma, paraganglioma, and multiple sclerosis suffer from genetic dissimilarities, tumor latency, and immunological divergence, all of which undermine their predictive validity.

Pulmonary hypertension models, despite diverse formats, frequently fail to recapitulate the full molecular etiology of the disease, while cancer models, including syngeneic, xenograft, and genetically engineered mice, face limitations in genetic fidelity, immune system interaction, and tumor microenvironment replication (9). Moreover, when preclinical standards, such as the ARRIVE guidelines, are neglected, animal models often yield non-reproducible or misleading results, further compromising their utility in translational research (6, 10).

These limitations have served as catalysts for the development of alternative human-relevant methods, which in modern toxicology and pharmacology are increasingly being viewed as more predictive and ethically responsible tools. As precision medicine demands models that account for inter-individual variability, immune complexity, and organ-level pathophysiology, traditional animal models fail to capture the heterogeneity of human responses. Indeed, meta-analyses suggest that fewer than 10% of animal studies translate into successful outcomes in human trials, underscoring the urgent need for more human-representative testing strategies (11).

ESTABLISHED IN VITRO SUBSTITUTES

Several well-established in vitro methods have been developed and validated to reduce or replace the use of animals in safety testing, pharmacology, and immunogenicity assessments (Table 1). Among these, receptor binding assays (RBA), the Monocyte Activation Test (MAT), and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA) have played prominent roles. These methods align with the 3Rs principle, while also offering more human-relevant and ethically sound testing strategies. However, their limitations dictate a cautious, complementary approach, rather than complete substitution.

Established alternative test methods and their advantages and limitations

RBAs are widely used to quantify the interactions between ligands and receptors, particularly in drug discovery and toxicological screening. They offer high-throughput capability and specificity, while avoiding interspecies extrapolation (12). RBAs have become essential tools to study G-protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) and nuclear receptors (13, 14). RBAs typically quantify ligand–receptor interactions based on binding kinetics, such as dissociation constant (Kd) values, by employing radiolabeled or fluorescence-tagged ligands. These methods mimic the physiological interactions between ligands and GPCRs or nuclear hormone receptors, providing insights into their binding affinity and specificity. However, several technical challenges remain unaddressed. Factors such as non-specific binding, ligand depletion, fluorescence quenching in nonradioactive assays, or variability in receptor expression can limit reproducibility and data interpretation (15, 16). These assays also lack the physiological context of complex cellular and systemic responses, which limits their predictive accuracy for in vivo outcomes (17).

MAT serves as an alternative to the Rabbit Pyrogen Test (RPT) and the Limulus Amebocyte Lysate (LAL) assays by detecting a wider range of pyrogens, including non-endotoxin pyrogens (NEPs). It evaluates the release of interleukin–6 (IL−6) or other cytokines from human monocytes in response to pyrogens (18, 19). Mechanistically, MAT activates human monocytes via innate immune pattern recognition receptors, most notably Toll-like receptors (TLRs), such as TLR4 in response to endotoxins, triggering cytokine secretion (e.g., IL−1β, IL−6) as a quantifiable immune response (20). MAT has demonstrated high sensitivity and specificity in detecting biologically relevant contaminants in parenteral products, including therapeutic monoclonal antibodies. Despite these strengths, the test is limited by donor variability, serum composition, heat inactivation effects, and challenges in distinguishing certain material-mediated pyrogens (21, 22). Regulatory acceptance also remains inconsistent across global agencies, although harmonization efforts are ongoing.

ELISA has become a foundational tool to evaluate vaccine potency, immunogenicity, and host antibody responses. As an alternative to in vivo challenge tests, ELISAs provide reliable quantification of antigen-specific antibodies, and have been extensively used in assessing tetanus vaccines (23). ELISA operates through high-affinity antigen–antibody binding, where an enzyme-linked secondary antibody catalyzes a chromogenic or fluorogenic substrate (e.g., the HRP/TMB system), producing a signal that is proportional to the antigen concentration. This quantitative output allows precise measurement of analyte-specific humoral responses (24). Variants, such as biotin–streptavidin-enhanced ELISAs, offer improvements in sensitivity and reproducibility (25). However, cross-reactivity, lot-to-lot variation in reagents, and differences between commercial kits can influence ELISA results (26). Furthermore, while ELISA measures antibody presence, it does not directly assess functional neutralization capacity, often necessitating supplemental assays.

Beyond these assays, other classical in vitro methods—such as skin irritation and corrosion tests using reconstructed human epidermis (OECD TG 431 and TG 439), or ocular irritation assays—like the Bovine Corneal Opacity and Permeability (BCOP) and Isolated Chicken Eye (ICE) tests—have gained regulatory traction. These tests use three-dimensional (3D) tissue models and quantifiable endpoints to replace the historically used Draize rabbit test, demonstrating a strong correlation with human data in specific contexts (27). Although none of these methods can single-handedly replace animal studies across all domains, their combinatorial use offers a powerful toolkit to reduce reliance on animal models. Integrated testing strategies (ITS) and defined approaches (DA) that combine these in vitro methods with in silico tools and exposure modeling are increasingly seen to be the most effective pathways toward full animal replacement in toxicology and pharmacology.

EMERGING TECHNOLOGIES IN HUMAN-RELEVANT TESTING

In addition to the traditional animal replacement methods, advanced alternative testing methods are being developed by integrating and enhancing various technologies. These cutting-edge approaches aim to accurately recapitulate human physiological responses and pathophysiology, thereby enhancing their translational relevance in preclinical research. Central to these innovations are complex in vitro models (CIVMs), which encompass 3D cell cultures, human induced pluripotent stem cell (hiPSC)- or patient cell-derived organoids, and microfluidic device-based co-culture systems, such as Organ-on-a-Chip (OoC), and MPS (32). Together with in silico and artificial intelligence (AI)-based modeling platforms, these technologies represent a transformative paradigm in toxicological science.

Organoids: self-assembled miniature organs

Organoids are self-organizing stem cells in 3D cultures that exhibit organ-like structures. Compared to two-dimensional (2D) cell cultures, 3D organoid cultures provide cellular responses that are closer to real physiological conditions. Organoids are typically generated from human pluripotent stem cells (hPSCs), including both embryonic stem cells (ESCs) and induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs), which are capable of indefinite self-renewal and multilineage differentiation. Mechanistically, organoids are derived from pluripotent stem cells or adult progenitor cells that undergo spatial self-organization and lineage-specific differentiation in a 3D extracellular matrix (ECM), such as Matrigel, under defined growth factor gradients. This environment simulates morphogenetic processes similar to those of in vivo organogenesis (33). Various organoids have been developed, including those for the intestine, lungs, liver, kidneys, brain, and thyroid. These organoids have proven valuable in modeling organ development, disease progression, and drug responses. The integration of CRISPR−Cas-based genetic editing further extends their utility in functional genomics and disease modeling. Advances in high-throughput screening using organoids are closely related to 3D bioprinting technologies. Bioprinting methods for organoids include inkjet-based, laser-assisted, extrusion-based, and photo-assisted bioprinting. These allow spatial control over cell distribution and matrix composition, enabling the fabrication of more consistent and scalable tissue models.

Despite their promise, organoids are at present unstable tools that cannot replace animal testing. The primary issue is that organoids lack a vascular system, which prevents them from achieving terminal growth, and accurately mimicking in vivo drug delivery. Furthermore, replicating the complexity and heterogeneity of the whole organs in organoids is challenging. Long differentiation times, such as the over six months required for retina organoid development, also limit practical application in routine toxicity assessments. To enhance functional fidelity, researchers are increasingly combining organoids with MPS, incorporating mechanical stimuli, perfusion, and immune components. Combining organoids with MPS or advanced bioprinting techniques can improve their functionality and reliability, potentially making them more suitable for toxicity testing while reducing the need for animal models (34).

Organ-on-a-Chip and MPS: engineering physiological complexity

OoC technology aims to replicate the environmental, functional, and interrelated characteristics of organs and tissues. These platforms consist of microfluidic compartments lined with primary human cells or organoids that simulate the architecture and biochemical environment of target organs. These devices function by integrating microfluidic channels, flexible membranes, and biosensors, enabling dynamic perfusion, mechanical strain (e.g., stretching and shear stress), and compartmentalized co-culture of multiple cell types (35). This architecture allows the real-time monitoring of organotypic responses under conditions that are physiologically relevant. One of the most sophisticated examples is the multi-organ MPS (36), where a four-organ chip incorporating intestinal, liver, brain, and kidney tissues derived from autologous iPSCs was co-cultured under continuous flow. Notably, this co-culture model was maintained for over 14 days in a common medium deprived of tissue-specific growth factors, highlighting its physiological compatibility and potential for longitudinal studies.

Various OoC platforms have emerged as physiologically relevant models of specific tissue and disease states (Table 2). These microfluidic devices replicate the critical structural and functional characteristics of human organs, and offer novel approaches to assess drug responses and toxicity profiles. For example, lung-on-a-chip systems replicate the alveolar–capillary interface using two parallel microchannels separated by a microporous membrane, thereby enabling realistic modeling of pulmonary physiology. These platforms have been successfully used to evaluate the toxicity of airborne nanoparticles (37), assess the safety of T–cell bispecific antibodies (38), and screen potential antiviral agents against COVID−19 (39). Similarly, liver-on-a-chip platforms, which integrate hepatocytes with Kupffer cells, provide dynamic environments for drug metabolism studies, and have effectively recapitulated human-specific hepatotoxic responses to drugs such as diclofenac (40, 41).

Representative Organ-on-a-Chip models and applications

In neurobiology, brain-on-a-chip models incorporate differentiated human neural stem cells co-cultured with endothelial-lined perfusion channels to mimic neurovascular interactions. These systems have been employed to simulate infections of the central nervous system, such as those caused by Cryptococcus neoformans, and have facilitated the evaluation of neurotherapeutic candidates (42). Blood vessel-on-a-chip technologies have enabled researchers to investigate angiogenesis and endothelial dysfunction under mechanical stress. These models have replicated disease conditions such as Hutchinson–Gilford progeria syndrome, and have enabled drug screening using patient-derived renal carcinoma cells (43, 44).

These platforms provide strong evidence of clinical mimicry, especially in drug development and personalized medicine, by reproducing organ-specific responses that are otherwise unattainable in animal models. Nevertheless, the complexity and specificity required for each organ system result in the mass production of OoCs remaining challenging. Substantial hurdles that remain are the standardization across organ types, robustness of fabrication processes, and costs (45).

In silico and AI-powered modeling

Prediction of the biological activity of drug candidate toxins using simulation software based on databases or chemical structures is a rapidly advancing field. These in silico models leverage single-cell transcriptomics, proteomics, and machine learning algorithms to simulate tissue-level responses to chemicals and drugs. Computational models simulate molecular interactions, cellular signaling networks, or organ-level physiology using rule-based algorithms, quantitative structure–activity relationships (QSAR), and systems pharmacology. AI-powered platforms incorporate supervised and unsupervised learning techniques to uncover latent patterns across multidimensional datasets, refining predictive outputs for toxicity or efficacy (46). A striking example is the use of iPSC-derived cardiomyocyte electrophysiological datasets to predict cardiotoxicity (47).

Complementing these biological platforms, (AI and machine learning technologies now play central roles in accelerating the transition from traditional animal models. For example, AstraZeneca successfully integrated AI models for early safety screening, reportedly reducing Phase I trial attrition rates to zero by preemptively identifying toxic compounds (48). Similarly, Merck has phased out routine animal testing, and is collaborating with Quris, a biotechnology company that is developing patient-on-a-chip systems integrated with machine learning-based safety profiling tools. Another notable example is VeriSIM Life, which has developed “digital animal” simulations. These models combine systems biology with AI-driven algorithms to predict drug efficacy and toxicity, thereby substantially enhancing the early-stage drug candidate triage (49).

These approaches have also been integrated into personalized medical pipelines. Companies like Sophia Genetics and OncoDNA combine patient-specific genomic and clinical data to tailor drug response models, which are particularly valuable for tumor-targeted therapies, such as CAR−T cells. Despite these advances in in vitro and in silico modeling, systemic toxicity remains challenging. Regulatory guidelines, such as those from the Food and Drug Administration, mandate at least one small and two large animal models for safety assessment. However, data from immune MPS can enhance in silico tools to predict complex, in vivo-like responses, potentially reducing reliance on animal testing (50).

Practical applications across biomedical sectors

These cutting-edge technologies have been translated into diverse industrial applications. In toxicology, MPS and in silico models enable the early prediction of organ-specific toxicities, such as cardiotoxicity, hepatotoxicity, and neurotoxicity, streamlining candidate selection while reducing development costs. In personalized medicine, patient-derived cells and genomic data are used to predict individual drug responses, and facilitate the design of precision therapy. These platforms also offer critical solutions to evaluate therapies for rare and immune-mediated diseases, where conventional animal models lack predictive validity. Their use in drug repurposing, gene therapy validation, and safety assessments of next-generation biologics underscores the growing industrial reliance on integrated human-relevant testing systems. Collectively, these cases demonstrate the translational value and scalability of organoid–MPS–AI hybrid technologies.

FUTURE OUTLOOK AND INTEGRATION

Immune responses are crucial to understand a wide array of biological processes, from organ development to disease progression and inflammation-related pathophysiology. Recent advances in immune system modeling, such as the development of lymphoid follicle chips, a critical structure for B and T lymphocyte interactions and antibody production, highlight this importance. For example, a commercial influenza vaccine increased follicle size and number, promoted plasma cell formation, and enhanced antibody production in this model, suggesting that in the future, such approaches could potentially replace animal testing for vaccine development (51).

The paradigm for cancer treatment is increasingly shifting towards immunotherapy, with a variety of therapeutics being actively developed. In this context, immune organ chips have emerged as valuable tools to evaluate immunotherapy efficacy. When combined with tumor cell spheroids or patient-derived tumor organoids, which are well-established patient-specific preclinical models, these systems can significantly improve the translational relevance of immunotherapy testing (51). Cell-based therapies, such as CAR−T cells, have attracted significant attention due to their potential for complete remission in certain leukemia cases. However, their use poses a major safety concern, because of the possibility of fatal toxicity resulting from uncontrolled immune activation. Designing appropriate nonclinical studies to evaluate the safety and efficacy of CAR−T therapies remains challenging, as their pharmacodynamic and pharmacokinetic behavior differs markedly between humans and animals. Human tumor xenografts in immunocompromised mice, such as the NOD−SCID−IL2rgnull (NSG) mouse strain, are commonly used models. However, these models possess a critical limitation: non-tumor tissues are of murine origin, which because of the limited antigen homology between species, cannot accurately reflect the toxicity of CAR−T-cells against normal human tissues (52). In addition, these models fail to capture the complex immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) or the developmental dynamics of human T–cells, such as the generation of memory cells.

To address these limitations, MPSs have been developed to better replicate human TME. These models integrate tumor organoids or engineered target cells with immune components under perfused culture conditions. Studies have demonstrated that MPS systems and organoids can express physiologically relevant levels of cell surface targets that are often absent in traditional 2D well-plate models, making them more predictive of in vivo behavior (38, 53). In CAR−T cell development, such models enable functional evaluation using antigen-expressing target cells for efficacy screening, and may serve as reliable alternatives to murine in vivo studies. Researchers are increasingly incorporating immune cells into organoids or MPS to predict the immunotoxicity of new drugs or biologics, and to conduct animal replacement studies for immunotherapy evaluation (53). These immune-integrated models are also being explored for broader disease contexts beyond cancer immunotherapy, including autoimmune disorders, allergy models, and viral infections, in which conventional animal models often fail to mimic human immune complexity and response specificity. Hematopoiesis, the developmental process of lymphocytes and blood cells, primarily occurs in the bone marrow and thymus, and nearly all organs harbor tissue-resident immune systems. High-fidelity modeling necessitates the replication of such environments in vitro.

A 3D culture system is critical to recapitulate germinal center reactions, a primary mechanism of the antigen-specific humoral response (54). Accordingly, bone marrow-on-a-chip (BMOC) models have been developed using mouse and human cells. These platforms maintain hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs) and support multilineage differentiation, thereby enabling advanced applications in hematopoietic toxicity testing and immune system development studies (55, 56). Furthermore, lymph node-on-a-chip (LNoC) models have been investigated for their ability to evaluate antigen-specific immune responses. These systems can be multiplexed with other MPS units, enabling integrated, multi-organ immune analyses (57). More complex multi-organ MPS platforms with circulating immune cells are also under development. These next-generation immune-integrated models have strong potential to replace humanized animal models by better capturing human-specific immune interactions, systemic immune responses, and inter-organ crosstalk (58).

Immune-relevant MPS technologies represent a powerful frontier to replace animal models, particularly in immuno-oncology, vaccine development, and immunotoxicity testing. As these systems continue to evolve in complexity and physiological relevance, they are expected to become indispensable to preclinical pipelines, offering human-specific insights that traditional animal models often fail to provide. Collectively, these innovations indicate a future in which complex human-relevant immune models, ranging from lymphoid organ-on-a-chip systems to integrated MPS platforms with circulating immune cells, may fundamentally redefine preclinical testing. Although the full regulatory replacement of animal models remains a long-term goal, these advanced technologies offer a scientifically superior and ethically progressive path. Continuous refinement, standardization, and global regulatory alignment are essential to integrate these systems into mainstream drug development pipelines.

SUMMARY

Over the past decade, preclinical testing has undergone a transformative shift driven by scientific, ethical, and regulatory pressures to reduce reliance on traditional animal models. This review outlines the limitations of animal-based approaches, particularly their inability to replicate complex human pathophysiology. In response, a diverse array of alternative methods, ranging from classical in vitro assays, such as ELISA and MAT, to advanced technologies, such as organoids, Organ-on-a-Chip systems, and AI-powered in silico platforms, have emerged as powerful tools for human-relevant toxicity and efficacy assessments. While each approach presents unique advantages and limitations, their integration into defined testing strategies and complex human models is reshaping the future of biomedical research. Ultimately, the path forward lies in harmonizing these innovations under a regulatory framework that values both scientific rigor, and ethical responsibility.
