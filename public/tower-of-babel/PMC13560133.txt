Pharmacokinetics, safety, and efficacy of fuzuloparib in combination with abiraterone acetate and prednisone in patients with metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer: a phase 1 dose escalation and expansion study
Authors: Tao Dai, Zhenzhou Xu, Haitao Wang, Weiqing Han, Dahong Zhang, Kaifa Tang, Xinshuai Wang, Jian Huang, Chaohong He, Xianfeng Zhou, Dapeng Zhang, Chun Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStudies have shown that poly (adenosine diphosphate-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitors can potentiate the antitumor effect of abiraterone acetate plus prednisone (AA-P) in metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC). This study evaluated the pharmacokinetics, safety, and efficacy of fuzuloparib, a PARP inhibitor, in combination with AA-P in mCRPC patients who had not received prior novel hormonal agents.MethodsThis was a dose escalation and expansion study. In dose escalation, eligible patients received fuzuloparib at 100 or 150 mg BID for 5 days, followed by fuzuloparib plus AA-P (abiraterone acetate 1000 mg QD, prednisone 5 mg BID) in 28-day treatment cycles. The higher tolerated dose of fuzuloparib was selected for dose expansion. In dose expansion, two treatment groups were planned. The fuzuloparib group received fuzuloparib for 5 days, and the abiraterone group received AA-P for 5 days. Both groups were then treated with the combination therapy.ResultsA total of 39 patients were enrolled and treated. As of July 12, 2023, the median follow-up time was 23.2 months (range, 3.9−44.3). No obvious drug-drug interaction was observed between 150 mg fuzuloparib and AA-P, and no dose-limiting toxicities were identified. Treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs) occurred in 36 (92.3%) patients, of which 20 (51.3%) reported grade ≥ 3 TRAEs. At the end of Week 12, prostate-specific antigen (PSA) response rate was 71.8% (95% CI, 55.1−85.0). The median time to PSA progression was 19.4 months (95% CI, 11.3−27.8). Objective response rate was 60% and disease control rate was 90% among patients with evaluable target lesions. The median duration of response was 8.1 months (95% CI, 4.6−31.5), and the median time to radiographic progression was 27.9 months (95% CI, 14.0−not reached). Time to disease progression was generally longer in patients with homologous recombination repair gene mutations, including those with BRCA mutations.ConclusionsFuzuloparib plus AA-P had an acceptable safety profile and showed promising efficacy among mCRPC patients.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov, NCT04108247 (Registered on September 26, 2019).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12885-026-16182-0.Keywords: Abiraterone acetate, Fuzuloparib, PARP inhibitor, Metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer

Introduction

Prostate cancer is one of the most diagnosed cancers in men worldwide. The growth of prostate cancer cells is characteristically androgen-dependent [1, 2]. Standard hormone treatment includes androgen deprivation therapy that suppresses the synthesis of testosterone, and androgen receptor antagonists that block the binding of androgens to androgen receptors [3, 4]. However, metastatic prostate cancer often progresses to an advanced form known as metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC) approximately 12 to 24 months after androgen deprivation therapy [5–7]. Recent research has clarified the molecular basis of this progression, highlighting lineage plasticity and neuroendocrine differentiation as drivers of resistance [8, 9]. Furthermore, the tumor microenvironment significantly promotes metastasis [10, 11]. Concurrently, specific signaling axes have been identified as critical for epithelial-mesenchymal transition and metastatic potential, alongside the accumulation of diverse genetic aberrations [12, 13].

Abiraterone acetate is an oral inhibitor of 17α-hydroxylase/C17-20 lyase (CYP17A1), a steroidal enzyme involved in androgen biosynthesis. Clinical studies have demonstrated abiraterone’s efficacy in lowering prostate-specific antigen (PSA) and testosterone levels, thereby improving survival in men with metastatic CRPC [14–16]. Following these findings, abiraterone acetate in combination with prednisone (AA-P) was approved by the Food and Drug Administration for the treatment of metastatic CRPC in 2011 [17]. Emerging evidence suggested that the addition of poly (adenosine diphosphate-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitors to AA-P further enhances antitumor efficacy.

PARP inhibitors act by preventing PARP-dependent repair of DNA single-strand breaks, thereby promoting the formation of double-strand breaks [18, 19]. This process induces synthetic lethality, particularly in patients with homologous recombination repair (HRR) deficiencies, leading to cancer cell death [19–21]. Approximately 20% of metastatic CRPC patients harbor germline and somatic HRR mutations, most frequently involving breast cancer susceptibility gene (BRCA) and ataxia-telangiectasia mutated gene [22, 23]. Recent studies indicated that PARP enzymes interact with the androgen receptor transcriptional pathway, contributing to the resistance to hormone therapies in metastatic CRPC [24–26]. Therefore, the synergy between androgen receptor targeting and PARP inhibition suggests a potential benefit for the combination, even in patients with intact HRR function.

Fuzuloparib is a novel oral PARP inhibitor that has demonstrated efficacy in multiple cancer types. A previous phase 1 trial had demonstrated fuzuloparib’s single-agent antitumor activity in advanced solid malignancies, with a maximum tolerated dose of 150 mg twice daily (BID) [27]. Here, we conducted this phase 1 dose escalation and dose expansion study, aiming to evaluate the pharmacokinetics (PK), safety, and efficacy of fuzuloparib in combination with standard AA-P in patients with metastatic CRPC who had not previously received novel hormonal agents.

Methods

Study population

Eligible patients were males aged 18 years or older with histologically or cytologically confirmed prostate adenocarcinoma and at least one metastatic lesion. Patients were required to have undergone surgical castration or under medical castration with a serum testosterone level of ≤ 50 ng/dL (1.73 nmol/L) at screening. Those who had not undergone bilateral orchiectomy were required to receive ongoing treatment with luteinizing hormone-releasing hormone agonists throughout the study. Progressive disease at study entry was defined according to one or more of the following criteria under androgen deprivation therapy: (1) PSA progression per Prostate Cancer Working Group 3 (PCWG3) criteria, (2) disease progression per Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) version 1.1, or (3) bone disease progression per PCWG3 criteria. Further eligibility criteria included Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status of 0 or 1, a life expectancy of at least 3 months, and adequate organ function. Key exclusion criteria included prior exposure to PARP inhibitors, abiraterone acetate or other CYP17 inhibitors, novel androgen receptor antagonists such as enzalutamide, apalutamide, darolutamide, rezvilutamide and proxalutamide (first-generation androgen antagonists were permitted), or ketoconazole; any anti-tumor therapies within 4 weeks before washout (6 weeks for bicalutamide); use of any medications that may reduce PSA levels within 4 weeks before dosing; use of any strong CYP3A4 inhibitors or inducers within 14 days before dosing; radiographically confirmed brain metastases; systolic blood pressure of ≥ 160 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure of ≥ 95 mmHg; or a history of pituitary or adrenal gland dysfunction.

The protocol and all amendments were approved by the Ethics Committees at the study centers. The study was conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki, Guidelines for Good Clinical Practice, and local laws and regulations. All subjects provided written informed consent.

Study design and treatment

This was a multicenter, open-label, dose escalation and expansion phase 1 study of fuzuloparib in combination with AA-P in patients with metastatic CRPC who had not received prior novel androgen receptor targeted therapy (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT04108247).

The dose escalation stage adopted a 3 + 3 design. Eligible patients first entered a PK run-in period, where they orally received fuzuloparib at 100 or 150 mg BID for 5 days, followed by a combination treatment period, where they received fuzuloparib at the same dosage and frequency plus oral abiraterone acetate 1000 mg once daily (QD) and prednisone 5 mg BID in 28-day treatment cycles. Dose-limiting toxicities were monitored during the PK run-in period and the first cycle of combination therapy (33 days). Dose-limiting toxicities were defined as treatment-related non-hematological and hematological toxicities, graded according to the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 5.0. Specifically, non-hematological toxicities included grade ≥ 3 non‑hematological toxicities leading to hospitalization and grade ≥ 3 nausea, vomiting, or diarrhea that persists for ≥ 3 days despite symptomatic treatment. Hematological toxicities included grade 3 thrombocytopenia lasting ≥ 7 days or accompanied by clinically significant bleeding, grade 4 thrombocytopenia, grade ≥ 3 febrile neutropenia, grade 4 neutropenia, and grade 4 anemia. The dose selection rationale was based on a previous phase 1 trial of fuzuloparib in patients with advanced solid tumors, in which the recommended phase 2 dose of fuzuloparib was established as 150 mg twice daily [27]. Given the good safety profile of fuzuloparib and the low likelihood of drug-drug interactions between fuzuloparib and abiraterone acetate, 100 mg and 150 mg twice daily were selected for dose escalation in this study.

After completing the dose escalation stage, the higher tolerated dose of fuzuloparib was selected for dose expansion. Two treatment groups, the fuzuloparib group and the abiraterone group, were planned for the dose expansion stage. Patients in the fuzuloparib group received fuzuloparib monotherapy for 5 days, while patients in the abiraterone group received AA-P for 5 days during the PK run-in period. Both groups were then treated with the combination therapy until disease progression or intolerable toxicity. The five‑day run‑in design was implemented specifically to assess the potential drug‑drug interaction between fuzuloparib and AA-P. During this run‑in period, the steady‑state PK data were obtained for either fuzuloparib or AA-P when administered alone. The half‑life of fuzuloparib is approximately 10 to 15 h, and that of abiraterone acetate is approximately 12 ± 5 h. Based on these pharmacokinetic properties, steady state is expected to be achieved after approximately four days (five half-lives) of continuous dosing. Therefore, a five-day run-in period was chosen to assess steady‑state PK on Day 5 of monotherapy. This design allowed us to compare the PK profiles of fuzuloparib or AA-P alone and in combination, thereby enabling a robust evaluation of their interaction. Moreover, the five-day run-in period also allowed for a self-controlled study design. When fuzuloparib and AA-P were administered alone, the PK profiles for each regimen were established, serving as a self-control when compared with the combination therapy. Consequently, patient randomization to the two run-in cohorts was not required.

Endpoints and assessments

The primary objectives were to evaluate the PK characteristics and safety profile of fuzuloparib in combination with AA-P. Blood samples for PK analysis were collected on Day 5 of the PK run-in period and on Day 5 of the first treatment cycle. The plasma concentrations of fuzuloparib and abiraterone were quantified to calculate key PK parameters, including minimum plasma concentration at steady state (Cmin, ss), maximum plasma concentration at steady state (Cmax, ss), time to reach Cmax, ss (Tmax, ss), area under the concentration-time curve from dosing to the last measurable concentration (AUC0-t), and area under the concentration-time curve at steady state (AUCss). Safety was monitored throughout the study via assessment of adverse events (AEs), laboratory tests, vital signs, physical examinations, ECOG performance status, and 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG).

Secondary objectives included the evaluation of efficacy and the determination of the recommended phase 2 dose. Efficacy endpoints included PSA response rate, time to PSA progression, objective response rate (ORR), disease control rate (DCR), duration of response (DoR), and radiographic progression-free survival (rPFS). The PSA response rate was defined as the proportion of patients achieving a ≥ 50% reduction in serum PSA at the end of Week 12 compared to baseline. Time to PSA progression was defined as the time from the first dose to the first PSA progression per PCWG3 criteria. PSA levels were monitored on Day 15 of the first treatment cycle, Day 1 of cycles 2−4, and once every three cycles thereafter. Disease progression was evaluated using RECIST version 1.1 and PCWG3. Tumor response per RECIST version 1.1 was assessed via computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging. Bone disease progression per PCWG3 was assessed via bone scan. Radiographic assessments occurred on Day 1 of treatment cycle 4 and once every three cycles during the treatment period. A complete response (CR) or partial response (PR) required confirmation at least 4 weeks after the initial assessment. DoR was defined as the time from the first CR or PR to disease progression or death. rPFS was defined as the time from the first dose to radiographic disease progression or death.

The association between efficacy and the mutation status of HRR genes was also explored.

Statistical analysis

During the 3 + 3 dose escalation stage, 6 to 12 patients were enrolled. Assuming that the geometric mean ratio of AUC or Cmax in combination therapy to monotherapy was 2.0, the tolerance probability was 0.8, and the within-subject standard deviation was 0.2 to 0.3, 16 patients each in the fuzuloparib group (including those who received the same dosage in dose escalation) and abiraterone group were required for the dose expansion stage.

PK was analyzed in all enrolled patients who had received at least one dose of study treatment and had at least one PK concentration or parameter data. Patients treated with the same dosage of fuzuloparib in dose escalation and dose expansion were combined for PK analysis. Plasma concentrations of fuzuloparib and abiraterone were measured, and PK parameters were calculated and summarized using descriptive statistics. Natural log transformed AUCss and Cmax, ss of fuzuloparib and abiraterone were analyzed using a fit mixed effects model, with treatment method (combination therapy or monotherapy) as the fixed effect and patients as the random effect. The geometric mean ratios of AUCss and Cmax, ss between combination therapy and monotherapy and the corresponding 90% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated.

Safety was analyzed in all treated patients and those in the dose escalation stage with completed dose limiting toxicity assessment. Safety data were summarized descriptively. Efficacy analyses were conducted on all treated patients, all treated patients who had non-missing PSA data at baseline and at least one PSA data at post-dose, and all treated patients with at least one evaluable lesion per RECIST version 1.1 and had at least one radiographic assessment after treatment administration. PSA response rate, ORR, and DCR were calculated as proportions, with corresponding 95% CIs estimated via the Clopper-Pearson method. Time to PSA progression, DoR, and rPFS were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method, and the associated 95% CIs were calculated using the Brookmeyer Crowley method.

PK parameters were calculated using Phoenix WinNonlin (version 8.1 or higher). Other analyses were conducted using SAS (version 9.2 or higher).

Results

Patients

Between September 2019 and July 2020, 39 eligible patients were enrolled and treated. Six were assigned to the dose escalation stage (n = 3 in 100 mg dose; n = 3 in 150 mg dose), followed by 33 in the dose expansion stage (n = 13 in fuzuloparib group; n = 20 in abiraterone group). Based on the dose escalation results, fuzuloparib at 150 mg BID was selected for dose expansion; consequently, a total of 36 patients received fuzuloparib at 150 mg BID. As of July 12, 2023, all 39 patients discontinued study treatment, primarily due to disease progression (Supplemental Table S1). No patients discontinued treatment during the run-in period. The median follow-up time was 23.2 months (range, 3.9−44.3).

Demographics and baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The median age was 70.0 years (interquartile range, 64.0−76.0). Most patients had an ECOG performance status of 1 (84.6%) and a Gleason score of ≥ 8 (69.2%). At baseline, the median PSA level was 28.7 ng/mL (interquartile range, 9.6−90.8). Regarding genetic profile, 21 (53.8%) harbored HRR mutations and 14 (35.9%) had BRCA mutations. All patients had previously received androgen deprivation therapy, while 11 (28.2%) and 3 (7.7%) patients had received prior chemotherapy and radiotherapy, respectively.

PK

PK data for fuzuloparib are presented in Supplemental Table S2. For 150 mg fuzuloparib (n = 16; including those in dose escalation and those from the fuzuloparib group in dose expansion), the geometric mean Cmax, ss was 6394 ng/mL under combination therapy with AA-P and 6913 ng/mL when fuzuloparib was administered alone. The geometric mean ratio of Cmax, ss between the combination therapy and monotherapy was 0.92 (90% CI, 0.87−0.99; Table 2). The geometric mean AUCss for 150 mg fuzuloparib was 50.6 h*µg/mL in combination therapy and 60.5 h*µg/mL in monotherapy, with an AUCss geometric mean ratio of 0.84 (90% CI, 0.79−0.89). Exposure to abiraterone in the combination and monotherapy was evaluated in the dose expansion abiraterone group (n = 20). The geometric mean ratio for Cmax was 0.97 (90% CI, 0.75−1.24), and that for AUCss was 0.84 (90% CI, 0.67−1.04).

Safety

All 39 patients received study treatment and were included in the safety analysis. Dose limiting toxicities were not identified in the dose escalation stage. Treatment-emergent AEs (TEAEs) occurred in 38 (97.4%) patients, with 28 (71.8%) experiencing grade ≥ 3 TEAEs (Table 3). Treatment-related AEs (TRAEs) were reported by 36 (92.3%) patients, of whom 20 (51.3%) experienced grade ≥ 3 TRAEs. Serious AEs (SAEs) occurred in 20 (51.3%) patients, all from the fuzuloparib 150 mg cohort; of these, 11 (28.2%) were considered treatment-related. Most SAEs were resolved after corrective treatment. There were three deaths in the 150 mg cohort, but none of which were related to the study treatment. Treatment was discontinued for 4 (10.3%) patients due to TEAEs and for 3 (7.7%) patients due to TRAEs. The most common grade ≥ 3 TRAEs (incidence ≥ 5%) included anemia (17.9%), decreased platelet count, decreased white blood cell count, increased alanine aminotransferase, hypertriglyceridemia, hypertension, pulmonary embolism, and pneumonia (5.1% for each).

All patients in the 100 mg cohort and most patients in the 150 mg cohort had normal laboratory test results or abnormalities with no clinical significance. ECG results showed no QTcF prolongation of clinical concern with fuzuloparib at 100 or 150 mg, either as monotherapy or in combination with AA-P.

Efficacy

All 39 patients had evaluable PSA data and were included in the PSA analysis. By the end of Week 12, 28 out of 39 patients had achieved a PSA reduction of ≥ 50% from baseline, resulting in a PSA response rate of 71.8% (95% CI, 55.1−85.0; Fig. 1A). The PSA response rate was 100% (3/3; 95% CI, 29.2−100.0) in the 100 mg cohort and 69.4% (25/36; 95% CI, 51.9−83.7) in the 150 mg cohort. Among all patients, the maximal percentage reduction in PSA exceeded 50% for 31 (79.5%) patients and 90% for 24 (61.5%) patients (Fig. 1B). A total of 19 (48.7%) patients had PSA progression, with a median time to PSA progression of 19.4 months (95% CI, 11.3−27.8; Fig. 2).

Efficacy was further analyzed in patients with BRCA mutations and in patients with HRR mutations (Supplemental Table S3). In the BRCA-mutated subgroup (n = 14), PSA response rate was 64.3% (9/14; 95% CI, 35.1−87.2). Six (42.9%) patients had PSA progression, with a median time to PSA progression of 27.8 months (95% CI, 3.0−NR). The median DoR was 5.5 months (95% CI, 5.2−NR), and the median rPFS was 36.1 months (95% CI, 8.2 −NR). In the HRR-mutated subgroup (n = 21), PSA response rate was 66.7% (14/21; 95% CI, 43.0−85.4). Ten (47.6%) patients had PSA progression, with a median time to PSA progression of 25.0 months (95% CI, 5.7−NR). The median DoR was 15.3 months (95% CI, 5.2−NR), and the median rPFS was 27.9 months (95% CI, 8.5−NR).

Discussion

In this phase 1 dose escalation and expansion study, fuzuloparib in combination with AA-P showed no obvious drug-drug interaction and exhibited a manageable safety and tolerability profile. PK analyses indicated that the plasma exposure of both fuzuloparib and abiraterone, assessed via Cmax, ss and AUCss, remained unaffected by concomitant administration, confirming the absence of significant drug-drug interaction.

Dose-limiting toxicity was not identified. In general, fuzuloparib plus AA-P was well tolerated in metastatic CRPC patients. The safety profile of the combination therapy was consistent with the established profiles of fuzuloparib monotherapy and AA-P [16, 28, 29]. The most frequent TRAEs were anemia, decreased weight, asthenia, decreased platelet count, white blood cell count, and red blood cell count. Most of the TRAEs were hematological toxicities, which are commonly associated with fuzuloparib monotherapy and are manageable through dose reduction and corrective treatment. There were no new safety concerns identified. The types and frequency of AEs reported in this study were consistent with those observed for other PARP inhibitors, such as olaparib, niraparib, and talazoparib, when combined with novel endocrine therapies [30–33].

Fuzuloparib plus AA-P demonstrated promising efficacy in the treatment of metastatic CRPC. At Week 12, the PSA response rate was 71.8% (95% CI, 55.1−85.0). PSA progression occurred in 48.7% of patients, with a median time to progression of 19.4 months (95% CI, 11.3−27.8). These findings compare favorably with the data for AA-P treatment alone, which reported a PSA response rate of 29.5% and a median time to PSA progression of 8.5 months (95% CI, 8.3−11.1) [16]. Furthermore, the PSA response rate observed in this study exceeds the 48% reported in the phase 2 trial of olaparib in combination with AA-P [34]. Analysis based on evaluable target lesions showed an ORR of 60.0% and DCR of 90% in this study, comparable to and numerically higher than that in olaparib plus AA-P (ORR, 27−58%; DCR, 75−82%) [30, 34]. The median rPFS for olaparib plus AA-P was 13.8 months in the phase 2 trial and 24.8 months in the phase 3 trial (PROpel), while the overall median rPFS in this study was 27.9 months [30, 34]. The higher PSA and tumor response rate as well as the longer time to disease progression suggest that fuzuloparib may possess potentiated antitumor efficacy when combined with AA-P. Nevertheless, direct comparisons between trials should be interpreted with caution.

Subgroup analyses showed that patients harboring HRR and BRCA mutations experienced greater clinical benefit from the fuzuloparib plus AA-P combination. Compared to the overall population, the HRR- and BRCA-mutated subgroups had higher PSA response rate and longer time to PSA progression and radiographic progression. This observation aligns with findings from other PARP inhibitor trials combined with AA-P. For example, in the PROpel trial, both HRR-mutated and HRR wild-type patients benefited from olaparib plus AA-P, but the median rPFS was longer in patients harboring HRR mutations (NR vs. 24.1 months) [30]. In the phase 3 trial of niraparib plus AA-P (MAGNITUDE), the combination therapy resulted in much improved rPFS in the HRR-positive population, with the greatest benefit in the BRCA1/2 subgroup [31, 32]. The median rPFS in the HRR-positive cohort was 19.0 months, while that in the BRCA1/2 subgroup was 19.3 months. Similarly, in this study, the BRCA-mutated subgroup had a longer rPFS than the HRR-mutated subgroup (36.1 months [95% CI, 8.2−NR] vs. 27.9 months [95% CI, 8.5−NR]). These data suggest that fuzuloparib plus AA-P has the potential to prolong the time to disease progression in patients with HRR mutations, especially in those with BRCA mutations.

As a phase 1 trial, the relatively small sample size limits the power to detect less frequent AEs. The trial participants may not fully represent the broader spectrum of metastatic CRPC patients. Secondly, the efficacy data are preliminary. The absence of a randomized control group precludes a definitive assessment of the clinical benefit of fuzuloparib plus AA-P compared with existing standards of care. A larger-scale, multicenter phase 3 trial is ongoing to further evaluate the efficacy and safety of the combination of fuzuloparib plus AA-P in the treatment of metastatic CRPC (ClinicalTrials.gov, NCT04691804).

Conclusion

Fuzuloparib at 150 mg and AA-P showed no PK interactions when administered concomitantly. This combination regimen was well-tolerated, with a safety profile consistent with the established monotherapies and no emerging safety concerns. The combination also showed promising efficacy with a high PSA response rate and prolonged time to disease progression. These findings suggest that fuzuloparib plus AA-P represents a viable and effective treatment option for the management of metastatic CRPC.
