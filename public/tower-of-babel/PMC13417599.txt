A dual-lock strategy for tumor-specific pyroptosis activation plus gas synergism
Authors: Xiaosheng Liu, Haidong Li, Wen Li, Zhihan Guo, Xiao Zhou, Jeongyeon Hong, Yifu Hao, Yichu Yu, Shuang Zeng, Jingyun Wang, Juyoung Yoon, Xiaojun Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pyroptosis, a canonical type of immunogenic cell death (ICD), has recently garnered extensive attention and become a highly promising strategy to enhance the efficacy of tumor immunotherapy. Unfortunately, current pyroptosis inducers face critical limitations in clinical practice, stemming from poor tumor selectivity, severe off-target effects, acquired resistance, and inadequate efficacy. Therefore, the precise induction of tumor cell pyroptosis is crucial for cancer immunotherapy, especially for the safe treatment of tumors. Herein, we designed and developed a small-molecule pyroptosis inducer (Cy-DNBS) synergistically activated by intratumoral overexpressed glutathione (GSH) and near-infrared (NIR) light. Upon GSH-triggered activation in tumors, Cy-DNBS generates fluorescence/photoacoustic (FL/PA) multimodal signals, owing to the suppression of photoinduced electron transfer (PET), and can subsequently produce large amounts of reactive oxygen species (ROS) through a photodynamic process. Meanwhile, SO2 released during activation further consumes GSH and amplifies the ROS storm, which ultimately triggers the caspase-3/GSDME-mediated pyroptosis pathway in tumor cells, remodels the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, and exerts potent therapeutic efficacy in tumor-bearing mouse models. This study not only exploits a new molecular platform for multimodal tumor theranostics, but also offers a safe and potent innovative strategy to precisely trigger tumor cell pyroptosis for high-efficiency cancer immunotherapy.

Introduction

Unlike the immunologically silent apoptosis, pyroptosis is a gasdermin-mediated programmed cell death that elicits robust inflammatory responses.1,2 During tumor cell pyroptosis, gasdermin proteins form pores in the plasma membrane, leading to cellular swelling and eventual rupture.3,4 The aforementioned process results in the release of numerous damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs, including CRT, HMGB1, ATP, etc.) and proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-18, which function as endogenous danger signals.5,6 These released molecules serve dual immunostimulatory roles: they potently recruit and activate antigen-presenting cells including dendritic cells, while simultaneously facilitating the cross-presentation of tumor-associated antigens to T cells.7–9 Consequently, this mechanism efficiently initiates adaptive immune responses, transforming immunologically “cold” tumor microenvironments into “hot” microenvironments.10 Moreover, the release of tumor antigens can generate an effect akin to “in situ vaccination”, potentially establishing long-term antitumor immune memory.11 As such, pyroptosis induction represents a promising strategy in cancer immunotherapy and has also received widespread attention.12 However, it is crucial to recognize that pyroptosis is inherently a pathological process, and its non-specific activation may lead to severe systemic complications including systemic inflammatory response syndrome, sepsis, and multiple organ failure.13,14 Therefore, achieving precise induction of pyroptosis specifically within tumor cells is of paramount importance and poses a challenge.

Currently, pyroptosis inducers can be mainly divided into two categories: biomacromolecules and small chemical molecules.15,16 Biomacromolecular inducers, such as RNA or recombinant proteins, have high target specificity, but their in vivo applications are frequently limited by poor stability and low membrane permeability, and they often rely on complex delivery systems (such as viral vectors or nanoparticles) to achieve effective intracellular delivery.17–19 As typical chemical small-molecule inducers, traditional chemotherapeutic drugs are widely used in clinical practice. However, they lack target-specific mechanisms, often cause severe systemic toxicity and side effects, are prone to inducing multidrug resistance, and generally show low efficiency in triggering pyroptosis.20,21

In recent years, photodynamic therapy (PDT) has emerged as a promising strategy for the precise induction of tumor pyroptosis. This approach employs a photosensitizer that, upon excitation by an external light source, controllably generates ROS within a localized area.22–24 The highly reactive ROS can damage key biomolecules such as proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids, thereby triggering pyroptosis in cancer cells. PDT offers advantages including good spatiotemporal controllability, relatively low cost, and fewer side effects.25–27 However, conventional PDT is limited by the lack of tumor specificity of the photosensitizers, which can lead to non-selective damage to normal tissues within the irradiated area, provoking local inflammatory responses and compromising both precision and safety.28,29 Therefore, the development of novel photosensitizers capable of tumor-specific activation has become crucial to overcoming this limitation. Such photosensitizers are designed to respond exclusively to tumor-associated stimuli (such as overexpressed enzymes or an acidic pH), thereby enabling selective activation within tumor cells or the tumor microenvironment. This confines ROS generation and cytotoxic effects precisely to malignant cells, significantly reducing off-target damage to healthy tissues. This strategy enhances therapeutic precision at the source, allowing for the targeted induction of tumor pyroptosis while minimizing systemic toxicity, thus offering a new pathway toward safer and more accurate cancer therapy.30,31

Nevertheless, during PDT treatment, tumor cells can upregulate antioxidant defense systems, such as GSH, to counteract the ROS generated by the photosensitizer, thereby attenuating PDT-induced cell death.32 To overcome this limitation, SO2 has attracted attention due to its unique chemical properties: it not only effectively depletes overexpressed GSH in tumor cells, disrupting their redox balance, but also further elevates ROS levels, exacerbating oxidative stress.33,34 However, while exerting its therapeutic function, high-dose or uncontrolled release of SO2 may also cause damage to normal tissues.35 Hence, achieving precise control over the timing, location, and dosage of SO2 release is key to ensuring both efficacy and safety.36 In this context, the development of activatable photosensitizers that integrate high biocompatibility, tumor-specific responsiveness, and gas-synergistic therapeutic functions has become an urgent requirement for advancing tumor-specific pyroptosis induction and photo-immunotherapy.

To address the challenges and practical limitations of the aforementioned pyroptosis inducers, this study designed and synthesized a novel small-molecule pyroptosis inducer, Cy-DNBS, based on a hemicyanine dye scaffold. The molecule employs a unique “dual-lock” regulation strategy, enabling tumor-specific pyroptosis induction through a dual-control mechanism responsive to GSH and near-infrared (NIR) light activation. In normal tissues, Cy-DNBS remains in an “off” state, whereas in the tumor microenvironment, high GSH concentrations activate Cy-DNBS, triggering SO2 release and depletion of intracellular antioxidants,37 subsequently efficiently generating superoxide anions (O2˙−) and singlet oxygen (1O2) under NIR light irradiation. The synergistic interaction between SO2 and photodynamically produced ROS initiates a ROS storm, ultimately inducing precise pyroptosis via the caspase-3/GSDME signaling pathway. Notably, Cy-DNBS also exhibits multimodal imaging capabilities, with its tumor-specific activation of NIR fluorescence and photoacoustic (FL/PA) dual signals enabling real-time monitoring of drug accumulation and activation at the tumor site, thereby providing an effective tool for image-guided precise pyroptosis therapy (Scheme 1). This integrated theranostic design strategy opens new avenues for achieving safe and efficient tumor immunotherapy.

Results and discussion

Design and synthesis of Cy-DNBS

Based on the reported hemicyanine dye platform with a donor–π–acceptor (D–π–A) structure, we selected a heavy-atom-modified hemicyanine derivative, Cy-Br, as the molecular scaffold by virtue of its outstanding features including NIR absorption and emission, PA signal, as well as structural modifiability.38 The introduction of a bromine atom enhances the intersystem crossing efficiency of Cy-Br, endowing it with excellent ROS generation capability and photoacoustic imaging potential, thus providing an ideal platform for constructing a pyroptosis inducer. Subsequently, we successfully designed and synthesized the target compound Cy-DNBS through a triethylamine-catalyzed nucleophilic reaction between 2,4-dinitrobenzenesulfonyl chloride (DNBS) and the hydroxyl group of Cy-Br (Fig. S1). The chemical structures of all intermediates and Cy-DNBS were unambiguously confirmed by nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy and high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS), with detailed structural characterization data provided in Fig. S19–S27. In the designed pyroptosis inducer Cy-DNBS, the DNBS moiety serves not only as the SO2 donor but also as an electron-accepting unit that quenches the fluorescence, photoacoustic signal, and photosensitizing activity of Cy-Br through a photoinduced electron transfer (PET) process. Moreover, DNBS acts as a GSH-specific responsive site, enabling efficient, rapid, and precise activation of Cy-DNBS in the tumor microenvironment characterized by high GSH concentrations. This design ensures that Cy-DNBS remains non-toxic to normal tissues while achieving integrated functions in tumor cells, including multimodal imaging, gas therapy, and pyroptosis induction.

In vitro study on the response of Cy-DNBS to GSH

To evaluate the GSH-responsive activation characteristics of Cy-DNBS, we first examined the changes in its ultraviolet-visible absorption spectra before and after the incubation with GSH. As shown in Fig. 1a, the Cy-DNBS solution exhibited a maximum absorption peak at 580 nm. After incubation with GSH, this absorption peak red-shifted to 690 nm, accompanied by a visible color change from purple to blue. The resulting spectral profile closely resembled that of Cy-Br, indicating that the DNBS moiety was cleaved from Cy-DNBSvia reaction with GSH and subsequently converted back to the Cy-Br structure. Further fluorescence spectral analysis (Fig. 1b) revealed that Cy-DNBS itself displayed only weak fluorescence, whereas upon incubation with GSH, a rapid fluorescence response was observed, with an approximately 15-fold enhancement in intensity at 710 nm (Fig. 1c). Furthermore, LC-MS analysis confirmed the formation of Cy-Br, as [M − I]+ corresponding to Cy-Br was detected after GSH activation of Cy-DNBS (Fig. S2).

These results demonstrate that Cy-DNBS can be rapidly activated in the tumor microenvironment characterized by elevated GSH concentrations. We subsequently investigated the selectivity of Cy-DNBS toward GSH. After incubating Cy-DNBS with various substrates commonly found in biological systems, such as metal ions, amino acids, and different biothiols, fluorescence intensities of each group at 710 nm were recorded. As seen in Fig. 1d, the results indicated that, except for biothiols, other substances had no significant effect on the fluorescence of Cy-DNBS. Additionally, in the tumor cell microenvironment, the content of GSH is much higher than that of other biothiols (Cys and Hcy). So, Cy-DNBS could be selectively activated by GSH in cancer cells.

Furthermore, theoretical calculations were carried out to systematically elucidate the difference in fluorescence behavior between Cy-DNBS and Cy-Br upon photoexcitation, combining frontier molecular orbital (FMO) analysis with excited-state dynamics simulations. As illustrated in Fig. 1e, in Cy-DNBS, the strong electron-withdrawing DNBS group induces a pronounced spatial separation between the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO), establishing the orbital foundation for intramolecular direct photoinduced electron transfer (d-PET). Upon photoexcitation, instead of returning to the ground state radiatively, the electron from the HOMO (−5.43 eV) sequentially populates unoccupied orbitals such as LUMO+2 (−3.28 eV), LUMO+1 (−3.57 eV), and LUMO (−4.15 eV) via the d-PET process. This acts as an efficient non-radiative decay pathway, fully suppressing fluorescence emission.

Excited-state dynamics simulations further support this mechanism (Fig. S3). For Cy-DNBS, after photoexcitation from the ground state (S0) to the vertical excited state S1 (VES) (2.03 eV, oscillator strength f = 1.14), the system rapidly relaxes via internal conversion (IC) to the locally excited state S1 (LE) (1.86 eV, f = 1.05). Instead of undergoing radiative transition, this state preferentially undergoes an efficient intramolecular PET process, sequentially forming the charge-transfer states S1 (CT-2) (1.85 eV, f = 0) and S1 (CT-1) (1.29 eV, f = 0.01). The excited-state energy is rapidly dissipated through this non-radiative electron-transfer pathway, ultimately relaxing to a low-energy S1 equilibrium geometry (0.44 eV). Fluorescence emission is thus completely quenched due to the strong competition from non-radiative processes. In contrast, Cy-Br exhibits a more uniform orbital energy distribution without significant spatial separation favorable for PET. Its excited-state electrons mainly return to the ground state through radiative transition, a process characterized by a relatively high oscillator strength, leading to significant fluorescence emission. Consequently, when Cy-DNBS reacts specifically with GSH and loses the DNBS group, its molecular structure converts to that of Cy-Br. Accompanying this transformation, the efficient PET-mediated non-radiative pathway is shut off, and the photophysical behavior switches to a radiatively dominated regime, thereby turning on a distinct fluorescence signal.

Based on literature reports indicating the photoacoustic imaging potential of hemicyanine, we then verified that its photoacoustic signal exhibited a linear relationship with concentration in a phantom (Fig. S4).39 This finding suggested that the photoacoustic signal of Cy-DNBS might also be activated by GSH. Under the same experimental conditions as those used for the fluorescence measurements, we investigated both the photoacoustic spectra and the selectivity of the photoacoustic signal following the reaction between Cy-DNBS and GSH. A significant enhancement in the mean photoacoustic intensity was observed in the phantom cross-section following the reaction of the Cy-DNBS solution with GSH (Fig. 1f, g and S5). Additionally, in selectivity tests of the Cy-DNBS photoacoustic signal (Fig. 1h), Cy-DNBS exhibited significant signal responses only to biothiols, particularly GSH. These results collectively demonstrate that both the fluorescence and photoacoustic multimodal signals of Cy-DNBS possess GSH-selective “turn-on” capabilities. This characteristic not only enables tumor-specific identification and drug distribution tracking but also serves as the foundation for achieving precise killing in tumor cells.

Detection of SO2 and ROS generation by Cy-DNBSin vitro

Previous studies have indicated that the DNBS moiety could undergo a nucleophilic reaction with GSH to release SO2.40 To verify whether SO2 is released during the GSH-activated process of Cy-DNBS, a SO2 fluorescent probe was synthesized following a reported method (Fig. S6),41 and its chemical structure was further confirmed by NMR spectroscopy and HRMS (Fig. S28–S31). The detection mechanism of this SO2 probe is schematically shown in Fig. 2a, SO2 undergoes an addition reaction with the double bond in the probe, disrupting its conjugated system. This structural change shifts the fluorescence emission from red to green, thereby enabling SO2 detection. We first incubated the probe with sodium bisulfite (NaHSO3) as an exogenous SO2 donor, which led to a significant increase in fluorescence intensity at 470 nm, confirming the probe's suitability for SO2 detection.

Subsequently, Cy-DNBS, GSH, and the SO2 probe were co-incubated. NIR fluorescence at 730 nm emerged, corresponding to the activated Cy-DNBS (converted to Cy-Br), while simultaneous fluorescence enhancement at 470 nm was observed from the SO2 probe (Fig. 2b). The co-incubation system exhibited approximately 10.24-fold and 10.07-fold enhancements in fluorescence intensity at 470 nm and 730 nm, respectively (Fig. 2c), which concurrently confirmed the responsive release of SO2 and the activation of the Cy-DNBS fluorescence signal. Furthermore, using the standard calibration curve of the SO2 probe (Fig. S7), the fluorescence intensity measured in the co-incubation system was applied to calculate the SO2 release efficiency, which reached 79.55% upon GSH activation of Cy-DNBS. Absorption spectra further corroborated this conclusion (Fig. S8). After co-incubation of Cy-DNBS with GSH and an SO2 probe, a new absorption peak emerged at 705 nm, consistent with the characteristic absorption of Cy-Br, indicating the conversion of Cy-DNBS by GSH. Concurrently, a decrease in absorption at 540 nm and an increase at 405 nm were observed, matching the spectral shift expected upon reaction of the SO2 probe with released SO2. Together, these data demonstrate that Cy-DNBS, upon GSH triggering, effectively releases SO2 and activates its NIR fluorescence signal.

To investigate whether the photosensitive activity of Cy-DNBS is regulated by GSH and capable of triggering a ROS storm upon light irradiation, we compared the ROS generation capabilities of Cy-DNBS and its activated product Cy-Br. Under continuous NIR light irradiation, detection with the DCFH probe (Fig. 2d) showed that the fluorescence intensity at 525 nm of the Cy-Br-containing solution increased rapidly over time, while both the Cy-DNBS group and the blank probe group showed no significant changes. This indicates that Cy-DNBS itself lacks photodynamic activity and must be activated by GSH to Cy-Br before it can effectively generate ROS. Subsequently, DPBF (1O2 probe) and DHR123 (O2˙− probe) were used to test different types of ROS produced by the photosensitizer. As shown in Fig. 2e and f, only Cy-Br could effectively generate both 1O2 and O2˙−, suggesting that it could simultaneously function via both Type I and Type II photodynamic mechanisms upon illumination. Notably, the Type I mechanism relies on a cascade cycle formed by the Fenton reaction and the Haber–Weiss reaction, which efficiently generates ROS and, meanwhile, produces O2, thereby offering a distinct advantage for the treatment of hypoxic tumors.42 These experiments demonstrate that ROS generation by Cy-DNBS is strictly controlled by dual regulation of GSH and NIR light. This “dual-lock” design ensures tumor-specific activation while effectively preventing photodamage to normal tissues, providing the foundation for precisely inducing tumor pyroptosis.

Cellular uptake and subcellular localization

On the basis of confirming that the fluorescence signal of Cy-DNBS can be specifically activated by GSH, we further evaluated its potential for tumor cell-specific recognition. Through co-incubation of Cy-Br and Cy-DNBS with human breast cancer cells (MCF-7) and human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVEC), respectively, followed by confocal microscopy imaging analysis (Fig. 3a and b), the results demonstrated that Cy-Br exhibited strong nonspecific fluorescence signals in both cells, while Cy-DNBS only produced significant fluorescence signal in MCF-7 cells and almost no signal in HUVEC cells, showing excellent tumor cell selectivity. This finding confirms that the incorporation of the DNBS functional group enables Cy-DNBS to distinguish between tumor cells and normal cells.

After establishing its cellular selectivity, we further investigated the subcellular localization of Cy-DNBS. Through co-localization imaging with different organelle-specific dyes, we found that Cy-DNBS in MCF-7 cells showed a high Pearson correlation of 0.95 with ER-Tracker Green (Fig. 3c), significantly exceeding the coefficients with LysoTracker Green (0.41) and Hoechst 33342 (0.35). This endoplasmic reticulum (ER) targeting preference was similarly validated in mouse breast cancer cells (4T1, Fig. 3d). These experimental findings not only confirm the tumor cell-specific recognition capability of Cy-DNBS but, more importantly, reveal its specific distribution in the endoplasmic reticulum. This provides crucial spatial localization evidence for its mechanism of activating tumor cell pyroptosis through the induction of endoplasmic reticulum stress.

Investigation of cytotoxic mechanisms

After confirming that GSH can activate Cy-DNBS and release SO2 in a solution system, we further evaluated its gas therapeutic ability at the cellular level. Through detection with the SO2 probe (Fig. 4a), it was found that Cy-DNBS could effectively release SO2 in MCF-7 cells, and its fluorescence signal intensity was 54-fold higher than that of the Cy-Br group (Fig. 4b), indicating that the DNBS unit could still be used as an efficient SO2 donor in cells. Subsequently, DCFH-DA and DHE commercial probes were used to detect the levels of total ROS and O2˙− in MCF-7 cells under different treatment conditions (Fig. 4c). Under dark conditions, no obvious fluorescence signal was detected in the untreated group and the Cy-Br group, while the Cy-DNBS group showed a slight fluorescence enhancement. This suggests that the released SO2 consumed intracellular GSH, leading to disruption of redox homeostasis. After 660 nm NIR light irradiation, both Cy-Br and Cy-DNBS showed significant DCFH green fluorescence and DHE red fluorescence, indicating that both of them could efficiently generate ROS and O2˙− in cells through PDT. These results were further confirmed by quantitative analysis of intracellular mean fluorescence intensity (Fig. 4d and S9), and this phenomenon was also consistently verified in 4T1 cells (Fig. S10).

Based on the capability of Cy-DNBS to generate both SO2 and ROS in cells, we further evaluated its anticancer efficacy. The cell killing effect was detected using a Calcein-AM/PI live-dead cell staining kit (Fig. 4e). The results showed that the cells showed uniform green fluorescence without light irradiation, indicating that Cy-Br and Cy-DNBS were non-toxic; after light exposure, both groups showed significant PI red fluorescence and almost no Calcein-AM green fluorescence, indicating that both of them can effectively kill tumor cells under illumination.

To further quantify the cytotoxicity, we evaluated the dark toxicity and phototoxicity of Cy-Br and Cy-DNBS against normal cells (HUVEC) and various tumor cells using the MTT assay. Cy-DNBS exhibited no dark toxicity toward HUVEC cells but showed significant dark toxicity against both MCF-7 and 4T1 cells (Fig. 4g), which we attribute to SO2 release triggered by the high intracellular GSH levels in tumor cells. In contrast, Cy-Br demonstrated no dark toxicity toward the three types of cells (Fig. 4f). Upon light irradiation, both Cy-DNBS and Cy-Br demonstrated potent cytotoxic effects against tumor cells under normoxic conditions (Fig. 4h) and also exhibited considerable killing activity under hypoxic conditions (Fig. S11). Notably, for normal cells (HUVEC), the IC50 of Cy-DNBS was 52.88 µM, substantially higher than that of Cy-Br (2.76 µM) in the same cell (Fig. 4i). This indicates that the phototoxicity of Cy-DNBS is selectively dependent on GSH activation within the tumor microenvironment. Its dual-lock regulatory mechanism enables precise eradication of tumor cells while markedly reducing off-target effects on normal cells.

Validation of pyroptosis and immunogenic cell death

In the process of exploring the cytotoxicity mechanism of Cy-DNBS, we performed Annexin V-FITC/PI double staining on MCF-7 cells under different treatment conditions. Annexin V can specifically bind to phosphatidylserine, which is an important marker of cell death. As shown in Fig. 5a, no significant fluorescence was detected in MCF-7 cells without light treatment, confirming cell integrity. In contrast, cells pre-incubated with Cy-Br or Cy-DNBS and then exposed to light irradiation (660 nm, 40 mW/cm2, 10 min) displayed both green Annexin V-FITC fluorescence and red PI fluorescence, indicating the destruction of cell membrane integrity and cell death. Additionally, cell death in the Cy-DNBS + L group was accompanied by morphological changes such as cellular swelling and membrane bubbling, features highly consistent with pyroptosis as documented in the literature.43 This suggests that Cy-DNBS likely exerts its cytotoxic effect by inducing pyroptosis.

Based on the above morphological evidence, we further investigated the activation of key pyroptosis-related molecules using the GreenNuc™ caspase-3 assay kit and western blot analysis. The results showed that only cells in the Cy-DNBS + L group exhibited intense green fluorescence within the nuclei (Fig. 5b), indicating caspase-3 activation. Furthermore, the obvious membrane bubbling observed in the corresponding bright-field images suggested the probable occurrence of pyroptosis (Fig. S13). In addition, as shown in Fig. 5c, western blot analysis revealed that neither the control group nor the Cy-DNBS alone treatment group exhibited detectable caspase-3 cleavage bands or GSDME-N-terminal fragments, whereas in the Cy-DNBS with NIR light irradiation group, both were significantly expressed, confirming specific activation of the pyroptosis pathway.

To elucidate the upstream mechanisms of Cy-DNBS-induced pyroptosis, we employed Fluo-3 AM and rhodamine 123 probes to monitor endoplasmic reticulum (ER) calcium release and mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) changes, respectively. As shown in Fig. 5d and S12, both Cy-DNBS and Cy-Br under light irradiation triggered a significant increase in cytosolic Ca2+ levels (enhanced Fluo-3 green fluorescence) and concurrent MMP collapse (quenched rhodamine 123 fluorescence). These findings indicate that Cy-DNBS initiates ER oxidative stress and Ca2+ release, leading to mitochondrial calcium overload and functional impairment, ultimately triggering the caspase-3/GSDME-mediated pyroptosis pathway.

To evaluate whether the pyroptosis induced by Cy-DNBS in cancer cells possesses immunogenicity, we next examined the release of multiple damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs). As an important DAMP molecule, HMGB1 normally functions within the nucleus under physiological conditions but is released extracellularly following the loss of plasma membrane integrity, where it promotes dendritic cell maturation and inflammatory cytokine production.44 As shown in Fig. 5e, immunofluorescence staining results demonstrated that in MCF-7 cells pre-incubated with Cy-DNBS and subjected to light treatment, the green fluorescence signal diffused from the nucleus to the cytoplasm, indicating significant nuclear-to-cytoplasmic translocation of HMGB1. Concurrently, we observed that after Cy-DNBS combined with light treatment induced pyroptosis in MCF-7 cells, substantial exposure of CRT occurred on the cell surface, demonstrating the active initiation of immune recognition mechanisms by the cells.45 Furthermore, as a key chemotactic factor, ATP efflux can effectively recruit antigen-presenting cells such as dendritic cells to tumor sites.46 As shown in Fig. 5f, comparative analysis of different treatment groups revealed that the ATP concentration in the supernatant of MCF-7 cells pre-incubated with Cy-DNBS after light exposure was 10.25-fold higher than that of the control group. This phenomenon was also validated in 4T1 cells (Fig. S15), where significant ATP efflux further confirmed the occurrence of immunogenic cell death (ICD).47 Notably, the release of lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) in the supernatant of MCF-7 cells in the Cy-DNBS + L group also significantly increased, indicating that the pores formed by GSDME-N-terminal fragments effectively disrupted membrane integrity, leading to the leakage of intracellular contents.48 These results collectively demonstrate that Cy-DNBS-induced pyroptosis not only achieves tumor cell killing but also successfully stimulates anti-tumor immune responses through the ordered release of DAMPs.

Taken together, the complete cellular pathway is illustrated in Fig. 5h: after the pyroptosis inducer Cy-DNBS is specifically activated by GSH in tumor cells, the synergistic effect between the released SO2 and photodynamic action triggers endoplasmic reticulum oxidative stress, promoting Ca2+ release into the cytoplasm. This subsequently induces mitochondrial Ca2+ overload and functional damage, ultimately leading to pyroptosis through the caspase-3/GSDME pathway. More importantly, key signaling molecules such as HMGB1 and ATP released during the pyroptosis process establish a crucial bridge between cell death and immune activation, thereby endowing pyroptotic tumor cells with immunogenic potential.

Multimodal imaging in tumor-bearing mice

After confirming that the fluorescence and photoacoustic signals of Cy-DNBS can be specifically activated by tumor-overexpressed GSH in solution and at the cellular level, we further evaluated its in vivo imaging performance in 4T1 tumor-bearing mouse models. This investigation aimed to verify its tumor specificity in complex physiological environments and provide evidence for image-guided precise pyroptosis research. First, we confirmed through in vitro tube experiments that the small animal fluorescence imaging system could effectively detect the activation response of Cy-DNBS towards GSH (Fig. S16). Subsequently, after intratumoral and contralateral subcutaneous injection of Cy-DNBS in tumor-bearing mice, the fluorescence intensity in the tumor region rapidly increased over time, while signals in normal tissues remained largely unchanged (Fig. 6a). 30 min after injection, the signal-to-background ratio (SBR) between tumor and normal tissues reached 1.68 (Fig. 6b). Fluorescence intensity analysis of major organs after dissection further confirmed that significant fluorescence signals were exclusively present in tumor tissue (Fig. S17), demonstrating that Cy-DNBS retains excellent tumor specificity in vivo.

To further explore its potential for clinical applications, we topically sprayed the Cy-DNBS solution onto subcutaneous solid tumors (Fig. 6c). The results showed that it could clearly distinguish tumor tissue from normal tissue boundaries, achieving an SBR of 1.97 within 10 minutes of application, which further increased to 3.81 after 30 minutes (Fig. 6d). These rapid activation characteristics and high-contrast imaging capability demonstrate its significant potential for intraoperative image-guided surgical applications.

Considering the limitations in tissue penetration depth of fluorescence imaging, we employed photoacoustic tomography to evaluate the solid tumor imaging capability of Cy-DNBS. As shown in Fig. 6e, a marked enhancement in the photoacoustic signal was observed at the tumor site 30 minutes post-injection. The photoacoustic spectrum from the cross-sectional photoacoustic image of the solid tumor closely resembled that of activated Cy-DNBS in phantoms, further validating the specific activation of Cy-DNBS by overexpressed GSH in the tumor microenvironment (Fig. 5e). Quantitative analysis revealed a 6.66-fold increase in photoacoustic intensity in the Cy-DNBS-treated group compared to the control group (Fig. 5f). These results confirm that Cy-DNBS is also well-suited for photoacoustic imaging modalities with superior penetration depth, thereby offering a multimodal imaging-guided strategy for precision tumor therapy.

In vivo antitumor efficacy evaluation

After confirming the specific killing ability of Cy-DNBS at the cellular level, we further evaluated its antitumor efficacy in vivo. The treatment regimen is shown in Fig. 7a: when the tumor volume reached approximately 100 mm3, mice were randomly divided into five groups: control group, Cy-Br group, Cy-DNBS group, Cy-Br + L group, and Cy-DNBS + L group. For the light irradiation groups, localized irradiation (671 nm, 100 mW/cm2, 15 min) was applied to the tumor site 30 min after intratumoral drug injection. All groups received two treatment sessions. Throughout the 14-day observation period, the body weight of mice in all groups remained stable (Fig. 7b), indicating good biocompatibility of both Cy-DNBS and Cy-Br. Regarding tumor inhibition efficacy, as shown in Fig. 7c and d, the non-irradiated Cy-DNBS group demonstrated certain tumor growth inhibition, suggesting that the released SO2 itself possesses gas therapy potential. Both Cy-Br + L and Cy-DNBS + L groups exhibited significant tumor suppression effects, with their antitumor efficacy being substantially stronger than that of the other groups, indicating that photodynamic therapy plays a dominant role in Cy-DNBS-mediated tumor killing.

To further evaluate the biosafety of Cy-DNBS, we performed H&E staining analysis on the major organs of the treated mice (Fig. S18). The results revealed no significant histopathological damage in any group, confirming that Cy-DNBS causes no substantial systemic toxicity during in vivo application. Meanwhile, H&E and TUNEL staining of tumor tissues (Fig. 7e) demonstrated that both Cy-Br and Cy-DNBS induced extensive tumor tissue damage following NIR light irradiation. More importantly, the Cy-DNBS + L group showed significantly increased infiltration of CD8+ T and CD4+ T cells within tumors, indicating the activation of potent antitumor immune responses. We propose that this immune activation closely correlates with the pyroptosis process induced by Cy-DNBS: the tumor antigens and danger signals such as HMGB1 and ATP released by pyroptotic cells effectively activate dendritic cells, thereby initiating T cell-mediated immunity. These results demonstrate that Cy-DNBS not only directly kills tumor cells through photodynamic and gas therapy but also alleviates the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment and promotes antitumor immune recruitment by inducing immunogenic cell death, thereby activating antitumor immunity.

Conclusions

This study successfully developed a novel pyroptosis inducer, Cy-DNBS, based on a “gas-synergistic dual-lock” strategy. Through a dual-regulation mechanism involving GSH activation and NIR light irradiation, this molecule specifically initiates the pyroptosis program in the tumor microenvironment, achieving precise tumor elimination. Our research demonstrates that Cy-DNBS effectively releases SO2 in the high-GSH environment of tumor cells while efficiently generating reactive oxygen species under near-infrared light irradiation. The synergistic effect of these two actions triggers endoplasmic reticulum stress and mitochondrial damage, ultimately leading to precise induction of pyroptosis via the caspase-3/GSDME signaling axis. More importantly, this pyroptosis process exhibits prominent immunogenicity. Damage-associated molecular patterns including HMGB1 and ATP secreted by dying tumor cells can activate dendritic cells and robustly elevate intratumoral CD4+ and CD8+ T lymphocyte infiltration. Such immune cell recruitment partially mitigates local tumor immune suppression and shifts the anti-tumor mode from direct tumor cell elimination to systemic immune activation.

In summary, leveraging its unique gas-synergistic effect and dual-lock regulation mechanism, Cy-DNBS precisely eliminates tumors while effectively protecting normal tissues and successfully stimulating the body's antitumor immune response. This study not only provides an innovative strategy for tumor-specific pyroptosis induction but also lays an important foundation for developing highly efficient and low-toxicity immunogenic cell death inducers, as well as achieving image-guided precise pyroptosis and immunotherapy.
