1 Introduction
The overall goals of agriculture are similar whether crops grow inside, as in controlled
environment agriculture (CEA), or outside in fields—maximizing crop quantity and quality
while minimizing resource inputs. For crops grown outdoors, sunlight is a free, although
uncontrollable, resource. Indoor CEA crops consume energy and money, but they benefit from
the controllability of the lighting input, the ambient temperature and humidity, and the potential
for an extended growing season.

This paper focuses on CEA lighting consumption and examines two common types of buildings
(plant factories and greenhouses), as well as two types of greenhouse lighting controls (threshold
and LASSI [light and shade system implementation]). Figure 1 shows the combinations
examined in this report. The goal is to see which combination minimizes lighting energy use in
CEA applications.

1.1 Background on Greenhouses, Plant Factories, and Lighting
 Approaches
CEA is not just crop production in enclosed facilities, like greenhouse and plant factories (CEA
without glazing). It also incorporates different systems that bring a technology-based approach to
farming. CEA can range from growing plants in a simple envelope structure to full indoor
vertical farms. Designing to provide optimal conditions for crop production in those scenarios
involves controlling the following parameters:
 • Environmental: temperature, relative humidity, carbon dioxide, light
 • Agricultural: water quality, nutrient concentration, nutrient pH, crop duration and density,
 cultivation, pest controls.
Although all the above-mentioned controllable variables are important, light-related metrics
(intensity, spectrum, duration, and intervals) play especially critical roles in CEA. Different crop
types have different lighting needs for not only optimal growth but also best return on
investment.

 1

1.1.1 Photosynthetically Active Radiation and Daily Light Integral
Unlike lighting requirements in human-occupied spaces, lighting requirements in CEA spaces
center around the needs of the plants. Crop growth benefits from light only within a particular
range of wavelengths (400–700 nm) for photosynthesis. Light falling in this range is called
photosynthetically active radiation (PAR). The most helpful ranges of the light spectrum are
shown in Figure 2—for human perception on the left and for plant photosynthetic efficiency on
the right. Each crop type has a different optimal number of PAR photons—for example,
individual particles of light in the PAR range over a 24-hour period. This number is often
referred to as the daily light integral (DLI), quantified in moles of light per square meter per day
(mol/m2-day) (Neilson n.d.). The target DLI of a few common crop types are listed below. (Note:
These targets may vary over the life cycle of the crops.)
 • Hydroponic lettuce: 15–17 mol/m2-day
 • Strawberries: 20–22 mol/m2-day
 • Tomatoes: 20–30+ mol/m2-day (for maximum yield).

1.1.2 Lighting Control Approaches for Greenhouses
To fulfill a crop’s lighting requirements, the lighting system must deliver enough DLI to the
plant growth area. Typically, we can assume a plant factory has entirely artificial lighting needs
due to its opaque envelope, whereas a greenhouse can utilize daylighting with semitransparent
external walls and roofs to lower the lighting energy consumption. With daylighting levels
varying between locations and times of year, the supplemental lighting for a greenhouse should
be controlled to deliver the crop’s light requirements. This control is especially important for the
many greenhouses that operate year-round. This report compares one typical control method
(threshold) and one new method (light and shade system implementation, LASSI) for artificial
lighting control in greenhouses:

 2

• Threshold (also known as time clock): Each month, the controller is set to a fixed amount
 of time per day when the artificial lighting system is turned on. The amount of time is
 chosen to ensure the system provides enough supplemental lighting to achieve the
 required DLI on the day predicted to have the smallest daylighting contribution.
 However, for most days of the month, the sunlight is brighter than this minimum level, so
 this control method tends to oversupply artificial lighting.
• Light and shade system implementation (LASSI) (Mattson 2018): This method uses
 forecasted weather data, shading devices, and lighting controllers to maintain the daily
 DLI provided. The portion of DLI provided by the artificial lighting system is designed to
 be just enough to meet the crop’s daily needs. Because it has less tendency to oversupply
 artificial lighting, this control method uses less electricity for lighting compared to the
 threshold method. The LASSI method also deploys shades when the DLI is higher than is
 healthy for the crop. Because this report focuses on minimizing electricity use for
 artificial lighting, which is deployed when DLI is too low, incorporating shading controls
 is outside its scope.

2 Methodology
To estimate the effects of different lighting control methods on energy consumption in CEA
facilities, we conducted physics-based simulations using EnergyPlus® and OpenStudio®
software. In general, the software creates building models using the building geometry; exterior
conditions; building material properties; heating, ventilating, and air-conditioning (HVAC)
systems; and other inputs to calculate the energy consumption and conditions of the building
throughout the duration of the evaluation period, which is typically 1 year. Because this study
focuses on lighting energy consumption, however, HVAC systems and plug/process loads were
not included in the models. The presence of daylighting or electric lighting would affect the
HVAC loads, but incorporating HVAC or plug/process loads would not affect how much light
the crops received.

Meeting the lighting requirements for each crop is the primary goal for the lighting system in a
CEA facility, so the lighting energy needed is specified using the daily crop requirements, the
climate, and the lighting control method. We ran these simulations for greenhouses in 16
different ASHRAE 169-2013 climate zones shown in Table 1 to see how much climate variation
impacted the results.

greenhouse model was developed. It had 95% percent glazing for the envelope, using the thermal

2.5 Supplemental Lighting Schedule
In Section 2.3, the supplemental lighting system is sized; thus, the supplemental PAR lighting
energy and electrical lighting input per hour are specified for the crop during the hours for which
the supplemental lighting system is activated.

For a plant factory, the lights are scheduled to remain on for the exact number of hours used in
the sizing calculations. This schedule remains constant for every day of the year for which the
crop is growing. In the Section 2.3 sizing example, this quantity is 7.1 hours.

For a greenhouse with threshold controls, the number of hours during which the lights are
activated is reset each month (unless daylighting conditions happen to be identical between
months) based on the minimum daylighting DLI predicted for the month. The schedule remains
the same for each day of the month and changes at the start of a new month. Using the example
from Section 2.3, the crop requires 15 mol/m2-day of daylight. If the least-daylit day of the
upcoming month is predicted to provide 10 mol/m2-day, then the supplemental lighting must
provide the difference of 5 mol/m2-day. Because running the lamps for 7.1 hours per day
provides 15 mol/m2-day, then running them for one-third of that time, or 2.4 hours per day,
would provide one-third of the DLI, or 5 mol/m2-day. For the upcoming month in question, the
lights would turn on for 2.4 hours every day, regardless of the actual lighting conditions.

For a greenhouse with LASSI controls, the schedule gets reset daily based on the forecast for the
following day’s lighting conditions. The calculations for the number of hours per day are the
same as those in the preceding paragraph, except the predictions happen daily instead of
monthly. The threshold and LASSI control rules are also detailed in Appendix A. Figure 4 shows
the daily and monthly variation in scheduled hours for each of the three scenarios described for
simulations in Denver, Colorado. Because the lights are sized for the worst possible daylighting
conditions—no daylight at all—the number of hours scheduled should never exceed this amount
(e.g., 7.1 hours in the sizing example).

2.6 Validation of Daily Light Integral Simulation Output
To verify the accuracy of our modeled daylighting sensor outputs, we compared the average
monthly DLI from the outputs in Denver, Colorado (converted to DLI units, per Section 2.4),
with calculated DLI from the NASA POWER Agroclimatology dataset (also for Denver,
Colorado) (Sparks 2018). We estimated the outdoor DLI value from the simulation, which
should be comparable to the outdoor DLI from the NASA dataset, by dividing the calculated
indoor DLI by the visible transmittance of the modeled window. As an approximation, this
adjustment to the DLI attempts to remove the effects of the glazing to gauge whether the two sets
of monthly values align.

4 Conclusions
The results shown in Section 3 indicate the savings potential shown for LASSI controls makes
them worthy of further analysis (see Section 4.1, Next Steps). In greenhouses, LASSI controls
performed better (i.e., consumed less energy) than threshold controls, and both control types
performed better than plant factories in all climates.

Plants that require a higher DLI for optimal growth will generally require more supplemental
electric lighting. In this study, tomatoes required more supplemental lighting than strawberries,
which required more than lettuce. The differences were not always linear, though.

The trend of the diminishing difference in lighting electricity use between crops in better-daylit
locations, shown in Figure 11 and Figure 12, likely occurs because these better-daylit locations
experience more days on which daylighting alone provides all the required DLI for the crops.
More days of zero supplemental lighting requirements for the neediest crop, in this case
tomatoes, would cause the same number of zero-lighting days for the less-needy crops. When
daylighting provided, for example, 100% of the required DLI for tomatoes, 122% of the required
DLI for strawberries, and 183% of the required DLI for lettuce, the difference in supplemental
lighting electricity use would not vary linearly with the DLI requirement—it would be zero in all
three cases. As LASSI controls reduce excess supplementary lighting provided, they result in
more zero-lighting days than threshold controls. Thus, the LASSI simulations show smaller
differences in lighting electricity use in the better-daylit locations than the threshold controls.

4.1 Future Work
The simulations discussed in this study predicted lighting electricity use reliably across different
control methods, crops, and locations. The following suggestions for future work could add
much more insight and valuable information, however. These suggestions include:
 • Review the impact from different glazing types in greenhouses.
 • Incorporate HVAC into models to determine any trade-offs between lighting and HVAC
 energy use and cost with different lighting controls. When HVAC is considered, it will be
 important to use the shading feature of LASSI controls.
 • Try a more flexible light schedule to match time-of-use tariffs.
 • Conduct a life cycle cost analysis of both greenhouse lighting control methods with
 different light bulb types.
 • Explore the impacts of lighting controls when growing crops during different times of the
 year (this study looks only at year-round operation).
