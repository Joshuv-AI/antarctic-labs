Trends and Associated Factors of Malnutrition Among Adolescents With Diarrhoea in Bangladesh: A Retrospective Cross‐Sectional Surveillance Study Using Adaptive LASSO
Authors: Md Fuad Al Fidah, Md Ridwan Islam, Syed Jayedul Bashar, Rukaeya Amin, A. S. G. Faruque, Tahmeed Ahmed, Sharika Nuzhat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsAdolescence is a critical phase of rapid growth with long‐term health implications. We aimed to examine the trends and associated factors of malnutrition among adolescents with diarrhoea in urban Bangladesh.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cross‐sectional study and analysed data from 1597 adolescents aged 10–19 years enrolled in the Diarrhoeal Disease Surveillance System at icddr,b's Dhaka Hospital between 2012 and 2023. Nutritional status was defined using WHO standards. Trends were assessed using the Jonckheere–Terpstra test. Logistic regression models were developed after variable selection with least absolute shrinkage and selection operator.ResultsThe pooled prevalence of stunting, thinness, and overweight across 2012–2023 was 473/1597 (29.6%), 388/1597 (24.3%), and 106/1597 (6.6%), respectively. Stunting declined significantly from 40.8% in 2012 to 25.7% in 2023, while thinness increased from 21.8% to 24.3%. In adjusted models, stunting was positively associated with age (aOR: 1.06; 95% CI: 1.02–1.10) and negatively with maternal post‐primary education (aOR: 0.71; 95% CI: 0.53–0.94) and rich households (aOR: 0.66; 95% CI: 0.46–0.96). Thinness was less likely among females (aOR: 0.46; 95% CI: 0.35–0.60), older adolescents (aOR: 0.90; 95% CI: 0.86–0.94), and the richest households (aOR: 0.52; 95% CI: 0.30–0.90). Overweight was inversely associated with age (aOR: 0.92; 95% CI: 0.86–0.99) but increased with post‐primary maternal education (aOR: 3.22; 95% CI: 2.09–4.96) and richest households (aOR: 2.72; 95% CI: 1.13–6.55).ConclusionAdolescent stunting decreased while thinness increased over the 12‐year period, highlighting a dual burden of malnutrition. Male adolescents and those from socioeconomically disadvantaged households remain particularly vulnerable to malnutrition. These findings underscore the need for integrated adolescent nutrition strategies within diarrhoeal disease management and urban health programmes in Bangladesh.

Introduction

Adolescence is characterised by rapid physical changes during puberty, accompanied by psychological development [1]. In 2019, UNICEF estimated 1.2 billion adolescents worldwide, of whom 89% lived in developing countries. In Bangladesh, adolescents represent about 20% of the total population [2].

Adequate nutrition is crucial at every stage of life [3]. Along with infancy, adolescence is a period of rapid growth with lasting impact into adulthood [4]. Weight gain during adolescence accounts for nearly half of adult body weight and skeletal mass, and one‐sixth of adult height [5]. Inadequate adolescent nutrition is linked to long‐term adverse health outcomes [5].

Malnutrition encompasses a range of conditions resulting from deficits, imbalances, or excesses in an individual's intake of energy and/or nutrients which can be categorised into undernutrition, micronutrient‐related malnutrition, and overnutrition [6]. In 2022, about 190 million children and adolescents (5–19 years) had thinness, 390 million were overweight, and 160 million were obese [6]. Historically, underweight was the main nutritional problem in low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs), but obesity and overweight have risen markedly. Most overweight or obese children now live in LMICs [7]. This trend is associated with rapid urbanisation, sedentary lifestyles, and higher consumption of processed foods [8].

In Bangladesh, recent data on adolescents aged 15–19 years showed 11% of males were undernourished and 9% overweight. Among ever‐married females, 4% were undernourished and 16% overweight; among unmarried females, 8% and 10%, respectively [2]. A 2018 study of Bangladeshi females aged 10–19 years found thinness at 15.7%, overweight at 6.0%, and stunting at 40.5% [4].

Malnutrition and diarrhoea fuel a relentless cycle. Undernourished children face higher infection rates and prolonged illness, while recurrent diarrhoea depletes nutrients, deepening malnutrition. This bidirectional relationship is well‐established, although among under‐5 children [9]. A study conducted in Bangladesh reports that 14 per 1000 individuals aged 5–14 years were suffering from diarrhoea [10]. However, despite a substantial burden, adolescent‐specific data covering the full 10–19‐year age range and both sexes remain scarce [11]. Adolescent health behaviours have long‐term consequences. Those with diarrhoea are important to study because undernutrition heightens infection risk, while recurrent diarrhoea worsens nutritional deficits. Monitoring their nutritional status provides insight into the double burden of disease and informs preventive and curative nutrition policies. This study aimed to address the evidence gap by examining trends and risk factors of stunting, thinness, and overweight among adolescents aged 10–19 years attending a diarrhoeal disease hospital in urban Bangladesh.

Methods

Study Site

This study was a retrospective cross‐sectional analysis of routinely collected data from the Diarrhoeal Disease Surveillance System (DDSS) at Dhaka Hospital, International Centre for Diarrhoeal Disease Research, Bangladesh (icddr,b), between 2012 and 2023. Established in 1962, Dhaka Hospital is the world's largest facility dedicated to the treatment of diarrhoeal diseases [12]. The DDSS began in 1979 and, since 1996, has systematically enrolled every 50th patient [4, 12]. Further details of the surveillance system are published elsewhere [4]. The DDSS routinely records age, sex, socio‐demographic characteristics, causative organisms, related clinical information, and anthropometric measurements. Height and weight are measured at both admission and discharge by trained surveillance staff using standardised procedures [13].

Sample Selection and Study Flowchart

Between 2012 and 2023, a total of 36,817 patients were included in the DDSS of Dhaka Hospital, icddr,b. Among them, 2104 were adolescents aged 10–19 years. The World Health Organization (WHO) defines ‘adolescents’ as individuals who belonged to the 10–19 years age group [14]. We included adolescents with available discharge height and weight measurements required for anthropometric assessment. Records with missing values or ineligible BMI values (< 9 or > 50 kg/m2) and missing height at discharge were excluded as data‐quality checks. The final analytic sample comprised 1597 adolescents. Operational Definition

Outcome Variables

The current study considered three outcome variables: stunting, thinness, and overweight. For the present analysis, weight at discharge and height at discharge, routinely collected in the DDSS database, were used. Weight was measured using a digital scale to the nearest 100 g, and height to the nearest 0.2 cm by trained staff following standard protocols [4]. BMI‐for‐age Z‐scores (BMI‐Z) and height‐for‐age Z‐scores (HAZ) were then generated according to the WHO 2007 growth reference for children and adolescents aged 5–19 years. Thinness was defined as BMI‐Z < ‐2 SD, overweight as BMI‐Z > + 1 SD, and stunting as HAZ < ‐2 SD [2, 4].

Independent Variables

The independent variables considered for the study were selected based on availability and literature review [4, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19]. They include age (in complete years), sex of the adolescent (male/female), sanitary latrine (no/yes), family member (≤ 4/ > 4), father's educational level (up to primary/post‐primary), mother's educational level (up to primary/post‐primary), source of drinking water (tubewell/others), treated drinking water (no/yes), garbage disposal at courtyard (no/yes), maternal media exposure (poor/adequate), blood or mucous in stool (absent/present), wealth index (poorest/poor/middle/rich/richest), and social class (lower/middle/upper).

Sanitary Latrine

This was a binary variable. We defined sanitary latrine use (‘Yes’) as any facility other than a hanging latrine, open pit, service latrine, dug hole, or open defecation (‘No’) [20].

Treated Drinking Water

This was also a binary variable. We defined ‘Yes’ as any household report of treating their drinking water by boiling or filtering [20].

Maternal Media Exposure

We assessed media exposure using a composite score based on the mother's frequency of: (1) reading a newspaper, (2) watching television, and (3) listening to the radio. For each activity, we assigned points: 0 for never, 1 for less than 7 days a week, and 2 for 7 days a week. We summed the points to create a total score (range 0–6) and categorised it as ‘Poor’ (< 3) or ‘Adequate’ (3–6) [16].

Wealth Index

We constructed a household wealth index to measure socioeconomic status using principal component analysis (PCA). The index was based on household assets, including construction materials (wall, floor, and roof) and ownership of specific items (television, cots, fans, almirahs, and cooking gas). This analysis produced five wealth quintiles: poorest, poor, middle, rich, and richest. To ensure comparability, we calculated the index independently for each survey year [16].

Social Class

We classified households into three social classes based on total monthly household income (in Bangladeshi Taka, BDT) following the methodology of previous literature [21, 22]. The ‘middle class’ reflects households with income that ranges between 75% and 125% of the national median income. The final categories were ‘lower class’ (< 12,500 BDT), ‘middle class’ (12,500–21,000 BDT) and upper class (> 21,000 BDT), after currency conversion to 2022 value [21, 22].

Statistical Analysis

Data analysis was performed according to variable type. Normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Continuous variables are presented as median (IQR), and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. The Jonckheere–Terpstra (JT) test assessed trends in adolescent malnutrition, thinness, and overweight. All tests were two‐sided, with significance set at α = 0.05. Analyses were conducted in Stata version 19 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). Simple binomial logistic regression models examined associations between independent variables and forms of malnutrition. Unadjusted odds ratios (uOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. Separate binary logistic regression models were fitted for stunting, thinness, and overweight because these WHO‐defined outcomes represent distinct nutritional conditions and were not treated as mutually exclusive categories.

Variable Selection for the Adjusted Models

All regression models used these binary WHO‐standardised outcome variables. Predictor variables for adjusted logistic regression models were selected using the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO). LASSO reduces variance and prediction error by applying a penalty to the log‐likelihood, thereby limiting model complexity [23]. It is widely used in high‐dimensional analysis for regularisation and variable selection, yielding parsimonious models with predictors most associated with the outcome [24, 25]. The enrolment year was considered as a continuous covariate in all multiple regression models to account for secular temporal trends during 2012–2023.

In this study, model selection was optimised using adaptive LASSO. Model accuracy was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, area under the curve (AUC), and omnibus model test p‐values. An AUC ≥ 0.6 was considered acceptable, and ≥ 0.7 as fair discrimination. Model adequacy was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness‐of‐fit test, pseudo R 2 statistics, and classification performance measures where appropriate (Supporting Table 2). Separate binary logistic regression models were fitted for stunting, thinness, and overweight. In each model, participants meeting the specified WHO definition were coded as cases, whereas all remaining participants not meeting that definition were included in the reference group, irrespective of the presence of other forms of malnutrition. For the CFM model, adolescents with co‐existing forms of malnutrition were classified as cases, while the reference group included adolescents with no malnutrition, as well as those with a single form of malnutrition.

Supplementary Analysis

In a supplementary analysis, we examined co‐existing forms of malnutrition (CFM), defined as the presence of two or more concurrent nutritional conditions among stunting, thinness, and overweight. Factors associated with CFM (yes/no) were assessed using binary logistic regression after variable selection with the same adaptive LASSO framework applied in the primary analyses.

Discussion

This study examined adolescent nutritional status in Bangladesh using DDSS data from 2012 to 2023, focusing on trends in stunting, thinness, and overweight, and their associated factors. The pooled prevalence of stunting, thinness, and overweight across 2012–2023 was 29.6%, 24.3%, and 6.6%, respectively. Stunting showed a clear decline across the study period, thinness increased, and overweight remained largely stable, with a slightly higher endpoint. Although the primary analyses considered each nutritional outcome separately, supplementary findings confirmed that a subgroup of adolescents experienced overlapping nutritional deficits, supporting the value of comprehensive nutritional assessment.

Stunting declined from 40.8% in 2012 to 25.7% in 2023, reaching its lowest level of 20.2% in 2019. The decline in stunting aligns with previous studies on Bangladeshi adolescents [4]. The recent fluctuation in stunting should be interpreted cautiously, as stunting reflects cumulative long‐term growth restriction influenced by multiple determinants, including maternal nutrition, recurrent childhood illness, household socioeconomic conditions, environmental exposures, and genetic growth potential. Additionally, recent disruptions such as the COVID‐19 pandemic may have contributed indirectly, as noted in the literature [26]. In our data, increasing age was linked with higher stunting prevalence, consistent with higher nutritional demands during late adolescence [27]. The overall decline is broadly consistent with previous evidence from Bangladesh indicating gradual improvements in adolescent nutritional status over time, although important socioeconomic disparities remain [28, 29].

Thinness peaked at 39.7% in 2021 and decreased to 24.3% in 2023, though this remained higher than 21.8% in 2012. The increase during this period may partly reflect disruptions related to the COVID‐19 pandemic [26]. Thinness was inversely associated with age, consistent with findings from the Global School Health Survey across LMICs [30]. The increase during the pandemic years may partly reflect COVID‐19‐related challenges [26]. Thinness was more common among males, consistent with national survey findings in Bangladesh [29]. As the growth spurt and development of secondary sexual characteristics vary between sexes, BMI among female adolescents tends to be higher [31]. Previous studies from Bangladesh have also reported a substantial burden of adolescent thinness in both rural and urban settings [28, 32]. Potential contributors may include inadequate dietary intake, meal skipping, household food insecurity, recurrent illness, and limited school‐based nutrition support during a period of increased nutritional demand.

After adjustment for other covariates, later enrolment year remained associated with lower odds of stunting and higher odds of thinness, whereas no significant adjusted temporal association was observed for overweight. These findings suggest that changes over time were not fully explained by shifts in the measured socio‐demographic characteristics and may reflect broader secular transitions in nutrition, health, and living conditions [4, 26].

Overweight declined with age in this study. While our data showed the association, possible explanations include increased autonomy [33], peer influence on dietary habits [31, 32], and changes in energy expenditure with age [33, 34]. It has also been suggested that as older adolescents become mature, they are more likely to take care of their nutritional needs themselves [34].

A child's nutrition is often impacted by the level of education of the mother, as reported by several other studies [4, 35]. Maternal education emerged as a consistent determinant of adolescent nutritional outcomes in Bangladesh. The World Bank's 2019 analysis found low maternal education to be significantly associated with adolescent undernutrition across multiple survey years [36]. In contrast, we found that adolescents with educated mothers had a higher chance of being overweight compared to adolescents who had mothers with lower levels of education. Similar findings have been reported in several other Asian countries [37, 38].

The association between wealth and nutritional status showed a dual pattern: richer households were protective against stunting and thinness but increased the likelihood of overweight. Similar findings have been reported in Bangladesh and other LMICs, reflecting the ongoing nutrition transition whereby improved socioeconomic conditions reduce chronic undernutrition but simultaneously predispose adolescents to overweight due to greater access to energy‐dense foods and more sedentary lifestyles [4, 27, 39].

In addition to isolated nutritional deficits, 8.6% of adolescents had co‐existing forms of malnutrition, indicating a subgroup with multiple anthropometric vulnerabilities. The clustering of nutritional deficits may reflect shared underlying disadvantages such as poor dietary quality, recurrent infection, and household socioeconomic constraints. Supplementary analyses showed that co‐existing malnutrition was less common among older adolescents, females, and those with maternal primary or higher education, which may relate to differences in caregiving practices, health awareness, and resource access. Similar overlapping nutritional burdens have been reported in low‐ and middle‐income settings, where adolescents often experience multiple forms of nutritional risk simultaneously [40, 41].

The use of 12 years of surveillance data collected through systematic random sampling is a major strength of this study. Limitations include reliance on surveillance data from a single urban hospital, limiting generalisability. As patients presented with diarrhoeal illness, infection and dehydration may have affected anthropometry, possibly overestimating thinness and underestimating overweight despite discharge measurements. Concurrent morbidities and dehydration severity were not included in the present models. The dataset lacked information on parental occupation, diet, and pubertal stage, factors that strongly influence adolescent growth and nutrition. Pubertal timing strongly affects adolescent growth trajectories, and the absence of such information restricts the interpretation of anthropometric outcomes. Stunting, thinness, overweight, and CFM may co‐occur within individuals. In addition, the CFM reference group included adolescents with no malnutrition and those with a single form of malnutrition. Therefore, comparisons of effect estimates across outcomes should be interpreted with caution.

Public Health Implications

Our findings have several public health implications. First, the persistence of thinness and co‐existing forms of malnutrition suggests the need for routine nutritional screening among adolescents attending health facilities, particularly males and those from socioeconomically disadvantaged households. Second, the association of maternal education with nutritional outcomes highlights the potential long‐term value of female education and household nutrition awareness. Third, the co‐existence of undernutrition with emerging overweight supports integrated adolescent nutrition strategies that address both nutritional deficits and excess weight gain. Finally, hospital‐based surveillance systems may provide a useful platform for identifying vulnerable adolescent groups and monitoring nutritional trends in urban settings.

Conclusion

This study identified a rising trend in adolescent thinness and a declining trend in stunting over time. Targeted interventions should prioritise male adolescents, those from lower socioeconomic groups, and adolescents experiencing co‐existing forms of malnutrition. Investment in female education may yield long‐term nutritional benefits. Although based on adolescents with diarrhoeal illness, the findings carry broader public health relevance. Malnourished adolescents presenting with diarrhoea may be at increased risk of adverse outcomes, underscoring the importance of integrated nutritional and clinical management. At the policy level, the results highlight the need to strengthen adolescent nutrition interventions within primary care and urban health systems, alongside surveillance approaches using hospital data to identify nutritionally vulnerable groups.
