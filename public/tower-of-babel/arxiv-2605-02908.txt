Memorization In Stable Diffusion Is Unexpectedly Driven by CLIP Embeddings
Bumjun Kim, Albert No

Abstract
Understanding how textual embeddings contribute to memorization in text-to-image diffusion models is crucial for both interpretability and safety. This paper investigates an unexpected behavior of CLIP embeddings in Stable Diffusion, revealing that the model disproportionately relies on specific embeddings. We categorize input tokens as <startoftext> , <prompt> , <endoftext> and <pad> with corresponding embeddings 𝐯 𝐬𝐨𝐭 , 𝐯 𝐩𝐫 , 𝐯 𝐞𝐨𝐭 , 𝐯 𝐩𝐚𝐝 \mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{sot}},\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{pr}},\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{eot}},\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{pad}} . We discover that 𝐯 𝐩𝐫 \mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{pr}} contribute minimally to generation in memorized cases. In contrast, 𝐯 𝐩𝐚𝐝 \mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{pad}} strongly affect memorization due to their structural duplication of 𝐯 𝐞𝐨𝐭 \mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{eot}} , the only embedding explicitly optimized during CLIP training. This duplication unintentionally amplifies the influence of 𝐯 𝐞𝐨𝐭 \mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{eot}} , causing the model to over-rely on it, thereby driving memorization. Based on these observations, we propose two simple yet effective inference-time mitigation strategies: (1) Replacing the tokenizer’s default <pad> from <eot> to the ! token before embedding, and masking the 𝐯 𝐞𝐨𝐭 \mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{eot}} ; (2) Partial masking of 𝐯 𝐩𝐚𝐝 \mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{pad}} . Both suppress memorization without degrading quality, and are readily deployable without prior detection. 1 1 1 Code is available here .

Our empirical analysis reveals a surprising trend: Stable Diffusion does not rely heavily on the
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{pr}}_{i}
during generation.
Instead,
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{pad}}_{i}
play an unexpectedly dominant role, particularly in memorized prompts.
We attribute this to a fundamental misalignment between how CLIP is trained and how diffusion models condition on input text embeddings.
Specifically, CLIP is trained via contrastive learning to optimize only
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{eot}}
to represent entire sentences, ignoring both
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{pr}}_{i}
and
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{pad}}_{i}
.
Diffusion models, however, condition on all embeddings during generation, leaving them exposed to embeddings that are not explicitly optimized.

The issue is further exacerbated by the tokenizer’s design:
prompts shorter than 77 tokens are padded by repeating
<eot>
.
As a result, the diffusion model attends to multiple near-identical
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{eot}}
in the form of
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{pad}}_{i}
, unintentionally amplifying their influence and increasing memorization risk.
Indeed, we find that all previously reported memorized prompts are fewer than 40 tokens,
resulting in over 30 near duplicated
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{eot}}
(see Appendix
B
).

Consistent with this mechanism, we confirm that the vulnerability largely disappears in Stable Diffusion v2.1.
While v2.1 adopts OpenCLIP
(
5
)
primarily for performance improvements, it also unintentionally removes
<eot>
from padding positions.
By removing duplicated
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{eot}}
, v2.1 substantially reduces exact-match memorization. This direct correspondence between our analysis and the behavior of v2.1 provides further support for our central claim.

Building on this insight, we propose two simple yet effective inference-time mitigation strategies that target the overemphasized role of
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{eot}}
and
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{pad}}_{i}
: (1) a tokenizer-level fix that replaces the default
<pad>
from
<eot>
to the
!
token and masks the
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{eot}}
; (2) partially masking the
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{pad}}_{i}
.
Both methods significantly reduce memorization without degrading image quality or increasing computational cost.
Crucially, our mitigation strategies not only suppress memorization but also restore diversity across different random seeds,
preventing deterministic reproduction of memorized content
and enabling richer generations even under identical prompts.
Unlike prior approaches that rely on dataset filtering, prompt tuning, or model retraining,
our approach requires no model modifications and can be applied efficiently, making them highly practical for real-world deployment.
Our contributions can be summarized as follows:

•

We present a new perspective on memorization by shifting the focus from model internals to the overlooked structure of text embeddings.
We show that a mismatch between training dynamics of CLIP and its use during diffusion model inference introduces structural biases.

•

We discover that
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{pad}}_{i}
are structural duplications of
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{eot}}
, unintentionally amplifying its influence during generation. This embedding-level mechanism creates a new pathway for memorization distinct from data duplication.

•

We propose two simple inference-time mitigation strategies:
(1) replacing the tokenizer’s default
<pad>
(
<eot>

!

\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{eot}}
;
(2) partially masking
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{pad}}_{i}

Preliminaries

2.1
CLIP Embeddings and Training Dynamics

CLIP
(
23
)
is a multimodal model that embeds both images and text into a shared representation space.
It consists of a Text Encoder and an Image Encoder,
whose embeddings are optimized through contrastive learning.
This process maximizes the similarity of text-image pairs while pushing apart unpaired embeddings.
A key aspect of CLIP’s text embedding module is its reliance on the
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{eot}}
as the main representative of textual information.
CLIP focuses on learning a compact representation centered around
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{eot}}
.
While all tokens in the text sequence contribute to the final embedding, their role is largely implicit,
as the optimization during training predominantly emphasizes the representation quality of
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{eot}}
.
Similarly, CLIP’s Image Encoder processes an input image through a Vision Transformer, where each patch is embedded and then aggregated via a learnable
[CLS]
.
The
[CLS]
embedding serves as a summary representation of the entire image.
This design choice simplifies downstream applications and ensures alignment between textual and visual modalities
in the shared embedding space.
The dominance of
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{eot}}
in CLIP’s text embedding process provides theoretical support for
prior findings
(
37
;
3
)
that highlight the importance of
<eot>
.

2.2
T2I Diffusion Model and Stable Diffusion

Text-to-image diffusion models, such as Stable Diffusion
(
25
;
22
)
,
leverage pretrained multimodal representations to generate images from prompts.
A common text encoder in diffusion models is CLIPText,
which transforms input prompts into a structured embedding representation.
While CLIPText training emphasizes
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{eot}}
as the primary representation, Stable Diffusion incorporates all token embeddings,
including those only implicitly trained through contrastive learning.
This distinction matters, as Stable Diffusion distributes attention across the entire sequence rather than relying on a single representative embedding.

Specifically, the input prompt is tokenized and embedded into a sequence with a maximum length of 77.
Prompts exceeding this limit are truncated, while shorter prompts are padded for uniform length.
These embeddings are processed in the cross-attention layers,
allowing the diffusion model to condition on the entire embedding sequence during generation as illustrated in Fig.
2
.
This design introduces potential side effects, as the model may attend to embeddings
that were not explicitly trained to carry semantic meaning,
potentially resulting in unintended behavior.

2.3
Memorization in Diffusion

Since the discovery of memorization
(
2
)
in Stable Diffusion, numerous studies have sought to understand its underlying causes and mechanisms from diverse perspectives.

31
showed that caption specificity is a major driver of memorization, and proposed multi-caption training and randomized token perturbations for mitigation.

9
pointed out that small dataset size and uninformative conditioning signals are critical factors behind memorization.

36
detected memorization by analyzing the magnitude of text-conditional noise predictions, identified trigger tokens, and mitigated memorization by perturbing tokens.

14
adopted a geometric framework to demonstrate that memorization corresponds to sharp, isolated peaks in the learned density and mitigated it by optimizing the initial Gaussian noise to lie in smoother regions.
Several works have analyzed cross-attention patterns to explain memorization.

3
discovered the “Bright Ending” phenomenon, where attention concentrates on the
<eot>
during the final denoising steps,
while
24
observed that memorized prompts exhibit lower cross-attention entropy, with attention disproportionately concentrated on specific trigger tokens.

3
Rethinking the Importance of Embeddings

Text-to-Image Diffusion models are conditioned on a sequence of text embeddings derived from user prompts.
As discussed in Section
2.2
, there is a fundamental discrepancy between CLIP’s training objective, which optimizes only the
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{eot}}
through contrastive learning, and the inference of models, which rely on the full embedding sequence including
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{pr}}_{i}
and
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{pad}}_{i}
.
This misalignment suggests that the factors influencing memorization may arise from structural properties of the embedding space rather than tokens.
Prior studies
(
31
;
36
)
have focused on “trigger tokens,” showing that certain tokens strongly drive memorization.
These findings are valid at the token level: modifying a trigger token before embedding can alter the output.

However, once the token sequence is encoded by CLIP, its causal encoder aggregates information across the prompt.
As a result, the influence that trigger tokens exert at the token level is largely absorbed into
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{eot}}
, and the individual
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{pr}}_{i}
play a substantially reduced role.
This illustrates a key point: the importance of a token before embedding does not translate directly to its importance after embedding.
Because diffusion models condition on all embeddings, not the raw tokens, understanding memorization requires analyzing how each embedding contributes to the generation.
Motivated by this gap, we shift the analysis from token-level to embedding-level mechanisms.
We perform a series of controlled embedding interventions to quantify how each element of the CLIP embedding sequence contributes to memorization.
Surprisingly, we find that the
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{pr}}_{i}
contribute minimally to memorization,
while
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{pad}}_{i}
, which are often dismissed as mere placeholders,
exert a much stronger influence in memorized prompts.

Claim 1
: Prompt embeddings (
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{pr}}_{i}
) have surprisingly minimal influence on memorization.

Claim 2
: Padding embeddings (
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{pad}}_{i}
) play a dominant role in both memorization and image quality, contradicting prior assumptions of their irrelevance.

Dataset.

35
released 500 candidate memorized prompts and labeled 345 of them as Matching Verbatim (MV), Retrieval Verbatim (RV), or Template Verbatim (TV). Prior studies
(
36

;
3
;

However, not all 500 qualify as reliable “memorized” cases: some reproduce only under specific random seeds, some yield severely degraded images, and others appear to match training images that are not the intended targets. Such issues can contaminate subsequent analyses. Examples of problematic cases are provided in Appendix

To avoid this, we restrict our primary analysis to MV prompts, which reproduce a near-identical copy of a training image consistently across seeds.
However, the original set includes only 86 MV prompts, limiting statistical reliability.
To expand this set, we leverage a subset of Membench
(
13
)
and apply two strict filtering criteria:
(1) Exact Match:
The prompt must reproduce a single training image.
(2) Consistency:
For each prompt, images generated using 10 different seeds must exhibit an SSCD similarity score
(
21
)

\geq 0.5
to the training image.
This yields 372 additional prompts, resulting in a final set of 458 prompts.
To assess the generalizability and robustness beyond memorized cases, we also evaluate 500 non-memorized prompts each from LAION
(

)

(
19
)

(

Evaluation Metrics.

Following prior work on memorization in Stable Diffusion
(
31
;
3
;
36
;
18
;
16
)
, we adopt SSCD
(
21
)
as the primary metric to determine whether memorization occurs.
To ensure a stricter and more conservative evaluation, we follow the most rigorous threshold used in previous studies, setting SSCD
\geq 0.5
as the criterion for memorization.
Instead of comparing to the real training image, we measure the SSCD between each intervention output and the image generated using the original embedding. A score near 0.5 indicates minimal visual difference from the image generated using the original embedding.
For generation quality, we use CLIPScore
(
10
)
to assess text-image alignment, and Aesthetic Score
(
29
)
to evaluate overall visual quality, as it provides a more direct and interpretable
measure of per-image quality than FID
(
13
)
.
Results are averaged over 10 random seeds; means are computed over all generated images, and
\pm
values denote the average image-wise standard deviation across seeds.

4
Understanding & Mitigating Memorization

4.1
Repeated
<eot>
Drives Memorization

Figure 6
:
Comparison of mitigation results across five methods using the same prompt and seed.
Our method (second column) mitigates memorization while preserving structure and prompt alignment.

24
reduce memorization but introduce distortion, while
36
, RTA and RNA
(
31
)
often fail to mitigate memorization. Additional examples are in Appendix
A.2
.

Our experiments reveal that the
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{pad}}_{i}
play a disproportionately large role in memorization.
We hypothesize that this effect arises from a structural flaw in the tokenizer design of Stable Diffusion v1.4.
As detailed in Section
2.2
, this version uses CLIPText, which pads all prompts to a fixed length of 77 tokens by repeatedly inserting the
<eot>
.

This results in multiple occurrences of the same token,
<eot>
, within a single input sequence: once at its intended position and repeatedly in the
<pad>
region.
Because these tokens are identical, the resulting embeddings are also nearly identical (see Appendix
C
for PCA and t-SNE visualizations).
Moreover, CLIP is trained via contrastive learning where only
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{eot}}
is explicitly optimized to represent sentence-level semantics.
This makes
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{eot}}
far more semantically meaningful than other embeddings.

Our key claim is that the repetition of
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{eot}}
across the input sequence unintentionally amplifies its effect during conditioning in diffusion,
leading the model to overfit to specific examples, especially for short prompts where the padding region occupies the majority of the input.

To further validate this claim, we compare Stable Diffusion v1.4 and v2.1.
We observe that exact matching memorization is significantly less frequent in v2.1.
While this reduction has often been attributed to dataset de-duplication
(
35
)
,
prior work
(
31
)
argued that data de-duplication alone cannot fully eliminate memorization.
We argue that this phenomenon is consistent with the removal of
<eot>
from the padding region.
Because
<eot>
no longer appears in padded positions, its influence on the model’s attention and embedding dynamics is naturally diminished, leading to a substantial drop in memorization.

Notably, v2.1 replaces the original CLIP text encoder with OpenCLIP, primarily for performance improvements.
However, to the best of our knowledge, no official documentation or prior work on v2.1 has discussed the motivation behind replacing
<eot>
with a semantically neutral padding token.
This modification was likely incidental rather than intentional,
yet it inadvertently removed a key source of memorization observed in v1.4.
Thus, the absence of
<eot>
duplication, rather than dataset de-duplication or architectural changes, serves as the major driver
explaining why v2.1 exhibits almost no exact matching memorization.

Consequently, this design change reduces the model’s reliance on the semantically dominant embedding
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{eot}}
, thereby lowering the risk of memorization during both training and inference.
Taken together, these findings support our central claim: the unintended duplication of
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{eot}}
in the padding region is a key mechanism underlying memorization in Stable Diffusion.

Reducing
<eot>

Decoupling
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{pad}}_{i}
from
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{eot}}

To mitigate memorization, we find that reducing the influence of
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{eot}}
is essential. We therefore modify the tokenizer to replace the default
<pad>
token from
<eot>
to a semantically neutral
!
token. This change ensures that
<pad>
produce embeddings distinct from
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{eot}}
, thus decoupling
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{pad}}
from
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{eot}}
.
Empirically, this modification leads to a substantial reduction in memorization, as reflected in decreased SSCD scores (see Table
4
).
To fully eliminate residual memorization, we further mask
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{eot}}
by replacing it with
\mathbf{0}
.

Under this setting, memorization is almost completely removed (see Fig.
1
).
These results confirm our hypothesis that the memorization risk is amplified by the repeated occurrence of
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{eot}}
in
<pad>
positions.
By structurally decoupling
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{pad}}
from
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{eot}}
, we suppress memorization without degrading prompt alignment or image quality as evidenced by comparable CLIPScore and Aesthetic Score.

\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{pad}}_{i}

Building upon our previous findings, we propose an additional mitigation strategy grounded in the same core insight: reducing the influence of
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{eot}}
effectively suppresses memorization.
While replacing the default
<pad>
can reduce duplication at the tokenizer level (Section

\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{eot}}

A naive approach might suggest masking all
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{pad}}_{i}

partial masking
to the
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{pad}}_{i}
.
This reduces the semantic redundancy induced by duplicated
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{eot}}

Empirically, we find that masking 70% of
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{pad}}_{i}
is sufficient to suppress memorization across most prompts while preserving semantic alignment and perceptual quality (Fig.
7

A key advantage of this method is its simplicity and flexibility: since it does not require tokenizer modification or model retraining, it can be easily integrated into existing pipelines.

5
Conclusion

Stable Diffusion exhibits unexpected memorization due to a misalignment between CLIP’s training objective and how diffusion models utilize embeddings.
Specifically, the repeated use of
<eot>
as padding leads to multiple near-identical copies of the semantically dominant
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{eot}}
, amplifying its influence.
We propose two simple mitigation strategies: (1) replacing
<pad>
with a neutral token like
!
and masking
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{eot}}
; (2) partially masking the
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{pad}}_{i}
.
Both approaches reduce memorization without
compromising image quality. Our findings highlight a structural cause of memorization and offer practical methods to address it.

Appendix C
Difference of embeddings between CLIP and OpenCLIP

In the CLIPText encoder used in Stable Diffusion v1.4, text embeddings are grouped into three clusters:
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{sot}}
,
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{pr}}_{i}
, and a merged cluster of
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{eot}}
and
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{pad}}_{i}
.
This is because the tokenizer duplicates the
<eot>
for padding, making
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{pad}}_{i}
nearly identical to
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{eot}}
after encoding.
In contrast, the CLIPText encoder of Stable Diffusion v2.1 yields a separate cluster for
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{eot}}
and
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{pad}}_{i}
, indicating a semantic distinction between the two.

Stable Diffusion v1.4 employs the original CLIP text encoder, where the tokenizer uses
<eot>
as the default
<pad>
. As a result,
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{pad}}_{i}
are near duplicates of
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{eot}}
, which amplifies the semantic influence of
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{eot}}
and contributes significantly to memorization.

Stable Diffusion v2.1 employs an OpenCLIP text encoder.
Importantly, OpenCLIP maintains the same overall architecture and embedding mechanism as the original CLIP,
with only minor differences in training scale and data.
The critical difference relevant to our analysis is that the OpenCLIP tokenizer uses a neutral
!
token for
<pad>
instead of duplicating
<eot>
.
As a result,
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{pad}}_{i}
are semantically distinct from the
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{eot}}
,
eliminating the structural duplication that contributed to memorization in Stable Diffusion v1.4.

The PCA and t-SNE visualizations in Figure
14
further illustrate this difference.

(a)
CLIP: PCA (top) and t-SNE (bottom)

(b)
OpenCLIP: PCA (top) and t-SNE (bottom)
