Ramsey-finiteness for graph pairs: A complete solution to the Burr-Erdős-Faudree-Schelp conjectures Thanks: Supported by the National Natural Science Foundation of China (Nos. 12471329 and 12061059).
Yaping Mao

Abstract
. For finite graphs G G and H H , let ℛ ⁡ ( G , H ) \mathcal{R}(G,H) denote the isomorphism classes of Ramsey-minimal graphs for ( G , H ) (G,H) . We prove two 1981 conjectures of Burr, Erdős, Faudree, Rousseau, and Schelp: Ramsey-finiteness is preserved by adjoining disjoint matchings, and ( G , H ) (G,H) is Ramsey-infinite unless both graphs are odd stars or one graph has a K 2 K_{2} component. We also refute Burr’s stronger 1979 survey conjecture via an explicit finite counterexample from the star-forest theorem, and reformulate Faudree’s 1991 theorem as a complete forest-classification corollary. Keywords: Ramsey-minimal graphs, Ramsey-finiteness, graph Ramsey theory, Matchings, Star forests AMS subject classification 2020: 05C55; 05D10; 05C70; 05C80.

1.
Introduction

All graphs in this paper are finite and simple. Unless explicitly stated otherwise, all target graphs have no isolated vertices and have at least one edge. For a graph
X
, let
V(X)
and
E(X)
denote its vertex and edge sets. If
Y
is isomorphic to a subgraph of
X
, we write
Y\preceq X
. For an edge
e\in E(X)
, the graph
X-e
is obtained by deleting
e
. The disjoint union of two graphs is denoted by
X\cup Y
, and
jK_{2}
denotes the disjoint union of
j
copies of
K_{2}
; thus
jK_{2}
is a matching of size
j
, and
0K_{2}
is the empty graph. We write

S(r)=K_{1,r}

for the star with
r
edges. A
star forest
is a forest whose connected components are stars, and an
odd star
is a star
S(r)
with
r
odd.

For graphs
F
,
G
, and
H
, we write

F\to(G,H)

if every red-blue coloring of the edges of
F
contains either a red copy of
G
or a blue copy of
H
. A graph
F
is
Ramsey-minimal
for the pair
(G,H)
if
F\to(G,H)
but no proper subgraph of
F
has this property. Such an
F
has no isolated vertices; consequently, under our standing hypotheses this is equivalent to requiring
F-e\not\to(G,H)
for every edge
e\in E(F)
. The family of all Ramsey-minimal graphs for
(G,H)
, considered up to isomorphism, is denoted by
\mathcal{R}(G,H)
. The pair
(G,H)
is called
Ramsey-finite
if
\mathcal{R}(G,H)
is finite, and
Ramsey-infinite
otherwise.

This finiteness problem is different from the usual Ramsey-number problem. Ramsey’s theorem guarantees the existence of some complete graph
K_{N}
with
K_{N}\to(G,H)
, but it does not describe the edge-minimal graphs with this property. The class
\mathcal{R}(G,H)
records all Ramsey-minimal witnesses to the Ramsey property. In many natural cases it is infinite, often with members of arbitrarily large order and very different structure. The Ramsey-finite cases are therefore exceptional, and the main question is to identify precisely which pairs
(G,H)
produce only finitely many minimal witnesses.

The study of Ramsey-minimal graphs began with the work of Burr, Erdős, and Lovász
[
3
]
, who investigated graphs of Ramsey type and already exhibited infinite phenomena for complete graphs. Nešetřil and Rödl proved several foundational infinitude results: for instance,
\mathcal{R}(G,H)
is infinite when both graphs are sufficiently connected, and also in the forest setting when neither graph is a union of stars
[
22
,
23
]
. These results introduced a guiding theme that persists throughout the subject: most graph pairs are Ramsey-infinite, while finite pairs arise from special low-complexity structures such as matchings and stars.

The first major finite family was obtained by Burr, Erdős, Faudree, and Schelp, who proved that if one member of the pair is a matching, then the pair is Ramsey-finite
[
8
]
. Around the same period, a series of papers of Burr, Erdős, Faudree, Rousseau, and Schelp treated stars, star forests, and forests. For star forests with no single-edge components, they proved that Ramsey-finiteness occurs exactly when both graphs are single odd stars
[
12
]
. For forests more generally, they proved broad infinitude theorems whenever a non-star component is present
[
11
]
. Faudree later completed the forest case by giving an explicit classification of the Ramsey-finite pairs of forests
[
15
]
.

There is also a substantial body of work on Ramsey-infinite pairs outside the forest setting. Burr, Erdős, Faudree, and Schelp proved infinitude results for pairs consisting of a star and a nontrivial connected or
2
-connected graph
[
9
]
. Burr, Faudree, and Schelp developed block-based criteria for Ramsey-infiniteness
[
4
]
. Łuczak proved that if one graph is a forest that is not a matching and the other contains a cycle, then the pair is Ramsey-infinite
[
16
]
. Bollobás, Donadelli, Kohayakawa, and Schelp studied further cyclic cases, including cycle versus suitable
2
-connected graphs
[
2
]
. Rödl and Siggers later showed that for complete graphs there are exponentially many nonisomorphic Ramsey-minimal graphs of bounded order, strengthening the qualitative infinitude picture
[
25
]
.

Ramsey-minimal graphs have also been studied from several more structural viewpoints. Examples include the classification of particular minimal families such as
\mathcal{R}(K_{1,2},K_{1,m})
and
\mathcal{R}(K_{1,2},K_{3})

[
5
,
7
]
, constructions involving stars versus graph families
[
6
]
, and descriptions of minimal graphs when one target is a matching
[
27
]
. Another active direction concerns minimum degrees of Ramsey-minimal graphs. Starting from the parameter introduced by Burr, Erdős, and Lovász
[
3
]
, later work obtained bounds and exact results for many classes of graphs and color numbers; see, for example,
[
26
,
17
,
18
,
1
]
. These developments are not needed in the proofs below, but they place the present finiteness problem within the broader theory of Ramsey-minimal graphs.

The only recent ingredient used in this paper comes from random Ramsey theory. Rödl and Ruciński located the threshold for the symmetric random Ramsey property
[
24
]
. The asymmetric analogue was conjectured by Kohayakawa and Kreuter
[
19
]
. Its
1
-statement was proved by Mousset, Nenadov, and Samotij
[
21
]
; further threshold cases were obtained by Kuperwasser and Samotij
[
20
]
; and the conjecture was resolved by Christoph, Martinsson, Steiner, and Wigderson
[
14
]
. We use this theorem only to prove that if both
G
and
H
contain a cycle, then
\mathcal{R}(G,H)
is infinite. Once this cyclic–cyclic case is available, the remaining cases follow from the classical forest and forest–cyclic literature described above.

We now recall the three conjectural statements that motivate the paper. The first two were proposed by Burr, Erdős, Faudree, Rousseau, and Schelp in their 1981 paper on Ramsey-minimal graphs for matchings.

Conjecture 1.1

(Burr–Erdős–Faudree–Rousseau–Schelp
[
10
]
)
.

If
(G,H)
is Ramsey-finite, then
(G\cup\ell K_{2},H\cup mK_{2})
is Ramsey-finite for all integers
\ell,m\geq 0
.

Conjecture 1.2

(Burr–Erdős–Faudree–Rousseau–Schelp
[
10
]
)
.

The pair
(G,H)
is Ramsey-infinite unless both
G
and
H
are odd stars or at least one of
G
and
H
contains a
K_{2}
-component.

It is important that Conjecture
1.2
is a one-sided statement. The original paper explicitly notes that the converse fails: there are pairs with a
K_{2}
-component which are still Ramsey-infinite
[
10
, p. 161]
. Thus the conjecture does not say that the two exceptional classes are exactly the finite classes; rather, it says that outside these classes infinitude must occur.

Burr’s earlier survey suggested a stronger finite characterization.

Conjecture 1.3

(Burr
[
13
]
)
.

The family
\mathcal{R}(G,H)
is finite if and only if either one of
G
and
H
is a matching, or both
G
and
H
are of the form
S(r)\cup qK_{2}
with
r
a positive odd integer and
q\geq 0
.

The purpose of the present paper is to record the corrected resolution of these three statements. The two 1981 conjectures are true, whereas Burr’s stronger survey conjecture is false. More precisely, we prove the following theorem.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
G
and
H
be finite graphs with at least one edge and without isolated vertices.

(i)

If neither
G
nor
H
has a
K_{2}
-component and
G,H
are not both odd stars, then
\mathcal{R}(G,H)
is infinite.

(ii)

If
\mathcal{R}(G,H)
is finite, then
\mathcal{R}(G\cup\ell K_{2},H\cup mK_{2})
is finite for all
\ell,m\geq 0
.

(iii)

Conjecture
1.3
is false. In particular,
\mathcal{R}(S(5)\cup S(2),\,S(3)\cup 122K_{2})
is finite.

The first assertion is exactly Conjecture
1.2
. The second assertion is Conjecture
1.1
. The third assertion explains why Burr’s stronger formulation cannot be correct: there are finite star-forest pairs in which one side has more than one nontrivial star component, and these pairs are not covered by the form
S(r)\cup qK_{2}
.

The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 collects the classical results used in the proof. Section 3 proves the cyclic–cyclic case using the asymmetric random Ramsey threshold theorem; see Theorem
3.2
. Section 4 derives the two 1981 conjectures. Section 5 gives the counterexample to Burr’s stronger conjecture and states Faudree’s complete classification of Ramsey-finite forest pairs.

3.
The cyclic–cyclic case

For a graph
X
, let

\rho(X):=\max\Bigl\{\frac{\edg(J)}{\vtx(J)}:J\subseteq X,\ \vtx(J)\geq 1\Bigr\}.

If
X
contains a cycle, define

m_{2}(X):=\max\Bigl\{\frac{\edg(J)-1}{\vtx(J)-2}:J\subseteq X,\ \vtx(J)\geq 3\Bigr\}.

Whenever
m_{2}(G)\geq m_{2}(H)>1
, set

m_{2}(G,H):=\max\Bigl\{\frac{\edg(J)}{\vtx(J)-2+1/m_{2}(H)}:J\subseteq G,\ \vtx(J)\geq 2\Bigr\}.

We write
G_{n,p}
for the binomial random graph on vertex set
[n]
with edge probability
p
.
The next theorem is the special case we need from the resolved Kohayakawa–Kreuter conjecture.

Theorem 3.1

(Asymmetric Ramsey threshold theorem
[
21
,
20
,
14
]
)
.

Let
G
and
H
be finite graphs, each containing a cycle, and assume
m_{2}(G)\geq m_{2}(H)
. Then there exist constants
0<c<C
such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\bigl(G_{n,p}\to(G,H)\bigr)=\begin{cases}0,&p\leq cn^{-1/m_{2}(G,H)},\\[2.84526pt]
1,&p\geq Cn^{-1/m_{2}(G,H)}.\end{cases}

We are now in a position to give the proof of our main theorem.

Theorem 3.2
.

If both
G
and
H
contain a cycle, then
\mathcal{R}(G,H)
is infinite.

Proof.

Because
F\to(G,H)
if and only if
F\to(H,G)
, we may assume
m_{2}(G)\geq m_{2}(H)
and set

d:=m_{2}(G,H).

Suppose, for a contradiction, that
\mathcal{R}(G,H)
is finite. Let
F_{1},\dots,F_{t}
be representatives of all isomorphism classes in
\mathcal{R}(G,H)
. Then for every graph
X
,

(1)

X\to(G,H)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\text{$F_{i}\subseteq X$ for some $i\in\{1,\dots,t\}$.}

Indeed, if
X\to(G,H)
, choose a subgraph of
X
which is minimal with respect to inclusion among all Ramsey graphs for
(G,H)
; this subgraph belongs to
\mathcal{R}(G,H)
and hence is isomorphic to one of the
F_{i}
. The converse follows from monotonicity.

We first show that every
F_{i}
has density strictly larger than
d
.

Claim.
For each
i\in\{1,\dots,t\}
, one has
\rho(F_{i})>d
.

Proof of the claim.
Suppose instead that
\rho(F_{i})\leq d
for some fixed
i
. Let
X_{i}
count the labeled copies of
F_{i}
in
G_{n,p}
with
p=cn^{-1/d}
, where
c
is the constant from Theorem
3.1
. Since
F_{i}
is fixed,

\mathbb{E}X_{i}=\Theta\!\left(n^{\vtx(F_{i})}p^{\edg(F_{i})}\right)=\Theta\!\left(n^{\vtx(F_{i})-\edg(F_{i})/d}\right).

Because
\edg(F_{i})/\vtx(F_{i})\leq\rho(F_{i})\leq d
, the exponent is nonnegative, so
\mathbb{E}X_{i}
is bounded away from
0
.

For the second moment, group ordered pairs of labeled copies by the isomorphism type of their intersection. This gives

\mathbb{E}X_{i}^{2}=\sum_{L\subseteq F_{i}}O\!\left(n^{2\vtx(F_{i})-\vtx(L)}p^{2\edg(F_{i})-\edg(L)}\right).

Dividing by
(\mathbb{E}X_{i})^{2}
, we obtain

\frac{\mathbb{E}X_{i}^{2}}{(\mathbb{E}X_{i})^{2}}=O\!\left(\sum_{L\subseteq F_{i}}n^{-\vtx(L)+\edg(L)/d}\right).

For every nonempty
L\subseteq F_{i}
, we have
\edg(L)/\vtx(L)\leq\rho(F_{i})\leq d
, so
-\vtx(L)+\edg(L)/d\leq 0
; the empty intersection contributes
1
. Hence
\mathbb{E}X_{i}^{2}=O((\mathbb{E}X_{i})^{2})
, and the second moment method yields

\mathbb{P}(X_{i}>0)\geq\frac{(\mathbb{E}X_{i})^{2}}{\mathbb{E}X_{i}^{2}}\geq\eta_{i}>0

for some constant
\eta_{i}
and all sufficiently large
n
. Since
X_{i}>0
implies
F_{i}\subseteq G_{n,p}
, equivalence (
1
) gives

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\bigl(G_{n,p}\to(G,H)\bigr)\geq\eta_{i}>0,

contradicting the
0
-statement in Theorem
3.1
. This proves the claim.

Now fix
i
. By the claim, choose a subgraph
J_{i}\subseteq F_{i}
such that

\frac{\edg(J_{i})}{\vtx(J_{i})}=\rho(F_{i})>d.

Let
Y_{i}
count the labeled copies of
J_{i}
in
G_{n,p}
with
p=Cn^{-1/d}
, where
C
is the constant from Theorem
3.1
. Then

\mathbb{E}Y_{i}=\Theta\!\left(n^{\vtx(J_{i})}p^{\edg(J_{i})}\right)=\Theta\!\left(n^{\vtx(J_{i})-\edg(J_{i})/d}\right)\to 0,

because
\edg(J_{i})/\vtx(J_{i})=\rho(F_{i})>d
. By Markov’s inequality,

\mathbb{P}(F_{i}\subseteq G_{n,p})\leq\mathbb{P}(Y_{i}>0)\leq\mathbb{E}Y_{i}\to 0.

Summing over
i
and using (
1
), we get

\mathbb{P}\bigl(G_{n,p}\to(G,H)\bigr)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{t}\mathbb{P}(F_{i}\subseteq G_{n,p})\longrightarrow 0,

contradicting the
1
-statement in Theorem
3.1
. Hence
\mathcal{R}(G,H)
is infinite.
∎

4.
The two 1981 conjectures

We now derive the original one-sided conjecture.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
G
and
H
be finite graphs with at least one edge and without isolated vertices. Assume that neither
G
nor
H
has a
K_{2}
-component. If
G
and
H
are not both odd stars, then
\mathcal{R}(G,H)
is infinite.

Proof.

We distinguish three cases.

Case 1: both
G
and
H
are forests.

If both are star forests, then Theorem
2.2
applies, because neither graph has a
K_{2}
-component; under this hypothesis an odd star has at least three edges. Thus
\mathcal{R}(G,H)
is infinite unless both graphs are odd stars. If at least one of
G
and
H
has a component that is not a star, then neither graph is a matching and Theorem
2.3
implies that
\mathcal{R}(G,H)
is infinite.

Case 2: exactly one of
G
and
H
is a forest.

Without loss of generality, let
H
be a forest and let
G
contain a cycle. Since
H
has no
K_{2}
-component, it is not a matching. Therefore Theorem
2.4
yields that
\mathcal{R}(G,H)
is infinite.

Case 3: both
G
and
H
contain a cycle.

This is exactly Theorem
3.2
.
∎

The following consequence is convenient for the proof of Conjecture
1.1
.

Corollary 4.2
.

If
\mathcal{R}(G,H)
is finite and neither
G
nor
H
is a matching, then both
G
and
H
are forests.

Proof.

Suppose one of the graphs, say
G
, contains a cycle. If
H
also contains a cycle, then Theorem
3.2
gives a contradiction. If
H
is a forest, then
H
is not a matching by assumption, and Theorem
2.4
again gives a contradiction. Therefore neither graph contains a cycle.
∎

We can now prove the matching-extension conjecture.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
G
and
H
be finite graphs with at least one edge and without isolated vertices. If
\mathcal{R}(G,H)
is finite, then
\mathcal{R}(G\cup\ell K_{2},H\cup mK_{2})
is finite for all integers
\ell,m\geq 0
.

Proof.

Assume that
\mathcal{R}(G,H)
is finite.

If one of
G
and
H
is a matching, say
G=tK_{2}
, then
G\cup\ell K_{2}=(t+\ell)K_{2}
is again a matching. Hence Theorem
2.1
immediately gives that
\mathcal{R}(G\cup\ell K_{2},H\cup mK_{2})
is finite.

Assume now that neither
G
nor
H
is a matching. By Corollary
4.2
, both
G
and
H
are forests. Starting from
\mathcal{R}(G,H)
finite, Lemma
2.5
implies that
\mathcal{R}(G,H\cup K_{2})
is finite. Iterating, we obtain finiteness of
\mathcal{R}(G,H\cup mK_{2})
for every
m\geq 0
. By symmetry,
\mathcal{R}(H\cup mK_{2},G)
is also finite. Applying Lemma
2.5
another
\ell
times to the second coordinate gives finiteness of
\mathcal{R}(H\cup mK_{2},G\cup\ell K_{2})
, and symmetry once more yields finiteness of
\mathcal{R}(G\cup\ell K_{2},H\cup mK_{2})
.
∎

Theorem
4.1
is precisely Conjecture
1.2
. Thus the original 1981 one-sided conjecture is true.

5.
Burr’s stronger conjecture is false

We now turn to Conjecture
1.3
. This stronger survey conjecture was already disproved by Burr, Erdős, Faudree, Rousseau, and Schelp in their 1981 paper on star forests.

Proposition 5.1
.

Conjecture
1.3
is false.

Proof.

Theorem 11 of
[
12
]
states that if
\ell
,
n
, and
s
are positive integers with
\ell
and
n
odd and

n\geq\ell+s-1,

then the pair

(S(n)\cup S(s),\,S(\ell)\cup kK_{2})

is Ramsey-finite for every

k\geq(n+2\ell+s-2)^{2}+1.

Take
n=5
,
\ell=3
,
s=2
, and
k=122
. Then

k\geq(5+2\cdot 3+2-2)^{2}+1=122,

so the pair

(S(5)\cup S(2),\,S(3)\cup 122K_{2})

is Ramsey-finite. However, neither graph is a matching, and the first graph
S(5)\cup S(2)
is not of the form
S(r)\cup qK_{2}
with
r
odd. Thus the finite pair above is not covered by either alternative in Conjecture
1.3
.
∎

Corollary 5.2

(Faudree’s classification of Ramsey-finite forest pairs)
.

Let
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
be forests without isolated vertices. Then
\mathcal{R}(F_{1},F_{2})
is finite if and only if, after possibly interchanging
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
, there exist integers
s\geq t\geq 0
,
m,n\geq 0
, and sequences

m_{1}\geq\cdots\geq m_{s}\geq 2,\qquad n_{1}\geq\cdots\geq n_{t}\geq 2,

such that

F_{1}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{s}S(m_{i})\cup mK_{2},\qquad F_{2}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{t}S(n_{i})\cup nK_{2},

and one of the following conditions holds:

(i)

t=0
and
n>0
;

(ii)

s=t=1
and both
m_{1}
and
n_{1}
are odd;

(iii)

s\geq 2
,
t=1
, both
m_{1}
and
n_{1}
are odd,
m_{1}\geq n_{1}+m_{2}-1
, and
n\geq n_{0}(F_{1},F_{2})
for the threshold integer from Faudree’s theorem.

Proof.

This is exactly Theorem 1 of Faudree
[
15
]
, rewritten in the notation of the present paper.
∎

Remark 5.3
.

Case (i) of Corollary
5.2
is the matching case from Theorem
2.1
, case (ii) is the odd-star case from Theorem

, and case (iii) is the extra finite family omitted from Burr’s stronger survey conjecture. In particular, Proposition

Combining Theorem
