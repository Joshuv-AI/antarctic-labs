Evidence of scaling advantage on an NP-complete problem with enhanced quantum solvers
Authors: Quanfeng Lu, Shijie Wei, Keren Li, Pan Gao, Bao Yan, Muxi Zheng, Haoran Zhang, Jinfeng Zeng, Gui-Lu Long
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Achieving quantum advantage remains a milestone in the noisy intermediate-scale quantum era. Without complexity proofs, scaling advantage—where quantum resource requirements grow more slowly than their classical counterparts—is the primary indicator. However, direct applications of quantum optimization algorithms to classically intractable problems have yet to demonstrate this advantage. Here we develop enhanced quantum solvers for the NP-complete one-in-three Boolean satisfiability problem. We propose a restricting space reduction algorithm that achieves optimal search-space dimensionality under mod-2 arithmetic, thereby reducing qubit requirements and time complexity. Numerical studies on instances with up to 70 variables demonstrate that our enhanced quantum approximate optimization algorithm- and quantum adiabatic algorithm-based solvers outperform state-of-the-art classical solvers; the quantum adiabatic algorithm-based solver serves as a lower-bound reference while retaining scaling advantage. Furthermore, experiments on a 13-qubit superconducting processor confirm the predicted improvements. Collectively, our results provide empirical evidence of quantum speedup for an NP-complete problem.

Main

Quantum advantage remains the central objective in quantum computing. Quantum optimization algorithms, including the quantum approximate optimization algorithm (QAOA)1 and the variational quantum eigensolver (VQE)2, are promising candidates for achieving quantum advantage in the noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) era3, which is characterized by limited qubit counts4–6 and the absence of effective error correction mechanisms7–10. QAOA balances optimization accuracy with gate usage to mitigate noise, while VQE reduces quantum resource demands through classical optimization. Because these algorithms rely on classical optimization, their time complexities lack rigorous characterization. Demonstrating quantum advantage therefore requires comparing the scalings of their time complexities with those of the best classical algorithms. So far, systematic studies in this area remain limited. Notably, two previous studies have investigated the scaling advantages of QAOA for the Boolean satisfiability problem11 and the low autocorrelation binary sequences problem12.

The Boolean satisfiability problem (SAT) is a central topic in computer science, combinatorics and logic, with broad applications spanning model checking13–16, combinatorial optimization17–19, automated deduction20,21 and cryptography22–25. The 3-SAT problem expressed in conjunctive normal form (CNF), as a conjunction of clauses where each clause is a disjunction of three literals, was the first problem proven to be NP-complete by the Cook–Levin theorem26. Here, NP denotes the class of decision problems whose solutions can be verified in polynomial time, and NP-complete problems are those in NP to which any other NP problem can be efficiently reduced, making them the hardest problems in NP. Notably, 3-SAT can also be expressed in one-in-three form, where each clause is satisfied if and only if exactly one literal is true. This problem is mutually reducible to 3-SAT in CNF and admits a simpler Hamiltonian representation, making it well suited for quantum optimization (Supplementary Section 1). Beyond its role as a canonical NP-complete benchmark, one-in-three SAT directly models exactly-one selection constraints that arise broadly in planning, scheduling, circuit design and verification, and structured cryptanalytic formulations, making it a practically motivated class of combinatorial optimization problems. To the best of our knowledge, there is no widely adopted dedicated benchmark solver for the one-in-three SAT problem; existing classical methods typically rely on reductions to exact cover or SAT in CNF. These characteristics make the one-in-three SAT problem a compelling candidate for demonstrating quantum advantage over classical approaches.

Several quantum algorithms have been proposed for solving the SAT problem using various quantum ground state solvers, including quantum annealing27, the quantum adiabatic algorithm (QAA)28–33 and the QAOA1,34–36. A recent numerical study11 demonstrated that the time complexity of QAOA with fixed parameters and constant depth, obtained from data training, for random 8-SAT problems with n ≤ 20 variables scales as 1.23n. However, these quantum algorithms are direct implementations of quantum optimization techniques applied to the SAT problem without optimizing the dimensionality of the search space, which can lead to large scalings or difficulties in parameter training11, hindering their ability to demonstrate quantum advantage over classical methods.

Here we introduce enhanced quantum one-in-three SAT solvers that exhibit quantum scaling advantage. We propose the restricting space reduction algorithm (RSRA), which reduces the number of candidate solutions from 2n to approximately 2n−m, where n denotes the number of variables and m represents the number of clauses. By integrating the RSRA with several quantum optimization algorithms, we develop enhanced VQE-, QAOA- and QAA-based solvers with reduced time complexities. Results

Overview of the enhanced quantum solvers

We study the one-in-three SAT problem, in which each clause is satisfied only when exactly one of its three literals is true. We mainly consider randomly generated positive instances, where all literals are unnegated, with clause-to-variable ratios \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{m}{n}$$\end{document}mn around the critical region near 0.626 (ref. 37). Here, n is the number of variables and m is the number of clauses. Formal definitions, Hamiltonian encodings and baseline reductions to classical solvers are provided in Methods.

As summarized in Fig. 1, our approach combines a classical RSRA with quantum ground-state solvers. The RSRA first relaxes each one-in-three constraint to an odd-in-three parity constraint over mod-2 arithmetic. The solutions of this relaxed problem define a reduced subspace of dimension 2n−k, where k is the number of mod-2 independent clauses and is typically close to m for the random instances considered here. Within this subspace, the remaining violation of the original one-in-three condition can be represented as a 2-SAT constraint. The following results quantify these effects through numerical simulations, scaling analyses and superconducting-processor experiments, and compare the enhanced quantum solvers with both original quantum solvers and classical baselines.

First, we randomly generate a one-in-three SAT problem with 150 variables and 94 clauses, in which 131 variables actually occur. We solve this instance using the enhanced VQE-based solver with random initialization and the Nesterov-accelerated Adaptive Moment Estimation (Nadam) optimizer. The expectation value of the Hamiltonian is calculated by multiplying 4 × 4 matrices (Supplementary Section 3d). We terminate the simulation when the norm of the gradient vector falls below 0.1, and determine success if the final energy is less than 0.5. In our experiments, 166 out of 2,000 attempts succeed, yielding a success probability of 8.3%, which is notable considering the problem size. We plot the energy curves for three successful examples and three failed attempts in Fig. 2c.

Next, we randomly generate a SAT instance with 100 variables and 63 clauses, in which 86 variables actually occur. Figure 2d shows that, as the number of layers NQAA increases from 2 to 53, the energy drops from above 12 to below 0.25, while the probability of finding a satisfying assignment rises from nearly zero to more than 0.8. We apply our enhanced QAOA-based solver to the same instance and optimize the circuit parameters with the Broyden–Fletcher–Goldfarb–Shanno optimizer. As NQAOA increases from 1 to 16, the minimum energy decreases from above 10 to below 1, and the corresponding success probability increases from below 0.1 to above 0.4, as shown in Fig. 2e.

Scalings of time complexities

In this subsection, we evaluate the performance of our enhanced solvers on random instances, following the standard convention in SAT problem research concerning scaling11,32,33. Scaling is defined as the exponential factor cn governing the typical effort required to solve randomly generated instances, and we use c as a quantitative measure of solver efficiency. To enable fair comparisons across paradigms, we avoid runtime, which is highly sensitive to implementation choices, hardware and parallelization, and instead adopt hardware-independent complexity measures. For quantum solvers, we characterize time complexity by the inverse success probability, which quantifies the expected number of executions required to observe a satisfying assignment. For classical solvers, we use the number of conflicts and propagations as realization-independent work units that track the dominant workload. These choices are consistent with recent scaling comparison cross-paradigm studies, which likewise quantify quantum complexity via inverse success probabilities and classical complexity via implementation-independent work counters11. For each solver and each n, we estimate the corresponding measures and perform a linear regression of its logarithm versus n; the fitted slope yields łog clogc and, hence, the scaling base c. Details of the parameter schedules, optimization protocols, sample sizes, classical baselines and fitting ranges are provided in Methods.

To evaluate the required number of layers, we first analyze the average success probability of the enhanced QAA- and QAOA-based solvers with increasing layer counts, averaged over 2,000 cases. Specifically, we calculate the scalings for QAA-based solvers up to 150 layers and for QAOA-based solvers up to 45 layers at the critical ratio \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{m}{n}=0.626$$\end{document}mn=0.626. The data are collected for problem sizes up to 40, which can be efficiently simulated classically. As shown in Fig. 2f,g, increasing the number of layers improves the scaling, reaching approximately 1.0032n for the enhanced QAA-based solver and 1.0076n for the enhanced QAOA-based solver. To strengthen the evidence for a quantum advantage, we fix the circuit depth to 150 layers for the QAA-based solver and 40 layers for the QAOA-based solver throughout the scaling analysis.

Discussion

Our findings suggest that problem-structured reductions can play a central role in the design of quantum solvers for combinatorial optimization. Rather than serving only as classical preprocessing steps that simplify input instances, such reductions can define the quantum search manifold itself. In the present setting, the RSRA exploits the parity structure of one-in-three SAT clauses to reduce the relevant search space from 2n to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${O}({2}^{n-m})$$\end{document}O(2n−m), and the resulting reduced manifold can be encoded directly into the QAA and QAOA ansätze. This construction provides a mechanism by which quantum dynamics can remain confined to a lower-dimensional, problem-informed subspace, rather than exploring the full computational basis. More broadly, this perspective may be useful for other constraint-satisfaction problems in which algebraic relaxations reveal lower-dimensional feasible subspaces.

The scaling estimates of various solvers presented in this work are derived from extensive calculations over the largest problem sizes accessible within our current computational capability. All results are reported with bootstrap confidence intervals, which improves the robustness of our conclusions. At this scale, our simulations indicate that the enhanced quantum solvers demonstrate superior scaling advantages in time complexity compared to classical counterparts. Three observations support our conclusion. First, the coefficients of determination for most solvers are relatively high after truncation, suggesting that the influence of polynomial factors has been largely mitigated. Second, the scaling of our QAA- and QAOA-based solvers could be further reduced through amplitude amplification40 and better schedule functions, while the performance of our VQE-based solver can be improved via more effective classical optimizers or better ansatz design. Third, the parameters for QAA-based solvers are deterministically chosen according to specific rules rather than through classical optimization, resulting in more reliable scaling estimates. While our findings provide empirical evidence of a scaling separation between the proposed quantum solvers and state-of-the-art classical approaches such as CaDiCaL, we acknowledge that the potential for highly specialized classical heuristics to further narrow this gap remains an open and intriguing question.

By employing the RSRA, we transform the one-in-three SAT problem into a 2-SAT problem within the solution space of the ‘relaxed problem’. As demonstrated in refs. 41,42, 2-SAT and quantum 2-SAT problems can be solved in polynomial time. We are exploring more efficient methods for solving the 2-SAT problem while maintaining the state within the solution space of the relaxed problem. For instance, we may employ the Quantum Monte Carlo method or quantum imaginary time evolution techniques to implement e−βH on the superposition state in the subspace, thereby finding the solution to the original one-in-three SAT problem.

Methods

One-in-three SAT formulation

The one-in-three SAT problem takes as input an instance with n Boolean variables and m clauses, where each clause contains exactly three literals. A clause is satisfied precisely when exactly one of its three literals evaluates to true. The objective is to find an assignment satisfying all clauses, or to certify that no such assignment exists. In randomly generated instances, clauses are generated by selecting literals from the variable set {1, 2, …, n}, and therefore the number of variables that actually occur in the clauses can be smaller than n. In this work, we primarily focus on positive one-in-three SAT instances, in which all literals are unnegated. These instances have a critical clause-to-variable ratio near \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{m}{n}=0.626$$\end{document}mn=0.626. Away from this value, typical instances are easier to solve, and we therefore mainly consider clause-to-variable ratios between 0.55 and 0.75.

For the pth clause, we denote its ith literal and associated variable by Cp,i and xp,i, respectively. The corresponding variable index is denoted by lp,i, so that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${x}_{{l}_{p,i}}={x}_{p,i}$$\end{document}xlp,i=xp,i. We define Wp,i(x) as x or 1 − x, depending on whether the literal is positive or negated, such that Wp,i(xp,i) = Cp,i. RSRA

We observe that existing SAT solvers have not fully exploited the independence of clauses as constraints. To enhance the efficiency of quantum solvers, we propose a classical reduction method—the RSRA. This algorithm transforms the original one-in-three SAT problem into a 2-SAT problem within the solution space of the ‘relaxed problem’, obtained by relaxing clauses from ‘one-in-three’ to ‘odd-in-three’. Each independent clause under modulo 2 reduces the search space dimensionality by half, leading to optimal search space dimensionality.

The ‘relaxed problem’ is defined by relaxing the clause restrictions from ‘one-in-three’ to ‘odd in three’. If all three literals in a clause are true, the relaxed clause is also satisfied. Accordingly, the pth relaxed clause can be expressed as Cp,1 + Cp,2 + Cp,3 ≡ 1, where ‘≡’ denotes equivalence under modulo 2. We impose the following restriction: two literals are selected from each clause and cannot be assigned 1 simultaneously. A solution to the relaxed problem is a solution to the original one-in-three SAT problem if and only if it satisfies this constraint. Applications to quantum SAT solvers

The RSRA immediately yields a quantum solver with time complexity of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${O}(\sqrt{{2}^{n-m}})$$\end{document}O(2n−m) by preparing a superposition state in the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathbb{S}}$$\end{document}S representation (Supplementary Section 3c) and applying Grover’s search, for which each iterate has gate complexity bounded by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${O}({n}^{2})$$\end{document}O(n2) (Supplementary Section 4c). The RSRA also facilitates the construction of ansätze that ensure the quantum state remains within the solution space of the relaxed problem. Consequently, a solution to the one-in-three SAT problem can be obtained by employing these ansätze and identifying the ground state of the Hamiltonian corresponding to the 2-SAT problem using corresponding quantum ground state solvers, including VQE, QAA and QAOA. These solvers operate within search spaces with optimal dimensionality, potentially achieving optimal time complexity.

All enhanced quantum solvers employ the same encoding as existing quantum SAT solvers and share the same Hamiltonian:6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${H}_{{\rm{RSRA}}}=\mathop{\sum }\limits_{p=1}^{m}{W}_{p,1}\left(\frac{1-{\sigma }_{z}^{{l}_{p,1}}}{2}\right){W}_{p,2}\left(\frac{1-{\sigma }_{z}^{{l}_{p,2}}}{2}\right),$$\end{document}HRSRA=∑p=1mWp,11−σzlp,12Wp,21−σzlp,22,where p, 1 and p, 2 can be freely replaced by p, 3. The phenomenon of barren plateaus has been extensively studied in quantum optimization, where the cost functions of parameterized quantum circuits exhibit regions in which gradients vanish exponentially with the number of qubits. This characteristic poses challenges for optimization algorithms in identifying directions for improvement, thereby reducing the efficiency of quantum algorithms. Recent studies have focused on mitigating and understanding the impact of barren plateaus. The emergence of barren plateaus is influenced by several factors, including complexity of the quantum circuit43, ansatz expressibility44, degree of entanglement in the quantum states45, noise46 and structure of the cost function43,47.

Furthermore, research indicates that the presence of barren plateaus is closely related to the two-design characteristics of circuits48 and can be assessed through the gradient variance of the loss function43. We demonstrate that, for positive one-in-three SAT problems, our enhanced VQE-based solver is free from barren plateaus if appropriate forms of L and T in equation (3) are chosen, as detailed in the ‘Absence of barren plateaus in VQE-based solver’ section. This characteristic indicates that our VQE-based solver is trainable, thereby enhancing its feasibility.

Scaling-analysis protocol

Throughout this work, we adopt a fixed-parameter protocol to enhance the reliability of the scaling analysis11,12. This protocol avoids hidden tuning overhead and reduces the risk of overfitting to individual instances. For each problem size, a single parameter set is determined and then kept fixed across all test instances of that size. Specifically, the parameters for QAA-based solvers are chosen empirically according to an adiabatic schedule, whereas those for QAOA-based solvers are obtained through classical optimization. The reported success probabilities are then computed by applying the fixed parameter set to each test instance and averaging over the resulting success probabilities.

For QAA-based solvers, as well as for initializing QAOA-based solvers, the parameter sets are chosen according to the principles of the QAA. In adiabatic quantum computing, one considers an interpolating Hamiltonian of the form17\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H(\,f(s))=(1-f(s)){H}_{0}+f(s){H}_{1},$$\end{document}H(f(s))=(1−f(s))H0+f(s)H1,where f is the scheduling function. In this study, we choose18\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f(s)={c}_{e}^{-1}{\int }_{0}^{s}\exp \left[-5{s}^{{\prime} }(1-{s}^{{\prime} })\right]{\rm{d}}{s}^{{\prime} },$$\end{document}f(s)=ce−1∫0sexp−5s′(1−s′)ds′,where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${c}_{e}={\int }_{0}^{1}\exp \left[-5{s}^{{\prime} }(1-{s}^{{\prime} })\right]{\rm{d}}{s}^{{\prime} }$$\end{document}ce=∫01exp−5s′(1−s′)ds′ is a normalization constant ensuring f(1) = 1. This schedule changes more rapidly near the beginning and the end of the evolution, while slowing down near the middle, where small spectral gaps are more likely to occur. In the discrete implementation, the continuous schedule is approximated by replacing the integrals with finite sums. The duration of each discrete time step is set to 0.3 for QAA-based solvers and to 0.1 for the initial parameters of QAOA-based solvers.

For QAA-based solvers, the performance is evaluated over 10,000 instances. For QAOA-based solvers, the parameters initialized from the above adiabatic schedule are further optimized using 2,000 training instances with a mini-batch size of 500 and the Nadam optimizer. The optimized parameters are then evaluated on 5,000 independent test instances. For VQE-based solvers, the average success probability is estimated from 1,000 test instances, using random initialization and the Nadam optimizer.

For the classical benchmarks, we are not aware of a widely adopted dedicated benchmark solver for random one-in-three SAT. Standard competitive baselines typically rely on reductions either to SAT problems in CNF or to exact cover problems. The former can be efficiently handled by state-of-the-art classical SAT solvers, including MiniSAT49,50, Glucose51, Lingeling52,53 and CaDiCaL54, while the latter can be solved using the Dancing Links X algorithm55. For each problem size, we generate 5,000 instances and estimate the average numbers of conflicts and propagations for all classical methods using the Python package PYSAT. For Grover-based solvers, the dominant complexity is estimated from the square root of the average search-space dimensionality.

Data for different solvers are collected up to different maximum problem sizes and are truncated appropriately to mitigate finite-size effects11,12. Data for the enhanced QAA- and QAOA-based solvers are collected up to n = 70 and fitted after truncation at n = 45. Data for QAA-based solvers without classical reduction are collected only for 5 ≤ n ≤ 12. Data for QAOA-based solvers without classical reduction are collected up to n = 17 and fitted after truncation at n = 9. Data for the classical and VQE-based solvers are collected up to n = 125 and fitted after truncation at n = 70. Data for the Grover-based solver are collected up to n = 20 and fitted after truncation at n = 10.

To empirically disentangle the gains arising from the RSRA from those arising from quantum effects, we additionally evaluate two RSRA-aware classical baselines. The first baseline performs random sampling from the RSRA-induced subspace. If this subspace has size 2n−k and contains a satisfying assignments of the original one-in-three SAT instance, then the single-trial success probability is a/2n−k, and the expected time complexity scales as 2n−k/a. The second baseline is a stronger RSRA-aware heuristic based on a random-walk search over the restricted subspace. Starting from a random initial state, the algorithm repeatedly identifies a violated clause and applies a structured ‘flip’ that keeps the state within the RSRA-induced subspace. This provides a best-effort classical search strategy under the same subspace restriction as our quantum ansätze, and its performance is quantified by the inverse success probability after a fixed number of flips. Detailed definitions and scaling results for these RSRA-aware classical solvers are provided in Supplementary Section 5b.
