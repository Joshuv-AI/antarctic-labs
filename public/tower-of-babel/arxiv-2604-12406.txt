Abstract
Deploying machine learning (ML) algorithms on mobile phones is bottlenecked by performance degradation under dynamic, real-world conditions that differ from the offline training conditions. While continual learning and adaptation are essential to mitigate this distributional shift, conventional online learning methods are often computationally prohibitive for resource-constrained devices. In this paper, we propose LightTune, a lightweight, backpropagation-free online fine-tuning framework with provable convergence guarantees. LightTune opportunistically refines ML models using live test-time data only when performance falls below a predefined threshold, ensuring minimal computational overhead and efficient responsiveness. As a practical demonstration, we integrate LightTune into a block error rate (BLER) prediction algorithm for 6G mobile systems. This integration enables the ML BLER prediction model to dynamically adapt to previously unseen channel conditions in real time. Simulation results show a substantial reduction in the average BLER prediction error by up to 48.8 % 48.8\% with online fine-tuning. Furthermore, we leverage this BLER prediction algorithm for link adaptation and demonstrate average throughput improvements by up to 15.5 % 15.5\% compared to a conventional table-based outer loop link adaptation (OLLA) algorithm.

These ML models are typically trained offline using synthetic datasets that fail to completely capture the complexity and variability of real-world environments. Even when empirical data is utilized during training, the highly dynamic nature of wireless channels frequently causes significant post-deployment performance degradation

To mitigate this training-test mismatch, ML models must continuously adapt to dynamic deployment environments through online learning

LightTune is specifically designed for prediction tasks where ground-truth metrics, such as link-level throughput or block error rate (BLER), become available after a brief delay. Such metrics are inherently available in wireless communications and networked systems. By leveraging this delayed ground-truth data, LightTune performs targeted updates to continuously improve model accuracy in a direct, supervised manner. II-A

The Forward-Forward (FF) Algorithm

In contrast to the usual ML training using backpropagation (BP), the FF algorithm trains each layer locally sequentially without backpropagation. This offers the advantage of avoiding the complex operations of propagating the error derivatives backward through the layers. Hence, it is appealing when used for resource-constrained devices such as UE that may not support BP. The FF algorithm is primarily used for image classification problems and is based on two forward passes
[
11
]
.

1.

Forward Pass with Positive Data Samples

A positive data sample is defined as the tuple
(\bm{x},y_{\text{+}})
, where
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the input
d
-dimensional feature vector and
y_{\text{+}}\in\mathcal{Y}=\{y_{1},\dots,y_{C}\}
is the corresponding true label. The positive pass operates on the positive data and adjusts the model parameters with the goal of increasing a goodness value in every layer above a predefined threshold
T
.

2.

Forward Pass with Negative Data Samples

A negative data sample is defined as the tuple
(\bm{x},y_{\text{-}})
, where
y_{\text{-}}\in\mathcal{Y}\setminus{\{y_{\text{+}}\}}
is an incorrect label drawn from a distribution over incorrect labels (e.g., uniformly at random). The negative pass operates on negative data and adjusts the model parameters to decrease a goodness value in every layer below the threshold
T
.

•

Label Encoding.
While label encoding can be implemented via various methods
[
11
]
, the standard approach for image-based tasks involves embedding a one-hot representation of the label into a specific subset of input pixels. This “overlay” ensures that label and spatial features are processed jointly from the initial layers of the network. Given that LightTune operates on one-dimensional feature vectors, we adopt a concatenation-based encoding. Specifically, the label
y\in\mathcal{Y}
is appended to the feature vector
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
to form the augmented input vector
\bm{z}=[\bm{x}^{\top},y]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
.

•

End-to-end Mapping.
We denote the model parameters as
\bm{\theta}=[\bm{\theta}_{1}^{\top},\bm{\theta}_{2}^{\top},\dots,\bm{\theta}_{L}^{\top}]^{\top}
, where
\bm{\theta}_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{l}}
represents the parameter vector of layer
l\in\{1,2,\dots,L\}
. Each layer implements a transformation
f_{l}(\cdot;\bm{\theta}_{l})
that maps an input representation to a subsequent hidden state. For a given feature vector
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a candidate scalar label
y\in\mathcal{Y}
, we construct the augmented input
\bm{z}=[\bm{x}^{\top},y]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
, which serves as the initial activation
\bm{h}_{0}
. The model’s forward pass is defined by the recursive composition of these layer-wise functions,
\bm{h}_{l}=f_{l}(\bm{h}_{l-1};\bm{\theta}_{l})
, such that the final activation vector
\bm{h}_{L}
is expressed as

\bm{h}_{L}:=F_{\bm{\theta}}^{(L)}(\bm{z})=(f_{L}\circ f_{L-1}\circ\cdots\circ f_{1})(\bm{z}),

(1)

where
F_{\bm{\theta}}^{(l)}:\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\to\mathbb{R}^{M_{l}}
denotes the cumulative mapping from the initial augmented input space to the activation space of the
l
-th layer. By convention, the base case is defined as
\bm{h}_{0}=F_{\bm{\theta}}^{(0)}(\bm{z})=\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
and
M_{0}=d+1
.

•

Goodness Function.
The compatibility between the features
\bm{x}
and the candidate label
y
is quantified by a goodness function
G_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x},y)
, defined as the squared
\ell_{2}
-norm of the activations at the terminal layer as follows

G_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x},y)=\|\bm{h}_{L}\|_{2}^{2}=\|F_{\bm{\theta}}^{(L)}([\bm{x}^{\top},y]^{\top})\|_{2}^{2}.

(2)

The learning objective is to maximize
G_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x},y)
for positive samples (ground-truth pairings) and minimize it for negative samples (incorrect or corrupted pairings). By optimizing the goodness, the ML model learns to map the high-dimensional feature-label relationship into a latent space where correct associations (i.e., ground-truth pairings) exhibit a higher magnitude of activation.

•

Let
\bm{h}_{\text{+},l}^{(i)}
and
\bm{h}_{\text{-},l}^{(i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{M_{l}}
denote the activation vectors of layer
l
for the
i
-th positive and negative samples, respectively. The per-neuron positive and negative goodness values are then defined as

\displaystyle g_{\text{+},l}^{(i)}[j]=(h_{\text{+},l}^{(i)}[j])^{2},\ g_{\text{-},l}^{(i)}[j]=(h_{\text{-},l}^{(i)}[j])^{2},

(3)

for each neuron
j\in\{1,2,\dots,M_{l}\}
, where
M_{l}
represents the number of neurons in layer
l
. The positive and negative goodness vectors for layer
l
are then given as
\bm{g}_{\text{+},l}^{(i)}=\left[g_{\text{+},l}^{(i)}[1],g_{\text{+},l}^{(i)}[2],\dots,g_{\text{+},l}^{(i)}[M_{l}]\right]^{\top}
and
\bm{g}_{\text{-},l}^{(i)}=\left[g_{\text{-},l}^{(i)}[1],g_{\text{-},l}^{(i)}[2],\dots,g_{\text{-},l}^{(i)}[M_{l}]\right]^{\top}
.

It is worth noting that the goodness function
G_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x},y)
defined in (
2
) corresponds to the sum of these per-neuron goodness values at the terminal layer
L
, such that
G_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x},y)=\sum_{j=1}^{M_{L}}g_{L}[j]
, where
g_{L}[j]=(h_{L}[j])^{2}
.

For a set of positive samples
\mathcal{S}_{\text{+}}
and negative samples
\mathcal{S}_{\text{-}}
, the Softplus loss for layer
l
is expressed as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{Softplus},l}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}_{\text{+}}|M_{l}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}_{\text{+}}}\sum_{j=1}^{M_{l}}\ln\left(1+e^{-(g_{\text{+},l}^{(i)}[j]-T)}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}_{\text{-}}|M_{l}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}_{\text{-}}}\sum_{j=1}^{M_{l}}\ln\left(1+e^{g_{\text{-},l}^{(i)}[j]-T}\right),

(4)

where

T
for positive samples and below
T
for negative samples.

II-B

Experience Replays

Experience replay (ER) is widely utilized in RL and continual learning to decouple temporal correlations and mitigate catastrophic forgetting by storing and replaying past observations
[
26
,
29
,
19
]
. While uniform experience replay methods treat all stored experiences with equal importance
[
19
]
, prioritized experience replay (PER) selectively samples “surprising” experiences that have high prediction error to accelerate convergence
[
29
]
.

LightTune is inspired by the prioritized selection, focusing its computational resources on high-error samples to drive online adaptation. However, unlike traditional approaches that require memory-intensive replay buffers to store and re-sample data, LightTune operates in a
buffer-less
manner. By dynamically identifying informative samples on a per-sample basis using a threshold-based policy, LightTune performs immediate fine-tuning on-the-fly. This eliminates the storage overhead associated with experience replay, making the framework uniquely suited for the stringent memory and latency constraints of wireless modems and edge devices.

II-C

Related Work

The problem of adapting ML-based wireless algorithms after deployment under real-world practical constraints such as avoiding backpropagation has received limited attention in the literature. We review the closely-related prior works with focus on the link adaptation problem.

1.

Offline Training.
Several prior works adopt an offline training paradigm, where the ML model is trained once and its parameters remain fixed after deployment
[
10
,
32
,
5
]
. In this offline phase, the primary objective is to construct a training dataset that broadly anticipates and represents all potential deployment scenarios which is an exhaustive process that is inherently time-consuming. Furthermore, despite this significant upfront complex training process, this static approach suffers from training-test mismatches, often resulting in significantly degraded inference performance within dynamic environments.

2.

Offline Training with Online Calibration/Adaptation.
Several calibration techniques attempt to mitigate training-test mismatches by adjusting the model’s output rather than its internal parameters
[
8
,
14
,
5
]
. These approaches rely on acknowledgment (ACK) and negative acknowledgment (NACK) signals obtained from the cyclic redundancy check (CRC) validation of physical downlink shared channel (PDSCH) transmissions. By tracking these signals, an empirical metric, such as the short-term BLER, is computed to dynamically adjust or scale the ML predictions. However, because the underlying ML model parameters remain frozen, these methods act as a superficial outdated correction rather than enabling the model to inherently adapt to the deployment environment.

3.

Reinforcement Learning.
Although deep RL has been widely explored for continuous adaptation of ML-based wireless algorithms
[
28
,
7
,
35
]
, such solutions typically overlook the strict hardware limitations of wireless modems. Specifically, continuous RL training demands substantial memory for experience replay buffers, alongside significant computational power for ongoing backpropagation and gradient evaluations, making it largely prohibitive for resource-constrained devices. Furthermore, by operating purely on indirect reward signals, RL methodologies fail to exploit a critical structural advantage in wireless systems: the inherent availability of delayed
ground-truth labels
(e.g., ACKs/NACKs). Rather than relying on trial-and-error exploration, these delayed labels can be utilized to enable highly efficient, targeted supervised fine-tuning.

4.

Table-based Baselines.
Prior to the proliferation of ML-driven techniques, table-based algorithms served as the de facto standard for wireless communications. Traditional link adaptation maps estimated channel conditions, such as signal-to-interference-plus-noise ratio (SINR) or mean mutual information per bit (MMIB), to a channel quality indicator (CQI) index using predefined lookup tables
[
23
]
.

To adapt to dynamic environments, outer loop link adaptation (OLLA) algorithms utilize ACK/NACK feedback to continuously adjust an offset margin. While recent advancements, such as the exponential decay mechanism
[
21
]
, aim to accelerate OLLA convergence, a fundamental structural limitation remains. Specifically, conventional OLLA updates are typically isolated to the specific CQI currently scheduled for transmission. Because the remainder of the lookup table is not jointly updated, transitioning to a different CQI relies on stale, uncalibrated mappings. This severely bottlenecks the overall adaptation process and frequently results in the selection of CQIs with high short-term BLER, as pointed out in our earlier work
[
6
]
.

5.

Parametric Adaptation.
Concurrent
[
33
]
and subsequent research
[
20
]
tackle the link adaptation problem by modeling the mapping between signal quality and BLER using differentiable sigmoid functions. These approaches propose an online convex optimization framework, implemented at the
base station
which applies continuous gradient descent to minimize the cross-entropy loss between ACK/NACK feedback and predicted BLER. While these methods provide smoother margin adjustments than conventional OLLA, they rely on one-dimensional parametric heuristics and require continuous gradient evaluations. Consequently, they remain agnostic to high-dimensional UE-side channel dynamics, such as Doppler and delay spreads, and lack the representational capacity required to proactively adapt to the complex non-stationarities of 6G networks.

Our Work:
To overcome the limitations of the prior approaches, LightTune opportunistically fine-tunes an expressive ML model capable of processing multi-dimensional feature set. By employing a backpropagation-free online fine-tuning strategy based on closed-form local gradients, LightTune adapts to shifting environmental distributions.

III
Proposed Fine-tuning Algorithm: LightTune

We propose a closed-loop learning framework that enables adaptive refinement of a deployed ML model, initially trained offline, when its performance deviates from desired behavior. Initially, the FF algorithm is used to train a baseline ML model offline. Subsequently, the FF algorithm is employed for online fine-tuning of the baseline ML model. This mechanism is feasible in scenarios where ground-truth labels can be inferred at a later time. In wireless communication systems, such ground-truth labels can be derived from key performance indicators (KPIs), including BLER, bit error rate (BER) and throughput.

III-A

Proposed Loss: Quadratic Softplus Approximation Loss

The gradient computation for the standard Softplus loss function requires evaluating exponentiations and divisions. These operations introduce undesirable computational latency and hardware complexity for resource-constrained wireless modems. To circumvent this overhead, we propose an alternative smooth quadratic loss function that closely approximates the Softplus objective using only basic arithmetic operations. For layer
l
, the proposed loss is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{Prop},l}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}_{\text{+}}|M_{l}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}_{\text{+}}}\sum_{j=1}^{M_{l}}\left((g_{\text{+},l}^{(i)}[j]-T)^{2}-4(g_{\text{+},l}^{(i)}[j]-T)\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}_{\text{-}}|M_{l}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}_{\text{-}}}\sum_{j=1}^{M_{l}}\left((g_{\text{-},l}^{(i)}[j]-T)^{2}+4(g_{\text{-},l}^{(i)}[j]-T)\right).

(6)

Similar to the Softplus loss, this quadratic formulation serves as a soft thresholding mechanism that encourages the goodness values to exceed the threshold
T
for positive samples and fall below the threshold
T
for negative samples.
In the balanced case where the number of positive and negative samples is equal, denoted as
|\mathcal{S}_{\text{+}}|=|\mathcal{S}_{\text{-}}|:=S
, this loss function simplifies to

\mathcal{L}_{\text{Prop},l}=\frac{1}{SM_{l}}\left[\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}_{\text{+}}}\sum_{j=1}^{M_{l}}\left((g_{\text{+},l}^{(i)}[j]-T)^{2}-4g_{\text{+},l}^{(i)}[j]\right)\right.\\
\left.+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}_{\text{-}}}\sum_{j=1}^{M_{l}}\left((g_{\text{-},l}^{(i)}[j]-T)^{2}+4g_{\text{-},l}^{(i)}[j]\right)\right].

(7)

We provide the derivation and experimental validation of the proposed loss function in Appendix
A
, demonstrating that it achieves accuracy comparable to the standard Softplus loss. Throughout the remainder of this paper, the proposed loss function for layer
l
is denoted by
\mathcal{L}_{l}
for simplicity.

III-B

Closed-Form Gradient Computations

To optimize the ML model using the proposed quadratic loss, we compute the local closed-form gradients for each layer
l
of the multilayer perceptron (MLP). Let
\bm{W}_{l}
and
\bm{b}_{l}
denote the weight matrix and bias vector of layer
l
, respectively. We define the augmented parameter matrix
\bm{\Theta}_{l}=[\bm{W}_{l},\bm{b}_{l}]\in\mathbb{R}^{M_{l}\times(M_{l-1}+1)}
. This matrix formulation is equivalent to the flattened parameter vector
\bm{\theta}_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{l}}
introduced previously, where the total number of layer parameters is
d_{l}:=M_{l}(M_{l-1}+1)
. Correspondingly, we absorb the bias term into the forward pass by defining the augmented input activations for the positive and negative samples as
\tilde{\bm{h}}_{\text{+},l-1}=[\bm{h}_{\text{+},l-1}^{\top},1]^{\top}
and
\tilde{\bm{h}}_{\text{-},l-1}=[\bm{h}_{\text{-},l-1}^{\top},1]^{\top}
, respectively.

In LightTune, each update step utilizes a single positive sample
\bm{z}_{\text{+}}=[\bm{x}^{\top},y_{\text{+}}]^{\top}
and a single negative sample
\bm{z}_{\text{-}}=[\bm{x}^{\top},y_{\text{-}}]^{\top}
, where
y_{\text{-}}\in\mathcal{Y}\setminus\{y_{\text{+}}\}
. Consequently, we have
|\mathcal{S}_{\text{+}}|=|\mathcal{S}_{\text{-}}|=1
. The derivative of the proposed layer-wise loss with respect to the augmented parameter matrix is computed as

\nabla_{\bm{\Theta}_{l}}\mathcal{L}_{l}=\nabla_{\bm{\Theta}_{l}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{+},l}+\nabla_{\bm{\Theta}_{l}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{-},l},

(8)

where
\nabla_{\bm{\Theta}_{l}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{+},l}
and
\nabla_{\bm{\Theta}_{l}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{-},l}
denote the gradient components corresponding to the positive and negative samples, respectively. Assuming the use of rectified linear unit (ReLU) activations, these gradients can be derived using the chain rule and expressed in a closed-form matrix notation as

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\Theta}_{l}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{+},l}

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{M_{l}}\big[(\bm{g}_{\text{+},l}-T-2)\odot\bm{h}_{\text{+},l}\odot\mathds{1}(\bm{h}_{\text{+},l}>0)\big]\tilde{\bm{h}}_{\text{+},l-1}^{\top},

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\Theta}_{l}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{-},l}

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{M_{l}}\big[(\bm{g}_{\text{-},l}-T+2)\odot\bm{h}_{\text{-},l}\odot\mathds{1}(\bm{h}_{\text{-},l}>0)\big]\tilde{\bm{h}}_{\text{-},l-1}^{\top},

(9)

where
\odot
denotes the element-wise Hadamard product and
\mathds{1}(\cdot)
is the indicator function applied element-wise.

Since the online fine-tuning process utilizes exactly one positive and one negative sample, both the gradient computation and the parameter update are performed only once at each fine-tuning step. Crucially, each parameter update is purely local to its respective layer. By requiring only the current layer’s activations and the forward-propagated inputs from the preceding layer, this localized approach eliminates the memory and computational overhead associated with backpropagation.

III-C

Threshold-based Fine-tuning Without Replay Buffer

To entirely eliminate the memory overhead associated with experience replay buffers at the UE, LightTune employs a threshold-based update policy, enabling the model to be fine-tuned in an opportunistic, sample-by-sample manner. The
surprise
of an observation at time
t
is quantified by the prediction error, denoted as
e(\hat{y}^{(t)},y_{\text{+}}^{(t)})
, between the model’s predicted label
\hat{y}^{(t)}
and the delayed ground-truth label
y_{\text{+}}^{(t)}
. When this error meets or exceeds a predefined threshold
\delta
, the model identifies a distribution shift and triggers a local update. For this update, the true observation
\bm{z}_{\text{+}}^{(t)}=[{\bm{x}^{(t)}}^{\top},y_{\text{+}}^{(t)}]^{\top}
is utilized as the positive training pair. Simultaneously, a corresponding negative sample
\bm{z}_{\text{-}}^{(t)}=[{\bm{x}^{(t)}}^{\top},y_{\text{-}}^{(t)}]^{\top}
is constructed by pairing the current input feature vector
\bm{x}^{(t)}
with an incorrect label
y_{\text{-}}^{(t)}\in\mathcal{Y}\setminus\{y_{\text{+}}^{(t)}\}
. This lightweight, on-the-fly fine-tuning mechanism is detailed in Algorithm
1
and illustrated in Fig.
2
.

Negative Sampling
. We consider two strategies for selecting the negative label
y_{\text{-}}^{(t)}
used during the fine-tuning process.

1.

Uniform Negative Sampling.
In this scheme, the negative label
y_{\text{-}}^{(t)}
is sampled uniformly at random from the set of incorrect candidates

III-D

Fine-tuning Variants

While LightTune is compatible with stochastic gradient descent (SGD) and any optimizer, we focus on two variants based on Adam optimizer
[
17
]
. Adam is a widely used optimizer that adapts each parameter by maintaining two running averages (moments): the first moment (mean of gradients) at time
t
denoted by
\bm{m}_{t}
and the second moment (variance of gradients) at time
t
denoted by
\bm{v}_{t}
.

1.

Standard Adam Update:
In this variant, the Adam optimizer maintains an internal counter
t_{\text{A}}\in\{1,2,\dots\}
that exclusively tracks the number of executed fine-tuning updates. Specifically,
t_{\text{A}}
is incremented only when the fine-tuning condition
e^{(t)}\geq\delta
is triggered. This ensures the bias correction mechanism accurately reflects the true number of parameter updates. The updates reuse the moment estimates from the previous fine-tuning instance, following the standard recursive form

\displaystyle\bm{m}_{t_{\text{A}}}

\displaystyle=\beta_{1}\bm{m}_{t_{\text{A}}-1}+(1-\beta_{1})\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{L}^{(t)},

\displaystyle\bm{v}_{t_{\text{A}}}

\displaystyle=\beta_{2}\bm{v}_{t_{\text{A}}-1}+(1-\beta_{2})(\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{L}^{(t)})^{2},

(10)

where
\beta_{1}
and
\beta_{2}
are the exponential decay rates for the first and second moments, respectively, and
(\cdot)^{2}
is applied element-wise.

2.

One-step Update:
To entirely eliminate the need for storing the previous moments, this variant resets the Adam internal counter to
t_{\text{A}}=1
at every fine-tuning instance. Consequently, the historical moments are effectively reinitialized to zero (
\bm{m}_{0}=\bm{0}
and
\bm{v}_{0}=\bm{0}
), and the update simplifies to

\displaystyle\bm{m}_{1}

\displaystyle=(1-\beta_{1})\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{L}^{(t)},

\displaystyle\bm{v}_{1}

\displaystyle=(1-\beta_{2})(\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{L}^{(t)})^{2}.

(11)

This variant is memory-efficient because the UE is not required to store or maintain the high-dimensional first and second moment vectors across different fine-tuning steps, thereby significantly reducing the dynamic RAM footprint.

Remark 2

(
Rationale for One-step Update
)
.

In the online, sparse-update setting of LightTune, “surprising” samples frequently indicate a fundamental distribution shift in the underlying wireless channel. By resetting the internal Adam moments, the one-step variant ensures that the parameter update relies strictly on the current gradient. Consequently, the update simplifies to a normalized gradient step given by

\bm{\theta}^{(t+1)}\leftarrow\bm{\theta}^{(t)}-\alpha_{f}\cdot\frac{\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{L}^{(t)}}{\sqrt{(\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{L}^{(t)})^{2}}+\epsilon}.

(12)

When
\epsilon=0
, this one-step update rule reduces to

\bm{\theta}^{(t+1)}\leftarrow\bm{\theta}^{(t)}-\alpha_{f}\cdot\text{sgn}\left(\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{L}^{(t)}\right),

(13)

where
\text{sgn}(\cdot)
denotes the element-wise sign function. This gradient update is advantageous for a resource-limited UE, as it replaces computationally expensive divisions with highly efficient bit-sign extractions. Furthermore, by eliminating the inertia of historical gradients, this variant not only minimizes the memory footprint of the UE but also enables real-time adaptation to channel variations.

IV
Convergence of LightTune

We now provide the convergence guarantee for LightTune, showing that the average frequency of prediction errors reaching or exceeding any fixed threshold
\delta>0
converges to zero as the number of fine-tuning steps increases, despite the training-test distribution mismatch. The core intuition is that whenever the prediction error reaches or exceeds the threshold
\delta
, the model update reduces the loss by a guaranteed amount. Since the loss is bounded below, the total number of such improvements is finite. Consequently, large errors can occur only finitely many times, and their frequency must decay to zero.

We start with definitions, assumptions and useful lemmas that we build on to derive our main convergence result.

IV-A

Definitions and Assumptions

Definition 1

(
Error-driven Update Indicator
)
.

For an error tolerance
\delta>0
, the update at step
t
is controlled by a binary random variable
I^{(t)}_{\delta}
, defined as

I^{(t)}_{\delta}:=\mathds{1}\{e^{(t)}\geq\delta\},

(14)

where
e^{(t)}(\hat{y}^{(t)},y^{(t)}_{\text{+}})
denotes the prediction error.

Hence,
\mathbb{E}[I^{(t)}_{\delta}]=\Pr(e^{(t)}\geq\delta)
.

Definition 2

(
Filtration
)
.

The filtration

represents the history of the stochastic process up to time
t
and is defined as the
\sigma

t

\mathcal{F}_{t}=\sigma\bigl(\bm{\theta}^{(1)},\bm{z}^{(1)}_{\text{+}},\dots,\bm{z}^{(t)}_{\text{+}}\bigr).

We now state our assumptions.

Assumption 1

(
ReLU MLPs
)
.

The underlying ML model of LightTune is assumed to be a ReLU MLP.

Assumption 2

(
Bounded Input and Model
)
.

There exist positive constants

such that for all time steps
t
and all layers
l
:

(i)

\|\bm{z}^{(t)}\|_{2}\leq B_{z}
, where
\bm{z}^{(t)}=[{\bm{x}^{(t)}}^{\top},y^{(t)}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
,

(ii)

\|\bm{\theta}_{l,j}^{(t)}\|_{2}\leq B_{\theta}
for all neurons
j
in layer
l
,

where
\bm{\theta}_{l,j}^{(t)}
denote the parameters of neuron
j
in layer
l

Next, to model the training-test mismatch problem, we consider Assumption
3

Assumption 3

(
Training and Online Data Distributions
)
.

The offline training data are i.i.d. from distribution
\mathcal{D}_{1}
, and the online fine-tuning data

Assumption 4

(
Gradient Lower Bound
)
.

For any fixed
\delta>0
, there exists a constant
\gamma_{2}(\delta)>0
such that whenever

, the expected squared norm of the gradient (with respect to the random negative sample) under
\mathcal{D}_{2}
satisfies
1
1

1

\mathcal{D}_{2}

\mathbb{E}_{y^{(t)}_{\text{-}}}\!\left[\|\nabla_{\bm{\theta}_{L}}\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(t)}(\bm{\theta}_{L}^{(t)})\|_{2}^{2}\;\big|\;\mathcal{F}_{t},\;e^{(t)}\geq\delta\right]\geq\gamma_{2}(\delta).

This assumption is analogous to the gradient dominance or Polyak–Łojasiewicz (PL) conditions frequently invoked in non-convex optimization literature

. It ensures that the “learning signal” remains strictly bounded away from zero whenever the model’s performance is not as desired (i.e.,

IV-B

We now present the foundational lemmas that we build on to prove our convergence theorem. While detailed proofs are deferred to Appendix
D

Lemma 1

(

)
.

Under Assumption

(

)
.

Under Assumption
2
(i.e., bounded input and model), for all time steps
t
, layers
l

For any

IV-C

Convergence Theorem

The proof follows a non‑convex optimization approach adapted to the threshold‑based updates of LightTune. The key steps are:

1.

Local decrease.
When a large error occurs (i.e.,
I^{(t)}_{\delta}=1
), the gradient step reduces the loss by at least
\frac{\alpha_{f}}{2}\|\nabla\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(t)}\|^{2}
(by the smoothness and the descent lemmas).

2.

Gradient lower bound.
Assumption
4
guarantees that whenever the error is large, the expected squared gradient norm is at least
\gamma_{2}(\delta)>0
. Thus, each large error produces an expected decrease of at least
\frac{\alpha_{f}\gamma_{2}(\delta)}{2}
.

3.

Telescoping sum.
Summing these expected decreases over time and using stationarity of the data distribution yields the following upper bound on the total expected number of large errors up to time
N\geq 1
:

\frac{\alpha_{f}\gamma_{2}(\delta)}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}[I^{(t)}_{\delta}]\leq\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(1)}(\bm{\theta}_{L}^{(1)})]-\mathcal{L}_{L}^{*},

where
\mathcal{L}_{L}^{*}=\inf_{\bm{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{2}}[\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(t)}(\bm{\theta})]

4.

Consequences.
Rearranging gives
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N}\Pr_{\mathcal{D}_{2}}(e^{(t)}\geq\delta)\leq O(1/N)
when the offline and online distributions are identical. In that case,
\Pr(e^{(t)}\geq\delta)\to 0
.

5.

Distribution shift.
When
\mathcal{D}_{1}\neq\mathcal{D}_{2}
, Pinsker’s inequality introduces an extra term
\sqrt{2D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mathcal{D}_{2}\|\mathcal{D}_{1})/N}
, and the average frequency still tends to zero.

Theorem 1

(
Convergence under Distribution Shift
)
.

Suppose Assumptions
1
,
2
,
4
, and
3
hold. For a fixed error tolerance
\delta>0
and a learning rate
\alpha_{f}\in(0,1/\rho_{L})
, LightTune with SGD satisfies for any
N\geq 1
:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N}\Pr_{\hskip 5.0pt\mathcal{D}_{2}}\bigl(e^{(t)}\geq\delta\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\left[\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{1}}[\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(1)}(\bm{\theta}_{L}^{(1)})]-\mathcal{L}_{L}^{*}\right]}{\alpha_{f}\gamma_{2}(\delta)N}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{2M}{\alpha_{f}\gamma_{2}(\delta)}\sqrt{\frac{2D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mathcal{D}_{2}\|\mathcal{D}_{1})}{N}},

(22)

where
\mathcal{L}_{L}^{*}=\inf_{\bm{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{2}}[\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(t)}(\bm{\theta})]
is the minimum achievable expected loss under
\mathcal{D}_{2}
, the constant
M
satisfies
\sup_{t\geq 1}\lvert\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(t)}(\bm{\theta}_{L}^{(t)})\rvert\leq M
, and
\gamma_{2}(\delta)
is the gradient lower bound from Assumption
4
.

Corollary 1

(Asymptotic Convergence of Average Error Probability)
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
1
, for any fixed error tolerance
\delta>0
, the average probability of a significant prediction error satisfies

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N}\Pr_{\hskip 5.0pt\mathcal{D}_{2}}\bigl(e^{(t)}\geq\delta\bigr)=0.

(23)

Corollary 2

(
Pointwise Convergence under Identical Distributions
)
.

If
\mathcal{D}_{1}=\mathcal{D}_{2}
, then under the conditions of Theorem
1
, the series
\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\Pr(e^{(t)}\geq\delta)
converges, and consequently

\lim_{t\to\infty}\Pr\bigl(e^{(t)}\geq\delta\bigr)=0.

(24)

V
Applications of LightTune

In this section, we leverage LightTune for short-term BLER prediction and link adaptation.

V-A

BLER Prediction

We consider the downlink short-term BLER prediction problem as a practical application of LightTune. In this context, “short-term” refers to a prediction horizon spanning a few PDSCH transmission slots, typically corresponding to the periodicity of the CSI reports. Such short-term BLER prediction at the UE side is essential in advanced cellular systems (e.g., 5G/6G) for enabling accurate and timely CSI reporting.

We begin with a brief background. The CSI reference signal (CSI-RS) period denotes the interval between consecutive reference signals used for CSI acquisition. This interval determines how frequently the receiver (i.e., UE) can update its channel estimates and report CSI to the transmitter (i.e., next-generation NodeB (gNB)). CSI-RS transmissions can be configured as either periodic or aperiodic. In periodic mode, reference signals are transmitted at regular intervals. In contrast, aperiodic mode involves dynamically triggered transmissions, where the interval between reference signals is variable.

Remark 3

(
Using the FF Algorithm for Regression
)
.

The FF algorithm was originally designed for classification tasks. To adapt it for regression, we discretize the continuous target variable (i.e., BLER) into a finite set of discrete classes defined by
\mathcal{P}=\{p_{1},p_{2},\dots,p_{C}\}

to improve resolution correspondingly increases the computational complexity and inference latency. However, as discussed in Sec.
V-B
, our link adaptation framework only requires a coarse estimate to reliably determine whether the short-term BLER exceeds a predefined threshold, fully justifying this quantized approach.

V-B

In 5G, the UE reports a channel quality indicator (CQI) and a rank indicator (RI) to the next-generation NodeB (gNB), which reflect the perceived downlink channel conditions. Based on the reported CQI, the gNB dynamically selects an appropriate modulation and coding scheme (MCS) to optimize throughput while maintaining reliable communication.

The online BLER prediction algorithm of Section

It is worth noting that CQI-Tune only requires a coarse estimate of the BLER. Specifically, the goal of the underlying BLER prediction algorithm in CQI-Tune is to determine if the short-term BLER is below the threshold
\tau_{\text{BLER}}
or not. That is, CQI-Tune does not need to predict the short-term BLER accurately, but rather needs to predict if it is below the threshold
\tau_{\text{BLER}}
or not. Hence, it is critical to analyze the false alarm (FA) and the missed detection (MD) probabilities of the underlying BLER prediction algorithm denoted as
P_{\text{FA}}
and
P_{\text{MD}}
, respectively. The FA and MD events are defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\text{FA}}

\displaystyle:=\{\hat{P}_{\text{BLER}}\geq\tau_{\text{BLER}}\mid P_{\text{BLER}}<\tau_{\text{BLER}}\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\text{MD}}

\displaystyle:=\{\hat{P}_{\text{BLER}}<\tau_{\text{BLER}}\mid P_{\text{BLER}}\geq\tau_{\text{BLER}}\}.

\mathcal{E}_{\text{FA}}
is specifically critical as it leads to changing the decision of the table-based algorithm.
\mathcal{E}_{\text{MD}}
is also an important event, but it does not change the decision of the table-based algorithm.

V-C

Rank Indicator (RI) and Channel Quality Indicator (CQI) Selection

In 5G New Radio (NR) systems, the RI determines the number of spatial data streams (i.e., layers) transmitted in parallel over the MIMO channel. While the underlying physical channel may support a higher rank according to singular value decomposition (SVD), the lack of ideal waterfilling across layers often creates an inherent optimization gap between theoretical capacity and practical throughput. Consequently, the UE may strategically report a lower RI to facilitate a higher-order MCS (e.g., 256-QAM) on the dominant layers, yielding better aggregate throughput than a full-rank transmission that is bottlenecked by the weakest layers forcing a lower-order modulation (e.g., 64-QAM).

We begin with a brief overview of the RI selection problem.
The conventional table-based baseline algorithms typically first estimate the best CQI for each possible RI. Then, they select the RI that maximizes the expected spectral efficiency (SE).
Specifically, denoting the CQI selected by the table-based algorithm for
\mathrm{RI}=i
as
\mathrm{CQI}^{(i)}
, the RI selected by the conventional table-based algorithm is given by

\displaystyle r\triangleq\arg\max_{i\in\{1,\cdots,r_{\text{max}}\}}\ \{i\cdot\mathrm{SE}(i,\mathrm{CQI}^{(i)})\},

(27)

where
r_{\text{max}}
denotes the maximum supported rank and
\mathrm{SE}(i,\mathrm{CQI}^{(i)})
denotes the estimated SE per-layer when
\mathrm{RI}=i
and
\mathrm{CQI}^{(i)}
are selected. The per-layer SE is defined as

\displaystyle\mathrm{SE}(i,\mathrm{CQI}^{(i)})=Q_{m}^{(i)}\cdot R^{(i)},

(28)

where
Q_{m}^{(i)}
and
R^{(i)}
denote the modulation order and code rate, respectively, corresponding to the selected
\mathrm{CQI}^{(i)}
. Finally, the UE reports the optimal rank
\mathrm{RI}=r
and the associated
\mathrm{CQI}=\mathrm{CQI}^{(r)}
to the gNB.

Our algorithm, RI-CQI-Tune, builds on CQI-Tune to jointly select the RI and the CQI that maximize the estimated spectral efficiency while avoiding the excessively high BLER typically associated with conventional algorithms. Specifically, RI-CQI-Tune applies CQI-Tune for each candidate
\mathrm{RI}=i
to refine the baseline CQI selected by the table-based algorithm, denoted as
\mathrm{CQI}^{(i)}

\mathrm{CQI}^{(i)}_{\text{L}}

Remark 5

(
Motivation for RI-CQI-Tune
)
.

RI-CQI-Tune is motivated by empirical observations in live network deployments, where it is noted that while the gNB typically adheres to the RI reported by the UE, it frequently overrides the recommended CQI. This behavior is consistent with the flexibility granted to the gNB in 3GPP specifications
[
2
]
, which allow the network to determine the final transmission parameters based on its own scheduling constraints and load. By performing joint optimization, RI-CQI-Tune provides a robust feedback pair that better aligns with these practical scheduling behaviors, reducing the likelihood of high-BLER events caused by stale table-based mappings.

Furthermore, a key distinction is that while CQI-Tune is restricted to lowering the CQI selected by the table-based algorithm (representing a conservative back-off), RI-CQI-Tune has the flexibility to re-optimize the RI. This transition occurs when the CQI of the RI selected by the table-based algorithm reduces such that this specific rank is no longer the best estimated rank. In such cases, RI-CQI-Tune can shift the selection to a different rank that offers a higher SE.

Complexity of the FF and the BP algorithms
. A primary motivation for adopting the FF algorithm is its significant advantages for resource-constrained devices
[

1.

Dynamic Memory (RAM) Footprint.
Standard backpropagation requires storing all activations during the backward pass; hence, the required RAM scales with the network depth and layer sizes (i.e.,
\sum_{l}M_{l}
for an MLP). In contrast, LightTune trains each layer locally, which eliminates the necessity to store intermediate activations across the entire depth. Consequently, the peak memory footprint scales only with the size of the largest layer (i.e.,
\max_{l}M_{l}
for an MLP).

2.

Implementation Simplicity.
Standard backpropagation necessitates a complex automatic differentiation (Autodiff) engine to build a computational graph and execute the chain rule. This typically requires a full deep-learning framework (e.g., Torch, TensorFlow). LightTune avoids this overhead entirely by leveraging the FF algorithm which utilizes local sequential update rules that do not require global graph tracking.

Complexity of CQI-Tune.

The complexity of the CQI-Tune back-off algorithm (Alg.
3

Complexity of RI-CQI-Tune.

The joint RI-CQI-Tune selection framework (Alg.
4

[r_{\text{low}},r_{\text{high}}]
covering at most 3 candidate ranks. Since each candidate rank invokes at most one pre-transmission BLER prediction (given
K=1

VII
Conclusion

This paper presented LightTune, an efficient online fine-tuning framework that enables post-deployment model adaptation through a backpropagation-free, FF mechanism. By leveraging a buffer-less, opportunistic update strategy, LightTune enables real-time refinement of ML models on resource-constrained devices. When applied to link adaptation, LightTune significantly improved throughput compared to the conventional outer-loop table-based baseline. While we utilized the FF algorithm for a lightweight UE-side implementation, the core principles of this opportunistic, buffer-less framework could be extended to backpropagation-based architectures. Extending LightTune to other 6G physical layer tasks, such as PMI and beam selection, is an area for future investigation.

Appendix A
Theoretical Insights and Experimental Validation of the Proposed Loss Function

We derive our alternative quadratic loss from the second-order Taylor expansion of the function
f(x)=\ln(1+e^{x})
, centered at
x=0
that is given as

f(x)=\ln 2+\frac{1}{2}x+\frac{1}{8}x^{2}+R_{2}(x),

(30)

where
R_{2}(x)
denotes the Lagrange remainder term, defined as
R_{2}(x)=\frac{f^{(3)}(\xi)}{3!}x^{3}=\frac{1}{6}\frac{e^{\xi}(1-e^{\xi})}{(1+e^{\xi})^{3}}x^{3}
for some
\xi\in[0,x]
.

We apply this expansion to the Softplus loss while discarding the higher-order remainder
R_{2}(x)
. Because the optimization landscape is invariant to constant shifts, we discard the constant
\ln 2
term. Finally, to eliminate the fractional coefficients and simplify the hardware arithmetic, we apply a uniform scaling factor of
8
. This yields our proposed loss function

Appendix D
Convergence Analysis of LightTune

This appendix provides the convergence proof of LightTune. We begin with our notations.

D-A

Probability Space and Notations

All random variables are defined on a common probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
. The initial parameters
\bm{\theta}^{(1)}
are obtained from offline training on data drawn i.i.d. from
\mathcal{D}_{1}
and are independent of the online data stream. The online data
\{\bm{z}^{(t)}_{\text{+}}=(\bm{x}^{(t)},y^{(t)}_{\text{+}})\}_{t=1}^{\infty}
are i.i.d. from
\mathcal{D}_{2}
. At each time
t
, a negative label
y^{(t)}_{\text{-}}
is drawn independently from a conditional distribution
R(\cdot\mid y^{(t)}_{\text{+}})
over
\mathcal{Y}\setminus\{y^{(t)}_{\text{+}}\}
; for example,
R
could be uniform over the other labels. The exact form of
R
is not needed beyond the existence of a gradient lower bound (Assumption
4
). The algorithm updates the parameters according to the rule of LightTune, producing a sequence
\bm{\theta}^{(t)}
. The filtration
\mathcal{F}_{t}
is defined as

\mathcal{F}_{t}=\sigma\bigl(\bm{\theta}^{(1)},\bm{z}^{(1)}_{\text{+}},\dots,\bm{z}^{(t)}_{\text{+}}\bigr),

(32)

i.e., the
\sigma
-algebra generated by all random variables up to time
t
, containing all information available at that time. Note that
y^{(t)}_{\text{-}}
is not included because it is used only to construct
\bm{\theta}^{(t+1)}
and is not needed for conditioning. When we write
\mathbb{E}_{y^{(t)}_{\text{-}}}[\cdot\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}]
, we mean expectation with respect to the random draw of
y^{(t)}_{\text{-}}
given the past and the current sample. We denote by
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{2}}[\cdot]
expectation under the online data distribution, and by
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{1}}[\cdot]
expectation under the offline training data distribution. The unconditional expectation
\mathbb{E}[\cdot]
without a subscript denotes expectation over all sources of randomness; when needed we indicate the measure explicitly.

D-B

Justification of Assumptions

We provide an intuitive rationale for our assumptions. While these justifications are not intended as formal proofs, they clarify the logic of our convergence analysis. We first note that Algorithm
1
does not explicitly enforce the parameter bound in Assumption
2
(ii) via projection or weight decay. This is a standard assumption in the analysis of non-convex stochastic optimization, and in our experiments we empirically observe that the iterates remain bounded throughout fine-tuning.

Next, we note that Assumption
4
is motivated by the observation that a vanishing expected squared norm would necessitate a perfect cancellation between the positive and negative gradients in expectation. In the context of the FF algorithm, such systematic cancellation is highly improbable as long as the model is driven by non-negligible prediction errors. In particular, if the negative label
y^{(t)}_{\text{-}}
is drawn from a distribution that assigns positive probability to every incorrect label (e.g., uniform distribution over
\mathcal{Y}\setminus\{y^{(t)}_{\text{+}}\}
), then

\mathbb{E}_{y^{(t)}_{\text{-}}}\bigl[\|\nabla\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(t)}\|_{2}^{2}\bigr]=0\;\Longrightarrow\;\|\bm{p}+\bm{n}_{c}\|_{2}=0\quad\text{for every }c\neq y^{(t)}_{\text{+}},

where
\bm{p}=\nabla\mathcal{L}_{\text{+},l}^{(t)}
and
\bm{n}_{c}=\nabla\mathcal{L}_{\text{-},l}^{(t)}
for label
c
. Hence
\bm{n}_{c}=-\bm{p}
for all
c
. This would mean that the gradient does not depend on the label
c
, implying that the network’s response to different incorrect labels is identical in the sense of its gradient. When the prediction error is large, the true label and the predicted label differ, consequently, the gradients for those labels cannot all be the same. Therefore, for any reasonable sampling scheme that covers all incorrect labels (such as uniform sampling), the expected squared gradient norm must be bounded below by a positive constant
\gamma_{2}(\delta)>0
. This justifies Assumption
4
.

D-C

Preliminary Lemmas

We recall and introduce some notations. The input to layer
l
is denoted as
\bm{h}_{\text{+},l-1}^{(t)}
for a positive sample and by
\bm{h}_{\text{-},l-1}^{(t)}
for a negative sample. We use the augmented notation
\tilde{\bm{h}}_{\text{+},l-1}^{(t)}=[\bm{h}_{\text{+},l-1}^{(t)},1]^{\top}
and
\tilde{\bm{h}}_{\text{-},l-1}^{(t)}=[\bm{h}_{\text{-},l-1}^{(t)},1]^{\top}
for positive and negative samples, respectively. The parameters of neuron
j
in layer
l
are
\bm{\theta}_{l,j}=[\bm{w}_{l,j},b_{l,j}]^{\top}
. Hence, the positive pre‑activation is given as
p_{\text{+},l}^{(t)}[j]=\bm{\theta}_{l,j}^{\top}\tilde{\bm{h}}_{\text{+},l-1}^{(t)}
, and the negative pre‑activation is given as
p_{\text{-},l}^{(t)}[j]=\bm{\theta}_{l,j}^{\top}\tilde{\bm{h}}_{\text{-},l-1}^{(t)}
.

We recall from (
III-A
), the loss function for a single neuron
j
in layer
l
combines positive and negative contributions as follows

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{l}^{(t)}[j](\bm{\theta}_{l,j})

\displaystyle=\bigl((h_{\text{+},l}^{(t)}[j])^{2}-T\bigr)^{2}-4\bigl((h_{\text{+},l}^{(t)}[j])^{2}-T\bigr)

\displaystyle\quad+\bigl((h_{\text{-},l}^{(t)}[j])^{2}-T\bigr)^{2}+4\bigl((h_{\text{-},l}^{(t)}[j])^{2}-T\bigr),

(33)

where
h_{\text{+},l}^{(t)}[j]=\max(0,p_{\text{+},l}^{(t)}[j])
and
h_{\text{-},l}^{(t)}[j]=\max(0,p_{\text{-},l}^{(t)}[j])
. The total loss for layer
l
is the average over all neurons:

\mathcal{L}_{l}^{(t)}(\bm{\theta})=\frac{1}{M_{l}}\sum_{j=1}^{M_{l}}\mathcal{L}_{l}^{(t)}[j](\bm{\theta}_{l,j}).

(34)

As derived in (
III-B
), the gradient of the positive branch with respect to its parameter vector
\bm{\theta}_{l,j}
for a neuron
j
in layer
l
is given as

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\theta}_{l,j}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{+},l}^{(t)}=4\left[(h_{\text{+},l}^{(t)}[j])^{2}-T-2\right]

\displaystyle\times h_{\text{+},l}^{(t)}[j]\cdot\mathds{1}\{p_{\text{+},l}^{(t)}[j]>0\}\cdot\tilde{\bm{h}}_{\text{+},l-1}^{(t)},

(35)

and the gradient of the negative branch is given similarly as

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\theta}_{l,j}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{-},l}^{(t)}=4\left[(h_{\text{-},l}^{(t)}[j])^{2}-T+2\right]

\displaystyle\times h_{\text{-},l}^{(t)}[j]\cdot\mathds{1}\{p_{\text{-},l}^{(t)}[j]>0\}\cdot\tilde{\bm{h}}_{\text{-},l-1}^{(t)}.

(36)

We begin with the proof of Lemma
1
which shows that the activations are bounded.

Proof.

We prove by induction on the layer index
l
.

Base case
l=0
: By definition,
\bm{h}_{0}^{(t)}=\bm{z}^{(t)}
, and Assumption
2
(i) gives
\|\bm{z}^{(t)}\|_{2}\leq B_{z}
. Hence
\|\bm{h}_{0}^{(t)}\|_{2}\leq B_{z}=:B_{0}
.

Inductive step
: Assume that for some
l\geq 1
, we have
\|\bm{h}_{l-1}^{(t)}\|_{2}\leq B_{l-1}
. Consider layer
l
. For any neuron
j
in this layer, the pre‑activation is

p_{l}^{(t)}[j]=\bm{\theta}_{l,j}^{\top}\tilde{\bm{h}}_{l-1}^{(t)},

(37)

where
\tilde{\bm{h}}_{l-1}^{(t)}=[\bm{h}_{l-1}^{(t)},1]^{\top}
is the augmented input. By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality and Assumption
2
(ii),

\displaystyle|p_{l}^{(t)}[j]|\leq\|\bm{\theta}_{l,j}\|_{2}\,\|\tilde{\bm{h}}_{l-1}^{(t)}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq B_{\theta}(\|\bm{h}_{l-1}^{(t)}\|_{2}+1)

\displaystyle\leq B_{\theta}(B_{l-1}+1).

(38)

The activation is
h_{l}^{(t)}[j]=\max(0,p_{l}^{(t)}[j])
, so
|h_{l}^{(t)}[j]|\leq|p_{l}^{(t)}[j]|
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\|\bm{h}_{l}^{(t)}\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{M_{l}}(h_{l}^{(t)}[j])^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{M_{l}}\,B_{\theta}(B_{l-1}+1)=:B_{l}.

(39)

This completes the induction.

Finally, since the network has a finite number of layers
L
, the set
\{B_{0},B_{1},\dots,B_{L}\}
is finite. Taking
B_{h}=\max_{0\leq l\leq L}B_{l}
gives a uniform bound valid for all layers and all time steps.
∎

Next, we provide the proof of Lemma
2
which shows that the gradients are also bounded.

Proof.

The gradient for neuron
j
is given as

\nabla_{\bm{\theta}_{l,j}}\mathcal{L}_{l}^{(t)}(\bm{\theta}_{l})=\frac{1}{M_{l}}\Bigl(\nabla_{\bm{\theta}_{l,j}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{+},l}^{(t)}[j]+\nabla_{\bm{\theta}_{l,j}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{-},l}^{(t)}[j]\Bigr),

(40)

where all quantities on the right-hand side are evaluated at the current parameters
\bm{\theta}_{l,j}^{(t)}
and the fixed inputs from the previous layer. We first bound each branch separately using the bounds from Assumption
2
and Lemma
1
. For the positive branch, the derivative of the scalar loss component is
2(g_{\text{+},l}^{(t)}[j]-T)-4=2(g_{\text{+},l}^{(t)}[j]-T-2)
. Applying the chain rule with
g_{\text{+},l}^{(t)}[j]=(h_{\text{+},l}^{(t)}[j])^{2}
yields an additional factor of
2h_{\text{+},l}^{(t)}[j]
. Thus, we can bound each term in the gradient as follows:

\displaystyle|4((h_{\text{+},l}^{(t)}[j])^{2}-T-2)|\leq 4(B_{h}^{2}+T+2),

\displaystyle|h_{\text{+},l}^{(t)}[j]|\leq B_{h},

\displaystyle\|\tilde{\bm{h}}_{\text{+},l-1}^{(t)}\|_{2}\leq B_{h}+1,

\displaystyle|\mathds{1}\{p_{\text{+},l}^{(t)}[j]>0\}|\leq 1.

Multiplying these bounds (since the gradient is the product of these terms) yields

\|\nabla_{\bm{\theta}_{l,j}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{+},l}^{(t)}[j]\|_{2}\leq 4(B_{h}^{2}+T+2)B_{h}(B_{h}+1).

(41)

Applying the same reasoning to the negative branch gives the identical bound:

\|\nabla_{\bm{\theta}_{l,j}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{-},l}^{(t)}[j]\|_{2}\leq 4(B_{h}^{2}+T+2)B_{h}(B_{h}+1).

(42)

Finally, using the triangle inequality and the expression for the total gradient evaluated at
\bm{\theta}_{l}^{(t)}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{\bm{\theta}_{l,j}}\mathcal{L}_{l}^{(t)}(\bm{\theta}_{l}^{(t)})\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{M_{l}}\Bigl(\|\nabla_{\bm{\theta}_{l,j}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{+},l}^{(t)}[j]\|_{2}+\|\nabla_{\bm{\theta}_{l,j}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{-},l}^{(t)}[j]\|_{2}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{8(B_{h}^{2}+T+2)B_{h}(B_{h}+1)}{M_{l}}.

∎

Next, we prove Lemma
3
showing that our proposed loss function is bounded.

Proof.

Recall that for any neuron,
g=h^{2}
. From Lemma
1
, we have
|h|\leq B_{h}
, so
0\leq g\leq B_{h}^{2}
. Since
g
is bounded by
B_{h}^{2}
, we have

\displaystyle|(g-T)^{2}-4(g-T)|

\displaystyle\leq(B_{h}^{2}+T)^{2}+4(B_{h}^{2}+T)

\displaystyle\leq(B_{h}^{2}+T+2)^{2}+4(B_{h}^{2}+T+2),

and similarly for the negative branch term. Adding the two bounds and noting that the loss for the layer is the average over neurons, we obtain

|\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(t)}(\bm{\theta}_{L})|\leq(B_{h}^{2}+T+2)^{2}+4(B_{h}^{2}+T+2)=:M.

∎

Finally, we prove Lemma
4
, which demonstrates that the proposed loss function is smooth. This smoothness property is a key enabler for our main convergence result.

Proof.

Let
d_{l}
be the total number of parameters in layer
l
. The parameter vector
\bm{\theta}_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{l}}
can be written as the concatenation of the parameter vectors for each neuron:

\bm{\theta}_{l}=[\bm{\theta}_{l,1}^{\top},\bm{\theta}_{l,2}^{\top},\dots,\bm{\theta}_{l,M_{l}}^{\top}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{l}},

(43)

where
\bm{\theta}_{l,j}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{n}}
are the parameters of neuron
j
(each neuron has the same number of parameters, including bias). The loss function for layer
l
is the average over neurons:

\mathcal{L}_{l}^{(t)}(\bm{\theta}_{l})=\frac{1}{M_{l}}\sum_{j=1}^{M_{l}}\mathcal{L}_{l}^{(t)}[j](\bm{\theta}_{l,j}),

(44)

where each
\mathcal{L}_{l}^{(t)}[j](\bm{\theta}_{l,j})
depends only on
\bm{\theta}_{l,j}
and not on the parameters of other neurons.

Gradient structure.

Because the loss separates over neurons, the gradient with respect to
\bm{\theta}_{l}
is the concatenation of the per‑neuron gradients:

\nabla\mathcal{L}_{l}^{(t)}(\bm{\theta}_{l})=\frac{1}{M_{l}}\begin{bmatrix}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}_{l,1}}\mathcal{L}_{l}^{(t)}[1](\bm{\theta}_{l,1})\\
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}_{l,2}}\mathcal{L}_{l}^{(t)}[2](\bm{\theta}_{l,2})\\
\vdots\\
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}_{l,M_{l}}}\mathcal{L}_{l}^{(t)}[M_{l}](\bm{\theta}_{l,M_{l}})\end{bmatrix}.

(45)

Hessian structure.

Differentiating again, the Hessian matrix
\nabla^{2}\mathcal{L}_{l}^{(t)}(\bm{\theta}_{l})\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{l}\times d_{l}}
is block‑diagonal:

\nabla^{2}\mathcal{L}_{l}^{(t)}(\bm{\theta}_{l})=\frac{1}{M_{l}}\text{diag}\left(\nabla^{2}_{\bm{\theta}_{l,1}}\mathcal{L}_{l}^{(t)}[1],\dots,\nabla^{2}_{\bm{\theta}_{l,M_{l}}}\mathcal{L}_{l}^{(t)}[M_{l}]\right).

(46)

This is because cross‑partial derivatives
\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\bm{\theta}_{l,i}\partial\bm{\theta}_{l,j}}
for
i\neq j
are zero, as each term in the sum depends only on its own neuron’s parameters.

Spectral norm of a block‑diagonal matrix.

For a block‑diagonal matrix, the spectral norm equals the maximum of the spectral norms of the individual blocks
[
12
]
. Therefore,

\|\nabla^{2}\mathcal{L}_{l}^{(t)}(\bm{\theta}_{l})\|_{2}=\frac{1}{M_{l}}\max_{1\leq j\leq M_{l}}\bigl\|\nabla^{2}_{\bm{\theta}_{l,j}}\mathcal{L}_{l}^{(t)}[j](\bm{\theta}_{l,j})\bigr\|_{2}.

(47)

Thus it suffices to bound the Hessian of a single neuron; the full Hessian norm will be at most that bound divided by
M_{l}
.

Bounding the per‑neuron Hessian.

Fix a neuron
j
and time
t
, and drop the indices
l,j,t
for brevity. When
p_{\text{+}}>0
(neuron active), we have

\nabla\mathcal{L}_{\text{+}}=\bigl[4p_{\text{+}}^{3}-4(T+2)p_{\text{+}}\bigr]\tilde{\bm{h}}_{\text{+}}.

(48)

Differentiating again with respect to
\bm{\theta}
(using
\frac{\partial p_{\text{+}}}{\partial\bm{\theta}}=\tilde{\bm{h}}_{\text{+}}
) yields the Hessian

\nabla^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\text{+}}=\bigl[12p_{\text{+}}^{2}-4(T+2)\bigr]\tilde{\bm{h}}_{\text{+}}\tilde{\bm{h}}_{\text{+}}^{\top}.

(49)

When
p_{\text{+}}\leq 0
, the neuron is inactive and the Hessian is zero. Similarly, when
p_{\text{-}}>0
,

\nabla^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\text{-}}=\bigl[12p_{\text{-}}^{2}-4(T+2)\bigr]\tilde{\bm{h}}_{\text{-}}\tilde{\bm{h}}_{\text{-}}^{\top},

(50)

and zero otherwise. By Lemma
1
,
\|\tilde{\bm{h}}_{\text{+}}\|_{2}\leq B_{h}+1
and
\|\tilde{\bm{h}}_{\text{-}}\|_{2}\leq B_{h}+1
. Moreover, when
p_{\text{+}}>0
, we have
p_{\text{+}}=h_{\text{+}}\leq B_{h}
, so
|p_{\text{+}}|\leq B_{h}
. Hence

\|\nabla^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\text{+}}\|_{2}\leq(12B_{h}^{2}+4T+8)(B_{h}+1)^{2}.

(51)

The same bound holds for
\|\nabla^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\text{-}}\|_{2}
. By the triangle inequality,

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\text{+}}+\nabla^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\text{-}}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2(12B_{h}^{2}+4T+8)(B_{h}+1)^{2}

\displaystyle=8(3B_{h}^{2}+T+2)(B_{h}+1)^{2}

\displaystyle:=\bar{\rho}_{l}.

(52)

Thus we have shown that for any neuron
j
,

\|\nabla^{2}_{\bm{\theta}_{l,j}}\mathcal{L}_{l}^{(t)}[j](\bm{\theta}_{l,j})\|_{2}\leq\bar{\rho}_{l}=8\bigl(3B_{h}^{2}+T+2\bigr)(B_{h}+1)^{2}.

(53)

Using the block‑diagonal structure and the fact that the spectral norm of a block‑diagonal matrix is the maximum of the block norms scaled by the overall factor
1/M_{l}
, we obtain

\|\nabla^{2}\mathcal{L}_{l}^{(t)}(\bm{\theta}_{l})\|_{2}\leq\frac{1}{M_{l}}\max_{j}\|\nabla^{2}_{\bm{\theta}_{l,j}}\mathcal{L}_{l}^{(t)}[j]\|_{2}\leq\frac{\bar{\rho}_{l}}{M_{l}}=:\rho_{l}.

(54)

From Hessian bound to Lipschitz gradient.

Now we prove that this bound on the Hessian implies the gradient is
\rho_{l}
-Lipschitz everywhere. Take any
\bm{\theta}_{l},\bm{\theta}^{\prime}_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{l}}
and consider the line segment

\bm{\theta}_{l}(s)=\bm{\theta}_{l}+s(\bm{\theta}^{\prime}_{l}-\bm{\theta}_{l}),\quad s\in[0,1].

(55)

1.

Points where the gradient may not be differentiable.

For each neuron
k
in layer
l
, its pre‑activation along the segment is

p_{k}(s)=\bm{\theta}_{l,k}(s)^{\top}\tilde{\bm{h}}_{l-1},

(56)

where
\bm{\theta}_{l,k}(s)
is the part of
\bm{\theta}(s)
corresponding to neuron
k
, and
\tilde{\bm{h}}_{l-1}
is fixed (it comes from the sample at time
t
and does not depend on
s
). This is an affine function of
s
, i.e.,
p_{k}(s)=a_{k}s+b_{k}
for some constants
a_{k},b_{k}
.

2.

Zeros of affine functions are isolated.

For a fixed
k
, the equation
p_{k}(s)=0
is linear in
s
. Hence it has either: 1) no solution (if
a_{k}=0
and
b_{k}\neq 0
), 2) exactly one solution
s_{k}^{*}
(if
a_{k}\neq 0
) or 3) the whole interval (if
a_{k}=0
and
b_{k}=0
, which would mean the pre‑activation is identically zero; this degenerate case occurs on a set of measure zero and can be ignored). Thus each neuron contributes at most one point where
p_{k}(s)=0
.

3.

The exceptional set is finite.

Since there are finitely many neurons, the set

S_{0}=\{s\in[0,1]:\exists k\text{ such that }p_{k}(s)=0\}

(57)

is finite. Order its elements as
0\leq s_{1}<\dots<s_{m}\leq 1
. Remove these points to obtain a partition of
[0,1]
into subintervals

[0,s_{1}],\;[s_{1},s_{2}],\;\dots,\;[s_{m},1].

On each such subinterval, no pre‑activation changes sign, so the activation pattern (which neurons are active) remains fixed. Consequently, on each subinterval, the gradient
\nabla\mathcal{L}_{l}^{(t)}(\bm{\theta}(s))
is a polynomial in
s
(because the per‑neuron contributions are polynomials in the parameters, and the parameters are affine in
s
). Hence it is infinitely differentiable on the open interval and extends continuously to the endpoints.

4.

Derivative on a smooth subinterval.

On any subinterval where
\nabla\mathcal{L}_{l}^{(t)}(\bm{\theta}(s))
is
C^{1}
, we can differentiate:

\frac{d}{ds}\nabla\mathcal{L}_{l}^{(t)}(\bm{\theta}_{l}(s))=\nabla^{2}\mathcal{L}_{l}^{(t)}(\bm{\theta}_{l}(s))\,(\bm{\theta}_{l}^{\prime}-\bm{\theta}_{l}),

(58)

where the Hessian exists everywhere on the interval because the activation pattern is constant. From the bound on the Hessian, we have
\|\nabla^{2}\mathcal{L}_{l}^{(t)}(\bm{\theta}_{l}(s))\|_{2}\leq\rho_{l}
, so

\left\|\frac{d}{ds}\nabla\mathcal{L}_{l}^{(t)}(\bm{\theta}_{l}(s))\right\|_{2}\leq\rho_{l}\|\bm{\theta}^{\prime}_{l}-\bm{\theta}_{l}\|_{2}.

(59)

5.

Integration over each subinterval.

Apply the fundamental theorem of calculus on each subinterval. Because
\nabla\mathcal{L}_{l}^{(t)}(\bm{\theta}(s))
is continuously differentiable on the open interval and continuous up to the endpoints, we have

\displaystyle\nabla\mathcal{L}_{l}^{(t)}(\bm{\theta}_{l}(s_{i+1}))-\nabla\mathcal{L}_{l}^{(t)}(\bm{\theta}_{l}(s_{i}))

\displaystyle=\int_{s_{i}}^{s_{i+1}}\nabla^{2}\mathcal{L}_{l}^{(t)}(\bm{\theta}_{l}(s))\,(\bm{\theta}_{l}^{\prime}-\bm{\theta}_{l})\,ds.

Summing these equalities from
i=0
to
m
(with
s_{0}=0
,
s_{m+1}=1
) telescopes the left‑hand side, giving

\displaystyle\nabla\mathcal{L}_{l}^{(t)}(\bm{\theta}_{l}^{\prime})-\nabla\mathcal{L}_{l}^{(t)}(\bm{\theta}_{l})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{m}\int_{s_{i}}^{s_{i+1}}\nabla^{2}\mathcal{L}_{l}^{(t)}(\bm{\theta}_{l}(s))\,(\bm{\theta}^{\prime}_{l}-\bm{\theta}_{l})\,ds.

6.

Norm estimate.

Taking norms and using the triangle inequality,

\displaystyle\|\nabla\mathcal{L}_{l}^{(t)}(\bm{\theta}_{l}^{\prime})-\nabla\mathcal{L}_{l}^{(t)}(\bm{\theta}_{l})\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=0}^{m}\int_{s_{i}}^{s_{i+1}}\|\nabla^{2}\mathcal{L}_{l}^{(t)}(\bm{\theta}_{l}(s))\|_{2}\,\|\bm{\theta}_{l}^{\prime}-\bm{\theta}_{l}\|_{2}\,ds

\displaystyle\leq\rho_{l}\|\bm{\theta}_{l}^{\prime}-\bm{\theta}_{l}\|_{2}\sum_{i=0}^{m}(s_{i+1}-s_{i})=\rho_{l}\|\bm{\theta}_{l}^{\prime}-\bm{\theta}_{l}\|_{2}.

Thus,
\mathcal{L}_{l}^{(t)}
is
\rho_{l}
-smooth.
∎

D-D

Convergence Theorem

We now provide the proof of Theorem
1
.

Proof.

We proceed in steps as follows.

1.

Local decrease.
For any
t
, if
I^{(t)}_{\delta}=1
, the algorithm performs a gradient update:

\bm{\theta}_{L}^{(t+1)}=\bm{\theta}_{L}^{(t)}-\alpha_{f}\nabla\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(t)}(\bm{\theta}_{L}^{(t)}).

(60)

Because
\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(t)}
is
\rho_{L}
-smooth (Lemma
4
), we can apply the descent lemma (Lemma
5
) with
\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}_{L}^{(t)}
and
\bm{\theta}^{\prime}=\bm{\theta}_{L}^{(t+1)}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(t)}(\bm{\theta}_{L}^{(t+1)})

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(t)}(\bm{\theta}_{L}^{(t)})+\nabla\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(t)}(\bm{\theta}_{L}^{(t)})^{\top}(\bm{\theta}_{L}^{(t+1)}-\bm{\theta}_{L}^{(t)})

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\rho_{L}}{2}\|\bm{\theta}_{L}^{(t+1)}-\bm{\theta}_{L}^{(t)}\|_{2}^{2}.

(61)

Substituting the update
\bm{\theta}_{L}^{(t+1)}-\bm{\theta}_{L}^{(t)}=-\alpha_{f}\nabla\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(t)}(\bm{\theta}_{L}^{(t)})
gives

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(t)}(\bm{\theta}_{L}^{(t+1)})

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(t)}(\bm{\theta}_{L}^{(t)})-\alpha_{f}\|\nabla\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(t)}(\bm{\theta}_{L}^{(t)})\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\rho_{L}\alpha_{f}^{2}}{2}\|\nabla\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(t)}(\bm{\theta}_{L}^{(t)})\|_{2}^{2}

(62)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(t)}(\bm{\theta}_{L}^{(t)})-\alpha_{f}\!\left(1-\frac{\rho_{L}\alpha_{f}}{2}\right)\|\nabla\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(t)}(\bm{\theta}_{L}^{(t)})\|_{2}^{2}.

(63)

Since
\alpha_{f}<1/\rho_{L}
, we have
\frac{\rho_{L}\alpha_{f}}{2}<\frac{1}{2}
, hence
1-\frac{\rho_{L}\alpha_{f}}{2}>\frac{1}{2}
. Therefore,

\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(t)}(\bm{\theta}_{L}^{(t+1)})\leq\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(t)}(\bm{\theta}_{L}^{(t)})-\frac{\alpha_{f}}{2}\|\nabla\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(t)}(\bm{\theta}_{L}^{(t)})\|_{2}^{2}.

(64)

If
I^{(t)}_{\delta}=0
, no update occurs, so
\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(t)}(\bm{\theta}_{L}^{(t+1)})=\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(t)}(\bm{\theta}_{L}^{(t)})
. Combining both cases yields

\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(t)}(\bm{\theta}_{L}^{(t+1)})\leq\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(t)}(\bm{\theta}_{L}^{(t)})-\frac{\alpha_{f}}{2}\|\nabla\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(t)}(\bm{\theta}_{L}^{(t)})\|_{2}^{2}I^{(t)}_{\delta}.

(65)

2.

Conditional expectation under
\mathcal{D}_{2}
.
Conditioning on
\mathcal{F}_{t}
(which fixes
\bm{\theta}_{L}^{(t)}
,
\bm{x}^{(t)}
,
y^{(t)}_{\text{+}}
) and using the gradient lower bound (Assumption
4
),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{y^{(t)}_{\text{-}}}\bigl[\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(t)}(\bm{\theta}_{L}^{(t+1)})\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}\bigr]

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(t)}(\bm{\theta}_{L}^{(t)})-\frac{\alpha_{f}}{2}I^{(t)}_{\delta}\,\mathbb{E}_{y^{(t)}_{\text{-}}}\bigl[\|\nabla\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(t)}(\bm{\theta}_{L}^{(t)})\|_{2}^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t},I^{(t)}_{\delta}=1\bigr]

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(t)}(\bm{\theta}_{L}^{(t)})-\frac{\alpha_{f}\gamma_{2}(\delta)}{2}I^{(t)}_{\delta}.

(66)

3.

Total expectation under
\mathcal{D}_{2}
.
Taking expectation under
\mathcal{D}_{2}
,

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{2}}[\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(t)}(\bm{\theta}_{L}^{(t+1)})]\leq\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{2}}[\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(t)}(\bm{\theta}_{L}^{(t)})]-\frac{\alpha_{f}\gamma_{2}(\delta)}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{2}}[I^{(t)}_{\delta}].

(67)

4.

Relating to
\mathcal{D}_{1}
via Pinsker.
For any fixed
\bm{\theta}
, by Lemma
6
applied with
P=\mathcal{D}_{2}
,
Q=\mathcal{D}_{1}
, and
f=\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(t)}(\bm{\theta})
,

|\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{2}}[\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(t)}(\bm{\theta})]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{1}}[\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(t)}(\bm{\theta})]|\leq M\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mathcal{D}_{2}\|\mathcal{D}_{1})}.

(68)

Since
\bm{\theta}_{L}^{(t)}
is independent of the sample at time
t
, we can condition on
\bm{\theta}_{L}^{(t)}
and integrate:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{2}}[\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(t)}(\bm{\theta}_{L}^{(t)})]=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{2}}[\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(t)}(\bm{\theta})\mid\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}_{L}^{(t)}]\bigr]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{1}}[\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(t)}(\bm{\theta})\mid\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}_{L}^{(t)}]+M\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2}D_{\mathrm{KL}}}\bigr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{1}}[\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(t)}(\bm{\theta}_{L}^{(t)})]+M\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2}D_{\mathrm{KL}}}.

(69)

By Assumption
3
, the offline training data are i.i.d. from
\mathcal{D}_{1}
, so for any fixed
\bm{\theta}
,
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{1}}[\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(t)}(\bm{\theta})]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{1}}[\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(1)}(\bm{\theta})]
. Moreover, because
\bm{\theta}_{L}^{(t)}
is independent of the sample at time
t
under
\mathcal{D}_{1}
as well,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{1}}[\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(t)}(\bm{\theta}_{L}^{(t)})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{1}}[\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(t)}(\bm{\theta})\mid\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}_{L}^{(t)}]\bigr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{1}}[\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(1)}(\bm{\theta})\mid\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}_{L}^{(t)}]\bigr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{1}}[\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(1)}(\bm{\theta}_{L}^{(t)})].

(70)

Thus

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{2}}[\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(t)}(\bm{\theta}_{L}^{(t)})]\leq\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{1}}[\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(1)}(\bm{\theta}_{L}^{(t)})]+M\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2}D_{\mathrm{KL}}}.

(71)

The same inequality holds for
\bm{\theta}_{L}^{(t+1)}
. Substituting these upper bounds into (
67
) yields

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{1}}[\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(1)}(\bm{\theta}_{L}^{(t+1)})]\leq\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{1}}[\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(1)}(\bm{\theta}_{L}^{(t)})]-\frac{\alpha_{f}\gamma_{2}(\delta)}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{2}}[I^{(t)}_{\delta}].

(72)

5.

Summation and telescoping.
Summing (
72
) from
t=1
to
N
,

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha_{f}\gamma_{2}(\delta)}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{2}}[I^{(t)}_{\delta}]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{1}}[\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(1)}(\bm{\theta}_{L}^{(1)})]-

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{1}}[\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(1)}(\bm{\theta}_{L}^{(N+1)})].

(73)

6.

Bounding the final term.
Let
\mathcal{L}_{L}^{*}=\inf_{\bm{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{2}}[\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(1)}(\bm{\theta})]
. Applying Lemma
6
again,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{1}}[\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(1)}(\bm{\theta}_{L}^{(N+1)})]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{2}}[\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(1)}(\bm{\theta}_{L}^{(N+1)})]-M\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2}D_{\mathrm{KL}}}

\displaystyle\geq\mathcal{L}_{L}^{*}-M\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2}D_{\mathrm{KL}}}.

(74)

Hence

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{1}}[\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(1)}(\bm{\theta}_{L}^{(1)})]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{1}}[\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(1)}(\bm{\theta}_{L}^{(N+1)})]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{1}}[\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(1)}(\bm{\theta}_{L}^{(1)})]-\mathcal{L}_{L}^{*}+M\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2}D_{\mathrm{KL}}}.

(75)

7.

Final bound.
Combining and dividing by
N
yields our bound

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{2}}[I^{(t)}_{\delta}]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\bigl[\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{1}}[\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(1)}(\bm{\theta}_{L}^{(1)})]-\mathcal{L}_{L}^{*}\bigr]}{\alpha_{f}\gamma_{2}(\delta)N}

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{2M}{\alpha_{f}\gamma_{2}(\delta)}\sqrt{\frac{2D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mathcal{D}_{2}\|\mathcal{D}_{1})}{N}}.

(76)

Recalling that
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{2}}[I^{(t)}_{\delta}]=\Pr_{\mathcal{D}_{2}}(e^{(t)}\geq\delta)
completes the proof.

∎

Next, we provide the proof of Corollary
1
.

Proof.

From Theorem
1
, we have for every
N\geq 1
,

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N}\Pr_{\hskip 5.0pt\mathcal{D}_{2}}(e^{(t)}\geq\delta)\leq\frac{A}{N}+\frac{B}{\sqrt{N}},

where
A=\frac{2[\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{1}}[\mathcal{L}_{L}^{(1)}(\bm{\theta}_{L}^{(1)})]-\mathcal{L}_{L}^{*}]}{\alpha_{f}\gamma_{2}(\delta)}
and
B=\frac{2M}{\alpha_{f}\gamma_{2}(\delta)}\sqrt{2D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mathcal{D}_{2}\|\mathcal{D}_{1})}
are constants independent of
N
. As
N\to\infty
, both
A/N\to 0
and
B/\sqrt{N}\to 0
. By the squeeze theorem, the left‑hand side also tends to
0
.
∎

Finally, we provide the proof of Corollary
2
.

Proof.

When
\mathcal{D}_{1}=\mathcal{D}_{2}
, we have
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mathcal{D}_{2}\|\mathcal{D}_{1})=0
. Substituting into Theorem
1
yields

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N}\Pr(e^{(t)}\geq\delta)\leq\frac{A}{N},

with
A
as defined above. Multiplying by
N
, we obtain for every
N\geq 1
,

\sum_{t=1}^{N}\Pr(e^{(t)}\geq\delta)\leq A.

The right‑hand side
A
is a constant independent of
N
, and all terms are non‑negative. Hence, the partial sums are uniformly bounded and nondecreasing, so they converge to a finite limit as
N\to\infty
. Therefore,

\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\Pr(e^{(t)}\geq\delta)<\infty.

A necessary condition for a convergent series is that its terms tend to zero; thus
\Pr(e^{(t)}\geq\delta)\to 0
as
t\to\infty
.
∎
