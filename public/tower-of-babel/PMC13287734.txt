IL-2 mutein promotes antigen-specific transplant acceptance in mice through expansion of ST2+ regulatory T cells
Authors: Yoshikazu Ganchiku, Guilherme T. Ribas, Karina Lima, Rodrigo B. Gassen, Kaifeng Liu, Orhan Efe, Jason W. Griffith, Andrew D. Luster, Zachary Shriver, Gregory J. Babcock, Ivy A. Rosales, Christian LeGuern, Thiago J. Borges, Leonardo V. Riella
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Although transplantation is the preferred treatment for end-stage organ disease, long-term outcomes are limited by immunosuppressive drug toxicity and immune-mediated injury. Selective in vivo expansion of regulatory T cells (Tregs) using interleukin-2 (IL-2) analogs has emerged as a strategy to induce antigen-specific transplant tolerance with fewer side effects. Herein, we investigate the therapeutic efficacy of an IL-2 mutein molecule (mIL-2) with enhanced receptor specificity and extended half-life in murine models of solid organ transplantation. mIL-2 therapy significantly improves allograft survival in an antigen-specific manner, accompanied by increased Treg activation, decreased effector T cell activation and reduced donor-specific antibody production. Transcriptional profiling reveals expansion of Tregs expressing the suppression of tumorigenicity 2 (ST2) Tregs with heightened activation status and suppressive function. Accordingly, the mIL-2-induced long-term allograft survival is abrogated in Treg-specific ST2 knockout graft recipients, underscoring the critical role of ST2+ Tregs. These findings identify mIL-2 as an approach to promote long-term transplant tolerance while reducing reliance on conventional immunosuppression.

Introduction

Solid organ transplantation has become the standard treatment for end-stage organ failure, offering patients a chance at extended survival and improved quality of life. However, transplant recipients require systemic immunosuppression to prevent allograft rejection, which introduces significant challenges. The side effects of long-term immunosuppressive therapy, including increased susceptibility to infections1,2 and the development of de novo malignancies3,4, pose serious risks to transplant recipients. Moreover, despite this intense immunosuppression, kidney and heart transplant recipients experience chronic allograft damage5,6, often leading to graft failure and the need for re-transplantation. These limitations underscore the urgent need for more effective and less harmful strategies to promote immune regulation and prolong graft survival.

Regulatory T cell (Treg)-based therapies have emerged as promising approaches for inducing antigen-specific immune graft acceptance. Cellular therapies using ex vivo-expanded Tregs7,8 or chimeric antigen-reactive (CAR) Tregs9,10 have shown potential in mitigating immune responses against alloantigens. Concurrently, low-dose interleukin-2 (IL-2) therapy has gained attention for selectively expanding Tregs in vivo11,12. However, its effectiveness in clinical application, including the induction of transplantation tolerance13,14, is limited by the off-target expansion of potentially graft-reactive CD8+ T and NK cells15. Additionally, IL-2 has an extremely short half-life16, limiting its pharmacodynamic effect. Therefore, more stable and selective IL-2 analogs are required to maximize the benefit of IL-2 treatment.

We recently described an IL-2 mutein molecule (mIL-2) engineered to selectively bind high-affinity IL-2 receptors and incorporate a modified human IgG Fc binding domain to enhance its stability and therapeutic potential. In our prior study, mIL-2 was shown to selectively and sustainably expand Tregs, thereby facilitating the acceptance of skin allografts17. While promising, skin transplantation models fail to fully capture the complexities of vascularized solid organ grafts, such as kidneys and hearts, which are critical in clinical transplantation and more relevant for evaluating the translational potential of this therapy. Additionally, the precise characteristics and mechanisms of mIL-2-expanded Tregs that drive prolonged graft survival remain partially understood.

In this study, we aim to address critical knowledge gaps by assessing the efficacy of mIL-2 in promoting graft acceptance of solid organ transplants and dissecting the underlying mechanisms. The mIL-2 therapy induces alloantigen-specific tolerance by expanding a highly functional subset of ST2-positive Tregs, which are essential for sustained graft survival. The immunomodulatory effects of mIL-2 persisted beyond treatment cessation, highlighting its durable therapeutic potential and distinguishing it from traditional immunosuppression approaches.

Results

mIL-2 treatment combined with brief co-stimulation blockade induces long-term graft acceptance

To evaluate the efficacy of mIL-2 therapy in heart transplantation, BALB/c cardiac allografts were transplanted into C57BL/6 (B6) recipients, and mIL-2 was administered subcutaneously twice a week starting on the day of transplantation (Fig. 1a). mIL-2 monotherapy significantly prolonged allograft survival compared to the IgG control group, which exhibited rapid rejection within 7 to 10 days post-transplantation. However, cardiac grafts in mIL-2-treated recipients were ultimately rejected by postoperative day (POD) 22 (Supplementary Fig. 1). In contrast, combining mIL-2 with transient co-stimulation blockade resulted in long-term graft acceptance, with sustained graft function even after biweekly mIL-2 treatment was discontinued on POD 60 (Fig. 1b). Histopathological analysis corroborated these findings, demonstrating preserved myocyte structure and reduced perivascular fibrosis in mIL-2-treated animals on POD 30, while the control IgG group exhibited severe inflammation and tissue injury (Fig. 1c, d). Importantly, the immunomodulatory effect of mIL-2 was maintained even after the mIL-2 treatment discontinuation, as evidenced by sustained graft survival and histopathological integrity at POD 100 (Fig. 1b-d).

The mIL-2 therapy did not induce the expansion of potentially harmful lymphocytes such as CD4+Foxp3- conventional T cells (CD4+ Tconv), CD8+ T cells, or NK cells, nor were there any hematopoietic abnormalities observed. Stable levels of white blood cells, red blood cells, and platelets were maintained throughout the study (Supplementary Fig. 2a, b). Additionally, while mIL-2 treatment transiently increased eosinophil numbers during therapy (Supplementary Fig. 2a), these levels normalized shortly after treatment cessation, consistent with the expected pharmacodynamic effects of the therapy17.

Taken together, mIL-2 therapy, combined with co-stimulation blockade, induces long-term cardiac graft survival in murine heart transplantation models without significant off-target effects or hematopoietic toxicity. Notably, the immunomodulatory effects of mIL-2 persist after treatment discontinuation, a critical advantage over conventional immunosuppressive regimens.

The therapeutic effect of mIL-2 treatment is alloantigen-specific

To investigate whether mIL-2-induced graft acceptance is donor-specific, long-term B6 recipients with stable and beating BALB/c cardiac allografts were subjected to a second alloantigen challenge. At 100 days post-transplantation, a second heart graft – either from BALB/c (same as the first graft), C3H (third-party), or B6 (syngeneic to the host) – was implanted into the right cervical area of B6 acceptors. No additional immunosuppressive treatment was administered, and the original BALB/c allografts remained stable and fully functional throughout the observation period (Fig. 1e). In this secondary challenge, all third-party C3H allografts were rejected within 21 days post-transplantation, while BALB/c second allografts exhibited significantly prolonged survival, with approximately 60% achieving long-term acceptance (Fig. 1f). Histopathological analyses revealed severe tissue injury with mononuclear cell infiltration in rejected C3H grafts, while BALB/c second grafts showed minimal tissue damage (Fig. 1g), consistent with an antigen-specific immune modulation. Importantly, the original BALB/c first allografts remained structurally intact, with no evidence of inflammation or tissue injury indicative of rejection (Fig. 1g). These results indicate that mIL-2 therapy combined with co-stimulation blockade induces long-lasting donor-specific graft tolerance. Importantly, this effect occurs without the need for ongoing immunosuppression, highlighting an antigen-specific immune regulation that avoids generalized immunosuppression.

mIL-2 treatment significantly increased the proportion of CD4+Foxp3+ Tregs. On the other hand, the proportion of graft-infiltrating and spleen CD8+ T cells and spleen CD4+ Tconv cells was significantly reduced, while the decrease of graft-infiltrating CD4+ Tconv was not significant (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 3b). Next, we evaluated the activation status of effector T cells. In the CD4+ Tconv compartment, the proportion of the graft-infiltrating effector (CD44+CD62L-) cells was significantly decreased by mIL-2 treatment (Fig. 2c), while remained unchanged in the spleen (Supplementary Fig. 3c). Functionally, the mIL-2 treatment decreased the fraction of interferon-γ (IFN-γ)-producing and Ki67+ proliferating CD4+ Tconv cells in the graft (Fig. 2d), while the decrease of Ki67 expression in the spleen was not significant (Supplementary Fig. 3d), with no noticeable changes in the proportion of IL-17+ cells as compared to controls. These findings indicate that the mIL-2 treatment effectively suppresses CD4+ T cell-mediated type I and CD8+ T cell immune responses, typically involved in graft rejection. The activation and function of CD8+ T cells were also suppressed by mIL-2 treatment. Within the graft, there was a significant reduction in effector CD8+ T cells and a decreased proportion of IFN-γ and Granzyme B-producing cells (Fig. 2e, f). The proportion of effector cells was also reduced in splenic CD8+ T cells, where effector functions remained unchanged (Supplementary Fig. 3e, f).

To determine whether mIL-2 treatment selectively reduced the immune reactivity of donor-specific T cells, we ex vivo stimulated splenocytes from mIL-2-treated and control recipients with irradiated donor (BALB/c), third-party (C3H), or syngeneic (B6) cells. Enzyme-linked immunosorbent spot (ELISpot) assays showed a significant reduction of T cell responses against donor BALB/c target cells (Fig. 2g). In contrast, only a smaller fraction of third-party C3H responding cells was inhibited by the mIL-2 treatment (Fig. 2g). Taken together, mIL-2 treatment significantly attenuates T cell-mediated immune response in an alloantigen-specific manner, characterized by enhanced Treg presence, reduced effector CD4+ Tconv and CD8+ T cell activity, and suppression of type I immune responses within the graft and spleen.

mIL-2 treatment attenuates antibody-mediated immune response

Antibody-mediated chronic rejection remains a leading cause of long-term graft loss5,6. To evaluate whether mIL-2 therapy can mitigate humoral alloimmunity, we measured donor-specific antibodies (DSAs) in the serum of heart transplant recipients over time. The levels of anti-donor MHC-I and MHC-II IgG DSA levels progressively increased in the control group, whereas they remained significantly lower in the mIL-2-treated group (Fig. 3a). To investigate the mechanisms underlying this effect, spleens were harvested on POD 28 from heart transplant recipients treated with CTLA-4 Ig and either mIL-2 or control IgG. IL-2 signaling inhibits follicular helper T cell (Tfh) differentiation, thereby attenuating GC B cell activation and alloantibody production21,22. Unexpectedly, the proportion of splenic Tfh cells was similar between the groups (CD4+PD-1+CXCR5+Foxp3-, Fig. 3d) on day 28 after transplantation. Instead, the proportion of follicular regulatory T cells was significantly increased in the mIL-2 group (Tfr, CD4+PD-1+CXCR5+Foxp3+; Fig. 3d), which are known to suppress GC responses23–25. This increase in Tfr cells correlates with the observed reduction in GC B cell activation and DSA production, indicating that mIL-2 therapy modulates the B cell-mediated immune responses.

Consistent with these findings, long-term allografts in the mIL-2-treated group showed less myocardial injury and perivascular fibrosis (Fig. 1c). This suggests that mIL-2 effectively prevents antibody-mediated injury, preserving graft integrity. In sum, mIL-2 treatment attenuates B cell-mediated immune responses by reducing GC B cell activation, which correlates with an increase in splenic Tfr/Tfh ratio and bulk Tregs. These findings underscore the potential of mIL-2 in preventing antibody-mediated rejection and chronic graft injury.

mIL-2 treatment is effective across multiple transplantation models

The combined treatment of graft recipients with mIL-2 and co-stimulation blockade induced long-term survival of fully allogeneic cardiac transplants without the need for additional immunosuppressive agents. To further validate the immunomodulatory potential of mIL-2 therapy, we investigated its efficacy in additional transplant models and donor-recipient strain combinations26.

Although co-stimulation blockade effectively controls effector T cell responses, it can also impair Treg homeostasis27,28. To assess whether mIL-2 retains its therapeutic benefit independent of co-stimulation blockade, we employed a semi-allogeneic cardiac transplant model. CB6F1 cardiac allografts, derived from a BALB/c and B6 cross, were transplanted into B6 recipients. This model, characterized by a haplotype MHC mismatch, elicits a less aggressive allogeneic response than fully mismatched MHC transplants29. mIL-2 was administered biweekly starting on the day of surgery without co-stimulation blockade (Fig. 4a). mIL-2 treatment significantly prolonged graft survival (Fig. 4b) and preserved graft histology at both mid and long-term observations (Fig. 4c, d). Mechanistically, graft acceptance was achieved by attenuating T cell-mediated rejection, as evidenced by increased Treg proportion (Supplementary Fig. 4a), decreased activation of CD4+ Tconv (Supplementary Fig. 4b, c) and CD8+ T cell (Supplementary Fig. 4d, e), and reduced alloreactive IFN-γ production (Fig. 4e) on POD 28. In addition, mIL-2 attenuated B cell-mediated immune responses, as shown by decreased serum DSA levels (Fig. 4f), reduced IgG-producing B cells (Supplementary Fig. 4f), and fewer GC B cells (Supplementary Fig. 4g). These effects were associated with an increased Tfr/Tfh ratio (Supplementary Fig. 4h). Next, we evaluated mIL-2’s effectiveness in a kidney transplantation model, in which rejection is predominantly mediated by B cell effector responses30. To ensure robust immune activation, BALB/c donors and B6 recipients were used, given the tendency for spontaneous graft acceptance in less aggressive kidney transplant models (Fig. 4g)31. Once again, mIL-2 was administered biweekly, starting on the day of the surgery, without co-stimulation blockade. Biweekly mIL-2 treatment significantly attenuated graft injury, with pathology showing well-preserved tubular and glomerular structures in the mIL-2 group. In contrast, the control IgG group exhibited severe AMR characterized by microvascular inflammation and extensive necrosis with arterial fibrinoid necrosis (Fig. 4h and Supplementary Table 1). The immunomodulatory effect of mIL-2 treatment was maintained on POD 63 (Fig. 4h).

Mechanistic analysis on POD 21 revealed an increased proportion of CD4+Foxp3+ Tregs in the mIL-2 group, although the proportions of CD4+ Tconv and CD8+ cells were unchanged (Supplementary Fig. 5a). While mIL-2 reduced the effector phenotype of CD4+ Tconv cells (Supplementary Fig. 5b), Ki67 expression among CD4+ Tconv and CD8+ T cells was not significantly altered between groups (Supplementary Fig. 5c-e). Importantly, mIL-2 effectively suppressed anti-MHC I and MHC II DSA production over time (Fig. 4h), correlating with decreased IgG1 production by spleen B cells and reduced GC B cell differentiation (Supplementary Fig. 5f, g). In addition, mIL-2 significantly increased the Tfr cells and decreased the proportion of Tfh cells, although the reduction in Tfh was not statistically significant (Supplementary Fig. 5h). In summary, mIL-2 treatment demonstrated consistent efficacy across heart and kidney transplantation models by attenuating T cell- and B cell-mediated immune responses. In kidney transplantation, the primary effect was on B cell-mediated rejection, with suppression of DSA production and GC responses, which correlated with an increased Tfr/Tfh ratio.

mIL-2 preferentially expands Tregs with a unique ST2-positive phenotype

While the proportion of CXCR3+ Tregs was similar in graft Tregs and reduced in splenic Tregs in the mIL-2 group, ST2+ Tregs were consistently increased in splenic and graft Tregs (Fig. 5e-g). Discussion

This study demonstrates that an IL-2 mutein (mIL-2) induces long-term allograft survival by attenuating alloantigen-specific immune responses. The therapeutic effects were evident in both T cell- and B cell-mediated rejection and were consistent across multiple organ transplantation models. Mechanistically, mIL-2 expanded a highly functional Treg population characterized by ST2 expression, a critical determinant of its immunosuppressive properties.

The concentration-dependent binding of IL-2 to intermediate- and high-affinity receptors determines its selectivity for immune cell subsets15. High-affinity IL-2 receptors, predominantly expressed on CD4⁺ regulatory T cells (Tregs), are preferentially activated at low IL-2 concentrations, making them an attractive target for in vivo Treg expansion. Low-dose IL-2 therapy has shown promise in preclinical11,12 and clinical studies, including for refractory GVHD, but its use is limited by safety concerns stemming from its extremely short half-life and off-target expansion of effector cells, which can prevent transplant tolerance13,14. To address these limitations, engineered IL-2 analogs have been developed15, including PEGylated IL-2, IL-2-CD25 fusion protein, IL-2-anti-IL-2 immune complexes39, orthogonal IL-2-IL-2R mutant pairs40, and IL-2 mutein41. Our mIL-2 incorporates mutations to selectively bind high-affinity IL-2 receptors, along with a modified human Fc region to extend its half-life. Previous work from our group demonstrated the selective and stable expansion of Tregs with minimal side effects using mIL-2 in a skin transplantation model17. Here, we further validate its efficacy in heart and kidney transplantation models, demonstrating alloantigen-specific graft acceptance without significant adverse events.

Previous studies showed that recombinant IL-2 treatment expands multiple Treg phenotypes, which vary across IL-2 analogs41–43. Therefore, understanding the exact Treg phenotype expanded by our mIL-2 treatment is important for its clinical application. Importantly, this study highlights the expansion of a unique ST2⁺ tissue-resident Treg phenotype as a hallmark of mIL-2 therapy. Transcriptomics and immune profiling revealed that mIL-2 preferentially induces ST2⁺ Tregs, which are essential in graft acceptance. Using a genetic approach, we confirmed that ST2 expression in Tregs is critical for mIL-2-mediated immune regulation, as ST2-deficient Tregs failed to suppress rejection.

Allograft rejection in solid organ transplantation is primarily driven by type I immune responses, which are typically restrained by tissue-resident Tregs with T-bet⁺ or CXCR3⁺ phenotypes44,45. However, mIL-2 selectively expanded ST2⁺ Tregs rather than these conventional phenotypes. ST2+ Tregs are known to mediate tissue repair and homeostasis through interactions with innate immune and non-immune cells, such as endothelial and mesenchymal cells33,46. Previous studies with ST2⁺ Tregs expanded by IL-33 suggested limited direct suppressive activity in vitro but demonstrated in vivo efficacy in skin transplantation47. More recently, IL-33 signaling has been implicated in chronic rejection, where graft-derived IL-33⁺ fibroblasts interact with ST2⁺ Tregs to drive amphiregulin-mediated tissue repair, paradoxically promoting graft fibrosis and vasculopathy48. These findings suggest that while ST2⁺ Tregs play a critical role in graft tolerance, their contributions to tissue remodeling could have unintended pathological consequences. Our selective genetic deletion of ST2 in Tregs underscores the essential role of ST2 signaling in mIL-2-expanded Tregs for immune regulation while highlighting the need to disentangle their dual roles in balancing tissue repair and immune suppression in transplantation. Our findings also extend to B cell-mediated rejection, as mIL-2 therapy effectively controlled DSA production, reduced germinal center (GC) B cell responses, and increased Tfr/Tfh ratio (Figs. 3, 4, Supplementary Fig. 4, 5). The Tfr/Tfh balance is crucial for modulating GC activity23,24, and its disruption is associated with DSA formation and chronic allograft dysfunction49. The expansion of bulk Tregs may also suppress Tfh priming and subsequent T cell–B cell interactions within secondary lymphoid tissues, thereby contributing to the observed immune regulation. Interestingly, ST2⁺ Tregs appeared to attenuate B cell-mediated immunity, as ST2 deficiency led to a significant decrease in Tfr/Tfh ratios and an increase in DSA levels. Further investigation, including the function of the Tfh and Tfr, should be warranted to elucidate the detailed mechanisms.

One important observation was that graft acceptance persisted even after mIL-2 treatment was stopped despite the reduced circulating Tregs. Histopathological analysis showed minimal chronic rejection in the long-term, suggesting that expansion of Tregs during the induction phase is a critical window for promoting long-term immune regulation, which may lead to sustained alloantigen-specific unresponsiveness. Furthermore, our observation supports the previous findings on mixed chimerism-induced tolerance that circulating levels of Tregs are poor markers of antigen-specific graft acceptance50, indicating that other strategies will be needed to assess tissue-specific Tregs, which will be more relevant in addressing this question in the clinic.

This study has several limitations. The durability and mechanistic basis of donor-specific unresponsiveness after cessation of mIL-2 therapy remain to be fully elucidated. Whether other ST2-negative Treg subsets contribute to long-term graft acceptance should be examined in future studies. It is also unclear whether mIL-2-expanded Tregs can effectively suppress memory alloimmune responses. While ST2⁺ Tregs are associated with tissue homeostasis, their direct suppressive functions and influence on the local microenvironment require further investigation. Lastly, comprehensive safety assessments, including potential risks of infection and malignancy, are needed to inform clinical translation. Importantly, we envision mIL-2 not as a standalone tolerance-inducing agent, but as an adjunct to conventional immunosuppression to promote immune regulation and enable long-term minimization of immunosuppression.

In summary, our study demonstrates that mIL-2 therapy induces a robust immunomodulatory effect, promoting graft acceptance in multiple organ transplantation models by expanding a unique ST2⁺ tissue-resident Treg phenotype. These findings not only provide an additional therapeutic strategy to complement existing immunosuppressive regimens but also pave the way for alloantigen-specific immune acceptance with reduced reliance on conventional global immunosuppression.

Methods

IL-2 mutein

The IL-2 mutein (clone: VIS171) was provided by Visterra Inc. The histidine residue of position 16 of the IL-2 molecule was replaced with leucine (H16L substitution) to reduce the interaction with the medium-affinity IL-2 receptor, and the Fc domain of a human IgG1 was fused to IL-2 with Fc modifications to abrogate effector functions17.

Animals

C57BL/6 J (B6) (stock no. 000664), BALB/c (stock no. 000651), C3H (stock no. 000659), CB6F1/J (stock no. 100007), and B6 Foxp3YFPCre mice (stock no. 016959)51 were obtained from The Jackson Laboratory. B6 Foxp3GFP mice52 were generous gifts from the Alexander Y. Rudensky laboratory. B6 Il1rl1fl/fl mice53 were obtained from R. Lee (Brigham and Women’s Hospital) and crossed with Foxp3YFPCre mice to generate Il1rl1fl/flFoxp3YFPCre mice. Il1rl1fl/flFoxp3YFPCre mice were bred and maintained by Luster lab and kindly provided to Riella lab. Experimental and control mice were co-housed in a specific pathogen-free facility, under 12 h light/12 h dark cycle, at 22 °C and 42% humidity. All the animals used in the experiment were 7–12 weeks of age. Both male and female animals were used in the experiment, and no significant differences in immunologic response were observed. All animals were bred and maintained in the animal facility of the Massachusetts General Hospital in specific pathogen-free conditions in accordance with the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) and National Institutes of Health Animal Care guidelines. The Massachusetts General Hospital IACUC approved all experiments (protocol numbers 2020N000085 and 2020N000125).

Murine heterotopic heart transplantation

Vascularized heterotopic cardiac transplantation was performed under isoflurane anesthesia54. Cardiac allografts were isolated from the donor mice after adequate cannulation and preserved with heparinized saline on ice until the recipients were ready for transplantation. Subsequently, the recipient mice were anesthetized, and the heart graft was transplanted into the recipient’s abdomen using microsurgical techniques. The allograft’s ascending aorta and pulmonary artery were end-to-side anastomosed to the recipient’s abdominal aorta and inferior vena cava, respectively. The second allograft was implanted into the right cervical area, and the allograft’s ascending aorta and pulmonary artery were end-to-end anastomosed to the recipient’s right external carotid artery and vein, respectively. The time of cold ischemia of the graft was 20 to 25 min, and the warm ischemia was 30 to 35 min. Functionality of the cardiac grafts was monitored daily by palpation. Rejection was defined by the cessation of palpable heartbeat and confirmed by direct visualization. Loss of graft function within 48 h after transplantation was classified as a technical failure and animals in which this occurred were omitted from the analysis.

0.5 mg/kg of mIL-2 or equivalent weight of control IgG was administered subcutaneously on days 0, 2, 5, and twice a week thereafter. When indicated, 10 mg/kg of CTLA-4 Ig (Abatacept; Bristol Myers Squibb) was administered intraperitoneally on day 2 after transplantation.

Murine kidney transplantation

The donor kidney and its ureter were transplanted into the abdominal cavity of the recipient mice55. Briefly, the donor’s left kidney, including the left renal artery, abdominal aorta, left renal vein, and left ureter, was isolated en bloc from the donor mice. Under isoflurane anesthesia, the kidney allograft was transplanted into the recipients’ abdomen by the end-to-side anastomosis of the graft’s abdominal aorta and left renal vein to the recipient’s abdominal aorta and inferior vena cava, respectively. The ureter was directly anastomosed into the bladder by using a pull-through technique. The recipient’s right native kidney was removed during the surgery to make space for graft implantation, and the left kidney remained untouched. The time of cold ischemia of the allograft was 30 min, and the warm ischemia was 30 to 35 min. 0.5 mg/kg of mIL-2 or equivalent weight of control IgG was administered subcutaneously on days 0, 2, 5, and twice a week thereafter.

Blood collection and analysis

Peripheral blood was collected in a heparinized collection tube. Collected blood was immediately provided for complete blood count and flow cytometric analysis. A complete blood counts were measured using a Heska Element HT5 analyzer, which employed electrical impedance, colorimetric method, and flow cytometry to quantify RBC, PLT, HBG, and WBC, respectively. At a specific time point, the serum was collected with a collection tube without an anticoagulant. After centrifugation, the serum was separated and stored at -80C until the analysis.

Tissue procurement and isolation of tissue-resident cells from allografts and spleens

Some animals were treated with i.v. injection of 10 μg/g of Brefeldin A (BioLegend) 4 h before tissue procurement for cytokine expression analysis56. Animals were anesthetized with isoflurane and i.v. injection of 3 μg/mouse of fluorochrome-conjugated anti-CD45 antibody (30-F11, BioLegend) was performed to enable flow cytometric differentiation of circulating (i.v. antibody positive) and tissue-resident (i.v. antibody negative) cells20. After 3 min, the animals were euthanized, and blood was washed out by injecting 10 mL of PBS from the left ventricle. The allografts and spleens were then procured for downstream analyses.

To isolate cells from the cardiac allografts and spleens, the tissues were excised, minced, and digested with 500 U/mL collagenase D (Roche) for 30 min at 37 °C, followed by incubation with 0.1 M EDTA in PBS, pH 7.2, buffer for 5 min before final suspension in 5 mM EDTA (Gibco), 1% FBS (Gibco) in PBS 1x, pH 7.2 (Boston BioProducts). Isolated cells were then mechanically dissociated through a 70 μm cell strainer (Corning), and RBCs were lysed using hypotonic ACK buffer (Gibco). For kidney allografts, organs were excised, minced, and digested with a dissociation solution containing 1 mg/mL collagenase type I (Gibco) and 0.1 mg/mL of DNase I (Stem Cell) for 30 min at 37 °C. Isolated cells were then mechanically dissociated through a 70 μm cell strainer (Corning) in a 50 ml tube, washed and resuspended in 1x PBS. The cells were then subjected to density gradient centrifugation using Lympholyte M (Cedarlane) and isolated mononuclear cells. Total recovered cells were counted by flow cytometry using AccuCheck Counting Beads (Thermo Fisher) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Histology

The procured allografts were fixed in 10% formalin for 24 to 48 h and then stored in 70% ethanol until embedding. Fixed grafts were embedded in paraffin, sectioned at 5 μm of thickness, and stained with Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), Verhoeff–Van Gieson (VVG), or Masson’s trichrome. A transplant pathologist blinded to the experimental groups performed pathologic evaluation using the revised ISHLT classification for heart grafts57, and the current Banff Classification of Renal Allograft Pathology for kidney grafts58. Photomicrographs were taken using a Zeiss AXIO Imager M2 with Axiocam 506 color and Zeiss ZEN software.

Flow cytometry and cell sorting

Single-cell suspensions were washed with PBS and stained with LIVE/DEAD Fixable Blue Dead Cell Stain Kit (Thermo Fisher). Cells were then blocked with TruStain FcX (anti-mouse CD16/32, clone 93, BioLegend) for 20 min on ice, followed by 30 min of cell surface staining on ice. The surface staining of peripheral blood was performed immediately after blood collection for 30 min at room temperature, followed by 10 min of incubation with BD FACS lysis buffer. For intracellular staining, cells were fixed and permeabilized with the eBioscience Foxp3/Transcription factor Staining Buffer Set (Thermo Fisher) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and stained with the indicated antibodies overnight at 4 C. All cytokine analyses were performed after 4 h of in vivo brefeldin A stimulation (BioLegend, 10 μg/g body weight) before procurement, and cells did not undergo additional stimulation after single-cell suspension. All flow cytometry analyses were performed with the BD Fortessa X-20 (BD Biosciences) with FACS Diva software (BD Biosciences). Data were analyzed using FlowJo software (version X; BD Biosciences). Gating strategies can be found in Supplementary Fig. 3a.

To isolate spleen Tregs, spleens from Foxp3GFP heart transplant recipients were recovered, and CD4+ cells were magnetically isolated using a negative selection kit from StemCell Technologies (EasySep Mouse CD4+ T cell isolation kit) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Enriched cells were stained and sorted using a FACSAria Fusion cell sorter (70 μm nozzle) using optimal purity settings. Tregs were gated as CD45 i.v. negative CD4+Foxp3(GFP)+ cells (Supplementary Fig. 6).

Fluorochrome-labeled monoclonal antibodies were purchased from BioLegend or BD biosciences: anti-CD45 (30-F11), anti-CD45.2 (104), anti-CD3e (145-2c11), anti-CD4 (GK1.5), anti-CD8 (53-6.7), anti-CD25 (PC61), CD44 (IM8), CD62L (MEL-14), anti-Gr-1 (RB6-8C5), anti-CD11b (M1/70), anti-CD11c (N418), anti-CD19 (6D5), anti-NK1.1 (PK136), anti-PD-1 (29F-1A12), anti-CXCR5 (L138D7), anti-B220 (RA3-6B2), anti-IgD (11-26 c.2a), anti-FAS (SA367H8), anti-GL-7 (GL7), anti-IgG1 (RMG1-1), anti-ST2 (DIH9), anti-CXCR3 (CXCR3-173), anti-KLRG1 (2F1/KLRG1), anti-ICOS (C398.4A), anti-LAG-3 (C9B7W) and anti-Tim-3 (RMT3-23) for cell surface staining and anti-Foxp3 (MF-14), anti-Ki67 (16A8), anti-CTLA-4 (UC10-4B9), anti-GATA3 (16E10A23), anti-T-bet (4B10), anti-IFN-γ (XMG1.2), anti-IL-17 (TC11-18H10.1), anti-Granzyme B (QA16A12), anti-LAP (TW7-16B4), and anti-IL-10 (JES5-16E3) for intracellular staining.

Donor Specific anti-MHC Antibody (DSA) quantification

For the donor-specific anti-IgG measurement, we used BALB/c or C57BL/6 mice as probes for DSA quantification in the serum. 3 × 105 splenocytes were added to 96-well plates, and after two washes, the cells were incubated for 30 min with serum from naïve animals or organ transplant recipients at specific time points (1:40 dilution). Cells were then stained with 7-AAD (BioLegend), anti-B220 (BioLegend) and anti-CD3 (BioLegend) in the presence of Fc-block (BioLegend). Cells were washed twice and incubated for 30 minutes with anti-IgG antibodies (BioLegend). Donor reactive antibody against MHC class II was measured by assessing anti-IgG signal on total B220+ cells and against MHC class I by assessing anti-IgG signal on total CD3+ cells by flow cytometry and expressed as mean fluorescence intensity (MFI).

Enzyme-linked immunospot assay (ELISpot)

Mouse IFN-γ ELISpot assays were performed using kits from BD Biosciences according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, 0.45 μm hydrophobic, high-protein-binding Immobilon-P membrane plates (Millipore) were coated with IFN-γ capture antibody at 4 °C overnight, followed by blocking with 10% FBS-supplemented RPMI1640 for 1 h at room temperature. The 5 × 105 spleen cells isolated from the recipient mouse were seeded into the plate and incubated with 5 × 105 irradiated BALB/c, B6, or C3H splenocytes at 37 °C and 5% CO2 for 24 h. Spots were detected and counted using an ImmunoSpot analyzer (Cellular Technology).

In vitro regulatory T cell suppression assay

Spleen cells from heart-transplanted recipients were isolated at 28 days after transplantation, and CD4+Foxp3-GFP+ Tregs from control IgG-treated recipients, CD4+Foxp3-GFP+ST2- Tregs from mIL-2 treated recipients, and CD4+Foxp3-GFP+ST2+ Tregs from mIL-2 treated recipients were flow sorted (gating is shown in Fig. 6b). CD4+Foxp3-GFP- Tconv cells in control group were flow sorted as responder cells and stained with 1 μM of Cell Trace Violet Proliferation Dye (Thermo Fisher) for 20 min at room temperature, followed by a 5-min recovery in culture media at 37 C. Tconv were cocultured with control Treg, mIL-2 ST2-, and mIL-2 ST2+ Tregs from each group at 4: 1 ratios in the presence of anti-mouse CD3/CD28-conjugated beads (Thermo Fisher). Cells were incubated at 37 °C for 72 h and proliferation was analyzed by flow cytometry.

RNA sequencing and data analysis

Total RNA was isolated from flow-sorted spleen Tregs using RNeasy Plus (Qiagen). Dual-indexed libraries were generated using Takara/Clontech Stranded Total RNA-Seq Kit v2 - Pico Input Mammalian reagents (Takara). RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) libraries were sequenced on a NovaSeq sequencer (Illumina) according to the manufacturer’s instructions in pair-ended 2 × 150 bp reads. All expected barcodes were detected and the mean depth is ≥40 M reads. We used Fastp 0.22.0 63 to detect and trim the adaptor sequences, keeping only reads with at least 60% of bases with a phred quality ≥15. After quality control, we estimated the transcription abundance with Salmon 1.9.064 using the reference mouse transcriptome and annotation GRCm39 from release M34-Ensembl 111 from the Gencode65 portal (https://www.gencodegenes.org/mouse/). We used the R 4.2.1 package tximport 1.26.1 to summarize the transcript-level abundances to use in DESeq2 1.38.3 to perform the differential expression analysis. The PCA analysis was performed with scikit-learn 1.5.259. The distance correlation between expression data was performed with scipy 1.14.160. The graphs were made with matplotlib 3.8.0 and seaborn 0.11.2. The dendrogram was plotted with ggtree 3.2061. The heatmap was generated with ClustVis (https://biit.cs.ut.ee/clustvis/)62.

Statistics

Sample sizes were determined based on our and other investigators experience with the respective animal experiments. No statistical methods were used to predetermine sample size as we observed many statistically significant effects in the data with the above methods of sample size selection without a priori sample size calculations. No data were excluded from the analyses. In pathological evaluation, a transplant pathologist blinded to the experimental groups performed the evaluation. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were constructed, and a log-rank test was used to compare the allograft survival between groups. For independent 2-group comparisons, we used the unpaired 2-tailed Student’s t test for analysis. Two-tailed t tests were corrected for multiple comparisons using the Holm-Sidak method. For multiple group comparisons, the 1-way ANOVA or 2-way ANOVA test was used to determine differences, depending on the number of comparison groups. Multiple comparisons between levels were checked with the Tukey post hoc test for 1-way ANOVA and Sidak test for a 2-way ANOVA. Differences were considered significant at P ≤ 0.05. Prism 10 software was used for data analysis and graphs (GraphPad Software, Inc.).
