Tumor-derived progranulin promotes macrophage cholesterol efflux and associated immunosuppressive features in oral squamous cell carcinoma
Authors: Yijun Luan, Yan Xu, Simin Zhao, Hao Li, Zheming Liu, Pishan Yang, Chengzhe Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCholesterol efflux is increasingly recognized as an important regulator of macrophage polarization and immunosuppressive features; however, the tumor-derived signals that govern this process in oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) remain poorly defined.MethodsWe performed integrated single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) on primary OSCC tumors (n = 3) and validated the findings in 77 head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC)-related samples across five public datasets. The role of tumor-derived progranulin (PGRN) in macrophage cholesterol efflux and associated phenotypic changes was examined using genetic knockdown, pharmacologic inhibition of the PGRN-SORT1 interaction, and activation of downstream PPARγ signaling in vitro and in vivo.ResultsA malignant epithelial subpopulation with high PGRN expression was identified and associated with macrophage cholesterol efflux regulation through a prominent SORT1-associated signaling axis. Functional assays suggested that tumor-derived PGRN promoted a SORT1-linked PPARγ-LXRα-ABCA1/ABCG1 program, accompanied by enhanced cholesterol efflux and reduced intracellular cholesterol levels in macrophages. This metabolic rewiring was associated with immunosuppressive macrophage features, accompanied by increased secretion of IL-6, IL-10, and TGF-β. PGRN knockdown or SORT1 inhibition attenuated cholesterol efflux and increased intracellular cholesterol retention in vitro. In vivo, PGRN knockdown was associated with changes in macrophage polarization-related features, as reflected by an increased CD86+/CD206+ ratio. Notably, PPARγ agonism with rosiglitazone partially restored these phenotypic changes in PGRN-deficient tumors, supporting the involvement of this signaling axis.ConclusionTumor-derived PGRN is associated with immunosuppressive macrophage features in OSCC, at least in part through a SORT1-linked cholesterol efflux program involving downstream PPARγ-LXRα activation. These findings support a role for the PGRN-SORT1-associated axis as a potential immunometabolic pathway associated with macrophage cholesterol efflux and phenotypic features in OSCC.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12967-026-08422-5.

Introduction

Oral cancer, particularly oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC), represents a major global health burden, with over 389,000 new cases and 188,000 deaths annually [1, 2]. Despite advances in surgical resection, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy, the 5-year survival rate remains disappointingly below 50% [3, 4]. This clinical reality underscores the need to better define the molecular mechanisms that shape OSCC progression and its immune microenvironment.

Recent advances in cancer biology have reframed tumors as complex ecosystems rather than isolated masses of malignant cells. The tumor microenvironment (TME), composed of various stromal and immune cells, plays a critical role in cancer progression and immune evasion. Among these, tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) are particularly abundant and exhibit remarkable plasticity, capable of adopting diverse phenotypes that either support or suppress anti-tumor immunity [5–7]. TAMs in OSCC have been associated with tumor growth, metastasis, and treatment resistance, highlighting the importance of understanding the mechanisms that shape their functional states.

Beyond cytokine signaling, increasing evidence points to metabolic reprogramming as a fundamental mechanism regulating TAM function. Cholesterol, an essential lipid component of cellular membranes, influences numerous macrophage activities, including phagocytosis, antigen presentation, and cytokine secretion [8–11]. Dysregulated cholesterol metabolism in TAMs not only alters immune responsiveness but also contributes to the formation of an immunosuppressive TME that facilitates tumor progression [8, 12, 13]. Consequently, understanding the mechanisms that regulate macrophage cholesterol efflux may provide insight into phenotypic regulation in tumor-associated macrophages.

Emerging evidence suggests that tumor-derived secreted factors may directly modulate macrophage lipid metabolism [9, 14]. However, the molecular mediators responsible for this regulation remain incompletely defined. One such candidate is progranulin (PGRN; gene symbol GRN), a multifunctional growth factor implicated in tumor proliferation, angiogenesis, and chemoresistance [15, 16]. It has also been implicated in tumor-associated signaling and macrophage-related immune regulation [17–21], but its role in macrophage cholesterol efflux and related phenotypic changes remains unclear.

In this study, we integrated single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and experimental validation to investigate the role of tumor-derived PGRN in macrophage cholesterol efflux in OSCC. Our findings suggest that PGRN enhances macrophage cholesterol efflux through SORT1, accompanied by activation of the PPARγ-LXRα pathway and immunosuppressive macrophage-associated features. Together, these findings link tumor-derived PGRN to macrophage cholesterol efflux and associated phenotypic features in OSCC.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition and scRNA-seq preprocessing

The scRNA-seq data were obtained from a cohort of three OSCC patients and deposited in GSA-Human under accession number HRA007439 at the National Genomics Data Center (https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/gsa-human) [22]. Detailed protocols for tissue collection and single-cell suspension preparation using the BD Rhapsody™ platform are available in the original publication [22]. In addition, public datasets from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (GSE103322, GSE164690, GSE181919, GSE195832, and GSE215403) were analyzed, comprising 68 HNSCC samples and 9 normal adjacent tissue (NAT) samples.

Raw count matrices were imported into Seurat (v4.1.0) in R and integrated for downstream analysis. Before integration, each dataset was independently normalized, dimensionally reduced, and clustered to inspect its intrinsic structure and reduce potential cross-platform bias. Quality control excluded cells with fewer than 200 or more than 5,000 detected genes, abnormal UMI counts, or mitochondrial read percentages > 10%. Cells with high erythrocyte-related signatures or potential multiplet features were also excluded. After filtering, data were log-normalized with a scale factor of 10,000, and 3,000 highly variable genes were selected using the variance-stabilizing transformation method. Scaling was performed with regression of mitochondrial read percentages and UMI counts. To reduce batch effects across samples and datasets, merged samples were harmonized using Harmony (v1.2.0) to correct for technical variability related to dataset origin, sequencing batch, and donor-specific effects. Batch-corrected embeddings were visualized using t-SNE or UMAP, as indicated in the corresponding figures. After filtering, 180,754 cells were retained, including 164,371 cells from HNSCC samples and 16,383 cells from NAT samples.

Unsupervised clusters were identified using the “FindClusters” function. Cell types were annotated based on established marker genes, and differentially expressed genes were identified using the “FindAllMarkers” function with the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Heatmaps and violin plots were generated using ggplot2.

Cell-cell communication networks were inferred using the CellChat R package [23], beginning with the initialization of a CellChat object using the “createCellChat” function. Preprocessing steps included identification of overexpressed genes (“identifyOverExpressedGenes”), ligand-receptor interactions (“identifyOverExpressedInteractions”), and data projection (“projectData”) using default parameters from the human CellChatDB. Interaction probabilities were calculated using “computeCommunProb”, filtered by cell abundance (“filterCommunication”, min.cells = 3), and analyzed at the pathway level using “computeCommunProbPathway”. Aggregated networks were generated using “aggregateNet”.

Pathway activity at the cluster level was assessed using gene set variation analysis (GSVA) with KEGG pathway gene sets, and selected pathway scores were visualized across macrophage subsets. Functional enrichment analysis of Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG pathways was conducted using clusterProfiler. Enriched pathways were identified using “compareCluster” with an adjusted p value < 0.05 and visualized as dot plots.

Tissue distribution for each cluster was evaluated using the STARTRAC-dist index [24]. Ro/e represents the ratio of observed to expected cell counts; values greater than 1 indicate enrichment, whereas values less than 1 indicate depletion of a given subcluster in the corresponding tissue or cell category. Single-cell copy-number variation analysis was performed using inferCNV, with immune cells as the reference population, to help distinguish malignant from non-malignant epithelial cells. inferCNV analysis was performed using the denoise option, standard hidden Markov model settings, and a cutoff value of 0.1.

Patients and specimens

Clinical specimens were obtained from the pathology biobank at Qilu Hospital of Shandong University from 40 patients diagnosed with OSCC between 2006 and 2015, with approval from the Ethics Committee of Qilu Hospital (KYLL-202210-052). All patients underwent surgical resection of visible tumor lesions, and none received cancer-specific treatments prior to surgery. Tumor specimens were embedded in paraffin and subjected to histopathological evaluation. Clinical characteristics, including age, sex, tumor size, lymph node involvement, surrounding tissue invasion, survival status, and follow-up information were documented. Prognostic correlations with GRN gene expression and HNSCC survival outcomes were evaluated using the GEPIA2 database (http://gepia2.cancer-pku.cn/#survival).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) of PGRN and CD68

Tumor and NAT specimens were sectioned at 5 μm thickness for immunohistochemistry. Sections were baked at 65 °C for 2 h, deparaffinized in xylene, and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. Antigen retrieval was performed in EDTA buffer (pH 9.0, 1:50 dilution) using microwave heating for 20 min. After cooling, sections were blocked with goat serum at room temperature for 1 h. Slides were incubated overnight at 4 °C with either rabbit anti-PGRN polyclonal antibody (1:1000, Cat No. 18410-1-AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, China) or mouse anti-CD68 monoclonal antibody (1:100, clone KP1, Cat No. ab955, Abcam, USA). Signal detection was performed using a non-biotin detection system (PV-9000, ZSGB-BIO, Beijing, China). Immunoreactivity was visualized with DAB, and nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin. Slides were examined under an Olympus BX53 microscope, and whole-slide images were captured using the SLIDEVIEW™ VS200 system (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

PGRN expression was semi-quantitatively evaluated based on staining intensity (scored from 0 to 3) and the percentage of positive tumor cells (scored from 0 to 4). PGRN high expression was defined as strong intensity (score 3) with > 25% positive cells (score 2–4), or moderate intensity (score 2) with > 75% positive cells (score 4). Other cases were considered as low PGRN expression. For CD68 assessment, CD68+ macrophages and total nucleated cells were counted in six non-overlapping high-power fields (400×) from representative tumor areas for each patient. The percentage of CD68⁺ cells among total cells was calculated for each field, and the mean value per patient was used for analysis. Each data point in the CD68 quantification plot represented one patient. All slides were independently evaluated by two blinded pathologists, with final review by a senior pathologist.

Cell culture and treatment

Human immortalized oral epithelial cells (HIOEC) were obtained from the Shanghai Cell Bank and cultured in Keratinocyte Serum Free Medium supplemented with bovine pituitary extract (Gibco-BRL, 10744019, NY, USA). The CAL27 human OSCC cell line, THP-1 human monocyte line, and RAW264.7 murine macrophage-like cell line were sourced from ATCC. All cell lines were maintained in a humidified atmosphere at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

THP-1 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 (Vivacell, C3010-0500, Shanghai, China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco 10099-141, NY, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Biosharp, BL505A, Anhui, China), while CAL27 and RAW264.7 cells were kept in DMEM (Vivacell, C3103-0500, Shanghai, China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin.

Transfection assay

To generate GRN-knockdown CAL27 cells (shPGRN-CAL27), CAL27 cells were transduced with lentiviral shRNA vectors targeting GRN (GeneChem Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Control cells were transduced with a scrambled shRNA vector (shNC-CAL27). Knockdown efficiency was evaluated using qRT-PCR and western blotting, and stable cell lines were selected by treating with puromycin (2 µg/mL) for 7 days.

Conditioned medium preparation

Conditioned media (CMs) were prepared by plating 5 × 106 CM donor cells in 10 mL of complete medium and allowing them to grow to 80–90% confluence, which typically required approximately 48 h after passaging. The medium was then replaced with serum-free RPMI 1640. After a 24-hour incubation, the supernatants were collected and centrifuged at 2,680 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove cell debris. The cleared supernatants were aliquoted and stored at -80 °C until use. These CMs from HIOEC, CAL27, shPGRN-CAL27, and shNC-CAL27 were designated as HIOEC-CM, CAL27-CM, shPGRN-CAL27-CM, and shNC-CAL27-CM.

Indirect co-culture and CM treatment of THP-1 and RAW264.7 cells

THP-1 or RAW264.7 cells were seeded at a density of 5 × 105 cells per well in 6-well plates and allowed to stabilize for 24 h in complete RPMI 1640 (for THP-1) or DMEM (for RAW264.7), both supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. For CM treatment, the culture medium was then replaced with a 1:1 mixture of the indicated CM and fresh complete medium, and cells were incubated for an additional 48 h. Unless otherwise specified, THP-1 cells were used in their undifferentiated, non-adherent state, as previous studies have used tumor-cell-derived CM to induce TAM-like activation without prior PMA treatment [25].

For SORT1 blockade, HY-115,213 (MCE, NJ, USA), a PGRN-SORT1 interaction inhibitor reported to disrupt ligand-receptor binding with an IC₅₀ of approximately 2 µM, was applied at 2 µM. The inhibitor was added 2 h prior to CM treatment and maintained throughout the 48-h incubation. Its specificity has been characterized in biochemical and structural assays [26].

For PPARγ activation, 2 µM rosiglitazone (RSG, a PPARγ agonist; HY-17386, MCE, NJ, USA) was added at the beginning of CM treatment for 48 h. After treatment, cells and supernatants were collected for qRT-PCR, Western blotting, ELISA, and cholesterol quantification.

Immunocytochemistry for PGRN-SORT1 co-localization

To examine PGRN-SORT1 co-localization, immunocytochemistry (ICC) was performed in RAW264.7 macrophages cultured on 35-mm glass-bottom dishes (Nest). Recombinant mouse PGRN protein (rmPGRN; 400 ng/mL; RP02801, ABclonal, Wuhan, China) was added to RAW264.7 cell cultures for 2 h before fixation to assess PGRN–SORT1 co-localization following exogenous ligand exposure. Cells were then washed with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min at room temperature.

For PGRN detection, cells were blocked with 5% goat serum (ZLI-9022, ZSGB-BIO, Beijing, China) for 1 h and incubated with rabbit anti-PGRN antibody (1:500, 18410-1-AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, China) at 4 °C overnight. After three PBS washes, cells were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (PV8000, ZSGB-BIO, Beijing, China) for 1 h, followed by tyramide signal amplification (TSA)-Cy5 signal development for 10 min at room temperature in the dark. Antibody complexes from the first staining round were stripped using antibody elution buffer (abs994, Absin, Shanghai, China) for 20 min at 37 °C, followed by PBS washes.

For SORT1 staining, cells were re-blocked with 5% goat serum for 1 h and incubated with rabbit anti-SORT1 antibody (1:400, 12369-1-AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, China) for 1 h at room temperature, followed by Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG (1:300, SA00013-2, Proteintech, Wuhan, China) for 1 h. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus IX73), and co-localization was qualitatively assessed by examining membrane-proximal overlap of PGRN and SORT1 signals.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cell cultures using the Fastagen Biotech™ RNAfast200 Extreme Extraction Kit (Fastagen Biotech Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). Reverse transcription was conducted using Hifair® Ⅲ 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix for qRT-PCR (gDNA digester plus) (11141ES10, YEASEN, Shanghai, China). cDNA amplification was performed using Hieff® qRT-PCR SYBR Green Master Mix (No ROX) (11201ES08, YEASEN, Shanghai, China), following the manufacturer’s protocols. The qRT-PCR conditions included an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 30 s. Each sample was run in triplicate, and relative expression levels were calculated using the 2-ΔΔCt method. ACTB was used as the internal control for human cells, and Actb was used as the internal control for mouse cells. Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S1. Each experiment was repeated independently three times.

Western blotting analysis

Cell lysates were prepared using ice-cold RIPA buffer with PMSF (R0020, Solarbio, Beijing, China). Protein concentrations were measured using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (PC0020, Solarbio, Beijing, China). Equal amounts of protein were loaded onto SDS-PAGE gels (PAGE Gel Fast Preparation Kit, PG112, Epizyme Biotech, Shanghai, China) and subsequently transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, MA, USA). Membranes were blocked and then incubated with primary antibodies against PGRN (1:500, 10826-RP03, Sino Biological, Beijing, China), ABCA1 (1:1000, #96292, Cell Signaling Technology, MA, USA), ABCG1 (1:1000, A17907, ABclonal, Wuhan, China), SR-B1 (1:800, A0827, ABclonal, Wuhan, China), PPARγ (1:2500, 16643-1-AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), LXRα (1:5000, 14351-1-AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), and GAPDH (1:20,000, 10494-1-AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, China). After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (ZB2301, ZSGB-BIO, Beijing, China) and detected using an ECL kit (BL520A, Biosharp, Anhui, China). Imaging and analysis were performed using the EasyCL-50 system (Nanjing Zhiheng Intelligent Technology Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China). Each experiment was independently conducted three times.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

PGRN concentrations in CMs and IL-6, IL-10, and TGF-β concentrations in macrophage culture supernatants were measured using species-specific ELISA kits following the manufacturers’ instructions. PGRN ELISA kits were obtained from Boster Biological Technology (Wuhan, China), and IL-6, IL-10, and TGF-β ELISA kits were obtained from 4 A Biotech (Suzhou, China).

Total cholesterol measurement

Total cholesterol levels in culture supernatants and cell lysates were determined using a total cholesterol assay kit (A111-1-1, Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Culture supernatants were collected after treatment and clarified before measurement. For intracellular cholesterol measurement, cells were washed with PBS and disrupted by sonication on ice, and cholesterol levels were normalized to total protein concentration. Cholesterol content was calculated according to the kit formula.

Filipin III staining

RAW264.7 cells were seeded in 35-mm glass-bottom dishes at a density of 1 × 105 cells per dish and incubated with the indicated CMs or treatments for 48 h. After washing three times with PBS, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Biosharp, BL539A, China) for 30 min at room temperature. Cells were then incubated with 50 µg/mL Filipin III (SAE0087, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) in the dark at 37 °C for 1 h. Subsequently, the cells were washed three times with PBS and imaged using an Olympus IX73 fluorescence microscope. For semi-quantitative analysis, the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of Filipin III staining was measured using ImageJ software. Six randomly selected fields per sample were analyzed, and the average MFI was calculated for each experimental condition.

Xenograft experiments

Animal experiments were conducted in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines and approved by the Ethics Committee of Experimental Animals of Qilu Hospital, Shandong University (Approval No: DWLL-202400115). Eighteen male BALB/c nude mice (nu/nu, aged 4–5 weeks) were obtained from Jinan Pengyue Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Jinan, China) and housed under specific-pathogen-free (SPF) conditions at the Laboratory Animal Center of Qilu Hospital, Shandong University. The mice were randomly divided into three groups (n = 6/group): the shNC-CAL27 group (mice inoculated with scrambled shRNA-transfected CAL27 cells), the shPGRN-CAL27 group (mice inoculated with PGRN-knockdown CAL27 cells), and the shPGRN-CAL27 + RSG group (mice inoculated with PGRN-knockdown CAL27 cells and treated with rosiglitazone in vivo). CAL27-shNC and CAL27-shPGRN cells were harvested during logarithmic growth, washed, and resuspended in serum-free RPMI-1640. Importantly, no pharmacologic agents (including RSG or inhibitors) were applied to the cells prior to inoculation. Each mouse received a subcutaneous injection of 1 × 106 cells into the right flank. Once tumors became palpable (day 10 after inoculation), mice in the shPGRN-CAL27 + RSG group were administered RSG (10 mg/kg, intraperitoneally, in 0.5% CMC-Na) every 48 h, whereas mice in the other groups received an equivalent volume of vehicle (0.5% CMC-Na) on the same schedule. After 14 days of treatment, mice were euthanized using CO2 asphyxiation. During the study, four mice died before the endpoint, including two in the shPGRN-CAL27 + RSG group and one in each of the other two groups, leaving 14 evaluable tumor specimens for endpoint analysis.

Tumor processing

After euthanasia, tumors were excised, rinsed in cold PBS, and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C for 24 h. Fixed tissues were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, cleared in xylene, and paraffin-embedded. Tumors were sectioned at 4-µm thickness and mounted on poly-L-lysine-coated slides. Sections were subsequently subjected to antigen retrieval, blocking, and multiplex immunofluorescence (mIF) staining as described below.

Multiplex immunofluorescence staining

mIF was performed using a TSA-based Enhanced Polymer Detection System (ZSGB-BIO, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, deparaffinized and rehydrated tissue sections underwent antigen retrieval in EDTA buffer (pH 9.0) followed by blocking with 5% goat serum. Sections were sequentially incubated with primary antibodies against F4/80 (1:3000, GB113373, Servicebio, Wuhan, China), CD86 (1:1000, GB115630, Servicebio, Wuhan, China), and CD206 (1:5000, GB113497, Servicebio, Wuhan, China), each followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies and corresponding TSA fluorophore development (PerkinElmer). After each round of staining, slides were subjected to heat-mediated stripping to remove bound antibodies before applying the next marker. No-primary-antibody controls were processed identically, with primary antibodies omitted and replaced with PBS. Only cells with marker-positive signals above the background threshold defined by these controls were included for quantification. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Slides were scanned using a Pannoramic Digital Slide Scanner (3DHISTECH, Hungary). Image analysis and quantification were performed using ImageJ (Fiji). For each tumor, five non-overlapping high-power fields (HPFs, 400×) from representative tumor areas were selected. Total nucleated cells were defined as DAPI⁺ cells, and total macrophages were defined as F4/80⁺ cells. F4/80⁺ macrophage infiltration (%) was calculated as F4/80⁺ cells / total DAPI⁺ cells × 100%. Subset proportions were calculated as follows: F4/80⁺CD86⁺ (%) = F4/80⁺CD86⁺ cells / total F4/80⁺ cells × 100%; F4/80⁺CD206⁺ (%) = F4/80⁺CD206⁺ cells / total F4/80⁺ cells × 100%. The F4/80⁺CD86⁺/F4/80⁺CD206⁺ ratio was calculated for each field and averaged for each tumor. Two independent examiners blinded to group allocation performed the quantification.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS (v25.0) and GraphPad Prism (v9.0). Data are presented as mean ± SD. Unless otherwise indicated in the figure legends, in vitro experiments were performed with at least three independent biological replicates. Two-group comparisons were analyzed using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test. For experiments involving three or more groups, overall differences were assessed using one-way ANOVA. Post hoc testing was performed using Tukey’s multiple-comparisons test for all pairwise comparisons or Holm-adjusted prespecified pairwise comparisons, as indicated in the figure legends. For clinicopathological variables in Table 1, categorical data were analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate, and continuous variables were analyzed using an unpaired Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test depending on data distribution. Survival differences were evaluated using the log-rank test. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. For experiments with n = 3 independent biological replicates, formal normality testing has limited power. Accordingly, parametric tests were applied under an assumption of approximate normality.

Results

Single-cell transcriptomics identifies a PGRN-SORT1-associated epithelial-macrophage interaction linked to macrophage cholesterol metabolism in OSCC

Integrated analysis of scRNA-seq data from three OSCC patients identified seven epithelial subpopulations (Epi_1-Epi_7) (Supplementary Fig. S1A) and six macrophage subsets (Mac_1-Mac_6) (Supplementary Fig. S1C). Notably, Epi_6 was enriched in OSCC tissues compared with paracancerous tissues (Supplementary Fig. S1B) and showed markedly elevated GRN expression (Supplementary Fig. S1E). CellChat analysis revealed prominent inferred GRN signaling from Epi_6 (ligand sender) to Mac_1 (receiver), the predominant TAM subset (Supplementary Fig. S1D, F, G). Within this communication network, GRN-SORT1 was identified as a notable inferred GRN-related ligand-receptor pair (Supplementary Fig. S1H). At the expression level, SORT1 was readily detectable in Mac_1 (Supplementary Fig. S1I). We further examined selected GRN-related receptors across macrophage subsets. SORT1 and tumor necrosis factor receptor superfamily members 1 A and 1B (TNFRSF1A and TNFRSF1B) were detectable, whereas epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) and ephrin type-A receptor 2 (EPHA2) showed relatively low expression (Supplementary Fig. S1J). These data supported the prioritization of SORT1 as a major candidate receptor in this context, while possible contributions from other PGRN-responsive receptors could not be excluded. Gene set variation analysis (GSVA) indicated enrichment of cholesterol metabolism and PPAR signaling pathways in Mac_1 (Supplementary Fig. S1K). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis further identified lipid- and lipoprotein-related terms, including regulation of lipoprotein lipase activity and chylomicron remodeling (Supplementary Fig. S1L). Together, these findings suggested a potential link between GRN-SORT1–related epithelial–macrophage signaling and lipid–processing programs in macrophages.

To corroborate these findings in independent datasets, we analyzed multiple HNSCC scRNA-seq datasets from GEO. Unsupervised clustering identified eight epithelial cell subpopulations (Epi_1-Epi_8; Fig. 1A) and five macrophage subsets (Mac_1-Mac_5; Fig. 1D). Ro/e analysis showed relative enrichment of Epi_1 in malignant epithelial cells, with Epi_1 comprising 33.4% of epithelial cells in HNSCC tissues (Fig. 1B, C). Among macrophage subsets, Mac_4 showed relative enrichment in HNSCC tissues by Ro/e analysis and was more abundant in HNSCC than in NAT samples (17.6% vs. 6.6%, p < 0.001; Fig. 1E, F). We next examined GRN signaling between Epi_1 and Mac_4 and found that Epi_1 showed the highest GRN expression among epithelial subsets (Fig. 1G). CellChat analysis identified a prominent inferred GRN signaling network from Epi_1 (ligand sender) to Mac_4 (receiver) (Fig. 1H, I). Within the GRN signaling contribution analysis, GRN-SORT1 was the only GRN-related ligand–receptor pair contributing to this Epi_1–Mac_4 communication (Fig. 1J). Additionally, Mac_4 expressed the highest SORT1 level among macrophage subsets (Fig. 1K). These observations supported the prioritization of SORT1 for downstream functional validation, although contributions from alternative receptors could not be excluded [16].

PGRN expression is associated with increased CD68⁺ macrophage infiltration and poor prognosis in OSCC

To examine the relationship between PGRN expression and macrophage infiltration, as well as the clinical significance of elevated PGRN levels, we performed IHC analysis on 40 OSCC patient samples. PGRN staining was markedly increased in tumor tissues compared with adjacent normal tissues (Fig. 2A). Consistently, PGRN protein levels were higher in CAL27 cells than in HIOEC cells, as shown by Western blotting (Fig. 2B) and ELISA (Fig. 2C). Clinically, high PGRN expression correlated significantly with lymph node metastasis (p = 0.037), surrounding tissue invasion (p = 0.034), and reduced overall survival (p = 0.025; log-rank test, Table 1). For external validation, we analyzed GRN expression in the GEPIA2 HNSCC cohort, which indicated that high GRN mRNA levels were associated with poorer overall survival (p = 0.047; Fig. 2D) and reduced disease-free survival (p = 0.015; Fig. 2E) in HNSCC. IHC staining (Fig. 2F) revealed higher CD68+ macrophage infiltration in the PGRN-high group than in the PGRN-low group (Fig. 2G, p = 0.0396). Together, these findings suggest that elevated PGRN expression is associated with increased macrophage infiltration and poorer clinical outcomes in OSCC.

OSCC-derived PGRN promotes macrophage cholesterol efflux via SORT1

TAMs undergo marked alterations in cholesterol metabolism, and enhanced cholesterol efflux has been linked to IL-4-mediated macrophage reprogramming and immunosuppressive features [9]. Consistent with this, pathway enrichment analysis of HNSCC single-cell data revealed enrichment of cholesterol metabolism and PPAR signaling pathways in the Mac_4 subset (Fig. 1L), suggesting that tumor-derived signals may influence macrophage cholesterol export. Given the prominent inferred GRN-SORT1 signaling identified between malignant epithelial cells and TAM subsets (Fig. 1H-K), we investigated whether OSCC-derived PGRN affects macrophage cholesterol efflux.

We first generated GRN-knockdown CAL27 cells (shPGRN-CAL27), with efficient silencing confirmed by qRT-PCR, Western blotting, and ELISA (Fig. 3A-C). Conditioned media were collected from HIOEC, CAL27, shNC-CAL27, and shPGRN-CAL27 cells for functional assays. THP-1 cells cultured with CAL27-CM exhibited significantly decreased intracellular cholesterol and increased extracellular cholesterol compared with HIOEC-CM, whereas shPGRN-CAL27-CM reversed these changes relative to shNC-CAL27-CM (Fig. 3D). Similar trends were observed in RAW264.7 cells (Fig. 3E). Filipin III staining further confirmed intracellular cholesterol depletion in CAL27-CM-treated RAW264.7 cells and increased cholesterol retention in the shPGRN-CAL27-CM group (Fig. 3I).

To further assess whether PGRN supplementation could restore cholesterol efflux in THP-1 cells, recombinant human PGRN (rhPGRN) was added to shPGRN-CAL27-CM. rhPGRN partially restored cholesterol efflux under shPGRN-CAL27-CM conditions, with reduced intracellular cholesterol across 50–400 ng/mL and increased extracellular cholesterol at 200 and 400 ng/mL (Fig. 3F).

We next evaluated the involvement of SORT1 in this process using an inhibitor targeting the PGRN-SORT1 interaction (HY-115213). Pretreatment with this inhibitor (2 µM, 2 h) markedly reduced CAL27-CM-induced cholesterol efflux in both THP-1 and RAW264.7 cells and led to intracellular cholesterol accumulation, as confirmed by biochemical assays and Filipin III staining (Fig. 3G-I). Furthermore, rhPGRN-induced cholesterol efflux (200 ng/mL) was largely blocked by SORT1 inhibition (Fig. 3J). These results support tumor-derived PGRN as a regulator of macrophage cholesterol efflux and indicate an important role for SORT1 in this metabolic response. ICC analysis further showed membrane-proximal co-localization of PGRN and SORT1 in RAW264.7 cells upon rmPGRN stimulation, providing additional support for the predicted interaction (Supplementary Fig. S2).

OSCC-derived PGRN promotes macrophage cholesterol efflux through a SORT1-linked PPARγ-LXRα-ABCA1/ABCG1 pathway

Cholesterol efflux in macrophages is primarily governed by the transporters ABCA1 (ATP-binding cassette transporter A1, encoded by ABCA1) and ABCG1 (ATP-binding cassette transporter G1, encoded by ABCG1), with scavenger receptor class B type I (SR-B1, encoded by SCARB1) playing a comparatively limited role [27]. These transporters are transcriptionally regulated by the nuclear receptors liver X receptor alpha (LXRα, encoded by NR1H3) and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ, encoded by PPARG), which cooperatively drive cholesterol export [28–31]. To determine whether OSCC-derived PGRN engages this pathway, we exposed THP-1 cells to CAL27-CM or HIOEC-CM. CAL27-CM markedly upregulated PPARγ, LXRα, ABCA1, and ABCG1, whereas SR-B1 remained unchanged (Fig. 4A, B). This induction was at least partly dependent on tumor-derived PGRN, because shPGRN-CAL27-CM markedly reduced the expression of these regulators compared with shNC-CAL27-CM (Fig. 4C, D), whereas their expression did not differ significantly between the shNC-CAL27-CM and CAL27-CM groups (Supplementary Fig. S4).

Blocking the PGRN-SORT1 interaction attenuated CAL27-CM-induced activation of the PPARγ-LXRα-ABCA1/ABCG1 pathway at the protein level and reduced the mRNA expression of several pathway components, indicating that SORT1 contributes to this response under the present experimental conditions (Fig. 4E, F). Tumor-derived PGRN modulates macrophage production of immunosuppressive mediators

Macrophages exert immunoregulatory functions through cytokines such as IL-6, IL-10, and TGF-β [32, 33]. To determine whether OSCC-derived PGRN modulates macrophage cytokine production, we first compared RAW264.7 and THP-1 cells cultured with CAL27-CM versus HIOEC-CM. CAL27-CM markedly increased the mRNA and secreted protein levels of IL-6, IL-10, and TGF-β in both RAW264.7 and THP-1 cells (Fig. 5A, C, D, F). This induction was at least partly dependent on tumor-derived PGRN, as shPGRN-CAL27-CM substantially reduced the expression and secretion of these cytokines relative to shNC-CAL27-CM (Fig. 5B, C, E, F).

Blockade of the PGRN-SORT1 interaction produced cell-context-dependent effects. In RAW264.7 cells, SORT1 inhibition markedly reduced CAL27-CM-induced IL-6, IL-10, and TGF-β production at both the transcriptional and protein levels (Fig. 5A, C), supporting SORT1 involvement in this response. In contrast, THP-1 cells displayed stable IL-6 and TGF-β expression following SORT1 inhibition, whereas IL-10 secretion was reduced despite minimal changes in mRNA (Fig. 5D, F), suggesting that post-transcriptional or secretion-related regulatory mechanisms may contribute in this model. Furthermore, activation of PPARγ with rosiglitazone (RSG) partially restored the suppressed cytokine expression and secretion observed in PGRN-deficient conditions. PGRN knockdown alters macrophage polarization-related features in tumor xenografts

To further examine the in vivo relevance of PGRN-associated macrophage changes, we established xenograft models using three groups of nude mice: shNC-CAL27, shPGRN-CAL27, and shPGRN-CAL27 + RSG. Beginning on day 10 after tumor implantation, mice in the shPGRN-CAL27 + RSG group received intraperitoneal injections of RSG (10 mg/kg in 0.5% CMC-Na) every 48 h, while the remaining groups were administered vehicle (0.5% CMC-Na). Mice were euthanized on day 24.

Although tumor weights did not show statistically significant intergroup differences (one-way ANOVA), shPGRN-CAL27 tumors exhibited a modest trend toward reduced mass compared with shNC-CAL27 tumors, whereas no significant effect of RSG was observed (Fig. 6B).

Discussion

Dysregulated cholesterol metabolism in TAMs is increasingly recognized as an important regulator of immune function within the tumor microenvironment, influencing antigen presentation, cytokine production, and macrophage polarization [8, 9, 11–13, 34]. Previous studies have linked macrophage cholesterol efflux to changes in macrophage polarization and tumor-promoting immune states [9, 13]. For example, Goossens et al. showed that ovarian cancer cells promote membrane cholesterol efflux in macrophages, thereby enhancing IL-4-mediated M2-like reprogramming while suppressing IFNγ responses [9]. However, the upstream tumor-derived signals that connect malignant cells to macrophage cholesterol metabolism remain incompletely defined. By integrating single-cell transcriptomic analyses with functional experiments, we identified tumor-derived PGRN as a candidate regulator of macrophage cholesterol efflux in OSCC. Further functional analyses suggest that PGRN-associated cholesterol efflux involves a SORT1-linked PPARγ-LXRα-ABCA1/ABCG1 pathway and is accompanied by immunosuppressive macrophage-associated features. These findings extend the concept of tumor-driven macrophage cholesterol remodeling to OSCC and highlight PGRN as a tumor-derived signal linked to macrophage cholesterol efflux.

The receptor context of PGRN is important for interpreting the SORT1-related findings in this study. PGRN is known to interact with multiple membrane-associated receptors and binding partners, including SORT1, prosaposin, the tumor necrosis factor receptors TNFR1 and TNFR2, and EphA2 [16]. SORT1 has established roles in lipid-related trafficking and is expressed in several cell types involved in lipid metabolism, including hepatocytes and macrophages [35]. In the datasets analyzed in this study, GRN-SORT1 emerged as a prominent inferred epithelial-macrophage interaction candidate. TNFRSF1A and TNFRSF1B were also detectable in macrophage subsets, whereas EGFR and EPHA2 showed relatively low expression. Our subsequent functional experiments therefore focused on SORT1, although the contribution of other potential PGRN-responsive receptors in macrophages was not systematically evaluated. Thus, our findings support SORT1 as a major receptor candidate involved in PGRN-associated regulation of macrophage cholesterol efflux and related phenotypic features in this context.

Additional support for the relevance of SORT1 to PGRN biology comes from studies in other tumor contexts, although prior work has mainly focused on tumor cell-intrinsic functions rather than immune regulation. In breast cancer, PGRN-SORT1-related signaling has been associated with tumor progression and cancer stem-like properties, whereas in prostate and other genitourinary cancers, SORT1 has been implicated in PGRN trafficking and lysosomal degradation [36–39]. These studies suggest that the role of PGRN-SORT1 interactions may differ by tumor type. Our findings extend this line of evidence by suggesting that tumor-derived PGRN-SORT1 signaling is linked to macrophage cholesterol metabolism and associated phenotypic features in OSCC. However, whether the macrophage-associated PGRN-SORT1-cholesterol efflux program described here is conserved across other squamous cell carcinomas remains unclear and will require dedicated comparative studies.

Mechanistically, the cholesterol-efflux changes observed in our system were consistent with established regulators of macrophage cholesterol export. ABCA1, ABCG1, and SR-B1 have been identified as key cholesterol transport-related proteins [27, 28], while LXRα and PPARγ regulate macrophage cholesterol efflux in part through ABCA1 and ABCG1 [29–31]. In our co-culture system, conditioned media derived from shPGRN-CAL27 cells reduced the mRNA and protein expression of PPARγ, LXRα, ABCA1, and ABCG1, and impaired cholesterol efflux in macrophages. Activation of PPARγ with rosiglitazone largely restored the expression of these molecules and rescued cholesterol efflux. These findings support the involvement of the PGRN-SORT1-linked PPARγ-LXRα-ABCA1/ABCG1 pathway. Although the rosiglitazone rescue experiments support PPARγ as a downstream node in this pathway, PPARγ antagonism was not directly tested and should be considered a future hypothesis rather than an established therapeutic strategy in OSCC.

The functional relevance of this cholesterol-efflux program is supported by previous studies linking macrophage cholesterol efflux to phenotypic regulation, including effects on membrane microdomains, cytokine signaling, and antigen presentation [9, 14, 40]. Although cholesterol metabolism and PPAR signaling were prioritized based on our single-cell analysis and functional assays, other metabolic programs, including glycolysis, fatty acid oxidation, and mitochondrial respiration, may also contribute to macrophage phenotypic regulation [10, 34]. In our study, PGRN-SORT1-associated upregulation of PPARγ, LXRα, ABCA1, and ABCG1 was accompanied by enhanced macrophage cholesterol efflux and increased production of IL-6, IL-10, and TGF-β. Rosiglitazone restored selected cytokine changes in PGRN-deficient conditions, and in vivo PGRN knockdown was associated with macrophage polarization-related changes that were partially restored by rosiglitazone. Together, these findings support an association between PGRN-SORT1-linked cholesterol efflux and immunosuppressive macrophage-associated features in OSCC, although the downstream signaling events connecting cholesterol depletion to IL-10 and TGF-β regulation remain to be defined.

For clinical translation, the association among PGRN expression, macrophage infiltration, and patient outcomes should first be validated in larger independent OSCC cohorts. Direct clinical therapies designed to inhibit the PGRN-SORT1 axis in OSCC have not been established. SORT1-related therapeutic strategies have been explored in other contexts, including anti-SORT1 antibody approaches aimed at increasing PGRN bioavailability in frontotemporal dementia and SORT1-targeted peptide-drug conjugates designed to deliver cytotoxic agents to SORT1-positive tumor cells [41–43]. These studies suggest that SORT1 has translational relevance in selected disease settings, but they differ from selective disruption of tumor- or macrophage-associated PGRN-SORT1 signaling. Thus, before this pathway can be considered for therapeutic development in OSCC, its tissue selectivity, systemic metabolic consequences, and context-dependent effects on PGRN bioavailability will need to be carefully defined. This is particularly relevant because SORT1 has physiological roles in lipid handling and PGRN trafficking, and systemic blockade may affect lipoprotein metabolism and macrophage lipid homeostasis beyond the tumor microenvironment [35, 38, 44].

Beyond these translational considerations, the interpretation of these findings should also take into account several unresolved biological questions and model-related issues. While the present study focused on the downstream effects of tumor-derived PGRN, the signals that regulate PGRN expression or secretion in OSCC cells remain to be defined. Microenvironmental stressors such as hypoxia, nutrient stress, or inflammatory cues may contribute to this regulation. The modest divergence in cytokine responses between THP-1 and RAW264.7 cells suggests that downstream effects of the PGRN-SORT1 axis may vary with species background, differentiation state, and cell-line-specific regulatory programs [45–47]. In particular, the discordance between IL-10 mRNA expression and secreted protein in THP-1 cells following SORT1 inhibition suggests that post-transcriptional or secretion-related regulation may contribute in this model. As THP-1 cells were used in an undifferentiated, non-adherent state, they should be interpreted as a complementary in vitro model rather than a fully representative model of primary human TAMs [45, 46]. Similarly, the BALB/c nude mouse xenograft model allowed us to evaluate tumor growth and macrophage-related changes, although it was not suited for evaluating adaptive T-cell responses, including CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration or activation. Validation using primary human macrophages and immunocompetent or humanized models would help clarify the broader tumor-immune relevance of the PGRN-SORT1-associated macrophage changes described here.

Conclusions

In summary, our study supports a model in which tumor-derived PGRN promotes a SORT1-linked macrophage cholesterol-efflux program involving PPARγ-LXRα-ABCA1/ABCG1 signaling, accompanied by immunosuppressive macrophage-associated features in OSCC. These findings provide a mechanistic framework for understanding how tumor-derived signals regulate macrophage cholesterol efflux and related phenotypic changes in the OSCC microenvironment. Further validation in physiologically relevant models, together with careful evaluation of receptor selectivity and systemic safety, will help define the translational potential of this pathway for therapeutic exploration in OSCC.
