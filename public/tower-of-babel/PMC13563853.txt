Integrating nutrition as a transversal competence: a curriculum mapping and implementation study in undergraduate medical education
Authors: Sonja Lackner, Regina Roller-Wirnsberger, Sabrina Mörkl, Attila Várnagy, Martina Zöbl, Anna Taberhofer, Diana Zabini, Helmut Lackner, Julia Traub, Simone Manhal, Erwin Petek, Sandra Holasek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNutrition is widely recognised as essential to clinical practice, yet its integration into undergraduate medical curricula remains inconsistent and fragmented. While previous work has primarily focused on identifying gaps or evaluating isolated interventions, less attention has been paid to how nutrition can be systematically integrated within existing competency-based curricula.MethodsWe conducted a curriculum mapping study to identify nutrition-related learning objectives across an undergraduate medical programme, complemented by stakeholder engagement across disciplines, and a student needs assessment. Based on these findings, an integrated curriculum framework was developed to align nutrition as a transversal competence across disciplines and study phases.ResultsOf 3,423 preclinical and clinical learning objectives, 574 (16.8%) were identified as nutrition-related. These were widely distributed across the curriculum but were not previously conceptualised as a coherent domain of competence. The mapping process enabled the identification of implicit content and informed the development of a structured framework comprising three domains: fundamentals of nutrition, applied nutrition and public health, and clinical nutrition. Integration was achieved through alignment of existing curricular elements rather than the addition of new content. Student survey data indicated moderate general nutrition knowledge but low confidence in applying nutrition in clinical contexts, with around 79.1% of respondents rating their clinical nutrition knowledge as inadequate.ConclusionThis study demonstrates how curriculum mapping, combined with stakeholder engagement, can support the integration of nutrition as a transversal competence within undergraduate medical education. The findings indicate that nutrition is already present as an implicit transversal theme within the curriculum but lacks visibility and structural coherence. Beyond curriculum description, this study provides theory-informed insights into structural, organizational, and learner-level educational outcomes, including curriculum integration, institutional embedding, and identified competence gaps among medical students. It further offers a transferable, system-level approach for integrating cross-cutting competencies within existing medical curricula.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-026-09785-5.

Background

Nutrition is a major determinant of global morbidity and mortality, yet its integration into undergraduate medical education remains inconsistent and fragmented [1, 2]. Although physicians are expected to address diet-related diseases in routine clinical practice, many report insufficient competence and confidence in providing nutrition care [3, 4]. International reviews show that nutrition teaching is frequently limited to isolated biochemical content rather than embedded within clinical reasoning and patient counselling across the curriculum [1, 2, 5]. Importantly, nutrition competence in medicine extends beyond knowledge acquisition and includes recognising when referral to appropriately qualified nutrition professionals, such as registered dietitians, is indicated, thereby supporting interprofessional and patient-centred care [1].

Contemporary curriculum theory emphasises longitudinal, competency-based and integrated approaches that connect foundational sciences with clinical practice across disciplines and training phases [6–8]. Within competency-based medical education (CBME), transversal competencies require structured horizontal and vertical alignment rather than stand-alone teaching formats [7, 8]. However, nutrition education is frequently implemented in formats that remain weakly integrated into core curricula and lack sustainable institutional embedding [1, 2, 9, 10]. Furthermore, curriculum analyses suggest that reform efforts often occur within already crowded programmes, complicating sustainable embedding of cross-cutting domains [5, 11, 12].

To address this gap, the European Society for Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism (ESPEN) developed the Nutrition Education in Medical Schools (NEMS) framework, defining core domains for undergraduate medical training [13]. Comparable national and international initiatives similarly advocate curriculum-wide integration rather than isolated courses [14–16]. Yet, little is known about how such frameworks can be operationalised within existing CBME at an institutional level.

Guided by the ESPEN NEMS framework and principles of curriculum integration, we conducted a structured, multi-phase implementation study at a public medical university. This study examines how transversal nutrition competencies can be systematically embedded within an established CBME and analyses contextual enablers and barriers relevant to sustainable integration.

This study addresses the following research questions: (1) How can transversal nutrition competencies be embedded within an existing CBME curriculum without curricular expansion? (2) Which institutional conditions enable or constrain sustainable integration?

Methods

Study design and conceptual framework

We conducted a theory-informed mixed-methods implementation study [17] examining the embedding of transversal nutrition competencies within an existing CBME-curriculum. The study was guided by curriculum integration theory [6–8].

Three complementary data sources were triangulated: (1) systematic curriculum mapping of learning objectives, (2) structured stakeholder engagement with discipline leads and curriculum governance bodies, and (3) a student survey assessing perceived and factual nutrition competence. Methodological triangulation enabled analysis of structural, organisational and learner-level dimensions of integration [18, 19].

Results were interpreted together to strengthen conclusions and to inform understanding of how transversal competencies can be embedded within existing curricula. The study is reported in accordance with established standards (SQUIRE 2.0; SRQR) [20, 21].

Context and setting

The study was conducted at a public European medical university offering a six-year CBME-undergraduate-programme. The curriculum follows a spiral, modular structure integrating preclinical and clinical learning across study years [7, 22, 23]. Learning objectives are defined through national clinical and institutional preclinical catalogues [24, 25]. In response to international recommendations to strengthen nutrition education, including the ESPEN Nutrition Education in Medical Schools framework [13], the university initiated a structured, institution-wide process to embed nutrition competencies longitudinally across the curriculum.

Participants

Participants comprised members of the curriculum commission, discipline leads from preclinical and clinical modules involved in embedding nutrition-related learning objectives, and medical students who completed an anonymous survey. The curriculum commission working group included experts in nutritional sciences, medicine, dietetics, medical education, curriculum development, and study management. Discipline leads were senior academic staff with responsibility for the content and delivery of their respective disciplines and included professors, department heads, and delegated teaching coordinators from preclinical and clinical departments.

To enhance reflexivity, analytic interpretations were discussed within an interdisciplinary team independent of curricular grading and progression decisions. This process supported critical examination of implementation dynamics.

Data sources and collection procedures

Curriculum mapping

A systematic mapping of preclinical and clinical learning objectives was conducted using the official catalogues [24, 25]. Mapping aimed to make implicit nutrition content visible across horizontally and vertically distributed curricular structures. Two investigators independently screened all objectives to identify nutrition-related content. Relevant objectives were coded and assigned to three domains of the integrated nutrition framework—Fundamentals, Applied/Public Health, and Clinical Nutrition (see Additional file 1).

Discrepancies were resolved through consensus discussion, with a third investigator consulted when necessary. Mapping results were synthesised to inform subsequent stakeholder consultation and curricular alignment.

Stakeholder engagement

Stakeholder engagement occurred on two complementary levels: curriculum governance and disciplinary consultation. The initiative originated from curriculum reform activities initiated during revision of the preclinical learning objectives and was formally advanced through the establishment of a dedicated Nutrition Working Group by the Curriculum Commission in 2024. The working group was responsible for developing the integrated curriculum framework, coordinating stakeholder consultation, and reporting progress to the Curriculum Commission throughout the implementation process.

Following the initial curriculum mapping, discipline-specific mapping tables and a process document outlining the rationale, objectives, and proposed curriculum framework were distributed to heads of preclinical and clinical departments for review. Stakeholders were invited to review, revise, and supplement the proposed mappings within predefined review periods. Written feedback was collected between October and November 2024, with reminder procedures used where necessary to maximise participation. Feedback was incorporated iteratively into subsequent mapping versions.

Stakeholders reviewed the positioning of nutrition-related objectives within existing modules and identified opportunities for integration within current teaching structures. Insights from these consultations informed iterative revision of the mapping and supported institutional alignment of the final integration strategy.

Following completion of the consultation phase, the revised framework was presented and discussed with preclinical and clinical stakeholders prior to formal approval by the Curriculum Commission in January 2025. Stakeholder input was documented through written feedback, revised mapping tables, and official meeting minutes. An overview of the curriculum development and consultation process is provided in Additional File 4.

The process functioned as a mechanism for interdisciplinary negotiation and shared ownership of transversal competencies.

Student survey: needs assessment

To complement the structural and stakeholder analyses, an anonymous online survey was administered to medical students to assess perceived and factual nutrition competence. Participation was voluntary. The survey was conducted as an exploratory needs assessment across three academic semesters during initiation of the integrated nutrition curriculum. Its primary purpose was to provide a learner perspective on perceived educational needs and to inform discussions regarding curriculum development and integration. Medical students were recruited through teaching activities. An institutional mailing list was additionally used to disseminate the survey; however, participation through this route was marginal. No formal sample size calculation was performed. Consequently, findings should be interpreted as indicative rather than representative estimate of nutrition competence across the student population.

The survey instrument was developed for the purpose of this study, drawing on and adapting items for the institutional context from previously validated questionnaires, including the Short Food Literacy Questionnaire (SFLQ) [26], a questionnaire for the assessment of perceived nutrition competence [27], the General Nutrition Knowledge Questionnaire (GNKQ) [28] and an instrument assessing clinical nutrition knowledge [29]. The survey comprised domains addressing (1) self-perceived nutrition knowledge and relevance, (2) general nutrition knowledge, (3) clinical nutrition knowledge, and (4) demographic and dietary characteristics. The questionnaire underwent internal pre-testing by members of the project team to assess functionality, usability, and technical implementation prior to deployment. A total of 184 completed questionnaires were obtained. The median completion time among completed questionnaires was 28 min (IQR 8 min). In addition, 30 surveys were initiated but not completed. Some of completed questionnaires contained missing responses to individual survey items, limiting the calculation of some composite scores. The survey provided a learner-level perspective on existing competence and perceived learning needs. The survey has been approved by the University’s Ethical Committee (EK:33-286ex20/21). The full survey instrument is provided as Supplementary Material (Additional file 3).

Data analysis

Quantitative analysis

Survey data were analysed descriptively (IBM SPSS Statistics v29). Results are presented as frequencies and percentages or as medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs), as appropriate. Where applicable, composite domain scores were calculated. As the survey was conducted as an exploratory needs assessment, no inferential statistical analyses was performed.

Review and synthesis of stakeholder feedback

Curriculum documents, mapping outputs, and stakeholder feedback were reviewed iteratively throughout the implementation process to support refinement and validation of the curriculum mapping. Stakeholder feedback was synthesised and incorporated into subsequent mapping versions through discussion within the project team and consultation with curriculum governance representatives. Mapping decisions were reviewed by two investigators, with discrepancies resolved through consensus procedures as described for the curriculum mapping. This iterative review process supported consistency and contextual validity of the final framework.

Integration and triangulation

Findings from curriculum mapping, stakeholder consultations and the student survey were brought together during interpretation. Results were compared to examine areas of alignment, divergence and complementarity across structural, organisational and learner-level perspectives.

This integrative analysis informed the synthesis of implementation processes and identified patterns relevant to institutional embedding. An overview of the study design and integration process is provided in Fig. 1.

Results

Baseline educational needs: students’ reported nutrition competence and knowledge

A total of 184 questionnaires were completed Most participants were in the clinical phase of training (median semester 7, IQR 3). The median age was 24 years (IQR 3), and approximately 70% of respondents were female. Only five students reported prior educational training in a nutrition-related field.

Self-perceived competence in clinical nutrition was low. Nearly four out of five students rated their clinical nutrition competence as inadequate, and only around 12% reported having received sufficient training. Almost half of respondents (48.5%) rated their food literacy as inadequate or problematic.

Factual knowledge scores indicated moderate performance overall. While median scores were comparatively high in general nutrition domains, clinical nutrition items revealed greater uncertainty and lower performance, particularly regarding nutritional support and disease-related applications. These findings indicated a discrepancy between perceived importance of nutrition and structured clinical competence. Of note, results of the student survey are not reported comprehensively here.

Development of the integrated nutrition curriculum framework

An institutional curriculum framework was developed to structure transversal nutrition competencies across the undergraduate programme. The framework comprised three domains (Fundamentals of Nutrition, Applied Nutrition and Public Health, and Clinical Nutrition) reflecting both international recommendations and national curricular requirements.

To ensure contextual alignment, the ESPEN domains were adapted to local learning objectives and supplemented with site-specific thematic priorities. This process resulted in a comprehensive framework that systematically organises nutrition-related content across foundational, population-based, and disease-specific domains (see Additional file 2).

The framework provided a reference structure for subsequent screening and mapping of existing learning objectives and enabled systematic identification of nutrition-related content across disciplines and study years.

Systematic screening and mapping of learning objectives

A total of 3,423 learning objectives (LOs) across preclinical and clinical disciplines were screened for nutrition relevance. Of these, 574 were identified as nutrition-related.

Nutrition-relevant LOs were identifiable across nearly all disciplines (49 of 51), indicating that existing curricular structures offered implicit entry points for transversal integration. However, these objectives were not explicitly framed as a coherent nutrition domain. The presence of nutrition-related LOs across multiple disciplines highlights the interdisciplinary embedding of nutrition within the existing curriculum structure. Nutrition-related LOs were distributed across both preclinical and clinical phases of the curriculum, indicating longitudinal integration of nutrition competencies across the undergraduate programme.

As individual LOs could align with multiple thematic areas, mapping resulted in 843 allocations across the framework (see Additional file 2). This process enabled systematic identification of horizontal and vertical connections within the curriculum.

Overall, this distribution illustrates the transversal character of nutrition within the curriculum, spanning biomedical foundations, public health perspectives and clinical care across multiple disciplines and study phases.

Mapping decisions were subsequently reviewed with discipline representatives to ensure contextual validity and curricular feasibility.

Institutional anchoring and implementation

Stakeholder consultation revealed broad engagement across disciplines. All preclinical disciplines (10/10) and a majority of clinical disciplines (24/39) provided formal feedback during the consultation phase. Mapping outcomes were iteratively refined in collaboration with discipline representatives to ensure curricular feasibility within existing teaching structures.

The process highlighted the importance of institutional negotiation in embedding transversal competencies across disciplinary boundaries. Rather than introducing stand-alone courses, integration focused on repositioning nutrition-relevant objectives within established modules.

Following institutional approval, the framework was formally adopted within the undergraduate curriculum. To support sustained coordination and interdisciplinary alignment, a dedicated Teaching Unit for Nutritional Medicine (“nutriMEDucation”) was established. The unit provides ongoing oversight, facilitates curricular updates, and supports cross-disciplinary exchange.

Alignment with ESPEN-recommended implementation factors

The institutional implementation process was examined in relation to key factors proposed in the ESPEN NEMS framework. Several elements of the integration process corresponded to ESPEN’s recommendations, including institutional endorsement, cross-disciplinary engagement, and formal curricular anchoring.

Integration occurred through structured embedding within existing modules and governance mechanisms. This alignment illustrates how international framework recommendations can be operationalised within an established CBME-curriculum.

Discussion

Principal findings

This study examined how nutrition is represented within an undergraduate medical curriculum through systematic screening and mapping of learning objectives, complemented by stakeholder engagement and a student needs assessment. The analysis identified a substantial number of nutrition-related learning objectives distributed across disciplines and study phases, which had not previously been conceptualised as a coherent domain of competence. Mapping these objectives to an integrated framework revealed extensive horizontal and longitudinal connections across the curriculum.

Findings from the student needs assessment indicated limited self-perceived and objective nutrition competencies, particularly in clinically relevant domains. A discrepancy between factual knowledge and perceived clinical competence was evident, especially in areas such as malnutrition recognition and nutrition-related decision-making. This pattern aligns with previous studies demonstrating a gap between knowledge acquisition and its application in clinical practice [3, 4, 9, 30–33].

Taken together, the findings suggest that nutrition is already present as a transversal theme within the curriculum, but that its educational coherence depends on explicit curricular structuring. Strengthening nutrition education may therefore be less a question of adding content than of improving alignment and visibility of existing learning objectives. This study provides a practical illustration of how widely reported gaps in nutrition education can be addressed within existing curricular structures [1, 4].

Conceptualising nutrition as a transversal competence within integrated curricula

From a medical education perspective, the findings support understanding nutrition as a transversal competence distributed across disciplinary and temporal boundaries. Although nutrition-related content was embedded within biomedical, public health and clinical teaching, it remained largely invisible as a coherent construct. This reflects a broader challenge in CBME, where cross-cutting competencies often exist in dispersed form unless made explicit through deliberate curricular design [6–8, 34–36].

Interpreted through the lens of curriculum integration theory, nutrition is not absent but positioned across multiple levels of integration. Integration frameworks emphasise that coherent competence development depends on alignment of learning objectives across disciplines and stages of training [7]. When viewed through Harden’s integration ladder, nutrition-related content reflects lower to intermediate levels of integration, where disciplinary contributions remain fragmented despite thematic relevance [7].

Here, curriculum mapping functioned as a conceptual tool that made implicit connections visible and enabled the articulation of nutrition as a longitudinal, integrated competence. The contribution of this work therefore lies not in introducing a new curricular domain, but in aligning previously dispersed learning objectives within a coherent framework. This perspective aligns with broader goals of CBME and supports the integration of nutritional reasoning into routine clinical decision-making and professional identity formation [37–39].

Mechanisms of implementation: mapping, negotiation and curricular governance

Beyond identifying existing learning objectives, the implementation process highlighted how curriculum integration emerges through iterative negotiation within established curricular governance structures [40, 41]. Systematic screening and mapping created a shared evidence base that enabled interdisciplinary dialogue and supported validation of mapping decisions.

In this context, curriculum mapping functioned as a governance tool that facilitated collaboration with discipline representatives and enabled integration without requiring additional teaching hours. By making nutrition-related content explicit within existing structures, the process supported coordinated curriculum development rather than additive expansion [42–44].

Implementation was further strengthened by institutional embedding within governance and organisational structures. Formal curricular anchoring enabled alignment with ongoing revision processes and provided legitimacy for cross-disciplinary coordination. The establishment of a dedicated Teaching Unit (“nutriMEDucation”) supported continuity and coordination, functioning as an enabling structure rather than a separate teaching entity [37, 45, 46].

Together, these elements demonstrate how integration of cross-cutting competencies can be achieved through structural alignment and institutional embedding within complex educational environments [37].

Relation to existing literature and international frameworks

Previous research has consistently shown that nutrition education in undergraduate medical curricula is fragmented and insufficiently integrated into clinical contexts [1, 2, 9, 10, 12, 30, 35], with learners reporting limited confidence in applying nutrition in practice [3, 4, 30, 31, 33, 47]. While this literature has been essential in identifying gaps, it has provided limited insight into how integration can be operationalised within CBME-curricula.

This study extends existing work by demonstrating how nutrition can be organised as a transversal competence within an existing curriculum. By aligning dispersed learning objectives within a longitudinal framework, the findings shift the focus from identifying deficits towards enabling structural integration.

This approach aligns with international initiatives such as the ESPEN Nutrition Education in Medical Schools (NEMS) framework, which emphasises longitudinal integration, clinical relevance and institutional anchoring of competencies [13]. By linking such frameworks with local curricular structures and governance processes, the study illustrates how cross-cutting competencies can be embedded in a sustainable and institution-wide manner.

Implications and transferability for curriculum development

The findings suggest that strengthening cross-cutting competencies in medical education depends on the explicit structuring and coordination of existing curricular elements rather than on curricular expansion. Curriculum integration emerges not only as a design task but as an organisational process requiring engagement with governance structures and collaboration across disciplines. An important component of such collaboration is the involvement of appropriately qualified nutrition professionals, including registered dietitians and nutrition scientists, in both curriculum development and educational delivery. Beyond supporting nutrition competence among future physicians, medical curricula should also foster understanding of professional roles and referral pathways within interdisciplinary nutrition care [1, 13].

Although conducted within a specific institutional context, the study identifies key elements that may inform similar efforts in other medical schools. In particular, embedding cross-cutting competencies by building on existing structures may be especially relevant for competency-based, learning-objective-driven programmes. At the same time, transferability depends on contextual factors such as national frameworks, institutional governance and available resources, and the approach should therefore be understood as adaptable rather than prescriptive.

Taken together, these findings highlight the importance of a system-level perspective on curriculum development, in which learning objectives, teaching practices and organisational structures are aligned to support integrated and sustainable medical education [37, 42, 48].

Strengths and limitations

This study combines systematic analysis of curricular documents with stakeholder engagement and a student needs assessment, allowing triangulation of multiple data sources. The inclusion of both preclinical and clinical phases provides a comprehensive view of how nutrition-related competencies are represented across the curriculum. The use of an integrated framework further enabled structured analysis of coherence and alignment.

Several limitations should be considered. The study reflects the context of a single medical university and may not be directly transferable to institutions with different structures or governance models. The analysis relied on documented learning objectives, which may not fully capture how content is enacted in practice. Although mapping decisions were reviewed with discipline representatives, they involved a degree of researcher interpretation, and not all clinical disciplines provided formal feedback during initial implementation.

The student survey was based on voluntary participation and primarily recruited students through lectures. As participation was not mandatory and the survey achieved a relatively low response rate; findings should therefore be interpreted as indicative rather than representative of the entire medical student population. Recruitment through voluntary educational activities may also have introduced selection bias, as participating students may have had a greater interest in nutrition than non-participants. Although most participants who initiated the survey completed it, the relatively extensive survey instrument may have influenced participation and contributed to non-response. Future evaluations of curriculum implementation may benefit from shorter, more focused instruments targeting specific aspects of nutrition competence in order to reduce participant burden and facilitate broader participation. Self-reported competence may not fully reflect clinical performance. Finally, curriculum mapping captures structural coverage but not the depth or quality of teaching, and this study focused on implementation rather than educational outcomes. Further research is needed to examine how integration translates into learning outcomes and clinical practice.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that nutrition is already embedded across undergraduate medical curricula as an implicit transversal competence, but remains insufficiently visible and coordinated. By systematically aligning existing learning objectives within a coherent framework, nutrition can be integrated as a longitudinal and cross-cutting domain without requiring additional curricular content.

The findings highlight that the integration of transversal competencies depends not only on curriculum design but also on institutional processes, including governance structures, stakeholder engagement and organisational alignment.

More broadly, the study illustrates how curriculum integration can be achieved through the explicit structuring of existing curricular elements. This approach may offer a transferable strategy for embedding cross-cutting competencies within complex, CBME-programmes.
