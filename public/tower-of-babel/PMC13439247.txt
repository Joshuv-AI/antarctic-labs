Metabolites stratify future major depressive disorder risk in obese population: a longitudinal machine learning analysis
Authors: Xiaobing Zhai, Abao Xing, Yaoqi Deng, Chi Kin Lam, Lihuan Wang, Hui Yu, Henry H.Y. Tong, Nuno Lourenço, Li Zhao, Kefeng Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundObesity is a well-established risk factor for major depressive disorder (MDD), yet the risk is not uniform, highlighting the need for precise risk stratification. This study aimed to develop a metabolomics-based prediction model to identify high-risk metabolic phenotypes among obese participants and to elucidate the causal metabolic pathways involved.MethodsForty-one-thousand-four-hundred-fifty-nine obese participants were followed for a median of 14.4 years. We integrated multiple machine learning (ML) algorithms to develop predictive models for 3-, 5-, and 9-year MDD risk, with a temporal validation within the same biobank. Furthermore, we investigated the potential causal relationships within the obesity-metabolite-MDD using mediation Mendelian randomization (MR).ResultsDuring follow-up, 3,642 incident MDD cases were documented. The optimized LightGBM model demonstrated superior predictive performance, achieving AUCs of 0.844 (95% CI: 0.773–0.914), 0.824 (95% CI: 0.771–0.875), and 0.834 (95% CI: 0.796–0.871) for 3-, 5-, and 9-year intervals, respectively, significantly outperforming existing clinical models. Temporal validation confirmed the model’s robustness (AUCs: 0.738–0.776). MR analysis confirmed that key metabolites causally mediate the pathway from obesity to MDD (mediation proportions: −11.5% and −35.3%, all P ME < 0.05).ConclusionsThese findings challenge the notion of a uniform obesity–MDD association, demonstrating that metabolomic signatures can effectively stratify MDD risk. We present a validated ML framework for the early identification of high-risk individuals with obesity, offering a precision medicine approach to guide targeted metabolic and psychiatric interventions.

Introduction

Obesity and major depressive disorder (MDD) represent two of the most pressing public health challenges worldwide, with profound socioeconomic consequences. Globally, 890 million people worldwide were living with obesity, representing ~16% of the global population (World Health Organization, 2024), with rates exceeding 40% in countries like the United States and several Pacific Island nations (GBD 2021 Adult BMI Collaborators, 2025). Concurrently, MDD afflicts ~5% of the world’s population annually, with a lifetime prevalence of 11–15% across different regions (Bromet et al., 2011). As leading drivers of global morbidity and mortality, high body mass index (BMI) and MDD collectively account for ~160 million DALYs worldwide, alongside an immense economic burden (GBD 2019 Mental Disorders Collaborators, 2022; Xie et al., 2025). Epidemiological evidence consistently demonstrates that obesity significantly increases MDD risk, with meta-analyses indicating obese individuals face 1.2–1.6 times higher odds of developing MDD compared to normal-weight counterparts (Luppino et al., 2010; Mannan, Mamun, Doi, & Clavarino, 2016; Pereira-Miranda et al., 2017). When these conditions co-occur, patients experience worse health outcomes, treatment resistance to both conditions, substantially reduced quality of life, and an increased risk of premature mortality, with psychological distress explaining up to 10% of this obesity-related mortality risk (Putra et al., 2023). This detrimental synergy creates an urgent need for early identification of at-risk obese individuals, as timely intervention could significantly reduce the compounded burden on global healthcare systems and improve patient outcomes.

Despite growing recognition of the obesity-MDD link, current predictive approaches for MDD remain inadequate for obese populations. Existing prediction models primarily rely on psychological factors, sociodemographic variables, and general biomarkers developed for the broader population (Ma et al., 2024; Meng, Speier, Ong, & Arnold, 2021; Radford-Smith et al., 2023). Recent efforts have incorporated genetic-risk scores, inflammatory markers (C-reactive protein and apolipoproteins), and basic metabolic indicators, achieving moderate predictive performance (AUCs: 0.66–0.72) in general populations (Radford-Smith et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2017). However, these models overlook the unique metabolic landscape of obesity, which fundamentally alters the biological pathways potentially leading to MDD. Most studies have assumed a uniform obesity–MDD association, failing to account for the considerable metabolic heterogeneity among obese individuals. For example, metabolically healthy obese persons may differ significantly from those with dysregulated profiles (Jokela et al., 2014), rendering general models less applicable. Moreover, previous studies are often limited by small sample sizes, short follow-up periods, and a lack of validation in independent cohorts, while the absence of causal inference frameworks further hinders mechanistic insights, underscoring the need for obesity-specific predictive tools.

Investigating metabolic stratification in obesity is crucial for improving the prediction of MDD risk, due to the complex interplay between these conditions. The complex relationship between obesity and MDD extends beyond simple weight metrics, with bidirectional interactions through multiple physiological pathways. Chronic inflammation, hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis dysregulation, insulin resistance, and altered gut microbiota have all been implicated in this relationship (Milaneschi, Simmons, van Rossum, & Penninx, 2019). Crucially, these mechanisms vary substantially among obese individuals, challenging the outdated view of obesity as a metabolically homogeneous condition. Recent research has identified distinct metabolic phenotypes within obesity, ranging from metabolically healthy to severely dysregulated profiles – that may differentially influence MDD vulnerability (Brandão, Martins, & Monteiro, 2020). Traditional clinical criteria for metabolically healthy/unhealthy obesity, however, were developed for cardiometabolic outcomes and may inadequately capture neural and psychological risk factors. Blood metabolomics offers a particularly promising approach by providing comprehensive metabolic signatures that reflect both systemic processes and brain-specific pathways relevant to mood regulation (Pan et al., 2025). This method captures thousands of small molecules representing diverse biological processes, potentially identifying novel predictive markers and therapeutic targets overlooked by conventional clinical measures or isolated biomarkers (Meng et al., 2024).

To address these critical gaps, our study challenges the longstanding assumption of uniform obesity–MDD associations by investigating baseline metabolic profiles as a key determinant for MDD risk stratification in obese individuals. We aimed to identify specific metabolic biomarkers that effectively stratify MDD risk and develop a robust prediction model tailored specifically for obese populations. Our approach leverages the UK Biobank (UKB)’s large European cohort, following 41,459 obese individuals for a median of 14.4 years, substantially longer than previous studies. We integrate comprehensive metabolomics data with various advanced machine learning (ML) techniques, including restricted cubic splines and mutual information analysis, to capture nonlinear relationships between metabolites and MDD. Crucially, we investigate causal pathways through mediation Mendelian randomization (MR) using large-scale European GWAS datasets and validate our findings on a temporal subset. This predictive model enables MDD risk stratification in obesity up to 9 years pre-diagnosis. Crucially, the identified metabolic mediators elucidate the obesity-MDD link and provide concrete targets for early intervention.

Methods

Data source and study sample

This was a prospective cohort and blood metabolomic study using data from UKB, a large-scale, long-term biomedical database of over half a million individuals aged 40–69 years from across the United Kingdom between 2006 and 2010. The reporting of this study adheres to the Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis Or Diagnosis (TRIPOD) statement, specifically the TRIPOD-AI extension for ML-based prediction models (Collins et al., 2024). At baseline, participants provided detailed information on their demographics, lifestyle factors, medical history, and family history through questionnaires and interviews. Biological samples, including blood, urine, and saliva, were also collected for biochemical and genetic analyses (Bycroft et al., 2018).

Our study used the 2006–2010 survey as the baseline, including a total of 502,355 UKB participants. While metabolic biomarkers were available for a randomly selected subset of ~300,000 participants, we sequentially excluded individuals from the total population. First, we excluded nonobese participants and those with missing data on BMI (n = 380,176). Subsequently, we excluded those with missing data on metabolic biomarkers (n = 56,636), those with missing data on MDD or having MDD at baseline (n = 7,273), and those with missing data on demographic characteristics at baseline (n = 16,811). Finally, 41,459 obese participants were included in the analyses (Figure 1). The UKB has generic ethical approval from the Northwest Multi-center Research Ethics Committee (Ref: 11/NW/0382). Definition of major depressive disorder

MDD phenotypes were ascertained using hospital inpatient records from Hospital Episode Statistics for England, Scottish Morbidity Record data for Scotland, Patient Episode Database for Wales (Zhao et al., 2023), and the online Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ)-9 (Davis et al., 2020). Diagnoses were recorded according to the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10) system codes, including depressive episode (F32.0–9) and recurrent depressive disorder (F33.0–9).

Definition of obesity

BMI is a commonly used index to classify obesity in adults, and obesity was defined as a BMI ≥ 30 (kg/m2), according to the World Health Organization (WHO) definition (World Health Organization, 2021). The formula utilized for BMI calculation is as follows: BMI=weightkg/heightm2BMI equals weightkg slash heightm 2

Blood metabolic measurements

The UKB blood sample collection was undertaken at baseline in 22 local assessment centers across the United Kingdom between 2006 and 2010. The blood sample handling, storage, and experimental protocol have been previously described (Buergel et al., 2022; Julkunen et al., 2023). Concentrations of 279 blood metabolomic biomarkers were measured in a randomly selected subset of ~300,000 participants. Specifically, 30 biomarkers were measured from blood samples by clinical chemistry. Furthermore, plasma samples were collected and subjected to NMR-based metabolome profiling at baseline (Nightingale Health, Helsinki, Finland), yielding 249 metabolomic biomarkers (107 non-derived metabolites, 61 composite metabolites, and 81 ratio parameters) (Pan et al., 2025). Blood metabolite levels were preprocessed using log-transformation. Biomarkers with more than 20% missing data were excluded. For the remaining biomarkers, missing values were handled using multiple imputation, resulting in a final set of 260 biomarkers included in the study (Supplementary Tables S1–S3).

Covariates

Age, sex, education level, race/ethnicity, Townsend deprivation index (TDI), activity metabolic equivalent task (defining low, medium, and high levels by aMET tertiles), sleep duration, fruit consumption, vegetable consumption, meat consumption, taking prescription medications (TPM), alcohol consumption, smoking status, cardiovascular disease (CVD), diabetes hypertension, and cancer were collected using standardized questionnaires and examination protocols during interviews (Allen et al., 2024). Alcohol consumption was categorized as never, previous, and current. Smoking status was classified as never, previous, and current. Histories of diabetes mellitus, hypertension, cancer, and cardiovascular disease were obtained through self-reported or prior medical diagnoses (ICD-10).

Machine learning model training and evaluation

The data from the derivation cohort comprising UKB were divided, with 90% utilized for training and 10% for internal validation, with model selection based on repeated undersampling and averaged metrics.

Missing data were handled using the Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations algorithm (Austin, White, Lee, & van Buuren, 2021). The imputation models incorporated all 260 selected metabolites, demographic covariates, and the MDD outcome to ensure the preservation of the underlying data structure and complex relationships without introducing target leakage. To systematically select features for our predictive models, we employed a sequential analytical pipeline. First, we used restricted cubic splines (RCS) as an initial screening tool to assess the comprehensive dose–response associations between all 260 metabolites and MDD risk, which filtered out metabolites with no significant relationship to MDD. Second, building on the subset of metabolites identified by RCS, we applied mutual information (MI) analysis and point-biserial correlation (PBC) analysis in parallel to further evaluate their predictive dependencies with the binary MDD outcome. These two methods are highly complementary. Specifically, PBC captures the strength and direction of linear associations between continuous metabolite levels and MDD, whereas MI is sensitive to complex, nonlinear dependencies that linear correlation metrics might miss. Finally, by cross-comparing and integrating the top-ranked features from both MI and PBC, we identified a robust and prioritized set of metabolites. This combined approach ensures that both linear and nonlinear predictive signals are captured, providing a robust basis for developing the subsequent ML predictive models. We then constructed 14 predictive models based on the biomarkers identified through both methodologies, namely, Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), Random Forest Classifier (RF), Extremely Randomized Trees Classifier (ET), Gaussian Naive Bayes (GaussianNB), Logistic Regression (LR), k-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Nu-Support Vector Classification (NuSVC), Quadratic Discriminant Analysis (QDA), Calibrated Classifier with Cross-Validation (CalibratedClassifierCV), Bagging Classifier (Bagging), Support Vector Machine (SVM), Decision Tree Classifier (DT), and Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) were used to predict MDD in obese individuals.

Several commonly used evaluation indices, such as area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC), accuracy, precision, recall, F1 score, calibration curves, and decision curve analysis (DCA), were used to evaluate the reliability of 14 models.

Furthermore, to evaluate the generalizability of the model, we conducted a temporal validation using data from the UKB repeat assessment phase (2012–2013). This independent validation set comprised 2,330 participants with obesity, with an MDD prevalence of 4.2%. The inclusion and exclusion criteria were identical to those applied to the primary derivation cohort. To ensure robust performance estimation, we employed bootstrap sampling during the evaluation process.

Feature selection and model explanation

Achieving interpretability in ML models is indeed a significant challenge, particularly as models grow more complex. The SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) method is an approach that could rank the importance of input features and explain the results of the prediction model, and it is implemented to overcome the ‘black-box’ issue (Yi, Sun, & Wu, 2024). The SHAP method offers global and local explanations for the model. The global explanation could give consistent and accurate attribution values for each feature within a model to show the associations between input features and MDD. The local explanation could demonstrate a specific prediction for an obese individual by inputting the specific data. Overall, the SHAP method provides a robust framework for understanding feature importance and clarifying the predictions made by these models.

Two-step Mendelian randomization framework for causal mediation analysis

Causal mediation analysis with MR was performed using the R packages ‘TwoSampleMR (version 0.5.7)’ and ‘Mendelian Randomization (v0.9.0)’. We employed a two-step Mendelian Randomization framework (Carter et al., 2021) to investigate whether selected metabolic biomarkers mediate the causal pathway between BMI and MDD. We used ‘BMI’ (GWAS ID: ukb-b-19953 and ukb-b-2303) as the exposure variable, and ‘selected metabolic biomarkers’ were used as mediator variables. The main outcomes include ‘Major Depressive Disorder (GWAS ID: ebi-a-GCST005904, ebi-a-GCST009979)’, ‘Major depressive disorder in trauma exposed individuals (GWAS ID: ebi-a-GCST009980)’, and ‘Depression (GWAS ID: finn-b-F5_DEPRESSIO)’. The GWAS summary data were obtained from the IEU GWAS database (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/). Details of the exposure, mediators, and outcomes are presented in Supplementary Table S4. Lead SNPs significantly associated with the exposure traits were extracted from the GWAS summary data at genome-wide significance (P < 5 × 10−7) and selected as instrumental variables (IVs). IVs were pruned for linkage disequilibrium (r 2 < 0.001, distance 10,000 kb) and filtered for F > 10 (Supplementary Tables S5–S13). Causal estimates for the exposure-mediator, mediator-outcome, and exposure-outcome relationships were generated using inverse-variance weighted (IVW) analysis. The indirect effect (the mediation pathway) was estimated using the product of coefficients method (VanderWeele, 2016). Standard errors for the indirect effect were derived using the Delta method (Carter et al., 2019). Finally, the proportion mediated was calculated by dividing the indirect effect by the total effect.

Statistical analysis

We provided descriptive statistics for the baseline characteristics of the study population. Categorical variables were expressed as numbers and proportions, while continuous variables were reported as mean and standard deviation (mean ± SD).

We utilized RCS to assess the dose–response relationships between 260 blood metabolites and the future development of MDD. Mutual information analysis and point-biserial correlation analysis were further employed to screen potential features from meaningful metabolites obtained based on RCS. Pearson correlation analysis was used to examine intra-group correlations of metabolites. Additionally, pathway enrichment analysis was conducted using the MetaboAnalyst 6.0 platform ( https://dev.metaboanalyst.ca/home.xhtml ), utilizing its comprehensive functional databases (comprising 15 libraries with ~13,000 biologically meaningful metabolite sets) to reveal the biological pathways associated with the specific metabolites (FDR-adjusted P < 0.05).

Additionally, ML techniques were used to develop predictive models for identifying obese individuals at high risk of developing MDD based on accuracy, precision, recall, F1 score, and ROC curve. Fourteen widely used supervised learning algorithms were evaluated based on model hyperparameters, tuning procedures, and the training, validation, and testing protocols used to ensure robust and unbiased performance evaluation. Additionally, to assess the specificity of the metabolites identified in predicting future MDD in obese individuals, our predictive model was compared to models developed using biomarkers identified in previous studies conducted in the general population (Ma et al., 2024; Radford-Smith et al., 2023).

Finally, causal mediation analysis with MR was performed to validate the robustness of the results from ML techniques, using BMI, metabolites, and MDD data obtained from the IEU GWAS database as exposure, mediators, and outcome, respectively.

All analyses were conducted using SAS software version 9.4, R version 4.4.2, Jupyter Notebook 6.5.4, or GraphPad Prism 9.0, unless specified. A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Subject participation

A total of 41,459 participants (mean age 56.7 ± 7.9 years) were included in the study. Participants were classified into MDD and non-MDD groups. The MDD group had a lower mean age (56.1 ± 8.1 years) compared to the non-MDD group (56.8 ± 7.9 years), with different education levels. Males made up 51.6% of the total, but only 40.0% in the MDD group. Most participants identified as British (89.0%), with no significant ethnic differences (P = 0.625). MDD individuals had higher rates of current smoking (13.7% vs. 8.6%), while current drinkers were fewer (85.3% vs. 91.9%). Moreover, there was little difference in eating habits, except for meat intake (P < 0.001).

Sleep duration was slightly less for MDD participants (7.0 ± 1.4 hours) versus non-MDD (7.1 ± 1.1 hours). Medical history revealed that MDD participants had higher rates of CVD (12.4% vs. 8.3%), cancer (8.6% vs. 7.3%), hypertension (39.8% vs. 36.2%), and diabetes (16.4% vs. 10.9%), and used more prescription medications (73.2% vs. 52.2%). Then, mutual information analysis and point-biserial correlation analysis were further utilized to screen specific features from general information and 99 metabolites, and the top 20 features were cross-compared, leading to the identification of 14 metabolites for developing of predictive models (Figure 2b), including glucose, proteins (albumin and glycoprotein acetyls [GlycA]), fatty acids (omega-3 fatty acids to total fatty acids percentage (Omega-3/TFA), degree of unsaturation (DU), docosahexaenoic acid to total fatty acids percentage (DHA/TFA), monounsaturated fatty acids to total fatty acids percentage (MUFA/TFA), polyunsaturated fatty acids to total fatty acids percentage (PUFA/TFA), polyunsaturated fatty acids to monounsaturated fatty acids ratio (PUFA/MUFA), and lipids (triglycerides to total lipids in large LDL percentage [TGTLLLDLp], cholesterol to total lipids in IDL percentage [CTLIDLp], triglycerides to total lipids in medium LDL percentage [TGTLMLDLp], total lipids in VLDL [TLVLDL], and free cholesterol in small HDL [FCSHDL]). While Supplementary Figure S1 indicated the risk trends for MDD associated with these metabolic biomarkers and identified thresholds that distinguish high- from low-risk groups, some of which have relatively high correlations with each other (Figure 2c).

Furthermore, enrichment analysis underscored the dominance of metabolite alterations in shaping the metabolic profiles of participants destined to develop MDD, including regulation of hormone metabolism, gene expression and signaling, biological transcription and regulation, cell biogenesis, and small molecule biochemistry (P < 0.001) (Figure 2d). Notably, our analysis identified three major disrupted metabolic pathways: the synthesis, secretion, and inactivation of glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) and incretin, as well as α-Linolenic acid metabolism.

Developing models for early prediction of future MDD among obese individuals

Next, we developed predictive models for 3-, 5-, and 9-year MDD risks based on the 14 identified metabolites and demographic features. A total of 14 different ML algorithms were evaluated. To assess model robustness and account for variance, each algorithm underwent 10 independent training and testing iterations, from which the average AUC, accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 score were derived. Among all models, LightGBM consistently outperformed others across all prediction time frames, demonstrating the best predictive performance. After identifying LightGBM as the optimal algorithm during the initial screening (Supplementary Figures S2–S4), we performed extensive hyperparameter tuning to construct the final optimized predictive models. The final optimized LightGBM models demonstrated robust predictive performance, achieving an AUC of 0.844 (95% CI: 0.773–0.914), accuracy of 0.777, precision of 0.800, recall of 0.788, and F1 score of 0.794 for the 3-year prediction model, an AUC of 0.824 (95% CI: 0.771–0.875), accuracy of 0.722, precision of 0.741, recall of 0.673, and F1 score of 0.705 for the 5-year prediction model, and an AUC of 0.834 (95% CI: 0.796–0.871), accuracy of 0.758, precision of 0.748, recall of 0.755, and F1 score of 0.751 for the 9-year prediction model (Figure 3b,c). For the temporal validation, the LightGBM model once again achieved good predictive performance (Supplementary Figure S5 ), with AUCs (95% CIs) of 0.776 (0.679–0.932) for the 3-year risk prediction, 0.738 (0.526–0.944) for the 5-year risk prediction, and 0.767 (0.609–0.895) for the 9-year risk prediction, indicating that the final model showed great generalization performance.

Furthermore, to ensure the transparency and reliability of our missing data handling, we evaluated the proportion of imputed data and its impact on model performance. Before imputation, the overall mean proportion of missing data across the 260 included metabolites was 3.4%, with individual metabolite missingness ranging from 0% to strictly under 20%. We conducted a sensitivity analysis by training and evaluating the LightGBM models on a subset excluding participants with any missing metabolite values. The subset analysis yielded highly comparable predictive performance, achieving AUCs of 0.821, 0.809, and 0.818 for the 3-, 5-, and 9-year MDD predictions (Supplementary Figure S6), respectively, compared to 0.844, 0.824, and 0.834 in the imputed dataset. This minimal variance indicates that the imputation procedure did not introduce significant bias, confirming the robustness of our predictive models across all timeframes.

Validation of metabolite specificity for MDD in the obese population by comparative modeling

To verify the specificity of the identified metabolites for MDD in the obese population, we developed comparison LightGBM models using metabolites identified in the general population (Ma et al., 2024; Radford-Smith et al., 2023).

Notably, the comparison models demonstrated marked inferiority compared to the predictive model based on the specific metabolites we identified (Supplementary Figure S7). The changes in AUC, accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 score for two reference models predicting MDD were as follows: AUCs ranged from 0.770 to 0.795 and 0.747 to 0.808 (Supplementary Figure S7a and 7b), accuracy from 0.693 to 0.731 and 0.667 to 0.736, precision from 0.690 to 0.766 and 0.720 to 0.797, recall from 0.645 to 0.668 and 0.631 to 0.698, and F1 score from 0.667 to 0.714 and 0.672 to 0.733 (Supplementary Figure S7c and 7d). Overall, our metabolite-based prediction models exhibit significant advantages in identifying MDD risk in obese populations, particularly in short-term predictions.

Mediation Mendelian randomization of metabolic biomarkers

Furthermore, we performed causal mediation analysis with MR to assess the robustness of the results using 14 metabolic biomarkers to predict MDD risk in the obese population. Expectedly, the mediation MR analysis revealed a significant causal association between BMI and MDD, mediated by metabolic biomarkers (Prop: 10.0% for MUFA/TFA, −11.5% for glucose, 14.5% for DU, 9.7% for DHA/TFA, 6.0% for omega 3/TFA, 35.3% for PUFA/MUFA, and 24.8% for PUFA/TFA, all P ME < 0.05) (Supplementary Figure S8 and Supplementary Table S15). These findings greatly supported the findings that 14 metabolic biomarkers were utilized to predict MDD risk in the obese population, based on 14 ML algorithms.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this was the first study using ML algorithms and novel metabolomic biomarkers to optimize stratified detection of MDD in obese individuals. We identified 14 specific metabolic biomarkers in the obese population and further developed and validated a LightGBM model that exhibits excellent performance for predicting MDD. Additionally, causal mediation analysis highlighted the intricate biological relationships between these biomarkers and MDD risk. In summary, we systematically revealed novel biomarkers that underpin the risk and resilience associated with MDD in the obese population, emphasizing their potential in addressing global public health priorities for integrated management of these intertwined conditions.

Historically, the link between obesity and MDD has been acknowledged, yet the underlying metabolic mechanisms remain less understood. Our findings reveal metabolic heterogeneity among obese individuals, indicating that not all obesity poses the same risk for MDD. By employing a comprehensive metabolomic approach and Mendelian randomization (MR), we demonstrated that metabolic dysregulation actively contributes to the onset of MDD rather than simply coexisting with it. This finding is particularly relevant in the debate over biological versus psychosocial determinants of MDD. Specifically, our SHAP and MR analyses convergently highlighted the causal roles of glucose, specific lipid fractions, and polyunsaturated fatty acids. Elevated glucose and specific lipoproteins indicate insulin resistance and dyslipidemia, which are associated with systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, and reduced brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) – all of which are implicated in MDD pathophysiology (Huang & Chen, 2005; Meng, Wang, Wang, & Zou, 2025; Porter & O’Connor, 2022). Conversely, higher levels of omega-3 fatty acids provide resilience due to their anti-inflammatory effects on brain health (Su, Matsuoka, & Pae, 2015). Furthermore, our pathway analysis identified the synthesis, secretion, and inactivation of GLP-1 and incretin as crucial disrupted pathways. Elevated levels of free fatty acids and inflammatory cytokines in obese individuals can impair GLP-1 secretion and receptor sensitivity (Nauck et al., 2017; Pathak, Vasu, Flatt, & Irwin, 2014). Given that GLP-1 has neuroprotective effects and influences mood regulation in the central nervous system (Zhang, Zhang, & Tian, 2023). Its dysfunction may further exacerbate depressive symptoms. Together, these interconnected pathways provide a comprehensive biological explanation for the metabolic mediation of MDD risk in obesity.

We established a robust predictive framework that enhances the early identification of individuals at risk for MDD. To date, large-scale predictive studies of MDD have primarily focused on the general population, typically achieving moderate predictive performance with AUCs ranging from 0.658 to 0.720 (Ma et al., 2024; Radford-Smith et al., 2023). By developing models specifically tailored to the obese population, we achieved remarkable predictive accuracy. Among the 14 distinct ML models evaluated, the LightGBM model exhibited a significant advantage across various timeframes (3-, 5-, and 9-year predictions), successfully passing independent temporal validation. Notably, the predictive performance of these models exceeded 80%, highlighting the superiority of integrating specific metabolomic data with advanced computational methods over traditional general-population models. However, we also observed a substantial performance discrepancy of ~20% across the 14 evaluated models, with the accuracy of simpler algorithms dropping to 60%. This gap underscores the complex, nonlinear nature of the metabolomic data associated with MDD risk. Traditional algorithms often struggle to capture intricate biological interactions and are highly sensitive to the multicollinearity prevalent among lipid and fatty acid fractions. In contrast, advanced tree-based ensemble methods like LightGBM excel in handling complex tabular data, effectively managing nonlinear relationships and feature interactions without requiring manual specification. Furthermore, SHAP analysis enhances model interpretability by ranking clinical feature importance. Although LightGBM natively handles missing data, relying on this for primary predictors may reduce prognostic accuracy. Therefore, we recommend confirmatory testing or clinical imputation for missing top-ranked features, while leveraging the model’s intrinsic robustness to handle missing data among lower-ranked metabolites. By focusing on these specific metabolic signatures, clinicians could better stratify patients according to their risk profiles, enabling earlier and more tailored interventions.

Moreover, MR was utilized to investigate the biological causal pathways linking obesity to MDD through these complex metabolic biomarkers, thereby reinforcing the validity of our ML predictions. While the MR analysis revealed that the proportion of the obesity-MDD effect mediated by individual metabolites is relatively modest, these magnitudes are highly consistent with the complex and multifactorial etiology of MDD (Cui et al., 2024). This finding is particularly relevant in the ongoing debate over biological versus psychosocial determinants of MDD. By pinpointing these specific metabolic pathways, we enhance our understanding of how physiological dysregulation directly interconnects with psychological vulnerability. In other words, metabolic disturbances, marked by altered lipid and amino acid profiles, are not merely passive bystanders or epiphenomena coexisting with obesity, but active, causal contributors to the pathogenesis of MDD.

While the LightGBM model natively manages missing values by determining optimal directions during tree construction, sole reliance on this feature for primary predictors may reduce prognostic certainty. Therefore, for features identified as critical by SHAP analysis, we advise that missing or unreliable values trigger confirmatory retesting or standardized clinical imputation rather than model-based default assumptions. For lower-ranked metabolites, however, the model’s intrinsic resilience to sparsity is sufficient to maintain predictive validity, ensuring the tool’s robustness and utility even in the absence of comprehensive metabolomic profiling.

Our novel investigation has several strengths. First, the UKB comprises over 500,000 participants, providing a robust foundation for statistical analyses and enhancing the credibility of the research. Second, it offers a rich array of blood data, enabling comprehensive analyses of the complex interplay between obesity and MDD, thus aiding in the elucidation of potential biological mechanisms. Third, the use of a prospective cohort design allows for more effective identification of causal relationships by observing changes in metabolic biomarkers before the onset of MDD. Finally, the application of multiple ML algorithms demonstrates exceptional predictive performance in 3-, 5-, and 9-year prediction models. A high level of predictive capability facilitates the early identification of obese individuals at high risk for MDD, informing clinical interventions and personalized treatment strategies.

Our study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the UKB predominantly comprises individuals from the United Kingdom, limiting racial and geographic diversity. Although we conducted temporal validation within the same biobank, true external validation in independent, multi-ethnic cohorts is necessary to confirm the generalizability of our findings. Second, despite adjusting for a comprehensive set of covariates, the potential for residual or unmeasured confounding remains, which could influence the observed associations. Third, the long-term follow-up introduces challenges such as participant attrition and unrecorded dynamic changes in health status or metabolic profiles, potentially adding uncertainty to our risk assessments. Finally, our study focused on overall incident MDD without differentiating clinical subtypes. Given the clinical heterogeneity of MDD, future research is warranted to explore whether specific metabolic signatures map onto distinct depressive phenotypes.

Conclusion

Overall, our study elucidates the intricate relationship between obesity and MDD through an innovative blend of metabolomic profiling and ML techniques. We identified a set of novel metabolic biomarkers that can reliably predict MDD risk in obese individuals utilizing an advanced ML algorithm, and help to determine the underlying molecular mechanisms associated with MDD. Our findings highlight the critical importance of metabolic health in mental health outcomes, suggesting that targeted interventions in metabolic pathways could be pivotal in mitigating the intertwined burdens of obesity and MDD, thereby addressing the escalating mental health crisis.
