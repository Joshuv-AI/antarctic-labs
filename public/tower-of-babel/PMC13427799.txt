Extradural anterior transpetrosal approach: how I do it
Authors: Thibault Passeri, Gabriel C Heemann, Michelle Grouls, Sébastien Froelich
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPetroclival and brainstem lesions remain challenging to access due to their deep-seated location and proximity to critical neurovascular structures.MethodWe describe the step-by-step technique of the standard extradural anterior transpetrosal approach, and its extensions, illustrated through cadaveric and surgical illustrations. A surgical video demonstrates its application in a patient with a petroclival meningioma with posterolateral extension.ConclusionThe extradural anterior transpetrosal approach provides a direct « looking-down» trajectory to petroclival lesions with minimal brain retraction, making it a valuable route for safe and effective access to this complex anatomical region.Keywords: Anterior transpetrosal approach, Anterior petrosectomy, Extradural anterior transpetrosal approach, Petroclival meningioma

Introduction

The extradural anterior transpetrosal approach (EAPA), described by Kawase et al. [4], offers a “looking-down” trajectory providing access to the temporal fossa floor (TPF), Meckel’s cave (MC), petrous apex (PA), petroclival region, mid-clivus, and the anterolateral part of the pons. Despites its advantages [7], the approach remains technically demanding due to the complex anatomy, narrow working space and the need for temporal lobe (TL) retraction which becomes increasingly significant as the exposure increases upward.

Relevant surgical anatomy

The TPF presents a complex topography, resembling a series of “hills and valleys”, which correspond to key surgical landmarks, essential for safe and effective access to the petrous region. From lateral to medial, the main landmarks include: the tegmen tympani, arcuate eminence, meatal depression, trigeminal prominence, trigeminal depression.

When peeling the middle fossa floor, the foramen spinosum (FS) is the most lateral bony landmark. Anteromedially, at a variable distance of 0.5 to over 5 mm, lies the foramen ovale (FO) [5]. Posteriorly, the arcuate eminence (AE), overlying the superior semicircular canal (SSC) is the largest proeminence – although this correlation is not always reliable [8]. Anterolateral to the AE, the facial hiatus marks the exit of the greater superficial petrosal nerve (GSPN) from the geniculate ganglion (GG). The GSPN courses anteromedially over the superior surface of the temporal bone, following the sphenopetrosal groove, above the horizontal segment of the petrous internal carotid artery (ICA), and then passes beneath V3 (Fig. 1A). The bone coverage above the horizontal segment of the petrous ICA thins progressively as the artery proceeds medially beneath V3 (Fig. 1B-C). Dorsal dehiscence of petrous ICA represents a common anatomical variants, which some cases may extend lateral to V3 [3]. The position of internal auditory canal (IAC) can be estimated under a virtual line bisecting GSPN and AE [2]. The cochlea lies below the floor of the middle fossa at the angle between the labyrinthine segment of the facial nerve and GSPN, just medial to the GG, anterior to the fundus of the IAC and posterosuperior to the posterior genu of the ICA (Fig. 1D). The rhomboid fossa [1] is limited anteriorly by the foramen ovale and V3, posteriorly by the AE and laterally by the GSPN. It can be divided into two triangles according to the IAC line (yellow line): the Kawase triangle delimitating the premeatal area, and the retromeatal triangle (Fig. 1E).

The superior petrosal sinus (SPS) lies in the superior petrosal sulcus along the petrous ridge (PR), and receiving venous drainage from the superior petrosal venous complex, most often between MC and the IAC [6, 10]. The SPS may drain into both the CS and the transverse-sigmoid junction (TSJ) (65%), exclusively into the TSJ (37%), or exclusively into the CS (3%) [10]. The inferior petrosal sinus (IPS) courses along the petro-occipital fissure, forming the main venous drainage of CS, and connects the CS to the jugular bulb.

Description of the surgical technique

Patient positioning and preparation

Patient is placed in a supine position, with the head fixed in a head holder and rotated 60–70° with a slight downward tilt facilitating gravitational retraction of the TL. The thorax is elevated. Abdomen is prepared for fat harvesting. Cranial nerves, somatosensory and motor evoked potentials, are monitored. Neuronavigation is prepared. Lumbar drainage is not routinely used to keep CSF, which serves as a natural brain protection.

Skin incision and soft tissue dissection

A frontotemporal question mark incision is made anterior to the tragus. After elevating the skin, an interfascial dissection of the temporalis muscle (TM) is performed to provide additional anterior retraction and facial nerve branches protection. The zygomatic root is exposed following the deep layer of the deep temporalis fascia. An incision is made all around the TM and a retrograde elevation of the muscle is performed.

Temporal craniotomy and anterior petrosectomy

A temporal craniotomy is performed. The remaining temporal bone is drilled flush with the middle fossa floor. Opened mastoid air cells are sealed with bone wax. Additional anterior drilling below the TM improves exposure. A small dural incision allows CSF release.

The temporal dura (TD) is elevated from posterior-to-anterior. The middle meningeal artery is identified, coagulated and cut 2–3 mm distal to FS (Fig. 2A). FO is identified anterior and medial to the FS. The periosteum along FO is incised and the dura propria is peeled from V3 (Fig. 2B). The AE is exposed posteriorly (Fig. 2C). Anterior to the AE, the GSPN existing from the facial hiatus is identified, and its position is confirmed with neurostimulation. The dura is peeled from the GSPN from back to front using a periosteal elevator and scissor, until V3 is reached (Fig. 2D). Along V3, the dura propria is further elevated to expose the PA and MC (Fig. 2E). V3 and MC can be further elevated from the trigeminal impression and petrous ICA to expose the true PA. Finally, PR is exposed (Fig. 2F).

A stepwise drilling of the rhomboid fossa (Fig. 3) is then performed using a 4–3 mm diamond drills with continuous irrigation. Four drilling zone can be identified: the pre-meatal zone, supra-meatal zone, post-meatal zone and true PA. The drilling begins in the pre-meatal volume at the antero-medial part of the rhomboid fossa along V3 (1), and is progressively enlarged, exposing the PFD. It then proceeds laterally toward GSPN to eventually identify the petrous ICA (2). Then, drilling continues in a posterolateral direction toward the petrous ridge (3). From this point, it is extended further posterolaterally, above and posterior to the dura of the IAC toward the postmeatal area (4), and around the cochlea and more laterally (5). This lateral extension is critical as the more the drilling extents laterally toward the cochlea, the lower exposure of the clivus will be obtained (Fig. 4). Infero-medial drilling exposes the IPS. A slight anterior elevation of V3 and MC allows to drill the true PA (6). Depending on the lateral extension of the lesion, the post-meatal area can be further drilled to improve exposure to the middle cerebellar peduncle and cisternal segment of CNs VII/VIII.

Dura opening

The PFD is opened carefully to avoid injuring of CN V that may be pushed laterally by the tumor. TD is opened along the SPS and anteriorly to the roof of MC (Fig. 5A). SPS is ligated and cut close to the trigeminal porus preserving the superior petrosal venous complex (Fig. 5B). The tentorium is sectioned toward its free edge, after identifying the CN IV (Fig. 5C, D). MC is opened along V3 if invaded (Fig. 5E).

Closure

TD is reapproximated in a non-watertight fashion. Bone wax and pericranium seal the PA air cells, IAC (Fig. 6A). Fat is then used to fill the dural defect (Fig. 6B). A pericranial graft is placed on the middle fossa floor and a piece of TachoSil® is placed to improve closure (Fig. 6C-D). The bone flap is repositioned and fixed using titanium microplates. TM is reattached, and skin closed in layers.

B) Fat graft is used to fill the dural defect. The middle fossa floor is then waxed and covered with pericranium to reinforce the closure. Limitations

Superior extension above the tentorium may requires excessive TL retraction. In such cases, particularly to achieve better control of the oculomotor nerve (CN III) and the posterior communicating artery (PCom), which may be displaced medially and superiorly by the tumor, a complementary approach should be considered (e.g. pretemporal transcavernous approach or petrosal transpetrosal approach). Posterior extension beyond the IAC can be addressed by drilling the postmeatal triangle, which allows improved control of the cisternal portion of CNs VII/VIII. Inferior extension below the IAC is not an absolute limitation, as it can be exposed and resected under endoscopic assistance and selected drilling near to the cochlea, providing a more favorable superior-to-inferior trajectory.

How to avoid complications

Detailed preoperative imaging is essential to evaluate bone anatomy, vascular structures, and tumor relationship [9]. Anatomical variations, such as pneumatization of the PA, dehiscence of GG or ICA, depth and position of the cochlea should be carefully assessed [2]. A thorough evaluation of the PA air cell anatomy, the transition between cancellous and cortical bone, and the thickness of the cortical bone overlying critical structures can greatly aid safe intraoperative navigation through the petrous bone. Drilling of the PA should always be tailored to the specific pathology, particularly with regard to the extent of bone removal close to the cochlea and within the postmeatal triangle.

Specific information for the patient

The patient should also be informed about approach related complications: TL injury, venous problems, hearing loss, ICA injury, CSF leak & infections, CNs injury (GSPN, CN IV, V and VII/VII). Neurovascular complication due to tumor resection should be explain (perforators, brain stem damage, CNs injury ect.).
