Investigating the Relationship Between Sensory Processing, Pain and Toe Walking Gait: A Survey Study
Authors: Jack H. Donne, Verity Pacey, Desiree Polt, Jennifer A. Powell, Michael C. Fahey, Cylie M. Williams
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimThis study aimed to examine sensory processing patterns, the frequency and impact of parent‐reported pain in children who toe walk across a range of diagnoses using validated caregiver‐report tools.MethodsAn online cross‐sectional survey was distributed internationally between July 2024 and March 2025. Parents of children who toe walk aged 3–14 years, regardless of diagnosis, completed the Short Sensory Profile (SSP) and the Child Activity Limitations Interview‐9 (CALI‐9).ResultsThe survey collected responses from 176 parents of 187 children with toe walking gait. Sensory processing challenges were frequently reported, particularly in children with autism spectrum disorder (98%). Nearly half (48% of 187) of children were reported to experience pain, most frequently in the feet/ankles, lower legs, and hips/thighs. Most parents (94%) and their child's medical professional (81%) attributed their child's pain to toe walking. Parents of children experiencing pain described significantly greater limitations in activities such as sports (OR 3.10, p < 0.001), running (OR 3.46, p < 0.001), and social participation (OR 2.43, p < 0.001).ConclusionSensory processing differences and pain were frequently reported among children who toe walk in this sample. Routine assessment of sensory processing and pain in clinical practice may guide targeted interventions to improve participation, function, and quality of life.

Introduction

Sensory processing challenges can significantly affect a child's learning, development, and participation in daily life [1]. Children with atypical sensory processing may have difficulty interpreting and responding to sensory stimuli, which can influence their behaviour, attention, motor coordination, and emotional regulation [1, 2]. This means that atypical sensory processing can affect how a child moves, concentrates, and interacts with family, friends, and peers at school [1, 2].

Similarly, chronic musculoskeletal pain in children is increasingly recognised as a complex biopsychosocial condition that can interfere with physical function, school attendance, social interactions, and emotional well‐being [3, 4]. Pain experienced by children can have wide‐reaching consequences not only for the child but also for family dynamics and caregiver burden [4]. Children with disabilities are at greater risk for both sensory processing challenges and pain [5, 6]. Yet, the co‐occurrence of these issues, particularly in the context of altered gait patterns such as toe walking, remains poorly understood [7].

Toe walking gait is characterised by the absence of heel strike during the initial stance phase of the gait cycle, with children predominantly walking on their toes rather than using the entire foot [8]. While toe walking can be a normal phase in typical development in young children, persistent toe walking can indicate underlying neurological or musculoskeletal conditions, including cerebral palsy (CP), autism spectrum disorders (ASD), muscular dystrophies, or tethered cord syndrome [8]. When no underlying medical cause is identified, the gait is termed idiopathic toe walking (ITW). Toe walking has been reported in up to 5% of healthy children [8], 82% of children with ASD [9], and 93% of children with CP [10].

Many children who toe walk, regardless of the cause, demonstrate sensory processing challenges. Sensory processing challenges are frequently described as a feature of ASD [11]. Children with CP also exhibit behaviours linked to sensory processing challenges [12]. Some studies have also linked sensory processing challenges with idiopathic toe walking [13, 14]. However, any relationships between sensory processing differences in children who toe walk, regardless of any underlying diagnosis, and the presence or absence of pain relating to their gait pattern remain unexplored. This relationship may be bidirectional. Pain may arise from the biomechanical stress that toe walking places on the lower limbs [15]. Sensory processing differences may be associated with both the gait pattern and altered pain perception [16]. Both pain and persistent toe walking can limit participation in physical, social, and recreational activities [10, 17]. Emerging research indicates that children who toe walk may also be at greater risk of falling [7, 18], and underperform in physical activities or sports [19]. Aesthetic differences can also lead to social challenges [20]. Exploring the prevalence and impact of pain among these children is vital to understanding participation limitations [17, 21].

The primary aim of our study was to describe the sensory processing profiles and pain presence of children who toe walk using the Short Sensory Profile and to examine how these might differ across diagnoses. Our secondary aims were to explore the impact of pain in this cohort on a child's ability to be physically active and whether the child's sensory profile interacted with this impact. A better understanding of these factors may guide clinicians to explore these in depth with children who toe walk and their families in the clinical setting.

Methods

Study Design

A cross‐sectional internet survey with the CHERRIES checklist guided the research reporting [22]. The completed CHERRIES checklist is provided in Supporting Information S1. The Human Research Ethics Committee of Monash University, Victoria, approved the study (41093).

Participants

Any parent or guardian who reported a child who toe walked, aged 3–14 years, was eligible to participate. All medical conditions known to cause or be associated with toe walking, in addition to idiopathic toe walking, were of interest. Eligibility was not restricted to any specific geographic area.

Measurements

All participant data were collected via a single electronic survey using Qualtrics [23]. During development, authors (VP, DP and CMW) trialled the questionnaire to refine the flow and question wording. The survey collected demographic information, including the child's age, diagnosis related to their toe walking, and sex at birth. It also collected pain data, including whether a child experiences pain, pain location, and the suspected relationship between pain and toe walking. The Demographics section of the survey is in Table 1. Two validated tools were used to gather information about sensory processing and pain, and due to licensing agreements, they are not published in the Appendix A. The Short Sensory Profile (SSP) [24] was used to capture information about how a child interprets sensory input. This is a standardised 38‐question form that assesses a child's sensory processing patterns across home, school, and community‐based activities [24]. The SSP uses a 5‐point scale from “Never” to “Always”, representing the frequency with which a specific behaviour occurs. The Child Activity Limitations Interview 9 (CALI‐9) [25] was used to collect the impact of pain on function in children who toe walk. This is a validated 9‐question measure of activity limitations in children [25]. The CALI‐9ordinal 5‐point scale to rate the difficulty of each activity from “not very difficult” to “extremely difficult”, with higher scores indicating greater limitation. The CALI‐9 was developed and validated children and adolescents with chronic pain aged 8–18. The SSP is normalised for children aged 3–14 years. In this study the CALI‐9 was answered by parents, not children, and the questions relating to pain were deemed relevant to our population the experienced clinical team. Data was not compared against normative data, hence it was decided that the CALI 9 was the most suitable tool for collecting data about the impact of pain, despite a small percentage of children falling outside of the validated age range.

Procedure

The study was advertised online through Facebook, Instagram, and LinkedIn accounts of the research team and the university. Advertising was also placed in online health professional groups to facilitate snowball distribution, and in an online toe walking support group, which is predominantly family‐based, with parent and health professional moderation. Participants were required to indicate consent to complete the survey. The survey was open from July 15, 2024, to March 28, 2025, and was published only in English.

The order of the survey questions was not randomised, and the number of questions was set, then repeated if the parent participant indicated multiple numbers of children who toe walked. The SSP CALI‐9 and pain questions were administered to each child, using a forced‐choice format to ensure completeness.

Potential duplicate participant responses were screened by Qualtrics processes and deleted if explicitly linked through identical responses. Participant demographic data were extracted from Qualtrics, and consent‐only responses were removed before analysis. Qualtrics' cache mechanism was enabled to ensure participants could continue answering the questionnaire even after accidentally exiting it for up to 1 week. Bot screening was conducted manually and via Qualtrics software. Responses were screened for automated or fraudulent activity using objective criteria. A response was excluded as a suspected bot or fraudulent entry if it met any of the following: an IP‐address geolocation inconsistent with the reported country, a completion time under 1 min (implausible given the questionnaire length), or free‐text answers inconsistent with the question asked. The first two criteria were applied automatically from Qualtrics IP and timing metadata, free‐text inconsistencies were identified by manual review (JD). Participants could withdraw at any time by closing the browser, and any non‐completed questions were treated as missing data for the remaining non‐completed variables. A random draw was held at survey closure for AUD 50 gift cards as an incentive for completing the survey.

Data Analysis

Data were sorted into individual participant rows in Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corp, Washington) and analysed in Stata v15 (StataCorp LP, Texas). The sensory processing data were scored according to the scoring manual [24], and were categorised into seven domains: tactile sensitivity, taste/smell sensitivity, movement sensitivity, under‐responsiveness/seeking sensation, auditory filtering, low energy/weakness, and visual/auditory sensitivity, according to the SSP. The total SSP scores and domain sub‐scores were calculated, and raw scores were categorised as “Typical Performance,” “Probable Difference,” or “Definite Difference” for the total and domain sub‐scores based on normative cut‐offs [24]. During data collection, one SSP item (Withdraws from splashing water) from the tactile sensitivity domain was accidentally missed. It was added as soon as the researchers realised. A sensitivity analysis was performed for affected data responses. Where the missing item could not have altered the final SSP classification, the score was retained. Where it changed the classification, the final SSP classification was excluded. The final scores were then combined into a binary score of typical or probably/definitely different.

Participant data were summarised as means and standard deviations (SD) or median and interquartile ranges (IQR) and sorted into sub‐categories according to reason for toe walking (i.e., Idiopathic toe walking, autism spectrum disorder, cerebral palsy, unknown, and other). Initially, regression analyses were used to determine differences in variables between those with and without pain; results were reported as odds ratios, 95% confidence intervals (95% CI), and p‐values. A similar approach was taken to explore SSP categories and determine whether there were differences in the number of children across SSP categories, to guide further analysis. It was planned to examine the mediating role of the sensory profile categorisation across all toe walking groups This analysis plan was developed a priori to examine the relationship between sensory profile categorisation scores and pain impact rating across all toe‐walking groups, including whether sensory profile categories were associated with differences in pain experiences Due to a small number of responses in the group of children with cerebral palsy, complexity in the descriptions of what children were being assessed for in the “unknown group”, and heterogeneity in the conditions parents reported as “other” reasons for toe walking, mediation analyses were only undertaken in the group with ITW and Autism Spectrum Disorders.

Results

Table 1 provides a response summary from 176 parents about 187 children. Most parents (n = 158, 84%) reported toe walking in only one child and children with a mean age (SD) of 7.7 (3.2) years. There were 431 suspected bot answers and seven responses about children outside of the target age range removed. Parents were predominantly from the United States (n = 79, 45%) or Australia (n = 49, 28%), and most children had their toe walking described by a medical professional as idiopathic toe walking (ITW; n = 93, 50%), followed by toe walking related to ASD (n = 42, 22%).

There were 90 (48%) parents reporting their children experienced pain in the past 4 weeks, with 85 (94% of 90) attributing the pain to toe walking. Notably, parents reported that this was not fully reflected in medical care professionals' advice, with 73 parents (81% of 90) reporting that medical professionals linked the pain to the toe walking gait. Parents reported that the most affected body regions were the lower legs (n = 65, 72%) and ankles/feet (n = 63, 70%). Children within the ASD cohort had the highest proportion reporting pain (n = 24, 57% of 42 responses), followed by children in the ITW cohort (n = 43, 45% of 95 responses).

Table 2 provides details on the SSP results across the whole cohort and subgroups, and Figure 1 illustrates sensory processing difficulties compared to those of typically developing children. The omitted SSP item described in the methods affected 58 participants. For 39 of these, the missing response did not alter the final SSP classification, and their scores were retained. For the remaining 19, the missing data potentially changed the classification, and these scores were excluded from the SSP analysis only. Excluding these 19 participants did not materially alter the distribution of SSP classifications or the overall pattern of results. Be, it is unlikely this exclusion would have introduced bias because exclusion only depended on whether the missing item could have changed the score. Sensory processing differences were frequently reported among children who toe walked in these responses, with 72% of children, regardless of the reason for toe walking, classified as showing probable or definite differences in sensory processing compared with normative data. These differences were particularly pronounced among children with ASD, where 98% displayed atypical sensory profiles. There were no statistically significant differences in SSP total classification between children who experienced pain and those who did not experience pain across any subgroup (p > 0.05).

Discussion

This study provides new insights into the potential relationship between sensory processing differences, pain and the impact of both on daily activity in children who toe walk. Sensory processing differences varied across subgroups in most children with toe walking, at a higher rate than what is commonly reported in the general population [24]. This indicates that altered sensory processing is a frequently reported feature in many children who toe walk, but this was particularly present in children with ASD. This aligns with previous knowledge about ASD [26]. A novel finding was that nearly half of all children, regardless of the reason for toe walking, were reported by parents to experience pain. Parents also reported that medical professionals often linked this pain with toe walking, and that for the children experiencing pain, the pain had a substantial impact on their lives. Previous studies report associations between toe walking gait, muscle weakness, and altered muscle length with or without the presence of sensory processing challenges or pain [15]. As such, it is feasible to consider gait alterations disrupting lower limb load patterns, leading to increased strain on soft tissues that are not typically subjected to high stress. These biomechanical deviations have already been linked to pain in children with Cerebral Palsy who toe walk, making it plausible that comparable mechanisms may contribute to pain in children who toe walk for other reasons [27]. For instance, musculoskeletal strain from prolonged plantarflexion during walking may result in nociceptive pain associated with tissue overload, fatigue, or joint stress. Repeated loading through the gastrocnemius–soleus complex may also create cumulative strain, which would be consistent with a nociceptive contribution, although the present design cannot confirm this.

In our study, we asked questions about pain and its impact on activities, with pain most frequently reported during activities involving lower‐limb loading, such as running and sports. One hypothesis consistent with this pattern is an activity‐related nociceptive contribution. However, our cross‐sectional study design with parent‐reported data cannot establish the underlying mechanism. Similarly, the mediation analysis was undertaken as an exploratory examination of the relationship among sensory processing as categorised by the SSP and pain impact scores. These analyses should not be interpreted as causal mediation or mechanistic pathways. Instead, this analysis was intended to identify potential associations, potentially informing future research. Although pain intensity and quality of life were not directly examined, the interference of pain with physical and social activities highlights its functional impact. It is therefore plausible that the discomfort experienced by children who toe walk is closely linked to biomechanical demands rather than to central pain processes or to any perception of pain due to sensory processing challenges. Nonetheless, the possibility of overlapping mechanisms explaining pain cannot be ruled out, particularly in children with co‐occurring neurodevelopmental conditions or sensory differences.

Sensory or central processing differences may contribute to a nociplastic component of pain sensation. Children with sensory processing challenges may perceive and experience pain differently. A recent study of adolescents with chronic pain found that children who showed heightened sensory responsiveness were more impacted by pain [28]. Although sensory hypersensitivity was not explicitly measured in the current study, the high prevalence of sensory processing differences among children who toe walk, especially those diagnosed with ASD or ITW, may point to similar mechanisms contributing to these issues. Given the high prevalence of sensory processing differences among children with idiopathic toe walking and those with ASD, it remains possible that sensory hypersensitivity further amplifies their experience of pain. This should be an area of future research.

Our study was limited by its survey design and reliance on parent‐reported diagnosis and data. Caution should be applied to our findings due to recall bias and potential inaccuracies from proxy reporting, especially regarding pain location, intensity, and its perceived relationship to toe walking gait [17]. Second, our use of an online convenience sample may introduce selection bias, as participation was limited to families with internet access, English literacy, and a willingness to complete a relatively lengthy survey. Third, online surveys of this kind carry reporting bias. Recall bias, social desirability bias, and self‐selection by more engaged or concerned families all influence data. It is hard to quantify the impact of this and the absence of clinical verification makes attributing symptoms such as pain to toe walking a reflection of a parent's interpretation. As our data came entirely from parents, our pain and activity‐limitation scores reflect what parents perceive rather than what children experienced. Finally, we acknowledge that the CALI‐9 was validated in children aged 8–18, so the youngest children in our cohort fell below its validated range. We judged the CALI‐9 the most suitable available measure of pain‐related activity limitation, and because we used it descriptively rather than comparing scores against normative data, this mismatch is unlikely to have biased our findings. It nonetheless means results for children under 8 should be read with caution, and future work should establish the performance of activity‐limitation measures across the full paediatric age range relevant to toe walking.

Longitudinal studies incorporating clinician‐assessed gait, sensory profiles, and pain metrics are needed to validate further and extend these findings. Our study also highlights the potential for exploring larger populations of children who toe walk and experience pain as a broader cohort to understand the similarities and differences based on the potential mechanisms for gait initiation. It also highlights that pain experience should be a feature of the assessment of children who toe walk, potentially prompting earlier treatment initiation. Our research also contributes to the growing body of research exploring the relationship between toe walking gait and sensory processing challenges. Importantly, this study is the first to examine the frequency and functional impact of parent‐reported pain in children who toe walk using a validated activity limitation measure (CALI‐9). Given that nearly half of the children in the sample reportedly experienced pain, most of whom had pain attributed to toe walking by both parents and medical professionals, pain assessment should be a routine component of clinical management.

Conclusion

This study suggests that both sensory processing differences and pain were frequently reported and may be clinically relevant among children who toe walk. These findings underscore the importance of incorporating both sensory and pain assessments into routine clinical evaluation. Recognising and addressing these domains may facilitate more targeted interventions and ultimately improve participation and quality of life for children who toe walk. Further research is needed to validate these findings through longitudinal studies that incorporate clinician‐assessed gait and objective sensory.
