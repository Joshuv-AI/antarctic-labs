Comparative efficacy of different treatment modalities in the management of macular oedema in retinitis pigmentosa: a systematic review and network meta-analysis
Authors: Hsi-An Yang, Wun-Long Jheng, Yih-Shiou Hwang, Wei-Chi Wu, Chi-Chun Lai, Hung-Da Chou, Eugene Yu-Chuan Kang, Laura Liu, An-Ning Chao, Yen-Ting Chen, Kuan-Jen Chen, Yi-Hsing Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo compare the efficacy of current treatments for retinitis pigmentosa (RP)-associated macular oedema using network meta-analysis (NMA).Methods and analysisElectronic databases were searched for studies reporting changes in best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) and central macular thickness (CMT). A frequentist NMA was performed and treatment hierarchies were estimated using the surface under the cumulative ranking curve.Results12 studies were included to compare the following: oral acetazolamide 500 mg/day, oral methazolamide 50–100 mg/day, topical dorzolamide 2% three times daily, topical ketorolac 0.5% four times daily, intravenous umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells (UCMSCs; single administration of 3×10⁶ cells), intravitreal dexamethasone implant (DEXi) 0.7 mg, intravitreal triamcinolone acetonide (IVTA) 4 mg, sub-Tenon TA (STTA) 20 mg, intravitreal bevacizumab 1.25 mg and intravitreal ranibizumab 0.5 mg. Regarding changes in BCVA at 3–4 months, oral carbonic anhydrase inhibitors (CAIs) showed greater BCVA improvement compared with anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (anti-VEGF) therapies; however, no intervention demonstrated statistically significant superiority in BCVA at 12 months. At 3–4 months, DEXi, acetazolamide, methazolamide and dorzolamide demonstrated comparable CMT reduction and outperformed anti-VEGF therapies, IVTA, STTA, intravenous UCMSC and control. By 12 months, dorzolamide (vs acetazolamide: −169.50 µm (95% CI −331.83 to −7.17) and vs methazolamide: −185.49 µm (95% CI −354.35 to −16.63)) and DEXi (vs acetazolamide: −147.00 µm (95% CI −206.58 to −87.42) and vs methazolamide: −162.99 µm (95% CI −238.56 to −87.42)) provided significantly greater CMT reduction than acetazolamide and methazolamide.ConclusionsCAIs and DEXi demonstrated the greatest long-term anatomical benefit for RP-associated macular oedema, whereas durable BCVA gains remained limited across available therapies. While oral and topical CAIs showed comparable short-term efficacy, topical dorzolamide and DEXi provided greater long-term CMT reduction than oral CAIs. Given the limited number of studies and small sample sizes, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and validated in larger randomised controlled trials.Keywords: Drugs, Dystrophy, Treatment Medical, Vision

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

Macular oedema is a common complication of retinitis pigmentosa (RP), and carbonic anhydrase inhibitors (CAIs) are currently considered the first-line treatment. Recently, multiple treatment options, including corticosteroids, anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (anti-VEGF) agents, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and stem cell therapies, have shown potential benefits for RP-associated macular oedema in small and heterogeneous studies. However, the comparative efficacy of these treatment modalities remains unclear, and an evidence-based consensus on optimal management is lacking.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

This network meta-analysis provides a comprehensive comparison of currently available therapies for RP-associated macular oedema. CAIs and intravitreal dexamethasone implant (DEXi) demonstrated superior efficacy in reducing central macular thickness (CMT) compared with anti-VEGF agents and several other interventions. Furthermore, topical dorzolamide and DEXi provided greater long-term CMT reduction compared with oral CAIs.

HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY

Our findings suggest that topical dorzolamide may serve as a pragmatic first-line option for RP-associated macular oedema. DEXi represents a reasonable alternative for patients with inadequate response or recurrence during CAI therapy. By contrast, anti-VEGF agents appear to have limited overall benefit and may be best reserved for selected cases rather than used routinely. The results also highlight the need for well-designed prospective trials to clarify long-term visual outcomes.

Introduction

Retinitis pigmentosa (RP) is an inherited retinal dystrophy characterised by progressive degeneration of photoreceptors.1 2 Patients typically present with night blindness, followed by progressive constriction of the visual field and, ultimately, central vision loss and complete blindness.2 Cystoid macular oedema (CME) may develop at any stage of the disease and can further compromise visual acuity.3 The reported prevalence of CME in RP varies widely, ranging from 10% to 50% of affected eyes.4 Although the association between macular oedema and RP has long been recognised, the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms of macular oedema in RP remain incompletely understood.5 6 Therefore, the optimal therapeutic strategy for managing RP-associated macular oedema remains controversial.6

In current clinical practice, carbonic anhydrase inhibitors (CAIs) are regarded as first-line therapy for RP-associated macular oedema due to their well-documented efficacy and safety profile.7 8 For patients with recurrence or intolerance to CAIs, intravitreal anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (anti-VEGF) agents and corticosteroids have been reported as alternative options.4 7 Additional treatment modalities, including topical non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and stem cell-based therapies, have also been investigated, although their relative efficacy remains inadequately defined.6 9 These heterogeneous findings highlight the need for a systematic comparative evaluation of available treatments for RP-associated macular oedema.

Network meta-analysis (NMA) allows simultaneous comparison of multiple interventions by integrating direct and indirect evidence within a single analytical framework.10 To date, no NMA has systematically examined the comparative efficacy of available therapeutic modalities for RP-associated macular oedema. Accordingly, the present NMA study aims to comprehensively evaluate and rank the treatment effects of current available therapies for RP-associated macular oedema.

Materials and methods

Database and search strategy

This systematic review and NMA followed Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines and was registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251267707). PubMed, Web of Science and the Cochrane Library were searched from inception to December 2025 without restrictions on publication date. The search strategy is presented in online supplemental table 1.

Eligibility criteria

Studies were considered eligible if they were randomised or non-randomised interventional studies in RP-associated macular oedema reporting best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) and/or central macular thickness (CMT) outcomes at prespecified follow-up intervals. For BCVA outcomes, studies reporting results at 2–4 weeks were analysed using the proportion of patients achieving a ≥5- Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study (ETDRS)-letter improvement in BCVA, whereas studies reporting outcomes at 3–4 months, 6 months and 12 months were analysed using mean changes in BCVA measured in Logarithm of the Minimum Angle of Resolution (logMAR) units, according to the outcome measures available in the original studies. Studies evaluating interventions other than the following were excluded: control (placebo or no treatment), oral acetazolamide 500 mg/day, oral methazolamide 50–100 mg/day, topical dorzolamide 2% three times daily, topical ketorolac 0.5% four times daily, intravenous umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells (UCMSC; single administration of 3×10⁶ cells), intravitreal dexamethasone implant (DEXi) 0.7 mg, intravitreal triamcinolone acetonide (IVTA) 4 mg, sub-Tenon TA (STTA) 20 mg, intravitreal bevacizumab (IVB) 1.25 mg and intravitreal ranibizumab (IVR) 0.5 mg. Intervention nodes were defined according to the specific treatment and route of administration, with standard doses specified when available. Given the limited number of eligible studies, variations in treatment protocols, including treatment duration, injection frequency and retreatment criteria, were not used to further subdivide treatment nodes. Detailed treatment regimens for individual studies are provided in online supplemental table 3. The detailed eligibility criteria are summarised in online supplemental table 2.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Study characteristics and outcome data were extracted. The endpoints of the present study include CMT reduction and improvement in BCVA. The methodological quality of randomised controlled trials (RCTs) was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool (V.2.0).11 For non-randomised studies, the ROBINS-I tool was applied.12 Discrepancies in assessments were resolved through discussion until consensus was achieved, with final endorsement by the corresponding author.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using MetaInsight (V.6.4.0), a web-based platform for NMA developed by Owen et al.13 A frequentist framework with a random-effects model was adopted. ORs and mean differences (MDs) with 95% CIs were calculated to estimate comparative efficacy across follow-up timepoints. To provide a probabilistic ranking of interventions for CMT reduction, the surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA) was calculated for each treatment. SUCRA values range from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating a greater likelihood that an intervention ranks among the most effective options for the outcome of interest.

Results

Study inclusion and characteristics

Of 295 records identified, 12 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the NMA study. These studies include four RCTs, five prospective comparative studies, one prospective interventional case series, one retrospective comparative cohort study and one retrospective non-comparative cohort study (figure 1).14–25

BCVA outcomes at different follow-up intervals

≥5-ETDRS-letter improvement in BCVA at 2–4 weeks

Four studies reported the proportion of patients achieving at least a 5-ETDRS-letter improvement in BCVA at 2–4 weeks.19 21 22 25 A total of 86 eyes were included. Four interventions were evaluated: control, oral acetazolamide, oral methazolamide and topical dorzolamide. The corresponding network plot is presented in online supplemental figure 3A.

A forest plot depicting the ORs for achieving a ≥5-letter improvement in BCVA at 2–4 weeks, relative to the control group, is presented in online supplemental figure 3B. Both oral acetazolamide and oral methazolamide demonstrated superior efficacy in improving BCVA compared with the control, whereas topical dorzolamide did not show a statistically significant difference from the control. The league table of NMA estimates is shown below the leading diagonal in online supplemental figure 3C. As illustrated, no significant difference was detected between oral acetazolamide and oral methazolamide with respect to BCVA improvement. A sensitivity analysis restricted to RCTs was also performed (online supplemental figure 3D–F). After exclusion of the non-randomised study evaluating topical dorzolamide, both oral acetazolamide and oral methazolamide remained significantly superior to the control group (OR for acetazolamide vs control: 4.41 (95% CI 1.17 to 16.62) and OR for methazolamide vs control: 25.71 (95% CI 1.43 to 462.31)). No statistically significant difference was observed between oral acetazolamide and oral methazolamide.

The average number of injections increased to 1.6 for IVR, 1.9 for IVB and remained 1.0 for IVTA at 6 months. DEXi, oral CAIs and topical dorzolamide achieved superior CMT reduction at 3–4 months

The forest plot (figure 3B) of the CMT change at 3–4 months demonstrated that all evaluated interventions, compared with control, exhibited statistically significant reductions in CMT, including IVTA or STTA, topical ketorolac, topical dorzolamide, IVR, IVB, oral acetazolamide, oral methazolamide, DEXi and UCMSC (figure 3B). The league table derived from the NMA (figure 3C) revealed that DEXi, oral acetazolamide, oral methazolamide and topical dorzolamide achieved significantly greater CMT reduction than IVB, IVR, TA, UCMSC and control. Notably, no statistically significant differences were observed among DEXi, oral acetazolamide, oral methazolamide and topical dorzolamide (figure 3C).

Oral acetazolamide demonstrated the greatest 6-month CMT reduction

The forest plot illustrating the change in CMT from baseline to 6 months is presented in figure 3E. At this time point, oral acetazolamide, oral methazolamide, IVTA or STTA, UCMSC, IVB and IVR all demonstrated significantly greater CMT reduction compared with the control group (figure 3E). Estimates from the NMA at 6 months are presented below the leading diagonal in figure 3F. These results revealed that oral acetazolamide achieved significantly greater CMT reduction than oral methazolamide, IVB, IVR, UCMSC, IVTA, STTA and the control group. No statistically significant differences were observed among IVB, IVR, intravenous UCMSC, IVTA, STTA at 6 months (MD for acetazolamide vs methazolamide: −93.03 (95% CI −140.97 to −45.09); MD for acetazolamide vs IVB: −126.84 (95% CI −173.97 to −79.71); MD for acetazolamide vs IVR: −138.66 (95% CI −177.94 to −99.38); MD for acetazolamide vs UCMSC: −160.32 (95% CI −253.33 to −67.31) and MD for acetazolamide vs IVTA/STTA: −179.66 (95% CI −266.89 to −92.43), figure 3F).

Topical dorzolamide and DEXi exhibited superior 12-month CMT reduction

At 12 months, both the DEXi and topical dorzolamide achieved significantly greater CMT reduction compared with oral acetazolamide (figure 3H). In contrast, no statistically significant differences were observed between oral acetazolamide and oral methazolamide, IVB, IVR or topical ketorolac (MD for DEXi vs acetazolamide: −147.00 (95% CI −206.58 to −87.42) and MD for dorzolamide vs acetazolamide: −169.50 (95% CI −331.83 to −7.17), figure 3H). NMA estimates at 12 months presented that topical dorzolamide and DEXi provided comparable efficacy, with no significant difference between the two interventions. Importantly, both treatments showed superior CMT reduction when compared with oral acetazolamide, oral methazolamide, IVB and IVR (figure 3I).

SUCRA curve analysis across follow-up time points

SUCRA curve analyses were performed in MetaInsight to estimate the probability-based ranking of each intervention for CMT reduction at 3–4 months, 6 months and 12 months (figure 4A–C). At 3–4 months, the SUCRA-based hierarchy was: DEXi (80.2%)>oral acetazolamide (70.2%)>topical dorzolamide (65.0%)>oral methazolamide (62.8%)>topical ketorolac (59.5%)>IVB (45.4%)>IVR (41.5%)>IVTA/STTA (39.0%)>UCMSC (28.5%)>control (7.9%). At 12 months, topical dorzolamide demonstrated the highest probability of being the most effective intervention (SUCRA: 77.4%), closely followed by DEXi (75.9%) and topical ketorolac (73.9%), whereas oral acetazolamide (35.8%), IVB (29.5%), oral methazolamide (29.3%) and IVR (28.2%) showed comparatively lower probabilities of efficacy.

Discussion

The present study demonstrated that at 3–4 months, DEXi, CAIs (oral and topical) and topical ketorolac yielded comparable reductions in CMT. DEXi and CAIs ranked higher than UCMSC, IVTA, STTA and intravitreal anti-VEGF agents. By 12 months, topical dorzolamide and DEXi demonstrated greater and more sustained anatomical benefit than oral CAIs and anti-VEGF agents. Regarding visual outcomes, oral CAIs produced greater BCVA improvement than control at 3–4 months; however, no intervention showed statistically significant superiority for BCVA at 12 months. Taken together, these findings suggest that CAIs and DEXi play central roles in anatomical improvement, whereas durable gains in BCVA remain limited across currently available therapies.

The underlying pathogenesis of RP-associated macular oedema remains unclear. However, prior studies have proposed that impaired fluid transport by the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) and Müller cell dysregulation are two of the key contributors.4 Arrigo et al reported that RP-associated CME predominantly localises to the inner nuclear layer, a region enriched in Müller cell bodies.26 They further observed higher intraretinal vessel density and greater choroidal thickness in oedematous versus non-oedematous eyes.26 These findings suggest disruption of retinal–choroidal fluid exchange rather than predominantly VEGF-driven vascular leakage.26 Carbonic anhydrase isoenzymes are expressed in both the RPE and Müller cells, and inhibition of these enzymes can acidify the subretinal space, thereby promoting fluid movement from the retina towards the choroid.27 These mechanisms may explain the significant CMT improvement observed with both oral and topical CAIs in the present study. Notably, the time-dependent performance of oral versus topical CAIs in our analysis is broadly concordant with prior reports. In a double-masked crossover study, Grover et al found oral acetazolamide to be more effective than topical dorzolamide over 4 weeks.19 By contrast, Liew et al suggested that topical dorzolamide achieved greater CMT reduction after a median follow-up of 3–4 months.20 Currently, the comparative long-term efficacy between oral and topical CAIs has not been well established. The time-dependent divergence in treatment efficacy between oral and topical CAIs observed in our study may be attributable to several factors. First, oral CAIs are often limited by systemic side effects, including paraesthesia, dysgeusia and gastrointestinal discomfort, which may necessitate dose reduction or discontinuation, ultimately limiting sustained efficacy.27 Stewart et al demonstrated that oral acetazolamide had a significantly higher incidence of systemic adverse events and discontinuations compared with topical dorzolamide.28 In contrast, topical CAIs minimise systemic exposure and are generally well tolerated, facilitating sustained use.29 30 Second, preclinical pharmacokinetic data suggest that topical dorzolamide can penetrate posterior segment tissues and accumulate after repeated dosing.31 32 This pharmacokinetic advantage may contribute to the sustained anatomical improvement observed in our analysis. Consistent with this interpretation, Ikeda et al demonstrated that a prolonged (>1 year) use of topical dorzolamide maintained therapeutic efficacy in RP-associated macular oedema, proposing it as a first-choice treatment for long-term management.33 Collectively, these findings support topical dorzolamide as a practical option for sustained management of RP-associated macular oedema.

Beyond fluid transport dysfunction, chronic intraocular inflammation may also contribute to RP-associated macular oedema.34 35 Elevated aqueous levels of inflammatory mediators, including Interleukin 23 (IL-23), Chemokine (C-C motif) ligand 1 (CCL1), C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 1 (CXCL1), have been reported in RP eyes with CME compared with those without CME.34 This inflammatory milieu provides a biologically plausible rationale for steroid therapy. Corticosteroids can stabilise the blood–retinal barrier and suppress inflammatory cascades that sustain fluid retention within the macula.36 In our analysis, 0.7 mg DEXi did not differ significantly from topical dorzolamide in CMT reduction at 3–4 months or 12 months. This finding may reflect partial mechanistic overlap between CAIs and corticosteroids, including modulation of Müller cell-mediated fluid regulation.36 A study conducted by Colombo et al observed that while the 0.7 mg DEXi led to a more rapid and pronounced reduction in CMT in patients with RP at early time points (months 1 and 7), the difference in CMT reduction between the DEXi and topical dorzolamide was not significant at month 12, which is consistent with the results of our study.23 Given the favourable safety profile of topical dorzolamide compared with intravitreal corticosteroids, which carry risks of ocular hypertension and cataract progression, topical CAIs may represent a pragmatic first-line option for long-term management.37 38

Regarding visual acuity outcomes, our NMA revealed that oral CAIs demonstrated superior BCVA improvement compared with the control group at 3–4 months, but no intervention demonstrated significant superiority in BCVA at 12 months. This dissociation between anatomical and functional outcomes likely reflects the underlying photoreceptor degeneration characteristic of RP and may be attributable to several factors. First, BCVA in RP is more tightly linked to the integrity of the photoreceptor layer, particularly the ellipsoid zone (EZ), rather than by macular thickness per se.39 Sousa et al found that the presence of an intact EZ was the most significant predictor of BCVA in patients with RP.40 The underlying structural damage to the EZ may limit the potential for visual recovery even when macular oedema is successfully resolved.41 Second, prolonged CME may cause cumulative damage to photoreceptors that is not reversible on oedema resolution.42 Kitahata et al demonstrated that patients with RP with previous or current CME exhibited significantly reduced photoreceptor density and regularity compared with those without CME, and longer CME duration was associated with greater photoreceptor loss.42 Third, intraretinal cysts may cause mechanical stress and axonal compression, contributing to visual impairment independent of photoreceptor status.41 Collectively, these observations support early intervention to maximise the window for functional recovery, yet also reflect the limited capacity of oedema-targeted therapies to overcome underlying photoreceptor degeneration.

Several limitations should be acknowledged in the present study. First, the number of available studies and total sample size were limited, reflecting the rarity of RP-associated macular oedema and potentially increasing uncertainty in treatment rankings. In addition, sample sizes varied considerably across treatment groups, with some interventions being represented by relatively few eyes. Given the substantial interindividual variability in treatment response known to occur in RP-associated macular oedema, some estimates, particularly those for long-term outcomes, were associated with wide CIs and should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Second, the included evidence consisted of heterogeneous study designs, including RCTs, prospective comparative studies and retrospective cohorts, with non-randomised studies comprising the majority of the available evidence. Differences in baseline characteristics and disease stage across studies may have introduced additional heterogeneity and potentially affected the validity of indirect comparisons. Although established risk-of-bias tools and random-effects models were applied, residual confounding and selection bias cannot be fully excluded. Third, variations in treatment protocols, such as injection frequency, treatment duration and retreatment criteria, may have influenced outcomes and reduced cross-study comparability. Fourth, outcome assessment was primarily based on CMT and BCVA; however, RP-relevant functional measures such as central 10° visual field sensitivity and microperimetric macular sensitivity were insufficiently reported and thus could not be analysed in the present study. Accordingly, our findings should be interpreted cautiously, and larger, well-designed RCTs with standardised protocols and broader functional endpoints are warranted.

Conclusions

In conclusion, oral CAIs provide short-term anatomical improvement and modest early BCVA gains in RP-associated macular oedema, whereas topical dorzolamide and DEXi are associated with more sustained CMT reduction. Topical dorzolamide may serve as a pragmatic first-line long-term option, with DEXi reserved for inadequate response or recurrence. Anti-VEGF agents appear to have limited overall benefit and may be best reserved for selected cases. Future well-designed RCTs with standardised protocols and broader RP-relevant functional endpoints are warranted to confirm these findings.
