 ABSTRACT

 The SAM code is under development and supported by DOE-NE’s Nuclear Energy Advanced
Modeling and Simulation program as a modern system-level modeling and simulation tool for
advanced non-light water reactor safety analyses. These advanced reactor concepts incorporate
novel and improved approaches to achieve safety and economic feasibility. This report summa-
rizes recent efforts and progress in addressing the code capability gaps in SAM for the modeling
of the air-ingress phenomenon in High-Temperature Gas-Cooled Reactors (HTGRs). The capabil-
ity enhancements implemented to the code include: a multi-component flow model to capture the
air-helium mixture during air ingress, a 0-D graphite oxidation model to capture the reaction of
graphite with oxygen in the air, and an isentropic critical flow model to more accurately predict the
de-pressurization of the reactor system due to a small break in the primary loop. Verification cases
and demonstrations are provided to showcase these capabilities in SAM. Existing gaps in the code’s
capability are also identified.

1 Introduction
 High-Temperature Gas-Cooled Reactor (HTGR) is one of the candidates for the Gen-IV reactor
designs that is currently being developed worldwide. There are two typical designs of HTGRs,
namely the pebble-bed type and the prismatic type. The pebble-bed type HTGR has a core filled
with pebble fuels containing tristructural isotropic (TRISO) particles. Meanwhile, in the prismatic
type HTGR, TRISO particles are packed into pellet forms and turned into long fuel rods that are
then arranged in some forms of hexagonal patterns in the core. In both designs of HTGR, helium
is widely used as the coolant due to it being chemically inert, its lower neutron absorption cross
section, and its relatively high specific heat capacity. However, due to its low density, HTGRs are
pressurized to increase the density of helium, enhancing its ability to remove heat from the fuel.
 The use of pressurized helium in HTGRs presents some unique challenges, especially during
accident scenarios. For example, in the event of a de-pressurized loss of forced cooling (DLOFC)
accident where the core is de-pressurized, the system pressure may drop significantly that the low
density of helium may hinder the formation of natural circulation within the core. With the absence
of natural circulation, conduction and thermal radiation will be the dominant mechanisms to allow
heat to escape from the core to the decay heat removal system. In this scenario, fuel temperature
may rise significantly above the nominal value during normal operating condition and risk being
damaged. Furthermore, the evacuation of helium may cause air from the reactor cavity to enter the
core. During this so-called air-ingress scenario, graphite in the pebble fuels and the reflectors in
the core may undergo oxidation by reacting with the oxygen in the air. This may compromise the
integrity of the graphite and release fission products from the fuel pebbles into the reactor cavity
or weaken the graphite structures located under the pebble bed. The importance of the air ingress
scenario is evident as it is one of the scenarios whose safety related phenomena are identified using
the Phenomena Identification and Ranking Table (PIRT) process for the High-Temperature Gas
Cooled Reactor–Pebble-Bed Module (HTR-PM) [1]. Air ingress following a primary system breach
is considered one of the most significant postulated accidents for safety analysis of HTGRs.
 Air ingress can occur as a consequence of a DLOFC event, where a loss of coolant inventory is
followed by a loss of confinement integrity (e.g., a break in the vessel or connecting piping). The
process typically unfolds in three stages:
 1. De-pressurization and Initial Gas Exchange – Helium coolant rapidly vents to the contain-
ment or environment, creating a pressure differential that allows external air to enter the primary
circuit.
 2. Density-Driven and Natural Circulation Flow – The higher density of air relative to helium
promotes buoyancy-driven mixing and stratification, leading to gradual air replacement in the lower
regions of the reactor.
 3. Graphite Oxidation and Core Heat-Up – Oxygen transported into the core reacts with
graphite structural components and fuel element blocks, producing CO and CO2 . Oxidation is
exothermic and can lead to material loss, weakening of structural integrity, and potential reconfigu-
ration of the flow field.
 The safety importance of air-ingress phenomena has motivated decades of experimental and
modeling work. Key experimental campaigns have been carried out to investigate the fundamental
physics. A variety of computational approaches and tools have been used or developed by many
researchers to investigate the effects of air-ingress in HTGRs, ranging from system-level codes such
as TINTE, SPECTRA, and GAMMA to higher fidelity Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) codes

 1

[2–9]. The multiphysics nature of the air ingress scenario that combines thermal fluids phenomena
with chemical reactions presents substantial challenges to these computational tools. Furthermore,
there are also difficulties due to the difference in the time scales of the mechanisms with which air
enters the core. Significant gaps remain in predicting coupled multi-physics behavior across scales,
particularly for the transition from molecular diffusion–dominated ingress to buoyancy-driven con-
vection, and for oxidation effects on flow patterns and structural integrity.
 The System Analysis Module (SAM) code [10] is under development and supported by DOE-
NE’s Nuclear Energy Advanced Modeling and Simulation (NEAMS) program as a modern system-
level modeling and simulation tool for advanced non-light water reactor safety analyses. The SAM
code capabilities for HTGR applications have been extensively demonstrated in the simulations of
various HTGR designs, such as MHTGR-350 (General Atomics) [11], a generic pebble bed gas-
cooled reactor [12, 13], the HTR-PM [14], Holos Quad (Holos Gen) [15], and Horizontal-Compact
HTGR (MIT/Boston Atomics) [16], under various transient conditions. However, it still lacks the
capabilities to model the air ingress events of HTGRs.
 This work documents the progress made to the SAM code to model the air ingress event. In the
coming sections, the mechanisms and progression of air ingress are described in details, followed
by a review of past experimental and modeling studies. Next, the capabilities that need to be imple-
mented in SAM to allow the code to model the air ingress scenario are discussed. After that, recent
enhancements and improvements implemented to SAM are presented, along with demonstrations
of these capabilities. Finally, a summary is provided and the future steps are discussed.

2 Air Ingress and Modeling Needs
 In this chapter, the air ingress scenario in High-Temperature Gas-Cooled Reactor (HTGR) is
discussed in details. A review of previous modeling work is provided. Furthermore, the capabil-
ities that need to be implemented in SAM to allow the code to model the air ingress scenario are
discussed.

2.1 Literature Review
 A loss-of-forced-cooling (LOFC) accident is considered a critical event for a High-Temperature
Gas-Cooled Reactor (HTGR). Generally, there are two type of LOFC accidents, namely the pres-
surized loss of forced cooling (PLOFC) and the de-pressurized loss of forced cooling (DLOFC).
During PLOFC, the reactor is scrammed and the helium blower is tripped but the primary circuit
of the reactor remains intact. Some possible initiating events that could lead to PLOFC include
the inadvertent withdrawal of a control rod, uncontrolled acceleration of the helium blower, loss of
offsite power, and loss of feed water [1]. Natural circulation can establish by the pressurized helium
during PLOFC, which can help the removal of decay heat from the core to the reactor cavity cool-
ing system (RCCS). The maximum fuel temperature is typically not a concern during PLOFC as it
is postulated to stay far below the safety limit due to the enhanced heat removal from the natural
circulation in the core.
 On the other hand, the DLOFC is typically characterized by a rapid de-pressurization of the
core and the primary circuit followed by an emergency shutdown of the reactor. Possible initiating
events include un-isolatable breaks of the pressure boundary such as an instrumentation tube. Due
to low helium density in the de-pressurized system, natural circulation cannot establish, which leads
to poorer decay heat redistribution and removal than the PLOFC. Thermal radiation and conduction
are the dominant decay heat removal mechanism during DLOFC. An extreme form of DLOFC
originates from the so-called double ended guillotine break (DEGB) at the horizontal co-axial duct
of the core, which is considered a Beyond Design Basis Accident (BDBA) in HTGRs [17]. In this
scenario, the rapid de-pressurization of the system is followed by the ingress of air into the core first
through molecular diffusion and then buoyancy effect. Eventually, if the buoyancy force surpasses
the pressure drop of the core, a stable natural circulation is established. The ingress of air into the
core can possibly lead to the oxidation of graphite which can compromise the integrity of the fuel
and release fission products into the environment. At the same time, the lower graphite structures
and reflectors can also undergo oxidation that can potentially challenge the structural integrity of
the core.
 The air ingress event in HTGRs is described in detail by Oh and Kim [18] and is illustrated in
Figure 2.1. Following the DEGB, the core is rapidly de-pressurized and helium is discharged from
the core. After de-pressurization, air in the containment enters the reactor vessel through the bottom
part of the horizontal co-axial pipe forming a stratified flow due to the difference in air and helium
densities. At this stage, molecular diffusion due to the difference in air concentration inside and
outside the reactor can also play a role in introducing air into the bottom plenum. However, several
studies compared the time scale of stratified flow to the time scale of molecular diffusion and found
that the latter had negligible effects at this stage of the transient [5, 18].
 After the bottom plenum of the reactor is filled with air, the temperature difference between the
air inside and outside the vessel results in a density gradient that in turn drives a counter-current flow

 3

in the bottom plenum. At this stage, the counter-current flow is non-stop because there is always a
temperature difference between the air inside and outside of the vessel. At the same time, due to
heating in the lower plenum, some of the air from the counter-current flow will flow into the reactor
core if the buoyancy force in the lower plenum is sufficient to overcome the hydrostatic head and
frictional pressure drop in the core, thus initiating the onset of natural circulation. Conversely, if the
buoyancy force is insufficient to overcome the hydrostatic head and core frictional pressure drop, the
flow of air from the lower plenum to the core will be dominated by molecular diffusion. In the study
by Oh and Kim [18] comparing buoyancy force to hydrostatic head, it was found that the flow of air
into the prismatic core of the 600 MWth Modular High Temperature Gas-Cooled Reactor (MHTGR)
was dominated by buoyancy force; whereas in the Naturzug im Core mit Korrosion (NACOK) test
facility molecular diffusion was the dominant mechanism due to insufficient buoyancy force from
the air in the lower plenum to overcome the hydrostatic head of the core. Liu et al. [5] performed
similar calculation for the HTR-PM and found that the flow of air into the pebble bed core was
dominated by molecular diffusion. These studies highlight that the design of the core plays an
important role in determining the dominant mechanism for air to flow from the lower plenum into
the core during an air ingress scenario. Finally, as air continues to flow into the core, and the density
difference between the gas mixture in the core and the riser channels eventually grows enough to
provide sufficient buoyancy force to generate a stable natural circulation, a massive air-ingress is
initiated.

 Once air enters the lower plenum and the core, oxidation occurs between the oxygen in the air
and the graphite pebbles and structures. Oxidation is essentially a two-step process that involves
the diffusion of oxidizing gas into the graphite and the chemical reactions between the gas and the
graphite. Several possible chemical reactions are listed as Eqs. 2.1 to 2.4.

                                          2C + O2 → 2CO                                           (2.2)

                                         2CO + O2 → 2CO2                                          (2.4)
 The graphite oxidation rate is a strong function of temperature [17]. Generally, the oxidation of
graphite can be divided into three temperature-dependent regimes, namely 1) the chemical regime
for temperature below 500 ◦ C, 2) the in-pore diffusion controlled regime for temperature ranging
from 500 - 900 ◦ C, and 3) the boundary later diffusion controlled regime for temperature higher than
900 ◦ C [19]. In the chemical regime, the chemical reaction rate between the graphite and reaction
gas is relatively low, which allows the oxidizing gas to penetrate deep into the graphite. Due to
the uniform concentration of reaction gas in the graphite, the oxidation rate tends to be relatively
uniform across the depth of the graphite. The oxidation rate in this regime is mainly controlled
by the chemical reaction rate, and the oxidation rate is shown to increase with temperature. In the
in-pore diffusion controlled regime, the chemical reaction rate is faster but the gas can still diffuse
into the graphite. The reaction rate is determined by the diffusion rate of the oxidizing gas into the
graphite and the chemical reaction rate. This means that deeper in the graphite the reaction rate
is slower due to a lower concentration of oxidizing gas. In the boundary later diffusion controlled
regime, the chemical reaction rate of graphite and oxidizing gas is fast, which hinders the diffusion
of oxidizing gas into the graphite. As a result, the reaction occurs only near the surface of the
graphite with almost no reaction deep inside the graphite. Given the fast chemical reaction rate,
the oxidation rate in this regime is mainly dictated by the rate of diffusion of oxidizing gas into
the graphite. Factors affecting the diffusion rate and chemical reaction can be categorized into
two main groups, namely the internal factors of graphite materials and the environmental factors
during oxidation. The former comprises of factors such as the porosity of the graphite, the impurity
content the graphite, and the grain size of the graphite; whereas the latter comprises of factors such
as the reaction temperature, reaction gas flow rate, reaction gas concentration, ambient pressure,
and graphite irradiation [17].
 Past experimental works carried out to study graphite oxidation primarily focused on investi-
gating the mechanism of graphite oxidation and the changes in graphite physicochemical properties
after oxidation. An extensive summary of these studies was provided by Zhang et al. [17]. Addi-
tionally, other researchers performed experiments to study the mechanisms of air ingress, including
the reverse U-tube facility by Hishida and Takeda [20], the NACOK facility [21], and the h-loop by
Gould et al. [22]. These studies focused on the formation of loop-wide natural circulation through
molecular diffusion and/or buoyancy effects after de-pressurization.
 Several computational studies were performed to model the air ingress phenomenon in HTGRs
using system-level and CFD software. One of the system-level codes used was GAMMA by Lim
and No [2] to model the High Temperature Test Reactor (HTTR)-simulated test facility, and by
Kim et al. [23] to model the MHTGR-600 reactor. The SPECTRA code was used by Zheng and
Stempniewicz [3] to model the NACOK facility. Another widely used code was TINTE, which

 5

was used by Zheng and Stempniewicz [3] to model the NACOK facility and numerous researchers
to model the HTR-PM reactor [4–7]. RELAP5-3D was used by Davis et al. [24] to model the
nitrogen/air ingress in the reverse U-tube and NACOK facilities. The work by Davis et al. [24]
focused solely on the flow of nitrogen/air into the facilities without the consideration of oxidation,
whereas, some forms of oxidation models were included in the other studies. Kim et al. [23]
considered only the C/O2 reaction in their GAMMA model. Similarly, Zheng and Stempniewicz
[3] included only the C/O2 reaction in their TINTE model, though, an additional C/CO2 reaction
was considered in their SPECTRA model. The models by Lim and No [2], Liu et al. [5], and Xu et
al. [4, 6, 7] considered the C/O2 , C/CO2 , and CO/O2 reactions. In these studies, oxidation rate was
modeled using a series of correlations that were dependent on factors such as temperature, partial
pressure, gas velocity, and gas molar concentration. Furthermore, due to different concentration of
oxygen and temperature, the oxidation rate and the dominance of oxidation reactions were shown
to vary spatially within the core.
 CFD analysis was performed by Oh et al. [8] to study the patterns of the stratified flow and
counter-current flow in the lower plenum after the core of the 600 MWth MHTGR reactor is de-
pressurized. Liu et al. [25] performed CFD analysis to investigate the influence of temperature
and Reynolds number on the graphite oxidation shape function, defined as the relation between
the maximum and the average burn-off of a pebble, under different pebble stacking configurations.
Ferng and Chi [9] used CFD to investigate the characteristics of graphite oxidation corrosion in the
HTR-10 reactor. Franken et al. [26] constructed a CFD model of the h-loop to study the transition
of air ingress from molecular diffusion to natural circulation.
 The above discussion highlights that air ingress after the de-pressurization of an HTGR is a
complex phenomenon that is still actively being actively researched. The onset of natural circulation
in the core is shown to be dependent on the reactor design. On top of that, the oxidation of graphite
after the ingress of air into the core adds further complexity to the problem. In the next section, the
enhancement needed in the SAM code to model air ingress is discussed.

2.2 SAM Modeling Needs
 In this section, the modeling needs for SAM to capture the air ingress phenomenon are dis-
cussed:
 1. Expansion of multi-component gas model in SAM: During normal operation and most
accident scenarios, there is typically one type of gas (helium) in the HTGR. However, during a
DLOFC accident followed by air-ingress, the introduction of air into the reactor core creates an air-
helium mixture. This means that SAM needs to be able to model the air-helium mixture and track
the concentration of each gas component in the core. In SAM, this can be achieved through the
multi-component gas flow model which was first implemented to the code in 2021 [27]. Since then,
enhancements have been made to improve the numerical robustness of the multi-component flow
model. However, the current implementation of the multi-component gas flow model in SAM can
only handle up to two types of gases. This capability will need to be further expanded to increase
the number of gas types that can be handled by the model. This is necessary because during air-
ingress, the oxidation of graphite may produce carbon dioxide, that in turn may react with graphite
to produce carbon monoxide, which is flammable. Detailed discussions of the multi component gas
model are provided in Section 3.1.
 2. Expansion of graphite oxidation model in SAM: Currently, a 0-D graphite oxidation model

6

is implemented in SAM to support the source term evaluation modeling in Fluoride-Cooled High-
Temperature Pebble-Bed Reactor (FHR)s [28]. The model calculates the reaction rate of the C +
O2 → CO2 reaction using the correlation by Roes [29]. As described by Xu et al. [4] and Liu et
al. [5], the oxidation rate in the core of a HTGR is not uniform due to the temperature and gases
distributions. Xu et al. [4] shows that in the core of the HTR-PM reactor, the C + O2 → CO2
reaction is dominant in the lower reflector region. However, in the middle region of the core, the
oxidation of graphite is dominated by the C + CO2 → 2CO reaction. The reaction rate of this
reaction can be modeled using the Moormann correlation [30]. Additionally, other improvements
such as the thermal feedback of solid structures, the tracking of oxygen consumption rate, and the
2-D modeling of oxidation within structures may be needed to improve the overall fidelity and
accuracy of the model. Detailed discussions of these items are provided in Section 3.3.
 3. Implementation of gas diffusion model in SAM: As discussed in Section 2.1, diffusion
may play a role during air ingress. Depending on the design and geometry of the reactor, buoyancy
force alone may not be sufficient to overcome the hydrostatic head and frictional pressure drop in
the core to allow air to enter the core from the lower plenum. In this scenario, the introduction of air
into the core is dominated by diffusion. Diffusion can be modeled using Fick’s Law as a function
of diffusion coefficient, which can be calculated using various correlations, such as those provided
by Oh and Kim [18] and Liu et al. [5].
 4. Implementation of a critical flow model in SAM: The discussion so far has focused on
DLOFC due to large breaks in the system, especially at the co-axial duct, leading to the so-called
double ended guillotine break (DEGB) accident, which is considered a Beyond Design Basis Ac-
cident (BDBA) in HTGRs. In some scenarios, small breaks may occur in the primary circuit and
cause the system to undergo de-pressurization. Under this scenario, due to the large difference
between the system pressure and the ambient pressure, the helium leaking from the system may
undergo critical flow. During critical flow, the leakage flow is choked and the leakage velocity is
capped at the speed of sound. Without a critical flow model, the code may overpredict the leakage
velocity and the rate of de-pressurization of the system, which in turn may impact the prediction of
the decay heat removal rate. For this purpose, an isentropic critical flow model is implemented in
SAM, and more discussions are provided in Section 3.2.
 In the next section, the enhancements implemented in SAM are discussed in details, along with
validation and demonstration cases.

3 Recent SAM Code Capability Improvement and Enhancement
 This section describes the recent capability improvement and enhancement implemented in
SAM for the modeling of the air-ingress scenario. The multi component flow model is first dis-
cussed, followed by the isentropic critical flow model, and lastly the graphite oxidation model. Test
cases and demonstrations are also included to showcase these capabilities.

3.1 Multi-component Flow Model
3.1.1   Theory
 As discussed before, when a rupture happens on the main coolant pipes on the body of the reac-
tor pressure vessel during a DLOFC accident in the HTGR, the air ingress scenario proceeds quickly
from diffusion to natural circulation, which leads to considerable rate of air ingress to severely ox-
idize the fuel and core [31]. To capture the graphite oxidation damage to the fuel and core because
of potential air ingress during DLOFC, it is important to track the temporal and spatial distributions
of different gas species. For this reason, a multi-component gas mixture model is needed. For sim-
plicity, a two-component gas mixture flow model, involving component 1 and 2, is implemented in
SAM. Assuming mechanical and thermal equilibrium between the two gas components, i.e., trav-

described by the mass conservation equation for component 1 (or 2), and a set of mass, momentum,
and energy balance equations for the gas mixture. In one-dimensional, these equations are given as

3.2 Critical Flow Model
3.2.1    Theory
 In some scenarios where there is a break in the primary loop the helium leaking into the cavity
may undergo critical due to the high pressure of the primary system. In this scenario the helium
leaking out system will be choked and will be traveling at the speed of sound. To capture the phe-
nomenon, a critical flow model is developed in SAM based on the isentropic ideal gas formulation
[34]. It is intended for the modeling of a break in a pressurized system where fluid is evacuated to
the ambient where the model essentially treats a break as a converging-diverging nozzle. The model
is implemented in a PBVolumeBranch component and is enabled with a user-defined function.

3.2.2 Test Cases

 Several cases are simulated to demonstrate SAM’s capability to model critical flows. In the
first case, a simple one dimensional channel is modeled. The channel has an inlet mass flow rate
of 0.3 kg/s and an outlet pressure of 1 MPa. Initially the channel is intact. At t = 20 s, a break of
a size of 1×10-4 m-2 is initiated at the middle of the channel, causing some of the flow to escape
from the channel into the environment with an ambient pressure of 100 kPa. At t = 40 s, the break
is disabled, and the system returns to the initial condition. A schematic of the model is shown in
Figure 3.12. The total, outlet and leakage mass flow rates predicted by SAM are shown in Figure
3.13. The red solid line represents the inlet (total) mass flow rate in the channel and the green solid
line represents the outlet mass flow rate. The leakage mass flow rate is represented by the black
dashed line. Prior to t = 20 s, steady-state is achieved in the channel where the inlet and outlet mass
flow rates are equal, whereas the leakage mass flow rate is at zero. At t = 20 s where the break is
initiated, the leakage mass flow rate increases to about 0.1 kg/s. At the same time, the outlet mass
flow rate decreases by the same amount to about 0.2 kg/s. The mass flow rates remain stable until t
= 40 s where the break is disabled, after which the leakage mass flow rate reduces back to zero and
the outlet mass flow rate recovers to 0.3 kg/s. This simple demonstration shows the conservation of
mass flow rate in the critical flow model.

 The average and maximum pebble temperatures during the accident are shown in Figures 3.26

maximum pebble temperature experience a significant increase. The maximum pebble temperature
shows a continuous increases once it reaches its peak at about t = 20 hr. Note that a uniform power
density is used in the core here for demonstration purpose, and the maximum temperature may not

 This exercise demonstrates SAM’s capability in modeling critical flow when a small break oc-

data, the behavior of the system during the accident is as expected.

 Overall, the 0-D oxidation model in SAM is able to predict the measured weight loss with
some over-prediction at lower temperatures and under-prediction at higher temperatures. Several
items should be addressed to improve the overall fidelity and accuracy of the model for air ingress
simulation, including but not limited to:
 1. Thermal feedback with solid structures: Due to the exothermic nature of the reaction, the
oxidation phenomena may be accelerated as it progresses. The heat released from oxidation should
be accounted for in the heat conduction modeling of graphite structures.
 2. Tracking the consumption of oxygen: The rate of oxidation will be limited by the available
oxygen (or carbon monoxide/dioxide), thus it is important to consider the local concentration of
oxygen and also the consumption/sink term attributed to the oxidation process. Various approaches
may be considered for modeling the transport of oxygen (compounds), whether they are treated
as passively transported quantities or directly as components of a multi-component gas fluid as
discussed in Section 3.1.1.
 3. Consideration for all oxidation reactions: The current implementation only considers the
basic carbon dioxide production reaction (Eq. 2.1), although there are other reactions that also
occur. At a minimum, it would be important to also calculate the carbon monoxide production rate
(Eq. 2.3), which is considered by the Moormann correlation [30]. In addition to being an oxidation
source, carbon monoxide is a flammable gas which poses additional safety concerns. Modeling each
of these chemical reactions also necessitates tracking the transport and consumption of each of the
constituent components in the fluid flow.
 4. 2-D modeling of oxidation within structures: Depending on the temperature regime of
the process, oxidation may occur at the outer surface of the structure or within the internal porous
structure, or a combination of these interactions. Various factors may also come into play, as the
oxidation within pores may be accelerated as further pore surface area is exposed. Some assess-
ment will be needed to determine if an engineering-scale structural oxidation model is required for
accurate modeling of air ingress events.

4 Summary and Future Work
 This report summarizes the activities and progresses made in the SAM code implementations
to support the modeling and simulation of the air-ingress phenomenon in a High-Temperature Gas-
Cooled Reactor (HTGR). To capture the air-helium mixture during the air-ingress phenomenon,
a multi component flow model is implemented in SAM. Additionally, a 0-D graphite oxidation
model is added to the code to model the C + O2 → CO2 reaction using the Roes correlation [29].
Furthermore, an isentropic critical flow model is implemented to more accurately capture the de-
pressurization of the system due to small breaks in the primary loop. Verification cases and demon-
strations are also provided to showcase these capabilities in SAM.
 Despite the enhancements made to SAM, some gaps still exist in the code and will be addressed
to improve the modeling of the air-ingress phenomenon. These gaps include the lack of a diffusion
model and the limit of two gas types in the multi-component gas mixture model, the robustness and
further verification and the validation of the multi-component gas mixture model, and the lack of a
model to capture the C + CO2 → 2CO reaction and thermal feedback. These gaps will be closed in
FY26. Furthermore, a demonstration model of a reference HTGR will be developed to simulate the
air ingress phenomenon with SAM.
