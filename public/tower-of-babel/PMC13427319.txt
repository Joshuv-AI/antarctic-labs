Formulated Curcumin Exerts Potent Anti-Influenza Activity by Inhibition of Akt Activation and Viral Genome Replication
Authors: Hui-Ming Chang, Ping-Shan Lai, Cun-Zhao Li, Fong-Yuan Lin, Da-Yuan Chen, Ruei-Sheng Tsai, Shan-Chia Ou, Nianhan Ma, Wei-Li Hsu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionInfluenza A viruses (IAVs) cause not only seasonal epidemics but also pandemics in humans. The emergence of H5N1 variants resistant to oseltamivir highlights the need to develop new antiviral therapeutic strategies. Accumulated evidence has indicated the potent anti-influenza activity of curcumin (Cur). Nevertheless, the unsatisfactory bioavailability of Cur limits its clinical use.MethodsIn the present study, the anti-influenza activity and underlying mechanism of encapsulated Cur in nanoparticle form, referred to as Cur-micelle (Cur-M), were evaluated.ResultsOur results demonstrated that, as with native Cur treatment, Cur-M treatment remarkably reduced IAV infection. Interestingly, Cur-M significantly inhibited IAV infectivity post-viral entry, while treatment with Cur alone showed no effect, as demonstrated by differences in plaque formation ability. By monitoring intracellular processing, including the signaling pathway required for IAV propagation and the critical viral replication step, we discovered that viral RNA replication and the activation of Akt signaling were significantly suppressed under Cur-M treatment.ConclusionThis study demonstrates for the first time that Cur-M interferes with intracellular processes required for influenza virus infection, highlighting its potential for further evaluation in both cell-based and in vivo systems.

Introduction

Infection with influenza A virus (IAV), known as flu, causes an acute, respirational, highly contagious, and zoonotic disease that circulates in humans and many animal species. Owing to the mechanism of antigenic shift and drift, mutated or reassortant IAV strains frequently emerge and sweep through the globe in the form of seasonal flu epidemics and occasional pandemics. As reported, two flu pandemics in the last century caused millions of deaths and enormous economic losses [1]. Since 1997, zoonotic “avian flu,” caused by different subtypes of IAV, including the H5N1, H5N6, H7N9, and H10N8 subtypes, has drawn attention due to its ability to transmit from avian livestock to humans with high mortality (>50%) [2]. According to guidelines from the World Health Organization (WHO), vaccinations and anti-influenza drugs are the main strategies designed to intervene in viral replication to ultimately prevent infection and control the spread of influenza viruses. However, rising concerns regarding the emergence of drug-resistant strains necessitate the need to develop novel anti-influenza strategies [3]. In addition, interference with the cellular machinery hijacked by these viruses to facilitate infection has become an alternative target for antiviral drug development [3].

Curcumin (Cur; diferuloylmethane), the major active compound (>77%) extracted from the herb turmeric (Curcuma longa) [4], has been widely used for thousands of years as a flavoring and preservative in Indian cuisine and as a medication to promote health in Asian traditional medicine [5]. Accumulated evidence has demonstrated that Cur and its structural analogs, known as curcuminoids, have various bioactivities through their regulation of the cellular transduction of signals involved in different mechanisms [6]. The wide range of known molecular targets modulated by Cur in cells includes activator protein-1, nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB), signal transducer and activator of transcription, tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interleukins, cyclin D1, p53, and growth factor-induced signal cascades (reviewed in [7]). Upon this activity, Cur has been demonstrated to act as a phytocompound with a broad range of health properties, including its antioxidant, analgesic, anti-inflammatory, antiseptic, anticarcinogenic, and antiviral activities [7].

Several lines of evidence have indicated that Cur potentially inhibits numerous viruses, such as human immunodeficiency virus [8], herpes simplex virus [9], hepatitis C virus [10], enterovirus [11], IAV [12], and other enveloped viruses, such as flaviviruses [13]. Cur interacts and interferes with not only cellular targets but also viral factors, a fascinating feature of Cur in novel drug development. Previously, we reported that Cur inhibits IAV infection by blocking viral hemagglutination (HA) activity via competing for the cellular receptor-binding site (RBS) on the HA protein, as evident by an HA inhibition (HI) assay and a molecular docking simulation [12–14]. Despite these encouraging findings, the application of Cur is severely limited by its intrinsic properties, such as its low aqueous solubility, fast metabolism, and rapid elimination [15].

To counteract these limitations, strategies have been developed to improve the bioavailability of Cur, for instance, the administration of Cur in combination with other compounds or the use of various modified forms of Cur. In particular, co-administration of Cur with piperine, which is a potent inhibitor of biotransformation, especially the glucuronidation of Cur, enhanced the serum concentration and absorption level of Cur in animal models [16]. In addition, improvements in the aqueous solubility and oral bioavailability of Cur were achieved by the use of a wide range of formulations, including phospholipid complexes [17], nanoparticles with chitosan [18], micelles encapsulating Cur [19], and amorphous solid dispersions [20]. Very recently, self-assembled micelles loaded with Cur (namely, Cur-M) in an oil-free-based system were generated in an attempt to enhance the water dispersion of Cur [21]. This formulation not only significantly increased the bioactivity/stability of Cur but also remained the inhibitory effect on the IAV infection. However, the comparative study indicated the mechanism of action between native Cur and Cur-M is likely different. Hence, in the current study, the anti-influenza activity of a novel aqueous formulation of polymeric encapsulated Cur, Cur-M, was evaluated in comparison with native Cur. Moreover, the intracellular antiviral mechanism of Cur was elucidated, for the first time, by using Cur-M.

Materials and Methods

Cell Culture and Virus Infection

Human A549 lung carcinoma cells and HEK 293T stably expressing luciferase under control of the NF-κB promoter, namely, the NF-κB reporter cell [22], were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Hyclone) with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS) and antibiotics including penicillin 100 U/mL and streptomycin 10 µg/mL. Canine Madin-Darby canine kidney (MDCK) cells, one of the most permissive cell lines that is commonly used for influenza study, were maintained in minimal-essential medium (MEM, Hyclone) with supplements of 10% FBS and antibiotics.

Human influenza virus PR8, A/Puerto Rico/8/34 (H1N1), was amplified in MDCK cells. Before infection, cells were washed with PBS and cultured in an infection medium supplemented with antibiotics and 1 mg/mL of TPCK-treated trypsin.

Compounds

Cur, obtained from Sigma-Aldrich, was dissolved in DMSO (Sigma-Aldrich) at a concentration of 0.1 M that was kept in −80°C for further use. Cur-loaded solid self-emulsion micelle (Cur-M), was prepared by self-emulsion technique following the previous reports [21, 23]. Briefly, Cur was added to a mixture of surfactant and co-surfactant dissolved in ethanol and stirred gently at 60°C. After the removal of ethanol, the self-assembled formulation was obtained. The formulation was then hydrated with an appropriate amount of water to achieve the desired concentration before use. The aqueous Cur-M stock solution (0.1 M) was diluted with distilled water and stored at 4°C in the dark until further evaluation. For experiments, Cur solutions were freshly diluted to the indicated concentrations in infection medium.

Viral Infections and Cur Treatment

For the time-of-drug addition experiment, the indicated concentrations of test compounds or solvent control (DMSO, MEM for Cur, and Cur-M, respectively) were added to the medium at various time points of infection. Briefly, (1) co-treatment: Cur was mixed with viruses in the infection medium, and the drug-virus mixture was added simultaneously to cells during viral absorption (i.e., 1 h post-infection [hpi]) and also included in the course of infection; (2) post-treatment: cells were infected with viruses without tested compounds, and after viral entry (1 hpi), cells were washed twice with PBS followed by treatment of test compounds throughout the time of infection.

Plaque Assay

MDCK cells grown to 80% confluence were washed with PBS followed by infection with serial dilutions of viruses for 1 h at 37°C. The unbound viruses were removed, and cells were then cultured with 1 × MEM supplemented with 0.6% agarose at 37°C, 5% CO2 for 2 days. Viral plaques were fixed with 100% methanol and visualized by staining with crystal violet (Sigma).

Evaluation of the Anti-Flu Activity of the Tested Compounds by Plaque Reduction Assay

MDCK cell monolayers seeded in a 12-well plate were washed twice with PBS, followed by infection with influenza virus (strain PR8, 100 pfu) for 1 h. Subsequently, the virus inocula were replaced with MEM supplemented with 0.6% agarose and 30 µm (unless otherwise stated) of Cur, Cur-M, or equivalent concentrations of solvent controls. After culturing at 37°C, 5% CO2 for 2 days, viral plaques were visualized by staining with crystal violet (Sigma).

Transfection

Transfection was conducted for transient expression of the desired proteins by using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, one night before transfection, cells were seeded in a 24-well plate. In total, 0.8 μg of plasmids was incubated with 2 μL of liposome that was diluted in 50 μL of OPTIMEM (without FBS or antibiotics) at room temperature for 20 min. Subsequently, the mixture of DNA and liposome was added dropwise onto cell monolayers and incubated for 24 h for further analysis.

Western Blot Analysis

Sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis was carried out with a MINI-PROTEAN III apparatus (BioRad). Subsequently, the proteins were electrophoretically transferred to PVDF membranes according to the manufacturer’s recommendations, followed by a blocking procedure with PBS containing 0.1% Tween-20 (PBS-T) and 5% dried milk for 1 h at room temperature. The membrane was then reacted with the antibody for influenza NS1 (sc-130568, Santa Cruz Biotechnology), NP (ab20343, Abcam), α-actin (NB600501, Novus), Akt (N3C2, GTX121937, GeneTex), Phospho-Akt (Ser473) (GTX61708, GeneTex), diluted in PBS-T containing 5% dried milk at room temperature overnight. The next day, the membrane was washed with PBS-T six times, followed by incubation with the secondary antibody conjugated with horseradish peroxidase for 1 h. After thorough washes with PBS-T, the signal was visualized by enhanced chemiluminescence and ImageQuant LAS 4000 (GE Healthcare, Uppsala, Sweden).

Immunofluorescence Assay

Infected cells were fixed with 1.875% formaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.5% NP-40, and then incubated with antibodies specific for influenza NS1 (sc-130568, Santa Cruz Biotechnology) or NP (ab20343, Abcam) proteins for 1 h. Cells were further incubated with secondary antibodies conjugated with Alexa Fluor 488 (Invitrogen) for 1 hr at room temperature. After thorough wash procedures with PBS containing 1% FBS, images were acquired by confocal microscopy (FV1000, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Minigenome Reporter Assay

Briefly, HEK 293T cells seeded in 48-well plates were co-transfected with plasmids expressing PR8 NP (20 ng), PB1 (20 ng), PB2 (20 ng), PA (20 ng), a firefly luciferase expression plasmid (10 ng) as an internal control for transfection efficiency, and a reporter plasmid, pPol I-Flu-Rluc (100 ng) [24], encoding Renilla luciferase of which the initial RNA transcription was driven by the promoter of human RNA polymerase I, while the expression of Renilla luciferase was controlled by influenza polymerase complex that was kindly offered by Dr. Laurence Tiley at the University of Cambridge. The next day, the transfected cells were harvested for luciferase analysis using the Dual-Glo luciferase assay system, according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Promega). Luciferase activity was then measured by a FLUOstar OPTIMA microplate reader (BMG Labtech GmbH, Offenburg, Germany). Relative light units were estimated as the ratio of Renilla to firefly luciferase luminescence.

Measurement of Viral RNA Levels by Quantitative Real-Time RT-PCR

MDCK cells were infected with PR8 viruses at an MOI of 0.5 in three independent experiments. At 4 and 8 hpi, total RNA was extracted from cells by Trizol reagent (Invitrogen). One µg of total RNA was reverse-transcribed by Superscript III (Invitrogen) with random primers, followed by quantitative real-time RT-PCR (qRT-PCR). Expression of GAPDF and viral M gene was detected following the method described previously [25]. Sequences of the primer set for GAPDH were as follows: forward 5′-GCC​AAA​AGG​GTC​ATC​ATC​TC, reverse 5′-GGC​CAT​CCA​CAG​TCT​TCT), and M segment: forward 5′-CAGRTACTGGGCHATAAGRAC-3′, reverse 5′-GCA​TTG​TCT​CCG​AAG​AAA​TAA​G-3′. Expression data from three independent experiments were estimated using the 2−ΔΔCt method with an endogenous GAPDH standard, and all values were expressed as fold changes relative to the mock group.

Replication of Cur- or Cur-M-Treated IAV in Embryonated Eggs

The embryonic eggs were purchased from the Livestock Research Institute, Chunan, Taiwan, and incubated in the hatchery until 10 days old. Fifty pfu PR8 viruses (diluted in 0.5 mL of PBS) were injected into the allantoic sac of embryonic eggs. Subsequently, 0.5 mL of PBS, 30 μm of Cur, or Cur-M in a total volume was delivered into the allantoic sac. Treated eggs were incubated in 37°C incubator for 18 h or 24 h, and the yield of virus progeny was determined by HA test.

HA Test

HA test was used to determine the HA protein content of influenza viruses. Briefly, 50 μL of viruses obtained from allantoic fluid were added into the well of round-bottomed 96-well microplates, followed by 2-fold serial dilutions with PBS 11 times. Subsequently, 50 μL of 0.75% chicken erythrocyte stocks were added to each well. The HA reaction was observed after 30 min incubation.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SAS software. All experiments were performed with at least three independent replicates, and data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). For experiments involving comparisons among multiple groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test was used, as specified where applicable. For predefined pairwise comparisons between treatment groups and their corresponding controls, independent Student’s t tests were applied. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05 (*), p < 0.01 (**), p < 0.001 (***), and p < 0.0001 (****), throughout the study. Graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism version 6.00 (GraphPad Software).

Results

Cur-M Significantly Reduced Viral Protein Expression and Plaque Formation

Our previous study showed that Cur-M inhibited influenza virus plaque formation more effectively than native Cur [21], prompting further investigation of the underlying mechanism. To evaluate viral infection, 30 μm of Cur or Cur-M, the concentration shown to be non-cytotoxic in our previous study [21], was added to the cell culture medium upon viral attachment and maintained throughout the course of infection for 12 hpi. As indicated in Figure 1, the expression of two viral proteins, NS1 and NP, which are responsible for counteracting innate immunity and viral genome replication, respectively, was strongly affected by the presence of either Cur or Cur-M. Accumulated levels of viral protein were significantly decreased in infected cells treated with either Cur or Cur-M compared with cells treated with their corresponding solvent controls, DMSO and Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium, respectively (Fig. 1). Noticeably, the anti-influenza activity of encapsulated Cur-M was milder than that of native Cur, as evaluated by viral protein expression (Fig. 1b).

We previously found that Cur inhibits influenza virus plaque formation primarily by interfering with viral attachment, whereas its effect after viral entry is limited [12]. In contrast, the inhibitory activity of Cur-M was mainly observed during post-entry treatment [21]. To further evaluate the therapeutic potential of Cur-M, the minimal concentration required to inhibit viral infectivity was determined using a plaque formation assay. As shown in Figure 2, Cur consistently failed to abrogate PR8 plaque formation when it was included in the culture medium after viral entry [12]. Intriguingly, a dramatic decrease in plaque number was noticed when cells were treated with Cur-M at 30 µm (Fig. 2a), and this suppressive effect on plaque formation was dose-dependent (Fig. 2b).

Results from three independent experiments are shown in b. The relative effect of Cur-M on plaque formation was compared with that of Cur, which was arbitrarily set as 100%. Statistical significance was analyzed using one-way analysis of variance followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test. *p < 0.05 and ****p < 0.0001.

Since the reduction in IAV plaque formation might have been due to the inhibition of viral HA function, the effect of Cur-M on HA activity was next determined. As indicated in online supplementary Figure S11; for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000552999, unlike native Cur, Cur-M (i.e., encapsulated Cur) did not have a typical inhibitory effect on HA (online suppl. Fig. S1b).

Post-Treatment with Cur-M Decreased Viral Production

Next, overall viral production was monitored in cells treated with Cur-M after viral entry. Native Cur, Cur-M, or DMSO, as a solvent control, was added to the culture medium 1 h after infection. At 18 hpi, cell-released viruses (in the supernatant) and infected cells were individually harvested for the measurement of virus progeny (Fig. 3a), and viral protein (NS1 and NP) synthesis (Fig. 3b, d), respectively. Overall, both Cur and Cur-M inhibited virus production compared with the DMSO control. Notably, treatment with 30 µm Cur-M reduced viral yield by more than 90% (Fig. 3a). Noticeably, Cur-M exerted a significantly greater inhibitory effect on viral yield than Cur.

Viral protein expression was simultaneously monitored by immunofluorescent assay (IFA) and Western blot analysis. A decrease in the signal intensities of the IAV proteins NS1 and NP in Cur-M-treated cells compared to solvent-treated control cells or native Cur-treated cells was observed by IFA (Fig. 3b). This phenomenon was also reflected in the Western blot analysis (Fig. 3c). As shown in Figure 3d, the levels of both accumulated NS1 and accumulated NP were significantly lower in cells treated with Cur-M than in the other two groups, indicating that viral protein synthesis during the course of virus replication had been downregulated by post-treatment with Cur-M.

Cur-M Reduced Transcriptional Efficiency of the Influenza A Viral Polymerase

As suggested above, Cur-M may affect influenza virus infection by interfering with steps required for efficient viral propagation in cells. Because viral genome replication after entry is mediated by the viral RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp) complex, viral RNA synthesis was, therefore, examined under Cur-M treatment. To address this issue, two techniques, namely, minigenome reporter assay and qRT-PCR, were used to evaluate viral genome activity.

In the minigenome reporter assay, expression of the reporter, the firefly luciferase gene, is under control by the influenza promoter, which can be driven by the viral RdRp [26]. As a result, the firefly luciferase signal indicates the efficiency of viral genome replication. In cells transiently expressing the viral RdRp, Cur-M treatment remarkably reduced expression of the reporter gene in the viral replicon when compared with its expression in two solvent-treated control groups, and in cells treated with native Cur (Fig. 4a). Moreover, overall viral RNA transcription was also monitored in MDCK cells infected with PR8 virus. The qRT-PCR results demonstrated that at both 4 and 8 hpi, the transcription level of the viral M gene was reduced in infected cells treated with Cur or Cur-M. Notably, a significant decrease in RNA synthesis at both time points was observed only in cells under Cur-M treatment (Fig. 4b), indicating that Cur-M likely exerts a significant inhibitory effect on viral protein synthesis through affecting viral RNA transcription and replication.

Cur-M Decreased Activation of the Akt Signaling Pathway in IAV-Infected Cells

Because several cellular signaling pathways involved in IAV propagation, including NF-κB and AKT, are inhibited by Cur [23, 27, 28], we examined the activation status of this signaling pathway in IAV-infected cells following Cur-M treatment. Initially, the general effect of formulated Cur-M on NF-κB activation was evaluated in a reporter cell system in which the exogenous luciferase gene was expressed under the control of the NF-κB promoter upon stimulation with an inducer, e.g., TNF-α [22]. As demonstrated in Figure 5a, treatment with 10 ng/mL TNF-α greatly elevated firefly luciferase levels, indicating activation of the NF-κB promoter. Moreover, Cur-M significantly diminished the activity of the NF-κB promoter. Subsequently, whether the anti-influenza activity of Cur-M is brought about via its effect on NF-κB was examined in infected cells.

As the expression and phosphorylation of Akt can be regulated by NF-κB [29, 30], and IAV hijacks the Akt signal pathway for efficient infection [28], we then detected Akt phosphorylation levels to evaluate the effect of Cur-M in IAV-infected cells. At 8 hpi, the phosphorylated Akt level was significantly reduced in IAV-infected cells treated with Cur-M (Fig. 5b, c). Moreover, this decrease in the phosphorylation of Akt coincided with a pronounced reduction in the NS1 protein level in IAV-infected cells (Fig. 5d), indicating that the phosphorylation of Akt is associated with the expression of viral NS1.

Treatment with Cur-M Inhibited IAV Propagation in Embryonic Eggs

Next, the effect of Cur-M on IAV propagation was further investigated in chicken embryonic eggs. Simultaneous injection of Cur-M and IAV (100 pfu, plaque formation unit) into embryonic eggs significantly reduced the viral load in the allantoic fluid, as determined by HA assay (Fig. 6), whereas no anti-influenza effect of Cur treatment was observed.

Discussion

Although the therapeutic potential of Cur has not yet been tested in clinical trials, extensive research has revealed that native Cur abrogates IAV infection via several mechanisms. Among these mechanisms, Cur has been reported to block viral entry by abrogating hemagglutinin activity [12, 14] and to inactivate viral infectivity by disrupting viral envelope integrity [13]. However, the presence of Cur after viral entry had a marginal effect [12]. In this study, as summarized in the online supplementary Figure S2, a series of experiments was conducted to further evaluate the effects of Cur-M on intracellular processes during IAV infection, as well as its impact on viral yield in embryonated eggs. Our findings provide further evidence of the novel antiviral action of Cur against IAV replication; when formulated in a micellar form (i.e., Cur-M), it decreased viral RNA transcription (Fig. 4) and suppressed Akt activation (Fig. 5) in infected cells.

Cur is generally recognized as safe. The working concentrations were selected based on our previous study, in which Cur showed an EC50 of approximately 0.47 μm in plaque reduction assays, with a selective index of 92.5 [12]. In our cell culture system, Cur concentrations above 100 μm slightly inhibited MDCK cell growth, whereas Cur-M showed no detectable cytotoxicity up to 60 μm [21]. Therefore, concentrations below 60 μm were used in the comparative study (Cur vs. Cur-M) to minimize potential cytotoxic effects. Notably, treatment of Cur and Cur-M (30 μm) exhibited distinct antiviral effects; the intracellular inhibitory activity observed with Cur-M was not detected when native Cur was administered after viral entry. It is likely that Cur encapsulated in the form of Cur-M exerts stronger actions within infected cells than native Cur. The intrinsic properties of Cur lower its bioavailability. Nevertheless, the formulation of Cur-M in an oil-free self-emulsion system and the average particle size of Cur encapsulated into a micellar form (13.5 nm) possibly facilitate the cellular uptake of Cur-M. Moreover, unlike Cur, Cur-M is aqueous and completely dissolved in solution, e.g., culture medium. Hence, the distinct antiviral activity of Cur-M might be due to the improvement of Cur solubility and an increase in the intracellular uptake of Cur. Ultimately, the intracellular antiviral activity of Cur was evident when formulated as Cur-M, particularly during post-entry stages of viral infection and in the embryonated egg system.

As previously reported, the most effective action of Cur is its blockade of viral HA activity via interaction with the RBS of the HA protein [12, 14]. An attempt was made to evaluate the effect of Cur-M on HA activity. As indicated in online supplementary Figure S1, Cur at concentrations higher than 31.2 µm effectively abrogated HA activity, which is consistent with our previous finding [12]. However, the inhibitory effects on HA were ambiguous; in the presence of Cur-M at high concentrations, RBCs seem to settle in the center of the well, but this is unlike the typical inhibitory effect of Cur on HA for the following reasons: (1) the red spots were not solid; (2) a “tear-drop pattern” was not observed when the plates were tilted at 45–60° [31]; (3) the same HA pattern of Cur-M appeared when empty micelles without Cur were used. Hence, it is unlikely that Cur-M affects HA activity. We suspect that because Cur-M is loaded with Cur, they can no longer fit the RBS of viral HA; the RBC spots observed in micelles and Cur-M might have been due to the nonspecific association of polymers with RBCs but was not due to the inhibition of viral HA mediated by Cur-M.

The currently recommended medicine for influenza is a neuraminidase inhibitor, i.e., oseltamivir (Tamiflu), which reduces the ability of the virus to infect other respiratory cells by inhibiting neuraminidase activity. However, as with an older class of anti-influenza drugs, the M2 blockers adamantanes, the substantial emergence of oseltamivir-resistant IAV has also been observed [32]. Hence, to circumvent this obstacle, in the future, a drug that specifically targets cellular signaling pathways would have therapeutic potential as a medicine to treat IAV. Accumulated evidence has shown that a specific inhibitor that blocks Akt activation can suppress IAV replication [28]. It affects the viral life cycle at multiple stages, namely, early intake and late genome replication, but does not alter the cellular immune response. Similarly, this study demonstrated that while the level of Akt phosphorylation was increased at the early stage of IAV infection, treatment with Cur-M after viral entry significantly reduced the phosphorylation level of Akt. Cur-M treatment also noticeably decreased expression of the viral nonstructural protein NS1. NS1 has been demonstrated to trigger the activation of several cellular signaling pathways, including the PI3K/Akt pathway. This activation of the PI3K/Akt pathway facilitates the entry and intake of viral particles into host cells and prohibits activation of the apoptosis cascade in infected cells [33]. All these known functions of NS1 indicate its ultimate goal, to prolong replication and increase the progeny yield of IAV in host cells. Hence, natural compounds or chemicals that specifically target cellular signaling pathways such as the Akt pathway could have therapeutic potential for the development of medicines targeting IAV in the future.

In addition to the Akt pathway, the NF-κB pathway is also required for efficient IAV propagation [34]. Acetylsalicylic acid, also known as aspirin and an IKK2 blocker, was also found to interrupt virus replication through inhibition of the NF-κB pathway. Akt is known to regulate downstream NF-κB signal transduction [35]. Furthermore, the p65 subunit of NF-κB is involved in IAV vRNA synthesis [36]. Thus, inhibition of NF-κB activation clearly suppressed virus replication, possibly by interrupting RNA synthesis and viral protein expression [36]. Similarly, in our study, a reporter cell system clearly showed that Cur-M downregulated NF-κB activity, which might likely contribute to its anti-flu effect.

On the other hand, the results of immunoblot and IFA analyses indicated that the expression levels of both the viral NP and NS1 proteins were significantly lower in Cur-M-treated cells than in control cells. A previous study reported that the exportation of viral ribonucleoprotein (vRNP) from the nucleus to the cytoplasm was interrupted due to inhibition of the NF-κB pathway [37]. However, as indicated by the cellular distribution of NP, the main component of the vRNP complex, NP could be detected in both the nucleus and cytoplasm. Indeed, more nuclear NP was observed in Cur-M-treated cells (Fig. 3b). However, considering that the NP protein contains a nuclear localization signal and the overall low expression level of NP, it is speculative to associate the increased signal intensity of NP with the interference of nuclear vRNP exportation. Further investigations with a real-time imaging system may help verify the effects of Cur-M on vRNP movement. Moreover, to clarify whether inhibition of these signaling pathways directly contributes to the antiviral effect of Cur-M, future studies using pathway-specific modulators or genetic approaches targeting key molecules in these pathways will be necessary.

In conclusion, this study proves for the first time that Cur in the form of Cur-M can inhibit several processes of intracellular IAV infection by effects including its limitation of viral genome replication and blockade of cellular signaling pathway induction by IAV. These results shed light on a novel strategy without safety concerns to prevent and control IAV infection using phytocompounds with possible clinical applications. It is worth noting that, in the current study, the anti-influenza effect of Cur-M has been revealed by monitoring several key steps of the viral replication cycle in cell models. At present, in vivo evaluation has not yet been performed. Further studies using animal models are required to evaluate the therapeutic and translational potential of Cur-M.
