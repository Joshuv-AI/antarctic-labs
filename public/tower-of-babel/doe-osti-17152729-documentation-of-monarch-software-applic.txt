1. Introduction

MONARCH software consists of several codes/input scripts developed for simulations of semi-
conductor and optoelectronic devices at normal service and under irradiated conditions, a soft-
ware product generated from LANL LDRD DR project 20240033DR “M3 ONARCH: Making,
Measuring, and Modeling Optoelectronics for Next-generation Applied Radiation-hard Compo-
nents and Hardware". It is distributed under LANL gitlab (https://lisdi-git.lanl.gov/)
and is available for download and installation.

The development is based on the MOOSE (Multiphysics Object-Oriented Simulation Environ-
ment) finite element framework, developed by Idaho National Laboratory, originally designed
for simulating complex thermomechanical and nuclear systems [1]. Two representative device
structures, a gallium arsenide (GaAs)-based PiN diode and a GaAs-based laser diode, are fo-
cused in the work. Modeling these devices requires solving a system of nonlinear partial differ-
ential equations (PDEs) that govern charge carrier transport, electrostatics, and electromagnetic
wave dynamics in optically active regions.

Besides the baseline model that simulates the devices, the “nonisothermal" model describes
additional physics incorporated to treat the effect of heat generation and dissipation in devices.
The model includes the thermal conduction equations, besides the self-consistent solution of
electrical and optical equations.

Radiation introduces ionization and atomic displacement damage, creating recombination cen-
ters that disrupt recombination dynamics. Modeling of the radiation induced carrier trapping
and defect evolution is the key to simulate the performance degradation. The cluster dynamics
approach, which is also implemented in the MOOSE framework via the Centipede code [2, 3],
is used to solve the myriad of coupled differential equations that describe charged and non-
charged defect transport and reactions (including reactions with carriers) under non-equilibrium
conditions.

The flow of this document is as follows: Firstly, the code installation from LANL gitlab (https:
//lisdi-git.lanl.gov/) repository is explained. This is followed by model descriptions of
various codes. Then, several test cases of the simulation codes are demonstrated, involving both
explanation of input scripts and selected simulation result as a validation check for readers.

Finally, for laser diode simulations, we currently use real number based waveguide sub-application
in all test cases in the main texts. The complex number based waveguide has been developed

 4

and we arrange the relevant information on the complex number based waveguide including the
installation and a test example in the appendix. Our initial evaluation suggests that the light
output after the threshold for typical laser diode simulations using the complex number based
waveguide changes less than 5% compared to the result using the real number based waveg-
uide.

built upon existing libraries. The primary MONARCH “App” consists of the MOOSE frame-

laser-diodes.

intrinsic region (i region) located between the p-type (P region) and n-type (N region) semicon-

drift–diffusion model, which accounts for charge transport under the influence of electric fields
and concentration gradients. This framework requires solving a set of coupled partial differ-

incorporating carrier generation, recombination, drift, and diffusion processes.

3.3. Radiation damage model

The radiation damage model simulates a uniform defect production rate across space, typically
representative of neutron irradiation in a reactor. To probe the dynamics of charged point defects
in a real device, we have implemented a cluster dynamics model [2, 3, 11] which incorporates
charge carriers and their interactions with point defects [12, 13] and parametrized by recent
density functional theory calculations [14]. The model uses coupled rate equations parametrized
by free energy data to represent the interactions between distinct species. The model includes
electrons, holes, donors, acceptors, the six types of simple point defects – Ga and As interstitials,
vacancies, and antisites – in each of the distinct charge states (-2, -1, 0, +1, +2) as distinct
species.

The master equation for the model is given as:

 ∂xi
           = ∑ R˙ j − ∇ · Ji + gi                                                                (3.26)
 ∂t j

Here, xi is the site fraction of species i. The species in the model include electrons, holes, and
the point defects Gai , Asi , VGa , VAs , GaAs , and AsGa . For defects, the site fraction is defined in
reference to the number density of intrinsic GaAs; for electrons and holes, the site fraction is
defined based on the effective densities of state in the conduction and valence bands, respectively.
∑ j R˙ j is the sum of all contributions by reactions between i and other species j, ∇·Ji is the spatial
transport term, and gi is the source term. For all simulations, the source term was chosen to be
uniform across space and time, and to source each defect charge state at an equal rate for mass
and charge conservation. Finally, the predicted defect density in the system is coupled to the
device model through non-radiated recombination rates.

4.1.6. Kernel Definition

A [Kernel] block defines a contribution to the governing physics and can represent one or more
terms or operators in the weak form of a partial differential equation (PDE). After all terms are
collected on the left-hand side of PDEs, the resulting expression is known as the residual.

For the Poisson equation, the Laplacian kernel represents the electrostatic potential term, where
the permittivity is used as the diffusion coefficient. The kernels Poisson_source_carrier_n,
Poisson_source_carrier_p, Poisson_source_carrier_Na, and Poisson_source_carrier_Nd
provide the space-charge source terms arising from electrons, holes, acceptor dopants, and donor
dopants, respectively.

For the electron continuity equation, the model includes a time-derivative term, a drift term
driven by the electric field (electron_advection), and a diffusion term (electron_diffusion).
Additional source terms (Rn1, Rn2, and Rn4) account for carrier recombination through the SRH,
spontaneous, and Auger recombination mechanisms.

Similarly, the hole continuity equation contains a time-derivative term, an electric-field-driven
drift term (hole_advection), and a diffusion term (hole_diffusion). The kernels Rp1, Rp2,

 22

and Rp4 describe the corresponding SRH, spontaneous, and Auger recombination processes
affecting the hole concentration.

Overall, these kernels solve the electrostatic potential together with the electron and hole trans-
port equations, including carrier drift, diffusion, and recombination effects.

4.1.7. AuxKernel Definition

This [AuxKernels] block computes several auxiliary quantities that are derived from the pri-
mary solution variables and are mainly used for post-processing and analysis of the semicon-
ductor device behavior.

The auxiliary variable thermal_voltage calculates the thermal voltage (VT = kB T /q) from the
local temperature field. The charge_density kernel evaluates the net space-charge density by
combining the contributions from electrons, holes, donor dopants, and acceptor dopants.

To determine the electric field, grad_V computes the spatial gradient of the electrostatic poten-
tial in the x-direction, and E_field evaluates the electric field as the negative potential gradi-
ent. Similarly, grad_electron and grad_hole calculate the carrier concentration gradients for
electrons and holes, respectively.

The electron current density is separated into diffusion and drift components. Jn_diff computes
the electron diffusion current using the carrier concentration gradient, thermal voltage, and elec-
tron mobility, while Jn_drift calculates the drift current generated by the electric field. These
two contributions are then combined in Jn to obtain the total electron current density.

Likewise, the hole current density is decomposed into diffusion (Jp_diff) and drift (Jp_drift)
components. The total hole current density is calculated in Jp as the sum of these two terms.

Finally, J computes the overall current density by summing the electron and hole current densi-
ties. Together, these auxiliary variables provide access to important physical quantities such as
charge density, electric field, carrier transport components, and total device current.

4.1.8. Material Definition

The material properties required for the drift-diffusion model are defined in the [Materials]
block. These properties include the elementary charge, intrinsic carrier concentration, carrier
mobilities, permittivity, thermal voltage, and charge density. The material system provides the
constitutive relationships required by the Poisson and carrier continuity equations.

 23

The recombination mechanisms are represented by four material properties. recombSRHn and
recombSRHp describe the electron and hole contributions to Shockley–Read–Hall (SRH) recom-
bination, respectively, using carrier lifetimes and carrier concentrations. recombAuger models
Auger recombination, where the recombination rate depends on both carrier concentrations and
the Auger coefficients. recombSP represents spontaneous (radiative) recombination, which is
proportional to the excess electron–hole product relative to equilibrium.

Carrier diffusivities are calculated through the Einstein relation. The electron_diffusivity
and hole_diffusivity material properties are computed from the corresponding carrier mo-
bilities, temperature, and the ratio kB /q, thereby linking diffusion coefficients to carrier transport
properties.

The carrier mobilities are specified separately for different device regions. electron_mobility_P,
electron_mobility_I, and electron_mobility_N define the electron mobility in the P, in-
trinsic, and N regions, respectively, using material-dependent values for AlGaAs and GaAs.
Similarly, neg_hole_mobility_P, neg_hole_mobility_I, and neg_hole_mobility_N de-
fine the hole mobility with a negative sign, which is used in the drift formulation to account for
the opposite charge polarity of holes.

Finally, the hole_mobility material property converts the negative hole mobility into its posi-
tive physical value for use in auxiliary calculations such as current-density evaluation.

Overall, this Materials block provides the transport parameters and recombination models nec-
essary to describe carrier motion and carrier loss mechanisms throughout the semiconductor
device.

4.1.9. Executioner and Time Stepper Definition

The [Executioner] block defines the numerical solution strategy to solve the coupled Poisson
and drift-diffusion equations in the PiN diode simulation. This block specifies the simulation
type, nonlinear solver, PETSc linear solver options, convergence criteria, time-step limits, and
steady-state detection settings. In this model, the simulation is formulated as a transient problem
and is advanced in time until a steady-state diode response is obtained.

The time-step range is controlled by dtmin = 1e-22 and dtmax = 1e-9, while num_steps =
1000 sets the maximum number of time steps. The nonlinear solver convergence criteria are
defined by nl_rel_tol = 1e-8 and nl_abs_tol = 1e-50, with nl_max_its = 20 limiting
the maximum nonlinear iterations per step and l_max_its = 100 limiting the linear solver iter-
ations. Steady-state detection is enabled through steady_state_detection = true, and the
simulation stops when the steady-state criterion reaches steady_state_tolerance = 6e-6.

Within the [Executioner] block, the nested [TimeStepper] block controls the adaptive time-

 24

step strategy. The IterationAdaptiveDT time stepper adjusts the time-step size based on non-
linear iteration behavior, allowing smaller steps when convergence is difficult and larger steps
when the solution evolves smoothly. The initial time-step size is set by dt = 1e-12. Together,
the executioner and time-stepper settings provide a robust numerical strategy for obtaining stable
and self-consistent PiN diode solutions under applied bias conditions.

4.3. 1D PiN diode: nonisothermal case

The nonisothermal case incorporates time-dependent temperature variation via a new temperature
variable, as well as temperature dependence for several physical parameters such as the mobil-
ities. Several changes are made to the input script in order to implement this. The input script
for the nonisothermal case is PiN_thermal.i.

There are a number of additional parameter definitions made in the opening portion of the input
script. The first of these is:
1   specific_heat = 0.325

This is the specific heat of GaAs in J/g · K. Note that this quantity is roughly constant from
room temperature through to melting, so we are free to define it as a simple constant. The
second parameter definition is:
1   density = 5.32e-12

This is the mass density of GaAs in g/µm3 , where the length unit µm is the same as that assumed
throughout the input script. The final parameter is:
1   alpha = 0.0

Here alpha is the quantity α - described in Section 3.2 - which appears in the expressions for the
thermal diffusion coefficients DTn and DTp . That α = 0 reflects the fact that the thermophoresis
terms in the current densities (Eq. (3.21) and Eq. (3.22)) are turned off in this model.

An additional variable, temperature, is added to the Variables block:
1 [temperature]
2 initial_condition = ${T0}
3 []

Here temperature is defined to have a uniform initial condition T0, which is defined as 300K
at the beginning of the input script. Boundary conditions for temperature are also added to the
BCs block:

 32

 1 [temperature_top]
 2 type = DirichletBC
 3 variable = temperature
 4 boundary = top
 5     value = ${T0}
 6 []
 7 [temperature_bottom]
 8 type = DirichletBC
 9 variable = temperature
10 boundary = bottom

12 []

This establishes that temperature has a fixed Dirichlet boundary condition of T0 at the top
and bottom of the domain. Note that such a boundary condition is equivalent to having the
entire device in thermal contact with a room temperature heat sink. This means that any heating
predictions made using this model will constitute a lower bound on the temperature rise in
the diode. The boundary condition 0.99999 * T0 used at the bottom of the diode may seem
peculiar, but it is necessary in order to force a non-zero residual in the calculation at t = 0.

A. Complex Waveguide

A.1. Complex waveguide model

The model equation is given as follows [5, Chapter 12],

        ∆E + k02 n2 E = β2 E,                                                             (A.1)

where E is the electric field, k0 = 2π/λ is the wave number, n is the refractive index, β is the
propagation constant. Suppose now we are in the complex domain, we write the matrix form of
Eq. (A.1) as

       (A + A j) − k02 n2 (M + M j) E = −β2 (M + M j) E,
  
 (A.2)

where A is the matrix form after discretizating −∆, M is the mass matrix, j is the imaginary
unit. The refractive index n is also complex,

        n = n1 + n2 j,     n2 = n21 − n22 + 2n1 n2 j.                                     (A.3)

Substitute Eq. (A.3) into Eq. (A.2), we have

        A − k02 n21 − n22 − 2n1 n2 M + A j − k02 n21 − n22 + 2n1 n2 M j E = −β2 (M + M j) E,
     

 (A.4)

where we note that

      A − k02 n21 − n22 − 2n1 n2 M
   
 (A.5)

is the real part of the matrix, and

        A − k02 n21 − n22 + 2n1 n2 M
   
 (A.6)

is the imaginary part of the matrix. We need to Eqs. (A.5) and (A.6) separately.

MOOSE does not support complex numbers. Fortunately, the real and imaginary parts of a
complex number are both real numbers. For instance, n1 and n2 in Eq. (A.3) are both real
numbers. Therefore, the order of the complex waveguide is

 1. Assemble matrices (the real and imaginary parts) in MOOSE.

 42

 • Equation (A.5), A − k02 n21 − n22 − 2n1 n2 M , is assembled by default in MOOSE
  

 • Equation (A.6), A − k02 n21 − n22 + 2n1 n2 M , is assembled using the additional
  

 matrix tag.

 2. MOOSE writes out the matrices in .dat file

 3. Python reads the .dat files and assemble the complex matrices using PETSC4PY.

 4. Solve the complex eigenvalue equation using SLEPC4PY.

 5. Python writes the results including eigenvalues and eigenvectors in .dat files.

 6. MOOSE reads the results and stores as solution. .

Assume the eigenvalue has the form β = β1 + β2 j, and its corresponding eigenvector is E =
E1 + E2 j, then the output of the eigenvalue is

       β2 = β21 − β22 + 2β1 β2 j.                                                                       (A.7)

and its corresponding eigenvector in the output is

       E = |E1 | + |E2 |.                                                                               (A.8)
