Optimal Demixing of Nonparametric Densities
Jianqing Fan jqfan@princeton.edu, Zheng Tracy Ke zke@fas.harvard.edu, Zhaoyang Shi zshi@fas.harvard.edu

Abstract
Motivated by applications in statistics and machine learning, we consider a problem of unmixing convex combinations of nonparametric densities. Suppose we observe n n groups of samples, where the i i th group consists of N i N_{i} independent samples from a d d -variate density f i ​ ( x ) = ∑ k = 1 K π i ​ ( k ) ​ g k ​ ( x ) f_{i}(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{i}(k)g_{k}(x) . Here, each g k ​ ( x ) g_{k}(x) is a nonparametric density, and each π i \pi_{i} is a K K -dimensional mixed membership vector. We aim to estimate g 1 ​ ( x ) , … , g K ​ ( x ) g_{1}(x),\ldots,g_{K}(x) . This problem generalizes topic modeling from discrete to continuous variables and finds its applications in LLMs with word embeddings. In this paper, we propose an estimator for the above problem, which modifies the classical kernel density estimator by assigning group-specific weights that are computed by topic modeling on histogram vectors and de-biased by U-statistics. For any β > 0 \beta>0 , assuming that each g k ​ ( x ) g_{k}(x) is in the Nikol’ski class with a smooth parameter β \beta , we show that the sum of integrated squared errors of the constructed estimators has a convergence rate that depends on n n , K K , d d , and the per-group sample size N N . We also provide a matching lower bound, which suggests that our estimator is rate-optimal.

Introduction

Linear unmixing is a technique widely used in hyperspectral imaging
(
8
)
, chemical engineering
(
24
;
5
)
, and biomedical data analysis
(
13
;
35
)
. Given
n
data points, it aims to find
K
base vectors (‘end members’) such that each data point is a convex combination of base vectors, where
K
is often much smaller than
n
. In this paper, we consider a linear unmixing problem on the space of nonparametric densities. Suppose
f_{1}(x),\ldots,f_{n}(x)
and
g_{1}(x),\ldots,g_{K}(x)
are densities on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfying that

f_{i}(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{i}(k)g_{k}(x),\qquad\mbox{for $1\leq i\leq n$},

(1.1)

where each
\pi_{i}
takes value in the standard probability simplex
{\cal S}_{0}\subset\mathbb{R}^{K}
(i.e., the entries of
\pi_{i}
are nonzero and sum to
1
).
We further assume that
N_{i}
samples are drawn from each
f_{i}(x)
:

X_{i1},X_{i2},\ldots,X_{iN_{i}}\;\overset{iid}{\sim}\;f_{i}(x).

(1.2)

The goal is to use
\{X_{ij}\}_{1\leq i\leq n,1\leq j\leq N_{i}}
to estimate the base densities
g_{1}(x),\ldots,g_{K}(x)
.

When
K=1
, all
X_{ij}
’s are independent samples from a single density
g_{1}(x)
, so the problem reduces to classical nonparametric density estimation. When
K>1
, however, the setting is fundamentally different, with few results in the literature. In this paper, we develop both new methodology and minimax theory for the above problem.

Our study is motivated by several application examples:

•

Topic modeling with word embeddings
: Traditional topic modeling is based on word count data. Modern large language models (LLMs) provide word embeddings that contain richer information than word counts. Recently,

4
introduce a new topic model that is equivalent to (
1.1
)-(
1.2
), where
X_{ij}
is the contextualized word embedding of the
j
th word in the
i
th document, and each
g_{k}(x)
represents an abstract “topic”, depicting the distribution of the word embedding in topic
k
. The estimated
g_{1}(x),\ldots,g_{K}(x)
are useful for uncovering the underlying topic structure in a corpus.

•

Archetypal analysis on distributional data
: Archetypal analysis
(
11
)
aims to express
n
data points as convex combinations of
K
latent archetypes. In the original setting, ‘data’ and ‘archetypes’ are feature vectors, but in certain applications they can be distributional data
(
6
;
37
)
.
Existing methods typically apply the kernel trick, but the formulation in (
1.1
)-(
1.2
) provides an alternative solution to archetypal analysis on distributional data.

•

Decontamination of mutual contamination models
:
19
consider mutual contamination models, in which a learner observes random samples from different convex combinations of a set of unknown base distributions. The goal is to infer the base distributions (i.e., decontamination). Our model
in (
1.1
)-(
1.2
)
can be viewed a special mutual contamination model, where each base distribution has a continuous density.
The decontamination problem has several applications, such as in multi-source domain adaption
(
40
)

1.1
Why classical approaches do not work

Kernel density estimator (KDE) is a text-book method for nonparametric density estimation. It assumes that i.i.d. samples are observed from the density to be estimated.
Let
{\cal K}(\cdot)
be a kernel function such that
\int{\cal K}(x)dx=1
, and write
{\cal K}_{h}(x):=\frac{1}{h^{d}}{\cal K}\bigl(\frac{1}{h}x\bigr)
for brevity.
In light of (
1.2
), the KDE for
f_{i}(x)
is

\hat{f}_{i}^{\text{KDE}}(x)=\frac{1}{N_{i}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{i}}{\cal K}_{h}(X_{ij}-x),\qquad 1\leq i\leq n.

(1.3)

However, how to use
\hat{f}_{1}^{\text{KDE}}(x),\ldots,\hat{f}_{n}^{\text{KDE}}(x)
to estimate
g_{1}(x),\ldots,g_{K}(x)
is unclear.
Moreover, the rate of estimating each
f_{i}(x)
only depends on
N_{i}
, but we expect a much faster rate of convergence on estimating
g_{k}(x)
, which should depend on
\sum_{i=1}^{n}N_{i}
.

In the mixture density estimation (MDE) problem, we observe i.i.d. samples from a density

f(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\beta_{k}g(x;\lambda_{k})
, where
g(x;\lambda)
is a parametric density family, and
\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\ldots,\beta_{K}
are the mixing weights. This model can be recast as a latent variable model
and solved by the expectation-maximization (EM) algorithm.
In a similar spirit, we introduce a parametric version of the model in (
1.1
)-(
1.2
) by

\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{n}\,\overset{iid}{\sim}\,\mathrm{Dirichlet}(\alpha),\quad\;\;X_{i1},\ldots,X_{iN_{i}}|\pi_{i}\;\overset{iid}{\sim}\;f_{i}(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{i}(k)g(\cdot;\lambda_{k}),

(1.4)

and the parameters can be estimated by the variational EM algorithm
(
9
)
.
Unfortunately,
this requires each
g_{k}(x)
to have a parametric form.

There exist approaches that approximate a nonparametric density by parametric ones, primarily for the case of estimating a single density
g(x)
. The Dirichlet Process Mixture Model (DPMM)
(
15
)
expresses a nonparametric density as an infinite mixture of parametric densities, with mixing weights generated from a ‘stick-breaking construction’ process.
The variational auto-encoder (VAE) expresses a nonparametric density as an infinite mixture of normal densities
(
12
)
, parametrized by neural networks.
However, if we use these ideas to extend the model in (
1.4
) to a nonparametric version, several issues arise: How to design a valid estimation procedure?
Is the obtained
\hat{g}_{k}(x)
smooth? What rate of convergence can be guaranteed? There are no clear answers.

In the machine learning literature,
19
propose an interesting method for estimating
g_{1}(x),\ldots,g_{K}(x)
when
n=K
. Let
\hat{F}_{i}(x)
be the empirical distribution associated with
X_{i1},\ldots,X_{iN_{i}}
. This method computes the “residual” of each
\hat{F}_{i}
after removing a maximal linear effect of
\{\hat{F}_{j}:j\neq i\}
. These “residuals” are then used to construct estimators of base distributions. However, the estimated distributions typically do not admit smooth densities; moreover, this method relies on strong conditions on the mixing weights
\pi_{i}
, which may be hard to satisfy in practice.

1.2
The induced topic models

Interestingly, the model in (
1.1
)-(
1.2
) has a connection to the topic model
(
10
)
, which is widely used in text analysis. In detail, fix a partition of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
into
M
bins,

\mathbb{R}^{d}=\cup_{m=1}^{M}{\cal B}_{m},

(1.5)

and construct a ‘multivariate histogram’ vector
Y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
for the
i
th group of samples by

Y_{i}(m)=|\{X_{ij}:1\leq j\leq N_{i}\}\cap{\cal B}_{m}|,\qquad 1\leq m\leq M.

(1.6)

Following (
1.2
), it is easy to see that
Y_{i}\sim\mathrm{Multinomial}(N_{i},\Omega^{\cal B}_{i})
, where
\Omega^{\cal B}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
is a vector such that
\Omega^{\cal B}_{i}(m)=\int_{x\in{\cal B}_{m}}f_{i}(x)dx
. Combining this with the expression of
f_{i}(x)
in (
1.1
), we immediately have the following result:

Y_{i}\sim\mathrm{Multinimial}\biggl(N_{i},\;\;\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{i}(k)g^{\cal B}_{k}\biggr),\quad\mbox{where }g^{\cal B}_{k}(m)=\int_{x\in{\cal B}_{m}}g_{k}(x)dx.

(1.7)

This is in fact a topic model with
n
documents,
K
topics, and a size-
M
vocabulary, where
g_{1}^{\cal B},\ldots,g_{K}^{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
are the “topic vectors” and
\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
are the “topic weight vectors.”

Inspired by this connection,
4
propose a two-step method for estimating the densities
g_{1}(x),\ldots,g_{K}(x)
:

•

Apply topic modeling on the matrix
Y=[Y_{1},Y_{2},\ldots,Y_{n}]
to obtain
\hat{g}_{1}^{\cal B},\ldots,\hat{g}_{K}^{\cal B}
.

•

For each
1\leq k\leq K
, construct
\hat{g}_{k}(x)
from the entries of
\hat{g}_{k}^{\cal B}
by kernel smoothing.

This provides the first method for the nonparametric linear unmixing problem in (
1.1
)-(
1.2
) with a provable rate of convergence.
Unfortunately, the rate does not match the information-theoretic lower bound when
g_{1}(x),\ldots,g_{K}(x)
have high smoothness (see Section
4.4
).

In this paper, we introduce a new estimator, which also leverages the induced topic model in (
1.7
), but in a fundamentally different manner. We show that the new estimator addresses the above limitation and is minimax optimal.

1.3
A new estimator

Let
Y
be the same as defined in (
1.6
). Suppose we have applied an existing topic modeling algorithm on
Y
, and let
\widehat{G}=[\hat{g}_{1}^{\cal B},\ldots,\hat{g}_{K}^{\cal B}]
be the estimated topic matrix.
Our estimator is inspired by several key insights:

•

First, we consider an idealized case where
\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{n}
are known. In this case, we construct an ideal estimator for
g_{k}(x)
, which is a weighted KDE by leveraging these known
\pi_{i}
’s.

•

Next, we develop an estimator of
\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{n}
based on the estimated topic matrix
\widehat{G}
and we plug
\hat{\pi}_{1},\ldots,\hat{\pi}_{n}
into the ideal estimator. This is a natural plug-in estimator for
g_{k}(x)
, but unfortunately it has a large bias.

•

Finally, we de-bias the above plug-in estimator by replacing some quadratic terms by corresponding U-statistics.

The detailed derivation and rationale are deferred to Section
2
, but let us describe the estimator first.
With the notation in (
1.5
)-(
1.6
), we define a matrix
T\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}
with elements

T_{mm^{\prime}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{N_{i}(N_{i}-1)}\sum_{1\leq j\neq j^{\prime}\leq N_{i}}U_{ijm}U_{ij^{\prime}m^{\prime}},\quad\mbox{where}\;\;U_{ijm}=1\{X_{ij}\in{\cal B}_{m}\}.

(1.8)

For any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we define a matrix
S(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times n}
by

S_{mi}(x)=\frac{1}{N_{i}(N_{i}-1)}\sum_{1\leq j\neq j^{\prime}\leq N_{i}}K_{ij}(x)U_{ij^{\prime}m},\quad\mbox{where}\;\;K_{ij}(x)={\cal K}_{h}(x-X_{ij}),

(1.9)

where
{\cal K}_{h}
is defined in (
1.3
).
Our proposed estimator has the form:

\widehat{\bm{g}}(x)=\widehat{G}^{\prime}\widehat{G}(\widehat{G}^{\prime}T\widehat{G})^{-1}\widehat{G}^{\prime}S(x){\bf 1}_{n},

(1.10)

where for each
1\leq k\leq K
,
\hat{g}_{k}(x)
is the
k
th entry of
\widehat{\bm{g}}(x)
.

Here, each
\hat{g}_{k}(x)
is a weighted U-statistic, with weights depending on the estimated topic matrix
\widehat{G}
. It has an entirely different form from the estimator in
4
.
As a consequence, the choice of the bin size
M
is also fundamentally different.
They choose
M
as a power of
Nn
, whereas we simply set
M\asymp K\log(Nn)
;
see Section
2
.

1.4
The minimax optimal rate

For any fixed
\beta>0
, let
\Theta^{*}_{\beta}
denote the Nikol’ski class
(
31
)
of smooth probability densities with a smoothness parameter
\beta
.
Write for brevity
\bm{g}(x)=(g_{1}(x),\ldots,g_{K}(x))^{\prime}
and
\widehat{\bm{g}}(x)=(\hat{g}_{1}(x),\ldots,\hat{g}_{K}(x))^{\prime}
. We denote
\bm{g}(x)\in\Theta^{*}_{\beta}
when each individual
g_{k}(x)
belongs to the Nikol’ski class. One of our main results is the minimax rate of convergence for the integrated squared error: Suppose
N_{i}\asymp N
for
1\leq i\leq n
. As
nN\to\infty
, if
K=O((Nn)^{c^{*}_{\beta,d}})
, where
c^{*}_{\beta,d}\in(0,1/4)
is a constant that only depends on
\beta
, then up to a permutation of
\hat{g}_{1}(x),\ldots,\hat{g}_{K}(x)
,

\inf_{\widehat{\bm{g}}(x)}\sup_{\bm{g}(x)\in\Theta^{*}_{\beta}}\mathbb{E}\Biggl[\sum_{k=1}^{K}\int[\hat{g}_{k}(x)-g_{k}(x)]^{2}dx\Biggr]\;\;\asymp\;\;K\left(\frac{K}{Nn}\right)^{\frac{2\beta}{2\beta+d}}.

(1.11)

To establish (
1.11
), we need a minimax upper bound and a minimax lower bound.
The minimax upper bound is obtained by analyzing the error rate of our estimator. In the proof, we have developed a large-deviation inequality for a specific class of incomplete U-statistics and an integrated variance bound for incomplete U-processes. The minimax lower bound is proved by choosing a least-favorable configuration to uncover the role of a diverging
K
.

1.5
Summary, organization and notations

We consider unmixing convex combinations of nonparametric densities, a problem with applications in topic modeling, archetypal analysis and multi-source domain adaption. Compared to classical nonparametric density estimation, this problem is more challenging and much less studied. Our contributions include:

•

We propose a new estimator, which are significantly different from existing methods (e.g.,
19
;
4
).

•

We derive an explicit rate of convergence for our estimator. Our results allow both
N
and
K
to grow with
n
and cover a full range of smoothness on the base densities.

•

We provide a matching lower bound and show that our estimator is optimal. In contrast, existing estimators either have no explicit error rate or suffer a non-optimal rate.

•

As a component of our analysis, we also provide a new theoretical result on estimating a topic model under a growing
K
, which is of independent interest.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows:
In Section
2
, we derive our estimator and explain the insights. Section
3
discusses the topic modeling on
Y
, focusing an existing algorithm Topic-SCORE.
Our main theoretical results are presented in Section
4
.
Simulation studies are contained in Section
5
.
Sections
6
-
7
prove the minimax upper bound and the minimax lower bound, respectively. Section
8
concludes the paper with discussions. Proofs of secondary lemmas are relegated to the supplementary material.

2
Topic-Weighted Kernel Density Estimator

In this section, we explain the rationale of our proposed estimator (
1.10
). We show that it is equivalent to a de-biased weighted kernel density estimator with estimated weights from topic modeling.

2.1
An oracle estimator when
\Pi
is known

We first consider an oracle setting where the mixed membership matrix
\Pi=[\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{n}]^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times K}
is given. Let
{\bm{g}}(x)=(g_{1}(x),\ldots,g_{K}(x))^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
and
{\bm{f}}(x)=(f_{1}(x),\ldots,f_{n}(x))^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. It follows from the model in (
1.1
) that

{\bm{f}}(x)=\Pi{\bm{g}}(x)\qquad\mbox{or}\qquad{\bm{g}}(x)=(\Pi^{\prime}\Pi)^{-1}\Pi^{\prime}{\bm{f}}(x)

The unknown density vector
{\bm{f}}(x)
can be estimated by
\widehat{\bm{f}}^{\text{KDE}}(x)=(\hat{f}^{\text{KDE}}_{1}(x),\ldots,\hat{f}^{\text{KDE}}_{n}(x))^{\prime}
, where
\hat{f}_{i}^{\text{KDE}}(x)
is given by (
1.3
). This leads naturally to

\widehat{\bm{g}}^{\text{oracle}}(x)=(\Pi^{\prime}\Pi)^{-1}\Pi^{\prime}\widehat{\bm{f}}^{\text{KDE}}(x).

The
k^{th}
component of the above estimator can be written as

\hat{g}_{k}^{\text{oracle}}(x)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}(k)\hat{f}_{i}^{\text{KDE}}(x),

(2.1)

where
b_{i}(k)
is given by

B=(n^{-1}\Pi^{\prime}\Pi)^{-1}\Pi^{\prime}:=[b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{n}]\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times n}.

(2.2)

It is the weight on the
i
th group of samples when they are used to estimate
g_{k}(x)
.
Putting more explicitly, we propose a weighted kernel density estimator (KDE):

\hat{g}_{k}^{\text{oracle}}(x)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}(k)\frac{1}{N_{i}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{i}}{\cal K}_{h}(x-X_{ij}),\qquad 1\leq k\leq K,

(2.3)

The rate of convergence of this oracle estimator can be fairly easily derived (see the supplement), and it matches the lower bound to be introduced in Section
4
. We thereby use this estimator as a good starting point.

2.2
Estimation of
\Pi
and the plug-in estimator

To extend the oracle estimator to the real case, we need an estimate of
\Pi
. In light of the induced topic model in (
1.7
), we can apply topic modeling to
Y
.
However, if we directly apply an existing topic modeling algorithm on estimating
\Pi
, the rate of convergence is not fast enough. Assuming
K
is finite, the minimax optimal rate is
(
23
;
38
)

\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\|\hat{\pi}_{i}-\pi_{i}\|_{1}=O_{\mathbb{P}}\bigl(N^{-1/2}\bigr).

This rate does not even decrease with
n
; consequently, the plug-in error caused by estimating
\Pi
is too large. In order to get the desirable rate, we must account for the dependence among
\hat{\pi}_{i}
’s. This is made possible if

\mbox{each $\hat{\pi}_{i}$ can be well-approximated by a simple function of $X$}.

(2.4)

To find an estimator to satisfy (
2.4
), we notice that, while the rate on estimating
\pi_{i}
is slow, the rate on estimating the topic matrix
G
can be made very fast. In fact, the minimax optimal rate
(
22
;
7
)
is

\sum_{k=1}^{K}\|\hat{g}_{k}-g_{k}\|_{1}=\widetilde{O}_{\mathbb{P}}\left(K\sqrt{\frac{KM}{Nn}}\right).

(2.5)

Here,
M
is the bin size that a user can choose. If we choose
M
appropriately small, then the rate on estimating
G
can be much faster than the optimal rate in (
1.11
),
so that we can almost treat
G
as known.

The above observation inspires us to consider a regression estimator of
\Pi
. In detail, let
L\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be a diagonal matrix whose
i
th diagonal entry is equal to
N_{i}
. It follows from (
1.7
) that
\mathbb{E}Y=G\Pi^{\prime}L
. We thereby estimate
\Pi
by

\displaystyle\widehat{\Pi}=L^{-1}Y^{\prime}\widehat{G}(\widehat{G}^{\prime}\widehat{G})^{-1}.

(2.6)

Since the plug-in error rate in
\widehat{G}
is much faster than the desirable rate, we can almost treat
G
as known and approximate the
\widehat{\Pi}
in (
2.6
) by

\widehat{\Pi}\quad\approx\quad\widehat{\Pi}^{*}:=L^{-1}Y^{\prime}G(G^{\prime}G)^{-1}.

(2.7)

The right hand side of (
2.7
) is a linear function of
Y
, and
Y
is constructed from
X_{ij}
’s explicitly. Hence, the estimator
\widehat{\Pi}
satisfies (
2.4
) as we hope.

Note that this estimator does not yield a faster rate on estimating
\Pi
than existing estimators (e.g.,
23
;
38
). The advantage lies in avoiding a major analytical hurdle when we use
\widehat{\Pi}
to construct other estimators: Since the dependence among
\hat{\pi}_{1},\ldots,\hat{\pi}_{n}
can be quantified explicitly as in (
2.7
), we obtain a much tighter control on the plug-in error.
On the other hand, this advantage is tied to our problem: As the choice of bins vary, we have many induced topic models, and all of them share the same
\Pi
. This gives us the freedom to pick a proper induced topic model so that (
2.7
) holds.

We now plug the
\widehat{\Pi}
in (
2.6
) into the oracle estimator in (
2.2
)-(
2.3
). Write for brevity

\widehat{Q}:=\widehat{G}(\widehat{G}^{\prime}\widehat{G})^{-1},\qquad\widetilde{W}:=n(\widehat{Q}^{\prime}YL^{-2}Y^{\prime}\widehat{Q})^{-1}\widehat{Q}^{\prime}.

(2.8)

We use
\tilde{w}_{i}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
to denote the
i
th row of
\widetilde{W}
.
It is not hard to see that the plug-in estimator has the following form:

\hat{g}_{k}^{\text{plug-in}}(x):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{N_{i}^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\tilde{w}_{m}(k){\cal K}_{h}(x-X_{ij})Y_{i}(m),\quad 1\leq k\leq K.

(2.9)

We write
\widehat{\bm{g}}^{\text{plug-in}}(x)=(\hat{g}^{\text{plug-in}}_{1}(x),\ldots,\hat{g}^{\text{plug-in}}_{K}(x))^{\prime}
for brevity.

2.3
De-biasing the plug-in estimator

Unfortunately, the plug-in estimator in (
2.9
) has a non-negligible bias.
In this subsection, we explain where the bias comes from and introduce a de-biasing technique.
The following lemma is proved in the supplemental material:

Lemma 1
.

Let
\mathbb{R}^{d}=\cup_{m=1}^{M}{\cal B}_{m}
be the bins and
\widehat{Q}
be as in (
2.8
). Write
U_{ijm}=1\{X_{ij}\in{\cal B}_{m}\}
, for all
1\leq i\leq n
,
1\leq j\leq N_{i}
and
1\leq m\leq M
. Then, the plug-in estimator in (
2.9
) satisfies that
\widehat{\bm{g}}^{\text{plug-in}}(x)=\widetilde{\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}\widetilde{\bm{h}}(x)
, where
\widetilde{\bm{h}}(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
and
\widetilde{\bm{\Sigma}}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times K}
are defined by

\displaystyle\widetilde{\bm{h}}_{k}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{N_{i}^{2}}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{j,j^{\prime}=1}^{N_{i}}\widehat{Q}_{mk}{\cal K}_{h}(x-X_{ij})U_{ij^{\prime}m},\quad 1\leq k\leq K,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\bm{\Sigma}}_{k\ell}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{N_{i}^{2}}\sum_{m,m^{\prime}=1}^{M}\sum_{j,j^{\prime}=1}^{N_{i}}\widehat{Q}_{mk}\widehat{Q}_{m^{\prime}\ell}U_{ijm}U_{ij^{\prime}m^{\prime}},\quad 1\leq k,\ell\leq K.

Lemma
1
gives an equivalent form of the plug-in estimator. We first study the matrix
\widetilde{\bm{\Sigma}}
. Let
\bm{U}_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
be the vector consisting of
\{U_{ijm}:1\leq m\leq M\}
. For each
i
,
\bm{U}_{i1},\ldots,\bm{U}_{iN_{i}}
are i.i.d. from
\mathrm{Multinomial}(1,\xi_{i})
, with
\xi_{i}:=\mathbb{E}[\bm{U}_{i1}]
. It is seen that

\widetilde{\bm{\Sigma}}=\widehat{Q}^{\prime}\biggl[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{\xi_{i}\xi_{i}^{\prime}}\biggr]\widehat{Q},\qquad\mbox{where}\quad\widehat{\xi_{i}\xi_{i}^{\prime}}:=\frac{1}{N^{2}_{i}}\sum_{j,j^{\prime}=1}^{N_{i}}\bm{U}_{ij}\bm{U}^{\prime}_{ij^{\prime}}.

However,
\mathbb{E}[\bm{U}_{ij}\bm{U}^{\prime}_{ij^{\prime}}]\neq\xi_{i}\xi_{i}^{\prime}
when
j=j^{\prime}
. Therefore, the estimator
\widehat{\xi_{i}\xi_{i}^{\prime}}
is biased. To fix this issue, we consider the following U-statistics, which employ a diagonal deletion strategy by excluding
j=j^{\prime}
in the sum:

\widehat{\xi_{i}\xi_{i}^{\prime}}^{*}:=\frac{1}{N_{i}(N_{i}-1)}\sum_{1\leq j\neq j^{\prime}\leq N_{i}}\bm{U}_{ij}\bm{U}^{\prime}_{ij^{\prime}}.

This is an unbiased estimator of
\xi_{i}\xi_{i}^{\prime}
.
Replacing
\widehat{\xi_{i}\xi_{i}^{\prime}}
by this estimator gives a modification of
\widetilde{\bm{\Sigma}}
in (
2.10
) below. We also modify
\widetilde{\bm{h}}_{k}(x)
by a similar diagonal deletion strategy.
Let
\widehat{\bm{h}}(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
and
\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times K}
be such that

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{h}}_{k}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{N_{i}(N_{i}-1)}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{1\leq j\neq j^{\prime}\leq N_{i}}\widehat{Q}_{mk}{\cal K}_{h}(x-X_{ij})U_{ij^{\prime}m},\quad 1\leq k\leq K,

(2.10)

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{k\ell}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{N_{i}(N_{i}-1)}\sum_{m,m^{\prime}=1}^{M}\sum_{1\leq j\neq j^{\prime}\leq N_{i}}\widehat{Q}_{mk}\widehat{Q}_{m^{\prime}\ell}U_{ijm}U_{ij^{\prime}m^{\prime}},\;\;1\leq k,\ell\leq K.

(2.11)

Our final estimator of
\bm{g}(x)
is the diagnonal deletion version:

\widehat{{\bm{g}}}(x)=(\hat{g}_{1}(x),\ldots,\hat{g}_{K}(x))^{\prime}:=\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}\widehat{\bm{h}}(x).

(2.12)

Recall that

\widehat{Q}=\widehat{G}(\widehat{G}^{\prime}\widehat{G})^{-1}
and
Q=G(G^{\prime}G)^{-1}
.
Using the matrices
S
and
T
defined in (
1.8
)-(
1.9
), we can re-express
\widehat{{\bm{h}}}(x)
and
\widehat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}
in (
2.10
) by

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{h}}(x)=n^{-1}\widehat{Q}^{\prime}S(x){\bf 1}_{n}=n^{-1}(\widehat{G}^{\prime}\widehat{G})^{-1}\widehat{G}^{\prime}S(x){\bf 1}_{n},

(2.13)

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}=n^{-1}\widehat{Q}^{\prime}T\widehat{Q}=n^{-1}(\widehat{G}^{\prime}\widehat{G})^{-1}\widehat{G}^{\prime}T\widehat{G}(\widehat{G}^{\prime}\widehat{G})^{-1}.

(2.14)

It follows immediately that

\widehat{\bm{g}}(x)=\widehat{G}^{\prime}\widehat{G}(\widehat{G}^{\prime}T\widehat{G})^{-1}\widehat{G}^{\prime}S(x){\bf 1}_{n}.

(2.15)

This simplifies the expression in (
2.12
), which is also the one we introduce in (
1.10
).

2.4
The choice of tuning parameters and bins

Our method requires two tuning parameters, the bandwidth
h
and the bin size
M
. In addition, given
M
, we need to choose the bins
{\cal B}_{1},\ldots,{\cal B}_{M}
.
In this subsection, we discuss how to determine them in practice.

First, based on our theory in Section
4
, the optimal
M
is at the order of
K\log(Nn)
, which does not depend on unknown parameters. In Section
5
, we also observe that the performance of our estimator is insensitive to
M
in a wide range (see Figure
2
).
We thus fix
M
as

M=2\lfloor K\log(Nn)\rfloor.

(2.16)

Next, we discuss how to choose the bins
{\cal B}_{1},\ldots,{\cal B}_{M}
.
Let
\bar{g}(x)=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}g_{k}(x)
.
For our theoretical analysis to carry through, we hope:

\int_{x\in{\cal B}_{m}}\bar{g}(x)dx\asymp 1/M,\qquad\mbox{for all bins }{\cal B}_{1},{\cal B}_{2},\ldots,{\cal B}_{M}.

(2.17)

When
\bar{g}(x)
has a bounded support and is uniformly lower bounded by a constant, we can use a simple equi-size cubic bins, which guarantees (
2.17
).
In the more general case where
\bar{g}(x)
has unbounded support, we can show that as long as the
K
entries of
\alpha=\Pi^{\prime}\mathrm{diag}(N_{1},\ldots,N_{n}){\bf 1}_{n}
are of the same order,
(
2.17
) is equivalent to

\mathbb{E}[\#\{X_{ij}\in{\cal B}_{m}\}]\asymp\bar{N}n/M.

(2.18)

This inspires a practical choice of bins: we make sure that the total number of samples falling into each bin is of the order
\bar{N}n/M
. For example, when
d=1
, we can sort

X_{ij}
’s and let
\delta_{m}
be the
m/M
quantile. The bins are

(-\infty,\delta_{1}]\cup(\delta_{1},\delta_{2}]\cup\ldots\cup(\delta_{M-2},\delta_{M-1}]\cup(\delta_{M-1},\infty).

(2.19)

Finally, we describe how to select
h
. The optimal
h
depends on the unknown smoothness of base densities. Therefore, we face the same challenge as in classical nonparametric density estimation.
Fortunately, there is a careful study of bandwidth selection in that literature. One approach is to minimize the asymptotic mean integrated error squared error (AMISE), which can be estimated by leave-one-out cross validation (abbreviated as CV; e.g., see
32
) or plug-in estimators (e.g., see
17
).
Another approach is the Lepski’s method
(
28
)
, which takes an ordered sequence of
h
and finds
\hat{h}
such that the resulting estimator does not differ too much from all estimators from smaller bandwidths. We can possibly extend these approaches to our setting. For convenience, we only consider the CV approach. Following
36
, we define AMSE as

\text{AMISE}(h;\widehat{\bm{g}})=\int\|\widehat{\bm{g}}(x)\|^{2}dx-2\int\bigl\langle\bm{g}(x),\widehat{\bm{g}}(x)\bigr\rangle dx:=\int\|\widehat{\bm{g}}(x)\|^{2}dx-J(h;\widehat{\bm{g}}).

Only the second term
\bm{g}(x)=B\bm{f}(x)
is unknown, where
B=(n^{-1}\Pi^{\prime}\Pi)^{-1}\Pi^{\prime}=[b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{n}]
is as in (
2.2
). Let
\{X^{*}_{ij}\}
be an independent copy of
\{X_{ij}\}
. It is seen that

J(h;\widehat{\bm{g}})=\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int b_{i}^{\prime}\widehat{\bm{g}}(x)f_{i}(x)dx=\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Biggl\{\frac{1}{N_{i}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{i}}b_{i}^{\prime}\mathbb{E}\bigl[\widehat{\bm{g}}(X^{*}_{ij})\bigr]\Biggr\}.

Borrowing the CV idea, we leave-out each
X_{ij}
and use the remaining samples to construct
\widehat{\bm{g}}_{(-ij)}
. This also yields
\widehat{\Pi}_{-(ij)}
as an intermediate quantity, which we plug into (
2.6
) to obtain
\widehat{B}_{-(ij)}=[\hat{b}_{1(-ij)},\ldots,\hat{b}_{n(-ij)}]
.
We select
h
by minimizing the following criteria:

\widehat{\text{AMISE}}(h;\widehat{\bm{g}})=\int\|\widehat{\bm{g}}(x)\|^{2}dx-\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Biggl[\frac{1}{N_{i}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{i}}\hat{b}_{i(-ij)}^{\prime}\widehat{\bm{g}}_{(-ij)}(X_{ij})\Biggr].

(2.20)

This procedure requires conducting topic modeling for
\sum_{i=1}^{n}N_{i}
times. To save the computational cost, we propose a modified version that only runs topic modeling once.
Note that
B=\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\Pi^{\prime}
. We plug in
\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}
in (
2.13
) and
\widehat{\Pi}
in (
2.6
) to get
\widehat{B}=n(\widehat{G}^{\prime}\widehat{G})(\widehat{G}^{\prime}T\widehat{G})^{-1}\widehat{G}^{\prime}YL^{-1}
.
Here, only
\widehat{G}
comes from topic modeling. Similarly, in the expression of
\widehat{\bm{g}}
in (
2.15
), only
\widehat{G}
comes from topic modeling. Therefore, we use all data to obtain
\widehat{G}
but perform leave-one-out in the remaining quantities. This gives the following leave-one-out estimators:

\displaystyle\widetilde{B}_{(-ij)}=n(\widehat{G}^{\prime}\widehat{G})\bigl[\widehat{G}^{\prime}T_{(-ij)}\widehat{G}\bigr]^{-1}\widehat{G}^{\prime}(YL^{-1})_{-(ij)},

(2.21)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\bm{g}}_{-(ij)}(x)=\widehat{G}^{\prime}\widehat{G}\bigl[\widehat{G}^{\prime}T_{(-ij)}\widehat{G}\bigr]^{-1}\widehat{G}^{\prime}S_{(-ij)}(x){\bf 1}_{n},

(2.22)

where
T_{(-ij)}
,
S_{(-ij)}(x)
and
(YL^{-1})_{-(ij)}
are the corresponding leave-one-out versions of
T
,
S(x)
and
YL^{-1}
, respectively.
Let
\widetilde{\text{AMISE}}(h;\widehat{\bm{g}})
be a counterpart of (
2.20
), where we modify the second term using the quantities in (
2.21
).
We select
h
by minimizing
\widetilde{\text{AMISE}}(h;\widehat{\bm{g}})
.
This procedure only requires conducting topic modeling once.

2.5
Comparison with existing estimators

There are few existing methods for our problem.
19
consider a similar model where the base distributions do not necessarily have smooth densities. They define a quantity

\begin{array}[]{rl}\kappa^{*}(f_{i}|\{f_{j},j\neq i\})=\max\bigl\{\kappa\in[0,1]:&\mbox{there exists $R_{i}$ such that $f_{i}=\kappa R_{i}+\sum_{j\neq i}\nu_{j}f_{j}$ for}\\
&\mbox{some $\nu_{j}\geq 0$ with $\sum_{j\neq i}\nu_{j}=1-\kappa$}.\bigr\}\end{array}

The resulting
R_{i}
is also a distribution and called a “residual” of
f_{i}
.
19
show that
R_{i}
can be estimated from the empirical CDFs
\hat{F}_{1},\ldots,\hat{F}_{n}
. They also show that when
n=K
and when
\Pi
satisfies certain conditions,
the base distributions can be recovered by recursively computing such “residuals.”
The key insight is to exploit a simplex geometry in the distribution space.

However, this approach imposes restrictive conditions on
\Pi
, which essentially requires that each
\pi_{i}
is close to being degenerate. In contrast, our method bypasses this limitation by considering the induced topic model. While topic modeling also relies on a simplex geometry, the geometry arises in the standard Euclidean space, which can be effectively exploited using existing topic modeling algorithms, hence requiring very mild conditions on
\Pi
.

In theory,
19
only establish a consistency guarantee but do not provide any explicit rate of convergence. In contrast, we not only derive the explicit rate of convergence for our estimator but also show that it is minimax optimal.

Our method is connected to the one in
4
in leveraging the induced topic model.
However, the two methods differ fundamentally in how to use the estimated topic model.

4
treat the topic matrix
G
as a “discretization” of the continuous densities
g_{1}(x),\ldots,g_{K}(x)
.
The bin size
M
leads to a bias-variance trade-off: on one hand,
M
needs to be properly large so that
G
contains sufficient information of
g_{1}(x),\ldots,g_{K}(x)
;
on the other hand, since
M
affects the estimation error on
G
, it cannot be too large.
Unfortunately, in some parameter regime (e.g., when
\beta>1
), there does not exist a sweet spot of
M
. This explains why their method cannot be optimal across the whole parameter regime.

In contrast, our estimator builds on the oracle estimator in (
2.3
), which is already minimax optimal across the whole parameter regime. We only need to develop a
\widehat{\Pi}
such that the plug-in error is negligible.
This seems challenging, as
\Pi
is a large matrix. Fortunately, we leverage the induced topic model to design a
\widehat{\Pi}
in which the dependence among its entries can be well characterized. This allows us to have a sharp control of the plug-in error and eventually prove that our estimator has the same error rate as the oracle estimator, thus being optimal.

3
Topic Modeling: Background and Algorithms

One step in our estimator is to conduct topic modeling on the matrix
Y
. In this section, we review the topic model and existing topic modeling algorithms. In particular, we describe Topic-SCORE
(
22
)
, the algorithm we use in our numerical experiments and theoretical analysis.

3.1
Topic modeling algorithms and their error rates

Suppose we observe
n
documents written on a vocabulary of
M
words. Let
Y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
be the word count vector for the
i
th document, where
Y_{i}(m)
is the count of the
m
th word, and
N_{i}=\sum_{m=1}^{M}Y_{i}(m)
is the total length of this document. Let
g_{1},g_{2},\ldots,g_{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
be
K
topic vectors, each being a probability mass function (PMF) and representing a discrete distribution on the vocabulary.
Each document
i
is associated with a topic weight vector
\pi_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
, where
\pi_{i}(k)
is the fractional weight this document puts on the
k
th topic, for
1\leq k\leq K
. The topic model assumes that
Y_{1},Y_{2},\ldots,Y_{n}
are independent, and

Y_{i}\sim\mathrm{Multinomial}(N_{i},\Omega_{i}),\qquad\Omega_{i}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{i}(k)g_{k}.

(3.1)

The goal of topic modeling is to use the data matrix
Y=[Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}]
to estimate the parameters
G=[g_{1},\ldots,g_{K}]
and
\Pi=[\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{n}]^{\prime}
.

There are many existing methods for this problem. They can be roughly divided into two categories depending on the key assumption: In the first category, it is assumed that
\pi_{i}
’s are i.i.d. from a Dirichlet prior.
A popular method in this category is the latent Dirichlet allocation
(
10
)
.
In the second category, it is assumed that each topic has at least one anchor word, where a word
m
is an anchor word if the
m
th row of
G
has only one nonzero entry.
Representative methods include the NMF approach
(
3
)
, the Topic-SCORE algorithm
(
22
)
, and the LOVE algorithm
(
7
)
.

We can plug in any of these algorithms into our estimator. However, to achieve the best performance, we prefer a topic modeling algorithm that has a fast rate of convergence. Define the
\ell^{1}
-error as (subject to a column permutation of
\widehat{G}
)

{\cal L}(\widehat{G},G)=\sum_{1\leq m\leq M}\sum_{1\leq k\leq K}|\widehat{G}_{mk}-G_{mk}|.

Assuming
K
is fixed,
21
summarize the rates of convergence of
{\cal L}(\widehat{G},G)
for different algorithms and find that only Topic-SCORE
(
22
)
and LOVE
(
7
)
attain the optima rate
\sqrt{M/(\bar{N}n)}
, up to logarithmic factors.
In comparison, LOVE requires that
M\ll\bar{N}
, while Topic-SCORE does not have this requirement.
When
K
grows with
n
, LOVE has the following rate of convergence,

{\cal L}(\widehat{G},G)\leq C(\bar{N}n)^{-1/2}\sqrt{K^{3}M\log(\bar{N}n)},

(3.2)

which is again under the assumption of
M\ll\bar{N}
. The rate of convergence of Topic-SCORE for growing
K
has not been given in the literature. In Section
4.3
, we close this gap and show that Topic-SCORE also attains the rate in (
3.2
); and this is true for both the case of
M\ll\bar{N}
and
M=O(\bar{N})
. The rate in (
3.2
) is minimax optimal subject to a logarithm factor, according to the lower bound in
7
.
Based on these observations, we recommend using Topic-SCORE.

3.2
The Topic-SCORE algorithm

Topic-SCORE
(
22
)
belongs to the category of topic modeling algorithms that assume the anchor word condition. A word
1\leq m\leq M
is called an anchor word of topic
k
if and only if

g_{k}(m)\neq 0,\quad\mbox{and}\quad g_{\ell}(m)=0\mbox{ for all }\ell\neq k.

(3.3)

The anchor word condition requires that each topic has at least one anchor word.

We now briefly describe the key idea behind Topic-SCORE. Let
L=\mathrm{diag}(N_{1},N_{2},\ldots,N_{n})
and
\widetilde{Y}=YL^{-1}
.
Let
\hat{\xi}_{1},\ldots,\hat{\xi}_{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
be the first
K
left singular vectors of
\widetilde{Y}
and define

\widehat{R}=[\mathrm{diag}(\hat{\xi}_{1})]^{-1}[\hat{\xi}_{2},\ldots,\hat{\xi}_{K}]\;\in\;\mathbb{R}^{M\times(K-1)}.

22
show that there exists a low-dimensional
K
-vertex simplex associated with the rows of
\widehat{R}
. As long as each topic has at least one anchor word, this simplex can be estimated from
\widehat{R}
. Once this simplex is available, it can be used to obtain an explicit formula for converting the singular vectors to valid estimates of the topic vectors.
The full algorithm is as follows:

1.

(SVD)
. Let
D=\mathrm{diag}(\widetilde{Y}{\bf 1}_{n})
. Obtain the first
K
left singular vectors
D^{-1/2}\widetilde{Y}
, and denote them by
\hat{\xi}_{1},\ldots,\hat{\xi}_{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
. Construct a matrix
\widehat{R}=[\mathrm{diag}(\hat{\xi}_{1})]^{-1}[\hat{\xi}_{2},\ldots,\hat{\xi}_{K}]
.

2.

(Simplex vertex hunting)
. Apply a vertex hunting algorithm
(
20
, Section 3.4)
to the rows of
\widehat{R}
to obtain
\hat{v}_{1},\hat{v}_{2},\ldots,\hat{v}_{K}
. Write
\widehat{V}=[\hat{v}_{1},\hat{v}_{2},\ldots,\hat{v}_{K}]
.

3.

(Topic matrix estimation)
. For each
1\leq m\leq M
, obtain
\hat{u}_{m}
by solving the equations:
{\bf 1}_{K}^{\prime}\hat{u}_{m}=1
, and
\widehat{V}\hat{u}_{m}=\hat{r}_{m}
.
If
\hat{u}_{m}
contains negative entries, set these entries to zero and renormalize the whole vector to have a unit
\ell^{1}
-norm. For each
1\leq k\leq K
, estimate
g_{k}
by normalizing the
k
th column of
D^{1/2}\mathrm{diag}(\hat{\xi}_{1})[\hat{u}_{1},\hat{u}_{2},\ldots,\hat{u}_{M}]^{\prime}
to have a unit
\ell^{1}
-norm.

In Section
4.3
, we provide a tight error bound for this algorithm, which extends the existing bounds from fixed
K

(
21
)
to growing
K
. This result is not only useful for the analysis of our estimator but also of independent interest.

4
Main Results

In this section, we present our main theoretical results. For technical reasons, we introduce a slightly modified version of the estimator in (
1.10
):

\widehat{\bm{g}}^{+}(x)=\widehat{G}^{\prime}\widehat{G}\bigl(\widehat{G}^{\prime}T\widehat{G}+\epsilon I_{K}\bigr)^{-1}\widehat{G}^{\prime}S(x){\bf 1}_{n},

(4.1)

where

\epsilon=\epsilon_{n}=\frac{Kn}{M^{2}}\cdot 1\biggl\{\lambda_{\min}(\widehat{G}^{\prime}T\widehat{G})<\frac{Kn}{M^{2}\log^{2}(Nn)}\biggr\}.

In fact,
\lambda_{\min}(\widehat{G}^{\prime}T\widehat{G})
is often much larger than
\frac{Kn}{M^{2}\log^{2}(Nn)}
, so that
\widehat{\bm{g}}^{+}(x)
and
\widehat{\bm{g}}(x)
are identical except for a negligible probability. However, when we bound the expected error of
\widehat{\bm{g}}(x)
, we must study the behavior of our estimator on this small-probability event, which is technically challenging.
To avoid this hurdle, we study the above modified version.

In Sections
4.1
-
4.5
, we focus on
d=1
, for which the regularity conditions and main results are both easy to present and interpret. The extension to
d>1
is deferred to Section
4.6
.

4.1
Regularity conditions

When
d=1
, each
g_{k}(x)
is a continuous density on
\mathbb{R}
.
We assume they are smooth.
Some commonly used smooth classes include the Hölder class, the Sobolev class, and the Nikol’ski class.
The Hölder class focuses on a form of local smoothness, whereas the other two provide a form of integrated smoothness, with the Nikol’ski class broader than the Sobolev class.
Since we aim to control the integrated estimation error, we use the Nikol’ski class, with a smoothness parameter
\beta
:

Assumption 1

(Nikol’ski Smoothness)
.

Fix
\beta>0
and
L_{0}>0
. Let
\lfloor\beta\rfloor
denote the largest integer that is strictly smaller than
\beta
. For each
1\leq k\leq K
, the
\lfloor\beta\rfloor
th order derivative of
g_{k}
exists and satisfies:

\left(\int\left|g_{k}^{(\lfloor\beta\rfloor)}(x+t)-g_{k}^{(\lfloor\beta\rfloor)}(x)\right|^{2}dx\right)^{1/2}\leq L_{0}|t|^{\beta-\lfloor\beta\rfloor},\qquad\mbox{for all $t\in\mathbb{R}$}.

Under this assumption, we use an order-
\beta
kernel.
Besides requiring that
\int{\cal K}(u)du=1
as in (
1.3
), we also require:

\int u^{j}{\cal K}(u)du=0,\;1\leq j\leq\lfloor\beta\rfloor,\quad\int|u|^{\beta}|{\cal K}(u)|du+\int|\mathcal{K}(u)|du+\sup_{u}|\mathcal{K}(u)|<\infty.

(4.2)

We also need an assumption to guarantee that the model is identifiable:

Assumption 2
.

For
1\leq k\leq K
, let
S_{k}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}:g_{k}(x)>0,g_{\ell}(x)=0\mbox{ for all $\ell\neq k$}\}
. Suppose the Lesbegue measure of each
S_{k}
is lower bounded by a constant
c_{3}>0
.

Each
S_{k}
is called the anchor region of
g_{k}(x)
. It is analogous to the anchor word definition (see (
3.3
)) in a classical topic model.
Under this assumption,
g_{1}(x),\ldots,g_{K}(x)
are identifiable
(
4
)
.
While other identifiability conditions may be used, a key advantage of adopting Assumption
2
is that it naturally implies the anchor-word condition in the induced topic model. This allows us to apply existing anchor-word-based topic modeling algorithms, such as Topic-SCORE.

Note that unlike the classical nonparametric density estimation setting, we have
K
different densities that are mixed together. We need additional conditions to ensure that “unmixing” them is possible.
Intuitively, we require that (a) the
K
densities are not too “similar”, and (b) the “effective sample size” for estimating each individual
g_{k}(x)
is properly large.
We formally state these conditions as follows:

Assumption 3
.

Let
\bar{g}(x)=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}g_{k}(x)dx
and
\Sigma_{\bm{g}}=\frac{1}{K}\int{\bm{g}}(x){\bm{g}}(x)^{\prime}dx
. There exists a constant
c_{1}\in(0,1)
such that

c_{1}\leq\lambda_{\min}(\Sigma_{\bm{g}})\leq\lambda_{\max}(\Sigma_{\bm{g}})\leq c_{1}^{-1},\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\max_{x}\bar{g}(x)\leq c_{1}^{-1}.

Let
\Sigma_{\Pi}=(K/n)\Pi^{\prime}\Pi
and
\eta=\Pi^{\prime}{\bf 1}_{n}
. Denote by
\eta_{\max}
and
\eta_{\min}
the maximum and minimum entries of
\eta
. There exists a constant
c_{2}\in(0,1)
such that

c_{2}<\lambda_{\min}(\Sigma_{\Pi})\leq\lambda_{\max}(\Sigma_{\Pi})\leq c_{2}^{-1},\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad c_{2}K^{-1}n\leq\eta_{\min}\leq\eta_{\max}\leq c^{-1}_{2}K^{-1}n.

The
K\times K
matrix
\Sigma_{\bm{g}}
captures the ‘similarity’ of
K
densities.
If
\Sigma_{\bm{g}}
is ill-conditioned, some of the
g_{k}(x)
are too similar to each other, making the unmixing task too difficult.
The
K\times K
matrix
\Sigma_{\Pi}
and the vector
{\bf 1}_{n}^{\prime}\Pi
both describe the ‘sample size balance’ on
K
densities. When
\Sigma_{\Pi}
is ill-conditioned or when
{\bf 1}_{n}^{\prime}\Pi
has severely unbalanced entries, there exists at least one
g_{k}(x)
so that the
n
samples’ total weights on this density are significantly smaller than
n/K
; in such a case, this density cannot be estimated accurately enough. With the above being said, Assumption
3
is almost necessary for the success of the unmixing task.

\Sigma_{G}
is implied by the condition on
\Sigma_{\bm{g}}
in Assumption
3
, as long as

and the bins are properly chosen (see Lemma
16
in the supplement). On the other hand, even when the condition on
\Sigma_{\bm{g}}
is not satisfied, the condition on

may still hold. To maintain the broadness of this theorem, we choose to present the condition on
\Sigma_{G}
directly.

(ii)

\lambda_{\min}(\Sigma_{\Pi})\asymp\lambda_{\max}(\Sigma_{\Pi})\asymp 1
, and
\|{\bf 1}_{n}^{\prime}\Pi\|_{\infty}=O(K^{-1}n)
.

(iii)

N_{i}\asymp N
for all
1\leq i\leq n
.

(iv)

h\to 0
and
Nnh\to\infty
.

(v)

There is a constant
C>0
such that
{\cal L}(\widehat{G},G)\leq C\delta_{n}
with probability
1-o((Nn)^{-5})
.

Let
\widehat{\bm{g}}^{+}(x)
be the estimator in (
4.1
). Suppose
K\leq M\leq[Nn/\log^{2}(Nn)]^{1/2}
and
(Nn)^{-1}K\ll h\ll\log^{-1}(Nn)
.
Then, there exists a constant
C_{0}>0
such that

\mathbb{E}\left[\int\|\widehat{\bm{g}}^{+}(x)-\bm{g}(x)\|^{2}dx\right]\leq C_{0}\cdot K\left(h^{2\beta}+\frac{K}{Nnh}+\frac{M}{K}\delta_{n}^{2}+\frac{K^{2}}{Nn}\right).

(4.3)

In (
4.3
), the first two terms are analogous to the ‘bias’ and ‘variance’ in classical nonparametric density estimation, which can be optimized by choosing a proper bandwidth
h
:

{cor}

Under the conditions of Theorem
4.2
, if
h\asymp[K/(Nn)]^{\frac{1}{2\beta+1}}
, then

\mathbb{E}\left[\int\|\widehat{\bm{g}}^{+}(x)-\bm{g}(x)\|^{2}dx\right]\leq C_{0}\cdot K\left[\Bigl(\frac{K}{Nn}\Bigr)^{\frac{2\beta}{2\beta+1}}+\frac{M}{K}\delta_{n}^{2}+\frac{K^{2}}{Nn}\right].

The last two terms in (

) still persist here. They are the extra price paid for ‘unmixing’ densities. Specifically, the third term comes from the error of estimating the topic matrix
G
, and the last term is the additional error of using
\widehat{G}
to estimate
\Pi
.

4.3
The error for estimating
G

We now study the topic modeling error and derive the order of
\delta_{n}
.
As explained in Section
3
, we apply the Topic-SCORE algorithm. The following theorem is proved in the supplement:

{thm}

Consider the topic model in (

). Define
\Sigma_{\Pi}=(K/n)\Pi^{\prime}\Pi
, and
\Sigma_{G}=(M/K)G^{\prime}G
. We assume:

(i’)

\lambda_{\min}(\Sigma_{G})\asymp\lambda_{\max}(\Sigma_{G})\asymp 1
, and the smallest entry of
G{\bf 1}_{K}
is of the order
M^{-1}K
.

(ii’)

\lambda_{\min}(\Sigma_{\Pi})\asymp\lambda_{\max}(\Sigma_{\Pi})\asymp 1
, and all of the
K

\Pi^{\prime}{\bf 1}_{n}
are of the order
K^{-1}n
.

Additionally, suppose all
N_{i}

N
, and each topic has at least one anchor word (see (

)).
Let
\widehat{G}=[\hat{g}_{1},\ldots,\hat{g}_{K}]
be the output of Topic-SCORE.
Suppose
\min\{M,N\}\geq\log^{3}(Nn)
,
\log(N)=O(\log(n))
,
K\leq M
, and
K^{3}M\log^{2}(Nn)\leq Nn
.
There exists a constant
C_{\text{TM}}>0
such that with probability

,

\|e_{m}^{\prime}(\widehat{G}-G)\|_{1}\leq\|e_{m}^{\prime}G\|_{1}\cdot C_{\text{TM}}\sqrt{\frac{KM\log(Nn)}{Nn}};

Furthermore, with probability

, the

-error satisfies that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\widehat{G},G)\leq C\sqrt{\frac{K^{3}M\log(Nn)}{Nn}}.

(4.4)

Theorem

extends the analysis of Topic-SCORE from finite

(

to growing

Therefore, our result shows that Topic-SCORE is rate-optimal (up to a logarithmic factor) in the growing-

4.4
The rate of convergence of our estimator

We insert the topic modeling error bound from Section

into the error bound from Section

\mathbb{E}\left[\int\|\widehat{\bm{g}}^{+}(x)-\bm{g}(x)\|^{2}dx\right]\leq C_{0}\cdot K\left[\Bigl(\frac{K}{Nn}\Bigr)^{\frac{2\beta}{2\beta+1}}+\frac{K^{2}M^{2}\log(Nn)}{Nn}+\frac{K^{2}}{Nn}\right].

This bound holds under the conditions of Theorem

and Theorem

These conditions are implied by Assumptions

-

,
if

and the bins are properly chosen (see Section

and Lemma

Moreover, when

{thm}

Consider the model (

)-(

) with

, where Assumptions

-

hold, and

for all

It is interesting to compare this rate with those of existing methods. The method in

is only shown to be consistent under a relatively weak guarantee, without an explicit error rate. When

is fixed, the method in

has an error rate of

, where
3
3

3

Precisely, the paper considers a Hölder class with smoothness parameter

\theta^{*}(\beta)=\begin{cases}2\beta/(2\beta+1),&\mbox{when }\beta\leq 1,\cr 2/3,&\mbox{when }1<\beta\leq 2,\cr 2\beta/(5\beta+2),&\mbox{when }\beta>2.\end{cases}

4.5
A matching lower bound

To show the optimality of the error rate established above, we also provide a matching lower bound:

{thm}

Let
\Theta_{\beta}
be the collection of all

such that Assumptions

-

are satisfied.
Under the model in (

)-(

\inf_{\widehat{\bm{g}}(x)}\sup_{(\bm{g}(x),\Pi)\in\Theta_{\beta}}\mathbb{E}\left[\int\|\widehat{\bm{g}}(x)-\bm{g}(x)\|^{2}dx\right]\geq C_{0}^{\prime}K\left(\frac{K}{Nn}\right)^{\frac{2\beta}{2\beta+1}}.

Comparing Theorem

The proof of Theorem

4.6
Extension to a general
d

Our results can be extended to any fixed dimension
d
. In this case, each
g_{k}(x)
is a multivariate density.
We assume that
g_{1}(x),\ldots,g_{K}(x)
belong to the anisotropic Nikol’ski class
(
27
)
defined as follows.

Assumption 1
′

(Multivariate Nikol’ski Smoothness)
.

Fix
d\geq 1
,
\bm{\beta}=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{d})\in(0,\infty)^{d}

and
L>0
. For each
j\in\{1,\dots,d\}
, let

r_{j}=\lfloor\beta_{j}\rfloor
denote the largest integer strictly smaller than
\beta_{j}
,
and write
\bm{r}=(r_{1},\dots,r_{d})
. For each
1\leq k\leq K
, the mixed partial derivative

\partial^{\bm{r}}g_{k}=\partial_{1}^{\,r_{1}}\cdots\partial_{d}^{\,r_{d}}g_{k}

exists and satisfies

\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\partial^{\bm{r}}g_{k}(x+t)-\partial^{\bm{r}}g_{k}(x)\right|^{2}dx\right)^{1/2}\leq L\sum_{j=1}^{d}|t_{j}|^{\beta_{j}-r_{j}},\qquad\text{for all }t\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Our estimator requires having a multivariate kernel
{\cal K}(x)
to obtain
K_{ij}(x)={\cal K}_{h}(x-X_{ij})
(see (
1.9
)), where
{\cal K}_{h}(x)=\frac{1}{h^{d}}{\cal K}(\frac{x}{h})
for a bandwidth
h>0
.
However, under Assumption
1′
, we should use different bandwidths for different coordinates. Let
\bm{h}=(h_{1},h_{2},\ldots,h_{d})^{\prime}
be a bandwidth matrix.
We define

{\cal K}_{\bm{h}}(x)=\prod_{j=1}^{d}\frac{1}{h_{j}}{\cal K}_{j}\bigl(\frac{x_{j}}{h_{j}}\bigr),\quad\mbox{where ${\cal K}_{j}$ satisfies \eqref{cond-kernel} for $\beta=\beta_{j}$.}

(4.5)

In the supplementary material, we present a counterpart of Theorem
4.2
when Assumption
1
is replaced by Assumption
1′
. The error bound has the following form:

\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|\widehat{\bm{g}}^{+}(\bm{x})-\bm{g}(\bm{x})\|^{2}d\bm{x}\right]\leq C_{0}\cdot K\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}h_{j}^{2\beta_{j}}+\frac{K}{Nn\prod_{j=1}^{d}h_{j}}+\frac{M}{K}\delta_{n}^{2}+\frac{K^{2}}{Nn}\right).

Compared to (
4.3
), only the first two terms are different. The last two terms, which correspond to the extra price paid for “unmixing” densities, are the same as before.
We recall that these two terms arise from the error of estimating
G
and the additional error of using
\widehat{G}
to estimate
\Pi
. These errors are only related to the induced topic model, which are independent of the dimension
d
(especially, the bin size
M
does not depend on
d
).

Similarly as in the case of

, we choose
\bm{h}
to optimize the first two terms; in addition, when
K
grows with
Nn
at a speed not too fast, the last two terms will be negligible. We thus have the following theorem:

{thm}

Fix
d\geq 1
and consider the model (
1.1
)-(
1.2
), where Assumptions
1′
and Assumptions
2
-
3
hold, and
N_{i}\asymp N
for all
1\leq i\leq n
.
Define the harmonic mean
(\beta^{*})^{-1}=d^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\beta_{j}^{-1}
.
Suppose
K=O\bigl(\max\bigl\{(Nn)^{\frac{1}{4}}[\log(Nn)]^{-1},(Nn)^{\frac{d}{4\beta^{*}+3d}}\log^{-\frac{4\beta^{*}+2d}{4\beta^{*}+3d}}(Nn)\bigr\}\bigr)
,
N\geq\log(n)
, and
\log(N)=O(\log(n))
.
Let
\widehat{\bm{g}}^{+}(\bm{x})
be the estimator in (
4.1
), where
\mathcal{K}_{\bm{h}}(x)
has the product form in (
4.5
), the plug-in
\widehat{G}
is obtained by Topic-SCORE,
and the bin size
M
and the bandwidth vector
\bm{h}
satisfy that
M\asymp K\log(Nn)
and

for all

\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|\widehat{\bm{g}}(\bm{x})^{+}-\bm{g}(\bm{x})\|^{2}d\bm{x}\right]\leq C\cdot K\left(\frac{K}{Nn}\right)^{\frac{2\beta^{*}}{2\beta^{*}+d}}.

A special case is that

belong to the isotropic Nikol’ski class, corresponding to

.
A matching lower bound can also be proved. It is a straightforward extension of Theorem

Proof of the Minimax Upper Bound

In this section, we prove Theorem

. Specifically, Section

6.1
Preliminaries

Our analysis frequently uses properties of a certain type of incomplete
U
-statistics.
Suppose we have random variables:

\mbox{$\{x_{ij}\}_{1\leq i\leq n,1\leq j\leq N_{i}}$ are independent across $i$; for any fixed $i$, they are i.i.d. across $j$}.

(6.1)

We first consider an incomplete U-process defined by these random variables and an arbitrary trivariate function
h(x;y,z)
:

H_{n,N}(x)=\frac{1}{nN(N-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{1\leq j\neq j^{\prime}\leq N}h(x;x_{ij},x_{ij^{\prime}}).

(6.2)

Lemma 2

(Integrated variance bound for incomplete
U
-processes)
.

Consider the incomplete
U
-process in (
6.2
).
Suppose there exists
b_{i}>0
such that
\mathbb{E}[\int h^{2}(x;x_{i1},x_{i2})dx]\leq b_{i}^{2}
, for each
1\leq i\leq n
. Let
\theta_{i}(x)=\mathbb{E}[h(x;x_{i1},x_{i2})]
and
\theta(x)=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\theta_{i}(x)
. Then,

\mathbb{E}\biggl[\int(H_{n,N}(x)-\theta(x))^{2}dx\biggr]\leq\frac{4(\sigma^{2}+b^{2})}{nN},

where
\sigma^{2}=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\int(h(x;x_{i1},x_{i2})-\theta_{i}(x))^{2}dx\right]
and
b^{2}=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}^{2}
.

We then consider an incomplete U-statistics defined by
x_{ij}
’s and an arbitrary bi-viarate function
h^{*}(x_{1},x_{2})
:

H^{*}_{n,N}=\frac{1}{nN(N-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{1\leq j\neq j^{\prime}\leq N}h^{*}(x_{ij},x_{ij^{\prime}}).

(6.3)

Lemma 3

(Bernstein inequality for incomplete
U
-statistics)
.

Consider the incomplete
U
-statistic in (
6.3
). Suppose there exists
b>0
such that
\max_{1\leq i\leq n,1\leq j\neq j^{\prime}\leq N}|h^{*}(x_{ij},x_{ij^{\prime}})|\leq b
.
Let
\theta=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}h^{*}(x_{i1},x_{i2})
and
\sigma^{2}=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\text{Var}(h^{*}(x_{i1},x_{i2}))
. For all
\delta\in(0,1)
, with probability
1-\delta
,

|H^{*}_{n,N}-\theta|\leq 2\sigma\sqrt{\frac{2\log(2/\delta)}{nN}}+\frac{16b\log(2/\delta)}{3nN}.

Both lemmas are proved in the supplementary material. The proofs use some known techniques for analyzing U-statistics, specially tailored to our setting where the random variables satisfy (
6.1
). For instance, the proof of Lemma
3
relies on decoupling the randomness in the tuple
(x_{ij},x_{ij^{\prime}},x_{i\ell},x_{i\ell^{\prime}})
for
j\neq j^{\prime}
and
\ell\neq\ell^{\prime}
.

6.2
A decomposition of the error

For any
\bar{\epsilon}>0
and matrices
\bar{G}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times K}
,
\bar{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times M}
, and
\bar{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}
, we define a mapping:

{\bm{g}}(\bar{G},\bar{S},\bar{T},\bar{\epsilon}):=\bar{G}^{\prime}\bar{G}\bigl(\bar{G}^{\prime}\bar{T}\bar{G}+\bar{\epsilon}I_{K}\bigr)^{-1}\bar{G}^{\prime}\bar{S}{\bf 1}_{n}.

(6.4)

Our estimator in (
4.1
) is defined as

\widehat{\bm{g}}^{+}(x)={\bm{g}}\bigl(\widehat{G},S(x),T,\epsilon\bigr).

To facilitate the analysis, we further introduce two intermediate quantities:

{\bm{g}}^{*}(x)={\bm{g}}\bigl(G,\,\mathbb{E}[S(x)],\,\mathbb{E}[T],0\bigr),\qquad\widehat{\bm{g}}^{*}(x)={\bm{g}}\bigl(G,S(x),T,\epsilon\bigr).

(6.5)

The first estimator
{\bm{g}}^{*}(x)
is the population counterpart of our estimator. The second one
\widehat{\bm{g}}^{*}(x)
is an ideal version of our estimator assuming that the true topic matrix
G
is given.

Now, we decompose the error
\widehat{\bm{g}}(x)-{\bm{g}}(x)
into three terms:

\widehat{\bm{g}}^{+}(x)-{\bm{g}}(x)=\underbrace{{\bm{g}}^{*}(x)-{\bm{g}}(x)}_{\text{bias}}+\underbrace{\widehat{\bm{g}}^{*}(x)-{\bm{g}}^{*}(x)}_{\text{variance}}+\underbrace{\widehat{\bm{g}}^{+}(x)-\widehat{\bm{g}}^{*}(x)}_{\text{secondary error}}.

(6.6)

The first term is the non-stochastic ‘bias’ term,
the second term is the main stochastic error term, arising from the randomness in
(S,T)
, and the third term is the secondary stochastic error term, due to estimating
G
by
\widehat{G}
. We will control these three terms separately.

6.3
The non-stochastic ‘bias’ term

We bound the first term in (
6.6
). Recall that
T
and
S(x)
are defined in (
1.8
)-(
1.9
). Let
K_{ij}(x)
and
U_{ijm}
be the same as in those definitions.
We introduce a vector

\bm{k}(x)=\bigl(K_{11}(x),K_{21}(x),\ldots,K_{n1}(x)\bigr)^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(6.7)

For any fixed
(i,m)
,
\mathbb{E}[U_{ijm}]
and
\mathbb{E}[K_{ij}(x)]
are the same across
j
. In addition,
(U_{ijm},K_{ij}(x))
is independent of
U_{ij^{\prime}m^{\prime}}
when
j\neq j^{\prime}
(this is true even when
m=m^{\prime}
). We immediately have:

\mathbb{E}[T_{mm^{\prime}}]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[U_{i1m}]\cdot\mathbb{E}[U_{i1m^{\prime}}],\qquad\mathbb{E}[S_{mi}(x)]=\mathbb{E}[k_{i}(x)]\cdot\mathbb{E}[U_{i1m}].

Moreover, we note that
\mathbb{E}[U_{i1m}]=\mathbb{P}(X_{ij}\in{\cal B}_{m})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{i}(k)G_{mk}=(G\Pi^{\prime})_{mi}
.
Combining it with the above equations gives

\mathbb{E}[S(x)]=G\Pi^{\prime}\mathrm{diag}\bigl(\mathbb{E}[k(x)]\bigr),\qquad\mathbb{E}[T]=G\Pi^{\prime}\Pi G^{\prime}.

We plug
(\bar{G},\bar{S},\bar{T})=(G,\mathbb{E}[S(x)],\mathbb{E}[T])
into (
6.4
). It follows by direct calculations that

{\bm{g}}^{*}(x)=(\Pi^{\prime}\Pi)^{-1}\Pi^{\prime}\mathbb{E}[{\bm{k}}(x)].

(6.8)

Meanwhile, since our model implies that
{\bm{f}}(x)=\Pi{\bm{g}}(x)
, we can write

{\bm{g}}(x)=(\Pi^{\prime}\Pi)^{-1}\Pi^{\prime}{\bm{f}}(x).

(6.9)

Comparing (
6.9
) with (
6.8
), the ‘bias’ term comes from approximating
{\bm{f}}(x)
by
\mathbb{E}[{\bm{k}}(x)]
. Using properties of the kernel function
{\cal K}(\cdot)
, we can prove the following lemma:

Lemma 4
.

Under Assumptions
1
-
3
, there exists a constant
C_{1}>0
such that

\int\|{\bm{g}}^{*}(x)-{\bm{g}}(x)\|^{2}dx\leq C_{1}Kh^{2\beta}.

6.4
The main stochastic error

We study the second term in (
6.6
). Define an event

{\cal F}=\biggl\{\lambda_{\min}(\widehat{G}^{\prime}T\widehat{G})\geq\frac{\tilde{c}Kn}{M^{2}\log^{2}(n)}\biggr\}.

(6.10)

Let
I_{\cal F}
be a Bernoulli variable indicating that the event
{\cal F}
happens.
We write

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\bm{g}}^{*}(x)-{\bm{g}}^{*}(x)\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\widehat{\bm{g}}^{*}(x)-{\bm{g}}^{*}(x)\|^{2}\cdot I_{\cal F}+\|\widehat{\bm{g}}^{*}(x)-{\bm{g}}^{*}(x)\|^{2}\cdot I_{{\cal F}^{c}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\widehat{\bm{g}}^{*}(x)-{\bm{g}}^{*}(x)\|^{2}\cdot I_{\cal F}+2\|\widehat{\bm{g}}^{*}(x)\|^{2}\cdot I_{{\cal F}^{c}}+2\|{\bm{g}}^{*}(x)\|^{2}\cdot I_{{\cal F}^{c}}.

It follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\biggl[\int\|\widehat{\bm{g}}^{*}(x)

\displaystyle-{\bm{g}}^{*}(x)\|^{2}dx\biggr]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\int\|\widehat{\bm{g}}^{*}(x)-{\bm{g}}^{*}(x)\|^{2}dx\cdot I_{\cal F}\right]

(6.11)

\displaystyle\qquad+2\mathbb{E}\left[\int\|\widehat{\bm{g}}^{*}(x)\|^{2}dx\cdot I_{{\cal F}^{c}}\right]+2\mathbb{P}({\cal F}^{c})\cdot\int\|{\bm{g}}^{*}(x)\|^{2}dx.

(6.12)

On the right hand side of (
6.11
), the first term is dominating. Therefore, we focus on controlling the first term. The other two terms will be considered in the end of this subsection.

To study the first term in (
6.11
), we notice that on the event

{\cal F}
, the quantity
\epsilon
in (
4.1
) is exactly zero, so that

\widehat{\bm{g}}^{*}(x)={\bm{g}}\bigl(G,S(x),T,0\bigr).

(6.13)

When (
6.13
) holds, we can express
\widehat{{\bm{g}}}^{*}(x)
in terms of
{\bm{g}}^{*}(x)
and some error terms. Recall that
Q=G(G^{\prime}G)^{-1}
. We further write
\Omega=Q(\Pi^{\prime}\Pi)^{-1}
for brevity.

Lemma 5
.

Let
\widehat{{\bm{g}}}^{*}(x)
and
{\bm{g}}^{*}(x)
be as defined in (
6.5
). On the event
{\cal F}
,

\widehat{\bm{g}}^{*}(x)=\bigl[I_{K}+\Omega^{\prime}(T-\mathbb{E}T)Q\bigr]^{-1}\bigl[{\bm{g}}^{*}(x)+\Omega^{\prime}(S-\mathbb{E}S){\bf 1}_{n}\bigr].

(6.14)

When
\Omega^{\prime}(S-\mathbb{E}S){\bf 1}_{n}
and
\Omega^{\prime}(T-\mathbb{E}T)Q
are zero, the right hand side of (
6.14
) is exactly equal to
{\bm{g}}^{*}(x)
.
Consequently, the difference between
\widehat{{\bm{g}}}^{*}(x)
and
{\bm{g}}^{*}(x)
can be controlled by

\|\Omega^{\prime}(S-\mathbb{E}S){\bf 1}_{n}\|\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\|\Omega^{\prime}(T-\mathbb{E}T)Q\|.

Below, we study these two error terms separately.

First, we consider the
K
-dimensional random vector
\Omega^{\prime}(S-\mathbb{E}S){\bf 1}_{n}
. For each
1\leq k\leq K
, define a tri-variate function

h_{k}(x;x_{1},x_{2})=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\Omega_{mk}{\cal K}_{h}(x-x_{1})1\{x_{2}\in{\cal B}_{m}\}.

Using the definition of
S=S(x)
in (
1.9
), we can immediately deduce that

e_{k}^{\prime}\Omega^{\prime}(S-\mathbb{E}S){\bf 1}_{n}=u_{k}(x)-\mathbb{E}u_{k}(x),\quad u_{k}(x):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\sum_{1\leq j\neq j^{\prime}\leq N_{i}}h_{k}(x;X_{ij},X_{ij^{\prime}})}{N_{i}(N_{i}-1)}.

(6.15)

Here, the variables
\{X_{ij}\}_{1\leq i\leq n,1\leq j\leq N}
are independent across
i
; and for any fixed
i
, they are i.i.d. across
j
. Hence,
u_{k}(x)
is an incomplete
U
-process indexed by
x
.
Applying Lemma
2
to the specific form in (
6.15
), we can prove the following result:

Lemma 6
.

Under Assumption
3
,
there exists a constant
C_{2}>0
such that

\mathbb{E}\left[\int\bigl\|\Omega^{\prime}[S(x)-\mathbb{E}S(x)]{\bf 1}_{n}\bigr\|^{2}dx\right]\leq C_{2}(Nnh)^{-1}K^{2}.

Next, we study
\Omega^{\prime}(T-\mathbb{E}T)Q
. This is an asymmetric matrix. We first shift our attention to a symmetric matrix as follows: Note that
\Omega=Q(\Pi^{\prime}\Pi)^{-1}
. Since
\|(\Pi^{\prime}\Pi)^{-1}\|\leq Cn^{-1}K
by Assumption
3
(a), it follows that

\|\Omega^{\prime}(T-\mathbb{E}T)Q\|\leq Cn^{-1}K\cdot\|Q^{\prime}(T-\mathbb{E}T)Q\|.

(6.16)

It remains to bound
\|Q^{\prime}(T-\mathbb{E}T)Q\|
.

We apply the approach in
34
to bound the spectral norm of the symmetric matrix
Q^{\prime}(T-\mathbb{E}T)Q
.
Let
{\cal N}_{\epsilon}
denote an
\epsilon
-net of the unit sphere of
\mathbb{R}^{K}
. By
(
34
, Lemma 5.4)
,

\|W\|\leq(1-2\epsilon)^{-1}\max_{v\in{\cal N}_{\epsilon}}|v^{\prime}Wv|,\qquad\mbox{for any symmetric matrix $W\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times K}$}.

(6.17)

To this end, the analysis reduces to bounding
|v^{\prime}Q^{\prime}(T-\mathbb{E}T)Qv|
for a fixed unit-norm vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
.
By (
1.8
),

T=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{N_{i}(N_{i}-1)}\sum_{1\leq j\neq j^{\prime}\leq N}Q^{\prime}U_{ij}U_{ij^{\prime}}^{\prime}Q.

Let
s_{ij}=v^{\prime}Q^{\prime}U_{ij}
.
We have

v^{\prime}Q^{\prime}(T-\mathbb{E}T)Qv=\frac{1}{N(N-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{1\leq j\neq j^{\prime}\leq N}\bigl(s_{ij}s_{ij^{\prime}}^{\prime}-\mathbb{E}[s_{ij}s_{ij^{\prime}}^{\prime}]\bigr).

(6.18)

Since
\{s_{ij}\}_{1\leq i\leq n,1\leq j\leq N}
are independent random variables, the above defines an incomplete
U
-statistic. We apply Lemma
3
to bound the right hand side of (
6.18
) and further combine this result with (
6.17
). It leads to the following lemma:

Lemma 7
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
4.2
,
there exists a constant
C_{3}>0
such that with probability
1-o((Nn)^{-5})
,

\|\Omega^{\prime}(T-\mathbb{E}T)Q\|\leq C_{3}(nN)^{-1/2}\sqrt{K(\log(Nn)+K)}.

Finally, we combine Lemmas
6
-
7
with the expression in (
6.14
). By some elementary analysis, we can prove the following lemma.

Lemma 8
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
4.2
, there is a constant
C_{4}>0
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\int\|\bm{\widehat{g}}^{*}(x)-\bm{g}^{*}(x)\|^{2}dx\cdot I_{\cal F}\right]\leq C_{4}K\left(\frac{K^{2}}{Nn}+\frac{K}{Nnh}\right).

Lemma
8
bounds the first term in (
6.11
). We still need to bound the other two terms. In our analysis, we first deduce a high-probability lower bound for
\lambda_{\min}(\widehat{G}^{\prime}T\widehat{G})
. It implies that

\mathbb{P}({\cal F}^{c})=o((Nn)^{-5}).

This permits us to control the third term in (
6.11
).
Moreover, on the event
{\cal F}^{c}
,
\lambda_{\min}(\widehat{G}^{\prime}T\widehat{G})
is either larger than
\frac{Kn}{M^{2}\log^{3}(n)}
, or smaller. In the first case,
\epsilon=0
; and in the second case,
\epsilon=Kn/M^{2}
. In both cases, it holds that

\lambda_{\min}\bigl(\widehat{G}^{\prime}T\widehat{G}+\epsilon I_{K}\bigr)\geq\frac{Kn}{M^{2}\log^{3}(n)}.

Using this result, we can derive an upper bound for
\|\widehat{\bm{g}}^{*}(x)\|^{2}
over the event
{\cal F}^{c}
. Denote this upper bound by
\zeta_{n}
.
Then, the second term in (
6.11
) is upper bounded by
\zeta_{n}^{2}\cdot\mathbb{P}({\cal F}^{c})
, which can also be well-controlled.
We formally prove the following lemma:

Lemma 9
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
4.2
, there is a constant
C^{\prime}_{4}>0
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\int\|\bm{\widehat{g}}^{*}(x)-\bm{g}^{*}(x)\|^{2}dx\right]\leq C^{\prime}_{4}K\left(\frac{K^{2}}{Nn}+\frac{K}{Nnh}\right).

6.5
The secondary stochastic error, and proof of Theorem
4.2

We now control the last term in (
6.6
). This error arises from not knowing
G
and estimating it by
\widehat{G}
.

Let
{\cal F}
be the event in (
6.10
).
Over the event
{\cal F}
, we re-write
\widehat{{\bm{g}}}(x)
as a more explicit expression of
\widehat{{\bm{g}}}^{*}(x)
.

Lemma 10
.

Write for brevity
R=(Q^{\prime}TQ)^{-1}(G^{\prime}G)^{-1}
,
\Delta_{1}=\widehat{G}^{\prime}\widehat{G}-G^{\prime}G
, and
\Delta_{2}=\widehat{G}^{\prime}T\widehat{G}-G^{\prime}TG
. Over the event
{\cal F}
,

\widehat{\bm{g}}(x)=\bigl[I_{K}+(R\Delta_{2}-\Delta_{1})(G^{\prime}G+\Delta_{1})^{-1}\bigr]^{-1}\bigl[\widehat{{\bm{g}}}^{*}(x)+R(\widehat{G}-G)^{\prime}S{\bf 1}_{n}\bigr].

(6.19)

We note that the matrix
R
is relatively easy to study, using the result of Lemma
7
. What remains is to study the matrices
\Delta_{1}
and
\Delta_{2}
and the vector
R(\widehat{G}-G)^{\prime}S{\bf 1}_{n}
. All three quantities are related to the deviation of
\widehat{G}
from
G
and
T
from
\mathbb{E}[T]
. We need the following lemma:

Lemma 11
.

Suppose the conditions of Theorem
4.2
hold. Let
{\cal E}
denote the event that
\|T-\mathbb{E}[T]\|\leq M^{-1}n
and
\|Q^{\prime}(T-\mathbb{E}[T])Q\|\leq n/(K\log^{1/2}(Nn))
, and let
I_{\cal E}
be the Bernoulli variable indicating that this event happens. There exist positive constants
C_{5}
-
C_{7}
such that the following statements are true:

•

\|\Delta_{1}\|\leq C_{5}\sqrt{\frac{K}{M}}\|\widehat{G}-G\|
, over the event
{\cal E}
.

•

\|R\Delta_{2}\|\leq C_{6}\sqrt{\frac{K}{M}}\|\widehat{G}-G\|
, over the event
{\cal E}
.

•

\mathbb{E}\left[\int\|R(\widehat{G}-G)^{\prime}S{\bf 1}_{n}\|^{2}dx\cdot I_{\cal E}\right]\leq C_{7}M\|\widehat{G}-G\|^{2}
.

In order to apply Lemma
11
, we must study the probability of the event
\mathcal{E}
.
This is contained in Lemma
17
of the supplement, where we bound
\|T-\mathbb{E}[T]\|
and
\|Q^{\prime}(T-\mathbb{E}[T])Q\|
and show that the event
\mathcal{E}
has an overwhelming probability.

We now combine the statements in Lemma
11
with the expression of
\widehat{{\bm{g}}}(x)
in Lemma
10
.
Since this expression is only correct on the event
{\cal F}
and the statements in Lemma
11
are with respect to the event
{\cal E}
, we will consider an event

{\cal F}^{*}={\cal F}\cap{\cal E}.

We then decompose the error similarly as in (
6.11
). We no longer provide these details but directly present the following lemma, whose proof is in the supplementary material.

Lemma 12
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
4.2
, there is a constant
C_{8}>0
such that

\mathbb{E}\left[\int\|\widehat{\bm{g}}^{*}(x)-\widehat{\bm{g}}(x)\|^{2}dx\right]\leq C_{8}\left[M\delta_{n}^{2}+\frac{KM^{4}\log^{4}(Nn)}{(Nn)^{5}h}\right].

The conclusion of Theorem
4.2
follows immediately by plugging Lemma
4
, Lemma
9
and Lemma
12
into the decomposition in (
6.6
).

7
Proof sketch of the Minimax Lower Bound

In this section, we provide a sketch of the proof of Theorem
4.5
, leaving the full version in the supplementary material.

Let
{\cal F}_{K}^{*}
be the space of
K
-densities, i.e., each element in
{\cal F}_{K}^{*}
takes the form of
\bm{g}(x)=(g_{1}(x),\ldots,g_{K}(x))^{\prime}
. For any
\bm{g},\widetilde{\bm{g}}\in{\cal F}_{K}^{*}
, let

d(\bm{g},\widetilde{\bm{g}}):=\left(\int\|\bm{g}(x)-\widetilde{\bm{g}}(x)\|^{2}dx\right)^{1/2}=\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\|g_{k}-\tilde{g}_{K}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\right)^{1/2},

where
\|\cdot\|_{L^{2}}
is the standard
L^{2}
-norm. It is easy to see that
d(\cdot,\cdot)
is a distance defined on
\Theta^{*}
(i.e., the triangular inequality holds). Recall that
\Theta_{\beta}
is the collection of all
({\bm{g}},\Pi)
such that Assumptions
1
-
3
are satisfied. We fix
\Pi^{*}
such that
\pi^{*}_{i}=e_{k}
for
n_{k}
out of
n
groups, where
n_{k}\asymp n/K
and
n_{1}+n_{2}+\cdots+n_{K}=n
. Let
{\cal F}_{K}(\beta,\Pi^{*})
be the collection of
\bm{g}
such that
(\bm{g},\Pi^{*})\in\Theta_{\beta}
. To show the claim, it is sufficient to show that

\inf_{\widehat{g}}\sup_{\bm{g}\in{\cal F}_{K}(\beta,\Pi^{*})}\mathbb{E}_{(\bm{g},\Pi^{*})}\bigl[d^{2}(\widehat{\bm{g}},\bm{g})\bigr]\geq c\epsilon^{2}_{n},\qquad\mbox{with}\quad\epsilon^{2}_{n}:=K\Bigl(\frac{K}{Nn}\Bigr)^{\frac{2\beta}{2\beta+1}}.

(7.1)

According to Theorem 2.7 of
33
, (
7.1
) holds if for some integer
J\geq 2
, we can construct
\bm{g}^{(0)},\bm{g}^{(1)},\ldots,\bm{g}^{(J)}
such that

\min_{0\leq s\neq s^{\prime}\leq J}d^{2}(\bm{g}^{(s)},\bm{g}^{(s^{\prime})})\geq c\epsilon_{n}^{2},\qquad\frac{1}{J}\sum_{s=1}^{J}\mathrm{KL}(\mathbb{P}_{s},\mathbb{P}_{0})=O(\log(J)),

(7.2)

where
\mathbb{P}_{s}
denotes the probability measure associated with the model (
1.1
)-(
1.2
) with parameters
\bm{g}^{(s)}
and
\Pi^{*}
, and
\text{KL}(\cdot,\cdot)
is the Kullback-Leibler divergence.

We outline how to construct
\{\bm{g}^{(s)}\}_{s=0}^{J}
. In the first step, we fix a properly large constant
T>0
and construct
\bm{g}^{(0)}
such that (i) each
g_{k}^{(0)}
is in the Nikol’ski class with smoothness
\beta
(i.e., satisfying Assumption
1
), (b) each
g_{k}^{(0)}
has an anchor region (see Assumption
2
) that is located in
(-\infty,-T)\cup(T,\infty)
, and (c) each
g_{k}^{(0)}
is uniformly lower bounded by a constant in
[-T,T]
. This step uses certain bump functions as in (
5.1
), and the construction details are in the supplement.

In the second step, we perturb each
g_{k}^{(0)}
in the region of
[-T,T]
. Let

B=\Big\lceil\Bigl(\frac{Nn}{K}\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{2\beta+1}}\Bigr\rceil,\qquad\delta=a_{0}\Bigl(\frac{K}{Nn}\Bigr)^{\frac{\beta}{2\beta+1}},\;\;\mbox{for a properly small constant $a_{0}>0$}.

We divide
[-T,T]
into
B
equal intervals, and let
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{B}
be the centers of these intervals.
Let
\psi
be a function that is in the Nikol’ski class with smoothness
\beta
and satisfies:

\mathrm{Support}(\psi)=[-1,1],\quad\int_{0}^{1}\psi(x)dx=0,\quad\int_{0}^{1}\psi^{2}(x)dx=1,\quad\|\psi\|_{\infty}<\infty.

It can be shown that such
\psi
exists. We let

\phi_{b}(x):=\delta\cdot\psi\left(Bx-x_{b}\right),\qquad 1\leq b\leq B.

It is seen that
\{\phi_{b}\}_{b=1}^{B}
have disjoint support within
[-T,T]
,
\int\phi_{b}(u)du=0
, and

\int\phi^{2}_{b}(u)du\asymp\delta^{2}B^{-1}.

Let
J=\lceil 2^{KB/8}\rceil
. The Gilbert-Varshamov bound implies that there exist
(J+1)
binary vectors
\nu^{(0)},\ldots,\nu^{(J)}\in\{0,1\}^{KB}
, such that
\nu^{(0)}={\bf 0}
and
\|\nu^{(s)}-\nu^{(s^{\prime})}\|_{1}\geq KB/8
for all
0\leq s\neq s^{\prime}\leq J
. We re-arrange each
\nu^{(s)}
into an
K\times B
matrix
\Omega^{(s)}
, and let
\omega_{k,b}^{(s)}
denote its
(k,b)
th entry, for
1\leq k\leq K
and
1\leq b\leq B
. It follows that

|\Omega^{(s)}-\Omega^{(s^{\prime})}|_{1}:=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{b=1}^{B}|\omega_{k,b}^{(s)}-\omega_{k,b}^{(s^{\prime})}|\geq KB/8.

(7.3)

We then construct
\bm{g}^{(1)},\ldots,\bm{g}^{(J)}
by

g_{k}^{(s)}(x)=g_{k}^{(0)}(x)+\sum_{b=1}^{B}\omega_{k,b}^{(s)}\phi_{b}(x),\quad\text{for }\ 1\leq s\leq J\mbox{ and }1\leq k\leq K.

(7.4)

To show that these constructed
\bm{g}^{(s)}
satisfy (
7.2
), we note that by our construction, each
\phi_{b}
belongs to the Nikol’ski class with smoothness
\beta
,
\phi_{b}
’s have disjoint supports,
\int\phi_{b}(u)du=0
, and
\|\phi_{b}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}=\|\phi_{1}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\asymp\delta^{2}B^{-1}
. It follows that each
g_{k}^{(s)}
is a density belonging to the Nikol’ski class, and by direct calculations,

d^{2}\bigl(\bm{g}^{(s)},\bm{g}^{(s^{\prime})}\bigr)=|\Omega^{(s)}-\Omega^{(s^{\prime})}|\cdot\|\phi_{1}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\geq CK\delta^{2}\geq c\epsilon_{n}^{2}.

This proves the first part of (
7.2
). Furthermore, in the supplementary material, we show that

\max_{1\leq s\leq J}\mathrm{KL}(\mathbb{P}_{s},\mathbb{P}_{0})=O(Nn\delta^{2}).

Noticing that
Nn\delta^{2}=K^{\frac{2\beta}{2\beta+1}}(Nn)^{\frac{1}{2\beta+1}}=KB
, we immediately obtain:

\frac{1}{J}\sum_{s=1}^{J}\mathrm{KL}(\mathbb{P}_{s},\mathbb{P}_{0})=O(Nn\delta^{2})=O(KB)=O(\log J).

This proves the second part of (
7.2
) and completes the proof.

8
Discussion

We consider linearly unmixing
n
convex combinations of nonparametric densities.
We propose a novel estimator and show that it attains the optimal rate of convergence in the whole smooth regime, offering a much stronger theoretical guarantee than existing methods in the literature.
One limitation of our work is that we need
K=O((Nn)^{c^{*}_{\beta,d}})
for some constant
c^{*}_{\beta,d}\in(0,1/4)
, in order to achieve the optimal rate.
When
K\gg(Nn)^{c^{*}_{\beta d}}
, we still obtain an upper bound (by letting
M=K\log(Nn)
in the first equation of Section
4.4
):

\mathbb{E}\left[\int\|\widehat{\bm{g}}^{+}(x)-\bm{g}(x)\|^{2}dx\right]\leq C_{0}\cdot K\left[\Bigl(\frac{K}{Nn}\Bigr)^{\frac{2\beta}{2\beta+1}}+\frac{K^{4}\log^{2}(Nn)}{Nn}\right].

Compared to the lower bound, there is an extra term. Whether this term can be improved is unclear. The first term above arises from the oracle estimator that is given
\Pi
, and the second term comes from the error of estimating
\Pi
.
We conjecture that when
K
grows with
Nn
at a fast speed, the plug-in error of
\Pi
is fundamentally non-negligible. To support this conjecture, we may need tighter lower bound and/or tighter upper bound. We leave it to future work.

In our framework, the dimension

is fixed. When

grows with

, we may assume that
X_{ij}
’s are mappings of latent low-dimensional variables (e.g., this may hold in the first application in Section

’s represent the contextual word embeddings from a pre-trained language model). Suppose the latent variables

are in dimension

be i.i.d. variables from

, and let

be a

. A simple model is

, where

follow the model in (

)-(

).
In this set-up, we let

be obtained by re-arranging all data points into a matrix.
We can estimate

’s from the first

right singular vectors of

.
Then, estimating

based on

It is possible to adapt our estimator to this setting. This is an interesting future direction.

While we focus on the nonparametric setting, our methodology is also useful in the parametric setting where

(e.g., see
41
;
14
about related problems).
The two steps of binning data into counts and applying topic modeling to estimate

are still applicable.
In the last step, instead of using KDE, we can consider a plug-in maximum likelihood estimator (MLE) for
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{K}
, possibly with de-biasing. Compared to the variational EM, this approach will be computationally more efficient, due to leveraging existing topic modeling algorithms.

Our model is closely related to mixed membership models in various applications
(
1
;
30
)
.
In these problems, the settings are typically fully parametric.
Our methodology and theory together offer a possible way of extending them to nonparametric settings.
In a broad context, our model is also related to many statistical problems involving latent structure and nonparametric components, where the estimation difficulty depends jointly on structural complexity and function smoothness; see, e.g., graphon estimation
(
16
)
, semiparametric latent variable models
(
29
)
, and smoothed tensor estimation
(

;
18
)
.

In this paper, we assume the data are i.i.d. within each of the

groups, and our analysis relies on properties of incomplete U-statistics and incomplete U-processes. When the data within each group are non-independent, we may continue to apply our estimator, but the analysis will require more sophisticated technical tools (e.g.,

where we have used the assumption

for some constant

\displaystyle\widehat{{\bm{g}}}(x)=\widehat{G}^{\prime}\widehat{G}(\widehat{G}^{\prime}T\widehat{G})^{-1}\widehat{G}^{\prime}S{\bf 1}_{n}.

Since

\|\widehat{G}\|\leq\sqrt{K}\|\widehat{G}\|_{1}=\sqrt{K}

Therefore, on the event

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[1\{(\mathcal{E}\cap\Delta^{*})^{c}\}\int\|\bm{\widehat{g}}^{*}(x)-\widehat{\bm{g}}(x)\|^{2}dx\right]\leq C\frac{KM^{4}\log^{4}(Nn)}{(Nn)^{5}h}.

Together, we obtain:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\int\|\bm{\widehat{g}}^{*}(x)-\widehat{\bm{g}}(x)\|^{2}dx\right]\leq C\left[M\delta_{n}^{2}+\frac{KM^{4}\log^{4}(Nn)}{(Nn)^{5}h}\right].

\displaystyle f_{i}^{(s)}(x)=\sum_{\ell\neq k}\pi_{i}(\ell)g_{0}(x)+\pi_{i}(k)g_{k}^{(s)}(x),\quad f_{i}^{(0)}(x)=g_{0}(x).

It implies:

\displaystyle f_{i}^{(s)}(x)-f_{i}^{(0)}(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{i}(k)(g_{k}^{(s)}(x)-g_{0}(x))=\delta\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{i}(k)\sum_{b=1}^{B}\omega_{b}^{(s,k)}\psi_{b}(x)=:\Delta_{i}^{(s)}(x).

Note that the KL divergence between
\mathbb{P}^{(s)}
and
\mathbb{P}^{(0)}
is:

\mathrm{KL}(\mathbb{P}^{(s)},\mathbb{P}^{(0)})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\int f_{i}^{(s)}(x)\log\left(\frac{f_{i}^{(s)}(x)}{f_{i}^{(0)}(x)}\right)dx.

Since
\{\psi_{b}(x)\}
have disjoint supports, we have for all
x\in[-T,T]
,
|\Delta_{i}^{(s)}(x)/f_{i}^{(0)}(x)|\leq\delta\|\psi\|_{\infty}/c_{0}=o(1)
. Then using the inequality
\log(1+u)\leq u-\frac{u^{2}}{2(1+|u|)}
for
|u|<1
, and noting that
\int\Delta_{i}^{(s)}(x)dx=0
such that the first term order vanishes, there exists a constant
C>0
such that for all
1\leq i\leq n
and each
s
,

\int f_{i}^{(s)}(x)\log\left(\frac{f_{i}^{(s)}(x)}{f_{i}^{(0)}(x)}\right)dx\leq C\int\frac{(\Delta_{i}^{(s)}(x))^{2}}{f_{i}^{(0)}(x)}dx\leq Cc_{0}^{-1}\|\Delta_{i}^{(s)}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

It then remains to bound
\|\Delta_{i}^{(s)}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}
. Due to the disjoint support of the bumps
\{\psi_{b}\}
, we have:

\displaystyle\|\Delta_{i}^{(s)}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|\delta\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{i}(k)\sum_{b=1}^{B}\omega_{b}^{(s,k)}\psi_{b}(x)\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\delta^{2}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{i}(k)\omega_{b}^{(s,k)}\right)^{2}\frac{1}{B}

\displaystyle\leq\delta^{2}.

Therefore, the KL divergence between
\mathbb{P}^{(s)}
and
\mathbb{P}^{(0)}
satisfies:

\mathrm{KL}(\mathbb{P}^{(s)},\mathbb{P}^{(0)})=O(Nn\delta^{2}).

Recall that
\delta\asymp\left(\frac{K}{Nn}\right)^{\frac{\beta}{2\beta+1}}
and
B\asymp(Nn/K)^{\frac{1}{2\beta+1}}
. Then, it is seen that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{J}\sum_{s=1}^{J}\text{KL}(\mathbb{P}_{s},\mathbb{P}_{0})=O(Nn\delta^{2})=O(KB)=O(\log J).

Therefore, the condition (b) is also satisfied by picking the constant in
B
properly small.

(B.2)

). Hence, the number of such bins

contained in
\mathcal{A}_{k}

. Therefore, there exist constant

\displaystyle\Sigma_{G,a}=\mathrm{diag}(\sigma_{a,1},\sigma_{a,2},\ldots,\sigma_{a,K}),

for all

. Hence,

. As for the largest eigenvalue, we will then bound

.

Consider

{thm}

Fix

. Consider the model (
1.1
)-(
1.2
) in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and Assumption
1′
. Suppose the assumptions (i)-(iii) and (v) in Theorem
4.2
hold. In addition, suppose
\prod_{j=1}^{d}h_{j}\rightarrow 0
and
Nn\prod_{j=1}^{d}h_{j}\rightarrow\infty
. Now, consider the estimator
\widehat{\bm{g}}^{+}(\bm{x})
in (
4.1
) using the above multivariate product kernel
\mathcal{K}_{h}^{\text{prod}}
. Suppose

and
(Nn)^{-1}K\ll\prod_{j=1}^{d}h_{j}\ll\log^{-1}(Nn)

Then, there exists a constant

such that

C.1
Proof of Theorem
C

Note that as mentioned in Section
4.6
. The last two terms of the bound in Theorem
C
are irrelevant of the dimension. It thus suffices to prove the first two terms. The second term is obtained simply by replacing the bandwidth
h
by the product of bandwidths
\prod_{j=1}^{d}h_{j}
due to the use of the product kernel in our method. Then, in the following, we will only prove the first term.

The first term relates to the bias of KDE for a multivariate density. It is from a generalized version of Lemma
4
, which we present as follows.

Lemma 28

(Multivariate analogue of Lemma
4
)
.

Let
d>1
. For each
1\leq k\leq K
let
g_{k}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be a probability density belonging to an
anisotropic Nikol’ski class
with smoothness vector
\bm{\beta}=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{d})
and constant
L>0
in the sense that for each multi-index
r=(r_{1},\dots,r_{d})
with
0\leq r_{j}\leq\lfloor\beta_{j}\rfloor
the mixed partial
\partial^{r}g_{k}
exists in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and for all
t=(t_{1},\dots,t_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

\Big\|\partial^{r}g_{k}(\cdot+t)-\partial^{r}g_{k}(\cdot)\Big\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq L\sum_{j=1}^{d}|t_{j}|^{\beta_{j}-r_{j}}.

Recall that the multivariate kernel is the product kernel

\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{h}}(u)=\prod_{j=1}^{d}\frac{1}{h_{j}}\mathcal{K}_{j}\!\left(\frac{u_{j}}{h_{j}}\right),\qquad\mathbf{h}=(h_{1},\dots,h_{d}),

where the univariate kernel
\mathcal{K}_{j}
has order
\ell_{j}=\lfloor\beta_{j}\rfloor
in each coordinate (i.e.
\int u^{m}K(u)\,du=0
for
1\leq m\leq\ell_{j}
) and satisfies the integrability condition
\int|u|^{\beta_{j}}|K(u)|\,du<\infty
for each
j
. Define the kernel-smoothed target:

g_{k}^{*}(x)\;=\;\int K_{\mathbf{h}}(u)\,g_{k}(x+u)\,du.

Then, there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\sum_{k=1}^{K}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big(g_{k}^{*}(x)-g_{k}(x)\big)^{2}\,dx\;\leq\;C\,K\sum_{j=1}^{d}h_{j}^{2\beta_{j}}.

Proof.

The proof follows the same high-level steps as the univariate case (Taylor expansion with cancellation of lower order terms by kernel moments and bounding the remainder by the anisotropic Nikol’ski condition), but we keep track of each coordinate’s smoothness.

Fix
k
. Write the bias

\Delta_{k}(x):=g_{k}^{*}(x)-g_{k}(x)=\int\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{h}}(u)\big(g_{k}(x+u)-g_{k}(x)\big)\,du.

Let
\ell_{j}=\lfloor\beta_{j}\rfloor
for
j=1,\dots,d
, and let
\ell=(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d})
. We perform a multivariate Taylor expansion with integral-form remainder. For any multi-index
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{d})
with
0\leq\alpha_{j}\leq\ell_{j}
, denote
|\alpha|=\sum_{j}\alpha_{j}
and
\partial^{\alpha}
the mixed partial derivative. The multivariate Taylor expansion about
x
up to order
\ell
reads

g_{k}(x+u)-g_{k}(x)=\sum_{0<|\alpha|\leq|\ell|}\frac{u^{\alpha}}{\alpha!}\,\partial^{\alpha}g_{k}(x)\;+\;R_{\ell}(x,u),

where
u^{\alpha}=\prod_{j=1}^{d}u_{j}^{\alpha_{j}}
and the remainder has the integral representation

R_{\ell}(x,u)=\sum_{|\alpha|=|\ell|}\frac{|\ell|}{\alpha!}\int_{0}^{1}(1-\tau)^{|\ell|-1}\Big(\partial^{\alpha}g_{k}(x+\tau u)-\partial^{\alpha}g_{k}(x)\Big)\,d\tau\;u^{\alpha}.

Because the kernel is a product kernel and each univariate kernel
\mathcal{K}_{j}
has vanishing moments up to order
\ell_{j}
in coordinate
j
, every polynomial term in the finite sum above integrates to zero against
\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{h}}(u)
. Concretely, for any multi-index
\alpha
with
0<\alpha_{j}\leq\ell_{j}
for some
j
, by the product structure

\int\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{h}}(u)\,u^{\alpha}\,du=\prod_{j=1}^{d}\Big(\int\frac{1}{h_{j}}\mathcal{K}_{j}\!\Big(\frac{u_{j}}{h_{j}}\Big)u_{j}^{\alpha_{j}}\,du_{j}\Big)=\prod_{j=1}^{d}h_{j}^{\alpha_{j}}\Big(\int v^{\alpha_{j}}\mathcal{K}_{j}(v)\,dv\Big)=0,

because at least one coordinate integral vanishes. Hence all polynomial terms vanish and only the remainder contributes:

\Delta_{k}(x)=\int\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{h}}(u)\,R_{\ell}(x,u)\,du.

We now bound the
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
norm of
\Delta_{k}
. Using Minkowski’s integral inequality (twice) and Fubini,

\displaystyle\|\Delta_{k}\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=\Big\|\int\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{h}}(u)\,R_{\ell}(\cdot,u)\,du\Big\|_{L^{2}}\leq\int|\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{h}}(u)|\,\|R_{\ell}(\cdot,u)\|_{L^{2}}\,du.

Plug in the integral expression for
R_{\ell}
and use triangle inequality and Jensen inequality (integrating in
\tau
):

\|R_{\ell}(\cdot,u)\|_{L^{2}}\leq C_{\ell}\sum_{|\alpha|=|\ell|}|u^{\alpha}|\int_{0}^{1}(1-\tau)^{|\ell|-1}\|\partial^{\alpha}g_{k}(\cdot+\tau u)-\partial^{\alpha}g_{k}(\cdot)\|_{L^{2}}\,d\tau,

where
C_{\ell}
is a combinatorial constant depending on
\ell
. By the anisotropic Nikol’ski condition we have, for each such
\alpha
,

\|\partial^{\alpha}g_{k}(\cdot+\tau u)-\partial^{\alpha}g_{k}(\cdot)\|_{L^{2}}\leq L\sum_{j=1}^{d}|\tau u_{j}|^{\beta_{j}-\alpha_{j}}\leq L\sum_{j=1}^{d}|u_{j}|^{\beta_{j}-\alpha_{j}}.

Hence, there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\|R_{\ell}(\cdot,u)\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\sum_{|\alpha|=|\ell|}|u^{\alpha}|\sum_{j=1}^{d}|u_{j}|^{\beta_{j}-\alpha_{j}}\leq C\sum_{j=1}^{d}|u_{j}|^{\beta_{j}}\cdot P(u),

where
P(u)
is a polynomial factor in
|u_{1}|,\dots,|u_{d}|
whose degree depends only on
\ell
and
d
. Concretely one can bound
P(u)\leq C^{\prime}(1+\sum_{j}|u_{j}|^{\ell_{j}})^{|\ell|-1}
, which is harmless because of the integrability of the kernel moments assumed below.

Now, return to the
\|\Delta_{k}\|_{L^{2}}
bound:

\|\Delta_{k}\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\int|\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{h}}(u)|\sum_{j=1}^{d}|u_{j}|^{\beta_{j}}P(u)\,du\leq C\sum_{j=1}^{d}\int\prod_{m=1}^{d}\frac{1}{h_{m}}\big|\mathcal{K}_{j}\big(\tfrac{u_{m}}{h_{m}}\big)\big|\,|u_{j}|^{\beta_{j}}P(u)\,du.

Change variables
v_{m}=u_{m}/h_{m}
. Then

\|\Delta_{k}\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\sum_{j=1}^{d}h_{j}^{\beta_{j}}\int\prod_{m=1}^{d}|\mathcal{K}_{j}(v_{m})|\,|v_{j}|^{\beta_{j}}\tilde{P}(v)\,dv\leq C^{\prime\prime}\sum_{j=1}^{d}h_{j}^{\beta_{j}},

where
\tilde{P}(v)
is the rescaled polynomial and the final integral is finite by the kernel integrability assumptions
\int|v|^{\beta_{j}}|\mathcal{K}_{j}(v)|\,dv<\infty
and bounded moments. Thus

\|\Delta_{k}\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\sum_{j=1}^{d}h_{j}^{\beta_{j}}.

Squaring and summing over
k=1,\dots,K
gives

\sum_{k=1}^{K}\|\Delta_{k}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq K\cdot C^{2}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{d}h_{j}^{\beta_{j}}\Big)^{2}\leq C^{\prime}K\sum_{j=1}^{d}h_{j}^{2\beta_{j}},

where the last inequality follows since
(\sum_{j}a_{j})^{2}\leq d\sum_{j}a_{j}^{2}
and constants are absorbed into
C^{\prime}
. This is the desired bound.
∎
