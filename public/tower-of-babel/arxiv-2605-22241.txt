Abstract
. We provide a complete combinatorial and asymptotic analysis of positive linear systems of equations in one catalytic variable that appear in several combinatorial problems such as in lattice path counting or stack-sortable permutation counting. We show that the corresponding generating functions satisfy a positive polynomial system of equations (which is associated to a context-free grammar). Furthermore we prove a universal asymptotic behaviour.

Primary: 05A15, Secondary: 05A16.

1.
Introduction

Many combinatorial structures resist a direct simple enumeration,
while, paradoxically, attempting to enumerate a problem that is, a priori, harder
(a refinement of these structures taking into account one more parameter)
often helps to get a recursive description which yields a solvable functional equation for the corresponding bivariate generating function. This additional parameter requires an additional variable (that we will denote by
u
) in the functional equation,
which is thus called a
catalytic variable
, and the corresponding functional equation is called a
catalytic equation
:

P(x,u,F(x,u),F(x,0))=0.

This terminology, natural in chemistry, was popularized in combinatorics in particular by Zeilberger
[
25
]
.

This is, for example, an approach which was used successfully by Tutte for the enumeration of planar maps
[
24
]
and by Knuth for the enumeration of permutations sortable via a stack
[
19
]
.
Solving such catalytic equations can seem, at first glance, to be quite a challenge; it requires ad-hoc methods
such as the quadratic method of Tutte and Brown for maps
[
11
]
,
or the kernel method of Knuth for permutations and lattice paths.
These methods were later generalized beyond equations of degree 2
(see e.g.
[
9
,
10
,
6
,
4
]
), and to systems
[
2
,
22
]
,
while also allowing the study of various parameters and structures
[
3
,
7
,
1
]
.

The purpose of this contribution is to prove a complete combinatorial and asymptotic analysis of
positive
linear
systems of equations in
one
catalytic variable.
We show that the corresponding generating functions satisfy a
so-called positive polynomial system of equations (which corresponds to a context-free grammar)
and that the coefficients satisfy (under natural conditions) a universal asymptotic behaviour.
This provides a broadly framed response to the asymptotic question raised by Knuth
[
19
, Section 2.2.1, Exercise 12]
.

1.1.
Example for a linear catalytic equation

As a first example, let us consider the equation associated to Knuth’s seminal problem (we refer to
[
19
, Section 2.2.1, Exercises 4 and 11]
for the presentation
of the combinatorial and computer science motivations behind this equation):

(1.1)

F(x,u)=1+xuF(x,u)+x\frac{F(x,u)-F(x,0)}{u},

where we assume that
F(x,u)
is a power series – and it is an easy exercise to show that
there is actually a unique solution with nonnegative coefficients.
If we just multiply this equation by
u
and set then
u=0
then we get
0=0
and, thus, no information.
However, if we regroup (
1.1
) as

\left(1-xu-\frac{x}{u}\right)F(x,u)=1-x\frac{F(x,0)}{u},

then it is natural to assume that
x
and
u
are related by the relation (or kernel equation)

(1.2)

1-xu-\frac{x}{u}=0\quad\mbox{or}\quad u=x(1+u^{2}).

Formally we can write
u=u(x)
as the solution of the equation
u=x(1+u^{2})

(with
u(0)=0
1
1

1

The other solution of the equation (
1.2
) does not lead to
a power series solution of (
1.1
).
) which can be explicitly computed to

u(x)=({1-\sqrt{1-4x^{2}}})/(2x)
.

If we restrict ourselves to
x>0
und
u>0
then
F(x,u)>0
and we are left with the relation

1-x\frac{F(x,0)}{u(x)}=0

and we can compute
F(x,0)
and
F(x,u)
as

F(x,0)=\frac{u(x)}{x}=\frac{1-\sqrt{1-4x^{2}}}{2x^{2}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad F(x,u)=\frac{1-x\frac{F(x,0)}{u}}{1-xu-\frac{x}{u}}.

Since these functions are power series they constitute the unique solution of (
1.1
).

Actually, this problem is just a rephrasing of a simple lattice-path counting problem. Let
F_{k}(x)
be the generating function of lattice paths (where the variable
x
marks the total number of steps)
that start at the origin
(0,0)
and end at
(n,k)
, where only up-steps
(1,1)
and down-steps
(1,-1)
are allowed and all paths stay above the
x
-axis
(these are the ubiquitous
Dyck paths
).
They clearly satisfy the infinite system of equations,
as seen by splitting any Dyck path before its last step:

\begin{cases}F_{0}(x)=1+F_{1}(x)x,\\
F_{1}(x)=F_{0}(x)x+F_{2}(x)x,\\
F_{2}(x)=F_{1}(x)x+F_{3}(x)x,\\
\phantom{F_{3}(x)\,\,\,}\vdots\end{cases}

Clearly, by multiplying the
k
-th equation by
u^{k}
and summing it up, we directly obtain (
1.1
) with
F(x,u)=\sum_{k\geq 0}F_{k}(x)u^{k}
.
It is classical that Dyck paths can be directly counted in many ways but note that in addition to the above
infinite
system,
we also have a
finite positive
system (a context-free grammar). Indeed, let
A_{0}(x)
denote the
generating function of paths from
(0,0)
to
(n,0)
with
n>0
that stay strictly
above the
x
-axis in between. Then we have

(1.3)

A_{0}(x)=xF_{0}(x)x,\qquad F_{0}(x)=1+F_{0}(x)A_{0}(x)

and consequently
F_{0}(x)=(1-\sqrt{1-4x^{2}})/(2x^{2})
(as before).

We note that
F_{0}(x)
and consequently all functions
F_{k}(x)
have a
common dominating square-root singularity at
x_{0}=\pm\frac{1}{2}
.
Our article shows that these claims hold in fact not just for this simple equation associated to the Dyck case,
but, in full generality, for
systems
of linear catalytic equations of
higher order
.

1.2.
Systems of linear catalytic equations

In order to define the general problem, we consider the
\ell
-th difference of a function
G(x,u)=\sum_{k\geq 0}G_{k}(x)u^{k}
(with
\ell\geq 1
) as

\Delta^{\ell}G(x,u)=\frac{G(x,u)-G_{0}(x)-G_{1}(x)u-\cdots-G_{\ell-1}(x)u^{\ell-1}}{u^{\ell}}=\sum_{k\geq 0}G_{k+\ell}(x)u^{k}.

Furthermore, we set
\Delta^{0}G(x,u)=G(x,u)
.

Definition 1
.

We say that a set of
d\geq 1
generating functions
F_{1}(x,u),\ldots,F_{d}(x,u)

satisfies a positive linear system of polynomial catalytic equations of order
L\geq 1
in one
catalytic variable
u
if there are polynomials
P_{i}(x,u)
and
Q_{i,j,\ell}(x,u)

1\leq i,j\leq d
,
0\leq\ell\leq L
, with nonnegative coefficients
such that

(1.4)

F_{i}(x,u)=P_{i}(x,u)+x\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{\ell=0}^{L}Q_{i,j,\ell}(x,u)\Delta^{\ell}F_{j}(x,u),\qquad(1\leq i\leq d).

It is known that such systems of equations can always be solved (see
[
2
,
22
]
)
by an extended kernel method (the easiest case being Equation (
1.1
)). This method shows that all solutions are algebraic functions.
Thus, it is always possible to get a complete asymptotic analysis for the coefficients
[x^{n}]F_{i,0}(x)
by using a proper singularity analysis.
However, if we focus on positive systems (as defined here), it turns out that we
are in a quite special situation. First, the solution function is also the solutions
of a positive polynomial system (Theorem
1
) and, second, there is a universal asymptotic behaviour
in the strongly connected case, where all dominant singularities are of square-root type (Theorem
2
).

This was already proved in the case of one linear equation
for order
L\leq 2
(see
[
14
]
).
Thus, our results are not only a far-reaching generalization of the results of
[
14
]

but provide a complete solution for systems of linear catalytic equations.

The main feature of this paper is to present a solution procedure that avoids
the kernel method but rephrases the problem into a proper weighted lattice-path counting problem.
In particular, this lattice path problem can then be solved with the help of a strongly connected positive non-linear polynomial system (similarly as for
A_{0}
and
F_{0}
in the above example).
Since the solutions of strongly connected positive non-linear polynomial systems have a common dominant square-root singularity we obtain our main result.

If the system is not strongly connected, then the transfer to a positive nonlinear polynomial system still applies, and we also get a characterization of the analytic structure.

We note that the corresponding problem for non-linear systems of catalytic equations is not solved yet.
Only the case of one equation could be handled (see
[
15
,
14
,
16
]
for the cases
L=1
and
L=2
and
[
12
]
for all
L\geq 1
).

2.
Results

We say that a generating function
F(x)
corresponds to a
finite grammar

or, equivalently, satisfies a
positive polynomial system
if there is a polynomial system of equations

F_{i}(x)=R_{i}(x,F_{1}(x),\ldots,F_{d}(x))
,
1\leq i\leq d
,
where
R_{i}(x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{d})
are polynomials with nonnegative coefficients and
where
F(x)=F_{1}(x)
is the first component of the (unique) power series solutions.

The first result provides a very strong link to systems of linear catalytic systems.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose that
F_{i}(x,u)=\sum_{k\geq 0}F_{i;k}(x)u^{k}
is a solution of a positive linear system of polynomial catalytic equations of the form (
1.4
).
Then for each
k\geq 0
the function
F_{i;k}(x)
corresponds to a finite grammar.

It has been shown with different approaches by Popescu (via Artin approximation
[
23
]
)
and by Bousquet-Mélou and Jehanne (via algebraic elimination
[
9
]
)
that all solutions of (systems of) polynomial catalytic equations are algebraic. Thus, all singularities are algebraic.
Our article shows that it is possible to get rid of the minus sign involved by design in many of these catalytic equations, and that one then gets a
positive
polynomial system.
Now, functions solutions of such positive systems have the property that their dominant singularities are quite restricted:
Banderier and Drmota have shown in
[
5
]
that the exponents
\alpha
of the Puiseux expansions at the dominant singularities can only be
of the form
\alpha=2^{-k}
for some
k\geq 1
or of the form
\alpha=-m2^{-k}

for some
m\geq 1
and
k\geq 0
.
What it is more, many systems have a strongly connected graph of dependence (in particular, it is the case when the system has a single equation);
it implies a dominant square-root singularity (
\alpha=\frac{1}{2}
).
This is what Flajolet and Sedgewick called the Drmota–Lalley–Woods theorem (we refer to the books
[
13
]
and
[
18
]

for more on this theorem).

Combining these results, we obtain a quite universal asymptotic behaviour for positive linear systems of polynomial
catalytic
equations (under natural combinatorial conditions).

Theorem 2
.

Suppose that the functions
F_{i}(x,u)
,
1\leq i\leq d
are the solutions of a positive linear system of polynomial catalytic equations of the form (
1.4
),
where the corresponding infinite linear system for the functions
[u^{k}]F_{i}(x,u)
(for
1\leq i\leq d
and
k\geq 0
) is strongly connected.
Then all solution functions
[u^{k}]F_{i}(x,u)
have a common dominating square-root singularity.

Furthermore, we also get
universal asymptotic expansions
for the coefficients that are of type

[x^{n}]F_{i;k}(x)\sim c_{m}\rho^{-n}n^{-3/2},

for
n\equiv m\bmod M
and some constant
c_{m}
that depends on
m
(with
m\in\{0,1,\ldots,M-1\}
),
compare with
[
5
]
.

Plan of the proofs

The proofs of Theorems
1
and
2
follow the main lines that
are presented in Section
1.1
(when we dealt with a very simple example).
However, one has to add some more ideas to make the method work in general:

(1)

In a first step the solution functions
F_{i;k}(x)=[u^{k}]F_{i}(x,u)
of
the system (
1.4
) are interpreted as generating functions
of a weighted coloured lattice path problem with paths that stay above the
x
-axis.
In the case of a system, one has to distinguish between types of paths and
types (colours) of steps (according to the number of equations).

(2)

In a second step, one solves the weighted lattice path problem in a direct
way (similarly to the example of Section
1.1
) by introducing
so-called
prime walks
(see
[
8
]
).
This leads to an infinite positive non-linear polynomial system of equations (without a catalytic variable).
It is technical, but one can then show that there is a proper finite subsystem from which
all other solution functions can be determined (in the above example, this was the context-free grammar associated to the system (
1.3
)).
This proves Theorem
1
.

(3)

In order to prove Theorem
2
one needs a proper strongly-connected subsystem.
Actually, this is not immediate (the generic case is less obvious than what the system (
1.3
) may suggest).
The point is that the step sets that can be applied at some level
i
vary with
i
, however,
in a monotonely increasing way and they get constant from a certain level on.
This property is reflected in a monotonicity property of the corresponding generating function.
One can then introduce a collection of new (positive) generating functions as differences of
these monotone generating functions. And it turns out that, with the help of these
differences, one obtains a positive polynomial subsystem that is strongly connected.
Thus, all appearing generating functions have a dominating square-root singularity
which proves Theorem
2
.

We start with the analysis of one equation in Section
3
.
The general case is then discussed in Section
4
.

Before we start, we want to mention that the enumeration of generalized Dyck paths – and this is precisely what we will do – has a quite long history. We just mention the papers
[
21
,
20
,
17
]
.

3.
One catalytic equation

We first discuss the case of one catalytic equation

(3.1)

F(x,u)=P(x,u)+x\sum_{\ell=0}^{L}Q_{\ell}(x,u)\Delta^{\ell}F(x,u).

We first focus on the case
P(x,u)=1
and will show later
how the general case can be reduced to this case (see Section
3.4
).

3.1.
Weighted lattice paths

In a first step, we show that the solution of a catalytic equation that is directly related to weighted lattice paths problem. We consider the catalytic equation of the form

(3.2)

F(x,u)=1+x\sum_{\ell=0}^{L}\sum_{j=0}^{J}Q_{\ell,j}(x)u^{j}\Delta^{\ell}F(x,u),

where the polynomials
Q_{\ell,j}(x)
have nonnegative coefficients.
If we set

F(x,u)=\sum_{k\geq 0}F_{k,0}(x)u^{k}

the catalytic equation (
3.2
) transforms
into the following infinite system:

\displaystyle F_{0,0}(x)

\displaystyle=1+x\sum_{\ell=0}^{L}Q_{\ell,j}(x)F_{\ell,0}(x),

(3.3)

\displaystyle F_{k,0}(x)

\displaystyle=x\sum_{\ell=0}^{L}\sum_{j=0}^{k}Q_{\ell,j}(x)F_{\ell+k-j,0}(x),\qquad(0<k<J)

\displaystyle F_{k,0}(x)

\displaystyle=x\sum_{\ell=}^{L}\sum_{j=0}^{J}Q_{\ell,j}(x)F_{\ell+J-j,0}(x)\qquad(k>J)

We now consider lattice walks that connect the points
(0,k)
with
(n,0)
with
k,n\geq 0

that stay above the
x
-axis. Every path
p
consists of a sequence of steps

p=s_{1}s_{2}\cdots s_{M}
(with
M\geq 0
). Formally, the steps are just added up

(0,k)+s_{1}+s_{2}+\cdots+s_{M}=(n,0)

so that the second component of
(0,k)+s_{1}+s_{2}+\cdots+s_{j}
is nonnegative
for all
0\leq j\leq M
.

For every level
k
we define a
multi-set
\mathcal{S}_{k}
of allowed steps that start at this level:

(3.4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{k}

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{\ell=0}^{L}\bigcup_{j=0}^{k}\{(1+r,\ell-j):0\leq r\leq{\rm deg}Q_{\ell,j},[x^{r}]Q_{\ell,j}(x)\neq 0\}\qquad(k<J)

(3.5)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{k}

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{\ell=0}^{L}\bigcup_{j=0}^{J}\{(1+r,\ell-j):0\leq r\leq{\rm deg}Q_{\ell,j},[x^{r}]Q_{\ell,j}(x)\neq 0\}\qquad(k\geq J).

Note that in all cases

1+r\geq 1\quad\mbox{and}\quad-k\leq\ell-j\leq L.

Thus, every step is going to the right, and if we start at level
k\geq 0
then
we stay above the
x
-axis if we add a step from
\mathcal{S}_{k}
.
Further note that the step multi-sets are monotone and stable, that is,

\mathcal{S}_{k}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{k+1}
for all
k\geq 0
and

\mathcal{S}_{k}=\mathcal{S}_{J}
for all
k\geq J
.

We say that a path
p=s_{1}s_{2}\cdots s_{M}
is a path from
(k,0)
to
(0,n)

is allowed if we have that property that
s_{m}\in\mathcal{S}_{k_{m}}

(for
0\leq m\leq M
) if
s_{m}
starts at level
k_{m}
.
Let
\mathcal{P}_{n,k}
denote the multi-set of allowed paths that connect
(0,0)
with

(k,n)
.

Furthermore, we define weights

\displaystyle w(1+r,\ell-j)

\displaystyle=[x^{r}]Q_{\ell,j}(x),\qquad(0\leq\ell\leq L,\ 0\leq j\leq J)

for all possible steps. Note again that the steps are in a multi-set.
Thus, if a step
(1+r,\ell-j)
appears several times (according to the non-zero polynomial coefficients

[x^{r}]Q_{\ell,j}(x)
with the same
r
and the same difference
\ell-j
) then we have to assign the
corresponding weights
[x^{r}]Q_{\ell,j}(x)
accordingly.

Finally, if
p=s_{1}s_{2}\cdots s_{M}\in\mathcal{P}_{n,k}
is an allowed path from
(k,0)
to
(0,n)

then the weight of
p
is defined by

w(p)=\prod_{m=1}^{M}w(s_{m}).

Note again that the same path
p
might appear several times in
\mathcal{P}_{n,k}
with
potentially different weights.

The relation between the catalytic equation (
3.2
) and the lattice paths that
have been described here is the following one.

Lemma 1
.

Suppose that
Q_{\ell,j}(x)
,
0\leq\ell\leq L
,
0\leq j\leq J
, are given polynomials
with nonnegative coefficients. With the help of these polynomials define so-called
allowed multi-sets of lattice paths
\mathcal{P}_{n,k}
as described above.
Furthermore, set

F_{k,0}(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{n,k}}w(p)\,x^{n}

and

F(x,u)=\sum_{k\geq 0}F_{k,0}(x)u^{k}.

Then
F(x,u)
satisfies then equation (
3.2
) and is also
the unique power series solution of this equation.

Proof.

This is a standard combinatorial procedure.
We just have to split up the paths from
(0,k)
to
(n,0)

after the first step that has to be contained in
\mathcal{S}_{k}
.
Furthermore note that the polynomials

xQ_{\ell,j}(x)
,
0\leq\ell\leq L
,
0\leq j\leq\min(k,J)
, encode the
multi-stepset
\mathcal{S}_{k}
with corresponding weights.
Thus, the system (
3.3
) is satisfied,
which is equivalent to (
3.2
).
∎

3.2.
Prime walks

We again start with polynomials
Q_{\ell,j}(x)
,
0\leq\ell\leq L
,
0\leq j\leq J
,
and define multi-sets of steps
\mathcal{S}_{k}

as in (
3.4
) and (
3.5
). The weights
w(s)
of a step
s

is defined in the same way, too.
However, instead of considering just allowed lattice paths
from
(k,0)
to
(n,0)
we consider now (allowed) lattice paths
p=s_{1}s_{2}\cdots s_{M}

that connect
(k_{1},0)
to
(n,k_{2})
for
k_{1},k_{2},n\geq 0
(with the property that

s_{m}\in\mathcal{S}_{k_{m}}
if the step
s_{m}
starts at level
k_{m}
,
1\leq m\leq M

the weight
w(p)=w(s_{1})w(s_{2})\cdots w(s_{M})
is defined accordingly.
The multi-set of (allowed) paths from
(k_{1},0)
to
(n,k_{2})
is now denoted
by
\mathcal{P}_{n,k_{1},k_{2}}
.
Furthermore, we denote by
\mathcal{P}_{n,k_{1},k_{2}}^{[\geq k]}

the sub-multi-set of paths
p\in\mathcal{P}_{n,k_{1},k_{2}}

that contains just paths that stay
\geq k
and set

F_{k_{1},k_{2}}^{[\geq k]}(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{n,k_{1},k_{2}}^{[\geq k]}}w(p)\,x^{n}.

Clearly, we have

F_{k,0}(x)=F_{k,0}^{[\geq 0]}(x).

Further we note that the step multi-sets satisfy
\mathcal{S}_{k}=\mathcal{S}_{J}
for
k\geq J
.
This implies that

(3.6)

F_{k_{1},k_{2}}^{[\geq k]}(x)=F_{k_{1}-k+J,k_{2}-k+J}^{[\geq J]}(x)

for
k\geq J
.

We now do the lattice path counting problem, that is, finding proper relations
between the functions
F_{k_{1},k_{2}}^{[\geq k]}(x)
, in a different way.
For this purpose, we introduce the so-called
prime walks
(see
[
8
]

We fix some
k\geq 0
and
i\geq 0
and consider the multi-set of
all allowed paths
\mathcal{A}_{n,k,i}
that
start at
(0,k)
and end at
(n,k+i)
for some
n>0
and have
the property that they stay strictly above the level
k+i
in between, in particular only the last step ends at level
k+i
.
Furthermore, let

A_{k,i}(x)=\sum_{n>0}\sum_{p\in\mathcal{A}_{n,k,i}}w(p)\,x^{n}

denote the corresponding generating functions. For example,
A_{0,0}(x)

corresponds to the excursions from
0
to
0
.

In the same way, we define for
k\geq 0
,
0\leq i\leq k
,
n>0
the
reverse prime walks

\overline{\mathcal{A}}_{n,k,i}

that start at
(0,k)
and end at
(n,k-1)
and have
the property that they stay strictly above the level
k

The corresponding generating function is given by

\overline{A}_{k,i}(x)=\sum_{n>0}\sum_{p\in\overline{\mathcal{A}}_{n,k,i}}w(p)\,x^{n}.

Note that
A_{k,0}(x)=\overline{A}_{k,0}(x)
. Furthermore, since the step sets satisfy

\mathcal{S}_{k}=\mathcal{S}_{J}
for
k\geq J
it follows that

(3.7)

A_{k,i}(x)=A_{J,i}(x)\quad\mbox{and}\quad\overline{A}_{k,i}(x)=\overline{A}_{J,i}(x)\quad\mbox{for $k\geq J$.}

We now set

\displaystyle\overline{d}_{k}

\displaystyle=\max\{\ell-j:0\leq\ell\leq L,\ 0\leq j\leq k,\ Q_{\ell,j}(x)\neq 0\},\qquad(k\leq J),

and

\displaystyle\underline{d}_{k}

\displaystyle=\max\{j-\ell:0\leq\ell\leq L,\ 0\leq j\leq k,\ Q_{\ell,j}(x)\neq 0\}\qquad(k\leq J).

Then we certainly have

\displaystyle A_{k,i}(x)

\displaystyle=0\qquad\mbox{for $i>\overline{d}_{k}$ and $0\leq k<J$},

(3.8)

\displaystyle A_{k,i}(x)

\displaystyle=0\qquad\mbox{for $i>\overline{d}_{J}$ and $k\geq J$},

\displaystyle\overline{A}_{k,i}(x)

\displaystyle=0\qquad\mbox{for $i>\underline{d}_{k}$ and $0\leq k<J$},

\displaystyle\overline{A}_{k,i}(x)

\displaystyle=0\qquad\mbox{for $i>\underline{d}_{J}$ and $k\geq J$}.

Furthermore, we have

\displaystyle A_{k,\overline{d}_{k}}(x)

\displaystyle=x\sum_{\ell-j=\overline{d}_{k}}Q_{\ell,j}(x)\qquad(0\leq k<J),

(3.9)

\displaystyle A_{k,\overline{d}_{J}}(x)

\displaystyle=x\sum_{\ell-j=\overline{d}_{J}}Q_{\ell,j}(x)\qquad(k\geq J),

\displaystyle\overline{A}_{k,\underline{d}_{k}}(x)

\displaystyle=x\sum_{\ell-j=-\underline{d}_{k}}Q_{\ell,j}(x)\qquad(0\leq k<J),

\displaystyle\overline{A}_{k,\underline{d}_{J}}(x)

\displaystyle=x\sum_{\ell-j=-\underline{d}_{J}}Q_{\ell,j}(x)\qquad(k\geq J).

Thus, the only unknown prime walk generating functions are

(3.10)

A_{k,i}(x),\qquad 0\leq k\leq J,\ 0\leq i<\overline{d}_{k},\qquad\overline{A}_{k,i}(x),\qquad 0\leq k\leq J,\ 0\leq i<\underline{d}_{k}

The next lemma provides a general relation between the
generating functions
F_{k_{1},k_{2}}^{[\geq k]}(x)
and the prime walk generating
functions
A_{k,i}(x)
and
\overline{A}_{k,i}(x)
.

Lemma 2
.

We have the following relations between the generating functions

F_{k_{1},k_{2}}^{[\geq k]}(x)
,
A_{k,i}(x)
and
\overline{A}_{k,i}(x)
:

\displaystyle F_{k_{1},k_{1}}^{[\geq k]}(x)

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{m=k}^{k_{1}}\overline{A}_{k_{1},k_{1}-m}(x)F_{m,k_{1}}^{[\geq k]}(x)\qquad(k\leq k_{1}),

\displaystyle F_{k_{1},k_{2}}^{[\geq k]}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=k}^{k_{1}}\overline{A}_{k_{1},k_{1}-m}(x)F_{m,k_{2}}^{[\geq k]}(x)+\sum_{m=k_{1}+1}^{k_{2}}A_{k_{1},m-k_{1}}(x)F_{m,k_{2}}^{[\geq m]}(x)\qquad(k\leq k_{1}<k_{2}),

\displaystyle F_{k_{1},k_{2}}^{[\geq k]}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=k}^{k_{1}}\overline{A}_{k_{1},k_{1}-m}(x)F_{m,k_{2}}^{[\geq k]}(x)\qquad(k\leq k_{2}<k_{1}),

and

\displaystyle A_{k,i}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{0\leq\ell\leq L,0\leq j\leq k\,:\,\ell-j=i}xQ_{\ell,j}(x)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i_{1}>i,\,i_{2}>i}\ \sum_{0\leq\ell_{1}\leq L,0\leq j_{1}\leq k\,:\,\ell_{1}-j_{1}=i_{1}}xQ_{\ell_{1},j_{1}}(x)\ F_{k+i_{1},k+i_{2}}^{[\geq k+i+1]}(x)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\sum_{0\leq\ell_{2}\leq L,0\leq j_{2}\leq\min(k+i_{2},J)\,:\,\ell_{2}-j_{2}=-(i_{2}-i)}xQ_{\ell_{2},j_{2}}(x)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad(0\leq k<J,\ 0\leq i\leq\overline{d}_{k}),

\displaystyle A_{k,i}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{0\leq\ell\leq L,0\leq j\leq J\,:\,\ell-j=i}xQ_{\ell,j}(x)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i_{1}>i,\,i_{2}>i}\ \sum_{0\leq\ell_{1}\leq L,0\leq j_{1}\leq J\,:\,\ell_{1}-j_{1}=i_{1}}xQ_{\ell_{1},j_{1}}(x)\ F_{k+i_{1},k+i_{2}}^{[\geq k+i+1]}(x)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\sum_{0\leq\ell_{2}\leq L,0\leq j_{2}\leq J\,:\,\ell_{2}-j_{2}=-(i_{2}-i)}xQ_{\ell_{2},j_{2}}(x),

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad(k\geq J,\ 0\leq i\leq\overline{d}_{J}),

as well as

\displaystyle\overline{A}_{k,i}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{0\leq\ell\leq L,0\leq j\leq k\,:\,\ell-j=-i}xQ_{\ell,j}(x)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i_{1}>0,\,i_{2}>0}\ \sum_{0\leq\ell_{1}\leq L,0\leq j_{1}\leq k\,:\,\ell_{1}-j_{1}=i_{1}}xQ_{\ell_{1},j_{1}}(x)\ F_{k+i_{1},k+i_{2}}^{[\geq k+1]}(x)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\sum_{0\leq\ell_{2}\leq L,0\leq j_{2}\leq\min(k+i_{2},J)\,:\,\ell_{2}-j_{2}=-(i+i_{2})}xQ_{\ell_{2},j_{2}}(x),

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad(0\leq k<J,\ 0\leq i\leq\underline{d}_{k}),

\displaystyle\overline{A}_{k,i}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{0\leq\ell\leq L,0\leq j\leq J\,:\,\ell-j=-i}xQ_{\ell,j}(x)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i_{1}>0,\,i_{2}>0}\ \sum_{0\leq\ell_{1}\leq L,0\leq j_{1}\leq J\,:\,\ell_{1}-j_{1}=i_{1}}xQ_{\ell_{1},j_{1}}(x)\ F_{k+i_{1},k+i_{2}}^{[\geq k+1]}(x)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\sum_{0\leq\ell_{2}\leq L,0\leq j_{2}\leq J\,:\,\ell_{2}-j_{2}=-(i+i_{2})}xQ_{\ell_{2},j_{2}}(x),

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad(k\geq J,\ 0\leq i\leq\underline{d}_{J}).

Proof.

The proof of the relations for
F_{k_{1},k_{2}}^{[\geq k]}(x)
is (again) standard
(see
[
8
]
).
In order to get the relation for
F_{k_{1},k_{1}}^{[\geq k]}(x)
, we split at the first point
different from the starting point
(0,k_{1})
that reaches a level
\leq k_{1}
.
The same splitting applies for the (third) case
k_{1}>k_{2}
. If
k_{1}>k_{2}
then there is
either a point (different from the starting point) that reaches a level
\leq k_{1}
– this
provides the fist term – or the path stays at levels
>k_{1}
. In the latter case
let
m\in\{k_{1}+1,\ldots,k_{2}\}
be the minimum level that occurs after the first step.
Here we split at the first occurrence of the level
m
. This gives the second part.

The equation for
A_{k,i}(x)
can be seen in the following way. We either reach
the endpoint
(n,k+i)
in one step – this provides the first term – or the first
step reaches a level
k+i_{1}
for some
i_{1}>0
, then we stay at a level
\geq k+i+1
until the last step that goes from level
k+i_{2}>k+i
to the final level
k+i
. This
provides the second term. The argument for
\overline{A}_{k,i}(x)
is very similar.
∎

We note that there are other ways to describe prime walks.
For example, we can also use the prime-walk decomposition to get a recursive description.

Secondly, it is also possible to split the lattice paths in a way that the prime walk
is put to the very end. For example, in this way we get the relation

(3.11)

F_{k_{1},k_{2}}^{[\geq k]}(x)=\sum_{m=k}^{k_{2}}F_{k_{1},m}^{[\geq k]}(x)A_{m,k_{2}-m}(x)\qquad(k\leq k_{1}<k_{2}).

3.3.
A finite grammar

The main difference between the infinite linear system (
3.3
) and the non-linear system (from Lemma
2
) is that we can find a finite subsystem that contains all the information.

Lemma 3
.

The finite polynomial system of equations for the
F_{k_{1},k_{2}}^{[\geq k]}(x)
(with
0\leq k_{1}<L+J
,
0\leq k_{2}<2J
,
0\leq k\leq\min(J,k_{1},k_{2})
)

A_{k,i}(x)
(with
0\leq k\leq J
,
0\leq i<\overline{d}_{k}
) and
\overline{A}_{k,i}(x)

(with
0\leq k\leq J
,
0\leq i<\underline{d}_{k}
)
from Lemma
2
is a positive polynomial system
of equations that uniquely determines all these functions. Furthermore, all other
functions
F_{k_{1},k_{2}}^{[\geq k]}(x)
can be expressed in a polynomial way with
nonnegative coefficients from them. Thus, all functions
F_{k_{1},k_{2}}^{[\geq k]}(x)

correspond to a finite grammar.

Proof.

By the above discussion, we know that the only unknown prime walk generating functions
are
A_{k,i}(x)
for
0\leq k\leq J
,
0\leq i<\overline{d}_{k}
, and

\overline{A}_{k,i}(x)
,
0\leq k\leq J
,
0\leq i<\underline{d}_{k}
(see
3.10
).
We also note that by (
3.7
) all appearing prime walk generating functions
can be reduced to these functions; all the other ones are either zero or explicitly
known (see (
3.8
) and (
4.6
)).

We study first the equation for
A_{k,i}(x)

(from Lemma
2
), where
0\leq k\leq J
and
0\leq i\leq\overline{d}_{k}\leq L
.
On the right hand side the function
F_{k+i_{1},k+i_{2}}^{[\geq k+i+1]}(x)
appears, where

i_{1}\leq L
and
i_{2}\leq i+J
. If
k+i+1\leq J
then we certainly have

k+i_{1}<J+L
and
k+i_{2}=k+i+i-i_{2}<J+J
. However, if
k+i+1>J
we have

F_{k+i_{1},k+i_{2}}^{[\geq k+i+1]}(x)=F_{i_{1}-i+J-1,i_{2}-i+J-1}^{[\geq J]}(x),

where we (again) have
i_{1}-i+J-1<L+J
and
i_{2}-i+J-1<2J
.

Similarly we can study the equation for
\overline{A}_{k,i}(x)
, where

0\leq k\leq J
and
0\leq i\leq\underline{d}_{k}\leq J
.
In this case on the right hand side the function
F_{k+i_{1},k+i_{2}}^{[\geq k+1]}(x)
appears,
where
i_{1}\leq L
and
i_{2}+i\leq J
.
If
k+1\leq J
we certainly have
k+i_{1}<J+L
and
k+i_{2}<2J
.
Secondly, if
k+1>J
we have

F_{k+i_{1},k+i_{2}}^{[\geq k+1]}(x)=F_{i_{1}+J-1,i_{2}+J-1}^{[\geq J]}(x),

where
i_{1}+J-1<L+J
and
i_{2}+J-1<2J
.

Summing up, we rewriting the right hand side of the equations for

A_{k,i}(x)
and
\overline{A}_{k,i}(x)
the only functions that appear
are of the form
F_{k_{1},k_{2}}^{[\geq k]}
with
0\leq k_{1}<L+J
,
0\leq k_{2}<2J
,
0\leq k\leq\min(J,k_{1},k_{2})
.

Finally we check the equations for
F_{k_{1},k_{2}}^{[\geq k]}(x)
(from Lemma
2
) in this
range. As already mentioned all appearing prime walk generating functions can be reduced
to the cases
A_{k,i}(x)
for
0\leq k\leq J
,
0\leq i<\overline{d}_{k}
, and

\overline{A}_{k,i}(x)
,
0\leq k\leq J
,
0\leq i<\underline{d}_{k}
. Thus, we only have
to take care of the
F
-functions that appear on the right hand side.
In the first case (where
k_{1}=k_{2}
) and in the third case (where
k_{2}<k_{1}
)
the right hand side contains only functions of the form

F_{m,k_{2}}^{[\geq k]}(x)
with
k\leq m\leq k_{1}
. Thus, we certainly have
m<L+J
and
k_{2}<2J

and we are done. In the second case (
k_{1}<k_{2}
) we have to be more careful.
There is no problem for the first sum, where
F_{m,k_{2}}^{[\geq k]}
appears (with
k\leq m\leq k_{1})
.
The second sum contains the function
F_{m,k_{2}}^{[\geq m]}(x)
. If
m\leq J
then we certainly have

m<L+J
. Conversely if
m>J
then we have

F_{m,k_{2}}^{[\geq m]}(x)=F_{J,k_{2}-m+J}^{[\geq J]}(x),

where
J<L+J
and
k_{2}-m+J<k_{2}<2J
.

This finishes the first part of the proof.

For the second part we first observe that the functions

\frac{1}{1-A_{k,0}(x)}=\frac{1}{1-\overline{A}_{k,0}(x)}\qquad(k\geq 0)

are known, actually they can directly written in terms of the solution
functions of the above finite system.
In any case (in particular for
k\geq J
) we have

\frac{1}{1-A_{k,0}(x)}=F_{k,k}^{[\geq k]}(x),

and for
k>J
we also have

\frac{1}{1-A_{k,0}(x)}=\frac{1}{1-A_{J,0}(x)}=F_{J,J}^{[\geq J]}(x)

Secondly we use now a slightly modified system for the
functions
F_{k_{1},k_{2}}^{[\geq k]}(x)
that follows from
Lemma
2
and (
3.11
):

\displaystyle F_{k_{1},k_{1}}^{[\geq k]}(x)

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{m=k}^{k_{1}}\overline{A}_{k_{1},k_{1}-m}(x)F_{m,k_{1}}^{[\geq k]}(x)\qquad(k\leq k_{1}),

\displaystyle F_{k_{1},k_{2}}^{[\geq k]}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=k}^{k_{2}}F_{k_{1},m}^{[\geq k]}(x)A_{m,k_{2}-m}(x)\qquad(k\leq k_{1}<k_{2}),

\displaystyle F_{k_{1},k_{2}}^{[\geq k]}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=k}^{k_{1}}\overline{A}_{k_{1},k_{1}-m}(x)F_{m,k_{2}}^{[\geq k]}(x)\qquad(k\leq k_{2}<k_{1}).

We rewrite this system to:

\displaystyle F_{k_{1},k_{1}}^{[\geq k]}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-\overline{A}_{k_{1},0}(x)}\left(1+\sum_{m=k}^{k_{1}-1}\overline{A}_{k_{1},k_{1}-m}(x)F_{m,k_{1}}^{[\geq k]}(x)\right)\qquad(k\leq k_{1}),

\displaystyle F_{k_{1},k_{2}}^{[\geq k]}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-A_{k_{2},0}(x)}\left(\sum_{m=k}^{k_{2}-1}F_{k_{1},m}^{[\geq k]}(x)A_{m,k_{2}-m}(x)\right)\qquad(k\leq k_{1}<k_{2}),

\displaystyle F_{k_{1},k_{2}}^{[\geq k]}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-\overline{A}_{k_{1},0}(x)}\left(\sum_{m=k}^{k_{1}-1}\overline{A}_{k_{1},k_{1}-m}(x)F_{m,k_{2}}^{[\geq k]}(x)\right)\qquad(k\leq k_{2}<k_{1}).

Now, for every given
k\geq 0
, we proceed on induction on the sum
k_{1}+k_{2}
.
The starting point is the case
k_{1}=k_{2}=k
. Here we already observed that
the functions
F_{k,k}^{[\geq k]}(x)
can be considered as known functions
(that are part of the solution of the above finite system).
The induction step is now trivial since the sum of the indices on the right hand
side is always smaller than the sum
k_{1}+k_{2}
on the left hand side. Furthermore
all prime walk generating functions are either zero, explicit, or part of the
above finite system. This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

3.4.
Proof of Theorem
1
for one equation

Finally, we consider the general case (
3.1
), where we represent
the polynomial
P(x,u)
as

P(x,u)=\sum_{m=0}^{M}P_{m}(x)u^{m}.

By linearity the solution
F(x,u)
can be represented by

(3.12)

F(x,u)=\sum_{m=0}^{m}P_{m}(x)G_{m}(x,u),

where
G_{m}(x,u)
is the solution of the equation

(3.13)

G_{m}(x,u)=u^{m}+x\sum_{\ell=0}^{L}Q_{\ell}(x,u)\Delta^{\ell}G_{m}(x,u).

If we write

G_{m}(x,u)=\sum_{k\geq 0}G_{m;k}(x)u^{k}

then (
3.13
) rewrites to the infinite system

\displaystyle G_{m;0}(x)

\displaystyle=\delta_{0,m}+x\sum_{\ell=0}^{L}Q_{\ell,j}(x)G_{m;\ell}(x),

(3.14)

\displaystyle G_{m;k}(x)

\displaystyle=\delta_{k,m}+x\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\sum_{j=0}^{k}Q_{\ell,j}(x)G_{m;\ell+k-j}(x),\qquad(0<k<J)

\displaystyle G_{m;k}(x)

\displaystyle=\delta_{k,m}+x\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\sum_{j=0}^{J}Q_{\ell,j}(x)G_{m;\ell+J-j}(x)\qquad(k>J).

Lemma 4
.

We have the relation

G_{m;k}(x)=F_{k,m}^{[\geq 0]}(x),

that is, the functions
G_{m;k}(x)
are the generation function of the allowed lattice paths
that start at level
k
and end at level
m
.

Proof.

The proof is precisely the same at that of Lemma
1
. We
just partition the paths after the first step. Note that the
1=\delta_{m,m}

appears now from the zero step path from
(0,m)
to
(0,m)
.
∎

Now we can finish the proof of Theorem
1
for one equation.
By Lemma
2
and Lemma
4
it follows that
the functions
G_{m;k}(x)
corresponds to a finite grammar.
By (
3.12
) we have

F_{k}(x)=\sum_{m=0}^{m}P_{m}(x)G_{m;k}(x)

and, thus,
F_{k}(x)
corresponds to a finite grammar, too.

3.5.
An example

Let us consider the example

(3.15)

F(x,u)=1+xuF(x,u)+x(1+u^{3})\Delta^{1}F(x,u).

This equation translates into the following infinite system:

\displaystyle F_{0,0}(x)

\displaystyle=1+xF_{1,0}(x),

\displaystyle F_{1,0}(x)

\displaystyle=xF_{0,0}(x)+xF_{2,0}(x),

\displaystyle F_{2,0}(x)

\displaystyle=xF_{1,0}(x)+xF_{3,0}(x),

\displaystyle F_{3,0}(x)

\displaystyle=xF_{2,0}(x)+xF_{4,0}(x)+xF_{1,0}(x),

\displaystyle F_{4,0}(x)

\displaystyle=xF_{3,0}(x)+xF_{5}(x)+xF_{2,0}(x),

This means that the equations for
F_{k,0}(x)
are stable for
k\geq 3
but
have a different scheme for
k<3
. By using the general notation we have

L=1
and
J=3
and the step (multi-)sets are given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{0}

\displaystyle=\{(1,1)\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{1}

\displaystyle=\{(1,1),(1,-1)\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{2}

\displaystyle=\{(1,1),(1,-1)\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{k}

\displaystyle=\{(1,1),(1,-1),(1,-2)\}\qquad(k\geq 3).

The first observation is that the functions
F_{k_{1},k_{2}}^{[\geq k]}(x)
are stable
quite early. Actually we have

F_{k_{1},k_{2}}^{[\geq k]}(x)=F_{k_{1}-k+1,k_{2}-k+1}^{[\geq 1]}\qquad(k\geq 1).

We start with an analysis of the prime walks. By Lemma
2
we have

A_{0,0}(x)=xF_{1,1}^{[\geq 1]}(x)x,\qquad A_{0,1}(x)=x,\qquad A_{1,i}(x)=0\quad(i\geq 2)

and

\displaystyle A_{k,0}(x)

\displaystyle=xF_{k+1,k+1}^{[\geq k+1]}(x)x+xF_{k+1,k+2}^{[\geq k+1]}(x)x=xF_{1,1}^{[\geq 1]}(x)x+xF_{1,2}^{[\geq 1]}(x)x=A_{1,0}(x)\quad(k\geq 1),

\displaystyle A_{k,1}(x)

\displaystyle=x\quad(k\geq 1),\qquad A_{k,i}(x)=0\quad(k\geq i\geq 2).

as well as

\displaystyle\overline{A}_{k,0}(x)

\displaystyle=A_{k,0}(x)\quad(k\geq 0),

\displaystyle\overline{A}_{1,1}(x)

\displaystyle=x,\qquad\overline{A}_{k,1}(x)=x+xF_{k+1,k+1}^{[\geq k+1]}(x)x=x+xF_{1,1}^{[\geq 1]}(x)x=\overline{A}_{2,1}\quad(k\geq 2),

\displaystyle\overline{A}_{k,2}(x)

\displaystyle=\overline{A}_{3,2}(x)=x\quad(k\geq 3),\qquad\overline{A}_{k,i}(x)=0\quad(k\geq i\geq 3).

This means that the only unknown prime walk functions are

A_{0,0}(x),\ A_{1,0}(x),\ \overline{A}_{2,1}(x),

where (only) the functions

F_{1,1}^{[\geq 1]}(x),F_{1,2}^{[\geq 1]}(x)

appear on the right hand of the equation. For these two functions we have
(again by Lemma
2
and by the above relations for the prime walk functions)

\displaystyle F_{1,1}^{[\geq 1]}(x)

\displaystyle=1+\overline{A}_{1,0}(x)F_{1,1}^{[\geq 1]}(x)=1+A_{1,0}(x)F_{1,1}^{[\geq 1]}(x),

\displaystyle F_{1,2}^{[\geq 1]}(x)

\displaystyle=\overline{A}_{1,0}(x)F_{1,2}^{[\geq 1]}(x)+A_{1,1}(x)F_{2,2}^{[\geq 2]}(x)=A_{1,0}(x)F_{1,2}^{[\geq 1]}(x)+xF_{1,1}^{[\geq 1]}(x).

Already this system of
3+2
equations is solvable. Actually we can start with
the strongly connected system of
3
equations for

A_{1,0}(x)
,
F_{1,1}^{[\geq 1]}(x)
,
F_{1,2}^{[\geq 1]}(x)
:

\displaystyle A_{1,0}(x)

\displaystyle=xF_{1,1}^{[\geq 1]}(x)x+xF_{1,2}^{[\geq 1]}(x)x,

\displaystyle F_{1,1}^{[\geq 1]}(x)

\displaystyle=1+A_{1,0}(x)F_{1,1}^{[\geq 1]}(x),

\displaystyle F_{1,2}^{[\geq 1]}(x)

\displaystyle=A_{1,0}(x)F_{1,2}^{[\geq 1]}(x)+xF_{1,1}^{[\geq 1]}(x).

By general theory (see
[
13
]
) it is known that all three solution functions
have a common dominating squareroot singularity
x_{0}>0
.
This holds then also for
A_{0,0}(x)
and
\overline{A}_{2,1}(x)
.

However, it is not clear that holds for all the other functions.
For example, from

F_{0,0}^{[\geq 0]}(x)=1+\overline{A}_{0,0}(x)F_{0,0}^{[\geq 0]}(x)

it follows that

F_{0,0}^{[\geq 0]}(x)=\frac{1}{1-\overline{A}_{0,0}(x)}.

If
\overline{A}_{0,0}(x_{0})\geq 1
then
F_{0,0}^{[\geq 0]}(x)
has a different
kind of dominating singularity.

But there is a simple trick that shows that such a situation cannot occur.
By using the observation that the step sets are weakly increasing from one level to the next,
it follows that

[x^{n}]F_{1,1}^{[\geq 1]}(x)\geq[x^{n}]F_{0,0}^{[\geq 0]}(x)\quad\mbox{and}\quad[x^{n}]A_{1,i}(x)\geq[x^{n}]A_{0,i}(x)

for all
n\geq 0
. In particular we can write

\displaystyle F_{1,1}^{[\geq 1]}(x)

\displaystyle=F_{0,0}^{[\geq 0]}(x)+D_{1}(x),

\displaystyle A_{1,0}(x)

\displaystyle=A_{0,0}(x)+B_{1}(x).

where
D_{1}(x),B_{1}(x)
are power series with nonnegative coefficients.

Due to the monotonicity of the step sets, there is also a monotonicity property in the corresponding equations. That is, the equations for
F_{1,1}^{[\geq 1]}(x),A_{1,0}(x),A_{1,1}(x)
contain at least the corresponding terms as the equations for
F_{0,0}^{[\geq 0]}(x),A_{0,0}(x),A_{0,1}(x)
, respectively.
For example, the equation
A_{1,0}(x)=xF_{1,1}^{[\geq 1]}(x)x+xF_{1,2}^{[\geq 1]}(x)
has one more term than the equation
A_{0,0}(x)=xF_{1,1}^{[\geq 1]}(x)x
.
This implies that we split the above system of equations in the following way:

\displaystyle F_{0,0}^{[\geq 0]}(x)+D_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=1+(A_{0,0}(x)+B_{1}(x))(F_{0,0}^{[\geq 0]}(x)+D_{1}(x)),

\displaystyle A_{0,0}(x)+B_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=x(F_{0,0}^{[\geq 0]}(x)+D_{1}(x))x+xF_{1,2}^{[\geq 2]}(x)x.

Due to the monotonicity property and by comparing with the two equations

\displaystyle F_{0,0}^{[\geq 0]}(x)

\displaystyle=1+A_{0,0}(x)F_{0,0}^{[\geq 0]}(x),

\displaystyle A_{0,0}(x)

\displaystyle=xF_{1,1}^{[\geq 1]}(x)x=x(F_{0,0}^{[\geq 0]}(x)+D_{1}(x))x,

we obtain new equations for
D_{1}(x),B_{1}(x)
:

\displaystyle D_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=B_{1}(x)F_{0,0}^{[\geq 0]}(x)+(A_{0,0}(x)+B_{1}(x))D_{1}(x),

\displaystyle B_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=xF_{1,2}^{[\geq 2]}(x)x.

The only unknown function here is
F_{1,2}^{[\geq 2]}(x)
. By adding the corresponding
equation

F_{1,2}^{[\geq 1]}(x)=A_{1,0}(x)F_{1,2}^{[\geq 1]}(x)+xF_{1,1}^{[\geq 1]}(x)=(A_{0,0}(x)+B_{1}(x))F_{1,2}^{[\geq 1]}(x)+x(F_{0,0}^{[\geq 0]}(x)+D_{1}(x)),

we end up with a strongly connected positive system of equations that contains
the function
F_{0,0}^{[\geq 0]}(x)
as well:

\displaystyle F_{0,0}^{[\geq 0]}(x)

\displaystyle=1+A_{0,0}(x)F_{0,0}^{[\geq 0]}(x),

\displaystyle A_{0,0}(x)

\displaystyle=x(F_{0,0}^{[\geq 0]}(x)+D_{1}(x))x,

\displaystyle D_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=B_{1}(x)F_{0,0}^{[\geq 0]}(x)+(A_{0,0}(x)+B_{1}(x))D_{1}(x),

\displaystyle B_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=xF_{1,2}^{[\geq 2]}(x)x,

\displaystyle F_{1,2}^{[\geq 1]}(x)

\displaystyle=(A_{0,0}(x)+B_{1}(x))F_{1,2}^{[\geq 1]}(x)+x(F_{0,0}^{[\geq 0]}(x)+D_{1}(x)).

Thus, it follows that all these functions have a common square-root singularity at
the dominant singularity at
x_{0}>0
.

There are now several ways to show that all appearing generating functions
have the same dominating square-root singularity. In this example there is a simple
and direct way.
Since
F_{1,1}^{[\geq 1]}(x)=F_{0,0}^{[\geq 0]}(x)+D_{1}(x)
and
A_{1,0}(x)=A_{0,0}(x)+B_{1}(x)

the same holds for
F_{1,1}^{[\geq 1]}(x)
and
A_{1,0}(x)
.
Furthermore, since
\overline{A}_{2,1}(x)=x+x(F_{0,0}^{[\geq 0]}(x)+D_{1}(x))x
we get also
the same property for
\overline{A}_{2,1}(x)
. Now we can proceed as in the last part of the
proof of Lemma
3
and show inductively that all functions

F_{k_{1},k_{2}}^{[\geq k]}(x)
have a dominating square-root singularity at
x_{0}
.

3.6.
Proof of Theorem
2
for one equation

Now we use the assumption that from every level
k_{1}
we can reach every level
k_{2}

(either the same or any other) by an allowed lattice path that stays
\geq k
, where
k\geq\min(k_{1},k_{2})
.
This is equivalent to the statement that the (infinite) system of equations
(
3.3
) is strongly connected. (If
F_{k,0}(x)
depends (after some iterations)
on
F_{0,0}(x)
then there has to be an allowed path from level
k
to level
0
. Similarly if

F_{0,0}(x)
depends (after some iterations) on
F_{k,0}(x)
then there has to be an
allowed path from level
0
to level
k
. By combining such paths we can start at any level
k_{1}

and reach with an allowed path every level
k_{2}
. Due to monotonicity property and by shifting
a path accordingly we can also assume that we stay
\geq k
, where
k\geq\min(k_{1},k_{2})
.)

We recall that we already know that
all appearing generating functions correspond to a finite grammar.
Thus, the kind of singularities (as well as the asymptotic behavior of the coefficients)
is quite restricted, see
[
5
]
.

The starting point of the proof of Theorem
2
is the following observation on
the singular behavior of the functions
F_{k,k}^{[\geq k]}(x)
.

Lemma 5
.

Suppose that the assumptions of Theorem
2
are satisfied. Then
the functions
F_{k,k}^{[\geq k]}(x)
,
k\geq 0
, have a common dominating
square-root singularity at some
x_{0}>0
.

Proof.

We recall that it is sufficient to consider the cases
0\leq k\leq J
.
For
k>J
we have
F_{k,k}^{[\geq k]}(x)=F_{J,J}^{[\geq J]}(x)
.
Furthermore by the monotonicity property of the step multi-sets
\mathcal{S}_{k}
we
can write

(3.16)

F_{k,k}^{[\geq k]}(x)=F_{0,0}^{[\geq 0]}(x)+D_{1}(x)+\cdots+D_{k}(x),\qquad(k\leq J),

where the functions
D_{j}(x)
,
1\leq j\leq J
, have nonnegative coefficients.
Similarly we can represent

(3.17)

A_{k,0}(x)=A_{0,0}(x)+B_{1}(x)+\cdots+B_{k}(x),,\qquad(k\leq J),

where the functions
B_{j}(x)
,
1\leq j\leq J
, have nonnegative coefficients.
Since we have the functional equations

F_{k,k}^{[\geq k]}(x)=1+A_{k,0}(x)F_{k,k}^{[\geq k]}(x)\quad(k\leq J)

it follows (by taking differences and by using the above representations) that

(3.18)

\displaystyle D_{j}(x)

\displaystyle=B_{j}(x)\left(F_{0,0}^{[\geq 0]}(x)+D_{1}(x)+\cdots+D_{j-1}(x)\right)

\displaystyle+\left(A_{0,0}(x)+B_{1}(x)+\cdots+B_{j}(x)\right)D_{j}(x),\qquad(1\leq j\leq J).

In particular, (
3.18
) shows that
D_{j}(x)\neq 0
if and only if
B_{j}(x)\neq 0

(note that by assumption
A_{0,0}(x)\neq 0
and
F_{0,0}^{[\geq 0]}(x)\neq 0
).
Thus, we can restrict ourselves to those
j
for which
D_{j}(x)\neq 0
.
Let
J^{\prime}
the maximum
j\in\{1,2,\ldots,J\}
for which
D_{j}(x)\neq 0
.
Then the equation for
D_{J^{\prime}}(x)
contains on the right hand side the
functions
A_{0,0}(x)
,
F_{0,0}^{[\geq 0]}(x)
and all functions

B_{j}(x)
,
D_{j}(x)
with
1\leq j\leq J
that are non-zero.
Furthermore, the equation
F_{0,0}^{[\geq 0]}(x)=1+A_{0,0}(x)F_{0,0}^{[\geq 0]}(x)

as well as the equations for
D_{j}(x)

1\leq j\leq J
, for which
D_{j}(x)\neq 0
, contains
on the right hand side
A_{0,0}(x)
,
F_{0,0}^{[\geq 0]}(x)
.

Similarly to the decompositions (
3.16
) and (
3.17
) we can
decompose all other functions
F_{k_{1},k_{2}}^{[\geq k]}(x)
,
A_{k,i}(x)
,
\overline{A}_{k,i}(x)

and due to the monotonicity property of the step multi-sets
\mathcal{S}_{k}

we can replace the old system for
F_{k_{1},k_{2}}^{[\geq k]}(x)
,
A_{k,i}(x)
,
\overline{A}_{k,i}(x)

by a new one that is again a positive polynomial system.
By Lemma
3
it is sufficient to consider a finite (old) system of equations.
Hence, it is also sufficient to consider a finite new system (where – without loss of
generality – we remove zero equations).

The first goal is to find a strongly connected system of equations that computes

F_{0,0}^{[\geq 0]}(x)
. For this purpose we consider the corresponding dependency
graph
G
and restrict ourselves to the strongly connected component
G

that contains
F_{0,0}^{[\geq 0]}(x)
.
By the above observations all functions
D_{j}(x)

1\leq j\leq J
, for which
D_{j}(x)\neq 0
, as
well as
A_{0,0}(x)
and all functions
B_{j}(x)

1\leq j\leq J
, for which
D_{j}(x)\neq 0
,
are contained in this subsystem. Hence, all theses functions have a common
dominant square-root singularity
x_{0}>0
. Consequently the functions

F_{k,k}^{[\geq k]}(x)=F_{0,0}^{[\geq 0]}(x)+D_{1}(x)+\cdots+D_{k}(x),\qquad(k\leq J),

and as well the functions
F_{k,k}^{[\geq k]}(x)=F_{J,J}^{[\geq J]}(x)
,
k\geq J
, have this
dominant square-root singularity
x_{0}>0
.
∎

The next lemma is a general property for generating functions that correspond to
a finite grammar. By
[
5
]
the dominant singularities can be only of
special (dyadic) types
(1-x/x_{0})^{\alpha}
. Furthermore,
the asymptotics of the coefficients – at least if
we restrict ourselves to a proper residue class
n\equiv r\bmod m
– correspond to the
dominant singularity, that is
cx_{0}^{-n}n^{-\alpha-1}

(with some
c>0
). We say that a generating function
f(x)
(of this type)
with leading asymptotics
cx_{0}^{-n}n^{-\alpha-1}
,
n\equiv r\bmod m
, is dominated by
g(x)

with leading asymptotics
\overline{c}\overline{x}_{0}^{-n}n^{-\overline{\alpha}-1}
,

n\equiv\overline{r}\bmod\overline{m}
, if

x_{0}^{-n}n^{-\alpha-1}=O\left(\overline{x}_{0}^{-n}n^{-\overline{\alpha}-1}\right)
, that is,

\overline{x}_{0}<x_{0}
or
\alpha\leq\overline{\alpha}
(if
\overline{x}_{0}=x_{0}
).

Lemma 6
.

Suppose that
f(x)
and
g(x)
are non-polynomial generating functions that correspond
to a finite grammar such that

g(x)=P(x)f(x)+R(x),

where
P(x)
is a non-zero polynomial with nonnegative coefficients and

R(x)
a power series with nonnegative coefficients.
Then
f(x)
is dominated by
g(x)
.

Proof.

First we observe that
P(x)f(x)
has the same type of singularity as
f(x)
.
Furthermore, since

[x^{n}]g(x)\geq[x^{n}]P(x)f(x)

it directly follows that
f(x)
is dominated by
g(x)
.
∎

The next observation will then conclude the proof of Theorem
2
.

Lemma 7
.

Suppose that the assumptions of Theorem
2
are satisfied.
Then all functions
F_{k,k}^{[\geq k]}(x)
have the same dominating singularity
as
F_{0,0}^{[\geq 0]}(x)
. Furthermore the prime walk functions

A_{k,i}(x)
,
\overline{A}_{k,i}(x)
are either polynomials or have again
the same dominating singularity as
F_{0,0}^{[\geq 0]}(x)
.

Proof.

By Lemma
5
all functions
F_{k,k}^{[\geq k]}(x)
,
0\leq k\leq J
, have
the same dominating (square-root) singularity (namely that of
F_{0,0}^{[\geq 0]}(x)
).

We consider now a function
F_{k_{1},k_{2}}^{[\geq k]}(x)
with
k\leq\min(k_{1},k_{2})
. By assumption there is an
allowed path
p_{1}
from level
k_{1}
to level
k
(that stays at levels
\geq k
and goes
n_{1}
units
into the
x
-direction) as well an
allowed path
p_{2}
from level
k
to level
k_{2}
(that stays at levels
\geq k
, too, and
goes
n_{2}
units into the
x
-direction). Then we certainly have

F_{k_{1},k_{2}}^{[\geq k]}(x)=w(p_{1})x^{n_{1}}F_{k,k}^{[\geq k]}(x)w(p_{2})x^{n_{2}}+r(x)

for some power series
r(x)
with nonnegative coefficients. We just observe that all allowed paths
from level
k_{1}
to level
k_{2}
that stay at levels
\geq k
contain the concatenation of

p_{1}
, the paths corresponding to
F_{k,k}^{[\geq k]}(x)
, and
p_{2}
.
Thus,
F_{k,k}^{[\geq k]}(x)
is dominated by
F_{k_{1},k_{2}}^{[\geq k]}(x)
.

Similarly, we can concatenate
p_{2}
, the paths corresponding to
F_{k_{1},k_{2}}^{[\geq k]}(x)
, and
p_{1}

and obtain a part of all paths corresponding to
F_{k,k}^{[\geq k]}(x)
. In other terms we have

F_{k,k}^{[\geq k]}(x)=w(p_{2})x^{n_{2}}F_{k_{1},k_{2}}^{[\geq k]}(x)w(p_{1})x^{n_{1}}+\overline{r}(x)

with a power series
\overline{r}(x)
with nonnegative coefficients.
Hence,
F_{k_{1},k_{2}}^{[\geq k]}(x)
is dominated by
F_{k,k}^{[\geq k]}(x)
.

Putting these two observations together it follows that
F_{k_{1},k_{2}}^{[\geq k]}(x)
and
F_{k,k}^{[\geq k]}(x)

have the same kind of dominating (square-root) singularity.

Finally, by applying the relations from Lemma
2
it follows that
the prime walk functions
A_{k,i}(x)
,
\overline{A}_{k,i}(x)
are either polynomials in
x

(with nonnegative coefficients) or polynomials (again with nonnegative coefficients) in

x
and finitely many functions
F_{k_{1},k_{2}}^{[\geq k]}(x)
. Thus, the
functions
A_{k,i}(x)
,
\overline{A}_{k,i}(x)
are either polynomials or have
the same dominating (square-root) singularity as
F_{0,0}^{[\geq 0]}(x)
.
∎

4.
Several catalytic equations

We suppose now that we have a system of linear catalytic equations of the form (
1.4
).
Similarly to the above procedure, we translate these equations into a corresponding lattice-path problem.

Again, we start with a simplified version, where we assume that

P_{s}(x,u)=1
:

(4.1)

\displaystyle F_{s}(x,u)

\displaystyle=1+x\sum_{t=1}^{d}\sum_{\ell=0}^{L}Q_{s,t,\ell}(x,u)\Delta^{\ell}F_{t}(x,u),\qquad(1\leq s\leq d)

(4.2)

\displaystyle=1+x\sum_{t=1}^{d}\sum_{\ell=0}^{L}\sum_{j=0}^{J}Q_{s,t,\ell,j}(x)u^{j}\Delta^{\ell}F_{t}(x,u)

Clearly this system translates into an infinite system (
1\leq s\leq d
,
k\geq 0
):

(4.3)

F_{s;k,0}(x)=\delta_{k,0}+x\sum_{t=1}^{d}\sum_{\ell=0}^{L}\sum_{m=0}^{\min(J,k)}Q_{s,t,\ell,j}(x)F_{t;k+\ell-j,0}(x),\qquad(1\leq s\leq d,\,k\geq 0).

In contract to the case of one equation we have to distinguish between

d
types (or “colors”) for lattice paths. In particular we have
d^{2}
sets
of step multi-sets
\mathcal{S}_{s,t,k}
,
1\leq i,j\leq d
,
0\leq k\leq J
, that are defined in the following way:

(4.4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{s,t,k}

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{\ell=0}^{L}\bigcup_{j=0}^{\min(k,J)}\{(1+r,\ell-j):0\leq r\leq{\rm deg}Q_{s,t,\ell,j},[x^{r}]Q_{\ell,j}(x)\neq 0\}\qquad(0\leq k\leq J)

Observe that the multi-sets
\mathcal{S}_{s,t,k}
,
1\leq s,t,\leq d
, might contain
a specific step several times, that is, they have to be labeled accordingly.
In order to simplify the notation we do not make this labels explicit.

Furthermore we define weights
w
of a step
(1+r,\ell-j)\in\mathcal{S}_{s,t,k}
by

\displaystyle w(1+r,\ell-j)

\displaystyle=[x^{r}]Q_{s,t,\ell,j}(x).

We now say that a path
p=s_{1}\cdots s_{M}
(of at least one step) that starts at level
k

is of type
s
and is allowed if the first step
s_{1}
is contained in
\mathcal{S}_{s,t_{0}}
for some
t_{0}
, and if consecutive steps are consistent in the following way:
after the first step
s_{1}
that is contained in
S_{s,t_{0},k}
for some
t_{0}
and
connects level
k
to level
k_{1}
we shall have
s_{2}\in S_{t_{0},t_{1},k_{1}}
for some
t_{1}

and some
k_{1}
that connects level
k_{1}
to level
k_{2}
,

s_{3}\in S_{t_{1},t_{2},k_{2}}
for some
t_{2}
and some
k_{2}
etc.
And clearly the weight
w(p)
of an allowed path
p=s_{1}\cdots s_{M}
is defined by

w(p)=\prod_{m=1}^{M}w(s_{m}).

For the empty walk with zero steps, that is of every type, we set (as usual)
w(\emptyset)=1
.

As in the case of a single catalytic equation, we now define as
\mathcal{P}_{s;n,k}
the multi-set
of allowed paths of type
s
from
(0,0)
to
(n,k)
that stay above the
x
-axis.
(By convention, the empty path is contained in
\mathcal{P}_{s;0,0}
for
1\leq s\leq d
.)
Furthermore, if we set

F_{s;k,0}(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{s;n,k}}w(p)x^{n}\qquad(1\leq s\leq d,\,k\geq 0)

then it is immediate that these functions satisfy the infinite system (
4.3
).
We just have to split the paths after the first step.

Finally, we show (very similarly to the case of one catalytic equation) that there is also a prime walk decomposition. For this purpose, we define
prime walks
\mathcal{A}_{s,t;k,i,n}
of type
(s,t)
,
1\leq s,t\leq d
, as
all paths from
(0,k)
to
(n,k+i)
, where the first step is in
S_{s,t_{0},k}
for some
t_{0}
, where
the last step is in
S_{s_{0},t,k_{1}}
for some
s_{1}
and
k_{1}>k+i
and connects
level
k_{1}
with level
k
, and where the path stays above the level
k

in between. The corresponding generating functions are defined by

A_{s,t;k,i}(x)=\sum_{n>0}\sum_{p\in\mathcal{A}_{s,t;k,i,n}}w(p)x^{n}.

Similarly, we define reverse prime walks
\overline{\mathcal{A}}_{s,t;k,i,n}
that connect
(0,k)

to
(n,k-i)
and their generating functions
\overline{A}_{s,t;k,i}(x)
.

We set

\displaystyle\overline{d}_{s,t,k}

\displaystyle=\max\{\ell-j:0\leq\ell\leq L,\ 0\leq j\leq k,\ Q_{s,t,\ell,j}(x)\neq 0\},\qquad(k\leq J),

and

\displaystyle\underline{d}_{s,t,k}

\displaystyle=\max\{j-\ell:0\leq\ell\leq L,\ 0\leq j\leq k,\ Q_{s,t\ell,j}(x)\neq 0\}\qquad(k\leq J).

Then we certainly have

\displaystyle A_{s,t;k,i}(x)

\displaystyle=0\qquad\mbox{for $i>\overline{d}_{s,t,k}$ and $0\leq k<J$},

(4.5)

\displaystyle A_{s,t;k,i}(x)

\displaystyle=0\qquad\mbox{for $i>\overline{d}_{s,t,J}$ and $k\geq J$},

\displaystyle\overline{A}_{s,t;k,i}(x)

\displaystyle=0\qquad\mbox{for $i>\underline{d}_{s,t,k}$ and $0\leq k<J$},

\displaystyle\overline{A}_{s,t;k,i}(x)

\displaystyle=0\qquad\mbox{for $i>\underline{d}_{s,t,J}$ and $k\geq J$}.

Furthermore we have

\displaystyle A_{s,t,k,\overline{d}_{k}}(x)

\displaystyle=x\sum_{\ell-j=\overline{d}_{k}}Q_{s,t,\ell,j}(x)\qquad(0\leq k<J),

(4.6)

\displaystyle A_{s,t;k,\overline{d}_{J}}(x)

\displaystyle=x\sum_{\ell-j=\overline{d}_{J}}Q_{s,t,\ell,j}(x)\qquad(k\geq J),

\displaystyle\overline{A}_{s,t;k,\underline{d}_{k}}(x)

\displaystyle=x\sum_{\ell-j=-\underline{d}_{k}}Q_{s,t,\ell,j}(x)\qquad(0\leq k<J),

\displaystyle\overline{A}_{s,t;k,\underline{d}_{J}}(x)

\displaystyle=x\sum_{\ell-j=-\underline{d}_{J}}Q_{s,t,\ell,j}(x)\qquad(k\geq J).

We consider now a slightly more refined counting problem of lattice paths.
We define as
\mathcal{P}_{s,t;n,k_{1},k_{2}}^{[\geq k]}
the set
of allowed paths from
(0,k_{1})
to
(n,k_{2})
that stay at level
\geq k
,
where the first step is in
S_{s,t_{0},k_{1}}
for some
t_{0}
and where
the last step is in
S_{s_{0},t,k^{\prime}}
for some
s_{0}
and some
k^{\prime}
that connects
level
k^{\prime}
and level
k_{2}
.
The corresponding generating functions are

F_{s,t;k_{1},k_{2}}^{[\geq k]}(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{s,t;n,k_{1},k_{2}}^{[\geq k]}}w(p)\,x^{n}\qquad(1\leq s,t,\leq d,\,k_{1},k_{2}\geq 0,\,k\geq\min(k_{1},k_{2})),

and as in Lemma
2
we have

\displaystyle F_{s,t;k_{1},k_{1}}^{[\geq k]}(x)

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{t_{0}=1}^{d}\sum_{m=k}^{k_{1}}\overline{A}_{s,t_{0};k_{1},k_{1}-m}(x)F_{t_{0},t;m,k_{1}}^{[\geq k]}(x)\qquad(k\leq k_{1}),

\displaystyle F_{s,t;k_{1},k_{2}}^{[\geq k]}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t_{0}=1}^{d}\sum_{m=k}^{k_{1}}\overline{A}_{s,t_{0};k_{1},k_{1}-m}(x)F_{t_{0},t;m,k_{2}}^{[\geq k]}(x)+\sum_{t_{0}=1}^{d}\sum_{m=k_{1}+1}^{k_{2}}A_{s,t_{0};k_{1},m-k_{1}}(x)F_{t_{0},t;m,k_{2}}^{[\geq m]}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t_{0}=1}^{d}\sum_{m=k_{1}}^{k_{2}}F_{s,t_{0};k_{1},m}^{[\geq k]}(x)A_{t_{0},t;m,k_{2}-m}(x)\qquad(k\leq k_{1}<k_{2}),

\displaystyle F_{s,t;k_{1},k_{2}}^{[\geq k]}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t_{0}=1}^{d}\sum_{m=k}^{k_{1}}\overline{A}_{s,t_{0};k_{1},k_{1}-m}(x)F_{t_{0},t;m,k_{2}}^{[\geq k]}(x)\qquad(k\leq k_{2}<k_{1}),

and

\displaystyle A_{s,t;k,i}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{0\leq\ell\leq L,0\leq j\leq k\,:\,\ell-j=i}xQ_{s,t,\ell,j}(x)

\displaystyle+\sum_{t_{0}=1}^{d}\sum_{t_{1}=1}^{d}\sum_{i_{1}>i,\,i_{2}>i}\ \sum_{0\leq\ell_{1}\leq L,0\leq j_{1}\leq k\,:\,\ell_{1}-j_{1}=i_{1}}xQ_{s,t_{0}\ell_{1},j_{1}}(x)\ F_{t_{0},t_{1};k+i_{1},k+i_{2}}^{[\geq k+i+1]}(x)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\sum_{0\leq\ell_{2}\leq L,0\leq j_{2}\leq\min(k+i_{2},J)\,:\,\ell_{2}-j_{2}=-(i_{2}-i)}xQ_{t_{1},t,\ell_{2},j_{2}}(x)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad(0\leq k<J,\ 0\leq i\leq\overline{d}_{s,t,k}),

\displaystyle A_{s,t,k,i}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{0\leq\ell\leq L,0\leq j\leq J\,:\,\ell-j=i}xQ_{s,t,\ell,j}(x)

\displaystyle+\sum_{t_{0}=1}^{d}\sum_{t_{1}=1}^{d}\sum_{i_{1}>i,\,i_{2}>i}\ \sum_{0\leq\ell_{1}\leq L,0\leq j_{1}\leq J\,:\,\ell_{1}-j_{1}=i_{1}}xQ_{s,t_{0}\ell_{1},j_{1}}(x)\ F_{t_{0},t_{1};k+i_{1},k+i_{2}}^{[\geq k+i+1]}(x)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\sum_{0\leq\ell_{2}\leq L,0\leq j_{2}\leq J\,:\,\ell_{2}-j_{2}=-(i_{2}-i)}xQ_{t_{1},t\ell_{2},j_{2}}(x),

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad(k\geq J,\ 0\leq i\leq\overline{d}_{s,t,J}),

as well as

\displaystyle\overline{A}_{s,t;k,i}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{0\leq\ell\leq L,0\leq j\leq k\,:\,\ell-j=-i}xQ_{s,t,\ell,j}(x)

\displaystyle+\sum_{t_{0}=1}^{d}\sum_{t_{1}=1}^{d}\sum_{i_{1}>0,\,i_{2}>0}\ \sum_{0\leq\ell_{1}\leq L,0\leq j_{1}\leq k\,:\,\ell_{1}-j_{1}=i_{1}}xQ_{s,t_{0}\ell_{1},j_{1}}(x)\ F_{t_{0},t_{1};k+i_{1},k+i_{2}}^{[\geq k+1]}(x)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\sum_{0\leq\ell_{2}\leq L,0\leq j_{2}\leq\min(k+i_{2},J)\,:\,\ell_{2}-j_{2}=-(i+i_{2})}xQ_{t_{1},t,\ell_{2},j_{2}}(x),

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad(0\leq k<J,\ 0\leq i\leq\underline{d}_{s,t,k}),

\displaystyle\overline{A}_{s,t;k,i}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{0\leq\ell\leq L,0\leq j\leq J\,:\,\ell-j=-i}xQ_{s,t,\ell,j}(x)

\displaystyle+\sum_{t_{0}=1}^{d}\sum_{t_{1}=1}^{d}\sum_{i_{1}>0,\,i_{2}>0}\ \sum_{0\leq\ell_{1}\leq L,0\leq j_{1}\leq J\,:\,\ell_{1}-j_{1}=i_{1}}xQ_{s,t_{0},\ell_{1},j_{1}}(x)\ F_{t_{0},t_{1};k+i_{1},k+i_{2}}^{[\geq k+1]}(x)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\sum_{0\leq\ell_{2}\leq L,0\leq j_{2}\leq J\,:\,\ell_{2}-j_{2}=-(i+i_{2})}xQ_{t_{0},t,\ell_{2},j_{2}}(x),

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad(k\geq J,\ 0\leq i\leq\underline{d}_{s,t,J}).

This provides again an infinite system of equations for the
generating function
F_{s,t;k_{1},k_{2}}^{[\geq k]}(x)
,
A_{s,t,k,i}(x)
, and
\overline{A}_{s,t,k,i}(x)
.
As in the case of a single equation we can consider a finite subsystem for the function

\displaystyle F_{s,t;k_{1},k_{2}}^{[\geq k]}(x)

\displaystyle:\quad 1\leq s,t,\leq d,\ 0\leq k_{1}<L+J,\ 0\leq k_{2}<2J,\ 0\leq k\leq\min(J,k_{1},k_{2}),

\displaystyle A_{s,t;k,i}(x)

\displaystyle:\quad 1\leq s,t,\leq d,\ 0\leq k\leq J,\ 0\leq i<\overline{d}_{s,t,k},

\displaystyle\overline{A}_{s,t;k,i}(x)

\displaystyle:\quad 1\leq s,t,\leq d,\ 0\leq k\leq J,\ 0\leq i<\underline{d}_{s,t,k},

that can be independently solved. Again, all other functions can be expressed
in a positive polynomial way with these functions (compare Lemma
3
).
This completes the proof of Theorem
1
.

For the proof of Theorem
2
we apply a similar procedure as described in

. The modifications are (again) obvious.

Note that we use the assumption that for every pair
(s,t)\in\{1,\ldots,d\}^{2}

we can reach from every level
k_{1}
every level
k_{2}
by an allowed lattice path
of type
(s,t)
that stays
\geq k
, where
k\geq\min(k_{1},k_{2})
.
This is equivalent to the statement that the (infinite) system of equations
(
4.3
) is strongly connected.
