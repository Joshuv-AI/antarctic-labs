Access to healthcare amongst adolescent girls and young women in Ugandan artisanal and small-scale mining communities
Authors: Hanna Chidwick, Sabahat Balaban, Betty Kwagala, David Owiredu, Lydia Kapiriri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundArtisanal small scale-mining (ASM) provides work to many adolescent girls and young women (AGYW) in Uganda. AGYW working in ASM face a unique set of health challenges such as exposure to mercury, HIV and sexually transmitted infections. AGYW in ASM are more vulnerable since they work and live in remote areas with limited health infrastructure which may limit their access to care. However, the AGYW’s access to care, including SRH care, is not well understood or documented. This paper aims to explore the current healthcare access challenges and bottlenecks amongst Ugandan AGYW working in ASM communities.MethodsThe findings are part of a large study which was conducted in Busia, Mubende, and Namayingo communities, from three mining regions in Uganda. While the large study employed a mixed methods approach involving a survey with AGYW and qualitative data (in depth interviews, focus group discussions with adolescent boys, older women, and AGYW, key informant interviews with local leaders and policymakers). Data were analyzed deductively based on the Tanahashi Model on access to care.ResultsOverall, AGYW working in ASM communities expressed interest in using the health care facilities to treat their illnesses and for family planning services. However, with the exception of a few, they reported various challenges they face in accessing healthcare. All participants discussed challenges such as lack of personnel at facilities, lack of youth friendly health services, limited resources, distance to facilities, cost of care, and negative provider attitudes especially towards sexual and reproductive health care for unmarried AGYW.ConclusionsDespite some availability, accessibility, acceptability, and contact coverage, the gaps in AGYW’s access to healthcare highlight a critical need to avail and strengthen health systems and especially sexual and reproductive health care in ASM communities in Uganda. This will contribute to achieving UHC and ultimately health equity. Mitigating the inequities in accessing care amongst ASM AGYW involves processes such as formalization, advocacy and rights-based accountability, and leveraging existing initiatives proposed in national Ugandan health policies.

Background

Artisanal Small-scale Mining (ASM) is a form of low-technolgy, labour-intensive mineral processing commonly operating outside of legal regulations to extract minerals such as gold [1–3]. These informal practices are usually conducted using hand tools by individuals or small groups [1, 4]. In Uganda it is estimated that ASM revenue generation is 5% of the Gross Domestic Product (GDP) [4, 5]. The sector, which is primarily located in Buhweju, Amudat, Moroto, Busia, Namayingo, Kassanda, and Kisoro. The ASM sector employs about 400,000– 600,000 women and men of whom 31,600 are involved in manual gold extraction. However, the sector supports an additional estimated 2 million people who work in allied and support services [1, 4]. ASM has been particularly important for women and children as the sector provides a source of income to these vulnerable groups. In fact, 40–50% of miners working in ASM mines in Africa are women [6]. Furthermore, approximately 2 million children and adolescents aged 5 to 17 years are involved in child labour in Uganda and about 51% of these children work in exploitative and hazardous work environments such as mines [7].

Adolescents working in the artisanal and small-scale mining (ASM) sector in Uganda face a unique set of inequities which impact their health, including exposure to mercury, physical remoteness, limited health infrastructure, lack of occupational health standards, and unequal cultural norms [1, 5, 6, 8–10]. Despite these inequities and challenges, the ASM sector is a common workplace for many adolescents, including adolescent girls and young women (AGYW) in Uganda. Cultural gender norms make AGYW especially more vulnerable to economic and sexual exploitation- which increases their risk to sexually transmitted diseases.

The literature discussed the pros and cons of ASM. Some scholars support the sector as they argue that it contributes to the social and economic development of people living in impoverished conditions, by providing employment and means of livelihood for many [1]. However, other scholars highlight the adverse environmental, health, and safety concerns that arise due to the unlicensed, unregulated, and, in most cases, illegal operations of these mines [1, 2]. For example, in gold ASM in Uganda, artisanal miners are often exposed to is mercury through the processing of gold which results in various health challenges and disease [4, 6, 8, 11]. In addition to direct exposure to toxins and work hazards, adolescents in particular also experience heightened inequities and thus vulnerability in ASM communities leading to health challenges such as early sexual debut, early and unintended pregnancy, high HIV transmission, and sexual transmitted infections [12, 13]. Similar to the literature, there are both positive and negative impacts of ASM in relation to the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs). For example, ASM impacts both SDG 3 (Good Health and Well-being) and SDG 8 (Decent Work and Economic Growth) through providing a source of income to access healthcare and contrastingly, contributing to the threat of disease and limited access to care [5, 8, 10, 14]. There are poor occupational health safeguards and health concerns that are significantly more prevalent in ASM due to informality and lack of regulation in these communities [5, 6]. While formalization may have alleviated these challenges, licensing complexities, costs, a bias toward large-scale mining companies, and lack of political will have made this unattainable [15–18]. Limited capital, government distrust and difficulties in monitoring small-scale mining affect ASM in Uganda [17, 19].

The lack of formalization has also meant that critical problems such as child labour and gender inequality to flourish in ASM [20, 21], whereby access to quality education (SDG 4), gender equality (SDG 5), and access to decent work (SDG 8) are undermined [22, 23]. Gender equality and access to decent work are exemplified in that while men typically engage in ‘masculine’ tasks, e.g. digging, which tend to be more physical, they are lucrative and well paid [24]. Women, on the other hand, engage in tasks such as cleaning, and cooking) which are deemed less valuable, and hence, poorly paid. This, in addition to the gender roles, contributes to male dominance in the ASM sector and the marginalization of women, which negatively impacts their health and wellbeing [24, 25].

Responding to the above issues and SDG 5, a 2023 World Bank global report on the” State of the Artisanal and Small-Scale Mining Sector”. The report (based on primary survey data with over 1,900 participants from government, women in mining groups, and miners themselves, and a review of mining and legal frameworks from 21 countries), shades light on the status of women in ASM including their health and access to services. This global report talks about the limited availability of maternal health and sexual health and reproductive care in or close to mining communities and the high risk of child marriages for girls in ASM communities. While there has been progress in governments’ recognition of the important role played by women and increasing ASM women’s participation in decision making, they also note a lack of gender-sensitive language in national laws, and limited mechanisms for reporting gender-based violence which is prevalent in ASM [26]. Gender-blind policy interventions such as a lack of prioritizing women’s sanitation and hygiene infrastructure at mine sites, childcare provisions, or lighting to improve women’s safety at work hamper the progress towards gender equity in ASM [26].

Not addressing people’s health in ASM communities, limits achievements towards SDGs and perpetuates health inequities [5, 8]. Addressing the health of adolescent populations, especially AGYW, in these communities is of particular concern, as AGYW are often more vulnerable and susceptible to several different health conditions.

In Uganda, healthcare is decentralized whereby districts are in charge of healthcare delivery and are overseen by the Ministry of Health [27, 28]. Private and non-governmental organizations (NGOs), religious groups, community medicine distributors, traditional healers, and drug shops also provide services [29, 30]. Health services range from health prevention, promotion, education, testing, and reproductive care to complex services such as surgery, and consist of several levels of care – primary level care consists of Health Centres II (HCII), Health Centres III (HCIII), Health Centres IV (HC IV), and lower units Village Health Teams (VHT); secondary level consists of a network of district and rural hospitals; tertiary level includes all Referral Hospitals located in regional capitals; and, quaternary or the highest level care includes four national hospitals in Mulago, Kawempe, Kiruddu, and Butabiika [30, 31].

Currently, Uganda has made a commitment to Universal Health Coverage (UHC) delivered through the Uganda National Minimum Health Care Package (UNMHCP) which includes childhood immunization, health promotion and education, treatment, and control of common infectious diseases like HIV and tuberculosis, as well as control of communicable and non-communicable diseases [32]. Despite this commitment to UHC, most recent data show that the number of individuals within a five-kilometer distance of a healthcare facility who can access a facility is low resulting in distance and transportation as two key challenges in access healthcare [32, 33]. According to the latest data from the Uganda Demographic Health Survey, 41% of women aged 15–49 years across the country have problems accessing healthcare due to the long distances to health facilities [32]. In addition, access to care challenges in Uganda include inadequate care, dysfunctional facilities, limited infrastructure and resources, and lack of staff to provide care [32]. Amongst adolescents and AGYW in particular, access to care is generally discussed in terms of SRH care specifically [34–38]. Barriers to accessing care include lack of privacy, limited youth-friendly services, stigma, and limited resources/testing for SRH [37].

AGYW in ASM are more vulnerable and less likely to access care given their remoteness and work in the informal sector [26], yet their access to care, including SRH care, is not well understood or documented making it difficult to address despite Uganda’s commitment to UHC. Using the Tanahashi Model on access to care [39], this paper aims to explore and outline the current health access challenges and bottlenecks amongst Ugandan AGYW working in ASM communities.

Methods

As part of a larger mixed-methods study on health and economic resilience amongst AGYW in ASM communities [40], this paper used both quantitative and qualitative analysis of survey responses, focus group discussions, and in-depth interviews from AGYW in Uganda (Busia, Namayingo, Kassanda, Mubende). For comprehensive understanding of the AGYW’s experiences, the study also included perspectives from older women, adolescent boys, and key informants (i.e., community leaders, policymakers, and government officials) in the study communities to gather a wide perspective of the AGYW experiences in accessing healthcare. Data collection was led by BK and involved trained young women as research assistants. Both were knowledgeable of the study context but had no relationship with the participants.

The study was approved by the research ethics committees in three countries – Uganda (The AIDS Support Organization-2022-169 and registered by the Uganda National Council for Science and Technology, UNCST – SS149ES); Ghana, (University of Ghana Ethics Committee for the Humanities (ECH109/22-23)); and, Canada (McMaster University Research Ethics Board, MREB #6257). All participation was voluntary, and all participants provided informed consent.

Data collection

The survey involved only AGYW while the qualitative study, in addition to AGYW involved young men, opinion leaders, policy makers and older women in ASM communities. Respondents who were knowledgeable of the AGYW’s experiences were identified with the support of community facilitators. A community information meeting, where the researchers informed community members about the study. After which, potential respondents were individually requested to participate. Those who agreed to participate were interviewed face to face, in the respective respondents’ contexts. Data were collected between March- August 2023.

The quantitative survey included 809 AGYW in Uganda. We used the Yamane’s [41] formula to determine the sample size, taking into consideration the design effect of 2 and anticipated response rate of 98%. We included young women aged 20–24 because most adolescent young women involved in ASM communities were within that range and had started work as younger adolescents. AGYW were eligible to participate if they either directly worked in the mines or provided services to ASM workers (i.e., food provision etc.). Data was collected through the Kobo Collect tool using a pre-tested survey questionnaire that was translated into the relevant local languages. Questions asked AGYW about their experience accessing healthcare services, where they access these services and for what health challenges.

Qualitative data collection was completed through in-depth interviews with AGYW, focus groups discussions with AGYW, adolescent boys, and older women, and key informant interviews with leaders and policymakers. A pilot tested interview guide was used with flexibility such that any emerging themes were followed up. Relevant questions focused on AGYW’s experiences accessing healthcare such as; what do AGYW do when ill? What kind of healthcare services do AGYW commonly use? Why do they use these services? where this was provided? What are the AGYW’s experiences with the services?

Interviews were conducted in a convenient place identified by the respondent. Interviews were audio-recorded with permission from the participants. RAs also kept field notes.

In depth interviews with AGYW

We analyzed 8/10 in depth interviews with AGYW (2 in Busia, 3 in Kassanda, 2 in Mubende, and 1 in Namayingo). Trained adolescent peer research assistants conducted interviews who were purposively selected to include a variety of AGYW including those that were pregnant, have children, single, and married. These interviews ranged between 25–30 minutes and aimed to gather in-depth sensitive information on AGYW’s experiences and opinions that would not have been possible to collect in a group context. Interviews at community level were conducted in a private place and in the relevant local languages.

Key informant interviews

Key informant interviews were conducted at national and subnational levels. We conduced a total of 27 key informant interviews 4 from the national level, 9 in Mubende, 6 in Busia, 8 in Namayingo. Participants were purposefully selected from relevant ministries, national level NGOs and multilateral organizations. At subnational levels, participants included relevant district officers, NGO representatives, ASM association leaders, health workers and local leaders. Key informant interviews which ranged between 30–45 minutes, provided perspectives on adolescents’ health access experiences.

Focus group discussions

We conducted 8 focus group discussions (FGD) with AGYW (one each in Busia, Namayingo, and Mubende), older women (one each in Namayingo, Busia, and Mubende), and adolescent boys (one each in Namayingo and Mubende). Each FGD lasted a duration of 60- 90 minutes. These discussions were to gather wider community perspectives on AGYW’s experiences accessing healthcare. Most of the adolescent boys who participated worked in the mines and made their main sources of income from gold extraction. AGYW participants largely worked in ore processing and provision of services in mining communities (i.e., food, salons etc.).

The initial interpretation of the findings was shared with the study participants during a community meeting.

Analysis

The analysis was guided by Tanahashi’s Model [39] which is used to identify bottlenecks in health service coverage. Health service coverage is defined as the proportion of the target population that can receive or have received the service [39]. The model is a well-established and has been used and found to be useful in similar contexts [42, 43], and by the World Health Organization to guide the assessment of effective coverage on the road to universal health coverage [44]. It also provides a comprehensive framework to understand complex aspects of coverage at a community level, and an opportunity to consider these aspects in terms of equity. Quantitative survey responses were synthesized and analyzed descriptively using Stata statistical software version 15. Qualitative data were transcribed verbatim, translated into English when necessary. HC & SB manually conducted the thematic analysis. This involved inductive line-by-line coding of transcripts, which involved identifying and coding portions of the scripts which talked about any aspect of access to healthcare such as availability of health services, types of services available, barriers to access etc. and allocating them a label. The codes were clustered emerging themes. Both qualitative and quantitative data were then further analyzed and synthesized based on the identified aspects of coverage identified in the Tanahashi access to care model (see Table 1) [39, 42]. Based on the data collected, we analyzed availability, accessibility, acceptability, and contact coverage but did not analyze effectiveness of coverage as it was out of the scope of this study.

Results

The results section is organized according to the coverage aspects in Tanahashi’s model. The findings are reported according to the study sites. Any similarities or differences, between responses according to data sources and type of respondent, are highlighted.

Availability coverage

According to the model, availability refers to resources such as facilities, drugs and personnel that support health service provision. Study participants discussed varying perspectives on the availability of health care and specifically youth friendly health services in their communities.

According to the survey results, an average of 53% of the sampled AGYW from the three study communities indicated that adolescent friendly services are available at health centres. This finding was consistent with the qualitative results whereby depending on their location, some participants decried the challenges faced by AGYW in accessing health care services generally and especially youth friendly health services due to limited availability; while others reported that youth friendly health services were available.

Accessibility coverage

This aspect refers to the degree to which services are physically and financially accessible (also referred to as affordability). Both physical and financial access to care was discussed by participants.

Physical access

All study participants from the three locations discussed the difficulties faced by the AGYW and other populations in physically accessing the available services. As discussed earlier, populations in ASM communities face physical barriers in accessing health care since most of the services are located outside the mining communities. This was a source of concern since respondents emphasized the importance of closer health facilities because of the high exposure to occupational and environmental hazards AGYW experience while working in the mines.

For example, respondents from Busia and Mubende discussed the lack of health facilities within their vicinity and the poor quality of those within their vicinity. Acceptability coverage

Acceptability coverage explains the degree to which those people who access the services find them to be of high quality and to be culturally, financially and quality wise appropriate enough for them to use the services.

There were variations in the responses with regards to this aspect with some respondents- even those in the same community giving varying responses. For example, while one adolescent reported that services are mostly used by older women,”“Youth-friendly services are provided in the clinics and at the health center though are mostly used by older women.” (Adolescent girl − 3, Mubende), another adolescent from the same community reported that, “… .Youth-friendly services are very accessible and very easy to get and it is no shame because you get used to it. (Adolescent girl − 4, Mubende)

“Youth-friendly services are provided in the clinics and at the health center though are mostly used by older women.” (Adolescent girl − 3, Mubende), another adolescent from the same community reported that, “… .Youth-friendly services are very accessible and very easy to get and it is no shame because you get used to it. (Adolescent girl − 4, Mubende)

These quotes illustrate that except those who have overcome the “shame” associated with SRH, most youths are not using the available services.

Some of the responses shed more light on some of the factors which impact ASM AGYW’s acceptability of the youth friendly health services. Some of the respondents indicated reservations with accepting healthcare services Uganda due to previous negative experiences with providers holding religious reservations and fear of side effects. Many participants discussed lack of acceptable youth-friendly, SRH care, negative provider attitudes, limited SRH care counselling and information For example, one key informant from Busia narrated a story where a young girl had negative experiences with a religious provider;“… There is a girl who said she went to a health facility, and when she started explaining, the nurse started casting the demons away from her, and the girl was confused, so such a girl, when she starts feeling issues, she will not go back(to the health facility) …” (Key informant − 5, Busia). In addition to staff members’ religious affiliations, respondents also noted problematic and negative staff attitudes which may be a reflection of the cultural expectations which stigmatize adolescents involved in sexual activities within these communities. There was also a lack of trust and the perception that health workers’ lack of reliable information.

A few respondents who talked about family planning specifically had varying perspectives with some alluding to a fear of the potential side effects; “… The reason I didn’t seek some of these services especially family planning services was because of fear of its side effects like over bleeding, the last time I tried using injectable I almost died.” (Adolescent mother − 7, Busia); while other AGYW noted that they embrace SRH care in particular including HIV/AIDS counselling, STI testing, and family planning/contraceptives indicating some acceptability coverage. Injectable and oral contraceptives were commonly referred to in interviews with AGYW.Some young women especially use short term methods like injectables … which last for three months, [they also use] oral contraceptives. Contact coverage

This aspect of the framework refers to those people who have utilized the services.

This aspect has been touched in the above sections. There were varying reports with regards to AGYW who actually use the various services including those who brave the government health units, despite their limitations, and those who use the accessible private clinics despite the costs.

Specifically, focus group participants in Namayingo noted that young people do seek and use healthcare services as expressed by the older women from Namayingo, “… Yes they [young people] go [to seek health services] even the government health facility is in Nsango.”;“Young people/youth make the effort to seek health care services.” (Older woman − 1 & 2, Namayingo). However, when we asked married AGYW the extent to which workers in mines in Namayingo use public healthcare services, one participant estimated that approximately 10% use services. An estimate which may be indicative of the perceived underutilization of the healthcare system in this ASM community.

Similarly, in Busia, adolescent mothers noted use of health facilities including private clinics and different levels of health centres. The limited use of healthcare services was also exemplified by the AGYW survey respondents when asked specifically what they did when they experienced suspected symptoms of COVID-19 sickness including cough and fever. Of the 33% of participants that indicated they had experienced cough or fever during the pandemic, 40% of participants said that they sought care at a health facility and 17% said they obtained medications from a drug shop or pharmacy. The rest of participants said they used either herbal or other treatment methods outside of facilities. This indicates that when sick, only about half (57%) of the AGYW access care at health facilities or drug shops.

Discussion

Exploring the experiences shared by AGYW living and working in ASM communities enabled us to highlight the inequitable challenges they experience while accessing healthcare and report on what is currently available to the community. Results indicate that despite some availability, accessibility, acceptability, and contact coverage of the health care services, there are significant gaps in these accessibility aspects within the study communities which need to be addressed in order to ensure equitable access to healthcare services and especially SRH care for AGYW in ASM communities in Uganda.

In regard to availability of coverage, results show that health centers, drugs, and staffing are only somewhat available depending on the ASM community and type of facility (i.e., public or private). In Busoga and Bukedi regions which host Namayingo and Busia study sites respectively, 2016 DHS data shows that only 27% to 32% of reproductive age women (15–49 years) have their family planning needs satisfied with modern contraceptives [45]. However, in 2024, amongst women of reproductive age in Uganda overall, about 64% had their family planning needs satisfied with modern contraceptives, indicating regional inequities in availability and access to care [46]. Existing literature further discusses the challenges with availability of care amongst last mile populations including adolescent refugees and migrants in Uganda including limited facilities and resources, distance to facilities, and lack of youth-friendly services showing that the inequities in accessing care are similar to those amongst last-mile AGYW [47–49].

Interestingly, there were contradictions between responses from key informants and AGYW specifically in terms of availability of youth friendly health services. For example, in Busia, key informants explained that youth friendly health services are non-existent whereas AGYW in Busia and Mubende described youth friendly health services as available at certain facilities. Similar to the results on availability coverage, accessibility coverage was discussed in contradictory ways. Respondents discussed both the physical and financial barriers to services and in contrast, the accessibility of facilities such as private clinics. These contradictions could be the result of a lack of information on what youth friendly health services technically entail including access to counselling, quality and stigma-free treatment, and family planning commodities [46–48]. AGYW who indicated youth friendly health services are available could be referring to Village Health Teams or services from private (for profit) sources that distribute or provide family planning commodities, but do not provide the full scope of counselling and stigma-free treatment. There also could be variations in services provided due to funding variations for youth friendly health services in different communities.

In terms of acceptability coverage, study results show that SRH care including family planning services are not always acceptable amongst AGYW due to stigma, lack of resources and availability of quality information. Various participants including key informants, adolescents, and older women in Busia and Mubende in particular, shared a lack of trust in public healthcare services especially in regard to SRH. These findings align with the current literature about SRH care in Uganda that discusses a lack of capacity and training of service providers to provide youth friendly care, and ongoing judgement and discriminatory attitudes from care providers [36, 38, 50]. However, Uganda has two national policies that emphasize the importance of youth friendly SRH care under the UHC umbrella [49, 51]. These policies emphasize the need for greater access to SRH care, minimizing stigma and providing SRH information and counselling especially for adolescents [49, 51]. These national policies create a strong basis for strengthening youth friendly SRH services through a coordinated, multi-faceted approach which is needed to improve access to SRH services as part of UHC for last-mile populations [50].

The contradictions with care availability and limited acceptability of care, also indicate a lack of quality of care where despite some services being available, the quality of those services is limited. Quality of healthcare has been discussed in terms of various aspects including timeliness, effectiveness, safety, patient-centred goals, resource availability, and training of personnel [52]. For example, in a study found that in Busoga and Bukedi regions which include Busia and Namayingo respectively, 53% of women indicated that when in contact with health personnel or at a health facility, they have not discussed family planning options [45]. Consistent with or study some limitations to quality of SRH information and subsequent care (which could be a result of high patient to provider ratios and limited time to provide adequate family planning services and counselling) were discussed. Previous reports on quality of care in the Karamoja region outline a lack of integrated SRH services [53] which is aligned with findings that the quality of SRH care in ASM communities is limited due to lack of resources, personnel, and stigma from health personnel.

For ASM AGYW, these availability and accessibility challenges could be addressed within the framework of formalization that is a significant discussion in the sector. Formalization pushes for structures of accountability amongst governments and companies while enforcing labour, health, and safety regulations [6, 54–56]. Formalizing the ASM sector could lead to better availability and accessibility of care due to potential mandates for mining companies to ensure healthcare facilities are available in ASM communities that include quality of care standards and adequate, context-specific training for healthcare providers [26]. While the formalization is directly related to people working within the mining sector, community responsibility requires mining companies extend health and social services to the communities within which the mines are located. Thus, formalization provides an opportunity for development towards SDG 3, 5 and SDG 8 by improving working conditions, environmental regulations, and health and safety standards, especially for women [26]. This is aligned with the UHC goals and ultimately contributes to equitable access to health services amongst last mile populations.

To increase the quality of care available to AGYW and thus ultimately health equity, staff training, capacity building and resource development needs to be prioritized. Furthermore, to enable acceptability of SRH care especially amongst adolescents it is critical for healthcare providers, leaders, and community members including AGYW to build trust within ASM communities, creating safe spaces to access SRH information and care [57]. Some NGOs in Uganda prioritize advocacy and work through a rights-based accountability approach which emphasizes the importance of community-led SRH discussions and including AGYW into decision making through committees and community-based accountability structures [26, 58–60]. By promoting inclusion of adolescents in SRH care decision making there is opportunity to build more acceptable care in ASM communities.

Despite the challenges in availability, accessibility, and acceptability of care, results show that various health services are utilized. This is promising because it indicates that AGYW in ASM communities are seeking healthcare services despite existing barriers. Removing barriers associated with availability, accessibility, and acceptability can increase the contact and support of AGYW working in hazardous and unsafe ASM work environments, ultimately improving UHC and health equity. Considering the health issues associated with working in ASM, removing these barriers is critical.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. First, due to feasibility considerations, it did not include perspectives from healthcare providers. Health care providers perspectives would have provided a holistic explanation of the AGYW’s experiences with the healthcare system, especially considering the reported negative experiences. The study, however, included a wide range of perspectives including AGYW, older women, adolescent boys and multi-sectoral stakeholders to provide a robust understanding of access to healthcare and the subsequent inequities experienced by AGYW. Second, the study only focused on access amongst AGYW and did not contrast this experience with other populations in ASM communities. Perspectives from additional population groups would provide an overview of the access issues faced within these communities which would inform community health services planning. However, since the study participants’ responses, e.g. about the availability of health services within the community, provided insight into the availability of the physical infrastructure, the study provides information which is also relevant to the other populations. However, since we did not gather information on the other accessibility attributes from the wider populations, there is opportunity in future research to examine how the experiences of AGYW contrast with other populations including adolescent boys and older women.

Conclusion

With the ASM sector continuing to grow and engage various adolescents in work, it becomes increasingly important to critically examine the health and access to healthcare that AGYW in the sector experience. Study results show that equitable access to healthcare for AGYW working in ASM communities is limited due to challenges such as lack of personnel, resource challenges, limited facilities and distance to facilities, lack of youth-friendly services, and negative staff attitudes towards SRH care especially.

Findings indicate that despite barriers to care, AGYW in ASM communities are keen to use both public and private care where available, accessible, and acceptable. Concerted efforts are necessary to strengthen the equitable access to health services by ASM AGYW and ASM communities. Such efforts could leverage existing initiatives proposed in the national Ugandan health policies e.g. the adolescent SRHR and UHC.

Building on these study findings, participatory implementation studies which engage the ASM-AGYW in identifying and implementing priority health care interventions which should be vetted by the community and policy makers, should be implemented. However, since the ASM- AGYW’s problems are multi-faceted, effective interventions should be multi-sectoral and should involve a broad range of stakeholders, especially the ASM communities.
