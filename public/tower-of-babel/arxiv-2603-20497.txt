Abstract
. We obtain partition regularity results for homogeneous quadratic equations whose parametrized solutions admit nice factorizations into linear forms over rings of integers of imaginary quadratic fields. To do so, we develop number-theoretic results of independent interest on such fields, as Halász’s theorem (which actually holds on arbitrary number fields), a characterization for aperiodic completely multiplicative functions, the Turán-Kubilius inequality, and a new concentration estimate for multiplicative functions.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Partition and density regularity

A central problem in Ramsey theory is to determine when the existence of solutions of a polynomial equation is preserved under finitely many partitions. For example, let
q(x,y,z)=0
be a polynomial equation with 3 variables. Is it true that for any finite coloring of the domain
D
of the polynomial
q
, there exist
x,y,z
of the same color with
q(x,y,z)=0
? We say that the equation
q(x,y,z)=0
is
partition regular over
D
if the answer to this question is affirmative. A famous example due to Schur
[
27
]
is that the linear equation
x+y=z
is partition regular over
{\mathbb{N}}
. This result was extended by Rado in
[
24
]
where he characterized all the linear equations that are partition regular.

On the other hand, the partition regularity problem for non-linear equations is significantly harder. In
[
12
]
and
[
13
]
, Erdős and Graham asked whether the Pythagorean equation
x^{2}+y^{2}=z^{2}
is partition regular over
{\mathbb{N}}
.
Although this question remains open, significant progress has been made in the last decade. Specifically, for the Pythagorean equation and variations of it, one can find pairs from the set
\{x,y,z\}
that are of the same color. We formalize this relaxation of the problem with the following definition.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
D\subset{\mathbb{C}}
be a set and
q\colon D^{3}\to{\mathbb{C}}
be a function. We say that
q(x,y,z)=0
is
partition regular over
D
in
(x,y)
if for every finite coloring of
D
, there exist distinct nonzero
x,y,z\in D
with
x,y
having the same color such that
q(x,y,z)=0
. Partition regularity over
D
in
(x,z)
and
(y,z)
is defined analogously.

Pioneer work achieving quadratic partition regularity results includes that of Sárközy in
[
26
]
, who showed that the equation
x-y=z^{2}
is partition regular over
{\mathbb{N}}
in
(x,y)
, as well as the work of Khalfalah and Szemerédi (see
[
17
]
), who showed that the equation
x+y=z^{2}
is partition regular over
{\mathbb{N}}
in
(x,y)
.

Using a decomposition result for multiplicative functions in terms of Gowers norms (introduced in
[
11
]
, and which have been successfully employed in the study of patterns and very general partition regularity results, e.g.,
[
14
]
,
[
2
]
,
[
10
]
,
[
9
]
,
[
6
]
, among many others), Frantzikinakis and Host showed in
[
6
]
that given
a,b,c\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, if
\sqrt{-ac},\sqrt{-bc},\sqrt{-(a+b)c}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, then the equation
ax^{2}+by^{2}+cz^{2}=0
is partition regular over
{\mathbb{N}}
in
(x,y)
(such an example is the equation
9x^{2}+16y^{2}-z^{2}=0
).
This result was extended in
[
28
,
29
]
, where it was shown that if
\sqrt{-ac},\sqrt{-bc},\sqrt{-(a+b)c}
belong to some number field
K
, then
the equation
ax^{2}+by^{2}+cz^{2}=0
is partition regular over
\mathcal{O}_{K}
(the ring of integers of
K
) in
(x,y)
(for example
x^{2}+y^{2}-z^{2}=0
is partition regular over
{\mathbb{Z}}[\sqrt{2}]
in
(x,y)
, and is partition regular over
{\mathbb{Z}}[i]
in
(x,z)
and
(y,z)
).

The result of
[
6
]
was improved recently by Frantzikinakis, Klurman and Moreira
[
8
]
where the original assumption on the square roots was weakened to
\sqrt{-ac},\sqrt{-bc}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. This was further improved to
\sqrt{-ac}
or
\sqrt{-bc}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
by the same authors in
[
7
]
. As a special case, they showed that
x^{2}+y^{2}-z^{2}=0
is partition regular over
{\mathbb{N}}
in any two of the variables. One of the key innovations of
[
7
,
8
]
is that the authors replaced the usage of a decomposition result for multiplicative functions introduced in
[
6
]
by a convenient splitting of the set of multiplicative functions into aperiodic and non-aperiodic, and then studied each of the components separately (as opposed to the previous approach, which decomposed all functions simultaneously).
Indeed, using this new methodology, it was shown in
[
8
]
that
x^{2}+y^{2}-z^{2}=0
is also partition regular over
{\mathbb{N}}
in
(x,z)
and
(y,z)
. It is important to highlight that, in order to do so, a quadratic concentration estimate for the non-aperiodic functions was developed and used.

In this paper, we extend the splitting approach in
[
7
,
8
]
in the setting of imaginary quadratic number fields (i.e.,
K={\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{-d})
for some squarefree
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
) and provide novel applications to various partition regularity problems, extending many results from
[
6
,
7
,
8
,
28
,
29
]
.
In this setting, the ring of integers
{\mathcal{O}_{K}}
of
K
is given by
{\mathcal{O}_{K}}={\mathbb{Z}}[\tau_{d}]
, where
\tau_{d}=\sqrt{-d}
if
d\equiv 2,3\pmod{4}
, and
\tau_{d}=\frac{1+\sqrt{-d}}{2}
if
d\equiv 1\pmod{4}
.
The next result is an extension we obtain of the aforementioned partition result
[
8
, Corollary 1.3]
to imaginary quadratic number fields.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
be squarefree. For
a,b,c\in{\mathbb{Z}}\backslash\{0\}
, if
\sqrt{-ac},\sqrt{-bc}\in{\mathbb{Z}}[\tau_{d}]
, then the equation
ax^{2}+by^{2}+cz^{2}=0
is partition regular over
{\mathbb{Z}}[\tau_{d}]
in
(x,y)
.

In fact, we obtain a density version of Theorem
1.2
.
Recall that a
multiplicative Følner sequence in

{\mathcal{O}_{K}}
is a sequence of finite subsets of
\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times}
such that

\lim_{M\to\infty}\frac{|\Phi_{M}\cap(x^{-1}\cdot\Phi_{M})|}{|\Phi_{M}|}=1,\text{ for all }x\in\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times}.

The precise Følner sequences that we will use (cf. (
59
)) are a bit intricate to define, so for now, the reader can assume that Følner sequences look like the following example when
{\mathcal{O}_{K}}={\mathbb{Z}}
, which contains all the main ingredients for the generalization that appears in (
59
):

\Phi_{M}:=\{p_{1}^{a_{1}}\dots p_{k}^{a_{k}}:M\leq a_{i}\leq 2M,1\leq i\leq M\}.

where
(p_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
are the prime numbers.

For each Følner sequence we have a notion of largeness for subsets
\Lambda\subseteq R^{\times}
, defined via

(1)

\overline{d}_{\Phi}(\Lambda):=\limsup_{M\to\infty}\frac{|\Lambda\cap\Phi_{M}|}{|\Phi_{M}|},

where the upper bar is dropped if the limit does actually exist. We say that
\Lambda\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times}
has positive
multiplicative upper Banach density
if there exists some multiplicative Følner sequence
\Phi=(\Phi_{M})_{M}
along which we have
\overline{d}_{\Phi}(\Lambda)>0
.

Definition 1.3

(Density regularity)
.

Let
q\colon D^{3}\to{\mathbb{C}}
be a function. We say that
q(x,y,z)=0
is
density regular over
D
in
(x,y)
if for every subset
\Lambda\subseteq D
with positive multiplicative upper Banach density, there exist distinct nonzero
x,y,z\in D
with
x,y\in\Lambda
such that
q(x,y,z)=0
. Density regularity over
D
in
(x,z)
and
(y,z)
is defined analogously.

We have the following result, which is stronger than (and easily implies) Theorem
1.2
, given the subadditivity of the density we just introduced.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
be squarefree. For
a,b,c\in{\mathbb{Z}}\backslash\{0\}
, if
\sqrt{-ac},\sqrt{-bc}\in{\mathbb{Z}}[\tau_{d}]
, then the equation
ax^{2}+by^{2}+cz^{2}=0
is density regular over
{\mathbb{Z}}[\tau_{d}]
in
(x,y)
.

It is important to note that more standard additive versions of density will not be helpful to deal with the equation
x^{2}+y^{2}=z^{2}
, since if, for example, we look at
\Lambda=\{u\in{\mathbb{Z}}[i]^{\times}:u\equiv 1\pmod{2}\}
, then having
x,y\in\Lambda
, implies that
x^{2}+y^{2}
can never be a square (we can still look at it modulo
4
and argue similarly as in the case of
{\mathbb{N}}
).
It turns out that the more convenient form of density will be one that is multiplicative instead of additive, given the nature of the quadratic equations under consideration.

We remark that Theorems
1.2
and
1.4
improve the results in
[
28
,
29
]
by dropping the requirement that
\sqrt{-(a+b)c}\in{\mathbb{Z}}[\tau_{d}]
.
For example, Theorem
1.2
implies that if
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
is squarefree, then the equation
x^{2}+dy^{2}-z^{2}=0
is partition regular over the ring of integers of
{\mathbb{Z}}[\tau_{d}]
. This result was previously known only over the ring of integers coming from the number field
{\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{-d},\sqrt{-(d+1)})
(see
[
29
]
).

Our method applies to not only quadratic forms of the form
ax^{2}+by^{2}+cz^{2}
, but to any quadratic forms
q(x,y,z)=0
for which the parametrized solutions for
x
and
y
factorize linearly in
{\mathbb{Z}}[\tau_{d}]
. We refer the reader to Theorem
2.2
for details.

It is natural to ask whether Theorems
1.2
and
1.4
hold when
d<0
, or whether they hold when only one of
\sqrt{-ac},\sqrt{-bc}
belongs to
{\mathbb{Z}}[\tau_{d}]
.
The discussion of these natural questions is postponed to Section
8.1
.

1.2.
Number-Theoretic tools

In order to prove the above-mentioned partition regularity results, we obtain a series of results in analytic number theory for imaginary quadratic number fields. To the best of our knowledge, these results were previously unknown, and many of them require substantial new ideas compared to the classical results over integers. We believe these results are of independent interest, and may have potential applications in other number-theoretic problems.

Here is a summary of the number-theoretic tools we develop in this paper. We postpone their precise statements to the next Section.

1.2.1.
Characterizations for completely multiplicative functions

It is a classical result of Daboussi and Delange (see
[
3
, Corollary 1]
) that a completely multiplicative function
f\colon{\mathbb{Z}}^{\times}\to{\mathbb{U}}
is either
aperiodic
, meaning that its average along every arithmetic progression is zero, or
pretentious
, meaning that it behaves similarly to the product of a Dirichlet character and an Archimedean character.
This is a crucial tool for the splitting method introduced in
[
7
,
8
]
.
In this paper, we obtain an analog of
[
3
, Corollary 1]
in the setting of imaginary quadratic number fields (i.e., Theorem
2.9
) by providing a list of equivalent definitions of aperiodic completely multiplicative functions (see Proposition
5.3
).
An interesting feature is that to define a pretentious multiplicative function, one needs to consider its extension to ideal domained functions. We refer the readers to Section
5
for details.

1.2.2.
Halász’s theorem over number fields

The proof of the aforementioned result of Daboussi and Delange relies on Halász’s theorem, which is a profound result in analytic number theory that characterizes the asymptotic behavior of the average of a multiplicative function. In this paper, we obtain an analog of Halász’s theorem over arbitrary number fields (not just imaginary quadratic ones) for multiplicative functions (see Theorem
2.12
for the precise statement). The essential obstacles to carry out the proof of the classical Halász’s theorem are that number fields may not be uniquely factorizable, and that there may be infinitely many units. To overcome these difficulties, we consider functions whose domains are ideals instead of numbers, and this is good enough for the purpose of this paper. We refer the readers to Section
6
for details.

1.2.3.
The Turán-Kubilius inequality

Another important step in proving the partition regularity results of this paper is obtaining an analog of
[
8
, Proposition 2.5]
(namely, Theorem
7.5
), which is a linear concentration estimate that allows us to easily treat the pretentious multiplicative functions. As concentration estimates are based on the Turán-Kubilius inequality, in order to prove Theorem
7.5
, we obtain an analog of the inequality for imaginary quadratic fields in Theorem
2.13
.
We refer the readers to Section
7
for all the details.

Organization of the paper

We state all the main results in density regularity and number theory in Section

.
Then in Section

we explain the strategy for the proof of the main density regularity result (i.e., Theorem
2.2
) and break it down into several positivity results. A lot of the key reductions that help properly frame the problem are also explained in detail.

Next, in Section

we provide some necessary number theoretical background. We then prove the characterization result for completely multiplicative functions in Section
5
, then
Halász’s theorem over number fields in Section

respectively.

2.2
.

.

Precise statements of the main results

In this section we will introduce the relevant technical definitions so that the main results can be precisely stated.

2.1.
The main density regularity result and applications

As was stated in the Introduction, Theorem
1.4
can be generalized to any quadratic equation
q(x,y,z)=0
for which the parametrized solutions for
x
and
y
factorizes linearly in
{\mathbb{Z}}[\tau_{d}]
. In order to make this more precise, we introduce the following definition.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
K
be a number field and

be a map. We say that

admits an
\mathcal{O}_{K}
-factorization in

the following is a solution to the equation

x=kL_{1}(m,n)L_{2}(m,n),y=kL_{3}(m,n)L_{4}(m,n)\text{ and }z=kP(m,n).

We say that
p

strong
\mathcal{O}_{K}
-factorization in
(x,y)
if we may further require

We will proceed by proving the following result which implies Theorems

Theorem 2.2
.

Let

\mathcal{O}_{K}
-factorization in
(x,y)
.
Then

is density regular over
{\mathcal{O}_{K}}
in
(x,y)

It was essentially proved in

2.2.
Characterizations for completely multiplicative functions

The strategy of the proofs for the partition regularity results of this paper is to replace the decomposition method used in
[
28
,
29
]
with the splitting method introduced in
[
7
,
8
]
. Recall that a function
f\colon{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{U}}
, where
{\mathbb{U}}:=\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}:|z|\leq 1\}
is
multiplicative
if
f(mn)=f(m)\cdot f(n)

2.3.
Halász’s theorem over number fields

The first step in the study of the aperiodicity properties of a multiplicative function
f
on
{\mathcal{O}_{K}}={\mathbb{Z}}[\tau_{d}]
(for some squarefree
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
) is to study its average along the entire space instead of along arithmetic progressions, i.e., we want to focus on expressions of the form

\lim_{x\to\infty}\E_{{\mathcal{N}}(u)\leq x}f(u).

When
f
is a multiplicative function on
{\mathbb{Z}}
, the behavior of these averages is well understood via Halász’s theorem (cf.
[
16
, Satz 1 and Satz 1
′
]
). While the method of the proof of Halász’s theorem can be extended to principal ideal domains such as
{\mathbb{Z}}[i]
(see
[
4
, Thoerems A and 1.2]
), which proved a special case of Halász’s theorem over the Gaussian integers for real valued bounded completely multiplicative functions, difficulties arise when the elements in the domain cannot be factorized in a unique way. To resolve this issue, our strategy is to consider
\mathfrak{I}(K)
-domained functions instead of
\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times}
ones.
This leads us to the following result.

Theorem 2.12
.

Let
K
be a number field and
g\colon\mathfrak{I}(K)\to{\mathbb{U}}
be a multiplicative function. Then, we have the following.

(i)

If
\inf_{\tau\in{\mathbb{R}}}{\mathbb{D}}(g,{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{u})^{i\tau})=\infty
, then
\lim_{x\to\infty}\E_{{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{u})\leq x}g(\mathfrak{u})=0
.

(ii)

If
{\mathbb{D}}(g,{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{u})^{i\tau})<\infty
for some
\tau\in{\mathbb{R}}
, then

\sum_{1\leq{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{u})\leq x}g(\mathfrak{u})=R_{1}\cdot\frac{x^{1+i\tau}}{1+i\tau}\prod_{\mathfrak{p}\colon\text{prime ideal in }\mathcal{O}_{K},{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})\leq x}(1-1/{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p}))(1+h_{1+i\tau}(\mathfrak{p}))+o(x),

where
h_{s}(\mathfrak{p}):=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}g(\mathfrak{p}^{k}){\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p}^{k})^{-s}
, and
R_{1}
is the residue of the Dedekind zeta function of our number field at its simple pole at
s=1
.

We remark that Theorem
2.12
applies to any number field not just the imaginary quadratic ones. When
K={\mathbb{Q}}
, Theorem
2.12
is the standard version of Halász’s theorem.
The major application of Theorem
2.12
in this paper is that it allows us to prove the classification theorem (Theorem
2.9
).
We believe that Theorem
2.12
could lead to other applications in number theory in the future.

2.4.
The Turán-Kubilius inequality

The Turán-Kubilius inequality is an important tool in analytic number theory (see for example
[
5
, Lemma 4.1]
). In this paper, we need an analog of it for additive functions over imaginary quadratic fields whose domains are ideals.
We say that
h:\mathfrak{I}(K)\to{\mathbb{C}}
is
additive
if
h(\mathfrak{u}\mathfrak{v})=h(\mathfrak{u})+h(\mathfrak{v})
for any coprime ideals
\mathfrak{u},\mathfrak{v}
.
We have the following Turán-Kubilius inequality for additive functions.
It is worth pointing out that a different version of this result is proved in
[
19
, Chapter 10]
for
{\mathbb{Z}}[i]
.

Theorem 2.13

(Turán-Kubilius inequality)
.

Let
K={\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{-d})
for some squarefree
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
h\colon\mathfrak{I}(K)\to{\mathbb{C}}
be an additive function,
a,Q\in\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times}
with
{\mathcal{N}}(a)\leq{\mathcal{N}}(Q)
and
(a)
coprime to
(Q)
, and also fix
M,N\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Put
N^{\prime}:=2{\mathcal{N}}(Q)(N+1)
. Suppose that
Q\in\mathfrak{p}
for all prime ideal
\mathfrak{p}
with
{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})\leq M
.
Then

(6)

\begin{split}\E_{u\in{\mathcal{O}_{K}}\colon{\mathcal{N}}(u)\leq N}\left|h((Qu+a))-A_{Q,N}\right|^{2}\leq(2+o_{Q;N\to\infty}(1))B_{N^{\prime}}+O(C_{N}),\end{split}

where the implicit constant depends only on
d
,

A_{Q,N}:=\sum_{{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p}^{k})\leq N,\mathfrak{p}\nmid Q}h(\mathfrak{p}^{k}){\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-k}(1-{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-1}),

B_{N}:=\sum_{{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p}^{k})\leq N,{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})>M,k\geq 1}|h(\mathfrak{p}^{k})|^{2}{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p}^{k})^{-1},

and

C_{N}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p}^{k})\leq N^{\prime},{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})>M}|h(\mathfrak{p}^{k})|^{2}\cdot{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p}^{k})^{-1/2}.

We remark that Theorem
2.13
differs from the classical Turán-Kubilius inequality in the following ways. The first is that in Theorem
2.13
we deal with averages along arithmetic progressions
Qu+a
instead of along intervals. The second is that in Theorem
2.13
there is an extra error term
O(C_{K})
which does not exist in the case when
K={\mathbb{Q}}
. We do not know how to remove this term in Proposition
2.13
. However, this term is harmless in our applications.

3.1.
Reductions

In order to ease the notation for the proof of Theorem

Next, it follows that

3.2.

Q\in{\mathcal{O}_{K}}
that will be appropriately chosen later, depending only on the measure
\sigma
. Since we are only concerned with positivity, going along this grid is enough to establish (

). Now, let
\ell,\ell^{\prime}\in\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times}
and
\alpha,\beta\in{\mathcal{O}_{K}}
be fixed. For
\delta>0
,

and
Q,m,n\in\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times}
, we put

(10)

A_{\delta}(f,Q;m,n):=w_{\delta}(m,n)\cdot f\Bigl(\frac{\ell(Qm+1+Q\alpha n)(Qm+1+Q\beta n)}{\ell^{\prime}(Qm+1)Qn}\Bigr),

where

below which is supported on

. Since

, and we also have the positivity property (

will follow from the following.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
\sigma
be a Borel probability measure on
\mathcal{M}

\delta>0
and
Q\in\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times}
(depending only on

(11)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\E_{{\mathcal{N}}(n),{\mathcal{N}}(m)\leq N}\int_{\mathcal{M}}A_{\delta}(f,Q;m,n)\ d\sigma(f)>0.

In order to analyse the limit in (

is aperiodic (the precise definition will be given later in Section

).

Proposition 3.4
.

Let

be an aperiodic completely multiplicative function. Then, for every
\delta>0
and
Q\in\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times}
we have

(12)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\E_{{\mathcal{N}}(n),{\mathcal{N}}(m)\leq N}A_{\delta}(f,Q;m,n)=0.

Proposition
3.4
is an analog of
[

, Proposition 4.1]
.
The proof of Proposition
3.4
is based on Proposition

, a variation of which was essentially proved in

]
. We postpone the details until Section

.

We now turn our attention to the complement of the aperiodic completely multiplicative functions. As we will demonstrate in Section
6
, these are exactly the pretentious completely multiplicative functions, so we introduce the following notation:

(13)

\mathcal{M}_{p}:=\{f:\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times}\to\mathbb{S}^{1}:f\text{ is a pretentious completely multiplicative function}\}.

Lemma

establishes that

\mathcal{M}

\lim_{N\to\infty}\E_{{\mathcal{N}}(n),{\mathcal{N}}(m)\leq N}\int_{\mathcal{M}_{p}}A_{\delta}(f,Q;m,n)\ d\sigma(f)>0.

When

We mention in passing that we will use it mostly for the value

3.3.
Averaging schemes

In this subsection we wish to highlight the fact that the choice of the averaging scheme is very important to us for a number of reasons.
In order to make the discussion easier to follow, we will focus our attention on the Gaussian integers, but the same points we shall discuss equally apply to other quadratic imaginary fields.
To study the averages of a multiplicative function of the form

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{u\in\Phi_{N}}f(u),

there are at least 4 natural choices for the sequence
(\Phi_{N})_{N}
:

(i)

symmetric balls
D_{N}:=\{a+bi\colon a,b\in{\mathbb{Z}},a^{2}+b^{2}\leq N\}
;

(ii)

symmetric boxes
B_{N}:=\{a+bi\colon-N\leq a,b\leq N\}
;

(iii)

aysmmetric balls
D_{N}^{+}:=\{a+bi\colon a,b\in{\mathbb{N}},a^{2}+b^{2}\leq N\}
;

(iv)

asymmetric boxes
B_{N}^{+}:=\{a+bi\colon 1\leq a,b\leq N\}
.

In
[
28
,
29
]
, all the averages are taken over boxes. The advantage in doing so is that the box average is well defined for any number field while the ball averages can only be defined for quadratic imaginary fields, as for a general number field
K
, the set
\{u\in{\mathcal{O}_{K}}\colon{\mathcal{N}}(u)\leq N\}
can be infinite. However, we are unable to prove an analog of Theorem
2.9
if we define aperiodic functions using box averages as was done in
[
28
,
29
]
, mainly because we do not know if an analog of Halász’s Theorem (Theorem
2.12
) holds for box averages. Therefore, in this paper we favor ball averages over box averages.

Next we explain why it is more convenient to work with symmetric balls instead of the asymmetric ones. Consider the multiplicative function
f(u)=\frac{u}{{\mathcal{N}}(u)^{1/2}}
. One can show that
f
does not have finite distance to the product of Dirichlet characters and Archimedean characters either. On the other hand,
the average of
f
over asymmetric boxes does not converge to 0 as
f
takes values only in the first quadrant. So an analog of Halász’s theorem fails to hold in this setting, unless we expand the definition of pretentious functions to include functions
f
of this form (however we do not have this issue for symmetric ball averages as the symmetric ball average of
f
does converge to 0, as can be checked with a Riemann sums argument).
Because of the above reasons, in this paper we choose to work with (i): averages over symmetric balls.

4.
Number theoretical background

In this section we will review some basic number theoretical notions and notation, and also introduce some basic estimates we will need to make use of in the sequel.

4.1.
Basic facts on number fields and their rings of integers

An
(algebraic) number field

K
is a finite degree (and hence algebraic) field extension of the field of rational numbers
{\mathbb{Q}}
. The
ring of integers

{\mathcal{O}_{K}}
of a number field
K
is the ring of all
integral elements
in
K
(i.e., roots of polynomials with integer coefficients and leading coefficient 1). Let
D=[K:{\mathbb{Q}}]
denote the degree of the extension. It is classical that there exists an
integral basis

\mathcal{B}=\{b_{1},\dots,b_{D}\}
of
{\mathcal{O}_{K}}
, i.e., a basis of the
{\mathbb{Q}}
-vector space
K
such that each element
x\in{\mathcal{O}_{K}}
can be uniquely represented as
x=\sum_{i=1}^{D}c_{i}b_{i}
for some
c_{i}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Let
\iota\colon{\mathbb{Z}}^{D}\to{\mathcal{O}_{K}}
be the map given by
\iota(n_{1},\dots,n_{D})=n_{1}b_{1}+\dots+n_{D}b_{D}
. For
x\in K
, let
A_{x}
be the unique
d\times d
matrix such that
\begin{bmatrix}xb_{1}\\
\vdots\\
xb_{D}\end{bmatrix}=A_{x}\begin{bmatrix}b_{1}\\
\vdots\\
b_{D}\end{bmatrix}
. The
K
-
norm
of
x\in K
is defined to be
{\mathcal{N}}_{K}(x):=\det(A_{x})
. When there is no risk of confusion regarding the underlying field
K
, we simply write
{\mathcal{N}}
instead of
{\mathcal{N}}_{K}
.

In this paper, our main focus is on quadratic fields, i.e., number fields of the form
K={\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{-d})
for some squarefree
d\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.
In this case, we have
{\mathcal{O}_{K}}={\mathbb{Z}}[\tau_{d}]
and every
z\in{\mathcal{O}_{K}}
can be written as
m+n\tau_{d}
for some
m,n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
in a unique way. The element
\tau_{d}
is given by
\tau_{d}=\sqrt{-d}
if
d\equiv 1,2\mod 4
and
\tau_{d}=\frac{1+\sqrt{-d}}{2}
if
d\equiv 3\mod 4
. Moreover, we have that
{\mathcal{N}}(m+n\tau_{d})
is equal to
m^{2}+dn^{2}
if
d\equiv 1,2\mod 4
and equal to
m^{2}+mn+\frac{d+1}{4}n^{2}
if
d\equiv 3\mod 4
.

We will use the following classical result on properties of the norm
{\mathcal{N}}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
K
be a number field. Then
{\mathcal{N}}(xy)={\mathcal{N}}(x){\mathcal{N}}(y)
for all
x,y\in K
. Also, for any
x\in{\mathcal{O}_{K}}
we have
{\mathcal{N}}(x)\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.

We say that
\epsilon\in{\mathcal{O}_{K}}
is a unit if
{\mathcal{N}}(\epsilon)=\pm 1
.
It follows from Dirichlet’s unit theorem
[
23
, Theorem 8.1]
that
{\mathcal{O}_{K}}
has finitely many units if and only if
K={\mathbb{Q}}
or
{\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{-d})
for some square-free
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Moreover,
we have a complete description for the units in this case.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
K={\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{-d})
for some squarefree
d\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.

(i)

If
d=1
, then the units of
{\mathcal{O}_{K}}
are
\pm 1,\pm i
.

(ii)

If
d=3
, then the units of
{\mathcal{O}_{K}}
are
e^{k\pi i/3},0\leq k\leq 5
.

(iii)

If
d>0,d\neq 1,3
, then the units of
{\mathcal{O}_{K}}
are
\pm 1
.

This follows easily from computing the elements in
{\mathcal{O}_{K}}
whose norm is equal to
\pm 1
and solving the resulting diophantine equations, which is straightforward, as in the imaginary quadratic case the norm is non-negative.

Let
K
be a number field. We use
\mathfrak{I}(K)
to denote the set of all ideals of
{\mathcal{O}_{K}}
,
\mathfrak{Q}(K)
to denote the set of all fractional ideals of
K
(recall that a fractional ideal has the form
x^{-1}I
for some
x\in\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times}
and non-trivial ideal
I\subseteq{\mathcal{O}_{K}})
. For
I\in\mathfrak{I}(K)
, we say that its
norm
is
{\mathcal{N}}(I):=[{\mathcal{O}_{K}}:I]
. In the case where
x^{-1}I\in\mathfrak{I}(K)
, the
norm
is given by
{\mathcal{N}}(x^{-1}I):={\mathcal{N}}(I)/{\mathcal{N}}(x)
.

We say that two ideals
I,J\in\mathfrak{I}(K)
are
coprime
if
I+J=(1)
. For Dedekind domains, this is equivalent to
I,J
sharing no elements in their factorization into prime ideals.
Let
a,b\in{\mathcal{O}_{K}}^{\times}
,
\mathfrak{p}\in\mathfrak{I}(K)
and
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
. We write
\mathfrak{p}|a
if
a\in\mathfrak{p}
(or equivalently
\mathfrak{p}|(a)
). Write
\mathfrak{p}^{k}||a
if
k
is the largest integer for which
\mathfrak{p}^{k}|a
.

We recall a definition.

Definition 4.3
.

Let
K
be a number field. We say that the quotient
\mathfrak{Q}(K)/\mathfrak{I}(K)
is the
ideal class group of

K
.

Given a number field
K
, its ideal class group
G
is always finite (e.g., see
[
23
, Theorem 6.3]
), and we say that the order of
G
is the
class number
of
K
.

The ideal class group of a number field
K
depends on the algebraic properties of the number field under consideration. For example, if
K={\mathbb{Q}}(i)
, its ideal class group is trivial (this is a consequence of
{\mathbb{Z}}[i]
being a PID), but for
{\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{-5})
it is isomorphic to
{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}}
.

The following lemma will be used in the sequel.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
K
be a number field and
D=[K:{\mathbb{Q}}]
. For any
I\in\mathfrak{I}(K)
, there exists a finite union of
D
-dimensional infinite arithmetic progressions
P
such that for
u,v\in{\mathcal{O}_{K}}^{\times}

\frac{(u)}{(v)}I\in\mathfrak{I}(K)\Leftrightarrow\frac{(u)}{(v)}=\frac{(u^{\prime})}{({\mathcal{N}}(I))}\text{ for some }u^{\prime}\in P.

In fact,
P
is the set of
u^{\prime}
such that
{\mathcal{N}}(I)^{D-1}|{\mathcal{N}}(u^{\prime})
.

Proof.

By multiplying with conjugates of
v
if necessary, we may assume without loss of generality that
v\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. Then
\frac{(u)}{(v)}I\in\mathfrak{I}(K)\Leftrightarrow v^{D}|{\mathcal{N}}(u){\mathcal{N}}(I)
.
Write
{\mathcal{N}}(I)=u_{1}^{t_{1}}\dots u_{n}^{t_{n}}
for some disticnt prime numbers
u_{i}
and some powers
t_{i}\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

We now proceed by cases. If a prime number
t
is such that
t|v
but
t\nmid{\mathcal{N}}(I)
, then
t^{D}|{\mathcal{N}}(u)
and so
u/t\in\mathcal{O}_{K}
. So
\frac{(u)}{(v)}=\frac{(u/t)}{(v/t)}
. If
u_{i}^{t_{i}+1}|q
for some
i
, then
p^{D}|{\mathcal{N}}(u){\mathcal{N}}(I)
implies that
u_{i}^{D}|{\mathcal{N}}(p)
and again
\frac{(u)}{(v)}=\frac{(u/u_{i})}{(v/u_{i})}
.
Thus, we may freely assume that
v={\mathcal{N}}(I)
.

We have that
v^{D}|{\mathcal{N}}(u){\mathcal{N}}(I)\Leftrightarrow{\mathcal{N}}(I)^{D-1}|{\mathcal{N}}(u)
. The set of such
u
is clearly a finite union of
D
-dimensional infinite arithmetic progressions. This completes the proof.
∎

We conclude this section with a counting property on the number of
ideals in a given ideal class. These results are given in the following lemmas.

Lemma 4.5

(Theorem 2,
[
22
]
)
.

Let
K
be a number field of degree
D
and
C
be an ideal class of it. For
x>0
, let
N(x,C)
denote the number of ideals in the class
C
whose norms are at most
x
. Then there exist constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
depending on the number field
K
only, such that

C_{1}(x^{\frac{1}{D}}-C_{2})^{D}\leq N(x,C)\leq C_{1}(x^{\frac{1}{D}}+C_{2})^{D}

for all
x>1
for some
C_{1}>0
and
0<C_{2}<1
depending only on
K
.

As a consequence of Lemma
4.5
, we have (see also
[
20
, Chapter 6, Theorem 39]
for a reference):

Corollary 4.6
.

Let
K
be a number field of degree
D
. Then, there exists a universal constant
\gamma_{K}>0
such that for any ideal class
C
of
K
, we have that

\frac{1}{x}\sum_{\mathfrak{u}\in C\colon{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{u})\leq x}1=\gamma_{K}+o(1).

Lemma 4.7
.

For any number field
K
, there exists a constant
C
such that for any
x>0
, the number of ideals of
{\mathcal{O}_{K}}
which is a power of a prime ideal and whose norm is
x
is at most
[K\colon{\mathbb{Q}}]
.

Proof.

Let
D:=[K\colon{\mathbb{Q}}]
.
By
[
29
, Lemma 2.9]
, the norm of every prime ideal is a power of prime in
{\mathbb{N}}
. So we may assume that
x=p^{r}
for some
r\in{\mathbb{N}}
and prime
p\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Suppose that
{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p}^{k})=p^{r}
, then we must have that
r=kt
for some
t\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})=p^{t}
. Again by
[
29
, Lemma 2.9]
, we must have that
t\leq D
and there are at most
D
such
\mathfrak{p}
. Now for each such
\mathfrak{p}
, there is at most one choice of
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p}^{k})=p^{r}
. So the number of ideals of the form
\mathfrak{p}^{k}
with norm equal to
p^{r}
is at most
D
.
∎

4.2.
Sum of reciprocals of norms of primes and the Dedekind zeta function

Convention
.

Throughout the paper,
\mathfrak{p}
and
\mathfrak{q}
always denote prime ideals of
\mathcal{O}_{K}
. Whenever we sum over
\mathfrak{p}
or
\mathfrak{q}
, this sum is assumed to be taken along prime ideals.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
K
be a number field. We have

\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathfrak{I}(K)}\frac{1}{{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})}=\infty\text{ and }\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathfrak{I}(K)}\frac{1}{{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{\sigma}}<\infty

for all
\sigma>1
.

Proof.

We say that
p\in{\mathcal{O}_{K}}
is a
prime element
if whenever
p=ab
for some
a,b\in{\mathcal{O}_{K}}
, one of
a,b
must be a unit.
Then
[
29
, Theorem 2.18]
says that

\sum_{p\colon\text{ prime element in }\mathcal{O}_{K}}\frac{1}{{\mathcal{N}}(p)}=\infty

(for a proof, see
[
21
, pp. 148–149]
). This immediately implies that

\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathfrak{I}(K)}\frac{1}{{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})}\geq\sum_{p\colon\text{ prime element in }\mathcal{O}_{K}}\frac{1}{{\mathcal{N}}((p))}=\sum_{p\colon\text{ prime element in }\mathcal{O}_{K}}\frac{1}{{\mathcal{N}}(p)}=\infty.

For
\sigma>1
, it follows from Lemma
4.5
that there exists some constant
C
depending only on
K
such that

\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathfrak{I}(K)}\frac{1}{{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})}\leq C\sum_{\text{$p:$ prime element in ${\mathbb{N}}$}}\frac{1}{p^{\sigma}}<\infty.

∎

The
Dedekind zeta function
of a number field
K
is the map
\zeta_{K}
given by

(17)

\zeta_{K}(s):=\sum_{\mathfrak{u}\in\mathfrak{I}(K)}{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{u})^{-s}.

For simplicity, in this paper we write
\zeta
instead of
\zeta_{K}
since the underlying number field will always be clear from the context.
As in the integer case, we have (see
[
23
, Theorem 5.11]
, for example) the following.

Lemma 4.9
.

The function
\zeta
only has a simple pole at
s=1
and admits an analytic extension in the rest of the complex plane.

A few comments regarding this result: note that, just as is in the case of the standard Riemann-zeta function, we easily notice that
\zeta(s)
is defined for
\text{Re}(s)>1
, and that it has a simple pole at
s=1
. We then extend it meromorphically on the whole complex plane using the analog of the functional equation that the Riemann-zeta function satisfies, and abusing notation, we keep the same symbol
\zeta
for it. It is important to highlight that the functional equation is slightly different because our context is that of a number field
K
.
The following estimate for the Dedekind zeta function holds.

Lemma 4.10
.

Let
M>0
and
\sigma,\tau\in{\mathbb{R}}
. We have

\zeta(\sigma+i\tau)=O\left(\frac{1}{\sigma-1}\right)

as
\sigma\to 1^{+}
uniformly for
-M\leq\tau\leq M
.

Proof.

Let
s=\sigma+i\tau
. Then, recall that we can write

\left|\sum_{\mathfrak{u}\in\mathfrak{I}(K)}{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{u})^{-s}\right|=\lim_{x\to\infty}\sum_{1\leq{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{u})\leq x}{\mathcal{N}}(u)^{-s}.

We now apply the Riemann-Stieltjes summation by parts trick (see also Section
6
, where this same idea is used many times, for example in (
23
) or (
31
)), and we see that

\frac{\zeta(s)}{s}=\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{x^{s+1}}H(x)\ dx,

where
H(x)=\sum_{1\leq{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{u})\leq x}1
. Corollary
4.6
implies that
H(x)=O(x)
, so the resulting integral is of order

\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{x^{\sigma}}\ dx=\frac{1}{\sigma-1}.

Notice then that for
s=1+i\tau
,
|s|\leq M+1
is a uniform bound, so that

\zeta(\sigma+i\tau)=O\left(\frac{1}{\sigma-1}\right)

uniformly in
-M\leq\tau\leq M
, as
\sigma\to 1^{+}
.
∎

We conclude this section by some estimates related to the distributions of prime ideals which will be used in later sections.

Lemma 4.11
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
. Then as
N\to\infty
, we have

(i)

\sum_{{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p}^{k})\leq N}1=O\left(\frac{N}{\log N}\right);

(ii)

\sum_{{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p}^{k})\leq N}{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p}^{k})^{-1}=O(\log\log N);

(iii)

\sum_{N^{\varepsilon}\leq{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})\leq N}{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-1}=O_{\epsilon}(1);

(iv)

\sum_{{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})>N^{\varepsilon}}{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-(1+(\log N)^{-1})}=O_{\epsilon}(1);

(v)

\frac{1}{\log N}\sum_{{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})<N^{\varepsilon}}\frac{\log{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})}{{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})}=O(\epsilon);

(vi)

\sum_{{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p}^{k}\mathfrak{q}^{\ell})\leq N,\mathfrak{p}\neq\mathfrak{q}}1=O\left(\frac{N\log\log N}{\log N}\right)
.

Proof.

For
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
, let
\Pi_{i}(N)
denote the set of integers
x\in\{1,\dots,n\}
with at most
i
disinct factors. Let
\pi_{i}(N):=|\Pi_{i}(N)|
.
Throughout the proof, we make use of the following facts without explicitly stating them:

•

It is known that (see
[
30
, Chapter II.6]
, for example)

\pi_{i}(N)\sim C_{i}\frac{N(\log\log N)^{i-1}}{\log N};

•

By the Merten’s second theorem (see for example
[
30
, Chapter I.1, Theorem 9]
),

\sum_{p\leq N,\text{ $p$ is a prime}}p^{-1}=\log\log N+O(1);

•

By
[
29
, Lemma 2.9]
, the norm of every prime ideal is of the form
p^{r}
for some prime
p\in{\mathbb{N}}
and some
1\leq i\leq[K:{\mathbb{Q}}]
;

•

By Lemma
4.7
, we have

\sum_{{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p}^{k})=a}1\ll 1
for all
a\in{\mathbb{N}}
;

•

For any
I\subset{\mathbb{N}}\backslash\{1\}
and
s\geq 1
, we have

\sum_{a\in I,\text{ $a$ is a power of prime}}a^{-s}\leq\sum_{a\in I,\text{ $a$ is a prime}}a^{-s}+O(1),

since
\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}n^{-sk}\leq\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}n^{-k}=\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n(n-1)}=1.

Let us show each of the asserted statements.
First, for part (i), we have

\sum_{{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p}^{k})\leq N}1=\sum_{a\in\Pi_{1}(N)}\sum_{{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p}^{k})=a}1\ll\sum_{a\in\Pi_{1}(N)}1=\pi_{1}(N)=O\left(\frac{N}{\log N}\right).

Next, part (ii) follows from the fact that

\displaystyle\sum_{{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p}^{k})\leq N}{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p}^{k})^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{a\in\Pi_{1}(N)}\sum_{{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p}^{k})=a}a^{-1}

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle\sum_{a\in\Pi_{1}(N)}a^{-1}\ll\sum_{p\leq N,p\text{ is a prime}}p^{-1}=O(\log\log N).

As for part (iii), it is

\displaystyle\sum_{N^{\varepsilon}\leq{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})\leq N}{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{N^{\varepsilon}\leq a\leq N,\text{ $a$ is a power of a prime}}\sum_{{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})=a}a^{-1}

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle\sum_{N^{\varepsilon}\leq a\leq N,\text{ $a$ is a power of a prime}}a^{-1}\ll\sum_{N^{\varepsilon}\leq a\leq N,\text{ $a$ is a prime}}a^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\log\log N-\log\log N^{\varepsilon}+O(1)=O_{\varepsilon}(1).

We continue with part (iv). Note that

\displaystyle\sum_{{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})>N^{\varepsilon}}{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-(1+(\log N)^{-1})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{a>N^{\varepsilon},\text{ $a$ is a power of a prime}}\sum_{{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})=a}a^{-(1+(\log N)^{-1})}

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle\sum_{a>N^{\varepsilon},\text{ $a$ is a power of a prime}}a^{-(1+(\log N)^{-1})}

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle\sum_{a>N^{\varepsilon},\text{ $a$ is a prime}}a^{-(1+(\log N)^{-1})}+O(1),

where the right hand side is
O_{\varepsilon}(1)
by following the same argument as in the bottom of
[
5
, p. 246]
.

Regarding part (v), we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\log N}\sum_{{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})<N^{\varepsilon}}\frac{\log{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})}{{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\log N}\sum_{a<N^{\varepsilon},\text{ $a$ power of a prime}}\sum_{{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})=a}\frac{\log a}{a}

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\log N}\sum_{a<N^{\varepsilon},\text{ $a$ power of a prime}}\frac{\log a}{a}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\log N}\sum_{a<N^{\varepsilon},\text{ $a$ prime}}\frac{\log a}{a}+\Theta=O(\varepsilon)+\Theta,

where the last inequality follows from the same estimate as in
[
5
, p. 247]
, and

\begin{split}|\Theta|\leq\frac{1}{\log N}\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{\log n^{k}}{n^{k}}=\frac{1}{\log N}\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{(2n-1)\log n}{(n-1)^{2}n}=O\left(\frac{1}{\log N}\right),\end{split}

after swapping the other of the sums, and summing the inner sum using differentiation of a geometric series. As for the last claim, simply note that the resulting series is clearly convergent to some absolute constant.

Lastly, for part (vi), let
\Pi_{i,T}(N)
denote the set of natural numbers no larger than
N
which are of the form
p^{k}q^{\ell}
for some primes
p,q
and some
0\leq k,\ell\leq T
. Then,

(18)

\begin{split}&\qquad\sum_{{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p}^{k}\mathfrak{q}^{\ell})\leq N,\mathfrak{p}\neq\mathfrak{q}}1=\sum_{a,b\in\Pi_{1,D}(N),k,\ell\in{\mathbb{N}},a^{k}b^{\ell}\leq N}\sum_{{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})=a,{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{q})=b}1\ll\sum_{a,b\in\Pi_{1,D}(N),k,\ell\in{\mathbb{N}},a^{k}b^{\ell}\leq N}1,\end{split}

where
D:=[K:{\mathbb{Q}}]
.
Fix any
a,b\in\Pi_{1,D}(N),k,\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
with
a^{k}b^{\ell}\leq N
. If
a,b
are powers of different primes, then
a^{k}b^{\ell}
belongs to
\pi_{2}(N)
, and for each
x\in\pi_{2}(N)
,
x
can be written in the form
a^{k}b^{\ell}
for some
a,b\in\Pi_{1,D}(N),k,\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
in at most
2D^{2}
ways. If
a,b
are powers of the same prime, then
a^{k}b^{\ell}
belongs to
\pi_{1}(N)
. In this case, for each
x=p^{r}\in\pi_{1}(N)
with
p
being a prime, the number of ways

can be written in the form
a^{k}b^{\ell}
for some
a,b\in\Pi_{1,D}(N),k,\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
is at most
D^{2}
. So the right hand side of (

) can be bounded by

2D^{2}\pi_{2}(N)+D^{2}\sum_{p^{r}\in\pi_{1}(N)}1\leq O\left(\frac{N\log\log N}{\log N}\right)+\sum_{r=1}^{\lceil\frac{\log N}{\log 2}\rceil}\pi_{1}({N}^{1/r}).

The estimate

\displaystyle\sum_{r=1}^{\lceil\frac{\log N}{\log 2}\rceil}\pi_{1}({N}^{1/r})

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle\sum_{r=1}^{\lceil\frac{\log N}{\log 2}\rceil}\frac{r{N}^{1/r}}{\log N}\leq\frac{N}{\log N}+\sum_{r=2}^{\lceil\frac{\log N}{\log 2}\rceil}\frac{r\sqrt{N}}{\log N}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{N}{\log N}+\log N\sqrt{N}\leq O\left(\frac{N\log\log N}{\log N}\right),

gives the conclusion.
∎

5.
A classification result for completely multiplicative functions

The purpose of this section is to obtain a useful classification result for multiplicative functions, which will be the cornerstone of our analysis of the set
\mathcal{M}
which we must split into different pieces according to the asymptotic behavior of the multiplicative function
f
. We begin with extensions of multiplicative functions.

5.1.
Extensions of multiplicative functions

In this section, we prove Theorem
2.9
.
Before that, we need to classify all the extensions of a completely multiplicative function
f
.
Let
D=\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times}
or
\mathfrak{I}(K)
.
We say that
f\colon D\to{\mathbb{U}}
is
completely multiplicative
(on
D
) if
f(mn)=f(m)f(n)
for all
m,n\in D
.
For any completely multiplicative function
f\colon\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times}\to{\mathbb{U}}

with
f(\epsilon)=1
for all units
\epsilon
,
8
8

8

It is clear that if
f
admits an extension, then
f(\epsilon)=\tilde{f}((\epsilon))=\tilde{f}((1))=f(1)=1
for all units
\epsilon
, so naturally we only define extensions of multiplicative functions
f
if they satisfy this additional condition.
we say that a completely multiplicative function
\tilde{f}\colon\mathfrak{Q}(K)\to{\mathbb{U}}
is an
extension
of
f
if
\tilde{f}((x))=f(x)
for all
x\in\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times}
. One of the key ideas in this paper is the study of averages of completely multiplicative functions on a non-uniquely factorizable domain which is done by passing to extended versions of the original multiplicative functions.

Let
G
be the ideal class group of
K
. By
[
23
, Theorem 6.3]
,
G
is a finite group, and it is also clearly abelian by construction. Therefore, by the classification of finite abelian groups, we can find
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
and generators
g_{1},\dots,g_{k}
of
G
of orders
d_{1},\dots,d_{k}\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
G=\langle g_{1},\dots,g_{k}\rangle
.

For each
1\leq i\leq k
, let
I_{i}\in\mathfrak{Q}(K)
be a representative of
g_{i}
. We may assume without loss of generality that
I_{i}\in\mathfrak{I}(K)
. If so, then we say that
(I_{1},\dots,I_{k})
is an
ideal class group representation
for
{\mathcal{O}_{K}}
.
It follows that
I_{i}^{d_{i}}=(x_{i})
for some
x_{i}\in\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times}
.
It is clear that if
\tilde{f}
is an extension of
f
, then we must have that
\tilde{f}(I_{i})^{d_{i}}=f(x_{i})
for all
1\leq i\leq k
.
Conversely, we have the following.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
f\colon\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times}\to\mathbb{S}
be a completely multiplicative function with
f(\epsilon)=1
for all units
\epsilon
. Suppose that
(I_{1},\dots,I_{k})
is an ideal class representation of
{\mathcal{O}_{K}}
with

I_{i}^{d_{i}}=(x_{i})
for some
x_{i}\in\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times}
for
1\leq i\leq k
. Then for any
a_{1},\dots,a_{k}\in{\mathbb{C}}
with
a_{i}^{d_{i}}=f(x_{i}),1\leq i\leq k
, there exists a unique extension
\tilde{f}
of
f
such that
\tilde{f}(I_{i})=a_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq k
.

Proof.

The uniqueness part is obvious and so we now prove the existence of such an extension. Note that every ideal can be written as
\frac{(x)}{(y)}I
, where
I=I_{1}^{b_{1}}\dots I_{k}^{b_{k}}
, and for some
x,y\in\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times}
and
0\leq b_{i}\leq d_{i}-1
.
By Lemma
4.4
, we may assume that
y={\mathcal{N}}(I)
. For such an ideal,
define

\tilde{f}\left(\frac{(x)}{({\mathcal{N}}(I))}I\right):=f(x)f({\mathcal{N}}(I))^{-1}a_{1}^{b_{1}}\dots a_{k}^{b_{k}}

for all
x,y\in\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times}
and
0\leq b_{i}\leq d_{i}-1
, which is well defined since
x
is uniquely determined up to a unit and
f(\epsilon)=1
for all units
\epsilon
. Since clearly
a_{1},\dots,a_{k}\in\mathbb{S}
, it follows that
\tilde{f}
takes values in
\mathbb{S}
.

It is clear that
\tilde{f}((x))=f(x)
for all
x\in\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times}
(in which case we must have that
b_{1}=\dots=b_{k}=0
).
We now show that
\tilde{f}
is multiplicative.

Let
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times}
and
0\leq b_{i},b^{\prime}_{i}\leq d_{i}-1
be such that
J:=\frac{(x)}{({\mathcal{N}}(I))}I
and
J^{\prime}:=\frac{(x^{\prime})}{({\mathcal{N}}(I^{\prime}))}I^{\prime}
are ideals, where
I=I_{1}^{b_{1}}\dots I_{k}^{b_{k}}
and
I^{\prime}=I_{1}^{b^{\prime}_{1}}\dots I_{k}^{b^{\prime}_{k}}
.
Put
c_{i}=b_{i}+b^{\prime}_{i}

and
c_{i}=b_{i}+b^{\prime}_{i}-d_{i}
otherwise. Denote

.
Then

JJ^{\prime}=\frac{(xx^{\prime}x_{1}^{r_{1}}\dots x_{k}^{r_{k}})}{({\mathcal{N}}(I_{1}^{b_{1}+b^{\prime}_{1}}\dots I_{k}^{b_{k}+b^{\prime}_{k}}))}I_{1}^{c_{1}}\dots I_{k}^{c_{k}}.

Since

JJ^{\prime}=\frac{(y)}{({\mathcal{N}}(I_{1}^{c_{1}}\dots I_{k}^{c_{k}}))}I_{1}^{c_{1}}\dots I_{k}^{c_{k}}

for some

.
Therefore, we have that

By definition,

\begin{split}&\qquad\tilde{f}(JJ^{\prime})=f(y)f({\mathcal{N}}(I_{1}^{c_{1}}\dots I_{k}^{c_{k}}))^{-1}a_{1}^{c_{1}}\dots a_{k}^{c_{k}}.\end{split}

On the other hand,

\tilde{f}(J)\tilde{f}(J^{\prime})=f(x)f({\mathcal{N}}(I_{1}^{b_{1}}\dots I_{k}^{b_{k}}))^{-1}f(x^{\prime})f(I_{1}^{b^{\prime}_{1}}\dots I_{k}^{b^{\prime}_{k}})a_{1}^{b_{1}+b^{\prime}_{1}}\dots a_{k}^{b_{k}+b^{\prime}_{k}}.

One can easily check that

, completing the proof.
∎

Lemma 5.2

Let

This follows from the Følner property that the balls

6.
Halász’s theorem and applications

The goal of this section is to prove an analog of Halász’s theorem (Theorem
2.12
) as well as a classification result for multiplicative functions (Theorem
2.9
) over imaginary quadratic number fields. We are interested in obtaining Halász’s theorem because it will give us a convenient characterization of pretentious multiplicative functions on the set of ideals of a number field
\mathcal{O}_{K}
, which we denote by
\mathfrak{I}(K)
. This, in turn, will allow us to show a measurability result we need, in order to carry out the strategy of the proof, as delineated in Section
3
.

We will adapt the ideas from
[
5
, Chapter 6]
to the context of quadratic imaginary rings of integers. It is worth pointing out that many of the proofs in this section are much simpler in the few cases where the quadratic imaginary field is also a UFD: ideal-domained functions in that case do not need to be invoked, and one can work with standard multiplicative functions on the ring of integers.

Throughout this section,
g(\mathfrak{u})
will denote an arbitrary multiplicative function on
\mathfrak{I}(K)
.When using the complex variable
s\in{\mathbb{C}}
, we will often write
s=\sigma+i\tau
. Also throughout, the function
G(s)
will be given by:

G(s):=\sum_{\mathfrak{u}\in\mathfrak{I}(K)}g(\mathfrak{u}){\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{u})^{-s}=\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{1\leq{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{u})\leq N}g(\mathfrak{u}){\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{u})^{-s}.

6.1.
Asymptotic average formula for multiplicative functions

The purpose of this section is to prove the following analog of

Theorem 6.1

Let

11

11

We say that

\sum_{1\leq{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{u})\leq x}g(\mathfrak{u})=\frac{x^{1+i\alpha}}{1+i\alpha}CL(\log x)+o(x),\text{ as }x\to\infty

Proof of (

Note that

Using the properties of the Riemann-Stieltjes integral,

\begin{split}&\qquad\frac{G(s)}{s}=\frac{1}{s}\lim_{R\to\infty}\int_{1_{-}}^{R}\frac{1}{x^{s}}\,dH(x)=-\lim_{R\to\infty}\int_{1}^{R}H(x)\,d\left(\frac{1}{sx^{s}}\right)\\

This exhibits the function of interest

Lemma 6.4

\displaystyle 2\int_{-2T}^{2T}\left(1-\frac{|\tau|}{2T}\right)|A(s)|^{2}\ d\tau

4.10
, we have that

\begin{split}|G(s)|\to o\left(\frac{1}{\sigma-1}\right)\end{split}

as
\sigma\to 1+
with the same uniformity for all
|\tau|\leq M
.
It then follows from Theorem
6.1
that
\lim_{N\to\infty}\E_{{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{u})\leq N}g(\mathfrak{u})=0
(setting
C=\alpha=0
and
L\equiv 1
).

We now assume that
{\mathbb{D}}(g,{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{u})^{i\alpha})<\infty
for some
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}
. As we saw in the very last steps of the proof of Theorem
6.1
, it is enough to establish the asymptotic estimate in the case where
\alpha=0
, so assume that this is the case, namely

(42)

\sum_{\mathfrak{p}}{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-1}(1-\text{Re}(g(\mathfrak{p})))<\infty.

We caution the reader that in the rest of the proof, the constant term
O(1)
is allowed to depend implicitly on the function
g
.
Let
K\geq 1
. For
\sigma>1
and
|\tau|\leq K(\sigma-1)
, by Lemma
6.3
, we may write

(43)

G(s)/\zeta(s)=Q(s)\exp\left(-\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathfrak{I}(K),{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})\geq 3}(1-g(\mathfrak{p})){\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-s}\right)

for some function
Q
which is continuous in every rectangle
1\leq\sigma\leq 2,|\tau|\leq M
.

Denote

L\left(\frac{1}{\sigma-1}\right):=\exp\left(i\text{Im}\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathfrak{I}(K),{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})\geq 3}{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-\sigma}g(\mathfrak{p})\right).

To show that
L(w)
is a slowly varying function, we need the following Lemma.

Lemma 6.6
.

Let
M_{1}\geq 1
. If (
42
) holds,
then

\sum_{\mathfrak{p}}|{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-\sigma^{\prime}}-{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-s}|\cdot|1-g(\mathfrak{p})|\to 0,

as
\sigma^{\prime}\to 1+
, uniformly for
|\tau|\leq M_{1}(\sigma^{\prime}-1),(\sigma^{\prime}-1)/2\leq\sigma-1\leq\sigma^{\prime}-1
.

Proof.

The proof is similar to
[
5
, Lemma 6.8]
.
Note that since
|1-z|^{2}\leq 2(1-\text{Re }z)
for
|z|\leq 1
. We have

\displaystyle\left|{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-\sigma^{\prime}}-{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-s}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-2\sigma^{\prime}}\left|1-{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-(\sigma^{\prime}-s)}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-2\sigma}2(1-\text{Re}(\exp(-(\sigma^{\prime}-s)\log{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p}))))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-2\sigma}|\sigma^{\prime}-s|\log{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2(1+2K){\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-2\sigma}(\sigma-1)\log{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p}),

where we used that
1-\text{Re }(e^{-(\sigma^{\prime}-s)\log{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})})=\text{Re }\left((\sigma^{\prime}-s)\int_{0}^{1}e^{-t(\sigma^{\prime}-s)\log{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})}\ dt\right)
, which is bounded above (using trivial bounds, since
\text{Re }(\sigma^{\prime}-s)\geq 0
) by
|\sigma^{\prime}-s|\log{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})
. Thus, there exists some universal constant
C>0
such that

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathfrak{p}}{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})|{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-\sigma^{\prime}}-{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-s}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 6K(\sigma-1)\sum_{\mathfrak{p}}{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-(2\sigma-1)}\log{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 6K(\sigma-1)\left(-\frac{\zeta^{\prime}(2\sigma-1)}{\zeta(2\sigma-1)}+O(1)\right)\leq CK,

where in the second to last step we applied Lemma
6.3
once more to the derivative of
\log\zeta(s)
. Let
0<\varepsilon<1
. Again, since
|1-z|^{2}\leq 2(1-\text{Re}(z))
, by (
42
), there exists
N
such that

\sum_{{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})>N}\frac{|1-g(\mathfrak{p})|^{2}}{{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})}<\varepsilon^{2}.

Therefore, by the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality, we have that

\begin{split}&\qquad\sum_{{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})>N}\left|{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-\sigma^{\prime}}-{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-s}\right|\cdot|1-g(\mathfrak{p})|\leq(CK)^{1/2}\varepsilon.\end{split}

On the other hand, for all
{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})<N
,

\begin{split}|{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-\sigma^{\prime}}-{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-s}|\cdot|1-g(\mathfrak{p})|\to 0\end{split}

as
\sigma^{\prime}\to 1+
uniformly. So the sum we want is at most
o(1)+O(K^{1/2}\varepsilon)
as
\sigma^{\prime}\to 1+
. Since
\varepsilon
is arbitrary, we are done.
∎

For
(\sigma-1)/2\leq\sigma^{\prime}-1\leq\sigma-1
, by Lemma
6.6
,

\begin{split}&\qquad L\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^{\prime}-1}\right)/L\left(\frac{1}{\sigma-1}\right)\\
&=\exp\left(i\text{Im}\left(\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathfrak{I}(K),{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})\geq 3}({\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-\sigma^{\prime}}-{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-\sigma})(1-g(\mathfrak{p}))\right)\right)=\exp(o(1))\to 1\end{split}

as
\sigma\to 1+
(note that we used that
\text{Im }(1-g(\mathfrak{p}))=-\text{Im }g(\mathfrak{p})
).
Thus,
L
is a slowing varying function.

Now let

C:=Q(1)\exp\left(-\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathfrak{I}(K),{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})\geq 3}{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-1}(1-\text{Re}(g(\mathfrak{p})))\right).

By Lemma
6.6
again, we have

(44)

\begin{split}&\qquad\frac{G(s)}{L\left(\frac{1}{\sigma-1}\right)\zeta(s)}=Q(s)\exp\left(-\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathfrak{I}(K),{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})\geq 3}\left({\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-s}(1-g(\mathfrak{p}))+i\text{Im}{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-\sigma}g(\mathfrak{p})\right)\right)\\
&=Q(s)\exp\left(-\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathfrak{I}(K),{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})\geq 3}(({\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-s}-{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-\sigma})(1-g(\mathfrak{p}))+{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-\sigma}(1-\text{Re}(g(\mathfrak{p}))))\right)\\
&=(C+o(1))\exp(o(1))\to C\end{split}

as
\sigma\to 1+
uniformly for
|\tau|\leq M_{1}(\sigma-1)
. Let
R_{1}
be the residue of the Dedekind
\zeta
function at the pole
s=1
(see for example
[
23
, Theorem VII.7.6]
).
Then

(45)

\begin{split}&\qquad\left|G(s)-\frac{C\cdot R_{1}\cdot L\left(\frac{1}{\sigma-1}\right)}{s-1}\right|=\left|\frac{1}{s-1}\right|\left|\frac{G(s)}{L\left(\frac{1}{\sigma-1}\right)\zeta(s)}\cdot\zeta(s)(s-1)-CR_{1}\right|\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\;\;\quad\leq\frac{1}{\sigma-1}\left|\frac{G(s)}{L\left(\frac{1}{\sigma-1}\right)\zeta(s)}\cdot\zeta(s)(s-1)-CR_{1}\right|.\end{split}

Combining (
44
) with (
45
), we have that

(46)

\begin{split}G(s)-\frac{CR_{1}L\left(\frac{1}{\sigma-1}\right)}{s-1}=O\left(\frac{1}{K^{1/2}(\sigma-1)}\right)\end{split}

uniformly for
|\tau|\leq M_{1}(\sigma-1)
if
\sigma
is sufficiently close to 1.

Next we need to establish (
46
) for
M_{1}(\sigma-1)<|\tau|\leq M_{2}
for some
M_{2}>M_{1}(\sigma-1)
. We need the following estimate:

Lemma 6.7
.

Let
M_{2}>M_{1}(\sigma-1)
. If (
42
) holds, then

|G(s)|\leq O(M_{1}^{-1/2}(\sigma-1)^{-1})

uniformly for
M_{1}(\sigma-1)\leq|\tau|\leq M_{2}
, for all
\sigma
sufficiently near to
1,\sigma>1
.

Proof.

For each prime ideal
\mathfrak{p}
and
\tau\in{\mathbb{R}}
, since

\begin{split}&\qquad 2(1-\text{Re }{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{i\tau})=|1-{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{i\tau}|^{2}\leq 2|1-g(\mathfrak{p})|^{2}+2|g(\mathfrak{p})-{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{i\tau}|^{2}\\
&\leq 2|1-g(\mathfrak{p})|^{2}+4(1-\text{Re }{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-i\tau}g(\mathfrak{p})),\end{split}

we have

2\sum_{\mathfrak{p}}{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-\sigma}(1-\text{Re }{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{i\tau})\leq 2\sum_{\mathfrak{p}}{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-\sigma}|1-g(\mathfrak{p})|^{2}+4\sum_{\mathfrak{p}}{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-\sigma}(1-\text{Re }{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-i\tau}g(\mathfrak{p})).

By Lemma
6.3
, we have

\exp\left(\text{Re }\sum_{\mathfrak{p}}{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-\sigma}(1-{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{i\tau})\right)\geq C_{1}\frac{\zeta(\sigma)}{|\zeta(\sigma-i\tau)|},

for some universal constant
C_{1}>0
, and also

\exp\left(\text{Re }\sum_{\mathfrak{p}}{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-\sigma}(1-{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-i\tau}g(\mathfrak{p}))\right)\leq C_{2}\frac{\zeta(\sigma)}{|G(\sigma+i\tau)|},

for some universal constant
C_{2}>0
. On the other hand, we have that

\sum_{\mathfrak{p}}{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-\sigma}|1-g(\mathfrak{p})|^{2}\leq\sum_{\mathfrak{p}}{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-1}2(1-\text{Re }g(\mathfrak{p}))\leq C_{3}

for some constant
C_{3}>0
. Thus,

\begin{split}&\qquad|G(s)|\leq\frac{C_{2}\zeta(\sigma)}{\exp\left(\text{Re }\sum_{\mathfrak{p}}{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-\sigma}(1-{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-i\tau}g(\mathfrak{p}))\right)}\\
&\leq\frac{C_{2}\zeta(\sigma)\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\text{Re }\sum_{\mathfrak{p}}{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-\sigma}|1-g(\mathfrak{p})|^{2}\right)}{\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\text{Re }\sum_{\mathfrak{p}}{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-\sigma}(1-\text{Re }{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{i\tau})\right)}\leq C_{1}^{-1/2}C_{2}\exp(C_{3}/2)(\zeta(\sigma)|\zeta(\sigma-i\tau)|)^{1/2}.\end{split}

Finally, since

\left|\frac{1}{\sigma-i\tau-1}\right|=\frac{1}{\sqrt{(\sigma-1)^{2}+M_{1}^{2}(\sigma-1)^{2}}}\leq\frac{1}{M_{1}(\sigma-1)},

the desired bound follows from the fact that
\zeta(s)
has a simple pole at
s=1
, but is otherwise analytic in the disc
|s-1|<2M_{2}
.
∎

Note that when
M_{1}(\sigma-1)\leq|\tau|\leq M_{2}
, we have
\frac{1}{s-1}=O\left(\frac{1}{M_{1}(\sigma-1)}\right)
. So Lemma
6.7
implies that (
46
) also holds
uniformly for
M_{1}(\sigma-1)<|\tau|\leq M_{2}
if
\sigma
is sufficiently close to 1.
So we have

\limsup_{\sigma\to 1+}\sup_{|\tau|\leq M_{2}}(\sigma-1)\left|G(s)-\frac{C\cdot R_{1}\cdot L\left(\frac{1}{\sigma-1}\right)}{s-1}\right|\leq O(M_{1}^{-1/2}).

Setting
M_{2}=2M_{1}
and then letting
M_{1}\to\infty
,
it follows from Theorem
6.1
that
\E_{{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{u})\leq x}g(\mathfrak{u})
converges to
CL(\log x)
. Since we assume that
\alpha=0
, it suffices to show that

CL(\log x)=\prod_{{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})\leq x}(1-1/{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p}))(1+h_{1}(\mathfrak{p}))+o_{x\to\infty}(1).

From now on we set

\sigma:=1+(\log x)^{-1}
. By (
44
) and (
43
),

(47)

\begin{split}CL(\log x)=\frac{G(\sigma)}{\zeta(\sigma)}+o_{x\to\infty}(1)=Q(\sigma)\exp\left(-\sum_{{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})\geq 3}{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-\sigma}(1-g(\mathfrak{p}))\right)+o_{x\to\infty}(1).\end{split}

Let
0<\varepsilon\leq 1
to be chosen later. By Cauchy-Schwarz, Part (iv) of Lemma
4.11
and the assumption that
{\mathbb{D}}(g,1)<\infty
, we have

\begin{split}&\Bigl|\sum_{{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})\geq x^{\varepsilon}}{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-\sigma}(1-g(\mathfrak{p}))\Bigr|\leq\left(\sum_{{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})\geq x^{\varepsilon}}{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-\sigma}\right)^{1/2}\left(\sum_{{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})\geq x^{\epsilon}}{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-\sigma}\left|1-g(\mathfrak{p})\right|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\\
&\ll\Bigl(\sum_{{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})\geq x^{\varepsilon}}{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-\sigma}|1-g(\mathfrak{p})|^{2}\Bigr)^{1/2}\leq\Bigl(2\sum_{{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})\geq x^{\varepsilon}}{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-1}(1-\text{Re}(g(\mathfrak{p})))\Bigr)^{1/2}=o_{x\to\infty}(1).\end{split}

Similarly, by Part (iii) of Lemma
4.11
,

\begin{split}&\Bigl|\sum_{x^{\varepsilon}\leq{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})\leq x}{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-1}(1-g(\mathfrak{p}))\Bigr|\leq\left(\sum_{x^{\varepsilon}\leq{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})\leq x}{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-1}\right)^{1/2}\left(\sum_{x^{\epsilon}\leq{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})\leq x}{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-1}|1-g(\mathfrak{p})|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\\
&\ll\Bigl(\sum_{x^{\varepsilon}\leq{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})\leq x}{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-1}|1-g(\mathfrak{p})|^{2}\Bigr)^{1/2}\leq\Bigl(2\sum_{{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})\geq x^{\varepsilon}}{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-1}(1-\text{Re}(g(\mathfrak{p})))\Bigr)^{1/2}=o_{x\to\infty}(1).\end{split}

Therefore, we have that

(48)

\begin{split}\Bigl|\sum_{{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})\leq x}{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-1}(1-g(\mathfrak{p}))\Bigr|=o_{x\to\infty}(1).\end{split}

Finally, by the mean value inequality and Part (v) of Lemma
4.11
,

(49)

\begin{split}&\qquad\sum_{{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})\leq x^{\varepsilon}}({\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-1}-{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-\sigma})|1-g(\mathfrak{p})|\\
&\leq 2\sum_{{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})\leq x^{\varepsilon}}{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-1}(1-\exp(-\log{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})/\log x))\leq\frac{2}{\log x}\sum_{{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})\leq x^{\varepsilon}}\frac{\log{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})}{{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})}=O(\varepsilon).\end{split}

Combining (
48
) and (
49
), we have

\begin{split}\qquad&\left|\sum_{\mathfrak{p}}{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-\sigma}(1-g(\mathfrak{p}))-\sum_{{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})\leq x}{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-1}(1-g(\mathfrak{p}))\right|\\
&\leq\sum_{{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})\leq x^{\varepsilon}}({\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-1}-{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-\sigma})|1-g(\mathfrak{p})|+\left|\sum_{x^{\varepsilon}\leq{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})\leq x}{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-1}(1-g(\mathfrak{p}))\right|\leq O(\varepsilon).\end{split}

Since
\varepsilon
is arbitrary, we have

\sum_{\mathfrak{p}}{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-\sigma}(1-g(\mathfrak{p}))=\sum_{{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})\leq x}{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-1}(1-g(\mathfrak{p}))+o_{x\to\infty}(1).

Combining this with (
47
), together with the fact that
Q(1)
can be written as a quotient of two converging infinite products, we are done.
∎

Corollary 6.8
.

Let
g\colon\mathfrak{I}(K)\to{\mathbb{C}}
be a multiplicative function with
|g|\leq 1
.

(i)

If
{\mathbb{D}}(g,1)<\infty
and if for all prime ideal
\mathfrak{p}
with
{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})=2
, we have
g(\mathfrak{p}^{k})\neq-1
for some
k\geq 1
, then
\E_{\mathfrak{u}\in\mathfrak{I}(K)}g(\mathfrak{u})
exists but is not equal to 0.

(ii)

If
{\mathbb{D}}(g,{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{u})^{i\tau})<\infty
for some
\tau\neq 0
and if for all prime ideals
\mathfrak{p}
with
{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})=2
, we have
g(\mathfrak{p}^{k})\neq-2^{ik\tau}
for some
k\geq 1
, then
\E_{\mathfrak{u}\in\mathfrak{I}(K)}g(\mathfrak{u})
does not exist.

(iii)

If
{\mathbb{D}}(g,{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{u})^{i\tau})<\infty
for some
\tau\in{\mathbb{R}}
and if for some prime ideal
\mathfrak{p}
with
{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})=2
, we have
g(\mathfrak{p}^{k})=-2^{ik\tau}
for all
k\geq 1
, then
\E_{\mathfrak{u}\in\mathfrak{I}(K)}g(\mathfrak{u})
exists and equals to 0.

(iv)

If
\inf_{\tau\in{\mathbb{R}}}{\mathbb{D}}(g,{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{u})^{i\tau})=\infty
, then
\E_{\mathfrak{u}\in\mathfrak{I}(K)}g(\mathfrak{u})
exists and equals to 0.

Proof.

Let the notations be the same as in Theorem
2.12
and Theorem
6.1
. We begin by showing (i). If
{\mathbb{D}}(g,1)<\infty
, then by Theorem
2.12
, the limit of the averages
\E_{u\in\mathfrak{I}(K)}g(\mathfrak{u})
is equal to

L_{0}:=\lim_{x\to\infty}\prod_{\mathfrak{p}:\text{ prime ideals in }{\mathcal{O}_{K}},{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})\leq x}(1-1/{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p}))(1+h_{1}(\mathfrak{p})).

By our previous work in Lemma
6.7
above, the limit
L_{0}
exists, and can only be zero (by general theory of infinite products) if one of the factors
(1+h_{1}(\mathfrak{p}))
is equal to zero. This can only happen, given the definition of
h_{s}(\mathfrak{p})
as a geometric sum, if
{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})=2
, and simultaneously,
g(\mathfrak{p}^{k})=-1
for all
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

Given the conditions of (i), it follows that the limit exists, but is non-zero.

A similar argument (but to show that the limit is now actually equal to
0
) works for (iii).

First, note that, the limit

L_{\tau}:=\lim_{x\to\infty}\prod_{\mathfrak{p}:\text{ prime ideals in }{\mathcal{O}_{K}},{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})\leq x}(1-1/{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p}))(1+h_{1+i\tau}(\mathfrak{p})),

will exist for the
\tau\in{\mathbb{R}}
appearing in assertion (iii) given the distance assumption on
g
.

Furthermore, the condition stated in (iii) forces one of the factors in the product defining
L_{\tau}
to be
0
, and we get the result.

For Part (ii), assume for the sake of contradiction, that
L_{\tau}
exists. Then, as we have argued above, it must be non-zero.
Thus, it must be that

\frac{1}{x}\E_{{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{u})\leq x}g(\mathfrak{u}){\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{u})^{i\tau}\to L,

which, by the summation by parts trick, tells us that, in fact,

\frac{1}{x}\E_{{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{u})\leq x}g(\mathfrak{u})=Lx^{i\tau}(1+i\tau)^{-1}+o_{x\to\infty}(1).

By taking complex conjugates we may assume that
\tau\geq 0
, so now suppose that
\tau>0
. We set
x=\exp(2\pi m\tau^{-1})
, with
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and this allows us to deduce that
L(1+i\tau)^{-1}
is real, but now, if we let
x=\exp(2\pi(m+1/2)\tau^{-1})
, with
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we see that
iL(1+i\tau)^{-1}
is also real. This is an impossibility, since
L\neq 0
, which means that the averages do not converge, as claimed.

Finally, Part (iv) follows from Theorem
2.12
(i).
∎

7.
Proof of the Turán-Kubilius inequality and applications

In this section we prove the Turán-Kubilius inequality Theorem
2.13
and then use it to obtain an analog concentration estimates of
[
8
, Proposition 2.5]
.
Throughout this section, when
K={\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{-d})
for some squarefree
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
let
\gamma_{K}:=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{{\mathcal{N}}(u)\leq N}1

be the constant given by Corollary
4.6
.
We will need a counting lemma, but in order to properly show it, we must first have a certain estimate for
C
-Lipschitz regions of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, the definition of which we recall next.

7.1.
Proof of the Turán-Kubilius inequality

In this section we prove Theorem
2.13
.

First we need the definition of
C
-Lipschitz regions of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
.

Definition 7.1
.

Let
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
be the region given in polar coordinates by

S:=\{(\rho\cos\theta,\rho\sin\theta)\colon\theta\in[0,2\pi),0\leq\rho\leq r(\theta)\}

for some continuous
2\pi
-periodic map
r\colon{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
, i.e., with
r(\cdot+2\pi)=r(\cdot)
. We say that
S
is
C
-Lipschitz
if

r(\theta)\leq C\text{ and }|r(\theta)-r(\theta^{\prime})|\leq C|\theta-\theta^{\prime}|

for all
\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

We can now give a counting lemma, based on
[
19
, Lemma 10.1]
.

Lemma 7.2
.

Let
C\geq 1
.
For any
C
-Lipschitz region
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
, any
v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
and any
N\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+},N\geq 1
, we have that

\left|\left|(S_{N}+v)\cap{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\right|-N^{2}\cdot m(S)\right|\leq O(CN),

where
S_{N}+v:=\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\colon\frac{1}{N}(x-v)\in S\}
and
m(S)
is the area of
S
.

Proof.

Since
\lceil C\rceil\leq 2C
, we may assume without loss of generality that
C\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
Let
B_{N}
be the union of unit boxes in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
whose lower left corner belongs is a lattice point belonging to
(S_{N}+v)\cap{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
. Then, by construction, we ensured that
m(B_{N})=|(S_{N}+v)\cap{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}|
. Fix
N
. Divide
[0,2\pi)
into intervals
I_{i}=[\frac{2\pi(i-1)}{CN},\frac{2\pi i}{CN})

for
1\leq i\leq CN
, let
r_{i,\inf}:=\inf_{\theta\in I_{i}}r(\theta)
and
r_{i,\sup}:=\sup_{\theta\in I_{i}}r(\theta)
, and define

D_{N,i}:=\{(\rho\cos\theta,\rho\sin\theta)\colon\theta\in I_{i},\rho\in[r_{i,\inf}N,r_{i,\sup}N]\}

and
D^{\prime}_{N,i}
be the set of points whose distance to
D_{N,i}
is at most 2.

We first claim that
m(D^{\prime}_{N,i})=O(1)
.
Fix
N
and
i
. Let

E_{1}:=\{(\rho\cos\theta,\rho\sin\theta)\colon\theta\in I_{i},\rho\in[r_{i,\inf}N-10,r_{i,\sup}N+10]\},

E_{2}
be the rectangle adjacent to
E_{1}
with one of its edge being

\left\{(\rho\cos\theta,\rho\sin\theta)\colon\theta=\frac{2\pi(i-1)}{CN},\rho\in[r_{i,\inf}N-10,r_{i,\sup}N+10]\right\}

and the other edge of length 10 pointing out of
E_{1}
, and
E_{3}
be the rectangle adjacent to
E_{1}
with one of its edge being

\{(\rho\cos\theta,\rho\sin\theta)\colon\theta=\frac{2\pi i}{CN},\rho\in[r_{i,\inf}N-10,r_{i,\sup}N+10]\}

and the other edge of length 10 pointing out of
E_{1}
. Then
D^{\prime}_{N,i}\subseteq E_{1}\cup E_{2}\cup E_{3}

Since

\begin{split}&\quad\E_{{\mathcal{N}}(u)\leq N}\sum_{\mathfrak{p}^{k}\|Qu+a,{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})>M}|h(\mathfrak{p}^{k})|^{2}=\sum_{{\mathcal{N}}(\mathfrak{p})>M,k\geq 1}|h(\mathfrak{p}^{k})|^{2}\frac{1}{\gamma_{K}N}\sum_{{\mathcal{N}}(u)\leq N}\mathds{1}_{\mathfrak{p}^{k}\|Qu+a}\\

For the first term, by Theorem

We will make use of the next two simple lemmas:

Lemma 8.1
.

Let
f:\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times}\to{\mathbb{U}}
be a non-trivial multiplicative function (that is,
f\neq 1
). Let
\Phi_{M}
be a multiplicative Følner sequence in
\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times}
. Then,

\lim_{M\to\infty}\E_{u\in\Phi_{M}}f(u)=0.

Proof.

Since
{\mathbb{U}}
is compact, we consider limit points of the sequence
(\E_{u\in\Phi_{M}}f(u))_{M\in{\mathbb{N}}}
. Abusing notation, suppose that the limit of this sequence exists.

Since
f\neq 1
, let
v\in\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times}
be such that
f(v)\neq 1
. Since
\Phi_{M}
is a Følner sequence, we can write

\lim_{M\to\infty}\E_{u\in\Phi_{M}}f(u)=\lim_{K\to\infty}\E_{u\in v\Phi_{M}}f(u)=\lim_{M\to\infty}\E_{u\in\Phi_{M}}f(vu)=f(v)\lim_{M\to\infty}\E_{u\in\Phi_{M}}f(u).

Since
f(v)\neq 1
, this implies that the limit in question must be
0
. Therefore,
0
is the only possible accumulation point for this sequence, so compactness of
{\mathbb{U}}
completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 8.2
.

Let
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
be squarefree and

be a sequence. Let

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\E_{{\mathcal{N}}(u)\leq N}|a(u)-b(N)|\leq\varepsilon.

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\E_{{\mathcal{N}}(n),{\mathcal{N}}(m)\leq N}|a(l_{1}m+l_{2}n)-b(lN)|\ll_{K,l}\varepsilon,

where

Proof.

We can estimate

\E_{{\mathcal{N}}(n),{\mathcal{N}}(m)\leq N}|a(l_{1}m+l_{2}n)-b(lN)|\ll_{K,l}\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{{\mathcal{N}}(u)\leq lN}w_{N}(u)|a(u)-b(lN)|,

Since, given an

\liminf_{N\to\infty}\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times}}\text{Re}\left(\E_{{\mathcal{N}}(m),{\mathcal{N}}(n)\leq N}\int_{\mathcal{A}_{T_{0}}}w_{\delta}(m,n)f\Bigl(\frac{\ell(Qm+1+\alpha Qn)(Qm+1+\beta Qn)}{\ell^{\prime}(Qm+1)Qn}\Bigr)\,d\sigma(f)\right)

is at least
\sigma(\mathcal{A}_{T_{0}})\mu_{\delta_{0}}\geq\sigma(\{1\})\mu_{\delta_{0}}=a\mu_{\delta_{0}}
.
On the other hand,

\liminf_{N\to\infty}\sup_{Q\in\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times}}\left|\E_{{\mathcal{N}}(m),{\mathcal{N}}(n)\leq N}\int_{\mathcal{A}\backslash\mathcal{A}_{T_{0}}}w_{\delta}(m,n)f\Bigl(\frac{\ell(Qm+1+\alpha Qn)(Qm+1+\beta Qn)}{\ell^{\prime}(Qm+1)Qn}\Bigr)\,d\sigma(f)\right|

is bounded above by
a\mu_{\delta_{0}}/2
. We are done by setting
\rho_{0}:=a\mu_{\delta_{0}}/2
.
∎

8.1.
Further discussions

We end this article with a discussion of potential directions that can be studied next. More particularly, we will discuss the case of real quadratic fields, and also comment on the potential to develop quadratic concentration estimates (as a generalization of the linear concentration estimates that were obtained in Section
7
).

8.1.1.
Real quadratic fields

The main issue with real quadratic fields is the fact that they have infinitely many units. This breaks down many of our arguments irreparably, especially because the balls one could hope to define with the norm (or even its absolute value) have infinitely many elements. This means that the connection with our improved version of Halász’s theorem, one of the key ingredients of this work, cannot be made.

A potential approach one may consider is to average along a suitable cut of the ball, where we only take a few representatives of each element times powers of the units. Next, it would be key to be able to apply a suitable version of Halász’s theorem in this context, switching to boxes instead of balls, if necessary (and then showing that the two averaging methods can be compared). It might by useful to start by assuming that
f(\epsilon)=1
for all units
\epsilon
, and then upgrade to an arbitrary
f
.

8.1.2.
Quadratic concentration estimates for rings of integers

We conjecture that it is possible to develop quadratic concentration estimates in the spirit of
[
7
]
, but due to space considerations we have not attempted to develop them in the present paper. As a first step, one could try to prove them for
{\mathbb{Z}}[i]
given that it is the closest to
{\mathbb{Z}}
, having a norm that is easy to work with, and still preserving the UFD property. Then, one can see if they can be extended to arbitrary quadratic imaginary fields, and from there try to move it to general number fields (of course, the issue we raised in the previous subsection of the existence of infinitely many units still needs to be circumvented).
