The Molecular Heterogeneity of NRAS Variants in Thyroid Nodules
Authors: Emma De Ravin, Mohammed Alshalalfa, Ruochen Jiang, Yangyang Hao, Joshua P Klopper, Elizabeth Cottrill
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjective NRAS variants are the most common mutations in thyroid nodules with (B)ethesda III/IV cytology. This study aims to characterize co‐occurring variants, molecular signatures, and hallmarks of cancer pathways in nodules with NRAS variants.Study DesignRetrospective database analysis.MethodsThe database of nodules sent for Afirma GSC testing was analyzed for the most common NRAS hotspot mutations, co‐occurring mutations, BRAF‐RAS score (BRS), thyroid differentiation score (TDS), ERK expression, and activity levels of hallmarks of cancer pathways.ResultsOf 87,817 Afirma GSC‐suspicious or BV/VI nodules, 12.6% had NRAS mutations. Of those, 79.7% were NRAS p.Q61R and 19.4% were NRAS p.Q61K. 421 nodules (3.8%) had co‐occurring mutations, EIF1AX being the most common (1%, about twice the rate seen alone). Of 872 nodules with TERT promoter mutations (TERTp), 7.8% had concurrent NRAS + TERT mutations (more than twice the rate of TERT alone). The presence of NRAS (+/− co‐mutations) was highly RAS‐like on the BRS score. Hallmarks of cancer pathways analyzed across NRAS+ nodules, with TERTp profiling, revealed 2 distinct clusters: one with higher inflammatory pathway activity, and another with higher DNA repair, estrogen response, and MYC activity. The latter had a higher rate of BV/VI nodules. NRASp.Q61R was equally distributed across the 2 clusters.Conclusion NRAS p.Q61R/K comprises 99% of reported NRAS variants. TERTp is the most frequent co‐mutation. Among NRAS p.Q61R/K nodules, there are 2 different clusters of samples based on the activity of hallmarks of cancer pathways. Further studies correlating these clusters with clinical and pathological outcomes are necessary.Keywords: gene expression profiling, molecular diagnostic techniques, NRAS mutation, thyroid nodule

Thyroid cancer is the most common endocrine malignancy, with an estimated 44,020 new cases to be diagnosed in 2025. 1 Although thyroid nodules are a common clinical finding, detected in up to 65% of the population, only a small proportion (approximately 1%‐5%) harbor malignancy and warrant further workup and managment. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 Fine needle aspiration (FNA) with cytologic evaluation is the standard initial diagnostic approach; however, approximately 10% to 20% of nodules yield indeterminate cytologic results, including Bethesda III (BIII, atypia of undetermined significance) and Bethesda IV (BIV, follicular neoplasm) nodules. 8 , 9 , 10 The risk of malignancy for these indeterminate thyroid nodules (ITN) is highly variable, ranging from 6% to 40%, thus historically, standard of care for these patients included a diagnostic thyroid lobectomy. 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 However, this approach is suboptimal given the cost and morbidity of a thyroidectomy procedure, and the fact that ~75% of ITN is proven benign on final histopathology. 18 In this context, genetic analysis and molecular diagnostics have become an increasingly useful adjunct to FNA to refine risk assessment, increase diagnostic accuracy, and guide clinical and surgical decision‐making. 11 , 19 , 20

RAS isoform mutations, most frequently NRAS mutations, are the most common mutations found in ITN and therefore the focus of this study. 19 , 21 , 22 , 23 The NRAS gene encodes a GTPase that is a key regulator of cell signaling and gene expression involved in cellular growth and cell cycle progression and differentiation, particularly in the RAS‐RAF‐MEK‐ERK pathway. 24 Oncogenic NRAS mutations result in constitutive, aberrant activation of the MAPK pathway, resulting in persistent downstream signaling, which drives uncontrolled proliferation and resistance to apoptosis in thyroid follicular cells. 25 However, NRAS mutation detection provides limited clinical information for risk stratification even amongst ITN, as malignancy rates and clinical outcomes vary significantly, suggesting underlying molecular heterogeneity. 22 , 26 , 27 , 28 , 29

One source of this clinical and biologic heterogeneity is the interaction of NRAS mutations with other co‐occurring mutations, including in the TERT promoter, TP53, EIF1AX, PI3K, PTEN, JAK2, and AKT1. These co‐mutations can drive more aggressive phenotypes and clinicopathologic features, thus analyzing these additional alterations is important. 25 , 30

The Afirma Genomic Sequencing Classifier (GSC) was developed to address the diagnostic challenge of indeterminate cytology. Through exome enriched next generation RNA sequencing (RNA‐seq), thyroid nodule aspirates are sequenced for more than 21,000 genes, identifying driver mutations, fusion genes, and expression‐based risk molecular classifiers and signatures. 31 , 32 , 33 , 34 , 35 Thus, the database of samples with Afirma molecular testing has a wealth of genomic information. Evaluating these additional oncogenic alterations may provide key information for risk stratification and management.

The purpose of this study was to conduct an overarching analysis of the database of Afirma testing samples to characterize co‐occurring variants, molecular signatures, and hallmarks of cancer pathways in BIII or BIV thyroid nodules with NRAS variants. We also further explore NRAS mutant nodules to identify biologically distinct subgroups that may provide insight into the observed differences in NRAS mutated tumor behavior and clinical outcomes.

Methods

Consecutive FNA samples that were sent for the Afirma GSC testing at the discretion of the ordering provider from January 2018 till June 2024 and passed quality control and reporting measures were evaluated. Each Afirma GSC sample has exome‐enriched open RNA sequencing, and this information is maintained in a database where population‐level information can be evaluated in a deidentified manner. 33 Samples with indeterminate cytology (BIII/IV) that were classified as GSC‐(S)uspicious or samples with BV/VI were selected for further analysis as these samples are not Afirma GSC‐Benign and would more likely to be considered for surgical intervention. The genomic alteration (variants, fusions) landscape from the Afirma Xpression Atlas (XA) of the selected nodules was analyzed. In addition, TERT promoter (TERTp) mutations were available for a subset of these samples that were sent from March 2023 until June 2024.

The BRAF‐RAS (BRS) scores, ERK activity, follicular mesenchymal transition (FMT), thyroid differentiation score (TDS), and hallmarks of cancer activity pathways scores were calculated for the samples of interest. 36 , 37 , 38 We assessed the activity of 50 hallmark gene sets from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) for each sample. Each gene set comprises approximately 50 to 200 genes representing well‐defined biological processes or cellular states. For each pathway, normalized gene expression values (z‐scores) of the constituent genes were averaged to derive a pathway activity score. Consequently, each sample was assigned activity scores for all 50 hallmark pathways. Hierarchical clustering was used to group samples based on hallmarks activity scores. Wilcoxon test was used to assess molecular signatures differences between samples with different NRAS variants.

Results

Of 87,817 samples that were either GSC‐S or had BV/VI cytology, 11,107 (12.6%) had an expressed NRAS variant (Table 1). Of those, 79.7% were NRAS p.Q61R and 19.4% were NRAS p.Q61K (Table 2). Overall, 421 nodules (3.8%) had co‐occurring mutations (Table 3) and the proportion was similar whether the index mutation was NRAS p.Q61R or K (Supplemental Table S1, available online). Within a subset of 872 NRAS+ samples with TERTp profiled, 7.8% had a concomitant TERTp promoter mutation (Table 3). There was no difference between rate of co‐occurring variants in samples with the Q61R or Q61K mutation.

To further assess the molecular difference between NRAS+ samples with Q61K and Q61R, we found that the BRS, ERK, FMT, and TDS molecular signatures were statistically different between the 2 variants, but more similar to each other than compared to samples that were GSC‐S or BV/VI that were XA‐negative (samples that did not have a detectable expressed molecular variant or fusion, Figure 1). The NRAS+ samples were more RAS‐like, had higher ERK activity scores, and higher TDS.

Molecular differences between NRAS+ samples with Q61R and Q61K mutations compared to XA‐negative Bethesda III/IV samples. Boxplot demonstrates that NRAS p.Q61R and p.Q61K are different, but more similar compared to XA‐negative samples.

To further decipher the molecular heterogeneity of samples with NRAS, we clustered a subset of 872 samples (with known TERTp status) based on the activity of hallmarks of cancer pathways (Figure 2A). We observed 2 clusters of samples where 1 is enriched with Bethesda V/VI, TERTp+, and had lower inflammation scores, but higher activity of metabolism and DNA repair pathways. The other XA variants were rare and not associated with a cluster.

We next applied clustering of pathways to GSC‐S, NRAS pQ61R+ samples without co‐occurring mutations (n = 596) and observed a similar clustering of samples (Figure 2B) suggesting that there is molecular heterogeneity beyond what is influenced by expressed variants of other genes.

Discussion

In the present study, we have investigated the molecular landscape and differences of thyroid nodules with NRAS mutations sent for molecular testing from 2018 to 2024. The primary clinical use of thyroid nodule molecular testing is to risk‐stratify ITNs and potentially avoid diagnostic surgery in molecularly benign nodules. 39 When molecular testing is positive, knowledge of the expressed molecular variants and fusions may change the diagnostic risk. For example, the risk of malignancy for an Afirma GSC‐Suspicious nodule with no expressed mutations is reported at 50%. However, the detection of an NRAS p.Q61R mutation is reported as a 75% risk of malignancy or NIFTP. 32 In ITNs that are not Afirma GSC‐Benign, the RAS family of mutations are the most common expressed alteration. 40 Among RAS+ nodules, the NRAS variants are the most common, and thus were the area of focus for this study.

The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) described the transcriptional and mutational landscape primarily of classic papillary thyroid cancers (PTC). 36 Though PTC is the most common type of thyroid cancer overall, follicular adenomas, NIFTP, and follicular subtypes of thyroid carcinoma are more frequently found in ITN. 41 Yoo et al extended the findings of the TCGA by including follicular‐derived lesions and describing 3 distinct molecular subtypes regardless of their histology (BRAF‐like, RAS‐like, and Non‐BRAF‐Non‐RAS [NBNR]). 42 In this study, the transcriptomic profiles of follicular adenomas, minimally invasive follicular thyroid carcinoma (FTC) or encapsulated follicular variant of PTC were indistinguishable. On final histopathology, the tumors studied were almost all benign or indolent/low‐risk thyroid cancers.

The BRAF‐like, RAS‐like, and Non‐BRAF‐Non‐RAS (NBNR) categorization not only provided nomenclature for molecularly similar mutations but also introduced prognostic information based on the subtype. For example, thyroid tumors with RAS‐like mutations have a lower frequency of regional lymph node metastases and extrathyroidal extension compared to cancers with BRAF‐like mutations, and a higher frequency than NBNR. 43 Over a population, RAS‐like mutations predict low risk thyroid cancer yet, as stated, cannot discriminate benign from malignant lesions with high accuracy. 44 Additionally, some RAS mutated thyroid cancers will present as high‐risk disease including distant metastases and elevated disease‐specific mortality. 45 Thus, analyses beyond the presence or absence of RAS mutations are required to optimize personalized genomic information for diagnostic and prognostic purposes.

In this analysis of thyroid nodules sent for Afirma GSC testing as part of routine clinical practice, mutations at position 61 of the NRAS gene predominated, particularly the Q61R amino acid substitution. Across all Bethesda categories, the NRAS p.Q61R or K mutation accounted for almost 100% of the NRAS mutations. Co‐mutations were rare, with <5% cumulative NRAS mutations occurring in the presence of another mutation. Therefore, genomic differences amongst NRAS‐mutated nodules are very unlikely to be driven by another classically described thyroid tumor mutation. As expected, NRAS‐mutated nodules were more RAS‐like on the BRS score than those that were XA negative. Interestingly, ERK activity was higher along with TDS compared to XA‐negative lesions. Other notable findings were statistically significant, albeit small absolute, differences in BRS, ERK, FMT, and TDS expression between the NRASp.Q61 hotspot mutations. All were less favorable for NRASp.Q61R relative to K. In an in vitro model system with constitutive expression NRASp.Q61R, there was upregulation of SNAI2 and TWIST2 gene expression which demonstrated cellular morphologic changes consistent with epithelial‐mesenchymal transition (EMT). 46 However, this study, nor any other study we could find, showed isolated or comparative genomic changes compared to expression of NRASp.Q61K. The novel finding in this manuscript may be a catalyst for parsing the differences between these 2 types of alterations.

Notably, the hallmarks of cancer pathways activity scores show distinct clustering of NRAS+ tumors with differential activity of inflammation and metabolism pathways. Thus, we demonstrate that NRAS mutations are not a monolith, and deeper genomic differences are detectable in this cohort. These pathway differences may reflect different mechanisms of tumorigenesis within these samples. For example, samples with high inflammation pathways may represent those lesions with chronic inflammation that create a tumor environment predisposed to cellular plasticity and can contribute to the development of oncogenic mutations. 47 In these lesions with overexpressed inflammatory pathways, NRAS may not be the primary neoplasia driver. Alternatively, those tumors with overexpressed DNA repair, MYC activity, and estrogen response pathways may be more likely to be follicular variant lesions that may be more directly RAS driven via MAPK pathway activation with more propensity for progression. 48 , 49 , 50 , 51 A recent study by Golding et al., evaluating the ability of novel thyroid tumor invasion and regional lymph node metastases classifiers to rule out clinically significant disease showed that only ~50 to 60% of RAS‐mutated nodules were assigned a low‐risk result, further substantiating the observed heterogeneity in this study. 52

There are several limitations to this study, most notably a lack of final histopathology outcomes and long‐term oncologic follow up of the assessed nodules. Though statistically significant differences are noted molecularly, the clinical relevance is inferred based on knowledge of cancer progression but is ultimately unknown. Additionally, there is no information on the rationale or potential bias for ordering Afirma GSC on the tested nodules. Finally, only a subset of the studied nodules had TERTp testing, which is an opt‐in choice when ordering Afirma molecular testing. Albeit rare, this important prognostic mutation may be underreported in this cohort. Alternatively, we cannot account for potential clinical bias that led ordering clinicians to request TERTp testing. Perhaps larger tumor sizes, more concerning ultrasound features, or concerning clinical history informed this decision, which may actually enrich for the relative detection of TERTp.

In summary, although NRAS is the most common single mutation found in ITNs sent for molecular testing, there is molecular heterogeneity evident amongst lesions with this same canonical mutation. As we strive to provide truly personalized testing and care, this study demonstrates deeper genomic analyses will likely be necessary to differentiate RAS‐mutated thyroid nodules, including HRAS and KRAS variants, that are benign or malignant and to improve upon current prognostic models.
